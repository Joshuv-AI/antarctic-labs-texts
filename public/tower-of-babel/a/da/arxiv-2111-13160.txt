Introduction to SPDEs from Probability and PDE
A. Mayorcas

Abstract




Chapter 1
Introduction and Preliminaries

This mini-course aims to serve as a brief introduction to the study of stochastic partial differential equations. The main goal of these notes is to provide a useful resource for both PDE and stochastic analysts. We aim to demonstrate both how some standard PDE techniques can be extended to the stochastic setting and how the tools of stochastic analysis can be extended to infinite dimensional settings, common to the analysis of PDE. The main focus of this course is on the variational method for coercive-monotone SPDE. The tools we introduce along the way should be familiar to researchers in both areas and hopefully this provides a sufficiently broad base of tools for applications to problems beyond the scope of this course. In Chapter
4
we give a brief introduction to the pathwise approach to SPDE, which has been a topic of significant research in recent years, sparked by the development of novel approaches to singular SPDE,
[
Hai09
,
GIP15
]
.

The variational method for SPDE stems from early work by E. Pardoux,
[
Par72
]
, which extended the variational approach to PDE developed by J. Lions,
[
Lio11
]
. The variational approach to SPDE has been developed in a number of directions and we refer to
[
LR17
,
PR07
]
for a more comprehensive treatment of the literature and material. In particular, however, we mention the early works by I. Gyongy & N. Krylov,
[
GK81a
,
GK81b
,
Gyö82
]
in which the authors extend the results presented here to the case of general semi-martingale drivers. Alongside the variational approach there are broadly two other, non-pathwise, frameworks for solving SPDE; the perspective of martingale problems,
[
MV88
,
Par07
]
and the semi-group approach,
[
DPZ14
,
Hai09
]
. We also wish to mention the book by F. Flandoli,
[
Fla11
]
that focuses on stochastic perturbations of fluid equations, the collected work,
[
DKM+09
]
, that contains an overview of a number of different problems in the field of SPDE and the book by L. Zambotti on stochastic obstacle problems,
[
Zam17
]
.

These notes were written to accompany a
6
hour mini-course delivered at the University of Oxford in April 2021. The four chapters broadly follow the material presented across four lectures, with more background, references and details to some arguments. In the remainder of this chapter we first list some conventions, notation and terminology that we use throughout, Subsection
1.0.1
. Then in Section
1.1
we give some background on various motivations for studying SPDE problems, focussing in particular on a presentation of a simplified version of the Kraichnan model for a passive scalar in a turbulent fluid. In the second half of that section, Subsection
1.1.2
, we give a worked example of the variational method that is presented abstractly in Chapter
3
. The remainder of the first chapter is dedicated to recalling some necessary background material and developing the theory of stochastic integration in Hilbert spaces. In Chapter
2
we return to deterministic problems and present a summary of J. Lions variational approach to coercive PDE. Chapter
3
contains the main focus of the course, the variational approach to coercive-monotone SPDE. We present the abstract method along with an extension of the worked example given in Subsection
1.1.2
. Finally in Chapter
4
we give a short introduction to the pathwise approach to non-singular SPDE, using a stochastic Burger’s equation with white noise forcing as an example.

1.0.1
Conventions, Notation and Terminology

Throughout these notes we use the conventional notation for stochastic equations, that is we use the shorthand

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{t}=A(u_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+B(u_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad\iff\quad u_{t}=u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}A(u_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}B(u_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

(1.0.1)

Furthermore, when we refer to
weak solutions
for problems such as (
1.0.1
), we mean a weak solution in the PDE sense, that is we ask (
1.0.1
) to hold in the sense of distributions. This is not to be confused with the notion of weak solution in stochastic analysis, which typically means a solution not adapted to a given filtration. The solutions we consider will always be weak PDE solutions but strong stochastic solutions. We adopt a slightly unusual convention and write
[X]_{t}
for the quadratic variation of a stochastic process, instead of the usual angle brackets. This is to avoid confusion with the standard notation for inner products on Hilbert spaces and duality pairings between Banach spaces.

Unless otherwise specified, all Banach and Hilbert spaces are separable and whenever we refer to a
basis

(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of a Hilbert space we mean a complete, orthonormal system. For a Banach space
E
and a Hilbert space
U
we typically write
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{E}
for the norm on
E
and
|\,\cdot\,|_{U},\,\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{U}
for the norm and inner product on
U
. If such an expression appears without an explicit label it will always be explained what is meant. We write
E^{\ast}
for the dual of
E
, the set of bounded, linear functionals
\varphi:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We say that a sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset E
converges weakly in
E
, if
(\varphi(u_{n}))_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}
converges in
\mathbb{R}
for every
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
. We say that a sequence
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset E^{\ast}
converges in weak-
*
if the evaluations
(\varphi_{n}(u))_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}
are a convergent sequence for every
u\in E
. Through the Riesz representation theorem we identify the dual of a Hilbert space with itself, so that we say a sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset U
converges weakly if
(\langle h,u_{n}\rangle_{U})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}
is convergent for every
h\in U
.
We say that a map
[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto u_{t}\in E
is continuous if it is continuous with respect to the strong topology on
E
. Given a
T>0
, we equip the space of continuous maps
u:[0,T]\rightarrow E
with the structure of a Banach space, by defining the norm

\|u\|_{C_{T}E}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u_{t}\|_{E}.

Given a measure space
(E,\mathcal{B}(E),\mu)
, a measurable space
(F,\mathcal{B}(F))
and a measurable map
X:E\rightarrow F
, the push-forward of
\mu
by
X
,
X\#\mu=\mu(X^{-1}(\,\,\cdot\,))
defines a measure on
F
and for any integrable function
g:F\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(F)
, we have the identity

\int_{A}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X\#\mu(x)=\int_{X^{-1}(A)}g(X(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Given a family of sets
\{A\}
, we write
\sigma(\{A\})
for the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing the collection. Given a measurable space
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
we write
\mathcal{P}(E)
for the set of probability measures on
E
. We say that a sequence of probability measures
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{P}(E)
converges weakly to
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
if
\int_{E}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu^{n}\rightarrow\int_{E}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
for all
f:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, continuous and bounded. We say that a sequence of random variables converges weakly if their laws converge weakly. The strong topology on
\mathcal{P}(E)
is the topology induced by the total variation distance.

For two scalars,
s,\,t\in\mathbb{R}
, we use the notation
s\wedge t
to denote their minimum and
s\vee t
to denote their maximum. Typically we use this when considering functions
u:[0,\infty)\rightarrow E
, where we write
u_{t\wedge s}
to mean the value of
u
at the minimum of
s,\,t
. For functions of multiple variables,
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we use standard multi-index notation to denote partial derivatives, i.e for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
\partial_{x}^{k}u
denotes the
|k|^{\text{th}}
partial derivative in the coordinates describe by the multi-index
k=(k_{1},\,\ldots,k_{d})
. If we write
D^{|k|}
we mean the vector of all possible
|k|^{\text{th}}
order derivatives.

1.1
Randomness in PDE

In very abstract terms we may think of a typical PDE problem as being constituted of the following elements; a finite dimensional metric space,
\Gamma
, a partial differential operator
L(u,Du,\ldots)
, acting on functions
u:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a non-linear functional,
F
, and some data
g
defined on the boundary
\partial\Gamma
. The associated PDE problem is to find
u
, solving

\begin{cases}Lu=F(u,Du,D^{2}u,\ldots),&\text{inside }\Gamma,\\
u|_{\partial\Gamma}=g,\end{cases}

(1.1.1)

in a suitable way. The distinction between the linear part,
L
, and non-linear part,
F
, is not necessary but provides a useful framing for the equations considered in these notes.

In this setting there are essentially four ways in which noise can be included in a PDE problem; through the linear operator
L
, through the non-linearity
F
, through the domain,
\Gamma
or the boundary data
u|_{\partial\Gamma}=g
. We will not explicitly consider the latter two cases; some treatment of random data equations can be found in
[
Fla17
,
Tzv16
]
while some treatment of equation on random domains can be found in
[
XT06
,
HPS16
]
. In fact we will mostly restrict ourselves to cases where randomness occurs in the
right hand side
of the equation,
F
.

Motivations for studying SPDE come from many directions in both mathematics and the applied sciences. To mention only a few references, they have found applications in: constructive quantum field theory,
[
DH87
,
GH18
,
Hai14b
,
Kup18
]
; the study of disordered media,
[
Lab19
,
AC15
]
; non-linear filtering,
[
Zak69
,
Par79
]
; population dynamics,
[
Daw72
,
Fle75
]
and stochastic fluid dynamics including models of turbulence,
[
Kra68
,
Fla11
]
. In particular we refer to the introductions of
[
DPZ14
,
Par07
,
DKM+09
]
for more detailed summaries of some of these applications along with others not mentioned here. In order to motivate this course a little better we proceed to describe how an SPDE model can arise in the study of a passive scalar transported by a turbulent flow. This model is related to the Kraichnan model of turbulence,
[
Kra68
]
, mentioned above.

1.1.1
Passive Scalar in a Turbulent Vector Field

We present a simplified version of a model developed by separately by R. Kraichnan,
[
Kra68
]
and A. Kazantsev
[
Kaz68
]
, both of which were inspired by the work of G. Batchelor on turbulence,
[
Bat00
,
Mof02
]
. More detailed presentations and analysis of this model and its variants can be found among the non-exhaustive references,
[
Fla11
,
FL19
,
FGL21
,
GY21
,
MK99
,
CDG07
]
. We will use SPDE related to this model as recurring examples throughout the course.

Let us imagine that we are interested in the diffusion of a given concentration in a two dimensional fluid. We may model this situation by the simple transport equation, on
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\Gamma_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
closed, bounded,
\partial\Gamma_{0}
sufficiently smooth, and
(v_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a family of divergence free vector fields,

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{t}-v_{t}\cdot\nabla u_{t}=0,&\text{ in }\Gamma\\
u|_{t=0}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{0}|}\mathds{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}.&\end{cases}

(1.1.2)

The solution
u_{t}:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
describes the concentration at time
t\geq 0
. Formally speaking, due to the divergence free assumption, for any solution to (
1.1.2
), one has
\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\int_{\Gamma}u_{0}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\int_{\Gamma}u_{t}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
. That is mass cannot be lost during the evolution, but the local density may vary. For simplicity, let us assume that
\Gamma=\mathbb{T}^{2}
, the two dimensional torus and as a toy example let us consider the simple vector fields,

v_{t}(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}-y\\
x\end{bmatrix}\theta_{t},

for
t\mapsto\theta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
a differentiable function. The characteristics of
(
1.1.2
)
are the system of ODEs,

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}X_{t}=Y_{t}\frac{d}{dt}\theta_{t},\\
\frac{d}{dt}Y_{t}=-X_{t}\frac{d}{dt}\theta_{t}.\end{cases}

(1.1.3)

Provided the paths
t\mapsto\theta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
are sufficiently regular (and we ignore the issue of boundary data) we can hope to solve (
1.1.3
) and using a flow map then give the solution to (
1.1.2
) as
u(t,x,y)=u_{0}(X^{-1}_{t}(x),Y^{-1}_{t}(y))
, where
(X^{-1}_{t},Y^{-1}_{t})
denotes the backwards flow associated to (
1.1.3
), see
[
Eva10
, Sec. 3.2]
.

Now we imagine that there is some uncertainty over the coefficient
\theta_{t}
. For example the concentration might be a dye diffusing far out at sea and we only have intermittent readings on the local fluid velocity. Alternatively, as in
[
Kra68
]
, we might intentionally introduce some randomness to model a turbulent velocity field. We can account for both situations by fixing a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and now considering random coefficients, given by a measurable map,
(\omega,t)\mapsto\theta_{t}(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}
. We can also see the map
\Omega\ni\omega\mapsto\theta(\omega)
as a random mapping into the space of paths. If we assume that for
\mathbb{P}
-a.a.
\omega\in\Omega
, we still have
\theta(\omega)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R})
, then we may replace (
1.1.3
) with the random system of characteristics,

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}X_{t}(\omega)=Y_{t}(\omega)\frac{d}{dt}\theta_{t}(\omega),\\
\frac{d}{dt}Y_{t}(\omega)=-X_{t}(\omega)\frac{d}{dt}\theta_{t}(\omega).\end{cases}

(1.1.4)

Again, up to solving this system, we provide a solution the random PDE,

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{t}(\omega)-v_{t}(\omega)\cdot\nabla u_{t}(\omega)=0,&\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{2}\\
u|_{t=0}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{0}|}\mathds{1}_{\Gamma_{0}}.\end{cases}

(1.1.5)

By integrating over
\omega\in\Omega
we can now answer more nuanced questions regarding the evolution of
u
. For example we could study the expected density in a certain region, or the expected time to total mixing. The idea is that if the random coefficients are described by a sufficiently rich probability space, and of a form suitable to some statistical properties of the situation at hand, then our model can be made robust to many realised scenarios.

To this end, we note that the model considered so far is very simple and unlikely to describe many situations of interest sufficiently well. In order to enrich the model let us first develop the form of vector fields we consider. We now define,

v_{t}(x,y):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{k}v_{k}(x,y)\theta^{k}_{t},\quad v_{k}(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}-(y-a_{k})\\
x-b_{k}\end{bmatrix},\quad\theta^{k}_{t}\sim\theta_{t}\quad\text{i.i.d,}

(1.1.6)

with
(a_{k},b_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset\Gamma
given centres of rotation, the coefficients
\sigma_{k}
chosen so as to ensure the sum converges suitably,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. (note that this is possible since we specified that
\Gamma
be bounded) and
(\theta^{k}_{t})_{k\geq 1}
a family of i.i.d random scalar paths. Without loss of generality we may assume that
\mathbb{E}[\theta^{k}_{t}]=0
for all
k,\,t
. This superposition of random, divergence free, vector fields now gives a lot of scope to accommodate an expected fluid flow and with randomness built in to account for uncertainty. In the Kraichnan model the spatial features of the vector field flow is achieved in a different manner, but the end results are qualitatively similar, see
[
CDG07
, Sec. III]
and
[
GY21
, Sec. 5]
for presentations of this more detailed model.

While the spatial properties of the flow (
1.1.6
) can now be adjusted to a large extent, the time dependence is constrained by requiring that
t\mapsto\theta^{k}_{t}
be at least
C^{1}
. For example, if we choose the
\sigma_{k}
so that the series is
\ell^{2}
convergent, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|v_{t}(x,y)-v_{s}(x,y)|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{k}^{2}|v_{k}(x,y)|^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\theta_{t}^{k}-\theta_{s}^{k}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{E}[|\partial_{t}\theta^{k}_{s}|^{2}]|t-s|^{2}\|\sigma_{\,\cdot\,}v_{\,\cdot\,}(x,y)\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}.

That is, the values
v_{t}(x,y)
and
v_{s}(x,y)
must be relatively well correlated. So if we expect the vector fields at a given location to oscillate faster than linearly in time we will be unable to capture this by our current model. Following similar considerations, in
[
Kra68
]
, Kraichnan proposed an extension, allowing for arbitrarily short correlation times for the vector flows. In order to achieve this one can replace the
(\theta^{k})_{k\geq 1}
with less regular trajectories, a common choice being Brownian motions,
(W^{k})_{k\geq 1}
, where one has,

\mathbb{E}[|W^{k}_{t}-W^{k}_{s}|^{2}]=|t-s|.

More pertinently, when we consider the characteristic equations, we replace
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\theta_{t}
with the differential
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}
and if we compute the covariation between
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}
we formally have,

\mathbb{E}[\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}]=\delta(t-s).

So we see that replacing
(\theta^{k})_{k\geq 1}
with
(W^{k})_{k\geq 1}
allows for a fluid flow with arbitrarily small correlation times. While this extreme may also not be physically well justified it provides more flexibility than the previous regularity requirements, gives way to a mathematically rich theory and can be further adapted to more physically relevant settings.

The price to pay for introducing vector fields with this more singular behaviour is that the ODEs, (
1.1.4
), are no longer trivially well-posed. In particular, one has that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., the paths
t\mapsto W^{k}_{t}
are only almost
1/2
-Hölder continuous and so
t\mapsto\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{k}_{t}
represents a proper distribution. In order to solve (
1.1.4
) in this case then we require a definition of integrals
\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{k}_{s}
for paths
t\mapsto X_{t}
of no better regularity than
t\mapsto W_{t}
. This is a primary aim of stochastic analysis, which the remainder of this chapter is dedicated to. While we refer to (
1.1.5
) as a random PDE, we refer to,

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{t}-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{k}v_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{k}=0,&\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{2},\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0},&\end{cases}

(1.1.7)

as a stochastic PDE (SPDE). This is to emphasise the fact that one requires some additional theory in order to handle the time integral in (
1.1.7
).

1.1.2
A Worked Example of the Variational Method

In the final subsection of this introductory portion of the notes let us present a model example of the variational method that we will cover in more detail in the rest of the course. This example serves both as a motivation for the model described at the end of Chapter
3
and an example walk through of the abstract method in a relatively simple case. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space, carrying
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a standard,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
valued Brownian motion. We consider the semi-linear, parabolic SPDE, for simplicity on the
d
-dimensional torus,
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\Delta u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\sigma}\nabla u\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},&\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}.\end{cases}

(1.1.8)

Remark 1.1.1
.

Note that (
1.1.8
) has the structure of a diffusive equivalent of the transport equation (
1.1.2
) with
v_{t}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}
. This can be generalised to the superposition example discussed in that context
v_{t}=\sum_{k}\sqrt{\sigma}_{k}v_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{k}_{t}
.

For the sake of presentation we restrict to the one dimensional case here, although the method applies almost
mutatis mutandi
in higher dimensions. Let
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
be the standard Fourier basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. Then defining
u_{k;t}:=\langle u_{t},e_{k}\rangle
, we derive from (
1.1.8
) the system of SDEs,

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{k;t}=-(2\pi k)^{2}u_{k;t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-2\sqrt{\sigma}\pi iku_{k;t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad u_{k;0}=\langle u_{0},e_{k}\rangle.

(1.1.9)

The linear system, (
1.1.9
), is seen to be well-posed by a stochastic analogue of the standard Cauchy–Lipschitz theory, see
[
Øks13
, Thm. 5.2.1]
. Granting that for any
n\geq 1
, we have global, unique solutions,
(u_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
, we set

u_{n;t}=\sum_{|k|\leq n}u_{k;t}e_{k},\quad u_{n;0}:=\sum_{|k|\leq n}u_{k;0}e_{k},

So, at least formally,
u_{n}
, is a smooth approximation to
u
solving (
1.1.8
). The question is whether we can really take the limit and define a solution to (
1.1.8
) as
u:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}
. The key is to obtain a suitable
a priori
bound on the solutions
u_{n}
, uniform in
n\geq 1
. By the usual Parseval’s identity, it holds that

\|u_{n;t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\sum_{|k|\leq n}|u_{k;t}|^{2}.

Applying Itô’s formula (the chain rule for Itô processes, see
[
Øks13
, Thm. 4.1.2 & 4.2.1]
and Section
3.1
below), we see that for each
k=1,\ldots n
,
|u_{k;t}|^{2}
solves the SDE,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}|u_{k;t}|^{2}

\displaystyle=2u_{k;t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{k;t}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[u_{k}]_{t}

\displaystyle=-2(2\pi k)^{2}u_{k;t}u_{k;t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+2\sqrt{\sigma}(2\pi ku_{k;t})u_{k;t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\sigma(2\pi iku_{k;t})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=2\partial_{xx}u_{k;t}u_{k;t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+2\sqrt{\sigma}\partial_{x}u_{k;t}u_{k;t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\sigma|\partial_{x}u_{k;t}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Here
[u_{k}]_{t}
denotes the quadratic variation of the process
t\mapsto u_{k;t}
, in our case this is just
4\sigma\pi^{2}|u_{k;t}|^{2}t
, since
[W]_{t}=t
. For more details see
[
Øks13
, Ex. 2.17]
and Section
3.1
below. Summing these equations up, or equivalently applying Itô’s formula directly to the PDE, and converting to integral form, we get the identity,

\|u_{n;t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\|u_{n;0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(2\langle\partial_{xx}u_{n;t},u_{n;t}\rangle+\sigma\langle\partial_{x}u_{n;s},\partial_{x}u_{n;s}\rangle\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+2\sqrt{\sigma}\int_{0}^{t}\langle u_{n;s},\partial_{x}u_{n;s}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

The final integral may, at this stage be understood, as a finite dimensional stochastic integral. Integrating by parts in the first term and applying the product rule in the final term we write,

\|u_{n;t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\|u_{n;0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-(2-\sigma)\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{x}u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{\sigma}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{x}(u_{n;s}(x))^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

(1.1.10)

We see that the last term contains the integral of a derivative on
\mathbb{T}
and so it disappears for all
\omega\in\Omega
such that the integral is well defined, that is,

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{x}(u_{n;s}(x))^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}(\omega)=0\quad\text{for }\mathbb{P}\text{-a.a. }\omega\in\Omega.

(1.1.11)

This is no accident and will also be true in higher dimensions if this term is of the form
\nabla\cdot(v_{t}u_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}
with
\nabla\cdot v_{t}=0
. However, in many cases, even if the stochastic integral does not vanish
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. it will be zero in expectation, so long as it is well-defined. In this case a stopping time argument can be run under the expectation.

With this in hand, we now define, for
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
, the norm
\|f\|^{2}_{H^{1}}:=\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
, and rewrite (
1.1.10
) as,

\displaystyle\|u_{n;t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+(2-\sigma)\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\|u_{n;0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+(2-\sigma)\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(1.1.12)

which by (
1.1.11
) holds for
\mathbb{P}
-a.a.
\omega\in\Omega
. So now, if
(2-\sigma)>0
and we fix some
T>0
, we may apply Grönwall’s inequality, to obtain the estimate,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u_{n;t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+(2-\sigma)\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq\|u_{n;0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\,e^{(2-\sigma)T}

(1.1.13)

So, assuming
(2-\sigma)>0
, we have shown,

u_{n}(\omega)\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}))\cap L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{T})),\quad\text{for }\mathbb{P}\text{-a.a. }\omega\in\Omega.

Furthermore, the quantity
\|u_{n}(\omega)\|_{C_{T}L^{2}}+\|u_{n}(\omega)\|_{L^{2}_{T}H^{1}}
depends only on
\|u_{n;0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
- which we assume to be deterministic for ease. Now if
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
we easily have,

\|u_{n;0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\sum_{|k|\leq n}|u_{k;0}|^{2}\leq\sum_{k}|u_{k;0}|^{2}=\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Therefore the bound (
1.1.12
), and hence the norm
\|u_{n}(\omega)\|_{C_{T}L^{2}}+\|u_{n}(\omega)\|_{L^{2}_{T}H^{1}}
is controlled independently of
n\geq 1
. Concretely,

\sup_{n\geq 1}\left(\|u_{n}(\omega)\|_{C_{T}L^{2}}+\|u_{n}(\omega)\|_{L^{2}_{T}H^{1}}\right)\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}e^{(2-\sigma)T}.

This uniform bound is essentially enough to obtain a weak solution to (
1.1.8
) as the limit
u_{n}\rightarrow u
. We will detail this final step in the Chapter
3
since it requires some functional analysis which we present in a more general setting.

The main takeaway, however, is that the above argument applies only for
\sqrt{\sigma}<\sqrt{2}
. Philosophically, this strategy is based on the ellipticity of the operator
u\mapsto(-\Delta\,\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\sigma}\nabla\,\,\cdot\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t})u
which we have seen does not hold for
\sqrt{\sigma}\geq\sqrt{2}
. In fact, using the notion of a Stratonovich integral (a stochastic integral which does obey the usual chain rule), we can re-write (
1.1.8
) in the form,

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{t}=\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)\Delta u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\sigma}\,\nabla u\circ\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}&\text{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}.\end{cases}

(1.1.14)

Now if we take
\sigma>2
we see that the deterministic part of (
1.1.14
) becomes a backwards heat equation.

[
Bre11
,
Eva10

DPZ14
,
CE15
]
for details where they are omitted.

1.2
Bounded Operators on Infinite Dimensional Spaces

Given Banach spaces
E,\,F
we write
L(E;F)
for the set of bounded, linear operators
O:E\rightarrow F
. We define the operator norm of
O
by the expression

\|O\|_{\text{Op}(E;F)}:=\sup_{\|x\|_{E}\leq 1}\|Ox\|_{F}=\sup_{x\in F}\frac{\|Ox\|_{F}}{\|x\|_{E}}.

L(E;F)
is itself a Banach space, although non-separable if both
E,\,F
are infinite dimensional.

Definition 1.2.1

(Finite Rank and Compact Operators)
.

Given Banach spaces,
E,\,F
we say that an operator
O\in L(E;F)
has
finite rank
if
O(E)
is a finite dimensional subspace of
F
. We say that an operator
O\in L(E;F)
is
compact
if for any bounded set
B\subset E
,
O(B)\subset E
is compact.

By definition, any finite rank operator is also compact. The following theorem says that in fact all compact operators can be approximated by finite rank operators.

Theorem 1.2.2

(Representation of Compact Operators)
.

An operator
O\in L(E;F)
is compact if there exists a sequence
(O_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset L(E;F)
of finite rank operators such that
\|O-O_{n}\|_{\text{Op}(E;F)}\rightarrow 0
. If
U,\,H
are a Hilbert spaces and
O\in L(U;H)
is compact, then there exist orthonormal families,
(\tilde{e}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of
U
and
(\tilde{f}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of
H
, and a sequence of real numbers,
(\lambda_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, which if they converge must converge to zero, such that

Ox=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\langle x,\tilde{e}_{k}\rangle_{U}\tilde{f}_{k},\quad\text{for all }x\in U.

(1.2.1)

Proof.

See
[
Bre11
, Ch. 6]
in particular Corollary 6.2 and Theorem 6.8.
∎

Note that the families
(\tilde{e}_{k})_{k\geq 1},\,(\tilde{f}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
need not be complete, in the sense that they need not form bases of
U,\,H
. Consider
O
a finite rank operator, for example. If
U,\,H
are Hilbert spaces and
O\in L(U;H)
, then there is an operator
O^{\ast}\in L(H;U)
such that,

\langle Ox,h\rangle_{H}=\langle x,O^{\ast}h\rangle_{U},\quad\text{ for all }x\in U,\,h\in H.

The operator
O^{\ast}
is known as the adjoint (or Hermitian adjoint) of
O
. If
O\in L(H):=L(H;H)
is such that
O=O^{\ast}
, then
O
is said to be self-adjoint. If
O
is a compact, self-adjoint operator then there exists a representation of the form (
1.2.1
) with
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}=(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
an orthonormal basis of
H
. See
[
Bre11
, Thm. 6.11]
.

An operator
O\in L(H)
is said to be positive if for any
h\in H
,

\langle Oh,h\rangle\geq 0.

The class of non-negative, symmetric operators
O\in L(H)
will be of particular importance. To every non-negative operator,
O\in L(H)
, there exists a second non-negative operator,
O^{1/2},\in L(H)
, known as the square root of
O
, such that
O=O^{1/2}(O^{1/2})^{\ast}
. See
[
LR17
, Thm. 3.1.28]
. If
O\in L(H)
is symmetric and non-negative, then
O^{1/2}=(O^{1/2})^{\ast}\in L(H)
.

1.2.1
Trace Class and Hilbert–Schmidt Operators

We refer to
[
DPZ14
, App. C]
for more details on much of the material in this subsection.

Definition 1.2.3

(Trace Class Operators)
.

Let
E,\,F
be Banach spaces and
T\in L(E;F)
. We say that
T
is a
trace class
(or
nuclear
) operator, if there exist two sequences,
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset E^{\ast},\,(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset F
, such that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|a_{k}\|_{E^{*}}\|b_{k}\|_{F}<\infty,

and for any
x\in E
,

Tx=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}(x)b_{k}.

We write
L_{1}(E;F)
for the set of trace class operators and equip this space with the norm,

\|T\|_{L_{1}}:=\inf\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|a_{k}\|_{E^{\ast}}\|b_{k}\|_{F}<\infty\,:\,Tx=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}(x)b_{k}\right\},

(1.2.2)

under which
L_{1}(E;F)
becomes a Banach space.

Let
U,\,H
be Hilbert spaces and
T\in L_{1}(U;H)
, then by making the identification,
U^{\ast}\cong U
, there exist sequences
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset U,\,(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset H
such that, for all
x\in U
,

Tx=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle a_{k},x\rangle b_{k}.

If
T\in L_{1}(H):=L_{1}(H;H)
, and
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset H
is a basis of
H
, then we define the trace of
T
by the expression,

\Tr T:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle Te_{k},e_{k}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.2.3)

It is relatively straightforward to check that the definition of
\Tr T
is independent of the choice of basis
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset H
, see
[
DPZ14
, Prop. C.1]
, and that
|\Tr T|\leq\|T\|_{L_{1}}
. The following proposition says that the equality
|\Tr T|=\|T\|_{L_{1}}
holds when
T\in L_{1}(H)
is symmetric and non-negative. In this case, the property of finite trace is equivalent to being trace class.

Proposition 1.2.4

(Symmetric Non-negative Trace Class Operators)
.

Let
T\in L(H)
, be symmetric and non-negative. Then
T\in L_{1}(H)
if and only if, for some (equivalently any) basis
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset H
,

|\Tr T|=\Tr T=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle Te_{k},e_{k}\rangle<\infty.

Furthermore, in this case,
\Tr T=\|T\|_{L_{1}}
.

Proof.

Let
T^{1/2}
, denote the non-negative square root of
T
, and
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a basis of
H
, so that for any
x\in H
,

T^{1/2}x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle T^{1/2}x,e_{k}\rangle e_{k}.

Therefore, for any
x\in H
and
N\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left\|T^{1/2}x-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\langle T^{1/2}x,e_{k}\rangle e_{k}\right\|_{H}^{2}=\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}|\langle T^{1/2}x,e_{k}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|x|^{2}\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\|T^{1/2}e_{k}\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle=|x|^{2}\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\langle Te_{k},e_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq|x|^{2}\Tr T.

It follows that
T^{1/2}
is the limit in operator norm of a sequence of finite rank operators and so
T^{1/2}
is a compact operator and hence
T=T^{1/2}T^{1/2}
is also compact. Therefore, by Theorem
1.2.2
, there exists a basis
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset H
and a sequence of non-negative, real numbers,
(\lambda_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
, accumulating to
0
, such that

Tx=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\langle x,f_{k}\rangle f_{k}.

(1.2.4)

Using this formula, we have that
\langle Te_{k},e_{k}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\langle e_{k},f_{k}\rangle^{2}
and so,

\Tr T=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle Te_{k},e_{k}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}|\langle e_{k},f_{l}\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}<\infty.

Thus we have shown that
T\in L_{1}(H)
, with sequences
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}=(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and
(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}=(\lambda_{k}f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, and that
\Tr T\geq\|T\|_{L_{1}}
. Hence
\Tr T=\|T\|_{L_{1}}
.
∎

Definition 1.2.5

(Hilbert–Schmidt Operator)
.

Let
U,\,H
be Hilbert spaces with bases
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1},\,(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
respectively. Then
T\in L(U;H)
is said to be a
Hilbert–Schmidt
operator, if,

\|T\|^{2}_{L_{2}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|Te_{k}\|^{2}_{H}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\langle Te_{k},f_{l}\rangle_{H}^{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\|T^{\ast}f_{l}\|_{U}^{2}\,<\,\infty.

(1.2.5)

Remark 1.2.6
.

The space
L_{2}(U;H)
is a separable Hilbert space when equipped with the norm
\|T\|_{L_{2}}
and scalar product,

\langle T,S\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle Te_{k},Se_{k}\rangle_{H},\quad\text{ for }T,\,S\in L_{2}(U;H),

and the sequence
(e_{k}\otimes f_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset U\otimes H
provides a complete orthonormal basis for
L_{2}(U;H)
. It follows that
L(U;H)
is densely embedded in
L_{2}(U;H)
.

Remark 1.2.7
.

It follows from (
1.2.5
) that the definition of
\|T\|_{L_{2}}
is independent of the choice of bases for
U,\,H
and that
\|T^{\ast}\|_{L_{2}}=\|T\|_{L_{2}}
.

We see that
T\in L(H)
is trace class if and only if
|T|^{1/2}:=(TT^{\ast})^{1/2}\in L_{2}(H)
. Furthermore, since both trace class and Hilbert–Schmidt operators are seen to be compact, from Theorem
1.2.2
, for any
T\in L_{2}(U;H)
and
TT^{\ast}\in L_{1}(H)
, there exist bases
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset U
and
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset H
and a sequence of positive real numbers,
(\lambda_{k})_{k\geq 1}
such that, for all
x\in U
,
h\in H
,

\displaystyle Tx

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\langle x,e_{k}\rangle_{U}f_{k},

(1.2.6)

\displaystyle(TT^{\ast})h

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}\langle h,f_{k}\rangle_{H}f_{k}.

1.2.2
Pseudo Inverses of Linear Operators

In the following sections we turn to the study of Gaussian random variables and Wiener processes taking values in infinite dimensional spaces. Instead of being described by a covariance matrix these random variables are described by covariance operators. In developing the theory of stochastic integration with respect to generalised Wiener processes, Subsection
1.5.5
, we will be particularly concerned with the pre-images of these operators. It will therefore be useful to recall some facts regarding the images and pre-imagines of linear operators and their pseudo-inverses. Throughout we let
U,\,H
be two Hilbert spaces and recall that
L(U;H)
is the set of bounded linear operators from
U
to
H
.

Lemma 1.2.8
.

Let
O\in L(U;H)
and
R\geq 0
. Then,

•

the set
O\left(\overline{B_{U}(0,R)}\right):=\{Ox\in H\,:\,x\in U,\,\|x\|_{U}\,\leq\,R\}
is convex and closed,

•

the set
O^{-1}\left(\overline{B_{H}(0,R)}\right):=\{x\in U\,:\,\|Ox\|_{H}\,\leq\,R\}
is convex and closed.

Proof.

Convexity of both sets follows from linearity of
O
. We only show that
O\left(\overline{B_{U}(0,R)}\right)
is closed, since it follows directly by continuity of
O
that
O^{-1}\left(\overline{B_{H}(0,R)}\right)
, as the pre-image of a closed set, is closed. Recall that a convex subset of a Hilbert space is closed if and only if it is weakly compact. By the Banach–Alaoglou theorem, any bounded set in
U
is weakly compact, and since
O
is strongly, and therefore also weakly, continuous, the image of a weakly compact set under
O
is also weakly compact in
H
. It therefore follows from convexity that
O\left(\overline{B_{U}(0,R)}\right)
is also closed.
∎

For
h\in H\setminus\{0\}
, let us define the set,

O^{-1}(h):=\{x\in U\,:\,Ox=h\}.

It follows from Lemma
1.2.8
that
O^{-1}(h)
is convex and closed. By continuity of
O
it also does not contain
0\in U
. Therefore, there is a unique element of
O^{-1}(h)
that minimizes the distance to
0\in U
. This leads to the following definition.

Definition 1.2.9

(Pseudo-inverse)
.

Let
O\in L(U;H)
and the map
H\setminus\{0\}\ni h\mapsto O^{-1}h
be defined by setting,

O^{-1}h:=\left\{y\in O^{-1}(h)\,:\,\|y\|_{U}\leq\|x\|_{U},\,\forall\,x\in O^{-1}(h)\right\}.

Then
O^{-1}\in L(H\setminus\{0\};U)
is the
pseudo-inverse
of
O
.

Recall that for a linear operator
O\in L(U;H)
, we define
\text{Ker}(O):=\{x\in U\,:\,Ox=0\in H\}
and
\text{Ker}(O)^{\perp}=\{y\in U\,:\,\langle x,y\rangle_{U}=0,\forall\,x\in\text{Ker}(O)\}=O^{\ast}(H)
.

Proposition 1.2.10
.

Let
O\in L(U;H)
and
O^{-1}
be its pseudo-inverse.

i)

The space
O(U)
is a Hilbert space when equipped with the inner product,

\langle h,g\rangle_{O(U)}:=\langle O^{-1}h,O^{-1}h\rangle_{U},\quad\text{ for all }h,\,g\in O(U).

ii)

Let
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a basis of
\text{Ker}(O)^{\perp}
. Then,
(Oe_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a basis of
(O(U),\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{O(U)})
.

Proof.

Both conclusions are a consequence of the fact that
O:\text{Ker}(O)^{\perp}\rightarrow O(U)
is an isometry when
\text{Ker}(O)^{\perp}
is equipped with the inner product of
U
and
O(U)
is equipped with the inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{O(U)}
defined above.
∎

Proposition 1.2.11
.

Let
(U_{1},\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{1}),\,(U_{2},\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{2})
be two Hilbert spaces and
O_{1}\in L(U_{1},H)
,
O_{2}\in L(U_{2},H)
. Then the following both hold,

i)

If there exists a
c\geq 0
such that for all
h\in H
,
\|O^{\ast}_{1}h\|_{1}\leq c\|O^{\ast}_{2}h\|_{2}
then

\{O_{1}x\,:\,x\in U_{1},\,\|x\|_{1}\leq 1\}\subseteq\{O_{2}y\,:\,y\in U_{2},\,\|x\|_{2}\leq 1\}.

In particular,
\text{Im}O_{1}\subseteq\text{Im}O_{2}
.

ii)

If for all
h\in H
,
\|O_{1}^{\ast}h\|_{1}=\|O_{2}^{\ast}h\|_{2}
, then
\text{Im}O_{1}=\text{Im}(O_{2})
and for all
h\in\text{Im}O_{2}
,
\|O^{-1}_{1}h\|_{1}=\|O^{-1}_{2}h\|_{2}
.

Proof.

See the proof of
[
PR07
, Prop. C.0.5]

∎

Corollary 1.2.12
.

Let
O\in L(U;H)
and set
Q=OO^{\ast}\in L(H)
. Then it holds that,

\text{Im}Q^{1/2}=\text{Im}O,\quad\text{and}\quad\|Q^{-1/2}h\|_{H}=\|O^{-1}h\|_{U},\quad\text{for all}\quad h\in\text{Im}O,

where
Q^{-1/2}
is the pseudo-inverse of
Q^{1/2}
.

Proof.

First note that
Q^{1/2}
is well defined since
OO^{\ast}
is always a non-negative operator. Furthermore, since
Q^{1/2}
is symmetric, for all
h\in H
, we have that

\|(Q^{1/2})^{\ast}h\|^{2}_{H}=\|Q^{1/2}h\|^{2}_{H}=\langle Qh,h\rangle_{H}=\langle OO^{\ast}h,h\rangle_{H}=\|O^{\ast}h\|^{2}_{U}.

Therefore, the conclusion follows by Item
ii)
of Proposition
1.2.11
.
∎

1.3
Lebesgue and Bochner Integration

We very briefly recall the construction of the Lebesgue and Bochner integrals. We will build the stochastic integral in a similar manner as we build the Lebesgue integral below. The expectation of infinite dimensional random variables gives a recurring example of a Bochner integral in our analysis.

Let
(E,\mathcal{B}(E),\mu)
be a topological, measure space, equipped with its Borel sigma algebra,
\mathcal{B}(E)
. We define the set of simple functions,
\mathcal{E}
, to be all functions,
g:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form

g=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mathds{1}_{A_{k}},\quad c_{k}\in\mathbb{R},\,\,A_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(E),\,N\in\mathbb{N}.

Define the integral of
g\in\mathcal{E}
by the expression

\int_{E}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mu(A_{k})\,\in\,\mathbb{R},

and then note that for any
p\in[1,\infty]
, the space
\mathcal{E}
equipped with the norm,

\|g\|_{L^{p}}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}|c_{k}|^{p}|A_{k},&p\in[1,\infty)\\
\displaystyle\sup_{k=1,\ldots,N}|c_{k}|,&p=\infty,\end{cases}

defines a complete, normed, vector space. We set
L^{p}(\mu)
to be the completion of
\mathcal{E}
under the norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{L^{p}}
and the Lebesgue integral of a measurable function
f:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as the equivalence class of limit points of all simple approximating sequences
g_{n}\rightarrow f\in L^{p}(\mu)
. Extending to vector valued functions can be done component by component.

A similar notion of integral can be defined for maps taking values in infinite dimensional spaces, this is the Bochner integral. Let
(V,\|\,\cdot\,\|_{V})
be a Banach space and define by
\mathcal{E}_{V}
the set of
V
valued simple maps
g:E\rightarrow V
of the form

g=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mathds{1}_{A_{k}},\quad c_{k}\in V,A_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(E),\,N\in\mathbb{N},

and we now set

\int_{E}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mu(A_{k})\,\in V.

This is
mutatis mutandi
the same construction as for the Lebesgue integral. However, in order to extend to general measurable maps
f:E\rightarrow V
we now say that
f
is Bochner integrable if there exists a sequence of simple function
(g_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{E}_{V}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{E}\|f(x)-g_{n}(x)\|_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0,

where each integral is understood as the Lebesgue integral of the map
E\ni x\mapsto\|f(x)-g_{n}(x)\|_{V}\,\in\mathbb{R}
.

A version of the dominated convergence theorem holds for the Bochner integral.

Theorem 1.3.1

(DCT for Bochner Integrals)
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{B}(E),\mu)
be a topological measure space and
V
be a Banach space,
f:E\rightarrow V
be Bochner integrable and
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
a sequence of Bochner integral maps, such that for
\mu
-a.e
x\in E
,
f(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n}(x)
. Then, if there exists a real, integrable function
g\in L^{1}(\mu;\mathbb{R})
such that for
\mu
-a.e
x\in E
,
\|f_{n}(x)\|_{V}\leq g(x)
, it holds that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{E}\|f-f_{n}\|_{V}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=0,

and so

\int_{E}f_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\rightarrow\int_{E}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Proof.

The first assertion is a consequence of the DCT for Lebesgue integrals, since by assumption
\|f_{n}(x)\|_{V}\rightarrow\|f(x)\|_{V}\in\mathbb{R}
for
\mu
-a.e
x\in E
. The second assertion follows from the first and the discussion above regarding construction of the Bochner integral.
∎

1.4
Probability and Random Variables

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, consisting of an abstract measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, equipped with
\mathbb{P}
a probability. We also assume we are given,
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
a measurable, separable, Banach space, with
\mathcal{B}(E)
the Borel sigma algebra induced by the norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{E}
. We will assume both these to be in place without further comment. We say that a measurable map
X:\Omega\rightarrow E
is an
E
valued random variable. We write
\mathcal{L}(X):=X_{\#}\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}(E)
that is, for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(E)
,

\mathcal{L}(X)(A)=\mathbb{P}[\omega\in\Omega\,:\,X(\omega)\in A].

We say that a property holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. if the
\mathbb{P}
measure of the set where the property does not hold is zero. The following proposition says that the Borel
\sigma
-algebra,
\mathcal{B}(E)
is generated by the cylinder sets of
E^{\ast}
. We recall that given a family of sets
A\in 2^{E}
, the notation
\sigma(A)
denotes the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing
A
.

Proposition 1.4.1
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
be a separable, Banach space equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Firstly, there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset E^{\ast}
such that,

\|x\|_{E}=\sup_{n\geq 1}|\varphi_{n}(x)|,\quad\text{for any}\quad x\in E.

(1.4.1)

As a result,

\mathcal{B}(E)=\sigma(\{x\in E\,:\,\varphi(x)\leq R,\,R\in\mathbb{R},\,\varphi\in E^{\ast}\}),

(1.4.2)

and so
X
is an
E
valued random variable, if and only if
\varphi(X)
is a real random variable for all
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
.

Proof.

We show (
1.4.1
) first. Let
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset E
be a dense set. By the Hahn–Banach theorem,
[
Bre11
, Thm. 1.1]
, for all
n\geq 1
there exists a
\varphi_{n}\in E^{\ast}
such that
\varphi_{n}(x_{n})=\|x_{n}\|_{E}
and
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{E^{\ast}}=1
. We show that the sequence
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has the property (
1.4.1
). Fix
x\in E
and since
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{E^{\ast}}\leq 1
it holds that
|\varphi_{n}(x)|\leq\|x\|_{E}
for each
n\geq 1
and therefore
\sup_{n\geq 1}|\varphi_{n}(x)|\leq\|x\|_{E}
. On the other hand, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
x_{n}
, such that
\|x-x_{n}\|_{E}<\varepsilon
and so
|\varphi_{n}(x)-\|x_{n}\|_{E}|=|\varphi_{n}(x-x_{n})|\leq\varepsilon
. Therefore,
|\varphi_{n}(x)|>\|x\|_{E}-2\varepsilon
, and since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary we conclude that (
1.4.1
) holds.

We use (
1.4.1
) to prove (
1.4.2
). It follows from (
1.4.1
), that for
a\in E
,
R>0
,

\overline{B}_{E}(a,R)=\{x\in E\,:\,\|x-a\|_{E}\leq R\}=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\{x\in E\,:\,|\varphi_{n}(x)-\varphi_{n}(a)|\leq R\}.

Therefore, the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing all sets of the form
\{x\in E\,:\,\varphi(x)\leq R,\,R\in\mathbb{R},\,\varphi\in E^{\ast}\}
contains the closed balls of
E
and thus contains
\mathcal{B}(E)
. On the other hand, every
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
is a continuous map from
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))\rightarrow(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
and so is measurable. Therefore, the reverse inclusion holds from which (
1.4.2
) follows. Since a map
X:\Omega\rightarrow E
is an
E
valued random variable if and only if it is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{B}(E)
, the final assertion follows since (
1.4.2
) is equivalent to the statement
\mathcal{B}(E)=\sigma(\{\varphi\in E^{\ast}\})
.
∎

The following corollary of Proposition
1.4.1
allows us to turn some questions regarding
E
valued random variables into questions regarding real valued random variables through the dual mapping
\omega\mapsto\varphi(X(\omega))
for
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
.

Corollary 1.4.2
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
be a separable Banach space equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Then the following all hold,

i)

The set of
E
valued random variables is a vector space. That is, if
X,\,Y
are
E
valued random variables, then for any
\alpha,\,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha X+\beta Y
is an
E
valued random variable.

ii)

If
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of
E
valued random variables such that
X_{n}(\omega)\rightharpoonup X(\omega)\in E
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. then
X:\Omega\rightarrow E
is an
E
valued random variable.

iii)

If
X
is an
E
valued random variable, then
\Omega\ni\omega\mapsto\|X(\omega)\|_{E}
is a real random variable.

iv)

If
X,\,Y
are two
E
valued random variables. Then
X=Y
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. if and only if
\varphi(X)=\varphi(Y)

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for all
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
.

Proof.

Items
i)
and
ii)
follow from Proposition
1.4.1
and the fact that both properties hold for real random variables. Item
iii)
follows from the fact that by definition
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{E}\in E^{\ast}
. In order to show item
iv)
, by linearity of the space
E
it suffices to show that if
\varphi(X)=0\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for all
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
, then
X=0\in E
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.. Let,
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence from (
1.4.1
), then if by assumption
\varphi_{n}(X)=0
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. it holds that
\sup_{n\geq 1}|\varphi_{n}(X)|=\|X\|=0
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., which implies the conclusion.
∎

We say that an
E
valued random variable,
X
, is simple if there exists an
N\geq 1
and sequences
(x_{k})_{k=1}^{N}\subset E
,
(A_{k})_{k=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{F}
, such that,

X(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}(\omega),\quad\text{for}\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.a. }\omega\in\Omega.

(1.4.3)

It follows from, Corollary
1.4.2
, that for any sequence of simple random variables
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converging, weakly or strongly, to
X\in E
, defines an
E
valued random variable. Conversely, it also holds that to any
E
valued random variable, there exists a sequence of simple random variables converging strongly to it. We give this result in the more general setting of separable metric spaces.

Proposition 1.4.3
.

Let
(E,\rho_{E})
be a separable metric space and
X
be an
E
valued random variable. Then there exists a sequence of simple random variables
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, of the form, (
1.4.3
), such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{E}(X(\omega),X_{n}(\omega))\rightarrow 0
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s..

Proof.

Let
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset E
be a countable dense set. Then for every
n\geq 1
and
\mathbb{P}
-a.a.
\omega\in\Omega
, we define

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\min\{\rho(X(\omega),x_{k}),\,k=1,\ldots,n\},

\displaystyle k_{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\min\{k\leq n\,:\,\rho_{n}(\omega)=\rho(X(\omega),x_{k})\},

\displaystyle X_{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle=x_{k_{n}(\omega)}.

The random variables
X_{n}
are simple, since each
X_{n}
takes values only in the set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. Furthermore, since the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is dense, one has that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{n}(\omega)=0
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. Since
\rho_{n}(\omega)=\rho_{E}(X(\omega),X_{n}(\omega))
, the conclusion follows.
∎

For a simple random variable we define the Bochner integrals,

\mathbb{E}[X]:=\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[x_{k}]\mathbb{P}[A_{k}],

and

\mathbb{E}[\|X\|_{E}]=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}\|_{E}]\mathbb{P}[A_{k}].

By completeness of
(E,\,\|\,\cdot\,\|_{E})
, an
E
valued random variable is Bochner integrable if and only if

\int_{\Omega}\|X(\omega)\|_{E}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)<\infty.

Therefore, using Proposition
1.4.3
we may extend the Bochner integral,
\mathbb{E}[X]
, to all
E
valued random variables, as the limit of the Bochner integral along a sequence of approximating simple random variables. For
p\in[1,\infty]
we define the spaces
L^{p}(\Omega;E)
as the completions of the simple random variables under the norms,

\|X\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}\left[\|X\|_{E}^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}},&\text{ if }p\in[1,\infty),\\
\displaystyle\esssup_{\omega\in\Omega}\|X(\omega)\|_{E},&\text{ if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

Note that
\mathbb{E}[X]\in E
while
\mathbb{E}[\|X\|_{E}^{p}]\in\mathbb{R}
. For concision and where it will not cause confusion we sometimes write the Bochner integral as

\int_{\Omega}X\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}:=\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega).

The Bochner integral of a random variable obeys analogues of the dominated convergence and monotone convergence theorems for the Lebesgue integral.
Let
(F,\|\,\cdot\,\|_{E})
be a second separable Banach space and consider a linear operator
A:D(A)\subseteq E\rightarrow F
. Recall that we say
A
is closed if the set,

\text{graph}(A):=\{(x,y)\in E\times F\,:\,x\in D(A),\,y=Ax\},

is closed in the product space
E\times F
. We equip
D(A)
with the graph norm,
\|x\|_{D(A)}:=\|x\|_{E}+\|Ax\|_{F}
. We have the following result regarding
D(A)\subseteq E
valued random variables.

Theorem 1.4.4
.

Let
E,\,F
be separable Banach spaces and
A:D(A)\subseteq E\rightarrow F
be a closed operator such that
D(A)\in\mathcal{B}(E)
. If
X:\Omega\rightarrow E
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
D(A)
valued, then
AX
is an
F
valued random variable and
X
is a
D(A)
valued random variable (seen as a Banach space equipped with the graph norm). If
X
is Bochner integrable and

\int_{\Omega}\|AX(\omega)\|_{E}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)<\infty,

then

A\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\int_{\Omega}AX(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega).

(1.4.4)

Proof.

The first statements essentially follow from the definitions, using the fact that
(D(A),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{D(A)})
is itself a Banach space. To prove (
1.4.4
) consider a sequence of simple random variables
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset D(A)
approximating
X
in
D(A)
, that is

\|X-X_{n}\|_{D(A)}:=\|X-X_{n}\|_{E}+\|AX-AX_{n}\|_{F}\rightarrow 0.

It follows directly that we have

\int_{\Omega}\|X(\omega)-X_{n}(\omega)\|_{D(A)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\rightarrow 0.

Thus, by definition of the Bochner integral, we have that both

\int_{\Omega}X_{n}(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega),\qquad\int_{\Omega}AX_{n}(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}AX(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega).

However, it also follows from the definition of the Bochner integral of simple random variables that

\int_{\Omega}AX_{n}(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)=A\int_{\Omega}X_{n}(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega),

therefore (
1.4.4
) follows from the closedness of
A
.
∎

Remark 1.4.5
.

It follows from
1.4.4
that
\varphi\in L(E,F)
and if
X
is
E
valued random variable that is Bochner integrable then,

\varphi\left(\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\right)=\int_{\Omega}\varphi(X(\omega))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega).

That is
\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X])=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]
. In fact, the expectation is characterised as the unique element
x\in E
such that,

\varphi(x)=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)],\quad\text{ for all }\varphi\in E^{\ast}.

In the sequel we employ the notion of a conditional expectation, in particular when studying martingales taking values in Hilbert spaces. Existence and uniqueness of the conditional expectation for
E
valued random variables follows in a similar manner as in the finite dimensional case.

Theorem 1.4.6
.

Let
X
be a Bochner integrable,
E
valued random variable and let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a sub sigma-algebra of
\mathcal{F}
. Then there exists a unique, up to a
\mathbb{P}
measure zero set, integrable,
E
valued random variable,
Z
, that is
\mathcal{G}
measurable and such that

\int_{A}X\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=\int_{A}Z\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P},\quad\text{ for all }A\in\mathcal{G}.

(1.4.5)

We write
\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{G}]
for the random variable
Z
and refer to it as the conditional expectation of
X
with respect to
\mathcal{G}
.

Remark 1.4.7
.

Often, we wish to talk about the conditional expectation of a random variable,
X
, with respect to another random variable
Y
. In this case we use the abuse of notation
\mathbb{E}[X|Y]
by which we really mean
\mathbb{E}[X|\sigma(Y)]
, where
\sigma(Y)
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
Y
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4.6
.

We begin by showing uniqueness. Assume that there exists two random variables
Z,\,\tilde{Z}
such that both equal
\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{G}]
. Therefore, using Remark
1.4.5
it holds that for any
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
,

\int_{A}\varphi(Z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=\varphi\left(\int_{A}Z\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)=\int_{A}\varphi(X)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P},\quad\text{ for all }A\in\mathcal{G}.

Similarly,
\int_{A}\varphi(\tilde{Z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=\int_{A}\varphi(X)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}
. Therefore, for all
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
,
\varphi(Z),\,\varphi(\tilde{Z})
both equal the real, conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)|\mathcal{G}]
. Since uniqueness holds for the finite dimensional conditional expectation we have
\varphi(Z)=\varphi(\tilde{Z})
for all
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
and so by Item
iv)
of Corollary
1.4.2
we have
Z=\tilde{Z}

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

To prove existence, let
X=\sum_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}
be a simple random variable, then we define,

Z=\sum_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}\mathbb{P}[A_{k}|\mathcal{G}],

where
\mathbb{P}[A_{k}|\mathcal{G}]
is the usual conditional probability of
A_{k}
with respect to
\mathcal{G}
. It is clear that
Z
satisfies (
1.4.5
) and by the law of total expectation we have

\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{E}]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}\|_{E}]\mathbb{P}[A_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[\|X\|_{E}].

(1.4.6)

Now let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of simple random variables, approximating
X
and
Z_{n}=\mathbb{E}[X_{n}|\mathcal{G}]
. So then for
n,\,m\in\mathbb{N}
, by (
1.4.6
), we have

\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{n}-Z_{m}\|_{E}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|X_{n}-X_{m}\|_{E}]\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{ as }n,\,m\rightarrow\infty.

Therefore
Z_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P};E)
and thus there exists a
\mathcal{G}
-measurable random variable
Z:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}Z_{n}
which by continuity of the Bochner integral also satisfies (
1.4.5
).
∎

1.4.1
Gaussian Measures on Hilbert Spaces

Recall that a real random variable
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called Gaussian if there exist
m\in\mathbb{R}
,
q\in[0,\infty)
such that for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
,

\mathcal{L}(X)(A):=\mathbb{P}[X\in A]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\sigma}}\int_{A}e^{-\frac{|x-m|^{2}}{2q}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

In the multivariate case we say that an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
valued random variable is Gaussian if there exist
m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, a non-negative, symmetric matrix such that for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\mathcal{L}(X)(A):=\mathbb{P}[X\in A]=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{d/2}\sqrt{\det{Q}}}\int_{A}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle Q^{-1}(x-m),x-m\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

In both cases, if the variance is zero we interpret the measure,
\mathcal{L}(X)
, as a dirac mass centred at
m
. Both definitions can also be concisely given in terms of characteristic functions (Fourier transforms) of measures. A measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a Gaussian measure if and only if its characteristic function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni\lambda\mapsto\hat{\mu}(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}[e^{i\langle\lambda,X\rangle}]
is given by the expression

\hat{\mu}(\lambda)=e^{i\langle m,\lambda\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle Q\lambda,\lambda\rangle}\quad\text{ for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.4.7)

where
m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a symmetric, non-negative matrix. We say that an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
valued random variable is Gaussian if its law,
\mathcal{L}(X)
is a Gaussian measure in this sense.

These definitions motivate the notion of Hilbert space valued random variables in possibly infinite dimensions. We fix a separable, Hilbert space
U
and identify
U^{\ast}
with
U
from now on. As a result we change notation from the previous section, instead of writing
g(h)
for
g\in U^{\ast},\,f\in U
, we write
\langle f,g\rangle
, where the inner product is understood as the inner product on
U
.

Definition 1.4.8

(
U
-Valued Gaussian Random Variable)
.

Let
X
be a
U
-valued random variable. Then we say that
X
is Gaussian if and only if
\langle X,g\rangle
is a real Gaussian random variable, for every
g\in U
. We say that a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(U)
is Gaussian if
\mu(A)=\mathbb{P}[X\in A]
for a Gaussian random variable
X
.

It follows from the Riesz representation theorem, that given a Gaussian measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(U)
, there exists
m\in U
and
Q:U\rightarrow U
, such that for all
h,\,g\in U
,

\displaystyle\langle m,h\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\langle x,h\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\mathbb{E}[\langle X,h\rangle],

(1.4.8)

\displaystyle\langle Qh,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\langle x,h\rangle\langle x,g\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\mathbb{E}[\langle X,h\rangle\langle X,g\rangle].

From now on we only deal with centred Gaussian measures, that is measures for which
m=0
. By linearity one may always translate a centred measure to a non-centred measure. It is immediate that
Q
is linear, symmetric and non-negative. We will shortly demonstrate that in fact
Q
defines a trace class operator from
U
to itself.

At this point we remark that much of the theory presented below for Gaussian measures on Hilbert spaces extends quite naturally, to the more general setting of separable, reflexive Banach spaces, satisfying either a
2
-martingale or uniform martingale difference (UMD) property, see
[
vNVW07
]
for an overview on these topics. A benefit of the theory of stochastic integration in UMD Banach spaces is that it leads to maximal regularity of the constructed integrals. On the other hand, the theory presented here can be implemented to develop a theory for Banach space valued integrals at the price of a small loss in regularity, see
[
Hai09
, Sec. 5]
. For an approach to SPDEs valued in
L^{\infty}
based spaces, such as
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}
,
W^{k,\infty}
we refer to the pathwise approach briefly presented in Chapter
4
here.

For a measure,
\mu
, on a Hilbert space
U
, we define the characteristic function
U\ni h\mapsto\hat{\mu}(h)\in\mathbb{C}
, by the expression,

\hat{\mu}(h):=\int_{U}e^{i\langle g,h\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(g).

It follows from Definition
1.4.8
and (
1.4.8
) that for
\mu
a centred, Gaussian measure on
U
, one has

\hat{\mu}(h)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle Qh,h\rangle},\quad\text{ for all }h\in U.

(1.4.9)

To see this, use the fact that by definition, there exists a
U
valued random variable,
X
, such that
\langle X,h\rangle
is a one dimensional Gaussian and the expression (
1.4.7
) for the Fourier transform of finite dimensional Gaussian measures. In fact, Theorem
1.4.12
below, shows that any measure with characteristic function of the form (
1.4.9
) is a Gaussian measure.

We say that two measures,
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(U)
are equal, if
\mu(A)=\nu(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(U)
. A useful extension of Item
iv)
of Corollary
1.4.2
shows that the characteristic function uniquely determines the measure. We note that the following sequence of results hold directly on separable Banach spaces, not only Hilbert spaces provided one appropriately defines
\hat{\mu}
in terms of elements of
E^{\ast}
.

Proposition 1.4.9
.

Let
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(U)
be two measures on a Hilbert space
U
. Then
\mu=\nu
if and only if
\hat{\mu}(g)=\hat{\nu}(g)
for all
g\in U
.

Proof.

By linearity, it suffices to show that if
\hat{\mu}\equiv 0
then
\mu=0
. For
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
one has

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{\varphi}(\xi)e^{-i\xi x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\,\tilde{\mu}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{\varphi}(\xi)\hat{\tilde{\mu}}(-\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.

Therefore, approximating any continuous and bounded function by smooth functions we see that the Fourier transform uniquely determines one dimensional probability measures. The infinite dimensional case then follows from Item
iv)
of Corollary
1.4.2
.
∎

So far we have not in fact shown that measures satisfying Definition
1.4.8
in fact exist. In order to do so we establish some a priori properties of Gaussian measures. Recall that given an angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
a vector in
U^{2}
is rotated under the map
R_{\theta}(h,g):=(h\cos\theta-g\sin\theta,h\sin\theta+g\cos\theta)
. By extension we define the rotation of
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(U)
by
\theta
, as the image of
\mu\otimes\mu
under the map,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{\theta}:\mathcal{P}(U)\otimes\mathcal{P}(U)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(U)\otimes\mathcal{P}(U)

\displaystyle\mu\otimes\nu

\displaystyle\mapsto R_{\theta}\#(\mu\otimes\nu)

Lemma 1.4.10
.

Let
\mu
be a Gaussian measure on
U
. Then for any
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, one has
\mathfrak{R}_{\theta}(\mu\times\mu)=\mu\otimes\mu
.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
1.4.9
that is enough to check that the identity,

\left(\widehat{\mu\otimes\mu}\right)\circ R^{-1}_{\theta}=\widehat{\mu\otimes\mu}:U^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
, holds for the characteristic functions. This can be checked using the explicit expression (
1.4.9
).
∎

A consequence of this rotation invariance is a bound on exponential moments of Gaussian measures known as Fernique’s theorem. Note that the theorem holds for all measures that are invariant by rotations of
\pi/4
degrees, not just Gaussian measures.

Theorem 1.4.11

(Fernique’s Theorem)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(U)
be a probability measure such that
\mathfrak{R}_{\pi/4}(\mu\otimes\mu)=\mu\otimes\mu
. Then there exists an
\alpha>0
such that

\int_{U}e^{\alpha\|h\|^{2}_{U}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)<\infty.

Proof.

Let
\tau,\,\sigma>0
. Then by
\pi/4
-rotation invariance we have that

\displaystyle\mu(\|h\|_{U}\leq\tau)\mu(\|g\|_{U}>\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\left\|\frac{h-g}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|_{U}\leq\tau\right)\mu\left(\frac{\|h+g\|_{U}}{\sqrt{2}}>\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\|\frac{h-g}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|_{U}\leq\tau}\int_{\left\|\frac{h+g}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|_{U}>\sigma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(g)

By the triangle inequality we see that

\min\{\|h\|_{U},\,\|g\|_{U}\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\|h+g\|_{U}-\|h-g\|_{U}\right),

and so it follows that together, the conditions,
\|h-g\|_{U}\leq\sqrt{2}\tau
and
\|h+g\|_{U}>\sqrt{2}\sigma
, that
\min\{\|h\|_{U},\,\|g\|_{U}\}\geq\frac{\sigma-\tau}{\sqrt{2}}
. So using the above equality, we have that

\mu(\|h\|_{U}\leq\tau)\mu(\|g\|_{U}>\sigma)\leq\int_{\|h\|_{U}>\frac{\sigma-\tau}{\sqrt{2}}}\int_{\|g\|_{U}>\frac{\sigma-\tau}{\sqrt{2}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(g)=\mu\left(\|h\|_{U}>\frac{\tau-\sigma}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}.

Since
\|h\|_{U}<\infty

\mu
-a.s., there exists some
\sigma>0
such that
\mu(\|h\|_{U}\leq\sigma)\geq\frac{3}{4}
. So now we set
t_{0}=\sigma
to be the minimal value such that this holds and for
n>0
we define,
t_{n}=\frac{t_{n+1}-\sigma}{\sqrt{2}}
. So it follows that

\mu(\|h\|_{U}>t_{n+1})\leq\frac{\mu\left(\|h\|_{U}>\frac{t_{n+1}-\sigma}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}{\mu(\|h\|_{U}\leq\sigma)}\leq\frac{4}{3}\mu(\|h\|_{U}>t_{n})^{2}.

Therefore, defining the shorthand,
k_{n}:=\frac{4}{3}\mu(\|h\|_{U}>t_{n})
, we have shown the recursion
k_{n+1}\leq k_{n}^{2}
with
k_{0}\leq\frac{1}{3}
. Repeatedly applying this inequality gives the bound
\mu(\|h\|_{U}>t_{n})\leq 3^{-2n}
. On the other hand, one can check explicitly that
t_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{2}^{n+1}-1}{\sqrt{2}-1}\sigma\leq 2^{n/2}(2+\sqrt{2})\sigma
so that in particular
t_{n+1}\leq 2^{n/5}5\sigma
. Therefore in combination we have

\mu(\|h\|_{U}>t_{n})\leq 3^{-\frac{t^{2}_{n+1}}{25\sigma^{2}}}.

So it follows that there exists a universal constant
\tilde{\alpha}>0
such that
\mu(\|h\|_{U}>t)\leq e^{-\frac{2\tilde{\alpha}t^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}
, holds for every
t>\sigma
. So integrating by parts we have that

\displaystyle\int_{U}\exp\left(\frac{\tilde{\alpha}\|h\|^{2}_{U}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\tilde{\alpha}}+\frac{2\tilde{\alpha}}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{\sigma}^{\infty}te^{\frac{\tilde{\alpha}t^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\mu(\|h\|_{U}>t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq e^{\tilde{\alpha}}+2\tilde{\alpha}\int_{1}^{\infty}te^{-\tilde{\alpha}t^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle<\infty,

which is the stated result with
\alpha=\tilde{\alpha}/\sigma
.
∎

It follows from Fernique’s theorem that Gaussian measures have finite moments of all orders. In fact one can show an even stronger result, namely that the second moment of a Gaussian measure controls all higher moments. We postpone this proof to Chapter
4
since we do not make use of it here. We employ Theorem
1.4.11
here to give the following characterisation of Gaussian measures on a Hilbert space.

Theorem 1.4.12
.

Let
U
be a Hilbert space. A measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(U)
is Gaussian if and only if there exist
m\in U
and
Q\in L_{1}(U)
such that

\hat{\mu}(h)=e^{i\langle m,h\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle Qh,h\rangle},\quad\text{ for all }h\in U.

(1.4.10)

Furthermore one has the identity,

\int_{U}\|h\|^{2}_{U}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)=\Tr Q.

(1.4.11)

Finally, if
\mu=\mathcal{L}(X)
then we say that
X\sim\mathcal{N}(m,Q)
.

Proof.

As above, without loss of generality we may prove the result only for centred Gaussian measures, so we set
m=0
. Furthermore, we have already seen in (
1.4.9
) that given a Gaussian measure there exists a linear, non-negative, symmetric operator
Q:U\rightarrow U
such that (
1.4.10
) holds. Therefore, in one direction it suffices to show that this operator
Q
is in fact trace class and in turn that the expression (
1.4.11
) is finite.

It follows from Theorem
1.4.11
that given a Gaussian measure, a priori one has the bound

\int_{U}\|h\|^{2}_{U}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{U}e^{\alpha\|h\|^{2}_{U}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)<\infty.

Now let
(e_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset U
be a basis, so that applying Lebesgue’s dominated convergence in the first step, we have,

\int_{U}\|h\|^{2}_{U}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{U}\langle h,e_{k}\rangle^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle Qe_{k},e_{k}\rangle=\Tr Q.

That is, if a measure,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(U)
, satisfies (
1.4.8
), then
Q\in L_{1}(U)
.

To prove the converse, let
Q\in L_{1}(U)
so that by (
1.2.4
) there exists a basis
(e_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset U
such that
Qe_{n}=\lambda_{n}e_{n}
for a summable sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Then, let
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a family of i.i.d, real valued standard normal random variables, which exist by Kolmogorov’s extensions theorem. By definition,
\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}\mathbb{E}[\beta_{n}^{2}]=\Tr Q
and so the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}\beta_{n}e_{n}
is finite in mean square and so there exists a subsequence of partial sums converging
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. in
U
. It follows that
X:=\sum_{n\geq 1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}\beta_{n}e_{n}
defines a
U
valued Gaussian random variable in the sense of Definition
1.4.8
.

In both cases the form of the characteristic function, (
1.4.10
), can be computed explicitly.
∎

In the case of Banach space valued Gaussian random variables, one can instead show that the covariance operators is a compact operator. See
[
Hai09
, Ex. 3.16]
.

1.4.2
Generalised Gaussian Random Variables

One may wonder if we can allow for more general covariance operators. An example of particular interest is the white-noise, otherwise known as a cylindrical Gaussian random variable. Let
U
be as above,
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be any orthonormal basis and
(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be i.i.d, real, standard normal, random variables. Then consider the random variable defined by the formal sum,

W:=\sum_{k=1}\beta_{k}e_{k}.

(1.4.12)

This corresponds to formally setting
Q=\text{Id}_{U}
, the identity map on
U
. Since the identity map is not trace-class,
W
, cannot be a
U
valued Gaussian random variable. However, if we consider
W
as a random variable taking values in a suitable space larger than
U
, it turns out to be perfectly well defined Gaussian random. For example, consider the Hilbert–Schmidt extension,
U_{\text{ex}}
, of
U
, defined to be the completion of
U
under the norm,

\|x\|^{2}_{U_{\text{ex}}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\langle x,e_{k}\rangle^{2}.

The inclusion map
\iota:U\rightarrow U_{\text{ex}}
is Hilbert–Schmidt and since
U\subset U_{\text{ex}}
, for any
h\in U
, the action
\langle W,h\rangle_{U_{\text{ex}}}
is well defined. Interpreting all inner products in the only admissible sense, we then have, for
h,\,g\in E
,

\mathbb{E}[\langle W,h\rangle]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}[\langle W,h\rangle\langle W,g\rangle]=\langle h,g\rangle.

Thus we may treat
W
as defining a Gaussian random variable on
U
, which takes values almost surely in
U_{\text{ex}}
. This leads us to the following definition.

Definition 1.4.13

(Generalised Gaussian Random Variable)
.

Given a Hilbert space,
U
, we say that a linear map
h\mapsto X_{h}
defines a generalised Gaussian random variable, if
X_{h}
is a real Gaussian random variable for every
h\in U
and if
h_{n}\rightarrow h\in U
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{h}-X_{h_{n}}|^{2}\right]\rightarrow 0.

Consider the example of the white noise,
W
, above. The map
h\mapsto\langle W,h\rangle=:W_{h}
is linear and defines a family of real, Gaussian random variables. Furthermore, for
(h_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset U
converging to
h\in U
we have,

\mathbb{E}\left[|W_{h}-W_{h_{n}}|^{2}\right]=\|h_{n}-h\|^{2}_{U}\rightarrow 0.

It follows from the definition of a generalised Gaussian random variable, that there exists a bi-linear form,
K:E\times E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a symmetric, non-negative operator
Q:E\rightarrow E
such that,

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{h}X_{g}\right]=K(h,g)=\langle Qh,g\rangle,\quad\text{ for all }h,\,g\in U.

As before, we call
Q
the covariance operator of
X
. In the case of the white noise,
W
, the covariance operator is the identity on
U
. In fact, all generalised Gaussian random variables can be written formally as

X=\sum_{k=1}\beta_{k}Q^{1/2}e_{k},

(1.4.13)

where
(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are a family of i.i.d, real, standard normals,
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is an orthonormal basis of
U
and
Q\in L(U)
is a symmetric and non-negative operator. Note that
Q
need not be trace class, however, since it is symmetric and non-negative it admits an invertible, square root. From this we define the reproducing kernel space of
X
.

Definition 1.4.14

(Reproducing Kernel Space)
.

Let
X
be a generalised Gaussian random variable on a Hilbert space
U
, with covariance
Q
. Then, we define the reproducing kernel of
X
to be the space
U_{0}:=Q^{1/2}(U)
. Note that
U_{0}
is itself a Hilbert space, with inner product
\langle h,g\rangle_{U_{0}}=\langle Q^{-1/2}h,Q^{-1/2}g\rangle_{U}
.

This definition allows us to give proper meaning to the generalised Gaussian random variables as defined in (
1.4.13
).

Proposition 1.4.15
.

Let
U,\,U_{1}
be Hilbert spaces and
Q\in L(U)
be such that
Q^{1/2}(U)=:U_{0}\subseteq U_{1}
with Hilbert–Schmidt embedding
\iota Q^{1/2}:U\rightarrow U_{1}
. Then the sum on the right hand side of (
1.4.13
) defines a generalised Gaussian random variable on
U
in the sense of Definition
1.4.13
. Furthermore, the definition of
X
as a generalised Gaussian random variable on
U
is independent of the choice of
U_{1}
and
\iota
. Finally, defining
Q_{1}:=\iota Q\iota^{\ast}
, one has that
\iota Q^{1/2}:U\rightarrow Q^{1/2}_{1}(U_{1})
defines an isomorphism

Proof.

Almost sure convergence of the sum
(
1.4.13
)
in
U_{1}
follows from the bound,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{E}[\beta^{2}_{k}]\langle Q^{1/2}e_{k},Q^{1/2}e_{k}\rangle_{U_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\langle\iota Q^{1/2}e_{k},\iota Q^{1/2}e_{k}\rangle_{U_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\langle\iota Q\iota^{\ast}e_{k},e_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Tr\iota Q\iota^{\ast}<\infty.

Where the last bracket is understood as the duality bracket between
U
and
U_{1}
. Recall that since
\iota Q^{1/2}
is Hilbert–Schmidt,
\iota Q\iota^{\ast}
is trace class by definition. It therefore follows that
X=\sum_{k\geq 1}\beta_{k}Q^{1/2}e_{k}
defines a
U_{1}
valued Gaussian random variable, on
U
in the sense of Definition
1.4.8
. Furthermore, it is readily checked that for
X
defined in this way, the map,

U\ni h\mapsto X_{h}:=\sum_{k=1}\beta_{k}\langle Q^{1/2}e_{k},e_{k}\rangle\langle h,e_{k}\rangle,

defines a generalised Gaussian random variable, with zero mean, in the sense of Definition
1.4.13
. Furthermore, since the choice of
\iota,\,U_{1}
does not play a role in the action
h\mapsto X_{h}
they do not affect the definition, provided the embedding
\iota Q^{1/2}:U\rightarrow U_{1}
is Hilbert–Schmidt.

To see that
\iota Q^{1/2}
defines an isomorphism between
U
and
Q_{1}^{1/2}(U_{1})
, we first note that since
Q_{1}=(\iota Q)(\iota Q^{1/2})^{\ast}
, by Corollary
1.2.12
, we have
Q^{1/2}_{1}(U_{1})=\iota(U_{0})
. Furthermore, from the same result we have that
\|Q^{-1/2}_{1}h_{1}\|_{U_{1}}=\|\iota^{-1}u_{1}\|_{U_{0}}
for all
h_{1}\in\iota(U_{0})
. However, since the mapping
\iota:U_{0}\rightarrow U_{1}
is surjective, we may replace
h_{1}
by
\iota h_{0}
for any
h_{0}\in U_{0}
and the final claim is shown.
∎

In the case of the white noise,
W
, defined above, the Hilbert–Schmidt extension of
U
provides an example of
U_{1}
. However, due to the final assertion of Proposition
1.4.15
, the definition of the white noise is independent of the choice of
U_{1}
, provided the embedding
\iota:U_{0}\rightarrow U_{1}
is Hilbert–Schmidt.

Example 1.4.16
.

Let
U=L^{2}([0,T])
and
W
be a white noise defined on
U
. Then, define the real process,
t\mapsto W_{t}:=\langle W,\mathds{1}_{[0,t]}\rangle
. For any
t,\,s\in[0,T]
we have,

\mathbb{E}[W_{t}]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}[W_{t}W_{s}]=t\wedge s.

The mapping
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto W_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is by definition
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. continuous. Finally, if we consider the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
generated by
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, then by linearity, for any
t>s
we have

\mathbb{E}[W_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=\mathbb{E}[W_{s}+W_{t-s}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=W_{s}.

So by Levy’s characterisation,
t\mapsto W_{t}
defines a real Brownian motion.

1.5
Stochastic Analysis in Infinite Dimensions

The main purpose of this chapter is to build up a theory of stochastic analysis in infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces. In Chapter
3
we will use this theory to understand a certain class of SPDE as infinite dimensional stochastic evolution equations. We first develop some theory of abstract martingales in Banach spaces, after which we turn to the specific case of Gaussian processes in Hilbert spaces and conclude by developing a theory of stochastic integration for these processes.

Definition 1.5.1

(Stochastic Processes)
.

Given a Banach space
E
, equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra and
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
an abstract probability space, we say that a measurable map
X:[0,\infty)\times\Omega\rightarrow E
is an
E
valued stochastic process.

We typically suppress the dependence of
X
on
\omega\in\Omega
and use the notations
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
and
[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto X_{t}
to denote these processes.

Definition 1.5.2

(Modifications of Stochastic Processes)
.

Let
X,\,Y:[0,\infty)\times\Omega\rightarrow E
be two stochastic processes. Then we say that
Y
is a modification of
X
if for all
t\in[0,\infty)
, one has

\mathbb{P}[X_{t}=Y_{t}]=1.

In what follows we often deal with stochastic processes restricted to an interval
[0,T]
for
T\in(0,\infty)
. In this case these definition only change up to modifying the interval of definition.

1.5.1
Banach Space Valued Martingales

As in finite dimensional stochastic analysis, the class of Martingale processes will play a central role. We introduce the concept of a filtered probability space.
We say that a family
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, of
\sigma
-algebras is non-decreasing if
\mathcal{F}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
0\leq s<t<\infty
. We also introduce the notations,

\mathcal{F}_{t+}=\bigcap_{s>t}\mathcal{F}_{s},\quad\mathcal{F}_{t-}=\bigcap_{s<t}\mathcal{F}_{s}.

Definition 1.5.3

(Filtered Probability space)
.

We say that a tuple,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, consisting of the the usual triple; state space
\Omega
,
\sigma
-algebra,
\mathcal{F}
, and probability measure,
\mathbb{P}
, and in addition a non-decreasing family of sub-
\sigma
-algebras,
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a filtered probability space. We say that a filtration is normal if
\mathcal{F}_{0}
contains all
\mathbb{P}
-null sets of
\mathcal{F}
and if

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t+}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

Given a filtered probability space, we define the notion of an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-martingale.

Definition 1.5.4

(Martingales)
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic process, taking values in
E
and
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a filtration on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Then we say that
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-martingale if,

1.

\mathbb{E}[\|M_{t}\|_{E}]<\infty
for all
t\geq 0
,

2.

M_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
measurable for all
t\geq 0
,

3.

\mathbb{E}[M_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{s}]=M_{s}

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for all
0\leq s\leq t<\infty
.

We say that a stochastic process is a sub-martingale if the final property is replaced with the inequality
\mathbb{E}[M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\geq M_{s}
and a super-martingale if it is replaced by the inequality
\mathbb{E}[M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\leq M_{s}
.

Given a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, we recall the notion of a stopping time.

Definition 1.5.5

(Stopping Times)
.

We say that a random variable
\Omega\ni\omega\mapsto\tau(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
stopping time, if for any
t\geq 0
,

\{\omega\in\Omega\,:\,\tau(\omega)\leq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}.

Remark 1.5.6
.

If
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is normal, in the sense of Definition
1.5.3
, then any deterministic time
T\geq 0
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
stopping time.

Remark 1.5.7
.

We will mostly be concerned with so called hitting times, that is given an
E
valued process,
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, for
R\geq 0
and
\varphi\in E^{\ast}
, we define

\tau_{R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:|\varphi(X_{t})|>R\}.

If
t\mapsto X_{t}
is almost surely continuous and
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is normal then any random time of this form is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
stopping time.

The following example shows that in some cases the requirements of being a true martingale in the sense of Definition
1.5.4
are too stringent.

Example 1.5.8
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space carrying two random variables,
\xi
which is
\mathcal{F}_{1}
measurable, takes values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and such that
\mathbb{E}[\xi]=\infty
and
\eta
which is
\mathcal{F}_{2}
measurable, independent of
\mathcal{F}_{2-}
and takes values
\pm 1
each with probability
1/2
. We define the process
[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto X_{t}:=\mathds{1}_{t\geq 2}\eta\xi
. It follows immediately that
\mathbb{E}[|X_{2}|]=\mathbb{E}[\xi]=\infty
so that
X
cannot be a martingale. However, we can define the sequence of stopping times, for
R\geq 0
,

\tau_{R}:=\begin{cases}R&\text{if }\xi\leq R,\\
1&\text{if }\xi>R.\end{cases}

Since
\xi(\omega)<\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
\tau_{R}\nearrow\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. and for every
n\geq 0
,
X^{\tau_{R}}_{\,\cdot\,}:=X_{\tau_{R}\wedge\,\cdot\,}
is a bounded martingale.

This leads us to the definition local martingales, which will be recurring objects in our analysis. In particular the stochastic integrals we define in Subsections
1.5.4
&
1.5.5
will only be local martingales for the integrands we typically consider.

Definition 1.5.9

(Local Martingale)
.

We say that an
E
valued stochastic process
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a local martingale, if there exists a sequence of
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
stopping times
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}
such that
\tau_{n}\rightarrow\infty
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. and for any
n\geq 0
, the stopped process
(M^{\tau_{n}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}:=(M_{\tau_{n}\wedge t})_{t\geq 0}
is a martingale in the sense of Definition
1.5.4
. Local sub/super-martingales are defined analogously. The sequence
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is called a localising sequence for the local martingale
M
.

As in the previous section we make a connection to real martingales.

Proposition 1.5.10
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an
E
valued stochastic process such that
\mathbb{E}[\|M_{t}\|_{E}]<\infty
for all
t\geq 0
and
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a filtration as in Definition
1.5.4
. Then
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an
E
valued martingale if and only if
(\langle M_{t},h\rangle)_{t\geq 0}
is a real,
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-martingale for all
h\in E
. Furthermore, if
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-sub-martingale, then
(\|M_{t}\|^{p}_{E})_{t\geq 0}
is a real
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-sub-martingale for any
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Proof.

To prove the equivalence, it is clear that if
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an
E
valued
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-martingale then
(\langle M_{t},h\rangle)_{t\geq 0}
is a real
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-martingale for all
h\in E
. On the other hand, if
\langle M_{t},h\rangle
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
measurable for all
t\geq 0
and
h\in E
then the element
M_{t}\in E
must be
\mathcal{F}_{t}
measurable for every
t\geq 0
. To show the martingale property it suffices to recall the construction of the conditional expectation in Hilbert spaces from Theorem
1.4.6
.

To show that the norm is a real martingale, recall that by Hahn–Banach there exists a sequence of elements
(h_{k})_{k\geq 1}
such that
\|M_{t}\|_{E}=\sup_{k\geq 1}\langle M_{t},h_{k}\rangle
. Therefore, from the first equivalence,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|M_{t}\|_{E}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\geq\sup_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{E}[\langle M_{t},h_{k}\rangle|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=\sup_{k\geq 1}\langle M_{s},h_{k}\rangle=\|M_{t}\|_{E}.

This proves the claim for
p=1
, to show the same for
p>1
it suffices to apply Jensen in the first inequality.
∎

We have the following version of Doob’s maximal inequality.

Theorem 1.5.11
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a right continuous,
E
valued
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
-martingale. Then for any
p>1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|M_{t}\|^{p}_{E}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\frac{p}{p-1}\mathbb{E}[\|M_{T}\|^{p}_{E}]^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Proof.

The result follows from Proposition
1.5.10
and the same result for real valued, non-negative sub-martingales. See
[
CE15
, Thm. 4.5.6]

∎

From now on we will focus in particular on the square integrable, continuous, martingales, i.e
E
valued, continuous,
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
-martingales such that

\|M\|_{\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|M_{t}\|^{2}_{E}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|M_{T}\|^{2}_{E}\right]<\infty.

(1.5.1)

Identifying
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(E)
as a space as a subspace of the Banach space
L^{2}(\Omega;C([0,T];E))
it follows that
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(E)
is itself a Banach space if it can be shown to be closed. However, by construction of the conditional expectation, it is easy to show that even convergence in
L^{1}(\Omega;C([0,T];E))
preserves the martingale property and so
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(E)
is a Banach space, with norm given by (
1.5.1
). Note that finiteness of (
1.5.1
) is not enough to ensure that a local martingale,
M
, is a true martingale, so that we may also speak of continuous, square integrable, local martingales and we write
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T;\text{loc}}(E)
for this space.

Our ultimate goal in this chapter is to build the stochastic integral with respect to a given element of
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(E)
, concretely a
Q
-Wiener process. In order to do so we proceed in analogy with the construction of the Lebesgue integral. We first identify a suitable space of simple processes, for which the integral is naturally defined. We then show that this integral is an isometry from the simple processes, equipped with a suitable norm, to the space
\mathcal{M}_{T}^{2}
. We then define the stochastic integral as the limit under approximation by simple processes. From now on we keep as implicit a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]};\mathbb{P})
and assume that all martingales are with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. We also assume without further comment that all martingales are zero at
t=0
.

1.5.2
Hilbert Space Valued Wiener Processes

In the context of stochastic evolution equations we will wish to consider noise terms given by evolving processes of random variables. That is a Hilbert space valued stochastic process. Given a spatial domain,
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we typically think of
U
in the previous section, being a Hilbert space of functions
f:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the noise as being a sequence of random elements in
U
(or some larger space) indexed continuously by
t\geq 0
. We begin by defining the class of
Q
-Wiener processes.

Definition 1.5.12
.

Given a Hilbert space
U
and a trace class operator
Q\in L_{1}(U)
, we say that
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard
Q
-Wiener process if,

1.

W_{0}=0\in U
,

2.

the map
\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni t\mapsto W_{t}\in U
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. continuous,

3.

for any
0\leq s<t<\infty
, one has

W_{t}-W_{s}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(t-s)Q)\quad\text{and}\quad W_{t}-W_{s}\perp W_{s}.

The qualification
standard
here refers to the condition
W_{0}=0
, which entails by the martingale property (see below) that
\mathbb{E}[\langle W_{t},h\rangle]=0
for all
t\geq 0
. Setting
W_{0}=m
for a deterministic element
m\in E
gives a non-centred
Q
-Wiener process. One can also consider random initial data, provided the independent increments assumption still holds. This can be useful for example when studying invariant behaviour of stochastic systems, as one may wish to start the system already from its invariant distribution. From now on we only treat standard
Q
-Wiener processes and remove the qualifier from all subsequent statements.

Given Theorem
1.4.12
we can give a characterisation of the
Q
-Wiener processes.

Theorem 1.5.13
.

Let
Q\in L_{1}(U)
and
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1},\,(\lambda_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be an orthonormal basis of
U
generated by
Q
and associated eigenvalues. Then
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
Q
-Wiener process if and only if,

W_{t}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\beta^{k}_{t}e_{k},

(1.5.2)

where
(\beta^{k})_{k\geq 1}
are independent, real, Brownian motions.

Proof.

This result essentially follows from the characterisation of Gaussian random variables given by Theorem
1.4.12
. It follows directly that the random variables
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined in this way are centred, Gaussian,
U
-valued random variables. Since
Q
is trace class, the right hand side of (
1.5.2
) is square summable in expectation and so by the dominated convergence theorem we may exchange summation and the expectation. Therefore, independence of the increments,
W_{t}-W_{s}
, follows from independence of the increments
\beta^{k}_{t}-\beta_{s}^{k}
. finally, almost sure continuity of the paths follows also from almost sure continuity of the maps
t\mapsto\beta^{k}_{t}
, although in Chapter
4
we show this more directly by a generalisation of the Kolmogorov continuity criterion.
∎

For the remainder of this chapter, unless otherwise mentioned
Q\in L_{1}(U)
. Given a filtered probability space we define the notion of a
Q
-Wiener process with respect to a filtration.

Definition 1.5.14
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a normal, filtered probability space and
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
Q
-Wiener process. We say that
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
Q
-Wiener process with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
if,

1.

W_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
measurable for every
t\geq 0
,

2.

W_{t}-W_{s}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}
for all
0\leq s<t<\infty
.

Every
Q
-Wiener process naturally induces a normal filtration, defined by setting, for all
t\geq 0
,

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}:=\sigma(W_{t}\,:\,s\leq t),\quad\tilde{\mathcal{F}}^{0}_{t}:=\sigma(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\cup\{A\in\mathcal{F}\,:\,\mathbb{P}[A]=0\}),\quad\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\bigcap_{s>t}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}^{0}_{s}.

From now on we will always consider filtrations generated in this way and so without further comment we will assume that any
Q
-Wiener process is adapted to the given filtration. A probabilistically strong solution to our SPDE will be a stochastic process
(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}
also adapted to the given filtration.

As in the finite dimensional case, the
Q
-Wiener processes are martingales. Let us a fix a
T>0
and adapt the definitions above accordingly. Then we have the following lemma.

Lemma 1.5.15
.

Let
(W)_{t\in[0,T]}
be a
Q
-Wiener process and
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be its natural filtration. Then
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(U)
.

Proof.

The fact that
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(U)
follows from the definition of
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
and the assumption of independent increments of
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. If we equip
U
with the orthonormal basis generated by
Q
, then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[\|W_{t}\|^{2}_{U}]=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[|\langle W_{t},W_{t}\rangle|\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\langle W_{t},e_{k}\rangle|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=T\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle Qe_{k},e_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Tr Q\,T<\infty.

∎

1.5.3
Generalised Wiener Processes

As in subsection
1.4.2
, it is natural to ask if we can extend the definition of a Wiener process beyond that of Definition
1.5.12
. This leads to the definition of generalised Wiener processes, which includes the white noise (or cylindrical Wiener) process.

Definition 1.5.16

(Generalised Wiener Process)
.

Given a Hilbert space
U
, we say that a family of linear maps
[0,T]\times U\ni(t,h)\mapsto W_{h;t}\in\mathbb{R}
defines a generalised Wiener process if for each
h\in U
,
t\mapsto W_{h;t}
defines a real Wiener process and if for any sequence
(h_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset U
converging to
h\in U
we have,

\mathbb{E}\left[|W_{h;t}-W_{h_{n};t}|^{2}\right]\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

We employ the same ideas as in Subsection
1.4.2
to understand
(W_{h;t})_{t\geq 0}
as a Wiener process taking values in a space slightly larger than
U
. From the definition of a generalised Wiener process it follows that there exists a bi-linear form
K:U\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a symmetric, non-negative operator
Q\in L(U)
such that for all
s,\,t\geq 0
and
h,g\in U
,

\mathbb{E}[W_{h;t}W_{g;s}]=t\wedge sK(h,g)=t\wedge s\langle Qh,g\rangle.

We write
W_{t}
as a formal sum,

W_{t}:=\sum_{k\geq 1}\beta^{k}_{t}Qe_{k},

where
(\beta^{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a family of i.i.d, standard, real, Brownian motions and
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is an orthonormal basis of
U
. As in Subsection
1.4.2
we define the reproducing kernel of
W
, as the space
U_{0}:=Q^{1/2}(U)
and we recall that for any
t\geq 0
,
h\mapsto W_{t;h}
defines a generalised Gaussian random variable on
U
and it takes values in any space
U_{1}\hookleftarrow U_{0}
such that the embedding
\iota:U_{0}\rightarrow U_{1}
is Hilbert–Schmidt. Thus, we may consider
(W_{h;t})_{t\in[0,T],h\in U}
as a
U_{1}
valued
Q
-Wiener process on
U
and its definition is independent of the choice of
U_{1}
. By definition, the generalised Wiener process is an element of
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(U_{1})
.

Example 1.5.17

(Space-Time White Noise)
.

A common example of a generalised Wiener process is the space-time white noise, sometimes called a cylindrical Wiener process. It is the natural infinite dimensional analogue of the finite dimensional white noise familiar from classical stochastic analysis. Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded, domain and
U:=L^{2}(\Gamma)
. Then the space-time white noise on
[0,T]\times\Gamma
is the generalised Wiener process
[0,T]\times L^{2}(\Gamma)\ni(t,h)\mapsto W_{h;t}
such that, for all
h,\,g\in U
and
s,\,t\in[0,T]
,

\mathbb{E}[W_{t;h}]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}[W_{t;h}W_{s;g}]=t\wedge s\langle h,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}.

One can check that for given a family of i.i.d, standard, real Brownian motions,
(\beta^{k})_{k\geq 1}
and a orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Gamma)
,
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, the formal sum

W_{t}:=\sum_{k\geq 1}\beta^{k}_{t}e_{k},

defines a generalised Gaussian process in the above sense, known as the cylindrical Gaussian process, and that the sum converges in any Hilbert space
U_{1}
such that the embedding
\iota:L^{2}(\Gamma)\rightarrow U_{1}
is Hilbert–Schmidt. The formal derivative,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{k}_{t}e_{k}
is referred to as the space-time white-noise.

An alternative approach to defining the space-time white noise is to return to Subsection
1.4.2
and consider the Hilbert space
U:=L_{0}^{2}([0,T]\times\Gamma)
of square integrable space-time functions, zero at zero. Then it is an easy exercise to check that the white noise on
L_{0}^{2}([0,T]\times\Gamma)
coincides with the space-time white noise defined above.

1.5.4
Stochastic Integration Against
Q
-Wiener Processes

We conclude this chapter by giving meaning to integrals of the form

[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s},

(1.5.3)

where
(\varphi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is an adapted, suitably regular, operator valued process. We first treat the case when
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a
Q
-Wiener process from a Hilbert space to itself and treat this case in the most detail. We follow this by defining the stochastic integral for
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
a generalised Wiener process, of which the space-time white noise will be our canonical example.

We fix a Hilbert space,
U
and assume that
W
is an
U
valued
Q
-Wiener process. We recall the definition of its reproducing kernel, the Hilbert space
U_{0}:=Q^{1/2}(U)\subseteq U
equipped with the inner product
\langle Q^{-1/2}f,Q^{-1/2}g\rangle_{U}
. We also fix a second Hilbert space,
H
; we will subsequently assume
(\varphi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
takes values in a subspace of
L(U_{0};H)
, so that the stochastic integral, (
1.5.3
), takes values in
H
.

As in the finite dimensional case we first define the stochastic integral for a suitable class of simple processes, before extending it to a larger space of integrands.

Definition 1.5.18

(Simple Processes in
L(U;H)
)
.

We say that a process
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\varphi_{t}\in L(U;H)
is simple if, there exist a sequence of deterministic times,
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n-1}=T
, and a set of
L(U;H)
valued,
(\mathcal{F}_{t_{m}})^{n-1}_{m=0}
-measurable, random variables,
(\varphi_{m})_{m=1}^{n-1}
, taking only finitely many values, such that,

\varphi_{t}=\varphi_{0}\mathds{1}_{\{0\}}(t)+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\varphi_{m}\mathds{1}_{(t_{m},t_{m+1}]}(t).

We write
\mathcal{E}_{T}(U;H)
for the set of
L(U;H)
valued simple processes on
[0,T]
.

We define the space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators
L^{0}_{2}(U_{0};H)
equipped with the norm,

\|\varphi\|^{2}_{L^{0}_{2}}:=\Tr\left[(Q^{1/2}\varphi)(Q^{1/2}\varphi)^{\ast}\right].

Note that since
L^{0}_{2}
is the space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators defined on the image of a Hilbert–Schmidt operator
Q^{1/2}:U\rightarrow U
, it follows that that
L(U;H)\subset L^{0}_{2}(U,H)
and so example the simple process
\mathcal{E}_{T}(U;H)
are measurable,
L^{0}_{2}(U_{0},H)
, valued processes.

This allows us to define the set of processes that are square integrable with respect to a
Q
-Wiener process. For a measurable process
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\varphi_{t}\in L^{0}_{2}(U_{0},H)
, we set,

\|\varphi\|_{\mathscr{H}_{T}^{2}}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\varphi_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{0}_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\Tr\left[(Q^{1/2}\varphi_{s})(Q^{1/2}\varphi_{s})^{\ast}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

If
\varphi\in\mathcal{E}_{T}
then, since we specified that the random variables
(\varphi_{m})_{m=1}^{n-1}
all take only finitely many values in
L(U;H)
, it follows directly that
\|\varphi\|_{\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}}<\infty
for simple processes.

We are now able to define the stochastic integral for simple process. We will then show that the map sending
\varphi\in\mathcal{E}_{T}(U;H)
to its stochastic integral, (
1.5.3
), is an isometry between
\mathcal{E}_{T}(U;H)
and
L^{2}(\Omega;H)
. Given
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
we define the linear map,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{T}(U;H)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(H)

(1.5.4)

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{\,\cdot\,}:=\int_{0}^{\,\cdot\,}\varphi_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\varphi_{k}(W_{t_{k+1}\wedge\,\cdot\,}-W_{t_{k}\wedge\,\cdot\,}).

We show in the next proposition that this map does indeed define an isometry from
(\mathcal{E}_{T},\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}})
to the space of square integrable martingales,
(\mathcal{M}_{T}^{2}(H),\,\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}})
, where the norm is defined in (
1.5.1
).

Proposition 1.5.19
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{E}_{T}(U;H)
. Then,
(\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, defined in (
1.5.4
), is a continuous, square integrable,
H
-valued martingale, adapted to the natural filtration of
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. Furthermore, one has that

\|\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W\|_{\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}}=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[\|\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{t}\|_{H}^{2}]=\|\varphi\|_{\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}}.

(1.5.5)

Proof.

The fact that
t\mapsto\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{t}
defines a continuous,
H
-valued martingale is clear from the definitions. Once can check the martingale property in detail using optional stopping, see proof of
[
PR07
, Prop. 2.3.2]
. We check square integrabillity by proving (
1.5.5
) directly. Note that it suffices to show (
1.5.5
) for
t=t_{k}\in[0,T]
. Let
\zeta_{k}:=W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_{k}}
so that we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|(\varphi\cdot W)_{t}\|^{2}_{H}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi_{k}\zeta_{k}\right\|_{E}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|\varphi_{k}\zeta_{k}\|_{H}^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{2}=1\\
k_{1}<k_{2}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\langle\varphi_{k_{1}}\zeta_{k_{1}},\varphi_{k_{2}}\zeta_{k_{2}}\rangle\right].

Considering the first term, taking each summand individually and using the definition of a
Q
-Wiener process, and
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
the basis of
U_{0}
associated to the Hilbert–Schmidt operator
\varphi_{k}\in L_{2}^{0}(U_{0},H)
, and defining
\varphi^{\ast}_{k}\in L_{2}(H;U_{0})
to be its adjoint, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\varphi_{k}\zeta_{k}\|_{E}^{2}]=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[|\langle\varphi_{k}\zeta_{k},e_{m}\rangle|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[|\langle\zeta_{k},\varphi_{k}^{\ast}e_{m}\rangle|^{2}]

\displaystyle=(t_{k+1}-t_{k})\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|\langle Q\varphi_{k}^{\ast}e_{m},\varphi_{k}^{\ast}e_{m}\rangle|

\displaystyle=(t_{k+1}-t_{k})\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\|Q^{1/2}\varphi_{k}^{\ast}e_{m}\|^{2}_{E}

\displaystyle=(t_{k+1}-t_{k})\|\varphi_{k}\|_{L^{0}_{2}}.

Similarly, by the independence of increments, one has

\mathbb{E}[\langle\varphi_{k_{1}}\zeta_{k_{1}},\varphi_{k_{2}}\zeta_{k_{2}}\rangle]=0,\quad k_{1}\neq k_{2}.

Putting these two results together and summing over

gives equality (
1.5.5
).
∎

We now wish to extend the definition of
\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W
to the set of all
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
L^{0}_{2}
-predictable processes. Before doing so however, we introduce the predictable
\sigma
-algebra and the notion of a predictable process. This is necessary since we will want our stochastic integration to preserve the martingale property and the notion of a predictable process ensures it does not look into the future. Note that for simple processes this was not a problem, since almost by definition their integrals were martingales.

Definition 1.5.20
.

[Predictable
\sigma
-algebra]
Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a given filtration. Then we define,
\mathscr{P}_{T}
, to be the
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
[0,T]\times\Omega
given by,

\mathscr{P}_{T}:=\sigma\left(\{(s,t]\times F\,:\,0\leq s<t\leq T,\,F\in\mathcal{F}_{s}\}\cup\{\{0\}\times F\,:\,F\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\}\right).

Given a Hilbert space,
\mathcal{X}
, we say that a process
\varphi:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
is predictable if it is
\mathscr{P}_{T}
-measurable.

Let us define the measure space
(\Omega_{T},\mathcal{P}_{T},\mathbb{P}_{T})
where
\Omega_{T}=[0,T]\times\Omega
,
\mathcal{P}_{T}
is as above and
\mathbb{P}_{T}=\lambda_{T}\otimes\mathbb{P}
with
\lambda_{T}
the Lebesgue measure on
[0,T]
. Note that the norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}}
defined above corresponds to the Bochner integral,

\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}}=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\|\varphi_{t}(\omega)\|^{2}_{L^{0}_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{T}(t,\omega).

Recall that since
L^{0}_{2}
is a separable Banach space, this Bochner integral is well defined, see Section
1.3
.

With
W
fixed, let us define the space of square integrable stochastic integrands - the fact that they are suitable stochastic integrands will be proved in Theorem
1.5.23
.

Definition 1.5.21
.

[Stochastic Integrands]
We say that
[0,T]\times\Omega\ni(t,\omega)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(\omega)\in L^{0}_{2}(U_{0},H)
is stochastically integrable with respect to
W
if it is
\mathscr{P}_{T}
measurable with respect to the natural filtration of
W
and is such that
\|\varphi\|_{\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}}<\infty
. We denote this space by
\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}(W)
and observe that it forms a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\varphi,\tilde{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}}=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\varphi_{t},\tilde{\varphi}_{t}\rangle_{L^{0}_{2}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

The following proposition allows us to approximate predictable elements of
\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}(W)
by simple processes in
\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}(W)\cap\mathcal{E}_{T}
.

Proposition 1.5.22
.

The following statements both hold:

i)

If a mapping
\varphi:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow L(U;H)
is
L(U;H)
-predictable, then it is also
L^{0}_{2}
-predictable.

ii)

If
\varphi\in\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}(W)
is

-predictable then there exists a sequence of simple processes
(\varphi^{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|\varphi-\varphi^{n}\|_{\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}}=0
.

Proof.

To prove
i)
, first recall from Remark
1.2.6
that given bases
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1},\,(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of
U
and
H
respectively, the family
(e_{k}\otimes f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a basis of
L_{2}(U;H)
. So it follows that
L(U;H)
is a dense, linear subspace of
L_{2}(U;H)
. Furthermore, since
Q^{1/2}(U)=U_{0}\subset U
has continuous image in

, it follows that
L(U;H)\subset L_{2}(U_{0};H)=L^{0}_{2}
with continuous embedding. By an adaptation of Theorem
1.4.4
it follows that any
L(U;H)
valued process is also an
L^{0}_{2}
valued process, with continuous image. Since predictability is preserved by continuous transformations the first claim follows.

As in the proof of
i)
we use the fact that
L(U;H)
is densely embedded in
L^{0}_{2}
and Proposition
1.4.3
that for
\mathbb{P}_{T}
-a.e
(t,\omega)\in\Omega_{T}
there exists a sequence of simple
L(U;H)
valued random variables,
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{E}_{T}
such that

\|\varphi_{t}(\omega)-\varphi_{n;t}(\omega)\|_{L^{2}_{0}}\rightarrow 0,\quad\mathbb{P}_{T}\,{\text{-a.s.}}.

It follows from the assumptions, that therefore
\|\varphi-\varphi_{n}\|_{\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}}\rightarrow 0
. Note, however, that we have not yet treated predictability. So to conclude it suffices to show that any predictable set
A\in\mathscr{P}_{T}
can be approximated arbitrarily by predictable events in
\mathbb{P}_{T}
. However, this follows from Dynkin’s lemma, since the predictable sets form a
\pi
-system which generates
\mathcal{P}_{T}
. See the last paragraph in the proof of
[
DPZ14
, Prop. 4.22]
for details.
∎

Therefore, for fixed
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, we have defined an isometry,
\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}(W)\ni\varphi\mapsto(\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W)\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(U)
, on a linear, dense subspace. This allows us to extend the map, first to all of
\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}(W)
and then to all
L^{0}_{2}
-predictable processes,
\varphi
such that

\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\varphi_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{0}_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s<\infty\right]=1.

(1.5.6)

We denote this, the full space of integrands, by
\mathscr{H}_{T}(W)
.

Theorem 1.5.23
.

Given
\varphi\in\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}(W)
, the stochastic integral,

[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}=:\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{t},

(1.5.7)

defines a continuous, square integrable,
H
-valued martingale. Furthermore, if instead
\varphi\in\mathscr{H}_{T}(W)
then (
1.5.7
) defines a continuous, local martingale.

Proof.

Combining Proposition
1.5.22
and the isometry property, (
1.5.5
), for the Itô integral defined for simple processes, allows us to extend the integral to all of
\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}(W)
by density. Since the martingale property and continuity of paths are preserved under convergence in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, it follows that the integral takes values in
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(H)
.

To extend the integral to all of
\mathscr{H}_{T}(W)
, we first claim that for
\tau
an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
stopping time and
\varphi\in\mathscr{H}^{2}_{T}(W)
, it holds that,

\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{[0,\tau]}(s)\varphi_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}=\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{t\wedge\tau},\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,T],\,\,\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

(1.5.8)

In other words, stopping the integrand at
\tau
amounts to stopping the integral. Identity (
1.5.8
) can be shown by approximating
\tau
by simple stopping times (stopping times taking only finitely many values) and
\varphi

[
DPZ14
, Lem. 4.24]
. So then for
\varphi\in\mathscr{H}_{T}(W)
, i.e such that (
1.5.6
) holds, let us define,

\tau_{n}:=\inf\left\{t\in[0,T]\,:\,\int_{0}^{t}\|\varphi_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{0}_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\geq n\right\},\quad n\geq 0,

(1.5.9)

with the convention that
\tau_{n}=T
if the set is empty. Therefore, by (

\mathds{1}_{[0,\tau_{n}]}\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W=\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{\,\cdot\,\wedge\tau_{n}}
are well defined. Furthermore, due to (
1.5.6

(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. increasing so for any

and
\mathbb{P}
-a.a.
\omega\in\Omega
there exists an

such that
t\leq\tau_{n}(\omega)
. Therefore, the integral
t\mapsto\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{t}=\mathds{1}_{[0,\tau_{n}]}(t)\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{t}
, with

, is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. well defined for all

and the definition is independent of the chosen sequence
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfying (

). Since
\mathscr{H}_{T}(W)
contains elements which are not stochastically square integrable on

, the resulting stochastic integral is only a local martingale, with the localisation given by a sequence of the form (

).
∎

1.5.5
Stochastic Integration Against Generalised Wiener Processes

We recall that given a, non-negative, symmetric, bounded, linear operator
Q\in L(U)
, there exists a generalised Wiener process
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
on
U
, with covariance
Q
. Furthermore, there exists a pair of Hilbert spaces
U_{0}:=Q^{1/2}(U),\,U_{1}
such that
U_{0}\hookrightarrow U_{1}
and the embedding
\iota U_{0}\rightarrow U_{1}
is Hilbert–Schmidt. As a result, the generalised Wiener process,
[0,T]\times U\ni(t,h)\mapsto W_{h;t}
, can be seen as taking values in
U_{1}
, although the particular choice of
U_{1}
is not important.

As such, we may almost immediately define the stochastic integral with respect to a generalised Wiener process for all predictable, processes,
(\varphi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\in L_{2}(Q^{1/2}(U_{1});H)
such that,

\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\varphi_{s}\|^{2}_{L_{2}(Q^{1/2}(U_{1});H)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s<\infty\right]=1.

It suffices to replace
U_{0}
with
Q^{1/2}(U_{1})
in the previous section. However, this is unsatisfactory, since we know that the definition of the generalised Wiener process is independent of the choice
U_{1}
and so it would be natural for the integral to retain this property. It turns out that we can retain the same space of integrands as in Subsection
1.5.4
. Note that
U_{0}:=Q^{1/2}(U)
is still well defined in this case, only now it does not hold that the embedding
U_{0}\hookrightarrow U
is Hilbert–Schmidt.

Proposition 1.5.24
.

Let
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be an orthonormal basis of
U
,
(\beta^{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a family of i.i.d, standard, real Brownian motions,
Q\in L(U)
be non-negative and symmetric and
\iota:Q^{1/2}(U)=:U_{0}\rightarrow U_{1}
define a Hilbert–Schmidt embedding as above. Then, defining the non-negative, symmetric and trace class operator,
Q_{1}:=\iota Q\iota^{\ast}\in L_{1}(U_{1})
, it follows that,

[0,T]\ni t\mapsto W_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta^{k}_{t}\iota Q^{1/2}e_{k},

(1.5.10)

defines
Q_{1}
-Wiener process in
\mathcal{M}^{2}_{T}(U_{1})
. Moreover, one has that
Q_{1}^{1/2}(U_{1})=\iota(U_{0})\subset U_{1}
and for all
h_{0}\in U_{0}
,

\|h_{0}\|_{U_{0}}=\|Q_{1}^{-1/2}\iota h_{0}\|_{U_{1}}=\|\iota h_{0}\|_{Q^{1/2}_{1}U_{1}},

where
Q^{-1/2}_{1}
is the pseudo-inverse of
Q_{1}^{1/2}
, see Definition
1.2.9
. In other words,
\iota:U_{0}\rightarrow Q^{1/2}_{1}U_{1}
is an isometry.

Proof.

By assumption,
\iota Q^{1/2}
, defines a trace class operator from
U
to
U_{1}
and so it follows that the right hand side of (
1.5.10
) defines a
U_{1}
valued Wiener process. To see that
W
defines a generalised Wiener process on
U
in the sense of Definition
1.5.16
we may apply the same arguments as in the proof of Proposition
1.4.15
. The independence of the definition on
U_{1},\,\iota
also follows from the same arguments and we recall, using, Corollary
1.2.12
, that
\iota:U_{0}\rightarrow Q^{1/2}_{1}U_{1}
is an isometry.
∎

Theorem 1.5.25
.

Let
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a generalised Wiener process on
U
,
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\varphi_{t}\in L_{2}(U_{0};H)=:L^{0}_{2}
be a stochastic process, such that,

\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\varphi_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{0}_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty\right]=1.

Then, letting
U_{1}
,
\iota:U_{0}\rightarrow U_{1}
be as above, the stochastic integral defined by setting

[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\varphi\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}W_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}(\varphi_{s}\circ\iota^{-1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s},

(1.5.11)

is a continuous,
H

Proof.

By Proposition
1.5.24
, for
Q_{1}=\iota\iota^{\ast}\in N(U_{1})
, we have
Q^{1/2}_{1}(U_{1})=\iota(U_{0})
and by the polarisation identity, for any
\varphi_{0},\psi_{0}\in U_{0}
,

\langle\iota\varphi_{0},\iota\psi_{0}\rangle_{Q^{1/2}_{1}U_{1}}=\langle Q^{-1/2}_{1}\iota\varphi_{0},Q^{-1/2}_{1}\iota\psi_{0}\rangle_{U_{1}}=\langle\varphi_{0},\psi_{0}\rangle_{U_{0}}.

In particular, given

a basis of

is a basis of

\varphi\in L_{2}(U_{0};H)\quad\iff\quad\varphi\circ\iota^{-1}\in L_{2}(Q_{1}^{1/2}(U_{1});H),

and so the stochastic integral in (

. The independence on the choice of

∎

Chapter 2
Variational Approach to PDE

In this chapter we present an introduction to the variational approach to partial differential evolution equations. The method relies on viewing time dependent PDE in spirit as ordinary differential equations in infinite dimensional spaces. We use this is our starting point for viewing SPDE as infinite dimensional stochastic differential equations in the next chapter. A useful outcome of this approach to PDE and SPDE is a natural approach to numerical analysis of such problems through the finite element method. This approach is also sometimes known as the Galerkin method.

2.1
Unbounded Operators Between Banach Spaces

We begin by recalling the notion of unbounded operators on Banach spaces. We refer to
[
Bre11
, Sec. 2.6]
for more details.

Definition 2.1.1

(Unbounded Linear Operators)
.

Given Banach spaces
E,\,F
, an unbounded, linear operator
A
from
E
to
F
is a pair
(A,D(A))
, where
D(A)\subseteq E
is a linear subspace and
A:D(A)\rightarrow F
is a linear map. We write write
\mathscr{L}(E;F)
for the set of unbounded linear operators from
X
to
Y
, leaving the dependence on defining a domain inside
X
implicit in the notation.

We say that
A
is densely defined if
D(A)
is a dense subset of
E
. If
A
is densely defined from
E
to
F
and continuous on its domain then there exists a unique, continuous extension of
A
defined on all of
E\rightarrow F
.

We define the graph of
A
,
G_{A}:=\{(x,y)\in E\oplus F\,:\,y=Ax\}
and say that
A
is a closed operator if
G_{A}\subset E\oplus F
is a closed set. Explicitly,
A
is closed if and only if for any sequence
\{(x_{n},Ax_{n})\}_{n\geq 0}\subset E\oplus F
such that
x_{n}\rightarrow x
and
Ax_{n}\rightarrow y
, we have
x\in D(A)
and
y=Tx
.

Example 2.1.2
.

Let
C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
be the set of continuous, real valued functions on the unit interval, equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|f(x)|
. Then, for
f\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, define
Af:=\frac{d}{dx}f
and

D\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right):=C^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R})\subset C([0,1];\mathbb{R}),

the set of continuously differentiable maps
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{C^{1}}:=\|f\|_{\infty}+\|\frac{d}{dx}f\|_{\infty}
. It follows that
(\frac{d}{dx},C^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R}))
is an unbounded, linear operator from
C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
to itself in the sense of Definition
2.1.1
.

By Stone–Weierstrass,
\frac{d}{dx}
is densely defined, however, it is also unbounded on its domain and so cannot be continuously extended to all of
C([0,1];\mathbb{R})
. To see this, define the sequence
f_{n}(x):=\sin(nx)
, we have
\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}\equiv 1
but
\|\frac{d}{dx}f_{n}\|_{\infty}=n\rightarrow\infty
. On the other hand, it is easy to see that
\frac{d}{dx}
is a closed operator, since if
\|f-f_{n}\|_{C^{1}}\rightarrow 0
, then by definition
\|\frac{d}{dx}(f-f_{n})\|_{\infty}\rightarrow 0
.

Example 2.1.3

(Dirichlet Laplacian)
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth, bounded domain and define the second order differential operator, for mappings
f:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\Delta f:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial^{2}_{ii}f.

We consider
\Delta
as an unbounded operator on the set,

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Gamma):=\left\{f:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\,:\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+\sup_{i=\{1,\ldots,d\}}\|\partial_{i}f\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}<\infty,\,f|_{\partial\Gamma}=0\right\},

with domain of definition,
D(\Delta)
,

C^{2}_{c}(\Gamma):=\left\{f:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\,:\,\sup_{i,j=\{1,\,\ldots,d\}}\|\partial_{ij}f\|_{\infty}<\infty,\,\text{supp}(f)\text{ is compact in }\Gamma\right\}.

The lack of boundedness in Example
2.1.2
arose because we chose too small a target space for the derivative map. In order that
\Delta
remain bounded on its domain in this case, we define
\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Gamma):=(\mathcal{H}(\Gamma))^{\ast}
, which can be explicitly represented as,

\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Gamma):=\left\{h=f+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}g,\,f,\,g\in L^{2}(\Gamma;\mathbb{R})\right\}.

(2.1.1)

Then we consider the unbounded operator,
\Delta:C^{2}_{c}(\Gamma)\subset\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Gamma)
. It follows from the density of
C^{2}_{c}(\Gamma)
in
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Gamma)
and the fact that
\Delta
is continuous as a map from
C^{2}_{c}(\Gamma)
to
\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Gamma)
that there exists a unique extension of
\Delta:\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Gamma)
.

Note that by setting
g=0
in (
2.1.1
), we see that
L^{2}(\Gamma)\subset\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Gamma)
. Furthermore, it is clear that
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Gamma)\subset L^{2}(\Gamma)
and so we have the ordering,

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Gamma)\subset L^{2}(\Gamma)\subset\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Gamma)=(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Gamma))^{\ast}.

This is an example of a Gelfand triple, and
\Delta:\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\Gamma)
defines a continuous, coercive operator. We explore this setting in more general abstraction in the next section.

2.2
Linear and Coercive PDE

Let
V
be a separable, reflexive, Banach space with
V\subset H
a separable Hilbert space. It is readily checked that for Banach spaces
V,\,H
, one has,

V\subset H\quad\iff\quad H^{\ast}\subset V^{\ast}.

However, for
H
a Hilbert space, since we also have
H\cong H^{\ast}
, it follows that

V\subset H\cong H^{\ast}\subset V^{\ast}.

The triple,
(V,H,V^{\ast})
is called a
Gelfand triple
. In what follows we write
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{V}
for the canonical norm on
V
and
|\,\cdot\,|_{H}
for the canonical norm on
H
. We use
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
to denote both the duality pairing between
V,\,V^{\ast}
and the inner product on
H
and interpret this notation accordingly wherever it occurs. Since the two expressions agree only when both arguments lie in
H
this should not cause too much confusion. We recall the following important theorem, regarding weak compactness in normed vector spaces.

Theorem 2.2.1

(Banach–Alaoglu)
.

Let
E
be a normed vector space. Then the closed unit ball in
E^{\ast}
is compact with respect to the weak
-\ast
topology. Furthermore,
E
is reflexive if and only if the closed unit ball in
E
is weakly compact.

Proof.

See
[
Bre11
, Thm. 3.16 & Thm. 3.17]
.
∎

Remark 2.2.2
.

It follows from Theorem
2.2.1
, that the closed unit ball of any Hilbert space is weakly compact.

Let
A:D(A)\subset V\rightarrow V^{\ast}
be an unbounded linear operator,
T>0
and
B\in L^{2}([0,T];V^{\ast})
be a square integrable map. Then we consider the deterministic, linear PDE

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-Au=B_{t},\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}.\end{cases}

(2.2.1)

One might be concerned that (
2.2.1
) does not involve a specified spatial domain. However, this is intentional and we leave any specification of spatial domain and any boundary conditions to the definition of the spaces
V
and
H
. It may be that in particular cases of interest more care must be taken, for example when considering domains with non-standard geometry or more involved boundary conditions.

In order to give a proper definition of weak solutions to (
2.2.1
) on
[0,T]
we need a preliminary definition of weak derivatives in time.

Definition 2.2.3

(Weak Derivative in Time)
.

Let
E
be a real Banach space and
u\in L^{1}([0,T];E)
. Then we say that
v\in L^{1}([0,T];E)
, is the weak derivative in time of
u
and write
v=\frac{d}{dt}u
, if for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
,

\int_{0}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}\varphi_{t}u_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{0}^{T}\varphi_{t}v_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\in E,

where both integrals are understood as Bochner integrals.

We are now in a position to give a meaningful definition of weak solutions to (
2.2.1
).

Definition 2.2.4

(PDE Weak Solution)
.

We say that
u\in L^{2}([0,T];V)
is a weak solution to (
2.2.1
) if
\frac{d}{dt}u\in L^{2}([0,T];V^{\ast})
and for any
v\in D(A)\subset V
and
t\in(0,T]
, one has the identity,

\left\langle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}u_{t},v\right\rangle=\langle Au_{t},v\rangle+\langle B_{t},v\rangle.

(2.2.2)

In other words, a weak solution is such that the identity,
\frac{d}{dt}u=Au_{t}+B_{t}
, holds in
V^{\ast}
.

Remark 2.2.5
.

Note that the duality pairings on the right hand side are all well defined since by assumption
A
takes values in
V^{\ast}
on
V
and
B\in L^{2}([0,T];V^{\ast})
.

Remark 2.2.6
.

One could alternatively phrase (
2.2.2
) in integral form and avoid introducing Definition
2.2.3
at this stage. This is the perspective that we take in Chapter
3
, where the inclusion of an Itô integral term in the PDE makes the integral form more informative.

Our starting point is to show that under the assumption of
coercivity
on
A
, there exists a unique, weak solution to (
2.2.1
).

Assumption 2.2.7

(Coercivity I)
.

There exist
\lambda,\,\alpha>0
such that for any
u\in V\subset H
,

2\langle Au,u\rangle+\alpha\|u\|_{V}^{2}\leq\lambda|u|^{2}_{H}

(H1)

We will require the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2.8

(Lions–Magenes)
.

Let
(V,H,V^{\ast})
be a Gelfand triple as above and
u\in L^{2}([0,T];V)
be such that
\frac{d}{dt}u\in L^{2}([0,T];V^{\ast})
. Then
u\in C([0,T];H)
, the map
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto|u_{t}|_{H}\in\mathbb{R}
is absolutely continuous and

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}|u_{t}|^{2}_{H}=2\left\langle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}u_{t},u_{t}\right\rangle,\quad\text{for a.a. }t\in[0,T].

Sketch of Proof.

The proof follows by first convolving
u
with a standard mollifier in
t
, to give
u^{\varepsilon}:=u\ast_{t}\eta_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}([0,T];V)
and
|u^{\varepsilon}|_{H}\in C^{\infty}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
. Since the weak derivative agrees with the strong derivative when the latter is well defined, the results of Lemma
2.2.8
obtain directly for
u^{\varepsilon}
. Then using the ordering
V\subset H\subset V^{\ast}
and assumptions on
u
, the results also hold in the limit. For a detailed exposition in the case
H=L^{2}
,
V=H^{1}_{0}
and
V^{\ast}=H^{-1}
see
[
Eva10
, Thm. 3; Sec. 5.9.2]

∎

Theorem 2.2.9
.

Let
T>0
,
u_{0}\in H
and
B\in L^{2}([0,T];V^{\ast})
and
A\in\mathscr{L}(V;V^{\ast})
satisfy (
H1
) and be continuously defined from
V\rightarrow V^{\ast}
. Then there exists a unique weak solution
u\in L^{2}([0,T];V)\cap C([0,T];H)
to (
2.2.1
).

Proof.

We prove uniqueness of solutions first, using the coercivity assumption.

Uniqueness:

Let
u,\,w
be two weak solutions to (
2.2.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.2.4
. Then by linearity, for any
t\in(0,T]
and
v\in D(A)
, we have

\left\langle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(u_{t}-w_{t}),v\right\rangle=\langle A(u_{t}-w_{t}),v\rangle

Applying Lemma
2.2.8
and the coercivity assumption, (
H1
), we see that

|u_{t}-w_{t}|_{H}^{2}=2\int_{0}^{t}\langle A(u_{s}-w_{s}),u_{s}-w_{s}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq\lambda\int_{0}^{t}|u_{s}-w_{s}|^{2}_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Then applying Grönwall shows that
|u_{t}-w_{t}|_{H}=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, from which it follows that
u=w\in L^{2}([0,T];V)
.

Existence:

We obtain existence by a generalisation of the argument described in Subsection
1.1.2
. Let
\{e_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\subset V
be an orthonormal basis of
H
. For
n\geq 1
we define,

V_{n}:=\text{span}\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

For any
n\geq 1
, we consider the finite dimensional system of linear, real, ODEs,

\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\langle u_{n;t},e_{k}\rangle&=\langle Au_{n;t},e_{k}\rangle+\langle B_{t},e_{k}\rangle,\\
\langle u_{n;0},e_{k}\rangle&=\langle u_{0},e_{k}\rangle,\end{aligned}\quad\text{for }k=1,\ldots,n.

(2.2.3)

For any
n\geq 1
, there exists a unique, classical solution to the system of ODEs, (
2.2.3
). Furthermore, setting

[0,T]\ni t\mapsto u_{n;t}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle u_{n;t},e_{k}\rangle e_{k},

defines a weak solution to (
2.2.1
) with initial data
u_{n;0}
. We now derive a strong enough
a priori
bound on the family
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
to allow us pass to the limit. Since
u_{n;0}\rightarrow u_{0}
and the right hand side of (
2.2.1
) is linear this limit will define a weak solution to (
2.2.1
).

Note that by definition we have

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\langle u_{n;t},e_{n}\rangle=\left\langle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}u_{n;t},e_{n}\right\rangle,

where the first derivative is understood as a usual finite dimensional derivative and the second in the sense of Definition
2.2.3
. Therefore, applying Lemma
2.2.8
, we observe the identity,

|u_{n;t}|_{H}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle u_{0},e_{k}\rangle^{2}+2\int_{0}^{t}\langle Au_{n;s}+B_{s},u_{n;s}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

So by the coercivity assumption, (
H1
), the bound
\langle B,v\rangle\leq\|B\|_{V^{\ast}}\|v\|_{V}
and Young’s product inequality we see that for some
\lambda,\,\alpha>0
,

|u_{n;t}|_{H}^{2}+\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\leq\,|u_{n;0}|_{H}^{2}+\frac{2}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{T}\|B_{s}\|^{2}_{V^{\ast}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}|u_{n;s}|_{H}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Absorbing the third term on the RHS into the LHS, applying Grönwall and taking suprema on both sides, gives the bound

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|u_{n;t}|_{H}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\leq\,\left(|u_{n;0}|_{H}^{2}+\frac{2}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{T}\|B_{s}\|^{2}_{V^{\ast}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)e^{\lambda T}.

We note that
|u_{n;0}|_{H}\leq|u_{0}|^{2}_{H}
and so the right hand side is bounded uniformly in
n\geq 1
. Thus we also have,

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|u_{n;t}|^{2}_{H}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\leq\left(|u_{0}|^{2}_{H}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|B_{s}\|^{2}_{V^{\ast}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)e^{\lambda T}<\infty.

(2.2.4)

This shows that the set
(u_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
lies in a closed, bounded subset of
C([0,T];H)\cap L^{2}([0,T];V)
. Since
V
was assumed to be reflexive, from Theorem
2.2.1
, there exists a weakly convergent subsequence (which we do not relabel) such that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\in L^{2}([0,T];V)
. Since
A:V\rightarrow V^{\ast}
is strongly continuous, it is also continuous with respect to the weak topology on
V
and so we have
Au_{n}\rightharpoonup Au\in L^{2}([0,T];V^{\ast})
. Thus, we may pass to the limit in (
2.2.3
) to see that, for any
v\in D(A)\subset V
,

\left\langle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}u_{t},v\right\rangle=\langle Au_{t},v\rangle+\langle B_{t},v\rangle.

Furthermore, by definition
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}u_{t}=Au_{t}+B_{t}\in V^{\ast}
means that
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}u\in L^{2}([0,T];V^{\ast})
and so we may apply the first part of the proof to establish that
u
defined as the weak limit of (a subsequence of) the
u_{n}
is the unique weak solution to (
2.2.1
). Furthermore, it follows from Lemma
2.2.8
that
u\in C([0,T];H)
.
∎

Example 2.2.10
.

To a linear, self-adjoint, coercive operator

as above, one can associate a natural scale of

based function spaces that resemble the Sobolev spaces. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, one defines
H_{A}^{k}:=D(A^{k/2}):=\{u\in L^{2}\,:\,A^{k/2}u\in L^{2}\}
. Here

is the operator such that for any

,
\langle Au,v\rangle=\langle A^{1/2}u,A^{1/2}v\rangle

(H_{A}^{1},L^{2},H^{-1}_{A})
where

. Note that when

this scale exactly defines the Hilbertian Sobolev spaces

and their topological duals. In the following section where we consider non-linear
A(u)
, it will usually be the case that

with
A_{1}
satisfying the above assumptions and
A_{2}
non-linear and of lower order. In that case one defines the natural scale using
A_{1}
.

We now turn to the question of non-linear equations, that is we no longer assume
A:V\rightarrow V^{\ast}
to be a linear operator. As remarked above, we typically assume
A=A_{1}u+A_{2}u
where
A_{1}
is linear and coercive and

is non-linear and often of lower order. The main issue in the above argument is that even for strongly continuous non-linear operators

in the form of monotonicity and regularity, or additional assumptions on the spaces in question,

Proof.

Where the sum converges in probability along any decreasing sequence of partitions to a real valued process. By polarisation we have that

\displaystyle F(X_{t})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\Tr\left(\varphi^{\perp}_{s}\nabla^{2}F(X_{s})\varphi_{s}^{\perp}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Sketch of Proof.

We only give a sketched proof, details can be found as

a polynomial. First, we let

be two semi-martingales and show that

X_{t}Y_{t}=X_{0}Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[X,Y]_{s}.

(3.1.10)

Note that this is the usual integration by parts formula for finite variation process with the addition of the integral against
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[X,Y]_{s}
- which is an artefact of the Itô integral. To show (

), let

be fixed and

be a partition of

. Then,

-a.s. we have,

\displaystyle X_{t}Y_{t}-X_{0}Y_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{t_{i+1}}Y_{t_{i+1}}-X_{t_{i}}Y_{t_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{t_{i}}(Y_{t_{i+1}}-Y_{t_{i}})+Y_{t_{i}}(X_{t_{i+1}}-X_{t_{i}})+(X_{t_{i+1}}-X_{t_{i}})(Y_{t_{i+1}}-Y_{t_{i}})

\displaystyle\overset{N\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[X,Y]_{s}.

If
X,\,Y
were finite variation processes the last term can only have zero as a limit and we recover the usual integration by parts formula. With (
3.1.10
) in hand, let us assume that

X_{t}^{n}=X^{n}_{0}+n\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{s}X_{t}^{n-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+n\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{s}X_{s}^{n-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{s}^{2}X_{s}^{n-2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

It follows therefore, using that

, that,

\displaystyle X_{t}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=X_{0}^{n+1}+(n+1)\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{s}X_{s}^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+(n+1)\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{s}X_{s}^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(n+1)n}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{s}^{2}X_{s}^{n-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Since (
3.1.9
) holds by definition for

we conclude by induction that (

) holds for any polynomial and so by Stone–Weierstrass for any

∎

One can see from the proof of Theorem

\tau_{R}:=\inf\left\{t\in(0,T]\,:\,\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\geq R\right\},

and consider the stopped process,

\mathbb{E}[|u_{n;t\wedge\tau_{R}}|_{H}^{2}]\leq|u_{0}|^{2}_{H}+2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\langle A(u_{n;s}),u_{n;s}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}|B(u_{n;s})|^{2}_{L_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right].

So applying (
H

2
′

) we obtain,

\mathbb{E}\left[|u_{n;t\wedge\tau_{R}}|_{H}^{2}+\alpha\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right]\leq|u_{0}|^{2}_{H}+\nu\mathbb{E}[t\wedge\tau_{R}].

Thus, since the right hand side is independent of

and
\tau_{R}\rightarrow\infty

\mathbb{P}

\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[|u_{n;t}|_{H}^{2}+\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{n;s}\|^{2}_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right]<\infty.

(3.2.7)

This is almost the a priori bound we want, we only need to exchange the supremum over

with the expectation. Since the supremum is a convex function and the expectation is really an integral, exchanging their order in (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\langle u_{n;s},B(u_{n;s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}\rangle\right|_{H}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\lambda_{k;t}}\langle u_{n;t},e_{k}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\langle u_{n;t},e_{k}\rangle|\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\lambda_{k;s}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|u_{n;t}|^{2}_{H}\right]+C^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|B(u_{n;t})\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right].

The final inequality here follows from Hölder and Young’s product inequality. If we take a supremum over

, before taking the expectation in (

), we therefore obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|u_{n;t}|_{H}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq|u_{0}|^{2}_{H}+2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|\langle A(u_{n;s}),u_{n;s}\rangle|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right]+(1+2C^{2})\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|B(u_{n;s})\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|u_{n;t}|^{2}_{H}\right].

From which it follows, using (
H

1
′

), Remark
3.2.6
and linearity of the expectation, that we have

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|u_{n;t}|_{H}^{2}\right]\leq|u_{0}|^{2}_{H}+\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}(1+\tilde{C})\|u_{n;t}\|^{2}_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right],

(3.2.8)

for a new constant
\tilde{C}>0
that incorporates the constant
\nu>0
in (

1
′

). It follows then from (

) that the right hand side of (

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|u_{n;t}|^{2}_{H}+\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{n;t}\|^{2}_{V}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]<\infty.

(3.2.9)

From (

), (

) and Remark

it follows that:

1.

3.

Thus, there exist subsequences, which we do not relabel converging weakly in the following senses,

\displaystyle u_{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup u\quad\text{ in }L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T];V),

\displaystyle A(u_{n})

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\xi\quad\text{ in }L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T];V^{\ast})

\displaystyle B(u_{n})

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\eta\quad\text{ in }L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T];L_{2}).

Furthermore, we have that

in

. So just as in the proof of Theorem

it is only left to show that

and
\eta=B(u)
. The argument in this case is very similar.

From (

′

), for all

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}2\langle A(u_{n;t})-A(v_{t}),u_{n;t}-v_{t}\rangle+\|B(u_{n;t})-B(v_{t})\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq 0.

We then claim that we have the inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}

To see this, we recall from finite dimensional Itô formula, that we have

\mathbb{E}\left[|u_{n;T}|_{H}^{2}-|u_{n;0}|^{2}_{H}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\langle A(u_{n;t}),u_{t}\rangle+\|B(u_{n;t})\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right],

and passing to the limit,

\mathbb{E}\left[|u_{T}|_{H}^{2}-|u_{0}|^{2}_{H}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\langle\zeta_{t},u_{t}\rangle+\|\eta_{t}\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right].

By essentially the same reasoning we have that

in

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}2\langle\zeta_{t}-A(v_{t}),u_{t}-v_{t}\rangle+\|\eta_{t}-B(v_{t})\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq 0.

If we set

in (

\eta=B(u)
. To show that
\zeta=A(u)
, let
v=u-\theta w
for

and

. Diving both sides by

we see that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\langle\zeta_{t}-A(u_{t}-\theta w_{t}),w_{t}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq 0.

Letting
\theta\rightarrow 0
and appealing to (

) we deduce that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\langle\zeta_{t}-A(u_{t}),w_{t}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq 0,\quad\forall\,w\in L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T];V),

from which it follows that
\zeta=A(u)
in
L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T];V^{\ast})
.

So it follows that
u\in L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T];V)\cap L^{2}(\Omega;L^{\infty}([0,T];H))
is the unique solution to (
3.0.1
) in the sense of Definition
3.2.9
. Finally, we prove that in fact,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
u\in C([0,T];H)
. To see this we recall that since
H
is a Hilbert space, if
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\in H
, and
|u_{n}|_{H}\rightarrow|u|\in\mathbb{R}
one has

|u_{n}-u|^{2}_{H}=\langle u-u_{n},u-u_{n}\rangle=|u|^{2}_{H}-2\langle u,u_{n}\rangle+|u_{n}|^{2}\rightarrow 0.

Therefore, since the ad hoc Itô formula, (
3.2
) show that for a sequence
t_{k}\rightarrow t
, it holds that
|u_{t_{k}}|_{H}\rightarrow|u_{t}|_{H}
, it suffices to show that
u_{t_{k}}\rightharpoonup u_{t}\in H
. Let
h\in H
be arbitrary and
(h_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset V
be a sequence converging to

strongly in
H
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
n_{\varepsilon}\geq 1
be large enough such that,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|u_{t}|_{H}||h-h_{n}|_{H}\,\leq\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\quad\text{for all }n\geq n_{\varepsilon}.

So it follows that

\displaystyle|\langle u_{t},h\rangle-\langle u_{t_{k}},h\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq|\langle u_{t},h-h_{n_{\varepsilon}}\rangle|+|\langle u_{t}-u_{t_{k}},h_{n_{\varepsilon}}\rangle|+|\langle u_{t_{k}},h-h_{n_{\varepsilon}}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\|u_{t}-u_{t_{k}}\|_{V^{\ast}}\|h_{n_{\varepsilon}}\|_{V}.

By definition of the weak form of the equation, we have that
u_{t_{k}}\rightarrow u_{t}
strongly in
V^{\ast}
and so we have that,

\limsup_{n\geq n_{\varepsilon}}|\langle u_{t},h\rangle-\langle u_{t_{k}},h\rangle|\,\leq\,\varepsilon.

Hence, by the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon>0
the necessary convergence holds and we conclude the proof.

Example 3.3.1

(A Transport Noise SPDE)
.

Let us fix
\Gamma=\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
H=L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
V=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\,V^{\ast}=\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. By the Poincaré inequality,
[
Eva10
, Subsec 5.8.1]
, we have the bound,

\|u-u_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}},\quad u_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}:=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{T}^{d}|}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.3.1)

Therefore we may in principle describe the space
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to be the set of all functions
u:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with square integrable gradient. We see below that this suffices for our application.

Then, assume we have a sequence of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
basis functions,
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such that
\sup_{k\geq 1}\|e_{k}\|_{L^{2}}=1
. Furthermore, we assume that we have a sequence of non-negative real numbers
(\sigma_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, then we consider the SPDE,

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{t}=\Delta u_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}e_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{t}^{k},&\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\end{cases}

(3.3.2)

where
(\beta^{k})_{k\geq 1}
a family of i.i.d, real, Brownian motions. So under the assumption that
\sum_{k\geq 1}\sigma_{k}=\|\sigma\|_{\ell^{1}}<\infty
, writing
B(u_{t}):=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}e_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{t}
we have,

\|B(u_{t})\|^{2}_{L_{2}}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sigma_{k}\|e_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\|\nabla u_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\|\sigma\|_{\ell^{1}}\|\nabla u_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Note that the quantity
u_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
is (at least formally) preserved by (
3.3.2
), therefore for simplicity let us set
u_{0}
to have zero mean. So we may work in the space of mean free functions and we truly have
\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}
.

Since (
3.3.2
) is linear, by analogy with the arguments of Chapter
2
, we only need to establish coercivity in the sense of
H

1
′

. With
A=\Delta
, for any
\alpha>0
, we directly have

2\langle\Delta u,u\rangle_{L^{2}}+\|B(u)\|^{2}_{L_{2}}+\alpha\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}}=(\|\sigma\|_{\ell^{1}}\|-2+\alpha)\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

So in order to satisfy (
H

1
′

) we require the bound
\|\sigma\|_{\ell^{1}}<2
. This mirrors the result shown in Subsection
1.1.2
but now in arbitrary dimensions and with infinite dimensional noise. As in that case, if we also have
\div e_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 1
then the martingale term arising from Itô’s formula for
\|u_{t}\|_{L^{2}}
, formally becomes,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\sigma_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(e_{k}(x)\cdot\nabla u_{s}(x))u_{s}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{s}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e_{k}(x)\cdot\nabla(u^{2}_{s}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{s}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\cdot(e_{k}(x)u^{2}_{s}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{s}^{k}

\displaystyle=0.

So with some care one can show that in this case, the martingale term in fact disappears
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., not only in expectation.

Before presenting the next two examples, we recall the Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities on bounded domains, we refer in general to
[
Eva10
, Sec. 5.6 & Subsec. 5.8.1]
for this material. Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a closed, bounded, domain with smooth boundary and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, let
W^{k,p}(\Gamma)
denote the space of maps
u:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
whose first
k
distributional derivatives are bounded in
L^{p}(\Gamma)
. We retain the special notation
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma):=W^{1,2}(\Gamma)
. We equip these spaces with the norms,

\|u\|_{W^{k,p}}:=\sum_{n\leq k}\|D^{n}u\|_{L^{p}}.

Let
l<k
,
q\in(p,\infty]
be such that
\frac{1}{p}-\frac{k}{d}=\frac{1}{q}-\frac{l}{d}
. Then one has the bound,

\|u\|_{W^{l,q}}\leq\|u\|_{W^{k,p}}.

That is lower derivatives, in higher integrabillity are controlled by higher derivatives in lower integrabillity. As a particular case, for
d\geq 2
and
p>d
, one always has,

\|u\|_{L^{p^{*}}}\leq\|u\|_{W^{1,p}},\quad\text{ for }p^{*}:=\frac{pd}{d-p}

In the edge case
p=d=2
one in fact also has
\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u\|_{W^{1,2}}
and in fact this embedding is compact. Furthermore, since
\Gamma
is compact, we also have
\|u\|_{L^{m}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{p}}
for
m\leq p
and so in the above setting we have

\|u\|_{L^{m}}\leq\|u\|_{W^{1,p}}\quad\text{ for all }m\leq p^{*}.

From which it follows that
W^{k,p}(\Gamma)\cap L^{m}(\Gamma)=W^{k,p}(\Gamma)
for all
m\leq\frac{pd}{d-p}
.

The Poincaré inequality allows us to control the
L^{p}
norms of a function by
L^{p}
norms on its gradient. These inequalities are related to the spectral properties of the heat semi-group, see
[
BGL14
]
. We recall that for any
p\in[1,\infty]
there is a map, known as the trace map,

\displaystyle T:W^{1,p}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\rightarrow L^{p}(\partial\Gamma)

\displaystyle u\mapsto u|_{\partial\Gamma}.

For all
k\geq 1
, we define
W^{k,p}_{0}(\Gamma)=W^{k,p}(\Gamma)\cap\text{Ker}(T)
. Then, the classical Poincaré inequality says that for all
p\in[1,\infty]
there exists a
C:=C(\Gamma,p)>0
such that for all
u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Gamma)
,

\|u\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}}.

Thus for example we have
\|u\|_{W^{1,p}}\leq(1+C)\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}}
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
. By considering
u=D^{k-1}v
we also have
\|u\|_{W^{k-1,p}}\leq(1+kC)\|D^{k}u\|_{L^{p}}
. Of particular relevance for us will be the bound
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}:=\|u\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}\leq(1+C)\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}=:\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}}
. We furthermore equip
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{1}(\Gamma)
with the inner product,

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}}:=\int_{\Gamma}\nabla u(x)\nabla v(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

The first term is always non-positive and for the second, we note that if

\mathcal{H}^{-1}

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{-1}}=-\langle\Delta^{-1/2}u,\Delta^{-1/2}v\rangle_{L^{2}},

(3.3.4)

where
(-\Delta)^{-1}:\mathcal{H}^{-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}
is the inverse isomorphism. Note that when we identify
\mathcal{H}^{-1}\cong\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}}

(L^{m})^{\ast}\cong L^{\frac{m}{m-1}}

Before establishing (

A(u)

-\Delta

\Delta:L^{\frac{m}{m-1}}\rightarrow(L^{m})^{\ast}

|u|^{m-2}u\in L^{m}

it follows that

Pathwise Approach to SPDE

In this chapter we present a brief introduction to the pathwise approach to SPDE. To motivate this consideration, recall Examples
3.3.2
and
3.3.3
from the previous Chapter. For these equations, where the noise enters additively, we had to directly consider a

One approach is to use the mild formulation, provided the operator

[

We do not discuss these more singular cases here, but instead present, by way of an example, some of the ideas behind these approaches, which had already been applied by Giuseppe Da-Prato, Jerzy Zabzyck and collaborators to SPDE problems motivated by quantum field theory, fluid dynamics and interface models,

4.1
Regularity of Stochastic Processes

As in the case of finite dimensional stochastic processes, the Kolmogorov continuity criterion is a useful tool for establishing
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. regularity statements for infinite dimensional stochastic processes. This result actually holds in the far more general context of complete metric spaces.

Definition 4.1.1
.

Given a metric space
(E,\rho)
,
T>0
and
\kappa\in(0,1)
, we say that a map
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto X_{t}\in E
is
\kappa
-Hölder continuous if,

\|X\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}E}:=\sup_{s\neq t\in[0,T]}\frac{\rho(X_{t},X_{s})}{|t-s|^{\kappa}}<\infty.

If
\kappa>1
we say that
X\in\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}E
if and only if
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto X_{t}\in E
is
\lfloor\kappa\rfloor
times continuous differentiable the
\lfloor\kappa\rfloor
derivative is
\kappa-\lfloor\kappa\rfloor
-Hölder continuous in the above sense.

Theorem 4.1.2

(Kolmogorov Criterion)
.

Assume that
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a stochastic process taking values in a complete metric space
(E,\rho)
and that there exist constants
C>0
,
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>1
such that for all
s,\,t\in[0,T]
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(X_{t},X_{s})^{\delta}\right]\leq C|t-s|^{1+\varepsilon}.

Then there exists a version of
X
(which we do not relabel) such that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
X\in\mathcal{C}^{\kappa}E
, for all
\kappa<\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}
.

Proof.

The proof is a natural generalisation of the usual argument for finite dimensional stochastic processes. See
[
DPZ14
, Thm. 3.3]
for details.
∎

A useful property of Gaussian measures is the following corollary of Theorem
1.4.11
. In short it says that all higher moments of a Gaussian random variable are controlled by its first moment. This is related to a much more powerful result in the study of Gaussian measures, known as Nelson’s hypercontractivity estimate, from which it follows that all moments of iterated integrals of a Gaussian random variable can be controlled by the second moment of the same iterated integral. This fact is related to the spectral gap of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semi-group, for an overview see
[
Nua10
, Ch. 1]
. We state the result only for Gaussian random variables and in the context of separable Hilbert spaces even thought it holds almost without modification in separable Banach spaces, see
[
Hai09
, Prop. 3.14]
.

Proposition 4.1.3
.

Let
H
be a separable Hilbert space,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(H)
be a Gaussian measure and let
M:=\int_{H}\|h\|_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)
. Then there exist constants
\alpha,\,C>0
such that for any,
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
with the property that
f(x)\lesssim e^{\alpha x^{2}}
, one has

\int_{H}f\left(\frac{\|h\|_{H}}{M}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)\lesssim_{f}C.

(4.1.1)

In particular, for any
n\geq 1
, it holds that

\int_{H}\|h\|_{H}^{2n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)\leq n!C\alpha^{-n}M^{2n}.

(4.1.2)

Proof.

We recall from the proof of Fernique’s theorem, Theorem
1.4.11
, that we obtained the bound,

\displaystyle\int_{H}\exp\left(\frac{\tilde{\alpha}\|h\|^{2}_{H}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(h)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\tilde{\alpha}}+2\tilde{\alpha}\int_{1}^{\infty}te^{-\tilde{\alpha}t^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

where, for any
\sigma>0
,
\tilde{\alpha}>0
is such that
\mu(\|h\|_{H}>x)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2\,\tilde{\alpha}\,x^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
for all
x>\sigma
. Therefore, (
4.1.1
) follows after setting
\sigma=4M
, for example, and using Chebyshev’s inequality. The second bound, (
4.1.2
) follows from Taylor’s theorem which gives the bound
\frac{\alpha^{n}x^{2n}}{n!}\leq e^{\alpha x^{2}}
, for any
\alpha>0
and
n\geq 1
.
∎

Corollary 4.1.4
.

It follows that for an
H
-valued, Gaussian random variable
X
, and any
p>1
, there exists a constant,
C_{p}>0
such that,
\mathbb{E}[\|X\|^{p}_{H}]\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}[\|X\|^{2}_{H}]
.

Proof.

From Proposition
4.1.3
and Hölder’s inequality it follows that for any
p>1
there exists a possibly different
C_{p}>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[\|X\|_{H}^{p}]\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}[\|X\|_{H}]
. Therefore, controlling the first moment by its second gives the result.
∎

To see how the Kolmogorov theorem,
4.1.2
applies along with Corollary
4.1.4
, let us consider an example of a Gaussian process solving a linear SPDE.

Example 4.1.5
.

Let
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
denote a cylindrical Gaussian process, see Subsection
1.4.2
and
(v_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
denote the solution to the linear, scalar, equation,

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{t}=\Delta v_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},&\text{ on }[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
v|_{t=0}=0,&\text{ on }\mathbb{T}^{d}.\end{cases}

(4.1.3)

We can try to apply the method of Chapter
3
, however, since we already saw that the white-noise
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. does not take values in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
this approach becomes tricky. We recall the representation of the cylindrical Wiener process, for some basis
(e_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
W_{t}=\sum_{k\geq 1}e_{k}\beta^{k}_{t}
, we may decompose (
4.1.3
) into the finite dimensional system of real, scalar, SDEs,

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{k;t}=-|k|^{2}v_{k;t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{k}_{t},\quad v_{k;0}=0,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

This defines, for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, a real, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, which we can solve explicitly,

v_{k;t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-|k|^{2}(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{k}_{s}.

We would like to define the solution to (
4.1.3
) as

v_{t}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}v_{k;t}e_{k}.

However, this raises the question of convergence of the sum and in tun, what space should we expect
t\mapsto v_{t}
to live in. We can answer both questions using the Kolmogorov test, Theorem
4.1.2
. Recall the scale of Sobolev spaces, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

H^{\alpha}:=H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):=\left\{\varphi\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\,:\,\|\varphi\|^{2}_{H^{\alpha}}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|k|^{2})^{\alpha}|\langle\varphi,e_{k}\rangle|^{2}<\infty\right\}.

We observe that for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
t\mapsto v_{k;t}
is a Gaussian process and so from Corollary
4.1.4
, for any
p\geq 1
, there exists a
C_{p}>0
such that, for all
s,\,t\in[0,T]
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|v_{k;t}-v_{k;s}|^{2p}\right]\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[|v_{k;t}-v_{k;s}|^{2}\right]^{p}.

We will use this bound to estimate the
L^{2p}(\Omega;H^{\alpha})
norm of
v_{t}-v_{s}
. Strictly speaking we should first truncate the sum, at some
N>0
, only considering frequencies with
|k|\leq N
, then obtain uniform bounds in
N
and take the limit. However, since the calculations are essentially the same we work directly with the formal limit
v
and leave the precise limiting argument as a technical step. For
p\geq 1
we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|v_{t}-v_{s}\|^{2p}_{H^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{p}(1+|k_{i}|^{2})^{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{p}|v_{k_{i};t}-v_{k_{i};s}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{p}(1+|k_{i}|^{2})^{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[|v_{k_{i};t}-v_{k_{i};s}|^{2p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{p}(1+|k_{i}|^{2})^{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[|v_{k_{i};t}-v_{k_{i};s}|^{2}\right].

So it suffices to obtain good bounds on
\mathbb{E}\left[|v_{k_{i};t}-v_{k_{i};s}|^{2}\right]
. Note that for,
0\leq s<t\leq T
and any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have that

v_{k;t}-v_{k;s}=(e^{-|k|^{2}(t-s)}-1)\int_{0}^{s}e^{-|k|^{2}(s-r)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{k}_{r}+\int_{s}^{t}e^{-|k|^{2}(t-r)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{k}_{r}.

So by the usual finite dimensional, Itô isometry, and the independent increments property of the Brownian motion, for each
i=1,\ldots,p
we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|v_{k_{i};t}-v_{k_{i};s}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2|k_{i}|^{2}}\left(e^{-|k_{i}|^{2}(t-s)}-1\right)^{2}\left(1-e^{-2|k_{i}|^{2}s}\right)+\frac{1}{2|k_{i}|^{2}}\left(1-e^{-2|k_{i}|^{2}(t-s)}\right).

We can bound this expression using Taylor, so that for any
\kappa\in[0,1]
, we have,

\mathbb{E}[|v_{k_{i};t}-v_{k_{i};s}|^{2}]\leq C_{\kappa}|k_{i}|^{2(\kappa-1)}|t-s|^{\kappa}.

Returning to our bound on the
L^{2p}(\Omega;H^{\alpha})
norm of
v_{t}-v_{s}
, we now have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|v_{t}-v_{s}\|^{2p}_{H^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{p,\kappa}(t-s)^{\kappa p}\prod_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{p}(1+|k_{i}|^{2})^{\alpha}|k_{i}|^{2(\kappa-1)}

\displaystyle=C_{p,\kappa}(t-s)^{\kappa p}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left((1+|k|^{2})^{\alpha}|k|^{2(\kappa-1)}\right)^{p}.

So provided
\alpha<-\frac{d}{2}+1-\kappa
, the sum on the right hand side converges to a finite constant. Applying Theorem
4.1.2
we establish the existence of a modification, which we do not relabel, such that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
v\in\mathcal{C}^{\kappa_{p}}H^{\alpha}
for all
\kappa_{p}<=\frac{\kappa}{2}-\frac{1}{2p}
. Taking
p>1
arbitrarily large we obtain that
v\in\mathcal{C}^{\kappa^{\prime}}H^{\alpha-2\kappa^{\prime}}
for all
\alpha<-\frac{d}{2}+1
and
\kappa^{\prime}\in[0,1)
.

This approach can be directly adapted to incorporate non-zero initial data, one only need include a finite variation term in the SDEs and analogous estimates hold, provided
v|_{t=0}=v_{0}\in H^{\alpha+2\kappa}
. As with the deterministic heat equation, the method can be further extended for less regular initial data, at the expense of measuring the solution in weighted Hölder spaces with prescribed blow-up as
t\searrow 0
.

Notice that only for
d=1
is
v_{t}
a well defined spatial function. For all
d\geq 2
,
v_{t}
, only defines a distribution on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. In the next section we will see that this causes an issue when considering for example, non-linear equations driven by additive space-time white noise in
d\geq 2
.

4.2
Pathwise Approach to SPDE: Stochastic Burger’s Equation

The above analysis gives us a way into a different perspective on SPDE, that being the pathwise approach. Instead of viewing solutions to SPDE as infinite dimensional stochastic processes and applying the tools of stochastic calculus, in the pathwise approach, one views solutions to SPDE as solutions to PDE driven by random coefficients. The idea is to build suitable input objects, typically coming from the noise source, a linearised equation or coupled system, and then solve a non-linear PDE involving these inputs for
\mathbb{P}
-a.a
\omega\in\Omega
independently. We give an example of this method applied to the stochastic Burger’s equation in one spatial dimension,

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{t}=(\partial_{xx}u_{t}+\partial_{x}(u_{t}^{2}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},&\text{ on }[0,T]\times\mathbb{T},\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0},&\text{ on }\mathbb{T}^{,}\end{cases}

(4.2.1)

where
(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a space-time white noise and
u_{0}
is a specified initial data. This problem was studied in
[
DDT94
]
on the unit interval
(0,1)
with Dirichlet boundary data. Although we work on the torus, mostly for presentational ease, our presentation is very similar to that of
[
DDT94
]
.

We note that (
4.2.1
) does not fall into the class of monotone equations - it can easily be checked that the non-linear transport term,
A(u):=\partial_{x}(u)^{2}=2u\partial_{x}u
, does not satisfy
\langle Au_{1}-Au_{2},u_{1}-u_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}}
for any proportionality constant. However, being a one dimensional analogue of the Navier–Stokes equations, it can be approached using compactness methods,
[
MV88
]
and this approach has been used to study more singular version of the equation,
[
GP20
]
.

An alternative approach, however, is to treat (
4.2.1
) as a PDE with random forcing. An advantage of this approach is that in principle, the tools of PDE analysis, that have been well developed for deterministic counterparts of such equations can be brought to bear.

We recall that in the previous section, we obtain the solution to (
4.1.3
) as a stochastic process
(v_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, with
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. continuous trajectories taking values in
H^{\alpha}
for all
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
. We do not expect the non-linear term to increase the regularity of
u
over
v
and so we see that we should look for solutions
t\mapsto u_{t}\in H^{1/2-}:=\cap_{\alpha<1/2}H^{\alpha}
. This we can do almost directly by the Duhamel principle, and obtaining a fixed point locally in time. However, anticipating that in order to obtain global well-posedness we aim to obtain
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. a priori bounds on the quantities
\|u\|_{C_{T}L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}
, we realise that we will need more than
1/2-
spatial regularity. Therefore we do not work with
u
directly but instead subtract the solution to the linear equation, (
4.1.3
) in order to leave behind a more regular remainder.

Observe that if
u
solves (
4.2.1
) and we define
w:=u-v
, where
v
solves (
4.1.3
), then
w
solves,

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{t}=(\partial_{xx}w+\partial_{x}(w_{t}+v_{t})^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,&\text{ on }[0,T]\times\mathbb{T},\\
w|_{t=0}=w_{0},&\text{ on }\mathbb{T}.\end{cases}

(4.2.2)

Since any solution to (
4.2.2
) also defines a solution to (
4.2.1
) by the formula,
u=w+v
, we now attempt to find solutions
w
in a suitable Sobolev space. Our hope is that having subtracted the least regular part from
u
, our solution to (
4.2.2
) will live in a Sobolev space of regularity index above one.

It will also be useful to extend the scale of Sobolev spaces introduced above to the scale of Bessel potential spaces. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\in(1,\infty)
we define,

W^{\alpha,p}:=W^{\alpha,p}(\mathbb{T}):=\left\{\varphi\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})\,:\,\|\varphi\|^{2}_{W^{\alpha,p}}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+|k|^{2})^{\frac{\alpha p}{2}}|\langle\varphi,e_{k}\rangle|^{p}<\infty\right\}.

(4.2.3)

We make some remarks regarding the space
W^{\alpha,p}
.

•

The definition extends naturally for
d>1
.

•

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
these spaces agree with the usual Sobolev spaces.

•

The analogous definition when
p=\infty
defines the scales of Hölder spaces on
\mathbb{T}
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
, and when
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and
p=\infty
these spaces agree with the usual
W^{k,\infty}
spaces.

•

For
\alpha=0
and
p\in(1,\infty)
, we have
W^{0,p}(\mathbb{T})=L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
. When
\alpha\neq 0
and
p=2
we retain the notation
H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
.

•

The derivative
\partial_{x}
is a bounded linear map from
W^{\alpha,p}(\mathbb{T})\rightarrow W^{\alpha-1,p}(\mathbb{T})
. This is easily seen through Fourier multipliers.

•

For
p>q\in(1,\infty)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, one has the Sobolev type inequality,

\|\varphi\|_{W^{\alpha,p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C_{\alpha,p,q}\|\varphi\|_{W^{\beta,q}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},\quad\beta=\alpha+d\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}\right).

(4.2.4)

•

The heat semi-group,
e^{t\Delta}
, has the following regularising effect, for all
q>p\in(1,\infty)
and
\beta-2<\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

\|e^{t\Delta}\varphi\|_{W^{\beta,q}}\leq C_{\alpha,\beta,p,q}t^{-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}-d\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\|\varphi\|_{W^{\alpha,p}}.

(4.2.5)

For more details on these spaces and the listed properties see for example
[
BCD11
, Ch. 2]
.

Fix
T>0
, a
\mathbb{P}
-null set
\mathscr{N}\subset\Omega
and an
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\mathscr{N}
, such that
v:=v(\omega)\in\mathcal{C}_{T}^{\kappa^{\prime}}H^{\alpha-2\kappa^{\prime}}
for some
\kappa^{\prime}\in[0,1)
and
\alpha<1/2
. We drop the explicit dependence on
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\mathscr{N}
from now on. By the Sobolev embedding, (
4.2.4
), for any
\alpha\in[0,1/2)
, we have that

\|v\|_{C_{T}L^{p}}\leq C_{p}\|v\|_{C_{T}H^{\alpha}},\quad p:=\frac{2}{1-2\alpha},

Note that we can chose
\alpha\in[0,1/2)
so as to achieve any
p\in[0,\infty)
. By Duhamel’s principle, a solution to (
4.2.2
) will be given by a fixed point of the map,

\Psi w_{t}:=e^{t\Delta}w_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\partial_{x}(w_{s}+v_{s})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.2.6)

We introduce the notation,
\lesssim
, to indicate that an inequality holds up to an unimportant constant. If we wish to specify some parameters on which this constant does depend, we write for example,
\lesssim_{\beta,p}
.

Theorem 4.2.1
.

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
and
w_{0}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
. Then there exists a
T_{\ast}\in(0,T]
such that a unique, mild solution,
w\in C_{T_{*}}L^{p}
, exists for (
4.2.2
). Furthermore, if
p>4
, there exists a
\beta>1
such that for any
t\in(0,T_{*}]
,
\|w_{t}\|_{H^{\beta}}<\infty
.

Proof.

The argument for existence and uniqueness is fairly standard and so we only sketch it. For more details in this particular case see
[
DDT94
, Lem. 2.1]
.

First we define the unit ball, for any
T_{**}\in(0,T]
, we set

\mathfrak{B}_{T_{**}}:=\left\{w\in C([0,T_{**}];L^{q}(\mathbb{T}))\,:\,\|w\|_{C_{T_{**}}L^{p}}\leq 1\right\}.

Then, with
\Psi
defined as in (
4.2.6
), we have

\displaystyle\|\Psi w_{t}\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\|e^{t\Delta}w_{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\partial_{x}(w_{s}+v_{s})^{2}\|_{L^{p}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\partial_{x}(w_{s}+v_{s})^{2}\|_{W^{\frac{1}{p},\frac{p}{2}}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2p}-\frac{1}{2}}\|\partial_{x}(w_{s}+v_{s})^{2}\|_{W^{-1,\frac{p}{2}}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2p}-\frac{1}{2}}\|(w_{s}+v_{s})^{2}\|_{L^{\frac{p}{2}}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left(\|w_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{p}}+\|v_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{p}}\right)t^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2p}}.

This shows both that the right hand side of (
4.2.6
) is well defined for any
w\in\mathfrak{B}_{T_{**}}
and that choosing
T_{**}\in(0,T]
sufficiently small we have that
\Psi:\mathfrak{B}_{T_{**}}\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}_{T_{**}}
. Considering
w,\,\tilde{w}\in\mathfrak{B}_{T_{**}}
and using similar estimates one additionally obtains, for some
T_{*}\in(0,T_{**}]
,

\sup_{t\in T_{*}}\|\Psi w_{t}-\Psi w_{s}\|_{L^{p}}<\|w-\tilde{w}\|_{C_{T_{*}}L^{p}}.

Applying Banach’s fixed point theorem we obtain a unique fixed point of
\Psi
in
\mathfrak{B}_{T^{\ast}}
, which by definition is a mild solution to (
4.2.2
). It is not difficult to show that this fixed point is in fact unique in all of
C_{T_{*}}L^{p}
which completes the proof of local existence and uniqueness. From now on we write
w\in C_{T_{*}}L^{p}
for this solution.

In order to show high regularity at positive times we may use the fact that
\|w\|_{C_{T_{*}}L^{p}}\leq 1
and
\|v\|_{C_{T}L^{p}}<\infty
. Let,
p>4
and fix
\beta\in(1,2-4/p)
, by assumption this interval is non-trivial. Therefore, for any
t\in(0,T_{*})
, and with
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
as above,

\displaystyle\|w_{t}\|_{H^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq\|e^{t\Delta}w_{0}\|_{H^{\beta}}+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\partial_{x}(w_{s}+v_{s})^{2}\|_{H^{\beta}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\beta}t^{-\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{\beta+1}{2}-\frac{2}{p}+\frac{1}{2}}\|\partial_{x}(w_{s}+v_{s})^{2}\|_{W^{-1,\frac{p}{2}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\beta}t^{-\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{\beta+1}{2}-\frac{2}{p}+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\|w_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{p}}+\|v_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{p}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\beta}t^{-\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{2}}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{p}}+t^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{2}{p}}\left(1+\|v\|^{2}_{C_{T}H^{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle<\infty.

∎

We proceed to establish an a priori bound on
\sup_{t\in T_{*}}\|w_{t}\|_{L^{p}}
, for
p\geq 2
. First, one needs to show that the mild solution constructed above is a weak solution, that is for any
\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T})
,
t\in(0,T_{*}]
, one has

\langle w_{t},\varphi\rangle=\langle w_{0},\varphi\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\partial_{x}w_{s},\partial_{x}\varphi\rangle+\langle\partial_{x}(w_{s}+v_{s})^{2},\varphi\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Since we have already established,
w\in C_{T_{*}}H^{\beta}\hookrightarrow C_{T_{*}}H^{1}
, one may argue, either by finite dimensional approximations or considering an increasing sequence of partitions of
[0,t]
, that we have the identity,

\frac{1}{p}\left(\|w_{t}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}-\|w_{0}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}\right)=-\int_{0}^{t}\langle\partial_{x}w_{s},\partial_{x}w_{s}^{p-1}\rangle+\langle(w_{s}+v_{s})^{2},\partial_{x}w_{s}^{p-1}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.2.7)

For details of this kind of argument see
[
DDT94
, Sec. 3]
,
[
MW17
, Sec. 6]
. Taking the identity (
4.2.7
) as given we establish the following a priori estimate.

Lemma 4.2.2
.

Let
p\geq 2
,
\bar{T}>0
and
w\in C([0,\bar{T});L^{p}(\mathbb{T}))\cap C((0,\bar{T});H^{1}(\mathbb{T}))
be a solution to (
4.2.2
). Then, for any
t\in(0,\bar{T})
, with
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
as above, we have that

\|w_{t}\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim_{p,T}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{p}}+\|v\|_{C_{T}H^{\alpha}}.

(4.2.8)

Proof.

Integrating by parts on the right hand side of (
4.2.7
), it follows that, for
p\geq 2
and even, one has,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p(p-1)}\left(\|w_{t}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}-\|w_{0}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\|w_{s}^{p-2}|\partial_{x}w_{s}|^{2}\|_{L^{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\langle w_{s}^{p},\partial_{x}w_{s}\rangle+2\langle w_{s}^{p-1}v_{s},\partial_{x}w_{s}\rangle+\langle v_{s}^{2},w^{p-2}_{s}\partial_{x}w_{s}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

We control the final three terms separately: firstly we have

\langle w^{p}_{s},\partial_{x}w_{s}\rangle=\frac{1}{p+1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{x}w_{s}^{p+1}=0;

for the second, letting
c>1
be unspecified for now, we have

\displaystyle\langle w_{s}^{p-1}v_{s},\partial_{x}w_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{s}\|_{L^{\infty}}|\langle w_{s}^{p/2},w_{s}^{p/2-1}\partial_{x}w_{s}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{s}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|w_{s}\|^{p/2}_{L^{p}}\|w_{s}^{p-2}|\partial_{x}w_{s}|^{2}\|^{1/2}_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{2}\|v_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|w_{s}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}+\frac{1}{2c}\|w_{s}^{p-2}|\partial_{x}w_{s}|^{2}\|_{L^{1}};

and for the third, with
c>1
as above,

\displaystyle\langle v_{s}^{2},w^{p-2}_{s}\partial_{x}w_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}|\langle w_{s}^{p/2-1},w_{s}^{p/2-1}\partial_{x}w_{s}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|w_{s}^{p-2}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{1}}\|w_{s}^{p-2}|\partial_{x}w_{s}|^{2}\|^{1/2}_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{4}\|v_{s}\|^{2p}_{L^{\infty}}+\frac{c}{4}\|w_{s}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}+\frac{1}{2c}\|w_{s}^{p-2}|\partial_{x}w_{s}|^{2}\|_{L^{1}}.

Putting these together gives that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p(p-1)}\left(\|w_{t}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}-\|w_{0}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2c-3}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|w_{s}^{p-2}|\partial_{x}w_{s}|^{2}\|_{L^{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|w_{s}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}\left(c\|v_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}+\frac{c}{4}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{c}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\|v_{s}\|^{2p}_{L^{\infty}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Choosing
c>3/2
so that the first term is negative, and applying Grönwall we have that for all
t\in T_{*}
,

\|w_{t}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}\leq\left(\|w_{0}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}+\frac{cp(p-1)}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\|v_{s}\|^{2p}_{L^{p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)e^{t\left(c\|v\|^{2}_{C_{T}L^{\infty}}+\frac{c}{4}\right)}.

Taking the supremum outside the integral and abstracting the unimportant constants gives (
4.2.8
) for
p\geq 2
and even. To obtain the same for
p
odd one may use the embedding
L^{p}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T})
for
p>p^{\prime}
.
∎

With this a priori bound in hand, it is straightforward to demonstrate global well-posedness for (
4.2.2
), at least for
w_{0}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
, with
p>4
. Note that in
[
DDT94
]
, the same result is obtained by a more refined version of this method, for
p\geq 2
.

Theorem 4.2.3
.

Let
T>0
,
p>4
and
w_{0}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
. Then there exists a unique, mild solution,
w\in C_{T}L^{p}
, to (
4.2.2
).

Proof.

From Theorem
4.2.1
, it is clear that for any
\bar{T}\in(0,T]
and a solution
w\in C([0,\bar{T});L^{p}(\mathbb{T}))
to (
4.2.2
), then either
\lim_{t\rightarrow\bar{T}}\|w_{t}\|_{L^{p}}=\infty
or
w
is in fact a solution on all of
[0,\bar{T}]
. However, Lemma
4.2.2
shows that the former cannot be the case and so we may extend the solution indefinitely.
∎

It follows that
u:=w+v
defines a global solution to (
4.2.1
). In
[
DDT94
]
it was additionally shown that for every
u_{0}\in L^{p}((0,1))
, with
p\geq 2
, there exists an invariant measure,
\nu_{u_{0}}\in\mathcal{P}(L^{p}((0,1)))
, for (
4.2.1
). Further results concerning both the Burgers and Navier–Stokes equations with additive noise, including uniqueness of the invariant measure and exponential ergodicity, can be found in
[
GM05
,
FM95
,
DD04
]
. Some general introductions to ergodic theory for SPDE can be found in
[
DPZ96
,
Hai09
,
Hai08
]
.
