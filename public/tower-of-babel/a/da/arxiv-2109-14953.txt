About One-point Statistics of the Ratio of Two Fourier-transformed Cosmic Fields and an Application
Ming Li, Jun Pan, Pengjie Zhang, Jie Wang, Longlong Feng, Liang Gao, Xi Kang, Guoliang Li, Weipeng Lin, Haihui Wang

Abstract
The Fourier transformation is an effective and efficient operation of Gaussianization at the one-point level. Using a set of N-body simulation data, we verified that the one-point distribution functions of the dark matter momentum divergence and density fields closely follow complex Gaussian distributions. The one-point distribution function of the quotient of two complex Gaussian variables is introduced and studied. Statistical theories are then applied to model one-point statistics about the growth of individual Fourier mode of the dark matter density field, which can be obtained by the ratio of two Fourier transformed cosmic fields. Our simulation results proved that the models based on the Gaussian approximation are impressively accurate, and our analysis revealed many interesting aspects about the growth of dark matter’s density fluctuation in Fourier space.



I
Introduction

Understanding the statistical properties of the inhomogeneity in the spatial distribution of dark matter and its evolution in an expanding universe is one of the most crucial subjects in cosmology. The growing process of density fluctuation is not simple, even for a system composed of collisionless dark matter particles solely. Complexities lie in many aspects. Statistically, though the primordial density fluctuation is assumed to be Gaussian or nearly so with possibly a small amount of primordial non-Gaussianity
(see the review of
Chen 2010
, and references therein)
, the nonlinear gravitation evolution and other physical processes would drive the distribution of late-time dark matter to be highly non-Gaussian. Gaussian distribution is mathematically simple for practical application and can be fully described by its first two moments. However, the non-Gaussianity means that an entire hierarchy of higher-order cumulants or correlation functions is awaiting exploration. Furthermore, it has been shown that the distribution of dark matter is very close to the lognormal distribution and cannot be completely specified by its moments
(
Coles & Jones 1991
;
Carron 2011
)
.

Non-Gaussianity makes measurement complex and difficult. If one intends to extract information from late-time density fields, statistics beyond two-point level
(
Scoccimarro et al. 2001
;
Bernardeau et al. 2002
;
Sefusatti et al. 2006
)
are needed. In attempts to simplify the analysis, several Gaussianization schemes have been proposed and applied with notable success
(
Neyrinck et al. 2009
;
Scherrer et al. 2010
;
Yu et al. 2011
;
Carron & Szapudi 2013
, e.g.)
.
The schemes really help reduce the non-Gaussianity and enhance the cosmological information in the two-point statistics
(
Yu et al. 2016
;
Repp & Szapudi 2018
, e.g.)
. The essence of Gaussianization is to perform a local transformation, so that the non-Gaussianity in statistics of the transformed field at the first few low orders might be significantly suppressed. Then the Gaussian approximation could be applied to model the corresponding statistics. One has to keep in mind that non-Gaussianity never vanishes, but instead has a different appearance
(
Qin et al. 2020
)
.

Matsubara 2007
formally derived that, for an arbitrary random field in a spatially homogeneous and sufficiently large space, the one-point probability distribution function (one-point PDF) of its Fourier mode shall approach Gaussian, provided that the polyspectra
P^{(2n)}(\mathbf{k},\ldots,\mathbf{k};-\mathbf{k},\ldots,-\mathbf{k})
are finite for any positive integer
n
. For a cosmic density field, spatial homogeneity is normally ensured. The condition of finite polyspectra at arbitrary even orders has not been exhaustively verified. But non-Gaussianity in the density field on most scales with
\mathbf{k}\neq 0
is believed to be finite, since the volume average correlation functions derived from cosmological simulations and galaxy surveys are always finite up to detected ranks
(
Meiksin et al. 1992
;
Bouchet & Hernquist 1992
;
Gaztañaga & Frieman 1994
;
Croton et al. 2004
;
Hellwing et al. 2010
;
Cappi et al. 2015
, e.g.)
. Actually, part of the conclusions of
Matsubara 2007
have already been proved to be applicable to the cosmic density field in N-body simulations by
Hikage et al. 2004
.

Falck et al. 2021
recently measured the one-point PDFs of Fourier modes of the dark matter density fields of the
Indra
simulation suite. They found that the Gaussian approximation of
Matsubara 2007
is consistent with simulations on scales as large as
\left|{\mathbf{k}}\right|\approx 0.6\ {h\ \rm Mpc^{-1}}
with relatively good accuracy. Another independent research of
Qin et al. 2022
comprehensively tested the conclusions of
Matsubara 2007
, validated that the Gaussian approximation can hold up to scales of
\left|{\mathbf{k}}\right|\sim 1\ {h\ \rm Mpc^{-1}}
for one-point PDFs of both modulus and phases. Thereof the density field in Fourier space poses very interesting statistical features. Its Fourier mode (even in the non-linear regime) closely follows the Gaussian distribution, while phase correlation of two points is already non-negligible, and then bispectrum and trispectrum are apparently significant
(
Matarrese et al. 1997
;
Scoccimarro 2000
;
Verde & Heavens 2001
;
Gualdi et al. 2021
, see also)
.

It appears that the Fourier transformation acts as a special type of Gaussianization, enabling us to understand the statistics of the comic density field in Fourier space at the one-point level.
This work goes further and aims to apply the idea to model the particular type of statistics about the ratio of two different cosmic fields
A
and
B
in Fourier space, namely
X_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv A_{\mathbf{k}}/B_{\mathbf{k}}
.

The mode-dependent growth function of the dark matter density field utilized in
Falck et al. 2021
falls into this category. In theory, the evolution of individual modes of the density field is intrinsically deterministic if actions other than gravitational force can be ignored
(
Sahni & Coles 1995
;
Bernardeau et al. 2002
, e.g.)
. However, the complicated effects of mode coupling will make the growth have a distribution over different modes. Using multiple realizations of their
Indra
simulation suits,
Falck et al. 2021
characterize the growth of Fourier modes of the density field by defining the mode-dependent growth function as
D_{\mathbf{k}}(z)=\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z)/\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{i})}\right|
. The Fourier-transformed density contrast
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}
at a given redshift
z
and the initial redshift
z_{i}
of their simulation are used. They found that
D_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
is stochastic over different realizations, and its distribution becomes wider at later epochs and higher
k=\left|{{\mathbf{k}}}\right|
. The medians of
D_{\mathbf{k}}
and the averages of
\ln D_{\mathbf{k}}
were also shown on various scales and at different redshifts, but no analytical formulae are provided for the distribution and related statistics. Here, we will demonstrate that the analytical approximation to the one-point PDF of
D_{\mathbf{k}}
can be derived based on the results of
Matsubara 2007
.

In principle, the Fourier mode of any type of nonlinear cosmic field, including momentum divergence, shall also obey the theorem of
Matsubara 2007
, as long as prerequisite conditions are satisfied.
Jennings & Jennings 2015
proposed a concept of stochastic growth rate to help develop perturbative theories of the redshift distortion effect, based on their numerical findings on the ratio of the velocity divergence field to the density field in Fourier space. This is another example of the ratio of cosmic fields in Fourier space, whose distribution could be examined and formulated. It is still challenging to conduct robust and accurate statistics of the volume-averaged velocity field from discrete samples deep into the nonlinear regime. Therefore, we instead consider using the momentum divergence field, since the momentum divergence could be easily measured from simulation
(
Pan 2020
)
.

As will be shown later, for dark matter, the quotient of momentum divergence to density is a direct measure of the growth rate of the density field at any given moment
z
and scale
\left|{\mathbf{k}}\right|
. Thus, the mode growth rate defined in this way could be linked to the mode-dependent growth function
D_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
. But unlike the stochastic growth rate in
Jennings & Jennings 2015
, it could not be directly used for the redshift distortion effect.

The entire paper is structured as follows. The definition and theoretical foundation are laid mainly in Section
II
, which is then compared with the simulation data in Section
III
. Section
IV
is about the performance test of empirical models for the mean mode growth rate. This study ends with a summary and discussion in the last section.

II
Theories on statistics of quotient of two complex Gaussian random variables

Starting from a more general expression of the distribution of two complex Gaussians, we summarize the major mathematical background. The results are then extended with two particular statistics, namely, the mode-dependent growth function and the mode growth rate. Their statistical meanings are stated and will be investigated in the following part of this work.

II.1
Distribution of the ratio of two complex Gaussians

Without loss of generality,
A_{\mathbf{k}}
and
B_{\mathbf{k}}
are supposed counterparts in the Fourier space of two random fields
A
and
B
of any kind, then
X_{\mathbf{k}}
is defined as the quotient of the two complex random variables,
X_{\mathbf{k}}=A_{\mathbf{k}}/B_{\mathbf{k}}
. Calculating the distribution of
X_{\mathbf{k}}
requires complete knowledge of the joint distribution of
A_{\mathbf{k}}
and
B_{\mathbf{k}}
. Practically, the closed-form analytical expression is intractable, except for a few cases. Fortunately, if
A_{\mathbf{k}}
and
B_{\mathbf{k}}
are both distributed as Gaussian, analytical formulae exist in the literature, which we reproduce here as the basis of our work.

Let
{\bf q}=[A_{\mathbf{k}},B_{\mathbf{k}}]^{T}
be a
2\times 1
complex Gaussian random vector, there are the mean of the vector
{\bf u}=\langle{{\bf q}}\rangle=[u_{A},u_{B}]^{T}
and the covariance matrix

\displaystyle\langle{({\bf q}-{\bf u})({\bf q}-{\bf u})^{H}}\rangle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma_{A}^{2}&r\sigma_{A}\sigma_{B}\\
r^{*}\sigma_{A}\sigma_{B}&\sigma_{B}^{2}\end{array}\right]\ ,

\langle{\ldots}\rangle
means the average,
(\ldots)^{*}
is the conjugate,
(\ldots)^{T}
and
(\ldots)^{H}
refer to the transpose and conjugate transpose respectively, and r is the parameter of the correlation coefficient. Let
X_{\mathbf{k}}=A_{\mathbf{k}}/B_{\mathbf{k}}=X_{R}+iX_{I}
,
Li & He 2019
derive that the joint PDF of
X_{R}
and
X_{I}
can be written as

p(X_{R},X_{I})=\frac{e^{-c_{2}}}{c_{1}\pi\sigma_{B}^{2}\gamma^{2}(X_{\mathbf{k}})}e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}(X_{\mathbf{k}})}{\gamma(X_{\mathbf{k}})}}\left[1+\frac{\lambda^{2}(X_{\mathbf{k}})}{\gamma(X_{\mathbf{k}})}\right]\ ,

(4)

in which

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{A}^{2}(1-\left|{r}\right|^{2}),

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=\left|{a_{2}}\right|^{2}/c_{1}+\left|{u_{B}}\right|^{2}/\sigma^{2}_{B},

\displaystyle\gamma(X_{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=\left|{X_{\mathbf{k}}-a_{1}}\right|^{2}/c_{1}+1/\sigma_{B}^{2},

\displaystyle\lambda(X_{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=\left|{a_{2}(X_{\mathbf{k}}-a_{1})^{*}/c_{1}+u_{B}/\sigma^{2}_{B}}\right|,

with
a_{1}=r\sigma_{A}/\sigma_{B}
and
a_{2}=u_{A}-a_{1}u_{B}
. Note that the formula is general and is not restricted to the case of independently distributed
A_{\mathbf{k}}
and
B_{\mathbf{k}}
.

The mean of
X_{\mathbf{k}}
, if
u_{B}\neq 0
, is given by

\langle{X_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle=\frac{u_{A}}{u_{B}}+\left(r\frac{\sigma_{A}}{\sigma_{B}}-\frac{u_{A}}{u_{B}}\right)e^{-\left|{u_{B}}\right|^{2}/\sigma_{B}^{2}}\ ,

(5)

and if
u_{B}=0

\langle{X_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle=r\frac{\sigma_{A}}{\sigma_{B}}\ ,

(6)

while higher-order moments
\langle{\left|{X_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|^{n\geq 2}}\rangle
do not exist unless
\left|{r}\right|=1

(
Wu 2019
;
Li & He 2019
)
.

If
A_{\mathbf{k}}
and
B_{\mathbf{k}}
are independent, then
r=0
and the joint PDF reduces to

p(X_{R},X_{I})=\frac{\sigma_{A}^{2}\sigma_{B}^{2}e^{-\left(\frac{\left|{u_{A}}\right|^{2}}{\sigma_{A}^{2}}+\frac{\left|{u_{B}}\right|^{2}}{\sigma_{B}^{2}}\right)}}{\pi(\left|{X_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|^{2}\sigma_{B}^{2}+\sigma_{A}^{2})^{2}}e^{\kappa(X_{\mathbf{k}})}[1+\kappa(X_{\mathbf{k}})]

(7)

with

\kappa(X_{\mathbf{k}})=\frac{\left|{X_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}u_{A}/\sigma_{A}^{2}+u_{B}/\sigma_{B}^{2}}\right|^{2}}{(\left|{X_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|^{2}/\sigma_{A}^{2}+1/\sigma_{B}^{2})}

(
Pham-Gia et al. 2006
;
Nadimi et al. 2018
, e.g.)
.

A more special case is
u_{A}=u_{B}=0
, the joint PDF is in the form of

p(X_{R},X_{I})=\frac{1-\left|{r}\right|^{2}}{\pi\left[\left|{X_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|^{2}\frac{\sigma_{B}}{\sigma_{A}}+\frac{\sigma_{A}}{\sigma_{B}}-2(r_{R}X_{R}+r_{I}x_{I})\right]^{2}}\ ,

(8)

in which
r=r_{R}+ir_{I}

(
Baxley et al. 2010
)
. At this stage, we have derived the main equation, which will be extended to two particular statistics in the context of structure formation in the following part of this study.

II.2
The mode-dependent growth function

For generality, we slightly extended the notion of
Falck et al. 2021
, by defining a complex mode-dependent growth function
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{k}}}
,

X_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1})}{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{2})}\equiv\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1},z_{2})\ ,

(9)

where
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
is the Fourier counterpart of the dimensionless density contrast
\delta=\rho/\langle{\rho}\rangle-1
at redshift
z
, with
\rho
denoting the density and
z_{2}>z_{1}
. If we denote
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1},z_{2})=D_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1},z_{2})e^{i\phi_{D}}
, the amplitude
D_{\mathbf{k}}=\left|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|
is commonly used and is the target function that we want to analyze.
As formerly mentioned in Section
I
, the nonlinear field of
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
in the Fourier space could be approximately treated as Gaussian distributed. This generally guarantees the usage of Equation (
4
) to model
p(D_{\mathbf{k}},\phi_{D})
.

Let the density power spectrum at
z
be
P_{z}({\mathbf{k}},z)\equiv\langle{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z)\delta^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}(z)}\rangle
, and let the density cross-spectrum at two different redshifts be
P_{z_{1},z_{2}}({\mathbf{k}},z_{1},z_{2})\equiv\langle{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1})\delta^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{2})}\rangle
.
With the consideration that the density field is statistically isotropic and homogeneous,
\delta_{{\mathbf{k}}}
satisfies
\langle{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle=0
and
P_{z_{1},z_{2}}=P^{*}_{z_{1},z_{2}}
. This exactly corresponds to the case of Equation (
8
), but with the correlation coefficient
r_{R}=P_{z_{1},z_{2}}/\sqrt{P_{z_{1}}P_{z_{2}}}
and
r_{I}=0
.
If we further let
t=\sqrt{P_{z_{1}}/P_{z_{2}}}
, Equation (
8
) proceeds to another form

\displaystyle p(D_{\mathbf{k}},\phi_{D})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-r^{2}}{\pi}\frac{D_{\mathbf{k}}t^{2}}{\left(D^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}+t^{2}-2D_{\mathbf{k}}rt\cos\phi_{D}\right)^{2}}\ ,

(10)

\displaystyle p(D_{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=\frac{2D_{\mathbf{k}}t^{2}(D_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+t^{2})(1-r^{2})}{\left[(D_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+t^{2})^{2}-4D_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}t^{2}r^{2}\right]^{3/2}}\ ,

\displaystyle p(\phi_{D})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-r^{2}}{2\pi(1-r^{2}\cos^{2}\phi_{D})^{3/2}}\{\sqrt{1-r^{2}\cos^{2}\phi_{D}}

\displaystyle+[\pi-\arccos(r\cos\phi_{D})]r\cos\phi_{D}\}\ ,

with
r<1
and
-\pi\leq\phi_{D}<\pi
.

With the results of Section
II.1
, we could derive the mean
\langle{\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle=rt
and the median
D_{{\mathbf{k}},1/2}=t
. What is important is that we have identified the meanings of two often used quantities. The first is that
P_{z_{1},z_{2}}/P_{z_{2}}
is the mean of the complex mode-dependent growth function
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{k}}}
; the second is that
\sqrt{P_{z_{1}}/P_{z_{2}}}
is the median value of
D_{{\mathbf{k}}}
.

Unfortunately, the mean
\langle{D_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle
does not have a simple analytic form and could only be calculated with numerical integration. In real applications, one could always numerically compute an averaged
D_{\mathbf{k}}
over many simulation realizations. However, the mean value of the logarithm of
D_{\mathbf{k}}
used in
Falck et al. 2021
,
\langle{\ln D_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle
, exists. Under the Gaussian approximation,
\ln\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|
obeys the dubbed log-Rayleigh distribution, in which the expectation is

\langle{\ln\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|}\rangle=\ln\sqrt{\frac{P_{\delta}}{2}}+\frac{\ln 2}{2}-\frac{C}{2}\ ,

(11)

where
C
is the Euler constant defined by
C=-\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-x}\ln x{\mathrm{d}}x

(
Rivet et al. 2007
)
. Subsequently,
\langle{\ln D_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle
could be related to the density power spectrum as

\langle{\ln D_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle=\langle{\ln\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1})}\right|}\rangle-\langle{\ln\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{2})}\right|}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P_{z_{1}}}{P_{z_{2}}}\ ,

(12)

which can be used straightforwardly to interpret the results of
Falck et al. 2021
.

II.3
A minor note about possible applications to bias

If we go beyond the dark matter density field by considering the density field of biased tracers in the Fourier space
\delta_{g}({\mathbf{k}})
, which has a complex bias function as
\widetilde{b}_{\mathbf{k}}=\delta_{g}({\mathbf{k}})/\delta_{m}({\mathbf{k}})
relative to dark matter
\delta_{m}({\mathbf{k}})
, mathematical results in Section
II.2
can be essentially adopted after the replacements of
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1})\rightarrow\delta_{g}({\mathbf{k}})
and
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{2})\rightarrow\delta_{m}({\mathbf{k}})
. Therefore, the ordinary bias function
b_{o}=\sqrt{P_{g}/P_{m}}=\sqrt{\langle{\delta_{g}\delta_{g}^{*}}\rangle/\langle{\delta_{m}\delta_{m}^{*}}\rangle}
is the median value of
\left|{\widetilde{b}_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|
. While another commonly cited bias function
b_{x}=P_{gm}/P_{m}=\langle{\delta_{g}\delta_{m}^{*}}\rangle/\langle{\delta_{m}\delta_{m}^{*}}\rangle
is the average of
\widetilde{b}_{\mathbf{k}}
, with denoting
P_{gm}=\langle{\delta_{g}\delta_{m}^{*}}\rangle
as the cross-spectrum of the two field. The difference between
b_{o}
and
b_{x}
vanishes in the limit of
P_{gm}/\sqrt{P_{g}P_{m}}\rightarrow 1
, that is the case when bias shows little stochasticity.

The number density of biased traces such as halos and galaxies is usually much lower than that of dark matter in simulations. Understanding and devising methods to suppress discreteness effects on
\delta_{g}({\mathbf{k}})
is not an easy task. Therefore, we will leave further exploration of biased density fields for future research and concentrate on the distribution of dark matter only.

II.4
The mode growth rate

In analogy with
Jennings & Jennings 2015
, we start from the continuity equation, which is valid for dark matter as long as dark matter does not annihilate significantly,

a\frac{\partial\delta({\mathbf{r}},\tau)}{\partial\tau}+\nabla\cdot\{[1+\delta({\mathbf{r}},\tau)]{\mathbf{v}}({\mathbf{r}},\tau)\}=0\ ,

(13)

in which
a=1/(1+z)
with
z
being the redshift corresponding to cosmic time
\tau
,
\delta
is the dark matter density contrast at position
{\mathbf{r}}
, and
{\bf v}
labels the peculiar velocity. In Fourier space, it turns out to be

\frac{\partial\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(a)}{\partial\ln a}=f\psi_{\mathbf{k}}(a)\ ,

(14)

in which
\psi_{\mathbf{k}}
(shorthand for
\psi({\mathbf{k}})
) is the Fourier transform of the momentum divergence
\psi(\mathbf{r})\equiv-\nabla\cdot\left[(1+\delta){\bf v}(\mathbf{r})\right]/Haf
, where
H
is the Hubble parameter, and
f={\mathrm{d}}\ln D/{\mathrm{d}}\ln a
is the linear growth rate defined with respect to the linear growth function
D
. Unlike
D_{\mathbf{k}}
, the linear growth function is scale independent and is defined as the amplitude factor of the density contrast
\delta
relative to that at the present time.

On the other hand, if we denote
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|e^{i\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}
, then

\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\frac{\partial\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial\ln{a}}=\frac{1}{\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|}\frac{\partial\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|}{\partial\ln{a}}+i\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial\ln{a}}\ ,

(15)

of which the real part is only about the growth of the moduli and the imaginary component is solely about phases.
Let
X_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv\psi_{\mathbf{k}}/\delta_{\mathbf{k}}
, combining Equation (
14
) with Equation (
15
) yields the following,

X_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\frac{\partial\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial\ln D}=\Delta^{\prime}+i\phi^{\prime}\ .

(16)

\Delta^{\prime}
and
\phi^{\prime}
refer to the real and imaginary parts of
X_{\mathbf{k}}
, respectively,

\Delta^{\prime}\equiv X_{R}=\frac{\partial\ln\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|}{\partial\ln D}\ ,\quad\phi^{\prime}\equiv X_{I}=\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial\ln D}\ .

(17)

Here,
a
is replaced by the linear growth function
D
as the time variable.

It is apparent that
X_{\mathbf{k}}
effectively becomes the
mode growth rate
of dark matter’s density field, of which
\Delta^{\prime}
is the
mode’s amplitude growth rate
and
\phi^{\prime}
stands for the
mode’s phase growth rate
.
In the linear regime
\psi_{\mathbf{k}}=\delta_{\mathbf{k}}
,
\Delta^{\prime}=1
and
\phi^{\prime}=0
, the amplitudes will grow linearly with
D
while the phases will remain invariant. However, when gravitational nonlinearity increases,
\psi_{\mathbf{k}}\neq\delta_{\mathbf{k}}
, it is expected that stochasticity in
\Delta^{\prime}
and
\phi^{\prime}
will become stronger due to the effects of mode coupling and multi-streaming.

Exact modeling of the distribution of
X_{\mathbf{k}}
requires complete knowledge of the joint distribution of
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\psi_{\mathbf{k}}
. Again, based on the results of
Matsubara 2007
, if
\psi_{\mathbf{k}}
closely follows the Gaussian distribution in a similar way as
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}
does, then the results of Section
II.1
can be used to model
p(\Delta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})
.

Since
\langle{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle=\langle{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle=0
, after denoting

\displaystyle P_{\delta}(\mathbf{k})=\langle{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}}\rangle,\,P_{\psi}({\mathbf{k}})=\langle{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}\psi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle,\,P_{\delta\psi}=\langle{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}\psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}}\rangle=\langle{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}\psi_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle

and

\displaystyle\alpha\equiv P_{\delta\psi}/\sqrt{P_{\delta}P_{\psi}},\quad\beta\equiv\sqrt{P_{\psi}/P_{\delta}},

the one-point PDFs of
X_{\mathbf{k}}
,
\Delta^{\prime}
and
\phi^{\prime}
can be derived from Equation (
8
) without difficulty,

\displaystyle p(\Delta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\beta^{2}(1-\alpha^{2})}{\left[(\Delta^{\prime}-\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle)^{2}+\phi^{\prime 2}+\beta^{2}(1-\alpha^{2})\right]^{2}}

(18)

\displaystyle p(\Delta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta^{2}(1-\alpha^{2})}{\left[(\Delta^{\prime}-\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle)^{2}+\beta^{2}(1-\alpha^{2})\right]^{3/2}}

\displaystyle p(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta^{2}(1-\alpha^{2})}{\left[\phi^{\prime 2}+\beta^{2}(1-\alpha^{2})\right]^{3/2}}\ ,

both
p(\Delta^{\prime})
and
p(\phi^{\prime})
are actually the Student’s t-distribution with 2 degrees of freedom.

A nice property of defining the mode growth rate with Equation (
17
) is that the first-order moment
\langle{X_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle
is well determined,

\displaystyle\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{X_{R}}\rangle=\left\langle\frac{\psi_{\mathbf{k}}}{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\right\rangle=\alpha\beta=\frac{P_{\delta\psi}}{P_{\delta}}\ ,

(19)

\displaystyle\langle{\phi^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{X_{I}}\rangle=0\ .

In fact, Equation (
19
) also points out that
\langle{X_{\mathbf{k}}}\rangle
can be estimated by the ratio of two nonzero power spectra, free of the possible numerical catastrophe when
\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|\sim 0
.

II.5
Links among mode-dependent growth function, mode growth rate and power spectrum

The amplitude of the mode-dependent growth function at redshift
z_{1}
with respect to
z_{2}
(
z_{2}>z_{1}
) could actually be related to
\Delta^{\prime}
through

\ln D_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1},z_{2})=\ln\frac{\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1})}\right|}{\left|{\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{2})}\right|}=\int_{D(z_{2})}^{D(z_{1})}\Delta^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}\ln D\ .

(20)

Taking the average in the Gaussian ansatz then leads to

\frac{P_{\delta}(z_{1})}{P_{\delta}(z_{2})}=e^{2\langle{\ln D_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1},z_{2})}\rangle}=\exp\left(2\int_{D(z_{2})}^{D(z_{1})}\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle{\mathrm{d}}\ln D\right)\ ,

(21)

or

{\mathrm{d}}\ln P_{\delta}/{\mathrm{d}}\ln D=2\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle\ .

(22)

This means that
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
is a measure of the logarithmic growth rate of the nonlinear density power spectrum, as long as the Gaussian approximation to
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\psi_{\mathbf{k}}
holds its effectiveness.

III
Comparison with N-body simulation

In Section
II
, the analytical expressions of the distributions of the mode-dependent growth function and the mode growth rate are derived under the assumption that the density field
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}
and the momentum divergence field
\psi_{\mathbf{k}}
follow the Gaussian distribution. In this section, simulation data are used to examine whether the prerequisite conditions are met and then the analytical formulas of two statistics are compared.

III.1
The simulation and numerical issues

In this work, the
Pangu
simulation in
Li et al. 2012
is recruited. The dark matter-only simulation is conducted with a memory-optimized version of
GADGET2

(
Springel 2005
)
. The cosmological parameters are
\Omega_{m}=0.26
,
\Omega_{b}=0.044
,
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.74
,
h=0.71
and
\sigma_{8}=0.8
. The simulation uses
N=3072^{3}
dark matter particles in a cubic periodic box with
L=1\ h^{-1}{\rm Gpc}
on one side. Each particle has a mass of
2.48915\times 10^{9}\ {h^{-1}\rm M_{\odot}}
and the Plummer-equivalent force softening length is
7\ {h^{-1}\rm kpc}
. The simulation starts from an initial redshift of
z=127
. Since we are mainly interested in regimes with considerably developed non-Gaussianity, about 13 snapshots at redshifts from
z=2
to 0 are selected. The mass resolution of the simulation supports accurate measurement of power spectrum at scales
1<k\lesssim 10\ {h\ \rm Mpc^{-1}}
, meanwhile its box size can provide sufficient numbers of modes at large scales
k<0.1\ {h\ \rm Mpc^{-1}}
and makes the systematics in modes of short wavelengths induced by the absence of long-wavelength modes negligible
(
Crocce & Scoccimarro 2006
;
Takahashi et al. 2008
, e.g.)
.

The numerical fast Fourier transformation (FFT) is implemented with the
FFTW3
package
(
Frigo & Johnson 2005
)
. To obtain accurate measurements of Fourier modes of density, momentum divergence, and related power spectra, the prescription of
Yang et al. 2009
and
Pan 2020
is adopted to compensate the effects of aliasing and smoothing. The third-order orthogonalized Battle-Lemarié spline function is used when assigning dark matter particles upon FFT grids. But aliasing cannot be completely removed, and numerical experiments indicate that if
1\%
precision is required, it is safe to use Fourier modes on scales
k<0.67\ k_{N}
(Appendix
A
), where
k_{N}
is the Nyquist frequency.

The quality of the numerical measurement is further controlled by applying criteria based on shot noise. We restrict our exploration to the scale range within which the influence of the power spectrum of the shot noise is less than
1\%
. Additionally, the number of FFT grids is set by the requirement that the mean number of particles in a grid cell should be greater than one. Therefore, the largest number of FFT grids used in this work is
2048^{3}
. By applying these conservative rules, the influence of discreteness becomes minuscule; henceforth, we do not make any shot noise correction unless specified otherwise.

The last point we want to address here is that in this work only one simulation realization is used. Thus, the ergodicity principle is assumed when comparing simulations with theories. Specifically, statistics computed with all modes identical to
k=\left|{{\mathbf{k}}}\right|
from a single simulation are considered statistically equivalent to statistics of the mode at
{\mathbf{k}}
over many realizations, with some reasonable fluctuations attributed to cosmic variance.

If we further decompose the momentum divergence into two independent components,
\psi=\psi_{s}+\psi_{\delta}
, with
\psi_{s}
the stochastic part uncorrelated with density at all, and
\psi_{\delta}
the part fully correlated with the density field that can be expressed as
\psi_{\delta}({\mathbf{k}})=T_{\delta\rightarrow\psi}({\mathbf{k}})\delta({\mathbf{k}})
with a certain transfer function
T_{\delta\rightarrow\psi}
in Fourier space. If one denotes the power spectrum of
\psi_{s}
as

S^{2}=(P_{\delta}P_{\psi}-P_{\delta\psi}^{2})/P_{\delta}^{2}=P_{\psi_{s}}/P_{\delta}\ .

(24)

From this decomposition, it turns out that the distribution widths of
p(\Delta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})
are mainly caused by the stochastic part of the momentum divergence
\psi_{s}
.

It is interesting to note that there is a crude scaling relation
S(z)\propto D(z)
whose accuracy is better than
10\%

5\%

for

scales with

(

;

, e.g.)

But in the nonlinear regime, where the one-loop theory breaks seriously, the scaling relation still holds valid with considerable precision, and the performance turns out to be even better at lower redshifts, which is really intriguing. However, it is necessary to be cautious when quoting the precision of the scaling relation for
k<0.1\ {h\ \rm Mpc^{-1}}
,
S(z)
is sensitive to the treatment of shot noise in the weakly nonlinear regime (see Appendix
B
for more details).

The mean mode growth rate

IV
Empirical models for the mean mode growth rate in nonlinear regime

IV.1
Fitting formulae

Developing theoretical models of
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
in the strongly nonlinear regime from first principles is a difficult task, desperately in demand of ingenious ideas. Therefore, we turn to building templates for nonlinear
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
empirically in different cosmologies. The solution is already provided by Equation (
22
), together the algorithm based on the first-order finite difference method. We first pick up a fitting formula for
P_{\delta}
and construct a table as a function of redshift
z
, with a fine increment of
\Delta z
. Then
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
at a given redshift
z
is calculated numerically with the derivative

\frac{\ln P_{\delta}(z-\Delta z)-\ln P_{\delta}(z+\Delta z)}{2\Delta\ln D(z)},

(25)

where
\Delta\ln D(z)=\ln D(z-\Delta z)-\ln D(z+\Delta z)
,
z\pm\Delta z
are two interpolation boundaries that enclose
z
in the constructed
P_{\delta}
table. We have tried different values of
\Delta z
and
\Delta\ln D
, and once the result becomes stable as
\Delta\ln D\rightarrow 0
, we take it as a phenomenological model of
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
.

In this work, emulators of
HMCODE 2020

(
Mead et al. 2021
)
,
EUCLID EMU

(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2020
, version 2;)
,
BACCO EMU

(
Angulo et al. 2020
)
,
MIRA TITAN

(
Lawrence et al. 2017
)
, and the empirical model of
Takahashi 2012

(
Takahashi et al. 2012
)
, are chosen to generate the nonlinear power spectrum. In Figure
9
, the performance of these prescriptions is presented in comparison to the simulation results. These fitting formulae do recover the power spectrum very well, but cannot accurately reproduce
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
in the strongly nonlinear regime, for example,
k\gtrsim 1\ {h\ \rm Mpc^{-1}}
for all shown cases. Again, the results presented are of a single simulation set and more realizations are needed to draw reliable conclusions. Nevertheless, one thing is sure that it seems
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
can be a useful tool for distinguishing the performance of different fitting formulae of the power spectrum in the strongly nonlinear regime.

IV.2
Scale transformation

It is well known that the nonlinear dimensionless power spectrum of the dark matter density field,
\Delta^{2}(k)=P(k)k^{3}/(2\pi^{2})
, can be approximated as a function of the linear dimensionless power spectrum
\Delta^{2}_{L}(k_{L},z)=(D(z)/D(0))^{2}\Delta^{2}_{L}(k_{L},0)
after a scale transformation,

k_{L}=[1+\Delta^{2}(k)]^{-1/3}k.

(26)

The scaling
ansatz
was first proposed to model the two-point correlation function by
Hamilton et al. 1991
, then developed in the Fourier space by
Peacock & Dodds 1994
. It is found that Equation (
26
) could also be applied to approximately recover the bispectrum when nonlinearity is not strong
(
Pan et al. 2007
)
. Along with Equation (
22
), we conjecture that the transformation of Equation (
26
) could help to understand
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
.

,

are given by Equation (

). The left panel shows the results computed from the

)

The two formulae of
HMCode 2020
and
Takahashi 2012
are called to facilitate our experiment. The results of the simulation
Pangu
simulation and the two models are shown in Figure
10
. It turns out that the scale transformation of Equation (
26
) is really useful, although an extra factor is needed in practice. For
\Delta^{2}_{L}D(0)/D(z)\lesssim 1.3
, the mean mode growth rates at different epochs can be written as

\langle{\Delta^{\prime}(k,z)}\rangle=F[\Delta^{2}_{L}(k_{L},z)D(0)/D(z)]\ ,

(27)

rather than
F[\Delta^{2}_{L}]
as one might naively expect. Here,
F[\ldots]
refers to a general function of a certain form that may contain additional dependencies on the cosmological parameters, and the accuracy is better than
5\%
.

The peaks of
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
of the simulation and the two empirical models are basically relocated to a narrow range of
\Delta^{2}_{L}D(0)/D(z)\sim 1.3-2
after the scale transformation. But the differences between the simulation and the empirical models are still very prominent for
\Delta^{2}_{L}D(0)/D(z)\gtrsim 1.3
. Empirical models of
HMCode 2020
and
Takahashi 2012
(the middle and right panels of Figure
10
) produce rising tails on scales greater than peak locations, and these tails arise systematically higher at lower redshifts, which are not present in the
{\tt Pangu}
simulation. Considering that empirical models are obtained through extensive calibration against simulations, the evolution of dark matter clustering in very strongly nonlinear regimes may be sensitive to numerical details of different simulations. One needs to be careful when using fitting formulae as templates if their
\Delta^{2}_{L}D(0)/D(z)
goes beyond 1.3.

V
Summary

In this study, we demonstrate that nonlinear cosmic fields such as the dark matter density
\delta
and the momentum divergence
\psi
can be effectively and efficiently Gaussianized by the Fourier transformation at the one-point level, which is an extension of the work of
Matsubara 2007
. Gaussianity of the one-point distributions of Fourier modes greatly simplifies analysis of the spatial distribution of dark matter and its evolution, as what has been shown in this work about functions given by the ratio of two complex random variables.

Analytical formulae about the one-point PDF of the quotient of two correlated complex Gaussian random variables are introduced, and then applied to explore statistical properties of two quantities used to describe the clustering evolution of dark matter. The first is the complex mode-dependent growth function
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1},z_{2})
, defined as the ratio of
\delta_{\mathbf{k}}
at two different epochs,
X_{\mathbf{k}}=\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1})/\delta_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{2})
. The explicit model of the one-point PDF of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{k}}
is in good agreement with the results of the N-body simulation. The amplitude of the complex mode-dependent growth function
D_{{\mathbf{k}}}
and its logarithm are also investigated. It is really intriguing that, for the dark matter density, the ratio of the cross-power spectrum to the auto-power spectrum and the ratio of two auto-power spectra have particular statistical meanings. The former one is the mean of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{k}}
, while the square root of the latter is the median of the amplitude
D_{{\mathbf{k}}}
.

Another instance studied in this work is the complex mode growth rate, which is defined in Fourier space by the ratio
X_{\mathbf{k}}=\psi_{\mathbf{k}}/\delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta^{\prime}+i\phi^{\prime}
. With the continuity equation, we identify that the real part of
X_{\mathbf{k}}
,
\Delta^{\prime}
is the growth rate of the amplitude of the density mode, while the imaginary part
\phi^{\prime}
relates to the phase growth rate. The Gaussian approximation to the one-point PDF
p(\Delta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})
is again developed and confirmed by simulation with good accuracy. It turns out that the mode’s amplitude growth rate
\Delta^{\prime}
and the mode’s phase growth rate
\phi^{\prime}
both follow the Student’s t-distribution with 2 degrees of freedom. The distribution could be characterized by an alternative width parameter
S
, which increases with the strength of nonlinearity, and has an approximate scaling relation
S\propto D
among different epochs.

As
\langle{\phi^{\prime}}\rangle
is always zero, the information about the nonlinear evolution process of the density field in
X_{\mathbf{k}}
is mainly packed in the mean mode growth rate
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
. On large scales where nonlinearity is very weak,
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle\approx 1
at all redshifts. When goes to the nonlinear regime, the increasing nonlinearity will drive
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
away from unity. We further show that empirical formulae and theoretical models of the density power spectrum can provide satisfactory templates for
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
in the weakly and intermediate nonlinear regime, but the agreement begins to drop drastically in the strongly nonlinear regime. The phenomenon becomes even more apparent after applying a scale transformation.

In summary, with the Gaussian approximation to cosmic fields in Fourier space, the statistical properties about the quotient of two complex Gaussian variables provide a novel approach to measure the growth of individual mode of dark matter density field. It has been proven to be very useful and can be readily applied in other large-scale structure studies with similar types of quantities. The results presented here emphasize the simplicity of the analysis in Fourier space and are worthy of more attention for further studies of cosmic structure formations.

Appendix A
Aliasing

In the ratio of
P_{\delta\psi}/P_{\delta}
, the aliasing effect induced by the sampling function cannot be completely eliminated. We adopted the third-order Battle-Lemarié spline function to assign simulation particles onto FFT grids with different resolutions,
N_{\mathrm{grid}}=512,1024,2048
. Then the mean growth rates
\langle{\Delta^{\prime}}\rangle
are measured to carry on the convergence test. The results are shown in Figure
11
. We find that a difference greater than
1\%
occurs on the scales
k>\sim 0.7\ k_{N}
, where
k_{N}
is the Nyquist frequency, which is consistent with
Pan 2020
. After careful examination, we determine that the maximal

Appendix B
Effect of shot noise deduction on
S

The shot noises in
P_{\delta}
and
P_{\psi}
are minuscule, as the number of particles in our simulations is fairly large. As shown in Figure
12
, after subtracting the shot noise predicted by the local Poisson approximation
(
Pan 2020

well on scales

, whether this is a challenge to the way of subtracting shot noises is beyond the scope of this paper and will be left for future research.
