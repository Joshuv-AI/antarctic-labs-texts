Commuting Clifford actions
John W. Barrett

Abstract
It shown that if a vector space carries commuting actions of two Clifford algebras, then the quadratic monomials using generators from either Clifford algebra determine a spinor representation of an orthogonal Lie algebra. Examples of this construction have applications to high energy physics, particularly to the standard model and unification. It is shown how to use Clifford data to construct spectral triples for the Pati-Salam model that admit an action of Spin(10).



1
Introduction

A Clifford module is a representation of a Clifford algebra on a complex vector space. A standard result is that taking quadratic monomials in the generators (gamma matrices) determines a spinor representation of the corresponding orthogonal Lie algebra.

This paper studies commuting actions of two real Clifford algebras,

{\mathrm{Cl}}(p_{1},q_{1})
and
{\mathrm{Cl}}(p_{2},q_{2})
, on a vector space
\mathcal{H}
. According to Theorems
1
and
2
, the quadratic monomials in pairs of generators taken from either Clifford algebra determine a spinor representation of the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{so}}(q_{1}+p_{2},p_{1}+q_{2})
on
\mathcal{H}
. This result is despite the fact that the gamma matrices from one Clifford algebra
commute
with the gamma matrices of the other rather than anticommute, which would be the case if they both belonged to one larger Clifford algebra. Note that in some (but not all) cases the tensor product of two Clifford algebras can be made into one big Clifford algebra
[
10
,
3
]
, but this is
not
the result reported here (although this does come into the proof of Theorem
2
).

The general results in this paper were inspired by several different examples (for specific
p_{i}
,
q_{i}
) that appear in
[
9
]
in connection with the fields of particle physics. In particular, one can take commuting actions of
{\mathrm{Cl}}(0,q)
and
{\mathrm{Cl}}(p,0)
with any
p+q=10
, giving a representation of the unification group
{\mathrm{Spin}}(10)
on a Hilbert space formed by the fermions of the standard model.

For the case
q=6
and
p=4
, the
Clifford algebras are
{\mathrm{Cl}}(4,0)\cong M_{2}({\mathbb{H}})
and
{\mathrm{Cl}}(0,6)\cong M_{8}({\mathbb{R}})
, and the spin groups for each factor are
{\mathrm{Spin}}(4)
and
{\mathrm{Spin}}(6)
.
Thus this structure determines, in a natural way, a representation of the Pati-Salam group
G_{PS}={\mathrm{Spin}}(4)\times{\mathrm{Spin}}(6)/{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
on
\mathcal{H}
as a subgroup of
{\mathrm{Spin}}(10)
. The two subgroups lie in the even parts of these Clifford algebras, which are
{\mathbb{H}}\oplus{\mathbb{H}}
and
M_{4}({\mathbb{C}})
.

It is shown that a simple construction from this determines real spectral triples with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and algebra
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{H}}\oplus{\mathbb{H}}\oplus M_{4}({\mathbb{C}})
. The Clifford structures allow one to define an action of the unification group
{\mathrm{Spin}}(10)
on the Hilbert space of these spectral triples.

The quadratic monomials of

2.3
Commuting actions

Consider a finite-dimensional vector space
\mathcal{H}
with commuting actions of two Clifford algebras. The following result is that this determines a representation of a spin group using the generators from both algebras.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Clifford module for both
{\mathrm{Cl}}(p_{1},q_{1})
and
{\mathrm{Cl}}(p_{2},q_{2})
, such that the two actions commute. Denote the gamma matrices for the first action by
\{\Gamma_{1}^{a}\}
and for the second action
\{\Gamma_{2}^{\alpha}\}
.
The quadratic monomials formed from pairs of different generators in the set
\{\Gamma_{1}^{a},\Gamma_{2}^{\alpha}\}
determine a representation of the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{so}}(q_{1}+p_{2},p_{1}+q_{2})
on
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Define generators of the Lie algebra by
T_{1}^{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{1}^{a}\Gamma_{1}^{b}
,
T_{2}^{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{2}^{\alpha}\Gamma_{2}^{\beta}
(for
a\neq b
and
\alpha\neq\beta
) and
U^{a\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{1}^{a}\Gamma_{2}^{\beta}
for all
a,\beta
. For convenience, define
T_{1}^{aa}=T_{2}^{\alpha\alpha}=0
. Then the Lie brackets are

\displaystyle[T_{1}^{ab},T_{1}^{cd}]

\displaystyle=\eta_{1}^{bc}\,T_{1}^{ad}-\eta_{1}^{ac}\,T_{1}^{bd}+\eta_{1}^{bd}\,T_{1}^{ca}-\eta_{1}^{ad}\,T_{1}^{cb}

(8)

\displaystyle[T_{2}^{\alpha\beta},T_{2}^{\gamma\delta}]

\displaystyle=\eta_{2}^{\beta\gamma}\,T_{2}^{\alpha\delta}-\eta_{2}^{\alpha\gamma}\,T_{2}^{\beta\delta}+\eta_{2}^{\beta\delta}\,T_{2}^{\gamma\alpha}-\eta_{2}^{\alpha\delta}\,T_{2}^{\gamma\beta}

\displaystyle[T_{1}^{ab},U^{c\delta}]

\displaystyle=\eta_{1}^{bc}\,U^{a\delta}-\eta_{1}^{ac}\,U^{b\delta}

\displaystyle[U^{a\beta},T_{2}^{\gamma\delta}]

\displaystyle=\eta_{2}^{\beta\gamma}\,U^{a\delta}-\eta_{2}^{\beta\delta}\,U^{a\gamma}

\displaystyle[U^{a\beta},U^{c\delta}]

\displaystyle=\eta_{1}^{ac}\,T_{2}^{\beta\delta}-\eta_{2}^{\beta\delta}\,T_{1}^{ca}

Note that these are not the generators of
{\mathfrak{so}}(p_{1}+p_{2},q_{1}+q_{2})
because of the signs in the last line, which are due to the fact that
\Gamma_{1}^{a}
and
\Gamma_{2}^{\alpha}
commute. Instead, the generators are mapped to those of
{\mathfrak{so}}(q_{1}+p_{2},p_{1}+q_{2})
by defining
n_{1}=p_{1}+q_{1}
and

\displaystyle T^{ab}=-T_{1}^{ab}

(9)

\displaystyle T^{\alpha+n_{1},\beta+n_{1}}=T_{2}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle T^{a,\beta+n_{1}}=U^{a\beta}

\displaystyle T^{\alpha+n_{1},b}=-U^{b\alpha}

and
\eta=(-\eta_{1})\oplus\eta_{2}
.
∎

Note that by exchanging the two actions, one can also describe the Lie algebra as
{\mathfrak{so}}(q_{2}+p_{1},p_{2}+q_{1})
, which is isomorphic, as noted in Section
2.2
. The relative signature flip is a bit surprising, and one might wonder what happens with the commuting actions of three Clifford algebras. However in that case, the commutators of quadratic expressions do not close to a Lie algebra.

The representation determined by Theorem
1
is determined by spinor representations of the Lie algebra, as one might expect. The Hilbert space splits into irreducible representations of the two Clifford algebras, so it suffices to consider this case.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that there are irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{Cl}}(p_{1},q_{1})
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{k_{1}}
with gamma matrices
\gamma_{1}^{a}
, and
{\mathrm{Cl}}(p_{2},q_{2})
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{k_{2}}
with gamma matrices
\gamma_{2}^{\alpha}
. Then the representation of the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{so}}(q_{1}+p_{2},p_{1}+q_{2})
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{k_{1}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{k_{2}}
given by Theorem
1
with
\Gamma_{1}^{a}=\gamma_{1}^{a}\otimes 1
and
\Gamma_{2}^{\alpha}=1\otimes\gamma_{2}^{\alpha}
is equivalent to a Dirac spinor representation if
n_{1}=p_{1}+q_{1}
is even or
n_{2}=p_{2}+q_{2}
is even. If both
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are odd, the representation is one of the two Weyl spinor representations.

Proof.

Consider the odd-odd case. According to
[
3
]
, an irreducible Clifford module of type
(p,q)=(q_{1}+p_{2},p_{1}+q_{2})
is given by the gamma matrices

\begin{pmatrix}0&\gamma_{1}^{a}\\
-\gamma_{1}^{a}&0\end{pmatrix}\otimes 1,\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\gamma_{2}^{\alpha}

(10)

Note that the first set of matrices square to
-\eta_{1}^{aa}
, so the signature for these is reversed.
The quadratic monomials for this Clifford module are

\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-\gamma_{1}^{a}\gamma_{1}^{b}&0\\
0&-\gamma_{1}^{a}\gamma_{1}^{b}\end{pmatrix}\otimes 1,\quad\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}^{a}&0\\
0&-\gamma_{1}^{a}\end{pmatrix}\otimes\gamma_{2}^{\beta},\quad\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\gamma_{2}^{\alpha}\gamma_{2}^{\beta}.

(11)

generating the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{so}}(q_{1}+p_{2},p_{1}+q_{2})
.
The matrix
t=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\otimes 1
commutes with these generators, and so the
\pm
eigenspaces of
t
are the two different spinor representations. However, the
t=1
eigenspace is exactly the representation (
9
), which proves the result.

Now suppose
n_{1}
is even. Then an irreducible Clifford module of type
(q_{1}+p_{2},p_{1}+q_{2})
is given by the gamma matrices

i\gamma_{1}^{a}\otimes 1,\quad\gamma_{1}\otimes\gamma^{\beta}_{2}

(12)

using the chirality operator
\gamma_{1}
, which anti-commutes with the
\gamma_{1}^{a}
.
Therefore the quadratic expressions formed from these,

S^{ab}=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}^{a}\gamma_{1}^{b}\otimes 1,\quad S^{a,n_{1}+\beta}=\frac{i}{2}\gamma_{1}^{a}\gamma_{1}\otimes\gamma^{\beta}_{2},\quad S^{n_{1}+\alpha,n_{1}+\beta}=\frac{1}{2}1\otimes\gamma^{\alpha}_{2}\gamma^{\beta}_{2}

(13)

generate the Dirac spinor representation of
{\mathfrak{so}}(q_{1}+p_{2},p_{1}+q_{2})
. Define the operator
V=\exp(i\pi\gamma_{1}/4)\otimes 1
(which is unitary if the Clifford module is unitary). This has the property that
V(\gamma^{a}_{1}\otimes 1)V^{-1}=i\gamma_{1}\gamma^{a}_{1}\otimes 1
. This operator determines the equivalent representation

\displaystyle VS^{ab}V^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}^{a}\gamma_{1}^{b}\otimes 1=-T_{1}^{ab}

(14)

\displaystyle VS^{a,\beta+n_{1}}V^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}^{a}\otimes\gamma_{2}^{\beta}=U^{a\beta}

\displaystyle VS^{\alpha+n_{1},\beta+n_{1}}V^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}1\otimes\gamma^{\alpha}_{2}\gamma^{\beta}_{2}=T_{2}^{\alpha\beta}

which agrees exactly with (
9
). There is a similar argument if
n_{2}
is even.
∎

It remains to construct the structure maps for the spin representations constructed in Theorem
2
.
It is useful to write
p=q_{1}+p_{2}
and
q=p_{1}+q_{2}
. Then,
s=q-p=s_{2}-s_{1}
, with
s_{i}=q_{i}-p_{i}
. For the cases when
s
is even, the operator
P
can be defined so that its eigenvalues distinguish the Weyl spinor submodules in the results of Theorem
2
.
In the even-even case, multiplying the gamma matrices in (
12
) gives

P=(-1)^{n_{1}/2}P_{1}\otimes P_{2},

(15)

which is unchanged by the unitary transformation, i.e.,
VPV^{-1}=P
. In the odd-odd case, multiplying the gamma matrices shows that

P=(-1)^{(n_{1}-1)/2}P_{1}\otimes P_{2}.

(16)

An antilinear structure map
J
for the tensor product is obtained from a pair of structure maps, one for each factor, providing they either both commute with the gamma matrices or both anti-commute with the gamma matrices. The formula

J=J_{1}\otimes J_{2}

(17)

commutes with the Lie algebra in the even-even cases, the odd-odd cases where
s=0
or
4
, or the even-odd (and odd-even) cases
where
s_{1}
(or
s_{2}
) is
3
or
7
.
In the even-odd cases with
s_{2}=1
or
5
one has to take

J=\widehat{J}_{1}\otimes J_{2}

(18)

and similarly in the odd-even cases with
s_{1}=1
or
5
,

J=J_{1}\otimes\widehat{J}_{2}.

(19)

The remaining odd-odd cases don’t have a general formula for
J
because the Weyl spinor representation does not have an antilinear structure.
In the even-even cases there is also the antilinear structure map

\widehat{J}=JP=(-1)^{n_{1}/2}\widehat{J}_{1}\otimes\widehat{J}_{2}.

(20)

Examples

Instances of this construction in particle physics appear in
[
9
]
, including Examples
1
and
2
.

Example 1
.

The quaternion algebra
{\mathbb{H}}
has an irreducible representation
\sigma\colon{\mathbb{H}}\to M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
. Applied to the
imaginary quaternions
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}\in{\mathbb{H}}
this gives the gamma matrices
\gamma_{1}^{a}=\sigma(q_{a})
for a Clifford algebra
{\mathrm{Cl}}(0,3)
acting in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. The number
\gamma_{2}^{1}=i
acting in
{\mathbb{C}}
is the single gamma matrix for the type
(0,1)
Clifford algebra. The tensor product of the two underlying real algebras is
{\mathbb{H}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{C}}_{\mathbb{R}}=M_{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{\mathbb{R}})
, which acts in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
by matrix multiplication. The generators of
{\mathfrak{so}}(3,1)
are
T_{1}=\{\frac{1}{2}\sigma(q_{1}),\frac{1}{2}\sigma(q_{2}),\frac{1}{2}\sigma(q_{3})\}
and
U=\{\frac{i}{2}\sigma(q_{1}),\frac{i}{2}\sigma(q_{2}),\frac{i}{2}\sigma(q_{3})\}
and the representation in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is a Weyl spinor representation. This agrees with the holomorphic representation of
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.

Example 2
.

The quaternions
{\mathbb{H}}
and octonions
{\mathbb{O}}
can be considered real vector spaces. Let
\mathcal{H}={\mathbb{C}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{H}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{O}}
, a complex vector space by scalar multiplication in the first factor. This has the structure of a non-associative algebra (the Dixon algebra). Left multiplication by the imaginary octonions generates a module for
{\mathrm{Cl}}(0,7)
and left multiplication by
i
times the imaginary quaternions generates a module for
{\mathrm{Cl}}(3,0)
.
Applying Theorem
2
gives a representation of
{\mathrm{Spin}}(10)
on
\mathcal{H}\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{16}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
that is a Weyl spinor representation with multiplicity two.

The next example is new.

Example 3
.

Consider the tensor product of unitary irreducible modules for
{\mathrm{Cl}}(4,0)
and
{\mathrm{Cl}}(0,6)
, as in Theorem
2
. This results in the Dirac spinor representation of
{\mathrm{Spin}}(10)
on
\mathcal{H}={\mathbb{C}}^{4}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{8}={\mathbb{C}}^{32}
.

This construction has a distinguished subgroup of
{\mathrm{Spin}}(10)
, the Pati-Salam group

G_{PS}={\mathrm{Spin}}(4)\times{\mathrm{Spin}}(6)/{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
. It is shown in the next section that Example
3
can be used to construct spectral triples for the internal space of the Pati-Salam model.

4
Spectral triple

A real spectral triple
[
7
]
is a mathematical structure for a Dirac operator on a spin manifold or its non-commutative generalisation. There is a
*
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
generalising the algebra of functions in the manifold case. The fermion fields form a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
that is a bimodule over
\mathcal{A}
.
The real structure is an antilinear operator
J
that interchanges the left and right actions, generalising the real structure of a Clifford module. There is a Dirac operator
D
and a chirality operator
\gamma
on
\mathcal{H}
obeying the same relations with
J
as in Section
2.1
, with
D
in place of
\gamma^{a}
.

An example of a spectral triple is constructed here, starting from the data in Example
3
.
The real spectral triple is defined using the same Hilbert space
\mathcal{H},J=J_{1}\otimes J_{2},\gamma=\gamma_{1}\otimes\gamma_{2}
and algebra

\mathcal{A}={\mathrm{Cl}}(4,0)_{\text{even}}\oplus{\mathrm{Cl}}(0,6)_{\text{even}}\cong({\mathbb{H}}\oplus{\mathbb{H}})\oplus M_{4}({\mathbb{C}}).

(21)

Denote the projection onto the positive chirality eigenspace for the second action by
\pi_{2}^{+}
, and write
\pi_{2}^{-}=1-\pi_{2}^{+}
. Then
J\pi_{2}^{+}=\pi_{2}^{-}J
.

The left action of
a=(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathcal{A}
on
\mathcal{H}
is defined to be

l(a)=c_{1}(a_{1})\otimes\pi_{2}^{+}+1\otimes c_{2}(a_{2})\pi_{2}^{-}.

(22)

The right action of the spectral triple can be computed from it

r(a)=Jl(a^{*})J^{-1}=c_{1}(a_{1}^{*})\otimes\pi_{2}^{-}+1\otimes c_{2}(a_{2}^{*})\pi_{2}^{+}

(23)

and it can be seen that the
zeroth-order condition

[l(a),r(b)]=0

(24)

is satisfied, making
\mathcal{H}
a bimodule over
\mathcal{A}
. This part of the construction gives the same Hilbert space and algebra as the Connes-Chamsesddine spectral triples for the internal space of the Pati-Salam model
[
4
,
5
]
. An internal space for a particle physics model has the same fields but with the spacetime manifold replaced by a single point.

In noncommutative geometry, the gauge group consists of unitary elements of the algebra (
u^{*}=u^{-1}
) acting on
\mathcal{H}
by the adjoint action

u\mapsto l(u)r(u^{*}).

(25)

However, if one takes
all
unitary elements, the resulting group in this example is
{\mathrm{SU}}(2)\times{\mathrm{SU}}(2)\times{\mathrm{U}}(4)/{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, which is larger than
G_{PS}
. One has to add a rather ad hoc extra condition called Connes’ unimodular condition
[
8
]
to reduce the gauge group to
G_{PS}
. This condition is that
\det(l(u))=1
.

However in the current construction, the Clifford structure gives a natural alternative way of defining the gauge group. It is defined by the elements
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in{\mathrm{Spin}}(4)\times{\mathrm{Spin}}(6)
, which form a subgroup of the unitary elements of
\mathcal{A}
. This subgroup automatically satisfies the unimodular condition. The gauge group action on
\mathcal{H}
is the adjoint action

l(u)r(u^{*})=(c_{1}(u_{1})\otimes\pi_{2}^{+}+1\otimes c_{2}(u_{2})\pi_{2}^{-})(c_{1}(u_{1})\otimes\pi_{2}^{-}+1\otimes c_{2}(u_{2})\pi_{2}^{+})\\
=c_{1}(u_{1})\otimes c_{2}(u_{2}),

(26)

which gives a faithful action of the correct subgroup
G_{PS}\subset{\mathrm{Spin}}(10)
. Another bonus from this construction is that it automatically leads to the correct charges of the fermions in
\mathcal{H}
for one generation of the Standard Model in a conceptual way: it is because the representation of
G_{PS}
extends to a Dirac spinor representation of
{\mathrm{Spin}}(10)
by an application of Theorem
2
. This is the correct representation for the gauge charges of the fermion fields and their conjugates
[
2
]
.

Dirac operators for this spectral triple are defined by

D=c_{1}(d)\otimes 1

(27)

where
d
is any Hermitian element of
{\mathrm{Cl}}(4,0)_{\text{odd}}
. This can be written in terms of the gamma matrices as
D=d_{a}\gamma^{a}_{1}\otimes 1
for a vector
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
. The operator
D
is Hermitian, commutes with
J
, anticommutes with
\gamma
and satisfies the
first order condition

[[D,l(a)],r(b)]=0

(28)

for all
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
, defining a real spectral triple of
KO
-dimension
s=6
.

In an internal space, the physical interpretation of the components of the Dirac operator depends on the action of the gauge group,

D\mapsto l(u)r(u^{*})\,D\,l(u^{*})r(u).

(29)

The components of
D
