Off-equilibrium non-Gaussian fluctuations near the QCD critical point: an effective field theory perspective
Noriyuki Sogabe, and Yi Yin

Abstract
The non-Gaussian fluctuations of baryon density are sensitive to the presence of the conjectured QCD critical point. Their observational consequences are crucial for the ongoing experimental search for this critical point through the beam energy scan program at Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC). In the expanding fireball created in a heavy-ion collision, critical fluctuations would inescapably fall out of equilibrium, and require a systematic description within a dynamical framework. In this paper, we employ newly developed effective field theory (EFT) for fluctuating hydrodynamics to study the real-time critical non-Gaussian fluctuations of a conserved charge density. In particular, we derive the evolution equations for multi-point correlators of density fluctuations and obtain the closed-form solutions with arbitrary initial conditions that can readily be implemented in realistic simulations for heavy-ion collisions. We find that non-linear interactions among noise fields, which are missing in traditional stochastic hydrodynamics, could potentially contribute to the quartic (fourth order) fluctuations in scaling regime even at tree level in off-equilibrium situations.



1
Introduction

The search for the conjectured QCD critical point has triggered much recent interest in both theory and experiment.
This critical point is the endpoint of the first-order transition line, which separates the ordinary hadronic matter and quark-gluon plasma (QGP)
Stephanov et al. 1998
;
Stephanov et al. 1999
, and is considered as one of the landmarks on the phase diagram of strong interaction matter
Aprahamian et al. 2015
;
Busza et al. 2018
;
Bzdak et al. 2020
.
Many QCD-inspired models predict the existence of this critical point but disagree widely on its location (see ref
Fukushima and Hatsuda 2011
for a reivew).
Investigating the QCD critical point from the first principle lattice QCD calculation is currently formidable due to the sign problem at finite baryon density.
The future development in quantum computing may help solve this sign problem someday (e.g., refs
Ikeda et al. 2021
;
Honda et al. 2021
), but this is not today.
On the observational frontier, looking for the QCD critical point is one of the main goals of the second phase of the beam energy scan (BES) program at Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) at Brookhaven National laboratory
Bzdak et al. 2020
.
In addition, there are several other approved experiments anticipated in the coming years, such as the multipurpose detector (MPD) at the Nuclotron-based Ion Collider Facility (NICA) in Dubna and the compressed baryonic matter (CBM) experiment at the Facility for Antiproton and Ion Research (FAIR).

The experimental exploration of the QCD critical point decisively relies on the observational consequences of baryon density fluctuations which are enhanced with the growth of the critical correlation length
\xi
.
In equilibrium, their behaviors are fully determined by the equation of state (EoS) with a critical point.
However, those fluctuations would inescapably fall out of equilibrium in heavy-ion collisions because of the expansion of the fireball and the critical slowing down. There is mounting evidence indicating the off-equilibrium fluctuations differs from the equilibrium expectation not only quantitatively but also qualitatively
Berdnikov and Rajagopal 2000
;
Mukherjee et al. 2015
;
Yin 2018
;
Nahrgang et al. 2019
.
This entails the development of dynamical models for density fluctuations;
see ref.
An et al. 2022
which summarizes the current status of the dynamical modelling for BES physics.

The appropriate theoretical framework that serves this purpose is relativistic fluctuating hydrodynamics
Kapusta et al. 2012
.
In the conventional approach, a la, Landau-Lifshitz,
one adds white noise into hydrodynamic equations and fixes their magnitude by imposing fluctuation-dissipation theorem.
Besides simulating stochastic hydrodynamics directly, one can alternatively derive the time evolution equation for hydrodynamic correlators
Akamatsu et al. 2017
;
Stephanov and Yin 2018
;
Martinez and Schäfer 2019
;
An et al. 2020
.
For example, such an equation for the two-point correlator of density fluctuations in systems relevant to the QCD critical point search has been investigated based on Hydro-Kinetic
Akamatsu et al. 2019
and Hydro+
formalism
Stephanov and Yin 2018
;
Rajagopal et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2020
.

The primary goal of the present work is to study the dynamics of non-Gaussian fluctuations of a generic conserved density near a critical point characterized by higher point correlators.
Our study is motivated by the significant sensitivity of the non-Gaussian fluctuations to the growth of the critical correlation
Stephanov 2009
;
Asakawa et al. 2009
;
Stephanov 2011
.
In fact,
the measurement of non-Gaussianity in the fluctuation of produced proton multiplicities as a function of beam energy is considered as one of the key observables for the criticality
Adam et al. 2021
;
Abdallah et al. 2021
.
Recently, there has been significant progress in describing the real-time evolution of non-Gaussian fluctuations.
This includes the formulation of the evolution equation for higher point correlators of spatial-averaged order parameter field
Mukherjee et al. 2015
;
Mukherjee et al. 2016
and diffusive mode
An et al. 2021
, as well as the simulations of stochastic hydrodynamics
Nahrgang et al. 2019
.

In contrast to existing works,
in this paper, we consider newly developed effective field theory (EFT) on the Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) contour for fluctuting fluid
Crossley et al. 2017a
;
Crossley et al. 2017b
, see also refs.
Kovtun et al. 2014
;
Haehl et al. 2016
;
Jensen et al. 2018
.
This EFT is constructed from action principle and symmetry.
Given the difference in this formalism from the other approaches used in the above-mentioned works,
it would be useful to revisit the dynamics of the higher point hydrodynamic correlators using this EFT method.
Such a study will shed light on to what extent this new formalism would complement the existing ones.
Particularly,
the analysis so far has been limited to the Gaussian noise.
While this is sufficient to generate non-Gaussian density fluctuations in equilibrium with an appropriate critical EoS
Mukherjee et al. 2015
;
Nahrgang et al. 2019
;
An et al. 2021
,
the effects of non-linear interactions among noises on off-equilibrium non-Gaussian fluctuations remain unclear to date.

We have applied the familiar field theory techniques for deriving the Schwinger-Dyson equation to obtain the tree-level evolution equations for multi-point fluctuations of a generic conserved density which is assumed to be the order parameter near a critical point, see eq. (
79
) together with eqs. (
74
), (
3.3
), and (
3.3
).
We also derive the closed-form solutions to those equations with arbitrary initial conditions, (
4
), which are new in literature.
Those results are ready for implementation in realistic simulations for heavy-ion collisions.
We have included non-linear coupling among noise fields discussed recently by Jain-Kovtun
Jain and Kovtun 2020
, see eq. (
2.1
).
Those interactions are parametrized by a set of ”stochastic coefficients,” which do not enter into the hydrodynamic constitutive relation and are absent in stochastic hydrodynamics.
But as pointed out in ref.
Jain and Kovtun 2020
, they do contribute to the hydrodynamic behavior of four-point retarded density correlators.
For Gaussian and cubic fluctuations, we recover the previous results on evolution equations
An et al. 2021
(see also ref.
Mukherjee et al. 2015
) in hydrodynamic regime, but our expressions also apply to the scaling regime of a critical point.
Remarkably, we find that,
depending on the critical behavior of “stochastic coefficients,” the missing non-linear couplings among noise fields in the traditional fluctuating hydrodynamic do contribute to off-equilibrium quartic fluctuations.

This work is organized as follows. In section
2.1
,
we review the construction of EFT for a generic conserved density
Crossley et al. 2017a
;
Jain and Kovtun 2020
. Then, we define the multi-point correlators for density fluctuations in section
2.2
and derive their evolution equation following the textbook method of obtaining Schwinger-Dyson equation in section
2.3
.
After introducing two small expansion parameters, we show evolution equations for Wigner transformed correlator at tree-level in section
3
and acquire the closed-form solutions to those evolution equations in section
4
.
Section
5
is devoted to discussing the application of our result to the QCD critical point and related future directions.

2
Formalism

2.1
EFT for a conserved density

In this section, we review the ingredients of EFT for fluctuating hydrodynamics pertinent to the present study.
The interested reader might refer to the lecture notes
Liu and Glorioso 2018
for a pedagogical introduction.

Consider a many-body system described by a given microscopic theory.
One could imagine formally integrating out fast modes in the long-wavelength and low-frequency limit.
As a result, the system should be described by an effective action with a set of slow variables including conserved charge densities, i.e., hydrodynamic modes.
This work will focus on EFT for a generic conserved charge density
n
and assume
n
becomes the order parameter near a critical point.

We can define this EFT action
I
through the path integral representation of the generating functional
W
on the Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) contour,

\displaystyle Z={\rm e}^{{\rm i}W[A_{\rm r},A_{\rm a}]}=\int{\cal D}\psi_{\rm r}{\cal D}\psi_{\rm a}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}I[\psi_{\rm a},\psi_{\rm r};A_{\rm a},A_{\rm r}]}\,,

(1)

where
I
is a functional of the external gauge fields
A_{\rm r}
and
A_{\rm a}
, and the dynamical fields
\psi_{\rm r}
and
\psi_{\rm a}
. In SK formalism,
the
\rm r
-variables are related to the physical observables (the charge density
n
in the present case), while the
\rm a
-variables are the corresponding noise variables. One can also interpret
\psi_{{\rm r}}
and
\psi_{{\rm a}}
as the
\rm{U}(1)
phase degrees of freedom on each fluid element (see refs.
Crossley et al. 2017a
;
Liu and Glorioso 2018
for more details).

The EFT action
I
and the associated Lagrangian density
{\cal L}
must satisfy various symmetry and consistency requirements, as we now explain:

1.

The action
I
is invariant under the gauge transformation parametrized by an arbitrary
{\rm U}(1)
phase
\phi_{\rm r/a}
,

\displaystyle A_{{\rm r/a},\alpha}\to A_{{\rm r/a},\alpha}-\partial_{\alpha}\phi_{\rm r/a}\,,\quad\psi_{\rm r/a}\to\psi_{\rm r/a}+\phi_{\rm r/a}\,.

(2)

Therefore,
I
should be constructed from the gauge invariant combination:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{{\rm r/a},\alpha}=A_{{\rm r/a},\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}\psi_{\rm r/a}\,.

(3)

The chemical potential is gauge invariant and can be identified with the temporal component of
{\cal A}_{{\rm r},\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\mu={\mathcal{A}}_{r,0}\,.

(4)

2.

The EFT has a local shift symmetry:

\displaystyle\psi_{\rm r}\rightarrow\psi_{\rm r}+\zeta({\bm{x}})\,.

(5)

Physically, this is because each fluid element is free to make independent
{\rm U}(1)
phase rotations for time-independent
\zeta(\bm{x})
.
This shift symmetries will be absent when the global
{\rm U}(1)
symmetry is spontaneously broken (see refs.
Dubovsky et al. 2012
;
Crossley et al. 2017a
for further details).

3.

Fluctuation-dissipation relation is implemented through the invariance of the action under the so-called dynamical Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) transformation
Glorioso et al. 2017
;
Glorioso and Liu 2016
. In the “classical” limit that quantum fluctuations are small compared with the thermodynamic fluctuations, the dynamical KMS transformation is defined as

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{{\rm r},\alpha}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Theta{\cal A}_{{\rm r},\alpha}\,,\quad{\cal A}_{{\rm a},\alpha}\rightarrow\Theta{\cal A}_{{\rm a},\alpha}+{\rm i}\beta\Theta\partial_{t}{\cal A}_{{\rm r},\alpha}\,.

(6)

Here
\Theta={\cal P}{\cal T}
represents a
Z_{2}
discrete parity transformation and
\beta=T^{-1}
with
T
being the temperature.

4.

The unitarity of the underlying system requires that (suppressing the Lorentz indices)

\displaystyle I[\psi_{\rm r},A_{\rm r};\psi_{\rm a}=0,A_{\rm a}=0]=0\,,\qquad I^{*}[\psi_{\rm r},A_{\rm r};\psi_{\rm a},A_{\rm a}]=-I[\psi_{\rm r},A_{\rm r};-\psi_{\rm a},-A_{\rm a}]\,.

(7)

Given the above conditions,

{\cal L}
can be constructed from the double expansion in the a-fields and gradient.
Because of the requirement by the unitarity (
7
),

{\cal L}
has to comprise at least one power of a-field.
Up to fourth order in a-fields and gradient,
the resulting EFT Lagragian density reads
Crossley et al. 2017a
;
Jain and Kovtun 2020

\displaystyle{\cal L}

\displaystyle=n{\mathcal{A}}_{\rm r,0}-\lambda{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rm r}\cdot(\beta\partial_{t}{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rm r}-{\rm i}{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rm a})

\displaystyle\quad+{\rm i}\vartheta_{1}\left[({\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rm a}\cdot\beta\partial_{t}{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rm r})^{2}-{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rm a}^{2}(\beta\partial_{t}{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rm r})^{2}\right]-{\rm i}\vartheta_{2}{\bm{{\mathcal{A}}}}^{2}_{\rm a}(\beta\partial_{t}{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rm r}-{\rm i}{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\rm a})^{2}\,,

(8)

where
\lambda
is the conductivity and
\vartheta_{1,2}
are additional ‘‘stochastic coefficients’’ characterizing non-linear interactions among the density and noise fields.
1
1

1

The
\vartheta_{1,2}
defined in the present paper is different from those in ref.
Jain and Kovtun 2020
by a factor of
T^{2}
.

As usual, the current can be read from the variation of the action with respect to the external gauge field, e.g.,

\displaystyle J^{\alpha}_{{\rm r}}=\frac{\delta I_{\rm eff}}{\delta A_{{\rm a},\alpha}}\longrightarrow J^{0}_{\rm r}=n\,,\qquad{\bm{J}}_{\rm r}=-\lambda{\bm{\nabla}}\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)+2{\rm i}\lambda{\bm{\nabla}}\psi_{\rm a}+\ldots\,,

(9)

where
\ldots
denotes contributions from
\vartheta_{1,2}
.
The first term in
{\bm{J}}_{\rm r}
represents the familiar Fick’s law, while the second term accounts for the effect of Gaussian noise.
The usual diffusive equation can be obtained by varying
{\cal L}
with respect to
\psi_{\rm a}
and then setting all the a-fields to be zero.

In the absence of
\vartheta_{1,2}
,
the EFT action (
2.1
) is quadratic in a-field and can be matched with the traditional stochastic hydrodynamics for diffusive mode with white Gaussian noise
Liu and Glorioso 2018
.
The
\vartheta_{1}
-contribution to the Lagrangian density (
2.1
) is quadratic in noise field while
\vartheta_{2}
-contribution also contains cubic and quadratic interactions among noise fields.
Both contributions do not enter into the standard stochastic hydrodynamics.
As a prior, the cubic and quartic interactions among noise fields might be parametrized by a handful of “stochastic coefficients” even at fixed order in gradient.
The dynamical KMS symmetry fixes the form of those terms, as shown in ref.
Jain and Kovtun 2020
.
This demonstrates that one can employ the EFT approach to investigate non-linear noise-noise and noise-density interactions, systematically.

In what follows, we shall consider the EFT Lagrangian density (
2.1
) at vanishing external field as our starting point to analyze the fluctuation dynamics of density
n
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=n\partial_{t}\psi-\lambda{\bm{\nabla}}\psi\cdot(\beta{\bm{\nabla}}\mu-{\rm i}{\bm{\nabla}}\psi)

\displaystyle\quad+{\rm i}\vartheta_{1}\left[({\bm{\nabla}}\psi\cdot\beta{\bm{\nabla}}\mu)^{2}-({\bm{\nabla}}\psi)^{2}(\beta{\bm{\nabla}}\mu)^{2}\right]-{\rm i}\vartheta_{2}(\bm{\nabla}\psi)^{2}(\beta\bm{\nabla}\mu-{\rm i}\bm{\nabla}\psi)^{2}\,,

(10)

where we have used eq. (
4
).
Here and hereafter, we drop the subscript for
\psi\equiv\psi_{\rm a}
.
The chemical potential
\mu
and
n
will be related by equation of state (EoS) which encodes the information on the critical point.

As mentioned earlier, our study will focus on the non-Gaussian fluctuations near a critical point.

\vartheta_{1,2}
terms are at least fourth-order in gradient and can be safely dropped in the hydrodynamic regime
q\xi\ll 1
, where
q
is the typical momentum of fluctuating density mode and
\xi
is the critical correlation length. However, they could be potentially important for off-equilibrium non-Gaussian fluctuations in the scaling (critical) regime
q\xi\geq 1
, (c.f., eq. (
3.1
)).
As we shall see below,
those
\vartheta_{1,2}
-contributions indeed enter into the evolution equation for quartic density fluctuations even at tree-level.

2.2
Multi-point correlators

We are interested in the density fluctuation around a given hydrodynamic background

\displaystyle n(x)=(n(x))_{0}\,,\qquad\psi=0\,,

(11)

which corresponds to the saddle point of the action (
2.1
).
Here, we use
x=(t,\bm{x})
to denote space time coordinates and adopt the subscript
0
for the background value.
The background charge density satisfies the diffusive equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(n(x))_{0}=(F)_{0}(x)\,,\qquad F(x)=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\bm{J}}_{\rm r}\,,

(12)

To describe the correlation among the fluctuations around the background profile,

\displaystyle\delta n(x)=n(x)-(n(x))_{0}\,,

(13)

we define multi-point equal-time correlators,

\displaystyle W_{n}(t;\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n})\equiv\left<\prod^{n}_{i=1}\,\delta n(t,\bm{x}_{i})\right>_{\rm c}\,,

(14)

where the subscript “c” denotes the connected contribution and the average is given by

\displaystyle\langle\ldots\rangle=\frac{1}{Z}\int{\cal D}n{\cal D}\psi e^{{\rm i}I}(\ldots)\,.

(15)

2.2.1
Equilibrium

Before studying the real-time evolution of multi-point correlators (
14
), we here first review their properties in equilibrium.
In the present subsection, we shall assume the hydrodynamic background is static and homogeneous.

We begin by considering the free-energy functional of the form,

\displaystyle\beta{\cal E}[\delta n]=\int_{\bm{x}}\left[\frac{1}{2}a(\delta n)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}K({\bm{\nabla}}\delta n)^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}a^{\prime}(\delta n)^{3}+\frac{1}{4!}a^{\prime\prime}(\delta n)^{4}\right]\,,

(16)

where
\int_{\bm{x}}\equiv\int{\rm{d}}{\bm{x}}
,
a=\beta\,\mu^{\prime}
with prime denoting the derivative with respect to
n
with
T
fixed, and
K
corresponds to the surface tension.
Then, the multi-point equal-time correlator in equilibrium (denoted by bar),
\overline{W}_{n}
, is given by (c.f. eq. (
14
)),

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{n}(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n})=\left<\prod^{n}_{i=1}\delta n(t,\bm{x}_{i})\right>_{\rm c,{\rm eq}}\,,

(17)

where the equilibrium expectation can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\langle\ldots\rangle_{\rm eq}=\frac{1}{Z_{{\rm eq}}}\int{\cal D}\delta ne^{-\beta{\cal E}[\delta n]}(\ldots)\,,\qquad Z_{{\rm eq}}\equiv\int{\cal D}\delta ne^{-\beta{\cal E}[\delta n]}\,.

(18)

Below, we shall consider
\overline{W}_{n}
s in Fourier space,

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{n}(\bm{q}_{1},...,\bm{q}_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bm{y}}_{1}}\cdots\int_{{\bm{y}}_{n}}\overline{W}_{n}(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n})\delta\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{x}_{n}}{n}\right)\prod^{n}_{j=1}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\bm{q}_{j}\cdot{\bm{x}_{j}}}\,.

(19)

we can evaluate eq. (
17

\overline{W}_{2}

2.3
Schwinger-Dyson equations

In this section,
we shall follow the textbook derivation of Schwinger-Dyson equations from the path integral to obtain the analogous equations for
W_{n}
.

We begin by considering the one-point function:

\displaystyle\langle\delta n(x)\rangle=\frac{1}{Z}\int{\cal D}n{\cal D}\psi e^{{\rm i}I}\delta n(x)\,,

(25)

and replace
\psi(x)\to\psi(x)+\epsilon(x)
with
\epsilon(x)
being an arbitrary local function with a small magnitude.
Since this is just a field redefinition and the path integral integrates over all configurations, the L.H.S of eq. (
25
) should be the same.
Therefore, the change of the R.H.S. of eq. (
25
) must add to zero.
At first order in
\epsilon(x)
, we arrive at the condition

\displaystyle\left<\frac{\delta I}{\delta\psi(x_{1})}\delta n(x)\right>=0\,,

(26)

or equivalently

\displaystyle\left<\partial_{t_{1}}\delta n(x_{1})\delta n(x)\right>=\left<F(x_{1})\delta n(x)\right>\,.

(27)

By substituting eq. (
12
) into the above equation,
we have

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t_{1}}n(x_{1})\delta n(x)\rangle=\langle\delta F(x_{1})\,\delta n(x)\rangle\,,

(28)

where
\delta F=F-(F)_{0}
.
Similar steps give

\displaystyle\left<\partial_{t_{1}}n(x_{1})\prod^{n}_{j=2}\delta n(x_{j})\right>=\left<\delta F(x_{1})\prod^{n}_{j=2}\delta n(x_{j})\right>\,.

(29)

Now, we are ready to obtain the evolution equation for
W_{n}
.
For this purpose, we consider

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i=1}\partial_{t_{i}}\left<\prod^{n}_{i=1}\delta n(x_{i})\right>_{\rm c}\,,

(30)

where we can view
\langle\delta n(x_{i})\rangle_{\rm c}
as a function of

\displaystyle t=\left(t_{1}+\ldots+t_{n}\right)/n\,,\qquad\Delta t_{1}=t_{1}-t\,,\quad...\,,\quad\Delta t_{n-1}=t_{n-1}-t\,.

(31)

Noting
\partial_{t}=\sum^{n}_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}
,
we conclude that taking the equal-time limit
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\to t
on eq. (
30
) gives
\partial_{t}W_{n}(t;\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n})
.
On the other hand,
eq. (
30
) can be evaluated using eq. (
29
),
leading to the evolution equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W_{n}(t;\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rm equ.}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left<F(x_{i})\prod_{j\neq i}\delta n(x_{j})\right>_{\rm c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{t\to t^{\prime}}\left[\left<F(t,\bm{x}_{1})\,\prod^{n}_{j=2}\,\delta n(t^{\prime},\bm{x}_{j})\right>_{\rm c}+\left<F(t^{\prime},\bm{x}_{1})\,\prod^{n}_{j=2}\,\delta n(t,\bm{x}_{j})\right>_{\rm c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}}}\,,

(32)

where
{\cal C}_{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}}
denotes the n-cyclic permutation of
(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n})
.
For example, for a generic function
w(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{3})
, the operation gives

\displaystyle\left[w(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{3})\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{3}}}=w(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{3})+w(\bm{x}_{3},\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})+w(\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{3},\bm{x}_{1})\,.

(33)

On the last expression of eq. (
2.3
),
we have specified the prescription for taking the equal-time limit such that the results will not depend on whether
t-t^{\prime}\to 0^{+}
or
t-t^{\prime}\to 0^{-}
.

Before closing this section, we remark that the method presented in this section can equally apply to derive evolution equation for un-equal time correlators, see appendix.
B
for illustrative examples.

3
Evolution equations

3.1
Perturbative scheme

At this juncture, the Schwinger-Dyson equation (
2.3
) is exact and non-perturbative.
To make progress,
we consider an perturbative evaluation of eq. (
2.3
).

First, we expand the Lagrangian density (
2.1
) in fluctuating fields
\delta n
and
\psi
:

\displaystyle{\cal L}=({\cal L})_{1}+({\cal L})_{2}+({\cal L})_{3}+\ldots\,,

(34)

where the subscript of
(\ldots)
counts the power of fluctuating fields.
Note
({\cal L})_{1}
is simply a total derivative for expansion around the saddle point of the action.
Explicitly, we have from eq. (
2.1
),

\displaystyle({\cal L})_{2}

\displaystyle=-\psi\partial_{t}\delta n+\psi\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[\gamma\bm{\nabla}\left(1-c{\bm{\nabla}}^{2}\right)\delta n-{\rm i}\lambda\bm{\nabla}\psi\right]\,,

(35)

\displaystyle({\cal L})_{3}

\displaystyle=\psi\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{\prime}\bm{\nabla}(\delta n)^{2}-ac\lambda^{\prime}\delta n{\bm{\nabla}}({\bm{\nabla}}^{2}\delta n)-{\rm i}\lambda^{\prime}\delta n{\bm{\nabla}}\psi\right]\,,

(36)

\displaystyle({\cal L})_{4}

\displaystyle=\psi\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{6}\gamma^{\prime\prime}\bm{\nabla}(\delta n)^{3}-\frac{1}{2}ac\lambda^{\prime\prime}(\delta n)^{2}{\bm{\nabla}}({\bm{\nabla}}^{2}\delta n)-\frac{i}{2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(\delta n)^{2}\bm{\nabla}\psi\right]

\displaystyle\quad+{\rm i}\vartheta_{1}a^{2}\left[({\bm{\nabla}}\psi\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}(1-c{\bm{\nabla}}^{2})\delta n)^{2}-({\bm{\nabla}}\psi)^{2}\left(\bm{\nabla}(1-c{\bm{\nabla}}^{2})\delta n\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-{\rm i}\vartheta_{2}(\bm{\nabla}\psi)^{2}\,\left(a\bm{\nabla}(1-c{\bm{\nabla}}^{2}\right)\delta n-{\rm i}\bm{\nabla}\psi)^{2}\,,

(37)

where we have used eq. (
16
) to give the relation between
\delta\mu
and
\delta n
,

\displaystyle\beta\delta\mu=\beta\frac{\delta{\cal E}}{\delta n}=a\,\left(1-c{\bm{\nabla}}^{2}\right)\,\delta n+\frac{1}{2!}\,a^{\prime}(\delta n)^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}a^{\prime\prime}(\delta n)^{3}\,.

(38)

In the above expressions,
the diffusive constant is given by

\displaystyle\gamma=a\,\lambda\,.

(39)

The interacting Lagrangian density
\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}=({\cal L})_{3}+({\cal L})_{4}+\cdots
describes non-linear interaction among
\delta n
and noise field
\psi
.
They receive contributions depending on the variations of inverse susceptibility
a
and conductivity
\lambda
with respect to thermodynamic quantities.
The latter contribution, e.g.,
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}
, is sometimes referred to as the multiplicative noise effects because
\lambda
controls the magnitude of Gaussian noise in conventional stochastic hydrodynamics. Although those multiplicative noise contributions can involve two powers of fluctuating
density
fields in
{\cal L}_{{\rm int}}
, to say
\propto\psi\psi\delta n\delta n
, they only involve two powers of
a-field
and do not capture the non-linear interaction among the noise fields.
In contrast,
\vartheta_{2}
-contribution involves cubic and quartic power in noise-field, such as
\propto\psi\psi\psi\delta n
and
\propto\psi\psi\psi\psi
, so it represents a different class of noise-density interactions from multiplicative noise.

In hydrodynamic regime
q\xi\ll 1
, both surface tension (terms proportional to
c
) and
\vartheta_{1,2}
contribution are unimportant as they involve four powers of gradient.
However, in the scaling regime
q\xi\geq 1
, one can not even obtain the correct equilibrium expectation for
\overline{W}_{2}
without including
c
-contributions since
c\sim\xi^{2}
and hence is not suppressed by
q\xi
(see eq. (
20
)).
Similarly, we expect the significance of
\vartheta_{1,2}
contribution if they exhibit non-trivial
\xi
-dependence near the critical point.
As shown in ref.
Jain and Kovtun 2020
,

\vartheta_{1,2}
can be extracted from four-point retarded correlator for any given microscopic theory.
Currently, to the best of our knowledge, the critical behavior of “stochastic coefficients”
\vartheta_{1,2}
remains elusive but deserves investigation in the future.

Next, we set up a power counting scheme. Following the standard procedure in EFT, we introduce the rescaled fields:

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{n}=g\delta n\,,\qquad\tilde{\psi}=g^{-1}\psi\,,

(40)

where
g^{-2}=a^{-1}
is the equilibrium Gaussian fluctuation of
\delta n
per unit volume (see eq. (
20
)).
So, the Gaussian fluctuation of the rescaled variable
\delta\tilde{n}
will be the order of the unity (c.f. eq. (
20
)),
which is one primary motivation for the definition (
40
).
Even away from the critical point,
g
would become small when the degree of freedom per phase space volume, i.e., entropy density, is large.
For example, for the QGP,
g^{2}
will be suppressed if the number of color charge
N_{c}
and quark flavor
N_{f}
become large.
Near the critical point,

g^{2}
is suppressed as
\xi^{-2}
due to the growth of the correlation length
\xi
.
To make the dependence of
g
on entropy density and on correlation length more transparent,
we introduce the parametrization:

\displaystyle g=g_{0}\tilde{\xi}^{-1}\,,\qquad\tilde{\xi}\equiv\frac{\xi}{\xi_{0}}\,,

(41)

where
\xi_{0}
is the typical microscopic correlation length.
By design, we require
g_{0}
to be small for systems with large entropy density but is insenstive to the correlation length.

By expressing
({\cal L})_{2}
in terms of the rescaled field (
40
), it is easy to check that
({\cal L})_{2}\sim{\cal O}(g^{0})
(note
\lambda\sim g^{-2}
).
Turning to
({\cal L})_{n>2}
,
we first note that for a generic thermodynamic function
A
,

\displaystyle\frac{A^{\prime}}{A}=\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial A}{\partial\mu}\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\right)^{-1}\sim g^{2}\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial A}{\partial\mu}\,.

(42)

Since
A^{-1}\partial A/\partial\mu
should not depend on the density degrees of freedom, it can be counted as
O(1)
in a small
g_{0}
limit.
On the other hand, if
A
scales with correlation length near the critical point, so does its derivative with respect to
\mu
.
For definiteness,
we shall assume the critical point under consideration is in the 3d Ising model universality class so that
T
and
\mu
can be mapped to
r
and
h
, where
r
is the reduced Ising temperature and
h
is the Ising magnetic field.
In general,

\partial/\partial\mu
can be expressed as a linear combination of
\partial_{h}
and
\partial_{\rm r}
. Given that
\partial_{h}
gives stronger dependence on the correlation length, we then have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial A}{\partial\mu}\sim\frac{\partial_{h}A}{A}\sim\tilde{\xi}^{\frac{2\delta_{c}}{\delta_{c}-1}}\,,

(43)

where
\delta_{c}\approx 5
is the well-known Ising critical exponents.
This leads to the relation

\displaystyle\frac{A^{\prime}}{A}\delta n\sim g\tilde{\xi}^{\frac{2\delta_{c}}{\delta_{c}-1}}\delta\tilde{n}=g_{0}\tilde{\xi}^{\frac{\delta_{c}+1}{\delta_{c}-1}}\delta\tilde{n}=g_{\rm eff}\,,

(44)

where we have defined

\displaystyle g_{\rm eff}\equiv g_{0}(\tilde{\xi})^{\frac{\delta_{c}+1}{\delta_{c}-1}}=g_{0}(\tilde{\xi})^{\frac{3}{2}}\,.

(45)

This implies that
{\cal L}_{3}\sim(\partial{\cal L}_{2}/\partial n)\delta n
should be counted as
g_{\rm eff}
.
More generally, one can show by following the analogous steps that

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{n}\sim g^{n-2}_{\rm eff}\,,

(46)

meaning
g_{\rm eff}
can be employed as an effective coupling constant organizing the loop corrections to the tree-level results.
It also follows from eq. (
46
) that

\displaystyle\left<\prod^{n}_{i=1}\delta\tilde{n}\right>_{\rm c}\sim g^{n-2}_{\rm eff}\,,

(47)

and we consequently obtain the power counting for cumulants

\displaystyle W_{n}\sim g^{-n}g^{n-2}_{\rm eff}\sim g^{-2}_{0}\xi^{\frac{5(n-2)}{2}+n}\,,

(48)

which gives the familiar critical behavior for non-Gaussian fluctuations,
W_{3}\sim\xi^{9/2},W_{4}\sim\xi^{7}
.
The power counting for
W_{n}
works for equilibrium correlators and we shall assume this counting for off-equilibrium situations.

Now, we estimate the parametric behavior of loop corrections, which is simply given by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}\equiv g^{2}_{\rm eff}Q^{3}_{*}\,,

(49)

where
Q_{*}
denotes the characteristic loop momentum (see ref.
Sogabe et al. 2021
for an explicit demonstration of the counting (
49
)).
To obtain eq. (
49
), we have used the fact that propagators for rescaled fields is counted as order
1
.
The small parameters which bear certain similarity to
\epsilon_{0}
have been introduced in refs
Mukherjee et al. 2015
;
An et al. 2021
.

In this paper,
we shall work in the limit
\epsilon_{0}\to 0
and only keep tree-level contribution to the R.H.S. of the evolution equation (
2.3
).
We then have the following expression in terms of original fluctuating fields (not for the re-scaled fields (
40
)):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W_{2}(t;{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\langle(\delta F)_{1}(x_{1})\delta n(x_{2})\rangle_{\rm c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}}}\,,

(50a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W_{3}(t;{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{3})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\left<(\delta F)_{1}(x_{1})\,\prod^{3}_{j=2}\delta n(x_{i})\right>_{\rm c}+\left<(\delta F)_{2}(x_{1})\prod^{3}_{j=2}\delta n(x_{i})\right>_{\rm c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{3}}}\,,

(50b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W_{4}(t;{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{4})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\left<(\delta F)_{1}(x_{1})\prod^{4}_{j=2}\delta n(x_{i})\right>_{\rm c}+\ldots+\left<(\delta F)_{3}(x_{1})\prod^{4}_{j=2}\delta n(x_{i})\right>_{\rm c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{4}}}\,,

(50c)

where the prescription for taking the equal-time limit is specified below eq. (
2.3
).
The expansion of
\delta F
in fluctuating field,
(\delta F)_{n}
, can be obtained from the variation of
({\cal L})_{n+1}
with respect to
\psi
or directly by expanding
F
, e.g.,

\displaystyle(\delta F)_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[\gamma{\bm{\nabla}}(1-c{\bm{\nabla}}^{2})\delta n-2{\rm i}\lambda\bm{\nabla}\psi\right]\,,

(51)

\displaystyle(\delta F)_{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[\lambda a^{\prime}\delta n\bm{\nabla}\delta n+\lambda^{\prime}a\delta n\bm{\nabla}(1-c{\bm{\nabla}}^{2})\delta n-2{\rm i}\lambda^{\prime}\delta n\bm{\nabla}\psi\right]\,.

(52)

To evaluate the R.H.S of eq. (
50
) at tree level,
we can use Wick theorem and express
\langle\ldots\rangle_{\rm c}
in terms of
propagators:

\displaystyle G^{\rm rr}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\delta n(x_{1})\,\psi(x_{2})\rangle\,,

(53)

\displaystyle G^{\rm ra}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\delta n(x_{1})\,\psi(x_{2})\rangle\,,\qquad G^{\rm ar}(x_{1},x_{2})\equiv\langle\psi(x_{1})\,\delta n(x_{2})\rangle\,,

(54)

where the two-point function of
a
-field is zero by causality (see also appendix
A
).
We shall not present the explicit expression for the resulting (somewhat complicated) equations here, although obtaining them is straightforward.
Instead, in the next section,
we shall first assume an additional separation of scale and further simplify the evolution equations.

3.2
Patches

In many situations of interest,
the typical expansion rate
\omega
and gradient
\bm{k}
of the background profile
(n(x))_{0}
is much smaller than the characteristic momentum
q_{*}
of the fluctuating modes,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}\sim\frac{(\omega,k)}{q_{*}}\ll 1\,.

(55)

For example,
q_{*}
can be estimated by equating the diffusive rate of the fluctuating modes
\gamma q^{2}_{*}
with the expansion rate
\omega
, yielding
q_{*}\sim\sqrt{\omega/\gamma}
, which is parametrically larger than
\omega

Akamatsu et al. 2017
(see also refs.
An et al. 2020
).
Note,
q_{*}
is associated with the inverse of the Kibble-Zurek length near the critical point
Mukherjee et al. 2016
, see ref.
Akamatsu et al. 2019
for an estimation of this scale for heavy-ion collisions.
In what follows, we shall assume the limit eq. (
55
), as refs.
An et al. 2020
;
An et al. 2021
did.

When the separation of scale (
55
) is satisfied,
we can divide the whole system at a given time into many patches with typical length
l_{\rm patch}
satisfying
q^{-1}\ll l_{\rm patch}\ll k^{-1}
.
In this limit,
we can neglect the variation of the background fields within each patch and ignore the correlation of fluctuations at different patches.
In this sense, we may call those patches “locally homogeneous and causally disconnected.”
Then,
it is convenient to introduce the generalized Wigner transform (WT) of the multi-point correlator as well as their inverse
An et al. 2021
:

\displaystyle W_{n}(\bar{\bm{x}};\bm{q}_{1},...,\bm{q}_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bm{y}}_{1}}\cdots\int_{{\bm{y}}_{n}}W_{n}(\bar{\bm{x}}+\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bm{x}}+\bm{y}_{n})\delta\left(\frac{{\bm{y}}_{1}+\cdots+{\bm{y}}_{n}}{n}\right)\prod^{n}_{j=1}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\bm{q}_{j}\cdot{\bm{y}_{j}}}\,;

(56)

\displaystyle W_{n}(\bm{x}_{1},...,\bm{x}_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{q}_{1}}\cdots\int_{\bm{q}_{n}}\,W_{n}(\bar{\bm{x}};\bm{q}_{1},...,\bm{q}_{n-1})\,(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{\bm{q}_{1}+\ldots+\bm{q}_{n}}\,\prod^{n}_{j=1}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\bm{q}_{j}\cdot{\bm{x}_{j}}}\,.

(57)

Here, the integral in the momentum space is abbreviated as
\int_{{\bm{q}}}\equiv\int{\rm{d}}{\bm{q}}^{3}/(2\pi)^{3}
. For
n=2
, eq. (
56
) is reduced to the familiar expression for the Wigner function of Gaussian fluctuations. In eq. (
56
),

\bar{\bm{x}}
labels each patch, to say the position of its center of energy, while
\bm{q}_{i}
is the wave vector of the fluctuation field inside the patch.
In each patch,
the Wigner transform corresponds to the Fourier transform inside the patch.
With this understanding, we shall use Wigner transform and Fourier transform interchangeable when we refer to a patch.
Since
W_{n}
is invariant under the shift of all momentum
\bm{q}_{i}
by the same vector, we can impose the requirement
\sum_{i}\bm{q}_{i}=0
, meaning only
(n-1)

\bm{q}
s are non-redundant.
Hereafter, we shall drop
{\bm{q}}_{n}
in the argument of
W_{n}
to simplify our notations and write
W_{n}(t,\bar{\bm{x}};{\bm{q}}_{1},...,{\bm{q}}_{n-1})\equiv W_{n}(t,\bar{\bm{x}};{\bm{q}}_{1},...,{\bm{q}}_{n})
.

We are interested in deriving evolution equation for
W_{n}
in Fourier space.
Applying the generalized Wigner transform to eq. (
50
),
its L.H.S. will be replaced by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W_{n}(t,\bar{{\bm{x}}};\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n-1})=\cdots\,.

(58)

The remaining task is to evaluate the Wigner transform of the R.H.S..
For this purpose,
we must consider the Fourier transform of Lagrangian density
({\cal L})_{n}
and
\delta F
on each patch.

First,
by the virtue that intra-patch interaction among fluctuating fields is suppressed,
we can define Lagrangian density
{\cal L}
on a given patch
\bar{\bm{x}}
as

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{{\rm patch}}(\bm{x}=\bar{\bm{x}}+\bm{y})={\cal L}[(n(\bm{x}))_{0}\to(n(\bar{\bm{x}}))_{0};\delta n(\bm{x})\to\delta n(\bm{y}),\psi(\bm{x})\to\psi(\bm{y})]\,.

(59)

In another word,
for
\bm{x}=\bar{\bm{x}}+\bm{y}
lives in the patch
\bar{\bm{x}}
,
we replace the background fields
(n)_{0}(\bm{x})
and
(T)_{0}(\bm{x})
by
(n)_{0}(\bar{\bm{x}})
and
(T)_{0}(\bar{\bm{x}})
, and the fluctuating fields
\delta n(\bm{x})
and
\psi(\bm{x})
by
\delta n(\bm{y})
and
\psi(\bm{y})
, respectively. Next, on each patch, we consider the action in Fourier space.
For example,

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{y}}({\cal L}_{\rm patch})_{2}(\bar{\bm{x}}+\bm{y})=\int{\rm d}t\int_{\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}}\left[-\psi(\bm{q}_{1})\Gamma(\bm{q}_{2})\delta n(\bm{q}_{2})+{\rm i}\lambda(\bm{q}_{1}\cdot\bm{q}_{2})\psi(\bm{q}_{1})\psi(\bm{q}_{2})\right]\delta_{\bm{q}_{1}+\bm{q}_{2}}\,,

(60)

where the damping rate of the fluctuating modes at momentum
\bm{q}
is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{q})=\gamma\bm{q}^{2}f(q)\,,

(61)

with
f
defined in eq. (
21
).
Similarly,
we can introduce vertex functions
U_{n,m}
describing non-linear interaction among fluctuating fields from interacting part of the Lagrangian density (
n>2
),

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}t\int_{\bm{y}}({\cal L}_{{\rm patch}})_{n}(\bar{\bm{x}}+\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}t\int_{\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n}}\sum^{m_{0}(n)}_{m=1}\frac{1}{m}U_{n,m}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n})\prod^{m}_{i=1}\psi(\bm{q}_{i})\prod^{n}_{j=m+1}\delta n(\bm{q}_{j})\delta_{{\bm{q}}_{1},...,{\bm{q}}_{n}}\,,

(62)

where once again
n
counts the number of fluctuating fields, whereas
m
counts that of a-fields.
In eq. (
3.2
),

m_{0}(n)\leq n
is the maximum number of
a
-fields in
({\cal L})_{n}
, and one can read these numbers from the Lagrangian, e.g.,
m_{0}(3)=2
,
m_{0}(3)=4
.
By construction, we require
U_{n,m}
to be symmetric under each permutation over
\left({\bm{q}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{q}}_{m}\right)
and
\left({\bm{q}}_{m+1},\ldots,{\bm{q}}_{n}\right)
.
In the expression of
U
, the parameters,
\gamma,\lambda,\vartheta_{1,2}
, e.t.c., will depend on
(t,\bar{\bm{x}})
,
but we shall keep this dependence inexplicit.
Likewise, we will suppress
(t,\bar{\bm{x}})
-dependence in
W_{n}
s from now on.
Note the Lagrangian density considered in this work is even in spatial gradient, so that the vertex function is invariant under the spatial inversion:

\displaystyle U_{n,m}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n})=U_{n,m}(-\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,-\bm{q}_{n})\,.

(63)

With eqs. (
60
) and (
3.2
),
we obtain the Fourier transform of
(\delta F)_{n}

\displaystyle\left(\delta F\right)_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm WT}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2}}\left[-\Gamma(\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2})\delta n(\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2})+2{\rm i}\lambda(\bm{q}_{1}\cdot\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2})\psi(\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2})\right]\delta_{-\bm{q}_{1}+\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2}}\,,

(64)

and for
n>2
,

\displaystyle\left(\delta F\right)_{n-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm WT}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2},\ldots\bm{q}^{\prime}_{n}}\sum^{m_{0}(n)}_{m=1}\left[U_{n,m}(-\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,\bm{q}^{\prime}_{n})\prod^{m}_{i=2}\psi(\bm{q}^{\prime}_{i})\prod^{n}_{j=m+1}\delta n(\bm{q}^{\prime}_{j})\right]\delta_{-\bm{q}_{1}+\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2}+\ldots+\bm{q}^{\prime}_{n}}\,.

(65)

3.3
Evolution equations in Fourier space

We first consider the evolution equation for
W_{2}
characterizing Gaussian fluctuations.
The R.H.S of eq. (
50a
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\langle(\delta F)_{1}(t,\bm{x}_{1})\,\delta n(t_{2},\bm{x}_{2})\,\rangle_{\rm c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm WT}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\langle(\delta F)_{1}(t,\bm{q}_{1})\,\delta n(t^{\prime},\bm{q}_{2})\rangle_{\rm c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}}}\,,

(66)

can be divided into two parts from the decomposition
(\delta F)_{1}=(\delta F)_{1,0}+(\delta F)_{1,0}
, where
(\delta F)_{1,m}
with
m=0,1
being the
m
-th power of a-field:

\displaystyle\left(\delta F\right)_{1,0}(\bm{q}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2}}\Gamma(\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2})\delta n(\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2})\delta_{-\bm{q}_{1}+\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2}}\,,

(67)

\displaystyle\left(\delta F\right)_{1,1}(\bm{q}_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2}}2{\rm i}\lambda(\bm{q}_{1}\cdot\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2})\psi(\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2})\delta_{-\bm{q}_{1}+\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2}}\,.

(68)

The first part, corresponding to the contribution from
(\delta F)_{1,0}
, is given by

\displaystyle\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\langle(\delta F)_{1,0}(t,\bm{q}_{1})\delta n(t^{\prime},\bm{q}_{2})\rangle_{c}\right]_{\mathcal{C}_{{\bm{q}}_{1},{\bm{q}}_{2}}}=-2\Gamma(\bm{q}_{1})W_{2}(\bm{q}_{1})\,.

(69)

Turning to the second part coming from
(\delta F)_{1,1}
,
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\langle(\delta F)_{1,1}(t,\bm{q}_{1})\delta n(t^{\prime},\bm{q}_{2})\rangle_{\rm c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{{\bm{q}}_{1},{\bm{q}}_{2}}}=2\lambda\bm{q}^{2}_{1}\,,

(70)

where we have used the relation,

\displaystyle\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\langle\delta n(t,\bm{x}_{1})\,\psi(t^{\prime},\bm{x}_{2})\right]_{{\cal C}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm WT}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[G^{\rm ra}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q}_{1})+G^{\rm ar}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q}_{1})\right]=-{\rm i}\,.

(71)

To get eq. (
71
), we first note
G^{\rm ra}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})
and
G^{\rm ar}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})
should approach a common
\bm{q}
-dependent constant in the limit
t-t^{\prime}\to 0^{+}
and
t-t^{\prime}\to 0^{-}
, and vanish when
t<t^{\prime}
and
t>t^{\prime}
, respectively.
Then, we determine this constant to be
-{\rm i}
from the expression for
G^{\rm ra}
and
G^{\rm ra}
in the static background (
115
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{t-t^{\prime}\to 0^{+}}G^{\rm ra}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q}_{1})=-{\rm i}\,,\qquad\lim_{t-t^{\prime}\to 0^{-}}G^{\rm ar}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q}_{1})=-{\rm i}\,.

(72)

Combining eqs. (
69
) and (
70
),
we arrive at the evolution equation for
W_{2}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\,W_{2}(\bm{q}_{1})=\left[-\Gamma(\bm{q}_{1})W_{2}(\bm{q}_{1})\right]_{{\cal P}_{\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2}}}+S_{2}(\bm{q}_{1})\,,

(73)

where
{\cal P}_{\bm{q}_{n}}
denotes the permutation around
\left(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n}\right)
. Note this is different from the cyclic operation, e.g., (
33
), in general.
The source term for
W_{2}
is given by

\displaystyle S_{2}(\bm{q}_{1})=2\lambda\bm{q}^{2}_{1}\,.

(74)

Before continuing,
a further remark on the treatment of retarded/advanced response function,
G^{\rm ra}
and
G^{\rm ar}
, at equal time is due here.
When computing eq. (
2.3
),
we evaluate functions symmetric under the permutation of
(t,t^{\prime})
. Therefore,
G^{\rm ra}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})
and
G^{\rm ar}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})
should always appear in the combination as
G^{\rm ra}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})+G^{\rm ar}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})
and the results should not depend on whether
t-t^{\prime}\to 0^{+}
or
t-t^{\prime}\to 0^{-}
.
As a consequence,
we can practically implement eq. (
71
) with the prescription,

\displaystyle\lim_{\rm equ.}\,G^{\rm ra}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q}_{1})=\lim_{\rm equ.}G^{\rm ar}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q}_{1})=-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\,.

(75)

Now, we return to determine the evolution equations for non-Gaussian fluctuations.
First,
we observe the following generalization of eq. (
69
):

\displaystyle\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\left<\bm{\nabla}\left(\delta F\right)_{1,0}(t,\bm{q}_{1})\prod^{n}_{j=2}\delta n(t_{j},\bm{q}_{j})\right>_{\rm c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n}}}=-n\,\Gamma(\bm{q}_{1})\,W_{n}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n-1})\,.

(76)

Moreover, due to the causal structure of SK EFT,
we have the relation (see appendix.
A
for a derivation)

\displaystyle\lim_{\rm equ.}\left<\left({\bm{\nabla}}^{2}\psi\right)(t^{\prime},\bm{x}_{1})\prod^{n}_{j=2}\delta n(t,\bm{x}_{j})\right>_{\rm c}=0\,,\qquad(\text{for }n>2)\,,

(77)

which implies

\displaystyle\lim_{t^{\prime}\to t}\left<\bm{\nabla}\left(\delta F\right)_{1,1}(t^{\prime},\bm{x}_{1})\prod^{n}_{j=2}\delta n(t,\bm{x}_{j})\right>_{\rm c}=0\,,\qquad(\text{for }n>2)\,.

(78)

As detailed in appendix.
A
,
eq. (
77
) is general and holds for arbitrary
\epsilon_{0}
and
\epsilon_{1}
.
With eqs. (
76
) and (
77
) at hand,
the equations for
W_{3}
and
W_{4}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W_{n}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n-1})=\left[-\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\Gamma(\bm{q}_{1})W_{n}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n-1})\right]_{{\cal P}_{\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n}}}+S_{n}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n})\,,

(79)

where the source terms are given by

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\left<\left(\delta F\right)_{2}(\bm{q}_{1})\,\prod^{3}_{j=2}\delta n(\bm{q}_{j})\right>_{c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{3}}}\,,

(80)

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rm equ.}\left[\left<\left(\delta F\right)_{2}(\bm{q}_{1})\,\prod^{4}_{j=2}\delta n(\bm{q}_{j})\right>_{\rm c}+\left<\left(\delta F\right)_{3}(\bm{q}_{1})\,\prod^{4}_{j=2}\delta n(\bm{q}_{j})\right>_{\rm c}\right]_{{\cal C}_{\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{4}}}\,.

(81)

65

\displaystyle\langle(\delta F)_{n-1}(\bm{q}_{1})\,\prod^{n}_{l=2}\delta n(\bm{q}_{l})\rangle_{\rm c}

\displaystyle=\sum^{n-1}_{m=1}\left[U_{n,m}(-\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,-\bm{q}_{n})\left(-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\right)^{m-1}\prod^{n}_{j=m+1}W_{2}(\bm{q}_{j})\right]_{{\cal P}_{\bm{q}_{2},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n}}}+\cdots

\displaystyle=\sum^{n-1}_{m=1}\left[U_{n,m}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n})\left(-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\right)^{m-1}\prod^{n}_{j=m+1}W_{2}(\bm{q}_{j})\right]_{{\cal P}_{\bm{q}_{2},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n}}}+\cdots\,,

(82)

where
{\cal P}_{{\bm{q}}_{2},\ldots,{\bm{q}}_{n}}
arises from

ways to assign
\bm{q}_{2},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n}
to
-\bm{q}^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,-\bm{q}^{\prime}_{n}
in

and we have used eq. (
63
).
Note eq. (
3.3

\cdots

\displaystyle S_{3}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{3})

\displaystyle=\left[\sum^{2}_{m=1}U_{3,m}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{3})\left(-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\right)^{m-1}\prod^{3}_{j=m+1}W_{2}(\bm{q}_{j})\right]_{{\cal P}_{\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{3}}}\,,

(83)

\displaystyle S_{4}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{4})

\displaystyle=\left[\sum^{4}_{m=1}U_{4,m}(\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{4})\left(-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\right)^{m-1}\prod_{j=m+1}W_{2}(\bm{q}_{j})\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\left.\sum^{2}_{m=1}U_{3,m}(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},-\bm{q}_{12})\left(-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\right)^{m-1}\left(W_{2}(\bm{q}_{2})\right)^{1-\delta_{m2}}W_{3}(\bm{q}_{3},\bm{q}_{4})\right]_{{\cal P}_{\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{4}}}\,,

The solution

We are interested in obtaining the solutions to the evolution equation (

5
Summary and outlook

In the view that non-Gaussian fluctuations of baryon density would lead to the important observational signature on the presence of the conjectured QCD critical point,
we have studied the multi-point fluctuations of a generic conserved density based on novel EFT formalism for hydrodynamic fluctuations.
We obtain not only evolution equations for multi-point equal-time correlators of density fluctuation (see eqs. (
79
), (
74
), (
83
), and (
3.3
)), which is related to the Schwinger-Dyson equation in the field theory language,
but also their solutions expressed in terms of the response function (propagator) (
4
) for arbitrary initial conditions.
To the best of our knowledge, the present work is the first one that applies the EFT formalism to study non-Gaussian fluctuations of a diffusive mode near a critical point.
Our work demonstrates that this EFT approach can be utilized to investigate this intriguing problem systematically based on familiar field theoretical methods.

While the traditional stochastic hydrodynamics only includes Gaussian white noise,
we find non-linear interactions among noise fields, parametrized by (new) “stochastic coefficients”
\vartheta_{1,2}
in eq. (
2.1
)
Jain and Kovtun 2020
, contribute additional to quartic fluctuations (
n=4
) when the system is out of equilibrium.
Given their potential importance, it would be interesting and instructive to understand the critical behavior of those coefficients in general and in QCD system specifically.

We focus on the pure diffusive mode and does not account for the full interaction among hydrodynamic modes.
We hope that the main features we obtain here will survive in future more comprehensive studies.
This anticipation’s validity should be checked with the full hydrodynamic EFT theory as shown in refs.
Crossley et al. 2017a
;
Crossley et al. 2017b
.
That said,
in the view toward providing quantitative and benchmark guidance for the BESII data anticipated in the upcoming years,
we believe that incorporating the present results, including the compact form solution to the evolution equation, (
4
) in realistic modelings, as was done previously for Gaussian fluctuations in refs.
Rajagopal et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2020
, is a more immediate priority.

For the conjectured QCD critical point,
the slowest hydrodynamic mode is entropy per baryon density,
m\equiv s/n

Stephanov and Yin 2018
;
Akamatsu et al. 2019
.
It is also shown in ref.
Akamatsu et al. 2019
that the Gaussian fluctuation of this mode
\delta m
will always diverge with the largest Ising critical exponent
\gamma\approx 1.23
.
We therefore expect that one should use evolution equation (
79
) as well as the solution eq. (
4
) with the replacement for the QCD critical point (see ref.
An et al. 2021
):

\displaystyle\delta n\to\delta m\,,\qquad a\to\frac{n^{2}}{c_{p}}\,,\qquad\lambda\to\kappa\,,

(104)

where
c_{p}
is the heat capacity at constant pressure and
\kappa
is thermal conductivity.
In a realistic situation, other refinements are needed. For example, the interpretation of
t
in those equations should be elaborated
An et al. 2020
and might be related to the affine parameter parametrizing the streamline in the background hydrodynamic solution.
To make contact with experimental observations, a freezeout prescription for
W_{n}
s has to be formulated
Pradeep et al. 2021
(see also refs.
Oliinychenko and Koch 2019
;
Oliinychenko et al. 2020
).

To obtain the evolution equation (
79
), we have assumed the smallness of two expansion parameter
\epsilon_{0}
and
\epsilon_{1}
, defined in eqs. (
49
) and (
55
), respectively.
The former controls the loop corrections to the present tree-level results, while the latter, if is small, justifies dividing the whole system into a collection of locally homogeneous patches.
For given hydrodynamic background, whether
\epsilon_{1}
is small or not can be checked directly , along the line of section IV. (E) in ref.
Du et al. 2020
.
Even in the case that
\epsilon_{1}
is not small, one can return to the evolution equation in real space (
50
) which is valid for arbitrary
\epsilon_{1}
.
The importance of the loop corrections
\epsilon_{0}
also needs to be examined, which we defer to future work.

Appendix B
The evolution equations for un-equal time correlators

In the main body of the present work, we focus on the equal-time correlators of density fluctuations.
Our method can be readily generalized to study un-equal time correlators, such as
G^{\rm rr}(x,x^{\prime})
defined in eq. (
53
).
From Schwinger-Dyson equation (
29
), we have:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G^{\rm rr}(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\delta F(x)\,\delta n(x^{\prime})\rangle\,.

(111)

Assuming
\epsilon_{0}
is small, we could keep the tree level contribution which comes from
(\delta F)_{1}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G^{\rm rr}(x,x^{\prime})=\langle(\delta F)_{1}(x)\,\delta n(x^{\prime})\rangle\,,\qquad t>t^{\prime}\,.

(112)

Without losing generality, we assume
t>t^{\prime}
and consequently
(\delta F)_{1,1}
will not contribute because of LTE (
105
).
Further consider the limit
\epsilon_{1}\to 0
and apply the Wigner transform to eq. (
112
),
we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G^{\rm rr}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})=-\Gamma(\bm{q})\,G^{\rm rr}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})\,,\qquad t>t^{\prime}\,.

(113)

Solving eq. (
113
) with the initial condition
G(t,t;\bm{q})=W_{2}(t;\bm{q})
,
we obtain eq. (
102
).
Following a similar procedure,
we have the equation for
G^{\rm ra}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}G^{\rm ra}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})=-\Gamma(\bm{q})\,G^{\rm ra}(t,t^{\prime};\bm{q})\,,\qquad t>t^{\prime}\,.

(114)

with the initial condition eq. (
71
).
This leads to eq. (
101
).

We can test eq. (
71
) by considering the static background.
In this situation, the tree-level propagators can be read from eq. (
35
):

\displaystyle G^{\rm ra}(t,t^{\prime};{\bm{q}})

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\,{\rm e}^{-\Gamma(\bm{q})(t-t^{\prime})}\,,\qquad G^{\rm ar}(t,t^{\prime};{\bm{q}})=-{\rm i}\theta(t^{\prime}-t)\,{\rm e}^{-\Gamma(\bm{q})(t-t^{\prime})}\,,

(115)

which agrees with eq. (
71
).
