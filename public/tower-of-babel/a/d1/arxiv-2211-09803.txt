Exact Quantum Algorithms for Quantum Phase Recognition: Renormalization Group and Error Correction Preprint: MIT-CTP/5498
Ethan Lake, Shankar Balasubramanian, Soonwon Choi

Abstract
We explore the relationship between renormalization group (RG) flow and error correction by constructing quantum algorithms that exactly recognize 1D symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases protected by finite internal Abelian symmetries. For each SPT phase, our algorithm runs a quantum circuit which emulates RG flow: an arbitrary input ground state wavefunction in the phase is mapped to a unique minimally-entangled reference state, thereby allowing for efficient phase identification. This construction is enabled by viewing a generic input state in the phase as a collection of coherent ‘errors’ applied to the reference state, and engineering a quantum circuit to efficiently detect and correct such errors. Importantly, the error correction threshold is proven to coincide exactly with the phase boundary. We discuss the implications of our results in the context of condensed matter physics, machine learning, and near-term quantum algorithms.

I
Introduction

The renormalization group (RG) is one of the most important concepts to arise in theoretical physics over the past century
Kadanoff 1966
;
Wilson and Fisher 1972
;
Wilson 1975
;
Gell-Mann and Low 1954
. Given a microscopic description of a many-body system, the framework of RG provides a systematic way of extracting the universal features capturing the system’s low-energy physics. RG is an indispensable tool in condensed matter physics, where it is used to classify phases of matter.

It is interesting to note that there is an important concept in information theory which shares many parallels with RG, namely that of error correction (EC). Writ large, EC is a class of techniques developed to protect information (both classical and quantum) from the effects of noise. The key idea of EC is to hide a small amount of important logical information redundantly in a larger number of physical degrees of freedom, and it shares with RG the ability to separate universal information from unimportant details. Due to this conceptual similarity, there have been several works drawing connections between RG and EC from a variety of perspectives, ranging from the theory of quantum channels to the physics of black holes
Furuya et al. 2022
;
Furuya et al. 2021
;
Gómez 2021
;
Almheiri et al. 2015
;
Pastawski et al. 2015
;
Kim and Kastoryano 2017
;
Yang et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Cong et al. 2019
;
Cong et al. 2022
.
However, these connections are often heuristic, based on empirical findings, or else appear in highly formalized settings.

In this work, we establish an explicit connection between RG and EC by presenting a class of quantum circuit algorithms that solve quantum phase recognition (QPR) problems. Given a ground state wavefunction
|\psi\rangle
and a phase of matter
\mathsf{P}
, the QPR problem asks whether or not
|\psi\rangle
belongs to
\mathsf{P}
.
Our quantum circuits efficiently solve this problem for a large class of gapped 1D symmetry-protected topological (SPT) and symmetry-breaking phases. We do this by implementing a simple form of error correction in a hierarchical multi-scale way, in a manner which emulates RG flow (Fig.
1
a). We show that the convergence of this RG flow coincides
exactly
with the phase boundary of
\mathsf{P}
,
implying that our algorithm faithfully recognizes input wavefunctions even very close to phase boundaries.
Our circuit is built from 3-local gates with a depth of at most
O(\log(L))
, and has a substantially smaller sample complexity compared to more conventional approaches that directly measure order parameters, making it a practical solution for near-term quantum hardware.

Our RG quantum circuits are inspired by the quantum convolutional neural network (QCNN) architecture introduced in
Cong et al. 2019
and experimentally demonstrated in
Herrmann et al. 2021
, and have some conceptual similarities with the measurement-based approach of Refs.
Bartlett et al. 2010
;
Miller and Miyake 2015
. For our purposes, a QCNN is a unitary circuit
\mathcal{Q}
built from layers of local unitaries chained together in a hierarchical fashion, similar to the multiscale entanglement renormalization ansatz (MERA)
Vidal 2008
;
Aguado and Vidal 2008
and tree tensor networks.
The hierarchical nature of
\mathcal{Q}
originates from an iterative filtering of the Hilbert space which occurs at each layer of the circuit. At a given layer,
\mathcal{Q}

effectively separates the system’s Hilbert space into two subspaces,
\mathcal{H}_{re}
and
\mathcal{H}_{an}
. The
renormalized subspace

\mathcal{H}_{re}
contains error-corrected degrees of freedom that are further renormalized in subsequent layers, while the
ancilla subspace

\mathcal{H}_{an}
stores detailed microscopic information about the detected errors, and the degrees of freedom therein do not participate in further layers (Fig.
1
b).

The nontrivial task we address in this paper is to design a single universal circuit
\mathcal{Q}
that performs phase recognition on
all
input states
|\psi\rangle
.
We accomplish this by addressing three challenges:
(i) constructing a local EC protocol that isolates non-universal information present in
|\psi\rangle
and deposits it in
\mathcal{H}_{an}
,
(ii) ensuring any input state in
\mathsf{P}
flows under our circuit to a unique fixed point
|\Psi_{\mathsf{P}}\rangle
, while simultaneously ensuring that any state outside of
\mathsf{P}
does
not
flow to
|\Psi_{\mathsf{P}}\rangle
, and
(iii) efficiently performing phase recognition given the converged fixed point wavefunction.
As we show, these desiderata can be all satisfied by a simple analytic construction that does not require any numerical optimization or training.

We emphasize that in the scenario under consideration, the input to the QPR problem is a quantum wavefunction
|\psi\rangle
— which may be handed to us from a physical experiment — rather than a tensor network or other efficient classical description thereof.
This means that we are not allowed to perform any nonlinear data processing that requires a classical description of an input state, such as probing the state’s entanglement structure, or performing tensor-network renormalization
Pollmann et al. 2010
;
Pollmann and Turner 2012
;
Gu and Wen 2009
;
Levin and Nave 2007
;
Evenbly and Vidal 2015
.
While a classical description can always be obtained using quantum state tomography, such an approach will generically require a large sample complexity, and is conceptually distinct from the RG-inspired treatment we propose.

II
Recognizing 1D Symmetry-Protected Topological phases

II.1
Overall strategy

Before presenting technical results, we briefly review the definition of gapped phases in 1D, establish notation, and summarize the overall strategy of our approach.

Two gapped local Hamiltonians are said to be in the same phase if and only if their ground states
|\psi\rangle
and
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
are related by a constant-depth local circuit
\mathcal{U}
satisfying
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle=\mathcal{U}|\psi\rangle
up to negligible corrections
Albash and Lidar 2018
.
Each gapped phase
\mathsf{P}
can be thus defined as an equivalence class with a representative element
|\Psi_{\mathsf{P}}\rangle
, where all other ground states in
\mathsf{P}
can be written as
|\psi\rangle=\mathcal{U}_{\psi}|\Psi_{\mathsf{P}}\rangle
for some constant-depth circuit
\mathcal{U}_{\psi}
that depends on the microscopic details contained in
|\psi\rangle
.
Here we focus on a class of Hamiltonians possessing an internal unitary Abelian symmetry
G
, which is unbroken in their ground states
1
1

1

A similar approach is possible for cases in which
G
is spontaneously broken; see App.
G
for the details.
.
In this restricted setting,
symmetric
local circuits similarly define distinct gapped phases of matter, called symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases
Chen et al. 2013
;
Huang et al. 2015
;
Schuch et al. 2011
;
Senthil 2015
.

The classification of SPT phases in 1D has by now been fully worked out, and can be understood from the entanglement structure of the ground state wavefunction.
Consider acting with a symmetry transformation on some subregion
A
.
In non-trivial SPT phases, the wavefunction remains unchanged on the interior of
A
, but the quantum correlations between
A
and its complement become ‘twisted’ near the boundaries of
A
, creating a pair of excitations that cannot be eliminated locally in a symmetry-preserving way.
Each of these excitations individually transform under a
projective
representation
V^{(\omega)}_{g}
of
G
. While
V^{(\omega)}_{g}
is in general not a linear representation of
G
, the combined symmetry action on both excitations, i.e.
V^{(\omega)*}_{g}\otimes V^{(\omega)}_{g}
, is always a linear representation.
Thus, the excitation associated with
V^{(\omega)}_{g}
can be loosely thought of as carrying a ‘fractionalized’ symmetry charge.
The distinct choices of
V^{(\omega)}_{g}
are determined by algebraic objects
\omega
known as second cohomology classes of
G
, and these in turn enumerate the distinct SPT phases, which we denote by
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
.

Given a symmetry
G
and a target phase
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, we construct our phase recognition algorithm in three steps:

Step 1
: we choose a specific reference state
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
for
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
. This reference state can be constructed directly using the matrices
V^{(\omega)}_{g}
and satisfies a number of desired conditions, to be elaborated on below.

Step 2
: we identify a set of
indicator observables

\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g}
that exactly recognize the desired reference state
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
, while rejecting reference states for other phases:

{\langle\Psi_{\omega}}|\mathcal{S}^{(\omega^{\prime})}_{g}|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle=\delta_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}.

(1)

In the present setting, the indicator observables can be chosen to be an appropriate set of string order parameters
Pollmann and Turner 2012
.

Step 3
: we construct a hierarchical unitary circuit
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}

(Fig. 1b) such that after a sufficiently large number of layers, applying
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
to any input state in the target phase
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
yields
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle\otimes|\phi_{an}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{re}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{an},
where
|\phi_{an}\rangle
stores the non-universal information present in
|\psi\rangle
. In essence, the role of
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
is to approximately ‘undo’ the action of the circuit
\mathcal{U}_{\psi}
appearing in
|\psi\rangle=\mathcal{U}_{\psi}|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
, and to do so without explicit knowledge of
\mathcal{U}_{\psi}
(Fig. 1a, red arrows).
Note that under our circuit, all such
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
flow to the fixed point
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
without violating unitarity: this is made possible by the presence of
\mathcal{H}_{an}
, which serves as a bath that stores all non-universal information about the input state.
At the same time, we design
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
such that for all input states in distinct phases
|\psi_{\omega^{\prime}}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega^{\prime}}
with
\omega^{\prime}\neq\omega
, the output state on
\mathcal{H}_{re}
never flows to
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
, thus preventing ‘false positives’ from occurring.

Combined together, our goal is to ensure that
\langle\psi|\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{an})\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}|\psi\rangle
is asymptotically close to
1
if
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, and asymptotically close to zero otherwise.
If this is achieved, the sharp difference in the value of the indicator observable in the two cases enables us to efficiently answer the QPR problem with high confidence, using only a small number of measurements.
In other words, running the circuit
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
and subsequently measuring
\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g}
constitutes an exact quantum algorithm to recognize the phase
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, and it does so with low sample complexity.

We should note that one could always simply directly measure the indicator observables in the input state, without first acting with the circuit
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
.
While in principle such an approach will generically work, the number of measurements required to get sufficiently good statistics can be very large, especially when input states lie close to phase boundaries (where the expectation values of
\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g}
often become vanishingly small).
Our algorithm (namely the application of the RG circuit
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
) eliminates this problem.

The success of our approach hinges on the construction of

\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
, which we perform using ideas inspired by error correction.
Specifically, we take the viewpoint that any input state in
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}

should be thought of as a collection of coherent ‘errors’ applied to the reference state.
The precise types of errors that arise are identified in Theorem
1
below, which provides the foundation needed to design
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
.
Intuitively, our Theorem states that for ground states of SPT phases, the errors can be represented as collections of operators that create pairs of (virtual) fractionalized excitations (this perspective is closely related to the operational viewpoint on topological phases adopted in Refs.
Jamadagni and
Weimer 2022a
;
Jamadagni and
Weimer 2022b
).
As a single local error results in a pair of such excitations,
one can leverage this redundancy as a primitive for error correction.
Specifically, by detecting fractionalized excitations supported on parts of a system, one can identify occurrences of local errors, and correct for their effects in the remaining system.
Our RG circuit
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
is constructed based on this inuition while still evolving the wavefunction in a unitary way.

II.2
Reference states and indicator observables

Our main technical idea is to utilize tensor network methods to construct both
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
.
For concreteness, let us consider a periodic 1D chain consisting of
L
qudits.
As inputs to the QPR problem, we are given the symmetry group
G
, its unitary representation
R_{g}
,
g\in G
, a target phase
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, and the phase’s associated projective representation
V^{(\omega)}_{g}
.
To simplify our presentation, we will focus on a subclass of phases whose projective representations are
maximally non-commutative
, meaning that for all
g\neq e\in G
,
V_{g}^{(\omega)}
fails to commute with at least one
V_{h}^{(\omega)}

;

;
Stephen et al. 2017

Most familiar SPT phases satisfy this property (such as the Haldane phase);
an example of one which does not is the nontrivial SPT phase protected by

The generalization of our construction to arbitrary Abelian
G

Finally, to simplify notation we will also assume that the local Hilbert space

Under these assumptions,

II.3
Constructing the RG circuits

Having determined the representative wavefunction and indicator observables, we turn to the most non-trivial step in our construction: finding the RG circuit
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
.
To do so, we first show that any
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
can be understood as
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
locally deformed by a collection of ‘errors’, which take the form of products of local symmetry actions applied at each site (see Fig.
3
):

Theorem 1
.

A generic ground state wavefunction
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
may be written as

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{g}\in G^{L}}C_{\mathbf{g}}R_{\mathbf{g}}|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle,\qquad R_{\mathbf{g}}\equiv\bigotimes_{i}R_{g_{i}},

(5)

where the complex coefficients
C_{\mathbf{g}}
characterize the amplitude for a collection of errors labeled by the string of group elements
\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{L})
to occur,
with the errors being correlated over a finite distance set by the correlation length
\xi
of
|\psi\rangle
.

This Theorem strongly constrains the types of errors that our circuits need to correct, namely only those errors caused by local symmetry operators
R_{g}
.
Since all of the
R_{g}
commute in our case, the error correction process we will design will be essentially classical in nature: this is made possible by the presence of the symmetry
G
, which prevents ‘bit flip’ errors (
i.e.
those off-diagonal in our choice of basis) from arising.

The proof of this Theorem proceeds by first studying the general structure of symmetric finite-depth quantum circuits
\mathcal{U}_{\psi}
and finding an appropriate basis of operators in which to decompose the action of
\mathcal{U}_{\psi}
on
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
; the symmetric nature of
\mathcal{U}_{\psi}
and the special fixed-point properties of
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
guarantee that only
R_{\mathbf{g}}
operators appear in the resulting decomposition. The precise statement of our Theorem and details of the full proof can be found in App.
D
.

Since quantum circuits are linear, we can focus our attention on correcting each term in the sum Eq. (
5
) separately. We will also restrict our attention to distributions of errors which are generic, meaning that there are no fine-tuned degeneracies among the coefficients
C_{\mathbf{g}}
. Adversarially chosen wavefunctions for which errors associated with distinct group elements occur with
exactly
equal probabilities will be able to fool our RG circuits, but since such wavefunctions constitute a set of measure zero in the space of all SPT ground states, we will not be concerned with this issue.

II.4
Diagnosing convergence of the RG flow

For a given depth
d
, our RG circuit
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d)}
is constructed by applying
d

\mathcal{C}
unitaries to the renormalized degrees of freedom in a hierarchical fashion, as indicated in Fig.
1
b.
Subsequent rounds of majority voting dilute the concentration of errors present in the renormalized wavefunction, so that the density of errors decays exponentially when
d\gtrsim\log_{3}(\xi)
, with
\xi
the correlation length of
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle
(more detailed arguments establishing this will be given below).
For sufficiently large
d
the renormalized wavefunction will thus converge to one in which no errors occur. In this limit a single layer of the RG circuit simply disentangles the ancilla qubits from the renormalized ones, thereby mapping
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
to a smaller copy of itself, tensored with the trivial product state
|0\cdots 0\rangle
— precisely the behavior desired from a fixed-point reference state. On the other hand, if the input wavefunction
|\psi_{\omega^{\prime}}\rangle
is
not
drawn from
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, the expectation value of the indicator observable in the state
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}|\psi_{\omega^{\prime}}\rangle
will exactly vanish. This is a simple consequence of the selection rule in Eq. (
1
) and the fact that
our circuits are symmetric; see App.
F
for the proof. Thus, no ‘false positives’ occur, and our circuit faithfully performs phase recognition for this class of SPT phases.

The convergence of the RG flow can be summarized by saying that
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d)}
flows any
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
to a copy of the reference state
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
supported on the renormalized Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{re}^{(d)}
, tensored with a non-universal state on the ancilla space:

\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d)}|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\langle\psi_{\omega}|\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d){\dagger}}\approx|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\omega}|\otimes\rho_{an}

(7)

with the approximation becoming an equality in the limit of large depth
d
. We will find it helpful to define a renormalized density matrix obtained by tracing out
\mathcal{H}_{an}
as

\rho_{re,\omega}^{(d)}\equiv\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{an}}[\mathcal{Q}^{(d)}_{\omega}\rho^{(0)}\mathcal{Q}^{(d){\dagger}}_{\omega}],

(8)

where
\rho^{(0)}
is the initial density matrix. The convergence of the RG flow means that
\rho_{re,\omega}^{(d\rightarrow\infty)}=|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\omega}|
if
\rho^{(0)}
is a ground state in the target SPT phase. The fact that
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d)}
can be written as a tree tensor network (i.e. as a tensor product of unitaries supported on blocks of size
3^{d}
) implies that
\rho_{re,\omega}^{(d)}
is obtained from
\rho_{re,\omega}^{(d-1)}
through LOCC, where the ‘local’ in LOCC is defined with respect to the sites of
\mathcal{H}_{re}^{(d)}
. This means that any mixed-state entanglement measure (negativity, distillable entanglement, etc.) is monotonically decreasing with
d
, as entanglement measures monotonically decrease under LOCC by definition (note that this holds regardless of the input state
\rho^{(0)}
). This concretely establishes the sense in which our RG flow successively eliminates non-universal entanglement.

We note in passing that while our focus until now has been on recognizing ground states, the initial state
\rho^{(0)}
need not be pure, and our circuit also recognizes mixed states with well-defined patterns of symmetry fractionalization. More precisely,
\rho^{(d)}_{re,\omega}
flows to the fixed point
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\omega}|
for any input states of the form

\rho^{(0)}=\sum_{a,b}c_{a,b}|{\psi_{\omega,a}}\rangle\langle{\psi_{\omega,b}}|,

(9)

where the
c_{a,b}
are complex coefficients, and for all
a
,
|\psi_{\omega,a}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
. This follows as a simple consequence of Eq. (
7
).

A more direct approach to diagnose the convergence of the RG flow is to measure the distribution of errors detected at each layer of the RG circuit.
Let
\mathcal{H}^{(d)}_{an}
denote the degrees of freedom which become part of the ancilla space at layer
d
of the circuit. As in Eq. (
8
), we define the density matrix of these degrees of freedom as

\rho_{an,\omega}^{(d)}=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{H}^{(d)}_{an}}[\mathcal{Q}^{(d)}_{\omega}\rho^{(0)}\mathcal{Q}^{(d){\dagger}}_{\omega}].

(10)

If
\rho^{(0)}
is a ground state in
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
(or a mixed state of the form Eq. (
9
)), then
\rho_{an,\omega}^{(d)}
will approach the pure state
|0\cdots 0\rangle\langle 0\cdots 0|
at large
d
, after the errors present in
\rho^{(0)}
have been corrected away. For all other
\rho^{(0)}
,
\rho_{an,\omega}^{(d)}
at sufficiently large
d
will instead be an equal weight mixture of all
|g\rangle
at each site.
We can quantify this by defining

\mathcal{F}^{(d)}\equiv\frac{1}{\log_{|G|}\dim\mathcal{H}_{an}^{(d)}}\sum_{i}\frac{\text{Tr}[\rho_{an}^{(d)}\Pi_{0,i}]-1/|G|}{1-1/|G|},

(11)

where
\Pi_{0,i}
is the projector onto the state
|0\rangle
at site
i
. For large
d
,
\mathcal{F}^{(d)}
will approach 1 in the target phase and vanish outside of it (thus faithfully performing phase recognition),and in a subsequent section we will demonstrate numerically that this indeed holds. Note that since
\mathcal{F}^{(d)}
is obtained from the expectation value of a single-site operator, it has the advantage of being simpler to measure than the string operators, which are necessarily supported on multiple sites. However, unlike for the string operators, there is no selection rule enforcing
\mathcal{F}^{(d)}
to exactly vanish outside of the target phase for all
d
.

II.5
Convergence of iterated majority vote

We now give an intuitive explanation for why errors are generically quickly diluted under successive rounds of majority voting. For the purposes of this discussion we will assume that the errors have finite correlation length and are distributed in a way which is statistically translation-invariant on long scales, an assumption which may fail for distributions of errors that break translation only in thermodynamically large scales (
e.g.
errors obtained by applying a symmetry operator on one half of the system).

With this assumption we are able to rigorously prove that the density of errors is monotonically decreasing with
d
, and provide strong evidence
that the convergence is exponentially fast when
d>\log_{3}(\xi)
.
Let
p_{d}(\mathbf{g})
be the probability for an error
R_{\mathbf{g}}
to occur at depth
d
of the RG flow. We claim that
p_{d\rightarrow\infty}(\mathbf{g})=\prod_{i}\delta_{g_{i},g_{*}}
for some fixed group element
g_{*}\in G
, which is equivalent to the statement that the iterative majority vote process converges to a definite ‘winner’
g_{*}
.

To argue this, we first note that the treelike structure of
\mathcal{Q}^{(d)}
means that as long as
3^{d}\gg\xi
, the probability distribution at depth
d
approximately factorizes,
p_{d}(\mathbf{g})\approx\prod_{i}p_{d}(g_{i})
, where
p_{d}(g)
are the single-site marginals of
p_{d}(\mathbf{g})
. Intuitively, this can be seen by a simple geometric argument. After
d
rounds of majority voting on a given length-
3^{d}
string of group elements
\mathbf{g}
, the majority
\mathsf{maj}^{d}(\mathbf{g})
will on average not be sensitive to changing a small (
\ll 3^{d}
) number of the
g_{i}
. Consider then the two-site marginal distribution
p_{d}(g_{i},g_{i+1})
. If
\xi\ll 3^{d}
, this distribution can be computed approximately by ignoring the group elements located within a distance
\xi
of the joining point between the two depth-
d
majority-vote trees used to compute
g_{i},g_{i+1}
. Doing so then leads to the distribution factorizing as
p_{d}(g_{i},g_{i+1})\approx p_{d}(g_{i})p_{d}(g_{i+1})
, and other marginals can be shown to factorize by similar arguments.
To prove convergence we can therefore focus on distributions in which errors occur independently on each site. The proof from this starting point is straightforward, as it is easy to show that the difference between the largest and second-largest values of
p_{d}(g)
is monotonically increasing with
d
, reaching a value of
1-\varepsilon
in a number of layers going roughly as
\log(1/\varepsilon)
.

A more direct proof is possible if one makes an additional (reasonable) assumption about the three-site marginals
p_{d}(g,h,k)
. Let
g_{1}
(
g_{2}
) be the element with the largest (second-largest) single-site marginal probability
p_{d}(g)
. Define the function

f_{d}(g)\equiv\sum_{h\in G}(p_{d}(g,h,g)-p_{d}(h,g,h)).

(12)

It is then straightforward to show that the difference
\delta_{d}\equiv p_{d}(g_{1})-p_{d}(g_{2})
satisfies
\delta_{d+1}-\delta_{d}=f_{d}(g_{1})-f_{d}(g_{2}).

Thus if we assume that
f_{d}(g_{1})>f_{d}(g_{2})
, then
\delta_{d}
is monotonically increasing with
d
, implying convergence of the majority vote. This essentially amounts to assuming that for two configurations of errors with the same number of domain walls, the one with more instances of
g_{1}
has higher probability of occurring (this can be viewed as an assumption that the majority vote is not ‘Gerrymandered’). The full details are given in App.
F
.

II.7
Universal multiscale string operators

As discussed above, the string operators serve as a set of ‘indicator observables’ that distinguish the different reference states
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
. The convergence of our RG circuits can thus be summarized in the equation

\langle\psi_{\omega}|\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d){\dagger}}(\mathcal{S}^{(\omega^{\prime})}_{g;i\rightarrow j}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{an})\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d)}|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\xrightarrow{d\gg\log_{3}(\xi)}\delta_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}

(15)

for any
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
(which we saw borne out in the numerics of Fig.
5
).
In the ‘Schrödinger’ picture, Eq. (
15
) measures the expectation value of a bare string operator
\mathcal{S}^{(\omega^{\prime})}_{g;i\rightarrow j}
in the renormalized state
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d)}|\psi_{\omega}\rangle
.
It is however also helpful to change to the ‘Heisenberg’ picture, where
Eq. (
15
) can be viewed as the expectation value of a renormalized string operator with respect in the original ‘UV’ state
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle

Cong et al. 2022
. This is formalized by defining
the
mutliscale string operator

Cong et al. 2019
(MSO) as

\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{(\omega,d)}_{g;i\rightarrow j}\equiv\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d){\dagger}}(\mathcal{S}_{g;i\rightarrow j}^{(\omega)}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{an})\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d)}.

(16)

Owing to the hierarchical structure of
\mathcal{Q}^{(d)}
, the MSO is a linear combination of a number of terms that grows doubly-exponentially in
d
, with each term given by a product of elementary string operators
Cong et al. 2019
;
Cong et al. 2022
(see App.
E
for a recursion relation that explicitly constructs the MSOs).

Since we have shown that our RG circuit accurately performs phase identification, expectation values of the MSOs serve as a sharp diagnostic of the target SPT phase. That this is possible is rather remarkable, as the MSOs are constructed
solely
from the algebraic data that characterize the target SPT phase (a priori, one might expect that the renormalized string operators would depend on microscopic details of the specific state to be recognized).
This construction provides a physically transparent way of revealing the universal order present in SPT ground states, since the MSOs implement (arbitrarily accurate) projections onto the space of
all
ground state wavefunctions in a target phase.
This approach is complementary to other protocols which disentangle the SPT
Else et al. 2013
, directly measure the projective symmetry action using swap gates
Haegeman et al. 2012
, or use symmetry twists to create MSO-like operators in theories with
U(1)
symmetry
Tasaki 2018
;
Huang et al. 2021
.

III
Discussion and implications

The goal of this work has been to advocate that renormalization group (RG) flow and error correction (EC) should really be viewed as two sides of the same coin.
2
2

2

Note that we are not trying to protect any logical information beyond that required to recognize a given phase. Whether or not we can
e.g.
guarantee that the logical information contained in the degenerate SPT edge modes can always be preserved throughout the RG flow is a question we leave to the future.

We arrived at this perspective by implementing RG flow in a unitary way, via the action of a certain class of quantum circuits. This is rather distinct from conventional approaches to RG, which is usually viewed as an irreversible flow performed on Hamiltonians and Lagrangians. Nevertheless, the mechanics of our construction is quite similar in spirit to the original block spin approach of Kadanoff
Kadanoff 1966
, which in some sense we simply embed into a larger operational framework that allows unitarity to be preserved and enables us to identify phases that lack local order parameters.

Since we are interested in making rigorous statements, we have restricted our attention to 1D gapped phases of matter with Abelian internal symmetries, which can be analyzed using powerful group-theoretic and tensor network methods. In the main text we have focused on SPT phases, but as shown in App.
G
a similar treatment is also possible for the (simpler) case of symmetry-breaking orders. Our belief is however that the general connection between RG and EC extends well beyond these examples, bringing with it a diverse array of implications across multiple fields of physics. We describe some of these implications and discuss future directions below.

Quantum matter:
From the quantum matter perspective, the most important implication of our work is the existence of the
multiscale string operators (MSOs): operators that recognize all wavefunctions belonging to the same phase (see Sec.
II.7
).
Another consequence of our work stems from our circuit’s ability to function as a
universal variational ansatz
for ground state wavefunctions. The basic idea is to turn EC on its head, by running our RG circuit
\mathcal{Q}
‘in reverse’. We do this by starting from a variational family of errors—parametrized by a wavefunction
|\phi_{an}\rangle
—which we then encode into a variational wavefunction by acting with
\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}
on
|\Psi\rangle\otimes|\phi_{an}\rangle
. This performs a
reversed
RG flow from the IR to the UV, and generates a state in the target SPT phase whose pattern of local entanglement is controlled by the choice of
|\phi_{an}\rangle
.
Crucially, our results imply that choices of
|\phi_{an}\rangle
are in one-to-one correspondence with ground state wavefunctions in the target phase (up to a set of measure zero).
The hierarchical construction of this encoding map shares many parallels with that of the Multiscale Entanglement Renormalizaton Ansatz (MERA)
Vidal 2008
, but is distinguished by having features native to the target phase ‘hard-coded’ into the ansatz. The EC machinery has the added benefit of explicitly separating out the universal degrees of freedom
(|\Psi\rangle)
from the non-universal ones (
|\phi_{an}\rangle
).

Quantum simulation:

From a practical point of view, our circuits provide a way of performing phase recognition even very close to critical points, where signals from conventional order parameters are often smeared out due to strong quantum fluctuations (or long correlation lengths). This problem is particularly acute in near-term quantum simulators, which typically have rather modest system sizes
Herrmann et al. 2021
.
Furthermore, our protocol for performing RG involves applying a circuit whose depth is at most
logarithmic
in the system size, making it suitable for implementation on near-term quantum devices.

As discussed in Sec.
II.7
, our phase recognition can be effected in practice in two different ways: a) first acting on the input state
|\psi\rangle
with a
d
-layer RG circuit
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d)}
and then measuring the expectation value of the elementary string operator
\mathcal{S}_{g}^{(\omega)}
, or b) directly measuring the expectation value
\langle\psi|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{(\omega,d)}_{g}|\psi\rangle
of the MSO in the input state.
The advantage of b) is that no unitary gates are needed: the form of the MSOs implies that their expectation values can be reconstructed from simultaneous measurements of all of the exponentially many (in
d
) mutually-commuting elementary string operators (each supported only on two sites).
Together with classical post-processing, this allows estimating the MSOs at arbitrary depth
d
without applying
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}^{(d)}
.
This feature enables the immediate implementation of our approach in experiments, as already done in two different quantum simulation platforms
Herrmann et al. 2021
;
Cong et al. 2022
, prior to our rigorous theoretical understanding.
On the other hand, an advantage of approach a) is its robustness against experimental imperfections
that could arise in future digital fault-tolerant quantum computers.
This is due to the exponential decrease with
d
in the size of operators that need to be measured, meaning that approach a) is exponentially less sensitive to final readout errors than approach b). In essence, our circuit
\mathcal{Q}
concentrates important and universal information about the input state onto a small number of output qubits.

Complexity of QPR:

A key performance metric for an algorithm solving the QPR problem is the
sample complexity

Haah et al. 2017
, namely the number of copies of a given state
|\psi\rangle
needed to confidently decide if
|\psi\rangle
belongs to the target phase.
Our multiscale string operators (MSOs) take on a value asymptotically close to unity
everywhere
in the target phase (and vanish outside of it), implying that measurements of the string operators performed after acting with our RG circuits lead to nearly deterministic outcomes, and correspondingly to low sample complexities for a fixed system size.
This low sample complexity is particularly desirable from the perspective of near-term quantum simulators, where finite-size effects are often quite large.
3
3

3

Note that on a system of size
L
, the number of well-separated string operators with size
\gg\xi
diverges as
L\rightarrow\infty
, giving a trivial sample complexity in the thermodynamic limit. Thus this reduction in sample complexity is only well-defined for finite systems.

It would be interesting to see if one could provide a fundamental lower bound on the sample complexity needed in this context, and if so, to check whether or not our algorithm saturates it.

Machine learning:

There has recently been a concerted effort to tackle the phase recognition problem using machine learning (ML) methods
Aaronson 2007
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Carleo and Troyer 2017
;
Wang 2016
;
Carrasquilla and
Melko 2017
;
Carleo et al. 2019
.
In this context, our MSOs
suggest that our circuits may serve as a good reference point from which to benchmark such heuristic approaches.
At the same time, our circuit provides a good starting point for numerical optimization schemes in QCNN-based ML approaches
Cong et al. 2019
.
Furthermore, the existence of MSOs has implications on the rigorous performance guarantee of classical shadow-based ML approaches to QPR problems.
In previous work
Huang et al. 2021
, the performance guarantee for recognizing SPT phases was limited to
the case of a 1D
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
SPT phase with enhanced
O(2)
symmetry. Our work extends these results to a broader class of phases: all 1D SPT phases with Abelian
internal symmetries.
Relatedly, we also note that while we have focused on performing phase recognition by measuring expectation values of observables, our RG circuits may also allow for the efficient extraction of other universal (potentially non-linear) properties of the quantum phase in question.

Intrinsic topological phases:

A clear next step is to rigorously characterize circuit architectures that recognize phases with intrinsic topological order (perhaps in a way which builds on string-net tensor network methods
Levin and Wen 2005
;
Levin and Nave 2007
;
Gu and Wen 2009
;
Evenbly and Vidal 2015
or RG-inspired decoders
Bravyi and Haah 2011
), with first steps recently being taken in
Cong et al. 2022
;
Cian et al. 2022
.
Given the recent realization of topologically ordered states in quantum simulators
Satzinger et al. 2021
;
Semeghini et al. 2021
and measurement-based protocols for preparing topological orders
Verresen et al. 2021
;
Tantivasadakarn
et al. 2022
, addressing this question is of immediate practical importance. Since the local unitary circuits (in the language of EC, the ‘errors’) which relate different wavefunctions in a topological phase can be completely generic — as opposed to the
symmetric
local circuits relevant for SPT phases — we expect that the EC procedure in this case must be one of
quantum
error correction. Whether or not the EC threshold in these phases can be made to coincide with the phase boundary is an outstanding question for the future.

More broadly, connections between EC and RG may have implications for the design of useful RG schemes in contexts very different from those considered in this paper. While the particular RG scheme we have employed incorporates spatial coarse-graining, the connection between RG and EC may be deeper than this, and approaching RG from the perspective of EC may give us fresh insights into systems for which there is no useful real-space RG scheme, such as (non)-Fermi liquids
Shankar 1994
and fracton-inspired models with UV/IR mixing
You and Moessner 2021
;
Gorantla et al. 2021
;
Lake 2022
.

In a different context, it is natural to ask whether the integration of EC into the MERA framework provides any new lessons for tensor network approaches to holography
Swingle 2012
;
Jahn and Eisert 2021
;
Hayden et al. 2016
, or for the role that EC plays in quantum gravity more generally
Almheiri et al. 2015
;
Pastawski et al. 2015
;
Kim and Kastoryano 2017
;
Yang et al. 2016
. This direction seems especially promising given that the layout of our RG quantum circuits resembles the geometry of holographic tensor networks and that our elementary MPS tensor
A
is perfect, potentially allowing for more general constructions of holographic error correcting codes
Pastawski et al. 2015
.

Appendix A
Mathematical preliminaries

In this appendix, we provide some of the background group theory details used in this work.

Let
G
be a finite Abelian group. We will let
R_{g}
denote the local symmetry action of
g\in G
, which until App.
I
we take to be in the regular representation, acting on a Hilbert space of dimension
|G|
. Explicitly,

R_{g}=\sum_{h\in G}\chi_{h}(g)|h\rangle\langle h|.

(17)

Let
V^{(\omega)}_{g}
be a projective representation of
G
with factor set
\omega:G\times G\rightarrow U(1)
, so that

V^{(\omega)}_{g}V^{(\omega)}_{h}=\omega_{g,h}V^{(\omega)}_{g+h}.

(18)

For ease of notation, we will omit the dependence of
V_{g}
on
\omega
when there is no cause for confusion.

Since the
V_{g}
are only defined up to
g
-dependent phases,
\omega_{g,h}
is equivalent to
\omega_{g,h}\alpha_{g}\alpha_{h}/\alpha_{g+h}
for some set of phases
\alpha_{g}
. For all of the cases we will be interested in,
\omega
can always be chosen to satisfy

\omega_{g,h}=\omega_{-g,h}^{*}=\omega_{g,-h}^{*}.

(19)

We define the function

Note that

from which one sees that Hermitian conjugates commute in the same way:
V_{g}^{\dagger}V_{h}^{\dagger}=\lambda_{\omega}(g,h)V_{h}^{\dagger}V_{g}^{\dagger}

We will also make use of the function

\varsigma_{g}\equiv\omega(g,g)

which can be used to convert between

and

(25)

Definition 1
.

The projective center

is defined to contain all those elements which are projectively represented by scalars:

C_{\omega}\equiv\{g\in G\,:\,\lambda_{\omega}(g,h)=1\,\forall\,h\in G\}.

(26)

We furthermore define a

.

A maximally non-commutative factor set is one with trivial projective center, viz. one for which

For a given factor set, we may construct a homomorphism
\Lambda
as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\omega}

\displaystyle:G\rightarrow G^{*}

(27)

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\mapsto\lambda_{\omega}(\cdot,g)

where

.

and

are injective. Thus since

,

and

are isomorphisms iff

iff

is maximally non-commutative. This property greatly simplifies the error correcting technology used in the construction of our RG circuits (and also simplifies the analysis of SPT phases as resource states for measurement-based quantum computation

We now state some useful math facts that we will not go through the trouble of proving; proofs can be extracted from
Berkovich and Zhmud_ 1998
,
Karpilovsky 1994
:

Proposition 1
.

The following facts hold:

•

Maximally non-Abelian factor sets exist iff
G=G^{\prime}\times G^{\prime}
is a square.

•

All projective representations with factor set
\omega
have dimension
\sqrt{|G|/|C_{\omega}|}
.

•

Distinct projective irreps with factor set
\omega
are enumerated by the linear characters of the group
C_{\omega}
; there are thus
|C_{\omega}|
distinct projective irreps. In particular, if
\omega
is maximally non-commutative, there is only a single projective irrep up to isomorphism.

One result that we could not find in the literature is the grand orthogonality theorem for projective representations:

Theorem 2

(Schur orthogonality for projective representations)
.

For
V^{\mu}
and
V^{\nu}
any two projective irreps of
G
,

\int_{g}[(V^{\mu}_{g})^{\dagger}]_{ij}[V^{\nu}_{g}]_{kl}=\frac{1}{\dim(V^{\mu})}\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\delta_{\mu\nu}.

(31)

Proof.

The proof is straightforward and mimicks the linear case: we construct an intertwiner with the map

F_{ij}^{\mu\nu}=\int_{g}(V^{\mu}_{g})^{\dagger}E_{ij}V_{g}^{\nu},

(32)

which satisfies

\displaystyle V^{\mu}_{h}F_{ij}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\int_{g}\varsigma_{g}\omega(h,-g)V^{\mu}_{-g+h}E_{ij}V_{g}^{\nu}

(33)

\displaystyle=\int_{g}\varsigma_{g}\varsigma_{-g+h}^{*}\omega_{h,-g}(V^{\mu}_{g-h})^{\dagger}E_{ij}V_{g-h+h}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\int_{g}\varsigma_{g}\varsigma_{-g+h}^{*}\omega_{h,-g}\omega_{g-h,h}^{*}((V^{\mu}_{g-h})^{\dagger}E_{ij}V_{g-h}^{\nu})V_{h}^{\nu}.

Crucially, the factor

\varsigma_{g}\varsigma_{-g+h}^{*}\omega_{h,-g}\omega_{g-h,h}^{*}=1

(34)

due to the cocycle condition
\delta\omega=1
, which expanded out is simply

\omega(g_{1},g_{2})\omega(g_{1}+g_{2},g_{3})=\omega(g_{2},g_{3})\omega(g_{1},g_{2}+g_{3}).

(35)

Therefore
[F^{\mu\nu}_{ij}]_{kl}=\delta_{\mu,\nu}\delta_{k,l}\lambda_{i,j}
for some constant factor
\lambda_{i,j}
. This can be identified by computing

\lambda_{i,j}=\frac{\text{Tr}[F_{ij}^{\mu\mu}]}{\dim(V^{\mu})}=\frac{\delta_{i,j}}{\dim(V^{\mu})},

(36)

thereby proving the claim.

∎

Corollary 1
.

If
V_{g}
is a projective irrep and
\omega
is maximally non-commutative, then

{\rm Tr}[V_{g}]=\delta_{g,e}\sqrt{|G|}.

(37)

In particular, the only nonzero character of

is the trivial character.

Proof.

We use Shur orthogonality to write

\displaystyle\int_{g}|\text{Tr}[V_{g}]|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int_{g}[V^{\dagger}_{g}]_{ii}[V_{g}]_{jj}=1.

(38)

Since
\int_{g}=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}
we must have

\sum_{g\in G}|\text{Tr}[V_{g}]|^{2}=|G|,

(39)

analogously to the case of linear representations. Now if
\omega
is maximally non-commutative, then
\dim(V)=\sqrt{|G|}
. Since

, the above sum is then fully saturated by

, proving the claim. ∎

Let

and write elements in

d>\log_{3}(\xi)\implies\sum_{h\in G}p_{d}(g,h,k)=p_{d}(g)p_{d}(k).

(150)

Let
d_{\xi}=\lceil\log_{3}(\xi)\rceil
and define
l\equiv d-d_{\xi}
. Then

\displaystyle p_{d}(g,h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\in G^{3^{l}}}p_{d_{\xi}}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\delta_{\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g}),g}\delta_{\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{h}),h}.

(151)

Because of (
150
),
p_{d}(g,h)
would factorize exactly if we were able to perform an unrestricted sum on the element
g_{3^{l}}
, which is located at the boundary between the bases of the two depth-
l

\mathsf{maj}
trees leading to
g
and
h
. The thing which prevents us from doing so is of course the factor of
\delta_{\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g}),g}
. However, when
3^{l}\gg\xi
, we expect the outcome of the majority vote
\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g})
to be largely insensitive to the exact value of
g_{3^{l}}
: in the vast majority of cases, the value of the element
g_{3^{l}}
will not by itself be able to sway the outcome of the majority vote. We thus claim that

p_{d_{\xi}+l}(g,h)=p_{d_{\xi}+l}(g)p_{d_{\xi}+l}(h)+\cdots,

(152)

where
\cdots
is exponentially small in
l
.

We can quantify this expectation by computing the conditional entropy
H(\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g})|g_{1},\dots,g_{3^{l}-1})
. This entropy vanishes if
\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g})
can be determined from
g_{1},\dots,g_{3^{l}-1}
alone, without the need to know the value of the remaining element
g_{3^{l}}
. In general
H(\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g})|g_{1},\dots,g_{3^{l}-1})
is nonzero, but is nevertheless exponentially small in
l
. Indeed, given a scheme for predicting
\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g})
from
g_{1},\dots,g_{3^{l}-1}
, we can bound
H(\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g})|g_{1},\dots,g_{3^{l}-1})
using Fano’s inequality as

H(\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g})|g_{1},\dots,g_{3^{l}-1})\leq H(p_{e})+p_{e}\ln(|G|-1),

(153)

where
p_{e}
is the probability of our prediction scheme giving the wrong answer, and
H(x)=-x\ln x-(1-x)\ln(1-x)
is the binary Shannon entropy.

Given
g_{1},\dots,g_{3^{l}-1}
, we can exactly determine the values of all nodes in the ternary tree computing
\mathsf{maj}^{l}
except for those on the tree’s rightmost flank. For example, when
l=2
we can determine all of the nodes of the following tree, except those marked in yellow:

(154)

One estimate for
\mathsf{maj}^{l}(\mathbf{g})
given
g_{1},\dots,g_{3^{l}-1}
is to assume that the result of the majority vote on the last three elements
g_{3^{l}-2},g_{3^{l}-1},g_{3^{l}}
is just the central element
g_{3^{l}-1}
(since the central element wins in the case of ties), and to then assign the remaining nodes of the majority tree according to this choice. In order for this estimate to be in error, the choice of
g_{3^{l}}
must be able to change the outcomes of every majority vote occuring on the right flank of the
\mathsf{maj}
tree (the yellow nodes in the above figure). Since the probability of changing each of these majority votes is less than 1, the error probability

l

More precisely, in order for this estimate to be incorrect, we first need that
g_{3^{l}-2}=g_{3^{l}}\neq g_{3^{l}-1}

\mathsf{maj}
tree, viz.
\mathsf{maj}(g_{3^{l}-2},g_{3^{l}-1},g_{3^{l}})
, disagrees with our guess of
g_{3^{l}-1}
. The probability of this disagreement happening is less than 1 unless
p_{d_{\xi}}(g,h,k)\propto\delta_{g,k}
for all
g,h,k
— but this is impossible, as summing over
h
would produce
p_{d_{\xi}}(g)p_{d_{\xi}}(k)\propto\delta_{g,k}
, which can be true only if
p_{d_{\xi}}(g)\neq 0
only for a single

(in which case
\mathsf{maj}
has already converged). A similar argument applies for each node along the right flank of the majority tree, where the probability of an error occuring given a change in the rightmost member of a
\mathsf{maj}
vote is strictly less than 1, unless
\mathsf{maj}
has already converged. Thus
p_{e}
is a product of
l

(unfortunately explicitly calculating

is rather complicated). By Fano’s inequality this implies that

is also exponentially small in
l
, meaning that performing an unrestricted sum over
g_{3^{l}}
in (
151

F.3
No false positives

We now show that
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
does not produce false positives, i.e. that for
\nu\neq\omega
,
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
does not mistakenly converge to
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
if given
|\psi_{\nu}\rangle
as input. That this holds can in fact be argued on general grounds using the results of
de Groot et al. 2021
, wherein it was shown that any quantum channel whose Kraus operators commute with the symmetry action cannot map
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
to
|\psi_{\nu}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\nu}
if
\omega\neq\nu
.
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
enjoys precisely this symmetry-property: from (
115
) we see that
(\mathbf{1}\otimes R_{g}\otimes\mathbf{1})\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}R_{g}^{\otimes 3}
,
implying that the symmetry pushes through
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
to operate on just the renormalized legs:

\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}[(R_{g}^{\dagger})^{\otimes 3^{l}}\rho\,R_{g}^{\otimes 3^{l}}]=(R_{g}^{\dagger})^{\otimes 3^{l-1}}\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}(\rho)R_{g}^{\otimes 3^{l-1}}

(158)

for any density matrix
\rho
.
This then implies that exactly the desired condition is satisfied by the Kraus operators of
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
, so that the claim follows.

Appendix G
Identifying spontaneous symmetry breaking with RG circuits

In this appendix we briefly discuss how to construct an RG circuit which recognizes phases with and without spontaneous symmetry breaking of an internal finite Abelian symmetry
G
. As with the SPT case, the RG circuit has the merit of being able to reduce the sample complexity of phase recognition near the critical point, and provides a direct link between error correction and RG flow.

The construction parallels that of the architecture used for recognizing SPT phases, but as there exists a standard local order parameter for diagnosing symmetry breaking the construction is much simpler (see
Furuya et al. 2021
;
Friedman et al. 2022
). As in the rest of the paper, the setting will be on a 1d chain of length
L
.

Define the linear symmetry and shift operators as

R_{g}\equiv\sum_{h\in G}\chi_{g}(h)|h\rangle\langle h|,\qquad S_{g}\equiv\sum_{h\in G}|{g+h}\rangle\langle{h}|,

(159)

which obey
R_{g}S_{h}=\chi_{g}(h)S_{h}R_{g}
. We will take the global symmetry to be generated by the operators
S_{g}^{\otimes L}

7
7

7

In our discussion of SPT phases we worked in a basis where the symmetry generators were diagonal, since in that case we were only interested in symmetric phases. Here we are focused on phases with SSB, and taking the symmetry generators to be off-diagonal is more natural.
. By character orthogonality, any operator can be expanded in terms of the
R_{g}
and
S_{h}
; the proof is analogous to that of (
97
). While we will not specify a particular Hamiltonian, for concreteness one could simply take a
G

H=-\sum_{i}\sum_{g\in G}\left(R_{-g,i}\otimes R_{g,i+1}+\lambda S_{g,i}\right)

(160)

where the real constant
\lambda
tunes between the symmetric and SSB phases.

Denote the symmetric phase by
\mathsf{PARA}
, and let
\mathsf{SSB}
denote the phase where

is spontaneously broken. We take
\mathsf{SSB}
to be a direct sum over
all
superslection sectors, including states where
R_{g}
has all nonzero expectation values. Our goal will be to construct a circuit
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
for classifying whether a given wavefunction is in
\mathsf{PARA}
, or in
\mathsf{SSB}
.
The reason for defining
\mathsf{SSB}
as a union over all superselection sectors is that we will not require

Consider a wavefunction

Appendix H
Error correction with measurements and classical post-processing

We have seen that the RG circuit essentially performs a classical error correction procedure on an input wavefunction
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
. One is naturally then led to ask: to what extent is the full RG circuit architecture actually needed for phase identification? As demonstrated above, the whole implementation of the RG circuit circuit can be effectively replaced by the direct measurement of the multiscale string order parameters, at least as far as phase identification is concerned. However, the multiscale string operator is rather complicated, and contains a very large number of terms. Is there an easier way out, which lets a collection of simple local measurements bypass the work done by the RG circuit? In particular, given that the job of the circuit is to perform a classical error correction of the local symmetry actions, can it simply be replaced by a simpler one-shot measurement of the local errors, together with classical data post-processing? In this appendix we argue that the answer to this question is likely no.

For the purposes of this discussion it will help to introduce the
disentangling operators

\mathscr{D}_{\omega}\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{g}\in G^{2L}}\text{Tr}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{L}(A_{\omega}^{g_{2i}})^{*}(A_{\omega}^{g_{2i+1}})^{T}\right]\bigotimes_{j=1}^{L}|{g_{2j}}\rangle\langle{g_{2j+1}}|=\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[width]{figures/disentangler}}}.

(166)

The disentangler
\mathscr{D}_{\omega}
gets its name from the fact that it converts
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
to the product state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle=|0\rangle^{\otimes L}
and vice-versa:

\mathscr{D}_{\omega}|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle=|\Psi_{0}\rangle,

(167)

as can easily be checked using the perfect tensor condition (
59
).

The utility of
\mathscr{D}_{\omega}
is that given
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, it lets us easily sample from the distribution of errors acting on
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle
.
As before, let the error amplitudes of
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle
be
C_{\mathbf{g}}
. Using (
57
) and (
106
), we obtain

\mathscr{D}_{\omega}|\psi_{\omega}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{g}\in G^{L}}C_{\mathbf{g}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L}|\Gamma_{\omega}(g_{i+1}-g_{i})\rangle.

(168)

Applying
\mathscr{D}_{\omega}
and then measuring in the
R_{g}
basis thus lets us directly sample the probability distribution of errors. Indeed, consider the distribution
p^{\omega}_{e}(\mathbf{g})
, defined by

p^{\omega}_{e}(\mathbf{g})=\langle\psi_{\omega}|\mathscr{D}_{\omega}^{\dagger}\Pi_{\mathbf{g}}\mathscr{D}_{\omega}|\psi_{\omega}\rangle,

(169)

with the projector

\Pi_{\mathbf{g}}\equiv\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L}\int_{h\in G}\chi^{*}_{h}(g_{i})R_{h,i}.

(170)

As argued above, at a generic point in
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, the recursive majority function
\mathsf{maj}
applied to samples obtained from the distribution
p_{e}^{\omega}(\mathbf{g})
will converge to a fixed point where
p_{e}^{\omega}(g)
has support on only a single group element
g_{*}
. Furthermore, the convergence scale obtained in this way will agree with the convergence scale of the actual RG circuit. Thus measurements and classical data processing suffice to determine how ‘far’
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle
is from
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
, given the promise that
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
.

However, if one is not promised that
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, the full architecture of the RG circuit cannot simply be replaced by the depth-1 disentangling circuit
\mathscr{D}_{\omega}
, together with the classical data processing required to compute
\mathsf{maj}^{d}(p^{\omega}_{e}(\mathbf{g}))
. To see this, define the distributions

p_{e}^{\omega,\nu}(\mathbf{g})=\langle\psi_{\nu}|\mathscr{D}_{\omega}^{\dagger}\Pi_{\mathbf{g}}\mathscr{D}_{\omega}|\psi_{\nu}\rangle,

(171)

where
\psi_{\nu}\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\nu}
(with
\nu=0
being the trivial paramagnetic phase). In order for the phase identification ability of the circuit to be replaced by the action of
\mathscr{D}_{\omega}
together with classical data processing, we require that if
\nu\neq\omega
, then
\mathsf{maj}^{d}
does
not
converge when acting on samples obtained from
p_{e}^{\omega,\nu}(\mathbf{g})
. That is, for
\omega\neq\nu

p_{e}^{\omega,\nu}(g)
be uniformly random on
G

Consider first the case where the input wavefunction is the product state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
(we will see in a moment that this case is special). Since
\mathscr{D}_{\omega}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
, the marginal probability for a single-site error is

p_{e}^{\omega,0}(g)=\langle\Psi_{0}|\mathscr{D}_{\omega}^{\dagger}\Pi_{g}\mathscr{D}_{\omega}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\int_{h}\chi^{*}_{h}(g)\langle\Psi_{\omega}|R_{h}|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle=\widetilde{f}(g),

(172)

where
\widetilde{f}(g)
is the Fourier transform of

For
\mathsf{maj}^{d}(p_{e}^{\omega,0}(\mathbf{g}))

, i.e. only if
\langle\Psi_{\omega}|R_{g}|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle\propto\delta_{g,e}

\text{Tr}[V_{g}]=\propto\delta_{g,e}

However, while

8

Appendix J
Sample complexity

In this section we describe how to estimate the sample complexity of phase recognition using the expectation values of the multiscale string operators (MSOs)
\mathcal{S}_{g}^{(\omega)}
defined in the main text. Given an SPT ground state wavefunction
|\psi\rangle
, the sample complexity
M_{\delta}^{\psi}
is defined as the number of measurements of the MSOs in the state
|\psi\rangle
needed to conclude whether or not
|\psi\rangle
is in the target phase
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
of interest, given some tolerance for error
\delta
.

The eigenvalues of any
\mathcal{S}_{g}^{(\omega)}
are given by the phases
\chi_{g}(h),h\in G
. This fact can be proven by demonstrating it in the case where
\mathcal{S}_{g}^{(\omega)}=S^{R}_{g}\otimes R_{g}\otimes S^{L}_{g}
is a ‘bare’ 3-site string operator (which can in turn be shown using the graphical technology developed in previous appendices), and noting that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{S}_{g}^{(\omega)}
do not change under conjugation by
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
. Since
G
is a finite group, for any
g
the character
\chi_{g}(h)
will be a multiple of some primitive
n
-th root of unity for all
h
, for some integer
n
(with
n>1
if
g\neq e
). We can thus select out the eigenspace of
\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g}
spanned by those eigenvectors with eigenvalue
\zeta_{n}^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
(recall
\zeta_{n}\equiv e^{{2\pi}i/n}
) by forming the projector

\Pi_{k}^{(\omega,g)}\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\zeta_{n}^{kl}(\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g})^{l}.

(188)

If
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
is an RG fixed point, then
|\psi\rangle
is an eigenstate of
\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g}
with eigenvalue 1, and has support only on the image of
\Pi_{e}^{(\omega,g)}
. When
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
but
|\psi\rangle
is not a fixed point,
\langle\psi|\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g}|\psi\rangle
will be some
O(1)
number which approaches
1
exponentially fast in the QCNN circuit depth, but which for small circuit depths and near phase boundaries can still be rather small, giving nonzero weight to the
\Pi_{k\neq e}^{(\omega,g)}
. On the other hand, if
|\psi\rangle\not\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, then by the selection rules discussed earlier,
\langle\psi|\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g;i\rightarrow j}|\psi\rangle
will vanish as
e^{-|i-j|/\xi}
, meaning that the measurements of the
\Pi_{k}^{(\omega,g)}
will be very nearly uniformly distributed over all
k
. When doing numerics, this means that as soon as one sees a
\langle\psi|\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g;i\rightarrow j}|\psi\rangle
whose value is above machine precision and not exponentially small in the limit where
|i-j|/\xi\gg 1
, one can be confident that
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
. However in an experimental context there will always be some finite level of noise present in the measurement process, mandating that a stronger signal be observed before one can be confident about phase identification.

The criterion we will adopt is as follows. Define the binary random variable
\xi_{\psi}\in\{0,1\}
to be the outcome of measuring the projector
\Pi^{(\omega,g_{\bullet})}_{e}
in the state
|\psi\rangle
,
where
g_{\bullet}
is some fixed element of maximal order
n
in
G
. Let
p\equiv\mathbb{E}[\xi_{\psi}]
. Assuming
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, the sample complexity
M^{\psi}_{\delta}
is defined as the number of samples needed so that
p
is separated from
1/n
(which would be the expectation value of
\xi
if
|\psi\rangle\not\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
) by
\delta
times the standard deviation of the emperical average of
\xi_{\psi}
(in practice, one may be able to do slightly better by using a more sophisticated estimator of the average
Cong et al. 2019
). That is,
M^{\psi}_{\delta}
is the minimum integer satisfying

p-\delta\frac{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}{\sqrt{M^{\psi}_{\delta}}}>1/n.

(189)

Thus

M^{\psi}_{\delta}=\lceil\frac{\delta^{2}p(1-p)}{(p-1/n)^{2}}\rceil.

(190)

In the setting of the
\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}
SPT considered in Fig.
5
of the main text, we take
g_{\bullet}=(1,0)
, so that

and

p=\frac{1}{3}(1+2S),\qquad S\equiv{\rm Re}[\langle\psi|\mathcal{S}^{(\omega)}_{g_{\bullet}}|\psi\rangle],

(191)

giving

M_{\delta}^{\psi}=\lceil\delta^{2}\frac{(1+2S)(1-S)}{2S^{2}}\rceil.

(192)

Note that
M^{\psi}_{\delta}=1
as
S\rightarrow 1
from below; in this case one is nearly at the fixed point
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
, which can be recognized immediately. In the opposite limit of
S\rightarrow 0
the string operator contains no signal of the SPT order, and
M^{\psi}_{1}
accordingly diverges. The inset of Fig.

Appendix K
The case when
\omega
is not MNC

In this appendix we explain how the technology used in the construction of our RG circuit can be generalized to the case when the cohomology class
\omega
in question is not maximally non-commutative (MNC). This allows us to give a provable performance guarantee for identifying
any
SPT phase protected by an internal finite Abelian symmetry.

A summary of the material contained in this appendix is as follows. We begin in Sec.
K.1
by discussing an alternate way of formulating the tools (reference states, shift operators, etc.) used to construct the RG circuits
\mathcal{Q}_{\omega}
in the MNC case, which will allow us to more easily generalize to non-MNC
\omega
. In Sec.
K.2
we extend this technology to general
\omega
. The main result of this section is to prove that for any SPT phase, the protecting symmetry
G
can always be factored into a ‘MNC part’ and a ‘flavor’ part. The Hilbert space can similarly be factored as
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{MNC}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{flav}
, with
G
acting projectively on
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
and linearly on
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
. The degrees of freeom in
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
are the ones responsible for the phase’s protected edge modes, while those in
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
are in some sense trivial (in our reference states, the degrees of freedom in
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
are frozen out in a product state and unentangled with the rest of the system).

In Sec.
K.3
we discuss how general wavefunctions
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle
can be represented in terms of collections of ‘errors’ applied to the reference states
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
constructed in Sec.
K.2
. Some errors involve local actions of the symmetry that act only on
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
. These errors can be corrected using the same framework developed above for the case of MNC
\omega
. Other errors act only on
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
; these errors are unimportant and can essentially simply be ignored. The main difficulty is that there exist errors which involve a
mixed
action on
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
and
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
. From the perspective of either one of these subsystems, these mixed errors look as though they break the symmetry; that they are in fact symmetric is only seen when both subsystems are considered. To deal with these errors, the strategy we adopt is to convert them into errors that act
only
on
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
and to then run an MNC RG circuit on
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
, with
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
being ignored for the remaineder of phase recognition procedure.

The conversion process is done by measuring the degrees of freedom on
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
, performing an
O(L)
amount of classical data processing on the measurement outcome, and then using the result of this data processing to construct a certain depth-1 local symmetric circuit
\mathcal{U}
; we will show that
\mathcal{U}
can always be chosen to turn mixed errors into errors that act only on
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
. After acting with
\mathcal{U}
, the wavefunction is left in a form amenable to be adressed with an MNC RG circuit applied to
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
, and phase recognition proceeds as in the MNC case developed previously. The upshot is that phases with non-MNC
\omega
can be dealt with using more or less the same framework as the MNC case, provided that one pays an
O(L)
amount of classical data processing as overhead.

K.2.2
Form of
\mathcal{R}_{g}

We now generalize the entangled pair representation
\mathcal{R}_{g}
of the symmetry operators (
194
) to the case of general
\omega
.
The idea is to split
\mathcal{R}_{g}
into a projective action controlled by the part
\underline{g}
of
G
belonging to
\mathcal{G}
, together with an ordinary linear action controlled by the part
\widetilde{g}
belonging to
C
.
To facilitate this decomposition, we will break up the
|G|
-dimensional physical Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
at each site as

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{l}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{flav}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{re},

(227)

where
\dim\mathcal{H}_{l}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{re}=\sqrt{|\mathcal{G}|}=|G^{\prime}|
and
\dim\mathcal{H}_{flav}=|C|
. We will refer to
\mathcal{H}_{l}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{re}
as the ‘MNC’ subspace
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
; it is on this subspace that the symmetry fractionalization occurs. We will also use the symbols
\mathcal{H}_{flav},\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
to refer to the tensor products of the single-site flavor / MNC Hilbert spaces across
all
sites; the distinction between the two should be clear from context.

In order to write down
\mathcal{R}_{g}
, which will split as a tensor product according to the above decomposition, we need a further definition:

Definition 11
.

For any
g\in G
, the matrix
\widetilde{R}_{g}\in L(\mathbb{C}^{|C|})
is defined as the linear represetation
R_{g}
restricted to
\theta(C)
(again with the same fixed section used to define
\theta
). Explicitly, for
\{|\widetilde{h}\rangle\,:\,\widetilde{h}\in C\}
a basis of
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
,

\widetilde{R}_{g}\equiv\sum_{\widetilde{h}\in C}\chi_{\theta(\widetilde{h})}(g)|\widetilde{h}\rangle\langle\widetilde{h}|.

(228)

We can now state the main result of this subsection, which shows that
R_{g}
can be equivalently written in a form that explicitly separates the symmetry action into a projective action on
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
and a linear action on
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
:

Theorem 5

(entangled pair representation, general case)
.

For a factor set
\omega
with projective center
C
, the regular representation
R_{g}
is unitarily equivalent to the representation

\mathcal{R}_{g}=V^{*}_{\underline{g}}\otimes\widetilde{R}_{g}\otimes V_{\underline{g}}.

(229)

Proof.

It is easy to check that
\mathcal{R}_{g}
is linear, viz. that
\mathcal{R}_{g}\mathcal{R}_{h}=\mathcal{R}_{g+_{G}h}
. Thus as with the case of Prop.
11
, it sufficies simply to show that the eigenvalues of
R_{g}
are identical to the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{R}_{g}
. Since the eigenvalues of
V_{\underline{g}}^{*}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{|C|}\otimes V_{\underline{g}}
are given by
\chi_{\underline{g}}^{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{G})
by virtue of
\omega
being MNC when restricted to
\mathcal{G}
, from the definition of
\widetilde{R}_{g}
we have

\mathsf{Eigvals}(\mathcal{R}_{g})=\{\chi^{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{h}}(\underline{g})\chi_{\theta(\widetilde{h})}(g)\,\,|\,\,(\bar{h},\widetilde{h})\in\mathcal{G}\times C\}.

(230)

From the character decomposition formula (
215
), this set is simply equivalent to the set
\chi_{g}(G)
, which is indeed equal to the eigenvalues of the regular representation
R_{g}
.
∎

K.2.3
MPS representation of reference states
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle

We are finally in the position to apply the above group theory technology to the problem at hand. Our goal is to construct a wavefunction
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
that will serve as our chosen RG fixed point for the phase
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
.
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
should be symmetric, and should also be stabilized by a collection of string operators.
Furthermore,
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
should reduce to the simple form of the generalized AKLT state (
198
) in the MNC case, i.e. in the case when
C
is trivial. We claim that the following wavefunction does the job:

|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{g}\in G^{L}}\text{Tr}[A^{g_{1}}_{\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}}\cdots A^{g_{L}}_{\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}}],

(231)

where the MPS tensors are (writing
g\in G
as the triple
(\bar{g}_{1},\widetilde{g},\bar{g}_{2})\in G^{\prime}\times C\times G^{\prime}
in accordance with the factorization
\mathcal{H}_{l}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{flav}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{re}

A^{g}_{\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}}=A^{(\bar{g}_{1},\widetilde{g},\bar{g}_{2})}_{\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}}=|{\bar{g}_{1}}\rangle\langle{\bar{g}_{2}}|\delta_{\widetilde{g},\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}},

(232)

where the virtual space has dimension
\dim\mathcal{H}_{virt}=|G^{\prime}|
and where
\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}
is an arbitrary fixed element of
C
.

A^{(\bar{g}_{1},\widetilde{g},\bar{g}_{2})}_{\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}}
is simply given by the generalized AKLT state MPS tensor used in the MNC case (
198
), plus a projector onto a trivial product state for the degrees of freedom in
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
. Graphically,

A^{g}_{\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}}=\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[width]{figures/nonmna_mps}}},

(233)

where the orange leg lives in
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
and the circle denotes a projection onto the state
\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}
.

|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
has zero correlation length, is stabilized (modulo
\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}
-dependent phases) by all of the global symmetry operators
\mathcal{R}_{g}^{\otimes L}
, and has the correct pattern of symmetry fractionalization; hence it defines a suitable reference state for the phase
\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
. It is clear from this presentation that the degrees of freedom in
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
are not essential to defining the topological properties of the SPT phase, and as such it is convenient to work with a reference state which trivializes them away. Relatedly, we see that on a finite chain,
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
has
|G^{\prime}|
symmetry-protected modes localized to each edge; this shows that the number of protected edge modes is determined
only
by the dimension of the MNC subgroup
\mathcal{G}=G^{\prime}\times G^{\prime}
(see also e.g.
Pollmann et al. 2010
;
de Groot et al. 2021
).

Physically, the purpose of string operators is that they allow one to move between distinct degenerate edge states. Since there are only
|G^{\prime}|
symmetry-protected modes, the space of operators which acts on the zero mode subspace has dimension
|G^{\prime}|^{2}=|\mathcal{G}|
. Thus we will only define
|\mathcal{G}|
(and not
|G|
) different string operators, done in the obvious way by focusing only on the action of the symmetry on the
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}

\mathcal{H}
:

Definition 12

(string operators for general
\omega
)
.

For
\underline{g}\in\mathcal{G}
, the string operators
\mathcal{S}_{\underline{g};i\rightarrow j}
are defined as

\mathcal{S}_{\underline{g};i\rightarrow j}\equiv S^{R}_{\underline{g},i}\otimes\bigotimes_{l=i+1}^{j-1}\mathcal{R}_{\pi^{-1}(\underline{g}),l}\otimes S^{L}_{\underline{g},j},

(234)

where the left and right shift operators are defined as

S^{L}_{\underline{g}}\equiv V_{\underline{g}}^{*}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{flav}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{r},\qquad S^{R}_{\underline{g}}\equiv\mathbf{1}_{l}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{flav}\otimes V_{\underline{g}}.

(235)

Note that we have chosen the linear symmetry operators
\mathcal{R}_{g}
appearing in the interior of the string operator’s support to be defined with a particular section of
G
picked out by
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{G})
— when evaluated on states where the
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
degrees of freedom are in a product state (such as
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
), the exact choice of section only modifies the action of
\mathcal{S}_{\underline{g};i\rightarrow j}
by an unimportant overall phase factor. It is easy to check that the choice of
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
in (
232
) gives a reference state stabilized (up to a phase) by all
|\mathcal{G}|
string operators:

\mathcal{S}_{\underline{g};i\rightarrow j}|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle=\chi_{\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}}(\pi^{-1}(\underline{g}))^{|j-i|}|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle\,\,\forall\,\,\underline{g}\in\mathcal{G},\,i<j.

|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle

\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}
) is as in (
200

K.2.4
edge mode counting and accidental symmetries

Since the number of edge modes is determined only by
|\mathcal{G}|
, it is natural to ask if in fact a
G
-SPT with MNC subgroup
\mathcal{G}
is always secretly equivalent to a fine-tuned version of a
\mathcal{G}
-SPT with MNC factor set and an ‘accidental’ enlarged symmetry group
G
. It turns out that this is sometimes — but not generically — the case.

If a
G
-SPT is to be equivalent to a fine-tuned version of a
\mathcal{G}
-SPT, one should be able to break the symmetry down as
G\leadsto\mathcal{G}
, without reducing the number of protected edge modes. The only natural embedding of
\mathcal{G}
into
G
that exists is the one given by the map
\pi_{*}
; thus this pattern of symmetry breaking is acheived by preserving the subgroup
\pi_{*}(\mathcal{G})\subset G
, and breaking the rest of
G
. In order to determine the number of edge modes that remain after symmetry breaking, we need simply to compute the projective center of
\pi_{*}(\mathcal{G})
, defined as

C^{\pi_{*}(\mathcal{G})}_{\omega}=\{\underline{g}\in\pi_{*}(\mathcal{G})\,:\,\lambda_{\omega}(g,h)=1\,\,\forall\,\,\underline{h}\in\pi_{*}(\mathcal{G})\}.

(238)

The number of protected edge modes (on a given edge) is then determined by

{\rm\#\,edge\,\,modes}=\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{G}|}{|C^{\pi_{*}(\mathcal{G})}_{\omega}|}}.

(239)

Depending on the scenario in question, the number of protected edge modes could be either unchanged (in which case
G
should indeed be viewed as an‘accidental’ symmetry) or eliminated entirely. We will see how both possibilities can be realized in the following example.

K.2.5
Example:
G=\mathbb{Z}_{pq}^{2}

We return to our example of
G=\mathbb{Z}_{pq}^{2}
, with
p,q
prime and the non-MNC cohomology class
\omega=q
. As a reminder, in this case we have
C=p\mathbb{Z}_{pq}^{2}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{2},\mathcal{G}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{2}
.

As in our discussion of the MNC case for
G=\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{2}
, we will choose the projective representation
V_{\underline{g}}
of
\mathcal{G}
as
V_{\underline{g}}=X^{\underline{g}_{1}}Z^{\underline{g}_{2}}
, where
X,Z
are the
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
clock and shift matrices. Thus the explicit form of
\mathcal{R}_{g}
is

\mathcal{R}_{g}=V_{\underline{g}}^{*}\otimes\widetilde{R}_{g}\otimes V_{\underline{g}}=X^{\underline{g}_{1}}Z^{\underline{g}_{2}}\otimes\left(\sum_{\widetilde{h}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{2}}Z^{\widetilde{h}_{1}(\underline{g}_{1}+q\underaccent{\sim}{g}_{1})/q}\otimes Z^{\widetilde{h}_{2}(\underline{g}_{2}+q\underaccent{\sim}{g}_{2})/q}\right)\otimes X^{\underline{g}_{1}}Z^{-\underline{g}_{2}},

(240)

where our choice of lift
\theta:C\rightarrow G
is manifested in the factors of
1/q
appearing in the exponents of the operators constituting
\widetilde{R}_{g}
.

On a given edge, our
G
-SPT has only
\sqrt{|\mathcal{G}|}=p
protected zero modes. Is this SPT then secretely a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{2}
SPT with an accidentally enhanced symmetry? To answer this question, we consider breaking the symmetry down as
G\leadsto\pi_{*}(\mathcal{G})=q\mathbb{Z}_{pq}^{2}
(
i.e.
the generators of
G
are broken, but their
q
-fold powers are not). The projective center of
\pi_{*}(\mathcal{G})
is

C_{q}^{q\mathbb{Z}_{pq}^{2}}=\frac{p}{\gcd(p,q)}\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{p}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\gcd(p,q)}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}_{1}&q\neq p\\
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{2}&q=p\end{cases}

(241)

where in the last equality we used that
p,q
are prime. Thus the number of edge modes is unchanged upon breaking
\mathbb{Z}_{pq}^{2}\leadsto\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{2}
if
p\neq q
(and hence in this case the full
G
symmetry should be viewed as being accidental), while the edge modes are completely eliminated if
q=p
(so that in this case
G
cannot be broken to any subgroup without completely trivializing the theory). The latter fact is especially clear when one realizes that when
q=p
,
\pi_{*}(\mathcal{G})=p\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}^{2}=C
.

K.3
Picture of general SPT ground states

We now need the counterpart to theorem
3
, which established that for MNC
\omega
, any
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
can be written as a collection of linear symmetry actions (‘errors’) acting on the reference state
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
. In what follows we will set
\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}=e
to be the identity for simplicity.

We begin by characterizing a basis for operators which is particularly well-suited for the task at hand. Our previous operator decomposition formula can be generalized as follows:

Proposition 13
.

Any single-site operator
\mathcal{O}
can be decomposed as

\mathcal{O}=\sum_{g,h\in G}C_{g,h}\mathcal{R}_{g}S^{R}_{\bar{h}}\mathcal{W}_{\widetilde{h}},

(242)

where
C_{g,h}
are complex coefficients, and the operators
\mathcal{W}_{\widetilde{h}}
are defined as

\mathcal{W}_{\widetilde{h}}\equiv\sum_{\widetilde{l}\in C}\mathbf{1}_{l}\otimes|{\widetilde{l}+\widetilde{h}}\rangle\langle{\widetilde{l}}|\otimes V_{\Gamma^{-1}_{\omega}(\bar{\delta}(\widetilde{l},\widetilde{h}))}.

(243)

Proof.

Consider working in the original basis, in which
R_{g}
is diagonal and acts in the regular representation (
193
). This representation is naturally associated with the basis
\{|g\rangle\,:\,g\in G\}
for the local Hilbert space. Define the shift operators

X_{g}=\sum_{h\in G}|{h+g}\rangle\langle{h}|.

(244)

It is then straightforward to check that the set
\{X_{g}R_{h}\,:\,g,h\in G\}
generates all on-site operators. Indeed, this set clearly contains
|G|^{2}=\dim(\mathcal{H})^{2}
operators which are all linearly independent, as

The

To find an operator basis better suited for the entangled pairs representation, one possibility is to simply transform the

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{g}\mathcal{X}_{h}

(248)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widetilde{l}\in C}V_{\underline{g}}^{*}\otimes\chi_{\theta(\widetilde{l})+\theta(\widetilde{h})-\delta\theta(\widetilde{l},\widetilde{h})}(g)|{\widetilde{l}+\widetilde{h}}\rangle\langle{\widetilde{l}}|\otimes V_{\Gamma_{\omega}^{-1}(\bar{h})}V_{\Gamma^{-1}_{\omega}(\bar{\delta}(\widetilde{l},\widetilde{h}))}V_{\underline{g}}\chi^{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{g}}(\underline{h}+\bar{\delta}(\widetilde{l},\widetilde{h}))

\displaystyle=\chi_{\bar{g}}^{\mathcal{G}}(\underline{h})\chi_{\theta(\widetilde{g})}(h)\mathcal{X}_{h}\mathcal{R}_{g}

where the last line follows from our character decomposition formula (

For our analysis of SPT ground states, we further need to know how

Proposition 14

Any multi-site symmetric operator

\mathcal{O}_{sym}=\sum_{\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\in G^{L}}C_{\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}}\delta\left(\sum_{i}h_{i}\right)\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{R}_{g_{i}}\mathcal{X}_{h_{i}},

Proof.

The proof is straightforward, and simply involves making use of the commutation relation (

, we have

\mathcal{O}\mathcal{R}_{g}^{\otimes L}=\mathcal{R}_{g}^{\otimes L}\sum_{\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\in G^{L}}\chi_{g}(\sum_{i}h_{i})C_{\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{R}_{g_{i}}\mathcal{X}_{h_{i}}.

(257)

Since all of the terms in this linear combination are linearly independent, character orthgoonality means that
\mathcal{O}

(261)

to be the remainder in
\mathcal{G}
accumulated when adding the components of
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}
up to the position

.

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward consequence of the decomposition (
256

\mathbf{h}
in (
256
) is restricted to those vectors such that
\sum_{i}h_{i}=e
, together with the addition rule (

).

∎

Note that because we have chosen
\widetilde{g}_{\bullet}=e
, the

(that is, they act trivially on

As written in (

|\psi_{\omega}\rangle

. When

, symmetry is restored only when one performs a compensating action on
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
, which is reflected in the constraint on
\bar{\mathbf{h}}
in (

This means that when we look only at

, the errors in (

\sum\bar{h}_{i}
is

— whose performance is predicated on the assumption that only symmetric errors are present — will yield garbage. In order to use

to perform phase recognition, we need to convert the errors appearing in (

. Doing this is the task addressed in the following subsection.

K.4
Removing the mixed errors

Our goal in this subsection is to construct a procedure by which the errors in (
258
) can be converted into a set of errors which are symmetric when restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
.

Suppose we are given the value of
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}
for a particular term in the linear combination of (
258
). We can then proceed as follows. Starting from site
i=1
, we move along the chain and compute a running sum
\sum_{j=1}^{i}\theta(\widetilde{h}_{j})
. Each time this sum ‘overflows’ in
C
and generates a remainder of an amount
\mathsf{rem}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})_{i}\in\mathcal{G}
, one acts on the wavefunction with the operator
S^{R}_{-\Gamma^{-1}_{\omega}(\mathsf{rem}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})_{i}),i}
. Sweeping across the entire chain in this matter thus ensures that one is left with a state in which
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
is acted on by
S^{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}},
where
\sum_{i}\bar{h}_{i}^{\prime}=e
. Adding up the values of
\widetilde{h}_{i}
in a local way ensures that the
S^{R}_{-\Gamma^{-1}_{\omega}(\mathsf{rem}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})_{i}),i}
operators are inserted in such a way that the combined product of shift operators acting on
|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle
always combine into charge-neutral blocks over distances of a correlation length
\xi
(with
\xi
set by the coefficients
C_{\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}}
as usual).

The process just described is results in the following protocol to eliminate the mixed errors and perform phase recognition:

1.

Measure the flavor degrees of freedom in the computational basis of
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
. Let the outcome of this measurement be
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}
(after measuring, the subsystem
\mathcal{H}_{flav}
can be discarded, as it will be decoupled from
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
for the remainder of the protocol).

2.

Use an
O(L)
amount of classical data processing to compute
\mathsf{rem}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})
.

3.

Apply
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{rem}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})
to the measured state, where

\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{rem}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})\equiv\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L}S^{R}_{-\Gamma^{-1}_{\omega}(\mathsf{rem}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})_{i}),i}

(262)

is a product of single-site unitaries that converts the mixed errors into errors acting only on
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
.

4.

Proceed by applying the error correction circuit
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{\omega}
to
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
.

While
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{rem}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})
is simply a tensor product of single-qudit rotations, the data processing involved in computing
\mathsf{rem}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})_{i}
is non-local, since
\mathsf{rem}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})_{i}
depends on
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{j}
for all
j\leq i
. Also note that we do not need to post-select on the measurement outcomes
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}
: any outcome is as good as any other for the purposes of performing phase recognition, as each set of terms with a fixed
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}
in the linear combination (
258
) contain all of the information about the universal aspects of the SPT phase.

That step 4 of the above protocol sufficies to perform phase recognition is established by the following proposition:

Proposition 15
.

For any
|\psi_{\omega}\rangle\in\mathsf{SPT}_{\omega}
, the state
|\psi_{\omega}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}}\rangle
obtained after measuring the flavor degrees of freedom to obtain the outcome
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}
and applying the corrective circuit
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{rem}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})
can be written as

|\psi_{\omega}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}}\rangle=\sum_{\underline{\mathbf{g}}\in G^{L}}C_{\underline{\mathbf{g}}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{G}}_{\underline{\mathbf{g}}}|\Psi_{\omega}\rangle,

(263)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\underline{g}}^{\mathcal{G}}
is the action of the symmetry restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
:

\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{G}}_{\underline{g}}=V_{\underline{g}}^{*}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{flav}\otimes V_{\underline{g}}.

(264)

Proof.

By construction, we have

|\psi^{\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}}_{\omega}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{g}\in G^{L},\bar{\mathbf{h}}\in\mathcal{G}^{L}}C_{\mathbf{g},\bar{\mathbf{h}}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{g}}S^{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}}|\Psi_{\omega}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}}\rangle,

(265)

where the
C_{\mathbf{g},\bar{\mathbf{h}}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})
are again some set of complex coefficients, and

|\Psi_{\omega}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{g}\in G^{L}}\text{Tr}[A^{g_{1}}_{\widetilde{h}_{1}}\cdots A^{g_{L}}_{\widetilde{h}_{L}}]

(266)

is the canonical reference state with the flavor degrees of freedom fixed to be
\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}
. Since the action of the corrective circuit
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{rem}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})
yields a state in which the errors are symmetric when restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{MNC}
, the coefficients
C_{\mathbf{g},\bar{\mathbf{h}}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{h}})
appearing in (
265
) are now constrained to satisfy
\sum_{i}\bar{h}_{i}=e
(c.f. (
260
)). Thus when acting on
|\Psi^{\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}}_{\omega}\rangle
,
S^{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}}
can be replaced by a product of
\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}}
operators for the same reasons as discussed in the proof of theorem
3
. When acting on
|\Psi^{\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}}_{\omega}\rangle
the distinction between between
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{g}}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{G}}_{\underline{\mathbf{g}}}
is not important, since the action of the two differs only by an unimportant overall phase. Thus there is no important dependence on
\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}
in the sum, and so after changing notation for the complex coefficients appearing in the linear combination, we indeed arrive at (
263
).

∎

That this allows us to perform phase recognition then follows from the fact that
|\psi_{\omega}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}}\rangle
has the same structure as the groud state wavefunction of a MNC SPT phase. This means that our previously developed RG circuit
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{\omega}
can simply be applied directly to
|\psi_{\omega}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}}\rangle
, with the remainder of the phase recognition procedure proceeding as in the MNC case.
