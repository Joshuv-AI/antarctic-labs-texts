1.
Introduction

There has been growing interest in polynomial equation (and word equation) in associative algebras (and groups), in recent years.
This line of research has an origin in the work of two great mathematicians of the last century, namely Armand Borel (for group-theoretic questions) and Irving Kaplansky (for ring theoretic questions).
We should keep in mind that the results for groups differ substantially from those concerning algebras, mainly because of the existence of non-trivial identities (polynomial identities, more precisely) for simple matrix algebras, which can not occur for simple matrix groups due to Tit’s alternative.
There is a plethora of tools used to solve these problems in the case of algebras, mostly coming from algebraic geometry, and arithmetic properties of polynomial equations in associative algebras,
whereas people have been using algebraic groups, character theory, and deep results
from the classification of finite group theory to handle these problems in case of groups. Let us start with a brief introduction to both of the problems in the subsequent discussion.

Let
F_{n}
denotes the free group on
n
symbols
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
and
K
be an algebraically closed field.
The elements of the group ring
K[F_{n}]
will be called as

polynomials in non-commutative variables
. Given an algebra
\mathcal{A}
over
K
and
p\in K[F_{n}]
, consider the following map

\displaystyle\Phi_{p}:\mathcal{A}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A},\quad(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\mapsto p(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}).

These are known as
polynomial maps
on
\mathcal{A}
. Obviously, we need the polynomial to be in non-commutative variables as the algebra need not be commutative. Images of polynomials are crucial to answer different questions of non-commutative algebra. A problem due to Kaplansky
[
14
]
was settled down after the construction of central polynomials
(i.e., a polynomial
f
such that
f(\mathcal{A})
lies inside
Z(\mathcal{A})
) by Formanek
[
8
]
. There are questions regarding the image of polynomial maps on an associative algebra, for example,
M_{n}(K)
, motivated by several questions raised by Kaplansky.
Note that the image can vary substantially depending upon the polynomial. For example, it can be a field of scalars when the polynomial is multilinear central (e.g. multilinearization of the polynomial of
[x_{1},x_{2}]^{2}
is a central polynomial of the smallest possible degree for
M_{2}(K)
),
or take the case
f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}x_{2}-x_{2}x_{1}
, where the image is
[M_{n}(K),M_{n}(K)]
, the set of all trace
0
matrices (this is due to a theorem of Albert and Muckenhoupt
[
1
]
).
Determining the image is difficult, as the image need not be a vector space (at least not in an obvious way), although it is a cone invariant under matrix conjugation. At this point let us digress a little, to give a glimpse of the growth of the problem in the context of group theory.

The parallel study in group theory is concerned with the maps corresponding to an element of
F_{n}
, known as
word maps
. This problem is motivated by the
classical Waring problem in number theory
which asks whether every positive integer can be written as
g(k)
many
k
-th powers for a suitable function
g
. For a prime
q
, to investigate
the image of a word map in pro-
q
-groups, it is important to know the image of Lie polynomials over the algebra of
n\times n
matrices. Indeed, this has helped Zelmanov
[
23
]
to prove that for

q\gg n
, the free pro-
q
-group can not be embedded into the algebra of
n\times n
matrices. It has been proved that for semisimple algebraic group
G
and any word
w\in F_{n}
,
the map
w:G^{n}\longrightarrow G
is dominant, by Borel
[
2
]
and Larsen
[
15
]
independently. This has further motivated mathematicians to investigate word maps on finite simple groups of Lie type.
A recent breakthrough in this direction is the affirmation of
Ore’s conjecture (which states that the commutator map corresponding to the word
xyx^{-1}y^{-1}\in F_{2}
is surjective in the case of finite non-abelian simple groups), by Liebeck, O’Brien,
Shalev and Tiep
[
17
]
, using the methods from character theory.
Indeed the authors prove that if
G
is any quasisimple classical group over a finite field,
then every element of
G
is a commutator, using character-theoretic results due to
Frobenius. Later they proved results about the product of squares in finite non-abelian
simple group in
[
18
]
.
They proved that
every element of a non-abelian finite simple group
G
is a product of two squares. For a survey of these problems in the context of group theory, we refer the reader to the excellent article
[
20
]
due to Shalev and a survey article
[
16
]
due to Larsen, Shalev and Tiep.

Coming back to the case of associative algebras, let us start by stating the famous Kaplansky-Lvov conjecture which is concerned with the image of multilinear maps in non-commutative variables over the algebra
M_{n}(K)
of
n\times n
matrices. It states the following;

Conjecture 1
.

Let
p
be a multilinear polynomial in
m
variables, over a field
k
. Consider the image set of
M_{n}(k)
under the
polynomial map. Then
\textup{Im}\,\Phi_{p}
is either
\{0\}
,
\{aI:a\in k\}
,
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(k)
or
M_{n}(k)
, where
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(k)
denotes the set of all
trace
0
matrices in

.

At the time of writing this paper the conjecture stands open for
n\geq 2
. The case
n=2
has been solved for quadratically closed fields in
[
10
]
by Kanel-Belov, Malev, and Rowen and for

substantial progress has been made in
[
11
]
by the same authors. One of the lemma in
[
10
]
(essentially a corollary of a theorem of Amitsur) is that a generic matrix algebra with characteristic coefficients adjoined
does not contain zero divisors. This enables to study of eigenvalues of elements of the image of the polynomial. But, unfortunately, this method of comparing eigenvalues starts to break down for larger
n
. This necessitates the use of more intrinsic machinery of algebraic geometry for the case of
3\times 3
matrices.
Some partial results are known due to
[
12
]
, in case
n=4
. For survey of these results in case of algebras, we suggest the reader the beautiful article
[
13
]
by Kanel-Belov, Malev, Rowen, and Yavich. Since over an algebraically closed field, any matrix can be conjugated to an upper triangular matrix, variations of the Kaplansky-Lvov conjecture are being studied for upper triangular matrix algebras
T_{m}(K)
, in recent times. The following conjecture was posed in 2019 by Fagundes and de Mello
[
7
]
.

Conjecture 2
.

The images of a multilinear polynomial in non-commutative variables over a field
K
, on the algebra
T_{m}(K)
is a vector space.

This conjecture has been settled recently by Luo, Wang in
[
19
]
and Gargate, de Mello in
[

]
independently. The results are as follows.

Theorem

(Luo, Wang)
.

Let

and

be two integers. Let

be an infinite field and

be a nonzero multilinear polynomial in non-commutative variables over

for some integer
-1\leq k\leq\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]-1
, where

is the integer part of
\frac{m}{2}
.

Theorem

(Gargate, de Mello)
.

Let

on

is

T_{m}(K)

In this paper, we consider the problem of finding the images of non-commutative polynomials evaluated on upper triangular matrix algebras.

Question 1

Let
p

This question has been answered completely in case of

Theorem

(Theorem 3.1,

)
.

Let

\displaystyle p(T_{2}(K))=T_{2}(K)\setminus\left\{\begin{pmatrix}c&a\\

Preliminaries

Throughout this section
T_{m}(K)
will denote the algebra of upper triangular matrices of size
m
over the field
K
. For a ring
R
, the set of all invertible elements of
R
will be denoted by
R^{\times}
. Consider
T_{m}(K)
as a subspace of
T_{m+1}(K)
by the embedding

\displaystyle T_{m+1}(K)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&\vec{v}\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}:A\in T_{m}(K),\vec{v}\in K^{m},a\in K\right\}.

Then we have a chain of algebras as follows

\displaystyle K=T_{1}(K)\subseteq T_{2}(K)\subseteq T_{3}(K)\cdots.

We reserve the notation
T_{m}(K)^{(t)}
to denote all the matrices in
T_{m}(K)
such that
ij^{\text{th}}
entry is
0
whenever
j-i\leq t
.

Example 2.1
.

Take
m=4
and
t=2
. Then

\displaystyle T_{4}(K)^{(2)}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&x\\
&0&0&0\\
&&0&0\\
&&&0\end{pmatrix}:x\in K\right\}.

Given a polynomial
f\in K[F_{n}]
and an associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
,

is called a
polynomial identity of
\mathcal{A}
if

\displaystyle f(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})=0,\quad\text{for all }a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}.

For an algebra
\mathcal{A}
, we denote the set of all polynomial identities as
\mathrm{Id}(\mathcal{A})
. We have the following sequence of subsets

\displaystyle\mathrm{Id}(K)\supseteq\mathrm{Id}(T_{2}(K))\supseteq\mathrm{Id}(T_{3}(K))\supseteq\cdots.

Example 2.2
.

Consider the polynomial
p\in K[F_{2}]
as
p(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}x_{2}-x_{2}x_{1})^{2}
. Take
u_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&c_{i}\\
0&b_{i}\end{pmatrix}\in T_{2}(K)
for

. Then we have

\displaystyle\left[\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&c_{1}\\
&b_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&c_{2}\\
&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&c_{2}\\
&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&c_{1}\\
&b_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&a_{1}c_{2}+b_{2}c_{1}-a_{2}c_{1}-b_{1}c_{2}\\
&0\end{pmatrix}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
&0\end{pmatrix}.

But note that

\displaystyle\left[\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\
&1&3\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&4&0\\
&2&5\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}1&4&0\\
&2&5\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\
&1&3\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}\right]^{2}\neq\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
&0&0\\
&&0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus,
p\in\mathrm{Id}(T_{2}(K))
but
p\notin\mathrm{Id}(T_{3}(K))
. This motivates us to define the following.

Definition 2.3
.

A polynomial

will be called to have

but

where

, and will be denoted by

. A polynomial

will be said to have order

We would like to point out that this coincides with the definition of commutator degree of a polynomial
[
9
, Definition 3.1]
.

Example 2.4
.

The polynomial

The polynomial identities for the algebra of upper triangular matrix are well known, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.5
.

[
6

Let
K
be an infinite field and let

be the algebra of

The polynomial identity

\displaystyle[x_{1},x_{2}][x_{3},x_{4}]\cdots[x_{2m-1},x_{2m}]=0

, where
[x_{l},x_{l+1}]=x_{1}x_{l+1}-x_{l+1}x_{l}

Next we recall some terminology from algebraic geometry, for completeness. The space
K^{n}
will be referred to as

affine
n
-space
. A

is given by common zeros of ideal in

is closed if there exists an ideal

such that

\displaystyle T=\{(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\in K^{n}:f(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})=0\quad\text{for all }f\in I\}.

The set of complements of these closed sets form the so called

A subset

will be called

, if for all open sets
V\subseteq K^{n}
, we have
U\cap V\neq\emptyset
.
This is the usual definition of dense subsets in topological sense. A subset of the affine space will always be considered to be endowed with Zariski subspace topology. We end this section by providing the structure of the general polynomial we will be working with. Henceforth,
p\in K[F_{n}]
will be assumed to be
satisfying

, and

(1)

p(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sum\limits_{1\leq i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\leq n}\mu_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}\right),

where

is the degree of the polynomial and
\mu_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}\in K
.

In the section we will generalize the results obtained in

Lemma 5.1
.

Inside

we have the following similarity of matrices.

\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&\cdots&a_{1m}\\
&a_{22}&\cdots&a_{2m}\\
&&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&a_{mm}\\

Let

We can give the immediate result using the above lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
p\in K[F_{n}]
satisfying
p(K)\neq 0
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\left\{A=(a_{\alpha\beta})\in T_{m}(K):a_{\delta\delta}\neq a_{\sigma\sigma}\text{ for all }1\leq\delta\neq\sigma\leq m\right\}\subseteq p\left(T_{m}(K)\right).

Proof.

Let
A
satisfy that all the diagonal entries are pairwise distinct. Then the following equivalence follows from
5.1
.

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&\cdots&a_{1m}\\
&a_{22}&\cdots&a_{2m}\\
&&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&a_{mm}\\
\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathrm{diag}\left(a_{11},a_{22},\ldots,a_{mm}\right).

Now note that, if
u_{i}\in T_{m}(K)
,

,

, we have that

\displaystyle v\cdot p(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})\cdot v^{-1}=p(vu_{1}v^{-1},vu_{2}v^{-1},\ldots,vu_{n}v^{-1}).

Indeed,
\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}(vx_{i_{j}}v^{-1})=vx_{i_{1}}v^{-1}\cdot vx_{i_{2}}v^{-1}\cdots vx_{i_{l-1}}v^{-1}vx_{i_{l}}v^{-1}
. Hence, to prove the containment it is enough to show that

\displaystyle\textbf{diag}(a_{11},a_{22},\ldots,a_{mm})\in p\left(T_{m}(K)\right).

Since

, for

and

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
p\in K[F_{n}]
satisfy
p(K)\neq 0
. Then
p(T_{m}(K))
is a Zariski dense subset of

.

Proof.

This has been proved in case of

in
[
21

]
. We present a proof for the general case, necessarily imitating the proof therein. Consider
T_{m}(K)
as the
affine space
K^{\frac{m(m+1)}{2}}
.
By
5.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\left\{A=(a_{\alpha\beta})\in T_{m}(K):a_{\delta\delta}\neq a_{\sigma\sigma}\text{ for all }1\leq\delta\neq\sigma\leq m\right\}\subseteq p(T_{m}(K)).

Hence, it is enough to prove that
\mathcal{D}
is dense in
T_{m}(K)
.

Take a point

and an open set

containing

. Hence, there exists an ideal

of
K\left[y_{1},\ldots,y_{\frac{m(m+1)}{2}}\right]
such that
U=K^{\frac{m(m+1)}{2}}\setminus Z(I)
, where
Z(I)
denotes the zero set of

. This implies there exists
f\in I
with

that is

f\left(a_{11},a_{22},\ldots,a_{mm},a_{12},a_{23},\ldots,a_{m-1m},\ldots,a_{1m}\right)\neq 0.

If
a_{ii}\neq a_{jj}
for all
i\neq j
, then

. So we will assume that there exists some
i_{0}\neq j_{0}
but
a_{i_{0}i_{0}}=a_{j_{0}j_{0}}
. Without loss of generality assume that

and
j_{0}=2
. Set
a_{11}^{\prime}=a_{11}
. We want to find
a_{22}^{\prime}\neq a_{22}
such that
f\left(a_{11}^{\prime},a_{22}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{mm},a_{12},a_{23},\ldots,a_{m-1m},\ldots,a_{1m}\right)\neq 0

Note that the function is not identically zero. As

is an algebraically closed field (hence infinite), there exists

such that

Since

is infinite, this procedure can be continued to obtain an element

such that

Proposition 5.4
.

Let

be a polynomial in non-commutative variable. Suppose

. Then

.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{ord}(p)=m-1
, we have

but

. Since
p\left(T_{m-1}(K)\right)=0
, the multi-index
p
-inductive polynomials of index size less than or equal to

are identically zero. Hence,
p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)\subseteq T_{m}(K)^{(m-2)}
. For showing the other inequality, we take a matrix

X_{ij}=\begin{cases}x,&\text{ if }i=1,j=n\\

Since

, there exists a multi-index

such that

There has been a growing interest about the Waring problem for matrix algebras. For example, it has been proved that any traceless matrix can be written as sum of two matrices from

The above example motivates the following proposition.

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
p\in K[F_{n}]
be a polynomial in non-commutative variables. Let
\mathrm{ord}(p)=t
and
I_{p}\geq 2
.
Then

\underbrace{p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)+p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)+\ldots+p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)}_{d\text{-times}}=T_{m}(K)^{(t-1)},

where
d=\min\{t,m-t\}
.

Proof.

Let us continue with the notations of the discussion at the beginning of this section.
Let us write
X=X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{t}
, with

R_{b}(X_{a})=\begin{cases}R_{b}(X)&\text{ if }b\equiv a\pmod{t}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},

where
R_{b}(A)
denotes the
b^{\text{th}}
row of the matrix
A
, for
1\leq a\leq t
. We claim that
X_{a}\in p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)
for all
1\leq a\leq t
.
Note that if
m-t<t
, then in the above decomposition of
X
we will be left with only
m-t
many terms. This justifies the choice of
d
in the theorem. It remains to prove our claim.
We go through the following steps for constructing the matrices
u_{i_{j}}^{(a)}
for
1\leq a\leq d
. Firstly set the diagonal of
u_{i_{j}}
to be
\mathrm{diag}(\vec{a}_{11}(i_{j}),\vec{a}_{22}(i_{j}),\ldots,\vec{a}_{mm}(i_{j}))
. The next choices will be made in several steps.

Step
1
: Choose
u_{i_{j}}^{(a)}\left((k-1)t+j+a-1,(k-1)t+j+a\right)=1
, for all
1\leq j\leq t-1
,
1\leq a\leq\min{\{t,m-t\}}
and a suitable choice of
k\geq 1
and
kt+a\leq m
.
We set

u_{i_{t}}^{(a)}\left(kt+a-1,kt+a\right)=\left(\lambda^{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t}}_{(k-1)t+a,(k-1)t+a+1,\ldots,kt+a}\right)^{-1}x_{(k-1)t+a,kt+a},

for the choices of
k
as above.
All the other
(c,c+1)
entries of
u_{i_{j}}^{(a)}
are taken to be zero.

Step
T+1

(T\geq 1,~kt+a\leq m)
: For
kt+a+T\leq m
,
We define

\displaystyle u_{i_{t}}^{(a)}\left(kt+a-1,kt+a+T\right)=\left(\lambda^{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t}}_{(k-1)t+a,(k-1)t+a+1,\ldots,kt+a-1,kt+a+T}\right)^{-1}x_{(k-1)t+a,kt+a+T}

for a suitable choice of
\beta^{\prime}s
. All other entries of
U_{i_{j}}^{(a)}
are taken to be zero. Now let us define the set
\Pi(j)=\{\gamma|i_{\gamma}=i_{j}\}
and
\pi(j)=\min\Pi(j)
.
Further, take
\Delta(p)=\{\pi(j)|1\leq j\leq t\}
. For
j\in\Delta(p)
, we define

\displaystyle u_{i_{j}}^{(a)}=\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\Pi(j)}u_{i_{\gamma}}^{(a)}.

For all
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}\setminus\Delta(p)
, define
u_{i_{j}}^{(a)}=\mathrm{diag}(\vec{a}_{11}(i_{j}),\vec{a}_{22}(i_{j}),\ldots,\vec{a}_{mm}(i_{j}))
.
Then with the prescribed choice we have that
X_{a}=p\left(u_{1}^{(a)},u_{2}^{(a)},\ldots,u_{n}^{(a)}\right)\in p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)
.
This proves that if all the
x_{ij}
’s of
X
are nonzero then we have

X\in\underbrace{p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)+p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)+\ldots+p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)}_{\min\{t,m-t\}\text{-times}}.

The result follows immediately.
∎

We note down here the following result in case
\mathrm{ord}(p)=2
, where it can be shown
d=2
without using
5.16
.

Proposition 5.17
.

Let
p\in K[F_{n}]
be a polynomial in non-commutative variables. Suppose
\mathrm{ord}(p)=2
. Then
p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)+p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)=T_{m}(K)^{(1)}
.

Proof.

Assume
X=\left(x_{ab}\right)\in T_{m}(K)^{(1)}
satisfies
x_{ab}\neq 0
for all
b-a\geq 2
. We want to show that
X\in p\left(T_{M}(K)\right)
. Continuing with the notation of this section, we see that
I_{p}=1
or
2
. If
I_{p}=1
, then the result follows from
5.11
.
If
I_{p}=2
and
p_{i_{1}i_{2}}p_{i_{2}i_{1}}\neq 0
, then the result follows from
5.14
.
Hence we are left with the case when either
p_{i_{0}j_{0}}
or
p_{j_{0}i_{0}}
is the zero function. Without loss of generality, we assume that
p_{i_{0}j_{0}}
to be nonzero. We can assume that
p_{i_{0}i_{0}}
and
p_{j_{0}j_{0}}
are the zero polynomials. Using
4.8
, we assume that

\lambda_{\sigma(1),\sigma(2)}=p_{i_{0}j_{0}}\left(\vec{\mathbf{a}}_{\{\sigma(1),\sigma(2)\}}\right)\neq 0,~\text{for all }\sigma\in S_{m}.

We choose the matrix
u_{i_{0}}
as follows. For
l\leq m-1
and
1\leq s\leq m-l
, choose the entries to be

\displaystyle u_{i_{0}}(1,2)

\displaystyle=u_{i_{0}}(2,3)=\cdots=u_{i_{0}}(l-1,l)=1

\displaystyle u_{i_{0}}(l,l+1)

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{l-1,l+1}\cdot u_{i_{0}}({l-1,l})\right)^{-1}\left[x_{l-1,l+1}\right]

\displaystyle u_{i_{0}}(l,l+2)

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{l-1,l+2}\cdot u_{i_{0}}({l-1,l})\right)^{-1}\left[x_{l-1,l+2}-\beta_{l-1,l+2}\right]

\displaystyle u_{i_{0}}(l,l+s)

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{l-1,l+s}\cdot u_{i_{0}}({l-1,l})\right)^{-1}.\left[x_{l-1,l+s}-\beta_{l-1,l+s}\right],

for suitable choices of
\beta
’s. Further take
u_{j_{0}}
to be the matrix with
(i,i)
diagonal entries to be
\vec{a}_{ii}(j_{0})
and all others above the diagonal entries to be
1
. For all
i\neq i_{0},j_{0}
we take

u_{i}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\vec{a}_{11}(i),\vec{a}_{22}(i),\ldots,\vec{a}_{mm}(i)\right).

Then with the prescribed choices, we have that
p\left(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}\right)=X
. Now the result is immediate.
∎

5.7
,
5.11

, we now state the following theorem which follows from the mentioned results.

Theorem 5.18
.

Let
K
be an algebraically closed field,
p\in K\left[F_{n}\right]
be a non-commutative polynomial such that
\mathrm{ord}(p)=t
such that the following results hold:

(1)

If
t=0
, then
p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)
is Zariski dense in
T_{m}(K)
.

(2)

If
t=1
, then
p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)=T_{m}(K)^{(0)}
.

(3)

If
1<t<m-1
, then
p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)\subset T_{m}(K)^{(t-1)}
, and equality might not hold in general. Furthermore, for every
m
and
t
there exists
d
such that each element of
T_{m}(K)^{(t-1)}
can be written as
d
many elements from
p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)
.

(4)

If
t=m-1
, then
p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)=T_{m}(K)^{(m-2)}
.

(5)

For
t\geq m
,
p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)=\{0\}
.

We believe that it is true that for all
m\geq 5
and
2\leq\mathrm{ord}(p)\leq m-2
, the value of
d
is
2
and hence we propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture
.

Let
p\in K[F_{n}]
be a polynomial in non-commutative variables. Suppose
\mathrm{ord}(p)=t
where
1<t<m-1
. Then
p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)+p\left(T_{m}(K)\right)=T_{m}(K)^{(t-1)}
.

6.
Image of word maps on
T_{m}(K)^{\times}

Let
G
be an algebraic group. For
k\geq 2
the map
P_{k}:G\to G
defined as
P_{k}(g)=g^{k}
is known as the
k^{\text{th}}
-power map
. This section is concerned with the question when such a map has a dense image. A well known result of A. Borel states that the image of a word map (in particular a power map) on a semisimple algebraic group
G
is Zariski dense, see
[
2
]
. Note that the image of the power map is not always dense for real points of an algebraic group in real topology. For example, in case of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
the image of
P_{2}
is not dense in real topology, but its image is Zariski dense in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. We present similar kind of result concerning Zariski density of the image of a word map on Borel subgroups of
GL_{n}(K)
which are by definition maximal connected solvable subgroup. We will present a general result concerning any element
w\in F_{n}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
w\in F_{n}
be a word which is not a group law. Then
w\left(T_{m}(K)^{\times}\right)
is dense in
T_{m}(K)^{\times}
. Furthermore, for
B
a Borel subgroup of
GL_{m}(K)
we have that
w\left(B\right)
is Zariski dense in
B
.

The proof is similar to the proof of
5.3
. For the completeness we are writing the proof.

Proof of
6.1
.

Note that
T_{m}(K)^{\times}
can be viewed as
\mathcal{K}=\left(K^{\times}\right)^{m}\times K^{\frac{m(m-1)}{2}}
with the subspace topology.
By
5.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\left\{A=(a_{\alpha\beta})\in T_{m}(K)^{\times}:a_{\delta\delta}\neq a_{\sigma\sigma}\text{ for all }1\leq\delta\neq\sigma\leq m\right\}\subseteq w\left(T_{m}(K)^{\times}\right).

Hence, it is enough to prove that
\mathcal{D}
is dense in
T_{m}(K)^{\times}
.

Take a point
P=\left(a_{11},a_{22},\ldots,a_{mm},a_{12},a_{23},\ldots,a_{m-1m},\ldots,a_{1m}\right)\in\mathcal{K}
and an open set
U
containing
P
. Hence, there exists an ideal
I
of
K\left[y_{1},\ldots,y_{\frac{m(m+1)}{2}}\right]
such that
U=\left(K^{\frac{m(m+1)}{2}}\setminus Z(I)\right)\cap\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}\setminus Z(I)
, where
Z(I)
denotes the zero set of
I
. This implies there exists
f\in I
with
f(P)\neq 0
that is

f\left(a_{11},a_{22},\ldots,a_{mm},a_{12},a_{23},\ldots,a_{m-1m},\ldots,a_{1m}\right)\neq 0.

If
a_{ii}\neq a_{jj}
for all
i\neq j
, then
P\in\mathcal{D}
. So we will assume that there exists some
i_{0}\neq j_{0}
but
a_{i_{0}i_{0}}=a_{j_{0}j_{0}}
. Without loss of generality assume that
i_{0}=1
and
j_{0}=2
. Set
a_{11}^{\prime}=a_{11}
. We want to find
a_{22}^{\prime}\notin\left\{a_{22},0\right\}
such that
f\left(a_{11}^{\prime},a_{22}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{mm},a_{12},a_{23},\ldots,a_{m-1m},\ldots,a_{1m}\right)\neq 0
. Define

h_{22}(x)=f\left(a_{11}^{\prime},x,\ldots,a_{mm},a_{12},a_{23},\ldots,a_{m-1m},\ldots,a_{1m}\right).

Note that the function is not identically zero. As
K
is an algebraically closed field (hence infinite), there exists
a_{22}^{\prime}\notin\left\{a_{11}^{\prime},0\right\}
such that
h_{22}\left(a_{22}^{\prime}\right)\neq 0
.

Since
K
is infinite, this procedure can be continued to obtain an element

P^{\prime}=\left(a_{11}^{\prime},a_{22}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{mm}^{\prime},a_{12},\ldots,a_{1m}\right)

such that
a_{ii}^{\prime}\neq a_{jj}^{\prime}\neq 0
for all
i\neq j
and
f\left(P^{\prime}\right)\neq 0
. Hence,
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
.

The later part of the theorem follows from the fact that all Borel subgroups of
GL_{m}(K)
are conjugate.
∎
