Attention based End to end network for Offline Writer Identification on Word level data
Vineet Kumar, Suresh Sundaram

Abstract
Writer identification due to its widespread application in various fields has gained popularity over the years. In scenarios where optimum handwriting samples are available, whether they be in the form of a single line, a sentence, or an entire page, writer identification algorithms have demonstrated noteworthy levels of accuracy. However, in scenarios where only a limited number of handwritten samples are available, particularly in the form of word images, there is a significant scope for improvement. In this paper, we propose a writer identification system based on an attention-driven Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). The system is trained utilizing image segments, known as fragments, extracted from word images, employing a pyramid-based strategy. This methodology enables the system to capture a comprehensive representation of the data, encompassing both fine-grained details and coarse features across various levels of abstraction. These extracted fragments serve as the training data for the convolutional network, enabling it to learn a more robust representation compared to traditional convolution-based networks trained on word images. Additionally, the paper explores the integration of an attention mechanism to enhance the representational power of the learned features. The efficacy of the proposed algorithm is evaluated on three benchmark databases, demonstrating its proficiency in writer identification tasks, particularly in scenarios with limited access to handwriting data.

II
RESEARCH FRAMEWORK

In this paper, we introduce an end-to-end writer identification system based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) designed to determine the authorship of handwritten documents using word images. Traditional neural network methods typically involve inputting the image directly into the network for feature extraction and subsequent classification. While effective for page-level or sentence-level data, these methods often falter in scenarios where access to handwritten samples is restricted, such as with word images containing fewer than 5 letters. Several factors contribute to this decline in performance:

•

The majority of existing deep learning algorithms are trained to prioritize the most discriminative parts of an image, neglecting other equally vital local features crucial for accurate writer identification.

•

Limited handwritten data with diverse content poses challenges in effectively encoding dependencies between neighboring strokes of the word image.

These factors collectively contribute to the reduced performance of the system in scenarios with constrained access to handwritten samples, particularly those involving word images.

A more effective strategy for addressing this challenge involves viewing the input image not as a single entity but as a collection of fragments that encapsulate the structural attributes of the image. This approach provides a notable advantage by shifting the focus away from emphasizing the most discriminative feature of the input training image. Instead, it compels the network to acquire a more resilient representation from a collection of fragments. Operating on this principle, rather than employing the entire word image for feature extraction and classification, we employ features extracted from a collection of local patches (referred to as fragments) to identify the writer of the word image.

For extracting fragments from an input image SIFT algorithm
[
42
]
is employed, which uses a multi-scale analysis to locate regions containing distinct features ranging from characters to allographas and graphemes from an input word image. These fragments are then fed into a CNN network featuring two parallel streams of convolution modules. One of which is trained on writer-dependent samples, while the other is trained on handwritten samples that is language as well as writer-independent. In this setup, the former stream encodes the writer-specific local structure from the word fragment, while the latter captures writer-independent general features. This configuration is expected to outperform the baseline, which relies solely on the writer-dependent model, as a writer module trained solely on writer data may lead to overfitting, resulting in the loss of generality of the writer features. By integrating the writer features learned by the writer-independent auxiliary network, we tend to introduce regularization into the overall network, aiming to mitigate the impact of overfitting
[
43
]
.

In summary, the key contributions of this paper include:

•

Investigating writer characterization through fragments extracted from word images.

•

Proposing a multi-stream end-to-end Convolution network trained on writer-dependent and independent datasets showcasing its effectiveness in capturing writer features.

•

Examining the impact of integrating an attention mechanism on the overall performance of the system.

III
PATCH EXTRACTION USING SIFT ALGORITHM

A writer identification algorithm depends on characterizing the writer information obtained from input images, which can be collected from pages, text lines, or individual words. The underyling information is extracted from these input by analyzing attributes such as image texture, graphemes, allographs, or characters, representing different levels of observation. These extracted features impact the overall performance of the system. As such, feature selection forms an integral part of any writer identification system. Majority of the techniques (traditional as well as deep learning based)
[
11
,
12
,
20
,
6
,
44
]
use dense sampling, by employing a fixed-sized window in an overlapping/ non-overlapping framework to segment regions around an image. However, as the discriminative ability of different characters in handwritten text varies, selecting areas around an interest point or keypoint may prove more effective in describing a writer compared to random sampling. Consequently, interest point-based methods have gained widespread application in the field of writer identification. These approaches typically employ a fixed-sized window to extract the region of interest around the image, incorporating features such as junctions
[
22
]
and corners
[
45
,
32
]
to characterize a writer.

One notable limitation of employing a fixed-size feature detection window is its challenge in accurately capturing components of varying sizes, especially when image features demonstrate frequent fluctuations in shapes and dimensions. Therefore, rather than adopting a fixed-size window approach that identifies interest points at a predefined level of abstraction, we turn to the Scale-Invariant Feature Detector, commonly known as SIFT
[
42
]
. This detector utilizes a pyramidal-based method to detect and extract key points at multiple resolutions, thereby facilitating localization in diverse scenarios.

This algorithm can be broken into the following steps. In the initial phase, the original image undergoes successive down-sampling, forming a Gaussian pyramid with each level referred to as an octave. This pyramid is further divided into several sub-levels by convolving the initial image with Difference of Gaussian (DoG) filters of varying variance. In the subsequent stage, the DoG images produced in the preceding step are analyzed to derive a collection of stable key points. Following this, orientation and scale details is extracted from the stable keypoint, determining the size of the associated window. Some of the fragments generated using SIFT algorithm is shown in
1
.

IV
CNN MODEL

Following patch extraction, there is a requirement to map it to a feature for identification or classification purposes. Prior studies predominantly relied on texture and codebook based methods to represent a writer’s feature
[
11
,
20
,
22
]
. However, recent years have witnessed a significant shift in the approach towards writer identification, with hand-engineered traditional methods being replaced by machine learning and deep learning-based algorithms
[
46
,
28
,
6
]
. These algorithms adopt a data-driven approach to directly learn features from the input data. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) falls in this category, it characterized by a series of convolution and sub-sampling layers within the network. Each convolution layer in a convolutional block comprises a set of filters, employed to extract features from the preceding layers through the convolution operation. The weights of these filters in a convolution block is updated during the training process using backpropagation. Typically, this network terminates with a series of fully connected layers, serving as a mapping function between the outputs of the convolutional layers. These densely connected layers play a crucial role in exploring non-linearities associated with high-level features extracted from the convolution block, thus facilitating the classification of input data into predefined classes.

. This model consists of two parallel CNN blocks trained on writer-dependent and writer-independent data to capture the writer-specific and writer-independent features associated with the input samples. The subsequent subsections delve into the specifics of each block’s composition and function.

IV-A

Writer dependent module

Every writer possesses a unique handwriting style, characterized by distinct segments or patterns that may exhibit certain similarities among them. The process of identifying these shared traits within a handwritten sample serves a crucial role in authenticating the authorship of the document. The objective of the writer-dependent Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) module is to extract pertinent details from the input fragments at both the character and sub-character levels.

The architecture of the writer-dependent module comprises a series of convolutional layers, each followed by BatchNormalization and a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) layer sequentially. These convolution layers are equipped with a range of filters spanning from 64 to 512, featuring a kernel size of (
n\times n
), where
n
takes values from the set
(1,3,7)
. Notably, certain convolution blocks incorporate skip connections, thereby transforming them into residual blocks, as depicted in Figure
3
. The utilization of skip connections enhances the network’s ability to capture intricate features while mitigating the vanishing gradient problem.

A significant portion of the convolutional operations within the writer-dependent block is executed via separable convolution. Unlike standard convolution, which applies a kernel across the entire input volume simultaneously, separable convolution operates in two stages. Initially, a convolution operation is conducted along each channel of the input independently. Subsequently, a (
1\times 1
) point-wise convolution is performed across the channels
[
47
]
. This strategy effectively reduces the total number of trainable parameters compared to traditional convolution, thereby enhancing computational efficiency without much compromise in performance.

Fig. 3:
The architecture of the residual block used in the convolution network. Each residual block in the network is followed by entries N1 and N2 specifying the number of filters in the residual block.

IV-B

Writer Independent module

The main objective of this module is to extract general features from the input image that is writer as well as data independent. Most of the convolution networks are trained using a classification framework which emphasizes on learning patterns from the training data. One of the limitations of this framework is that it requires a large amount of training data an absence of which may hinder the deep learning models’ ability to accurately model various classes, leading to poor performance. Consequently, networks structured within a classification framework are predominantly designed to identify local features and may not effectively capture broader, generalized features.

To achieve the objective of generalization, we employ a dissimilarity-based approach. In contrast to traditional classifiers that prioritize pattern identification, this approach focuses on recognizing distinctions or dissimilarities between data points. This dissimilarity measure is typically framed as a distance metric within an acquired embedding space, wherein inputs sharing commonalities or belonging to the same class are brought closer together, while those from disparate classes are pushed farther apart. The objective of dissimilarity learning is implemented using a class of neural networks called the Siamese network. Such a network uses the concept of few-shot learning
[
48
,
49
]
to extract visual attributes from a given sample by quantifying its similarity to the enrolled samples. Thus effectively tackling the challenge of training models to recognize and generalize from a limited number of labeled examples per class. Similar to the writer-dependent module this module also follows the same sequence of layers (i.e convolution layer being followed by BatchNormalization and ReLU).

In context of the present work, the weights of the Siamese network is trained on the Omniglot dataset
[
50
]
details of which is elaborated in section
V
. The decision to utilize the language-independent Omniglot dataset
[
50
]
is derived from its diverse collection of characters sourced from various languages, which enhances the generalization of writer features.

IV-C

Classification block

The features outputted by both the writer-dependent and writer-independent blocks are combined and then sent to a classification block, which includes a Global Average Pooling (GAP) layer, a dropout layer, and a fully connected layer. Before reaching the classification module, the combined output undergoes a convolution operation, which serves as a bottleneck layer to obtain a lower-dimensional representation of the input feature. Such a configuration is intended to decrease the quantity of trainable parameters, thereby mitigating the risk of overfitting the network.

V
Attention module

Given the constrained receptive field of convolutional operators, addressing long-range dependencies requires traversing multiple convolutional layers. However, learning these dependencies presents challenges on several fronts: smaller models may lack the capacity to adequately capture them, optimization algorithms often struggle to optimize parameters across multiple layers, and parameter configurations intended to capture these dependencies. While enlarging the convolution kernels can enhance the network’s representational prowess, it comes at the cost of sacrificing the computational efficiencies.

In response to the challenges posed by capturing intricate dependencies within data, attention mechanisms have emerged as transformative tools in deep neural networks. These mechanisms empower models to attentively prioritize elements within input data, assigning degrees of significance to different areas. Such adaptability markedly enhances the model’s proficiency in recognizing intricate patterns and connections, facilitating more efficient feature acquisition. Our proposed model integrates the self-attention module introduced by
et.al.

[
51
]
to capture long-range dependencies within writer fragments.

In contrast to conventional attention mechanisms that rely on external context to assess various segments of an input sequence, self-attention focuses on evaluating the internal relationships within the sequence directly. This is accomplished through a series of mathematical operations applied to the convolution output, allowing the model to determine attention scores for each element in relation to every other element within the sequence itself. Subsequently, these attention scores are leveraged to compute weighted sums of the input sequence, effectively capturing the contextual dependencies between its elements.

The features from the previous convolution layer
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}
are first transformed into feature spaces
\mathbf{f(x)=W_{f}x}
and
\mathbf{g(x)=W_{g}x}

\beta_{j,i}=\frac{\exp(s_{ij})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp(s_{ij})}

(1)

Where,
S_{ij}=\mathbf{f(x_{i})^{T}g(x_{i})}
and
\beta_{j,i}
is the attention weights for each pair of input
i
and output at position
j
. Here
C
and
N
represent the number of channels and number of feature locations of features from the previous layers respectively. These attention weights are subsequently incorporated to obtain the final output
\mathbf{o=(o_{1},o_{2},...,o_{j},...,o_{N})}\in^{N\times C}
based on the below formulation:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{o_{j}}&=\mathbf{v}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{j,i}\mathbf{h(x_{i})}\bigg)\\
\mathbf{h(x_{i})}&=\mathbf{W_{h}x_{i}}\\
\mathbf{v(x_{i})}&=\mathbf{W_{v}x_{i}}\end{split}

(2)

Furthermore, the module conducts an additional operation of scaling the output of the attention layer and then adding it back to the input feature map. Representing the final output as:

\mathbf{y_{i}}=\gamma\mathbf{o_{i}}+\mathbf{x_{i}}

(3)

VI
CNN model training and testing process

The segment extracted from the word image serves as input to the unified model, incorporating both the writer-dependent and writer-independent modules. The writer-dependent module concentrates on capturing intricate details associated with the writer at the character and sub-character levels, refining its weights iteratively solely from the writer fragments.

In contrast, as the primary objective of the writer-independent block is to capture general features of the writer. It is trained using transfer learning, where the weights of this module are frozen up to the second residual block, using the pre-trained weights acquired from the Omniglot dataset (as elaborated in Section
IV-B
), while the weights of the third residual block onwards are fine-tuned based on input fragments.

The weights of the resulting network is updated using the label smoothing cross-entropy loss function
[
52
]
. This technique converts the hard label (
y_{i}
) into soft labels (
\hat{y}_{i}
) by employing a weighted average between the uniform distribution and the hard label.

\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}(1-\epsilon)+\frac{\epsilon}{K}\begin{cases}1-\epsilon,&{i=target}\\
\frac{\epsilon}{K},&{i\neq target}\end{cases}

(4)

Here,
K
and
\epsilon
denote the number of classes and the label smoothing factor respectively. With this approach, the total loss is obtained by computing the cross-entropy loss between the predicted label and the adjusted ground truth label, formulated as follows:

L_{i}=-\sum_{j}^{M}\hat{y}_{ij}.\mbox{log}(p_{ij})

(5)

Where
M
is the number of writers in the training set, while
\hat{y}_{ij}
and
p_{ij}
denote the modified ground truth and the predicted label respectively, for the
i^{th}
fragment corresponding to the
j^{th}
writer. The overall training loss is then computed as
L=\frac{\sum_{i}^{B}L_{i}}{B}
, where
B
represents the number of word fragments.

n_{T}

P_{i}(w)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n_{T}}p_{ki}}{n_{T}}

(6)

Here,
p_{ki}
denotes the individual classification score of the
k^{th}
fragment corresponding to the
i^{th}
writer. The final predictor is made based on the classifier label
(i)

VII-B

Pre-Processing

As an initial preprocessing step, all the word images are first converted into grayscale. Owing to the fact that segmented word images are used as input in our proposed system the common noise source associated with the page image such as inkblots, extraneous masks etc. is mostly taken care of during the word segmentation process. However, some lighting and background issue is observed in the segmented Images of IAM and CERUG-EN database. To cater to this issue, a two-stage operation is performed. An input image is first filtered using

Our Convolution Neural Network is implemented on TensorFlow framework. The batch size is set to 16 during training. The network is optimized using Adam optimizer

As part of this investigation, the impact of adding various modules is examined, assessing its effects on the overall performance of the proposed system.

VII-E

Performance comparison of feature representation with prior works

In this experiment, we evaluate the effectiveness of feature representation learned by the proposed convolution module for writer identification and compare it with other handcrafted features as well as deep-learning-based methods. In this setup, the fragment extracted from a word image using the SIFT algorithm corresponding to each writer’s training samples is passed through the proposed network. The penultimate layer output of this network serves as the feature representation. These feature representations, derived from each fragment linked to a writer’s word image in the training datasets, is then employed to train a one-class SVM classifier tailored to the respective writer being analyzed.

Each SVM classifier assigns a value to the input fragment depending on its proximity to the separating hyperplane. The obtained values are then bounded between
[0-1]
by passing them through a sigmoid function. Following this, the final classification score is generated by accumulating the individual score generated for each fragment across trained SVM classifiers.

P_{i}(word)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{T}}p_{j}^{i}

(8)

Here,
p_{j}^{i}
represents the classification score of
j^{th}
fragment generated by an SVM classifier trained on samples of the
i^{th}
writer.
P_{i}(word)
represents the overall classification score obtained for an input word image containing
n_{T}
number of fragments.

The final predictor is made based on the classifier label
(i)
for which the highest classification score is obtained.

\phi(w)=\underset{i\in\{1,...,W\}}{argmax}P_{i}(word)

(9)

From the table it can be inferred that the performance of handcrafted features based on word images is very low compared to deep learning base methods. This is owing to the fact that handcrafted features extract mostly rely on extracting statistical information from the handwritten image, which necessitates the use of large amount of text sample for obtaining a stable representation. On the other hand presence of multiple filter in deep learning based method helps it capturing diverse information from the word image, thus increasing the overall performance of the system. Moreover, when compared with other deep learning-based methods (i.e WordIamgeNet, FragNet-64, GR-RNN and its variants) our proposed algorithm and its variants (indicated in bold) achieves better accuracy across the three databases. This superiority in performance can be credited to the extraction of image fragments at various abstraction levels, allowing it to capture a wide range of information and thereby improving its overall robustness.

VII-F

Performance evaluation of Writer Identification system within and cross-Datasets

In this section, we study the effectiveness of the feature representation learned by the proposed network across and within a dataset. This analysis aids in understanding the impact of integrating two modules: the Writer-Independent module and the Attention block, on the learned feature representation in contrast to the baseline method. Consequently, this assists us in evaluating the robustness of the learned feature representation.

Given a trained Convolution network, we extract the feature from its penultimate layer and evaluate the model performance. In the within-dataset evaluation, features are extracted by the network trained with samples from the same dataset. Conversely, in cross-dataset evaluation, features are extracted by the network trained on samples from different datasets. Following this, a one-class classifier is constructed forming the basis for the final decision-making process as elaborated in section
VII-E
.

The result of this analysis is shown in Figure
8
. Based on the figure it can be observed that the optimal performance is achieved when the dataset used for feature extraction aligns with the dataset employed for network training. In such instances, the network effectively captures writer-specific features, and the accuracy consistently follows the general trend of the model incorporating an attention mechanism, showcasing the highest performance compared to the other two models. However, in the cross-dataset scenario, we see a departure from this trend as the combined model surpasses the attention-based model. This discrepancy arises due to the attention module’s property of selectively focusing on specific regions or features within the input data, potentially resulting in a loss of generality. This observation can be substantiated by visualizing the heat map projection of the convolution layer just before the fully connected layer corresponding to the three configurations as shown in Figure
9
. The figure clearly shows that, in contrast to the baseline, the other two configurations capture additional data features, as highlighted by the red box corresponding to the region indicated by the yellow box in the input image. However, between the two neural network configurations, the model incorporating the attention module captures more refined, data-dependent features, potentially resulting in a loss of generality across datasets and impacting its overall performance.

VII-G

Performance of proposed system with word images of different lengths

To examine how the proposed model adapts to variations in word length, we conducted an experiment assessing its performance on word images spanning from two to ten characters. Figure

shows the Top1 performance of writer identification for different word lengths on the IAM and CVL datasets. Moreover, for the two databases under consideration, the attention-based model provides better results than the other two configurations.

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a multi-channel convolution-based End-to-end network for offline text-independent writer identification systems centred on word images. The proposed network combines writer-specific local features with writer-independent global features to generate a robust representation of writer features. Additionally, we also explore the impact of incorporating an attention mechanism on the overall system performance. Based on the experimental result it is observed that the network trained on individual fragments performs better compared to those trained on word images or a combination of word fragments in limited sample scenarios.
