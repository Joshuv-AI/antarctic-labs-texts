Weak error analysis for a nonlinear SPDE approximation of the Dean-Kawasaki equation
Helena Kremp, Ana Djurdjevac, Helena Kremp, Nicolas Perkowski

Abstract
We consider a nonlinear SPDE approximation of the Dean-Kawasaki equation for independent particles. Our approximation satisfies the physical constraints of the particle system, i.e. its solution is a probability measure for all times (preservation of positivity and mass conservation). Using a duality argument, we prove that the weak error between particle system and nonlinear SPDE is of the order N − 1 − 1 / ( d / 2 + 1 ) ​ log ⁡ N N^{-1-1/(d/2+1)}\log N . Along the way we show well-posedness, a comparison principle, and an entropy estimate for a class of nonlinear regularized Dean-Kawasaki equations with Itô noise. Keywords: Dean-Kawasaki equation, weak error analysis, Laplace duality MSC2020: 60H17, 60H35

Introduction

The Dean-Kawasaki (DK) equation, named after
[
Dea96
]
and
[
Kaw94
]
, is a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) for the evolution of the empirical measure of particles following Langevin dynamics with pairwise interaction. It is formally given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u+\nabla\cdot(u(\nabla W\ast u))+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{u}\xi),

(1.1)

where
\xi
is a vector-valued space-time white noise and
\alpha>0
. In
[
Dea96
]
, (
1.1
) is formally derived as a closed equation for the dynamics of the empirical measure of mean-field interacting diffusions: Let
(X^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
solve the Langevin dynamics with interaction potential
W
, that is

\displaystyle dX^{i}_{t}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla W(X^{i}_{t}-X^{j}_{t})dt+dB^{i}_{t}

(1.2)

for
N
independent Brownian motions
(B^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
. Dean
[
Dea96
]
argues that the empirical measure
\mu^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i}_{t}}
solves (
1.1
) with
\alpha=N
. This is based on an ad-hoc replacement of the (stochastic integral against the) Brownian noise by a (stochastic integral against a) space-time white noise which formally has the same law.

Due to the singular gradient noise and because of the square root nonlinearity, the mathematical meaning of (
1.1
) is dubious, although there has been a lot of progress in recent years, see the literature review below. Nonetheless, the Dean-Kawasaki equation is very useful for applications and SPDE models of DK-type are very popular in physics (e.g.
[
MT00
,
VCCK08
,
GNS+12
,
DOL+16
,
DCG+18
]
), where they are also known as (stochastic) dynamical density functional theory, see the recent survey
[
tVLW20
]
.

The motivation for this work is the successful use of the Dean-Kawasaki equation as a computational tool for simulations of fluid dynamics
[
DFVE14
]
or social dynamics
[
DCKD22
,
HCD+21
]
. If
N
is large and if the particles live in a low-dimensional space, a discretization of (
1.1
) can be computationally much cheaper than simulating
N
particles with their interactions. This is particularly interesting for moderate sizes of
N
, where the hydrodynamic limit (
N\to\infty
) would miss important stochastic fluctuations; for example, in social dynamics it is interesting to consider
N=O(10^{4}-10^{5})
. However, because of the singular nature of the DK equation and because of its fragile solution theory (see the literature section below), it is far from obvious why a discretization of (
1.1
) should give meaningful results for (
1.2
).

To understand this on a conceptual level, we focus on the case without interaction (
W=0
) and we introduce a regularized version of the DK-equation, where we truncate the noise, we mollify the square root nonlinearity and we consider on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
the equation

\displaystyle d\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\nabla(f_{\delta_{N}}(\tilde{\mu}_{t}^{N})e_{k})\cdot dB_{t}^{k}

(1.3)

for the Fourier-basis
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, for a Lipschitz function
f_{\delta_{N}}
which approximates the square root, and for
d
-dimensional complex Brownian motions
(B^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. Under appropriate conditions we prove the well-posedness of this approximate DK-equation. The equation is not locally monotone in the sense of
[
LR15
]
and the nonlinear Itô gradient noise is quite tricky (much more so than Stratonovich noise), therefore we prove well-posedness through a suitable transformation of the equation in combination with a priori energy bounds. Furthermore, we prove a comparison principle which yields non-negativity of the solution for non-negative initial data and the conservation of the
L^{1}
-norm. This is important because
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
should represent the empirical distribution
\mu^{N}
of particles, which is a probability measure by the mass conservation of the particle system. Our main result concerns the quality of approximation of
\mu^{N}
by
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
. We do not expect that
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
and
\mu^{N}
are pathwise close and we do not even couple the noises in their dynamics to each other. Instead, we focus on the weak approximation quality, and we show that for suitable test functions
F

\displaystyle\lvert\mathds{E}[F(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})]-\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]\rvert\lesssim N^{-1-\frac{1}{d/2+1}}(t+\log(N)).

(1.4)

Since the equation for
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
should be amenable to discretization, this gives a partial justification for the use of DK-type equations in numerical simulations. Although our methods crucially rely on the absence of interactions because then we can compute
\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]
explicitly by duality arguments (cf.
[
KLvR19
]
), we expect similar results to hold in the interacting case, and we expect that our general approach is quite powerful and can possibly be generalized to the interaction case.

The paper is structured as follows. Below we review some mathematical literature on the Dean-Kawasaki equation and we discuss why its solution theory is subtle and some recent breakthroughs.
Section

2
introduces the notation and assumptions for our approximate Dean-Kawasaki equation, and we state the main results. In
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
we prove the well-posedness and a comparison principle for regularized Dean-Kawasaki-type equations, which in particular applies to our approximation (
1.3
).
Section

4
provides the weak error estimates.

Some mathematical literature on Dean-Kawasaki type equations

Giving a pathwise meaning to the DK equation (
1.1
) is an open problem even in the case without interaction potential,
W=0
. The equation is highly singular because the noise is effectively a derivative of the space-time white noise and thus extremely irregular, and therefore the solution can only be a distribution and not a function and thus the square root
\sqrt{\mu^{N}_{t}}
and the product
\sqrt{\mu^{N}_{t}}\xi
have no meaning. Even in dimension
d=1
, the equation is scaling supercritical in the language of regularity structures (
[
Hai14
]
) and paracontrolled distributions (
[
GIP15
]
), which are techniques to tackle subcritical singular SPDEs.

Entropy solutions of approximate DK-type equations with Stratonovich noise
\sum_{k}\nabla\cdot\sigma^{k}(x,u)\circ dB^{k}_{t}
were studied in
[
DG20
]
. Using a pathwise approach via rough paths techniques,
[
FG19
]
established well-posedness for porous media equations (replacing
\Delta u
by
\Delta(\lvert u\rvert^{m-1}u)
with exponent
m\in(0,\infty)
), again for Stratonovich noise. Both of these results require very regular diffusion coefficients
\sigma
.
The well-posedness of the Dean-Kawasaki equation with truncated Stratonovich space-time white noise and with square-root diffusion coefficient was recently shown in
[
FG21
]
, where the authors establish existence and uniqueness of stochastic kinetic solutions. The Stratonovich noise enables them to obtain a priori entropy-type estimates on the kinetic solutions (cf.
[
FG21
, Section 5.1]
), which yield the relative compactness of solutions with regularized square root nonlinearity. We refer to
[
FG21
]
and the references therein for more details on the well-posedness of related equations.

Furthermore, the Dean-Kawasaki equation is related to scaling limits of interacting particle systems, see e.g.
[
GLP98
]
.
[
FG22
]
show that the Stratonovich DK equation correctly predicts the large deviation rate function for non-equilibrium fluctuations of the zero range process.

Dean-Kasawaki type equations with regularized square root and truncated Itô noise have been considered also in
[
Bec21
]
, where the existence of weak solutions is shown.

Regularized Dean-Kawasaki equations for underdamped kinetic particles were investigated in
[
CSZ19
,
CSZ20
]
. The authors impose a cutoff on the noise, which is formally justified by an approximation of the Dirac deltas in
\mu^{N}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i}_{t}}
with mollifying kernels. They establish the existence of mild solutions with high probability.

The mathematically correct interpretation of the “full” (not truncated) Dean-Kawasaki equation was found only recently
[
KLvR19
,
KLvR20
]
. The authors show that (
1.1
) should be interpreted as a martingale problem, and that by similar duality arguments as for superprocesses
[
Eth00
]
uniqueness holds for this martingale problem. In fact, the authors prove much more. Namely, at least for bounded interaction potentials, the Dean-Kawasaki equation has a unique martingale solution if and only if the parameter
N
is a natural number and the initial condition is an atomic probability measure (given by the sum of
N
Dirac measures). If we replace
\mu_{0}^{N}
by an initial condition
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
that is not of the form
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{y^{i}}
, or if we replace
N
by a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}\setminus\mathbb{N}
, then there exists no martingale solution. In particular, the well-posedness is very fragile with respect to changes of the parameters, and as such, the equation is not suitable for a stable numerical approximation.

From a numerical perspective, this instability in the parameter
N
is worrisome. That is, slight changes in
N
yield an ill-posed problem and possibly to large numerical errors. Nonetheless, numerical schemes for Dean-Kawasaki-type equations were considered in
[
CS22
,
CF21
]
.
[
CS22
]
introduce a discontinuous Galerkin scheme for the regularized DK equation from
[
CSZ19
,
CSZ20
]
. The work most related to ours is Cornalba-Fischer
[
CF21
]
, who consider finite element and finite difference approximations of (
1.1
) without interaction (
W=0
) and who also prove weak error estimates. Their weak distance is parametrized by the Sobolev regularity of the test functions and the rate measured in their distance can be arbitrarily high, only limited by the numerical error and the error coming from the negative part of the approximation. However, the authors do not prove positivity for the approximations (hence the consideration of the negative part). Additionally they impose a strong assumption (
[
CF21
, Assumption FD4]
) on the existence of lower and upper bounds for the solution of a discrete heat equation, which means that initially the particles must be fairly spread out. Our results are orthogonal in the sense that we consider an SPDE approximation and not a discrete model, and we do not have any restrictions on the initial distribution of particles. Our convergence rate is upper bounded by
N^{-5/3}
in
d=1
and it gets worse in higher dimensions, which is due to constraints on the choice of parameters coming from the solution theory of the SPDE. Our methods are very different (Laplace duality/Kolmogorov backward equation compared to a clever recursive scheme and choice of distance in
[
CF21
]
).

2
Preliminaries and statement of the main results

Consider the particle system of
N\in\mathbb{N}
independent standard
d-
dimensional Brownian motions
(X^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
projected onto the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(with generator being the Laplacian with periodic boundary conditions)
started at
X^{i}_{0}=x^{i}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. We are interested in the empirical measure

\mu^{N}_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i}_{t}}.

(2.1)

Applying Itô’s formula to
\langle\mu^{N}_{t},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(X_{t}^{i})
,
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we see that
\mu^{N}
formally solves the Dean-Kawasaki equation without interaction and with atomic initial condition,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu^{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\mu^{N}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\mu^{N}}\xi)

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{0}^{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{i}},

where
\xi:=(\xi^{j})_{j=1,\dots,d}
with independent space-time white noise processes
\xi^{j}
, see
[
Dea96
]
. As in
[
KLvR19
, Definition 2.1]
we will interpret (
2.2
) as a martingale problem. The state space of the solution process is the space
\mathcal{M}
of probability measures on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, equipped with the topology of weak convergence.
For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
and
\varphi\in C(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we write
\mu(\varphi)=\langle\mu,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi d\mu
.

Definition 2.1
.

We call a stochastic process
(\mu_{t}^{N})_{t\geqslant 0}
on a complete filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0},\mathds{P})
with values in
C(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{M})

a solution to (
2.2
) if for any test function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the process

\displaystyle t\mapsto\langle\mu_{t}^{N},\varphi\rangle-\langle\mu_{0}^{N},\varphi\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mu_{s}^{N},\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi\rangle ds

is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
–martingale with quadratic variation

\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{\cdot}\langle\mu_{s}^{N},\lvert\nabla\varphi\rvert^{2}\rangle ds.

(2.3)

By
[
KLvR19
, Theorem 2.2]
this martingale problem has a unique (in law) solution given by (
2.1
). And if in (
2.2
) we replace
\mu_{0}^{N}
by an initial condition
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
that is not of the form
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{y^{i}}
, or if we replace
N
by a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}\setminus\mathbb{N}
, then there exists no martingale solution to the equation. In particular, the well-posedness is very fragile with respect to changes of the parameters. Our goal is to approximate the equation (
2.2
) in a controlled manner with an equation that has good stability properties and that preserves the physical constraints of
\mu^{N}
, i.e. positivity and mass conservation.

We aim for a bound of the form

\displaystyle\lvert\mathds{E}[F(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})]-\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]\rvert\lesssim_{F,t}N^{-\alpha_{d}},

for
t>0
and for suitable nonlinear test functions
F:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
. As usual,
a\lesssim b
means that there exists a constant
C>0
(not depending on the relevant parameters; above the parameters are
\delta,x
), such that
a\leqslant Cb
. If we want to indicate the dependence of the constant
C(\kappa)
on a parameter

, we write
a\lesssim_{\kappa}b
. We will see that for
F(\mu):=\exp(\langle\mu,\varphi\rangle)
,
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
the expectation
\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]
can be computed in closed form and therefore we consider such

.
Due to the factor
1/N
in front on the quadratic variation, a direct computation shows that the hydrodynamic/mean-field limit, that is, the solution
\rho
of the heat equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\rho,\qquad\rho_{0}=\mu^{N}_{0},

achieves a rate of convergence
\alpha_{d}=1
. However,
\rho
is deterministic and it does not capture random fluctuations in the particle system
\mu^{N}
. For the Gaussian approximation
\hat{\rho}
of the fluctuations of the particle system around the hydrodynamic limit, that is,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\hat{\rho}+\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\rho}\xi),\qquad\hat{\rho}_{0}=0,

with the same vector-valued space-time white noise

as above, we can prove that
\rho+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\hat{\rho}
achieves a weak error of
\alpha_{d}=3/2
. But
\rho+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\hat{\rho}
is a distribution of regularity

, it is not a measure, let alone positive, and it does not conserve the mass of the initial condition (in fact
\lVert\rho_{t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\hat{\rho}_{t}\rVert_{L^{1}}=\infty
for all

).

Therefore, we consider a nonlinear approximation of (
2.2

. The parameters

will influence the order of the approximation and they will be chosen subsequently in the error estimate, depending on

. Let for now
\delta=\delta_{N}>0
and

. We then define the Lipschitz function

as follows

f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}x&\lvert x\rvert\leqslant\delta/2,\\
\text{smooth}&\delta/2\leqslant\lvert x\rvert\leqslant\delta,\\
\operatorname{sign}(x)\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}&\lvert x\rvert\geqslant\delta.\\
\end{cases}

(2.4)

The smooth interpolation should be such that
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}},\quad\lvert f^{\prime}(x)\rvert\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}},\text{ for all }x>0

(2.5)

and

\displaystyle\lvert f(x)\rvert\lesssim\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert},\quad\lvert f(x)^{2}-x\rvert\lesssim\delta,\text{ for all }x\geqslant 0.

(2.6)

Any

approximation of the square root satisfying those bounds works for our analysis and a particular example of such a function is given in the following example.

Example 2.2
.

Consider, for example,

f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}x&\lvert x\rvert\leqslant\delta/2,\\
-\frac{2\sqrt{\delta}}{\delta^{3}}x^{3}+\operatorname{sign}(x)\frac{4}{\delta\sqrt{\delta}}x^{2}-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{\delta}}x+\operatorname{sign}(x)\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{2}&\delta/2\leqslant\lvert x\rvert\leqslant\delta,\\

(2.7)

It is not hard to see that

and that

) and (

We denote by

the solution of the approximated equation

\displaystyle d\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\nabla\cdot(f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})dW^{N}_{t}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}

\displaystyle=\rho^{N}\ast\mu_{0}^{N}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

where

(2.9)

for

and independent

(that is,

3
Well-posedness and comparison for regularized DK-type SPDEs

3.1
Well-posedness

In this section, we prove strong well-posedness for SPDEs of the type

\displaystyle du_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u_{t}dt+\nabla\cdot(b(u_{t})dW_{t}),

(3.1)

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}.

Similar stochastic conservation laws with Stratonovich noise have been intensely studied in the past years, see for example
[
DG20
,
FG19
,
FG21
]
, among many others. However, due to the gradient noise the solution theory for Itô noise is more subtle and we need to make an additional smallness assumption to even get the existence of solutions; see the discussion before
3.4
below. Also, the coefficients of (
3.1
) are not locally monotone in the sense of
[
LR15
, Theorem 5.1.3]
, and therefore the solution theory does not follow from standard theory.

There is the related work
[
Bec21
]
by Bechtold on equations resembling (
3.1
), and we are in the case of “critical unboundedness” from
[
Bec21
, Section 4]
. However,
[
Bec21
]
only proves the existence of a probabilistically weak solution with paths in
C([0,T],H^{-\varepsilon})\cap L^{2}([0,T],H^{1})
for any
\varepsilon>0
. By a pathwise uniqueness argument using the a priori energy bound that we derive below, it should be possible to show the strong existence and uniqueness of a solution with paths in
C([0,T],H^{-\varepsilon})\cap L^{2}([0,T],H^{1})
for any
\varepsilon>0
.
Instead, we directly show the stronger statement of strong existence and uniqueness of a solution with paths in
C([0,T],L^{2})\cap L^{2}([0,T],H^{1})
.

We make the following assumptions.

Assumption 3.1

(Assumption on the noise)
.

Let
(\phi_{k})_{k}\subset C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathbb{C})
with
\overline{\phi_{k}}=\phi_{-k}

and consider independent
d
-dimensional complex-valued Brownian motions
(B^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
with
\overline{B^{k}}=B^{-k}
. We assume that the noise
(W_{t})_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle W_{t}(x):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\phi_{k}(x)B^{k}_{t}

such that

\displaystyle C^{W}_{1}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\lVert\phi_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}<\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad C^{W}_{2}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\lVert\nabla\phi_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}<\infty,

(3.2)

with
\lVert\phi_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\lvert\phi_{k}(x)\rvert^{2}
and
\lVert\nabla\phi_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lvert\partial_{i}\phi_{k}(x)\rvert^{2}
. For some results we only require
C^{W}_{1}<\infty
, and we call this the
relaxed
3.1
.

Remark 3.2
.

An example for a noise expansion satisfying the assumption is the Fourier expansion with cut-off
M_{N}\in\mathbb{N}
from (
2.9
). The summability assumptions are trivially satisfied due to the finite cut-off.
Similar to
[
FG21
, Remark 2.3]
, we can also consider a noise

\displaystyle W_{t}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}a_{k}\exp(2\pi ik\cdot x)B^{k}_{t},

for a real sequence
(a_{k})
with
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\lvert k\rvert^{2}a_{k}^{2}<\infty
, which also satisfies our assumption.

Assumption 3.3

(Assumptions on the diffusion coefficient)
.

We assume that the diffusion coefficient
b\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
has a bounded derivative with bound
L>0
,

\displaystyle\lVert b^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant L.

(3.3)

In particular,
b
is of linear growth: There exist
C^{b}_{1},C^{b}_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\lvert b(x)\rvert^{2}\leqslant C^{b}_{1}\lvert x\rvert^{2}+C^{b}_{2},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.4)

From the assumption
3.3
, it follows that
b
is Lipschitz continuous with bound
L
, that is
|b(x)-b(y)|\leq L|x-y|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

If we would consider Stratonovich noise, these two assumptions would be sufficient. For Itô noise we need an additional smallness condition for the gradient noise, because otherwise it could break the parabolic nature of the equation; see
[
Par21
, Section 2.4.2]
.

Assumption 3.4

(Stochastic parabolicity)
.

We assume that the parameters from the previous assumptions satisfy
C_{1}^{W}\max(L^{2},C_{1}^{b})<1
.

The well-posedness theory of this section will apply to the approximating Dean-Kawasaki equation (
2.8
), where
b=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}f
with
f
from (
2.4
) and
L=C^{b}_{1}=1/\sqrt{N\delta}
. Furthermore, the noise expansion is given with respect to the Fourier basis
(\phi_{k})_{k}
with cut-off
M_{N}\in\mathbb{N}
. In that case, we have that
C_{1}^{W}\leqslant(2M_{N})^{d}
and
C_{2}^{W}\leqslant(2M_{N})^{d}(2\pi M_{N})^{2}
. Hence, (
3.2
) is fulfilled.

Note that due to the gradient noise term the local monotonicity condition is violated for SPDEs of the form (
3.1
) and the variational approach of
[
LR15
]
cannot be applied directly. Instead, we transform the equation (by applying
(\Delta-1)^{1/2}
) into an equation for which the variational theory can be applied and we deduce well-posedness of the original equation by using a priori energy bounds.

The setting for the variational theory is defined as follows. Let
V=H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with
V^{\ast}=H^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
H=L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, such that we have the Gelfand triple
V\subset H\subset V^{\ast}
. We consider the Laplacian with periodic boundary conditions, that is,
\Delta:V=H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to(H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))^{\ast}=V^{\ast}
with
\Delta u(v):=\langle\Delta u,v\rangle_{V^{\ast},V}
,
u\in V^{\ast},v\in V
. For the duality pairing, we have that
\langle\Delta u,v\rangle_{V^{\ast},V}=-\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle_{H}=-\int\nabla u\cdot\nabla v
when
u,v\in V
. Then, we define a solution to the equation (
3.1
) as follows.

Definition 3.5
.

A stochastic process
(u_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
with paths in
L^{2}([0,T],H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap C([0,T],L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
is a (probabilistically strong and analytically weak) solution to the equation (
3.1
) for the initial condition
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, if for all

\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\langle u_{t},\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u_{0},\varphi\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla u_{s},\nabla\varphi\rangle ds+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla(b(u_{t})\phi_{k}),\varphi\rangle\cdot dB^{k}_{s}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\langle u_{0},\varphi\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\langle\partial_{i}u_{s},\partial_{i}\varphi\rangle ds+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\partial_{i}(b(u_{t})\phi_{k}),\varphi\rangle dB^{k,i}_{s}.

First, we study well-posedness of a transformed equation with the variational approach:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let Assumptions
3.3
and
3.4
hold, as well as the relaxed Assumption
3.1
, and consider an initial condition
v_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Then, there exists a unique probabilistically strong solution
v\in L^{2}([0,T],H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap C([0,T],L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
to

\displaystyle d\langle v_{t},\varphi\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla v_{t},\nabla\varphi\rangle dt+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\langle G_{k}(v_{t}),\varphi\rangle\cdot dB^{k}_{t}

(3.6)

for all
\varphi\in H^{1}
, where
G_{k}(v):=(1-\Delta)^{-1/2}\nabla(b((1-\Delta)^{1/2}v)\phi_{k})
.

Proof.

We will check the conditions of
[
LR15
, Section 4]
to apply the variational theory.
Let us define
G(v)w:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1-\Delta)^{-1/2}\nabla(b((1-\Delta)^{1/2}v)\phi_{k})w_{k}
for
w\in l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
and
v\in H^{1}
.
Using (
3.4
), we obtain the following coercivity bound (that is, (H3) from
[
LR15
, Section 4]
):

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta v,v\right\rangle_{V^{\ast},V}+\|G(v)\|^{2}_{L_{2}(l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}),H)}

\displaystyle=-\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sum_{k}\|(1-\Delta)^{-1/2}\nabla(b((1-\Delta)^{1/2}v)\phi_{k})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sum_{k}\|b((1-\Delta)^{1/2}v)\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sum_{k}\|\phi_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}\lVert b((1-\Delta)^{1/2}v)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+C_{1}^{W}(C_{1}^{b}\lVert(1-\Delta)^{1/2}v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C^{b}_{2})

\displaystyle=(C_{1}^{W}C^{b}_{1}-1)\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+C_{1}^{W}C_{1}^{b}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C_{1}^{W}C^{b}_{2},

where we used the Assumption
3.2
. Here, the first inequality follows from the Plancherel theorem for the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
on the torus (
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(k):=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e^{-2\pi ik\cdot x}f(x)dx
), such that for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\lVert(1-\Delta)^{-1/2}\nabla f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+\lvert 2\pi k\rvert^{2})^{-1}\lvert 2\pi k\rvert^{2}\lvert\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(f)(k)\rvert^{2}\!\leqslant\!\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\lvert\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(f)(k)\rvert^{2}=\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2},

and similarly we get
\lVert(1-\Delta)^{1/2}v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
. The coercivity then follows by the Assumption
3.4
on the parameters, since
C_{1}^{W}C_{1}^{b}<1
.
The weak monotonicity condition (that is, condition (H2) from
[
LR15
, Section 4]
) follows from the analogue estimate, using the global Lipschitz bound
L
due to (
3.3
):

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta(v^{1}-v^{2}),v^{1}-v^{2}\right\rangle_{V^{\ast},V}+\|G(v^{1})-G(v^{2})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}),H)}

\displaystyle=-\|\nabla(v^{1}-v^{2})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sum_{k}\|(1-\Delta)^{-1/2}\nabla((b((1-\Delta)^{1/2}v^{1}_{t})-b((1-\Delta)^{1/2}v^{2}_{t}))\phi_{k})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\|\nabla(v^{1}-v^{2})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sum_{k}\|(b((1-\Delta)^{1/2}v^{1}_{t})-b((1-\Delta)^{1/2}v^{2}_{t}))\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\|\nabla(v^{1}-v^{2})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sum_{k}\|\phi_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}L^{2}\|(1-\Delta)^{1/2}(v^{1}_{t}-v^{2}_{t})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\|\nabla(v^{1}-v^{2})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C_{1}^{W}L^{2}(\|v^{1}_{t}-v^{2}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla(v^{1}_{t}-v^{2}_{t})\|_{L^{2}}^{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}^{W}L^{2}\|v^{1}_{t}-v^{2}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

using
C_{1}^{W}L^{2}<1
in the last step. Thus, we obtain the weak monotonicity.
The hemicontinuity from
[
LR15
, Section 4, H1]
follows by linearity of the Laplacian. The boundedness condition from
[
LR15
, Section 4, H4]
is trivially satisfied due to continuity, that is,
\lVert\Delta u\rVert_{V^{\ast}}\leqslant\lVert v\rVert_{V}
.
∎

Lemma 3.8

Let Assumptions
3.1
,
3.3
and
3.4

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\lVert u^{R}_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}]+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\lVert u^{R}_{s}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}]ds\leqslant 2C_{2}C^{2}t\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\exp(t\tilde{\lambda}),

(3.9)

where

\lambda:=1-C_{1}^{W}L^{2}>0
and
\tilde{\lambda}:=C_{2}^{W}C^{b}_{1}

Proof.

Let
u^{R}=(1-\Delta)^{1/2}\Pi_{R}v^{R}=\Pi_{R}(1-\Delta)^{1/2}v^{R}
for the projection
\Pi_{R}
defined as above. As

a.s. Furthermore, by the equation that

solves,

solves

\displaystyle\langle u_{t}^{R},\varphi\rangle=\langle u_{0}^{R},\varphi\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\langle\Delta u_{s}^{R},\varphi\rangle ds+\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\Pi_{R}\nabla(b(u_{t}^{R})\phi_{k}),\varphi\rangle\cdot dB^{k}_{s},

(3.10)

for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, and since

\displaystyle u^{R}_{t}(x)=u^{R}_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\Delta u^{R}_{s}(x)ds+\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{R}\nabla(b(u^{R}_{s})\phi_{k})(x)\cdot dB^{k}_{s}.

\displaystyle d\lVert u^{R}_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int d(u^{R}_{t}(x))^{2}dx

\displaystyle=2\int u^{R}_{t}(x)\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\Delta u^{R}_{t}(x)dt+\sum_{k}\Pi_{R}\nabla(b(u^{R}_{t}(x))\phi_{k}(x))\cdot dB^{k}_{t}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k}\int\lvert\Pi_{R}\nabla(b(u^{R}_{t}(x))\phi_{k}(x))\rvert^{2}dxdt.

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\lVert u^{R}_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert u^{R}_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla u^{R}_{s}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{R}_{s}(x)dx\bigg]ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k}\mathds{E}[\lVert b^{\prime}(u^{R}_{s})\phi_{k}\nabla u^{R}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}]ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k}\mathds{E}[\lVert b(u^{R}_{s})\nabla\phi_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}]ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert u^{R}_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\lVert\nabla u^{R}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}]ds+C_{1}^{W}L^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\lVert\nabla u^{R}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}]ds+C_{2}^{W}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\lVert b(u^{R}_{s})\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}]ds.

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\lVert u^{R}_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}]

and
3.4
hold. Furthermore, let
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
be two solutions of (
3.5
) with initial conditions
u^{+}_{0},u^{-}_{0}\in L^{2}
, respectively, such that
u^{+}_{0}(x)\geqslant u^{-}_{0}(x)
for Lebesgue-almost all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\big(u^{+}_{t}\geqslant u^{-}_{t}\,\operatorname{Leb}\text{-a.e.}\,\forall t\in[0,T]\big)=1,

where
\operatorname{Leb}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
DMP93
, Theorem 2.1]
.
The main idea is an application of Itô’s formula to a suitable
C^{2}
approximation of the map
x\mapsto\max(x,0)^{2}
, applied to the difference of the solutions. More precisely, let for
p>0
,

\varphi_{p}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
be defined by

\varphi_{p}(x):=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{y}[2pz\mathbf{1}_{[0,\frac{1}{p}]}(z)+2\,\mathbf{1}_{(\frac{1}{p},\infty)}(z)]dzdy.

Note that
\varphi_{p}
satisfies

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\varphi^{\prime}_{p}(x)\leqslant 2\max(x,0)\quad\text{ and }\quad 0\leqslant\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{p}(x)\leqslant 2\,\mathbf{1}_{x\geqslant 0}.

Next, we define

\displaystyle\Phi_{p}(h):=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi_{p}(h(x))dx.

Let
w_{t}:=u^{-}_{t}-u^{+}_{t}
for
t>0
. Since
\varphi_{p}(x)\uparrow\max(x,0)^{2}
for
p\to\infty
, by monotone convergence we conclude that
\Phi_{p}(w_{t})\uparrow\lVert\max(w_{t},0)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
for
p\to\infty
.
Moreover,
\Phi_{p}
is twice Fréchet differentiable and we obtain by the Itô formula from
[
Par80
, Theorem 1.2]
that

\displaystyle d\Phi_{p}(w_{t})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\langle\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{p}(w_{t}),\lvert\nabla w_{t}\rvert^{2}\rangle dt+\sum_{k}\big\langle\varphi^{\prime}_{p}(w_{t}),\nabla\big((b(u^{-}_{t})-b(u^{+}_{t}))\phi_{k}\big)\big\rangle\cdot dB_{t}^{k}

(3.16)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\int\big\lvert\nabla\big((b(u^{-}_{t})-b(u^{+}_{t}))\phi_{k}\big)\big\rvert^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{p}(w_{t})dxdt.

(3.17)

Notice that
\Phi_{p}(w_{0})=0
, since by assumption
w_{0}\leq 0
a.e., and that the martingale term in (
3.16
) is a indeed a martingale.
From Young’s weighted inequality for products we obtain
(x+y)^{2}=x^{2}+2xy+y^{2}\leqslant(1+\kappa)x^{2}+(1+1/(2\kappa))y^{2}
for any
\kappa>0
, and using Assumption
3.3
on
b
we can thus estimate the quadratic variation term (
3.17
) by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\left(\nabla\big((b(u^{-}_{t})-b(u^{+}_{t}))\phi_{k}\big)\right)^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{p}(w_{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\left[(1+\kappa)\left(\nabla(b(u^{-}_{t})-b(u^{+}_{t}))\phi_{k}\right)^{2}+(1+\frac{1}{2\kappa})\left((b(u^{-}_{t})-b(u^{+}_{t}))\nabla\phi_{k}\right)^{2}\right]\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{p}(w_{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\left[(1+\kappa)C^{W}_{1}L^{2}|\nabla w_{t}|^{2}+(1+\frac{1}{2\kappa})C^{W}_{2}L^{2}2\cdot\lvert w_{t}\rvert^{2}\right]\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{p}(w_{t}).

Note that
\lvert w_{t}\rvert^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{p}(w_{t})\leqslant 2\max{(w_{t},0)}^{2}
, and that
C_{1}^{W}L^{2}<1
by Assumption
3.4
which means that there exists
\kappa>0
with
(1+\kappa)C_{1}^{W}L^{2}<1
. For such
\kappa
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\phi_{p}(w_{t})]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\Big((1+\kappa)C_{1}^{W}L^{2}-1\Big)\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathds{E}\left[\langle\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{p}(w_{s}),\lvert\nabla w_{s}\rvert^{2}\rangle\right]ds

\displaystyle\quad+2(1+\frac{1}{2\kappa})C^{W}_{2}L^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathds{E}\left[\lVert\max(w_{s},0)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right]ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}\left[\lVert\max(w_{s},0)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right]ds.

Taking
p\to\infty
and applying Gronwall’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\lVert\max(w_{s},0)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right]=0.

Hence,
(\mathds{P}\otimes\operatorname{Leb})(w_{t}\leqslant 0)=1
for all
t\geqslant 0
. By continuity,
w\in C([0,T],L^{2})
, the claim follows.
∎

(Non-negativity of the solution)

3.1
,
3.3
and

. Let

.

Proof.

∎

Proposition 3.15

(Conservation of mass)
.

Under the assumptions of the previous corollary, let

4
Weak error estimate

In this section we estimate the weak error between the martingale solution
\mu^{N}
of the Dean-Kawasaki equation and the strong solution
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
of the approximate Dean-Kawasaki equation. For that purpose we first have to discuss the solution theory of the approximate Dean-Kawasaki equation:

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider the equation

\displaystyle d\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\nabla\cdot(f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})dW^{N}_{t}),

(4.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

where
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}},\quad\lvert f^{\prime}(x)\rvert\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}},\text{ for all }x>0

(4.2)

and

\displaystyle\lvert f(x)\rvert\lesssim\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert},\quad\lvert f(x)^{2}-x\rvert\lesssim\delta,\text{ for all }x\geqslant 0,

(4.3)

and where

W^{N}_{t}(x):=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}e_{k}(x)B_{t}^{k}:=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\exp(2\pi ik\cdot x)B_{t}^{k}

(4.4)

for
d
-dimensional complex-valued Brownian motions
(B^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. If
\frac{C_{N}(2M_{N}+1)^{d}}{N\delta_{N}}<1
holds (“coercivity condition”), where
C_{N}>0
is such that
\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\leqslant\frac{C_{N}}{\delta_{N}}
, then Equation (
4.1
) has a unique strong solution in the sense of Section
3.1
. If
\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}\geqslant 0
, then
\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}\geqslant 0
and
\lVert\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}\rVert_{L^{1}}=\lVert\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}\rVert_{L^{1}}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

This follows from the results in Section
3.1
once we verify that our equation satisfies the assumptions of that section. For that purpose, note that

C^{W}_{1}=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}=(2M_{N}+1)^{d},\qquad C^{W}_{2}=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}|2\pi k|^{2}\lVert e_{k}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}<\infty,

and, since
f(0)=0
, we also have
L^{2}=C^{b}_{1}\leqslant\frac{C_{N}}{N\delta_{N}}
by assumption, and
C^{b}_{2}=0
. Therefore, Assumptions
3.1
and
3.3
hold. Moreover, the condition
\frac{C_{N}(2M_{N}+1)^{d}}{N\delta_{N}}<1
is nothing else than
C^{1}_{W}\max\{L^{2},C^{b}_{1}\}<1
, i.e. also Assumption
3.4
holds.
∎

This well-posedness result together with the energy estimate of Section
3.1
would be sufficient to derive a weak error estimate for the approximation of
\mu^{N}
with
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
. However, through the energy estimate the weak error would depend on
\lVert\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
. Therefore, inspired by
[
FG21
]
, we derive an entropy estimate below.

The main advantage is that this estimate only depends on
\int\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\log(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N})
, which typically is much smaller than
\|\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
and this leads to better error estimates. To be precise, we will show that
\int\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\log(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N})\lesssim\log(N)
, while typically
\lVert\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim N^{d}
, cf.
Lemma

4.3
below.

Proposition 4.2

(Entropy estimate)
.

Let
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
be a solution of the approximate Dean-Kawasaki equation (
3.5
) with the initial condition
\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}:=\rho^{N}\ast\mu_{0}^{N}
for
\mu_{0}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{i}}
and let
(\rho^{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
be a mollifying sequence such that
\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\geqslant 0

and
\lVert\rho^{N}\ast\mu_{0}^{N}\rVert_{L^{1}}=1
. Furthermore, assume the coercivity condition
\frac{C_{N}(2M_{N}+1)^{d}}{N\delta_{N}}<1
, where
C_{N}>0
is such that
\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\leqslant\frac{C_{N}}{\delta_{N}}
. Then the following entropy estimate holds

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathds{E}\bigg[\int\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}\log(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})\bigg]+\lambda\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{E}\bigg[\int\frac{\lvert\nabla\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}\rvert^{2}}{\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}}\bigg]dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}\log(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0})+\frac{TM_{N}^{d+2}}{N},

(4.5)

for
\lambda:=\frac{1}{4}(1-\frac{C(2M_{N}+1)^{d}}{N\delta_{N}})
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma>0
and
g_{\gamma}(y):=(\gamma+y)\log(\gamma+y),y\in[0,\infty)
. Then
g_{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
, with
(g_{\gamma})^{\prime}(y)=\log(\gamma+y)+1
and
(g_{\gamma})^{\prime\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{\gamma+y}
, and we extend
g_{\gamma}
to
\mathbb{R}
so that the extension is in
C^{2}
. But recall that
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
is positive by the comparison principle, and therefore we only evaluate
g_{\gamma}
on
[0,\infty)
below. By the Itô formula from
[
Par80
, Theorem 1.2]
we have

\displaystyle d\bigg(\int g_{\gamma}(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\langle g_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}),|\nabla\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}|^{2}\rangle dt+\sum_{|k|\leqslant M_{N}}\big\langle g_{\gamma}^{\prime}(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}),\nabla(f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})e_{k})\big\rangle\cdot dB_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{|k|\leqslant M_{N}}\langle g_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}),\lvert\nabla(f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})e_{k})\rvert^{2}\rangle dt

\displaystyle=\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{|\nabla\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}|^{2}}{\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}}+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{|k|\leqslant M_{N}}\int\frac{\lvert\nabla(f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})e_{k})\rvert^{2}}{\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}}\bigg)dt+dM_{t},

where
M
denotes the local martingale term. We estimate the Itô correction term for
\kappa>0
(applying Young’s inequality in the same way as in the proof of the comparison principle) by

\displaystyle\int\frac{\lvert\nabla(f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})e_{k})\rvert^{2}}{\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\kappa)\int\frac{\lvert\nabla(f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}))e_{k}\rvert^{2}}{\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}}+(1+\frac{1}{2\kappa})\frac{\lvert f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})\nabla e_{k}\rvert^{2}}{\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\kappa)\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\int\frac{|\nabla\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}|^{2}}{\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}}+(1+\frac{1}{2\kappa})\bigg\lVert\frac{f}{\sqrt{\cdot}}\bigg\rVert_{\infty}|2\pi k|^{2}.

Summing over
k
and estimating
\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\leqslant C/\delta_{N}
, we obtain for some
K>0

d\bigg(\int g_{\gamma}(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})\bigg)\leqslant\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\bigg((1+\kappa)\frac{C(2M_{N}+1)^{d}}{N\delta_{N}}-1\bigg)\int\frac{|\nabla\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}|^{2}}{\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}}+(1+\frac{1}{2\kappa})K\frac{M_{N}^{d+2}}{N}\bigg)dt+dM_{t},

and by choosing
\kappa>0
appropriately we can achieve that
\frac{1}{2}((1+\kappa)\frac{C(2M_{N}+1)^{d}}{N\delta_{N}}-1)=-\lambda
. Next, we show that the local martingale is a true martingale: Since
\lvert f(\mu)\rvert^{2}\lesssim\lvert\mu\rvert
, its quadratic variation satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}\bigl[\big\lvert\bigl\langle\log(\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})+1,\nabla(f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})e_{k})\bigr\rangle\big\rvert^{2}\bigr]ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}\bigg[\bigg\lvert\int\frac{(f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})e_{k})\nabla u_{s}}{\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}}\bigg\rvert^{2}\bigg]ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\lvert\nabla\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}\rvert^{2}]ds<\infty.

Together, the above yields the bound

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\int(\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})\log(\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})\bigg]+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}\bigg[\int\frac{\lvert\nabla\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}\rvert^{2}}{\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}}\bigg]ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\int(\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0})\log(\gamma+\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0})+\frac{tM_{N}^{d+2}}{N}

As
[0,\infty)\ni x\to x\log(x)
is bounded from below, we can apply Fatou’s lemma to the left-hand side, and since
\lvert x\log(x)\rvert\leqslant x^{2}+1
for
x\geqslant 0
and
\lVert\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}<\infty
, we can apply the dominated convergence theorem to the right-hand side, such that we obtain for
\gamma\to 0
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\bigg[\int\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}\log(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})\bigg]+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}\bigg[\int\frac{\lvert\nabla\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}\rvert^{2}}{\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}}\bigg]ds\lesssim\int\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}\log(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0})+\frac{tM_{N}^{d+2}}{N}.

The claim now follows by taking the supremum in
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

As discussed above, there are good approximations of Dirac masses with moderately large entropy:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(y)dy=1
, such that
\rho\geqslant 0
and
\rho
is symmetric in the sense that
\rho(x)=\rho(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define
\rho^{N}(y):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}N^{d}\rho(N(y+k))
,
y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Then
\rho^{N}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is positive,
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{N}(y)dy=1
, and with
\mu^{N}_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}:=\mu^{N}\ast\rho^{N}
we have

\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}\geqslant 0,\qquad\|\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=1,\qquad\int\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\log(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N})\lesssim\log N.

Moreover, we have for all
\varphi\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\lvert\langle\mu^{N}_{0},\varphi\rangle-\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0},\varphi\rangle\rvert\lesssim N^{-2}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{C^{2}_{b}},

where
\lVert\varphi\rVert_{C^{2}_{b}}:=\sum_{j=0}^{2}\lVert D^{j}\varphi\rVert_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Positivity is obvious because
\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}
is a convolution of positive measures. The statement about the
L^{1}
norm follows by Fubini’s theorem. For the entropy, we first trivially bound

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\lVert\rho^{N}\ast\mu_{0}^{N}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leqslant N^{d}\lVert\rho\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim N^{d}.

Since
x\log x
is negative on
[0,1)
, we get together with the trivial
L^{\infty}
bound:

\displaystyle\int(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N})\log(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N})

\displaystyle\leqslant\int(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N})\log(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N})\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\geqslant 1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\rVert_{L^{1}}\lVert\log(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N})\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\geqslant 1}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\log(\lVert\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\rVert_{L^{\infty}})

\displaystyle\lesssim\log(N^{d})=d\log(N).

To compare
\langle\mu^{N}_{0},\varphi\rangle
and
\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0},\varphi\rangle
, note that

\displaystyle\lvert\langle\mu^{N}_{0},\varphi\rangle-\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0},\varphi\rangle\rvert

\displaystyle=\bigg\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(x_{i})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}N^{d}\rho(N(x_{i}+k-y))\varphi(y)dy\bigg\rvert

\displaystyle=\bigg\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\varphi(x_{i})-\psi^{n}\ast\varphi(x_{i}))\bigg\rvert\leqslant\lVert\varphi-\psi^{N}\ast\varphi\rVert_{\infty},

where
\psi^{N}(x)=N^{d}\rho(Nx)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and where the convolutions in the second line are on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and not on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Using the symmetry of
\rho
, we get for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\lvert(\varphi-\psi^{N}\ast\varphi)(x)\rvert

\displaystyle=\bigg\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(Ny)N^{d}\Big(\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot y-\varphi(y)\Big)dy\bigg\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert\varphi\rVert_{C^{2}_{b}}\bigg\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(Ny)N^{d}|y|^{2}dy\bigg\rvert=N^{-2}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}_{b}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(y)|y|^{2}dy,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Note that the entropy of
\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}
is much smaller than its
L^{2}
norm, which in general we can only bound by
N^{d}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mu^{N}
be the martingale solution of the Dean-Kawasaki equation in the sense of
Definition

2.1
with initial condition
\mu_{0}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{i}}

\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{0}
be as in Lemma

and let
f
and

be as in

\tilde{\mu}^{N}
be the solution of the approximate Dean-Kawasaki equation (

) with initial condition
\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}

Then for any

,
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and

\displaystyle\lvert\mathds{E}[F(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})]-\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]\rvert\lesssim_{\varphi}N^{-2}+\frac{\delta_{N}}{N}t+\frac{t}{M_{N}^{2}N}+\frac{M_{N}^{d}}{N^{2}}t+\frac{\log N}{M_{N}^{2}N}.

(4.6)

For
M_{N}=\delta_{N}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\lvert\mathds{E}[F(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})]-\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]\rvert\lesssim_{\varphi}N^{-1-\frac{1}{d/2+1}}(t+\log(N)).

(4.7)

Remark 4.5
.

With the functions
F_{k}(\mu)=\exp(\langle\mu,\varphi_{k}\rangle)
for a suitable dense set
(\varphi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we could use
[
EK86
, Theorem 3.4.5]
to construct a metric for the topology of weak convergence of probability measures on
\mathcal{M}
and then replace the left-hand side of (

) by the distance of
\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}
and
\mu^{N}_{t}
in this metric.

Proof of
Theorem

4.4
.

To prove the weak error bound (
4.7
), we apply the duality argument of
[
KLvR19
]
.
For that purpose, let
v
solve the Hamilton-Jacobi equation with initial condition
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v=\frac{1}{2}\Delta v+\frac{1}{2N}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2},\quad v_{0}=\varphi.

(4.8)

The Cole-Hopf transformation
w=e^{\frac{1}{N}v}
solves
\partial_{t}w=\frac{1}{2}\Delta w
, and therefore with the heat kernel
p
of
\frac{1}{2}\Delta
(on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with periodic boundary conditions):

v_{t}=N\log(p_{t}\ast e^{\frac{1}{N}\varphi}).

By the maximum principle we have
v_{t}\in[\min_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi(x),\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi(x)]
for all

, and by the explicit representation of the solution we get

|\partial_{i}v_{t}|=\bigg\lvert N\frac{p_{t}\ast\big(\frac{1}{N}\partial_{i}\varphi e^{\frac{1}{N}\varphi}\big)}{p_{t}\ast e^{\frac{1}{N}\varphi}}\bigg\rvert\lesssim_{\varphi}1

uniformly in
N
and
t
, and by a similar application of the chain rule also
|\partial_{ij}v_{t}|\lesssim_{\varphi}1
uniformly in
N
and
t
, so overall
\sup_{t\geqslant 0,N\in\mathbb{N}}\lVert v\rVert_{C^{2}_{b}}\lesssim_{\varphi}1
.

By definition, we have
F(\mu^{N}_{t})=\exp(\langle\mu^{N}_{t},v_{t-t}\rangle)
and with the Laplace duality of
[
KLvR19

we obtain

\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]=\exp(\langle\mu_{0}^{N},v_{t}\rangle).

For
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
we have on
[0,t]

\displaystyle d\langle\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{N},v_{t-s}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{N},\big(-\partial_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\big)v_{t-s}\rangle ds-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\langle f(\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{N})e_{k},\nabla v_{t-s}\rangle dB^{k}_{s},

(4.9)

and using equation (
4.8
) for
v
, we arrive at

\displaystyle d\big(\exp(\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},v_{t-s}\rangle)\big)_{s}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\exp(\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},v_{t-s}\rangle)\langle f(\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{N})e_{k},\nabla v_{t-s}\rangle dB_{s}^{k}

(4.10)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2N}\exp(\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},v_{t-s}\rangle)\bigg(\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\!\!\lvert\langle f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})e_{k},\nabla v_{t-s}\rangle\rvert^{2}-\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},\lvert\nabla v_{t-s}\rvert^{2}\rangle\bigg)ds.

Note that the stochastic integral on the right hand side is indeed a martingale (not only a local martingale). This can be concluded from the bounds
\exp(\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},v_{t-s}\rangle)\lesssim 1
(by mass conservation of
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
and the uniform bounds for
v
),
|\nabla v_{t-s}|\lesssim 1
and
f(u)^{2}\lesssim u
, which yield

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\exp(\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},v_{t-s}\rangle)\lvert\langle f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})e_{k},\nabla v_{t-s}\rangle\rvert^{2}]ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\lVert f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})\nabla v_{t-s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}]ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\lVert\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}\rVert_{L^{1}}]ds=t.

Writing (
4.10
) in integral form and taking the expectation, we thus obtain the bound

\displaystyle\lvert\mathds{E}[F(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})]-\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lvert\exp(\langle\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N},v_{t}\rangle)-\exp(\langle\mu_{0}^{N},v_{t}\rangle)\rvert

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2N}\mathds{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\exp(\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},v_{t-s}\rangle)\bigg\lvert\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\!\!\!\lvert\langle f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})e_{k},\nabla v_{t-s}\rangle\rvert^{2}-\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},\lvert\nabla v_{t-s}\rvert^{2}\rangle\bigg\rvert ds\bigg].

(4.11)

Since
\exp(\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},v_{t-s}\rangle)\lesssim 1
, we are left with estimating the term in the brackets in (
4.11
).
To that aim, we write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N}\bigg(\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\lvert\langle f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})e_{k},\nabla v_{t-s}\rangle\rvert^{2}-\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},\lvert\nabla v_{t-s}\rvert^{2}\rangle\bigg)=\frac{A_{s}+C_{s}}{2N},

where

A_{s}:=\langle f(\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{N})^{2},|\nabla v_{t-s}|^{2}\rangle-\langle\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{N},|\nabla v_{t-s}|^{2}\rangle

and

\displaystyle C_{s}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\lvert\langle f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})e_{k},\nabla v_{t-s}\rangle\rvert^{2}-\langle f(\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{N})^{2},|\nabla v_{t-s}|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert k\rvert\leqslant M_{N}}\lvert\langle f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})\nabla v_{t-s},e_{k}\rangle\rvert^{2}-\lVert f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})\nabla v_{t-s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

To estimate
A
, we use positivity of
\tilde{\mu}^{N}
from
Corollary

3.14
, as well as (
4.3
), such that
\lvert x-f(x)^{2}\rvert\lesssim\delta_{N}
for
x\geqslant 0
:

\displaystyle\lvert A_{s}\rvert=\lvert\langle\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s},\lvert\nabla v_{t-s}\rvert^{2}\rangle-\langle f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})^{2},\lvert\nabla v_{t-s}\rvert^{2}\rangle\rvert\lesssim\delta_{N}\int\lvert\nabla v_{t-s}\rvert^{2}(x)dx\lesssim\delta_{N}.

(4.12)

In order to estimate the term
C_{s}
, we apply Parseval’s identity and obtain with
g_{s}:=f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})\nabla v_{t-s}

\displaystyle\lvert C_{s}\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lvert k\rvert>M_{N}}\lvert\langle g_{s},e_{k}\rangle\rvert^{2}\leqslant M_{N}^{-2}\sum_{\lvert m\rvert>M_{N}}\lvert m\rvert^{2}\lvert\hat{g}_{s}(m)\rvert^{2}\leqslant M_{N}^{-2}\lVert\nabla g_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Now we get from the chain rule

\lVert\nabla g_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\lVert f(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lVert v_{t-s}\rVert_{C^{2}_{b}}^{2}+\lVert f^{\prime}(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s})\nabla\mu^{N}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lVert v_{t-s}\rVert_{C^{1}_{b}}^{2}\lesssim\lVert v_{t-s}\rVert_{C^{2}_{b}}\bigg(\lVert\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}\rVert_{L^{1}}+\int\frac{\lvert\nabla\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}\rvert^{2}}{\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}}\bigg),

where we used that

f(x)^{2}\lesssim\lvert x\rvert
and
f^{\prime}(x)^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{x}
for
x>0
by (
4.2
). Integrating over time, using the entropy estimates from
Proposition

4.2
to bound the second term and conservation of mass,
\lVert\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{s}\rVert_{L^{1}}=1
, for the first term (here we need the uniform-in-
N
coercivity bound to not pick up some
\lambda_{N}^{-1}
in the estimates), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}[\lvert C_{s}\rvert]ds\lesssim_{\varphi}\frac{1}{M_{N}^{2}}\bigg(t+\frac{tM_{N}^{d+2}}{N}+\int\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}\log(\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N})\bigg)\lesssim\frac{1}{M_{N}^{2}}\bigg(t+\frac{tM_{N}^{d+2}}{N}+\log N\bigg),

(4.13)

where the last bound holds by Lemma
4.3
. We estimate the error from the initial condition with the mean value theorem as follows:

\displaystyle\lvert\exp(\langle\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N},v_{t}\rangle)-\exp(\langle\mu_{0}^{N},v_{t}\rangle)\rvert\leqslant\lvert\langle\tilde{\mu}_{0}^{N}-\mu_{0}^{N},v_{t}\rangle\rvert\exp(\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{\infty})\lesssim_{\varphi}N^{-2},

(4.14)

where the last bound holds again by Lemma
4.3
. Plugging (
4.12
)–(
4.14
) into (
4.11
), we obtain for the weak error:

\displaystyle\lvert\mathds{E}[F(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})]-\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]\rvert\lesssim_{\varphi}N^{-2}+\frac{\delta_{N}}{N}t+\frac{t}{M_{N}^{2}N}+\frac{M_{N}^{d}}{N^{2}}t+\frac{\log N}{M_{N}^{2}N}.

The coercivity assumption dictates
M_{N}^{d}\lesssim\delta_{N}N
. Hence, if we estimate
M_{N}^{d}
by
\delta_{N}N
in the fourth term, we get

\displaystyle\lvert\mathds{E}[F(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})]-\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]\rvert\lesssim_{\varphi}N^{-2}+\frac{\delta_{N}}{N}t+\frac{t}{M_{N}^{2}N}+\frac{\log N}{M_{N}^{2}N}.

(4.15)

Choosing
M_{N}=\delta_{N}^{-1/2}
and respecting the coercivity assumption, we arrive at the optimal choice
\delta_{N}\simeq N^{-\frac{1}{d/2+1}}
. Altogether, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\lvert\mathds{E}[F(\tilde{\mu}^{N}_{t})]-\mathds{E}[F(\mu^{N}_{t})]\rvert\lesssim_{\varphi}N^{-2}+N^{-1-\frac{1}{d/2+1}}(t+\log N)\lesssim N^{-1-\frac{1}{d/2+1}}(t+\log N).

∎
