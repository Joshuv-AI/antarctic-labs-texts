An optimization dichotomy for capital injections and absolutely continuous dividend strategies
Jean-François Renaud, Alexandre Roch, Clarence Simard

Abstract
. We consider an optimal stochastic control problem in which a firm’s cash/surplus process is controlled by dividend payments and capital injections. Stockholders aim to maximize their dividend stream minus the cost of injecting capital, if needed. We consider absolutely continuous dividend policies subject to a level-dependent upper bound on the dividend rate while we allow for general capital injections behavior. We prove that the optimal strategy can only be of two types: dividends are paid according to a mean-reverting strategy with capital injections performed each time the cash process reaches zero; or, dividends are paid according to another mean-reverting strategy and no injection of capital is ever made, until ruin is reached. We give a complete solution to this problem and characterize this dichotomy by comparing (the derivatives of) the value functions at zero of two sub-problems. The first sub-problem is concerned solely with the maximization of dividends, while the second sub-problem is the corresponding bail-out optimal dividend problem for which we provide also a complete solution.



1.
Introduction

The problem of maximizing a stream of dividend payments under a ruin constraint goes back to Bruno de Finetti
[
13
]
in the context of insurance mathematics and Jeanblanc & Shiryaev
[
17
]
in a Brownian setup. In this paper, we consider absolutely continuous dividend policies and we allow general capital injection controls. We give a complete solution to the problem of optimizing simultaneously dividends and capital injections when the value of the firm follows an arithmetic Brownian motion and the rate of dividends is bounded by an increasing concave function of the controlled process. The optimal pair depends on the parameters and the upper bound of the dividend rate according to the following dichotomy: dividends are paid using a bang-bang strategy while capital injections must be made each time the cash/surplus process reaches zero, so the firm is never ruined; or, dividends are paid using another bang-bang strategy and no injection of capital is ever made until ruin. No in-between strategy is optimal. If it is optimal to inject capital, then those injections will take the form of bail-out payments, i.e., they will occur only when the cash process hits zero, making the controlled process a reflected (at zero) process. On the other hand, the dividend component of the optimal strategy always consists in using the maximum dividend rate allowed when the controlled process is above an optimal threshold, giving rise to a refracted process in between capital injections (or up to the time of ruin). Our methods of proof rely on a combination of probabilistic and analytical techniques, namely fluctuation identities for diffusions and the theory of viscosity solutions for the associated Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equations.

1.1.
Problem formulation

On a filtered probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}=\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t},t\geq 0\right\},\mathbb{P}\right)
, consider a Brownian motion with drift
X=\left\{X_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
given by

(1)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\mu\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},

where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma>0
, and where
B=\left\{B_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
is a standard Brownian motion.

A control strategy is a pair
\pi=(\ell,G)
of adapted processes
\ell=\left\{\ell_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
and
G=\left\{G_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
, where
\ell_{t}
is the dividend rate at time
t
and
G_{t}
is the amount of capital injections made up to time
t
. It is assumed that
G
is nondecreasing and right-continuous with
G_{0-}=0
. The corresponding controlled process
X^{\pi}
is given by

\mathrm{d}X^{\pi}_{t}=\left(\mu-\ell_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}G_{t},

with
X^{\pi}_{0-}=X_{0}
. We also define the termination time by
T^{\pi}=\inf\{t>0:X^{\pi}_{t}<0\}
. In the spirit of
[
25
]
and
[
20
]
, we will impose a state-dependent bound on the dividend rate. More specifically, let
F\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a nondecreasing, concave and continuously differentiable function such that
F(0)\geq 0
. A strategy
\pi=(\ell,G)
is admissible if, for all
t\geq 0
, we have

0\leq\ell_{t}\leq F(X^{\pi}_{t}).

The set of admissible strategies will be denoted by
\Pi
. Also, we use the Markovian notation
\mathbb{E}_{x}
to denote the expectation given that
X^{\pi}_{0-}=X_{0}=x
, for a fixed
x\geq 0
.

We aim at solving the following control problem. Let
q>0
be the discounting rate and
\beta>1
the cost of injecting capital. For
x\geq 0
, we define the performance function of a strategy
\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi
by

J(x;\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G_{t})\right].

We want to compute the value function defined by

(2)

V(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}J(x;\pi),\quad x\geq 0,

and find an optimal strategy, i.e., an admissible strategy for which the supremum is attained.

1.2.
Related literature, contributions and organization of the paper

In the literature, optimal dividend strategies found are often of the bang-bang type. For instance, in the absolutely continuous problem, the optimal policy often consists in paying at the maximum (constant) rate allowed when the cash process reaches a certain threshold and nothing otherwise. Similarly, in the singular case, the optimal behavior yields a reflected (at an optimal barrier) controlled process and no dividend payments below
[
3
,
4
,
17
]
. We follow a new trend of dividend maximization problems aiming for more realistic optimal dividend strategies by imposing a level-dependent bound on the dividend rate in the absolutely continuous case
[
25
,
20
]
. For instance, in the linear-bound case of
[
25
]
, dividend rates are bounded by a
proportion
of the current level of cash, allowing for the more natural situation in which the dividend rate increases as a function of the financial health of the firm.

Maximization of dividends with or without bail-out payments has been studied extensively in a variety of models. The expression “bail-out payments” usually refers to forced injections of capital when the cash process reaches (or goes below) zero, making the controlled process a reflected process. Sethi and Taksar
[
26
]
consider a model in which dividends are paid as singular (reflective) controls and the firm is not allowed to go bankrupt and must issue external equity to keep the firm afloat. Shreve et al.
[
27
]
present a similar mathematical model in the context of inventory control. The optimal dichotomy between injecting capital or letting the cash process get ruined has been first studied by Løkka and Zervos
[
21
]
(a result sometimes called the “Løkka-Zervos alternative”). Avram et al.
[
7
,
6
]
consider a similar dichotomy for Cramér-Lundberg models. This control problem in a singular setting has also been studied in a Lévy model with upward jumps by Avanzi et al.
[
4
]
. Adding upward jumps to a Brownian motion does not significantly alter the difficulty of the problem, but it becomes more difficult when there are downward jumps
[
6
]
.

The theory of viscosity solutions for variational inequalities has been used extensively in the literature to study stochastic control problems. However, the absence of explicit expressions for the optimal performance function and the optimal strategy often forbids a thorough study of the optimal controlled process. In control problems related to the payment of dividends, a number of authors have tackled the question by proposing a large number of financial models and used viscosity theory to give a solution. Choulli et al.
[
11
]
describe the optimal dividend and business policy in a diffusion model using a surplus process in which dividends are paid in a reflective way and the optimal business risk can be described in terms of optimal thresholds. Décamps and Villeneuve
[
14
]
consider a dividend control problem of the singular type (reflective) with a growth option (modelled by a stopping time) giving rise to a dichotomy in which one possible solution involves exercising the growth option when the surplus reaches a certain threshold and the other solution is to entirely ignore the option. Similarly, Ly Vath et al.
[
22
]
present a dividend model with reversible technology investments. Chevalier et al.
[
9
]
present a dividend and capital injection model in which a grace period is allowed before the firm is declared bankrupt. Using viscosity comparison arguments, it is shown that the optimal capital injection strategy occurs as a reflection when the surplus hits zero after some time spent in the grace period and as an impulse when the grace period expires. More recently, Albrecher et al.
[
1
,
2
]
proposed a dividend model in which the dividend rate is not allowed to decrease over time (so-called ratcheting strategies) and use the theory of viscosity solutions to characterize the value function in terms of its HJB equation and provide numerical examples.

Commonly, the main difficulty in optimal dividend problems (for both the refracted and reflected types) is to show the existence of an optimal threshold, corresponding to an optimal barrier strategy, and characterize its value. Fluctuation theory, when applied to stochastic control problems, often leads to explicit (or semi-explicit) expressions for performance functions associated to barrier-type strategies. The optimal performance function among this subset of strategies is then computed by using probabilistic arguments together with fluctuation identities. If the subset of strategies contains the value function of the initial problem and if one can identify it, then the optimality can then be shown using a verification lemma, as long as it is smooth enough.

In this work, we solve two stochastic control problems: a (forced) bail-out optimal dividends problem and the main optimization problem in which capital injections are not forced. For the bail-out problem, we combine ideas from the fluctuation theory approach and the viscosity theory approach to fully characterize the solution. In particular, we are able to show the existence of the optimal barrier and characterize it. This allows us to consider a general set of admissible strategies while maintaining the advantage of HJB equations analysis (which involve derivatives of the value function) to describe the behavior of optimal strategies, even though smoothness of the value function is not known a priori. Therefore, one of our main contributions is methodological. In the bail-out optimal dividends problem, a strategy is one-dimensional as it consists of a dividend payment rate, while capital injections behaviors is pre-determined. In the main optimization problem, we consider two-dimensional strategies consisting of a capital injection policy and a dividend payment rate. In the literature, it is frequently argued that optimal injections should occur only at zero, involving an inspection of the associated variational inequality and/or using analytical properties of the performance function in combination with verification theorems. As a first step to solve our general control problem, we directly show that among the large class of admissible capital injection strategies (i.e., adapted and nondecreasing processes) the only potentially optimal behaviors are continuous and can only increase at zero. This is done by comparing solutions of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) controlled by a nondecreasing process. In other words, the optimal injections make the controlled process a reflected (at zero) process. As such, optimal injections can be seen as a last resort to keep the cash process from ruin, when it is optimal to do so. Then, the theory of viscosity solutions also allows us to derive a dichotomy that it is optimal to either always inject at zero or never inject, with the help of a comparison principle for solutions of the associated HJB equation. This new comparison result is based on either the initial values of the super and subsolutions or their initial derivatives.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we present the main results of this paper. In Section
3
, notation and preliminary results, needed in the rest of the paper, are defined and given. In Section
4
, the solution to the problem without injections is presented, while in Section
5
, we solve the bail-out dividend problem, i.e., the problem with forced injections. Finally, in Section
6
, a solution to the main problem is provided.

2.
Main results

In this section, we present the solutions of three optimization problems, of which two are new.

2.1.
Maximization of dividends without capital injections

First, we consider the maximization of dividends when capital injections are not allowed. To wit, define

\Pi_{d}=\left\{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi\colon G\equiv 0\right\},

with corresponding value function

V_{d}(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{d}}J(x;\pi),\quad x\geq 0.

When
F(x)=S
for a given constant
S>0
, we recover the classical problem studied by
[
17
]
. If
F(x)=Kx
, for a given constant
K>0
, then we recover the control problem studied by
[
25
]
. Finally, the general case, i.e., when
F
is a function with the abovementioned properties, the problem has been solved recently by
[
20
]
. To state their result, let
b\geq 0
and define the mean-reverting dividend strategy at level
b
by
\ell^{b}_{t}=F(X^{b}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{b}_{t}\geq b\}}
with the associated controlled process

\mathrm{d}X^{b}_{t}=\left(\mu-F(X^{b}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{b}_{t}\geq b\}}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},

and with termination time
T^{b}=\inf\{t>0:X^{b}_{t}<0\}
. Existence of a strong solution to this stochastic differential equation (SDE) is given in
[
19
]
.

Theorem 1

(
[
20
]
)
.

There exists a constant
b_{d}\geq 0
such that a mean-reverting dividend strategy at level
b_{d}
is optimal for the control problem without capital injections. More precisely, for all
x\geq 0
, we have

V_{d}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{b_{d}}}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}F(X^{b_{d}}_{s})\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{b_{d}}_{s}\geq b_{d}\}}\mathrm{d}s\right].

The optimal barrier level
b_{d}
is a solution of Equation (
7
) in Proposition
1
. Also, an analytical expression for
V_{d}
is available. Of course, we have
V_{d}(0)=0
. More details in Section
4
.

Remark 1
.

The term ”mean-reverting strategy” originates from
[
5
]
. Since then, it has been used also by the follow-up literature, even when it is not standard mean-reversion. We also adopt this terminology.

2.2.
Maximization of dividends with forced capital injections

Second, we consider the maximization of dividends with forced capital injections, i.e., preventing the controlled process from going below zero. Specifically, define

\Pi_{c}=\left\{\pi\in\Pi\colon X^{\pi}_{t}\geq 0\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0\right\}

with corresponding value function

V_{c}(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{c}}J(x;\pi),\quad x\geq 0.

Note that, for each
\pi\in\Pi_{c}
, we have
T^{\pi}=\infty
.

In order to present our solution to this optimization problem, we introduce controlled processes reflected at zero. Fix an admissible dividend rate
\ell
. For
t\geq 0
, define
\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t}=-\min\{X^{(\ell,0)}_{s}\wedge 0:0\leq s\leq t\}
.
In this case,
\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi_{c}
and the corresponding controlled process is nonnegative at all times, i.e., the process given by

\mathrm{d}X^{\pi}_{t}=(\mu-\ell_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t},

with
X^{\pi}_{0}\geq 0
, is such that
X^{\pi}_{t}\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
and

\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{\pi}_{s}=0\}}\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{\ell}_{s}.

In other words, the process
X^{\pi}
is reflected at zero. This is the famous Skorohod problem. See, for instance, Lemma 3.6.14 in
[
18
]
.

Extending the notation from the previous section, for a given
b\geq 0
, we define the reflected mean-reverting dividend strategy at level
b
by the dividend rate
\underline{\ell}^{b}_{t}=F(\underline{X}^{b}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{\underline{X}^{b}_{t}\geq b\}}
, with associated controlled reflected process

\mathrm{d}\underline{X}^{b}_{t}=\left(\mu-F(\underline{X}^{b}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{\underline{X}^{b}_{t}\geq b\}}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{b}_{t},

where the injection process
\underline{G}^{b}
satisfies

\underline{G}^{b}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{\underline{X}^{b}_{s}=0\}}\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{b}_{s}.

Of course, we have
\underline{X}^{b}_{t}\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
. The existence of a solution to this specific Skorohod problem can be found in Lemma
4
in Appendix
B
. Note that the representation
\underline{G}^{b}=\underline{G}^{\ell^{b}}
is valid, but can only be well defined once
\underline{X}^{b}
is shown to exist.

A solution of this second maximization problem is given in the following theorem and its proof is the objective of Section
5
.

Theorem 2
.

There exists a constant
b_{c}>0
such that a reflected mean-reverting dividend strategy at level
b_{c}
is optimal for the control problem with forced capital injections. More precisely, for all
x\geq 0
, we have

V_{c}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}\left(F(\underline{X}^{b_{c}}_{s})\mathbf{1}_{\{\underline{X}^{b_{c}}_{s}\geq b_{c}\}}\mathrm{d}s-\beta\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{b_{c}}_{s}\right)\right].

The optimal barrier level
b_{c}
is characterized as the unique solution of either Equation (
18
) or Equation (
19
) given below. An analytical expression for
V_{c}
is also derived in Section
5
. In particular, we will show that
V^{\prime}_{c}(0+)=\beta
.

2.3.
Optimization of capital injections and dividend payments

Using the solutions of the previous two maximization problems, our solution of the main optimization problem (
2
) is as follows.

Theorem 3
.

The value function
V
is characterized by the following dichotomy:

•

if
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)<\beta
, then an optimal strategy is given by
(\ell^{b_{d}},0)
and
V=V_{d}
;

•

if
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)>\beta
, then an optimal strategy is given by
(\underline{\ell}^{b_{c}},\underline{G}^{b_{c}})
and
V=V_{c}
.

Equivalently,

•

if
V_{c}(0)<0
, then an optimal strategy is given by
(\ell^{b_{d}},0)
and
V=V_{d}
;

•

if
V_{c}(0)>0
, then an optimal strategy is given by
(\underline{\ell}^{b_{c}},\underline{G}^{b_{c}})
and
V=V_{c}
.

Finally, if
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
or
V_{c}(0)=0
, then both
(\ell^{b_{d}},0)
and
(\underline{\ell}^{b_{c}},\underline{G}^{b_{c}})
are optimal strategies, and we have
V=V_{c}=V_{d}
.

Intuitively, the condition
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)>\beta=V_{c}^{\prime}(0+)
means that, when the process reaches zero, then it is worth injecting capital and keep on going for more dividends later. On the other hand, the condition
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)<\beta
means that the marginal benefit of keeping the process
alive
is less than the marginal cost of injecting capital.

Similarly, if
V_{c}(0)<0=V_{d}(0)
, then the value of keeping the process nonnegative with injections is less than the value of letting it get ruined when reaching zero. For the condition
V_{c}(0)>0
, we have the opposite interpretation.

Note that, if
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
or
V_{c}(0)=0
, then the firm is indifferent between paying dividends at level
b_{c}
and injecting capital each time zero is attained, or paying dividends at level
b_{d}
and never inject capital (which means getting ruined at the first passage at level zero).

4.
Solution of the problem without injections

First, we define the performance function of an arbitrary mean-reverting strategy at level
b
, when injections are not allowed: for
x\geq 0
, define

J_{d}(x;b)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{b}}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}F(X^{b}_{s})\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{b}_{s}\geq b\}}\mathrm{d}s\right].

Of course, for any fixed
b\geq 0
, we have
J_{d}(x;b)\leq V_{d}(x)
for all
x\geq 0
, which of course yields
\sup_{b\geq 0}J_{d}(x;b)\leq V_{d}(x)
for all
x\geq 0
.

In this section, we briefly summarize the main findings of Locas & Renaud
[
20
]
who showed that there exists a level
b_{d}
such that
J_{d}(x;b_{d})=V_{d}(x)
for all
x\geq 0
. They also gave a characterization of this threshold. In the next theorem, we give a slightly different but equivalent statement of their main result.

Theorem 4
.

If
b=0
, then
J_{d}(x;b)=-I_{F}(0)\varphi(x)+I_{F}(x)
. If
b>0
, then

J_{d}(x;b)=\begin{cases}D_{1}(b)\psi(x),&\text{if $0\leq x\leq b$,}\\
D_{2}(b)\varphi(x)+I_{F}(x),&\text{if $x\geq b$,}\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle D_{1}(b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi(b)I_{F}^{\prime}(b)-\varphi^{\prime}(b)I_{F}(b)}{\varphi(b)\psi^{\prime}(b)-\varphi^{\prime}(b)\psi(b)},

\displaystyle D_{2}(b)=\frac{\psi(b)I_{F}^{\prime}(b)-\psi^{\prime}(b)I_{F}(b)}{\varphi(b)\psi^{\prime}(b)-\varphi^{\prime}(b)\psi(b)}.

First, it is interesting to note that
D_{2}(0)=-I_{F}(0)
. Second, it is even more interesting to note that, in the above theorem, the function
I_{F}
can be replaced by
I_{F}^{\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, in
[
20
]
, the result is stated with
\tilde{I}_{F}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as defined in (
6
).

Finally, it is straightforward to show that, for
b>0
, we have
J_{d}^{\prime}(b-;b)=J_{d}^{\prime}(b+;b)
, which means that
x\mapsto J_{d}(x;b)
is a continuously differentiable function. Moreover, the optimal threshold
b_{d}
is the value (of
b
) that makes
J_{d}(\cdot;b_{d})
a twice continuously differentiable function or, equivalently, such that
J_{d}^{\prime}(b_{d};b_{d})=1
.

Proposition 1
.

The optimal barrier level
b_{d}
is characterized as follows:

(1)

if
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi^{\prime}(0)\leq 1
, then
b_{d}=0
;

(2)

if
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi^{\prime}(0)>1
, then
b_{d}>0
and it is a solution of

(7)

I_{F}^{\prime}(b_{d})\varphi(b_{d})-I_{F}(b_{d})\varphi^{\prime}(b_{d})=\varphi(b_{d})-\frac{\psi(b_{d})}{\psi^{\prime}(b_{d})}\varphi^{\prime}(b_{d}).

In both cases,
V_{d}(\cdot)=J_{d}(\cdot;b_{d})\in C^{2}([0,\infty))
.

Putting all of the above together gives Theorem
1
and more. In particular, as we have an
explicit
expression for
V_{d}
, we can use it with the optimization dichotomy (in Theorem
3
). Indeed, if
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi^{\prime}(0)\leq 1
, then
b_{d}=0
and hence
V^{\prime}_{d}(0+)=I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi^{\prime}(0)<\beta
. From Theorem
3
, we can deduce that, if
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi^{\prime}(0)\leq 1
, then
V=V_{d}
. If
I_{F}^{\prime}(0)-I_{F}(0)\varphi^{\prime}(0)>1
, then
b_{d}>0
and hence
V^{\prime}_{d}(0+)=\frac{\psi^{\prime}(0+)}{\psi^{\prime}(b_{d})}
. As
\psi^{\prime}(0+)=2/\sigma^{2}
, we can further write

V^{\prime}_{d}(0+)=\frac{(2/\sigma^{2})\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2q\sigma^{2}}}{\alpha_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{1}b_{d}}-\alpha_{2}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{2}b_{d}}},

which can be compared numerically with
\beta
in order to apply the dichotomy result of Theorem
3
.

5.
Solution of the problem with forced injections

In this section, we provide a solution to the maximization problem with forced capital injections, also called the bail-out optimal dividend problem. In particular, we prove Theorem
2
.

However, we start by showing in the next section that
optimal
capital injections should only take the form of bail-out payments. This will lead to a new representation for
V_{c}
but also for
V
, the value function of our main optimization problem; see Proposition
2
below.

5.1.
Bail-out payments

Let us introduce the following sets of admissible controls in which capital injections can only be performed when the corresponding controlled process reaches level zero. To this purpose, we define

\Pi^{0}_{c}=\{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi_{c}\colon G_{t}=\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t}\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0\}

and

\Pi^{0}=\{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi\colon\mbox{ there exists }\tau\in\mathcal{T}\mbox{ such that }G_{t}=\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t\wedge\tau}\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0\},

where
\mathcal{T}
is the set of all
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times.

Remark 3
.

In those sub-families of controls, capital injections are performed only at level zero. However, a control
\pi
in
\Pi^{0}
may differ from a process in
\Pi^{0}_{c}
since the latter keeps the process
X^{\pi}
nonnegative (by reflecting it at zero) but not necessarily the former. This means that, if
\pi\in\Pi^{0}
, then we might have
T^{\pi}<\infty
.

It is possible to pair each control
\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi
with a control
\pi^{0}=(\ell,G^{0})\in\Pi^{0}
by using
G^{0}_{t}:=\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t\wedge\tau}
with
\tau=T^{\pi}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi
(resp.
\Pi_{c}
). Then there exists
\pi^{0}=(\ell,G^{0})\in\Pi^{0}
(resp.
\Pi_{c}^{0}
) such that
J(x;\pi)\leq J(x;\pi^{0})
for all
x\geq 0.

Proof.

Let
\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi
(resp.
\Pi_{c}
) and then let
\pi^{0}=(\ell,G^{0})\in\Pi^{0}
(resp.
\Pi_{c}^{0}
) where
G^{0}_{t}:=\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t\wedge\tau}
with
\tau=T^{\pi}
. Then,
T^{\pi}=T^{\pi^{0}}
by Lemma
3
of Appendix
B
. In the case
\pi\in\Pi_{c}
, since we have
T^{\pi}=\infty
, then
G^{0}_{t}=\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
. In either case, by Lemma
3
of Appendix
B
, we have

\int_{0}^{T^{\pi^{0}}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}G^{0}_{t}\leq\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}G_{t}.

As a result, for
x\geq 0
,

J(x;\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G_{t})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\pi^{0}}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G^{0}_{t})\right]=J(x;\pi^{0}).

∎

As a consequence of the previous lemma, we deduce the following characterization of the value functions
V_{c}
and
V
:

Proposition 2
.

For all
x\geq 0
,

V_{c}(x)=\sup_{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi_{c}^{0}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G_{t})\right]

and

V(x)=\sup_{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi^{0}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G_{t})\right].

This last representation of
V
can be interpreted as follows: given an admissible strategy
\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi^{0}
and its associated stopping time
\tau
, the only injection strategies that may be optimal consist in injecting only when
X^{\pi}
hits zero, by means of a reflection using
\underline{G}^{\ell}
, up to time
\tau,
and avoiding all capital injections thereafter. In fact, in the proof of Lemma
1
, we see that the only relevant stopping times
\tau
are the ruin time of
X^{\pi}
for some given general policy
\pi\in\Pi.
However, the Markovian structure of the problem also suggests that an optimal strategy (and its associated ruin time) is a function of the controlled process
X
. In particular, we expect the ruin time of the optimal strategy to be the hitting time of
0
, or no ruin at all. In other words, if it is optimal to stop reflecting when
X^{\pi}
hits zero after some time, it should in fact be optimal to do so the first time it does (hence no reflection at all). Otherwise, it is never optimal to let the process reach ruin, and reflection is used throughout. To prove this dichotomy, we use a comparison principle for solution of the associated ODE and compare the initial values of
V_{d}
and
V_{c}
at. See Section
6
below.

5.2.
Viscosity characterization of the value function

The HJB equation associated to
V_{c}
is given by: for
x>0
,

(8)

\displaystyle qu(x)-\sup_{0\leq l\leq F(x)}\mathcal{L}_{l}[u](x)=0,

with initial condition

(9)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(0)=\beta,

where, for
u\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
and a constant
l\geq 0
,

\mathcal{L}_{l}[u](x):=(\mu-l)u^{\prime}(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}u^{\prime\prime}(x)+l.

In this section, we show that
V_{c}
is a viscosity solution of (
8
)-(
9
), and use this to characterize the optimal threshold
b_{c}
of Theorem
2
. We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for a definition of viscosity solutions and a comparison principle for (
8
), as these notions will be used frequently in the rest of the paper.

Remark 4
.

Note that
V_{d}
is a (classical) solution of the HJB equation (
8
), with initial condition
u(0)=0
. See
[
20
]
for details.

Remark 5
.

Since the value function
V_{c}
is nondecreasing (which simply follows from its definition) and since it is always possible to inject an amount
\epsilon
, we know that

V_{c}(x+\epsilon)\geq V_{c}(x)\geq V_{c}(x+\epsilon)-\beta\epsilon,\quad x\geq 0,

which can be re-written as

0\leq\frac{V_{c}(x+\epsilon)-V_{c}(x)}{\epsilon}\leq\beta.

In particular, we deduce that
V_{c}
is continuous.

In the rest of the paper, we will need the following dynamic programming principle (DPP) for the value functions
V_{c}
and
V
, which uses the characterizations obtained in Proposition
2
. See
[
16
]
for a similar DPP and its proof.

Proposition 3
.

For any stopping time
\tau
, we have

V_{c}(x)=\sup_{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi_{c}^{0}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G_{t})+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau}V_{c}(X^{\pi}_{\tau})\right].

The result is also true for
V
with
\Pi_{c}^{0}
replaced by
\Pi^{0}
.

The following proposition presents other basic properties of this value function.

Proposition 4
.

The value function
V_{c}
is concave. Furthermore, there exist two constants
A<1
and
C>0
such that, for
x\geq 0
,

(10)

V_{c}(x)\leq C+Ax.

In particular,
V_{c}\in\mathcal{W}
.

Proof.

To show concavity, fix
x_{1},x_{2}\in(0,\infty)
and
0<\lambda<1
, and then define
x=\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2}
. For an arbitrary strategy
\pi^{i}=(\ell^{i},G^{i})\in\Pi_{c}
, set
X^{\pi^{i}}_{0-}=x_{i}
, for each
i=1,2
. Finally, let
X^{\pi}
be the process controlled by
\pi=(\ell,G):=(\lambda\ell^{1}+(1-\lambda)\ell^{2},\lambda G^{1}+(1-\lambda)G^{2})
and such that
X^{\pi}_{0-}=x
. By the linearity of the dynamics, we know that
X^{\pi}=\lambda X^{\pi^{1}}+(1-\lambda)X^{\pi^{2}}
and hence
X^{\pi}_{t}\geq 0
, for all
t\geq 0
. Furthermore, since
F
is concave, we have that, for any
t\geq 0
,

\ell_{t}=\lambda\ell^{1}_{t}+(1-\lambda)\ell^{2}_{t}\leq\lambda F(X^{\pi^{1}}_{t})+(1-\lambda)F(X^{\pi^{2}}_{t})\leq F(X^{\pi}_{t}).

Therefore,
\pi\in\Pi_{c}
and

\displaystyle V_{c}(x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle J(x;\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbb{E}_{x_{1}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}^{1}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}G^{1}_{t})\right]+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}_{x_{2}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\beta dG^{2}_{t})\right].

Since
\pi^{1}
and
\pi^{2}
are arbitrary in
\Pi_{c}
, we can further write
V_{c}(x)\geq\lambda V_{c}(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)V_{c}(x_{2})
. This proves that
V_{c}
is concave.

Set
A=F^{\prime}(0)/(q+F^{\prime}(0))
. For a constant
C>0
to be determined, define
\bar{V}(x)=C+Ax
. First, note that, for
x>0
,

\displaystyle q\bar{V}(x)-\sup_{0\leq l\leq F(x)}\mathcal{L}_{l}[\bar{V}](x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q\bar{V}(x)-(\mu-F(x))A-F(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle qC-\mu A+qAx+F(x)(A-1)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle qC-\mu A-\tfrac{q}{q+F^{\prime}(0)}F(0),

where we used the fact that
F(x)\leq F(0)+A(q+F^{\prime}(0))x
, due to its concavity. Clearly, for
C\geq V_{c}(0)
sufficiently large, we have

(11)

q\bar{V}(x)-\sup_{0\leq l\leq F(x)}\mathcal{L}_{l}[\bar{V}](x)\geq 0,\quad x>0.

Second, choose an arbitrary
\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi_{c}^{0}
and set
\tau_{0}=\inf\left\{t>0\colon X^{\pi}_{t}=0\right\}
. Using Itô’s Formula and the fact that
\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t}=0
for all
t\in[0,\tau_{0}]
, we can write

\displaystyle\bar{V}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(q\bar{V}(X^{\pi}_{t})-(\mu-\ell_{t})\bar{V}^{\prime}(X^{\pi}_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}}\bar{V}(X^{\pi}_{\tau_{0}})\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(q\bar{V}(X^{\pi}_{t})-\sup_{0\leq l\leq F(X^{\pi}_{t})}\mathcal{L}_{l}[\bar{V}](X^{\pi}_{t})+\ell_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}}C\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}}V_{c}(0)\right],

where, in the last inequality, we used (
11
) and the fact that
C\geq V_{c}(0)
. By taking a supremum over
\pi\in\Pi_{c}^{0}
and using the DPP for
V_{c}
, we find that
V_{c}\leq\bar{V}.
∎

Proposition 5
.

The value function
V_{c}
is differentiable at
x=0
with derivative
V_{c}^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
.

Proof.

Take
x=0
and
\epsilon<\mu/q
. Choose an arbitrary
\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi_{c}^{0}
and set
\tau_{\epsilon}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{\pi}_{t}=\epsilon\}
. Since
\underline{G}^{\ell}
is continuous,
X^{\pi}
is also continuous and
X^{\pi}_{\tau_{\epsilon}}=\epsilon
.

Consider the strategy
\tilde{\pi}=(\tilde{\ell},\underline{G}^{\tilde{\ell}})\in\Pi^{0}_{c},
with
\tilde{\ell}\equiv F(\epsilon).
Since
\ell_{t}\leq F(\epsilon)
on
[0,\tau_{\epsilon}]
, Lemma
3
of Appendix
B
gives us that
X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}\leq X^{\pi}_{t}
,
\mathbb{P}_{0}
-a.s. Therefore,
\tau_{\epsilon}\leq\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}=\epsilon\}
,
\mathbb{P}_{0}
-a.s.

Then, by Proposition
2
and the DPP for
V_{c}
,

\displaystyle V_{c}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi^{0}_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\beta\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t})+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}V_{c}(\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi^{0}_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\beta\int_{0}^{\tau_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t-\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t})+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}V_{c}(\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi^{0}_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\beta\int_{0}^{\tau_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\mu\mathrm{d}t-\mathrm{d}X^{\pi}_{t})+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}V_{c}(\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi^{0}_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[-\beta\epsilon\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}+\beta\int_{0}^{\tau_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\mu-qX^{\pi}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}V_{c}(\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi^{0}_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[-\beta X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}+\beta\int_{0}^{\tau_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\mu-qX^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}V_{c}(\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi^{0}_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[-\beta X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}+\beta\int_{0}^{\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\mu-qX^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}V_{c}(\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\beta\int_{0}^{\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(F(\epsilon)\mathrm{d}t-\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{\tilde{\ell}}_{t})\right]+\sup_{\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi^{0}_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}V_{c}(\epsilon)\right].

where, in the first inequality, we used that
\beta>1
. Also, we have used that
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right]=0
(which follows from the fact that

and

Note that

0\leq\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}}V_{c}(\epsilon)\leq V_{c}(\epsilon)-(1-\mathrm{e}^{-q\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}})\min(V_{c}(\epsilon),0),\mbox{ $\mathbb{P}_{0}$-a.s.}

\displaystyle V_{c}(\epsilon)-V_{c}(0)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\beta\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{\tilde{\ell}}_{t}-F(\epsilon)\mathrm{d}t)\right]+\min(V_{c}(\epsilon),0)\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\beta\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{\tilde{\ell}}_{t}\right]+(\min(V_{c}(\epsilon),0)-\beta F(\epsilon)/q)\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\right]\right).

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{\tilde{\ell}}_{t}\right]=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{-1}u_{0,\epsilon}(0)=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{-1}\left[\frac{\Psi(0)}{\Psi(\epsilon)}\left(\overline{\Psi}(\epsilon)+\frac{\mu}{q}\right)-\left(\overline{\Psi}(0)+\frac{\mu}{q}\right)\right]=1

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{-1}\left(\min(V_{c}(\epsilon),0)-\beta\frac{F(\epsilon)}{q}\right)\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{0}[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}]\right)=\min\left(V_{c}(0)-\beta\frac{F(0)}{q},0\right)\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\Psi(\epsilon)-\Psi(0)}{\Psi(\epsilon)\epsilon}=0,

and

, have been used with

. As a result,

\displaystyle\liminf_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{V_{c}(\epsilon)-V_{c}(0)}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\beta.

By Remark

,
\displaystyle{\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{V_{c}(\epsilon)-V_{c}(0)}{\epsilon}\leq\beta}
and the result follows.
∎

Theorem 5

(Viscosity Characterization)
.

The value function
V_{c}
is the unique viscosity solution in
\mathcal{W}
of the HJB equation in (
8
) with initial condition in (
9
).

We already know, from Proposition
5
, that
V_{c}
satisfies the initial condition in (
9
). The proof that it is a viscosity solution of (
8
) is standard; see, for instance,
[
9
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
22
]
. The uniqueness in
\mathcal{W}
follows from the comparison principle in Theorem
10
of Appendix
A
.

A first consequence of the viscosity characterization is that
V_{c}
is a smooth function.

Proposition 6
.

The value function
V_{c}
is continuously differentiable on
[0,\infty)
.

A proof of this last proposition is provided in Appendix
C
.

The concavity and smoothness of
V_{c}
allow us to expect that the following (analytical) values will correspond to (probabilistic) levels for optimal capital injections and optimal dividend payments: define

a_{c}=\inf\{x\geq 0:V_{c}^{\prime}(x)<\beta\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad b_{c}=\inf\{x\geq 0:V_{c}^{\prime}(x)=1\}.

By concavity and since
V_{c}^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
, we know that
V_{c}^{\prime}(x)=\beta
for all
x\leq a_{c}
. In particular, if
a_{c}>0
, we deduce that
V_{c}(x)=V_{c}(a_{c})-\beta(a_{c}-x),
for
x<a_{c}.
In words, this means we expect that it should be optimal to inject an amount
a_{c}-x
when the process is at level
x\in(0,a_{c})
, i.e., the process jumps upward from
x
to
a_{c}
by paying
\beta(a_{c}-x)
. Intuitively, the condition
V^{\prime}(x)=\beta
tells us when to inject. On the other hand, the threshold
b_{c}
should correspond to the barrier of an optimal bang-bang dividend strategy. Indeed, concavity of
V_{c}
implies that
V_{c}^{\prime}\geq 1
on
[0,b_{c}]
and
V_{c}^{\prime}\leq 1
on
[b_{c},\infty).
The significance of this observation comes from the HJB equation (
8
), in which the supremum is attained for
l=0
when
V_{c}^{\prime}(x)>1
, and for
l=F(x)
when
V_{c}^{\prime}(x)<1
.

The next proposition is more specific about those values. In particular, it is (intuitively) coherent with Proposition
2
.

Proposition 7
.

We have
a_{c}=0
and
0<b_{c}<\infty
.

Proof.

Assume
a_{c}>0
. Since
V_{c}
is concave and such that
V^{\prime}_{c}(0+)=\beta
, we must have
V_{c}(x)=V_{c}(0)+\beta x
for all
x\leq a_{c}
. As a consequence,
V_{c}
is twice continuously differentiable on
(0,a_{c})
and, since it is a viscosity solution of the HJB equation in (
8
), it is also a classical solution on
(0,a_{c})
. Therefore, plugging this expression in the HJB equation, we find that

qV_{c}(x)-\sup_{0\leq l\leq F(x)}\mathcal{L}_{l}[V_{c}](x)=qV_{c}(0)+q\beta x-\mu\beta.

Clearly, this last expression cannot be equal to zero for all
x\in(0,a_{c})
, yielding a contradiction of the assumption that
a_{c}>0
.

Again, since
V_{c}
is concave and such that
V^{\prime}_{c}(0+)=\beta
, we have
b_{c}>0
. Now, assume
b_{c}=\infty
, i.e.,
V_{c}^{\prime}(x)>1
for all
x\geq 0.
From Proposition
4
, we know that
\frac{V_{c}(x)}{C+Ax}\leq 1
for all
x\geq 0
, where
C>0
and
A<1
. Since
V_{c}
is concave, we also have that
V_{c}(x)\geq V_{c}(0)+V^{\prime}_{c}(x)x
for all
x\geq 0
. Consequently,

1\geq\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{V_{c}(x)}{C+Ax}\geq\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{V_{c}(0)+V^{\prime}_{c}(x)x}{C+Ax}\geq\frac{1}{A}>1,

which is a contradiction.
∎

The fact that
b_{c}
lies in
(0,\infty)
splits the HJB equation, of which
V_{c}
is a viscosity solution, into the following two ODEs: on
[0,b_{c}]
,

(12)

\displaystyle qu(x)-\mu u^{\prime}(x)-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}u^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad 0<x<b_{c},

(13)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(0+)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta,

(14)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(b_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,

and, on
[b_{c},\infty)
,

(15)

\displaystyle qu(x)-(\mu-F(x))u^{\prime}(x)-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}u^{\prime\prime}(x)-F(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad b_{c}<x<\infty,

(16)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(b_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1.

These two ODEs further characterize the value function
V_{c}
as a classical solution, i.e., as a twice continuously differentiable solution, of each of these ODEs:

Theorem 6
.

The restriction of
V_{c}
to
[0,b_{c}]
is the unique classical solution in
\mathcal{W}
of (
12
)-(
13
)-(
14
), while the restriction of
V_{c}
to
[b_{c},\infty)
is the unique classical solution in
\mathcal{W}
of (
15
)-(
16
).
Furthermore, we have
\lim_{x\uparrow b_{c}}V_{c}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\lim_{x\downarrow b_{c}}V_{c}^{\prime\prime}(x)
and hence
V_{c}
is twice continuously differentiable on
[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

First, we show that each restriction is a viscosity solution of the corresponding ODE, i.e., of (
12
) and (
15
), respectively.

Let
x\in(0,b_{c}).
Following the definition of viscosity subsolutions (see Definition
1
of Appendix
A
), let
\phi\in C^{2}(0,b_{c})
be such that
\phi(x)=V_{c}(x)
and
\phi\geq V_{c}
. We need to show that
\phi
is a subsolution of (
12
). By definition of
b_{c}
and the fact that
a_{c}=0
, we have
1<V_{c}^{\prime}(x)=\phi^{\prime}(x)<\beta
. Since
V_{c}
is a viscosity subsolution of (
8
) and

0\leq\sup_{l\leq F(x)}\mathcal{L}_{l}[\phi](x)=\mu\phi^{\prime}(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\phi^{\prime\prime}(x),

then
\phi
satisfies (
12
) at the point
x
with an inequality (
\leq
) instead of an equality. This shows that
V_{c}
is a viscosity subsolution of (
12
).

Let
x\in(b_{c},\infty)
and let
\phi\in C^{2}(b_{c},\infty)
be such that
\phi(x)=V_{c}(x)
and
\phi\geq V_{c}
. As before, we need to show that
\phi
is a subsolution of (
15
). By definition of
b_{c}
, we have
V_{c}^{\prime}(x)=\phi^{\prime}(x)\leq 1
. Since
V_{c}
is a viscosity subsolution of (
8
) and

0\leq\sup_{l\leq F(x)}\mathcal{L}_{l}[\phi](x)=(\mu-F(x))\phi^{\prime}(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)+F(x),

then
\phi
satisfies (
15
) at the point
x
with an inequality (
\leq
) instead of an equality. This shows that
V_{c}
is viscosity subsolution of (
15
).

The supersolution property is proved similarly. It is also clear that
V_{c}
satisfies (
13
)-(
14
)-(
16
).

Standard results from ordinary differential equations theory imply that the solution of (
12
)-(
13
)-(
14
) (resp. (
15
)-(
16
)) is in fact unique and in
C^{2}
. Since any classical solution is also a viscosity solution, we know that
V_{c}
is
C^{2}
on
[0,b_{c})
and
(b_{c},\infty)
by uniqueness. In particular, the left and right second derivatives of
V_{c}
at the point
b_{c}
are well defined. Since
V_{c}^{\prime}(b_{c})=1
, we readily find from (
12
) and (
15
) that the left and right second derivatives are equal at
x=b_{c}
. Therefore,
V_{c}\in C^{2}[0,\infty).

∎

5.3.
Mean-reverting strategies with forced injections

From the previous section, it seems now natural to study the family of reflected mean-reverting strategies, i.e., bang-bang strategies
(\ell^{b},\underline{G}^{b})
consisting in paying out dividends at maximal rate above level
b>0
and (always) injecting at level
0
. We will compute the performance function of an arbitrary reflected mean-reverting strategy. As a by-product, an equation for a candidate optimal barrier level can be deduced. Then, in the next section, we will show that this optimal barrier level is well defined and indeed equal to
b_{c}
, and that the value function of the control problem is equal to the performance function of the corresponding reflected mean-reverting strategy. More details in Section
5.4
.

Now, let us specify the performance function of an arbitrary reflected mean-reverting strategy at level
b
: for
x\geq 0
, define

J_{c}(x;b)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}\left(F(\underline{X}^{b}_{s})\mathbf{1}_{\{\underline{X}^{b}_{s}\geq b\}}\mathrm{d}s-\beta\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{b}_{s}\right)\right].

Of course, for any fixed
b\geq 0
, we have
J_{c}(x;b)\leq V_{c}(x)
for all
x\geq 0
, which of course yields
\sup_{b\geq 0}J_{c}(x;b)\leq V_{c}(x)
for all
x\geq 0
.

Theorem 7
.

For a fixed
b>0
, we have

J_{c}(x;b)=\begin{cases}\left(C_{1}(b)-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{3}(b)\right)\Psi(x)+\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\psi(x),&\text{if $0\leq x<b$,}\\
\left(C_{2}(b)-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{4}(b)\right)\varphi(x)+I_{F}(x),&\text{if $x\geq b$,}\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle C_{1}(b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi(b)I_{F}^{\prime}(b)-\varphi^{\prime}(b)I_{F}(b)}{\varphi(b)\Psi^{\prime}(b)-\varphi^{\prime}(b)\Psi(b)},

\displaystyle C_{2}(b)=\frac{\Psi(b)I_{F}^{\prime}(b)-\Psi^{\prime}(b)I_{F}(b)}{\varphi(b)\Psi^{\prime}(b)-\varphi^{\prime}(b)\Psi(b)},

\displaystyle C_{3}(b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi^{\prime}(b)\varphi(b)-\psi(b)\varphi^{\prime}(b)}{\varphi(b)\Psi^{\prime}(b)-\varphi^{\prime}(b)\Psi(b)},

\displaystyle C_{4}(b)=\frac{\Psi(b)\psi^{\prime}(b)-\Psi^{\prime}(b)\psi(b)}{\varphi(b)\Psi^{\prime}(b)-\varphi^{\prime}(b)\Psi(b)}.

Proof.

For the purpose of this proof, we define the following two functions:

f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(\underline{X}^{b}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{\underline{X}^{b}_{t}>b\}}\mathrm{d}t\right]\quad\text{and}\quad g(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}\underline{G}^{b}_{t}\right].

First, we compute
f(x)
. For
x>0
, by the strong Markov property of
\underline{X}^{b}
and then by the fact that the processes
\underline{X}^{b}
and
X^{b}
have the same distribution up to their first passage at level
0
(with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{x}
), we can write

f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(X^{b}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{b}_{t}\geq b\}}\mathrm{d}t\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}}\right]f(0).

Note that Lemma
5
in the Appendix gives us an expression for
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}}\right]
, while the following identity has been computed in Proposition 3.2 of
[
20
]
:

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}F(X^{b}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{b}_{t}\geq b\}}\mathrm{d}t\right]=\begin{cases}\psi(x)\left(\frac{I_{F}^{\prime}(b)\varphi(b)-I_{F}(b)\varphi^{\prime}(b)}{\psi^{\prime}(b)\varphi(b)-\psi(b)\varphi^{\prime}(b)}\right)&\text{if $0\leq x\leq b$,}\\
I_{F}(x)+\varphi(x)\left(\frac{I_{F}^{\prime}(b)\psi(b)-I_{F}(b)\psi^{\prime}(b)}{\psi^{\prime}(b)\varphi(b)-\psi(b)\varphi^{\prime}(b)}\right)&\text{if $x\geq b$.}\end{cases}

Similarly, by the strong Markov property of
\underline{X}^{b}
and then by the fact that the processes
\underline{X}^{b}
and
Y
have the same distribution up to their first passage at level
b
(for example, with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{0}
), we can write

f(0)=\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau^{Y}_{b}}\right]f(b)=\frac{1}{\Psi(b)}f(b).

In conclusion, after solving for
f(b)
in the obtained expression, we get

f(x)=\begin{cases}C_{1}(b)\Psi(x),&\text{if $0\leq x<b$,}\\
I_{F}(x)+C_{2}(b)\varphi(x),&\text{if $x\geq b$.}\end{cases}

Second, we compute
g(x)
. For
0\leq x\leq b
, using Markovian arguments as above we can write

g(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{Y}_{b}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}\underline{G}_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau^{Y}_{b}}\right]g(b)=u_{0,b}(x)+\frac{\Psi(x)}{\Psi(b)}g(b).

In particular, we can compute
g^{\prime}(0+)=u_{0,b}^{\prime}(0+)=-1
since
\Psi^{\prime}(0)=0.

Moreover, for any
x\geq 0
, we have

(17)

g(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}}\right]g(0)=h(x)g(0),

in which
h
is defined in Lemma
5
of Appendix
D
and given by the expression

h(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Psi(x)-\psi(x)/C_{3}(b),&\hbox{if $0\leq x<b$,}\\
\varphi(x)C_{4}(b)/C_{3}(b),&\hbox{if $x\geq b$.}\end{array}\right.

Since
h^{\prime}(0+)
exists, we deduce from (
17
) that
-1=g^{\prime}(0+)=h^{\prime}(0+)g(0)
which implies that
g(0)=-\frac{1}{h^{\prime}(0+)}=\frac{C_{3}(b)}{\psi^{\prime}(0)}=\frac{\sigma^{2}C_{3}(b)}{2}.
The result follows.
∎

Remark 6
.

Given that
V_{c}(x)\geq J_{c}(x;b)
for any
x,b\geq 0
, it is now clear from Theorem
7
that
V_{c}(x)>-\infty
.

It is easy to verify that
J_{c}(\cdot;b)
is continuous. Also, for
x\in(0,b)
, we have

J_{c}^{\prime}(x;b)=\left(C_{1}(b)-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{3}(b)\right)\Psi^{\prime}(x)-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\psi^{\prime}(x)

and, for
x\in(b,\infty)
, we have

J_{c}^{\prime}(x;b)=C_{2}(b)\varphi^{\prime}(x)+I_{F}^{\prime}(x)-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{4}(b)\varphi^{\prime}(x).

It is also easy to verify that

C_{1}(b)\Psi^{\prime}(b)=I_{F}^{\prime}(b)+C_{2}(b)\varphi^{\prime}(b)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad C_{3}(b)\Psi^{\prime}(x)+\psi^{\prime}(x)=C_{4}(b)\varphi^{\prime}(x)

from the definitions of
C_{1}
,
C_{2},

C_{3}
and
C_{4}
. The details are left to the reader.

In particular, we have the following smooth-fit condition:

Lemma 2
.

For
b>0
, we have
J_{c}^{\prime}(b-;b)=J_{c}^{\prime}(b+;b)
and hence
J_{c}(\cdot;b)
is continuously differentiable on
[0,\infty)
.

In general,
J_{c}(\cdot;b)
is not twice continuously differentiable but it is clearly twice continuously differentiable on
(0,b)
and on
(b,\infty)
. Moreover, as
\psi^{\prime}(0)=\tfrac{2}{\sigma^{2}}
and
\Psi^{\prime}(0)=0,
we have
J_{c}^{\prime}(0+;b)=\beta
. Also, note that, as a linear combination of solutions to Equations (
3
) and (
4
),
J_{c}(\cdot;b)
is a solution to

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}u^{\prime\prime}(x)+\mu u^{\prime}(x)-qu(x)=0,\;x\in(0,b)

and

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}u^{\prime\prime}(x)+(\mu-F(x))u^{\prime}(x)-qu(x)+F(x)=0,\;x\in(b,\infty).

5.4.
The optimal reflected mean-reverting strategy

We are now ready to solve our control problem. Using the representation of the performance function
J_{c}(\cdot;b_{c})
, as given in Theorem
7
, with barrier level
b_{c}
defined in Section
5.2
, we will show it is equal to
V_{c}
.

Theorem 8
.

The optimal barrier of the mean-reverting strategy with forced injections is
b_{c}
and
J_{c}(x;b_{c})=V_{c}(x)
for all
x\geq 0
.

Proof.

One one hand, recall that
\psi
and
\Psi
are two linearly independent solutions to

qu(x)-\mu u^{\prime}(x)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}u^{\prime\prime}(x)=0,\quad x>0,

such that
\psi^{\prime}(0)=\tfrac{2}{\sigma^{2}}
and
\Psi^{\prime}(0)=0
. Recall, from Theorem
6
, that the restriction of
V_{c}
to
[0,b_{c}]
is also a (classical) solution of this ODE and that
V^{\prime}_{c}(0+)=\beta
. Consequently, for some
B\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

V_{c}(x)=B_{1}\Psi(x)+\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\psi(x)
for all
x\in[0,b_{c})
. Similarly, we have that
I_{F}\in\mathcal{W}
is a solution to

qu(x)-(\mu-F(x))u^{\prime}(x)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}u^{\prime\prime}(x)=F(x),\quad x>0,

while
\varphi\in\mathcal{W}
is a solution to the homogeneous version of this same ODE. Using again Theorem
6
, we have
V_{c}(x)=B_{2}\varphi(x)+I_{F}(x)
for all
x\in[b_{c},\infty)
, for some
B_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

On the other hand, from Theorem
7
, we have

J_{c}(x;b_{c})=\begin{cases}D_{1}\Psi(x)+\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\psi(x)&\text{if $0\leq x\leq b_{c}$,}\\
D_{2}\varphi(x)+I_{F}(x)&\text{if $x\geq b_{c}$,}\end{cases}

with
D_{1}=C_{1}(b_{c})-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{3}(b_{c})
and
D_{2}=C_{2}(b_{c})-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{4}(b_{c})
, and from Lemma
2
we have that
J_{c}(\cdot;b_{c})
is continuously differentiable. The continuity and smoothnes of
J_{c}(\cdot;b_{c})
at
x=b_{c}
means that
(D_{1},D_{2})
is a solution of

\displaystyle D_{1}\Psi(b_{c})+\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\psi(b_{c})

\displaystyle=D_{2}\varphi(b_{c})+I_{F}(b_{c}),

\displaystyle D_{1}\Psi^{\prime}(b_{c})+\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\psi^{\prime}(b_{c})

\displaystyle=D_{2}\varphi^{\prime}(b_{c})+I_{F}^{\prime}(b_{c}).

From Proposition
6
(or Theorem
6
), we know that
V_{c}
is also continuously differentiable, meaning that
(B_{1},B_{2})
is a solution of the same system of equations. We conclude that
(D_{1},D_{2})=(B_{1},B_{2})
and the result follows.
∎

Corollary 1
.

The optimal barrier level
b_{c}
is characterized by the following supercontact condition:

J_{c}^{\prime\prime}(b_{c}-;b_{c})=J_{c}^{\prime\prime}(b_{c}+;b_{c})

or, equivalently, by

J_{c}^{\prime}(b_{c}-;b_{c})=1=J_{c}^{\prime}(b_{c}+;b_{c}).

In particular,
b_{c}
satisfies

(18)

\frac{1-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\psi^{\prime}(b_{c})}{\Psi^{\prime}(b_{c})}=C_{1}(b_{c})-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{3}(b_{c})

and

(19)

\frac{1-I_{F}^{\prime}(b_{c})}{\varphi^{\prime}(b_{c})}=C_{2}(b_{c})-\beta\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{4}(b_{c}).

Theorem
2
then follows from the above corollary and from Theorem
8
.

6.
Solution of the main optimization problem

In this section, we provide a solution to the main optimization problem. Recall first that, by definition of each value function, we have

V(x)\geq\max\left(V_{d}(x),V_{c}(x)\right),

for all
x\geq 0
. In what follows, we give a proof of Theorem
3
by proving the reverse inequality. It is based on repeated applications of the comparison principle in Theorem
10
of Appendix
A
.

Let us recall the HJB equation given by (
8
): for
x>0
,

\displaystyle qu(x)-\sup_{0\leq l\leq F(x)}\mathcal{L}_{l}[u](x)=0.

Note that
V_{d}
is, in particular, a viscosity solution in
\mathcal{W}
of this HJB equation with initial condition
V_{d}(0)=0
, while
V_{c}
is a viscosity solution in
\mathcal{W}
with initial condition
V_{c}^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
(cf. Theorem
5
). In order to compare the three value functions
V
,
V_{d}
and
V_{c}
using the above comparison principle, we must have that
V
is also a viscosity solution of this HJB equation with its own initial condition.

Theorem 9

(Viscosity Characterization)
.

The value function
V
is a continuous viscosity solution in
\mathcal{W}
of (
8
) with initial condition

(20)

\displaystyle\min\left(V(0),\beta-V^{\prime}(0+)\right)=0.

Proof.

The proof that
V
is a viscosity solution of (
8
) is classical and omitted. Applying the argument in Remark
5
to
V
, we get that, for all
x\geq 0
and
\epsilon>0
,

0\leq\frac{V(x+\epsilon)-V(x)}{\epsilon}\leq\beta.

Therefore,
V
is continuous and, moreover, we have

(21)

\limsup_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{V(x+\epsilon)-V(x)}{\epsilon}\leq\beta

To prove that
V\in\mathcal{W}
, we will proceed as in the proof of Proposition
4
. Define
\bar{V}(x)=V(0)+\frac{\mu\beta}{q}+\beta x
. First, note that, for
x>0
,

(22)

q\bar{V}(x)-\sup_{0\leq l\leq F(x)}\mathcal{L}_{l}[\bar{V}](x)=qV(0)+q\beta x+F(x)(\beta-1)\geq 0.

Second, choose an arbitrary
\pi=(\ell,\underline{G}^{\ell})\in\Pi^{0}
and set
\tau_{0}=\inf\left\{t>0\colon X^{\pi}_{t}=0\right\}
. Using Itô’s Formula and the fact that
\underline{G}^{\ell}_{t}=0
for all
t\in[0,\tau_{0}]
, we can write

\displaystyle\bar{V}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(q\bar{V}(X^{\pi}_{t})-(\mu-\ell_{t})\bar{V}^{\prime}(X^{\pi}_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}}\bar{V}(0)\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\left(q\bar{V}(X^{\pi}_{t})-\sup_{0\leq l\leq F(X^{\pi}_{t})}\mathcal{L}_{l}[\bar{V}](X^{\pi}_{t})+\ell_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}}\bar{V}(0)\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\ell_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}}V(0)\right],

where in the last inequality we used (
22
) and the fact that
\bar{V}(0)\geq V(0)
. By taking a supremum over all
\pi\in\Pi^{0}
, and using the DPP for
V
, we find that
V\leq\bar{V}.
This proves that
V\in\mathcal{W}
.

All is left to prove is that
V
satisfies the initial condition in (
20
). Note that
V(0)\geq 0
. First, if
V(0)=0
, then by the Comparison Principle (see Theorem
10
in Appendix
A
), we know that
V=V_{d}
. Indeed,
V_{d}
is a viscosity solution of the HJB equation and, by definition,
V_{d}\leq V
. Also, in this case, we have that
V^{\prime}(0+)=V_{d}(0+)
exists and is such that
V^{\prime}(0+)\leq\beta
by (
21
). In conclusion, if
V(0)=0
, then
\min\left(V(0),\beta-V^{\prime}(0+)\right)=0
. Second, if
V(0)>0
, then to prove
V^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
, in view of (
21
), it suffices to show that

\liminf_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{V(\epsilon)-V(0)}{\epsilon}\geq\beta.

We want to argue by contradiction that for all
\eta>0
there exists
\epsilon
such that

V(\epsilon)\leq V(0)+(\beta-\eta)\epsilon.

Fix
\eta>0
and take
\epsilon<\min(V(0)/\eta,1)
. For
\pi\in\Pi^{0}
, define
\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{\pi}_{t}=\epsilon\}
. By the DPP for
V
(see Proposition
3
) and Proposition
2
,

\displaystyle V(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi^{0}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}\wedge\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}(\ell_{s}\mathrm{d}s-\beta\mathrm{d}G_{s})+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}<T^{\pi}\}}V(\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi^{0}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}\wedge\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}\beta(\ell_{s}\mathrm{d}s-\mathrm{d}G_{s})+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}<T^{\pi}\}}(V(0)+(\beta-\eta)\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle V(0)-\eta\epsilon+\beta\sup_{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi^{0}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}\wedge\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}(\ell_{s}\mathrm{d}s-\mathrm{d}G_{s})+\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}}X^{\pi}_{\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}\wedge T^{\pi}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(0)-\eta\epsilon+\beta\sup_{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi^{0}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}\wedge\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qs}(\mu-qX^{\pi}_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle V(0)-\eta\epsilon+\frac{\beta\mu}{q}\sup_{\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi^{0}}\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q(T^{\pi}\wedge\tau_{\epsilon}^{\pi})}\right]\right).

Set
\tilde{\pi}=(F(1),\tilde{G}^{0})\in\Pi_{c}
, i.e., the strategy with constant dividend rate
F(1)
and forced injections at zero, and set
\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}=\epsilon\}
. Let
\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi^{0}
. Since
\ell_{t}\leq F(1)
for all
t\leq T^{\pi}\wedge\tau^{\pi}_{\epsilon}
, we have that
X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}\leq X^{\pi}_{t}
for all
t\leq T^{\pi}\wedge\tau^{\pi}_{\epsilon}
.

Furthermore, if
\tau^{\pi}_{\epsilon}<T^{\pi}
then
\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}\geq\tau^{\pi}_{\epsilon}
, while if
T^{\pi}<\tau^{\pi}_{\epsilon}
then
\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}\geq T^{\pi}
. Therefore,
\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}\geq\tau^{\pi}_{\epsilon}\wedge T^{\pi}
. As a result,

V(0)\leq V(0)-\eta\epsilon+\frac{\beta\mu}{q}\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\right]\right)=V(0)-\eta\epsilon+\frac{\beta\mu}{q}\left(1-\frac{\tilde{\Psi}(0)}{\tilde{\Psi}(\epsilon)}\right)

in which
\tilde{\Psi}
is defined as
\Psi
but with
\mu
replaced by
\mu-F(1)
. This implies that

\eta\leq\frac{\beta\mu}{q\epsilon}\left(1-\tilde{\Psi}(0)/\tilde{\Psi}(\epsilon)\right)\xrightarrow[\epsilon\to 0]{}\frac{\beta\mu}{q}\tilde{\Psi}^{\prime}(0)=0,

which is a contradiction with
\eta>0
.
∎

6.1.
Proof of Theorem
3

Taking into account the solution of the problem without injections in Section
4
and the solution of the problem with forced injections in Section
5
, to complete the proof of Theorem
3
, we only need to prove that:

(23)

V=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}V_{d},&\hbox{if $V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)\leq\beta$,}\\
V_{c},&\hbox{if $V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)>\beta$,}\end{array}\right.

or, equivalently,

(24)

V=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}V_{d},&\hbox{if $V_{c}(0)<0$,}\\
V_{c},&\hbox{if $V_{c}(0)\geq 0.$}\end{array}\right.

Before proceeding with the proofs of (
23
) and (
24
), recall that
V
,
V_{d}
and
V_{c}
are all viscosity solutions of the same HJB equation, albeit with different initial conditions. This fact will be used repeatedly, together with the Comparison Principle (see Theorem
10
in Appendix
A
), in what follows.

Recall that, by definition of the control problem, we have
V(0)\geq 0
. As an intermediate step, we first prove that

(25)

V=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}V_{d},&\hbox{if $V(0)=0$,}\\
V_{c},&\hbox{if $V(0)>0$.}\end{array}\right.

First, in the proof of Theorem
9
, it was already shown that if
V(0)=0
then
V=V_{d}
. It was also shown that, if
V(0)>0
then
V^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
. Recall, from Theorem
5
, that
V_{c}
is a viscosity supersolution of (
8
) with initial condition
V_{c}^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
. Consequently, since
V
is a viscosity subsolution of (
8
) (cf. Theorem
9
), using the Comparison Principe, we can conclude that
V_{c}\geq V
and hence
V=V_{c}
. This completes the proof of (
25
).

Now, to prove the dichotomy in (
23
), we need to show that, if
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)\leq\beta
, then
V_{d}\geq V
, while if
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)>\beta
, then
V_{c}\geq V
. We proceed as follows:

(a)

Assume
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)>\beta
. By the dichotomy in (
25
), if
V(0)=0
then
V=V_{d}
, meaning that
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)=V^{\prime}(0+)>\beta
. This last inequality is in contradiction with (
20
), the initial condition satisfied by
V
, when
V(0)=0
. So, if
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)>\beta
then
V(0)>0
and, by the dichotomy in (
25
), we have
V=V_{c}
.

(b)

Assume
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)\leq\beta
. Again, if
V(0)=0
, then
V=V_{d}
. Otherwise, if
V(0)>0
, then by (
20
), the initial condition satisfied by
V
, we must have
V^{\prime}(0+)=\beta\geq V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)
. Then, by the Comparison Principle, we have
V_{d}\geq V
.

Similarly, to prove the dichotomy in (
24
), we proceed as follows:

(a)

Assume
V_{c}(0)<0
. Again, if
V(0)=0
, then
V=V_{d}
. If
V(0)>0
, then by (
25
) we have
V=V_{c}
and thus
V(0)=V_{c}(0)<0
, which is a contradiction.

(b)

Assume
V_{c}(0)\geq 0.
If
V(0)=0
, then by the Comparison Principle we have
V\leq V_{c}
. Instead, if
V(0)>0
, then by (
25
) we have
V=V_{c}
.

Note that, using the Comparison Principle, we can conclude that if
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
then
V_{c}=V_{d}
, while if
V_{c}(0)=0
then
V_{d}=V_{c}
. With the above results, this means that if
V_{d}^{\prime}(0+)=\beta
or
V_{c}(0)=0
, then
V=V_{d}=V_{c}
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
3
.

Appendix A
A primer on viscosity solutions and the comparison principle

The theory of viscosity solutions allows us to characterize a value function as the solution of the HJB equation even though it is not known a priori that this function is differentiable. The definition of a viscosity solution of the HJB equation (
8
) is as follows:

Definition 1
.

A continuous function
u
is a viscosity supersolution (resp. subsolution) of (
8
) if for all
x\in(0,\infty)
, and for all
\phi\in C^{2}[0,\infty)
for which
\phi(x)=u(x)
and
u\geq\phi
(resp.
u\leq\phi
), we have

(26)

\displaystyle q\phi(x)-\sup_{0\leq l\leq F(x)}\mathcal{L}_{l}[\phi](x)\geq 0\mbox{ (resp.\ $\leq 0$)}.

The function
u
is a viscosity solution of (
8
) if it is both a supersolution and subsolution of (
8
).

A powerful tool in the theory of viscosity solutions is the comparison principle, which allows the comparison of a supersolution and a subsolution of the HJB equation by only comparing their value (or derivative) at the boundary of the domain. Comparison principles are often used to show uniqueness of the solution given specific initial conditions. Indeed, if
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are two solutions of (
8
) with the same initial condition than they are equal as they are both super and subsolutions. On the other hand, we use the following comparison principle extensively in Section
6
to prove the dichotomy result by comparing the value functions
V_{c}
and
V_{d}
with the value function
V
.

Theorem 10

(Comparison Principle)
.

If
V_{1}\in\mathcal{W}
(resp.
V_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
) is a viscosity supersolution (resp. subsolution) of (
8
) and if one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(1)

V_{1}(0)\geq V_{2}(0)
;

(2)

V^{\prime}_{1}(0+)\leq V^{\prime}_{2}(0+)
, assuming both derivatives exist,

then
V_{1}\geq V_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
0<\lambda<1,

A_{1}<V_{1}^{\prime}(0+)
,
A_{2}>0
and
A_{0}>V_{1}(0)
large enough that
V_{3}(x):=A_{0}+A_{1}x+A_{2}x^{2}
is a strict supersolution of (
8
). It is then clear that
V_{1}^{\lambda}:=(1-\lambda)V_{1}+\lambda V_{3}
is a strict viscosity supersolution of (
8
). Define

u_{\lambda}(x)=V_{2}(x)-V_{1}^{\lambda}(x).

To prove that
V_{1}\geq V_{2}
it suffices to prove that
u_{\lambda}\leq 0
for all
\lambda.
To prove this, we use a typical argument and assume by contradiction that
M:=\sup_{x}u_{\lambda}(x)>0.
Since
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
, we know that
\lim_{x\to\infty}u_{\lambda}(x)=-\infty.
Therefore, there exists
x_{0}\geq 0
such that
M=u_{\lambda}(x_{0}).
If
V_{1}(0)\geq V_{2}(0)
, then
u_{\lambda}(0)<0
, therefore
x_{0}>0.
On the other hand, if Condition (2) of the theorem is satisfied, then
u_{\lambda}^{\prime}(0+)=V_{2}^{\prime}(0+)-(1-\lambda)V_{1}^{\prime}(0+)-\lambda A_{1}\geq\lambda(V_{1}^{\prime}(0+)-A_{1})>0.
Consequently,
x_{0}>0
.

For
\epsilon>0
, let
\Phi^{\epsilon}(x,y)=V_{2}(x)-V_{1}^{\lambda}(y)-\phi_{\epsilon}(x,y)
with

\phi_{\epsilon}(x,y)=\frac{1}{4}|x-x_{0}|^{4}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|x-y|^{2}.

For each
\epsilon>0,
the function
\Phi^{\epsilon}
attains its maximum
M_{\epsilon}
at some point
(x_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})\in(0,\infty)^{2}.
The rest of the proof is classical and consists in showing that
(x_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})
converges to
(x_{0},x_{0})
as
\epsilon\to 0,
and

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{|x_{\epsilon}-y_{\epsilon}|}{\epsilon}=0,

in order to apply Theorem 3.2 of
[
12
]
to obtain a contradiction. See for example
[
10
]
, and
[
23
]
for the absolutely continuous control case.
∎

Appendix B
Auxiliary results for reflected SDEs

Here are two technical lemmas.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\pi=(\ell,G)\in\Pi
and consider
\tilde{\pi}=(\tilde{\ell},\tilde{G})\in\Pi^{0}
such that
\tilde{\ell}\geq\ell
and
\tilde{G}_{t}=\underline{G}^{\tilde{\ell}}_{t\wedge T^{\pi}}
.

If
X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{0}=X^{\pi}_{0}
, then
T^{\tilde{\pi}}=T^{\pi}
and
X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}\leq X^{\pi}_{t}
for all
t\leq T^{\pi}.
In particular, if
\tilde{\ell}=\ell
, then
\tilde{G}_{t}\leq G_{t}
for all
t\leq T^{\pi}
and

\int_{0}^{T^{\tilde{\pi}}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}\tilde{G}_{t}\leq\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}G_{t}.

Proof.

Set
Y_{t}=X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}-X^{\pi}_{t}
, which is such that
\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=(\ell_{t}-\tilde{\ell}_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\mathrm{d}G_{t}+\mathrm{d}\tilde{G}_{t}
. Note that, for
t\leq T^{\pi}
, if
Y_{t}>0
then
X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}>0
. As a result,
\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}>0\}}\mathrm{d}\tilde{G}_{t}=0
. Since
Y
has paths of finite variation and
\tilde{G}
has continuous paths, we can use the Change of Variables formula (see Theorem II.31 in
[
24
]
) and write, for
t<T^{\pi}
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\max(0,Y_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\max(0,Y_{s})\left((\ell_{s}-\tilde{\ell}_{s})\mathrm{d}s-\mathrm{d}G_{s}+\mathrm{d}\tilde{G}_{s}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s\leq t}(\max(0,Y_{s-}-(G_{s}-G_{s-}))^{2}-\max(0,Y_{s-})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{s}>0\}}\left(-\mathrm{d}G_{s}+\mathrm{d}\tilde{G}_{s}\right)=-\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{s}>0\}}\mathrm{d}G_{s}\leq 0,

where we used that
G_{t}\geq G_{t-}
for all
t\geq 0
. Consequently, we have
Y_{t}\leq 0
, which means that
X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}\leq X^{\pi}_{t}
for all
t\leq T^{\pi}
, and hence
T^{\tilde{\pi}}\leq T^{\pi}
. But, since by definition
\tilde{G}_{t}=\underline{G}^{\tilde{\ell}}_{t}
for all
t\leq T^{\pi}
, which implies that
X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}\geq 0
for all
t\leq T^{\pi}
, we also have that
T^{\tilde{\pi}}\geq T^{\pi}
.

Now, if
\tilde{\ell}=\ell
, then it is clear that
X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{t}\leq X^{\pi}_{t}
implies
\tilde{G}_{t}\leq G_{t}
for
t\leq T^{\pi}
. Consequently, by integration by parts, we have

\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}(\tilde{G}_{t}-G_{t})=\mathrm{e}^{-qT^{\pi}}(\tilde{G}_{T^{\pi}}-G_{T^{\pi}})+q\int_{0}^{T^{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}(\tilde{G}_{t}-G_{t})\mathrm{d}t\leq 0.

∎

Lemma 4
.

For
b>0
and
x\geq 0
, there exist an adapted nonnegative process
\underline{X}^{b}
such that

\mathrm{d}\underline{X}^{b}_{t}=\left(\mu-F(\underline{X}^{b}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{\underline{X}^{b}_{t}\geq b\}}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}G_{t},\quad\underline{X}^{b}_{0}=x,

and an adapted nondecreasing process
G
such that

G_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{\underline{X}^{b}_{s}=0\}}\mathrm{d}G_{s}.

Proof.

Set
H(y):=\mu-F(y)\mathbf{1}_{\{y\geq b\}}
. Recall that
F:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a continuously differentiable, nondecreasing and concave function, therefore a Lipschitz function. Recall also that
X_{t}=x+\mu t+\sigma B_{t}
.

Set
T_{0}=0
and let
X^{0}
be the solution to the following SDE on
[0,\infty)
:

\mathrm{d}X^{0}_{t}=H(X^{0}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad X^{0}_{0}=x.

This process is indeed the same as the controlled process associated with a mean-reverting strategy at level
b
, but we use a different notation only for the purpose of this proof.

Set
G^{0}\equiv 0
and
T_{1}=\inf\{t\geq T_{0}:X^{0}_{t}=b\}
. By induction, for
n\geq 1
:

•

if
n
is odd: for
t\geq T_{n}
, define
G^{n}_{t}=-\min_{T_{n}\leq s\leq t}((X_{s}-X_{T_{n}})\wedge 0)
and
X^{n}_{t}=X_{t}-X_{T_{n}}+G^{n}_{t}
. Set
T_{n+1}=\inf\{t\geq T_{n}:X^{n}_{t}=b\}
.

•

if
n
is even: for
t\geq T_{n}
, define
X^{n}
as the solution of the following SDE:

\mathrm{d}X^{n}_{t}=H(X^{n}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad X^{n}_{T_{n}}=b.

Also, set
G^{n}\equiv 0
and
T_{n+1}=\inf\{t\geq T_{n}:X^{n}_{t}=0\}
.

Note that, for
n\geq 1
odd, the pair
(X^{n},G^{n})
is the solution to the following Skorohod problem on
[T_{n},T_{n+1})
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{n}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}G^{n}_{t},\quad X^{n}_{T_{n}}=0,

\displaystyle G^{n}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{T_{n}}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{n}_{s}=0\}}\mathrm{d}G^{n}_{s}.

Finally, define

\underline{X}^{b}_{t}=\sum_{n\geq 0}X^{n}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{n}\leq t<T_{n+1}\}}\quad\text{and}\quad G_{t}=\sum_{n\geq 0}G^{n}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{t\leq T_{n+1}\}}.

The pair
(\underline{X}^{b},G)
satisfies the conditions in the statement of the lemma. The details are left to the reader.
∎

Since

V_{c}^{{}^{\prime},-},V_{c}^{{}^{\prime},+}

(a)

and

such that

Appendix D
A first-passage identity

Here is a solution to a first-passage problem for the refracted diffusion process
X^{b}
. We use the notation introduced in Section
3
.

Lemma 5
.

For a fixed
b\geq 0
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}}\right]=\begin{cases}\Psi(x)+\psi(x)\left[\frac{\Psi(b)\varphi^{\prime}(b)-\Psi^{\prime}(b)\varphi(b)}{\psi^{\prime}(b)\varphi(b)-\psi(b)\varphi^{\prime}(b)}\right]&\text{if $0\leq x\leq b$,}\\
\varphi(x)\left[\frac{\Psi(b)\psi^{\prime}(b)-\Psi^{\prime}(b)\psi(b)}{\psi^{\prime}(b)\varphi(b)-\psi(b)\varphi^{\prime}(b)}\right]&\text{if $x\geq b$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Define
h(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}}\right]
. For
0\leq x\leq b
, the process
\left\{X^{b}_{t},0\leq t\leq\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}\wedge\tau_{b}^{X^{b}}\right\}
has the same distribution (with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{x}
) as
\left\{X_{t},0\leq t\leq\tau_{0}^{X}\wedge\tau_{b}^{X}\right\}
. Consequently, using the strong Markov property and the almost-sure continuity of trajectories, we can write

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}<\tau_{b}^{X^{b}}\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{b}^{X^{b}}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{b}^{X^{b}}<\tau_{0}^{X^{b}}\}}\right]h(b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{X}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{0}^{X}<\tau_{b}^{X}\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{b}^{X}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{b}^{X}<\tau_{0}^{X}\}}\right]h(b)

\displaystyle=\left(\Psi(x)-\frac{\Psi(b)}{\psi(b)}\psi(x)\right)+\frac{\psi(x)}{\psi(b)}h(b),

where, in the last equality, we used two well-known fluctuation identities for a Brownian motion with drift; see, e.g.,
[
8
]
.

For
x\geq b
, the process
\left\{X^{b}_{t},0\leq t\leq\tau_{b}^{X^{b}}\right\}
has the same distribution (with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{x}
) as
\left\{X^{0}_{t},0\leq t\leq\tau_{b}^{X^{0}}\right\}
. Consequently, using the strong Markov property and the almost-sure continuity of trajectories, we can write

h(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{b}^{X^{0}}}\right]h(b)=\frac{\varphi(x)}{\varphi(b)}h(b).

where, in the last equality, we used a known fluctuation identity for the process
X^{0}
; see, e.g.,
[
20
]
.

To compute
h(b)
, we will use the following approximating sequence. This methodology has been used several times in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
20
]
. For
n\geq 1
and initial value
x<b
, let
X^{b,n}
have the same dynamics as that of
X
, until it reaches level
b
. Then, its dynamics change to that of
X^{0}
, until it goes back to level
b-1/n
, in which case its dynamics goes back to that of
X
. This procedure is repeated until level
0
is attained.

Define
h_{n}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{n}}\right]
, where
\tau^{n}_{0}=\inf\left\{t>0\colon X^{b,n}_{t}=0\right\}
. Using arguments as above (such as the Markov property and almost-sure continuity of trajectories), we can write

h_{n}(b-1/n)=\mathbb{E}_{b-1/n}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{0}^{X}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{0}^{X}<\tau_{b}^{X}\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{b-1/n}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{b}^{X}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{b}^{X}<\tau_{0}^{X}\}}\right]h_{n}(b),

where
h_{n}(b)=\mathbb{E}_{b}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-q\tau_{b-1/n}^{X^{0}}}\right]h_{n}(b-1/n)
. Replacing, solving for
h_{n}(b-1/n)
and then computing the expectations, we get

h_{n}(b-1/n)=\frac{\Psi(b-1/n)-\frac{\Psi(b)}{\psi(b)}\psi(b-1/n)}{1-\frac{\psi(b-1/n)}{\psi(b)}\frac{\varphi(b)}{\varphi(b-1/n)}}.

Taking the limit, we further get

h(b)=\frac{-\Psi^{\prime}(b)+\frac{\Psi(b)}{\psi(b)}\psi^{\prime}(b)}{\frac{\psi^{\prime}(b)}{\psi(b)}-\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(b)}{\varphi(b)}}

and the result follows.
∎
