Local Near-Field Scattering Data Enables Unique Reconstruction of Rough Electric Potentials
Manuel Cañizares

Abstract
. The focus of this paper is the study of the inverse point-source scattering problem, specifically in relation to a certain class of electric potentials. Our research provides a novel uniqueness result for the inverse problem with local data, obtained from the near field pattern. Our work improves the work of Caro and Garcia, who investigated both the direct problem and the inverse problem with global near field data for critically singular and δ \delta -shell potentials. The primary contribution of our research is the introduction of a Runge approximation result for the near field data on the scattering problem which, in combination with an interior regularity argument, enables us to establish a uniqueness result for the inverse problem with local data. Additionaly, we manage to consider a slightly wider class of potentials.



1.
Introduction

Along the paper, we will consider real potentials in dimension
d\geq 3
that can be written as

(1)

V=V^{0}+\gamma^{s}+\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,

where
V^{0}\in L^{d/2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}),

\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
denotes the surface measure of a compact hypersurface
\Gamma
which is locally described by the graph of Lipschitz functions,
\alpha\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma;\mathbb{R})
and
\gamma^{s}
is of the form

\gamma^{s}=\chi^{2}\,D^{s}g,

for some
s<1
,
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
, and
\chi\,\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};[0,1])
is a cut-off function. Here
D^{s}
denotes the Riesz derivative, defined as
\widehat{D^{s}f}(\xi)=|\xi|^{s}\widehat{f}(\xi)
. The supports of
V^{0}
and
\chi
will be assumed to be compact.
In essence, this means that the whole potential
V
is compactly supported.
Note that the novel potentials that we introduce in this work are just those of the class of
\gamma^{s}
, while
V^{0}
and
\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
are those introduced by Caro and García in
[
4
]
, which they call
critically singular
and
\delta
-shell
potentials, respectively.

The direct problem consists in finding the wave scattered by the potential, when an incident wave is emitted at fixed energy by a point source away from its support. Mathematically, this translates to finding
u_{sc}
solving

(S)

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta+\lambda-V\right)u_{sc}(\centerdot\,,y)=Vu_{in}(\centerdot\,,y)&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u_{sc}(\centerdot\,,y)\textrm{ satisfying SRC},\end{cases}

Here, SRC stands for Sommerfeld Radiation Condition. A function
u
is said to satisfy SRC if

(2)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|x|^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\left(\frac{x}{|x|}\cdot\nabla u(x)-i\lambda^{1/2}u(x)\right)=0

uniformly in every direction, and
u_{in}(x,y)=\Phi_{\lambda}(x-y)
denotes the incident wave emitted from the point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,V
, where
\Phi_{\lambda}
represents the fundamental solution for the Helmholtz equation with SRC, which solves the distributional problem:

(F)

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta+\lambda\right)\Phi_{\lambda}=\delta_{0}&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\Phi_{\lambda}\textrm{ satisfying SRC}.\end{cases}

The SRC is classically introduced so that the solution to the Helmholtz equation is unique and physically corresponds to a radiating wave
[
26
]
.

In section
2
, we will arrive to the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
V
is of the form (
1
). Then, there exists
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(V,d)
such that, for every
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, there is an unique solution
u_{sc}(\centerdot\,,y)\in X_{\lambda}^{*}
to the problem (
S
) for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,V
.

Moreover, the mapping
Vu_{in}(\centerdot\,,y)\mapsto u_{sc}(\centerdot\,,y)
is bounded from
X_{\lambda}
to
X_{\lambda}^{*}
.

The spaces
X_{\lambda}
and
X_{\lambda}^{*}
are introduced in
[
4
]
and their definition will be given at the beginning of section 2. The elements in the space
X_{\lambda}^{*}
can be thought of as functions with one derivative in
L^{2}
that exhibit a certain degree of integrability in frequences
|\xi|\sim\lambda^{1/2}
, and
X_{\lambda}
will be the pre-dual of
X_{\lambda}^{*}
. To prove the theorem, we will follow the argument in
[
4
]
and make use of many of their results. In Caro and Garcia’s work, the first step was to deal with the critically singular part of the potential by obtaining an inverse for the operator
\Delta+\lambda-V^{0}
via a Neumann-series approach.

If we denote the inverse above by
(\Delta+\lambda+i0-V^{0})^{-1}
, it can be applied to both sides of (
S
) to turn the problem into finding the inverse of the operator

I-(\Delta+\lambda+i0-V^{0})^{-1}\circ(\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\gamma^{s}).

The appropiate framework to study this operator is the Fredholm theory, due to the fact that the operator defined as multiplication by
(\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\gamma^{s})
is compact from
X_{\lambda}^{*}
to
X_{\lambda}
. Fredholm alternative assures the existence of such an inverse as long as the operator
I-(\Delta+\lambda+i0-V^{0})^{-1}\circ(\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\gamma^{s})
is injective. Proving this injectivity will be equivalent to obtaining uniqueness for the problem

(H)

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta+\lambda-V\right)u=0&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u\textrm{ satisfying SRC}.\end{cases}

The fundamental ingredient here is an unique continuation argument for the operator
\Delta+\lambda-V
. In our case, it will be based on a Carleman estimate proved by Caro and Rogers in
[
5
]
for a modified family of Bourgain-type spaces. They were themselves based on those introduced by Haberman and Tataru in
[
14
]
to study the Calderón problem. As we will note below, this Carleman estimate will prove to be crucial for the solution of the inverse problem.

Caro and Garcia applied this framework to invert the operator when when
\alpha
is in
L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. We noticed that there was a possibility to consider a wider range of potentials here. The idea is that
\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
acts as multiplication operator from
H^{s}(\Omega)
to
H^{-s}_{0}(\Omega)
with
s<1
, where
\Omega
is any domain containing the support of
V
. Then, the fact that
X_{\lambda}^{*}
is related to
H^{1}
, along with
\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
being compactly supported, and the fact
H^{1}(\Omega)
is compactly embedded in
H^{s}(\Omega)
, give the necessary ideas to prove compactness of the multiplication operator. In principle, one could hope to consider
\alpha\in L^{r}(\Gamma)
, with
r>d-1
, since
\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
would still behave as a compact multiplication operator form
H^{1}(\Omega)
to
H^{-1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Nonetheless, the Carleman estimate doesn’t seem to work in that case, and therefore we did not manage to relax this condition in the solution of the inverse problem.

On the other hand, we attempted to consider distributions of the form
D^{s}g
, with
s<1
and
g\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, hoping to find a class of such distributions that generalized those of the form
\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
. However, in principle, the set of indexes
(s,p)
that made the Carleman estimate work for these distributions did not allow them to
see
the hypersurface
\Gamma
. Therefore, we opted to consider potentials that could be decomposed as a sum of those of both classes.

The arguments to find the scattering solution via Fredholm theory can be found in section
2
. Here, the structure is akin to that in
[
4
]
, but some proofs have to be redone in order for them to work for our wider class of potentials.

Afterwards, in sections
3
and
4
, we will devote ourselves to proving the following theorem concerning uniqueness with partial data for the inverse problem.

Theorem 2
.

Consider
d\geq 3
. Let
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
be two relatively open sets of dimension
d-1
, separated from
\mathrm{supp}\,V
, and that can be expressed as the graph of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions, and let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be two potentials of the form described in (
1
). Let also
u_{sc,1}
and
u_{sc_{2}}
be the scattering solutions to the problem (
S
) with respective potentials
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.
Then, there exists
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(V,d)
such that, for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
except for at most a countable set, it holds that

{\left.\kern-1.2ptu_{sc,1}\right|_{\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2}}}={\left.\kern-1.2ptu_{sc,2}\right|_{\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2}}}\implies V_{1}=V_{2}.

From a physics perspective, we are stating that the identifiability of the potential is possible by placing sources and detectors only in small pieces of hypersurfaces away from the support of
V
. These sources will be emitting monochromatic waves with a fixed energy
\lambda
. Note that
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
could well be the same set, they could be different but intersecting, or be completely separate and non-intersecting.

We will prove this theorem via an orthogonality relation in the spirit of Alessandrini’s identity for the Calderón problem
[
2
]
and the construction of CGO solutions as in
[
28
,
5
,
16
]
. This orthogonality relation will be proven in section
3
. The main ingredient will be a Runge approximation result, indexed as proposition
3.4
, given in section
3.1
. We will take a bounded open domain
\Omega
, of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, whose boundary contains both
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, and the Runge approximation will allow us to approximate solutions in
\Omega
by single layer potentials with densities that are supported in any subset
\Sigma\subset\partial\Omega
:

\mathcal{S}f(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f(y)\,u_{to}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
f\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega)
,
\mathrm{supp}\,f\subset\Sigma
. Here the total wave is defined as
u_{to}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=u_{in}+u_{sc}
.
The result will give an approximation in the
L^{2}
norm of a smaller open domain
\Omega^{\prime}
strictly contained in
\Omega
and containing the support of
V
. The proof of this lemma was inspired by that of Isakov for the Calderón problem for
C^{2}
conductivities
[
19
]
and that of Harrach, Pohjola and Salo for the recovery of an
L^{\infty}
scattering coefficient function in the Helmholtz equation
[
15
]
. Nonetheless, the argument is slightly different, since we consider approximating solutions of a different kind. Also, there are some technicalities concerning the singularities of the fundamental solutions, which have to be treated with a little bit of care. This argument will allow us to extend an orthogonality relation of the type

(3)

\braket{(V_{1}-V_{2})v_{1},v_{2}}=0,

with
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
being solutions that only see
\Sigma_{j}
, to an orthogonality relation of this type for all solutions to the equation.

However, the approximation in
L^{2}
won’t be enough, due to the low integrability of our potentials. We will in fact need to approximate our solutions in an
H^{1}
norm so that the solutions can be integrated against the potential. Therefore, we will provide in lemma
3.5
with an interior regularity result. In particular, for any
u
solution of (
S
), we will obtain

\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})},

as long as
\Omega
is a domain strictly contained in
\Omega^{\prime}
and also containing the support of
V
. We will prove this inequality following an argument by Chen that appears in
[
6
]
, who proved
H^{2}
regularity for second order elliptic equations with a degree of regularity that in our case would ask for
V\in L^{\infty}
. We adapt the argument to work for our kind of potentials, which can be done thanks to the fact that
\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\gamma^{s}
acts as a bilinear form over
H^{s}
, which is an interpolation space between
L^{2}
and
H^{1}
.

Then, the obtention of the final orthogonality relation will be given in section
3.2
.

Remark
.

It might be interesting to note that, in our case, the only sets that are given by the problem and that have a clear physical meaning are the supports of the potentials and the measuring sets
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
. We will construct
ad hoc
the domains in which we obtain the orthogonality relation (
3
). To make the arguments work, we need the frequency
\lambda
not to be a Neumann eigenvalue for these domains. Since the set of Neuman eigenvalues in each domain is countable, we can already assert that the proofs go through for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}

except for at most a countable set
, as in the statement of Theorem
2
. In
[
27
]
, Stefanov used the monotonicity of Dirichlet eigenvalues with respect to domain inclusion to prove that, for any value of
\lambda
, it is possible to construct a domain such that
\lambda
is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue. However, this monotonicity doesn’t seem to hold for Neumann eigenvalues for the Laplacian
[
11
]
, and we have no reason to think that it holds for
-\Delta+V
. Because of this, we have not been able to prove the existence of an appropiate domain for every value of
\lambda
. Nonetheless, given the amount of freedom that one has when choosing the domain, it would be reasonable to expect that such a domain exists. The analyis of this question is left for a future work.

Finally, to end the proof of Theorem
2
, we will test the aforementioned identity with a special type of solutions, the so-called complex geometric optics (CGO) solutions. This is a classical method that goes back to Sylvester and Uhlmann’s work
[
28
]
in the Calderón problem. In our case, these special solutions are of the form

v_{j}(x)=e^{\zeta_{j}\cdot x}(1+w_{j}(x)),

where
\zeta_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
are chosen such that
\zeta_{j}\cdot\zeta_{j}=-\lambda
and
\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}=-i\kappa
for an arbitray
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
-which is possible in dimension
d\geq 3
- and the correction term
w_{j}
vanishes in a certain sense when
|\zeta_{j}|
grows. To prove the existence of these solutions, we will follow the construction of both Caro and García in
[
4
]
and Caro and Rogers in
[
5
]
. The key ingredient again will be the aforementioned Carleman estimate. In particular, applying the operator
(\Delta+\lambda-V)
to
v_{j}
yields

(\Delta+2\zeta_{j}\cdot\nabla-V)w_{j}=V.

Therefore, to construct the CGO solutions it is enough to prove injectivity of the adjoint operator
(\Delta-2\zeta_{j}\cdot\nabla-V)
, which can be done via a priori estimates. We will define a family of Bourgain spaces
X_{\zeta}^{s}
via the norm

\lVert u\rVert_{X_{\zeta}^{s}}=\lVert(M|\Re(\zeta)|^{2}+M^{-1}|p_{\zeta}|^{2})^{s/2}\widehat{u}\rVert_{L^{2}}

with
M>1
, where
\Re
denotes the real part and

p_{\zeta}(\xi)=-|\xi|^{2}+2i\zeta\cdot\xi+\zeta\cdot\zeta.

The index
s={1/2}
will play an important role. Realising that the dual of
X_{\zeta}^{s}
is
X_{-\zeta}^{-s}
, if we want to prove the existence of solutions in
X_{\zeta}^{1/2}
with potentials
V\in X_{\zeta}^{-{1/2}}
, we will need to prove the injectivity of the operator from
X_{-\zeta}^{-{1/2}}
to
X_{-\zeta}^{1/2}
. Therefore, the a priori estimate that we will seek will be of the form

\lVert u\rVert_{X_{-\zeta}^{1/2}}\lesssim\lVert(\Delta-2\zeta\cdot\nabla-V)\,u\rVert_{X_{-\zeta}^{-{1/2}}}

This will be done in section
4
.

2.
Direct Scattering

In this section, we follow the strategy by Caro and García to solve the direct problem for the point-source scattering. As the name of the problem suggests, we consider an incident wave emitted by a point source at fixed energy
\lambda>0
. This means that the spatial part of the incident wave emitted at a point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and measured at another point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
will be given by
u_{in}(x,y)=\Phi_{\lambda}(x-y)
, where
\Phi_{\lambda}
is the radiating fundamental equation to the Helmholtz equation, i.e.
\Phi_{\lambda}
is the solution to the problem

(4)

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta+\lambda\right)\Phi_{\lambda}=\delta_{0}&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\Phi_{\lambda}\textrm{ satisfying SRC}.\end{cases}

We denote by
\left(\Delta+\lambda+i0\right)^{-1}
the solution operator for the Helmholtz equation with SRC (
2
). Under this notation, we can write
\Phi_{\lambda}=\left(\Delta+\lambda+i0\right)^{-1}\delta_{0}
, which for instance can be expressed in terms of the Fourier symbol for the operator
\Delta+\lambda
. If we denote the modulus of this symbol by
m_{\lambda}(\xi)=|\lambda-|\xi|^{2}|
, it can be checked that the fundamental solution
\Phi_{\lambda}
is given, in the distributional sense, as

(5)

\braket{\Phi_{\lambda},f}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\left[\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{m_{\lambda}>\varepsilon}\frac{\widehat{f}(\xi)}{\lambda-|\xi|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi-i\frac{\pi}{2\lambda^{1/2}}\int_{S_{\lambda}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}S_{\lambda}(\xi)\right],

for
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Here,
S_{\lambda}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,|\xi|=\lambda^{1/2}\}
is the critical hypersurface of the symbol
m_{\lambda}
, and
\,\mathrm{d}S_{\lambda}
denotes its volume form.

2.1.
The free resolvent and Neumann series

As mentioned in the introduction, the scattering solution is constructed in certain spaces
X_{\lambda}^{*}
that were considered by Caro and García in
[
4
]
. In those spaces, refinements of estimates by Agmon and Hörmander
[
1
]
, by Kenig, Ruiz and Sogge
[
22
]
and by Ruiz and Vega
[
25
]
give us a good estimate for the free resolvent
(\Delta+\lambda+i0)^{-1}
. We are going to recall the definition of these spaces. For this, we need to construct a partition of unity.
Indeed, choose a function
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported in
\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,|\xi|\leq 2\}
such that
\phi(\xi)=1
whenever
|\xi|\leq 1
and define, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\psi_{k}(\xi)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\phi(2^{-k}\xi)-\phi(2^{-k+1}\xi)
. Note that
\psi_{k}
is supported in
{\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,2^{k-1}\leq|\xi|\leq 2^{k+1}\}}
and
{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi_{k}(\xi)=1}
for
\xi\neq 0
. We define the Littlewood-Paley projectors as

(6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{P_{k}f}(\xi)&\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\psi_{k}(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi),\\
\widehat{P_{\leq k}f}(\xi)&\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{j\leq k}\widehat{P_{j}f}(\xi)=\phi(2^{-k}\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi).\end{split}

Now, for
\lambda>0
, let
k_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
2^{k_{\lambda}-1}<\lambda^{1/2}\leq 2^{k_{\lambda}}
. Then, the set of indices in the projectors above that
see
the critical frequencies is

I=\{k_{\lambda}-2,k_{\lambda}-1,k_{\lambda},k_{\lambda}+1\}.

For simplicity, we will call
P_{<I}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=P_{\leq k_{\lambda}-3}
. Define now the space
B
and its dual
B^{*}
, as in
[
1
]
, via the norms

\lVert f\rVert_{B}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\,\left(2^{j/2}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(D_{j})}\right),\qquad\lVert u\rVert_{B^{*}}=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\,\left(2^{-j/2}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(D_{j})}\right),

with
D_{j}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,2^{j-1}<|x|\leq 2^{j}\}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
D_{0}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,|x|\leq 1\}.
From now on, denote by
q_{d}
the end-point index for the Stein-Tomas trace theorem,
2/q_{d}=(d-1)/(d+1)
, by
p_{d}
the end-point index for the
\dot{H}^{1}
-Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev embedding theorem,
1/p_{d}=1/2-1/d
for
d\geq 3
, and by
q^{\prime}_{d}
and
p^{\prime}_{d}
their respective Hölder conjugates. The space
X_{\lambda}
can be defined as the sum of two spaces,
Y_{\lambda}
and
Z_{\lambda}
, which are defined as elements in
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with norms

\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{Y_{\lambda}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lVert m_{\lambda}^{-{1/2}}\widehat{P_{<I}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sum_{k\in I}\lambda^{-{1/2}}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert^{2}_{B}+\sum_{k>k_{\lambda}+1}\lVert m_{\lambda}^{-{1/2}}\widehat{P_{k}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}},

and

\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{Z_{\lambda}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lVert m_{\lambda}^{-{1/2}}\widehat{P_{<I}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sum_{k\in I}\lambda^{d(\frac{1}{q_{d}^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{p_{d}^{\prime}})}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert^{2}_{L^{q_{d}^{\prime}}}+\sum_{k>k_{\lambda}+1}\lVert m_{\lambda}^{-{1/2}}\widehat{P_{k}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}.

The norm in
X_{\lambda}
will be the usual for the sum of normed spaces:

\lVert f\rVert_{X_{\lambda}}=\inf_{g+h=f}\{\lVert g\rVert_{Y_{\lambda}}+\lVert h\rVert_{Z_{\lambda}}\}.

The spaces above have respective dual spaces
Y_{\lambda}^{*}
and
Z_{\lambda}^{*}
defined by the norms

\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{Y^{*}_{\lambda}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lVert m_{\lambda}^{{1/2}}\widehat{P_{<I}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sum_{k\in I}\lambda^{{1/2}}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert^{2}_{B^{*}}+\sum_{k>k_{\lambda}+1}\lVert m_{\lambda}^{{1/2}}\widehat{P_{k}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}},

and

\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{Z^{*}_{\lambda}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lVert m_{\lambda}^{{1/2}}\widehat{P_{<I}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sum_{k\in I}\lambda^{d(\frac{1}{q_{d}}-\frac{1}{p_{d}})}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert^{2}_{L^{q_{d}}}+\sum_{k>k_{\lambda}+1}\lVert m_{\lambda}^{{1/2}}\widehat{P_{k}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Then, the space
X^{*}_{\lambda}
can be defined as the dual space of
X_{\lambda}
, which will be isomorphic to the intersection of
Y_{\lambda}^{*}
and
Z_{\lambda}^{*}
, with norm

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{X^{*}_{\lambda}}=

\displaystyle\lVert m_{\lambda}^{{1/2}}\widehat{P_{<I}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\sum_{k\in I}\left(\lambda^{{1/2}}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert^{2}_{B^{*}}+\lambda^{d(\frac{1}{q_{d}}-\frac{1}{p_{d}})}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert^{2}_{L^{q_{d}}}\right)+

\displaystyle\sum_{k>k_{\lambda}+1}\lVert m_{\lambda}^{{1/2}}\widehat{P_{k}f}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\sim\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{Y^{*}_{\lambda}}+\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{Z^{*}_{\lambda}}.

It will be interesting to note that the Schwartz class is dense in all the above spaces with respect to their corresponding norms
[
4
]
. We will now obtain an estimate for the solution operator
X_{\lambda}
and
X_{\lambda}^{*}
. However, let us first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Define
D^{s}
as the Fourier multiplier with symbol
|\xi|^{s}
. Then, it holds that

\lVert D^{s}P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}\sim 2^{ks}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}.

Proof:
.

Fix
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We have, by definition,

\widehat{D^{s}P_{k}f}(\xi)=|\xi|^{s}\psi_{k}(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)=2^{ks}\left(\frac{|\xi|}{2^{k}}\right)^{s}\psi\left(\frac{\xi}{2^{k}}\right)\widehat{f}(\xi).

If we take
\chi
to be a smooth cut-off function such that
0\leq\chi\leq 1
,
\chi=0
around the origin and
\chi=1
on
\mathrm{supp}\,\psi
, then

\widehat{D^{s}P_{k}f}(\xi)=2^{ks}\left(\frac{|\xi|}{2^{k}}\right)^{s}\chi\left(\frac{\xi}{2^{k}}\right)\psi\left(\frac{\xi}{2^{k}}\right)\widehat{f}(\xi).

Note that the function
\rho(\xi)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=|\xi|^{s}\chi(\xi)
is compactly supported away from
0
and therefore
\rho\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any value of
s
. Now,

\widehat{D^{s}P_{k}f}(\xi)=2^{ks}\rho\left(\frac{\xi}{2^{k}}\right)\widehat{P_{k}f}(\xi),

and thus

D^{s}P_{k}f\sim 2^{ks}\left[(P_{k}f)\ast 2^{kd}\check{\rho}(2^{k}\centerdot)\right].

Therefore, by Young’s inequality,

\lVert D^{s}P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}\lesssim 2^{ks}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}\lVert 2^{kd}\check{\rho}(2^{k}\centerdot)\rVert_{L^{1}}=2^{ks}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}\lVert\check{\rho}\rVert_{L^{1}}\lesssim 2^{ks}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}.

Now, to get the reverse inequality, observe that

\widehat{P_{k}f}(\xi)=|\xi|^{-s}|\xi|^{s}\widehat{P_{k}f}(\xi)=\mathcal{F}[D^{-s}D^{s}P_{k}f](\xi),

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform. Therefore,

\lVert P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}\sim\lVert D^{-s}D^{s}P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}\lesssim 2^{-ks}\lVert D^{s}P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}},

and thus

2^{ks}\lVert P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}\lesssim\lVert D^{s}P_{k}f\rVert_{L^{p}}.

∎

We are ready now for the desired estimate.

Theorem 2.2
.

There exists a constant

On the other hand, Theorem 3.1 in
[

Proposition 2.4

\displaystyle\lVert u_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus I}\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq

Proposition 3.1
.

Consider
d\geq 3
. Let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be two potentials of the form (
1
), and let
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
be two relatively open sets of dimension
d-1
, separated from
\mathrm{supp}\,V
, and that can be expressed as the graph of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions. Choose a bounded open domain
\Omega
of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
such that
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\subset\partial\Omega
,
\mathrm{supp}\,V_{j}\subset\Omega
,
j=1,2

Then, there exists
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(V,d)
such that, for every
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
except for at most a countable set, it holds that

{\left.\kern-1.2ptu_{sc,1}\right|_{\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2}}}={\left.\kern-1.2ptu_{sc,2}\right|_{\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2}}}\implies\braket{(V_{1}-V_{2})v_{1},v_{2}}=0,

\text{for all }v_{1},v_{2}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\textrm{ such that }\left(\Delta+\lambda-V_{j}\right)v_{j}=0\textrm{ in }\Omega.

We will divide the proof of this proposition in two parts. We will prove that the orthogonality relation is fullfiled for solutions that can be represented as single layer potentials with densities supported in
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
respectively, to then extend it to every solution by approximating them by these single layer potentials. We will start by providing with a Runge approximation result, that allows us to do this approximation in a
L^{2}
norm. The ideas for the proof take inspiration from
[
19
]
and
[
15
]
. However, due to the low-regularity of the potential
V
, we need an approximation in a space of higher regularity. Lemma
3.5
below will provide us with an estimate to make the approximation in a
H^{1}
norm. In this section, we will constantly refer to appendix
A
. There, we remember some classical results regarding layer potentials and their use in solving the Neumann problem for the Helmholtz equation, which is a key ingredient in our proof or the Runge approximation.

3.1.
Runge approximation

We will start the section by providing with two technical lemmas that make the proof of the Runge approximation more readable. The notation
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
will mean here and throughout the paper that
\overline{\Omega}^{\prime}\subset\Omega
. Also, note that, given the nature of
\gamma^{s}
, the integration has to be understood at times as a duality pairing.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
V
be as in (
1
). Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be two open bounded domains of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
such that
\mathrm{supp}\,V\subset\Omega^{\prime}
, and let
v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be also supported in
\Omega^{\prime}
. Suppose that
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is a solution to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\;(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,\varphi=v&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\;\partial_{\nu}\varphi=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Then, for every
y\in\partial\Omega
, it holds that

(17)

\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)=-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\left(\varphi(x)\,V(x)+v(x)\right)u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proof:
.

This lemma boils down to a careful integration by parts, analysing the singularity of
u_{in}(x,y)
when
x=y
.
Let
B_{y,\varepsilon}
be the ball of radius
\varepsilon>0
centered in
y\in\partial\Omega
. In appendix
A
we give the following expression:

(18)

\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)=G(y,x)|x-y|^{2-d},

with
G
a bounded function on
\partial\Omega\times\partial\Omega
. This means that
|\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}|
is a weakly singular kernel of order
d-2
, which in turn ensures the absolute integrability of
\varphi(\centerdot)\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(\centerdot\,,y)
in
\partial\Omega
(see lemma
A.2
in Appendix
A
). Therefore, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem and Green’s formula, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}(1-\mathbbm{1}_{B_{y,\varepsilon}}(x))\,\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\partial\left(\Omega\setminus B_{y,\varepsilon}\right)}\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\int_{(\partial B_{y,\varepsilon})\cap\Omega}\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)\right),

First, we will see that the second term is negligible. Indeed, we can use again the limiting expression for
\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}
in (
18
), that is
|\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)|\lesssim|x-y|^{2-d}
when
x
approaches
y
. Changing to polar coordinates centered in
y
we obtain, for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small,

(19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\int_{(\partial B_{y,\varepsilon})\cap\Omega}\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)\right|\leq&\,\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\cap}}|\varphi(\varepsilon,\theta)|\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)\\
\lesssim&\,\varepsilon\sup_{x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}}|\varphi(x)|\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0,\end{split}

where we have denoted by
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\cap}
the relevant half sphere in the change of variables, and we have used the fact that, since
\varphi
solves
(\Delta+\lambda)\varphi=0
in
\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
, its restriction to that domain is smooth by Theorem 11.1.1 in
[
17
]
, and therefore bounded.
Next, we can use integration by parts in
\Omega\setminus B_{y,\varepsilon}
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\left(\Omega\setminus B_{y,\varepsilon}\right)}\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{y,\varepsilon}}\varphi(x)\left(\Delta_{x}+\lambda-V(x)\right)u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{y,\varepsilon}}\left(\Delta+\lambda-V(x)\right)\varphi(x)\,u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\left(\Omega\setminus B_{y,\varepsilon}\right)}\partial_{\nu}\varphi(x)\,u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x).

On the one hand, since
\partial_{\nu}\varphi=0
on
\partial\Omega
, we have that

\int_{\partial\left(\Omega\setminus B_{y,\varepsilon}\right)}\partial_{\nu}\varphi(x)\,u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)=\int_{(\partial B_{y,\varepsilon})\cap\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\varphi(x)\,u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x).

Now, observing that the limiting expression for
u_{in}
is

u_{in}(x,y)=F(y,x)|x-y|^{2-d},

with
F
a bounded function on
\partial\Omega\times\partial\Omega
(again refer to appendix
A
), we can do an identical argument as in (
19
) to obtain that

\left|\int_{(\partial B_{y,\varepsilon})\cap\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\varphi(x)\,u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x).\right|\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0.

On the other hand, we have that
\left(\Delta_{x}+\lambda\right)u_{in}(x,y)=0
for
x\neq y
, while

{\left(\Delta+\lambda-V(x)\right)\varphi(x)=v(x)}
.
Therefore, since both
V
and
v
are supported in
\Omega^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\left(-\varphi(x)\,V(x)-v(x)\right)u_{in}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x.

However, the integral in the limit does not depend on
\varepsilon
in any way, and the lemma is proved.
∎

Remember now that total wave is
u_{to}=u_{in}+u_{sc}
, and consider the single layer potential with density
f\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega)
as defined by (see Appendix
A
for details)

(20)

\left(\mathcal{S}f\right)(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f(y)\,u_{to}(x,y)\,\,\mathrm{d}S(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and the normal derivative operator as

(21)

\left(\mathcal{N}f\right)(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f(y)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{to}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y),\quad x\in\partial\Omega.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
V
be as in (
1
). Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be two open bounded domains of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
such that
\mathrm{supp}\,V\subset\Omega^{\prime}
, and let

also supported in
\Omega^{\prime}
. Suppose
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is a solution to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\;(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,\varphi=v&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\;\partial_{\nu}\varphi=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Then, it holds that

\int_{\partial\Omega}(\mathcal{N}f)(x)\,\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)=-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\mathcal{S}f)(x)\,v(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proof:
.

First note that scattering part
u_{sc}
solves
\left(\Delta+\lambda\right)u_{sc}(\centerdot\,,y)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,V
as long as
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,V
, so it is smooth away from the potential, by Theorem 11.1.1 in
[
17
]
, and in particular it is smooth on
\partial\Omega
. On the other hand, expression (
18
) means that
|\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}|
is a weakly singular kernel of order
d-2
, which in turn ensures the absolute integrability of
\varphi(\centerdot)f(\circ)\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(\centerdot\,,\circ)
, and allows to use Fubini’s Theorem (see lemma
A.2
in Appendix
A
). Therefore,

(22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\mathcal{N}f)(x)\,\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)&=\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x)\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}f(y)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{to}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y)\right)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)\\
&=\int_{\partial\Omega}f(y)\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{to}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)\right)\,\mathrm{d}S(y).\end{split}

Now, for any
y\in\partial\Omega
we obtain, by integrating by parts in
\Omega
with respect to
x
,

(23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x)\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{sc}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)=&\int_{\Omega}\varphi(x)\left(\Delta_{x}+\lambda-V(x)\right)u_{sc}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&-\int_{\Omega}(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,\varphi(x)\,u_{sc}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\varphi(x)\,u_{sc}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)\\
=&\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\left(\varphi(x)\,V(x)\,u_{in}(x,y)-v(x)\,u_{sc}(x,y)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

In the last identity we have used that
\left(\Delta_{x}+\lambda-V(x)\right)u_{sc}(\centerdot\,,y)=Vu_{in}(\centerdot\,,y)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and also that
(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,\varphi=v
in
\Omega
and
\partial_{\nu}\varphi=0
on
\partial\Omega
, as well as the fact that both
V
and
v
are supported in
\Omega^{\prime}
. Joining (
23
) above with (
17
) in lemma
3.2
yields

\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x)\,\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{to}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)=-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}v(x)\,u_{to}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Applying this identity in (
22
) and using Fubini’s Theorem again yields

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}(\mathcal{N}f)(x)\,\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}v(x)\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}f(y)\,u_{to}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}v(x)\mathcal{(}Sf)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

∎

Note that the single-layer potential
\mathcal{S}f

\displaystyle X^{\Sigma}=\left\{u=\mathcal{S}f\in H^{1}(\Omega)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\;f\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega),\;\mathrm{supp}\,f\subset\Sigma\right\}

\displaystyle X^{\Sigma}_{\Omega^{\prime}}=\left\{{\left.\kern-1.2ptu\right|_{\Omega^{\prime}}}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,u\in X^{\Sigma}\right\}\subset H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})

Proposition 3.4

(Runge approximation)
.

Let
V
a potential of the form (
1
). Let
\Sigma
be a relatively open set of dimension
d-1
, separated from
\mathrm{supp}\,V
, and that can be expressed as the graph of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions.

Choose a bounded open domain
\Omega
of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
such that
\Sigma\subset\partial\Omega
and
\mathrm{supp}\,V\subset\Omega
, and let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be a smooth domain such that
\mathrm{supp}\,V\subset\Omega^{\prime}
, and such that
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}
is connected.

Then, there exists
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(V,d)
such that, for every
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
except for at most a countable set,
X^{\Sigma}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
is dense in
X_{\Omega^{\prime}}
under the
L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
norm, this is, any function in
X_{\Omega^{\prime}}
can be approximated by functions in
X^{\Sigma}_{\Omega^{\prime}}
in this norm.

Proof:
.

We will show that
X_{\Omega^{\prime}}\subset{({X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}})^{\perp}}^{\perp}=\overline{X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}}
, where the orthogonal complement and closure are taken with respect to the
L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
inner product. Indeed, let
v\in L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
, and let
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})

such that

(24)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\;(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,\varphi=E_{0}v&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\;\partial_{\nu}\varphi=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
E_{0}v
denotes the extension by
0
of
v
to
\Omega
. This
\varphi
will be guaranteed to exist as long as
\lambda
is not a Neumann eigenvalue for the operator
-\Delta+V
in
\Omega
. The set of these eigenvalues is a countable set. For a proof of these claims, see appendix
A
. Now, we have that

\displaystyle v\in({X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}})^{\perp}\iff 0=\braket{r_{\Omega^{\prime}}u,v}_{\Omega^{\prime}}=\braket{u,E_{0}v}_{\Omega}=\braket{u,(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,\varphi}_{\Omega},\quad\forall u\in X^{\Sigma},

Applying Green’s identity, and the fact that
(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,u=0
in
\Omega
if
u\in X^{\Sigma}
,

\displaystyle v\in({X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}})^{\perp}\iff 0=

\displaystyle\braket{u,(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,\varphi}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\braket{(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,u,\varphi}_{\Omega}+\braket{u,\partial_{\nu}\varphi}_{\partial\Omega}-\braket{\partial_{\nu}u,\varphi}_{\partial\Omega},\quad\forall u\in X^{\Sigma}.

\displaystyle\iff 0=

\displaystyle\braket{\partial_{\nu}u,\varphi}_{\partial\Omega},\quad\forall u\in X^{\Sigma}.

Now, since
u=\mathcal{S}f
, we have that (see lemma
A.3
), for
x\in\partial\Omega
,

\partial_{\nu}u(x)=\frac{1}{2}f(x)+\left(\mathcal{N}f\right)(x),

with the operator
\mathcal{N}
as defined in (
21
).
Therefore,

v\in({X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}})^{\perp}\iff 0=\frac{1}{2}\braket{f,\varphi}_{\partial\Omega}+\braket{\mathcal{N}f,\varphi}_{\partial\Omega},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega)\,\text{ s.th. }\mathrm{supp}\,f\subset\Sigma.\\

Now, by lemma
3.3
, we have that, for
f\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega)
supported on
\Sigma
,

\braket{\mathcal{N}f,\varphi}_{\partial\Omega}=-\braket{\mathcal{S}f,v}_{\Omega^{\prime}}=0,

since
v\in({X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}})^{\perp}
by assumption and
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{S}f\right|_{\Omega^{\prime}}}\in X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}
by definition. This yields

v\in({X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}})^{\perp}\iff 0=\braket{f,\varphi}_{\partial\Omega},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega)\,\text{ s.th. }\mathrm{supp}\,f\subset\Sigma.\\

Consequently,

(25)

v\in({X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}})^{\perp}\iff\varphi=0\quad\textrm{on }\Sigma

Then,
\varphi
solves

\begin{cases}\;(\Delta+\lambda)\,\varphi=0&\text{in }\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\prime}},\\
\;\partial_{\nu}\varphi=\varphi=0&\text{on }\Sigma,\end{cases}

which, by the Unique Continuation property for the operator
(\Delta-\lambda)

[
18
]
, implies that
\varphi=0
in
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}.
Also, since
\varphi
solves
(\Delta+\lambda)\varphi=v
in
\Omega\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,V
, its restriction to this set belongs to
H^{2}(\Omega\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,V)
which means that
\varphi=\partial_{\nu}\varphi=0
on
{\partial\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,V}
.

Finally, let
w\in X_{\Omega^{\prime}}
. We want to show that
w\in{\left(({X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}})^{\perp}\right)}^{\perp}
. Indeed, let
{v\in({X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}})^{\perp}}
and
\varphi
as in (
24
). Then, using Green’s Identity again,

\displaystyle\braket{w,v}_{\Omega^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\braket{w,(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,\varphi}_{\Omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\braket{(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,w,\varphi}_{\Omega^{\prime}}+\braket{w,\partial_{\nu}\varphi}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}-\braket{\partial_{\nu}w,\varphi}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\braket{w,\partial_{\nu}\varphi}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}-\braket{\partial_{\nu}w,\varphi}_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}=0,

where we have used that
(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,w=0
in
\Omega^{\prime}
. This proves that
X_{\Omega^{\prime}}\subset\overline{X_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\Sigma}}
, which ends the proof.
∎

As we commented above, we need to make the approximation of proposition
3.4
in a stronger norm than
L^{2}
. This will be clear later in the proof of proposition
3.1
, but the main reason is that the potential
V
doesn’t act as a bilinear operator over
L^{2}
, but over
H^{1}
. The next lemma is an interior regularity result, also called Caccioppoli inequality in the literature
[
12
]
. It will allow us to get
H^{1}
convergence in a smaller set than the one in which we have proven the
L^{2}
approximation. Both the statement and the proof of the lemma are inspired by
[
6
]
. However, due to the nature of our potential
V
, we will obtain less regularity, and we will need to use both Sobolev embeddings and interpolation of Sobolev spaces to complete the proof.

Lemma 3.5

(Interior regularity)
.

Consider
d\geq 3
. Let V be a potential of the form (
1
). Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
be two Lipschitz open domains such that
\mathrm{supp}\,V\subset\Omega^{\prime}
. Then, if
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
solves

(26)

(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,u=0\quad\textrm{in }\Omega,

then it holds that

\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})}\lesssim\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Proof:
.

We will assume that
u
is a real function. For a complex
u
, both its real and imaginary parts are real functions that satisfy (
26
), since
V
is real, thus the result will follow.

The weak formulation of equation (
26
) is

(27)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\phi=\int_{\Omega}(V-\lambda)\,u\,\varphi,\quad\forall\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

Consider now a real cut-off function
\eta
between
\Omega^{\prime}
and
\partial\Omega
, namely,
\eta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
such that
\eta\equiv 1
in
\Omega^{\prime}
and
0\leq\eta\leq 1
in
\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
. Consider then the test function
\varphi=\eta^{2}u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. With this test function, eq. (
27
) becomes

\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\eta^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\,2\eta\nabla\eta\,u=\int_{\Omega}(V-\lambda)\,u^{2}\eta^{2}.

Therefore, since
\mathrm{supp}\,V\subset\Omega^{\prime}
,

(28)

\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\eta^{2}\leq\left|\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\,2\eta\,u\nabla\eta\right|+{\left|\int_{\Omega}\lambda\,u^{2}\eta^{2}\right|}+\left|\int_{\Omega}V\,u^{2}\right|.

Now, let’s analyze these three summands separately. For the second one we have, since
0\leq\eta\leq 1
,

(29)

\left|\int_{\Omega}\lambda\,u^{2}\eta^{2}\right|\leq\lambda\int_{\Omega}u^{2}=\lambda\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

For the first one, set any
0<\varepsilon<1
. By Young’s inequality for products, we have that
2ab\leq\varepsilon a^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}b^{2}
for any two
a,b\geq 0
. This, along with Cauchy-Schwartz inequality gives

(30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\,2\eta\,u\nabla\eta\right|\leq&\,\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\eta^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\eta\right|^{2}u^{2}\\
\leq&\,\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\eta^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\lVert\nabla\eta\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

Finally, in the last summand of (
28
) we will analyze
V^{0}
and
V^{F}=\alpha\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\gamma^{s}
separately:

\left|\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}V\,u^{2}\right|\leq\left|\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}V^{0}u^{2}\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}V^{F}u^{2}\right|.

To estimate the second term, use (
13
) in the proof of proposition
2.7
. Indeed, there exists
K>0
such that

\left|\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}V^{F}u^{2}\right|\leq K\left(\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\lVert\alpha\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\right)\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s}(\Omega^{\prime})}^{2}.

Now, note that in a domain with smooth boundary, the fractional order Sobolev space
H^{s}(\Omega^{\prime})
is equivalent to the interpolation space between
L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
and
H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})
with interpolation index
s
(see, for example,
[
29
]
), that is

\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{H^{s}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq\lVert u\rVert^{2(1-s)}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}\lVert u\rVert^{2s}_{H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})}.

Also, if we take
\delta>0
, using again Young’s inequality for products, we have that
ab\leq\delta^{-p}\frac{a^{p}}{p}+\delta^{p^{\prime}}\frac{b^{p^{\prime}}}{p^{\prime}}
, for any
a,b\geq 0
and
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. Choosing
p=\frac{1}{1-s}
,
p^{\prime}=\frac{1}{s}
gives

\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{H^{s}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq(1-s)\delta^{-\frac{1}{1-s}}\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}+s\delta^{1/s}\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})}.

Therefore, since
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, we have that

(31)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}V^{F}u^{2}\right|\leq K\left(\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\lVert\alpha\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\right)\left[(1-s)\delta^{-\frac{1}{1-s}}\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+s\delta^{1/s}\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})}\right].

Now, to estimate the
V^{0}
term, let
N>0
and consider the set

F=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,|V^{0}(x)|>N\},

and define
E=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus F
. Then,

(61)

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta+\lambda-V\right)v=0&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}v=g&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

This can be done by taking a function
w
that satisties the equation

(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,w=f.

This function can be constructed in
X_{\lambda}^{*}
, for instance, by using the techniques in section
2
after observing that, if
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, then its extension by
0
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is in
X_{\lambda}
.

Also, since
\mathrm{supp}\,f\subset\Omega
, we have by Theorem 11.1.1 in
[
17
]
that
w
is smooth near
\partial\Omega
. Therefore, we can define its outward normal derivative in
\partial\Omega
, which will belong to
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
.
Now, if we can solve problem (
61
), set
g=\partial_{\nu}w
, and denote by
v
the solution to this problem. Then, it is easy to check that
u={\left.\kern-1.2ptw\right|_{\Omega}}-v
solves the problem (
59
).

Note that
{\left.\kern-1.2ptw\right|_{\Omega}}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
by proposition
2.4
, so we will only need to prove that
v
belongs to
H^{1}(\Omega)
to conclude that
u
belongs too.

In this case it will be useful to think of the fundamental solution for the operator
\Delta+\lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
\Phi_{\lambda}
, not in the distributional sense as in (
5
), but as a Hankel function. In fact,
\Phi_{\lambda}
will take the form

(62)

\Phi_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{i}{4}\left(\frac{\lambda^{1/2}}{2\pi|x|}\right)^{d/2-1}H_{d/2-1}^{(1)}\left(\lambda^{1/2}|x|\right),

where
H_{\nu}^{(1)}
denotes the Hankel function of the first kind (or Bessel function of the third kind). If we define
u_{in}(x,y)=\Phi_{\lambda}(x-y)
and recall the limiting properties of the Hankel functions, it’s relatively easy to check that

(63)

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{in}(x,y)&=F(x,y)|x-y|^{2-d},\\
\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)&=G(x,y)|x-y|^{2-d},\\
\partial_{\nu_{y}}u_{in}(x,y)&=\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(y,x)=G(y,x)|x-y|^{2-d},\end{split}

with
F
and
G
being two bounded functions on
\partial\Omega\times\partial\Omega
. Then,
u_{in}
,
\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}
and
\partial_{\nu_{y}}u_{in}
are, by definition,
weakly singular kernels
of order
d-2
on
\partial\Omega\times\partial\Omega
. The following lemma is a combination of those in
[
10
]
, chapter 3B, and is an important piece to solve the problem (
61
).

Lemma A.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

If we denote by
T
the integral operator over
\partial\Omega
defined by a weakly singular kernel
K
of order
\alpha
on
\partial\Omega\times\partial\Omega
, with
0<\alpha<d-1
, as

\left(Tf\right)(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}T(x,y)\,f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y),

then the following statements hold:

(1)

T
is compact on
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
,

(2)

T
transforms bounded functions into continuous functions, and

(3)

if
f\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and
f+Tf\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega)
, then
f\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega)
.

Take now
u_{to}=u_{in}+u_{sc}
, where
u_{sc}
is the scattering solution defined in (
S
) and constructed in section
2
, and define for
f
continuous on
\partial\Omega
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial\Omega
the
single layer potential
with moment
f
as

\left(\mathcal{S}f\right)(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{to}(x,y)\,f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y),

and the
double layer potential
as

\left(\mathcal{D}f\right)(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu_{y}}u_{to}(x,y)\,f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y),.

Define further the operator
\mathcal{N}
, which is the adjoint of
\mathcal{D}
over
\partial\Omega
, as

\left(\mathcal{N}f\right)(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{to}(x,y)\,f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

that must be understood as an improper integral.
Now, we have the following lemmas, which are similar to classical results as in
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
8
]
for the Helmholtz and Laplace equations. We denote by
u_{+}
the trace on
\partial\Omega
of
{\left.\kern-1.2ptu\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}}}
, and by
u_{-}
the trace on
\partial\Omega
of
{\left.\kern-1.2ptu\right|_{\Omega}}
. As well, we denote by
\partial_{\nu}u_{+}
and
\partial_{\nu}u_{-}
the normal derivative of those, always with respect to the outward-pointing normal vector of
\partial\Omega
(as seen from inside
\Omega
).

Lemma A.3
.

Consider
d\geq 3
. Lef
f\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega)
. Then, the single layer potential
u=\mathcal{S}f
is continuous throughout
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and we have the limiting values

(64)

\partial_{\nu}u_{\pm}(x)=\left(\mathcal{N}f\right)(x)\mp\frac{1}{2}f(x),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

where the integral exists as an improper integral.
Consequently, we have the jump relation
\partial_{\nu}u_{-}-\partial_{\nu}u_{+}=f
on
\partial\Omega
. Furthermore,
u
it is a solution in
H^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial\Omega
and fullfils SRC (
2
).

Proof:
.

First, note that the single layer potential for the homogeneuos Helmholtz equation

v(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{in}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y)

can be extended to the boundary, is a solution in
H^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\left(\Delta+\lambda\right)v=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial\Omega
, fullfils SRC (
2
) and has boundary values

\partial_{\nu}v_{\pm}(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{in}(x,y)\,f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y)\mp\frac{1}{2}f(x),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

which is a classical result, see for example
[
9
]
.
Now, define

w(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{sc}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y).

To see that
w
is in
H^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, take
K\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
compact, and observe that, by proposition
2.4
and Theorem
1
,

\lVert w\rVert_{H^{1}(K)}\lesssim\sup_{y\in\partial\Omega}\lVert u_{sc}({\centerdot\,,y})\rVert_{H^{1}(K)}\lesssim\sup_{y\in\partial\Omega}\lVert u_{sc}({\centerdot\,,y})\rVert_{X_{\lambda}^{*}}\lesssim\sup_{y\in\partial\Omega}\lVert Vu_{in}({\centerdot\,,y})\rVert_{X_{\lambda}}.

If we take a smooth cut-off function
\eta
such that
\eta\equiv 1
in
\mathrm{supp}\,V
and
\eta\equiv 0
in
\partial\Omega
, we have that
V(x)u_{in}(x,y)=V(x)\eta(x)u_{in}(x,y)
and

\lVert Vu_{in}({\centerdot\,,y})\rVert_{X_{\lambda}}\lesssim\lVert\eta u_{in}(\centerdot\,,y)\rVert_{X_{\lambda}^{*}}\lesssim 1,

where we have used that multiplication by
V
is bounded from
X_{\lambda}^{*}
to
X_{\lambda}
, as showed in section
2
, and that
u_{in}(\centerdot\,,y)
is smooth away from
y
by Theorem 11.1.1 in
[
17
]
, since it solves
(\Delta+\lambda)u_{in}(\centerdot\,,y)=0
. This proves that
w
is in
H^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Moreover, since
u_{sc}
solves the problem (
S
), it is easy to check that
u=v+w
solves
(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial\Omega
and fullfils the SRC (
2
). Also, since for any
y\in\partial\Omega
,
u_{sc}(\centerdot\,,y)
solves
\left(\Delta+\lambda\right)u_{sc}(\centerdot\,y)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathrm{supp}\,V
, it is smooth in this set by Theorem 11.1.1 in
[
17
]
, and in particular it is smooth near
\partial\Omega
. Therefore, the limiting values of
w
on the boundary are just

\partial_{\nu}w_{\pm}(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu_{x}}u_{sc}(x,y)\,f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

and therefore (
64
) is fullfiled.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Consider
d\geq 3
. Lef
f\in\mathcal{C}(\partial\Omega)
. Then, the double layer potential
u=\mathcal{D}f
can be extended continuosly to
\partial\Omega
, and we have the limiting values

(65)

u_{\pm}(x)=\left(\mathcal{D}f\right)(x)\pm\frac{1}{2}f(x),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

where the integral exists as an improper integral. Consequently, we have the jump relation
u_{+}-u_{-}=f
on
\partial\Omega
. Furthermore,
u
a solution in
H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
(\Delta+\lambda-V)\,u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial\Omega
, it fullfils SRC (
2
) and
\partial_{\nu}u_{-}-\partial_{\nu}u_{+}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

Proof:
.

The proof goes exactly as the proof of lemma
A.3
above, we just need to make a comment on how to prove the last statement. Indeed, the fact that the double layer potential for the homogeneous Helmholtz equation

v(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu_{y}}u_{in}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y)

fulfills that
\partial_{\nu}v_{-}-\partial_{\nu}v_{+}=0
on
\partial\Omega
is classical (see for example
[
9
]
). Meanwhile, the function

w(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu_{y}}u_{sc}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}S(y)

is smooth away from
\mathrm{supp}\,V
, since
u_{sc}(\centerdot,y)
is smooth away from
\mathrm{supp}\,V
as well.
∎

With lemma
A.3
, we can find an
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution to the problem (
61
) for
g\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
if we can find
\varphi\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
solving the integral equation
\mathcal{N}\varphi+\frac{1}{2}\varphi=g
. We will solve this equation via Fredholm theory. For simplicity, define the operators

\mathcal{K}=2\mathcal{D},\quad\mathcal{K}^{*}=2\mathcal{N},

and the equation we are trying to solve can be written as
\left(\mathcal{K}^{*}+I\right)\varphi=2g
.
Note that
\mathcal{K}^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{K}
and, by lemma
A.2
, both are compact operators over
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. To prove the existence of a solution, it is enough to prove that the operator
\mathcal{K}^{*}+I
is surjective, which by Fredholm alternative is equivalent to proving that
\mathcal{K}+I
is injective. Indeed, we have the following lemma:

Lemma A.5
.

Suppose that
\lambda>0
is not a Neumann eigenvalue for the operator
-\Delta+V
in
\Omega
. Then,

\ker(\mathcal{K}+I)=\{0\}.

Proof:
.

Let
\psi\in\ker(\mathcal{K}+I)
, which, by lemma
A.2
, will be continuous on
\partial\Omega
. Now define
v=\mathcal{D}\psi
. By lemma
A.4
,
v
is a solution to the problem

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta+\lambda\right)v=0&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\\
v_{+}=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\\
v\textrm{ satisfying SRC}.\end{cases}

Then,
v=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega
, by uniqueness of the exterior Dirichlet problem
[
8
]
. Therefore,
\partial_{\nu}v_{+}=0
on
\partial\Omega
and, again by lemma
A.4
,
\partial_{\nu}v_{-}=0
. This means that
v
is as well a solution to the problem

\begin{cases}\left(\Delta+\lambda-V\right)v=0&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}v_{-}=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\\
\end{cases}

but, since
\lambda
is not a Neumann eigenvalue for
-\Delta+V
in
\Omega
, we have that
v=0
in
\Omega
, and in particular
v_{-}=0
. Finally, by lemma
A.4
,
\psi=u_{+}-u_{-}=0
, and the lemma is proved.
∎

With this last lemma, the proof of Theorem
A.1
is concluded.
