Shortest Reconfiguration Sequence for Sliding Tokens on Spiders
Duc A. Hoang, Amanj Khorramian, Ryuhei Uehara

Abstract
Suppose that two independent sets I I and J J of a graph with | I | = | J | |I|=|J| are given, and a token is placed on each vertex in I I . The Sliding Token problem is to determine whether there exists a sequence of independent sets which transforms I I into J J so that each independent set in the sequence results from the previous one by sliding exactly one token along an edge in the graph. It is one of the representative reconfiguration problems that attract the attention from the viewpoint of theoretical computer science. For a yes-instance of a reconfiguration problem, finding a shortest reconfiguration sequence has a different aspect. In general, even if it is polynomial time solvable to decide whether two instances are reconfigured with each other, it can be 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard to find a shortest sequence between them. In this paper, we show that the problem for finding a shortest sequence between two independent sets is polynomial time solvable for spiders (i.e., trees having exactly one vertex of degree at least three).

Introduction

Recently, the
reconfiguration problems
attracted the attention
from the viewpoint of theoretical computer science.
These problem arise when we like to find a step-by-step transformation between
two feasible solutions of a problem such that all intermediate results are also feasible and
each step abides by a fixed reconfiguration rule, that is,
an adjacency relation defined on feasible solutions of the original problem.
The reconfiguration problems have been studied extensively for several well-known problems, including

Independent Set

[
11
,
15
,
16
,
18
]
,

Satisfiability

[
10
,
17
]
,
Set Cover
,
Clique
,
Matching

[
15
]
, and so on.

A reconfiguration problem can be seen as a natural “puzzle” from the viewpoint of
recreational mathematics.
The
15-puzzle
is one of the most famous classic puzzles,
that had the greatest impact on American and European societies (see
[
22
]
for its rich history).
It is well known that the 15-puzzle has a parity, and
one can solve the problem in linear time just by checking whether the parity of
one placement coincides with the other or not.
Moreover, the distance between any two reconfigurable placements is
O(n^{3})
,
that is, we can reconfigure from one to the other in
O(n^{3})
sliding pieces when the size of the board is
n\times n
.
However, surprisingly, for these two reconfigurable placements,
finding a shortest path is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete in general
[
4
,
20
]
.
Namely, although we know that there is a path of length in
O(n^{3})
, finding a shortest one is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.
While every piece is a unit square in the 15-puzzle, we obtain
the other famous classic puzzle when we allow to have rectangular pieces,
which is called “Dad puzzle” and its variants can be found in the whole world
(e.g., it is called “hako-iri-musume” in Japanese).
Gardner said that “these puzzles are very much in want of a theory” in 1964
[
9
]
,
and Hearn and Demaine gave the theory after 40 years
[
11
]
;
they are
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete in general
[
12
]
.

Summarizing up, these sliding block puzzles characterize representative computational complexity classes;
the decision problem for unit squares can be solved in linear time just by checking parities,
finding a shortest reconfiguration for the unit squares is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete, and
the decision problem becomes
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for rectangular pieces.
That is, this simple reconfiguration problem gives us a new sight of these
representative computational complexity classes.

In general, the reconfiguration problems tend to be
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete,
and some polynomial time algorithms are shown in restricted cases.
Finding a shortest sequence in the context of the reconfiguration problems
is a new trend in theoretical computer science because it has
a great potential to characterize the class
\mathsf{NP}
from
a different viewpoint from the classic ones.

One of the important
\mathsf{NP}

the

For this notion, a natural reconfiguration problem called
Sliding Token
was introduced by Hearn and Demaine
[

]
.
(See
[
16

Independent Set

Suppose that we are given two independent sets
I
and
J
of
a graph
G=(V,E)
such that
{\left|I\right|}={\left|J\right|}
,
and imagine that a
token
(coin) is placed on each vertex in
I
.
For convenience, sometimes we identify the token with the vertex it is placed on and simply say “a token in an independent set.”
Then, the
Sliding Token
problem is to determine whether there exists a sequence

S=\langle I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{\ell}\rangle
of independent sets of
G
such that

(a)

I_{1}=I
,
I_{\ell}=J
,
and
{\left|I_{i}\right|}={\left|I\right|}={\left|J\right|}
for all
i
,
1\leq i\leq\ell
; and

(b)

for each
i
,
2\leq i\leq\ell
,
there is an edge
xy
in
G
such that
I_{i-1}\setminus I_{i}=\{x\}

and
I_{i}\setminus I_{i-1}=\{y\}
.

That is,
I_{i}
can be obtained from
I_{i-1}
by sliding exactly
one token on a vertex
x\in I_{i-1}
to its adjacent vertex
y\in I_{i}

along an edge
xy\in E(G)
.
Such a sequence
S
, if exists, is called a
\mathsf{TS}
-
sequence
in
G
between
I
and
J
.
We denote by a
3
-tuple
(G,I,J)
an instance of
Sliding Token
problem.
If a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence
S
in
G
between
I
and
J
exists, we say that
I
is
reconfigurable
to
J
(and vice versa), and write
I\overset{G}{\leftrightsquigarrow}J
.
The sets
I
and
J
are the
initial
and
target
independent sets, respectively.
For a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence
S
, the
length

\mathsf{len}(S)
of
S
is defined as the number of independent sets in
S
minus one.
In other words,
\mathsf{len}(S)
is the number of token-slides described in
S
.
Figure
1
illustrates a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence of length
4
between two independent sets
I=I_{1}
and
J=I_{5}
.

For the
Sliding Token
problem, linear-time algorithms have been shown for cographs
(also known as
P_{4}
-free graphs)
[
16
]
and trees
[
6
]
.
Polynomial-time algorithms are shown for bipartite permutation graphs
[
8
]
,
claw-free graphs
[
3
]
, cacti
[
13
]
, and interval graphs
[
2
]

On the other hand,

.

In this context, for a given
yes
-instance
(G,I,J)
of
Sliding Token
, we aim to find a shortest
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence between
I
and
J
.
Such a problem is called the
Shortest Sliding Token
problem.
As seen for the 15-puzzle, the
Shortest Sliding Token
problem can be intractable
even for these graph classes which the decision problem can be solved in polynomial time.
Moreover, in the 15-puzzle, we already know that it has a solution of polynomial length for two configurations.
However, in the
Sliding Token
problem, we have no upper bound of the length of a solution in general.
To deal with this delicate issue, we have to distinguish two variants of this problem.
In the
decision variant
, an integer
\ell
is also given as a part of input,
and we have to decide whether there exists a sequence between
I
and
J
of length at most
\ell
.
In the
non-decision variant
, we are asked to output a specific shortest
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence.
The length
\ell
is not necessarily polynomial in
{\left|V(G)\right|}
in general.
When
\ell
is super-polynomial, we may have that the decision variant is in
\mathsf{P}
,
while the non-decision one is not in
\mathsf{P}
since it takes super-polynomial time to output the sequence.
On the other hand, even when
G
is a perfect graph and
\ell
is polynomial in
{\left|V(G)\right|}
, the decision variant of
Shortest Sliding Token
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete (see
[
16
, Theorem 5]
).
In short, in the decision variant, we focus on the
length
of a shortest
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence, while in the non-decision variant, we focus on the
construction
of a shortest
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence itself.

From this viewpoint, the length of a token sliding is a key feature of the

Shortest Sliding Token
problem. If the length is super-polynomial in total,
there exists at least one token that slides super-polynomial times.
That is, the token visits the same vertex many times in its slides.
That is, some tokens make
detours
in the sequence
(the notion of detour is important and precisely defined later).
In general, it seems to be more difficult to analyze “detours of tokens” for graphs containing cycle(s).
As a result, one may first consider the problem for trees.
The
Sliding Token
problem on a tree
can be solved in linear time
[
6
]
.
Polynomial-time algorithms for the
Shortest Sliding Token
problem were first investigated in

[
26
]
.
In
[
26
]
, the authors gave polynomial-time algorithms for solving
Shortest Sliding Token
when the input graph is either a proper interval graph, a trivially perfect graph, or a caterpillar.
We note that caterpillars is the first graph class that required detours to solve
the
Shortest Sliding Token
problem.
A caterpillar is a tree that consists of a “backbone” called a
spine
with many
pendants
,
or leaves attached to the spine. Each pendant can be used to escape a token,
however, the other tokens cannot pass through it. Therefore, the ordering of tokens on the spine is fixed.
In this paper, we consider the
Shortest Sliding Token
problem on a spider,
which is a tree with one central vertex of degree more than
2
.
On this graph, we can use each “leg” as a stack and exchange tokens using these stacks.
Therefore, we have many ways to handle the tokens, and hence we need more analyses to find a shortest sequence.
In this paper, we give an
O(n^{2})
time algorithms for the
Shortest Sliding Token
problem on a spider,
where
n
is the number of vertices.
The algorithm is constructive, and the sequence itself can be output in
O(n^{2})
time.
As mentioned in
[
26
]
, the number of required token-slides in a sequence can be
\Omega(n^{2})
,
hence our algorithm is optimal for the number of token-slides.

Note:
Recently, it is announced that the
Shortest Sliding Token
problem on
a tree can be solved in polynomial time by Sugimori
[
23
]
.
His algorithm is based on a dynamic programming on a tree
[
24
]
:
though it runs in polynomial time, it seems to have much larger degree comparing
to our case-analysis based algorithm.

Preliminaries

For common graph theoretic definitions, we refer the readers to the textbook
[
7
]
.
Throughout this paper, we denote by
V(G)
and

the vertex-set and edge-set of a graph
G
, respectively.
We always use
n
for denoting
{\left|V(G)\right|}
.
For a vertex
x\in V(G)
, we denote by
N_{G}(x)
the set
\{y\in V(G):xy\in E(G)\}
of

of
x
, and by

of

.
In a similar manner, for an induced subgraph

of

, the set

is defined as
\bigcup_{x\in V(H)}N_{G}[x]
.
The

of

, denoted by

, is the size of

.
For

, the
distance

\mathsf{dist}_{G}(x,y)
between

and

is simply the length (i.e., the number of edges) of a shortest

-path in
G
.

For a tree
T
, we denote by

the (unique) shortest

-path in

, and by

the subtree of

induced by

and its descendants when regarding

as the tree rooted at

.
A

(or

) is a tree having exactly one vertex (called its

) of degree at least

.
For a spider

with body

and a vertex

, the path

is called a

of

.
By definition, it is not hard to see that two different legs of

have no common vertex.

is a spider with body

and

.

Let
(G,I,J)
be an instance of
Shortest Sliding Token
.
A
target assignment
from
I
to
J
is simply a bijective mapping
f:I\to J
.
A target assignment

is called

if there exists a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence in
G
between
I
and

that moves the token on

to

for every

.
Given a target assignment
f:I\to J
from
I
to
J
, one can also define the target assignment
f^{-1}:J\to I
from
J
to
I
as follows: for every
x\in J
,

.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of all target assignments from

to

.
We define
{M^{*}}(G,I,J)=\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{w\in I}\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w,f(w))
.
Intuitively, observe that any
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence between
I
and
J

Let
S=\langle I_{1},I_{2},\dotsc,I_{\ell}\rangle
be a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence between two independent sets

and

of a graph

.
Indeed, one can describe

, and

.
The

of

(which reconfigures

to

), denoted by
\mathsf{rev}(S)
, is defined by
\mathsf{rev}(S)=\langle I_{\ell},\dotsc,I_{2},I_{1}\rangle
.
One can also describe
\mathsf{rev}(S)
in term of token-slides:
\mathsf{rev}(S)=\langle y_{\ell-1}\to x_{\ell-1},\dots,y_{2}\to x_{2},y_{1}\to x_{1}\rangle
.
For example, the
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence

described in Figure

S=\langle v_{4}\to v_{5},v_{3}\to v_{2},v_{2}\to v_{1},v_{5}\to v_{4}\rangle

Similarly,
\mathsf{rev}(S)=\langle I_{5},\dotsc,I_{1}\rangle=\langle v_{4}\to v_{5},v_{1}\to v_{2},v_{2}\to v_{3},v_{5}\to v_{4}\rangle
.

For an edge
e=xy\in E(G)
, we say that
S

makes detour over

if both
x\to y
and
y\to x
are members of
S
.
We emphasize that the steps
x\to y
and
y\to x
is
not
necessarily made by the same token.
The
number of detours

makes over

, denoted by
D_{G}(S,e)
, is defined to be twice the minimum between the number of appearances of
x\to y
and the number of appearances of
y\to x
.
The
total number of detours
S
makes in
G
, denoted by
D_{G}(S)
, is defined to be
\sum_{e\in E(G)}D_{G}(S,e)
.
As an example, one can verify that the
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence
S
described in Figure
1
satisfies
D_{G}(S,v_{4}v_{5})=2
and
D_{G}(S)=2
.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of all
\mathsf{TS}
-sequences in
G
between two independent sets
I,J
.
We define by
{D^{*}}(G,I,J)=\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}}D_{G}(S)
the smallest number of detours that a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence between
I
and
J
in
G
can possibly make.

For two
\mathsf{TS}
-sequences
S_{1}=\langle x_{1}\to y_{1},x_{2}\to y_{2},\dotsc,x_{\ell-1}\to y_{\ell-1}\rangle
and
S_{2}=\langle x_{1}^{\prime}\to y_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}\to y_{2}^{\prime},\dotsc,x_{p}^{\prime}\to y_{p}^{\prime}\rangle
in a graph

, if the sequence of token-slides
S=\langle x_{1}\to y_{1},x_{2}\to y_{2},\dotsc,x_{\ell-1}\to y_{\ell-1},x_{1}^{\prime}\to y_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}\to y_{2}^{\prime},\dotsc,x_{p}^{\prime}\to y_{p}^{\prime}\rangle
forms a

-sequence in

, we define

, and say that

is obtained by taking the

of

and

.

3
Shortest Sliding Token
for spiders

In this section, we show that
Shortest Sliding Token
for spiders can be solved in polynomial time.
More precisely, we claim that

Theorem 1
.

Given an instance
(G,I,J)
of
Shortest Sliding Token
for spiders, one can construct a shortest
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence between
I
and
J
in
O(n^{2})
time, where
n
denotes the number of vertices of the given spider
G
.

First of all, from the linear-time algorithm for solving
Sliding Token
for trees (which also applies for spiders as well) presented in
[
6
]
, we can simplify our problem as follows.
For an independent set
I
of a tree
T
, the token on
u\in I
is said to be
(T,I)
-
rigid
if for any
{I^{\prime}}
with
I\overset{T}{\leftrightsquigarrow}{I^{\prime}}
,
u\in{I^{\prime}}
.
Intuitively, a
(T,I)
-rigid token cannot be moved by any
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence in
T
.
One can find all
(T,I)
-rigid tokens in a given tree
T
in linear time.
Moreover, a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence between
I
and
J
in
T
exists if and only if the
(T,I)
-rigid tokens and
(T,J)
-rigid tokens are the same, and for any component
F
of the forest obtained from
T
by removing all vertices where
(T,I)
-rigid tokens are placed and their neighbors,
{\left|I\cap F\right|}={\left|J\cap F\right|}
.
Thus, for an instance
(G,I,J)
of
Shortest Sliding Token
for spiders, we can assume without loss of generality that
I\overset{G}{\leftrightsquigarrow}J
and there are no
(G,I)
-rigid and
(G,J)
-rigid tokens.

3.1
General idea

We now give a brief overview of our approach.
For convenience, from now on, let
(G,I,J)
be an instance of
Shortest Sliding Token
for spiders satisfying the above assumption.
Rough speaking, we aim to construct a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence in
G
between
I
and
J
of
minimum
length
{M^{*}}(G,I,J)+{D^{*}}(G,I,J)
, where
{M^{*}}(G,I,J)
and
{D^{*}}(G,I,J)
are respectively the smallest number of token-slides and the smallest number of detours that a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence between
I
and
J
in
G
can possibly perform, as defined in the previous section.
Indeed, the following lemma implies that any
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence in
G
between
I
and
J
must be of length at least
{M^{*}}(G,I,J)+{D^{*}}(G,I,J)
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
I,J
be two independent sets of a tree
T
such that
I\overset{T}{\leftrightsquigarrow}J
.
Then, for every
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence
S
between
I
and
J
,
\mathsf{len}(S)\geq{M^{*}}(T,I,J)+{D^{*}}(T,I,J)
.

Proof.

Let
I=\{w_{1},w_{2},\dotsc,w_{\left|I\right|}\}
.
Let
S
be a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence between
I
and
J
that moves the token
t_{i}
on
w_{i}
to
f(w_{i})
for some target assignment
f:I\to J
.
For each
i\in\{1,2,\dotsc,{\left|I\right|}\}
, let
S_{i}
be the sequence of
\mathsf{dist}_{T}(w_{i},f(w_{i}))
token-slides that moves
t_{i}
from
w_{i}
to
f(w_{i})
along the (unique) path
P_{w_{i}f(w_{i})}
.
Note that
S_{i}
is not necessarily a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence.

Let consider the movements of
t_{i}
from
w_{i}
to
f(w_{i})
in the
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence
S
. First of all, it is clear that
t_{i}
needs to make all moves in
S_{i}
.
Since the path
P_{w_{i}f(w_{i})}
is unique, if
t_{i}
makes any move
x\to y
that is not in
S_{i}
for some edge
xy\in E(T)
, it must also make the move
y\to x
later, hence forming detour over
e
.
Let
D_{1}
be the number of detours formed by the token-slides in
S\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}S_{i}
.
Clearly,
\mathsf{len}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}\mathsf{dist}_{T}(w_{i},f(w_{i}))+D_{1}
.

The token-slides in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}S_{i}
may also form detour.
Let
i,j\in\{1,2,\dotsc,{\left|I\right|}\}
be such that the sequence
S_{i}
moves
t_{i}
from
w_{i}
to
f(w_{i})
and at some point makes the move
x\to y
, and the sequence
S_{j}
moves
t_{j}
from
w_{j}
to
f(w_{j})
and at some point makes the move
y\to x
.
Together,
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
form detour over an edge
e=xy\in E(P_{w_{i}f(w_{i})})\cap E(P_{w_{j}f(w_{j})})
.
Let
D_{2}
be the number of detours formed by such token-slides.
Clearly,
D_{G}(S)=D_{1}+D_{2}
.

Suppose that for an edge
e=xy\in E(T)
, there exists
k_{e}
pairs
(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2}),\dotsc,(i_{k_{e}},j_{k_{e}})
with
1\leq i_{p},j_{p}\leq{\left|I\right|}
,
i_{p}\neq j_{p}
, and for any two pairs
(i_{p},j_{p})
and
(i_{q},j_{q})
,
i_{p}\neq i_{q}
and
j_{p}\neq j_{q}
(
1\leq p,q\leq k_{e}
) such that for each
p\in\{1,2,\dotsc,k_{e}\}
, the sequence
S_{i_{p}}
at some point makes the move
x\to y
, and the sequence
S_{j_{p}}
at some point makes the move
y\to x
.
It follows that the vertices
\{w_{i_{p}}\}_{1\leq p\leq k_{e}}
and
\{f(w_{j_{p}})\}_{1\leq p\leq k_{e}}
are in
V(T^{y}_{x})
,
and the vertices
\{w_{j_{p}}\}_{1\leq p\leq k_{e}}
and
\{f(w_{i_{p}})\}_{1\leq p\leq k_{e}}

We note that
1\leq k_{e}\leq\lfloor{\left|I\right|}/2\rfloor

and
S_{j_{p}}
are not necessarily
\mathsf{TS}
-sequences.
Let
\mathcal{E}_{f}
be the set of all edges of

satisfying the described property with respect to the target assignment
f
.
Then,
D_{2}=2\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{f}}k_{e}
.

Let
e\in\mathcal{E}_{f}
be an edge of
T
as described above.
Let
g
be the target assignment defined as follows: for
1\leq p\leq k_{e}
,
g(w_{i_{p}})=f(w_{j_{p}})
,
g(w_{j_{p}})=f(w_{i_{p}})
, and
g(w_{i})=f(w_{i})
for
i\notin\{i_{1},i_{2},\dotsc,i_{k_{e}},j_{1},j_{2},\dotsc,j_{k_{e}}\}
.
Then,
\sum_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}\mathsf{dist}_{T}(w_{i},f(w_{i}))=\sum_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}\mathsf{dist}_{T}(w_{i},g(w_{i}))+2k_{e}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{g}=\mathcal{E}_{f}\setminus\{e\}
.
Using this property repeatedly, we can finally find a target assignment

such that
\mathcal{E}_{g}=\emptyset
and
\sum_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}\mathsf{dist}_{T}(w_{i},f(w_{i}))=\sum_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}\mathsf{dist}_{T}(w_{i},g(w_{i}))+D_{2}
.

Therefore,
\mathsf{len}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}\mathsf{dist}_{T}(w_{i},f(w_{i}))+D_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}\mathsf{dist}_{T}(w_{i},g(w_{i}))+D_{2}+D_{1}\geq{M^{*}}(T,I,J)+{D^{*}}(T,I,J)
.
∎

As a result, it remains to show that any
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence in
G
between

and

must be of length at most

\mathsf{TS}

in

and

To this end, we shall analyze the following cases.

1.

Case 1:

.

2.

Case 2:

3.

Case 3:

\max\{{\left|I\cap N_{G}(v)\right|},{\left|J\cap N_{G}(v)\right|}\}\geq 2

In each case, we claim that it is possible to simultaneously determine

and construct a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence in

between

and

whose length is minimum.
More precisely, in

-sequence between

\mathsf{TS}

\mathsf{TS}
-sequence must make detours at least one time at some edge of

More precisely, in such situations, we show that it is possible to construct a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence between

As a by-product, we also describe how one can calculate this (smallest) number of detours precisely.

For an arbitrary target assignment

Base case:

\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},f(w_{j}))+\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{j},f(w_{i}))\geq\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},f(w_{i}))+\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{j},f(w_{j}))
.

\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},g(w_{i}))+\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{j},g(w_{j}))\geq\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},f(w_{i}))+\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{j},f(w_{j}))

Inductive step:
Given a target assignment
f
produced from Algorithm
1
and any target assignment

\sum_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},g(w_{i}))\geq\sum_{i=1}^{{\left|I\right|}}\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},f(w_{i}))

We show that for every target assignment

produced from Algorithm

and every target assignment

\mathsf{k}_{gf}=k\leq{\left|I\right|}
, the above inequality holds.

Suppose to the contrary that there exist a target assignment

.
Assume that there exists a leg
L
of
G
with
{\left|I_{L}\right|}\neq{\left|J_{L}\right|}
.
Then, in
O(n^{2})
time, one can construct a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence
S
between
I
and
J
such that
\mathsf{len}(S)={M^{*}}(G,I,J)
.

Proof.

Let
f
be a target assignment produced from Algorithm
1
and
\prec
be a corresponding total ordering defined in Algorithm
2
.
For convenience, for
x,y\in I
, if
x<y
and
x\prec y
, we say that Algorithm
2

preserves
the ordering between
x
and
y
.

Assume that
I=\{w_{1},\dotsc,w_{{\left|I\right|}}\}
is such that
w_{1}\prec\dots\prec w_{{\left|I\right|}}
.
Let
S
be a sequence of token-slides constructed as follows: for each
w_{i}\in I
(
i\in\{1,2,\dotsc,{\left|I\right|}\}
), slide the token
t_{i}
on
w_{i}
to
f(w_{i})
along the path
P_{w_{i}f(w_{i})}
(using exactly
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},f(w_{i}))
token-slides).
Clearly,
\mathsf{len}(S)={M^{*}}(G,I,J)
(Lemma
3
).

Since Algorithm
1
takes
O(n)
time, Algorithm
2
takes
O(n^{2})
time, and
S
uses
O(n)
token-slides for each token in
I
, it follows that the construction of
S
takes
O(n^{2})
time.

To conclude this proof, we show that
S
is actually a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence in
G
by induction on
i\in\{1,2,\dotsc,{\left|I\right|}\}
.

Base case:

i=1
.
Since for every
j>1
,
w_{j}\notin N_{G}[P_{w_{1}f(w_{1})}]
,
t_{1}
clearly can be slid from
w_{1}
to
f(w_{1})
along
P_{w_{1}f(w_{1})}
.

Inductive step:
Assume that for
j\leq i-1
(
i\in\{2,3,\dotsc,{\left|I\right|}\}
),
t_{j}
can be slid from
w_{j}
to
f(w_{j})
along
P_{w_{j}f(w_{j})}
.
We show that
t_{i}
can be slid from
w_{i}
to
f(w_{i})
along
P_{w_{i}f(w_{i})}
.
Suppose to the contrary that it is not.
Note that by Algorithm
2
, for every
j>i
,
w_{j}\notin N_{G}[P_{w_{i}f(w_{i})}]
.
Thus, there must be some
j<i
such that
f(w_{j})\in N_{G}[P_{w_{i}f(w_{i})}]
.
In other words, after
t_{j}
is moved from
w_{j}
to
f(w_{j})
, it becomes an “obstacle” that forbids sliding
t_{i}
from
w_{i}
to
f(w_{i})
.
We consider the following cases.

1.

Case 1:
w_{i}\in I_{L}
and
f(w_{i})\in J_{L}
for some leg
L
of
G
.

Since
f(w_{j})\in N_{G}[P_{w_{i}f(w_{i})}]
, we have
f(w_{j})\in J_{L}
.
Since
t_{j}
is moved before
t_{i}
from
w_{j}
to
f(w_{j})\in J_{L}
, it follows that
w_{j}\in I_{L}
.
Indeed, if
w_{j}\notin I_{L}
, the vertex
w_{i}
must be assigned before
w_{j}
in Algorithm
1
, and Algorithm
2
preserves that ordering (see Lemma
5
(ii)), which means
w_{i}\prec w_{j}
, a contradiction.
Now, if
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{j},v)>\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},v)
, we must have
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{j}),v)>\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},v)
; otherwise
t_{i}
is an obstacle that forbids sliding
t_{j}
to
f(w_{j})
, which contradicts our inductive hypothesis.
We note that the existence of
f(w_{j})
implies that
w_{i}\neq f(w_{i})
.
If
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{i}),v)<\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},v)
, then
f(w_{j})\in N_{G}[w_{i}]
, which also means
w_{i}\in N_{G}[P_{w_{j}f(w_{j})}]
.
This contradicts the assumption
w_{j}\prec w_{i}
.
Therefore,
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{i}),v)>\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},v)
. Since
f(w_{j})\in N_{G}[w_{i}]
and
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{j}),v)>\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},v)
, we must have
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},v)<\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{j}),v)<\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{i}),v)
. On the other hand, since
w_{i},w_{j}\in I_{L}
,
f(w_{i}),f(w_{j})\in J_{L}
, and
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{j},v)>\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},v)
, by Algorithm
1
, we must have
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{j}),v)>\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{i}),v)
, which is a contradiction. Using a similar argument, one can show that the case
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{j},v)<\mathsf{dist}_{G}(w_{i},v)
also leads to a contradiction.

2.

Case 2:
w_{i}\in I_{L}
and
f(w_{i})\in J_{L^{\prime}}
, where
L
and
L^{\prime}
are two distinct legs of
G
.

First of all, recall that the legs

are labeled such that
{\left|I\cap V(L_{i})\right|}-{\left|J\cap V(L_{i})\right|}\leq{\left|I\cap V(L_{j})\right|}-{\left|J\cap V(L_{j})\right|}
for

.
Therefore, if
{\left|I_{L}\right|}\neq{\left|J_{L}\right|}
for some leg

of

, we have
{\left|I_{L_{1}}\right|}\leq{\left|J_{L_{1}}\right|}
.
To see this, note that if
{\left|I_{L_{1}}\right|}>{\left|J_{L_{1}}\right|}
, there must be some

such that
{\left|I\cap V(L_{i})\right|}={\left|I_{L_{i}}\right|}<{\left|J_{L_{i}}\right|}={\left|J\cap V(L_{i})\right|}
(because
\sum_{i=1}^{\deg_{G}(v)}I_{L_{i}}={\left|I\right|}={\left|J\right|}=\sum_{i=1}^{\deg_{G}(v)}J_{L_{i}}
), which means
{\left|I\cap V(L_{i})\right|}-{\left|J\cap V(L_{i})\right|}\leq-1
.
On the other hand, one can verify that
{\left|I\cap V(L_{1})\right|}-{\left|J\cap V(L_{1})\right|}>-1
.
This contradicts our assumption that
{\left|I\cap V(L_{1})\right|}-{\left|J\cap V(L_{1})\right|}\leq{\left|I\cap V(L_{i})\right|}-{\left|J\cap V(L_{i})\right|}
.
Thus,
{\left|I_{L_{1}}\right|}\leq{\left|J_{L_{1}}\right|}
.
Note that if
v\in J
and
{\left|I_{L_{1}}\right|}={\left|J_{L_{1}}\right|}
then one can verify that
{\left|I\cap V(L_{1})\right|}-{\left|J\cap V(L_{1})\right|}\geq 0
.
As before, there must be some

such that
{\left|I\cap V(L_{i})\right|}={\left|I_{L_{i}}\right|}<{\left|J_{L_{i}}\right|}={\left|J\cap V(L_{i})\right|}
, which means
{\left|I\cap V(L_{i})\right|}-{\left|J\cap V(L_{i})\right|}<0\leq{\left|I\cap V(L_{1})\right|}-{\left|J\cap V(V_{1})\right|}
, a contradiction.
Therefore, if
v\in J
, we have
{\left|I_{L_{1}}\right|}<{\left|J_{L_{1}}\right|}
.
It follows that if
v\in I
(resp.
v\in J
), then
v\in I_{L_{1}}^{2}
(resp.
f^{-1}(v)\in I_{L_{1}}^{1}
).
Moreover, Algorithm
1
and Lemma

(ii) implies that

, then
f^{-1}(v)=\max_{\prec}\{w:w\in\bigcup_{L}I_{L}^{1}\}
.
Since

, we have
w_{j}\neq f^{-1}(v)
, and hence
f(w_{j})\neq v
.
Since

,

, and
f(w_{j})\neq v
,

must belong to either
J_{L}
or
J_{L^{\prime}}
.
If
f(w_{j})
belongs to

, then since

, it follows that
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{j}),v)<\mathsf{dist}_{G}(f(w_{i},v))
, which means that

.
It follows that
w_{j}
is assigned after

, and since Algorithm

preserves this ordering,

, which is a contradiction.
Hence,

belongs to

.
Additionally, from Algorithm

, since

,

must belong to

and

.
As

is not an obstacle that forbid sliding

from

to

, it follows that

, which means
w_{i}\in N_{G}[P_{w_{j}f(w_{j})}]
.
This contradicts our assumption that

.

Hence,
t_{i}
can be slid from
w_{i}
to
f(w_{i})
along
P_{w_{i}f(w_{i})}
.
Our proof is now complete.
∎

Lemma 10

The value of

\mathsf{TS}
-sequence

Proof.

We modify the algorithm described in
[
5

First, regard

as the root of
T

Then, we define
\phi(y)
for each vertex

For each leave

For each internal vertex

For each internal vertex

\phi(y)=\min_{\text{child $w$ of $y$}}\phi(w)

By definition, it is not hard to see that the above algorithm correctly computes
\mathsf{cost}(T,I,xy)

For each

Assume that

We now show how to construct

For each

with

For

For every

One can verify that the sequence

\mathsf{TS}
-sequence in

The
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence

\bigoplus_{w\in I\cap N_{T^{x}_{y}}(y)}S(T,I,wc(w))\oplus\langle x\to y\rangle

Clearly, we can use this recursive relation to construct

\mathsf{TS}
-sequence
S
obviously takes
O(n^{2})
time.

We remark that in the described algorithm,
D_{G}(S)
was not explicitly calculated.
In the remaining part of this proof, we show how to calculate
D_{G}(S)
.
It is sufficient to show how to calculate
D_{G}(S)
in
Case 1.1
; other cases can be done in similar manner.
From
Case 1.1
,
S=S_{1}^{i}\oplus S_{2}^{i}
.
We note that from Lemma
10

S_{1}^{i}
itself does not make detour over any edge of
G
.
On the other hand, Lemma
8
implies that
S_{2}^{i}
itself makes detour over at most one edge of
G
(due to whether Lemma
8
(i) holds).
From Lemma
2
, it remains to calculate the number of detours made by
S_{1}^{i}
and
S_{2}^{i}
together.
From the construction of
S_{1}^{i}
, note that each move
x\to y
in
S_{1}^{i}
appears exactly once.
Consider a move
x\to y
in
S_{1}^{i}
such that
(y,x)
is a directed edge of the corresponding auxiliary graph
A(G,I,J)
.
By definition,
{\left|I\cap G^{x}_{y}\right|}\geq{\left|J\cap G^{x}_{y}\right|}
.
Let
{I^{\prime}}
be the resulting independent set after the move
x\to y
.
Then, it can be shown by induction on the number of such moves in
S_{1}^{i}
that
{\left|{I^{\prime}}\cap G^{x}_{y}\right|}>{\left|J\cap G^{x}_{y}\right|}
.
It follows that at some point,
S_{2}^{i}
will have to make a move
y\to x
.
Together, these moves form detour over
e=xy\in E(G)
.
Since each move
x\to y
in
S_{1}^{i}
appears exactly once, we must have
D_{G}(S)=D_{G}(S_{2}^{i})+2{\left|\{\langle x\to y\rangle\in S_{1}^{i}:(y,x)\in E(A(G,I,J))\}\right|}
.
In a similar manner, in
Case 1.2
,
D_{G}(S)
can be calculated.
In
Case 2
, we argue with tokens in
J
(instead of
I
) and the auxiliary graph
A(G,J,I)
(instead of
A(G,I,J)
).
Finally, in
Case 3
, we simply combine the arguments in
Cases 1
and
Case 2
.
∎

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we have shown that one can indeed construct a
\mathsf{TS}
-sequence of shortest length between two given independent sets of a spider graph (if exists).
We hope that our ideas and approaches described here will provide a useful framework for improving the polynomial-time algorithm for
Shortest Sliding Token
for trees
[
23
]
.
