A new simple family of Cantor sets in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} all of whose projections are one-dimensional
Olga Frolkina

Abstract
In 1994, J. Cobb described a Cantor set in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} each of whose projections into 2 2 -planes is one-dimensional. A series of works by other authors developing this field followed. We present another very simple series of Cantor sets in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} all of whose projections are connected and one-dimensional. These are self-similar Cantor sets which go back to the work of Louis Antoine, and we celebrate their centenary birthday in 2020–2021. Keywords: Euclidean space, projection, Cantor set, embedding, topological dimension, Antoine’s necklace, self-similarity, Hausdorff measure, Hausdorff dimension, Lebesgue measure, demension. MSC: Primary 54F45; Secondary 57N12, 28A80.

Introduction

In 1994, J. Cobb
constructed a Cantor set in

2

[

In the same paper, he stated a general question:

Such sets will be called

The case

L. Antoine
constructed

\mathbb{R}^{2}

whose projections coincide
with those of a regular hexagon

[
5
,
9
, p. 272; and fig. 2 on p. 273]
.
(Another
(2,1,1)

[

, p. 124]
;
for a different example and further results on planar sets with prescribed projections,
see
[
15
]
.)
K. Borsuk constructed an
(n,m,m)
Cantor set in
[
12
]
;
as a corollary, he obtained a positive answer to the question of R.H. Fox:
there exists a simple closed curve in

\mathbb{R}^{3}

Later, examples for the following triples appeared:
for
(n,m,m-1)
in
[
17
]
and
for
(n,n-1,k)
in
[
9
]
— both generalized Cobb’s idea;
and
a different construction for
(n,n-1,n-2)
in
[
18
]
,
where further references can be found.
Interestingly, all projections of a
typical
Cantor set are Cantor sets
[
19
]
.

Cobb’s construction of a
(3,2,1)
Cantor set is rather complicated;
the resulting set is tame.
I suggested a different approach observing that
there exist Antoine’s necklaces all of whose projections are one-dimensional

[
18

To obtain a necklace with this property,
I controlled the location and thickness of solid tori
at prelimit levels of construction;
such control can also be generalized to all Cantor sets in all dimensions:
For each Cantor set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
,
there is an arbitrarily small isotopy
\{h_{t}\}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}

such that
the projection of
h_{1}(K)
into each
(n-1)
-plane has dimension

(n-2)

[
18
, Thm. 2]
.

The control process is not trivial.
It is based on the
plank theorem
(for the planar case, see
English translation of
A. Tarski’s and H. Moese’s papers (1931–32)
in
[
26

the high-dimensional case is treated in
[
8

The control process is necessary. In fact:
For each Cantor set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
,
there exists an arbitrarily small isotopy
\{h_{t}\}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}

such that
for each proper subspace
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

the dimension of the projection of
h_{1}(K)
into
\Pi

\dim\Pi

[
18
, Stat. 12]
.

In this article, I restore the original definition of Antoine’s necklaces
from
[
4
]
. These sets are self-similar.
In Theorem
2.6
, we formulate
simple additional conditions
which ensure that all projections of these sets are one-dimensional. Theorems
2.7

2.8

Self-similarity eliminates the need for thickness control:
one-dimensionality is now
derived from properties of

We also consider a high-dimensional case (Theorem
3.2
).
Using Rushing’s idea to combine results of Shtan’ko and
Väisälä
[
29
, p. 599]
,
we get a
new shorter — but less elementary — proof of the above proposition
concerning
(n,n-1,n-2)
-sets,

Finally, we observe that the sets

from
[

are
(3,2,1)

By this reason we first give an “elementary” exposition, and

For our purpose, we will only restrict ourselves by the class of self-similar

Definition 2.1

Let
\Pi
be a plane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Let
D\subset\Pi
be a disk of radius
r>0

with center
Q
, and let
\ell\subset\Pi
be a straight line such that
d(Q,\ell)=R>r
.
A
standard solid torus

T

is the solid torus of revolution
generated by
revolving
D
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
about
\ell
.
The
central circle
of
T

is the circle generated by rotating the point
Q
. The
center
of
T
is the center
of its central circle.
For a given standard solid torus
T
, the corresponding radii
will be denoted by
r_{T}
and
R_{T}
, the central circle by
C_{T}
, and the center by
Q_{T}
.

Definition 2.2

A simple chain

in a standard solid torus
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

is a finite family

T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}
,
k\geqslant 3
,
of pairwise disjoint
standard solid tori such that

1)
T_{1}\cup\ldots\cup T_{k}\subset T
;

2) centers of
T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}

are subsequent vertices of a regular convex
k
-gon inscribed in the
central circle of
T
;

3)
T_{i}
and
T_{j}
are linked for

|i-j|\equiv 1\mod k
, and are not linked
otherwise.

See Fig.
1(a)
.

A simple chain
T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}

looks like a “usual necklace” which
winds once around the hole of
T
;
no one of the tori
T_{i}
embraces the hole of
T
.

A simple chain.

(b)

A regular simple chain.

:

Definition 2.3

A simple chain
T_{1},\ldots,T_{2m}
,
m\geqslant 2

in a standard solid torus
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

will be called

regular

if

1) all
T_{i}
’s are congruent,

2)

\operatorname{Aff}C_{T_{1}}=\operatorname{Aff}C_{T_{3}}=\ldots=\operatorname{Aff}C_{T_{2m-1}}=\operatorname{Aff}C_{T}
,

3)

\operatorname{Aff}C_{T_{2i}}\bot\operatorname{Aff}C_{T}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
, and

4)
each straight line

\operatorname{Aff}C_{T_{2i}}\cap\operatorname{Aff}C_{T}
,

touches the circle

.

See Fig.

.

Definition 2.4

Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

be a standard solid torus, and
let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}

be similarity transformations such that

T_{1}:=S_{1}(T),\ldots,T_{k}:=S_{k}(T)

is a simple chain in
T
.
For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
take

M_{\lambda}=\bigcup\limits_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\lambda})\in\{1,\ldots,k\}^{\lambda}}S_{i_{1}}\circ S_{i_{2}}\circ\ldots\circ S_{i_{\lambda}}(T).

We have

T\supset M_{1}\supset M_{2}\supset M_{3}\supset\ldots
. The intersection

\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}):=\bigcap\limits_{\lambda=1}^{\infty}M_{\lambda}

can be easily seen to be a Cantor set;
we call it
a self-similar Antoine’s Necklace generated by

(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})
.
For each
i=1,\ldots,k
the piece

\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\cap T_{i}
equals

S_{i}(\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}))
, hence it
is geometrically similar to
\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})
.

A self-similar Antoine’s Necklace will be called
regular

if the simple chain
T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}
in
T
is regular.
(In particular,
k
is even, and
the similarity coefficients of all
S_{i}
’s are equal.)
See Fig.
2
.

Theorem 2.6

Let
\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
self-similar Antoine’s necklace,
and let
s_{i}
be the similarity coefficient of
S_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
.
If
s_{1}^{2}+\ldots+s_{k}^{2}<1
,
then

p_{\Pi}\bigl(\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\bigr)
is a connected one-dimensional set
for each plane
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

In particular, a regular self-similar Antoine’s necklace

\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{2m})

with
2ms^{2}<1
is a
(3,2,1)
-set;
here
s
is the similitude coefficient of
S_{i}
’s.

Below we will return to this result
from a more general point of view, see Theorem
3.2
.

Two points should be discussed.
The first one: do regular self-similar Antoine’s necklaces exist?
(Equivalently, can one construct
a standard solid torus
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

together with a regular simple chain
T_{1},\ldots,T_{2m}
in it such that each

is similar to

If “yes”, the second one: do regular self-similar Antoine’s necklaces with

exist?

If
T_{1},\ldots,T_{2m}
is a regular simple chain in
T
,
and

’s are similar to
T
, then
the numbers
\frac{r_{T}}{R_{T}}
,

, and

should satisfy several inequalities,

[

]
,
[
28
]
; it seems that no exact description of solutions exist.
A picture representing a regular self-similar Antoine’s necklace with

can be found in
[

, p. 294]
.
Using numerical methods,
it is shown in
[

]
that

for any regular self-similar Antoine’s necklace; and a picture for

is given.
For

, existence of
regular self-similar Antoine’s necklaces follows from

[

, p. 146]
, where computer software was also used.
Given a standard solid torus
T

with
\frac{r_{T}}{R_{T}}
sufficiently small,

seems plausible that for all sufficiently large integers

2m

I have not find an exact statement in the literature.
More or less close assertions can be found without proofs
in
[

(“It can be verified

, there exist

- not too big and not too small - and corresponding thicknesses of

such that…”; formulaes and a picture follow saying that the standard

contains a regular simple chain of

Theorem 2.7

For a standard solid torus

,
the following are equivalent:

(1)

;

(2) for each sufficiently large integer

Now let us discuss

Are we possible to satisfy it? Yes.
Briefly speaking, the similarity coefficient

Let
\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{2m})\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

A generalization using Hausdorff measure

Definition 3.1

A zero-dimensional compact set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
tame

if there exists
a homeomorphism
h
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

onto itself such that

h(X)
is a subset
of a straight line in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;
otherwise,
X
is called
wild
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
each zero-dimensional compactum
is tame

[
4
,
75
, p. 87–89]

(one may also refer to

[
27
, Chap. 13]
).

Antoine proved that the necklaces he described are wild

[
4
,
80
, p. 93]
.
A later proof using fundamental group can
be found in
[
27
, Sect. 18]
or
[
14
, 2.1]
.

Antoine’s construction was generalized to higher dimensions by W.A. Blankinship and
by A.A. Ivanov, see
[
18
]
for references;
hence

\mathbb{R}^{n}
contains wild Cantor sets provided that

.

Theorem 3.2

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

be a wild Cantor set of a
zero
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure:

m_{n-1}(X)=0
.
(In particular, this holds if the Hausdorff dimension
\dim_{H}X<n-1
.)
Then
X
is an
(n,n-1,n-2)
-set.

For examples to this theorem, see Remark
3.4
and Corollary
3.6
.

Theorem
3.2
covers Theorem
2.6
except connectedness of projections
because of the following:

Statement 3.3

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
self-similar Antoine’s necklace,
and let
s_{i}
be the similarity coefficient of
S_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
.
If
s_{1}^{2}+\ldots+s_{k}^{2}<1
, then
m_{2}(\mathcal{A})=0
.

Statement
3.3
follows from Moran’s theorem,
see

[
29
, p. 600]
,
[
22
, 5.3(1)]
;
we give a short proof for our simple case.

Proof of Statement 1.

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathcal{A}\subset M_{\lambda}
,
see Definition
2.4
.
We can assume (zooming if necessary) that
\operatorname{diam}T=1
.
Now
\mathcal{A}
is covered by
the union of
k^{\lambda}
sets

S_{i_{1}}\circ S_{i_{2}}\circ\ldots\circ S_{i_{\lambda}}(T)
, where

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\lambda})\in\{1,\ldots,k\}^{\lambda}
,
and

\operatorname{diam}S_{i_{1}}\circ S_{i_{2}}\circ\ldots\circ S_{i_{\lambda}}(T)=s_{i_{1}}\cdot s_{i_{2}}\cdot\ldots\cdot s_{i_{\lambda}}.

We have

\sum\limits_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\lambda})\in\{1,\ldots,k\}^{\lambda}}\Bigl(\operatorname{diam}S_{i_{1}}\circ S_{i_{2}}\circ\ldots\circ S_{i_{\lambda}}(T)\Bigr)^{2}\leqslant

\leqslant\sum\limits_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\lambda})\in\{1,\ldots,k\}^{\lambda}}\bigl(s_{i_{1}}\cdot s_{i_{2}}\cdot\ldots\cdot s_{i_{\lambda}}\bigr)^{2}=\bigl(s_{1}^{2}+\ldots+s_{k}^{2}\bigr)^{\lambda}.

This tends to zero as
\lambda\to\infty
,
hence the
2
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
m_{2}(\mathcal{A})=0
.

\square

Remark 3.4

For each
s
with
1\leqslant s\leqslant 3
, T.B. Rushing
[

described a wild Cantor set

Theorem

Rushing’s scheme is too

the aim of this paper
is to give an example which is as simple as possible.

Proof of Theorem 1.

We already derived Theorem
2.6
(except connectedness
of projections) from Theorem

3.2
.
Here we give an independent “elementary” proof.

For brevity, denote

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(T;S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})
.
By

[
4
,
80-82
, p. 93-96]
,
no topological
2
-sphere separates a necklace.
Hence all projections of a necklace are connected (see

[
18
, Stat. 2]
for details).

Let
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be any plane.
It is clear that
p_{\Pi}\mathcal{A}
contains more than one point;
so it remains to show that

\dim p_{\Pi}\mathcal{A}\neq 2
.

A subset
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

is
n
-dimensional iff it contains an
n
-ball

[
21
, Thm. IV 3]
.
Therefore it suffices to prove that

\lambda_{2}(p_{\Pi}\mathcal{A})=0
,
where
\lambda_{2}
denotes planar Lebesgue measure.
Suppose that
\lambda_{2}(p_{\Pi}\mathcal{A})>0
.
(In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n
-dimensional Lebesgue and Hausdorff measures differ
by a constant multiple. We thus may use
m_{2}
instead of
\lambda_{2}
.
Here I decided to use
\lambda_{2}
due to its wider popularity.)

For each non-empty compact subset
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

define a real number

\alpha(X)=\sup\{\lambda_{2}(p_{L}X)\ |\ L\text{ is a plane in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\}.

Any similarity transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a composition of
a scaling and an isometry.
As a corollary, for any
similarity transformation

S:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and any non-empty compactum
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

we have

\alpha(S(X))=s^{2}\cdot\alpha(X)
, where
s
is the similarity coefficient of
S
.

By assumption,
\alpha(\mathcal{A})>0
.
Take a positive number

\varepsilon\leqslant\alpha(\mathcal{A})-\bigl(s_{1}^{2}+\ldots+s_{k}^{2}\bigr)\cdot\alpha(\mathcal{A}).

Let
L(\varepsilon)
be any plane such that

\alpha(\mathcal{A})-\varepsilon<\lambda_{2}(p_{L(\varepsilon)}\mathcal{A})\leqslant\alpha(\mathcal{A})
.
Recall that

\mathcal{A}=S_{1}(\mathcal{A})\cup\ldots\cup S_{k}(\mathcal{A}).

We have

\alpha(\mathcal{A})-\varepsilon<\lambda_{2}(p_{L(\varepsilon)}\mathcal{A})=\lambda_{2}\Bigl(p_{L(\varepsilon)}\bigl(S_{1}(\mathcal{A})\cup\ldots\cup S_{k}(\mathcal{A})\bigr)\Bigr)\leqslant

\leqslant\lambda_{2}\Bigl(p_{L(\varepsilon)}\bigl(S_{1}(\mathcal{A})\bigr)\Bigr)+\ldots+\lambda_{2}\Bigl(p_{L(\varepsilon)}\bigl(S_{k}(\mathcal{A})\bigr)\Bigr)\leqslant

\leqslant\alpha\bigl(S_{1}(\mathcal{A})\bigr)+\ldots+\alpha\bigl(S_{k}(\mathcal{A})\bigr)=\bigl(s_{1}^{2}+\ldots+s_{k}^{2}\bigr)\cdot\alpha(\mathcal{A}).

This contradicts the choice of
\varepsilon
.
Thus

\lambda_{2}(p_{\Pi}\mathcal{A})=0
,
and the proof is finished.

\square

Let us show that the distance

Take any points
P_{1}\in C_{B_{1}}
,

(A-R_{B}\cos u)\sin v=0\\
