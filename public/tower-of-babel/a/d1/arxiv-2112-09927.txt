Abstract
The goal of this paper is to establish a global well-posedness, cone condition and loss of regularity for singular hyperbolic equations with coefficients in L 1 ​ ( ( 0 , T ] , C ∞ ​ ( ℝ n ) ) ∩ C 1 ​ ( ( 0 , T ] , C ∞ ​ ( ℝ n ) ) L^{1}((0,T];C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\cap C^{1}((0,T];C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})) and Cauchy data in an appropriate Sobolev space tailored to a metric on the phase space. The coefficients are unbounded near the singular hyperplane t = 0 t=0 and polynomially growing as | x | → ∞ . |x|\to\infty. The singular behavior is characterized by the blow-up rate of the coefficients and their first t t -derivatives near t = 0 . t=0. In order to study the interplay of the singularity in t t and unboundedness in x x , we consider a class of metrics on the phase space. Our methodology relies on the use of the Planck function associated to the metric to subdivide the extended phase and to define an infinite order pseudodifferential operator for the conjugation. We also give some counterexamples. 2
Tools

In this section we introduce the main tools of this paper. The first part is devoted to a class of metrics on the phase space that govern the geometry of the symbols in our consideration while the second for properties of structure functions. In the third part, we define Sobolev spaces associated to the metric. Lastly, we device a localization technique based on the Planck function associated to the metric.

2.1
Our Choice of Metric on the Phase Space

In this section, we review some notation and terminology used in the study of metrics on the phase space, see
[
9
, Chapter 2]
and
[
10
]
for details. Let us denote by
\sigma(X,Y)
the standard symplectic form on
T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
: if
X=(x,\xi)
and
Y=(y,\eta)
, then
\sigma
is given by

\sigma(X,Y)=\xi\cdot y-\eta\cdot x.

We can identify
\sigma
with the isomorphism of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
such that
\sigma^{*}=-\sigma
, with the formula
\sigma(X,Y)=\langle\sigma X,Y\rangle
. Consider a Riemannian metric
g_{X}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
(which is a measurable function of
X
) to which we associate the dual metric
g_{X}^{\sigma}
by

g_{X}^{\sigma}(Y)=\sup_{0\neq Y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\frac{\langle\sigma Y,Y^{\prime}\rangle^{2}}{g_{X}(Y^{\prime})},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }Y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}.

Considering
g_{X}
as a matrix associated to positive definite quadratic form on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
,
g_{X}^{\sigma}=\sigma^{*}g_{X}^{-1}\sigma
.
We define the Planck function
[
10
]
which plays a crucial role in the development of pseudodifferential calculus as

h_{g}(x,\xi):=\sup_{0\neq Y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\Bigg(\frac{g_{X}(Y)}{g_{X}^{\sigma}(Y)}\Bigg)^{1/2}.

The uncertainty principle is quantified as the upper bound
h_{g}(x,\xi)\leq 1
. In the following, we often make use of the strong uncertainty principle, that is, for some
\kappa>0
, we have

h_{g}(x,\xi)\leq(1+|x|+|\xi|)^{-\kappa},\hskip 10.00002pt(x,\xi)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}.

In general, we use the metrics of the form

g_{\Phi,k}^{\rho,r}=\left(\frac{\langle\xi\rangle_{k}^{\rho_{2}}}{\Phi(x)^{\tilde{\rho}_{1}}}\right)^{2}|dx|^{2}+\left(\frac{\Phi(x)^{\tilde{\rho}_{2}}}{\langle\xi\rangle_{k}^{\rho_{1}}}\right)^{2}|d\xi|^{2}.

(2.1)

Here
\rho=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
,
\tilde{\rho}=(\tilde{\rho}_{1},\tilde{\rho}_{2})
for
\rho_{j},\tilde{\rho}_{j}\in[0,1]
,
j=1,2
are such that
0\leq\rho_{2}<\rho_{1}\leq 1
and
0\leq\tilde{\rho}_{2}<\tilde{\rho}_{1}\leq 1
.
The Planck function associated to the metric in (
2.1
) is
\Phi(x)^{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}\langle\xi\rangle_{k}^{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}.

For the sake of calculations arising in the development of symbol calculus related to the metrics

\displaystyle\Phi(x+y)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(x)+\Phi(y),

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{\beta}\Phi(x)|

\displaystyle\Phi(ax)

2.3
Sobolev Spaces

We now introduce the Sobolev space related to the metric

.

The Sobolev space

6
Cone Condition

Existence and uniqueness follow from the a priori estimate established in the previous section. It now remains to prove the existence of cone of dependence.

We note here that the
L^{1}
integrability of the characteristics plays a crucial role in arriving at the finite propagation speed. The implications of the discussion in
[
16
, Section 2.3 & 2.5]
to the global setting suggest that if the Cauchy data in (
3.1
) is such that
f\equiv 0
and
f_{1},f_{2}
are supported in the ball
|x|\leq R
, then the solution to Cauchy problem (
3.1
) is supported in the ball
|x|\leq R+c^{*}\omega(x)t^{1-\frac{p}{2}}
. The constant
c^{*}
is such that the quantity
c^{*}\omega(x)t^{-\frac{p}{2}}
dominates the characteristic roots, i.e.,

c^{*}=\sup\Big\{\sqrt{a(t,x,\xi)}\omega(x)^{-1}t^{\frac{p}{2}}:(t,x,\xi)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{x}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\xi},\>|\xi|=1\Big\}.

(6.1)

Note that the support of the solution increases as
|x|
increases since
\omega(x)
is monotone increasing function of
|x|
.

In the following we prove the cone condition for the Cauchy problem
(\ref{eq1})
. Let
K(x^{0},t^{0})
denote the cone with the vertex
(x^{0},t^{0})
:

K(x^{0},t^{0})=\{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:|x-x^{0}|\leq c^{*}\omega(x)(t^{0}-t)^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\}.

Observe that the slope of the cone is anisotropic, that is, it varies with both
x
and

.

Proposition 6.1
.

The Cauchy problem (
3.1
) has a cone dependence, that is, if

(6.3)

Proof.

7
Counterexamples

In this section, we show by a set of counterexamples how the lower order terms of operator

\omega(x)=\Phi(x)=1,\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}
in (
1.2
).

Following example shows that one can encounter finite loss when the coefficients of lower order terms are not
L^{1}
integrable.

Example 7.1
.

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2t}\left(\partial_{t}-(4m+1)\partial_{x}\right)\right)u(t,x)=0,\\
&\;u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\;\;\partial_{t}u(0,x)=(4m+1)\partial_{x}u_{0}(x),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(7.1)

for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.
Note that the above problem corresponds to the case
p=q=0
and
r=1
in (
3.2
). The solution to the above Cauchy problem is given by

u(t,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}C_{j}^{(m)}t^{j}\partial_{x}^{j}u_{0}(x+t),

for
C_{j}^{(m)}
of the form

C_{0}=1,\hskip 10.00002ptC_{j}^{(m)}=\frac{(-2)^{j}}{j!}\;\frac{(m)_{j}}{\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{j}},\hskip 10.00002ptj\geq 1,

where
(y)_{j},y\in{\mathbb{R}},
is the
j^{th}
falling factorial of
y

[
18
]
given by

(y)_{j}=y(y-1)\cdots(y-j+1).

For example, when
m=2,

u(t,x)
is of the form

u(t,x)=u_{0}(x+t)+8t\partial_{x}u_{0}(x+t)+\frac{16}{3}t^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0}(x+t).

We observe loss of derivatives: if

then

where

is the usual Sobolev space with

From Theorem

Example 7.2

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}-\frac{2}{t}\partial_{x}\right)u(t,x)=0\\
&u(0,x)=0,\;\partial_{t}u(0,x)=u_{0}(x).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(7.2)

This corresponds to the case
p=q=0
and
r=1
in (
1.2
). The solution to the above Cauchy problem is given by

The following example demonstrates that when the coefficient of the top order term is oscillatory but in

and that of the lower order term is in

Example 7.3

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\big(2+\sin\sqrt{t}\big)^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}-\frac{\cos\sqrt{t}}{2\sqrt{t}}\partial_{x}\right)u(t,x)=0\\
&u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\;\partial_{t}u(0,x)=2\partial_{x}u_{0}(x).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(7.3)

This corresponds to the case
p=0,q=\frac{1}{2}

in (
1.2

The following example demonstrates that one may encounter nonuniqueness when the lower order terms are not
