Weak solutions and singular limits for a compressible fluid-structure interaction problem with slip boundary conditions
Yadong Liu ∗ , Sourav Mitra † , and Šárka Nečasová ‡

Abstract
. We study a system describing the compressible barotropic fluids interacting with (visco) elastic solid shell/plate. In particular, the elastic structure is part of the moving boundary of the fluid, and the Navier-slip type boundary condition is taken into account. Depending on the reference geometry (flat or not), we show the existence of weak solutions to the coupled system provided the adiabatic exponent satisfies γ > 12 7 \gamma>\frac{12}{7} without damping and γ > 3 2 \gamma>\frac{3}{2} with structure damping, utilizing the domain extension and regularization approximation. Moreover, via a modified relative entropy method in time-dependent domains, we give a rigorous justification of the incompressible inviscid limit of the compressible fluid-structure interaction problem with a flat reference geometry, in the regime of low Mach number, high Reynolds number, and well-prepared initial data. As a byproduct, with a fixed Reynolds number, we derive the incompressible limit without extra assumption. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result concerning the singular limit problem for compressible fluids interacting with elastic structures.

Introduction

This contribution considers a fluid-structure interaction problem, which arises in many fields, such as aerodynamics, hemodynamics, and engineering. More precisely, the model is a 3D-2D coupling problem, consisting of 3D compressible fluids and a 2D thin elastic structure.

1.2.
Literature Review

In the past few decades, mathematical analysis in fluid dynamics has developed significantly, leading to a wealth of literature. Particularly, in the last twenty years, extensive exploration has been conducted on the interaction between fluid and elastic structures.

The investigation of weak solutions to incompressible Navier–Stokes interacting with elastic structures (moving boundary) starts from the beginning of this century. The first result went back to Chambolle–Desjardins–Esteban–Grandmont
[
9
]
, where they proved the existence of weak solutions. The solid equation there is assumed to be of fourth order damped, which was later improved by Grandmont
[
29
]
without damping.
Since Muha–Čanić
[
50
]
, the existence of weak solutions was extended to the case with a one-dimensional cylindrical Koiter shell model. A time discretization method combined with an operator splitting method was proposed to prove the existence of weak solutions, inspired by numerical studies. See also
[
51
]
for the extension to the Navier-slip boundary condition. In the meanwhile, Lengeler–Růžička
[
40
]
considered the general geometry via Hanzawa transformation, by which the weak solution was showed to be existed by a regularization procedure and a set-valued fixed-point argument. Recently, Muha–Schwarzacher
[
52
]
took a nonlinear Koiter shell model into account, for which they explored more spatial regularity of the displacement hidden in the model, such that the existence of weak solutions is available. We would like to mention that there was an interesting work
[
4
]
, which took the variational viewpoint to prove the existence of weak solutions for the bulk fluid-solid interactions.
Concerning the well-posedness, Cheng–Coutand–Shkoller
[
10
]
studied the Navier–Stokes equations interacting with a nonlinear elastic biofluid shell via higher-order energy methods, obtaining the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions to such model, also in Cheng–Shkoller
[
11
]
. The strong solutions of the (regularized) linear model were investigated by Beirão da Veiga
[

proved the strong well-posedness of an incompressible fluid-structure interaction problem with the Navier-slip boundary condition. Kukavica–Tuffaha

showed the local well-posedness of a model coupling the

With the development of effective techniques for handling fluid-structure interaction problems, the work on compressible coupled problems arose. The first result of global weak solutions started from Breit–Schwarzacher
[

In the study of compressible fluids, a particularly intriguing problem involves singular limits – specifically, the incompressible and inviscid limit, where a compressible viscous fluid becomes effectively incompressible and inviscid. This occurs in the regime of low Mach number and high Reynolds number (with

Moreover, studying the incompressible inviscid limit enhances our understanding of how solutions to the Navier–Stokes equations behave as viscosity tends to zero, and how compressible fluids transition to incompressibility, especially near solid boundaries.
There are tons of literature in this direction and we only want to mention several typical ones.

The second way is to use the dimensional analysis, see Klein

1.3.
Main results

Let us now state the main results of the article. The first theorem is the existence of weak solutions to the compressible fluid-structure interaction model with the Navier-slip boundary condition.

Theorem 1.4

(Weak solution, proved in Section
3
)
.

Assume that the initial data satisfies (
3.1
). Let either
\nu_{s}>0
,
\gamma>\frac{3}{2}
or
\Omega\in\mathbbm{2}
,
\nu_{s}=0
,
\gamma>\frac{12}{7}
. Then there exists

and a weak solution to (

) in the sense of Definition
3.2
. Moreover, either
T=+\infty
or the domain
\Omega^{w}(t)
degenerates in the sense of self-contact as

.

Remark 1.5
.

Here different restrictions on elastic damping
\nu_{s}
and adiabatic exponent
\gamma
come essentially from geometric settings and boundary conditions. In the case of general reference domain with
\nu_{s}=0
, it is still unknown about the existence of weak solutions to such models with
Navier-slip boundary condition
.

Remark 1.6
.

It is noticed that the weak-strong uniqueness principle for the weak solutions constructed in Theorem
1.4
is addressed in a subsequent manuscript
[
44
]
by authors.

The second result concerns the singular limit problems in fluid dynamics. In particular, after certain rescalings, we justify the limit system rigorously in the regime of
low Mach number and high Reynolds number
, i.e., incompressible inviscid limit. The following is an abbreviated version.
For a precise description of the limit system and convergent rate, see Section
4
and Theorem
4.8
.

Theorem 1.7

(Incompressible Inviscid limit)
.

Assume that the initial data is well-prepared. Let
\Omega\in\mathbbm{2}
,
\nu_{s}>0
,
\gamma>\frac{3}{2}
, and
(\varrho,\mathbf{u},w)
be a weak solution to the compressible fluid-structure interaction problem (
1.1
) constructed in Theorem
1.4
, with
w
satisfying an additional regularity. Then up to a certain time
T>0
, the weak solution
(\varrho,\mathbf{u},w)
converges to the strong solution of an incompressible Euler-plate interaction system, as the Mach number goes to zero and the Reynolds number tends to infinity. Moreover, we have a certain convergent rate depending on the initial data.

Remark 1.8
.

In this result, we only consider the case
\nu_{s}>0
. This is essential as in the limit passage we have to control the error term containing
\nabla\partial_{t}w
, cf. (
4.27
), whose regularity is not available from the a priori energy estimate. On the other hand, with the inviscid limit, i.e.,
\nu\to 0
, one cannot employ any regularity of
\mathbf{u}
, which means we may not gain the regularity of
\partial_{t}w
via the kinematic condition (
1.2
).

Remark 1.9
.

Note that we require an additional regularity assumption on the weak solution
w
, cf. (
4.18
). Indeed, it is necessary in the current framework. Since the domain depends on
w
, we later carry out a transform of the strong limit solution back to the weak domain, which causes much trouble as the a priori regularity of
w
is not sufficient to define a uniform Lipschitz domain. Consequently, a lot of techniques do not apply and no matter how smooth the strong solution is, it will eventually have a relatively low regularity comparable to
w
, cf. Corollary
4.6
.

Moreover, here the incompressible inviscid limit is investigated, meaning the uniform regularity of the weak solution is much lower. This is another reason we require the additional assumption of
w
, cf. Remark
4.13
.

In a related context of incompressible fluid-structure interaction problems, an extra Lipschitz regularity was assumed by Breit–Mensah–Schwarzacher–Su
[
5
]
to deal with the so-called Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin condition.

Remark 1.10

(Incompressible limit)
.

If we only consider the low Mach number limit, the convergence holds as well. Namely, the compressible fluid-structure interaction system will converge to a system coupled the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations and a viscoelastic plate equation with slip boundary conditions. In particular, one can show that the limit passage is valid for
\gamma>3
without any regularity assumption. cf. Theorem
4.14
.

1.4.
Technical Discussions

Concerning the
existence of weak solutions
, it is the ‘bad’ elastic boundary that causes much trouble in the approximate scheme. The idea would be either regularizing the elastic boundary (cf.
[
40
,
6
]
), or approximating the boundary with a lager smooth domain (cf.
[
33
,
45
]
) in the literature. In this work, we will complete the proof via the argument in
[
45
]
, cf. Section
3
. Note that the domain now is not uniform-in-time Lipschitz anymore, due to the low regularity of the displacement. Most of the steps of the proof (cf. Section
3.1
) go through quite well compared to
[
6
,
45
]
, while the significant differences come out, encountering the
Navier-slip
boundary conditions.
Particularly, here it is not very convincing to show the existence of weak solutions in the general geometry without structure damping (
\nu_{s}=0
), as we have to recover the nonlinear kinematic condition on the boundary.
Instead, we differ from the type of reference geometries (flat or not) and include the structure viscosity to compensate for the boundary conditions. More precisely, in flat geometry,
\Omega=\Gamma\times[0,1]
, we observe that (
1.1d
) is actually can be rewritten as
\partial_{t}w=(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w})\circ\Phi_{w}
, cf. (
1.2
). Then one gains more regularity of
\partial_{t}w
by the multiplication of Sobolev–Slobodeckij spaces, as well as the trace regularity of
\mathbf{u}
. This essentially facilitates further analysis for
additional spatial regularities for
w
and for
equi-integrability of
\varrho
near the boundary
, cf. Proposition
3.9
and Remark
3.8
. Note that the
additional spatial regularity
is required here to recover the kinematic condition, which involves the deformed normal vector
\mathbf{n}^{w}
, depending nonlinearly on
\nabla w
. However, in general geometry, we are only able to use (
1.1d
), the
Navier-slip
kinematic condition
(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w})\circ\Phi_{w}=(\partial_{t}w\mathbf{n})\cdot(\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\Phi_{w})
, by which no more regularity for
\partial_{t}w
can be achieved. Hence, to compensate for the loss, we require
\nu_{s}>0
, i.e.,
\partial_{t}w\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Gamma))
, which plays the same role as above. In this case, to prove the equi-integrability of
\varrho
near the boundary (Lemma
3.7
), one can use this regularity directly and the range of
\gamma
can be relaxed to
\gamma>\frac{3}{2}
(compared to the case
\nu_{s}=0
with
\gamma>\frac{12}{7}
).

In order to investigate the
incompressible inviscid limit
(Mach number
\varepsilon\to 0
, reciprocal of the Reynolds number
\nu\to 0
, simultaneously), we employ a modified
relative entropy method
, which is usually valid for fixed domains. In our case, one shall compare the weak solutions and the limit strong solution that are in fact defined in different domains. If we perform the usual transformation
\mathbf{\Psi}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\eta}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}^{-1}
that simply composes two natural mappings, cf. (
2.1
), (
4.17
) below, the transformed velocity is not divergence-free anymore, while the limit velocity should be solenoidal doe to low Mach number. Moreover, there will be an error term on the boundary by transforming, as the
Navier-slip boundary condition
depends on
\mathbf{n}^{w}
, which is not invariant along with the transform above.
To overcome the issue, one needs to correct the transform both on the boundary and in the bulk.
Thus, a
Piola transforma
based on
\mathbf{\Psi}
is proposed, inspired by
[
30
,
56
]
. Precisely, for the transformed velocity
\mathbf{v}
one takes the form of
\mathbf{v}=(\det\nabla\mathbf{\Psi})\nabla\mathbf{\Psi}^{-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi})
for
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
defined in
\Omega^{\eta}
with respect to
\eta
. In this case, the
Piola identity
is valid
\Div\big((\det\nabla\mathbf{\Psi})\nabla\mathbf{\Psi}^{-1}\big)=0
and hence the transformed variable is divergence-free (cf. (
4.24
)). In the meantime, in view of the transformation between outer normal vectors, it is exactly satisfied with a factor
(\det\nabla\mathbf{\Psi})\nabla\mathbf{\Psi}^{-\top}
(cf. (
4.25
)).
Note that the high Reynolds number limit prevents us from cooking the argument up with the regularity of the velocity (of weak solution).
Though we consider a flat reference geometry here and hence
\partial_{t}w
could inherit the trace regularity of
\mathbf{u}
formally, it is not applied as no spatial regularity of
\mathbf{u}
is available. To close the estimates, it is demanded the high integrability of the error terms, cf. (
4.29
), which contains the Jacobian and the deformation gradient of
\mathbf{\Psi}
by the Piola transform. In this case, one may require more regularity of the weak solutions, which essentially depends on the elastic damping
\nu_{s}>0
and additional regularities assumed, cf. Remark
4.13
.

Note that the boundary is missing in the fluid subproblem. So we penalize the kinematic boundary condtion as

\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma^{w}(t)}\big((\mathbf{u}-\partial_{t}w\mathbf{n}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w}\big)\big((\bm{\varphi}-\psi\mathbf{n}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t,

for
\kappa>0
, initiated by
[

]
. Further, we apply a fundamental lemma which roughly says that if the initial density is extended by zero to a large domain

, also the density is zero on
B\setminus\Omega^{w}(t)
in the case of the smooth domain and sufficiently regular density, see
[
33
,
45
]
.

(2)

Uniform bounds and equi-integrability
. Once solving the approximate system, one may derive the uniform both in
\kappa
and
\delta
estimates for
(\varrho^{\delta},\mathbf{u}^{\delta},w^{\delta})
(only labeled with
\delta
for simplicity). Depending on the presence of structure damping, i.e.,
\nu_{s}>0
or
\nu_{s}=0
, we derive uniform estimates with different restrictions. In particular, the equi-integrability of the density
\varrho^{\delta}
in this case replies strongly on the adiabatic exponent, due to the
Navier-slip boundary condition
, cf. Lemma

and Remark

.

(3)

Passing to the limit as
\kappa,\delta\rightarrow 0
.
Based on the uniform (in
\delta
and
\kappa
) bounds, we are hence able to extract subsequences to justify the limit functions, as
\kappa=\delta\rightarrow 0

, which finally completes the proof of Theorem

. Note that again one shall distinguish different reference geometries and elastic damping

, to recover boundary data, cf. Proposition

and Section

, resulting from the presence of the

.

In the following, we will discuss some necessary details about each step above. For the full argument, we refer to

]
.

3.2.
Extended problem with artificial pressure

Following the extension argument in
[
22
,
33
,
45
]
, for a fixed
0<\kappa\ll 1
we approximate the viscosity coefficients
\mu,\lambda
by

\mu_{\kappa}\coloneqq\chi_{\kappa}\mu,\quad\lambda_{\kappa}\coloneqq\chi_{\kappa}\lambda,

where the function
\chi_{\kappa}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T];[0,1])
fulfills

\displaystyle 0<\kappa<\chi_{\kappa}\leq 1\text{ in }B\times[0,T],

\displaystyle{\chi_{\kappa}}_{|\Omega^{w}(t)}=1\text{ for all }t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle\left\|\chi_{\kappa}\right\|_{L^{p}((B\times(0,T))\setminus Q_{T}^{w})}\leq C\kappa\text{ for some }p\geq 1,

\displaystyle\text{the mapping }w\mapsto\chi_{\kappa}\text{ is Lipschitz}.

Moreover, for
\delta>0
we introduce an approximate pressure
p_{\delta}(\varrho)
with an artificial pressure
\delta\varrho^{\beta}
,

p_{\delta}(\varrho)=\varrho^{\gamma}+\delta\varrho^{\beta}.

The initial data
\varrho_{0}
and
\mathbf{m}_{0}
are extended to
\varrho_{0,\delta}
,
(\varrho\mathbf{u})_{0,\delta}
in
B
such that

(3.8)

\begin{gathered}\varrho_{0,\delta}\geq 0,\ \varrho_{0,\delta}\not\equiv 0,\ {\varrho_{0,\delta}}_{|\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega^{w_{0}}}=0,\ \int_{B}\left(\varrho_{0,\delta}^{\gamma}+\delta\varrho_{0,\delta}^{\beta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C,\\
\varrho_{0,\delta}\to\varrho_{0}\text{ in }L^{\gamma}(\Omega^{w_{0}}),\ (\varrho\mathbf{u})_{0,\delta}\to\mathbf{m}_{0}\text{ in }L^{1}(\Omega^{w_{0}}),\text{ as }\delta\to 0,\\
\int_{B}\frac{1}{\varrho_{0,\delta}}\left|(\varrho\mathbf{u})_{0,\delta}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C,\ \int_{B}\frac{\left|(\varrho\mathbf{u})_{0,\delta}\right|^{2}}{\varrho_{0,\delta}}\,\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega^{w_{0}}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{m}_{0}\right|^{2}}{\varrho_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}x,\text{ as }\delta\to 0.\end{gathered}

Now we give the definition of weak solutions to the extended problem with pressure regularization as follows.

Definition 3.5
.

We say that
(\varrho,\mathbf{u},w)
is a weak solution to the extended compressible Navier–Stokes equations interacting with a viscoelastic structure with the Navier-slip boundary in
B

if the following conditions hold:

(1)

(\varrho,\mathbf{u},w)
satisfies

(3.9)

\displaystyle\varrho\geq 0,\text{ a.e. in }B,\ \varrho\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\beta}(B)),\ p_{\delta}(\varrho)\in L^{1}((0,T)\times B),

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(B)),\ \varrho\left|\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(B)),

(3.11)

\displaystyle w\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Gamma))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma)),\ \partial_{t}w\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma)).

(2)

The renormalized continuity equation

(3.12)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\varrho\mathcal{B}(\varrho)\varrho(\partial_{t}\varphi

\displaystyle+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}b(\varrho)\Div\mathbf{u}\varphi(t,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\varrho(t,\cdot)\mathcal{B}(\varrho(t,\cdot))\varphi(t,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{B}\varrho_{0,\delta}\mathcal{B}(\varrho_{0,\delta})\varphi(0,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x,

holds for all
t\in[0,T]
,
\varphi\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and
b\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)\cap C[0,\infty)
such that
b(0)=0
with
\mathcal{B}(\varrho)=\mathcal{B}(1)+\int_{1}^{\varrho}\frac{b(z)}{z^{2}}\mathrm{d}z
.

(3)

The coupled momentum equation is satisfied in the sense of

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\Big(\varrho\mathbf{u}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\varphi}+(\varrho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}):\nabla\bm{\varphi}+p_{\delta}(\varrho)\Div\bm{\varphi}-\mathbb{S}_{\kappa}(\nabla\mathbf{u}):\nabla\bm{\varphi}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{t}w\partial_{t}\psi-\Delta w\Delta\psi-\nu_{s}\nabla\partial_{t}w\cdot\nabla\psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau+\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma^{w}(\tau)}\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}^{w}}\cdot\bm{\varphi}_{\bm{\tau}^{w}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.13)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma^{w}(\tau)}\big((\mathbf{u}-\partial_{t}w\mathbf{n}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w}\big)\big((\bm{\varphi}-\psi\mathbf{n}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{B}(\varrho\mathbf{u})\cdot\bm{\varphi}(t,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}w\psi(t,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}-\int_{B}(\varrho\mathbf{u})_{0,\delta}\cdot\bm{\varphi}(0,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}w_{0,1}\psi(0,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2},

for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\bm{\varphi}\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\psi\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\Gamma)
satisfying
(\psi\mathbf{n})\cdot(\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w})=\mathrm{tr}_{\Gamma^{w}}\bm{\varphi}\cdot(\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w})
on
\Gamma_{T}
. Here the tensor
\mathbb{S}_{\kappa}
is defined by
\mathbb{S}_{\kappa}(\nabla\mathbf{u})=\mu_{\kappa}(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\top})+\lambda_{\kappa}\Div\mathbf{u}\mathbb{I}
.

(4)

The energy inequality

\displaystyle\int_{B}\left(\frac{1}{2}\varrho\left|\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}+\frac{p_{\delta}(\varrho)}{\gamma-1}\right)(t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\partial_{t}w\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left|\Delta w\right|^{2}\right)(t)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\mathbb{S}_{\kappa}(\nabla\mathbf{u}):\nabla\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma^{w}(\tau)}\left|(\mathbf{u}-\partial_{t}w\mathbf{n}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma^{w}(\tau)}\left|(\mathbf{u}-\partial_{t}w\mathbf{n}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}^{-1})_{\bm{\tau}^{w}}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau+\nu_{s}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}\left|\nabla\partial_{t}w\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.14)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\leq\int_{\Omega^{w_{0}}}\left(\frac{1}{2\varrho_{0,\delta}}\left|(\varrho\mathbf{u})_{0,\delta}\right|^{2}+\frac{p_{\delta}(\varrho_{0,\delta})}{\gamma-1}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|w_{0,1}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left|\Delta w_{0}\right|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2},

holds for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
.

The aim is to prove the existence of the approximate solutions.

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume that the initial data satisfies (
3.1
) and (
3.8
). Then there exists
T>0
and a weak solution
(\varrho,\mathbf{u},w)
to the approximate system in the sense of Definition
3.5
.

Proof.

The proof relies on a time discretization scheme, together with an operator splitting method, which was employed in
[
33
,
45
]
for other related fluid-structure interaction problems. For the splitting method, we decouple the problem into two subproblems: fluid and structure, then we proceed with the method from
[
33
,
45
]
and passing with time layer to zero we get the existence of the approximate solution. The differences result from the Navier-slip boundary, which is not an issue to prove the existence of weak solutions to the extended regularized problem. For simplicity, we omit the details here.
∎

3.3.
Uniform estimates

In this section, we are going to derive uniform in
\kappa
and
\delta
estimates so that one is able to further pass to the limit as
\kappa=\delta\to 0
step by step, i.e., the extension limit and the vanishing artificial pressure limit. Let
(\varrho^{\delta},\mathbf{u}^{\delta},w^{\delta})
be a pair of approximate solutions constructed in Theorem

, for which we suppress the dependence on
\kappa
for simplicity. Then in view of (

), we conclude that following uniform (in
\delta
and

\displaystyle w^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Gamma)),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma)),

\displaystyle\sqrt{\nu_{s}}\partial_{t}w^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma)),

\displaystyle\varrho^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma}(B)),

\displaystyle\delta^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\varrho^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\beta}(B)),

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varrho^{\delta}}\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(B)),

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{\delta}(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta}):\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{1}((0,T)\times B).

Moreover, a direct use of Korn’s inequality on Hölder domain (e.g.,
[
45
, Lemma 3.9]

(3.15)

\displaystyle\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega^{w^{\delta}}(t))),\text{ for }1\leq q<2,

which by Sobolev embedding implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;L^{p}(B)),\text{ for }1\leq p<6.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{T}^{w^{\delta}}}

\displaystyle\Big(\mu\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta}+(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta})^{\top}\right|^{2}+\lambda\left|\Div\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\mathbb{I}\right|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{Q_{T}^{w^{\delta}}}\Big(\mu(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta}+(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta})^{\top})+\lambda\Div\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\mathbb{I}\Big):\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{(0,T)\times B}\mathbb{S}_{\delta}(\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta}):\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\leq C,

where
C>0
does not depend on
\delta>0
. Then by
[

, one has

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

is bounded in

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})),\text{ for }1\leq p<6.

Now we derive the integrability of the density. Since the domain now is not uniformly Lipschitz due to elastic structure regularity, the usual technique of the Bogovskiĭ operator (e.g.,
[

,
53
]

(3.16)

\int_{D}\left((\varrho^{\delta})^{\gamma+\theta}+\delta(\varrho^{\delta})^{\beta+\theta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\leq C(D),

for some
\theta>0
and any
D\subset\subset\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\{t\}\times(\overline{B}\setminus\Gamma^{w^{\delta}}(t))
, where

is independent of
\delta
.
Moreover, we need to exclude the possible concentration of pressure at the moving boundary as follows:

Lemma 3.7

(Equi-integrability)
.

Let either
\nu_{s}>0
,
\gamma>\frac{3}{2}
or
\Omega\in\mathbbm{2}
,
\nu_{s}=0
,

A_{\varepsilon}\cap(\Gamma^{w^{\delta}}_{T})=\emptyset,\quad\int_{((0,T)\times B)\setminus A_{\varepsilon}}\left((\varrho^{\delta})^{\gamma}+\delta(\varrho^{\delta})^{\beta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\leq\varepsilon.

Remark 3.8
.

For the proof, we refer to
[
6
,
33
,
45
]
. This problem was first handled in
[

, Lemma 7.4]
for compressible fluid-structure interaction problems initiated by

Recently in

Following the proof of

, one can shows there is no extra restriction on

with the structure damping

In the case of

3.4.
Existence of weak solutions

Based on the uniform estimates derived in the previous section, we are now devoted to proving Theorem
1.4
by passing to the limit as
\kappa=\delta\to 0
. Up to a non-relabeled subsequence, we have

\displaystyle w^{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow w,\quad

\displaystyle\text{weakly-}*\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Gamma)),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\partial_{t}w,\quad

\displaystyle\text{weakly-}*\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma)),

\displaystyle\sqrt{\nu_{s}}\partial_{t}w^{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\sqrt{\nu_{s}}\partial_{t}w,\quad

weakly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma)),

\displaystyle\varrho^{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\varrho,\quad

\displaystyle\text{weakly-}*\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma}(B)),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{u},\quad

weakly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})),\text{ for }1\leq q<2.

Note that by a similar argument to that in
[

\varrho_{|B\setminus\Omega^{w^{\delta}}(t)}=0
for a.e.

A direct application of the Kondrachov embedding theorem and the Aubin–Lions lemma tells us

\displaystyle\rightarrow w,\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }C([0,T];H^{r}(\Gamma)),

for

.
and

\displaystyle w^{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow w,\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }C([0,T]\times\Gamma).

By definition, we know that

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{\Phi}_{w},\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }C([0,T]\times\Gamma).

Now we are going to give an additional regularity for the displacement

[

to tackle the incompressible Navier–Stokes equation interacting with a nonlinear Koiter shell, and then developed in

Proposition 3.9

(Additional spatial regularity)
.

Let

be a weak solution of the extended problem in the sense of Definition

If
\nu_{s}>0
,

.
If
\Omega\in\mathbbm{2}
,
\nu_{s}=0
,

(3.20)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\delta}\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{s,r}(\Gamma))\ \text{ for all }0<s<2-\frac{3}{r},\ \frac{3}{2}<r<2,

(3.21)

\displaystyle w^{\delta}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2+s}(\Gamma))\ \text{ for some }0<s<\frac{1}{4},

\Gamma

Proof.

For the case
\nu_{s}>0,
in view of the regularity
\partial_{t}w^{\delta}\in L^{2}(H^{1}(\Gamma))
one can adapt the arguments from
[

,

]
in a rather straight forward manner to show (

If
\nu_{s}=0
, in order to use the arguments from
[
52
,
58
]
, we need some spatial regularity of
\partial_{t}w^{\delta}
. We obtain that regularity in the following discussion by exploiting the interface coupling condition which allows to transfer the effect of fluid dissipation to the structure. Let us make use of the kinematic boundary condition (

) for

to gain regularity from velocity, i.e.,

More precisely, in view of (

\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,r}(\Gamma))

. Then by Lemma

, the multiplication property of Sobolev spaces, it follows

\|\partial_{t}w^{\delta}\|_{L_{t}^{2}W_{\Gamma}^{\sigma,r}}\leq C\|\mathbf{u}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}\|_{L_{t}^{2}W_{\Gamma}^{1-\frac{1}{r},r}}\|\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}W_{\Gamma}^{1,r}}<\infty,

with any

and
\frac{3}{2}<r<2
, where

depends on the

, the solution
\mathbf{u}^{\delta}
and

.

,

]
, one is able to recover the regularity of

for some

and all

.
Indeed, in this case

can be chosen precisely as

(3.22)

If one only takes some

sufficiently close to

, we get lower bound

. For the derivation of (

) as in

To find some suitable

).
∎

3.4.1.
Recover the boundary identity

This subsection is devoted to recovering the continuity of the limiting normal velocities at the boundary. In general, it is rather easier for the case of Dirichlet boundary condition, which does not require more compactness of the structure displacement; see, e.g.
[
33
, Section 6.1.2]
. However, encountering the
Navier-slip
boundary condition, this is more difficult, since we need more compactness to justify the limit of the deformed normal
\mathbf{n}^{w}
depending nonlinearly on
\nabla w
.
On noting (
3.19
) and the uniform boundedness of
\partial_{t}w^{\delta}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\nu_{s}}\partial_{t}w^{\delta}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma)),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\delta}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{s,r}(\Gamma)),\ \text{ for some }0<s<\frac{1}{2},\ \frac{3}{2}<r<2,

an employment of the Kondrachov embedding theorem and the Aubin–Lions compactness lemma yields

\displaystyle w^{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow w,\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{2+\epsilon}(\Gamma)),

where
0<\epsilon<s
, implying

\displaystyle\nabla w^{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\nabla w,\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;C(\Gamma)).

Then we record

(3.23)

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}^{w^{\delta}}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w},\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;C(\Gamma)).

In fact, the outer normal
\mathbf{n}^{w^{\delta}}
inherits the same regularity as
\nabla w^{\delta}
, in view of (
3.18
),
\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}=\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}^{-\top}\mathbf{n}\circ\Phi
, and that
\mathbf{n}
,
\Phi
are smooth. Specifically, by (

),

\left\|\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}^{-\top}-\nabla\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}^{-\top}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;C(\Gamma))}\leq C\left\|\nabla w^{\delta}-\nabla w\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;C(\Gamma))}\to 0,\text{ as }\delta\to 0.

Remark 3.10
.

Note that in Muha–Čanić
[
51
, Corollary 3]
, a two-dimensional fluid-structure interaction problem with full displacement and Navier-slip boundary condition was considered, and the deformed normal has the convergence

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}^{w^{\delta}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{n}^{w},\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T;C(\Gamma)).

However, in our case of 3D fluids interacting with a 2D structure, this is no longer possible, even with structure dissipation. The reason is that Sobolev embeddings strongly depend on the dimensions and we are not able to obtain the same compactness of
w^{\delta}
as in
[
51
, Theorem 5 & Corollary 2]
. Thanks to the additional regularity (
3.19
) and (
3.21
), one can finally recover the spatial regularity with a bit of sacrifice of time integrability, compared to
[
51
]
.

Now we justify the kinematic condition. Recall that by the penalization we have

\mathrm{tr}_{\Gamma^{w^{\delta}}}\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w^{\delta}}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}\big)=\big(\partial_{t}{w^{\delta}}\mathbf{n}\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w^{\delta}}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}\big),

for all
\delta>0
. In the following one verifies that the limit solution
(\mathbf{u},\partial_{t}w)
in fact satisfies

\mathrm{tr}_{\Gamma^{w}}\mathbf{u}\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}\big)=\partial_{t}w\mathbf{n}\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}\big),\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{T}.

By virtue of (
3.23
) and the uniform weakly convergence of
\{\partial_{t}w^{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
, we derive

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}{w^{\delta}}\mathbf{n}\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w^{\delta}}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}\big)\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}-\int_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{t}{w}\mathbf{n}\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}\big)\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\big((\partial_{t}{w^{\delta}}-\partial_{t}w)\mathbf{n}\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}\big)\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}+\int_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{t}{w^{\delta}}\mathbf{n}\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w^{\delta}}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}-\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}\big)\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma}\big((\partial_{t}{w^{\delta}}-\partial_{t}w)\mathbf{n}\big)\cdot\Big(\big(\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}\big)\phi\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2}+C\left\|\mathbf{n}^{w^{\delta}}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}-\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;C(\Gamma))}

\displaystyle\to 0,\quad\text{as }\delta\to 0,

for all
\phi\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Gamma)
.
In view of the fact that
\{\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}\}_{\delta>0}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
uniformly for any
q\in[1,2)
, there is a function
\overline{\mathbf{u}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}}
in
L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
such that we are able to extract a non-relabeled subsequence fulfilling

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\overline{\mathbf{u}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}},\quad

weakly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})),\text{ for }1\leq q<2.

Then by Lemma
2.4
, one infers that

\left.\overline{\mathbf{u}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}}\right|_{\Gamma}\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w^{\delta}}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\delta}}\big)=\partial_{t}w\cdot\big(\mathbf{n}^{w}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}\big)
on
\Gamma_{T}
, where limit of
\overline{\mathbf{u}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}}
is identified by
\mathbf{u}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w}
following analogously
[
33
, Section 6.1.2]
.

3.4.2.
Convergence of the convection term in the momentum equation

Now we would like to derive more convergences, in order to obtain the limit passage of the momentum equation. In particular, we will show the convergence regarding the convection term

\displaystyle\varrho^{\delta}\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\otimes\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\to\varrho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u},\quad

weakly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{1}((0,T)\times B).

First of all, one records

(3.24)

\displaystyle\varrho^{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\varrho,\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }C_{w}([0,T];L^{\gamma}(B)).

Indeed, by the continuity equation (

) in the sense of distribution, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{B}\varrho^{\delta}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{B}\varrho^{\delta}\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}x,\text{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(0,T)\text{ for all }\phi\in\mathcal{D}(B).

Consequently,
\varrho^{\delta}
is uniformly continuous in
W^{-1,\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(B)
. Note that
\varrho^{\delta}\in C_{w}([0,T];L^{\gamma}(B))
and
\varrho^{\delta}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\gamma}(B)
. Then in view of
[
53
, Lemma 6.2]
, one concludes (
3.24
). Moreover, it follows from the boundedness of
\sqrt{\varrho^{\delta}}\mathbf{u}^{\delta}
, weak convergence of
\mathbf{u}^{\delta}
and the strong convergence (
3.24
) that

\displaystyle\varrho^{\delta}\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\to\varrho\mathbf{u},\quad

\displaystyle\text{weakly-}*\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(B)).

Employing the similar argument as in
[
24
,
53
]
along with the weak formulation of the approximate momentum equation (
3.13
) gives rise to

\displaystyle\varrho^{\delta}\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\to\varrho\mathbf{u},\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }C_{w}([0,T];L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(B)).

As
\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}>\frac{6}{5}=\frac{3\cdot 2}{3+2}
, we have the compact embedding
L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(B)\subset\subset W^{-1,2}(B)

[

implies

\displaystyle\varrho^{\delta}\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\to\varrho\mathbf{u},\quad

strongly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;W^{-1,2}(B)).

Then one infers from the weak convergence of
\mathbf{u}^{\delta}
in

\displaystyle\varrho^{\delta}\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\otimes\mathbf{u}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\to\varrho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u},\quad

weakly

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{1}((0,T)\times B).

3.4.3.
Convergence of the pressure term in momentum equation

To handle the approximate pressure in the momentum equation, we first conclude an observation by the uniform estimates.

Lemma 3.11
.

Under the assumption of Theorem

, there exists an integrable function

such that

\displaystyle p_{\delta}(\varrho)

\displaystyle\to\overline{p(\varrho)},\quad

up to a non-relabeled subsequence for any

A_{\varepsilon}\cap(\Gamma^{w^{\delta}}_{T})=\emptyset,\quad\int_{((0,T)\times B)\setminus A_{\varepsilon}}\overline{p(\varrho)}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

First it follows that, as a direct consequence of (

3.4.4.
An effective viscous flux identity

In the following, we are devoted to proving some compactness of
\varrho^{\delta}
so that one can identify the weak limit pressure term
\overline{p(\varrho)}
with
p(\varrho)
. To this end, one follows similar ideas as in the general framework of compressible Navier–Stokes equations
[
24
,
53
]
, with corresponding adaptations in compressible fluid-structure interaction problems
[
6
,
33
,
45
]
. Namely, we will make use of the so-called
effective viscous flux
, which provides more information than a single term.

We define the
L^{\infty}
-truncation

T_{k}(z)\coloneqq kT\left(\frac{z}{k}\right)\quad z\in\mathbb{R},k\in\mathbb{N},

where
T
is a smooth concave function on
\mathbb{R}
such that
T(z)=z
for
z\in[0,1)
and
T(z)=2
for
z\geq 3
. Then the
effective viscous flux identity
is stated as follows, exactly similar to
[
6
,
33
]
. We omit the proof here.

Lemma 3.12
.

Up to a non-relabeled subsequence, the following identity holds

(3.26)

\int_{Q_{T}^{w^{\varepsilon}}}\left(p_{\delta}(\varrho^{\delta})-(\lambda+2\mu)\Div\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\right)T_{k}(\varrho^{\delta})\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\to\int_{Q_{T}^{w}}\left(\overline{p(\varrho)}-(\lambda+2\mu)\Div\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\right)\overline{T_{k}(\varrho)}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,

as
\delta\to 0
for a fixed
k
.

3.4.5.
Compactness of the density

By means of the renormalized formulation of the continuity equation, we show the strong convergence of the density. Let
L_{k}(z)=z\ln z
for
0\leq z<k
and
L_{k}(z)=z\int_{k}^{z}\frac{T_{k}(s)}{s^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s
for
z\geq k
with
zL_{k}^{\prime}(z)-L_{k}(z)=T_{k}(z)
. On noting that
\varrho^{\delta}
and
\varrho
are defined zero outside
\Omega^{w^{\delta}}
and
\Omega^{w}
respectively, the functions
L_{k}(\varrho^{\delta})
and
L_{k}(\varrho)
vanish outside
\Omega^{w^{\delta}}
and
\Omega^{w}
respectively as well.
Invoking the renormalized formulation of the continuity equation (
3.12
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(L_{k}(\varrho^{\delta})-L_{k}(\varrho))(t)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(T_{k}(\varrho)\Div\mathbf{u}-\overline{T_{k}(\varrho)}\Div\mathbf{u}^{\delta})\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\overline{T_{k}(\varrho)}-T_{k}(\varrho^{\delta}))\Div\mathbf{u}^{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau,

for all
t\in[0,T]
. In view of the
effective viscous flux identity
(
3.26
), with exactly the same argument as in
[
6
,
33
,
45
]
, one is able to derive the strong convergence of the density

\varrho^{\delta}\to\varrho,\quad\text{a.e.~in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Combining all the ingredients above, together with the verification of energy inequality, the attainment of initial data, and the maximal interval of existence, cf.
[
33
, Section 6.1.7–6.1.10]
, we finally prove Theorem
1.4
.
∎

4.3.

There are different ways to justify the singular limits in the context of compressible fluids, for example, uniform estimates with compactness argument, energy methods for strong solutions, as well as relative entropy methods, which are endowed with different properties. As a starting point to investigating the singular limits for fluid-structure interaction problems, we will employ modified relative entropy methods.

\partial_{t}g,\nabla g\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,\mathfrak{p}}(\Omega^{w^{\varepsilon}}(t)))

if
\partial_{t}f,\nabla f\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,\mathfrak{p}}(\Omega^{\eta}(t)))
.

Remark 4.11
.

The additional regularity assumption (
4.18
) can be achieved by considering fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s+1}
for some
s>0
. In other words, if one substitutes the Laplacian operator and the bi-Laplacian operator in the elastic equation with
(-\Delta)^{s+1}
and
(-\Delta)^{s+2}
, we would obtain from the energy estimates that
w\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2+s}(\Gamma))\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{1+s}(\Gamma))
and no more assumptions needed. Note that the definition of the fractional Laplacian varies depending on the contexts, which agree with each other in the Hilbert space setting, see for example
[
16
]
. Then choosing
s>1-\frac{2}{\mathfrak{p}}
entails the desired regularity by Sobolev embeddings in two dimensions. In this case, the existence of weak solutions in Section
3
can be extended easily with the same arguments, since the fractional Laplacian is a linear operator.

Alternatively, one may consider the generalized
\mathfrak{p}
-biLaplacian energy and
\mathfrak{p}
-Laplacian damping with the same
\mathfrak{p}
as above,

\partial_{t}^{2}w+\Delta(\Div(|\Delta w|^{\mathfrak{p}-2}\nabla w))-\nu_{s}\Div(|\nabla\partial_{t}w|^{\mathfrak{p}-2}\nabla\partial_{t}w)=\mathcal{F}^{w}.

Then by testing the above equation with
\partial_{t}w
, we get

w\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,\mathfrak{p}}(\Gamma))\cap W^{1,2}(0,T;W^{1,\mathfrak{p}}(\Gamma))
fulfilling the requirement. In this situation, the existence of weak solutions is not trivial and cannot be extended naturally, as the solid equation now is nonlinear. Thus, one shall employ more compactness arguments to find the weak solutions.

Remark 4.12
.

It is expected that additional regularity assumptions are necessary up to now in the context of inviscid limit for fluid-structure interaction problems as the regularity of the weak solution is not sufficient to justify the limit, cf. Remark
4.13
. If only the incompressible limit is considered, we can get rid of these assumptions with a bit of sacrifice of
\gamma
, cf. Theorem
4.14
.

4.6.1.
Construct suitable test functions

In order to prove Theorem
4.8
, we would like to employ the scaled relative energy inequality (
4.4
).
Since the limit system is defined in the domain
\Omega^{\eta}
, one needs to transform it back to
\Omega^{w}
concerning the weak solution, to compare it with the weak solution of the compressible fluid-structure interaction problem. A naive idea is to take the same strategy as in Section
4.5
, namely, to define the transformation between two domains by

\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\mathbf{\Phi}_{\eta}\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\varepsilon}}^{-1}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\eta-w^{\varepsilon}},\quad J^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\det(\nabla\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}),\quad\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(\nabla\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon})^{-1}.

Note that all these share the same uniform (in
\varepsilon>0
) regularities as in Lemmata
4.4
.

However, simple analog does not apply here directly and it is twofold. First, observing that in relative entropy inequality, the test function should be admissible in the sense of boundary condition
(\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w^{\varepsilon}})\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{t}W
holds on
\Gamma^{w^{\varepsilon}}
, as in Section
4.5
. Moreover, the limit system is incompressible, meaning the test function should be divergence-free. Otherwise, when deriving the uniform in
\varepsilon
Lipschitz-like estimates for the relative entropy, we would need to control terms like
\Div\mathbf{v}
, which are not quadratic. Hence, one must correct the transform
\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}
so that the constructed test function is admissible.

To handle this problem, we take the well-known
Piola transform
, cf.
[
12
]
, which is endowed with two important properties: (i) it preserves the nullity of normal components; (ii) it maps divergence-free vectors to divergence-free vectors.
A similar idea can be found, e.g., in
[
30
,
56
]
for simple 2D/1D fluid-structure interaction problems, in
[
19
]
for an isogeometric analysis of a Darcy–Stokes–Brinkman system.
To this end, we define

(4.22)

\mathbf{v}=J^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon},\quad\Pi=\tilde{\Pi}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}.

By Remark
4.10
, (
4.3
), and Sobolev embedding, we know

(4.23)

\begin{gathered}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T^{\prime};W^{1,\infty}(\Omega^{w^{\varepsilon}}(t))),\quad\partial_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}),\nabla(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon})\in L^{2}(0,T^{\prime};W^{1,\mathfrak{p}}(\Omega^{w^{\varepsilon}}(t))),\\
\Pi\in L^{\infty}(0,T^{\prime};W^{1,\infty}(\Omega^{w^{\varepsilon}}(t))),\end{gathered}

for all
\mathfrak{p}>4
.
In view of the Piola identity
\Div(J^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})^{\top})=0
, and the identity
\Div(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{v})={\mathbf{T}}^{\top}:\nabla{\mathbf{v}}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\Div\mathbf{T}^{\top}
for
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
,
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

(4.24)

\Div\mathbf{v}=\Div(J^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})^{\top})\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}+J^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})^{\top}:\nabla(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon})=J^{\varepsilon}(\Div\tilde{\mathbf{v}})\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}=0.

In addition, the deformation of the normal vector entails

\mathbf{n}^{\eta}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}=J^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})^{\top}\mathbf{n}^{w^{\varepsilon}},\text{ on }\Gamma^{w^{\varepsilon}}.

Then one obtains the boundary condition on
\Gamma_{T^{\prime}}

(4.25)

\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w^{\varepsilon}}=J^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon})\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w^{\varepsilon}}=(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon})\cdot(J^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})^{\top}\mathbf{n}^{w^{\varepsilon}})=(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{\eta})\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}=\partial_{t}\eta\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\varepsilon}}.

With (
4.22
) and identities above, we arrive at the transformed system of (
4.2
) in the time-space cylinder denoted by
Q_{T^{\prime}}^{w^{\varepsilon}}\coloneqq\bigcup_{t\in(0,T^{\prime})}\Omega^{w^{\varepsilon}}(t)\times\{t\}
,

(4.26a)

\displaystyle\bar{\varrho}(\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v})+\nabla\Pi

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }Q_{T^{\prime}}^{w^{\varepsilon}},

(4.26b)

\displaystyle\Div\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }Q_{T^{\prime}}^{w^{\varepsilon}},

(4.26c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\eta+\Delta^{2}\eta-\nu_{s}\Delta\partial_{t}\eta

\displaystyle=\Pi\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\varepsilon}},

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{T^{\prime}},

(4.26d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\eta-(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{w^{\varepsilon}})\circ\mathbf{\Phi}_{w^{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{T^{\prime}},

(4.26e)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{T^{\prime}}^{0},

(4.26f)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{v},\eta,\partial_{t}\eta)(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{v}_{0},\eta_{0},\eta_{1}),

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\Omega^{w_{0}^{\varepsilon}},

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bar{\varrho}J^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{t}\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}\cdot(\nabla\tilde{\mathbf{v}})\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon})+\bar{\varrho}\partial_{t}(J^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\quad+\bar{\varrho}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla(J^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon})+\bar{\varrho}(J^{\varepsilon}-1)J^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\mathbf{v}})\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\quad+((\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})^{-1}-\mathbb{I})(\nabla\tilde{\Pi})\circ\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}).

At this stage, we would like to exploit the uniform estimate of
\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
in terms of
w^{\varepsilon}-\eta
. In view of Lemma
4.4
, most of the terms can be handled, except for terms with
\partial_{t}(J^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})
and
\nabla(J^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})
. In general, one cannot expect good control of it if we ignore the viscous damping
\nu_{s}\Delta\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon}
. More precisely, with
\nu_{s}>0
, we know
\sqrt{\nu_{s}}\nabla\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon}\in L_{t}^{2}L_{\Gamma}^{2}
. Then one concludes from Remark

with a bit of calculations

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{w^{\varepsilon}})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\nu_{s}\|\nabla\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon}(\tau)-\nabla\partial_{t}\eta(\tau)\|_{L_{\Gamma}^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{0}^{t}\big(\|\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon}(\tau)-\partial_{t}\eta(\tau)\|_{L_{\Gamma}^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta w^{\varepsilon}(\tau)-\Delta\eta(\tau)\|_{L_{\Gamma}^{2}}^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

which holds for
\partial_{t}J^{\varepsilon}
as well. Here the argument strongly relies on the additional regularity (
4.18
), i.e.,
\nabla w\in L_{t}^{\infty}L_{\Gamma}^{\infty}
(and hence
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon},J^{\varepsilon}\in L_{t}^{\infty}L_{w^{\varepsilon}}^{\infty}
).
Therefore, with
\nabla w^{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Gamma))
,

(4.27)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{w^{\varepsilon}})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\|\nabla\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon}(\tau)-\nabla\partial_{t}\eta(\tau)\|_{L_{\Gamma}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon}(\tau)-\partial_{t}\eta(\tau)\|_{L_{\Gamma}^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta w^{\varepsilon}(\tau)-\Delta\eta(\tau)\|_{L_{\Gamma}^{2}}^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where
C>0
depends on (
4.18
), but is independent of
\varepsilon>0
.

Moreover, the assumption (
4.18
) helps us to exploit better uniform bounds for
\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
, which is essentially necessary to handle the residual term in (
4.29
). For

, we have

(4.28)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\right\|_{L_{w^{\varepsilon}}^{\mathfrak{p}}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\big(\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{\Psi}^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\|_{L_{w^{\varepsilon}}^{\mathfrak{p}}}^{2}+\|\nabla J^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\|_{L_{w^{\varepsilon}}^{\mathfrak{p}}}^{2}+\|\nabla\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}(\tau)\|_{L_{w^{\varepsilon}}^{\mathfrak{p}}}^{2}+1\big)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

where
C>0
depends on (
4.18
), but is independent of
\varepsilon>0
.

At the moment we are ready to justify the incompressible inviscid limit.

Remark 4.13
.

In the argument above, we take the
Piola transform
, which preserves the divergence-free property and nullity of normal components. However, it requires a slightly more regularity of
w^{\varepsilon}
. Indeed, among the error term
\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
, we would have to handle
\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
and
\nabla\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
, which are at most
L^{2}
-integrable in space with
\nu_{s}>0
by standard energy and dissipation (namely,
\partial_{t}\nabla w^{\varepsilon}
and
\Delta w^{\varepsilon}
). This would cause much trouble when we derive the limit passage and try to control
\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
, cf. (
4.29
), and it is essentially where we need the additional regularity assumption (
4.18
) to close the estimates.

4.6.3.
Incompressible limit

\displaystyle(\tilde{\mathbf{v}},\eta,\partial_{t}\eta)(\cdot,0)

Remark A.1
.

Here the relative energy inequality is derived in the deformed domain

depending on time. In
