Lower Bounds for Nonparametric Estimation of Ordinary Differential Equations
Christof Schötz, Maximilian Siebel

Abstract
We noisily observe solutions of an ordinary differential equation u ˙ = f ⁡ ( u ) \dot{u}=f(u) at given times, where u u lives in a d d -dimensional state space. The model function f f is unknown and belongs to a Hölder-type smoothness class with parameter β \beta . For the nonparametric problem of estimating f f , we provide lower bounds on the error in two complementary model specifications: the snake model with few, long observed solutions and the stubble model with many short ones. The lower bounds are minimax optimal in some settings. They depend on various parameters, which in the optimal asymptotic regime leads to the same rate for the squared error in both models: it is characterized by the exponent − 2 β / ( 2 ( β + 1 ) + d ) -2\beta/(2(\beta+1)+d) for the total number of observations n n . To derive these results, we establish a master theorem for lower bounds in general nonparametric regression problems, which makes the proofs more comparable and seems to be a useful tool for future use.



Keywords:
Nonparametric Regression, Ordinary Differential Equations, Nonparametric Estimation, Lower Bounds, Minimax-Optimality

Motivation.

In numerous scientific domains spanning from physics, engineering, and chemistry to biology, aspects of the real world are modeled as dynamical systems. See
[
Ott02
,
Str24
]
for a comprehensive overview. If the dynamics of such systems are not fully known, we may want to learn them from observational data and statistics comes into play. Reviews of current methods for statistical inference and data analysis for dynamical systems are given in
[
MMP15
,
Dat21
]
.

Model.

Here we consider
d
-dimensional, autonomous, first order ordinary differential equations (ODEs) as simple, yet general instances of a dynamical systems. They have the form

\dot{u}(t)=f(u(t))\,,

(1)

where
t\in\mathbb{R}
is called
time
,
u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
describes the
state
of the system at given time with its time-derivative denoted as
\dot{u}:=\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}
, and
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the so-called
model function
describing the dynamics of the system. The ODE is
autonomous
as
f
does not explicitly depend on time. It is of
first order
as only the first derivative of
u
is part of the equation. We denote by
t\mapsto U(f,x,t)
the solution to the
initial value problem
that is given by (
1
) and
initial conditions

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}U(f,x,t)=f(U(f,x,t))
and
U(f,x,0)=x
.
Classical textbook results provide existences and uniqueness of solutions to such initial value problems if

is globally Lipschitz continuous, see for instance
[
Mag23
]
. In our statistical model, we assume
f
to be unknown, but to belong to some (infinite-dimensional) smoothness class, which is a subset of the Lipschitz continuous functions
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For known initial conditions
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
, we assume to measure
t\mapsto U(f,x_{j},t)
at known time points
t_{j,1},\dots t_{j,n_{j}}
to collect a total of
n=\sum_{j}n_{j}
observations. The measurement error is modeled as centered and independent
d
-dimensional random variables
\varepsilon_{j,i}
. Thus, our observations
Y_{j,i}
follow the model equation

Y_{j,i}=U(f,x_{j},t_{j,i})+\varepsilon_{j,i}\,.

(2)

Our goal is to investigate lower bounds on the rate of convergence for estimating the model function
f
. See

Inverse Problems.

Since the parameter of interest
f
is observed only indirectly via the state variable
U(f,x,\cdot)
, the problem can be naturally interpreted as a statistical inverse problem. The non-linearity of the solution map (also called
forward map
)
f\mapsto U(f,x,\cdot)
, renders classical results from linear inverse problem theory inapplicable. While (deterministic) non-linear inverse problems have a long mathematical tradition (see
[
EHN00
]
for instance), more recent work is concerned with nonparametric statistical theory for regression models driven by differential equations with non-linear forward maps. For instance,
[
MNP21
]
and
[
NGW20
]
investigate PDE-constrained regression models from a Bayesian perspective and through M-estimation techniques, respectively. This emerging field has shown considerable promise for several PDE models (see
[
GN20
,
BN21
,
Kek22
]
, and
[
NT24
]
for instance). These models are more complex than (
1
) in that they incorporate second order derivatives and derivatives in more than one variable. But they are also simpler in that they are linear differential equations, i.e., linear combinations of derivatives with coefficient functions to be estimated. In contrast, we allow arbitrary smooth model functions
f
in (
1
).

Related Work.

For the ODE-model (
2
), the parametric setting, in which
f=f_{\theta}
is determined by a finite-dimensional parameter vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, is well-studied. In
[
Bru08
,
QZ10
,
GK12
,
DK15
]
different methods are explored that achieve the optimal
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
-error rate for estimating
\theta
. These estimators typically consist of two steps, in which initially the trajectory itself is estimated nonparametrically, and subsequently,
\theta
is estimated using a least squares or maximum likelihood approach. See also
[
RH17
]
for a comprehensive overview.

In a parametric ODE model, each observation typically contains information on the whole, global parameter vector
\theta
. This is different in the nonparametric setting, where
f
lives in an infinite dimensional space of functions. An observation at one location
z=U(f,x,t)
is only locally informative, i.e., only contains information about
f(\tilde{z})
with
\tilde{z}
close to
z
. As the location
z
is determined by the unknown model function
f
, the nonparametric setting requires fundamentally different approaches than the parametric one.

Several algorithms have been proposed in the nonparametric setting of (
2
) or similar ones, but almost no theoretical results on the estimation error have been proven:
The problem can be approached, e.g., by using Gaussian processes
[
Hei+18
]
, neural networks
[
Che+18
]
, or random feature maps and ensemble Kalman filters
[
GR21
]
. In
[
Lah+24
]
, an algorithm for nonparametric estimation in the model (
2
) based on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces is introduced. The authors also prove a
n^{-\frac{1}{4}}
-convergences rate upper bound for the root mean integrated squared error of estimating the observed trajectory
u
within the observed time span.

Another novel statistical perspective has been developed in
[
Mar+23
]
, where distribution learning via neural differential equations is studied. Utilizing transport of measure theory, the authors establish nonparametric statistical convergence analysis for distribution learning via ODE models trained through likelihood maximization.

Upper Bounds, Stubble and Snake.
Upper bounds on the error rate for nonparametric estimation of the model function
f
have recently been studied in
[
Sch24
]
. To make consistent estimation possible, one of two different sets of conditions are assumed, leading to the so-called
snake
and
stubble
models. The observations need to contain sufficient information about the model function
f
in the subset of its domain where we want to estimate it (the
domain of interest
, e.g.,
[0,1]^{d}
). But the location of observation itself is determined by the dynamics of the system, i.e., by

. In the observation scheme of the stubble model, we assume to observe many short trajectories

that start from initial conditions which uniformly cover the domain of interest. In contrast to this, in the snake model, we measure only few, but long trajectories that are required to cover the domain of interest in a suitable way. Both observation schemes are illustrated in
Figure

2
. For both settings
[
Sch24
]
provides estimation algorithms with upper bounds on the rate of convergence.

General Model

2.1
Model Description

We consider an autonomous, first order, ordinary differential equation (ODE) of the form
\dot{u}=f(u)
, where
f
is an unknown smooth function, which we call the model function. One or several solutions to this ODE are observed with noise at given times. The noise is assumed to be independent.
The task we consider is to estimate the model function
f
.

As we will show in the following, care has to be taken when defining the precise model and estimation task. In a straight-forward setting, no consistent estimation is possible at all.

Assumption 2.2
.

•

Noise
:
There is

such that, for all

\mathrm{K\!L}(v_{1}+P^{\varepsilon},v_{2}+P^{\varepsilon})\leq C_{\mathsf{noise}}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{2}^{2},

Remark 2.3
.

(i)

Example.

The normal distribution
P^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{N}(0,A)
, where

is symmetric and positive definite, fulfills

C_{\mathsf{noise}}=\frac{1}{2\lambda_{\mathsf{min}}}\,,

(12)

(ii)

Noise

2.3
Target: Estimation of the Model Function

Notation 2.4
.

(i)

For
p\in[1,\infty]
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, a measurable set

\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}({A})}=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{A}\left|g\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\text{ for }p\in[1,\infty)\,,\\
\esssup_{x\in A}\left|g\right|&\text{ for }p=\infty\,.\end{cases}

(13)

Let us consider the estimation of
f^{\star}
via an estimator denoted
\hat{f}

A first ingredient for a typical loss function for such an objective is a way to compare

\|\hat{f}-f^{\star}\|_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,,x\mapsto\|\hat{f}-f^{\star}\|_{2}(x):=\|\hat{f}(x)-f^{\star}(x)\|_{2}\,.

(14)

For a given state

\|\hat{f}-f^{\star}\|_{2}(x_{0})=\left\|\hat{f}(x_{0})-f^{\star}(x_{0})\right\|_{2}\,.

(15)

To obtain a global evaluation of an estimate, we can instead integrate
\|\hat{f}-f^{\star}\|_{2}
over some measurable subset

. A general form of this loss is

\left\|\left\|\hat{f}-f^{\star}\right\|_{2}\right\|_{L^{p}({A})}\,,

(16)

where
p\in[1,\infty]
.
The set

may be chosen beforehand as a fixed domain of interest such as

. However, it is not possible to obtain a consistent estimate without making further assumptions. Consider following example. We observe

on

. But the initial conditions

are so that

. Then we will only make observations at

. Therefore, we will not be able to create a meaningful estimate of, say,

To obtain non-trivial results, we make restrictions on the general model so that consistent estimation is possible. The restrictions ensure a suitable distribution of observations across the domain of interest (see

). In the snake model, the number of initial conditions and their location is not directly restricted, but the observed trajectory or trajectories must have forms to suitably cover of the domain of interest (e.g.,

As we are interested in a lower bound, we make a complementary assumption: We require that no ball in the domain of interest contains too many initial conditions, which could yield over-proportionally good estimates locally.

3.3
Lower Bound – Deterministic

We assume here that we make observations at equally spaced timepoints. In this setting, we always have a non-vanishing error term – even when observing infinitely many trajectories starting at every point in the domain of interest (and outside), and even when observing without any measurement noise. This is demonstrated with the following theorem. We show the existence of two different model functions that induce trajectories that coincide periodically. If this period is equal to the constant timestep of observation times, it is impossible to distinguish the two functions.

4
Snake Model

In this section, we consider the model described in
Section

2.2
with the target of estimating
f^{\star}
on the domain of interest
[0,1]^{d}
. We make a restriction on the location of the observed trajectories in the state space to avoid trivial settings in which consistent estimation is impossible. We call the resulting model the
snake model
. In this model, we derive two different types of lower bounds – one
deterministic
, which is independent of the noise and sample size
n
and another
probabilistic
depending on these objects. Then the rates are combined for a full picture. The obtained lower bounds on the error rates are shown to be minimax optimal in at least one specific setting of interest.

4.1
Model

If there is a location in state space which has a large distance to every point of the observed trajectories, we are not able to estimate the model function at this location well. In other words, the error rate for estimation of the model function
f^{\star}
depends on how densely the trajectories cover the domain of interest. We make this dependence visible by designing a parameter class that not only requires a minimum smoothness of
f^{\star}
, but also requires a bound
\delta
on the maximal distance of any point in the domain of interest to the observed trajectories. Clearly, longer trajectories have a higher potential to fulfill the latter requirement. Moreover, the length of a trajectory
u_{j}:=U(f^{\star},x_{j},\cdot)\colon[0,T_{j}]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be bounded from above by the product of its maximum speed
|\dot{u}_{j}|_{\infty}
and the length of time interval
T_{j}
. If the corresponding model function is in
\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}=\Sigma^{d\to d}(\beta,L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\ell\right\rrbracket}},L_{\beta})
, we have
|\dot{u}_{j}|_{\infty}\leq L_{0}
. With these considerations in mind, we design the parameter class depending on the smoothness parameters
\beta,L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\ell\right\rrbracket}},L_{\beta}
, the covering parameter
\delta
, and the total observed time
T_{\mathsf{sum}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}T_{j}
.

In contrast to the stubble model, we do not prescribe initial conditions, but make them part of the parameter vector that describes the probability distribution of observations: For
m\in\mathbb{N}
trajectories, the parameter vector
\theta=(f,x_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}},T_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}})\in\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m}\times(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{m}
contains the model function
f
, the initial conditions
x_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}}
and the maximum observation time
T_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}}
for each trajectory. Our observations are described by
\theta^{\star}=(f^{\star},x_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}},T_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}})
and the equation

Y_{j,i}=U(f^{\star},x_{j},t_{j,i})+\varepsilon_{j,i}\,,\ i\in{\left\llbracket n_{j}\right\rrbracket},j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}

(47)

with – as before – independent, identically distributed, and centered noise
\varepsilon_{j,i}\sim P^{\varepsilon}
. We denote by
\mathbf{P}_{\theta^{\star}}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\theta^{\star}}[\cdot]
the corresponding probability measure and expectation, respectively.

As suggested above, to provide lower bounds on estimating
f^{\star}
, we need some restrictions on
\theta^{\star}
.
To describe the set of admissible parameters, we need the following definitions.

Definition 4.1

(Tube)
.

(i)

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
T\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Let
u\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be continuously differentiable and denote its derivative as
\dot{u}\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Define the (closed)
tube
with radius
\delta
around
u
as

\mathcal{T}\!\left(u,\delta\right):=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\{u(t)+v\,\,\kern-1.2pt\mathopen{}\left|\vphantom{u(t)+v\,}\,\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{u}(t)\neq 0,\cr v^{\!\top}\!\dot{u}(t)=0,\cr\left\|v\right\|_{2}\leq\delta\end{array}\right.\mathclose{}\kern-1.2pt\right\}\,.

(48)

(ii)

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
Lipschitz continuous,
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Let
\theta:=(f,x_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}},T_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}})
. Let
u_{j}:=U(f^{\star},x_{j},\cdot)\colon[0,T_{j}]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define the
tube distance
as

d_{\mathcal{T}}(A,\theta):=\inf\left\{\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\,\kern-1.2pt\mathopen{}\left|\vphantom{\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\,A\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{T}\!\left(u_{j},\delta\right)\right.\mathclose{}\kern-1.2pt\right\}\,.

(49)

Remark 4.2
.

Our definition here yields
\mathcal{T}(u,\delta)=\emptyset
if
\dot{u}=0
. If we would remove the condition
\dot{u}(t)\neq 0
from (
48
), the model function
f=0
, would be able to cover the hypercube with a sufficient amount of trajectories
m
, initial conditions on a uniform grid, and
T_{j}=0
. But
f(x)=\epsilon\mathbf{e}_{1}
with sufficiently small
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
would not fulfill this condition. Our convention avoids this discontinuity. For an analysis of settings with a large number of very short trajectories, see the stubble model.

Now we can define the parameter class
\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}
, for which we show error lower bounds in this section.

Definition 4.3

(Snake Parameter Class)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
\beta,L_{0},\dots,L_{\ell},L_{\beta},\delta,T_{\mathsf{sum}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Set
\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}:=\Sigma^{d\to d}(\beta,L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\ell\right\rrbracket}},L_{\beta})
. Define

\Theta_{d,\beta}^{m}:=\Theta_{d,\beta}^{m}(L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\ell\right\rrbracket}},L_{\beta}):=\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}

(50)

and

\displaystyle\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}

\displaystyle:=\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}(L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\ell\right\rrbracket}},L_{\beta},\delta,T_{\mathsf{sum}})

(51)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\theta=(f,x_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}},T_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}})\in\Theta_{d,\beta}^{m}\,\kern-1.2pt\mathopen{}\left|\vphantom{\theta=(f,x_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}},T_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}})\in\Theta_{d,\beta}^{m}}\,\begin{array}[]{l}m\in\mathbb{N},\cr\sum_{j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}}T_{j}=T_{\mathsf{sum}},\cr d_{\mathcal{T}}([0,1]^{d},\theta)\leq\delta\end{array}\right.\mathclose{}\kern-1.2pt\right\}\,.

For
\theta=(f,x_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}},T_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}})\in\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}
, denote
f_{\theta}:=f
.

Remark 4.4
.

The volume of a tube in dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
of radius
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is bounded from above by the volume of sphere with radius
r
in
d-1
dimensions, i.e.,
c_{d}\delta^{d-1}
, times the length of the tube. The length of the tubes generated by trajectories
U(f,x_{j},\cdot)\colon[0,T_{j}]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}
is bounded from above by
L_{0}T_{\mathsf{sum}}
, where
L_{0}:=|f|_{\infty}
and
T_{\mathsf{sum}}:=\sum_{j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}}T_{j}
.
Thus, we require
c_{d}\delta^{d-1}L_{0}T_{\mathsf{sum}}\geq 1
to make it possible to achieve
d_{\mathcal{T}}([0,1]^{d},\theta)\leq\delta
for
\theta=(f,x_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}},T_{{\left\llbracket 1,m\right\rrbracket}})
. In other words, if
L_{0}T_{\mathsf{sum}}<\left(c_{d}\delta^{d-1}\right)^{-1}
, then
\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}(L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\ell\right\rrbracket}},L_{\beta},\delta,T_{\mathsf{sum}})=\emptyset
.

4.2
Lower Bound – Probabilistic

We consider the model described by
Equation

47
. We assume that the data points do not accumulate too much within an area, which is guaranteed by the following assumption.

Assumption 4.5
.

•

CoverTime
:
There is
C_{\mathsf{cvrtm}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with the following property:
For all
j\in{\left\llbracket m\right\rrbracket}
,
a,b\in[0,T_{j}]
with
a<b
, we have

\sum_{i\in{\left\llbracket n_{j}\right\rrbracket}}\mathds{1}_{[a,b]}(t_{i,j})\leq\max\!\left(1,C_{\mathsf{cvrtm}}(b-a)\frac{n}{T_{\mathsf{sum}}}\right)\,.

(55)

Remark 4.6
.

(i)

Example.

EquidistantTime
implies
CoverTime
with
C_{\mathsf{cvrtm}}=3
.

(ii)

Interpretation.

The term
n/T_{\mathsf{sum}}
is the average time destiny of the observations. The condition requires that the observation density is never too far above average, which would lead to potentially better estimates at that location. Compare with
Cover
, which requires a similar bound in space instead of time.

We have the following lower bound results.

Theorem 4.7
.

Use the model of
2.2
with parameter class
\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}
from

.
Assume

,

Let

(59)

Remark 4.8
.

(i)

Dimension.

In our construction, we require
d\geq 2
. Furthermore, the tubes of
Definition

4.1
are only meaningful in
d\geq 2
. Note that
d=1
is a special case, where all solutions
U(f,x,\cdot)
are monotone functions.

(ii)

Equidistant Time.

If we assume
EquidistantTime
, then
CoverTime
is fulfilled and
T_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}n=\Delta\!t^{-1}
.

(iii)

Relation Assumptions.

The lower bounds are formulated in terms of the overall sample size
n
, the total observation time
T_{\mathsf{sum}}
, and the radius
\delta
of the tube which is required to cover the domain of interest. A typical setting we may be interested in is

\delta\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\text{ and }T_{\mathsf{sum}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\infty\text{ and }\frac{n}{T_{\mathsf{sum}}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\infty\,.

(60)

Then the assumptions reduce to the requirement that
\delta\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
is in some sense slower than
T_{\mathsf{sum}}/n\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
and
T_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
. For example,

\delta=n^{-\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)+d}}\text{ and }T_{\mathsf{sum}}=C\delta^{-(d-1)}=Cn^{\frac{d-1}{2(\beta+1)+d}}

(61)

fulfill the requirements. This choice becomes relevant when balancing against the deterministic error presented below.

(iv)

Error Tendencies.
We can understand
T_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}n
as the observation density in time. If it increases, more accurate estimations can be made, reducing the error lower bound. The term
\delta^{-(d-1)}
can be interpreted as the minimum required length of the (combined) trajectories that is needed to cover the domain of interest with
\delta
-tubes. A smaller
\delta
means a smaller parameter class
\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}
, decreasing the error lower bound.

(v)

Exponent.

In comparison to the lower bounds in
Theorem

3.4
, the exponent for the error term
Theorem

4.7
does match the intuitions for nonparametric regression models, cf.
Remark

3.5
. If the available time
T_{\mathsf{sum}}
is used in an optimal way, one can balance
T_{\mathsf{sum}}=c\delta^{-(d-1)}
so that the exponent stays the same when considering a lower error bound that only depends on
n
.

4.3
Lower Bound – Deterministic

With
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}
, we always have an error term of order
\delta^{\beta}
– even when observing trajectories continuously in time over an arbitrarily long time range, and even when observing without any measurement noise. This is due to the points in the domain of interest where the closest trajectory is a distance
\delta
away.
This is demonstrated with the following theorem. We show the existence of two different model functions that induce trajectories that coincide for certain initial conditions and cover the domain of interest as required. It is impossible to distinguish the two model functions from observations.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}
,
\ell:=\llfloor\beta\rrfloor

\mathcal{F}_{d,\beta}:=\Sigma^{d\to d}(\beta,L_{{\left\llbracket 0,\ell\right\rrbracket}},L_{\beta})

Let
p\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}

Then there is
C\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
large enough, depending only
d,\beta,L_{0},\dots,L_{\ell},L_{\beta},p
, with the following property:
Let

and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
\delta\leq C^{-1}
.
Assume
CT_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}\leq\delta^{d-1}
.
Then there are

of the form

,

(65)

.

(i)

instead of

4.4
Combined Lower Bounds

We have now proven two different lower bounds, which van be combined to the following lower bounds of the estimation risk.

Corollary 4.11
.

Use the model of
2.2
with parameter class
\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}
from
Definition

4.3
.
Assume
d\geq 2
,
Noise
, and
CoverTime
.
Then there is a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
large enough, depending only on
\beta,d,L_{0},\dots,L_{\ell},L_{\beta},C_{\mathsf{noise}},C_{\mathsf{cvrtm}}
, with the following property:
Assume

C\max\!\left(\left(\frac{T_{\mathsf{sum}}}{n}\right)^{2\beta+d+1},\,T_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}\right)\leq\delta^{d-1}\leq C^{-1}\min\!\left(1,\,\frac{n}{T_{\mathsf{sum}}}\right)\,.

(69)

Then

\displaystyle\forall x_{0}\in[0,1]^{d}\colon\ C\inf_{\hat{f}}\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}}\mathbf{E}_{\theta^{\star}}\!\left[\left\|\hat{f}-f_{\theta^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}(x_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\delta^{2\beta}+\left(\delta^{-(d-1)}nT_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}\right)^{-\frac{2\beta}{2(\beta+1)+d}}

(70)

\displaystyle C\inf_{\hat{f}}\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}}\mathbf{E}_{\theta^{\star}}\!\left[\sup_{x\in[0,1]^{d}}\left\|\hat{f}-f_{\theta^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\delta^{2\beta}+\left(\frac{\delta^{-(d-1)}nT_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}}{\log(\delta^{-(d-1)}nT_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1})}\right)^{-\frac{2\beta}{2(\beta+1)+d}}

(71)

\displaystyle C\inf_{\hat{f}}\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in\Theta^{\mathsf{snake}}_{d,\beta}}\mathbf{E}_{\theta^{\star}}\!\left[\left\|\left\|\hat{f}-f_{\theta^{\star}}\right\|_{2}\right\|_{L^{2}({[0,1]^{d}})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\delta^{2\beta}+\left(\delta^{-(d-1)}nT_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}\right)^{-\frac{2\beta}{2(\beta+1)+d}}\,,

(72)

where the infima range over all estimators
\hat{f}
of
f_{\theta^{\star}}
based on the observations
Y_{\mathcal{I}}
.

Remark 4.12
.

(i)

Wasting Time.
One only needs
T_{\mathsf{sum}}=c_{d}\left(L_{0}\delta^{d-1}\right)^{-1}
to cover the domain of interest
[0,1]^{d}
, see
Remark

4.4
. If
T_{\mathsf{sum}}
is much larger, this time and the corresponding observations can be wasted for non-informative observations to achieve a large lower bound.

(ii)

Optimal Covering.
If
T_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}=c_{d}L_{0}\delta^{d-1}
then (
69
) becomes

Cn^{-\frac{2(\beta+1)+d-1}{2(\beta+1)+d}}\leq\delta^{d-1}\leq C^{-1}\,.

(73)

Setting
\delta=cn^{-\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)+d}}
fulfills this condition.

(iii)

Balancing.
The deterministic error term dominates,

\delta^{2\beta}\geq c\left(\delta^{-(d-1)}nT_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}\right)^{-\frac{2\beta}{2(\beta+1)+d}}\,,

(74)

if and only if

\delta\geq c\left(\frac{n}{T_{\mathsf{sum}}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)+1}}\,.

(75)

If we further assume that no time is wasted,
T_{\mathsf{sum}}^{-1}=c_{d}L_{0}\delta^{d-1}
, we arrive at

\delta\geq cn^{-\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)+d}}\,.

(76)

(iv)

Minimax Optimality.

In
[
Sch24
, Corollary 4.17]
, for
m=1
, an upper bound of the form

\sup_{x\in[0,1]^{d}}\left\|\hat{f}-f_{\theta^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}(x)\in\mathbf{O}_{\mathbf{P}}\!\left(\delta^{2\beta}+\left(\frac{n}{T_{\mathsf{sum}}\log n}\right)^{-\frac{2\beta}{2(\beta+1)+1}}\right)

(77)

is shown for a certain estimator. That estimator is known to be flawed as the smoothness of

Definition A.6

(Kernel)
.

A kernel is a continuous function
K\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

(i)

The kernel is nonnegative if
K(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

The kernel is symmetric if
K(x)=K(-x)

x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

A symmetric kernel is monotone if
K(x)\geq K(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
x\leq y
.

(iv)

The support of a kernel

is
\operatorname{supp}(K):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid K(x)\neq 0\}
.

B.1
General Reduction Scheme

Let
(\Theta,d)
be a pseudometric space, i.e., a set
\Theta
equipped with a map

(i)

(ii)

d(\theta,\tilde{\theta})=d(\tilde{\theta},\theta)

(iii)

d(\theta,\tilde{\theta})\leq d(\theta,\theta^{\prime})+d(\theta^{\prime},\tilde{\theta})
(Triangle Inequality)

for all
\theta,\tilde{\theta},\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
. In the following, let us consider a
statistical model

\{\mathbf{P}_{\theta}|\,\theta\in\Theta\}
, i.e., a family of probability measures index by the
parameter space

\Theta

The next two lemmas show how a lower bound on the error of estimating
\theta
can be achieved by reducing
\Theta
to only finitely many elements.
Recall that
\mathrm{K\!L}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence of two probability measures.

(Two Hypotheses)
.

\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\in\Theta
.

\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in\Theta}\mathbf{P}_{\theta^{\star}}\!\left(d(\hat{\theta},\theta^{\star})\geq\frac{1}{2}d(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}\,,

(165)

where the infimum ranges over all estimators
\hat{\theta}
of
\theta^{\star}
based on an observation
Y\sim\mathbf{P}_{\theta^{\star}}
.

Proof of
Lemma

B.1
.

[

, Equation (2.9) and Theorem 2.2]
with

and
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}

.

Let
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\in\Theta

\mathbf{P}_{\theta_{0}}=\mathbf{P}_{\theta_{1}}\,.

\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbf{P}_{\theta}\!\left(d(\hat{\theta},\theta)\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)

Next, we note that it suffices to show lower bounds on the probability of error, since this also implies bounds on expectation.

Theorem B.4

(Reduction to bounds in probability)
.

Let

\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in\Theta}\mathbf{P}_{\theta^{\star}}\!\left(d(\hat{\theta},\theta^{\star})\geq s\right)\geq p\,.

(175)

be increasing.
Then

\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta^{\star}\in\Theta}\mathbf{E}_{\theta^{\star}}\!\left[\nu\!\left(d(\hat{\theta},\theta^{\star})\right)\right]\geq p\nu(s)\,.

(176)

Here, all infima range over all estimators
\hat{\theta}
of
\theta^{\star}
based on an observation
Y\sim\mathbf{P}_{\theta^{\star}}
.

Theorem

B.4
.

Let
\theta\in\Theta
and
\hat{\theta}
be an arbitrary estimator. Assume

. Then

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}_{\theta}\!\left(d(\hat{\theta},\theta)\geq s\right)\leq\mathbf{P}_{\theta}\!\left(\nu\!\left(d(\hat{\theta},\theta)\right)\geq\nu(s)\right)\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}_{\theta}\!\left[\nu\!\left(d(\hat{\theta},\theta)\right)\right]}{\nu(s)}\,,

(177)

where we used Markov’s inequality in the last step. As all inequalities do not depend on the choice of
\theta\in\Theta
and
\hat{\theta}
, the claim follows immediately.

Appendix E

The snake parameter class of

First, note that the
