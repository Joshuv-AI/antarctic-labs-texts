Abstract
We prove an asymptotic formula for the recurrence coefficients of orthogonal polynomials with orthogonality measure log ⁡ ( 2 1 − x ) ​ d ​ x \log\bigl(\frac{2}{1-x}\bigr){\rm d}x on ( − 1 , 1 ) (-1,1) . The asymptotic formula confirms a special case of a conjecture by Magnus and extends earlier results by Conway and one of the authors. The proof relies on the Riemann–Hilbert method. The main difficulty in applying the method to the problem at hand is the lack of an appropriate local parametrix near the logarithmic singularity at x = + 1 x=+1 .

Theorem

is a special case of a conjecture by Magnus, who analyzed in
[

]
a few examples of continuous weight functions with logarithmic singularities at the edge and in the bulk of the support, among others

which is equivalent to (

•

and

of the corresponding orthogonal polynomials satisfy for

\displaystyle a_{M,n}=\frac{\beta^{2}-\alpha^{2}}{4n^{2}}+\frac{2B}{n^{2}\log n}+\frac{2C}{n^{2}\log^{2}n}+o\big((n\log n)^{-2}\big)

(1.4)

and

\displaystyle b_{M,n}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}-\frac{1}{2}}{8n^{2}}+\frac{B}{n^{2}\log n}+\frac{C}{n^{2}\log^{2}n}+o\big((n\log n)^{-2}\big).

(1.5)

Additionally, it was conjectured that for the special case
w_{M}=w
from (
1.1
),
B=0
and

holds, which is confirmed by Theorem

. Note that for
w
, we have
\alpha=0
and
\beta=1
.

Remark 1.2
.

For a general orthogonality measure
{\rm d}\mu(x)
supported on

with recurrence coefficients

,
B_{n}
, the orthogonality measure defined by
{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}(t):={\rm d}\mu(1-2t)

leads to recurrence coefficients given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{A_{n}}{2},\qquad\widetilde{B}_{n}=\frac{B_{n}}{2}.

1.2
State of the art

Earlier work on Magnus’ conjecture was done by Conway and one of the authors in
[
4
]
using Riemann–Hilbert (RH) techniques. The weight function considered therein had the form

\displaystyle w_{k}(x)=\log\left(\frac{2k}{1-x}\right),\qquad x\in[-1,1),\quad k>1.

(1.6)

The authors prove the conjecture for this special case corresponding to
\alpha=\beta=0
, and also obtain
B=0
and
C=-\frac{3}{32}
, further suggesting that these constants do not depend on the behaviour of the weight function away from the logarithmic singularity.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
4
]
)
.

The recurrence coefficients
\big\{a_{n}^{(k)}\big\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
,
\big\{b_{n}^{(k)}\big\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of the orthogonal polynomials with orthogonality measure
w_{k}(x){\rm d}x
, with
w_{k}
given in (
1.6
), satisfy

\displaystyle a_{n}^{(k)}=-\frac{3}{16n^{2}\log^{2}n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right)\qquad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty

and

\displaystyle b_{n}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{16n^{2}}-\frac{3}{32n^{2}\log^{2}n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right)\qquad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Note that for
k>1
, the weight function
w_{k}(x)
has a positive finite value at
x=-1
, while
\lim_{k\to 1+}w_{k}(x)=w(x)
for
x\in[-1,1)
. Hence, we see different
1/n^{2}
-terms in Theorem
1.1
compared to Theorem
1.3
, consistent with the conjectural (
1.4
) and (
1.5
). Also observe that terms of order
1/\big(n^{2}\log^{2}n\big)
are identical in both theorems, in particular they do not depend on
k
. These terms can be interpreted as contributions from the logarithmic singularity at
x=+1
, which does not depend on
k\geq 1
.

For
k<1
, the weight function
w_{k}
would have a simple zero in the interior of
(-1,1)
and the uniqueness of the corresponding polynomials is no longer guaranteed. We will not deal with this case in the present paper.

The main difficulty encountered in
[
4
]
was the lack of a known parametrix in the vicinity of the logarithmic singularity, a key ingredient in the usual nonlinear steepest descent analysis. Hence, the authors relied instead on a technically involved comparison to the Legendre problem with weight function
w_{Leg}(x)\equiv 1
,
x\in(-1,1)
. Surprisingly, their argument could not be generalized in an obvious way to the weight function
w=\lim_{k\to 1}w_{k}
, due to the appearance of a simple zero of
w
at
x=-1
. While, an analogous comparison to the Jacobi problem with weight function
w_{Jac}(x)=1+x
(or something similar) seems suggestive, significant challenges remain due to the presence of the simple zero. In particular, the crucial Theorem 4.7 in
[
4
]
requires a different proof in this case: now one needs to control the behaviour of the Cauchy operator acting on spaces with Muckenhoupt weights.

Orthogonal polynomials with logarithmic weight functions, have applications to both pure mathematics and physics. In particular, apart from logarithmic singularities, these weight functions also tend to have zeros (see
[
15
, Section 8]
and
[
20
]
). Such applications motivate us in the present paper to extend the results in
[
4
]
to the weight function
w
in (
1.1
), having both a logarithmic singularity and a simple zero.

Remark 1.4
.

One might think, a priori, that the vanishing of a weight
w(x)
at a point should not give rise to serious technical difficulties. Naively, it would appear that only singularities in the weight, and not zeros, should present obstacles. In this regard, we recall the hope and the prophecy of Lenard’s 1972 paper
[
13
]
:

“It is the author’s hope that a
rigorous analysis will someday carry the results to the point where the true role of the zeros of the
generating function will be understood. When that day comes a capstone will have been put on a
beautiful edifice to whose construction many contributed and whose foundations lie in the studies
of Gabor Szegő half a century ago”.

1.3
Relation of the present work to
[
4
]

Significant parts of the analysis performed in the present paper are based on the analysis introduced in
[
4
]
. Hence, we will repeatedly refer to that paper for proofs of certain statements. This is justified by the fact that the majority of estimates found in
[
4
]
do not depend on the distinction
k>1
and
k=1
in (
1.6
). Thus, the proofs of many of the results will also hold for the weight function (
1.1
) that we are interested in. There are however certain propositions which have their analogs in
[
4
]
, but still deserve a separate proof due to some minor differences. These are Proposition
2.7
which is the analog of Proposition 2.5 in
[
4
]
, and Proposition
7.5
which is the analog of Propositions 5.3 and 5.4 in
[
4
]
. In the case of Proposition
2.7
, it is necessary to prove a slightly more general result than Proposition 2.5 in
[
4
]
. Meanwhile, Proposition 6.5 contains an application of Proposition
2.7
in its more general form, and uses different RH solutions than Propositions 5.3 and 5.4 in
[
4
]
. Both results are proven in Appendix
A
.

Finally, let us reiterate that the analog of Theorem 4.7 in
[
4
]
concerning the uniform boundedness of the inverse of a certain singular integral operator requires a completely different approach to the case
k=1
, due to the appearance of a simple zero in the weight function
w
at
x=-1
. This necessitates the construction of an appropriate local parametrix in the vicinity of the zero, which is then used to invert the RH problem locally. While the local parametrix is well-known and can be expressed in terms of Bessel functions, see
[
12
, Section 6]
, its appearance significantly complicates the analysis that follows. Crucially, the method of proving Theorem 4.7 in
[
4
]
is no longer sufficient in this new setting. In fact, the material found in Sections
4
–
6
is entirely devoted to formulating and proving Theorems
6.1
and
6.2
, which are the analogs of Theorem 4.7 in
[
4
]
. Here, a key role is played by Proposition
4.6
, which in a sense localizes the effect that the logarithmic singularity has on the uniform invertibility of the associated singular integral operator. Interestingly, Theorem 4.7 in
[
4
]
itself plays a crucial role in the proofs of these results. Sections
4
–
6
contain the main novelties of the present work.

1.4
Outline of the paper

In the following, we will briefly summarize the content of each section.

•

In Section
2
, we introduce two auxiliary weight functions, the model and the Legendre weight function, together with related quantities, which will be relevant for the RH analysis. We also list certain estimates and asymptotic results which will be used in later sections.

•

In Section
3
, we introduce the Fokas–Its–Kitaev RH problem for orthogonal polynomials. We proceed to perform the necessary conjugation and deformation steps to arrive at three distinct RH problems amenable to asymptotics analysis: the logarithmic, model and Legendre RH problems. We then state the relation between solutions of these RH problems and the corresponding recurrence coefficients.

•

In Section
4
, we derive an explicit formula for the Legendre resolvent, that is, the inverse of a singular integral operator associated to the Legendre RH problem. This formula is not used in
[
4
]
.

•

In Section
5
, we perform a detailed analysis of the known local parametrices for the RH problem near the point
-1
, which can be constructed explicitly using Bessel functions, as in
[
12
, Section 6]
. This leads, in particular, to uniform asymptotics on their growth as
n\to\infty
.

•

In Section
6
, we introduce modified versions of the three previously mentioned RH problems using the appropriate local parametrices from Section
3
around
z=-1
.
These modified RH problems are better suited when comparing their associated resolvents.
Thus, by showing the uniform invertibility of the modified Legendre resolvent, we obtain the uniform invertibility of the modified logarithmic and model resolvents, thereby proving an analog of Theorem 4.7 in
[
4
]
.

•

In Section
7
, we derive an asymptotic formula expressing the difference between the recurrence coefficients for the logarithmic weight and the recurrence coefficients for the model weight. The aforementioned uniform invertibility of the associated resolvents plays a crucial part in this argument. As the asymptotics of the recurrence coefficients for the model weight is known, Theorem
1.1
follows.

•

In Appendix
A
, we provide some proofs of more technical nature that are omitted from the main text.

It is also worth mentioning that a common step in the RH analysis – the construction of a local parametrix – is not performed in the vicinity of the logarithmic singularity at
+1
. The reason is, quite simply, as in
[
4
]
, that we were unable to find the local parametrix in the presence of such a singularity. Similar instances in which the local parametrix was not constructed explicitly can be found in
[
6
, Section 5]
and
[
11
]
, where non-constructive Fredholm methods were used instead. For a discussion of RH problems without explicitly solvable local parametrices, see
[
19
]
.

1.5
Notation

Throughout this paper, all contours that arise are finite unions of smooth and oriented arcs, with a finite number of points of (self)intersection. More details can be found in the book
[
3
]
which treats a more general class of so-called Carleson contours.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}
be such a contour and
m
an analytic function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
. For
s\in\Gamma
, we will denote by
m_{\pm}(s)
the limit of
m(z)
as
z\to s\pm
, provided this limit exists. The notation
z\to s\pm
denotes a nontangential limit in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
to
s\in\Gamma
, from the
+
, resp.
-
, side of the contour, see Figure
2
. Recall that as
\Gamma
is taken to be oriented, this notion is well-defined away from the points of intersection. Everything generalizes to matrix-valued functions
m
in a straightforward manner.

Figure 2:
A contour with a point of intersection.

We will denote by
\mathbb{C}_{\pm}
the upper, resp. lower open half plane of
\mathbb{C}
and use the notation
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
for the Riemann sphere
\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
. Unless specified otherwise,
z^{1/2}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0)
will denote the principal branch of the square root.

For two sequences
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
in a normed space, we will use the notation
A_{n}\lesssim B_{n}
if there exists a
c>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|A_{n}\|\leq c\|B_{n}\|
for all
n\geq N
. Equivalently, we will sometimes use the notation
A_{n}=O(B_{n})
. A similar definition holds if
n
is substituted for a continuous variable, e.g.,
f(x)\lesssim g(x)
(equivalently
f(x)=O(g(x))
) for
x\to x_{0}
means
\|f(x)\|\leq c\|g(x)\|
for some
c>0
and all
x
satisfying
|x-x_{0}|\leq\varepsilon
.

Finally, for a
d\times d
dimensional measurable matrix-valued function
f(s)
,
s\in\Gamma
, we write
f\in L^{p}(\Sigma)
, with
p\in[1,\infty)
, if and only if

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Gamma)}:=\Biggl(\int_{\Gamma}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}|f_{ij}(s)|^{p}|{\rm d}s|\Biggr)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty,

where
|{\rm d}s|
denotes the arc length measure of
\Gamma
, and
f\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
if and only if

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}:=\max_{i,j=1\dots d}\{\|f_{ij}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\}<\infty.

In particular,
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
denotes the
L^{2}
-norm on
\Gamma
of the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
f(s)
.
Generalizations to weighted
L^{p}
-spaces are introduced in Section
4
.

2.1
The model and Legendre weight functions

To obtain the recurrence coefficients related to the weight function
w
, we will have to compare with a different
model
weight function given by

\displaystyle\widehat{w}(x)=(1+x)\mathrm{e}^{d_{0}x},\qquad x\in[-1,1],

where
d_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is determined via (
2.7
). As we will see, the choice of
d_{0}
gives an error estimate in (
2.13
) of order
O\big(|1+z|^{3/2}\big)
, rather that
O\big(|1+z|^{1/2}\big)
, as in part 3 of Proposition 2.3 in
[
4
]
. This extra decay as
z\to-1
considerably simplifies the proof of the key Lemma
7.6
.

Note that
\widehat{w}
can be analytically extended to an entire function. Moreover,
\widehat{w}(x)
has a simple zero at
x=-1
and a finite positive value at
x=+1
. As it lies in the class of weight functions considered in
[
12
]
, the corresponding RH analysis is well understood. Observe that our requirements on
\widehat{w}
do not specify it uniquely, hence one could have performed the comparison argument with other choices of model weight functions as well.

As we will heavily rely on the arguments found in
[
4
]
, we will also introduce the Legendre weight function

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}(x)=1,\qquad x\in[-1,1].

As shown in
[
4
, Theorem 4.7]
(see also Theorem
4.2
), the weight
\widetilde{w}
gives rise to a singular integral operator defined in Section
4
, with an inverse that is uniformly bounded as
n\to\infty
. However,
\widetilde{w}
does not approximate the logarithmic weight function
w
for
x\to-1
, due to the presence of a simple zero at that point. In contrast, the weight
\widehat{w}
approximates the logarithmic weight function
w
for
x\to-1
, however for
\widehat{w}
the analogous singular integral operator is not invertible in
L^{2}
(see property (iv) in RH problem (see Section
3.4
) showing that the solution will not be square integrable). This is the essential technical difficulty that we face in this paper.

2.2
The Szegő functions

To perform the nonlinear steepest descent analysis, we need to define the Szegő function
F
associated to the logarithmic weight function
w
:

\displaystyle F(z)=\exp\Bigg(\dfrac{\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{\log w(s)}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\dfrac{{\rm d}s}{z-s}\Bigg),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1].

(2.1)

Here
\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}
is uniquely specified as an analytic function having a branch cut along
(-1,1)
, and
\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}\approx z
for
z\to\infty
. Analogously, we define
\widehat{F}
to be the Szegő function associated to the model weight function
\widehat{w}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(z)=\exp\Bigg(\dfrac{\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{\log\widehat{w}(s)}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\dfrac{{\rm d}s}{z-s}\Bigg),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1].

Note that as
\log\widetilde{w}\equiv 0
, the Szegő function for the Legendre weight
\widetilde{w}
is trivial:
\widetilde{F}\equiv 1
.

The Szegő functions
F
,
\widehat{F}
satisfy the following properties which are crucial for the RH analysis.

Proposition 2.1
.

The functions
F,\widehat{F}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]\to\mathbb{C}
satisfy the following properties:

(i)

F(z)
,
\widehat{F}(z)
are analytic for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
, with
F(\overline{z})=\overline{F(z)}
and
\widehat{F}(\overline{z})=\overline{\widehat{F}(z)}
,

(ii)

\lim_{z\to\infty}F(z)=F_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\lim_{z\to\infty}\widehat{F}(z)=\widehat{F}_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

(iii)

F_{+}(x)F_{-}(x)=w(x)
,
\widehat{F}_{+}(x)\widehat{F}_{-}(x)=\widehat{w}(x)
for
x\in[-1,1)
,

(iv)

|F_{\pm}(x)|^{2}=w(x)
,
\big|\widehat{F}_{\pm}(x)\big|^{2}=\widehat{w}(x)
for
x\in[-1,1)
.

Note that at
x=-1
, the limits of
F(z)
,
\widehat{F}(z)
are equal to
0
. In particular, they are independent of the path
z\to-1
, and we write
F_{\pm}(-1)=\lim_{z\to-1}F(z)=\lim_{x\downarrow-1}F_{\pm}(x)=0
and
\widehat{F}_{\pm}(-1)=\lim_{z\to-1}\widehat{F}(z)=\lim_{x\downarrow-1}\widehat{F}_{\pm}(x)=0
in this case.
We also introduce the function
\phi
given by (see
[
4

)

\displaystyle\phi(z)=z+\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1].

With this choice, we see that
\phi
defines a biholomorphism between
\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\setminus[-1,1]
and
{\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\setminus\{z\colon|z|\leq 1\}}
, mapping
\infty
to itself. In particular, the function
\phi
satisfies

\displaystyle|\phi(z)|>1,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1],

(2.2)

and

\displaystyle\phi(z)=2z+O\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\qquad\mbox{as}\quad z\to\infty.

Moreover, the inequality (
2.2
) holds uniformly away from the interval
[-1,1]
, while on the interval we have

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to x}|\phi(z)|=1,\qquad x\in[-1,1].

(2.3)

Additionally, as
\phi(\overline{z})=\overline{\phi(z)}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
, it follows from (
2.3
) that

\displaystyle\phi_{+}(x)\phi_{-}(x)=1,\qquad x\in(-1,1).

Near the points
z=\pm 1
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phi(z)&=1+\sqrt{2}(z-1)^{1/2}+O(|z-1|),\qquad z\to+1,\\
\phi(z)&=-1\pm\sqrt{2}\mathrm{i}(z+1)^{1/2}+O(|z+1|),\qquad z\to-1,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}_{\pm}.\end{split}

(2.4)

For the subsequent analysis, it is necessary to understand the behaviour of the functions
\frac{F^{2}}{w}(z)
,
\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}(z)
near the points
z=\pm 1
, as these functions show up in the jump matrices of the corresponding RH problems, see Section
3
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The function
\frac{F^{2}}{w}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{F^{2}}{w}(z)=1\mp\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{w(z)}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2w^{2}(z)}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{3}(z-1)}\right)

uniformly for any path
z\to+1
in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,\infty)
, where the
-
is taken for
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z)>0
and
+
for
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z)<0
, and also

\displaystyle\frac{F^{2}}{w}(z)=\phi(z)^{-1}\exp\bigl\{-\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}\big(d_{0}+O(|z+1|)\big)\bigr\},

(2.6)

uniformly for any path
z\to-1
in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,\infty)
, where

\displaystyle d_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\log\big(w(\zeta)/(1+\zeta)\big)}{(\zeta^{2}-1)^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}\zeta}{\zeta+1}

(2.7)

and
\gamma
is an oriented contour originating from the point
\zeta=1
, going anticlockwise around the interval
[-1,1]
and ending again at the point
\zeta=1
as depicted in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
The contour
\gamma
encircling the point
z
.

Proof.

For the proof of statement (
2.5
), see
[
4
, Proposition A.1]
. Statement (
2.6
) is a special case of
[
12
, Lemma 6.6]
, but with an additional restriction on the choice of the contour
\gamma
stemming from the logarithmic singularity of
w
. Due to this technicality, we repeat the proof found therein.

First, let us note that if
F_{1}
is the Szegő function of a weight
w_{1}
and
F_{2}
the Szegő function of a weight
w_{2}
, then the Szegő function
F_{12}
of the product
w_{1}w_{2}
is given by the product of the individual Szegő functions:
F_{12}=F_{1}F_{2}
. As the Szegő function of a Jacobi weight
w_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
with
\alpha,\beta>-1
, is given by (see
[
12
, Remark 5.1]
)

\displaystyle F_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\frac{(z-1)^{\alpha/2}(z+1)^{\beta/2}}{\phi(z)^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}},

we can conclude that

\displaystyle F(z)={}

\displaystyle\frac{(z+1)^{1/2}}{\phi(z)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\Bigg(\dfrac{\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{\log(w(s)/(1+s))}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\dfrac{{\rm d}s}{z-s}\Bigg),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1].

(2.8)

Again,
(z+1)^{1/2}
and
\phi(z)^{1/2}
denote the principal branches (a simple calculation shows that
\phi(z)\not\in(-\infty,-1]
for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,1]
). Moreover, for
x<-1
,
\big(\phi(x)^{1/2}\big)_{+}=-\big(\phi(x)^{1/2}\big)_{-}
as
\phi(z)\sim 2z
for
z\to\infty
. Thus
(z+1)^{1/2}/\phi(z)^{1/2}
is indeed analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
.

To analyze the argument of the exponential in (
2.8
), we choose for a fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
a contour
\gamma
as shown in Figure
3
. Then a residue calculation shows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log(w(s)/(1+s))}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\frac{{\rm d}s}{z-s}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{\log(w(z)/(1+z))}{\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\log(w(\zeta)/(1+\zeta))}{\big(\zeta^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}\zeta}{\zeta-z}.

(2.9)

Here we note the
w(z)/(1+z)
is analytic and non-zero in the simply connected region
\mathbb{C}\setminus[1,\infty)
, and hence
\log(w(z)/(1+z))
exists and is analytic in this region. Moreover, the integrand in (
2.9
) is integrable along
\gamma
as long as
z\not\in\gamma
.

Note also that as
\log(w(\zeta)/(1+\zeta))
has an iterated logarithmic singularity at
\zeta=1
, we cannot deform
\gamma
away from this point. Plugging (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) into the definition of
\frac{F^{2}}{w}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{F^{2}}{w}(z)=\phi(z)^{-1}\exp\Biggl(-\frac{\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\log(w(\zeta)/(1+\zeta))}{\big(\zeta^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}\zeta}{\zeta-z}\Biggr).

We compute the Taylor expansion around
\zeta=-1
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\log(w(\zeta)/(1+\zeta))}{\big(\zeta^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}\zeta}{\zeta-z}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}d_{k}(z+1)^{k},

where
d_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle d_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\log\big(w(\zeta)/(1+\zeta)\big)}{(\zeta^{2}-1)^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}\zeta}{(\zeta+1)^{k+1}}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

The function

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}(z)=\phi(z)^{-1}\exp\bigl\{-\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}d_{0}\bigr\}.

Proof.

Statement (
2.10
) follows directly from
[

, Lemma 6.4]
, while statement (

) can be obtained with the same line of reasoning as in Proposition

, but now the integral simplifies:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\log(\widehat{w}(\zeta)/(1+\zeta))}{\big(\zeta^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}\zeta}{\zeta-z}=\frac{d_{0}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{\zeta}{\big(\zeta^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}\zeta}{\zeta-z}.

In particular, we can deform the contour

to infinity leading to
\int_{\gamma}\frac{\zeta}{(\zeta^{2}-1)^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}\zeta}{\zeta-z}=2\pi\mathrm{i}
and finishing the proof.

3
The Riemann–Hilbert problem for orthogonal polynomials

In the following section, we recall the celebrated Fokas–Its–Kitaev characterization of orthogonal polynomials via RH problems
[
10
]
. We will state the problem explicitly in the case where the weight function is
w(x)
,
x\in[-1,1]
, but similar characterizations hold for the other weight functions
\widehat{w}
and
\widetilde{w}
.

3.1
Fokas–Its–Kitaev RH problem for the logarithmic weight

Find a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
Y=Y^{(n)}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]\to\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

Y(z)
is analytic for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
,

(ii)

Y
satisfies the jump condition

\displaystyle Y_{+}(s)=Y_{-}(s)\begin{pmatrix}1&w(s)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad s\in[-1,1],

(iii)

Y(z)\begin{pmatrix}z^{-n}&0\\
0&z^{n}\end{pmatrix}=I+O\big(z^{-1}\big)
, as
z\rightarrow\infty
,

(iv)

Y
is bounded away from the points
\pm 1
, and has the following behaviours near the points
\pm 1
:

\displaystyle Y(z)=\begin{pmatrix}O(1)&O\big(\log^{2}|z-1|\big)\\
O(1)&O\big(\log^{2}|z-1|\big)\end{pmatrix},\qquad z\to+1,

and

\displaystyle Y(z)=\begin{pmatrix}O(1)&O(1)\\
O(1)&O(1)\end{pmatrix},\qquad z\to-1.

The condition (iv) for
z\to+1
has been shown in
[
4
, Section 3.1]
for the case of the weight function having a logarithmic singularity, while the behaviour for
z\to-1
is a special case of the algebraic-type singularity of the weight function treated in
[
12
, Section 2]
.

To understand the connection between the RH problem for
Y
and orthogonal polynomials with respect to the orthogonality measure
w(x){\rm d}x
on
[-1,1]
, we need to introduce the Cauchy operator
\mathcal{C}_{[-1,1]}
on the interval
[-1,1]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{[-1,1]}\colon\ L^{2}([-1,1])

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]),

\displaystyle f(s)

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{[-1,1]}(f)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{f(s)}{s-z}{\rm d}s.

Here
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1])
denotes the space of functions holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
. When
\mathcal{C}_{[-1,1]}
is applied to matrix-valued functions, it is understood to act componentwise. Cauchy operators can also be defined on different contours, as in Section
4
.

We can now state the seminal result by Fokas, Its and Kitaev which characterizes orthogonal polynomials via a RH problem:

Theorem 3.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

The RH problem for
Y
is solved uniquely by

\displaystyle Y(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\pi_{n}(z)&\mathcal{C}_{[-1,1]}(\pi_{n}w)(z)\\
-2\pi\mathrm{i}\gamma^{2}_{n-1}\pi_{n-1}(z)&-2\pi\mathrm{i}\gamma^{2}_{n-1}\mathcal{C}_{[-1,1]}(\pi_{n-1}w)(z)\end{pmatrix},

where
\pi_{n}(z)
is the
n
-th monic orthogonal polynomial with respect to the weight function
w(x)
and
\gamma_{n}>0
is the leading coefficients of the orthonormal polynomial
p_{n}
, meaning
p_{n}=\gamma_{n}\pi_{n}
.

The fact that the Fokas–Its–Kitaev RH problem characterizes the corresponding orthogonal polynomials has lead to numerous new results, in particular in the case where the associated weight function satisfies local analyticity properties (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
12
]
), but also in the case of nonanalytic weights (see
[
1
,
11
,
16
,
17
,
21
]
). Instrumental in the derivation of those results has been the nonlinear steepest descent method, first presented in
[
8
]
to study the long-time asymptotics of the mKdV equation and later generalized to the Fokas–Its–Kitaev RH problem in
[
2
,
6
,
7
]
. It turns out that the recurrence coefficients can also be simply expressed in term of
Y
(see
[
5
]
):

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
Y_{1}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
be given through the expansion

\displaystyle Y^{(n)}(z)z^{-n\sigma_{3}}=I+\frac{Y_{1}^{(n)}}{z}+O\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)\qquad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty.

Then the recurrence coefficients
a_{n}
and
b_{n-1}
can be extracted from
Y_{1}^{(n)}
via the formulas

\displaystyle a_{n}=\big(Y_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{11}-\big(Y_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{11}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{n-1}^{2}=\big(Y_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{12}\big(Y_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{21}.

The nonlinear steepest descent analysis for weight functions supported on a single finite interval has been performed in
[
12
]
, and in the following we shall repeat the conjugation and deformation steps found therein. In the first step one normalizes the RH problem at infinity through an appropriate conjugation, viz.,

\displaystyle T(z):=\left(\frac{2}{F_{\infty}}\right)^{n\sigma_{3}}Y(z)\left(\frac{F(z)}{\phi^{n}(z)}\right)^{\sigma_{3}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1].

(3.1)

Then
T
turns out to be the unique solution of the following RH problem.

Find a
2\times 2

,

(ii)

\displaystyle T_{+}(s)=T_{-}(s)\begin{pmatrix}\frac{F_{+}^{2}}{w}(s)\phi_{+}^{-2n}(s)&1\\

(iii)

(iv)

\displaystyle T(z)=\begin{pmatrix}O\big(|z+1|^{1/2}\big)&O\big(|z+1|^{-1/2}\big)\\

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

-term:

Corollary 3.3

(
[
12
, Theorem. 1.10]
)
.

The recurrence coefficients
\widehat{a}_{n}
,
\widehat{b}_{n}
associated to the weight function
\widehat{w}
satisfy

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{n}=\frac{1}{4n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right),\qquad\widehat{b}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{16n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right).

To compute the asymptotics of the recurrence coefficients
a_{n}
,
b_{n}
, we will first compute the asymptotics of
a_{n}-\widehat{a}_{n}
,
b_{n}^{2}-\widehat{b}_{n}^{2}
and then use Corollary
3.3
. Hence, we will need an analog of Proposition
3.2
above for the differences of recurrence coefficients, which we express in terms of
Q
and
{\widehat{Q}}
.

Proposition 3.4

(
[
4
, Proposition 3.6]
)
.

Let
Q_{1}^{(n)}
and
{\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}
be given through the expansion

\displaystyle Q^{(n)}=I+\frac{Q_{1}^{(n)}}{z}+O\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)\qquad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty,

and

\displaystyle{\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}=I+\frac{{\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}}{z}+O\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)\qquad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty.

Then the differences
a_{n}-\widehat{a}_{n}
and
b_{n}^{2}-\widehat{b}_{n}^{2}
can be expressed via

\displaystyle a_{n}-\widehat{a}_{n}=\big(Q_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{11}-\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{11}-\big(\big(Q_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{11}-\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{11}\big),

(3.7)

and

\displaystyle b_{n-1}^{2}-\widehat{b}_{n-1}^{2}={}

\displaystyle\big(\big(Q_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{12}-\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{12}\big)\big(\big(Q_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{21}-\big(Q_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{21}\big)

\displaystyle+\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{12}\big[\big(\big(Q_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{21}-\big(Q_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{21}\big)-\big(\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{21}-\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{21}\big)\big].

(3.8)

The usefulness of Proposition
3.4
comes from the fact that
Q^{(n)}_{1}-{\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}_{1}
has a simple integral representation.

Proposition 3.5

(
[
4
, Proposition 4.9]
)
.

The following formula holds:

\displaystyle Q^{(n)}_{1}-{\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}_{1}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma}Q^{(n)}_{-}(s)\big(v^{(n)}(s)-{\widehat{v}}^{(n)}(s)\big)\big[{\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}_{-}(s)\big]^{-1}{\rm d}s.

(3.9)

Proof.

In the following, we drop the subscript
(n)
for better readability, i.e., write
Q_{1}
for
Q^{(n)}_{1}
and so on. Let us define the matrix-valued function
X(z)=Q(z)\big[{\widehat{Q}}(z)\big]^{-1}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma
. Note that

\displaystyle X(z)=I+\frac{Q_{1}-{\widehat{Q}}_{1}}{z}+O\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)\qquad\text{as }z\to\infty.

(3.10)

Moreover,
X
satisfies the jump condition

\displaystyle X_{+}(s)=X_{-}(s)v_{X}(s),\qquad s\in\Sigma,

where
v_{X}={\widehat{Q}}_{-}v{\widehat{v}}^{-1}{\widehat{Q}}_{-}^{-1}
. We claim that
X_{\pm}\in L^{1}(\Sigma)
. To see this let us first introduce the analog of
T
for the weight function
\widehat{w}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{T}(z)=\left(\frac{2}{\widehat{F}_{\infty}}\right)^{n\sigma_{3}}\widehat{Y}(z)\left(\frac{\widehat{F}(z)}{\phi^{n}(z)}\right)^{\sigma_{3}}.

Here
\hat{Y}
is the solution to the Fokas–Its–Kitaev problem for the weight function
\widehat{w}
. Then for
{z\in\Omega_{0}}
(cf. Figure
4
), we have

\displaystyle X(z)=T(z)\big[\widehat{T}(z)\big]^{-1}=O(1),\qquad z\in\Omega_{0},\quad z\to-1,

where we have used (
3.3
) and its analog (see
[
12
, Section 2]
)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}O\big(|z+1|^{1/2}\big)&O\big(|z+1|^{-1/2}\big)\\
O\big(|z+1|^{1/2}\big)&O\big(|z+1|^{-1/2}\big)\end{pmatrix},\qquad z\to-1.

(3.11)

Note that the analog of (
3.4
) remains valid

\displaystyle{\widehat{Q}}(z)=\begin{cases}\widehat{T}(z),&z\in\Omega_{0},\\
\widehat{T}(z)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}(z)\phi^{-2n}(z)&1\end{pmatrix},&z\in\Omega_{1},\\
\widehat{T}(z)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}(z)\phi^{-2n}(z)&1\end{pmatrix},&z\in\Omega_{2}.\end{cases}

Thus for
z\in\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle X(z)=T(z)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\mp\big(\frac{F^{2}}{w}(z)-\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}(z)\big)\phi^{-2n}(z)&1\end{pmatrix}\big[\widehat{T}(z)\big]^{-1}=O(1),

\displaystyle z\in\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2},\qquad z\to-1,

where the
+
, resp.
-
sign refer to
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
. Apart from (
3.3
) and (
3.11
), we have used in addition (
2.13
). As
X
can have only logarithmic singularities for
z\to 1
and otherwise the limits to the contour are analytic, it follows that
X_{\pm}\in L^{1}(\Sigma)
.

Next, we can use the Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem to conclude that
X
can be represented as

\displaystyle X(z)

\displaystyle=I+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{X_{+}(s)-X_{-}(s)}{s-z}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=I+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{Q_{-}(s)\big(v(s)[{\widehat{v}}(s)]^{-1}-I\big)\big[{\widehat{Q}}_{-}(s)\big]^{-1}}{s-z}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=I+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{Q_{-}(s)(v(s)-{\widehat{v}}(s))[{\widehat{Q}}_{-}(s)]^{-1}}{s-z}{\rm d}s,

where we made use of the particular form of the jump matrices
v
and
{\widehat{v}}
. This representation together with (
3.10
) implies (
3.9
).
∎

Finally, we recall the Legendre RH problem taken from
[
4
, Section 3.4]
. While this RH problem does not approximate the logarithmic RH problem globally, it does so near the logarithmic singularity and additionally gives rise to a singular integral operator whose inverse is uniformly bounded as
n\to\infty
(see Theorem
4.2
). The existence of a RH problem with these properties will be crucial for the proof of Theorem
6.2
. Note that the Szegő function for the Legendre weight is just given by
\widetilde{F}\equiv 1
.

3.5
Legendre RH problem

Find a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
{\widetilde{Q}}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
satisfying the following properties (see
[
4
, Section 3.4]
):

(i)

{\widetilde{Q}}(z)
is analytic for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma
,

(ii)

{\widetilde{Q}}
satisfies the jump condition

\displaystyle{\widetilde{Q}}_{+}(s)={\widetilde{Q}}_{-}(s){\widetilde{v}}(s),\qquad s\in\Sigma,

where

\displaystyle{\widetilde{v}}(s)=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\phi^{-2n}(s)&1\end{pmatrix},&\mbox{for}\ s\in\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2},\\
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
2\phi^{-2n}(s)&1\end{pmatrix},&\mbox{for}\ s\in(1,1+\delta),\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},&\mbox{for}\ s\in(-1,1),\end{cases}

(iii)

{\widetilde{Q}}(z)=I+O\big(z^{-1}\big)
as
z\rightarrow\infty
,

(iv)

{\widetilde{Q}}(z)
is bounded away from the points
\pm 1
, and has the following behaviours near the points
\pm 1
:

\displaystyle{\widetilde{Q}}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}O(\log|z-1|)&O(\log|z-1|)\\
O(\log|z-1|)&O(\log|z-1|)\end{pmatrix},\qquad z\to+1

and

\displaystyle{\widetilde{Q}}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}O(\log|z+1|)&O(\log|z+1|)\\
O(\log|z+1|)&O(\log|z+1|)\end{pmatrix},\qquad z\to-1.

The weight function
\widetilde{w}(x)=1
,
x\in(-1,1)
lies in the class of weight functions considered in
[
12
]
, and it follow from the calculations therein that the solution
{\widetilde{Q}}
can be globally approximated with arbitrary small errors. Moreover, unlike the logarithmic and model RH problems found in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
, the Legendre RH problem can be stated with a weaker
L^{2}
-condition instead of condition (iv) (cf.
[
4
, Proposition 3.2]
), as follows.

Proposition 3.6
.

The matrix-valued function
{\widetilde{Q}}
is the unique solution of the Legendre RH problem with the condition
(iv)
being replaced by the condition
{\widetilde{Q}}_{\pm}\in L^{2}([-1,1])
.

Proof.

Let
L
be another solution of the Legendre RH problem, but with
L_{\pm}\in L^{2}([-1,1])
instead of condition (iv). Then
\det L
will be a holomorphic function in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
with

\det L_{+}=\det L_{-}
on
\Sigma
and
\det L_{\pm}\in L^{1}(\Sigma)
. By Morera’s theorem, it follows that
\det L
is in fact an entire function with
\lim_{z\to\infty}\det L(z)=1
. Hence by Liouville’s theorem
\det L\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{C}
.

We conclude that
L
is invertible in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma
and we can define
{\widetilde{Q}}[L]^{-1}
. As with the determinant,
{\widetilde{Q}}[L]^{-1}
will have no jump across the contour
\Sigma
:
\big({\widetilde{Q}}[L]^{-1}\big)_{+}=\big({\widetilde{Q}}[L]^{-1}\big)_{-}
. Moreover, by the
L^{2}
-condition for
{\widetilde{Q}}
and
L
, we have that
\big({\widetilde{Q}}[L]^{-1}\big)_{\pm}\in L^{1}(\Sigma)
. It follows that
{\widetilde{Q}}[L]^{-1}
can be extended to an entire function. By Liouville’s theorem, we have that
{\widetilde{Q}}[L]^{-1}\equiv\lim_{z\to\infty}{\widetilde{Q}}(z)[L(z)]^{-1}=I
, hence
{\widetilde{Q}}=L
.
∎

4
An explicit formula for the Legendre resolvent

In this section, we will associate to the Legendre RH problem a singular integral operator. First, let us define the Cauchy operator on
\Sigma
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\colon\ L^{2}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma),\qquad f(s)\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}(f)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{f(s)}{s-z}{\rm d}s,

where
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma)
denotes the set of analytic functions on the open set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma
. For
f\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
, we further define the two Cauchy boundary operators by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{\pm}(f)(s)=\lim_{z\to s\pm}\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}(f)(z).

(4.1)

In our setting, the curve
\Sigma
is clearly a Carleson curve and hence the limit in (
4.1
) exists for almost all
s\in\Sigma

and satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{\pm}(f)\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
, see
[
3
]
for more details. We define the two Cauchy boundary operators via

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{\pm}\colon\ L^{2}(\Sigma)\to L^{2}(\Sigma).

These are bounded operators on
L^{2}(\Sigma)
, cf. Theorem
4.1
below. Note that
C^{\pm}_{\Sigma}
satisfy the important identity
C^{+}_{\Sigma}-C^{-}_{\Sigma}=1
.

More generally, the mapping in (
4.1
) induces a bounded operator on certain weighted
L^{p}
-spaces. To be precise let
\Gamma
be an oriented composed locally rectifiable curve (see
[
3
, Section 1]
) and
p\in(1,\infty)
. Given a weight function
r\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
,
r\geq 0
, define the Banach space
L^{p}(\Gamma,r)
of all measurable functions
f
on
\Gamma
, such that the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Gamma,r)}=\bigg(\int_{\Gamma}|f(s)|^{p}r(s)^{p}|{\rm d}s|\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

remains finite. Note that there is a
p
-th power of
r
in the integral. We say that
r
is a
Muckenhoupt weight
if
r\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(\Gamma)
,
1/r\in L^{q}_{\rm loc}(\Gamma)
and

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in\Gamma}\sup_{\rho>0}\bigg(\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\Gamma\cap D(s,\rho)}r(s^{\prime})^{p}|{\rm d}s^{\prime}|\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\Gamma\cap D(s,\rho)}r(s^{\prime})^{-q}|{\rm d}s^{\prime}|\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}<\infty,

where
D(s,\rho)
is the open disc around
s
of radius
\rho
and
1/p+1/q=1
. For any
p\in(1,\infty)
we denote the set of all Muckenhoupt weights by
A_{p}(\Gamma)
. The following results holds.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and let
\Gamma
be an oriented composed locally rectifiable curve. Assume
r\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
,
r\geq 0
is a given weight. Then the mappings

\displaystyle f\mapsto\lim_{z\to s\pm}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{f(s)}{s-z}{\rm d}s

(4.2)

define bounded operators from
L^{p}(\Gamma,r)\to L^{p}(\Gamma,r)
if and only if
r
is a Muckenhoupt weight, i.e.,
r\in A_{p}(\Gamma)
.

The proof can be found in
[
3
, Theorem 4.15]
, for more material on this topic with emphasis on RH theory see
[
14
]
. We will abuse notation and denote the mapping (
4.2
) by
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{\Gamma}
irrespectively of the choice of domain
L^{p}(\Gamma,r)
.

Next, let us define the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}\colon\ L^{2}(\Sigma)\to L^{2}(\Sigma),\qquad f\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}(f({\widetilde{v}}-I)),

and consider the following singular integral equation in
L^{2}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}){\widetilde{\mu}}=I.

(4.3)

Note that as the contour is bounded,
I
is indeed an element of
L^{2}(\Sigma)
. Equation (
4.3
) is, in fact, equivalent to the Legendre RH problem. More explicitly, any solution
{\widetilde{\mu}}
will give rise to a solution
L=I+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}({\widetilde{\mu}}({\widetilde{v}}-I))
of the Legendre RH problem with the condition
L_{\pm}\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
instead of condition (iv), as can be verified by direct computation. By Proposition
3.6
, the solution to the Legendre RH problem (see Section
3.5
) exists and is unique, hence we must have
{\widetilde{Q}}=L
implying

\displaystyle{\widetilde{Q}}=I+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}({\widetilde{\mu}}({\widetilde{v}}-I)).

(4.4)

Moreover, from the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula

\displaystyle{\widetilde{Q}}=I+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\big({\widetilde{Q}}_{+}-{\widetilde{Q}}_{-}\big)=I+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\big({\widetilde{Q}}_{-}({\widetilde{v}}-I)\big),

it follows, after taking the minus limit to the contour
\Sigma
, that
{\widetilde{\mu}}:={\widetilde{Q}}_{-}
is indeed a solution to (
4.3
). Moreover, any solution
{\widetilde{\mu}}
of (
4.3
) must be equal to
{\widetilde{Q}}_{-}
as can be seen from (
4.4
) and

\displaystyle{\widetilde{Q}}_{-}=I+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}({\widetilde{\mu}}({\widetilde{v}}-I))=I+\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}{\widetilde{\mu}}={\widetilde{\mu}}.

Together these arguments imply that (
4.3
) has a unique solution and hence
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}
must be injective. In
[
4
]
, it has been shown that
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}
is in fact uniformly invertible for
n\to\infty
, as described in the following result.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
4
, Theorem 4.5]
)
.

The operator
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}
is invertible for all sufficiently large
n
. Moreover, the operator bound of
(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}})^{-1}
as an operator
L^{2}(\Sigma)\to L^{2}(\Sigma)
remains uniformly bounded for
n\to\infty
.

Theorem
4.2
played a central role in
[
4
]
. The uniform invertibility of the operator
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}
will also be crucial in the approach presented here and is the motivation for introducing the Legendre RH problem in addition to the logarithmic and model RH problems. However, in order to use Theorem
4.2
, we will need to derive an explicit representation of the operator
(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}})^{-1}
. To accomplish this, we recall the definition of an
inhomogeneous RH problem of the first kind
, as introduced in
[
9
, Section 2.6]
. This notion has been instrumental in the proof of Theorem
4.2
in
[
4
]
. In the following,
h
will denote a matrix-valued function on a contour
\Gamma
, with
h^{\pm 1}\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
.

Inhomogeneous RH problem of the first kind

For a given
g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
, one seeks an
f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
, such that
m_{\pm}=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{\pm}(f)+g
satisfies the jump relation:

\displaystyle m_{+}(s)

\displaystyle=m_{-}(s)h(s),\qquad s\in\Gamma.

(4.5)

A similar notion of an inhomogeneous RH problem of the second kind can be found in
[
9
, Section 2]
but will not be needed here.

The importance of the above inhomogeneous RH problem comes from the following result proven in
[
9
, Proposition 2.6]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

The mapping
1-\mathcal{C}_{h}\colon L^{2}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\Gamma)
is invertible if and only if the corresponding inhomogeneous RH problem of the first kind is uniquely solvable for each
g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
. Moreover, the inverse satisfies
\big\|(1-\mathcal{C}_{h})^{-1}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq c
if and only if
\|m_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq c\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
for all
{g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)}
and the same constant
c>0
.

Remark 4.4
.

Note that by (
4.5
),
m_{+}=m_{-}h
on
\Gamma
, hence
\|m_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq c\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
implies
\|m_{\pm}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq c^{\prime}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
with
c^{\prime}=c\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}
.

As a corollary we obtain.

Corollary 4.5
.

Given a sequence
h_{n}
of matrix-valued functions with
h_{n}^{\pm 1}\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
, the operator
(1-\mathcal{C}_{h_{n}})^{-1}
is uniformly bounded if and only if the corresponding inhomogeneous RH problems are uniquely solvable with
\|m_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq c\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
, and
c>0
independent of
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
.

Note that Theorem
4.2
is proven in
[
4
, Section 4.2]
via Corollary
4.5
. We will use Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
to derive an explicit expression for the inverse
(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}})^{-1}
in Proposition
4.6
below. This allows us to identify locally the contribution of the logarithmic singularity to the uniform boundedness of
(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}})^{-1}
, which is central to our approach as it enables us to prove Theorem
6.2
.

Proposition 4.6
.

The inverse of
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}
has the explicit form

\displaystyle(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}})^{-1}\colon\ L^{2}(\Sigma)\to L^{2}(\Sigma),\qquad g\mapsto g+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}\big(g({\widetilde{v}}-I){\widetilde{Q}}_{+}^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}}_{-}.

(4.6)

Proof.

From Theorem
4.3
, we know that for
g\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
, the (unique) solvability of the equation

\displaystyle(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}})\psi=g,\quad g\in L^{2}(\Sigma),

(4.7)

in
L^{2}(\Sigma)
is equivalent to the (unique) solvability of the following inhomogeneous RH problem.

Inhomogeneous Legendre RH problem

For a given
g\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
, one seeks an
f\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
, such that
m_{\pm}=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{\pm}(f)+g
satisfies the jump relation:

\displaystyle m_{+}(s)

\displaystyle=m_{-}(s){\widetilde{v}}(s),\qquad s\in\Sigma.

Note that Theorem
4.2
together with Corollary
4.5
imply that there exists a constant
c
independent of
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
g\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
such that the inhomogeneous Legendre RH problem has a unique solution
m_{\pm}
with
\|m_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}\leq c\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}
. We shall briefly recall the exact relation between (
4.7
) and the inhomogeneous Legendre RH problem (cf.
[
9
, Section 2]
), as it will be needed later in the proof. First, note that if we have a solution
m_{\pm}
to the inhomogeneous Legendre RH problem, we must have
f=m_{+}-m_{-}=m_{-}({\widetilde{v}}-I)
. If we now set
\psi:=m_{-}=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}(f)+g\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
, it follows that

\displaystyle(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}})\psi

\displaystyle=\psi-\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}(\psi({\widetilde{v}}-I))=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}(f)+g-\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}(m_{-}({\widetilde{v}}-I)))=g.

On the other hand, having a solution
\psi
to the integral equation (
4.7
), we can define
m_{\pm}:=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{\pm}(\psi({\widetilde{v}}-I))+g
and compute, using the Sokhotski–Plemelj (SP) formula,

\displaystyle m_{+}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{+}(\psi({\widetilde{v}}-I))+g\overset{\text{SP}}{=}\psi({\widetilde{v}}-I)+\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}(\psi)+g=\psi{\widetilde{v}}=m_{-}{\widetilde{v}},

(4.8)

as
m_{-}=\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}}\psi+g=\psi
.
We can now derive an expression for the operator
(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}})^{-1}
. Assume
g\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
is given and let
\psi\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
be the unique solution of
(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}}})\psi=g
. Then
m_{\pm}=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{\pm}(f)+g
with
f=\psi({\widetilde{v}}-I)
solves the corresponding inhomogeneous RH problem, as we have seen in equation (
4.8
). We want to find an expression for
\psi=m_{-}
in terms of
g
. To derive (
4.6
), we start with

\displaystyle m_{+}=m_{-}{\widetilde{v}},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{+}(f)+g=(\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}(f)+g)\tilde{v},\qquad\big(\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{+}(f)+g\big){\widetilde{Q}}_{+}^{-1}=(\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}(f)+g){\widetilde{Q}}_{-}^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{+}(f){\widetilde{Q}}_{+}^{-1}-\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}(f){\widetilde{Q}}_{-}^{-1}=g\big({\widetilde{Q}}_{-}^{-1}-{\widetilde{Q}}_{+}^{-1}\big).

(4.9)

Observe that the left- and right-hand sides in the last line might not lie in
L^{2}(\Sigma)
. However, using property (iv) of
{\widetilde{Q}}
from the RH problem (see Section
3.5
), we see that they lie in
L^{2-\epsilon}(\Sigma)
for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Hence, if we define
H=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}(f){\widetilde{Q}}^{-1}
, we see that
H
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma
, satisfies
H_{\pm}\in L^{2-\epsilon}(\Sigma)
and vanishes at
\infty
. By the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula, it follows that
H=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}(H_{+}-H_{-})
. Thus, applying
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}
, which is understood to act on the space
L^{2-\epsilon}(\Sigma)
, in the last line of (
4.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}(f){\widetilde{Q}}^{-1}=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\big(\underbrace{g\big({\widetilde{Q}}_{-}^{-1}-{\widetilde{Q}}_{+}^{-1}\big)}_{g(\tilde{v}-I){\widetilde{Q}}_{+}^{-1}}\big),\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}(f)=\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}\big(g({\widetilde{v}}-I){\widetilde{Q}}_{+}^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}(f)+g=g+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}\big(g({\widetilde{v}}-I){\widetilde{Q}}_{+}^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}}_{-},\qquad\psi=g+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{-}\big(g({\widetilde{v}}-I){\widetilde{Q}}_{+}^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}}_{-}.

Note here that

is a priori a function in

We have thus proved that

6
Modified RH problems

We are now in a position to define modified versions of the three RH problems found in Section
3
, which will be referred to as the respective
⋆
-analogs. The three
⋆
RH problems are introduced implicitly by defining their respective solutions. Here
U
is a small neighbourhood of
-1
, as before.

\displaystyle Q^{\star}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}Q(z),&z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\Sigma\cup U),\\
Q(z)[P(z)]^{-1},&z\in U,\end{cases}

\displaystyle{\widehat{Q}^{\star}}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{\widehat{Q}}(z),&z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\Sigma\cup U),\\
{\widehat{Q}}(z)[{\widehat{P}}(z)]^{-1},&z\in U,\end{cases}

\displaystyle{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{\widetilde{Q}}(z),&z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\Sigma\cup U),\\
{\widetilde{Q}}(z)[{\widetilde{P}}(z)]^{-1},&z\in U.\end{cases}

Note that because of (
5.7
), the jumps on
U\cap\Sigma
cancel out, which means that all three solutions can be uniquely defined on all of
U
. We will denote the new contour, which is the same for all three problems, by
\Sigma^{\star}=(\Sigma\setminus U)\cup\partial U
and assume that
\partial U
is oriented clockwise. The corresponding jump matrices are denoted by
v^{\star}
,
{\widehat{v}^{\star}}
and
{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}
, hence

\displaystyle Q^{\star}_{+}(s)=Q^{\star}_{-}(s)v^{\star}(s),\qquad s\in\Sigma^{\star},

and so on. Note also that the normalization at infinity remains unchanged and the jump matrices on
\partial U
are just the corresponding local parametrices:

\displaystyle v^{\star}(s)=P(s),\qquad{\widehat{v}^{\star}}(s)={\widehat{P}}(s),\qquad{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}(s)={\widetilde{P}}(s),\qquad s\in\partial U.

Now consider the singular integral operator

\displaystyle 1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}}\colon\ L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star}),\qquad f\mapsto f-\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(f({\widetilde{v}^{\star}}-I)).

We claim that this operator is invertible and that the inverse is given by

\displaystyle(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}})^{-1}\colon\ L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star}),\qquad f\mapsto f+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(f({\widetilde{v}^{\star}}-I)[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}.

At the moment, it is not even clear whether the above operator is well-defined as a map from
L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})
to itself, as
{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{\pm}
has a logarithmic singularity near
+1
.

To prove the claim, we proceed as follows: First, we partition the jump contour
\Sigma^{\star}=\underbrace{\partial U}_{\Sigma^{\ell}}\,\cup\,\underbrace{\Sigma\setminus U}_{\Sigma^{r}}
as shown in Figure
9
.

Figure 9:
The jump contour
\Sigma^{\star}=\Sigma^{\ell}\cup\Sigma^{r}
.

Next, we decompose the operator
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(\ \cdot\ ({\widetilde{v}^{\star}}-I)[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}
. Setting
{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}={\widetilde{v}^{\star}}-I
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(\ \cdot\ {\widetilde{u}^{\star}}[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}={}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(\ \cdot\ \chi_{\Sigma^{\ell}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(\ \cdot\ \chi_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(\ \cdot\ \chi_{\Sigma^{\ell}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(\ \cdot\ \chi_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}\chi_{\Sigma^{r}}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(\ \cdot\ \chi_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}\chi_{\Sigma^{\ell}},

where
\chi_{\Sigma^{j}}
is the characteristic function of
\Sigma^{j}
for
j=\ell,r
. By definition, we have
{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{\pm}\chi_{\Sigma^{r}}={\widetilde{Q}}_{\pm}\chi_{\Sigma^{r}}
. Note that the mapping

\displaystyle f\mapsto f\chi_{\Sigma^{\ell}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}\big[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}\big]^{-1}

is an operator uniformly bounded in
n
from
L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}\big(\Sigma^{\star},|z-1|^{-1/4}\big)
, as
\big[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}\big]^{-1}
converges uniformly on
\Sigma^{\ell}=\partial U
to the outer parametrix
N
, see (
5.8
) and (
5.9
). Here and in the following, we refer to
uniform boundedness in
n
of some operator
T=T_{n}
, to the operator norm
\|T_{n}\|
being bounded by an
n
-independent constant, cf. Theorem
4.2
. As
|z-1|^{-1/4}\in A_{2}(\Sigma^{\star})
(see Theorem
4.1
), we have that the mapping

\displaystyle f\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(f\chi_{\Sigma^{\ell}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}\big[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}\big]^{-1}\big)

defines a uniformly bounded operator from
L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}\big(\Sigma^{\star},|z-1|^{-1/4}\big)
. Finally, using
{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}={\widetilde{Q}}
in
\mathbb{C}\setminus U
and the estimate (
5.15
), we conclude that

\displaystyle f\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(f\chi_{\Sigma^{\ell}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}

defines a uniformly bounded operator from
L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})
.

The uniform boundedness of the mapping

\displaystyle f\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(f\chi_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}\chi_{\Sigma^{r}}

as an operator from
L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})
follows directly from Proposition
4.6
together with Theorem
4.2
.

Finally, the mapping

\displaystyle f\mapsto f\chi_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}\big[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}\big]^{-1}

defines a uniformly bounded operator from
L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})
to
L^{2}\big(\Sigma^{\star},|z-1|^{1/4}\big)
. As
|z-1|^{1/4}\in A_{2}(\Sigma^{\star})
, the mapping

\displaystyle f\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(f\chi_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}\big[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}\big]^{-1}\big)

is a uniformly bounded operator from
L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}\big(\Sigma^{\star},|z-1|^{1/4}\big)
as well. Moreover, the multiplication with
{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}\chi_{\Sigma^{\ell}}
defines a uniformly bounded operator from
L^{2}\big(\Sigma^{\star},|z-1|^{1/4}\big)\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})
, implying that

\displaystyle f\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(f\chi_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widetilde{u}^{\star}}\big[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}\big]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}\chi_{\Sigma^{\ell}}

is a uniformly bounded operator from
L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})
. We obtain:

Theorem 6.1
.

The inverse of the operator
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}}
on
L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})
exists and is given by

\displaystyle(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}})^{-1}\colon\ L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star}),\ \ f\mapsto f+\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}\big(f({\widetilde{v}^{\star}}-I)\big[{\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{+}\big]^{-1}\big){\widetilde{Q}^{\star}}_{-}.

Moreover, the operator norm is uniformly bounded as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

That the mapping is indeed uniformly bounded follows from the preceding argument. The fact that it coincides with the inverse of
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}}
follows from a computation analogous to the one given in the proof of Proposition
4.6
.
∎

6.1
Uniform invertibility of the
⋆
resolvents

The uniform boundedness stated in Theorem
6.1
can now be extended to the uniform invertibility of the operators
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}}
,
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}}
. Note that the jump matrices satisfy

\displaystyle\|v^{\star}-{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\star})},\ \|{\widehat{v}^{\star}}-{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\star})}\to 0

(6.1)

for
n\to\infty
. In fact, we have

\displaystyle v^{\star}-{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\big(\frac{F^{2}}{w}(s)-1\big)\phi^{-2n}(s)&0\end{pmatrix},&s\in(\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2})\cap\Sigma^{r},\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\big(\frac{F^{2}}{w}_{+}(s)+\frac{F^{2}}{w}_{-}(s)-2\big)\phi^{-2n}(s)&0\end{pmatrix},&s\in(1,1+\delta),\\
0,&s\in(-1,1)\cap\Sigma^{r},\\
P(s)-{\widetilde{P}}(s),&s\in\Sigma^{\ell},\end{cases}

(6.2)

and the corresponding claim in (
6.1
) for the difference
v^{\star}-{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}
follows from (
2.2
), Corollary
2.4
and (
5.8
). An analog formula of the form (
6.2
) can be written down for
{\widehat{v}^{\star}}-{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}
:

\displaystyle{\widehat{v}^{\star}}-{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\big(\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}(s)-1\big)\phi^{-2n}(s)&0\end{pmatrix},&s\in(\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2})\cap\Sigma^{r},\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
2\big(\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}(s)-1\big)\phi^{-2n}(s)&0\end{pmatrix},&s\in(1,1+\delta),\\
0,&s\in(-1,1)\cap\Sigma^{r},\\
{\widehat{P}}(s)-{\widetilde{P}}(s),&s\in\Sigma^{\ell}.\end{cases}

Here (
6.1
) follows as before after using estimate (
2.10
) instead of (
2.5
).

Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\|(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}})-(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}\to 0,\qquad\|(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}})-(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}\to 0

as

. As the operator
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widetilde{v}^{\star}}}
is uniformly invertible, a standard argument (see, e.g.,
[

, Theorem 4.7]
) shows that the operators
1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}
,
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}}
are also uniformly invertible for
n
large enough. We summarize:

Theorem 6.2
.

The operators
1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}
and
1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}}
are invertible for
n
large enough and the operator norms of their inverses are uniformly bounded as
n\to\infty
.

Note that Theorem
6.2
is the analog of Theorem
4.2
initially proved in
[
4
, Theorem 4.5]
, but for the logarithmic weight function and on a contour avoiding the problematic point

4.2
played a crucial role in the proof of Theorem

.

7.1
Some norm estimates

Let us define
\mu^{\star}_{n}=Q_{-}^{\star(n)}
and
\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}=\widehat{Q}_{-}^{\star(n)}
, where in the following we will make the
n
-dependence explicit. In particular, we will denote the jump matrices with a subscript
n
and the Cauchy operator with a superscript
(n)
for notational convenience, i.e.,
v^{\star}_{n}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}^{(n)}
and so on. We will now prove an analog of
[
4
, Proposition 5.1]
in the
⋆
-case.

Proposition 7.1
.

The following estimates hold for
n\to\infty
:

(i)

\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}=O\big(\frac{1}{n^{1/2}\log^{2}n}\big)
,

(ii)

\|\mu^{\star}_{n}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}=O\big(\frac{1}{n^{1/2}\log^{2}n}\big)
,

(iii)

\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1}(v^{\star}_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}=O\big(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}\log^{2}n}\big)
,

(iv)

\|(\mu^{\star}_{n}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n})-(\mu^{\star}_{n+1}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}=O\big(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}\log^{2}n}\big)
.

Proof.

The proof is for the most part taken from
[
4
, Proposition B.2]
, with the only difference being the contribution from
\Sigma^{\ell}=\partial U
instead of
\Sigma\cap U
.

For (i), let us write

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}^{2}=\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\ell})}^{2}+\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}^{2}.

(7.1)

As
{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}|_{s\in\Sigma^{r}}=\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}|_{s\in\Sigma^{r}}
,
v_{n}|_{s\in\Sigma^{r}}=v^{\star}_{n}|_{s\in\Sigma^{r}}
and
{\widehat{v}}_{n}|_{s\in\Sigma^{r}}={\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n}|_{s\in\Sigma^{r}}
, one can conclude from
[
4
, equation 5.1]
that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/2}\log^{2}n}\right).

(7.2)

Hence it remains to consider the term
\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\ell})}
. Recall that for
s\in\Sigma^{\ell}
we have
v^{\star}_{n}(s)=P^{(n)}(s)
and
{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n}(s)={\widehat{P}}^{(n)}(s)
and so it follows from Lemma
5.2
(ii) that

\displaystyle\|v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\ell})}=O\big(n^{-1}\big).

(7.3)

Moreover, from Lemma
5.2
(iii) it also follows that
\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(s)={\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}(s)\big[{\widehat{P}}^{(n)}(s)\big]^{-1}
is uniformly bounded for
s\in\Sigma^{\ell}
. Hence we conclude

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\ell})}=O\bigl(n^{-1}\bigr)

which together with (
7.2
) proves (i).

Point (ii) follows from (i) by considering

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}_{n}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}

\displaystyle=\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}I-\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}I=\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}\big(\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}I

\displaystyle=\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}\big(\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}=\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})).

As
\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}
are bounded operators (uniformly in
n
), it follows that

\displaystyle\|\mu^{\star}_{n}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}\lesssim\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/2}\log^{2}n}\right)

showing (ii).

For (iii), we will again decompose the norm as in (
7.1
). As before, following the arguments found in
[
4
, Proposition B.2]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1}(v^{\star}_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}\log^{2}n}\right).

For the remaining term, we will use the fact that the local parametrices
P^{(n)}(s)
,
{\widehat{P}}^{(n)}(s)
have an infinite series expansion in powers of
n^{-1}
which is uniform on
\Sigma^{\ell}
, see
[
12
, equation (8.2)]
:

\displaystyle v_{n}^{\star}(s)=P^{(n)}(s)\sim\left(I+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Delta_{k}(s)}{n^{k}}\right)N(s),

\displaystyle{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n}(s)={\widehat{P}}^{(n)}(s)\sim\left(I+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(s)}{n^{k}}\right)N(s),

where
\Delta_{k}
and
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}
are
n
independent and can be explicitly computed. In particular,

\displaystyle v^{\star}_{n}(s)-v^{\star}_{n+1}(s)=O\big(n^{-2}\big),\qquad{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n}(s)-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n+1}(s)=O\big(n^{-2}\big)

(7.4)

uniformly for
s\in\Sigma^{\ell}
. Hence, using the fact
\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(s)=I+O\big(n^{-1}\big)
uniformly for
s\in\Sigma^{\ell}
(see (
5.9
)), we can write

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1}(v^{\star}_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\ell})}\lesssim\|(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1})(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\ell})}+O\big(n^{-2}\big).

Additionally, it follows that
\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(s)-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1}(s)=O\big(n^{-1}\big)
, (even
O\big(n^{-2}\big)
see
[
12
, equation (8.7)]
) uniformly for
s\in\Sigma^{\ell}
. Together with (
7.3
), we can conclude that

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1})(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\ell})}=O\big(n^{-2}\big),

which proves (iii).

In order to prove (iv), we first write

\displaystyle(\mu^{\star}_{n}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n})-(\mu^{\star}_{n+1}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1})={}

\displaystyle\big(\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}I-\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}I\big)

\displaystyle-\big(\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}I-\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{{\widehat{v}}^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}I\big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n}))

\displaystyle-\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1}(v^{\star}_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n+1}))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\big(\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}-\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}\big)\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n}))

\displaystyle+\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1}(v^{\star}_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n+1})).

From the uniform boundedness of
\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}
and point (iii), it follows that

\displaystyle\big\|\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n+1}(v^{\star}_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n+1}))\big\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}\log^{2}n}\right).

For the remaining term, we have

\displaystyle\big(\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}-\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}\big)\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n}))

\displaystyle\qquad=\big(\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}\big(\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}\big)\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n})).

(7.5)

Now observe that

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}\lesssim\|v^{\star}_{n}-v^{\star}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\star})}.

(7.6)

It has been shown in
[
4
, p. 54]
that
\|v^{\star}_{n}-v^{\star}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\big(n^{-1}\big)
, hence with (
7.4
) it follows that

\displaystyle\|v^{\star}_{n}-v^{\star}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\star})}=O\big(n^{-1}\big),

which implies using the bound (
7.6
)

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}=O\big(n^{-1}\big).

Plugging this estimate together with (i) into (
7.5
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\big\|\big(\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n)}\big)^{-1}-\big(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}}^{(n+1)}\big)^{-1}\big)\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}_{n}(v^{\star}_{n}-{\widehat{v}^{\star}}_{n}))\big\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\star})}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}\log^{2}n}\right),

which implies (iv) and finishes the proof.
∎

We immediately get from Proposition
7.1
:

Corollary 7.2
.

The following estimates hold for
n\to\infty
:

(i)

\|{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\big(\frac{1}{n^{1/2}\log^{2}n}\big)
,

(ii)

\|\mu_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\big(\frac{1}{n^{1/2}\log^{2}n}\big)
,

(iii)

\|{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n})-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\big(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}\log^{2}}\big)
,

(iv)

\|(\mu_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n})-(\mu_{n+1}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\big(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}\log^{2}n}\big)
.

Note that we restricted the path of integration to
\Sigma^{r}
. The contributions coming from
\Sigma\cap U
turn out to be of smaller order as will be shown in Lemma
7.6
.

To obtain an asymptotic formula for the recurrence coefficients, we need to obtain an asymptotic formula for (
3.9
) which in our current notation reads

\displaystyle Q_{1}^{(n)}-{\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma}{\mu}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n}{\rm d}s.

(7.7)

The key in proving Theorem
1.1
lies in the following proposition.

Proposition 7.3
.

For
n\to\infty
the following estimates hold:

\displaystyle Q_{1}^{(n)}-{\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}=\frac{3}{16n\log^{2}n}\begin{pmatrix}-1&\mathrm{i}\\
\phantom{-}\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}n}\right)

and

\displaystyle Q_{1}^{(n)}-{\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}-\big(Q_{1}^{(n+1)}-{\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)=\frac{3}{16n^{2}\log^{2}n}\begin{pmatrix}-1&\mathrm{i}\\
\phantom{-}\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right).

(7.8)

We will make use of two important results stated in
[
4
, Section 5.2]
, but restricted to the contour
\Sigma^{r}
:

Proposition 7.4
.

The following estimates hold:

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\mu}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s=\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{4}n}\right),

(7.9)

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\mu}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n}{\rm d}s-\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\mu}_{n+1}\big(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}\big){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n}{\rm d}s-\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}\big(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}\big){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}{\rm d}s+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{4}n}\right).

(7.10)

Proof.

For (
7.9
), observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\mu}_{n}

\displaystyle(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s=\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s+\int_{\Sigma^{r}}({\mu}_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n})(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s.

(7.11)

As
\det{\mu}=\det{\widehat{\mu}}\equiv 1
and
v-{\widehat{v}}
has a nonzero entry only in the
21
-entry, it follows through explicit calculation that
\|(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=\|{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}
.
Now using estimates (i) and (ii) from Corollary
7.2

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Sigma^{r}}({\mu}_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n})(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s\bigg|\lesssim\|\mu_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}\big\|(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{4}n}\right).

In a similar fashion for (

), we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\mu}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n}{\rm d}s-\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\mu}_{n+1}(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n}{\rm d}s-\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad\phantom{=}{}+\int_{\Sigma^{r}}({\mu}_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n})\big[(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n}-(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}\big]{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad\phantom{=}{}+\int_{\Sigma^{r}}\big[({\mu}_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n})-({\mu}_{n+1}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1})\big](v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}{\rm d}s.

(7.12)

We can now estimate the two error terms in (
7.12
), using Corollary
7.2
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Sigma^{r}}({\mu}_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n})\big[(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n}-(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}\big]{\rm d}s\bigg|

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\|\mu_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}\|{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n})-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{4}n}\right),

and

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Sigma^{r}}\big[({\mu}_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n})-({\mu}_{n+1}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1})\big](v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}{\rm d}s\bigg|

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\|(\mu_{n}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n})-(\mu_{n+1}-{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}\|{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{r})}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{4}n}\right),

proving (
7.10
).
∎

The next result, stated in

4
, Section 5]

:

Proposition 7.5
.

The following estimates holds:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s=\frac{3}{16n\log^{2}n}\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}1&-\mathrm{i}\\
-\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}n}\right)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{3}{16n^{2}\log^{2}n}\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}1&-\mathrm{i}\\
-\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right).

Proof.

The proof is essentially given in

{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}

∎

Note that in the formula (

) the integral is over
\Sigma
, while in the Propositions

. It remains to show that the remaining integral over

, which is localized around the point

Lemma 7.6
.

For

, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{U}}{\mu}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right).

(7.13)

Proof.

First, we need to show that
Q^{\star}(z)=Q(z)[P(z)]^{-1}
for

is uniformly bounded as

. Note that this statement is more intricate than the corresponding statements for
{\widehat{Q}}(z)\big[{\widehat{P}}(z)\big]^{-1}
and
{\widetilde{Q}}(z)\big[{\widetilde{P}}(z)\big]^{-1}
, as the logarithmic weight function is not covered in
[

and hence we do not have equation (

) for

at our disposal. Recall that for
{s\!\in\!\Sigma^{\ell}=\partial U}

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}(s)=Q^{\star}_{-}(s)=Q(s)\big[P(s)\big]^{-1}.

(7.14)

From (

,
\mu^{\star}
is in fact the restriction of the analytic function
Q^{\star}(z)=Q(z)[P(z)]^{-1}
,

. Moreover,

for

, as
\mu^{\star}=(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}})^{-1}I
and
{(1-\mathcal{C}_{v^{\star}})^{-1}}

. Thus, using Cauchy’s integral formula, we get for
z\in U

\displaystyle|Q^{\star}(z)|=\bigg|\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\partial U}\frac{\mu^{\star}(s){\rm d}s}{s-z}\bigg|\lesssim O\left(\frac{1}{\mathop{\mathrm{dist}}(z,\partial U)}\right).

We have some freedom to choose
U
, so if necessary we can shrink it and conclude

\displaystyle|Q^{\star}(z)|=O(1),\qquad z\in U,

(7.15)

uniformly as
n\to\infty
. Let us now rewrite (
7.13
), where we drop the
n
-dependence for better readability:

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{U}}{\mu}(v-{\widehat{v}}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}{\rm d}s=\int_{\Sigma^{U}}Q^{\star}(s)P_{-}(s)(v(s)-{\widehat{v}}(s))\big[{\widehat{P}}_{-}(s)\big]^{-1}\big[{\widehat{Q}^{\star}}(s)\big]^{-1}{\rm d}s.

We now list bounds for each of the factors in the integrand:

•

(
2.4
) and (

\displaystyle v(s)-{\widehat{v}}(s)=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\big(\frac{F^{2}}{w}(s)-\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}(s)\big)\phi^{-2n}(s)&0\end{pmatrix}\\
\quad=O\big(|s+1|^{3/2}\mathrm{e}^{-cn|s+1|^{1/2}}\big),\qquad s\in\Sigma^{U}\setminus(-1,1),\\
0,\qquad s\in\Sigma^{U}\cap(-1,1),\end{cases}

•

(
5.9
) and (
7.15
) implies that

•

and (

Taking the contributions of all factors in (

∎

7.2
Asymptotics of the recurrence coefficients

Next, we will derive the asymptotics for the recurrence coefficients stated in Theorem

[

, Section 5.2]
.

Recall the result of Corollary

, which states that the recurrence coefficients of the orthogonal polynomials with weight function

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{n}=\frac{1}{4n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right),\qquad\widehat{b}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{16n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right).

(7.17)

With the help of Proposition
7.3
, we can now expand (
3.7
):

\displaystyle a_{n}-\widehat{a}_{n}

\displaystyle=\big(Q_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{11}-\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{11}-\big(\big(Q_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{11}-\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{11}\big)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{3}{16n\log^{2}n}\begin{pmatrix}-1&\mathrm{i}\\
\phantom{-}\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}n}\right)\Bigg)_{11}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{16n^{2}\log^{2}n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right).

Now substituting the asymptotic formula (
7.17
) for

implies (
1.2
).

The proof of the asymptotic formula (
1.3
) is more involved. First recall
[
12
, Section 8]
, in which it is shown that for
z
away from
\pm 1
, we have

\displaystyle{\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}(z)=\left(I+\frac{R_{1}(z)}{n}+Er(z,n)\right)N(z),

(7.18)

where
R_{1}(z)
,
Er(z,n)
are matrix-valued functions, holomorphic for
z\in\Omega_{0}
(cf. Figure
4
), satisfying

\displaystyle|R_{1}(z)|\leq\frac{c_{1}}{|z|},\qquad|Er(z,n)|\leq\frac{c_{2}}{|z|n^{2}},\qquad z\to\infty

(7.19)

for some

. Importantly,
R_{1}
is
n
-independent. As a consequence of (
7.18
) and (
7.19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\big|{\widehat{Q}}^{(n+1)}(z)-{\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}(z)\big|\lesssim\frac{c_{2}}{|z|n^{2}},\qquad z\to\infty,

from which

\displaystyle{\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}_{1}-{\widehat{Q}}^{(n+1)}_{1}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

(7.20)

follows. Additionally, direct computation leads to
N_{12}(z)=-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}z}+O\big(z^{-2}\big)
implying

\displaystyle\big({\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}_{1}\big)_{12}=-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

(7.21)

Now using (
7.8
), we conclude from (
7.20
) that

\displaystyle Q^{(n)}_{1}-Q^{(n+1)}_{1}

\displaystyle={\widehat{Q}}^{(n)}_{1}-{\widehat{Q}}^{(n+1)}_{1}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{2}n}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

(7.22)

Regarding formula (
3.8
), we now obtain using (
7.21
) and (
7.22
), together with Proposition
7.3
:

\displaystyle b_{n}^{2}-\widehat{b}_{n}^{2}={}

\displaystyle\big(\big(Q_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{12}-\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{12}\big)\big(\big(Q_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{21}-\big(Q_{1}^{(n+2)}\big)_{21}\big)

\displaystyle+\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}\big)_{12}\big[\big(\big(Q_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{21}-\big(Q_{1}^{(n+2)}\big)_{21}\big)-\big(\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n+1)}\big)_{21}-\big({\widehat{Q}}_{1}^{(n+2)}\big)_{21}\big)\big]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{3\mathrm{i}}{16n\log^{2}n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}n}\right)\right)O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\left(\frac{3\mathrm{i}}{16n^{2}\log^{2}n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{32n^{2}\log^{2}n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right).

(7.23)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle b_{n}^{2}-\widehat{b}_{n}^{2}=\big(b_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}\big)\big(b_{n}+\widehat{b}_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(b_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}\big)\big(2\widehat{b}_{n}+b_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(b_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}\big)\big(1+b_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}+O\big(n^{-2}\big)\big)

(7.24)

As
\widehat{b}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}+O\big(n^{-2}\big)
, see Corollary
3.3
, and
b_{n}>0
, we have that

, which with (

\displaystyle b_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{2}n}\right).

(7.25)

Substituting (
7.25
) once again into the term
\big(1+b_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}+O\big(n^{-2}\big)\big)
, we conclude from (

) that in fact

\displaystyle b_{n}^{2}-\widehat{b}_{n}^{2}=\big(b_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}\big)\big(1+O\big(n^{-2}\big)\big),

which with (

) implies

\displaystyle b_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}=-\frac{3}{32n^{2}\log^{2}n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right).

That together with the asymptotic formula (

.

Proposition A.1
.

Fix
R>0
. Let
r_{n},\tilde{r}_{n}\in(0,1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
be two sequences satisfying
r_{n},\tilde{r}_{n}\to 0
, such that
n\big|\frac{r_{n}}{\tilde{r}_{n}}-1|<R
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}(1+r_{n})-\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})+\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}(1+r_{n})-\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad=O(r_{n}\log|\log r_{n}|)+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}r_{n}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

where the implied constants in the
O
-terms depend only on
R
.

Proof.

Let us assume without loss of generality
r_{n}\geq\tilde{r}_{n}
. It follows from definition (
2.1
) that

\displaystyle\log F(1+r_{n})

\displaystyle=\big((1+r_{n})^{2}-1\big)^{1/2}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(s)}{\big(s^{2}-1\big)_{+}^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s-(1+r_{n})}

\displaystyle=\big(\sqrt{2}r_{n}^{1/2}+O\big(r_{n}^{3/2}\big)\big)\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(s)}{\big(s^{2}-1\big)_{+}^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s-(1+r_{n})}.

Moreover, by
[
4
, equation A.11]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(s)}{\big(s^{2}-1\big)_{+}^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s-(1+r_{n})}=O\left(\frac{\log|\log r_{n}|}{r_{n}^{1/2}}\right),

implying that, in fact,

\displaystyle\log F(1+r_{n})=\sqrt{2}r_{n}^{1/2}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(s)}{\big(s^{2}-1\big)_{+}^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s-(1+r_{n})}+O(r_{n}\log|\log r_{n}|).

In particular, we obtain

\displaystyle\log\frac{F(1+r_{n})}{F(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}={}

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}r_{n}^{1/2}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log w(s)}{\big(s^{2}-1\big)_{+}^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s-(1+r_{n})}

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log w(s)}{\big(s^{2}-1\big)_{+}^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s-(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{\log w(s)}{\big(s^{2}-1\big)_{+}^{1/2}}\Bigg[\frac{\sqrt{2}r_{n}^{1/2}}{s-(1+r_{n})}-\frac{\sqrt{2}\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}}{s-(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}\Bigg]{\rm d}s

\displaystyle+O(r_{n}\log|\log r_{n}|).

(A.1)

The term in the bracket can be estimated as follow:

\displaystyle\Bigg|\frac{\sqrt{2}r_{n}^{1/2}}{s-(1+r_{n})}-\frac{\sqrt{2}\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}}{s-(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}\Bigg|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\sqrt{2}\frac{r_{n}^{1/2}(s-(1+\tilde{r}_{n}))-\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}(s-(1+r_{n}))}{(s-(1+r_{n}))(s-(1+\tilde{r}_{n}))}\Bigg|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\sqrt{2}\frac{\big(s-1+r_{n}^{1/2}\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\big)\big(r_{n}^{1/2}-\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\big)}{(s-(1+r_{n}))(s-(1+\tilde{r}_{n}))}\Bigg|

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Bigg|\sqrt{2}\frac{\big(s-1+r_{n}^{1/2}\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\big)\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}}{(s-(1+r_{n}))(s-(1+\tilde{r}_{n}))}\Bigg|}_{=O\big(r_{n}^{1/2}\big)}\cdot\underbrace{\left|\left(\frac{r_{n}}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\right)^{1/2}-1\right|}_{<\frac{R}{n}}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{r_{n}^{1/2}}{n}\right),

where we used that
s\in(-1,0)
. Thus (
A.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\log\frac{F(1+r_{n})}{F(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}={}

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}r_{n}^{1/2}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log w(s)}{\big(s^{2}-1\big)_{+}^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s-(1+r_{n})}

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log w(s)}{\big(s^{2}-1\big)_{+}^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s-(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}

\displaystyle+O(r_{n}\log|\log r_{n}|)+O\left(\frac{r_{n}^{1/2}}{n}\right),

Now, after the change of variables
t=1-s
and some algebraic manipulation, we get (cf. proof of
[
4

)

\displaystyle\log\frac{F(1+r_{n})}{F(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}={}

\displaystyle\frac{r_{n}^{1/2}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log w(1-t)}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+r_{n}}-\frac{\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log w(1-t)}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+\tilde{r}_{n}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi}H(r_{n},\tilde{r}_{n})+O(r_{n}\log|\log r_{n}|)+O\left(\frac{r_{n}^{1/2}}{n}\right),

(A.2)

where

\displaystyle H(r_{n},\tilde{r}_{n})={}

\displaystyle\big(r_{n}^{1/2}-\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\big)\int_{0}^{1}\log w(1-t)\left[\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2-t}+(2-t)}\right]\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+r_{n}}

\displaystyle+\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1}\log w(1-t)\left[\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2-t}+(2-t)}\right]\left(\frac{1}{t+r_{n}}-\frac{1}{t+\tilde{r}_{n}}\right){\rm d}t.

Thus, we can estimate

\displaystyle|H(r_{n},\tilde{r}_{n})|\leq{}

\displaystyle\big|r_{n}^{1/2}-\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\big|\int_{0}^{1}|\log w(1-t)|\left[\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2-t}+(2-t)}\right]\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}

\displaystyle+\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1}|\log w(1-t)|\left[\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2-t}+(2-t)}\right]\frac{|\tilde{r}_{n}-r_{n}|{\rm d}t}{|t+r_{n}|\cdot|t+\tilde{r}_{n}|}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle c\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\left|\frac{r_{n}^{1/2}}{\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}}-1\right|+\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\left|\frac{\tilde{r}_{n}-r_{n}}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\right|\int_{0}^{1}|\log w(1-t)|\left[\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2-t}+(2-t)}\right]\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}

So, we see that

can be included in the error term

For the remaining integrals in (

\displaystyle r_{n}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log w(1-t)}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+r_{n}}-\tilde{r}_{n}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log w(1-t)}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+\tilde{r}_{n}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{1/r_{n}}\frac{\log w(1-r_{n}t)}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+1}-\int_{0}^{1/\tilde{r}_{n}}\frac{\log w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+1}

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{1/r_{n}}\frac{\big[\log w(1-r_{n}t)-\log w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)\big]}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+1}-\int_{1/r_{n}}^{1/\tilde{r}_{n}}\frac{\log w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+1}.

(A.3)

Using the fact that

is uniformly bounded for

, the last integral in (

\displaystyle\left|\int_{1/r_{n}}^{1/\tilde{r}_{n}}\frac{\log w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t+1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{1}{t+1}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(1/r_{n},1/\tilde{r}_{n})}\int_{1/r_{n}}^{1/\tilde{r}_{n}}|\log w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)|{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq cr_{n}^{3/2}\left|\frac{1}{\tilde{r}_{n}}-\frac{1}{r_{n}}\right|=cr_{n}^{1/2}\left|\frac{r_{n}}{\tilde{r}_{n}}-1\right|=O\left(\frac{r_{n}^{1/2}}{n}\right),

and hence can be again included in the

-term in (
A.2
).

Let us now consider the remaining integral in the last line of (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/r_{n}}\big[\log w(1-r_{n}t)-\log w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)\big]\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{1/r_{n}}\log\left(\frac{w(1-r_{n}t)}{w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)}\right)\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}

(A.4)

Now define
a=a(r_{n},\tilde{r}_{n};n):=n\big(\frac{r_{n}}{\tilde{r}_{n}}-1\big)\in[-R,R]
. Note that for
t\in\big(0,\frac{1}{r_{n}}\big)

\log 2\leq\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}
, hence

\displaystyle\frac{w(1-r_{n}t)}{w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\frac{2}{r_{n}t}}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}=1+\frac{\log\frac{2}{r_{n}t}-\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}=1+\frac{\log\frac{\tilde{r}_{n}}{r_{n}}}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\log(1+\frac{a}{n})}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}=1+\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

Thus, we can estimate the integrand of (
A.4
) by

\displaystyle\log\frac{w(1-r_{n}t)}{w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)}=\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

where the
O\big(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\big)
-term is uniform for
t\in\big[0,\frac{1}{r_{n}}\big]
. Substituting this into (
A.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/r_{n}}\left(\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(\int_{0}^{r_{n}^{1/2}}+\int_{r_{n}^{1/2}}^{1/r_{n}^{1/2}}+\int_{1/r_{n}^{1/2}}^{1/r_{n}}\right)\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

(A.5)

Note that here we use the assumption
r_{n}<1
.
Two of the integrals in (
A.5
) can be estimated by

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{r_{n}^{1/2}}\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}\right|\leq\left|\int_{0}^{r_{n}^{1/2}}\frac{c_{1}}{n\log r_{n}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{1/2}}\right|=\frac{2c_{1}r_{n}^{1/4}}{n|\log r_{n}|}=O\left(\frac{r_{n}^{1/4}}{n\log r_{n}}\right)

and

\displaystyle\left|\int_{1/r_{n}^{1/2}}^{1/r_{n}}\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}\right|\leq\left|\int_{1/r_{n}^{1/2}}^{1/r_{n}}\frac{c_{2}}{n}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}}\right|\leq\frac{c_{2}r_{n}^{1/4}}{n}=O\left(\frac{r_{n}^{1/4}}{n}\right).

For the remaining integral, we have

\displaystyle\int_{r_{n}^{1/2}}^{1/r_{n}^{1/2}}\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}t}}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{n}^{1/2}}^{1/r_{n}^{1/2}}\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}-n\log t}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{n}^{1/2}}^{1/r_{n}^{1/2}}\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{1-\frac{\log t}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}}\Bigg)\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}.

(A.6)

Note that because
t\in\big[r_{n}^{1/2},r_{n}^{-1/2}\big]
we have
|\log t|\leq\frac{1}{2}\log{\frac{1}{r_{n}}}<\frac{1}{2}\log{\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}
for
n
sufficiently large depending on
R
, so the last integral in (
A.6
) can be estimated by

\displaystyle\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}\int_{r_{n}^{1/2}}^{1/r_{n}^{1/2}}\left(1+\frac{\log t}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}t}{\log^{2}\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}\right)\right)\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}.

Making the change of variables
\gamma=t^{1/2}
, this can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{2a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}\int_{r_{n}^{1/4}}^{1/r_{n}^{1/4}}\left(1+\frac{2\log\gamma}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}\gamma}{\log^{2}\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}\right)\right)\frac{{\rm d}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+1}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{2a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}\left(\int_{r_{n}^{1/4}}^{1/r_{n}^{1/4}}\frac{{\rm d}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+1}+\int_{r_{n}^{1/4}}^{1/r_{n}^{1/4}}\frac{2\log\gamma}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}\frac{{\rm d}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+1}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}r_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{2a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+1}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2\log\gamma}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}\frac{{\rm d}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+1}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}r_{n}}\right).

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+1}=\arctan\gamma\Big|^{\infty}_{0}=\frac{\pi}{2},

while the substitution
\eta=\gamma^{-1}
yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log(\gamma){\rm d}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+1}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log(\eta){\rm d}\eta}{\eta^{2}+1}

implying
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log(\gamma){\rm d}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+1}=0
.

Summarizing, we have shown that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/r_{n}}\big[\log w(1-r_{n}t)-\log w(1-\tilde{r}_{n}t)\big]\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}+t^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{a\pi}{n\log\big(\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\big)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}r_{n}}\right).

We can substitute this estimate in (
A.3
) and then (
A.2
) to obtain

\displaystyle\log\frac{F^{2}(1+r_{n})}{F^{2}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}=2\log\frac{F(1+r_{n})}{F(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}=\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+\Theta(r_{n},n),

(A.7)

where
\Theta(r_{n},n)
is short for
O(r_{n}\log|\log r_{n}|)+O\big(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}r_{n}}\big)+O\big(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\big)
. Exponentiating the expression (
A.7
) leads to

\displaystyle\frac{F^{2}(1+r_{n})}{F^{2}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}=1+\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+\Theta(r_{n},n).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{F^{2}}{w_{\pm}}(1+r_{n})-\frac{F^{2}}{w_{\pm}}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{F^{2}(1+r_{n})-F^{2}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}+F^{2}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})\left(\frac{1}{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}-\frac{1}{w_{\pm}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{F^{2}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}\left(\frac{F^{2}(1+r_{n})}{F^{2}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}-1\right)+\frac{F^{2}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}\left(1-\frac{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}{w_{\pm}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{F^{2}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}\left(\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+\Theta(r_{n},n)+1-\frac{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}{w_{\pm}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}\right).

(A.8)

For the ratio of the weight functions, we have (here
\pm
refers to the limit from
\mathbb{C}_{\pm}
):

\displaystyle\frac{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}{w_{\pm}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\frac{2}{r_{n}}\pm\pi\mathrm{i}}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\pm\pi\mathrm{i}}=1+\frac{\log\frac{2}{r_{n}}-\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\pm\pi\mathrm{i}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\log\frac{\tilde{r}_{n}}{r_{n}}}{\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\pm\pi\mathrm{i}}=1+\frac{a}{n\big(\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\pm\pi\mathrm{i}\big)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

so

\displaystyle\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+1-\frac{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}{w_{\pm}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}-\frac{a}{n\big(\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\pm\pi\mathrm{i}\big)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}a}{n\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\big(\log\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}\pm\pi\mathrm{i}\big)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}a}{n\log^{2}\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}r_{n}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

(A.9)

Note that both error terms in (
A.9
) already appear in
\Theta(r_{n},n)
. Substituting (
A.9
) into (
A.8
) and using (
2.5
) leads to

\displaystyle\frac{F^{2}}{w_{\pm}}(1+r_{n})-\frac{F^{2}}{w_{\pm}}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{F^{2}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})}{w_{\pm}(1+r_{n})}\left(\pm\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}a}{n\log^{2}\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+\Theta(r_{n},n)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log r_{n}}\right)\right)\left(\pm\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}a}{n\log^{2}\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+\Theta(r_{n},n)\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}a}{n\log^{2}\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_{n}}}+\Theta(r_{n},n).

In particular,

\displaystyle\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}(1+r_{n})-\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})+\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}(1+r_{n})-\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}(1+\tilde{r}_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad=\Theta(r_{n},n)=O(r_{n}\log|\log r_{n}|)+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}r_{n}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

finishing the proof.
∎

\displaystyle{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\big(f_{1}^{-1}\big)^{\prime}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{n^{2}}\right){\rm d}y_{1}=\frac{2{\rm d}y_{1}}{n^{2}}\left(1+\frac{ay_{1}}{n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right),

\displaystyle{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\big(f_{1}^{-1}\big)^{\prime}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\right){\rm d}y_{2}=\frac{2{\rm d}y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\left(1+\frac{ay_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right).

Now performing the substitution
y_{1}=n^{2}f(s)
in the expression (
A.16
), we get

\displaystyle-\int_{1}^{1+1/n}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{4\pi}{n}\int_{0}^{n^{2}f(1+1/n)}\left(I+\frac{\mathcal{R}_{1}\big(f_{1}^{-1}\big(\frac{y_{1}}{n^{2}}\big)\big)}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\mathcal{E}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{n^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}\times\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2\pi n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{K}_{1}(y_{1})\mathcal{K}_{2}(y_{1})&-\mathcal{K}^{2}_{1}(y_{1})\\
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{2}(y_{1})&-\mathcal{K}_{1}(y_{1})\mathcal{K}_{2}(y_{1})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2\pi n}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}\times\mathcal{E}^{-1}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\left(I-\frac{\mathcal{R}_{1}\big(f_{1}^{-1}\big(\frac{y_{1}}{n^{2}}\big)\big)}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}\times\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}}{\widehat{F}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\right)^{2}\left(\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{n^{2}}\right)\right)+\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{n^{2}}\right)\right)-2\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}\times\left(1+\frac{ay_{1}}{n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right){\rm d}y_{1}.

(A.17)

We get a similar expression for the second integral in (
A.11
) after the change of variables
s=f_{1}^{-1}\big(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\big)
:

\displaystyle-\int_{1}^{1+1/(n+1)}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}^{-1}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{4\pi}{n+1}\int_{0}^{(n+1)^{2}f(1+1/(n+1))}\left(I+\frac{\mathcal{R}_{1}\big(f_{1}^{-1}\big(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\big)\big)}{n+1}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}\times\mathcal{E}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2\pi(n+1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{K}_{1}(y_{2})\mathcal{K}_{2}(y_{2})&-\mathcal{K}^{2}_{1}(y_{2})\\
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{2}(y_{2})&-\mathcal{K}_{1}(y_{2})\mathcal{K}_{2}(y_{2})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}\times\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2\pi(n+1)}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{E}^{-1}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)\left(I-\frac{\mathcal{R}_{1}\big(f_{1}^{-1}\big(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\big)\big)}{n+1}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}\times\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}}{\widehat{F}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)\right)^{2}\bigg(\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)+\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=\times}{}-2\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)\bigg)\left(1+\frac{ay_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right){\rm d}y_{2}.

(A.18)

Now for
0<y<cn
, we have uniformly

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{R}_{1}\big(f_{1}^{-1}\big(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\big)\big)}{n}-\frac{\mathcal{R}_{1}\big(f_{1}^{-1}\big(\frac{y}{(n+1)^{2}}\big)\big)}{n+1}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)-\mathcal{E}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)=O\left(\frac{y}{n^{3}}\right)\lesssim O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2\pi n}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2\pi(n+1)}\end{pmatrix}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{ay}{n^{2}}-\frac{ay}{(n+1)^{2}}=O\left(\frac{y}{n^{3}}\right)\lesssim O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{W}}{\widehat{F}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)-\frac{\mathcal{W}}{\widehat{F}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)=O\left(\frac{y^{1/2}}{n^{2}}\right),

where we used
[
12
, Lemma 6.4]
in the last estimate. Note that all these error terms can be uniformly bounded by
O\big(\frac{1+y^{1/2}}{n^{2}}\big)
. Additionally, we can choose
r_{n}=f_{1}^{-1}\big(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\big)-1=O\big(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\big)
,
\tilde{r}_{n}=f_{1}^{-1}\big(\frac{y}{(n+1)^{2}}\big)-1=O\big(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\big)
in Proposition
2.7
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)+\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)-\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)\right)=O\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\log\left|\log\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right|\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}n}\right).

Note that indeed
n\big|\frac{r_{n}}{\tilde{r}_{n}}-1\big|<R
for an appropriate
R>0
, and
r_{n},\tilde{r}_{n}\in(0,1)
for
n
large enough due to
0<y<cn
.

From
[
18
, equation (10.40)]
, we have that

\displaystyle K_{0}(u)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2u}}\mathrm{e}^{-u},\qquad K_{0}^{\prime}(u)\sim-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2u}}\mathrm{e}^{-u}

(A.19)

for
u\to\infty
with
|\arg u|<\frac{3\pi}{2}-\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon>0
, implying that the
\mathcal{K}_{j}(y)
decay exponentially for
y\to\infty
. Therefore, changing the limit of integration from
(n+1)^{2}f(1+1/(n+1))
to
n^{2}f(1+1/n)
in (
A.18
) will only introduce an exponentially small error which we will neglect. We are now in a position to evaluate (
A.11
), by taking the difference of (
A.17
) and (
A.18
):

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s+\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{4\pi}{n}+\frac{4\pi}{n+1}\right)\int_{0}^{n^{2}f(1+1/n)}\left(I+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\mathcal{E}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2\pi n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{K}_{1}(y)\mathcal{K}_{2}(y)&-\mathcal{K}^{2}_{1}(y)\\
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{2}(y)&-\mathcal{K}_{1}(y)\mathcal{K}_{2}(y)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2\pi n}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathcal{E}^{-1}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\left(I+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}}{\widehat{F}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)+\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)-2\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\right)\frac{1}{2}(f_{1}^{-1})^{\prime}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right){\rm d}y

\displaystyle\qquad\phantom{\times}+\frac{4\pi}{n}O\left(\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{K}_{1}(y)\mathcal{K}_{2}(y)&-\mathcal{K}^{2}_{1}(y)\\
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{2}(y)&-\mathcal{K}_{1}(y)\mathcal{K}_{2}(y)\end{pmatrix}\left(\frac{1+y^{1/2}}{n^{2}}\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(0,n^{2}f(1+1/n))}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\phantom{\times}+\frac{4\pi}{n}\bigg\|\left[O\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\log\left|\log\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right|\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{K}_{1}(y)\mathcal{K}_{2}(y)&-\mathcal{K}^{2}_{1}(y)\\
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{2}(y)&-\mathcal{K}_{1}(y)\mathcal{K}_{2}(y)\end{pmatrix}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(0,n^{2}f(1+1/n))}.

(A.20)

Note that the
y^{1/2}
in the first

-norm is absorbed by the exponential decay of
\mathcal{K}_{j}(y)
, which are in

because of (

) and (

), implying that this norm is finite and of order

. Now observe that for
\mathrm{e}^{-1}\leq y<cn

\displaystyle\log\left|\log\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right|=\log\big(-\log y+\log n^{2}\big)\leq\log\big(1+\log n^{2}\big)\lesssim O(\log\log n),

by the monotonicity of the logarithm, while for

we have

\displaystyle\log\left|\log\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right|=\log\big(-\log y+\log n^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\log(-2\log y)+\log\big(2\log n^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim O(\log|\log y|)+O(\log\log n),

again by the monotonicity of the logarithm.

Hence, for
0<y<cn
, we have

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\log\left|\log\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right|\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim O\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\left[\log|\log y|\chi_{(0,\mathrm{e}^{-1})}+\log\log n\right]\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim O\left(\frac{\log\log n}{n^{2}}\right)\big(O(y)+1\big).

We see that the growth of

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n}(v_{n}-{\widehat{v}}_{n}){\widehat{\mu}}_{n}^{-1}{\rm d}s+\int_{\Sigma^{r}}{\widehat{\mu}}_{n+1}(v_{n+1}-{\widehat{v}}_{n+1}){\widehat{\mu}}^{-1}_{n+1}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi}{n(n+1)}\int_{0}^{n^{2}f(1+1/n)}\left(I+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\mathcal{E}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2\pi n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{K}_{1}(y)\mathcal{K}_{2}(y)&-\mathcal{K}^{2}_{1}(y)\\
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{2}(y)&-\mathcal{K}_{1}(y)\mathcal{K}_{2}(y)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2\pi n}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathcal{E}^{-1}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\left(I+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}}{\widehat{F}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)+\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)-2\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}\left(f_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\right)\frac{1}{2}\big(f_{1}^{-1}\big)^{\prime}\left(\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right){\rm d}y

\displaystyle\phantom{\quad\times}{}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right).

Inverting the change of variables

and Taylor expanding

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi n}{n+1}\int_{1}^{1+1/n}\left(\mathcal{E}(1)+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2\pi n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{K}_{1}\mathcal{K}_{2}&-\mathcal{K}^{2}_{1}\\
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{2}&-\mathcal{K}_{1}\mathcal{K}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2\pi n}&0\\

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}}{\widehat{F}}(s)\right)^{2}\left(\frac{F^{2}}{w_{+}}(s)+\frac{F^{2}}{w_{-}}(s)-2\frac{\widehat{F}^{2}}{\widehat{w}}(s)\right){\rm d}s+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\log^{3}n}\right).

(A.21)

However, (
A.21
) is precisely
\frac{1}{n+1}
-times the integral in the last line of (
A.14

\frac{3\pi\mathrm{i}}{8n\log^{2}n}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\phantom{-}1&-\mathrm{i}\\
-\mathrm{i}&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)+O\big(\frac{1}{n\log^{3}n}\big)
in equation (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\left(\frac{3\pi\mathrm{i}}{8n\log^{2}n}\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}1&-\mathrm{i}\\

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{3\pi\mathrm{i}}{8n^{2}\log^{2}n}\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}1&-\mathrm{i}\\

Taking into account the
