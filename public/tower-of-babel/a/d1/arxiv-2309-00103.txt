Abstract
. Suppose that A A and B B are sets in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , and we form the sumset of A A with n n random points of B B . Given the volumes of A A and B B , how should we choose them to minimize the expected volume of this sumset? Our aim in this paper is to show that we should take A A and B B to be Euclidean balls. We also consider the analogous question in the torus 𝕋 d \mathbb{T}^{d} , and we show that in this case the optimal choices of A A and B B are bands, in other words, sets of the form [ x , y ] × 𝕋 d − 1 [x,y]\times\mathbb{T}^{d-1} . We also give stability versions of our results.

Introduction

Let
A
and
B
be sets of finite positive measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The fundamental
Brunn–Minkowski inequality (see for example
[
1

) asserts that the volume

|A+B|
of the sumset

is at least
(|A|^{1/d}+|B|^{1/d})^{d}
.
Note that we may obtain equality for any convex
body
A
by setting
B
to be a homothetic copy of
A
. But suppose we try to
‘approximate’
B
by a finite set – in the sense that, for a fixed value
of
n
, we form the sumset
A+S
, where
S
consists of
n

and uniformly at random from

A
and

so as to minimize the expected size of
A+S

Note that now there is no reason at all to believe that this minimum may be attained
using any given (convex) choice of
A
by a suitably related choice of
B
, unlike
for the Brunn–Minkowski inequality itself. Indeed, it is natural to guess, based for example upon isoperimetric notions, that Euclidean
balls might actually be best. And this is what we prove.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

\mathbb{R}^{d}

In the torus, our starting point is again the above discrete result. We pass to a continuous version,
and then we try to symmetrize on the torus. However, the torus is not isotropic, so one does not
have the power of ‘symmetrizing in all possible directions’ that one has in

Nevertheless, it turns out that there are just enough symmetrizations that we are allowed to
perform that we can solve the problem in two dimensions: essentially, we can symmetrize in
any ‘direction’ given by a member of

Our notation is standard. However, we would like to highlight one of the conventions that we use.

The case

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcap$}}_{i}(A_{i}^{c}+b_{i})|)\leq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcap$}}_{i}(A^{\prime c}_{i}+b^{\prime}_{i})|).

\sum_{b_{1}\in B_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in B_{n}}|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcap$}}_{i}(A_{i}^{c}+b_{i})|\leq\sum_{b^{\prime}_{1}\in B^{\prime}_{1},\dots,b^{\prime}_{n}\in B^{\prime}_{n}}|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcap$}}_{i}(A^{\prime c}_{i}+b^{\prime}_{i})|,

Lemma 2.3
.

We first claim that for any

\sum_{x=r}^{p-1}f_{i}(x)>\sum_{x=r}^{p-1}g_{i}(x)

By setting

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}}F(x)f_{i}(x)

Y^{\prime}_{i}
respectively and

\mathbb{P}(y_{i}\neq y^{\prime}_{i})=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\theta\geq\frac{|Y_{i}\cap Y^{\prime}_{i}|}{\max(|Y_{i}|,|Y_{i}^{\prime}|)}\bigg)=1-\frac{|Y_{i}\cap Y^{\prime}_{i}|}{\max(|Y_{i}|,|Y^{\prime}_{i}|)}\leq\frac{|Y_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}Y^{\prime}_{i}|}{\max(|Y_{i}|,|Y^{\prime}_{i}|)}.

Let
E
be the event that for every
i\in[n]
we have
y_{i}=y^{\prime}_{i}
. By the above estimate, we have

\mathbb{P}(E^{c})\leq\sum_{i}\mathbb{P}(y_{i}\neq y_{i}^{\prime})\leq\sum_{i}\frac{|Y_{i}\cap Y^{\prime}_{i}|}{\max(|Y_{i}|,|Y_{i}^{\prime}|)}

Conditioned on the event
E
, we have the inequality

\Big||\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{\prime}_{i}+y^{\prime}_{i})|-|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i}+y_{i})|\Big|\leq\sum_{i}|X_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}_{i}|.

In general, we have the trivial inequality

\Big||\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{\prime}_{i}+y^{\prime}_{i})|-|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i}+y_{i})|\Big|\leq\sum_{i}(|X_{i}|+|X^{\prime}_{i}|).

Combining the last three estimates, we conclude

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{\prime}_{i}+y^{\prime}_{i})|)-\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i}

\displaystyle+y_{i})|)\Big|=\Big|\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{\prime}_{i}+y^{\prime}_{i})|-|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i}+y_{i})|)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i}|X_{i}|+|X^{\prime}_{i}|\Big)\mathbb{P}(E)+\Big(\sum_{i}|X_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}_{i}|\Big)\mathbb{P}(E^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i}|X_{i}|+|X^{\prime}_{i}|\Big)\sum_{i}\frac{|Y_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}Y^{\prime}_{i}|}{\max(|Y_{i}|,|Y^{\prime}_{i}|)}+\sum_{i}|X_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i}|X_{i}|+2|X_{i}|\Big)\sum_{i}\delta+\sum_{i}\delta|X_{i}|

\displaystyle=(3n+1)\delta\sum_{i}|X_{i}|.

The last inequality follows from the hypotheses that
|X_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}_{i}|\leq\delta|X_{i}|

and
|Y_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}Y^{\prime}_{i}|\leq\delta|Y_{i}|
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let

and let

and

\mathbb{T}

Construct intervals

,

Then for

and

chosen independently and uniformly at random from
B_{i}
and
B^{\prime}_{i}
,
respectively, we have

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A_{i}+b_{i})|)\geq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A^{\prime}_{i}+b^{\prime}_{i})|).

Proof.

We identify
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
with
(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
, and fix a small parameter
\varepsilon
with

.
As all

,
B_{i}
,
A^{\prime}_{i}
and

,
D_{i}
,
C^{\prime}_{i}
and

, respectively, so that for

we have

C_{i}=\bigcup_{j\in[k]}\big(\tfrac{q_{i,j}}{p},\tfrac{r_{i,j}}{p}\big],\qquad D_{i}=\bigcup_{j\in[k]}\big(\tfrac{s_{i,j}}{p},\tfrac{t_{i,j}}{p}\big],\qquad C^{\prime}_{i}=\big(\tfrac{-h_{i}}{p},\tfrac{h_{i}}{p}\big]\quad\text{ and }\quad D^{\prime}_{i}=\big(\tfrac{-\ell_{i}}{p},\tfrac{\ell_{i}}{p}\big],

where

,

, and finally

|C_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}A_{i}|\leq\varepsilon|A_{i}|
,
|D_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}B_{i}|\leq\varepsilon|B_{i}|
,

|C^{\prime}_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}A^{\prime}_{i}|\leq\varepsilon|A_{i}^{\prime}|
and
|D^{\prime}_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}B^{\prime}_{i}|\leq\varepsilon|B_{i}^{\prime}|
.

Claim 3.3
.

For
d_{i}
and
d^{\prime}_{i}
chosen uniformly at random from
D_{i}
and
D^{\prime}_{i}
respectively, we have

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(C_{i}+d_{i})|)\geq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(C^{\prime}_{i}+d^{\prime}_{i})|)-2n\varepsilon.

For

, construct subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}

and

X^{\prime}_{i}=\{1-h_{i},\dots,h_{i}\}\quad\text{and}\quad Y^{\prime}_{i}=\{1-\ell_{i},\dots,\ell_{i}\}.

Note that
|X_{i}|=|X^{\prime}_{i}|
and
|Y_{i}|=|Y^{\prime}_{i}|
. Moreover,
X^{\prime}_{i}
and
Y^{\prime}_{i}

are discrete intervals centred around
1/2
. Finally, write

y_{i}=\lceil pd_{i}\rceil\quad\text{and}\quad y^{\prime}_{i}=\lceil pd^{\prime}_{i}\rceil.

Note that if

,
d^{\prime}_{i}

,

\big||\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(C_{i}+d_{i})|-\tfrac{1}{p}|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i}+y_{i})|\big|\leq\tfrac{kn}{p}\quad\text{and}\quad\big||\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(C^{\prime}_{i}+d^{\prime}_{i})|-\tfrac{1}{p}|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{\prime}_{i}+y^{\prime}_{i})|\big|\leq\tfrac{kn}{p}.

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i}+y_{i})|)\geq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{\prime}_{i}+y^{\prime}_{i})|).

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(C_{i}+d_{i})|)\geq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(C^{\prime}_{i}+d^{\prime}_{i})|)-\tfrac{2kn}{p}\geq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(C^{\prime}_{i}+d^{\prime}_{i})|)-2n\varepsilon.\qed

By
3.1
and the fact that
|C_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}A_{i}|\leq\varepsilon|A_{i}|
,

|D_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}B_{i}|\leq\varepsilon|B_{i}|
,
|C^{\prime}_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}A^{\prime}_{i}|\leq\varepsilon|A^{\prime}_{i}|

and
|D^{\prime}_{i}\mathbin{\triangle}B^{\prime}_{i}|\leq\varepsilon|B^{\prime}_{i}|
, for

,

,

and

,

,

, respectively, we have

\Big|\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(C_{i}+d_{i})|)-\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A_{i}+b_{i})|)\Big|\leq 4n\varepsilon\sum_{i}|A_{i}|\leq 4n^{2}\varepsilon

and

\Big|\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(C^{\prime}_{i}+d^{\prime}_{i})|)-\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A^{\prime}_{i}+b^{\prime}_{i})|)\Big|\leq 4n\varepsilon\sum_{i}|A^{\prime}_{i}|\leq 4n^{2}\varepsilon.

Combining the last inequalities with

, we have

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A_{i}+b_{i})|)\geq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A^{\prime}_{i}+b^{\prime}_{i})|)-2n\varepsilon-8n^{2}\varepsilon.

As

can be chosen arbitrarily small, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A_{i}+b_{i})|)\geq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A^{\prime}_{i}+b^{\prime}_{i})|).\qed

4.
Derivation of
1.3

For
u\in\mathbb{T}^{d-1}
and any subset
X
of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, write

X_{u}=\{x:(x,u)\in X\},

for the section of
X
over
u
, where we identify
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}^{d-1}
.
We define the
Steiner symmetrization
of a measurable set
X
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
as
the measurable subset
S_{d}(X)
of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
which has the property that for every

u\in\mathbb{T}^{d-1}
,
S_{d}(X)_{u}
is a closed interval in
\mathbb{T}
centred around the origin
with
|S_{d}(X)_{u}|=|X_{u}|
.
For the sake of definiteness, when the closed interval could be either a single point or the empty set we choose it to
be the empty set. This is purely for convenience, and any other choice would make no difference to any of the arguments.
An alternative would have been to be more careful with this choice, as a way of ensuring that closed sets always do map
to closed sets, for example, but there is no need for this.

More generally, given an integer
d\times d
matrix
M
with
\det(M)=1
, i.e.,
M\in SL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
,
the Steiner symmetrization
S_{M}
transforms a measurable set
X
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
into the set
X^{M}=S_{M}(X)

in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
where
X^{M}=S_{M}(X)=M^{-1}(S_{d}(M(X)))
.
Note that
M
induces a measure preserving bijection
\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
M\in SL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
and for any measurable subsets
X
and
Y
of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
we have

|X^{M}\mathbin{\triangle}Y^{M}|\leq|X\mathbin{\triangle}Y|.

Proof.

As
\det(M)=1
, for any measurable sets
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
we have

|M(X^{\prime})\mathbin{\triangle}M(Y^{\prime})|=|M(X^{\prime}\mathbin{\triangle}Y^{\prime})|=|X^{\prime}\mathbin{\triangle}Y^{\prime}|.

Therefore, it is enough to show the result when
M
is the identity:

|S_{d}(X)\mathbin{\triangle}S_{d}(Y)|\leq|X\mathbin{\triangle}Y|.

As
S_{d}
acts in each section
\mathbb{T}\times\{u\}
independently, it is enough
to show the result in dimension
d=1
. So assume
d=1
and note that

|S_{1}(X)\mathbin{\triangle}S_{1}(Y)|=\big||S_{1}(X)|-|S_{1}(Y)|\big|=\big||X|-|Y|\big|\leq|X\mathbin{\triangle}Y|.\qed

We now start to work towards our result, but just in two dimensions. As we will see,
this is where the work actually comes, and afterwards it will be a straightforward
matter to deduce the result in higher dimensions.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exist matrices
M_{j}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds.
Let
X
be a measurable set in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and let
X^{\prime}=[-c,c]\times\mathbb{T}

be a band in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with the same area as
X
. Define recursively the sets

X_{0}=X
and
X_{j+1}=X_{j}^{M_{j}}
, each
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\lim_{j\to\infty}|X_{j}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|=0

uniformly in
X
.

Here, as usual, by ‘uniformly in
X
’ we mean that for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists

n
such that for any
X
the value of
|X_{j}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|
is at most
\varepsilon
for all
j\geq n
.

Proof.

For
j\in\mathbb{N}
define
M_{j}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
so that
M_{2j}(x,y)=(x,y)
and
M_{2j+1}(x,y)=(x,y+jx)

and note that
M_{j}(X^{\prime})=X^{\prime}
and hence
(X^{\prime})^{M_{j}}=X^{\prime}
for all
j
.
Hence, by
4.1
,
|X_{j}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|
decreases as
j
increases.

Fix
X
with
|X|=|X^{\prime}|=2c
and assume for some
j>0
,
|X_{2j+1}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|>4\varepsilon
.
Note that we must have
2c\in[2\varepsilon,1-2\varepsilon]
otherwise we automatically have

|X_{2j+1}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|\leq 4\varepsilon
. For each
y\in\mathbb{T}
we have
X^{\prime}_{y}=[-c,c]
and

X_{2j+1,y}=S_{2}(X_{2j})_{y}=[-c(y),c(y)]
is also an interval in
\mathbb{T}
centred
at the origin whose width in general will depend on
y
. Define the sets

S=\{y\in\mathbb{T}:c(y)\leq c-\varepsilon/2\}\quad\text{and}\quad B=\{y\in\mathbb{T}:c(y)\geq c+\varepsilon/2\}.

Note that
|X_{2j+1}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|>4\varepsilon
and
|X_{2j+1}|=|X^{\prime}|
implies

|X^{\prime}\setminus X_{2j+1}|
,
|X_{2j+1}\setminus X^{\prime}|>2\varepsilon
, and hence

|S|\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad|B|\geq\varepsilon.

By the definition of the Steiner symmetrization
S_{M_{2j+1}}
, we note that for every

t\in\mathbb{T}
the set
I^{\prime}_{t}=\{x:(x,t-jx)\in X_{2j+2}\}
is an interval in
\mathbb{T}
centred
at the origin with the same size as the set
I_{t}=\{x:(x,t-jx)\in X_{2j+1}\}
. Moreover,
for every
t\in\mathbb{T}
we have that the set
\{x:(x,t-jx)\in X^{\prime}\}
is equal to
[-c,c]
. which we will call

I
for convenience.

Claim 4.3
.

For every
j\geq 2/\varepsilon
and
t\in\mathbb{T}
we have

|I^{\prime}_{t}\cap I|\geq|I_{t}\cap I|+j^{-1}\varepsilon,

Proof.

As
I
and
I^{\prime}_{t}
are centred intervals with
|I^{\prime}_{t}|=|I_{t}|
, it is enough to show that

|I\setminus I_{t}|\geq j^{-1}\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad|I_{t}\setminus I|\geq j^{-1}\varepsilon.

Consider the functions
f\colon[c-j^{-1},c)\to\mathbb{T}
and
g\colon(c,c+j^{-1}]\to\mathbb{T}

defined by
f(x)=g(x)=t-jx
and note that these functions are bijective and scale
the measure by a factor
j
. Hence if we set
S^{\prime}=f^{-1}(S)
and
B^{\prime}=g^{-1}(B)
then

|S^{\prime}|\geq j^{-1}\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad|B^{\prime}|\geq j^{-1}\varepsilon.

As
j^{-1}\leq\varepsilon/2
and
2c\in[2\varepsilon,1-2\varepsilon]
, we have

c-j^{-1}>-c
and
c+j^{-1}<1-c
, so
S^{\prime}\subseteq I=[-c,c]
and
B^{\prime}\cap I=\emptyset

in
\mathbb{T}
. Therefore, it is enough to show that

S^{\prime}\cap I_{t}=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad B^{\prime}\subseteq I_{t}.

Assume for a contradiction that

x\in S^{\prime}\cap I_{t},

and note that if we let
y=t-jx
then this assumption is equivalent to

x\in S^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad(x,y)\in X_{2j+1}.

As
x\in S^{\prime}
and
y=f(x)
, we have
y\in S
. By construction of
S
,
we have
x\in[-c+\varepsilon/2,c-\varepsilon/2]
. However, by construction

x\in S^{\prime}\subseteq[c-j^{-1},c)
. This gives the desired contradiction
as
j^{-1}\leq\varepsilon/2
. We conclude that

S^{\prime}\cap I_{t}=\emptyset.

Analogously, we conclude that

B^{\prime}\subseteq I_{t}.

This finishes the proof of the claim.
∎

To conclude our proof, note that
4.3
implies that for every
j\geq 2/\varepsilon

either
|X_{2j+1}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|\leq 4\varepsilon
, or

|X_{2j+2}\cap X^{\prime}|\geq|X_{2j+1}\cap X^{\prime}|+j^{-1}\varepsilon,

which is equivalent to

|X_{2j+2}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|\leq|X_{2j+1}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|-2j^{-1}\varepsilon.

Again by
4.1
,
|X_{j}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|
decreases as
j
increases, which implies that

|X_{2j+2}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|\leq|X_{2j}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|-2j^{-1}\varepsilon.

As
\sum_{j}j^{-1}
diverges, there must exist some
j_{0}
depending only on
\varepsilon

such that
|X_{j}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|\leq 4\varepsilon
for all
j\geq j_{0}
, and we note that
j_{0}

can be chosen independently of
X
. We conclude that

\lim_{j\to\infty}|X_{j}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|=0\quad\text{uniformly in }X.\qed

Corollary 4.4
.

There exists matrices
M_{j}\in SL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds.
Let
X
be a measurable set in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and let

with the same volume as

. Define recursively
the sets

. Then

\lim_{j\to\infty}|X_{j}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{\prime}|=0

uniformly in

.

Proof.

Let
M_{j,k}\in SL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
be the matrix that applies the
M_{j}
from
4.2

to coordinates 1 and
k
and leaves all other coordinates fixed.
Using the sequence
M_{j,2}
,
j=1,\dots,n_{1}
, we have that for sufficiently large

,
|X_{n_{1}}\mathbin{\triangle}X^{(2)}|<\varepsilon

,

∎

Lemma 4.5

Let

,

\mathbb{T}^{d}

,

\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}^{d-1}
. For

, write

and
Y^{M}_{i,u_{i}}
respectively.
Also choose

uniformly at random and independent from everything else. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i}+y_{i})|)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(z\in\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i,u-u_{i}}+z_{i}))=\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i,u-u_{i}}+z_{i})|)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{M}_{i}+y^{M}_{i})|)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}((z,u)\in\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{M}_{i}+(z^{M}_{i},u_{i})))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(z\in\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{M}_{i,u-u_{i}}+z^{M}_{i}))=\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{M}_{i,u-u_{i}}+z^{M}_{i})|).

and show that if we choose

and

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X_{i,u-u_{i}}+z_{i})|)\geq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(X^{M}_{i,u-u_{i}}+z^{M}_{i})|).

Write
A_{i}=X_{i,u-u_{i}}
and

Where

and

are chosen uniformly
at random from

and
B^{\prime}_{i}=Y^{M}_{i,u_{i}}

∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let

and let

and

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A_{i}+b_{i})|)\geq\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A^{\prime}_{i}+b^{\prime}_{i})|).

Proof.

For

Proof of

.

By
1.3
, we may assume that
A
and
B
are intervals with
A=[0,a]
and
B=[0,b]
, say.
Order the points
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}
so that
0<b_{1}<b_{2}<\dots<b_{n}<b
. Then it is easy to see that,
for
i>1
,
b_{i}-b_{i-1}
has the same distribution as
b_{1}
. Indeed, if we condition on
b_{i}
the set

\{b_{1},\dots,b_{i-1}\}
is just a uniformly chosen random set of
i-1
points in
[0,b_{i}]
and so by
symmetry
b_{i}-b_{i-1}
has the same distribution as
b_{1}-0=b_{1}
. As this holds conditioned on
b_{i}

for any choice of
b_{i}
, it also holds unconditionally. We also note that the distribution of
b_{1}

is just the minimum of
n
i.i.d. random variables that are uniform on the interval
[0,b]
.

Clearly
A+b_{1}=[b_{1},a+b_{1}]
has length
a
. Now each
A+b_{i}
,
i>1
adds an additional length
of
\min\{a,b_{i}-b_{i-1}\}
. Indeed, it adds the interval
(a+b_{i-1},a+b_{i}]
except when

b_{i}-b_{i-1}>a
, in which case it adds the interval
[b_{i},a+b_{i}]
. Hence, provided
a+b\leq 1

so that there is no wrap around the torus, linearity of expectation and the above result on the
distribution of
b_{i}-b_{i-1}
implies

\mathbb{E}(|\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A+b_{i})|)=a+(n-1)\mathbb{E}(\min\{a,b_{1}\})=a+(n-1)b\cdot\mathbb{P}(b_{*}\leq\min\{a,b_{1}\}),

where
b_{*}
is chosen independently and uniformly at random from
[0,b]
.
But this last probability is just
the probability that on picking
n+1
points
\{b_{*},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}
at random in
[0,b]

that
b_{*}
is the smallest, and that the smallest is at most
a
. By symmetry this is
1/(n+1)
times the
probability that the smallest of
n+1
uniform numbers in
[0,b]
is at most
a
, which is 1 if

a\geq b
and
1-(1-a/b)^{n+1}
if
a<b
. The result follows for
a+b\leq 1
.

For
a+b>1
we need to subtract the overlap
X:=|A+(b_{n}-1)\cap A+b_{1}|
that can occur as a result of wrap around.
We note that the result holds for
n=1
, so assume
n\geq 2
.
By reflecting the interval
[b_{1},b]
we see that
b-b_{n}
has the same distribution as
b_{2}-b_{1}
,
even conditioned on
b_{1}
. Thus

has the same distribution as
\max\{a+b-1-b_{2},0\}
.
It is easy to see that the pdf of

is

and thus

can be

\int_{0}^{a+b-1}\mkern-20.0mun(n-1)x(b-x)^{n-2}b^{-n}(a+b-1-x)\,dx=a+\tfrac{n-1}{n+1}b-1+\big(\tfrac{1-a}{b}\big)^{n}\big(b-\tfrac{n-1}{n+1}(1-a)\big).

The result then follows after some algebraic simplifications.
∎

Proof of
1.2
.

By
1.1
, we may assume

and

are balls centred at the origin.
If

if and only if

Hence for a uniformly random choice of

The expected volume

is then given simply as

\int_{A+B}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\mathbb{P}(x\in A+b_{i}))\,dx=\int_{A+B}\big(1-\tfrac{|(x-A)\cap B|}{|B|}\big)^{n}\,dx.

As we are considering
n
very large, the integral will dominated by the region very close to the boundary
of the ball
A+B
where
(x-A)\cap B
is very small. Let the radii of
A
and
B
be
a
and
b
respectively,
and assume

is at distance
a+b-\varepsilon
from the centre of
A+B
. Then

(3)

\frac{|(x-A)\cap B|}{|B|}=\frac{a^{d}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\sin^{d}\theta\,d\theta+b^{d}\int_{0}^{\beta}\sin^{d}\theta\,d\theta}{b^{d}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{d}\theta\,d\theta},

where the triangle with side lengths
a
,
b
and
a+b-\varepsilon
forms angles
\alpha
and
\beta
between
the side of length
a+b-\varepsilon
and the sides of length
a
and
b
respectively. To see this note that

(x-A)\cap B
is formed from two caps, a cap of
x-A
subtending an angle
\alpha
at its centre and a

B
subtending an angle
\beta
at its centre. The integrals that give the volumes of these caps
is of the form
\int_{a\cos\alpha}^{a}v_{d-1}(\sqrt{a^{2}-t^{2}})^{d-1}\,dt
for

, and similarly for
B
with

equal to the volume of a unit

-sphere. A change of variables

puts this in the form
v_{d-1}a^{d}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\sin^{d}\theta\,d\theta
, and similarly for the cap of
B
.

Now fix
a
,
b
and
d
and consider the case when
\varepsilon\to 0
. We have
a\sin\alpha=b\sin\beta

and

. As
\varepsilon\to 0
this gives
\alpha^{2}=(1+o(1))\frac{2\varepsilon b/a}{a+b}
,

\beta^{2}=(1+o(1))\frac{2\varepsilon a/b}{a+b}
and, for

\sin^{d}\theta=(1+o(1))\theta^{d}
. Thus

\frac{|(x-A)\cap B|}{|B|}=\frac{(1+o(1))(a^{-1}+b^{-1})(2\varepsilon ab)^{(d+1)/2}}{(d+1)(a+b)^{(d+1)/2}b^{d}(v_{d}/v_{d-1})}=(1+o(1))C_{a,b,d}\varepsilon^{(d+1)/2},

where

is a positive constant that depends on
a
,
b
and
d
, and the

is as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The expected fraction of
A+B
not covered by
\mathbin{\scalebox{0.95}{$\bigcup$}}_{i}(A+b_{i})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A+B|}\int_{A+B}\big(1-\tfrac{|(x-A)\cap B|}{|B|}\big)^{n}\,dx

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\tfrac{d}{a+b}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(-C_{a,b,d}n\varepsilon^{(d+1)/2}\big)\,d\varepsilon

is very small, in which case we can approximate
the

∎

7.

Our aim in this section is to prove a stability result for

Recall that a

,

1-\big(1-\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A^{\prime}}*\mathbbm{1}_{B^{\prime}}(x)}{|B|}\big)^{n}

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1-\bigg(1-\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A}*\mathbbm{1}_{B}(x)}{|B|}\bigg)^{n}dx\geq(\gamma\varepsilon^{d})^{n}\alpha+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1-\bigg(1-\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A^{\prime}}*\mathbbm{1}_{B^{\prime}}(x)}{|B^{\prime}|}\bigg)^{n}dx

The conclusion follows with

9.
Open problems

Perhaps the most interesting question that leads on from our results is to ask what would happen if we chose points uniformly at random not from the whole of
B
but just from its
boundary (whether we are in Euclidean space or the torus). There are two different natural ways to quantify this
problem. For a given
\varepsilon>0
, we could choose uniformly from the set
(1+\varepsilon)B-B
, or we could
choose uniformly from
B_{\varepsilon}-B
, where
B_{\varepsilon}
denotes the set of all points at distance at most

\varepsilon
from
B
. The difference between these two lies in how they ‘bias’ the boundary measure towards the actual
shape and curvature of the boundary – we feel that both forms are very natural for this problem.

In a different direction, what happens if we use
n
random points in
A
and in
B
(in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) and take the convex hull?
Is it still true that Euclidean balls minimize the expected value of this quantity? Here
A
and
B

would be convex sets of given volumes.

In the results in the paper, we have formed the sumset of
A
with
n
random points of
B
. But what would
happen if, instead of
points
, we used random copies of a fixed body? For example, what would happen
if we used unit intervals, with centres chosen uniformly at random from
B
but with orientations also chosen at random? To be more precise, given our sets

A
and
B
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we first
choose
n
points uniformly at random from
B
and then for each of these points we take a unit interval centred at
this point with direction chosen uniformly at random –
we wish to minimize the expected volume of the sum of

A
with the union of these
n
intervals.

One could also allow this ‘extra’ body (above, the unit
interval) to vary not by a random rotation but rather by being related to
B
:
perhaps the most natural example would be if it were a homothetic copy of
B
. Thus one could
ask for example the following question: given convex sets
A
and
B
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of given volumes, how do we minimize
the expected volume of the sumset of
A
with
n
random copies of
B/2
, chosen uniformly at random from
the copies of
B/2
inside
B
? In an equivalent, but
perhaps less attractive, formulation, this is asking to
minimize the sum of
A+B
with
n
random points of
B
.
