Strain correlation functions in isotropic elastic bodies: Large wavelength limit for two-dimensional systems
J.P. Wittmer, A.N. Semenov, J. Baschnagel

Abstract
Strain correlation functions in two-dimensional isotropic elastic bodies are shown both theoretically (using the general structure of isotropic tensor fields) and numerically (using a glass-forming model system) to depend on the coordinates of the field variable (position vector 𝐫 \bf r in real space or wavevector 𝐪 \bf q in reciprocal space) and thus on the direction of the field vector and the orientation of the coordinate system. Since the fluctuations of the longitudinal and transverse components of the strain field in reciprocal space are known in the long-wavelength limit from the equipartition theorem, all components of the correlation function tensor field are imposed and no additional physical assumptions are needed. An observed dependence on the field vector direction thus cannot be used as an indication for anisotropy or for a plastic rearrangement. This dependence is different for the associated strain response field containing also information on the localized stress perturbation.

As already emphasized in Sec.

We elaborate here this general point focusing, naturally, on correlation functions of the instantaneous

V.2
Fluctuation-dissipation theorem

Let us switch on at time

For a general “growth function”

VI
Conclusion

VI.1
Summary

Strain correlation functions.

The present work has focused on
correlation functions of components of strain tensor fields
in two-dimensional, isotropic and achiral elastic bodies.
This was done theoretically using

•

the general mathematical structure of isotropic tensor fields
as summarized in Sec.
II.2
,
cf. Eq. (
5
), and in more detail in Appendix
A
and

•

the well-known equipartition theorem of statistical physics for
macroscopic strain fluctuations in reciprocal space,
cf. Eq. (
2
).

Numerically we have tested our predictions by means of glass-forming particles deep in the glass regime.
This shows that these correlation functions may depend on the coordinates of the field variable
(
q_{\alpha}
in reciprocal space or
r_{\alpha}
in real space)
and implies in turn that they depend in general on the direction of the field vector and
on the orientation of the coordinate system.
Scaling with
x=\theta-\alpha
these angular dependencies are distinct from those of ordinary anisotropic systems.
Importantly, correlation functions of strain tensor fields are components
of an isotropic forth-order tensor field, Eq. (
5
), being characterized by the two ICFs

c_{\mathrm{L}}(q)
and
c_{\mathrm{T}}(q)
.
With the asymptotic plateau values being given by two Lamé coefficients,
Eq. (
2
), all strain correlation functions are determined
and all (finite) real-space strain correlations must be long-ranged decaying as
1/r^{2}

(cf. Fig.
5
).
We thus obtain similar results as in our recent study on correlation functions of stress tensor fields
spmP5a
.
Note that
time-averaged
stress fields have been probed in the latter study while
correlations of
instantaneous
strain fields have been considered here.
Our numerical findings do agree with other studies of strain
correlations
Fuchs16
;
Fuchs18b
;
Reichman21b
being, however, now traced back to
the isotropy of the system and the tensor field nature of the probed correlations.
Importantly, we have given here a complete and asymptotically exact description for the
correlation functions of strain tensor fields of isotropic elastic bodies.
No additional physical assumption is thus needed (for sufficiently small wavevectors).

Response to tensorial point sources.

We also discussed the associated linear response fields
as defined in general mathematical terms by the tensorial contraction of the
correlation function tensor by means of a source tensor
and, more physically, by the FDT relation for the strain increment due to an imposed small
stress perturbation, cf. Eq. (
28
) and Eq. (
29
).
Naturally, the response must by definition contain information from both the correlation functions,
characterizing the system, and from the imposed source tensor which may not be isotropic.
We have emphasized that the summation over repeated indices must be properly performed,
i.e. the response field is
not
given by one correlation function times a scalar
but by the sum over
all
eigenvalues of the source tensor.
For this reason response and correlation fields, albeit closely related, have in general different
angular dependences, e.g., the shear strain correlation function
c_{1212}(\mbox{$\bf r$})
in an
isotropic system must be octupolar, cf. Eq. (
25
), while the
shear strain response
\delta\varepsilon_{12}(\mbox{$\bf r$})
may be either quadrupolar
for an isotropic source, cf. Eq. (
36
), or octupolar for
an anisotropic source corresponding to two force dipoles of opposite signs,
cf. Eq. (
37
).
Albeit all contributing correlation functions are isotropic
the response field is anisotropic in the latter case due to the source.
It is thus important to not lump together correlation functions and response fields.
Mesoscopic elasto-plastic models
Tanguy11
;
Barrat18
thus must specify not only the correlation functions
but also the source tensors characterizing the local plastic events.

•

The general mathematical framework for isotropic tensor fields and the discussed relations and
numerical procedure for correlation and response fields naturally generalize to higher spatial dimensions,
especially for the three-dimensional case.

•

Appendix A
Summary of isotropic tensor fields

A.1
Background

Isotropic systems are described by “isotropic tensors” and “isotropic tensor fields”.
We give here a brief recap of various useful aspects already presented elsewhere
Schultz_Piszachich
;
spmP5a
.
Quite generally, a tensor field assigns a tensor to each point of the mathematical space,
in our case a
d
-dimensional Euclidean vector space
Schultz_Piszachich
.
We denote an element of this vector space by the “spatial position”
\bf r
(real space)
or by the “wavevector”
\bf q
for the corresponding reciprocal space.
The relations for tensor fields are formulated in reciprocal space since this is more
convenient both on theoretical and numerical grounds due to the assumed spatial homogeneity
(“translational invariance”).
The corresponding real space tensor field is finally obtained by inverse FT.

For simplicity we assume Cartesian coordinates with an orthonormal basis

\{\mbox{$\bf e$}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{$\bf e$}_{d}\}

McConnell
;
Schultz_Piszachich
;
TadmorCMTBook
.
Greek letters
\alpha,\beta,\ldots
are used for the indices of the tensor (field) components.
A twice repeated index
\alpha
is summed over the values
1,\ldots,d
, e.g.,

\mbox{$\bf q$}=q_{\alpha}\mbox{$\bf e$}_{\alpha}
with
q_{\alpha}
standing for the vector coordinates.
This work is chiefly concerned with tensors

\mbox{$\bf T$}^{(o)}=T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}\mbox{$\bf e$}_{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\mbox{$\bf e$}_{\alpha_{o}}

of “order”
o=2
and
o=4
and their corresponding tensor fields
with components depending either on
\bf r
or
\bf q
.
The order of a component is given by the number of indices.
Note that

T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}(\mbox{$\bf q$})={\cal F}[T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}(\mbox{$\bf r$})]

(41)

for the
d^{o}
coordinates in real and reciprocal space
(with
{\cal F}[\ldots]
denoting the FT as discussed in Appendix
B
).

We consider linear orthogonal coordinate transformations (marked by “
\ast
”)

\mbox{$\bf e$}_{\alpha}^{\ast}=c_{\alpha\beta}\mbox{$\bf e$}_{\beta}
with matrix coefficients

c_{\alpha\beta}
given by the direction cosine

c_{\alpha\beta}\equiv\cos(\mbox{$\bf e$}_{\alpha}^{\ast},\mbox{$\bf e$}_{\beta})

Schultz_Piszachich
.
We remind that
Schultz_Piszachich

T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}^{\ast}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=c_{\alpha_{1}\nu_{1}}\ldots c_{\alpha_{o}\nu_{o}}T_{\nu_{1}\ldots\nu_{o}}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

(42)

under a general orthogonal transform.
For a reflection of the
1
-axis we thus have, e.g.,

T_{1222}^{\ast}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=-T_{1222}(\mbox{$\bf q$}),T_{1221}^{\ast}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=T_{1221}(\mbox{$\bf q$}),

(43)

i.e. we have sign inversion for an
odd number
of indices equal to the index of the inverted axis.
The field vector
\mbox{$\bf q$}=q_{\alpha}\mbox{$\bf e$}_{\alpha}=q_{\alpha}^{\ast}\mbox{$\bf e$}_{\alpha}^{\ast}

remains unchanged by these “passive” transforms albeit its coordinates change.

A.2
Definitions, properties and construction of
general isotropic tensors and tensor fields

Isotropic tensors.

Components of an isotropic tensor remain unchanged by
any
orthogonal coordinate transformation

Schultz_Piszachich
;
TadmorCMTBook
, i.e.

T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}^{\ast}=T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}.

(44)

As noted at the end of Sec.
A.1
the sign of tensor components change
for a reflection at one axis if the number of indices equal to the inverted axis is
odd
.
Consistency with Eq. (
44
) implies that

all tensor components with an odd number of equal indices must vanish
, e.g.,

T_{12}=T_{1112}=T_{1222}=0.

(45)

Isotropic tensor fields.

The corresponding isotropy condition for tensor fields is given by
Schultz_Piszachich

T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}^{\ast}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d})=T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}(q_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,q_{d}^{\ast})

(46)

with
q_{\alpha}^{\ast}=c_{\alpha\beta}q_{\beta}

which reduces to Eq. (
44
) for
\mbox{$\bf q$}={\bf 0}
.
Please note that the fields on the left handside of Eq. (
46
)
are evaluated with the original coordinates
of the vector field variable
\bf q

while the fields on the right handside are evaluated with the transformed coordinates.
Another way to state this is to say that the left hand fields are computed at
the original vector
\mbox{$\bf q$}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d})
while the right hand fields are computed at
the “actively transformed” vector
\mbox{$\bf q$}^{\ast}=(q_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,q_{d}^{\ast})
.
It is for this reason that finite components with an odd number of equal indices, e.g.,

T_{1222}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\neq 0
, are possible in principle for finite
\bf q
.

Natural Rotated Coordinates.

Fortunately, there is a convenient coordinate system where the nice symmetry Eq. (
45
)
for isotropic tensors can be also used for isotropic tensor fields. In these “Natural Rotated Coordinates” (NRC)
the coordinate system for
each
wavevector
\bf q
is rotated until the
1
-axis coincides with the

\bf q
-direction, i.e.
q_{\alpha}^{\circ}=q\delta_{1\alpha}
with
q=|\mbox{$\bf q$}|
.
These tensor field components in NRC are marked by “
\circ
” to distinguish them from standard rotated
tensor fields (marked by primes “
\prime
”) where the
same
rotation is used for all
\bf q
.
If in addition
T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}^{\circ}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
is an
even
function of its field variable
\bf q

(as in the case of achiral systems for even order
o
)
it can be shown
spmP5a
that all tensor field components with an odd number of equal indices must vanish.

Product theorem for isotropic tensor fields.

Let us consider a tensor field

\mbox{$\bf C$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=\mbox{$\bf A$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\otimes\mbox{$\bf B$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

with
\mbox{$\bf A$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
and
\mbox{$\bf B$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
being two isotropic tensor fields
and
\otimes
standing either for an outer product,
e.g.
C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=A_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})B_{\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
,
or an inner product,
e.g.
C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=A_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}(\mbox{$\bf q$})B_{\nu\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\mbox{$\bf C$}^{\ast}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mbox{$\bf A$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\otimes\mbox{$\bf B$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\right)^{\ast}=\mbox{$\bf A$}^{\ast}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\otimes\mbox{$\bf B$}^{\ast}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{$\bf A$}(\mbox{$\bf q$}^{\ast})\otimes\mbox{$\bf B$}(\mbox{$\bf q$}^{\ast})=\mbox{$\bf C$}(\mbox{$\bf q$}^{\ast})

using in the second step a general property of tensor (field) products, due to
Eq. (
42
), and in the third step
Eq. (
46
) for the fields
\mbox{$\bf A$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
and
\mbox{$\bf B$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

where
\mbox{$\bf q$}^{\ast}
stands for the “actively” transformed field variable.

\mbox{$\bf C$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
is thus also an isotropic tensor field.
One may use this theorem to construct
isotropic tensor fields from known isotropic tensor fields
\mbox{$\bf A$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
and
\mbox{$\bf B$}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
.

All second-order tensors in this work are symmetric,
T_{\alpha\beta}=T_{\beta\alpha}
,
and the same applies for the corresponding tensor fields in either
\bf r
- or
\bf q
-space.
This is, e.g., the case for the strain field

\displaystyle T_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{\beta\alpha\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=T_{\alpha\beta\delta\gamma}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

(48)

\displaystyle T_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{\gamma\delta\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\mbox{ and }

(49)

\displaystyle T_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

\displaystyle T_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(-\mbox{$\bf q$}).

) is necessarily valid both for achiral and

Forth-order tensor fields are often constructed by taking

of second-order tensor fields.

The evenness of forth-order tensor fields,

achiral
systems.
It implies that

is real and, moreover, Eq. (
49
) for correlation functions since

(51)

As already emphasized, all our systems are assumed to be

,

General structure of tensors.

Isotropic tensors of different order are discussed, e.g., in Sec. 2.5.6 of Ref.

Due to Eq. (

\displaystyle T_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}

B.1
Continuous Fourier transform

We consider real-valued functions
f(\mbox{$\bf r$})
in
d
dimensions.
As in Refs.

;

;
spmP5a

and

\displaystyle iq_{\alpha}f(\mbox{$\bf q$})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\exp(-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf v$})

(66)
