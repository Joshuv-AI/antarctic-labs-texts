IgA-BEM for 3D Helmholtz problems on multi-patch domains using B-spline tailored numerical integration
Antonella Falini, Tadej Kanduč*, Maria Lucia Sampoli, Alessandra Sestini

Abstract
An Isogeometric Boundary Element Method (IgA-BEM) is considered for the numerical solution of Helmholtz problems on 3D bounded or unbounded domains, admitting a smooth conformal multi-patch representation of their finite boundary surface. The discretization space is formed by C 0 C^{0} inter-patch continuous basis functions whose restriction to a patch simplifies to the span of tensor product B-splines composed with the given patch parameterization. For both regular and singular integration, the proposed model utilizes a numerical procedure defined on the support of each trial B-spline function, which makes possible a function–by–function implementation of the matrix assembly phase. Spline quasi-interpolation is the common ingredient of all the considered quadrature rules; in the singular case it is combined with a B-spline recursion over the spline degree and with a singularity extraction technique, extended to the multi-patch setting for the first time. A threshold selection strategy is proposed to automatically distinguish between nearly singular and regular integrals. Numerical examples on relevant benchmarks show that the expected convergence orders are achieved with uniform discretization and a small number of uniformly spaced quadrature nodes.



1
Introduction

Isogeometric Analysis (IgA)
[
1
]
is a powerful tool to obtain a numerical solution of problems governed by partial differential equations, introduced in the literature at the beginning of the new millennium. This new paradigm was motivated by the observation that in engineering applications a domain is described by its boundary parametric representation generated by Computer Aided Design (CAD) software. For this aim CAD relies on flexible forms, often based on multi-patch tensor product B-spline or rational B-spline (NURBS) spaces, suited also for applications
[
2
]
. The IgA idea consists in adopting the CAD functional spaces also for approximating the solution of the differential problem, taking into account the well-known optimal approximation power of spline spaces
[
3
,
4
]
. The increasing success of the IgA paradigm in the context of both domain and boundary element methods is easily explained considering that, besides being capable to keep an exact representation of complex domains described in a multi-patch CAD form, IgA formulations guarantee a certain level of accuracy with considerably less degrees of freedom than traditional Finite Element Analysis (FEA), which relies on larger piecewise polynomial spaces with lower inter-element smoothness within each patch
[
5
]
. The attractiveness of IgA is even higher for Boundary Element Methods (BEMs), see for example
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
and references therein, because, in contrast to domain methods like FEM, they do not require any preliminary volumetric parameterization, which can be a remarkably time consuming task. BEMs rely on a boundary integral formulation of the problem, which can be derived whenever the fundamental solution of the associated differential operator is known. Hence, they require only the definition of a mesh on the boundary of the domain, which is a much easier task especially in the IgA context, where the CAD representation of the boundary is available.

The precursor of this work, dealing with 3D IgA-BEM, is presented in
[
12
]
, where exterior Laplace problems with Dirichlet boundary conditions on single patch domains are considered and, in order to deal also with screen problems, an indirect boundary integral formulation is adopted. In this paper the IgA multi-patch formulation of BEMs (multi-patch IgA-BEMs) based on a direct boundary integral formulation is considered for the numerical solution of problems governed by the Helmholtz equation on 3D bounded or unbounded domains, equipped with either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions. Such problems are of interest in acoustics, since they model radiation and rigid scattering in the frequency domain
[
13
]
, where radiation refers to the pressure field produced by an object vibrating within a fluid (usually air or water), while rigid scattering relates to the disturbance caused by an obstacle immersed within an existing acoustic field. The adopted Boundary Integral Equation (BIE) discretized with a collocation approach is the so-called conventional BIE (CBIE), which involves only weakly singular integrals and is augmented with the Sommerfield radiation condition at infinity for exterior problems. The Helmholtz operator can thus be simply written as a sum of the Laplacian
\Delta
with an identity operator scaled with a positive parameter
\kappa^{2}
. However, even when dealing with exterior Helmholtz problems admitting a unique solution, such BIE generates spurious solutions when
\kappa^{2}
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
for the considered boundary conditions. This is the reason why other papers also relying on IgA-BEMs for the numerical solution of the Helmholtz equation introduce its alternative Burton-Miller (BM) boundary integral formulation, which does not suffer from such drawback. Obtained as a linear combination of the mentioned CBIE with the hypersingular BIE (HBIE) also paired with the Helmholtz operator, the BM integral formulation is clearly more difficult, see for example
[
14
]
, where it is combined with a regularization technique to avoid to deal with hypersingular integrals in the assembly phase. However, successive researches have given numerical evidence that, when
\kappa^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of
-\Delta,
better numerical results are achieved by using the simpler CBIE, see for example
[
15
,
16
]
. Since clearly only a finite number of different values of the parameter
\kappa^{2}
can be considered and this can be anyway sufficient for a reasonable reconstruction of an associated time-dependent acoustic field, we have opted for applying the IgA-BEM approach just to the CBIE associated with Helmholtz, with the implicit assumption to avoid values of
\kappa^{2}
equal to eigenvalues of
-\Delta.

The IgA-BEM implementation in this study relies on the B-spline tailored cubature formulas for regular and weakly singular integrals, introduced in
[
17
]
in the setting of weakly singular surface integrals. The theoretical convergence order of such rules is analyzed in this work for the first time. Since the proposed quadrature schemes are constructed on the support of every bivariate B-spline, a function-by-function implementation of the matrix assembly phase can be adopted, similarly to work for 2D Laplace problems in
[
18
]
.
This attractive feature markedly reduces the amount of data accessing in the assembly phase, compared to a more common element–by–element strategy. Another remarkable feature of the proposed quadratures is the freedom of choice for the quadrature nodes – no reparameterization of nodes near the singularities is needed and, by choosing uniformly spaced nodes in the parametric space for all the required regular and singular numerical integrations, it ensures the possibility of using a small overall number of unique nodes.
By applying a singularity subtraction technique on singular integrals, the singular and regular part of the integrand are effectively separated. Thanks to the thorough study developed in
[
19
]
for the weakly singular kernel expansions, the continuity of the regularized integrands can be controlled and the developed integration rules have consequently better approximation properties for general smooth geometry parameterizations.
A novelty in this work is a careful decoupling of integrals appearing in the Helmholtz BIE into simpler, real and imaginary, regular and singular integrals. An original strategy for the automatic detection of near singularity is also introduced. Another new key feature lies in coupling the integration routines with multi-patch geometry representations; when the source point and the integration domain are separated by a patch interface, the unmatching parameterization across it poses an additional challenge. To overcome difficulties of this type, for nearly singular inter-patch integrals a new approximated singularity subtraction technique is introduced. It replaces the source point with its projection on an extrapolation of the patch surface, where the integration has to be performed.

The paper is organized as follows. The next section introduces the Helmholtz problem and its conventional boundary integral formulation for both Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions. Then, in Section
3
the multi-patch IgA setting for the standard CAD representation of a bounded or unbounded domain is described, as well as the finite dimensional functional spaces adopted for the analysis. Section
4
describes the developed quadrature formulas, based on the tensor-product formulation of a discrete spline quasi-interpolation approach. The formulas are combined with a subtraction regularization technique and applied to the single and double layer Helmholtz kernels. In Section
5
the results obtained for some benchmark problems are reported and commented, while some conclusive remarks are given in Section
6
. Finally, some technical details related to considered singular kernels are available in Appendix.

2
The Helmholtz problem and its boundary integral formulation

We study 3D potential problems described by the Helmholtz equation with Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions on domains
\Omega\subset{\mathop{{\rm I}\kern-1.99997pt{\rm R}}\nolimits}^{3}
admitting a connected closed boundary surface
\Gamma=\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u+\kappa^{2}u=0&{\rm in}\;\Omega,\\
u=u_{\rm D}\quad{\rm or}\;\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\bf n}}=u_{\rm N}&{\rm on}\;\Gamma,\end{array}\right.

where
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
denotes the unknown
potential
,
{\bf n}
the unit normal on
\Gamma
pointing outward from
\Omega
and
\kappa>0.
When the domain is a finite volume the problem is
interior
and we use the notation
\Omega^{[i]}
to underline the type of this domain when it is useful. In the opposite case, we set
\Omega^{[e]}
and we study the
exterior
problem.
Concerning the regularity of the boundary surface
\Gamma,
for simplicity we assume to be smooth, at least without self-intersections and with a tangent plane well defined at each point and varying continuously.
Note that for exterior problems, equation (
2
) has to be augmented with an additional condition, the so called
Sommerfeld radiation condition
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}-i\kappa u=o\left(\frac{1}{r}\right),

(3)

where
r
denotes the point distance from the origin of the reference system and
i
is the imaginary unit; see for example
[
13
]
. This condition at infinity is necessary to ensure for any positive
\kappa
the existence and uniqueness of the solution
u
for exterior Helmholtz problems for both Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions. Uniqueness of
u
for interior problems is ensured when
\kappa^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of the reversed Laplacian operator
-\Delta
in
\Omega

1
1

1

The scalar
\lambda
is an eigenvalue for
-\Delta
if there exists a non vanishing function
u_{\lambda}
such that
-\Delta u_{\lambda}=\lambda u_{\lambda}
, also fulfilling homogeneous boundary condition of the assigned type. It is possible to prove that the operator
-\Delta
admits only positive eigenvalues, which define an unbounded infinite sequence of positive numbers depending on the considered kind of boundary conditions and on the shape of the finite domain
\Omega,
see for example
[
20
]
.
.
Regarding the regularity of the weak solution
u
of (
2
), we observe that
u
belongs to the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega),
provided that, for Dirichlet (Neumann) boundary conditions,
u_{\rm D}\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma)\,,\,\left(u_{\rm N}\in H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\right)
.

The Helmholtz equation is of particular interest in acoustic because the solution
u=u({\bf x},\kappa)
,
{\bf x}\in\Omega
of (
2
) can be interpreted as the inverse Fourier transform
[
21
]
of the time-dependent scattered pressure field
p=p({\bf x}\,,\,t)
generated by the scatterer in a given fluid domain, which fulfills the wave equation,

\frac{1}{c_{f}^{2}}\ \frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial t^{2}}=\Delta p.

(4)

Namely, setting

\breve{p}=\breve{p}({\bf x}\,,\,\nu):=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}p({\bf x}\,,\,t)e^{i\nu t}\,dt

it is easy to check that
\breve{p}
verifies the Helmholtz equation with
\kappa=\nu/c_{f}
and that, conversely, it is

p=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\breve{p}({\bf x}\,,\,\nu)e^{i\nu t}\,d\nu\,.

This is of interest because it allows us to deal with a differential problem formulated just in terms of spatial variables.

In order to approximate the solution of (
2
) with IgA-BEMs, we consider the Conventional Boundary Integral Equation (CBIE) associated with the Helmholtz equation and derived by direct approach,

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}({\bf x},{\bf y})\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\bf n}}({\bf y})d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}=c({\bf x})u({\bf x})+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{{\bf y}}}({\bf x},{\bf y})\ u({\bf y})\ d\Gamma_{{\bf y}},\qquad{\bf x}\in\Gamma,

(5)

where
r:=\|{\bf r}\|_{2}
with
{\bf r}:={\bf x}-{\bf y}
. The kernel
{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}
and its derivative with respect to the exterior unit normal
{\bf n}
to
\Gamma
computed in the point
{\bf y}\in\Gamma
are defined as

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}({\bf x},{\bf y}):=\frac{1}{4\pi r}e^{{\rm i}\kappa r},\qquad\qquad\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{{\bf y}}}({\bf x},{\bf y})\,=\,\frac{1}{4\pi r}e^{{\rm i}\kappa r}(-\frac{1}{r}+{\rm i}\kappa)\ \frac{\partial r}{\partial{\bf n}_{{\bf y}}}\,,

with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r}{\partial{\bf n}_{{\bf y}}}=-\frac{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{{\bf y}}}{r},\qquad\qquad c({\bf x}):=-\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{G}}_{0}}{\partial{\bf n}_{{\bf y}}}({\bf x},{\bf y})d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}\,,

being
c({\bf x})\equiv 1/2
for the assumed smooth surface
\Gamma
. The restrictions of
u
and
({\partial u}/{\partial{\bf n}})({\bf y})
to
\Gamma
are usually called
Cauchy data
, being one of them coincident with the available boundary datum and the other the unknown.
By separating the real and imaginary parts of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa},
we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{\cos(\kappa r)}{r}+{\rm i}\frac{\sin(\kappa r)}{r}\right).

(6)

Note that the real part of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}
goes to infinity as
1/r
when
r\rightarrow 0^{+}\,,
while its imaginary part tends to
{\kappa}/{(4\pi)}
. The other kernel involved in (
5
) is
{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}/{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}
, which can be written as follows, again separating its real and imaginary parts,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}({\bf x},{\bf y})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\ \left[\left(\frac{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{\bf y}}{r^{3}}\left(\cos(\kappa r)+\kappa r\sin(\kappa r)\right)\right)\,+\,{\rm i}\frac{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{\bf y}}{r^{2}}\left(\frac{\sin(\kappa r)}{r}-\kappa\cos(\kappa r)\right)\right].

(7)

For a regular surface
\Gamma
described by a regular smooth parameterization, the quantity
{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{\bf y}}/{r^{2}}
is bounded for
r\rightarrow 0^{+}
but in general it is not continuous at
r=0
; see the Appendix for the proof. The bounded behavior at
r=0
of such quantity implies that, analogously to
{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}
, the kernel
{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}/{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}
is just weakly singular, see Section 9.1 in
[
22
]
and also the analytical related insights explicitly reported in the Appendix. Indeed, its real part goes to infinity as
1/r
when
r\rightarrow 0^{+}
while its imaginary part tends to zero because
{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{\bf y}}/{r^{2}}
remains bounded and it is multiplied by a factor going to zero.

Clearly, when the boundary conditions in (
2
) are of Dirichlet type, the unknown in (
5
) is the flux
\phi:=({\partial u}/{\partial{\bf n}})|\Gamma,
belonging to
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
and the CBIE becomes

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}({\bf x},{\bf y})\phi({\bf y})d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}\,=\,c({\bf x})u_{\rm D}({\bf x})+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}({\bf x},{\bf y})u_{\rm D}({\bf y})d\Gamma_{{\bf y}},\qquad{\bf x}\in\Gamma.

(8)

Note that this is an integral equation of the general type

V_{\kappa}\phi({\bf x})=f({\bf x}),\qquad{\bf x}\in\Gamma,

(9)

where
f
denotes a known function and the single layer operator
V_{\kappa}:H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
is an elliptic isomorphism defined as,

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}\phi({\bf x}):=\int_{\Gamma}{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}({\bf x},{\bf y})\phi({\bf y})\,d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}.

Conversely, when Neumann conditions are dealt with, the unknown in (
5
) is the acoustic potential
\phi:=u|\Gamma,
with
\phi\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
and the CBIE becomes

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}({\bf x},{\bf y})\phi({\bf y})\,d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}+c({\bf x})\phi({\bf x})\,=\,+\int_{\Gamma}{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}({\bf x},{\bf y})u_{\rm N}({\bf y})d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}\qquad{\bf x}\in\Gamma.

(10)

This is an integral equation of the general type

(c(x)I+K_{\kappa})\phi({\bf x})=f({\bf x}),\qquad{\bf x}\in\Gamma,

(11)

associated with the operator
cI+K_{\kappa},
where
I
denotes the identity operator and
K_{\kappa}:H^{1/2}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
is the following double layer operator,

\displaystyle K_{\kappa}\phi({\bf x}):=\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}({\bf x},{\bf y})\ \phi({\bf y})\,d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}.

Note that in our case the right hand side
f
in both (
9
) and (
11
) is just the right hand side respectively of (
8
) and (
10
) and also that clearly it is in any case
\phi=\phi({\bf x},\kappa)
.

If
\phi
is available, the solution
u=u({\bf x},\kappa)
,
{\bf x}\in\Omega
of the BVP in (
2
) is obtained by using the so-called
representation formula
,

\displaystyle u({\bf x},\kappa)=\pm\left(\int_{\Gamma}{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}({\bf x},{\bf y})\ \frac{\partial u}{\partial{\bf n}}({\bf y})\ d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}-\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}({\bf x},{\bf y})\ u({\bf y})\ d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}\right),\qquad{\bf x}\in\Omega,

(12)

where the sign is positive if
\Omega=\Omega^{[i]}
(interior problem) and negative otherwise. We note that the numerical implementation of this formula is not completely trivial because near singular integrals have to be approximated, when
{\bf x}
is very near to
\Gamma
. However, one can be merely interested in
\phi
or in evaluating
u
just at points sufficiently far from
\Gamma.
For example, when an exterior problem is taken into account, often the interest is just in the
far field pattern
of
u,
that is into the recovery of
u_{\infty}\,,
where

\displaystyle u_{\infty}({\bf w}):=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}re^{-i\kappa r}u(r{\bf w})\,,\qquad{\bf w}\in S^{2},

where
S^{2}
is a unit sphere in
{\mathop{{\rm I}\kern-1.99997pt{\rm R}}\nolimits}^{3}
centered at the origin of the considered reference system. In such case we can rely on the following formula whose computation just requires rules for regular integrals
[
15
]
,

\displaystyle u_{\infty}({\bf w})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\int_{\Gamma}\left(i\kappa u({\bf y})({\bf w}\cdot{\bf n}({\bf y}))\,+\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\bf n}}({\bf y})\right)e^{-i\kappa({\bf w}\cdot{\bf y})}d\Gamma_{{\bf y}}.

3
Multi-patch Isogeometric model

In this section we introduce the IgA-BEM model, with respect to the geometry representation and the discretization space to express the numerical solution. The flexibility to model complex geometries in the 3D case is facilitated by adopting its multi-patch formulation.
We assume that the boundary
{\Gamma}
is a union of
M
patches
\Gamma^{(\ell)},\ell=1,\ldots,M,
and for
\ell\neq k
it holds
\Gamma^{(\ell)}\cap\Gamma^{(k)}=\emptyset
and
\partial\Gamma^{(\ell)}\cap\partial\Gamma^{(k)}
is a boundary edge curve of each patch, a corner point of each
patch or an empty set. To each patch
\Gamma^{(\ell)}
we assign a geometry map
{\bf F}^{(\ell)}
, such that
\overline{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}=\mbox{Image}({\bf F}^{(\ell)})
,
{\bf F}^{(\ell)}~:~[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\overline{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}\subset{\mathop{{\rm I}\kern-1.99997pt{\rm R}}\nolimits}^{3}
is regular and belongs to
C^{2}([0,1]^{2})
.
Each
{\bf F}^{(\ell)}
is written in the following standard NURBS form
[
2
]
,

\displaystyle{\bf F}^{(\ell)}({\bf t}):=\frac{\displaystyle{\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal J_{\rm g}}^{(\ell)}}}w_{{\bf i}}^{(\ell)}\,{\bf Q}_{{\bf i}}^{(\ell)}\,\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf d}_{\rm g}}^{(\ell)}({\bf t})}{\displaystyle{\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal J_{\rm g}}^{(\ell)}}}w_{{\bf i}}^{(\ell)}\,\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf d}_{\rm g}}^{(\ell)}({\bf t})},\quad{\bf t}\in[0,1]^{2},

(13)

where a set
\{{\bf Q}_{{\bf i}}^{(\ell)}\in{\mathop{{\rm I}\kern-1.99997pt{\rm R}}\nolimits}^{3},{\bf i}\in{\cal J_{\rm g}}^{(\ell)}\}
defines a net of control points which, together with the associated set of positive weights
\{w_{{\bf i}}^{(\ell)}\in{\mathop{{\rm I}\kern-1.99997pt{\rm R}}\nolimits},{\bf i}\in{\cal J_{\rm g}}^{(\ell)}\},
is the basic element typically used in the CAD environment to design free-form surfaces. The tensor product B-spline basis function
\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf d}_{\rm g}}^{(\ell)}({\bf t}):=\hat{B}_{i_{1},d_{\rm g,1}}^{(\ell)}(t_{1})\ \hat{B}_{i_{2},d_{\rm g,2}}^{(\ell)}(t_{2})
, where
{\bf i}:=(i_{1},i_{2})\in\cal J_{\rm g}^{(\ell)}
, is of bi-degree
{\bf d}_{\rm g}=(d_{\rm g,1},d_{\rm g,2})
, defined on
[0,1]^{2}
in variable
{\bf t}:=(t_{1},t_{2})
and with respect to the bidimensional knot array
T_{\rm g}^{(\ell)}
associated with the
\ell
-th patch,
T_{\rm g}^{(\ell)}:=T_{\rm g,1}^{(\ell)}\times T_{\rm g,2}^{(\ell)}
, and
T_{\rm g,1}^{(\ell)}
and
T_{{\rm g},2}^{(\ell)}
are both clamped knot vectors with entries in the interval
[0,1]
. When two patches share an edge
{\cal C}_{\ell,k}:=\partial{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}\cap\partial{\Gamma}^{(k)}
for
\ell\neq k
, the shared edge is a NURBS curve. For simplicity we usually assume it to be parameterized with the same geometry map for both patches, up to reversion in directions and direction swapping in the parametric space.

As commonly done also in the literature, in order to gain more flexibility, in our generalized IgA-BEM setting the unknown Cauchy datum
\phi
is approximated in a functional space whose restriction to
\bar{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}
is the geometric counterpart of the space generated from bivariate B-splines always defined in
[0,1]^{2}
but not necessarily obtained with a refinement procedure from
\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf d}_{\rm g}}^{(\ell)},\,{\bf i}\in{\cal J_{\rm g}}^{(\ell)}
. Such space is characterized by its own degree
{\bf d}=(d_{1},d_{2})
(common to all patches) and its extended knot vectors,
{T}_{j}^{(\ell)},j=1,2.

Then, denoting with
h_{j}^{(\ell)}
the maximal distance between successive knots in
{T}_{j}^{(\ell)},
setting
h^{(\ell)}:=\max\{h_{1}^{(\ell)},\,h_{2}^{(\ell)}\}
and introducing a set of bivariate indices
{\cal J}^{(\ell)}
for the basis functions, we can introduce
\hat{\cal S}^{(\ell)}_{h^{(\ell)}}
which denotes the spline space generated by the tensor product B-spline basis
\{\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}:{\bf j}=(j_{1},j_{2})\in{\cal J}^{(\ell)}\}
of bi-degree
{\bf d}
defined on
[0,1]^{2}
with respect to the bidimensional knot array
{T}^{(\ell)}:={T}_{1}^{(\ell)}\times{T}_{2}^{(\ell)}
.

3.1
Discontinuous basis

When no continuity constraint is imposed across patches for the basis functions, the global space used to approximate the unknown Cauchy datum
\phi
is

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{h,{\bf d}}\,:=\,\mathrm{span}\{{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}:{\bf j}\in{\cal J}^{(\ell)},\quad\ell=1,\ldots,M\},

where
h:=\max\{h^{(\ell)},\ell=1,\ldots,M\}
and

\displaystyle{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}({\bf x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}\circ{{\bf F}^{(\ell)}}^{-1}({\bf x}),&\mbox{if }{\bf x}\in{\Gamma}^{(\ell)},\cr 0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Clearly with this setting the global number
N_{\rm DOF}
of degrees of freedom used to approximate
\phi
is

\displaystyle{N}_{\rm DOF}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}|{\cal J}^{(\ell)}|,

where
|{\cal J}^{(\ell)}|
is the cardinality of the set.
In this setting it is convenient to select
|\cal J^{(\ell)}|
distinct collocation points belonging to the interior of each patch
\Gamma^{(\ell)}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,M.
In particular we fix
{\bf x}_{\bf j}^{(\ell)}={\bf F}^{(\ell)}({\bf s}_{\bf j})
,
{\bf j}\in{\cal J}^{(\ell)},
where
\{{\bf s}_{\bf j}^{(\ell)}:{\bf j}\in{\cal J}_{\ell}\}
denotes a set of points in
(0,1)^{2}
, defined as the Cartesian product of two sets of abscissas, the so-called improved Greville abscissas associated with
{T}_{1}^{(\ell)}
and
{T}_{2}^{(\ell)}

[
23
]
. We recall that the improved Greville abscissae related to a set of Greville abscissae
\bar{\xi}_{1},\bar{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{n}
, are Greville abscissae whose first and last elements are modified as follows:

\bar{\xi}_{1}=\bar{\xi}_{1}+\omega(\bar{\xi}_{2}-\bar{\xi}_{1}),\;\;\;\bar{\xi}_{n}=\bar{\xi}_{n}-\omega(\bar{\xi}_{n}-\bar{\xi}_{n-1})\,,\quad\mbox{with}\,\,0<\omega<1\,.

(14)

Thus, a discrete version of the integral formulation of the Dirichlet (
8
) or Neumann (
10
) problem is obtained by approximating

\phi
in the finite dimensional composite space
{\cal S}_{h,{\bf d}}
.
The applied collocation method leads to a linear system

\displaystyle A\,\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}.

(15)

The unknown entries in the vector
\alpha
are the coefficients which allow us to define our approximation
\phi_{h}
of
\phi
in the space
{\cal S}_{h,{\bf d}}
patch-wisely as follows,

\displaystyle\phi_{h}({\bf x}):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\sum_{{\bf j}\in{\cal J}^{(\ell)}}\alpha^{(\ell)}_{\bf j}{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}({\bf x}),\qquad{\bf x}\in{\Gamma}.

The square system matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{{N_{\rm DOF}}\times N_{\rm DOF}}
and the right-hand side vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\rm DOF}}
have a block representation,
A=(A^{(\ell,k)}),\,\,\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(\ell)})
, for
\ell,k=1,\dots,M
, where
A^{(\ell,k)}\in\mathbb{C}^{|{\cal J}_{\ell}|\times|{\cal J}_{k}|}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{C}^{|{\cal J}_{\ell}|}
.
For each pair of patches
\Gamma^{\ell},\Gamma^{k}
, the rows (columns) of the related submatrix are ordered by using a lexicographical ordering of the elements in
\mathcal{J}^{(\ell)}
(
\mathcal{J}^{(k)}
), namely we identify a single index
i
with
{\bf i}
and
j
with
{\bf j}
. After this simplification, for the Dirichlet case such entries read as follows in both the physical and the parametric domain,

\displaystyle A^{(\ell,k)}_{i,j}:=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma^{(k)}}{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)},{\bf y})B^{(k)}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf y})\ d\Gamma\,=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)},{\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\hat{B}^{(k)}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\ J^{(k)}({\bf t})\ d{\bf t},

(16)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(\ell)}_{i}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}\int_{\Gamma^{(k)}}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)},{\bf y})\ u_{\rm D}({\bf y})\ d\Gamma+\frac{1}{2}u_{\rm D}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)})=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}\ \int_{[0,1]^{2}}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)},{\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\ u_{\rm D}({\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\ J^{(k)}({\bf t})\ d{\bf t}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\rm D}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)}),

(17)

where
i=1,\ldots,|{\cal J}^{(\ell)}|,\quad j=1,\ldots,|{\cal J}^{(k)}|,\,\,\hat{B}^{(k)}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}\in{\hat{\cal S}}^{(k)}_{h^{(k)}}
and
J^{(k)}
represents the infinitesimal surface area element on the

-th patch,

J^{(k)}(\cdot):=\left\|\frac{\partial{\bf F}^{(k)}}{\partial t_{1}}(\cdot)\times\frac{\partial{\bf F}^{(k)}}{\partial t_{2}}(\cdot)\right\|_{2}.

Conversely, for the Neumann case we have

\displaystyle A^{(\ell,k)}_{i,j}=

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma^{(k)}}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)},{\bf y})B_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(k)}({\bf y})\ d\Gamma+\frac{1}{2}B_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(k)}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)})=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)},{\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(k)}({\bf t})J^{(k)}({\bf t})\ d{\bf t}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(k)}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)}),

(18)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(\ell)}_{i}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}\ \int_{\Gamma^{(k)}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)},{\bf y})\ u_{\rm N}({\bf y})\ d\Gamma\,=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\ \int_{[0,1]^{2}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)},{\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\ u_{\rm N}({\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\ J^{(k)}({\bf t})\ d{\bf t},

(19)

Referring for brevity just to the Dirichlet case, we report below an equivalent scaled expression of these entries, since it will be useful in Section
4
to develop a reasonably scaled procedure to detect near singular integrals to be computed during the assembly phase. Denoting with
\mu_{k}
a reference length for the
k
-th patch (e.g., the square root of its area or its diameter), with
\varphi_{k}
the associated uniform scaling such that
\tilde{\bf z}=\varphi_{k}({\bf z})={\bf z}/\mu_{k}
and with
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(k)}
the conformally scaled version of the
k
–th patch, we have also

\displaystyle A^{(\ell,k)}_{i,j}=

\displaystyle\mu_{k}\ \int_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa_{k}}(\varphi_{k}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)}),\tilde{{\bf y}})B^{(k)}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}(\mu_{k}\tilde{{\bf y}})\ d\tilde{\Gamma}=

\displaystyle\mu_{k}\ \int_{[0,1]^{2}}{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa_{k}}(\varphi_{k}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)}),\tilde{{\bf F}}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\hat{B}^{(k)}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\ \tilde{J}^{(k)}({\bf t})\ d{\bf t},

(20)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(\ell)}_{i}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}\ \int_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{(k)}}\frac{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa^{(k)}}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\tilde{y}}}(\varphi_{k}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)}),\tilde{\bf y})\ u_{\rm D}(\mu_{k}\tilde{\bf y})\ d\tilde{\Gamma}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\rm D}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)})=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}\ \int_{[0,1]^{2}}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa_{k}}}{\partial{\bf n}_{\tilde{{\bf y}}}}(\varphi_{k}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)}),\tilde{{\bf F}}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\ u_{\rm D}(\mu_{k}\tilde{{\bf F}}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\ \tilde{J}^{(k)}({\bf t})\ d{\bf t}+\frac{1}{2}u_{\rm D}({\bf x}_{\bf i}^{(\ell)}),

(21)

where
\tilde{\bf F}^{(k)}:={\bf F}^{(k)}/\mu_{k},\,\tilde{J}^{(k)}
is the related infinitesimal scaled area element and
\kappa_{k}:=\kappa\mu_{k}.

3.2
C^{0}
continuous basis

Let us denote the space of B-spline functions that join with
C^{0}
regularity across patches by
{\cal S}^{0}_{h,{\bf d}}:={\cal S}_{h,{\bf d}}\cap C^{0}({\Gamma})
. For simplicity let us assume that any two adjacent patches
\Gamma^{(\ell)},\Gamma^{(k)}
with a common boundary curve
{\cal C}_{\ell,k}
have the same knot vector on the edges of the patches. Namely, on
\ell
-th patch we consider the knot vector
T_{1}^{(\ell)}
if
{\bf F}^{(\ell)}(t_{1},0)
or
{\bf F}^{(\ell)}(t_{1},1)
define the curve
{\cal C}_{\ell,k}
, and
T_{2}^{(\ell)}
in the other case. The same procedure is done for
k
-th patch. Our assumption then states that the two vectors
T_{i}^{(\ell)}
,
T_{j}^{(k)}
need to coincide (up to a possible reversion of one knot sequence, e.g., when
{\bf F}^{(\ell)}(1,t_{2})={\bf F}^{(k)}(1,1-t_{2})
). For a more general setting to describe the geometry and the discretization space without the matching of adjacent knot vectors we refer to
[
1
]
.

The so-called interior basis functions for
{\cal S}^{0}_{h,{\bf d}}
includes all basis functions
{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}
from
{\cal S}_{h,{\bf d}}
, whose corresponding B-splines
\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}
vanish on
\partial([0,1]^{2})
. The so-called edge or vertex basis functions are obtained by identifying and summing together the remaining basis functions from
{\cal S}_{h,{\bf d}}
with shared knot vectors. More precisely, an edge basis function

associated to
{\cal C}_{\ell,k}
is a sum of two remaining (not interior) functions
B_{{\bf i},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}
and

from
{\cal S}_{h,{\bf d}}

{\cal C}_{\ell,k}
and satisfy

\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}\circ{{\bf F}^{(\ell)}}^{-1}({\bf x})=\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}^{(k)}\circ{{\bf F}^{(k)}}^{-1}({\bf x})
for every
{\bf x}\in{\cal C}_{\ell,k}
. Similarly, a vertex function associated to a patch corner vertex
{\bf x}_{v}
, is defined as a sum of all
basis functions
B_{{\bf i},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}
from
{\cal S}_{h,{\bf d}}
,
\ell=1,\dots,M
and
{\bf i}\in\cal J^{(\ell)}
, that satisfy
\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf d}}^{(\ell)}\circ{{\bf F}^{(\ell)}}^{-1}({\bf x}_{v})\neq 0
.

In all our experiments with the
C^{0}
continuous basis we have fixed the collocation points as a set of images of Cartesian products of standard Greville abscissas on
[0,1]^{2}
, mapped to
\overline{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}
via map
{\bf F}^{(\ell)}
for all
\ell=1,\dots,M
, and by counting just once the repeated points, which appear on the boundary curves of patches.

The approximate solution
\phi_{h}
is constructed similarly to the discontinuous case, bearing in mind a variation in the collocation points and a sum of adequate entries in
(
16
), (
3.1
) over all suitable

\Gamma^{(k)}
adjacent to

.

4
Quadratures

4.1
Singularity extraction

Let us introduce our novel numerical integration method which makes possible a function–by–function assembly phase. For the sake of simplicity, we refer just to the one patch setting, i.e., we assume
M=1,
thus avoiding any reference to the patch index, when the source point and the integration domain belong to the same patch. Some considerations about integration in the more general multi-patch setting are also given.

It is convenient to write the two singular kernels
{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}
and
{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}/{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}
as in (
6
) and (
7
) and analyze which terms are singular, hence a singular integration has to be considered, and which terms define regular integrals. Referring to the Appendix for some explicit insights, as already noted in Section
2
, the real part in (
6
) is weakly singular at
r=0
with its non-singular part
\cos(\kappa r)
smooth under the assumption of a smooth geometry parameterization. For the same reason, its imaginary part is a smooth function. Similarly, the factor
{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{\bf y}/r^{3}
of the real part in (
7
) is weakly singular, while the other factor,
(\cos(\kappa r)+\kappa\sin(\kappa r)r),
is smooth. Finally, the imaginary part in (
7
) is in general only
C^{1}
continuous at
r=0
because the factor
{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{\bf y}}/{r^{2}}
is bounded and
(\sin(\kappa r)/r-\kappa\cos(\kappa r))
has a double zero at
r=0
.
In our numerical experiments such smoothness of the integrands was sufficient to numerically integrate the corresponding integrals with the required accuracy.

Remark 1

To improve accuracy of the numerical integration for integrals involving the imaginary part of (
7
), a more sophisticated approach could be considered. For example, the integrals could be handled similarly as the singular ones, treating the part
{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{\bf y}}/{r^{2}}
as the “less regular part” of the integrand, since the remaining part
\left({\sin(\kappa r)}/r-\kappa\cos(\kappa r)\right)
is an analytic function.

In order to give a compact introduction of our quadrature approach, first we denote with
{\bf s}\in[0,1]^{2}
the pre-image in the parametric domain of a fixed generic collocation point
{\bf x}
. Note that for brevity in the following we refer to the patch index only when it is essential. Conversely, we refer to the patch scaled integrals appearing for example in formulas (
3.1
)–(
3.1
), as well as to the related notation also introduced in Section
3
. So for example we denote with
\tilde{\bf x}
the scaling of
{\bf x}
, assuming that the considered scaling is that associated with the patch where the integration is developed. Then, we can introduce the following general form representing any integral to be dealt with,

\displaystyle\int_{R_{\bf j}}U({\bf s},{\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\ g({\bf t})\,d{\bf t},

(22)

where
g
is a given smooth function,
\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}
is a B-spline basis function,
R_{\bf j}:=\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\{\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}\}
and
U
refers to any of the following two weakly singular kernels respectively associated with the single and double layer potential,

\displaystyle U({\bf s},{\bf t})=U_{\rm SL}({\bf s},{\bf t}):=\frac{1}{\|\tilde{\bf x}({\bf s})-\tilde{\bf F}({\bf t})\|_{2}}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad U({\bf s},{\bf t})=U_{\rm DL}({\bf s},{\bf t}):={\frac{(\tilde{\bf x}({\bf s})-\tilde{\bf F}({\bf t}))\cdot\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}({\bf t})}{\|\tilde{\bf x}({\bf s})-\tilde{\bf F}({\bf t})\|_{2}^{3}}},

(23)

with
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}({\bf t}):=({\partial\tilde{\bf F}}/{\partial t_{1}})({\bf t})\times({\partial\tilde{\bf F}}/{\partial t_{2}})({\bf t})
and
\tilde{\bf F}
denoting the scaled normal and the scaled mapping associated to the conformally scaled surface patch.

Remark 2

Integrals defining the entries of
\beta
do not include the basis functions
\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}
directly. To split the integration domain
[0,1]^{2}
into smaller subdomains and arrive to the above general form, we introduce additional knot vectors,
T_{{\rm r},1},T_{{\rm r},2}
, and inside (
3.1
) and (
19
) we add a sum of all B-spline basis functions
\hat{B}_{{\bf j},\boldsymbol{0}}
of degree zero, defined on
T_{{\rm r},1}\times T_{{\rm r},2}
. Therefore, the approach to evaluate integrals in
\beta
is analogous to the one for the system matrix
A
.

It is easy to determine when the integral in (
22
) is actually singular. Indeed, since we are dealing with smooth surfaces without self-intersections, this occurs if and only if
\tilde{\bf x}
belongs to the scaled patch where the integration is developed, and the associated
{\bf s}
belongs to
R_{\bf j}
.
On the other hand, detection of nearly singular integrals that require analogous treatment is more delicate if we want to preserve the accuracy of the numerical scheme without deteriorating its efficiency.
In such setting we propose to consider nearly singular the integral in (
22
) when

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{{\rm min},{\bf j}}({\bf x}):=\min_{{\bf t}\in R_{\bf j}}\|\tilde{\bf x}-\tilde{\bf F}({\bf t})\|_{2}\leq\,c\ \delta,

(24)

where
0<c<1
is a user-defined constant and
\delta
is a suitable patch-depended threshold.
For further details concerning the definition of
\delta
see the discussion in Subection
4.5
.

When the condition (
24
) is not satisfied, the kernel
U
is considered regular and integration rules for regular integrals are applied. Conversely, when the kernel
U
in (
22
) is weakly singular or near singular, we preliminarily adopt a regularization technique based on the subtraction of singularity,

\displaystyle\int_{R_{\bf j}}U({\bf s},{\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})g({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}=\int_{R_{\bf j}}\big(U({\bf s},{\bf t})-{U}_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})\big)\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})g({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}+\int_{R_{\bf j}}{U}_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})g({\bf t})\,d{\bf t},

(25)

where
{U}_{{\bf s}}^{m}
is an approximation of
U({\bf s},\bullet)
, obtained by truncating a particular series expansion of
U
about the singular point
{\bf t}={\bf s}
after the
m
-th term; see
[
17
]
for the first application of these expansions in the simplest form and
[
19
]
for detailed construction and analysis.
Note that the singularity subtraction technique that splits an integral into two ones is applied only to a small portion of integrals. In this case, the first term of the right hand side of (
25
) becomes a
regularized integral
while the second one is a singular integral with a simplified singular kernel. The continuity of the integrand in the regularized integral is controlled by the number
m
of terms in
U_{{\bf s}}^{m}
and is equal to
C^{m-2}
at
{\bf t}={\bf s}
for the chosen
m\geq 1
(with
C^{-1}
we denote functions that are integrable in the standard sense but have discontinuities of the first kind at
{\bf t}={\bf s}
), if the geometry map
{\bf F}
is a smooth enough function
[
19
]
.

By defining local coordinates
{\bf z}:={\bf s}-{\bf t}
, the kernel reads

\displaystyle U^{m}_{\bf s}({\bf z})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}R_{\bf s}({\bf z})^{-2(k+\gamma)+1}\;P_{{\bf s},3k+2\gamma-3}^{[k]}({\bf z}),

(26)

where
R_{\bf s}({\bf z}):=\sqrt{{\bf z}^{\top}M_{\bf s}{\bf z}}
is a square root of a bivariate quadratic homogeneous
polynomial and
M_{\bf s}
is the
2\times 2
symmetric positive definite matrix associated with the first fundamental form of a smooth surface (e.g., see formulas (
44
) and (
46
) in the Appendix),

P_{{\bf s},3k+2\gamma-3}^{[k]}
are suitable homogeneous polynomials of degrees
3k+2\gamma-3
, and
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=1
for
{\mathcal{G}}_{\kappa}
and
{\partial{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\kappa}}/{\partial{\bf n}_{\bf y}}
, respectively. Note that coefficients inside
R_{\bf s}
and
P_{{\bf s},3k+2\gamma-3}^{[k]}
depend on a chosen
{\bf s}
and on derivatives of the map
\tilde{\bf F}
.
For example, for
m=1
the kernels of the single and double layer potentials are approximated as follows,

\displaystyle U^{1}_{\bf s}({\bf z})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[R_{\bf s}({\bf z})^{-1}\right]&\mbox{if }U=U_{\rm SL}\,,\cr\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[R_{\bf s}({\bf z})^{-3}P^{[1]}_{{\bf s},2}({\bf z})\right]&\mbox{if }U=U_{\rm DL}\,,\cr\end{array}\right.

where
P^{[1]}_{{\bf s},2}({\bf z})=\frac{1}{2}\left(L\ z_{1}^{2}+2M\ z_{1}z_{2}+Nz_{2}^{2}\right)
,
and
L,M,N
are the coefficients of the second fundamental form of the geometry, evaluated at
{\bf s}
, see (
45

Relating to the more general multi-patch setting, when source point
{\bf x}={\bf F}^{(\ell)}({\bf s})
and integration variable

belong to different adjacent patches,
\Gamma^{(\ell)}
and

In order to explain our procedure in such setting, we need first to make the notation more precise for
\ell\neq k
, adding the superscript
(\ell,k)
when referring to the kernel
U,\ U\rightarrow U^{(\ell,k)},
whenever
U_{\rm SL}
or
U_{\rm DL}
is referred to,

\displaystyle U^{(\ell,k)}_{\rm SL}({\bf s},{\bf t}):=\frac{1}{\|\tilde{\bf F}^{(\ell)}({\bf s})-\tilde{\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf t})\|_{2}},\quad U^{(\ell,k)}_{\rm DL}({\bf s},{\bf t}):={\frac{(\tilde{\bf F}^{(\ell)}({\bf s})-\tilde{\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf t}))\cdot\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}^{(k)}({\bf t})}{\|\tilde{\bf F}^{(\ell)}({\bf s})-\tilde{\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf t})\|_{2}^{3}}},

(27)

indicating that the source point
\tilde{\bf x}
is connected with
{\bf s}
in the parametric domain via the geometry map
\tilde{\bf F}^{(\ell)}
but integration has to be developed in the

-th patch.
Since the parameterization of the geometry is prescribed patch-wisely, the proposed series expansion

is limited to the

-th patch. On the other hand, since
{\bf F}^{(k)}
is regular, we can (slightly) enlarge its definition outside the original domain
[0,1]^{2}
and still have a regular geometry map; for convenience let us use the same notation for the extended function,

{\bf F}^{(k)}:[0,1]^{2}\subset D_{k}\rightarrow{\mathop{{\rm I}\kern-1.99997pt{\rm R}}\nolimits}^{3}

describes just an approximation of the actual geometry, which is exactly described by

. By replacing
\tilde{\bf x}=\tilde{\bf F}^{(\ell)}({\bf s})
in (

\tilde{\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf s}_{e})

\displaystyle{\bf s}_{e}:=\arg\min_{{\bf s}^{\prime}\in D_{k}}\|\tilde{\bf x}-\tilde{\bf F}^{(k)}({\bf s}^{\prime})\|_{2}\,,

we obtain the kernel

4.2
B-spline quasi-interpolation scheme

To approximate integrals in 3D collocation BEM, we use the 2D Quasi Interpolation (QI) scheme firstly introduced in

[
24
]
which is the tensor-product extension of the univariate Hermite QI scheme introduced in
[
25
]
. With such approach a bivariate function
f
is approximated on a rectangle
R
by using a QI spline tensor product operator
f\rightarrow\sigma_{f}:=\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal J}_{R}}\lambda_{\bf i}\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf p}}
, where
\{\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf p}},{\bf i}\in{\cal J}_{R}\}
is the tensor-product B-spline basis generating the chosen finite dimensional space of splines defined on
R,
with
{\cal J}_{R}
denoting a local set of multi–indices used to identify the basis elements. In the considered QI scheme each coefficient
\lambda_{\bf i},{\bf i}\in{\cal J}_{R},
is computed as a linear combination of the values assumed in a suitable local subset of breakpoints by
f,
by both its first partial derivatives and also by its second mixed derivative, see
[
24
]
for details. However, in the present work we rely on a derivative-free variant of such QI scheme, since we are interested to employ it for numerical integration. Such variant is obtained approximating the required derivative values by suitable finite difference formulas. Then, collecting with lexicographical order all the coefficients
\lambda_{\bf i},{\bf i}\in{\cal J}_{R}
in a vector
\Lambda
, in compact matrix notation we have

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}=\left({\hat{C}}^{(p)}\right)^{\top}{\bf f}\,,

(28)

where
{\bf f}
is a vector collecting with the same ordering all the values of
f
at the breakpoints and

\displaystyle{\hat{C}}^{(p)}:=\left(({\hat{A}_{2}}^{\top}\otimes{\hat{A}_{1}}^{\top})-({\hat{A}_{2}}^{\top}\otimes({\hat{B}_{1}}^{\top}D_{t_{1}}))-(({\hat{B}_{2}}^{\top}D_{t_{2}})\otimes{\hat{A}_{1}}^{\top})+(({\hat{B}_{2}}^{\top}D_{t_{2}})\otimes({\hat{B}_{1}}^{\top}D_{t_{1}}))\right)^{\top}\,.

(29)

In the above formula
\hat{A_{i}},\,\hat{B_{i}}
denote the two banded matrices, explicitly reported for example in
[
26
]
, which are associated to the univariate original Hermite scheme formulated in the
i
-th direction,
i=1,2;

D_{t_{i}}
are the matrices associated with the two analogous directional finite difference formulas used by the considered QI variant of the original scheme for approximating the first partial derivatives. Clearly, by incorporating a sufficiently accurate finite differences formula to approximate the required derivatives, the resulting derivative-free QI scheme shares the approximation power with the original scheme.
On this concern, referring to the considered case with uniform breakpoints on
R
, the following error bound can be obtained for sufficiently smooth functions
[
24
]
,

\displaystyle\|f\,-\,\sigma_{f}\|_{\infty,R}\leq\,\|\partial^{p_{1}+1,0}f\|_{\infty,R}\left(\frac{H_{1}}{\nu_{1}-1}\right)^{p_{1}+1}+\|\partial^{0,p_{2}+1}f\|_{\infty,R}\left(\frac{H_{2}}{\nu_{2}-1}\right)^{p_{2}+1}

(30)

\displaystyle+\|\partial^{p_{1}+1,p_{2}+1}f\|_{\infty,R}\left(\frac{H_{1}}{\nu_{1}-1}\right)^{p_{1}+1}\left(\frac{H_{2}}{\nu_{2}-1}\right)^{p_{2}+1}\,,

where
H_{i}
and
\nu_{i}
respectively denote the size of
R
and the number of uniform breakpoints in the
i
-th coordinate direction,
i=1,2,
and
\partial^{i_{1},i_{2}}f
stands for
{\partial^{i_{1}+i_{2}}f}/{(\partial t_{1}^{i_{1}}\partial t_{2}^{i_{2}})}.

Thus the convergence order of the scheme with respect to
\max\{H_{1},H_{2}\}\rightarrow 0
but also to
\max\{(\nu_{1}-1)^{-1},(\nu_{2}-1)^{-1}\}
is equal to
\min\{p_{1},p_{2}\}+1
.

4.3
B-spline quadrature rules for regular integrals

Since in the assayed BIEs the regular integrands consist of the product between a bivariate regular function
f
with a tensor product B-spline
\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}
, we need to construct a quadrature rule to evaluate numerically the following integral:

\int_{R_{\bf j}}f({\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}.

(31)

The first step consists in approximating
f
with the QI scheme previously described. In this way we can write:

\displaystyle f\approx\sigma_{f}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal J}_{R_{\bf i}}}\lambda_{\bf i}\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf p}},

where
\hat{B}_{{\bf i},{\bf p}}
denotes a tensor product B-spline of bi-degree
{\bf p}=(p_{1},p_{2})
, which is a trial function of a local spline space defined in
R_{\bf j}
.
Hence

\displaystyle\int_{R_{\bf j}}f({\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}\approx\int_{R_{\bf j}}\sigma_{f}({\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}.

(32)

The product
\sigma_{f}\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}
is a new spline that can be expressed in terms of another B-spline basis defined in a suitable local “product” spline space of bi-degree
{\bf p}+{\bf d}
. Generalizing the idea developed in
[
27
,
28
]
for the one-dimensional case, the coefficients of
\sigma_{f}\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}
in the B-spline basis of the product space can be written as
G_{{\bf j}}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$},
with
\Lambda
defined by QI as specified in (
28
).
In the current bivariate setting the matrix
G_{\bf j}
is defined as
G_{\bf j}:=G_{j_{1}}^{(p_{1},d_{1})}\otimes G_{j_{2}}^{(p_{2},d_{2})},
where
G_{j_{1}}^{(p_{1},d_{1})}
and
G_{j_{2}}^{(p_{2},d_{2})}
are suitable direction-wise coefficient matrices and
{\bf j}=(j_{1},j_{2})
. The dimension of
G_{\bf j}
is
|{\cal J}_{R_{\bf j}}|\times N_{\pi_{\bf j}}
, where
N_{\pi_{\bf j}}
denotes the dimension of the introduced local product spline space. Thus, the integral in (
32
) can finally be evaluated as,

\displaystyle\int_{R_{{\bf j}}}f({\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})d{\bf t}\approx{\bf v}^{\top}G_{\bf j}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}={\bf v}^{\top}G_{\bf j}^{\top}(\hat{C}^{(p)})^{\top}{\bf f},

where the matrix
\hat{C}^{(p)}
is defined in (
29
) and
{\bf v}
denotes a vector of suitable length collecting in lexicographical order the following integrals of each B-spline
\hat{B}_{{\bf k},{\bf p}+{\bf d}}
of the basis of the local product spline space,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\int_{R_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}\hat{B}_{{\bf k},{\bf p}+{\bf d}}({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}\,=\,\frac{|\mbox{supp}(\hat{B}_{{\bf k},{\bf p}+{\bf d}})|}{(p_{1}+d_{1}+1)(p_{2}+d_{2}+1)}}\,,\qquad{\bf k}\in{\cal J}_{\pi_{\bf j}}\,,

where
{\cal J}_{\pi_{\bf j}}
is a set of
N_{\pi_{j}}
distinct multi-indices, as usually done to identify a tensor-product B-spline basis, and
{R_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}
is the support of the spline.

As already mentioned, the derived integration rule is the tensor product extension of a variant specific for integrals including a B-spline weight of the 1D quadrature formula introduced in
[
29
]
. Therefore, the error analysis developed in
[
29
]
can be easily extended to such variant and lifted to the tensor product setting. To be more concise, we assume that a uniform distribution of quadrature nodes is used in each
R_{\bf j}
(i.e., uniform breakpoints in the associated local spline space are used by the QI scheme), since this is our common setting in numerical experiments. Assuming
f
is a sufficiently smooth function, the quadrature error of the rule to approximate the integral in (
31
) is upper bounded by a positive constant times the following quantity,

\displaystyle\frac{H_{1,{\bf j}}H_{2,{\bf j}}}{(d_{1}+1)(d_{2}+1)}\,\left\{\,\|\partial^{p_{1}+r_{1}+1,0}f\|_{\infty,R_{\bf j}}\left(\frac{H_{1,{\bf j}}}{\nu_{1}-1}\right)^{p_{1}+r_{1}+1}+\|\partial^{0,p_{2}+r_{2}+1}f\|_{\infty,R_{\bf j}}\left(\frac{H_{2,{\bf j}}}{\nu_{2}-1}\right)^{p_{2}+r_{2}+1}\right.

(33)

\displaystyle+\left.\|\partial^{p_{1}+r_{1}+1,p_{2}+r_{2}+1}f\|_{\infty,R_{\bf j}}\left(\frac{H_{1,{\bf j}}}{\nu_{1}-1}\right)^{p_{1}+r_{1}+1}\left(\frac{H_{2,{\bf j}}}{\nu_{2}-1}\right)^{p_{2}+r_{2}+1}\right\}\,,

where
H_{i,{\bf j}}
and
\nu_{i}
respectively denote the size of
R_{{\bf j}}
and the number of uniform quadrature nodes in the
i
-th coordinate direction,
i=1,2
and
r_{i}=1(0)
for
p_{i}
even (odd).

For fixed numbers
\nu_{i}
of nodes, the convergence order of the quadrature scheme with respect to
\max\{H_{1,{\bf j}},H_{2,{\bf j}}\}\rightarrow 0
is

\displaystyle C_{1}\min\{H_{1,{\bf j}}^{p_{1}+r_{1}+3},H_{1,{\bf j}}^{p_{2}+r_{2}+3}\},

if the quotient
H_{1,{\bf j}}/H_{2,{\bf j}}
is bounded from below and above for all nested integration subdomains
R_{\bf j}
. The constant
C_{1}
contains norms of all the involved derivatives of
f
in (
33
) for the largest considered
R_{\bf j}
. For fixed
R_{\bf j}
, the convergence order with respect to
\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}
is equal to

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{1}\min\{(\nu_{1}-1)^{-p_{1}-r_{1}-1},(\nu_{2}-1)^{-p_{2}-r_{2}-1}\},

where the positive constant
\tilde{C}_{1}
contains also constants
H_{i,{\bf j}}
but not varying
\nu_{i}
.

4.4
B-spline quadrature rules for singular integrals

Let us focus now on the rules developed for the numerical approximation of the second addend on the right of (
25
), which involves the simplified singular kernel
U_{\bf s}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t}).

The first step is analogous to that adopted for regular integrals, since
g
is approximated with
\sigma_{g}
by using the same QI approach and then multiplied by
\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}.
Thus we can write

\displaystyle\int_{R_{\bf j}}{U}_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})g({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}\approx\int_{R_{\bf j}}{U}_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\sigma_{g}({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}={\bf v}^{\top}G_{\bf j}^{\top}(\hat{C}^{(p)})^{\top}{\bf g}\,,

(34)

where
{\bf g}
is the vector whose entries are the values of
g
used by the QI operator but now
{\bf v}
collects in a suitable order all the following integrals

\displaystyle\int_{R_{\bf j}}{U}_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})\hat{B}_{{\bf k},{\bf p}+{\bf d}}({\bf t})\ d{\bf t}\,,\qquad{\bf k}\in{\cal J}_{\pi_{\bf j}}.

(35)

Thus, in order to complete the definition of the quadrature rule, we are interested in deriving the exact expression of the above integrals (
35
). For this aim we introduce the following notation,

I^{q_{1},q_{2}}_{r_{1},r_{2}}(k_{1},k_{2}):=\int_{\tau^{(1)}_{k_{1}}}^{\tau^{(1)}_{k_{1}+r_{1}+1}}\int_{\tau^{(2)}_{k_{2}}}^{\tau^{(2)}_{k_{2}+r_{2}+1}}{U}_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})(t_{1}-s_{1})^{q_{1}}(t_{2}-s_{2})^{q_{2}}\hat{B}_{k_{1},r_{1}}(t_{1})\hat{B}_{k_{2},r_{2}}(t_{2})dt_{1}dt_{2}\,,

where
{\bf k}=(k_{1},k_{2})
and
\tau^{(\ell)}_{k_{\ell}+j},j=0,\ldots,r_{\ell},\ell=1,2
define the two univariate knot vectors active in the definition of
\hat{B}_{{\bf k},{\bf p}+{\bf d}},
where for brevity we have set
r_{\ell}:=p_{\ell}+d_{\ell}
, which is the univariate degree in the
\ell
-th direction of the product space. Then, using the tensor product extension of the Cox-de Boor recurrence relation for B-splines
[
3
]
,
we can express
I^{q_{1},q_{2}}_{r_{1},r_{2}}(k_{1},k_{2})
as a linear combination of
I^{q_{1}+m_{1},q_{2}+m_{2}}_{r_{1}-1,r_{2}-1}(k_{1}+w_{1},k_{2}+w_{2})
, where
m_{1},m_{2},w_{1},w_{2}=0,1
.
Starting with
q_{1}=q_{2}=0
and iterating this recurrence up to B-splines of degree 0 in both coordinate directions, the procedure can be completed, provided that the following
basic moments
have been preliminarily computed for
q_{\ell}
and
i_{\ell}
, respectively ranging in the set of indices
0,\ldots,p_{\ell}+r_{\ell}
and
k_{\ell},\ldots,k_{\ell}+r_{\ell},\ell=1,2,

\displaystyle I^{q_{1},q_{2}}_{0,0}(i_{1},i_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau^{(1)}_{i_{1}}}^{\tau^{(1)}_{i_{1}+1}}\int_{\tau^{(2)}_{i_{2}}}^{\tau^{(2)}_{i_{2}+1}}{U}_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})(t_{1}-s_{1})^{q_{1}}(t_{2}-s_{2})^{q_{2}}dt_{1}dt_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{s_{1}-\tau^{(1)}_{i_{1}+1}}^{s_{1}-\tau^{(1)}_{i_{1}}}\int_{s_{2}-\tau^{(2)}_{i_{2}+1}}^{s_{2}-\tau^{(2)}_{i_{2}}}{U}_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf z})(-z_{1})^{q_{1}}(-z_{2})^{q_{2}}dz_{1}dz_{2}.

For this aim we rely on the analytical expressions derived in
[
19
]
computed with the help of Wolfram Mathematica software.

We are left to derive the asymptotic accuracy of these rules with respect to the
\max\{H_{1,{\bf j}}\,,H_{2,{\bf j}}\}\rightarrow 0,
where, as in the previous subsection,
H_{i,{\bf j}}
denotes the size of the integration domain
R_{\bf j}
in the
i
-th coordinate direction. Now from (
34
) we can easily derive that the absolute value of the related quadrature error can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle\|g-\sigma_{g}\|_{\infty,R_{\bf j}}\ \int_{R_{\bf j}}|U_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})|\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}.

(36)

Using the triangle inequality
|U_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})|\leq|U({\bf s},{\bf t})|+|U_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})-U({\bf s},{\bf t})|
, we can bound the integral in (
36
) by a sum of two new integrals. Then, by applying Proposition
2
reported in the Appendix, we can estimate the first integral as

\displaystyle\ \int_{R_{\bf j}}|U({\bf s},{\bf t})|\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}\leq\ \int_{R_{\bf j}}|U({\bf s},{\bf t})|\,d{\bf t}\leq C_{2}\max\{H_{1},H_{2}\}.

(37)

The second integral involves a tail of the series expansion of
U({\bf s},\bullet)
about the source point
{\bf s}
. Referring to results in
[
19
]
, on this concern we can state that there exists a positive constant
C_{3}
involving the infinity norm of derivatives of
\tilde{\bf F}
of degree
\leq m
on
R_{\bf j}
, such that

\displaystyle\|U_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})-U({\bf s},{\bf t})\|_{\infty,R_{\bf j}}\leq C_{3}\frac{\left(\max\{H_{1,{\bf j}}\,,\,H_{2,{\bf j}}\}\right)^{m-1}}{m!},

hence

\displaystyle\int_{R_{\bf j}}|U_{{\bf s}}^{m}({\bf s}-{\bf t})-U({\bf s},{\bf t})|\hat{B}_{{\bf j},{\bf d}}({\bf t})\,d{\bf t}\leq C_{3}\frac{\left(\max\{H_{1,{\bf j}}\,,\,H_{2,{\bf j}}\}\right)^{m}}{m!(d_{1}+1)(d_{2}+1)}.

(38)

By combining these estimates with the bound in (
30
) for the QI error, we finally derive the following error bound for the singular integral in (
34
) with respect to size of
R_{\bf j}
,

\displaystyle C\max\{H_{1,{\bf j}}^{p_{1}+2},H_{2,{\bf j}}^{p_{2}+2}\},

where
C=C_{1}\max\{C_{2},C_{3}\}
and
C_{1}
contains norms of all the involved derivatives of
f=g-\sigma_{g}
in (
33
) for the largest considered
R_{\bf j}
. Instead, by fixing domain
R_{\bf j}
, the error bound with respect to
\min\{\nu_{1}-1,\nu_{2}-1\}
is equal to

\displaystyle\tilde{C}\max\{(\nu_{1}-1)^{-p_{1}-1},(\nu_{2}-1)^{-p_{2}-1}\},

where
\tilde{C}
contains a corresponding constant
\tilde{C}_{1}
to the one at the end of Section
4.3
, involving the function
f=g-\sigma_{g}
, multiplied by right-hand side estimates of (
37
) and (
38
).

4.5
The threshold for near singularity detection

The efficient and accurate evaluation of different types of integrals (
22
) appearing in the considered BIEs is crucial, see also
[
30
]
on such concern. For this aim in this subsection we are interested in determining a reasonable threshold
\delta
to be used in (
24
) to switch between nearly singular and regular integrals.
The goal is to apply the error bound (
33
) introduced in Section
4.3
to
f=gU,
in order to derive on each patch a mesh dependent definition of the threshold
\delta
to be used in (
24
).

By analyzing the nearly singular nature of the considered integral in (
22
), it is important to observe that the function
f:=gU({\bf s},\bullet)
for a fixed
{\bf s}=\tilde{\bf F}^{-1}(\tilde{\bf x})
includes a factor proportional to
1/\tilde{r}
, where
\tilde{r}=\|\tilde{\bf x}-\tilde{\bf F}(\bullet)\|_{2}
. Hence,
\|\partial^{i_{1},i_{2}}f\|_{\infty,R_{\bf j}}
includes a term
\tilde{r}_{\rm{min},{\bf j}}({\bf x})^{-i_{1}-i_{2}-1},
where
\tilde{r}_{\rm{min},{\bf j}}({\bf x})
is the minimal distance between
\tilde{\bf x}
and the physical integration domain image of
R_{\bf j}
via the geometry map
\tilde{\bf F},
see its definition in formula (
24
). Thus, considering the expression of the error bound in (
33
) for a fixed number of uniformly spaced quadrature nodes, we can infer a remarkable reduction of the convergence order with respect to
\max\{H_{1,{\bf j}},H_{2,{\bf j}}\}\rightarrow 0
if
\tilde{r}_{\rm{min},{\bf j}}({\bf x})
would reduce proportionally to it, due to increasing terms “
\|\partial^{i_{1},i_{2}}f\|_{\infty,R_{\bf j}}
”. Instead, by setting

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{{\rm min},{\bf j}}({\bf x})\geq\eta_{\bf j}:=\max\left\{H_{1,{\bf j}}^{\frac{1}{2(p_{1}+r_{1}+2)}}\,,\,H_{2,{\bf j}}^{\frac{1}{2(p_{2}+r_{2}+2)}}\right\}

this guarantees that for all the scaled integrals treated with the regular rule in the governing BIEs, the quadrature convergence order is at least equal to
5/2+\min\{p_{1}+r_{1},p_{2}+r_{2}\}.

In order to have just one threshold
\delta
for all the integrals involving the same geometry map, for the
k
-th patch we set

\displaystyle\delta=\delta_{k}:=\max_{{\bf j}\in{\cal I}_{k}}\eta_{\bf j}.

[

6
Conclusion

In this paper we address 3D Helmholtz problems using isogeometric BEMs and relying on conformal multi-patch smooth geometries and spline discretization spaces. The governing boundary integral equations are numerically approximated by a collocation scheme. The adopted quadrature rules for both regular and singular integrals are tailored for B-splines and they allow a profitable function-by-function matrix assembly. A procedure is proposed for the automatic detection of near singular integrals. Besides studying the approximation power of the considered rules, an arrangement with a singularity extraction technique is proposed, extending it to the multi-patch setting for the first time. Numerical results confirm the effectiveness of the method by achieving sufficient accuracy of the numerical solution with a small number of uniformly distributed quadrature nodes.

A possible future work includes a generalization of the proposed simulation model to more flexible non-conformal discretization spaces and parameterizations. Further testing of the proposed methodology in both the conformal and non-conformal setting for more general types of contacts between patches could be also taken into account.

Appendix

To provide a self-contained analysis of the developed integration procedures, a more technical part related to the behavior of the considered singular kernels is given in this appendix.

Let
\Gamma
be a regular surface admitting a
C^{2}
regular parameterization on
D:=[0\,,\,1]^{2}\,,\,\,{\bf F}:D\rightarrow\Gamma,\,\,{\bf t}=(t_{1},t_{2})\mapsto{\bf F}({\bf t})
.
Furthermore, for any fixed
{\bf s}\in D,
let us introduce the following notation,

{\bf r}_{\bf s}({\bf t}):={\bf F}({\bf t})-{\bf F}({\bf s})\,,\,\,r_{\bf s}({\bf t}):=\|{\bf r}_{\bf s}({\bf t})\|_{2}\,,\,\,\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}({\bf t}):=\frac{\partial{\bf F}}{\partial t_{1}}({\bf t})\times\frac{\partial{\bf F}}{\partial t_{2}}({\bf t})\,.

Proposition 1

For each fixed
{\bf s}\in D
and for any unit vector
{\bf u}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
such that
{\bf s}+\rho{\bf u}
is included in
D
for
\rho
positive and sufficiently small, it is

\lim_{\rho\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\rho}{r_{\bf s}({\bf s}+\rho{\bf u})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{E\cos^{2}\theta+2F\sin\theta\cos\theta+G\sin^{2}\theta}},

(42)

\lim_{\rho\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{{\bf r}_{\bf s}({\bf s}+\rho{\bf u})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}({\bf s}+\rho{\bf u})}{r^{2}({\bf s}+\rho{\bf u})}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{L\cos^{2}\theta+2M\sin\theta\cos\theta+N\sin^{2}\theta}{E\cos^{2}\theta+2F\sin\theta\cos\theta+G\sin^{2}\theta},

(43)

which are both finite limits, with
E,F,G
and
L,M,N
respectively denoting the coefficients of the first and second fundamental forms (with respect to the non-normalized normal
\nu
) of
{\bf F}
evaluated at
{\bf s}
,

\displaystyle E:=\frac{\partial{\bf F}}{\partial t_{1}}({\bf s})\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf F}}{\partial t_{1}}({\bf s}),

\displaystyle F:=\frac{\partial{\bf F}}{\partial t_{1}}({\bf s})\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf F}}{\partial t_{2}}({\bf s}),

\displaystyle G:=\frac{\partial{\bf F}}{\partial t_{2}}({\bf s})\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf F}}{\partial t_{2}}({\bf s}),

(44)

\displaystyle L:=\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}({\bf s})\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}{\bf F}}{\partial t_{1}^{2}}({\bf s}),

\displaystyle M:=\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}({\bf s})\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}{\bf F}}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}({\bf s}),

\displaystyle N:=\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}({\bf s})\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}{\bf F}}{\partial t_{2}^{2}}({\bf s}).

(45)

Proof.
Setting for brevity
{\bf F}_{i}:=\frac{\partial{\bf F}}{\partial t_{i}}({\bf s}),\,\,i=1,2
and
{\bf F}_{i,j}:=\frac{\partial^{2}{\bf F}}{\partial t_{i}\partial t_{j}}({\bf s}),\,i,j=1,2,
and using the short notation
{\bf r}={\bf r}({\bf s}+\rho{\bf u}),r=r({\bf s}+\rho{\bf u}),\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}({\bf s}+\rho{\bf u}),
we can write,

{\bf r}=\rho({\bf F}_{1}\cos\theta+{\bf F}_{2}\sin\theta)+\rho^{2}(\frac{1}{2}{\bf F}_{1,1}\cos^{2}\theta+{\bf F}_{1,2}\cos\theta\sin\theta+\frac{1}{2}{\bf F}_{2,2}\sin^{2}\theta)+O(\rho^{3})

and

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}&=[{\bf F}_{1}+\rho\big({\bf F}_{1,1}\cos\theta+{\bf F}_{1,2}\sin\theta\big)+O(\rho^{2})]\times[{\bf F}_{2}+\rho({\bf F}_{1,2}\cos\theta+{\bf F}_{2,2}\sin\theta)+O(\rho^{2})]=\cr&=({\bf F}_{1}\times{\bf F}_{2})+\rho[({\bf F}_{1}\times{\bf F}_{1,2}-{\bf F}_{2}\times{\bf F}_{1,1})\cos\theta+({\bf F}_{1}\times{\bf F}_{2,2}-{\bf F}_{2}\times{\bf F}_{1,2})\sin\theta]+O(\rho^{2})\,.\end{array}

Since
r^{2}=\rho^{2}(E\cos^{2}\theta+2F\sin\theta\cos\theta+G\sin^{2}\theta)+O(\rho^{3})\,,
we have,

\frac{\rho}{r}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{E\cos^{2}\theta+2F\sin\theta\cos\theta+G\sin^{2}\theta+O(\rho)}}\,,\quad{\bf r}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}=-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}\big(L\cos^{2}\theta+2M\sin\theta\cos\theta+N\sin^{2}\theta\big)+O(\rho^{3})\,.

In order to confirm that the two limits in (
42
) and (
43
) are always finite, we observe that their denominator can be respectively written as
\sqrt{{\bf u}^{T}M_{\bf s}{\bf u}}
and
{\bf u}^{T}M_{\bf s}{\bf u}\,,
with
M_{\bf s}
denoting the following symmetric
2\times 2
positive definite matrix associated to the first fundamental form of
{\bf F}
evaluated in
{\bf s},

M_{\bf s}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}E&F\cr F&G\end{array}\right)\,.

(46)

Now the proof is complete.

Note that the previous result implies that both the functions
{\bf t}\rightarrow\|{\bf t}-{\bf s}\|_{2}/r_{\bf s}({\bf t})
and
{\bf t}\rightarrow({\bf r}_{\bf s}({\bf t})\cdot\nu({\bf t}))/r_{\bf s}({\bf t})^{2}
are discontinuous at
{\bf t}={\bf s}.
Despite this, the following corollary allows one to verify their uniform boundedness in
D
.

Corollary 1

Under the assumptions of the previous proposition, both the functions
g_{1,{\bf s}}({\bf t}):=\|{\bf t}-{\bf s}\|_{2}/r_{\bf s}({\bf t})
and
g_{2,{\bf s}}({\bf t}):=({\bf r}_{\bf s}({\bf t})\cdot\nu({\bf t}))/r_{\bf s}^{2}({\bf t})
are bounded in their domain
D\setminus\{{\bf s}\},
uniformly with respect to
{\bf s}\in D.

Proof.
Since the proof is analogous for
g_{1,{\bf s}}
and
g_{2,{\bf s}},
let us refer only to
g_{1,{\bf s}}
.

Using proof by contradiction, we can assume that there exists a convergent sequence
\{({\bf s}_{k_{1}},{\bf t}_{k_{1}})\}
in
D^{2}
such that
g_{1,{\bf s}_{k_{1}}}({\bf t}_{k_{1}})\rightarrow+\infty
. Then, considering that
{\bf F}
is continuous and injective, it holds
\rho_{k_{1}}:=\|{\bf t}_{k_{1}}-{\bf s}_{k_{1}}\|_{2}\rightarrow 0
and the limit of the convergent sequence is equal to
({\bf s},{\bf s})
for some
{\bf s}\in D
. Now, setting
{\bf t}_{k_{1}}={\bf s}_{k_{1}}+\rho_{k_{1}}(\cos\theta_{k_{1}},\sin\theta_{k_{1}}),
and by considering that
\theta_{k_{1}}
varies in the periodic interval
[0,2\pi]
, we can find a subsequence
\{({\bf s}_{k_{2}},{\bf t}_{k_{2}})\}
of
\{({\bf s}_{k_{1}},{\bf t}_{k_{1}})\}
such that
\{\theta_{k_{2}}\}
tends to a certain angle
\theta
. Then, using arguments analogous to the ones used in the proof of the previous proposition, it can be proven that

\displaystyle\lim_{k_{2}\rightarrow\infty}g_{1,{\bf s}_{k_{2}}}({\bf t}_{k_{2}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{E\cos^{2}\theta+2F\sin\theta\cos\theta+G\sin^{2}\theta}},

(47)

where
E,F,G
are defined in (
44
).

Since the limit in (
47
) exists, we obtain a contradiction, since
g_{1,{\bf s}_{k_{3}}}({\bf t}_{k_{3}})\rightarrow+\infty
for every subsequence
\{({\bf s}_{k_{3}},{\bf t}_{k_{3}})\}
of sequence
\{({\bf s}_{k_{1}},{\bf t}_{k_{1}})\}
. Since
{\bf F}
is regular, we can bound
E,F,G
for all
{\bf s}\in D
and obtain a common upper bound for the limit (
47
) for all
{\bf s}\in D
.

The following last proposition is useful to estimate an upper bound for our quadrature rules for singular integrals.

Proposition 2

Let
R\subset D
be a rectangular integration domain with edges of dimensions
H_{1}
and
H_{2}.
Then there exists a constant
C
such that for each
{\bf s}\in R
it is

\int_{R}|U({\bf s},{\bf t})|d{\bf t}\,\,\leq C\max\{H_{1},H_{2}\}\,,

(48)

where
U
is one of the two kernels
U_{\rm SL}
and
U_{\rm DL}
defined in (
23
).

Proof.
First of all, setting
\rho_{\bf s}({\bf t}):=\|{\bf t}-{\bf s}\|_{2},
we observe that we can write
U_{\rm SL}({\bf s},{\bf t})=g_{1,{\bf s}}({\bf t})/\rho_{\bf s}({\bf t})
and

U_{\rm DL}({\bf s},{\bf t})=g_{1,{\bf s}}({\bf t})g_{2,{\bf s}}({\bf t})/\rho_{\bf s}({\bf t})\,,
where
g_{1,{\bf s}}
and
g_{2,{\bf s}}
are the two functions defined in the previous corollary. Thus we can derive from the corollary itself that, for both the considered definitions of
U,
there exists a positive constant
Q
such that

|U({\bf s},{\bf t})|\leq Q/\rho_{\bf s}({\bf t})\,,\,\,\forall({\bf s},{\bf t})\in D^{2},\,\,{\bf s}\neq{\bf t}\,.

Then, denoting with
{\cal A}_{\bf s}
the circle centered at
{\bf s}
with radius
\sqrt{2}\max\{H_{1},H_{2}\}
we have that
R\subset D\cap A_{\bf s}
which implies the following inequalities,

\int_{R}|U({\bf s},{\bf t})|d{\bf t}\,\,\leq\int_{D\cap{\cal A}_{\bf s}}|U({\bf s},{\bf t})|d{\bf t}\leq Q\int_{{\cal A}_{\bf s}}\frac{1}{\rho_{\bf s}({\bf t})}d{\bf t}\,.

Thus, using polar coordinates centered at
{\bf s}
the thesis easily descends.

The constant
C
in (
48
) depends on
{\bf F}
. By prescribing common bounds for coefficients in (
44
) and (
45
) for all “well-behaved” functions
{\bf F}
considered in practical applications, a common constant
C
can be sufficiently well estimated.
