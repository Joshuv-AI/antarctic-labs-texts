Multi-Agent Causal Discovery Using Large Language Models
Hao Duong Le, Xin Xia & Zhang Chen

Abstract
Causal discovery aims to identify causal relationships between variables and is a critical research area in machine learning. Traditional methods focus on statistical or machine learning algorithms to uncover causal links from structured data, often overlooking the valuable contextual information provided by metadata. Large language models (LLMs) have shown promise in creating unified causal discovery frameworks by incorporating both structured data and metadata. However, their potential in multi-agent settings remains largely unexplored. To address this gap, we introduce the Multi-Agent Causal Discovery Framework (MAC), which consists of two key modules: the Debate-Coding Module (DCM) and the Meta-Debate Module (MDM). The DCM begins with a multi-agent debating and coding process, where agents use both structured data and metadata to collaboratively select the most suitable statistical causal discovery (SCD) method. The selected SCD is then applied to the structured data to generate an initial causal graph. This causal graph is transformed into causal metadata through the Meta Fusion mechanism. With all the metadata, MDM then refines the causal structure by leveraging a multi-agent debating framework. Extensive experiments across five datasets demonstrate that MAC outperforms both traditional statistical causal discovery methods and existing LLM-based approaches, achieving state-of-the-art performance.

Understanding causal relationships between variables is fundamental across scientific fields. Statistical causal discovery (SCD) methods have been extensively researched to uncover these relationships. However, these methods face two significant challenges. First, they heavily rely on large volumes of structured data for accurate inference. In large-scale systems with thousands of variables (or nodes), obtaining sufficient structured data is often infeasible. Second, traditional SCD methods fail to leverage metadata—additional contextual information such as variable contexts, domain knowledge, and external factors—that can enhance the causal discovery process and improve inference accuracy.

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs), trained on vast datasets, has opened new avenues for addressing the challenges mentioned above. LLMs possess a wide range of knowledge, encompassing common sense, specialized domains, and advanced reasoning abilities
Wei et al. 2023
;
Rozière et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2023b
;
Yao et al. 2023a
. Leveraging LLMs for causal discovery has gained traction due to their ability to replicate expert knowledge in a cost-effective and accessible manner. Recent work
Kıcıman et al. 2023
;
Choi et al. 2022
;
Long et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024a
has explored LLM-based approaches for causal discovery, focusing on metadata and knowledge-driven reasoning similar to human experts. However, these methods largely treat LLMs as single-agent systems, which may limit their reasoning capabilities, especially when handling complex causal relationships or large-scale, dense causal graphs. In contrast, multi-agent LLM systems, where multiple models collaborate to collectively discover causal relationships, offer greater potential for tackling complex cases.

This paper introduces MAC (Multi-Agent Causal Discovery Framework), the first work that explores multi-agent LLMs for causal discovery. MAC integrates two core components:

•

Meta-Debate Module (MDM)
: A structured, multi-agent debate framework for metadata causal analysis. It has three specialized LLM-based agents—a Causal Affirmative Debater, a Causal Negative Debater, and a Causal Judge—iteratively refine causal graphs through adversarial reasoning.

•

Debate-Coding Module (DCM)
: A statistical execution module for structure data causal analysis. It first leverages the agent’s ability to select a suitable SCD method and then executes this method on structured data for causal relationship learning.

In particular, DCM is first implemented. It embeds an MDM inside to use metadata and a subset of structured data to select the most suitable statistical causal discovery (SCD) method. The selected SCD is further applied to the structured data to learn the causal relationships. Then, the learned causal relationships are transferred into new causal metadata through a Meta Fusion mechanism. Last, using this causal metadata and other available metadata as input, MDM is implemented to further refine and optimize the causal relationships. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.

Causality Analysis: MAC addresses a critical gap in causal discovery by integrating statistical methods with multi-agent LLM reasoning. This approach ensures both algorithmic precision and expert-level interpretability, enabling robust learning and refinement of causal structures.

2.

Multi-Agent LLM: MAC enhances the faithfulness and interoperability of multi-agent LLM systems through role-playing and agent debates. Notably, the number of debating rounds required is minimal, with just a few rounds proving sufficient for effective refinement.

3.

General Machine Learning: This work demonstrates that incorporating metadata can significantly improve performance across various machine learning tasks.

Related Works

Statistical causal discovery has become a significant area of research, with various algorithms developed to tackle different types of variables and causal relationships. These methods can be broadly categorized into three main approaches: constraint-based, score-based, and functional causal models
Glymour et al. 2019
. Constraint-based algorithms, such as PC
Spirtes et al. 2000
and FCI
Spirtes et al. 2013
, rely on conditional independence tests to identify causal relationships. The PC algorithm assumes no confounders, while FCI can handle confounders and provides accurate results. These methods are widely applicable, as they can handle various data distributions and causal relationships. Another notable approach is Greedy Equivalence Search (GES)
Huang et al. 2018
. More recently, algorithms based on functional causal models have been developed to distinguish between different causal relationships within the same equivalence class. For example, the DirectLiNGAM algorithm can leverage prior knowledge of causal ordering, as demonstrated in the "LiNGAM" project
Shimizu et al. 2011
.

In recent years, knowledge-driven approaches using large language models (LLMs) have gained attention in causal discovery, though this is still a relatively new research area. Some works have explored using LLMs for causal inference through prompt-based learning, where LLMs are queried in a pairwise fashion about the causal relationships between variables
Kıcıman et al. 2023
;
Zečević et al. 2023
. To increase efficiency,
Jiralerspong et al. 2024
employs a breadth-first search (BFS) approach, minimizing the number of queries to be linear with respect to the number of nodes. Additionally,
Chen et al. 2024a
introduces various prompting techniques, including in-context learning (ICL), zero-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT), adversarial prompts, manual CoT, and explicit functions (e.g., encouraging language in prompts) for causal discovery. These methods primarily rely on metadata for inference. Further integration of LLMs with SCD approaches with both metadata and structured data has been explored by
Ban et al. 2023
and
Vashishtha et al. 2023
. However, no existing work has fully explored LLM-agentic workflows for causal graph discovery. We summarize the current state of causal discovery methods using LLMs in Table

3.1
Problem Definition

Suppose the dataset
D
has
V=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\}

X_{i},i=1,\ldots,n

G=(V,E)
, where
E\subseteq V\times V
denotes directed and weighted adjacency matrix, capturing causal relationships between variables. The objective is to learn
E
. To achieve this, various sources of dataset information are incorporated, including structured data and additional metadata.

•

O
represents the structured data, which is an
n\times d
matrix.
d
is the number of observations for each variable.

•

I
represents the metadata, such as additional contextual information about the dataset, expert knowledge about causal factors in the particular field, etc.

•

S
represents a small subset of
O

3.2
Meta-Debate Module (MDM)

MDM employs a structured adversarial framework for causal discovery from metadata, designed to iteratively refine causal graphs by leveraging the distinct roles and perspectives of three specialized agents: the
Causal Affirmative Debater
, the
Causal Negative Debater
, and the
Causal Judge
. This framework fosters multi-agent interactions to identify, critique, and assess causal relationships, ultimately converging on a robust and refined solution. The architecture and functionality of MDM are illustrated in Figure
1
.

Causal Affirmative Debater (
A
):
The Affirmative Debater constructs the initial DAG
G_{A}=(V,E_{A})
, where
E_{A}\subseteq V\times V
. Using the dataset
D
, particularly its metadata
I
, it identifies causal relationships by adhering to causal inference principles, such as temporal ordering, and accounting for potential confounders. For example, given variables
A,B,C
, the Affirmative Debater might propose
A\to B\to C
, asserting that
A
affects
B
, which in turn affects
C
. The purpose of this Causal Affirmative Debater is to establish a strong baseline by constructing the DAG.

Causal Negative Debater (
N
):
The Negative Debater critiques the graph
G_{A}
, identifying potential flaws such as unaccounted confounders, incorrect temporal ordering, or omitted variables. Based on this critique, the Negative Debater revises the graph, producing
G_{N}=(V,E_{N})
, where
E_{N}\subseteq V\times V
. For instance, if
G_{A}
suggests
A\to B
, the Negative Debater may argue that
A
and
B
are both influenced by a common confounder
C
, revising the graph to
C\to A
and
C\to B
. The Negative Debater introduces alternative perspectives by addressing potential flaws and proposing refinements.

Causal Judge (
J
):

The Judge adjudicates the competing graphs
G_{A}
and
G_{N}
, comparing their logical consistency, adherence to causal inference principles, and alignment with observed data. The Judge selects the most plausible graph as the final output, ensuring that the selected graph represents the best possible explanation of the dataset. The Judge ensures logical rigor and statistical validity, selecting the most plausible graph based on predefined criteria.

3.3
Debate-Coding Module (DCM)

DCM utilizes SCD algorithms for precise causal discovery through a structured two-phase process. It involves four agents, organized into two phases: debating the algorithm and executing the algorithm.

In the second phase, a
Causal Coding Executor

3.4
MAC: Multi-Agent Causal Discovery Framework

The MAC framework integrates two modules. DCM first constructs an initial, data-driven causal graph. The
Meta Fusion
module then extracts additional metadata from this graph, which is used to further refine the causal structure with expert knowledge from LLM.

DCM Constructs the Initial Graph
: Initially, DCM constructs a causal graph based on the provided dataset. This graph captures the relationships between variables in a causal framework.

Meta Fusion
: Next, the initial causal graph is transformed into a set of edges, which represent the causal relationships and orderings between the variables. This set of edges serves as additional metadata, which is subsequently input into the MDM module. For example, consider the following
4\times 4
binary matrix representing causal relationships between four nodes: "Hot Weather", "Ice Cream Sales", "People Swimming", and "Shark Attack". The learned initial causal graph
G_{C}
would be as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}&\text{Hot Weather}&\text{Ice Cream Sells}&\text{People Swimming}&\text{Shark Attack}\\
\hline\cr\text{Hot Weather}&0&1&1&0\\
\text{Ice Cream Sells}&0&0&0&0\\
\text{People Swimming}&0&0&0&1\\
\text{Shark Attack}&0&0&0&0\\
\end{array}

Then the Meta Fusion process converts this matrix into the following set of edges, we call causal metadata (CMD):

•

(Hot Weather
\rightarrow
Ice Cream Sells)

•

(Hot Weather
\rightarrow
People Swimming)

•

(People Swimming
\rightarrow
Shark Attack)

CMD captures the causal relationships and causal order and is ready to be processed by the MDM module.

MDM Refinement
: Finally, the newly constructed CMD is passed to MDM, where it utilizes the foundational knowledge of the LLM and the multi-agent debate framework to uncover the final causal graph.

4.1
Experimental Setup

To answer
R1
, we design our experiments to evaluate the performance of MAC across multiple datasets, comparing it against well-established baselines.

Datasets:
We conduct experiments using five well-known datasets to assess the robustness and generalizability of the MAC framework. These datasets span a range of domains:

•

Auto

Quinlan 1993
: The Auto dataset was originally made available in the UCI Machine Learning Repository and has been widely used as a benchmark for causal inference. This dataset includes various variables related to car fuel consumption, and we select five key variables: “Weight,” “Displacement,” “Horsepower,” “Acceleration,” and “MPG” (miles per gallon). It consists of 392 continuous observations.

•

Child

Spiegelhalter 1992
: The Child causal graph is a moderately sized model that focuses on congenital heart disease in newborns. Its ground-truth graph consists of 20 nodes and 25 edges. Key variables in this graph include Birth Asphyxia, Lung Flow, and Chest X-Ray. It includes 10,000 integer observations.

•

Cancer

Korb and Nicholson 2010
: This dataset models medical diagnostics, specifically in the context of metastatic cancer. It consists of 5 nodes and 4 edges, with key variables such as Pollution, Cancer, Smoker, X-ray, and Dyspnoea. It includes 10,000 binary observations.

•

Earthquake

Korb and Nicholson 2010
: A basic Bayesian network designed for earthquake detection, containing 5 nodes and 4 edges. The variables in this network include Burglary, Alarm, Earthquake, JohnCalls, and MaryCalls. It includes 10,000 binary observations.

•

Survey

Scutari and Denis 2021
: This dataset is used to model causal relationships between various demographic and behavioral variables. It includes 6 variables: Age, Education, Sex, Occupation, Residence, and Travel, with a total of 6 edges. It includes 10,000 integer observations.

5
Conclusion

We introduce MAC, the first multi-agent LLM framework for causal discovery by utilizing both structured data and metadata. MAC consists of two key modules: the Debate-Coding Module (DCM) and the Meta-Debate Module (MDM).
DCM begins with a debating and coding process. In the debating step, the agents collaboratively select the most suitable statistical causal discovery (SCD) method for causal analysis. In the coding step, the selected SCD is applied to the structured data to generate the causal structure. Next, the Meta Fusion mechanism transforms the causal structure into causal metadata. Using this metadata, along with other available metadata, MDM employs a multi-agent debating framework to further refine and optimize the causal structure. Extensive evaluations across five datasets demonstrate that MAC consistently outperforms traditional SCD methods and LLM-based methods.

6
Limitations

While MAC significantly improves causal discovery, it still has several limitations. First, it relies solely on observational data, lacking mechanisms for interventional validation, which is crucial in domains like medicine, economics, and biology.
Second, computational overhead is a major challenge for MAC, since the multi-agent debating process requires high processing power, making its scalability and accessibility difficult. Future improvements could include optimizing the debate process, leveraging smaller open-source models, or integrating statistical pre-processing steps to reduce computational costs.

Appendix A
Prompting of Causal Agent

The prompts for the Meta-Debate Module (MDM) agents are meticulously crafted to guide their respective roles in the causal discovery process with clarity and precision. Each prompt begins with a
Role Definition
, explicitly assigning the agent’s task within the framework. For example, the Affirmative Debater is tasked with acting as an expert in causality and proposing an initial causal graph based on the given dataset. Conversely, the Negative Debater is directed to critically evaluate this graph by identifying flaws and offering alternative perspectives. For instance, if the Affirmative Debater proposes a graph where
A\to B
, the Negative Debater might argue that
A
and
B
are only correlated due to a common confounder
C
and propose an alternative graph with
C\to A
and
C\to B
. The judge is explicitly instructed to act as a neutral evaluator, focusing solely on assessing the factual and logical rigor of the graphs, and deciding the most plausible causal structure. This explicit role assignment is crucial, as experiments revealed that agents sometimes deviate from their roles. For instance, the judge might inadvertently engage in debating rather than making the final decision, which necessitated clearer role prompts.

The
Context
section situates the agents within a competitive debate format, emphasizing the dynamics of proposing, critiquing, and evaluating causal graphs. The Affirmative Debater’s objective is to construct a robust causal graph based on the dataset, while the Negative Debater critically assesses this graph, proposing refinements or alternatives. For example, if the Affirmative Debater presents a graph with a direct edge
D\to E
, the Negative Debater might challenge this by suggesting that
D\to E
could be part of a causal chain, proposing instead
D\to F\to E
. Meanwhile, the judge evaluates these arguments based on factual accuracy and logical coherence, considering the evidence presented by the debaters to finalize the decision.

The
Causal Prompting
section outlines essential principles and provides examples to guide the agents in their reasoning. Agents are first instructed to assess whether a direct causal relationship exists while accounting for confounders. For example, if the dataset suggests
X_{1}\to X_{2}
, agents must evaluate whether
X_{3}
influences both
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, providing an alternative explanation such as
X_{3}\to X_{1}
and
X_{3}\to X_{2}
. Second, agents are required to ensure the correct temporal order of variables. For instance, if
Y
is observed before
Z
, the edge
Z\to Y
would violate temporal order and must be corrected to
Y\to Z
. Third, agents are tasked with providing clear explanations of the causal relationships they identify, enabling other agents to understand the rationale behind their proposals. Fourth, proposed graphs must adhere to the principle of acyclicity (DAGs), avoiding circular dependencies. For example, a graph with
G\to H\to I\to G
would be invalid and require restructuring. Finally, when crafting prompts, agents often overlook confounders. Therefore, agents are reminded not to omit variables, particularly confounders that might create indirect pathways. For example, instead of directly linking
A\to C
, agents must consider whether a pathway like
A\to B\to C
better explains the data.

These structured prompts, enriched with examples and clearly defined roles, ensure that the agents adhere to rigorous causal discovery principles. They foster a competitive yet collaborative dynamic that enables the Meta-Debate Module to iteratively refine causal graphs while maintaining logical and statistical rigor. Appendix B
More Related Works

B.1
Prompt Engineering

Several advanced techniques for leveraging large language models (LLMs) have been identified. Zero-shot prompting, introduced by
Radford et al. 2019
, guides LLMs to perform novel tasks using carefully crafted prompts without the need for training data, allowing the model to leverage its existing knowledge to generate predictions. Few-shot prompting, as described by
Brown et al. 2020
, enhances model performance by providing a few input-output examples, though it requires more tokens and careful example selection to mitigate biases. For reasoning and logic,
Wei et al. 2023
introduced Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, which guides LLMs through step-by-step reasoning processes, significantly improving accuracy in complex tasks such as math and commonsense reasoning. Building on this,
Wang et al. 2023
proposed self-consistency, a strategy that generates diverse reasoning chains and identifies the most consistent final answer, further enhancing accuracy. Additionally,
Yao et al. 2023c
developed ReAct, enabling LLMs to generate reasoning traces and task-specific actions concurrently, thereby improving performance in question answering, fact verification, and interactive decision-making by enhancing the synergy between reasoning and action. In this work, we design the prompt to adhere to key principles, ensuring the correct temporal order of variables, identifying potential confounders, and maintaining an acyclic structure.

B.2
LLMs’ Agentic Workflow

A general LLM agent framework consists of core components: user request, agent/brain, planning, memory, and tools. The agent/brain acts as the main coordinator, activated by a prompt template. It can be profiled with specific details to define its role, using handcrafted, LLM-generated, or data-driven strategies. Planning employs techniques like Chain of Thought and Tree of Thoughts, and for complex tasks, feedback mechanisms like ReAct
Yao et al. 2023c
and Reflexion
Shinn et al. 2023
refine plans based on past actions and observations. Memory stores the agent’s logs, with short-term memory for the current context and long-term memory for past behaviors. Hybrid memory combines both to enhance reasoning and experience accumulation. Tools enable interaction with external environments, such as APIs and code interpreters. Frameworks like MRKL
Karpas et al. 2022
, Toolformer
Schick et al. 2023
, Function Calling
OpenAI 2024
, and HuggingGPT
Shen et al. 2023
integrate tools to solve tasks effectively.

However, for more complex problems where a single LLM agent may struggle, LLM-MA (multi-agent) systems excel. Current LLM-MA systems primarily employ three communication paradigms: Cooperative, Competitive, and Debating. In the Cooperative paradigm, agents collaborate towards a shared goal, typically exchanging information to enhance a collective solution
Qian et al. 2023
,
Chen et al. 2024b
. In the Competitive paradigm, agents work towards their own goals, which might conflict with those of other agents
Zhao et al. 2023a
. The Debating paradigm involves agents engaging in argumentative interactions, where they present and defend their viewpoints or solutions while critiquing those of others. This approach is ideal for reaching a consensus or a more refined solution
Li et al. 2023
;
Liang et al. 2023
;
Xiong et al. 2023
. In this work, the debating paradigm will be implemented, as the nature of causal discovery problems requires diverse and potentially conflicting opinions to approach the truth.

B.3
Statistical and LLM-based Causal Methods

Traditional methods of statistical causal inference often depend heavily on assumed causal graphs to identify and measure causal impacts. To overcome this limitation, data-driven algorithmic approaches have been developed into statistical causal discovery (SCD) methods, encompassing both non-parametric (e.g.,
Spirtes et al. 2000
;
Yuan and Malone 2013
;
Huang et al. 2018
;
Xie et al. 2020
) and semi-parametric (e.g.,
Shimizu et al. 2006
;
Hoyer et al. 2009
;
Shimizu et al. 2011
) techniques. Many SCD algorithms can be systematically augmented with background knowledge and have accessible software packages. For example, the non-parametric and constraint-based Peter-Clerk (PC) algorithm
Spirtes et al. 2000
in “causal-learn” integrates background knowledge of mandatory or forbidden directed edges. “Causal-learn” also includes the Exact Search algorithm
Shimizu et al. 2011
;
Yuan and Malone 2013
, a non-parametric and score-based SCD method that can incorporate background knowledge in the form of a super structure matrix of forbidden directed edges. Furthermore, the semi-parametric DirectLiNGAM
Shimizu et al. 2011
algorithm can use prior knowledge of causal order
Inazumi et al. 2010
in the “LiNGAM” project
Ikeuchi et al. 2023
.

In the context of knowledge-driven approaches using large language models (LLMs), applying LLMs for causal inference is relatively new. There have been a few significant efforts to use LLMs for causal inference among variables by merely prompting with the variable names, without going through the traditional SCD process with benchmark datasets
Kıcıman et al. 2023
;
Zečević et al. 2023
.
Jiralerspong et al. 2024
even uses a breadth-first search (BFS) approach which allows it to use only a linear number of queries to have a higher efficiency. Additionally,
Chen et al. 2024a
proposes 9 prompting techniques comprising ICL, 0-shot CoT, adversarial prompt, manual CoT, and explicit function (e.g. using encouraging language in prompts). Moreover, the works of
Ban et al. 2023
and
Vashishtha et al. 2023
introduce interesting approaches by integrating LLMs into traditional data-driven approaches. However, most of the above-mentioned works have not investigated the LLM-agentic work-flows for causal graph discovery (Table
1
), which indeed requires heavy investigation on various models and graphs’ scales.
