New and old Saito-Kurokawa lifts classically via L 2 L^{2} norms and bounds on their supnorms: level aspect
Pramath Anamby, Soumya Das

Abstract
. In the first half of the paper, we lay down a classical approach to the study of Saito-Kurokawa (SK) lifts of (Hecke congruence) square-free level, including the allied new-oldform theory. Our treatment of this relies on a novel idea of computing ranks of certain matrices whose entries are L 2 L^{2} -norms of eigenforms. For computing the L 2 L^{2} norms we work with the Hecke algebra of GSp ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{GSp}(2) . In the second half, we formulate precise conjectures on the L ∞ L^{\infty} size of the space of SK lifts of square-free level, measured by the supremum of its Bergman kernel, and prove bounds towards them using the results from the first half. Here we rely on counting points on lattices, and on the geometric side of the Bergman kernels of spaces of Jacobi forms underlying the SK lifts. Along the way, we prove a non-trivial bound for the sup-norm of a Jacobi newform of square-free level and also discuss about their size on average.

1.
Introduction

One of the primary objectives of this paper is to initiate
a study of the classical sup-norm problem in the context of holomorphic Siegel cusp forms of degree
2
(denoted as
S_{k}^{2}(N)
) in the level aspect.
The sup-norm problem is in a nascent stage for higher rank groups – especially for the symplectic groups, which are the objects of interest here – and only recently some results have been obtained: viz. Das-Krishna
[
19
]
, Kramer-Mandal
[
40
]
, Saha et al
[
18
]
in the scalar weight aspect, or the restriction-to-compact setting in the Laplace eigenvalue aspect by Blomer-Pohl
[
11
]
. It is well known that automorphic forms on higher rank groups, which do not have global Whittaker models, like the case of holomorphic Siegel modular forms, pose significant obstacles to analytical questions. There are two distinct aspects of the problem: one is to obtain the correct
L^{\infty}
-size of the space (referred to as ‘size’ henceforth) measured by the supremum of its Bergman kernel (BK for short); and second, to obtain a good bound on the size of a single eigenform. We briefly discuss these topics below in the current context.

As an initial investigation, in this paper we focus our attention on the subspace of Saito-Kurokawa lifts (SK lifts in short) of (Hecke congruence) level
N
which is a subspace of
S^{(2)}_{k}(N)
obtained as functorial lifting from the space of holomorphic elliptic cusp forms, denoted by
S_{k}(N)
. We denote the space of the SK lifts by
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
.
See the works of R. Schmidt
[
51
]
for a representation-theoretic treatment where they arise via CAP representations, and that of Eichler-Zagier
[
22
]
and T. Ibukiyama
[
28
]
for a classical construction. Both approaches will play a role in this paper, but the classical setting will be predominant. Even though it is helpful that SK lifts are related to
\mathrm{GL}(2)

objects; but in the context of sup-norm questions, some of the standard tools that are needed (viz. the geometric side of the Bergman kernel), are not available at our disposal in a useful way. In this way, the problem is nicely balanced.

Also as far as we are aware, nothing is known about the correct size of
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
. Our first task in this direction is then to formulate a conjecture about this and test it over certain subspaces. We easily get a global lower bound (viz.
N^{-2}
), and can check that at least the same appears as an upper bound for the oldspace. Even this requires some work, see sections
11
and
12.1.1
.

With the above remarks in view, the primary objectives of this paper are twofold:

(i)

To describe the new-oldform theory in the space of SK lifts in the classical language for square-free levels, which is interesting in its own right, and

(ii)

Use this description to obtain bounds towards the sizes of various objects related to SK lifts.

Throughout this paper, we call

1.2.
Maaß relations characterize EZI lifts

From Figure 2 it is evident that not all SK lifts are EZI lifts. This leads us to wonder if one can characterize the EZI lifts inside the space of SK lifts uniquely. It is quite pleasant that the answer turns out to be the so-called Maaß relations, first considered by H. Maaß in his proof of the Maaß conjectures (see
[
44
]
,
[

]
). In our setting, it is as follows. Let
G\in S^{(2)}_{k}(N)

G
satisfies the Maaß relations if for all
T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}n&r/2\\
r/2&m\end{smallmatrix}\right)
(cf.
[
28

)

(1.1)

A_{G}(T):=A_{G}(n,r,m)=\sum_{d|(n,r,m),(d,N)=1}d^{k-1}A_{G}(nm/d^{2},r/d,1).

Then in Theorem
4.10

1.1
). The proof depends on the description of oldforms discussed above. Let us mention here the related work of B. Heim

1.3.
The matrix
M_{p}
that controls SK lifts

We now say a few words about our treatment of (i). Usually, the classical treatment of new-oldform theory, say for elliptic modular forms, goes via their Fourier expansion á la Atkin-Lehner
[
8
]
. However in the present situation, especially for the oldspace, this entails knowledge of Fourier expansions at various cusps. For instance, one has to look at the Fourier expansion of
F|U_{S}(p)W_{p}
with
F
being of level
N
and
p\nmid N
– which may not be easy to obtain. See Remark
4.6
for more on this point. To get around this, we consider instead an approach based solely on
L^{2}
norms – this forms the backbone of the part (i) discussed above. Thus this method works only for cusp forms, but for the Eisenstein series, we believe a direct Fourier expansion-based approach should work. To describe this idea, we need a bit of notation.

Throughout this paper
k
is even and all of our Petersson norms are not normalized by volume (cf. (
2.3
)).

As will be discussed in detail in Section
4
, there are four types of operators, denoted as
R_{i}=R_{i}(p)
,
i=1,\ldots,4
with
R_{1}=\mathrm{Id.},R_{2}=U_{S}(p),R_{3}=W_{p},R_{4}=U_{S}(p)W_{p}
, via which oldforms arise in level
Np
from level
N
, where
p\nmid N
– see subsection
4.2
. Put
F_{j,p}:=F|R_{j}(p)
.
With this data,
we associate a matrix
M_{p}
, (which we call the ‘inner-product’ matrix) as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

(1.2)

M_{p}=M_{p}(F):=\left(\langle F_{i,p},F_{j,p}\rangle_{Np}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq 4}.

In fact, the new-oldform theory would follow from the following characterization of the space of SK lifts in terms of the
4\times 4
matrix
M_{p}
, which therefore plays a central role.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
N\geq 1
be square-free and
F\in S^{(2)}_{k}(N)
be a eigenform with eigenvalues
\lambda_{F}(n)
for
(n,N)=1
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

F
is an SK lift of a elliptic eigenform of level
N
and weight
2k-2
.

(2)

The inner product matrix
M_{p}
has rank
3
for some prime
p\nmid N
.

(3)

There exists a prime
p\nmid N
, such that
\displaystyle\lambda_{F}(p^{2})=\lambda_{F}(p)^{2}-(p^{k-1}+p^{k-2})\lambda_{F}(p)+p^{2k-2}
.

Let us mention here that Theorem
1.2
(3) was known in level one from
[
48
]
,
[
23
]
, whereas we mainly work with (2). From Theorem
1.2
we can obtain an explicit orthonormal basis for the oldspace since we can show that the leading
3\times 3
minor of
M_{p}
is non-zero. Thus
\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\}
is a basis for the old-class of
F
.
This then enables us to study its
L^{\infty}
size.
Further, one needs to suitably choose a
W_{p}
invariant orthonormal basis from this set.
The
W_{p}
invariance helps in localizing the study of the sup-norm problem in suitable regions.
Also a byproduct of Theorem
1.2
, we see that for non- SK lifts, the oldclass of an eigenform
F
must be
4
dimensional, and is spanned by
F_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,4
. This is in conformity with the investigations in
[
50
]
.

1.4.
Computations in the Hecke algebra

In order to obtain Theorem
1.2
for the treatment of new-oldform theory, we need extensive information about various inner product relations. This is the content of Section
3
where we express the entries
\displaystyle\langle F_{i,p},F_{j,p}\rangle_{Np}
of
M_{p}
as

(1.3)

\displaystyle\langle F_{i,p},F_{j,p}\rangle_{Np}=P(\lambda_{F}(p),\lambda_{F}(p^{2}))\cdot\langle F,F\rangle_{Np},

where
P
is a polynomial in
\mathbf{Q}[x,y]
. This leads us to the discussion about obtaining such relations. A by-now standard method (which was our initial approach) is to use the Kohnen-Skoruppa Dirichlet series
\displaystyle D(F,G,s)=\sum\nolimits_{m\geq 1}\langle\phi_{F,m},\phi_{G,m}\rangle m^{-s}
, where
\phi_{F,m}
denotes the
m
-th Fourier-Jacobi coefficient of
F
. The idea is to compare the residues of this series at
s=k
in two ways.
Indeed, for a newform
F
, this is the approach taken in
[
2
]
. However, there seem to be some inaccuracies in this paper – this will be discussed in some detail in Section
7
. Most importantly, we just mention here that the method loc. cit. works
only if
at least one of the components is a newform, and
not
otherwise. Since we have to work with oldforms also, we have to take a different route.

Namely, we work with the operators
R_{j}
introduced above and related objects viewed inside the Hecke algebra over
\mathbf{Q}
for
\Gamma^{(2)}_{0}(N)
and thus most of our calculations here are algebraic. Even though we sometimes have used a computer for some of the computations in this part (codes available); we feel it is quite remarkable that the Hecke relations in Theorem
1.2
(3) precisely show up in the factorization of the intricate expression of
\det(M_{p})
etc. In a sense, this also serves as a cross-check for all of our inner product calculations.
Some of the calculations in the Appendix to
[
11
]
are quite useful to us here.

As final remarks, let us mention that our method can be adapted to study various problems about
S_{k}^{(2)}(N)
; for instance, it seems likely that one can reprove Böcherer’s result that
U_{S}(p)
is injective on
S_{k}^{(2)}(N)
for all
p|N
(
N
square-free, cf.
[
12
]
) by this method. Moreover, this approach is expected to work for other spaces, e.g., Hermitian modular forms, etc.

1.5.
Some background on the sup-norm problem in the level aspect

The second part of the paper depends on (i) and addresses the sup-norm questions for various spaces related to
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
mentioned in (ii) above. We begin with a brief discussion of the problem in degree
1
on average.

Let
S_{k}(N)
denote the space of holomorphic cusp forms of weight
k
and level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
. Denote by
\mathcal{B}_{k}(N)
an orthonormal basis for
S_{k}(N)
. Then the Bergman kernel for the space
S_{k}(N)
is given by

(1.4)

\mathbb{B}_{k,N}(\tau):=\sum\nolimits_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{k}(N)}v^{k}|f(\tau)|^{2}.

The size of the space
S_{k}(N)
is then measured by

(1.5)

\displaystyle\sup(S_{k}(N)):=\sup\nolimits_{\tau\in\mathbf{H}}\mathbb{B}_{k,N}(\tau).

From the work of Michel-Ullmo
[
46
]
and later by Kramer-Jorgensson
[
31
]
it is known that for
k=2
and square-free
N
, the bounds
\sup(S_{2}(N))\ll\tau_{5}(N)\log N
and
\ll 1
respectively, hold. Whereas Michel-Ullmo use the spectral Large sieve, Kramer-Jorgensson use the heat kernel method to arrive at the above bounds. Steiner has shown for
k>2
the hybrid result
\sup(S_{k}(N))\ll_{A}1+\nu_{N}\,k^{-A}
for any
A>0
. Here
\nu_{N}\ll N\log N
denotes the index of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
. From this, it is not clear whether one gets a bound
O_{k}(1)
(which is expected), as the implied constant depends on
A
and
k
is fixed. However, we note that Steiner’s result is quite general—valid for all real weights and congruence subgroups.

In an appendix to this paper – see the Introduction, subsection
1.11
and Section
13
– we will prove in particular the bound
\displaystyle\sup(S_{\kappa}(N))\asymp_{k}1
e.g., for all
N
square-free and for all
\kappa\geq 5/2
, which could be half-integral.

1.6.
Conjectures and results on sup-norms

In the remaining part of the introduction, we will discuss our results about the sizes of various spaces that are intertwined with SK lifts. First, we discuss about Jacobi forms, whose sizes have a direct bearing on that of SK lifts, apart from their interest.

1.7.
Size of Jacobi forms of index 1

Let
\phi\in J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
, space of Jacobi forms of index
1
and level
N
. It is well-known that such Jacobi forms can be uniquely expressed as

(1.6)

\displaystyle\phi=h_{0}(\tau)\theta_{0}(\tau,z)+h_{1}(\tau)\theta_{1}(\tau,z),

called the theta decomposition, and both
h_{0},h_{1}
are determined by a single
h\in S_{k-1/2}(4N)
– in fact the assignment
\phi\leftrightarrow h
is a Hecke equivariant isomorphism. Here
\theta_{j}
are certain Jacobi theta series, see subsection
6.1
.
This provides the link to pass to half-integral weights, but there is one catch. Namely from (
1.6
) one can expect that, say when
v\approx N^{-1}
, (with
\widetilde{\phi}
being the invariant function related
\phi
)

(1.7)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\phi}\widetilde{\phi}(\tau,z)^{2}\approx\mathbb{B}_{k-1/2,N}(\tau)\cdot v^{1/2}(|\theta_{0}|^{2}+|\theta_{1}|^{2})\ll 1\cdot v^{-1/2}\ll N^{1/2},

since the theta series behave like
v^{-1/2}
for small
v
. This extra contribution from the theta series is the “catch” mentioned above, without which one can just get the bound
O_{k}(1)
confirming the Conjecture
1.4
below.
Technicalities aside, we indeed prove that the bound in (
1.7
) is true.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
k\geq 4
be even and
N
square-free. Then,

(1.8)

\displaystyle 1\ll\sup(J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N))\ll N^{1/2}.

Given the lower bound
1
it seems reasonable to expect that the size of
J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
is
1
, but we can not corroborate this at the moment.

Conjecture 1.4
.

Let
k\geq 4
be even. Then for large
N
, one has
\displaystyle\sup(J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N))\asymp 1
.

1.8.
Non-trivial bound for a Jacobi newform

However, quite interestingly, we are able to prove a non-trivial bound for the sup-norm of an individual Jacobi form, relying upon the corresponding result for half-integral weights by
[
33
]
. The ‘trivial bound’ is
O_{k}(1)
, as follows from Conjecture
1.4
. Alternatively, we can refer to Proposition
10.1
for the bound
O(N^{\epsilon})
, which we also call the trivial bound.
Here also we encounter the “catch” mentioned around (
1.7
), and have to work to overcome it. We prove the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
N
be an odd, square-free integer and
\phi\in J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
be an
L^{2}
-normalized eigenform of all Hecke operators away from the level
N
. Then

(1.9)

\sup\nolimits_{(\tau,z)\in\mathbf{H}\times\mathbf{C}}\widetilde{\phi}(\tau,z)\ll_{k,\epsilon}N^{-1/36+\epsilon}.

The main point behind the success (see subsection
6.1.4
) here is that even though one obtains an ‘extra’
v^{1/2}
from
\displaystyle v^{k}|\phi|^{2}\approx v^{k-1/2}|h|^{2}\cdot v^{1/2}|\theta|^{2}
after reduction to a convenient region (where
v\gg 1/N
), one still has some room to ‘absorb’ the factor
v^{1/2}
into the bounds for
h
(via its Fourier expansion) and thus can proceed further. Note that the weight of
h
is
k-1/2
.
Without this feature, we wouldn’t have been able to succeed, since the theta series doesn’t decay with
N
in the region
v\gg 1/N
. The reader might be curious why the same idea can’t be used in the previous subsection: the answer is that the room available for a single
\phi
is lost for the sum of squares, since this produces a factor
\approx N
from the number of summands.

The bulk of our calculations are focused on a single
\phi
in Section
6
. The average result follows along the same lines. We end this discussion with a conjecture.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
k\geq 4
be even and
\phi\in J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
be an
L^{2}
-normalized newform. Then for large
N
and any
\epsilon>0
, one has
\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{\infty}\ll_{\epsilon}N^{-1/2+\epsilon}
.

1.9.
Size of spaces of SK lifts

We now move to the next level of complexity and consider the space of Saito-Kurokawa lifts of elliptic modular forms of level
N
– the main objects of interest in this paper. In the weight aspect in level
1
, such a problem was studied by V. Blomer
[
9
]
under generalized Lindelöf hypothesis, by relying on the Fourier expansion directly. In
[
20
]
an approach using the Fourier-Jacobi expansion was laid down. The size of
\mathrm{SK}_{k}
was precisely computed in
[
4
]
:
\displaystyle\sup(\mathrm{SK}_{k})\asymp k^{5/2}
.

Let
B_{k}^{SK}(N)
denote an orthonormal basis for the space
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
, and put

(1.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{N}(Z):=\sum\nolimits_{F\in B_{k}^{SK}(N)}\det(Y)^{k}|F(Z)|^{2};\quad\sup(\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)):=\sup\nolimits_{Z\in\mathbf{H}_{2}}\mathbb{B}_{N}(Z).

For SK lifts of level
p
, we prove the following result (restriction to prime levels is mainly for convenience and length).

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
k>5
be even and
p
be a prime. Then for any
\epsilon>0
,

(1.11)

\displaystyle p^{-2}\ll\sup(\mathrm{SK}_{k}(p))\ll_{k,\epsilon}p^{-1+\epsilon}.

Going by the above lower bound, and the size of the oldspace at level
p
, viz.
\displaystyle\sup(\mathrm{SK}_{k}(p)^{old})\ll p^{-2}
(see Sections
11
and
12.1.1
) – which surprisingly seems not at all trivial – leads us to propose the following conjecture about the size of
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
.

Conjecture 1.8
.

For all even
k\geq 4
and
N\geq 1
, one has

\displaystyle\sup(\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N))\asymp k^{5/2}N^{-2}
.

For a newform, we propose the following.

Conjecture 1.9
.

Let
k\geq 4
be even and
N\geq 1
. For an
L^{2}
-normalized newform
F\in\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
one has
\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{\infty}\ll N^{-3/2}
.

These conjectures are consistent with the expected size of a Hecke eigenform in
S_{k}^{(2)}(N)
, viz.,
\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{\infty}\ll N^{-3/2}
, where the latter is inspired from the expected abound
\sup(S_{k}^{(2)}(N))\asymp_{k}1
. In a way, testing the global sup-norm over smaller and smaller subspaces, say e.g., the SK lifts as in this paper, lends support towards its plausibility.

Perhaps it is worth remarking here that in order to arrive at the statement of Conjecture
1.8
, one invariably has to consider norm relations between, say, a Jacobi newform
\phi
and its EZI lift
F
. However various conflicting normalizations in the literature, and in some cases, ambiguous statements on this point (cf.
[
2
]
,
[
27
]
) makes the path misleading. To us, arriving at the statement of Conjecture
1.8
was as subtle as compared to working on it. We refer the reader to Section
7
for a more detailed discussion.

1.10.
Discussion about the proofs

The lower bound in Theorem
1.7

follows from bounding below the size of the newspace. Here we first use the norm relation between the SK newforms and the corresponding Jacobi newforms. There are some complications here already (cf. Section
7
). In a few words, the norm relations (cf. (
1.3
)) are much more subtle for the oldspace, and need to be worked out from scratch, see Proposition
5.7
. In fact, this is one of the other reasons to write Section

in the first place.

Next, we use Waldspurger’s formula to relate the Fourier coefficients to the twisted central

-values of the corresponding elliptic newforms. This reduces the problem of obtaining a lower bound for

to one such for the average of these central

and gives us the desired global lower bound

. This picture most likely generalizes to square-free levels, see Remark

. In fact, all of our results on the sizes of SK lifts should in principle generalize to square-free levels at the cost of some more delicate technicalities, and most of the ingredients are already present in this paper. We choose to work with prime levels mainly because the paper is already long and technical.

1.10.1.
The three regions: Type 0, 1, 2

To obtain an upper bound for the size of
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(p)
, it is necessary to choose a suitable fundamental domain for
\Gamma_{0}^{(2)}(p)\backslash\mathbf{H}_{2}
. This is described in Section
8
. This essentially leads to three types of regions, which we call Type 0,1,2 – and the analysis proceeds differently for each of these. Moreover, we estimate the sizes of the newspace and oldspace separately, as we can’t fruitfully use the geometric side of the Bergman kernel. Moreover, we have to pass via Jacobi forms – for this, we need norm relations between
F
and
\phi
. But due to the non-uniform nature of the norm relations (see Section
7
), we again need to handle the oldforms separately.

1.10.2.
Type 0 – Fourier expansion

This is the easiest region, where
Y:=\mathrm{Im}(Z)\gg 1
, and even the upper bound
O(p^{-2})
(cf. (
7
)) follows from the Fourier expansion itself. The method used here is the same as that in
[
19
]
or
[
4
]
– viz. looking at the second moment of Fourier coefficients, which are controlled by Fourier coefficients of certain Poincaré series. See Section
9
, which also contains some general results about estimating the Fourier expansion in the level aspect. These may be useful elsewhere as well.

1.10.3.
Type 1 – Fourier-Jacobi expansion

This is at the next level of difficulty. Here we resort to the Fourier-Jacobi (FJ) expansion of
F
: namely, we look at

(1.12)

F(Z)=\sum\nolimits_{m}\phi_{F,m}(\tau,z)e(m\tau^{\prime}),

where
Z=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\tau&z\\
z&\tau^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. We then transfer the problem to the FJ coefficients
\phi_{m}=\phi|V_{m}
(here
V_{m}
is a certain Hecke operator, see subsection
2.1
) and ultimately to one on
\phi_{F,1}
via the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality – i.e., to the corresponding question about the size of
J_{k,1}^{cusp}(p)
, which was discussed in the Introduction, subsection
1.7
. In this region we obtain the bound
O(p^{-3/2})
unconditionally and
O(p^{-2})
assuming Conjecture
1.4
.
We refer the reader to Section
12
for more details.

1.10.4.
Type 2 – counting matrices

This is the hardest of the three regions, where we have
Y\gg 1/p
.
It is worth mentioning here that having an old-basis that is invariant under
W_{p}
at our disposal is crucial, to be able to reduce to this region. This is done in subsection
5.2
. Recall that we bound the oldspace separately.

It is well known that the Fourier expansion is hopeless in this region. Thus, we again fall back to the FJ expansion of
F
. We view the FJ coefficients (cf. (
1.12
)) akin to Fourier coefficients and start with a Rankin-Selberg type argument: roughly we write, after suitably truncating the

|F|^{2}\ll(\sum\nolimits_{m}|\phi_{F,m}|^{2})(\sum\nolimits_{m}e(-4\pi mv^{\prime})),

and then sum over

on both sides and appeal to the geometric side of the Bergman kernel for the sum over
F
i.e.,
\phi

O(p^{-1})
in this region for the newspace. The counting problem can be thought of as the usual one weighted by certain upper triangular matrices: e.g., in we have to count

(1.14)

\mathcal{C}(\tau,m,\delta):=\#\{(\gamma,\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}):\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N),|A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)|<\delta\}.

Here
\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}
are certain upper triangular matrices,
\displaystyle A_{g}(\tau):=(g\tau-\overline{\tau})j(g,\tau)\mathrm{Im}(\tau)^{-1}
,
\mathrm{Im}(\tau)\gg p^{-1}
, and

\approx p
, see subsection
10.4.1
. It may be worth noting that the counting arguments used here are written for any square-free

.

Along the way, we prove various miscellaneous results. For instance we show in Proposition
10.1
the ‘trivial bound’
\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{\infty}\ll N^{\epsilon}m^{1+\epsilon}
for any

-normalized

in the
N

This bound (in fact any polynomial bound) is sufficient for our purposes of truncating a certain Fourier-Jacobi expansion, see subsections
10.3
,
10.6
.

1.10.5.
Contribution of oldforms.

As stated earlier, we bound the oldspace separately. This is due to two reasons. First, as mentioned before, the non-uniform nature of the norm relations. Second, the basis of oldspace is not an EZI lift of a basis of Jacobi oldforms (see Section
5
), and thus is not captured fully by the BK of the space of Jacobi forms.

As in the case of newspace, the size of the oldspace is estimated separately in Type 0, 1, and Type 2 regions. In fact, in regions 1 and 2 we have to work with a bit more complicated version of the counting argument while dealing with the oldspace
\mathrm{SK}_{k}|U_{S}(p)
, see sections
11
and
12.1.1
. When
n=1
, estimating the contribution of the subspaces arising due to the
U(p)
operator is quite straightforward because of a linear Hecke relation between the Hecke operators
T(p)
,
U(p)
and
B_{p}
(see subsection
13.3
). However, no such useful relation exists for the
U_{S}(p)
operator. The other methods such as using the Fourier expansion of
F|U_{S}(p)
or using the double coset decomposition of
U_{S}(p)
do not seem to yield the desired bounds. Thus, the use of BK of Jacobi forms of level
1
and the counting argument becomes necessary in this case.

1.11.
Appendix: the case

In an Appendix (Section

Theorem 1.10

Let

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{p^{2}-1}{p^{3}}x_{1}x_{2}+\frac{1}{p}(x_{1}+x_{2})(1+x_{1}x_{2})\right),

\displaystyle T_{S}(p,p,p,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{3}}x^{2}_{0}x_{1}x_{2}.

Thus to express
T_{S}(1,1,p^{3},p^{3})
as a polynomial in the similitude
p,p^{2}
operators, it is enough to do so for their images. We obtain

(3.64)

\displaystyle T_{S}(1,1,p^{3},p^{3})

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{3}(1+x_{1})(1+x_{2})\left((1-\frac{1}{p}x_{1}+x_{1}^{2})(1-\frac{1}{p}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2})+\frac{p-1}{p}x_{1}x_{2}\right)

(3.65)

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{3}(1+x_{1})(1+x_{2})\left((1+x_{1}^{2})(1+x_{2}^{2})-\frac{1}{p}(x_{1}+x_{2})(1+x_{1}x_{2})+\frac{1-p+p^{2}}{p^{2}}x_{1}x_{2}\right)

(3.66)

\displaystyle=T_{S}(p)\left(T_{S}(1,1,p^{2},p^{2})-pT_{S}(1,p,p^{2},p)+p^{2}T_{S}(p,p,p,p)\right)

(3.67)

\displaystyle=T_{S}(p)\left((p+1)T_{S}(1,1,p^{2},p^{2})-pT_{S}(p^{2})+p(p+1)T_{S}(p,p,p,p)\right).

Since
T_{S}(1,1,p^{2},p^{2})=T_{S}(p)^{2}-(1+\frac{1}{p})T^{\prime}_{S}(p)+p(p+1)T_{S}(p,p,p,p)
, the terms inside the bracket can be written as

(3.68)

\displaystyle(p+1)T_{S}(p)^{2}-p^{-1}(p+1)^{2}T^{\prime}_{S}(p)-pT_{S}(p^{2})+p(p+1)(p+2)T_{S}(p,p,p,p)

(3.69)

\displaystyle=T_{S}(p)^{2}+pT_{S}^{\prime}(p)-p^{-1}(p+1)^{2}T^{\prime}_{S}(p)+p(p+1)(p+2)T_{S}(p,p,p,p)

(3.70)

\displaystyle=T_{S}(p)^{2}-\left(2+\frac{1}{p}\right)T_{S}^{\prime}(p)+p(p+1)(p+2)T_{S}(p,p,p,p).

Thus we get

(3.71)

T_{S}(1,1,p^{3},p^{3})=T_{S}(p)\left(T_{S}(p)^{2}-\left(2+\frac{1}{p}\right)T_{S}^{\prime}(p)+p(p+1)(p+2)T_{S}(p,p,p,p)\right).

The proof is now complete by substituting the above expression for
T_{S}(1,1,p^{3},p^{3})
in (
3.51
).
∎

4.
Saito-Kurokawa lifts of higher level

Apart from our immediate (analytic) applications, this part also has its independent interest and can serve as a classical treatment of SK lifts of higher levels.

Higher level SK newforms have been described from the viewpoint of representation theory, see e.g.,
[
51
]
.
Namely Schmidt shows that when
k
is even,
N
is square-free, and
f\in S_{2k-2}^{new}(N)
is newform there is a functorial lifting from
\mathrm{SL}(2)\times\mathrm{SL}(2)\rightarrow\mathrm{PGSp}(4)
such that
1\times\pi_{f}
defines an automorphic form, which is the SK lift in question. Other local sign conditions lead to paramodular forms.

The definition via
L
-functions which we adopt, is a consequence of this approach, recalled briefly below. However, for the purpose of this paper, the classical version is the backbone, and so we reformulate the situation in the classical language as well.

4.1.
Definition of SK lifts

Let
k
be even and
N
square-free. Let
F\in S_{k}^{(2)}(N)
be a Hecke eigenform of all Hecke operators
T_{S}(m)
for
(m,N)=1
and
\lambda_{F}(m)
be the corresponding Hecke eigenvalues. Define

(4.1)

L^{N}(F,s):=\zeta^{N}(2s+1)\sum\nolimits_{(m,N)=1}\frac{\lambda_{F}(m)}{m^{s}};

the prime-to-
N
spinor zeta function of
F
.
Then
F
is called a
classical Saito-Kurokawa lift
, if there exists an integral weight newform
f
of weight
2k-2
and level
M|N
such that

(4.2)

L^{N}(F,s)=\zeta^{N}(s+1/2)\zeta^{N}(s-1/2)L^{N}(f,s).

Then the subspace of Saito-Kurokawa lifts of level
N
,
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
is defined as the span of all such Hecke eigenforms
F\in S_{k}^{(2)}(N)
that satisfy the property (
4.2
) (see
[
21
,
51
]
for more details).

4.2.
Old and newforms in degree 2

For
N
square-free, we now briefly recall the theory of oldforms in the classical language from
[
50
]
– namely
F\in S_{k}^{(2)}(N)
is ‘old’ at level
N
if there is a prime
p|N
and
F
arises as the image of one of the following operators acting on modular forms of level
N/p
:

(1)

The identity map
T_{0}(p)
.

(2)

The Atkin-Lehner operator
W_{p}
defined as in (
3.1
).

(3)

The
U_{S}(p)
operator defined as in (
3.2
).

(4)

The operator
U_{S}(p)W_{p}
.

The span of such ‘old’ forms is the oldspace – denoted by
S_{k}^{(2),old}(N)
. The space of newforms
S_{k}^{(2),new}(N)
is defined as the orthogonal complement of
S_{k}^{(2),old}(N)
. We put

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(N):=S_{k}^{(2),new}(N)\cap\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N);\quad\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(N):=S_{k}^{(2),old}(N)\cap\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N).

To proceed further, it will be necessary for us to describe the above spaces more concretely – namely as functorial lifts of spaces of Jacobi forms – which was the classical approach taken by Maaß, Eichler-Zagier
[
22
]
and finally Ibukiyama
[
28
]
in level
N
.
This is what we discuss next.

4.3.
Classical lifts from Jacobi forms (EZI lifts):

Let
\mathcal{L}_{N}:J_{k,1}^{cusp}(N)\longrightarrow S_{k}^{(2)}(N)
(

) denote the lifting map of level

from

defined via an explicit Fourier expansion as given below:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}\phi(\tau,z,\tau^{\prime}):=\sum\nolimits_{m\geq 1}(\phi|V_{m})(\tau,z)e(m\tau^{\prime}),

where

is the operator defined as in (

In
[

, it is shown that

is injective and Hecke-equivariant in a certain sense. Let

denote the
\mathcal{L}_{N}
- image of

inside

where he shows (

The newspaces in each coincide, however, this is a strict inclusion for the corresponding oldspaces.
For example, it turns out that

captures all the newforms in

Let

and

Now

4.4.
SK newforms as EZI lifts

In this subsection, we show that the EZI lifts of Jacobi newforms of level
N
are precisely those in the sense of R. Schmidt’s definition – i.e., they are orthogonal to the oldspace. This is known from
[
47
, Lem. 5.2.2]
, but our proof is classical, and in the spirit of this paper.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(N)
be the space of newforms in
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
as defined in (
4.3
) and let
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{J,new}(N)
be the space of spanned by the EZI lifts of newforms in
J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
. Then
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(N)=\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{J,new}(N)
and all newforms in
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
are precisely the EZI lifts of the Jacobi newforms as above.

Proof.

The inclusion
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{J,new}(N)\subset\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(N)
would follow if we can show that any EZI lift
F=\mathcal{L}_{N}(\psi)\in\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{J,new}(N)
is orthogonal to all oldforms. We prove the inclusion by induction on the number of prime factors of
N
. When
N=1
, this is true by the classical theory of Eichler-Zagier. Now assume that the inclusion holds for level
N/p
with square-free
N
and
(p,N/p)=1
. Then we know that
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(N)=\sum_{M|N,M<N}\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(M)|\prod_{q|\frac{N}{M}}R(q)
.
Thus by induction hypothesis, we have to check that

(4.6)

\langle\mathcal{L}_{N}(\psi),\mathcal{L}_{M}(\phi)|R(q)\rangle=0\text{ for }q|\frac{N}{M},

where
\phi\in J_{k,1}^{new}(M)
and
\psi\in J_{k,1}^{new}(N)
. Note that the Hecke operators
T_{S}(q^{\prime})
with
(q^{\prime},N)=1
act the same way on both
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(N)
and
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(M)
and commute with the operators

. The assertion now follows, since
\psi
,
\phi
are newforms of different levels,
\langle\psi,\phi\rangle=0
, via multiplicity-one for
\mathrm{GL}(2)
.

For the other inclusion, we note that the spaces have the same dimension, as follows on the one hand from the injectiveness of the EZI map
\mathcal{L}_{N}
(cf.
[
28
]
), and on the other hand from Schmidt’s construction and uniqueness
[

, Thm. 5.2 (ii)]
or from
[

, Lem.5.2.2]
respectively.
∎

(4.18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)=\sum\nolimits_{M|N}\sum\nolimits_{d,e\colon de|\frac{N}{M}}\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(M)|W_{d}U_{e}

Therefore by (generalized) Möbius inversion one obtains the relation

\dim\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(N)=\sum\nolimits_{M|N}\beta(N/M)\dim\mathrm{SK}_{k}(M),

where
\beta(n)
are defined by the generating series
\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n\geq 1}\beta(n)n^{-s}=\zeta(s)^{-3}
, and thus also a formula for
\dim\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(N)
.

4.7.
Distinguishing EZI lifts via Maaß relations

We know that the space of EZI lifts sit inside the space of SK lifts and satisfy the so-called Maaß relations (cf.
[
28
, Prop. 3.8]
). In this subsection we show that this condition actually characterizes them among all SK lifts of level
N
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
p|N
and
F\in\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{J}(N/p)
be non-zero. Then
F|W_{p}\not\in\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{J}(N)
.

Proof.

From
[
26
]
,
G\in\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{J}(N)
iff the Maaß relations (
1.1
) are satisfied.
Thus it is enough to show that the Fourier coefficients of
F|W_{p}
do not satisfy (
1.1
). From (
3.5
) applied to
N/p
, let us recall that
\gamma W_{p}=B_{p}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N/p)
and
thus
F|W_{p}=F|B_{p}=p^{k}F(pZ)
. Therefore its Fourier expansion can be written as

(4.19)

F|W_{p}=\sum\nolimits_{T}A^{*}_{F}(T)e(\mathrm{tr}(TZ)),

where
A^{*}_{F}(T)=p^{k}A_{F}(T/p)
when
p|\mathbf{c}(T)
and is zero otherwise. If
F|W_{p}
satisfied the Maaß relations, then we would have

(4.20)

\displaystyle A^{*}_{F}(n,r,m)=\sum_{d|(n,r,m),(d,N)=1}d^{k-1}A^{*}_{F}(\frac{nm}{d^{2}},\frac{r}{d},1).

By the aforementioned property of
A^{*}_{F}(T)
, each term on the RHS above is zero. Since this is true for any
A^{*}_{F}(T)
, we see that
F|W_{p}=0
, which is impossible. This gives us the desired conclusion.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

An element
F\in\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
is an EZI lift (i.e., lies in
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{J}(N)
) if and only if it satisfies the Maaß relations (
1.1
).

Proof.

Let
F\in\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N)
be an EZI lift. Then from
[
26
]
,
F
satisfies the Maaß relations.

For the converse, from Lemma
4.1

\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(N)=\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{J,new}(N)
. Thus it is enough to consider the oldspace
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(N)
. From Lemma
4.5
and
4.7
, we have

(4.21)

\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(N)=\sum\nolimits_{p|N}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\frac{N}{p}}(J_{k,1}^{cusp}(\frac{N}{p}))+\mathcal{L}_{\frac{N}{p}}(J_{k,1}^{cusp}(\frac{N}{p}))|W_{p}+\mathcal{L}_{\frac{N}{p}}(J_{k,1}^{cusp}(\frac{N}{p}))|U_{S}(p)\right).

From
[
28
]
, we know the relation
\mathcal{L}_{N}(\phi)=\mathcal{L}_{N/p}(\phi)-p^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{N/p}(\phi)|W_{p}
. Thus combining with the fact that
\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
U_{S}(p)
operators commute, we can write

(4.22)

\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(N)=\sum\nolimits_{p|N}\left(\mathcal{L}_{N}(J_{k,1}^{cusp}(\frac{N}{p}))+\mathcal{L}_{N}(U_{J}(p)(J_{k,1}^{cusp}(\frac{N}{p})))\right)+\sum\nolimits_{p|N}\mathcal{L}_{\frac{N}{p}}(J_{k,1}^{cusp}(\frac{N}{p}))|W_{p}.

By definition, the first two components satisfy the Maaß relation (
1.1
) and all the EZI lifts are contained inside them. Clearly the third component
\sum_{p|N}\mathcal{L}_{\frac{N}{p}}(J_{k,1}^{cusp}(\frac{N}{p}))|W_{p}
is non-zero. Thus it is enough to show that the third component doesn’t satisfy the relations and this is given by Lemma
4.9
.
∎

5.
An orthonormal basis for SK lifts of level
p
.

For any
\phi\in J_{k,1}^{cusp}
, from Lemma
4.5
, we know that the old-space corresponding to
\phi
is
3
dimensional. Let us denote this space by
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p;\phi)
. In view of the supnorm problem, our first goal is to obtain an explicit orthonormal basis for SK lifts of level
p
which is
W_{p}
invariant. In this direction, for
\phi\in J_{k,1}^{cusp}
and operators
R_{i}(p)
introduced in subsection
1.3
we put

(5.1)

F_{i,\phi}=\mathcal{L}_{1}(\phi)|R_{i}(p)\text{ and }G_{i,\phi}=\mathcal{L}_{p}(\phi)|R_{i}(p).

Whenever
\phi
is fixed and there is no ambiguity, we drop
\phi
from the notations and write as
F_{i}
and
G_{i}
.
The following lemma allows us to freely switch between the forms
F_{j,\phi}
and
G_{j,\phi}
in subsequent calculations.

Lemma 5.1
.

With the above notation, the
\mathbf{C}
-spans of
\{F_{1,\phi},F_{2,\phi},F_{3,\phi},F_{4,\phi}\}
and
\{G_{1,\phi},G_{2,\phi},G_{3,\phi},G_{4,\phi}\}
are equal.

Proof.

We first note that
F(pZ)=p^{-k}F|B_{p}
. We claim that
G_{1}=F_{1}-p^{-1}F_{3},G_{2}=F_{2}-p^{-1}F_{1},G_{3}=F_{3}-p^{-1}F_{1},G_{4}=F_{4}-p^{k-1}F_{3}
.

We start from the observation
F|B_{p}=F|W_{p}
since
F
has level
1
and from the relation given in
[
28
]
:

(5.2)

\displaystyle G_{1}=F_{1}-p^{k-1}F_{1}(pZ)=F_{1}-p^{-1}F_{1}|W_{p}=F_{1}-p^{-1}F_{3}.

Next, one computes

(5.3)

\displaystyle G_{2}=(F_{1}-p^{-1}F_{3})|U_{S}(p)=F_{2}-p^{-1}F_{1}|W_{p}|U_{S}(p)=F_{2}-p^{-1}F_{1}|B_{p}|U_{S}(p)=F_{2}-p^{k-1}F_{1}.

For
G_{3},G_{4}
one similarly gets

(5.4)

G_{3}=(F_{1}-p^{-1}F_{3})|W_{p}=F_{3}-p^{-1}F_{1};\quad G_{4}=(F_{2}-p^{k-1}F_{1})|W_{p}=F_{4}-p^{k-1}F_{3}.\qed

Lemma 5.2
.

The set
\{G_{1,\phi},G_{2,\phi},G_{3,\phi}\}
is a basis for the
\phi
-oldspace
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p;\phi)
in
\mathrm{SK}_{k}(p)
.

Proof.

In principle, this follows from Lemma
4.3
. But the following proof is more illuminating – and also helpful while constructing a
W_{p}
-invariant old-basis.

From Lemma
4.7
, we know that
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p;\phi)
is spanned by
\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3},F_{4}\}
, which equals the span of
\{G_{1},G_{2},G_{3},G_{4}\}
by Lemma
5.1
. Moreover from Lemma
4.5
, we know that
\dim\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p)(\phi)=3
and from
[
28
]
, that
G_{1},G_{2}
are linearly independent.

If we put
V=\mathbf{C}G_{1}+\mathbf{C}G_{2}
and
W=\mathbf{C}G_{1}|W_{p}+\mathbf{C}G_{2}|W_{p}
, then from the above we conclude that
\dim V=\dim W=2
, and that
\dim(V+W)=3
– which implies that
\dim(V\cap W)=1
. Now let
V\cap W=\mathbf{C}v
. We can then write

(5.5)

\displaystyle v=c_{1}G_{1}+c_{2}G_{2}=d_{1}G_{1}|W_{p}+d_{2}G_{2}|W_{p}

for some scalars
c_{j},d_{j}
. By applying
W_{p}
on both sides we obtain, by the uniqueness of
v
up to scalars that
d_{j}=\alpha c_{j}
for some
\alpha\neq 0
and
j=1,2
. Then (
5.5
) reads

(5.6)

\displaystyle v=G_{12}=\alpha G_{12}|W_{p},\quad\quad(G_{12}:=c_{1}G_{1}+c_{2}G_{2}).

Clearly
\alpha^{2}=1
. From this one can also write

(5.7)

\displaystyle c_{1}(G_{1}\pm G_{3})=-c_{2}(G_{2}\pm G_{4}).

Clearly,
c_{2}\neq 0
as otherwise we must have
F_{1}=\pm F_{3}
, which is impossible. Then note that
G_{3}\not\in\mathbf{C}G_{1}+\mathbf{C}G_{2}
as otherwise from (
5.7
), we would have
G_{4}\in\mathbf{C}G_{1}+\mathbf{C}G_{2}
which would imply
\dim(V+W)=2
.
Therefore,
\{G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}\}
are linearly independent. Of course, we can also replace
G_{3}
with
G_{4}
. This proves the lemma.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{B}^{J}_{k,1}=\{\phi\}
be an orthogonal basis of Hecke eigenforms for
J_{k,1}^{cusp}
. Then the set
B^{SK,old}:=\{G_{1,\phi},G_{2,\phi},G_{3,\phi}:\phi\in\mathcal{B}^{J}_{k,1}\}
is a basis for the space
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p)
. The spaces
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p;\phi)
with
\phi
as above, are mutually orthogonal.

Proof.

From Lemma
5.2
, we know that
\{G_{1,\phi},G_{2,\phi},G_{3,\phi}\}
is a basis for
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p;\phi)
and we have that
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p)=\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{B}^{J}_{k,1}}\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p;\phi)
. That this is a direct sum, follows from the statement about orthogonality. But this in turn, follows immediately from multiplicity-one in degree
1
, the fact that the operators
U_{S}(p),W_{p}
commute with Hecke operators
T_{S}(q)
with
q\neq p
and the equivariance relations from
[
28
, Theorem 4.1]
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

From Proposition

, we can also obtain (the known fact from subsection

5.1.
An orthonormal basis for oldspace:

To obtain an orthonormal basis for the oldspace of level
p
, we make use of the basis obtained in Proposition
5.3
. In this direction, we first calculate the inner products
\langle G_{i},G_{j}\rangle
.
For this, we use Lemmas
3.1
,
3.3
,
3.4
and
3.5
and the relations (
5.2
), (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), which say that
G_{1}=F_{1}-p^{-1}F_{3}
,
G_{2}=F_{2}-p^{k-1}F_{1}

G_{3}=F_{3}-p^{-1}F_{1}
.
We summarize these inner product relations in the following Proposition.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\phi\in J_{k,1}^{cusp}
and let
F_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{1}(\phi)
,
G_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{p}(\phi)
. Put
G_{2}:=G_{1}|U_{S}(p)
and
G_{3}:=G_{1}|W_{p}
. Further, let
F_{1}
be an eigenfunction of the Hecke operators
T_{S}(p)
and
T_{S}^{\prime}(p)
with the eigenvalue
\lambda_{F_{1}}(p)
and
\lambda_{F_{1}}^{\prime}(p)
, respectively. Then we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle\langle G_{1},G_{1}\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{2\lambda_{F_{1}}(p)}{\mu(p)p^{k-2}}\right)\langle F_{1},F_{1}\rangle_{p};

(5.9)

\displaystyle\langle G_{1},G_{2}\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda_{F_{1}}^{\prime}(p)}{p^{k-1}(p^{2}+1)}-\frac{\lambda_{F_{1}}(p)^{2}}{\mu(p)p^{k-2}}+\frac{p\lambda_{F_{1}}(p)}{p+1}-\frac{p^{k-3}(p^{4}+p^{2}+1)}{p^{2}+1}\right)\langle F_{1},F_{1}\rangle_{p};

(5.10)

\displaystyle\langle G_{1},G_{3}\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p^{1-k}\lambda_{F_{1}}(p)}{p+1}-\frac{2}{p}\right)\langle F_{1},F_{1}\rangle_{p};

(5.11)

\displaystyle\langle G_{2},G_{2}\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p^{2}(p-1)}{\mu(p)}\lambda_{F_{1}}(p)^{2}-\frac{2p^{k+2}}{\mu(p)}\lambda_{F_{1}}(p)+p^{2k-4}+p^{2k-2}\right)\langle F_{1},F_{1}\rangle_{p};

(5.12)

\displaystyle\langle G_{2},G_{3}\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda_{F}(p)^{2}}{\mu(p)p^{k-3}}-\frac{2p^{2}\lambda_{F_{1}}(p)}{\mu(p)}-\frac{\lambda_{F}^{\prime}(p)}{p^{k-2}(p^{2}+1)}+\frac{p^{k-2}(p^{2}+2)}{p^{2}+1}\right)\langle F_{1},F_{1}\rangle_{p}.

Remark 5.6
.

The inner products
\langle G_{i},G_{i}\rangle_{p}
in Proposition
5.5
are indeed positive. To see this, recall from (
4.5
) that
\lambda_{F_{1}}(p)=p^{k-1}+p^{k-2}+\lambda_{\phi}(p)
. Then one can verify that
\langle G_{1},G_{1}\rangle_{p}\asymp\langle F_{1},F_{1}\rangle_{p}
which is clearly positive. For
G_{2}
, we have
\langle G_{2},G_{2}\rangle_{p}\asymp\mu(p)^{-1}(p^{2}(p-1)\lambda_{\phi}(p)^{2}-2p^{k}\lambda_{\phi}(p)+p^{2k-1})\langle F_{1},F_{1}\rangle_{p}
. Since
|\lambda_{\phi}(p)|\leq 2p^{k-3/2}
, we see that
\langle G_{2},G_{2}\rangle_{p}>0
.

5.2.
W_{p}
invariant old-basis

It is desirable to obtain an orthogonal basis consisting of eigenvectors of
W_{p}
. This is in particular quite useful in the context of the sup-norm problem, as it helps us to narrow the realm of search for large values in convenient regions of
\mathbf{H}_{2}
.

As in Proposition
5.3
, let
\mathcal{B}_{k,1}^{J}
be an orthonormal basis of Hecke eigenforms for
J_{k,1}^{cusp}
. For
\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{k,1}^{J}
, let
F_{1,\phi}:=\mathcal{L}_{1}(\phi)
and
G_{1,\phi}:=\mathcal{L}_{p}(\phi)
. By construction, clearly
W_{p}
acts on
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p)
. Moreover,

(5.13)

G_{\pm,\phi}:=G_{1,\phi}\pm G_{3,\phi}

are orthogonal and are eigenvectors for
W_{p}
with eigenvalues
\pm 1
respectively. To obtain the third eigenvector, let

(5.14)

\displaystyle H_{\phi}:=G_{2,\phi}-a_{+,\phi}G_{+,\phi}-a_{-,\phi}G_{-,\phi}

be orthogonal to both
G_{+,\phi},G_{-,\phi}
. Therefore,

(5.15)

\displaystyle a_{\pm,\phi}=\frac{\langle G_{2,\phi},G_{\pm,\phi}\rangle_{p}}{\langle G_{\pm,\phi},G_{\pm,\phi}\rangle_{p}}.

Next note that
H_{\phi}|W_{p}
is also orthogonal to both
G_{+,\phi},G_{-,\phi}
. Since the orthogonal complement of
<G_{+,\phi},G_{-,\phi}>
is one dimensional, we must have
H_{\phi}|W_{p}=\pm H_{\phi}
, giving
H_{\phi}
to be the last of our desired basis elements. We do not work out the exact sign, as this is not required for us.

From Proposition
5.5
, we see that

(5.16)

\displaystyle\langle G_{\pm,\phi},G_{\pm,\phi}\rangle_{p}\asymp\langle G_{1,\phi},G_{1,\phi}\rangle_{p}\pm\langle G_{1,\phi},G_{3,\phi}\rangle_{p}\asymp\langle F_{1,\phi},F_{1,\phi}\rangle_{p};

(5.17)

\displaystyle|\langle G_{2,\phi},G_{\pm,\phi}\rangle_{p}|\ll p^{k-3/2}\langle F_{1,\phi},F_{1,\phi}\rangle_{p};\quad\langle H_{\phi},H_{\phi}\rangle_{p}\asymp p^{2k-4}\langle F_{1,\phi},F_{1,\phi}\rangle_{p}.

We also have the relation
[
36
, pp. 551]
:
\langle F_{1},F_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{Sp}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})}=c_{k}\langle\phi,\phi\rangle_{\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})}
. Thus, the level
p
norms can be related as
\langle F_{1},F_{1}\rangle_{p}=(p^{2}+1)c_{k}\langle\phi,\phi\rangle_{p}
.

This takes care of the oldspace. For the newspace, all SK newforms are EZI lifts of unique Jacobi newforms of level
p
. That each of these are invariant under
W_{p}
follows from multiplicity-one in a standard manner.
We summarize the above discussion in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{k,1}^{J}
denote an orthonormal basis of Hecke eigenforms for
J_{k,1}^{cusp}
. Then the set

(5.18)

\mathcal{B}^{SK,old}(p)=\left\{G_{+,\phi},G_{-,\phi},H_{\phi}:\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{k,1}^{J}\right\}

is an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors of
W_{p}
for the oldspace
\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{old}(p)
with
G_{+,\phi},H_{\phi}
as in (
5.13
), (
5.14
) respectively. Moreover, we have that

(5.19)

\langle G_{\pm,\phi},G_{\pm,\phi}\rangle_{p}\asymp\langle F_{1,\phi},F_{1,\phi}\rangle_{p}\asymp p^{3}\langle\phi,\phi\rangle_{1};\quad\langle H_{\phi},H_{\phi}\rangle_{p}\asymp p^{2k-4}\langle F_{1,\phi},F_{1,\phi}\rangle_{p}\asymp p^{2k-1}\langle\phi,\phi\rangle_{1}.

For the new space,
\mathcal{B}^{SK,new}(p)=\{\mathcal{L}_{p}(\phi)\colon\phi\in J_{k,1}^{cusp}(p)^{new}\}
is the

invariant basis.

6.
Sup-norm of Jacobi forms

The purpose of this section is twofold:

(i) To study the size of
J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
– and this is done by naturally reducing the problem to one about half-integral weight forms; and

(ii) Prove a non-trivial bound for a Hecke eigenform
\phi\in J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
by the same strategy as (i) above, but there are quite subtle issues here – things do not follow in a straightforward way, and one has to go at the root of the half-integral case treated by Kiral
[
33
]
and account for the extra subtlety mentioned above.

6.1.
Theta decomposition of Jacobi forms

Let
N
be an odd, square-free integer and
\phi\in J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
be an eigenform of all Hecke operators away from the level
N
.
The periodicity properties of
\phi
allows one to write (cf.
[
22
]
)

(6.1)

\displaystyle\phi=h_{0}(\tau)\theta_{0}(\tau,z)+h_{1}(\tau)\theta_{1}(\tau,z).

where the classical Jacobi theta functions
\theta_{i}
of weight
1/2
and index
1
are given by

(6.2)

\theta_{i}(\tau,z)=\sum\nolimits_{n\equiv i\bmod 2}e(n^{2}\tau/4)e(nz).

Then the Eichler-Zagier isomorphism is the map
EZ:\phi\mapsto h(\tau)=h_{0}(4\tau)+h_{0}(4\tau)
. Crucial to our approach is a convenient description of theta components
h_{j}
of
\phi
in terms of the half-integral weight cusp form
h
. This is clear when
N=1
from
[
22
]
. But it is not as trivial when
N>1
; we refer the reader to
[
39
, Satz 8]
. For convenience, we recall the result here.

Proposition 6.1
.

h_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\big(h(\frac{\tau-1}{4})+h(\frac{\tau+1}{4})\big);\quad h_{1}=\frac{1}{2i}\big(h(\frac{\tau-1}{4})-h(\frac{\tau+1}{4})\big)
.

Further, from
[
39
, Satz 9]
we see that
h\in S_{k-1/2}^{+}(4N)
– the Kohnen’s plus space of level
4N
(see
[
37
]
).
From Proposition
6.1
, we can therefore write

(6.3)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(h(\frac{\tau-1}{4})(\theta_{0}(\tau,z)-i\theta_{1}(\tau,z))+h(\frac{\tau+1}{4})(\theta_{0}(\tau,z)+i\theta_{1}(\tau,z))\right).

6.1.1.
A convenient fundamental domain

To start with, note that the invariant function attached to
\phi\in J_{k,m}^{cusp}(N)
, written as
\widetilde{\phi}
is defined as

(6.4)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}:=v^{k/2}e^{-2\pi my^{2}/v}|\phi|.

Let us now fix a fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}_{N}\subset\mathbf{H}
for the action of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
on
\mathbf{H}
defined by

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}=\cup_{q\mid N}\cup_{0\leq j\leq d-1}A_{q,j}\mathcal{F}_{1};

where
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is the classical fundamental domain for
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
on

(contained in the Siegel set
\{z\in\mathbf{H}\mid\Im(z)\geq\sqrt{3}/2
), and
A_{q,j}
are a set of left-coset representatives of

in

]

\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})=\cup_{q\mid N}\cup_{0\leq j\leq q-1}\Gamma_{0}(N)A_{q,j};\quad A_{q,j}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta&1\\
q\gamma&\frac{N}{q}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&j\end{pmatrix}\quad(\beta\frac{N}{q}-q\gamma=1).

Next, note that for any function

on

,

\displaystyle\left(v^{k/2}e^{-2\pi y^{2}/v}|\Psi|\right)\circ(M(\tau,z))=v^{k/2}e^{-2\pi y^{2}/v}|(\Psi|_{k,1}M)(\tau,z)|.

This can be checked from

Lemma 6.2
.

(i) A complete set of coset representatives of

in

can be taken to be
(\gamma,[0,0])
where

runs over
\Gamma_{0}(N)\backslash\Gamma_{1}

Clearly it is enough to prove (i). If

∎

We now come back to our goal of bounding

.
First, by invariance under

along with (

\displaystyle\sup\nolimits_{(\tau,z)\in\mathbf{H}\times\mathbf{C}}\widetilde{\phi}(\tau,z)=\sup\nolimits_{(\tau,z)\in\mathcal{F}^{J}_{N}}\widetilde{\phi}(\tau,z)=\max\nolimits_{q,j}\sup\nolimits_{(\tau,z)\in\mathcal{F}^{J}_{1}}T_{q,j},

where the quantities

are defined by

T_{q,j}:=v^{k/2}e^{-2\pi y^{2}/v}|(\phi|_{k,1}A_{q,j})(\tau,z)|,

and
A_{q,j}
runs over a set of coset representatives of
\Gamma_{0}(N)

\displaystyle\sup(\mathrm{SK}_{k}(N))\geq\det(Y_{0})^{k}\exp(-4\pi\mathrm{tr}(T_{0}Y_{0}))\sum\nolimits_{F\in H^{*}_{k}(N)}|a_{F}(T_{0})|^{2}\big/\langle F,F\rangle.

We can choose any

.
A good choice is (if we refer to the Waldspurger’s formula and the exponent of

We write (

For the rest of this section, let us assume that

We now appeal to Waldspurger’s formula (cf.

\displaystyle\frac{4^{k-1}}{6}\frac{|c_{\phi}(-4)|^{2}}{\langle\phi_{F},\phi_{F}\rangle}=\frac{|c_{\phi_{F}}(-4)|^{2}}{\langle h_{F},h_{F}\rangle}=2\frac{\Gamma(k-1)4^{k-3/2}}{\pi^{k-1}}\frac{L(1/2,f\otimes\chi_{-4})}{\langle f,f\rangle},

Next we appeal to the following average result from

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{f\in H^{*}_{k}(p)}\frac{L(1/2,f\otimes\chi_{-4})}{\langle f,f\rangle_{p}}\gg 1,

for all

Remark 7.2

(

\displaystyle\sup(\mathrm{SK}_{k}^{new}(p))\gg\det(Y_{0})^{k}\exp(-4\pi\mathrm{tr}(Y_{0}))\frac{4^{k}p^{-2}}{\Gamma(k)}\frac{(4\pi)^{k}\pi^{k}}{\Gamma(k-3/2)}\cdot\frac{1}{p^{2}};

and for any
\mathcal{D}\equiv 0,-1\bmod{4}

\mathcal{D}>0

\displaystyle c_{\phi,F}(\mathcal{D}):=c_{\phi_{1,F}}(\mathcal{D})

Then we have, for
T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}n&r/2\\

and
\mathbf{c}(T)=(n,r,m)

(9.2)

\displaystyle a_{F}(T)=\sum\nolimits_{a|\mathbf{c}(T),\,(a,N)=1}a^{k-1}c_{\phi,F}\left(D/a^{2}\right).

, where

denotes any orthonormal basis of

, then we see that

(9.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{*}(T)\ll p^{-2}\,(\sum\nolimits_{a}a^{k-1}p_{*}(D/a^{2})^{1/2})^{2}.

This follows by using the relations (
9.2
) and (
7.5

(9.4)

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{*}(T)&=\sum\nolimits_{F}\Big|\sum\nolimits_{a|\mathbf{c}(T)}a^{k-1}c_{\phi,F}\left(\frac{D}{a^{2}}\right)\Big|^{2}\ll p^{-2}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{a}a^{k-1}\big(\sum\nolimits_{\phi}|c_{\phi,F}\left(\frac{D}{a^{2}}\right)|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\Big)^{2}.\end{split}

Let
P_{D,r}\in J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
be the
D
-th Poincaré series, it is well-known that it depends only on
D
. The lemma below gives bounds on its Fourier coefficients, which follows from
[
16

and standard estimates.

Lemma 9.1
.

c_{D,r}=1+O((D,N)^{1/2}D^{1/2}N^{-1+\epsilon})
.

Now using the bound for the Fourier coefficients of Poincaré series from Proposition Lemma
9.1
we see that (see
[
4
]
for similar arguments in level

)

(9.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{*}(T)\ll_{\epsilon}p^{-2}\frac{4^{k}\pi^{2k}}{\Gamma(k)\Gamma(k-3/2)}\cdot\left(D^{k-3/2}c(T)^{1+\epsilon}+p^{-1/2+\epsilon}k^{-1/3}D^{k-1}c(T)^{\epsilon}\right).

Next, note that we can write by
[
19
, Lem. 4.4]
(follows from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality) that

(9.6)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{F\in\mathcal{B}^{SK,new}(p)}\frac{\det(Y)^{k}|F(Z)|^{2}}{\left\lVert F\right\rVert^{2}_{p}}\leq q_{*}(Y)^{2},

where for
Y>0
, we have put

(9.7)

\displaystyle q_{*}(Y):=\sum\nolimits_{T}\mathcal{P}_{*}(T)^{1/2}\det(Y)^{k/2}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{tr}TY).

Plugging in the bound for
\mathcal{P}_{*}(T)
in (

) we get that

is

(9.8)

\displaystyle\ll p^{-1}Q(1/2,3/4-\epsilon;Y)+p^{-5/4+\epsilon}Q(\epsilon,1/2;Y),

where the implied constant depends polynomially on
k
and

(9.9)

\displaystyle Q(\alpha,\beta;Y):=\frac{(4\pi)^{k}}{\Gamma(k)}\sum\nolimits_{T}\mathbf{c}(T)^{\alpha}\det(T)^{k/2-\beta+\epsilon}\det(Y)^{k/2}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{tr}TY).

9.1.
Analysis of the Fourier expansion

This section collects various bounds coming from the Fourier expansions of modular forms in level
N
. We start with a general set-up and consider any
n\geq 1
.
For any
S\subseteq\Lambda_{n}^{+}
we put

(9.10)

Q_{S}(\beta;Y):=\sum\nolimits_{T\in S}\det(T)^{k/2-\beta}\det(Y)^{k/2}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{tr}TY),

Then we can write
Q_{\Lambda_{n}^{+}}(\beta;Y)
as

(9.11)

Q_{\Lambda_{n}^{+}}(\beta;Y)=Q_{\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})}(\beta;Y)+Q_{\Lambda_{n}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})}(\beta;Y),

where the set
\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})
is defined as below.

(9.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon}):=\{T\in\Lambda^{+}_{n}|\text{ all the eigenvalues of }TY\text{ are of the magnitude }\frac{k}{4\pi}+O(k^{1/2+\epsilon}N^{\epsilon})\}.

Now we show that the tail in (
9.11
) has sub-exponential decay. To be precise, we show that (assuming that
\beta\geq 0
):

Lemma 9.2
.

For
Y>0
Minkowski reduced,
N
large enough, one has

(9.13)

\displaystyle Q_{\Lambda_{n}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})}(\beta;Y)\ll\exp(-cN^{\epsilon})\det(Y)^{\beta-(n+1)/2}.

Moreover, if one of the diagonal entries
v,v^{\prime}\gg N^{\epsilon}
, one has a sub-exponential decay:
\displaystyle Q_{\Lambda_{n}^{+}}(\beta;Y)\ll\exp(-c_{1}N^{\epsilon})\det(Y)^{\beta-(n+1)/2}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{1}(TY)\leq\lambda_{2}(TY)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}(TY)
denote the eigenvalues of
TY
. Then
\Lambda_{n}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})
is the set of
T
such that
\lambda_{n}(TY)>N^{\epsilon}
. For any
A>0
and an integer
t\geq 0
, let us put

(9.14)

\displaystyle D_{t}(A):=\{T\in\Lambda_{n}|2^{t}A\leq\lambda_{n}(TY)<2^{t+1}A\}.

Clearly
Q_{\Lambda_{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})}(\alpha,\beta,Y)
is bounded by

(9.15)

\displaystyle\det(Y)^{\beta}\sum\nolimits_{t\geq 0}\sum\nolimits_{T\in D_{t}(N^{\epsilon})}\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{n}m(\lambda_{j}(TY)),

where
m(\lambda):=\exp\big((\frac{k}{2}-\beta)\log(\lambda)-2\pi\lambda\big)
.

The function
m(x)
is bounded on positive reals and decays sub-exponentially when
x>N^{\epsilon}
. With this in mind, the expression in (
9.15
) is bounded up to a constant depending only on
k
, by

(9.16)

\displaystyle\exp(-c_{1}N^{\epsilon})\det(Y)^{\beta}\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^{\infty}\#D_{t}(N^{\epsilon})\exp(-c_{1}2^{t}N^{\epsilon}).

The proof is now complete after we observe the following bound for the cardinality of the set
D_{t}(N^{\epsilon})
:

(9.17)

D_{t}(N^{\epsilon})\ll N^{\epsilon}\cdot 2^{(n+1)t}(y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{n})^{-(n+1)/2}\asymp N^{\epsilon}\cdot 2^{3t}\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}.\qed

For the last assertion of the lemma, notice that
\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})
is empty if either
v
or
v^{\prime}
are
\gg N^{\epsilon}
, since then
\mathrm{tr}(TY)\gg N^{\epsilon}
for large enough
N
.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})
be as defined in (
9.12
) and
Y
Minkowski-reduced.
Then

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})\ll\frac{N^{\epsilon}}{\det(Y)^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}.

Proof.

First, we note that since
k
is fixed, for large
N
,
T\in\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})
implies that
\mathrm{tr}(TY)\ll N^{\epsilon}
. It is easy to see that
\displaystyle t_{1}y_{1}+t_{2}y_{2}+\cdots+t_{n}y_{n}\ll N^{\epsilon}
. This gives
N^{\epsilon}y_{j}^{-1}
choices for the
t_{j}
. Moreover we must have
y_{j}\ll N^{\epsilon}
, otherwise the count is zero.

The off-diagonal elements
t_{ij}
(where
i\neq j
) satisfy
2|t_{ij}|\leq(t_{i}t_{j})^{1/2}
. Given
t_{j}\geq 1
(
1\leq j\leq n
), the total count is given by

(9.18)

N^{\epsilon}\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{-1}\prod\nolimits_{j>i}(y_{i}y_{j})^{-1/2}=N^{\epsilon}\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}.\qed

Remark 9.4
.

More generally in degree
n
, following the lines of
[
19
, Lem. 4.9]
one can also arrive at the estimate
\#\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})\ll_{n,k}N^{\frac{n+1}{2}+\epsilon}\det(Y)^{-\frac{n+1}{4}}
valid for
Y\gg 1/N
. We note however that this result leads to the same bound that we get in (
10.6
).

Clearly in
Q_{\Lambda_{n}^{+}}(\beta;Y)
we can assume that
Y
is Minkowski reduced, hence it is enough to bound it with this assumption. For the
T\in\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})
we simply note that since
m(x)\ll_{k}1
for all
x>0
(cf. (
9.15
)),

(9.19)

Q_{\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})}(\beta;Y)\ll\det(Y)^{\beta}\sum\nolimits_{T\in\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon})}1\ll\det(Y)^{\beta}\#\mathcal{C}(Y,N^{\epsilon}).

Therefore, combining Lemma
9.2
and Lemma
9.3
, we get the following bound for
Q_{\Lambda_{n}^{+}}(\beta;Y)
.

Proposition 9.5
.

For any
Y>0
, one has

(9.20)

Q_{\Lambda_{n}^{+}}(\beta;Y)\ll_{k,n}N^{\epsilon}\det(Y)^{\beta-(n+1)/2}.

Now we return to the case at hand and assume
n=2
. Using the analysis of the Fourier expansion and the counting argument in the previous section, we arrive at the following estimate for the function
Q(\alpha,\beta;Y)
.

Lemma 9.6
.

Let
\alpha<2
,
\beta>0
and
Q(\alpha,\beta;Y)
be as in (
9.9
), then we have

(9.21)

Q(\alpha,\beta;Y)\ll p^{\epsilon}\det(Y)^{\beta-3/2}.

Proof.

We start from (
9.9
), and put
d=\mathbf{c}(T)
and write
T=dT_{0}
where
T_{0}
is primitive. Further,
Y_{d}:=dY
. Then we can write,

(9.22)

\displaystyle Q(\alpha,\beta;Y)

\displaystyle\ll\sum\nolimits_{d\ll p^{\epsilon}/\det(Y)^{1/2}}\frac{1}{d^{2\beta-\alpha-\epsilon}}\sum\nolimits_{T_{0}\text{ primitive}}\frac{\det(T_{0}Y_{d})^{k/2}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{tr}T_{0}Y_{d})}{\det(T_{0})^{\beta}\Gamma(k)}

(9.23)

\displaystyle\ll p^{\epsilon}\det(Y)^{\beta-3/2}\sum\nolimits_{d\ll p^{\epsilon}/\det(Y)^{1/2}}d^{-3+\alpha+\epsilon}\ll p^{\epsilon}\det(Y)^{\beta-3/2},

where we use Proposition
9.5
for the sum over
T_{0}
and the fact that the sum over
d
, extended to
\mathbf{N}
, is convergent for
\alpha<2
.∎

Now we bound the space of newforms in the region
Y\gg 1
.
Putting
\alpha=1/2
,
\beta=3/4-\epsilon
, we get from (
9.9
) and (
9.8
) that

\displaystyle q_{*}(Y)\ll p^{-1+\epsilon}\det(Y)^{\epsilon}
.
Thus

(9.24)

\sup_{Z\in\mathbf{H}_{2},Y\gg 1}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{B}^{SK,new}(p)}\frac{\det(Y)^{k}|F(Z)|^{2}}{\left\lVert F\right\rVert^{2}_{p}}\ll p^{-2+\epsilon}.

Newform contribution in Type 2 region.

\displaystyle Z\in\mathbf{H}_{2},\,Y=\mathrm{Im}(Z)\gg p^{-1}\text{ and }v,v^{\prime}\ll p^{\epsilon},

Using the arguments as in Section

10.2.
Treatment of Type 2 region via the Fourier-Jacobi expansion

We will attempt to treat the Fourier Jacobi coefficients in the same footing as the Fourier coefficients of cusp forms.

The arguments for the rest of the section are valid for any square-free
N
, thus we use
N
, instead of
p
.

We start with (note that
tv=\det(Y)
)

(10.7)

\displaystyle\det Y^{k}\sum\nolimits_{F}\frac{|F(Z)|^{2}}{\langle F,F\rangle}\asymp N^{-2}\det(vt)^{k}\sum\nolimits_{\phi}\frac{|\sum\nolimits_{m}\phi_{m}e(m\tau^{\prime})|^{2}}{\langle\phi,\phi\rangle}.

First we show that the
m
sum above can be truncated, at a negligible cost, at

(10.8)

m\leq L:=N^{\epsilon}/v^{\prime}.

The result given below is certainly not the best possible, but it is enough for our purposes. Also we consider more generally for
any
index
m
Jacobi form, not necessarily of the form
V_{m}\phi
, with
\phi\in J^{cusp}_{k,1}(N)
. Perhaps this will have uses elsewhere, and is new, anyway. Recall
\widetilde{\phi}
from (
6.4
). We have the following estimate, which is the ‘trivial bound’ in the
N
aspect.

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
\phi\in J_{k,m}^{cusp}(N)
. Then we have

(10.9)

\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{\infty}=\sup\nolimits_{\mathbf{H}\times\mathbf{C}}\widetilde{\phi}/\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{N}\ll N^{\epsilon}m^{1+\epsilon}.

Proof.

We take the most expedient route just by appealing to the theta expansion and bounding the Theta components of
\phi
on the one hand, and on the other, bound the
h_{\mu}
individually by considering them as cusp forms on
\Gamma(4mN)
. Put
r=k-1/2
. By Cauchy-Schwarz (denoted by CS henceforth), we have

(10.10)

\displaystyle v^{k}e^{-4\pi y^{2}/v}|\phi(\tau,z)|^{2}/\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert^{2}_{N}\leq\,\sum_{\mu\bmod{2m}}v^{r}|h_{\mu}(\tau)|^{2}/\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert^{2}_{N}\cdot\sum_{\mu\bmod{2m}}v^{1/2}e^{-4\pi y^{2}/v}|\theta_{\mu}(\tau,z)|^{2}.

We will be brief, since the proof follows in spirit the arguments from the cases
m=1
along with
N=1
(cf.
[
4
]
).

As in Section
6
, we can work in the region
\cup_{j}\gamma_{j}\mathcal{F}^{J}_{1}
– which shows that it is sufficient to the bound on
\mathcal{F}^{J}_{1}
. We first consider the theta series.

(10.11)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\mu\bmod{2m}}v^{1/2}e^{-4\pi my^{2}/v}|\theta_{\mu}(\tau,z)|\gamma_{j}|^{2}=v^{1/2}e^{-4\pi my^{2}/v}\sum\nolimits_{\mu}\Big|\sum\nolimits_{\nu}\varepsilon(\nu,\mu;\gamma_{j})\theta_{\nu}(\tau,z)\Big|^{2}

(10.12)

\displaystyle\ll v^{1/2}e^{-4\pi my^{2}/v}\left(\sum\nolimits_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}|^{2}\right)\ll m^{1/2}(1+\frac{1}{vm})^{1/2}(1+\frac{v}{m})^{1/2}\ll m^{1/2}v^{1/2}.

Next, we recall that
h_{\mu}\in S_{r}(\Gamma(4mN))
(see
[
13
, Section 4, Part B]
for a proof). Thus the Petersson norm relation between
\phi
and the
h_{\mu}
can be rewritten as follows:

(10.13)

\displaystyle(mN)^{2}(1+o(mN))\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert^{2}_{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum\nolimits_{\mu}\left\lVert h_{\mu}\right\rVert_{\Gamma(4mN)}^{2}\gg m^{-1/2}\left\lVert h_{\mu}\right\rVert_{\Gamma(4mN)}^{2}.

The above follows by noting that (see

We are left with bounding the quantity

in the region
\mathcal{F}_{1}
. Note that

and has a Fourier expansion of the form

. Thus using the arguments as in

, we see that

(10.15)

Summing over

(10.16)

\displaystyle v^{k}e^{-4\pi y^{2}/v}|\phi(\tau,z)|^{2}/\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert^{2}_{N}\ll N^{\epsilon}m^{2+\epsilon}v^{-1/2}\ll N^{\epsilon}m^{2+\epsilon}.

The last inequality follows since

and this completes the proof.
∎

We now want to return back to our claim about sub-exponential decay, and look at (

Recall that

and

(10.18)

for some absolute constant

10.4.
Non-trivial bound for the BK

We note here that the arguments in this subsection work for all

To bound the BK non-trivially, first note that

(10.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{\phi}\sum_{m}\frac{|\phi_{m}|^{2}}{m^{k-1}}\frac{v^{k}e^{-4\pi my^{2}/v}}{\langle\phi,\phi\rangle}=\sum_{\phi}\sum_{m}\frac{v^{k}e^{-4\pi my^{2}/v}}{m^{-k+1}\langle\phi,\phi\rangle}\Big|\sum_{d|m}\sum_{b\bmod d}d^{-k}\phi\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{d},az\right)\Big|^{2}.

Expanding the square, we see that the RHS can be written as

(10.23)

\mathcal{M}(\tau,z):=\sum\nolimits_{m}\frac{v^{k}e^{-4\pi my^{2}/v}}{m^{-k+1}}\sum\nolimits_{\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}}(dd^{\prime})^{-k}B_{J}(\alpha_{m}\tau,mz/d;\beta_{m}\tau,mz/d^{\prime}),

where
B_{J}(\tau,z;\tau^{\prime},z^{\prime})
denotes the index one, level
N
Jacobi BK and

(10.24)

\alpha_{m}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
0&d\end{smallmatrix}\right),\,\beta_{m}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a^{\prime}&b^{\prime}\\
0&d^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\,ad=a^{\prime}d^{\prime}=m,\,b\bmod d,\,b^{\prime}\bmod d^{\prime}.

Choice of residue classes:

The operator
V_{m}
is independent of the choice of residue class for

. Thus, in (
10.23

and
b^{\prime}\bmod d^{\prime}
. Keeping in mind the future calculations, we choose the residue classes such that

(10.25)

As example, we can take
d^{\prime 2}/d+d<b\leq d^{\prime 2}/d+2d
and
0\leq b^{\prime}\leq d^{\prime}
.

The geometric side of the index one Jacobi BK is given by (see
[
55
, pp. 184]
)

(10.26)

B_{J}(\tau_{1},z_{1};\tau_{2},z_{2})=C_{k}\ \sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)}(\Theta|^{(1)}_{k}\gamma)(\tau_{1},z_{1};\tau_{2},z_{2}),

where
\Theta|^{(1)}\gamma
indicates the actions of
\gamma
with respect to the first set of variables, and

(10.27)

\displaystyle\Theta=\Theta_{k}(\tau_{1},z_{1};\tau_{2},z_{2})

\displaystyle:=(\tau_{1}-\bar{\tau}_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}-k}\sum\nolimits_{\eta\in\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}}\theta_{\eta}(\tau_{1},z_{1})\overline{\theta_{\eta}(\tau_{2},z_{2})},\quad\quad\text{and}

(10.28)

\displaystyle\theta_{\eta}(\tau,z)

\displaystyle:=\sum\nolimits_{r\in\mathbf{Z}}e((r+\eta)^{2}\tau+2(\eta+r)z).

Let
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})

(10.29)

\theta_{\mu}\left(\gamma(\tau,z)\right)(c\tau+d)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e\left(-cz^{2}(c\tau+d)^{-1}\right)=\sum\nolimits_{\eta\in\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}}\varepsilon(\eta,\mu;\gamma)\theta_{\eta}(\tau,z),

where
\varepsilon(\eta,\mu;\gamma)
are complex numbers such that
\rho(\gamma):=\left(\varepsilon(\nu,\mu;\gamma)\right)_{\nu,\mu}
is a unitary matrix (see e.g.,
[
7
]
). The Bergman kernel
B_{J}(\tau_{1},z_{1};\tau_{2},z_{2})
can now be written as

(10.30)

\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)}\ \left(j(\gamma,\tau_{1})(\gamma(\tau_{1})-\overline{\tau_{2}})\right)^{-k+\frac{1}{2}}\sum\nolimits_{\eta,\mu}\varepsilon(\eta,\mu;\gamma)\theta_{\eta}(\tau_{1},z_{1})\overline{\theta_{\mu}(\tau_{2},z_{2})}.

From (
10.30
), we can bound
B_{J}(\tau_{1},z_{1};\tau_{2},z_{2})
by

(10.31)

\ll\sum\nolimits_{\gamma}\left|j(\gamma,\tau_{1})(\gamma(\tau_{1})-\overline{\tau_{2}})\right|^{-k+\frac{1}{2}}\big|\sum\nolimits_{\nu,\mu}\varepsilon(\nu,\mu;\gamma)\theta_{\nu}(\tau_{1},z_{1})\overline{\theta_{\mu}(\tau_{2},z_{2})}\big|.

Let
\rho(\gamma)
be as above and
\Theta=(\theta_{\mu})_{\mu}
. Then the sum over
\nu,\mu
is

(10.32)

\displaystyle=\left|\langle\rho(\gamma)\Theta(\tau_{1},z_{1}),\Theta(\tau_{2},z_{2})\rangle\right|{\ll}\langle\rho(\gamma)\Theta(\tau_{1},z_{1}),\rho(\gamma)\Theta(\tau_{1},z_{1})\rangle^{1/2}\langle\Theta(\tau_{2},z_{2}),\Theta(\tau_{2},z_{2})\rangle^{1/2},

where we use Cauchy-Schwarz for the first inequality, and the unitary property of
\rho_{m}(\gamma)
for the second. Here

denotes the standard inner product on Euclidean space.

Now we have that

(10.33)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\mu}|\theta_{\mu}(\tau,z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\ v^{-1/2}e^{4\pi y^{2}/v}(1+v^{1/2})(1+v^{-1/2})=e^{4\pi y^{2}/v}(1+v^{-1/2})^{2}.

Thus

(10.34)

\big|\sum\nolimits_{\nu,\mu}\varepsilon(\nu,\mu;\gamma)\theta_{\nu}(\tau_{1},z_{1})\overline{\theta_{\mu}(\tau_{2},z_{2})}\big|\ll e^{2\pi(y_{1}^{2}/v_{1}+y_{2}^{2}/v_{2})}(1+v_{1}^{-1/2})(1+v_{2}^{-1/2}).

In our case,
\tau_{1}=(a\tau+b)/d
,
\tau_{2}=(a^{\prime}\tau+b^{\prime})/d^{\prime}
and we have
ad=m
,
a^{\prime}d^{\prime}=m
. Thus the RHS can be written as

(10.35)

e^{(...)}\left(1+d(mv)^{-1/2}\right)\left(1+d^{\prime}(mv)^{-1/2}\right).

Now we bound the sum over
\gamma
in (
10.31
). Write
r=k-\frac{1}{2}
and define

(10.36)

M(\alpha_{m}\tau,\beta_{m}\tau):=\mathrm{Im}(\beta_{m}\tau)^{-r}\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)}\,\big|(\gamma\alpha_{m}\tau-\overline{\beta_{m}\tau})j(\gamma,\alpha_{m}\tau)\mathrm{Im}(\beta_{m}\tau)^{-1}\big|^{-r}.

10.4.1.
The counting argument.

First, for any
g\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
let us define

(10.37)

A_{g}(\tau,w):=(g\tau-\overline{w})j(g,\tau)\mathrm{Im}(w)^{-1},

For the sake of simplicity, when
\tau=w
, we write
A_{g}(\tau):=A_{g}(\tau,\tau)
.

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
\alpha_{m}
,
\beta_{m}
and
A_{g}(\tau,w)
be as above. Then we have

(10.38)

A_{\gamma}(\alpha_{m}\tau,\beta_{m}\tau)=A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau).

Proof.

Let us write
u_{m}+iv_{m}=w_{m}:=\beta_{m}\tau
and

(10.39)

\displaystyle A_{\gamma}(\alpha_{m}\tau,w_{m}):=(\gamma\alpha_{m}\tau-\overline{w_{m}})j(\gamma,\alpha_{m}\tau)v_{m}^{-1}.

Using the cocycle conditions, we see that

(10.40)

\displaystyle A_{\gamma}(\alpha_{m}\tau,w_{m})

\displaystyle=A_{\gamma}(\alpha_{m}\beta_{m}^{-1}w_{m},w_{m})=(\gamma\alpha_{m}\beta_{m}^{-1}w_{m}-\overline{w_{m}})j(\gamma,\alpha_{m}\beta_{m}^{-1}w_{m})v_{m}^{-1}

(10.41)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{\prime}}{d}(\gamma\alpha_{m}\beta_{m}^{-1}w_{m}-\overline{w_{m}})j(\gamma\alpha_{m}\beta_{m}^{-1},w_{m})v_{m}^{-1}=\frac{d^{\prime}}{d}A_{\gamma\alpha_{m}\beta_{m}^{-1}}(w_{m},w_{m}).

Next, we note that
\beta_{m}\tau=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\beta_{m})\tau
and for any
M\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
,
A_{g}(M\tau,M\tau)=A_{M^{-1}gM}(\tau,\tau)
. Thus we get that

(10.42)

\displaystyle A_{\gamma\alpha_{m}\beta_{m}^{-1}}(\beta_{m}\tau,\beta_{m}\tau)=A_{\gamma\alpha_{m}\beta_{m}^{-1}}((\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\beta_{m})\tau,(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\beta_{m})\tau)=A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau).

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We need global lower bounds on the quantities
A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)
. Towards this, we prove the following.

Lemma 10.3
.

Let
A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)
be as above. Then we have

(10.43)

|A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)|\geq 2.

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity, let us write
g=\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}
and
u_{m}^{\prime}+iv_{m}^{\prime}=w_{m}:=g\tau
.
Then we have that

(10.44)

\left|(w_{m}-\overline{\tau})j(g,\tau)v^{-1}\right|=|(w_{m}-\overline{\tau})(v_{m}v)^{-1/2}|\geq(v_{m}+v)/(v_{m}v)^{1/2}.

Now we use the AM-GM inequality to complete the proof. ∎

We now define a counting function
\mathcal{C}(\tau,m,\delta)
. Suitable bounds on it will be instrumental in handling the Type 2 region.

(10.45)

\mathcal{C}(\tau,m,\delta):=\#\{(\gamma,\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}):\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N),|A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)|<\delta\}.

Next, from the Lemma
10.3
, we see that
\mathcal{C}(\tau,m,\delta)=0
if
\delta<2
.

Let
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{\gamma}&b_{\gamma}\\
c_{\gamma}&d_{\gamma}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Consider the case when
c_{\gamma}=0
. In this case,
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&b_{\gamma}\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}d^{\prime}/d&m^{-1}(b_{\gamma}dd^{\prime}-b^{\prime}d+bd^{\prime})\\
0&d/d^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Thus
|A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)|<\delta
implies

(10.46)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d^{\prime}}{d}\tau+m^{-1}(b_{\gamma}dd^{\prime}-b^{\prime}d+bd^{\prime})-\frac{d}{d^{\prime}}\overline{\tau}\right|\leq v\delta.

Separating real and imaginary parts, we get that

(10.47)

\displaystyle|\frac{d^{\prime}}{d}v+\frac{d}{d^{\prime}}v|\leq v\delta\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\frac{d^{\prime}}{d}u-\frac{d}{d^{\prime}}u+m^{-1}(b_{\gamma}dd^{\prime}-b^{\prime}d+bd^{\prime})\right|\leq v\delta.

From (
10.47
), the number of choices for
b,b^{\prime}
is seen to be

(10.48)

\displaystyle 1+\frac{v\delta m}{d^{\prime}},\quad 1+\frac{v\delta m}{d}\text{ respectively.}

Further,
m^{-1}b_{\gamma}dd^{\prime}
is in an interval of length
v\delta
, thus there are
1+mv\delta/dd^{\prime}
such
b_{\gamma}
. Thus the number of
(\gamma,\alpha_{m},\beta_{m})
is at most

(10.49)

\displaystyle\sum_{d^{\prime},d|m}(1+\frac{mv\delta}{d^{\prime}})(1+\frac{mv\delta}{d})\left(1+\frac{mv\delta}{dd^{\prime}}\right).

Note that
mv\leq mv^{\prime}\ll N^{\epsilon}
. Thus in this case, we get

(10.50)

\mathcal{C}(\tau,m,\delta)|_{c_{\gamma}=0}\ll N^{\epsilon}\delta^{3}\sum_{d,d^{\prime}|m}1\ll N^{\epsilon}\delta^{3}.

Next, we consider the case
c_{\gamma}\neq 0
. We have that

(10.51)

\displaystyle\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{-c_{\gamma}b^{\prime}+a_{\gamma}d^{\prime}}{d}&\frac{-c_{\gamma}bb^{\prime}-d_{\gamma}b^{\prime}d+a_{\gamma}bd^{\prime}+b_{\gamma}dd^{\prime}}{m}\\
\frac{mc_{\gamma}}{dd^{\prime}}&\frac{c_{\gamma}b+d_{\gamma}d}{d^{\prime}}\end{smallmatrix}\right).

Now,
|A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)|<\delta
implies that
\left|(A\tau+B-C|\tau|^{2}-D\overline{\tau})v^{-1}\right|<\delta
. On separating the real and imaginary parts, we get

(10.52)

\displaystyle|A+D|<\delta;\quad|Au+B-C|\tau|^{2}-Du|<\delta v.

From
|A+D|<\delta
, we get

(10.53)

\big|\frac{-c_{\gamma}b^{\prime}+a_{\gamma}d^{\prime}}{d}+\frac{c_{\gamma}b+d_{\gamma}d}{d^{\prime}}\big|<\delta.

We first note that
a_{\gamma}d_{\gamma}\equiv 1\mod N
and thus
a_{\gamma}d_{\gamma}\neq 0
. By noting that
a_{\gamma}d^{\prime}/d
and
d_{\gamma}d/d^{\prime}
lie in a interval length
\delta
, we get that
\displaystyle\#\{d_{\gamma}\}\ll\frac{d^{\prime}\delta}{d}
and
\displaystyle\#\{a_{\gamma}\}\ll\frac{d\delta}{d^{\prime}}
.

We can without loss assume that both the above counts are at least
\geq 1

\gamma
and the count is

. These in unison then give the strong bounds
\displaystyle d/d^{\prime}\ll\delta,\quad d^{\prime}/d\ll\delta

\displaystyle\#\{a_{\gamma},d_{\gamma}\}\ll\delta^{2}
.

Next, to count the

\delta

(10.54)

\#\{c_{\gamma}\}\leq\frac{\delta dd^{\prime}}{N(db-d^{\prime}b^{\prime})}\leq\frac{\delta dd^{\prime}}{Nd^{2}}\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{N}.

Combining the counts of
a_{\gamma}
,
b_{\gamma}
and

The number of choices for

is seen to be at most

(10.55)

\displaystyle 1+\frac{d\delta}{c_{\gamma}}\leq 1+\frac{d\delta}{N}.

Thus the total count for
(\alpha_{m},\beta_{m})

(10.56)

\displaystyle\sum_{d|m}d(1+\frac{d\delta}{N})\sum_{d^{\prime}|m}1\ll m+\frac{m^{2}\delta}{N}.

Thus we have the following estimate for the total count.

(10.57)

\mathcal{C}(\tau,m,\delta)|_{c_{\gamma}\neq 0}\ll\left(m+\frac{m^{2}\delta}{N}\right)\cdot\frac{\delta^{4}}{N}.

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
Y\gg 1/N
be reduced and
\mathcal{C}(\tau,m,\delta)
be as above. Then for

We now return to estimating the quantity
\mathcal{M}(\tau,z)
. Recall that

(10.59)

\mathcal{M}(\tau,z)\ll\sum_{m}\frac{v^{k}e^{-4\pi my^{2}/v}}{m^{-k+1}}\big|\sum_{\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}}(dd^{\prime})^{-k}B_{J}(\alpha_{m}\tau,mz/d;\beta_{m}\tau,mz/d^{\prime})\big|.

Using (
10.31
) and (
10.35
) we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\tau,z)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m}\frac{v^{k}}{m^{-k+1}}\sum_{\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}}(dd^{\prime})^{-k}\left(1+\frac{d}{(mv)^{1/2}}\right)\left(1+\frac{d^{\prime}}{(mv)^{1/2}}\right)\left|M(\alpha_{m}\tau,\beta_{m}\tau)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\sum_{\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}}\frac{v^{1/2}}{m^{1/2}}\left(\frac{1}{d^{1/2}}+\frac{d^{1/2}}{(mv)^{1/2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{d^{1/2}}+\frac{d^{\prime 1/2}}{(mv)^{1/2}}\right)\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)}\,\big|A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)|^{-r}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{m}\sum_{\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}}\frac{v^{1/2}}{m^{1/2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{v^{1/2}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)}\,\big|A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)|^{-r}

\displaystyle\ll v^{-1/2}\sum_{m}\frac{1}{m^{1/2}}\sum_{\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)}\,\big|A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)|^{-r}.

We split the sum over
(\gamma,\alpha_{m},\beta_{m})
into dyadic intervals as follows.

(10.61)

\begin{split}\sum_{\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)}\,\big|A_{\beta_{m}^{-1}\gamma\alpha_{m}}(\tau)|^{-r}&\ll\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}2^{-rt}\mathcal{C}(\tau,m,2^{t+1})\\

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}(\tau,z)&\ll N^{\epsilon}v^{-1/2}\sum_{m}\frac{1}{m^{1/2}}\left(1+\frac{m}{N}+\frac{m^{2}}{N^{2}}\right)\ll N^{\epsilon}\left(\frac{1}{(vv^{\prime})^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{Nv^{\prime 3/2}}+\frac{1}{N^{2}v^{\prime 5/2}}\right)\\

Now, coming back to the case

, we get

(10.63)

\sup_{Z\in\mathbf{H}_{2},Y\gg p^{-1}}\sum\nolimits_{F\in\mathcal{B}^{SK,new}(p)}\frac{\det(Y)^{k}|F(Z)|^{2}}{\left\lVert F\right\rVert^{2}_{p}}\ll p^{-2}\sup|\mathcal{M}(\tau,z)|\ll p^{-1+\epsilon}.

Now we bound the size of oldspace

The region

. Here
h
denotes the width of the cusp at
\infty
and
P_{k,\sigma}(n)
is the
n
-th Fourier coefficient of the
n
-th Poincaré series at
\infty
. In our case, we have
h=N/(N^{2}/M^{2},N)
. Now, by the choice of
\sigma
and
M
, we have
M^{2}|N
. Thus
h=1
and this implies that (see
[
29
, 3.19]
)

(13.21)

P_{k,\sigma}(n)=1+2\pi i^{-k}\sum\nolimits_{c>0}c^{-1}S(n,n,c)J_{k-1}(4\pi n/c),

where
S(n,n,c)
is the Kloosterman sum at the cusp defined by
\sigma
:

S(n,n,c)=\sum_{\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&*\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{\sigma}/\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})_{\infty}}e_{c}(an+dn).

Now from the previous discussions we know that
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{\sigma}
and
\sigma^{-1}\Gamma_{0}(N)\sigma\subset\Gamma_{0}(N/M)
. Thus the coset representatives for
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{\sigma}/\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})_{\infty}
is given by the set (see
[
29
, Proposition 2.7]
)

(13.22)

\{\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&*\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{\sigma}:(c,d)=1,\frac{N}{M}|c,a,d\bmod c;ad\equiv 1\bmod c\}.

Now consider the sum over
c
. Using the bound
J_{\ell}(x)\leq\min\{1,\frac{(x/2)^{\ell}}{\Gamma(\ell+1)}\}
for the Bessel function and splitting the sum over
c
into dyadic intervals, we note that the sum over
c
is bounded by

(13.23)

\displaystyle\sum_{N/M\leq c\leq 2\pi n}c^{-1}|S(n,n,c)|+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{2^{j}\cdot 2\pi n<c\leq 2^{j+1}\cdot 2\pi n}c^{-1}|S(n,n,c)|\frac{(2\pi n/c)^{k-1}}{\Gamma(k)}.

Now using the bound
\sum_{c\leq X}c^{-1}{S(n,n,c)}\leq MX/N
(see
[
29
, (4.2)]
) we see that the sum over
c
is (provided
k>2
)

(13.24)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{Mn}{N}+\frac{Mn}{N\Gamma(k)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{j(k-1)-j-1}}\ll\frac{Mn}{N}.

This implies the following bound on
P_{k,\sigma}(n)
.

(13.25)

P_{k,\sigma}(n)=1+O(N^{-1}Mn).

Thus, by the same analysis as in (
13.7
), we see that when
v\geq 1
,

(13.26)

\mathbb{B}_{k,\sigma^{-1}\Gamma_{0}(N)\sigma}(\tau)\ll_{k}\left(1+N^{-1}M\right)\ll_{k}1.

13.3.
The size of
U(p)
images

Let
N
be any positive integer and
p
be prime co-prime to
N
. In this section we obtain the size of
U(p)\left(S_{k}(N)\right)
. First, we note that
U(p)=T(p)-p^{k/2-1}B_{p}
, where
B_{p}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Thus for any eigenform
f
of level
1
, we have
U(p)f=\lambda_{f}(p)f-p^{k-1}f(pz)
. Next, we have the relation (see e.g.,
[
3
]
)

(13.27)

\left\lVert U(p)f\right\rVert_{p}^{2}=\left(p^{k-2}+\frac{(p-1)}{(p+1)}|\lambda_{f}(p)|^{2}\right)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{p}^{2}.

Thus

(13.28)

\displaystyle\sum_{f}\frac{v^{k}|U(p)f|^{2}}{\left\lVert U(p)f\right\rVert_{p}^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\lambda_{f}(p)^{2}}{\left(p^{k-2}+\frac{(p-1)}{(p+1)}|\lambda_{f}(p)^{2}|\right)}\sum_{f}\frac{v^{k}|f|^{2}}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{p}^{2}}+\frac{p^{2k-2}}{\left(p^{k-2}+\frac{(p-1)}{(p+1)}|\lambda_{f}(p)|^{2}\right)}\sum_{f}\frac{v^{k}|f(pz)|^{2}}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{p}^{2}}

(13.29)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{p+1}\sum_{f}\frac{v^{k}|f|^{2}}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}+\frac{p^{2k-2}}{p^{k-2}}\frac{1}{p+1}\cdot p^{-k}\sum_{f}\frac{v^{k}|f(z)|^{2}}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{p}.

This gives us that
\sup(U(p)\left(S_{k}(N)\right))\ll 1/p
.

13.4.
Handling congruence subgroups

Let us denote by
i_{\Gamma}
the index of a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
and
\mathcal{B}(\Gamma)
denote an orthonormal basis for
S_{k}(\Gamma)
. Let

(13.30)

\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(z)=\sum\nolimits_{f\in\mathcal{B}(\Gamma)}y^{k}|f(z)|^{2}.

We suppose that
\Gamma=\Gamma_{N}
. For each
f
as above, consider

(13.31)

g_{f}(z)=f|_{k}\alpha_{N},

where
f|_{k}\alpha_{N}=N^{k/2}f(Nz)
. It is easy to see that
g\in S_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N^{2}))
more precisely,
g\in S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N^{2}),\chi^{\prime})
where
\chi^{\prime}
is induced from a Dirichlet character
\chi\bmod N
. To see this, recall the decomposition of
S_{k}(\Gamma(N))
by characters
\chi\bmod N
and note that each eigenspace is mapped under
\alpha_{N}
to
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N^{2}),\chi^{\prime})
isomorphically.
Moreover, this operation preserves orthogonality.
Now

(13.32)

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbf{H}}\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(z)=\sup_{z\in\mathbf{H}}\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Nz)=\sup_{z\in\mathbf{H}}\sum\nolimits_{f}y^{k}|g_{f}(z)|^{2}.

Notice that (see
[
43
, III, Theorem 4.1]
),

\displaystyle i_{\Gamma}\langle g_{f},g_{f}\rangle=i_{\Gamma_{0}(N^{2})}\langle f,f\rangle
.
Therefore,

(13.33)

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in H}\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(z)\leq\frac{i_{\Gamma_{0}(N^{2})}}{i_{\Gamma}}\sup_{z\in\mathbf{H}}\sum\nolimits_{\chi}\mathbb{B}_{\kappa,\Gamma_{0}(4N),\chi}(z)\ll\frac{i_{\Gamma_{0}(N^{2})}}{i_{\Gamma}}\cdot\phi(N)\ll 1.

In the above, we have tacitly used the fact that
\sup\mathbb{B}_{\kappa,\Gamma_{0}(4N),\chi}\ll 1
– whose proof can be easily deduced from our calculations with
\chi=1
verbatim, and is left to the reader. ∎
