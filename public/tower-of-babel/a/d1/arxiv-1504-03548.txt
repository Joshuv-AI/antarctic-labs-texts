Further Properties and Applications of Koszul Pairs
Adrian MANEA and Dragoş ŞTEFAN

Abstract
Koszul pairs were introduced in [arXiv:1011.4243] as an instrument for the study of Koszul rings. In this paper, we continue the enquiry of such pairs, focusing on the description of the second component, as a follow-up of the study in [arXiv:1605.05458]. As such, we introduce Koszul corings and prove several equivalent characterizations for them. As applications, in the case of locally finite R R -rings, we show that a graded R R -ring is Koszul if and only if its left (or right) graded dual coring is Koszul. Finally, for finite graded posets, we obtain that the respective incidence ring is Koszul if and only if the incidence coring is so.

Introduction

The classical approach on Koszul rings is due to
[
1
]
, which defines an
\mathbb{N}
-graded ring
A=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A^{n}
to be Koszul if and only if
A^{0}
is a semisimple ring and there exists a resolution
P_{\bullet}
of
A^{0}
by projective graded left
A
-modules such that each term
P_{n}
is generated by its homogeneous component of degree
n
. Koszul rings evolved as natural generalisations of Koszul algebras, which in turn were discovered by Priddy in
[
15
]
. Since their early developments, Koszul algebras and rings proved to be very useful tools in various fields of mathematics, as are representation theory, algebraic geometry, algebraic topology, quantum groups, combinatorics and many more. A comprehensive read is, for example,
[
13
]
and the references therein.

While studying certain cohomological properties of Koszul rings in
[
5
]
, the authors were led to a new notion, the so-called
Koszul pairs
, which we explain in brief. Let
R
be a semisimple ring. A graded
R
-ring is a graded algebra in the tensor category of
R
-bimodules with respect to the tensor product (of bimodules). A graded
R
-ring
A=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A^{n}
is called
connected
if
A^{0}=R
. Connected graded
R
-corings are defined by duality. By definition, an
almost Koszul pair
consists of a graded connected
R
-ring
A
and a graded connected
R
-coring
C
, together with an
R
-bimodule isomorphism
\theta_{A,C}\colon C_{1}\to A^{1}
. These data must be compatible, in the sense that the composition of the three maps below must be zero:

\displaystyle C_{2}\xrightarrow{\Delta}C_{1}\otimes C_{1}\xrightarrow{\theta_{C,A}\otimes\theta_{C,A}}A^{1}\otimes A^{1}\xrightarrow{\mu^{1,1}}A^{2},

(1.1)

where
\Delta_{1,1}
and
\mu^{1,1}
denote the components of (co)multiplication maps for
C
and
A
, respectively.

By
[
5
]
, to an almost-Koszul pair correspond three chain complexes and three cochain complexes, which measure how far is
A
from being a Koszul ring. More precisely, an important feature of an almost Koszul pair is that any of the corresponding six complexes is exact if and only if all of them are so. In this case, the pair
(A,C)
is called
Koszul
. Furthermore, one proves that a connected graded
R
-ring
A
is Koszul if and only if there exists a connected graded
R
-coring
C
such that
(A,C)
is a Koszul pair.

In this paper, we continue the work on Koszul pairs and their applications, having in view two aims. Firstly, following our results in
[
9
]
which include several characterizations Koszul rings, we prove that after properly defining the dual notion, that of
Koszul corings
, an appropriate characterization theorem holds true. Furthermore, we obtain a new proof of the fact that a Koszul
R
-ring is quadratic and also a corresponding result for corings. In this sense, we refer to Theorem
4.4
for the general properties of Koszul corings and to Corollary
4.10
for quadraticity.

As applications, we remark that in the locally finite case, as one could expect, a ring is Koszul if and only if its graded dual coring is so, cf. Theorem
5.2
. In the proof of this result, we use again some Koszul pairs involving the graded duals as the starting structures. In particular, the incidence ring of a graded poset is Koszul if and only if its incidence coring is Koszul as well, as per Theorem
6.1
. In this class of finite graded posets, we have obtained in
[
9
]
some particular examples of Koszul posets and in this article we show that they also provide examples of Koszul corings. Taking this into account, this paper can be seen as an announced follow-up of
[
9
]
.

More examples of Koszul corings, related to Hopf algebras, will be considered in a forthcoming paper.

Since the literature is abundant on this subject, some remarks regarding the scope and position of the present paper are due. First off, a vast majority of the theory is known and developed for the algebra or
R
-rings case. In this respect, we introduce and study the dual case, that of corings. By these means, we obtain new insights on the theory. Moreover, although recent developments of the theory have dealt with a categorical setting (e.g.,
[
6
]
or
[
11
]
), the present study is, in majority, self-contained, by using the tool of Koszul pairs that the second author most introduced in
[
5
]
.

We acknowledge as well that further generalizations on the theory of Koszul rings were treated, for example, in
[
4
,
7
,
8
]
, eliminating the semisimplicity condition on the part of degree zero. Many other directions which relax the starting basic assumptions were considered in the literature (as it is the case, for example, in
[
2
]
and some other articles of the same author). However, as remarked in the previous paragraph, the present article focuses on the dualization of the more “classical” case by means of Koszul pairs. This way, the overlap or even connection with the references above is at its minimum and the subjects treated therein exceed the purposes of the present paper.

The article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the preliminary notions and results regarding Koszul pairs that are needed in the paper. Section
3
briefly revisits the case of Koszul
R
-rings, whereas Section
4
introduces and discusses Koszul
R
-corings. Sections
5
and
6
study applications and examples.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we recall some basic concepts and notations from
[
5
]
and then we shall prove some new preliminary results, which are needed later on.

2.1
Connected (co)rings

Let
R
be a semisimple ring that we fix throughout the article. Since we will always work with algebras and coalgebras in the tensor category of
R
-bimodules, an unadorned tensor product
\otimes
will mean
\otimes_{R}
. Let
V
be an
R
-bimodule. The notation
V^{(n)}
will be used for the
n
-th tensor power
V\otimes\cdots\otimes V
. Conventionally,
V^{(0)}=R
. Similarly, for any bimodule morphism
f\colon M\to N
, the tensor product
f\otimes\cdots\otimes f
with
n
factors will be denoted by
f^{(n)}
. For a set
X
, the identity morphism will be denoted by
\operatorname{Id}_{X}
or simpler,
\operatorname{I}_{X}
or even
X
when there is no risk of confusion.

An
\mathbb{N}
-graded algebra
A=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A^{n}
is called an
R
-ring
if it is an algebra in the tensor category of
R
-bimodules and it is connected if
A^{0}=R
. Dually, a connected
R
-coring is a graded coalgebra
C=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the category of
R
-bimodules such that
C_{0}=R
. The augmentation ideal of
A
will be denoted by
A_{+}=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}}A^{n}
and similarly for
C
. Note that the comultiplication map
\Delta
induces a canonical coassociative map
\Delta_{+}\colon C_{+}\to C_{+}\otimes C_{+}
, where
C_{+}
can also be viewed as
C/R
.

For a graded connected
R
-ring
A
, the multiplication
\mu
is defined by some maps
\mu^{p,q}\colon

A^{p}\otimes A^{q}\to A^{p+q}
. When all of these maps are surjective, for all
p,q\geq 0
, we say that
A
is
strongly graded
. Equivalently,
A
is strongly graded if and only if the iterated multiplication
\mu_{n}\colon(A^{1})^{(n)}\to A^{n}
is surjective for all
n\geq 2
.

Similarly, for a graded connected
R
-coring
C
, the comultiplication is defined by some
R
-bimodule maps
\Delta_{p,q}\colon C_{p+q}\to C_{p}\otimes C_{q}
and the coring
C
is called
strongly graded
if and only if all of them are injective.

Let
\Delta^{1}=\operatorname{Id}_{C_{1}}
and for
n\geq 2
define a map
\Delta^{n}\colon C_{n}\to C_{1}^{(n)}
by the recurrence relation

\displaystyle\Delta^{n}=\big(\operatorname{Id}_{C_{1}}\otimes\Delta^{n-1}\big)\circ\Delta_{1,n-1}.

It is immediate from the definition and the coassociativity property that the following equality holds true

\displaystyle\Delta^{p+q}=(\Delta^{p}\otimes\Delta^{q})\circ\Delta_{p,q}.

Another remark is that the strong grading on
C
can be seen to be equivalent to the injectivity of all of the maps
\Delta^{n}
,
\forall\,n
and further that
\Delta_{1,n}
is injective for all
n
if and only if
\Delta_{n,1}
is injective for all
n
.

When
C
is a connected
R
-coring, the unit of
R
is a group-like element, that is
\Delta(1)=1\otimes 1
, cf.
[
5
]
. Therefore, we can speak of primitive elements of
C
, namely those
c\in C
for which
\Delta(c)=1\otimes c+c\otimes 1
. The set of all primitive elements in
C
contains
C_{1}
and will be denoted by
PC
. In general, the inclusion of
C_{1}
in
PC
is strict, but as per
[
9
, Lemma 1.4]
,
PC=C_{1}
if and only if
C
is strongly graded.

Indecomposable elements of an augmented
R
-ring correspond by duality to primitive elements. They were introduced by May in
[
10
]
and we will see how they relate to the strong grading on an
R
-ring. In the graded case, the
R
-bimodule
QA
of indecomposable elements is defined by the exact sequence

\displaystyle A_{+}\otimes A_{+}\xrightarrow{\mu}A_{+}\to QA\to 0.

There is a canonical morphism
A^{1}\to QA
, which maps
a\in A^{1}
to its class
a+A_{+}^{2}\in QA
. This map has a left inverse, induced by the projection
A_{+}\to A^{1}
.

There is a canonical morphism
A^{1}\to QA
which maps an element in
A^{1}
to its class modulo the square of the augmentation ideal
A_{+}
in
QA
. This map has a left inverse, induced by the projection
A_{+}\to A^{1}
.

We can prove a first result, which is a dual version of
[
9
, Lemma 1.4]
and which shows the role of
QA
, the bimodule of indecomposable elements in an
R
-ring. Moreover, the following lemma will be used for obtaining our main characterization theorem for Koszul corings (i.e., Theorem
4.4
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A
be a graded and connected
R
-ring.

1.

A
is strongly graded if and only if the canonical map
QA\to A^{1}
is injective, if and only if the canonical map
A^{1}\to QA
is surjective.

2.

If
A
is strongly graded,
B
is a connected graded
R
-ring and
g\colon B\rightarrow A
is a morphism of graded
R
-rings such that its components
g^{0}
and
g^{1}
are surjective, then
g
is surjective.

3.

Let
A=\oplus_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}A^{n,m}
be bigraded. If
\operatorname{gr}A
is strongly graded and
A^{n,m}=0
for
n=0,1
and all
m\neq n
, then
A^{n,m}=0
for all
n\geq 2
,
m\neq n
.

Proof.

Since
QA\to A^{1}
is a left inverse of
A^{1}\to QA
it follows that the former map is injective if and only if the latter is surjective. On the other hand,
A
is strongly graded if and only if
A_{n}\subseteq A_{+}^{2}
, for all
n\geq 2
. Consequently, the first statement holds, as the above inclusions are true if and only if the map
A^{1}\to QA
is surjective.

The proof of the second statement follows as in the case of corings
[
9
, Lemma 1.4]
, using the diagram below

\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces B_{n}\otimes B_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mu^{n,1}_{B}}
\scriptstyle{g_{n}\otimes g_{1}}
\textstyle{B_{n+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{g_{n+1}}
\textstyle{A_{n}\otimes A_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mu^{n,1}_{A}}
\textstyle{A_{n+1}.}

For proving the last part of the lemma we define
\operatorname{Diag}(A)
to be graded subring of
\operatorname{gr}(A)
given by
\operatorname{Diag}(A)=\oplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}A^{n,n}
. Let
\iota\colon\operatorname{Diag}(A)\to\operatorname{gr}(A)
denote the inclusion map. Obviously, by the standing assumptions,
\iota^{0}
and
\iota^{1}
are surjective maps. Hence, by the second part of the lemma,
\iota
is surjective. Thus
A^{n,m}
must be zero for all
n
and
m
, with
n\neq m
.
∎

2.2
The
\boldsymbol{R}
-ring
\boldsymbol{\langle V,W\rangle}
and the
\boldsymbol{R}
-coring
\boldsymbol{\{V,W\}}

For an
R
-bimodule
V
and a sub-bimodule
W\subseteq V\otimes V
, we define the
R
-ring
\langle V,W\rangle
to be the quotient
T^{a}_{R}(V)/\langle W\rangle
of the tensor algebra of the
R
-bimodule
V
by the two-sided ideal generated by
W
. Note that
\langle W\rangle=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\langle W\rangle^{n}
, where

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}V^{(i-1)}\otimes W\otimes V^{(n-i-1)}.

For
V
and
W
as above, one also constructs a graded
R
-coring
\{V,W\}
by taking
\{V,W\}_{0}=R
and
\{V,W\}_{1}=V
. For all
n\geq 2
define

\displaystyle\{V,W\}_{n}=\bigcap_{p=1}^{n-1}V^{(p-1)}\otimes W\otimes V^{(n-p-1)}.

As it is shown in
[
5
]
, the direct sum
\{V,W\}=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{V,W\}_{n}
is a graded subcoring of
T^{c}_{R}(V)
, the tensor coalgebra of the
R
-bimodule
V
.

If
A
is a connected graded
R
-ring and
C
is a connected graded
R
-coring then the graded coring
\{A^{1},\Ker\mu^{1,1}\}
and the graded ring
\langle C_{1},\im\Delta_{1,1}\rangle
will be denoted by
A^{!}
and
C^{!}
, respectively and called the
shriek coring
and the
shriek ring
, respectively. The homogeneous component of degree
n
of
A^{!}
will be denoted by
A^{!}_{n}
. To simplify the notation, for the ring
C^{!}
we shall write
C^{!}_{n}
instead of
(C^{!})^{n}
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the literature, the shriek construction (also known as
the quadratic dual

[
1
, Section 2.8]
, for example) is defined and used slightly different that our setting. However, we note that throughout this paper, the only meaning of the shriek structures is that introduced above. Note that, unlike the classical case of
[
1
]
, for example, here the shriek construction changes an
R
-ring into an
R
-coring and viceversa.

2.3
Bigraded corings

Some basic facts regarding bigraded corings are due, since many of the structures which we will use are of this kind.

An
R
-coring
C
is called
bigraded
if it has a decomposition as a direct sum of
R
-bimodules
C=\oplus_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}C_{n,m}
such that its comultiplication induces a collection of maps

\displaystyle C_{n+n^{\prime},m+m^{\prime}}\xrightarrow{\Delta_{n,m}^{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}C_{n,m}\otimes C_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}.

For this case, coassociativity means that the diagram below is commutative, for all positive integers
m
,
n
,
p
,
m^{\prime}
,
n^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime}
,

\textstyle{C_{n+m+p,n^{\prime}+m^{\prime}+p^{\prime}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Delta_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m+p,m^{\prime}+p^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\Delta_{n+m,n^{\prime}+m^{\prime}}^{p,p^{\prime}}}
\textstyle{C_{n+m,n^{\prime}+m^{\prime}}\otimes C_{p,p^{\prime}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Delta_{n,n^{\prime}}^{m,m^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{I}_{C_{p,p^{\prime}}}}
\textstyle{C_{n,n^{\prime}}\otimes C_{m+p,m^{\prime}+p^{\prime}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\operatorname{I}_{C_{n,n^{\prime}}}\otimes\Delta_{m,m^{\prime}}^{p,p^{\prime}}}
\textstyle{C_{n,n^{\prime}}\otimes C_{m,m^{\prime}}\otimes C_{p,p^{\prime}}.}

By definition, we also impose to the counit to vanish on
C_{n,m}
, provided that either
n>0
or
m>0
.

Starting with a bigraded coring, one can associate to it a graded coring
\operatorname{gr}(C)
, whose homogeneous component of degree
n
is
\operatorname{gr}_{n}(C)=\oplus_{m}C_{n,m}
. Therefore, define
\operatorname{gr}(C):=\oplus_{n}\operatorname{gr}_{n}(C)
. We will be interested only in
connected bigraded corings
, namely those bigraded corings for which
C_{0,0}=R
and
C_{0,m}=0
, for
m>0
. Note that
C
to be connected implies
\operatorname{gr}(C)
is connected as well.

Let
C
be a (connected) bigraded coring. Define

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{n,m}=\begin{cases}C_{n,m},&n=m,\\
0,&n\neq m.\end{cases}

Then
{C^{\prime}}:=\oplus_{n,m}{C}_{n,m}^{\prime}
becomes a (connected) bigraded coring. We denote the graded coring
\operatorname{gr}({C^{\prime}})
associated to it by
\operatorname{Diag}(C)
.

Keeping the notations and the context above, there exist canonical
R
-bimodule morphisms
\pi_{n,m}\colon C_{n,m}\to C^{\prime}_{n,m}
which are identities on
C_{n,n}
and zero maps in rest. Their collection,
\pi=\{\pi_{n,m}\}_{n,m}
, defines a morphism in the category of graded corings. Since
\operatorname{Diag}(C)=\operatorname{gr}(C^{\prime})
, it follows that the map
\pi
induces a morphism at the level of graded corings, namely
\operatorname{gr}(\pi)\colon\operatorname{gr}(C)\to\operatorname{Diag}(C)
. Surely the homogeneous component of degree
n
of
\operatorname{Ker}\pi
is
\oplus_{n\neq m}C_{n,m}
.

Note that one can define by duality the corresponding notions of bigraded
R
-rings. We omit the details here, since this case is better known than the one for corings. See, for example,
[
12
]
for the differential graded setting.

2.4
The normalized (co)chain complex

We shall compute
T_{n}(A):=\Tor_{n}^{A}(R,R)
as the
n
th homology group of the normalized bar complex
(\Omega_{\bullet}(A),d_{\bullet})
, where
\Omega_{n}(A)=A_{+}^{(n)}
. The morphisms
d_{n}\colon\Omega_{n}(A)\to\Omega_{n-1}(A)
are defined by
d_{1}=0
and, for
n>1
,

\displaystyle d_{n}(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{i-1}a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{i}a_{i+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n}.

Since the normalized bar complex has a canonical structure of DG-coalgebra in the category of
R
-bimodules with respect to the comultiplication
\Delta_{p,q}(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{p+q})=(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{p})\otimes(a_{p+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{p+q})
it follows that
T(A)=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}T_{n}(A)
has a canonical structure of connected
R
-coring.

One can express the complex
\Omega_{\bullet}(A)
as a direct sum
\Omega_{\bullet}(A)=\oplus_{m\geq 0}\Omega_{\bullet}(A,m)
of subcomplexes. As in
[
9
, Section 1.6]
, we introduce the following terminology and notation. An
n
-tuple
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
is called
a positive
n
-partition of
m
if and only if
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}=m
and all
m_{i}
are positive. The set of all positive
n
-partitions of
m
will be denoted by
\mathscr{P}_{n}(m)
. Furthermore, if
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
is a positive
n
-partition and
A
is a connected
R
-ring then for the tensor product
A^{m_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes A^{m_{n}}
we shall use the notation
A^{\boldsymbol{m}}
. For a positive
n
-partition
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
of
m
, the multiplication
\mu
of
A
induces bimodule maps
\mu^{\boldsymbol{m}}\colon A^{\boldsymbol{m}}\to A^{m}
and
\mu_{\boldsymbol{m}}\colon(A^{1})^{(m_{1})}\otimes\cdots\otimes(A^{1})^{(m_{n})}\to A^{\boldsymbol{m}}
. Note that, by definition,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{m}}=\mu(m_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu(m_{n})
.

Hence, with this notation,
\Omega_{\bullet}(A,m)
is the following subcomplex of
\Omega_{\bullet}(A)
:

\displaystyle 0\xleftarrow{d_{0}^{m}}0\xleftarrow{d_{1}^{m}}\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}_{1}\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(m)}A^{\boldsymbol{m}_{1}}\xleftarrow{d_{2}^{m}}\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}_{2}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}A^{\boldsymbol{m}_{2}}\xleftarrow{d_{3}^{m}}\cdots\xleftarrow{d^{m}_{n}}\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(m)}A^{\boldsymbol{m}_{n}}\xleftarrow{\ \ \ }\cdots.

Of course, there is only one
1
-partition of
m
, namely
\boldsymbol{m}_{1}=(m)
. Thus the first direct sum in
\Omega_{\bullet}(A,m)
coincides with
A^{m}
. The homology in degree
n
of
\Omega_{\bullet}(A,m)
will be denoted either by
T_{n,m}(A)
or
\operatorname{Tor}_{n,m}^{A}(R,R)
. Clearly, we have
T(A)=\oplus_{m\geq 0}T_{n,m}(A)
and this decomposition is compatible with the coring structure, in the sense that
T(A)
is a bigraded coring with
T_{n,m}(A)
as
(n,m)
-homogeneous component.

Dually, for a connected
R
-coring
C
, the normalized bar cochain complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}\big(C\big),d^{\bullet})
is defined by
\Omega^{n}\big(C\big)=C_{+}^{(n)}
and
d^{0}=0
, while

\displaystyle d^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i-1}\I_{C_{+}^{(i-1)}}\otimes\;\Delta_{+}\otimes\I_{C_{+}^{(n-i)}}.

Here
C_{+}:=C/C_{0}
and
\Delta_{+}\colon C_{+}\to C_{+}\otimes C_{+}
is the map induced by the comultiplication of
C
. It is well-known that
E^{n}(C)=\operatorname{Ext}^{n}_{C}(R,R)
is the
n
th cohomology group of
\Omega^{\bullet}(C)
and that
E(C)=\oplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ E^{n}(C)
is a connected
R
-ring with respect to the multiplication induced by the DG-algebra structure (in the category of
R
-bimodules) on
\Omega^{\bullet}\big(C\big)
which is defined by concatenation of tensor monomials (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Section 1.1]
).

The complex
\Omega^{\bullet}(C)
is a direct sum of subcomplexes
\Omega^{\bullet}(C,m)
, that are defined as follows. For a positive
n
-partition of
m
let
C_{\boldsymbol{m}}:=C_{m_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes C_{m_{n}}
. Hence
\Omega^{\bullet}(C,m)
is the subcomplex

\displaystyle\ 0\xrightarrow{\ \ \ }0\xrightarrow{d_{m}^{0}}\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}_{1}\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(m)}C_{\boldsymbol{m}_{1}}\xrightarrow{d_{m}^{1}}\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}_{2}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}C_{\boldsymbol{m}_{2}}\xrightarrow{d_{m}^{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{d^{n-1}_{m}}\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}(m)}C_{\boldsymbol{m}_{n}}\xrightarrow{d_{m}^{n}}\cdots.

Of course,
\Omega^{1}(C,m)=C_{m}
. The homology in degree
n
of
\Omega^{\bullet}(C,m)
will be denoted either by
E^{n,m}(C)
or
\operatorname{Ext}^{n,m}_{C}(R,R)
. Clearly, we have
E(C)=\oplus_{m\geq 0}E^{n,m}(C)
and this decomposition is compatible with the ring structure in the sense that
E(C)
is a bigraded ring with
E^{n,m}(C)
as
(n,m)
-homogeneous component. For more details the reader is referred to
[
5
, Section 1.15]
.

2.5
Almost-Koszul pairs

As mentioned in the introduction, an almost Koszul pair
(A,C)
consists of a connected and graded
R
-ring
A
and a connected graded
R
-coring
C
, together with an isomorphism of
R
-bimodules
\theta_{A,C}\colon C_{1}\rightarrow A^{1}
which satisfies the relation (
1.1
). Using the graded version of Sweedler’s notation for the comultiplication of
C
, the above equation is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum\theta_{C,A}(c_{1,1})\theta_{C,A}(c_{2,1})=0,

(2.1)

where
c
is an arbitrary element of
C_{2}
.

If
(A,C)
and
(B,D)
are almost-Koszul pairs, then a morphism of almost-Koszul pairs is a couple
(\phi,\psi)
, where
\phi\colon A\rightarrow B
is a morphism of graded
R
-rings and
\psi\colon C\rightarrow D
is a morphism of graded
R
-corings such that they commute with the isomorphisms
\theta_{A,C}
and
\theta_{B,D}
. Henceforth, the diagram below is commutative,

\textstyle{A^{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\theta_{A,C}}
\textstyle{B^{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\theta_{B,D}}
\textstyle{C_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi_{1}}
\textstyle{D_{1}.}

A first example of an almost-Koszul pair is given in
[
5
, Proposition 1.8]
. For any connected and strongly graded
R
-ring
A
, the pair
\big(A,T(A)\big)
is almost-Koszul. Here, the coring structure of
T(A)
is defined as in Section
2.4
, and the map
\theta_{A,T(A)}\colon T_{1}(A)\to A^{1}
is induced by the projection
A_{+}\to A^{1}
. Note that
T_{1}(A)=A_{+}/A_{+}^{2}\cong A^{1}
, as
A
is strongly graded, so
\theta_{A,T(A)}
is an isomorphism.

The couple
(A,A^{!})
is another example of an almost-Koszul pair. Recall that
A^{!}_{1}=A^{1}
, so we can take
\theta_{A,A^{!}}:=\operatorname{I}_{A^{1}}
. The condition (
1.1
) is trivial in this particular case as, by construction,
A^{!}_{2}=\Ker\mu^{1,1}
.

Dually, if
C
is a connected and strongly graded
R
-coring, the pair
(E(C),C)
is almost-Koszul, cf.
[
5
, Proposition 1.18]
. In this example the
R
-ring structure of
E(C)
is defined in Section
2.4
. Since
E^{1}(C)=\Ker\Delta_{+}
, the map
\theta_{E(C),C}\colon C_{1}\to E^{1}(C)
, given by
\theta_{E(C),C}(c):=c+C_{0}\in C/C_{0}
, is well defined and it is an isomorphism of
R
-bimodules.

The couple
(C^{!},C)
can be seen as an almost-Koszul pair with respect to the map
\theta_{C^{!},C}=\operatorname{I}_{C_{1}}
. The relation (
1.1
) is verified since
C^{!}_{2}:=(C_{1}\otimes C_{1})/\im\Delta_{1,1}
and the component
C_{1}^{!}\otimes C_{1}^{!}\to C^{!}_{2}
of the multiplication on
C^{!}
coincides with the canonical projection from
C_{1}\otimes C_{1}
onto
C^{!}_{2}
.

2.6
Koszul pairs

Following
[
5
]
we shall briefly recall the definition of Koszul pairs, which are our main tool to investigate Koszul
R
-(co)rings. For any almost-Koszul pair
(A,C)
and
n\geq 0
we define a complex of graded right
C
-comodules by

\displaystyle\operatorname{K}_{r}^{-1}(A,C)=R\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{K}^{n}_{r}(A,C)=A^{n}\otimes C,\qquad\forall\,n\geq 0.

The differential map
d_{r}^{n}\colon A^{n}\otimes C\rightarrow A^{n+1}\otimes C
is zero on
A^{n}\otimes C_{0}
and, for
p>0
and
a\otimes c\in A^{n}\otimes C_{p},

\displaystyle d_{r}^{n}(a\otimes c)=\sum\limits a\theta_{C,A}(c_{1,{1}})\otimes c_{2,{p-1}}.

The differential
d^{n}_{r}
maps
A^{n}\otimes C_{p}
to
A^{n+1}\otimes C_{p-1}
. Thus
\K^{\bullet}_{r}(A,C,m)
is a subcomplex of
\K^{\bullet}_{r}(A,C)
, for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\K^{n}_{r}(A,C,m)=A^{n}\otimes C_{m-n}
. Note that, by convention,
C_{p}=0
for
p<0
, so
\K^{n}_{r}(A,C,m)
is trivial if either
n<-1
or
n>m
.

Using the fact that
(A^{\rm op},C^{\rm op})
is an almost-Koszul pair over
R^{\rm op}
, cf.
[
5
, Remark 1.4]
), a complex of left
C
-comodules is obtained by setting
\operatorname{K}_{l}^{\bullet}(A,C)=\operatorname{K}_{r}^{\bullet}(A^{\rm op},C^{\rm op})
.

By combining the complexes
\operatorname{K}_{l}^{\bullet}(A,C)
and
\operatorname{K}_{r}^{\bullet}(A,C)
, in
[
5
]
one constructs another cochain complex
\operatorname{K}^{\bullet}(A,C)
in the category of
C
-bicomodules. Since we do not use it in this paper, we omit its definition.

By duality, to an almost-Koszul pair correspond also three chain complexes
\operatorname{K}^{l}_{\bullet}\!(A{,}C)
,
\operatorname{K}^{r}_{\bullet}\!(A{,}C)
and
\operatorname{K}_{\bullet}(A,C)
. For instance,
\operatorname{K}^{l}_{\bullet}(A,C)
is the complex of left
A
-modules,

\displaystyle\operatorname{K}_{-1}^{l}(A,C)=R\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{K}^{l}_{n}(A,C)=A\otimes C_{n},

whose differential
d_{0}^{l}
is given by the left action of
A
on
R=C_{0}
. For
n>0
, the map
d_{n}^{l}
acts as

\displaystyle d_{n}^{l}(a\otimes c)=\sum\limits a\theta_{C,A}(c_{1,{1}})\otimes c_{2,{n-1}}.

The complex
\operatorname{K}_{\bullet}^{l}(A,C)
also decomposes as a direct sum
\oplus_{m\geq 0}\operatorname{K}^{l}_{\bullet}(A,C,m)
of subcomplexes, where
{K}^{l}_{n}(A,C,m)=A^{m-n}\otimes C_{n}
. Note that,
{K}^{l}_{n}(A,C,m)=0
, for
n>m
.

We conclude this subsection by recalling that the exactness of any of these six complexes implies the exactness of all the others, cf.
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
. Whenever this is the case,
(A,C)
is called
Koszul
. Thus, for a Koszul pair
(A,C)
, the complexe
\operatorname{K}_{\bullet}^{l}(A,C)
is a resolution of
R
by graded projective left
A
-modules. Similarly, for such a pair,
\operatorname{K}^{\bullet}_{r}(A,C)
is a resolution of
R
by injective graded right
C
-comodules.

The subcomplex
\K_{\bullet}^{l}(A,C,0)
is trivial in degree
n
, for all
n\neq-1,0
and
d_{-1}^{l}\colon R\to A^{0}\otimes C_{0}
, coincides with the canonical isomorphism
R\cong R\otimes_{R}R
. Thus,
\K_{n}^{l}(A,C,0)
is always exact. We deduce that the pair
(A,C)
is Koszul if and only if the complexes
\K_{\bullet}^{l}(A,C,m)
are exact for all
m>0
. By duality,
(A,C)
is Koszul if and only if
\K^{\bullet}_{r}(A,C,m)
is Koszul for all
m>0
.

2.7
Examples of morphisms of almost-Koszul pairs

We start by defining a morphism
(\operatorname{I}_{A},\phi^{A})
from
(A,A^{!})
to
\big(A,T(A)\big)
. The graded coring morphism
\phi^{A}
is obtained applying
[
5
, Proposition 1.24]
as follows. We keep the notation from the above mentioned result. Deleting the component of degree
-1
and then applying the functor
R\otimes_{A}(-)
to
\phi_{\bullet}\colon\K_{\bullet}^{l}(A,A^{!})\to\beta_{\bullet}^{l}(A)
we get a morphism of complexes from
R\otimes_{A}\K_{\bullet}^{l}(A,A^{!})
to
R\otimes_{A}\beta_{\bullet}^{l}(A)
. Note that the last two complexes are concentrated in non-negative degrees. By
[
5
, Proposition 1.23]
the former complex is isomorphic to
(A^{!},0)
, the complex with trivial differential maps and whose module of
n
-chains coincides with
A^{!}_{n}
. On the other hand, by definition, the latter complex equals
\Omega_{\bullet}(A)
. Since
\operatorname{I}_{R}\otimes\phi_{\bullet}
is compatible with the coring structure of
A^{!}
and
\Omega^{\bullet}\big(A\big)
, by applying the homology functor we get the desired coring map
\phi^{A}\colon A^{!}\to T(A)
. Let us remark that

\displaystyle A^{!}_{n}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1}\big({A^{1}}\big)^{(i-1)}\otimes\Ker\mu^{1,1}\otimes\big({A^{1}}\big)^{(n-i-1)}\subseteq A_{+}^{(n)},

so any
x
in
A^{!}_{n}
is an
n
-cycle in
\Omega_{\bullet}(A)
and
\phi^{A}(x)
as the homology class of
x
. It is easy to check now that
(\operatorname{I}_{A},\phi^{A})
is a morphism of almost-Koszul pairs.

In a similar way one defines a canonical morphism of almost Koszul-pairs
(\phi_{C},\operatorname{I}_{C})
from
(E(C),C)
to
(C^{!},C)
. Deleting the component of degree
-1
of
\phi^{\bullet}\colon\beta^{\bullet}_{r}(C)\to\K^{\bullet}_{r}(C^{!},C)
and then applying the functor
\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,-)
to the resulting map of complexes we get a morphism
\overline{\phi}^{\bullet}
from
\Omega^{\bullet}\big(C\big)
to the cochain complex with trivial differential maps
(C^{!},0)
, cf.
[
5
, Propositions 1.23 and 1.24]
. Henceforth, we can define the coring map
\phi_{C}\colon E(C)\to C^{!}
by
\phi_{C}=H^{\bullet}(\overline{\phi}^{\bullet})
. Let us notice that, for any
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in C_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}^{n}(x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n})=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}\in C^{!}_{n},

where
a_{i}=(\theta_{C^{!},C}\circ\overline{\pi}_{1})(x_{i})\in C^{!}_{1}
and
\overline{\pi}_{1}\colon C_{+}\to C_{1}
denotes the map induced by the projection
C\to C_{1}
. Thus,
\phi_{C}
maps the cohomology class of an
n
-cocyle
\omega
to
\overline{\phi}^{n}(\omega)
.

3
Koszul
\boldsymbol{R}
-rings revisited

The main goal of this section is to characterize Koszul
R
-rings in terms of properties of the
R
-coring
T(A)
. In particular, we shall recover the well known result that
A
is Koszul if and only if
T_{n,m}(A)=0
for
n\neq m
(cf.
[
9
, Theorem 1.9(6)]
). We also include here a new proof of the fact that a Koszul
R
-ring is quadratic, cf.
[
1
, Proposition 1.2.3]
. Our approach will allow us to show in the next section that similar results holds true for connected graded
R
-corings.

A comprehensive characterization of Koszul rings was given in
[
9
, Theorem 1.10]
. For completeness, let us recall it here, in a slightly modified form, to put in evidence the interplay with Koszul pairs.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A
be a connected strongly graded
R
-ring. The following are equivalent:

(1)

the
R
-ring
A
is Koszul;

(2)

the pair
(A,T(A))
is Koszul;

(3)

the pair
(A,A^{!})
is Koszul;

(4)

the canonical
R
-coring morphism
\phi^{A}\colon A^{!}\to T(A)
is an isomorphism;

(5)

the
R
-coring
T(A)
is strongly graded;

(6)

any primitive element of
T(A)
is homogeneous of degree
1
;

(7)

if
n\neq m
, then
T_{n,m}(A)=0
.

Note that compared to the cited result, we have added statement (2), which is equivalent with (1), taking into account
[
5
, Theorem 2.13]
.

Next we give a new proof of the fact that a Koszul
R
-ring is quadratic, which is based on Lemma
3.2
and Proposition
3.3
. These results may be of interest in their own right.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any connected strongly graded
R
-ring
A=\oplus_{n\geq 0}A^{n}
, the following sequence is exact

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}^{A}_{2,m}(R,R)\rightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{A/{A^{\geq m}}}(R,R)\rightarrow A^{m}\rightarrow 0,

where
A^{\geq m}
denotes the ideal
\oplus_{p\geq m}A^{p}
and
\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{A/A^{\geq m}}(R,R)
is the component of homological degree
m
of
\operatorname{Tor}_{2}^{A/A^{\geq m}}(R,R)
.

Proof.

Since
A
is strongly graded, for any positive
2
-partition
\boldsymbol{m}
of
m
, we have that
A^{\boldsymbol{m}}\xrightarrow{d_{2}^{m}}A^{m}
is surjective, so the following sequence is exact:

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\Ker d^{m}_{2}\rightarrow\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}A^{\boldsymbol{m}}\rightarrow A^{m}\rightarrow 0.

(3.1)

Using the complex
\Omega_{\bullet}(A,m)
we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{A}(R,R)=\operatorname{Ker}d_{2}^{m}/\operatorname{Im}d_{3}^{m}.

(3.2)

On the other hand, to work with the algebra
A/A^{\geq m}
, we use the complex
\Omega_{\bullet}(A/A^{\geq m},m)
. Since in degree
1
this complex is trivial, for any
m
, we have

\displaystyle\Tor_{2,m}^{A/A^{\geq m}}(R,R)=\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}A^{\boldsymbol{m}}/\im d_{3}^{m}.

(3.3)

Putting all the information together, we get the sequence

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\Ker d^{m}_{2}/\im d_{3}^{m}\longrightarrow\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}A^{\boldsymbol{m}}/\im d_{3}^{m}\longrightarrow A^{m}\longrightarrow 0,

which is also exact due to (
3.1
) and gives the desired sequence, because of the relations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
).
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\pi\colon B\to A
denote a morphism of strongly graded connected
R
-rings. If
m
is a positive integer such that the components
\pi^{i}
are bijective, for all
0\leq i<m
, and
\Tor_{2,m}^{A}(R,R)=0
, then
\pi^{m}
is bijective as well.

Proof.

By the relations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), it follows that
\pi
induces the maps:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}^{m}\colon\ \operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{B/B^{\geq m}}(R,R)\rightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{A/A^{\geq m}}(R,R)_{m},\qquad\overline{\pi}^{m}\colon\ \operatorname{Tor}^{B}_{2,m}(R.R)\rightarrow\operatorname{Tor}^{A}_{2,m}(R,R).

By construction, the squares of the following diagram are commutative,

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Tor}^{B}_{2,m}(R,R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i_{B}^{m}}
\scriptstyle{\overline{\pi}^{m}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{B/B^{\geq m}}(R,R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{p_{B}^{m}}
\scriptstyle{\widetilde{\pi}^{m}}
\textstyle{B^{m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi^{m}}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{A}(R,R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i_{A}^{m}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{A/A^{\geq m}}(R,R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{p_{A}^{m}}
\textstyle{A^{m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0.}

Since the homogeneous components of the graded
R
-rings
A/A^{\geq m}
and
B/B^{\geq m}
are zero in degree
i\geq m
, and
\pi^{i}
is bijective for
i<m
, we deduce that
\pi
induces an isomorphism
\Omega_{\bullet}(B/B^{\geq m})\cong\Omega_{\bullet}(A/A^{\geq m})
. In particular, we get that
\widetilde{\pi}^{m}
is an isomorphism of
R
-bimodules. By the Snake lemma, the kernel of
\pi^{m}
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Coker}\overline{\pi}^{m}
. As
\Tor_{2,m}^{A}(R,R)=0
it follows that
\pi^{m}
is injective. Clearly,
\coker\pi^{m}=0
.
∎

To a connected graded
R
-ring
A
we associate the
R
-ring
\langle A^{1},K_{A}\rangle
, where
K_{A}
denotes the kernel of
\mu^{1,1}\colon A^{1}\otimes A^{1}\rightarrow A^{2}
. There is a unique
R
-ring morphism
\phi_{A}\colon\langle A^{1},K_{A}\rangle\to A
which lifts
\mu^{1,1}
. This morphism is surjective, provided that
A
is strongly graded. Following
[
1
]
, we say that
A
is
quadratic
if and only if
A
is a connected strongly graded
R
-ring such that
\phi_{A}
is an isomorphism.

We now fix a connected strongly graded
R
-ring
A
. Let
V=A^{1}
. The kernel of the canonical
R
-ring map
T_{R}^{a}(V)\to A
will be denoted by
\widetilde{K}_{A}=\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}
. Obviously, the
m
-degree component
\widetilde{K}^{m}_{A}
of
\widetilde{K}_{A}
coincides with the kernel of the iterated multiplication
\mu_{m}\colon V^{(m)}\to A^{m}
, and
\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}\subseteq\langle K_{A}\rangle^{m}
.

Let
Z_{2,m}=\Ker d_{2}^{m}
and
B_{2,m}=\im d_{3}^{m}
. Recall that
\{a_{\boldsymbol{m}}\}_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}
is a
2
-cycle if every
a_{\boldsymbol{m}}
is an element in
A^{\boldsymbol{m}}
and
\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}\mu^{\boldsymbol{m}}(a_{\boldsymbol{m}})=0
. For any positive integer
m
we set
\alpha_{m}(x):=\big\{\mu_{\boldsymbol{m}}(x)\big\}_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}
. It is easy to see that
\alpha_{m}(x)
belongs to
Z_{2,m}
, as
x
is an element in the kernel of
\mu_{m}=\mu^{\boldsymbol{m}}\circ\mu_{\boldsymbol{m}}
, for any positive
2
-partition
\boldsymbol{m}
of
m
. Thus
x\mapsto\alpha_{m}(x)
defines an
R
-bimodule map
\alpha_{m}\colon\langle K_{A}\rangle^{m}\to Z_{2,m}
.

Furthermore, for any element
x\in\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}
, we have
\alpha_{m}(x)\in B_{2,m}
. Indeed, by definition of
\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}
, it is enough to show that
\alpha_{m}(x)
is a boundary for all
x\in V^{(i-1)}\otimes K_{A}\otimes V^{(m-i-1)}
and
1\leq i\leq m-1
. Let
i=1
. Since
x\in K_{A}\otimes V^{(m-2)}
, we have
\alpha_{m}(x)=\{x_{\boldsymbol{m}}\}_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}
, where

\displaystyle x_{\boldsymbol{m}}=\begin{cases}\big(V\otimes\mu(m-1)\big)(x),&\text{if}\ \boldsymbol{m}=(1,m-1),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus
\alpha_{m}(x)=-d_{3}^{m}(y)
, where
y=\big(V^{(2)}\otimes\mu(m-2)\big)(x)
. For all other values of
i
, the proof of the fact that
\alpha_{m}(x)
is a boundary is similar, so it will be omitted.

Summarizing,
\alpha_{m}
defines a map from
\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}
to
B_{2,m}
, still denoted by
\alpha_{m}
. In other words, the diagram

\textstyle{\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha_{m}}
\textstyle{B_{2,m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\langle K_{A}\rangle^{m}}
\scriptstyle{\alpha_{m}}
\textstyle{Z_{2,m}}

is commutative, where the vertical arrows denote the canonical inclusions.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
A
be a connected strongly graded
R
-ring. If
\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}{=}\langle K_{A}\rangle^{m}\!
, then
\Tor_{2,m}^{A}(R,R){=}0
.

Proof.

Let
\xi=a_{1}\cdots a_{i}\otimes a_{i+1}\cdots a_{m}
, where
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}
are arbitrary elements in
V=A^{1}
. Since

\displaystyle\xi=a_{1}\cdots a_{i-1}\otimes a_{i}\cdots a_{m}+d_{3}^{m}(a_{1}\cdots a_{i-1}\otimes a_{i}\otimes a_{i+1}\cdots a_{m}),

we deduce by induction on
i
that
\xi=a_{1}\otimes a_{2}\cdots a_{m}+d_{3}^{m}(\zeta)
, for some
\zeta\in\Omega_{3}(A,m)
.

We now pick a
2
-cycle
x\in Z_{2,m}
. Since
A
is strongly graded, in view of the above remarks, there exist
y\in A^{1}\otimes A^{m-1}
and
z\in\Omega_{3}(A,m)
such that
x=y+d_{3}^{m}(z)
. Note that
y
is a
2
-cycle as well.

Our goal is to show that
x
is a boundary. It is enough to prove that
y\in B_{2,m}
. As
A
is strongly graded, the map
f=V\otimes\mu_{m-1}
is surjective. Hence, there exists
y^{\prime}\in V^{(m)}
such that
f(y^{\prime})=y
. Since
y
is a cycle, we also get that
y^{\prime}
belongs to
\langle K_{A}\rangle^{m}
, the kernel of
\mu_{m}
.

By assumption,
\langle K_{A}\rangle^{m}=\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}
. Thus
y^{\prime}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m-1}y^{\prime}_{i}
, for some
y^{\prime}_{i}\in V^{(i-1)}\otimes K_{A}\otimes V^{(m-i-1)}
. Note that
f(y^{\prime}_{i})=0
, for any
i>1
. Thus
y=f(y^{\prime}_{1})
. As
\mu_{\boldsymbol{m}}(y^{\prime}_{1})=0
, for any positive
2
-partition
\boldsymbol{m}\neq(1,m-1)
, it follows that
y=\alpha_{m}(y^{\prime}_{1})
. We conclude the proof that

is a boundary by remarking that
\alpha_{m}
maps
\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}
to
B_{2,m}
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
A
be a strongly graded
R
-ring. Then
A
is quadratic if and only if
\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{A}(R,R)
vanishes for all
m\geq 3
.

Proof.

Let
B:=\langle A^{1},K_{A}\rangle
. We first prove that
A
is a quadratic
R
-ring, provided that
\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{A}(R,R)=0
, for all
m\geq 3
. It is enough to show that the components
\phi_{A}^{m}
of the canonical map
\phi_{A}\colon B\rightarrow A
are all isomorphisms. We proceed by induction on
m
. The maps
\phi_{A}^{0}
and
\phi^{1}_{A}
are obviously injective, because they coincide with the identity maps of
R
and
A^{1}
, respectively. Let us assume that
\phi^{k}_{A}
is an isomorphism, for all

. By assumption,
\operatorname{Tor}_{2,m}^{A}(R,R)=0
for all

. Hence, using Proposition
3.3

\phi_{A}^{m}
is bijective.

The converse follows directly by the preceding lemma, as
\widetilde{K}_{A}^{m}=\langle K_{A}\rangle^{m}
, for all

-rings.
∎

4
Koszul
\boldsymbol{R}
-corings

In this section we introduce and study the properties of Koszul corings, the dual notion of Koszul
R
-rings. Here we shall also show that any Koszul coring is quadratic.

Strongly graded comodules, the substitute for strongly graded rings when one works with graded corings instead of graded rings, will play an important role in this part of the paper. Hence, we start by discussing their main properties.

Let
(X,\rho^{X})
be a graded right comodule over a graded
R
-coring
C
. For every
n
, the map
\rho^{X}
defines a morphism of graded
C
-comodules
\rho_{n}^{X}
from
X
to
X_{n}\otimes C
, whose component of degree
k
is
\rho_{n,k-n}^{X}
. Note that the component of degree
k
of
X_{n}\otimes C
is
X_{n}\otimes C_{k-n}
, where
C_{i}=0
, for all
i<0
. Thus, in particular,
\rho_{n,k-n}^{X}=0
for
k<n
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
X=\oplus_{n\geq 0}X_{n}
be a graded right
C
-comodule, with structure map
\rho^{X}
. We say that
X
is
strongly graded in degree

n
if the morphism of graded
C
-comodules
\rho^{X}_{n}\colon X\rightarrow X_{n}\otimes C
is injective.

Let us remark that, by definition, a graded right
C
-comodule is strongly graded in degree
n
if and only if
X_{p}=0
for all
p<n
and
\rho_{n,q}\colon X_{n+q}\to X_{n}\otimes C_{q}
is injective for all
q\geq 0
.

Some useful properties of graded comodules are collected in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
C
be a strongly graded
R
-coring. Let
(X,\rho^{X})
denote a graded right
C
-comodule.

1.

The inclusion
\Ker\rho^{X}_{i,m-i}\subseteq\Ker\rho^{X}_{j,m-j}
holds for all
0\leq j\leq i<m
.

2.

There is a canonical isomorphism
\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,X)_{m}\cong\bigcap_{i=0}^{m-1}\Ker\rho^{X}_{i,m-i}=\Ker\rho^{X}_{m-1,1}
.

3.

X
is strongly graded in degree
n
if and only if
X_{i}=0
, for
i<n
, and
\rho_{i,1}^{X}
is injective for
i\geq n
.

4.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a morphism of graded right
C
-comodules. If
X
is strongly graded in degree
n
and
f_{n}\colon X_{n}\to Y_{n}
is an injective map then
f
is injective as well.

Proof.

To prove (1), consider the following commutative diagram:

\textstyle{X_{m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho_{i,m-i}^{X}}
\scriptstyle{\rho_{j,m-j}^{X}}
\textstyle{X_{i}\otimes C_{m-i}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho_{j,i-j}^{X}}
\textstyle{X_{j}\otimes C_{m-j}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\operatorname{I}_{X_{j}}\otimes\Delta^{C}_{i-j,m-i}}
\textstyle{X_{j}\otimes C_{i-j}\otimes C_{m-i}.}

Let
x\in\operatorname{Ker}\rho_{i,m-i}^{X}
. Using the fact that
\operatorname{I}_{X_{j}}\otimes\Delta^{C}_{i-j,m-i}
is injective, as
C
is strongly graded, it follows that
\rho^{X}_{j,m-j}(x)
is zero, hence
x\in\operatorname{Ker}\rho_{j,m-j}^{X}
, as required.

(2) Let
X
and
Y
denote two graded
C
-comodule. By definition,
f\in\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(X,Y)_{m}
if and only if

is a morphism of
C
-comodules and
f(X_{k})\subseteq Y_{m+k}
, for all

C
-colinear map

of degree

and
\rho^{X}(f(1))=f(1)\otimes 1

\rho^{X}_{i,m-i}(f(1))=0
, for all
0\leq i\leq m-1

In conclusion, the required isomorphism is given by the map

. The equation from the statement follows by the first part of the lemma.

(3) In view of the remark just after the Definition
4.1
, the condition
X_{i}=0
for

is part of the hypothesis for both implications. We must prove that
\rho^{X}_{i,1}
is injective for all

if and only if
\rho^{X}_{n,q}\colon X_{n+q}\to X_{n}\otimes C_{q}
is injective for all
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

To prove that
\rho_{n,q}^{X}
is injective for
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
we proceed by induction on

. The map
\rho_{n,0}^{M}
coincides with the canonical isomorphism

and
\rho_{n,1}^{M}
is injective by assumption. Let us assume that
\rho^{X}_{n,q}
is injective. Thus, the horizontal arrow on the bottom of the following commutative diagram is injective,

\textstyle{X_{n+q+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho_{n,q+1}^{X}}
\scriptstyle{\rho_{n+q,1}^{X}}
\textstyle{X_{n}\otimes C_{q+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\operatorname{I}_{X_{n}}\otimes\Delta^{C}_{q,1}}
\textstyle{X_{n+q}\otimes C_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho^{X}_{n,q}\otimes\operatorname{I}_{C_{1}}}
\textstyle{X_{n}\otimes C_{q}\otimes C_{1}.}

Since, by hypothesis, the leftmost vertical map is injective it follows that
\rho^{X}_{n,q+1}
is also injective.

For the converse one uses the commutative diagram

\textstyle{X_{i+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho^{X}_{i,1}}
\scriptstyle{\rho^{X}_{n,i+1-n}}
\textstyle{X_{i}\otimes C_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho^{X}_{n,i-n}\otimes\operatorname{I}_{C_{1}}}
\textstyle{X_{n}\otimes C_{i+1-n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\operatorname{I}_{X_{n}}\otimes\Delta^{C}_{i-n,1}}
\textstyle{X_{n}\otimes C_{i-n}\otimes C_{1}.}

The component
\rho^{X}_{n,i+1-n}
is injective by assumption, while
\operatorname{I}_{X_{n}}\otimes\Delta_{i-n,1}^{C}
is so as

is strongly graded. We conclude that
\rho^{X}_{i,1}
is injective, as required.

(4) We have to prove that all components

are injective. For

this property trivially holds as, by preceding part of the lemma, we have
X_{k}=0
. The map
f_{n}
is injective by assumption, so we can suppose that

. The following diagram is commutative, as

is a morphism of graded comodules,

\textstyle{X_{k}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{k}}
\scriptstyle{\rho^{X}_{n,k-n}}
\textstyle{Y_{k}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rho^{Y}_{n,k-n}}
\textstyle{X_{n}\otimes C_{k-n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{n}\otimes\operatorname{I}_{C_{k-n}}}
\textstyle{Y_{n}\otimes C_{k-n}.}

By hypothesis,
f_{n}\otimes\operatorname{I}_{C_{k-n}}
is injective. On the other hand,
\rho_{n,k-n}^{X}
is injective, as

is strongly graded in degree

. We conclude that
x=0
.
∎

We can now introduce Koszul corings by dualizing

Definition 4.3
.

A connected
R
-coring

is called
Koszul
if

has an resolution

by injective graded right
C
-comodules such that every term

is strongly graded in degree
n
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
C
be a connected, strongly graded
R
-coring. The following are equivalent:

(1)

the coring
C
is Koszul;

(2)

the pair
(E(C),C)
is Koszul;

(3)

the pair
(C^{!},C)
is Koszul;

(4)

the canonical morphism
E(C)\rightarrow C^{!}
is an isomorphism;

(5)

the
R
-ring
E(C)
is strongly graded;

(6)

the canonical map
QE(C)\to E^{1}(C)
is an isomorphism;

(7)

the relation
E^{n,m}(C)=0
holds for all

.

Proof.

Note that, for any Koszul pair
(A,C)
, the complex
\K^{\bullet}_{r}(A,C)
is a resolution of
R
satisfying the conditions from the definition of Koszul corings. In particular it follows that
C
is Koszul, so the implications
(2)\Longrightarrow(1)
and
(3)\Longrightarrow(1)
are obvious.

To prove
(1)\Longrightarrow(7)
, assume that
C
is Koszul. That is, following the definition,
R
has an injective resolution
0\rightarrow R\rightarrow Q^{\bullet}
such that
Q^{n}
is strongly graded in degree
n
. Note that
E^{n,m}(C)=\operatorname{Ext}_{n,m}^{C}(R,R)
is the cohomology in degree
n
of the complex obtained by applying the functor
\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,-)_{m}
to this resolution. Thus, to conclude the proof of this implication, it is enough to show that
\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,Q^{n})_{m}
is trivial for
m\neq n
. By Lemma
4.2
(2) we have
\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,Q^{n})_{m}=\operatorname{Ker}\rho^{Q^{n}}_{m-1,1}

Q^{n}
is strongly graded since, in view of Lemma
4.2
, we have
Q^{n,m}=0
for
m<n
, and the component
\rho^{Q^{n}}_{m-1,1}
is injective for all
m>n
.

For
(7)\Longrightarrow(3)
, let us assume that
E(C)
is diagonal. We shall prove by induction on
n
that
\K_{r}^{\bullet}(C^{!},C)
is exact in degree

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow C_{0}^{!}\otimes C\longrightarrow C_{1}^{!}\otimes C

and the fact that

[

We now assume that
\K^{\bullet}(C^{!},C)
is exact in degree

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow R\rightarrow C^{!}_{0}\otimes C\xrightarrow{d^{0}_{r}}C_{1}^{!}\otimes C\xrightarrow{d^{1}_{r}}\cdots\xrightarrow{d^{n-2}_{r}}C^{!}_{n-1}\otimes C\xrightarrow{d^{n-1}_{r}}C_{n}^{!}\otimes C\xrightarrow{\alpha}Y\rightarrow 0,

Y_{k}
denotes

for all

Let

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow X\rightarrow C_{n}^{!}\otimes C\rightarrow Q^{n+1}\rightarrow Q^{n+2}\rightarrow\cdots

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow R\rightarrow C_{0}^{!}\otimes C\rightarrow C_{1}^{!}\otimes C\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow C_{n}^{!}\otimes C\rightarrow Q^{n+1}\rightarrow Q^{n+2}\rightarrow\cdots.

Thus we can compute

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,C_{0}^{!}\otimes C)_{m}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,C_{1}^{!}\otimes C)_{m}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,C_{n}^{!}\otimes C)_{m}

Since

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,C_{n}^{!}\otimes C)_{m}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,Q^{n+1})_{m}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}^{C}\big(R,Q^{n+2}\big)_{m}\rightarrow\cdots

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,X)_{m}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,C_{n}^{!}\otimes C)_{m}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}^{C}(R,Y)_{m}\rightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{1,m}_{C}(R,X)\rightarrow\cdots.

We can now prove that

Let

Remark 4.5
.

By the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 2.14]
, the complex
\K_{\bullet}^{l}(A,A^{!})
is isomorphic to the Koszul complex of
A
, which was introduced in
[
1

. By analogy,
\operatorname{K}^{\bullet}(C^{!},C)
will be seen as the Koszul complex associated to a connected
R
-coring
C
.

Using the method from the previous section we are going to show that any Koszul coring is quadratic. This property of Koszul corings will follow as a direct application of a cohomological criterion for bijectivity of a morphism between two strongly graded corings.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
C
be a strongly graded
R
-coring. Then the following sequence is exact:

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow C_{m}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{2,m}_{C_{<m}}(R,R)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^{2,m}_{C}(R,R)\longrightarrow 0.

Proof.

The differential
d_{m}^{1}
of
\Omega^{\bullet}(C,m)
maps
c\in C_{m}
to the family
\{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}(c)\}_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}
where, by notation,
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}
is the component
\Delta_{m_{1},m_{2}}\colon C_{m_{1}+m_{2}}\to C_{m_{1}}\otimes C_{m_{2}}
, for any positive
2
-partition
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},m_{2})
of
m
. By hypothesis
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}
is injective for all
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)
, so
d_{m}^{1}
is injective as well. Thus the sequence

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow C_{m}\xrightarrow{d_{m}^{1}}\Ker d_{m}^{2}\longrightarrow\Ker d_{m}^{2}/\im d_{m}^{1}\longrightarrow 0

is exact. Note that
\operatorname{Ext}^{2,m}_{C}(R,R)=\Ker d_{m}^{2}/\im d_{m}^{1}
. On the other hand, the complex
\Omega^{\bullet}(C_{<m},m)
coincides in small degrees with

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow 0\xrightarrow{d_{m}^{0}}0\xrightarrow{d_{m}^{1}}\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}_{2}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}C_{\boldsymbol{m}_{2}}\xrightarrow{d_{m}^{2}}\bigoplus\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}_{3}\in\mathscr{P}_{3}(m)}C_{\boldsymbol{m}_{3}},

so we conclude the proof by remarking that
\operatorname{Ext}^{2,m}_{C_{<m}}(R,R)\simeq\Ker d_{m}^{2}.

∎

As in the case of graded

such that the following diagram is commutative,

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\scriptstyle{\pi_{m}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Ext}_{C_{<m}}^{2,m}(R,R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\widetilde{\pi}_{m}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Ext}^{2,m}_{C}(R,R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\overline{\pi}_{m}}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{D_{m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Ext}_{D_{<m}}^{2,m}(R,R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Ext}^{2,m}_{D}(R,R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0.}

The map
\widetilde{\pi}_{m}
is an isomorphism, as
\pi_{i}
is bijective, for all
i<m
. Therefore, by Snake lemma, we have
\Ker\pi_{m}=0
and
\coker\pi_{m}\cong\Ker\overline{\pi}_{m}=0
.
∎

4.1
Quadratic corings

For a connected
R
-coring
C
, the family
\{\Delta(n)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of iterated comultiplications
\Delta(n)\colon C_{n}\to C^{(n)}_{1}
defines a morphism
\phi_{C}\colon C\to T_{R}^{c}(C_{1})
. The image of
\phi_{C}
is a subcoring
\overline{C}
of
\widetilde{C}:=\{C_{1},\im\Delta_{1,1}\}
. Hence we may regard
\phi_{C}
as a coring morphism from
C
to
\widetilde{C}
. We shall say that
C
is
quadratic
if and only if this morphism is a bijection. Therefore,
C
is quadratic if and only if it is strongly graded and
\overline{C}=\widetilde{C}
.

Let
C
be a strongly graded
R
-coring. As in the case of
R
-rings, we can relate
Z^{2,m}=\Ker d^{2}_{m}
, the set of all
2
-cocycles in
\Omega^{\bullet}\big(C,m\big)
, and
\widetilde{C}_{m}
. To do this we first notice that, for any
2
-cocycle
x=\{x_{\boldsymbol{m}}\}_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}
, the element
\Delta(\boldsymbol{m})(x_{\boldsymbol{m}})
does not depend on
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)
, as the components of the (iterated) comultiplication are all injective. Recall that
\Delta(\boldsymbol{m})=\Delta({m_{1}})\otimes\Delta({m_{2}})
, for any positive
2
-partition
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},m_{2})
. Since
\Delta(m)=\Delta(\boldsymbol{m})\circ\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}
, for any positive
2
-partition of
m
, it is not difficult to see that
\Delta(\boldsymbol{m})(x_{\boldsymbol{m}})\in\widetilde{C}_{m}
. Hence, we can define the function
\alpha^{m}\colon Z^{2,m}\to\widetilde{C}_{m}
by
\alpha^{m}(x)=\Delta(\boldsymbol{m})(x_{\boldsymbol{m}})
.

Let
B^{2,m}:=\im d^{1}_{m}
denote the set of
2
-coboundaries. If
x=\{x_{\boldsymbol{m}}\}_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}
is a
2
-coboundary, then there is
c\in C_{m}
such that
x_{\boldsymbol{m}}=\Delta^{\boldsymbol{m}}(c)
, for all positive
2
-partitions
\boldsymbol{m}
. Thus
\alpha^{m}(x)\in\overline{C}_{m}
. Henceforth,
\alpha^{m}
induces a map, still denoted by
\alpha^{m}
, from
B^{2,m}
to
\overline{C}_{m}
. We get the following commutative diagram

\textstyle{B^{2,m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha^{m}}
\textstyle{\overline{C}_{m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces Z^{2,m}}
\scriptstyle{\alpha^{m}}
\textstyle{\widetilde{C}_{m},}

where the vertical arrows represent the canonical inclusions.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
C
be a strongly graded
R
-coring. If
\overline{C}_{m}=\widetilde{C}_{m}
, then
\operatorname{Ext}^{2,m}_{C}(R,R)=0
.

Proof.

If
x=\{x_{\boldsymbol{m}}\}_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(m)}
is a
2
-cocycle, then
\alpha^{m}(x)\in\widetilde{C}_{m}=\overline{C}_{m}
. Since
\overline{C}_{m}
is the image of
\Delta(m)
, there is
c\in C_{m}
such that
\Delta(\boldsymbol{m})(x_{\boldsymbol{m}})=\Delta(m)(c)
. As
\Delta(m)=\Delta(\boldsymbol{m})\circ\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}
and
\Delta(\boldsymbol{m})
is injective, we deduce that
x_{\boldsymbol{m}}=\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m}}(c)
, for any positive
2
-partition
\boldsymbol{m}
. Thus
x
is a
2
-coboundary.
∎

Proposition 4.9
.

A strongly graded
R
-coring
C
is quadratic if and only if
\operatorname{Ext}^{2,m}_{C}(R,R)=0
, for all
m\geq 3
.

Proof.

In view of the preceding Lemma, the vanishing of
\operatorname{Ext}^{2,m}_{C}(R,R)
is a necessary condition. To prove that it is a sufficient condition as well, using Proposition
4.7
and proceeding as in the proof of Proposition
3.5
, one shows by induction that the components of
\phi_{C}\colon C\to\widetilde{C}
are all isomorphisms.
∎

Corollary 4.10
.

Any Koszul
R
-coring
C
is quadratic.

Proof.

The conclusion follows by combining the results in Proposition
4.9
and the fact the statements
(1)
and
(7)
of Theorem
4.4
are equivalent.
∎

Finally, let us add that a direct (co)product of two Koszul (co)rings is still Koszul. For
R
-rings the result was used in
[
9
, Section 2.10]
to “patch” tiles forming a Koszul poset. We restate and prove the proposition using Koszul pairs, obtaining at the same time a similar result for Koszul corings.

Let
R
and
S
be semisimple rings. We assume that
A
is a connected
R
-ring,
B
is a connected
S
-ring,
C
is a connected
R
-coring and
D
is a connected
S
-coring. We also suppose that
(A,C)
and
(B,D)
are almost-Koszul pairs with respect to the
R
-bilinear map
\theta_{C,A}\colon C_{1}\to A^{1}
and the
S
-bilinear map
\theta_{D,B}\colon D_{1}\to B^{1}
, respectively.

For an
R
-bimodule
V
and an
S
-bimodule
W
we shall regard the abelian group
V\oplus W
as an
R\times S
-bimodule with respect to the actions:
(r,s)\cdot(v,w)=(rv,sw)
and
(v,w)\cdot(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})=(vr^{\prime},ws^{\prime})
.
Note that we have an isomorphism of left
A\times B
-modules

\displaystyle(A\times B)\otimes_{R\times S}(V\oplus W)\cong(A\otimes_{R}V)\oplus(B\otimes_{S}W),

(4.2)

where the
A\times B
-action on the direct sum of
A\otimes_{R}V
and
B\otimes_{S}W
is also defined component-wise. This isomorphism is defined by the map
(a,b)\otimes_{R\times S}(v,w)\mapsto(a\otimes_{R}v,b\otimes_{S}w)
.

Recall that the comultiplication of the coproduct
C\oplus D
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{p,q}(c,d)=\sum(c_{1,p},0)\otimes_{R\times S}(c_{2,q},0)+\sum(0,d_{1,p})\otimes_{R\times S}(0,d_{2,q}).

Let
\theta^{\prime}:=\theta_{C,A}
and
\theta^{\prime\prime}:=\theta_{D,B}
. We define
\theta\colon C_{1}\oplus D_{1}\to A^{1}\times B^{1}
given by
\theta(c,d)=\big(\theta^{\prime}(c),\theta^{\prime\prime}(d)\big)
.

Proposition 4.11
.

The pair
(A\times B,C\oplus D)
is almost-Koszul with respect to the isomorphism
\theta
. Moreover, this pair is Koszul if and only if the pairs
(A,C)
and
(B,D)
are so.

Proof.

To show that
(A\times B,C\oplus D)
is almost-Koszul we take
(c,d)\in C_{2}\oplus D_{2}
. We have

\displaystyle\big(\mu^{1,1}\circ(\theta\otimes_{R\times S}\theta)\circ\Delta_{1,1}\big)(c,d)=\sum\big(\theta^{\prime}(c_{1,1})\theta^{\prime}(c_{2,1}),0\big)+\sum\big(0,\theta^{\prime\prime}(d_{1,1})\theta^{\prime\prime}(d_{2,1})\big)=0.

Using the identification (
4.2
) and performing a similar computation as above one can show easily that

\displaystyle\K_{\bullet}^{l}(A\times B,C\oplus D)\cong\K_{\bullet}^{l}(A,C)\oplus\K_{\bullet}^{l}(B,D).

Thus, obviously,
(A\times B,C\oplus D)
is Koszul if and only if both
(A,C)
and
(B,D)
are so.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

We have seen that a connected
R
-ring
A
is Koszul if and only if there is a connected graded
R
-coring
C
such that
(A,C)
is Koszul. Similarly, a given connected
R
-coring
C
is Koszul if and only if there is a connected graded
R
-ring
A
such that
(A,C)
is Koszul. In view of the preceding proposition, we deduce that the connected graded
R\times S
-ring
A\times B
is Koszul if and only if
A
and
B
are Koszul too. Similarly, the connected graded
R\times S
-coring
C\times D
is Koszul if and only if
C
and
D
are so.

5
Locally finite Koszul (co)rings

In this section, we provide a first application of the results developed so far. More precisely, we shall prove that a left (right) locally finite
R
-ring is Koszul if and only if its left (right) graded dual
R
-coring is Koszul as well. This result will allow us to show in the subsequent section that in a more particular case, the incidence
R
-ring of a graded finite poset is Koszul if and only if its incidence
R
-coring is also Koszul.

Recall that a graded left
R
-bimodule
V=\oplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}V_{n}
is called
left
(
right
)
locally finite
if and only if its components
V_{n}
are finitely generated as left (right) modules. In the case when
V
is both left and right locally finite, we will simply say that
V
is
locally finite
. Throughout this section we keep the assumption that
R
is a semisimple ring, so all
R
-bimodules are projective as left (and right)
R
-modules.

5.1
The left dual of an
\boldsymbol{R}
-bimodule

Let
V
be an
R
-bimodule. Define the left dual of
V
by
{}^{*}V=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(_{R}V,{}_{R}R)
. This becomes a bimodule over the opposed ring
R^{\rm op}
with respect to the actions defined, for
r\in R
and
\alpha\in{{}^{*}V}
, by

\displaystyle(r\cdot\alpha)(v)=\alpha(v)r\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\alpha\cdot r)(v)=\alpha(vr).

In the case that
V=\oplus_{n\in N}V_{n}
is a graded
R
-bimodule we define the
left graded dual
of
V
to be the
R^{\rm op}
-bimodule
{}^{*\text{-gr}}V:=\oplus_{n\in N}{{}^{*}V_{n}}
.

It is known that the dual of the tensor product of two finite-dimensional vector spaces is the tensor product of their duals (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Section 2.7]
). A similar property holds for
R
-bimodules. More precisely, if
V
and
W
are
R
-bimodules, there exists a bi-additive map

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}\colon\ {{}^{*}V}\times{{}^{*}W}\rightarrow{{}^{*}(V\otimes_{R}W)},\qquad\phi^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)(v\otimes_{R}w)=\alpha(v\beta(w)).

Furthermore, we have
\phi^{\prime}((\alpha\cdot r)\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}\beta)(v\otimes_{R}w)=(\alpha\cdot r)(v\beta(w))=\alpha(rv\beta(w))=\phi(\alpha\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}(r\cdot\beta))(v\otimes_{R}w)
. Thus
\phi^{\prime}
is
R^{\rm op}
-balanced, so it induces a morphism of abelian groups

\displaystyle\phi\colon\ {{}^{*}V}\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}{{}^{*}W}\rightarrow{{}^{*}(V\otimes_{R}W)},\qquad\phi(\alpha\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}\beta)(v\otimes_{R}w)=\alpha(v\beta(w)).

As a matter of fact,
\phi
is an
R^{\rm op}
-bimodule map, as
\phi((r\cdot\alpha)\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}\beta)(v\otimes_{R}w)=(r\cdot\alpha)(v\beta(w))=\alpha(v\beta(w))r.
On the other hand,
[r\cdot\phi(\alpha\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}\beta)](v\otimes_{R}w)=\phi(\alpha\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}\beta)(v\otimes_{R}w)r=\alpha(v\beta(w))r
. Right-linearity is proved analogously.

We claim that, under the additional assumption that
W
is finitely generated as a left
R
-module, the map
\phi
is a bijection. Indeed, since
R
is semisimple,
W
is projective as a left
R
-module. So, there are finite dual bases on
W
, that is two sets
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}\subseteq W
and
\{{{}^{*}}w_{1},\dots,{{}^{*}}w_{n}\}\subseteq{{}^{*}}W
such that
w=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{{}^{*}}w_{i}(w)w_{i}
, for all
w\in W
.
Let
\psi\colon{}^{*}(V\otimes_{R}W)\to{}^{*}V\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}{{}^{*}W}
be the map given by

\displaystyle\psi(\gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma(-\otimes_{R}w_{i})\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}{}^{*}w_{i}.

In the above formula
\gamma
denotes an element in
{{}^{*}}(V\otimes_{R}W)
and the application
\gamma(-\otimes_{R}w_{i})\colon V\rightarrow R
acts as
v\mapsto\gamma(v\otimes_{R}w_{i})
. The only thing left to show is that
\psi
and
\phi
are mutual inverses.

Let
\gamma=:\phi(\alpha\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}\beta)
, for some
\alpha\in{}^{*}V
and
\beta\in{}^{*}W
. Thus
\gamma(v\otimes_{R}w)=\alpha(v\beta(w))
, for all
v\in V
and
w\in W
. Hence, by the definition of
\psi
, we get

\displaystyle\psi(\gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma(-\otimes w_{i})\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}{{}^{*}}w_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(-\beta(w_{i}))\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}{{}^{*}}w_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha\cdot\beta(w_{i})\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}{{}^{*}}w_{i}.

By the definition of the left
R
-action on
{}^{*}W
and the definition of dual bases, together with the fact that
\beta
is a morphism of left
R
-modules, we have
\beta=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\beta(w_{i})\cdot{{}^{*}}w_{i}
. Hence

\displaystyle\psi(\gamma)=\alpha\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta(w_{i})\cdot{{}^{*}}w_{i}\right)=\alpha\otimes\beta,

so
\psi
is a left inverse of
\phi
.
On the other hand, if
\gamma
belongs to the left dual of
V\otimes_{R}W
then

\displaystyle[\phi(\psi(\gamma))](v\otimes_{R}w)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi\big(\gamma(-\otimes_{R}w_{i})\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}{{}^{*}}w_{i})\big)(v\otimes w)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma(-\otimes_{R}w_{i})\big(v{}^{*}w_{i}(w)\big).

Thus the computation below implies that
\psi
is a right inverse of
\phi
as well

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma(-\otimes_{R}w_{i})\big(v{}^{*}w_{i}(w)\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma(v{}^{*}w_{i}(w)\otimes_{R}w_{i})=\gamma(v\otimes_{R}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{}^{*}w_{i}(w)w_{i})=\gamma(v\otimes_{R}w).

5.2
The graded dual of an
\boldsymbol{R}
-(co)ring

The left (right) dual of a left (right) finitely generated
R
-ring was first introduced in
[
3
, Section 17.9]
. This construction can be easily adapted for a left locally finite connected graded
R
-ring
A=\oplus_{n}A^{n}
. First, we define
{{}^{*\text{-gr}}}\!A=\oplus_{n}{{}^{*}}(A^{n})
, which is an
R^{\rm op}
-bimodule with respect to the actions defined in the previous subsection. When there is no risk of confusion, we shall drop the parentheses to avoid unnecessary clutter. As such, we will write
{{}^{*}}\!A^{n}
instead of
{{}^{*}}(A^{n})
.

Furthermore, for making
{{}^{*\text{-gr}}}\!A
a graded connected
R^{\rm op}
-coring, we consider the following diagram:

\textstyle{{{}^{*}}\!A^{n+m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{{{}^{*}}\mu_{n,m}}
\textstyle{{{}^{*}}\!A^{n+m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Delta_{n,m}}
\textstyle{{{}^{*}}(A^{n}\otimes_{R}A^{m})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi}
\textstyle{{{}^{*}}\!A^{n}\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}{{}^{*}}\!A^{m}.}

The leftmost vertical arrow denotes the transposed map of
\mu^{n,m}\colon A^{n}\otimes A^{m}\rightarrow A^{n+m}
, the component of the multiplication of
A
. The lower morphism
\psi
is the isomorphism described in the previous subsection. Then
\Delta_{n,m}:=\psi\circ{{}^{*}}\mu_{n,m}
is a morphism of
R^{\rm op}
-bimodules and the family
\{\Delta_{n,m}\}_{n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
induces a map
\Delta\colon{{}^{*\text{-gr}}}\!A\rightarrow{{}^{*\text{-gr}}}\!A\otimes{{}^{*\text{-gr}}}\!A
that respects the gradings on
{{}^{*\text{-gr}}}\!A
and
{{}^{*\text{-gr}}}\!A\otimes{{}^{*\text{-gr}}}\!A
.

Let
\alpha\in{{}^{*}}\!A^{n+m}
. One can show that the relation

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,m}(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\otimes_{R^{\rm op}}\alpha_{i}^{\prime\prime}

holds true for some
\alpha^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\alpha^{\prime}_{p}
and
\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{1},\dots,\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{p}
if and only if we have

\displaystyle\alpha(a^{\prime}a^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}(a^{\prime}\alpha_{i}^{\prime\prime}(a^{\prime\prime})),

(5.1)

for all
a^{\prime}\in A^{n}
and
a^{\prime\prime}\in A^{m}
. Using this equivalence it is easy to see now that
\Delta
defines a coassociative comultiplication on the left graded dual of
A
, which respects the grading on
{}^{*\text{-gr}}\!A
. Let us note that we have an isomorphism of rings
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}({{}_{R}}R,{{}_{R}}R)\cong R^{\rm op}
, so we can identify
{{}^{*}}\!A^{0}
and
R^{\rm op}
as
R^{\rm op}
-bimodules. This isomorphism can be extended in a unique way to an
R^{\rm op}
-bimodule morphism
\varepsilon\colon{{}^{*\text{-gr}}\!A}\to R^{\rm op}
so that it vanishes on all other homogeneous components of
{}^{*\text{-gr}}\!A
. Obviously,
({}^{*\text{-gr}}\!A,\Delta,\varepsilon)
is a connected graded
R^{\rm op}
-coring, which will be called the
graded left dual
R^{\rm op}
-coring
of
A
. For the comultiplication of
{}^{*\text{-gr}}\!A
we will use the Sweedler type notation

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,m}(\alpha)=\sum\alpha_{1,n}\otimes\alpha_{2,m}.

(5.2)

Thus, for
\alpha\in{}^{\ast}\!A^{n+m}
,
a^{\prime}\in A^{n}
and
a^{\prime\prime}\in A^{m}
, the relation (
5.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\alpha(a^{\prime}a^{\prime\prime})=\sum\alpha_{1,n}(a^{\prime}\alpha_{2,m}(a^{\prime\prime})).

(5.3)

Dually, to any graded connected
R
-coring
C
corresponds a graded connected
R^{\rm op}
-ring
{}^{*\text{-gr}}C
that we will call the
graded left dual of

C
. By definition we have
(^{*\text{-gr}}C)^{n}={}^{*}(C_{n})
. To simplify the notation we shall write
{}^{*}C_{n}
instead of
{}^{*}(C_{n})
. The
graded convolution product
of
\alpha\in{{}^{*}C}_{n}
and
\beta\in{{}^{*}C}_{m}
is given by the relation
\alpha*\beta=\mu^{n,m}(\alpha\otimes\beta)
. Hence, for
c\in C_{n+m}
, we have

\displaystyle(\alpha*\beta)(c)=\sum\alpha\big(c_{1,n}\beta(c_{2,m})\big).

The unit of the graded left dual of
C
coincides with the counit of
C
. Note that the graded left dual makes sense for a graded
R
-coring
C
which is not necessarily left locally finite.

In a similar way we can define the graded right dual
A^{{*\text{-gr}}}
of a right locally finite connected graded
R
-ring
A
and the graded right dual
C^{*\text{-gr}}
of a right locally finite connected graded
R
-coring
C
. One can prove that, for a locally finite
R
-ring
A
, there are canonical isomorphisms

(^{*\text{-gr}}\!A)^{*\text{-gr}}\cong A\cong{}^{*\text{-gr}}(A^{*\text{-gr}})
of graded
R
-rings. Similar isomorphisms can be proved for a locally finite
R
-coring
C
.

In order to investigate the Koszulity of the dual of a locally finite
R
-ring we shall use once again almost-Koszul pairs. More precisely, we have the following result.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(A,C)
be an almost-Koszul pair. If
A
and
C
are left locally finite, then the pair
({{}^{*\text{\rm-gr}}}C,{{}^{*\text{\rm-gr}}}\!A)
is almost-Koszul. Similarly, if
A
and
C
are right locally finite then
(C^{*\text{\rm-gr}},A^{*\text{\rm-gr}})
is almost-Koszul.

Proof.

Let
\theta:=\theta_{C,A}
denote the isomorphism from the definition of almost-Koszul pairs. We claim that
(^{*\text{-gr}}\!A,{}^{*\text{-gr}}C)
is almost-Koszul with respect to the transposed map
{}^{*}\theta\colon{}^{\ast}\!A^{2}\to{}^{\ast}C_{2}
. Clearly,
{}^{*}\theta
is an isomorphism of
R^{\rm op}
-bimodules, so we have to prove equation (
2.1
). For
\alpha\in{}^{*}\!A^{2}
and
c\in C_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\sum{}\big(^{*}\theta(\alpha_{1,1})*{}^{*}\theta(\alpha_{2,1})\big)(c)=\sum{}^{*}\theta(\alpha_{1,1})\big(c_{1,1}{}^{*}\theta(\alpha_{2,1})(c_{2,1})\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\sum{}\big(^*\theta(\alpha_{1,1})*{}^*\theta(\alpha_{2,1})\big)(c)}{}=\sum{}\alpha_{1,1}\big(\theta(c_{1,1})\alpha_{2,1}(\theta(c_{2,1}))\big)=\sum{}\alpha(\theta(c_{1,1})\theta(c_{2,1}))=0.

Note that the first and the second equalities follow by the definitions of the product of
{}^{*\text{-gr}}C
and of the transposed map, respectively. Taking into account the equivalence between the relations (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) we get the third equality. The ultimate equation holds as
\theta
satisfies the relation (
2.1
).

The fact that
(A^{*\text{-gr}},C^{*\text{-gr}})
is almost-Koszul can be proved in a similar way.
∎

We can take this result a step forward and prove that a Koszul pair corresponds by left duality to a Koszul pair.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
(A,C)
be a Koszul pair. If
A
and
C
are left locally finite, then
({{}^{*\text{\rm-gr}}}C,{{}^{*\text{\rm-gr}}}\!A)
is Koszul. Similarly, if
A
and
C
are right locally finite, then
(C^{*\text{\rm-gr}},A^{*\text{\rm-gr}})
is Koszul.

Proof.

Let us assume that
(A,C)
is a Koszul pair, so the complex
\operatorname{K}_{\bullet}^{l}({A},{C},m)
is exact for all
m>1
, see Section
2.6
. We will show that
\operatorname{K}^{\bullet}_{l}({}^{\ast\text{-gr}}C,{}^{\ast\text{-gr}}A,m)
is also exact, for all
m>0
, as a consequence of the fact that it is isomorphic to the graded left dual of
\operatorname{K}_{\bullet}^{l}(A,C,m)

6
Incidence (co)rings

In this final section of the article, we will use the general results developed in the first three sections, restricting ourselves to the locally finite context provided by the fourth section. The main direction of applications in this section is that of incidence algebras of finite graded posets, which we endow with
R
-(co)ring structures, such that our theory of Koszul pairs and Koszul corings could be applied. Lastly, we add that these results could be seen as a framework in which particular cases of Koszul posets could be studied, as per
[
9
, Section 2]
.

Since the theory is generally well established, we only state the particular hypotheses and notations which are used here. As such, we restrict our study to the case of
finite graded posets

\mathscr{P}
, i.e., those in which every maximal chain included in an interval is of the same length (which we denote by
l([x,y])
for the closed interval
[x,y]
). Let
\mathscr{I}_{p}
denote the set of all intervals of length
p
in the poset.

Fixing a base field
\Bbbk
, denote by
\Bbbk^{a}[\mathscr{P}]
the
incidence algebra of
\mathscr{P}
. As a vector space, it has a basis of the form
\mathscr{B}=\{e_{x,y}\,|\,x\leq y\}
and introducing a multiplication

\displaystyle e_{x,y}\cdot e_{z,u}=\delta_{y,z}e_{x,u},

which is extended by linearity, we obtain the associative unital algebra structure. Note furthermore that
\Bbbk^{a}[\mathscr{P}]
is an
R
-bimodule, where
R=\langle e_{x,x}\,|\,x\in\mathscr{P}\rangle\simeq\Bbbk^{|\mathscr{P}|}
is regarded as a
\Bbbk
-algebra as above and the actions are defined by the relation

\displaystyle e_{x,x}\cdot e_{y,z}\cdot e_{u,u}=\delta_{x,y}\delta_{z,u}e_{y,z}.

Finally,
\Bbbk^{a}[\mathscr{P}]
becomes an
R
-ring, which will be referred to as
the incidence
R
-ring of
\mathscr{P}
.

By duality, to every finite poset corresponds a coalgebra, namely its
incidence coalgebra

\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]
, which as a linear space coincides with
\Bbbk^{a}[\mathscr{P}]
. The comultiplication is given by the formula

\displaystyle\Delta(e_{x,y})=\sum_{z\in[x,y]}e_{x,z}\otimes_{\Bbbk}e_{z,x}.

The counit
\varepsilon
is uniquely defined such that
\varepsilon(e_{x,y})=\delta_{x,y}
.

It is not difficult to see that
\xi\circ\Delta\colon\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]\to\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]\otimes_{R}\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]
define an
R
-coring structure, where
\xi\colon\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]\otimes_{\Bbbk}\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]\to\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]\otimes_{R}\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]
is the canonical map. The counit of this coring maps
e_{x,y}
to
\delta_{x,y}e_{x,x}
. The comultiplication and the counit of
\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]
, regarded as a coring, will still be denoted by
\Delta
and
\varepsilon
. Let us notice that

\displaystyle\Delta(e_{x,y})=\sum_{z\in[x,y]}e_{x,z}\otimes e_{z,x},

where, as usual in this paper,
\otimes=\otimes_{R}
.

The relation between
\Bbbk^{a}[\mathscr{P}]
and
\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]
is explained in the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

We keep the notation from the preceding subsection. If
\mathscr{P}
is a finite graded poset then
\Bbbk^{a}[\mathscr{P}]\cong{}^{*\text{\rm-gr}}\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]
. In particular, the
R
-ring
\Bbbk^{a}[\mathscr{P}]
is Koszul if and only if the
R
-coring
\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]
is Koszul.

Proof.

Let
A:=\Bbbk^{a}[\mathscr{P}]
and
C:=\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]
. Therefore, we have to prove that there exists an isomorphism of graded
R
-rings
A\cong{{}^{*\text{-gr}}}C
. For two comparable elements
x\leq y
we define the
\Bbbk
-linear map
f_{x,y}\colon C_{p}\to R
by
f_{x,y}(e_{u,v})=\delta_{x,u}\delta_{y,v}e_{u,u}
, for all
u\leq v
such that
l([u,v])=p
. An easy computation shows that
f_{x,y}
is left
R
-linear. Moreover, if
f\in{{}^{*}}C_{p}
and
[x,y]\in\mathscr{I}_{p}
, then there is a a certain element
\alpha_{x,y}
in
\Bbbk
such that
f(e_{x,y})=\alpha_{x,y}e_{x,x}
. It follows that
f=\sum\limits_{[x,y]\in\mathscr{I}_{p}}\alpha_{x,y}f_{x,y}
. Thus
f
can be written in a unique way as linear combination of the elements of
\{f_{x,y}\,|\,{[x,y]\in\mathscr{I}_{p}}\}
, so this set is a linear basis of
{{}^{*}}C_{p}
.

For
x\leq y
,
z\leq t
and
v\leq w
one proves, by a straightforward computation, that

\displaystyle(f_{x,y}\ast f_{z,t})(e_{v,w})=\begin{cases}e_{v,v},&\text{if}\ y=z,\;x=v\ \text{and}\ t=w,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

On the other hand, by definition,
f_{x,t}(e_{v,w})=\delta_{x,v}\delta_{t,w}e_{v,v}
. Thus
f_{x,y}\ast f_{z,t}=\delta_{y,z}f_{x,t}
, for all
x
,
y
,
z
,
v
and
w
as above.

Summarizing, the
\Bbbk
-linear map
\chi_{p}\colon A^{p}\to{}^{*}C_{p}
, defined by
\chi_{p}(e_{x,y})=f_{x,y}
, for all
x\leq y
such that
l([x,y])=p
, is the component of degree
p
of an isomorphism of graded
R
-rings.

By Corollary
5.4
, if
C
is a Koszul coring, then
A\cong{}^{*\text{-gr}}C
is a Koszul ring. Both
A
and
C
are locally finite, being finite dimensional linear spaces. Thus,
C\cong{}(^{*\text{-gr}}C){}^{*\text{-gr}}\cong A^{*\text{-gr}}
. Hence
C
is Koszul, provided that
A
is so, cf. Corollary
5.3
.
∎

Definition 6.2
.

We say that a graded poset
\mathscr{P}
is
Koszul
if its incidence ring
\Bbbk^{a}[\mathscr{P}]
is Koszul.

For a graded poset
\mathscr{P}
let us denote the homogeneous component of degree
1
of its incidence ring by
V
. For every interval
[x,y]
of length
2
we define the element

\displaystyle\zeta_{x,y}:=\sum_{z\in(x,y)}e_{x,z}\otimes_{R}e_{z,y}.

Let
I_{\mathscr{P}}
denote the ideal generated in
T^{a}_{R}(V)
by the set
\{\zeta_{x,y}\,|\,l([x,y])=2\}
. With this notation in our hands, we have the following result.

Theorem 6.3
.

If
A
is the incidence ring of a Koszul poset
\mathscr{P}
, then the
R
-ring
T_{R}^{a}(V)/I_{\mathscr{P}}
is Koszul.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.1
the incidence coring
C:=\Bbbk^{c}[\mathscr{P}]
is Koszul. Hence, in view of Theorem
4.4
, the
R
-ring
C^{!}
is Koszul as well. We conclude by remarking that
C^{!}=T_{R}^{a}(V)/I_{\mathscr{P}}
, as
\zeta_{x,y}=\Delta_{1,1}(e_{x,y})
, for any interval
[x,y]
of length
2
.
∎
