ON MATRIX PRODUCT FACTORIZATION OF GRAPHS
Farzad Maghsoudi Babak Miraftab Sho Suda

Abstract
In this paper, we explore the concept of the “matrix product of graphs,” initially introduced by Prasad, Sudhakara, Sujatha, and M. Vinay. This operation involves the multiplication of adjacency matrices of two graphs with assigned labels, resulting in a weighted digraph. Our primary focus is on identifying graphs that can be expressed as the graphical matrix product of two other graphs. Notably, we establish that the only complete graph fitting this framework is K 4 ​ n + 1 K_{4n+1} , and moreover the factorization is not unique. In addition, the only complete bipartite graph that can be expressed as the graphical matrix product of two other graphs is K 2 ​ n , 2 ​ m K_{2n,2m} Furthermore, we introduce several families of graphs that exhibit such factorization and, conversely, some families that do not admit any factorization such as wheel graphs, friendship graphs, hypercubes and paths.

Introduction

The concept of the “matrix product of graphs” was introduced in the work by Prasad, Sudhakara, Sujatha, and M. Vinay, in

Factorization of Graphs via Matrix Product

A graph
X
without loops is a

if

can be expressed as a matrix product of adjacency matrices of two graphs.
More precisely we have:

Definition 3.1

A simple graph

((G,g),(H,h))
of simple graphs, and also a labeling of

The definition provided above naturally leads to the following question: which graphs are MPF? In the following, we will present a couple of lemmas that assist us in showing that certain families of graphs do not possess the MPF graph property.
First, we show that the property of being MFD for a given graph

Lemma 3.2

Assume a graph

of

((G,g),(H,h))
such that
A(X)=A(G)A(H)
.

Proof.

Let

be a graph on
n
vertices. Since

is an MPF graph there are labelings of
G
,

, and

A(G)A(H)=A(X)
.
We now assume that
Z
is the adjacency matrix

Clearly,

for some permutation matrix

A(G)A(H)=PZP^{-1}
. So, we have
(P^{-1}A(G)P)(P^{-1}A(H)P)=A(X)

∎

Definition 3.37
.

The circulant graph
Ci_{2n+1}(n-1,n)
is a graph on
2n+1
vertices where
V(Ci_{2n+1})=\{0,1,\ldots,2n\}
and
E(Ci_{2n+1})=\{i\sim i+n-1\pmod{2n+1}
and
i\sim i+n\pmod{2n+1}
for each
i\in V(Ci_{2n+1})
}.

Proposition 3.38
.

The circulant graph
Ci_{2n+1}(n-1,n)
is MPF for
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

Let
C_{2n+1}
and
C^{\prime}_{2n+1}
be cycles with
g\colon V(C_{2n+1})\to\{0,1,\ldots,2n\}
and
h\colon V(C_{2n+1})\to\{0,1,\ldots,2n\}
, respectively, such that
E(C_{2n+1})=\{i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i+1)\pmod{2n+1}\mid i\in\{0,1,\ldots,2n\}\}
and
E(C^{\prime}_{2n+1})=\{i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i+n)\pmod{2n+1}\mid i\in\{0,1,\ldots,2n\}\}
. We claim that these two cycles are disjoint. Suppose otherwise, there is an
e\in E(C_{2n+1})\cap E(C^{\prime}_{2n+1})
. Without loss of generality, assume one vertex on
e
is
i
, then
i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i+1)\pmod{2n+1}
and
i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i+n)\pmod{2n+1}
, which implies that
n-1\equiv 0\pmod{2n+1}
, a contradiction.

Now we show that the diamond condition in
2.9
holds. Without loss of generality assume the edges of
C
are coloured by blue and the edges of
C^{\prime}
are coloured by red in
C\oplus C^{\prime}
. Let
i\in V(C\oplus C^{\prime})
, then we have the following two diamonds in
C\oplus C^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i+1)~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i+1+n)~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i+n)~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~i,

\displaystyle i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i-1)~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i-1-n)~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i-n)~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~i.

This implies that the graph
C_{g}\ast_{h}C^{\prime}
contains the following two set of edges
E_{1}=\{i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i+n-1)\pmod{2n+1}\mid i\in\{0,1,\ldots,2n\}\}
and
E_{2}=\{i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~(i+n+1)\pmod{2n+1}\mid i\in\{0,1,\ldots,2n\}\}
.
Now suppose that there are two vertices
i,j\in V(C\oplus C^{\prime})
such that they are on two different diamonds as follows:

\displaystyle i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~\ell_{1}~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~j~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~\ell_{1}^{\prime}~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~i,

\displaystyle i~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~\ell_{2}~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~j~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~\ell_{2}^{\prime}~\mathord{\,\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vrule width=12.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-1.5pt}\,~i

for some
\ell_{1},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2},\ell_{2}^{\prime}\in V(C\oplus C^{\prime})
. Without loss of generality assume
\ell_{1}=(i+1)
, then either
j=(i+1+n)
or
j=(i+1-n)
. If
j=(i+1+n)
, then
\ell_{1}^{\prime}=(i+n)
and
\ell_{2}^{\prime}=(i+2+n)
. This implies that either
(i+2+n)-n\equiv i\pmod{2n+1}
or
(i+2+n)+n\equiv i\pmod{2n+1}
, a contradiction.
A similar argument works for
j=(i+1-n)
. Hence the diamond condition in
2.9
holds and

\displaystyle E(C_{g}\ast_{h}C^{\prime})=E_{1}\sqcup E_{2},

which implies that
C_{g}\ast_{h}C^{\prime}=Ci_{2n+1}(n-1,n)
, as desired.
∎
