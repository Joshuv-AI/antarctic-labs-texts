A sparse spectral method for fractional differential equations in one-spatial dimension
Ioannis P. A. Papadopoulos, Sheehan Olver

Abstract
We develop a sparse spectral method for a class of fractional differential equations, posed on ℝ \mathbb{R} , in one dimension. These equations may include sqrt-Laplacian, Hilbert, derivative, and identity terms. The numerical method utilizes a basis consisting of weighted Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind in conjunction with their Hilbert transforms. The former functions are supported on [ − 1 , 1 ] [-1,1] whereas the latter have global support. The global approximation space may contain different affine transformations of the basis, mapping [ − 1 , 1 ] [-1,1] to other intervals. Remarkably, not only are the induced linear systems sparse, but the operator decouples across the different affine transformations. Hence, the solve reduces to solving K K independent sparse linear systems of size 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) × 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n)\times\mathcal{O}(n) , with 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) nonzero entries, where K K is the number of different intervals and n n is the highest polynomial degree contained in the sum space. This results in an 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) complexity solve. Applications to fractional heat and wave equations are considered.

Our approach is to approximate the function
u
with a space of functions consisting of weighted Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind and their Hilbert transforms, the span of which we refer to as a

\mathcal{H}[\mathcal{H}[u]]=-u

(\lambda\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{H})
maps the sum space to itself and the operator representation of

is sparse. This method naturally extends to combinations of affine transformations of the sum space where the Hilbert transform decouples across the affine transformations. By constructing a dual sum space that contains the range of the derivative of the sum space, we exploit a relationship between the sqrt-Laplacian, derivative and Hilbert transform (see

2.10
) to solve

There exist many methods for tackling problems involving fractional Laplacian terms. Finite element methods (FEM) are popular, for instance see

This implies that special care must be taken for the imposed boundary conditions as the different definitions of the fractional Laplacian are no longer equivalent. Moreover, the solutions themselves are often heavy-tailed, i.e. they decay at the rate of a Cauchy distribution rather than a Gaussian (see

[

Spectral methods for tackling Helmholtz-like problems posed on

is a specialized algorithm for

However, depending on the behaviour of the solution, the algorithm requires different choices for the setup (for instance the specific choice of the transformation to the bounded domain) which induces some subtleties in the implementation. We emphasize their algorithms are effective, however, we believe our spectral method is better suited for certain classes of nonsmooth right-hand sides (see
Section

), solutions with singularities that naturally arise in the case of the sqrt-Laplacian, and is better conditioned. We prove that the linear system to compute the solution may be preconditioned with a diagonal preconditioner such that the condition number is

\epsilon

We reiterate some advantages of our method. We can handle the terms
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
and
\mathcal{H}
as well as
(-\Delta)^{1/2}
and
\mathcal{I}
, the former of which are not included in the aforementioned works. Moreover, the ability to contain multiple affine transformations of the reference sum space allows one to better handle regions of discontinuity in the data resulting in better approximations. The resulting linear systems are banded and sparse. Each solve is divided into independent well-conditioned solves, that can be computed in parallel, involving only the functions contained in the same affine transformed sum space. This results in quick solve times. Moreover, there exists a diagonal preconditioner such that the preconditioned linear system has an
\mathcal{O}(1)
condition number, independent of the truncation degree or interval choice. The evaluation of the solution requires the integral of four independent special functions per differently scaled sum space, and if all sum spaces have the same scaling (i.e. they are just translations), we require only four integrals in total. The special functions are not dependent on the right-hand side of
Eq.

1.1
, however, they are dependent on
\lambda
,
\mu
, and
\eta
.

Given that the different affine transformed sum spaces have overlapping support, the fact that the operator decouples across the sum spaces seems implausible at first glance. There must be communication between the sum spaces for continuity purposes. The communication occurs during the expansion of the right-hand side: a process that cannot be decoupled across different affine transformed sum spaces. Hence, the computational expense shifts from the solve of the equation to an interpolation of a known function
[
2
,
3
]
.

Remark 1.1
.

As the sum spaces centred at multiple intervals are combined together to form the overall approximation space, it is tempting to call this a spectral
element
method. We avoid using the terminology “element” as it may be confusing. The support of a sum space function centred at an interval is not necessarily contained within the interval. In fact the support of the approximation space is always
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the expansion of the right-hand side is slightly more difficult than a typical finite/spectral element method as the sum space functions centred at different intervals all interact. However, we emphasize that the operator
L_{\lambda,\mu,\eta}
decouples across sum space functions centred at different intervals. Hence, given an expansion of the right-hand side, the solve complexity only increases linearly with the number of intervals used and the solve over different intervals can be done in parallel.

3
Sum space

The goal of this section is to define the four families of functions found in
Fig.

1
. The first row in
Fig.

1
form the primal set of functions that we approximate our solution with. The action of
(-\Delta)^{1/2}

By contrast, we construct the identity map from the top left family to the bottom right, and from the top right to the bottom left. These identity mappings only have two diagonals in their induced matrices that are non-zero entries. Throughout this work, we use the convention

Definition 3.1
.

Consider a Hilbert space
H
. An indexed family of functions
\{\phi_{n}\}\in H
is called a

for

if there exist constants

such that

\displaystyle c\|f\|^{2}_{H}\leq\sum_{n}|\langle f,\phi_{n}\rangle_{H}|^{2}\leq C\|f\|^{2}_{H}\;\;\text{for all}\;\;f\in H.

(3.13)

Definition 3.2

(Weighted Lebesgue space)
.

Let
w:[a,b]\to[c,\infty)
,
c>0
, be a measurable function. Then, the weighted Lebesgue space
L^{p}_{w}(a,b)
is equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{L^{p}_{w}(a,b)}\coloneqq(\int_{a}^{b}|f|^{p}w\,\mathrm{d}x)^{1/p}
. If
p=2
, then
L^{2}_{w}(a,b)
is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product
(f,g)_{L^{2}_{w}(a,b)}\coloneqq\int_{a}^{b}fgw\,\mathrm{d}x
.

The following proposition is equivalent to a similar result by Huybrechs
[
32
, Cor. 3.2]
concerning a frame for Fourier extensions, as our space restricted to
[-1,1]
is equivalent to the Fourier basis functions restricted to
[-1,1]
.

Proposition 3.3

(Frame)
.

Let
w(x)\coloneqq(1-x^{2})_{+}^{-1/2}
. Then, the sum space
S
, for
x\in(-1,1)
, is a frame on the weighted Lebesgue space
L^{2}_{w}(-1,1)
.

Proof.

The families of Chebyshev polynomials
T_{n}(x)
and
U_{n}(x)
are orthogonal with respect to the weights
(1-x^{2})^{-1/2}_{+}
and
(1-x^{2})^{1/2}_{+}
, respectively on

. Consider any
f\in L^{2}_{w}(-1,1)
. By noting
\tilde{T}_{n}(x)=T_{n}(x)
for

and two applications of Parseval’s identity, we see that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[|\langle f,\tilde{T}_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{w}(-1,1)}|^{2}+|\langle f,W_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}_{w}(-1,1)}|^{2}\right]\\
&\indent=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[|\langle f,wT_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(-1,1)}|^{2}+|\langle fw,W_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(-1,1)}|^{2}\right]\\
&\indent\indent=\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{w}(-1,1)}+\|fw\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{1/w}(-1,1)}=2\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{w}(-1,1)}.\end{split}

(3.14)

Thus the lower and upper bound constants in
Eq.

3.13
are

.
∎

Proposition 3.4

(Equality and derivatives)

Consider the interval

,
a<b
. Then, for any

,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}W^{I}_{n}(x)=-\frac{2(n+1)}{b-a}V^{I}_{n+1}(x).

\displaystyle W^{I}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2}[V^{I}_{n}(x)-V^{I}_{n+2}(x)].

Proof.

All three identities

[

We consider

. Consider the region

. From the definition of

, for

and

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{n}(x)=U_{n}(x),\;n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\;\;\text{for}\;\;x\in[-1,1],

where

are the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind. Hence, another classical identity
[

reveals that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\tilde{T}_{n}(x)=n\tilde{U}_{n-1}(x)\;\;\text{for}\;x\in(-1,1).

Consider

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\tilde{T}_{1}(x)=\tilde{U}_{0}(x)\;\;\text{and}\;\;\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\tilde{T}_{2}(x)=2\tilde{U}_{1}(x).

Moreover, another direct calculation shows that, for

\displaystyle n\tilde{U}_{n-1}(x)=2n\tilde{T}_{n-1}(x)+n\tilde{U}_{n-3}(x)=2n\tilde{T}_{n-1}(x)+\frac{n}{n-2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\tilde{T}_{n-2}(x),

4.1
Operators

For any

, we denote the
n
-th sum space and dual sum space quasimatrices, centred at an interval
I=[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
, by

\displaystyle{\bf S}^{I}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{T}_{0}(x)\;\vline&W^{I}_{0}(x)&\tilde{T}^{I}_{1}(x)\;\vline&\cdots\;\;\;\vline&W^{I}_{n}(x)&\tilde{T}^{I}_{n+1}(x)\end{pmatrix}

(4.1)

\displaystyle{\bf S}^{I,*}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{U}^{I}_{-2}(x)\;\vline&V^{I}_{0}(x)&\tilde{U}^{I}_{-1}(x)\;\vline&\cdots\;\;\;\vline&V^{I}_{n+2}(x)&\tilde{U}^{I}_{n+1}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(4.2)

Note that

and

. A quasimatrix is a matrix whose “columns” are functions defined on
\mathbb{R}

[

. In

the first column contains the constant function. Thereafter, we block together the columns

and
\tilde{T}^{I}_{k+1}(x)
for

. Similarly in
{\bf S}^{I,*}_{n}(x)
, the first column contains
\tilde{U}^{I}_{-2}(x)
. Thereafter, we block together the columns
V^{I}_{k}(x)
and
\tilde{U}^{I}_{k-1}(x)
for

. We note that
{\bf S}^{I,*}_{n}(x)
has four more columns than
{\bf S}^{I}_{n}(x)
. These are required to represent the identity map between the two sum spaces exactly.

Let

4.2
Single interval

We now collect the final ingredients for the spectral method centred on a single interval at

to solve

. Consider the expansion of the right-hand side

\displaystyle f(x)\approx{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}(x)\bm{\mathbf{f}}_{n}^{*},\;\;\bm{\mathbf{f}}_{n}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+7}.

Consider the matrix

is a linear operator mapping from the sum space

to the dual sum space
{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}(x)
. However,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n+7)\times(2n+3)}
is a rectangular matrix and the linear system

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}_{n}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{n}=\bm{\mathbf{f}}^{*}_{n},\;\;\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+3},

(4.9)

to find the coefficients of

in the truncated sum space is overdetermined. We wish to construct a method where the expansion of

is determined exactly i.e. we want to avoid a least squares solution for
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{n}
. Let
{{\bf S}}^{\circ}_{n}(x)
denote the sum space
{{\bf S}}_{n}(x)
without the first block, i.e. we drop the first function
\tilde{T}_{0}(x)
. We construct the following

\displaystyle{{\bf S}}^{+}_{n}(x)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{T}_{0}(x)\;\vline&v_{0}(x)&\tilde{u}_{-1}(x)\;\vline&v_{1}(x)&\tilde{u}_{0}(x)\;\vline&{{\bf S}}^{\circ}_{n}(x)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\mu,\eta}\tilde{u}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}_{n}(x),

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\mu,\eta}v_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=V_{n}(x).

(4.12)

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{\circ}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n+7)\times(2n+2)}
denote the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}_{n}
without the first column. By considering the map induced by
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\mu,\eta}
from the appended sum space
{{\bf S}}^{+}_{n}(x)
to the dual sum space
{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}(x)
, we see that the solution of

1.1
can be approximated by solving

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{+}_{n}\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{+}_{n}=\bm{\mathbf{f}}^{*}_{n},\;\;\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{+}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+7},

(4.13)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{+}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n+7)\times(2n+7)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{+}_{n}\coloneqq\scriptsize\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda&0&0&0&0&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\\
0&1&0&0&0&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\\

Remark 4.3
.

The choice of
v_{0}(x),\tilde{u}_{-1}(x),v_{1}(x)
and
\tilde{u}_{0}(x)
as the additional functions is not the only option. Indeed, if the goal was to improve the conditioning of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{+}_{n}
, then a better choice would be
\tilde{u}_{n+1}(x)
and
v_{n+2}(x)
rather than
\tilde{u}_{0}(x)
and
v_{1}(x)
. In this case, the conditioning of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{+}_{n}
only grows linearly with
n
, is robust for large parameter ranges of
\lambda,\mu
and
\eta
, and there exists a diagonal preconditioner such that the condition number is independent of

as described in
Section

5.3
. With the presented choice, the conditioning degrades if both
\mu\to 0
and
\lambda\to 0
. The disadvantage of
\tilde{u}_{n+1}(x)
and
v_{n+2}(x)
is that if multiple solves are required with different degrees
n
, then additional work is required to compute the required additional functions. Furthermore, in numerical experiments, we observed that the approximate identity mapping from the appended sum space
{{\bf S}}^{+}_{n}(x)
to the dual sum space
{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}(x)
is unstable when using
\tilde{u}_{n+1}(x)
and
v_{n+2}(x)
as the additional functions. Hence, for discretized time-dependent problems where we are required to map the current solution iterate expanded in the appended sum space to the expansion in the dual sum space (for the right-hand side), this poses a distinct issue. The choice of the additional functions is context dependent.

4.3
Multiple intervals

Although the sum space is dense on the interval it is centred on, it is not dense in
\mathbb{R}
and, therefore, cannot approximate general solutions. By combining intervals together we enlarge the subset of density in
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, in some applications, it can be helpful to decompose the domain into multiple intervals. This can be in regions where (spatially varying) coefficients or right-hand sides
f
have discontinuities best accounted for by approximating them with sum spaces centred over two or more intervals. Experimentally, we also found that the tails of a function
f
outside the intervals were better approximated when using the combination of sum spaces centred at multiple intervals. Examples of low-order multiple-interval sum space functions are given in
Fig.

2
.

In this section, we will discuss how to combine sum spaces centred at multiple intervals together. We will show that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\lambda,\eta}
decouples across the different affine transformed sum spaces and this results in a linear system with a block diagonal matrix. Hence, each block can be solved separately resulting in a highly parallelizable method.

centred at

and
[1,3]
.

,

.

5
Implementation notes

5.1
Expansion

As the family of approximating functions is not orthogonal, the expansion of a known function,
f(x)
, in the sum space or its dual is nontrivial. In particular, the expansion of a general function need not be unique. For our purposes, we desire an expansion that approximates our known function to the required tolerance and the coefficients of the expansion are (relatively) small in magnitude.

Aside from the known function
f
being an obvious composition of the functions in
\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I}}_{\bm{n}}(x)
, there are two cases to consider:

1.

f
is compactly supported on the real line and the intervals
I_{k}=[a_{k},b_{k}]
,
k=1,\dots,K
, are chosen such that either
f(x)\to 0
or
|f(x)|\to\infty
as
x\to a_{k}
and
x\to b_{k}
;

2.

Any function that does not fit under the first case.

In the first case, if
f(x)\to 0
at the endpoints of the interval
I_{k}
for some
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, then it might be well approximated in that interval by an expansion in
W^{I_{k}}_{n}(x)
. Similarly, if
f
blows up at the interval endpoints it might be well represented by an expansion in
V^{I_{k}}_{n}(x)
. The advantage of only expanding
f(x)
in
W^{I_{k}}_{n}(x)
and
V^{I_{k}}_{n}(x)
is that the coefficients of the expansion can be quickly computed to any given tolerance with an adaptive algorithm based on the discrete cosine transform that takes
\mathcal{O}(Kn\log n)
for
n
coefficients and
K
intervals
[
6
,
27
]
. If we partially expand
f(x)
in
V^{I_{k}}_{n}(x)
for some
k
, but we require the expansion in the dual sum space, we utilize the identity mappings as defined in Propositions
4.1
and
4.5
.

For functions that are not contained in the first case, we turn to the techniques used in
frame
theory
[
2
]
. Essentially, we find the coefficients of the expansion that optimally interpolate the values of
f(x)
(or a linear operator applied to
f
) at a set of collocation points in a least squares sense. Consider the collocation points
\bm{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})
. We note that the set of collocation points should include points outside the intervals in order to ensure the tails also match. The least-squares matrix for the dual sum space is given by:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{ij}=[l_{i}[\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I},*}_{\bm{n}}]]_{j},\;\;i=1\,\dots,M,\;j=1,\dots,1+2N+6K),

(5.1)

where
\{l_{i}\}
are a set of linear operators. Common choices include the identity, e.g.
l_{i}[\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I},*}_{\bm{n}}]=\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I},*}_{\bm{n}}(x_{i})
or linear operators designed to emulate Riemann sums
l_{i}[\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I},*}_{\bm{n}}]=(x_{i+1}-x_{i-1})\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I},*}_{\bm{n}}(x_{i})
. Similarly we compute
\bm{\mathbf{b}}_{i}=l_{i}[f]
and we solve the following least-squares problem for the expansion coefficients
\bm{\mathbf{f}}^{*}_{\bm{n}}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\mathbf{f}}}\|\bm{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\mathbf{f}}^{*}_{\bm{n}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2},

(5.2)

so that
f(x)\approx\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I},*}_{\bm{n}}(x)\bm{\mathbf{f}}^{*}_{\bm{n}}
. The same technique can be used to find expansions in
\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I}}_{\bm{n}}(x)
. It is well known that the least-squares matrix in
Eq.

5.2
is ill-conditioned for increasing
M
,
K
and
n
. However, we still recover suitable least squares solutions if we sufficiently oversample the collocation points and an
\epsilon
-truncated SVD solver is used, cf.
[
46
, Sec. 5]
. We refer to the work of Adcock and Huybrechs for further details
[
2
,
3
]
.

5.2
Approximate identity map from
{\bf S}_{n}^{+}(x)
to
{\bf S}_{n}^{*}(x)

In our method, we find the approximate solution expanded in the appended sum space. A requirement in some problems is to reuse the computed solution as part of the right-hand side of the next solve. Hence, after each solve, we must map the appended sum space expansion of our current iterate to the expansion in the dual sum space. Propositions
4.1
and
4.5
provide identity mappings for the sum space. Hence, it remains to map the coefficients of the functions found in the appended sum space that are not in the sum space.

We outline the approach when utilizing a single interval at
[-1,1]
in the solver and briefly mention the extension to multiple intervals. Given
\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{+}_{n}
, the goal is to find the coefficient vector,
\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{n}
, such that

\displaystyle u(x)\approx{\bf S}_{n}^{+}(x)\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{+}_{n}={\bf S}_{n}^{*}(x)\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{n}.

(5.3)

The first step is to expand the four functions
v_{0}(x),\tilde{u}_{-1}(x),v_{1}(x)
and
\tilde{u}_{0}(x)
in the sum space and collect the coefficients in the vectors
\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{0},\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}_{-1},\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
, and
\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}_{0}
, respectively. Next we form the identity mapping from
{{\bf S}}^{+}_{n}(x)
to
{\bf S}_{n}(x)
, denoted
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{n}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{n}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&\vline&\vline&\vline&\vline&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{0}&\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}_{-1}&\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}&\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}_{0}&1&&\\
\vdots&\vline&\vline&\vline&\vline&&\ddots&\\
0&\vline&\vline&\vline&\vline&&&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n+3)\times(2n+7)}.

(5.4)

Essentially
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{n}
is constructed by assembling the
(2n+3)\times(2n+3)
identity matrix and adding the four coefficient vectors, of the additional functions, in
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{n}
between the first and second columns. The approximate identity operator
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n+7)\times(2n+7)}
such that
{\bf S}_{n}^{+}(x)\approx{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}(x)\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{n}
is defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}_{n}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n+7)\times(2n+7)},

(5.5)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{n}
is the identity operator from
{\bf S}_{n}(x)
to
{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}(x)
as defined in
Proposition

4.1
.

For multiple intervals, we denote the identity mapping as
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}^{\bm{I}}_{\bm{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(1+2N+2K)\times(1+2N+6K)}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}^{\bm{I}}_{\bm{n}}:\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I},+}_{\bm{n}}(x)\to\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I}}_{\bm{n}}(x)
, where
K
is the number of intervals and consists of the identity matrix with an additional
4K
dense columns in the positions of the additional functions in the appended sum space. As before, we define
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\bm{I}}_{\bm{n}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\bm{n}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}^{\bm{I}}_{\bm{n}}
so that
\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I},+}_{\bm{n}}(x)\approx\bm{\mathbf{S}}^{\bm{I},*}_{\bm{n}}(x)\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}^{\bm{I}}_{\bm{n}}
.

5.3
Numerical conditioning

Two potential sources of numerical ill-conditioning in
Algorithm

1
are:

1.

The expansion of the right-hand side
f
to find a coefficient vector
{\bf f}^{\bm{I},*}_{\bm{n}}
;

2.

Inverting the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{I},+}
to find
{\bf u}^{\bm{I},+}_{\bm{n}}
.

In
Corollary

6.1
, we prove that the dual sum space is a frame. Leveraging recent analysis on frames (which are overdetermined approximation spaces) it is known that an
\epsilon
-truncated SVD factorization alleviates the ill-conditioning that one might expect in solving such a least-squares problem. Hence, the expansion of the right-hand side is an understood and well-conditioned problem, resolving (1).

For (2), we note that the 2-norm condition number
\kappa(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{I},+})
only grows linearly with
n_{\text{max}}=\max(n_{1},\dots,n_{K})
and is independent on the number of intervals in the approximation space
K
due the block diagonal structure of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{I},+}
. Moreover, there exists a
diagonal
right preconditioner
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{I}}
such that
\kappa(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{I},+}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{I}})^{-1})=C
where
C
is independent of
\bm{n}
. This is typical of a coefficient-based spectral method, cf.
[
44
, Sec. 4.1]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Consider the interval
I=[-1,1]
,
\lambda=\mu=\eta=1
, and choose the appended sum space functions
\tilde{u}_{-1}
,
\tilde{u}_{n+1}

, and

as defined in
Section

4.2
. Then

\displaystyle\kappa(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{+}_{n})=\mathcal{O}(n),

(5.6)

where

denotes the 2-norm condition number of a matrix. Moreover, consider the diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}_{5}&\\
&\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}_{n}\end{pmatrix},\;\;\text{where}\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}1&&&&&&\\
&1&&&&&\\
&&2&&&&\\
&&&2&&&\\
&&&&\ddots&&\\
&&&&&n+1&\\
&&&&&&n+1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2n+2)\times(2n+2)},

(5.7)

and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{I}}_{5}

, such that

is independent of

and

\displaystyle\kappa(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}^{+}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}_{n})^{-1})=C.

The result follows by showing that

.
∎

.

Numerically we found that
C\approx 3.1
where

.

A diagonal preconditioner may be constructed for the multiple-interval case as follows:

The goal of this section is to prove that the dual sum space is a frame for the right-hand side

.

Let
w(x)=(1-x^{2})^{-1/2}_{+}
. Then the dual space
\hat{S}^{*}
is a frame on
(H^{1/2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R}))^{*}
.

Proof.

From
Theorem

6.1
and an application of Theorem 5.1 in
[
46
]
, we may deduce that
\{\hat{V}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\cup\{\hat{U}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a frame on
(H^{1/2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R}))^{*}
.

It remains to prove the frame bound condition for the remaining dual sum space functions
\tilde{U}_{-2}(x)
and
\tilde{U}_{-1}(x)
, and
V_{0}(x)
. The lower bound follows trivially. Moreover, it may be shown that
\tilde{U}_{-2},\tilde{U}_{-1},V_{0}\in(H^{1/2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}))^{*}
. Hence, the upper bound also follows.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Consider the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\lambda,\eta}
where
\mu,\lambda>0
and
\eta=0
. Let the weight
w(x)=(1-x^{2})^{-1/2}_{+}
. Suppose that
f\in H^{*}=(H^{1/2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R}))^{*}
. Consider the degree
n
. Suppose that an
\epsilon
-truncated SVD factorization is utilized to discover a coefficient vector
{\bf f}^{*}_{n}
such that
f(x)\approx\hat{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}(x){\bf f}^{*}_{n}
and fix
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}^{+}_{n}{\bf u}^{+}_{n}={\bf f}^{*}_{n}
. Here
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}^{+}_{n}
is the scaled matrix
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}^{+}_{n}
with respect to the orthonormalized sum space. Then, there exist constants
C,\kappa^{\epsilon}_{M,n},\theta^{\epsilon}_{M,n}>0
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|u-\hat{{\bf S}}^{+}_{n}{\bf u}^{+}_{n}\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&\leq C\inf_{{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+7}}\left\{\|f-\hat{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}{\bf v}\|_{H^{*}}+\kappa_{M,\bm{n}}^{\epsilon}\|f-\hat{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}{\bf v}\|_{M}+\epsilon\theta^{\epsilon}_{M,\bm{n}}\|{\bf v}\|_{\ell^{2}}\right\},\end{split}

(6.4)

where
\|u\|_{M}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{M}|l_{i}[u]|^{2}
and
l_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,M
are defined in
Section

5.1
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|u-\hat{{\bf S}}^{+}_{n}{\bf u}^{+}_{n}\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\lambda,0}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\lambda,0}(u-\hat{{\bf S}}^{+}_{n}{\bf u}^{+}_{n})\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&\indent=\|\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\lambda,0}^{-1}(f-\hat{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}^{+}_{n}{\bf u}^{+}_{n})\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&\indent=\|\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\lambda,0}^{-1}(f-\hat{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}{\bf f}^{*}_{n})\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&\indent\leq\|\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\lambda,0}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}))}\|f-\hat{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}{\bf f}^{*}_{n}\|_{H^{*}}.\end{split}

(6.5)

Since
H^{*}\subset H^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have that there exists a
C>0
such that
\|\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\lambda,0}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{*},H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}))}\leq C
.
Corollary

6.1
and an application of Theorem 3.7 in
[
3
]
achieves the result.
∎

Remark 6.1
.

The constants
\theta_{M,n}^{\epsilon}
and
\kappa_{M,n}^{\epsilon}
are problem and frame dependent. Ideally their magnitude is
\mathcal{O}(1)
, in which case we are guaranteed to reach an accuracy of
\epsilon
if
M
and
n
are taken sufficiently large. For a thorough discussion of the constants and their behaviour, we refer the reader to
[
3
]
.

A full convergence analysis for
\lambda,\mu,\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in H^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R})
, and sum spaces consisting of multiple intervals is nontrivial and beyond the scope of this work.

7.4
Fractional heat equation

Consider the fractional heat equation, i.e. the following time-dependent fractional PDE:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}u(x,t)+(-\Delta)^{1/2}u(x,t)=0,\;\;u(x,t)\to 0\;\text{as}\;|x|\to\infty.\end{split}

(7.11)

We pick the following two choices for the initial condition:

\displaystyle u(x,0)

\displaystyle=(x^{2}+1)^{-1},

(7.12)

\displaystyle u(x,0)

\displaystyle=W_{0}(x).

(7.13)

The initial condition
Eq.

7.12

t(2\pi(x^{2}+t^{2}))^{-1}

[
61

7.11

\displaystyle u(x,t)=\frac{1+t}{x^{2}+(1+t)^{2}}.

We discretize

k\geq 0

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda u_{k+1}(x)+(-\Delta)^{1/2}u_{k+1}(x)&=\lambda u_{k}(x),\\
u_{k+1}(x)&\to 0\;\text{as}\;|x|\to\infty,\end{split}

(7.15)

where
u_{0}(x)=(x^{2}+1)^{-1}
or
W_{0}(x)
and
\lambda=(\Delta t)^{-1}
where
\Delta t
is the time step. By choosing
\mu=\eta=0
, we recover the equation

1.1

,

\displaystyle(\lambda\bm{\mathbf{E}}+\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\bm{I}})\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}=\lambda(\bm{\mathbf{E}}\bm{\mathbf{R}})\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{k}.

\bm{\mathbf{E}}

7.5
Wave propagation

Consider the fractional Hilbert wave equation:

\displaystyle[(-\Delta)^{1/2}+\mathcal{H}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}]u(x,t)=f(x,t).

(7.20)

After a Fourier transform with respect to
t
, we recover the equation

\displaystyle[(-\Delta)^{1/2}+\mathcal{H}-\omega^{2}]\hat{u}(x,\omega)=\hat{f}(x,\omega).

(7.21)

Equation
Eq.

7.21
is of the form
Eq.

1.1
with
\lambda=-\omega^{2}
,
\mu=1
, and
\eta=0
. Thus the idea is to solve
Eq.

7.21
to find
\hat{u}(x,\omega)
, for a range of values of
\omega
, and then take the inverse Fourier transform to recover the approximation of the physical solution
u(x,t)
. We choose the datum

\displaystyle f(x,t)=W_{4}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}}\implies\hat{f}(x,\omega)=\sqrt{\pi}W_{4}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\omega^{2}/4}.

(7.22)

This corresponds to a forcing term supported on
x\in[-1,1]
that exponentially decays in time.

To fix a unique solution, we enforce that
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\hat{u}(x,\omega)\to 0
. For this example, we approximate the additional functions
\tilde{u}_{-1}(x)
,
\tilde{u}_{n+1}(x)
,
v_{0}(x)
and
v_{n+2}(x)
via an FFT (as described in
Appendix

A
). We pick
n=7
and a uniform distribution of
\omega
in the range
[0,20]
in increments of 1/10. The inverse Fourier transform from
\hat{u}(x,\omega)
to

is approximated via an FFT. We provide the contour plots of the approximate solutions of
\hat{u}(x,\omega)
and
u(x,t)

8
Conclusions

In this work we introduced a sparse spectral method for a one-dimensional fractional PDE posed on an unbounded domain that may contain the identity, Hilbert, derivative, and sqrt-Laplacian operators. The method is constructed by forming a sum space of weighted Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind extended to the whole of
\mathbb{R}
by zero and their Hilbert transforms. We derive explicit identities for the actions of the identity, Hilbert, derivative, and sqrt-Laplacian operators which allows us to build our method. The operator applied to different affine transformations of the sum spaces decouples during the solve. Hence, the solve can be performed in parallel over each interval separately. Moreover, the induced matrices are sparse leading to fast sparse solves. Numerically, we observe spectral convergence when the data is smooth. We emphasize that the coupling of the different interval sum spaces occurs during the expansion of the right-hand side.

In future work we plan to orthogonalize the basis as well as extend the method to general fractional Laplacians,
(-\Delta)^{s}
,
s\in(0,1)
by considering weighted Jacobi polynomials and their fractional Laplacian analogues
[
29
, Tab. 5]
. This will be achieved by combining the techniques introduced in
[
46
]
for expanding the right-hand side with the methods developed in
[
30
]
for the construction of linear systems. Moreover, by considering Zernike polynomials, the spectral method will be extended to two-dimensional problems posed on a disk
[
29
, Tab. 7]
.

Appendix A
Approximate inverse Fourier transform via the FFT

The setup of the spectral method requires four inverse Fourier transforms per interval. Although this can be reduced if the intervals are translations on
\mathbb{R}
and are of the same size as resulting solutions are simply the equivalent translations. In this section we describe how to use the FFT to approximate the inverse Fourier transform. The FFT implements the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) or its inverse (IDFT) with
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
complexity where
N
is the size of the input vector. Consider a vector
\bm{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We denote the IDFT as
\mathcal{F}_{D}^{-1}
and define it as (as implemented in
ifft
of
FFTW.jl
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{D}^{-1}[\bm{\mathbf{u}}]_{j}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi(j-1)(n-1)}{N}\right)\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{n},\;\;j=1,\dots,N.

(A.1)

Suppose that
N
is even. Then, we also define the shifted IDFT,
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{D}^{-1}
such that the components of
\mathcal{F}_{D}^{-1}[\bm{\mathbf{u}}]
are reordered from
j=N/2+1,\dots,N,1,\dots,N/2
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{D}^{-1}[\bm{\mathbf{u}}]_{j}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{F}_{D}^{-1}[\bm{\mathbf{u}}]_{j+N/2}&1\leq j\leq N/2,\\
\mathcal{F}_{D}^{-1}[\bm{\mathbf{u}}]_{j-N/2}&N/2+1\leq j\leq N.\end{cases}

(A.2)

This is implemented as
iffshift(ifft(u))
in
FFTW.jl
. Consider the approximation,
j=1,\dots,N
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}[u](x_{j})\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-W}^{W}u(\omega)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega x_{j}}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\approx\frac{\delta}{2\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u(\omega_{n})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}x_{j}}.

(A.3)

Here we choose the parameters
W\gg 1
and
N\gg 1
,
N
even. The points
\omega_{n}\coloneqq n\delta-W
,
n=0,\dots,N-1
, where
\delta\coloneqq 2W/N
. Substituting in the definition of
\omega_{n}
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{2\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u(\omega_{n})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}x_{j}}=\frac{\delta N\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Wx_{j}}}{2\pi}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u(\omega_{n})\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}x_{j}Wn/N}\right].

(A.4)

Define the vector
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{n}=u(\omega_{n-1})
,
n=1,\dots,N
. Moreover, we fix
x_{j}
as

\displaystyle x_{j}\coloneqq(-N/2+j-1)\frac{\pi}{W},\;\;j=1,\dots,N.

(A.5)

Then, the right-hand side of
Eq.

A.4
is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\delta N\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Wx_{j}}}{2\pi}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(j-1)(n-1)/N}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi(n-1)}\right].

(A.6)

By noting that
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi(n-1)}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi(n-1)}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, then for
1\leq j\leq N/2
,
Eq.

A.6
is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\delta N\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Wx_{j}}}{2\pi}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(j+N/2-1)(n-1)/N}\right],

(A.7)

and for
N/2+1\leq j\leq N
,
Eq.

A.6
is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\delta N\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Wx_{j}}}{2\pi}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(j-N/2-1)(n-1)/N}\right].

(A.8)

Hence, by the definition of the shifted IDFT in
Eq.

A.2
, we see that, for
j,n=1,\dots,N
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}[u](x_{j})\approx\frac{\delta N\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Wx_{j}}}{2\pi}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{D}^{-1}[\bm{\mathbf{u}}]_{j},

(A.9)

where
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{n}=u(2W(n-1)/N-W)
,
n=1,\dots,N
.

Appendix B
Approximating the appended sum space functions

With a naïve approach, it would take four integrals per interval to approximate the additional functions found in the appended sum space. This setup cost may become prohibitive for a large number of intervals. However, the next proposition reveals that the additional functions on intervals that are translated (and not scaled) are simply translations of the additional functions associated to one reference interval.

Proposition B.1

(Translations of the reference interval)
.

Consider the interval
I=[a,b]
and its associated reference interval
I_{R}=[-(b-a)/2,(b-a)/2]
. Then,

\displaystyle\begin{split}v^{I}_{0}(x)&=v^{I_{R}}_{0}(x-(a+b)/2),\;\;\tilde{u}^{I}_{-1}(x)=\tilde{u}^{I_{R}}_{-1}(x-(a+b)/2),\\
v^{I}_{1}(x)&=v^{I_{R}}_{1}(x-(a+b)/2),\;\;\tilde{u}^{I}_{0}(x)=\tilde{u}^{I_{R}}_{0}(x-(a+b)/2).\end{split}

(B.1)

Proof.

The result follows from the identity
\mathcal{F}[f(\diamond+\alpha)](\omega)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha\omega}\mathcal{F}[f(\diamond)](\omega)
and a routine calculation.
∎

The additional functions on the reference interval are approximated with one integral each. Moreover, all intervals of the same width map to the same reference interval. Hence, the setup of the method only requires four integrals per interval of different width. In particular, if all the intervals have the same width, we only require four integrals in total for the setup, irrespective of the number of intervals used.

Appendix C
Special cases when
\lambda=0

In this subsection we discuss the conditioning of
L^{+}
for choices of
\mu
and
\eta
when
\lambda=0
. The ill-conditioning is alleviated by removing the rows of
L^{+}
associated with functions that are no longer in the range of
\mathcal{L}_{0,\mu,\eta}S_{n}^{+}
.

C.1
\lambda=\mu=\eta=0

The first special case that we consider is
\lambda=\mu=\eta=0
. Here,
Eq.

1.1
reduces to finding
u\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})
that satisfies

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}[u]=f.

(C.1)

The first issue is that the matrix
L^{+}
, as constructed in
Eq.

4.14
, is singular. This is due to three rows and one column whose entries are all zero. The three rows correspond to the dual sum space functions
\tilde{U}_{-2}(x)
,
\tilde{U}_{n+1}(x)
, and
V_{n+2}(x)
which do not lie in the range of
(-\Delta)^{1/2}S_{n}^{+}(x)
. The column corresponds to the sum space function
\tilde{T}_{0}(x)
. Hence, we remove these rows and column from
L^{+}
resulting in an
(2n+4)\times(2n+6)
matrix. The second issue is that the additional functions computed to form the square matrix
L^{+}
become problematic or redundant. By
Proposition

3.5
, we have that
\tilde{u}_{j}(x)=W_{j}(x)
,
j\geq 0
and
v_{j}(x)=\tilde{T}_{j}(x)
,
j\geq 1
. Hence, two of the additional functions are already included in the sum space and correspond to two columns that should be removed. At first glance, this is advantageous as this reduces
L^{+}
to a
(2n+4)\times(2n+4)
matrix. However, computing the remaining additional functions
\tilde{u}_{-1}(x)
and
v_{0}(x)
poses a numerical difficulty. Although
\tilde{U}_{-1},V_{0}\in H^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R})
, since
\lambda=0
, the Lax–Milgram theorem does not guarantee existence of solutions. Attempting to compute these two solutions by a forward and inverse Fourier transform fails. Hence, as discussed in
Section

1
, there exist solutions that satisfy the Fourier multiplier reformulation
Eq.

2.14
but not
Eq.

2.3
.
Ignoring these technical issues and naïvely computing the inverse Fourier transform via
Eq.

2.5
does recover the non-decaying solutions:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\arcsin(x)&|x|<1,\\
-\mathrm{sgn}(x)\pi/2&|x|\geq 1,\end{cases}

(C.2)

\displaystyle v_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\log(2)-\gamma&|x|<1,\\
\log(2)-\gamma-\mathrm{arcsinh}(\sqrt{x^{2}-1})&|x|\geq 1.\end{cases}

(C.3)

However, as neither of these tend to zero as
|x|\to\infty
, they cannot live in
H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, we choose to remove the columns associated with
\tilde{u}_{-1}(x)
and
v_{0}(x)
as well. Since all the additional functions have been removed, we are only required to consider the range of
(-\Delta)^{1/2}S_{n}(x)
. The range does not include
\tilde{U}_{-1}(x)
and
V_{0}(x)
. Hence, these functions can be removed from the dual sum space (whilst still preserving the exact map
(-\Delta)^{1/2}:S_{n}\to{{\bf S}}^{*}_{n}
) and their corresponding rows from
L^{+}
. In summary, we remove the columns associated with
\tilde{T}_{0}(x)
,
v_{0}(x)
,
\tilde{u}_{-1}(x)
,
v_{1}(x)
, and
\tilde{u}_{0}(x)
, and the rows associated with
\tilde{U}_{-2}(x)
,
V_{0}(x)
,
\tilde{U}_{-1}(x)
,
V_{n+2}(x)
, and
\tilde{U}_{n+1}(x)
reducing
L^{+}
from a
(2n+7)\times(2n+7)
matrix to a
(2n+2)\times(2n+2)
matrix. We emphasize that the action of
(-\Delta)^{1/2}
on
S_{n}(x)
is still represented exactly by the reduced
L^{+}
.

C.2
\lambda=\mu=0
,
|\eta|>0

We now consider where
\lambda=\mu=0
but
|\eta|>0
. This corresponds to finding
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
that satisfies

\displaystyle\left(\eta\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+(-\Delta)^{1/2}\right)[u]=f.

This special case is similar to the previous one. Again
L^{+}
is singular due to three rows and a column which contain only zeroes. The rows are associated with the dual sum space functions
\tilde{U}_{-2}(x)
,
\tilde{U}_{n+1}(x)
, and

which do not lie in the range of

\tilde{T}_{0}(x)
. Hence, we remove those three rows and column. Moreover,

\tilde{u}_{-1}(x)

v_{1}(x)

\tilde{u}_{0}(x)

\tilde{U}_{-2}(x)

V_{0}(x)

\tilde{U}_{-1}(x)

V_{n+2}(x)
, and
\tilde{U}_{n+1}(x)
