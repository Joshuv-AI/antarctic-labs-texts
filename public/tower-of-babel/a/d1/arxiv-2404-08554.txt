The global and local limit of the continuous-time Mallows process
Radosław Adamczak, Michał Kotowski

Abstract
Continuous-time Mallows processes are processes of random permutations of the set { 1 , … , n } \{1,\ldots,n\} whose marginal at time t t is the Mallows distribution with parameter t t . Recently Corsini showed that there exists a unique Markov Mallows process whose left inversions are independent counting processes. We prove that this process admits a global and a local limit as n → ∞ n\to\infty . The global limit, obtained after suitably rescaling space and time, is an explicit stochastic process on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] whose description is based on the permuton limit of the Mallows distribution, analyzed by Starr. The local limit is a process of permutations of ℤ \mathbb{Z} which is closely related to the construction of the Mallows distribution on permutations of ℤ \mathbb{Z} due to Gnedin and Olshanski. Our results demonstrate an analogy between the asymptotic behavior of Mallows processes and the recently studied limiting properties of random sorting networks.



1
Introduction

The topic of this paper are limits of permutation-valued stochastic processes related to the Mallows model of random permutations. Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the group of permutations of
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For
q\in[0,\infty)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
the
Mallows distribution with parameter
q
is the probability measure
\pi_{n,q}
on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
defined by

\pi_{n,q}(\sigma)=\frac{q^{\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,q}},

(1)

where
\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma)=\left|\left\{(i,j)\,:\,i<j,\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\right\}\right|
is the number of inversions of
\sigma
and
\mathcal{Z}_{n,q}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}(1+q+\ldots+q^{i-1})
is the normalizing constant.

For
q=0
the measure is concentrated on the identity permutation, while
q=1
corresponds to the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Informally speaking, the Mallows distribution for
q\in[0,1)
tends to favor more ordered permutations, closer to the identity permutation
\mathrm{id}_{n}=(1\,2\,\ldots\,n)
, while for
q\in(1,\infty)
it favors permutations closer to the reverse permutation
\mathrm{rev}_{n}=(n\,\ldots\,2\,1)
.

This model, introduced originally by Mallows (
[
Mal57
]
) in statistical ranking theory, has attracted considerable attention in a number of contexts, including the study of its cycle structure (
[
GP18
]
,
[
HMV23
]
), asymptotics of the longest increasing subsequence (
[
BP15
]
,
[
BB17
]
,
[
MS13
]
), Markov chain mixing times (
[
DR00
]
,
[
BBHM05
]
), integrable systems in statistical physics (
[
Sta09
]
), binary search trees (
[
ABC21
]
,
[
Cor23
]
) and stable matchings (
[
AHHL21
]
). We would also like to highlight its role in recent investigations of interacting particle systems such as ASEP and its generalizations, see, e.g.
[
Buf20
]
,
[
BN22
]
,
[
BC24
]
. More general exponential families of permutations and their significance in statistics are discussed in
[
Muk16
]
.

To study the asymptotic behavior of Mallows random permutations it is natural to consider their convergence to an appropriately defined limiting object as
n\to\infty
. One such notion of a limit (a ‘global’ one) is provided by the theory of permutons (
[
HKM+13
]
), by now a well-developed subject. These are measures on the unit square with uniform marginals, obtained by looking at the permutation matrix of a permutation ‘from far away’ (which corresponds to scaling spacing by
\frac{1}{n}
). Starr (
[
Sta09
]
) and later Starr and Walters (
[
SW18
]
) proved that Mallows permutations sampled from
\pi_{n,q}
admit a deterministic limit in this sense when
q=q_{n}\to 1
is scaled appropriately as
n\to\infty
(more precisely,
q_{n}\approx 1+\frac{\beta}{n}
for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
).

Another kind of a limit, a ‘local’ one, is obtained when one does not rescale space and instead considers permutations (bijections) of
\mathbb{Z}
as limiting objects. This notion has its roots in the work of Gnedin and Olshanski (
[
GO10
]
,
[
GO12
]
), who proved the existence of the analog of the Mallows distribution for permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
for
q\in(0,1)
.

The focus of this work are similar kinds of limits for permutation-valued
stochastic processes
. Such processes have been of interest both from the point of view of probability theory and statistical physics (
[
GUW11
]
). Perhaps the most well-known example is the interchange process, obtained by composing transpositions along edges of a given finite or infinite graph. We refer the reader to
[
LP17
]
for some background on this process. Another example, which will be particularly relevant for us as a point of comparison, is given by
random sorting networks
(
[
AHRV07
]
). These are shortest paths in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
from the identity permutation to the reverse permutation, chosen uniformly at random, when adjacent transpositions are used as generators. Their remarkable structure was studied in a number of works, culminating in the proof of the sine curve conjecture (
[
Dau22
]
) – after appropriate space and time rescaling, elements in a random sorting network follow sine curves described by the so-called Archimedean process. The process also possesses a local limit (
[
ADHV19
]
,
[
GR19
]
) which is a stationary process of permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
, with interesting relations to random matrix theory.

We will be interested in continuous-time
Mallows processes
, which are processes of permutations whose marginal at time
t
is given by the Mallows distribution with parameter
t
. Thus, the parameter
q
in the definition of
\pi_{n,q}
plays the role of time. Such processes were introduced recently in
[
Cor22
]
. As
q=0
corresponds to the identity permutation and for
q\to\infty
the distribution
\pi_{n,q}
is concentrated close to the reverse permutation, Mallows processes can be thought of as interpolating between the identity and the reverse permutation in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, not unlike random sorting networks.

Indeed, we will prove that under certain assumptions a Mallows process, like a random sorting network, admits both a global limit (in which path of an element is a stochastic process with values in
[0,1]
) and a local limit (which is a process of permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
). In the global limit, with high probability elements follow random curves given by an explicit formula, like in the sine curve conjecture. The local limit is a transposition process which admits a description in terms of a family of Markov birth processes. This is the content of our main theorems, Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.3
, which we state below after providing necessary preliminary definitions.

1.1
Preliminaries

The Mallows distribution and inversions numbers.

It will be convenient to work with the following description of permutations. For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
we define its
left inversion vector
as the sequence
(\ell_{1}(\sigma),\ldots,\ell_{n}(\sigma))
, where

\ell_{i}(\sigma)=\left|\left\{j\in\{0,\ldots,i-1\}\,:\,\sigma(j)>\sigma(i)\right\}\right|

is the number of
left inversions
of element
i
in permutation
\sigma
. It is classical that admissible inversion vectors of length
n
are in bijection with permutations on
n
elements. Given a vector
\left(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\right)\in\{0\}\times\{0,1\}\times\ldots\times\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, the corresponding permutation
\sigma
is constructed by setting
\sigma(n)=n-\ell_{n}
and then recursively
\sigma(k)=x_{k-\ell_{k}}
, where
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
are elements of
[n]\setminus\{\sigma(n),\ldots,\sigma(k+1)\}]
sorted in ascending order.

An important property of the Mallows distribution is that for
\sigma\sim\pi_{n,q}
the inversion numbers
\ell_{i}(\sigma)
are independent, with
\ell_{i}(\sigma)
following the distribution

\mathbb{P}\left(\ell_{i}(\sigma)=j\right)=\frac{q^{j}}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{i-1}q^{k}},\,j=0,\ldots,i-1.

For
q<1
this is the truncated geometric distribution with parameter
1-q
. Thus the above bijection provides a simple way of sampling permutations from
\pi_{n,q}
.

Continuous-time Mallows processes.

Let
\sigma^{n}=\left(\sigma_{t}^{n}\right)_{t\in[0,\infty)}
be a continuous-time stochastic process with values in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. We will call
\sigma^{n}
a
continuous-time Mallows process
(or simply a Mallows process) if it is a càdlàg stochastic process such that for all
t\in[0,\infty)
the distribution of
\sigma^{n}_{t}
is
\pi_{n,t}
. Such processes were first considered in
[
Cor22
]
, where the reader can find a more general discussion.

In this paper we will be interested in a specific Mallows process possessing certain independence and Markovianity properties. Following
[
Cor22
]
we call a Mallows process
\left(\sigma_{t}^{n}\right)_{t\in[0,\infty)}

regular
if it satisfies the following three conditions:

•

for any
i\in[n]
and
t\leq t^{\prime}
we have
\ell_{i}\left(\sigma_{t}^{n}\right)\leq\ell_{i}\left(\sigma_{t^{\prime}}^{n}\right)
,

•

\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma_{t}^{n})\leq\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma_{t-}^{n})+1
,

•

the processes
\left(\ell_{i}\left(\sigma_{t}^{n}\right)\right)_{t\in[0,\infty)}
are independent over
i\in[n]
.

Consider now for any
i\in[n]
a time-inhomogeneous Markov birth process
(\ell^{n}_{i}(t))_{t\in[0,\infty)}
with values in
{0,\ldots,i-1}
, whose rate for jumping from
j
to
j+1
at time
t
is given by

\begin{cases}p_{i}(j,t)=\frac{1}{1-t}\left(j+1-\frac{it^{i-j-1}(t^{j+1}-1)}{t^{i}-1}\right),&\,j=0,\ldots,i-1,\,t\neq 1,\\
p_{i}(j,1)=\frac{1}{2}(j+1)(i-j-1),&\,t=1.\end{cases}

(2)

We now let
\sigma^{n}=(\sigma_{t}^{n})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
be the
birth Mallows process
obtained by considering
independent
processes
\left(\ell^{n}_{i}(t)\right)_{t\in[0,\infty)}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and defining
\sigma^{n}_{t}
to be the unique permutation
\sigma
with inversion vector
(\ell_{1}(\sigma),\ldots,\ell_{n}(\sigma))=\left(\ell^{n}_{1}(t),\ldots,\ell^{n}_{n}(t)\right)
. The definition is correct thanks to the bijection between inversion vectors and permutations. It was shown in
[
Cor22
]
that
\sigma^{n}
is indeed a Mallows process, i.e.,
\sigma_{t}^{n}\sim\pi_{n,t}
for each
t\in[0,\infty)
.

Furthermore, we have the following uniqueness result, which serves as an additional motivation to study the birth Mallows process – the birth Mallows process is the unique regular Mallows process which is also Markov (
[
Cor22
, Theorem 1.1]
). Note that in
[
Cor22
]
the formula for the rate
p_{i}(j,t)
was given in terms of a certain sum, but the expression actually simplifies to the one given in (
2
). From now on whenever we speak of ‘the Mallows process’ we will mean the birth Mallows process
\sigma^{n}
defined above.

1.2
Main results

We are now in a position to formulate the main results of our paper, describing two limits of the Mallows process as
n\to\infty
– the global one (in which we are interested in statistical behavior of elements in macroscopic neighborhoods) and the local one (in which we look at the asymptotic behavior of individual elements).

Before that, let us comment on the necessity (or lack thereof) of rescaling time in order to obtain a nontrivial limiting process. Let us consider
q\leq 1
(the argument for
q\geq 1
is symmetric). It is known (see, e.g.,
[
BP15
, Theorem 1.1]
) that for
\sigma\sim\pi_{n,q}
the probability of the displacement
|\sigma(i)-i|
being at least
k
is roughly
q^{k}
. Therefore, all the displacements will be
o(n)
(and thus vanishing after rescaling space) unless
q=q_{n}\approx 1-\frac{\beta}{n}
for some
\beta\geq 0
. This is consistent with the scaling for the permuton limit from
[
Sta09
]
mentioned in the introduction. Thus to see nontrivial displacements in the global limit we need to zoom into a window of size
\sim\frac{1}{n}
around time
t=1
.

Likewise, since in the local limit we would like to observe asymptotic trajectories of individual elements, we cannot expect to obtain a process defined up to and past
t=1
. As the distribution
\pi_{n,q}
for
q=1
is the uniform distribution, the limiting process would need to have as its marginal at time
1
a uniform distribution on permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
, which does not exist. This corresponds to the number of jumps of each element in the limiting process exhibiting an explosion as
t\to 1
.

Global limit.

To state our first result, the convergence of the Mallows process to a global limit, we introduce the following rescaling of time and space. For
i\in[n]
let
X_{i}^{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{e^{t/n}}^{n}(i)
denote the rescaled (global) trajectory of the element
i
in the Mallows process after
t\mapsto e^{t/n}
time rescaling. Thus each
\left(X_{i}^{n}(t)\right)_{t\in[0,\infty)}
is a random path with values in
[0,1]
. Let

z_{x,a}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\log\left(\frac{e^{xt}(1-a)+ae^{t}}{e^{xt}(1-a)+a}\right),

(3)

where
x,a\in[0,1]
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
and we take
z_{x,a}(0)=a
. Note that
\lim\limits_{t\to-\infty}z_{x,a}(t)=x
and
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}z_{x,a}(t)=1-x
.

Our first result is

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
T>0
,
\alpha>0
and
\varepsilon>0
we have the following convergence in probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\max\limits_{i\in(\alpha n,(1-\alpha)n)}\sup\limits_{t\in[-T,T]}\left|X_{i}^{n}(t)-z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t)\right|>\varepsilon\right)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,

where
z_{x,a}(t)
is given by (
3
).

The theorem can be interpreted in the following way – apart possibly from elements which start close to the boundary points
0
or
1
, with high probability each element
x=\frac{i}{n}
is assigned at random its desired position
a=X_{i}^{n}(0)
at time
0
and then approximately follows a deterministic trajectory
z_{x,a}(t)
depending only on
x
and
a
. In the original Mallows process without the
t\mapsto e^{t/n}
time rescaling this would correspond to starting at position
x
at time
0
and reaching position
a
at time
1
. Results of simulations illustrating the behavior of the Mallows process for
n=200
along with the limiting trajectories
z_{x,a}
are shown in Figure
1
and Figure
2
.

In fact, we expect the theorem to hold with maximum taken over all elements
i\in[n]
, not necessarily bounded away from
1
and
n
, but our proof stops short of showing that.

Theorem
1.1
implies the following corollary about the behavior of a randomly chosen element. Let
\left(Y^{n}\right)_{t\in[-T,T]}
be the
random particle process
defined by choosing
i\in[n]
uniformly at random and then following the trajectory
\left(X_{i}^{n}(t)\right)_{t\in[-T,T]}
. Note that for
t=0
we have
\sigma_{e^{t/n}}^{n}=\sigma_{1}^{n}
, which is uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
(as it corresponds to the Mallows distribution with parameter
1
), so each
X_{i}^{n}(0)
has uniform distribution on
\{1/n,2/n,\ldots,n/n\}
. Since
\alpha
in Theorem
1.1
above can be arbitrarily small, we obtain the following

Corollary 1.2
.

For any
T>0
let
Y=\left(Y^{n}\right)_{t\in[-T,T]}
be the random particle process, regarded as a random path in the Skorokhod space
D\left([-T,T],[0,1]\right)
. Then we have the following convergence in distribution as
n\to\infty
:

Y^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}Z,

where
Z=\left(z_{X,A}(t)\right)_{t\in[-T,T]}
with
X
,
A
independent and distributed uniformly on
[0,1]
.

Local limit.

We now state the result about the second kind of limit of the Mallows process, the local limit. Here, for reasons that will be apparent in a moment, we need to restrict our attention to times
t\in[0,1)
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}
denote the group of all permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
, i.e., all bijections of
\mathbb{Z}
with itself. A permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}
will be called
balanced
if

\left|\left\{i\leq 0\,:\,\sigma(i)\geq 1\right\}\right|=\left|\left\{i\geq 1\,:\,\sigma(i)\leq 0\right\}\right|<\infty.

The set of all balanced permutations will be denoted by
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
.

For
q\in[0,1)
Gnedin and Olshanski (
[
GO12
]
) constructed a measure
\Pi_{q}
on
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
which serves as an analog of the Mallows distribution
\pi_{n,q}
on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. This measure, whose existence underlies our local limit results, is discussed in more detail in Section
3
. By a slight abuse of language we will call
\Pi_{q}
the
Mallows measure on
\mathbb{Z}
with parameter
q
.

An
\mathfrak{S}
-valued path
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
will be called a
permutation path
if for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
the trajectory
\left(\Sigma_{t}(i)\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
is càdlàg. We say that a sequence of permutation paths
\left(\Sigma^{n}_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
converges to a permutation path
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
if for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
we have
\left(\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i)\right)_{t\in[0,1)}\to\left(\Sigma_{t}(i)\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
as
n\to\infty
, where the latter convergence is convergence of paths in the Skorokhod space. A random permutation path will be called a
permutation process
on
\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
\mathcal{D}=D([0,1),\mathfrak{S}^{bal})
be the space of all càdlàg functions on
[0,1)
with values in
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
. In other words,
\mathcal{D}
consists of permutation paths whose values are balanced permutations. We provide the details concerning this space in the Appendix. Note that convergence in
\mathcal{D}
implies convergence of permutation paths in the sense introduced above.

Given the Mallows process
\left(\sigma^{n}_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
and any sequence
k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define the following shifted process

\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i)=\sigma^{n}_{t}\left(k_{n}+i\right)-k_{n},

(4)

where
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t\in[0,1)
and we take
\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i)=i
if
k_{n}+i\notin[n]
. In this way
\left(\Sigma^{n}_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
can be considered as a permutation process on
\mathbb{Z}
for any value of
n
(in fact, a process of balanced permutations).

Our main result here is the following

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a permutation process
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
such that for any sequence
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
with
k_{n},n-k_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, we have the following convergence in distribution

\left(\Sigma^{n}_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}

in the Skorokhod topology on
\mathcal{D}
. The marginal distribution of
\Sigma_{t}
at time
t
is given by the Mallows measure
\Pi_{t}
on
\mathbb{Z}
with parameter
t
.

Remark 1.4
.

In fact our proof implies that for any
T\in[0,1)
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the process
(\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i))_{i\in\{-N,\ldots,N\},t\in[0,T]}
seen as an element of
D([0,T],\mathbb{Z}^{2N+1})
converges in the total variation distance to
(\Sigma_{t}(i))_{i\in\{-N,\ldots,N\},t\in[0,T]}
.

The intuitive picture behind Theorem
1.3
is as follows. We fix a spatial location
x\in(0,1)
together with a sequence
k_{n}
satisfying
\frac{k_{n}}{n}\to x
(this is a special case of the theorem) and observe the behavior of the Mallows process
\sigma^{n}_{t}
in a window of constant size around
\lfloor xn\rfloor
, and then take
n\to\infty
. Note in particular that the distribution of the limiting process does not depend on
x
, i.e., is the same for all locations as long as we are not close to the boundary. For locations near
0
or
1
, i.e., when either
k_{n}
or
n-k_{n}
does not go to infinity, one should consider processes of permutations of
\mathbb{N}
rather than of
\mathbb{Z}
and change all the statements accordingly, but this case is actually easier to handle. We discuss this briefly in Section
3
.

The limiting process
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
admits a description in terms of a family of independent Markov jump processes and it is a transposition process. This is discussed in more detail in Section
3
.

The fact that
\Sigma_{t}
is distributed according to the Mallows measure on
\mathbb{Z}
with parameter
t
explains why we have to limit ourselves to times
t\in[0,1)
. Indeed, the Mallows measure on
\mathbb{Z}
with parameter
t=1
would correspond to the uniform distribution over permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
, which does not exist. This is reflected in the fact that the processes of inversions used to define
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
explode at
t=1
, as mentioned in earlier.

1.3
Overview of the proofs and further questions

Overview of the proofs.

We briefly describe the techniques used in the proofs of our main results.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we first derive the so-called fluid limit (see
[
DN08
]
) of the inversion processes
\ell_{i}(\sigma_{e^{t/n}}^{n})
, which consists in approximating the trajectory of a Markov process by a solution to a corresponding ODE. In particular, the inversion number
\ell_{i}(\sigma_{e^{t/n}}^{n})
at any time
t
is essentially determined by the inversion number at time
t=0
. Next, as the permuton limit of the Mallows permutations is deterministic and explicitly known (thanks to
[
Sta09
]
), for a given element with initial position
x
its inversion number and its position at time
t=0
determine each other up to small error. The same is true for any other time
t
and since the inversion process is approximated by an ODE, this proves that the trajectory
X_{i}^{n}(t)
itself is almost deterministic. Along the way we employ a large deviation principle satisfied by Mallows permutations, which enables us to work separately with each time point from a sufficiently fine mesh of
[0,T]
. At the end we pass from
[0,T]
to
[-T,T]
by a symmetry argument.

As for Theorem
1.3
, we first construct the limiting process
\Sigma_{t}
in terms of a family of independent (time-inhomogeneous) birth processes
\ell_{i}(t)
, representing the left inversion numbers. This is analogous to what is done for the Mallows process
\sigma^{n}_{t}
on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, but more involved, since reconstructing a permutation of
\mathbb{Z}
from its inversion numbers is not straightforward. Then we construct coupled copies of all the processes
\sigma^{n}_{t}
and the limiting process by means of a dependent thinning procedure, in which successive jumps of the
\ell_{i}(t)
’s are rejected with appropriate probability. As the jump rates given by (
2
) approximate the jump rates of inversions of the limiting process, we are able to show that almost surely on any fixed interval
\{-N,\ldots,N\}
the processes
\Sigma^{n}_{t}
and
\Sigma_{t}
agree for large
n
enough.

Further questions.

We end the introductory section with a few questions and ideas for further research which might lead to additional insight into the Mallows process and permutation-valued processes in general.

•

The Archimedean process, which appears as the limit of a random sorting network (
[
Dau22
]
), admits an important variational characterization. This was actually noticed by Brenier (
[
Bre89
]
) in the study of incompressible flows long before the random sorting network conjecture was formulated. Let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be a continuous process with values in
[0,1]
whose marginal
X_{t}
for any
t
is uniform on
[0,1]
. Such processes are known as permuton processes (see
[
RVV19
]
) and they arise naturally as limits of permutation sequences. For a permuton process
X
we define its energy as

\mathcal{E}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{0}^{1}|\dot{X}_{t}|^{2}\,dt.

Brenier proved (see also
[
RVV19
]
) that the Archimedean process is the unique minimizer of energy among all permuton processes satisfying
X_{0}=1-X_{1}
. This constraint is analogous to the requirement that a permutation sequence connect the identity permutation to the reverse permutation. Since in the Archimedean process the trajectories are sine curves, they satisfy the harmonic oscillator equation

\ddot{X}_{t}=-X_{t}.

This is the Euler-Lagrange equation for the action
X\mapsto\int\limits_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{X}_{t}|^{2}-p(X_{t})\right)dt
, where
p(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
is the pressure function.

Is it possible to find a similar variational characterization for the global limit of the Mallows process from Theorem
1.1
? Since the limiting trajectories
z_{x,a}(t)
depend on two parameters, this strongly suggests that they should satisfy a second-order Euler-Lagrange equation like the trajectories of the Archimedean process, perhaps with the action being explicitly time-dependent.

•

To understand the asymptotic behavior of processes of permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
, it is natural to study the
speed distribution
of elements. For a variety of interacting particle systems, such as TASEP (
[
AAV11
]
), ASEP (
[
ACG23
]
) or the local limit of a random sorting network (
[
ADHV19
]
), it is known that if
X_{t}(i)
is the position of a given particle at time
i
, then the limit

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{X_{t}(i)-i}{t}

exists almost surely and one can describe the limiting distribution. Thus one can think of
i
as ‘choosing’ its speed at random and moving along the associated trajectory as
t\to\infty
. Does a similar limit exist for the local limit of the Mallows process from Theorem
1.3
? Here one should consider
t\to 1
and the known results about the typical displacement
\sigma_{t}(i)-i
(see, e.g.,
[
BP15
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
GO12
, Remark 5.2]
) suggest that the right normalization is
\frac{1}{1-t}
in the denominator. We note that
[
GO12
, Theorem 5.1]
provides a series representation for the time marginal of the displacement, which hints at a possible approach to work out the limiting speed distribution.

•

It would be interesting to obtain a more detailed description of the jump distribution of the local limit of the Mallows process. For example, what is the distribution of the displacement
\Sigma_{t}(i)-i
at the first time
i
is transposed with some other element? More generally, is it possible to describe the transpositions of
\Sigma_{t}
by means of an explicit point process, as was done for the local limit of random sorting networks in
[
GR19
]
? Some results on the distribution of early jump times for Mallows processes on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
were proved in
[
Cor22
]
.

•

In this work, following
[
Cor22
]
, we have defined the Mallows distribution
\pi_{n,q}
on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
in terms of left inversions
\ell_{i}
. We could just as well have chosen to work with right inversions
r_{i}
(as, e.g., is done in
[
GO12
]
for permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
), since both choices yield the same Mallows distribution for fixed parameter
q
. Note, however, that the corresponding Mallows processes are not the same, as can be easily checked on explicit examples for small
n
. An interesting question would be to construct a Mallows process still satisfying
\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma_{t}^{n})\leq\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma_{t^{\prime}}^{n})
for
t<t^{\prime}
and
\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma_{t}^{n})\leq\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma_{t-}^{n})+1
, but which would be at the same time ‘canonical’, in the sense of not distinguishing between left and right inversions in its definition.

2.1
Permutons and large deviations

Permuton limits.

We start by introducing necessary notions related to permutation limits. Let
\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})
denote the space of Borel probability measures on
[0,1]^{2}
. For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
its associated empirical measure is defined as

\mu_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\left(\frac{i}{n},\frac{\sigma(i)}{n}\right)}.

A
permuton
is a Borel probability measure on
[0,1]^{2}
with uniform marginals on each coordinate. We say that a sequence of permutations
\sigma^{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
converges to a permuton
\mu
if
\mu_{\sigma^{n}}\to\mu
weakly. It is readily seen that any weak limit of a sequence
\mu_{\sigma^{n}}
is necessarily a permuton and conversely, by general theory of permutation limits (
[
HKM+13
]
) any permuton can be realized as a limit of permutations in the above sense. The set of all permutons will be denoted by
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})
. Note that
\mathcal{P}
is closed in the topology of weak convergence.

For permutons the weak topology is actually equivalent to a stronger topology, the
box topology
(
[
HKM+13
, Lemma 5.3]
), which will be useful later on. It is given by the following metric
d_{\Box}
– for permutons
\mu,\nu
and random variables
(X_{1},Y_{1})\sim\mu
,
(X_{2},Y_{2})\sim\nu
we define

d_{\Box}(\mu,\nu):=\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}a<b\in[0,1]\\
c<d\in[0,1]\end{subarray}}\Big|\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in[a,b],Y_{1}\in[c,d])-\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in[a,b],Y_{2}\in[c,d])\Big|.

(5)

For any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
let
\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}(\cdot)
denote the probability measure corresponding to sampling
\sigma^{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
from the Mallows distribution
\pi_{n,q}
with parameter
q=e^{\beta/n}
. Thus under
\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}
the measure
\mu_{\sigma^{n}}
is a random empirical measure. Starr (
[
Sta09
]
) proved a law of large numbers for
\sigma^{n}
, which translated into the language of permuton limits reads as follows

Theorem 2.1

(
[
Sta09
]
, Theorem 1.1)
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\sigma^{n}
be sampled according to
\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}
. We have the following convergence in probability:

\mu_{\sigma^{n}}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\mu_{\beta},

where the (deterministic) measure
\mu_{\beta}
is given by the following density
\rho_{\beta}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{2}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}(x,y)=\frac{(\beta/2)\sinh(\beta/2)}{\left(e^{-\beta/4}\cosh(\beta[x-y]/2)-e^{\beta/4}\cosh(\beta[x+y-1]/2)\right)^{2}}.

(6)

Note that in
[
Sta09
]
the result was formulated for parameter
1-\frac{\beta}{n}
instead of
e^{\beta/n}
, but this does not influence the proof nor result, and we have accounted for the change of
-\beta
to
\beta
in the formula above. The density
\rho_{\beta}
is a continuous function bounded away from
0
for fixed
\beta
and for
\beta=0
we obtain
\rho_{0}(x,y)\equiv 1
as expected.

The law of large numbers was later refined by Starr and Walters (
[
SW18
]
) to a large deviation principle which we discuss below.

Large deviations.

For any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
let
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}:\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})\to[0,\infty]
be defined in the following way – we take
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu)=+\infty
if
\mu
is not a permuton, and otherwise

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu)=S(\mu|\lambda^{\otimes 2})+\beta\mathcal{E}(\mu)+p(\beta),

(7)

where

•

S(\mu|\nu)=\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}\log\left(\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}\right)d\mu
is the relative entropy of
\mu
with respect to
\nu
,

•

\lambda^{\otimes 2}
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{2}
,

•

\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\mathcal{E}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})<0\}}\,d\mu(x_{1},y_{1})\,d\mu(x_{2},y_{2}),

•

p(\beta)=\int\limits_{0}^{1}\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-\beta x}}{\beta x}\right)dx
, with
p(0)=0
.

The function
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\cdot)
is lower semi-continuous on
\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})
and has compact sublevel sets (
[
SW18
, Lemma 3.1]
), i.e., it is a good rate function in the terminology of the theory of large deviations (we refer the reader to the monographs
[
Ell85
,
DZ98
]
). Also,
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu)
is continuous as a function of
\beta
for fixed
\mu
.

The following large deviation principle was obtained by Starr and Walters (see also
[
Tra08
,
Muk16
]
). The notions of open and closed sets in its formulation are understood in the sense of weak topology on the space of probability measures.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
SW18
]
, Proposition 3.2)
.

Fix
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\sigma^{n}
be sampled according to
\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}
. With the notation as above, for any closed set
A\subseteq\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})
we have

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}\left(\mu_{\sigma^{n}}\in A\right)\leq-\inf\limits_{\mu\in A}\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu)

and for any open set
A\subseteq\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})

\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}\left(\mu_{\sigma^{n}}\in A\right)\geq-\inf\limits_{\mu\in A}\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu).

Remark 2.3
.

The large deviation result in
[
SW18
]
is stated and proved for the ‘continuous’ version of the Mallows model, which may be defined by replacing the empirical measure
\mu_{\sigma^{n}}
by a permuton with density
f_{\sigma^{n}}(x,y)=n\mathbbm{1}_{\sigma^{n}(\lceil nx\rceil)=\lceil ny\rceil}
. Nevertheless, the large deviation principle holds in the same form in both models, see
[
SW18
, Remark 3.4, Remark 3.5]
and Remark A.6 in version
4
of
[
SW18
]
on arXiv.

Let us remark that in our proofs we will only use the large deviation upper bound, exploiting the fact that the weak and box topology are equivalent on the set of permutons
\mathcal{P}
.

A key fact is that the rate function
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\cdot)
has a unique minimizer which is the measure
\mu_{\beta}
from the Law of Large Numbers (Theorem
2.1
).

Proposition 2.4

(
[
SW18
]
, Theorem 3.11)
.

For each
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
the measure
\mu_{\beta}
satisfies
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu_{\beta})=0
and is the unique minimizer of
I_{\beta}
over the space
\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})
.

The large deviation principle has the following corollary, which will be important for the proof of Theorem
1.1
. For a rectangle
R=[a,b]\times[c,d]\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
let

\displaystyle\Delta_{R}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n}\left|\left\{i\in[n]\,\colon\,\frac{i}{n}\in[a,b],\frac{\sigma(i)}{n}\in[c,d]\right\}\right|.

(8)

We have

Corollary 2.5
.

Fix
T>0
,
\varepsilon>0
and
\beta\in[0,T]
. Let
\sigma^{n}
be sampled according to
\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}
. We have

\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}\Bigg(\sup\limits_{R}\bigg|\Delta_{R}\left(\sigma^{n}\right)-\int\limits_{R}\rho_{\beta}(x,y)\,dx\,dy\bigg|\geq\varepsilon\Bigg)\leq 2e^{-cn},

where the supremum is over all rectangles
R=[a,b]\times[c,d]\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
,
\rho_{\beta}
is the density from Theorem
2.1
and the constant
c=c_{\varepsilon,T}>0
depends only on
\varepsilon
and
T
.

Since the proof is slightly technical, we defer it to the Appendix.

2.2
Evolution of inversions

Approximation of rates.

Recall that in the Mallows process
\left(\sigma^{n}_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,\infty)}
the left inversion vector at time
t
is given by
\left(\ell_{1}\left(\sigma^{n}_{t}\right),\ldots,\ell_{n}\left(\sigma^{n}_{t}\right)\right)=\left(\ell^{n}_{1}(t),\ldots,\ell^{n}_{n}(t)\right)
, where each
\left(\ell^{n}_{i}(t)\right)_{t\in[0,\infty)}
is a Markov birth process with birth rate given by (
2
). In this subsection we show that under the global space and time scaling the evolution of
\ell^{n}_{i}(t)
can be well-approximated by solutions of a deterministic ODE with random initial conditions.

Let
I_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\ell^{n}_{i}(e^{t/n})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, denote the rescaled processes of left inversions (with the dependence on
n
suppressed in the notation). For
x,y\in[0,1]
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
let

\begin{cases}\lambda_{x}(y,t)=\frac{1}{t}\left(-y+\frac{xe^{tx}}{e^{tx}-1}(1-e^{-ty})\right),&t\neq 0\\
\lambda_{x}(y,0)=\frac{1}{2}y(x-y),&t=0\end{cases}

(9)

where we set
\lambda_{0}(y,t)=\frac{1}{t}(-y+(1-e^{-ty})/t)
for
t\neq 0
. Note that
\lambda_{x}(y,t)
is continuous in
t
and Lipschitz in
x
and
y
, with Lipschitz constant uniform in
t\in[0,T]
for fixed
T>0
. For the sake of completeness we prove this as Lemma
4.4
in the Appendix.

Let us remark that for simplicity of notation we have defined
\lambda_{x}(y,t)
for all
x,y\in[0,1]
, but in fact we will only consider
y\leq x
in the sequel.

The functions
\lambda_{x}
approximate the rates
p_{i}
from (
2
) as explained in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any integers,
i,j
such that
0\leq j<i\leq n
, any
x,y\in[0,1]
and
t\in[0,T]
we have

\left|\frac{1}{n^{2}}p_{i}\left(j,e^{\frac{t}{n}}\right)-\lambda_{x}(y,t)\right|\leq C_{T}\left(\left|\frac{i}{n}-x\right|+\left|\frac{j}{n}-y\right|+\frac{1}{n}\right),

(10)

where
C_{T}
depends only on
T
.

Proof.

Observe that for
t>0
,

\frac{1}{n^{2}}p_{i}\left(j,e^{\frac{t}{n}}\right)=\frac{t/n}{e^{t/n}-1}\cdot\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}\left(\frac{j+1}{n},t\right)

and so

\left|\frac{1}{n^{2}}p_{i}\left(j,e^{\frac{t}{n}}\right)-\lambda_{x}(y,t)\right|\leq\frac{t/n}{e^{t/n}-1}\left|\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}\left(\frac{j+1}{n},t\right)-\lambda_{x}(y,t)\right|+\lambda_{x}(y,t)\left|\frac{t/n}{e^{t/n}-1}-1\right|.

The second factor in the last term on the right-hand side is bounded by
Ct/n
for some constant
C
, depending only on
T
. By Lemma
4.4
from the Appendix the second factor in the first term is bounded by
C(\left|\frac{i}{n}-x\right|+\left|\frac{j+1}{n}-y\right|)
. These two observations also imply that the remaining factors are bounded by a constant, which proves the lemma for
t>0
. The case of
t=0
is straightforward.
∎

In particular, there exists
C=C_{T}>0
, depending only on
T
, such that
p_{i}\left(j,e^{\frac{t}{n}}\right)\leq Cn^{2}
for any
i,j=1,\ldots,n
,
t\in[0,T]
, so that each
\ell^{n}_{i}(t)
is a counting process with intensity bounded by
Cn^{2}
(we recall the definition of a counting process and its intensity briefly in the Appendix).

The ODE limit.

We will now show the following ODE approximation for the joint dynamics of
I_{i}(t)
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(I_{1}(t),\ldots,I_{n}(t))
be as above and for
i=1,\ldots,n
let
y_{i}(t)
,
t\in[0,T]
, denote the solution of the ODE

\begin{cases}&\dot{y}_{i}(t)=\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}(y_{i}(t),t),\\
&y_{i}(0)=c_{i}\end{cases}

for some random variables
c_{i}\in[0,1]
. We have

\mathbb{P}\left(\forall\,i\in[n]\,\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\left|I_{i}\left(t\right)-y_{i}(t)\right|<C\left(\left|I_{i}(0)-y_{i}(0)\right|+\frac{C}{n^{1/4}}\right)\right)\geq 1-2e^{-c\sqrt{n}},

where
C,c>0
are constants depending only on
T
.

Remark 2.8
.

Note that for
0\leq y\leq x
we have
\lambda_{x}(t,y)\geq 0
, moreover
\lambda_{x}(t,x)=0
. Together with Lemma
4.4
this implies that for
0\leq c_{i}\leq\frac{i}{n}
the ODE in the proposition above has a unique solution. We will need its explicit form for the initial condition
y_{i}(0)=x(1-a)
(for
x,a\in[0,1]
), in which case we obtain

y_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\log\left(a+(1-a)e^{tx}\right).

(11)

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

In this proof
C
will denote a constant which depends only on
T
. The value of this constant may change between occurrences.

Fix
i\in[n]
. Since
p_{i}(\cdot,t)
is the jump intensity of
\ell_{i}^{n}(t)
at time
t
, we have for any
t\in[0,T]

\ell_{i}^{n}\left(e^{t/n}\right)-\ell_{i}^{n}(1)=M^{i}_{e^{t/n}}+\int\limits_{1}^{e^{t/n}}p_{i}\left(\ell_{i}^{n}(s),s\right)ds,

(12)

where
\left(M^{i}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 1}=(\ell_{i}^{n}(t)-\ell_{i}^{n}(1)-\int_{1}^{t}p_{i}(\ell_{i}(s),s)ds)_{t\geq 1}
is a martingale. Dividing by
n
and performing the change of variables
s=e^{u/n}
in the integral leads to

I_{i}(t)-I_{i}(0)=\frac{1}{n}M^{i}_{e^{t/n}}+\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{n^{2}}e^{u/n}p_{i}\left(\ell_{i}^{n}\left(e^{u/n}\right),e^{u/n}\right)du.

Now we employ the approximation (
10
) to get

\Big|\frac{1}{n^{2}}e^{u/n}p_{i}\left(\ell_{i}^{n}\left(e^{u/n}\right),e^{u/n}\right)-\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}(I_{i}(u),u)\Big|\\
\leq e^{u/n}\Big|\frac{1}{n^{2}}p_{i}\left(\ell_{i}^{n}\left(e^{u/n}\right),e^{u/n}\right)-\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}(I_{i}(u),u)\Big|+\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}(I_{i}(u),u)\Big|e^{u/n}-1\Big|\leq\frac{C}{n}.

As a consequence

I_{i}(t)-I_{i}(0)=\frac{1}{n}M^{i}_{e^{t/n}}+\int\limits_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}\left(I_{i}(u),u\right)du+\varepsilon_{n}(t),

where
\varepsilon_{n}(t)
is a stochastic process bounded in absolute value by
\frac{C}{n}
.

Now, since
y_{i}(t)
satisfies the relevant ODE, we have

y_{i}(t)-y_{i}(0)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}\left(y_{i}(s),s\right)ds,

so

I_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)=I_{i}(0)-y_{i}(0)+\frac{1}{n}M^{i}_{e^{t/n}}+\int\limits_{0}^{t}\left(\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}(I_{i}(s),s)-\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}(y_{i}(s),s)\right)ds+\varepsilon_{n}(t).

We get the following inequality

\left|I_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)\right|\leq|I_{i}(0)-y_{i}(0)|+\left|\frac{1}{n}M^{i}_{e^{t/n}}\right|+|\varepsilon_{n}(t)|+\int\limits_{0}^{t}\left|\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}(I_{i}(s),s)-\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}(y_{i}(s),s)\right|ds.

Since
\lambda_{\frac{i}{n}}(\cdot,t)
is
K
-Lipschitz on
[0,1]
with
K
depending only on
T
(see Lemma
4.4
from the Appendix), we can further write

\left|I_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)\right|\leq|I_{i}(0)-y_{i}(0)|+\left|\frac{1}{n}M^{i}_{e^{t/n}}\right|+|\varepsilon_{n}(t)|+K\int\limits_{0}^{t}\left|I_{i}(s)-y_{i}(s)\right|ds.

Taking the supremum gives

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}|I_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)|\leq|I_{i}(0)-y_{i}(0)|+\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\frac{1}{n}M^{i}_{e^{t/n}}\right|+\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}|\varepsilon_{n}(t)|+K\int\limits_{0}^{T}\sup\limits_{0\leq s\leq t}\left|I_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)\right|dt

and now an application of Gronwall’s inequality leads to

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}|I_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)|\leq\left(|I_{i}(0)-y_{i}(0)|+\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\frac{1}{n}M^{i}_{e^{t/n}}\right|+\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}|\varepsilon_{n}(t)|\right)e^{KT}.

We have
\sup_{t\leq T}|\varepsilon_{n}(t)|\leq\frac{C}{n}
. Taking into account that the intensity
p_{i}(j,e^{t/n})
is bounded on
[0,T]
by
Cn^{2}
we can now apply Lemma
4.1
from the Appendix to
N_{t}=\ell_{i}(1+t)-\ell_{i}(1)
,
a=Cn^{2}
,
s=e^{T/n}-1
, and
u=n^{3/4}
, and obtain (using the notation of the lemma)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{n}|M^{i}_{e^{t/n}}|\geq\frac{1}{n^{1/4}}\Big)=\mathbb{P}(\sup_{t\in[0,s]}|Z_{t}|\geq n^{3/4})\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{n^{3/2}}{4C(e^{T/n}-1)n^{2}+2n^{3/4}/3}\Big).

The right-hand side of the above inequality is clearly bounded by
2\exp(-c\sqrt{n})
, where
c
is a positive constant depending only on
T
. To finish the proof of the proposition it is now enough to take the union bound over
i\in[n]
and adjust the constants.
∎

2.3
Proof of Theorem
1.1

With the previous results we can now prove the global limit for the Mallows process.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

In the proof
C_{\cdot},c_{\cdot}
will denote constants depending only the parameters listed in the subscript. Their values may change between occurrences.

We will focus first on the behavior of
X_{i}^{n}(t)
for
t\in[0,T]
. At the end of the proof we will use the symmetry of the Mallows distribution to pass to the interval
[-T,T]
.

Consider the partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=T
of
[0,T]
into intervals of length
t_{l+1}-t_{l}=\frac{T}{N}
for
N=n^{7}
. By the remark following equation (
10
) the intensity of each counting process
\ell^{n}_{i}(t)
is bounded by
C_{T}n^{2}
, which easily implies that with high probability the whole process
(I_{1}(t),\ldots,I_{n}(t))
makes at most one jump in each interval
[t_{l},t_{l+1})
. Indeed, the probability that
\ell_{i}^{n}
makes a jump in an interval of length
\frac{1}{N}
is at most
\frac{C_{T}n^{2}}{N}
and by the strong Markov property, the probability of two jumps is at most
\frac{C_{T}^{2}n^{4}}{N^{2}}
. Taking the union bound over all
N
intervals and
i=1,\ldots,n
shows that the probability that the process makes more than one jump in any of the intervals is bounded by
\frac{C_{T}^{2}n^{4}Nn}{N^{2}}=\frac{C_{T}^{2}}{n^{2}}\to 0
.

Thus with high probability we have for each
i=1,\ldots,n
and
l=0,\ldots,N-1

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\in[t_{l},t_{l+1})}\left|X_{i}^{n}(t)-z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t)\right|\leq\max\left\{\left|X_{i}^{n}(t_{l})-z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t_{l})\right|,\left|X_{i}^{n}(t_{l+1})-z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t_{l+1})\right|\right\}

\displaystyle+\max\left\{\sup\limits_{t\in[t_{l},t_{l+1})}\left|z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t_{l})-z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t)\right|,\sup\limits_{t\in[t_{l},t_{l+1})}\left|z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t_{l+1})-z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t)\right|\right\}.

Since
z_{x,a}(t)
is continuous for
t\in[0,T]
(uniformly in
x
and
a
, see Lemma
4.3
), the second term is bounded by a deterministic quantity converging to
0
as
n\to\infty
, which altogether implies

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X_{i}^{n}(t)-z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t)\right|\leq\max\limits_{l=0,\ldots,N}\left|X_{i}^{n}(t_{l})-z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t_{l})\right|+o(1).

Thus it is enough to show that
\left|X_{i}^{n}(t)-z_{\frac{i}{n},X_{i}^{n}(0)}(t)\right|
is small with high enough probability (uniformly over
t
) for any given
i=1,\ldots,n
and
t\in[0,T]
.

From now on we fix
t\in[0,T]
and write for brevity
x_{i}=\frac{i}{n}
,
a_{i}=X_{i}^{n}(0)
.

We first show that specifying
a_{i}
determines the rescaled number of inversions of
i
,
I_{i}(t)
, up to a small error. Recall the quantity
\Delta_{R}
, defined in (
8
). For fixed
\varepsilon>0
by Corollary
2.5
applied to
R_{i}=\left[0,x_{i}\right]\times\left[a_{i},1\right]
and
\beta=0
we get (noting that
\rho_{0}(x,y)\equiv 1
)

\left|\Delta_{R_{i}}(\sigma_{1}^{n})-x_{i}(1-a_{i})\right|<\varepsilon

with probability at least
1-2e^{-c_{T,\varepsilon}n}
. Since
\Delta_{R_{i}}(\sigma_{1}^{n})=\frac{1}{n}\left|\left\{j\in[n]\,\colon\,j\in[0,x_{i}n],\sigma_{1}^{n}(j)\in[a_{i}n,n]\right\}\right|=\frac{1}{n}\left|\left\{j\in[n]\,\colon\,j\leq i,\,\sigma_{1}^{n}(j)\geq\sigma_{1}^{n}(i)\right\}\right|
, this differs from
\frac{1}{n}\ell_{i}^{n}(1)=I_{i}(0)
by
\frac{1}{n}
and thus

\left|I_{i}(0)-x_{i}(1-a_{i})\right|<\varepsilon+\frac{1}{n}.

(13)

Next, by Proposition
2.7
applied with
c_{i}=x_{i}(1-a_{i})
we get that with probability at least
1-2e^{-c_{T}\sqrt{n}}
,

\left|I_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)\right|<C_{T}\left(\left|I_{i}(0)-y_{i}(0)\right|+\frac{C_{T}}{n^{1/4}}\right),

(14)

where
y_{i}(t)
is the solution of

\begin{cases}&\dot{y}_{i}(t)=\lambda_{x_{i}}(y_{i}(t),t),\\
&y_{i}(0)=x_{i}(1-a_{i}),\end{cases}

given by the explicit formula (
11
) with
a=a_{i}
. Since we can choose
\varepsilon
to be arbitrarily small, by combining (
13
) with (
14
) together with a union bound we obtain that for any
\varepsilon>0
and large
n
, with probability at least
1-2e^{-c_{T,\varepsilon}\sqrt{n}}
we have

\left|I_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)\right|<\varepsilon

(15)

for all
i\leq n
.

Now that we know
I_{i}(t)
, we show that
X_{i}^{n}(t)
itself is essentially determined by
x_{i}
and
a_{i}
.

Let us write for simplicity
\sigma=\sigma^{n}_{e^{t/n}}
. By Lemma
4.5
we have that for
t\in[0,T]
the density
\rho_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is bounded from below by some
A_{T}>0
depending only on
T
.

By Corollary
2.5
applied with
\delta=\varepsilon
,
\beta=t
we get that

\max\limits_{u,v\in[0,1]}\bigg|\Delta_{[0,u]\times[v,1]}(\sigma)-\int\limits_{v}^{1}\int\limits_{0}^{u}\rho_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\,dy^{\prime}\bigg|<\varepsilon

(16)

with probability at least
1-2e^{-c_{T,\varepsilon}n}
.

Since
I_{i}(t)=\Delta_{\left[0,x_{i}\right]\times[X_{i}^{n}(t),1]}-\frac{1}{n}
, we thus obtain that with the same high probability, for all
i\leq n
,

\bigg|I_{i}(t)-\int\limits_{X_{i}^{n}(t)}^{1}\int\limits_{0}^{x_{i}}\rho_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\,dy^{\prime}\bigg|<\varepsilon+\frac{1}{n}.

Combined with (
15
), this shows that for large
n
, with probability at least
1-2e^{-c_{T,\varepsilon}\sqrt{n}}
for all
i\leq n
,

\bigg|y_{i}(t)-\int\limits_{X_{i}^{n}(t)}^{1}\int\limits_{0}^{x_{i}}\rho_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\,dy^{\prime}\bigg|<3\varepsilon.

For
x\in[0,1]
consider now a function
F_{x}\colon[0,1]\to[0,x]
, given by

F_{x}(z)=\int\limits_{z}^{1}\int\limits_{0}^{x}\rho_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\,dy^{\prime}.

(17)

The last inequality can be thus written as
|y_{i}(t)-F_{x_{i}}(X_{i}^{n}(t))|<3\varepsilon
.

Recall that
\rho_{t}
is bounded from below by
A_{T}>0
. Thus, for
x>0
,
F_{x}
is strictly decreasing and onto
[0,x]
. Moreover, for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in[0,1]
,
|F_{x}(z_{2})-F_{x}(z_{1})|\geq A_{T}x|z_{1}-z_{2}|
. In particular, with high probability for all
i\leq n
,

\big|F_{x_{i}}^{-1}(y_{i}(t))-X_{i}^{n}(t)\big|\leq\frac{n}{i}\cdot\frac{1}{A_{T}}\big|y_{i}(t)-F_{x_{i}}(X_{i}^{n}(t))\big|\leq\frac{3n}{iA_{T}}\varepsilon.

Thus for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max\limits_{i\in(\alpha n,(1-\alpha)n)}\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X_{i}^{n}(t)-F_{x_{i}}^{-1}(y_{i}(t))\right|>\varepsilon\right)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

To conclude the proof on the interval
[0,T]
it now suffices to show that
F_{x_{i}}^{-1}(y_{i}(t))=z_{x_{i},a}(t)
, i.e.,
y_{i}(t)=F_{x_{i}}(z_{x_{i},a}(t))
. Since
\rho_{t}
appearing in the formula (
17
) is explicitly known (recall formula (
6
), one can compute
F_{x}(z)
(e.g., using symbolic computation software such as Mathematica), which yields

F_{x}(z)=\frac{1}{t}\log\frac{e^{xt}(e^{t}-1)}{e^{(x+z)t}-e^{xt}-e^{zt}+e^{t}}.

Since
y_{i}(t)
is given by (
11
) and
z_{x_{i},a}(t)
by (
3
), one can perform the substitution and check the desired equality.

It remains to pass from
[0,T]
to
[-T,T]
. Let
\mathrm{rev}_{n}
be the order reversing permutation of
[n]
,
\mathrm{rev}_{n}(i)=n-i+1
for
i\in[n]
. For
t\geq 0
define
\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}_{t}=\mathrm{rev}_{n}\circ\sigma^{n}_{1/t}
(with the convention
\sigma^{n}_{\infty}=\mathrm{rev}_{n}
). Then, as one can easily check, we have
\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}_{t}\sim\pi_{n,t}
. Moreover, since time reversal preserves the Markov property,
\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}
is also a Markov process. Clearly
\ell_{i}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}_{t})=i-1-\ell_{i}(\sigma^{n}_{1/t})
, which implies that
\ell_{i}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}_{t})\leq\ell_{i}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}_{t^{\prime}})
whenever
t\leq t^{\prime}
and that the processes
(\ell_{i}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}_{t}))_{t\in[0,\infty)}
are independent over
i\in[n]
.

Consider now the process
\widehat{\sigma}^{n}_{t}=\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}_{t+}
. Since the probability that
\sigma^{n}
makes a jump at a deterministic time
t
is zero,
\widehat{\sigma}
is a modification of
\widetilde{\sigma}
and so it has the same finite-dimensional distributions. It is thus a càdlàg Markov process with values in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
whose marginal distribution at time
t
is equal to
\pi_{n,t}
. It also holds that
\mathrm{Inv}(\widehat{\sigma}_{t}^{n})\leq\mathrm{Inv}(\widehat{\sigma}_{t-}^{n})+1
, which together with independence and monotonicity properties of the inversion counts
\ell_{i}
implies that it is a regular Markov Mallows process. By Corsini’s uniqueness result mentioned in the introduction (
[
Cor22
, Theorem 1.1]
) it has the same distribution of paths as
\sigma^{n}
. In particular, from what we have already proved on
[0,T]
we get

\mathbb{P}\left(\max\limits_{i\in(\alpha n,(1-\alpha)n)}\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\bigg|\frac{1}{n}\widehat{\sigma}^{n}_{e^{t/n}}(i)-z_{\frac{i}{n},\frac{1}{n}\widehat{\sigma}^{n}_{1}(i)}(t)\bigg|>\varepsilon\right)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Since the limit
z_{x,a}(t)
is continuous in
t
and
\widehat{\sigma}^{n}_{1}(i)=\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}_{1}(i)
almost surely, the same holds for
\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}_{e^{t/n}}(i)=1-X_{i}^{n}(-t)
. In other words, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\max\limits_{i\in(\alpha n,(1-\alpha)n)}\sup\limits_{t\in[-T,0]}\left|X_{i}^{n}(t)-\left(1-z_{\frac{i}{n},1-X_{i}^{n}(0)}(-t)\right)\right|>\varepsilon\right)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

This ends the proof, since as one can easily check from (
3
)

1-z_{x,1-a}(-t)=z_{x,a}(t).

∎

3
Local limit

3.1
The Mallows distribution on
\mathbb{Z}

Mallows distribution on
\mathbb{Z}
– definition and existence.

To set the stage for the proof of the local limit of the Mallows process, we begin by recalling the construction of the
Mallows distribution on
\mathbb{Z}
due to Gnedin and Olshanski (
[
GO12
]
), which gives the analog of the Mallows distribution
\pi_{n,q}
for permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
. We will only mention results which are directly relevant to the proof of Theorem
1.3
– the reader is referred to
[
GO12
]
for a more in-depth discussion.

Recall that
\mathfrak{S}
denotes the set of all permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}
is the set of all balanced permutations. Let
\sigma_{i,i+1}\in\mathfrak{S}
denote the transposition swapping adjacent indices
i,i+1\in\mathbb{Z}
. Given
q>0
, we say that a measure
\mu
on
\mathfrak{S}
is
right
q
-exchangeable
if for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
the pushforward
\mu_{i,i+1}
of
\mu
under the map
\sigma\mapsto\sigma\sigma_{i,i+1}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
and the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\frac{d\mu_{i,i+1}}{d\mu}(\sigma)
equals
q^{\mathrm{sgn}\left(\sigma(i+1)-\sigma(i)\right)}
. Likewise, we say that
\mu
is
left
q
-exchangeable
if the analogous conditions hold under maps
\sigma\mapsto\sigma_{i,i+1}\sigma
with the Radon-Nikodym derivative
q^{\mathrm{sgn}\left(\sigma^{-1}(i+1)-\sigma^{-1}(i)\right)}
.

One readily checks that Mallows distributions
\pi_{n,q}
on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
satisfy the appropriately adjusted right and left
q
-exchangeability properties. The existence of a probability measure with such properties on
\mathfrak{S}
is nontrivial and was proved in
[
GO12
]
. More precisely, we have the following result (
[
GO12
, Theorem 3.3.]
)

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
. The two notions of
q
-exchangeability on
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
coincide. There exists a unique probability measure
\Pi_{q}
on
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
which is both right and left
q
-exchangeable.

Furthermore, the Mallows distribution
\pi_{n,q}
can be obtained by considering a restriction of
\Pi_{q}
to the set of permutations of any interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
of length
n
, where for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}
we identify
\sigma|_{I}
with an element of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
by relabeling the elements of
I
and the elements of
\sigma(I)
by the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
in increasing order.

Th measure
\Pi_{q}
is called the Mallows measure on
\mathbb{Z}
with parameter
q
. Let us remark that a related notion of the Mallows measure on the set of permutations of
\mathbb{N}
was considered by Gnedin and Olshanski in an earlier work
[
GO10
]
(see also Remark
3.10
).

Description of
\Pi_{q}
by inversion counts.

For our purposes it will be important that the measure
\Pi_{q}
can be described in terms of a family of independent geometric random variables. Recall that to sample a permutation
\sigma
from the Mallows distribution
\pi_{n,q}
, one can first sample an inversion vector
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})
, with all coordinates independent and
\ell_{i}
having a geometric distribution with parameter
1-q
, truncated at
i-1
, and then use the bijection between admissible inversion vectors and permutations of
[n]
. A somewhat analogous approach can be employed to sample permutations from
\Pi_{q}
. However, since on
\mathbb{Z}
there is no smallest or largest element to start with, one needs a recursive procedure which we now describe.

For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}
we define the number of
left inversions
of element
i
as

\ell_{i}(\sigma)=\left|\left\{j\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,j<i,\sigma(j)>\sigma(i)\right\}\right|.

Likewise, the number of
right inversions
of
i
is defined as

r_{i}(\sigma)=\left|\left\{j\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,j>i,\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)\right\}\right|.

Note that a priori these numbers can be infinite. It is proved in
[
GO12
]
(Proposition 4.1) that
\ell_{i}(\sigma),r_{i}(\sigma)
are in fact finite for all
i
if the permutation
\sigma
is balanced.

If one knows the left and right inversion numbers for a balanced permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
, the permutation itself can be easily reconstructed by the following formula (
[
GO12
, Lemma 4.6]
)

\sigma(i)=i+r_{i}(\sigma)-\ell_{i}(\sigma).

(18)

For
\sigma\sim\Pi_{q}
the left inversion numbers are independent and identically distributed (
[
GO12
]
), with
\ell_{i}(\sigma)
having geometric distribution with parameter
1-q

\mathbb{P}\left(\ell_{i}(\sigma)=j\right)=(1-q)q^{j},\,\,\,j=0,1,2,\ldots

Because the roles of the left and right inversion numbers are symmetric (thanks to Theorem
3.1
), the same applies to the right inversion numbers
r_{i}(\sigma)
. If one could compute the numbers
\ell_{i}(\sigma)
from the (bi-infinite) sequence
\left(r_{k}(\sigma)\right)_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
or vice versa, a simple method for sampling from
\Pi_{q}
would be available – sample an infinite i.i.d. sequence of geometric variables, say
\left(\ell_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
, compute the corresponding
r_{i}
and then employ formula (
18
).

Lemma 4.7 from
[
GO12
]
shows how to compute any
\ell_{i}
recursively given
(r_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
. However, to be consistent with the description of the finite Mallows distributions
\pi_{n,q}
in terms of the left rather than the right inversion vector, as we do in this paper and as is also done in
[
Cor22
]
, we would prefer to treat
\ell_{i}
as independent and compute
r_{i}
given
\left(\ell_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
. After making the necessary adjustments, the lemma reads as follows

Lemma 3.2

(
[
GO12
]
, Lemma 4.7, version for left inversions)
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
be a balanced permutation with the associated left and right inversion numbers
\ell_{i}=\ell_{i}(\sigma)
,
r_{i}=r_{i}(\sigma)
. Given
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\ell_{i}^{(i)}=\ell_{i}
and for
j\geq i
define recursively

\ell_{i}^{(j+1)}=\ell_{i}^{(j)}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{i}^{(j)}\geq\ell_{j+1}\}}.

Then we have

r_{i}=\sum\limits_{j:\,j>i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{i}^{(j)}<\ell_{j}\}}=\sum\limits_{j:\,j>i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{i}^{(j-1)}=\ell_{i}^{(j)}\}}.

(19)

Let us remark that
\ell_{i}^{(j)}
can be characterized by the property that there are exactly
\ell_{i}^{(j)}
elements greater than
\sigma(i)
in the sequence
(\sigma(k))_{k\leq j}
. In other words ,
\ell_{i}^{(j)}+1
is the reverse rank of
\sigma(i)
in
(\sigma(k))_{k\leq j}
. The last fact that we will need is a characterization of admissible inversion numbers. Again we state the lemma with the roles of left and right inversions switched with respect to the statement in
[
GO12
]
.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
GO12
]
, Theorem 7.1, version for left inversions)
.

A nonnegative integer sequence
\left(\ell_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
occurs as a sequence of left inversions counts for some permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
if and only if the following two conditions hold:

(i)

the values
\left(r_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
determined from (
19
) are finite,

(ii)

\ell_{i}=0
for infinitely many
i\leq 0
.

Under these conditions such
\sigma
is unique and has right inversion counts
\left(r_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
GO12
]
, Theorem 4.3, version for left inversions)
.

Let
\ell_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, be independent random variables distributed according to the geometric distribution with parameter
1-q
. Let
r_{i}
be defined by (
19
). Then with probability one, all
r_{i}
’s are finite and formula (
18
) defines a balanced permutation
\sigma
. Moreover,
\sigma
has law
\Pi_{q}
.

3.2
Construction of the limiting process

Based on the description of the Mallows distribution in terms of the left and right inversions numbers we can now construct the limiting process
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
. Similarly as for the Mallows process on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, the construction will be based on a family of independent processes defining the left inversion numbers. It will be slightly more convenient to first define these processes for
t\in[0,\infty)
and then perform a time change to
[0,1)
.

Let us thus consider a family of independent càdlàg time-homogeneous Markov birth processes
(\widetilde{\ell}_{i}(t))_{t\in[0,\infty)}
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, with
\widetilde{\ell}_{i}(t)
taking values in nonnegative integers, and having rate of jumping from
j
to
j+1
at time
t
given by
\widetilde{q}(j)=j+1
. We take
\widetilde{\ell}_{i}(0)=0
. Let
\tau:[0,1)\to[0,\infty)
be a (deterministic) change of time given by
\tau(t)=-\log(1-t)
and let us define for any
t\in[0,1)
,
\ell_{i}(t)=\widetilde{\ell}_{i}(\tau(t))
. It is readily seen that
(\ell_{i}(t))_{t\in[0,1)}
is a time-inhomogeneous Markov birth process with birth rate
q(j,t)=\widetilde{q}(j)\tau^{\prime}(t)
equal to

q(j,t)=\frac{j+1}{1-t},\,\,\,j=0,1,2,\ldots

(20)

The reader may notice that the jump rate is the same as for the inversions of Mallows process on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, up to a term which is small if
t<1
and
n\to\infty
(recall formula (
2
)). This observation will form the basis of the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proposition 3.5
.

The birth process with birth rates
q(j,t)
given by (
20
) and starting from
0
is non-explosive for
t\in[0,1)
. Moreover, the marginal distribution of the process at time
t\in[0,1)
is the geometric distribution with parameter
1-t
.

Proof.

We need to show that the process almost surely makes only finitely many jumps on any compact interval
[0,T]
. It is enough to show that the same holds for the process before the time change, that is, the process on
[0,\infty)
with jump rates
\widetilde{q}(j)=j+1
. This follows directly from the non-explosion criterion for time-homogeneous birth processes (
[
GS20
, Theorem 6.8.17]
), since
\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{q}(j)^{-1}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j+1}=+\infty
.

To find the marginal distribution at time
t
we solve the Chapman–Kolmogorov forward equation – if
P_{0,j}(t)
is the probability of the process being at
j
at time
t
, starting from
0
, then we have for any
t\in[0,1)

P^{\prime}_{0,0}(t)=-q(0,t)P_{0,0}(t)

and

P^{\prime}_{0,j}(t)=q(j-1,t)P_{0,j-1}(t)-q(j,t)P_{0,j}(t)

for
j\geq 1
, with
P_{0,j}(0)=\delta_{0j}
. One then directly checks that
P_{0,j}(t)=(1-t)t^{j}
,
j\geq 0
, is a solution, and since it is unique, the marginal distribution at time
t
is geometric with parameter
1-t
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

From the above proposition it follows in particular that each of the processes
\ell_{i}
makes infinitely many jumps in the interval
[0,1)
.

Now for a given
t\in[0,1)
we define
r_{i}(t)
to be the quantity
r_{i}
computed from (
19
) when the sequence
\left(\ell_{i}(t)\right)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is used as
\left(\ell_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, i.e., for
j\geq i
we take

\ell_{i}^{(j+1)}(t)=\ell_{i}^{(j)}(t)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{i}^{(j)}(t)\geq\ell_{j+1}(t)\}},

(21)

with
\ell_{i}^{(i)}(t)=\ell_{i}(t)
, and define

r_{i}(t)=\sum\limits_{j:\,j>i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{i}^{(j)}(t)<\ell_{j}(t)\}}=\sum\limits_{j:\,j>i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{i}^{(j-1)}(t)=\ell_{i}^{(j)}(t)\}}.

(22)

Finally, for any
t\in[0,1)
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}
we let

\Sigma_{t}(i)=i+r_{i}(t)-\ell_{i}(t).

(23)

Proposition 3.7
.

The process
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
defined by formula (
23
) is a càdlàg
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
-valued process whose marginal distribution at time
t
is the Mallows distribution
\Pi_{t}
with parameter
1-t
.

Proof.

In the proof we will assume without loss of generality that on the whole probability space the trajectories of
\ell_{i}
are càdlàg, nondecreasing on
[0,1)
and have jumps equal to one.

For any
t\in(0,1)
,
\ell_{i}(t)
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, are i.i.d. geometric variables with parameter
1-t
. Thus, by Theorem
3.4
,
\Sigma_{t}
is with probability one a well-defined balanced permutation, moreover it is distributed according to
\Pi_{t}
.

To conclude the proof, it is enough to show that for any
T\in(0,1)
, there exists an event
A_{T}
with
\mathbb{P}(A_{T})=1
, such that on
A_{T}
the trajectories
t\mapsto\Sigma_{t}
,
t\in[0,T]
, are
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
-valued and càdlàg.

Let

\displaystyle A_{T}=\Big\{

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(T)=0\textrm{ for infinitely many $i\geq 0$ and for infinitely many $i\leq 0$},

\displaystyle\;r_{i}(T)<\infty\textrm{ for all $i\in\mathbb{Z}$}\Big\}.

Theorem
3.4
implies that
\mathbb{P}(A_{T})=1
. From now on we will be working with a fixed
\omega\in A_{T}
, which most of the time will be suppressed in the notation. We will first prove that for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t\mapsto\Sigma_{t}(i)
is a càdlàg function for
t\in[0,T]
.

Fix thus
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and let
i_{1}\geq i
be such that
\ell_{i_{1}}(T)=0
. Recall (
21
) and (
22
).
Clearly, for any
t\leq T
we have
\ell_{i}^{(i_{1})}(t)\geq 0=\ell_{i_{1}}(T)=\ell_{i_{1}}^{(i_{1})}(T)
. It follows by induction that for any
j\geq i_{1}
,

\displaystyle\ell_{i}^{(j)}(t)\geq\ell_{i_{1}}^{(j)}(T).

(24)

Indeed, if (
24
) holds for some
j

\ell_{i}^{(j+1)}(t)=\ell_{i}^{(j)}(t)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{i}^{(j)}(t)\geq\ell_{j+1}(t)\}}\geq\ell_{i_{1}}^{(j)}(T)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\ell_{i_{1}}^{(j)}(T)\geq\ell_{j+1}(T)\}}=\ell_{i_{1}}^{(j+1)}(T),

where the inequality follows from the induction assumption and the estimate
\ell_{j+1}(t)\leq\ell_{j+1}(T)
.

From (
24

j\geq i_{1}
, if
\ell_{i_{1}}^{(j)}(T)\geq\ell_{j}(T)
, then
\ell_{i}^{(j)}(t)\geq\ell_{j}(t)
. By the finiteness of

, there exists
i_{2}\geq i_{1}
such that the former inequality holds for all

and

we have

In particular, it follows from (

) and (

) that for
t\leq T
,
r_{i}(t)
is fully determined by
\ell_{i}(t),\ldots,\ell_{i_{2}+1}(t)
, i.e., there exists a function
f=f_{T,i,\omega}\colon\mathbb{N}^{i_{2}-i+2}\to\mathbb{N}
such that for all
t\leq T

. Since
\ell_{i}(\cdot)
are càdlàg and take integer values,

is also càdlàg on
[0,T]
and by (
23
), so is the trajectory

.

Moreover, for all
t\leq T
,
\ell_{i}(t)=0
for infinitely many
i\leq 0
and

for all

, which by Lemma

implies that
\Sigma_{t}\in\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
. The same holds for

and

. Since the topology on

that we consider is that of pointwise convergence, the càdlàg property of
\ell_{i}(\cdot)
and

is also a càdlàg

∎

Remark 3.8
.

Thanks to the bijection between admissible inversion numbers and balanced permutations (Lemma
3.3
), the process
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
is a Markov process, since
\ell_{i}(t)
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, are Markov processes.

In the proposition below we show that
\Sigma_{t}
is a transposition process, that is, whenever it jumps, the permutations before and after the jump differ by a transposition
\sigma_{ij}
of two (not necessarily adjacent) elements. An analogous property for the Mallows process on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
was proved in
[
Cor22
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Proposition 3.9
.

With probability one the following holds – if the process
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
jumps at time
s\in(0,1)
, then letting
\Sigma_{s-}=\sigma
,
\Sigma_{s}=\sigma^{\prime}
we have
\sigma^{-1}\circ\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma_{ij}
for some
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\sigma_{ij}
is the transposition swapping
i
and
j
.

Proof.

Since the process
\Sigma_{t}
jumps only when one of the processes
\ell_{j}(t)
jumps and almost surely the jumps are not simultaneous, there exists exactly one
i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\ell_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})=\ell_{i}(s)=\ell_{i}(s-)+1=\ell_{i}(\sigma)+1
and
\ell_{j}(\sigma^{\prime})=\ell_{j}(\sigma)
for
j\neq i
. Note that by construction of the process
\Sigma_{t}
all the left and right inversion numbers
\ell_{j}(\sigma)
,
\ell_{j}(\sigma^{\prime})
,
r_{j}(\sigma)
,
r_{j}(\sigma^{\prime})
are almost surely finite.

Let us suppose that
\ell_{i}(\sigma)=k
for some
k\geq 0
, so that
\sigma(i)
is the
(k+1)
-st largest element in the sequence
(\sigma(j))_{j\leq i}
. Let
\sigma(i^{\prime})
be the
(k+2)
-nd largest element in this sequence (note that such an element must exist since otherwise
\ell_{i}(\sigma)
would not be finite). Let
\sigma_{ii^{\prime}}
be the transposition swapping
i
and
i^{\prime}
and consider the permutation
\widetilde{\sigma}=\sigma\circ\sigma_{ii^{\prime}}
. Note that
\widetilde{\sigma}
has exactly the same left inversion numbers
\ell_{j}
as
\sigma
except that
\ell_{i}(\widetilde{\sigma})=\ell_{i}(\sigma)+1
. It is also easy to see that the right inversion numbers
r_{j}(\widetilde{\sigma})
are finite, since
r_{j}(\sigma)
were finite and this property is preserved after transposing two elements. As by Lemma
3.3
the left inversion numbers uniquely characterize balanced permutations and
\sigma^{\prime}
,
\widetilde{\sigma}
have the same left inversion sequences, we must have
\sigma^{\prime}=\widetilde{\sigma}
, i.e.,
\sigma^{-1}\circ\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma_{ii^{\prime}}
, which ends the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

We will construct a coupling
\widetilde{\Sigma},\widetilde{\Sigma}^{n}
,
n\geq 1
, of the processes
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{n}
such that
with probability one the following holds – for any
T\in[0,1)
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}
there exists

such that for all

and

,
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\rho(\widetilde{\Sigma}^{n}(t),\widetilde{\Sigma}(t))\to 0
with probability one, where

metrizes pointwise convergence of permutations. It follows that

a.s. in

and as a consequence

converges weakly to

Note that the existence of such a coupling also implies that for any

and

, the processes

, as stated in Remark
1.4
.

In what follows to simplify the notation we will denote the constructed coupled processes just by
\Sigma^{n}
and
\Sigma
.

Let thus
\Sigma
be a Mallows process on
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
constructed via (
23
), where
\ell_{i}
have jump rates
q(\cdot,\cdot)
given by (
20

has the geometric distribution with parameter
1-t
). Recall also the definition (
2
) of jump rates

for the inversion counts of the Mallows process
\sigma^{n}
. Additionally, let us set

for

.

The idea of the proof is to define
\sigma^{n}
using the inversion counts
\ell^{n}_{i}
constructed by a dependent thinning of the jumps of
\ell_{i-k_{n}}
. Each jump of the latter process will be preserved with certain (conditional) probability, in such a way that the birth process defined by the preserved jumps will be a Markov process with jump rates

. The thinning will be implemented through independent random variables, uniform on

. Since the jump rates

are close to

for large

, this will allow us to show that with probability one the process
\ell^{n}_{i}
constructed this way coincide with
\ell_{i-k_{n}}
for any interval
[0,T]\subseteq[0,1)
and
n
large enough (depending on

).

Let thus
U_{i,j}
,

be a family of i.i.d. random variables uniform on
(0,1)
, independent of
\Sigma
. For
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
let
T_{i,j}
be the time of the
j
-th jump of
\ell_{i}
on
[0,1)
and let

.

Let us also for

define inductively the random variables

as

\displaystyle X^{n}_{i,0}

\displaystyle=0

and

\displaystyle X^{n}_{i,j+1}=\begin{cases}X^{n}_{i,j}+1,&\textrm{ if }U_{i-k_{n},j+1}\leq\frac{p_{i}(X^{n}_{i,j},T_{i-k_{n},j+1})}{q(\ell_{i-k_{n}}(T_{i-k_{n},j+1}-),T_{i-k_{n},j+1})},\\
X^{n}_{i,j},&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(25)

The variables

will keep track of which jumps of the process
\ell_{i-k_{n}}
have been preserved under the thinning. We remark that by definition of
T_{i,j}
we have
\ell_{i-k_{n}}(T_{i-k_{n},j+1}-)=j
, but as it will become clear in the sequel, it will be convenient to write

in terms of this more complicated expression.

Finally, we define the processes
\ell^{n}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, by setting for
t\in[T_{i-k_{n},j},T_{i-k_{n},j+1})
,

\displaystyle\ell^{n}_{i}(t)=X^{n}_{i,j}.

(26)

Note that the process
\ell^{n}_{i}
jumps only by one and is allowed to jump only at times of jumps of
\ell_{i-k_{n}}
. Whether the
j
-th jump of
\ell_{i}
becomes a jump of

depends on the auxilliary variable
U_{i-k_{n},j}
as well as on the past of the process
\ell^{n}_{i}
.

Since for a while we are going to work with fixed
i
and
n
, to simplify the notation we will temporarily write
T_{j}
instead of
T_{i-k_{n},j}
and
U_{j}
instead of
U_{i-k_{n},j}
.

We will consider two filtrations. The first one is
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, where
\mathcal{G}_{t}
is the
\sigma
-field generated by all events of the form

A=\{\ell_{i}(t_{1})=l_{1},\ldots,\ell_{i}(t_{m})=l_{m},\ell_{i}(t)=l,U_{1}\in B_{1},\ldots,U_{l}\in B_{l}\}
, where
m,l_{1},\ldots,l_{m},l\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq t_{1}<\ldots t_{m}<t
and
B_{1},\ldots,B_{l}
are Borel subsets of the real line. The second one is
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
is the
\sigma
-field generated by all events of the form

A=\{\ell_{i}(t_{i})=l_{1},\ldots,\ell_{i}(t_{m})=l_{m},\ell_{i}(t)=l,U_{1}\in B_{1},\ldots,U_{l-1}\in B_{l-1}\}
, where
m,l_{1},\ldots,l_{m},l\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq t_{1}<\ldots t_{m}<t
and
B_{1},\ldots,B_{l-1}
are Borel subsets of the real line. Thus the difference between the two filtration is in the ‘information’ about the value of
U_{l}
.

Note that
\ell_{i}^{n}
is
\mathcal{G}
-adapted. We will first show that
\ell^{n}_{i}
are counting processes with intensities
\lambda_{i}(t)=p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(t),t)
with respect to
\mathcal{G}
(the relevant notions are recalled briefly in the Appendix for the reader’s convenience).

Indeed, consider
0\leq t<s
and any
A\in\mathcal{G}_{t}
. Note that
A\cap\{t<T_{j}\leq s\}
is
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{T_{j}}
measurable for any
j\geq 0
. Moreover
U_{j}
is independent of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{T_{j}}
. Observe that (crucially)
p_{i}(j,t)\leq q(l,t)
for any
j,l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
j\leq l
. In particular, we have
\frac{p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(T_{j}-),T_{j})}{q(\ell_{i-k_{n}}(T_{j}-),T_{j})}\leq 1
and we can thus write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\ell^{n}_{i}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{A}-\mathbb{E}\ell^{n}_{i}(t)\mathbbm{1}_{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{A\cap\{t<T_{j}\leq s\}}\mathbbm{1}\Big\{U_{j}\leq\frac{p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(T_{j}-),T_{j})}{q(\ell_{i-k_{n}}(T_{j}-),T_{j})}\Big\}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{A\cap\{t<T_{j}\leq s\}}\mathbb{E}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\Big\{U_{j}\leq\frac{p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(T_{j}-),T_{j})}{q(\ell_{i-k_{n}}(T_{j}-),T_{j})}\Big\}\Big|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{T_{j}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{A\cap\{t<T_{j}\leq s\}}\frac{p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(T_{j}-),T_{j})}{q(\ell_{i-k_{n}}(T_{j}-),T_{j})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\int_{(t,s]}\frac{p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(u-),u)}{q(\ell_{i-k_{n}}(u-),u)}d\ell_{i-k_{n}}(u),

where we used independence of
U_{j}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{T_{j}}
as well as the fact that
\ell^{n}_{i}(T_{j}-)
is
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{T_{j}}
-measurable.

The process
M_{t}=\ell_{i}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}q(\ell_{i}(u),u)du
is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration of
\ell_{i}
. Using the
\pi-\lambda
system theorem and independence of
U_{i}
’s from
\ell_{i}
it is easy to show that it is also a martingale with respect to
\mathcal{G}
. Moreover, the process
Z:=(p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(u-),u)/q(\ell_{i-k_{n}}(u-),u))_{u\geq 0}
is
\mathcal{G}
-adapted and left-continuous, and thus
\mathcal{G}
-predictable. Thus,
\int_{0}^{t}Z_{u}dM_{u}
is a
\mathcal{G}
-martingale and we can further write

\mathbb{E}\ell^{n}_{i}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{A}-\mathbb{E}\ell^{n}_{i}(t)\mathbbm{1}_{A}=\mathbb{E}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\int_{t}^{s}\frac{p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(u-),u)}{q(\ell_{i-k_{n}}(u-),u)}q(\ell_{i-k_{n}}(u),u)du=\mathbb{E}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\int_{t}^{s}p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(u),u)du,

where we used that
\ell_{i}
has only countably many jumps on
[0,1)
(in fact finitely many on any compact interval). This shows that
\ell^{n}_{i}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(u),u)du
is a
\mathcal{G}
-martingale and so
\ell^{n}_{i}(t)
is indeed a counting process with intensity
\lambda_{i}(t)=p_{i}(\ell^{n}_{i}(t),t)
. Thus, by Lemma
4.2
from the Appendix,
\ell^{n}_{i}
is a Markov process on
\{0,\ldots,i-1\}
with jump rates
p_{i}
. In particular, the permutation process
\sigma^{n}_{t}
defined by the requirement that
\ell^{n}_{i}
,
i\in[n]
, be its left inversion counts is a birth Mallows process.

We will use the process
\sigma^{n}
to define
\Sigma^{n}
coupled with
\Sigma
. Set

\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i)=\sigma^{n}_{t}(k_{n}+i)-k_{n}

for
i=-k_{n}+1,\ldots,n-k_{n}
and
\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i)=i
otherwise.

We will show that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a random variable
M_{N}<\infty
such that, with probability one, for
n\geq M_{N}
, all
i\in\{-N,\ldots,N\}
and
t\leq T
we have the equality
\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i)=\Sigma_{t}(i)
.

Observe first that
\Sigma^{n}_{t}
is a balanced permutation of
\mathbb{Z}
with left inversion counts
\ell_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})=\ell_{i+k_{n}}^{n}
for
i\in\{-k_{n}+1,\ldots,n-k_{n}\}
and 0 otherwise.
We have

\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i)=i+r_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})-\ell_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t}).

where
r_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})
are the right inversion counts, which can be obtained from
\ell_{i}(\Sigma_{n}^{t})
using formulas (
21
) and (
19
).

Therefore, it suffices to show that for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, with probability one for
n
large enough and all
t\in[0,T]
we have
\ell_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})=\ell_{i}(t)
and
r_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})=r_{i}(t)
.

In what follows we will work with a fixed element of the probability space, denoted by
\omega
. All the quantities appearing in the argument may depend on
\omega
. Let
i_{1}\geq i
be such that
\ell_{i_{1}}(T)=0
. Recall that
\ell_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})
equals either to zero or to
\ell^{n}_{i+k_{n}}(t)
, and so
\ell_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})\leq\ell_{i}(t)\leq\ell_{i}(T)
. Using this inequality, similarly as in the proof of Proposition
3.7
, we show that there exists an integer
i_{2}\geq i_{1}
and a function
f=f_{T,i,\omega}\colon\mathbb{N}^{i_{2}-i+2}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
r_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})=f(\ell_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t}),\ldots,\ell_{i_{2}+1}(\Sigma^{n}_{t}))
and
r_{i}(t)=f(\ell_{i}(t),\ldots,\ell_{i_{2}+1}(t))
.
Therefore, if
\ell_{m}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})=\ell_{m}(t)
for all
m\in\{i,\ldots,i_{2}+1\}
and
t\leq T
, then also
r_{i}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})=r_{i}(t)
and as a consequence
\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i)=\Sigma_{t}(i)
for
t\leq T
.

Note that since
k_{n},n-k_{n}\to\infty
, for
n
large enough and all
m\in\{i,\ldots,i_{2}+1\}
we have
\ell_{m}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})=\ell^{n}_{m+k_{n}}(t)
. Thus, recalling (
26
),

\displaystyle\ell_{m}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})=X^{n}_{m+k_{n},j}\textrm{ for }t\in[T_{m,j},T_{m,j+1}),\;j=0,1,\ldots,

(27)

where
X^{n}_{i,j}
are defined in (
25
).

Let
A=\max\{\ell_{m}(T)\colon m\in\{i,\ldots,i_{2}+1\}\}
and
U=\max\{U_{m,j}\colon m\in\{i,\ldots,i_{2}+1\},j\in[A]\}<1
. Observe that for any
j\in\{0,\ldots,A\}
and
m\in\{i,\ldots,i_{2}+1\}
the jump rate

p_{m+k_{n}}(j,t)=\frac{1}{1-t}\left(j+1-\frac{(m+k_{n})t^{m+k_{n}-j-1}(t^{j+1}-1)}{t^{m+k_{n}}-1}\right)

converges to

q(j,t)=\frac{j+1}{1-t}

as
n\to\infty
, uniformly over
t\in[0,T]\subseteq[0,1)
.

In particular, there exists
M=M_{T,i,\omega}
such that for
n>M
,
m\in\{i,\ldots,i_{2}+1\}
, all
t\in[0,T]
and
j\leq A
, (
27
) holds and we have

\frac{p_{m+k_{n}}(j,t)}{q(j,t)}>U.

When combined with (
25
) and easy induction, this shows that all the jumps of
\ell_{m}
in
[0,T]
will be preserved when passing to
\ell^{n}_{m+k_{n}}
, i.e.,
X^{n}_{m+k_{n},j}=\ell_{m}(T_{m,j})
for all
m\in\{i,\ldots,i_{2}+1\}
and
j\in\{0,\ldots,T_{m,\ell_{m}(T)}\}
. By (
27
) this implies that for
n>M
indeed
\ell_{m}(\Sigma^{n}_{t})=\ell_{m}(t)
for all
m\in\{i,\ldots,i_{2}+1\}
and
t\in[0,T]
, and thus
\Sigma^{n}_{t}(i)=\Sigma_{t}(i)
. This ends the proof of the theorem.
∎

]
and
[
Bil99
]
). We will rely on their definitions, with an identification of
[0,1)
with
[0,\infty)
via a homeomorphism
\tau
. For concreteness let us take
\tau(t)=-\log(1-t)
, which is the same function as used in Subsection
3.2
.

To metrize the topology of pointwise convergence on
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
, let us endow it with the metric
\rho(\sigma,\eta)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-|n|}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma(i)\neq\eta(i)\}}
(the factor
\frac{1}{3}
is introduced just to make
r
bounded by one, which allows to simplify the formulas from
[
EK86
, Chapter 3.5]
). Let
\Lambda
be the collection of strictly increasing, Lipschitz functions
\lambda
, mapping
[0,\infty)
onto itself, such that

\gamma(\lambda):=\sup_{s>t\geq 0}\Big|\log\frac{\lambda(s)-\lambda(t)}{s-t}\Big|<\infty.

We define the space
\mathcal{D}=D([0,\infty),\mathfrak{S}^{bal})
as the space of all càdlàg functions on
[0,1)
with values in
\mathfrak{S}^{bal}
.

For
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
and
u\geq 0
we set

d(x,y,\lambda,u)=\sup_{t\in[0,\infty)}\rho(\widetilde{x}(t\wedge u),\widetilde{y}(\lambda(t)\wedge u)),

where
\widetilde{x}=x\circ\tau
,
\widetilde{y}=y\circ\tau
. Finally, we define

d(x,y)=\inf_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\max\Big(\gamma(\lambda),\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u}d(x,y,\lambda,u)du\Big).

Then
d
is a metric and the space
(\mathcal{D},d)
is separable and complete.

Let us comment that if
x_{n}\to x
in
\mathcal{D}
, then for any
T\in[0,1)
such that
x
is continuous at
T
, we have
x_{n}|_{[0,T]}\to x|_{[0,T]}
in the usual Skorokhod topology
J_{1}
on the space
D([0,T],\mathfrak{S}^{bal})
of càdlàg paths on a compact interval (see, e.g.,
[
Bil99
]
). In the other direction, if for every
T\in[0,1)
it holds that
x_{n}|_{[0,T]}\to x|_{[0,T]}
in
D([0,T],\mathfrak{S}^{bal})
, then
x_{n}\to x
in
\mathcal{D}
.

Counting processes and intensities.

Recall that
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
counting process
if it is a nondecreasing, integer-valued càdlàg stochastic process with jumps equal to one. If
N
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted, then a nonnegative process
(\lambda_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an
intensity
of
N
if it is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-progressively measurable,
\int\limits_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}\,ds<\infty
for every
t\geq 0
and the process
N_{t}-\int\limits_{0}^{t}\lambda_{s}\,ds
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-martingale.

The following lemma, used in the proof of Proposition
2.7
, follows easily from
[
RB06
, Corollary 1]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a counting process with intensity
(\lambda_{t})_{t\geq 0}
bounded by
a
. Set
Z_{t}=N_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{u}\,du
. Then for any
s,u>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\sup_{t\leq s}|Z_{t}|\geq u)\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{u^{2}}{4as+2u/3}\Big).

The next lemma, used in the proof of Theorem
1.3
, gives a criterion for showing when a counting process is in fact a Markov process.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a counting process with intensity
(\lambda_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. If
\lambda_{t}=p(N_{t},t)
, where
p(\cdot,\cdot)
is a bounded measurable function continuous in
t
, then
N_{t}
is a Markov birth process with birth rate given by
p
.

This rather intuitive statement can be proved by considering, for a given
s>0
and any
t\geq s
, the conditional transition probabilities of the form
\mathbb{P}(\{N_{t}=k\}|\{N_{s}=j\}\cap A)
, where
A\in\mathcal{F}_{s-}
. One then shows that they satisfy the same Chapman–Kolmogorov forward equations as the function
p(\cdot,\cdot)
and since the solution is unique, it follows that
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is in fact a Markov process. A related statement can be found in
[
Spr90
, Proposition 3.3]
(see also
[
Jac82
]
).

Auxilliary results for the global limit.

Here we collect several technical statements and proofs which are used in Section
2
along the way to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We start with the proof of Corollary
2.5
.

Proof of Corollary
2.5
.

In what follows we fix
T>0
and suppress the dependence of the constants on
T
in the notation.

Let
d_{w}
be any distance on
\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})
metrizing the weak convergence and recall the definition (
5
) of the box metric
d_{\Box}
.
By
B_{w}(\mu,\varepsilon)
and
B_{\Box}(\mu,\varepsilon)
we will denote the open balls with radius
\varepsilon
around
\mu
in
\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{2})
with respect to the metrics
d_{w}
,
d_{\Box}
.

Let
\mu_{\beta}
be the measure defined in Theorem
2.1
with density
\rho_{\beta}
given by (
6
). Note that for any
x,y\in[0,1]
the function
\beta\mapsto\rho_{\beta}(x,y)
is continuous. Thus, by Scheffé’s lemma the function
\beta\mapsto\mu_{\beta}
is continuous in the total variation distance.

For a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
let
\widehat{\mu}_{\sigma}
be the permuton with density
f_{\sigma}(x,y)=n\mathbbm{1}_{\sigma(\lceil nx\rceil)=\lceil ny\rceil}
. One easily checks that
d_{\Box}(\widehat{\mu}_{\sigma},\mu_{\sigma})\leq\frac{2}{n}
. Using the fact that the box topology and the weak topology agree on the set of permutons
\mathcal{P}
, together with continuity of the map
\beta\mapsto\mu_{\beta}
, by a compactness argument we conclude that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
such that for any
\beta\in[0,T]
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, if
\mu_{\sigma}\in B_{w}(\mu_{\beta},\varepsilon^{\prime})
then
\mu_{\sigma}\in B_{\Box}(\mu_{\beta},\varepsilon)
.

Thus for fixed
\varepsilon>0
we can apply the upper bound of Theorem
2.2
with
A=B_{w}(\mu_{\beta},\varepsilon^{\prime})^{c}
, which gives

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}\left(d_{\Box}\left(\mu_{\sigma^{n}},\mu_{\beta}\right)\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq-\inf\limits_{\mu\in B(\mu_{\beta},\varepsilon^{\prime})^{c}}\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu)=:-c_{\beta}.

By Proposition
2.4

\mu_{\beta}
is the unique minimizer of
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu_{\beta})=0
, so using lower-semicontinuity of
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}
together with compactness of its sublevel sets, we get
c_{\beta}>0
. Note that for any measure
\mu
, the ‘energy’
\mathcal{E}(\mu)
in the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu)
is bounded by
1/2
, moreover the function
\beta\mapsto p(\beta)
is continuous. This implies that the family of functions
\{\beta\mapsto\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\mu)\colon S(\mu|\lambda^{\otimes 2})<\infty\}
is uniformly equicontinuous on
[0,T]
. Thus, another compactness argument (using again the continuity of the map
\beta\mapsto\mu_{\beta}
) implies that
\inf_{\beta\in[0,T]}c_{\beta}>0
.

Using the definition of the box metric and of the measures
\mu_{\sigma_{n}}
,
\mu_{\beta}
, we thus obtain that for each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
b_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for any
\beta\in[0,T]
there exists
n_{\beta,\varepsilon}\geq 1
with the property that for all
n\geq n_{\beta,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}\left(\sup\limits_{R}\left|\Delta_{R}\left(\sigma^{n}\right)-\int\limits_{R}\rho_{\beta}(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq e^{-b_{\varepsilon}n}.

(28)

Our goal is now to argue that one may choose the constants
n_{\beta,\varepsilon}
to be uniformly bounded over
\beta\in[0,T]
. The proof will be then concluded by adjusting the constants.

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
. By continuity of the function
\beta\mapsto\mu_{\beta}
in the total variation distance, there exists
\delta>0
such that for any
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in[0,T]
less than
\delta
apart,

\sup_{R}\Big|\int\limits_{R}\rho_{\beta}(x,y)\,dx\,dy-\int\limits_{R}\rho_{\beta^{\prime}}(x,y)\,dx\,dy\Big|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Let
0=\beta_{0}<\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{N}=T
be a finite collection of points such that
\beta_{j+1}-\beta_{j}<\min(\delta,\frac{b_{\varepsilon/2}}{2})
. Consider now any
\beta\in[\beta_{j},\beta_{j+1}]
, denote the left-hand side of (
28
) by
p_{n,\beta}(\varepsilon)
and observe that

\displaystyle p_{n,\beta}(\varepsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}\left(\sup\limits_{R}\left|\Delta_{R}\left(\sigma^{n}\right)-\int\limits_{R}\rho_{\beta_{j}}(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right).

(29)

Recall the definition (
1
) of the Mallows distribution
\pi_{n,q}
. For any
B\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta}(\sigma^{n}\in B)=\int\limits_{B}e^{(\beta-\beta_{j})\frac{\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma)}{n}}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\exp(\beta_{j}/n)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\exp(\beta/n)}}d\pi_{n,\exp(\beta_{j}/n)}(\sigma)\\
\leq e^{(\beta-\beta_{j})\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\exp(\beta_{j}/n)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\exp(\beta/n)}}\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta_{j}}(\sigma^{n}\in B)\leq e^{(\beta-\beta_{j})\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{n,\beta_{j}}(\sigma^{n}\in B),

where in the first inequality we used
\mathrm{Inv}(\sigma)\leq\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
and in the second one monotonicity of
\mathcal{Z}_{n,\exp(\beta/n)}
with respect to
\beta
.
Taking into account (
29
) and (
28
) we thus obtain that for
n\geq\max\limits_{j<N}n_{\beta_{j},\varepsilon/2}
,

\sup_{\beta\in[0,T]}p_{n,\beta}(\varepsilon)\leq\max_{j<N}\Big(e^{(\beta-\beta_{j})\frac{n-1}{2}}p_{n,\beta_{j}}(\varepsilon/2)\Big)\leq e^{-(b_{\varepsilon/2}/2)n},

which ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
T>0
, the family of functions
\{z_{x,a}\colon x,a\in[0,1]\}
, where
z_{x,a}(t)
is given by (
3
), is uniformly equicontinuous on the interval
[0,T]
.

Proof.

We can rewrite the formula (
3
) as

z_{x,a}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\log\left(1+a\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{xt}(1-a)+a}\right).

Since the denominator under the logarithm is at least one for
x,a\in[0,1]
, we have

z_{x,a}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\cdot a\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{xt}(1-a)+a}+o\left(\frac{1}{t}(e^{t}-1)\right)

as
t\to 0
, which implies that
z_{x,a}(t)\to a
, uniformly over
a,x\in[0,1]
. Moreover, the function
(a,x,t)\mapsto z_{x,a}(t)
is clearly continuous and hence uniformly continuous on
[0,1]^{2}\times[\varepsilon,T]
for any
0<\varepsilon<T<\infty
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For each
T>0
there exists a constant
C=C_{T}>0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
the function
(x,y)\mapsto\lambda_{x}(y,t)
, with
\lambda_{x}(y,t)
defined by (
9
), is
C
-Lipschitz on
[0,1]^{2}
.

Proof.

The case
t=0
is straightforward, let us now focus on
t>0
.
Using that for some constant
D
and all
a\in[-T,T]
we have
|a|/D\leq|e^{a}-1|\leq D|a|
and
0\leq e^{a}-a-1\leq Da^{2}
, we obtain that for
t>0
,
x\in(0,1]
and
y\in[0,1]
,

\Big|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\lambda_{x}(y,t)\Big|=\Big|\frac{(1-e^{-ty})}{t}\cdot\frac{(e^{tx}+xte^{tx})(e^{tx}-1)-txe^{2tx}}{(e^{tx}-1)^{2}}\Big|=\Big|\frac{(1-e^{-ty})}{t}\cdot\frac{e^{tx}(e^{tx}-tx-1)}{(e^{tx}-1)^{2}}\Big|\\
\leq e^{tx}D^{4}y\leq D^{4}e^{T}.

Similarly, for
t>0
,
x\in(0,1]
,
y\in[0,1]
,

\Big|\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\lambda_{x}(y,t)\Big|=\frac{1}{t}\Big|-1+\frac{txe^{tx}}{e^{tx}-1}e^{-ty}\Big|=\Big|\frac{1-e^{tx}+txe^{tx}e^{-ty}}{t(e^{tx}-1)}\Big|\\
\leq\Big|\frac{(e^{tx}-tx-1)-tx(e^{tx}-1)-txe^{tx}(e^{-ty}-1)}{t(e^{tx}-1)}\Big|\leq D^{2}(2x+ye^{tx})\leq 3D^{2}e^{T}.

Thus for every
t\in(0,T]
, the function
(x,y)\mapsto\lambda_{x}(y,t)
is
C
-Lipschitz on
(0,1]\times[0,1]
with some constant
C
depending only on
T
. Since for fixed
y
and
t
the function
(x,y)\mapsto\lambda_{x}(y,t)
is continuous at
x=0
, this concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

For
\beta\in[0,T]
the density
\rho_{\beta}(x,y)
given by (
6
) is bounded from below by some
A=A_{T}>0
depending only on
T
.

Proof.

Let
u=\frac{x-y}{2}
,
v=\frac{x+y-1}{2}
, so that
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
corresponds to
(u,v)\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{2}
,
|u|+|v|\leq 1/2
. Let us further write

\rho_{\beta}(x,y)=\widetilde{\rho}_{\beta}(u,v)=\frac{(\beta/2)\sinh(\beta/2)}{\left(e^{-\beta/4}\cosh\beta u-e^{\beta/4}\cosh\beta v\right)^{2}}.

By differentiating the function in the denominator one easily checks that it cannot attain its maximum in the interior of the set
\{(u,v)\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{2}\,:\,|u|+|v|\leq 1/2\}
. Likewise, an elementary calculation shows that on the boundary its maximum may be attained only at one of the points
(u,v)\in\left\{(\frac{1}{2},0),(0,\frac{1}{2}),(-\frac{1}{2},0),(0,-\frac{1}{2})\right\}
. Thus it is enough to verify that
\widetilde{\rho}_{\beta}
is bounded away from
0
, uniformly in
\beta
, at these points. Since the calculation is similar in all cases, let us only check, e.g.,
(u,v)=(\frac{1}{2},0)
. We have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}_{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},0\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\beta/2)\sinh(\beta/2)}{\left(e^{-\beta/4}\cosh\frac{\beta}{2}-e^{\beta/4}\right)^{2}}=\frac{(\beta/2)\sinh(\beta/2)}{e^{-\beta/2}\cosh^{2}\frac{\beta}{2}+e^{\beta/2}-2\cosh\frac{\beta}{2}}=

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)\left(\frac{e^{\beta/2}-e^{-\beta/2}}{2}\right)}{e^{-\beta/2}\frac{e^{\beta}+e^{-\beta}+2}{4}-e^{-\beta/2}}=\frac{\beta(e^{\beta}-1)}{e^{\beta}+e^{-\beta}-2},

which is easily seen to be bounded away from
0
uniformly in
\beta\in[0,T]
.
∎
