Epstein Surfaces, W-Volume, and the Osgood-Stowe Differential
Martin Bridgeman, Kenneth Bromberg

Abstract
In a seminal paper, Epstein introduced the theory of what are now called Epstein surfaces, which construct surfaces in ℍ 3 {{\mathbb{H}}^{3}} associated to a conformal metric on a domain in ℂ ^ \widehat{{\mathbb{C}}} . More recently, these surfaces have been used by Krasnov-Schlenker to define the W-volume and renormalized volume associated with a convex co-compact hyperbolic 3-manifold. In this paper we consider Epstein surfaces, W-volume and renormalized volume in two main parts. In the first, we develop an alternate construction of Epstein surfaces using the Osgood-Stowe differential, a generalization of the Schwarzian derivative. Krasnov-Schlenker showed that the metric and shape operator of a surface in hyperbolic space is naturally dual to a conformal metric and shape operator on a projective structure via the hyperbolic Gauss map. We show that this projective shape operator can be derived from the Osgood-Stowe differential. This approach allows us to give a comprehensive and self-contained development of Epstein surfaces, W-volume, and renormalized volume. In the second part, we use the theory developed in the first to prove a number of new results including a generalization of Epstein’s univalence criterion, a variational formula for W-volume in terms of the Osgood-Stowe differential, and a use of W-volume to relate the length of the bending lamination of the convex core to the norm of the Schwarzian derivative of the associated univalent map on the conformal boundary.



1
Introduction

In
[
Eps4
]
Epstein gave a beautiful construction of a surface in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
associated to a conformal metric on a domain
\Omega\subset\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}=\partial{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. This construction has been very influential. For example, in a pair of follow-up papers
[
Eps2
,
Eps3
]
Epstein used these surface to give a new univalence condition for conformal maps of the disk. More recently, these surfaces have been used to define the
renormalized volume
of a convex co-compact hyperbolic 3-manifold.

In Epstein’s original construction the surface was described as an
envelope of horospheres
. Epstein gave parametric equations for these
Epstein surfaces
and then from this parameterization was able to calculate the first and second fundamental forms of the surface. Here will we give an alternate construction of Epstein’s surfaces. In our approach rather than calculate the first and second fundamental forms from a parameterization of the surface, motivated by work of Krasnov-Schlenker, we describe them as “dual” to certain forms on the domain
\Omega
(or more generally on a complex projective structure). These forms satisfy the classical surface equations of Gauss and Codazzi and therefore are realized as surfaces in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.

The pair of forms on
\Omega
are a conformal metric and the metric’s
Osgood-Stowe differential
(see
[
OS2
]
), which plays the role of the second fundamental form. The Osgood-Stowe differential generalizes the Schwarzian quadratic differential - if the conformal metric is the hyperbolic metric then the Osgood-Stowe differential is exactly the Schwarzian differential of the uniformizing map.

After developing the theory of Epstein surfaces we then revisit Epstein’s univalence criteria, giving new proofs that also generalize Epstein’s results.

In recent work motivated from physics, Krasnov-Shlenker
[
KS1
]
introduced the notion of W-volume for convex, compact hyperbolic 3-manifolds. Via Epstein surfaces, W-volume can be interpreted as a smooth functional on the space of conformal metrics on the boundary of conformally compact but infinite volume hyperbolic 3-manifold. In
[
KS1
]
and
[
Sch1
]
they prove many fundamental properties of W-volume and the related functional, renormalized volume
V_{R}
. These include a variational formula for W-volume and a new proof that
dV_{R}
is the Schwarzian derivative of the uniformization map for the conformal boundary. Our approach to Epstein surfaces naturally gives a new variational formula for W-volume in terms of the Osgood-Stowe differential from which the properties of W-volume developed by Krasnov-Schlenker can easily be extended. In particular, we give a new inequality bounding the
L^{2}
-norm of the Schwarzian derivative of a projective structure by the length of its associated bending lamination.

The paper has two parts, in the first we develop an alternative approach to the subject, and in the second we apply this new approach to prove a number of new results. In the first, we introduce a new framework for examining the interconnected areas of Epstein surfaces, W-volume, and renormalized volume. This framework facilitates a straightforward and comprehensive approach to the topic, enabling us to provide proofs of the main results found in
[
Eps2
,
Eps3
,
Eps4
]
and
[
KS1
]
. For completeness, we include a number of proofs of classical theorems such as Bonnet’s theorem on immersed surfaces. In the second part, we use the framework developed in the first, to establish several new results. These include a generalization of Epstein’s univalence criterion, a variational formula for W-volume in relation to the Osgood-Stowe differential, and a bound on the norm of the Schwarzian derivative in terms of the length of the bending lamination. We also mention recent work of Quinn who has extended many of these ideas in higher dimensions (see
[
Qui
]
).

2
Results

We now describe the main results of the paper. As well as these, we will also use our approach to give elementary proofs of many of the fundamental results on Epstein surfaces and W-volume. Our hope is that this will give a self-contained description to both subjects as well as a unifying approach.

2.1
Duality, Osgood-Stowe Differential

If
S
is a surface embedded in a hyperbolic 3-manifold then the induced metric on
g
and shape operator
B\colon TS\to TS
satisfy the
Gauss-Codazzi
equations. Conversely, if
g
is metric and
B
a bundle endomorpshism that satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations then, by a classical theorem of Bonnet, they are the metric and shape operator of a surface in a hyperbolic 3-manifold. Here we will develop a parallel theory for conformal metrics on
complex projective structures
where our replacement for the shape operator will be a tensor developed by Osgood-Stowe in
[
OS2
]
. Moreover we will see that there is a duality between these two theories.

If
g
is Riemannian metric then any conformally equivalent metric can be written as
e^{2\phi}g
where
\phi
is a real valued function. The
Osgood-Stowe tensor
is the traceless part of the Hessian of
\phi
and can be considered a measure of the difference between the two metrics.
While this is defined in all dimensions here we will restrict to the case of conformal metrics on a Riemann surface where the tensor can be naturally identified with a quadratic differential on the surface. One very concrete example is when
\Omega
is a hyperbolic domain in
\mathbb{C}
. Then the Osgood-Stowe tensor between the hyperbolic metric on
\Omega
and the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{C}
gives the Schwarzian derivative of the conformal map uniformizing
\Omega
. A slight variation of these ideas gives a version of the Osgood-Stowe tensor for any conformal metric on a complex projective structure on a surface.

We will see that this tensor plays a role similar to that of the second fundamental form of a surface embedded in a space. Namely, if
g
is a conformal metric on a projective structure
\Sigma
then we can define a
projective shape operator

B(\Sigma,g)
via the Osgood-Stowe tensor. Then the
projective Gauss-Codazzi equations
are

\begin{array}[]{ll}d^{\nabla}B=0&\qquad\mbox{(Codazzi)}\\
\operatorname{Tr}(B)=-2K(g)&\qquad\mbox{(Gauss)}\end{array}

where
K(g)
is the Gaussian curvature of
g
,
\nabla
the Riemannian connection for
g
, and
d^{\nabla}
is defined on an endomorphism
A:TS\rightarrow TS
by

d^{\nabla}A(X,Y)=\nabla_{X}(A(Y))-\nabla_{Y}(A(X))-A([X,Y]).

We prove:

Theorem 2.1

A pair
(g,B)
on a surface
S
satisfies the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations if and only if there exists a projective structure
\Sigma
on
S
such that
g
is a conformal metric on
\Sigma
and
B=B(\Sigma,g)
. Furthermore
\Sigma
is unique.

Before describing the duality we review the classical theory of immersed surfaces. Let
M
be a hyperbolic 3-manifold with Levi-Civita connection
\overline{\nabla}
and
S
an immersed surface in
M
with induced metric
g
. Then the
shape operator
of
S
is the endomorphism
B:TS\rightarrow TS
of the tangent bundle
TS
of
S
defined by
B(v)=\overline{\nabla}_{v}n
where
n
is a choice of unit normal vector field to
S
. For
S
the boundary of a manifold
N
our convention will be to let
n
be the outward normal
1
1

1

The standard convention is to define
B(v)=-\overline{\nabla}_{v}n
where
n
is the inward normal, which is equivalent.
.

The shape operator
B
is symmetric with respect to the metric
g
and its eigenvalues at a point are the
principal curvatures
. The pair
(g,B)
satisfy the
Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
given by

\begin{array}[]{ll}d^{\nabla}B=0&\qquad\mbox{(Codazzi)}\\
\det(B)=K(g)+1.&\qquad\mbox{(Gauss)}\end{array}

A classical result due to Bonnet, referred to as
the fundamental theorem for immersed surfaces
, gives the following converse.

Theorem 2.2
(Bonnet)

If
S
is simply connected and the pair
(g,B)
satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
then there is an immersion

f\colon S\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}

with
g=f^{*}g_{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
and shape operator
B
. Furthermore
f
is unique up to post-composition with isometries of
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.

In
[
KS1
]
Krasnov-Schlenker show that the image of an immersed surface in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
under the hyperbolic Gauss map to
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
defines a pair
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
which satisfy the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations. There is a natural duality of pairs
(g,B)\longleftrightarrow(\hat{g},\hat{B})
which can be defined by

\hat{g}(X,({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B})Y)=2g(({\operatorname{Id}}+B)X,Y)\qquad({\operatorname{Id}}+B)({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B})=2{\operatorname{Id}}.

Equivalently we can write the duality by

\hat{g}(X,Y)=g(({\operatorname{Id}}+B)X,({\operatorname{Id}}+B)Y)\qquad\hat{B}=({\operatorname{Id}}+B)^{-1}({\operatorname{Id}}-B)

and

g(X,Y)=\frac{1}{4}\hat{g}(({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B})X,({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B})Y)\qquad B=({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B})^{-1}({\operatorname{Id}}-\hat{B}).

We have:

Theorem 2.3
(see also Schlenker,
[
Sch2
]
)

Let
(g,B)
and
\left(\hat{g},\hat{B}\right)
be dual. Then
(g,B)
satisfies the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
if and only if
\left(\hat{g},\hat{B}\right)
satisfy the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations.

Observe that a local diffeomorpshism
f\colon S\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
defines a projective structure
\Sigma=(S,f)
on
S
. If
\hat{g}
is a conformal metric on
\Sigma
with
\hat{B}=B(\Sigma,\hat{g})
the projective shape operator then by the theorem of Bonnet there is an immersion
f_{0}\colon S\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
with metric and shape operator
(g,B)
that is dual to
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
. On the other hand with the exact same setup Epstein (
[
Eps4
]
) described a map
f_{\operatorname{ep}}\colon S\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
as an
envelope of horospheres
. We show that these surfaces are the same:

Theorem 2.4

Let
\Sigma=(S,f)
be a simply connected projective structure and
\hat{g}
a conformal metric on
\Sigma
. Then
f_{0}=f_{{\operatorname{ep}}}
.

2.2
Univalence criteria

In a pair of papers (see
[
Eps2
,
Eps3
]
) used (what are now called) Epstein surfaces to give new univalence criteria for conformal maps of the disk to
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
as well as a criterion for when the conformal map extended to a quasiconformal homeomorphism of the Riemann sphere.
These criteria generalized Nehari’s univalence condition (see
[
Neh
]
) as well as the Ahlfors-Weill Extension Theorem (see
[
AW
]
). Osgood-Stowe in
[
OS1
]
and subsequently Chuaqui in
[
Chu
]
, reinterpreted Epstein’s univalence criteria in terms of the Osgood-Stowe differential and proved generalizations.
Using a different approach, we give the following further generalization of Epstein and those of Osgood-Stowe and Chuaqui. Our results are most conveniently stated in terms of the
Schwarzian tensor

Q(\Sigma,g)
which is a complexification of the Osgood-Stowe tensor.

Theorem 2.5

Let
\hat{g}
be a complete metric on
\Delta
,
f\colon\Delta\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
a locally univalent map and
\Sigma=(\Delta,f)
the associated projective structure on
\Delta
.

1.

If

\|Q(\Sigma,\hat{g})\|\leq-\frac{1}{4}K(\hat{g})

then
f
is univalent and extends continuously to
\overline{\Delta}
.

2.

If

\|Q(\Sigma,\hat{g})\|<-\frac{1}{4}K(\hat{g})

then
f
extends to a homeomorphism of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
.

3.

If
K(\hat{g})<0
and

-\frac{4\|Q(\Sigma,\hat{g})\|}{K(\hat{g})}\leq k<1.

Then
f
extends to a quasiconformal homeomorphism with beltrami differential
\|\mu\|_{\infty}\leq k
.

Setting
\hat{g}=g_{{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}
we observe that the univalence criterion above gives Nehari’s univalence criterion that a locally univalent map
f:\Delta\rightarrow\hat{\mathbb{C}}
is univalent if the Schwarzian
\|Sf\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}
(see
[
Neh
]
). The quasiconformal criterion similarly generalizes the Ahlfors-Weill Extension Theorem which proves that if
\|Sf\|_{\infty}<\frac{k}{2}<\frac{1}{2}
then
f
extends to a
(1+k)/(1-k)
-quasiconformal homeomorphism (see
[
AW
]
).

2.3
W-volume

If
\bar{X}
is a compact, hyperbolizable 3-manifold with boundary then given any metric
\hat{g}
on the
\partial\bar{X}
there is a complete hyperbolic structure on
X
(the interior of
\bar{X}
) that is conformally compactified by a conformal structure in the conformal class of
\hat{g}
. The conformal boundary of this structure also determines a projective structure on
\partial\bar{X}
so there is a projective shape operator
\hat{B}=B(\partial\bar{X},\hat{g})
. The dual pair
(g,B)
will determine a surface in
X
. For now we will assume that the surface is embedded and therefore bounds a compact submanifold
N\subset X
. Then
the W-volume of
N
is defined by

W(N)=V(N)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial N}HdA

where
H
is the mean curvature of the boundary
\partial N
.
The W-volume can be considered as a functional on the space of smooth metric on
\partial\bar{X}
(again here we are ignoring some technicalities that arise when the surface determined by
(g,B)
is not embedded). If
\nu
is a variation of
\hat{g}
the we have a formula for the variation
dW(\nu)
which will be in terms of Schwarzian tensor
Q(\partial\bar{X},\hat{g})
for the projective boundary, the Beltrami differential
\mu(\nu)
which describes the infinitesimal change of conformal structure induced by
\nu
, and
dK(\nu)
, the infinitesimal change in curvature induced by
\nu
.

Theorem 2.6

The variational formula for
W
is given by

dW(v)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}dK(v)dA_{\hat{g}}-2\operatorname{Re}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}Q(\partial\bar{X},\hat{g})\cdot\mu(v).

For two metrics in the same conformal class the underlying hyperbolic 3-manifolds will be isometric. In each conformal class there is a unique hyperbolic metric so by restricting to these metrics we get a function on
\mathcal{CC}(X)
the space of convex co-compact hyperbolic metrics on
X
. This is the
renormalized volume
, denoted
V_{R}
. Then our variational formula reduces to the well known variational formula for
V_{R}
first proved by Takhtajan-Zograf and Takhtajan-Teo (see
[
TZ
]
,
[
TT
]
) in their work on the Liouville action. A proof of the variational formula for
V_{R}
using methods similar to those in this paper was given by Krasnov-Schlenker in
[
KS1
]
.

Theorem 2.7
(
[
TZ
]
,
[
TT
]
,
[
KS1
]
)

The variation of
V_{R}
on
\mathcal{CC}(X)
is given by

(dV_{R})(v)=\operatorname{Re}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}Q(X,\hat{g})\cdot\mu(v).

2.4
W-volume for pairs, projective structures

Given two conformal metrics
g,h
on
\partial\bar{X}
it is natural to define the W-volume of the pair by

W(g,h)=W(h)-W(g).

One motivation for defining this is that it can be extended to projective structures. In particular if
{\cal G}(\Sigma)
is the space of smooth conformal metrics on a projective structure
\Sigma
on a closed surface
S
of genus
g\geq 2
then for
g,h\in{\cal G}(\Sigma)
we define
W(g,h)
. This W-volume for pairs agrees with the W-volume for pairs on
\partial\bar{X}
by taking
\Sigma=\Sigma_{X}
.

In
[
KS1
]
, Krasnov-Schlenker proved a monotonicity property of W-volume, that if
g\leq h
pointwise and both have non-positive curvature, then
W(g)\leq W(h)
or equivalently that
W(g,h)\geq 0
. We generalize this monotonicity property as well as the scaling property of W-volume in the below theorem. If
g
is a smooth metric we define the curvature form by
\Omega_{g}=K(g)dA_{g}
.

Theorem 2.8

Let
g,h\in{\cal G}(\Sigma)
with
h=e^{2u}g
. Then

W(g,h)=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Sigma}u\left(\Omega_{g}+\Omega_{h}\right)=-\frac{1}{8}(\Omega_{g}+\Omega_{h})\left(\log\left(h/g\right)\right).

In particular for
g\leq h
pointwise and both non-positively curved, then
W(g,h)\geq 0
.

In
[
KS1
]
, Krasnov-Schlenker proved that in the quasifuchsian case, the hyperbolic metric had maximal W-volume over all conformal metrics with the same area. We prove the following generalization which gives a new proof of the original maximality property of Krasnov-Schlenker.

Theorem 2.9

Let
g,h\in{\cal G}(\Sigma)
with
h=e^{2u}g
such that
g,h
have the same area and
g
has constant curvature. Then

W(g,h)\leq-\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}

where
\nabla
is the gradient of
g
. In particular
h=g
is the unique maximum.

2.5
Schwarzian derivatives and bending laminations

Thurston showed that complex projective structures on a surface are parameterized by pairs
(g,\lambda)
where
g
is a hyperbolic metric and
\lambda
is a measured lamination. When
\Sigma
is the conformal boundary of a hyperbolic 3-manifold then
g
is the metric on the boundary convex core facing
\Sigma
and
\lambda
is the bending lamination (see
[
KT
]
for details). Also associated to the projective structure is the Schwarzian quadratic differential
\phi_{\Sigma}
.
Using W-volume, given a projective structure
\Sigma
we show the following surprising relation between the norms
\|\phi_{\Sigma}\|_{2},\|\phi_{\Sigma}\|_{\infty},
and the length
L(\lambda_{\Sigma})
of the bending lamination
\lambda_{\Sigma}
of
\Sigma
given by its Thurston parametrization.

Theorem 2.10

Let
\Sigma
be a projective structure with Thurston parameterization
\left(Y_{\Sigma},\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)
. Then

\|\phi_{\Sigma}\|_{2}\leq(1+\|\phi_{\Sigma}\|_{\infty})\sqrt{L(\lambda_{\Sigma})}.

By Nehari (see
[
Neh
]
) if
\Sigma
is the quotient of a domain in
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
then
\|\phi_{\Sigma}\|_{\infty}\leq 3/2
. Therefore we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 2.11

Let
\Sigma
be the quotient of a domain in
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
. Then

\|\phi_{\Sigma}\|_{2}\leq\frac{5}{2}\sqrt{L(\lambda_{\Sigma})}.

For example, this situation arises when
\Sigma
is an incompressible boundary component of a convex co-compact hyperbolic 3-manifold.

3
Linear algebra

As we will be working with conformal metrics on Riemann surfaces, it will be natural to describe tensors and forms in terms of the underlying complex structure even when the objects are
\mathbb{R}
-linear. We now describe the elementary linear algebra needed to do this.

Let
J
be the standard (almost) complex structure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If
{\partial_{x}}
and
{\partial_{y}}
are the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
then

{\partial_{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\left({\partial_{x}}-i{\partial_{y}}\right)\mbox{ and }{\partial_{\overline{z}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left({\partial_{x}}+i{\partial_{y}}\right)

are a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}
. If
dx
and
dy
are the dual basis (with respect to
\mathbb{R}
) of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
then

dz=dx+idy\mbox{ and }d{\overline{z}}=dx-idy

are the dual basis (with respect to
\mathbb{C}
) to
{\partial_{z}}
and
{\partial_{\overline{z}}}
. Note that both
dz
and
d{\overline{z}}
are
\mathbb{C}
-linear on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}
but when restricted to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
dz
is
\mathbb{C}
-linear with respect to the complex structure
\left(\mathbb{R}^{2},J\right)
and
d{\overline{z}}
is
\mathbb{C}
-anti-linear. It will be useful to write objects in complex coordinates (even if they are not
\mathbb{C}
-linear with respect to
\left(\mathbb{R}^{2},J\right)
).

The following “dot” notation will be useful. Let
T
be an
\mathbb{R}
-linear 2-tensor and
B
an
\mathbb{R}
-linear map. Then define the 2-tensor
T\cdot B
by

T\cdot B(X,Y)=T(BX,Y).

If we fix a metric on
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
we can use this to
change the type
of a tensor:
If we are given either
T
or
B
the formula

T=g\cdot B

determines the other.

We can then define the
trace
of
T
by
\operatorname{Tr}_{g}(T)=\operatorname{Tr}(B)
. As
B
depends on the metric so will
\operatorname{Tr}_{g}(T)
. The metric
g
is conformal (with respect to the complex structure
(\mathbb{R}^{2},J)
) if
J
is an isometry. This is equivalent to being a multiple of the usual Euclidean metric
{g_{\rm euc}}
. We note that
\operatorname{Tr}_{g_{\rm euc}}(T)=0
if and only
\operatorname{Tr}_{g}(T)=0
for all conformal metrics.

Both 2-tensors on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and linear maps of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to itself have unique
\mathbb{C}
-linear extensions to
\mathbb{R}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}
. If the
\mathbb{R}
-linear versions of
T
,
B
and
g
satisfy the equation
T=g\cdot B
then so will their
\mathbb{C}
-linear extensions.
The change of basis from complex coordinates to real coordinates is given by the matrix

{\mathcal{A}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1/2&1/2\\
-i/2&i/2\end{array}\right)

so if
T
is written as a matrix in the basis
\{{\partial_{x}},{\partial_{y}}\}
then its matrix representation in the complex basis
\{{\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\}
is

{\mathcal{A}}^{t}T{\mathcal{A}}.

For example, the usual Euclidean inner product
{g_{\rm euc}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is given by the identity matrix in the real basis so its
\mathbb{C}
-linear extension in the complex basis is given by

{\mathcal{A}}^{t}{\operatorname{Id}}{\mathcal{A}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1/2\\
1/2&0\end{array}\right).

In particular
{g_{\rm euc}}({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{z}})={g_{\rm euc}}({\partial_{\overline{z}}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}})=0
while
{g_{\rm euc}}({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}})={g_{\rm euc}}({\partial_{\overline{z}}},{\partial_{z}})=1/2
.

More generally if

T=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x+y&r+s\\
r-s&x-y\end{array}\right)

then

{\mathcal{A}}^{t}T{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}y-ir&x+is\\
x-is&y+ir\end{array}\right).

This gives us the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1

Let
T
be a
\mathbb{C}
-bilinear tensor on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}
that is real on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{R}
and
g
a conformal metric. If
T
is symmetric and traceless over
\mathbb{R}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{R}
then

T=Qdz^{2}+\bar{Q}d{\overline{z}}^{2}

where

Q=\frac{1}{2}\cdot(T({\partial_{x}},{\partial_{x}})-iT({\partial_{x}},{\partial_{y}})).

We also get formulas for the trace.

Lemma 3.2

Let
T
be a
\mathbb{C}
-bilinear tensor on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}
that is real on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{R}
and
g
a conformal metric. Then

\operatorname{Tr}_{g}(T)=\frac{T({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}})+T({\partial_{\overline{z}}},{\partial_{z}})}{g({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}})}

and if
T
is symmetric

\operatorname{Tr}_{g}(T)=2\operatorname{Re}\frac{T({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}})}{g({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}})}.

Given a conformal metric
g=\lambda\cdot{g_{\rm euc}}
the area form is the alternating 2-tensor given by the matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\lambda\\
-\lambda&0\end{array}\right)

in the basis
\{{\partial_{x}},{\partial_{y}}\}
for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In complex coordinates we then have:

Lemma 3.3

Let
g
be a conformal metric. Then the area form for
g
is

dA_{g}=2g({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}})dx\wedge dy=-ig({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}})dz\wedge d{\overline{z}}.

3.1
Pairing

We define a pairing between
T
an
B
by

\langle T,B\rangle_{g}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{g}(T\cdot B)dA_{g}

where
g
is any conformal metric with respect to
J
. It is clear that the definition is independent of the conformal metric
g
chosen and the pairing is
\mathbb{R}
-linear in both
T
and
B
. We will drop the subscript when the conformal class is obvious. Choosing
g=g_{euc}
by Lemma
3.2

\langle T,B\rangle=\left(T\left({\partial_{z}},B{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\right)+T\left({\partial_{\overline{z}}},B{\partial_{z}}\right)\right)dx\wedge dy.

Our normalization is chosen such that

\left\langle dz^{2},{\partial_{z}}\otimes d{\overline{z}}\right\rangle=dx\wedge dy.

This pairing is the usual pairing between quadratic differentials and Beltrami differentials from Teichmüller theory. The following is an immediate consequence of the definition.

Lemma 3.4

Let
g
be a conformal metric and
A,B
linear maps. Then

\langle g\cdot A,B\rangle=\langle g\cdot B,A\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(AB)dA_{g}.

Furthermore if
T
is symmetric and traceless then

\langle T,B\rangle=\langle T,B_{0}\rangle.

Proof:
As
(g\cdot A)\cdot B=g\cdot(AB)
and
\operatorname{Tr}_{g}(g\cdot M)=\operatorname{Tr}(M)
we have

\langle g\cdot A,B\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{g}((g\cdot A)\cdot B)dA_{g}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{g}(g\cdot(AB))dA_{g}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(AB)dA_{g}.

The first result follows by symmetry and the second by orthogonality.
\Box

3.2
Variations

If
X_{t}
is a one-parameter family of tensors then its time zero derivative
\delta X
is a tensor of the same type. Then
\delta X
is a
variation
of
X
. Often a variation of
X
will induce a variation of another tensor and we will use the
\delta
-notation to refer to the variation of this other tensor. For example, if
P
is a linear isomorphism and
\hat{P}
is its inverse the variation
\delta P
of
P
will induce a variation
\delta\hat{P}
of
\hat{P}
and by the product rule we have

\delta P\hat{P}+P\delta\hat{P}=0.

We now show that the pairing for two metrics satisfy a variational property.

Lemma 3.5

Let
g,\hat{g}
be metrics on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
related by
\hat{g}=P^{*}g
where
P
is symmetric with respect to
g
. Equivalently
g=\hat{P}^{*}\hat{g}
where
\hat{P}
is symmetric with respect to
\hat{g}
and
P\hat{P}={\operatorname{Id}}
.
Then under a smooth variation of
(g,P)

\langle\hat{g},\delta\hat{P}\rangle_{\hat{g}}+\langle\delta\hat{g},\hat{P}_{0}\rangle_{\hat{g}}=-\operatorname{sgn}(\det(P))\left(\langle g,\delta P\rangle_{g}+\langle\delta g,P_{0}\rangle_{g}\right)

where
P_{0},\hat{P}_{0}
are the traceless part of
P,\hat{P}
respectively.

Proof:

We differentiate
P\hat{P}={\operatorname{Id}}
. This gives

\delta\hat{P}=-P^{-1}\delta PP^{-1}.

By the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem for linear maps on
\mathbb{R}^{2}

P^{2}-\operatorname{Tr}(P)P+\det(P){\operatorname{Id}}=0.

Multiplying by
P^{-1}
we get

P=2P_{0}+\det(P)P^{-1}\qquad\det(P)(P^{-1})_{0}=-P_{0}\qquad{\operatorname{Id}}=2P_{0}P^{-1}+\det(P)P^{-2}.

As
\hat{P}=P^{-1}
then

\hat{P}_{0}=-\frac{P_{0}}{\det(P)}.

As
\hat{g}=P^{*}g
and
P
is symmetric with respect to
g
, then
\hat{g}=g\cdot P^{2}
. We define linear maps
\eta,\hat{\eta}
by
\delta g=g\cdot\eta,\delta\hat{g}=\hat{g}\cdot\hat{\eta}
. Then differentiating
\hat{g}=g\cdot P^{2}
gives

\delta\hat{g}=\delta g\cdot P^{2}+g\cdot(\delta PP+P\delta P)=g\cdot(\eta P^{2}+\delta PP+P\delta P).

As
\delta\hat{g}=\hat{g}\cdot\hat{\eta}=g\cdot(P^{2}\hat{\eta})
we get

\hat{\eta}=P^{-2}\eta P^{2}+P^{-2}\delta PP+P^{-1}\delta P.

By definition of the pairing
\langle\ ,\ \rangle_{\hat{g}}

\langle\hat{g},\delta\hat{P}\rangle_{\hat{g}}+\langle\delta\hat{g},\hat{P}_{0}\rangle_{\hat{g}}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{\hat{g}}(\hat{g}\cdot\delta\hat{P}+\delta\hat{g}\cdot\hat{P}_{0})dA_{\hat{g}}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\delta\hat{P}+\hat{\eta}\hat{P}_{0})dA_{\hat{g}}.

By definition of the area form we have

dA_{\hat{g}}=|\det(P)|dA_{g}=\operatorname{sgn}(\det(P))\det(P)dA_{g}.

Applying
\operatorname{Tr}(XY)=\operatorname{Tr}(YX)
and the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(\delta\hat{P}+\hat{\eta}\hat{P}_{0})dA_{\hat{g}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\operatorname{sgn}(\det(P))\operatorname{Tr}\left(\delta P\left(2P_{0}P^{-1}+\det(P)P^{-2}\right)+\eta P_{0}\right)dA_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\operatorname{sgn}(\det(P))\operatorname{Tr}(\delta P+\eta P_{0})dA_{g}.

The result then follows.
\Box

3.3
Metric variations and the strain tensor

If
g
is a metric in the conformal class of the usual complex structure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\delta g
is a variation of
g
then we define the linear map
\eta\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

\delta g=2g\cdot\eta.

A simple calculation shows that the variation of the area form
dA_{g}
satisfies

\delta(dA_{g})=\operatorname{Tr}(\eta)dA_{g}.

The traceless part
\eta_{0}
of
\eta
is the
strain
of the variation and it measures the change of conformal structure. If we complexify, by Lemma
3.1
we see that

\eta_{0}=\nu{\partial_{z}}\otimes d{\overline{z}}+\overline{\nu}{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\otimes dz

for some
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
. We then define the
Betrami differential
for the metric variation by
\mu(\delta g)=\nu{\partial_{z}}\otimes d{\overline{z}}
.

3.4
Linear algebra on the complexified tangent bundle

We can extend this discussion to the tangent bundle
TX
of a Riemann surface
X
. Namely, the one complex dimensional subspaces spanned by
{\partial_{z}}
and
{\partial_{\overline{z}}}
are invariant under automorphisms of the almost complex structure
\left(\mathbb{R}^{2},J\right)
. In particular, the bundle
TX\otimes\mathbb{C}
has a decomposition into complex line bundles
T^{1,0}X
and
T^{0,1}X
where the former is spanned by
{\partial_{z}}
and the later by
{\partial_{\overline{z}}}
. We will only use when
X
is a domain in
\mathbb{C}
where using complex coordinates will greatly simplify some computations.

For example if
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function then we can take the usual differential
df
which is
\mathbb{R}
-valued 1-form (a section of the co-tangent bundle) and extend it to a section of the dual bundle (over
\mathbb{C}
) of
TX\otimes\mathbb{C}
by declaring it to be
\mathbb{C}
-linear. With this extension we have that

df=f_{z}dz+f_{\overline{z}}d{\overline{z}}

is a section of the dual bundle of
TX\otimes\mathbb{C}
that when restricted to
TX\cong TX\otimes\mathbb{R}
this section is
\mathbb{R}
-valued and is the usual differential of
f
.

Let
g_{\phi}(,)=e^{2\phi}\langle,\rangle
be a conformal metric on
X
where
\langle,\rangle
is the usual Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\nabla^{\phi}
be the Levi-Civita connection for
g_{\phi}
. These both extend to
TX\otimes\mathbb{C}
by declaring them to be
\mathbb{C}
-linear. Then in real coordinates the gradient is

\nabla^{\phi}f=e^{-2\phi}\left(f_{x}{\partial_{x}}+f_{y}{\partial_{y}}\right).

Using the matrix
{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}
to change to complex coordinates this becomes

\nabla^{\phi}f=2e^{-2\phi}\left(f_{\overline{z}}{\partial_{z}}+f_{z}{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\right)

which naturally is a section of
TX\otimes\mathbb{C}
. We have the following elementary calculations.

Proposition 3.6

Let
g_{\phi}=e^{2\phi}{g_{\rm euc}}
with Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{\phi}
. Then

•

\nabla^{\phi}_{X}Y=\nabla_{X}Y+X(\phi)Y+Y(\phi)X-{g_{\rm euc}}(X,Y)\nabla\phi
;

•

\nabla^{\phi}_{{\partial_{z}}}{\partial_{z}}=2\phi_{z}{\partial_{z}}\qquad\nabla^{\phi}_{{\partial_{\overline{z}}}}{\partial_{\overline{z}}}=2\phi_{\overline{z}}{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\qquad\nabla^{\phi}_{{\partial_{z}}}{\partial_{\overline{z}}}=\nabla^{\phi}_{{\partial_{\overline{z}}}}{\partial_{z}}=0
;

•

{\operatorname{Hess}}f\left({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{z}}\right)=f_{zz}-2\phi_{z}f_{z}
.

Proof:
We take the equation relating
\nabla^{\phi}
and
\nabla
as the definition of
\nabla^{\phi}
. One then checks that (1) it is a connection, (2) it is torsion free and (3) it is compatible with the metric
g_{\phi}
. Therefore it must be the Levi-Civita connection. Once we have verified this over
\mathbb{R}
then the formula extends over
\mathbb{C}
.

The formulas for the covariant derivatives of
{\partial_{z}}
and
{\partial_{\overline{z}}}
then follow where we are using that
{g_{\rm euc}}\left({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{z}}\right)={g_{\rm euc}}\left({\partial_{\overline{z}}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\right)=0
and
{g_{\rm euc}}\left({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\right)={g_{\rm euc}}\left({\partial_{\overline{z}}},{\partial_{z}}\right)=1/2
.

To calculate the Hessian we use the formula

{\operatorname{Hess}}f\left(X,Y\right)=g_{\phi}\left(\nabla^{\phi}_{X}\nabla^{\phi}f,Y\right).

To evaluate this when
X=Y={\partial_{z}}
we only need to know the
{\partial_{\overline{z}}}
term of
\nabla^{\phi}_{\partial_{z}}\nabla^{\phi}f
and by the previous formulas this is

2e^{-2\phi}\left(-2\phi_{z}f_{z}+f_{zz}\right){\partial_{\overline{z}}}

and therefore

{\operatorname{Hess}}f\left({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{z}}\right)=2e^{-2\phi}\left(-2\phi_{z}f_{z}+f_{zz}\right)g_{\phi}\left({\partial_{\overline{z}}},{\partial_{z}}\right)=f_{zz}-2\phi_{z}f_{z}.

\Box

4
The Osgood-Stowe differential

If
(M,g)
is a Riemannian manifold and
f\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function then the
Osgood-Stowe differential

{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(f;g)
is the traceless part of the 2-tensor

{\operatorname{Hess}}f-df\otimes df.

If
M
is Riemann surface and
g
is a conformal metric then there is nice formula for
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(f;g)
in local coordinates.

Proposition 4.1

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}
and
g_{\phi}=e^{2\phi}{g_{\rm euc}}
a conformal metric on
\Omega
. If
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function then

{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(f;g_{\phi})=Q(f;g_{\phi})dz^{2}+\overline{Q(f;g_{\phi})}d{\overline{z}}^{2}

where

Q(f;g_{\phi})=f_{zz}-2\phi_{z}f_{z}-(f_{z})^{2}.

Proof:
By Lemma
3.1
, a symmetric traceless 2-tensor is of the form
Qdz^{2}+\overline{Q}d{\overline{z}}^{2}
so to find
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(f;g_{\phi})
we only need to find
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(f;g_{\phi})({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{z}})=Q
. By Proposition
3.6

{\operatorname{Hess}}f({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{z}})=f_{zz}-2\phi_{z}f_{z}.

As

df\otimes df({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{z}})=(f_{z})^{2}

the proposition follows.
\Box

Let
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
be conformal metrics on a Riemann surface
X
. Then
g_{1}=e^{2\phi}g_{0}
for some real valued function
\phi
. We then define

{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{0},g_{1})={\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(\phi;g_{0})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad Q(g_{0},g_{1})=Q(\phi;g_{0}).

The tensor
Q
is the
Schwarzian tensor
and has been studied in related papers by Dumas and Schlenker among others (see
[
Dum
]
and
[
Sch2
]
). The Schwarzian tensor is a quadratic differential, i.e. a section of the tensor product of the holomorphic cotangent bundle of
X
with itself.
Holomorphic quadratic differentials
appear frequently in Teichmüller theory, and will play a special role here, but in general the Schwarzian tensor will not be holomorphic. We now state some basic properties of the Schwarzian tensor and include the proofs for completeness.

Proposition 4.2

The Osgood-Stowe differential satisfies

•

{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{0},g_{1})+{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{1},g_{2})={\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{0},g_{2})
(cocycle property)

•

Q({g_{\rm euc}},f^{*}{g_{\rm euc}})=\frac{1}{2}Sf

•

{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g)=0
if
g
is a Euclidean metric on
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus\{z\}
, a complete metric of constant curvature on a round disk, or a metric of constant positive curvature on
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
.

Proof:
We first prove the cocycle property when
g_{0}={g_{\rm euc}}
,
g_{1}=e^{2\phi_{1}}{g_{\rm euc}}
and
g_{2}=e^{2\phi_{2}}g_{1}=e^{2(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})}{g_{\rm euc}}
. By Proposition
4.1

\displaystyle Q({g_{\rm euc}},g_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{1})_{zz}-(\phi_{1})_{z}^{2}

\displaystyle Q(g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{2})_{zz}-2(\phi_{1})_{z}(\phi_{2})_{z}-(\phi_{2})_{z}^{2}

\displaystyle Q({g_{\rm euc}},g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})_{zz}-(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})_{z}^{2}

and the sum of the first two lines equals the third. This proves the cocycle property in this case.

Note that if
g=g_{1}=g_{2}
we see that
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g)=-{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g,{g_{\rm euc}})
. Therefore

\displaystyle{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{0},g_{1})+{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{0},g_{1})+{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{1},{g_{\rm euc}})+{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g_{1})+{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{0},{g_{\rm euc}})+{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(g_{0},g_{2})

and we have the cocycle property in general.

For the second property we observe that

f^{*}{g_{\rm euc}}=e^{\log|f_{z}|^{2}}{g_{\rm euc}}=e^{2\phi}{g_{\rm euc}}

where
\phi=\frac{1}{2}\cdot(\log f_{z}+\log\overline{f_{z}})
. As
\log\overline{f_{z}}
is anti-holomorphic our formula for
Q
gives

Q({g_{\rm euc}},f^{*}{g_{\rm euc}})=\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{f_{zz}}{f_{z}}\right)_{z}-\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{f_{zz}}{f_{z}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}Sf.

For the last property, in all cases the isometries of
g
are restrictions of Möbius transformations and the stabilizer of any point is the full rotation group of the circle. By the cocycle property

{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},f^{*}{g_{\rm euc}})+{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(f^{*}{g_{\rm euc}},f^{*}g)={\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},f^{*}g).

If
f
is an isometry (and therefore a Möbius transformation) then
Sf=0
so the first term is zero. As
g=f^{*}g
this gives

{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(f^{*}{g_{\rm euc}},g)={\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g)

so
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g)
is invariant under the action of the isometry group of
g
.

{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g)(f_{*}X,f_{*}Y)={\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(f^{*}{g_{\rm euc}},f^{*}g)(X,Y)={\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g)(X,Y)

so at each point
z
,
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g)
is invariant under a faithful
S^{1}
-action. As
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}({g_{\rm euc}},g)
is traceless this implies that it is zero.
\Box

4.1
The Osgood-Stowe differential for projective structures

We now show how the Osgood-Stowe differential can be used to define a differential associated to complex projective structures on a Riemann surface.

A
complex projective structure

\Sigma
on a surface
S
is an atlas of charts to
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
with transition maps restrictions of Möbius transformations. A map between projective structures is an isomorphism if in every chart it is the restriction of a Möbius transformation. One way to define a complex projective structure is as a pair
(S,f)
where
S
is a surface and
f
is an immersion of
S
into
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
. Then two pairs
(S_{0},f_{0})
and
(S_{1},f_{1})
are equivalent projective structures if there is a homeomorphism
\phi\colon S_{0}\to S_{1}
and an element
\gamma\in{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
such that
\gamma\circ f_{0}=f_{1}\circ\phi
.

Not every projective structure
\Sigma
can be written as such a pair, however, one sufficient condition is for
S
to be simply connected. In particular,
\Sigma
is a projective structure on
S
then it lifts to a projective structure
\tilde{\Sigma}
on
\tilde{S}
and this projective structure can be represented by a pair
(\tilde{S},f)
. As
\tilde{\Sigma}
is the lift of a projective structure on
\Sigma
the map
f
will have a certain equivariance. That is there will be a homomorphism
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S)\to{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
such that for all
\beta\in\pi_{1}(S)
we have
f\circ\beta=\rho(\beta)\circ f
. Then
f
is the
developing map
for
\Sigma
and
\rho
the holonomy representation. The developing map will be unique up to post composition of maps in
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
. Post composing
f
with
\gamma\in{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
will have the effect of conjugating the holonomy representation
\rho
by
\gamma
.

A projective structure
\Sigma
also determines a conformal structure
X
on
S
. Let
g
be a conformal metric on
\Sigma
. Let
(U,\psi)
be a projective chart for
\Sigma
and assume that the image of
U
is in
\mathbb{C}
. Then
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(\psi^{*}{g_{\rm euc}},g)
defines a 2-tensor on
U\subset\Sigma
. By Proposition
4.1
this 2-tensor doesn’t depend on the choice of chart and determines a 2-tensor
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(\Sigma,g)
on all of
\Sigma
with associated quadratic differential
Q(\Sigma,g)
. We note that if
\Sigma
is a projective structure given by locally univalent map
f:\Delta\rightarrow\hat{\mathbb{C}}
then

Q(\Sigma,g_{{\mathbb{H}}^{2}})=Q(f^{*}{g_{\rm euc}},g_{{\mathbb{H}}^{2}})=-Q(g_{{\mathbb{H}}^{2}},f^{*}{g_{\rm euc}})=-\frac{1}{2}Sf.

If
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Sigma_{1}
are two different projective structures and
(U,\psi_{0})
and
(U,\psi_{1})
are charts for the same neighborhood
U
then
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(\psi^{*}_{0}{g_{\rm euc}},\psi^{*}_{1}{g_{\rm euc}})
defines a 2-tensor on
U
. This doesn’t depend on the choice of chart and gives a global tensor
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{1})
on
X
with associated quadratic differential
Q(\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{1})
.

Theorem 4.3

If
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Sigma_{1}
are projective structures on a conformal structure
X
then
Q(\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{1})
is a holomorphic quadratic differential. Furthermore for any projective structure
\Sigma_{0}
and any holomorphic quadratic differential
Q
on
X
there is a unique projective structure
\Sigma_{1}
with
Q(\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{1})=Q
.

Proof:
In a neighborhood
U
of
X
with charts
(U,\psi_{0})
and
(U,\psi_{1})
,
Q(\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{1})
is the Schwarzian of
\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{0}^{-1}
and hence holomorphic.

For the second statement we use that for any chart
(U,\psi_{0})
for
\Sigma
there is a locally univalent map

f\colon\psi_{0}(U)\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}

such that the Schwarzian
Sf
is
2Q
on
U
. Furthermore,
f
is unique up to post-composition by a Möbius transformation so if we let
\psi_{1}=f\circ\psi_{0}
then
(U,\psi_{1})
defines a projective atlas on
X
.
\Box

4.2
The projective second fundamental form

We define a
projective second fundamental form
of
(\Sigma,g)
by

{\rm II}(\Sigma,g)=2{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(\Sigma,g)-K(g)\cdot g

where
K(g)
is the curvature. We also define the
projective shape operator

B(\Sigma,g)\colon T\Sigma\to T\Sigma

by

{\rm II}(\Sigma,g)(X,Y)=g(B(\Sigma,g)X,Y).

We will see that the the second fundamental form for a projective structure plays a similar role as the second fundamental form for an immersed surface in a hyperbolic 3-manifold.

If

B\colon TS\to TS

is a bundle endomorphism we define the covariant derivative by

d^{\nabla}B(X,Y)=\nabla_{X}(B(Y))-\nabla_{Y}(B(Z))-B([X,Y]).

Then the
projective Gauss-Codazzi
equations
are:

\begin{array}[]{ll}d^{\nabla}B=0&\qquad\mbox{(Codazzi)}\\
\operatorname{Tr}(B)=-2K&\qquad\mbox{(Gauss)}\end{array}

We first make a preliminary calculation in a local conformal chart.

Lemma 4.4

Let
g=e^{2\phi}{g_{\rm euc}}
be a conformal metric on an open neighborhood
U
in
\mathbb{C}
.
Let

T=r(z)dz^{2}+\overline{r(z)}d\bar{z}^{2}-s(z)\cdot g

and
B
the bundle endomorphism of
TU\otimes\mathbb{C}

with
T=g\cdot B
.
Then
d^{\nabla}B=0
if and only if

2r_{\overline{z}}=-s_{z}e^{2\phi}.

Proof:
Note that
d^{\nabla}B
is alternating so we only need to evaluate
\left(d^{\nabla}B\right)\left({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\right)
and this is determined by its inner product with
{\partial_{z}}
and
{\partial_{\overline{z}}}
. Furthermore, as both
d^{\nabla}
and
g
are
\mathbb{C}
-linear extensions of
\mathbb{R}
-linear tensors, we have

g\left(\left(d^{\nabla}B\right)\left({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\right),{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\right)=\overline{g\left(\left(d^{\nabla}B\right)\left({\partial_{\overline{z}}},{\partial_{z}}\right),{\partial_{z}}\right)}.

Therefore
d^{\nabla}B=0
if and only if the above expression is zero.

We can now compute. By the definition of
d^{\nabla}B
we have

g\left(\left(d^{\nabla}B\right)\left({\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\right),{\partial_{z}}\right)=g(\nabla_{{\partial_{z}}}B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),{\partial_{z}})-g(\nabla_{{\partial_{\overline{z}}}}B({\partial_{z}}),{\partial_{z}})

as
[{\partial_{z}},{\partial_{\overline{z}}}]=0
. To compute the first term we first observe that

{\partial_{z}}g(B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),{\partial_{z}})={\partial_{z}}T({\partial_{\overline{z}}},{\partial_{z}})=-\frac{1}{2}\left(s_{z}e^{2\phi}+s(2\phi_{z}e^{2\phi})\right)

and by the compatibility of
\nabla
with the metric

\displaystyle{\partial_{z}}g(B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),{\partial_{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\nabla_{{\partial_{z}}}B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),{\partial_{z}})+g(B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),\nabla_{\partial_{z}}{\partial_{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\nabla_{{\partial_{z}}}B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),{\partial_{z}})+2\phi_{z}T({\partial_{\overline{z}}},{\partial_{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\nabla_{{\partial_{z}}}B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),{\partial_{z}})+2\phi_{z}\left(-\frac{s}{2}e^{2\phi}\right).

Setting the two equations for
{\partial_{z}}g(B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),{\partial_{z}})
equal to each other and solving for

g(\nabla_{{\partial_{z}}}B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),{\partial_{z}})
gives

g(\nabla_{{\partial_{z}}}B({\partial_{\overline{z}}}),{\partial_{z}})=-\frac{s_{z}}{2}e^{2\phi}.

Applying a similar strategy we find

g(\nabla_{{\partial_{\overline{z}}}}B({\partial_{z}}),{\partial_{z}})=r_{\overline{z}}

so
d^{\nabla}B=0
is equivalent to

2r_{\overline{z}}=-s_{z}e^{2\phi}.

\Box

We now restate and prove Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem
2.1

A pair
(g,B)
on a surface
S
satisfies the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations if and only if there exists a projective structure
\Sigma
on
S
such that
g
is a conformal metric on
\Sigma
and
B=B(\Sigma,g)
. Furthermore
\Sigma
is unique.

Proof:

First assume that
g
is a conformal metric on a projective structure
\Sigma
with a projective shape operator
B(\Sigma,g)
. If we write
{\rm II}_{\Sigma}(g)
in a local chart as in Lemma
4.4
then by the definition of
{\rm II}_{\Sigma}(g)
we have

s=K=-4e^{-2\phi}\phi_{z{\overline{z}}}

(since right hand side is the formula for the curvature) and

r=2Q(\Sigma,g)=2(\phi_{zz}-\phi_{z}^{2}).

As
-\operatorname{Tr}(B)=s
the Gauss equation holds.
It also follows that

2r_{\overline{z}}=-s_{z}e^{2\phi}

and therefore by Lemma
4.4
,
d^{\nabla}B_{\Sigma}(g)=0
.
Therefore the pair
(g,B(\Sigma,g))
satisfies the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations.

Now assume that the pair
(g,B)
satisfies the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations.

We again using the local representation of
B
from Lemma
4.4
. Then
-2\operatorname{Tr}(B)=s
and by the Gauss equation

s=K=-4e^{-2\phi}\phi_{z{\overline{z}}}.

By the Codazzi equation and Lemma
4.4
we also have

2r_{\overline{z}}=-s_{z}e^{2\phi}.

Now choose any projective structure
\Sigma_{0}
on the conformal structure
X
given by the pair
(S,g)
. Then
(g,B_{\Sigma_{0}}(g))
satisfies the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations so the
{\overline{z}}
-derivative of
2Q(\Sigma_{0},g)
is equal to the
z
-derivative of
-2Ke^{2\phi}=-2se^{2\phi}
. In particular, it is equal to
r_{\overline{z}}
so

r-2Q(\Sigma_{0},g)

is a holomorphic quadratic differential. Then by Theorem
4.3
there exists a unique projective structure
\Sigma
on
X
with

2Q(\Sigma_{0},\Sigma)=r-2Q(\Sigma_{0},g).

The cocycle property (Proposition
4.2
) then implies

2Q(\Sigma,g)=2Q(\Sigma,\Sigma_{0})+2Q(\Sigma_{0},g)=r

so
B=B(\Sigma,g)
.

\Box

We also note that the following is a direct corollary of Lemma

.

Corollary 4.5

Let

be a conformal metric on a projective structure
\Sigma
. Then
Q(\Sigma,g)
is holomorphic if and only if

has constant curvature.

Proof:
In a local chart

is of the form

. By Lemma

we have that

where

in the chart. Therefore

if and only if

. However,

is real valued so

if and only if

is constant.
\Box

We define the norm of the Schwarzian tensor by

\|Q(\Sigma,\hat{g})\|=\frac{|Q(\Sigma,\hat{g})|}{\hat{g}}.

Then we have the following description of the eigenvalues of

.

Lemma 4.6

The eigenvalues of

are

-K(\hat{g})\pm 4\|Q(\Sigma,\hat{g})\|.

Proof:
We have by definition,
{\rm II}(\Sigma,\hat{g})=\hat{g}\cdot B(\Sigma,\hat{g})

Therefore

5
The Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}

Let the pair
(g,B)
be a metric on
S
and symmetric bundle map
B\colon TS\to TS
that satisfy the
Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}d^{\nabla}B=0&\qquad\mbox{(Codazzi)}\\
\det(B)=K+1&\qquad\mbox{(Gauss)}\end{array}

where
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection for
g
and
K
is the curvature.

If
S
is an immersed surface in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
(or any hyperbolic 3-manifold) with
(g,B)
the induced metric and shape operator, then it is a classic fact that
(g,B)
satisfies the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. Conversely, if
(g,B)
is a pair that satisfies the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
then (locally)
(g,B)
is the induced metric and shape operator of an immersion. We will derive both of these facts in the course of our discussion.

We will see that there is a natural
dual pair

(\hat{g},\hat{B})
that satisfies the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations.

5.1
Dual pairs

Let
(g,B)
be a metric and tangent bundle isomorphism and assume that
-1
is not an eigenvalue of
B
. We define

\hat{g}=\left({\operatorname{Id}}+B\right)^{*}g

and we also define (if
-1
is not an eigenvalue of
B
)

\hat{B}=({\operatorname{Id}}+B)^{-1}({\operatorname{Id}}-B).

This can be inverted. Namely

g=\frac{1}{4}\left({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}\right)^{*}\hat{g}

and

B=\left({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}\right)^{-1}\left({\operatorname{Id}}-\hat{B}\right).

We then have the following fact that was also proven independently by Schlenker in a recent preprint (see
[
Sch2
]
).

Theorem 5.1

The pair
(g,B)
satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
if and only if the dual pair
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
satisfy the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations.

In
[
KS1
]
, Krasnov-Schlenker proved that if
(g,B)
satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
then
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
satisfies the projective Gauss-Codazzi equations. We essentially make the same observation as in
[
Sch2
]
that their proof works to give both implications.
The following lemma will be key to the proof of this.

Lemma 5.2

Let
(M,h)
be a Riemannian manifold with Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
. If

A\colon TM\to TM

is a bundle isomorphism and

h_{A}=A^{*}h

then

\nabla^{A}_{X}Y=A^{-1}\left(\nabla_{X}A(Y)\right)

is a connection on
M
compatible with
h_{A}
. Furthermore
\nabla^{A}
is torsion free (and hence the Levi-Civita connection for
h_{A}
) if and only if
d^{\nabla}A=0
. If
d^{\nabla}A=0
we also have
d^{\nabla^{A}}A^{-1}=0
. Finally if
M
is a surface and
A
is symmetric then

K_{A}=\frac{K}{\det(A)}

where
K
and
K_{A}
are the curvature of
h
and
h_{A}
.

Proof:
It is easy to check that the operator
\nabla^{A}
defined by the above formula is a connection and is compatible with the metric
h_{A}
. We also observe that

A\left(\nabla^{A}_{X}Y-\nabla^{A}_{Y}X-[X,Y]\right)=d^{\nabla}A(X,Y)

so
\nabla^{A}
is torsion free if and only if
d^{\nabla}A=0
. If we rearrange the formula relating the two connections to

\nabla_{X}Y=A\left(\nabla^{A}_{X}A^{-1}(Y)\right)

than as
\nabla
is torsion free the previous statement implies that
d^{\nabla^{A}}A^{-1}=0
.

To find the curvature we first observe that if
R
and
R_{A}
are the curvature tensors then
R_{A}=A^{-1}RA
giving

h_{A}\left(R_{A}(X,Y)Z,W\right)=h(R(X,Y)(AZ),(AW)).

If
M
is 2-dimensional and
A
is symmetric then we can choose
X
and
Y
to be eigenvectors of
A
. Note that they will be orthogonal with respect to both
h
and
h_{A}
. Then

\displaystyle K_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{h_{A}(R_{A}(X,Y)X,Y)}{h_{A}(X,X)h_{A}(Y,Y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{h(R(X,Y)(AX),AY)}{\det(A)^{2}h(X,X)h(Y,Y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\det(A)}{\det(A)^{2}}\cdot\frac{h(R(X,Y)X,Y)}{h(X,X)h(Y,Y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K}{\det(A)}.

\Box

Proof of Theorem
5.1
:
Let
A={\operatorname{Id}}+B
and let
(g,B)
satisfy the Codazzi equation. Then
d^{\nabla}A=d^{\nabla}{\operatorname{Id}}+d^{\nabla}B=0
(since
d^{\nabla}{\operatorname{Id}}=0
). Applying Lemma
5.2
this implies that
d^{\hat{\nabla}}A^{-1}=0
. But as
A^{-1}=({\operatorname{Id}}+B)^{-1}=\left({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}\right)/2
then
d^{\hat{\nabla}}\hat{B}=0
. It follows that
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
satisfies the Codazzi equation. Letting
A=\left({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}\right)/2
the same argument gives that if
\left(\hat{g},\hat{B}\right)
satisfies the Codazzi equation then so does
(g,B)
.

We note that for
A
a
2\times 2
matrix,

\operatorname{Tr}(A^{-1})=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(A)}{\det(A)}.

Therefore as
({\operatorname{Id}}+B)^{-1}=\left({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}\right)/2

2+\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{B})=\operatorname{Tr}({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B})=\frac{2\operatorname{Tr}({\operatorname{Id}}+B)}{\det({\operatorname{Id}}+B)}.

Solving we get

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\hat{B}\right)=\frac{2(\operatorname{Tr}({\operatorname{Id}}+B)-\det({\operatorname{Id}}+B))}{\det({\operatorname{Id}}+B)}=\frac{2(1-\det(B))}{\det({\operatorname{Id}}+B)}.

Applying the curvature formula from Lemma
5.2
this becomes

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\hat{B}\right)=2(1-\det(B))\cdot\frac{\hat{K}}{K}

and therefore
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\hat{B}\right)=-2\hat{K}
if and only if
\det B-1=K
.
\Box

We observe the following.

Lemma 5.3

Let
(g,B)
satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
and
\left(\hat{g},\hat{B}\right)
be its dual pair. Let
\epsilon=\operatorname{sgn}(\det({\operatorname{Id}}+B))=\operatorname{sgn}(\det({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}))
. Then

K(g)dA_{g}=\epsilon K(\hat{g})dA_{\hat{g}}\qquad H(g)dA_{g}=\frac{\epsilon}{4}\left(1-\det\left(\hat{B}\right)\right)dA_{\hat{g}},

where
K(g)dA_{g}
and
K(\hat{g})dA_{\hat{g}}
are the curvature forms of the metrics and
H(g)=\operatorname{Tr}(B)/2
is the mean curvature of
g
.

Proof:

We note that the equality of the curvature forms follows from Lemma
5.2
but we will give a proof of both that shows they are dual. As
({\operatorname{Id}}+B)({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B})=2{\operatorname{Id}},
by symmetry we have

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\hat{B}\right)=\frac{2(1-\det(B))}{\det({\operatorname{Id}}+B)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\operatorname{Tr}(B)=\frac{2\left(1-\det\left(\hat{B}\right)\right)}{\det\left({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}\right)}.

Then as

dA_{\hat{g}}=|\det({\operatorname{Id}}+B)|dA_{g}\qquad dA_{g}=\frac{1}{4}|\det({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B})|dA_{\hat{g}}.

Therefore

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\hat{B}\right)dA_{\hat{g}}=2\epsilon(1-\det(B))dA_{g}\qquad\operatorname{Tr}(B)dA_{g}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left(1-\det\left(\hat{B}\right)\right)dA_{\hat{g}}.

We then apply the Gauss equations
\det(B)-1=K(g)
and
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\hat{B}\right)=-2K(\hat{g})
.

\Box

5.2
Bonnet’s theorem

We now prove Bonnet’s theorem that a pair
(g,B)
on a simply connected surface
S
represents an immersion in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
if and only if they satisfy the hyperbolic Gauss-Codazzi equations. This proof was told to us by F. Labourie, see
[
Lab
, Section 2]
for his original argument in the context of real projective structures and cubic differentials. Among its advantages, this approach gives an elegant description of the variation of the fundamental pair of an immersion under normal flow.

First assume that
S
is immersed in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. We use the Minkowski model where
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
is in
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
. We then have three Levi-Civita connections:
\nabla
,
\bar{\nabla}
, and
\hat{\nabla}
for
(S,g)
,
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
. We let
N
be the normal vector field for
S
and define the vector field
X=\sum x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
. The shape operator
BY=\bar{\nabla}_{Y}N
is a bundle endomorphism of
TS
(which we implicitly identify as a sub-bundle of the restriction of
T\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
to
S
).

We let
E
be the restriction of the tangent bundle of
T\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
to
S
and
\bar{E}
the restriction of the tangent bundle
T{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
to

. Then we can write
E=\bar{E}\oplus\mathbb{R}
where the
\mathbb{R}
-bundle is spanned by
X
. Then we can write every section of
E
as
Z+\beta X
where
Z
is a section of
\bar{E}
and
\beta
is a function. With this decomposition we have

\hat{\nabla}_{Y}\left(\begin{matrix}Z\\
\beta\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bar{\nabla}_{Y}Z+\beta\hat{\nabla}_{Y}X\\
-{\langle}\hat{\nabla}_{Y}Z,X{\rangle}+Y(\beta)\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bar{\nabla}_{Y}Z+\beta Y\\
{\langle}Z,Y{\rangle}+Y(\beta)\end{matrix}\right).

We similarly have a decomposition
\bar{E}=TS\oplus\mathbb{R}
where the
\mathbb{R}
-bundle is spanned by
N
. Then any section of
\bar{E}
can be written
Z+\alpha N
where
Z
is a section of
TS
.
We have

\bar{\nabla}_{Y}\left(\begin{matrix}Z\\
\alpha\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\nabla_{Y}Z+\alpha\bar{\nabla}_{Y}N\\
{\langle}\bar{\nabla}_{Y}Z,N{\rangle}+Y(\alpha)\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\nabla_{Y}Z+\alpha B(Y)\\
-{\langle}Z,B(Y){\rangle}+Y(\alpha)\end{matrix}\right).

We can then put these two computations together as
E=\bar{E}\oplus\mathbb{R}=(TS\oplus\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{R}
and we write a section of
E
as
Z+\alpha N+\beta X
.

\hat{\nabla}_{Y}\left(\begin{matrix}Z\\
\alpha\\
\beta\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\nabla_{Y}Z+\alpha B(Y)+\beta Y\\
-{\langle}Z,B(Y){\rangle}+Y(\alpha)\\
{\langle}Z,Y{\rangle}+Y(\beta)\end{matrix}\right).

(5.1)

While the inner product
{\langle},{\rangle}
is defined on all of
E
, in the above expression we only apply it to sections of
TS
. We also observe that instead of letting
E
be the restriction of
T\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
to
S
we can define
E
is the direct sum of
TS
and the trivial
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-bundle over
S
. Then if
g
is a metric on
S
and
B
is a tangent bundle endomorphism of
TS
then we can use the above expression to define
\hat{\nabla}
as

\hat{\nabla}_{Y}\left(\begin{matrix}Z\\
\alpha\\
\beta\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\nabla_{Y}Z+\alpha B(Y)+\beta Y\\
-g(Z,B(Y))+Y(\alpha)\\
g(Z,Y)+Y(\beta)\end{matrix}\right).

Finally we define an inner product on
E
by

\left\langle\left(\begin{matrix}Y\\
\alpha_{1}\\
\beta_{1}\end{matrix}\right),\left(\begin{matrix}Z\\
\alpha_{2}\\
\beta_{2}\end{matrix}\right)\right\rangle=g(Y,Z)+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}-\beta_{1}\beta_{2}.

(5.2)

Denoting the curvature tensors by

and
\hat{R}

Finally let
N_{t}:S\rightarrow E
be the section

N_{t}=\left(\begin{matrix}0\\
\cosh t\\
\sinh t\end{matrix}\right).

Then
\pi_{p}\circ N_{t}:S\rightarrow E_{p}
is the normal vector to the surface
\pi_{p}\circ s_{t}:S\rightarrow E_{p}
.

Proof:

We have

(\pi_{p}\circ s_{t})_{*}W=(\pi_{p})_{*}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{W}\left(\begin{matrix}0\\
\sinh t\\
\cosh t\end{matrix}\right)\right)=(\pi_{p})_{*}\left(\begin{matrix}A_{t}W\\
0\\
0\end{matrix}\right)

and therefore, since
\pi_{p}
restricts to an isometry between fibers, we have

{\langle}(\pi_{p}\circ s)_{*}W,(\pi_{p}\circ s)_{*}T{\rangle}=g(A_{t}W,A_{t}T)=(A^{*}_{t}g)(W,T).

We now show that
B_{t}
is the shape operator.
For the section
N_{t}
we have

\displaystyle(\pi_{p}\circ s_{t})_{*}(B_{t}W)

\displaystyle=(\pi_{p})_{*}(\hat{\nabla}_{B_{t}W}s_{t})

\displaystyle=(\pi_{p})_{*}\left(\begin{matrix}A_{t}(B_{t}W)\\
0\\
0\end{matrix}\right)=(\pi_{p})_{*}\left(\begin{matrix}C_{t}W\\
0\\
0\end{matrix}\right)=(\pi_{p})_{*}\left(\hat{\nabla}_{W}\left(N_{t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(\pi_{p}\circ N_{t})_{*}(W).

Note that for all
W
tangent to
S
we have

0={\langle}N_{t},\hat{\nabla}_{W}s_{t}{\rangle}={\langle}\pi_{p}\circ N_{t},\pi_{p}(\hat{\nabla}_{W}s_{t}){\rangle}={\langle}\pi_{p}\circ N_{t},(\pi_{p}\circ s_{t})_{*}W{\rangle}

Thus
\pi_{p}\circ N_{t}(q)\in E_{p}
is the normal vector to the surface
\pi_{p}\circ s_{t}:S\rightarrow E_{p}
at
\pi_{p}\circ s_{t}(q)
. Therefore we define the vector field
n_{t}
to the surface in
E_{p}
given by
n_{t}(\pi_{p}\circ s_{t}(q))=(\pi_{p}\circ N_{t})(q)\in T_{\pi_{p}\circ s_{t}(q)}E_{p}
.

As
(E_{p},{\langle},{\rangle})
is just another copy of the Minkowski space it also has a Levi-Civita connection
\hat{\nabla}^{p}
. In fact this flat connection is the usual Euclidean connection so
\hat{\nabla}^{p}_{Y}Z=Y(Z)=Z_{*}Y
. If
\bar{\nabla}^{p}
is the Levi-Civita connection for
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\subset E_{p}
then
\bar{\nabla}^{p}_{Y}Z
is the part of
\hat{\nabla}^{p}_{Y}Z
that is tangent to
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. However, if
n
is normal to the surface and
W
is tangent then
\hat{\nabla}^{p}_{W}n
will be tangent to
S
so

\bar{\nabla}^{p}_{W}n=\hat{\nabla}^{p}_{W}n=n_{*}(W).

Therefore

\displaystyle(\pi_{p}\circ N_{t})_{*}(W)

\displaystyle=(n_{t}\circ(\pi_{p}\circ s_{t}))_{*}(W)

\displaystyle=(n_{t})_{*}\left((\pi_{p}\circ s_{t})_{*}(W)\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{\nabla}^{p}_{(\pi_{p}\circ s_{t})_{*}(W)}n_{t}.

Thus combining we have

(\pi_{p}\circ s_{t})_{*}(B_{t}W)=\bar{\nabla}^{p}_{(\pi_{p}\circ s_{t})_{*}(W)}n_{t}

and
B_{t}
is the shape operator to
\pi_{p}\circ s_{t}:S\rightarrow E_{p}
.

\Box

We now prove Bonnet’s theorem.

Theorem 5.8

Assume that
S
is a simply connected surface and the pair
(g,B)
is a metric and tangent bundle endomorphism of
S
. Then there is an isometric immersion

f\colon S\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}

with
g=f^{*}g_{{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}}
if and only if
(g,B)
satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.
Furthermore
f
is unique up to post-composition with isometries of
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.

Proof:
We first assume that
g=f^{*}g_{{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}}
where
f
is an immersion of
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
and
B
is the corresponding shape operator. Then the Levi-Civita connection
\hat{\nabla}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
is given by (
5.1
). This connection is flat (
\hat{R}\equiv 0
). By Lemma
5.4
,
\hat{R}\equiv 0
if and only if
(g,B)
satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.

If
(g,B)
satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations then Lemma
5.7
gives an isometric immersion of
(S,g)
in
E_{p}
(for any
p\in S
) with shape operator
B
. Is
(E_{p},{\langle},{\rangle})
is a copy of Minkowski space and (again by Lemma
5.7
) the image of immersion lies in the hyperboloid this gives the desired immersion in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.

Assume that
S
is embedded in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
. (As our calculation is local this is sufficient.) The bundle
E
is then the restriction of the tangent bundle of
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
to
S
. We have two flat connections on

. The restriction of the flat connection on
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
and the connection based on the metric and shape operator. By the discussion at the beginning of the subsection these two connections are the same. The Levi-Civita flat connection on
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
is the same as the usual flat connection on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. In particular the projections
\pi_{p}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\to E_{p}
are the identity map where

is canonically identified with
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}

\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
to

. However, the map
\pi_{p}\circ s_{0}

\Box

5.3
Normal flow

Let
f\colon S\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
be an immersion with
\hat{f}\colon S\to T^{1}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
a choice of lift to the unit tangent bundle over
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. Let
{\mathfrak{g}}\colon T^{1}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\times\mathbb{R}\to T^{1}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
be the geodesic flow and
\pi\colon T^{1}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
be projection to the basepoint in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. We can then define the
normal flow
of
f
to be the family of smooth maps
f_{t}=\pi\circ{\mathfrak{g}}_{t}\circ\hat{f}:S\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.

In the Minkowski metric,a simple calculation shows that geodesic
\gamma
in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
with
\gamma(0)=p
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v
is

\gamma(t)=\cosh(t)p+\sinh(t)v.

It follows that identifying
T^{1}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
as a subspace of
T\mathbb{R}^{3,1}=\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\times\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
, that

{\mathfrak{g}}_{t}(p,v)=(\cosh(t)p+\sinh(t)v,\sinh(t)p+\cosh(t)v).

(5.3)

Theorem 5.9

The metrics and shape operator for
f_{t}\colon S\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
are
(g_{t},B_{t})
where

g_{t}=A^{*}_{t}g
and
B_{t}=(A_{t})^{-1}C_{t}
.

Proof:

For an immersion
f:S\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
let
n:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
be a choice of unit normal. Therefore by the description of geodesics in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}

f_{t}(q)=\cosh(t)f(q)+\sinh(t)n(q).

By Lemma
5.7
for
f=\pi_{p}\circ s_{0}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
the normal is
n=\pi_{p}\circ N_{0}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
. Therefore by linearity of
\pi_{p}
on fibers

f_{t}(q)=\pi_{p}\circ(\cosh(t)s_{0}(q)+\sinh(t)N_{0}(q))=(\pi_{p}\circ s_{t})(q).

The result then follows by Lemma
5.7
.

\Box

5.4
A flow on metrics

If
\hat{g}
is a conformal metric on a projective structure we can define scaled metrics by
\hat{g}_{t}=e^{2t}\hat{g}
. Then as we will see below
\hat{B}_{t}=e^{-2t}\hat{B}
are the projective shape operators for
\hat{g}_{t}
. The formulas for the dual metrics are more involved. We now see that the dual metrics are the normal flow immersions for the original immersion.

Proposition 5.10

Let
\hat{g}
be a conformal metric on a complex projective structure
\Sigma
. Define the scaled metrics
\hat{g}_{t}=e^{2t}\hat{g}
and with projective shape operators
\hat{B}_{t}
and
(g_{t},B_{t})
be the dual pairs.

Then

\hat{B}_{t}=e^{-2t}\hat{B},\qquad g_{t}=A_{t}^{*}g,\quad\mbox{and}\quad B_{t}=\left(A_{t}\right)^{-1}C_{t}.

In particular if
f:S\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
is an immersion with fundamental pair
(g,B)
dual to
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
then the normal flow map
f_{t}:S\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
has fundamental pair
(g_{t},B_{t})
dual to
(e^{2t}\hat{g},e^{-2t}\hat{B})
.

Proof:
As the Osgood-Stowe differential is invariant under scaling we have
{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(\Sigma,\hat{g})={\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(\Sigma,\hat{g}_{t})
and it follows that
\hat{B}_{t}=e^{-2t}\hat{B}
.

By the definition of the dual metric we have

\displaystyle g_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}_{t}\right)^{*}\hat{g}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left({\operatorname{Id}}+e^{-2t}\hat{B}\right)^{*}e^{2t}\hat{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left({\operatorname{Id}}+e^{-2t}\hat{B}\right)^{*}e^{2t}\left({\operatorname{Id}}+B\right)^{*}g

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}e^{2t}\left({\operatorname{Id}}+B+e^{-2t}\left({\operatorname{Id}}-B\right)\right)^{*}g

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{*}_{t}g.

By the formula for the dual shape operator we have

\displaystyle B_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left({\operatorname{Id}}-\hat{B}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\operatorname{Id}}+e^{-2t}\hat{B}\right)^{-1}\left({\operatorname{Id}}-e^{-2t}\hat{B}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\operatorname{Id}}+e^{-2t}\left({\operatorname{Id}}+B\right)^{-1}\left({\operatorname{Id}}-B\right)\right)^{-1}\left({\operatorname{Id}}-e^{-2t}\left({\operatorname{Id}}+B\right)^{-1}\left({\operatorname{Id}}-B\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\operatorname{Id}}+B+e^{-2t}\left({\operatorname{Id}}-B\right)\right)^{-1}\left({\operatorname{Id}}+B-e^{-2t}\left({\operatorname{Id}}-B\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(A_{t}\right)^{-1}C_{t}.

The rest follows from Theorem
5.9
.

\Box

We remark that at a given point
p\in S
the metrics
g_{t}
can be singular (not positive definite) in which case the shape operator
B_{t}
will not be defined at
p
. However, for any given
p
this can happen at most two values of
t
.

We have the following description of the principal curvatures of
f_{t}:S\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.

Corollary 5.11

Let
f_{t}:S\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
have principal curvatures
\lambda_{1}(t),\lambda_{2}(t)
. Then

\lambda_{i}(t)=\frac{\tanh(t)+\lambda_{i}(0)}{1+\tanh(t)\lambda_{i}(0)}

and
f_{t}
is an immersion if
{\operatorname{cotanh}}(t)\neq-\lambda_{i}(0)
for
i=1,2
. Furthermore if

e^{2t}\geq\max\left\{\left|\frac{\lambda_{1}(0)-1}{\lambda_{1}(0)+1}\right|,\left|\frac{\lambda_{2}(0)-1}{\lambda_{2}(0)+1}\right|\right\}

then
f_{t}
is locally convex.

Proof:

By Theorem
5.9

(f_{t})^{*}g_{{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}}=g_{t}=A_{t}^{*}g
and the shape operator for
f_{t}
is
B_{t}=A_{t}^{-1}C_{t}
. The formula for
\lambda_{i}(t)
follows.

The map
f_{t}
will be locally convex if
B_{t}
has non-negative eigenvalues. Equivalently this holds if
\hat{B}_{t}=({\operatorname{Id}}-B_{t})^{-1}({\operatorname{Id}}+B_{t})
has eigenvalues in
[-1,1]
. As
\hat{B}_{t}=e^{-2t}\hat{B}
, then it follows that
f_{t}
is locally convex if
e^{2t}\geq\max\{|\hat{\lambda}_{1}(0)|,|\hat{\lambda}_{2}(0)|\}
. Rewriting in terms of
\lambda_{i}(0)
we obtain the result.

\Box

5.5
The flow space of a pair
(g,B)

The one-parameter family of metrics
g_{t}
can be combined to form a 3-manifold with a (possibly singular) Riemannian metric. We define the
flow space

{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(I)
to be the product
S\times I
with metric
g_{t}\oplus dt^{2}
. We have the following corollary.

Corollary 5.12

Let
(g,B)
satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations on
S
. Then
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(I)
is a hyperbolic manifold wherever the metric
g_{t}
is non-singular. If
S
is simply connected then there is a smooth map
F:S\times I\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
such that
F^{*}g_{{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}}=g_{t}\oplus dt^{2}
and
F
is unique up to post-composition by a Möbius transformation.

Proof:

We first observe that for
S
simply connected then by Theorem
5.9
, we have the smooth map
F:S\times I\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
defined by
F(q,t)=f_{t}(q)
and
F^{*}g_{{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}}=g_{t}\oplus dt^{2}
. By uniqueness of immersions,
F
restricted to
S\times\{0\}
is unique up to post-composition by Möbius transformation. As
F
is defined by normal flow on this immersion, it follows that
F
is unique up to post-composition by a Möbius tranformation. If
S
is not simply connected then we obtain a hyperbolic structure on the non-singular points of
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(I)
by choosing for each
U\subseteq S
open simply connected the map
F_{U}:U\times I\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. By uniqueness up to post-composition, this gives an atlas for a hyperbolic structure on the non-singular set.

\Box

Next we give conditions for the flow space to be complete.

Proposition 5.13

Let
(g,B)
be a metric and tangent bundle endomorphism on a surface
S
that satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. Assume that
g
is complete.

•

If the eigenvalues of
B
are
\geq-1
then
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}([0,\infty))
is a complete, hyperbolic 3-manifold with boundary.

•

If eigenvalues of
B
are contained in the interval
[-1,1]
then
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(\mathbb{R})
is a complete, hyperbolic 3-manifold. If we further have that
S
is simply connected then
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(\mathbb{R})
is isometric to
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.

Proof:
For both the first and second bullets the restrictions on the eigenvalues of
B
imply that
A_{t}
(and hence
g_{t}
) is non-singular. Thus the flow space is a hyperbolic manifold. We now show completeness.

Let
(p_{i},t_{i})
be a Cauchy sequence in
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(I)
where
I=[0,\infty)
or
\mathbb{R}
. The projection

{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(I)\to I

given by taking the last coordinate
is
1
-Lipschitz which implies that the sequence
t_{i}
is convergent and lies in some bounded interval
I\cap[-T,T]
. We also have that the projection

{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(I\cap[-T,T])\to S

to the first coordinate is
e^{T}
-Lipschitz to the
g
metric on
S
. To see this let
\lambda_{i}
be the eigenvalues of
B
then the eigenvalues of
A_{t}
are
\lambda_{i}(t)=\cosh t+\lambda_{i}\sinh t
and the Lipschitz constant is bounded by the reciprocal of the minimum of the eigenvalues for
t\in I\cap[-T,T]
. For
I=[0,\infty)
we have
\lambda_{i}\geq-1
and for
I=\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda_{i}\in[-1,1]
. In both cases we have for
t\in I\cap[-T,T]

\lambda_{i}(t)=\cosh t+\lambda_{i}\sinh t\geq\cosh t-\sinh t\geq e^{-T}.

In particular,
p_{i}
is convergent in
S
so
(p_{i},t_{i})
is convergent in
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(I)
.
Thus in both cases,
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(I)
is complete.

Finally if
S
is simply connected and the eigenvalues are in the interval
[-1,1]
then
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(\mathbb{R})
is a simply connected, complete hyperbolic manifold. Therefore by Corollary
5.12
. we have a local isometry
F:S\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. As a local isometry between complete hyperbolic manifolds is a covering map, then as
S\times\mathbb{R}
is simply connected,
F
must be an isometry.
\Box

We can also recover a result of Epstein.

(Epstein

Let

The pair

will satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equations in

so by Proposition
5.13
there is an isometry

from the flow space

to
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. The restriction

of

is an immersion of

5.6
The hyperbolic Gauss map

We now take the limit of the geodesic flow to obtain the hyperbolic Gauss map.
Namely, as
t\to\infty
,
{\mathfrak{g}}_{t}(p)
limits to
\partial{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}=\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
so we define the
hyperbolic Gauss map

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}\colon T^{1}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}

by setting

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\pi\circ{\mathfrak{g}}_{t}.

The map
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}
is particularly simple in the Minkowski model. We let
{\cal L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
be the light cone

{\cal L}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\ |\ {\langle}x,x{\rangle}=0,\ x\neq 0\}.

If
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
is the hyperboloid, then the Klein model is
P({{\mathbb{H}}^{3}})\subseteq\mathbb{RP}^{3}
, the projective hyperboloid with boundary
P({\cal L})
, the projective light cone. Then by the equation for geodesic flow in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
(see equation
5.3
)

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}(q,v)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}[\cosh(t)q+\sinh(t)v]=[q+v].

We therefore define
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}:T_{1}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\rightarrow{\cal L}
by

\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}(q,v)=q+v.

The identification
\partial{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}=\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
is then given by the map
p\colon{\cal L}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=(x_{1}/x_{4},x_{2}/x_{4},x_{3}/x_{4})

which descends to a homeomorphism on
P({\cal L})
. Therefore
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}:T_{1}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\rightarrow\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
satisfies

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}=p\circ\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}.

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 5.15

If
v,w\in T_{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})}{\cal L}
then

{\langle}v,w{\rangle}=\frac{1}{x_{4}^{2}}{\langle}p_{*}v,p_{*}w{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}.

Proof:
For
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4})\in\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
let
\hat{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Thus if
u\in T_{x}{\cal L}

p_{*}(u)=\frac{1}{x_{4}}\left(\hat{u}-\frac{u_{4}}{x_{4}}\hat{x}\right).

As
v,w\in T_{x}{\cal L}
then
{\langle}x,x{\rangle}={\langle}v,x{\rangle}={\langle}w,x{\rangle}=0
giving

{\langle}\hat{x},\hat{x}{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}=x^{2}_{4},\qquad{\langle}\hat{v},\hat{x}{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}=v_{4}x_{4},\qquad{\langle}\hat{w},\hat{x}{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}=w_{4}x_{4}.

Thus

\displaystyle{\langle}p_{*}v,p_{*}w{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x_{4}^{2}}\left\langle\hat{v}-\frac{v_{4}}{x_{4}}\hat{x},\hat{w}-\frac{w_{4}}{x_{4}}\hat{x}\right\rangle_{\rm{euc}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x_{4}^{2}}\left({\langle}\hat{v},\hat{w}{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}-\frac{w_{4}}{x_{4}}{\langle}\hat{v},\hat{x}{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}-\frac{v_{4}}{x_{4}}{\langle}\hat{w},\hat{x}{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}+\frac{v_{4}w_{4}}{x_{4}^{2}}{\langle}\hat{x},\hat{x}{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x_{4}^{2}}\left({\langle}\hat{v},\hat{w}{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}-v_{4}w_{4}\right)=\frac{1}{x^{2}_{4}}{\langle}v,w{\rangle}

\Box

For an immersion
f
the composition
f_{\infty}={\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}\circ\hat{f}
gives a map from
S
to
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
called the
Gauss map
of
f
. We now consider the pushforward of
\hat{g}
by the Gauss map
f_{\infty}
and show that it is conformal on
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
and can be described in terms of the visual metric.

For each
p\in{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
the sphere
T^{1}_{p}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
has an invariant metric (under isometries of
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
that fix
p
) that is unique up to scale. This is a spherical metric and we fix the scale so that the sphere has radius
1
. The
visual metric

\nu_{p}
based at
p
is the push-forward of this measure via the hyperbolic Gauss map.

Lemma 5.16

Let
f\colon S\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
be an immersion with shape operator
B
and let
\hat{f}
be the lift to
T^{1}{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. We also let
g=f^{*}g_{{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}}
. If
f_{\infty}={\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}\circ\hat{f}
then
f_{\infty}
is an immersion at
p\in S
if and only if
-1
is not an eigenvalue of
B
at
p
.
Furthermore at
p
we have

\hat{g}=({\operatorname{Id}}+B)^{*}g=(f_{\infty})^{*}(\nu_{f(p)})

so
f_{\infty}
is a conformal map from
\hat{g}

to
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
.

Proof:
By definition of
f_{\infty}
we have

f_{\infty}={\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}\circ\hat{f}=p\circ\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}\circ\hat{f}.

Let

\hat{f}:S\rightarrow T\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
be given by
\hat{f}(q)=(f(q),n(q))
. Then

(\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty})_{*}(v,w)=v+w\qquad(\hat{f})_{*}(v)=(f_{*}(v),f_{*}(Bv))

and therefore

(\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\infty}\circ\hat{f})_{*}(v)=f_{*}(v)+f_{*}(Bv)=f_{*}(({\operatorname{Id}}+B)v).

By post-composition by an isometry, we can assume that
f(q)=(0,0,0,1)
. Then
\nu_{f(q)}
is the restriction of the euclidean metric to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\hat{g}(v,w)

\displaystyle=g(({\operatorname{Id}}+B)v,({\operatorname{Id}}+B)w)

\displaystyle={\langle}f_{*}(({\operatorname{Id}}+B)v),f_{*}(({\operatorname{Id}}+B)w){\rangle}

\displaystyle={\langle}p_{*}(f_{*}(({\operatorname{Id}}+B)v)),p_{*}(f_{*}(({\operatorname{Id}}+B)w)){\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}

\displaystyle={\langle}(f_{\infty})_{*}v,(f_{\infty})_{*}w{\rangle}_{\rm{euc}}

\displaystyle=(f_{\infty})^{*}(\nu_{f(q)})(v,w)

\Box

We can also set

{\mathfrak{g}}_{-\infty}=\lim_{t\to-\infty}\pi\circ{\mathfrak{g}}_{t}

and let

f_{-\infty}={\mathfrak{g}}_{-\infty}\circ\hat{f}.

By reversing the orientation of
S
and observing that this reverses the sign of the shape operator we see that
f_{-\infty}
is conformal for the metric

\check{g}=({\operatorname{Id}}-B)^{*}g.

We note that as
\hat{g}=({\operatorname{Id}}+B)^{*}g
then

\check{g}=\left(({\operatorname{Id}}+B)^{-1}({\operatorname{Id}}-B)\right)^{*}\hat{g}=\hat{B}^{*}\hat{g}.

5.7
The hyperbolic Gauss map and projective structures

We have seen that given a conformal metric
\hat{g}
on a simply connected projective structure
\Sigma=(S,f)
we get a pair
(g,B)
that is dual to
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
where
\hat{B}
is the projective shape operator of
\hat{g}
. Then by Theorem
5.8
there is an immersion
f_{0}\colon S\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
with
g=f_{0}^{*}g_{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
and shape operator
B
. This is the
dual immersion
for the pair
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
. We can also consider it as the dual immersion for the pair
(\hat{g},\Sigma)
.

The dual immersion
f_{0}
also has a Gauss map
f_{\infty}\colon S\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
. By Lemma
5.16
,
f_{\infty}
is a
conformal map for
\hat{g}
and hence an immersion. Therefore
\Sigma_{(g,B)}=(S,f_{\infty})
is a projective structure on
S
that is in the same conformal class as
\Sigma
. We will show that
\Sigma=\Sigma_{(g,B)}
.

Theorem 5.17

Let
\hat{g}
be a conformal metric on a simply connected projective structure
\Sigma=(S,f)
. If
\hat{B}
is the projective shape operator, let
(g,B)
be the pair dual to
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
. Then
\Sigma_{(g,B)}=\Sigma
and there is a unique choice for isometric immersion
f_{0}
with associated Gauss map
f_{\infty}
such that
f_{\infty}=f
.

Proof:

The proof has three steps:

1.

Let
\hat{g}_{0}
be another conformal metric on
\Sigma
that agrees with
\hat{g}
on a open neighborhood. If
\hat{B}_{0}
is its projective shape operator and
(g_{0},B_{0})
the pair dual to
(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{B}_{0})
then

\Sigma_{(g,B)}=\Sigma_{(g_{0},B_{0})}
.

2.

If there is an open neighborhood where
\hat{g}
is the restriction of the hyperbolic metric on a round disk then
\Sigma=\Sigma_{g,B}
.

3.

For any metric
\hat{g}
on
\Sigma
there is another conformal metric
\hat{g}_{0}
such that
\hat{g}
and
\hat{g}_{0}
agree on open neighborhood and there is an open neighborhood where
\hat{g}_{0}
is the restriction of a hyperbolic metric on a round disk.

Assuming this we can prove the theorem.
By (3) we have a conformal metric
\hat{g}_{0}
on
\Sigma
that agrees with
\hat{g}
on a open neighborhood and on another open neighborhood is the restriction of the hyperbolic metric on a round disk. Therefore
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
and
(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{B}_{0})
agree on an open neighborhood so by (1) we have
\Sigma_{(g,B)}=\Sigma_{(g_{0},B_{0})}
. By (2) we have
\Sigma=\Sigma_{(g_{0},B_{0})}
. It follows that
\Sigma=\Sigma_{(g,B)}
.

We now prove the individual statements.

Proof of (1):
By Lemma
5.16
, the metrics
\hat{g}
and
\hat{g}_{0}
are conformal on
\Sigma_{(g,B)}
and
\Sigma_{(g_{0},B_{0})}
.
As
\hat{g}=\hat{g}_{0}
on an open neighborhood
U
we have that
(\hat{g},\hat{B})=(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{B}_{0})
on
U
. It follows that
(g,B)=(g_{0},B_{0})
on
U
. Therefore by Theorem
5.8
the isometric immersions for
(g,B)
and
(g_{0},B_{0})
can be chosen to agree on
U
and therefore so will their Gauss maps. The Gauss maps are conformal maps from
\hat{g}
to
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
so if they agree on an open neighborhood they agree everywhere. This implies that
\Sigma_{(g,B)}=\Sigma_{(g_{0},B_{0})}
.

Proof of (2):
We first observe a domain
\Omega\subset\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
is a projective structure and if
\hat{g}_{0}
is a conformal metric on
\Omega
then there is a dual immersion
r\colon\Omega\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
for the pair
(\Omega,\hat{g}_{0})
. Furthermore if
U
is an open neighborhood of
\Sigma=(S,f)
and
\hat{g}=f^{*}\hat{g}_{0}
on
U
then we can choose the isometric immersion
f_{0}
of
(\Sigma,\hat{g})
such that
f_{0}=r\circ f
on
U
.

We also observe that if
\Omega
is a round disk and
\hat{g}_{0}
is the hyperbolic metric then
r\colon\Omega\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
can be chosen such that Gauss map for
r
is the identity. Our assumption is that there is an open
U
where
\hat{g}=f^{*}\hat{g}_{0}
(with
\hat{g}_{0}
the hyperbolic metric on a round disk). Then on
U
the Gauss map
f_{\infty}
for
f_{0}
is the the composition of
f
with the Gauss map for
r
so
f=f_{\infty}
on
U
. Two conformal maps that agree on an open set agree everywhere so
f=f_{\infty}
and
\Sigma=\Sigma_{(g,B)}
.

Proof of (3):
Fix a point
p\in\Sigma
and let
\psi\colon\Sigma\to[0,1]
be a smooth function that is 1 in a neighborhood of
p
and has support contained in a round disk
D
. If
\hat{g}_{D}
is the hyperbolic metric on
D
we let
\hat{g}_{0}=\psi\cdot\hat{g}_{D}+(1-\psi)\cdot\hat{g}
.

Finally we note that in (2) we showed that
f_{0}
could be chosen such that
f=f_{\infty}
in the case when on an open neighborhood
\hat{g}
is the restriction of a hyperbolic metric on a round disk. In (1) we showed that the two Gauss maps can be chosen to agree. Together this implies that we can chose
f_{0}
so that
f=f_{\infty}
in the general case.
\Box

In what follows we will always choose the dual immersion
f_{0}
such that its Gauss map
f_{\infty}
has the property
f=f_{\infty}
.

We also have an equivariant version of Theorem
5.17
:

Corollary 5.18

Let
\hat{g}
be a conformal metric on a simply connected projective structure
\Sigma=(S,f)
. If
\hat{B}
is the projective shape operator, let
(g,B)
be the pair dual to
(\hat{g},\hat{B})
. Let

be the unique isometric immersion for
(g,B)
such that the

a homomorphism with

.

5.8
Epstein surfaces

Note that if
\Omega
is a domain in
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
the
\Omega
then the identity map gives
\Omega
a projective structure.
If
\hat{g}
is conformal metric on
\Omega
there then the pair
(\Omega,\hat{g})

[

) also gave a construction of an immersed surface in

associated to a conformal metric on

.
We will show that the Epstein surface is equal to the dual immersion of the pair
(\Omega,\hat{g})
.

In
[
Eps4
]
Epstein restricts to conformal metrics
\hat{g}
on domains
\Omega\subset\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
. However, the construction works just as well if we have a metric
\hat{g}
on a surface
S
and a map
f\colon S\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
that is conformal with respect to
\hat{g}
. We will work in this setting. If
S
was a domain in
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
then

would be the inclusion map.

If
\hat{g}
is a conformal metric at a point

then we let
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\hat{g}}(z)
be the set of points

in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
where the visual metric

agrees with

As before we can multiply

Theorem
2.4

Let

.

Note that we have chosen the isometric immersion

6
Univalence, quasiconformal extension and convexity

6.1
Univalence and quasidisk criteria after Epstein

Given a locally univalent map
f\colon\Delta\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
, two classical questions are what criteria on
f
imply univalence and what criteria imply its image is a quasidisk. In a sequence of important papers (see
[
Eps1
,
Eps2
,
Eps3
,
Eps4
]
), Epstein introduced a new approach to these problems using the theory of immersed surfaces in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
- the
Epstein surfaces
that we have been discussing. Following Epstein’s basic approach we reprove and strengthen Epstein’s results. These type of problems have a long history which we discuss at the end of this section.

Theorem 6.1

Let
\hat{g}
be a complete metric on
\Delta
, and
f\colon\Delta\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
a locally univalent map such that

\|Q(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})\|\leq-\frac{1}{4}K(\hat{g}).

Then the map
f
is univalent. Furthermore if
(g,B)
is the pair dual to
(\hat{g},\hat{B})

there is a unique isometry
F\colon{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(\mathbb{R})\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
which extends continuously to
\Delta\times\{\infty\}
with
F(z,\infty)=f(z)
.

Proof:

By Lemma
4.6
the eigenvalues of
\hat{B}=B(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})
are
-K(\hat{g})\pm 4\|Q(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})\|
and there by hypothesis are non-negative. Thus the eigenvalues of
B
are in the interval
(-1,1]
. By assumption
\hat{g}
is complete. Therefore as
g=\frac{1}{4}({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B})^{*}\hat{g}
, and
\hat{B}
has non-negative eigenvalues then
g
is also complete. Therefore by Proposition
5.13
, there is an isometry

F\colon{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(\mathbb{R})\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}.

As a space
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(\mathbb{R})
is homeomorphic to
\Delta\times\mathbb{R}
and
F
maps this space to
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
, we can add
\Delta\times\{+\infty\}
to the space and then continuously extend
F
to a map from
\Delta\times(-\infty,+\infty]
to
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\cup\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
. The extension is the hyperbolic Gauss map for the immersion obtained by restricting
F
to
\Delta\times\{0\}
. Therefore by Theorem
5.17
we have
F(z,+\infty)=f(z)
. As map
F
is injective on
\Delta\times\mathbb{R}
and locally injective on
\Delta\times\{+\infty\}
, this implies that
f
is injective, proving univalence.

\Box

Remark:

We note that in the above theorem we only need that the dual metric
g
is complete but stating the theorem in this generality gives a more complicated statement as it would have conditions on both metrics
\hat{g},g
.

The following is a more refined statement and has a more involved proof.

Theorem 6.2

Let
\hat{g}
be a complete metric on
\Delta
,
f\colon\Delta\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
a locally univalent map and
\hat{B}=B(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})
be the projective shape operator.

1.

If

\|Q(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})\|\leq-\frac{1}{4}K(\hat{g})

then
f
is univalent and extends continuously to the boundary of
\Delta
.

2.

If

\|Q(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})\|<-\frac{1}{4}K(\hat{g})

then
f
extends to a homeomorphism of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
and
f(\Delta)
is a Jordan domain.

3.

If
K(\hat{g})<0
and there is a
0\leq k<1
such that

-\frac{\|Q(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})\|}{4K(\hat{g})}\leq k<1.

Then
f
extends to a quasiconformal homeomorphism with beltrami differential
\|\mu\|_{\infty}\leq k
.

The are a number of natural approaches to the proof of this theorem. One involves showing that the embedding
f
extends continuously to the boundary by considering the limiting behavior of curves in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
of curvature
\leq 1
. Instead, we follow a more classical approach using Carathéodory’s theory of prime ends which we now briefly describe.

6.1.1
Extension of univalent maps, prime ends

The following theorem due to Marie Torhorst describes precisely when a univalent map of the disk extends continuously to its boundary.

Theorem 6.3
(Torhorst,
[
Tor
]
, see also
[
Pom2
]
)

Let
f:\Delta\rightarrow U\subseteq\hat{\mathbb{C}}
be a univalent map. Then
f
extends continuously to
\partial\Delta
if and only if
\partial U
is locally connected.

This theorem was proven using the theory of prime ends developed by Caratheodory (see
[
Pom2
]
for details). We now briefly describe this.

We let
U\subseteq\hat{\mathbb{C}}
be a simply connected domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. A
crosscut

C
of
U
is a simple arc in
U
with
\overline{C}=C\cup\{a,b\}
for some
a,b\in\partial U
(possibly equal).

A
null chain
of
U
is a sequence
\{C_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of crosscuts of
U
such that

•

\overline{C}_{n}\cap\overline{C}_{n+1}=\emptyset

•

C_{n}
separates
C_{0}
from
C_{n+1}

•

\operatorname{diam}(C_{n})\rightarrow 0

If
C_{n}
is a null-chain then we define
V_{n}
to be the component of
U-C_{n}
not containing
C_{0}
. Thus
V_{n}
are a decreasing collection. Two null-chains
C_{n},D_{n}
are equivalent if for sufficiently large
m
there exists an
n
such that

\mbox{$D_{m}$ separates $C_{n}$ from $C_{0}$}\qquad\mbox{$C_{m}$ separates $D_{n}$ from $D_{0}$}.

The equivalence class of null-chains is called a
prime end
of
U
. Given a prime end
p=[C_{n}]
then the
impression
of
p
is the compact set

I(p)=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\overline{V}_{n}.

This is well-defined. If
I(p)
is a single point then
p
is called
degenerate
.

Using the theory of prime ends, we have the following classical description of univalent maps with continuous extension due to Caratheodory and others.

Theorem 6.4
(see
[
Pom2
]
)

Let
f:\Delta\rightarrow U
be a univalent map. Then
f
extends continuously to
\partial\Delta
if and only if all prime ends of
U
are degenerate.

It follows from the above theorems that
\partial U
being locally connected is equivalent to
U
having only degenerate prime ends and it was this that Torhorst actually proved.

6.1.2
Continuous extension and quasidisk criterion

We let
\hat{g}
be a complete metric on
\Delta
,
f\colon\Delta\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
a locally-univalent map with projective shape operator
\hat{B}=B(\Sigma,\hat{g})
having non-negative eigenvalues. Then we have
f
is univalent with image
U=f(\Delta)
and there is an isometry
F\colon{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(\mathbb{R})\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
which extends continuously to
f
on
\Delta\times\{\infty\}
.

We let
G({{\mathbb{H}}^{3}})
be the space of oriented geodesics in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
and identify it with

G({{\mathbb{H}}^{3}})=\hat{\mathbb{C}}^{2}-\{(z,z)\ |\ z\in\hat{\mathbb{C}}\}.

The space
G({{\mathbb{H}}^{3}})
is naturally compactified by
\hat{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

Given map
F\colon{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(\mathbb{R})\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
we define map
\hat{F}:\Delta\rightarrow G({{\mathbb{H}}^{3}})
by letting
\hat{F}(z)
be the oriented geodesic
F(\{z\}\times\mathbb{R})
. In particular
\hat{F}(z)=(f(z),h(z))
for some
h:\Delta\rightarrow\hat{\mathbb{C}}
continuous.

Lemma 6.5

The map
\hat{F}:\Delta\rightarrow G({{\mathbb{H}}^{3}})
is proper and a homeomorphism onto its image. Furthermore
\hat{F}(z_{i})\rightarrow(p,p)
if and only if
f(z_{i})\rightarrow p\in\partial U
.

Proof:
we first prove properness. Let
\hat{F}(z_{i})\rightarrow L
and choose
y\in L
. Then as
F
is a homeomorphism, there is a unique
x=(z,t)
with
F(x)=y
and therefore
y\in\hat{F}(z)
. We choose a neighborhood
W\subseteq\Delta
of
z
, then
F
maps neighborhood
W\times\mathbb{R}
of
x
homeomorphically to a neighborhood of
y
. Thus geodesic
\hat{F}(z_{i})=F(\{z_{i}\}\times\mathbb{R})
intersects
F(W\times\mathbb{R})
for
i
large. As
F
is a homeomorphism this implies
z_{i}\in W
.
Thus
z_{i}\rightarrow z
and by continuity
L=\hat{F}(z)
. Thus
\hat{F}
is proper and therefore a homeomorphism onto its image. The second item follows also.
\Box

We use the above to prove continuous extension.

Theorem 6.6

Let
\hat{g}
be a complete metric on
\Delta
, and
f\colon\Delta\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
a locally univalent map such that projective shape operator
\hat{B}=B(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})
has non-negative eigenvalues. Then the map
f
is univalent and
f
extends continuously to its boundary.

Proof:
We have already proven
f
is univalent and by Caratheodory, it suffices to prove that the prime ends of
\partial U
are degenerate.

We let
F\colon{\cal F}_{(g,B)}(\mathbb{R})\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
be the isometry which extends continuously to
f
on
\Delta\times\{\infty\}
. Let
p=[C_{n}]
be a prime end of
U
. The crosscut
C_{n}
can be extended to a disk
E_{n}
in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. Explicitly we let
B_{n}=f^{-1}(C_{n})
and
E_{n}=F(B_{n}\times\mathbb{R})
. Thus
E_{n}
is a disk in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
with
\partial E_{n}\subseteq\hat{\mathbb{C}}
and
\partial E_{n}\cap U=C_{n}
. We consider
z_{n}\in B_{n}
such that (after reducing to a subsequence)
\hat{F}(z_{n})
converges. If
\hat{F}(z_{n})\rightarrow L
then by Lemma
6.5
we have
z_{n}\rightarrow z\in\Delta
with
L=\hat{F}(z)
. Therefore by continuity
f(z_{n})\rightarrow f(z)\in U
. As
f(z_{n})\in C_{n}
then
f(z)\in\partial U
a contradiction. Thus
\hat{F}(z_{n})\rightarrow(w,w)
with
w\in\partial U
and therefore
f(z_{n})\rightarrow w
. It follows that as
\operatorname{diam}(C_{n})\rightarrow 0
then
\operatorname{diam}(E_{n})\rightarrow 0
where the diameter for
E_{n}
is with respect to the Euclidean metric on the unit ball. We further have
\partial E_{n}
consists of
C_{n}
with endpoints
\partial C_{n}=\{a_{n},b_{n}\}
joined by a continuous arc in the complement of
U
. Thus
\partial E_{n}
separates
V_{n}
from
C_{0}
. It follows that
\operatorname{diam}(V_{n})\rightarrow 0
and therefore the impression
I(p)=\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\overline{V_{n}}
is a single point. Thus all the prime ends of
U
are degenerate.
It follows from Theorem
6.4
that
f
extends continuously to its boundary.

\Box

We now prove our quasidisk criterion. We will need to use a converse of the Jordan curve theorem due to Schoenflies. If
U
is open in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
then a set
K
is
accessible
from
U
if for every point
p\in K
there is a simple arc
\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow\hat{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\alpha(1)=p
and
\alpha(t)\in U
for
t\in[0,1)
. Clearly
K\subseteq\partial U
but in general it may not be true that
\partial U
is accessible from
U
although it is true for Jordan domains. We have the following;

Theorem 6.7
(Schoenflies,
[
New
, Theorem VI.16.1]
)

Let
K\subseteq\hat{\mathbb{C}}
be closed with complement two disjoint open simply connected sets
U,V
such that
K
is accessible from both
U
and
V
. Then
K
is a Jordan curve.

We apply this to show that when the shape operator has eigenvalues in
(-1,1)
then the image of
\partial\Delta
is a Jordan curve.

Theorem 6.8

Let
\hat{g}
be a complete metric on
\Delta
, and
f\colon\Delta\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
a locally univalent map such that
\hat{B}=B(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})
have positive eigenvalues. Then
U
is a Jordan domain and
f
extends to a homeomorphism on
\overline{\Delta}
.

Proof:
As
\hat{B}
has positive eigenvalues then
F
extends to a map
\Delta\times[-\infty,\infty]\rightarrow{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\cup\hat{\mathbb{C}}
with
F(z,\infty)=f(z)
and
F(z,-\infty)=h(z)
where
f
is conformal with respect to
\hat{g}
and
h
is conformal with respect to
\check{g}
. Further
U=f(\Delta)
and
V=h(\Delta)
are disjoint as if
f(z)=h(w)
then the geodesic
\hat{F}(z)
and
\hat{F}(w)
are asymptotic. Then we can choose sequence
z_{i}\rightarrow z
such that geodesics
\hat{F}(w),\hat{F}(z_{i})
intersect. But
\hat{F}(z)
foliate
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. Thus
z=w
.

Let
p\in(U\cup V)^{c}
. Then we can find
w_{i}\in{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
such that
w_{i}\rightarrow p
. We let
w_{i}\in\hat{F}(z_{i})
. If (after reducing to a subsequence)
\hat{F}(z_{i})\rightarrow L
then by Lemma
6.5

z_{i}\rightarrow z
with
L=\hat{F}(z)
. As
w_{i}
is on geodesic
\hat{F}(z_{i})
then
p
is an endpoint of
L
. Thus
p=f(z)
or
p=h(z)
contradicting
p\in(U\cup V)^{c}
. Thus
\hat{F}(z_{i})\rightarrow(p,p)
and by Lemma
6.5

p\in\partial U
and similarly
p\in\partial V
. Thus
\partial U=\partial V=(U\cup V)^{c}
and
U\cup V\cup\partial U
is a disjoint decomposition. It follows that
\partial U
is closed and as both maps
f,h
extend continuously to
\partial\Delta
, then
\partial U=\partial V
is accessible from both
U
and
V
. Thus by Schoenflies, converse of the Jordan curve theorem (Theorem
6.7
),
\partial U
is a Jordan curve.

\Box

We can now prove the more refined univalence result.

Proof of Theorem
6.2
:

By Lemma
4.6
the eigenvalues of
\hat{B}
are
-K(\hat{g})\pm 4\|Q(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})\|
. Therefore
\hat{B}
having non-negative eigenvalues is equivalent to item 1. Therefore
f
is univalent as before.

If
\hat{B}
satisfies items 2 or 3, then
\hat{B}
has positive eigenvalues. Therefore we have two hyperbolic Gauss maps
f:\Delta\rightarrow U
and
h:\Delta\rightarrow V
where
f,h
are conformal with respect to
\hat{g},\check{g}
respectively. Further by Theorem
6.8

U,V
are disjoint with
\partial U=\partial V=\partial S
a Jordan curve. Furthermore
f,h
extend to homeomorphisms on the closed disk
\overline{\Delta}
. To show they agree on
\partial\Delta
let
p\in\partial\Delta
and
f(p)=a,h(p)=b
. We choose
z_{i}\rightarrow p
and
\hat{F}(z_{i})\rightarrow(a,b)
. As
f(z_{i})\rightarrow a\in\partial U
, then by Lemma
6.5

a=b
and the maps agree on
\partial\Delta
.

Thus combining the two extensions, we obtain an extension
f_{{\operatorname{ext}}}
of
f
to
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. Explicitly we let
f_{{\operatorname{ext}}}(z)=f(z)
for
z\in\overline{\Delta}
and
f_{{\operatorname{ext}}}(z)=h(1/\overline{z})
for
z\in\Delta^{*}=\{z\in\hat{\mathbb{C}}\ |\ |z|>1\}
. By the above
f_{{\operatorname{ext}}}
is a homeomorphism extending
f
.

Now if (3) holds we show that
f_{{\operatorname{ext}}}
is a quasiconformal map. A standard property of quasiconformal mappings is if
U,V\subseteq\hat{\mathbb{C}}
are open and

is a homeomorphism which is quasiconformal on

is a quasiconformal homeomorphism on

). Thus as

is a quasiconformal on

.

This follows as
F
is conformal with respect to
\hat{g}
on
\Delta\times\{\infty\}
and conformal with respect to

\hat{B}
has eigenvalues

then

Thus

\Box

6.2
Quasi-conformal reflections

A Jordan curve
\gamma
admits a
quasiconformal reflection
if there is an orientation reversing quasiconformal homeomorphism
h:\hat{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow\hat{\mathbb{C}}
fixing
\gamma
and interchanging the two components of its complement. In
[
Ah
]
, Ahlfors gave a characterization for which Jordan curves admit a quasiconformal reflection. Epstein noted (
[
Eps2
]
) that his quasidisk criterion also gave a criterion for a Jordan curve to admit a quasiconformal reflection. We make the same observation in the following immediate corollary to Theorem
6.2

Corollary 6.9

Let
\hat{g}
be a complete metric on
\Delta
,
f\colon\Delta\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
a locally univalent map and
\hat{B}=B(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})
be the projective shape operator. If
\hat{g}
is negatively curved and there is a
0\leq k<1
such that

then
U=f(\Delta)
is a Jordan domain such that
\partial U
admits a quasiconformal reflection with beltrami differential

.

Proof:
We keep the same notation in case 3 of Theorem
6.2
above. Then we let
H:\hat{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow\hat{\mathbb{C}}
be given by
H=f_{{\operatorname{ext}}}\circ C\circ f_{{\operatorname{ext}}}^{-1}
where

. Trivially

on
\partial U
and as one of either

or

is conformal in the composition at any point in the complement of

\Box

6.2.1
History

The study of these two problems has a long history. Two foundational results are the Nehari univalence criterion that if
\|Sf\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}
then
f
is univalent (see
[
Neh
]
) and the Ahlfors-Weill Extension Theorem that if
\|Sf\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{k}{2}<\frac{1}{2}
, then
f
extends to a
K=\frac{1+k}{1-k}
quasiconformal homeomorphism (see
[
AW
]
). These results can be obtained by letting
\hat{g}
be they hyperbolic metric in Theorems
6.1
and
6.2
.

In
[
Eps2
]
Epstein proved Theorem
6.1
under two extra conditions. Namely he assumed that
\hat{g}=e^{2\phi}g_{{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}
is negatively curved (rather than just non-positively curved) and that

|\phi_{z}(w)|(1-|w|^{2})\leq c\max\left(|w|,\frac{1}{2|w|}\right).

In
[
Pom1
]
, Pommerenke replaced this later condition with the weaker assumption that
\phi=\log|h^{\prime}|
where
h:\Delta\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is conformal. We also note that these two authors did not state their conditions in terms of the Osgood-Stowe differential so their expressions appear different (and significantly longer).

In
[
OS1
]
, Osgood-Stowe were the first to state a univalence criteria in terms of their differential and their criteria also applied in higher dimensions. In our setting they showed that if
\hat{g}
is a geodesically convex conformal metric on
\Delta
with

\|Q(\Sigma_{f},\hat{g})\|_{\infty}\leq-\frac{1}{4}K

(6.4)

then the map is univalent. A crucial difference is that they do not require the metric
\hat{g}
to be complete (for example it could be the Euclidean metric on the disk) and they get better estimates when the diameter is finite. However, the sup-norm bound is a stronger assumption than the pointwise bound in Theorem
6.1
. Stronger statements of this type were later proved by Chuaqui (see
[
Chu
]
).

As we noted above when
\hat{g}
is the hyperbolic metric (3) of Theorem
6.2
is the classical result of Ahlfors-Weill (
[
AW
]
). When
\hat{g}
is is hyperbolic (2) was proven by Pommerenke-Gehring (
[
PG
]
). Under the extra technical assumptions on the metrics described above, Theorem
6.2
was proved by Epstein in
[
Eps2
]
.

As well as generalizing Epstein’s criteria, our approach is short (sections 4 to 6 of this paper), proving the main results of
[
Eps2
,
Eps3
,
Eps4
]
and does not require the use of Epstein’s unpublished paper
[
Eps1
]
where he proved a version of Shur’s Lemma for hyperbolic space. Also as Epstein’s first paper
[
Eps4
]
(where he introduced Epstein surfaces and proved many of their properties) remains unpublished, we hope that this paper will bring the results in this important paper to a wider audience.

We now consider the above univalence condition for the projective
\Sigma=(f,{\mathbb{H}}^{2})

\hat{g}=e^{2\phi}{g_{\rm euc}}

\phi=-\log(r)+h(\theta)

|Q(\Sigma,\hat{g})|\leq\frac{1}{4}\Delta\phi.

After computing we get

.

We now let

|4\mbox{cotan}^{2}(\theta)+c^{2}-2\mbox{cosec}^{2}(\theta)|\leq 2\mbox{cosec}^{2}(\theta).

After simplification, this reduces to

To compare, this gives a better univalence condition than the Nehari inequality

The hyperbolic extension of a projective structure

If a projective structure
\Sigma
is not simply connected the dual immersions will not map to
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. Instead we replace
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
with
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
, the
hyperbolic extension
of
\Sigma
.

We first define this when
\Sigma
is simply connected. In the general case we will take a quotient.

Let the pair
\Sigma=(S,f)
define a projective structure with
f\colon S\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
and immersion. Then
U\subset S
is a
round disk
in
\Sigma
if
f(U)
is a round disk. For each round disk
U
let
\bar{H}_{U}=U\times[0,1]
and
H_{U}=U\times[0,1)
. We then fix a continuous map
F_{U}\colon\bar{U}_{H}\to\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\cup\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
such that
F_{U}(z,1)=f(z)
and
F_{U}
restricted to
H_{U}
is a homeomorphism to a closed hyperbolic half-space in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. If
U_{0}
and
U_{1}
are round disks in
\Sigma
with
U_{0}\cap U_{1}\neq\emptyset
then we define
(z_{0},t_{0})\sim(z_{1},t_{1})
if
F_{U_{0}}(z_{0},t_{0})=F_{U_{1}}(z_{1},t_{1})
. This defines an equivalence relation of the disjoint union of
\bar{H}_{U}
and the
projective extension

{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
of
\Sigma
is the quotient of this union by the equivalence relation
\sim
. Similarly we define
{\operatorname{ext}}^{\rm o}(\Sigma)
as the subspace of
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
given by the equivalence relation on the disjoint union of
H_{U}
. Then
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
is homeomorphic to
S\times[0,1]
and there is a map
F\colon{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\cup\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
such that on each
\bar{U}_{H}
we have
F=F_{U}
. In particular
F(z,1)=f(z)
. Note that
F
pulls back a hyperbolic metric to

{\operatorname{ext}}^{\rm o}(\Sigma)
but the boundary is not smooth. Instead
S\times\{0\}
is a locally convex
pleated surface
. We also note that while if
(z_{0},1)\sim(z_{1},t_{1})
then
t_{1}=1
in general the equivalence relation does not respect the product structure on the
\bar{U}_{H}
. For further details see
[
KT
]
.

If
\Sigma
is a projective structure on a surface
S
that does not come from a pair
(S,f)
then we can lift
\Sigma
to a projective structure
\tilde{\Sigma}
on the universal cover
\tilde{S}
of
S
. Then
\tilde{\Sigma}
does come from a pair
(\tilde{S},f)
and furthermore the deck action on
\tilde{\Sigma}
will extend to a deck action on
{\operatorname{ext}}(\tilde{\Sigma})
where the action on the hyperbolic part of
{\operatorname{ext}}(\tilde{\Sigma})
will be by isometries. Then the quotient of
{\operatorname{ext}}(\tilde{\Sigma})
by this deck action will be
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
.

7.1
Embedding the flow space in
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)

We consider conformal metrics
\hat{g}
on
\Sigma
such that the dual pair
(g,B)
locally convex surfaces. We show that then the associated flow space
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}([0,\infty))
isometrically embeds in
{\operatorname{ext}}^{\rm o}(\Sigma)
. Further using this, we will show that for two such metrics
\hat{g}_{1},\hat{g}_{2}
with
\hat{g}_{1}\leq\hat{g}_{2}
then
{\cal F}_{(g_{2},B_{2})}([0,\infty))
isometrically embeds in
{\cal F}_{(g_{1},B_{1})}([0,\infty))
.

Proposition 7.1

Let
\hat{g}
be a conformal metric on a projective structure
\Sigma
on a surface
S
with projective shape operator
\hat{B}
and dual pair
(g,B)
. If
g
is complete and the eigenvalues
\hat{B}
are between
(-1,1]
the flow space
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}([0,\infty))
isometrically embeds in
{\operatorname{ext}}^{\rm o}(\Sigma)
.

The intersection of
\Sigma\times\{0\}
with the internal boundary of
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
is a union of plaques.

Proof:
First assume that
\Sigma
is simply connected. By Lemma
5.13
since
g
is complete the flow space
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}([0,\infty))
is complete and any piece of a hyperbolic plane tangent to
\Sigma\times\{0\}
will extend to an immersed copy of
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
. The local isometry from the flow space to
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
this plane will be embedded and hence it must be embedded in the flow space. In particular it will extend to a round disk
U
in
\Sigma
. One can then construct the flow space similarly to the construction of
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
however rather than take all round disks in
\Sigma
we just take those that arise from tangent planes to
\Sigma\times\{0\}
. Then the resulting quotient space will be a subspace of
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
. On the other hand this quotient space will be isometric to the original flow space
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}([0,\infty))
so the flow space isometrically embeds in
{\operatorname{ext}}^{\rm o}(\Sigma)
.

Note that the construction of
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
and the flow space is canonical so any deck action that preserves both the projective structure and the metric
\hat{g}
will extends to isometries of
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
and the flow space. In particular the quotient of the flow space (which will be the flow space of the quotient) will isometrically embed in the quotient of
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
(which will be the extension of the quotient). Therefore to prove the proposition when
\Sigma
isn’t simply connected we lift to the universal cover, construct the embedding there and then take the quotient.

\Box

There is a natural metric on
\Sigma
closely associated to
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
. As before assume that
\Sigma
is simply connected. If
U
is a round disk let
\hat{g}_{U}
be the hyperbolic metric. Then Thurston defines the
projective metric

\hat{g}_{\Sigma}
to be the infimum, at each point

of
\hat{g}_{U}
at
z
where
U
varies over all round disks that contain
z
. If
\Sigma
is hyperbolic then the infimum is realized and
\hat{g}_{\Sigma}
is everywhere positive. We note that while this metric will be continuous, it will not be smooth. If
\Sigma
is not simply connected we observe that the projective metric on the universal cover will be equivariant and descend to a metric on
\Sigma
. This is the projective metric
\hat{g}_{\Sigma}
on
\Sigma
.

An important special cases is when
\Sigma
is an open domain in
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
. That is
\Omega=\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}\smallsetminus\Lambda
for some closed set
\Lambda
in
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
. The convex hull of
\Lambda
in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
is the smallest, closed convex set whose closure in
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}=\partial{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
is
\Lambda
. The convex hull is the intersection of all half spaces in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
whose closure in
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
contains
\Lambda
. Then by definition
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
is the union of the complementary half-spaces. It follows that
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
is the complement of the interior of the convex hull.

Let
\Sigma
be a projective structure on a simply connected surface
S
determined by an immersion
f_{\infty}\colon S\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
. We can assume that the restriction of
F\colon{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\cup\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
to

is

. If
{\mathfrak{h}}
is a horoball in
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
based at

then
F({\mathfrak{h}})
is a horoball in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
based at

. Then
F({\mathfrak{h}})={\mathfrak{h}}_{g_{w}}(w)
where
g_{w}
is a conformal metric for the tangent space

. If
\hat{g}
is conformal metric on
\Sigma
and
\hat{g}=(f_{\infty})^{*}g_{w}
at

{\mathfrak{h}}_{\hat{g}}(z)={\mathfrak{h}}
.

The next lemma gives a condition on the conformal metric

for the horoballs

.

Lemma 7.2

If
\hat{g}
is a conformal metric on a simply connected projective structure

{\mathfrak{h}}_{\hat{g}}(z)

By the definition of the projective metric for any

Theorem 7.4

As

Hyperbolic 3-manifolds

Let
M
be a hyperbolic 3-manifold. Then
M={{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}/\Gamma
where the deck group
\Gamma
is a discrete group of isometries of
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. The group
\Gamma
will also act on
\partial{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}=\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
with domain of discontinuity
\Omega
. Then the quotient
\bar{M}=({{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}\cup\Omega)/\Gamma
is a 3-manifold with boundary. If
\bar{M}
is compact then
M
is a
conformally compact
hyperbolic 3-manifold. The boundary
\Sigma=\Omega/\Gamma
is a projective structure and is the
projective boundary
of
M
. While conformally compact is the usual terminology for Einstein manifolds, for hyperbolic 3-manifolds one usually refers to
\bar{M}
as
convex co-compact
because, as we will see shortly, there are convex
\Gamma
-invariant subsets of
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
with compact
\Gamma
-quotient. We will define a correspondence between convex, compact submanifolds of
M
and conformal metrics on
\Sigma
whose projective shape operator has eigenvalues in the interval
(-1,1]
.

We begin with the classical classification of Kleinian groups by their conformal boundary. We’ll state this theorem in a somewhat unusual way that will be convenient for our purposes.

Theorem 8.1

Let
\bar{X}
be a smooth, compact, 3-manifold with boundary with

the interior of
\bar{X}
. Assume that
\bar{X}
is homeomorphic to a convex co-compact hyperbolic 3-manifold and let
\hat{g}
be a smooth Riemannian metric on
\partial\bar{X}
. Then there exists a convex, co-compact hyperbolic 3-manifold
\bar{M}_{\hat{g}}
and a diffeomorphism
\phi_{\hat{g}}\colon\bar{X}\to\bar{M}_{\hat{g}}
such that the restriction of
\phi
to
\partial\bar{X}
is a conformal map from the conformal class of
\hat{g}
to
\partial_{c}M
.

Furthermore if
\hat{g}_{0}
is another Riemannian metric on
\partial\bar{X}
and there is a diffeomorphism of
\bar{X}
to itself such the restriction to
\partial\bar{X}
is a conformal map from the conformal class of
\hat{g}
to the conformal class of
\hat{g}_{0}
then there is an isometry from
\bar{M}_{\hat{g}}
to
\bar{M}_{\hat{g}_{0}}
whose restriction to the boundary is the given conformal map.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}(\partial\bar{X})
be the space of smooth Riemannian metrics on
\partial\bar{X}
. Then for
\hat{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}(\partial\bar{X})
we can use the diffeomorphism
\phi_{\hat{g}}\colon\bar{X}\to\bar{M}_{\hat{g}}
to pull back the projective structure on

to a projective structure

on
\partial\bar{X}
. Let
{\hat{B}}_{\hat{g}}
be the projective shape operator and
{\mathcal{G}}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})\subset{\mathcal{G}}(\partial\bar{X})
the subspace of metrics where

has eigenvalues in the interval

.

The pair

has a dual pair

by the naturality of the constructions. In fact the same argument gives a local isometry

.

The hyperbolic manifold

A convex co-compact hyperbolic 3-manifold is Fuchsian if its limit set is a round circle.

Lemma 8.2

Given

the dual immersion

is an embedding and the image bounds a convex manifold homeomorphic to

or

is Fuchsian and

is 2-to-1 with image a totally geodesic surface. In this last case

Proof:

We will show that the map

is an embedding unless

is the hyperbolic metric.
The restriction of

9
W-volume

As in the previous section we fix compact, hyperbolizable 3-manifold
\bar{X}
. We first define the
W-volume
on
{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
. We will then extend it to
{\cal G}(\partial\bar{X})
. Recall that associated to
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
we have a convex, hyperbolic 3-manifold
N_{\hat{g}}
that is homeomorphic
\bar{X}

•

M_{\hat{g}}
is a convex co-compact hyperbolic structure on
X
with
\hat{g}
a conformal metric on
\partial_{c}M_{\hat{g}}
;

•

{\hat{B}}_{\hat{g}}
is the projective shape operator for the metric
\hat{g}
and the projective boundary of
M_{\hat{g}}
;

•

(g,B_{g})
is the pair dual to
(\hat{g},{\hat{B}}_{\hat{g}})
;

•

N_{\hat{g}}
is a smooth convex submanifold of
M_{\hat{g}}
;

•

the induced metric on
\partial N_{\hat{g}}
is
g
and the shape operator is
B_{g}
.

The mean curvature
H_{g}
of
\partial N_{\hat{g}}
is one half the trace of
B_{g}
and is a function on
\partial N_{\hat{g}}=\partial\bar{X}
. We define the boundary term

{\cal B}(\hat{g})=\int_{\partial\bar{X}}H_{g}dA_{g}

where
dA_{g}
is the area form for
g
. Then the W-volume is the function
W\colon{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})\to\mathbb{R}
given by

W(\hat{g})=\operatorname{vol}(N_{\hat{g}})-\frac{1}{2}{\cal B}(\hat{g}).

The space of metric
{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
is an open subspace of the space of symmetric 2-tensors on
\partial X
with the
C^{\infty}
-topology. We are interested in calculating the variation of W-volume in this topology. More explicitly if
\hat{g}_{t}
is a smooth family of metrics in
{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
then we want to find

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}W(\hat{g}_{t})

The smooth family
\hat{g}_{t}
determines smooth families
B_{\hat{g}_{t}}
,
g_{t}
,
B_{g_{t}}
, etc. Furthermore if
\delta\hat{g}
is the time zero derivative of
\hat{g}_{t}
then
\delta\hat{g}
determines the time zero derivatives
\delta\hat{B}
,
\delta g
,
\delta B
, etc. for the associated objects.

We begin with the generalized Schäfli formula for the change in volume of
N_{\hat{g}}
due to Rivin-Schlenker. They give a formula for the variation of the volume of a compact hyperbolic
n
-manifold, or more generally, a compact Einstein manifold. We will only state it in the setting we will use and include a proof due to Souam (
[
Sou
]
) for completeness. While we only need the statement for smooth manifolds with boundary we state it for manifolds with corners as this will come up in the proof. For a manifold with corners at each point
x
in the co-dimension two face
E
there is
exterior dihedral angle

\theta_{E}(x)
.

Theorem 9.1
(Rivin-Schlenker,
[
RS
]
and Souam,
[
Sou
]
)

Let
M
be a compact hyperbolic 3-manifold with corners and
\delta g
a variation of the hyperbolic metric. Then

\delta V=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{E}\int_{E}\delta\theta_{E}dE+\int_{\partial M}\langle g,\delta B\rangle_{g}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta g,B\rangle_{g}

Proof:
We can decompose
M
into finitely many manifolds with corners each of which embeds in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. If we prove the formula for each piece then the terms on the faces that are paired will cancel. The corners will come in two types: some will come from corners of the original manifold while others will be new. In the latter case, after the corners in the pieces are glued the will become a smooth part of the interior, in which cases the total angle will be
0
, or a smooth part of the boundary, in which case the total angle will be
\pi
. In both cases, this implies the total change in dihedral angle is zero and and they will not contribute to the last term of the formula.

We now assume that
M
is a manifold with corners that embeds in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. It will be useful to think of
M
as both a fixed smooth manifold with corners and as a subspace of
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
. As
M
embeds in
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
there is a smooth vector field
\xi
, defined on a neighborhood of
M
, with flow
\phi_{t}
,
g_{t}=\phi_{t}^{*}g_{{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}}
and
\delta g
is the time zero derivative of
g_{t}
.
We also assume that
\xi
is non-zero on
M
. If it isn’t, by compactness, the norm of
\xi
is bounded on
\partial M
so we can add an infinitesimal isometry to
\xi
that is larger than this bound. Then the sum will be non-zero.

The family of metrics
g_{t}
on
M
also determines a family of shape operators
B_{t}
and second fundamental forms
{\rm II}_{t}
on
\partial M
. When there is a subscript we view the object as being on the fixed manifold
M
.

Fix
u\in T_{x}M
and define a parameterized surface
\sigma\colon(-\epsilon,\epsilon)^{2}\to{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
such that:

\sigma(0,0)=x\qquad\quad\sigma(s,t)=\phi_{t}(\sigma(s,0))\qquad\quad\sigma_{*}(0,0)\frac{\partial}{\partial s}=u.

This surface will be singular if
u
and
\xi
are parallel. We define the vector field
n
along
\sigma
such that
n(s,t)
is the normal vector to
\phi_{t}(\partial M)
at
\sigma(s,t)
. If we fix
t
the covariant derivative of
n
along the path
s\mapsto\sigma(s,t)
defines another vector field along
\sigma
which we label
\hat{B}u
as we have

\hat{B}u(0,t)=(\phi_{t})_{*}(B_{t}u).

We can also evaluate the curvature tensor in terms of covariant derivatives along
\sigma
:

\displaystyle R(\xi,u)n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{D}{dt}\left(\frac{Dn}{ds}\right)-\frac{D}{ds}\left(\frac{Dn}{dt}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi}\hat{B}u-\nabla_{u}\left(\nabla_{\xi}n\right).

Note that while the parameterized surface may be singular, the vector field
\hat{B}u
is a well defined vector field along the integral curve of
\xi
through
x
and
\nabla_{\xi}
is a smooth vector field on
\partial M
.

We let
{\langle},{\rangle}
be the usual inner product on
{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}
.
Since we are in constant curvature
=-1
we have

R(\xi,u)n={\langle}n,\xi{\rangle}u-{\langle}n,u{\rangle}\xi={\langle}n,\xi{\rangle}u.

Combining this equation with the one above gives

\nabla_{\xi}\hat{B}u={\langle}n,\xi{\rangle}u+\nabla_{u}\nabla_{\xi}n.

Given any vector
v\in T_{x}M
we define
\tilde{v}
to be the parallel vector field on
(-\epsilon,\epsilon)^{2}
such that
\sigma_{*}(0,0)\tilde{v}=v
. On the integral curve of
\xi
through
x
this determines a vector field which we also label
\tilde{v}
. We then have

g_{t}(u,v)={\langle}\tilde{u}(\phi_{t}(x)),\tilde{v}(\phi_{t}(x)){\rangle}

and

\delta g(u,v)=\xi{\langle}\tilde{u},\tilde{v}{\rangle}={\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\tilde{u},v{\rangle}+{\langle}u,\nabla_{\xi}\tilde{v}{\rangle}.

As
{\rm II}_{t}(u,v)={\langle}\hat{B}u,\tilde{v}{\rangle}
at
\phi_{t}(x)
then

\delta{\rm II}(u,v)={\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\hat{B}u,v{\rangle}+{\langle}Bu,\nabla_{\xi}\tilde{v}{\rangle}.

On the other hand

\delta{\rm II}(u,v)=\delta(g(Bu,v))=\delta g(Bu,v)+{\langle}\delta Bu,v{\rangle}.

Combining we get

\displaystyle{\langle}\delta Bu,v{\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta{\rm II}(u,v)-\delta g(Bu,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\hat{B}u,v{\rangle}+{\langle}Bu,\nabla_{\xi}\tilde{v}{\rangle}-{\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\widetilde{Bu},v{\rangle}-{\langle}Bu,\nabla_{\xi}\tilde{v}{\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\hat{B}u,v{\rangle}-{\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\widetilde{Bu},v{\rangle}.

Substituting for
\nabla_{\xi}\hat{B}u
we get

{\langle}n,\xi{\rangle}{\langle}u,v{\rangle}=-{\langle}\nabla_{\tilde{u}}\nabla_{\xi}n,v{\rangle}+{\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\widetilde{Bu},v{\rangle}+{\langle}\delta Bu,v{\rangle}.

This expression gives a relationship between four bilinear maps in
u
and
v
. We will take the trace of each of them. For the term on the left the trace is
2{\langle}n,\xi{\rangle}
. The trace of
{\langle}\nabla_{u}\left(\nabla_{\xi}n\right),v{\rangle}
is the divergence of
\nabla_{\xi}n
with respect to the induced metric on
\partial M
. For the middle term on the right we assume that
u
is an eigenvector of
B
with eigenvalue of
\lambda
and noting that
\widetilde{Bu}=\lambda\tilde{u}
we get

\delta g(Bu,u)={\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\widetilde{Bu},u{\rangle}+{\langle}Bu,\nabla_{\xi}\tilde{u}{\rangle}=2\lambda{\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\tilde{u},u{\rangle}=2{\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\widetilde{Bu},u{\rangle}

and therefore the trace of
\frac{1}{2}\delta g(Bu,v)
is equal to the trace of
{\langle}\nabla_{\xi}\widetilde{Bu},v{\rangle}
.
In our notation from section
3.1
this gives

{\langle}\xi,n{\rangle}dA=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{div}_{\partial M}\left(\nabla_{\xi}n\right)dA+{\langle}g,\delta B{\rangle}_{g}+\frac{1}{2}{\langle}\delta g,B{\rangle}_{g}

By the divergence theorem we have

\delta V=\int_{\partial M}\langle\xi,n\rangle dA

where
n
is the outward normal on
\partial M
and
dA
is the area form. We note that the divergence theorem holds for manifolds with corners.
Thus

\delta V=\int_{\partial M}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{div}_{\partial M}\left(\nabla_{\xi}n\right)dA+{\langle}g,\delta B{\rangle}_{g}+\frac{1}{2}{\langle}\delta g,B{\rangle}_{g}\right).

Finally we consider the divergence term
\operatorname{div}_{\partial M}\left(\nabla_{\xi}n\right)dA
.
We note that as
\langle n,n\rangle=1
then differentiating we have
\langle\nabla_{\xi}n,n\rangle=0.
Thus
\nabla_{\xi}n
is a tangent vector on
\partial M
and smooth except on the edges
E
. Each edge
E
belongs to exactly two faces
F^{\pm}_{E}
whose normal
n^{\pm}_{E}
extends to
E
. Further we define the conormal
v_{E}^{\pm}
to
E
to be the normal to
E
in the face
F^{\pm}_{E}
. Then the divergence theorem, applied to each face, gives

\int_{\partial M}\operatorname{div}_{\partial M}(\nabla_{\xi}n)dA=\sum_{E}\int_{E}\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{+}_{E},v^{+}_{E}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{-}_{E},v^{-}_{E}\rangle{d\ell}.

As

\langle n^{+}_{E},n^{-}_{E}\rangle=\cos\theta_{E}
, differentiating, we get

\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{+}_{E},n^{-}_{E}\rangle+\langle n^{+}_{E},\nabla_{\xi}n^{-}_{E}\rangle=-\sin\theta_{E}\delta\theta_{E}.

Also as
n^{+}_{E}
and
v^{+}_{E}
are an orthonormal basis for the 2-plane orthogonal to
E
and
\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{+}_{E},n^{+}_{E}\rangle=0
we have

\nabla_{\xi}n^{+}_{E}-{\langle}\nabla_{\xi}n^{+}_{E},v^{+}_{E}{\rangle}v^{+}_{E}

is tangent to
E
. Therefore the inner produce with
n^{-}_{E}
is zero giving

\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{+}_{E},n^{-}_{E}\rangle=\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{+}_{E},v^{+}_{E}\rangle\langle v^{+}_{E},n^{-}_{E}\rangle.

As
\langle v^{+}_{E},n^{-}_{E}\rangle=\sin(\theta_{E})
(and similarly
\langle v^{-}_{E},n^{+}_{E}\rangle=\sin(\theta_{E})
) we have

-\sin(\theta_{E})\delta\theta_{E}=(\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{+}_{E},v^{+}_{E}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{-}_{E},v^{-}_{E}\rangle)\sin(\theta_{E}).

Canceling, we obtain the equation

\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{+}_{E},v^{+}_{E}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{\xi}n^{-}_{E},v^{-}_{E}\rangle=-\delta\theta_{E}.

The result follows.
\Box

Next we state Krasnov and Schlenker’s variation formula for W-volume in terms of the interior data.

Theorem 9.2
(Krasnov-Schlenker,
[
KS1
]
)

Let
M
be a compact hyperbolic 3-manifold with boundary and
\delta g
a variation of the hyperbolic metric. If
\delta W
is the induced variation of the W-volume then

\delta W=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial M}\langle g,\delta B\rangle_{g}+\langle\delta g,B_{0}\rangle_{g}

where
(g,B)
is the dual pair,
B_{0}
is the traceless part of
B
and
\delta g
and
\delta B
are the induced variations.

Proof:

Let
\eta
be the tangent bundle isomorphism such that
\delta g=2g\cdot\eta
. Then

\delta(dA_{g})=\operatorname{Tr}(\eta)dA_{g}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{g}(\delta g)dA_{g}=\langle\delta g,{\operatorname{Id}}\rangle_{g}.

Applying the formula for
\delta V
from Theorem
9.1
we have

\displaystyle\delta W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta V-\delta{\cal B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\langle g,\delta B\rangle_{g}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta g,B\rangle_{g}-\frac{1}{2}\int\delta HdA_{g}+H\delta(dA_{g})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\langle g,\delta B\rangle_{g}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta g,B\rangle_{g}-\frac{1}{2}\int\langle g,\delta B\rangle_{g}+H\langle\delta g,{\operatorname{Id}}\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\langle g,\delta B\rangle_{g}+\langle\delta g,B-H{\operatorname{Id}}\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\langle g,\delta B\rangle_{g}+\langle\delta g,B_{0}\rangle_{g}.

\Box

Krasnov-Schlenker also gave a variational formula for W-volume in terms of data at infinity. We show that this formula follows directly from Lemma
3.5
comparing the variation of a pair of metrics.

Theorem 9.3
(Krasnov-Schlenker,
[
KS1
]
)

Given
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
and
\delta\hat{g}
a variation of
\hat{g}
. If
\delta W
is the induced variation of the W-volume then

\delta W=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}\langle\hat{g},\delta\hat{B}\rangle_{\hat{g}}+\langle\delta\hat{g},\hat{B}_{0}\rangle_{\hat{g}}.

where
\hat{B}_{0}
is the traceless part of the shape operator at infinity
\hat{B}
.

Proof:

We have
\hat{g}=P^{*}g
and
g=\hat{P}^{*}\hat{g}
where

P={\operatorname{Id}}+B\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\hat{P}=\frac{1}{2}({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}).

Further as
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
then
\det({\operatorname{Id}}+B)>0
.
Then by Lemma
3.5

\langle g,\delta P\rangle_{g}+\langle\delta g,P_{0}\rangle_{g}=-\langle\hat{g},\delta\hat{P}\rangle_{\hat{g}}-\langle\delta\hat{g},\hat{P}_{0}\rangle_{\hat{g}}.

As

P_{0}=B_{0}\qquad\delta P=\delta B,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad P_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{B}_{0}\qquad\delta\hat{P}=\frac{1}{2}\delta\hat{B},

then by linearity

\langle g,\delta B\rangle_{g}+\langle\delta g,B_{0}\rangle_{g}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\hat{g},\delta\hat{B}\rangle_{\hat{g}}+\langle\delta\hat{g},\hat{B}_{0}\rangle_{\hat{g}}\right).

\Box

We finally give the variational formula in terms of the Osgood-Stowe differential.

Theorem 9.4

The variational formula for W-volume on
\mathcal{G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
at a metric
\hat{g}
is given by

\delta W=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}\delta KdA_{\hat{g}}-2\operatorname{Re}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}\langle Q(\hat{g}),\mu(\delta\hat{g})\rangle_{\hat{g}}.

Proof:

By
[
KS1
]
(see also Theorem
9.3
above)

\delta W=-\frac{1}{4}\int\langle\hat{g},\delta\hat{B}\rangle_{\hat{g}}+\langle\delta\hat{g},\hat{B}_{0}\rangle_{\hat{g}}.

By the projective Gauss equation we have
\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{B})=-2K(\hat{g})
which gives

\langle\hat{g},\delta\hat{B}\rangle_{\hat{g}}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\delta\hat{B})dA_{\hat{g}}=-\delta K(\hat{g})dA_{\hat{g}}.

By Theorem
5.17
we have

\hat{{\rm II}}_{0}=2{\operatorname{\bf{OS}}}(\Sigma,\hat{g})=2Q(\Sigma,\hat{g})+2\overline{Q(\Sigma,\hat{g})}.

We have
\delta\hat{g}=2\hat{g}\cdot\eta
where the strain
\eta_{0}
is

\eta_{0}=\nu(\delta\hat{g}){\partial_{z}}\otimes d{\overline{z}}+\overline{\nu(\delta\hat{g})}{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\otimes dz

and
\mu(\delta\hat{g})=\nu(\delta\hat{g}){\partial_{z}}\otimes d{\overline{z}}
.
Thus as
\hat{{\rm II}}_{0}=\hat{g}\cdot\hat{B}_{0}
we have by Lemma
3.4

\langle\delta\hat{g},\hat{B}_{0}\rangle_{\hat{g}}=\langle 2\hat{g}\cdot\eta,\hat{B}_{0}\rangle_{\hat{g}}=\langle\hat{g}\cdot\hat{B}_{0},2\eta\rangle_{\hat{g}}=\langle\hat{{\rm II}}_{0},2\eta_{0}\rangle_{\hat{g}}=8\operatorname{Re}\langle Q(\Sigma,\hat{g}),\mu(\delta\hat{g})\rangle_{\hat{g}}.

Substituting, the variational formula follows.
\Box

9.1
Conformal variations

If we fix the conformal class of the metric
\hat{g}
then we are fixing the convex co-compact hyperbolic 3-manifold
M_{\hat{g}}
so we are studying the W-volume of convex submanifolds of
M_{\hat{g}}
. We note that the second term in Theorem
9.4
vanishes. This will allow us to make explicit computations which will enable to us to give a general definition of W-volume for any smooth metric on
\partial\bar{X}
.

We now prove Theorem
2.8
but only for metrics in
{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
. Once we have done this we will be able to define W-volume for general metrics.

Proposition 9.5

Let
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
and
u
a smooth function on
\partial\bar{X}
such that
\hat{g}_{t}=e^{2tu}\hat{g}
is in
{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
for
t\in[0,1]
. Then

W(\hat{g}_{1})-W(\hat{g}_{0})=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}u\left(\Omega_{\hat{g}_{0}}+\Omega_{\hat{g}_{1}}\right).

Proof:

Let
K_{t}=K(\hat{g}_{t})
be the Gaussian curvature of
\hat{g}_{t}
and
dA_{t}
be the area forms. For this proof we need more than the time zero derivative so we let
\dot{K}_{t}
,
\dot{dA}_{t}
, etc. represent time
t
derivatives. By Gauss-Bonnet

\int K_{t}dA_{t}=2\pi\chi(\partial M)

so

\int\dot{K}_{t}dA_{t}=-\int K_{t}\dot{dA}_{t}.

As
dA_{t}=e^{2tu}dA
we also have
\dot{dA_{t}}\ =2udA_{t}
.
As the family
\hat{g}_{t}
fixes the conformal structure there is only the curvature term in the formula from Theorem
9.4
and we have

\dot{W}_{t}=-\frac{1}{4}\int K_{t}\dot{dA}_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\int uK_{t}dA_{t}.

The formula for curvature under conformal change (see
[
Bes
, Theorem 1.159]
) gives

K_{t}=e^{-2tu}(K(\hat{g})-t\Delta u),

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian of
\hat{g}
. Therefore

\dot{W}_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\int u(K(\hat{g})-t\Delta u)dA_{\hat{g}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int uK(\hat{g})dA_{\hat{g}}+\frac{1}{2}t\int u\Delta udA_{\hat{g}}

Integrating we have

W(\hat{g}_{1})=W(\hat{g})-\frac{1}{2}\int u\cdot K(\hat{g})dA_{g}+\frac{1}{4}\int u\Delta(u)dA_{g}.

Then

K(h)=e^{-2u}(K(g)-\Delta u)
giving

W(h)=W(g)-\frac{1}{4}\int u\cdot K(g)dA_{g}-\frac{1}{4}\int uK(h)dA_{h}

Therefore

W(h)-W(g)=-\frac{1}{4}(\Omega_{g}+\Omega_{h})(u).

\Box

We then get Krasnov-Schlenker’s scaling formula for metrics in
{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
.

Corollary 9.6
(Krasnov-Schlenker,
[
KS1
]
)

If
g\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
and
s\geq 0
then

W\left(e^{2s}\hat{g}\right)=W(\hat{g})-s\pi\chi(\partial\bar{X}).

Proof:
When
s\geq 0
the metrics
e^{2t\cdot s}\hat{g}
are in
{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
for
t\in[0,1]
so we can apply Proposition
9.5
with
u\equiv s
. The result then follows by observing that

\int_{\partial\bar{X}}s\left(\Omega_{\hat{g}_{0}}+\Omega_{\hat{g}_{1}}\right)=2s(2\pi\chi(\partial\bar{X}))

by Gauss-Bonnet.
\Box

We can now define W-volume for an arbitrary
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}(\partial\bar{X})
as follows. By compactness the absolute value of eigenvalues of
\hat{B}_{\hat{g}}
are bounded by some constant
\Lambda>1
. Then for and
s>\frac{1}{2}\log\Lambda
we have that the projective shape operator of the metric
e^{2s}\hat{g}
has eigenvalues in the interval
(-1,1)
by Proposition
5.10
. We then set

W(\hat{g})=W(e^{2s}\hat{g})+s\pi\chi(\partial\bar{X}).

and observe that
W(\hat{g})
doesn’t depend on the choice of
s
by Corollary
9.6
.

We now observe that the results on W-volume on
{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
extend to
{\cal G}(\partial\bar{X})
. As already noted the scaling property is due to Krasnov-Schlenker (see
[
KS1
]
).

Theorem 9.7

The following hold for
W
on
{\cal G}(\partial\bar{X})
.

•

If
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}(\partial\bar{X})
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
then

W\left(e^{2s}\hat{g}\right)=W(\hat{g})-s\pi\chi(\partial\bar{X}).\qquad\mbox{\bf[Krasnov-Schlenker, \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{KS08}{}{}]}]}

•

The variational formula for W-volume on
{\cal G}(\partial\bar{X})
at a metric
\hat{g}
is given by

\delta W=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}\delta KdA_{\hat{g}}-2\operatorname{Re}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}\langle Q(\hat{g}),\mu(\delta\hat{g})\rangle_{\hat{g}}.

•

Let
\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1}\in{\cal G}(\partial\bar{X})
and
u
a smooth function on
\partial\bar{X}
such that
\hat{g}_{1}=e^{2u}\hat{g}_{0}
. Then

W(\hat{g}_{1})-W(\hat{g}_{0})=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}u\left(\Omega_{\hat{g}_{0}}+\Omega_{\hat{g}_{1}}\right).

Proof:

The first item follows trivially from the definition.

For the variational formula we let
\hat{g}_{t}
be a variation with
\hat{g}_{0}=\hat{g}
and
\delta W,\delta K,\delta\hat{g}
be the derivatives at
t=0
. We fix
s
such that
e^{2s}\hat{g}_{t}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
for
t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
. Then by definition

W(\hat{g}_{t})=W(e^{2s}\hat{g}_{t})-s\pi\chi(\partial\bar{X}).

It follows that

\delta W=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}\delta K(e^{2s}\hat{g}_{t})dA_{e^{2s}\hat{g}}-2\operatorname{Re}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}\langle Q(e^{2s}\hat{g}),\mu(\delta(e^{2s}\hat{g}))\rangle_{e^{2s}\hat{g}}.

We have
K(e^{2s}\hat{g}_{t})=e^{-2s}K(\hat{g}_{t})
giving
\delta K(e^{2s}\hat{g}_{t})=e^{-2s}\delta K
. Also
dA_{e^{2s}\hat{g}}=e^{2s}dA_{\hat{g}}
.
Further
Q(e^{2s}\hat{g})=Q(\hat{g})
, and
\mu(\delta(e^{2s}\hat{g}))=\mu(\delta\hat{g})
. Finally
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\hat{g}}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{e^{2s}\hat{g}}
as the pairing only depends on the conformal class of the metric. Thus

\delta W=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}\delta KdA_{\hat{g}}-2\operatorname{Re}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}\langle Q(\hat{g}),\mu(\delta\hat{g})\rangle_{\hat{g}}.

For the last item, let
\hat{g}_{t}=e^{2tu}\hat{g}_{0}
. By compactness of
\partial\bar{X}
we can choose a
T
such that
e^{2T}\hat{g}_{t}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Also the curvature form in invariant under scaling the metric giving
\Omega_{e^{2T}\hat{g}_{i}}=\Omega_{\hat{g}_{i}}
. Thus

W(\hat{g}_{1})-W(\hat{g}_{0})=W(e^{2T}\hat{g}_{1})-W(e^{2T}\hat{g}_{0})=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\partial\bar{X}}u\left(\Omega_{\hat{g}_{0}}+\Omega_{\hat{g}_{1}}\right).

\Box

We can now prove our generalization of the Krasnov-Schlenker maximality property.

Theorem
2.9

Let
\hat{g}
be a constant curvature metric on
\partial\bar{X}
and
let
\hat{h}=e^{2u}\hat{g}
be a smooth metric on
\partial\bar{X}
with the same area as
\hat{g}
. Then

W(\hat{g})-W\left(\hat{h}\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}

where
\nabla
is the gradient of
\hat{g}
.

Proof:

Assume
\hat{g}
has constant curvature
K<0
.
If
\Delta
is the Laplacian of
\hat{g}
, then
K(\hat{h})=e^{-2u}(K-\Delta u)
. Then by Theorem
2.8

W(\hat{g})-W\left(\hat{h}\right)=\frac{1}{4}\int u(\Omega_{\hat{g}}+\Omega_{\hat{h}})=-\frac{1}{4}\int u(\Delta u-2K)dA_{\hat{g}}.

Then by the divergence theorem applied to
u\nabla u
we have Green’s identity

0=\int\nabla\cdot(u\nabla u)dA_{\hat{g}}=\int u\Delta udA_{\hat{g}}+\int\nabla u\cdot\nabla u\ dA_{\hat{g}}=\int u\Delta udA_{\hat{g}}+\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus as
t\leq e^{t}-1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
K
is non-positive,

W(\hat{g})-W(\hat{h})-\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{K}{4}\int 2udA_{\hat{g}}\geq\frac{K}{4}\int(e^{2u}-1)dA_{\hat{g}}=\frac{K}{4}\int dA_{\hat{h}}-dA_{\hat{g}}=0

as
\hat{g},\hat{h}
have the same area.

\Box

9.2
W-volume for projective structures

We now define a relative version of W-volume and show that it naturally extends to a pair of conformal metrics on an arbitrary projective structure. In earlier work (see
[
KS2
]
) Krasnov-Schlenker define this for two conformal metrics on a projective structure where one is the projective metric.

We will see that much of the theory for W-volume for convex co-compact
3
-manifolds easily extends to this W-volume for pairs. We will also show that we can use this W-volume for pairs to obtain a surprising bound on the
L^{2}
-norm of a projective structure in terms of the length of the associated bending measured lamination.

Let
\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1}\in{\cal G}(\partial\bar{X})
be in the same conformal class and define

W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})=W(\hat{g}_{1})-W(\hat{g}_{0}).

Of course this definition makes sense for any pair of metrics in
{\cal G}(\partial\bar{X})
. However, when the metrics are in the same conformal class
N_{\hat{g}_{0}}
and
N_{\hat{g}_{1}}
are both submanifolds of the same convex co-compact hyperbolic 3-manifold. In fact if
N_{\hat{g}_{0}}\subset N_{\hat{g}_{1}}
then

W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})=\operatorname{vol}(N_{\hat{g}_{1}}\smallsetminus N_{\hat{g}_{0}})-\frac{1}{2}\left({\cal B}(\hat{g}_{1})-{\cal B}(\hat{g}_{0})\right).

With this as motivation we now define a relative W-volume for pairs of conformal metrics on a projective structure. Let
\Sigma
be a projective structure and let
{\cal G}(\Sigma)
be smooth conformal metrics on
\Sigma
and
{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)
the subspace of metrics such that the projective shape operator has eigenvalues in the interval
(-1,1]
.
For
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)
by Proposition
7.1
the flow space
{\cal F}_{(g,B)}([0,\infty))
embeds in
{\operatorname{ext}}^{\rm o}(\Sigma)
with compact complement. We define
N_{\hat{g}}={\operatorname{ext}}^{\rm o}(\Sigma)\backslash{\cal F}_{(g,B)}((0,\infty))
. Then for
\hat{g},\hat{h}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)
we define

W(\hat{g},\hat{h})=\operatorname{vol}(N_{\hat{h}})-\operatorname{vol}(N_{\hat{g}})-\frac{1}{2}({\cal B}(\hat{h})-{\cal B}(\hat{g})).

We would like to extend this definition to arbitrary pairs of conformal metrics. Our strategy will be the same as when we were considering convex hyperbolic 3-manifolds. In particular, the variational formulas will continue to hold with the only change being that in the definition of
W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})
the boundary
{\cal B}(\hat{g}_{0})
is positive so in the variational formulas the term coming from the variation of
\hat{g}_{0}
will have opposite sign. It follows that given
\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)
and
t\geq s\geq 0
we have

W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})=W(e^{2t}\hat{g}_{0},e^{2s}\hat{g}_{1})-(t-s)\pi\chi(\Sigma).

Then for two arbitrary smooth metrics
\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1}\in{\cal G}(\Sigma)
we can choose
s
and
t
such that
e^{2t}\hat{g}_{0},e^{2s}\hat{g}_{1}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)
and define

W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})=W(e^{2t}\hat{g}_{0},e^{2s}\hat{g}_{1})-(t-s)\pi\chi(\Sigma).

As before the choice of
s
and
t
aren’t unique but the definition of
W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})
is independent of this choice.

The following properties of W-volume for pairs of metrics in
{\cal G}(\Sigma)
follows easily from Theorem
9.7
.

Theorem 9.8

The following hold for
W
on
{\cal G}(\Sigma)
.

•

If
\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1}\in{\cal G}(\Sigma)
and
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
then

W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})=W(e^{2t}\hat{g}_{0},e^{2s}\hat{g}_{1})-(t-s)\pi\chi(\Sigma).

•

The variational formula for W-volume at
(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})\in{\cal G}(\Sigma)\times{\cal G}(\Sigma)
is

\delta W=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Sigma}\delta K(\hat{g}_{1})dA_{\hat{g}_{1}}-\delta K(\hat{g}_{0})dA_{\hat{g}_{0}}

•

Let
\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1}\in{\cal G}(\Sigma)
and
u
a smooth function such that
\hat{g}_{1}=e^{2u}\hat{g}_{0}
. Then

W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Sigma}u\left(\Omega_{\hat{g}_{0}}+\Omega_{\hat{g}_{1}}\right).

In particular if
\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1}
are non-positively curved with
\hat{g}_{1}\leq\hat{g}_{0}
then

W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})\leq 0.

•

If
\hat{g}_{0}
is constant curvature and
\hat{g}_{1}=e^{2u}\hat{g}_{0}
has the same area as
\hat{g}_{0}
then

W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})\leq-\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}

where
\nabla
is the gradient for
\hat{g}_{0}
.

Proposition 9.9

If
\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)
with
\hat{g}_{0}\leq\hat{g}_{1}
then

W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})\geq\frac{1}{2}\left({\cal B}(\hat{g}_{0})-{\cal B}(\hat{g}_{1})\right).

Proof:

Since
\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)
and
\hat{g}_{0}\leq\hat{g}_{1}
then by Proposition
7.3

N_{\hat{g}_{0}}\subseteq N_{\hat{g}_{1}}
. Therefore

\displaystyle W(\hat{g}_{0},\hat{g}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}\left(N_{\hat{g}_{1}})-\operatorname{vol}(N_{\hat{g}_{0}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left({\cal B}\left(\hat{g}_{1}\right)-{\cal B}\left(\hat{g}_{0}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left({\cal B}\left(\hat{g}_{0}\right)-{\cal B}\left(\hat{g}_{1}\right)\right)

\Box

9.3
W(\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma})

To prove Theorem
2.10
, we will obtain upper and lower bounds on
W(\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma})
. As the projective metric
\hat{g}_{\Sigma}
is not smooth, we first need to extend
W
. We define
{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\Sigma)=\overline{{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)}\subseteq{\cal G}^{0}(\Sigma)
where the closure is with respect to the sup-norm topology on the space of continuous conformal metrics
{\cal G}^{0}(\Sigma)
. In the appendix we will show that
W
extends continuously to
{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\Sigma)\times{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\Sigma)
and that
\hat{g}_{\Sigma}\in{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\Sigma)
. This will suffice for our purposes.

To get an upper bound we will rescale the metrics so that they have the same area and the use the maximality property for metrics of constant curvature. For the lower bound we scale the hyperbolic metric so that its Epstein surface is convex and then we’ll apply Proposition
9.9
. For the first estimate we need a formula for the area of the projective metric
\hat{g}_{\Sigma}
. For the second we will need formulas for the areas of the scaled metrics
(\hat{g}_{h})_{t}
and
(\hat{g}_{\Sigma})_{t}
and their Epstein surfaces. We begin with hyperbolic metric:

Lemma 9.10

Let
\hat{g}_{h}
be the hyperbolic metric on
\Sigma
and
(g_{h})_{t}
the dual metrics to
e^{2t}\hat{g}_{h}
.
Then for
t>\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+2\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{\infty}\right)
we have

{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((g_{h})_{t})=-2\pi\chi(\Sigma)\cosh^{2}(t)-e^{-2t}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|^{2}_{2}.

Proof:

If
d{\hat{A}}_{t}
is the area form for
(\hat{g}_{h})_{t}
and
dA_{t}
is the area form for
g_{t}
then by Proposition
5.10
we have

dA_{t}=\frac{1}{4}\left|\det\left({\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}_{t}\right)\right|d\hat{A}_{t}=\frac{1}{4}\left|\det\left({\operatorname{Id}}+e^{-2t}\hat{B}_{0}\right)\right|e^{2t}d\hat{A}_{0}.

The determinant of
{\operatorname{Id}}+\hat{B}_{t}
is non-negative when the eigenvalues of
\hat{B}_{t}
are
\geq-1
and by Proposition
7.4
this is exactly when
e^{2t}\geq 1+2\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{\infty}
. Therefore when
t>\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+2\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{\infty}\right)
we can drop the absolute value signs and integrate to get

{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((g_{h})_{t})=\int dA_{t}=\frac{1}{4}\int\det\left({\operatorname{Id}}+e^{-2t}\hat{B}_{0}\right)e^{2t}d\hat{A}_{0}.

By Proposition
4.6
,
\hat{B}
has eigenvalues
1\pm 2\|\phi_{\Sigma}(z)\|
. Thus

\displaystyle\det\left({\operatorname{Id}}+e^{-2t}\hat{B}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+e^{-2t}(1+2\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|)\right)\left(1+e^{-2t}(1-2\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+e^{-2t})^{2}-4\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|^{2}e^{-4t}.

As
{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g}_{h})=-2\pi\chi(\Sigma)
we have

\displaystyle{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((g_{h})_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(e^{t}+e^{-t})^{2}(-2\pi\chi(\Sigma)\right)-e^{-2t}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\pi\chi(\Sigma)\cosh^{2}(t)-e^{-2t}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|^{2}_{2}.

\Box

For the projective metric
\hat{g}_{\Sigma}
we have the following;

Lemma 9.11

Let
\Sigma
be a projective structure with Thurston parameterization
\left(Y_{\Sigma},\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)
. Then

•

{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g}_{\Sigma})=-2\pi\chi(\Sigma)+L\left(\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)

•

{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((g_{\Sigma})_{t})=-2\pi\cosh^{2}(t)\chi(\Sigma)+L\left(\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)\sinh(t)\cosh(t).

•

{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((g_{\Sigma})_{t})-{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((g_{h})_{t})=L\left(\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)\sinh(t)\cosh(t)+e^{-2t}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|^{2}_{2}.

•

{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((\hat{g}_{\Sigma})_{t})-{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((\hat{g}_{h})_{t})=e^{2t}L\left(\lambda_{\Sigma}\right).

Proof:
In the Thurston parameterization of projective structures
\Sigma
is given by a pair
\left(Y_{\Sigma},\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)
(see
[
KT
]
for a complete description of Thurston’s parametrization). We can assume that the support of
\lambda_{\Sigma}
is a single curve
\gamma
with weight
\theta
so that
L(\lambda_{\Sigma})=\theta\ell_{Y_{\Sigma}}(\gamma)
. The Epstein surface at time
t
is the union of a Euclidean cylinder of circumference
\ell_{Y_{\Sigma}}(\gamma)\cosh(t)
and width
\theta\sinh(t)
with the complement a surface of constant negative curvature equal to
\tanh^{2}(t)-1
. The area of the cylinder is
\theta\ell_{Y_{\Sigma}}(\gamma)\sinh(t)\cosh(t)=L(\lambda_{\Sigma})\sinh(t)\cosh(t)
. By the Gauss-Bonnet formula the area of the remainder of the surface is
-2\pi\cosh^{2}(t)\chi(\Sigma)
. This gives the formula for
{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((g_{\Sigma})_{t})
.

A similar computation gives the
{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g}_{\Sigma})
. In this metric the annulus has circumference
\ell_{Y_{\Sigma}}(\gamma)
and width
\theta
and the complementary surface is hyperbolic. The reasoning of the previous paragraph implies the formula for
{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g}_{\Sigma})
.

Laminations supported on single curve are dense in the space of all measure laminations and the lengths and areas will vary continuously. This gives the general case. The formulas for the difference of two areas then follows easily from the prior lemma.
\Box

We now give an elementary bound for
W(\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma})
in terms of the length of the bending lamination.

Lemma 9.12

W(\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma})\leq\frac{1}{4}L\left(\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)

Proof:

By Lemma
9.11

{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}\left(\hat{g}_{\Sigma}\right)=2\pi|\chi(\Sigma)|+L(\lambda_{\Sigma}).

The maximality property of Theorem
9.8
gives that if
\hat{g}
is smooth of the same area as
e^{2t}\hat{g}_{h}
then

W(e^{2t}\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g})\leq 0.

By Proposition
A.4
, W-volume is continuous on
{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\Sigma)\times{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\Sigma)
. Further by Proposition
A.5
we have
\hat{g}_{\Sigma}\in{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\Sigma)
giving,

W\left(\frac{2\pi|\chi(\Sigma)|+L(\lambda_{\Sigma})}{2\pi|\chi(\Sigma)|}\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma}\right)\leq 0.

Combining this with the scaling property we get

W(\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma})-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{2\pi|\chi(\Sigma)|+L(\lambda_{\Sigma})}{2\pi|\chi(\Sigma)|}\right)\pi|\chi(\Sigma)|\leq 0.

As
\log(1+x)\leq x
we have

W(\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma})\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{L(\lambda_{\Sigma})}{2\pi|\chi(\Sigma)|}\right)\pi|\chi(\Sigma)|\leq\frac{1}{4}L(\lambda_{\Sigma}).

\Box

Next we get a lower bound. The key here is that volumes are non-negative.

Lemma 9.13

W(\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma})\geq\frac{e^{-2T}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2}-\frac{L\left(\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)\cosh(2T)}{4}

where
e^{2T}=1+2\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{\infty}
.

Proof:

By Proposition
7.4
the eigenvalues for the shape operator for the Epstein surface of
(\hat{g}_{h})_{T}
are non-negative so
(\hat{g}_{h})_{T}
is the metric at infinity for a locally convex Epstein surface. For all
t\geq 0
,
(\hat{g}_{\Sigma})_{t}
is the metric at infinity for a locally convex Epstein surface. Since
\hat{g}_{h}\leq\hat{g}_{\Sigma}
therefore
e^{2T}\hat{g}_{h}\leq e^{2T}\hat{g}_{\Sigma}
and by Proposition
9.9

\displaystyle W(\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W\left((\hat{g}_{h})_{T},(\hat{g}_{\Sigma})_{T}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left({\cal B}\left((g_{h})_{T}\right)-{\cal B}\left((g_{\Sigma})_{T}\right)\right).

Applying Lemma
9.11
we have

\displaystyle{\cal B}\left((g_{h})_{T}\right)-{\cal B}\left((g_{\Sigma})_{T}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left({\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((\hat{g}_{h})_{T})-{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((\hat{g}_{\Sigma})_{T})\right)-\left({\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((g_{h})_{T})-{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}((g_{\Sigma})_{T})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}L(\lambda_{\Sigma})e^{2T}+L(\lambda_{\Sigma})\sinh(T)\cosh(T)+e^{-2T}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L(\lambda_{\Sigma})(2\sinh(T)\cosh(T)-e^{2T})+e^{-2T}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-2T}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}L(\lambda_{\Sigma})\cosh(2T).

Therefore it follows that

\displaystyle W(\hat{g}_{h},\hat{g}_{\Sigma})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{-2T}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{L(\lambda_{\Sigma})}{2}\cosh(2T)\right).

\Box

We now prove Theorem
2.10
.

Theorem
2.10

Let
\Sigma
be a projective structure with Thurston parameterization
\left(Y_{\Sigma},\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)
. Then

\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\|_{2}\leq(1+\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\|_{\infty})\sqrt{L(\lambda_{\Sigma})}.

Proof:
Combining Lemmas
9.12
and
9.13
we get

\frac{e^{-2T}\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2}-\frac{L\left(\lambda_{\Sigma}\right)\cosh(2T)}{4}\leq\frac{1}{4}L(\lambda_{\Sigma})

giving

\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}L(\lambda_{\Sigma})e^{2T}(1+\cosh(2T))=\frac{1}{4}L(\lambda_{\Sigma})\left(e^{2T}+1\right)^{2}.

Applying
e^{2T}=1+2\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{\infty}
we obtain

\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\leq(1+\left\|\Phi_{\Sigma}\right\|_{\infty})\sqrt{L(\lambda_{\Sigma})}.

\Box

A.1
Hyperbolic manifolds

As we noted above, a convex submanifold
N

M
determines a conformal metric
\hat{g}
on
\partial\bar{M}
. We would like to define the W-volume for
\hat{g}
(and
N
) even when
\hat{g}
is not smooth. While the volume term (the volume of
N
) is still well defined, the integral of mean curvature is not.

For these non-smooth convex manifolds it will be convenient to fix the ambient convex co-compact hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
. In Lemma
8.3
we saw that convex submanifolds of
M
determine conformal metrics on
\partial_{c}M
. We let
{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\partial\bar{X};M)
be the space of such metrics. This will contain the space of smooth metrics denoted
{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X};M)
. As all metrics are conformally equivalent, given
\hat{g}
and
\hat{h}
in
{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\partial\bar{X};M)
, we have
\hat{h}=e^{2u}\hat{g}
for some continuous function
u
. We then define the sup-norm by
\|\hat{g}-\hat{h}\|_{\infty}=\|u\|_{\infty}
. We also have
\hat{h}\geq\hat{g}
if
u\geq 0
and
\hat{h}\leq\hat{g}
if
u\leq 0
.

When
\partial N
is not smooth the integral of mean curvature is not defined but the area of
\partial N
is. The following proposition will then be useful for defining W-volume for non-smooth metrics.

Proposition A.1
(
[
BBB
, Lemma 3.1]
)

Let
\hat{g}
be a smooth conformal metric on a projective structure
\Sigma
and assume that the eigenvalues of the shape operator
B
for Epstein surface are non-negative. If
g
is the Epstein metric then

\int_{\Sigma}HdA=\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g})-{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(g)-\pi\chi(\Sigma)

where
H=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}B
is the mean curvature.

With this proposition as a motivation we can define

W(\hat{g})=\operatorname{vol}(N_{\hat{g}})-\frac{1}{4}{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g})+\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(g)+\frac{\pi}{2}\chi(\partial\bar{X})

for
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
. When
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
this agrees with our original definition. For this definition to be useful we need it to be continuous. We prove this next.

Proposition A.2

Let
M
be a convex co-compact hyperbolic structure on
X
. Then the W-volume is continuous on
{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\partial\bar{X};M)
with the sup-norm topology.

Proof:
Let
\hat{g}
be a metric in
{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\partial\bar{X};M)
and
N=N_{\hat{g}}
the corresponding convex submanifold of
M_{\hat{g}}
. Then for
t\geq 0
, we have
\hat{g}_{t}\in{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
as
N_{t}=N_{\hat{g}_{t}}
is the
t
-neighborhood of
N
. We also have that
{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g}_{t})
,
{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(g_{t})
(where
g_{t}
is the induced metric on
\partial N_{t}
) and
\operatorname{vol}(N_{t})
are continuous in
t
. Therefore, for
\epsilon>0
we can choose a
\delta>0
such that
\delta\leq\epsilon
,
|{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g})-{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g}_{2\delta})|<\epsilon
,
|{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(g)-{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(g_{2\delta})|<\epsilon
and
|\operatorname{vol}(N)-\operatorname{vol}(N_{2\delta})|<\epsilon
.

Now assume that
\hat{h}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
with
\hat{g}<\hat{h}<\hat{g}_{2\delta}
. Then
{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g})\leq{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{h})\leq{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g}_{2\delta})
. We also have that
N\subset N_{\hat{h}}\subset N_{2\delta}
which implies that
\operatorname{vol}(N)\leq V(N_{\hat{h}})\leq\operatorname{vol}(N_{2\delta})
and, as the nearest point projections of
\partial N_{\hat{h}}
to
\partial N
and
\partial N_{\hat{g}_{2\delta}}
to
\partial N_{\hat{h}}
are area decreasing,
{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(g)\leq{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(h)\leq{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(g_{2\delta})
. It then follows that

\left|{\cal B}(\hat{g})-{\cal B}(\hat{h})\right|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\epsilon=\frac{3\epsilon}{2}

and therefore

\left|W(\hat{g})-W(\hat{h})\right|\leq\epsilon+\frac{3\epsilon}{4}=\frac{7\epsilon}{4}.

Next we assume that
\hat{h}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X})
with
\|\hat{g}-\hat{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta
. Let
\hat{h}_{\delta}=e^{2\delta}\hat{h}
and we have
\hat{g}\leq\hat{h}_{\delta}\leq\hat{g}_{2\delta}
. Then, by the above,

\left|W(\hat{g})-W(\hat{h}_{\delta})\right|\leq\frac{7\epsilon}{4}.

Since
W(\hat{h}_{\delta})=W(\hat{h})+\delta\pi|\chi(\partial\bar{X})|
and
\delta<\epsilon
this implies that

\left|W(\hat{g})-W(\hat{h})\right|\leq\frac{7\epsilon}{4}+\epsilon\pi|\chi(\partial\bar{X})|=\left(\frac{7}{4}+\pi|\chi(\partial\bar{X})|\right)\epsilon.

Continuity follows.
\Box

To extend our results for smooth metrics to continuous metrics we further need to know that smooth metrics are dense in
{\cal G}_{c}^{0}(\partial\bar{X};M)
.

Proposition A.3

{\cal G}_{c}(\partial\bar{X};M)
is dense in
{\cal G}^{0}_{c}(\partial\bar{X};M)
.

Proof:
Fix
\hat{g}\in{\cal G}_{c}^{0}(\partial\bar{X};M)
and
let
\rho\colon M\to[0,\infty)
be the distance from
N_{\hat{g}}\subset M
. This function is convex along geodesics and is nowhere-constant on geodesics in
M\smallsetminus N_{\hat{g}}
. Therefore
\rho^{2}
is strictly convex on
M\smallsetminus N_{\hat{g}}
and by Theorem 2 in
[
GW
]
we have that
\rho^{2}
can be approximated by smooth, strictly convex functions. In particular, for all
\epsilon>0
we can find a smooth convex function
f\colon M\smallsetminus N\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\|f-\rho^{2}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon^{2}/4
. Note that
N_{e^{2\epsilon}\hat{g}}
is the
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
N_{\hat{g}}
and we have that
f\geq 3\epsilon^{2}/4
on
\partial N_{e^{2\epsilon}\hat{g}}
and
f\leq\epsilon^{2}/4
on
\partial N_{\hat{g}}
. Therefore
S=f^{-1}\left(\epsilon^{2}/2\right)
will be a smooth convex surface in
N_{e^{2\epsilon}\hat{g}}\smallsetminus N_{\hat{g}}
and it will separate
\partial N_{\hat{g}}
from
\partial N_{e^{2\epsilon}\hat{g}}
. This implies that
S
bounds a smooth, convex submanifold
N^{\prime}
in
M
. Finally we note that if
\hat{h}
is the metric for
N^{\prime}
then
\hat{g}<\hat{h}<e^{2\epsilon}\hat{g}
. To see this (after lifting to the universal cover) any horoball
{\mathfrak{h}}
that is tangent to
N^{\prime}
will be disjoint from
N_{\hat{g}}
and intersect will
N_{e^{2\epsilon}\hat{g}}
. Therefore
{\mathfrak{h}}
will be nested between the horoballs, based at the same point as
{\mathfrak{h}}
, tangent to
N_{\hat{g}}
and tangent to
N_{e^{2\epsilon}\hat{g}}
. This gives the estimate and that
\|\hat{g}-\hat{h}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
. As
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary and
\hat{h}
is smooth the proposition follows.
\Box

A.2
Projective structures

As above, we will now extend
W
on
{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)\times{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)
to general locally convex metrics. By Proposition
A.1
we have for
\hat{g},\hat{h}\in{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)

W(\hat{g},\hat{h})=\operatorname{vol}(N_{\hat{h}})-\operatorname{vol}(N_{\hat{g}})+\frac{1}{4}{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{g})-\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(g)-\frac{1}{4}{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(\hat{h})+\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{{\bf area}}}(h).

We define
{\cal G}_{c}^{0}(\Sigma)
to be the closure of
{\cal G}_{c}(\Sigma)
in the sup-norm topology on
\mathcal{{\cal G}}^{0}(\Sigma)
. Then repeating the proof of Proposition
A.2
we have

Proposition A.4

Let
\Sigma
be a projective structure on a closed surface
S
. Then
W
extends continuously to
{\cal G}_{c}^{0}(\Sigma)\times{\cal G}_{c}^{0}(\Sigma)
in the sup-norm topology.

Finally we show that
\hat{g}_{\Sigma}\in{\cal G}_{c}^{0}(\Sigma)
.

Proposition A.5

If
\Sigma
is a projective structure then
\hat{g}_{\Sigma}\in{\cal G}_{c}^{0}(\Sigma)
.

Proof:

The proof follows that of Proposition
A.3
using Greene-Wu, except now we do our analysis on
{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
. Let
\rho\colon{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)\to[0,\infty)
be the distance to
\Sigma\times\{0\}\subseteq{\operatorname{ext}}(\Sigma)
. This function is convex along geodesics and is nowhere-constant on geodesics in
\Sigma\times(0,\infty)
. Therefore
\rho^{2}
is strictly convex on
\Sigma\times(0,\infty)
and by Theorem 2 in
[
GW
]
we have that
\rho^{2}
can be approximated by smooth, strictly convex functions. We choose a smooth convex function
\Sigma\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\|f-\rho^{2}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon^{2}/4
. As in Proposition
A.3
the surface
S=f^{-1}\left(\epsilon^{2}/2\right)
will be a smooth convex surface approximating
\Sigma\times\{0\}
in the
\epsilon
neighborhood of
\Sigma\times\{0\}
. Specifically, the metric
g
on
S
will have dual metric
\hat{g}
with
\|\hat{g}-\hat{g}_{\Sigma}\|<\epsilon
.
\Box
