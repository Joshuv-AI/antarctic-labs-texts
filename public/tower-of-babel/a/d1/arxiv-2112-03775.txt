The quantum UV-IR map for line defects in 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 3 ) \mathfrak{gl}(3) -type class S S theories
Andrew Neitzke, and Fei Yan

Abstract
We consider the quantum UV-IR map for line defects in class S S theories of 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 3 ) \mathfrak{gl}(3) -type. This map computes the protected spin character which counts framed BPS states with spin for the bulk-defect system. We give a geometric method of computing this map motivated by the physics of five-dimensional 𝒩 = 2 {\mathcal{N}}=2 supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory, and compute it explicitly in various examples. As a spin-off we propose a new way of computing a certain specialization of the HOMFLY polynomial for links in ℝ 3 {{\mathbb{R}}}^{3} , as a sum over BPS webs attached to the link.

The quantum UV-IR map and protected spin characters

It was argued in

2

1.5
Our main results and outlook

As we have described, the quantum UV-IR map
unifies the computation of protected spin characters for line defects
in class
S
theories and the 1-parameter limit (
6
) of the HOMFLY polynomials for links in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
.
Thus it is desirable to understand this map better, and in particular
to have a concrete way of computing it.

In
Neitzke:2020jik
we set out a general scheme for computing the
quantum UV-IR map. This scheme is motivated by the physics of 5d
{\mathcal{N}}=2

U(N)
super
Yang-Mills theory; concretely it is a geometric recipe mapping a link
L\subset M
to a linear combination of links
\widetilde{L}\subset\widetilde{M}
.
The links
\widetilde{L}
consist partly of
lifts of segments of
L
and partly of extra contributions from
BPS webs

attached to
L
. We also described in
Neitzke:2020jik
the detailed
implementation of this scheme in the case of
N=2
. In particular, we determined
the precise
q
-dependent prefactors which weigh the contributions from each
\widetilde{L}

to
F(L)
, and gave sketch proofs that our scheme indeed produces a map
F

with all the expected properties.

In this paper we continue the construction of the quantum UV-IR map
to the case
N=3
. This case is considerably
more complicated than
N=2
, because the BPS webs which occur can have more complicated topologies. For
N=2
, each BPS web consists only of a single segment;
for
N=3
they can be general trivalent graphs, with or without loops.
Part of the problem of describing the UV-IR map is to
determine the
q
-dependent factors
accompanying BPS webs with all of these different topologies.
In
subsection 2.4
we give detailed formulas for the
factors associated to webs without loops, as well as for webs containing
a single loop, and all of the other
q
-dependent factors entering
the map.

These formulas are already enough to compute

in various examples,
and we obtain results which pass all tests we have available; some of
these are described in
section 4
.
In particular, the examples in
subsection 4.3
-
subsection 4.5

illustrate how the quantum UV-IR map leads to a new recipe for computing
the specialized HOMFLY polynomial (
6
), and
in
subsection 4.6

S

theories of type
\fgl(3)

Most of our computations were done with the aid of computer programs,

With the arXiv version of this preprint

2
The quantum UV-IR map

As described in
section 1
, the computation of framed BPS spectrum for line defects in class
S
theories and the computation of HOMFLY polynomials are unified through the quantum UV-IR map between the following two skein algebras:

F:\Sk(M,\fgl(N))\to\Sk(\widetilde{M},\fgl(1)).

(7)

We recall the definitions of these two skein algebras in
subsection 2.1
, motivated from considerations of line defects OPE. The map
F
depends on certain foliation data on the surface
C
; this is described in
subsection 2.3
.

Our approach to construct the map
F
is a combination of physical considerations and bootstrap-like methods. We outline the strategy in
subsection 2.2
, and then give a concrete description of
F
in
subsection 2.4
.

2.1
Line defect OPE and skein algebras

The set of line defects in a 4d
{\mathcal{N}}=2
theory is equipped with a natural algebra structure given by the line defect OPE, defined by considering the theory with the insertion of two line defects. Concretely we consider two
1/2
-BPS line defects
\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}
, extending along the time direction while inserted at two points
x_{1}
and

in the spatial
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
, preserving a common half of the
supercharges in the bulk 4d
{\mathcal{N}}=2

is
Q

Q
is one of the common preserved supercharges. Up to
Q
-exact terms the limit
x_{1}\to x_{2}
is nonsingular, and by locality this limit should be equivalent to another line defect
Gaiotto:2010be
, which we denote as
\mathbb{L}_{1}\cdot\mathbb{L}_{2}
. In this way the line defect OPE defines a ring multiplication on the space of line defects.

2.5
Bootstrapping the weight factors
\alpha(\widetilde{L})

In this section we briefly describe the bootstrap-like method which we used to determine the weight factors
\alpha(\widetilde{L})
, which are important ingredients in the construction of quantum UV-IR map described in
subsection 2.4
. We used two properties of the quantum UV-IR map
F
:

•

Given a framed oriented link
L
in
M
,
F(L)
must only depend on the ambient isotopy class of
L
. This puts very strong constraints on
F
, as there are a large number of ambient isotopies which need to be respected by the map
F
.

•

F
must be a homomorphism from the UV skein algebra
\Sk(M,\fgl(N))
to the IR skein algebra
\Sk(\widetilde{M},\fgl(1))
: said otherwise, it must respect the UV skein relations,
up to the IR skein relations.

Our general strategy is to cook up isotopies which relates webs with
K
legs to webs with less than
K
legs; an example of such isotopies is illustrated in
subsection 2.5
. This kind of isotopies allow us to solve for the weight factors in a recursive way.

Figure 24:
Here we demonstrate an example of an isotopy used to obtain the weight factors associated with webs. We show link segments (in blue) contained in a small contractible region inside

Concretely, we proceed as follows. We tune the link configuration to single out a
K
-legged web lying in a small contractible region. For example, in
subsection 2.5
we show on the left a 5-legged web attached to four link segments. Then we perform an isotopy which deforms part of a link segment near the point where it is attached to the web, stretching it past an internal vertex of the web. After making such an isotopy, the original
K

. As

should be the same before and after the isotopy, we obtain an equality relating the local factor for a
K
-legged web to those for webs with less than
K
legs. This enables us to determine the local web factors in an inductive way.

2.6
Comments on possible derivations of the weight factors

As described earlier, we obtained various local contributions to the weight factors
\alpha(\widetilde{L})
using bootstrap-like methods, by exploiting the homomorphism property of
F
and isotopy invariance. It would be very desirable to give a physical derivation for these factors, perhaps in the context of twisted
\Omega
-deformed 5d
{\mathcal{N}}=2
super Yang-Mills theory on
M\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}_{\epsilon}
with insertion of a fundamental Wilson line along
L\subset M
, as described in
subsection 2.2
. We leave this to future work.

We can also uplift the setup back to 6d, where the quantum UV-IR map is interpreted as the correspondence mapping a surface defect in the strongly-interacting 6d
(2,0)

\fgl(N)
theory to combinations of surface defects in the abelian
(2,0)
theory at a point of the tensor branch. In this context, the WKB webs stretched between link segments correspond to dynamical BPS strings in the 6d theory. It would be very interesting to derive the weight factors
\alpha(\widetilde{L})
from the 6d point of view, taking into account contributions from the 6d abelian theory as well as contributions from the worldvolume theory of the BPS strings. We leave this derivation to future work.

As one comment, we notice that the weight factor (
13
) associated with a tree web is proportional to
(q-q^{-1})^{P-Q}
, where
P
is the total number of legs in the tree web (including the internal legs) and
Q
is the total number of trivalent vertices. The simplest example of a tree web, namely an exchange, has
P=1,Q=0
and contributes a weight factor proportional to
(q-q^{-1})
. As already pointed out in
Gaiotto:2011nm
(see also
Galakhov:2016cji
), this factor can be interpreted as counting the ground states on the BPS string stretched between two strands of the link. Concretely this string has two states which differ by 1 in fermion number and angular momentum.
16
16

16

Recall that our definition (
3
) for protected spin character contains
q^{2J}
instead of
q^{J}
Similarly we can also hope to interpret
(q-q^{-1})^{P-Q}
as counting the ground states for the BPS string web stretched between link strands. For example, it might be possible to consider an effective quantum mechanical model, which assigns two Majorana fermions to each leg of the string web. Each internal vertex represents a certain interaction lifting off some of the degenerate states. It would be interesting to pursue this direction.

2.7
Comments on isotopy invariance

In the case of
N=2
, we gave a sketch proof in
Neitzke:2020jik
that the
F
we constructed indeed has the expected isotopy invariance
and homomorphism properties, by
checking that it behaves correctly under a set of Reidemeister-type moves. In so doing we were aided by the fact that we only had to consider
one foliation, and everything was (in an appropriate sense) local in the two-dimensional leaf space of that foliation.
For
N\geq 3
we do not have
this kind of locality available in general, and so it seems more difficult to find an appropriate finite set of Reidemeister-type moves. Thus we have not been able to
prove the full isotopy invariance or the homomorphism property for the
F

we construct; however, they must be true if our physical picture is
correct. It would be very desirable to give a proof.

Lacking a full proof, we have conducted various experimental checks, mostly with computer assistance (see
Appendix A
for some
discussion of the algorithms).
For instance, we generated random polygonal knots with

n\leq 8
vertices
in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
and verified that in all cases
our recipe produces the expected polynomial invariants.
We also studied various examples of knots on surfaces, again obtaining the expected
results in all cases.
A few of these checks are described in more detail
in
section 4
below.

\lambda^{3}+\phi_{1}\lambda^{2}+\phi_{2}\lambda+\phi_{3}=0,

where

Now suppose we take

Then the

\lambda_{1}\approx\sqrt{\phi_{2}}+\frac{\phi_{3}}{2\phi_{2}},\qquad\lambda_{2}\approx-\frac{\phi_{3}}{\phi_{2}},\qquad\lambda_{3}\approx-\sqrt{\phi_{2}}+\frac{\phi_{3}}{2\phi_{2}}.

(16)

Thus we have (writing
\lambda_{ij}=\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}
)

\lambda_{12}\approx\sqrt{\phi_{2}}+\frac{3\phi_{3}}{2\phi_{2}},\quad\lambda_{23}\approx\sqrt{\phi_{2}}-\frac{3\phi_{3}}{2\phi_{2}},\quad\lambda_{13}\approx 2\sqrt{\phi_{2}}.

(17)

In particular, all three of these have approximately the same phase (that of

), so that the
three local foliations collapse approximately to one foliation

.
If we take
\phi_{3}=0
, then they collapse exactly.

Next we consider the case where

Appendix A
Computer computations

In
section 4
we reported the results of various computations, most of which
were done with computer assistance. In this section we briefly describe the
algorithms involved.

A.1
For computations in
M={{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}

For the computations in
M={{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
reported in
section 4
,
we begin by fixing a polygonal
link
L\subset{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
, with vertices either chosen by hand or
randomly generated.
To compute
F(L)
, the main difficulty is to somehow identify all
possible webs which can attach to
L
.
For this purpose we proceed as follows. (We discuss here only the simple
case where there are no webs containing loops;
when there are loops, the problem becomes
more complicated.)

For any line segment
S
in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
, we let
P_{ij}(S)
denote a strip in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
,
with boundary
S
and foliated by half-leaves
of the foliation
F_{ij}
.
We begin by collecting the strips
P_{ij}(S)
for all possible
ij

and all segments
S
of the link
L
.
We then iteratively add new strips to the collection as follows.
We look at all the strips in the collection and compute their intersections.
If
P_{ij}(S)
intersects
P_{jk}(S^{\prime})
, then their intersection is a segment

S^{\prime\prime}
, and we add to our collection a new strip
P_{ik}(S^{\prime\prime})
.
We then check to see whether this new strip
intersects any of the old ones; if it does, each such intersection
may generate a new strip, and so on. We continue this iterative process until there
are no new intersections.

Once this process terminates, we look for intersections between
strips
P_{ij}(S)
and
P_{ji}(S^{\prime})
in our collection.
When such a “head-on” intersection exists,
the intersection segment is a leaf of
F_{ij}
.
If
S
and
S^{\prime}
are both segments of
L
, then such an intersection is
a leaf of
F_{ij}
with both ends on
L
, i.e. it is an exchange.
More generally,
S
and
S^{\prime}
may have been generated from intersections of other
strips. In this case the intersection segment is not an exchange but
rather a piece of a larger web. Our algorithm collects a list of
these intersection segments and afterward pieces them together into webs.
See
subsection A.1
.

Figure 51:

{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}

Each of the link segments (blue) emits six

Once the webs have been identified, there are no major conceptual difficulties
in enumerating all possible lifts, and calculating the factors associated with
each lift according to the rules of
subsection 2.4
. Having done so,
since
\widetilde{M}
is a
disjoint union of copies of
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
, each lift can be reduced
using the
GL(1)
skein relations to a multiple of the empty link. Practically speaking,
this reduction just requires us to compute the writhe of the link diagram with respect
to some projection, which is straightforward.

We implemented these algorithms in a Python program which we used to make the
various computations in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
reported above.
The code (unfortunately very lightly documented),
and a sample Jupyter notebook
sample-computations.ipynb
which can be
used to reproduce the results reported in
subsection 4.3
-
subsection 4.5
,
are included as auxiliary
files with the arXiv version of this preprint.

This algorithm heavily exploits the fact that
M={{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
and all of the
foliations are by straight lines. One could imagine a version of this
algorithm which would work in more general examples, by dividing
M
up into
domains, choosing coordinates in each domain for which the leaves
are straight lines, and introducing rules for patching
the domains together at their boundaries. This would be in the spirit of a
well-known maneuver in the study of compact Riemann surfaces carrying
holomorphic quadratic differentials: such a surface can always
be obtained by starting with
a polygon in
{\mathbb{C}}
carrying the quadratic differential
\varphi={\mathrm{d}}z^{2}
,
and then gluing the edges together via maps of the form
z\mapsto\pm z+c
.
