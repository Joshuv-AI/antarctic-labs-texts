Information projection approach to propensity score estimation for handling selection bias under missing at random
Hengfang Wang, Jae Kwang Kim

Abstract




Summary.

Propensity score weighting is widely used to improve the representativeness and correct the selection bias in the voluntary sample. The propensity score is often developed using a model for the sampling probability, which can be subject to model misspecification. In this paper, we consider an alternative approach of estimating the inverse of the propensity scores using the density ratio function satisfying the self-efficiency condition. The smoothed density ratio function is obtained by the solution to the information projection onto the space satisfying the moment conditions on the balancing scores.
By including the covariates for the outcome regression models only in the density ratio model, we can achieve efficient propensity score estimation. Penalized regression is used to identify important covariates.
We further extend the proposed approach to the multivariate missing case. Some limited simulation studies are presented to compare with the existing methods.

Keywords:
Calibration estimation;
Density ratio model; Self-efficiency;
Missing data

1
Introduction

In statistical analysis using sample data, the concept of representativeness is crucial. The fundamental issue is that the final sample might be subject to selection bias and might not accurately reflect the target population. A naive analysis that ignores selection bias can produce incorrect results. Another way to frame the selection bias problem is as a missing data problem.
Making valid statistical
inference with missing data is a fundamental problem in statistics
(
Little and
Rubin 2019
,
Kim and
Shao 2021
)
.

The propensity score (PS) weighting approach is widely used for adjusting the selection bias in the final sample. Frequently, the propensity score is calculated using the selection probability model. In principle, regression models for binary responses, such as logistic regression, can be used to estimate the selection probability in the presence of observed auxiliary data. The target parameter can then be estimated using an inverse probability weighting estimator to provide an unbiased estimate. However, correctly specifying the propensity score model can be tricky, and we frequently lack sufficient understanding of the selection mechanism. Furthermore, the final estimation can be unstable when some propensity scores are close to zero
(
Ma and Wang 2020
)
. The bias and variance of the resulting estimator are likely to be amplified by the nature of such an ‘inverse’ fashion.

The existing methods for propensity score estimation are either based on the maximum likelihood method

(
Rosenbaum and
Rubin 1983
,
Robins
et al. 1994
)

or calibration method
(
Folsom 1991
,
Tan 2010
,
Kott and Chang 2010
,
Graham
et al. 2012
,
Hainmueller 2012
,
Imai and
Ratkovic 2014
,
Chan
et al. 2016
,
Zhao 2019
)
. The calibration method can also be justified under an outcome model, which gives a doubly robust flavor. However, the objective function for calibration estimation is not fully agreed.

In this paper, we introduce the self-efficiency condition as a new paradigm for PS estimation. The self-efficiency condition means that the resulting PS estimator is equivalent to the prediction-based estimator using the outcome response model. Due to the equivalence, the self-efficient propensity score estimator is more efficient than existing approaches. To build a self-efficient PS estimation, we first model density ratios with information projection
(
Csiszár and
Shields 2004
)
, which minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence measure for models with moment constraints on the balance scores. Density ratio models are widely employed to address classification and two-sample problems
(
Qin 1998
,
Nguyen
et al. 2010
,
Chen and Liu 2013
)
. To our best knowledge, however, it has not been studied in the context of correcting selection bias. Once the density ratio model has been constructed using the information projection approach, the model parameters can be estimated using the empirical likelihood technique
(
Qin and
Lawless 1994
)
.

The self-efficient PS estimation is also closely related to weight smoothing. Information projection of the density ratio function combined with calibration estimation is used to accomplish weight smoothing. We give some asymptotic theories for the smoothed PS estimator utilizing the balance score function. The proposed estimator is doubly robust and locally efficient in the sense of
Robins
et al. 1994
. By applying the weight smoothing based on the covariate in the outcome model, we can achieve the efficiency gain.
In addition, a two-step estimation approach is designed to facilitate parameter estimation computation.
Variance estimation of the PS estimator can be implemented using either the linearization method or bootstrap.

Furthermore, the proposed paradigm includes discussing propensity score estimation in high-dimensional covariate scenarios. When there are more auxiliary variables than we require, the PS estimator can be inefficient. Only significant covariates for the outcome model should be included in the log density ratio model for effective PS estimation.
We consider penalized regression to identify important covariates. Given the observed study variable and the corresponding auxiliary variables, we can implement penalized regression methods to select important covariates and obtain an efficient PS estimator.

Finally, using the proposed framework, the PS estimation can be easily extended to handle multivariate missing data.
We can partition the sample into multiple groups based on missing patterns and apply the density ratio estimation method to obtain the inverse propensity scores. By constructing a self-efficient PS estimation, we can
combine information from multiple sources with different missing patterns.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, the basic setup is introduced. In Section 3, the proposed method is developed using the information projection technique. In Section 4,
its asymptotic properties are investigated. In Section 5, the proposed method is extended to handle high dimensional covariate cases. In Section 6, an extension to multivariate missing data setup is developed. Two limited simulation studies are presented in Section 7 to compare with other existing methods and to understand the effect of choice of calibration variables.
Concluding remarks are made in Section 8.

2
Basic Setup

Suppose that
the parameter of interest
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
can be written as the unique solution to
\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{X},Y)\}=\mathbf{0}
, where
Y
is the study variable that is subject to missingness,
\mathbf{X}
is the auxiliary variable that is always observed, and
\mathbf{U}
is a smooth function of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
with nonsingular gradient matrix
\mathbb{E}\{\partial_{\theta}\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
. Thus, the joint density of
(\mathbf{X},Y)
is completely unspecified except for the moment condition
\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{X},Y)\}=\mathbf{0}
.
We further assume that the second moments of
\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{X},Y)
exist.

Suppose we have an
N
independently and identically distributed realization of
(\mathbf{X},Y)
, denoted as
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i}):i=1,\ldots,N\}
. Since
Y
is subject to missingness, the actual dataset is
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\delta_{i}y_{i},\delta_{i}):i=1,\cdots,N\}
, where
\delta_{i}
is the sampling indicator variable defined as

\displaystyle\delta_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if\ }y_{i}\mbox{\ is observed,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

We assume that the sampling mechanism is
missing at random (MAR) in the sense of
Rubin 1976
. That is,

\displaystyle Y\perp\delta\mid\mathbf{X}.

(1)

Under MAR, we are interested in finding
the propensity weights
\omega(\bm{x}_{i})
such that the solution to

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\omega(\bm{x}_{i})U(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})=0

(2)

leads to consistent estimation of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Equation (
2
) is called the propensity score equation.
One popular choice for
\omega(\bm{x})
is

\omega(\bm{x})=\{\mathbb{P}(\delta=1\mid\bm{x})\}^{-1}.

(3)

There are two problems with the choice in (
3
). First, we do not know the true propensity score function
\mathbb{P}(\delta=1\mid\bm{x})
.
Second, the resulting estimator is not necessarily efficient. To resolve these problems, we may consider the following conditions:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\omega(\bm{x}_{i})U(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\delta_{i}U(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})+(1-\delta_{i})\hat{\mathbb{E}}\{U(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},Y)\mid\bm{x}_{i}\}\right],

(4)

where
\hat{\mathbb{E}}\{U(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},Y)\mid\bm{x}_{i}\}
is the best predictor of
U(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})
for
\delta_{i}=0
.
Condition (
4
) is attractive as the solution to the PS estimating equation in (
2
) is equivalent to the expected estimating equation, which leverages the best predictors of the unobserved parts of the population estimating equation. We call condition (
4
)
self-efficiency
condition, as we can obtain an efficient estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
by solving the expected estimating equation
(
Wang and Pepe 2000
)
. The term “self” is employed to emphasize that efficiency is achieved without including additional terms to the propensity score equation.

To compute the conditional expectation in (
4
), one could make an extra assumption on the outcome model
[y\mid\bm{x}]
and estimate the parameters in the model. To avoid the difficulty associated with the modeling for
[y\mid\bm{x}]
, we assume that

\mathbb{E}\{U(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x},Y)\mid\bm{x}\}\in\mbox{span}\{1,b_{1}(\bm{x}),\ldots,b_{L}(\bm{x})\}:=\mathcal{H}

(5)

for some
b_{1}(\bm{x}),\ldots,b_{L}(\bm{x})
. Once condition (
5
) is met, the additional modeling for
[y\mid\bm{x}]
can be omitted.
Under (
5
), by taking the conditional expectations on both terms in (
4
), we can check that the self-efficiency condition implies

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\omega(\bm{x}_{i})\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i}),

(6)

where
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=(1,b_{1}(\bm{x}),\cdots

,b_{L}(\bm{x}))
is a vector of basis functions in
\mathcal{H}
. Thus, the self-efficient propensity score method achieves the balancing property for
\bm{b}(\bm{x})
. The balancing property is also called calibration property in survey sampling
(
Deville and
Särndal 1992
,
Fuller 2009
,
Wu and
Thompson 2020
)
.

Another implication of the self-efficiency is related to weight smoothing.
Note that, under assumption (
5
), the self-efficiency condition in (
4
) implies that
\omega(\bm{x}_{i})
is a function of
b_{1}(\bm{x}),\cdots,b_{L}(\bm{x})
, which means that the final PS weights are functions of the covariates in the outcome regression model. By including the covariates in the outcome model only, we can achieve weight smoothing and efficient estimation.

Thus, under assumption (
5
), it makes sense to impose the self-efficiency for obtaining the propensity weights.
How to uniquely determine
\omega(\bm{x})
to satisfy the self-efficiency condition is our main research problem. We address this problem by introducing the density ratio function and information projection in the next section.

3
Proposed method

3.1
Density ratio function

To introduce the density ratio function, we use
f(\cdot)
to denote generic density functions.
Further, let
f_{j}(\bm{x})
denote the conditional density of
\mathbf{X}
given
\delta=j
for
j=0,1
. Using this notation, we define the following density ratio function:

\displaystyle{r}(\bm{x})=\frac{f_{0}(\bm{x})}{f_{1}(\bm{x})}.

By Bayes theorem, we obtain

\frac{\mathbb{P}(\delta=0\mid\bm{x})}{\mathbb{P}(\delta=1\mid\bm{x})}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\delta=0)}{\mathbb{P}(\delta=1)}\times r(\bm{x}).

Assuming
c=\mathbb{P}(\delta=0)/\mathbb{P}(\delta=1)
is known, we can express

\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\delta=1\mid\mathbf{X})}=1+c\cdot r(\mathbf{X})

(7)

and use

\omega_{i}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\delta_{i}=1\mid\bm{x}_{i})}=1+c\cdot r(\bm{x}_{i})

as the propensity score weight for unit
i
with
\delta_{i}=1
. So, our propensity score weighting problem reduces to density ratio function estimation problem. Note that we can easily estimate
c
by
\widehat{c}=N_{0}/N_{1}
,
N_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}
and
N_{0}=N-N_{1}
.

Let
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=(b_{1}(\bm{x}),\cdots

,b_{L}(\bm{x}))
be a vector of basis functions of
\mathcal{H}
. Condition (
5
) implies that, as far as estimation of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is concerned, the MAR condition holds conditional on
\bm{b}(\mathbf{X})
, that is,

Y\perp\delta\mid\bm{b}(\mathbf{X}).

(8)

According to
Rosenbaum and
Rubin 1983
,
\bm{b}(\bm{x})
in (
8
) is called the balancing score function.

Using
\bm{b}(\bm{x})
satisfying (
8
), we can construct

r^{\star}(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}\left\{r(\bm{x})\mid\bm{b}(\bm{x}),\delta=1\right\},

(9)

where
r(\bm{x})=f_{0}(\bm{x})/f_{1}(\bm{x})
is the true density ratio function.
To distinguish
{r}^{\star}(\bm{x})
from the original density ratio function
r(\bm{x})
, we may call
{r}^{\star}(\bm{x})
the smoothed density ratio function in the sense that it satisfies

\frac{1}{N_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}r^{\star}(\bm{x}_{i})[1,\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})]=\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\delta_{i}\right)[1,\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})].

(10)

Thus, the smoothed density ratio function
r^{\star}(\bm{x})
is regarded as a low-dimensional projection of
r(\bm{x})
onto the space satisfying (
10
).

We can use
{r}^{\star}(\bm{x})
in (
9
) to construct a smoothed PS estimating function of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
as follows:

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm SPS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\left\{1+(N_{0}/N_{1})r^{\star}(\bm{x}_{i})\right\}\mathbf{U}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},y_{i}\right).

(11)

Furthermore, since
r(\bm{x})
is a function of
\bm{x}
only, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left\{r(\bm{x})\mid\bm{b}(\bm{x}),y,\delta=1\right\}=\mathbb{E}\left\{r(\bm{x})\mid\bm{b}(\bm{x}),\delta=1\right\},

(12)

where
r(\bm{x})=f_{0}(\bm{x})/f_{1}(\bm{x})
is the true density ratio function.
Note that result (
12
) implies that

\mathbb{E}\{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm PS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\bm{b}(\bm{x}),y,\boldsymbol{\delta}\}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm SPS}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

where

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm PS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\left\{1+(N_{0}/N_{1}){r}(\bm{x}_{i})\right\}\mathbf{U}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},y_{i}\right).

Therefore, we can establish the following result.

Proposition 1

Under (
8
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm PS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}=\mathbb{E}\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm SPS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}

(13)

and

\mathbb{V}\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm PS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}\geq\mathbb{V}\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm SPS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}.

(14)

Proposition
1
implies that the solution to
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm SPS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{0}
is more efficient than the solution to
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm PS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{0}
. Such phenomenon has been recognized by
Little and
Vartivarian 2005
,
Beaumont 2008
,
Kim and
Skinner 2013
and
Park
et al. 2019
.
To find a model of the smoothed density ratio function
r^{\star}(\bm{x})
in (
9
), we introduce information projection in the following subsection.

3.2
Information projection

To motivate the proposed method, suppose that we have two probability distributions
\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{1}
, with
\mathbb{P}_{0}
absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{1}
. For simplicity, we also assume that
\mathbb{P}_{k}
are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure
\mu
, with density
f_{k}
with support
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, for
k=0,1
. The Kullback-Leibler divergence between
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is defined by

D(\mathbb{P}_{0}\parallel\mathbb{P}_{1})=\int\log\left(\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{0}}{d\mathbb{P}_{1}}\right)d\mathbb{P}_{0}=\int\log\left(\frac{f_{0}}{f_{1}}\right)f_{0}d\mu.

Finding the density ratio function can be formulated as an optimization problem using the KL divergence. Specifically, we wish to minimize

\displaystyle Q(f_{0})=\int\log\left(f_{0}/f_{1}\right)f_{0}d\mu

(15)

with respect to
f_{0}
satisfying some moment conditions of the variables that are available from the whole sample. Let
\bm{b}(\bm{x})
be the given balancing score obtained from assumption (
5
). Because
\bm{x}_{i}
are observed throughout the sample, we can estimate
\mathbb{E}\{\bm{b}(\mathbf{X})\}
from the sample.
Given
{\mathbb{E}}\{\bm{b}(\mathbf{X})\}
, to utilize the auxiliary information, we have

p\int\bm{b}(\bm{x})f_{1}(\bm{x})d\mu+(1-p)\int\bm{b}(\bm{x})f_{0}(\bm{x})d\mu=\mathbb{E}\{\bm{b}(\mathbf{X})\},

(16)

where
p=\mathbb{P}(\delta=1)
. Thus, we wish to find the minimizer of
Q(f_{0})
in (
15
) subject to the constraint in (
16
). The following lemma gives the form of the solution to the optimization problem.

Lemma 1

The minimizer of
Q(f_{0})
in (
15
) subject to the constraint in (
16
) is

{f}_{0}^{\star}(\bm{x})=f_{1}(\bm{x})\times\frac{\exp\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x})\}}{\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[\exp\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x})\}\right]},

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}
is chosen to satisfy (
16
).

The solution (
17
) is obtained using the information projection method in information geometry.
Roughly speaking, constraint in (
16
) is a moment condition on
f_{0}(\bm{x})
to satisfy (
9
). Thus, we can use Lagrange multiplier method to obtain the solution as an exponential tilting form.
Note that (
17
) is equivalent to assuming a model for the density ratio function. That is, our model is

\log\{r^{\star}(\bm{x};\boldsymbol{\lambda})\}=\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}),

(18)

where
\lambda_{0}
is the normalizing constant satisfying

\int r^{\star}(\bm{x};\boldsymbol{\lambda})f_{1}(\bm{x})d\mu=1.

By (
7
),
the log-linear density ratio model (
18
) is equivalent to the logistic regression model for the sample selection probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\delta=1\mid\bm{x})=\frac{1}{1+(1/p-1)\cdot\exp\left\{\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x})\right\}}.

(19)

Thus, our framework provides another justification of the logistic regression model for the sample selection probability that has been considered in
Folsom 1991
,
Kott 2006
, and
Tan 2020
, among others. But, the parameter estimation discussed in §3.3 is different from the existing methods. Also, the covariates in the parametric model in (
19
) is derived from the outcome model assumption in (
5
). Nonetheless, including other covariates in the calibration weighting can make the resulting PS estimator robust against model misspecification (
Han and
Wang 2013
,
Chan
et al. 2014
,
Chen and
Haziza 2017
,
Yang
et al. 2020
).

Using (
17
), we can express (
16
) as

\displaystyle p\int\bm{b}(\bm{x})\left[1+\frac{N_{0}}{N_{1}}\cdot\exp\left\{\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x})\right\}\right]f_{1}(\bm{x})d\mu=\mathbb{E}\{\bm{b}(\mathbf{X})\},

(20)

where
\lambda_{0}
satisfies

\int\exp\{\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x})\}f_{1}(\bm{x})d\mu=1.

(21)

The function
r^{\star}(\bm{x})\equiv\exp(\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}))
can be viewed as the projection of the true density ratio function onto the space satisfying (
16
).

3.3
Model parameter estimation

We now wish to estimate the parameters in the log-linear density ratio model in (
18
) and estimate
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Note that the parameter is defined through
\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{X},Y)\}=\mathbf{0}
, which can be expressed as

p\int{\mathbf{U}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x},y)\left[1+\frac{N_{0}}{N_{1}}\cdot\exp\left\{\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x})\right\}\right]f_{1}(\bm{x},y)d\mu=0

(22)

as an integral equation for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, where
\lambda_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}
satisfy the constraints (
20
) and (
21
).

To estimate the parameters, we use

{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}_{1}(x,y)=\frac{1}{{N_{1}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\delta_{i}}\mathbb{I}\{(\bm{x},y)={(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})}\}

to
find the minimizer of
D({\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{1}}\parallel{\mathbb{P}_{1}})
among
\mathbb{P}_{1}
satisfying the integral constraints (
20
), (
21
), and (
22
).
The problem can be formulated as an optimization problem using
the empirical likelihood (EL) method of
Qin and
Lawless 1994
. In the EL method, a fully nonparametric density
p_{i}
can be assumed to replace
f_{1}(\bm{x},y)
in the integral equations.
That is, we maximize

\ell(\bm{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\log(p_{i})

subject to

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}p_{i}=1,

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{N_{1}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\left\{1+\frac{N_{0}}{N_{1}}\cdot\exp\{{\lambda}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\}\right\}p_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i}),

(24)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}{\mathbf{U}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\left\{1+\frac{N_{0}}{N_{1}}\cdot\exp\{{\lambda}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\}\right\}{p}_{i}=\mathbf{0},

(25)

and

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\exp\{\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\}p_{i}=1.

(26)

The solution to the above optimization problem lead to
\widehat{p}_{i}=\widehat{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\theta})
and we obtain the profile empirical likelihood

\ell_{e}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\log\{\widehat{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}.

(27)

The maximizer of
\ell_{e}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
can be used as the final estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. However, such a joint optimization of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is computationally expensive.

To introduce an alternative computation,
note that the form of the smoothed propensity weights

\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}=\left\{1+\frac{N_{0}}{N_{1}}\cdot\exp\left(\widehat{\lambda}_{0}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)\right\}\widehat{p}_{i}

depends on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
only through
\widehat{p}_{i}
. Thus, we can first exclude (
25
) in the EL optimization to remove the dependency on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in
\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}
and then obtain
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
by solving

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})=\mathbf{0}

for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. This two-step procedure will greatly simplify the computation.

Note that maximizing
\ell(\bm{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\log(p_{i})
subject to (
23
) only gives
\widehat{p}_{i}=1/N_{1}
.
Imposing (
24
) and (
26
) to the unconstrained optimization does not play any role on the final solution because
\lambda_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}
are unknown. Thus, the solution to the optimization problem remains the same:

\widehat{p}_{i}=1/N_{1}

with
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\left\{1+(N_{0}/N_{1})\cdot\exp\{\widehat{\lambda}_{0}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\}\right\}\left[1,\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[1,\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\right],

(28)

which is a calibration equation for
[1,\bm{b}(\bm{x})]
.

Once the model parameters in (
18
) are estimated, we can compute the smoothed propensity score estimating function

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm SPS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},y_{i}),

(29)

where

\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}=1+(N_{0}/N_{1})\cdot\exp\{\widehat{\lambda}_{0}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\}

(30)

and
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}
are computed from (
28
).
The final estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is obtained by solving
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm SPS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{0}.

The proposed propensity score estimating function in (
29
) satisfies the self-efficiency. To see this, without loss of generality, assume that
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a scalar. Note that, for a fixed
\theta
, we can write

\widehat{U}_{\rm SPS}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}U_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[{m}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\delta_{i}\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}\left\{U_{i}-{m}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right\}\right]

(31)

where
U_{i}=U(\theta;\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})
and

{m}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{L}\beta_{j}b_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})

for any
\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{L}
.
Now, since
\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}=1+(N_{0}/N_{1})\cdot\exp\{\widehat{\lambda}_{0}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\}
, the smoothed PS estimator in (
31
) is algebraically equivalent to

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{\rm SPS}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\delta_{i}U_{i}+(1-\delta_{i})m_{i}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right\}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\exp\{\widehat{\lambda}_{0}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\}\left\{U_{i}-m_{i}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right\}.

(32)

The second term in the right hand side of (
32
) is zero at
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\exp\{\widehat{\lambda}_{0}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{b}(\bm{x}_{i})\}\left\{U_{i}-m_{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right\}=0.

Thus, we have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}U_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\delta_{i}U_{i}+(1-\delta_{i}){m}_{i}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right\}.

(33)

Therefore, the proposed estimator satisfies the self-efficiency condition in (
4
).

Equation (
33
) means that
the final inverse propensity weights
\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}
do not directly use the regression model for prediction, but it implements regression prediction indirectly. Thus, the smoothed PS estimator incorporates the outcome regression model through calibration equation in (
28
) and achieves the self-efficiency.
On the other hand, model calibration of
Wu and Sitter 2001
uses the outcome model directly in the calibration equation. Similar ideas have been considered in the context of nonparametric calibration estimation in survey sampling.
For examples,
Montanari and
Ranalli 2005
used a single-layer Neural Network model and
Breidt
et al. 2005
used penalized Spline model for nonparametric calibration estimation.

Remark 1

Assumption (
5
) states that the basis functions do not depend on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, which does not always hold.
If the basis functions in (
5
) depends on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, that is,

\mathbb{E}\{U(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x},Y)\mid\bm{x}\}\in\mbox{span}\{1,b_{1}(\bm{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}),\ldots,b_{L}(\bm{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\},

(34)

we can use the following iterative procedure.

1.

Given the current parameter estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}
, find
\hat{b}_{ki}^{(t)}=b_{k}(\bm{x}_{i};\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)})
, for
k=1,\cdots,L
, such that (
34
) holds for
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}
.

2.

Estimate the parameter in

\omega_{i}^{(t)}=1+(N_{0}/N_{1})\exp\left\{\phi_{0}+\phi_{1}\hat{b}_{1i}^{(t)}+\cdots+\phi_{L}\hat{b}_{Li}^{(t)}\right\}

(35)

by apply the calibration equation on
[1,b_{1i}^{(t)},\cdots,b_{Li}^{(t)}]
.

3.

Use

This is essentially an extension of the EM algorithm
(

et al. 1977
)
applied to regression model. Step (a)-(b) corresponds to the E-step and Step (c) corresponds to the M-step. In the E-step, instead of using a parametric model, we use the information projection to compute the conditional expectation under assumption (

).

(Remark 2)

Remark 3
(Remark 3)

5
Dimension Reduction

We now consider the case of
\theta=\mathbb{E}(Y)
.
In Section 4, we have seen that either
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\bm{x})
lies in the linear space
\mathcal{H}=\mbox{span}\{\bm{b}(\bm{x})\}
or
\bm{b}(\bm{x})
is the basis for logistic regression (
37
) can give the root-
n
-consistency of the proposed estimator. However, as pointed out in Remark 3, including other
\bm{x}
-variables outside the outcome model into

\mathcal{H}
may lead to efficiency loss.

To explain the idea further, we assume that
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}}
, where
\mathcal{M}
is an index set for a subset of
\bm{x}
.
The following lemma presents an interesting result.

Lemma 2

If MAR condition in (
1
) holds and the reduced model for
y
holds such that

f(y\mid\bm{x})=f(y\mid\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}})

(49)

for
\bm{x}=(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}},\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}^{c}})
, then we can obtain the reduced MAR given
\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}}
. That is,

Y\perp\delta\mid\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{M}}.

(50)

Note that (
50
) is a special case of the reduced MAR in (
8
) using
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}}
as the balancing score function. In the spirit of Remark 3, we can see that the smoothed PS estimator using
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}}
is more efficient than the PS estimator using
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}
.
Therefore, it is better to apply a model selection procedure to select the important variables
which satisfies (
49
).

To find
\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}}
satisfying (
49
), we utilize two-stage estimation strategy to complete the smoothed propensity score function:

1.

Step 1: Use a penalized regression method to select the basis function for the regression of
y
on
\bm{x}
.

2.

Step 2: Use the basis function in Step 1 to
to obtain the calibration equation in (
28
) and construct
\widehat{r}(\bm{x})=r^{\star}(\bm{x};\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\exp\{\hat{\lambda}_{0}+{\bm{b}}^{\rm T}(\bm{x}_{i})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}\}
.

In the first stage, we adapt the penalized estimating equations
(
Johnson
et al. 2008
)
to select important covariates in the outcome model. Generally speaking, we utilize an
Z
-estimator in the outcome model and we denote the corresponding score function as
\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
. For example,
\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{2}{N_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\bm{x}_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{\alpha}-y_{i}),

(51)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. The penalized estimating equations can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{P}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-q_{\lambda}(|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|)\mbox{sgn}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),

(52)

where
q_{\lambda}(|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|)=(q_{\lambda}(|\alpha_{0}|),\ldots,(q_{\lambda}(|\alpha_{d}|))^{\rm T}
,
q_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is a continuous function and
q_{\lambda}(|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|)\mbox{sgn}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
is an elementwise product between two vectors. Further, let
p_{\lambda}(x)=\int q_{\lambda}(x)dx
. In M-estimation framework,
p_{\lambda}(x)
usually serves as a penalization function. Various penalization functions can be used but we only consider the smoothly clipped absolute deviation function (SCAD)
(
Fan and
Li 2001
)
. In particular,

\displaystyle q_{\lambda}(\alpha)=\lambda\left\{\mathbb{I}(|\alpha|<\lambda)+\frac{(a\lambda-|\alpha|)_{+}}{(a-1)\lambda}\mathbb{I}(|\alpha|\geq\lambda)\right\},

(53)

where
(x)_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
,
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function and
a
is constant specified as
3.7
in
Fan and
Li 2001
. Further, we let
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
to denote the variable index set selected after SCAD procedure. Here we use a working model to select the variables.
According to
Johnson
et al. 2008
, under some regularity conditions, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\alpha}_{j}\neq 0)\rightarrow 1,\ \mbox{for}\ j\in\mathcal{M};

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\alpha}_{j}=0)\rightarrow 1,\ \mbox{for}\ j\in\mathcal{M}^{c},

(54)

which constructs the model selection consistency. After the first stage, we now obtain the important variable set
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
.

In the second stage, we use the selected variables to compute the smoothed density ratio function. That is, use

\log\{r^{\star}(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}})\}=\lambda_{0}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\rm T}\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}}.

The resulting PS estimator is then computed by

\widehat{\theta}_{\rm SPS}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\widehat{\omega}^{\star}(\bm{x}_{i})y_{i}

with
\widehat{\omega}^{\star}(\bm{x})=1+(N_{0}/N_{1})\exp(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

6
Multivariate missing data

We now consider the case of multivariate study variables, denoted by
Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{p}
, and they are subject to missingness. There are
2^{p}
possible missing patterns with
p
study variables.
Let

be the realized number of different missing patterns in the sample. Thus, the sample is partitioned into

\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{Y})\}=0

\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{p})^{\rm T}

Let
S_{t}
be the

consists of elements with complete response and that
S_{1}
is nonempty.
Without loss of generality, we may define
\delta_{i,t}=1
if

and
\delta_{i,t}=0
otherwise.
We wish to construct an estimating function using all available information:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{U}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in S_{t}}\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{y}_{i})\mid\bm{y}_{i,obs(t)}\}

where
\bm{y}_{i,obs(t)}
is the observed part of
\bm{y}_{i}
for

.
Instead of using a model for each conditional distribution, we can use the density ratio model such that

(56)

for
t=2,\ldots,T
. Condition (
56
) is the self-efficiency condition in (
4
).

To construct the density ratio function satisfying (
56
), we first find
\mathcal{H}_{t}=\mbox{span}\{b_{1}^{(t)}(\bm{y}_{obs(t)}),

\cdots,b_{L(t)}^{(t)}(\bm{y}_{obs(t)})\}
such that
\mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{y}_{i})\mid\bm{y}_{i,obs(t)}\}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
.
Thus, using the I-projection idea in Section 3, we may assume

\log\{r_{t}^{\star}(\bm{y}_{obs(t)};{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(t)}})\}={\phi_{0}^{(t)}}+\sum_{j=1}^{L(t)}{\phi_{j}^{(t)}}b_{j}^{(t)}(\bm{y}_{obs(t)})

(57)

The model parameters in (
57
) can be estimated by calibration equation derived from (
56
) and model assumption (
57
):

\displaystyle N_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i\in S_{1}}r_{t}^{\star}(\bm{y}_{i,obs(t)};{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(t)}})(1,\bm{b}_{i}^{(t)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in S_{t}}(1,\bm{b}_{i}^{(t)})

with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(t)}}
for
t=2,\cdots,T
, where
\bm{b}_{i}^{(t)}
is a vector of
b_{j}^{(t)}(\bm{y}_{i,obs(t)})
for
j=1,\cdots,L(t)
.

Now, the smoothed PS estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be obtained by solving

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm SPS}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\equiv N^{-1}\sum_{i\in S_{1}}\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{y}_{i})=\mathbf{0},

(58)

where

\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{N_{t}}{N_{1}}r^{\star}(\bm{y}_{i,obs(t)};\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(t)})

is the final weights for PS estimation.

To investigate the asymptotic behavior of the solution to (
58
),
the density ratio for missing pattern
t
can be simplified as
r_{t}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{i,t};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})
, for
t=2,\ldots,T
.
Let the true parameter of interest be
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
and the true parameter for density ratio be
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}=(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}^{\rm T},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}^{\rm T})^{\rm T}
. Then we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3

Under the regularity conditions stated in the Supplemtentary Material, for multivariate missing case, the solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\rm SPS}
to (
58
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\rm SPS}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i},\delta_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0})+o_{p}(1),

where

\displaystyle d(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i},\delta_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0})

(59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left[\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};\bm{x},\bm{y})\right\}\right]^{-1}\times\left[\delta_{i,1}\mathbf{U}_{i}+\sum_{t=2}^{T}{\delta}_{i,t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}\bm{z}_{i,t}+{\delta}_{i,1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\frac{N_{t}}{N_{1}}r_{t}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{i,t};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})\left\{\mathbf{U}_{i}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}\bm{z}_{i,t}\right\}\right],

\mathbf{U}_{i}=\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})
,
\bm{z}_{i,t}=(1,\bm{b}_{i}^{(t)})
and

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}
is the probability limit to the solution of

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\delta}_{i,1}r_{t}^{\star}(\bm{z}_{i,t};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})\left\{\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}\bm{z}_{i,t}\right\}\bm{z}_{i,t}^{\rm T}=\mathbf{0}.

The above theorem depicts the asymptotic behavior of our proposed estimators in multivariate missing case. Its proof is presented in the Supplementary Materials.

7.1
Simulation Study One

Two limited simulation studies are performed to check the performance of the proposed method.
In the first simulation study, we compare the proposed method with other existing methods under the MAR setup with few covariates.
The setup for the first simulation study employed a
2\times 2
factorial structure with two factors. The first factor is the outcome regression (OR) model that generates the sample. The second factor is the response mechanism (RM). We generate
\delta
and
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})^{\rm T}
based on the RM first. We have two different setup for the response mechanism as follows:

•

RM1 (Logsitic model):

\displaystyle x_{ik}

\displaystyle\sim N(2,1),\mbox{for\ }k=1,\ldots,4,

\displaystyle\delta_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mbox{Ber}(p_{i}),

\displaystyle\mbox{logit}(p_{i})

\displaystyle=1-x_{i1}+0.5x_{i2}+0.5x_{i3}-0.25x_{i4}.

•

RM2(Gaussian mixture model):

\displaystyle\delta_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mbox{Bern}(0.6)

\displaystyle x_{ik}

\displaystyle\sim N(2,1),\mbox{for\ }k=1,2,3,

\displaystyle x_{i4}

\displaystyle\sim\begin{cases}N(3,1),\mbox{if\ }\delta_{i}=1\\
N(1,1),\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Once
x
and
\delta
are generated, we generate
y

y

•

OR1:
y_{i}=1+x_{i1}+x_{i2}+x_{i3}+x_{i4}+e_{i}.

•

OR2:
y_{i}=1+0.5x_{i1}x_{i2}+0.5x_{i3}^{2}x_{i4}^{2}+e_{i}.

Here,

From each of the sample, we compare four different PS estimators of
\theta
:

1.

The proposed information projection (IP) PS estimator using the maximum entropy method in Section 3 using
\bm{x}_{i}=(1,x_{i1},x_{i2},x_{3i},x_{i4})^{\rm T}
as the control variable for calibration. Thus, the proposed PS estimator can be written as
\widehat{\theta}_{{\rm SPS}}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}y_{i}
where
\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}
is of the form in (

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}\widehat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}(1,x_{i1},x_{i2},x_{3i},x_{i4})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1,x_{i1},x_{i2},x_{3i},x_{i4}).

(60)

2.

The classical PS estimator using
maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) of the response probability with Bernoulli distribution with parameter
\mbox{logit}(p_{i})=\bm{x}_{i}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.

3.

Covariate balancing propensity score method (CBPS) of
Imai and
Ratkovic 2014
. The CBPS estimator of
\theta

l(\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\log(\omega_{i})

subject to (
60
).

4.

Entropy balancing propensity score method (EBPS) of
Hainmueller 2012
using calibration variable
(1,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})^{\rm T}

\omega_{i}
that maximize

Q(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\omega_{i}\log(\omega_{i})

60
).

To check the performance of the four estimators,
we use sample size
N=5,000
with
5,000
Monte Carlo samples. The results are presented in Figure
1
, where IP is our proposed method.

Figure 1:
Boxplots with four estimators for four models under simulation study one: (a) for OR1RM1, (b) OR1RM2, (c) for OR2RM1 and (d) for OR2RM2.

When we use
(1,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
as the calibration variable, OR1 matches with the working outcome model and RM1 matches with the working response model. Among the four methods considered, our proposed method and EBPS method perform better than the other two methods.
Entropy balancing propensity score method also shows good performances when OR1 or RM1 is true as it is doubly robust, but when both models fail (i.e. OR2RM2 setup), the performance is really poor. Our proposed method is also doubly robust and it performs reasonably well even when both models fail. Note that our proposed PS weights can be written as

\hat{\omega}_{i}^{\star}=1+{N_{0}}\times\frac{\exp(\bm{x}_{i}^{\rm T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{k}\exp(\bm{x}_{k}^{\rm T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}.

Thus, the PS estimator of
\theta=\mathbb{E}(Y)
can be expressed as

\widehat{\theta}_{\rm SPS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\delta_{i}y_{i}+(1-\delta_{i})\widehat{y}_{i}\right\}

where

\widehat{y}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{j}\exp(\bm{x}_{j}^{\rm T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})y_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{k}\exp(\bm{x}_{k}^{\rm T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}.

Thus, the proposed PS estimator can be expressed as a special case of fractional hot deck imputation of
Yang and Kim 2014
, which is a robust estimation method.
Other PS estimators does not allow for this interpretation.

8
Conclusion

In handling the selection bias of the voluntary sample, the problem of estimating the inverse propensity score is recast as a problem of estimating the density ratio function. The density ratio function is defined for the balancing score function, and the propensity scores that arise can be efficient if the balancing score includes the actual mean function.
The information projection technique builds a self-efficient propensity score estimating function. The
variable selection technique for the outcome model can be utilized to generate efficient propensity score weights.
The proposed method can be utilized as a unifying tool for merging information from multiple data sources.

There are several directions for further extensions of the proposed method. The proposed method can be extended to handle data integration problems
(
Chen
et al. 2020
)

in survey sampling.
Also, the proposed method is based on the assumption of missing at random. Extension to nonignorable nonresponse
(
Kim and
Yu 2011
)
can be also an interesting research direction.
Instead of using Kullback-Leibler divergence, we may use Hellinger divergence
(
Antoine and
Dovonon 2021
,
Li
et al. 2019
)
or
\gamma
-power divergence
(
Eguchi 2021
)
to achieve some robustness.
In addition, the proposed method can be used for causal inference, including the estimation of the average treatment effect from observational studies (
Yang and
Ding 2020
). Developing tools for causal inference using the proposed method will be an important extension of this research.
