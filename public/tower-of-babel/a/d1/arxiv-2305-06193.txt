Error Analysis of Mixed Residual Methods for Elliptic Equations
Kai Gu, Peng Fang, Zhiwei Sun, Rui Du

Abstract
. We present a rigorous theoretical analysis of the convergence rate of the deep mixed residual method (MIM) when applied to a linear elliptic equation with various types of boundary conditions. The MIM method has been proposed as a more effective numerical approximation method compared to the deep Galerkin method (DGM) and deep Ritz method (DRM) in various cases. Our analysis shows that MIM outperforms DRM and deep Galerkin method for weak solution (DGMW) in the Dirichlet case due to its ability to enforce the boundary condition. However, for the Neumann and Robin cases, MIM demonstrates similar performance to the other methods. Our results provides valuable insights into the strengths of MIM and its comparative performance in solving linear elliptic equations with different boundary conditions.

1.
Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) are widely used in various fields such as science, engineering, and finance
[
1
,
2
]
to model complex physical phenomena. Traditional numerical methods such as the finite difference method (FDM) and finite element method (FEM) have been successful in solving low-dimensional PDEs. However, when it comes to high-dimensional problems, the curse of dimensionality becomes a bottleneck, making classical methods less effective. In recent years, deep learning-based methods
[
3
]
such as the deep Ritz method (DRM)

[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, the deep Galerkin method (DGM)
[
8
]
, the physics informed neural network method
[
9
]
, the weak adversarial network method
[
10
]
, the deep least-squares methods
[
11
]
, least-squares ReLU neural network (LSNN) method
[
12
]
and the local deep learning method (LDPM)
[
13
]
have shown significant potential for solving high-dimensional PDEs, offering an attractive alternative to traditional methods.

Despite the success of deep learning methods such as DGM and DRM, they still suffer from convergence efficiency issues, especially when dealing with boundary conditions. Recently, the (deep) mixed residual method (MIM)
[
14
]
has been proposed to address these limitations. MIM is a novel approach that reformulates high-order PDEs into a first-order system. This idea is similar to the local discontinuous Galerkin method (LDGM) and the mixed finite element method (FEM). MIM also uses the residual of the first-order system in the least-squares sense as the loss function, which is closely related to the least-squares finite element method. Importantly, MIM can enforce exact boundary conditions, including mixed boundary conditions, which are difficult to deal with in previous deep learning methods
[
15
]
. In comparison to DGM and DRM, MIM provides better numerical approximation in most tested cases. However, rigorous analysis of the convergence rate of the MIM is still lacking, which limits our understanding of its theoretical properties and practical performance.

The analysis of MIM was carried out in
[
16
]
for the first time. This study asserts that MIM can approximate high-order derivatives more accurately than DRM, as confirmed by numerical experiments. Here, the total error is divided into the approximation error and the quadrature error, while in this paper, it is divided into three parts. Additionally, they only talk about two-layer network case, but the depth and width of the network in our work are not limited. One of the highlights of
[
16
]
is that the authors demonstrate that increasing the number of training samples is independent of network size.

In this article, we provide a rigorous theoretical analysis of the convergence rate of the MIM method applied to a linear elliptic equation with different kinds of boundary conditions, and compare its performance to existing results for DRM
[
17
]
and Deep Galerkin Method for weak solutions (DGMW)
[
18
]
. We use the same method that has been successfully applied to DRM and DGMW. To be more clear,
[
7
]
presents a convergence rate in
H^{1}
norm for DRM for Laplace equations with Dirichlet boundary condition by decomposing the total error into three parts, which are approximation error, statistical error, and the error caused by the boundary penalty. Specifically, we split the total error into approximation error, statistical error, and optimization error. The approximation error can be obtained by a recent work
[
19
]
and provides the information of the network that should be used. The derivation of statistical error is the main contribution of this paper, which uses Rademacher complexity to provide us the number of sampling points needed. The optimization error contains information about the iteration times and is beyond the scope of this paper. Although the main idea is similar to previous work
[
17
]
, there are some differences worth mentioning: (i) MIM uses the residual of the first-order system in the least-squares sense as the loss function, which needs an additional insensible variation condition on the coefficients of the elliptic problem to make the loss function non-degenerate. (ii) The neural network that MIM uses has a multi-dimensional output function, which makes the approximation analysis slightly different. (iii) An extra modification layer is added to the neural network to enforce exact boundary conditions, which complicates the estimates of statistical error.

2.
Neural Network and Mixed Residual Method

2.1.
Neural Network with modified layer

Let us first introduce some notations of neural networks that concerned in our later analysis.

Let
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
denote the depth of a network. We can define the function
\mathbf{f}
implemented by a neural network by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{0}(\bm{x})=\bm{x},

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\bm{x})=\rho(A_{\ell}\mathbf{f}_{\ell-1}+\mathbf{b}_{\ell})\quad\mbox{for $\ell=1,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1$},

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}\triangleq\mathbf{f}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x})=A_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathcal{D}-1}+\mathbf{b}_{\mathcal{D}},

where
A_{\ell}=\left(a_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}\times n_{\ell-1}}
,
\mathbf{b}_{\ell}=\left(b_{i}^{(\ell)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}}
,

n_{\ell}
is the width of a certain layer of the network,
and
\rho
is the activation function. In this article, we consider

(2.2)

\rho=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}\quad\mbox{or}\quad\rho=\frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{e^{x}+e^{-x}}.

While the network (
2.1
) is widely applied to DGM and DRM method, here we would like to introduce an extra modified layer in the framework of MIM, which can be written as

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\mathbf{mod}}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))=\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x})),

here
\phi_{g}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}
is
C^{1}
function depending on
g
and
\Omega
, and is constructed to modify the output function for the boundary condition. Furthermore, we may assume that
n_{\mathcal{D}}=d+1
, which means that the output function is a vector-valued function of
d+1
dimension.

Additionally, we define
\theta=\{A_{\ell},\mathbf{b}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\mathcal{D}}
as the weight parameters, and use
\mathfrak{n}_{i},\ i=1,\dots,\mathcal{D}
to denote the number of nonzero weights of the first
i
layers.
Generally, the function
\mathbf{f}
generated in (
2.1
) is called a parameterized function with respect to
\theta
, and we use the notation
\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{D}},B_{\theta})
to represent the collection of such functions that implemented by a
\rho
-neural network with depth
\mathcal{D}
, total number of nonzero weights
\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{D}}
, and all weights having a shared bound
B_{\theta}
.

For the analysis purpose, we conclude the following proposition for the activation function:

Proposition 2.1
.

For the activation function
\rho
given in (
2.2
), we have that
\rho\in C^{1}
satisfying the boundedness condition:

|\rho(x)|\leq 1,\qquad|\rho^{\prime}(x)|\leq 1,

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and the Lipschitz condition:

|\rho(x)-\rho(y)|\leq|x-y|,\qquad|\rho^{\prime}(x)-\rho^{\prime}(y)|\leq|x-y|,

for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

We also make the following assumption on modified function:

Assumption 1
.

For the modified function
\phi_{g}
in (
2.3
), we assume
\phi_{g}\in C^{1}
satisfying
the boundedness condition:

\begin{gathered}|\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))|\leq|\mathbf{f}(\bm{x})|+B_{g},\\
|\partial_{x_{p}}\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))|\leq|\partial_{x_{p}}\mathbf{f}(\bm{x})|+B_{\phi^{\prime}}|\mathbf{f}(\bm{x})|+B_{g^{\prime}},\end{gathered}

for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and the Lipschitz condition with respect to
\mathbf{f}
:

\begin{gathered}|\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))-\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{x}))|\leq|\mathbf{f}(\bm{x})-\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{x})|,\\
|\partial_{x_{p}}\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))-\partial_{x_{p}}\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{x}))|\leq|\partial_{x_{p}}\mathbf{f}(\bm{x})-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{x})|+B_{\phi^{\prime}}|\mathbf{f}(\bm{x})-\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{x})|,\end{gathered}

for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here
B_{g}
,
B_{g^{\prime}}
depend on the boundary condition
g
,
B_{\phi^{\prime}}
depends on the area
\Omega
and construction of
\phi_{g}
.

In the following, we introduce the Mixed Residual Method and provide an example of constructing a modified function
\phi_{g}
for the case where
\Omega
is a ball. We demonstrate that Assumption
1
holds in this case.

2.2.
Mixed Residual Method

Let
\Omega
be a convex bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\partial\Omega\in C^{\infty}
. For simplicity, we further assume that
\Omega\subset[0,1]^{d}
. We consider the following second order elliptic equation:

(2.4)

-\Delta u+\omega u=f\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$},

with three kinds of boundary conditions:

(2.5)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}

\displaystyle=g\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$},

\displaystyle\alpha u+\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}

\displaystyle=g\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$},\quad\alpha>0,

namely Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin boundary condition, respectively. Here we make the following assumption for this problem:

Assumption 2
.

Consider the elliptic problem (
2.4
)-(
2.5
), we assume the inhomogeneous term
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}(\Omega)
, the boundary term
g\in C^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, and coefficient
\omega\in C^{2}(\Omega)
. Moreover,
\omega
has positive lower bound

\omega(\bm{x})\geq c_{\omega}>0\quad\mbox{for any $\bm{x}\in\Omega$},

and a relatively insensible variation

\|\nabla\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<(c_{\omega})^{\frac{3}{2}}.

Under the regularity condition and lower bound of
\omega
in the Assumption
2
, one can conclude that problem (
2.4
)-(
2.5
) has a unique weak solution
u^{*}\in H^{3}(\Omega)
.
The relatively insensible variation of
\omega
is used to make the loss function non-degenerate in the decomposition of error.
In this paper we use the notation
C(coe)=C(f,g,\omega,\Omega)
to denote a constant depending upon the given problem (
2.4
)-(
2.5
).

Now from the MIM’s point of view, we can reconsider problem (
2.4
) as a first-order system. In fact, we introduce new variable
\bm{p}=\nabla u
, and consider
(u,\bm{p})
as independent
(d+1)
-dimension variable, which can be approximated by the output of neural network
\mathcal{P}
defined in (
2.1
)-(
2.3
), of solving a minimization problem

\min_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p}),

here the loss function is defined by

(2.6)

\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p}):=\int_{\Omega}\omega\big(\bm{p}-\nabla u\big)^{2}+\big(-\mathrm{div}\bm{p}+\omega u-f\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

Note that we have no penalty terms of boundary condition in the problem (
2.6
), since the extra modification layer (
2.3
) has been constructed such that the output function automatically fit the boundary condition. Here, we would like to mention that such a vanishing of penalty terms is the most highlight point in the networks of MIM.

While the construction of such modification layer relies on the structure of area
\Omega
, we introduction a simple example here for the case that
\Omega
is a ball. Suppose now
\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,|\bm{x}|^{2}\leq 1\}
, denote
\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))=\{\phi_{g,i}\}_{i=1}^{d+1}
and
\mathbf{f}=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d+1}
in (
2.3
). Then we can set the modification for Dirichlet case:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \phi_{g,1}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))&=(1-|\bm{x}|)^{2}f_{1}(\bm{x})+\tilde{g}(\bm{x})|\bm{x}|,\\
\phi_{g,i}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))&=f_{i}(\bm{x}),\quad i=2,\dots,d+1,\end{aligned}\right.

for Neumann case:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \phi_{g,1}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))&=f_{1},\\
\phi_{g,i}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))&=(1-|\bm{x}|)^{2}f_{i}(\bm{x})+\tilde{g}(\bm{x})x_{i},\quad i=2,\dots,d+1,\end{aligned}\right.

and for Robin case:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \phi_{g,1}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))&=f_{1},\\
\phi_{g,i}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x}))&=(1-|\bm{x}|)^{2}f_{i}(\bm{x})+\big(\tilde{g}(\bm{x})-f_{1}\big)x_{i},\quad i=2,\dots,d+1,\end{aligned}\right.

here
\tilde{g}(\bm{x})\in C^{3}(\Omega)
such that
\tilde{g}(\bm{x})=g(\bm{x})
for any
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
. Following the above construction, one can check the output of network (
2.1
)-(
2.3
) satisfies the boundary condition given in (
2.5
) automatically by the notations of

(u,\bm{p})=\mathbf{f}^{\mathbf{mod}}=\phi_{g}(\bm{x},\mathbf{f}(\bm{x})).

3.
Proof of Main Result

In this section we give a sketch of the poof of our main result. Note that in the statistical point of view, it is equivalent to write the loss function (
2.6
) in the form of

\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})=|\Omega|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim U(\Omega)}\Big[\omega(\mathbf{X})\big(\bm{p}(\mathbf{X})-\nabla u(\mathbf{X})\big)^{2}+\big(-\mathrm{div}\bm{p}(\mathbf{X})+\omega(\mathbf{X})u(\mathbf{X})-f(\mathbf{X})\big)^{2}\Big],

where
U(\Omega)
is the uniform distribution on
\Omega
.
As one can sample
N
points to obtain an approximation of
L(u,\bm{p})
, we define its discrete version as

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})=\frac{|\Omega|}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big[\omega(\mathbf{X}_{k})\big(\bm{p}(\mathbf{X}_{k})-\nabla u(\mathbf{X}_{k})\big)^{2}+\big(-\mathrm{div}\bm{p}(\mathbf{X}_{k})+\omega(\mathbf{X}_{k})u(\mathbf{X}_{k})-f(\mathbf{X}_{k})\big)^{2}\Big].

where
\{\mathbf{X}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
are i.i.d. random variables according to
U(\Omega)
.

3.1.
Error Decomposition

Now let us set
(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})
to be the exact solution of problem (
2.4
), thus is also the minimizer of Loss Function
\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})
. We also consider the problem

\min_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p}),

and denote its solution by
(\hat{u}_{\theta},\hat{\bm{p}}_{\theta})
. Finally, let us represent an SGD algorithm by
\mathcal{A}
, and denote its output solution by
(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})
. Now we introduce the approximation error which denotes the difference between
(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})
and its projection onto
\mathcal{P}
:

\mathcal{E}_{app}=\inf_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\|u-u_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\bm{p}-\bm{p}_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\big),

the statistical error between
\mathcal{L}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}
:

\mathcal{E}_{sta}=\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\big[\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})\big]+\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\big[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})-\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})\big],

and the optimization error between
(\hat{u}_{\theta},\hat{\bm{p}}_{\theta})
and
(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})
:

\mathcal{E}_{opt}=\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(\hat{u}_{\theta},\hat{\bm{p}}_{\theta}).

Then we have the following decomposition proposition:

Proposition 3.1
.

Under the Assumption
2
, it holds that the total error between exact solution
(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})
and output
(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})
can be decomposed as:

(3.1)

\|u_{\mathcal{A}}-u_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}}-\bm{p}_{e}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(coe)\big[\mathcal{E}_{app}+\mathcal{E}_{sta}+\mathcal{E}_{opt}\big]^{1/2}.

Proof.

For any
(u,\bm{p})\in\mathcal{P}
, set
\bar{u}=u-u_{e}
,
\bar{\bm{p}}=\bm{p}-\bm{p}_{e}
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bar{u}+u_{e},\bar{\bm{p}}+\bm{p}_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})+\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{div}\bar{\bm{p}}-\omega\bar{u}|^{2}+\omega|\nabla\bar{u}-\bar{\bm{p}}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle+2\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathrm{div}\bm{p}_{e}-\omega u_{e}+f\big)\big(\mathrm{div}\bar{\bm{p}}-\omega\bar{u}\big)+\omega\big(\nabla u_{e}-\bm{p}_{e}\big)\big(\nabla\bar{u}-\bar{\bm{p}}\big)\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})+\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{div}\bar{\bm{p}}-\omega\bar{u}|^{2}+\omega|\nabla\bar{u}-\bar{\bm{p}}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

The last line holds due to the fact that
(u_{R},\bm{p}_{R})
is the real solution of the equation.
Thus we can conclude that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})-\mathcal{L}(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{div}\bar{\bm{p}}-\omega\bar{u}|^{2}+\omega|\nabla\bar{u}-\bar{\bm{p}}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq 2\big(\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\bar{u}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\bar{\bm{p}}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\big).

On the other hand, by using integration by parts one has

(3.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{div}\bar{\bm{p}}-\omega\bar{u}|^{2}+\omega|\nabla\bar{u}-\bar{\bm{p}}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{div}\bar{\bm{p}}|^{2}+|\omega\bar{u}|^{2}+\omega|\nabla\bar{u}|^{2}+\omega|\bar{\bm{p}}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle-2\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega\bar{u}(\bm{\nu}\cdot\bar{\bm{p}})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+2\int_{\Omega}\bar{u}(\nabla\omega\cdot\bar{\bm{p}})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

Here the boundary term equals
0
for the Dirichlet and Neumann condition. As for the Robin case, we have that

\displaystyle-2\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega\bar{u}(\bm{\nu}\cdot\bar{\bm{p}})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=2\alpha\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega\bar{u}^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\geq 0.

As for the last term in (
3.3
), we have the estimate

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\bar{u}(\nabla\omega\cdot\bar{\bm{p}})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\geq-\|\nabla\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\frac{4}{3}}\|\bar{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\|\nabla\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2}{3}}\|\bar{\bm{p}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Using above estimates and turn back to (
3.3
), we finally obtain

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{div}\bar{\bm{p}}-\omega\bar{u}|^{2}+

\displaystyle\omega|\nabla\bar{u}-\bar{\bm{p}}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\geq c_{\omega}\|\nabla\bar{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+(c_{\omega}^{2}-\|\nabla\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\frac{4}{3}})\|\bar{u}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+(c_{\omega}-\|\nabla\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2}{3}})\|\bar{\bm{p}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Combining (
3.2
) and (
3.4
), and using Assumption
2
of
\omega
, it leads to

\displaystyle C\big(\|u-u_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+

\displaystyle\|\bm{p}-\bm{p}_{e}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L(u,\bm{p})-L(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})\leq C\big(\|u-u_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\bm{p}-\bm{p}_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\big).

Now, let us divide the difference of Loss function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})-\mathcal{L}(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})+\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(\hat{u}_{\theta},\hat{\bm{p}}_{\theta})+\widehat{L}(\hat{u}_{\theta},\hat{\bm{p}}_{\theta})

\displaystyle-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})+\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})-\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})+\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})-L(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\big[\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})\big]+\big[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(\hat{u}_{\theta},\hat{\bm{p}}_{\theta})\big]

\displaystyle+\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\big[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})-\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})\big]+\|u-u_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\bm{p}-\bm{p}_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Since
(u,\bm{p})
can be any element in
\mathcal{P}
, we take the infimum of
(u,\bm{p})\in\mathcal{P}
on right-hand sides of above estimate, and derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})-\mathcal{L}(u_{e},\bm{p}_{e})\leq\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\big[\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})\big]+\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\big[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})-\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})\big]

\displaystyle+\big[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{p}_{\mathcal{A}})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(\hat{u}_{\theta},\hat{\bm{p}}_{\theta})\big]+\inf_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\|u-u_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\bm{p}-\bm{p}_{e}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\big).

Combining the discussion above yields (
3.1
).

4.
Derivation of Statistical Error

In this section, we investigate statistical error
\mathcal{E}_{sta}
, which is the paramount part in this article.
Revisit the definition

\mathcal{E}_{sta}=\sup\limits_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\pm\left[\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})\right].

For the sake of simplicity, let us denote the density of loss function by
\Gamma_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,8
, which are given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(u,\bm{p},\bm{x})=|\bm{p}(\bm{x})|^{2},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(u,\bm{p},\bm{x})=

\displaystyle-2\bm{p}(\bm{x})\cdot\nabla u(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}(u,\bm{p},\bm{x})=|\nabla u(\bm{x})|^{2},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{4}(u,\bm{p},\bm{x})=

\displaystyle(\mathrm{div}\bm{p}(\bm{x}))^{2},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{5}(u,\bm{p},\bm{x})=-2\mathrm{div}\bm{p}(\bm{x})u(\bm{x})\omega(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{6}(u,\bm{p},\bm{x})=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{div}\bm{p}(\bm{x})f(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{7}(u,\bm{p},\bm{x})=\omega^{2}(\bm{x})u^{2}(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{8}(u,\bm{p},\bm{x})=

\displaystyle-2u(\bm{x})\omega(\bm{x})f(\bm{x}).

And denote the expectation and
N
-particle average of
\Gamma_{k}
by
\mathcal{L}_{k}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{N}
for
k=1,\dots,8
, separately, which are defined by

(4.1)

\mathcal{L}_{k}(u,\bm{p})=|\Omega|E_{\mathbf{X}\sim U(\Omega)}\Gamma_{k}(u,\bm{p},\mathbf{X}),\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{N}(u,p)=\frac{|\Omega|}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{k}(u,\bm{p},\mathbf{X}_{i}),

here
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are N i.i.d random variable yielding the uniform distribution on
\Omega
.
Then it follows directly from the triangle inequality that

(4.2)

\displaystyle E_{\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\pm

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{L}(u,\bm{p})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{N}(u,\bm{p})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{8}E_{\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\pm\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}(u,\bm{p})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{N}(u,\bm{p})\right].

4.1.
Rademacher complexity

We aim to bound the difference between continuous loss
\mathcal{L}_{k}
and empirical loss
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{N}
by Rademacher complexity. First, let’s introduce the definition of Rademacher complexity.

Definition 4.1
.

The Rademacher complexity of function class
\mathcal{F}
associate with random sample
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is defined as

(4.3)

\mathfrak{R}_{N}(\mathcal{F})=\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathbf{X}_{i},\sigma_{i}\}_{k=1}^{N}}\left[\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}u(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right],

where
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are N i.i.d Rademacher variable with
\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{k}=1)=\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{k}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
.

The definition of Rademacher complexity we introduced here is the generalization for the finite set case, see
[
17
]
. By the help of above notation, we can bound the right-hand side of (
4.2
) by the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
\mathcal{L}_{k}(u,\bm{p})
and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{N}(u,p)
given in (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\pm\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}(u,\bm{p})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{N}(u,\bm{p})\right]\leq C(coe)\mathfrak{R}_{N}(\mathcal{F}_{k}),

for
k=1,\dots,8,
here the function class
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}=

\displaystyle\{|\bm{p}(\bm{x})|^{2}:\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}=

\displaystyle\{\bm{p}(\bm{x})\nabla u(\bm{x}):u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{3}=

\displaystyle\{|\nabla u(\bm{x})|^{2}:u\in\mathcal{P}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{4}=

\displaystyle\{|\mathrm{div}\bm{p}(\bm{x})|^{2}:\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{5}=

\displaystyle\{\mathrm{div}\bm{p}(\bm{x})u(\bm{x}):u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{6}=

\displaystyle\{\mathrm{div}\bm{p}(\bm{x}):\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{7}=

\displaystyle\{u^{2}(\bm{x}):u\in\mathcal{P}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{8}=

\displaystyle\{u(\bm{x}):u\in\mathcal{P}\},

Proof.

In order to prove the Lemma, we recall the important property of Rademacher complexity: given
\omega\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
then for any function class
\mathcal{F}
, there holds (see Lemma 5.2 in
[
17
]
)

(4.4)

\mathfrak{R}_{N}(\omega\cdot\mathcal{F})\leq\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\mathfrak{R}_{N}(\mathcal{F}),

where
\omega\cdot\mathcal{F}:=\{\bar{u}:\bar{u}=\omega(\bm{x})u(\bm{x}),\,u\in\mathcal{F}\}
.

Now let us just prove one case. By taking
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
as an independent copy of
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{7}(u,\bm{p})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{7}^{N}(u,\bm{p})

\displaystyle=|\Omega|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim U(\Omega)}\omega^{2}(\mathbf{X})u^{2}(\mathbf{X})-|\Omega|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})u^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Omega|}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\omega^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i})u^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i})-\omega^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})u^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right],

Thus we can calculate that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}|\mathcal{L}_{7}(u,\bm{p})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{7}^{N}(u,\bm{p})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{|\Omega|}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathbf{X}_{i},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\omega^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i})u^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i})-\omega^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})u^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\Omega|}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathbf{X}_{i},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i},\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\left[\omega^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i})u^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i})-\omega^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})u^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right],

here the last step is due to the fact that the insertion of Rademacher variables doesn’t change the distribution since the symmetry structure in the summation.
By splitting the supremum into two terms, it continues to have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{|\Omega|}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i},\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\omega^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i})u^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i})

\displaystyle+\frac{|\Omega|}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathbf{X}_{i},\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}-\sigma_{i}\omega^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})u^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2|\Omega|}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathbf{X}_{i},\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sup_{u,\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\omega^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})u^{2}(\mathbf{X}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2|\Omega|\mathfrak{R}_{N}(\omega^{2}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{7})\leq C(\Omega,coe)\mathfrak{R}_{N}(\mathcal{F}_{7}).

Here we used the inequality (
4.4
) in the last step.
∎

The following Lemma states that the Rademacher complexity can be upper bounded by the Lipschitz constant of the parameter space.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a parameterized class of functions:
\mathcal{F}=\{f(\bm{x};\theta):\theta\in\Theta\}
. Let

||\cdot||_{\Theta}
be a norm on
\Theta
and let
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{F}}
be a norm on
\mathcal{F}
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
temains in a bounded set in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and the
mapping
\theta\mapsto f(x;\theta)
is L-Lipschitz, that is,

\|f(*;\theta)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq B,\qquad||f(x;\theta)-f(x;\tilde{\theta})||_{\mathcal{F}}\leq L||\theta-\tilde{\theta}||_{\Theta},

here the upper bound
B
is independent of
\theta
and constant
L
is independent of
\bm{x}
.
Then there holds

(4.6)

\mathfrak{R}_{N}(\mathcal{F})\leq\inf_{0<\delta<B/2}\left(4\delta+\frac{12}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{\delta}^{B/2}\Big\{\log\Big(\frac{2B\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon}\Big)^{d}\Big\}^{\frac{1}{2}}d\epsilon\right).

Proof.

One can find the proof of the Lemma in
[
17
]
, Lemma 5.5-5.8, which relies on the concept of
\epsilon
-covering set.
∎

4.2.
Upper Bound and Lipschitz Constant

In the following we will derive the upper bound and Lipschitz constant of the parameterized function class.

Lemma 4.3
.

Set the parameterized function class
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{D}},B_{\theta})
of network (
2.1
)-(
2.3
). Under Assumption
1
, for any
\mathbf{f}^{\mathbf{mod}}\in\mathcal{P}
we have

|f_{i}^{\mathbf{mod}}|\leq B_{\theta}+B_{g},

and

\displaystyle\left|f_{i}^{\mathbf{mod}}(\bm{x};\theta)-f_{i}^{\mathbf{mod}}(\bm{x};\tilde{\theta})\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{D}}}B_{\theta}^{\mathcal{D}-1}\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{\mathcal{D}-1}n_{j}\Bigg)\big\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\big\|_{2}.

Proof.

To compute the Lipschitz constant, we can write directly from the definition of
N_{i}
and proposition
2.1
that

\displaystyle\left|f_{i}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{f}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right|

\displaystyle=\Big|\rho\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}a_{ij}^{(\ell)}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}+b_{i}^{(l)}\Big)-\rho\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\tilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\tilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\tilde{b}_{i}^{(\ell)}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\tilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\tilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|+\left|b_{i}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{b}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right|\left|f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\tilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right|\left|\tilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|+\left|b_{i}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{b}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right|,

summing up the above estimate for
i=1,\dots,n_{\ell}
, we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\left|f_{i}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{f}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq B_{\theta}n_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\tilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right|+\left|b_{i}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{b}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right|\Big).

Now apply
\ell=1
to (
4.7
) in which case
\bm{f}^{(0)}=\tilde{\bm{f}}^{(0)}=\bm{x}
, then we can write

(4.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\left|f_{i}^{(1)}-\tilde{f}_{i}^{(1)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\left|a_{ij}^{(l)}-\tilde{a}_{ij}^{(l)}\right|+\left|b_{i}^{(l)}-\tilde{b}_{i}^{(l)}\right|\Big)=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{1}}\left|\theta_{j}-\tilde{\theta}_{j}\right|.

Similarly, take
\ell=2
in (
4.7
) and apply the result in (
4.8
), then one can derive

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{2}}\left|f_{i}^{(2)}-\tilde{f}_{i}^{(2)}\right|\leq

\displaystyle B_{\theta}n_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{1}}\left|\theta_{j}-\tilde{\theta}_{j}\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{2}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(2)}-\tilde{a}_{ij}^{(2)}\right|

\displaystyle+\left|b_{i}^{(2)}-\tilde{b}_{i}^{(2)}\right|\Big)\leq B_{\theta}n_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{2}}\left|\theta_{j}-\tilde{\theta}_{j}\right|.

Iterating above process,
we finally obtain for
\ell=1,\dots\mathcal{D}
that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\left|f_{i}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{f}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq B_{\theta}^{\ell-1}\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}n_{j}\Bigg)\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{\ell}}\left|\theta_{j}-\tilde{\theta}_{j}\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathfrak{n}_{\ell}}B_{\theta}^{\ell-1}\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}n_{j}\Bigg)\big\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\big\|_{2}.

Taking
\ell=\mathcal{D}
in above estimate, then the Lemma follows from
Assumption
1
.

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Set the parameterized function class
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{D}},B_{\theta})

2.1

1

(4.10)

\displaystyle|\partial_{\bm{x}_{p}}f_{i}^{\mathbf{mod}}(x;\theta)|\leq\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{\mathcal{D}-1}n_{j}\Bigg)B_{\theta}^{\mathcal{D}}+B_{g^{\prime}}.

Proof.

For
\ell=1,2,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\Bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}a_{ij}^{(\ell)}\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\rho^{\prime}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}a_{ij}^{(\ell)}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}+b_{q}^{(\ell)}\Big)\Bigg|

\ell=1,2,\dots\mathcal{D}-1

(4.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{i}^{(\ell)}|\leq\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}n_{j}\Bigg)B_{\theta}^{\ell}.

\ell=\mathcal{D}-1
in above estimate, and apply (
4.11
) similarly for the estimate of

\displaystyle|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{i}^{(\mathcal{D})}|\leq\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{\mathcal{D}-1}n_{j}\Bigg)B_{\theta}^{\mathcal{D}}.

1
the estimate (

) follows.

Lemma 4.5
.

Set the parameterized function class
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{D}},B_{\theta})
of network (
2.1
)-(
2.3
). Under Assumption
1
, for any
\mathbf{f}^{\mathbf{mod}}\in\mathcal{P}
we have

(4.13)

\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{i}^{\mathbf{mod}}(\bm{x};\theta)-\partial_{x_{p}}f_{i}^{\mathbf{mod}}(\bm{x};\tilde{\theta})\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{D}}}(\mathcal{D}+2)B_{\theta}^{2\mathcal{D}-1}\Bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{\mathcal{D}-1}n_{k}\Bigg)^{2}\big\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\big\|_{2}.

Proof.

From definition we can write for
\ell=1,\dots\mathcal{D}-1

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{i}^{(\ell)}-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right|=

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}a_{ij}^{(\ell)}\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\mathcal{\ell}-1)}\rho^{\prime}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}a_{ij}^{(\ell)}\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\mathcal{\ell}-1)}+b_{i}^{(\ell)}\Big)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\partial_{x_{p}}\tilde{f}_{j}^{(\mathcal{\ell}-1)}\rho^{\prime}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\partial_{x_{p}}\tilde{f}_{j}^{(\mathcal{\ell}-1)}+\widetilde{b}_{i}^{(\ell)}\Big)\Big|.

Using triangle inequality, it can be bounded from above by

\begin{gathered}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\Big|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}\Big|\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|\Big|\rho^{\prime}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}a_{ij}^{(\ell)}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}+b_{i}^{(\ell)}\Big)-\rho^{\prime}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}+\widetilde{b}_{i}^{(\ell)}\Big)\Big|\\
+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|\Big|\rho^{\prime}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}+\widetilde{b}_{i}^{(\ell)}\Big)\Big|,\end{gathered}

thus applying the boundedness and Lipschitz continuity of
\rho^{\prime}
, it continues to be bounded from above by

\begin{gathered}B_{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|+\left|b_{q}^{(\ell)}-\widetilde{b}_{q}^{(\ell)}\right|\Big)\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|.\end{gathered}

Again using the triangle inequality, we can bound above formula by

(4.14)

\begin{gathered}B_{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}-\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right|+B_{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\tilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|+\left|b_{i}^{(\ell)}-\widetilde{b}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right|\Big)\\
+B_{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\ell)}-\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right|\left|\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|.\end{gathered}

Now applying (
4.9
) and (
4.12
) to (
4.14
), the above estimates for
\ell=2,3,\dots\mathcal{D}-1
finally leads to

(4.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{i}^{(\ell)}-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{i}^{(\ell)}\right|\leq

\displaystyle B_{\theta}n_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\ell-1)}-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\ell-1)}\right|

\displaystyle+B_{\theta}^{2\ell-1}n_{\ell}\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}n_{i}\Bigg)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{\ell}}\left|\theta_{k}-\widetilde{\theta}_{k}\right|.

On the other hand, take
\ell=1
in (
4.14
) and note that
\bm{f}^{(0)}=\tilde{\bm{f}}^{(0)}=\bm{x}
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{i}^{(1)}-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{i}^{(1)}\right|\leq B_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\left|a_{ij}^{(1)}-\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(1)}\right|+\left|b_{i}^{(1)}-\widetilde{b}_{i}^{(1)}\right|\Big)\leq B_{\theta}\sum_{k=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{1}}\left|\theta_{k}-\widetilde{\theta}_{k}\right|.

Starting from above estimate, and iterating the process (
4.15
), we can get

(4.16)

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}-1}}\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{i}^{(\mathcal{D}-1)}-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{i}^{(\mathcal{D}-1)}\right|\leq\left(\mathcal{D}-1\right)B_{\theta}^{2\mathcal{D}-3}\Bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{\mathcal{D}-1}n_{k}\Bigg)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{D}}}\left|\theta_{k}-\widetilde{\theta}_{k}\right|.

As for the estimate of
f_{i}^{D}
, one can easily derive

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{i}^{(\mathcal{D})}-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{i}^{(\mathcal{D})}\right|\leq

\displaystyle B_{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}-1}}\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\mathcal{D}-1)}-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\mathcal{D}-1)}\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}-1}}\left|a_{ij}^{(\mathcal{D})}-\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{(\mathcal{D})}\right|\left|\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\mathcal{D}-1)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B_{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}-1}}\left|\partial_{x_{p}}f_{j}^{(\mathcal{D}-1)}-\partial_{x_{p}}\widetilde{f}_{j}^{(\mathcal{D}-1)}\right|+B_{\theta}^{\mathcal{D}-1}\Bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{\mathcal{D}-1}n_{j}\Bigg)\sum_{k=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{\mathcal{D}}}\left|\theta_{k}-\widetilde{\theta}_{k}\right|.

Under the Lipschitz condition in Assumption
1
, the estimate (

) follows.

5.
Conclusions and Extensions

In this paper, we analyze the convergence rate for the mixed residual method for second-order elliptic equations with Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin boundary conditions respectively. Additionally, we give some estimates for the settings of depth and width of neural networks such that a given convergence rate can be achieved in terms of training samples. To be clear, the convergence rate with respect to
n
we deduced is
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{dlogd}})
.

Based on the comparison in Table
1
, it can be concluded that MIM has an advantage over DRM and DGMW for the Dirichlet case due to its ability to enforce the boundary condition. However, for the Neumann and Robin cases, MIM shows similar results to the other methods. While MIM has shown better numerical approximation in most of the experiments conducted, it does not seem to be observable in our analysis. This could be attributed to the lack of analysis of the optimization error, which contains information about the iteration times. Additionally, it should be noted that MIM requires more regularity for the exact solution, as the strong form of the loss function in the least-squares sense has been used.
