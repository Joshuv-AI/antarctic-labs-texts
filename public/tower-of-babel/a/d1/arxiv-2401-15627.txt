Highest weight modules over Borcherds-Bozec superalgebras and their character formula Thanks: ∗ The corresponding author
Zhaobing Fan, Jiaqi Huang, Seok-Jin Kang, Yong-Su Shin ∗

Abstract
. We present and prove the Weyl-Kac type character formula for the irreducible highest weight modules over Borcherds-Bozec superalgebras with dominant integral highest weights.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
17B37, 17B67, 16G20

Introduction

The
Kac-Moody algebras
were introduced independently by V. G. Kac and R. V. Moody
as an infinite dimensional generalization of finite dimensional semisimple
Lie algebras over
\mathbf{C}

[
7
,
12
]
.
In
[
8
]
, Kac proved a character formula, called the
Weyl-Kac formula
,
for the irreducible highest weight modules with dominant integral highest weights.
It turned out that the Weyl-Kac formula for
affine Kac-Moody algebras

has significant applications to number theory,
combinatorics, mathematical physics, etc.

In
[
1
]
, R. E. Borcherds generalized the notion of Kac-Moody algebras
to define
Borcherds algebras
associated with Borcherds-Cartan matrices.
These matrices have non-positive diagonal entries, which means the Borcherds algebras
may have simple roots with the norm
\leq 0
, the
imaginary
simple roots.
Hence the index set
I
has a decomposition
I=I^{\text{re}}\sqcup I^{\text{im}}
,
where
{}^{\text{re}}=\{i\in I\mid a_{ii}=2\}
,

I^{\text{m}}=\{i\in I\mid a_{ii}\leq 0\}
.
He also generalized the Weyl-Kac formula for the case of Borcherds algebras.
A special case of Borcherds algebras, the
Monster Lie algebra
,
played an important role in Borcherds’ proof of the famous
Moonshine conjecture

[
2
]
.

The
Borcherds-Bozec algebras
are a further generalization of Borcherds algebras
associated with Borcherds-Cartan matrices having higher degree positive and negative
simple root vectors.
The index set for these simple root vectors is denoted by

I^{\infty}=(I^{\text{re}}\times\{1\})\sqcup(I^{\text{im}}\times\mathbf{Z}_{\textgreater 0})
.
Borcherds-Bozec algebras arise as a natural algebraic structure relevant to the theory of
perverse sheaves on the representation varieties of quivers with loops

[
3
,
4
]
.
The Weyl-Kac type character formula for the irreducible highest weight modules was proved in
[
5
]
.

A
Lie superalgebra
is a
\mathbf{Z}_{2}
-graded vector space

L=L_{\bar{0}}\oplus L_{\bar{1}}
with the
Lie superbracket

that satisfies the
super-symmetry
and the
super-Jacobi identity
.

The theory of Lie superalgebras was intensively developed by Kac
[
10
]
.
In
[
11
]
, Kac defined the notion of Kac-Moody superalgebras
and proved the super-version of Weyl-Kac formula.
Inspired by the idea of
[
11
]
and
[
1
]
, U. Ray proved the
Weyl-Kac formula for Borcherds superalgebras.

In this paper, we introduce the notion of
odd Borcherds-Cartan datum

and the associated
Borcherds-Bozec superalgebras
.
The highest weight representation theory is discussed as a mixture of
those over Kac-Moody superalgebras and Borcherds-Bozec algebras.
We also prove important commutation relations of
Casimir operator
and
higher degree simple root vectors.
Finally, combining all the above results together,
we present and prove the Weyl-Kac type character formula for the irreducible
highest weight modules over Borcherds-Bozec superalgebras with dominant integral
highest weights.

1.
Odd Borcherds-Cartan Datum

Let
I
be an index set which can be countably infinite.
An integer-valued matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
is called an
even symmetrizable Borcherds-Cartan matrix
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

a_{ii}=2,0,-2,-4,...
,

(ii)

a_{ij}\leq 0
for
i\neq j
,

(iii)

there exists a diagonal matrix
D=\text{diag}(d_{i}\in\mathbf{Z}_{>0}\mid i\in I)
such that
DA
is symmetric.

Set
I^{\text{re}}=\{i\in I\mid a_{ii}=2\}
,

I^{\text{im}}=\{i\in I\mid a_{ii}\leq 0\}
and

I^{\text{iso}}=\{i\in I\mid a_{ii}=0\}
.

An
odd Borcherds-Cartan datum
consists of :

(a)

an even symmetrizable Borcherds-Cartan matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
,

(b)

a free abelian group
P
, the
weight lattice
,

(c)

P^{\vee}:=\operatorname{Hom}(P,\mathbf{Z})
, the
dual weight lattice
,

(d)

\Pi=\{\alpha_{i}\in P\mid i\in I\}
, the set of
simple roots
,

(e)

\Pi^{\vee}=\{h_{i}\in P^{\vee}\mid i\in I\}
, the set of
simple coroots
,

(f)

a subset
I^{\text{odd}}
of
I
, the set of
odd indices
,

satisfying the following conditions

(i)

\langle h_{i},\alpha_{j}\rangle=a_{ij}
for all
i,j\in I
,

(ii)

\Pi
is linearly independent over
\mathbf{C}
,

(iii)

for all
i\in I^{\text{re}}\cap I^{\text{odd}}
, we have
a_{ij}\in 2\mathbf{Z}
,

(iv)

for each
i\in I
, there exists an element
\Lambda_{i}\in P
such that

\langle h_{j},\Lambda_{i}\rangle=\delta_{ij}\ \ \ \text{for all}\ i,j\in I,

We denote
I^{\text{even}}=I\setminus I^{\text{odd}}
, the set of
even indices
.
Given an even symmetrizable Borcherds-Cartan matrix, it can be shown that such
an odd Borcherds-Cartan datum always exists, which
is not necessarily unique. The
\Lambda_{i}

(i\in I)
are called the
fundamental weights
.

We define

(1.1)

P^{+}:=\{\lambda\in P\mid\langle h_{i},\lambda\rangle\geq 0\ \text{for}\ i\in I,\ \ \langle h_{i},\lambda\rangle\in 2\mathbf{Z}\ \text{for}\ i\in I^{\text{re}}\cap I^{\text{odd}}\}.

The elements in
P^{+}
are called the
dominant integral weighs
.

The free abelian group
Q:=\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathbf{Z}\,\alpha_{i}
is called the
root lattice
.
Set
Q_{+}:=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}\,\alpha_{i}
and
Q_{-}:=-Q_{+}
.
For
\beta=\sum k_{i}\alpha_{i}\in Q\textbf{}_{+}
, we define its
height
to be

\text{ht}(\beta):=\sum k_{i}
.

Let
{\mathfrak{h}}:=\mathbf{C}\otimes_{\mathbf{Z}}P^{\vee}
be the
Cartan subalgebra
.
We define
a partial ordering on
{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
by setting
\lambda\geq\mu

if and only if
\lambda-\mu\in Q_{+}
for
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
.

We decompose
{\mathfrak{h}}={\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}\oplus{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime\prime}
, where

{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathbf{C}\,h_{i}
and
{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime\prime}
is its complementary subspace.
Since
A
is symmetrizable, we can define
a symmetric bilinear form
(\ \,,\ \,)
on
{\mathfrak{h}}
by

(1.2)

\displaystyle(h,h_{i})=d_{i}^{-1}\langle h,\alpha_{i}\rangle\ \ \text{for}\ h\in{\mathfrak{h}},

\displaystyle(h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime})=0\ \ \text{for}\ h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime\prime}.

In particular, we have

(h_{i},h_{j})=d_{j}^{-1}a_{ij}=d_{i}^{-1}a_{ji}=(h_{j},h_{i}).

Moreover, since
\Pi
is linearly independent,
it is straightforward to verify that
(\ \,,\ \,)
is non-degenerate on
{\mathfrak{h}}
.
Hence there is a linear isomorphism
\nu:\mathfrak{h}\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}^{*}

defined by

(1.3)

\nu(h)(h^{\prime})=(h,h^{\prime})\ \ \text{for}\ h,h^{\prime}\in{\mathfrak{h}},

which implies

\nu(h_{i})=d_{i}^{-1}\alpha_{i},\ \ \nu^{-1}(\alpha_{i})=d_{i}h_{i}.

Then we obtain the induced non-degenerate symmetric bilinear for
(\ \,,\ \,)

on
{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
defined by

(1.4)

(\lambda,\mu):=(\nu^{-1}(\lambda),\mu^{-1}(\mu))\ \ \text{for}\ \lambda,\mu\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}.

In particular, we have

(1.5)

(\alpha_{i},\lambda)=d_{i}\langle h_{i},\lambda\rangle\ \ \text{for}\ i\in I.

For each
i\in I^{\text{re}}
, we define the
simple reflection

r_{i}:{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
by

(1.6)

r_{i}(\lambda)=\lambda-\langle h_{i},\lambda\rangle\,\alpha_{i}\ \ \text{for}\ \lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}.

The subgroup
W
of
GL({\mathfrak{h}}^{*})
generated by the simple reflections
r_{i}

(i\in I^{\text{re}})
is called the
Weyl group
of the odd Borcherds-Cartan datum given above.
It is easy to check that
(\ \,,\ \,)
is
W
-invariant.

For the rest of the paper, we fix a linear functional
\rho\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
satisfying

(1.7)

(\rho,\alpha_{i})=\ \dfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})\ \ \text{for all}\ i\in I.

2.
Borcherds-Bozec Superalgebras

In this section, we define the notion of
Borcherds-Bozec superalgebras
.
We first define a new index set

(2.1)

I^{\infty}:=(I^{\text{re}}\times\{1\})\sqcup(I^{\text{im}}\times\mathbf{Z}_{>0}).

For simplicity, we will often write
i
for
(i,1)
.

Definition 2.1
.

The
Borcherds-Bozec superalgebra
associated with
an odd Borcherds-Bozec Cartan datum
(A,P,P^{\vee},\Pi,\Pi^{\vee},I^{\text{odd}})
is the Lie superalgebra

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{g}}_{\bar{0}}\oplus{\mathfrak{g}}_{\bar{1}}

over
\mathbf{C}
generated by the elements
e_{il}
,
f_{il}

((i,l)\in I^{\infty})
and
{\mathfrak{h}}

with defining relations

(2.2)

\displaystyle[h,h^{\prime}]=0\ \ \text{for}\ h,h^{\prime}\in{\mathfrak{h}},

\displaystyle[e_{il},f_{jk}]=l\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}h_{i}\ \ \text{for}\ i,j\in I,k,l\in\mathbf{Z}_{>0},

\displaystyle[h,e_{jl}]=l\langle h,\alpha_{j}\rangle e_{jl},\ [h,f_{jl}]=-l\langle h,\alpha_{j}\rangle f_{jl},

\displaystyle(\text{ad}e_{i})^{1-la_{ij}}(e_{jl})=0\ \ \text{for}\ i\in I^{\text{re}},i\neq(j,l),

\displaystyle(\text{ad}f_{i})^{1-la_{ij}}(f_{jl})=0\ \ \text{for}\ i\in I^{\text{re}},i\neq(j,l),

\displaystyle[e_{il},e_{jk}]=[f_{il},f_{jk}]=0\ \ \text{for}a_{ij}=0.

Here,
h\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\bar{0}}
,
e_{il},f_{il}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\bar{0}}
for
i\in I^{\text{even}}

and
e_{il},f_{il}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\bar{1}}
for
i\in I^{\text{odd}}
.

We will denote by
|x|=\bar{0},\bar{1}
for homogeneous elements
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Hence
|h|=\bar{0}
,
|e_{il}|=|f_{il}|=\bar{0}
if
i\in I^{\text{even}}

and
|e_{il}|=|f_{il}|=\bar{1}
if
i\in I^{\text{odd}}
.

For an element
\alpha\in Q
, define

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}=\{x\in{\mathfrak{g}}\mid[h,x]=\langle h,\alpha\rangle x\ \ \text{for all}\ h\in{\mathfrak{h}}\}.

Then
{\mathfrak{g}}
has the
root space decomposition

{\mathfrak{g}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha>0}{\mathfrak{g}}_{-\alpha}\oplus{\mathfrak{h}}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha>0}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}.

If
{\mathfrak{g}}\neq 0
,
\alpha
is called a
root
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Let
\Delta
be the set of roots of
\mathfrak{g}
.
We denote by
\Delta_{+}
(respectively,
\Delta_{-}
) the set of
positive (respectively, negative) roots.
It can be shown that
\dim{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}=\dim{\mathfrak{g}}_{-\alpha}
.
A root is
real
if
(\alpha,\alpha)>0
and is
imaginary
if
(\alpha,\alpha)\leq 0
.
We use the notation
\Delta^{\text{re}}
and
\Delta^{\text{im}}

for the set of real roots and imaginary roots, respectively.

By setting

(2.3)

\text{deg}(h)=0,\ \ \text{deg}(e_{il})=l\alpha_{i},\ \ \text{deg}(f_{il})=-l\alpha_{i},

every positive root can be written as

\alpha=s_{1}\alpha_{i_{1}}+\cdots+s_{r}\alpha_{i_{r}}=\alpha_{j_{1}}+\cdots+{\alpha_{j_{t}}}\ (s_{k}>0,i_{k},j_{l}\in I).

A positive root
\alpha
is
even
(respectively,
odd
)
if the number of odd indices
j_{l}
in
\alpha
is even (respectively, odd).
Thus we get

(2.4)

{\mathfrak{g}}_{\bar{0}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}^{\text{even}}}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\pm\alpha}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ {\mathfrak{g}}_{\bar{1}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}^{\text{odd}}}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\pm\alpha},

where
\Delta_{+}^{\text{even}}
(respectively,
\Delta_{+}^{\text{odd}}
) denotes the set of
positive even (respectively, odd) roots.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}
be a
Borcherds-Bozec superalgebra.

Then the non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
(\ \,,\,\ )
on
{\mathfrak{h}}
can be extended
to a bilinear form on
{\mathfrak{g}}
satisfying the following properties.

(a)

({\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha},{\mathfrak{g}}_{\beta})=0
if
\alpha+\beta\neq 0
.

(b)

(\ \,,\ \,)
is non-degenerate on
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

(c)

(\ \,,\ \,)
is
super-symmetric
; i.e.,

(x,y)=-(-1)^{|x||y|}(y,x)\ \ \text{for all homogeneous elements}\ x,y\in{\mathfrak{g}}.

(d)

(\ \,,\ \,)
is
invariant
; i.e.,

([x,y],z)=(x,[y,z])\ \ \text{for all}\ x,y,z\in{\mathfrak{g}}.

(e)

For
\alpha\in\Delta_{+}
and
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}
,
y\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{-\alpha}
,
we have

[x,y]=(x,y)\nu^{-1}(\alpha).

Proof.

For each
N\in\mathbf{Z}_{>0}
, let
\mathfrak{g}(N)
(respectively,
\mathfrak{g}(-N)
) be the subspace of
\mathfrak{g}

spanned by the super-brackets

[e_{i_{1},l_{1}},\ldots,e_{i_{r},l_{r}}]
(respectively,

[f_{i_{1},l_{1}},\ldots,f_{i_{r},l_{r}}]
)
such that
l_{1}+\cdots+l_{r}=N.

Then
\mathfrak{g}
has a
\mathbf{Z}
-graded decomposition

\mathfrak{g}={\mathfrak{h}}\oplus\bigoplus_{N\in\mathbf{Z}}\mathfrak{g}(N).

We first extend the symmetric bilinear form
(\ \,,\ \,)
on
{\mathfrak{h}}
to
to a super-symmetric bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}(1)
by

\displaystyle(e_{il},f_{jk})

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\delta_{lk}d_{i}^{-1}\ \ \text{for all}\ i,j\in I,\ \ k,l\in\mathbf{Z}_{>0},

\displaystyle(e_{il},h)

\displaystyle=(f_{il},h)=(e_{il},e_{il})=(f_{il},f_{il})=0.

Then the bilinear form
(\ \,,\ \,)
on
\mathfrak{g}(1)
satisfies
(d)
as long as both
[x,y]
and
[y,z]

belong to
\mathfrak{g}_{(}1)
.
To see this, it suffices to verify that

([e_{i1},f_{i1}],h)=(e_{i1},[f_{i1},h])\ \text{for}\ h\in\mathfrak{h},

which follows from the definition of
\nu
.

Now using the induction on
N
, we would like to extend
(\ \,,\ \,)
to a bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}(N)

satisfying

(i)

(\mathfrak{g}_{i},\mathfrak{g}_{j})=0
if
|i|,|j|\leq N
,
i+j\neq 0
,

(ii)

the statement (d) holds as long as
[x,y]
and
[y,z]
belong to
\mathfrak{g}(N)
.

Assume that we already have the bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}(N-1)
satisfying (i) and (ii).
It suffices to define
(x,y)
for
x\in\mathfrak{g}(\pm N)
and
y\in\mathfrak{g}(\mp N)
.
Note that
y
can be expressed as a sum
y=\sum_{i}[u_{i},v_{i}]
, where

u_{i},v_{i}
are homogeneous elements of non-zero degree in
\mathfrak{g}(N-1)
.
Then
[x,u_{i}]\in\mathfrak{g}(N-1)
and we may define

(x,y)=\sum_{i}([x,u_{i}],v_{i}).

Actually, this is well-defined; if
i,j,s,t\in\mathbf{Z}
are integers such that

\displaystyle|i+j|=|s+t|=N,\ i+j+s+t=0,\ |i|,|j|,|s|,|t|<N,

\displaystyle x_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}(i),x_{j}\in\mathfrak{g}(j),x_{s}\in\mathfrak{g}(s),x_{t}\in\mathfrak{g}(t),

then we have

([[x_{i},x_{j}],x_{s}],x_{t})=(x_{i},[x_{j}[x_{s},x_{t}]]),

which gives the well-definedness.

Hence we have constructed a bilinear form satisfying (a) and (d).

Let
\alpha\in\Delta_{+}
and
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
,
y\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
.
Then for any
h\in\mathfrak{h}
, we have

([x,y]-(x,y)\nu^{-1}(\alpha),h)=(x,[y,h])-(x,y)\langle h,\alpha\rangle=0.

Thus the statement (e) holds.

The statement (c) follows from the definition.
∎

Let
\alpha\in\Delta_{+}
be a positive root.
Choose dual bases
\{e_{\alpha}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}

and
\{e_{-\alpha}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{-\alpha}

with respect to
(\ \,,\ \,)
, where
d_{\alpha}=\dim{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}
.
That is,

(e_{\alpha}^{(k)},e_{-\alpha}^{(l)})=\delta_{kl},\ \ \ (e_{-\alpha}^{(l)},e_{\alpha}^{(k)})=-\delta_{kl}.

Hence for
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}
and
y\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{-\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle x=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}(x,e_{-\alpha}^{(k)})e_{\alpha}^{(k)},\ \ \ y=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}(e_{\alpha}^{(k)},y)e_{-\alpha}^{(k)},

\displaystyle(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}(x,e_{-\alpha}^{(k)})(e_{\alpha}^{(k)},y).

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
and
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\beta-\alpha}
,
for any homogeneous element
z\in{\mathfrak{g}}
, we have

(a)

\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}e_{-\alpha}^{(k)}\otimes[z,e_{\alpha}^{(k)}]=(-1)^{|z|}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\beta}}[e_{-\beta}^{(l)}\otimes e_{\beta}^{(l)}

in
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}
,

(b)

\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}[e_{-\alpha}^{(k)}[z,e_{\alpha}^{(k)}]]=(-1)^{|z|}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\beta}}[[e_{-\beta}^{(l)},z],e_{\beta}^{(l)}]

in
{\mathfrak{g}}
,

(c)

\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}e_{-\alpha}^{(k)}[z,e_{\alpha}^{(k)}]=(-1)^{|z|}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\beta}}[e_{-\beta}^{(l)},z]e_{\beta}^{(l)}

in
U({\mathfrak{g}})
.

Proof.

We define the bilinear form
(\ ,\ )
on
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{g}
by
(a\otimes b,c\otimes d)=(a,c)(b,d)
.
For
e\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\ f\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\beta}
, we have

(2.5)

\displaystyle(\sum_{s}e_{-\alpha}^{s}\otimes[z,e_{\alpha}^{s}],e\otimes f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}(e_{-\alpha}^{s},e)([z,e_{\alpha}^{s}],f)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{g(\alpha)g(\beta)+1}(e,[z,f]).

(2.6)

\displaystyle(\sum_{s}[e_{-\beta}^{s},z]\otimes e_{\beta}^{s},e\otimes f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}([e_{-\beta}^{s},z],e)(e_{\beta}^{s},f)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{g(\alpha)g(\beta)+1+g(z)}(e,[z,f]).

Thus
\sum_{s}e_{-\alpha}^{s}\otimes[z,e_{\alpha}^{s}]=(-1)^{|z|}\sum_{s}[e_{-\beta}^{s},z]\otimes e_{\beta}^{s}
in
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{g}
.

Applying (a) to the linear maps

\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g},\ \ x\otimes y\mapsto[x,y]

and

\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow U(\mathfrak{g}),\ \ x\otimes y\mapsto xy.

we obtain (b) and (c).

3.
Representation Theory and Casimir Operator

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a Borcherds-Bozec superalgebra.
The category
{\mathscr{M}}
consists of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules
M

satisfying the following properties:

(i)

M=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}}M_{\lambda}
, where

M_{\lambda}=\{v\in M\mid hv=\langle h,\lambda\rangle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}v}\ \text{for all}\ h\in{\mathfrak{h}}\}
,

(ii)

\dim M_{\lambda}<\infty
for all
\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
,

(iii)

there exist finitely many
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{s}\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}

such that

\text{wt}(M)\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}(\lambda_{j}-Q_{+}),\ \ \text{where}\ \ \text{wt}(M)=\{\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}\mid M_{\lambda}\neq 0\}.

For a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}}M_{\lambda}

in the category
\mathscr{M}
, we define its
character
to be

\text{ch}M=\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}}(\dim V_{\lambda})e^{\lambda},

where
e^{\lambda}

(\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*})
denotes the additive basis of

\mathbf{C}[{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}]
.

Let
\lambda\in\text{wt}(M)
.
Since
e_{il}M_{\lambda}\subset M_{\lambda+l\alpha_{i}}
, due to the condition (iii),
it is easy to see that
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}M_{\lambda}=0
for all but finitely many

\alpha\in\Delta_{+}
.

We define an element in
U(\mathfrak{g})
by

(3.1)

\Omega_{0}=2\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}e_{-\alpha}^{(k)}e_{\alpha}^{(k)},

where
\{e_{\alpha}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}
and

\{e_{-\alpha}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}
are dual bases of
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}

and
\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\Delta_{+}
.
Then for any module
M
in the category
{\mathscr{M}}
, the operator

\Omega_{0}
is well-defined.

The
Casimir operator

\Omega
on
M=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}}M_{\lambda}

is defined as follows:

(3.2)

\Omega(v)=(\lambda+2\rho,\lambda)v+\Omega_{0}(v)\ \ \text{for}\ v\in M_{\lambda}.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
M=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}}M_{\lambda}
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module
in the category
{\mathscr{M}}
and let
v\in M_{\lambda}
.

Then the Casimir operator satisfies the following commutation relations:

(3.3)

\displaystyle[\Omega,f_{i}](v)=0\ \ \text{for}\ i\in I^{\text{re}},

\displaystyle[\Omega,f_{il}](v)=(l^{2}-l)(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})f_{il}v\ \ \text{for}\ i\in I^{\text{im}},l\in\mathbf{Z}_{>0}.

Proof.

For any
(i,l)\in I^{\infty}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}[e_{-\alpha}^{k}e_{\alpha}^{k},f_{il}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}(e_{-\alpha}^{k}e_{\alpha}^{k}f_{il}-f_{il}e_{-\alpha}^{k}e_{\alpha}^{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}(e_{-\alpha}^{k}[e_{\alpha}^{k},f_{il}]-[f_{il},e_{-\alpha}^{k}]e_{\alpha}^{k}).

Since

\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}e_{-\alpha}^{k}e_{\alpha}^{k}f_{il}=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{l\alpha_{i}}}e_{-l\alpha_{i}}^{k}[e_{l\alpha_{i}}^{k},f_{il}]+\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}-\{l\alpha_{i}\}}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\alpha}}e_{-\alpha}^{k}[e_{\alpha}^{k},f_{il}],

\sum_{k}e_{-l\alpha_{i}}^{k}[e_{l\alpha_{i}}^{k},f_{il}]=d_{i}lf_{il}h_{i},

by Lemma
2.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}-\{l\alpha_{i}\}}\sum_{k}e_{-\alpha}^{k}[e_{\alpha}^{k},f_{il}]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|\alpha||l\alpha_{i}|+1}\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}-\{l\alpha_{i}\}}\sum_{k}e_{-\alpha}^{k}[f_{il},e_{\alpha}^{k}]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|\alpha||l\alpha_{i}|+|l\alpha_{i}|+1}\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}-\{l\alpha_{i}\}}\sum_{k}[e_{-\alpha+l\alpha_{i}}^{k},f_{il}]e_{\alpha-l\alpha_{i}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}-\{l\alpha_{i}\}}\sum_{k}[f_{il},e_{-\alpha+l\alpha_{i}}^{k}]e_{\alpha-l\alpha_{i}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in\Delta_{+}}\sum_{k}[f_{il},e_{-\beta}^{k}]e_{\beta}^{k},

which yields

[\Omega_{0},f_{il}]=2d_{i}lf_{il}h_{i}.

Therefore we obtain

\displaystyle[\Omega,f_{il}]v

\displaystyle=(\lambda-l\alpha_{i}+2\rho,\lambda-l\alpha_{i})f_{il}v-(\lambda+2\rho,\lambda)f_{il}v+2(l\alpha_{i},\lambda)f_{il}v

\displaystyle=(l^{2}-l)(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})f_{il}v.

∎

A
\mathfrak{g}
-module

\lambda

,

(ii)

e_{il}v_{\lambda}=0
for all

,

(iii)

V=U(\mathfrak{g})v_{\lambda}
.

Such a vector
v_{\lambda}
is called a
highest weight vector
with highest weight
\lambda
.
Note that a highest weight module

has a decomposition

V=\bigoplus_{\mu\leq\lambda}V_{\mu}
.

For
\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
, let
J(\lambda)
be the left ideal of
U(\mathfrak{g})

generated by the elements
h-\langle h,\lambda\rangle\mathbf{1}
for

,
and
e_{il}

.

\mathfrak{g}
-module
with highest weight
\lambda
, called the

.
Since
M(\lambda)
is a free

-module

\mathfrak{g}

\lambda

.

It can be shown that

has a unique maximal submodule

.
We denote by

.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a weight
\mu
of
V(\lambda)
with
\lambda\in P^{+}
, set

Let
\tau
be the element in

such that
\text{ht}(\lambda-\tau)

Then

(\tau+\rho,\alpha_{i})\geq 0\ \ \text{for all}\ i\in I^{\text{re}}.

Proof.

Suppose

(4.1)

By Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt Theorem for Lie superalgebras

A similar argument in

For all

\mathfrak{g}

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
M(\lambda)
be the Verma module with highest weight
\lambda\in P^{+}
.

If

is a primitive vector, then we have

(4.5)

\Omega_{0}(m)=0.

Proof.

Let
m=\prod_{k=1}^{r}f_{i_{k},s_{k}}\in M(\lambda)_{\mu}v_{\lambda}
be a primitive vector.
We claim that

\Omega_{0}(m)=Cm\ \ \text{for some}\ C\in\mathbf{C}.

If

\Omega_{0}(f_{i_{1},s_{1}}v_{\lambda})=2d_{i_{1}}s_{1}\langle h_{i_{1}},\lambda\rangle f_{i_{1},s_{1}}v_{\lambda}

Hence our claim is true.

Assume that our assertion is true for the monomials of height
(\leq r-1)

Then the following calculation yields

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}(m)

\displaystyle=[\Omega_{0},f_{i_{1},s_{1}}]\prod_{k=2}^{r}f_{i_{k},s_{k}}v_{\lambda}+f_{i_{1},s_{1}}\Omega_{0}(\prod_{k=2}^{r}f_{i_{k},s_{k}}v_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=(s_{1}^{2}-s_{1})(\alpha_{i_{1}},\alpha_{i_{1}})\prod_{k=1}^{r}f_{i_{k},s_{k}}v_{\lambda}+C\prod_{k=1}^{r}f_{i_{k},s_{k}}v_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=((s_{1}^{2}-s_{1})(\alpha_{i_{1}},\alpha_{i_{1}})+C)\prod_{k=1}^{r}f_{i_{k},s_{k}}v_{\lambda},

as desired.

Since
m
is a primitive vector, there exists a submodule

satisfying (

).
Note that

\Omega_{0}(m)=2\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\sum e_{-\alpha}^{(k)}e_{\alpha}^{(k)}m\in U.

Let

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{i},\lambda-\beta+2\rho)=(\alpha_{i},\lambda-\beta)+(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{i},\lambda)-(\beta-\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})\geq 0.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r}

\displaystyle s_{k}(s_{k}-1)(\alpha_{i_{k}},\alpha_{i_{k}})=(\mu+2\rho,\mu)-(\lambda+2\rho,\lambda)

\displaystyle=(\mu-\lambda,\mu+\lambda+2\rho)=-\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(\alpha_{i},\lambda)-\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(\alpha_{i},\mu+2\rho)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(\alpha_{i},\lambda)-\sum_{i\in I^{\text{re}}}a_{i}(\alpha_{i},\mu+2\rho)-\sum_{i\in I^{\text{im}}}a_{i}(\alpha_{i},\mu+2\rho)

\displaystyle<-\sum_{i\in I}(\alpha_{i},\lambda)-\sum_{i\in I^{\text{re}}}a_{i}-\sum_{i\in I^{\text{im}}}a_{i}(\alpha_{i},\mu+\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\in I^{\text{re}}}a_{i}-\sum_{i\in I^{\text{im}}}a_{i}(\alpha_{i},\lambda)-\sum_{i\in I^{\text{im}}}a_{i}(\alpha_{i},\lambda-\sum_{j\in I^{\text{im}}}a_{j}\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle 0<
