A Compact Form of 3D Conformal Block
Chaoming Song

Abstract
We reveal an intriguing connection between conformal blocks and fractional calculus. By employing a modified form of half-derivates, we discover a compact representation of the three-dimensional conformal block, expressed as the product of two hypergeometric F 3 4 {}_{4}F_{3} functions. Furthermore, our method leads to several implications for two-dimensional Conformal Field Theory (CFT), which we explore towards the conclusion of this paper.



I
Overview

The global conformal block is a fundamental component of the modern conformal bootstrap. While close forms for these blocks are well-known in two and four dimensions (2D and 4D), a similarly compact form for 3D conformal blocks has been notably absent in the literature
Dolan and Osborn 2004
. In this note, we propose a novel representation for the global conformal block, given by:

\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell}({\rho},{\bar{\rho}})={\rho}^{\frac{{\Delta}-\ell-1}{2}}{\bar{\rho}}^{\frac{{\Delta}+\ell-1}{2}}\sum_{n,m\geq 0}A_{n,m}{\rho}^{n+m}{\bar{\rho}}^{n-m},

(1)

where
{\rho}
and
{\bar{\rho}}
are radial coordinates
Hogervorst and Rychkov 2013
, connected to
z
and
\bar{z}
via the quadratic transformations
z=4{\rho}/(1+{\rho})^{2}
and
\bar{z}=4{\bar{\rho}}/(1+{\bar{\rho}})^{2}
. The coefficient
A_{n,m}=A_{n,\ell-m}
is symmetric to ensure that
G_{{\Delta},\ell}
is symmetric under the exchange of
{\rho}
and
{\bar{\rho}}
. The conformal block
\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell}
in our representation corresponds to the conventional conformal block
G_{{\Delta},\ell}
, as

\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell}=\mathcal{T}^{1/2}G_{{\Delta},\ell},\;G_{{\Delta},\ell}=\mathcal{T}^{-1/2}\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell},

where the operator
\mathcal{T}^{\delta}
is defined as
\mathcal{T}^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=T^{\delta}_{z}T^{\delta}_{\bar{z}}
, with

\displaystyle T_{x}^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{-\delta}}{\mathrm{d}(-1/x)^{-\delta}},

(2)

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\delta}}{\mathrm{d}x^{\delta}}
is the Riemann–Liouville fractional derivative. Thus,
T^{-1/2}_{x}
represents the half-derivatives under the variable change
x\to-1/x
, and
T^{1/2}
denotes its inverse. A remarkable property, as demonstrated in Eq. (
10
), is

\displaystyle T^{1/2}_{z}k_{h}(z)={\rho}^{h-1/2},\;T^{-1/2}_{z}{\rho}^{h}=k_{h+1/2}(z),

(3)

where the one-dimensional conformal block
k_{h}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=4^{1/2-h}\frac{\Gamma(h)}{\Gamma(h-1/2)}x^{h}{}_{2}F_{1}(h,h,2h;z)
is defined using a non-traditional normalization prefactor.

The main purpose of this paper is to establish the following compact form for the three-dimensional block:

\begin{split}\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell}^{(3d)}({\rho},{\bar{\rho}})=({\rho}{\bar{\rho}})^{\frac{\Delta-1}{2}}&{}_{4}F_{3}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\a+1/2, \b+1/2,\a+\b+1/2, 1/2}{1+\a,1+\b,1+\a+\b};{\rho}{\bar{\rho}}\biggr]\\
\times({\bar{\rho}}/{\rho})^{\frac{\ell}{2}}&{}_{4}F_{3}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1/2-\a, \b+1/2,1/2-\a+\b, 1/2}{1-\a,1+\b,1-\a+\b};{\rho}/{\bar{\rho}}\biggr],\end{split}

(4)

where
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\Delta+\ell-1)/2
, and
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\Delta-(\ell+d-1))/2
. Moreover, combining Eqs. (
1
) and (
3
) yields the following series expansion in terms 2d conformal blocks:

G_{\Delta,\ell}(z,\bar{z})=\sum_{n,m\geq 0}A_{n,m}k_{n+m+\nu+\beta+1/2}(z)k_{n-m+\alpha+1/2}(\bar{z}),

(5)

In the three-dimensional case, the coefficient also has a compact symmetric form:

A^{(3d)}_{n,m}=\frac{(1/2)_{n}(1/2+\alpha)_{n}(\beta+1/2)_{n}(1/2+\alpha+\beta)_{n}}{n!(1+\alpha)_{n}(1+\beta)_{n}(1+\alpha+\beta)_{n}}\frac{(1/2)_{m}(1/2-\alpha)_{m}(\beta+1/2)_{m}(1/2-\alpha+\beta)_{m}}{m!(1-\alpha)_{m}(1+\beta)_{m}(1-\alpha+\beta)_{m}}.

(6)

The derived equations (
5
–
6
) are consistent with the form predicated on dimension reduction
Hogervorst 2016
, up to a scaling factor. However, to our best knowledge, the conjectured coefficients (
6
) have not been rigorously proven, nor has this symmetric form been recognized in previous studies.

II
Fractional Derivative

In this section, we review various definitions of fractional derivatives and their connection to the fractional derivative operator
T^{\delta}_{x}
as defined in Eq. (
2
), along with potential implications. We begin by considering the Riemann–Liouville fractional derivative:

\frac{d^{-\delta}}{dx^{-\delta}}f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(I^{\delta}f)(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\delta)}\int_{0}^{x}f(t)(t-x)^{\delta-1}dt,

(7)

when
\delta>0
. Otherwise, the formula can be analytically continued by applying a finite number of ordinary derivatives and shifting
\delta
, i.e.,
\frac{d^{\delta}}{dx^{\delta}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{d^{\lceil\delta\rceil}}{dx^{\lceil\delta\rceil}}I^{\lceil\delta\rceil-\delta}

Miller and Ross 1993
.

By applying the transformation
x\rightarrow-1/x
and
f(x)\rightarrow f(-1/x)
and substituting into the Riemann–Liouville integral, we obtain

\displaystyle(T^{\delta}_{x}f)(x)=\frac{x^{-\delta}}{\Gamma(\delta)}\int_{0}^{1}f(xt)(1-t)^{\delta-1}t^{-\delta-1}dt=\frac{x^{1-\delta}}{\Gamma(\delta)}\int_{0}^{x}f(t)(x-t)^{\delta-1}t^{-\delta-1}dt,

(8)

provided the integral converges; otherwise, an appropriate analytic continuation is utilized. The operator
T_{x}^{\delta}
is also a special case of the Erdelyi–Kober operator
Sneddon 2006

(K^{\alpha,\beta}f)(x)\equiv\frac{x^{\beta}}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{x}f(x)(x-t)^{\alpha-1}t^{\beta-1}dt,

(9)

with
T^{\delta}_{x}
being equivalent to
xK^{\delta,-\delta}
. It is straightforward to verify that
T^{\delta}x^{h}=\frac{\Gamma(h-\delta)}{\Gamma(h)}x^{h-\delta}
,
and thus

T_{z}^{1/2}k_{h}(z)=\frac{4^{1/2-h}\Gamma(h)}{\Gamma(h-1/2)}T_{z}^{1/2}z^{h}{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{h, h}{2h};z\biggr]=(z/4)^{h-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{h, h-1/2}{2h};z\biggr]={\rho}^{h-1/2},

(10)

which leads to Eq. (
3
). The final step employs the quadratic transformation (
32
).

The fractional derivatives
T_{x}^{\delta}
satisfy the following commutation relation:

T_{x}^{a}T^{b}=T_{x}^{b}T_{x}^{a}=T_{x}^{a+b}.

(11)

Furthermore, Eq. (
2
) indicates that
T^{-1}_{x}=\partial_{-1/x}=xD_{x}
, where
D_{x}\equiv x\partial_{x}
represents the dilation operator. The commutation relation (
11
) suggests

T_{x}^{\delta}xD_{z}T_{x}^{-\delta}=xD_{x}.

(12)

Moreover, since the dilation operator satisfies
D_{x}=-D_{-1/x}
, it follows that

T_{x}^{\delta}D_{x}T_{x}^{-\delta}=D_{x}+\delta.

(13)

III
A short proof

In this section, we offer a short proof of our main result as expressed in Eq. (
4
). We begin with the conformal Casimir equation
Dolan and Osborn 2011

\mathcal{D}G_{{\Delta},\ell}(z,{\bar{z}})=\frac{1}{2}C_{{\Delta},\ell}G_{{\Delta},\ell}(z,{\bar{z}}),

(14)

where the quadratic Camisir operator
\mathcal{D}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{D}_{z}+\mathcal{D}_{\bar{z}}+2\nu\frac{z{\bar{z}}}{z-{\bar{z}}}\left((1-z){\partial_{z}}-(1-{\bar{z}}){\partial_{\bar{z}}})\right)
with
\mathcal{D}_{z}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=z^{2}(1-z)\partial_{z}^{2}-z^{2}\partial_{z}
and
\nu\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(d-2)/2
, and eigenvalue
C_{{\Delta},\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\Delta}({\Delta}-d)+\ell(\ell+d-2)
. Rather than directly solving Eq. (
14
), we employ an alternate representation:

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell}=\frac{1}{2}C_{{\Delta},\ell}\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell},

(15)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{T}^{1/2}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{T}^{-1/2}
. Notably, the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{z}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=T_{z}^{1/2}\mathcal{D}_{z}T_{z}^{-1/2}
is significantly simplified in the new representation. By applying Eqs. (
12
–
13
) to
\mathcal{D}_{z}=(D_{z}-1-zD_{z})D_{z}
, it becomes clear that
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{z}\equiv T_{z}^{1/2}\mathcal{D}_{z}T_{z}^{-1/2}
simplifies to
(D_{z}-\frac{1}{2}-zD_{z})(D_{z}+\frac{1}{2})
, or

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{z}=D_{\rho}^{2}-1/4,

(16)

where we employ the identities
D_{z}=\frac{1+{\rho}}{1-{\rho}}D_{\rho}
and
zD_{z}=\frac{4{\rho}}{1-{\rho}^{2}}D_{\rho}
.

By applying the operator
(D_{z}-1)(D_{\bar{z}}-1)(z-\bar{z})
to the Casimir equation (
14
), and transforming it into the new representation, it can be shown that the Casimir equation (
15
) is equivalent to

\begin{split}{\rho}\left((D_{\bar{\rho}}-1/2)D_{\rho}\left(D_{\rho}^{2}+(D_{\bar{\rho}}-\nu)^{2}-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\right)-{\rho}{\bar{\rho}}(D_{\rho}+1/2)D_{\bar{\rho}}\left((D_{\rho}+\nu)^{2}+D_{\bar{\rho}}^{2}-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\right)\right)\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell}\\
={\bar{\rho}}\left((D_{\rho}-1/2)D_{\bar{\rho}}\left((D_{\rho}-\nu)^{2}+D_{\bar{\rho}}^{2}-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\right)-{\rho}{\bar{\rho}}(D_{\bar{\rho}}+1/2)D_{\rho}\left(D_{\rho}^{2}+(D_{\bar{\rho}}+\nu)^{2}-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\right)\right)\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell},\end{split}

Substituting Eq. (
1
) into the above equation yields a recursive relationship:

f_{1}(n,m)A_{n,m}-f_{2}(n,m)A_{n-1,m}-f_{3}(n,m)A_{n,m-1}+f_{4}(n,m)A_{n-1,m-1}=0,

(17)

with the boundary condition that
A_{n,m}=0
for
n<0
,
m<0
, or
m>\ell
, and
A_{0,0}=A_{0,\ell}=1
. The explicit forms of
f_{i}(n,m)
, for
i
ranging from 1 to 4, are rather involved and are elaborated in Eq. (
35
). Notably, we can deduce that

\displaystyle A_{n,0}=\frac{(\nu)_{n}(1/2+\alpha)_{n}(\beta+\nu)_{n}(\nu+\alpha+\beta)_{n}}{n!(1+\alpha)_{n}(\beta+\nu+1/2)_{n}(1+\alpha+\beta)_{n}},\;A_{0,m}=\frac{(\nu)_{m}(1/2-\alpha)_{m}(\beta+\nu)_{m}(\nu-\alpha+\beta)_{m}}{m!(1-\alpha)_{m}(\beta+\nu+1/2)_{m}(1-\alpha+\beta)_{m}}.

The explicit coefficient
A_{n,0}
obtained are already adequate to deduce the scalar block for
\ell=0
, as

\tilde{G}_{\Delta,0}=({\rho}{\bar{\rho}})^{\frac{\Delta-1}{2}}{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{(\dd-1)/2, \dd-1, \nu}{(1+\dd)/2, \dd-\nu};{\rho}{\bar{\rho}}\biggr],

(18)

or equivalently

G_{\Delta,0}(z,\bar{z})=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(\nu)_{n}\left(\frac{{\Delta}-1}{2}\right)_{n}({\Delta}-1)_{n}}{n!({\Delta}-\nu)_{n}\left(\frac{{\Delta}+1}{2}\right)_{n}}k_{n+{\Delta}/2}(z)k_{n+{\Delta}/2}(\bar{z}),

(19)

which is consistent with the established result
G_{{\Delta},0}=u^{{\Delta}/2}\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left[({\Delta}/2)_{n}({\Delta}/2)_{n+m})\right]^{2}}{n!m!\left({\Delta}+1-\frac{d}{2}\right)_{n}({\Delta})_{2n+m}}u^{n}(1-v)^{m}
, with
u\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=z{\bar{z}}
and
v\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(1-z)(1-{\bar{z}})
. This has been corroborated by verifying the first few terms. A thorough proof is reserved for future investigation.

Moreover, in the two-dimensional case (
\nu=0
), it is straightforward to verify that
A_{n,m}=\delta_{n,0}(\delta_{0,m}+\delta_{0,\ell-m})
, or

\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell}^{(2d)}({\rho},{\bar{\rho}})={\rho}^{\alpha}{\bar{\rho}}^{\beta}+{\rho}^{\beta}{\bar{\rho}}^{\alpha}.

(20)

This can also be derived directly from Eq. (
16
). Utilizing Eq. (
3
), we obtain the well-known form
G_{{\Delta},\ell}^{(2d)}({\rho},{\bar{\rho}})=k_{\alpha+1/2}(z)k_{\beta+1/2}({\bar{z}})+k_{\beta+1/2}(z)k_{\alpha+1/2}({\bar{z}})
. In three dimensions (
\nu=1/2
), Eq. (
17
) simplifies considerably, yielding

f(n,m)A_{n,m}-f(n-1/2,m)A_{n-1,m}-f(n,m-1/2)A_{n,m-1}+f(n-1/2,m-1/2)A_{n-1,m-1}=0,

where
f(n,m)=g(n)-h(m)
, with
g(n)=n(n+\alpha)(n+\beta)(n+\alpha+\beta)
and
h(m)=m(m-\alpha)(m+\beta)(m-\alpha+\beta)
. This results in the solution

A_{n,m}=\prod_{p=1}^{n}\frac{g(p-1/2)}{g(p)}\prod_{q=1}^{m}\frac{h(q-1/2)}{h(q)},

(21)

which concludes the proof of Eqs. (
4
) and (
6
). Moreover, utilizing the quadratic transformation (
30
), we arrive at a manifestly symmetric form:

\tilde{G}_{{\Delta},\ell}({\rho},{\bar{\rho}})=F\left(\alpha,\beta,\frac{4{\rho}{\bar{\rho}}}{(1+{\rho}{\bar{\rho}})^{2}}\right)F\left(-\alpha,\beta,\frac{4{\rho}{\bar{\rho}}}{({\rho}+{\bar{\rho}})^{2}}\right),

(22)

where

F(\alpha,\beta;x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(x/4)^{(\alpha+\beta)/2+1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha/2+\beta/2+1/4)_{n}(\alpha/2+\beta/2+3/4)_{n}}{(1+\alpha+\beta)_{n}n!}{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n, 1/2, 1/2}{1+\a,1+\b};1\biggr]x^{n}.

IV
Discussion

Besides deriving the 3D conformal block, the technique we introduce is highly versatile and applicable to various scenarios. For example, we obtain the following general expansion in terms of the 1D conformal block:

f(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{s+n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}k_{s+n}(z),

(23)

where the coefficients

\displaystyle c_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{4^{s}\Gamma(s-1/2)}{\Gamma(s)}\frac{(2s-1)_{n}(-1)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m=0}^{n}a_{m}\frac{(-n)_{m}(n+2s-1)_{m}}{(s)_{m}^{2}}.

(24)

To establish this, we employ the transformation
(T_{z}^{1/2}f)(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{\Gamma(s+n-1/2)}{\Gamma(s+n)}z^{s+n-1/2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\rho^{s+n-1/2}
. Equation (
24
) is then derived directly from the quadratic transformation (
27
). This equation facilitates the direct computation of operator product expansion (OPE) coefficients, particularly for 2D CFTs. For example, it can be used to expand chiral Virasoro blocks
G(z)
in terms of global blocks and thereby determine (squared) OPE coefficients directly
Behan 2018
. Note that there is a singularity at
s=0
, but we can circumvent this issue by subtracting the identity operator, which effectively elevates
s
to
1
.

Moreover, Virasoro blocks are solutions to the BPZ equations, which are derived from the null condition of the differential operator
\mathcal{L}_{-n}
that satisfies the Virasoro algebra. It is advantageous to consider a new representation:

\mathcal{L}_{-n}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{-n}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{T}^{1/2}\mathcal{L}_{-n}\mathcal{T}^{1/2}.

(25)

It is not difficult to discern that the BPZ equations, in our new representation, remain finite-order ordinary differential equations, thus permitting Frobenius series solutions:

\tilde{G}(\rho)=\sum_{n}c_{n}\rho^{n+s-1/2},

(26)

from which the OPE coefficients
c_{n}
can be directly extracted. Furthermore, this suggests that
c_{n}
generally satisfies recursive relations, thereby generalizing the results in
Behan 2018
. These findings underscore the efficacy of our method, but they represent merely a glimpse of its potential applications, which we aim to explore further in future research.

Appendix A
Quadratic Transformation

For a Frobenius series
f(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{s+n}
, a quadratic transformation can be expressed as

\left(\frac{4x}{(1+x)^{2}}\right)^{s}f\left(\frac{4x}{(1+x)^{2}}\right)=(4x)^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}x^{k},

(27)

where the coefficient

c_{k}=\frac{(2s)_{k}(-1)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{n=0}^{k}a_{n}\frac{(-k)_{n}(k+2s)_{n}}{(s)_{n}(s+1/2)_{n}}.

(28)

The proof unfolds straightforwardly:

\begin{split}(1+x)^{-2s}&f\left(\frac{4x}{(1+x)^{2}}\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}(4x)^{n}(1+x)^{-2(n+s)}\\
&=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}(4x)^{n}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(-2(n+s))!}{(-2n-2s-m)!m!}x^{m}\\
&=\sum_{k\geq 0}x^{k}\sum_{n\geq 0}^{k}a_{n}\frac{4^{n}(-2(n+s))!}{(-n-2s-k)!(k-n)!}\\
&=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(2s)_{k}}{k!}(-x)^{k}\sum_{n\geq 0}^{k}a_{n}\frac{(-k)_{n}(k+2s)_{n}}{(s)_{n}(s+1/2)_{n}}.\end{split}

(29)

As a result, we obtain the following quadratic transformation for the hypergeometric function:

\left(\frac{4x}{(1+x)^{2}}\right)^{s}{}_{p}F_{q}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_1, \ldots, a_p}{b_1, \ldots, b_q};\frac{4x}{(1+x)^{2}}\biggr]=(4x)^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2s)_{k}}{k!}{}_{p+2}F_{q+2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-k, k + 2s, a_1, \ldots, a_p}{s, s+1/2, b_1, \ldots, b_q};1\biggr](-x)^{k}.

(30)

Focusing on the Gauss hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
with
a_{1}=s
and
a_{2}=s+1/2
, the coefficient
{}_{p+2}F_{q+2}
simplifies to
{}_{2}F_{1}(-k,k+2s,b;1)=\frac{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(b-2s)}{\Gamma(b-k-2s)\Gamma(b+k)}=\frac{(2s-b+1)_{k}}{(b)_{k}}(-1)^{k}
, which reiterates the standard quadratic transformation:

\left(\frac{4x}{(1+x)^{2}}\right)^{s}{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{s, s+1/2}{b};\frac{4x}{(1+x)^{2}}\biggr]=(4x)^{s}{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{2s, 2s-b+1}{b};x\biggr].

(31)

By setting
s=h-1/2
and
b=2s+1=2h
in Eq. (
31
), we obtain

(z/4)^{h-1/2}{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{h, h-1/2}{2h};z\biggr]={\rho}^{h-1/2}.

(32)

Lastly, we compute the quadratic transformation for
F(\alpha,\beta;x)=4^{-s}\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{s+n}
, where

a_{n}=\frac{(s)_{n}(s+1/2)_{n}}{(2s+1/2)_{n}n!}{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n, 1/2, 1/2}{1+\a,1+\b};1\biggr],

(33)

with
s=(\alpha+\beta+1/2)/2
. The coefficient in Eq. (
28
) is given by

\begin{split}c_{k}&=\frac{(2s)_{k}(-1)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\frac{(-k)_{n}(k+2s)_{n}}{(2s+1/2)_{n}n!}{}_{3}F_{2}\biggl[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n, 1/2, 1/2}{1+\a,1+\b};1\biggr]\\
&=\frac{(2s)_{k}(-1)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\frac{(-k)_{n}(k+2s)_{n}}{(2s+1/2)_{n}n!}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{(-n)_{m}(1/2)_{m}^{2}}{m!(1+\alpha)_{m}(1+\beta)_{m}}\\
&=\frac{(2s)_{k}}{k!}\frac{(1/2)_{k}(\alpha+1/2)_{k}(\beta+1/2)_{k}}{(\alpha+1)_{k}(\beta+1)_{k}(2s+1/2)_{k}}.\end{split}

(34)
