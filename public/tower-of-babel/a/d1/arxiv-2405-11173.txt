Path-Integral Formulation of Truncated Wigner Approximation for Bosonic Markovian Open Quantum Systems
Toma Yoneya, Kazuya Fujimoto, Yuki Kawaguchi

Abstract
The truncated Wigner approximation (TWA) enables us to investigate bosonic quantum many-body dynamics, including open quantum systems described by the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) equation. In the TWA, the Weyl-Wigner transformation, a way of mapping from quantum-mechanical operators to c c -numbers, of the GKSL equation leads to the Fokker-Planck equation, which we calculate by reducing it to the corresponding stochastic differential equations. However, the Fokker-Planck equation is not always reduced to the stochastic differential equations depending on details of jump operators. In this work, we clarify the condition for obtaining the stochastic differential equations from the Fokker-Planck equation and derive analytical expressions of these equations for a system with an arbitrary Hamiltonian with jump operators that do not couple different states. This result enables us to shortcut the conventional complicated calculations in applying the TWA. In the course of the derivation, we formulate the GKSL equation by using the path-integral representation based on the Weyl-Wigner transformation, which gives us a clear interpretation of the relation between the TWA and quantum fluctuations and allows us to calculate the non-equal time correlation functions in the TWA. In the benchmark calculations, we numerically confirm that the relaxation dynamics of physical quantities including the non-equal time correlation functions obtained in our formulation agrees well with the exact ones in the numerically solvable models.

However, because the size of the Hilbert space exponentially grows with system size in many-body systems, the numerically exact calculation is limited to small systems. So far, various numerical techniques, such as the quantum Monte Carlo wave function method

[
14
,
15
]
describing the Markovian dynamics of open quantum systems. While these approaches can predict quantum dynamics with high accuracy, their application is still limited to low-dimensional or finite-size systems, and capturing the underlying physics from these simulations is not easy.
Although the mean-field approximation
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
is useful to discuss the physical picture of the system, it completely ignores the effects of quantum fluctuations and may miss the interesting quantum effects.
To take into account the effects of quantum fluctuations, the cluster mean-field
[
19
]
and dynamical mean-field approximations
[
20
]
have been extended to the open quantum systems.

The truncated Wigner approximation (TWA) is another a method that gives physical picture of quantum dynamics while accounting for quantum fluctuations
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.
It can describe quantum dynamics of weakly interacting bosonic many-body systems of large size.
According to the Weyl-Wigner transformation, operators are mapped into
c
-number functions and the density operator is represented as the Wigner function in the phase space.
In the TWA for isolated systems, we take into account fluctuations of the initial state by solving the Hamilton equation with various initial conditions which are sampled from the Wigner function for the initial state.
Owing to that the TWA incorporates the effects of quantum fluctuations based on the classical motion of the system, it provides us a physically understandable interpretation of the many-body phenomena.
Recently, the TWA has been generalized to describe the many-body dynamics of spins
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
and fermions
[
30
]
, and the performance of these TWAs are being investigated by comparing with experiments of large systems
[

Such TWA methods can be applied to the open quantum many-body dynamics described by the GKSL equation
[
16
,
38
,
39
]
.
In the TWA, the GKSL equation is approximated into the Fokker-Planck equation for the Wigner function, which we calculate by solving the corresponding stochastic differential equations with various initial conditions which are sampled from the initial Wigner function.
Here, the Fokker-Planck equation is not always reduced into the stochastic differential equations depending on details of jump operators that characterize couplings with environments
[
40
,
41
]
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, the condition against the jump operators for obtaining the stochastic differential equations from the Fokker-Planck equation and the analytical expressions of these equations have not been derived for arbitrary Hamiltonian and jump operators.
Consequently, we need to derive the Fokker-Planck equation and stochastic differential equations model by model before simulating the GKSL equation using the TWA, and complicated calculations are required in the course of the derivations.

In this work, we clarify the condition against the jump operators for obtaining the stochastic differential equations from the Fokker-Planck equation and derive the analytical expressions of these equations for arbitrary Hamiltonian and the jump operators that do not induce transitions between different degrees of freedom.
In the course of the derivations, we use the path-integral representation based on the Weyl-Wigner transformation
[
25
,
42
]
.
In isolated systems, this path-integral representation gives us a clear interpretation of the relation between the TWA and quantum fluctuations and allows us to calculate the non-equal time correlation functions in the TWA.
As a first step of this work, we formulate the path-integral representation of the GKSL equation based on the Weyl-Wigner transformation for systems with a single degree of freedom and derive the Fokker-Planck equation and stochastic differential equations.
The extension to systems with multiple degrees of freedom is straightforward when the jump operators are assumed not to mix different degrees of freedom.
As benchmark calculations, we investigate the relaxation dynamics of the scalar and spin-1 Bose-Einstein condensates without spatial degrees of freedom.
Comparing the relaxation dynamics of some observables including non-equal time correlation functions using the TWA with the numerically exact ones, we confirm that our results well reproduce the exact dynamics.
In the end of this work, we consider the systems with Hermitian jump operators and show that quantum fluctuations work strongly to the relaxation dynamics resulting in failing of the mean-field approximation to reproduce the exact dynamics of the GKSL equation.

2
Target of this paper

This paper aims to formulate the TWA for a bosonic open quantum system described by the GKSL equation. In this section, we introduce the GKSL equation and summarize the conditions under which our results are applicable.

2.1
Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad equation

We first provide the general description of open quantum systems.
Following the conventional literature
[
1
]
, we divide the total system into a system we focus on and an environment that couples with the system. The total system is assumed to be isolated from other systems such that its density operator
\hat{\rho}_{\rm tot}
obeys the von Neumann equation.
The dynamical map
\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t,t_{0})
that propagates the system’s density operator as
\hat{\rho}(t_{0})\to\hat{\rho}(t)
is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t,t_{0})\left[\hat{\rho}(t_{0})\right]={\rm Tr}_{{\rm B}}\left[\hat{U}(t,t_{0})\hat{\rho}_{{\rm tot}}(t_{0})\hat{U}(t,t_{0})\right],

(1)

where
{\rm Tr}_{{\rm B}}[\dots]
means the partial trace with respect to the degrees of freedom of the environment, and
\hat{U}(t,t_{0})
is an unitary time-evolution operator generated by the total system’s Hamiltonian.
Suppose there is no entanglement between the system and the environment in the initial state, i.e.,
\hat{\rho}_{\rm tot}(t_{0})=\hat{\rho}(t_{0})\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\rm B}(t_{0})
with
\rho_{\rm B}(t_{0})
being the initial density operator of the environment, the dynamical map becomes a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map and is written in the Kraus representation

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t,t_{0})\left[\hat{\rho}(t_{0})\right]=\sum_{k}\hat{M}_{k}(t,t_{0})\hat{\rho}(t_{0})\hat{M}^{\dagger}_{k}(t,t_{0}),

(2)

where
\hat{M}_{k}(t,t_{0})
is the Kraus operator satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\hat{M}_{k}^{\dagger}(t,t_{0})\hat{M}_{k}(t,t_{0})=\hat{1}

(3)

with
\hat{1}
being the identity operator.
This completeness condition ensures the trace-preserving property of the density operator, and the Kraus representation also guarantees the positive-semidefiniteness of
\hat{\rho}(t)
and
\hat{\rho}_{\rm tot}(t)
.
It is shown that the dynamical map is written in the Kraus representation if it is CPTP
[
43
]
.

A system described by the Kraus representation generally exhibits non-Markovian dynamics, where the quantum state depends on its history.
In this paper, however, we restrict ourselves to considering only Markovian open quantum systems, i.e., we suppose the dynamics of quantum states depend only on their instantaneous state.
For a Markovian open quantum system, the equation of motion for the density operator reduces to the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) equation
[
14
,
15
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\rho}(t)}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}(t)\right]_{-}+\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left(\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k},\hat{\rho}(t)\right]_{+}\right),

(4)

where
[\cdots]_{\mp}
denote the commutator
(-)
and anti-commutator
(+)
.
The first term of the right-hand side describes unitary dynamics generated by the system’s Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, and the second term describes non-unitary dynamics, where the jump operator
\hat{L}_{k}
characterizes the interaction between the system and the environment,
\gamma_{k}
represents the strength, and the subscript
k
distinguishes a variety of couplings with the environment.
Whereas we can include the term
\gamma_{k}/2[L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\rho(t)]_{+}
to the first term as a non-Hermitian part of the Hamiltonian, the term
\gamma_{k}L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}
describes the dynamics never expressed in the Schrodinger-type equation.
Below, we refer to the former as the quantum diffusion term and the latter as the quantum jump term.

2.2
Setup

In this paper, we consider a system satisfying following conditions:

1.

The system involves only bosonic degrees of freedom

Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
and jump operators
\hat{L}_{k}
for
\forall k
are composed of bosonic creation and annihilation operators
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m}
and
\hat{a}_{m}
satisfying the commutation relation
[\hat{a}_{m},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m^{\prime}}]_{-}=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}
, where the subscripts
m
and
m^{\prime}
denote internal or external degrees of freedom of bosons. In particular,
\hat{H}
can include higher-body interaction terms within this condition.

2.

The jump operators do not couple different degrees of freedom

Each jump operator
\hat{L}_{k}
involves only one bosonic state.
To specify the involved state
m
, we relabel the subscript
k
with
k,m
and expand the jump operator as

\hat{L}_{k,m}=\sum_{p,q}l_{kmpq}\hat{a}^{\dagger p}_{m}\hat{a}^{q}_{m}
, where the new subscript
k
runs
1,2,\cdots
for each
m
.

3
Review of Weyl-Wigner transformation

Before moving on to the path-integral formulation of the TWA, we briefly summarize the basic properties of the Weyl-Wigner transformation, which is a mapping from an operator to a
c
-number function in the phase space
[
21
]
.
This is the starting point of the TWA.
In this section, we consider a system with a single degree of freedom (see Sec.
5.2
for the Weyl-Wigner transformation of a system with multiple degrees of freedom).

3.1
Weyl-Wigner transformation and Wigner function

The Weyl-Wigner transformation maps an arbitrary operator
\hat{A}
consisting of bosonic creation and annihilation operators
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{a}
satisfying the commutation relation
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]_{-}=1
into a
c
-number function defined by

\displaystyle\hat{A}\mapsto A_{W}(\alpha)=\int\frac{d^{2}\eta}{\pi}\chi_{A}(\eta)e^{\alpha^{*}\eta-\alpha\eta^{*}},

(5)

where
\alpha=\alpha^{\rm re}+i\alpha^{\rm im}\in\mathbb{C}
(
\alpha^{\rm re},\alpha^{\rm im}\in\mathbb{R}
),

\int d^{2}\eta=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\eta^{\rm re}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\eta^{\rm im}
with
\eta=\eta^{\rm re}+i\eta^{\rm im}
(
\eta^{\rm re},\eta^{\rm im}\in\mathbb{R}
), and
\chi_{A}(\eta)
is a characteristic function given by

\displaystyle\chi_{A}(\eta)={\rm Tr}\left[\hat{A}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\eta)\right]

(6)

with the displacement operator
\hat{D}(\eta)=e^{\eta\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\eta^{*}\hat{a}}
.
We note that
A_{W}(\alpha)
is a function of two variables of
\alpha
and
\alpha^{*}
, but we omit
\alpha^{*}
in this paper for brevity.
The
c
-number function
A_{W}(\alpha)
is referred to as the Weyl-Wigner representation of
\hat{A}
.

We specifically refer to the Weyl-Wigner representation of the system’s density operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
as the Wigner function, which is given by

\displaystyle W(\alpha,t)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\eta}{\pi}\chi_{\rho}(\eta)e^{\alpha^{*}\eta-\alpha\eta^{*}},

(7)

\displaystyle\chi_{\rho}(\eta)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\eta)\right].

(8)

By using the relation

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\right]=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{\pi}A_{W}(\alpha)B_{W}(\alpha)

(9)

with
\hat{B}=\hat{\rho}(t)
, we can calculate a physical quantity
\braket{\hat{A}(t)}={\rm Tr}[\hat{A}\hat{\rho}(t)]
in the Weyl-Wigner representation as

\displaystyle\Braket{\hat{A}(t)}=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{\pi}A_{W}(\alpha)W(\alpha,t).

(10)

In addition, by replacing
\hat{A}
with an identity operator
\hat{1}
in Eq. (
10
) and using the fact
{\rm Tr}[\hat{\rho}(t)]=1
, we obtain the normalization condition for the Wigner function:

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{\pi}W(\alpha,t)=1.

(11)

According to Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
), the Wigner function behaves like a probability distribution function.
However, since the Wigner function in general takes negative values and does not preserve positivity, the Wigner function is often refereed to as a quasi probability distribution function.

3.2
Operator ordering

The Weyl-Wigner transformation maps a set of certain ordered products of non-commutable operators, so-called Weyl-ordered product, into a product of
c
-numbers. Here, the Weyl ordering for the bosonic operators is defined by

\displaystyle\left\{\hat{a}^{\dagger p}\hat{a}^{q}\right\}_{\rm Weyl}=\left.(-1)^{q}\frac{\partial^{p+q}}{\partial\alpha^{p}\partial\alpha^{*q}}\hat{D}(\alpha)\right|_{\alpha=0}.

(12)

The Weyl-orderd product consists of all possible ordering products of
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, e.g.,
\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\}_{\rm Weyl}=(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger})/2
and
\{\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}\hat{a}^{2}\}_{\rm Weyl}=(\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}\hat{a}^{2}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}^{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}\hat{a}+\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}^{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger 2})/6
.
The Weyl-Wigner transformation maps a Weyl-ordered product to a complex
c
-number as

\displaystyle\left\{\hat{a}^{\dagger p}\hat{a}^{q}\right\}_{\rm Weyl}\longmapsto\alpha^{*p}\alpha^{q}.

(13)

In order to obtain the analytical expression of
A_{W}(\alpha)
, we first expand
\hat{A}
in the Weyl ordering as
\hat{A}=\sum_{p,q}A_{pq}\{\hat{a}^{\dagger p}\hat{a}^{q}\}_{\rm Weyl}
and replace the operators to the
c
-numbers according to Eq. (
13
), obtaining

A_{W}(\alpha)=\sum_{p,q}A_{pq}\alpha^{*p}\alpha^{q}
.

3.3
Weyl-Wigner transformation of a product of two operators

The Weyl-Wigner transformation of a product of two operators
[\hat{A}\hat{B}]_{W}(\alpha)
is given by the well-known formula
[
21
]

\displaystyle\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\right]_{W}(\alpha)=A_{W}(\alpha)e^{\hat{\phi}/2}B_{W}(\alpha),

(14)

where
\hat{\phi}
is the differential operator defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}=\frac{\overleftarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha}\frac{\overrightarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha^{*}}-\frac{\overleftarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\frac{\overrightarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha}

(15)

with the arrows above the derivative symbols indicating which function, left or right, is to be differentiated.
We also use the following abbreviation

\displaystyle A_{W}(\alpha)e^{\hat{\phi}/2}B_{W}(\alpha)=A_{W}(\alpha)\star B_{W}(\alpha),

(16)

where
\star=e^{\hat{\phi}/2}
is referred to as the Moyal product.
In particular, taking one of
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
as
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
or
\hat{a}
, we obtain the following formulas:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left[\hat{a}\hat{A}\right]_{W}(\alpha)=\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\right)A_{W}(\alpha),\quad\left[\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{A}\right]_{W}(\alpha)=\left(\alpha^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\right)A_{W}(\alpha),\\
\left[\hat{A}\hat{a}\right]_{W}(\alpha)=\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\right)A_{W}(\alpha),\quad\left[\hat{A}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right]_{W}(\alpha)=\left(\alpha^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\right)A_{W}(\alpha),\end{aligned}

(17)

where the operators acting on
A_{W}(\alpha)
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
17
) are referred to as the Bopp operators.
The Bopp operators and the Moyal product are widely used to systematically calculate the Weyl-Wigner representations of the von Neumann and GKSL equations
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
38
,
39
]
.

An another representation of
[\hat{A}\hat{B}]_{W}(\alpha)
is the integral representation:

\displaystyle\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\right]_{W}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{0}d^{2}\eta}{\pi^{2}}A_{W}(\alpha_{0})B_{W}\left(\alpha_{0}+\frac{\eta}{2}\right)e^{\eta^{*}(\alpha-\alpha_{0})-\eta(\alpha^{*}-\alpha^{*}_{0})}

(18)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{0}d^{2}\eta}{\pi^{2}}A_{W}\left(\alpha_{0}-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)B_{W}\left(\alpha_{0}\right)e^{\eta^{*}(\alpha-\alpha_{0})-\eta(\alpha^{*}-\alpha^{*}_{0})},

(19)

where the first and second lines are transformed to each other by changing the integration variables.
The detailed derivations of above-discussed properties of the Weyl-Wigner representation are summarized in Refs.
[
21
,
44
]
.

4
Wigner functional representation of Markovian open quantum systems

In the following, we formulate the TWA for the GKSL equation from the path-integral approach.
We first derive the path-integral representation of the GKSL equation in Secs.
4.1
and
4.2
using the Wigner function and then formulate the TWA in Sec.
4.3
.
The results in Secs.
4.1
-
4.3
are for a system with a single-degree of freedom,
which we extend to a system with multiple degrees of freedom in Sec.
4.4
.
The path-integral formulation enables us to calculate non-equal time correlation functions, which will be explained in Sec.
4.5
.

4.1
Markov condition in the phase space

The Weyl-Wigner transformation of the Kraus representation of Eq. (
2
) is

\displaystyle W\left(\alpha_{\rm f},t\right)=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\pi}\varUpsilon\left(\alpha_{\rm f},t~;\alpha_{0},t_{0}\right)W\left(\alpha_{0},t_{0}\right),

(20)

where the propagator
\varUpsilon\left(\alpha_{\rm f},t~;\alpha_{0},t_{0}\right)
is given by

\displaystyle\varUpsilon\left(\alpha_{\rm f},t~;\alpha_{0},t_{0}\right)=\int\frac{d^{2}\xi d^{2}\eta}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{k}{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{M}_{k}\left(t,t_{0}\right)\hat{D}^{\dagger}\left(\eta\right)\hat{M}^{\dagger}_{k}\left(t,t_{0}\right)\right]e^{-\alpha_{\rm f}\xi^{*}+\alpha_{\rm f}^{*}\xi}e^{-\alpha_{0}\eta^{*}+\alpha^{*}_{0}\eta}.

(21)

We provide the derivations of Eq. (
21
) in
A.1
.
We can show that the propagator satisfies the Markov condition

\displaystyle\varUpsilon\left(\alpha_{\rm f},t~;\alpha_{0},t_{0}\right)=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}}{\pi}\varUpsilon(\alpha_{\rm f},t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})\varUpsilon(\alpha_{j},t_{j}~;\alpha_{0},t_{0})

(22)

when the dynamical map (
1
) satisfies the Markov condition. The detailed derivation is given in
A.2
.

4.2
Path-integral representation

Using Eq. (
22
), we can write the time-evolved Wigner function
W(\alpha_{\rm f},t)
as an infinite product of the infinitesimal-time propagators:

\displaystyle W\left(\alpha_{\rm f},t\right)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\prod_{j=0}^{N_{t}-1}\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}}{\pi}\varUpsilon(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})W\left(\alpha_{0},t_{0}\right),

(23)

where we discretize the time interval
[t_{0},t]
into
N_{t}
intervals with width
\Delta t
:

\displaystyle N_{t}=\frac{t-t_{0}}{\Delta t},\quad t_{j}=t_{0}+j\Delta t,\quad t_{N_{t}}=t,\quad\alpha_{N_{t}}=\alpha_{\rm f}.

(24)

Equation (
23
) is the path integral representation of the Wigner function.
Below, we derive the detailed expression of the propagator
\varUpsilon(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})
for the GKSL equation.
By substituting the propagator into Eq. (
23
), we obtain the path-integral representation of the GKSL equation.

According to the GKSL equation (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t_{j}+\Delta t)=\hat{\rho}(t_{j})-\frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar}\left(\hat{H}\hat{\rho}(t_{j})-\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\hat{H}\right)+\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\Delta t\left(\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}(t_{j})-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\right),

(25)

where we consider the infinitesimal time interval
\Delta t\ll 1
and neglect the higher-order term of
\Delta t
.
The Weyl-Wigner representation of Eq. (
25
) reads

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t)=

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j})-\frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar}\left(\left[\hat{H}\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\right]_{W}(\alpha_{j+1})-\left[\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\hat{H}\right]_{W}(\alpha_{j+1})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\Delta t\left(\left[\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\right]_{W}(\alpha_{j+1})-\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\right]_{W}(\alpha_{j+1})-\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\right]_{W}(\alpha_{j+1})\right).

(26)

The integral expressions of the Weyl-Wigner representation of the unitary and quantum diffusion terms are straightforwardly obtained by using the formulas (
18
) and (
19
) as

\displaystyle\left[\hat{H}\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\right]_{W}(\alpha_{j+1})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}d^{2}\eta_{j+1}}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta_{j+1}^{*}(\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j})-\eta_{j+1}(\alpha_{j+1}^{*}-\alpha_{j}^{*})}H_{W}\left(\alpha_{j}-\frac{\eta_{j+1}}{2}\right)W(\alpha_{j},t_{j}),

(27)

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\hat{H}\right]_{W}(\alpha_{j+1})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}d^{2}\eta_{j+1}}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta_{j+1}^{*}(\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j})-\eta_{j+1}(\alpha_{j+1}^{*}-\alpha_{j}^{*})}H_{W}\left(\alpha_{j}+\frac{\eta_{j+1}}{2}\right)W(\alpha_{j},t_{j}),

(28)

\displaystyle\left[\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\right]_{W}(\alpha_{j+1})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}d^{2}\eta_{j+1}}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta^{*}_{j+1}(\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j})-\eta_{j+1}(\alpha^{*}_{j+1}-\alpha^{*}_{j})}\left[L^{\ast}_{kW}\left(\alpha_{j}-\frac{\eta_{j+1}}{2}\right)\star L_{kW}\left(\alpha_{j}-\frac{\eta_{j+1}}{2}\right)\right]W(\alpha_{j},t_{j}),

(29)

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\rho}(t_{j})\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\right]_{W}(\alpha_{j+1})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}d^{2}\eta_{j+1}}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta^{*}_{j+1}(\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j})-\eta_{j+1}(\alpha^{*}_{j+1}-\alpha^{*}_{j})}\left[L^{\ast}_{kW}\left(\alpha_{j}+\frac{\eta_{j+1}}{2}\right)\star L_{kW}\left(\alpha_{j}+\frac{\eta_{j+1}}{2}\right)\right]W(\alpha_{j},t_{j}),

4.3
Equation of motion in the phase space

We summarize the results of this section in Fig.

In the first-order calculation, the integration with respect to

In Sec.

4.3.1

\displaystyle=i\hbar\left\{\lambda(\alpha_{j})\eta_{j+1}^{*2}+2\Lambda(\alpha_{j})\bigl|\eta_{j+1}\bigr|^{2}+\lambda^{*}(\alpha_{j})\eta_{j+1}^{2}\right\},

(68)

where
s_{j}^{(2)}
denotes the second-order term of
s_{j}
with respect to the quantum field, the first order term
s_{j}^{(1)}
is given by Eq. (
54
), and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
are defined by

\displaystyle\lambda(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\frac{\partial L^{*}_{kW}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\star\frac{\partial L_{kW}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}},

(69)

\displaystyle\Lambda(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{4}\left\{\frac{\partial L^{*}_{kW}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\star\frac{\partial L_{kW}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha}+\frac{\partial L^{*}_{kW}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha}\star\frac{\partial L_{kW}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\right\}.

(70)

Here,
\Lambda
takes a non-negative real value.
In the derivation of Eqs. (
69
) and (
70
), the quantum-jump and quantum-diffusion terms equally contribute to the second-order approximation, in contrast to the fact that only the quantum-jump term affects the classical path.
According to Eq. (
68
), the path-integral representation of the GKSL equation (
41
) within the second order of the quantum fluctuations becomes

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{\rm f},t)

\displaystyle\approx\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\prod_{j=0}^{N_{t}-1}\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}d^{2}\eta_{j+1}}{\pi^{2}}e^{i\Delta t\left(s_{j}^{(1)}+s_{j}^{(2)}\right)/\hbar}W(\alpha_{0},t_{0})

(71)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\prod_{j=0}^{N_{t}-1}\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}d^{2}\eta_{j+1}}{\pi^{2}}e^{i\Delta ts_{j}^{(1)}/\hbar}{\rm exp}\Biggl[-\Delta t\biggl\{\lambda(\alpha_{j})\eta_{j+1}^{*2}+2\Lambda(\alpha_{j})\bigl|\eta_{j+1}\bigr|^{2}+\lambda^{*}(\alpha_{j})\eta_{j+1}^{2}\biggr\}\Biggr]W(\alpha_{0},t_{0}).

(72)

In order to perform the integration with respect to the quantum field
\eta_{j+1}
, we introduce the auxiliary field
\Delta\Xi_{j}
as an integration variable of the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation:

\displaystyle e^{-2\Lambda\Delta t\left|\eta_{j+1}\right|^{2}-\lambda^{*}\Delta t\eta_{j+1}^{2}-\lambda\Delta t\eta_{j+1}^{*2}}=\int d^{2}\Delta\Xi_{j}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}_{j})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}_{j})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\rm exp}\left(\eta^{*}_{j+1}e^{i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi_{j}-\eta_{j+1}e^{-i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi^{*}_{j}\right),

(73)

where
\Delta\Xi_{j}=\Delta\Xi_{j}^{\rm re}+i\Delta\Xi_{j}^{\rm im}\in\mathbb{C}
(
\Delta\Xi_{j}^{\rm re},\Delta\Xi_{j}^{\rm im}\in\mathbb{R}
), and
\theta=\theta(\alpha)
is defined by

\displaystyle\theta(\alpha)={\rm arg}\left(\lambda(\alpha)\right).

(74)

This Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation is feasible only when

\displaystyle\Lambda(\alpha)\geq|\lambda(\alpha)|.

(75)

Especially when
\Lambda=|\lambda|
, the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation becomes

\displaystyle e^{-2\Lambda\Delta t\left|\eta_{j+1}\right|^{2}-\lambda^{*}\Delta t\eta_{j+1}^{2}-\lambda\Delta t\eta_{j+1}^{*2}}=\int d\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}_{j}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}_{j})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\rm exp}\left(i\eta^{*}_{j+1}e^{i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}_{j}+i\eta_{j+1}e^{-i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}_{j}\right),

(76)

which corresponds to fixing the real part of the auxiliary field to be zero,
\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}_{j}=0
, in Eq. (
73
).
We provide the derivations of these Hubbard-Stratonovich transformations in
C
.

According to the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation, Eq. (
72
) is transformed into

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{\rm f},t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\prod_{j=0}^{N_{t}-1}\int

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}d^{2}\eta_{j+1}d^{2}\Delta\Xi_{j}}{\pi^{2}}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}_{j})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}_{j})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}

\displaystyle\times{\rm exp}\left[\eta^{*}_{j+1}\left\{\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j}-\frac{\Delta t}{i\hbar}\frac{\partial H_{W}}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j}}-\Delta t\left.\frac{\partial\mathfrak{L}}{\partial\eta^{*}_{j+1}}\right|_{\eta_{j+1}=0}+e^{i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi_{j}\right\}-{\rm c.c.}\right]W(\alpha_{0},t_{0}).

(77)

Then, we can integrate out the quantum field
\eta_{j+1}
using Eq. (
36
), obtaining

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{\rm f},t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\prod_{j=0}^{N_{t}-1}\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}}{\pi}\varUpsilon^{(2)}(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})W(\alpha_{0},t_{0}),

(78)

where
\varUpsilon^{(2)}(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})
is a second-order propagator given by

\displaystyle\varUpsilon^{(2)}(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})=

\displaystyle~\pi\int d^{2}\Delta\Xi_{j}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}_{j})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}_{j})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}

\displaystyle\times\delta^{(2)}\left(\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j}-\frac{\Delta t}{i\hbar}\frac{\partial H_{W}}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j}}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left(L^{*}_{kW}\star\frac{\partial L_{kW}}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j}}-\frac{\partial L^{*}_{kW}}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j}}\star L_{kW}\right)+e^{i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi_{j}\right).

(79)

To derive Eq. (
79
), we use the expression of Eq. (
55
).

Equation (
78
) is the formal solution of the GKSL equation within the second order of the quantum fluctuations,
whose schematic image is illustrated in Fig.

(d).
Each points sampled from the initial Wigner function follows the path given by the argument of the Dirac delta function in Eq. (
79
):

\displaystyle\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j}=\frac{\Delta t}{i\hbar}\frac{\partial H_{W}(\alpha_{j})}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left\{L^{*}_{kW}(\alpha_{j})\star\frac{\partial L_{kW}(\alpha_{j})}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j}}-\frac{\partial L^{*}_{kW}(\alpha_{j})}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j}}\star L_{kW}(\alpha_{j})\right\}+e^{i\theta(\alpha_{j})/2}\Delta\Xi_{j}.

(80)

In the continuous limit, Eq. (
80
) becomes

\displaystyle i\hbar d\alpha(t)=\left[\frac{\partial H_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left\{L^{*}_{kW}(\alpha)\star\frac{\partial L_{kW}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}-\frac{\partial L^{*}_{kW}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\star L_{kW}(\alpha)\right\}\right]dt+i\hbar e^{i\theta(\alpha)/2}\cdot d\Xi(\alpha),

(81)

where
\cdot
is an Ito product
[
54
]
and
\Xi(\alpha)
is a complex stochastic process, whose real and imaginary parts are independent of each other and have Gaussian increments with a mean of
0
, i.e., the changes of
\Delta\Xi=\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}+i\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}\in\mathbb{C}
(
\Delta\Xi^{\rm re},\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}\in\mathbb{R}
) in the time interval
\Delta t

\displaystyle\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\Xi^{\rm re}(\alpha(t+\Delta t))-\Xi^{\rm re}(\alpha(t)),

(82)

\displaystyle\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\Xi^{\rm im}(\alpha(t+\Delta t))-\Xi^{\rm im}(\alpha(t))

(83)

obey the following Gaussian distribution functions:

\displaystyle P\left[\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}(\alpha)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda(\alpha)-|\lambda(\alpha)|\right)}}{\rm exp}\left[-\frac{\Bigl(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}(\alpha)\Bigr)^{2}}{2\Delta t\left(\Lambda(\alpha)-|\lambda(\alpha)|\right)}\right],

(84)

\displaystyle P\left[\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}(\alpha)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda(\alpha)+|\lambda(\alpha)|\right)}}{\rm exp}\left[-\frac{\Bigl(\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}(\alpha)\Bigr)^{2}}{2\Delta t\left(\Lambda(\alpha)+|\lambda(\alpha)|\right)}\right].

(85)

We note that the complex stochastic process
\Xi(\alpha)
has the different variances in its real and imaginary parts, which are given by
(\Lambda(\alpha)-|\lambda(\alpha)|)\Delta t
and
(\Lambda(\alpha)+|\lambda(\alpha)|)\Delta t
, respectively.
The difference between the classical equation of motion [Eq. (
52
)] and the stochastic differential equation [Eq. (
81
)] is only the existence of the complex stochastic process
\Xi(\alpha)
, which means that the effect of the second order of the quantum fluctuations is introduced as the complex Gaussian noises for the classical path.
Using Eqs. (
10
) and (
78
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Braket{\hat{A}(t)}=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{\rm f}}{\pi}A_{W}(\alpha_{\rm f})\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\prod_{j=0}^{N_{t}-1}\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}}{\pi}\varUpsilon^{(2)}(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})W(\alpha_{0},t_{0}).

(86)

Thus, we can calculate the physical quantity
\braket{\hat{A}(t)}
by iteratively solving the stochastic differential equation (
81
) with different initial conditions sampled from the initial Wigner function
W(\alpha_{0},t_{0})
, calculating the value
A_{W}(\alpha_{\rm f})
, and taking an ensemble average with respect to the results.

We derive the equation of motion of the Wigner function within the second-order approximation.
Expanding
H_{W}
and
\mathfrak{L}
in Eq. (
34
) with respect to the quantum field
\eta_{j+1}
up to second order, we obtain

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t)=~

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{j},t_{j})+\frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{j+1}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial H_{W}}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j+1}}-i\hbar\left.\frac{\partial\mathfrak{L}}{\partial\eta^{*}_{j+1}}\right|_{\eta_{j+1}=0}\right)W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j})\right\}-{\rm c.c.}\right]

\displaystyle-\Delta t\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}d^{2}\eta_{j+1}}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta^{*}_{j+1}(\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j})-\eta_{j+1}(\alpha^{*}_{j+1}-\alpha^{*}_{j})}\Biggl\{\lambda(\alpha_{j})\eta_{j+1}^{*2}+2\Lambda(\alpha_{j})\bigl|\eta_{j+1}\bigr|^{2}+\lambda^{*}(\alpha_{j})\eta_{j+1}^{2}\Biggr\}W(\alpha_{j},t_{j})

(87)

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{j},t_{j})+\frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{j+1}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial H_{W}}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j+1}}-i\hbar\left.\frac{\partial\mathfrak{L}}{\partial\eta^{*}_{j+1}}\right|_{\eta_{j+1}=0}\right)W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j})\right\}-{\rm c.c.}\right]

\displaystyle-\Delta t\int d^{2}\alpha_{j}\Biggl\{\lambda(\alpha_{j})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}_{j}}+2\Lambda(\alpha_{j})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{j}\alpha^{*}_{j}}+\lambda^{*}(\alpha_{j})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{*2}_{j}}\Biggr\}\left\{\int\frac{d^{2}\eta_{j+1}}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta^{*}_{j+1}(\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j})-\eta_{j+1}(\alpha^{*}_{j+1}-\alpha^{*}_{j})}\right\}W(\alpha_{j},t_{j}),

(88)

where we use Eq. (
65
) to obtain the first line.
Using Eq. (
36
), we can rewrite Eq. (
88
) as

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t)=~

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{j},t_{j})+\frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{j+1}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial H_{W}}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j+1}}-i\hbar\left.\frac{\partial\mathfrak{L}}{\partial\eta^{*}_{j+1}}\right|_{\eta_{j+1}=0}\right)W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j})\right\}-{\rm c.c.}\right]

\displaystyle-\Delta t\int d^{2}\alpha_{j}W(\alpha_{j},t_{j})\Biggl\{\lambda(\alpha_{j})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}_{j}}+2\Lambda(\alpha_{j})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{j}\alpha^{*}_{j}}+\lambda^{*}(\alpha_{j})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{*2}_{j}}\Biggr\}\delta^{(2)}(\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j}).

(89)

Performing the integration by part, we obtain

\displaystyle W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t)-W(\alpha_{j},t_{j})=~

\displaystyle\frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{j+1}}\left[\left(\frac{\partial H_{W}}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{j+1}}-i\hbar\left.\frac{\partial\mathfrak{L}}{\partial\eta^{*}_{j+1}}\right|_{\eta_{j+1}=0}\right)W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j})\right]

\displaystyle-\Delta t\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{j+1}^{2}}\biggl[\lambda W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j})\biggr]+\Delta t\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{j+1}\partial\alpha_{j+1}^{*}}\biggl[\Lambda W(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j})\biggr]+{\rm c.c.}

(90)

Taking the continuous limit of Eq. (
90
), we obtain the following Fokker-Planck equation:

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{dW(\alpha,t)}{dt}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\left[\left\{\frac{\partial H_{W}}{\partial\alpha^{*}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left(L^{\ast}_{kW}\star\frac{\partial L_{kW}}{\partial\alpha^{*}}-\frac{\partial L^{\ast}_{kW}}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\star L_{kW}\right)\right\}W(\alpha,t)\right]

\displaystyle-i\hbar\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\biggl[\lambda W(\alpha,t)\biggr]+i\hbar\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha\partial\alpha^{*}}\biggl[\Lambda W(\alpha,t)\biggr]-{\rm c.c.},

(91)

where we have used Eq. (
55
).

Before closing this section, we remark on the diffusion term of the Fokker-Planck equation (
91
).
The feasible condition of the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation,
\Lambda\geq|\lambda|
, corresponds to the positive-semidefiniteness condition on the diffusion matrix of the Fokker-Planck equation (
91
): Only when
\Lambda\geq|\lambda|
, we can reduce Eq. (
91
) into the stochastic differential equation (
81
).

4.4
Extension to a multi-mode system

In the previous sections, we formulate the path-integral representation of the GKSL equation and derive its analytical expressions within the first- and second-order quantum fluctuations for a system with a single degree of freedom.
In this section, we extend the results into a system with multiple degrees of freedom.
In the following, we consider a system with total
M
degrees of freedom and denote each mode by subscript
m~(=1,2,\cdots,M)
.
Here, we remark that the second-order results hold only for local jump operators that do not couple different degrees of freedom. See setup
2
in Sec.
2.2
.
We also note that the path-integral representation and the first-order results hold for arbitrary jump operators.

We first introduce the multi-mode displacement operator
\hat{D}(\vec{\alpha})
as

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\vec{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{m}\hat{D}(\alpha_{m}),

(92)

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\alpha_{m})

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha_{m}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m}-\alpha_{m}^{*}\hat{a}_{m}},

(93)

where
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m}
and
\hat{a}_{m}
are the creation and annihilation operator of bosons in mode
m
satisfying the commutation relation
[\hat{a}_{m},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m^{\prime}}]_{-}=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}
.
The Weyl-Wigner transformation maps an arbitrary operator
\hat{A}
consisting
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m}
and
\hat{a}_{m}
for
m=1,2,\cdots,M
into a
c
-number function
A_{W}(\vec{\alpha})
in the
2M
-dimensional phase space:

\displaystyle A_{W}(\vec{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{\eta}}{\pi^{M}}\chi_{A}(\vec{\eta})e^{\vec{\alpha}^{*}\cdot\vec{\eta}-\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{\eta}^{*}},

(94)

\displaystyle\chi_{A}(\vec{\eta})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left[\hat{A}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\vec{\eta})\right],

(95)

where
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{M})
and
\int d^{2}\vec{\eta}=\prod_{m}\int d^{2}\eta_{m}
.
The trace and the Weyl-Wigner representation of a product of two operators are given by

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{\alpha}}{\pi^{M}}A_{W}(\vec{\alpha})B_{W}(\vec{\alpha}),

(96)

\displaystyle\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\right]_{W}(\vec{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{\alpha}_{0}d^{2}\vec{\eta}}{\pi^{2M}}A_{W}(\vec{\alpha}_{0})B_{W}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{0}+\frac{\vec{\eta}}{2}\right)e^{(\vec{\alpha}-\vec{\alpha}_{0})\cdot\vec{\eta}^{*}-(\vec{\alpha}^{*}-\vec{\alpha}^{*}_{0})\cdot\vec{\eta}}

(97)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{\alpha}_{0}d^{2}\vec{\eta}}{\pi^{2M}}A_{W}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{0}-\frac{\vec{\eta}}{2}\right)B_{W}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{0}\right)e^{(\vec{\alpha}-\vec{\alpha}_{0})\cdot\vec{\eta}^{*}-(\vec{\alpha}^{*}-\vec{\alpha}^{*}_{0})\cdot\vec{\eta}}.

(98)

Following the same procedure in Secs.
4.1
and
4.2
with Eqs. (
96
), (
97
), and (
98
), we can straightforwardly formulate the path-integral representation of GKSL equation for a multi-mode system as

\displaystyle W(\vec{\alpha}_{\rm f},t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\prod_{j=0}^{N_{t}-1}\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{\alpha}_{j}d^{2}\vec{\eta}_{j+1}}{\pi^{2M}}e^{i\Delta ts_{j}/\hbar}W(\vec{\alpha}_{0},t_{0}),

(99)

\displaystyle s_{j}=i\hbar\left\{\vec{\eta}_{j+1}\cdot\Biggl(\frac{\vec{\alpha}_{j+1}^{*}-\vec{\alpha}_{j}^{*}}{\Delta t}\Biggr)-\vec{\eta}_{j+1}^{*}\cdot\left(\frac{\vec{\alpha}_{j+1}-\vec{\alpha}_{j}}{\Delta t}\right)\right\}+\sum_{n=0,1}(-1)^{n}H_{W}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{j}+(-1)^{n}\frac{\vec{\eta}_{j+1}}{2}\right)-i\hbar\mathfrak{L}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{j}+\frac{\vec{\eta}_{j+1}}{2},\vec{\alpha}_{j}-\frac{\vec{\eta}_{j+1}}{2}\right),

(100)

where
\cdot
indicates the inner product,
\vec{\alpha}_{j}
is the classical field vector,
\vec{\eta}_{j+1}
is the quantum field vector, and the non-unitary term
\mathfrak{L}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta})=\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left\{L^{\ast}_{kW}\left(\vec{\alpha}\right)\star^{e}L_{kW}(\vec{\beta})-\frac{1}{2}L^{\ast}_{kW}\left(\vec{\alpha}\right)\star L_{kW}\left(\vec{\alpha}\right)-\frac{1}{2}L^{\ast}_{kW}(\vec{\beta})\star L_{kW}(\vec{\beta})\right\}.

(101)

Here, the Moyal product
\star
and the extended Moyal product
\star^{e}
for a multi-mode system are defined by

\displaystyle A_{W}(\vec{\alpha})\star B_{W}(\vec{\alpha})

\displaystyle=A_{W}(\vec{\alpha}){\rm exp}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\left(\frac{\overleftarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha_{m}}\frac{\overrightarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}-\frac{\overleftarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}\frac{\overrightarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha_{m}}\right)\right\}B_{W}(\vec{\alpha}),

(102)

\displaystyle A_{W}(\vec{\alpha})\star^{e}B_{W}(\vec{\beta})

\displaystyle=A_{W}(\vec{\alpha}){\rm exp}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\left(\frac{\overleftarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha_{m}}\frac{\overrightarrow{\partial}}{\partial\beta_{m}^{*}}-\frac{\overleftarrow{\partial}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}\frac{\overrightarrow{\partial}}{\partial\beta_{m}}\right)\right\}B_{W}(\vec{\beta}).

(103)

In the continuous limit, Eq. (
99
) can be formally expressed as

\displaystyle W(\vec{\alpha},t)=\int\mathscr{D}^{2}\vec{\alpha}\mathscr{D}^{2}\vec{\eta}e^{iS[\vec{\alpha},\vec{\eta}]/\hbar}W(\vec{\alpha}_{0},t_{0}),

(104)

\displaystyle S\left[\vec{\alpha},\vec{\eta}\right]=\int^{t}_{t_{0}}d\tau\left\{i\hbar\left(\vec{\eta}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\alpha}^{*}}{\partial\tau}-\vec{\eta}^{*}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\alpha}}{\partial\tau}\right)+\sum_{n=0,1}(-1)^{n}H_{W}\left(\vec{\alpha}+(-1)^{n}\frac{\vec{\eta}}{2}\right)-i\hbar\mathfrak{L}\left(\vec{\alpha}+\frac{\vec{\eta}}{2},\vec{\alpha}-\frac{\vec{\eta}}{2}\right)\right\},

(105)

where
S[\vec{\alpha},\vec{\eta}]
is the action.

Within the first order of the quantum fluctuations, the GKSL equation is approximated into the following generalized Liouville equation for the Wigner function:

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{dW(\vec{\alpha},t)}{dt}=-\sum_{m}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{m}}\left[\left\{\frac{\partial H_{W}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left(L^{\ast}_{kW}\star\frac{\partial L_{kW}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}-\frac{\partial L^{\ast}_{kW}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}\star L_{kW}\right)\right\}W(\vec{\alpha},t)\right]\right)+{\rm c.c.},

(106)

which can be derived by the same procedures in Sec.
4.3.2
.
In order to solve this generalized Liouville equation, we need to calculate the classical equation of motion, which is given by

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d\alpha_{m}}{dt}=\frac{\partial H_{W}(\vec{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left\{L^{\ast}_{kW}(\vec{\alpha})\star\frac{\partial L_{kW}(\vec{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}-\frac{\partial L^{\ast}_{kW}(\vec{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}\star L_{kW}(\vec{\alpha})\right\}.

(107)

We can derive the same equation of motion by following the same procedures in Sec.
4.3.1
by using the action in Eq. (
105
).
Then, according to the discussion below Eq. (
61
), we can calculate the physical quantity by iteratively solving the classical equations of motion (
107
) with respect to
\alpha_{m}
for
\forall m
.

The extension of the second-order calculation is not straightforward because it is intractable to perform the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation analytically for a general multi-mode system. However, the transformation becomes tractable when we assume that the jump operators are local, i.e., each
L_{kW}(\vec{\alpha})
involves only one component of
\vec{\alpha}
and the jump operators do not induce transitions of bosons between different modes.
Under this assumption, we rewrite the subscript
k
of
L_{kW}(\vec{\alpha})
with
k,m
to indicate that
L_{k,mW}(\vec{\alpha})
is a function of only
\alpha_{m}
.
Then, we can straightforwardly extends the results in Sec.
4.3.3
to the system with multiple degrees of freedom and obtain the following Fokker-Planck equation:

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{dW\left(\vec{\alpha},t\right)}{dt}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{m}}\left[\left\{\frac{\partial H_{W}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k,m}\left(L^{\ast}_{k,mW}\star\frac{\partial L_{k,mW}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}-\frac{\partial L^{\ast}_{k,mW}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}\star L_{k,mW}\right)\right\}W\left(\vec{\alpha},t\right)\right]+{\rm c.c.}

\displaystyle-i\hbar\sum_{m}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{2}}\left[\lambda_{m}W\left(\vec{\alpha},t\right)\right]-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{m}\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}\left[\Lambda_{m}W\left(\vec{\alpha},t\right)\right]\right)-{\rm c.c.},

(108)

where
\lambda_{m}=\lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})\in\mathbb{C}
and
\Lambda_{m}=\Lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})\in\mathbb{R}
are given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{\gamma_{k,m}}{2}\frac{\partial L^{*}_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{m}}\star\frac{\partial L_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{m}},

(109)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{\gamma_{k,m}}{4}\left\{\frac{\partial L^{*}_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}\star\frac{\partial L_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})}{\partial\alpha_{m}}+\frac{\partial L^{*}_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})}{\partial\alpha_{m}}\star\frac{\partial L_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})}{\partial\alpha_{m}^{*}}\right\}.

(110)

The corresponding stochastic differential equation is given by

\displaystyle i\hbar d\alpha_{m}(t)=\left[\frac{\partial H_{W}(\vec{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{m}}+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k,m}\left\{L^{*}_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})\star\frac{\partial L_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{m}}-\frac{\partial L^{*}_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})}{\partial\alpha^{*}_{m}}\star L_{k,mW}(\alpha_{m})\right\}\right]dt+i\hbar e^{i\theta_{m}(\alpha_{m})/2}\cdot d\Xi_{m}(\alpha_{m}),

(111)

where
\theta_{m}
is defined by
\theta_{m}={\rm arg}(\lambda_{m})
and the complex stochastic process
\Xi_{m}(\alpha_{m})
has Gaussian increments with mean 0:

\displaystyle P\left[\Delta\Xi_{m}^{\rm re}(\alpha_{m})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})-|\lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})|\right)}}{\rm exp}\left[-\frac{\Bigl(\Delta\Xi_{m}^{\rm re}(\alpha_{m})\Bigr)^{2}}{2\Delta t\left(\Lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})-|\lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})|\right)}\right],

(112)

\displaystyle P\left[\Delta\Xi_{m}^{\rm im}(\alpha_{m})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})+|\lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})|\right)}}{\rm exp}\left[-\frac{\Bigl(\Delta\Xi_{m}^{\rm im}(\alpha_{m})\Bigr)^{2}}{2\Delta t\left(\Lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})+|\lambda_{m}(\alpha_{m})|\right)}\right].

(113)

Then, following the procedure below Eq. (
86
), we can calculate the physical quantity by iteratively solving the stochastic differential equation (
111
) with respect to
\alpha_{m}
for
\forall m
.

In the second-order calculation, we assume that the jump operators are local because otherwise, we cannot analytically perform the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation.
However, when we adopt to numerically diagonalize the quadratic terms, we can extend our theory to the system with the general jump operators, which will be published elsewhere.

4.5
Non-equal time correlation functions

The detailed expressions of the propagator of the Wigner function enables us to calculate the non-equal time correlation functions within the first- and second-order quantum fluctuations.
Here, we first consider a system with a single degree of freedom, and subsequently extend the results to a multi-mode system.
The non-equal two-time correlation is defined by
[
1
]

\displaystyle\Braket{\hat{A}(t)\hat{B}(t_{0})}={\rm Tr}\left[\hat{A}\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t,t_{0})\hat{B}\hat{\rho}(t_{0})\right]\quad t_{0}\leq t,

(114)

where
\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t,t_{0})
is defined by Eq. (
1
).
In the phase space, Eq. (
114
) becomes

\displaystyle\Braket{\hat{A}(t)\hat{B}(t_{0})}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{\rm f}}{\pi}A_{W}(\alpha_{\rm f})\left[\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t,t_{0})\hat{B}\hat{\rho}(t_{0})\right]_{W}(\alpha_{\rm f})

(115)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{\rm f}d^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\pi^{2}}A_{W}(\alpha_{\rm f})\varUpsilon\left(\alpha_{\rm f},t~;\alpha_{0},t_{0}\right)\Bigl[B_{W}(\alpha_{0})\star W(\alpha_{0},t_{0})\Bigr],

(116)

where we use Eq. (
9
) to transform Eq. (
114
) into Eq. (
115
).
In order to obtain Eq. (
116
), we follow the same procedure as to obtain Eq. (
20
).
Equation (
116
) is a general phase space representation of the non-equal two-time correlation function.
In the same way, the non-equal three-time correlation is defined by

\displaystyle\Braket{\hat{A}(t)\hat{B}(t_{j})\hat{C}(t_{0})}={\rm Tr}\left[\hat{A}\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t,t_{j})\hat{B}\hat{\mathcal{V}}(t_{j},t_{0})\hat{C}\hat{\rho}(t_{0})\right]\quad t_{0}\leq t_{j}\leq t,

(117)

and its phase space representation is given by

\displaystyle\Braket{\hat{A}(t)\hat{B}(t_{j})\hat{C}(t_{0})}=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{\rm f}d^{2}\alpha_{j}d^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\pi^{4}}A_{W}(\alpha_{\rm f})\varUpsilon(\alpha_{\rm f},t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})B_{W}(\alpha_{j})\star\left[\varUpsilon(\alpha_{j},t_{j}~;\alpha_{0},t_{0})\biggl\{C_{W}(\alpha_{0})\star W(\alpha_{0},t_{0})\biggr\}\right].

(118)

In a similar manner, we can obtain the phase space representation of a non-equal multi-time correlation function for a product of an arbitrary number of operators.

Now we focus on the non-equal two-time correlation function for the Markoian open quantum system.
By applying the Markov condition Eq. (
22
) to Eq. (
116
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Braket{\hat{A}(t)\hat{B}(t_{0})}=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{\rm f}}{\pi}A_{W}(\alpha_{\rm f})\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\prod_{j=0}^{N_{t}-1}\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}}{\pi}\varUpsilon(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})\Bigl[B_{W}(\alpha_{0})\star W(\alpha_{0},t_{0})\Bigr].

(119)

This is a general expression of the non-equal two-time correlation under the Markov condition.
As a specific case, we choose
\hat{B}=\hat{a}
and assume the initial state is given by a pure coherent state
\hat{\rho}(t_{0})=\ket{\alpha_{\rm I}}\bra{\alpha_{\rm I}}
, whose corresponding Wigner function is a two-dimensional Gaussian function
W(\alpha,t_{0})=2e^{-2|\alpha-\alpha_{\rm I}|^{2}}
.
Then, Eq. (
119
) becomes

\displaystyle\Braket{\hat{A}(t)\hat{a}(t_{0})}=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{\rm f}}{\pi}\alpha_{\rm I}A_{W}(\alpha_{\rm f})\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\prod_{j=0}^{N_{t}-1}\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{j}}{\pi}\varUpsilon(\alpha_{j+1},t_{j}+\Delta t~;\alpha_{j},t_{j})W(\alpha_{0},t_{0}).

(120)

Using the analytical expressions of the propagator in Eqs. (
59
) and (
79
), we can approximately calculate the non-equal two-time correlation function Eq. (
120
).
For example, within the first-order [second-order] quantum fluctuations, we first solve the equation of motion of
\alpha
, Eq. (
52
) [Eq. (
81
)] iteratively with different initial conditions sampled from the initial Wigner function
W(\alpha_{0},t_{0})
and calculate the value
\alpha_{\rm I}A_{W}(\alpha_{\rm f})
.
Then, we can calculate Eq. (
120
) by taking an ensemble average of the results.
In particular, when we choose
\hat{A}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, Eq. (
120
) gives the time-normally ordered correlation function.
Here, we note that when we choose
\gamma_{k}=0
for
\forall k
,
the TWA of Eq. (
120
) reduces to the one for an isolated system
[
42
]
.

The extension to the system with the multiple degrees of freedom is straightforward.
Here, we consider the two-time correlation function.
As in the case of Eq. (
120
), we choose
\hat{B}=\hat{a}_{m}
(
m=1,2,\cdots,M
) and assume the initial density matrix
\hat{\rho}(t_{0})=\bigotimes_{m}|\alpha_{{\rm I}m}\rangle\langle\alpha_{{\rm I}m}|
where
\hat{a}_{m}|\alpha_{{\rm I}m}\rangle=\alpha_{{\rm I}m}|\alpha_{{\rm I}m}\rangle
.
The corresponding initial Wigner function is a Gaussian function
\prod_{m}2e^{-2|\alpha_{m}-\alpha_{{\rm I}m}|^{2}}
.
The resulting two-time correlation function
\braket{\hat{A}(t)\hat{a}_{m}(t_{0})}
is given by the right-hand side of Eq. (
120
) with replacing
\alpha_{\rm I}
to
\alpha_{{\rm I}m}
and the other
\alpha
’s to
\vec{\alpha}
, which is numerically evaluated by solving the equation of motion of
\alpha_{m}
for
\forall m
according to Eq. (
107
) (first order) or Eq. (
111
) (second order) and taking an ensemble average of the results weighted by the initial Wigner function. This procedure enables us to calculate, e.g., the one-body non-equal time correlation
\braket{\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{a}_{n}(t_{0})}
.

6
Discussions

In the benchmark calculation, all the approximations reasonably agree with the exact one in Sec.
5.1
, whereas only the calculation with the Fokker-Plank equation well reproduces the exact result in Sec.
5.2
.
The difference comes from the Hermiticity of the jump operators. In this section, we show that the effects of the second-order quantum fluctuations become indispensable in determining relaxation dynamics when the system includes a Hermitian jump operator
\hat{L}=\hat{L}^{\dagger}
, i.e.,
L_{W}(\alpha)=L^{*}_{W}(\alpha)
.
For simplicity, we consider a system with a single degree of freedom and only one jump operator.

Let us suppose that the initial state is set to be a pure coherent state with the mean number of particles
N_{\rm I}
.
In Secs.
5.1
and
5.2
, the strengths of many-body interactions and jumps are properly scaled so that they do not vanish or diverge in the thermodynamic limit
N_{\rm I}\to\infty
:
Since
|\alpha|^{2}\sim O(N_{\rm I})
, the order of the classical field
\alpha
and that of the left-hand side of Eq. (
81
) are
O(\sqrt{N_{\rm I}})
;
It follows that the right-hand side of Eq. (
81
) should be
O(\sqrt{N_{\rm I}})
;
Suppose the jump operator is a
n
-body operator with
2n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, i.e.,
\hat{L}
is constructed with
2n
products of
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{a}
,
\gamma
should be scaled such that
\gamma N_{\rm I}^{2n-1}
is a constant in the limit of
N_{\rm I}\to\infty
, where we impose no restriction on
\hat{L}
and consider a general
\hat{L}
.
Taking the constant as
\Gamma
,
we can rewrite Eq. (
81
) as

\displaystyle i\hbar d\alpha=\left[\frac{\partial H_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}+\frac{i\hbar\Gamma}{N^{2n-1}_{\rm I}}\left\{L^{\ast}_{W}(\alpha)\star\frac{\partial L_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}-\frac{\partial L^{\ast}_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\star L_{W}(\alpha)\right\}\right]dt+i\hbar e^{i\theta(\alpha)/2}\cdot d\Xi(\alpha).

(145)

where we scale the strengths of the many-body interaction in
\hat{H}
such that the order of
H_{W}(\alpha)
is
O(N_{\rm I})
, and
\lambda(\alpha)
and
\Lambda(\alpha)
in the complex stochastic term are given by Eqs. (
69
) and (
70
) with
\gamma=\Gamma/N_{\rm I}^{2n-1}
.
The benchmark calculations in Secs.
5.1
and
5.2
correspond to the case of
n=1
in Eq. (
145
).
When the jump operator is non-Hermitian, while the order of the left-hand side and the classical-path term in the right-hand of Eq. (
145
) is
O(\sqrt{N_{\rm I}})
, the order of the stochastic term is
O(d\Xi)=O(\sqrt{\lambda\pm\Lambda})=O(1)
.
Hence, the classical path dominates the relaxation dynamics resulting that the classical equation of motion well reproduces the exact dynamics as in Sec.
5.1
.
On the other hand, when the jump operator is Hermitian, Eq. (
145
) becomes

\displaystyle i\hbar d\alpha=\left[\frac{\partial H_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}+\frac{i\hbar\Gamma}{2N^{2n-1}_{\rm I}}L_{W}(\alpha){\rm sinh}\frac{\hat{\phi}}{2}\frac{\partial L_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\right]dt+i\hbar e^{i\theta(\alpha)/2}\cdot d\Xi(\alpha),

(146)

where the differential operator
\hat{\phi}
is defined by Eq. (
15
).
Because the leading order of
{\rm sinh}\hat{\phi}/2
is
\hat{\phi}\sim O(N_{\rm I}^{-1})
, the non-unitary term of the classical path is in the order
O(N_{\rm I}^{-1/2})
, which is smaller than that of the stochastic term
O(1)
.
This means that the non-unitary dynamics of the GKSL equation is strongly affected by the stochastic term, and hence, the classical equation of motion fails to reproduce the exact dynamics as shown in Sec.
5.2
.

When we consider the thermodynamic limit in Eq. (
146
), since the non-unitary term of the classical-path term and the stochastic term become negligible, Eq. (
146
) is reduced to the Hamilton equation and fails to describe the non-unitary dynamics in the thermodynamic limit.
In order to capture the non-unitary dynamics of the GKSL equation with a Hermitian jump operator in the thermodynamic limit, we need to rescale
\gamma
such that
\tilde{\Gamma}=\gamma N_{\rm I}^{2n-2}
becomes a constant, in which Eq. (
146
) becomes

\displaystyle i\hbar d\alpha=\left[\frac{\partial H_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}+\frac{i\hbar\tilde{\Gamma}}{2N^{2n-2}_{\rm I}}L_{W}(\alpha){\rm sinh}\frac{\hat{\phi}}{2}\frac{\partial L_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}\right]dt+i\hbar e^{i\theta(\alpha)/2}\cdot d\Xi(\alpha),

(147)

where all the terms on the right-hand side, including the stochastic term, have the same order
O(\sqrt{N_{\rm I}})
.
We note that the above argument is valid only for
n\geq 1
.
In the case of
n=1/2
, where the jump operator is linear with respect to
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, the non-unitary term in the classical path becomes completely zero and thus the non-unitary effect comes only from the second order of quantum fluctuations.
To summarize, the Hermitian jump operator induces non-negligible effects of the second order of quantum fluctuations on the relaxation dynamics.
This indicates that neglecting quantum fluctuations (mean-field approximation) is not appropriate for simulating the dynamics of the GKSL equation with Hermitian jump operators.
Below, we introduce two examples that clearly show the necessity of the stochastic term.

The first example is the jump operator
\hat{L}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
,
which is the case of
n=1
in Eq. (
147
).
Under the assumption
[\hat{H},\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}]_{-}=0
, the total number of particles
N=\braket{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}
is preserved throughout the time evolution.
However, the classical path for this situation is analytically solved as

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d\alpha(t)}{dt}=\frac{\partial H_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}-\frac{i\hbar\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}\alpha(t)\quad\longrightarrow\quad N(t)=|\alpha(t_{0})|^{2}e^{-\tilde{\Gamma}(t-t_{0})}-\frac{1}{2},

(148)

and the total number of particles is not a conserved quantity but exponentially decreases with decay time
1/\tilde{\Gamma}
.
To deal with the dynamics while preserving the total number of particles, we need to consider the second-order quantum fluctuations.
We can analytically prove the conservation law from the stochastic differential equation:

\displaystyle i\hbar d\alpha(t)=\left[\frac{\partial H_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}-\frac{i\hbar\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}\alpha(t)\right]dt+i\hbar e^{i\theta(\alpha)/2}\cdot d\Xi(\alpha)\quad\longrightarrow\quad N(t)=N(t_{0}),

(149)

where
\theta(\alpha)
is given by
\theta(\alpha)={\rm arg}(\alpha^{2})
, and the complex stochastic process is purely imaginary with the increments following the Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and variance
\Delta t\tilde{\Gamma}|\alpha|^{2}
:

\displaystyle P\left[\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}(\alpha)\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\tilde{\Gamma}|\alpha|^{2}}}{\rm exp}\left[-\frac{\Bigl(\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}(\alpha)\Bigr)^{2}}{2\Delta t\tilde{\Gamma}|\alpha|^{2}}\right].

(150)

This result indicates that the quantum fluctuations play an important rule to determine the relaxation dynamics of the system with the jump operator
\hat{L}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
.

The second example is the jump operator
\hat{L}=c_{x}\hat{x}+c_{p}\hat{p}
with
c_{x},c_{p}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\hat{x}=(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}
and
\hat{p}=i(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a})/\sqrt{2}
.
This is the case of
n=1/2
in Eq. (
147
), where the classical path has zero non-unitary term.
The corresponding stochastic differential equation is given by

\displaystyle i\hbar d\alpha(t)=\frac{\partial H_{W}(\alpha)}{\partial\alpha^{*}}dt+i\hbar\cdot d\Xi(\alpha),

(151)

where the complex stochastic process is purely imaginary with the increments following the Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and variance
N_{\rm I}\tilde{\Gamma}(c_{x}^{2}+c_{y}^{2})/2
:

\displaystyle P\left[\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}(\alpha)\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\Delta t\tilde{\Gamma}(c_{x}^{2}+c_{y}^{2})}}{\rm exp}\left[-\frac{\Bigl(\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}(\alpha)\Bigr)^{2}}{\Delta t\tilde{\Gamma}(c_{x}^{2}+c_{y}^{2})}\right].

(152)

Since the jump operator is linear with respect to
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, there is no non-unitary terms which are higher than third order of quantum fluctuations.
Hence, the jump operator
\hat{L}=c_{x}\hat{x}+c_{p}\hat{p}
affect the dynamics only as the second order of quantum fluctuations, which act on the second-order moment of the Wigner function in the phase space.

We comment that the results in this section have been already obtained in the literature of the semi-classical approximation for the GKSL equation
[
56
]
.
In the semi-classical approximation, the GKSL equation is approximated into the Fokker-Planck equation based on the perturbative expansion with respect to the Planck constant
\hbar
.
Hence, the detailed forms of the Fokker-Planck and stochastic equations differ from those of TWA based on the expansion concerning quantum fields in the path-integral.
However, they become identical in the thermodynamic limit.
This fact ensures the validity of analytical expressions of the stochastic differential equations (
81
) and (
111
).

7
Summary and conclusions

The TWA is an efficient tool to study the quantum dynamics of bosonic many-body systems.
In the TWA, the GKSL equation is approximated into the Fokker-Planck equation for the Wigner function, which we calculate by solving the corresponding stochastic differential equations.
The main result of this work is the derivation of the analytical expressions of the Fokker-Planck equation and stochastic differential equations.
For systems with arbitrary Hamiltonian and the jump operators that do not couple different degrees of freedom, we derived the condition [Eq. (
75
)] for the jump operators so that the Fokker-Planck equation [Eqs. (
91
) and (
108
)] can be reduced to the stochastic differential equations [Eqs. (
81
) and (
111
)].
This result enables us to shortcut the complicated mappings from operators to
c
-numbers in the conventional procedure of the TWA.
We hope that these expressions will become a powerful tool in the practical calculations.

In the course of the derivations, we formulated the path-integral representation of the GKSL equation based on the Weyl-Wigner transformation [Eqs. (
41
) and (
99
)], which takes a form of a natural extension from the path-integral representation for isolated systems
[
25
,
42
]
.
The path-integral representation is characterized by the classical and quantum fields describing the classical motion and quantum fluctuations of systems, respectively.
To obtain the general expressions of the Fokker-Planck equation and stochastic differential equations, we assume small quantum fluctuations and expand the action with respect to the quantum field order by order.
Within the first-order quantum fluctuations, the GKSL equation is approximated into the generalized Liouville equation [Eqs. (
66
) and (
106
)], where each point in the phase space on which the initial Wigner function distributes follows the classical equation of motion [Eqs. (
52
) and (
107
)].
The second-order fluctuations introduce stochastic processes into the classical motion, resulting in the Fokker-Planck equation and stochastic differential equations.
Following Ref.
[
42
]
, we also provided the formula for calculating the non-equal time correlation functions described by the GKSL equation by using the TWA.
In the benchmark calculations, we numerically investigated the relaxation dynamics of some observables and non-equal time correlation functions for two models.
Although there is a clear discrepancy between the first-order calculation and the exact one, the second-order calculation agrees well with the exact one.
These results confirm the validity of our formulation of the TWA for the GKSL equation.

In the last part of this paper, we discussed the relaxation dynamics of systems with Hermitian jump operators.
In systems with non-Hermitian jump operators, the effect of the second-order of quantum fluctuations is small and being negligible in the thermodynamic limit, and thus, the dynamics is well reproduced by the classical equation of motion.
On the other hand in systems with Hermitian jump operators, we saw that the second-order quantum fluctuations have non-negligible contributions to the dynamics even in the thermodynamic limit.
In particular, when jump operators include only the linear terms of the creation and annihilation operators, they do not modify the classical path, i.e., within the first-order approximation we obtain the same dynamics as the corresponding isolated system. The effect of the jump operators appears first in the second-order approximation as the stochastic process.
Our result indicates that neglecting quantum fluctuations (mean-field approximation) is not appropriate for simulating the quantum dynamics with Hermitian jump operators.

There are several remaining issues.
One is to remove the restriction on the jump operators for the case with multiple degrees of freedom. As discussed in Sec.
4.4
, we introduced the restriction that jump operators do not mix the different degrees of freedom to analytically diagonalize the diffusion matrix. If we numerically diagonalize the diffusion matrix while solving the stochastic differential equation, we expect it will become possible to deal with more general jump operators.
Another issue is to clarify the correspondence between our approach and the Schwinger-Keldysh path-integral approach.
The Schwinger-Keldysh path-integral rerepsentation for the GKSL equation has been obtained in literatures of the Keldysh field theory for the Markovian open quantum systems
[
57
,
58
]
.
However, their action and the distribution function are different from ours.
This is due to the difference in the phase-space representation used in the path integrals:
The Keldysh field theory is for systems written in normal-ordered operators, while our formulation is for systems written in Weyl-ordered operators.
Since these differently ordered systems are transferred with each other by commutation relations, their path integral representations are physically equivalent.
This point is also noted in Ref.
[
59
]
.
We are working on these issues on-going.

\displaystyle\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\hat{C}\right]_{W}(\alpha)=\sum_{p,q=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!q!}

\displaystyle\left.\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{0}d^{2}\xi d^{2}\eta}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta^{*}(\alpha-\alpha_{0})-\eta(\alpha^{*}-\alpha^{*}_{0})}C_{W}\left(\alpha_{0}+\frac{\xi}{2}\right)\frac{\partial^{p+q}A_{W}(\sigma_{a})}{\partial\sigma_{a}^{p}\partial\sigma_{a}^{*q}}\right|_{\sigma_{a}=\alpha_{0}-\eta/2}B_{W}(\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle\times\int\frac{d^{2}\delta\zeta}{\pi^{2}}\delta\zeta^{p}\delta\zeta^{*q}e^{\delta\zeta^{*}(\eta-\xi)-\delta\zeta(\eta^{*}-\xi^{*})}

(172)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\hat{C}\right]_W(\alpha)}=\sum_{p,q=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{q}}{p!q!}

\displaystyle\left.\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{0}d^{2}\xi d^{2}\eta}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta^{*}(\alpha-\alpha_{0})-\eta(\alpha^{*}-\alpha^{*}_{0})}C_{W}\left(\alpha_{0}+\frac{\xi}{2}\right)\frac{\partial^{p+q}A_{W}(\sigma_{a})}{\partial\sigma_{a}^{p}\partial\sigma_{a}^{*q}}\right|_{\sigma_{a}=\alpha_{0}-\eta/2}B_{W}(\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle\times\frac{\partial^{p+q}}{\partial\xi^{*p}\partial\xi^{q}}\int\frac{d^{2}\delta\zeta}{\pi^{2}}e^{\delta\zeta^{*}(\eta-\xi)-\delta\zeta(\eta^{*}-\xi^{*})}

(173)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\hat{C}\right]_W(\alpha)}=\sum_{p,q=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{q}}{p!q!}

\displaystyle\left.\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{0}d^{2}\xi d^{2}\eta}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta^{*}(\alpha-\alpha_{0})-\eta(\alpha^{*}-\alpha^{*}_{0})}C_{W}\left(\alpha_{0}+\frac{\xi}{2}\right)\frac{\partial^{p+q}A_{W}(\sigma_{a})}{\partial\sigma_{a}^{p}\partial\sigma_{a}^{*q}}\right|_{\sigma_{a}=\alpha_{0}-\eta/2}B_{W}(\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle\times\frac{\partial^{p+q}}{\partial\xi^{*p}\partial\xi^{q}}\delta^{(2)}\left(\eta-\xi\right),

(174)

where we have used Eq. (
36
) in the last line.
Finally, performing integration by parts
p+q
times with respect to
\xi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\hat{C}\right]_{W}(\alpha)=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{0}d^{2}\xi d^{2}\eta}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta^{*}(\alpha-\alpha_{0})-\eta(\alpha^{*}-\alpha^{*}_{0})}\sum_{p,q=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{p}}{2^{p+q}p!q!}\left\{\left.\frac{\partial^{p+q}C_{W}\left(\sigma_{c}\right)}{\partial\sigma_{c}^{*p}\partial\sigma_{c}^{q}}\right|_{\sigma_{c}=\alpha_{0}+\eta/2}\left.\frac{\partial^{p+q}A_{W}(\sigma_{a})}{\partial\sigma_{a}^{p}\partial\sigma_{a}^{*q}}\right|_{\sigma_{a}=\alpha_{0}-\eta/2}\right\}B_{W}(\alpha_{0})

(175)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\left[\hat{A}\hat{B}\hat{C}\right]_W(\alpha)}=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{0}d^{2}\eta}{\pi^{2}}e^{\eta^{*}(\alpha-\alpha_{0})-\eta(\alpha^{*}-\alpha_{0}^{*})}C_{W}\left(\alpha_{0}+\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\star^{e}A_{W}\left(\alpha_{0}-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)B_{W}(\alpha_{0}),

(176)

where
\star^{e}
is the extended Moyal product defined by Eq. (
32
).

Appendix C
Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation: Derivation of Eqs. (
73
) and (
76
)

C.1
Phase-space Gaussian integral

We first derive the following Gaussian integral in the phase space necessary for deriving Eqs. (
73
) and (
76
):

\displaystyle{\rm exp}\left(-\left[\eta^{*},\eta\right]\bm{A}\begin{bmatrix}\eta\\
\eta^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{det}\bm{A}}}\int\frac{d^{2}\xi}{\pi}{\rm exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\xi^{*},\xi\right]\bm{A}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\xi\\
\xi^{*}\end{bmatrix}+\sqrt{2}i\left(\eta^{*}\xi+\eta\xi^{*}\right)\right),

(177)

where
\bm{A}
is a
2\times 2
positive-definite matrix and
\eta
is a complex value.
In the following, we calculate the right-hand side and show it agrees with the left-hand side.
For this purpose, we introduce the transformation matrix
\bm{V}
as

\displaystyle\bm{V}=\begin{bmatrix}1&i\\
1&-i\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{V}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
-i&i\end{bmatrix},

(178)

which acts on the vector
[\xi,\xi^{*}]^{\rm T}
as

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\xi\\
\xi^{*}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{\rm re}\\
\xi^{\rm im}\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{*},\xi\end{bmatrix}\bm{V}=2\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{\rm re},\xi^{\rm im}\end{bmatrix}.

(179)

Substituting the
2\times 2
identity matrix
\bm{V}\bm{V}^{-1}=\bm{1}
into both side of
\bm{A}^{-1}
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
177
) and using the fact that
\eta^{*}\xi+\eta\xi^{*}=[\eta^{\rm re},\eta^{\rm im}][\xi^{\rm re},\xi^{\rm im}]^{\rm T}
, we obtain

RHS of Eq. (
177
)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\text{det}\bm{A}}}\int\frac{d^{2}\xi}{2\pi}{\rm exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\xi^{\rm re},\xi^{\rm im}\right]2\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{V}\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{\rm re}\\
\xi^{\rm im}\end{bmatrix}+2\sqrt{2}i\begin{bmatrix}\eta^{\rm re},\eta^{\rm im}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{\rm re}\\
\xi^{\rm im}\end{bmatrix}\right).

(180)

Here, we can perform the integral by using the multiple-variables Gaussian integral formula:

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}\xi}{2\pi}~{\rm exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\xi^{\rm re},\xi^{\rm im}\right]\bm{X}\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{\rm re}\\
\xi^{\rm im}\end{bmatrix}+\left[u,v\right]\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{\rm re}\\
\xi^{\rm im}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{det}\bm{X}}}{\rm exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[u,v\right]\bm{X}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}\right),

(181)

where
\bm{X}
is a
2\times 2
positive-definite matrix and
u
and
v
are complex variables.
Substituting Eq. (
180
) with
\bm{X}=2\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{V}
and
[u,v]=[2\sqrt{2}i\eta^{\rm re},2\sqrt{2}i\eta^{\rm im}]
into the right-hand side of Eq. (
180
), we obtain the left-hand side of Eq. (
177
).

C.2
Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation for
\Lambda>|\lambda|
: Derivation of Eq. (
73
)

In the case of
\Lambda>|\lambda|
, the left-hand side of Eq. (
73
) can be written in the following form:

\displaystyle e^{-2\Lambda\Delta t|\eta|^{2}-\lambda^{*}\Delta t\eta^{2}-\lambda\Delta t\eta^{*2}}={\rm exp}\left(-\left[\eta^{*}\eta\right]\bm{A}\begin{bmatrix}\eta\\
\eta^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(182)

where we have replaced
\eta_{j+1}
with
\eta
for simplicity and defined

\displaystyle\bm{A}=\Delta t\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda&\lambda\\
\lambda^{*}&\Lambda\end{bmatrix}.

(183)

According to Eq. (
177
), when the matrix
\bm{A}
is positive-definite, i.e.,
\Lambda>|\lambda|
, we can transform the right-hand side of Eq. (
182
) into an integral form as

\displaystyle{\rm exp}\left(-\left[\eta^{*}\eta\right]\bm{A}\begin{bmatrix}\eta\\
\eta^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{det}\bm{A}}}\int\frac{d^{2}\xi}{\pi}{\rm exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\xi^{*},\xi\right]\bm{A}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\xi\\
\xi^{*}\end{bmatrix}+\sqrt{2}i\left(\eta^{*}\xi+\eta\xi^{*}\right)\right).

(184)

The matrix
\bm{A}
is diagonalized as

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{-1}\bm{A}\bm{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda-|\lambda|&0\\
0&\Lambda+|\lambda|\end{bmatrix},

(185)

where the unitary matrix
\bm{U}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{U}=\begin{bmatrix}-ie^{i\theta/2}&e^{i\theta/2}\\
ie^{-i\theta/2}&e^{-i\theta/2}\end{bmatrix}

(186)

with
\theta={\rm arg}(\lambda)
.
Inserting the identity matrix
\bm{U}\bm{U}^{-1}=\bm{1}
into the both side of the matrix
\bm{A}^{-1}
in the right-hand side of Eq. (
184
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm exp}\left(-\left[\eta^{*}\eta\right]\bm{A}\begin{bmatrix}\eta\\
\eta^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta t^{2}\left(\Lambda^{2}-|\lambda|^{2}\right)}}\int\frac{d^{2}\xi}{\pi}{\rm exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\left[\xi^{*},\xi\right]\bm{U}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda-|\lambda|&0\\
0&\Lambda+|\lambda|\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\bm{U}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\xi\\
\xi^{*}\end{bmatrix}+\sqrt{2}i\left(\eta^{*}\xi+\eta\xi^{*}\right)\right),

(187)

where we use
\text{det}\bm{A}=\Delta t^{2}(\Lambda^{2}-|\lambda|^{2})
.
Here, we transform the integral variable
\xi
to
\Delta\Xi
such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\bm{U}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\xi\\
\xi^{*}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}\\
\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}\end{bmatrix},

(188)

and
\Delta\Xi=\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}+i\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}
, where
\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}
and
\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}
are always real, and obtaining

\displaystyle{\rm exp}\left(-\Delta t\left[\eta^{*}\eta\right]\bm{A}\begin{bmatrix}\eta\\
\eta^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}\Delta\Xi\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm im})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\rm exp}\left(\eta^{*}e^{i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi-\eta e^{-i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi^{*}\right).

(189)

Finally, by comparing Eqs. (
182
) and (
189
), we obtain

\displaystyle e^{-2\Lambda\Delta t|\eta|^{2}-\lambda\Delta t\eta^{2}-\lambda^{*}\Delta t\eta^{*2}}=\int d^{2}\Delta\Xi\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm im})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda+|\lambda|\right)}}{\rm exp}\left(\eta^{*}e^{i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi-\eta e^{-i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi^{*}\right).

(190)

This completes the derivation of Eq. (
73
).

C.3
Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation for
\Lambda=|\lambda|
: Derivation of Eq. (
76
)

Our starting point is again the left-hand side of Eq. (
182
).
For the case of
\Lambda=|\lambda|
, we can not use Eq. (
177
) because the matrix
\bm{A}
has a zero eigenvalue and breaks the positive-definiteness.
Instead, by noting that the argument of the exponential function on the left-hand side of Eq. (
182
) is always real, given by

-2\Lambda\Delta t|\eta|^{2}-\lambda^{*}\Delta t\eta^{2}-\lambda\Delta t\eta^{*2}=-\Lambda\Delta t(\eta^{*}e^{i\theta/2}+\eta e^{-i\theta/2})^{2}=-\Lambda\Delta t\tilde{\eta}^{2}
,
\tilde{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}
,
we can use the single-variable Gaussian integral formula

\displaystyle e^{-\Lambda\Delta t\tilde{\eta}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\Lambda\Delta t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tilde{\xi}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{\xi}^{2}}{4\Lambda\Delta t}+i\tilde{\eta}\tilde{\xi}\right)

to rewrite the left-hand side of Eq. (
182
).
In particular, denoting
\tilde{\xi}
as
\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}
and recovering the detailed form of
\tilde{\eta}
, we derive Eq. (
76
) as

\displaystyle e^{-2\Lambda\Delta t|\eta|^{2}-\lambda\Delta t\eta^{2}-\lambda^{*}\Delta t\eta^{*2}}=\int d\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re})^{2}/4\Delta t\Lambda}}{\sqrt{4\pi\Delta t\Lambda}}{\rm exp}\left(i\eta^{*}e^{i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}+i\eta e^{-i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}\right).

(191)

We can also derive Eq. (
191
) as the
|\lambda|\to\Lambda
limit of Eq. (
190
).
In this limit, one of the Gaussian functions in the integrand of Eq. (
190
) behaves as the Dirac delta function,

\displaystyle\lim_{|\lambda|\to\Lambda}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re})^{2}/2\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\left(\Lambda-|\lambda|\right)}}=\delta\left(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re}\right).

(192)

We then obtain the
|\lambda|\to\Lambda
limit of Eq. (
190
) as

\displaystyle\lim_{|\lambda|\to\Lambda}e^{-2\Lambda\Delta t|\eta|^{2}-\lambda\Delta t\eta^{2}-\lambda^{*}\Delta t\eta^{*2}}

\displaystyle=\int d\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}\frac{e^{-(\Delta\Xi^{\rm re})^{2}/4\Delta t\Lambda}}{\sqrt{4\pi\Delta t\Lambda}}{\rm exp}\left(i\eta^{*}e^{i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}+i\eta e^{-i\theta/2}\Delta\Xi^{\rm im}\right),

(193)

which is identical to Eq. (
190
).
According to this result, we can conclude that the Hubbard-Storatonovich transformation Eq. (
73
) is feasible for
\Lambda\geq|\lambda|
as in Eq. (
75
).
