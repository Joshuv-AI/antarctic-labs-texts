BOF-UCB: A Bayesian-Optimistic Frequentist Algorithm for Non-Stationary Contextual Bandits PubID: pubid:
Nicklas Werge, Abdullah Akgül, and Melih Kandemir

Abstract
We propose a novel Bayesian-Optimistic Frequentist Upper Confidence Bound ( BOF-UCB ) algorithm for stochastic contextual linear bandits in non-stationary environments. This unique combination of Bayesian and frequentist principles enhances adaptability and performance in dynamic settings. The BOF-UCB algorithm utilizes sequential Bayesian updates to infer the posterior distribution of the unknown regression parameter, and subsequently employs a frequentist approach to compute the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) by maximizing the expected reward over the posterior distribution. We provide theoretical guarantees of BOF-UCB ’s performance and demonstrate its effectiveness in balancing exploration and exploitation on synthetic datasets and classical control tasks in a reinforcement learning setting. Our results show that BOF-UCB outperforms existing methods, making it a promising solution for sequential decision-making in non-stationary environments.

Introduction

Multi-Armed bandit algorithms have emerged as powerful tools for solving sequential decision-making problems in a variety of domains
[
31
,
8
,
26
]
. Contextual bandits and, in particular, contextual linear bandits have gained widespread attention due to their ability to incorporate contextual information to decision-making
[
27
,
13
,
32
,
12
,
1
,
24
]
.

Contextual bandits assume that the rewards associated with actions taken by the agent depend on some observed contextual information; for contextual linear bandits, this relationship between the contextual features and the rewards is modeled as a linear function. This information is often encoded as a feature vector, which is provided to the agent at each round of decision-making. The agent’s goal is to learn a mapping from context to actions that maximizes its cumulative reward.

In many real-world applications, the distribution of rewards may change over time
[
6
]
. For example, the user preferences in a recommendation system may shift over time, or the environment in which a robot operates may change. These changes can lead to a phenomenon known as non-stationarity, where the optimal mapping from context to action changes over time
[
10
]
. Non-stationarity can make it difficult for traditional contextual bandit algorithms to maintain high performance, as they may not be able to adapt to changes in the environment quickly enough.

The Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm is a popular and effective approach for solving multi-armed bandit problems
[
5
,
25
,
4
]
, including contextual linear bandits. It balances exploration and exploitation by selecting actions with high expected reward and high uncertainty, enabling the agent to learn quickly while still exploiting promising actions. Both frequentist UCB-based algorithms (e.g. see
[
1
,
27
,
12
]
) and Bayesian UCB-based algorithms (e.g. see
[
38
,
21
]
) have been proposed for stationary contextual linear bandits. However, these methods have limited applicability in non-stationary environments, where the underlying parameters may change over time.

To address the challenge of non-stationarity, recent studies
[
34
,
10
,
45
,
47
,
41
,
14
]
have explored how current UCB algorithms can handle drifting rewards by using strategies such as sliding window, periodical restart, or decaying weight to eliminate the influence from out-dated observations. The common property of these investigations is that they approach the problem from either purely frequentist
[
34
,
10
,
45
,
41
]
or purely Bayesian perspectives
[
47
,
14
]
.

The frequentist approaches propose policies that operate on confidence sets derived from tail probabilities of the error on point estimates of the reward function parameters. The Bayesian approaches, in turn, infer posterior distributions on reward parameters via sequential belief updates, and derive their confidence sets also from these distributions. While the frequentist approaches ignore epistemic uncertainty during parameter estimation, the Bayesian approaches ignore the asymptotic implications of their beliefs during confidence set construction.

In this work, we propose the first bandit algorithm that combines the best practices of the two mainstream statistical approaches. Our algorithm, called Bayesian-Optimistic Frequentist UCB (
BOF-UCB
) identifies the reward parameters of a stochastic linear bandit by sequential Bayesian updates and acts by applying UCB on a confidence set calculated on these Bayesian update rules according to the frequentist principles. Our particular way of using sequential Bayesian updates makes
BOF-UCB
well suited for non-stationary environments, where the reward function changes over time. Unlike earlier approaches,
BOF-UCB
constructs confidence set expands or shrinks adaptively to the observed rewards and taken actions.

The frequentist confidence set construction method employed in
BOF-UCB
enables us to establish analytical guarantees for its regret performance. Specifically, we can derive bounds on the regret, which quantifies the deviation of the algorithm’s performance from the optimal solution. By leveraging concentration inequalities and carefully controlling the exploration-exploitation trade-off,
BOF-UCB
achieves a regret that grows sublinearly with the number of time steps. In particular, we demonstrate that
BOF-UCB
achieves competitive performance compared to state-of-the-art algorithms in the linear bandit setting.

The aforementioned properties put
BOF-UCB
in a unique position among existing bandit algorithms. The well-established

and its non-stationary version

[
34

fit reward parameters as point estimates, hence are more prone to accumulating error especially in the early stages of training.

[
21
]
is more robust in estimating reward parameters, however it lacks theoretical regret guarantees. It also does not straightforwardly generalize to non-stationary environments. Our
BOF-UCB
stands out as the only algorithm that: i) accounts for the epistemic uncertainty on the reward parameters, ii) provides strong theoretical regret guarantees, iii) handles non-stationary bandit behavior. The qualitative comparison of our

While the achieved asymptotic regret performance of
BOF-UCB
is notable, it is important to consider the assumptions made.
BOF-UCB
relies on assumptions such as sub-Gaussian noise, bounded parameters and context features, and bounded variation-budget. These assumptions are commonly in contextual bandit literature
[
1
,
27
,
12
,
34
,
10
,
45
,
41
]
. However, it is worth exploring the applicability of
BOF-UCB
in settings where these assumptions may not hold, such as non-linear contextual bandits or alternative non-stationarity assumptions.

We evaluate the effectiveness of our
BOF-UCB
on four use cases. The first two are synthetic non-stationary bandit setups, one with abruptly changing and the other with slowly drifting reward function parameters. The other two are classical optimal control tasks;
pendulum
and
cartpole swingup
. To address these classical optimal control tasks, we formulate them as reinforcement learning tasks and develop control policies within a neural-linear bandit framework
[
43
,
46
,
42
]
.
In this framework, the context is generated from the observed environment state using a deep neural network. Policy search is conducted by playing a bandit game that takes the context generated by the neural network as input. The reward function is generated as the Bellman target corresponding to a given state (context) and the action taken. The estimated reward function assumes the role of the action-value function within the reinforcement learning framework. Learning occurs through the following iterative steps at each interaction round: (i) constructing the confidence set based on the current belief regarding the rewards, (ii) selecting actions according to the UCB on this confidence set, (iii) updating the belief based on the observed reward and context information (next environment state), and (iv) updating the neural network parameters based on the observed reward and context information.
Modeling the bandit formulation with a non-stationarity assumption is essential due to two key reasons: (a) the changing state transition distribution of the underlying Markov decision process. In dynamic environments, the relationship between contextual information and subsequent state transitions may evolve, leading to variations in the optimal mapping from context to action. (b) the iterative learning process of the context network. In reinforcement learning, where the context is generated by a deep neural network, the network parameters are continuously updated based on observed rewards and context information. As the network learns and refines its internal representations, the optimal mapping from context to action may shift.
In summary, our
BOF-UCB
algorithm compares favorably in all use cases.

Our proposed
BOF-UCB
algorithm addresses the challenge of non-stationarity in contextual linear bandits, contributing to the growing body of research on contextual bandits and non-stationarity in sequential decision-making. The unique combination of Bayesian and frequentist principles of the
BOF-UCB
algorithm provides a novel solution that improves adaptability and performance in changing environments, expanding the toolbox of dynamic decision-making algorithms.

III
BOF-UCB
Algorithm and its Guarantees

BOF-UCB
is a hybrid algorithm that combines the Bayesian inference with the frequentist framework of using UCB to guide the search. The key idea behind
BOF-UCB
is to use the Bayesian inference to estimate the objective function and the frequentist UCB to guide the search. The Bayesian inference provides a probabilistic estimate of the objective function, which is updated sequentially as new observations are made
[
7
]
. This estimate can be used to identify regions of the search space that are likely to contain the optimum, and to explore these regions more thoroughly. The frequentist UCB, on the other hand, provides a measure of uncertainty around the estimate. By choosing points with high UCB, the algorithm can explore regions of the search space where the uncertainty is high, which can help to identify the optimum more quickly.

The objective function used in
BOF-UCB
is the weighted least-squares estimator, which can be updated sequentially as new observations are made. Inspired by
[
34
]
, this estimator is defined with time-dependent (positive) weights
\{w_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
, allowing the algorithm to adapt to changes in the environment; both predictable from the past observations (i.e. actions and rewards). The objective function can be written as follows:

\hat{\theta}_{t}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}w_{i}(y_{i}-\langle\theta,x_{i}\rangle)^{2}\right).

(4)

In the contextual bandit literature, it is common to assume that the noise
\{\eta_{t}\}
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian, i.e.
1
. To facilitate the sequential Bayesian inference of the weighted least-squares estimate, we suppose that the noise sequence
\{\eta_{t}\}
is Gaussian with a known variance
\sigma^{2}>0
.

Assumption 4
.

The noise sequence
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
is centered Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}>0
.

By assuming the Gaussian noise in
4
, we can define the likelihood function
p(y_{t}|x_{t},\theta)
as a Gaussian distribution, given by
p(y_{t}|x_{t},\theta)=\mathcal{N}(y_{t}|\langle\theta,x_{t}\rangle,(\sigma^{2}/w_{t})\mathbb{I}_{d})

[
7
]
; here
\mathcal{N}(\cdot|\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Gaussian density function, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}(y|\mu,\sigma^{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp(-\frac{(y-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}})
.

To proceed with Bayesian inference, we need to specify a prior distribution. A common choice is to assume a Gaussian prior distribution
\pi(\theta)
with mean vector
\mu_{0}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma_{0}
, as it enables tractable posterior computations.
3
3

3

An alternative approach would be to model the joint distribution of
(\theta,\sigma^{2})
for each action, rather than just
\theta
as in this work. This would enable sequential estimation of
\sigma^{2}
for each action, allowing the algorithm to adaptively refine its estimates of
\theta

[
7
]
. However, we leave this topic for further exploration by interested readers or future work.
Using these assumptions, we can update the posterior distribution of
\theta
sequentially as new observations become available, denoted by
\rho_{t}(\theta)=\rho_{t}(\theta|y_{1:t},x_{1:t})
. The posterior distribution
\rho_{t}(\theta)
also takes a Gaussian form, which can be computed using Bayes’ theorem
[
7
]
. Specifically, the posterior distribution
\rho_{t}(\theta)
can be expressed as a product of Gaussian distributions using the Gaussian likelihood function and Gaussian prior, and yields a tractable update formula for
\rho_{t}(\theta)
. Specifically, the posterior distribution
\rho_{t}(\theta)
is Gaussian with mean vector
\mu_{t}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma_{t}
, given by

\mu_{t}=\Sigma_{t}\left(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t}\right),

(5)

and

\Sigma_{t}^{-1}=\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t},

(6)

where
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is a matrix whose rows are the vectors
x_{1}^{\top},\dots,x_{t}^{\top}
,
\mathbf{w}_{t}
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
w_{1},\dots,w_{t}
,
\mathbf{y}_{t}=[y_{1},\dots,y_{t}]^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}=[\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{t}]^{\top}
. To update
Equations

5
and
6
sequentially as new observations come in, we can use the following update rules:

\mu_{t}=\Sigma_{t}\left(\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}\mu_{t-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}w_{t}x_{t}y_{t}\right),

and

\Sigma_{t}^{-1}=\Sigma_{t-1}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}w_{t}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}.

This sequential update scheme eliminates the need to store past observations and reduces computations.

As new observations are collected, the mean and covariance of the posterior distribution of
\theta
can be updated using the Bayesian update rules in
Equations

5
and
6
. These computations are tractable, allowing us to compute the posterior distribution without computing the marginal likelihood. The weights
\{w_{t}\}
are not affected by the assumed noise distribution. The resulting probabilistic estimate of
\theta
provides a measure of uncertainty that guides the search in the
BOF-UCB
algorithm. Specifically, to compute the posterior distribution over the parameter vector
\theta
,
BOF-UCB
employs
Equation

4
and then uses it to guide the exploration-exploitation trade-off in the UCB algorithm. The algorithm selects the arm with the highest upper confidence bound, which is a combination of the mean and variance of the posterior distribution. The UCB algorithm ensures that the algorithm explores sufficiently to identify the optimal arm while exploiting the information gained from past observations.

The Bayesian update rules presented in
Equations

5
and
6
are designed for unbounded parameter spaces and are combined with
2

By incorporating the boundedness assumption, we ensure controlled regret growth, while the Bayesian update rules enable data-driven updates. This combination allows for Bayesian-driven updates and provides frequentist regret guarantees, as demonstrated in

Upper Confidence Bounds

We present our first result, which is the maximum deviation inequality of the Bayesian weighted least-squares estimator. This result is novel in the literature and extends the ideas introduced in
[
1
,
34
]

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}=\widetilde{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{t}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{t}.

(7)

This matrix is closely tied to the (inverse) posterior variance of the estimator, which depends on the squared weights
\{w_{t}^{2}\}

5
and
6

7

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}=\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}w_{t}^{2}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}.

Currently, we treat

.

Suppose

We can see that at each time step

Interestingly, despite the relevance of PAC-Bayesian theory in algorithm design, its application to non-stationary environments has not been explored thus far. In this regard, our

Reinforcement Learning for Classical Optimal Control Tasks

In this section, we investigate the performance of our
BOF-UCB
algorithm and compare it with existing approaches in a more challenging setting: reinforcement learning for optimal control tasks. We consider a MDP with an unknown state transition distribution
s_{t+1}\sim p(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})
, where the state
s_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and action
a_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{U}
are continuous vectors of dimensions
D\in\mathbb{N}
and
U\in\mathbb{N}
, respectively. The primary objective is to find an optimal control policy
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{U}
that maximizes the expected return. The expected return is defined as the sum of discounted future rewards:

\mathbb{E}[G_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\kappa^{j-1}r_{j}\right],

(9)

where
r_{t}:=r_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})
represents the unknown non-stationary reward function. The discount factor
\kappa\in[0,1)
determines the importance of immediate rewards compared to future rewards. Both the expectation and the sum are taken with respect to the state transition function.

The recursive structure of the expected return in
Equation

9
permits the following identity, called the
Bellman equation
, to hold for any state-action pair
(s_{t},a_{t})
:

\mathbb{E}[G_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]=r_{t}+\kappa\mathbb{E}[G_{t+1}|s_{t+1},\pi(s_{t+1})].

Value-based reinforcement learning fits a function approximator
Q(s_{t},a_{t})
called the
action-value function
to a set of observed tuples
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{(s_{j},a_{j},r_{j},a_{j+1})\}_{j=1}^{t}
from the beginning of the learning period until time step
t
, aiming to find an accurate estimate for
\mathbb{E}[G_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]
. We assume a parametric action-value function that follows a neural-linear design
[
20
,
30
,
42
,
46
]
:

Q_{\psi}(s_{t},a_{t})=\langle\theta_{t}^{*},\phi_{\psi}(s_{t},a_{t};\psi_{t})\rangle,

where
\phi
is a neural network with an arbitrary architecture and parameters at
\psi_{t}
. We refer to
\phi_{\psi}(\cdot)
as the
context network
, since it extracts the context features from a given state. We construct a bandit game that characterizes the reward function as

y_{t}=Q_{\psi_{t}}(s_{t},a_{t})+\eta_{t}\equiv\langle\theta_{t}^{*},x_{t}\rangle+\eta_{t},

where
x_{t}=\phi_{\psi_{t}}(s_{t},a_{t};\psi_{t})
.
We divide the action space into 41 equally separated points (20 for negative valued actions, 20 for positive valued actions, and 1 for 0). Increasing the number of points yields higher precision in the action space, but it also increases computational time. Based on our preliminary findings, we observed that all methods were capable of successfully solving the tasks using the 41-point discretization. Therefore, we opt to proceed with this configuration and select the action with the corresponding bandit algorithm, i.e.
a_{t}=\argmax_{a}\mathrm{UCB}(\phi_{\psi_{t}};\mathcal{D}_{t})

We train the context network after each interaction based on loss functions suitable to the related bandit algorithms. Since
LinUCB
and
D-LinUCB
learn point estimates, we train their context networks to minimize the mean square Bellman error

\min_{\psi_{t}}\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{j\in B}\lVert r_{j}+\kappa\widetilde{Q}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{j}}(s_{j},a_{j})-Q_{\psi_{j}}(s_{j},a_{j})\rVert^{2},

where
B
is a set of randomly chosen indices from
\{1,\ldots,t\}
to construct a mini-batch. The function
\widetilde{Q}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{t}}
denotes a target network whose parameters are copied from the original action-value function
\widetilde{\psi}_{t}=\psi_{t}
.
BayesUCB
and our
BOF-UCB
algorithm infer posterior distributions on reward parameters
\{\theta_{t}^{*}\}
given
\mathcal{D}_{t}
. For these two models we use Type II Maximum Likelihood method to learn the context network parameters:

\min_{\psi_{t}}\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{j\in B}\log p(y_{t}|\mathcal{D}_{t},\psi_{t}).

V
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we have proposed a novel algorithm, Bayesian-Optimistic Frequentist Upper Confidence Bound (
BOF-UCB
), for solving the stochastic contextual linear bandit problem in non-stationary environments. Our approach leverages Bayesian inference in a frequentist framework to handle changes in the environment and achieve low regret. Our experiments have shown that
BOF-UCB
outperforms existing methods in terms of cumulative regret, highlighting its potential in complex decision-making scenarios with time-varying contexts and parameters.

Despite its promising performance,
BOF-UCB
has limitations, which may point to avenues for future work:
Firstly, extending
BOF-UCB
to handle non-linear contextual bandits is an important endeavor since the linear-reward assumption may not always hold in practice
[
17
,
38
,
40
,
46
,
33
,
43
]
.
Secondly, it is worthwhile to explore alternative non-stationarity assumptions that can arise in contextual bandits, or to relax the existing assumptions
[
28
]
. For instance, relaxing the assumption on the knowledge of the variation-budget (
3
) could be achieved using a Bandit-Over-Bandit strategy
[
10
,
11
,
15
]
.
Thirdly, it would be valuable to investigate the applicability of
BOF-UCB
in other domains such as transfer learning, online meta-learning, and online control in dynamic environments.

By leveraging the capabilities of
BOF-UCB
, novel applications can be developed in safety-critical lifelong learning scenarios, such as wearable assistive robotics or brain-computer interfaces. These domains require continuous adaptation to individual users’ needs and preferences, and
BOF-UCB
can provide the necessary flexibility and analytical guarantees for efficient decision-making.
Another example is autonomous driving. In this context, vehicles must adapt to different locations and accommodate the preferences of their users. The algorithm’s ability to balance exploration and exploitation, coupled with its analytical guarantees, can facilitate personalized decision-making in dynamic environments. By incorporating
BOF-UCB
into autonomous driving systems, vehicles can efficiently learn and adapt their behavior based on real-time data and user preferences, enhancing both safety and user satisfaction.

Such exploration may reveal new insights and ideas for improvements. In particular, in these scenarios, it may be advantageous to consider an extension of the algorithm that incorporates data-dependent priors. Overall, our work contributes to the growing body of research on contextual bandits and non-stationarity in sequential decision-making. We believe that our proposed algorithm will inspire further advances in this exciting and important field.

\displaystyle+\beta_{t-1}(\delta)\lVert x_{t}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}},

and

\displaystyle\langle x_{t},\theta_{t}^{*}\rangle\geq

\displaystyle\langle x_{t},\mu_{t-1}\rangle+L\sum_{i=t-k}^{t-1}\lVert\theta_{i}^{*}-\theta_{i+1}^{*}\rVert+\frac{2L^{3}S\overline{w}_{t-k-1}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}w_{t-1}}

\displaystyle+\beta_{t-1}(\delta)\lVert x_{t}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}}.

Let
r_{t}^{a}
denote the instantaneous regret of an action
a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}
at round
t
, i.e.
\langle\theta_{t}^{*},x_{t}^{a_{t}^{*}}\rangle-\langle\theta_{t}^{*},x_{t}^{a}\rangle
. To ease notation, we let
r_{t}=r_{t}^{a_{t}}
and
r_{t}^{*}=r_{t}^{a_{t}^{*}}
. Thus, with the definition of the instantaneous regret
r_{t}=\langle\theta_{t}^{*},x_{t}^{*}-x_{t}\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle r_{t}\leq

\displaystyle\langle x_{t}^{*}-x_{t},\mu_{t-1}\rangle+2L\sum_{i=t-k}^{t-1}\lVert\theta_{i}^{*}-\theta_{i+1}^{*}\rVert+\frac{4L^{3}S\overline{w}_{t-k-1}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}w_{t-1}}

\displaystyle+\beta_{t-1}(\delta)\left(\lVert x_{t}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}}+\lVert x_{t}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2L\sum_{i=t-k}^{t-1}\lVert\theta_{i}^{*}-\theta_{i+1}^{*}\rVert+\frac{4L^{3}S\overline{w}_{t-k-1}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}w_{t-1}}

\displaystyle+2\beta_{t-1}(\delta)\lVert x_{t}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}},

where the second inequality comes from the upper confidence bound selection criteria;
\langle x_{t}^{*},\mu_{t-1}\rangle+\beta_{t-1}(\delta)\lVert x_{t}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}}\leq\langle x_{t},\mu_{t-1}\rangle+\beta_{t-1}(\delta)\lVert x_{t}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}}
.
Hence, for the (cumulative) regret
R_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}
defined in
Equation

2
, we thus have

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq

\displaystyle 2L\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=t-k}^{t-1}\lVert\theta_{i}^{*}-\theta_{i+1}^{*}\rVert+\frac{4L^{3}S}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\overline{w}_{t-k-1}}{w_{t-1}}

\displaystyle+2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\beta_{t-1}(\delta)\lVert x_{t}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}},

where the first term can be bounded using
3
;

2L\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=t-k}^{t-1}\lVert\theta_{i}^{*}-\theta_{i+1}^{*}\rVert\leq 2LB_{T}k,

and the thirds term can bounded as follows:

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\beta_{t-1}(\delta)\lVert x_{t}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta_{T}(\delta)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert x_{t}\rVert_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta_{T}(\delta)\sqrt{T}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert x_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma_{t-1}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\Sigma_{t-1}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{3/2}\sigma\beta_{T}(\delta)\sqrt{T}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{\det(\Sigma_{0})}{\det(\Sigma_{T})}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{3/2}\sigma\beta_{T}(\delta)\sqrt{dT}\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\frac{\trace(\Sigma_{0})L^{2}\overline{w}_{T}}{d\sigma^{2}}\right)},

where we used the fact that
\beta_{t}(\delta)
increases as
t
increases (e.g. see the definition in
Theorem

1
), Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
Proposition

A.2
, and
Proposition

A.1
with
p=1
.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

1
.

By substituting
w_{t}=\gamma^{-t}
into the regret bound
R_{T}
derived in
Theorem

2
, we obtain the following expression:

\displaystyle 2LB_{T}k+\frac{4L^{3}S}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{t-k-1}\gamma^{t-1-s}

\displaystyle+2^{3/2}\sigma\beta_{T}(\delta)\sqrt{dT}\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\frac{\trace(\Sigma_{0})L^{2}}{d\sigma^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{-t}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2LB_{T}k+\frac{4L^{3}S\gamma^{k}T}{\sigma_{0}^{2}(1-\gamma)}

\displaystyle+2^{3/2}\sigma\beta_{T}(\delta)\sqrt{dT}\sqrt{T\ln\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)+\ln\left(1+\frac{\trace(\Sigma_{0})L^{2}}{d\sigma^{2}(1-\gamma)}\right)},

where we utilized that
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{t-k-1}\gamma^{t-1-s}\leq\frac{\gamma^{k}T}{1-\gamma}
and
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{-t}=\frac{\gamma^{-T}-1}{1-\gamma}
.

To establish the desired result, we adopt the parameter choices of
\gamma
proposed in
[
34
,
14
]
: First, suppose
\gamma=1-(B_{T}/dT)^{2/3}
and
k=\ln(T)/(1-\gamma)
. With these selections,
k
can be expressed as
d^{2/3}B_{T}^{-2/3}T^{2/3}\ln(T)
, and consequently,
B_{T}k
is equivalent to
d^{2/3}B_{T}^{1/3}T^{2/3}\ln(T)
. Furthermore,
\gamma^{k}
is approximately
T^{-1}
, resulting in
T\gamma^{k}/(1-\gamma)
behaving as
d^{2/3}B_{T}^{-2/3}T^{2/3}
. Additionally,
\ln(1/\gamma)
behaves like
d^{-2/3}B_{T}^{2/3}T^{-2/3}
, implying that

. At last,

, which is
d^{2/3}B_{T}^{1/3}T^{2/3}\sqrt{\ln(T/B_{T})}
. By combining these findings, disregarding the logarithmic factors, we arrive at the desired result. Secondly, suppose

and

Appendix B
Self-normalized concentration inequality for vector-valued martingales

In this appendix, we derive a self-normalized concentration inequality for vector-valued martingales
[
29
]
, which often is used in the proofs; our construction is an adaptation of
[
2
,
34
]
.
Let
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a filtration.
Let
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
be a real-valued stochastic process such that
\eta_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable and
\eta_{t}
is (centered) conditionally
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}>0
, i.e.
\forall\nu\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\nu\eta_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\exp(\nu^{2}\sigma^{2}/2)
a.s.
The (conditionally)
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian condition implies that
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=0
and
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\eta_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq\sigma^{2}
, and thus, we can think of
\sigma^{2}
as the (conditional) variance.
Let
\{x_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued stochastic process such that
x_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable.
Let
\{\kappa_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
be a predictable positive-valued sequence (i.e.
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable).
For any
t\geq 1
, define the vector-valued martingale

S_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\kappa_{i}x_{i}\eta_{i},

and the matrix-valued processes

\overline{\Lambda}_{t}=\Lambda+\Lambda_{t}\quad\text{with}\quad\Lambda_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\kappa_{i}^{2}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top},

where
\Lambda
is a predictable positive definite matrix.

\overline{\Lambda}_{t}
can be updated sequentially by
\overline{\Lambda}_{t}=\overline{\Lambda}_{t-1}+\kappa_{t}^{2}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}
with
\overline{\Lambda}_{0}=\Lambda
.
Our notation suppress that
\Lambda
could be the
d
-dimensional identity matrix
\mathbb{I}_{d}
multiplied some positive-valued sequence.

The deviation of martingale
S_{t}
is norm weighted by the matrix
\overline{\Lambda}_{t}
, hence the name self-normalized.
The following concentration inequality shows with high probability that the self-normalized martingale stays close to zero.

Theorem B.1
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
t
, we have

\lVert S_{t}\rVert_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}}\leq\sigma\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\sqrt{\frac{\det(\overline{\Lambda}_{t})}{\det(\Lambda)}}\right)}.

For the proof of
Theorem

B.1
, we will need the following two lemmas;
Lemmas

B.1
and
B.2
.
The assumptions and notation used in
Theorem

B.1
are also employed in both lemmas.
The first lemma is a standard super-martingale argument adapted to randomly stopped, vector-valued processes.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be some arbitrary vector and consider for any
t\geq 1
,

M_{t}(x)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}x^{\top}S_{t}-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\Lambda_{t}x\right).

Let
\tau
be a stopping time with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
Then,
M_{t}(x)
is almost surely well-defined and
\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}(x)]\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof of
Lemma

B.1
.

First, we prove that
\{M_{t}(x)\}_{t\geq 1}
is a super-martingale. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{t}(x)|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}x^{\top}S_{t}-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\Lambda_{t}x\right)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\left[x^{\top}S_{t-1}+\kappa_{t}x^{\top}x_{t}\eta_{t}\right]\right.\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\left.\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\left[\Lambda_{t-1}+\kappa_{t}^{2}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\right]x\right)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M_{t-1}(x)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\kappa_{t}x^{\top}x_{t}\eta_{t}\right.\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\left.\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{t}^{2}x^{\top}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}x\right)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M_{t-1}(x)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\kappa_{t}x^{\top}x_{t}\eta_{t}\right)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{t}^{2}x^{\top}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}x\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{t-1}(x)\exp\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\kappa_{t}^{2}(x^{\top}x_{t})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{t}^{2}x^{\top}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M_{t-1}(x),

using that
S_{t-1}
and
\Lambda_{t-1}
are
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable,
\eta_{t}
is conditionally
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian, and
\kappa_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable, and the tower property for conditional expectations.
This shows that
\{M_{t}(x)\}_{t\geq 1}
is indeed a super-martingale and in fact
\mathbb{E}[M_{t}(x)]\leq 1
.

Now, we argue that
M_{\tau}(x)
is well-defined.
By the convergence theorem for non-negative super-martingales,
M_{\infty}(x)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}M_{t}(x)
is almost surely well-defined.
Hence,
M_{\tau}(x)
is indeed well-defined independently of whether
\tau
is finite or not.

Next, we show that
\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}(x)]\leq 1
.
For this, let
Q_{t}(x)=M_{\min\{\tau,t\}}(x)
be a stopped version of
\{M_{t}(x)\}
.
As
Q_{t}(x)
is also a super-martingale, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q_{t}(x)]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{\min\{\tau,t\}}(x)]\leq\mathbb{E}[M_{\min\{\tau,0\}}(x)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{0}(x)]=1.

By Fatou’s lemma,

\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}(x)]=\mathbb{E}[\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}Q_{t}(x)]\leq\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[Q_{t}(x)]\leq 1,

showing that
\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}(x)]\leq 1
indeed holds.
∎

Remark that
Lemma

B.1
can be adapted to the setting where
\{\eta_{t}\}
is Gaussian. In particular, if we consider Gaussianity,
Lemma

B.1
can be reformulated as a martingale with unit expectation.

In the second lemma, we will integrate
M_{t}(x)
with respect to a time-dependent probability measure.
This allows us to sequentially constrain the concentration inequality in
Theorem

B.1
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
be an independent sequences of centered Gaussian random vectors with covariance matrix
\Lambda^{-1}
.
For any
t\geq 1
, we define

M_{t}=\mathbb{E}[M_{t}(\mathbf{X}_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{\infty}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}M_{t}(x)f_{t}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
f_{t}
is the probability density function associated with
\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda^{-1})
, defined by

f_{t}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{(2\pi)^{d}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\Lambda^{-1}x\right).

Let
\tau
be a stopping time with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
Then,
M_{t}
is almost surely well-defined and
\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}]\leq 1
.

Proof of
Lemma

B.2
.

By
Lemma

B.1
, we have for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that
\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}(x)]\leq 1
.
In particular,
M_{\tau}(\mathbf{X}_{\tau})
conditioned on
\mathbf{X}
is on the form of
M_{\tau}(x)
for some fixed
x
;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}(\mathbf{X}_{\tau})|\mathcal{F}_{\infty}]]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}(\mathbf{X}_{\tau})|\mathcal{F}_{\infty}]|\mathbf{X}]]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}(\mathbf{X}_{\tau})|\mathbf{X}]|\mathcal{F}_{\infty}]]\leq 1.

∎

Proof of
Theorem

B.1
.

To shorten notation, we use
S_{t}^{\sigma}
to denote
\frac{1}{\sigma}S_{t}
, and
c(\mathbf{M})
to denote
\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{M})/(2\pi)^{d}}
, where
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a positive definite matrix.
From
Lemma

B.2
, we have that

\displaystyle M_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}M_{t}(x)f_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=c(\Lambda)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}M_{t}(x)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\Lambda^{-1}x\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=c(\Lambda)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}x^{\top}S_{t}-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\Lambda_{t}x-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\Lambda^{-1}x\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=c(\Lambda)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(x^{\top}S_{t}^{\sigma}-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\overline{\Lambda}_{t}x\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=c(\Lambda)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\lVert S_{t}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}}-\frac{1}{2}\lVert x-\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}S_{t}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=c(\Lambda)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\lVert S_{t}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\lVert x-\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}S_{t}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=c(\Lambda)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\lVert S_{t}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{\det(\overline{\Lambda}_{t})}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{\det(\overline{\Lambda}_{t})}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\lVert S_{t}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}}\right),

as
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-(1/2)\rVert x\lVert^{2}_{\mathbf{M}})\,\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}/\det(\mathbf{M})}
for any positive definite matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Next, we introduce the particular stopping time,

\tau=\min_{t\geq 1}\left\{\lVert S_{t}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}}>2\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\sqrt{\frac{\det(\overline{\Lambda}_{t})}{\det(\Lambda)}}\right)\right\}.

Hence, there exists
t\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert S_{t}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}}>2\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\sqrt{\frac{\det(\overline{\Lambda}_{t})}{\det(\Lambda)}}\right)\right)=\mathbb{P}(\tau<\infty)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\tau<\infty,\lVert S_{\tau}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{\tau}^{-1}}>2\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\sqrt{\frac{\det(\overline{\Lambda}_{\tau})}{\det(\Lambda)}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert S_{\tau}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{\tau}^{-1}}>2\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\sqrt{\frac{\det(\overline{\Lambda}_{\tau})}{\det(\Lambda)}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\lVert S_{\tau}^{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{\overline{\Lambda}_{\tau}^{-1}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{\det(\overline{\Lambda}_{\tau})}}>\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\leq\delta\mathbb{E}[M_{t}]\leq\delta,

for any
\delta\in(0,1)
by Markov’s inequality and
Lemma

B.2
. At last, writing this as a high probability bound for
\lVert S_{t}\rVert_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}}
, we have for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert S_{t}\rVert_{\overline{\Lambda}_{t}^{-1}}\leq\sigma\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\sqrt{\frac{\det(\overline{\Lambda}_{t})}{\det(\Lambda)}}\right)}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

∎

Note that it is possible to extend
Theorem

B.1
to heteroscedastic noise, i.e. when
\eta_{t}
is conditionally
\sigma_{t}
-sub-Gaussian, by defining
\overline{\Lambda}_{t}=\Lambda+\Lambda_{t}
with
\Lambda_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sigma_{i}^{2}\kappa_{i}^{2}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}

[
34
]
.
