Reasoning about Human-Friendly Strategies in Repeated Keyword Auctions Conference: Proc. of the 21st International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS 2022); May 9–13, 2022; Online 654
Francesco Belardinelli, Wojtek Jamroga, Vadim Malvone, Munyque Mittelmann, Aniello Murano, Laurent Perrussel

Abstract
. In online advertising, search engines sell ad placements for keywords continuously through auctions. This problem can be seen as an infinitely repeated game since the auction is executed whenever a user performs a query with the keyword. As advertisers may frequently change their bids, the game will have a large set of equilibria with potentially complex strategies. In this paper, we propose the use of natural strategies for reasoning in such setting as they are processable by artificial agents with limited memory and/or computational power as well as understandable by human users. To reach this goal, we introduce a quantitative version of Strategy Logic with natural strategies in the setting of imperfect information. In a first step, we show how to model strategies for repeated keyword auctions and take advantage of the model for proving properties evaluating this game. In a second step, we study the logic in relation to the distinguishing power, expressivity, and model-checking complexity for strategies with and without recall.



P. Faliszewski, V. Mascardi, C. Pelachaud,
M.E. Taylor (eds.)

1.
Introduction

In recent years a wealth of logic-based languages have been introduced to reason about the strategic abilities of autonomous agents in multi-agent systems (MAS), including Alternating-time Temporal Logic (
ATL
)
(
Alur et al., 2002
)
, Strategy Logic (
SL
)
(
Mogavero et al., 2014
;
Chatterjee et al., 2010
)
, and Game Logic
(
Pauly and Parikh, 2003
)
, just to name a few.
In conjunction with model checking techniques
(
Baier and Katoen, 2008
)
, these formal languages have allowed for the development of efficient verification tools
(
Lomuscio et al., 2015
;
Gammie and van der Meyden, 2004
;
Cermák et al., 2014
)
, which have been successfully applied to the certification of MAS as different as voting protocols
(
Belardinelli et al., 2017
;
Jamroga et al., 2020
)
, robot swarms
(
Dixon et al., 2012
;
Kouvaros and Lomuscio, 2015
)
, and business processes
(
Deutsch et al., 2009
;
Gonzalez et al., 2015
)
.

Still, verification tools and techniques are comparatively less developed for
data-driven
and
data-intensive
systems
1
1

1

“[Model checking] is mainly appropriate to control-intensive applications and less suited for data-intensive applications”
(
Baier and Katoen, 2008
, p. 15)
, that is, contexts where the data content of processes, or
agents
, is key to model and account for the evolution of the system
(
Belardinelli et al., 2014
;
Montali et al., 2014
)
.
This is the case also for online advertising, where search engines sell ad placements for keywords continuously through auctions. This problem can be seen as an infinitely repeated game since the auction is executed whenever a user performs a query with the keyword. As advertisers may frequently change their bids, the game will have a large set of equilibria with potentially complex strategies, thus making the specification and verification of keyword auctions a complex problem to solve for current model checking methods
2
2

2

“In principle, the sets of equilibria in such repeated games can be very large, with players potentially punishing each other for deviations. The strategies required to support such equilibria are usually quite complex, however, requiring precise knowledge of the environment and careful implementation. In theory, advertisers could implement such strategies via automated robots, but in practice they may not be able to: bidding software must first be authorized by the search engines, and search engines are unlikely to permit strategies that would allow advertisers to collude and substantially reduce revenues.”
(
Edelman et al., 2007
)
.

In this paper, we propose the use of
natural strategies

(
Jamroga et al., 2019a
;
Jamroga et al., 2019b
)
for reasoning about equilibria in keyword auctions.
Strategies in MAS are typically defined as functions from (sequences of) states to actions.
The authors of
(
Jamroga et al., 2019a
;
Jamroga et al., 2019b
)
argued that such “combinatorial” strategies might be appropriate to model the strategic abilities of a machine (robot, computer program) with extensive computational power. However, they do not provide good models of behaviour for agents with limited memory and computing capacity, such as humans.
As a remedy, they proposed to model “human-friendly” strategies by lists of condition-action pairs with bounded complexity. This is consistent with the empirical research on human concept learning
(
Bourne, 1970
;
Feldman, 2000
)
and social norms
(
Santos et al., 2018
;
Santos, 2018
)
, as well as some works on usability
(
Nielsen, 1994
)
and psychology of planning
(
Morris and Ward, 2014
)
.
Natural strategies have been already used to redefine some security requirements for voting protocols in
(
Jamroga et al., 2020
)
.

In our case, the bidding strategy in an auction should be executable for a
simple
artificial agent, as well as reasonably transparent to the human user, which makes natural strategies a good match.
Moreover, natural strategies provide a way to define complexity (and hence also “simplicity”)
metrics for various functionality, security, and usability properties in MAS. By focusing on simple strategies, one can make the verification of equilibrium properties decidable, or even tractable, despite the prohibitive complexity of the general problem. This is especially evident for strategies with memory, which normally make the synthesis and model checking problems undecidable
(
Dima and Tiplea, 2011
;
Vester, 2013
)
.

Contribution

By leveraging on natural strategies, we introduce a quantitative semantics for
SL
with natural strategies and imperfect information. As a first contribution, we show how to represent popular strategies for repeated keyword auctions in the proposed framework, as well as prove properties pertaining to this game.
Second, we analyse our novel variant of
SL
in relation with its distinguishing power, expressivity, and complexity of the model checking problem, for natural strategies with and without recall.

1.1.
Related work

Recently, there have been efforts to apply
formal methods to the (semi-)automatic
verification of some
decision-making mechanisms, including auctions and voting protocols.
A number of works
(
Caminati et al., 2015
;
Barthe et al., 2016
;
Kerber et al., 2016
)
expresses these mechanisms in high-level specification languages. However, in contrast with standard model checking techniques
(
Baier and Katoen, 2008
)
, their verification is not fully automated, but only assisted by a reasoner.

Troquard et al., 2011

introduce a framework for fully-automated verification of voting protocols. Still, their approach can only model one-shot mechanisms and thus does not capture multi-stage protocols and repeated auctions.
In preliminary works,

Pauly and Wooldridge, 2003
and
Wooldridge et al., 2007

advocate the use of
ATL

(
Alur et al., 2002
)
to reason about decision-making mechanisms.
As
ATL
lacks the expressivity to reason about quantitative aspects such as valuations and payments, and solution concepts such as equilibria,
Maubert et al., 2021
introduce
{\textnormal{{SLK}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
, a quantitative and epistemic version of
SL

(
Chatterjee et al., 2010
;
Mogavero et al., 2014
)
, and show how it can be used for reasoning about notions such as Nash equilibrium and strategyproofness.
Still, their approach considers strategies as functions from states to actions and cannot handle strategies with recall.

A key assumption of the present contribution is that agents have only partial observability of the global state of the system, as it is often the case in real-life applications.
Contexts of imperfect information have been extensively considered in the literature on formal verification
(
Dima and Tiplea, 2011
;
Kupferman and Vardi, 2000
;
Jamroga and Ågotnes, 2007
;
Reif, 1984
;
Bulling and Jamroga, 2014
)
.
Generally speaking, imperfect information immediately entails higher complexity of game solving.
In multi-player games, the complexity can go up to being non-elementary
(
Pnueli and Rosner, 1989
)
, or even undecidability when considered in the context of memoryful strategies
(
Dima and Tiplea, 2011
)
. Hence, it is of interest to analyse imperfect information systems where agents have finite or bounded memory, in order to retrieve a decidable model checking problem.
Works that are closest in spirit to our contribution concern modeling, specification, and reasoning about strategies of bounded-memory agents.
We directly build on the research by Jamroga, Malvone, and Murano on natural strategies
(
Jamroga et al., 2019a
;
Jamroga et al., 2019b
)
.
We generalize the approach by considering quantitative semantics for both natural strategies and the logic, which is more suitable for reasoning about mechanisms with monetary transfer (e.g., auctions). We also consider
SL
instead of
ATL
, due to its expressive power. In a related vein,
Ågotnes and Walther, 2009
investigate strategic abilities of agents with bounded memory, while
Belardinelli et al., 2018
consider bounded memory as an approximation of perfect recall. On a related direction, temporal and strategic logics have been extended to handle agents with bounded resources
(
Alechina et al., 2009
;
Alechina et al., 2010
;
Bulling and Farwer, 2010a
;
Bulling and Farwer, 2010b
)
. Issues related to bounded rationality are also investigated in
(
Barlo et al., 2008
;
Hörner and Olszewski, 2009
;
Gupta et al., 2015
)
.

Also relevant for the present contribution are papers that study explicit representations of strategies.
This category is much richer and includes extensions of
\textnormal{{ATL}}^{*}
with explicit reasoning about actions and strategies
(
van der Hoek et al., 2005
;
Ågotnes, 2006
;
Walther et al., 2007
;
Herzig et al., 2014
)
, as well as logics that combine features of temporal and dynamic logic
(
Harel and Kozen, 1982
;
Novák and Jamroga, 2009
)
.
Duijf and Broersen, 2016
present a variant of STIT logic, that enables reasoning about strategies and their performance in the object language.
Also, plans in agent-oriented programming are in fact rule-based descriptions of strategies. In particular, reasoning about agent programs using strategic logics was investigated in
(
Bordini et al., 2006
;
Alechina et al., 2007
;
Alechina et al., 2008
;
Dastani and Jamroga, 2010
;
Yadav and Sardiña, 2012
)
.

2.
Preliminaries

We first recall basic notions. For the remainder of the paper, we fix a set of atomic propositions
AP
, a
set of agents
Ag
and a set of strategy variables
Var
. We let
\mathsf{n}
be the number of agents in
Ag
.
Finally, let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\{f\colon[-1,1]^{m}\to[-1,1]\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a set of functions over
[-1,1]
of possibly different arities.

2.1.
Weighted Concurrent Game Structures

The semantics of natural strategies and
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
are interpreted over weighted concurrent game structures (
wCGS
).
A difference from classical structures is that the labelling of atomic propositions is replaced by a weight function. We consider weighted propositions for easily handling quantitative aspects (such as prices).

Definition 0.

A
weighted concurrent game structure with imperfect information
(
wCGS
) is a tuple

\mathcal{G}=(\textnormal{Ac},V,L,\delta,\ell,V_{\iota},\{\sim_{a}\}_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}})
where:
(i)
Ac
is a finite set of
actions
;
(ii)
V
is a finite set of
states
;
(iii)
L:\textnormal{Ag}\times V\to 2^{\textnormal{Ac}}
is a
legality function
, defining the availability of actions;
(iv)
\delta
is a transition function assigning a successor state
v^{\prime}=\delta(v,(c_{a})_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}})
to each state
v\in V
and any tuple of actions
(c_{a})_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}}
, where
c_{a}\in L(a,v)
;
(v)
\ell:V\times\textnormal{AP}\to[-1,1]
is a
weight function
;
(vi)
V_{\iota}\subseteq V
is a set of
initial states
;
and
(vii)
\sim_{a}\;\subseteq V\times V
is an equivalence relation called the
observation relation
of agent
a
.

We require that the
wCGS
is uniform, that is
v\sim_{a}v^{\prime}
implies
L(a,v)=L(a,v^{\prime})
.
We write
\bm{o}
for a tuple of objects
(o_{a})_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}}
, one for each agent, and such tuples are called
profiles
.
Given a profile
\bm{o}
and
a\in\textnormal{Ag}
, we let
o_{a}
be agent
a
’s component, and
o_{-a}
is
(o_{i})_{i\in\textnormal{Ag}\setminus\{a\}}
. Similarly, we let
\textnormal{Ag}_{-a}=\textnormal{Ag}\setminus\{a\}
.

In a state
v\in V
, each player
a
chooses an available action
c_{a}\in L(a,v)
,
and the game proceeds to state

\delta(v,\bm{c})
where
\bm{c}
is the
action profile

(c_{a})_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}}
.
A
play

\pi=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}...
is an infinite
sequence of states
such that for every
i\geq 0
there exists an action profile
\bm{c}

such that
\delta(v_{i},\bm{c})=v_{i+1}
.
We write
\pi_{i}=v_{i}
for the state at index
i
in play
\pi
. A
history

h=v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}...v_{n}
is a finite sequence of states. The last element of a history is denoted by
last(h)=v_{n}
.

H_{\mathcal{G}}
denotes the set of all histories in the wCGS
\mathcal{G}
.

2.2.
Natural Strategies

In this section we recall the notion of uniform natural strategies from
(
Jamroga et al., 2019b
)
.
Natural strategies are conditional plans, represented through an ordered list of condition-action rules
(
Jamroga et al., 2019b
)
. The intuition is that the first rule whose condition holds in the history of the game is selected, and the corresponding action is executed.
As we are considering the setting of imperfect information, the conditions are regular expressions over
weighted epistemic (WE) formulas
.
Given an agent
a
, the WE formulas over
AP
, denoted
{\mathit{WE}}(\textnormal{AP})
, are conditions on
a
’s knowledge and are expressed by the following Backus-Naur Form grammar:

\psi::=\top\mid K_{a}\varphi\mid f(\psi,...,\psi)

\varphi::=p\mid f(\varphi,...,\varphi)\mid K_{i}\varphi

where
f\in\mathcal{F}
is a function,
p\in\textnormal{AP}
is an atomic proposition and
i\in\textnormal{Ag}
is an agent.

Given a wCGS
\mathcal{G}
, a state
v\in V
and a
{\mathit{WE}}(\textnormal{AP})
formula
\varphi
, we inductively define the satisfaction value of
\varphi
in
v
, denoted
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(v)
:

\displaystyle\llbracket p\rrbracket(v)

\displaystyle=\ell(v,p)

\displaystyle\llbracket K_{a}\varphi\rrbracket(v)

\displaystyle=\min_{v^{\prime}\sim_{a}v}\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(v^{\prime})

\displaystyle\llbracket f(\varphi_{1},\!...,\varphi_{m})\rrbracket(v)

\displaystyle=f(\llbracket\varphi_{1}\rrbracket(v),...,\llbracket\varphi_{m}\rrbracket(v))

The semantics for the knowledge modality is the standard in the literature on fuzzy epistemic logic (e.g.
(
Maruyama, 2021
)
).
Let
Reg({\mathit{WE}}(\textnormal{AP}))
be the set of regular expressions over the weighted epistemic conditions
{\mathit{WE}}(\textnormal{AP})
, defined with the constructors
\cdot,\cup,\text{*}
representing concatenation, nondeterministic choice, and finite iteration, respectively.
Given a regular expression
r
and the language
\mathcal{L}(r)
on words generated by
r
, a history
h
is
consistent
with
r
iff there exists
b\in\mathcal{L}(r)
such that
|h|=|b|
and
\llbracket b[i]\rrbracket(h[i])=1
, for all
0\leq i\leq|h|
.
Intuitively, a history
h
is consistent with a regular expression
r
if the
i
-th weighted epistemic condition in
r
“holds” in the
i
-th state of
h
(for any position
i
in
h
).

A
uniform natural strategy with recall

\sigma_{a}
for agent
a
is a sequence of pairs
(r,c)
, where
r\in Reg({\mathit{WE}}(\textnormal{AP}))
is a regular expression, and
c
is an action available in
\mbox{last}(h)
, for all histories
h\in H_{\mathcal{G}}
consistent with
r
. The last pair on the sequence is required to be
(\top\text{*},c)
, with
c\in L(a,v)
for every
v\in V
and some
c\in\textnormal{Ac}
.

A
uniform memoryless natural strategy
is a special case of natural strategy in which each condition is a weighted epistemic formula (i.e., no regular operators are allowed).

Natural strategies are uniform in the sense they specify the same actions in indistinguishable states (see
(
Jamroga et al., 2019b
)
).
We define
\mbox{\emph{Str}}_{a}^{\rho}
to be the set of uniform
natural strategies for agent
a
and
\mbox{\emph{Str}}^{\rho}=\cup_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}}\mbox{\emph{Str}}_{a}^{\rho}
, where
\rho\in\{ir,iR\}
3
3

3

As usual in the verification process, we denote imperfect recall with r, perfect recall with R, imperfect information with i, and perfect information with I.
.
Let
size(\sigma_{a})
denote the number of guarded actions in
\sigma_{a}
,
cond_{i}(\sigma_{a})
be the
i
-th guarded condition on
\sigma_{a}
,
cond_{i}(\sigma_{a})[j]
be the
j
-th WE formula of the guarded condition
\sigma_{a}
, and
act_{i}(\sigma_{a})
be the corresponding action. Finally,
match(h,\sigma_{a})
is the smallest index
i\leq size(\sigma_{a})
such that for all
0\leq j\leq|last(h)|
,
\llbracket cond_{i}(\sigma_{a})[j]\rrbracket(h[j])=1
4
4

4

Note that, we considered the case in which the condition have the same length of the history. There is also the case in which the condition is shorter than the history. This is due to the usage of the finite iteration operator. In the latter case, we need to check a finite number of times the same weighted epistemic formula in different states of the history. For more details on this aspect see
(
Jamroga et al., 2019a
;
Jamroga et al., 2019b
)
.
and
act_{i}(\sigma_{a})\in L(a,last(h))
. In other words,
match(h,\sigma_{a})
matches the state
last(h)
with the first condition in
\sigma_{a}
that holds in
h
, and action available in
last(h)
.

Measurement of Natural Strategies.

The complexity of the strategy
\sigma
is the total size of its representation and is denoted as follows:

compl(\sigma):=\sum_{(r,c)\in\sigma}|r|
,
where
|r|
is the number of symbols in
r
, except by parentheses. If
r
is a
n
-ary function in

, then
|r|=n+1

3.
Natural Strategy Logic

{\textnormal{{SL}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}

(
Bouyer et al., 2019
)
proposes a quantitative semantics for Strategy Logic, in which strategies are functions mapping histories to actions.
For reasoning about intuitive and simple strategies, we introduce
{\textnormal{{SL}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
with natural strategies and imperfect information, denoted
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
.
Throughout this section, let
\rho\in\{ir,iR\}
denote whether the semantics considers memoryless or recall strategies.

An
assignment

\chi:\textnormal{Ag}\cup\textnormal{Var}\to\mbox{\emph{Str}}^{\rho}
is
a function from players and variables to strategies.
For an assignment

\chi
, an agent
a
and a strategy
\sigma
for
a
,

\chi[a\mapsto\sigma]
is the assignment that maps
a
to
\sigma
and is otherwise equal to

\chi
, and

\chi[s\mapsto\sigma]
is defined similarly, where
s
is a
variable.
For an assignment
\chi
and a state
v
we let

\textnormal{Out}(\chi,v)
be the unique play that starts in

v
and follows the strategies
assigned by
\chi
. Formally,
\textnormal{Out}(\chi,v)
is the play
v_{0}v_{1}...
such that
v_{0}=v
and for all
i\geq 0
,
v_{i+1}=\delta(v_{i},\bm{c})
where for all
a\in\textnormal{Ag}
,
\bm{c}_{a}=act_{match(v_{i},\chi(a))}(\chi(a))
.

3.1.
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
Syntax

Definition 0.

The syntax of
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\varphi::=p\mid\exists s_{a}^{\leq k}.\,\varphi\mid(a,s_{a})\varphi\mid f(\varphi,...,\varphi)\mid{\bf X}\varphi\mid\varphi{\bf U}\varphi

where
p\in\textnormal{AP}
,
s_{a}\in\textnormal{Var}\ \cup\ \mbox{\emph{Str}}^{\rho}_{a}
,
a\in\textnormal{Ag}
, and
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

The intuitive reading of the operators is as follows:
\exists s_{a}^{\leq k}.\,\varphi
means that there exists a strategy with complexity less or equal than
k
for agent
a
such that
\varphi
holds;
(a,s_{a})\varphi
means that when strategy
s_{a}
is assigned to agent
a
,
\varphi
holds;

{\bf X}
and
{\bf U}
are the usual temporal operators “next” and “until”. The meaning of
f(\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{n})
depends on the function
f
.
We use
\top
,
\vee
, and
\neg
to denote, respectively, function
1
, function
x,y\mapsto\max(x,y)
and
function
x\mapsto-x
.

A variable is
free
in formula
\varphi
if it is bound to an agent without being quantified upon, and an agent
a
is free in
\varphi
if
\varphi
contains a temporal operator (
{\bf X}
or
{\bf U}
) that is not in the scope of any binding for
a
. The set of free variables and agents in
\varphi
is written
\text{free}(\varphi)
, and a formula
\varphi
is a
sentence
if
\text{free}(\varphi)=\emptyset
.
The strategy quantifier
\exists s_{a}^{\leq k}.\,\varphi
quantifies on strategies
for agent
a
.

3.2.
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
Semantics

Definition 0.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\textnormal{Ac},V,\delta,\ell,V_{\iota},\{\sim_{a}\}_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}})
be a
wCGS
, and
\chi
an assignment. The satisfaction value
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)\in[-1,1]
of a
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
formula
\varphi
in a
state
v

is defined as follows, where
\pi
denotes
\textnormal{Out}(v,\chi)
:

\displaystyle\llbracket p\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)

\displaystyle=\ell(v,p)

\displaystyle\llbracket\exists s_{a}^{\leq k}.\,\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\{\alpha\in\mbox{\emph{Str}}_{a}^{\rho}:compl(\alpha)\leq k\}}\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi[s_{a}\mapsto\sigma]}(v)

\displaystyle\llbracket(a,s_{a})\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)

\displaystyle=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi[a\mapsto\chi(s_{a})]}(v)\text{ if }s_{a}\in\textnormal{Var}

\displaystyle\llbracket(a,\sigma_{a})\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)

\displaystyle=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi[a\mapsto\sigma_{a}]}(v)\text{ if }\sigma_{a}\not\in\textnormal{Var}

\displaystyle\llbracket f(\varphi_{1},\!...,\varphi_{m})\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)

\displaystyle=f(\llbracket\varphi_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v),...,\llbracket\varphi_{m}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v))

\displaystyle\llbracket{\bf X}\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)

\displaystyle=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{1})

\displaystyle\llbracket\varphi_{1}{\bf U}\varphi_{2}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)

\displaystyle=\sup_{i\geq 0}\min\Big(\llbracket\varphi_{2}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{i}),\!\min_{0\leq j<i}\llbracket\varphi_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{j})\Big)

If
\varphi
is a sentence, its satisfaction value does not depend on the assignment, and we write
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}(v)
for
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)
where
\chi
is any assignment. We also let
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}=\min_{v_{\iota}\in V_{\iota}}\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}(v_{\iota})
.

Remark 1
.

When propositions only take values in
\{-1,1\}
and
\mathcal{F}=\{\top,\lor,\neg\}
,
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
corresponds to a Boolean-valuated extension of
SL
with Natural Strategies.

We define the
classic abbreviations:
{\perp:=\neg\top}
,

{\varphi\rightarrow\varphi^{\prime}:=\neg\varphi\vee\varphi^{\prime}}
,

{\varphi\wedge\varphi^{\prime}:=\neg(\neg\varphi\vee\neg\varphi^{\prime})}
,

{{\bf F}\psi:=\top{\bf U}\psi}
,

{{\bf G}\psi:=\neg{\bf F}\neg\psi}
and
\forall s^{\leq k}.\,\varphi:=

\neg\exists s^{\leq k}.\,\neg\varphi
,
and check that they correspond to the intuition. For instance,
\wedge
corresponds to
\min
,
{\bf F}\psi
computes the supremum of the satisfaction value of
\psi
over all future points in time,
{\bf G}\psi
computes the infimum of these values, and
\forall s^{\leq k}.\,\varphi
minimizes the value of
\varphi
over all possible strategies
s
.

4.
Repeated Keyword Auctions

Modeling mechanisms with monetary transfer and private valuations require handling quantitative features and imperfect information. Memoryless strategies are enough for mechanisms in which all relevant information is encoded in the current state (e.g. English auction). In repeated auctions, agents may, as well, use information from the previous states for choosing their strategies.

We now focus on using
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
to model and verify repeated keyword auctions and related strategies.
Repeated keyword auctions are used by online search engines for selling advertising slots when users perform a search with a keyword
(
Cary et al., 2007
)
. For a keyword of interest, the advertisers (bidders) submit a bid stating the maximum amount she is willing to pay for a click on her sponsored link.
When a user submits a query, an auction is run to determinate the slot allocation among the advertisers bidding on the keyword of interest. The most common mechanism for keyword auctions is the Generalized Second Price (GSP)
(
Cary et al., 2007
)

s
is the bid of the agent allocated to the slot

4.1.
Solution concepts for
\mathcal{G}_{GSP}

In this section, we show how
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
can be used for the verification of mechanisms with natural strategies.
In sight of our motivating example, we aim at rephrasing conditions and properties usually considered in the analysis of keyword auctions
(
Cary et al., 2007
;
Edelman et al., 2007
;
Varian, 2007
)
.

Nash equilibrium

Since auctions are noncooperative, the solution concept in the pure strategy setting usually considered is the Nash equilibrium (
N
E
). The
N
E
captures the notion of stable solution: a strategy profile is
N
E
if no player can improve her utility through an unilateral change of strategy
(
Roughgarden, 2010
)

With
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
, we restrict the range of strategies to simple ones,
as it enables us to reason about artificial agents with limited capabilities and human-friendly strategies.
Let
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{a})_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}}
be a profile of strategies
and
k>0
and define the formula

\displaystyle\text{N}\text{E}(\bm{\sigma},k):=\bigwedge_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}}\forall t^{\leq k}.\,\big[(\textnormal{Ag}_{-a},\sigma_{-a})

\displaystyle(a,t){\bf X}\text{ut}_{a}\leq(\textnormal{Ag},\bm{\sigma}){\bf X}\text{ut}_{a}\big]

The formula
\text{N}\text{E}(\bm{\sigma},k)
means that, for every agent and alternative strategy
t
of complexity at most
k
, binding to
t
when everyone else binds to their strategies in
\bm{\sigma}
leads to at most the same utility as when she also binds to her strategy in
\bm{\sigma}
.
In relation to strategies with complexity at most
k
, the strategy profile
\bm{\sigma}
leads to a
N
E
in the next state of
v
if

\llbracket\text{N}\text{E}(\bm{\sigma},k)\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)=1
.

Predicting outcomes of a keyword auction is a difficult task given the infinite nature of NE continuum
(
Yuan et al., 2017
)
. For this reason, refined solution concepts have been proposed to reduce the
N
E
continuum to subsets.
Edelman

(
Edelman et al., 2007
)
studied the subset called locally envy-free equilibrium (
LEF
E
), in which no advertiser can improve her utility by exchanging her current slot to the one ranked one position above, given the current prices.

Locally envy free equilibrium

Let
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{a})_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}}
be a profile of strategies, we define the formula

\displaystyle\text{LEF}\text{E}(\bm{\sigma}):=\bigwedge_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}}(\textnormal{Ag},\bm{\sigma}){\bf X}\big[\text{LEF}_{wins}^{a}\land\text{LEF}_{loses}^{a}]

where
\text{LEF}_{wins}^{a}:=\bigwedge_{1<s\leq\mathsf{m}}(\text{all}_{a,s}=1\to\text{ut}_{a,s}\geq\text{ut}_{a,s-1})
indicates that when an agent is allocated to a slot, she does not prefer to switch to the slot right above and
\text{LEF}_{loses}^{a}:=(\bigwedge_{s\in S}\text{all}_{a,s}=0)\to 0\geq\text{ut}_{a,\mathsf{m}}
denotes that agents who were not assigned to any slot do not prefer to get the last slot.

\text{LEF}\text{E}(\bm{\sigma})
means that, for any agent, when everyone follows the strategies in
\bm{\sigma}
, it holds that (i) if she wins
s
, her utility for
s
is greater than for slot
s-1
(at current prices) and (ii) if she does not get any slot, then her utility for the last slot is at most zero.
Strategy profile
\bm{\sigma}
leads to

in the next state of

if
\llbracket\text{LEF}\text{E}(\bm{\sigma})\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)=1
.

(

,

4.2.
Natural Strategies for
\mathcal{G}_{GSP}

Given agent
a
and the wCGS
\mathcal{G}_{GSP}
, we exemplify strategies for
a
in a repeated keyword auction.
For readability, we omit the epistemic operator
K_{a}
from an epistemic condition
K_{a}\varphi
when the satisfaction value of
\varphi
is known by
a
in all states.
A common approach for an advertiser is to assume that all the other bids will remain fixed in the next round and target the slot that maximizes her utility at current prices. This mechanism allows a range of bids that will result in the same outcome from
a
’s perspective, so a number of strategies are distinguished by the bid choice within this range.

Balanced bidding

In the balanced bidding strategy (BB)
(
Cary et al., 2007
)
, the agent bids so as to be indifferent between successfully winning the targeted slot at its current price, or winning a slightly more desirable slot at her bid price.
The natural strategy representing balanced bidding for agent
a
is denoted
BB_{a}
and is constructed in three parts.
First, include the guarded actions
(BB_{a,1}(b),b)
for each action
b\in\textnormal{Ac}
. Second, include
(BB_{a,2}(b,s),b)
for each
b\in\textnormal{Ac}
and
1<s\leq\mathsf{m}
. Third, the last guarded action is
(\top,0)
. The condition
BB_{a,1}(b)
refers to the case in which the slot maximizing
a
’s utility is the top slot and
b

is
(\vartheta_{a}+\text{p}_{1})/2
:

BB_{a,1}(b):=b=\frac{\vartheta_{a}+\text{p}_{1}}{2}\land(argmax_{s\in S(\text{ut}_{a,s})})^{-1}=1

Condition
BB_{a,2}(b,s)
denotes the case in which the slot
s\neq 1
maximizes
a
’s utility and
b
is the bid value that is high enough to force the prices paid by her competitors to rise, but not so high that
she would mind getting a higher slot at a price just below
b
.

\displaystyle BB_{a,2}(b,s):=\text{ut}_{a,s}=\theta_{s-1}\times(\vartheta_{a}-b)\land(argmax_{s^{\prime}\in S}(\text{ut}_{a,s^{\prime}}))^{-1}=s^{-1}

Notice the guarded action
BB_{a,2}(b,s)
is defined for
s>1
since it compares the utility with the one for
s-1
. The case
s=1
is treated by the guarded action
BB_{a,1}(b)
.

Given a valuation profile
\bm{{\mathsf{v}}}=({\mathsf{v}}_{a})_{a\in\textnormal{Ag}}
, let
\eta_{x}
be the agent in the
x
-th position of

5.
Expressivity

In relation to
{\textnormal{{SL}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
with combinatorial strategies,
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
introduces a new, broader class of human-friendly strategies
and
a language for expressing properties of agents that use such strategies.
Clearly, strategies with quantitative conditions can be used to obtain goals that would not be achievable otherwise.
On the other hand, bounded natural strategies of
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
may not achieve some goals that can be enforced with combinatorial strategies of
{\textnormal{{SL}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
.
In this section, we show that the expressive power of
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
is incomparable to that of
{\textnormal{{SL}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
.
In other words, there are properties of quantitative games with natural strategies that cannot be equivalently translated to properties based on combinatorial strategies, and vice versa.
From this, we conclude that reasoning about human-friendly strategies offers an inherently different view of a multi-agent system from the “standard” one.

\mathcal{G}_{2}^{\prime}
, and hence
(\mathcal{G}_{2},q_{0})
and
(\mathcal{G}_{2}^{\prime},q_{0})
produce the same valuations of
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
formulas.

On the other hand, we have that
\exists s_{2}\forall s_{1}(1,s_{1})(2,s_{2}){\bf F}\mathsf{{win}}
of
{\textnormal{{SL}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
holds in
(\mathcal{G}_{2},q_{0})
, but not in
(\mathcal{G}_{2}^{\prime},q_{0})
.
∎

The following is an immediate consequence.

Theorem 5
.

{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
and
{\textnormal{{SL}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
have incomparable distinguishing power over the class of pointed
wCGS
(in both
ir
and
iR
semantics).

Corollary 0
.

{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
and
{\textnormal{{SL}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
have incomparable expressive power over the class of pointed
wCGS
(in both
ir
and
iR
semantics).

6.
Model Checking

In this section we show that the model checking problem for
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
with imperfect information is no harder than model checking LTL or classic SL with memoryless agents.
First of all, we define the quantitative model-checking problem for
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
.

Definition 0.

Given
\rho\in\{ir,iR\}
,
the model-checking problem for
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
consists in deciding, for a given sentence
\varphi
, wCGS
\mathcal{G}
, state
v\in V
and predicate
P\subseteq[-1;1]
, whether
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}(v)\in P
.

Now, we have all the ingredients to prove the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Assuming that functions in
\mathcal{F}
can be computed in polynomial space, model checking
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
with imperfect information, natural strategies with recall, and
k
as parameter of the problem is
Pspace
-complete.

Proof.

For the lower-bound we recall that
{\textnormal{LTL}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
model checking is
Pspace
-complete
(
Almagor et al., 2016
)
.
For the upper-bound, to verify that a given
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
formula
\varphi
is satisfied over a
wCGS

\mathcal{G}
at a state
v\in V
under assignments
\chi
over uniform natural strategies with recall, we make use of a recursive function as is done in
(
Cermák et al., 2018
)
.
We start by showing that each recursive call only needs at most polynomial space.
First, observe that each assignment
\chi
has a strategy
s_{a}
for each agent
a\in Ag

6
6

6

Note that, as defined in Section
3
, we consider only complete assignments. Thus, we can assume that a strategy is assigned for each agent.
. We know that each strategy
s_{a}
that can be assigned to agent
a
is bounded, and we have that
compl(s_{a})\leq k
. Thus, each strategy can be stored in
O(k\cdot|Act|)
and, by consequence, any assignment can be stored in space
O((|Ag|\cdot|\text{free}(\varphi)|)\cdot(k\cdot|Act|))
.
Now, we can analyse the recursive function. For the base case,
\llbracket p\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)
can be computed in constant space via the weight function. For strategy quantification
\llbracket\exists s_{a}^{\leq k}.\,\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)
, besides the recursive call to
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi[s\mapsto\sigma]}(v)
we need space
O(|k|\cdot|\textnormal{Ac}|)
to store the current strategy and the current maximum value computed.
For
\llbracket f(\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{m})\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)
, by assumption
f
is computed in polynomial space.
For
\llbracket{\bf X}\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)
, we only need to observe that the next state in
\textnormal{Out}(v,\chi)
is computed in constant space.
Finally, we detail how
\llbracket\varphi_{1}{\bf U}\varphi_{2}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)
is computed. Let
\pi=\textnormal{Out}(v,\chi)
. Since
\mathcal{G}
has finitely many states, there exist two indices
g<l
such that
\pi_{g}=\pi_{l}
, and since strategies are bounded by
k
, the suffix of
\pi
starting at index
l
is equal to the suffix starting at index
g
. So there exist
\rho_{1}=v_{0}...v_{g-1}
and
\rho_{2}=v_{g}...v_{l-1}
such that
\pi=\rho_{1}\cdot\rho_{2}^{\omega}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\llbracket\varphi_{1}{\bf U}\varphi_{2}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(v)

\displaystyle=\sup_{i\geq 0}\min\Big(\llbracket\varphi_{2}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{i}),\min_{0\leq j<i}\llbracket\varphi_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{j})\Big)

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq i<l}\min\Big(\llbracket\varphi_{2}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{i}),\min_{0\leq j<i}\llbracket\varphi_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{j})\Big)

This can be computed by a while loop that increases
i
, computes

\llbracket\varphi_{2}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{i})
and
\min_{0\leq j<i}\llbracket\varphi_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{j})
, their minimum, and records the result if it is bigger than the previous maximum. This requires to store the current value of
\min_{0\leq j<i}\llbracket\varphi_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathcal{G},\rho}_{\chi}(\pi_{i})
, the current maximum, and the list of states already visited, which are at most
k\cdot|V|
.
Finally, the number of nested recursive calls is at most
|\varphi|
, so the total space needed is bounded by
|\varphi|
times a polynomial in the size of the input, and is thus polynomial.
∎

Since memoryless natural strategies are a special case of natural strategies with recall, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 0
.

Assuming that functions in
\mathcal{F}
can be computed in polynomial space, model checking
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
with imperfect information, memoryless natural strategies, and
k
as parameter of the problem is
Pspace
-complete.

7.
Conclusion

In this work we have introduced Natural Strategy Logic with quantitative semantics and imperfect information (
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
) for reasoning about
strategic ability in auctions.

{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
provides a tool for mechanism design and offers a new perspective for formal verification and design of novel mechanisms and strategies. We demonstrated the usefulness of our approach by modelling and evaluating strategies for repeated keyword auctions.

In terms of technical results, we proved that the model checking problem for
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
is
Pspace
-complete, that is, no harder than model checking for the much less expressive language of quantitative
LTL
(
{\textnormal{LTL}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
).
We also showed that
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
has incomparable distinguishing and expressive power to
{\textnormal{{SL}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
. This means that the characterizations based on simple bounded strategies offer an inherently different view of auctions and mechanism design from characterizations using combinatorial strategies of arbitrary complexity.
Amazingly, this aspect has never been studied for natural strategies, not even for the original proposal of
NatATL

(
Jamroga et al., 2019a
)
.

We consider several directions for future work.
First, a probabilistic extension of Strategy Logic
(
Aminof et al., 2019
)
would allow handling
mechanisms in stochastic settings with mixed strategies.
Another direction
is to investigate the use of strategies with recall for learning other players’ valuations based on their behaviour.
Finally, the implementation of a model checker for
{\textnormal{{{NatSL}}}}{[\mathcal{F}]}
will enable the empirical evaluation of auctions with natural strategies.
