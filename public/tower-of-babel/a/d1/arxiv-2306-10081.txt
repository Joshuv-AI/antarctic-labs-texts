Optimizer’s Information Criterion: Dissecting and Correcting Bias in Data-Driven Optimization
Garud Iyengar, Henry Lam, Tianyu Wang

Abstract
In data-driven optimization, the sample performance of the obtained decision typically incurs an optimistic bias against the true performance, a phenomenon commonly known as the Optimizer’s Curse and intimately related to overfitting in machine learning. We develop a general approach that we call Optimizer’s Information Criterion (OIC) to correct this bias. OIC generalizes the celebrated Akaike Information Criterion from the evaluation of model adequacy, used primarily for model selection, to objective performance in data-driven optimization which is used for decision selection. Our approach analytically approximates and cancels out the bias that comprises the interplay between model fitting and downstream optimization. As such, it saves the computation need to repeatedly solve optimization problems in cross-validation, while operates more generally than other bias approximating scheme. We apply OIC to a range of data-driven optimization formulations comprising empirical and parametric models, their regularized counterparts, and furthermore contextual optimization. Finally, we provide numerical validation on the superior performance of our approach under synthetic and real-world datasets.

Introduction

We consider data-driven stochastic optimization problem of the form

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x;\xi)]
, where
h(\cdot;\cdot)
is a
known cost function and
x
is the decision variable in space
\mathcal{X}
. The expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\cdot]
under the (true) distribution
\mathbb{P}^{*}

that governs
\xi
is unknown; however, the decision maker has access to
samples

\mathcal{D}_{n}:=\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The goal is to identity a data-driven decision
with
the lowest objective with respect to the
true
distribution
\mathbb{P}^{*}
. This problem is ubiquitous –
from empirical
risk minimization in machine learning
(
Hastie et al. 2009
)
, to
decision making in
supply chain management
(
Snyder and Shen 2019
)
, revenue management
(
Talluri and Van Ryzin 2006
,
Chen et al. 2022
)
and portfolio optimization
(
Ban et al. 2018
)
.

Given the importance of the problem,
a range of different approaches have been proposed to identify
good data-driven decisions. A natural approach is empirical optimization, or sample average
approximation, where one replaces the unknown expectation with its
empirical counterpart
(
Shapiro et al. 2021
)
. This approach can
lead to to a significant
overfitting in complex problems with a huge decision space, or contextual
optimization where the decision depends on exogenous features. In such
situations, one often resorts to
parameterized decisions rules
\{x^{*}(\theta)\in\mathcal{X}|\theta\in\Theta\}
, where
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
, and the goal reduces to computing
parameter
\hat{\theta}\in\Theta
using the data
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Several data-driven optimization methods can be
viewed as a choice of the decision class
\{x^{*}(\theta)\in\mathcal{X}|\theta\in\Theta\}
and an estimation method
for choosing
\hat{\theta}\in\Theta
using the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
.
For example,
x^{*}(\theta)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}[h(x;\xi)]
for parametric distribution
model
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}=\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}|\theta\in\Theta\}
, with
\hat{\theta}
estimated using
two-stage estimate-then-optimize
procedure
(
Bertsimas and Kallus 2020
,
Bertsimas and Van Parys 2021
,
Hu et al. 2022
)

or integrated estimation-and-optimization procedure
(
Qi et al. 2021
,
Elmachtoub and Grigas 2022
)
.
Alternatively,
x^{*}(\theta)
is represented by an end-to-end architecture
(
Donti et al. 2017
)
that maps the data into a decision, and

\Theta
is the set of parameters that defines the function
approximation architecture.
Furthermore, one can exploit
distributionally robust
optimization (DRO)
(
Blanchet and Murthy 2019
,
Lam 2019
,
Duchi and Namkoong 2019
,
Gao and Kleywegt 2022
)

and other regularization
approaches
(
Maurer and Pontil 2009
,
Srivastava et al. 2021
)
to control the error propagation due to parameter
estimation and model bias.

In spite of a diversity of
approaches, the effectiveness of the methodologies is, typically, established using
bespoke analyses relying on
finite-sample generalization bounds
(
El Balghiti et al. 2019
,
Qi et al. 2021
,
Iyengar et al. 2023
)
. Consequently, the performance bounds tend to be overly conservative for
any particular problem instance.
Because of this, these approaches are not adequate for concluding
whether a particular
method is better than another for a given problem instance. To this end, some recent work
(
Hu et al. 2022
,
Lam 2021
,
Elmachtoub et al. 2023
)

use convergence rates or stochastic dominance ideas to compare different data-driven decision schemes; however, these approaches impose assumptions that can be difficult to verify.
In summary,
there are no practical methodologies for selecting
data-driven decisions for a particular problem instance and data
\mathcal{D}_{n}
.

Motivated by the above, our goal is to develop a practical methodology to accurately evaluate the performance of different data-driven decisions by only using the available
data, i.e., we provide a
decision selection
criterion.
This is analogous to the classical statistical problem of
model
selection
, but instead of focusing only on how well a probabilistic model fits the data,
we select decision rules
based on the decisions’ objective value. Note that this goal involves not only the adequacy of the fit of the probabilistic model,
but also its
interaction with the downstream optimization task.
More concretely, we want to evaluate

A_{u}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left[u\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)\right]\right)\right],

(1)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)]
is the
true
objective value of the
data-driven solution
x^{*}(\hat{\theta})
. Note that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)]
is a
random variable since
\hat{\theta}
is a function of the random
data
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. The user prescribed deterministic risk function
u(\cdot)

captures the distributional aspect of this random variable. For example, if
u(x)=x
, then (
1
) reduces to the expected objective performance, in the form

A:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)].

(2)

Thus, the smaller
A
is, the better the decision
x^{*}(\hat{\theta})
is in terms of its objective performance on average.

In order to focus on the key
underlying challenges our approach needs to address, we focus on (
2
), and later extend the results to (
1
). A natural estimate
\hat{A}_{o}
for (
2
) is the empirical average
\hat{A}_{o}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi_{i})
. However,
\hat{A}_{o}

typically suffers from an
optimistic bias
in that the estimate is better than the truth. To understand the source of this bias, consider the setting where
our decision rule is to find
\hat{\theta}
that minimizes the empirical
objective
\hat{A}_{o}
, i.e., the so-called
empirical optimization
problem.
Then a simple application of Jensen’s inequality establishes that the
bias
A-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}[\hat{A}_{o}]\geq 0
, i.e.,
a decision with a low empirical objective value may not necessarily have a
low out-of-sample performance. This is also known as the Optimizer’s
Curse
(
Smith and Winkler 2006
)
, and shares in the same spirit as overfitting in machine learning
(
Hastie et al. 2009
)
.

1.1
Our Contributions

Our main contribution is a new methodology

Optimizer’s Information Criterion (OIC)
to evaluate the
true decision performance
(
2
), and more generally
(
1
), for a very general class of decision rules by correcting
the optimistic bias associated with the empirical estimate.
OIC is
a generalization of the celebrated Akaike Information Criterion
(AIC)
(
Akaike 1974
)
, used for model selection, to selecting decision rules
for
data-driven optimization. OIC directly approximates and eliminates the first-order bias arising from the interplay between model fitting and downstream optimization
(
Definition

3.6
). OIC is both computationally efficient, in the sense that, unlike other methods, e.g., cross-validation, it does
not need to solve additional optimization problems; and statistically efficient, in the sense that it removes the bias of the empirical average without
increasing variability (
Theorem

3.7
). Furthermore, OIC serves as an effective decision selection criterion due to its similarity to LOOCV when evaluating (
2
) (
Corollary

3.11
). These main results arise from characterizing the evaluation bias of the empirical average through a second-order Taylor expansion (Theorem
3.5
), which we extend further to non-smooth optimization (
Theorem

3.8
). All these developments are presented in
Section

3
.

In
Section

4
, we showcase the generality of OIC by applying it to empirical and parametric decision rules, including regularized, distributionally robust, and constrained variants. For each decision rule, we provide explicit bias formulas that can be directly computed. To our best knowledge, OIC offers the most general explicit bias correction formula to-date, encompassing AIC and other variants in the literature. Additionally, in
Section

5
, we illustrate our results and their
interpretations for specific cost functions.

In
Section

6
, we extend
OIC on several fronts. In
Section

6.1
, we generalize OIC to Risk-OIC, used to evaluate performances for more general risk function
u(\cdot)
in (
1
).
Unlike (
2
), Risk-OIC involves the derivative of the risk function
and variance of the cost. In
Section

6.2
, we introduce
Parametric-OIC to evaluate distributional misspecification error for some
decision rules. In
Section

6.3
, we extend OIC to Context-OIC
that applies to contextual optimization. Lastly, in
Section

7
,
we discuss further utilities of OIC.

In
Section

8
, we discuss the results of our
numerical experiments using
both synthetic and real-world data with applications
ranging from portfolio optimization, newsvendor problems to regression
problems. The results
show that OIC exhibits a small bias compared with oracle evaluations and can be computed efficiently across various decision rules. Proofs of all theoretical results, additional discussions, and experimental results are deferred to the Appendix.

1.2
Motivating Illustration of Comparisons with Benchmark Approaches

In Section

2
, we will
discuss
in detail the benefits of OIC offers in computation and generality
compared to possible
alternatives. Here we give a brief overview of these comparisons and
present an illustrative example. A well-known approach
to address optimistic bias is to split
the data into two sets, using one set for training the decision and the other
for evaluating (
2
). However, this approach loses statistical efficiency since not all data are used to obtain the decision.
A standard remedy is to
use cross-validation (CV) that repeats the above approach, each time using
a different split
of the data into decision training
and evaluation. The two main variants are
K
-Fold CV that divides the data
into
K
batches (where
K
is small) and each time uses all but one batch
to train the decision, and Leave-One-Out CV (LOOCV), that
uses all but one observation to train the decision. Both
these two variants encounter either statistical or computational
challenges:
K
-Fold CV with a small and fixed
K
incurs a pessimistic bias because
fewer samples used in each round
(
Fushiki 2011
,
Gupta et al. 2022
)
, whereas LOOCV involves solving
n
additional optimization problems, rendering it computationally demanding.
More recently, the so-called Approximate Leave-One-Out (ALO) methods have been proposed to approximate LOOCV and
avoid repeatedly solving optimization problems by injecting some analytical information
(
Beirami et al. 2017
,
Giordano et al. 2019
,
Wilson et al. 2020
,
Rad and Maleki 2020
)
. However,
these ALO methods are built for specific estimation problems, e.g., for
u(x)=x
and
unconstrained optimization. Compared to these methods, OIC is more
computationally efficient than LOOCV as it does not involve repeatedly
solving optimization problems, more statistically efficient than
K
-Fold
CV as it does not incur a pessimistic bias, and more general than ALO
methods. On the other hand, like ALO methods, it does require a level of
analytical knowledge on the decision rule and target optimization
problem.

To illustrate the power of OIC, we briefly discuss the results of a

40
-dimensional portfolio optimization problem (see
Section

8.1
for details). The portfolio is selected
using DRO with the ambiguity set given by a

\chi^{2}
-divergence-based ball of radius
\rho
around
the empirical
distribution, and the goal is to select
\rho

that minimizes the
objective.
In

Figure

1
we compare the estimated
expected objective performance (
2
) for the DRO solutions
as a function of the ball size computed using naive empirical
average (Sample), 5-Fold CV (5-CV) and
OIC (OIC) against the true performance (Oracle). We see
that OIC approximates the true expected objective curve well enough
to find the true optimal
\rho^{*}
. This is in contrast to
naive empirical average that underestimates the objective, and 5-Fold CV
that overestimates the objective. Although not shown in the figure, the performance of
LOOCV is
similar to OIC, but has a
substantially higher
computation cost compared to
all the methods shown in
Figure
1
. Finally, ALO methods for DRO
have not been studied in the literature.

) for a 40-dimensional portfolio optimization problem (which minimizes a portfolio risk criterion in

) with

, formulated using

(

Existing Methods and Challenges

In this section, we elaborate the challenges in the existing methods to
evaluate (
1
) (or (
2
) specifically) and then
describe the rationale and benefits of OIC in more detail.

2.1
Cross Validation

There are two main variants of CV:

K
-Fold CV and LOOCV. In
K
-Fold CV,
K
is a small number that is fixed as a function of

n
. The dataset is divided into
K
subsets. For each of the
K
subsets,
the procedure uses the remaining

K-1
subsets to train the decision, and evaluates the performance on the given
subset. The final output is the average of the
K
evaluations. LOOCV is equivalent to
n
-Fold CV,
i.e., each time the procedure uses all but one observations to train the decision and evaluates the performance using the left-out observation. These methods are “model-free”, in the sense of not requiring specific structural knowledge
regarding the decision class
or the target optimization problem, and are, therefore, very
flexible. However, a limitation of
K
-Fold CV is that it introduces a pessimistic
bias of order
\Theta(1/n)
in estimating

A

(
Fushiki 2011
)
. This bias arises because each iteration uses a
smaller amount of training data.
Fushiki 2011
proposed using
a convex
combination of
K
-Fold CV and the empirical average
\hat{A}_{o}

to correct this bias. However, the optimal weight of this convex
combination depends on the
estimation procedure for
\hat{\theta}
, and needs to be empirically
estimated.
In comparison, by setting
K=n
, LOOCV removes the
\Theta(1/n)
bias,
even for high-dimensional
problems
(
Beirami et al. 2017
,
Wang et al. 2021
)
; however, it comes with a high
computationally cost of solving

n
optimization problems. Furthermore, existing CV methods only focus on
assessing (
2
) and do not address the evaluation problem
for general
u(\cdot)
in (
1
).

2.2
Approximate Leave-One-Out

ALO methods are designed to avoid the computational bottleneck of
repeatedly solving optimization problems in LOOCV. They can be categorized
into two categories depending on how
LOO solution is approximated: Newton’s step (NS) based methods
(
Beirami et al. 2017
)
and infinitesimal jackknife (IJ) based methods
(
Giordano et al. 2019
)
. Despite their computational gain
(
Wang et al. 2018
,
Rad and Maleki 2020
,
Wilson et al. 2020
)
, these methods require access to the so-called influence function
(
Cook and Weisberg 1982
)
that depends on specific problem structures.
In particular,
existing ALO literature primarily focuses on standard or penalized

M
-estimation prevalent in machine learning, and it is unclear if these
methods can be applied to various formulations that arise in the
data-driven optimization more generally. Moreover, even if ALO can be
applied, we will see that for some data-driven approaches, e.g.,
estimate-then-optimize, the computation benefits are not observed.
Furthermore, ALO methods attempt to approximate LOOCV instead of the
actual bias term in the objective value evaluation. This convoluted goal
of ALO methods, coupled with the lack of an explicit analytical formula,
means that they may not be able to reduce the original bias order, which
is typically
\Theta(1/n)
, with respect to the true objective
performance under the general
u(\cdot)
in (
1
).

2.3
Dedicated Bias Correction Formulas

Some methods aim to devise explicit bias correction formulas for certain
estimation tasks to assist in model selection. Prominent examples include
AIC
(
Akaike 1974
)
in maximum likelihood estimation, covariance
penalty
(
Efron 1986
)
in linear regression, and extensions to
regression via deep neural networks
(
Murata et al. 1994
)
. More
recently,
Koh and Liang 2017
examined various
empirical optimization approaches from the perspective of
M
-estimation, shedding light on how individual points can affect overall model prediction
performance.
Novak et al. 2018
empirically established a link
between the bias and the norm of the input-output Jacobian of a
network.
Siegel and Wagner 2021
,
Siegel and Wagner 2023
derived bias formulas
under well-specified parametric models and empirical optimization for the
newsvendor problem. These methods are similar in spirit to
OIC in that they provide an explicit bias correction formula; however, they focus on very
specific cost functions and decision
classes
(
Anderson and Burnham 2004
)
. OIC can be considered as a
substantial
generalization of these approaches; in fact, we show that several of these approaches are
special cases.

3
General Results

Consider the optimization problem in
Section
1
. Let the decision rule

x^{*}(\theta)\in\mathcal{X}
denote a known deterministic function of a parameter

\theta\in\Theta
. With data
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where each
\xi_{i}\in\Xi
is an observation drawn from
\mathbb{P}^{*}
, we obtain a parameter estimate
\hat{\theta}
from
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Specifically, we let
\hat{\theta}=T(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n})
,
where
T:M(\Xi)\rightarrow\Theta
denotes a calibration
function
that maps a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}\in M(\Xi)
to
a parameter value in
\Theta
with
M(\Xi)
being a set of probability distributions supported on
\Xi
.
This
setup covers
a wide range of data-driven optimization procedures.

Definition 3.1

(General Data-Driven Optimization Procedures)

1.

We parameterize the uncertainty
\xi
via a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}:=\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}|\theta\in\Theta\}
and
x^{*}(\theta)\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}[h(x;\xi)]
.

(a)

Estimate-Then-Optimize (ETO):
The calibration function
T
does not depend on
h
. A
canonical example is
maximum likelihood estimation, i.e.,
\hat{\theta}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}[\ln p_{\theta}(\xi)]
and
T(\mathbb{P}):=\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\ln p_{\theta}(\xi)]
for any
\mathbb{P}
supported on
\Xi
.

(b)

Integrated Estimation-and-Optimization (IEO):
The
calibration function
T(\mathbb{P}):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi)]

for any
\mathbb{P}
supported on
\Xi
.

2.

\{x^{*}(\theta)\in\mathcal{X}|\theta\in\Theta\}
is any parameterized family of
decision functions.

(a)

End-to-End (E2E):

\hat{\theta}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}[h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi)]
, i.e.,
T(\mathbb{P}):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi)]
for any
\mathbb{P}
supported on
\Xi
.

(b)

Regularized E2E (R-E2E):

\hat{\theta}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta}\{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}[h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi)]+\lambda R(x^{*}(\theta))\}
for some regularizer
R(\cdot)
, i.e.,
T(\mathbb{P}):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi)]+\lambda R(x^{*}(\theta))\}
for any
\mathbb{P}
supported on
\Xi
.

(c)

Distributionally Robust E2E (DR-E2E):

\hat{\theta}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta}\max_{d(\mathbb{P},\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n})\leq\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi)]
for some distance metric
d
,
i.e.,
T(\mathbb{P}):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\max_{d(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})\leq\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi)]
.

In
Definition

3.1
, parts (a) and (b) differ in how the
decision rule

For any

{assumption}

Before introducing assumptions on the calibration of

, we define the notion of influence function:

Definition 3.4

(Influence Function)

The
influence function

IF_{\theta}(\cdot)
is defined as follows:

IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi;T,\mathbb{P}^{*})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{T(\epsilon\delta_{\xi}+(1-\epsilon)\mathbb{P}^{*})-T(\mathbb{P}^{*})}{\epsilon}.

We abbreviate
IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi;T,\mathbb{P}^{*})
as

when no confusion arises. Influence functions were introduced originally in robust statistics
(
Hampel 1974
,
Cook and Weisberg 1982
)
and can be interpreted as the functional derivative of an estimator with respect to the underlying distribution
\mathbb{P}^{*}
. Intuitively, it measures the impact of a point
\xi
on the calibration procedure
T(\cdot)
.

{assumption}
[Statistical Properties of Parameter
Estimator]

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)]=0
, and the error in
parameter estimate
\hat{\theta}=T(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n})
is of the form

\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi_{i})+o_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(3)

Furthermore, the sequence
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*})
is asymptotically normal with mean zero and covariance matrix
\Psi(\theta^{*}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)^{\top}]
.

Section

3
stipulates that
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*})
is asymptotically linear and normal. This is satisfied by many
standard estimators in classical statistics

(
Bickel et al. 1993
,
Van der Vaart 2000
)
, and can be shown to hold
for procedures that also incorporate the cost function
h
. Note that the
assumption
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)]=0
is without loss of
generality because one
can always redefine
IF_{\theta^{*}}=IF_{\theta^{*}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)]
. With Assumption

3
, we have the following approximation for the
expected objective value (
2
):

Theorem 3.5

(Bias Characterization)

Suppose Assumptions
3
,
3
,
3

and
3
hold. Then the true expected performance
A=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)]
of the decision

can be expressed as:

A=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi_{i})\right]-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)^{\top}IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)]}{n}+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

(4)

In (
4
), the first term is the expected value of the empirical estimator
\hat{A}_{o}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi_{i})
.
Theorem

3.5
shows that the
bias
A_{c}
in using
\hat{A}_{o}

to estimate (
2
) is given by
A_{c}:=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)^{\top}IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)]/n=O(1/n)
. For
E2E (
Definition

3.1
(b)(1)
),
IF_{\theta}(\xi)=-I_{h}(\theta^{*})^{-1}\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)
, therefore
A_{c}=\text{\rm Tr}[I_{h}(\theta^{*})^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)^{\top}]]/n
, Then
Section

3
implies that
A_{c}>0
, i.e., the bias in
\hat{A}_{o}
is
optimistic
(see discussion in
Section

1
).
In general, however, the bias
A_{c}
can take any sign and
our approach applies regardless of its sign.
The proof of
Theorem

3.5
relies on the second-order Taylor expansion centered at
\theta^{*}
of both the
sample optimality gap

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi_{i})-h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi_{i})\right)
and
true optimality gap

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)]
.
Note that
Theorem

3.5
does not explicitly require the first- and
second-order optimality conditions for
\hat{\theta}
and
\theta^{*}
, since (
3
) already captures
the optimality conditions associated with
T(\cdot)
, an observation that
we will elaborate later. The bias characterization in
Theorem

3.5
also serves as the
theoretical foundation for standard bootstrap and jackknife approaches to
remove the bias (see Appendix
9.2
).
Motivated from
Theorem

, we introduce OIC as a bias-corrected estimator for (

Definition 3.6

(Optimizer’s Information Criterion (OIC))

The OIC

\hat{A}:=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi_{i})}_{\hat{A}_{o}}\ \underbrace{-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi_{i})^{\top}\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(\xi_{i}).}_{\hat{A}_{c}}

(5)

Definition
3.6
mimics the expressions inside the expectation on the RHS of
(
4
) by replacing the unknown
\theta^{*}
,
IF
and
\mathbb{P}^{*}
with their empirical
counterparts.
The bias correction term
\hat{A}_{c}
captures the interplay
between the decision space and parameter space. Since
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}[\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)^{\top}\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(\xi)]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}[\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)\|_{2}^{2}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}[\|\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(\xi)\|_{2}^{2}]}
, an
increase of either
\|\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)\|_{2}
or

\|\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(\xi)\|_{2}
can lead to a potential large
bias term.
On
the one hand, a larger
\|\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)\|_{2}
(or
\|\nabla_{\theta}x^{*}(\hat{\theta})\|_{2}
from chain rule) is usually associated with a more “complex” decision rule
x^{*}(\theta)
. We call a decision rule “complex” if
\nabla_{\theta}x^{*}(\cdot)
triggers a function class
\{\|\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta);\cdot)\|_{2}:\theta\in\Theta\}
that has a high complexity measure, such as the Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension, metric entropy, or localized Rademacher complexity in learning theory. A large complexity measure might lead to a larger
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\|\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi)\|_{2}]
, which upper bounds the term
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\|\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)\|_{2}]
.
On the other hand, a larger parametric space
\Theta
, which can be due to a higher dimension, typically increases
\|\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(\xi)\|_{2}
,
since a single sample can affect the parameter estimate more easily. Besides, if the two vector flows
\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)
and

\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(\xi)
are “more orthogonal”, i.e., when the
estimation of
\hat{\theta}
and
x^{*}(\theta)
are more “independent”, the
bias term would be small. We will elaborate these interactions further with Example
3.3
in
Section

5
.

In order to derive guarantees on the use of OIC, we make a mild assumption on the estimation of the influence function.

{assumption}
[Influence Function Estimation]
There exists an estimate
\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}
for the influence function such that, for all
\xi\in\Xi
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}[\|\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(\xi)\|_{2}^{2}]-\|IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)\|_{2}^{2}=o(1).

(6)

Assumption
3
imposes the statistical consistency of
an influence function estimator
\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}
. A simple
approach to obtain such an estimator is through a direct plug-in. That is,
we replace the unknown
\mathbb{P}^{*}
and
\theta^{*}
appearing in

with
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
and

\hat{\theta}
. Under suitable regularity conditions in
M
-estimation, such
a plug-in would result in consistency and furthermore asymptotic normality
(Section 3.4 in
Cook and Weisberg 1982
; Section 5 in
Van der Vaart 2000
).

While
Section

3
and
Section

3
are
intuitive, they are not easily
verifiable for specific procedures beyond standard estimation
problems. This is a tradeoff between generality and usability. These
assumptions provide the most general setting for
the statistical guarantees of OIC. In Section
4
, we will introduce a variety of more transparent
sufficient conditions for Assumptions
3
and

3
. These include moment, Lipschitzness and
optimality conditions that are catered to specific data-driven
formulations beyond the existing ALO literature.
The following result shows the statistical validity of OIC.

Suppose Assumptions

,

3.1
Comparisons with Existing Approaches

We compare OIC with existing evaluation approaches in the
literature, including AIC,
K
-Fold CV, LOOCV, and ALO. First, the
classical AIC, designed primarily for model selection, is a special case
of OIC.

Example 3.9

(AIC)

Let

, where

is the probability density function (p.d.f.) of the distribution
parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta
. Then AIC is the OIC for E2E.

Next, the techniques used to prove

Theorem

3.5
establish the following result of

-Fold CV:

Corollary 3.10

(Bias of
K
-Fold CV)

Suppose Assumptions
3
,
3
,
3

and
3
hold. Then the expected value of the

-Fold CV

-Fold CV is

when we estimate the decision

. This result shows that, unlike OIC, the

6.3
Contextual Optimization

In this section,
we extend
our results in Sections
3
and
4
to contextual
stochastic optimization problems, where the distribution of
\xi
depends on some covariates
z\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{z}}

(
Bertsimas and Kallus 2020
,
Elmachtoub and Grigas 2022
)
.
The ground-truth distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\xi|z}^{*}
is unknown. Instead, we have data
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(z_{i},\xi_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
from the joint distribution of
\mathbb{P}_{z}^{*}\times\mathbb{P}_{\xi|z}^{*}
, and observe the covariate
Z=z
before a decision is made.
In this setting, we parameterize the decision
x^{*}(\theta,z)
as a function of the parameter
\theta\in\Theta
and the associated covariate
z
, and estimate
\hat{\theta}
through
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. We are interested in evaluating the performance
A_{con}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{z}^{*}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\xi|z}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta},z);\xi)]
for decision
x^{*}(\hat{\theta},z)
.
With all model assumptions of
h,x
the same as those used for previous non-contextual optimization problems,
each calibration procedure in
Definition

3.1
can be generalized as:

Definition 6.7

(General Data-Driven Contextual Optimization Procedures)

1.

We parameterize
the uncertainty
\xi
within a family of
conditional probability distributions
\mathcal{\mathbb{P}}_{\Theta|z}:=\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta|z}|\theta\in\Theta\}
and
x^{*}(\theta,z)\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta|z}}[h(x;\xi)]
.

(a)

ETO:
\hat{\theta}
is estimated using a
statistical approach that uses data
\mathcal{D}_{n}
but not
h
such as maximum likelihood, i.e.,
\hat{\theta}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln p_{\theta}(z_{i},\xi_{i})
;

(b)

IEO:
\hat{\theta}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\theta,z_{i});\xi_{i})
.

2.

x^{*}(\theta,z)
is any parameterized family of decision functions. Denote
\hat{\mathbb{P}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(z_{i},\xi_{i})}
.

(a)

E2E:
\hat{\theta}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(z,\xi)\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}}[h(x^{*}(\theta,z);\xi)]
;

(b)

R-E2E:
\hat{\theta}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta}\{\mathbb{E}_{(z,\xi)\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}}[h(x^{*}(\theta,z);\xi)+\lambda R(x^{*}(\theta,z))]\}
for some regularizer
R(\cdot)
;

(c)

DR-E2E:
\hat{\theta}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta}\max_{d(\mathbb{P},\hat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{(z,\xi)\sim\mathbb{P}}[h(x^{*}(\theta,z);\xi)]
for some
metric
d
.

Proposition 6.9

(Contextual Optimizer’s Information Criterion (Context-OIC))

Suppose the same conditions as in

hold, but replacing

IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi),\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(\xi)

\hat{A}_{con}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta},z_{i});\xi_{i})\underbrace{-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta},z_{i});\xi_{i})^{\top}\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(z_{i},\xi_{i})}_{\hat{A}_{con-c}}.

(11)

Then
\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{con}]=A_{con}+o\left(1/n\right)

\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{con-c}]=-\mathbb{E}_{z,\xi}[\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*},z);\xi)^{\top}IF_{\theta^{*}}(z,\xi)]/n+o\left(1/n\right).

Like
\hat{A}_{c}

and provides suggestions for improved

is nearly orthogonal to the downstream optimization

\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta,z);\xi)

small bias. This gives us insight into how to design improved

7
Discussion

7.1
Further Comparison with Existing ALO Approaches

Given
our results in
Section

3
-
6
, we are now in a position to further discuss
related methods and follow-up investigations.
We first
highlight the differences between existing ALO approaches

(
Beirami et al. 2017
,
Giordano et al. 2019
)
and our OIC, in terms of
generality, computation demand and interpretability.

Generality of problem settings and conditions.
First, the
ALO literature does not
discuss general risk functions
u
for performance evaluation. Estimating such a general risk-incorporated
performance is natural since
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)]
is a random
variable, but is nontrivial since the variance of the sample objective
over
\mathcal{D}_{n}
becomes a significant component. In particular, plugging in
ALO or LOOCV there directly, i.e.,
u((1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta}_{-i});\xi_{i}))
, ignores this important variation that has
been shown to be
\Theta\left(1/n\right)
in
Corollary

6.2
.
Second,
even for the
expected performance, i.e.,
u(x)=x
, the
ALO literature
focuses on very specific
unconstrained problems where standard

M
-estimators (i.e., Chapter 5 in
Van der Vaart 2000
) apply. One of
the main
contributions of this paper
is to provide a general framework that can
be applied to a variety of data-driven stochastic optimization
formulations. In the
constrained case (including the DR-E2E in
Section

4.3
and
constrained problems in
Section

4.4
), the influence
function term
\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}
we estimate does not come from the
standard
M
-estimator, and we do not see any existing developments
(including assumptions and results) of ALO addressing this important case.

Computational efficiency in ETO and IEO settings.
Although ALO only
needs to compute
\hat{\theta}
once to approximate all leave-one-out

\hat{\theta}
’s, it still needs to evaluate
x^{*}(\cdot)
for
each of the approximated leave-one-out parameter
\tilde{\theta}_{-i}
,
i\in[n]
. In ETO (see
Section

4.1
) and IEO
(see
Section

4.2
), computing one
x^{*}(\cdot)
involves
solving an
optimization problem. Consequently, ALO approaches are likely to be computationally inefficient,
especially in a two-stage problem like ETO. In contrast, our
OIC avoids the need to repeatedly solve optimization problems by using the
closed form expressions in
Corollary

4.1
and
Corollary

4.2
. Consider the portfolio optimization problem in
Example

3.3
with the returns distributed
according to a Normal
distribution case. In this setting, it is very efficient
to estimate the empirical mean and
variance. However, we need to solve a portfolio
optimization problem when we evaluate
x^{*}(\cdot)
for each

\tilde{\theta}_{-i}
in the second stage. This means that the computational costs of the current ALO
approaches are no better than
LOOCV. In
contrast, OIC only solves the second-stage problem once.

Explicit expression for bias correction.
OIC expresses the bias explicitly and directly estimates it.
And, as we have illustrated in
Section

5
, the explicit
expression allows for an understanding of the
sources of the bias. Furthermore, we show closed-form expressions for nonsmooth objectives (see
Theorem

3.8
and the
newsvendor problem in
Section

5
). Although ALO has been extended to handle non-smooth objectives, we are not aware of
any existing ALO results
that allow for
interpreting the sources of bias in a similar manner.

7.2
Computation Overhead of OIC

The main computation overhead of OIC is computing the
empirical influence function
\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}
. For most problems from
Section

3
,
this requires computing the gradient, and the inverse of the empirical
Hessian of the cost function. For ETO, we may also need to compute

\nabla_{\theta}x^{*}(\hat{\theta})
via inverse functions. Thus, OIC is most suitable for problems where
computing the gradient and inverse Hessian
is more efficient than solving additional optimization problems. For computing the inverses one can use
fast matrix multiplication methods in
Koh and Liang 2017
and
Stephenson and
Broderick 2020
to
accelerate computation.

7.3
Relations to Debiasing Approaches for Linear Problems

When the objective is linear in the decision variable,

Ito et al. 2018
,
Gupta and Rusmevichientong 2021
,
Gupta et al. 2022
,
Gupta et al. 2024

remove the evaluation bias through Stein’s lemma or sensitivity
analysis. Their bias removal approaches are based on special structures in the
linear optimization, where the estimated uncertainty from data
\mathcal{D}_{n}

and decisions are decoupled. As such, these techniques do not apply to our
setting of nonlinear objectives. Moreover, some of these proposed methods
require solving additional optimization problems, as opposed to our
explicit characterization of the bias and direct estimation.

7.4
Incorporating OIC into Training Criterion

Since OIC provides an explicit form for the bias, one can potentially use OIC as the
training objective instead of post-training model evaluation, i.e.,
given a specific decision mapping
x^{*}(\theta)
, we solve the
following
optimization problem

\min_{\theta}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi_{i})-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi_{i})\widehat{IF}_{\theta}(\xi_{i})\right\},

where
the first-order bias estimator
\hat{A}_{c}
acts as a
regularization term. This approach is similar to DRO with
gradient regularization
(
Gao and Kleywegt 2022
,
Shafieezadeh-Abadeh et al. 2019
)
and
variance regularization
(
Duchi and Namkoong 2019
)
that has been shown to
be favorable
for certain problem classes (e.g., low-variance problems). Similarly, OIC-regularization could potentially favorable for some problems.

7.5
High-dimensional Problems

We have assumed that
\hat{\theta}
in
Definition

3.1
can be computed
exactly. This assumption is reasonable
for decision-making models
with a
moderate size. For high-dimensional problems such as those
involving overparameterized neural networks, approximating the influence
functions and utilizing it can be challenging since the
associated first-order optimality condition may not hold in practice. While we may not be able to
handle large neural networks at the moment, active advancements in the
estimation of their influence functions would potentially allow us to
apply OIC to these problems in the future.

We report the number of parameters and cost function values for
all the decision rules in
Table

3
. For the linear model, the performance of OIC is similar to or slightly better than
5-CV, and matches the

Also, OIC runs with less time in the whole evaluation process compared with ALO and 5-CV for most problem configurations. Specifically, for the neural network model,
the average computation time for OIC is
3
minutes and 5-CV is 14
minutes when
30\%
samples are used in training.
To summarize, OIC is closer to the true decision performance compared to ALO and 5-CV in most scenarios, especially when the number of parameters is not too large. We expect that OIC will gain further advantages with the advancements of more efficient computation in approximating the influence function.

9.2
Discussion on Alternative Estimators

Besides cross-validation, we discuss the computational procedure of the bootstrap and jackknife:

Bootstrap.
Denote the number of bootstrap replications as
B_{T}
. For
b\in[B_{T}]
, we resample
n
samples with replacement independently from
\mathcal{D}_{n}
as
\{\xi_{b,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
, and compute the corresponding estimator
\hat{\theta}_{b}
. Then we obtain:

\hat{A}_{B}=2\hat{A}_{o}-\frac{1}{B_{T}}\sum_{b=1}^{B_{T}}\hat{A}_{o,b},

where
\hat{A}_{o,b}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta}_{b});\xi_{b,i}),\forall b\in[B_{T}]
.

Jackknife.
Recall
\hat{\theta}_{-i}
as the parametric estimator using samples
\mathcal{D}_{n}\backslash\{\xi_{i}\}
. Then we obtain:

\hat{A}_{J}=n\hat{A}_{o}-\frac{n-1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{o,k},

where
\hat{A}_{o,k}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i\in[n],i\neq k}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta}_{-k});\xi_{i}),\forall k\in[n]
.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{o}]=A+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)^{\top}IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)]}{n}+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
from
Theorem

3.5
, we obtain the following result, which are standard results from
Quenouille 1956
,
Hall 1994
:

Lemma 9.1

(Debiasing effects of Bootstrap and Jackknife Approaches)

The bootstrap estimator
\hat{A}_{B}
and jackknife estimator
\hat{A}_{J}
satisfy:
\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{B}]=A+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{J}]=A+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
.

Lemma 10.1

\mathbb{E}[x^{\top}Bx]=\text{Tr}[BC]

We introduce several notations throughout this section:

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{o}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi_{i}),

(14)

\displaystyle A_{c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)^{\top}IF_{\theta^{*}}(\xi)]}{n},

10.1
Proof of Theorem
3.5

Recall the notations in (
14
).
Notice that
\hat{A}_{c}^{*}
is an unbiased estimator of

(
\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{c}^{*}]=A_{c}
) since each
\xi_{i}
is i.i.d. sampled from
\mathbb{P}^{*}
. Therefore, the conclusion of
Theorem

is equivalent to the following equation:

\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{o}+\hat{A}_{c}^{*}]=A+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

(15)

We show this equation holds through the following two steps.

Step 1: Translating bias to optimality gaps.
For any solution
x^{*}(\hat{\theta})
, we can decompose its true performance
A(:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)])
to:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi_{i})]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi_{i}),

where
A^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)-h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)]+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi_{i})
. We have
\mathbb{E}[A]=\mathbb{E}[A^{\prime}]
by:

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi_{i})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)]=0.

(16)

Therefore, to show (
15
), it is equivalent to showing that
\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{o}+\hat{A}_{c}^{*}-A^{\prime}]=o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
. First, we further decompose
A^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi_{i})+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi_{i})}_{T_{1}}

(17)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)-h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi)]}_{T_{2}}.

Denote the two terms
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
as the sample “optimistic gap” and true “optimistic gap” respectively. Here
T_{1}
is random and
T_{2}
is deterministic. Then showing (
15
) is equivalent to showing that:

\mathbb{E}[T_{1}]+T_{2}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_{c}^{*}]+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Step 2: Analyses on optimality gaps.
We analyze the two terms
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
respectively.

For the term

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi_{i})^{\top}(\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})-\frac{1}{2}(\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta\theta}^{2}h(x^{*}(\theta^{*});\xi_{i})\right)(\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta})+o\left(\|\theta^{*}-\hat{\theta}\|^{2}\right).

in
Section

13.2
Newsvendor Problem

13.2.1
Detailed Setups.

In the evaluation, we consider cross-validation with the fold number being
K=2,3,4,5,10
and
K=n
(LOOCV); We consider other model-free approaches including the bootstrap with
B=10,50
and the jackknife, which are discussed in Appendix
9.2
.

13.2.2
Decision Rules.

We detail the bias formulas for the decision rules if not specified in
Section

5.1
as follows.

SAA.
Recall the bias formula
A_{c}=\frac{c(p-c)}{npf(\theta^{*})}
for the SAA in
Section

5.1
. The terms we do not know include the true p.d.f.
f(\cdot)
and the limiting parameter
\theta^{*}
.
We estimate
\hat{f}
by the kernel density estimator and replace the unknown
\theta^{*}
with
\hat{\theta}
. Under mild conditions as
Siegel and Wagner 2021
, it is easy to see that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}[(\hat{f}(\hat{\theta})-f(\theta^{*}))^{2}]=o(1)
. Therefore, the estimated bias of SAA in the newsvendor problem is
\hat{A}_{c}=\frac{c(p-c)}{np\hat{f}(\hat{\theta})}
. And such
\hat{A}_{c}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{A}_{c}-A_{c})^{2}]=o(1)
and the conclusion of
Theorem

3.7
still holds.

Parametric decision rules.
We use the same bias computation formula as in
Section

5.1
.

13.3
Real-World Regression

13.3.1
Detailed Setups.

We randomly select 10% and 30% of all samples but stratify over red and white wine across different random seeds since the two kinds of wine appear to be quite different, which is reported in
Duchi et al. 2023
.

The evaluation performance of EM, OIC, and 5-CV is calculated the same as before in Appendices
13.1.1
and
13.2.1
. However, in this real-world dataset, the true (oracle) decision performance
A
is unknown and therefore we approximate it directly from the test set. Given the total sample size
n_{all}(=6498)=n+n_{te}
and the whole dataset
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\in[n_{all}]}
, in each problem instance (where
n=0.1n_{all}
or
n=0.3n_{all}
), we index
\{1,\ldots,n\}
as the training set and
\{n+1,\ldots,n_{all}\}
as the test set. That is, we fit each decision rule with the data
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in[n]}
and evaluate the decision performance via
\{\xi_{j+n}\}_{j\in[n_{te}]}
approximately. That is to say, for each model
x^{*}(\theta)
, we approximate the true decision performance
A\approx\frac{1}{n_{te}}\sum_{j\in[n_{te}]}h(x^{*}(\theta);\xi_{j+n})
.

13.3.2
Decision Rules.

The decision rules of linear and Ridge regression with 2-polynomial features with
\alpha=0,1,5
are implemented in the
sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression
and
sklearn.linear_model.ridge
module (with polynomial features generated from
sklearn.preprocessing.PolynomialFeature
).
The decision rule of the neural network is implemented through a two-hidden-layer architecture in
torch
, one with 8 hidden neurons, and use
nn.Softplus()
as the activation unit of each layer, total of 185 parameters. We set the learning rate to be 0.1, and batch size to be 128 during the training procedure of the neural network.

To obtain the bias formula of OIC in
Definition

3.6
, the empirical influence function of
\hat{\theta}
in each decision rule is computed as follows. Recall:

h(x^{*}(\theta);(\xi^{u},\xi^{v}))=((\xi^{v}-\theta(\xi^{u}))^{2}-\beta)^{+}.

Then the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}h(x^{*}(\hat{\theta});\xi)
above can be directly computed since
x^{*}(\theta)
is a closed-form function in the linear, quadratic or the neural network with
\theta
being the parameter in the decision rule. In terms of the estimated influence function
\widehat{I}F_{\hat{\theta}}(\cdot)
, denoting the linear regression as
\theta(\xi^{u})=\theta^{\top}\xi^{u}
, then the empirical influence function of
\hat{\theta}
is:

\displaystyle\widehat{IF}_{\hat{\theta}}(\xi_{i})

\displaystyle=(\xi_{i}^{v}-\hat{\theta}^{\top}\xi_{i}^{u})\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\xi_{i}^{u},

(51)

where
\hat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}^{u}(\xi_{i}^{u})^{\top}
. Similar formulas can be applied to quadratic regression and ridge regression. In the neural network, we use
torch.gradient
and
torch.hessian
to compute gradients and Hessians in the neural networks and compute the empirical influence function under the same formulation as in
Koh and Liang 2017
. However, the estimated influence function in
Koh and Liang 2017
is not accurate because modern neural networks rarely satisfy the first and second-order optimality conditions at the end of the training period. This issue has motivated refinements in neural networks, as discussed in
Yeh et al. 2018
,
Pruthi et al. 2020
. We leave the developments of better estimators in these deep learning models as future work.
