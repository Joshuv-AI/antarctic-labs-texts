On the sum of chemical reactions
Linard Hoessly, Carsten Wiuf, Panqiu Xia

Abstract
. It is standard in chemistry to represent a sequence of reactions by a single overall reaction, often called a complex reaction in contrast to an elementary reaction. Photosynthesis 6 ​ CO 2 + 6 ​ H 2 ​ O → C 6 ​ H 12 ​ O 6 6\text{CO}_{2}+6\text{H}_{2}\text{O}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}\ \text{C}_{6}\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_{6} + 6 ​ O 2 +\ 6\text{O}_{2} is an example of such complex reaction. We introduce a mathematical operation that corresponds to summing two chemical reactions. Specifically, we define an associative and non-communicative operation on the product space ℕ 0 n × ℕ 0 n \mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n} (representing the reactant and the product of a chemical reaction, respectively). The operation models the overall effect of two reactions happening in succession, one after the other. We study the algebraic properties of the operation and apply the results to stochastic reaction networks, in particular to reachability of states, and to reduction of reaction networks.

As an example, consider an RN describing single gene expression

When the number of molecules of each species (here

\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}

In the following, we examine a binary sum operation on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}

In terms of applications, we exhibit connections to discrete RNs and reachability properties, and to reductions of discrete RNs. Common to these applications is the idea of reactions happening in succession, one after the other.

Reactions often occur at different time-scales
[
24
]

[

13

If the reactions in a sequence occur at a fast rate (that is, with high intensity), it is natural to assume no other reactions take place before the last reaction of the sequence has occured. Rather than describing the entire sequence of reactions, one might summarize the sequence by a single complex reaction, the overall effect. In a sense, this complex reaction is obtained by
contraction
. We define contraction of reactions through the defined sum operation and subsequently define reduced RNs. These constructs are essentially graphical in nature. We show that they relate to stochastic approaches for reduction of RNs, in particular to reduction by elimination of so-called
intermediate

[
6
,
21
]

Furthermore, we study graphical properties of the state space of discrete RNs concerning the operation we introduce. We show that reachability can be expressed via the sum operation, and in particular that the closure of the sum operation determines reachability.

In Section

Algebra on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}

Denote by
\mathbb{Z}
the set of integers, and by
\mathbb{N}_{0}
the set of non-negative integers. Let
n
be a positive integer. For
x=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n}),
and
y=(y^{1},\dots,y^{n})

in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we write
x\leq y
if
x^{i}\leq y^{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and
x<y
if
x\leq y
and
x\neq y
. We also use the notation
x\ll y
if
x^{i}<y^{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Furthermore, we let
x\vee y=(x^{1}\vee y^{1},\dots,x^{n}\vee y^{n})=(\max\{x^{1},y^{1}\},\dots,\max\{x^{n},y^{n}\})
be the componentwise maximum, and let
x\wedge y=(x^{1}\wedge y^{1},\dots,x^{n}\wedge y^{n})=(\min\{x^{1},y^{1}\},\dots,\min\{x^{n},y^{n}\})
be the componentwise minimum.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
r_{1}=(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime}),r_{2}=(y_{2},y_{2}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
. Then
r_{1}\oplus r_{2}=(y,y^{\prime})
is the element in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
given by

y=y_{1}+0\vee(y_{2}-y_{1}^{\prime})

and
y^{\prime}=y_{2}^{\prime}+0\vee(y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{2}).

Proposition 2.2
.

(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n},\oplus)
forms a non-commutative monoid with identity
(0,0)
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that
\oplus
is a stable operation on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with
(0,0)\oplus r=r\oplus(0,0)=r
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
. Let
y_{1}\neq y_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
. Then

(y_{1},y_{2})\oplus(y_{2},y_{1})=(y_{1},y_{1})\neq(y_{2},y_{2})=(y_{2},y_{1})\oplus(y_{1},y_{2}),

hence
\oplus
is non-commutative.
To prove associativity, we assume without loss of generality, that
n=1
. For
n>1
, it follows by looking at each coordinate independently. Let
r_{i}=(y_{i},y_{i}^{\prime})
for
i=1,2,3
. Furthermore, let
(y,y^{\prime})=(r_{1}\oplus r_{2})\oplus r_{3}
and
(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{y}^{\prime})=r_{1}\oplus(r_{2}\oplus r_{3})
. Then,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=y_{1}+0\vee(y_{2}-y_{1}^{\prime})+0\vee(y_{3}-y_{2}^{\prime}-0\vee(y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{2})),

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=y_{3}^{\prime}+0\vee(y_{2}^{\prime}+0\vee(y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{2})-y_{3}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}

\displaystyle=y_{1}+0\vee(y_{2}+0\vee(y_{3}-y_{2}^{\prime})-y_{1}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=y_{3}^{\prime}+0\vee(y_{2}^{\prime}-y_{3})+0\vee(y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{2}-0\vee(y_{3}-y_{2}^{\prime})).

By distinguishing the following three cases a)
y_{2}\geq y_{1}^{\prime}
, b)
y_{2}<y_{1}^{\prime}
and
y_{3}\geq y_{2}^{\prime}
, and c)
y_{2}<y_{1}^{\prime}
and
y_{3}<y_{2}^{\prime}
, it is easy to verify that
y=\widetilde{y}
. A similar argument gives
y^{\prime}=\widetilde{y}^{\prime}
. The proof is complete.
∎

The sum operation reduces to standard addition in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
on the two axis, and it is the component-wise maximum (addition in max-plus algebras) on the diagonal. The proof of the next result is straightforward and omitted.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
r_{1}=(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime}),r_{2}=(y_{2},y_{2}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
. Then,

(i)

If
y_{1}=y_{2}=0
, then
r_{1}\oplus r_{2}=(0,y_{1}^{\prime}+y_{2}^{\prime})
.

(ii)

If
y_{1}^{\prime}=y_{2}^{\prime}=0
, then
r_{1}\oplus r_{2}=(y_{1}+y_{2},0)
.

(iii)

If
y_{1}=y_{1}^{\prime}
,
y_{2}=y_{2}^{\prime}
, then
r_{1}\oplus r_{2}=(y_{1}\vee y_{2},y_{1}^{\prime}\vee y_{2}^{\prime})
.

(iv)

(y_{1},y_{2}^{\prime})\leq r_{1}\oplus r_{2}\leq(y_{1}+y_{2},y_{1}^{\prime}+y_{2}^{\prime})
. Furthermore, the first equality holds if and only if
y_{1}^{\prime}=y_{2}
and the second equality holds if and only if
y_{1}^{\prime}\wedge y_{2}=0
.

The next statement characterizes the sum operation.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
r_{1}=(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime}),r_{2}=(y_{2},y_{2}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
and
r_{1}\oplus r_{2}=(y,y^{\prime})
. Then

(i)

y^{\prime}-y=(y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{1})+(y_{2}^{\prime}-y_{2})
,

(ii)

For
x\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\colon x\geq y
if and only if
x\geq y_{1}
and
x+(y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{1})\geq y_{2}
,

(iii)

For
x\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\colon x\geq y^{\prime}
if and only if
x\geq y_{2}^{\prime}
and
x+(y_{2}-y_{2}^{\prime})\geq y_{1}^{\prime}
.

Oppositively, if (
i
) and (
ii
), or alternatively, if (
i
) and (

), are fulfilled for some operation

on

.

Proof.

(
i
) The claim follows from
y^{\prime}-y=y_{2}^{\prime}+0\vee(y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{2})-y_{1}-0\vee(y_{2}-y_{1}^{\prime}),

as
0\vee(y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{2})-0\vee(y_{2}-y_{1}^{\prime})=y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{2}.

(
ii
) It is a direct consequence of
y=y_{1}+0\vee(y_{2}-y_{1}^{\prime})=y_{1}\vee(y_{1}+y_{2}-y_{1}^{\prime})
. (
iii
) follows similarly.

Oppositely, assume (
i
) and (

) are fulfilled for some operation
\oplus
. Then, for any

if and only if
x\geq y_{1}
and

. Combining this fact with (

y^{\prime}=y_{2}^{\prime}+0\vee(y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{2})
. If (

) and (

) are fulfilled for some operation

, the proof is similar. It completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let

\mathcal{F}=\{S_{4}+U_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{1}+U_{2},U_{2}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{2}\}
. Then, the reduced network
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}=\{S_{1}+S_{2}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}}{}S_{3}+S_{4}\}
is reversible. However,

(i)

\mathcal{R}_{0}=\{S_{1}+S_{2}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{3}+S_{4}\}
is not reversible.

(ii)

(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}})^{-1}=\{U_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{3}\}\neq\mathcal{F}\cap(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime})=\{U_{2}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{2}\}
.

(iii)

There does not exist a subset
\mathcal{F}_{0}\subseteq\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{F})
such that
(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}})\cup\mathcal{F}_{0}
is essential, because
U_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{3}\in(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}})^{-1}
cannot be represented as a sum of reactions in
(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}})\cup\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{F})
.

Therefore, Example
5.2
and Example
5.3
imply that the conditions provided in Theorem
5.1
are not necessary conditions for (weakly) reversibility of the reduced RN. The next example shows that weak reversibility of
(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}})\cup\mathcal{F}
in the case of non-interacting species does not ensure weak reversibility of the reduced network,
implying reversibility in Theorem
5.1
(
i
) cannot be replaced by weak reversibility and assumption
(*)
cannot be removed in Theorem
5.1
(
iv
).

Example 5.4
.

Consider the RN

\mathcal{R}=\{S_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}U_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{2}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}U_{2}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{1},S_{3}+U_{2}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}}{}S_{4}\},

with
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},U_{2}\}
,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}
.
Then,
(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}})\cup\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}})\cup\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{R}
is weakly reversible, but
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}=\{S_{1}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}}{}S_{2},S_{2}+S_{3}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{4}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{3}+S_{1}\}
is not weakly reversible.

The example below shows that Theorem
5.1
is not true beyond non-interacting species.

Example 5.5
.

Consider the RN given by

\mathcal{R}=\{S_{1}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}}{}U_{1}+U_{2},S_{2}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}}{}U_{1},S_{3}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits}$}}{}U_{2}\}

with
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},U_{2}\}
, and let
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}
. Then,
(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}})\cup\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{R}
is reversible,
(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}})^{-1}=\mathcal{F}\cap(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime})=\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{0}=\emptyset
. In particular (
i
) - (
iv
) with
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\mathcal{F}
of Theorem
5.1
are all fulfilled, but
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}=\{S_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{2}+S_{3}\}
is not weakly reversible.

The last theorem of this section concerns reachability of the original and reduced RNs.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be an RN and assume
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is eliminable with respect to

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}
, as in Definition
. Let
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
.

(i)

If
x
leads to
x^{\prime}
via
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}
, then
x
leads to
x^{\prime}
.

(ii)

Reversely, suppose that
\mathcal{U}
consists of intermediate species and
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}
. Assume
(\mathrm{supp}(x)\cup\mathrm{supp}(x^{\prime}))\cap\mathcal{U}=\emptyset
. Then if
x
leads to
x^{\prime}
via
\mathcal{R}
, then
x
leads also to
x^{\prime}
via
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}
.

Proof.

(
i
) It follows directly from the definition of the reduced RN.

(
ii
) Suppose
x
leads to
x^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{R}
and
(\mathrm{supp}(x)\cup\mathrm{supp}(x^{\prime}))\cap\mathcal{U}=\emptyset
. Then by Lemma
there are reactions
r_{1}\dots,r_{m}\in\mathcal{R}
(possibly with repetitions) such that
\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\leq(x,x^{\prime})
and
\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{k}\sim(x,x^{\prime})
. Without loss of generality, assume
\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}=(x,x^{\prime})
.

If this is not the case, then we proceed with
(z,z^{\prime})=\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}
, rather than
(x,x^{\prime})
, and show that
(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*})
. This subsequently implies that
x
leads to
x^{\prime}
via
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}
as
\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{k}\sim(x,x^{\prime})
.

If
r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}
, then
\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R}^{*}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}})
, and we are done. Otherwise, since
\mathrm{supp}(x)\cap\mathcal{U}=\emptyset
, by Lemma
, the reaction in
\{r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\}\cap(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime})
with the smallest index belongs to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}
. Without loss of generality, assume this reaction is
r_{1}=x_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}u_{1}
, where
u_{1}\in\mathcal{U}
(
\mathcal{U}
consists of intermediate species).
Otherwise, if
r_{k}
is the first one, then
r_{1},\dots,r_{k-1}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}
, and we might define
r_{1}^{\prime}=r_{k},r_{2}^{\prime}=r_{k+1},\dots,r_{m-k+1}^{\prime}=r_{m}
. Proceeding with the same argument as below, one can show that
\oplus_{i=1}^{m-k+1}r_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*})
, and thus
r_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus r_{k-1}\oplus r_{1}^{\prime}\oplus\cdots\oplus r_{m-k+1}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*})
as well. Hence, we take
k=1
.

Since
\mathrm{supp}(x^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{U}=\emptyset
, then there exists
k\in\{2,\dots,m\}
, such that
u_{1}
is the reactant of
r_{k}
, but not that of
r_{2},\dots,r_{k-1}
. Let
r_{2:k-1}=(x_{2:k-1},x_{2:k-1}^{\prime})=\oplus_{i=2}^{k-1}r_{i}
. We claim that

(5.2)

\displaystyle r_{1}\oplus r_{k}\oplus r_{2:k-1}\leq\oplus_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}\ \mathrm{and}\ r_{1}\oplus r_{k}\oplus r_{2:k-1}\sim\oplus_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}.

The equivalence in (
5.2
) is a consequence of Theorem
2.6
. It suffices to show the inequality. Let
r_{k}=u_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}x_{2}
, then
r_{1}\oplus r_{k}=(x_{1},x_{2})

and thus

r_{1}\oplus r_{k}\oplus r_{2:k-1}=\big(x_{1}+0\vee(x_{2:k-1}-x_{2}),x_{2:k-1}^{\prime}+0\vee(x_{2}-x_{2:k-1})\big).

On the other hand, by the choice of
r_{1}
and
r_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\oplus_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}=r_{1}\oplus r_{2:k+1}\oplus r_{k}=

\displaystyle(x_{1}+x_{2:k-1},u_{1}+x_{2:k-1}^{\prime})\oplus(u_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x_{1}+x_{2:k-1},x_{2:k-1}^{\prime}+x_{2}).

This proves conclusion (
5.2
). Note that
r_{k}=u_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}x_{2}
implies that either
x_{2}=u_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
or
\mathrm{supp}(x_{2})\cap\mathcal{U}=\emptyset
. Thus, the procedure can be repeated to obtain
r_{\sigma(1)},\dots,r_{\sigma(m)}
, where
\sigma
is a permutation of
\{1,\dots,m\}
, such that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}=(x,x^{\prime}),\quad\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{\sigma(i)}\sim(x,x^{\prime}),

which is implied by the fact that
\mathcal{U}
consists of intermediate species.
Moreover, there exist
0=k_{0}<k_{1}<\dots<k_{j}<k_{j+1}=m
, such that for each
i=0,\dots,j
, either
r_{\sigma(k_{i}+1)},\dots,r_{\sigma(k_{i+1})}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}
, or
r_{\sigma(k_{i}+1)}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}
,
r_{\sigma(k_{i}+1)},\dots,r_{\sigma(k_{i+1}-1)}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}
and
r_{\sigma(k_{i+1})}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}
with
r_{\sigma(k_{i}+1)}\oplus\ldots\oplus r_{\sigma(k_{i+1})}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
. Therefore,
r_{\sigma(k_{i}+1)}\oplus\dots\oplus r_{\sigma(k_{i+1})}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*})
for all
i=1,\dots,j
. This yields
\oplus_{i=1}^{m}r_{\sigma(i)}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*})
as well. Combining (
5.3
) and Lemma
, it follows that
x
leads to
x^{\prime}
via
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}
. The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem
5.6
(
ii
) does not hold in general, not even for non-interacting species. Consider the following counterexample,

\mathcal{R}=\{S_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{2}+U{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{3},S_{2}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{4},S_{4}+U{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{5}\}

with
\mathcal{U}=\{U\}
and
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U}}
. Then,

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}=\{S_{2}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{4},S_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{3},S_{1}+S_{4}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{2}+S_{5}\}.

Note that
(S_{1},S_{5})=(S_{1}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{2}+U)\oplus(S_{2}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{4})\oplus(S_{4}+U{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}S_{5})
. Thus
S_{1}
leads to
S_{5}
via
\mathcal{R}
, but not via
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}}^{*}
.

6.
Discussion and conclusion

We introduced and analysed the properties of a sum operation on chemical reactions. Thereby, we connect and characterise structural properties of RNs, such as reachability, (weakly) reversibility, and being essential via the closure of the sum operation. This extends previous characterisations
[
7
,
33
,
39
]
and connects such properties to the geometry of the closure
\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R})
in the product space
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
. In another direction, we defined reductions of RNs by elimination of species from an RN by adding reactions. Those reductions originate from connections to the slow-fast limits of stochastic RNs
[
6
]
. Furthermore, we studied the conservation of (weakly) reversibility, when reachability of the original and the reduced network coincide in some sense.

As the discrete dynamics of Petri Nets and vector addition systems correspond directly to dynamics of RNs
[
9
]
, the developed theory pertains to those areas as well. Correspondingly, problems and questions from theoretical computer science relate to the notions we have introduced. As an example, an undecidable problem relating to Section
3
asks whether two RNs given by their reaction sets
\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}
with initial values
x_{1},x_{2}
, respectively, have the same reachability sets, i.e. whether
\mathcal{R}_{1}(x_{1})=\mathcal{R}_{2}(x_{2})

[
20
]
.
Another example is the decidable reachability problem that asks whether given an RN and two states
x_{1},x_{2}
, we can reach
x_{2}
from
x_{1}

[
9
,
39
]
.

Furthermore, the closure
\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R})
of an RN has only sometimes the structure of a semi-linear set. This is not surprising as the set of reachable states of an RN directly relates to the closure
\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R})
of
\mathcal{R}
, see Section
3
. Reachability sets can be highly complex and are not necessarily semi-linear
[
22
,
46
]
. Nonetheless, it might be interesting to characterise and study the structure of RNs
\mathcal{R}
for which
\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R})
is semi-linear.

Overall we hope that the sum calculus on reactions we have introduced will find further applications, possibly even in areas which a priori are not directly linked to our areas of research.
