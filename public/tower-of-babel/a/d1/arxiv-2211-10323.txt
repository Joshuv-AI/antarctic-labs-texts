Möbius inversion of surfaces in the Minkowski 3-space
Marco Antônio do Couto Fernandes

Abstract
We define and present some proprieties of the Möbius inversion of surfaces in the Minkowski 3-space. We prove that the Möbius inversion preserves the lines of principal curvature and the locus of points where the metric is degenerate, but it does not preserve the parabolic set. For ovaloids, we show that it is possible to translate the surface so that the inversion remains an ovaloid.

1
Introduction

The Möbius inversion of a closed subset
F
in the Euclidean space
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
is given by

i_{E}(F)=cl\left\{\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle_{E}}:p\in F\setminus\{0\}\right\},

where
cl
denotes the topological closure in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
endowed with the topology induced by the Euclidean inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{E}
. Geometrically, points on the unit Euclidean sphere centered at the origin are fixed points of
i_{E}
. Moreover, the image of points inside this sphere are points outside it and vice-versa.

The Möbius inversion preserves the lines of principal curvature on surfaces in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
. Ghomi and Howard
[
2
]
use this fact to relate the lines of principal curvature of a closed and convex surface with one umbilic point removed to the lines of principal curvature of the graph of an asymptotically constant function. That lead to give the following new formulation of the Carathéodory Conjecture: the graph of an asymptotically constant function
f:\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{2}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}
has at least one umbilic point.

In this paper, we define the Möbius inversion of surfaces in the Minkowski 3-space
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
and show that it preserves their lines of principal curvature. Furthermore, the Möbius inversion preserves the set of points where the metric is degenerate and the discriminant of the lines of principal curvature.

We show that the parabolic set is not preserved by the Möbius inversion by means of a counterexample. However, when we work with ovaloids, which are closed surfaces with empty parabolic sets, we obtain conditions for the Möbius inversion of these surfaces to remain ovaloids.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the Minkowski 3-space
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
and some concepts of the differential geometry of surfaces in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
. In Section
3
, we define the Möbius inversion in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
and give some of its properties. Finally, in Section
4
, we show that the parabolic set is not always preserved by Möbius inversions, but for ovaloids it is possible to impose conditions in order to preserve the emptiness of the parabolic set.

2
Preliminaries

The
Minkowski 3-space

(\mathbb{R}_{1}^{3},\langle,\rangle)
is the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
endowed with the Minkowski metric induced by the pseudo-scalar product
\langle\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\rangle=u_{0}v_{0}+u_{1}v_{1}-u_{2}v_{2}
, for any vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}=(u_{0},u_{1},u_{2})
and
\mbox{\boldmath$v$}=(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. A non-zero vector
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{1}
is said to be
spacelike
if
\langle\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\rangle>0
,
lightlike
if
\langle\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\rangle=0
and
timelike
if
\langle\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\rangle<0
. The norm of a vector
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\in\mathbb{R}_{1}^{3}
is defined by

\|\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\|=\sqrt{|\langle\mbox{\boldmath$u$},\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\rangle|}.

If
p
is a point in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
, then
\langle p,p\rangle
denotes
\langle\vec{0p},\vec{0p}\rangle
, where
\vec{0p}
is the vector from the origin to
p
. The set of points
p\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
with
\langle p,p\rangle=0
is called the
light cone
and is denoted by
LC
. The
de Sitter sphere
, denoted by
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{1}
, is the set of points
q\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
such that
\langle q,q\rangle=1
. On the other hand, the
hyperbolic plane
, denoted by
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, is the set of the points
q\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
where
\langle q,q\rangle=-1
.

Let
S
be a smooth and regular surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{1}
and let

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}:U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{1}^{3}
be a local parametrization of
S
.
The concepts of differential geometry in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
can be extended to
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
, see for example
[
1
]
. The pseudo inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
induces a pseudo metric on
S
called the first fundamental form. Let
E=\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{u},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{u}\rangle
,
F=\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{u},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{v}\rangle
and
G=\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{v},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{v}\rangle
, where subscripts denote partial derivatives. The induced (pseudo) metric on
S
is Lorentzian (resp. Riemannian, degenerate) at
p=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(u,v)
if and only if
\delta(u,v)=(F^{2}-EG)(u,v)>0
(resp.
<0
,
=0
). The locus of points on the surface where the metric is degenerate is called the
locus of degeneracy
and is denoted by
LD
. We identify the
LD
on
S
with its pre-image in
U
by
x
.

At
p\in S\setminus LD
, we have a well defined unit normal vector (the Gauss map)
\mbox{\boldmath$N$}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{u}\times\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{v}/||\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{u}\times\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{v}||
,
which is timelike (resp. spacelike) if
p
is in the Riemannian (resp. Lorentzian) region of
S
. The self-adjoint operator
A_{p}=-d\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{p}:T_{p}S\to T_{p}S
on
S\setminus LD
defines a quadratic form
II_{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$v$})=\langle A_{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$v$}),\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\rangle
on
T_{p}S
called the second fundamental form. We denote by
l=\langle\mbox{\boldmath$N$},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{uu}\rangle
,
m=\langle\mbox{\boldmath$N$},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{uv}\rangle
and
n=\langle\mbox{\boldmath$N$},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{vv}\rangle
the coefficients of the second fundamental form on
S\setminus LD
.

When
A_{p}
has real eigenvalues
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
, we call them the
principal curvatures
and their associated eigenvectors the
principal directions

of
S
at
p
. There are always two principal curvatures at each point on the Riemannian part of
S
, but this is not always the case on its Lorentzian part.
A point
p
on
S
is called an
umbilic point
if
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}
at
p
(i.e., if
A_{p}
is a multiple of the identity map).

The
lines of principal curvature
on
S
, which are the integral curves of the principal directions, are the solutions of the binary differential equation (BDE)

(Fn-Gm)dv^{2}+(En-Gl)dvdu+(Em-Fl)du^{2}=0.

(1)

The discriminant of the BDE (
1
) consists of the umbilic points in the Riemannian region of
S
and is the locus of points in the Lorentzian region where two principal directions coincide and become lightlike. In the last case, it is labelled
Lightlike Principal Locus

(LPL)
in
[
4
]
.

The
Gaussian curvature
of
S
at
p
is given by
K(p)=\det(A_{p})
, that is,

K(p)=\frac{ln-m^{2}}{EG-F^{2}}(p).

The locus of points where the Gaussian curvature is zero is called the
parabolic set
.

One can extend the lines of principal curvature, the
LPL
and the parabolic set across the
LD
as follows (
[
5
]
).
As equation (
1
) is homogeneous in
l,m,n
,
we can multiply these coefficients by
||\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{u}\times\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{v}||
and substitute them in equation (
1
) by

\bar{l}=\langle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{u}\times\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{v},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{uu}\rangle,\quad\bar{m}=\langle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{u}\times\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{v},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{uv}\rangle,\quad\bar{n}=\langle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{u}\times\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{v},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{vv}\rangle.

The new equation

(G\bar{m}-F\bar{n})dv^{2}+(G\bar{l}-E\bar{n})dudv+(F\bar{l}-E\bar{m})du^{2}=0

(2)

is defined at points on the
LD
and
its solutions are the same as those of equation (
1
) in the Riemannian and Lorentzian regions of
S
. The discriminant of the BDE
(\ref{eq:principalLD})
extends the
LPL
to points on the
LD
. Thus, a point
p=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(u,v)
belongs to the
LPL
of
S
when

\tilde{\delta}(u,v)=\left((E\bar{n}-G\bar{l})^{2}-4(F\bar{n}-G\bar{m})(E\bar{m}-F\bar{l})\right)(u,v)=0.

For the parabolic set, note that
K=0
if and only if
\bar{l}\bar{n}-\bar{m}^{2}=0
. Thus, the parabolic set also extends to points on the
LD
as the zero set of the function
\bar{K}=\bar{l}\bar{n}-\bar{m}^{2}
.

3
Möbius inversion in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}

We define the
Möbius inversion
of a closed subset
F\subset\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
as

i_{M}(F)=cl\left\{\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle}:p\in F\setminus LC\right\},

where
cl
denotes the topological closure in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
with the same topology used to define
i_{E}
. When necessary, we consider the Möbius inversion as a function
i_{M}:\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}\setminus LC\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}\setminus LC
given by
i_{M}(p)=p/\langle p,p\rangle
. Consider the following regions of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
:

\begin{array}[]{l}R_{1}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}:x^{2}+y^{2}-z^{2}>0\},\\
R_{2}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}:x^{2}+y^{2}-z^{2}<0,\,z>0\},\\
R_{3}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}:x^{2}+y^{2}-z^{2}<0,\,z<0\}.\end{array}

(3)

Then
i_{M}
is a diffeomorphism from
R_{1}
to
R_{1}
and from
R_{2}
to
R_{3}
with
i_{M}^{-1}=i_{M}
. Points on the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and on the de Sitter sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{1}
are fixed points of
i_{M}
. In
R_{1}
,
i_{M}
is an inversion with respect to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, Figure
1
left. On the other hand, in
R_{2}
and
R_{3}
,
i_{M}
is an inversion with respect to the
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{1}
, Figure
1
center and right.

Figure 1
:
Möbius inversion in Minkowski space.

Theorem 3.1

Let
S
be a compact surface in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
. Then following properties hold:

1.

i_{M}(S)=\left\{\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle}:p\in S\setminus LC\right\}
;

2.

i_{M}(S)\cap LC=\emptyset
;

3.

i_{M}(S)
is bounded if and only if
S\cap LC=\emptyset
.

(1) Let
A=\left\{\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle}:p\in S\setminus LC\right\}
and
\{q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence in
A
that converges to some
q\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
, with
q_{i}=p_{i}/\langle p_{i},p_{i}\rangle
and
p_{i}\in S\setminus LC
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
S
is compact, there is a subsequence
\{p_{i_{k}}\}
of
\{p_{i}\}
that converges to some
p\in S
. If
p\in LC
, then
\|p_{i_{k}}\|\rightarrow 0
and
\|q_{i_{k}}\|\rightarrow\infty
, which contradicts the convergence of
\{q_{i}\}
. Thus,
p\in S\setminus LC
and

q=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}q_{i_{k}}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{p_{i_{k}}}{\langle p_{i_{k}},p_{i_{k}}\rangle}=\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle}\in A.

Therefore,
A
is closed and
i_{M}(S)=cl(A)=A
.

(2) Suppose that
q\in i_{M}(S)\cap LC
. It follows from (1) above that there exists
p\in S\setminus LC
such that
q=i_{M}(p)
. Then

0=\langle q,q\rangle=\left\langle\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle},\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\langle p,p\rangle},

which is not possible since
\langle p,p\rangle<\infty
. Therefore,
i_{M}(S)
does not intersect
LC
.

(3) If
S\cap LC\neq\emptyset
, then there is a sequence
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in
S\setminus LC
and
p\in S\cap LC
with
p_{i}\to p
. Then

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\|p_{i}\|=\|p\|=0\Rightarrow\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\|i_{M}(p_{i})\|=\infty,

so
i_{M}(S)
is unbounded. For the converse, if
S\cap LC=\emptyset
, then

\alpha=\sup\left\{\frac{1}{|\langle p,p\rangle|},p\in S\right\}<\infty\quad{\rm and}\quad\beta=\sup\{\|p\|,p\in S\}<\infty.

Therefore,
i_{M}(S)
is contained in the Euclidean sphere with center the origin and radius
\alpha\beta
.

\Box

An important property of the Möbius inversion in the Euclidean space
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
is that it preserves the lines of principal curvature of surfaces in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
, that is, if
C
is a line of principal curvature of a surface
S\subset\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
, then
i_{M}(C)
is a line of principal curvature of the surface
i_{M}(S)
. That property also hold for Möbius inversion in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
.

Theorem 3.2

The lines of principal curvature of surfaces in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
are preserved by the Möbius inversion.

Let
S\subset\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
be a surface and
\varphi:U\subset\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{2}\rightarrow\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
be a local parameterization of
S
. Then
\tilde{\varphi}=i_{M}\circ\varphi
is a local parameterization of
S_{M}=i_{M}(S)
, where
E
,
F
and
G
are the coefficients of the first fundamental form of
S
and
E_{M}
,
F_{M}
and
G_{M}
are those of
S_{M}
. It follows that

E_{M}=\frac{E}{\langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle^{2}},\quad F_{M}=\frac{F}{\langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle^{2}},\quad G_{M}=\frac{G}{\langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle^{2}}.

(4)

The coefficients
\bar{l}_{M}
,
\bar{m}_{M}
and
\bar{n}_{M}
of the second fundamental form of
S_{M}
with denominator removed are expressed as follows in terms of the coefficients
\bar{l}
,
\bar{m}
and
\bar{n}
of
S
:

\bar{l}_{M}=\frac{1}{\langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle^{3}}(\bar{l}+\alpha E),\qquad\bar{m}_{M}=\frac{1}{\langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle^{3}}(\bar{m}+\alpha F),\qquad\bar{n}_{M}=\frac{1}{\langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle^{3}}(\bar{n}+\alpha G),

with
\alpha=-2\langle N,\tilde{\varphi}\rangle
.

The equation of the lines of principal curvature of
S_{M}
is given by

(\bar{m}_{M}E_{M}-\bar{l}_{M}F_{M})dx^{2}+(\bar{n}_{M}E_{M}-\bar{l}_{M}G_{M})dxdy+(\bar{n}_{M}F_{M}-\bar{m}_{M}G_{M})dy^{2}=0.

(5)

Substituting the coefficients
E_{M}
,
F_{M}
,
G_{M}
,
\bar{l}_{M}
,
\bar{m}_{M}
and
\bar{n}_{M}
by their expressions in terms of
E
,
F
,
G
,
\bar{l}
,
\bar{m}
and
\bar{n}
, it follows that (
5
) is satisfied if and only if

(\bar{f}E-\bar{e}F)dx^{2}+(\bar{g}E-\bar{e}G)dxdy+(\bar{g}F-\bar{f}G)dy^{2}=0,

which is precisely the equation of the lines of principal curvatures of
S
.

\Box

Corollary 3.3

The LPL and the LD of a surface in the Minkowski 3-space are preserved by the Möbius inversion.

The proof for the preservation of the LPL is consequence of the Theorem
3.2
. As for the LD, observe from (
4
) that
F_{M}^{2}-E_{M}G_{M}=0
if and only if
F^{2}-EG=0
.

\Box

4
Möbius inversion and the parabolic set

Let
p_{0}=(a,b,c)\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
and

S(p_{0},r)=\{p\in\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}:\langle p-p_{0},p-p_{0}\rangle_{E}=r^{2}\},

so
S(p_{0},r)
is the Euclidean sphere with center
p_{0}
and radius
r>0
considered as a set of points in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
. Denote
S_{M}(p_{0},r)=i_{M}(S(p_{0},r))
.

Theorem 4.1

The surface
S_{M}(p_{0},r)=i_{M}(S(p_{0},r))
is homeomorfic to
S(p_{0},r)
if and only if
(\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-|c|)^{2}>2r^{2}
.

From item (3) of Theorem
3.1
and from the fact that
i_{M}:\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}\setminus LC\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}\setminus LC
is a diffeomorphism, it follows that
S_{M}(p_{0},r)
is homeomorfic to
S(p_{0},r)
if and only if
S(p_{0},r)\cap LC=\emptyset
. Note that
S(p_{0},r)\cap LC=\emptyset
when
d(p_{0},LC)>r
, where
d(p_{0},LC)
is the Euclidean distance between
p_{0}
and
LC
.

We need to obtain the minimum value of the function

f(x,y,z)=(x-a)^{2}+(y-b)^{2}+(z-c)^{2}

restricted to the light cone
x^{2}+y^{2}=z^{2}
. Using the Lagrange Multipliers method, we find the minimum point and it follows that

d(p_{0},LC)=\frac{\sqrt{(\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-|c|)^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}.

Therefore,
S(p_{0},r)\cap LC=\emptyset
if and only if
(\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-|c|)^{2}>2r^{2}
.

\Box

The surface
S_{M}(p_{0},r)
with
p_{0}=(2,0,0)
and
r=1
is closed (ie, compact without boundary) by Theorem
4.1
because the point
\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{\sqrt{15}}{12},0\right)\in S_{M}(p_{0},r)
, for example, belongs to the parabolic set, see Figure
2
. Thus, unlike the lines of principal curvature, the
LD
and the
LPL
, the parabolic set is not preserved by the Möbius inversion. We have the following conditions on
p_{0}
and
r
for
S_{M}(p_{0},r)
to be convex.

Figure 2
:
The surface
S_{M}(p_{0},r)
with
p_{0}=(2,0,0)
and
r=1
.

Theorem 4.2

The parabolic set of
S_{M}(p_{0},r)
is empty if and only if
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}>2r+\sqrt{c^{2}+r^{2}}
or
|c|>2r+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+r^{2}}
.

Let
L=S(p_{0},r)\cap LC
. Take a local parameterization
\varphi:U\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
of
S(p_{0},r)
given by
\varphi(u,v)=\left(r\cos(u)\sin(v)+a,r\sin(u)\sin(v)+b,r\cos(v)+c\right)
, with
U=(\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{0}+2\pi)\times(0,\pi)
for some
\epsilon_{0}>0
small enough. Then
\tilde{\varphi}:U\setminus L\to S_{M}(p_{0},r)
is a local parameterization of
S_{M}(p_{0},r)
with
\tilde{\varphi}(u,v)=i_{M}(\varphi(u,v))
. Calculating the Gaussian curvature of
S_{M}(p_{0},r)
, the point
\tilde{\varphi}(u,v)
belongs to the parabolic set of
S_{M}(p_{0},r)
if and only if
f(u,v)=0
or
g(u,v)=0
, with

\begin{array}[]{ccl}f(u,v)&=&-a^{2}-b^{2}+c^{2}+r^{2}+4cr\cos(v)+2r^{2}\cos(v)^{2},\\
g(u,v)&=&-a^{2}-b^{2}+c^{2}+4cr\cos(v)^{3}+3r^{2}\cos(2v)-4rh(u)\sin(v)^{3},\end{array}

and
h(u)=a\cos(u)+b\sin(u)
.

Note that
f(u,v)=0
if and only if

\cos(v)=\frac{-2c\pm\sqrt{2}\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}-r^{2}}}{2r}.

(6)

Equation (
6
) has no solutions when
a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}<r^{2}
,
|c|<-2r+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+r^{2}}
or
|c|>2r+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+r^{2}}
.

The image of the function
h
is the closed interval
\left[-\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}},\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\right]
, so
g_{min}(v)\leq g(u,v)\leq g_{max}(v)
for all
(u,v)
in
U
, with

\begin{array}[]{ccl}g_{min}(v)=-a^{2}-b^{2}+c^{2}+4cr\cos(v)^{3}+3r^{2}\cos(2v)-4r\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\sin(v)^{3},\\
g_{max}(v)=-a^{2}-b^{2}+c^{2}+4cr\cos(v)^{3}+3r^{2}\cos(2v)+4r\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\sin(v)^{3}.\end{array}

Computing the critical values of
g_{min}
in
[0,\pi]
, we get that
g_{min}>0
when
|c|>2r+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+r^{2}}
. Likewise,
g_{max}<0
when
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}>2r+\sqrt{c^{2}+r^{2}}
. Hence,
g(u,v)=0
has no solutions if and only if
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}>2r+\sqrt{c^{2}+r^{2}}
or
|c|>2r+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+r^{2}}
.

The parameterization
\varphi
does not include the points
(0,0,\pm r)\in S(p_{0},r)
. However, using other parameterization of
S(p_{0},r)
, it follows that
i_{M}(0,0,\pm r)
does not belong to the parabolic set of
S_{M}(p_{0},r)
when
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}>2r+\sqrt{c^{2}+r^{2}}
or
|c|>2r+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+r^{2}}
.

\Box

A surface
S
has a contact
A_{1}^{+}
with
T_{p}S
at
p\in S
when the height function
h
has a Morse singularity of index 0 or 2 at
p
. A closed surface
S
is an
ovaloid
if it is strictly convex, that is,it has a contact
A_{1}^{+}
with the tangent plane
T_{p}S
, for all
p\in S
. Note that the definition of ovaloid is independent of the metric (Euclidean or Lorentzian) in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
. For example, the Euclidean spheres are ovaloids.

Remark 4.3

A closed surface is an ovaloid if and only if its parabolic set is empty. Indeed, an ovaloid consists of eliptic points.

Corollary 4.4

S_{M}(p_{0},r)
is an ovaloid if and only if
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}>2r+\sqrt{c^{2}+r^{2}}
or
|c|>2r+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+r^{2}}
.

The proof follows from the Theorems
4.1
,
4.2
and Remark
4.3
.

\Box

Given an ovaloid
S
, let
N_{E}:S\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
be the Gauss map of
S
viewed as a surface on Euclidean 3-space and take
N_{E}
pointing to the interior of
S
. For
p\in S
and
r>0
, define
S_{p}(r)
to be the Euclidean sphere with center
p+rN_{E}(p)
and radius
r
. Thus,
S_{p}(r)
and
S
are tangent at
p
for any
r>0
.

Lemma 4.5

Let
S\subset\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
be an ovaloid. Then there exist
R>0
such that
S\setminus\{p\}
is contained inside
S_{p}(R)
, for all
p\in S
.

Let
p
be a point in
S
and
d
the Euclidean distance in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
. Denote by
\theta(q)\in[0,\pi]
the angle between the vectors
q-p
and
N_{E}(p)
, for all
q\in S\setminus\{p\}
.

Since the contact between
S
and
T_{p}S
in
p
is
A_{1}^{+}
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
p
in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}
such that, in
U
, no point of the interior of
S
belongs to
T_{p}S
(considering
T_{p}S
as the plane that passes through
p
). Suppose some
q\in S\setminus\{p\}
belongs to
T_{p}S
. For sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, the point
(1-\epsilon)p+\epsilon q
belong to
U
and
T_{p}S
, since the segment
[p,q]
is contained in
T_{p}S
. Therefore,
[p,q]
is not contained within
S
, which contradicts the convexity of
S
. Therefore,
S\cap T_{p}S=\{p\}
and
\theta(q)\in[0,\pi/2)
for all
q\in S\setminus\{p\}
.

Given
q\in S\setminus\{p\}
, let
q^{\prime}
be the orthogonal projection of
q
to the line passing through
p
with direction
N_{E}(p)
. We have

\begin{array}[]{ccl}d(q,p+rN_{E}(p))^{2}&=&d(q,q^{\prime})^{2}+d(q^{\prime},p+rN_{E}(p))^{2}\\
&=&d(q,q^{\prime})^{2}+\left(d(p,p+rN_{E}(p))-d(p,q^{\prime})\right)^{2}\\
&=&r^{2}-2d(q)r\cos(\theta(q))+d(q)^{2}.\end{array}

(7)

If
2r>d(q)^{2}/\cos(\theta(q))
, it follows from (
7
) that
d(q,p+rN_{E}(p))<r
. Since
S
is compact,

d_{max}=\sup\{d(p,q):q\in S\}<\infty\quad{\rm and}\quad c=\inf\{\cos(\theta(q)):q\in S\}>0

because
\theta(q)\in[0,\pi/2)
. Let
R(p)=d_{max}^{2}/2c
. For all
r>R(p)
the set
S\setminus\{p\}
is inside
S_{p}(r)
. Finally, let
R=\sup\{R(p):p\in S\}+1
, which is finite because
S
is compact. Therefore,
S\setminus\{p\}
is inside
S_{p}(R)
for all
p
.

\Box

Theorem 4.6

Given an ovaloid
S\subset\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
, there is a translation
T:\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
such that
i_{M}(T(S))
is an ovaloid.

Take
R>0
given by the Lemma
4.5
. From the Corollary
4.4
it is possible to obtain a translation
T:\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}^{3}_{1}
such that the Möbius inversion in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathbb{R}$}_{1}^{3}
of
T(S_{p}(R))
is an ovaloid for all
p\in S
.

It follows from item (3) of Theorem
3.1
that
T(S_{p}(R))
does not intersect
LC
for all
p\in S
and also
T(S)
do not intersect
LC
. Thus,
i_{M}(T(S))
is a closed surface.

Given
p\in S
,
i_{M}
is a diffeomorphism between the interior of
T(S_{p}(R))
and the interior of
i_{M}(T(S_{p}(R)))
because
T(S_{p}(R))\cap LC=\emptyset
. So
i_{M}(T(S\setminus\{p\}))
is contained inside
i_{M}(T(S_{p}(R)))
and
i_{M}(T(S))
is tangent to
i_{M}(T(S_{p}(R)))
in
i_{M}(T(p))
. Since
i_{M}(T(S_{p}(R)))
is an ovaloid, the contact between
i_{M}(T(S_{p}(R)))
and its tangent plane at
i_{M}(T(p))
is a
A_{1}^{+}
, then the contact of
i_{M}(T(S))
and its tangent plane at
i_{M}(T(p))
is also
A_{1}^{+}
. Therefore,
i_{M}(T(S))
is an ovaloid.

\Box
