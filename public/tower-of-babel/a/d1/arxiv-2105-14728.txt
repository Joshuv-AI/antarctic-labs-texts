Geophysical fluid models with simple energy backscatter: explicit flows and unbounded exponential growth
A. Prugger † {{\dagger}}, J.D.M. Rademacher † {{\dagger}}, J. Yang ‡ {{\ddagger}}

Abstract
Motivated by numerical schemes for large scale geophysical flow, we consider the rotating shallow water and Boussinesq equations on the whole space with horizontal kinetic energy backscatter source terms built from negative viscosity and stabilising hyperviscosity with constant parameters. We study the impact of this energy input through various explicit flows, which are simultaneously solving the nonlinear equations and the linear equations that arise upon dropping the transport nonlinearity, i.e. the linearisation in the zero state. These include barotropic, parallel and Kolmogorov flows as well as monochromatic inertia gravity waves. With focus on stable stratification we find that the backscatter generates numerous solutions of this type that grow exponentially and unboundedly, also with vertical structure. This signifies the possibility of undesired energy concentration into specific modes due to the backscatter. Families of steady state flows of this type arise as well and superposition principles in the nonlinear equations provide explicit sufficient conditions for instability of some of these. For certain steady barotropic flows of this type we provide numerical evidence of eigenmodes whose growth rates are proportional to the amplitude factor of the flow. For all other arising steady solutions we prove this is not possible. Keywords: explicit flows; instability; plane waves; rotating shallow water equations; rotating Boussinesq equations

Introduction

Spatial resolution of geophysical flows is limited not only in observational data, but also in numerical simulations for ocean and climate studies due to lack of computing power. Towards realistic simulations, this is compensated by so-called parameterisations of subgrid effects, i.e. subgrid models that are gauged a priori and simulate the influence of the missing small scale resolution on the resolved large scale flow. Moreover, these should account for numerical discretisation effects such as over-dissipation, and yet admit stable simulations of ocean and climate models. A practical solution to these problems that has come to frequent use are kinetic energy backscatter schemes that effectively introduce negative horizontal viscosity together with hyperviscosity, cf. e.g.
(
Jansen and Held 2014
;
Zurita-Gotor et al. 2015
;
Jansen et al. 2019
;
Juricke et al. 2020
;
Perezhogin 2020
)
; we refer to
(
Danilov et al. 2019
)
for a detailed discussion and relations to other approaches. Simulations with backscatter have been found to provide energy ‘at the right place’, matching the result more closely to observations and high resolution comparisons.

Motivated by this, we consider the rotating shallow water and Boussinesq equations on the whole space with simplified kinetic energy backscatter source terms built from negative horizontal viscosity and stabilising hyperviscosity with constant parameters. The backscatter terms in the numerical scheme have non-constant coefficients from a coupled energy equation that aims to regulate energy consistency and thus these terms vanish for infinite resolution. Our simplified consideration on the continuum level is amenable to an analysis of qualitative features and energy distribution, so that the results point out potential issues and can guide further study of backscatter schemes.

In particular, this idealisation admits a direct analytical study of the influence of backscatter through its impact on various explicit flows. Indeed, explicit flows are frequently used as a tool for benchmarking analytical and numerical studies in this and other contexts, e.g.
(
Chai et al. 2020
;
Drazin and Riley 2006
;
Dyck and Straatman 2019
;
Majda 2003
;
Majda and Wang 2006
;
Weinbaum and O’Brien 1967
)
, and also for turbulence studies, e.g.
(
Lelong and Dunkerton 1998
;
Ghaemsaidi and
Mathur 2019
;
Onuki et al. 2021
)
.

We start our investigations with the rotating shallow equations and then move to the rotating Boussinesq equations. In the spirit of
(
Prugger and
Rademacher 2021
)
, we consider flows and waves, which are simultaneously solving the nonlinear equations and the linear equation that arise from dropping the transport nonlinearity, i.e. the linearisation in the zero state. These include flows that correspond to barotropic, parallel and Kolmogorov flows as well as monochromatic inertia gravity waves, cf.
(
Achatz 2006
;
Balmforth and Young 2005
;
Prugger and
Rademacher 2021
;
Yau et al. 2004
)
for vanishing backscatter. More specifically, we identify these solutions from superpositions of plane waves with suitably selected wave vectors and wave directions. These are therefore specific eigenmodes of the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
arising from infinitesimal perturbations of the zero state and thus correspond to elements in the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
. Since these are equally solutions of the nonlinear equations we find subspaces of linear dynamics in the nonlinear system, where all these solutions come as a family with a free scaling factor. We confirm that the presence of backscatter implies unstable spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
, i.e. spectrum with positive growth rates, also for stable stratification. Moreover, we identify explicit solutions of the nonlinear problem that grow exponentially and unboundedly,
including solutions whose vertical velocity is growing unboundedly, despite the horizontal nature of the backscatter. This highlights the possibility of undesired concentration of energy due to backscatter, and is in contrast to the targeted energy redistribution. The solutions which have negative real spectrum likewise illustrate the possibility of ineffective energy input into certain scales. However, while solutions to consistent discretisations shadow continuum effects over finite times, the specific implications for numerical backscatter schemes require additional investigations beyond the present study.

We refer to this unbounded exponential growth of linear type in the nonlinear system as
unbounded instability
and note that it does not occur in generic damped-driven evolution equations, where unstable manifolds are nonlinear.
Among these explicit flows there are numerous steady states and we identify admissible superpositions in the nonlinear equations, which provide explicit conditions for unbounded instability in many cases. For certain steady barotropic flows of this type we provide numerical evidence of eigenmodes whose growth rates are proportional to the amplitude factor of these steady flows, thus featuring arbitrarily strong local instability. For all other steady solutions of the type considered, we analyse the resulting large amplitude regime and prove that growth rates must scale sublinear with the amplitude. We next summarise and describe the results in more detail.

These flows are in general not fully geostrophically balanced since the pressure not only compensates the Coriolis force, but also the gradient part of the nonlinear term, which may occur with angular superposition. We determine the loci and stability properties of steady states related to these flows – in particular the unboundedly unstable ones – and also the large amplitude regime. In the same spirit we investigate steady and unboundedly growing explicit flows with vertical structure and coupled buoyancy. These relate to known flows in absence of backscatter: parallel flow, Kolmogorov flow and monochromatic inertia gravity waves (MGWs).
While the existence of parallel flows is unaffected by the (horizontal) backscatter, superposition with small wave number horizontal flows implies unbounded instability of any parallel flow. However, the situation is more subtle for the other flows. Notably, we find that in Kolmogorov flows and MGWs, the purely horizontal backscatter triggers unbounded growth in the vertical velocity. We also identify possible superpositions of Kolmogorov flows and MGWs. Regarding the large amplitude regime, for these flows the growth rates cannot be proportional to their amplitude factors.

2.2
Stability analysis of steady solutions

We study stability of a steady state
(\boldsymbol{v}_{s},\eta_{s})
of (
1.1
) via the linear operator
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{v}_{s},\eta_{s})
, which results from linearising (
1.1
) in
(\boldsymbol{v}_{s},\eta_{s})
. A spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
with positive real part then implies that the steady solution
(\boldsymbol{v}_{s},\eta_{s})
is linearly unstable.
In the following we will show that the trivial steady state
(\boldsymbol{v}_{s},\eta_{s})\equiv(0,0,0)
is linearly unstable, and in certain cases even unboundedly unstable. Afterwards, we focus on the stability of non-trivial steady solutions (
2.2
). We first analyse the unbounded instability of these in the full nonlinear equations (
1.1
) with respect to solutions of the form (
2.2
). Moreover, we study a certain long-wavelength instability in this case and briefly consider the energy of the explicit solutions. Finally, we investigate the linear stability of all steady solutions (
2.2
) with small and large amplitudes.

2.2.1
Linear stability of trivial steady state

For the trivial homogeneous steady solution
(\boldsymbol{v}_{s},\eta_{s})\equiv(0,0,0)
the linearisation of (
1.1
) is exactly (
1.1
) without the nonlinear terms, and the corresponding linear operator
\mathcal{L}
is then the remaining right-hand side of (
1.1
). The spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
in this case can be determined by the dispersion relation

\displaystyle d(\lambda,\boldsymbol{k}):=\det(\lambda\mathrm{Id}-\widehat{\mathcal{L}})=0\,,

with wave vectors
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},k_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, temporal rates
\lambda=\lambda(\boldsymbol{k})\in\mathbb{C}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}=\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{k})
the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{L}
given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{k})=\begin{pmatrix}-d_{1}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{4}+b_{1}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}&f&-\mathrm{i}gk_{1}\\
-f&-d_{2}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{4}+b_{2}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}&-\mathrm{i}gk_{2}\\
-\mathrm{i}H_{0}k_{1}&-\mathrm{i}H_{0}k_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

The dispersion relation is thus explicitly

\displaystyle d(\lambda,\boldsymbol{k})=\lambda^{3}+c_{2}\lambda^{2}+c_{1}\lambda+c_{0}=0\,,

(2.15)

with the coefficients

\displaystyle c_{2}\

\displaystyle:=\ (d_{1}+d_{2})|\boldsymbol{k}|^{4}-(b_{1}+b_{2})|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\,,

\displaystyle c_{1}\

\displaystyle:=\ (d_{1}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{4}-b_{1}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2})(d_{2}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{4}-b_{2}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2})+gH_{0}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}+f^{2}\,,

\displaystyle c_{0}\

\displaystyle:=\ -gH_{0}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\left((b_{1}-d_{1}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2})k_{2}^{2}+(b_{2}-d_{2}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2})k_{1}^{2}\right)\,.

2.2

\boldsymbol{B}
given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})+\nabla p\,,

with a pressure
p
, that is trivial for the rotating shallow water equations (
1.1
) with
\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{v},\eta)
, and otherwise will derive from the incompressibility constraint
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0
.

We assume there exists a family of steady state solutions
\boldsymbol{u}=a\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
with amplitude parameter
a\in\mathbb{R}
and associated (possibly trivial) pressure
p=ap_{0}
. The spectral stability of the steady state
a\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
is determined by the linearised right-hand side in
a\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
and thus the solutions to the generalised eigenvalue problem

\lambda\boldsymbol{U}=\mathcal{L}_{a}\boldsymbol{U}+\nabla P\,,\quad\mathcal{L}_{a}:=\boldsymbol{L}+a\boldsymbol{L}_{0}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{L}_{0}:=\boldsymbol{B}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{u}_{0})+\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\cdot)\,,

with eigenvalue parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, eigenmode
\boldsymbol{U}=(\boldsymbol{v},\eta)
and
P
either trivial or determined by the linearised constraint
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0
.

Since the resulting spectrum is locally uniformly continuous with respect to the parameter
a
, we immediately note that for
|a|\ll 1
it is close to that for
a=0
associated to
\boldsymbol{L}
. Since its spectrum is unstable for the backscatter setting, as shown in the linear stability analysis of the zero state above, it follows that all the discussed explicit flows for small amplitudes inherit unstable modes of the trivial state, more so for smaller amplitudes.

Regarding large amplitudes,
|a|\gg 1
, we consider eigenvalue parameters that scale with the amplitude, i.e.
\lambda=a\widetilde{\lambda}
, and set
P=a\widetilde{P}
. This gives the (generalised) eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}\boldsymbol{U}=(a^{-1}\boldsymbol{L}+\boldsymbol{L}_{0})\boldsymbol{U}+\nabla\widetilde{P}\,.

(2.16)

The operator of the limiting problem, as
|a|\to\infty
, is
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
, and again by continuity of the spectrum, its stability properties partially predict those of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
for
|a|\gg 1
. In particular, an unstable eigenmode of
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
implies strongly unstable eigenmodes of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
for
|a|\gg 1
, for which the growth rate
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)
is proportional to the amplitude
a
of the steady solution. However, eigenmodes of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
for which
\lambda
is not proportional to
a
will move to the origin in the scaled operator as
|a|\to\infty
, and thus contribute to the kernel of
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
. In particular,
a\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
may be unstable for all
a
even though
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
does not possess unstable spectrum. Indeed, this turns out to be the case in the present setting. This is consistent with the unstable rates
\lambda
of the explicit flows from the analysis of unbounded instability above, which are associated with unbounded growth, as these are constant with respect to
a
so that in the scaling of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
satisfy
\widetilde{\lambda}=\lambda/a\to 0
as
a\to\infty
.

Hence, we consider the limiting problem

\widetilde{\lambda}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{L}_{0}\boldsymbol{U}+\nabla\widetilde{P}\,,

whose spectral properties do not seem to be known analytically for the explicit flows we are concerned with. Specifically, for (
2.2
) we have
\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{v},\eta)
, the bilinear form is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}\Big((\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\eta_{1}),(\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\eta_{2})\Big)=\begin{pmatrix}-(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\\
-(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\nabla)\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

and the steady state family is generated by
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\eta_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}
from (
2.2
), i.e. with
\xi=\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\cos(\xi)\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\,,\quad\eta_{0}=\alpha_{2}\sin(\xi)+s\,,

where
\alpha_{2}
is chosen so that (
2.3b
) holds. We are then interested in the spectrum of the operator
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\hfill\\
(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\cdot\nabla)\eta+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\eta_{0}+\eta_{0}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\eta\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\xi)(\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}-(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})\sin(\xi)\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\hfill\\
\cos(\xi)(\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla)\eta+\alpha_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})\cos(\xi)+(\alpha_{2}\sin(\xi)+s)(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\end{pmatrix}\,.

We immediately note that the kernel of
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
is infinite dimensional: any perturbation
\boldsymbol{v},\,\eta
of the same form as the steady flow
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})=\phi_{1}(\xi)\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}
,
\eta(\boldsymbol{x})=\phi_{2}(\xi)
with arbitrary
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
, lies in the kernel, since
(\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla)(\boldsymbol{v},\eta)=0
as well as
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}=0
and
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0
(in fact, one can show that here
\boldsymbol{v}
can be an arbitrary function of
\xi
). Next, we show that the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
is purely imaginary.

First let
\alpha_{2}=s=0
, i.e. the steady state
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
is in the intersection of the sets of steady and time-dependent solutions as in Figure
1
, so that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\xi)(\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}-(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})\sin(\xi)\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\\
\cos(\xi)(\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla)\eta\hfill\end{pmatrix}\,.

It is a diagonal operator where
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\eta
are decoupled. Let us change coordinates to
\xi=k_{1}x+k_{2}y
,
\zeta=-k_{2}x+k_{1}y
. Then
\nabla
becomes
(k_{1}\partial_{\xi}-k_{2}\partial_{\zeta},k_{2}\partial_{\xi}+k_{1}\partial_{\zeta})
so that
\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla
turns into
K\partial_{\zeta}
, where
K:=k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}
. We thus obtain the operator

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\xi)K\partial_{\zeta}\boldsymbol{v}-(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})\sin(\xi)\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\\
\cos(\xi)K\partial_{\zeta}\eta\hfill\end{pmatrix}\,,

whose Fourier transform with respect to
\zeta
with wave number parameter
\vartheta
read

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}i\cos(\xi)K\vartheta\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})\sin(\xi)\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\\
i\cos(\xi)K\vartheta\hat{\eta}\hfill\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}i\cos(\xi)K\vartheta\mathrm{Id}-\sin(\xi)A(\boldsymbol{k})&0\\
0&i\cos(\xi)K\vartheta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}\,,

where
A(\boldsymbol{k})=\begin{pmatrix}-k_{1}k_{2}&-k_{2}^{2}\\
k_{1}^{2}&k_{1}k_{2}\end{pmatrix}
.

The lower right entry, which corresponds to
\eta
, is a multiplication operator by
i\cos(\xi)K\vartheta
and so its spectrum is the range of this function, which is
iK\vartheta[-1,1]\subset i\mathbb{R}
. Since this multiplication operator appears in the upper left entry as multiplying the identity, which commutes with any matrix,
A(\boldsymbol{k})
can be brought to normal form. This features a double zero eigenvalue so that the operator on the upper left block possesses purely imaginary spectrum. In particular, the spectrum is neutrally stable.

For
\alpha_{2},\,s\neq 0
and writing
\boldsymbol{v}=(u,v)
we analogously obtain the transformed operator

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}i\cos(\xi)K\vartheta\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})\sin(\xi)\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\hfill\\
i\cos(\xi)K\vartheta\hat{\eta}+\mathcal{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\hfill\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{1}&0\\
\mathcal{B}&\mathcal{A}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}\,,

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}

\displaystyle:=-i\cos(\xi)K\vartheta\mathrm{Id}+\sin(\xi)A(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}

\displaystyle:=-i\cos(\xi)K\vartheta

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle:=-\alpha_{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})\cos(\xi)+(\alpha_{2}\sin(\xi)+s)(k_{1}\partial_{\xi}\hat{u}+k_{2}\partial_{\xi}\hat{v}+i\vartheta(k_{1}\hat{v}-k_{2}\hat{u}))\,.

If
\lambda
lies in the resolvent set of both operators on the diagonal
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
(by the above this includes any non-purely imaginary value), then
\lambda
is also in the resolvent set of the present
\widehat{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}
, since

\displaystyle(\widehat{\boldsymbol{L}}_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(\mathcal{A}_{1}-\lambda)^{-1}&0\\
-(\mathcal{A}_{2}-\lambda)^{-1}\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{A}_{1}-\lambda)^{-1}&(\mathcal{A}_{2}-\lambda)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Hence, as claimed, the asymptotic operator possesses marginally stable spectrum and we cannot immediately infer in/stability information for large amplitudes. However, numerical computations based on truncated Fourier series suggest that the spectrum is in fact rather strongly unstable, cf. Figure
7
.

3
Rotating Boussinesq equations with backscatter

We now turn to the study of various explicit solutions in the rotating Boussinesq equations augmented with backscatter (
1.3
). To ease notation, we write these in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial t}+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}+f\boldsymbol{e_{3}}\times\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla p-\boldsymbol{e_{3}}\mathtt{b}

\displaystyle=-\bigl(\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})\Delta^{2}+\mathrm{diag}(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})\Delta\bigr)\boldsymbol{v}

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=0

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathtt{b}}{\partial t}+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathtt{b}+N^{2}v_{3}

\displaystyle=\mu\Delta\mathtt{b}\,,

(3.1c)

where we focus on horizontal backscatter
d_{1},\,d_{2},\,b_{1},\,b_{2}>0
with usual viscosity vertically,
d_{3}=0,\,b_{3}=-\nu\leq 0
, and stable stratification
N^{2}>0
. For comparison and illustration we also discuss briefly the usual horizontal viscous or inviscid cases
d_{1}=d_{2}=0
,
b_{1},\,b_{2}\leq 0
, unstable stratification
N^{2}<0
, and artificial vertical backscatter
d_{3},\,b_{3}>0
.
As in §
2
, we are especially interested in the parameter relations and stability properties of steady solutions, in particular unbounded instability, as well as in unboundedly growing explicit solutions. We first investigate the horizontal flows, which are comparable with the explicit solutions of the shallow water equations in §
2
, but are less restricted and have additional properties in this case here. Afterwards, we analyse other explicit solutions with vertical structure and coupled buoyancy.

3.1
Horizontal flow and decoupled system

In order to compare with the results of the rotating shallow water equations with backscatter, we consider here the barotropic case with horizontal velocity field. We therefore choose a velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}
that is independent of the vertical coordinate
z
and has
v_{3}\equiv 0
, as well as a horizontally independent buoyancy
\mathtt{b}=\mathtt{b}(t,z)
. This ansatz yields the reduced equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial t}+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}+f\boldsymbol{v}^{\perp}+\nabla\tilde{p}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{diag}\bigl(d_{1}\Delta+b_{1},d_{2}\Delta+b_{2}\bigr)\Delta\boldsymbol{v}

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=0

(3.2b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathtt{b}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mu\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\mathtt{b}\,,

(3.2c)

with gradient and Laplacian for the horizontal directions
\boldsymbol{x}=(x,y)^{\mathsf{T}}
,
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\mathtt{b}=\mathtt{b}(t,z)
and
p=\tilde{p}+B(t,z)
, where
\frac{\partial B(t,z)}{\partial z}=\mathtt{b}(t,z)
. Here the buoyancy is decoupled from the velocity field and determined by the linear heat equation (
3.2c
); on the idealized whole space this can be readily solved by Fourier transform.

Regarding the momentum equations, compared with the shallow water equations we may view the equation for the fluid depth (
1.1c
) to be replaced by the incompressibility condition (
3.2b
). This is less restrictive and admits a larger set of explicit flow solutions as discussed in
(
Prugger and
Rademacher 2021
)
for the setting without backscatter. In particular, the form (
2.2
) for
\boldsymbol{v}
satisfies (
3.2b
) and can readily be adjusted to solutions of (
3.2a
). However, there are no additional a priori constraints for the pressure akin to condition (
1.1c
) or (
2.3c
), so that the resulting pressure can be exponentially decaying or growing along with the velocity field. Moreover, linear combinations of any of these solutions with the same wave vector direction but different wavelength (any wave vector on a whole ray like in Figure
1(c)
) also yield explicit solutions of (
3.2
).

The resulting set of explicit solutions of (
3.2a
) for which the nonlinear terms vanish can be identified by the following ansatz for wave shape
\psi
and pressure profile
\phi

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}=\psi(t,\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\,,\quad\tilde{p}=f\phi(t,\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})\,,

(3.3)

where
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and without loss of generality
|\boldsymbol{k}|=1
by the freedom in choosing
\psi
and
\phi
. Substitution into (
3.2a
) gives the linear equations for
\psi
and
\phi
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-(d_{1}k_{2}^{2}+d_{2}k_{1}^{2})\frac{\partial^{4}\psi}{\partial\xi^{4}}-(b_{1}k_{2}^{2}+b_{2}k_{1}^{2})\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\xi^{2}}

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{1}k_{2}}{f}\left((d_{1}-d_{2})\frac{\partial^{4}\psi}{\partial\xi^{4}}+(b_{1}-b_{2})\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\xi^{2}}\right)+\psi\,.

(3.4b)

For the Boussinesq equations with viscosity instead of the backscatter terms similar equations arise, cf.
(
Prugger and
Rademacher 2021
)
. However, in that case the pressure gradient fully compensates the buoyancy and the Coriolis term in the equations, which makes the velocity field geostrophically balanced. In contrast, in the present case, equation (
3.4b
) for the pressure shape
\phi
allows the pressure gradient to not only compensate the full Coriolis term, but also part of the backscatter terms. In particular, the velocity field (
3.3
) with (
3.4
) is in general not geostrophically balanced.

3.1.1
Superposition principles

The general wave shape
\psi
in (
3.3
) also contains the superpositions of arbitrary many sinusoidal waves in the
same wave vector direction

\boldsymbol{k}
and
any wave number

|\boldsymbol{k}|
. This is possible in the Boussinesq equations, since
\nabla p
in the momentum equation (
3.1a
) is not further constrained, unlike (
1.1c
) for
\nabla\eta
. It is also possible to superpose by integrating over the wave numbers in the same wave vector direction.

The structure of the Boussinesq equations admits superposing
\boldsymbol{v}
in the form (
2.2
) in another way, namely with
different wave vector directions
, but the
same wave number
, cf.
(
Prugger and
Rademacher 2021
)
. For the decoupled momentum equation (
3.2a
), and finite superposition of
N
waves with arbitary
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the resulting superposed sinusoidal explicit solutions of a form similar to (
3.3
) are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{\lambda_{i}t}\psi_{i}\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}_{i}\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\psi_{i}=\alpha_{i}\sin(\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}+\tau_{i})\,,\quad 1\leq i\leq N\,,

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}e^{(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})t}\Bigl((\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}_{j})\psi_{i}\psi_{j}+s^{2}\frac{\partial\psi_{i}}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial\psi_{j}}{\partial\xi}\Bigr)-f\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}e^{\lambda_{i}t}\frac{\partial\psi_{i}}{\partial\xi}\,,

(3.5b)

for any fixed
s>0
and arbitrary
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
\tau_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{k}_{i}=(k_{i,1},k_{i,2})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
|\boldsymbol{k}_{i}|=s
for any
1\leq i\leq N
. Here, each
\lambda_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}
is defined by

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle=(b_{1}-d_{1}s^{2})k_{i,2}^{2}+(b_{2}-d_{2}s^{2})k_{i,1}^{2}\,,

(3.6a)

\displaystyle f\frac{\gamma_{i}-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=\bigl((d_{1}-d_{2})s^{2}+b_{2}-b_{1}\bigr)k_{i,1}k_{i,2}\,,

(3.6b)

in order to solve (
3.2a
). Since each wave in (
3.5a
) is divergence free, the whole superposed velocity
\boldsymbol{v}
solves (
3.2b
) and thus is an explicit solution of (
3.2
).

It is also possible to superpose explicit solutions of the form (
3.5
) by integrating over the whole circle
S_{s}:=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid|\boldsymbol{k}|=s\}
for any fixed
s>0
. The exact form is then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\int\displaylimits_{S_{s}}\alpha(\boldsymbol{k})e^{\lambda(\boldsymbol{k})t}\sin\bigl(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}+\tau(\boldsymbol{k})\bigr)\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}d\boldsymbol{k}

(3.7a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{p}(t,\boldsymbol{x})&=-s^{4}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{\varphi_{1}}^{2\pi}\Bigl(\cos\bigl(\xi_{1}\bigr)\cos\bigl(\xi_{2}\bigr)+\cos(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})\sin\bigl(\xi_{1}\bigr)\sin\bigl(\xi_{2}\bigr)\Bigr)\\
&\hskip 12.80373pt\cdot\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}e^{(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})t}d\varphi_{2}d\varphi_{1}-f\int\displaylimits_{S_{s}}\gamma(\boldsymbol{k})e^{\lambda(\boldsymbol{k})t}\cos\bigl(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}+\tau(\boldsymbol{k})\bigr)d\boldsymbol{k}\,,\end{split}

(3.7b)

where, for
i=1,\,2
, we set
\alpha_{i}:=\alpha(\boldsymbol{k}_{i})
with
\boldsymbol{k}_{i}:=s\bigl(\cos(\varphi_{i}),\sin(\varphi_{i})\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}
,
\lambda_{i}:=\lambda(\boldsymbol{k}_{i})
and
\xi_{i}:=\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}+\tau(\boldsymbol{k}_{i})
, for all
0\leq\varphi_{i}<2\pi
. Sufficient for the convergence of the integrals is
\alpha\in L^{1}(S_{s})
,
\tau\in L^{\infty}(S_{s})
and for almost all
\boldsymbol{k}\in S_{s}
we require

\displaystyle\alpha(\boldsymbol{k})\lambda(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\alpha(\boldsymbol{k})\bigl((b_{1}-d_{1}s^{2})k_{2}^{2}+(b_{2}-d_{2}s^{2})k_{1}^{2}\bigr)\,,

(3.8a)

\displaystyle f\bigl(\gamma(\boldsymbol{k})-\alpha(\boldsymbol{k})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\alpha(\boldsymbol{k})k_{1}k_{2}\bigl((d_{1}-d_{2})s^{2}+b_{2}-b_{1}\bigr)\,,

(3.8b)

corresponding to (
3.6
) if
\alpha(\boldsymbol{k})\neq 0
.

The explicit solutions (
3.5
) with (
3.6
) differ from (
3.3
) with (
3.4
), as well as the other explicit solutions before, not only by the structure of the superposition, but also by the resulting nonlinear terms, which are not vanishing. Due to the special structure of
\boldsymbol{v}
in (
3.5
) and
|\boldsymbol{k}_{i}|=s
for all
1\leq i\leq N
, the nonlinear terms form a gradient that can be fully compensated by the pressure gradient in the momentum equation; this gives the first sum in (
3.5b
). The same holds for the solutions (
3.7
) with (
3.8
) correspondingly. We refer to
(
Prugger and
Rademacher 2021
)
for further discussion and literature references for explicit solutions with gradient nonlinearities without backscatter.

Comparing the explicit solutions (
3.5
) with (
3.6
), as well as (
3.7
) with (
3.8
), with those without backscatter we notice two major differences: First, in the present case the amplitudes of the pressure can be different from those of the velocity; the conditions on the amplitudes are given in (
3.6b
) and (
3.8b
) respectively. The reason is that the pressure gradient in the momentum equation can additionally compensate a part of the backscatter terms as well.
Second, the growth rates
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda(\boldsymbol{k})
can be different, so that each wave is decaying or growing differently. Both differences require anisotropy in the backscatter of the momentum equation, i.e.
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
or
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
. Indeed, the usual viscosity is isotropic in this sense.

In conclusion, the above constructions of explicit solutions can be viewed as superposition principles for the (nonlinear) Boussinesq equations in this setting: (
3.3
) expresses a
radial superposition principle
of flows in the same wave vector direction, and (
3.5
) an
angular superposition principle
of flows on the same scale. Superpositions of plane waves with different wave vector directions and scales are, in general, not giving solutions.

3.1.2
Unbounded instability of steady states

Analogous to §
2.2.2
, the possible superpositions of explicit solutions, as contained in (
3.3
), imply linear subspaces with linear dynamics, in particular unbounded exponential growth of perturbations. Compared with the rotating shallow water equations, restrictions on wave vectors are absent, and in this section we discuss implications for (in)stability of steady solutions, i.e. those with zero growth rate
\lambda_{i}
in (
3.6a
).

Since
b_{1},\,b_{2},\,d_{1},\,d_{2}>0
in (
3.6a
) we have
\lambda_{i}>0
for any sufficiently small wave numbers
s
and
\lambda_{i}<0
for any sufficiently large ones. In particular, the trivial flow with
\boldsymbol{v}\equiv 0
is unstable with exponential unbounded growth with respect to any small
s
. More importantly, the above radial superposition principle immediately implies that the same holds for every
single-wave
steady solution (
3.3
): Such steady explicit solutions also arise from (
3.5
) with
N=1
and
\lambda_{1}=0
in (
3.6a
). Since the definition of
\lambda_{i}
in (
3.6a
) is exactly the same as in (
2.4a
), we can use here the results from §
2.1.2
about the growth rate
\lambda
as well. Hence, for any
b_{1},\,b_{2},\,d_{1},\,d_{2}>0
the set of solutions with
\lambda_{1}=0
forms a simple closed curve around the origin in the wave vector space that is symmetric with respect to axis reflections and whose interior is star shaped. Furthermore,
\lambda
is positive in the interior of this closed curve, except
\lambda=0
at the origin
\boldsymbol{k}=(0,0)^{\mathsf{T}}
, and
\lambda
is negative outside the closed curve. Thus,
single-wave
steady solutions (
3.3
) exist in any direction and can be radially superposed with explicit solutions with any smaller wave numbers
s
, which makes them unboundedly unstable.

However, in the case
b_{1}/d_{1}\neq b_{2}/d_{2}
, for fixed
s
there are up to four wave vectors for which
\lambda_{i}=0
; to see this note that for
\lambda_{i}=0
(
3.6a
) is linear in

b_{1}/d_{1}\neq b_{2}/d_{2}

First, we note that for
b_{1}/d_{1}\neq b_{2}/d_{2}

\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{max}}
with length
s_{\mathrm{max}}

3.6a
) is satisfied with
\lambda_{i}=0
. See Figure

for a typical example. Indeed, due to the aformentioned structures,

is along an axis, though the set of
\boldsymbol{k}
with

3.5

s=s_{\mathrm{max}}
is built from
\pm\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{max}}
, which lie on the same line in wave vector space. Thus, we can use the radial superposition principle with the exponentially growing explicit solutions of smaller wave numbers, which leads to unbounded growth with respect to modes on any larger scale. The same applies for steady solutions with minimal wave vector length
s_{\mathrm{min}}
.

Second, we consider steady superposed solutions (
3.5

that can be built from two different directions, cf. the white dots in Figure

. Here, we apply the angular superposition principle and superpose with any explicit solution (
3.5
) on the same scale, i.e. whose wave vector has the same length
s
, cf. the white circle in Figure
8(b)
. Since for some wave vectors the corresponding
\lambda
defined by (
3.6a

s<s_{\mathrm{max}}
, at least for the wave vector
s/s_{\mathrm{max}}\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{max}}

s=s_{\mathrm{min}}

In case
b_{1}/d_{1}=b_{2}/d_{2}
we cannot infer the instability of steady superposed solutions (
3.5

.

3.1.3
Linear stability of steady states with small and large amplitudes

After the investigation of unbounded instability of steady states, we now turn to the linear stability of steady solutions with small and large amplitudes, analogous to §
2.2.3
. Concerning small amplitudes, we are naturally led to linear and spectral stability of the zero state, as for the rotating shallow water equations. Instead of analysing the full spectrum of the linearisation of (
3.2
) in the trivial steady flow
\boldsymbol{v}\equiv 0
, here we restrict attention to instability with respect to eigenmodes of the form of the horizontal flows (
3.3
), which means solutions to (
3.4a
). The Fourier transform of (
3.4a
) yields the dispersion relation for perturbation wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and temporal rate
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

d_{\psi}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{k})=-(d_{1}k_{2}^{2}+d_{2}k_{1}^{2})|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}+b_{1}k_{2}^{2}+b_{2}k_{1}^{2}-\lambda=0\,,

which is of course equivalent to (
3.6a
) with
\lambda=\lambda_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
. The above discussion for steady states built from single direction wave vectors
\pm\boldsymbol{k}
implies that the spectrum of the linearisation in such horizontal flows is at least as unstable as that of the zero state in this wave vector direction, since the spectra contain the growth rates of the corresponding explicit solutions in this direction. Of course the result is much stronger in that these modes actually grow unboundedly in the nonlinear Boussinesq equations. In contrast, for the linearisation of (
3.2
) in steady multi-mode horizontal flow, similar comparison of its spectrum with that of the zero state holds, but with different wave vector directions and the same wave number
|\boldsymbol{k}|=s
.

However, analogous to §
2.2.3
, any (superposed) steady horizontal flow inherit the instability of any unstable mode in the dispersion relation
d_{\psi}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{k})=0
of the zero state for sufficiently small amplitudes
0<|\alpha_{i}|\ll 1
,
1\leq i\leq N
, though the growth induced by such modes may be bounded. Recall that the explicit solutions of (
3.2
) also satisfy the full rotating Boussinesq equations (
3.1
), which admit modes that have vertical structures and are coupled with buoyancy. In §
3.2
we will discuss such explicit solutions of (
3.1
), which also satisfy these equations without the nonlinear terms. In particular, unboundedly growing flows of this type provide additional explicit unstable modes of the zero state
\boldsymbol{v}\equiv 0
, which – in contrast to the horizontal flows – are also influenced by the Brunt-Väisälä frequency
N^{2}
, thermal diffusivity
\mu
and the vertical viscosity. In addition, these imply linear instability modes of horizontal flows with sufficiently small amplitudes
0<|\alpha_{i}|\ll 1
,
1\leq i\leq N
.

As to large amplitude(s), where
1\ll|\alpha_{i}|
for at least one
i
, we first note that, in the notation of §
2.2.3
and with
\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{v},\mathtt{b})
, the bilinear form for the present case reads

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}\Big((\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\mathtt{b}_{1}),(\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\mathtt{b}_{2})\Big)=-\begin{pmatrix}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\\
(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\nabla)\mathtt{b}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,.

The steady state family
\boldsymbol{u}=a\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
in this case has
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\mathtt{b}_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}
and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\sin(\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\cdot x+\tau_{i})\boldsymbol{k}_{i}^{\perp}\,,

where we consider in this section only horizontal (wave) vectors
\boldsymbol{k}:=(\hat{\boldsymbol{k}},0)^{\mathsf{T}}
,
\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}:=(\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\perp},0)^{\mathsf{T}}
for any
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Here the third component of
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
vanishes, and
\mathtt{b}_{0}
is an arbitrary constant solving (
3.2c
).
In order to locate strongly unstable modes, whose growth rates
\lambda=a\widetilde{\lambda}
are proportional to the amplitude parameter
a
, we are concerned with the generalised eigenvalue problem (
2.16
), as
|a|\to\infty
, which here reads

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\mathtt{b}\end{pmatrix}=\boldsymbol{L}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\mathtt{b}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\bar{p}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\,,

(3.9)

with
p=a\bar{p}
and for the operator
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\mathtt{b}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\hfill\\
(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\cdot\nabla)\mathtt{b}\end{pmatrix}\,.

In order to simplify and illustrate the main finding, we investigate the stability of a certain superposed steady solution and reduce to only two modes, i.e.
\alpha_{i}=0
for
i>2
, translate so that
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=0
, and set
\xi=\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}
,
\zeta=\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}
so that with a certain wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\alpha_{1}\sin(\xi)\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}+\alpha_{2}\sin(\zeta)\boldsymbol{k}\,.

We note, that even in the anisotropic case there is at least one wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}
, such that both
\boldsymbol{k}
and
\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}
correspond to a steady mode. We omit the full proof, and instead explain the existence of such
\boldsymbol{k}
based on Figure
8
. We start with superposed steady solutions with wave number
s=s_{\max}
as in Figure
8(a)
. Reducing
s
towards the wave number
s_{\min}
as in Figure
8(b)
, there is an intermediate value of
s
such that a wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}
with
|\boldsymbol{k}|=s
exists, for which
\boldsymbol{k}
and
\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}
each correspond to a single mode steady solution. This is ensured by the symmetry of the curve defined by (
3.6
) with
\lambda_{i}=0
. We then obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\mathtt{b}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}\sin(\xi)(\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}+\alpha_{2}\sin(\zeta)(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}+\alpha_{1}\cos(\xi)(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}+\alpha_{2}\cos(\zeta)(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp})\boldsymbol{k}\hfill\\
\alpha_{1}\sin(\xi)(\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla)\mathtt{b}+\alpha_{2}\sin(\zeta)(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\nabla)\mathtt{b}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{diag}(L_{11},L_{22},L_{22})\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\mathtt{b}\end{pmatrix}\,,

with block diagonal matrix operator in which
L_{11}
is the 2-by-2 matrix operator

\displaystyle L_{11}=L_{22}\mathrm{Id}+\alpha_{1}\cos(\xi)A(\boldsymbol{k})-\alpha_{2}\cos(\zeta)A^{\mathsf{T}}(\boldsymbol{k})\,,

with
A(\boldsymbol{k})
as in §
2.2.3
(for which
A^{\mathsf{T}}(\boldsymbol{k})=-A(\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp})
) and

\displaystyle L_{22}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\sin(\xi)(\boldsymbol{k}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla)+\alpha_{2}\sin(\zeta)(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\nabla)\,.

Taking the divergence of (
3.9
) gives, using
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0
, the linear pressure Poisson equation
\Delta\bar{p}=\nabla\cdot(L_{1}\boldsymbol{v})
, with block matrix operator
L_{1}:=\mathrm{diag}(L_{11},L_{22})
. We denote the solution as
\bar{p}=\Delta^{-1}\nabla\cdot(L_{1}\boldsymbol{v})
and substitution into (
3.9
) yields the eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\mathtt{b}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\boldsymbol{L}_{0}+\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\Delta^{-1}\nabla\cdot(L_{1}\circ)&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\mathtt{b}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.10)

in which
\circ
denotes the slot for
\boldsymbol{v}
. The resulting operator on the right-hand side features a diagonal block structure such that the spectrum is the union of the spectra of
-L_{22}
and
-\widetilde{L}_{1}
defined by

\widetilde{L}_{1}:=L_{1}-\nabla\Delta^{-1}\nabla\cdot(L_{1}\circ)\,.

Since
L_{22}
is skew-adjoint (
\mathrm{i}L_{22}
is self-adjoint on suitable spaces), its spectrum is purely imaginary. In case the steady state is a single mode flow, i.e.
\alpha_{i}=0
for
i>1
, we readily infer as in §
2.2.3
that the spectrum of
-\widetilde{L}_{1}
is also purely imaginary, so that the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
is purely imaginary.

Otherwise, if
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\neq 0
, it appears difficult to determine the spectrum of
\widetilde{L}_{1}
analytically and we resort to numerical computations. For this let
(\cdot)_{m}
denote the projection onto the mode
\exp(\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})
. Then

\displaystyle(\widetilde{L}_{1}\boldsymbol{v})_{m}=(L_{1}\boldsymbol{v})_{m}-\frac{\boldsymbol{k}_{m}\cdot(L_{1}\boldsymbol{v})_{m}}{|\boldsymbol{k}_{m}|^{2}}\boldsymbol{k}_{m}=(\mathrm{Id}-B(\boldsymbol{k}_{m}))(L_{1}\boldsymbol{v})_{m}\,,

(3.11)

with suitable matrix
B(\boldsymbol{k}_{m})
. This admits straightforward numerical computation of spectra on truncated Fourier series. We plot results for an example in Figure
9
, which gives unstable spectrum and thus strong evidence for unstable spectrum of
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
.

Shown is an approximation of part of the spectrum of

. Using (

) we reduced to two-dimensional wave vectors by fixing the third component of

, and Bloch modes in the first component from the grid with distance

. In particular, the spectrum is unstable, so that large amplitude solutions are linear unstable with growth rates proportional to the amplitude.

Notably, this means that for steady states that are mixed mode flows (

) it is possible that linear growth rates are proportional to the amplitude parameter

. In contrast, such modes do not exist for steady single mode flows since in this case the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
is purely imaginary, as in §

. Again we remark that we expect the growth induced by these modes in the nonlinear system is bounded.

3.2
Flows with vertical structure and coupled buoyancy

The rotating Boussinesq equations with backscatter (
3.1
) also admit explicit solutions of different form in which the velocity and the buoyancy are coupled, and in which the vertical dependence and velocity component is non-trivial. Here we investigate parallel flows, Kolmogorov flows and monochromatic inertia gravity waves. As before, we are particularly interested in the occurrence of unboundedly growing explicit solutions as well as the existence of such steady solutions and their stability properties.

3.2.1
Parallel flow

This class of explicit flows is well-known in the inviscid and viscous case, e.g.
(
Wang 1990
)
. It possesses only a vertical velocity component and is thus different from the horizontal flows, and admits more general dependence on the horizontal space variables. Specifically,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=w(t,x,y)\boldsymbol{e_{3}}\,,\quad\mathtt{b}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\tilde{\mathtt{b}}(t,x,y)\,,\quad p(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\tilde{p}(t)z\,,

(3.12)

where
w
and
\tilde{\mathtt{b}}
satisfy (with horizontal Laplacian and bi-Laplacian)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}+\left(d_{3}\Delta^{2}+b_{3}\Delta\right)w+\tilde{p}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathtt{b}}

(3.13a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathtt{b}}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\tilde{\mathtt{b}}

\displaystyle=-N^{2}w\,.

(3.13b)

Recall that kinetic energy backscatter, which has
d_{3}=0
and
b_{3}\leq 0
, has no vertical impact so that parallel flows are in fact independent of backscatter. Plane wave parallel flows can be superposed with the horizontal flows (
3.3
) that have zero buoyancy, if their wave vector directions
\boldsymbol{k}
are the same. In this case the orthogonality conditions for wave vectors and wave directions are satisfied and the nonlinear terms vanish, so that the superposition of both solutions is also an explicit solution due to the remaining linear system. Thus, a priori, any parallel flow of this form is unboundedly unstable concerning perturbations (
3.3
) with the same wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}
and small enough wave number
|\boldsymbol{k}|
.

Existence and dynamics of parallel flows can be inferred from the dispersion relation of the linear equations (
3.13
). By Fourier transformation with wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}
and growth rate
\lambda
this is given by

\displaystyle\det(\lambda\mathrm{Id}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{L}})=0\,,\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{L}}:=\begin{pmatrix}-d_{3}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{4}+b_{3}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}&1\\
-N^{2}&-\mu|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

or equivalently as the characteristic polynomial

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}+c_{1}\lambda+c_{0}=0\,,

(3.14)

where
c_{1}:=d_{3}K^{2}+(\mu-b_{3})K
and
c_{0}:=\mu(d_{3}K-b_{3})K^{2}+N^{2}
with
K:=|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\geq 0
.
Steady solutions to (
3.13
) require constant
\tilde{p}
and consist of Fourier modes with
\boldsymbol{k}\neq 0
that solve (
3.14
) with
\lambda=0
, i.e.
c_{0}=0
. Growing spatially non-constant solutions to (
3.13
) exist if and only if (
3.14
) possesses a root with positive real part and
\boldsymbol{k}\neq 0
, and then do so exponentially and unboundedly. Note that both roots have negative real parts only for
c_{1},\,c_{0}>0
and complex conjugate solutions can be superposed to form a real parallel flow solution.

With vertical viscosity,
d_{3}=0
and
b_{3}<0
, and focusing on non-constant solutions
\boldsymbol{k}\neq 0
, we have
c_{1}>0
and
c_{0}=-\mu b_{3}K^{2}+N^{2}
. So steady states require
N^{2}<0
and then
K^{2}=N^{2}/(\mu b_{3})
, or
\mu=N^{2}=0
and any
K
. For
\mu>0
also the unstable case
c_{0}<0
requires unstable stratification
N^{2}<0
, and then
c_{0}<0
occurs on a disc of wave vectors.

Regarding small amplitudes, analogous to §
3.1.3
, any steady (or decaying) parallel flow with small amplitude is unstable, though typically not unboundedly, with respect to unstable modes of the trivial steady state that are exhausted for decreasing amplitude.
In the large amplitude scaling, the resulting operator
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
for steady parallel flow
w_{0}
is a lower triangular matrix operator with diagonal entries
L_{1}:=w_{0}(x,y)\partial_{z}
. Hence, as for the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
in §
3.1.3
, the spectrum is given by the diagonal entries. For any (smooth)
w_{0}
the operator
L_{1}
is skew self-adjoint, similar to
L_{22}
in §
3.1.3
, and thus the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
is purely imaginary. Hence, no real parts of the spectrum of the steady parallel flow are proportional to its amplitude
a
.

Finally, in order to illustrate the abstract structure and in preparation of the flows discussed below, next we briefly consider the artificial case
d_{3},\,b_{3}>0
.

Without thermal diffusion (
\mu=0
), we have
c_{0}=N^{2}
and
c_{1}=\delta_{3}(K):=(d_{3}K-b_{3})K
. For stable stratification,
N^{2}>0
, growing Fourier modes occur if and only if
c_{1}<0
and
c_{1}^{2}\geq 4c_{0}
, which is equivalent to
K<b_{3}/d_{3}
and
N^{2}\leq\delta_{3}^{2}(K)/4
, respectively. For
K\in(0,b_{3}/d_{3})
the global maximum of
\delta_{3}^{2}(K)/4
is
b_{3}^{4}/(64d_{3}^{2})
at
K=K_{1}:=b_{3}/(2d_{3})
; its global minimum is zero at
K=0
and
K=b_{3}/d_{3}
. Specifically, if
N^{2}\leq b_{3}^{4}/(64d_{3}^{2})
, i.e. the stability of the stratification is sufficiently weak compared with the backscatter destabilisation, then
c_{1}^{2}\geq 4c_{0}
for
K
in a positive interval
I_{1}\subset(0,b_{3}/d_{3})
; in particular,
I_{1}=\{K_{1}\}
if
N^{2}=b_{3}^{4}/(64d_{3}^{2})
. Hence, a parallel flow (
3.12
) grows exponentially and unboundedly if it contains a Fourier mode with wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}
in the annulus
\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\in I_{1}\}
.

In the presence of thermal diffusion (
\mu>0
), we first note that
c_{0}
is a cubic polynomial in
K
, so
c_{0}
has a local maximum at
K=0
and there is a global minimum at some
K>0
. Specifically, if
0<N^{2}<4\mu b_{3}^{3}/(27d_{3}^{2})
, then
c_{0}<0
in a positive interval
I_{2}\subset(0,b_{3}/d_{3})
; if
N^{2}<0
, then
c_{0}<0
in an interval
I_{3}:=[0,K_{2})
for some
K_{2}>b_{3}/d_{3}
. Hence, in this case, a parallel flow (
3.12
) grows exponentially and unboundedly if it contains a Fourier mode with wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}
in the annulus
\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\in I_{2}\}
for (not too strongly) stable stratification, or in the disc
\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\in I_{3}\}
for unstable stratification, and its Fourier coefficient vector is not an eigenvector for a possible negative root. Other modes that have
c_{0}\geq 0
also yield such growth if
c_{1}<0
and
c_{1}^{2}\geq 4c_{0}
. We omit details but note that
c_{1}<0
occurs for
K<(b_{3}-\mu)/d_{3}
, possibly containing
I_{2}
.

3.2.2
Kolmogorov flow

Another well-known class of explicit solutions for the Boussinesq equations in absence of backscatter are the so-called Kolmogorov flows, see e.g.
(
Balmforth and Young 2005
)
, with wave vectors of the form
\boldsymbol{k}=(k,0,-m)^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
k,\,m\in\mathbb{R}
. Here we study their occurrence in the case of backscatter and start with the ansatz

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=e^{\lambda t}\cos(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{a}\,,\quad\mathtt{b}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=ce^{\lambda t}\cos(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})\,,\quad p(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\gamma e^{\lambda t}\sin(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})\,,

(3.15)

and the flow direction
\boldsymbol{a}=\alpha(0,1,0)^{\mathsf{T}}+\beta(m,0,k)^{\mathsf{T}}.

Compared with the Kolmogorov flows without backscatter and rotation from
(
Prugger and
Rademacher 2021
)
, here we have rotation (
f\neq 0
), time dependence (
\lambda\neq 0
) and a nonzero second component of the velocity direction (
\alpha\neq 0
). A superposition of these Kolmogorov flows with the horizontal flow solutions (
3.3
) is not possible, since the orthogonality conditions of wave vectors and velocity directions are not satisfied, thus leading to non-gradient terms from the nonlinearity. However, superposition of different Kolmogorov flows is possible, as long as all wave vectors
\boldsymbol{k}
have the same direction, as is the superposition with such monochromatic inertia gravity waves as discussed in §
3.2.3
.

We next determine the necessary relations for the coefficients of (
3.15
) in order to solve the Boussinesq equations. For better readability we define the following terms resulting from the backscatter and thermal diffusion:

\displaystyle\delta_{\mu}(k,m)=\mu|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\,,\quad\delta_{i}(k,m)=d_{i}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{4}-b_{i}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\,\mbox{ for any }\,1\leq i\leq 3\,,

where
|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}=k^{2}+m^{2}
. Upon inserting (
3.15
) into (
3.1
) we find that the coefficients have to satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-f&m(\lambda+\delta_{1})&0&k\\
\lambda+\delta_{2}&fm&0&0\\
0&k(\lambda+\delta_{3})&-1&-m\\
0&N^{2}k&\lambda+\delta_{\mu}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\\
c\\
\gamma\end{pmatrix}=0\,.

(3.16)

For
(k,m)=(0,0)
these require
\alpha=0
and
c=0
, which is the zero state. From the second row of the 4-by-4 matrix in (
3.16
) and
f\neq 0
we immediately find that
\alpha=0
implies
\beta m=0
. In case
\beta=0
, the Kolmogorov flow (
3.15
) is the trivial zero solution, and in case
m=0
, (
3.15
) is a parallel flow. Hence, we may assume
\alpha\neq 0
. Since (
3.16
) is a homogeneous linear system in
(\alpha,\beta,c,\gamma)
, non-trivial solutions require a kernel of the associated matrix. Hence, either there is no non-trivial Kolmogorov flow or a linear space of these, which requires vanishing determinant of this matrix. Assuming
(k,m)\neq(0,0)
and dividing by
-|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}
, this gives

\displaystyle\lambda^{3}+c_{2}\lambda^{2}+c_{1}\lambda+c_{0}=0\,,

(3.17)

with coefficients

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle:=(\delta_{2}+\delta_{\mu})+|\boldsymbol{k}|^{-2}(\delta_{3}k^{2}+\delta_{1}m^{2})\,,

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle:=\delta_{2}\delta_{\mu}+|\boldsymbol{k}|^{-2}[(\delta_{2}+\delta_{\mu})(\delta_{3}k^{2}+\delta_{1}m^{2})+N^{2}k^{2}+f^{2}m^{2}]\,,

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle:=|\boldsymbol{k}|^{-2}[\delta_{2}\delta_{\mu}(\delta_{3}k^{2}+\delta_{1}m^{2})+\delta_{2}N^{2}k^{2}+\delta_{\mu}f^{2}m^{2}]\,.

Steady Kolmogorov flows and linear stability.

The condition
\lambda=0
for steady Kolmogorov flow reduces (
3.17
) to

\displaystyle\delta_{2}\delta_{\mu}(\delta_{3}k^{2}+\delta_{1}m^{2})+\delta_{2}N^{2}k^{2}+\delta_{\mu}f^{2}m^{2}=0\,.

(3.18)

For comparison, note that the left-hand side identically equals to zero in the absence of backscatter and viscosity (
d_{j},\,b_{j}=0
for
j=1,\,2,\,3
) and thermal diffusion (
\mu=0
) so that in this case steady and non-trivial Kolmogorov flow exists for all
(k,m)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
.
In contrast, in the presence of backscatter
d_{2},\,b_{2}>0
, but still without thermal diffusion (
\mu=0
), only
\delta_{2}N^{2}k^{2}=0
remains so that either
k=0
or
\delta_{2}=0
. The latter means
|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}=b_{2}/d_{2}
, so that non-trivial and steady Kolmogorov flow occurs on the
m
-axis and the circle in the
(k,m)
-plane with radius
\sqrt{b_{2}/d_{2}}
, cf. Fig
10(b)
,
10(d)
. Conversely, we can create steady Kolmogorov flows for any wave vector
(k,m)\neq(0,0)
by suitable choice of
d_{2},\,b_{2}
such that
\delta_{2}=0
.

Concerning stability, analogous to §
3.1.3
, small amplitude steady Kolmogorov flows are unstable, though typically not unboundedly, due to the instability of the zero state under backscatter. In the large amplitude scaling, the resulting operator
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
is a triangular block matrix operator, similar to §
3.2.1
, with skew-adjoint parts that imply purely imaginary spectrum. Hence, there are again no growth rates that are proportional to the amplitude of the steady Kolmogorov flow. Regarding stability of Kolmogorov flows without backscatter we refer to
(
Balmforth and Young 2005
)
.

A source of unbounded instability of steady Kolmogorov flows are possible superpositions with monochromatic inertia gravity waves discussed in §
3.2.3
below. In the following we will examine the existence of exponentially and unboundedly growing Kolmogorov flows, which then also proves unbounded instability of steady Kolmogorov flows due to possible superpositions.

Unboundedly growing Kolmogorov flows.

Such flows with
\beta,\,k\neq 0
transfer the horizontal backscatter to growing vertical velocity component. They correspond to positive roots of (
3.17
), which occur as follows in terms of the sign of
c_{0}
:

(1)

If

(2)

For

(3)

For

The conditions in (3) imply that the local minimum of the cubic polynomial in (

3.2.3
Monochromatic inertia gravity waves

The last kind of explicit plane wave-type solutions we are aware of are the so-called monochromatic inertia gravity waves (MGWs), which are for example discussed in
(
Achatz 2006
)
for the inviscid Boussinesq equations. Here we study the occurrence in the rotating Boussinesq equations with backscatter. These solutions again form an invariant subspace of linear dynamics since the nonlinear terms vanish, but structurally differ from the aforementioned flows. In particular, the wave profile of a MGW is a time-dependent travelling wave with phase variable
\xi=kx+mz-\omega t
and takes the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}e^{\lambda t}\sin(\xi)(0,1,0)^{\mathsf{T}}+\alpha_{2}\omega e^{\lambda t}\cos(\xi)(-m,0,k)^{\mathsf{T}}\,,

(3.19a)

\displaystyle\mathtt{b}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}e^{\lambda t}\sin(\xi)+\beta_{2}\omega e^{\lambda t}\cos(\xi)\,,

(3.19b)

\displaystyle p(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}e^{\lambda t}\cos(\xi)+\gamma_{2}\omega e^{\lambda t}\sin(\xi)\,.

(3.19c)

The conditions for these to be (non-trivial) explicit solutions of (
3.1
), with
\boldsymbol{k}=(k,m)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\{(0,0)^{\mathsf{T}}\}
, in particular depend on
\omega
. In fact, for
\omega=0
these are Kolmogorov flows from §
3.2.2
with
\beta=0
, and the existence conditions read, after inserting (
3.19
) into (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda+\delta_{2}&0&0\\
mf&-k&0\\
kf&m&|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\\
0&\lambda+\delta_{\mu}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\beta_{1}\\
\gamma_{1}\end{pmatrix}=0\,,

(3.20)

with
\delta_{j}=\delta_{j}(\boldsymbol{k}):=d_{j}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{4}-b_{j}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}
for any
1\leq j\leq 3
and
\delta_{\mu}=\delta_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{k}):=\mu|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}
as above.

For
\omega\neq 0
the existence conditions are at first the following eight:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda+\delta_{2}&0&0&0&0&0\\
mf&-k&0&\omega^{2}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}&0&0\\
kf&m&|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}&0&0&0\\
0&\lambda+\delta_{\mu}&0&0&\omega^{2}&0\\
1&0&0&mf&0&0\\
0&-1&0&kN^{2}&\lambda+\delta_{\mu}&0\\
0&0&0&\delta_{3}k^{2}+\delta_{1}m^{2}+\lambda|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}&-k&0\\
0&0&0&(\delta_{3}-\delta_{1})km&-m&|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\beta_{1}\\
\gamma_{1}\\

\beta_{2}\\
\gamma_{2}\end{pmatrix}=0\,.

(3.21)

However, most of these can be readily solved directly in terms of the coefficients. In the following we explicitly determine all non-trivial solutions (
3.19
). Afterwards we shortly discuss the stability of MGWs and the possible superpositions with other types of explicit solutions.

MGWs with steady phase (
\omega=0
).

We start with
\omega=0
, which gives a certain type of Kolmogorov flows. There is no propagation of the travelling wave profile and the second terms of
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\mathtt{b}
and
p
in (
3.19
) vanish. Solutions with

\displaystyle k=0\,,\quad\alpha_{1}=0\,,\quad\beta_{1}=-\gamma_{1}m\,,\quad\lambda=-\delta_{\mu}

have
\boldsymbol{v}\equiv 0
, the pressure depends on the buoyancy only, and there are no further conditions on the wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}=(0,m)^{\mathsf{T}}
.

Other solutions with
\omega=0
satisfy

\displaystyle k\neq 0\,,\quad\beta_{1}=\alpha_{1}\frac{m}{k}f\,,\quad\gamma_{1}=-\alpha_{1}\frac{f}{k}\,,\quad\lambda=-\delta_{\mu}\,,\quad\delta_{2}=\delta_{\mu}\,,

so
\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0
for
\alpha_{1}\neq 0
. The last of these equations gives a condition on the wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}
, which is equivalent to
d_{2}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}-b_{2}-\mu=0
and constrains the wave number to
|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}=\frac{b_{2}+\mu}{d_{2}}
.

In both of these cases the growth rate is defined by the thermal diffusion as
\lambda=-\mu|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}
, so that all these MGWs are exponentially decaying for
\mu>0
and steady for
\mu=0
. Furthermore, both solutions only depend on the parameter of the second momentum or the buoyancy equation, but are independent of the Brunt-Väisälä frequency
N^{2}
.

MGWs with oscillating phase (
\omega\neq 0
).

We turn to non-trivial MGW solutions (
3.19
) with
\omega\neq 0
. The simplest class are ‘vertically varying’ MGWs with

\displaystyle k=0\,,\quad\alpha_{1}=-\alpha_{2}mf\,,\quad\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0\,,\quad\lambda=-\delta_{2}\,,\quad\omega=\pm f\,,\quad\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}\,,

so that
\mathtt{b}\equiv p\equiv 0
, while
\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0
– in contrast to the case
k=\omega=0
above. Notably, these solutions depend on the parameters of the horizontal momentum equations only, while the solutions with
k=\omega=0
only depend on those from the buoyancy equation. The last of the above equations is a condition on the wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}=(0,m)^{\mathsf{T}}
, which is equivalent to
(d_{1}-d_{2})m^{2}+b_{2}-b_{1}=0
. Thus, these solutions exist for all
m\neq 0
in the isotropic case, while for
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
they are restricted to
m^{2}=\frac{b_{1}-b_{2}}{d_{1}-d_{2}}>0
.

Since
\lambda=-\delta_{2}
, these MGWs are exponentially and unboundedly growing for wave numbers
m^{2}<b_{2}/d_{2}
, and steady for
m^{2}=b_{2}/d_{2}
, thus transferring the horizontal backscatter to growing vertical dependence. In particular, the thermal diffusion
\mu
and Brunt-Väisälä frequency
N^{2}
have no impact, since these solutions trivially satisfy the buoyancy equation by
v_{3}\equiv\mathtt{b}\equiv 0
.

Another type are ‘zonally varying’ MGWs with the coefficients satisfying

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=m=0\,,\quad\beta_{1}=\alpha_{2}\Bigl(N^{2}-\frac{(\delta_{3}-\delta_{\mu})^{2}}{4}\Bigr)k\,,\quad\beta_{2}=\alpha_{2}\frac{\delta_{3}-\delta_{\mu}}{2}k\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0\,,\quad\lambda=-\frac{\delta_{3}+\delta_{\mu}}{2}\,,\quad\omega^{2}=N^{2}-\frac{(\delta_{3}-\delta_{\mu})^{2}}{4}\,.

These have vanishing pressure and depend only on the parameters of the buoyancy and vertical momentum equations. In contrast to the MGW solutions before, these here do also depend on the Brunt-Väisälä frequency
N^{2}
. Furthermore, different from the solutions before, the growth rate
\lambda
and phase frequency
\omega
depend on both, the vertical term
\delta_{3}
and the thermal diffusion. Since
\omega^{2}>0
, the last equation is a condition on the wave vector
\boldsymbol{k}=(k,0)^{\mathsf{T}}
, which requires stable stratification
N^{2}>0
satisfying
(\delta_{3}-\delta_{\mu})^{2}<4N^{2}
. In case
N^{2}>0
these MGW solutions exist at least for sufficiently small
k^{2}
, since
\delta_{3},\,\delta_{\mu}\rightarrow 0
as
|\boldsymbol{k}|\rightarrow 0
.
Due to the equation for the growth rate
\lambda
, these MGWs are exponentially decaying for kinetic energy backscatter, where
\delta_{3}=-b_{3}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}>0
. Hence, this kind of MGWs are steady or exhibit growth only in the artificial case
d_{3},\,b_{3}>0
for wave numbers
k^{2}\leq\frac{b_{3}-\mu}{d_{3}}
, i.e. only in the case
b_{3}>\mu
.

The existence analysis for the remaining solutions to (
3.19
) with
\omega,\,k,\,\alpha_{1}\neq 0
is more involved. These solutions have the coefficients

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=-\alpha_{2}mf\,,\quad\beta_{1}=\alpha_{1}\frac{m}{k}f+\alpha_{2}\frac{\omega^{2}}{k}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\,,\quad\beta_{2}=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{k}(\delta_{3}k^{2}+\delta_{1}m^{2}-\delta_{2}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2})\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}=\alpha_{2}(\delta_{1}-\delta_{3})\frac{km}{|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}}+\beta_{2}\frac{m}{|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}}\,,\quad\gamma_{1}=-\alpha_{1}\frac{kf}{|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}}-\beta_{1}\frac{m}{|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}}\,,\quad\lambda=-\delta_{2}\,,

with additional conditions, that also define the phase frequency
\omega
, given by

\displaystyle\omega^{2}=\Bigl((\delta_{3}-\delta_{2})(\delta_{\mu}-\delta_{2})+N^{2}\Bigr)\frac{k^{2}}{|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}}+\Bigl((\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})(\delta_{\mu}-\delta_{2})+f^{2}\Bigr)\frac{m^{2}}{|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}}>0\,,

\displaystyle\Bigl((\delta_{\mu}-\delta_{2})N^{2}+(\delta_{3}-\delta_{2})\bigl((\delta_{\mu}-\delta_{2})^{2}+\omega^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)k^{2}+(\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})\bigl((\delta_{\mu}-\delta_{2})^{2}+\omega^{2}\bigr)m^{2}=0\,.

for any parameter: For stable stratification

Superpositions and stability.

It is possible to superpose MGWs and Kolmogorov flows as a solution to (
3.1
) if these have the same direction of wave vectors
\boldsymbol{k}
, and this can also be in the form of an integral. Indeed, the similar structure of wave vector and velocity direction yields vanishing nonlinear terms and a remaining system of linear equations, which each solution satisfies. Note that Kolmogorov flows exist on the whole wave vector space
(k,m)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, while MGW in general not. This means, that superposition in one wave vector direction is possible for arbitrary wave number of the Kolmogorov flow, but the wave number of the MGW is in general restricted. Depending on the wave vectors, the Kolmogorov flows and MGWs in such a superposition can be steady, exponentially growing or decaying.

With this superposition we can prove in certain cases the unbounded instability of steady MGW solutions due to perturbations with exponentially growing Kolmogorov flows, and vice versa. In §
3.2.2
we found that growing Kolmogorov flows in case
\mu>0
may occur only in certain directions. In this case, steady MGW solutions are unboundedly unstable a priori only in these certain wave vector directions (see investigation of
c_{0}
in §
3.2.2
). Without thermal diffusion (
\mu=0
) there are always growing Kolmogorov flows in any direction with
k\neq 0
, for stable stratification
N^{2}>0
at least for all
|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}<b_{2}/d_{2}
, and for unstable stratification
N^{2}<0
for
|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}>b_{2}/d_{2}
. Thus, in these cases, the steady MGWs with
k\neq 0
are always unboundedly unstable with respect to certain Kolmogorov flows. However, steady MGWs with
k=0
, as well as Kolmogorov flows, are also unboundedly unstable, since there are exponentially and unboundedly growing MGWs with
k=0
and
m^{2}<b_{2}/d_{2}
, with which they can be superposed (see the case of ‘vertically varying’ MGWs with
\omega\neq 0
and
k=0
).

As in §
3.1.3
, steady small amplitude MGWs are unstable due to the unstable zero state under backscatter. In the large amplitude scaling, the resulting operator
\boldsymbol{L}_{0}
is a triangular block matrix operator with skew-adjoint parts that imply purely imaginary spectrum as for Kolmogorov flows. Hence, there are again no unstable eigenvalues that scale with the amplitude of the steady MGW.

4
Discussion

Motivated by the numerical backscatter scheme
(
Jansen and Held 2014
;
Danilov et al. 2019
;
Juricke et al. 2020
;
Perezhogin 2020
)
, we have studied the impact of simplified kinetic energy backscatter via classes of explicit flows in the shallow water and rotating Boussinesq equations on the whole space. Here we have found that backscatter induces unbounded instability of the zero state, as well as of certain non-trivial steady solutions, in the sense of unboundedly and exponentially growing flows, also with vertical structure and also for stable stratification. This highlights the possibility of concentration of energy due to backscatter, and is in contrast to the desired energy redistribution. Since all flows we have considered simultaneously solve the linear equations from dropping the transport nonlinearity and the full nonlinear equations, these are directly linked to spectrum and linear stability of the trivial flow. Indeed, in wave vector space, the condition to solve the nonlinear equations emposes additional constraints, which are stronger in the shallow water case than in the purely horizontal Boussinesq equations. We have discussed the corresponding linear and nonlinear constraints on coefficients for these and also for flows with vertical structure and coupled buoyancy that relate to parallel flows, Kolmogorov flows and monochromatic inertia gravity waves.

We have identified superposition principles of these flows in the nonlinear equations and have discussed the resulting unbounded instability of the flows themselves, in particular of steady flows induced by the backscatter. Due to the linear-nonlinear structure, these steady flows come as a family with an amplitude scaling parameter and we have discussed linear stability in the regimes of small and large amplitudes. Flows of small amplitudes inherit the instability of the zero state, but the treatment of large amplitudes is more subtle. Here we have considered a renormalised eigenvalue problem and have found that the resulting spectrum is purely imaginary in all except one class of the aforementioned flows. Based on numerical computations, in the exceptional case of steady multi mode horizontal flows unstable rates are proportional to the amplitude of the steady flow, thus leading to arbitrarily strong growth rates.

The approach that we have presented can be applied to operators with other constant coefficient linear or derivative terms. An interesting case that we will pursue further is the inclusion of bottom drag, which admits an onset of instability with finite wave number. Also forcing of plane wave form can be treated, similar to
(
Prugger and
Rademacher 2021
)
.

It would be interesting to study analytically and numerically in what way the undesired growth occurs in numerical discretisations. For fixed backscatter coefficients under decreasing grid size, the unbounded instability in the limit is expected to readily imply arbitrarily large growth that may however be bounded for any fixed small grid size. The actual backscatter scheme scales the strength of backscatter with the grid size so that it would be interesting to identify, for a given discretisation scheme, the relation between the growth of flows and the grid-scaling of backscatter coefficients. This may provide another approach to energetic consistency of backscatter schemes.
