Boundary feedback control for hyperbolic systems
Michael Herty, Ferdinand Thein

Abstract
We are interested in the feedback stabilization of general linear multi-dimensional first order hyperbolic systems in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . Using a Lyapunov function with a suited weight function depending on the system under consideration we show stabilization in L 2 L^{2} for the studied system using a suitable feedback control. Therefore the controllability of the studied system is related to the feasibility of an associated linear matrix inequality. We show the applicability discussing the barotropic Euler equations.

2
Stabilization of Multi-Dimensional Linear Hyperbolic Balance Laws

We are interested in the following initial boundary value problem (IBVP) for the given system of hyperbolic PDEs

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})&=0,\;(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\Omega\\
\mathbf{w}(0,\mathbf{x})&=\mathbf{w}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\;\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})&=\mathbf{w}_{BC}(t,\mathbf{x}),\;(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\partial\Omega\end{cases}

(2.1)

Here
\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\equiv(w_{1}(t,\mathbf{x}),\dots,w_{n}(t,\mathbf{x}))^{T}
is the vector of unknowns
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a bounded domain with piecewise
C^{1}
smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})
are sufficiently smooth and bounded
n\times n
real matrices. The
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})
are in particular symmetric.
Upon applying a suited variable transformation we may transform any symmetric hyperbolic system into the given form with the identity in front of the time derivative, cf.
[
5
]
.
The assumption of symmetry is no major restriction since it includes all systems equipped with an additional conservation law, cf.
[
23
,
18
]
.
This includes most systems relevant for applications, see
[
6
,
45
]
.
We define the matrix

\mathbf{A}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\nu}):=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\nu_{k}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})

with
\mathbf{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{d})^{T}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
being a unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and by construction
\mathbf{A}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\nu})
is also symmetric.
Adopting the notion given in
[
41
,
33
]
we refer to the matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\ast}
as the
pencil matrix
.
Concerning the definition of hyperbolicity of system (
2.1
) we follow
[
18
]
.
System (
2.1
) is said to be hyperbolic if the matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\nu})
has
n
real eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\nu}),\,i=1,\dots,n
,
and
n
corresponding linearly independent right eigenvectors
\mathbf{r}_{i}=\mathbf{r}_{i}(\mathbf{\mathbf{x},\nu}),i=1,\dots,n
for all
\mathbf{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Note that by choosing

\mathbf{\nu}=\mathbf{e}_{k}=(0,\dots,0,\underbrace{1}_{k},0,\dots,0)^{T}

we have
\mathbf{A}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}_{k})=\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})
and thus the individual Jacobians
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})
are also diagonalizable with real eigenvalues.
Further following
[
5
]
there exists a well-conditioned orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\nu})
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\nu})=\mathbf{T}^{T}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\nu})\mathbf{A}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\nu})\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\nu})

(2.2)

is a diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of the system (
2.1
).
The afore mentioned properties are distinctive features of multi–dimensional hyperbolic PDEs and we highly recommend the references
[
5
,
18
]
, cited above, for further reading.
A further delicate issue is the dependence of the eigenspaces and eigenvalues on
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
That this is in general not the case is shown in
[
5
]
where a particular system fails the continuous dependence of the eigenspaces with respect to
\nu
.
However, for constantly hyperbolic systems, i.e. systems with constant multiplicity of the eigenvalues, the eigenspaces are continuous functions of
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Hence it is possible to find locally an eigenbasis of
\mathbf{A}^{\ast}(\nu)
, which depends continuously on
\nu
.

Remark 2.1
.

Here we additionally assume that the transformation is continuous with respect to each smooth boundary part and
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Following
[
5
]
for systems with constant coefficients the class of constantly hyperbolic systems, for example, enjoys this property.

The boundary
\partial\Omega
will be separated in the controllable and uncontrollable part, i.e. for
i=1,\dots,n

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left.\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\,\right|\,\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))\geq 0\right\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left.\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\,\right|\,\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))<0\right\}.

We further need to discuss a LMI associated to the system (
2.1
) of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{m}):=C\mathbf{Id}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\leq 0,\,C\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

(2.3)

which obviously may be reformulated to

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{m}):=\mathbf{A}^{(0)}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\overline{m}_{k}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\geq 0,

(2.4)

with
\mathbf{A}^{(0)}=-\mathbf{Id}
and where the coefficients are given by
\overline{m}_{k}=-m_{k}/C
. The matrices
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
are the Jacobians of system (
2.1
).
In the case that the Jacobians of system (
2.1
) are space dependent the coefficients
\mathbf{m}
of the LMI may also depend on
\mathbf{x}
.
Further it will turn out that for the boundary feedback control we need
\mathbf{m}
to be the gradient of a function and hence we have the following result.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume there exists a function
\overline{\mu}(\mathbf{x})
such that

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{m}}:=\nabla\overline{\mu}(\mathbf{x})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{m}):=-\mathbf{Id}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\overline{m}_{k}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\geq 0.

(2.5)

Then system (
2.1
) has a
feasible Lyapunov potential

\mu(\mathbf{x})=-C\overline{\mu}(\mathbf{x})
such that (
2.3
) holds.

Of course the space dependent case is more intricate since we not only need any solution of the LMI, but also one which must be integrable.
However, for constant matrices we have for such coefficients
\mathbf{m}
that

\mu(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}x_{k}

is a suited potential.

Finally we want to recall the definition of exponential stability, cf.
[
4
,
32
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

A solution
\mathbf{w}\in C^{1}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega))^{n}
for
s\geq 1+\frac{d}{2}
of the initial boundary value problem (
2.1
) is called
exponentially stable
in the
L^{2}-
sense, iff

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}(t,.)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}t)\|\mathbf{w}(0,.)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\,C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\,t\in[0,+\infty).

(2.6)

The Lyapunov function we will introduce in Theorem
2.4
is equivalent to the
L^{2}(\Omega)
norm.
For later use, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:=\left(\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x}),\dots,\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(d)}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\right)^{T}\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x})),

\displaystyle m_{k}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mu(\mathbf{x}),\,k=1,\dots,d.

Now, we state the main result of this paper.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\in C^{1}\left((0,T),H^{s}(\Omega)\right)^{n}
,
s\geq 1+d/2,
be a solution to the IBVP (
2.1
).
Assume the problem (
2.1
) admits a feasible Lyapunov potential.
Thus there exist
\mu(\mathbf{x})\in H^{s}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle C_{A}\mathbf{Id}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0

(2.7)

holds true for some value
C_{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
C_{A}>C_{B}
and
C_{B}
given as in (
3.2
).
Define the Lyapunov function as follows

\displaystyle L(t)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})^{T}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}.

(2.8)

Let the boundary condition for (
2.1
) be given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{(i)}_{BC}(t,\mathbf{x})=u_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}),\,\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma_{i}^{-}

where the imposed feedback controls
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})^{T}
satisfy
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{T}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{-}}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))\tilde{u}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{+}}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))q_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x},\end{split}

(2.9)

with
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{T}^{T}\mathbf{w}
and (
2.2
).

Then, it holds for the Lyapunov function that for
0<C_{L}=C_{A}-C_{B}

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}L(t)\leq-C_{L}L(t).

Further, the solution
\mathbf{w}
of the IBVP (
2.1
) is exponentially stable in the
L^{2}-
sense (
2.6
).

The proof is given in the following section for better readability. We refer to Remark
3.3
for particular cases of interest and conditions under which
u_{i}
exists.
Note that the choice of
s
in the space
H^{s}
guarantees that
\mu
and
\mathbf{w}
are differentiable in
\mathbf{x}
by Sobolev embedding.

3
Proof of Theorem
2.4

Proof:

We want to show that the Lyapunov function (
2.8
) decays exponentially fast under the given conditions.
We obtain for the time derivative of the Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}L(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}=2\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}.

Replacing the time derivative of
\mathbf{w}
using the PDE system (
2.1
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}L(t)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left[-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\right]\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\Omega}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))+\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

Now with using the product rule and introducing the abbreviations
\mathcal{A}
and
m_{k}(\mathbf{x})
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}L(t)=\dots

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\left(\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\right)-\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\right.

\displaystyle-\left.m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\right]+2\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle-2\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=-\underbrace{\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{A}(t,\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}}_{=:\mathcal{B}(t)}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left[\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\right)-2\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\right]\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}}_{=:\mathcal{I}(t)}.

In the following we split the proof into two parts. First we show that the boundary term
\mathcal{B}(t)
is non-negative under the given conditions.
In the second part we estimate
\mathcal{I}(t)
such that we can use Gronwalls Lemma to show the decay of the Lyapunov function.

3.1
Estimating the Boundary Integral

In the following we want to discuss the boundary term. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(t,\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})n_{k}(\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}n_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}^{T}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x})).

Since the system is symmetric we can transform the quadratic form according to (
2.2
) and thus have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(t)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{A}(t,\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{q}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{q}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}.

Using the partitioning of the boundary introduced before we rewrite the boundary term and obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{q}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{q}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{-}}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\tilde{u}_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{+}}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))q_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Due to
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{T}^{T}\mathbf{w}
we have
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{T}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
.

3.2
Estimating the Volume Integral

We now want to study the expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left[\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\right)-2\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\right]\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})-2\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

We first want to discuss the term

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})-2\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x}).

(3.1)

If (
3.1
) is less or equal to zero there is nothing to do and it can simply be estimated by zero.
Assume (
3.1
) is strictly positive. In the case that
\mathbf{B}
is not symmetric it can be replaced by its symmetric part
\mathbf{B}^{sym}=(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^{T})/2
.
Since the
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
are symmetric this property is inherited by the derivatives of these matrices.
Hence the following estimate can be established

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})-2\mathbf{B}^{sym}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\leq C_{B}\|\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2},\,C_{B}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

(3.2)

Furthermore, by (
2.7
),
\mu(\mathbf{x})
and thus the
m_{k}(\mathbf{x})
, respectively, are chosen such that the linear matrix inequality

\displaystyle C_{A}\mathbf{Id}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0

holds.
Thus we have for
C_{A}>C_{B}
and
0<C_{L}\leq C_{A}-C_{B}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left[\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\right)-2\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\right]\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\left[C_{B}\mathbf{Id}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\right]\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq-C_{L}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}.

Altogether we yield for the Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}L(t)=-\mathcal{B}(t)+\mathcal{I}(t)\leq\mathcal{I}(t)\leq-C_{L}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp(\mu(\mathbf{x}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}=-C_{L}L(t).

Applying Gronwall’s Lemma gives the claimed exponential decay and thus the proof is complete.

\square

Remark 3.1
.

We want to remark that the obtained result can be extended to the exponential stability in the corresponding
H^{s}
-norm.
One just has to add other appropriate terms in the definition of the Lyapunov function. The
H^{s}
-norm of a function
u\in H^{s}(\Omega)
is given by

\|u\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}:=\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|^{2}_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For vector-valued functions the norm is interpreted component wise.
Now we define the Lyapunov function accordingly

\displaystyle L(t):=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\left\|\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu^{(\alpha)}\right)\partial^{\alpha}\mathbf{u}\right\|^{2}_{2}.

(3.3)

Note that by introducing the
\exp\left(\mu^{(\alpha)}\right)
we want to highlight that in principle it is possible to choose suited Lyapunov potentials for every occurring multi-index
|\alpha|\leq s
.
Each Lyapunov potential has to satisfy a LMI similar to (
2.3
) with respect to the system corresponding to the studied multi-index.
It has to be studied under which conditions this is possible for a given system.
Once the Lyapunov function is introduced the strategy is the same and due to the similar structure inherited by the governing PDE (
2.1
) the estimates can be adopted from the previous proof.
We exemplary want to give the Lyapunov function in the case
H^{1}

\displaystyle L(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})^{T}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\exp\left(\mu^{(0)}(\mathbf{x})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\left(\partial_{x_{k}}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\right)^{T}\left(\partial_{x_{k}}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})\right)\exp\left(\mu^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

Remark 3.2
.

During the proof we encounter the term (
3.1
).
In particular in the proof we only treat the situation when this expression is strictly positive since under the given assumptions, namely (
2.7
),
it can simply be estimated by zero in the case that it is less or equal to zero.
However, there may be situations when the estimate of the volume integral may benefit from this term.
In the case that the reminder (
3.1
) should be taken into account we introduce

\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})-2\mathbf{B}^{sym}(\mathbf{x})

and demand

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{m}):=C\mathbf{Id}+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0,\,C\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

(3.4)

The LMI (

Remark 3.3
.

The inequality for the (transformed) control
\tilde{u}_{i}
can be simplified under additional assumptions.

(i)

A possible simplifying assumption on the controls is that they are uniform in space, i.e.
\tilde{u}_{i}=\tilde{u}_{i}(t)
.
This leads to the following condition for the controls

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{u}_{i}(t)^{2}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{-}}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))e^{\mu(\mathbf{x})}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{+}}q_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))e^{\mu(\mathbf{x})}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}.

(ii)

Further the same control may be applied to all components which leads to
\tilde{u}_{i}\equiv\tilde{u}(t,\mathbf{x}),\,i=1,\dots,n
.
This leads to the following condition for the controls

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{i}}\tilde{u}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))e^{\mu(\mathbf{x})}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{+}}q_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))e^{\mu(\mathbf{x})}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}.

(iii)

Assume that both previous assumptions hold, i.e.
\tilde{u}_{i}\equiv\tilde{u}(t),\,i=1,\dots,n
. Then we can give an explicit condition for the feedback control as

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(t)^{2}\leq-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{i}}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))e^{\mu(\mathbf{x})}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{+}}q_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))e^{\mu(\mathbf{x})}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}.

(3.5)

Thus a possible control would be

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(t)=C\sqrt{-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{i}}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))e^{\mu(\mathbf{x})}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i}^{+}}q_{i}(t,\mathbf{x})^{2}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))e^{\mu(\mathbf{x})}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}}

(3.6)

for

.

Application to Hyperbolic Systems

In this section we discuss the application of the stabilization result.
As a first example we want to mention that recent results have shown the applicability of this approach to Hamilton-Jacobi equations, see
[
1
,
34
]
.
The study on the stabilization of Hamilton–Jacobi equation has been motivated by a stabilization problem arising in a forming process and we refer to the afore mentioned literature for further details.

We now want to study the general case and show that the linearization matches (
2.1
). Let us consider the following hyperbolic system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{U}(t,\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{U}(t,\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{U}_{x_{1}}(t,\mathbf{x}),\dots,\mathbf{U}_{x_{d}}(t,\mathbf{x}))&=0,\;(t,\mathbf{x})\in(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
\mathbf{U}(0,\mathbf{x})&=\mathbf{U}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\;\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
\mathbf{U}(t,\mathbf{x})&=\mathbf{U}_{BC}(t,\mathbf{x}),\;(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T)\times\partial\Omega\end{cases}

(4.1)

Here we have introduced the function

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\underbrace{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}_{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{d})\mapsto\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{d})\equiv\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{p}).

We want to linearize system (
4.1
) near a steady state solution
\overline{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{x})
of (
4.1
).
Thus we have to linearize
\mathbf{F}
near a reference state
(\overline{\mathbf{u}},\overline{\mathbf{p}})\equiv(\overline{\mathbf{u}},\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{1},\dots,\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{d})

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{u}},\overline{\mathbf{p}})+\underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{u}},\overline{\mathbf{p}})}_{=:\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{u}-\overline{\mathbf{u}})+\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{p}}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{u}},\overline{\mathbf{p}})(\mathbf{p}-\overline{\mathbf{p}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{u}},\overline{\mathbf{p}})+\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{u}-\overline{\mathbf{u}})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{p}_{k}}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{u}},\overline{\mathbf{p}})}_{=:\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{p}_{k}-\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{u}},\overline{\mathbf{p}})+\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{u}-\overline{\mathbf{u}})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{p}_{k}-\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{k}).

(4.2)

We now consider deviations of this desired state

\mathbf{U}(t,\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{\overline{U}}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})

and for some initial disturbance we assume
\mathbf{w}(0,\mathbf{x})=(w_{1}(0,\mathbf{x}),\dots,w_{n}(0,\mathbf{x}))\neq 0
.
By the following computation we obtain a linear system using (
4.2
) starting from (
4.1
)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{U}(t,\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{U}(t,\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{U}_{x_{1}}(t,\mathbf{x}),\dots,\mathbf{U}_{x_{d}}(t,\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{U}(t,\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{x}),\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{x_{1}}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{x_{d}}(\mathbf{x}))+\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{U}(t,\mathbf{x})-\overline{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{U}_{x_{k}}(t,\mathbf{x})-\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{x_{k}}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\mathbf{\overline{U}}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x}))+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{x}),\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{x_{1}}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{x_{d}}(\mathbf{x}))+\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})

Thus the linearized system has the desired form to apply the above results, highlighting the wide range of applicability of the present work.

4.1
Barotropic Euler Equations

The barotropic Euler system (isothermal or isentropic) is of huge interest in the literature. The system reads in two dimensions

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\right)&=0,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho v_{i})+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\rho v_{i}\mathbf{v}+p\mathbf{e}^{(i)}\right)&=0,\;i\in\{1,2\}\end{split}

(4.3)

where
\rho>0
denotes the mass density,
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2})^{T}
is the velocity and
p
is the pressure as function of the density.
The fluxes are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{(1)}(\mathbf{U})=\begin{pmatrix}\rho v_{1}\\
\rho v_{1}^{2}+p\\
\rho v_{1}v_{2}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{F}^{(2)}(\mathbf{U})=\begin{pmatrix}\rho v_{2}\\
\rho v_{1}v_{2}\\
\rho v_{2}^{2}+p\end{pmatrix}.

(4.4)

Thus we can write the system in the following conservative form

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{U}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\mathbf{F}^{(1)}(\mathbf{U})+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\mathbf{F}^{(2)}(\mathbf{U})=0

and hence obtain the following linearization

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{A}^{(1)}(\overline{\mathbf{U}})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{A}^{(2)}(\overline{\mathbf{U}})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\mathbf{w}=0.

Now we could apply the obtained results to the linearized conservative system. However, since we assume some regularity of the solution and the deviation, it is beneficial to use a suited set of primitive variables.
Here we will use
(\rho,\mathbf{v})
which results in the following systems, cf.
[
49
,
47
]
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{x}\rho+\rho\nabla_{x}\cdot\mathbf{v}&=0,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}v_{i}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{x}v_{i}+\frac{a^{2}}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\rho&=0,\;i\in\{1,2\}\end{split}

(4.5)

with
a>0
denoting the speed of sound.
The Jacobians are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}&\rho&0\\
\frac{a^{2}}{\rho}&v_{1}&0\\
0&0&v_{1}\end{pmatrix}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{A}^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{2}&0&\rho\\
0&v_{2}&0\\
\frac{a^{2}}{\rho}&0&v_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.6)

When linearizing the system we simply fix the Jacobians at the desired reference state.
We consider the reference state
\overline{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\overline{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
The linearized system can be symmetrized by applying the following variable transformation
\mathbf{w}\to\mathbf{w}^{\ast}=(r,\mathbf{v})^{T}

\rho\to r:=\frac{\overline{a}}{\overline{\rho}}\rho,

see also
[
25
]
. We hence obtain

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{w}^{\ast}+\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(1)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\mathbf{w}^{\ast}+\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(2)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\mathbf{w}^{\ast}=0

with the Jacobians

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{v}_{1}&\overline{a}&0\\
\overline{a}&\overline{v}_{1}&0\\
0&0&\overline{v}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{v}_{2}&0&\overline{a}\\
0&\overline{v}_{2}&0\\
\overline{a}&0&\overline{v}_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.7)

Since the Jacobians are constant and
\mathbf{B}\equiv\mathbf{0}
we do not have to discuss the term (
3.2
). Thus we have
C_{B}=0
and set
C_{L}:=C_{A}>0
.
We now discuss the LMI

\displaystyle C_{L}\mathbf{Id}+m_{1}\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(1)}+m_{2}\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(2)}\leq 0

(4.8)

which, as stated before, is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\overline{\mathbf{m}})

\displaystyle:=-\mathbf{Id}+\overline{m}_{1}\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(1)}+\overline{m}_{2}\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{(2)}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\overline{\mathbf{m}})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-1+\overline{m}_{1}\overline{v}_{1}+\overline{m}_{2}\overline{v}_{2}&\overline{m}_{1}\overline{a}&\overline{m}_{2}\overline{a}\\
\overline{m}_{1}\overline{a}&-1+\overline{m}_{1}\overline{v}_{1}+\overline{m}_{2}\overline{v}_{2}&0\\
\overline{m}_{2}\overline{a}&0&-1+\overline{m}_{1}\overline{v}_{1}+\overline{m}_{2}\overline{v}_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

A sufficient criterion would be to choose
\overline{m}_{1}
and
\overline{m}_{2}
such that
\mathbf{A}(\overline{\mathbf{m}})
becomes strongly diagonal dominant, i.e., the diagonal entries are strict positive and
larger than the sum of the absolute row entries. Due to the special structure of the matrix we hence look for coefficients with the property that

-1+\overline{m}_{1}\overline{v}_{1}+\overline{m}_{2}\overline{v}_{2}=\sigma\left(|\overline{m}_{1}|+|\overline{m}_{2}|\right)\overline{a},\;\sigma\in(1,\infty).

With this choice we have

-1+\overline{m}_{1}\overline{v}_{1}+\overline{m}_{2}\overline{v}_{2}>\left(|\overline{m}_{1}|+|\overline{m}_{2}|\right)\overline{a}\geq\max\{|\overline{m}_{1}|,|\overline{m}_{2}|\}\overline{a}\geq\min\{|\overline{m}_{1}|,|\overline{m}_{2}|\}\overline{a}\geq 0

and hence the matrix
\mathbf{A}(\overline{\mathbf{m}})
is positive definite.
Therefore
-\mathbf{A}(\overline{\mathbf{m}})
is negative definite and with the choice
\mathbf{m}=-C_{L}\overline{\mathbf{m}}
the LMI (
4.8
) holds and we further set

\mu(\mathbf{x})=m_{1}x_{1}+m_{2}x_{2}.

It remains to study the boundary term and prescribe boundary conditions, such that
\mathcal{BC}\geq 0
.
To this end we will make use of the eigenstructure of the system given in the appendix for the readers convenience.
For the sake of demonstration we consider the domain
\Omega=[0,1]\times[0,1]
and a flow with
\overline{\mathbf{v}}=(\overline{v}_{1},0)^{T}
and
0<\overline{a}<\overline{v}_{1}
.
For the boundary integral we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{BC}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{A}^{\ast}(\mathbf{n})\mathbf{w}\exp(\mu(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{q}^{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\ast}(\mathbf{n})\mathbf{q}\exp(\mu(x_{1},y_{2}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\partial\Omega}q_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{n})\exp(\mu(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

where
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{T}^{T}(\mathbf{n})\mathbf{w}
is calculated according to (
A.4
) and
\mathbf{n}
denotes the outward pointing normal of the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Now we identify the controllable and uncontrollable parts of the boundary, i.e. for
i=1,2,3

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left.\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\,\right|\,\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))\geq 0\right\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left.\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\,\right|\,\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))<0\right\}.

These are given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}^{-}

\displaystyle=\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{1}^{+},\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}^{+}

\displaystyle=\{1\}\times(0,1),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}^{-}

\displaystyle=\{0\}\times(0,1),\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}^{+}

\displaystyle=\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{2}^{-},

(4.9)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}^{-}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}^{+}

\displaystyle=\partial\Omega.

Let us denote the general controls by
\varphi(t,x_{1},x_{2})
for the first component and
\psi(t,x_{2})
for the second component, respectively.
These have to be chosen such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{BC}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{1}^{+}}\varphi(t,x_{1},x_{2})^{2}(\mathbf{n}(x_{1},x_{2})\overline{v}_{1}-\overline{a})\exp(\mu(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}-\overline{v}_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t,x_{2})^{2}\exp(\mu(0,x_{2}))\,\textup{d}x_{2}}_{\leq 0}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{(\overline{v}_{1}-\overline{a})\int_{0}^{1}q_{1}^{2}\exp(\mu(1,x_{2}))\,\textup{d}x_{2}+\overline{v}_{1}\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{2}^{-}}q_{2}^{2}\mathbf{n}(x_{1},x_{2})\exp(\mu(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\partial\Omega}q_{3}^{2}(\mathbf{n}(x_{1},x_{2})\overline{v}_{1}+\overline{a})\exp(\mu(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}}_{\geq 0}\geq 0.

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{1}^{+}}\varphi(t,x_{1},x_{2})^{2}(\overline{a}-\mathbf{n}(x_{1},x_{2})\overline{v}_{1})\exp(\mu(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}\leq{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\dots}

5
Summary

A novel Lyapunov function for
L^{2}-
control of multi–dimensional systems of hyperbolic equations has been presented.
We want to emphasize that up to our best knowledge this is the first result on stabilizing boundary feedback control applied to general multi–dimensional hyperbolic systems.
The analysis relies on the fact that the system can be diagonalized on the boundary and based on the proper Lyapunov function an estimate involving the symmetric Jacobians can be established.
A stabilizing feedback control has been derived and exponential decay of a weighted
L^{2}-
norm has been proven.
Illustrating examples have been presented to show the applicability of the presented result.
Future work concerns numerical studies, the application to complex domains, the investigation and classification of problems which possess a feasible Lyapunov potential.
It is of further interest to study whether this approach can also be generalized to the semilinear or the quasilinear case.
