Combinatorial Methods for Barcode Analysis
Edgar Jaramillo-Rodriguez

Abstract
A barcode is a finite multiset of intervals on the real line, B = { ( b i , d i ) } i = 1 n \displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n} . Barcodes are important objects in topological data analysis, where they serve as summaries of the persistent homology groups of a filtration. The combinatorial properties of barcodes have also been studied, mainly in the context of interval orders and interval graphs. In this paper, we define a new family of maps from the space of barcodes with n \displaystyle n bars to the permutation sets of various multisets, known as multipermutations . These multipermutations provide new combinatorial invariants on the space of barcodes. We then define an order relation on these multipermutations, which we show can be interpreted as a crossing number for barcodes, reminiscent of Túran’s crossing number for graphs. Next, we show that the resulting posets are order-isomorphic to principal ideals of a well known poset known as the multinomial Newman lattice. Consequently, these posets form the graded face-lattices of polytopes, which we refer to as barcode lattices or barcode polytopes . Finally, we show that for a large class of barcodes, these invariants can provide bounds on the Wasserstein and bottleneck distances between a pair of barcodes, linking these discrete invariants to continuous metrics on barcodes.



Keywords
Topological Data Analysis, Barcodes, Bruhat Order, Permutahedron, Multinomial Newman Lattice.

1
Introduction

A
barcode
is a finite multiset of intervals on the real line,
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Barcodes are important objects in persistent homology, where they serve as summaries of the persistent homology groups of a given filtration
[
17
]
. In this context, each bar in a barcode represents an interval of resolutions in a filtration during which a particular generator is present. By analyzing the lengths and arrangements of the different bars researchers are able to determine significant features in the filtration. This theory has been applied to the study of data, where a filtration is applied to some dataset and its persistent homology groups are used to determine features in the underlying distribution
[
5
,
10
]
.

It is often desirable to study statistics on barcodes and much work has been done in this area (see, for instance,
[
7
]
).
Recently, researchers have begun studying combinatorial invariants associated to barcodes. In particular, Kanari, Garin, and Hess discovered a natural mapping between the space of barcodes with
\displaystyle n+1
bars and the symmetric group
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
in
[
11
,
8
]
. In follow-up works, Brück and Garin refine this mapping in order to stratify the space of barcodes into regions with the same average and standard deviation in each endpoint and the same permutation types
[
4
]
. The coordinates obtained through this mapping are an elegant blend of continuous and discrete invariants.

1.1
Our Contributions

In this work, we define a new family of combinatorial invariants on the space of barcodes. The first of these invariants comes from constructing a map from the space of barcodes with
\displaystyle n
bars to certain equivalence classes of the multinomial Newman Lattice,
\displaystyle L(2^{n})
, whose elements are all permutations of the multiset
\displaystyle\{1^{2},2^{2},\ldots,n^{2}\}
. We then define a partial order on these equivalence classes and we call the resulting poset the
combinatorial barcode poset
. This poset admits an elegant construction based on an original notion of the
crossing number
of a barcode, reminiscent of Túran’s crossing number for graphs.

Corollary
1
.

Let
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a barcode. Then the rank of of
\displaystyle B
in the combinatorial barcode lattice, denoted
\displaystyle\rho{(g(B))}
, is given by

\rho(g(B))=\sum_{b_{i}<b_{j}}\cross(i,j).

Our first main result is that the unlabeled barcode poset, is order-isomorphic to a sublattice of
\displaystyle L(2^{n})
.

Theorem
1
.

The combinatorial barcode poset
\displaystyle(L(2^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n},\leq_{u})
is isomorphic to a principal ideal of the multinomial Newman lattice,
\displaystyle L(2^{n})
. Consequently,
\displaystyle(L(2^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n},\leq_{u})
is a lattice.

Next, we generalize this construction by considering a family of maps from the space of barcodes to equivalence classes of the lattice
\displaystyle L((2^{k}+1)^{n})
,
consisting of all permutations of the multiset
\displaystyle\{1^{2^{k}+1},2^{2^{k}+1},\ldots n^{2^{k}+1}\}
, where
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
This produces an entire family of new combinatorial invariants, denoted
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)
. We show that these invariants retain the graded lattice structure found in the
\displaystyle k=0
case, and so we call the poset of all
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)
the
power
\displaystyle k
barcode lattice
.

Theorem
2
.

The power
\displaystyle k
barcode poset
\displaystyle(L((2^{k}+1)^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n},\leq_{k})
is isomorphic to a principal ideal of the multinomial Newman lattice,
\displaystyle L((2^{k}+1)^{n})
. Consequently,
\displaystyle(L((2^{k}+1)^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n},\leq_{k})
is a lattice.

Moreover, we show that larger values of
\displaystyle k
produce invariants,
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)
which contain the invariants produced by smaller values. It follows that increasing
\displaystyle k
amounts to refining the information in the invariant. We then prove that as
\displaystyle k
goes to infinity, this invariant uniquely determines a large family of barcodes up to an affine transformation.

Theorem
3
.

Let
\displaystyle B,B^{\prime}
be fundamentally strict barcodes with
\displaystyle n
bars, where
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\displaystyle B^{\prime}=\{(b_{i}^{\prime},d_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Additionally, let
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)
denote its power-
\displaystyle k
invariant, and likewise for
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
. If
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)=g_{k}(B^{\prime})
for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
and the interval graph
\displaystyle G_{B}
(equivalently
\displaystyle G_{B^{\prime}}
) is connected, then there exist constants
\displaystyle\alpha>0
and
\displaystyle\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\displaystyle B=\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta
, where
\displaystyle\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta:=\{(\alpha b_{i}^{\prime}+\delta,\alpha d_{i}^{\prime}+\delta):i\in[n]\}
.

We also show that these invariants can provide upper bounds on the bottleneck and
\displaystyle q
-Wasserstein metrics (
\displaystyle d_{\infty}
and
\displaystyle d_{q}
, respectively) between barcodes, up to an affine transformation.

Theorem
4
.

Let
\displaystyle B,B^{\prime}
be
\displaystyle k
-strict barcodes with
\displaystyle n
bars such that their power-
\displaystyle k
invariants are equal,
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)=g_{k}(B^{\prime})
. Suppose there exists a bar
\displaystyle(b_{*},d_{*})\in B
(or equivalently in
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
) which contains all others, that is to say
\displaystyle b_{*}\leq b_{i}
and
\displaystyle d_{*}\geq d_{i}
for all
\displaystyle i\in[n]
.
Then there exist constants
\displaystyle\alpha>0
and
\displaystyle\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle d_{\infty}(B,\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lvert d_{*}-b_{*}\rvert}{2^{k}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\displaystyle d_{q}(B,\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq(n-1)^{\frac{1}{q}}\frac{\lvert d_{*}-b_{*}\rvert}{2^{k}}.

Finally, we show that the barcodes lattices form polytopes via an embedding into the classic permutahedron. In fact, these polytopes are a special case of
Bruhat interval polytopes
, studied at length in
[
16
]
. We use some results therein to compute the dimensions of our barcode polytopes (Corollary
2
).

Background

2.1
Barcodes

The language of barcodes used below comes from persistence homology; see
[
10
]
for an introduction and details.

Definition 1
.

A
barcode
is a finite multiset of intervals on the real line,
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where necessarily
\displaystyle b_{i}<d_{i}
for all
\displaystyle i\in[n]
. Each interval is called a
bar
; its left endpoint
\displaystyle b_{i}
is called its
birth (time)
and its right endpoint
\displaystyle d_{i}
is called its
death (time)
. We denote the set of all barcodes with
\displaystyle n
bars by
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{n}
.

Remark 1
.

Readers familiar with persistent homology will notice that we suppose that the bars corresponding to essential classes have finite values instead of being half-open intervals. This is reasonable in the context of computing Rips/Čech complexes of a dataset, where there is at most a single essential class and it is assigned a finite death-time in order to display it in a diagram.

Barcodes are often displayed as a stacked set of intervals above the real line as in Figure
1(b)
. Often one will refer to this diagram itself as a barcode. Barcodes are also commonly represented as points
\displaystyle(b_{i},d_{i})
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
in a figure known as a
persistence diagram
. Figure
1(c)
shows the persistence diagram for the barcode in Figure
1(b)
. Note that the points in Figure
1(c)
lie above the diagonal since we require that
\displaystyle b_{i}<d_{i}
for all
\displaystyle i\in[n]
.

(b)

(c)

:
We compute the Rips filtration of the sample points in (
1(a)
), then display the order 0 and order 1 persistence homology groups as both a barcode (
1(b)
) and a persistence diagram (
1(c)
).

Definition 2
.

A barcode
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is called
strict
if
\displaystyle b_{i}\neq d_{j}
for all
\displaystyle(i,j)\in[n]^{2}
and
\displaystyle b_{i}\neq b_{j}
,
\displaystyle d_{i}\neq d_{j}
for all
\displaystyle i\neq j
, i.e., if no birth/death times are repeated among the bars. We denote the set of strict barcodes with
\displaystyle n
bars by
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{n}_{st}
.

A similar definition of strict barcodes was first introduced in
[
11
]
to define a bijection between
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and an equivalence classes of barcodes defined therein. Their definition differed slightly from ours in that: (1) we do not require that there exist an essential bar
\displaystyle(b_{0},d_{0})
that contains all the others, and (2) we require that no times are shared between births and deaths,
\displaystyle b_{i}\neq d_{j}
, which is not present in their definition. Furthermore, we emphasize that the equivalence classes defined in
[
11
]
are different from those we will define in Section
3.1
.

We can define a topology on the space of all barcodes, regardless of strictness or the number of bars, by way of a distance function. Two popular and well-studied choices are the
bottleneck distance

and the
\displaystyle q
-Wasserstein distance

[
9
]
.

Definition 3
.

Let
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\displaystyle B^{\prime}=\{(b_{i}^{\prime},d_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{m}
be two barcodes. The
bottleneck distance
between
\displaystyle B
and
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
is

d_{\infty}(B,B^{\prime})=\inf_{\gamma}\max_{x\in B}\|x-\gamma(x)\|_{\infty},

where
\displaystyle\gamma
runs over all perfects matchings of the points (bars)
\displaystyle x_{i}=(b_{i},d_{i})
in
\displaystyle B
and the points (bars) in
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
, allowing bars to be matched to the diagonal
\displaystyle\Delta=\{(x,x):x\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Here
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the
\displaystyle\ell^{\infty}
-norm on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Intuitively, the bottleneck distance computes the largest distance in
\displaystyle\ell^{\infty}
-norm between a pair of matched points on the persistence diagrams of
\displaystyle B
and
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
, taking the infimum over all perfect matchings. The
\displaystyle q
-Wasserstein distance is similar; it can be thought of as the total distance between all matched points, again taking the infimum over all perfect matchings.

Definition 4
.

Let
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\displaystyle B^{\prime}=\{(b_{i}^{\prime},d_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{m}
be two barcodes. The
\displaystyle q
-Wasserstein distance
between
\displaystyle B
and
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
is

d_{q}(B,B^{\prime})=\inf_{\gamma}\big(\sum_{x\in B}\|x-\gamma(x)\|_{\infty}^{q}\big)^{1/q},

where
\displaystyle\gamma
runs over all perfects matchings of the points (bars)
\displaystyle x_{i}=(b_{i},d_{i})
in
\displaystyle B
and the points (bars) in
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
, allowing bars to be matched to the diagonal
\displaystyle\Delta=\{(x,x):x\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Here
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the
\displaystyle\ell^{\infty}
-norm on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

2.2
Weak Bruhat Orders and Newman Lattices

We will assume the reader is familiar with the basic definitions and properties of posets. For a complete introduction to partially ordered sets see
[
15
, Ch.3]
, for example.

We recall for the reader that a
lattice
is a poset
\displaystyle L
for which every pair of elements has a least upper bound and greatest lower bound. We also recall that a
principal ideal
of a lattice
\displaystyle L
is a subposet of the form
\displaystyle I=\{s\in L:s\leq\alpha\}
for some
\displaystyle\alpha\in L
; we say
\displaystyle I
is the principal ideal generated by
\displaystyle\alpha
. It is a well known result that a principal ideal
\displaystyle I
of a lattice
\displaystyle L
is a sublattice of
\displaystyle L
.

A lattice of particular interest to us is the
permutahedron

[
6
]
. Recall that for
\displaystyle\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
an
inversion
in
\displaystyle\pi
is a pair
\displaystyle(\pi_{i},\pi_{j})
such that
\displaystyle i<j
and
\displaystyle\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}
, i.e., it is a pair of elements that appear out of order. The
inversion set
of
\displaystyle\pi
,
\displaystyle\inv(\pi)
, is the set of all inversions in
\displaystyle\pi
. The
\displaystyle\emph{inversionnumber}
of
\displaystyle\pi
is the cardinality of its inversion set
\displaystyle\#\inv(\pi)
. For example, if
\displaystyle\pi=(1~2~5~4~3~6)\in\mathfrak{S}_{6}
then
\displaystyle\inv(\pi)=\{(5,4),(5,3),(4,3)\}
and
\displaystyle\#\inv(\pi)=3
. Notice that
\displaystyle\pi\neq\sigma\implies\inv(\pi)\neq\inv(\sigma)
, so we can think of
\displaystyle\inv
as an injective map from the permutations in
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
to subsets of
\displaystyle[n]^{2}
.

The
weak Bruhat order
(or
weak order
for short) is the relation
\displaystyle\leq_{W}
on
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{N}
defined by
\displaystyle\pi\leq_{W}\sigma
if and only if
\displaystyle\inv(\pi)\subseteq\inv(\sigma)
. Note that
\displaystyle\pi\leq_{W}\sigma\implies\#\inv(\pi)\leq\#\inv(\sigma)
, but the converse need not hold.
One can show that the
\displaystyle\pi\lessdot_{W}\sigma
if and only if
\displaystyle\#\inv(\pi)+1=\#\inv(\sigma)
and
\displaystyle\sigma=(i\,\,\,i+1)\pi
for some
\displaystyle i\in[n-1]
, which means that
\displaystyle\sigma
equals
\displaystyle\pi
after transposing a pair of its
adjacent
entries.

It is a well known result that the weak order on
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
forms the face lattice of a polytope known as the permutahedron
[
2
,
6
]
; therefore we to refer to the poset
\displaystyle(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\leq_{W})
as the permutahedron as well.
The weak Bruhat order can also be defined similarly on arbitrary Coxeter systems (see
[
3
]
), but for this manuscript the definition on the symmetric group is sufficient.

One can generalize the construction of the permutahedron to multiset permutations. For
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, let
\displaystyle L(\mathbf{m})
denote the set of permutations of the multiset
\displaystyle M=\{1^{m_{1}},\ldots,n^{m_{n}}\}
, with the usual multiset notation where exponents represent multiplicity.
An order relation on
\displaystyle L(\mathbf{m})
can be succinctly defined via the following cover relations. For
\displaystyle s,t\in L(\mathbf{m})
, we say that
\displaystyle t
covers
\displaystyle s
if and only if
\displaystyle s
and
\displaystyle t
differ only in swapping an adjacent pair of entries, which are in numerical order in
\displaystyle s
but are reversed in
\displaystyle t
. For instance, we have that
\displaystyle(1~1~2~3~2)\lessdot(1~2~1~3~2)
in
\displaystyle L(2,2,1)
. The poset
\displaystyle L(\mathbf{m})
is called the
multinomial Newman lattice
and was originally introduced by Bennett and Birkhoff in
[
1
]
.

The multinomial Newman order can also be defined explicitly as follows.
Consider the
set
,

S=\{1_{1},\ldots,1_{m_{1}},\ldots,n_{1},\ldots,n_{m_{n}}\},

which we endow with the lexicographic total ordering
\displaystyle 1_{1}\lessdot 1_{2}\lessdot\ldots\lessdot 1_{m_{1}}\lessdot\ldots\lessdot n_{m_{n}}
.
Note that we may identify a multipermutation
\displaystyle s\in L(\mathbf{m})
with a unique corresponding permutation
\displaystyle\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{S}
where we require that copies of the same elements appear in lexicographic order, that is to say
\displaystyle i_{j}
appears before
\displaystyle i_{k}
in
\displaystyle\pi
for all
\displaystyle i\in[n]
and all
\displaystyle j<k
. For example, the multipermutation
\displaystyle(1~2~1~3~2)\in L(2,2,1)
is identified with the permutation
\displaystyle(1_{1}~2_{1}~1_{2}~3_{1}~2_{2})
. Let
\displaystyle\iota:L(\mathbf{m})\to\mathfrak{S}_{S}
denote this mapping.

One can show that
\displaystyle\iota
is in fact an order-isomorphism from
\displaystyle L(\mathbf{m})
to a principal ideal of the permuhatedron
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{S}
; it follows that
\displaystyle L(\mathbf{m})
is also a lattice
[
1
,
13
,
14
]
. Specifically, the multinomial Newman lattice is isomorphic to the principal ideal generated by
\displaystyle(n_{1}\ldots n_{m_{n}}\ldots 1_{1}\ldots 1_{m_{1}})
. Thus
\displaystyle L(\mathbf{m})
has (necessarily unique) minimal and maximal elements, denoted
\displaystyle\hat{0}
and
\displaystyle\hat{1}
respectively.
\displaystyle\hat{0}
is the identity permutation
\displaystyle(1~1\ldots 1~2~2\ldots n)
while
\displaystyle\hat{1}
is the “fully reversed” permutation
\displaystyle(n~n\ldots n~(n-1)~(n-1)\ldots 1)
.

3
The Unlabeled Barcode Poset

3.1
Barcodes as Multiset Permutations

Let
\displaystyle L(2^{n})
denote the multinomial Newman lattice
\displaystyle L(\mathbf{m})
for
\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=(2,2,\ldots,2)\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.
Consider a strict barcode
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. By definition, the birth/death times in
\displaystyle B
are distinct, so the set
\displaystyle T=\{b_{1},d_{1},\ldots,b_{n},d_{n}\}
can be linearly ordered:
\displaystyle t_{i_{1}}<t_{i_{2}}<\ldots<t_{i_{2n}}
.
The indices in this order induce a multipermutation
\displaystyle(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{2n})\in L(2^{n})
.
For example, if
\displaystyle B_{1}
is the strict barcode with 3 bars given by
\displaystyle b_{1}=1.0,\,d_{1}=2.0,\,b_{2}=1.5,\,d_{2}=3.0,\,b_{3}=2.5,\,d_{3}=2.75
, then the birth/death times in
\displaystyle T
are ordered:
\displaystyle b_{1}<b_{2}<d_{1}<b_{3}<d_{3}<b_{2}
. This produces the permutation
\displaystyle(1~2~1~3~3~2)\in L(2^{3})
.

Let
\displaystyle f:\mathcal{B}^{n}_{st}\to L(2^{n})
denote this mapping, then we can use
\displaystyle f
to define an equivalence relation on
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{n}_{st}
, which we call
labeled barcode equivalence
. We leave it to the reader to verify that the following definition satisfies the necessary requirements of an equivalence relation.

Definition 5

(Labeled barcode equivalence)
.

Let
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a strict barcode whose birth/death times,
\displaystyle T=\{b_{1},d_{1},\ldots,b_{n},d_{n}\}
, are ordered:
\displaystyle t_{i_{1}}<t_{i_{2}}<\ldots<t_{i_{2n}}
. Define the map
\displaystyle f:\mathcal{B}^{n}_{st}\to L(2^{n})
so that
\displaystyle f(B)=(i_{1}~i_{2}~\ldots~i_{2n})
. Then, we say two barcodes
\displaystyle B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}^{n}_{st}
are
labeled barcode equivalent
if and only if
\displaystyle f(B_{1})=f(B_{2})
.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, one issue with labeled barcode equivalence is its dependence on a given labeling. For example, consider the strict barcode
\displaystyle B_{2}
given by:
\displaystyle b_{2}=1.0,\,d_{2}=2.0,\,b_{1}=1.5,\,d_{1}=3.0,\,b_{3}=2.5,\,d_{3}=2.75
. Clearly,
\displaystyle B_{2}
is the same barcode as
\displaystyle B_{1}
from the prior example, except that the labels of bars 1 and 2 have been swapped. As a result,
\displaystyle f(B_{1})=(1~2~1~3~3~2)
while
\displaystyle f(B_{2})=(2~1~2~3~3~1)
and hence the two are not labeled barcode equivalent. Ideally, we would want any two barcodes to remain equivalent if their images under
\displaystyle f
are the same up to some relabeling.

To that end, consider the group action
\displaystyle\Sigma:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times L(2^{n})\to L(2^{n})
given by
\displaystyle\Sigma(\pi,s)=\pi\circ s
, where

\displaystyle\pi\circ s
denotes
\displaystyle\pi
acting element-wise on the terms in
\displaystyle s
. Let
\displaystyle[s]
denote the orbit of
\displaystyle s\in L(2^{n})
and let
\displaystyle L(2^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the set of all orbits, which we will call the space of
combinatorial barcodes
with
\displaystyle n
bars.
We can use this action to define a label-agnostic equivalence relation on the space of barcodes, which we call
combinatorial barcode equivalence
. Again, we ask the reader to verify the following definition satisfies the necessary requirements.

Definition 6

(combinatorial barcode equivalence)
.

Let
\displaystyle B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{st}
and let
\displaystyle f:\mathcal{B}^{n}_{st}\to L(2^{n})
denote the labeled barcode equivalence map. Let
\displaystyle g:\mathcal{B}^{n}_{st}\to L(2^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the map given by
\displaystyle g(B)=[f(B)]
.
We say
\displaystyle B_{1},B_{2}
are
combinatorially equivalent
if and only if
\displaystyle g(B_{1})=g(B_{2})
.

3.2
Ordering Combinatorial Barcodes

We must now the decide on a convention for choosing a representative of a combinatorial barcode, which again is an orbit in
\displaystyle L(2^{n})
. Let
\displaystyle s\in L(2^{n})
and consider the substring
\displaystyle\tau_{s}
of
\displaystyle s
given by the first occurrence of each element (birth times). Now consider
\displaystyle\tau_{s}
as a permutation in
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
given in one-line notation. Then, the action of
\displaystyle\tau_{s}^{-1}
on
\displaystyle s
relabels
\displaystyle s
so that the birth times now appear in order, i.e., the first copy of 1 appears before the first copy of 2, which appears before the first copy of 3, and so on.
For example, for
\displaystyle s=(2~1~4~1~3~3~2~4)\in L(2^{n})
we have that
\displaystyle\tau_{s}=(2~1~4~3)
and so
\displaystyle\tau_{s}^{-1}\circ s=(1~2~3~2~4~4~1~3)
.
Note that if
\displaystyle u,v\in[s]
we have

\displaystyle\tau_{u}^{-1}\circ u=\tau_{v}^{-1}\circ v
. Hence the map
\displaystyle\psi:L(2^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to L(2^{n})
given by
\displaystyle\psi([s])=\tau_{s}^{-1}\circ s
does not depend on the choice of
\displaystyle s
. Therefore, we define the
canonical representative
of an orbit,
\displaystyle[s]
to be the permutation
\displaystyle\psi(s)\in L(2^{n})
.

Observe that
\displaystyle\psi
is injective, since by definition
\displaystyle\tau_{s}^{-1}\circ s\in[s]
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\psi

gives us an embedding of the combinatorial barcodes back into the multinomial Newman lattice, which we can use to extend the multinomial Newman order to
\displaystyle L(2^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

Definition 7
.

Let
\displaystyle[s],[t]\in L(2^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and let
\displaystyle\psi:L(2^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to L(2^{n})
be the map which sends each orbit to its canonical representative. Then, let
\displaystyle\leq_{c}
be denote relation given by
\displaystyle[s]\leq_{c}[t]
if and only if
\displaystyle\psi([s])\leq\psi([t])
, where
\displaystyle\leq
denotes the multinomial Newman order. We call the pair
\displaystyle(L(2^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n},\leq_{c})
the
combinatorial barcode poset
or
combinatorial barcode lattice
(we will see in a moment that this name is justified).

Remark 2
.

Note that we could have directly defined a map between
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{n}_{st}
and
\displaystyle L(2^{n})

4

\displaystyle k

In this section we generalize the construction in Section
3
, producing an entire family of multipermutations associated to barcodes. We will show that these new multipermutations share the same lattice structure as before, while also providing increasingly detailed information about the arrangement of the bars in a barcode. Ultimately, we show that for a large class of barcodes, these discrete invariants can provide bounds on two classical, continuous metrics on barcodes: the Wasserstein and bottleneck distances.

We first recall for the reader a mapping, defined in
[
11
,
8
]
, from the space of strict barcodes with
\displaystyle n
bars to the symmetric group
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Let
\displaystyle B\in\mathcal{B}_{st}^{n}
; by ordering the death times increasingly such that
\displaystyle d_{i_{1}}<d_{i_{2}}<\ldots<d_{i_{n}}

\displaystyle[n]
gives a permutation
\displaystyle\sigma_{d}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
defined by
\displaystyle\sigma_{B}(k)=i_{k}

\displaystyle\sigma_{B}
is the unique permutation such that
\displaystyle d_{\sigma_{B}(1)}<d_{\sigma_{B}(2)}<\ldots<d_{\sigma_{B}(n)}

\displaystyle\tau_{B}

\displaystyle\tau_{f(B)}

\displaystyle\psi

For example, if
\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle b_{2}=1.0,\,d_{2}=2.0,\,b_{1}=1.5,\,d_{1}=3.0,\,b_{3}=2.5,\,d_{3}=2.75

\displaystyle b_{2}<b_{1}<d_{2}<b_{3}<d_{3}<b_{1}

\displaystyle\psi(g(B))

\displaystyle\phi(B)

Proposition 2

Let
\displaystyle B\in\mathcal{B}_{st}^{n}

\displaystyle\phi(B)

is there a further generalization of this construction where we consider more points in each bar rather than just the birth/death times?

To that end, we must first determine a sensible way of selecting more points from each bar. A natural choice is to take the endpoints of all the intervals we get when splitting each bar into
\displaystyle 2^{k}

\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle k

, the invariant
\displaystyle\psi_{j}(g_{j}(B))
is a sub-permutation of
\displaystyle\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B))
for all
\displaystyle k>j
. Specifically, note that if we delete every other occurrence (beginning with the second) of
\displaystyle i
in
\displaystyle f_{k+1}(B)
, for each
\displaystyle i\in[n]
, then the resulting multipermutation is precisely
\displaystyle f_{k}(B)
. Hence, we have a map
\displaystyle\delta_{k}:(L((2^{k+1}+1)^{n})\to(L((2^{k}+1)^{n})
such that
\displaystyle\delta_{k}\circ f_{k+1}=f_{k}
.

For instance, consider the barcode
\displaystyle B
given by
\displaystyle b_{1}=1.0,\,d_{1}=2.5,\,b_{2}=1.5,\,d_{2}=4.0,\,b_{3}=3.0,\,d_{3}=3.5
. Taking
\displaystyle k=1
, we add the points
\displaystyle m_{1}=1.75,\,m_{2}=2.75,\,m_{3}=3.25
so
\displaystyle f_{1}(B)=(1~2~1~1~2~3~3~3~2)
. The map
\displaystyle\delta_{0}
deletes the points
\displaystyle m_{i}
, which gives
\displaystyle(\delta_{0}\circ f_{1})(B)=(1~2~1~3~3~2)=f_{0}(B)
.

Hence, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\displaystyle B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}^{n}
. If
\displaystyle g_{k}(B_{1})=g_{k}(B_{2})
, then
\displaystyle g_{j}(B_{1})=g_{j}(B_{2})
for all
\displaystyle j<k
.

Thus, we see that increasing
\displaystyle k
amounts to producing ever more sensitive invariants
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)
. These higher order invariants capture more nuanced information about the overlaps of pairs of bars. For instance, we have seen that if a barcode
\displaystyle B
contains two nested bars then
\displaystyle g_{0}(B)
will contain the pattern
\displaystyle(1~2~2~1)
. Going up a level,
\displaystyle g_{1}(B)
confirms that the bars are nested but also tells us whether bar 2 is contained in the left half of bar 1, in the right half of bar 1, or whether it straddles the midpoint of 1 (see Figure
5
).

In fact, we see that all the possible patterns of 1’s and 2’s tell you a distinct way the two bars intersect and how those intersections relate to the two halves of the bars.
By the same logic, higher values of
\displaystyle k
provide even more granular intersection data, now relating to quartiles (
\displaystyle k=2
), octiles (
\displaystyle k=3
), etc., of the bars.

.

\displaystyle g_{1}(B)=(1~1~2~2~2~1)

4.2
Connection to Bottleneck and Wasserstein Distances

From the observations above, it is clear that the power
\displaystyle k
invariant of a barcode
\displaystyle B
captures increasingly granular information about the relative positions of the bars as
\displaystyle k
increases. In fact, we will show that these invariants are closely related to classical metrics between barcodes such as the bottleneck and Wasserstein distances for a special family of barcodes.

Before explaining further, we first recall for the reader the notion of an interval graph
[
12
]
.

Definition 11
.

Let
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a barcode. The
interval graph
of
\displaystyle B
is the simple graph
\displaystyle G_{B}(V,E)
where
\displaystyle V=[n]
and an edge
\displaystyle(i,j)
is in
\displaystyle E
if and only if
\displaystyle(b_{i},d_{i})\cap(b_{j},d_{j})\neq\emptyset
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\displaystyle B,B^{\prime}
be strict barcodes. If
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)=g_{k}(B^{\prime})
for some
\displaystyle k\geq 0
then
\displaystyle G_{B}\cong G_{B^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

It is clear that the intersection of bars
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
can be determined from
\displaystyle\cross(i,j)
. Recall one can deduce
\displaystyle\cross\#(i,j)
from the power 0 invariant of a barcode for all
\displaystyle i\neq j
. Therefore, the power 0 invariant, and by Proposition
1
any power
\displaystyle k
invariant, completely determines the interval graph of its associated barcode.
∎

Theorem 3
.

Let
\displaystyle B,B^{\prime}
be fundamentally strict barcodes with
\displaystyle n
bars, where
\displaystyle B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\displaystyle B^{\prime}=\{(b_{i}^{\prime},d_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. If
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)=g_{k}(B^{\prime})
for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle G_{B}
(equivalently
\displaystyle G_{B^{\prime}}
) is connected, then there exist constants
\displaystyle\alpha>0
and
\displaystyle\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\displaystyle B=\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta
, where
\displaystyle\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta:=\{(\alpha b_{i}^{\prime}+\delta,\alpha d_{i}^{\prime}+\delta):i\in[n]\}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality assume that
\displaystyle B,B^{\prime}
are labeled according to increasing birth time, that is to say
\displaystyle b_{1}<b_{2}<\ldots<b_{n}
, and likewise for
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
. Now, let
\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime}}
,
\displaystyle\delta=b_{1}-\alpha b_{1}^{\prime}
, and define
\displaystyle T:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be function
\displaystyle T(x)=\alpha x+\delta
. Observe that
\displaystyle T(b_{1}^{\prime})=b_{1}
, and that

\displaystyle\displaystyle T(d_{1}^{\prime})=\frac{d_{1}^{\prime}(d_{1}-b_{1})}{d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime}}+b_{1}-\frac{b_{1}^{\prime}(d_{1}-b_{1})}{d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime}}=\frac{(d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime})(d_{1}-b_{1})}{d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime}}+b_{1}=d_{1}.

Now, let
\displaystyle i
be a neighbor of 1 in
\displaystyle G_{B}
(and hence in
\displaystyle G_{B^{\prime}}
, by Lemma
2
). We know at least one such
\displaystyle i
exists since
\displaystyle G_{B}
is connected. Take
\displaystyle k>0
to be arbitrary and let
\displaystyle m
denote the number of
\displaystyle 1
’s that appear before the first
\displaystyle i
in
\displaystyle\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B))
, or equivalently in
\displaystyle\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B^{\prime}))
. Note
\displaystyle 0<m<2^{k}
since necessarily
\displaystyle b_{1}<b_{i}<d_{1}
.
Thus, we have that

\displaystyle\displaystyle b_{1}+(m-1)\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,b_{i}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<b_{1}+m\frac{(d_{1}-b_{1})}{2^{k}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\displaystyle b_{1}^{\prime}+(m-1)\frac{d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime}}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,b_{i}^{\prime}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<b_{1}^{\prime}+m\frac{(d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime})}{2^{k}}.

Since
\displaystyle\alpha>0
, it follows that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha\Big(b_{1}^{\prime}+(m-1)\frac{d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime}}{2^{k}}\Big)+\delta

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,T(b_{i}^{\prime})\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\alpha\Big(b_{1}^{\prime}+m\frac{(d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime})}{2^{k}}\Big)+\delta

\displaystyle\displaystyle\implies b_{1}+\frac{(m-1)(d_{1}-b_{1})}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,T(b_{i}^{\prime})\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<b_{1}+\frac{m(d_{1}-b_{1})}{2^{k}}.

Therefore,
\displaystyle\lvert b_{i}-T(b_{i}^{\prime})\rvert<\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{2^{k}}
. Sending
\displaystyle k\to\infty
, it follows that
\displaystyle T(b_{i}^{\prime})=b_{i}
.

Now, if
\displaystyle b_{1}<d_{i}<d_{1}
, then repeating the argument above gives
\displaystyle T(d_{i}^{\prime})=d_{i}
. Otherwise, we have that
\displaystyle b_{1}<b_{i}<d_{1}<d_{i}
. Let
\displaystyle\ell
be the number of
\displaystyle i
’s that appear before the last 1 in
\displaystyle\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B))
. Note that
\displaystyle 0<\ell<2^{k}+1
, then we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\ell(d_{i}-b_{i})}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,d_{1}-b_{i}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\frac{(\ell+1)(d_{i}-b_{i})}{2^{k}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\ell(d_{i}^{\prime}-b_{i}^{\prime})}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{i}^{\prime}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\frac{(\ell+1)(d_{i}^{\prime}-b_{i}^{\prime})}{2^{k}}.

Again, as
\displaystyle\alpha>0
, it follows that,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\ell(\alpha d_{i}^{\prime}-\alpha b_{i}^{\prime})}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,\alpha d_{1}^{\prime}-\alpha b_{i}^{\prime}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\frac{(\ell+1)(\alpha d_{i}^{\prime}-\alpha b_{i}^{\prime})}{2^{k}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\implies\frac{\ell(T(d_{i}^{\prime})-T(b_{i}^{\prime}))}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,T(d_{1}^{\prime})-T(b_{i}^{\prime})\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\frac{(\ell+1)(T(d_{i}^{\prime})-T(b_{i}^{\prime}))}{2^{k}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\implies\frac{\ell(T(d_{i}^{\prime})-b_{i})}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,d_{1}-b_{i}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\frac{(\ell+1)(T(d_{i}^{\prime})-b_{i})}{2^{k}}.

Rearranging the inequalities above gives,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,\frac{d_{1}-b_{i}}{d_{i}-b_{i}}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\frac{(\ell+1)}{2^{k}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,\frac{d_{1}-b_{i}}{T(d_{i}^{\prime})-b_{i}}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\frac{(\ell+1)}{2^{k}}.

Sending
\displaystyle k\to\infty
it follows that
\displaystyle\frac{d_{1}-b_{i}}{d_{i}-b_{i}}=\frac{d_{1}-b_{i}}{T(d_{i}^{\prime})-b_{i}}
, from which we see that
\displaystyle T(d_{i}^{\prime})=d_{i}
.
Hence, if
\displaystyle i
is a neighbor of
\displaystyle 1
, then
\displaystyle(T(b_{i}^{\prime}),T(d_{i}^{\prime}))=(b_{i},d_{i})
.
Finally, note that we can repeat the arguments above to show that this holds for all vertices connected to 1 by some finite path. Since
\displaystyle G_{B}
is connected and finite, this gives the desired result.
∎

Thus, we see that the entire family of power
\displaystyle k
invariants completely determine a barcode
\displaystyle B
up to an affine transformation. Moreover, with an extra assumption on
\displaystyle B,B^{\prime}
, we get bounds on the rate of convergence.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\displaystyle B,B^{\prime}
be
\displaystyle k
-strict barcodes with
\displaystyle n
bars such that
\displaystyle g_{k}(B)=g_{k}(B^{\prime})
. Suppose there exists a bar
\displaystyle(b_{*},d_{*})\in B
(or equivalently in
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
) which contains all others, that is to say
\displaystyle b_{*}\leq b_{i}
and
\displaystyle d_{*}\geq d_{i}
for all
\displaystyle i\in[n]
.
Then there exist constants
\displaystyle\alpha>0
and
\displaystyle\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle d_{\infty}(B,\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lvert d_{*}-b_{*}\rvert}{2^{k}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\displaystyle d_{q}(B,\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq(n-1)^{\frac{1}{q}}\frac{\lvert d_{*}-b_{*}\rvert}{2^{k}}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality assume that
\displaystyle B,B^{\prime}
are labeled according to increasing birth time, that is to say
\displaystyle b_{1}<b_{2}<\ldots<b_{n}
, and likewise for
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
. Note that this implies that
\displaystyle(b_{*},d_{*})=(b_{1},d_{1})
. Moreover, since
\displaystyle\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B))=\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B^{\prime}))
we also have that
\displaystyle b_{1}^{\prime}\leq b_{i}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle d_{1}^{\prime}\geq d_{i}^{\prime}
for all
\displaystyle i\in[n]
.
Now, let
\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime}}
,
\displaystyle\delta=b_{1}-\alpha b_{1}^{\prime}
, and define
\displaystyle T:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be function
\displaystyle T(x)=\alpha x+\delta
. Observe that
\displaystyle T(b_{1}^{\prime})=b_{1}
, and that
\displaystyle T(d_{1}^{\prime})=d_{1}
. Now let
\displaystyle(b_{i},d_{i})
be another bar and let
\displaystyle m
denote the number of
\displaystyle 1
’s that appear before the first
\displaystyle i
in
\displaystyle\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B))
, or equivalently in
\displaystyle\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B^{\prime}))
. Note
\displaystyle 0<m<2^{k}
since
\displaystyle b_{1}<b_{i}<d_{1}
. Then we have that,

\displaystyle\displaystyle b_{1}+(m-1)\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,b_{i}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<b_{1}+m\frac{(d_{1}-b_{1})}{2^{k}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\displaystyle b_{1}^{\prime}+(m-1)\frac{d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime}}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle<\,b_{i}^{\prime}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle<b_{1}^{\prime}+m\frac{(d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime})}{2^{k}},

and since
\displaystyle\alpha>0
, it follows that

b_{1}+\frac{(m-1)(d_{1}-b_{1})}{2^{k}}<\,T(b_{i}^{\prime})\,<b_{1}+\frac{m(d_{1}-b_{1})}{2^{k}}.

Therefore,
\displaystyle\lvert b_{i}-T(b_{i}^{\prime})\rvert\leq\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{2^{k}}
. Recall that
\displaystyle d_{1}\geq d_{i}
for all
\displaystyle i\in[n]
, so by repeating the argument we get that
\displaystyle\lvert d_{i}-T(d_{i}^{\prime})\rvert\leq\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{2^{k}}
. Thus,
\displaystyle d_{\infty}(B,\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta)\leq\frac{\lvert d_{*}-b_{*}\rvert}{2^{k}}
. For the bound on the
\displaystyle q
-Wasserstein distance, observe that:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(b_{i},d_{i})-(T(b_{i}^{\prime})-T(d_{i}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}^{q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq\Big(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\big(\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{2^{k}}\big)^{q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Big((n-1)(\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{2^{k}}\big)^{q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(n-1)^{\frac{1}{q}}\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{2^{k}},

from which the result follows.
∎

Remark 5
.

We note that the extra assumption in Theorem
4
is necessary. Consider the barcodes
\displaystyle B=\{(0,1),(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\}
and
\displaystyle B^{\prime}=\{(0,1),(1-\varepsilon,2)\}
, where
\displaystyle\varepsilon<<\frac{1}{2^{k}}
. Let
\displaystyle T(x)=\alpha x+\delta
, with
\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{d_{1}-b_{1}}{d_{1}^{\prime}-b_{1}^{\prime}}
and
\displaystyle\delta=b_{1}-\alpha b_{1}^{\prime}
as in Theorems
3

4

\displaystyle T
is simply the identity map, so
\displaystyle\lvert T(2)-1+\varepsilon\rvert=1-\varepsilon
. Hence
\displaystyle d_{\infty}(B,\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta)=1-\varepsilon
. Thus, for fixed
\displaystyle\lvert d_{*}-b_{*}\rvert
and arbitrary
\displaystyle k
we can find a barcode
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
such that
\displaystyle\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B))=\psi_{k}(g_{k}(B^{\prime}))
and
\displaystyle d_{\infty}(B,\alpha B^{\prime}+\delta)
is arbitrarily close to 1.

4.3
Barcode Polytopes

It is a well known result that the permutahedron
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is also the face lattice of the polytope
\displaystyle P_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}=\conv\{(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\pi\in\mathfrak{S_{n}}\}
. Recall that by composing the maps
\displaystyle\iota\circ\psi_{k}
we get an embedding of
\displaystyle L((2^{k}+1)^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n}
into
\displaystyle P_{\mathfrak{S}_{n(2^{k}+1)}}
. This gives us a new polytope,
\displaystyle P_{n,k}=\conv\{(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n(2^{k}+1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n(2^{k}+1)}:\pi\in\image(\iota\circ\psi_{k})\}
. We call
\displaystyle P_{n,k}
the
power-
\displaystyle k
barcode polytope
.

Because this embedding sends
\displaystyle L((2^{k}+1)^{n})/\mathfrak{S}_{n}
to a prime-ideal, the polytope
\displaystyle P_{n,k}
is an example of a
Bruhat interval polytope

[
16
]
.

Definition 12
.

Let
\displaystyle u\leq v
be permutations in
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. The Bruhat interval polytope
\displaystyle Q_{u,v}
is the convex hull of all permutation vectors
\displaystyle(z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{n})
with
\displaystyle u\leq z\leq v
.

Note that
\displaystyle P_{n,k}
is equal to
\displaystyle Q_{u,v}
for
\displaystyle u=e\in\mathfrak{S}_{n(2k+1)}
and
\displaystyle v
the “fully nested” permutation
\displaystyle(1_{1}~2_{1}~\ldots~n_{1}~n_{2}~\ldots~n_{2k+1}~(n-1)_{2}~\ldots~1_{2^{k}}~1_{2^{k}+1})
, where
again we identify
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n(2k+1)}
with permutations of the totally ordered set
\displaystyle 1_{1}<1_{2}<\ldots n_{1}<\ldots<n_{2^{k}+1}
.

In
[
16
]
, the authors prove, among other things, the following formula for computing the dimension of a Bruhat interval polytope.
Let
\displaystyle u\leq v
be permutations in
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and let
\displaystyle C:u=x_{0}\lessdot x_{1}\lessdot\ldots\lessdot x_{\ell}=v
be any maximal chain from
\displaystyle u
to
\displaystyle v
.
Define a labeled graph
\displaystyle G^{C}
on
\displaystyle[n]
having an edge between vertices
\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle b
if and only if
\displaystyle x_{i}(ab)=x_{i+1}
for some
\displaystyle 0\leq i\leq\ell-1
. Define
\displaystyle\Pi_{C}={V_{1},V_{2},...,V_{r}}
to be the partition of
\displaystyle[n]
whose blocks
\displaystyle V_{j}
are the connected components of
\displaystyle G^{C}
.The authors show that the number of blocks does not depend on the choice of maximal chain
\displaystyle C
, so we let
\displaystyle\#\Pi_{u,v}
denote the number of blocks,
\displaystyle r
. The authors then prove the following.

Theorem 5

(
[
16
]
)
.

The dimension of the Bruhat interval polytope
\displaystyle Q_{u,v}
is
\displaystyle(n-\#\Pi_{u,v}).

From this result, it is easy to compute the dimension of the barcode
polytopes
\displaystyle P_{n,k}
.

Corollary 2
.

The dimension of the power-
\displaystyle k
barcode polytope,
\displaystyle P_{n,k}
is
\displaystyle n(2^{k}+1)-2
.

Proof.

Recall,
\displaystyle P_{n,k}=Q_{u,v}
for
\displaystyle u=e\in\mathfrak{S}_{n(2k+1)}
and
\displaystyle v
the “fully nested” permutation
\displaystyle(1_{1}~2_{1}~\ldots~n_{1}~n_{2}~\ldots~n_{2k+1}~(n-1)_{2}~\ldots~1_{2^{k}}~1_{2^{k}+1})
. Consider the maximal chain
\displaystyle C
from
\displaystyle u
to
\displaystyle v
given by moving each element into position one by one, starting with the 1’s in descending lexicographic order, then the 2’s is descending order, and so on. Note that traversing this chain requires that we use all adjacent transpositions except
\displaystyle(12)
since the
\displaystyle 1_{1}
term does not move. Hence,
\displaystyle\Pi_{C}=\{\{1_{1}\},\{1_{2},\ldots,1_{2k+1},2_{1},\ldots,n_{2k+1}\}
. Therefore
\displaystyle\Pi_{u,v}=2
, from which the result follows.
∎

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper we developed a new family of combinatorial invariants of barcodes by mapping barcodes into various equivalence classes of multipermutations. We then studied the poset structure of these equivalence classes. We showed that the rank of one such poset has an elegant interpretation in terms of a crossing number for barcodes. Moreover, we show that these posets in fact lattices, specifically the face lattices of interval polytopes in the classic permutahedron.
Finally, we showed that these multipermutations can provide bounds on the bottleneck and Wasserstein distances for a large class for barcodes. In this way, they are discrete signatures that reflect continuous information.

In
[
11
]
, the authors developed their own combinatorial invariant, which are permutations in
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and study the number of combinatorial trees whose barcodes can produce such an invariant. It would be interesting to study similar “inverse counting problems” for our invariants. At this time it not clear which types of filtrations or simplicial complexes give rise to barcodes that will produce nice inverse counting formulae.

The barcodes lattices are also of interest purely from the perspective of enumerative combinatorics. For instance, at this time we do not have a description of their rank generating functions, Möbius functions, number of maximal chains, or other classical results on posets.
