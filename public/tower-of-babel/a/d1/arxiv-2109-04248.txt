Improving quantum linear system solvers via a gradient descent perspective
Sander Gribling, Iordanis Kerenidis, Dániel Szilágyi

Abstract
Solving systems of linear equations is one of the most important primitives in quantum computing that has the potential to provide a practical quantum advantage in many different areas, including in optimization, simulation, and machine learning. In this work, we revisit quantum linear system solvers from the perspective of convex optimization, and in particular gradient descent-type algorithms. This leads to a considerable constant-factor improvement in the runtime (or, conversely, a several orders of magnitude smaller error with the same runtime/circuit depth). More precisely, we first show how the asymptotically optimal quantum linear system solver of Childs, Kothari, and Somma is related to the gradient descent algorithm on the convex function ‖ 𝑨 ​ 𝐱 − 𝐛 ‖ 2 2 \|\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}^{2} : their linear system solver is based on a truncation in the Chebyshev basis of the degree- ( t − 1 ) (t-1) polynomial (in 𝑨 \bm{A} ) that maps the initial solution 𝐱 1 : = 𝐛 \bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bm{\mathbf{b}} to the t t -th iterate 𝐱 t \bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t} in the basic gradient descent algorithm. Then, instead of starting from the basic gradient descent algorithm, we use the optimal Chebyshev iteration method (which can be viewed as an accelerated gradient descent algorithm) and show that this leads to considerable improvements in the quantum solver.

In this work, we revisit quantum linear system solvers by conceptually linking the previous and our techniques to classical gradient descent methods, and by providing the optimal quantum circuits within this framework. In particular, we point out a connection between classical iterative methods for solving

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Polynomials and approximations

Problem definition.

We consider linear systems that are defined by a Hermitian
n
-by-
n
matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and a unit vector
\bm{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We use
\kappa
to denote the condition number of
\bm{A}
, that is, we assume that all non-zero eigenvalues of
\bm{A}
lie in the set
D_{\kappa}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=[-1,-1/\kappa]\cup[1/\kappa,1]
. Our goal is to
approximately
solve the linear system

\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{\mathbf{b}}.

One can consider different notions of approximate solutions. Two natural ones are the following:

1)

return
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}
such that
\|\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}-\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|\leq\varepsilon
.

2)

return
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}
such that
\|\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|\leq\varepsilon
.

Up to a change in
\varepsilon
, the two notions are equivalent. Indeed, we have the chain of inequalities

(1)

\|\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|\leq\|\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|\leq\kappa\|\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|.

We will focus on algorithms that achieve a polylogarithmic dependence in
\varepsilon
. In
Section

3.2
we construct the optimal degree-
t
polynomial for approximation in the second notion, see
Definition

2
. Prior work
[
7
,
5
,
11
]
focused on the first notion of approximation, which is equivalent up to polylogarithm factors in the complexity. In
Section

3.4
we show (numerically) that our polynomials also improve over prior work with respect to approximation in the first notion.

Polynomials.

Given a polynomial
p(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}x^{t}
with coefficients
c_{t}\in\mathbb{C}
, and a Hermitian matrix
\bm{A}
, we define
p(\bm{A})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}\bm{A}^{t}
. If we let
\bm{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{*}
be the eigendecomposition of
\bm{A}
, then
p(\bm{A})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p(\lambda_{i})\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{*}
.

Chebyshev decomposition.

It is also useful to consider the
Chebyshev decomposition
of
p(x)
, i.e., the decomposition

p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{t}c_{i}\mathcal{T}_{i}(x)

in the basis
\{\mathcal{T}_{0}(x),\mathcal{T}_{1}(x),\dots,\mathcal{T}_{t}(x)\}
, for some vector
\bm{\mathbf{c}}=(c_{i})_{i\in\{0,\ldots,t\}}
of coefficients. One can give an analytic expression for the coefficients
c_{i}
using the fact that the Chebyshev polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the
Chebyshev measure
which is defined in terms of the Lebesgue measure as
\dif\mu(x)=(1-x^{2})^{-1/2}\,\dif x
. In other words,
c_{i}=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{p(x)\mathcal{T}_{i}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\dif x
. Note that in practice this integral is rarely computed explicitly, as there exist efficient interpolation-based methods for computing the coefficient-vector
\bm{\mathbf{c}}

[
10
]
.

Approximating the inverse.

We focus on methods to obtain a vector
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}
that approximates
\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{b}}
that are based on polynomials that approximate the inverse function
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{-1}
on the domain
[1/\kappa,1]
(in the case of positive definite matrices) or
D_{\kappa}
(in the general case). For example, let
\bm{A}
be a Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues in
[1/\kappa,1]
and let
p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial such that
|p(\lambda)-\lambda^{-1}|\leq\varepsilon
for
\lambda\in[1/\kappa,1]
. Then
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=p(\bm{A})\bm{\mathbf{b}}
satisfies

\|\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{x}}}-\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|=\|\sum_{i}(p(\lambda_{i})-\lambda_{i}^{-1})\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{*}\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|\leq\|\sum_{i}(p(\lambda_{i})-\lambda_{i}^{-1})\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{*}\|\|\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|\leq\varepsilon\|\bm{\mathbf{b}}\|.

2.2
Quantum preliminaries

There exist different input models that one might consider when solving the linear system problem.
In the standard case of a dense matrix

\mathcal{O}_{\text{nz}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ket{j,\ell}\mapsto\ket{j,\nu(j,\ell)}\text{ and }\mathcal{O}_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ket{j,k,z}\mapsto\ket{j,k,z\oplus A_{jk}},

[

,
5

Recent approaches for solving the QLS problem are based on applying a block-encoding of
p(\bm{A})
to
\ket{\bm{\mathbf{b}}}
. In the next two sections we describe two ways of computing a block-encoding of

2.2.1
QSVT approach

The most straightforward way for evaluating a polynomial quantumly is through the quantum singular value transformation framework
[
11
]
. Using QSVT, one can directly evaluate any polynomial
p
as long as its sup-norm is suitably bounded. Here the sup-norm of
p
is defined as

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}_{\infty}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|p(x)|.

This is achieved by performing a series of rotations by angles
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{t})
on a single qubit, that induces a degree-
t
polynomial transformation of the singular values of
\bm{A}
. Determining these angles efficiently in a numerically stable way is the subject of ongoing research
[
12
,
6
,
8
]
.
Below, we state a version of QSVT suitable for evaluating even and odd polynomials, since this is the case we are most interested in.

Theorem 3

(
[
11
]
*Corollary 18, for block-encodings)
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be Hermitian, and let
U_{\bm{A}}
be a 1-block-encoding of
\bm{A}
.
Let
\Pi=(\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0})^{\otimes a}\otimes I
, and suppose that
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
is a degree-
t
polynomial of parity-
(t\bmod 2)
satisfying
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq 1
. Then there exists a
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
such that

p(\bm{A})=\begin{cases}\left(\bra{+}\otimes\Pi\right)\left(\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}\otimes U_{\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}}+\ket{1}\!\!\bra{1}\otimes U_{-\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}}\right)\left(\ket{+}\otimes\Pi\right)&\text{ if }t\text{ is odd, and}\\
\left(\bra{+}\otimes\Pi\right)\left(\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}\otimes U_{\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}}+\ket{1}\!\!\bra{1}\otimes U_{-\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}}\right)\left(\ket{+}\otimes\Pi\right)&\text{ if }t\text{ is even,}\end{cases}

where
U_{\Phi}
is defined as the phased alternating sequence

U_{\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}e^{\mathbf{i}\phi_{1}(2\Pi-I)}U_{\bm{A}}\prod_{j=1}^{(t-1)/2}\left(e^{\mathbf{i}\phi_{2j}(2\Pi-I)}U_{\bm{A}}^{*}e^{\mathbf{i}\phi_{2j+1}(2\Pi-I)}U_{\bm{A}}\right)&\text{ if }n\text{ is odd, and}\\
\prod_{j=1}^{t/2}\left(e^{\mathbf{i}\phi_{2j-1}(2\Pi-I)}U_{\bm{A}}^{*}e^{\mathbf{i}\phi_{2j}(2\Pi-I)}U_{\bm{A}}\right)&\text{ if }n\text{ is even.}\end{cases}

Note that QSVT is fundamentally limited to evaluating polynomials that are bounded by
1
in absolute value on
[-1,1]
(since the output is a unitary matrix). Approximations
p
of

on
D_{\kappa}
are inherently not bounded by
1

[-1,1]
: they are around
\kappa

on
\bm{A}
where

is an upper bound on
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}_{\infty}
. This subnormalization reduces the success probability of for example a QSVT-based QLS-solver. It is thus important to obtain polynomial approximations

that moreover permit a good bound
M
.

2.2.2
LCU approach

An alternative approach is based on the Linear Combinations of Unitaries (LCU) lemma
[
4
]
. It uses the fact that Chebyshev polynomials have a particularly nice vector of angles, which permits an efficient implementation of the LCU circuit.

Lemma 4

(
[
11

)
.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
be such that
\phi_{1}=(1-t)\frac{\pi}{2}
and
\phi_{i}=\frac{\pi}{2}
for
2\leq i\leq t
. For this choice of
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
, the polynomial
p
from
3

Computing a single Chebyshev polynomial.

We consider in more detail the above circuit for computing
\mathcal{T}_{2t+1}(\bm{A})
for a matrix
\bm{A}
with a 1-block-encoding

\Pi=\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}\otimes I

3

U_{2t+1}=e^{-\pi\mathbf{i}t(2\Pi-I)}U_{\bm{A}}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\left(e^{\mathbf{i}\frac{\pi}{2}(2\Pi-I)}U_{\bm{A}}^{*}e^{\mathbf{i}\frac{\pi}{2}(2\Pi-I)}U_{\bm{A}}\right)

We first simplify the above. Note that

e^{-\pi\mathbf{i}t(2\Pi-I)}=(-1)^{t}I

\displaystyle U_{2t+1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{t}U_{\bm{A}}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\left(\mathbf{i}(2\Pi-I)U_{\bm{A}}^{*}\mathbf{i}(2\Pi-I)U_{\bm{A}}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{t}(\mathbf{i})^{2t}U_{\bm{A}}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\left((2\Pi-I)U_{\bm{A}}^{*}(2\Pi-I)U_{\bm{A}}\right)

\displaystyle=U_{\bm{A}}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\Big(\underbrace{(2\Pi-I)U_{\bm{A}}^{*}(2\Pi-I)U_{\bm{A}}}_{=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}W}\Big)=U_{\bm{A}}W^{t}.

In other words,

Computing a linear combination of Chebyshev polynomials.

Given the above circuit that computes block-encodings of

O(t\polylog(nt\kappa/\varepsilon))
.

Compared to the QSVT approach, for this circuit we only need to compute the Chebyshev coefficients
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
, as opposed to the vector of angles
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
– this comes, however, at the cost of using
O(\log t)
additional qubits. Moreover, the coefficient 1-norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{c}}\right\rVert}_{1}
represents an upper bound for
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}_{\infty}
, since

(3)

|p(x)|=\left|\sum_{i=0}^{t}c_{i}\mathcal{T}_{i}(x)\right|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t}|c_{i}|\cdot|\mathcal{T}_{i}(x)|\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{c}}\right\rVert}_{1},\;\text{ for }|x|\leq 1.

A natural question is how tight this bound is for general degree-
t
polynomials
p
with
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq 1
. By norm conversion (
Eq.

15
in particular), the ratio
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{c}}\right\rVert}_{1}/\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}_{\infty}
is provably upper bounded by
O(\sqrt{t})
but in
Appendix

A
we observe that for many “interesting” functions the ratio
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{c}}\right\rVert}_{1}/\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}_{\infty}
is in fact only
O(\log(t))
.
A notable exception is the complex exponential
e^{\mathbf{i}\kappa x}
(and thus
\sin(\kappa x)
and
\cos(\kappa x)
) for which numerical experiments suggest that it attains the
O(\sqrt{t})
upper bound.

3
Convex optimization perspective

In this section we introduce the convex optimization approach to linear system solving, and reinterpret the CKS polynomial in this framework. Let us first assume that
\bm{A}
is positive definite (PD). We start by defining the convex function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

as

f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}}{2}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{x}}.

Note that
\nabla f(\bm{\mathbf{x}})=\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}
, so the minimizer of
f
satisfies
\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{\mathbf{b}}
. This observation forms the basis of the convex optimization approach to linear system solving. We refer the reader to, for example,
[
21
,
25
]
for an overview of gradient descent type algorithms for solving linear systems.

3.1
Gradient descent

One of the most well-known algorithms for minimizing a convex function
f
is the family of gradient descent algorithms. Starting from an initial point
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
(we use 1-based indexing on purpose), such an algorithm performs the iterations

(4)

\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}),\qquad\text{where }t=2,3,\ldots

where
\eta_{t}\in[0,\infty)
is the ‘step size’ in the
t
-th iteration. For the most basic version of gradient descent we take a constant step size, i.e.,
\eta_{t}
is independent of
t
.

For our quadratic function
f
we can unpack this recurrence. As we have seen before
\nabla f(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1})=\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}
and hence

\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=(I-\eta_{t}\bm{A})\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}+\eta_{t}\bm{\mathbf{b}}.

If we set
\eta_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and use the initial point
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1}=\bm{\mathbf{b}}
(or even
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{0}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
, if we allow empty sums), then we obtain

\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(I-\bm{A})^{k}\bm{\mathbf{b}}.

Let us define the polynomial
p_{t}^{+}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(1-\lambda)^{k}
so that
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=p_{t}^{+}(\bm{A})\bm{\mathbf{b}}
. Observe that this is the degree-
(t-1)
Taylor expansion of the function
1/\lambda
around
1
.

Lemma 6
.

We have
|p_{t}^{+}(\lambda)-1/\lambda|\leq\varepsilon
for all
\lambda\in[1/\kappa,1]
whenever
t\geq\kappa\log(\kappa/\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

Indeed, substituting
\delta=1-\lambda
we have
(1-\delta)\cdot p_{t}^{+}(1-\delta)=(1-\delta)\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\delta^{k}=1-\delta^{t}
which shows that

p_{t}^{+}(\lambda)=\frac{1-(1-\lambda)^{t}}{\lambda}.

Therefore, for
\lambda\in[1/\kappa,1]
and
t\geq\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon)
we have

|p_{t}^{+}(\lambda)-1/\lambda|=|1/\lambda|\cdot|1-\lambda|^{t}\leq\kappa(1-1/\kappa)^{t}\leq\kappa e^{-\log(\kappa/\varepsilon)}=\varepsilon.\qed

3.2
Chebyshev iteration

In the previous section we saw that
t
-step iterative methods are roughly equivalent to degree-
(t-1)
polynomials that approximate the function
1/x
on
[1/\kappa,1]
. Thus, the natural question to ask is what is the best such polynomial
q_{t}^{+}
? Here we use the notion of optimality that comes from
Definition

2
. In other words, what is the degree-
(t-1)
polynomial
q_{t}^{+}
that minimizes

(5)

\max_{x\in[1/\kappa,1]}|xq_{t}^{+}(x)-1|.

First observe that all such polynomials can be expressed in the form
q_{t}^{+}(x)=\frac{1-r_{t}^{+}(x)}{x}
where
r_{t}^{+}
is a degree-
t
polynomial that satisfies
r_{t}^{+}(0)=1
.
5
5

5

For example, in the case of gradient descent we have
r_{t}^{+}(x)=(1-x)^{t}
.
Thus, our goal is to find a degree-
t
polynomial
r_{t}^{+}(x)
that has the smallest absolute value on the interval
[1/\kappa,1]
and satisfies the normalization constraint
r_{t}^{+}(0)=1
. It turns out that we can use extremal properties of the Chebyshev polynomials
\mathcal{T}_{t}(x)
to determine an optimal
r_{t}^{+}(x)
. We use the following well-known result (cf.
[
23
, Prop. 2.4]
).

Lemma 7
.

For any degree-
t
polynomial
p(x)
such that
|p(x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in[-1,1]
, and any
y
such that
|y|>1
, we have
|p(y)|\leq|\mathcal{T}_{t}(y)|
.

Using the affine transformation
x\mapsto\frac{1+1/\kappa-2x}{1-1/\kappa}
this gives the following corollary:

Corollary 8
.

Let
\kappa>1
be real, and let
t>0
be an integer. Then, the polynomial

r_{t}^{+}(x)=\left.\mathcal{T}_{t}\left(\frac{1+1/\kappa-2x}{1-1/\kappa}\right)\middle/\mathcal{T}_{t}\left(\frac{1+1/\kappa}{1-1/\kappa}\right)\right.

is a degree-
t
polynomial that satisfies
r_{t}^{+}(0)=1
, and minimizes the quantity
\max_{x\in[1,1/\kappa]}|r_{t}^{+}(x)|
.

Note that the polynomials
r_{t}^{+}
satisfy a Chebyshev-like
3
-term recurrence. As a consequence, the polynomials
q_{t}^{+}(x)=(1-r_{t}^{+}(x))/x
also satisfy such a recurrence. The corresponding iterative method is known as the Chebyshev iteration.

Remark 9

(Chebyshev iteration)
.

The polynomial
q_{t}^{+}(x)
satisfies the recurrence

(6)

q_{t+1}^{+}(x)=2\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}(\gamma)}{\mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\gamma)}\frac{\kappa+1-2\kappa x}{\kappa-1}q_{t}^{+}(x)-\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t-1}(\gamma)}{\mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\gamma)}q_{t-1}^{+}(x)-\frac{4\kappa}{\kappa-1}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}(\gamma)}{\mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\gamma)},

where
\gamma=\frac{1+1/\kappa}{1-1/\kappa}
.
This recurrence corresponds to the iterative method
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1}=\bm{\mathbf{b}}
and

\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}=2\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}(\gamma)}{\mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\gamma)}\frac{(\kappa+1)I-2\kappa\bm{A}}{\kappa-1}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}-\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t-1}(\gamma)}{\mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\gamma)}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}-\frac{4\kappa}{\kappa-1}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}(\gamma)}{\mathcal{T}_{t+1}(\gamma)}\bm{\mathbf{b}}.

The convergence rate of this method is summarized by the following theorem:

Theorem 10
.

Let
\kappa>1
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then, for
t\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa}\log(2\kappa/\varepsilon)
we have

\left\lvert q_{t}^{+}(x)-1/x\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon\text{ for all }x\in[1/\kappa,1].

Proof.

First, we define
s(x)=\frac{1+1/\kappa-2x}{1-1/\kappa}
, so that we have
r_{t}^{+}(x)=\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x))/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))
. Thus, for all
x\in[1/\kappa,1]
, we have

|q_{t}^{+}(x)-1/x|=|r_{t}^{+}(x)/x|\leq\kappa|r_{t}^{+}(x)|=\kappa\left\lvert\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x))/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))\right\rvert.

Additionally, since
|s(x)|\leq 1
on this interval, we also have
|\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x))|\leq 1
. Thus, it suffices to find
t
for which
\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))=\mathcal{T}_{t}(1+\frac{2}{\kappa-1})\geq\frac{\kappa}{\varepsilon}
. Since the Chebyshev polynomial
\mathcal{T}_{t}(\cdot)
can be computed as

(7)

\mathcal{T}_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(x-\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\right)^{t}+\left(x+\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\right)^{t}\right)\text{ for }|x|\geq 1,

we can conclude that
\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^{t}+\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}\right)^{t}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}\right)^{t}
. Using the inequality
(1+\frac{x}{n})^{n+x/2}\geq e^{x}
for
x,n\geq 0
, after substituting
t=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa}\log(2\kappa/\varepsilon)
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}\right)^{t}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}\right)^{(\sqrt{\kappa}-1+2/2)\frac{\log(2\kappa/\varepsilon)}{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp(\log(2\kappa/\varepsilon))=\frac{\kappa}{\varepsilon}.\qed

3.3
The general case

We now return to the setting where
\bm{A}
is a Hermitian matrix and has eigenvalues in the domain
D_{\kappa}=[-1,-1/\kappa]\cup[1/\kappa,1]
. One can still solve such systems using gradient descent methods by reducing to the convex case. That is, by considering the equivalent linear system
\bm{A}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{b}}
and the corresponding convex function
f(\bm{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
. In particular, this allows us to solve
\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{\mathbf{b}}
by using a method for solving PD systems applied to the system
\bm{A}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{b}}
.

Corollary 11
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
\kappa>1
, and let
P_{t}
be any degree-
(t-1)
polynomial such that
|P_{t}(\lambda)-1/\lambda|\leq\varepsilon
for all
\lambda\in[1/\kappa^{2},1]
. Then,
|P_{t}(\mu^{2})\mu-1/\mu|\leq\varepsilon
for all
\mu\in D_{\kappa}
.

Proof.

Since
|\mu|\leq 1
on
D_{\kappa}
, we have

|P_{t}(\mu^{2})\mu-1/\mu|=|\mu|\cdot|P_{t}(\mu^{2})-1/\mu^{2}|\leq\varepsilon
.
∎

We define the following two polynomials as the respective analogs of
p_{t}^{+}
and
q_{t}^{+}
for
D_{\kappa}
:

(8)

\displaystyle p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=xp_{t}^{+}(x^{2})=\frac{1-(1-x^{2})^{t}}{x},\text{ and}

(9)

\displaystyle q_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=xq_{t}^{+}(x^{2})=\frac{1-\mathcal{T}_{t}(\frac{1+1/\kappa^{2}-2x^{2}}{1-1/\kappa^{2}})/\mathcal{T}_{t}(\frac{1+\kappa^{2}}{1-\kappa^{2}})}{x}.

Both
p_{t}
and
q_{t}
are degree-
(2t-1)
polynomials, but different values of
t
are required in order to achieve an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
1/x
on
D_{\kappa}
. In particular, the following degrees are required:

Corollary 12
.

Let
\kappa>1
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then,

1.

|p_{t}(x)-1/x|\leq\varepsilon
for all
x\in D_{\kappa}
whenever
t\geq\kappa^{2}\log(\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon)
,

2.

|q_{t}(x)-1/x|\leq\varepsilon
for all
x\in D_{\kappa}
whenever
t\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa\log(2\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon)
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\kappa>1
. The polynomial
q_{t}
is a degree-
(2t-1)
polynomial that minimizes the quantity
\max_{x\in D_{\kappa}}|xP(x)-1|
among all degree-
(2t-1)
polynomials
P\in\mathbb{R}[x]
.

Proof.

For a given
t
, we define

\varepsilon^{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{+}\in\mathbb{R}[y]\\
\operatorname{deg}P^{+}=t-1\end{subarray}}\max_{y\in[1/\kappa^{2},1]}|yP^{+}(y)-1|,\qquad\varepsilon\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathbb{R}[x]\\
\operatorname{deg}P=2t-1\end{subarray}}\max_{x\in D_{\kappa}}|xP(x)-1|.

We first show that
q_{t}
certifies that
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{+}
, and then we show
\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{+}
. From
Section

3.2
, we know that
\varepsilon^{+}
is achieved by the degree-
t-1
polynomial
q_{t}^{+}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1-\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x))/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))}{x}
, where
s(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1+1/\kappa^{2}-2x}{1-1/\kappa^{2}}
. Then, for
q_{t}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1-\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x^{2}))/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))}{x}
we have

\max_{x\in D_{\kappa}}|xq_{t}(x)-1|=\max_{x\in D_{\kappa}}|x^{2}q_{t}^{+}(x^{2})-1|=\max_{y\in[1/\kappa^{2},1]}|yq_{t}^{+}(y)-1|=\varepsilon^{+},

where in the first equality we use
Eq.

9
. We now show that
\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{+}
. Let
P(x)
be a degree-
(2t-1)
polynomial that satisfies
\max_{x\in D_{\kappa}}|xP(x)-1|=\varepsilon
. We first show that
P
is odd. To do this, decompose
P
as
P(x)=P_{\mathrm{even}}(x)+P_{\mathrm{odd}}(x)
where
P_{\mathrm{even}}
is even and
P_{\mathrm{odd}}
is odd. Then

\displaystyle\max_{x\in D_{\kappa}}|xP(x)-1|

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in[1/\kappa,1]}\max\{|xP(x)-1|,|-xP(-x)-1|\}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in[1/\kappa,1]}\max\{|xP_{\mathrm{odd}}(x)+xP_{\mathrm{even}}(x)-1|,|xP_{\mathrm{odd}}(x)-xP_{\mathrm{even}}(x)-1|\}

\displaystyle\geq\max_{x\in[1/\kappa,1]}|xP_{\mathrm{odd}}(x)-1|=\max_{x\in D_{\kappa}}|xP_{\mathrm{odd}}(x)-1|.

Hence replacing
P
by
P_{\mathrm{odd}}
decreases
\varepsilon
, so we may assume that
P(x)
is odd. Then
P(x)/x
is a degree-
(2t-2)
even polynomial. Let
P^{+}(y)
be the degree-
(t-1)
polynomial for which
P(x)/x=P^{+}(x^{2})
. Then we have

\max_{y\in[1/\kappa^{2},1]}|yP^{+}(y)-1|=\max_{x\in[1/\kappa,1]}|x^{2}P^{+}(x^{2})-1|=\max_{x\in D_{\kappa}}|xP(x)-1|=\varepsilon

This shows that
\varepsilon^{+}\leq\varepsilon
which concludes the proof:
q_{t}
is the degree-
(2t-1)
polynomial that minimizes
\max_{x\in D_{\kappa}}|xP(x)-1|
over polynomials of degree
2t-1
.
∎

3.4
Relation to the Chebyshev approach of Childs-Kothari-Somma

In
[
7
]
, Childs, Kothari, and Somma approached the quantum linear system solver-problem by approximating the function
1/x
on the domain
D_{\kappa}
by (low-degree) polynomials. To start, they approximate
1/x
by a function that is bounded near the origin: they multiply
1/x
by a function that is small at the origin and close to 1 on
D_{\kappa}
. A natural choice for such a function is
1-(1-x^{2})^{t}
, so the function they end up with turns out to be exactly
p_{t}(x)
, the polynomial corresponding to
t
steps of gradient descent applied to the quadratic
\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}^{2}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
! So indeed, this is a good approximation of
1/x
whenever
t\geq\kappa^{2}\log(\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon)
.

The polynomial
p_{t}
can be written in the Chebyshev basis as follows:

(10)

p_{t}(x)=4\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}(-1)^{j}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=j+1}^{t}\binom{2t}{t+i}}{2^{2t}}\right)\mathcal{T}_{2j+1}(x).

The key insight of
[
7
]
is that this expansion can be truncated at
\tilde{O}(\kappa)
terms, since the Chebyshev coefficients decay exponentially. This can be shown by relating the absolute value of the
j
-th coefficient (for
j=0,1,\dots
) to the probability of more than
t+j
heads appearing in
2t
tosses of a fair coin. This probability decreases as
e^{-j^{2}/t}
which can be seen by applying the Chernoff bound. Thus, starting from
p_{t}
, an
\varepsilon
-approximation of the inverse, we obtain an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
p_{t}
by truncating the summation at
j=\sqrt{t\log(4t/\varepsilon)}=\tilde{O}(\kappa)
– so, for these parameters, the CKS polynomial is a
2\varepsilon
-approximation of the inverse on
D_{\kappa}
.

Although this truncated polynomial is asymptotically optimal, it is not an optimum of (
5
). Hence, the Chebyshev iteration polynomial provides a better approximation for a fixed degree, or conversely requires a lower degree to reach the same error on
D_{\kappa}
. In

4
A quantum algorithm

As mentioned before, our algorithm can be described as applying the polynomial
q_{t}
to a 1-block-encoding of the input matrix
\bm{A}
. This yields an
O(t)
-block-encoding of
q_{t}(\bm{A})
, which can then be applied to the input state
\ket{\bm{\mathbf{b}}}
. Formally, we show the following.

Theorem 14

(Main result)
.

Let
\bm{A}
be a Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues in
D_{\kappa}
, let
U_{\bm{A}}
be a 1-block-encoding of
\bm{A}
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then, for
t\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa\log(2\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon)
, a
2(1+\varepsilon/\kappa^{2})t
-block-encoding of
q_{t}(\bm{A})
can be constructed using
2t-1
calls to
U_{\bm{A}}
and
U_{\bm{A}}^{*}
.

Proof.

The algorithm consists of applying QSVT (
3
) to the polynomial
q_{t}(x)/\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{t}\right\rVert}_{\infty}
. This allows us to construct a
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{t}\right\rVert}_{\infty}
-block-encoding of
q_{t}(\bm{A})
with the desired complexity. It remains to upper bound
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q_{t}\right\rVert}_{\infty}
by
2(1+\varepsilon/\kappa^{2})t
. Motivated by
Eq.

3
, it suffices to upper bound the
1
-norm of the vector
\bm{\mathbf{c}}
of coefficients of
q_{t}
in the Chebyshev basis (again by
2(1+\varepsilon/\kappa^{2})t
). In
16
we show that
\|\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\|_{1}\leq 2(1+\varepsilon/\kappa^{2})t
.
∎

The block-encoding of
q_{t}(\bm{A})
can now be used as a black-box replacement for the block-encoding of the corresponding CKS polynomial evaluated at
\bm{A}
. For example, using variable-time amplitude amplification, an
\widetilde{O}(\kappa)
-query (to
U_{\bm{A}}
) complexity QLS algorithm can be derived. We refer the reader to
[
7
,
11
,
19
]
for an overview of these techniques.

As an alternative approach, one could use the fact that
\|\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\|_{1}
is bounded in order to evaluate
q_{t}
via LCU (
5
). At the cost of using
O(\log t)
additional qubits, an LCU-based approach would yield a more “natural” quantum algorithm, that does away with the classical angle computation preprocessing step required by QSVT – computing these angles efficiently in a numerically stable way is the subject of ongoing research
[
6
,
8
,
12
]
. Moreover, in
Appendix

A
we consider some other commonly-used functions, and bound their coefficient norms using similar techniques. For these functions, the coefficient norm is only a logarithmic factor away from the maximum absolute value on the interval
[-1,1]
, meaning that they can be approximately evaluated with LCU in addition to QSVT, with slightly deeper circuits (multiplicative logarithmic overhead) and slightly more qubits (additive logarithmic overhead).

4.1
Bounding the Chebyshev coefficients

As discussed above, in order to apply (a normalized version of)
q_{t}
to a block-encoding of a Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues in
D_{\kappa}
, we need a bound on the sup-norm of
q_{t}
on the interval
[-1,1]
. In order to derive such a bound, we express
q_{t}
in the basis of Chebyshev polynomials. Each of the Chebyshev polynomials has sup-norm equal to
1
and therefore a bound on the
1
-norm of the coefficient vector provides a bound on the sup-norm of
q_{t}
. Recall that since
q_{t}
is an odd polynomial, its expansion in the Chebyshev basis only involves the odd-degree Chebyshev polynomials. That is, we can write

(11)

q_{t}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}c_{t,i}\mathcal{T}_{2i+1}(x)

for some vector
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}=(c_{t,i})_{i\in\{0,\ldots,t-1\}}
of coefficients. One can give an analytic expression for
c_{t,i}
using the fact that the Chebyshev polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the
Chebyshev measure
.
Here we take a different approach and use the following discrete orthogonality relations. Fix a degree
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
be the roots of
\mathcal{T}_{m}(x)
. The
x_{k}
’s are called the
Chebyshev nodes
and they admit an analytic formula:

(12)

x_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{(k-\tfrac{1}{2})\pi}{m}\right)\qquad\text{for }k=1,\ldots,m

The discrete orthogonality relation that we will use is the following. For
0\leq i,j<m
, we have

(13)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathcal{T}_{i}(x_{k})\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{k})=\begin{cases}m&\text{ if }i=j=0,\\
\frac{m}{2}&\text{ if }i=j<m,\\
0&\text{ if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

Since
q_{t}
is a polynomial of degree
2t-1
, we will use the discrete orthogonality conditions corresponding to
m=2t
to recover the coefficient of
\mathcal{T}_{2i+1}
in
q_{t}
. We have

(14)

c_{t,i}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{2t}q_{t}(x_{k})\mathcal{T}_{2i+1}(x_{k})

for all
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,t-1\}
. We can equivalently write this in matrix form,
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}
, where

\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{T}_{1}(x_{1})&\mathcal{T}_{1}(x_{2})&\dots&\mathcal{T}_{1}(x_{2t})\\
\mathcal{T}_{3}(x_{1})&\mathcal{T}_{3}(x_{2})&\dots&\mathcal{T}_{3}(x_{2t})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathcal{T}_{2t-1}(x_{1})&\mathcal{T}_{2t-1}(x_{2})&\dots&\mathcal{T}_{2t-1}(x_{2t})\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}q_{t}(x_{1})\\
q_{t}(x_{2})\\
\vdots\\
q_{t}(x_{2t})\end{bmatrix}.

Our goal is to show that
\|\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\|_{1}\leq C\cdot t
for a small constant
C
. To do so, we first use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain

(15)

\|\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{t}\|\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\|_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\|\bm{\mathbf{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}}_{t}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}\|_{2}=\frac{\|\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\|}{\sqrt{t}}\|\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}\|_{2}=\|\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}\|_{2}

where the second to last equality follows from the discrete orthogonality relations
Eq.

13
: we see that
\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{t}^{*}=tI_{t}
and therefore
\|\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\|=\sqrt{t}
. We are thus left to bound
\|\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}\|_{2}
.

Lemma 15
.

We have
\|\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}\|_{2}\leq 2(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))})t
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, for
t\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa\log(2\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon)
we have
\|\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}\|_{2}\leq 2(1+\varepsilon/\kappa^{2})t
.

Proof.

We start by bounding
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{t}(x)\right\rvert}
on
[-1,1]
, and we recall that

q_{t}(x)=\frac{1-\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x))/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))}{x},\quad\text{where}\quad s(x)=\frac{1+1/\kappa^{2}-2x^{2}}{1-1/\kappa^{2}}.

On one hand, when
x\in D_{\kappa}
we have
s(x)\in[-1,1]
and thus
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert 1-\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x))/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))\right\rvert}\leq 1+1/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))
. On the other hand, when
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\leq 1/\kappa
we have
1\leq s(x)\leq s(0)=\frac{1+1/\kappa^{2}}{1-1/\kappa^{2}}
.
Since
\mathcal{T}_{t}(x)
is increasing for
x\geq 1
, it follows that
0\leq 1-\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x))/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))\leq 1
for all
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\leq 1/\kappa
. Together this shows that

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert q_{t}(x)\right\rvert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\frac{1-\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x))/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))}{x}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1+1/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}}\qquad\text{for all }x\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}.

We now bound the norm of
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2t}q_{t}(x_{k})^{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2t}\frac{1}{x_{k}^{2}}=\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2t}\frac{1}{\cos^{2}\left(\frac{2k-1}{4t}\pi\right)},

where we substituted the exact expression for the Chebyshev nodes
x_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{2k-1}{4t}\pi\right)
. Moreover, we have

\cos^{2}\left(\frac{2(2t-k+1)-1}{4t}\pi\right)=\cos^{2}\left(\frac{2k-1}{4t}\pi\right)=\frac{1-\cos\left(\frac{2k-1}{2t}\pi\right)}{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq k\leq t,

where the first equality comes from
x_{2t-k+1}=-x_{k}
. Therefore, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}\leq 4\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{1-\cos(\frac{2k-1}{2t}\pi)}.

We note that the roots of
\mathcal{T}_{t}(x)
are exactly
\cos(\frac{2k-1}{2t}\pi)
. For any polynomial
P(x)=C\prod_{k=1}^{t}(x-r_{k})
the following identity holds for all
x
for which
P(x)\neq 0
:

\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{x-r_{k}}=\frac{P^{\prime}(x)}{P(x)}.

Applying the above to
P(x)=\mathcal{T}_{t}(x)
and
x=1
(which is not a root of
\mathcal{T}_{t}
), we get

\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{1-\cos(\frac{2k-1}{2t}\pi)}=\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(1)}{\mathcal{T}_{t}(1)}=\frac{t\cdot\mathcal{U}_{t-1}(1)}{1}=t^{2}.

This concludes the main part of the proof: we have shown that
\|\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}\|\leq 2(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))})t
.

Finally, for
t\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa\log(2\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon)
, we bound
1/\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))
as in the proof of
10
. Namely, using the same inequalities, we have

\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\kappa+1}{\kappa-1}\right)^{t}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{2}{\kappa-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\kappa\log(2\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon)}\geq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\varepsilon}.\qed

Combining this lemma with
Eq.

15
, we derive the same bound for
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\right\rVert}_{1}
:

Corollary 16
.

For all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\right\rVert}_{1}\leq 2(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(0))})t
. In particular, for
t\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa\log(2\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon)
we have
\|\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}\|_{1}\leq 2(1+\varepsilon/\kappa^{2})t
.

4.2
Efficiently computing the coefficients

In the case of evaluating
q_{t}
via LCU, one question of practical relevance is how to compute the coefficients
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}
. Naively using the recurrence (
6
) to compute
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}
gives rise to an algorithm with
O(t^{2})
arithmetic operations with real numbers. Alternatively, one can use FFT-based Chebyshev interpolation algorithms that can compute
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}
with
O(t\log t)
operations given the vector
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{t}
of the values of
q_{t}(x)
at the order-
t
Chebyshev nodes
[
10
]
. Thus, in order to get an
O(t\log t)
-operation algorithm for computing
\bm{\mathbf{c}}_{t}
, it suffices to show that
q_{t}(x)
can be evaluated at a single Chebyshev node
x_{k}
with
O(\log t)
-operations. Given the form of
q_{t}
, this means that we need to compute
\mathcal{T}_{t}(s(x_{k}))
with
O(\log t)
operations. One way to do this is via the degree-halving identities

\mathcal{T}_{2t}(x)=2\mathcal{T}_{t}(x)^{2}-1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{T}_{2t+1}(x)=2\mathcal{T}_{t+1}(x)\mathcal{T}_{t}(x)-x.

4.3
A more natural quantum algorithm?

Given the reduction of the general linear system problem to the PD case (
11
), one might be tempted to mirror this reduction when designing a quantum algorithm, with the goal of achieving
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\kappa})
complexity for solving PD systems. The input of such an algorithm would be a (block-encoding of a) Hermitian matrix
\bm{A}
with eigenvalues in
[1/\kappa,1]
, and the output would be a block-encoding of
q_{t}^{+}(\bm{A})
. To evaluate this polynomial using QSVT, we first need to normalize it by dividing it by
\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|q_{t}^{+}(x)|
. It turns out that this maximum grows exponentially with
t
: one can lower bound it by
|q_{t}^{+}(-1)|
and we have

\displaystyle|q_{t}^{+}(-1)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}\left(\frac{1+1/\kappa+2}{1-1/\kappa}\right)}{\mathcal{T}_{t}\left(\frac{1+1/\kappa}{1-1/\kappa}\right)}-1\geq\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}(3+4/(\kappa-1))}{\mathcal{T}_{t}(1+2/(\kappa-1))}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathcal{T}_{t}(3)}{\mathcal{T}_{t}(2)}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3+2\sqrt{2}}{2+\sqrt{3}}\right)^{t}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{t}.

Therefore, amplifying the output of QSVT would take exponential time. In the case of LCU, the coefficient 1-norm is lower bounded by
|q_{t}^{+}(-1)|
(by
Eq.

3
), so the output of a LCU-based algorithm would also need to be amplified exponentially. Alternative approaches of multiplying
q_{t}^{+}(x)
by a rectangle function that is close to
1
on
[1/\kappa,1]
and close to
0
elsewhere are similarly fruitless as the degree of the resulting approximation polynomial would become linear in
\kappa
. It should be noted, however, that these issues can be avoided if we assume that the mapping
x\mapsto\frac{1+1/\kappa-2x}{1-1/\kappa}
has already been performed “ahead of time”: in
[
20
]
, Orsucci and Dunjko have shown that PD matrices can indeed be inverted in
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\kappa})
, provided that a block-encoding of
I-\alpha\bm{A}
is given as input (for suitable
\alpha
).

Another natural alternative approach would be to quantize a method such as momentum gradient descent, which also converges in
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\kappa})
for PD matrices
[
21
]
. One way to achieve this would be using the approach of Kerenidis and Prakash
[
15
]
, who quantized the basic gradient descent algorithm by implementing the recurrence
\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{t+1}=(I-\eta\bm{A})\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{t}
satisfied by the differences
\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}
of successive iterates. Applying this idea to momentum gradient descent, one gets a recurrence involving two successive differences:

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{t+1}\\
\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)I-\eta\bm{A}&-\beta I\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{M}}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{t}\\
\bm{\mathbf{r}}_{t-1}\end{bmatrix},

for suitable choices of
\eta
and
\beta
. For example, following
[
21
, Chapter 3]
, one can set
\eta=4/(1+\sqrt{1/\kappa})^{2}
and
\beta=\left(1-2/(1+\sqrt{\kappa})\right)^{2}
.
Implementing a similar approach as in
[
15
]
would require the construction of
O(1)
-block-encodings of powers of
\bm{M}
. In particular, this would require
\bm{M}
to have a small norm. Unfortunately, for large enough
\kappa\geq 9
and the above choice of
\eta,\beta
, one has
\|\bm{M}\|\geq\sqrt{2}
which means that a block-encoding of
\bm{M}^{t}
needs to have sub-normalization at least
2^{t/2}
.

5
Query lower bounds

So far, we have been considering algorithms (i.e. upper bounds) for the QLS problem. The complexity of the best algorithm for the QLS problem
depends linearly on
\kappa
(we ignore the polylogarithmic factors in this section), so a natural question is whether this dependence is optimal. In
[
13
]
it has been shown that this is indeed the case: in the sparse access input model (the setting in which such lower bounds are usually proven), the complexity of QLS for general systems is
\Omega(\min(\kappa,n))
. Recently, it has been shown
[
20
]
that the same
\Omega(\min(\kappa,n))
lower bound even holds for the restriction of QLS to PD matrices – this is surprising since in the classical setting a
\sqrt{\kappa}
-separation exists between the general and the PD case. We note that both of these lower bounds apply when the output of the QLS solver is the quantum state
\ket{\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{b}}}
. As a consequence, one can show that computing a classical description of
\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{b}}
is just as hard.

Both of the above results apply to the small-
\kappa
regime. In particular, they leave open the possibility of a
o(n^{\omega})
-time quantum algorithm for solving linear systems (with classical output). The existence of such an algorithm would speed up many classical optimization algorithms (e.g., interior point methods) in a black-box way.
In
[
9
]
it was shown that one cannot obtain a large quantum speedup when the output is required to be classical:
\Omega(n^{2})
quantum queries to the entries of
\bm{A}
are needed to obtain a classical description of a single coordinate of
\bm{A}^{-1}e_{n}
, where
e_{n}
is the
n
-th standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The statement is robust in the following sense: after normalizing
\bm{A}^{-1}e_{n}
, it suffices to obtain a
\delta
-additive approximation of the first coordinate for some
\delta=O(1/n^{2})
. We present a simplified proof of this result of
[
9
]
at the end of this section.
Note that this high precision prevents one from lifting the bound to the quantum-output setting: to obtain a
\delta
-additive approximation of a single coordinate of
\ket{\bm{A}^{-1}b}
one can use roughly
1/\delta
rounds of amplitude estimation on a QLS-solver
\mathcal{A}
. With
\delta=O(1/n^{2})
this only implies that
n^{2}\cdot\text{cost}(\mathcal{A})=\Omega(n^{2})
. A second type of quantum lower bound is described in
[
11
]
: roughly speaking, if a (smooth) function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I\to[-1,1]
has a derivative whose absolute value is
d
, then
\Omega(d)
uses of a
1
-block-encoding
U_{\bm{A}}
of
\bm{A}
are needed to create a block-encoding of
f(\bm{A})
. Here
I
is a subset of
[-1,1]
that contains the eigenvalues of the Hermitian matrix
\bm{A}
. Applied to
f(x)=1/(\kappa x)
, this shows that indeed
\Omega(\kappa)
applications of
U_{\bm{A}}
are needed to create a block-encoding of
\bm{A}^{-1}
. As mentioned before, a block-encoding of
\bm{A}^{-1}
can be combined with a state preparation oracle for
\bm{\mathbf{b}}
to solve the QLS problem. Such a strategy however naturally incurs a
\kappa
-dependence in the runtime, and it remains an interesting open question whether one could solve the QLS problem (with quantum output!) without such a dependence in
\kappa
and in time
o(n^{\omega})
.

5.1
Lower bound for matrix inversion with classical output

We present a simplified proof of a matrix-inversion lower bound result of
[
9
]
. It is based on the quantum query complexity of the majority function
\mathrm{MAJ}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
which is takes value
1
on input
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
if and only if
\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}>n/2
. It is well known that the quantum query complexity of
\mathrm{MAJ}_{n}
is
\Theta(n)

[
3
]
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\bm{X}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
. Then, the matrix
\bm{A}\in\{0,1\}^{(2n+2)\times(2n+2)}
defined as

\bm{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}}\begin{matrix}0&1_{n}^{*}\\
1_{n}&0\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}0&0\\
\bm{X}&0\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}0&\bm{X}^{*}\\
0&0\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}0&1_{n}\\
1_{n}^{*}&0\end{matrix}\end{array}\right]

satisfies
(\bm{A}^{3})_{1,2n+2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{i,j}
.

Proof.

\bm{A}
is the adjacency matrix of an undirected graph that can be described as follows. We start with a bipartite graph between two sets of
n
vertices whose edge set is described by
X
, then we add two vertices labeled
1
and
2n+2
that we connect respectively to the first set of vertices and the second set of vertices.
The entry
(1,2n+2)
of
\bm{A}^{3}
counts the number of paths of length
3
from
1
to
2n+2
in this graph. This equals the number of edges between the sets
\{2,\dots,n+1\}
and
\{n+2,\dots,2n+1\}
, that is,
(\bm{A}^{3})_{1,2n+2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{i,j}
.
∎

Corollary 18
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
. Determining a single off-diagonal entry of
\bm{A}^{3}
, with success probability
\geq 2/3
, takes
\Theta(n^{2})
quantum queries to
\bm{A}
.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
. Then, for
N=4n
, the matrix
\bm{B}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
defined by

\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}I&\bm{A}&&\\
&I&\bm{A}&\\
&&I&\bm{A}\\
&&&I\end{bmatrix},

satisfies
(\bm{B}^{-1})_{1,N}=-(\bm{A}^{3})_{1,n}
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify that the inverse of
\bm{B}
is

\bm{B}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}I&-\bm{A}&\bm{A}^{2}&-\bm{A}^{3}\\
&I&-\bm{A}&\bm{A}^{2}\\
&&I&-\bm{A}\\
&&&I\\
\end{bmatrix}.\qed

If
\bm{A}
is the adjacency matrix of the directed version of the graph described in Lemma
17
, we can also compute the norm of the last column as follows:

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert B^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{4n}\right\rVert}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i,j}X_{i,j}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{j}X_{i,j}\right)^{2}+n+1.

In particular, for the hard instances (where
|n/2-\sum_{i,j}X_{i,j}|\leq 1
), we have that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{4n}\right\rVert}=\Theta(n^{2})
.

Corollary 20
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
. Determining a single off-diagonal entry of
\bm{A}^{-1}
up to precision
<1/2
, with success probability
\geq 2/3
, takes
\Theta(n^{2})
quantum queries to
\bm{A}
.

Appendix A
Examples of functions with bounded Chebyshev coefficient norms

The inverse function is not the only function that can be efficiently evaluated using LCU of Chebyshev polynomials. Here we discuss several families of functions for which the 1-norm of the Chebyshev coefficients is of the order
\log(\text{degree})
.

A.1
Simple examples

We first observe that the monomial
x^{n}
has the following Chebyshev expansion:

x^{n}=2^{1-n}\mathop{{\sum}^{\prime}}_{j=0,\,n-j\,\mathrm{even}}^{n}{\binom{n}{\tfrac{n-j}{2}}}\mathcal{T}_{j}(x),

where the prime at the sum symbol indicates that the contribution of
j=0
needs to be halved (if it appears). The sum of these coefficients is bounded by 1. This implies that for any polynomial the
1
-norm of the coefficients in the Chebyshev basis is at most the
1
-norm of the coefficients in the monomial basis.
This means, for example, that the Chebyshev coefficient 1-norm of the scaled exponential is at most
1
. Similarly, for a degree
n
approximation of the (scaled) logarithm the
1
-norm grows as
O(\log n)
. In particular, they have the following Taylor expansions for
\kappa\geq 1

\displaystyle e^{\kappa(x-1)}=e^{-\kappa}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\kappa x)^{n}}{n!},

\displaystyle\operatorname{slog}_{\kappa}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\log(1/\kappa+((x+1)/2)(1-1/\kappa))=\log\left(\frac{\kappa+1}{2\kappa}\left(1+\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\kappa+1}{2\kappa}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^{n}x^{n}.

A.2
Approximating discontinuities – the error function

Some more interesting examples are the sign and the rectangle functions, defined as

\sign(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x>0,\\
0&\text{if }x=0,\\
-1&\text{if }x<0,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|x|\leq 1/2,\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

It is well-known
[
17
,
11
]
that the error-function
\erf(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-z^{2}}\dif z
is a fundamental building block for approximating discontinuous functions. For example, given
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, there exists a choice of
\kappa=O(\polylog(1/\varepsilon)/\delta)
such that
\erf(\kappa x)
is
\varepsilon
-close to
\sign(x)
on
[-1,1]\setminus[-\delta,\delta]
. We show below that the
1
-norm of the coefficients of the Chebyshev series of
\erf(\kappa x)
is
O(\log\kappa)
. We start with the following expansion from
[
17
]
:

\erf(\kappa x)=\frac{2\kappa e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(I_{0}(\kappa^{2}/2)x+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}I_{j}(\kappa^{2}/2)(-1)^{j}\left(\frac{\mathcal{T}_{2j+1}(x)}{2j+1}-\frac{\mathcal{T}_{2j-1}(x)}{2j-1}\right)\right).

By regrouping the terms, we get the following explicit form of the Chebyshev series of
\erf(\kappa x)
:

(16)

\erf(\kappa x)=\frac{2\kappa e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{I_{n}(\kappa^{2}/2)+I_{n+1}(\kappa^{2}/2)}{2n+1}\mathcal{T}_{2n+1}(x).

Now, in order to bound the coefficient norm, we use the following inequality from
[
2
]
:

e^{-x}x^{-n}(I_{n}(x)+I_{n+1}(x))\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(x+\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{-n-\frac{1}{2}}.

Note that
I_{n}(x)\geq 0
for
x\geq 0
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So, the above in fact bounds the absolute value of the left hand side. We use this inequality to bound the (absolute value of the) coefficient of
\mathcal{T}_{2n+1}(x)
in (
16
) as follows:

(17)

\frac{2\kappa e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{I_{n}(\kappa^{2}/2)+I_{n+1}\kappa^{2}/2)}{2n+1}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{1}{2n+1}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+n+1/2}\right)^{n+1/2}.

Using this inequality, we can bound the coefficient norm of the truncated Chebyshev series:

Lemma 21
.

Let
N>0
be an integer. Then,

\frac{2\kappa e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{I_{n}(\kappa^{2}/2)+I_{n+1}(\kappa^{2}/2)}{2n+1}\leq\frac{6+2\log N}{\pi}.

Proof.

Using (
17
), and the fact that
0\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+n+1/2}\leq 1
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{2\kappa e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{I_{n}(\kappa^{2}/2)+I_{n+1}(\kappa^{2}/2)}{2n+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{2n+1}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+n+1/2}\right)^{n+1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{2n+1}.

It is well-known that the last sum is
O(\log N)
. To be more precise,

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{2n+1}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2n}\right)=\frac{4}{\pi}+\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}+\frac{2}{\pi}(1+\log N)\leq\frac{6+2\log N}{\pi}.

∎

Now, if we just want to bound the coefficients’ 1-norm, it suffices to take
N=\lceil\kappa^{2}\rceil
, and bound the rest of the coefficients using the following simple tail bound:

Lemma 22
.

Let
N\geq\kappa^{2}
be an integer. Then,

\frac{2\kappa e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{I_{n}(\kappa^{2}/2)+I_{n+1}(\kappa^{2}/2)}{2n+1}\leq 2^{2-N}.

Proof.

Again, we start by using (
17
), but now we note that for
n\geq\kappa^{2}
,
0\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+n+1/2}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, so

\displaystyle\frac{2\kappa e^{-\kappa^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{I_{n}(\kappa^{2}/2)+I_{n+1}(\kappa^{2}/2)}{2n+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{2^{-n}}{2n+1}\leq\frac{2^{3-N}}{\pi}\leq 2^{2-N}.\qed

Therefore, the coefficient norm of the entire series is bounded by
\frac{6+2\log\kappa^{2}}{\pi}+2^{2-\kappa^{2}}\leq 4+2\log\kappa
. An easy consequence of this bound is that we can approximate
\erf(\kappa x)
up to error
0\leq\varepsilon\leq 2^{2-\kappa^{2}}
with a polynomial of degree
\log_{2}(4/\varepsilon)
.

If the desired error
\varepsilon
is larger than
2^{2-\kappa^{2}}
, a more careful analysis of the tail bound for
\kappa\leq N\leq\kappa^{2}
yields an
\varepsilon
-approximation polynomial of degree
O(k\sqrt{\log(\kappa/\varepsilon)})
.

Lemma 23
.

Let
1\leq\alpha\leq\kappa
be an integer. Then,

\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=\alpha\kappa}^{(\alpha+1)\kappa-1}\frac{1}{2n+1}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+n+1/2}\right)^{n+1/2}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}e^{\alpha^{2}/2}.

Proof.

First, we note that
\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+n+1/2}\right)^{n+1/2}\leq\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+n}\right)^{n}
, so we get

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\pi}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\alpha\kappa}^{(\alpha+1)\kappa-1}\frac{1}{2n+1}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+n+1/2}\right)^{n+1/2}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=\alpha\kappa}^{(\alpha+1)\kappa-1}\frac{1}{2n+1}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+n}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=\alpha\kappa}^{(\alpha+1)\kappa-1}\frac{1}{2(\alpha\kappa)+1}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+\alpha\kappa}\right)^{\alpha\kappa}=\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\kappa}{2(\alpha\kappa)+1}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+\alpha}\right)^{\alpha\kappa}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\kappa}{2(\alpha\kappa)+1}\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha/\kappa}\right)^{\alpha\kappa}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\kappa}{2(\alpha\kappa)+1}\left(e^{-\alpha/(2\kappa)}\right)^{\alpha\kappa}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\kappa}{2(\alpha\kappa)+1}e^{-\alpha^{2}/2}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}e^{-\alpha^{2}/2}.

The second to last inequality requires
\alpha\leq\kappa
.
∎

So, to get an
\varepsilon
-approximation polynomial, we just need to an integer
1\leq\alpha_{0}\leq\kappa
such that

2^{2-\kappa^{2}}+\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{\alpha=\alpha_{0}}^{\kappa}e^{-\alpha^{2}/2}\leq\varepsilon.

Indeed, if we let
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon-2^{2-\kappa^{2}}
, it suffices to choose
\alpha_{0}=\left\lceil\sqrt{2\log(\frac{4\kappa}{\pi\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\right\rceil
, so we get

2^{2-\kappa^{2}}+\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{\alpha=\alpha_{0}}^{\kappa}e^{-\alpha^{2}/2}\leq 2^{2-\kappa^{2}}+\frac{4\kappa}{\pi}e^{-\alpha_{0}^{2}/2}\leq 2^{2-\kappa^{2}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon.

Thus, the degree of the
\varepsilon
-approximating polynomial is
\alpha_{0}\kappa-1=O(\kappa\sqrt{\log(\kappa/\varepsilon)})
.
