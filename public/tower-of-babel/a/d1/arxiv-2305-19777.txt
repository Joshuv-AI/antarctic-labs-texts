On the Shortest Lattice Vector vs. the Shortest Basis
Yael Eisenberg, Itamar Rot, Muli Safra

Abstract
Given an arbitrary basis for a mathematical lattice, to find a “good” basis for it is one of the classic and important algorithmic problems. In this note, we give a new and simpler proof of a theorem by [ 10 ] : we construct a 18-dimensional lattice that does not have a basis that satisfies the following two properties simultaneously: 1. The basis includes the shortest non-zero lattice vector. 2. The basis is shortest, that is, minimizes the longest basis vector (alternatively: the sum or the sum-of-squares of the basis vectors). The vectors’ length can be measured in any ℓ q \ell^{q} norm, for q ∈ ℕ + q\in\mathbb{N}_{+} (albeit, via another lattice, of a somewhat larger dimension).

Introduction

Broadly, a lattice is defined as a discrete subgroup of a topological group.

Moreover, an invariant and finite measure of the quotient group must exist.

Here, we are interested in discrete subgroups of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Such a subgroup
{\mathcal{L}}
can be described as the integer linear combination of a collection of linear independent vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

{\mathcal{L}}={\mathrm{Span}}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\;\middle|\;n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

The collection
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}
is a

{\mathcal{L}}
.

Historically, lattices have been investigated since the late 18th century by great mathematicians including Lagrange, Gauss, and Minkowski
[
8
]
(mostly for number theoretic applications).

In the late 20th century, lattices became a hot topic of transformative research with regards to the complexity of solving related computational problems.
Research in the area has thrived in several directions. One of those is the quest for algorithms to solve computational problems over lattices as efficiently as possible.

The fundamental mathematical principles involved are crucial in various distinct fields.
The theorems proven by Minkowski
[
7
]
, more than a century ago, and the research program he laid out, continue to be very relevant.
Some aspects affect the complexity of computational problems over lattices, in particular, their average-case complexity, with natural applications to cryptography.

Note that a lattice may be represented by numerous bases.
From a computational perspective, a lattice is described by a particular basis and the complexity of the computational problem depends fundamentally on the specific basis given as input.
If the given basis is close to orthogonal, problem such as SVP could be computed efficiently, while, given an arbitrary basis, the problem may turn out to be infeasible to solve efficiently.

In other words, the hardness of some computational problems over lattices stems from the hardness of finding a ‘good’ basis given an arbitrary one.
The problem of finding the best lattice basis is well-studied (see
[

1.1
Best Lattice Basis

There are few distinct (not necessarily equivalent) plausible definitions for the
best basis
for a lattice;
these formulate different manners by which to assert that the basis is either
short
or close as possible to being
orthogonal
.
Several options have been proposed for how to define what it means for a basis to be short.
For instance, it could be the length of the longest basis vector, the sum of lengths, or the product of lengths.

Consider the following
greedy
algorithm (not necessarily efficient) to find such a basis:
start with the shortest vector in the lattice, and then add the next shortest vector, albeit, one that is linearly independent of previous basis vectors (that is, not in the span of the current partial basis), etc.
Those vectors achieve, in terms of norms, the
successive minima
(see
Definition

2.6
).
Nevertheless, the successive minima do not necessarily form a basis for the lattice;
in fact, there are canonical examples of lattices such as
Example

3.1
that have no basis that achieves the successive minima: in these cases, the successive minima spans only a sub-lattice.

In this paper, we completely characterize
non-standard
lattices;
that is, lattices that have no basis which achieves the successive minima. Informally, we prove that any
non-standard lattice
is a ”faulty”
standard lattice
. Such characterization could significantly improve our understanding of non-standard lattices.
Moreover, they may facilitate improved techniques regarding computability issues.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a non-standard lattice.
Take any sub-lattice
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}\subset{\mathcal{L}}
that has a basis whose vectors’ length agree with the successive minima of
{\mathcal{L}}
—the lattice spanned by these vectors is a standard sub-lattice of
{\mathcal{L}}
.
Observe that it is maximal, in the sense that any other
sub-standard
lattice may not have smaller successive minima.

Then, the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
is a product of the standard lattice
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}
with a rational matrix.
Specifically, select a subset of the vectors in
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}
, divide them each by an arbitrary natural number and add the scaled vectors to the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}
.
{\mathcal{L}}
will be the additive closure of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}
and the scaled vectors (namely, the minimal lattice containing all):
see
Theorem

3.3
.

1.2
Best Lattice Basis: Revisited

Once it has been established that the most natural greedy process (which collects vectors that achieve the successive minima) might fail, one considers other greedy processes for finding a short basis.

Here is an even more fundamental question: does every lattice have a basis that includes the shortest vector?

The answer is clearly: ‘Yes’—the Korkine–Zolotarev (KZ) basis is one that includes the shortest lattice vector
[
4
]
.
In general, any lattice vector that is not an integer multiple of another lattice vector,
can be completed into a basis of the lattice.

Let the shortest basis be the lattice basis which minimizes the longest basis vector. Consider the following more complex question:
will the shortest basis always include the shortest vector?
(The KZ process produce a basis that might be
O(\sqrt{n})
-approximation of the shortest basis).

The answer, somewhat surprisingly, established in
[
10
]
, is ‘No’.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

There is a lattice
{\mathcal{L}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{43}
such that no basis
M=\left[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{43}\right]
of
{\mathcal{L}}
can satisfy the following two properties simultaneously:

•

M
includes a shortest lattice vector: there exists an
i
such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\|_{2}=\lambda_{1}({\mathcal{L}})
.

•

M
is the shortest basis of
{\mathcal{L}}
, that is,
M
minimizes the longest basis vector among all bases of
{\mathcal{L}}
.

We extend this theme and exhibit a family of constructive lattices for which the shortest basis never includes the shortest vector.
Interestingly, the lattices we present show the same statement for general norms, namely, any
\ell^{q}
norm for any
q\in\mathbb{N}
.

We further extend this statement and prove that the same holds when the basis is minimal with respect to the
sum
of the basis vectors’ lengths;
it still applies to many other “reasonable” manners to measure the basis length.

.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
.

{\mathcal{L}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

{\mathcal{L}}
satisfies the following two properties simultaneously:

•

includes a shortest lattice vector:
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\|_{q}=\lambda_{1}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})

•

is the shortest basis of
{\mathcal{L}}

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\|_{q}^{p}

Preliminaries

The
\ell^{q}
norm of a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted as
\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{q}={\left({\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left\lvert{x_{i}}\right\rvert}^{q}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}}
.
The centralized
\ell^{q}
ball of radius
r
is denoted
{\mathcal{B}}_{q}(r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{q}<r\right\}}
.

We notate the Minkowski sum of sets
\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{B}={\left\{\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{x}\in\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{y}\in\boldsymbol{B}\right\}}
for
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.1
\ell^{q}
Norm Modulo

The grid scaled by a real number
m
is denoted
m\mathbb{Z}^{n}
;
one may measure the distance of a vector from that set of points:
the absolute value of
\alpha\mod m
is
{\left\lvert{\alpha}\right\rvert}_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}\min_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}{\left\lvert{\alpha-z}\right\rvert}
.

Observe that
{\left\lvert{\alpha}\right\rvert}_{m}={\left\lvert{-\alpha}\right\rvert}_{m}
and
{\left\lvert{\alpha}\right\rvert}_{m}=k
if and only if
\alpha\mod m=\pm k
.

Definition 2.1

(
\ell^{q}
-norm modulo
m
)
.

The
\ell^{q}\mod m
norm of
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\mod m\right\|_{q}={\left({\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left\lvert{x_{i}}\right\rvert}_{m}^{q}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}}

namely, it is the
\ell^{q}
—distance of
\boldsymbol{x}
from
m\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Observe that
\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{q}\geq\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\mod m\right\|_{q}
, and there is an equality if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\left[-\frac{m-1}{2},\frac{m-1}{2}\right]}^{n}
.

2.2
Lattices

Definition 2.2
.

A lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
is a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Equivalently, a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
with linearly independent columns specifies a lattice
{\mathcal{L}}=M\mathbb{Z}^{k}
.
Such a matrix is referred to as
a basis of the lattice
and the lattice is then denoted as
{\mathcal{L}}[M]
.

n
is the
dimension
of the lattice and
k
is the
rank
of the lattice. A lattice is called
full rank
if
n=k
. We will treat lattices as though they are full rank by implicitly identifying
\mathrm{Span}({\mathcal{L}})
with
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Any lattice of rank greater than
1
can be generated by infinitely many bases. The following fact specifies exactly when two bases span the same lattice:

Fact 2.3
.

M,M^{\prime}
are bases of the same lattice,
{\mathcal{L}}[M]={\mathcal{L}}[M^{\prime}]
, if and only if there exists
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathrm{GL}_{k}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
M^{\prime}=MU
.

As mentioned above, there are several possible manners to measure the length of a lattice basis.
In this paper, we use the following definition:

Definition 2.4

(Shortest lattice basis)
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a lattice of rank
k\leq n
.
The
length of
{\mathcal{L}}
’s shortest basis
, denoted
\overline{\lambda}({\mathcal{L}})
, is the basis of
{\mathcal{L}}
which minimizes the longest basis vector:

\overline{\lambda}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}\min_{\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon{\mathcal{L}}[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k}]={\mathcal{L}}}\,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\|_{q}

and any basis of
{\mathcal{L}}
that achieves the minimum length is
a shortest basis of
{\mathcal{L}}
.

There are other possible manners by which to measure the length of a basis:

Definition 2.5
.

The
\ell^{q^{\prime}}
length
of a matrix
M=\left[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is

\left\|M\right\|_{q}^{q^{\prime}}={\left({\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\|_{q}^{q^{\prime}}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}

for any
1\leq q^{\prime}<\infty
.

Let us establish a simple lower bound on the length of any basis for a particular lattice:

Definition 2.6
.

The
successive minima
of a lattice are

\lambda_{i}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}\inf{\left\{r>0\mid\dim\mathrm{Span}{({\mathcal{L}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{q}(r))}\geq i\right\}}

Observe that
\lambda_{1}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})
is the length of the shortest non-zero lattice vector.
Further, observe that a basis of
{\mathcal{L}}
that achieves the successive minima is a shortest basis of
{\mathcal{L}}
; this is obtainable provided the lattice is standard as elaborated next.

Fact 2.7

(Folklore)
.

For any
q\in\mathbb{N}
, lattice
{\mathcal{L}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of rank
k
there exist linear independent vectors
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\in{\mathcal{L}}
such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\right\|_{q}=\lambda_{i}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Our characterization of non-standard lattices

Once the successive minima are introduced,
a natural question to ask is whether every lattice has a basis such that the basis vectors achieve the successive minima.
The answer to this question is a resounding ‘No’:

Example 3.1
.

[
11
]

Let
n\geq 2^{q}+1
and consider the
n
-dimensional lattice basis

M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}1&&&\frac{1}{2}\\
&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&1&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\frac{1}{2}\par\end{pmatrix}

Observe that
\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{n}
are the shortest vectors of the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}[M]
and thus
\lambda_{1}^{(q)}=\ldots=\lambda_{n}^{(q)}=1
.
Any basis of
{\mathcal{L}}
, however, must include a vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{odd}^{n}
, where
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|\geq{\frac{1}{2}}n^{\frac{1}{q}}>1
.

Definition 3.2
.

A lattice
{\mathcal{L}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of rank
k
is
\ell^{q}
-standard
if there exists a basis
M=[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k}]
of
{\mathcal{L}}
such that for any
1\leq i\leq k
,
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\|_{q}=\lambda_{i}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Observe that in a standard lattice,
\overline{\lambda}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})=\lambda_{n}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})
.
In contrast, the lattice of
Example

3.1
is not standard, and in fact

\overline{\lambda}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})={\frac{1}{2}}n^{\frac{1}{q}}>1=\lambda_{n}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}}).

We remark that any lattice of dimension
4
or lower must be
\ell^{2}
-standard
[
3
]
. An extension of this result is discussed in
.
Moreover, it is clear that the lattice in
Example

3.1
is constructed by beginning with
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and adding a new vector, which is a vector of the grid divided by some natural number.
In
Theorem

3.3
we demonstrate the fact that any non-standard lattice is constructed in this manner.

Theorem 3.3

(Characterization of non-standard lattices)
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be any non-standard full rank lattice, let
q\in\mathbb{N}
; let
W_{1},\dots,W_{k}\in{\mathcal{L}}
be a collection of linearly independent vectors with
\left\|W_{i}\right\|^{q}=\lambda_{i}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})
(those exist by
2.7
) and let
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}\subset{\mathcal{L}}
be the lattice generated by the
W_{i}
s, i.e.,
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}{\mathcal{L}}[W]
. Then there are vectors
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}
and integers
n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
(
\ell\leq k
)
such that

{\mathcal{L}}=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Z}}{{\left({{{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}}+{\left\{\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}}{n_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}}{n_{\ell}}\right\}}}\right)}}.

Proof.

Let
W_{1},\dots,W_{n}\in{\mathcal{L}}
be a collection of linearly independent vectors with
\left\|W_{i}\right\|^{q}=\lambda_{i}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})
(those exist by
2.7
).

Denote
W\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}[W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}]
and further denote
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}{\mathcal{L}}[W]
.

The successive minima of a sub-lattice cannot decrease moving from
{\mathcal{L}}
to
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}
, as
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}\subseteq{\mathcal{L}}
and thus
\forall i\in[n]\colon\lambda_{i}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}})\geq\lambda_{i}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})
.
On the other hand,
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}
and thus
\lambda_{i}^{(q)}[{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}]\leq\left\|W_{i}\right\|_{q}=\lambda_{i}^{(q)}[{\mathcal{L}}]
;
altogether it must be the case that
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}}
have the same successive minima,
\forall i\in[n]\colon\lambda_{i}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{std}})=\lambda_{i}^{(q)}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Let
M=\left[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\right]
be a basis of the original lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
.
Since
W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}\in{\mathcal{L}}[M]
, there exist
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
W_{i}=MC_{i}
.
Denote
C=[C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}]\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}
and observe that
W=MC
.
Since
W,M
are invertible,
so is
C
, and hence
WC^{-1}=M
.

C
is an integer matrix and hence
C^{-1}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
.
We denote
C^{-1}_{ij}=\frac{r_{ij}}{t_{ij}}
,

and

\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{i}}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
for a particular
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}[W]
and the theorem is proven.

∎

Stage I: amending the Grid

Claim 4.2
.

[A Short Vector]
For any large enough prime
p
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\sigma}$}\in{\left[-\frac{p-1}{2},\frac{p-1}{2}\right]}^{n-1}
such that
\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\mod p\right\|_{q}={\left({p^{q}-1}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}}
, and for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus{\left\{0,1,-1\right\}}
,
\left\|k\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\mod p\right\|_{q}>\left\|\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\mod p\right\|_{q}
.

The proof that such a vector exists appears in
Appendix

A
.

Observe that the vector
(1,\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$})
is still the shortest up to multiplication in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Construction 4.3
.

Let
p,n
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\in{\left[-\frac{p-1}{2},\frac{p-1}{2}\right]}^{n-1}
be defined as in
4.2
, and denote
\boldsymbol{u}=(1,\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$})
.
Let
W_{i}=p\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the
p
-streched
n
-unit vectors.
Define the
n
-dimensional lattice

{\mathcal{L}}_{+}={\mathcal{L}}[W_{1},\ldots,W_{n},\boldsymbol{u}]

Lemma 4.4
.

\pm W_{1},\ldots,\pm W_{n},\pm\boldsymbol{u}
are the only vectors in
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
with
\ell^{q}
norm
p
, and they are the shortest vectors in the lattice (i.e., there are no non-zero lattice vectors in
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
with norm
<p
).

Proof.

First, observe that the norm of
\boldsymbol{u}
is
{\left({\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sigma_{i}^{q}+1}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}}=p
.

Assume, by way of contradiction, that there is another short lattice vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
, that is,
0,\pm W_{1},\ldots,\pm W_{n},\pm\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{x}
such that

\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{q}\leq p
.
Clearly,
\boldsymbol{x}\notin{\mathcal{L}}[W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}]=p\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and hence there is
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}
and
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}=k\boldsymbol{u}+p\boldsymbol{z}
.
We may also assume, without loss of generality, that
0<k\leq p-1
(if
k=pt+r
where
t\in\mathbb{Z}
and
r<p
, then
\boldsymbol{x}=r\boldsymbol{u}+p(t\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{z})
).
Now, observe that

\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{q}\geq\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\mod p\right\|_{q}=\left\|k\boldsymbol{u}+p\boldsymbol{z}\mod p\right\|_{q}=\left\|k\boldsymbol{u}\mod p\right\|_{q}\geq\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\mod p\right\|_{q}=\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{q}=p;

here we use the fact that the norm of vectors in
[-\frac{p-1}{2},\frac{p-1}{2}]^{n}
does not change modulo
p
.
By
4.2
, if
k\neq\pm 1
, then
\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|>p
, which is a contradiction;
therefore, consider the case that
k=\pm 1
and, without loss of generality, the case
k=1
.
In this case,
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{u}+p\boldsymbol{z}
, and such a vector is the shortest if it is in
{\left[-\frac{p-1}{2},\frac{p-1}{2}\right]}^{n}
. This is the case if and only if
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and thus
\pm\boldsymbol{u}
is the shortest vector in
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
∎

4.3
Stage II: shaking the lattice

After constructing
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a lattice with
2(n+1)
vectors of the shortest length, we are able to proceed to construct our “holy grail” lattice.
We start with the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
, and add a tiny noise (without increasing the rank of the lattice), such that
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=(W_{1},\varepsilon)
will be the shortest lattice vector.

Construction 4.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be as in
4.3
. Since a lattice is a discrete group, there exists
R>p
such that
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{q}(R)={\left\{\boldsymbol{0},\pm W_{1},\ldots,\pm W_{n},\pm\boldsymbol{u}\right\}}
.
Now, let
0<\varepsilon<\frac{p^{q}(R^{q}-p^{q})}{((n-1)(p-1)+1)^{q}},\frac{R-p}{2}

and define some new vectors:

\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=(W_{1},\varepsilon),\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=(W_{i},2\varepsilon),\qquad\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}={\left({\boldsymbol{u},\frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sigma_{i}+1}{p}\varepsilon}\right)}

for
2\leq i\leq n
.

We define
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}{\mathcal{L}}[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
\mathcal{S}={\left\{\boldsymbol{0},\pm\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\pm\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\pm\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\}}
.

We first prove that the noise we added does not create any new short vectors in the lattice. However, it did alter the lengths of the shortest vectors slightly.

Lemma 4.6
.

\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}
is an
n
-dimensional lattice and moreover

•

\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{q}(R)=\mathcal{S}
—the only vectors in
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}
whose
\ell^{q}
norm is at most
R
is
\mathcal{S}
.

•

\pm\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
is the shortest vector in the lattice
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}
.

Proof.

We observe that
(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n})
are linearly independent, while
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n})
; thus, the dimension of the lattice is
n
.

Let us calculate the length of the vectors in
\mathcal{S}
:

•

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right\|_{q}={\left({p^{q}+\varepsilon^{q}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}}
.

•

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\|_{q}={\left({p^{q}+2^{q}\varepsilon^{q}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}}
for
2\leq i\leq n
.

•

\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{q}={\left({p^{q}+\frac{(2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sigma_{i}+1)^{q}}{p^{q}}\varepsilon^{q}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq{\left({p^{q}+\frac{((n-1)(p-1)+1)^{q}}{p^{q}}\varepsilon^{q}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}}<R

Now, if
q>1
, then by
Appendix

A
,
2p<4n-3
,

\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{q}\geq{\left({p^{q}+\frac{(4n-3)^{q}}{p^{q}}\varepsilon^{q}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}}>{\left({p^{q}+2^{q}\varepsilon^{q}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{q}},

and thus
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right\|<\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\|_{q}<\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{q}<R
for any
2\leq i\leq n
. If
q=1
, then

\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{1}=p+\frac{2(p-1)+1}{p}\epsilon<p+2\epsilon=\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right\|_{1}<R

and

\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{1}=p+(2-\frac{1}{p})\epsilon>p+\epsilon=\left\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right\|_{1}.

We now show that
\mathcal{S}
contains the shortest vectors in
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}
, i.e., any other lattice vector has norm larger than
R
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1})\in\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}\setminus\mathcal{S}
. Then, the vector
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
has norm strictly greater than
R
by definition and the result follows.

∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Any short basis of the lattice
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}
(where
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\|M_{i}\right\|_{q}
is minimized) cannot include the shortest non-zero vector of
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}
.

This Lemma implies
Theorem

4.1
.

Proof.

The lattice
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}
has a basis
\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}
(as
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=p\boldsymbol{\tilde{u}}-\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
)
hence the length of its shortest basis is at most
\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|
.
By
Lemma

4.6
, any basis of the lattice
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}

, may include only vectors in
\mathcal{S}
.
Such a basis must include

\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n}

p

{\mathcal{L}}[\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n}]\subsetneq\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}
.
Therefore, (up to change of signs) the basis must be
M^{(i)}={\left\{\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\mid k\neq i\right\}}\cup{\left\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\}}

If

Indeed the

So, it must be the case that

\pm\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
, the shortest lattice vector. ∎
