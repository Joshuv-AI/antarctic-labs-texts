Systematic Review on Learning-based Spectral CT
Alexandre Bousse, Venkata Sai Sundar Kandarpa, Simon Rit, Alessandro Perelli, Mengzhou Li, Guobao Wang, Jian Zhou, Ge Wang

Abstract
Spectral computed tomography has recently emerged as an advanced version of medical computed tomography and significantly improves conventional (single-energy) computed tomography. Spectral computed tomography has two main forms: dual-energy computed tomography and photon-counting computed tomography, which offer image improvement, material decomposition, and feature quantification relative to conventional computed tomography. However, the inherent challenges of spectral computed tomography, evidenced by data and image artifacts, remain a bottleneck for clinical applications. To address these problems, machine learning techniques have been widely applied to spectral computed tomography. In this review, we present the state-of-the-art data-driven techniques for spectral computed tomography.

I
Introduction

Since Cormack and Hounsfield’s Nobel prize-winning breakthrough, X-ray computed tomography is extensively used in medical applications and produces a huge number of gray-scale computed tomography images. However, these images are often insufficient to distinguish crucial differences between biological tissues and contrast agents. From the perspective of physics, the X-ray spectrum from a medical device is polychromatic, and interactions between X-rays and biological tissues depend on the X-ray energy, which suggests the feasibility to obtain spectral, multi-energy, or true-color, computed tomography images.

Over the past decade, spectral computed tomography has been rapidly developed as a new generation of computed tomography technology. Dual-energy computed tomography and photon-counting computed tomography are the two main forms of spectral computed tomography. Dual-energy computed tomography is a method of acquiring two projection datasets at different energy levels. Photon-counting computed tomography, on the other hand, uses detectors that measure individual photons and their energy, promising significantly better performance with major improvements in energy resolution, spatial resolution and dose efficiency
[

. Despite the intrinsic merits of spectral computed tomography, there are technical challenges already, being or yet to be addressed
[

Traditionally, computed tomography algorithms are grouped into two categories, which are analytic and iterative reconstruction respectively. A new category of computed tomography algorithms has recently emerged: artificial intelligence-inspired, learning-based or data-driven reconstruction. These algorithms are commonly implemented as deep neural networks, which are iteratively trained for image reconstruction and post-processing, and then used for inference in the feed-forward fashion just like a closed-form solution.

II
DECT and PCCT Systems

The first attempt to differentiate materials using computed tomography with multiple X-ray energy spectra was made in the 1970s
[
8
]
. Since then, technologies in spectral computed tomography have been continuously evolving. Traditional Dual-energy computed tomography and spectrally-resolving photon-counting computed tomography are the two specific forms of spectral computed tomography that are both commercially available. The former uses a minimum of two separate X-ray energy spectra to differentiate two basis materials with different attenuation properties at various energy levels, while the latter usually involves the advanced detector technology known as energy resolving photon-counting detectors, which resolves spectral information of X-ray photons in two or more energy bins emitted from a polychromatic X-ray source. Dual-energy computed tomography overcomes several limitations of single energy spectrum computed tomography and has achieved clinical acceptance and widespread applications. In the following, several types of dual-energy computed tomography are briefly described. We will not cover all technologies, but we will focus on those that are currently representative. The interested readers may refer to

Sequential acquisition is perhaps the most straightforward dual-energy computed tomography imaging approach. It performs two consecutive or subsequent scans of the same anatomy using an X-ray source operated at a low-peak kilovoltage setting and then a high-peak kilovoltage setting. The approach requires no hardware modification, but may suffer from image mis-registration due to motion artifacts from the delay between low- and high-peak kilovoltage scans. Advanced dual-energy computed tomography technologies all utilize specific hardware to mitigate the misregistration problem and shorten the data acquisition time.

The dual-source dual-energy computed tomography scanner was first introduced in 2005
[
19
]
, which is featured by two source-detector systems orthogonally arranged in the same gantry to acquire the low- and the high-energy scan simultaneously. Although the 90-degree phase shift between the two scans creates a slight temporal offset, the two X-ray sources can select independent X-ray energy spectra to optimize the spectral separation for material differentiation in the data and/or image domains.

A dual-layer detector or a combination of two detector layers of scintillation material is also a good solution for dual-energy computed tomography
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. In this approach, low- and high-energy datasets are collected simultaneously by the two detector layers with perfect spatial alignment and excellent synchronicity. This advantage simplifies direct data-domain material decomposition.

Fast peak kilovoltage-switching dual-energy computed tomography is yet another technology that uses a highly specialized X-ray generator that can rapidly switch the tube voltage between low- and high-peak kilovoltage settings during data acquisition. The first commercially available fast peak kilovoltage-switching dual-energy computed tomography scanner (GE Discovery CT750 HD) is capable of changing the tube voltage for each projection angle, so that each low- and high-peak kilovoltage projection can be obtained almost simultaneously. The material decomposition can then be performed in the data domain. A similar design has been reported in
[
24
]
where the authors have utilized a linear accelerator as X-ray source to generate rapid switching electron pulses of 6 MeV and 9 MeV respectively. This has resulted in an experimental MeV dual-energy computed tomography system that has been developed to perform cargo container inspection. Another type of fast peak kilovoltage-switching dual-energy computed tomography scanner has recently been introduced (Canon Aquilion ONE/PRISM)
[
25
]
that switches the tube voltage less frequently, allowing it to acquire the same energy from multiple successive projection angles. This design simplifies tube current modulation, making dose balancing at the two energy levels less complex. Along with the fast peak kilovoltage-switching process, there is also a grating-based method that can help improve data acquisition
[
26
]
. In this method, an X-ray filter that combines absorption and filtering gratings is placed between the source and the patient. The gratings move relative to each other and are synchronized with the tube switching process to avoid spectral correlation. Simulation studies have shown improved spectral information with reduced motion-induced artifacts.

photon-counting detectors technology plays an important role in photon-counting computed tomography imaging. Photon-counting detectorss requires a single layer of semiconductor sensor that converts X-ray photons directly into electrical signals. The main converter materials at present are cadmium zinc telluride and Si. cadmium zinc telluride is a material with a higher atomic number Z than Si and has a relatively high X-ray stopping power. Thus, the cadmium zinc telluride-based photon-counting detectors can have thin sensor layers of only a few millimeters, whereas Si-based detector lengths must be long enough to ensure good X-ray absorption. In one example of Si-based detector, the Si wafers are mounted sideways or edge-on against incoming X-rays to form a deep Si strip detector
[
27
]
. Therefore, building a full-area Si detector system can be more challenging. For imaging performance, both types of photon-counting detectors have advantages and disadvantages in terms of signal quality as well as detection efficiency. More detailed comparisons can be found in
[
28
,
29
]
.

The innovation of photon-counting detectors makes photon-counting computed tomography more attractive and offers unique advantages over conventional computed tomography or dual-energy computed tomography. These include improved dose efficiency by elimination of electronic noise, improved contrast-to-noise ratio ratio through energy weighting
[
30
,
31
,
29
]
, higher spatial resolution due to the small sub-millimeter photon-counting detectors fabricated without any septa
[
32
,
29
]
, and most importantly, unprecedented material decomposition capabilities potentially for multi-tracer studies. Although photon-counting computed tomography is potentially more advantageous, it has to deal with technical challenges, including charge sharing and pile-up effects together with the need for substantial hardware and system research and development. Currently, the accessibility of photon-counting computed tomography for clinical applications is still limited.

Spectral computed tomography, i.e., dual-energy computed tomography and photon-counting computed tomography, offer the possibility to perform separate measurements, each measurement corresponding to an energy spectrum. One possibility is to reconstruct several attenuation computed tomography images at different energies from these binned raw data. These images can then be used, e.g., for image-based material decomposition

Traditional regularization methods, such as those described in Sections

III-E

Another paradigm shift has been the development of end-to-end learning architectures that directly map the raw projection data to the reconstructed images. This approach, known as learned reconstruction, has two main categories: direct reconstruction and unrolling techniques. Direct reconstruction involves training a single neural network to perform the reconstruction task, while unrolling techniques aim to mimic the iterative algorithm by “unrolling” its iterations into layers. IV
Material Decomposition

Spectral computed tomography techniques such as dual-energy computed tomography and photon-counting computed tomography are often used to characterize the materials of the scanned patient or object by decomposing the linear attenuation coefficient into material images. This process of material decomposition is based on the assumption that the energy dependence of the linear attenuation coefficient in each pixel can be expressed as a linear combination of a small number
M
of basis functions
[
115
]
. The linear attenuation
\mu(\bm{r},E)
can then be modeled as

\mu(\bm{r},E)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}f_{m}(E)x_{m}(\bm{r}),

(35)

where
f_{m}
represents the
m
th
energy-dependent basis function and
x_{m}
is the
m
th
material image. These basis functions describe physical effects such as photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering
[
115
]
or the linear attenuation coefficients of representative materials of the scanned object such as water and bone for patients. With this model, two basis functions are sufficient to describe the variations of the linear attenuation coefficients of human tissues with energy
[
116
,
117
,
118
]
. One or more basis function(s) may also be used to represent a specific contrast agent, e.g., a material with a K-edge discontinuity in its attenuation coefficient in the range of diagnostic energies (30–140 keV)
[
119
]
. The material images
x_{m}
can be represented in the discrete domain as a vector using the pixel basis functions
u_{j}(\bm{r})
(see Eq. (
3
)) with each pixel of the unknown image decomposed into the chosen material basis. The discrete object model for the basis decomposition is then

\mu(\bm{r},E)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}f_{m}(E)\sum_{j=1}^{J}x_{j,m}u_{j}(\bm{r})\,,\forall(\bm{r},E)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}

(36)

where

is the weight of the
m
th
basis function in the
j
th
pixel. Injecting (
36
) into (
2
) links the material decomposition to the expected value (e.g. the number of detected X-ray photons for photon-counting computed tomography)

\overline{y}_{i,k}(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}h_{i,k}(E)\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{m=1}^{M}f_{m}(E)[\bm{A}_{k}\bm{x}_{m}]_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}E+r_{i,k}\,,

(37)

where
\bm{x}_{m}=[x_{1,m},\dots,x_{J,m}]^{\top}
. Material decomposition aims at estimating the decomposed computed tomography images
\bm{x}=\{\bm{x}_{m}\}
by matching the expected values
\overline{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\{\overline{\bm{y}}_{k}(\bm{x})\}
,
\overline{\bm{y}}_{k}(\bm{x})=[\overline{y}_{1,k}(\bm{x}),\dots,\overline{y}_{I,k}(\bm{x})]^{\top}
, with the measurements
\bm{y}=\{\bm{y}_{k}\}
with different efficient spectra
h_{i,k}
.

This problem is the combination of two sub-problems: tomographic reconstruction and spectral unmixing. The two problems can be solved sequentially or jointly and most techniques of the literature fall into one of the following categories: image-based, projection-based or one-step material decomposition.

IV-A

Image-based Material Decomposition

Image-based algorithms decompose the multichannel computed tomography image
\bm{\mu}=\{\bm{\mu}_{k}\}

\bm{x}_{m}

IV-C

One limitation of projection-based decomposition is that some statistical information is lost in decomposed projections

V
Data Pre-processing and Image Post-processing

Computed tomography technology has been the front-line imaging tool in emergency rooms due to its fast, non-invasive, and high-resolution features, with millions of scans performed annually worldwide. However, due to the increased cancer incidence from radiation exposure, “as low as reasonably achievable” is the central principle to follow in radiology practice. Recent advances in computed tomography technology and deep learning techniques have led to great developments in reducing radiation doses in computed tomography scans
[
175
]
. For example, aided by deep learning techniques, much progress has been made in low-dose or few-view computed tomography reconstruction without sacrificing significant image quality. Furthermore, the use of dual-energy computed tomography technology allows further cuts in radiation dose by replacing previous non-contrast computed tomography scans with virtual unenhanced images in clinical practice
[
176
]
.

While many prior-regularized iterative reconstruction techniques described in Section

inherently suppress noise and artifact, network-based post-processing techniques are also popular for removing noise and artifacts from already reconstructed low-dose spectral images and are covered here. Moreover, photon-counting computed tomography with photon-counting detectorss is widely viewed as a comprehensive upgrade to dual-energy computed tomography since it produces less noise, better spectral separation, and higher spatial resolution while requiring less radiation dose

However, the photon-counting detectors often experiences increased nonuniformity and spectral distortion due to charge-sharing and pulse pile-up effects compared to the traditional energy-integrating detectors, and the correction of these imperfections in photon-counting detectors images is included here. Finally, we also review deep learning techniques that enhance clinical diagnosis with spectral computed tomography, which includes virtual monoenergetic image synthesis, virtual noncontrast image generation, iodine dose reduction, virtual calcium suppression, and other applications. Image Denoising

In computed tomography imaging, it is important to limit the radiation dose to patients, but reducing the dose often gives rise to image noise, which can strain radiologists’ interpretation. To address this issue, various image denoising methods have been developed that aim to recover a clean version

While our focus is on deep learning and spectral computed tomography, it is important to briefly cover classic post-processing denoising techniques and deep learning techniques for single energy computed tomography, as they can still be applied to spectral computed tomography in a channel-by-channel manner. We will then dive into recent trends of self-supervised learning deep denoising methods, as well as deep methods that incorporate the correlations between energy channels.

With the recent development of dual-energy computed tomography and photon-counting computed tomography techniques, spectral imaging is reshaping the clinical utilization of computed tomography. VI
Perspectives

Advances in spectral computed tomography is a major frontier of the medical computed tomography field, which combines cutting-edge hardware for photon-counting detection and artificial intelligence-empowered software for deep learning-based reconstruction. As we have reviewed above, photon-counting spectral computed tomography promises to significantly improve the medical computed tomography performance in terms of spatial resolution, spectral resolution, tissue contrast, and dose efficiency. The distinguished capability of photon-counting computed tomography in material decomposition is clinically attractive to perform novel multi-contrast-enhanced studies and boost computed tomography, not only in anatomical imaging but also functional or even cellular imaging tasks. All of these can be implemented using machine learning methods or coupled with machine learning methods. Most of such machine learning methods are deep neural networks, involving each key step in the whole imaging workflow.

Looking ahead, the convergence of photon-counting and deep-learning techniques will surely establish spectral computed tomography as the new standard of medical computed tomography. To realize the huge potential of photon-counting spectral computed tomography, there remain challenges to be addressed before task-specific methods and protocols can be successfully translated into clinical practice. These challenges include but are not limited to the following aspects.

Direct Reconstruction

Deep neural networks have been explored to reconstruct images from sinograms in a number of studies. In this approach, a neural network is trained on a large set of sinogram-image pairs until the network predicts realistic reconstructed images. Here, the neural network learns to reconstruct the image and at the same time to reduce noise and to incorporate any other corrections desirable for reconstruction. Early methods developed for tomographic reconstruction using deep networks include AUTOMAP

Direct reconstruction techniques may be extended to multichannel reconstruction including photon-counting spectral computed tomography reconstruction. One possible way would be to have multi-channel networks incorporating data in multiple energy bins or an ensemble of networks with weight sharing for each energy. Importantly, correlations among these data in these channels should be utilized; for example, as a term in the loss function.

Diffusion Models

As a score-matching-based generative approach, the diffusion models have recently drawn a major attention of the community as they effectively compete or even outperform generative adversarial networks for image generation and other tasks
[

Diffusion models can be used to solve inverse problems by adding a data fidelity gradient descent step in the inverse diffusion, or by using the pseudo a-posteriori probability distribution conditioned to the observed data, which work in an unsupervised manner. These methods have been used in various inverse problems such as deblurring on RGB multichannel images

Hardware Refinement

Over the past years, photon-counting detectors have been greatly refined. There are more efforts on CZT detectors, but deep-silicon detectors are also of great interest. While CZT detectors and alike are more compact, the silicon technology is more mature, reliable and cost-effective with the potential to give more quantitative spectral imaging results. A detailed comparison is yet to be seen. Since the photon-counting detector pitches are substantially smaller than that of the energy-integrating detectors, the spatial resolution of computed tomography images can be accordingly improved, coupled with a reduced X-ray source focal spot. However, a small focal spot usually means a low X-ray flux. Hence, the balance must be made between image resolution, noise and imaging speed. It is underlined that while the hardware refinement in either detectors or sources is important, this kind of research will be more often performed by leading companies than academic groups. Since this review is more focused on computational aspects of spectral computed tomography, in the following we discuss more artificial intelligence-related challenges.

Big Data Construction

It is well known that big data is a prerequisite for data-driven research. Clearly, it is not easy to have big photon-counting computed tomography data for several reasons, including limited accessibility to photon-counting computed tomography scans, patient privacy, industrial confidentiality, and so on. We believe that this issue must be addressed using simulation tools, and ideally done in a healthcare metaverse. Such an idea was discussed as the first use case in a recent perspective article
[
289
]
. Along that direction, virtual twins of physical photon-counting computed tomography scanner models can scan patient avatars to produce simulated data. Along a complementary direction, a limited number of real photon-counting computed tomography scans can be used to train a generative model for realistic image augmentation. For example, it was recently shown that the diffusion model can be used to synthesize realistic data with suppressed privacy leakage
[
290
]
. This will facilitate federated learning at the level of datasets.

AI model Development

When sufficiently informative photon-counting computed tomography data are available, more advanced artificial intelligence models should be developed to address current weaknesses of deep reconstruction networks in the computed tomography field. The well-known problems of deep networks include stability, generalizability, uncertainty, interpretability, fairness, and more. As briefly mentioned in our review, a unique opportunity in deep learning-based photon-counting computed tomography imaging is raw data correction for charge-sharing, pile-up and other effects. These effects are very complicated, nonlinear and stochastic, but deep learning-based solutions are few and there will be more in the future. Furthermore, large models are gaining great attention, with ChatGPT as a precursor of the next generation of artificial intelligence methods, i.e., as the first step into the future of artificial general intelligence (AGI). It is believed that large models, multi-modal large models in particular, will further improve the photon-counting computed tomography performance.

High-performance and High-efficiency Computing

Deep learning with large models takes computational resources. Parallel/cloud computing, model distillation and hybrid (combination of classic and deep learning) reconstruction methods can be synergistic to develop practical photon-counting computed tomography methods. Special hardware such as FPGAs
[
291
]
could be adapted in photon-counting computed tomography tasks for imaging speed and energy efficiency.

Clinical Translation

The development of accurate and robust photon-counting computed tomography methods should lead to diverse clinical applications, from screening and diagnosis to treatment planning and prognosis. photon-counting computed tomography can be also used to guide minimally invasive procedures, such as biopsy and ablation, by providing real-time information over a region of interest
[
292
]
. The integration of photon-counting computed tomography (and dual-energy computed tomography) with other imaging modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography, would be beneficial as well, leading to a better understanding of anatomical forms and pathological functions.

The integration of spectral CT with PET has the potential to open novel clinical applications. However, such an integrated system either requires a costly hardware upgrade or is associated with increased radiation exposure. Most existing spectral CT imaging methods are based on a single modality that uses X-rays. Alternatively, it is possible to explore a combination of X-ray and

VII
Conclusion

In conclusion, this review has systematically reviewed spectral computed tomography with an emphasis on photon-counting and deep learning techniques. This field has evolved from traditional dual-energy computed tomography with an established status in medical imaging to contemporary photon-counting computed tomography with promising results and new utilities. Several remaining challenges have been discussed. The future of this technology looks exciting, with numerous opportunities for us to explore so that our imaging dreams can be turned into reality.
