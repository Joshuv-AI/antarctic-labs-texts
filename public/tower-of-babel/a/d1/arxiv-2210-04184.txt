Guided Nonlocal Patch Regularization and Efficient Filtering-Based Inversion for Multiband Fusion
Unni V. S, Pravin Nair, Kunal N. Chaudhury

Abstract
In multiband fusion, an image with a high spatial and low spectral resolution is combined with an image with a low spatial but high spectral resolution to produce a single multiband image having high spatial and spectral resolutions. This comes up in remote sensing applications such as pansharpening (MS+PAN), hyperspectral sharpening (HS+PAN), and HS-MS fusion (HS+MS). Remote sensing images are textured and have repetitive structures. Motivated by nonlocal patch-based methods for image restoration, we propose a convex regularizer that (i) takes into account long-distance correlations, (ii) penalizes patch variation, which is more effective than pixel variation for capturing texture information, and (iii) uses the higher spatial resolution image as a guide image for weight computation. We come up with an efficient ADMM algorithm for optimizing the regularizer along with a standard least-squares loss function derived from the imaging model. The novelty of our algorithm is that by expressing patch variation as filtering operations and by judiciously splitting the original variables and introducing latent variables, we are able to solve the ADMM subproblems efficiently using FFT-based convolution and soft-thresholding. As far as the reconstruction quality is concerned, our method is shown to outperform state-of-the-art variational and deep learning techniques.



I
Introduction

With the rapid advent of satellite imaging, remote sensing images are now widely used for object classification and recognition
[
1
]
, tracking
[
2
]
, environmental monitoring
[
3
]
, etc. Multiband imaging sensors acquire information as a band of two-dimensional images, where each image captures a narrow wavelength of the electromagnetic spectrum. The precision of these sensors is determined by their spectral and spatial resolutions, i.e., how densely the spectral and spatial domains are sampled. However, existing sensors have a fundamental tradeoff between spatial and spectral resolutions
[
1
,
4
]
. For example, hyperspectral (HS) images have hundreds of bands but low spatial resolution, while multispectral (MS) images have fewer bands but higher spatial resolution. An extreme case is a panchromatic (PAN) image which has a single band, but its spatial resolution is much higher than HS and MS images. Reconstruction of high-resolution multiband images by fusing images with complementary spatial and spectral resolutions is thus of great interest
[
1
]
.

I-D

Contributions

In this work, we adopt the variational model in (
2
). The novelty is the form of the nonlocal regularizer
\phi(\mathbf{X})
and an efficient filtering-based solution of the associated optimization problem.

(i) Regularization model
: Motivated by prior work on nonlocal regularization, we introduce a regularizer for remote sensing images that can exploit self-similar structures such as textures using image patches. However, unlike existing nonlocal regularizers
[
27
,
29
,
30
]
, our regularizer is convex. We note that the convex nonlocal regularizer in
[
28
]
is designed to capture pixel-based variations. In contrast, we use patches for penalizing variations jointly across all bands. Moreover, unlike VTV and local regularizers
[
57
,
58
,
59
]
, where variations along just horizontal and vertical directions are considered, we use a wider search window in terms of nonlocality and directionality. Finally, since in fusion we have a high resolution image of the same scene, we use this as a guide for computing weights, which are incorporated in the regularizer.

(ii) Optimization algorithm
: In Section
III
, we point out that, owing to the use of patch variations, a straightforward ADMM solution of (
2
) leads to computational issues. Nevertheless, we show that by expressing patch variation using convolutions, and by judiciously splitting the original variables and introducing new latent variables, we can efficiently solve the ADMM subproblems using FFT-based convolutions and soft-thresholding. Importantly, since our regularizer is convex, we are able to guarantee convergence of the ADMM iterations to a global optimum.

Local pixel-based methods have powerful regularization capability
[

,
58
,
59
]
. However, in many applications, their suitability is limited by the type of signal they can promote. For example, total variation (TV) can preserve strong edges but promotes piecewise-constant solutions, and is hence less effective for capturing textures and geometrical patterns. In particular, TV cannot satisfactorily capture repetitive nonlocal structures such as textures. It has been demonstrated that this problem can be addressed using nonlocal regularization

A major bottleneck in using nonlocal regularization for remote sensing applications such as pansharpening and hyperspectral fusion is that the inversion process derived from such models is computationally intensive. We address this problem in the present work.

II-B

Definition

To formulate the proposed nonlocal patch regularizer (referred to as NLPR), we introduce some notations. We let
\Omega=[0,p-1]\times[0,q-1]
denote the image domain (with periodic boundary conditions), where
n_{h}=pq
is the total number of pixels. We let
X_{c}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
denote the
c
-th image band, i.e., the
c
-th column of matrix
\mathbf{X}
.
This will shortly be used for defining the patch operator and will also come up later for defining convolutions in Section
III
. The reason we introduce
X_{c}
is that it is difficult to define shifts and convolutions directly on the columns of
\mathbf{X}
(representing different bands).

Our regularizer uses patches from both the reconstruction
\mathbf{X}
and the observed image
\mathbf{Y}_{h}
. We use circular shifts on
\Omega
which goes with the circular convolution and the discrete Fourier transform that are used later. In particular,
for
\bm{i}\in\Omega
and
\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we define

\bm{i}-\bm{\tau}=(i_{1}-\tau_{1}\ \mathrm{mod}\,p,i_{2}-\tau_{2}\ \mathrm{mod}\,q).

Note that
\bm{i}-\bm{\tau}\in\Omega
by definition.

Assume that the number of channels
L
is either
L_{s}
or
L_{h}
. For
\bm{i}\in\Omega
, the patch-extraction operator
\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{h}\times L}\to\mathbb{R}^{(2K+1)^{2}L}
is defined as follows. Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{h}\times L}
. We first fix a one-dimensional ordering of the set

\Lambda=\Big\{(\bm{k},c):\ \bm{k}\in[-K,K]^{2},\,c\in[1,L]\Big\},

where
K
is the patch length. With respect to this ordering, we consider the intensity values
X_{c}(\bm{i}-\bm{k}),(\bm{k},c)\in\Lambda,
and represent it as a vector of length
(2K+1)^{2}L
. This is precisely
\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}}(\mathbf{X})
.

For given
\bm{i}\in\Omega
and
\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we define the patch-difference operator
\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{h}\times L}\to\mathbb{R}^{(2K+1)^{2}L}
to be

\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}}(\mathbf{X})-\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}-\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{X}),

i.e.,
\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{X})
is the difference of the patch vectors around
\bm{i}
and
\bm{i}-\bm{\tau}
, where
\bm{\tau}
corresponds to shifts over the search window
W=[-S,S]^{2}
with
S
being sufficiently large. We skip the description of
\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{Y}_{h})
which is defined similarly.

The proposed NLPR regularizer is defined as

\phi(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{\bm{i}\in\Omega}\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\omega_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}\|\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{X})\|_{1},

(3)

where

\omega_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{h^{2}}\|\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{Y}_{h})\|^{2}_{2}\Big),

(4)

h>0
is a smoothing parameter and
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is the
\ell_{p}
norm. The precise reason for using the weighted
\ell_{1}
norm in (
3
) is that its proximal map has a closed form solution. Note that the weights in (
4
) are similar to that used in nonlocal means
[
65
]
.

We use the
\ell_{1}
norm due to its better robustness to outliers compared to the
\ell_{2}
norm; this is a well-known property of the
\ell_{1}
norm
[
66
]
. In fact, this is also the choice of norm in recent works that have been shown to perform well
[
40
,
41
]
.

As a regularizer,
\phi(\mathbf{X})
measures the weighted patch variations within
\mathbf{X}
over a nonlocal neighborhood, where the weights are computed from patches extracted from the observed high-resolution image
\mathbf{Y}_{h}
.
The proposed weighting ensures that if pixels
\bm{i}
and
\bm{i}-\bm{\tau}
are relatively close in terms of the patch distance
\|\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{Y}_{h})\|_{2}
, then a larger penalty is incurred if the corresponding patches in the reconstruction
\mathbf{X}
are very different. An important technical point that will be useful later is that
\phi(\mathbf{X})
is a convex function of
\mathbf{X}
. However, it is not differentiable and we cannot use gradient-based algorithms.

In fact, NLPR is generally more powerful than local pixel regularizers and the improvement is visually apparent for applications such as inpainting and denoising. Two such results are shown in figures

and

III
Patch variation using convolutions

On plugging the proposed NLPR regularizer (
3
) into (
2
), we obtain the optimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{X}}{\min}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}\|}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}\|}^{2}\!

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\sum_{\bm{i}\in\Omega}\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\omega_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}{\|\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{X})\|}_{1}.

(5)

We wish to come up with an efficient solver for (
III
). This is an unconstrained nonsmooth convex optimization, where the objective function is not differentiable due to the presence of the
\ell_{1}
norm. However, since the proximal map of the weighted
\ell_{1}
norm has a closed-form solution, this can be solved using a proximal algorithm such as ADMM
[
68
,
69
]
. The advantage of ADMM is that the original variable can be split into latent variables so that the ensuing subproblems can be solved efficiently, often in closed-form.
For example, a straightforward splitting for (
III
) results in the following equivalent problem:

\begin{gathered}\min\ \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{E}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}-\mathbf{P}_{2}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}\|}^{2}\!+\!\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}g(\mathbf{Q})\\
\mbox{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{P}_{1}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X},\quad\mathbf{P}_{2}=\mathbf{X},\\
\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}\quad(\bm{i}\in\Omega,\bm{\tau}\in W),\end{gathered}

(6)

where

g(\mathbf{Q})=\sum_{\bm{i}\in\Omega}\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\omega_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}\|\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}\|_{1}.

The variables in this case are
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}
and
\mathbf{Q}=\{\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}:\bm{i}\in\Omega,\bm{\tau}\in W\}
.
For the corresponding ADMM algorithm, all variables have a closed-form update. However, the linear system corresponding to the
\mathbf{X}
-update is prohibitively expensive to invert.
More precisely, for the update
\mathbf{X}^{(t)}\to\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}
, we need to solve a linear system of the form

\Big(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{I}+\sum_{\bm{i}\in\Omega}\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}^{\top}\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}\Big)\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{C},

(7)

The coefficient matrix in (

, where
n_{h}
is the number of pixels. Hence, we cannot use a direct solver.
Even for iterative solvers such as CG
[
70
]
, we have to apply the coefficient matrix (as an operator) on an image of size

in each iteration. While the blur

). We remark that the quantity on the left is the
(i,c)
-th entry of matrix
\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}
, while the quantity on the right is output of the convolved image
d_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\circledast X_{c}
at pixel position
\bm{i}
. Note that there are a total of
|W||P|
filters, one for each
\bm{\tau}\in W
and
\bm{k}\in P
. Also note that, for a fixed
\bm{\tau}\in W
, the filters are shifted versions of each other: if we let
\bm{k}_{\textbf{0}}=(0,0)
, then
(d_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\circledast\mathbf{X}_{c})(\bm{i})=(d_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}_{\textbf{0}}}\circledast\mathbf{X}_{c})(\bm{i}-\bm{k})
for all
\bm{k}\in P\backslash\bm{k}_{\textbf{0}}
. This observation is used to simplify computations.

For fixed
\bm{i}
and
\bm{\tau}
, recall that the patch variation
\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{X})
is the vector consisting of the samples
X_{c}(\bm{i}-\bm{k})-X_{c}(\bm{i}-\bm{\tau}-\bm{k})
for
(\bm{k},c)\in\Lambda
. However, as per definition (
8
), this is just the vector representation of
\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}(i,c)
for
\bm{k}\in P
and
c\in[1,L_{s}]
. These two different ways of computing patch variation is illustrated in figure
3
.

In terms of the convolution filters, we can write

\lVert\mathscr{P}_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}(\mathbf{X})\rVert_{1}=\sum_{c=1}^{L_{s}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}|\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}(i,c)|,

and hence (
3
) as

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{\bm{i}\in\Omega}\sum_{c=1}^{L_{s}}\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}\omega_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}|\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}(i,c)|.

(9)

We remark that
\bm{i}
in the summation is the pixel position in the image domain
\Omega
and the
i
appearing in
\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}(i,c)
is the corresponding row index of the pixel in
\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}
.

IV
Optimization

The advantage with representation (
9
) will be clear in the context of the ADMM algorithm, where we will split the matrix variables
\{\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}:\bm{\tau}\in W,\bm{k}\in P\}
. In particular, instead of the splitting in (
6
), we consider the following equivalent formulation of (
III
):

\begin{gathered}\min\ \frac{1}{2}\!\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}\!-\!\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{E}\|^{2}\!+\!\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\!{\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}\!-\!\mathbf{P}_{2}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}\|}^{2}\!+\!\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\!g(\mathbf{Q})\\
\mbox{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{P}_{1}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X},\quad\mathbf{P}_{2}=\mathbf{X},\\
\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\quad(\bm{\tau}\in W,\bm{k}\in P),\end{gathered}

(10)

where
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}
and
\mathbf{Q}=\{\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}:\bm{\tau}\in W,\bm{k}\in P\}
are the (primal) variables, and where, following (
9
), we define

g(\mathbf{Q})=\sum_{\bm{i}\in\Omega}\sum_{c=1}^{L_{s}}\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}\ \omega_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}|{\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}}(i,c)|.

As explained next, the key advantage with formulation (
10
) is that the subproblems in the corresponding ADMM algorithm have closed-form solutions.

Let
\bm{\Lambda}_{1}
,
\bm{\Lambda}_{2}
and
\{\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}:\bm{\tau}\in W,\bm{k}\in P\}
be the dual variables for the equality constraints in (
10
). The augmented Lagrangian is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2},\mathbf{Q})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{E}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}-\mathbf{P}_{2}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}\|}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}g\big(\mathbf{Q})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{P}_{1}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}+\bm{\Lambda}_{1}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{P}_{2}-\mathbf{X}+\bm{\Lambda}_{2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}\|\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}-\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}+\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\|^{2}.

where
\rho>0
is a penalty parameter
[
69
]
.

Starting with initial variables
\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(0)}
,
\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(0)}
,
\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(0)}
,
\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(0)}
,
\bm{\Lambda}_{2}^{(0)}
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(0)}
, we now work out the ADMM updates.

IV-A

\mathbf{X}
update

The subproblem corresponding to
\mathbf{X}
update is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}=\underset{\mathbf{X}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t)},\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(t)},\mathbf{Q}^{(t)})

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbf{X}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t)}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}+\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t)}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(t)}-\mathbf{X}+\bm{\Lambda}_{2}^{(t)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}\|\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t)}-\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}+\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t)}\|^{2}.

(11)

The objective in (
IV-A
) is strongly convex and differentiable. Setting its gradient to zero, we obtain

\Big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}+\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\Big)\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{C}^{(t)},

(12)

where the matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
is given by

\mathbf{B}^{\top}\!\big(\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t)}+\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t)}\big)+\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(t)}+\bm{\Lambda}_{2}^{(t)}+\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{\top}\Big(\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t)}+\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t)}\Big).

The coefficient matrix in (
12
) is positive definite and the linear system is solvable. We now show how this can be solved using the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT).

Let
X^{(t+1)}_{c}
be the
c
-th band of
\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}
(i.e.,
c
-th column of
\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}
) and
C_{c}^{(t)}
be the
c
-th column of
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
. Writing (
12
) columnwise, we obtain for
c=1,\ldots,L_{s}:

\displaystyle\quad X^{(t+1)}_{c}+\check{b}\circledast b\circledast X^{(t+1)}_{c}+

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}\big(\check{d}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\circledast d_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\circledast X^{(t+1)}_{c}\big)=C^{(t)}_{c},

(13)

where we recall that
b
and
\check{b}
are the filters associated with
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\top}
, and
d_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}
and
\check{d}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}
are the filters associated with
\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}
and
\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{\top}
.
The DFT of both sides of (
IV-A
) gives us

\Big(1+{|\widehat{b}(\bm{\omega})|}^{2}+\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}{|\widehat{d}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}(\bm{\omega})|}^{2}\Big)\widehat{X}_{c}^{(t+1)}(\bm{\omega})=\widehat{C}^{(t)}_{c}(\bm{\omega}),

where
\widehat{X}_{c}^{(t+1)}
and
\widehat{C}^{(t)}_{c}
are the DFTs of
X_{c}^{(t+1)}
and
C^{(t)}_{c}
. Moreover, since
|\widehat{d}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}(\bm{\omega})|=|\widehat{d}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}_{\bm{0}}}(\bm{\omega})|
, we have

\widehat{X}_{c}^{(t+1)}(\bm{\omega})=\Big(1+{|\widehat{b}(\bm{\omega})|}^{2}+|P|\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}{|\widehat{d}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}_{\bm{0}}}(\bm{\omega})|}^{2}\Big)^{-1}\widehat{C}^{(t)}_{c}(\bm{\omega}).

(14)

On taking the inverse DFT, we obtain
X_{c}^{(t+1)}
for each
c
and hence the desired update
\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}
. Note that the inverse in (
14
) can be precomputed and stored.

IV-B

\mathbf{P}_{1}
update

The subproblem corresponding to
\mathbf{P}_{1}
update is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}=\underset{\mathbf{P}_{1}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(t)},\mathbf{Q}^{(t)})

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbf{P}_{1}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{E}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{P}_{1}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}+\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t)}\|^{2}.

(15)

The objective in (
IV-B
) is strongly convex and differentiable in
\mathbf{P}_{1}
. Setting its
the gradient to zero, we obtain

\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}+\rho\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}\mathbf{E}^{\top}+\rho(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t)})

(16)

Recall that
\mathbf{S}
is the sampling matrix. It has the property that

\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\ \mathbf{S}^{\top}=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S})^{2}=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}.

(17)

Multiplying (
16
) by
\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}
and
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S})
and using (
17
), we obtain

\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\ \mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}+\rho\mathbf{I})=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\ (\rho\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}+\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}\mathbf{E}^{\top}-\rho\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t)}),

and

(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S})\ \mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S})\ (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t)}).

Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\ \mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}+(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S})\ \mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\ (\rho\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}+\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}\mathbf{E}^{\top}-\rho\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t)})(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}+\rho\mathbf{I})^{-1}

\displaystyle+(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S})\ (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t)}).

(18)

Note that
\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}
is diagonal with
1
in positions corresponding to the sampled pixels and
0
elsewhere
[
4
]
(recall that
\mathbf{S}
is the spatial sampling operator). Since the matrix
\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}+\rho\mathbf{I}
is of size
L_{s}\times L_{s}
, its inverse can be precomputed and stored.

IV-C

\mathbf{P}_{2}
update

The subproblem corresponding to
\mathbf{P}_{2}
update is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(t+1)}=\underset{\mathbf{P}_{2}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{P}_{2},\mathbf{Q}^{(t)})

\displaystyle\hskip-7.11317pt=\underset{\mathbf{P}_{2}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}-\mathbf{P}_{2}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}\|}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{P}_{2}+\bm{\Lambda}_{2}^{(t)}-\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}\|^{2}.

(19)

Setting
\nabla_{\mathbf{P}_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(t)},\mathbf{Q}^{(t)})=\mathbf{0}
, we obtain the closed-form solution:

\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(t+1)}=(-\bm{\Lambda}_{2}^{(t)}+\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}+\lambda_{1}/\rho\ \mathbf{Y}_{h}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{E}^{\top})\mathbf{F},

(20)

where
\mathbf{F}=(\mathbf{I}+\lambda_{1}/\rho\ \mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{E}^{\top})^{-1}
is small (
L_{s}\times L_{s}
) and hence can be precomputed and stored.

IV-D

\mathbf{Q}
update

The subproblem corresponding to the
\mathbf{Q}
update is given by:

\mathbf{Q}^{(t+1)}=\underset{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{Q})

Using the separable structure, we can break this up into smaller subproblems. Namely, if we let

\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t)}=\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Sigma}_{j}^{(t)},

then, for
\bm{\tau}\in W,\bm{k}\in P
,
\forall i\in[1,n_{h}],\ c\in[1,L_{s}],

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t+1)}(i,c)=

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \Big\{\big|{\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}}(i,c)\big|

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{2\lambda_{2}\ \omega_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}}\Big(\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}(i,c)-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t)}(i,c)\Big)^{2}\Big\}.

(21)

For each
\bm{\tau}\in W
and
\bm{k}\in P
, the matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}
is updated using soft-thresholding operation (on account of the
\ell_{1}
-norm in the regularizer). The exact update is as follows:

{\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}}^{(t+1)}=\mathcal{S}\Big(\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t)},\lambda_{2}\omega_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}/\rho\Big),

(22)

where
\mathcal{S}(x,\mu)=\mbox{sign}(x)\max(|x|-\mu,0)
is the soft thresholding of
x
with threshold
\tau
; note that
\mathcal{S}
is applied componentwise on the matrix.

IV-E

Dual updates

The dual variables are updated as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t+1)}=\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{(t)}-(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}-\mathbf{P}_{1}^{(t+1)}),

(23)

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{2}^{(t+1)}=\bm{\Lambda}_{2}^{(t)}-(\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}-\mathbf{P}_{2}^{(t+1)}),

(24)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t+1)}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t)}-(\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}-\mathbf{Q}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}^{(t+1)}).

(25)

Recall that (
10
) is a convex program. As is well-known, under appropriate technical conditions, the iterates generated by the ADMM algorithm are guaranteed to converge to the global optimum
[
71
]
. For our algorithm, we have the following convergence result (see Appendix for proof).

Theorem 1
.

Assume that the original problem (
III
) is solvable, i.e., there exists
\mathbf{X}^{*}
where the minimum is attained. Then, for any arbitrary initialization and any
\rho>0
, the sequence of updates
(\mathbf{X}^{(t)})_{t\geqslant 0}
generated using (
12
), (
IV-B
), (
20
), (
22
), (
23
), (
24
) and (
25
) converges to
\mathbf{X}^{*}
.

IV-F

Computational details

Unlike the
\mathbf{X}
update for (
7
), which is performed iteratively and is expensive, the
\mathbf{X}
update in (
12
) can be computed efficiently. Indeed, since
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}
(and their transposes) are circular convolutions, they can be jointly diagonalized in a Fourier basis. Let
\widehat{b}
and
\widehat{d}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}
denote the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of filters
b
and
d_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}
. Recall that the DFT of the
\mathbf{X}
update is given by

\widehat{X}_{c}^{(t+1)}(\bm{\omega})=\!\Big(1+{|\widehat{b}(\bm{\omega})|}^{2}\!+\!\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}|\widehat{d}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}(\bm{\omega})|^{2}\Big)^{-1}\!\widehat{C}^{(t)}_{c}(\bm{\omega}),

where
C^{(t)}_{c}
is the image representation of
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}(:,c)
in (
12
) and
\widehat{C}^{(t)}_{c}
is its DFT.

Thus, the
\mathbf{X}
update can be performed using two FFTs that has complexity
\mathcal{O}(|W||P|L_{s}n_{h}\log(n_{h}))
. Note that the first term on the right involving the DFT of the filters can be precomputed.
Also, the matrix inverses in (
IV-B
) and (
20
) can be precomputed and stored, since the matrix size is
L_{s}\times L_{s}
where
L_{s}
is in the range
10\mbox{-}20
for HS-MS fusion and hyperspectral sharpening, and in the range
4\mbox{-}8
for pansharpening. For updates (
IV-B
) and (
20
), the complexity is
\mathcal{O}(L_{s}n_{h}\log(n_{h}))
for constructing the matrices and
\mathcal{O}(L_{s}^{3})
for inverting the matrix. The complexity of the thresholding operations in (
22
) is
\mathcal{O}(|W||P|L_{s}n_{h})
. Clearly, the cost per iteration of the proposed algorithm is dominated by the
\mathbf{X}
update which we perform efficiently using FFTs. For example, on multiband image of size
200\times 200\times 20
, solving (
7
) using CG takes
127
s (
50
CG iterations), whereas (
12
) takes just
135
ms (
940\times
speedup).

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a variational multiband image fusion technique using a nonlocal patch-based regularizer. We showed that the regularizer is more effective than local pixel-based regularizers and helps in preserving self-similar structures such as textures in remote sensing images. We also came up with a novel means of encoding patch variations using convolutions, and showed how (along with an appropriate variable splitting technique) this can be used to derive an ADMM algorithm where the subproblems have closed-form solutions. We presented exhaustive results on HS-MS fusion and pansharpening which show that our regularizer is competitive with state-of-the-art variational and deep learning methods and is better able to capture fine textures in remote sensing images.

Appendix: Proof of Theorem 1

Convergence of ADMM for convex programs is well established
[
71
,
69
,
85
,
86
]
. However, convergence typically holds under some technical conditions, which have to be verified for the optimization problem at hand. Unlike standard optimization problems where the variable is a vector, the optimization variable in (
III
) is a matrix. However, both vectors and matrices can be seen as elements of some finite-dimensional inner-product space and existing convergence results can be extended in a straightforward manner to this more general setting
[
85
]
. In particular, as explained next, the ADMM convergence results in
[
86
]
can be applied to our problem.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two finite dimensional real vector spaces and let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
be a linear operator. Consider the optimization problem

\underset{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}{\min}\ f\big(\mathbf{X}\big)+g\big(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X})\big),

(26)

where
f:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
are closed convex functions.

An ADMM algorithm for (
26
) is as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)}=\underset{\mathbf{X}}{\mathrm{min}}\ f(\mathbf{X})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X})-\mathbf{V}^{(t)}-\mathbf{U}^{(t)}\|_{\ast}

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}=\underset{\mathbf{V}}{\mathrm{min}}\ g(\mathbf{V})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)})-\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{U}^{(t)}\|_{\ast}

(28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{U}^{(t)}+\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X}^{(t+1)})-\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)},

(29)

where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
are the primal variables,
\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
is the dual variable and the norm used in the updates is induced by some arbitrary inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\ast}
defined on
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
By adapting the following convergence result in
[
86
]
, one can show that the above ADMM algorithm solves (
26
).

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the problem (
26
) is solvable, i.e., there exists
\mathbf{X}^{*}
where the minimum is attained. Also, assume that the linear operator
\mathcal{A}
is injective. Then, for any arbitrary initialization and any
\rho>0
, the sequence
(\mathbf{X}^{(t)})_{t\geqslant 0}
generated using (
27
), (
28
) and (
29
) converges to
\mathbf{X}^{*}
.

We note that our optimization problem (
III
) is an instance of (
26
), where

•

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}\times L_{s}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}(2+|W||P|)\times L_{s}}
, with the Frobenius inner product on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

•

\mathcal{A}
is defined as

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{W}\end{pmatrix},

(30)

where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}|W||P|\times L_{s}}
is obtained by stacking the matrices
\{\mathbf{D}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}\times L_{s}}:\bm{\tau}\in W,\bm{k}\in P\}
along rows. Clearly,
\mathcal{A}
is injective since
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{0}
implies
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{0}
.

•

f
is the zero function and
g:\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathbf{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle\quad g(\mathbf{V})=\frac{1}{2}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}\!-\!\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}_{1}\mathbf{E}\|}^{2}\!+\!\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{h}\!-\!\mathbf{V}_{2}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{R}\|}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\sum_{\bm{i}\in\Omega}\sum_{\bm{\tau}\in W}\sum_{\bm{k}\in P}\sum_{c=1}^{L_{s}}\omega_{\bm{i}\bm{\tau}}|\mathbf{V}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}(i,c)|,

(31)

where

\mathbf{V}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{V}_{1}\\
\mathbf{V}_{2}\\
\mathbf{V}_{3}\end{pmatrix}

(32)

where
\mathbf{V}_{1}
,
\mathbf{V}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}\times L_{s}}
and
\mathbf{V}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}|W||P|\times L_{s}}
is the row-wise concatenation of
|W||P|
matrices
\{\mathbf{V}_{\bm{\tau}\bm{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}\times L_{s}}:\bm{\tau}\in W,\bm{k}\in P\}
.

Next we need to set up the correspondence between updates (
IV-A
), (
IV-B
), (
IV-C
), (
IV-D
), (

), (

), (

) of the proposed ADMM algorithm and the updates in (

\mathbf{X}

. The dual variables can be related as

\mathbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\Lambda}_{1}\\
\bm{\Lambda}_{2}\\
\bm{\Sigma}\end{pmatrix}

(33)

where
