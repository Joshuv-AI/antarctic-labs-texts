Quantized Radio Map Estimation Using Tensor and Deep Generative Models
Subash Timilsina, Sagar Shrestha, Xiao Fu

Abstract
Spectrum cartography (SC), also known as radio map estimation (RME), aims at crafting multi-domain (e.g., frequency and space) radio power propagation maps from limited sensor measurements. While early methods often lacked theoretical support, recent works have demonstrated that radio maps can be provably recovered using low-dimensional models—such as the block-term tensor decomposition (BTD) model and certain deep generative models (DGMs)—of the high-dimensional multi-domain radio signals. However, these existing provable SC approaches assume that sensors send real-valued (full-resolution) measurements to the fusion center, which is unrealistic. This work puts forth a quantized SC framework that generalizes the BTD and DGM-based SC to scenarios where heavily quantized sensor measurements are used. A maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)-based SC framework under a Gaussian quantizer is proposed. Recoverability of the radio map using the MLE criterion is characterized under realistic conditions, e.g., imperfect radio map modeling and noisy measurements. Simulations and real-data experiments are used to showcase the effectiveness of the proposed approach.

The

From a signal processing viewpoint, estimating a multi-domain, high-dimensional and high-resolution radio map from limited sensor-acquired samples/measurements is an ill-posed inverse problem—where an infinite number of solutions exist in general, hindering the recoverability of the radio map.

Early SC/RME methods often assume that power propagation is smooth over the space. This made it possible to use various interpolation techniques for SC, e.g., the Kriging interpolation

From a low-rank model completion viewpoint, some of these methods were shown to ensure the recovery of the radio map; see, e.g.,

To better model heavily shadowed environments, a number of deep learning-based methods were employed for SC.
Compared to handcrafted prior-based approaches, deep learning-based methods need a training phase using oftentimes a large amount of training data, which creates extra workload.
However, deep neural networks can represent complex scenarios as nonlinear functions in a succinct way, and thus can recover the radio map accurately under heavy shadowing. Earlier deep learning-based SC works in

Challenges.

The recent developments of theory-backed SC (e.g., the works based on tensor
[
6
]
and DGM
[
7
]
) were built upon the premise that the sensors communicate with the fusion center using full-resolution real-valued measurements. This setup is unrealistic as real-world communication systems often designate limited bandwidth for signaling. The measurements sent to the fusion center by the sensors are usually heavily quantized.
Nonetheless, SC using quantized measurements was much less studied in the literature.
The line of “frugal sensing” work
[
20
,
21
]
considered 1-bit power spectrum estimation, but did not consider the reconstruction of the spatial information. The work in
[
10
]
used kernel regression for SC with quantized sensor feedback. However, it assumed that the power spectral density (PSD) of each emitter is known to the fusion center—but estimating the PSDs itself is a highly non-trivial task
[
22
,
23
]
.
To our best knowledge,
blind
SC methods (i.e., SC without assuming knowing the emitters’ PSDs) using quantized measurements and the associated theoretical understanding have not been studied—for both handcrafted prior and DGM-based SC frameworks.

Contributions.
In this work, we propose a quantized SC framework with provable recovery guarantees of the ground-truth radio map.
Our detailed contributions are as follows:

\bullet

A Quantized SC Framework.
We propose a quantized SC framework that can flexibly work with various radio map models. We consider the setting where the measurements sent by the sensors are outputs of a Gaussian quantizer.
Then, we formulate the quantized SC problem using the maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE) principle. We combine the quantization strategy with two radio map models, namely,
block-term tensor decomposition
(BTD) from
[
6
]
and DGM from
[
7
]
, and design algorithms for tackling their respective MLE formulations.
Both models are well-motivated and useful: The former is more economical to deploy (as no training data is needed) and the latter is more resilient to heavy shadowing.

\bullet

Recoverability Analysis.

On the theory side,
we show that the formulated MLE criteria under both the tensor model and the DGM of the radio maps ensure recovering the ground truth (up to bounded errors), if reasonable conditions are met.
In particular, we show that our framework enjoys provable recovery even under challenging scenarios, e.g., when the low-rank tensor model or the DGM do not exactly match the ground truth.
The results also reveal an intuitive trade-off between the model complexity and the sample complexity.
Our analysis is a nontrivial integration of generalization error analysis and quantized data recovery, with careful consideration given to sensing paradigms in the context of SC.

Part of the work will appear at ICASSP 2023
[
24
]
, which introduced the basic idea and the DGM-based formulation. The journal version additionally includes 1) a tensor decomposition-based model that does not require off-line training; 2) detailed and unified recoverability analysis under both the DGM and BTD models; 3) more comprehensive simulations; and 4) real-data experiments.

II-A

Problem Setup

We focus on the SC scenario illustrated in Fig.
1
(a). The scenario is widely considered in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
2
,

,

Specifically, we aim at recovering a

radio power density map induced by
R

N

For the ease of exposure, we assume that the space domain is a 2D rectangle and is discretized into
I\times J

The frequency domain is also discretized into
K

every spatial grid is associated with a
K

In other words, the power propagation patterns of the
R
emitters over the
K

I\times J\times K

\underline{\bm{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{I\times J\times K}
, where the entry
\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,k)
is the PSD of the signal received at location
(i,j)
and frequency
k
.
That is, every
fiber

[
25
]
of the tensor,
\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,:)
, represents the PSD of the received signal measured at the location
(i,j)
; see Fig.
1
(b). We use the notation

{\bm{\varOmega}}=\{(i,j)|i\in[I],j\in[J]\}

to denote the set of sensor locations. Note that we often have

|{\bm{\varOmega}}|=N\ll IJ;

i.e., only a small number of sensors are available.
We assume that every sensor acquires the full PSD
\underline{\bm{X}}(i_{s},j_{s},:)
at its location
(i_{s},j_{s})
, where
s=1,\ldots,N
.
If the sensors are able to transmit real-valued feedback to the fusion center,
the goal of SC is to recover the full
\underline{\bm{X}}
from the tensor fibers
\{\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,:)\}_{(i,j)\in{\bm{\varOmega}}}
at the fusion center.

II-B
1
Block-term Tensor Modeling and Provable Recovery

The recent work
[
6
]
proposed a recoverability-guaranteed SC method from a block-term tensor completion viewpoint.

[
6
]
.

The model in
[
6
]
starts by assuming that power propagation is coherent over the frequency band of interest, which in general holds when the ratio between the bandwidth of the frequencies of interest and its central frequency is not large
[
26
,
12
,
22
,
23
]
. Under such circumstances, the radio map can be decomposed into the latent factors associated with spatial and spectral information, respectively
[
6
,
7
,
2
,
10
]
:

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,k)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}(i,j)\bm{c}_{r}(k)\Longleftrightarrow\underline{\bm{X}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{S}_{r}\circ\bm{c}_{r},

(1)

where
\bm{S}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{I\times J}
is the
spatial loss field
(SLF) of emitter
r
,
\bm{c}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
is the PSD of emitter
r
, and
\circ
denotes the outer product. The SLF captures the spatial power propagation characteristics of an emitter, and the PSD reflects the emitter’s spectral band occupancy.

The work
[
6
]
modeled each SLF
\bm{S}_{r}
in (
1
) as a low-rank matrix, i.e.,

\bm{S}_{r}=\bm{A}_{r}\bm{B}_{r}^{\top},

where
\bm{A}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times L}
and
\bm{B}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times L}
with
{\rm rank}(\bm{A}_{r})={\rm rank}(\bm{B}_{r})=L\ll\min\{I,J\}
.
Fig.
2
illustrates the model.
This low-rank model of
\bm{S}_{r}
connected (
1
) with a tensor model, namely, the
block-term tensor decomposition
(BTD) with multilinear rank-
(L,L,1)
model
[
27
]
.

The model is well-motivated, as the individual SLFs often exhibit high correlations across the spatial domain, making
\bm{S}_{r}
’s approximately low-rank; see
[
6
]
for numerical evidence.

Using the BTD model of the radio map, the SC problem is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\{\bm{c}_{r},\bm{A}_{r},\bm{B}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R}}\left\|\underline{\bm{M}}_{\text{sens}}\circledast\left(\underline{\bm{X}}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\bm{A}_{r}\bm{B}_{r}^{\top})\circ\bm{c}_{r}\right)\right\|_{\rm F}^{2},

(2)

where
\underline{\bm{M}}_{\text{sens}}
is a sensing mask tensor such that
\underline{\bm{M}}_{\text{sens}}(i,j,:)=\bm{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}

(i,j)\in{\bm{\varOmega}}
and
\underline{\bm{M}}_{\text{sens}}(i,j,:)=\bm{0}

Notably,
[
6
]
leveraged the BTD model’s essential uniqueness to show that the spatio-spectral radio map is provably recoverable under both random and
regular

Deep Generative Model (DGM)-Based Provable SC

Spectrum cartography using deep generative model from

In recent years, deep learning-based SC attracted much attention. The reason is that deep neural networks can represent very complex and heavily shadowed scenarios in a parsimonious way, and thus can boost performance of SC in challenging scenarios.
However, most early attempts, e.g., those in
[
8
,
18
]
, formulated the SC problem as a supervised inpainting network learning problem. This formulation encountered training and generalization challenges, as the radio maps often have an extremely large latent state space. In addition, there is a lack of theoretical support to this line of work.

:
Using simulated SLFs to learn a DGM of the SLFs via GAN.

To exploit the representation power of deep neural networks with recoverability guarantees,
the work
[
7
]
provided an alternative solution.
The method can be regarded as an extension of the model in
[
6
]
.
Realizing that the low-rank model for the SLFs in
[

may not always hold in practice,
[
7

modeled the SLF of every emitter using a data-driven DGM. To be specific,
[
7
]
proposed to learn a DGM of the SLF by simulating a large number of SLFs following a physical model. Then, every
\bm{S}_{r}
can be approximated as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{r}\approx\bm{g}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{z}_{r}),

(3)

where
\bm{g}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{I\times J}
is a deep generative neural network that maps the “latent embedding”
\bm{z}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
such that
D\ll IJ
to an SLF—see Fig.
3
.
The DGM is learned off-line, e.g., via autoencoder, variational autoencoder (VAE), or generative adversarial network (GAN); see Fig.
4
.
Using this learned DGM
\bm{g}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{z}_{r})
, the SC problem was cast as follows:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\{\bm{c}_{r},\bm{z}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R}}\left\|\underline{\bm{M}}_{\text{sens}}\circledast\left(\underline{\bm{X}}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{g}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{z}_{r})\circ\bm{c}_{r}\right)\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}.

(4)

The method used a DGM to describe each
individual
SLF (instead of the entire radio map as in
[
18
,
6
]
). Consequently, the DGM is much easier to learn in the training stage compared to the networks in
[
8
,
18
]
, which used a neural networks to learn the
aggregated
radio map from multiple emitters. This is because each SLF has a much smaller “state space” compared to that of the aggregated radio map; see more discussions in
[
7
]
.
The work in
[
7
]
also showed that (
4
) guarantees recovering the radio map under reasonable conditions.

Remark 1

The BTD and DGM based methods have their respective advantages and disadvantages. The BTD model is simple to implement, but
the
key limitation
lies in the designated low-rank prior for the SLFs. The low rank of
\bm{S}_{r}
could be violated when the spatial correlation becomes weak—which typically happens when the environment has heavy shadowing effects, e.g., in urban areas.
The DGM-based approach is capable of “encoding” complex, non-analytical prior information using the neural representation of
\bm{g}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot)
, which is suitable for modeling heavily shadowed cases.
In addition, the DGM parameterization may substantially reduce the number of unknown parameters (if
{\rm card}(\bm{z}_{r})=D\ll IJ
), which can greatly reduce the sample complexity of SC.
However, the price to pay is that an extra offline training stage is needed. To generate training data for learning
\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
, a certain level of awareness of the shadowing situation is required. These were not needed in the BTD work
[
6
]
. Hence, both models are useful and meaningful. The choice of the model depends on the scenario of interest.

II-C

Challenges - Lack of Quantized SC Approaches

Most of the previous SC works, including the BTD and DGM based methods, assumed that real-valued measurements (e.g.,
\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,:)
for all
(i,j)\in\bm{\Omega}
) can be transmitted to the fusion center (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
). However, sending real-valued measurements is costly in terms of communication overhead. It is much more realistic that the sensors send quantized measurements. Quantized spectrum sensing was studied before (see, e.g.,
[
28
,
29
]
and the “frugal sensing” works
[
20
,
21
]
) but the spatial information was not taken into consideration. Quantized SC was tackled in
[
10
]
using kernel regression. However,
[
10
]
assumed that the emitters’ PSDs are known, but estimating the PSDs itself is a hard problem
[
22
,
23
]
.

In addition to the lack of effective methodology,
the theoretical aspects of quantized SC have yet to be studied. While the DGM-based framework in
[
7
]
and the BTD-based framework in
[
6
]
provided provable recoverability of the radio map with real-valued measurements, it remains an open question how to extend these results to handle quantized measurements without losing recoverability supports.

Proposed Approach

In this work, we propose a
quantized SC
framework where the sensors only feedback heavily quantized measurements.
In particular, we will design a system that can flexibly work with the BTD and DGM models of the radio map in
[
6
]
and
[
7
]

III-A

Sensing and Quantization

Following the setup in Sec.
II-A
, we assume that the sensor located at
(i,j)
can acquire the PSD of the received signal, i.e.,
\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,:)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. In addition, we consider the scenario where the sensors send a quantized version of
\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,:)
. To be more specific, we employ a quantizer

{\cal Q}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}

that maps each element of the PSD received at
(i,j)
to an integer, i.e.,

\underline{\bm{Y}}(i,j,k)={\cal Q}(\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,k)),~k=1,\ldots,K

where
\underline{\bm{Y}}(i,j,:)\in\mathbb{Z}^{K}
is the quantized PSD.

We employ the Gaussian quantization strategy that is widely in the literature
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
.
To be specific, the quantization strategy is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{Y}}(i,j,k)=\mathcal{Q}(h(\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,k))+\underline{\bm{V}}(i,j,k))

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(x)=q,\text{ if }b_{q-1}<x\leq b_{q},\quad q\in[Q],

where
\underline{\bm{V}}(i,j,k)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
for all
i,j,k
are i.i.d. zero-mean Gaussian noise, and
\{b_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
are the pre-specified quantization bins (i.e.,
[b_{q-1},b_{q}]
is the
q
th quantization interval). Here, the function
h(\cdot)
is an invertible transformation function,

\displaystyle h(x)=\log(x+a),

(6)

where
a>0
. For notation simplicity, we slightly abuse the notation by applying
h
to both entries and tensors, i.e.,
h(\underline{\bm{X}})
means taking the transform in (
6
) for each entry of
\underline{\bm{X}}
.
Note that the function
h(\cdot)
was not used in classic Gaussian quantization. In this work, we use this function to control the dynamic range of the data (also see Sec.
IV-C
for more discussions).
Note that as
h
is invertible, it does not lose information of
\underline{\bm{X}}
; i.e., recovering
h(\underline{\bm{X}})
from the quantized data recovers
\underline{\bm{X}}
.

Quantization with artificial noise like in (
5
) is called
dithering
in signal processing
[
37
]
. Dithering is known to be beneficial for retaining more information about the original signal in the quantized version by reducing the correlation between the quantization error and original signal
[
38
]
.

III-B

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Let
\underline{\bm{M}}=h(\underline{\bm{X}})
be the transformed measurements of radio map. The quantized observations
\underline{\bm{Y}}
has the following distribution:

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{Y}}(i,j,k)=q,

\displaystyle\text{ with probability}~f_{q}(\underline{\bm{M}}(i,j,k)),

\displaystyle\forall(i,j)\in{\bm{\varOmega}},k\in[K]

(7)

where
f_{q}
is defined as,

\displaystyle f_{q}(\underline{\bm{M}}(i,j,k))={\mathbb{P}}(\underline{\bm{Y}}(i,j,k)=q~|~\underline{\bm{M}}(i,j,k))

\displaystyle~=\Phi(b_{q}-\underline{\bm{M}}(i,j,k))-\Phi(b_{q-1}-\underline{\bm{M}}(i,j,k)),

(8)

in which
\Phi(x)
is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the zero-mean and
\sigma^{2}
-variance Gaussian variable (i.e., the CDF of the dithering noise in (
5
)).
Using (
III-B
) and a certain parameterization of the radio map, one can formulate the SC problem as an MLE problem. In the next two subsections, we will use the BTD model in
[
6
]
and the DGM model in
[
7
]
to formulate two MLE criteria.

III-B
1
BTD-Based Quantized SC

As in
[
6
]
, we first represent the radio map using the BTD model (see Fig.
2
), i.e.,

\underline{\bm{X}}^{\rm BTD}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\bm{A}_{r}\bm{B}_{r}^{\top})\circ\bm{c}_{r}.

Using the described sensing and quantization procedure as in the previous section, the fusion center receives a quantized and incomplete tensor
\underline{\bm{Y}}(\bm{\varOmega},:)
.
To recover the ground-truth radio map, we consider the following MLE:

MLE via BTD

\displaystyle\minimize_{(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C})\in{\cal S}^{\rm BTD}}~F^{\rm BTD}_{\bm{\varOmega},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C}),

(9)

where the objective function is defined as

\displaystyle F^{\rm BTD}_{\bm{\varOmega},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C})=

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{(i,j)\in{\bm{\varOmega}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\mathbb{1}_{[\underline{\bm{Y}}(i,j,k)=q]}\log(f_{q}\big(h(\underline{\bm{X}}^{\rm BTD}(i,j,k))\big)),

in which we have

\underline{\bm{X}}^{\rm BTD}(i,j,k)=\left[\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\bm{A}_{r}\bm{B}_{r}^{\top})\circ\bm{c}_{r}\right]_{i,j,k}
,
\bm{A}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{I\times L},\bm{B}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J\times L}
and
\bm{C}=[\bm{c}_{1},\ldots,\bm{c}_{R}]
. In addition, we have used the notation
\bm{A}=[\bm{A}_{1},\ldots,\bm{A}_{R}]
and
\bm{B}=[\bm{B}_{1},\ldots,\bm{B}_{R}]
.
The constraint set is defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal S}^{\rm BTD}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C})~|~\bm{A}\geq\bm{0},\bm{B}\geq\bm{0},\bm{C}\geq\bm{0}

(10)

\displaystyle\|\bm{A}_{r}\|_{\rm F}\leq\sqrt{\beta},\|\bm{B}_{r}\|_{\rm F}\leq\sqrt{\beta},\|\bm{c}_{r}\|_{2}\leq\kappa,\forall r\in[R]\bigg\}.

The constraints in (
10
)
mean that the SLFs and the PSDs are both nonnegative and bounded (with
\|\bm{S}_{r}\|_{\rm F}\leq\beta
)—which are mild assumptiions per their physical meaning.
Particularly, we assume that the nonnegativity and boundedness of
\bm{S}_{r}=\bm{A}_{r}\bm{B}_{r}^{\top}
are realized via having constraints on the latent factors of the BTD model. We also define the corresponding set:

\displaystyle{\cal X}^{\rm BTD}=

(11)

\displaystyle\bigg\{\underline{\bm{X}}~\bigg|~\underline{\bm{X}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\bm{A}_{r}\bm{B}_{r}^{\top})\circ\bm{c}_{r},~(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C})\in{\cal S}^{\rm BTD}\bigg\}.

III-B
2
DGM-Based Quantized SC

As mentioned, the BTD model-based approach may not work well when the spatial region exhibits heavy shadowing
[
7
]
.
In such cases, it is natural to employ the DGM-based model in (
3
) to parameterize the radio map, i.e.,

\underline{\bm{X}}^{\rm DGM}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{r})\circ\bm{c}_{r},

(12)

where
\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
is learned in an off-line manner as in
[
7
]
; see Fig.
3
.
The radio map model in (
12
) leads to the following MLE-based recovery criterion:

MLE via DGM

\displaystyle\minimize_{(\bm{C},\bm{Z})\in{\cal S}^{\rm DGM}}~F_{\bm{\varOmega},\underline{\bm{Y}}}^{\rm DGM}(\bm{Z},\bm{C}),

(13)

where we have

\displaystyle F_{\bm{\varOmega},\underline{\bm{Y}}}^{\rm DGM}(\bm{Z},\bm{C})=

(14)

\displaystyle-\sum_{(i,j)\in\bm{\varOmega}}~\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\mathbb{1}_{[{\underline{\bm{Y}}(i,j,k)=q}]}\log(f_{q}\big(h(\underline{\bm{X}}^{\rm DGM}(i,j,k))\big)),

in which
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\rm DGM}(i,j,k)=[\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{g}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{z}_{r})\circ\bm{c}_{r}]_{i,j,k}
,
\bm{C}=[\bm{c}_{1},\ldots,\bm{c}_{R}]
, and
\bm{Z}=[\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{R}]
.
The constraint set is defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal S}^{\rm DGM}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{(\bm{C},\bm{Z})~|~\bm{g}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{r})\geq\bm{0},\bm{C}\geq\bm{0},

(15)

\displaystyle\|\bm{g}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{r})\|_{\rm F}\leq\beta,\|\bm{c}_{r}\|_{2}\leq\kappa,\forall r\in[R]\bigg\},

where
\beta
and
\kappa
are positive constants.
Similar as before, we define a corresponding set in the data domain:

\displaystyle{\cal X}^{\rm DGM}=

(16)

\displaystyle\bigg\{\underline{\bm{X}}~\bigg|~\underline{\bm{X}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{r})\circ\bm{c}_{r},~(\bm{Z},\bm{C})\in{\cal S}^{\rm DGM}\bigg\}.

As in the continuous measurement case (cf. Remark
1
), the BTD-based formulation in (
9
) is better suited for cases where training data is unavailable or the training cost is not affordable.
The DGM-based formulation in (
13
) has a more expressive model to attain enhanced recovery accuracy, especially under challenging scenarios, e.g., heavy shadowing.

IV-A

Algorithm For BTD-Based MLE

To tackle Problem (
9
), we consider the following reformulated approximation:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C}}

\displaystyle~\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{\varOmega}},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C})

(17)

\displaystyle{\rm subject~to}

\displaystyle~\bm{A}\geq\bm{0},\bm{B}\geq\bm{0},\bm{C}\geq\bm{0},

where the objective function is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{\varOmega}},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C})=

\displaystyle F^{\rm BTD}_{\bm{\varOmega},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C})+\lambda_{1}r_{1}(\bm{A})

\displaystyle+\lambda_{2}r_{2}(\bm{B})+\lambda_{3}r_{3}(\bm{C}),

in which the regularization terms are as follows:

r_{i}(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{\rm F}^{2},~i=1,2,3.

Note that promoting small

and

Hence, such regularization terms

Nonetheless, using the regularized reformulation instead of the constraints in (

We propose a block coordinate descent (BCD) procedure to handle (

\displaystyle+\lambda_{3}r_{3}(\bm{C})),\bm{0}),

(18)

where

\bm{C}

\bm{C}

IV-B

Algorithm For DGM-Based MLE

Problem (
13

\displaystyle\minimize_{\bm{C},\bm{Z}}

\displaystyle~\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{\varOmega}},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{Z},\bm{C}),

(21)

\displaystyle{\rm subject~to}

\displaystyle~\bm{C}\geq\bm{0},

where

\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{\varOmega}},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{Z},\bm{C})=F^{\rm DGM}_{\bm{\varOmega},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{Z},\bm{C})+\mu_{1}q_{1}(\bm{Z})+\mu_{2}q_{2}(\bm{C}).

In practice,

\bm{Z}
(and thus also regularizing
\bm{S}_{r}=\bm{g}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{r})

q_{1}(\bm{Z})=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|\bm{z}_{r}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{Z}\|_{\rm F}^{2},

which would encourage the solution to reduce the Euclidean norm of the found SLFs, so that the specification in (
15

Similar as before, we set
q_{2}(\bm{C})=\|\bm{C}\|_{\rm F}^{2}
.
The nonnegativity constraint on
\bm{C}
is again added to reflect its physical meaning. Note that
\bm{Z}
needs not to be nonnegative and thus there is no such constraint on the latent embeddings. The SLFs are nonnegative and bounded. Hence,
\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
uses the sigmoid activation functions at the output layer. Using sigmoid makes
\|\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. This does not lose generality or hurt the recoverability.
To see this, assume that
\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}=\bm{S}_{r}
and
\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}=\bm{c}_{r}
are estimated perfectly. One can always let
\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}=\nicefrac{{\bm{S}_{r}}}{{\|\bm{S}_{r}\|_{\infty}}}
(so that
\|\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}\|_{\infty}=1
) and
\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}=\|\bm{S}_{r}\|_{\infty}\bm{c}_{r}
. Such scaling/counter-scaling does not change the outer product; i.e.,
\widehat{\bm{S}}_{r}\circ\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}=\bm{S}_{r}\circ\bm{c}_{r}
still holds—which means
\widehat{\underline{\bm{X}}}=\underline{\bm{X}}
.

The BCD algorithm for tackling Problem (
21

17

in the
k

\bm{C}
using gradient projection:

\displaystyle\bm{C}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow

(22)

\displaystyle\max(\bm{C}^{(k)}-\upsilon_{2}^{(k)}\overline{\nabla}_{\bm{C}}(\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{\varOmega}},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{Z}^{(k)},\bm{C}^{(k)})+\mu_{2}q_{2}(\bm{C})),\bm{0}).

Then, the
\bm{Z}
-update is carried out as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{Z}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\bm{Z}^{(k)}

\displaystyle-\upsilon_{1}^{(k)}\overline{\nabla}_{\bm{Z}}(\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{\varOmega}},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{Z}^{(k)},\bm{C}^{(k)})

\displaystyle+\mu_{1}q_{1}(\bm{Z}));

(23)

In this case, as a DGM is involved, the gradient-related directions w.r.t.
\bm{Z}
can be found by back propagation-based methods, also using numerical gradient-finding tools.
Again, when the algorithm stops, we obtain the estimated radio map
\widehat{\underline{\bm{X}}}
via

\widehat{\underline{\bm{X}}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}g_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{r})\circ\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r},

where
\widehat{\bm{z}}_{r}
and
\widehat{\bm{c}}_{r}
are the solutions found by the algorithm.

The algorithm in (
22
)-(
IV-B
) is referred to as the
quantized SC via DGM
(
QuantSC-DGM)

IV-C

Quantization Interval Design

One challenge for quantizing radio maps is that the PSD values across space are extremely skewed; see Fig.
5
. Directly applying simple quantizers (e.g., the uniform quantizer) does not work well.
As mentioned, we propose to apply the
\log
-transformation in (
6
) before quantization.
Recall that the transformation is expressed as
h(x)=\log(x+a)
with
a>0
, which “condenses” the dynamic range of the radio map. Log-transformation is commonly used in quantization and data pre-processing for nonnegative data; see, e.g.,
[
43
]
.

After the log-transformation, the
b_{q}
’s in (
5
) can be determined by making every interval roughly contain the same number of data points (i.e.,
\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,k)
). This strategy was employed in the matrix and tensor completion literature
[
44
,
45
]
and was proven effective. The intuition is that we want to make “maximum utilization” of all quantization levels.
In an SC system, the
\{b_{q}\}
should be determined by the system designers prior to deployment.
Our suggested steps are as follows: 1) The system generates a set of simulated radio maps from a range of environment parameters that are considered reasonably reflecting the RF situation of the region of interest. 2) Using the simulated data, the system learns
b_{q}
for
q\in[Q]
using the following principle:

\displaystyle b_{q}=\inf\left\{m\bigg|\frac{q-1}{Q-1}\leq F(m),m=h(x)\right\},\forall q=2,\ldots,Q-1,

where
F(m)
is the empirical CDF of the simulated
\underline{\bm{M}}
’s entries, and we set
b_{0}=-\infty
and
b_{Q}=+\infty
. We calculate bin boundaries from a large number (e.g., 1,000) of simulated radio maps and take average.
3) The fusion center broadcasts
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{Q}\}
to all the sensors. Then, the sensors use the
b_{q}
’s to implement their quantizer.

Technical Preparations

It is readily seen that the following fact holds:

Fact 1

For
\underline{\bm{X}}\in{\cal X}^{\rm BTD}
and
\underline{\bm{X}}\in{\cal X}^{\rm DGM}

and

\displaystyle\|\underline{\bm{X}}\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha,

\alpha=\alpha^{\rm BTD}

\alpha=\alpha^{\rm DGM}
if
\underline{\bm{X}}\in{\cal X}^{\rm DGM}
.

Proof:

The claim holds as the latent factors constituting
\underline{\bm{X}}
in

and

are bounded; see (
10
) and (
15
).
∎

As our framework uses a log-transformation to pre-process the sensor-acquired data, we will need the following lemma in our later analysis:

Lemma 1

Let
h(\underline{\bm{X}})
denote a tensor such that

[h(\underline{\bm{X}})]_{i,j,k}=\log(\underline{\bm{X}}(i,j,k)+a)

\underline{\bm{X}}\geq\bm{0}

.
Suppose that
\|\underline{\bm{X}}\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha

\underline{\bm{X}}\neq\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha+a}\leq\frac{\|h(\underline{\bm{X}})-h(\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\|_{\rm F}}{\|\underline{\bm{X}}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime}\|_{\rm F}}\leq\frac{1}{a}.

(25)

The lemma says that
h(\cdot)
exhibits continuity when the argument is nonnegative.
The parameter
a>0
is used to present the mapped value goes to
-\infty
, which can be an inconsequential small value (i.e., a value that does not dominate the input signal) in practice.
The proof is relegated to Appendix
B
.
We will use the following fact:

Fact 2

Let
{\cal X}\in\{{\cal X}^{\rm BTD},{\cal X}^{\rm DGM}\}
and
{\cal M}=\{h(\underline{\bm{X}})~|~\forall\underline{\bm{X}}\in{\cal X}\}.

Then, if
b_{\ell}\neq b_{j}
for any
\ell\neq j

\forall\ell,j\in[Q]
, there always exist bounded constants
{\sf U_{\alpha}}
,
{\sf L_{\alpha}}
and
{\sf F_{\alpha}}
such that

\displaystyle{\sf U_{\alpha}}:=

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\max_{\ell\in[Q]}\log\left(\frac{1}{f_{\ell}(m)}\right)

(26a)

\displaystyle{\sf L_{\alpha}}:=

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\max_{\ell\in[Q]}\frac{\left|\dot{f}_{\ell}(m)\right|}{f_{\ell}(m)}

(26b)

\displaystyle{\sf F_{\alpha}}:=

\displaystyle\inf_{m}\max_{\ell\in[Q]}\frac{(\dot{f}_{\ell}(m))^{2}}{f_{\ell}(m)},

(26c)

where
\alpha
is as defined in Fact
1
.

The proof of the Fact
2
is presented in Appendix
C
.
Fact
2
means that the if our radio map measurements are bounded, then

does not change too sharply or (for at least one

) too slowly, reflected in

and

The existence of these parameters makes the function locally analogous to functions that have gradient-Lipschitz continuity and strong convexity; see discussions in

V-B

Recoverability Under The BTD Model

We first analyze the recoverability of (
17

Before we proceed,
it should be mentioned that in the previous sections,

we slightly abused the notations by using
\underline{\bm{X}}
,

Assumption 1

\displaystyle\min_{{\underline{\bm{X}}}\in\mathcal{X}^{\rm BTD}}\|\underline{\bm{X}}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}\|_{\infty}\leq\nu^{\rm BTD},

Theorem 1

Suppose that Assumption

Assume that
{\bm{\varOmega}}
is uniformly sampled from
[I]\times[J]

\displaystyle(\bm{A}^{\star},\bm{B}^{\star},\bm{C}^{\star})=\arg\min_{(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C})\in{\cal S}^{\rm BTD}}~F^{\rm BTD}_{\bm{\varOmega},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C}),

Remark 2

V-C

Recoverability Under The DGM Model

The proof under the DGM of the radio map (
13
) follows a similar technical route, except that the DGM’s “complexity measure” needs to be carefully derived. As in the previous section, we make the following assumption:

Assumption 2

There exists a constant
\nu^{\rm DGM}\in[0,+\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\min_{{\underline{\bm{X}}}\in\mathcal{X}^{\rm DGM}}\|{\underline{\bm{X}}}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}\|_{\infty}\leq\nu^{\rm DGM}.

(30)

Ideally, if
\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
is a universal function approximator,
\nu^{\rm DGM}
would have been zero (provided that other types of noise do not exist).
However, in practice,
\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
is represented by a neural network with finite depth/width, which means that it is never universal and thus a nonzero
\nu^{\rm DGM}
exists.
Note that
\nu^{\rm DGM}
can be decreased if the neural network
\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
is more expressive. A more expressive neural network often admits a deeper and wider neural architecture, which has a increased number of parameters.

We also make the following structural assumption:

Assumption 3

The generator network is an
L
-layer neural network

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{z})=\text{mat}(\bm{\zeta}_{L}(\bm{A}_{L}(...\bm{\zeta}_{1}(\bm{A}_{1}\bm{z})))),

(31)

where
{\rm mat}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{IJ}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{I\times J}

reshapes a vector to a matrix (i.e., the inverse operation of vectorization),
\bm{A}_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\ell}\times D_{\ell-1}}
is the network weight in the
\ell
th layer, in which
D_{0}=D
and
D_{L}=IJ
,
\bm{\zeta}_{\ell}(.)=[\zeta(.),...,\zeta(.)]^{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\ell}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\ell}}
is a
\phi_{\ell}
-Lipschitz activation function with
\zeta(.):{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and
P=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\phi_{\ell}\|\bm{A}_{\ell}\|_{2}<\infty.

In addition, the latent embedding
\bm{z}
is from a bounded set, i.e.,
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}:\|\bm{z}\|_{2}\leq q\}
.

Assumption
3
follows the same setting in
[
7
]
.
This assumption characterizes the structure of generative neural network. Note that many commonly used activation functions are Lipschitz continuous, e.g., ReLU, sigmoid, and tanh. In addition, (
31
) subsumes many deep network architectures as its special cases, e.g., the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) and the convolutional neural network (CNN).

With the assumptions, we show the following recoverability theorem:

Theorem 2

Suppose that Assumption
2
and
3
hold.
Assume that
{\bm{\varOmega}}
is uniformly sampled from
[I]\times[J]
and that
|{\bm{\varOmega}}|=N
.
Define
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\star}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}^{\star}_{r})\circ\bm{c}_{r}^{\star}
, where

\displaystyle(\bm{C}^{\star},\bm{Z}^{\star})=\arg\min_{(\bm{C},\bm{Z})\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm DGM}}~F^{\rm DGM}_{\bm{\varOmega},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{C},\bm{Z}),

(32)

is an optimal solution of the
MLE via DGM
criterion in (
13
).
Then with probability at least
1-2\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\|\underline{\bm{X}}^{\star}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}\|_{\rm F}^{2}}{IJK}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8C_{1}C_{2}(1+\tau)}{K}\sqrt{\frac{R}{N}}+{\sf U_{\alpha}}C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{2N}}

\displaystyle+{\sf U_{\alpha}}C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{8\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}{N}}+C_{1}C_{2}\nu^{\rm DGM},

(33)

where

\displaystyle\tau=3\sqrt{K\log(\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{R}\kappa(\beta+\kappa))+D\log(3\sqrt{R}Pq(\beta+\kappa))},

and
\beta,\kappa
are defined in (
15
). The constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are given by
C_{1}=\nicefrac{{4(\alpha+a)^{2}}}{{{\sf F_{\alpha}}}},~{\rm and}~C_{2}=\nicefrac{{{\sf L_{\alpha}}}}{{a}}.

The proof of Theorem
2
is presented in Appendix
D-B
. Theorem
2
shows that the optimization criterion in (
13
) ensures the recoverability of the ground-truth radio map
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}
—even when the radio map is parameterized by a learned DGM other than a classic low-rank model.
There are two primary sources of errors in our recovery process. The first error occurs due to the limited fiber samples of radio maps measurement, also referred to as generalization (or statistical) error, which is given by the first term on the R.H.S. of (
2
). The second type of error is induced by the representation error of the DGM, i.e.,
\nu^{\rm DGM}
, which is the last term in (
2
).
One can see that the recovered radio map becomes closer to the ground truth with an increased sample size at the rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
. When the DGM becomes more complex (e.g., when the depth/width of network is increased), the approximation error
\nu^{\rm DGM}
decreases. However, the increased network complexity makes
\tau
larger. This presents a reasonable trade-off between model and sample complexities.
In short, one hopes to use a reasonably complex DGM so that
\nu^{\rm DGM}
is small, but not an overly expressive DGM that might lead to over-fitting effects.

Experiments

VI-A

Synthetic Data Experiments

VI-A
1
Data Generation

For data generation, we first consider the 2D geographical region of
50\times 50~{\rm m}^{2}
that has
51\times 51
grids. The region is discretized using indices
\{0,1,\ldots 50\}\times\{0,1,\ldots 50\}
such that
I=J=51
. The spectral domain has
K=64
subbands. We generate the radio maps by first generating the SLF of each emitters using the joint path loss model and spatial correlated log-normal shadowing model
[
48
]
.
The two key parameters for this model are the decorrelation distance
X_{c}
and the shadowing variance
\eta
. Larger
X_{c}
and smaller
\eta
correspond to milder shadowing environment; see
[
7
]
for more details. The PSDs are also generated following the same method as in
[
6
,
7
]
.

VI-A
2
DGM Learning

We learn the DGM using GAN. We set
D=256
. The latent embedding
\bm{z}_{r}
is sampled from the standard Gaussian distribution. The detailed architecture of the GAN is in Appendix
A
.
To generate training samples for learning the DGM, we create 5,000 random SLFs using random emitter locations, a range of path loss coefficients,
X_{c}
and
\eta
in [2,2.5], [30,100] and [3,8], respectively.
We then train our GAN using
Adam
optimizer with a batch size of 128 with 250 epochs. The initial learning rates for the discriminator and generator are set to be 0.0004 and 0.0001 respectively.

VI-A
3
Algorithm Settings

We initialize
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
as zero matrices in the BTD model. For the DGM model, we initialize
\bm{z}_{r}
using the standard normal distribution. For both models, we initialize the elements of
\bm{C}
using the uniform distribution between 0 and 1.
Under both the DGM and BTD models, we stop the algorithms when the relative change of the cost function is smaller than
10^{-3}
or when the BCD algorithms reach 300 iterations.
For both of our reformulations in (
21
) and (
17
), the regularization terms are all set to be
10^{-3}
. We set
L=10
for the BTD model following
[
6
]
.
For optimization, we use the
Adam
optimizer for both DGM and BTD block updates. For both models, we set the initial learning rates for
\bm{C}
to be
0.003
and for the other variables to be
0.006
.

VI-A
4
Quantization

We set
a=10^{-6}
in
h(x)=\log(x+a)
. For quantization, we set the noise variance using a validation set consisting of 20 simulated radio maps. The validation set is generated with shadowing parameters
X_{c}\in[30,100]
and
\eta\in[3.0,8.0]
. The recovering algorithms are applied to the validation set with various
\sigma^{2}\in[0.5,4.0]
for different bits cases and the average best-performing
\sigma^{2}=1.7
is selected.
The quantization bins are constructed using
1,000
simulated radio maps data generated following the description in Sec.
VI-A2
. The
b_{q}
’s are constructed using the method in Sec.
IV-C
for
B\in[1,8]
, where
B=\log_{2}(Q-1)
bits.

VI-A
5
Performance Metrics

We use
log-domain normalized reconstruction error
(LNRE) as our metric:

\displaystyle{\rm LNRE}=\frac{\|\widehat{\underline{\bm{M}}}-\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural}\|^{2}_{\rm F}}{\|\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural}\|^{2}_{\rm F}},

(34)

where
\widehat{\underline{\bm{M}}}=h(\widehat{\underline{\bm{X}}})
is the estimated log-transformed the radio map. The LNRE is an appropriate metric for skewed data as it prevents a few larger values in the
\underline{\bm{X}}
from dominating the performance evaluation. All the LNREs are averaged over 10 random trials.

[

, namely, “

[

[

based on kernel regression for quantized SC (referred to as “

VI-B

The data was obtained within a

The leftmost column in Fig.

This type of mixed deterministic (rooms) and random (hallway) missing pattern is not exactly covered by the conditions made in our recoverability theorems, but the dataset can still be employed to test the algorithms. The results can indicate their usefulness in real-world settings and robustness to violation of the conditions in the theorems.

VII
Conclusion

A novel framework has been introduced for SC that operates effectively with heavily quantized sensor measurements. Unlike previous approaches to provable SC, which assumed full-resolution real-valued measurements, the new maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)-based framework uses measurements discretized by a Gaussian quantizer. It is compatible with both BTD and DGM-based radio map representations, with the former being training-free, and the latter showing higher resilience to heavy shadowing. The recoverability of the framework has been characterized under realistic conditions, such as imperfect radio map modeling. Simulations and real-data experiments have demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The proposed quantized SC framework offers a practical and realistic solution to radio map estimation, with provable guarantees.

Let us assume that there are positive
\ell_{\min}
and
\ell_{\max}
such that the following holds:

\displaystyle\ell_{\rm min}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|h(x)-h(y)|}{|x-y|}\leq\ell_{\rm max}

(35)

\displaystyle\implies\ell_{\rm min}|x-y|

\displaystyle\leq|h(x)-h(y)|\leq\ell_{\rm max}|x-y|.

(36)

Applying the above to the coresponding entries of the two tensors
\underline{\bm{X}}
and
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime}
, and then squaring and summing over all the entries, we have

\displaystyle\ell^{2}_{\rm min}\|\underline{\bm{X}}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leq\|h(\underline{\bm{X}})-h(\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\|^{2}_{\rm F}\leq\ell^{2}_{\rm max}\|\underline{\bm{X}}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\rm F}

\displaystyle{\implies}\ell_{\rm min}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|h(\underline{\bm{X}})-h(\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\|_{\rm F}}{\|\underline{\bm{X}}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime}\|_{\rm F}}\leq\ell_{\rm max}.

(37)

To find out
\ell_{\min}
and
\ell_{\rm max}
, consider the following:

\displaystyle\min_{x,y\in[0,\alpha]}\frac{|\log(x+a)-\log(y+a)|}{|x-y|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|h(\underline{\bm{X}})-h(\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\|_{\rm F}}{\|\underline{\bm{X}}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime}\|_{\rm F}}\leq\max_{x,y\in[0,\alpha]}\frac{|\log(x+a)-\log(y+a)|}{|x-y|}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\implies}}\min_{x,y\in[0,\alpha]}\frac{\log(x+a)-\log(y+a)}{|x-y|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|h(\underline{\bm{X}})-h(\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\|_{\rm F}}{\|\underline{\bm{X}}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime}\|_{\rm F}}\leq\max_{x,y\in[0,\alpha]}\frac{\log(x+a)-\log(y+a)}{x-y},

(38)

where
x
and
y
represent two arbitrary entries of
\underline{\bm{X}}
and
\underline{\bm{X}}^{\prime}
, respectively, (b) holds because the numerator and the denominator have the same sign.

Using the mean value theorem, there exists
z\in[x,y]
such that

\dot{h}(z)=\frac{\log(x+a)-\log(y+a)}{x-y},

where
\dot{h}(z)
is the derivative of
h
at
z
.
We know that

\dot{h}(z)=\frac{1}{z+a}.

Since
\dot{h}(z)
is monotonically increasing with

, the minimum and maximum of (

\displaystyle{}\min_{x,y\in[0,\alpha]}\frac{\log(x+a)-\log(y+a)}{x-y}

Similarly, we have

Combining the above with (

\displaystyle|F_{{\bm{\varOmega}},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural})-{\mathbb{E}}_{\omega,\underline{\bm{Y}}}[g(\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural}_{\omega:},\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\omega:})]|\leq\sqrt{\nicefrac{{(K{\sf U_{\alpha}})^{2}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}}{{2N}}}.

(51)

For the third term in (
49
), observe that
F_{{\bm{\varOmega}},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\bm{M})
is a sample average of the samples of joint random variable
(\omega,\underline{\bm{Y}})
drawn independently from their joint distribution. And the corresponding expected value is
{\mathbb{E}}_{\omega,\underline{\bm{Y}}}[g(\underline{\bm{M}}_{\omega:},\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\omega:})]
. Hence, the third term can be bounded via Rademacher Complexity-based arguments
[
50
]
. Specifically, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the following holds

\displaystyle\sup_{\underline{\bm{M}}\in\mathcal{M}}|F_{{\bm{\varOmega}},\underline{\bm{Y}}}(\underline{\bm{M}})-{\mathbb{E}}_{\omega,\underline{\bm{Y}}}[g(\underline{\bm{M}}_{\omega:},\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\omega:})]|

\displaystyle\leq 2\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{G})+K{\sf U_{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{8\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}{N}},

(52)

where
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{G})
is the empirical Rademacher complexity of the function class
\mathcal{G}
defined as follows:

\mathcal{G}=\{(\omega,\underline{\bm{Y}})\mapsto g(\underline{\bm{M}}_{\omega:},\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\omega:})|\underline{\bm{M}}=h(\underline{\bm{X}}),\underline{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{X}\}.

Using Talagrand Lemma
[
54
, Lemma 4.2]
along with Lemma
1
and Lipschitz property of
g(\underline{\bm{M}}_{\omega:},\underline{\bm{Y}}_{\omega:})
in terms of
f_{\ell}
in Fact
2
,

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{G})\leq\frac{{\sf L_{\alpha}}}{a}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{X}),

where
{\cal X}\in\{{\cal X}^{\rm BTD},{\cal X}^{\rm DGM}\}
. Using Dudley’s entropy integral
[
55
, Lemma A.5]
, one can bound the empirical Rademacher complexity by the covering number (cf. Appendix
D-A
) of the set as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{X})\leq\zeta(\mathcal{X},\mu),

(53)

where

\displaystyle\zeta(\mathcal{X},\mu)=\inf_{\mu>0}\left(\frac{4\mu}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{12}{N}\int_{\mu}^{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{\log{\sf N}(\mathcal{X},\epsilon)}d\epsilon\right).

(54)

Combining (
49
), (
50
), (
51
), (
D-B
), and (
53
), we get

\displaystyle\text{KL}(\bm{M}^{\star}||\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural})\leq

(55)

\displaystyle\frac{2{\sf L_{\alpha}}\zeta(\mathcal{X},\mu)}{aK}+\frac{K{\sf U_{\alpha}}}{K}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{2N}}+\frac{{\sf U_{\alpha}}K}{K}\sqrt{\frac{8\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}{N}}+\frac{K{\sf L_{\alpha}}\nu}{aK}.

The relation between Hellinger distance and KL divergence
[
30
]
is as follows:

\displaystyle d_{H}^{2}(\underline{\bm{M}}^{\star},\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural})\leq\text{KL}(\underline{\bm{M}}^{\star}||\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural}).

(56)

From
[
32
, Lemma 2]
and Fact
2
, we can bound Hellinger distance using the following:

\displaystyle\frac{{\sf F_{\alpha}}}{4}\frac{\|\underline{\bm{M}}^{\star}-\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural}\|_{\rm F}^{2}}{IJK}\leq d_{H}^{2}(\underline{\bm{M}}^{\star},\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural}).

(57)

Combining (
56
), (
57
) and Lemma
1
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\|\underline{\bm{M}}^{\star}-\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural}\|_{\rm F}^{2}}{IJK}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{{\sf F_{\alpha}}}\text{KL}(\underline{\bm{M}}^{\star}||\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural})

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\|\underline{\bm{X}}^{\star}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}\|_{\rm F}^{2}}{IJK}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4(\alpha+a)^{2}}{{\sf F_{\alpha}}}\text{KL}(\underline{\bm{M}}^{\star}||\underline{\bm{M}}^{\natural}).

(58)

Let us define the following constants,

\displaystyle C_{1}=\frac{4(\alpha+a)^{2}}{{\sf F_{\alpha}}},\text{ and }C_{2}=\frac{{\sf L_{\alpha}}}{a}.

(59)

Combining (
55
), (
59
) and (
D-B
),

\displaystyle\frac{\|\underline{\bm{X}}^{\star}-\underline{\bm{X}}^{\natural}\|_{\rm F}^{2}}{IJK}\leq

(60)

\displaystyle C_{1}\bigg(\frac{2C_{2}\zeta(\mathcal{X},\mu)}{K}+{\sf U_{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{2N}}+{\sf U_{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{8\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}{N}}+C_{2}\nu\bigg).

Since

can be upper bounded as,

\displaystyle\int_{\mu}^{\sqrt{N}}(\log{\sf N}(\mathcal{X},\epsilon))^{1/2}d\epsilon

(61)
