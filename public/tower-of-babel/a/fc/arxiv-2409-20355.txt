Finding quadratic underestimators for optimal value functions of nonconvex all-quadratic problems via copositive optimization
Markus Gabl and Immanuel M. Bomze

Abstract
Modeling parts of an optimization problem as an optimal value function that depends on a top-level decision variable is a regular occurrence in optimization and an essential ingredient for methods such as Benders Decomposition. It often allows for the disentanglement of computational complexity and exploitation of special structures in the lower-level problem that define the optimal value functions. If this problem is convex, duality theory can be used to build piecewise affine models of the optimal value function over which the top-level problem can be optimized efficiently. In this text, we are interested in the optimal value function of an all-quadratic problem (also called quadratically constrained quadratic problem, QCQP) which is not necessarily convex, so that duality theory can not be applied without introducing a generally unquantifiable relaxation error. This issue can be bypassed by employing copositive reformulations of the underlying QCQP. We investigate two ways to parametrize these by the top-level variable. The first one leads to a copositive characterization of an underestimator that is sandwiched between the convex envelope of the optimal value function and that envelope’s lower-semicontinuous hull. The dual of that characterization allows us to derive affine underestimators. The second parametrization yields an alternative characterization of the optimal value function itself, which other than the original version has an exact dual counterpart. From the latter, we can derive convex and nonconvex quadratic underestimators of the optimal value function. In fact, we can show that any quadratic underestimator is associated with a dual feasible solution in a certain sense.



Keywords:
Quadratic Optimization, Conic Optimization, Benders Decomposition

Introduction

Many areas of optimization are dealing with problems of the following form

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\left\{f_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\phi(\mathbf{x})\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\right\}\quad\mbox{ with }{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\,,

(1)

where the function
\phi
is given by an inner optimization problem:

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\phi(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}}\left\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\colon\mathbf{y}\in{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x})\,\right\}\quad\mbox{with}\quad{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\colon[\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{F}}\right\}

(2)

where
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}

and
q
is a quadratic function
. Modeling optimization problems in this particular manner often arise from the desire to disentangle sources of computational complexity. For example, it might be hard to optimize over
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
simultaneously, while (
2
) may be easy to solve for a given value for
\mathbf{x}
, or this formulation may exhibit other exploitable structure.

Classical examples for such a constellation are given by two-stage stochastic optimization, robust optimization, some instances of large-scale optimization, bilevel optimization, and many more, for example applications related to Benders decomposition
[
24
]
which aim to build an approximate model of
\phi
. This is done via information gathered from evaluating
\phi
systematically at different points, used to approximately solve (
1
), mostly in an iterative manner based on said model, which is improved in every iteration.

If the optimization model defining
\phi
is convex, one can use duality theory to derive affine underestimators at any given point
\mathbf{x}
from the optimal dual objective. However, building approximations for
\phi
is usually much more challenging in the nonconvex case. The same is true for building nonlinear underestimators even in the convex regime. In this text, we will demonstrate that via copositive optimization theory, we can find affine and (convex and nonconvex) quadratic underestimators of
\phi
if it is the optimal value function of a quadratically constrained, quadratic optimization problem (QCQP).

Hence, we focus on the case where the function
f
is a, possibly
(separately in
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
or also jointly in
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
)
nonconvex, quadratic function in the variables
[\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}
and where the set-valued mapping

{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\colon[\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{F}}\right\}

as well as the set
{\mathcal{F}}
are not necessarily convex, possibly even disconnected, but of sufficient simplicity in a certain sense which we will elaborate later (see Assumptions
1
and
2
). Under these restrictions, we set out to achieve the following:

•

We show how to find affine underestimators of such a
\phi
, which are derived from a copositive characterization of a certain convex underestimator
\widehat{\phi}
of
\phi
. This new function
\widehat{\phi}
is constructed from a suitable parameterization of the copositive reformulation of the underlying QCQP and it is shown to be also an overestimator of the closure of the convex envelope of
\phi
. Some important properties of
\widehat{\phi}
are studied as well.

•

Switching to an alternative parameterization of said copositive reformulation, we propose a convexified characterization of
\phi
from which we can derive convex and nonconvex quadratic underestimators. We will show that, in a certain sense, any quadratic underestimator of
\phi
can be derived in this manner.

•

An attractive feature of our analysis is the fact that once the hurdle of finding a copositive reformulation of the underlying QCQP has been taken, the derivations involve merely dualizing an appropriate parametrization of the reformulations optimal value function in much the same way it is traditionally done in case when
\phi
were convex, to begin with. Thus, copositive optimization theory nicely interfaces between convex and nonconvex optimization.

•

Finally, we exemplify our theory in numerical experiments where we develop a Benders type method to tackle a certain nonconvex QCQP. Our experiments show that computational advantages are possible and merit further investigation in future research.

1.1
Relation to prior work

Our analysis will mostly rely on copositive optimization theory which has been used in the past to derive convex envelopes of quadratic functions over certain feasible sets, see
[
2
,
20
,
29
]
who characterized convex envelopes of quadratic functions over polyhedral sets. The present text can be seen as a generalization of their work, where we consider convex envelopes of optimal value functions over (at least in theory) arbitrary sets.

Optimal value functions of QCQPs have played a role in robust optimization as well as in semi-infinite optimization. Robust optimization where the uncertain function quadratically depends on the uncertainty parameter has been studied in
[
5
,
15
,
18
,
28
]
, where copositive optimization techniques have been used to derive conservative approximations and Benders-type solution schemes.

Similarly, techniques resembling copositive optimization have been used in order to tackle the implied optimal value functions in semi-infinite programming. For example
[
10
,
23
]
used SDP-based relaxations of such optimal value functions in order to derive for linear Benders-type cuts mentioned above.

These approaches deal with cases where the optimal value functions have feasible sets that are independent of
\mathbf{x}
, and they make some strong assumptions on the structure of the feasible set. In addition, they derive convex approximations and, in as much as they are concerned with cuts, they derive linear ones. Our analysis allows, at least in principle, for arbitrary feasible sets which may depend on
\mathbf{x}
. In addition, we also consider convex and non-convex quadratic cuts. Thus, our analysis unifies and extends prior work in this area.

Typically, the literature is focused on convex envelopes rather than

Since this object may be less familiar to some readers, we add the following proposition, which states that the closed convex envelope relates to the original function in a similar manner as the convex envelope.

Proposition 1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{-\infty,\infty\right\}
be an arbitrary extended real-valued function. Then
\cl\conv f
is the largest closed convex function majorized by
f
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the case where
\conv f
is improper. Any closed convex underestimator of
f
is also majorized by the largest convex underestimator
\conv f
, so
\cl\conv f
must then equal
-\infty
everywhere. Otherwise, if
\conv f
is proper, we have
\dom f\neq\emptyset
(otherwise
f\equiv+\infty
and so would be
\conv f
, rendering it improper in contradiction to our assumption). For any
\mathbf{x}\in\dom f
and any underestimator
g
of
f
we therefore have
g(\mathbf{x})\leq f(\mathbf{x})<+\infty
, and
\dom g\neq\emptyset
as well. Now
define

h(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sup\left\{g(\mathbf{x})\colon g\mbox{ is a closed convex underestimator of }f\right\}\,.

By definition,
h
majorizes all closed convex underestimators of
f
including
\cl\conv f
, hence
h\geq\cl\conv f
, which also implies that
h
is proper as
\dom h\supseteq\dom f\neq\emptyset
. For the converse, we first observe by elementary arguments that
h
, as a pointwise supremum of closed and convex functions, is itself closed and convex.
By definition of the convex envelope we have
\conv f\geq h
, so that
\epi h\supseteq\epi\conv f
implying that

\epi h=\epi\cl h=\cl\epi h\supseteq\cl\epi\conv f=\epi\cl\conv f\,,

so that
\cl\conv f\geq h
. In the above equalities, we twice used the fact that whenever
g
is proper, for the epigraphs we have
\epi\cl g
=
\cl\epi g
, see for instance
[
25
, Theorem 7.1]
and the discussion succeeding that theorem.
∎

Example 1
.

In order to illustrate the above concepts, consider the following nonconvex function and its convex and closed convex envelopes:

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274ptf(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1-x^{2}&\mbox{ if }x^{2}<1\\
1&\mbox{ if }x^{2}=1\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad\conv f(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }x^{2}<1\\

We see that the closed convex envelope differs from the convex envelope merely at some boundary points of the effective domain given by
\dom f=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon x^{2}\leq 1\right\}
. However, if the feasible set over which

2.1
Copositive optimization prerequisites

To carry out our analysis we will make heavy use of copositive optimization techniques. The core idea of copositive optimization is that for a possibly nonconvex quadratic optimization problem over a QCQP-feasible set
{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\hskip-17.07182pt\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{Q}}\mathbf{x}+2{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\right\}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\

(4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}}):=\mathrm{clconv}\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{array}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}:\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}\right\}\,.

A proof for (

{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{P}}_{0}+\mu{\overline{{\mathsf{P}}}})={\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{P}}_{0})+{\mathcal{A}}(\mu{\overline{{\mathsf{P}}}})=\mathbf{b}+\mu\mathbf{o}=\mathbf{b}
and
{\mathsf{P}}_{0}+\mu{\overline{{\mathsf{P}}}}\in\mathcal{CPP}
so that
{\overline{{\mathsf{P}}}}
is a direction of recession contradicting
Assumption

2
.
The last statement follows from the fact that the dual multipliers of the additional constraints can be set to zero.
∎

Later in the text, we will consider instances of the optimization problems in (
5
) with additional constraints, fixing parts of the primal variable
{\mathsf{P}}
to inputs of optimal value functions. This may render the resulting optimization problem infeasible even if
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})\neq\emptyset
as per
Assumption

2
. However, courtesy of
Assumption

2
we always avoid the doubly infeasible case so that the duality gap stays closed.

3
Underestimators based on copositivity

3.1
Affine underestimators via closed convex envelopes

It is known that convex reformulations based on
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})
can be used to construct convex envelopes of the objective function over
{\mathcal{F}}
by parametrizing the reformulations by the vector variable (see
[
2
, Theorem 1]
, later generalized in
[
29
, Proposition 3]
). This insight serves as inspiration for the main results of this section, as we seek to characterize closed convex envelopes for the optimal value function
\phi
. The following theorem presents some vital insights on
\cl\conv\phi
.

Theorem 6
.

We pose
Assumption

1
that
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})=\mathcal{CPP}\cap\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{b})
. Define

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\inf_{{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}}\left\{q({\mathsf{P}})\colon{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}}),\ {\mathcal{B}}_{1}({\mathsf{P}})=\mathbf{x}\right\}\,.\end{split}

Then the dual of the infimum problem is given by

\displaystyle\theta(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{w},\bm{\lambda}}\left\{\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}+\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}\colon{\mathsf{Q}}-{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\bm{\lambda})-{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{*}(\mathbf{w})\in\mathcal{COP}\right\}\,.

Also, let
h\coloneqq\cl\conv\phi
be the closed convex envelope of
\phi
, cf.
Proposition

1
. Then the following statements hold.

1.

\widehat{\phi}
is a convex underestimator for
\phi
with
\conv\dom\phi\subseteq\dom\widehat{\phi}\subseteq\cl\conv\dom\phi
,
and under
Assumption

2
the reverse inclusions hold as well.

2.

We have
\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\geq h(\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
and thus
\dom h\supseteq\dom\widehat{\phi}\supseteq\dom\phi\neq\emptyset
.

3.

If
{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=-\infty
for some
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
then
\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=-\infty
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\relint\dom\phi
, and
h\equiv-\infty
everywhere.

4.

If
\theta
has a feasible solution (e.g. under
Assumption

2
), then
h
is proper; furthermore,

5.

if
\theta(\mathbf{x})
exhibits no duality gap for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
(e.g. under
Assumption

2
), then actually
\widehat{\phi}=\theta=h
.

Proof.

We start by proving 1. For a given
\mathbf{x}\in\dom\phi
let
\mathbf{y}
be a feasible solution for the infimum problem.
By setting
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\,{\mathsf{Z}}=\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\,{\mathsf{Y}}=\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}
, we get a feasible solution for
\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
with the same objective function value, so we have a lower bound and
\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\leq\phi(\mathbf{x})
. Outside
\dom\phi
, the relation holds regardless.

Now assume
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\right\}\subseteq\dom\widehat{\phi}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. The convex combination with weight
\lambda
of the associated improving sequences of
\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{1})
and
\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{2})
give an improving sequence for
\widehat{\phi}\left(\lambda\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{2}\right)
so that, since the objective is linear, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}\left(\lambda\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{2}\right)\leq\lambda\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{1})+(1-\lambda)\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\,.

Outside
\dom\widehat{\phi}
, the function
\widehat{\phi}=+\infty
, so that in total
\widehat{\phi}
is convex, and by above arguments, a convex underestimator of
\phi
. It follows
\conv\dom\phi\subseteq\dom\widehat{\phi}
. For the second inclusion, consider an
\mathbf{x}\in\dom\widehat{\phi}
, so that there must be a feasible improving sequence
{\mathsf{P}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
q({\mathsf{P}}^{i})\rightarrow\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})<\infty
as
i\to\infty
, and moreover
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}({\mathsf{P}}^{i})=\mathbf{x}
for all
i
by feasibility. Since
{\mathsf{P}}^{i}
are themselves limit points of convex combinations of dyadic matrices
(i.e. matrices of the form
\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}
)
in
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})
by definition of that set, we may construct another sequence

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathsf{P}}}^{i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}^{\ell}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\\
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\\
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\quad\mbox{ with }\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\\
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{F}},\ \bm{\lambda}_{i}\in\Delta_{K},\mbox{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N}.

such that
\lim_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\|\widehat{{\mathsf{P}}}^{i}-{\mathsf{P}}^{i}\|_{F}=0
and in addition
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\in\dom\phi
for all
(\ell,i)\in[1\!:\!K]\times\mathbb{N}
. But we also get from the definition of the Frobenius norm that
\|\mathbf{x}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\lambda^{\ell}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\|_{2}=\|{\mathcal{B}}_{1}\left(\widehat{{\mathsf{P}}}^{i}-{\mathsf{P}}^{i}\right)\|_{2}\leq\|\widehat{{\mathsf{P}}}^{i}-{\mathsf{P}}^{i}\|_{F}
, and we see that indeed

\mathbf{x}=\lim_{i\to\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\lambda^{\ell}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell}\in\cl\conv\dom\phi\,.

Under
Assumption

2
, the effective domain
\dom{\phi}
is closed as it is the projection of a compact set by (
3
), so that the reverse inclusions follow.

Now, to prove 2., consider
h
, which by definition is a closed, convex underestimator of
\phi
. By
[
25
, Theorem 12.1.]
it is therefore given by
h(\mathbf{x})=\sup_{(\mathbf{v},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{V}}}\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+v_{0}\right\}
where
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}+1}
is an appropriate set of coefficients. If
{\mathcal{V}}=\emptyset
, we have
h\equiv-\infty
and the statement follows trivially. So assume the contrary and pick an arbitrary
(\mathbf{v},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{V}}
. By assumption, we have:

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{x})\geq h(\mathbf{x})\geq\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+v_{0},\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}},

which implies that

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\left\{\phi(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}-v_{0}\right\},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}\left\{{\mathsf{A}}\bullet\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}+2\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{C}}\mathbf{y}+2\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}-v_{0}\colon\left[\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{F}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\left\{\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}-v_{0}\right\},

where the first equality is true by definition, the second holds by the definition of
\widehat{\phi}
and (
4
). Considering the fact that
(\mathbf{v},v_{0})
was picked arbitrarily from
{\mathcal{V}}
, we get that

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\geq\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+v_{0},\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}},\ \forall(\mathbf{v},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{V}},

and consequently

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\geq\sup_{(\mathbf{v},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{V}}}\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+v_{0}\right\}=h(\mathbf{x}),\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}.

To observe 3., let
\phi(\mathbf{x}_{0})=-\infty
. From the first two observations, we get that
\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=h(\mathbf{x}_{0})=-\infty
so that both are improper convex functions. By
[
25
, Theorem 7.2]
these functions are
-\infty
everywhere except for some boundary points of their domain unless the domain is closed, in which case the function is
-\infty
everywhere.

Next, we prove 4. So let
(\mathbf{w}^{*},\bm{\lambda}^{*})
be a feasible solution for
\theta(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})
. Then, by
Assumption

1
and weak duality and the prior observations, we have

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\mathbf{w},\bm{\lambda}}\left\{\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}+\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}\colon{\mathsf{Q}}-{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\bm{\lambda})-{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{*}(\mathbf{w})\in\mathcal{COP}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}^{*}+\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}^{*},

where the final inequality follows since the feasible set of
\theta
does not depend on
\mathbf{x}
. Thus, the function is an affine underestimator for
\phi
, hence closed and convex. But this implies it is majorized by
h(\mathbf{x})
which is therefore proper, as point 2. implies

\dom h\neq\emptyset
.

Finally, to prove 5., assume that
\theta(\mathbf{x})
has a feasible point. Then given that
\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=\theta(\mathbf{x})>-\infty
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
, we have that
\widehat{\phi}
is a pointwise supremum of affine functions and therefore closed. Hence, it is a closed convex function majorizing the closure of the convex envelope
h
, so that by definition the two must coincide.
∎

This theorem implies that we can use feasible solutions of the dual
\theta
, to generate affine underestimators of
\phi
, which under mild conditions reproduce its closed convex envelope
\widehat{\phi}
. Such linear models can potentially be used to power cutting plane methods such as Benders decomposition. The following example illustrates some of the intricacies regarding the difference of the convex envelope of
\phi
, the closure of that envelope, and
\widehat{\phi}
.

Example 2
.

Consider the following instance of the optimal value function

\displaystyle\phi(x)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\inf_{y}\left\{2axy+by^{2}+cy\colon fx+gy=d,\ x\geq 0,\ y\geq 0\right\},\mbox{ so that }

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}(x)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\inf_{X,Y,Z,y}\left\{2aZ+bY+cy\colon\begin{array}[]{l}fx+gy=d,\\
f^{2}X+2fgZ+g^{2}Y=d^{2},\end{array}\begin{bmatrix}1&x&y\\
x&X&Z\\
y&Z&Y\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})\right\}.

Since the set
{\mathcal{F}}
of feasible
[x,y]
is the positive orthant,
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})
coincides with the constraints defining
\widehat{\phi}
by
Theorem

2
.

•

First, consider the case where
f=g=d=c=0
. This trivializes the linear constraint and eliminates the linear term in the objective. This setup makes for an easy demonstration that
\widehat{\phi}
can be either the convex envelope, or the closed convex envelope, or neither of both.

–

Let further
a,b\geq 0
. We find, that
\phi(x)=0
over its effective domain given by
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The same is true for
\widehat{\phi}(x)
since the conic constraint implies
x,Z,Y\geq 0
. Hence the latter function is indeed the convex envelope of the former which in this case happens to be closed.

–

If
a=0,\ b<0
we would find a similar relation. Both functions are
-\infty
across their identical effective domains (given by
\mathbb{R}_{+}
). Except, now
\widehat{\phi}(x)
is no longer the closure of the convex envelope as that would be the function that is
-\infty
everywhere, which is the only improper convex function that is also closed. However, incidentally,
\widehat{\phi}(x)=\phi(x)
and the latter is its own convex envelope so that
\widehat{\phi}
is as well.

–

Now, let
a<0,\ b=0
, then

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }x=0,\\
-\infty&\mbox{ if }x>0,\\
\infty&\mbox{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}otherwise}},\end{cases}

but for the conic problem the ray

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&x&0\\
x&x^{2}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}+\lambda\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&1&1\\
0&1&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{CPP}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}\right),\quad\lambda\geq 0,

whenever
x\geq 0
. Hence we get an improving ray, so that
\widehat{\phi}(x)=-\infty
for
x\geq 0
and
\infty
otherwise. The closed convex envelope is
-\infty
everywhere while
\phi
is its own convex envelope so that
\widehat{\phi}
is neither of those.

•

Now let’s consider
f=g=d=1,\ a=0,\ c=0
so that, by feasibility considerations, the effective domain of both functions is given by the line segment
[0,1]
. For instance, we can see that
\widehat{\phi}(x)=\infty
whenever
x\notin[0,1]
since in that case the constraints
x+y=1
cannot be fulfilled by any
x,y\geq 0
as necessitated by the conic constraint. More so, the feasible set of the conic problem is compact, so that a dual Slater point must exist, guaranteeing strong duality, which renders
\widehat{\phi}
to be the closed convex envelope of
\phi
by
Theorem

6
. Also,
\phi
becomes an optimization problem over a singleton and is thus evaluated directly as

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\begin{cases}bx^{2}-2bx+b&\mbox{ if }x\in[0,1],\\
\infty&\mbox{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}otherwise}},\end{cases}

where we plugged in
y=1-x
.

Note, that
\phi(1)=0
, while
\phi(0)=b
. We investigate the convex and the nonconvex case separately.

–

Let us set
b>0
so that
\phi(x)
is an overall convex function. We can evaluate
\widehat{\phi}(x)
in the following way. Inside the effective domain
[0,1]
we will now construct a relaxation of
\widehat{\phi}
that turns out to be tight. To that end,
consider the fact that a matrix is in
\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+})
if it is doubly nonnegative, i.e. psd and nonnegative. This implies
Y\geq y^{2}=(1-x)^{2}
.
We will now consider a lower bound of
\widehat{\phi}(x)
given by

\displaystyle bx^{2}-2bx+b=\inf_{Y}\left\{bY\colon Y\geq(1-x)^{2}\right\}\leq\widehat{\phi}(x)\leq\phi(x)=bx^{2}-2bx+b,\ \forall x\in[0,1],

where the leftmost inequality is
Theorem

6
.1. This, together with the fact that outside the unit interval both functions are
\infty
, implies
\widehat{\phi}=\phi
as it should since
\phi
is closed and convex and
\widehat{\phi}
is its closed convex envelopes by
Theorem

6
.5.

–

So consider the case
b<0
. First, observe the feasibility of
Y~=~1~-~x,\ X~=~x,\ Z~=~0
,
y~=~1~-~x
. Indeed, we can use Sylvester’s criterion to certify positive semidefiniteness whenever
x\in[0,1]
, and the linear constraints hold as well. The objective function value is
b(1-x)
. To certify optimality for
\widehat{\phi}
, observe that, due to the linear constraint on the lifted variables, we could increase
Y
by
\varepsilon_{Y}>0
only if we change
X
to
X+\varepsilon_{X}
and
Z=0
to
Z+\varepsilon_{Z}
with
\varepsilon_{Z}\geq 0
, in which case the increase in
Y
would be
\varepsilon_{Y}=-(\varepsilon_{X}+2\varepsilon_{Z})
, which implies
\varepsilon_{X}<-2\varepsilon_{Z}\leq 0
. However, since
x
and
y=1-x
are fixed by constraints, there is no feasible perturbation of other entries in the matrix variable, whose determinant would then become
-(\varepsilon_{X}+\varepsilon_{Z})^{2}<0
,
so that the matrix could not be psd anymore. We see that

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}(x)=\begin{cases}b(1-x)&\mbox{ if }x\in[0,1],\\
\infty&\mbox{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}otherwise}},\end{cases}

which is precisely the largest convex function underestimating
\phi
in that interval and it is indeed closed.

3.2
Quadratic underestimators via exact convexification

In this section, we will describe a convexified characterization of
\phi
whose dual objective is quadratic in the first-stage variable
\mathbf{x}
. Hence, the so-produced underestimators will be (possibly nonconvex) quadratic functions that, as we will show in
Section

3.2.1
, can exactly represent
\phi
in the limit. The theoretical core of the discussion is the following theorem.

Theorem 7
.

Under
Assumption

2
, we have
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\inf_{{\mathsf{P}}}\left\{q({\mathsf{P}})\colon{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\right\},\ j=1,2
where

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left\{{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})\colon{\mathcal{B}}_{2}({\mathsf{P}})=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}})\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{2}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left\{{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})\colon{\mathcal{B}}_{3}({\mathsf{P}})=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}})\right\}.

In fact,
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})={\mathcal{G}}_{2}(\mathbf{x})
and both sets are unchanged in case
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}
is relaxed to
\diag({\mathsf{X}})=\mathbf{x}\circ\mathbf{x}
.

Proof.

We can always rewrite
\phi(\mathbf{u})=\inf_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\left\{f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\colon[\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}]\in{\mathcal{F}},\ \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}\right\}
so that by (
4
) the first part of the theorem will follow if we can show that
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})=\left\{{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})\colon\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u},\ {\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}
, where
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}=\left\{[\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}]\in{\mathcal{F}}\colon\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}\right\}
. We have
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})
since any extreme point of the former set is of the form
{\mathsf{P}}=\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}},\ \mathbf{z}=[1,\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}]\in\left\{1\right\}\times{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
, so that
{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})
and the containment follows from the convexity of both sets. For the converse, from
Proposition

4
and the definition of
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})
we have
{\mathsf{P}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}},\ \mathbf{z}_{i}=[1,\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}]\in\left\{1\right\}\times{\mathcal{F}},\ i\in[1\!:\!k],\ \bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{k}
so that
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}},\ \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}
, which yields that

\displaystyle\hskip-25.6073pt\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\implies\lambda_{i}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}=\delta_{i}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{u}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{u}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}},\,\delta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\,i\in[1\!:\!k]\,,

(6)

by extremality of the rays generated by dyadic matrices in
{\mathcal{S}}^{n_{0}}_{+}
(see e.g. the proof of
[
7
, Proposition 11]
). If
\lambda_{i}=0
the respective term can be dropped from the sum so that we can assume
\lambda_{i}>0
. Then (
6
) implies
\delta_{i}=\lambda_{i}>0
(from the north-west entry of the final equation) which further implies
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{u}
so that
\mathbf{z}_{i}\in\left\{1\right\}\times{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
as desired. Clearly,
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}_{2}(\mathbf{x})
by a similar argument as before (note that the constraint is linear in
{\mathsf{P}}
, since
\mathbf{x}
is a constant) and
{\mathcal{G}}_{2}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})
by construction so that the two sets must coincide. Finally, said relaxation is tight since
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n_{x}+n_{y}+1}
so that by Schur complementation and the relaxed constraint we have
{\mathsf{O}}={\mathsf{X}}-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n_{x}}
since the zero matrix is the only psd with diagonal equal to zero. Hence,
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}
as desired.
∎

Remark
.

The equality
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})={\mathcal{G}}_{2}(\mathbf{x})
shows that the conditions in the former set are enough to force
{\mathsf{Y}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}
. This is a useful insight, which is for example used in
[
16
]
.

Under
Assumption

1
the dual of the problem over
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})
is given by

\displaystyle\sup_{{\mathsf{W}},\mathbf{w},\bm{\lambda}}\left\{\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}+\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bullet{\mathsf{W}}\colon{\mathsf{Q}}-{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\bm{\lambda})-{\mathcal{B}}_{2}^{*}(\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{W}})\in\mathcal{COP}\right\},

(7)

so that if we can close the duality gap, e.g. under
Assumption

2
, we get a dual characterization of
\phi
. If the constraint
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}
is replaced with
\diag({\mathsf{X}})=\mathbf{x}\circ\mathbf{x}
in the primal, then the dual variable
{\mathsf{W}}
would be restricted to be a diagonal matrix.

We see that feasible solutions to (
7
) yield underestimators of
\phi
that are quadratic in
\mathbf{x}
, where the convexity hinges on the value for
{\mathsf{W}}
. It is therefore vital to understand whether it is possible to find a convex underestimator among the dual solutions. To this end, we will now study quadratic underestimators more closely.

3.2.1
Quadratic envelopes

We will now investigate the properties of quadratic underestimators of
\phi
and show that, in a certain sense, all such underestimators, convex or not, can be found among the solutions of (
7
). A quadratic function
\widehat{q}
underestimates
\phi
if and only if

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{x})\geq\widehat{q}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,.

(8)

Any quadratic function can be cast in the form
\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+{\mathsf{W}}\bullet\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}+\nu
, where the choice of the coefficients denomination was purposeful as will be apparent shortly. Moreover, for
{\mathsf{P}}=\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}
with
\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}=[1,\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}]
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+{\mathsf{W}}\bullet\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}=\langle(\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{W}}),{\mathcal{B}}_{2}({\mathsf{P}})\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{B}}_{2}^{*}(\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{W}}),{\mathsf{P}}\rangle={\mathcal{B}}_{2}^{*}(\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{W}})\bullet{\mathsf{P}}\,.

With this in mind, we can now show that the relation between
\phi
and their quadratic underestimators is almost mediated entirely by the dually feasible set of (
7
).

Theorem 8
.

Given
Assumption

1
, the function

\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+{\mathsf{W}}\bullet\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}+\nu
is a quadratic underestimator of
\phi
, if there are
\bm{\lambda}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\bm{\lambda}=\lim\limits_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\left(\bm{\lambda}^{\ell}\right)
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}\geq\nu,\quad{\mathsf{Q}}-{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\bm{\lambda}^{\ell})-{\mathcal{B}}_{2}^{*}(\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{W}})\in\mathcal{COP},\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}\,.

In addition, if the problem

\displaystyle\inf_{{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}}\left\{q({\mathsf{P}})-{\mathcal{B}}_{2}^{*}(\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{W}})\bullet{\mathsf{P}}\colon{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{P}})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{P}}\in\mathcal{CPP}\right\},

exhibits no duality gap (for instance under
Assumption

2
), the above condition is also necessary, and if the dual attains its optimal value it is necessary and sufficient that there is
\bm{\lambda}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}\geq\nu,\quad{\mathsf{Q}}-{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\bm{\lambda})-{\mathcal{B}}_{2}^{*}(\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{W}})\in\mathcal{COP}\,.

Proof.

Condition (
8
) can be equivalently stated as

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{x})\geq

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+{\mathsf{W}}\bullet\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}+\nu\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\phi(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}-{\mathsf{W}}\bullet\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\}-\nu,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{{\mathsf{P}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}}\left\{q({\mathsf{P}})-{\mathcal{B}}_{2}^{*}(\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{W}})\bullet{\mathsf{P}}\colon{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{P}})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{P}}\in\mathcal{CPP}\right\}-\nu

\displaystyle\Longleftarrow\quad 0\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\lambda}}\left\{\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}\colon{\mathsf{Q}}-{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\bm{\lambda})-{\mathcal{B}}_{2}^{*}(\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{W}})\in\mathcal{COP}\right\}-\nu.

For the first implication we merely used the definitions of an infimum and
\phi
. The equality is a consequence of
Assumption

1
and (
4
). The second one-sided implication is a consequence of weak duality, with the reverse implication being valid in case the duality gap vanishes.
The theorem then is a consequence of the definition of a supremum, in terms of the limit (as
\ell\to\infty
) of a feasible improving sequence, while under strong duality the supremum is in fact attained.
∎

This theorem tells us that by searching the dual feasible set in (
7
) we may find all the quadratic underestimators of
\phi
, convex or not, at least in the limit. Thus, if a convex quadratic underestimator for
\phi
exists at a point
\mathbf{x}
, we may find it by solving the dual under additional positive semidefiniteness constraints on
{\mathsf{W}}
. One challenge we seek to meet in future research is the question of how to do this efficiently. For now, we leave the reader with some illustrative examples.

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\left(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},w,W\right)=\left(s,2s+1,-(s+\tfrac{1}{4s}),-2,1\right),\mbox{ for any }s>0,

If in addition, bilinear terms are present, i.e.

4
An application example

In this section, we will give an example of how to use the theory outlined in this text for numerical purposes. We will devise a Benders-type scheme to tackle a certain nonconvex QCQP, allowing for parallelization of some of the procedures. The so-obtained lower bounds may demonstrate that our results can have significant computational benefits. However, since this is not the main focus of the text, we will not discuss results on whether the algorithmic scheme converges, nor will we consider real-life instances, but merely artificial ones.

4.1
The model

We will consider the following optimization problem

\displaystyle v^{*}\coloneqq\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{Sn},\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{A}}\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{S}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{Q}}_{ij}\mathbf{x}_{i}=1,\ j\in[1\!:\!m],\ i\in[1\!:\!S],\\
\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\leq r,\ \mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},&\dots,&\mathbf{x}_{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{array}\right\},

where
{\mathsf{A}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{Sn},\ {\mathsf{A}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n},\ i\in[1\!:\!S],\ {\mathsf{Q}}_{ij}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n},\ j\in[1\!:\!m],\ i\in[1\!:\!S]
and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{Sn}
. Such a structure may arise if we have a QCQP where the nonconvex part can be separated and decomposed into
S
separate blocks of variables and constraints. The model can be reformulated into a two-stage form

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{Sn},\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{A}}\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{S}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\colon\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\leq r,\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},&\dots,&\mathbf{x}_{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{array}\right\},

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\begin{cases}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}&\mbox{if }\mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\colon\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{Q}}_{ij}\mathbf{x}_{i}=1,\ \mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\leq r\right\},\quad\forall i\in[1\!:\!S],

and by
Theorem

6
the closed convex envelopes of
\phi_{i},\ i\in[1\!:\!S]
are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\min_{{\mathsf{X}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}}\left\{{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}_{i}\colon(\mathbf{x}_{i},{\mathsf{X}}_{i})\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}&{\mathsf{X}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}}_{i})\right\},\quad i\in[1\!:\!S],

where the shorthand
(\mathbf{x}_{i},{\mathsf{X}}_{i})
for the matrix block will be used throughout this section.
We see that we can obtain a lower bound
v_{rel}^{*}
on
v^{*}
by solving the convex relaxation obtained by replacing
\phi_{i}
with
\hat{\phi}_{i},\ i\in[1\!:\!S]
and the remainder of this section will be concerned with calculating this lower bound by using a Bender type strategy.

Before proceeding we would like to point out a couple of noteworthy things. Firstly, we chose not to have a second stage variable here to keep the exposition simple and to demonstrate that a "natural" two-stage form is not necessary for our underestimators to be applicable. Secondly, in the construction of
{\mathcal{F}}_{i},\ i\in[1\!:\!S]
, we added a ball constraint which is redundant for the overall problem; however, its presence ensures the compactness of the sets. As a consequence,
Assumption

2
is fulfilled and
\hat{\phi}_{i},\ i\in[1\!:\!S]
are indeed exactly the closed convex envelopes, their defining convex optimization problems enjoy zero duality gap and, finally,
\dom(\hat{\phi})=\cl\conv\dom\phi=\conv{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
by
Theorem

6
. We will now proceed with presenting a solution scheme for the convex relaxation.

4.2

Remark
.

It is possible to initialize the algorithm with
\mathbf{x}_{1}
obtained from
v^{*}_{rel}
where, for all
i\in[1\!:\!S]
, the sets
{\mathcal{D}}^{o}_{i,1}={\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{i,1}=\emptyset
are empty and
\varphi_{i}
are subject to a trivial lower bound (or absent altogether). We found the method to perform better when we calculated initial solutions
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{i},\ i\in[1\!:\!S]
via
Gurobi
and then concatenate these vectors to
\mathbf{x}^{1}
. Solving this feasibility problem is not harder than the QCQP subproblems that we have to solve anyway when solving the copositive dual (see next section), so this effort seemed reasonable to us.

4.2.1
Solving the copositive subproblem

To solve the copositive dual, we will adapt a cutting plane strategy recently studied in
[
17
]
, for the case where copositivity was meant in the classic sense, i.e., with respect to the nonnegative orthant. We will generalize their approach here and discuss the algorithm for a fixed
i\in[1\!:\!S]
since the procedure is the same across all instances.
We start by constructing a polyhedral outer approximation
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{COP}({\mathcal{K}}_{i})
which is updated in every iteration so that in the
k
-th iteration we solve

\displaystyle\theta(\mathbf{x}_{i},{\mathcal{C}}_{k})\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha,\mathbf{w},{\mathsf{S}}_{k}}\left\{-\alpha-2\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}\colon\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{w}&{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\eqqcolon{\mathsf{S}}_{k}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\right\}.

Then, a copositivity check either certifies
{\mathsf{S}}_{k}\in\mathcal{COP}({\mathcal{K}}_{i})
or gives a negative certificate
\mathbf{z}_{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{i}
for which
\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{S}}_{k}\mathbf{z}_{k}<0
in which case the reverse constraint
\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{S}}_{k}\mathbf{z}_{k}\geq 0
can be used as a cutting plane to update
{\mathcal{C}}_{k+1}\coloneqq{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\cap\left\{{\mathsf{S}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n+1}\colon\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{S}}\mathbf{z}_{k}\geq 0\right\}
. The procedure is repeated until set-copositivity is successfully certified and can be summarized as follows.

To check set-copositivity it suffices to check whether

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{z}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{w}&{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{z}\end{bmatrix}=\alpha+2\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\mathbf{z}\geq 0,\quad\forall\mathbf{z}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{i},

since for
\mathbf{z}_{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{i}
we have
\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{S}}\mathbf{z}_{k}\geq 0
if and only if
(\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}_{k})^{-2}\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{S}}\mathbf{z}_{k}\geq 0
and
(\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}_{k})^{-1}\mathbf{z}_{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{i}
since
\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}_{k}>0
whenever
\mathbf{z}_{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{o}\right\}
by construction. To check whether the semi-infinite constraint holds we can simply solve

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}\left\{\alpha+2\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\mathbf{z}\colon\ \mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{Q}}_{ij}\mathbf{z}=1,\ j\in[1\!:\!m],\ \mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}\leq r\right\}.

If it is nonnegative, set-copositivity is certified successfully. Otherwise the optimizer
\mathbf{z}^{*}
can be used to update
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
using
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}\coloneqq[1,(\mathbf{z}^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
. Also, note that for the sake of producing improving rays that populate the feasibility cuts, we can apply the same procedure to (
11
) in an analogous manner.

Remark
.

The copositivity test still requires us to solve a nonconvex QCQP, in other words to solve the original QCQP with our proposed method we have to solve many QCQPs in the process. The benefit comes from the fact that the scale of the QCQPs we have to solve is much smaller. In our example, the size of the smaller QCQPs in the subproblems is
n
while the original QCQP is of size
Sn
, which my be significant if
S
is large enough. Thus, if the small-scale QCQPs can be managed well, our method allows us to leverage this capability for the sake of attacking the larger problem. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first framework that enables such an approach in this generality.

Remark
.

Let
K
denote the index of the last iteration of the cutting plane procedure. Since
\mathbf{z}_{k}
are feasible for the respective
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
for all
k\in[1\!:\!K]
, the procedure above, as a byproduct, produces potentially feasible solutions for the original problem. In our experiments we took the last iterate
\mathbf{z}_{K}^{i}
from each of the
S
subproblems and concatenated them into a vector
\mathbf{x}_{ub}=[(\mathbf{z}_{K}^{1})^{\mathsf{T}},\dots,(\mathbf{z}_{K}^{S})^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
, which in case
\mathbf{x}_{ub}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}_{ub}\leq r
was used to calculate an upper bound on the original problem. As we will see later, this ad-hoc approach performed reasonably well.

Remark
.

Finally, we would like to remark that solving the
S
copositive duals may be parallelized, which potentially saves computation time. We did not implement such a parallelization, but in our experiments, we calculated what the computation time would have been if we merely accounted for the longest of the
S
dual computations in every iteration, which gives an optimistic estimate of the computation time under parallelization.

4.2.2
A stabilizing regularization

During our numerical experiments we noticed that even if it was the case that an iterate
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{k}
was feasible for
\conv{\mathcal{F}}_{i}=\dom\hat{\phi}_{i}
by any reasonable numerical standard, the copositive dual was often still unbounded so that another feasibility cut would be generated, which eventually led the numerical instabilities and a failure to converge. To tackle this issue in an ad-hoc manner, we slightly modified the definition of
\hat{\phi}_{i}
, and consequently its dual representation, in the following way, involving a user-defined parameter
\varepsilon>0
:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathsf{X}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}_{i}\colon(\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{v},{\mathsf{X}}_{i})\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}}_{i}),\ \|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{-\alpha-2\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{w}+\varepsilon\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}\colon\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{w}&{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{COP}({\mathcal{K}}_{i})\right\}.

The advantage of this formulation is that
\hat{\phi}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{k})
is finite even if
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{k}
is only within an
\varepsilon
-distance of the effective domain of
\hat{\phi}_{i}
. Note, that the so-defined version of
\hat{\phi}_{i}
is an underestimator of the original one, and that any feasible solution of the newly regularized dual is feasible for the original dual. Thus, by solving this dual we are still able to produce valid optimality cuts. In our experiments, we found that
\varepsilon=0.05
was enough to eliminate the undesired behavior described above, while the iterates
\mathbf{x}^{k}
were eventually close to feasibility not only for
\conv{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
but actually for
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
. As stated before, this was an ad-hoc solution and we hope we can analyze the effect of this regularization more closely in future research.

Conclusion and future research

In this article, we derived affine, and convex/nonconvex quadratic underestimators of optimal value functions of QCQPs. The results generalize existing results in copositive optimization theory and complement existing results from convex duality theory since we can have more than just affine underestimators, even in the nonconvex case.
We also gave a preliminary example of how to exploit these underestimators in a generalized Benders approach, which was designed somewhat ad-hoc but performed reasonably well.

In future research, we will explore techniques on how to efficiently separate useful (i.e. convex or manageable nonconvex) quadratic underestimators and investigate their utility in global optimization frameworks.
