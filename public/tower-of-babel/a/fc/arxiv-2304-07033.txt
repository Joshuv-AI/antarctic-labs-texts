On deterministic, constant memory triangular searches on the integer lattice
J. Alfredo Cruz-Carlón

Abstract
Recently it has been shown that four constant memory, deterministic agents are able to discover the integer lattice if only local, constant-size communication is allowed. Moreover, if the agents’ choices are determined with the help of a fair coin, it has been shown that three are necessary and sufficient to discover the integer lattice. In this paper, we show that three deterministic agents cannot find the integer lattice and sketch a possible characterization for one explorer, three beacons type of exploration algorithms.



1
Introduction

In
[
1
]
Feinerman, et al. introduce the
Ants Nearby Treasure Search
(ANTS) problem, which is a generalization of the cow-path problem

[
2
]
. While this model (and a randomized version) has been studied, for example, Cohen, et al. in
[
3
]
recently proved that two randomized agents can not find the treasure in the integer lattice, it remains (to the best of our knowledge) an open problem (
[
4
]
) whether three deterministic agents can find the treasure. In this paper we show that they can not.

Our approach is based on two insights. The first is, communication is key; as we will see, if an agent gets lost, the region of the plane it can explore on its own is limited. The second (insight) is actually a question; if communication is key and we know two agents already met at some cell (for example we assume all agents start inside the same cell) what must happen so they meet again? To answer this question, we will explore the role of the automata, the scheduling policy and the communication protocol to determine the conditions every meeting must have. Following we will use these conditions to characterize the shape of the area of the integer plane one, two and three agents can effectively discover and finally we sketch a possible characterization of
one explorer, three beacons
exploration algorithms.

The paper is structured as follows: in Section
2
we define our model, following in Section
3
we develop or necessary tools and finally present our main result as well as a consequence. Finally in Section
4
we sketch a characterization of
one explorer, three beacons
exploration algorithms.

3.2
Two ants

In this subsection we determine the exploration power for two agents. To do so, we first introduce the concepts of waiting and meeting from the automaton’s perspective. Following we characterize the conditions and properties of the different types of meetings to finally determine their exploration power.

An agent may be in one of three different behaviors,
\mathrm{A}
may be waiting for another agent inside a cell, moving and expecting to meet another agent or moving oblivious to any other agent.

Formally
\mathrm{A}

\mathrm{A}

Arranged

Arranged meetings occurrence depends solely on the design of the protocol. Agents
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}

\mathrm{A}

only move in arranged meetings,

Discovered meetings occurrence depends mostly on the scheduler. If the half-bands are of equal size and

fixed

3.3
Three ants

In subsection
3.2
we determined the exploration power of two agents, in particular, we determined (Lemma
3
) that two agents using scheduled meetings have the same exploration power than two agents using arranged meetings. Please recall the equivalence emerged from the fact that the unbounded agent was only able to explore a fixed number of cells before meeting back with the bounded agent. This limitation is a consequence of the inability of the unbounded agent to
turn around
at an arbitrary point in time (Lemma
2
). Moreover, in Corollary
3
we characterized how much of the integer plane three agents can cover when they do not interact pairwise. In this subsection we will show that the two remaining interaction scenarios (one agent interacts with two and pairwise interaction) are the ones that define the exploration power for three agents; the main difference between the two is how the exploration of the wedge is divided between the agents.

With three agents, we can take advantage of the full exploration power of scheduled meetings. Two agents,
\mathrm{B}
and
\mathrm{C}
act as

for an explorer (unbounded agent)
\mathrm{A}

\mathrm{A}
with
\mathrm{B}
and
\mathrm{C}
is an arranged meeting and
\mathrm{A}
interacts with
\mathrm{B}
and
\mathrm{C}
but these two do not interact. Another possible exploration is when the agents alternate in changing roles from beacon to explorer. When an explorer meets a beacon, it becomes a beacon and the later an explorer in this type of interaction the agents interact pairwise.

If the beacons move in opposite directions (e.g.
\mathrm{B}
moves always west and
\mathrm{C}
always moves east) then the exploration area will be two opposite half-bands. However, if the beacons move in half-bands such that their respective directions have an angle less than
\pi
, the explorer will visit an unbounded number of cells that neither of the beacons will explore. For example, if the beacon
\mathrm{B}
moves to the north and then to the west and waits for
\mathrm{A}
and the beacon
\mathrm{C}
moves to the north and then to the east and waits for
\mathrm{A}
, then
\mathrm{B}
and
\mathrm{C}
would explore the sets
\{(-i,i),(-i,i+1)|i\in\mathbb{N}\},\{(i,i),(i,i+1)|i\in\mathbb{N}\}
respectively; while
\mathrm{A}
would explore the set
\{(x,i)|i\in\mathbb{N},-i\leq x\leq i\}
.

We call the shape resulting from a protocol such that the explorer visits cells the beacons do not a

. See Figures

Lemma 6

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a protocol such that agents
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
use only scheduled meetings. Let
\mathrm{A}
be the unbounded agent and
\mathrm{B}
the bounded one. If
\mathrm{B}
does not interact with any other agent, then exists an equivalent protocol
\mathbb{O}
such that
\mathrm{B}
can be replaced by a pebble that
\mathrm{A}
can pick up and place.

Proof

To prove the lemma we first construct a protocol
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
in which we remove the need for
\mathrm{A}
to expect to meet
\mathrm{B}
in any other state but the waiting state. Then we construct
\mathbb{O}
from
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
. In this proof we also assume that there are more than two agents because by Lemma
3
scheduled meetings for only two agents have the same exploration power than one agent.

Let
c_{i}
be a cell in which
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
meet, and
c_{k}
the cell of their next meeting. Let
D=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}),h_{l}\in\Delta_{\setminus}P
be the directions
\mathrm{B}
takes from
c_{i}
to
c_{k}
. Under
\mathbb{P}

\mathrm{A}
waits for
\mathrm{B}
to leave
c_{i}
(by means of a waiting cycle
\overline{\omega_{\mathrm{B}}}
) and then follows a chain
L_{\mathrm{A}}
until it meets back with
\mathrm{B}
. Let

be a chain such that each
q_{F}^{l}q_{F}^{l+1}
transition outputs the direction
h_{l+1}
,
{\omega_{\mathrm{B}}}_{\to}=\omega_{\mathrm{A}}
and
q_{B}^{l}q_{B}^{l+1}
outputs the direction
-h_{l+1}
. Let
Q^{\prime}_{\mathrm{A}}=Q_{\mathrm{A}}\cup\{q_{F}^{0},\ldots,q_{F}^{m},q_{B}^{0},\ldots,q_{B}^{m}\}
,
\delta^{\prime}(a,b)=\delta(a,b)\,\forall a\in Q_{\mathrm{A}},b\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{A}}
,
\delta^{\prime}(q_{F}^{l},\emptyset)=(q_{F}^{l+1},h_{l+1})
, and for each
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{A}}\setminus\{\emptyset,\tau\},\delta^{\prime}(q_{F}^{l},\sigma)=(q_{F}^{l},P)

\delta^{\prime}(q_{F}^{l},\tau)=h
where

is the halting state. Analogously for the states
q_{B}^{l}

\mathbb{P}^{\prime}

\mathrm{A}
by
\mathrm{A}^{\prime}
such that
\mathrm{A}^{\prime}=\langle Q^{\prime}_{\mathrm{A}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{A}},\Delta,\delta^{\prime},q_{\mathrm{A}}^{0}\rangle

By construction of
\mathrm{A}^{\prime}

\mathrm{B}
