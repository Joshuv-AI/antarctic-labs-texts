Asymptotics of two-dimensional hydroelastic waves: the zero mass, zero bending limit
Shunlian Liu, David M. Ambrose

Abstract
. We consider two-dimensional hydroelastic waves, in which a free fluid surface separates two fluids of infinite vertical extent. Elastic effects are accounted for at the interface, with a parameter measuring the elastic bending force and another parameter measuring the mass of the elastic sheet. In prior work, the authors have demonstrated well-posedness of this initial value problem in Sobolev spaces. We now take the limit as these two parameters vanish. Since the size of the time interval of existence given by this prior theory vanishes as the mass and bending parameters go to zero, we now establish estimates which are uniform with respect to these parameters. We may then make an additional estimate which demonstrates that the solutions form a Cauchy sequence as the parameters go to zero, so that the limit may be taken. This demonstrates that the vortex sheet with surface tension is the zero mass, zero bending limit of hydroelastic waves in two spatial dimensions.



1.
Introduction

We study a singular limit of an interfacial fluid flow problem, accounting for hydroelastic and capillary effects at the boundary
between two two-dimensional, irrotational, incompressible, inviscid fluids. As such, these two fluids each satisfy the incompressible
Euler equations in the interior of the fluid region. The fluids are of infinite vertical extent, and we consider periodic boundary conditions
in the horizontal direction. The hydroelastic bending term is the highest-order effect at the free surface, and we study the asymptotic
limit in which this coefficient goes to zero. We simultaneously send the mass of the sheet to zero. We show that in this limit, keeping
the positive coefficient of surface tension fixed, the solutions of the hydroelastic problem approach the solution of the vortex sheet with
surface tension.

2.
Equations of motion

The model of the elastic sheet we consider accounts for mass of the interface; we let
\displaystyle\rho_{0}
be the corresponding
mass density of the sheet. The upper and lower fluid of course each have their own densities, which we denote as
\displaystyle\rho_{1}

and
\displaystyle\rho_{2}
for the lower and upper fluids, respectively.
We denote the position of the free surface (which we note is one-dimensional) by

\displaystyle\bm{X}(\alpha,t)=(x(\alpha,t),y(\alpha,t)).
Here we have taken

\displaystyle\alpha
to be the parameter along the curve and as usual we have let
\displaystyle t
be the temporal variable.
We take this surface to be horizontally periodic with period
\displaystyle 2\pi,

meaning that the surface satisfies

\displaystyle\displaystyle x(\alpha+2\pi,t)=x(\alpha,t)+2\pi,\quad y(\alpha+2\pi,t)=y(\alpha,t),

for all
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle t.

We take a frame of unit tangent and normal vectors at each point along the surface, denoted
\displaystyle\bm{t},\bm{n},
and we
let
\displaystyle s
represent arclength of the curve as measured from a given point.
We have the following definitions for the vectors
\displaystyle\bm{t}
and
\displaystyle\bm{n},
and for the arclength element
\displaystyle s_{\alpha}:

\bm{t}=\frac{(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha})}{s_{\alpha}},\qquad\bm{n}=\frac{(-y_{\alpha},x_{\alpha})}{s_{\alpha}},

s_{\alpha}^{2}=x_{\alpha}^{2}+y_{\alpha}^{2}.

We take the surface to evolve according to a normal velocity,
\displaystyle U,
and a tangential velocity,
\displaystyle V,
i.e.

(1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{X}_{t}=U\bm{n}+V\bm{t}.

Rather than evolve the Cartesian coordinates
\displaystyle(x,y)
of the interface, inspired by the numerical works
[
31
]
,
[
32
]
,
and subsequent analytical works such as
[
5
]
, we will work with geometric dependent variables.
We have already defined the arclength element,
\displaystyle s_{\alpha},
and now we introduce the tangent angle the free surface forms with
the horizontal,
\displaystyle\theta=\tan^{-1}(y_{\alpha}/x_{\alpha}).

The curvature of the surface
\displaystyle\bm{X},
which we denote by
\displaystyle\kappa,
has a simple expression in terms of
\displaystyle\theta
and

\displaystyle s_{\alpha},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\kappa=\theta_{\alpha}/s_{\alpha}.

In terms of
\displaystyle\theta,
the tangent and normal vectors may be written as

(2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{t}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad\bm{n}=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta).

While the normal velocity,
\displaystyle U,
will be determined by the underlying physics, we are free to choose the tangential velocity to
enforce a convenient parameterization. We choose a normalized arclength parameterization, so that

\displaystyle s_{\alpha}(\alpha,t)=L(t)/2\pi
for all
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle t,
i.e. so that
\displaystyle s_{\alpha}
does not depend on
\displaystyle\alpha.
Here,
\displaystyle L(t)

is the length of one period of the interface at time
\displaystyle t.

We may use the expression for horizontal periodicity,
\displaystyle x(2\pi,t)-x(0,t)=2\pi,
and the expressions

(3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle x_{\alpha}=\frac{L}{2\pi}\cos(\theta),\qquad y_{\alpha}=\frac{L}{2\pi}\sin(\theta),

to find

(4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle L=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(\theta)\ d\alpha}\geq 2\pi.

We may regard (
4
) as defining
\displaystyle L
as an operator acting on
\displaystyle\theta,
i.e.
\displaystyle L=L[\theta].

Note that
\displaystyle\theta
must also satisfy

(5)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin(\theta)\ d\alpha=0,

as a further consequence of our periodicity assumption.
We denote the mean of a periodic function,
\displaystyle f,
by

\llangle f\rrangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\alpha)\ d\alpha,

so that our requirement (
5
) may be restated as
\displaystyle\llangle\sin(\theta)\rrangle=0.

The evolution (
1
) implies evolution equations for
\displaystyle s_{\alpha}
and
\displaystyle\theta,
which are

(6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle s_{\alpha t}=V_{\alpha}-\theta_{\alpha}U,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\theta_{t}=\frac{U_{\alpha}+V\theta_{\alpha}}{s_{\alpha}}.

We now determine the tangential velocity which enforces our normalized arclength parameterization; since our parameterization is

\displaystyle\displaystyle s_{\alpha}=L/2\pi,

then we will need
\displaystyle s_{\alpha t}
to satisfy

(7)

\displaystyle\displaystyle s_{\alpha t}=L_{t}/2\pi.

Using (
7
) together with (
6
), we may solve for
\displaystyle V_{\alpha};
we find

V_{\alpha}=\frac{L_{t}}{2\pi}+\theta_{\alpha}U.

We then see that
\displaystyle L_{t}=2\pi s_{\alpha t},
and averaging this over our interval
\displaystyle[0,2\pi],
we find

(8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle L_{t}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}s_{\alpha t}d\alpha=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\theta_{\alpha}U\ d\alpha.

We then conclude

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{\alpha}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\theta_{\alpha}Ud\alpha+\theta_{\alpha}U=\mathbb{P}(\theta_{\alpha}U),

where
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
is the projection which removes the mean of a periodic function.
We may then give the equation for
\displaystyle V,

(9)

\displaystyle\displaystyle V=\partial_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbb{P}(\theta_{\alpha}U)+V_{0}(t),

where
\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}^{-1}
is the mean zero antiderivative of a periodic function with zero mean.
We have introduced
\displaystyle V_{0}(t),
which is the mean of
\displaystyle V
at any time, and we will comment on this by the end of Section
2.1
.

We now address the physical principles which will allow us to deduce the normal velocity,
\displaystyle U.

We are considering irrotational fluids, but there is singular vorticity present at the free surface. That is, the two fluids are
each irrotational in the bulk, but the velocity is discontinuous across the free surface, and upon taking the curl of the velocity
one finds the vorticity is proportional to the Dirac mass of the curve separating the two fluids. Said another way, we take
a vortex sheet formulation of the problem. The Birkhoff-Rott integral,

\displaystyle\bm{W}=(W_{1},W_{2}),
determines the normal velocity of the interface,

(10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle U=\bm{W}\cdot\bm{n}.

We will give the formula for the Birkhoff-Rott integral,
and investigate approximations for it, in Section
2.1
below.
In a Lagrangian formulation of the problem (rather than our formulation in which we use an artificial tangential velocity), the
tangential velocity of the interface (at least in the density-matched case) would be
\displaystyle\bm{W}\cdot\bm{t};
the difference between the two tangential velocities is
an important quantity, so we denote

(11)

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{W}=V-\bm{W}\cdot\bm{t}.

Having introduced this notation, we may rewrite
\displaystyle\theta_{t}
as

(12)

\theta_{t}=\frac{1}{s_{\alpha}}(\bm{W}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{n}-V_{W}\theta_{\alpha}).

Here, we have used the geometric identity

\displaystyle\bm{n}_{\alpha}=-\theta_{\alpha}\bm{t}.

As we have said, there is a jump in tangential velocity across the interface between the two fluids, and we denote this jump
by
\displaystyle\gamma.
Then
\displaystyle\gamma
is also the vortex sheet strength, i.e. when we have said that the vorticity is proportional to the
Dirac mass of the curve, this amplitude is also given by
\displaystyle\gamma.

An evolution equation for
\displaystyle\gamma
may be inferred from the irrotational, incompressible Euler equations. Specifically
the potential in each fluid satisfies a Bernoulli equation, and we may take the difference of the
limit of these approaching the interface to find
the equation for the jump in velocity potential. Then, differentiating the result, one finds the equation for
\displaystyle\gamma,

(13)

\gamma_{t}=-\frac{2}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}[P]_{\alpha}+\frac{(V_{W}\gamma)_{\alpha}}{s_{\alpha}}-2A\left(s_{\alpha}\bm{W}_{t}\cdot\bm{t}+\frac{1}{8}\partial_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{s_{\alpha}^{2}}\right)-V_{W}\bm{W}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{t}+gy_{\alpha}\right),

where
\displaystyle[P]=p_{1}-p_{2}
is the jump in pressure across the interface,
\displaystyle g
is the constant
acceleration due to gravity, and the Atwood number is
\displaystyle A=\frac{\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}
.
The equation (
13
) can be found in several places, such as
[
9
]
,
or
[
11
]
, or
[
12
]
.
A formula for the jump in pressure is needed to close the model, and we use the expression developed in the model of
Plotnikov and Toland
[
38
]
.
The equation for
\displaystyle[P]
is

(14)

[P]=-\tau\kappa+\rho\partial_{tt}\bm{X}\cdot\bm{n}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\left\{\kappa_{ss}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{3}\right\}+g\rho\bm{j}\cdot\bm{n}.

where
\displaystyle\rho=\rho_{0}/s_{\alpha}
is the actual density of the deformed sheet,
\displaystyle\sigma
is the constant bending modulus,
and
\displaystyle\tau>0
is the surface tension coefficient.
Using (
14
) to substitute for
\displaystyle[P]_{\alpha},
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}\sigma\left(\kappa_{ss}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{3}\right)_{\alpha}+\frac{2\tau\kappa_{\alpha}}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}+\frac{(V_{W}\gamma)_{\alpha}}{s_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle-2A\left(s_{\alpha}\bm{W}_{t}\cdot\bm{t}+\frac{1}{8}\partial_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{s_{\alpha}^{2}}\right)-V_{W}\bm{W}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{t}+gy_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{2\rho}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}\left(\bm{W}_{\alpha t}\cdot\bm{n}-\bm{W}_{t}\cdot\bm{t}\theta_{\alpha}+(V_{W})_{\alpha}\theta_{t}+V_{W}\theta_{t\alpha}+g(x_{\alpha}/s_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\right).

We now rewrite this in terms of the tangent angle,
\displaystyle\theta,
and length of one period,
\displaystyle L,
since we have the relationships

\displaystyle\kappa=\theta_{\alpha}/s_{\alpha},

\displaystyle\partial_{s}=\partial_{\alpha}/s_{\alpha},
and
\displaystyle s_{\alpha}=\frac{L}{2\pi}.

These considerations yield the following equation for
\displaystyle\gamma_{t}:

(15)

\gamma_{t}=-\sigma\bar{A}\left(\partial_{\alpha}^{4}\theta+\frac{3\theta_{\alpha}^{2}\theta_{\alpha\alpha}}{2}\right)+\lambda\theta_{\alpha\alpha}+\frac{2\pi(V_{W}\gamma)_{\alpha}}{L}\\
-2A\left(s_{\alpha}\bm{W}_{t}\cdot\bm{t}+\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)\gamma\gamma_{\alpha}-V_{W}\bm{W}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{t}+gy_{\alpha}\right)\\
-2\widetilde{A}\rho\left(\bm{W}_{\alpha t}\cdot\bm{n}-\bm{W}_{t}\cdot\bm{t}\theta_{\alpha}+(V_{W})_{\alpha}\theta_{t}+V_{W}\theta_{t\alpha}-\frac{2\pi gx_{\alpha\alpha}}{L}\right).

Here, we have introduced a few groupings of constants, namely

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}=\frac{1}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}},

\displaystyle\bar{A}=\frac{8\pi^{3}}{L^{3}(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})},
and

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{4\tau\pi}{L(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}.
Notice that
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{A}
are independent of both
\displaystyle t
and
\displaystyle\alpha,

while
\displaystyle\bar{A}
and
\displaystyle\lambda
are time-dependent through their dependence on
\displaystyle L.

If
\displaystyle\rho=0
and
\displaystyle\sigma=0
, then the evolution of
\displaystyle\gamma
would be

\begin{array}[]{l}\gamma_{t}=\lambda\theta_{\alpha\alpha}+\frac{2\pi(V_{W}\gamma)_{\alpha}}{L}-2A\left(s_{\alpha}\bm{W}_{t}\cdot\bm{t}+\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)\gamma\gamma_{\alpha}-V_{W}\bm{W}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{t}+gy_{\alpha}\right).\end{array}

This is the equation for
\displaystyle\gamma
for the vortex sheet with surface tension
[
6
]
,
[
12
]
.

We will be considering the behavior of solutions as
\displaystyle(\rho_{0},\sigma)\rightarrow(0,0)
. We will demonstrate that as
\displaystyle(\rho_{0},\sigma)
vanishes, the sequence of solutions corresponding to
\displaystyle(\rho_{0},\sigma)
forms a Cauchy sequence in an appropriately chosen
function space. These solutions will then be shown to converge to the solution of the vortex sheet with surface tension initial value
problem.

2.1.
The Birkhoff-Rott integral, related operators, and consequences

We have said that the normal velocity of the interface is given by the normal component of the Birkhoff-Rott integral,

\displaystyle\bm{W}.
In this section we will give formulas for the Birkhoff-Rott integral, decomposing it to isolate its most singular part.
This will require the introduction of a few operators related to Hilbert transforms.

The introduction of complex notation simplifies the presentation of the Birkhoff-Rott integral, so we
define the complexification map
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:\mathds{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathds{C}
to be

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(x,y)=x+iy.

We will frequently denote
\displaystyle z=\mathcal{C}(x,y)=x+iy.
Given two real two-vectors
\displaystyle(a_{1},b_{1})
and
\displaystyle(a_{2},b_{2}),

the dot product may be expressed through the complexification map as

(a_{1},b_{1})\cdot(a_{2},b_{2})=\mathrm{Re}\left\{\mathcal{C}(a_{1},b_{1})^{*}\mathcal{C}(a_{2},b_{2})\right\},

where the star denotes complex conjugation.

Using the complexification, the Birkhoff-Rott integral
\displaystyle\bm{W}
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\bm{W})^{*}=W_{1}-iW_{2}=\frac{1}{4\pi i}PV\int_{0}^{2\pi}\gamma(\alpha^{\prime})\cot\left(\frac{1}{2}(z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime}))\right)\ d\alpha^{\prime}.

We also have the following expressions using the complexification:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\bm{t})=z_{\alpha}/s_{\alpha},\qquad\mathcal{C}(\bm{n})=iz_{\alpha}/s_{\alpha},\qquad\bm{W}\cdot\bm{n}=\mathrm{Re}\{\mathcal{C}(\bm{W})^{*}\mathcal{C}(\bm{n})\}.

Properties of the periodic Hilbert transform
[
30
]
will be important in what follows, so we now introduce it.
For any periodic function
\displaystyle f\in L^{2},
we may define its periodic Hilbert transform,
\displaystyle Hf,
through the singular integral

\displaystyle\displaystyle Hf(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi}PV\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\alpha^{\prime})\mathrm{cot}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})\right)\ d\alpha^{\prime}.

The Hilbert transform is a multiplier on Fourier space, with symbol
\displaystyle-i\mathrm{sgn}(k),
where
\displaystyle k
is the Fourier variable.

Notice that both the Hilbert transform and the Birkhoff-Rott integral are singular integrals, with the same order of singularity.
We will approximate the Birkhoff-Rott integral with a Hilbert transform, and we introduce an operator to be the difference of these.
Later, we will see that the error (given by this operator) is smooth. We define

(16)

K[z_{d}]f(\alpha)\\
=\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\alpha^{\prime})\left[\mathrm{cot}\left(\frac{1}{2}(z_{d}(\alpha)-z_{d}(\alpha^{\prime}))\right)-\frac{1}{z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})}\cot\left(\frac{1}{2}(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})\right)\right]\ d\alpha^{\prime}.

We have introduced here a slight modification of the curve
\displaystyle z,
as this will be convenient,

\displaystyle\displaystyle z_{d}(\alpha,t)=z(\alpha,t)-z(0,t);

this is convenient because when integrating functions of
\displaystyle\theta
to find the curve
\displaystyle z,
there is a question of the constant of integration. As the constant of integration has no bearing on the value of
\displaystyle\bm{W},
though, we use
\displaystyle z_{d}
which eliminates
the constant.
Specifically,
\displaystyle z_{d}(\alpha,t)-z_{d}(\alpha^{\prime},t)=z(\alpha,t)-z(\alpha^{\prime},t)
and
\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}z_{d}=\partial_{\alpha}z.

Furthermore, we may view
\displaystyle z_{d}
as being defined through

(17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle z_{d}(\alpha,t)=\frac{L}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\cos(\theta)+i\sin(\theta)d\alpha.

We will frequently need the commutator of the Hilbert transform and
multiplication by a smooth function
\displaystyle\phi
,

[H,\phi]f(\alpha)=H(\phi f)(\alpha)-\phi(\alpha)H(f)(\alpha).

This commutator
\displaystyle[H,\phi]
is a smoothing operator, and we will give results to this effect in Section

3.1
below.

We will be rewriting the
\displaystyle\theta_{t}
equation (
12
) to bring out the leading behaviour, and so will
will need a helpful expression for
\displaystyle\bf{W}_{\alpha}.
This will make use of the operators we have introduced just above.
We repeat the argument of
[
6
]
on this point, which is to differentiate
\displaystyle\bm{W}
with respect to
\displaystyle\alpha,

to recognize a convolution-like structure of the kernel in the integral, and then to integrate by parts. Then decomposing
in terms of our operators, we have

(18)

\mathcal{C}(\bm{W})^{*}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{4\pi i}PV\int_{0}^{2\pi}z_{\alpha}(\alpha)\partial_{\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\gamma(\alpha^{\prime})}{z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})}\right)\mathrm{cot}\left(\frac{1}{2}(z(\alpha)-z(\alpha^{\prime}))\right)\ d\alpha^{\prime}\\
=z_{\alpha}K[z_{d}]\left(\left(\frac{\gamma}{z_{\alpha}}\right)_{\alpha}\right)+\frac{z_{\alpha}}{2i}\left[H,\frac{1}{z_{\alpha}^{2}}\right]\left(z_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\gamma}{z_{\alpha}}\right)_{\alpha}\right)+\frac{1}{2iz_{\alpha}}H\left(\gamma_{\alpha}-\gamma\frac{z_{\alpha\alpha}}{z_{\alpha}}\right).

Introducing the notation
\displaystyle\bm{m}
for the following collection of terms,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\bm{m})^{*}=z_{\alpha}K[z_{d}]\left(\left(\frac{\gamma}{z_{\alpha}}\right)_{\alpha}\right)+\frac{z_{\alpha}}{2i}\left[H,\frac{1}{z_{\alpha}^{2}}\right]\left(z_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\gamma}{z_{\alpha}}\right)_{\alpha}\right),

our decomposition of
\displaystyle\bm{W}_{\alpha}
is

(19)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{W}_{\alpha}=\frac{\pi}{L}H(\gamma_{\alpha})\bm{n}-\frac{\pi}{L}H(\gamma\theta_{\alpha})\bm{t}+\bm{m}.

Note that we have used here that
\displaystyle z_{\alpha}=s_{\alpha}e^{i\theta},
which implies
\displaystyle z_{\alpha\alpha}=i\theta_{\alpha}z_{\alpha}.

Using (
19
) in (
12
), the
\displaystyle\theta_{t}
equation becomes

(20)

\theta_{t}=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}H(\gamma_{\alpha})+\frac{2\pi}{L}V_{W}\theta_{\alpha}+\frac{2\pi}{L}\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}.

We may further improve this expression for
\displaystyle\theta_{t}
by again using (
19
) to expand
\displaystyle V_{W}.

To begin, we recall the definition of
\displaystyle V_{W}
in (
11
), and we differentiate, finding

\partial_{\alpha}V_{W}=V_{\alpha}-\bm{W}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{t}-(\bm{W}\cdot\bm{n})\theta_{\alpha}.

Using the equations
\displaystyle V_{\alpha}=\frac{L_{t}}{2\pi}+\theta_{\alpha}U
and
\displaystyle U=\bm{W}\cdot\bm{n}
, this becomes

\partial_{\alpha}V_{W}=\frac{\pi}{L}H(\gamma\theta_{\alpha})-\bm{m}\cdot\bm{t}+\frac{L_{t}}{2\pi}.

We may simplify this by applying the projection
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
which removes the mean of a periodic function; since
the image of each of
\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}
and
\displaystyle H
has zero mean, and since
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
applied to a constant function is zero,
we have

\partial_{\alpha}V_{W}=\frac{\pi}{L}H(\gamma\theta_{\alpha})-\mathds{P}(\bm{m}\cdot\bm{t}).

We next integrate, applying the zero-mean antiderivative operator
\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}^{-1},

(21)

V_{W}=\partial_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\frac{\pi}{L}H(\gamma\theta_{\alpha})-\mathds{P}(\bm{m}\cdot\bm{t})\right).

We mentioned above that we would set the mean of
\displaystyle V
to be
\displaystyle V_{0}(t),
and this has now been set. We have chosen

\displaystyle\displaystyle R_{4}=\frac{\pi\theta_{t}}{L}H(\gamma\theta_{\alpha})-\frac{\pi L_{t}}{L^{2}}H(\gamma_{\alpha})-\frac{2\pi}{L}H(\gamma\theta_{\alpha}\theta_{t})+\frac{L_{t}}{L}\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}-\theta_{t}\bm{m}\cdot\bm{t}.

3.
Preliminary estimates

In what follows, we will need a number of estimates on Sobolev spaces, Hilbert transforms, and other integral operators.
We now state a number of lemmas giving these estimates.

3.1.
Estimates for the Hilbert transform and the Birkhoff-Rott integral

We begin with some standard facts about Sobolev spaces, such as the following basic interpolation result.
The proof can be found in a number of sources, such as
[
6
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle m\geq 0
and
\displaystyle s\geq m
be given. There exists
\displaystyle C>0
such that for all
\displaystyle f\in H^{s},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|f\|_{m}\leq C\|f\|_{s}^{m/s}\|f\|_{0}^{1-m/s}.

We will also make use of the Sobolev algebra property.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
\displaystyle s>1/2
,
\displaystyle H^{s}(\mathds{T})
is a Banach algebra. That is, there exists
\displaystyle c>0
such that for all
\displaystyle u,v\in H^{s}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|uv\|_{s}\leq c\|u\|_{s}\|v\|_{s}.

We also will need a basic composition estimate, such as can be found in
[
42
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\displaystyle F
is a smooth function and
\displaystyle u
is in
\displaystyle H^{k}\cap L^{\infty}
, then
\displaystyle\|F(u)\|_{k}\leq C(1+\|u\|_{k})
. The constant C depends on
\displaystyle|F^{(j)}(u)|_{L^{\infty}}
, for
\displaystyle 0\leq j\leq k
.

We will need to ensure that the interfaces we consider are non-self-intersecting, and for this purpose we follow
the approach of other works in the field such as
[
49
]
in using a chord-arc condition. As in
[
15
]
, we define
\displaystyle q_{1}
to be
the first divided difference for the position of the curve,

q_{1}[z](\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},t)=\frac{z(\alpha,t)-z(\alpha^{\prime},t)}{\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}}.

The chord-arc condition is that there exists a constant
\displaystyle\bar{c}>0
such that

(38)

|q_{1}[z_{d}]|=\left|\frac{z_{d}(\alpha)-z_{d}(\alpha^{\prime})}{\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}}\right|>\bar{c},\quad\forall\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}.

When proving existence of solutions for an initial value problem as in
[
6
]
,
[
33
]
,
(
38
) is assumed at the initial time, and then the condition is shown to hold for some additional amount of time.
In the present work we are not proving that solutions exist but instead are studying the convergence of solutions
already known to exist; we may therefore assume (
38
) for the solutions we study, on their entire interval
of existence.

Next we have bounds for the operator
\displaystyle K,
which has arisen in the approximation of the Birkhoff-Rott integral by
Hilbert transforms.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 3
be an integer. Assume
\displaystyle z_{d}\in H^{n}
and that (
38
) holds.
Then
\displaystyle K[z_{d}]:H^{1}\rightarrow H^{n-1}
and
\displaystyle K[z_{d}]:H^{0}\rightarrow H^{n-2},
with the following estimates:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|K[z_{d}]f\|_{n-1}\leq C\|f\|_{1}\exp\{C_{1}\|z_{d}\|_{n}\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|K[z_{d}]f\|_{n-2}\leq C\|f\|_{0}\exp\{C_{1}\|z_{d}\|_{n}\}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|K[z_{d}](f_{\alpha})\|_{n-3}\leq C\|f\|_{0}\exp\{C_{1}\|z_{d}\|_{n}\}.

We do not include the proof here, but the relevant details can be found instead in
[
6
]
.
The proof relies on writing the kernel for
\displaystyle K

[
15
]
.
In addition to the bounds of Lemma

we will also need a Lipschitz estimate for

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\displaystyle(\theta,\gamma)\in\mathcal{O}
be given.
Then
\displaystyle J[z_{d}]
and

(43)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|J[z_{d}]f-J[z_{d}^{\prime}]f\|_{1}\leq C_{1}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{1}\|f\|_{1},

\displaystyle J[z_{d}]f-J[z_{d}^{\prime}]f

\displaystyle S[z_{d}]f-S[z_{d}^{\prime}]f

=\mathrm{Re}\left\{z_{\alpha}K[z_{d}]f+\frac{z_{\alpha}}{2i}\left[H,\frac{1}{z_{\alpha}}\right]f\right\}-\mathrm{Re}\left\{z_{\alpha}^{\prime}K[z_{d}^{\prime}]f+\frac{z_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{2i}\left[H,\frac{1}{z_{\alpha}^{\prime}}\right]f\right\}\\
=\mathrm{Re}\left\{(z_{\alpha}-z_{\alpha}^{\prime})K[z_{d}]f+\frac{z_{\alpha}-z_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{2i}\left[H,\frac{1}{z_{\alpha}}\right]f\right\}\\
+\mathrm{Re}\left\{z_{\alpha}^{\prime}(K[z_{d}]-K[z_{d}^{\prime}])f+\frac{z_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{2i}\left[H,\frac{1}{z_{\alpha}}-\frac{1}{z_{\alpha}^{\prime}}\right]f\right\}.

Assumption 3.9
.

There exist
\displaystyle c^{*}>0,

\displaystyle\rho^{*}>0,
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{*}>0

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\in[0,\rho^{*}],

for all
\displaystyle\sigma\in(0,\sigma^{*}],

\displaystyle(\theta,\gamma)\in\mathcal{O},

\displaystyle I+\frac{2\rho\widetilde{A}}{L}H\partial_{\alpha}+T[\theta]
is invertible as an operator from

\displaystyle H^{0}
to
\displaystyle H^{0},
with the bound

\left\|\left(I+\frac{2\rho\widetilde{A}}{L}H\partial_{\alpha}+T[\theta]\right)^{-1}\right\|_{H^{0}\rightarrow H^{0}}\leq c^{*}.

Of course, we should show that this assumption can be satisfied. To develop an example of when Assumption
3.9

may be satisfied, we begin by noting the formula

(I+B_{1}+B_{2})^{-1}=(I+(I+B_{1})^{-1}B_{2})^{-1}(I+B_{1})^{-1}.

We further have the estimate

\left\|(I+B_{1})^{-1}B_{2}\right\|_{H^{0}\rightarrow H^{0}}\leq\left\|(I+B_{1})^{-1}\right\|_{H^{0}\rightarrow H^{0}}\|B_{2}\|_{H^{0}\rightarrow H^{0}}.

\displaystyle\left\|(I+B_{1})^{-1}\right\|_{H^{0}\rightarrow H^{0}}\leq 1
and
\displaystyle\|B_{2}\|_{H^{0}\rightarrow H^{0}}<1,
then we see that

\displaystyle I+B_{1}+B_{2}
is invertible with bounded inverse.
Here, the operator
\displaystyle B_{1}
is
\displaystyle B_{1}=\frac{2\rho\widetilde{A}}{L}H\partial_{\alpha},
and we see immediately from the Fourier symbol of

\left\|(I+B_{1})^{-1}\right\|_{H_{0}\rightarrow H_{0}}\leq 1.

The operator
\displaystyle B_{2}
is
\displaystyle B_{2}=T[\theta],
and so we see that Assumption
3.9
is satisfied if

\displaystyle T

may be satisfied more generally, but

In order to take the limit as
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\rho_{0}

We therefore revisit the energy estimates of the authors in

to this end. Throughout this section,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
as well as

\displaystyle\rho_{1}

\displaystyle\rho_{2}

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle(\theta_{0},\gamma_{0})\in\mathcal{O}
be given, with
\displaystyle\llangle\sin(\theta_{0})\rrangle=0
.
Assume that Assumption
3.9
holds. Then
there exists
\displaystyle T>0
such that for any
\displaystyle\rho_{0}\in[0,\rho^{*}]

\displaystyle\sigma\in(0,\sigma^{*}],

\displaystyle c_{3}\in(0,\infty)

Proof.

For simplicity, in the present proof we will drop the superscripts, and refer to
\displaystyle(\theta^{\sigma,\rho_{0}},\gamma^{\sigma,\rho_{0}})

\displaystyle(\theta,\gamma).

We define an energy functional
\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\displaystyle E(t)=E_{0}(t)+\sigma E_{1}(t)+E_{2}(t)+E_{3}(t)+\rho E_{4}(t),

\displaystyle E_{0}
,
\displaystyle E_{1},

\displaystyle E_{2},

\displaystyle E_{3},
and
\displaystyle E_{4}

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\theta)^{2}+(\gamma)^{2}\ d\alpha,

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{1}(t)=\frac{L^{2}\bar{A}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\partial_{\alpha}^{s}\theta)^{2}\ d\alpha,

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\partial^{s-2}_{\alpha}\gamma)(H\partial_{\alpha}^{s-1}\gamma)\ d\alpha,

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{3}(t)=\left(\frac{\tau L}{\pi(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})}+\frac{\sigma\bar{A}L^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\partial_{\alpha}^{s-1}\theta)^{2}\ d\alpha,

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{4}(t)=\frac{\pi\widetilde{A}}{L}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(H\partial_{\alpha}^{s-1}\gamma)^{2}\ d\alpha.

Recalling that
\displaystyle\rho=\rho_{0}/s_{\alpha},
we see that the terms
\displaystyle\sigma E_{1}
and
\displaystyle\rho E_{4}
are tracking leading-order contributions related to
the bending and mass of the filament.
We have that
\displaystyle\sigma\bar{A}\geq 0
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{A}\geq 0
and
\displaystyle L
is non-negative as well as bounded above and below.
Therefore we see that the energy is non-negative for all
\displaystyle t.

We take the time derivative of
\displaystyle E_{0}
:

\frac{dE_{0}}{dt}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\theta\theta_{t}+\gamma\gamma_{t}\ d\alpha.

Since the Sobolev index

\displaystyle s
is sufficiently largely, it is immediate, from the evolution equations (
20
) and (
33
), and related equations, as well as the estimates from Lemma
3.10
, that the following inequality holds:

\frac{dE_{0}}{dt}\leq C_{1}\exp(C_{2}E).

We next take the time derivative of
\displaystyle E_{1}
:

(64)

\frac{dE_{1}}{dt}=\frac{(L^{2}\bar{A})_{t}}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\partial_{\alpha}^{s}\theta)^{2}\ d\alpha+\frac{L^{2}\bar{A}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\partial_{\alpha}^{s}\theta)(\partial_{\alpha}^{s}\theta_{t})\ d\alpha.

To proceed with estimating (
64

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}^{s}
to
(
20

(65)

where
\displaystyle\Phi_{1}
is given by the formula

\Phi_{1}=\frac{2\pi}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}\binom{s}{j}(\partial_{\alpha}^{j}V_{W})(\partial_{\alpha}^{s+1-j}\theta)+\frac{2\pi}{L}\partial_{\alpha}^{s}(\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}).

We plug (
65

64

\displaystyle\sigma,

+\frac{L\sigma\bar{A}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\partial_{\alpha}^{s}\theta)V_{W}(\partial_{\alpha}^{s+1}\theta)\ d\alpha+\frac{L\sigma\bar{A}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\partial_{\alpha}^{s}\theta)(\partial_{\alpha}^{s}V_{W})\theta_{\alpha}\ d\alpha\\

\displaystyle\Phi

involve at most
\displaystyle s
derivatives of
\displaystyle\sigma\theta
and
\displaystyle\sigma V_{W}
