Let
\Omega(n)

Let

\Omega(N)\geq\frac{18\omega(N)-31}{7}

(1)

\Omega(N)\geq 2\omega(n)+51.

Note that Ochem and Rao’s second inequality is stronger than the first as long as
\omega(N)\leq 81

\omega(n)\geq 10
.

In this note we improve Ochem and Rao’s first inequality. In particular we have:

Theorem 1
.

If
N
is an odd perfect number, with
3\not|N
then

\Omega(N)\geq\frac{8}{3}\omega(N)-\frac{7}{3}.

(3)

If
N
is an odd perfect number, with
3|N

\Omega(N)\geq\frac{21}{8}\omega(N)-\frac{39}{8}.

(4)

Inequality

is always better than inequality
1

4
becomes stronger than inequality
1

\omega(N)\geq 9

Note that if one only uses Ochem and Rao’s original system of inequalities but assumes that
3\not|N
then one can improve the constant term of inequality

18/7

Proof of the main results

Our method of proof is very similar to that of Ochem and Rao; they created a series of linear inequalities involving the number of different types of prime factors (both total and distinct) of
N
and showed that the linear system in question forced a certain lower bound. We will use a similar method, but with additional inequalities.

We we will write
\Omega(N)
as just
\Omega
and
\omega(N)
as
\omega
.

Euler proved that
N
must have the form
N=q^{e}m^{2}
where
q
is a prime such that
q\equiv e\equiv 1
(mod 4),
(q,m)=1
. Traditionally
q
is called the special prime. Note that from this one one immediately has
\Omega(N)\geq 2\omega(N)-1
. For the remainder of this paper we will assume that
N
is an odd perfect with
q
,
e
and
m

The following Lemma is the primary insight that allows us to have a system that is tighter than that of Ochem and Rao:

Lemma 2
.

If
a
and
b
are distinct odd primes and
p
is a prime such that
p|(a^{2}+a+1)
and
p|(b^{2}+b+1)
. If
a\equiv b\equiv 2
(mod 3), then
p\leq\frac{a+b+1}{5}
. If
a\equiv b\equiv 1
(mod 3)
p\leq\frac{a+b+1}{3}
.

Proof.

We will prove this when
a\equiv b\equiv 2
(mod 3) (the 1 mod 3 proof is nearly identical).
Without loss of generality, assume that
a>b
. Note that one must have
p\equiv 1
(mod 3). We have

p|(a^{2}+a+1)-(b^{2}+b+1)=(a-b)(a+b+1).

So either
p|a-b
or
p|a+b+1
. In the first case, we note that
6|a-b
, so
6p|a-b
and thus

p\leq\frac{a-b}{6}\leq\frac{a+b+1}{5}.

In the second case, we have that
p|a+b+1
gives us
pk=a+b+1
for some
k
with
k\equiv 5
(mod 6) and so
k\geq 5
. Thus,

p\leq\frac{a+b+1}{5}.

Thus in both cases we have the desired inequality.

∎

Note that we do not have a version of Lemma
2
when
a\equiv 1
(mod 3) and

(mod 3), since one cannot in that case get beyond

, and the case of

,
