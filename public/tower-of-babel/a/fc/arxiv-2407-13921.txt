Abstract
In this paper, we study the optimality of the Bussgang linear minimum mean squared error (BLMMSE) channel estimator for multiple-input multiple-output systems with 1-bit analog-to-digital converters. We compare the BLMMSE with the optimal minimum mean squared error (MMSE) channel estimator, which is generally non-linear, and we develop a novel framework based on the orthant probability of a multivariate normal distribution to compute the MMSE channel estimate. Then, we analyze the equivalence of the MMSE and BLMMSE channel estimators under specific assumptions on the channel correlation or pilot symbols. Interestingly, the BLMMSE channel estimator turns out to be optimal in several specific cases. Our study culminates with the presentation of a necessary and sufficient condition for the BLMMSE channel estimator to be optimal.



I
Introduction

Massive multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) technology is a key enabler of current fifth-generation cellular networks and is envisioned to assume an even more prominent role in future wireless systems
[
1
]
. The large number of antennas in massive MIMO motivates the study of the impact of low-complexity, energy-efficient hardware on the system performance. Low-resolution and 1-bit analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) are considered a promising solution to decrease the complexity and power consumption of fully digital massive MIMO systems
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. Since coherent MIMO communications require instantaneous channel state information for the beamforming design, the subject of channel estimation with 1-bit ADCs has received special attention in recent years
[
3
,
4
,
6
]
. As a chief example, the Bussgang linear minimum mean squared error (BLMMSE) channel estimator proposed in
[
3
]
uses the second-order statistics
[
7
]
of 1-bit quantized signals and its linear structure has inspired subsequent signal processing methods applied to, e.g., data detection and beamforming design.

In general, the optimal minimum mean squared error (MMSE) channel estimator is a non-linear function of the 1-bit quantized signal received during the pilot transmission. Nonetheless, under certain assumptions, the MMSE channel estimator may assume a linear structure and thus be equivalent to its BLMMSE counterpart; in this setting, the BLMMSE channel estimator is clearly optimal in terms of mean squared error (MSE). Recently, it was revealed in
[
6
]
that the MMSE and BLMMSE channel estimators are equivalent in single-input single-output systems with a single pilot symbol or with specifically chosen pilot symbols in the noiseless case. Given the widespread use of the BLMMSE channel estimator, it is of great importance to analyze its optimality in general scenarios, and this is precisely the goal of this work.

In this paper, we develop a new framework based on the orthant probability of a multivariate normal (MVN) distribution to compute the MMSE channel estimate for MIMO systems with 1-bit ADCs. Building on this, we identify specific cases where the BLMMSE channel estimator is optimal. Then, we present a closed-form expression for the non-linear MMSE channel estimator in a single-input multiple-output (SIMO) system with three receive antennas. Although far from the massive MIMO regime that motivates the use of 1-bit ADCs, studying these specific cases paves the way for establishing a necessary and sufficient condition for the optimality of the BLMMSE channel estimator.

II
System Model and Problem Statement

Consider a general MIMO system with
N_{T}
transmit antennas and
N_{R}
receive antennas, where
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{R}\times N_{T}}
denotes the channel matrix between the transmitter and receiver. For the channel estimation,
\tau
pilot symbols per transmit antenna are transmitted, which are collectively denoted by
\mathbf{S}^{\mathrm{T}}=[\mathbf{s}_{1}\;\ldots\;\mathbf{s}_{\tau}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{T}\times\tau}
. The received signal at the input of the ADCs is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{S}^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathbf{N},

(1)

where
\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{R}\times\tau}
represents noise. Furthermore, we introduce

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{\tau N_{R}},

(2)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{S}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N_{R}}\in\mathbb{C}^{(\tau N_{R})\times(N_{T}N_{R})},

(3)

\mathbf{h}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{H})\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{T}N_{R}}
, and
\mathbf{n}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{\tau N_{R}}
. We assume a general correlated Rayleigh fading channel model with
\mathbf{h}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{(N_{T}N_{R})\times(N_{T}N_{R})}
is the channel covariance matrix. The probability density function for
\mathbf{h}
is expressed as

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{h})=\frac{1}{\pi^{N_{T}N_{R}}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|}e^{-\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{h}}.

(4)

Moreover, we assume
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{\tau N_{R}})
, and the resulting signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is
\textrm{SNR}={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{S\mathbf{S}^{\mathrm{H}}})/(\tau N_{T}\sigma^{2})
, which reduces to
|s|^{2}/\sigma^{2}
when
\tau=N_{T}=1
.

We denote the element-wise memoryless 1-bit quantization operation as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\textrm{1-bit}}(\cdot)=\mathrm{sgn}\big(\mathrm{Re}(\cdot)\big)+j\mathrm{sgn}\big(\mathrm{Im}(\cdot)\big).

(5)

The quantized received signal (at the output of the ADCs) is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}=\mathcal{Q}_{\textrm{1-bit}}(\mathbf{b})\in\mathbb{C}^{\tau N_{R}}.

(6)

Based on the quantized received signal
\mathbf{r}
, the MMSE estimate of
\mathbf{h}
is given by
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{\rm MMSE}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{h}|\mathbf{r})=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N_{T}N_{R}}}\mathbf{h}p(\mathbf{h}|\mathbf{r}){\rm d}\mathbf{h}
, with
p(\mathbf{h}|\mathbf{r})=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{h})p(\mathbf{h})/\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r})
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{\rm MMSE}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N_{T}N_{R}}}\mathbf{h}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{h})p(\mathbf{h}){\rm d}\mathbf{h}}{\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r})},

(7)

with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r})=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N_{T}N_{R}}}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{h})p(\mathbf{h}){\rm d}\mathbf{h}.

(8)

An explicit expression for the solution to (
7
) is in general unavailable. In the next section, we first develop a novel framework for computing (
7
). Then, in Section
IV
, we study some specific cases and determine a general condition under which the MMSE channel estimate is linear in
\mathbf{r}
, i.e., when the BLMMSE channel estimator is optimal.

III
A Framework for 1-Bit MMSE Channel Estimation

In this section, we analyze the structure of the MMSE channel estimator with 1-bit ADCs and reformulate the corresponding conditional mean expression into a form that involves the orthant probability of a MVN distribution. This will then be exploited to determine the optimality of the BLMMSE channel estimator.

Let us recall (
2
) and (
5
)–(
6
), and let

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{\mathsf{R}}=\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{r})=\mathrm{sgn}\left(\mathrm{Re}\left(\mathbf{b}\right)\right),\quad\mathbf{r}_{\mathsf{I}}=\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{r})=\mathrm{sgn}\left(\mathrm{Im}\left(\mathbf{b}\right)\right).

Then, we write the

th element of

and

as

and

, respectively, with

. Correspondingly, we write the
k
th element of
\mathbf{r}
as

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526ptr_{k}

\displaystyle=r_{\mathsf{R},k}+jr_{\mathsf{I},k}=\mathrm{sgn}(\mathrm{Re}(b_{k}))+j\mathrm{sgn}(\mathrm{Im}(b_{k}))

(10)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sgn}(\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{h})+\mathrm{Re}(n_{k}))+j\mathrm{sgn}(\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{h})+\mathrm{Im}(n_{k})),

(11)

where
\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}
denotes the

th row of
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathrm{Re}(n_{k})
and
\mathrm{Im}(n_{k})
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2})
random variables, and

\displaystyle(\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{h}))^{2}+(\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{h}))^{2}=\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{a}_{k}\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{h}.

(12)

It can be readily shown that

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\mathbb{P}(r_{\mathsf{R},k}|\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(x_{k}-r_{\mathsf{R},k}\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{h})\right)^{2}\right\}}{\sqrt{\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm d}x_{k},

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(r_{\mathsf{I},k}|\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(y_{k}-r_{\mathsf{I},k}\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{h})\right)^{2}\right\}}{\sqrt{\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm d}y_{k}.

(14)

Based on the distribution of the noise, we have
\mathbb{P}(r_{k}|\mathbf{h})=\mathbb{P}(r_{\mathsf{R},k}|\mathbf{h})\mathbb{P}(r_{\mathsf{I},k}|\mathbf{h})
and, hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{h})=\prod_{k=1}^{\tau N_{R}}\mathbb{P}(r_{k}|\mathbf{h}).

(15)

Define
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1}\;\ldots\;x_{\tau N_{R}}]^{\mathrm{T}}
,
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1}\;\ldots\;y_{\tau N_{R}}]^{\mathrm{T}}
, and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{R}}=\mathtt{diag}{{(}}\mathbf{r}_{\mathsf{R}}),\quad\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{I}}=\mathtt{diag}{{(}}\mathbf{r}_{\mathsf{I}}).

(16)

Clearly, we have
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{R}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{R}}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{I}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{I}}=\mathbf{I}_{\tau N_{R}}
. Furthermore, it can be shown that (
15
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{h})=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\tau N_{R}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\tau N_{R}}}\!\!\frac{\exp\left\{-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{R}}\mathbf{x}+j\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{I}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\}}{(\pi\sigma^{2})^{\tau N_{R}}}{\rm d}\mathbf{x}{\rm d}\mathbf{y}.

(17)

Now, introduce the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{b}
in (
2
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\rm b}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{H}}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{\tau N_{R}}\in\mathbb{C}^{(\tau N_{R})\times(\tau N_{R})},

(18)

and define

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\mathsf{R}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\rm b}^{-1}\right)=\mathbf{D}_{\mathsf{R}}^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tau N_{R})\times(\tau N_{R})},

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\mathsf{I}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Im}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\rm b}^{-1}\right)=-\mathbf{D}_{\mathsf{I}}^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tau N_{R})\times(\tau N_{R})}.

(20)

Based on (
4
) and (
17
), we rewrite (

) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N_{T}N_{R}}}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{h})\frac{e^{-\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{h}}}{\pi^{N_{T}N_{R}}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|}{\rm d}\mathbf{h}

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{\tau N_{R}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\rm b}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2\tau N_{R}}}e^{-\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{z}}{\rm d}\mathbf{z},

(22)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{R}}\mathbf{D}_{\mathsf{R}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{R}}&\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{R}}\mathbf{D}_{\mathsf{I}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathsf{I}}\\

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\;\;\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{T}}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\tau N_{R}}.

(24)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N_{T}N_{R}}}\mathbf{h}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{h})p(\mathbf{h}){\rm d}\mathbf{h}
