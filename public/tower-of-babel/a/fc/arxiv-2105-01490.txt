An improved setting for generalized functions: fine ultrafunctions
Vieri Benci

Abstract
Ultrafunctions are a particular class of functions defined on a Non Archimedean field 𝔼 ⊃ ℝ \mathbb{E}\supset\mathbb{R} . They have been introduced and studied in some previous works ( [ 6 ] , [ 9 ] , [ 15 ] ,.., [ 23 ] ). In this paper we develop the notion of fine ultrafunctions which improves the older definitions in many crucial points. Some applications are given to show how ultrafunctions can be applied in studing Partial Differential Equations. In particular, it is possible to prove the existence of ultrafunction solutions to ill posed evolution poblems. Keywords . Partial Differential Equations, generalized functions, distributions, Non Archimedean Mathematics, Non Standard Analysis, ill posed problems. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020): 35A01, 03H05, 46T30

Introduction

In many circumstances, the notion of real function is not sufficient to the
needs of a theory and it is necessary to extend it. The ultrafunctions are a
kind of generalized functions based on a field
\mathbb{E}
containing the
field of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
. The field
\mathbb{E}
(called field of
Euclidean numbers) is a peculiar hyperreal field which satisfies some
properties useful for the purposes of this paper.

The ultrafunctions provide generalized solutions to certain equations which
do not have any solution, not even among the distributions.

We list some of the main properties of the ultrafunctions:

•

the ultrafunctions are defined on a set
\Gamma,

\mathbb{R}^{N}\subset\Gamma\subset\mathbb{E}^{N},

and take values in
\mathbb{E}
; actually they form an algebra
V{{}^{\circ}}
over the field
\mathbb{E}
;

•

to every function
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
corresponds
a unique ultrafunction

f{{}^{\circ}}:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{E}

that extends
f
to
\Gamma
and satisfies suitable properties described
below;

•

there exists a linear functional

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}:V{{}^{\circ}}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}

called pointwise integral such that
\forall f\in C_{c}^{0,1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right),

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)dx=\int f(x)dx

•

there are
N
operators

D_{i}:V{{}^{\circ}}\rightarrow V{{}^{\circ}},\ i=1,...,N

called generalized partial derivatives such that
\forall f\in C_{c}^{1,1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right),

\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{\circ}=D_{i}f{{}^{\circ}}

•

to every distribution
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
corresponds an
ultrafunction
T{{}^{\circ}}
such that
\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}T{{}^{\circ}}(x)\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx=\left\langle T,\varphi\right\rangle

and

\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{\circ}=D_{i}T{{}^{\circ}}

•

if
u
is the solution of a PDE, then
u{{}^{\circ}}
is the solution of the same PDE ”translated” in the framework of
ultrafunctions.

•

\Gamma
is a hyperfinite set (see section
2.4
) so that we have
enough compactness to prove the existence of a solution for a very large
class of equations which includes many ill posed problems.

The ultrafunctions have been recently introduced in
[
6
]
and
developed in
[
9
]
,
[
15
]
,..,
[
23
]
. In these papers
different models of ultrafuctions have been analyzed and several
applications have been provided. In the present paper, we introduce an
improved model: the space of
fine
ultrafunctions. The fine
ultrafunctions form an algebra in which the pointwise integral and the
generalized derivative satisfy most of the familiar properties that are
consistent with the algebraic structure of
V{{}^{\circ}}
. In particular, these properties allow to solve many evolution problem in
the space
C^{1}(\mathbb{E},V{{}^{\circ}})
(see sections
3.6
and
5.5
).

This paper is organized as follow: in the rest of this introduction, we
frame the theory of ultrafunction and expose our point of view on
Non-Archimedean Mathematics and on the notion of generalized functions.

In section
2
, we present the preliminary material necessary to the
rest of the paper. In particular we present an approach to Non Standard
Analysis (NSA) suitable for the theory of ultrafunctions. This approach is
based on the notion of
\Lambda
-limit (see also
[
6
]
and
[
13
]
) which leads to the field of Euclidean numbers (see also
[
24
]
). This part has been written in such a way to be understood also by
a reader who is not familiar with NSA.

In section
3
we recall the notion of ultrafunction, we define the
spaces of fine ultrafunctions and of time dependent ultrafunctions.

The main properties of the fine ultrafunctions are examined in section
4

Section

ultrafunctions in PDE’s.

Section

is devoted to the proof that the definition of
ultrafunctions is consistent. In fact, even if this definition is based on
notions which appear quite natural, the consistency of these notions is a
delicate issue. We prove this consistency by the construction of a very
involved model; we do not know if a simpler model exists. This section is
very technical and we assume the reader to be used with the techniques of
NSA.

1.2
Few remarks on generalized function

The intensive use of the Laplace transform in engineering led to the
heuristic use of symbolic methods, called operational calculus. An
influential book on operational calculus was Oliver Heaviside’s
Electromagnetic Theory of 1899
[
30
]
. During the late 1920s and 1930s
further steps were taken, very important to future work. The Dirac delta
function was boldly defined by Paul Dirac as a central aspect of his
scientific formalism. Jean Leray and Sergei Sobolev, working in partial
differential equations, defined the first adequate theory of generalized
functions and generalized derivative in order to work with weak solutions of
partial differential equations. Sobolev’s work was further developed in an
extended form by Laurent Schwartz. Today, among people working in partial
differential equations, the theory of distributions of L. Schwartz is the
most commonly used, but also other notions of generalized functions have
been introduced by J.F. Colombeau
[
27
]
and M. Sato
[
37
]
.

After the discovery of Non Standard Analysis, many models of generalized
functions based on hyperreal fields appeared. The existence of infinite and
infinitesimal numbers allows to relate the
delta of Dirac
\delta

to a function which takes an infinite value in a neighborhood of
0
and vanishes in the other points. So, in this context, expression such as

\sqrt{\delta_{a}}
or
\delta_{a}^{2}
make absolutely sense.

The literature in this context is quite large and, without the hope to be
exhaustive, we refer to the following papers and their references: Albeverio

2.1
Non Archimedean Fields

Here, we recall the basic definitions and some facts regarding non
Archimedean fields.

Definition 2.1
.

A field
{\mathbb{K}}
is called ordered if there is a set
{\mathbb{K}}^{+}\subset{\mathbb{K}}
such that

1.

x,y\in{\mathbb{K}}^{+}\Rightarrow x+y,xy\in{\mathbb{K}}^{+}

{\mathbb{K=K}}^{+}\cup\left\{0\right\}\cup{\mathbb{K}}^{-}
where

{\mathbb{K}}^{-}=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{K\ }}|\ -x\in{\mathbb{K}}^{+}\right\}

In an ordered field the order relation is defined as follows:

x<y\Leftrightarrow y-x\in{\mathbb{K}}^{+}

In the following,
{\mathbb{K}}
will denote an ordered field. Its elements
will be called numbers. It is well known that every ordered field contains
(a copy of) the rational numbers; hence the following definitions makes
sense:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an ordered field. Let
\xi\in\mathbb{K}
. We say that:

•

\xi

•

such that
|\xi|<n

•

\xi

n\in\mathbb{N}

|\xi|>n

(equivalently, if
\xi
is not finite).

Definition 2.3
.

An ordered field
\mathbb{K}
is called Non-Archimedean if it contains an
infinite number.

It’s easily seen that all infinitesimal are finite, that the inverse of an
infinite number is a nonzero infinitesimal number. Infinitesimal numbers can
be used to formalize a new notion of ”closeness”:

Definition 2.4
.

We say that two numbers

\xi-\zeta
is infinitesimal. In this

\xi\sim\zeta

Clearly, the relation ”

2.4
Hyperfinite sets

An other fundamental notion in NSA is the following:

Definition 2.13

We say that a set

\sum_{x\in F}x=\ \lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\sum_{x\in F_{\lambda}}x.

One of the advantage to use the field of Euclidean numbers rather than a

Given a set

\left|F\right|^{\ast}:=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}~\left|F_{\lambda}\right|

Notice that the hypercardinality of

3
Ultrafunctions

If we have a differential equation, it is relatively easy to find an
approximated solution in a suitable space of grid functions. If this
equation has a ”classic” solution, this solution, in some sense,
approximates the classic solution. Then, if we take a grid, the ”grid
solutions” is almost equal to the classic solution. However the ”grid
solutions” cannot be considered as generalizations of the classic solutions
since
they do not coincide with them
. The theory of ultrafunctions
is based on the idea of a space of functions (defined on a hyperfinite grid)
in which the generalized derivative and the generalized integral coincide
with the usual ones for every function
f
in
C^{1}
and in
C_{c}^{0}

respectively. This fact implies that a ”ultrafunction solution” coincides
with the classical one if the latter exists and hence it is legitimate to be
considered a generalized solution.

3.1
Definition of ultrafunctions

Let
V=V\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
be a function space such that

\mathfrak{D}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)\subset V\subset\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)

and let
\left\{V_{\lambda}\right\}_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{L}}
be a net
of finite dimensional subspaces of
V
such that

\mathop{\displaystyle\bigcup}\limits_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{L}}V_{\lambda}=V.

Now we set

V_{\Lambda}=V_{\Lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}^{N}\right)=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ V_{\lambda}=\left\{\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ u_{\lambda}\ |\ u_{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda}\right\};

V_{\Lambda}
is an internal vector space of hyperfinite dimension. Clearly

V_{\Lambda}\subset V^{\ast}
since

V^{\ast}=\left\{\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ u_{\lambda}\ |\ u_{\lambda}\in V\right\}.

The space
V_{\Lambda}
allows to equip a space of grid functions
\mathfrak{G}(\Gamma)
of a richer structure:

Definition 3.1
.

A space of ultrafunctions
V{{}^{\circ}}(\Gamma)
modelled on
V_{\Lambda}
is a family of grid functions
\mathfrak{G}(\Gamma)
such that the restriction map

{{}^{\circ}}:V_{\Lambda}\rightarrow V{{}^{\circ}}(\Gamma)

(19)

is an internal isomorphism between hyperfinite dimensional vector spaces.

So,
u\in V{{}^{\circ}}(\Gamma)\
if and only if there exists a net
u_{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)
such that

u=\left(\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ u_{\lambda}\right)^{\circ}

In the following of this paper, if
u\in V{{}^{\circ}}(\Gamma),
such a net will be denoted by
u_{\lambda}
. We will denote by
\sigma_{a}(x)
the only function in
V_{\Lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}^{N}\right)
such that

\sigma_{a}^{\circ}=\chi_{a}

(20)

Clearly
\left\{\sigma_{a}(x)\right\}_{a\in\Gamma}
is a basis of
V_{\Lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}^{N}\right)
which will be called
\sigma
-basis. The
\sigma_{a}
’s allow to write the inverse of the map (
19
)

\left(\cdot\right)_{\Lambda}:V{{}^{\circ}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow V_{\Lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}^{N}\right)

(21)

as follows: if
u\in V{{}^{\circ}}(\Gamma),

u_{\Lambda}(x):=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}u(a)\sigma_{a}(x).

If
f\in\mathfrak{F}\left(\mathbb{E}^{N}\right),
in order to simplify
the notation, we will write
f_{\Lambda}
instead of
\left(f{{}^{\circ}}\right)_{\Lambda},
namely we have that

f_{\Lambda}(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}f^{\ast}(a)\sigma_{a}(x).

Notice that

f_{\Lambda}=f^{\ast}\Leftrightarrow f\in V\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)

(22)

More in general, if
w\in\mathfrak{F}\left(\mathbb{E}^{N}\right)^{\ast}
, we will write

w_{\Lambda}(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}w(a)\sigma_{a}(x),

(23)

In this case, the map

\left(\cdot\right)_{\Lambda}:\mathfrak{F}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)^{\ast}\rightarrow V_{\Lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}^{N}\right)

(24)

is just a projection.

If
u\in V{{}^{\circ}}\left(\Gamma\right)\
and
u_{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)^{\ast}
, the integral can be defined as follows:

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)dx:=\int^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}(x)dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int u_{\lambda}(x)dx.

(25)

We will refer to

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}:V{{}^{\circ}}\left(\Gamma\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{E}

as to the
pointwise integral.
The reason of this name is due to the
fact that (
15
) and (
25
) imply that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)dx=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}u(a)d(a)

(26)

where

d(a):=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{a}(x)dx=\int^{\ast}\sigma_{a}(x)dx.

(27)

We may think of
d(a)
as the ”measure” of the point
a\in\Gamma
. The
pointwise integral extends the usual Lebesgue integral from
V
to
V{{}^{\circ}}
, more exactly, if
f\in V\cap\mathcal{L}^{1},
then

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)dx=\int f(x)dx

(28)

However the equality above is not true for every Lebesgue integrable
function. In fact, if
a\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\int\chi_{a}(x)dx=0

but, by (
26
), we have that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{a}^{\circ}(x)dx>0

at least for some
a\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.
This fact is quite natural, in fact
when we work in a non-Archimedean world infinitesimals matter and cannot be
forgotten as the Riemann and the Lebesgue integrals do. Also the above
inequality shows that it is necessary to use a different symbol to
distinguish the pointwise integral from the Lebesgue integral (here we have
used
\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}
).

Given
u\in V{{}^{\circ}},\
if
u_{\lambda}\in C^{1}\cap V_{\lambda},
and
\partial_{i}u_{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda},
it is natural to define the
partial derivative
in a point
x=\ \lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}x_{\lambda}\in\Gamma
, as follows

D_{i}u(x):=\left[\partial_{i}^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}(x)\right]{{}^{\circ}}=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \partial_{i}u_{\lambda}(x_{\lambda}).

(29)

So, if
f\in

C^{1}\cap V,\
and
\partial f\in

C^{0}\cap V,
we have

D_{i}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)=\left[\partial_{i}^{\ast}f^{\ast}(x)\right]{{}^{\circ}}

and hence, if
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
D_{i}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)=\partial_{i}f(x).

In particular, if we choose

we have that the integral and the
derivative are defined for every ultrafunction in
V{{}^{\circ}}
.

3.2
Epilogic functions and the space
V

There are many ultrafunction spaces which depend on the choice of the space
V,
the net
\left\{V_{\lambda}\right\}
and the grid
\Gamma.
However
there are some basic properties which should be satisfied by a ”good” space
of ultrafunctions which make the theory rich and flexible. In order to get
such a space, the first step consists in choosing an appropriate space
V
.
The simplest choice is to take
V=C_{0}^{1}
so that the pointwise integral
and the generalized derivative be well defined for every ultrafunctions just
using (
36
) and (
29
). However this choice is too restrictive
for many applications. In fact it is useful to work with the characteristic
function
\chi_{\Omega}
of an open set at least when
\partial\Omega

is sufficiently smooth. Then, it seem reasonable to work in
BV\cap L_{c}^{\infty}.
Unfortunately this space is not suitable since a function
f\in L^{\infty}
is not pointwise defined and hence tha map
\left({{}^{\circ}}\right)
is not well defined. However we can overcome this difficulty
taking a space isomorphic to
L^{\infty}
by choosing one function in each
equivalence class of
L^{\infty}
. This choice must be done in a way
consistent with the main operations in
L^{\infty}
. Let us see how to do
it.

We start recalling the following standard terminology: for every function
f\in\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{1}
we say that a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a
Lebesgue point for
f
if

f(x)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{m(B_{r}(x))}\int_{B_{r}(x)}f(y)dy,

where
m(B_{r}(x))
is the Lebesgue measure of the ball
B_{r}(x);
we
recall the very important Lebesgue theorem (see e.g.
[
32
]
), that
we will need in the following:

Theorem 3.2
.

If
f\in\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
then a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a Lebesgue point for
f
.

Now, we fix once for ever an infinitesimal number
\eta>0
; for example we
can choose
\eta=\alpha^{-1}
(see (
7
)). Given a function
f\in\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{\infty},
we set

\overline{f}(x)=st\left(\frac{1}{m(B_{\eta}(x))}\int_{B_{\eta}(x)}f(y)dy\right),

(30)

where
m(B_{\eta}(x))
is the Lebesgue measure of the ball
B_{\eta}(x).

By this trick we can choose a unique function
\overline{f}
in the
equivalence class
\left[f\right]_{L^{\infty}}\in L^{\infty}.

Lemma 3.3
.

The operator
f\mapsto\overline{f}
satisfies the following
properties:

1.

if
x
is a Lebesgue point for
f
then
\overline{f}(x)=f(x);

2.

f(x)=\overline{f}(x)\
a.e.
;

3.

if
f(x)=g(x)
a.e. then
\overline{f}(x)=\overline{g}(x);

4.

\overline{\overline{f}}(x)=\overline{f}(x).

5.

\overline{f+g}=\overline{f}+\overline{g}
.

Proof
: (
1
) - If
x
is a Lebesgue point for
f
then

\frac{1}{m(B_{\eta}(x))}\int_{B_{\eta}(x)}f(y)dy\sim f(x),

so
\overline{f}(x)=f(x).

(
2
) - This follows immediately by Theorem
3.2
and (
1
).

(
3
) - Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.
Since
f(x)=g(x)
a.e., we obtain
that
\int_{B_{\eta}(x)}f(y)dy=\int_{B_{\eta}(x)}g(y)dy,
so

\displaystyle\overline{f}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle st\left(\frac{1}{m(B_{\eta}(x))}\int_{B_{\eta}(x)}f(y)dy\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle st\left(\frac{1}{m(B_{\eta}(x))}\int_{B_{\eta}(x)}g(y)dy\right)=\overline{g}(x).

(
4
) - This follows from (
2
) and (
3
).

(
5
) - Follows from the linearity of the function
st.

\square

We now define the space of
epilogic functions
as follows:

EPL:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{\infty}\ |\ \overline{u}(x)=u(x)\right\}

This name comes from the greek
\varepsilon\pi\iota\lambda o\gamma\eta

= ”choice” since each function in
EPL
has been chosen in an equivalence
class of
L_{loc}^{\infty}.

We list some properties of
EPL
that will be useful in the following:

Proposition 3.4
.

The following properties hold:

1.

if
u,v\in EPL
then
u=v\
a.e. if and only if
u=v
;

2.

EPL
is a module over the ring
C^{0},
namely,
\varphi\in C^{0}
and
f\in EPL
implies
\varphi f\in EPL
;

3.

the
L^{2}
norm is a norm for
EPL\cap\mathcal{L}^{2}

(and not a pseudonorm).

Proof
: (
1
) - Let
u,v\in EPL
. If
u=v
then clearly
\bar{u}=\bar{v}
a.e.; conversely, let us suppose that
u=v
a.e.; by Lemma
3.3
, (
3
) we deduce that
\overline{u}(x)=\overline{v}(x).
But
u,v\in EPL,
so
u(x)=\overline{u}(x)=\overline{v}(x)=v(x).

(
2
) - if
f(x)\in EPL,
then for every
\varphi
in
C^{k},

\displaystyle\overline{\varphi(x)f(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle st\left(\frac{1}{m(B_{\eta}(x))}\int_{B_{\eta}(x)}\varphi(y)f(y)dy\right)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle st\left(\frac{1}{m(B_{\eta}(x))}\int_{B_{\eta}(x)}\varphi(x)f(y)dy\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(x)st\left(\frac{1}{m(B_{\eta}(x))}\int_{B_{\eta}(x)\varphi(x)}f(y)dy\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(x)\overline{f(x)}=\varphi(x)f(x).

(
3
) - Let
u\in EPL
be such that
\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}}=0.
Then
u=0
a.e. and since

by (

), we
deduce that

\square

3.3
Definition of fine ultrafunctions

Roughly speaking a space of ultrafunctions is

if many of the
properties of standard functions are satisfied.

Definition 3.5
.

A space of ultrafunctions
V{{}^{\circ}},

V=BV\cap EPL\cap\mathcal{R},
is called fine if

1.

if
u,v\in V^{\circledcirc},
then

\overline{u_{\lambda}v_{\lambda}}\in V_{\lambda}

(35)

2.

there is a linear internal functional

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}:V{{}^{\circ}}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}

called

which satisfies the following properties:

(a)

if
u=\ \lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}u_{\lambda},

u_{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda},
then

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \mathop{\displaystyle\int}u_{\lambda}(x)dx=\int^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}dx.

(36)

(b)

for every

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{a}(x)dx>0;

(37)

(c)

if

then,

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)\ dx\sim\int f(x)\ dx.

(38)

3.

there are
N
internal operators

called
generalized partial derivatives
such that the following
properties are fulfilled:

(a)

for every
u,v\in V{{}^{\circ}},

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}u(x)v(x)dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int\partial_{i}u_{\lambda}(x)v_{\lambda}(x)dx=\int^{\ast}\left(\partial_{i}^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}\right)v_{\Lambda}dx\mathbf{;}

(39)

(b)

for every
a\in\Gamma,

\mathfrak{supp}\left[D_{i}\chi_{a}\right]\subset\mathfrak{mon}(a).

(40)

Now some comments on Def.
3.5
.

Assumptions (
1
) states that
V{{}^{\circ}}
is sufficiently large with respect to

V^{\circledcirc}=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}V\cap\lambda.

This is a technical assumption which simplifies the definition of regular
ultrafunctions (see section
3.5
).

Assumption (
2a
) is nothing else but the definition of the pointwise
integral. Assumption (
2b
) is quite natural, but it is not satisfied by
every space of ultrafunctions; it is very important and, among other things,
it implies that the bilinear form
(u,v)\mapsto\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}uv\ dx
defines a
scalar product (see section
3.4
).

Assumption (
3a
) defines the generalized derivative. By (

) we
get that for every function

and every

(see Corollary

),

D_{i}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)=\partial_{i}f(x);

(41)

hence the generalized derivative extends the usual derivative; by (

) we have that,
\ \forall x\in\Gamma
,

D_{i}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\partial_{i}^{\ast}f^{\ast}(a)\chi_{a}(x).

Moreover,

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}u(x)v(x)dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}u(x)v(x)dx

(42)

since

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}u(x)v(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int\partial_{i}u_{\lambda}(x)v_{\lambda}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int u_{\lambda}(x)\partial_{i}v_{\lambda}(x)dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}u(x)v(x)dx

Equation (

) is of primary importance in the theory of weak
derivatives, distributions, calculus of variations etc. Usually this
equality is deduced by the Leibniz rule

D(fg)=Dfg+fDg

However, it is inconsistent to assume that Leibniz rule be satisfied by
every pair of ultrafunction (see
[
39
]
and the discussion in
Section
3.5
). Nevertheless the identity (

) holds for the fine
ultrafunctions. In particular, by (

) and (

), we have that
\forall u\in V{{}^{\circ}},\ \forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D},

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}u\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}uD_{i}\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u~\left(\partial_{i}\varphi\right){{}^{\circ}}dx;

this equality relates the generalized derivative to the notion of weak
derivative.

Property (
3b
) is a natural request and you expect that it is satisfied
by (
39
); on the contrary, it does not follows from the other
properties and it needs to be stated explicitly.

While the construction of a generic space of ultrafunction is a relatively
easy task, the construction of a space of fine ultrafunctions is much more
delicate. We have the following theorem which is one of the main results of
this paper:

Theorem 3.6
.

The requests of Def.
3.5
are consistent.

The proof of this theorem is rather involved and it will be given in section

(see Th.

). The rest of this section and section

will be devoted in showing that a fine space ultrafunctions provides a quite
rich structure and many interesting and

proved in a relative simple way.

Remark 3.7
.

A space of ultrafunctions cannot be uniquely defined, since its existence

\mathbb{E}
and hence on Zorn’s Lemma. However, if we exclude
the choice of the space

, the properties required in Def.

are

From now on, we will treat only with fine ultrafunctions and the word ”fine”

3.4
The pointwise scalar product of ultrafunctions

By (
37
), we have that
d(a)>0
and hence, the pointwise integral
allows to defines the following scalar product which we will call
pointwise scalar product
:

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)v(x)dx=\sum_{x\in\Gamma}u(x)v(x)d(x).

(43)

If
f,g,fg\in V,
we have that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}g{{}^{\circ}}dx=\int^{\ast}f^{\ast}g^{\ast}dx=\int fg\ dx;

(44)

however we must be careful since, for some
u,v\in V{{}^{\circ}}
such that
u_{\Lambda}v_{\Lambda}\notin V_{\Lambda},
we might have
that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}uv\ dx\neq\int^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}v_{\Lambda}dx.

(45)

even if these two quantities are not too different.

Example:
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open set.
We have that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{\Omega}\chi_{\Omega}\ dx\neq\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{\overline{\Omega}}\chi_{\overline{\Omega}}\ dx;

in fact

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{\overline{\Omega}}\chi_{\overline{\Omega}}\ dx-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{\Omega}\chi_{\Omega}\ dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left(\chi_{\overline{\Omega}}-\chi_{\Omega}\right)dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{\partial\Omega}dx>0

since
\chi_{\partial\Omega}>0.
Hence, it is not possible that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}g{{}^{\circ}}dx=\int fg\ dx

for all measurable functions.

In any case, by (
35
), if
u,v\in\left(V\cap C^{0}\right)^{\circledcirc}

u,v\in V_{\Lambda}\Rightarrow\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}uv\ dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \mathop{\displaystyle\int}u_{\lambda}v_{\lambda}dx=\int^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}v_{\Lambda}dx.

(46)

The pointwise scalar product allows to get the ultrafunction analogous of
the Riesz representation theorem in the following form:

Theorem 3.8
.

If

\Phi:V{{}^{\circ}}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}

is an internal linear functional, there exists an unique ultrafunction
u_{\Phi}
such that,
\forall v\in V{{}^{\circ}},

\Phi\left(v\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u_{\Phi}v\ dx

Proof
: The scalar product (
43
) is the
\Lambda
-limit of a
net of scalar products
\left\langle\cdot\ ,\ \cdot\right\rangle_{{}_{\lambda}}
defined over
V_{\lambda}
. Since
\Phi
is an internal
functional, there exists a net of functionals
\Phi:V_{\lambda}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\Phi=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\Phi_{{}_{\lambda}}

and hence
\exists u_{\Phi,\lambda},\ \forall v\in V_{\lambda},

\Phi_{{}_{\lambda}}\left(v\right)=\int u_{\Phi,\lambda},v\ dx

Taking

u_{\Phi}:=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ u_{\Phi,\lambda}

we get that
\forall v\in V{{}^{\circ}},

\Phi\left(v\right)=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\Phi_{{}_{\lambda}}\left(v_{{}_{\lambda}}\right)=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \int u_{\Phi,\lambda},v\ dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u_{\Phi}v\ dx.

\square

The pointwise product provides the
pointwise (Euclidean) norm
of an
ultrafunction:

\left\|u\right\|=\left(\sum_{a\in\Gamma}|u(a)|^{2}d(a)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}|u(x)|^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Also, we can define other norms which might be useful in the applications:
for
p\in\left[1,\infty\right),
we set

\left\|u\right\|_{p}=\left(\sum_{a\in\Gamma}|u(a)|^{p}\ d(a)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}|u(x)|^{p}\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

and, for
p=\infty
, obviously, we set

\left\|u\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{a\in\Gamma}\ |u(a)|.

Notice that all these norms are equivalent in the sense that given any two
norms
\left\|u\right\|_{p}
and
\left\|u\right\|_{q},

there exists two numbers
m
and
M
such that
\forall u\in V{{}^{\circ}},

m\leq\frac{\left\|u\right\|_{p}}{\left\|u\right\|_{q}}\leq M

(47)

Of course, if
p\neq q
,
m
is an infinitesimal number and
M
is an
infinite number.

The scalar product (
43
) also allows to define the
delta (or
the Dirac) ultrafunction
as follows: for every
a\in\Gamma
,

\delta_{a}(x)=\frac{\chi_{a}(x)}{d(a)}.

(48)

As it is natural to expect, for every
u\in V{{}^{\circ}},
we have that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\delta_{a}(x)u(x)dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{x\in\Gamma}u(x)\delta_{a}(x)d(x)=\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{x\in\Gamma}u(x)\frac{\chi_{a}(x)}{d(a)}d(x)=u(a)

The delta ultrafunctions are orthogonal with each other with respect to the
pointwise scalar product; hence, if normalized, they provide an orthonormal
basis, called
delta-basis
, given by

\left\{\sqrt{\delta_{a}}\right\}_{a\in\Gamma}=\left\{\frac{\chi_{a}}{\sqrt{d(a)}}\right\}_{a\in\Gamma}

So, the identity (
15
) can be rewritten as follows:

u(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(\xi)\sqrt{\delta_{a}(\xi)}d\xi\right)\sqrt{\delta_{a}(x)}.

As we already remarked the Leibniz rule does not hold for ultrafunctions and
it is not possible to define a generalized derivative which has this
property. It is easy to check this fact for idempotent functions, in fact by
the Leibniz rule, we should have

D\chi_{E}^{2}=2\chi_{E}D\chi_{E}

and since
\chi_{E}^{2}=\chi_{E},
we deduce that

derivative. Actually the Schwartz impossibility theorem states that the
Leibniz rule cannot be satisfied by any algebra which contains, not only the
idempotent functions, but also the continuous functions (see
[
39
]
,
[
18
]
). So it is interesting to investigate the subspaces of
ultrafunctions for which the Leibniz rule holds and more in general to
determine spaces in which many of the usual properties of smooth functions
be satisfied. This is important for the applications when we want to study
the qualitative properties (and in particular the regularity) of the
solutions of an equation.

3.6

In evolution problems the time variable plays a different role that the
space variables; then the functional spaces used in these problems (e.g.

ultrafunctions. This section is devoted in the description of the
appropriate ultrafunction-spaces for evolution problems.

First of all we need to recall some well known facts about free modules:

Definition 3.15
.

Given a ring
R

M
over
R

B\subset M

for
M

•

B
is a generating set for
M

M

R

•

B

Definition 3.16

A free module is a module with a basis.

The following is a well known theorem:

Theorem 3.17

If
R
is a commutative ring and
M
is a free
R

bases of
E
have the same cardinality. The cardinality of a basis is called

We will describe some free modules which will be used in the following part
of this paper.

Then we may think of

as a real function defined in

or a function in

(ii) Let

be a vector
space of finite dimension, then

C^{k}(\mathbb{R},W):=C^{k}(\mathbb{R})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}W\subset\mathfrak{F}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\right)

is a free
C^{k}
-module of rank equal to
\dim W
, namely every function
f\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R},W)
can be written as follows:

f(t,x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\dim W}c_{k}(t)e_{k}(x)

where
c_{k}\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R})
and
\left\{e_{k}\right\}
is any basis
in
W
.

(iii) If
W=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ W_{\lambda}\subset\mathfrak{F}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)^{\ast}
is an internal vector space of
hyperfinite dimension, then by
C^{k}(\mathbb{E},W)
we denote the internal
C^{k}(\mathbb{R})^{\ast}
-module defined by

C^{k}(\mathbb{E},W)=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ C^{k}(\mathbb{R},W_{\lambda}).

Now we are ready to define the time-dependent ultrafunctions:

Definition 3.18
.

The space of
time dependent ultrafunctions
of order
k\in\mathbb{N}
is the free
C^{k}(\mathbb{R})^{\ast}
-module given by

C^{k}(\mathbb{E},V{{}^{\circ}}):=\left\{u{{}^{\circ}}=u|_{\mathbb{E}\times\Gamma}\ |\ u\in C^{k}(\mathbb{E},V_{\Lambda})\right\}.

Every time-dependent ultrafunction can be represented by the following
hyperfinite sum:

u(t,x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}c(t)\chi_{a}(x)

where
c(t)\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R})^{\ast}
and
\left\{\chi_{a}(x)\right\}_{a\in\Gamma}
is the canonical basis of
V{{}^{\circ}}
. The map

({{}^{\circ}}):C^{k}(\mathbb{E},V_{\Lambda})\rightarrow C^{k}(\mathbb{E},V{{}^{\circ}});\ \ u{{}^{\circ}}=u|_{\mathbb{E}\times\Gamma}

is an isomorphism between free
C^{k}(\mathbb{R})^{\ast}
-modules: in fact,
by using (
20
), we have that
\left\{\sigma_{a}\right\}_{a\in\Gamma}
is a basis of
C^{k}(\mathbb{E},V_{\Lambda})
and we have that

\left(\sum_{a\in\Gamma}c(t)\sigma_{a}(x)\right)^{\circ}=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}c(t)\chi_{a}(x)

(56)

The restriction map
({{}^{\circ}})
can be extended to a
C^{k}(\mathbb{R})^{\ast}
-module homomorphism

({{}^{\circ}}):C^{k}(\mathbb{R},\mathfrak{F}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right))^{\ast}\rightarrow C^{k}(\mathbb{E},V{{}^{\circ}})

by setting

w{{}^{\circ}}(t,x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}w(t,a)\chi_{a}(x)

and, of course, by (
51
),

w_{\Lambda}(t,x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}w(t,a)\sigma_{a}(x)\in C^{k}(\mathbb{E},V{{}^{\circ}})

In particular, if
f\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R},\mathfrak{F}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)),
we have that

f{{}^{\circ}}(t,x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}f^{\ast}(t,a)\chi_{a}(x)\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ f_{\Lambda}(t,x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}f^{\ast}(t,a)\sigma_{a}(x)

(57)

Observe that, unlikely of (
22
), in this case
f_{\Lambda}(t,x)\neq f^{\ast}(t,x)
for some
t\in\mathbb{E}\backslash\mathbb{R}
;
this fact does not prevent the theory to work, but, in some circumstances,
it is necessary to be careful.

The notion of generalized derivative in the space variable is trivially
defined by linearity:

D_{i}u(t,x)=D_{i}\left(\sum_{a\in\Gamma}c(t)\chi_{a}(x)\right)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}c(t)D_{i}\chi_{a}(x).

(58)

It is not necessary to introduce a generalized time-derivative, since the
natural derivative

\partial_{t}^{\ast}:C^{k+1}(\mathbb{E},V{{}^{\circ}})\rightarrow C^{k}(\mathbb{E},V{{}^{\circ}}),\ k\geq 0,

is well defined by setting

\partial_{t}^{\ast}u(t,x)=\partial_{t}^{\ast}\left(\sum_{a\in\Gamma}c(t)\chi_{a}(x)\right)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{\ast}c(t)\chi_{a}(x).

(59)

In our applications, we do not need a generalized nor a weak time-derivative
for the functions in
C^{0}(\mathbb{E},V{{}^{\circ}}).

Theorem 3.19
.

If
f\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R},C_{c}^{m,1})
and
m\geq 0
, then,
\ \partial_{t}^{\ast}f{{}^{\circ}}\in C^{k-1}(\mathbb{E},U^{m})
and

\partial_{t}^{\ast}f{{}^{\circ}}(t,x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{\ast}f^{\ast}(t,a)\chi_{a}(x).

Moreover, for
i=1,...,N\
and
m\geq 1
,
\ D_{i}f{{}^{\circ}}\in C^{k}(\mathbb{E},U^{m-1}),
and

D_{i}f{{}^{\circ}}(t,x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\partial_{i}^{\ast}f^{\ast}(t,a)\chi_{a}(x).

Proof
. The first equality follows immediately from (
57
) and
(
59
).

Let us prove the second statement. By the definition
3.11
, if
w\in C^{k}(\mathbb{E},U^{m}),

w_{\Lambda}\left(t,x\right)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}w(t,a)\sigma_{a}(x)\in C^{k}(\mathbb{E},U_{\Lambda}^{m});\

and by
3.12
.
2
,
\forall t\in\mathbb{E}
,
D_{i}w(t,\cdot)=\left[\partial_{i}^{\ast}w_{\Lambda}(t,\cdot)\right]{{}^{\circ}}\in U^{m-1}
and hence

D_{i}w(t,x)=\left[\partial_{i}^{\ast}w_{\Lambda}(t,x)\right]{{}^{\circ}}=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\partial_{i}^{\ast}w(t,a)\chi_{a}(x)

In particular, by (
57
), if
f\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R},C_{c}^{m,1}),

f_{\Lambda}\left(t,x\right)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}f^{\ast}(t,a)\sigma_{a}(x)\in C^{k}(\mathbb{E},U_{\Lambda}^{m})

and hence

D_{i}f{{}^{\circ}}(t,x)=\left[\partial_{i}^{\ast}f^{\ast}(t,x)\right]{{}^{\circ}}=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\partial_{i}^{\ast}f^{\ast}(t,a)\chi_{a}(x).

\square

4
Basic properties of ultrafunctions

In this section we analyze some properties of the fine ultrafunction that
seems interesting in themselves and/or relevant in the applications.

4.1
Ultrafunctions and measures

As we have seen, if
f\notin V
, in general

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)\ dx\neq\int f(x)\ dx

since
\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)dx
takes account of the value of
f
in any single point. Thus it is a
natural question to ask if there exists an ultrafunction
u
some way
related to
f
such that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u\ dx=\int f~dx.

(60)

This question has an easy answer if we think of
f
as the density of a
measure
\mu_{f}
. In fact, the following definition appears quite natural:

Definition 4.1
.

If
\mu\
is a Radon measure, we define an ultrafunction
\mu{{}^{\circ}}
as follows: for every
v\in V{{}^{\circ}},
we set

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}v(x)\mu{{}^{\circ}}(x)dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int v_{\lambda}(x)d\mu

Notice that the existence of
\mu{{}^{\circ}}
is guaranteed by Th.
3.8
, since

\Phi(v):=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int v_{\lambda}(x)d\mu

is an internal linear functional over
V{{}^{\circ}}
.

Example:
1 - If
\mu_{f}
is a measure whose density is
f\in\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{1},
then,
\forall v\in V{{}^{\circ}}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}v\mu_{f}^{\circ}~dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int f(x)v_{\lambda}(x)dx=\int^{\ast}f^{\ast}v_{\Lambda}dx.

then, taking
f\in\mathcal{L}^{1},
and
v=1{{}^{\circ}}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\mu_{f}^{\circ}dx=\int f~dx;

then (
60
) holds with
u=\mu_{f}^{\circ}.

Example:
2 - If
\mathbf{\delta}_{a}
is the Dirac measure, then
\mathbf{\delta}_{a}^{\circ}=\delta_{a}
where
\delta_{a}
is the Dirac
ultrafunction defined by (
48
).

Example:
3 - For all
u\in V{{}^{\circ}},\ \partial_{i}u_{\Lambda}
is a measure and we will denote by
\mu_{\partial_{i}u}
the related ultrafunction, namely, using the notation (
33
), we have that,
\forall v\in V{{}^{\circ}}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}v\mu_{\partial_{i}u}dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int\partial_{i}u_{\lambda}v_{\lambda}d\mu

and hence, by (
39
)

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}v\mu_{\partial_{i}u}dx=\int^{\ast}\left(\partial_{i}^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}\right)v_{\Lambda}dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}u~v~dx

(61)

namely

D_{i}u=\mu_{\partial_{i}u}.

(62)

Example:
4 - If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a set of finite
measure, and
\mu_{\Omega}
is the measure whose density is
\chi_{\Omega}
, then
\forall u\in V{{}^{\circ}},
we have that

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \int u_{\lambda}(x)d\mu_{\Omega}=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \int u_{\lambda}(x)\chi_{\Omega}dx

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}(x)\chi_{\Omega}^{\ast}~dx=\int_{\Omega^{\ast}}^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}(x)dx.

In particular, if
f\in V,

\int_{\Omega}f\left(x\right)dx=\int^{\ast}f^{\ast}(x)\chi_{\Omega}^{\ast}~dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)dx

(64)

Example:
5 - If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
has a smooth
boundary
\partial\Omega
whose
(N-1)
-dimensional measure is denoted by
S_{\partial\Omega},
we have that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)S_{\partial\Omega}^{\circ}(x)dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \int u_{\lambda}(x)dS_{\partial\Omega}=\int^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}(x)~dS_{\partial\Omega}^{\ast}.

(65)

Caveat:
It is absolutely natural to generalize equation (
26
) by the following definition:

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{E}u(x)dx:=\sum_{a\in E}u(a)d(a)

(66)

for every set
E\subset\Gamma.
At this point in is important to notice
that for a measurable set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
f\in C^{0}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}f{{}^{\circ}}\left(x\right)dx\neq\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)dx=\int_{\Omega}f\left(x\right)dx.

(67)

and

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}f{{}^{\circ}}\left(x\right)dx\neq\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)S_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\left(x\right)dS_{\partial\Omega}.

The next proposition shows a useful way to represent
\mu_{f}^{\circ}
:

Proposition 4.2
.

If

, then

\mu_{f}^{\circ}(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\left(\int^{\ast}f^{\ast}(y)\sigma_{a}(y)dy\right)\delta_{a}(x)

(68)

where
\left\{\sigma_{a}(x)\right\}_{a\in\Gamma}
is the
\sigma
-basis (see (
20
)).

Proof:
Let
\sigma_{a,\lambda}\
be the net such that
\sigma_{a}=\ \lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \sigma_{a,\lambda}.
Then, by (
48
),

\displaystyle\mu_{f}^{\circ}(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\mu_{f}^{\circ}(y)\delta_{a}(y)dy=\frac{1}{d(a)}\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\mu{{}^{\circ}}(y)\chi_{a}(y)dy=\frac{1}{d(a)}\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int f(y)\sigma_{a,\lambda}\left(y\right)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d(a)}\int^{\ast}f^{\ast}(y)\sigma_{a}(y)dy

Hence,

\displaystyle\mu_{f}^{\circ}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\mu_{f}^{\circ}(a)\chi_{a}(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\left(\frac{1}{d(a)}\int^{\ast}f^{\ast}(y)\sigma_{a}(y)dy\right)\chi_{a}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\left(\int^{\ast}f^{\ast}(y)\sigma_{a}(y)dy\right)\ \delta_{a}(x)

\square

Prop.
4.2
suggests to generalize the operator

\mu_{E}(x)=\mu_{\chi_{E}}(x).

(69)

By the pointwise representation of

(see (

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)\mu_{E}(x)dx=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}u(a)\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{a}(x)\mu_{E}(x)dx;

then setting

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)\mu_{E}(x)dx=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}u(a)d_{E}(a)

generalizing eq. (

) and (

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)\ d_{E}x=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)\mu_{E}(x)dx

In particular, if we take

\int_{\Omega}f(x)dx=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}f{{}^{\circ}}(a)d_{\Omega}(a)=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}(x)d_{\Omega}x

We end this section with the following

Proposition 4.3
.

\mu_{w}(x)
is the element of
V{{}^{\circ}}
characterized by the following identity:
\forall u\in V{{}^{\circ}},

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u\left(x\right)\mu_{w}(x)dx=\int^{\ast}u_{\Lambda}\left(x\right)w_{\Lambda}(x)dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}w(x)\mu_{u}\left(x\right)dx

(72)

Proof.
We have that

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\mu_{w}(x)u\left(x\right)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\left(\int^{\ast}w_{\Lambda}(y)\sigma_{a}(y)dy\right)\delta_{a}(x)u\left(x\right)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\left(\int^{\ast}w_{\Lambda}(y)\sigma_{a}(y)dy\right)\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\delta_{a}(x)u\left(x\right)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\left(\int^{\ast}w_{\Lambda}(y)\sigma_{a}(y)u\left(a\right)dy\right)=\int^{\ast}w_{\Lambda}(y)\left[\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\sigma_{a}(y)u\left(a\right)\right]dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{\ast}w_{\Lambda}(y)u_{\Lambda}\left(y\right)dy.

The last equality follows by symmetry.

\square

4.2
The
vicinity
of a set

Given an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
f\in C^{1},
then the
value of
\nabla f(x_{0})
in a point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
depends
only on the values which
f
takes in
\Omega
since

\nabla f(x_{0})=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Omega,\\
x\rightarrow x_{0}\end{subarray}}\ \nabla f(x_{0})

If
f
is not continuous,
\nabla f
is not defined, but
Df{{}^{\circ}}
makes sense; however
Df{{}^{\circ}}(x_{0})
in a point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}
depends on the values which
f
takes in suitable points
y\sim x_{0}

even if
y\notin\Omega{{}^{\circ}}.
Roughly speaking, the vicinity of a set
E
consists of the set of points
which influence the derivative of the points in
E.
In order to make this
definition precise, we need to define a function
\theta_{E}\in V{{}^{\circ}}
for every
E\subset\Gamma
. If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a set
such that
\bar{\chi}_{\Omega}\in V,
we set

\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}=\bar{\chi}_{\Omega}^{\circ}

(73)

where the operator
u\mapsto\bar{u}
has been defined by (
30
). We
want to generalize the above formula to every set
E\subset\Gamma.
We put

\left(\chi_{E}\right)_{\Lambda}=\sum_{a\in E}\sigma_{a}(x)

and

\theta_{E}=\left[\overline{\left(\chi_{E}\right)_{\Lambda}}\right]{{}^{\circ}}

In the above formula, the operator
u\mapsto\bar{u}
who has been defined
by (
30
) for
u\in\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{\infty},
has been extended to

\left(\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{\infty}\right)^{\ast}.\ \theta_{E}
is
similar to the measure density
\mu_{E}
but it is slightly different; for
example we have that
\forall f\in C^{1,1}
and
\forall\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N},
bounded and open,

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}\mu_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}dx=\int f\ dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}f{{}^{\circ}}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}dx

since
f{{}^{\circ}}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\in V;
however, if you take

you have that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\mu_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}dx=\int\bar{\chi}_{\Omega}\ dx=m\left(\Omega\right)

and, if we assume that
\partial\Omega
is smooth, by (
32
), we have
that

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\bar{\chi}_{\Omega}^{\circ}dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}dx-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{{}_{\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}}dx=m\left(\Omega\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\chi_{{}_{\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}}dx.

Now we can state the following definitions:

Definition 4.4
.

Given an internal set
E\subset\Gamma,
we define the

of

.

For any internal set

and we will call
\mathfrak{bd}\left(E\right)

\mathfrak{int}(E)

of

.

If we take

is called the

4.3
The Gauss’ divergence theorem

In this section we want to generalize the Gauss’ divergence theorem in the
framework of the ultrafunction; in particular it is interesting to analyze
the case when
\partial\Omega
is not smooth.

First, we will examine the smooth case. If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

is a bounded set with smooth boundary and
\phi
is a smooth vector field,
we have that

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\phi\ dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}\ dS_{\partial\Omega}

where
S_{\partial\Omega}
denotes the
(N-1)
-dimensional measure over
\partial\Omega
and
\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}(x)
is the exterior normal
derivative. For future purposes, we assume that
\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}(x)
is
a
C_{c}^{1}
-function defined in all
\mathbb{R}^{N}
which coincides with
the exterior normal derivative in
\partial\Omega.
We have the following
result:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open set such
that
\bar{\chi}_{\Omega}\in V
; then

S_{\partial\Omega}^{\circ}(x)=\left|D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(x)\right|

\forall x\in\mathfrak{bd}\left(\Omega\right)

\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}(x)=-\frac{D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(x)}{\left|D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(x)\right|}.

Proof
: The Gauss divergence theorem can be generalized to vector

\phi\in V^{N}

\int\nabla\cdot\phi\ \bar{\chi}_{\Omega}\ dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}\ dS_{\partial\Omega}

In this case
\nabla\cdot\phi
is a measure and we have used the notation
(
33
). By the transfer principle, we have that
\forall\phi\in\left(V^{N}\right)^{\ast}

By (

\int^{\ast}\nabla^{\ast}\cdot\phi\ v\ dx=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int^{\ast}\partial_{i}\phi_{i}\ v\ dx=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}\phi_{i}\ v{{}^{\circ}}\ dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D\cdot\phi\ v{{}^{\circ}}\ dx;

then, by (
73
) and (
42
), we have that

\int^{\ast}\nabla^{\ast}\cdot\phi\ \bar{\chi}_{\Omega}^{\ast}dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D\cdot\phi~\bar{\chi}_{\Omega}^{\circ}dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D\cdot\phi~\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi~D\cdot\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}dx

(75)

Moreover, by (
65

\int_{\partial\Omega^{\ast}}^{\ast}\phi^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\ast}dS_{\partial\Omega}=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\circ}\ S_{\partial\Omega}^{\circ}dx;

(76)

Then, by (
74
), (
75
) and (
76
),
\forall\phi\in\left(V{{}^{\circ}}\right)^{N}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi\cdot D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\circ}\ S_{\partial\Omega}^{\circ}dx

Now we take
\phi(x)=\delta_{a}(x)\mathbf{e}_{i}
where
a\in\Gamma
and

\left\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\right\}_{i=1,..,N}
is the canonical basis in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(and hence in
\mathbb{E^{N})}
, we replace
\phi
in the
above formula:

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\delta_{a}(x)\mathbf{e}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)\ S_{\partial\Omega}(x)dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\delta_{a}(x)\mathbf{e}_{i}\cdot D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(x)dx.

and we get

\left(\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\circ}(a)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{i}\right)S_{\partial\Omega}(a)=-\mathbf{e}_{i}\cdot D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(a)=-D_{i}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(a);

(77)

then

S_{\partial\Omega}(a)=\sqrt{\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left[\left(\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\circ}(a)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{i}\right)S_{\partial\Omega}(a)\right]^{2}}=\sqrt{\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left[D_{i}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(a)\right]^{2}}=\left|D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(a)\right|

Moreover, using again (
77
) we have that

\left(\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\circ}(a)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{i}\right)\left|D_{i}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(a)\right|=D_{i}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(a);

if
a\in\mathfrak{bd}\left(\Omega\right),
we have that
\left|D_{i}\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(a)\right|\neq 0
and hence

\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\circ}(a)=-\frac{D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(a)}{\left|D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(a)\right|}\cdot

Theorem
4.6
suggests the ”right” generalization of the Gauss’
theorem; given any set

we define

\mathbf{n}_{E}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\frac{D\theta_{E}(x)}{\left|D\theta_{E}(x)\right|}&if\ \ x\in\mathfrak{bd}(E)\\
&\\
0&if\ \ x\notin\mathfrak{bd}(E)\end{array}\right.

(78)

It is amazing that
\mathbf{n}_{E}(x)
makes sense even if
E
consists of a
single point
x_{0}.
Clearly in this case,
\mathfrak{supp}\left(\mathbf{n}_{\left\{x_{0}\right\}}\right)\subset\mathfrak{mon}\left(x_{0}\right).

Theorem 4.7
.

(
Generalized Gauss’ divergence theorem
) Let
\phi:\Gamma\rightarrow\left(V{{}^{\circ}}\right)^{N}
be a (ultrafunctions) vector field and let
E\subseteq\Gamma

be an internal set; then

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D\cdot\phi\ d_{E}x=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{E}\ \left|D\theta_{E}\right|\ dx

Proof
: By (
71
), and (
42
).

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D\cdot\phi\ d_{E}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D\cdot\phi~\theta_{E}\ dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi\cdot D\theta_{E}~dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi\cdot\frac{D\theta_{E}}{|D\theta_{E}|}~|D\theta_{E}|~dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{E}\ \left|D\theta_{E}\right|\ dx.

\square

It is interesting to compare the above results with the notion of
Caccioppoli set:

Definition 4.8
.

A Caccioppoli set
\Omega
is a Borel set such that
\chi_{\Omega}\in BV,

namely such that
\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})
(the distributional gradient of
\chi_{\Omega}
) is a finite Radon measure. If
\Omega
is a Caccioppoli
set, then the measure
|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|
is defined as follows:
\forall f\in C_{c}^{1},\ f\geq 0
,

\int f\ d\left(|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|\right):=\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\left(f\phi\right)dx\ |\ \phi\in\left(C^{1}\right)^{N},\ \left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1\right\}

The number

p(\Omega):=\int d\left(|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|\right)

(79)

is called Caccioppoli perimeter of
\Omega.
If the reduced boundary of
\Omega
coincides with
\partial\Omega,
we have that (see
[
29
, Section
5.7]
)

\int f(x)\ d\left(|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|\right)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}

(80)

where
\mathcal{H}^{N-1}
is the
(N-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
\partial\Omega.\
The Gauss’ theorem for a Caccioppoli set takes the
following form:

\int_{\partial\Omega}\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}\,d\left(|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|\right)=\int_{\partial\Omega^{\ast}}\nabla\cdot\phi\mathbf{\ }dx

By transfer, we have that for every
\ast
-Borellian vector field
\phi
,

\int_{\partial\Omega^{\ast}}^{\ast}\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\ast}\,d\left(|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|\right)^{\ast}=\int_{\partial\Omega^{\ast}}^{\ast}\nabla^{\ast}\cdot\phi\mathbf{\ }dx

and hence
\forall v\in V_{\Lambda},
and by (
65
) and Th.
4.6
,

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi{{}^{\circ}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}^{\circ}\ \mu_{|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|}\ dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}\ \left|D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\right|\ dx

and by the arbitrariness of
\phi,

\left|D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}(x)\right|=\mu_{|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|}

However, the measure
\left|D\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)\right|
is
more general than
\mu_{|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|}
. For example if
\partial\Omega
is a set with Hausdorff dimension
d>N-1
,
\mu_{|\nabla(\chi_{\Omega})|}
is not defined, while
\left|D\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)\right|
is well defined. Moreover, by the generalization
of (
79
)

p(\Omega):=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left|D\theta_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\right|\ dx

\left|D\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)\right|
allows to define the
perimeter of every set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\
even when
\partial\Omega
is very wild such as e.g.
\Omega=\partial\Omega
. With this
respect, the theory of fine ultrafunctions is a good improvement of the
theory developed in
[
9
]
.

4.4
Ultrafunctions and distributions

One of the most important properties of the ultrafunctions is that they can
be seen (in some sense that we will make precise in this section) as a
generalizations of the distributions.

Definition 4.9
.

We say that an ultrafunction
u
is
distribution-like
(
DL
) if
there exist a distribution
T
such that for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)\varphi{{}^{\circ}}(x)dx=\left\langle T,\varphi\right\rangle

We say that an ultrafunction
u
is
almost

distribution like
if there exist a distribution
T
such that for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u(x)\varphi{{}^{\circ}}(x)dx\sim\left\langle T,\varphi\right\rangle

Example:
The measures

\left|D\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)\right|\
and
\ S_{\partial\Omega}^{\circ}(x)
are
distribution-like since for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left|D\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}\right|\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}S_{\partial\Omega}^{\circ}\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx=\left\langle T_{S_{\partial\Omega}},\varphi\right\rangle

where
T_{S_{\partial\Omega}}
is the distribution related to the measure
S_{\partial\Omega}
.

It is easy to see that:

Proposition 4.10
.

For any distribution
T
there is a distribution-like
ultrafunction
u_{T}
.

Proof
: Let us consider any projection
P_{\lambda}:V_{\lambda}\rightarrow V_{\lambda}\cap\mathcal{D}
and set

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u_{T}(x)v(x)dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \left\langle T,P_{\lambda}v_{\lambda}\right\rangle

By Th.
3.8
,
u_{T}
is well defined. So,
\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u_{T}(x)\varphi{{}^{\circ}}(x)dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \left\langle T,P_{\lambda}\varphi\right\rangle=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \left\langle T,\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle T,\varphi\right\rangle

\square

Clearly
u_{T}
is not univocally defined since, in the proof of Prop.
4.10
, the projection
P_{\lambda}
can be defined arbitrarily. So it make
sense to set

\left[u\right]_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}=\left\{v\in V{{}^{\circ}}\ |\ v\approx_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}u\right\}

where

v\approx_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}u:\Leftrightarrow\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D},\ \mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left(u-v\right)\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx=0

Then there is a bijective map

\Psi:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rightarrow V_{DL}^{\circ}/\approx_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}

(81)

where
V_{DL}^{\circ}
is the set of distribution like ultrafunction and

\Psi(T)=\left\{u\in V{{}^{\circ}}\ |\ \forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D},\ \mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx=\left\langle T,\varphi\right\rangle\right\}

The linear map is
\Psi
consistent with the distributional derivative,
namely:

Proposition 4.11
.

If
\Psi(T)=\left[u\right]_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
then
\Psi(\partial_{i}T)=\left[D_{i}u\right]_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}

Proof
: If
\Psi(T)=\left[u\right]_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},
then

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}u\varphi{{}^{\circ}}~dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}uD_{i}\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx

Since
\varphi{{}^{\circ}}\in U^{\infty},
then by Th.
3.12
.
2
,
D_{i}\varphi{{}^{\circ}}=\left(\partial_{i}\varphi\right){{}^{\circ}}
and so

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}Du\varphi{{}^{\circ}}~dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}uD\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u\left(\partial_{i}\varphi\right){{}^{\circ}}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left\langle T,\partial_{i}\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle\partial_{i}T,\varphi\right\rangle

Hence
\left[D_{i}u\right]_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}=\Psi(\partial_{i}T).

\square

At this point it is a natural question to ask if there exists a linear map

\Phi:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rightarrow V{{}^{\circ}}

which selects in any equivalence class
\left[u\right]_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
a distribution-like ultrafunction
\phi(u)
in a way
consistent with the distributional derivative, namely

\Phi\left(\partial_{i}T\right)=D_{i}\Phi\left(T\right)

(82)

Actually this goal can be achieved in several ways. We will describe one of
them by using Th.
3.8
:

Definition 4.12
.

For every
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, we denote by
T{{}^{\circ}}
the unique ultrafunction in
U^{\infty}
(see(
52
)) such that
\forall\psi\in U^{\infty}

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}T{{}^{\circ}}(x)\psi(x)dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \left\langle T,\psi_{\lambda}\right\rangle=\left\langle T^{\ast},\psi_{\Lambda}\right\rangle^{\ast}

(83)

Clearly,
T{{}^{\circ}}
is a
DL
-ultrafunction since
\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
,
\varphi{{}^{\circ}}\in U^{\infty}
and

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}T{{}^{\circ}}(x)\varphi{{}^{\circ}}dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \left\langle T,\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle T,\varphi\right\rangle.

Theorem 4.13
.

The map
T\mapsto T{{}^{\circ}}
defined by (
83
) satisfies (
82
), namely

\left(\partial_{i}T\right){{}^{\circ}}=D_{i}T{{}^{\circ}}

Proof
: By (
53
) and Th.
3.12
.
50
, we have that
\forall\psi\in U^{\infty},

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D_{i}T{{}^{\circ}}\psi~dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}T{{}^{\circ}}D_{i}\psi~dx=-\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}T{{}^{\circ}}\partial_{i}^{\ast}\psi~dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left\langle T^{\ast},\partial_{i}^{\ast}\psi_{\Lambda}\right\rangle=\left\langle\partial_{i}^{\ast}T^{\ast},\psi_{\Lambda}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left(\partial_{i}T\right){{}^{\circ}}\psi~dx

\square

Every function
f\in\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{1}
defines a distribution
T_{f};

then, given
f
, we can define three ultrafunctions:
f{{}^{\circ}},

\mu_{f}^{\circ}
and
T_{f}^{\circ}.
What is the relation between
them? By Prop.
4.2
, we have that
\mu_{f}^{\circ}
is a projection
of
f^{\ast}
over
V{{}^{\circ}}
, namely

\mu_{f}^{\circ}(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\left(\int^{\ast}f^{\ast}(x)\sigma_{a}(y)dy\right)\delta_{a}(x)

Similarly also
T_{f}^{\circ}
is a projection, but over a smaller space as
the following proposition shows:

Proposition 4.14
.

If
f\in\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{1},
then

T_{f}^{\circ}=\Pi_{\infty}f{{}^{\circ}}.

where
\Pi_{\infty}
has been defined by (
54
).

Proof
: By (
53
), we have that
\psi\in U^{\infty},

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\Pi_{\infty}f{{}^{\circ}}\psi~dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\int f\psi_{\lambda}\ dx=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \left\langle T,\psi_{\lambda}\right\rangle=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}T_{f}^{\circ}\psi~dx

Since both
T_{f}^{\circ}
and
\Pi_{\infty}f{{}^{\circ}}\in U^{\infty}
, the conclusion follows.

\square

So, if
f\in\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{1},

T_{f}^{\circ},
similarly to
\mu_{f}^{\circ}
, destroys some information contained in
f;
namely
T_{f}^{\circ}
(resp.
\mu_{f}^{\circ}
) cannot be distiguished by
T_{g}^{\circ}
(resp.
\mu_{f}^{\circ}
) if
f
and
g
agree almost
everywere. Similarly, if
\mu
is any Radon measure and
T_{\mu}
is the
corresponding distribution, then
T_{\mu}^{\circ}
destroys some
information contained in
\mu{{}^{\circ}}
since

T_{\mu}^{\circ}=\Pi_{\infty}\mu{{}^{\circ}}

Example
: The
\delta_{a}
ultrafunction is distribution like since
for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\delta_{a}\varphi{{}^{\circ}}(x)dx=\varphi(a)=\left\langle\mathbf{\delta}_{a},\varphi\right\rangle;

(here we have used the boldface to distinguish the ultrafunction
\delta_{a}
from the distribution
\mathbf{\delta}_{a}
). However

\delta_{a}\neq\mathbf{\delta}_{a}^{\circ}

Actually, according to Def.
3.13
,

\delta_{a}=\mathbf{\delta}_{a}^{\circ}+\Pi_{\infty}^{\perp}\delta_{a},

namely
\mathbf{\delta}_{a}^{\circ}\
is the smooth part of
\delta_{a}.

5
Some applications

In this section we will sketch how the theory of fine ultrafunctions can be
used in the study of Partial Differential Equations. In the framework of
ultrafunctions, a very large class of problems is well posed and has
solutions. Very often, hard
a priori
estimates are not necessary in
proving the existence, but only in understanding the properties of a
solution (qualitative analysis). In particular, if you have a problem from
Physics or from Geometry, it is interesting to investigate whether the
generalized solutions describe the Physical or the Geometric phenomenon. We
refer to
[
6
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
15
]
,..,
[
23
]
where such kind of problems have been treated in the framework of
ultrafunction.
A fortiori
, these problems can be treated using fine
ultrafunctions. In this section, we limit ourselves to give some new
examples just to illustrate the use of fine ultrafunctions with a particular
emphasis in the study of ill posed problems. Obviously, each example is
treated superficially. A deep analysis of each case, probably, would deserve
a full paper.

5.1
Second order equations in divergence form

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open set with regular
boundary and let us consider the following boundary value problem:

-\nabla\cdot\left[k(x,u)\nabla u\right]+f(x,u)=0\ \ \ in\ \ \ \Omega

(84)

u(x)=0\ \ \ for\ \ \ x\in\partial\Omega

(85)

where
f
is a function and
k(x,u)
is a function or a
\left(N\times N\right)
-matrix.

A function which satisfies (
84
) and (
85
) is called classical
solution if
u\in C^{2}\left(\overline{\Omega}\right).
The natural
”translation” of this problem in the world of ultrafunctions is the
following:

-D\cdot\left[k^{\ast}(x,u)Du\right]+f^{\ast}(x,u)=0\ \ \ in\ \ \ \Omega{{}^{\circ}}

(86)

u(x)=0\ \text{\ }\ if\ \ \ x\in\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}

(87)

We must be very careful in the interpretation of eq. (
86
). In fact
Du

makes sense if
u
is defined in
\mathfrak{vic}\left(\Omega\right)\
and

D\cdot\left[k^{\ast}(x,u)Du\right]
makes sense if
u
is defined in
\mathfrak{vic}^{2}\left(\Omega\right):=\mathfrak{vic}\left(\mathfrak{vic}\left(\Omega\right)\right).
Thus the problem (
86
),(
84
) is
well posed if

f(x,\cdot)\ \text{and\ }k(x,\cdot)\ \text{are defined in a neighborhood of
}\Omega.

(88)

In particular we have that
f^{\ast}(x,\cdot)
,
k^{\ast}(x,\cdot)
and
consequently
u
are definite in

\Omega^{+}:=\mathfrak{vic}^{2}\left(\Omega\right).

In conclusion, we are lead to the following definition:

Definition 5.1
.

A solution of (
86
), (
87
), is the restriction to
\Omega{{}^{\circ}}
of an ultrafunction
u\in V{{}^{\circ}}.

u|_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}
will be called ultrafunction solution of the problem (
84
), (
85
).

This is the right definition as the following theorem shows:

Theorem 5.2
.

If
w
is a classical solution of (
84
),(
85
) then
u=w^{\ast}|_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}
is a ultrafunction solution.

Proof
: By our definition of
C^{2}\left(\overline{\Omega}\right),
we have that
w
can be extended in a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\left(\Omega\right)
of
\Omega;\
consequently also
f(x,\cdot)
and
k(x,\cdot)
can be extended in
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\left(\Omega\right)
and hence, by the transfer principle,
w^{\ast}

satisfies the equation

-\nabla^{\ast}\cdot\left[k^{\ast}(x,w^{\ast})\nabla^{\ast}w^{\ast}\right]+f^{\ast}(x,w^{\ast})=0\ \ \ \forall x\in\mathcal{N}\left(\Omega\right)^{\ast}

where, with some abuse of notation we have denoted with
f(x,\cdot)
and
k(x,\cdot)
the extension of the homonymous functions. Then,
u=w^{\ast}|_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}
satisfies (
86
),(
84
).

\square

If a problem does not have a classical solution, we can look for weak
solutions in some Sobolev space or in a space of distribution. However if
there are not weak solutions, we can find a ultrafunction solution
exploiting the following theorem:

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume (
88
). If the
\exists M,R\in\mathbb{E}^{+}
such
that

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle R\Rightarrow\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega^{+}}\left[k^{\ast}(x,u)Du\cdot Dv+f^{\ast}(x,u)u\right]\ dx

(89)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle M\cdot\sqrt{\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega^{+}}\left|u\right|^{2}dx},

then problem (
86
),(
87
) has at least one solution.

Proof
: First of all we set

V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right):=\left\{u\in V{{}^{\circ}}\ |\ \forall x\in\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}},\ u(x)=0\right\};

(90)

V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)
is a hyperfinite Hilbert space equipped with the norm

\left\|u\right\|:=\sqrt{\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left|u\right|^{2}dx}

Now let
\Psi
be a continuous function such that
\Psi(x)=1
if
x\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon/2}\left(\Omega\right),

\Psi(x)=0
if
x\notin\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\left(\Omega\right)\
and
0\leq\Psi\leq 1.
We put

\hat{f}(x,u)=f^{\ast}(x,u)\Psi^{\ast}(x)

\hat{k}(x,u)=k^{\ast}(x,u)\Psi^{\ast}(x)+\left[1-\Psi^{\ast}(x)\right]

and we define an operator

\mathcal{A}:V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)\rightarrow V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)

by setting,
\forall v\in V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right),

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\mathcal{A}\left[u\right]v\ dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left[\hat{k}(x,u)Du\cdot Dv+\hat{f}(x,u)v\right]\ dx.

By (
89
), we have that
\exists M,R\in\mathbb{E}^{+}
such that

\left\|u\right\|\geq R\Rightarrow\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\mathcal{A}\left[u\right]u\ dx\geq M\left\|u\right\|^{2}.

Then, by the Brower fixed point theorem and the fact that
V^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)
has hyperfinite dimension the equation
\mathcal{A}\left[u\right]=0\
has at least a solution
\bar{u}\in V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)
, and hence
\forall v\in V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\mathcal{A}\left[\bar{u}\right]v\ dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left[\hat{k}(x,\bar{u})D\bar{u}\cdot Dv+\hat{f}(x,\bar{u})v\right]\ dx

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left[-D\cdot\left(\hat{k}(x,\bar{u})D\bar{u}\right)+\hat{f}(x,\bar{u})\right]v\ dx

and hence, for every
x_{0}\in\Omega{{}^{\circ}},
taking
v=\delta_{x_{0}},

-D\cdot\left(k^{\ast}(x_{0},\bar{u})D\bar{u}\right)+f^{\ast}(x_{0},\bar{u})=0.

since for
x_{0}\in\Omega{{}^{\circ}},

\hat{k}=k^{\ast}
and
\hat{f}=f^{\ast}.
Then
\bar{u}|_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}
solves (
86
),(
87
).

\square

Remark 5.4
.

By the above theorem we have that
\bar{u}|_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}
might depend on the values that
k^{\ast}
and
f^{\ast}
assume in
\Omega^{+}
and hence, assumption (
88
) seems essential. However
Th.
5.2
shows that when
\bar{u}|_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}
is sufficiently regular, this dependence disappears.

The solution of (
86
),(
87
) is the restriction
u|_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}
of an ultrafunction
u\in V{{}^{\circ}}.
It is interesting to examine the nature of
u
near
\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}.
By the definition of
V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right),
we have that

v\in V{{}^{\circ}}\Longleftrightarrow v\chi_{\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\in V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)

So, the equation (
91
) is equivalent to

\forall v\in V{{}^{\circ}},\ \mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left[\hat{k}(x,u)Du\cdot D\left(v\chi_{\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\right)+f^{\ast}(x,u)\left(v\chi_{\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\right)\right]dx=0

Then, we have that,
\forall v\in V{{}^{\circ}},

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left[-D\cdot\left[\hat{k}(x,u)Du\right]\chi_{\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}+f^{\ast}(x,u)\chi_{\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\right]vdx=0

(92)

and hence

\left(-D\cdot\left[\hat{k}(x,u)Du\right]+f^{\ast}(x,u)\right)\chi_{\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}=0.

(93)

If we set

\displaystyle\left[V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)\right]^{\perp}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle=\left\{\Phi\in V{{}^{\circ}}\ |\ \forall v\in V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right),\ \mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\Phi(x)v(x)dx=0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\Phi\in V{{}^{\circ}}\ |\ \mathfrak{supp}\left(\Phi\right)\subseteq\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right\}

eq. (
93
) can be rewritten as follows:

-D\cdot\left[\hat{k}(x,u)Du\right]+\hat{f}(x,u)=\Phi\left(x\right)\ \ \ with\ \ \ \Phi\in\left[V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)\right]^{\perp}.

(94)

This equation describes in terms of infinitesimal analysis what happens near

\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}.

\Phi\left(x\right)
can be regarded as a force which constrains
u

to vanish on
\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}.
Essentially
\Phi
describes the reaction of a constraint.

Remark 5.5
.

If we want to consider equation (
84
) with non-homogeneous
boundary condition, i.e.

u(x)=g(x)\ \ \ for\ \ \ x\in\partial\Omega

we can adopt the standard trick to set

w(x)=u(x)-\bar{g}(x)

where
\bar{g}(x)
is any function which extends

in
\Omega
and to
solve the resulting equation in

with the homogeneous boundary conditions.

Remark 5.6
.

The equation (
84
) with the homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions,
i.e.

\frac{du}{d\mathbf{n}}(x)=\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}=0\ \ \ for\ \ \ x\in\partial\Omega

(95)

can be treated in a very similar way. The boundary condition, in the
framework of the ultrafunctions becomes

Du\cdot\mathbf{n}_{E}=0\ \ for\ x\in\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}

(96)

where
\mathbf{n}_{E}
has been defined by (

). The space

V_{\text{{neu}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right):=\left\{u\in V{{}^{\circ}}\ |\ \forall x\in\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}},\ Du\cdot\mathbf{n}_{E}=0\right\}.

Everything else proceeds in a similar way.

5.2
Examples

Example 1
: Let us consider the following problem:

-\nabla\cdot\left[k(x,u)\nabla u\right]+u=f(x)\ \ \ in\ \ \Omega

(97)

u(x)=0,\ \ for\ \ x\in\partial\Omega

(98)

where

. We set

k_{0}:=\ \inf\ \left\{k(x,s)\ |\ \left(x,s\right)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\right\}

and

k_{0}>0,

(99)

then, if

k\ \text{does\ not\ depend\ on\ }u,

(100)

A=-\nabla\cdot\left[k\left(x\right)\nabla u\right]+u
is a strictly

If (

(102)

Proof:
By Th.
5.3
we have to prove that the operator is (

) coercive, namely that (

) holds. By (

) and the

, there exists a constant

Then, setting

k_{\infty}=\sup\left\{-k(x,u)\ |\ \left|u\right|<M\right\}

for any
M\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega^{+}}\left[k(x,u)\left|Du\right|^{2}+\left|u\right|^{2}\right]dx

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega^{+},\left|u\right|\geq M}k(x,u)\left|Du\right|^{2}dx+\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega^{+},\left|u\right|<M}k(x,u)\left|Du\right|^{2}dx+\left\|u\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\left\|D\right\|^{2}}\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left|Du\right|^{2}dx-k_{\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega^{+},\left|u\right|<M}\left|Du\right|^{2}dx+\left\|u\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\left\|D\right\|^{2}}\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left|Du\right|^{2}dx-k_{\infty}\underset{u\neq 0,\left|u\right|\leq M}{\max}\left\|Du\right\|+\left\|u\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{\left\|Du\right\|^{2}}{2\left\|D\right\|^{2}}-C+\left\|u\right\|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\|u\right\|^{2}-C

\square

If

is not positive, our problem might have infinitely many solutions and

: Let us consider the following problem:

u\in C^{2}\left(\overline{\Omega}\right):

u(x)=0,\ \ for\ \ x\in\partial\Omega

Proof:
By Th.

5.3
Regular weak solutions

The expression

(108)

such that

, we have the following result:

.

If the operator

\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\mathcal{A}\left[u\right]v~dx=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}\left[k^{\ast}(x,u)Du\cdot Dv+f^{\ast}(x,u)v\right]dx\ \ \forall v\in U^{1}(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}),

) has at least one solution.

), (

).

Remark 5.10
.

If we adopt the strategy of using regular weak solutions, the more
appropriate functional framework is the use of the quotient space

\widetilde{U^{1}}:=V{{}^{\circ}}/I

where

I:=U_{\text{{dir}}}^{1}(\Omega{{}^{\circ}})^{\perp}=\left\{\psi\in V{{}^{\circ}}\ |\ \forall v\in U^{1},\ \mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\psi v\ dx=0\right\}

The space
\widetilde{U^{1}}
is the analogous, in the world of
ultrafunctions, of the Sobolev space
H^{1};
in fact in both cases the
functions are not defined pointwise, but they are classes of equivalence of
functions defined up to negligible functions.

Remark 5.11
.

For suitable choices of
k,

f
and
\Omega,
it is possible
that the regular weak solutions coincide with the ultrafunctions solutions,
however for ill posed problem in general this fact does not happens.
Clearly, in most cases the regular weak solutions are infinitely close to
ultrafunctions solutions in the appropriate topology (e.g. in the topology
of
H^{1}\left(\Omega\right)).
However, if
\partial\Omega
is very
wild they can differ from each other in a relevant way. Then, in dealing
with a problem relative to a physical phenomenon, the choice of the space in
which to work might be very relevant (see also Remark

).

5.4
Calculus of variations

Let us consider the minimization problem of the functional

J(u)=\int_{\Omega}F(x,u,\nabla u)\ dx

(112)

If we assume the Dirichlet boundary condition, the natural space where to
work is
C^{1}(\Omega)\cap C_{0}^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. If we translate
this problem in the framework of ultrafunctions the natural space is
V_{\text{{dir}}}^{1}(\Omega{{}^{\circ}})
defined by (
90
) and the condition (
88
) is translated in
the assumption that
F(x,u,\xi)
be defined in
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\left(\Omega\right)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}.
Then the
functional (
112
) becomes

J{{}^{\circ}}(u):=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}F^{\ast}(x,u,Du)\ d_{\Omega}x.

(113)

and we have the following result:

Theorem 5.12
.

If (
113
) is coercive, then
J{{}^{\circ}}(u)
has a minimizer
\bar{u}
in
V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right).
Moreover, if
J(u)
has a minimizer
w\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}),
then,
\forall x\in\overline{\Omega},

\bar{u}\left(x\right)=w^{\ast}(x).

Proof:
Trivial.

\square

Also in this case it is interesting to see the form assumed by The
Euler-Lagrange equations. For simplicity, we assume that

F^{\ast}(x,u,Du)=\frac{1}{2}k^{\ast}(x,u)\left|Du\right|^{2}+h^{\ast}(x,u)

then,

dJ{{}^{\circ}}(u)\left[v\right]:=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left[k^{\ast}(x,u)Du\cdot Dv+f(x,u)v\right]\ d_{\Omega}x;\ f(x,u)=\frac{\partial h(x,u)}{\partial u}.

By (
63
), we have that,
\forall v\in V_{\text{{dir}}}^{1}(\Omega{{}^{\circ}})

dJ{{}^{\circ}}(u)\left[v\right]:=\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\left[k^{\ast}(x,u)Du\cdot Dv+f(x,u)v\right]\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)\ dx.

and arguing as we did at the end of section
5.1
(see eq. (
94
)) we get the equation

-D\cdot\left[\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)k^{\ast}(x,u)Du\right]+f^{\ast}(x,u)\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)=\Phi\left(x\right)\ \ \ with\ \ \ \Phi\in\left[V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)\right]^{\perp}.

(114)

It is interesting to note that eqs. (
94
) and (
114
) are
slightly different; they coincide for the
x\in\Omega{{}^{\circ}}
when
mon(x)\subset\Omega{{}^{\circ}},
but they differ for some
x\sim\partial\Omega{{}^{\circ}}.
This fact depend on the fact that in the framework of the ultrafunction a
weak solution formulated with the measure
\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}
does not
coincide with a solution defined by (
86
),(
87
). In any case, we
have seen that the regular solutions are the same.

The minimization problem in
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}),
with no constraint
on the boundary, in the regular case leads to equation (
84
) with the
Neumann boundary conditions; in the world of ultrafunctions, this problem
gives to the equation

-D\cdot\left[\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)k^{\ast}(x,u)Du\right]+f^{\ast}(x,u)\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)=0.

(115)

Thus the boundary conditions are included in this equation.

When we deal with the calculus of variations the notion of regular weak
solutions arises in a natural way. In fact, it makes perfect sense to
minimize the functional (
113
) in the spaces

U_{\text{{dir}}}^{m}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right):=V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)\cap U^{m};\ m\geq 0.

If
J{{}^{\circ}}
is coercive, the minimizer exists in each space and if the minimizer in
V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)
is not
m
-regular it is different from the minimizer in
U_{\text{{dir}}}^{m}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)
; this phenomenon is typical of the ultrafunctions and it does not
have any analogous thing in the framework of
C^{m}
-functions or in the
Sobolev spaces.

Example
: Let us consider the functional defined in
C^{1}(0,1)\cap C_{0}^{0}(\left[0,1\right])

J(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left[\left(\left|\partial u\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}+u^{2}\right]\ dx

(116)

which presents the well known Lavrentiev phenomenon, namely every minimizing
sequence

converges uniformly to

but

0=\ \underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}J(u_{n})\neq J(0)=1.

If we use Th.
5.12
, the minimizing sequence
u_{\lambda}\in U_{\lambda}^{1}(\Omega)
has an infinitesimal
\Lambda
-limit
\bar{u}\in U_{\text{{dir}}}^{1}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right)
and
J{{}^{\circ}}(\bar{u})
is a positive infinitesimal which satisfies the following
Euler-Lagrange equations in
\mathfrak{int}(\Omega{{}^{\circ}})
:

D\left[\left(\left|D\bar{u}\right|^{2}-1\right)D\bar{u}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\bar{u}=\psi

(117)

where

\forall v\in U^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp},\ \mathop{\displaystyle\oint}\psi v\ dx=0.

However, it is possible to minimize the functional

in
V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right);
in this case we get a minimizer
\hat{u}
such that
J{{}^{\circ}}(\hat{u})<J{{}^{\circ}}(\bar{u})
and in
\mathfrak{int}(\Omega{{}^{\circ}})
we have:

D\left[\left(\left|D\hat{u}\right|^{2}-1\right)D\hat{u}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\hat{u}=0.

The function
\psi
in equation (

) can be interpreted ad a
structural ”force” which prevents

to form ”angles”. The presence of this
force, increases the energy level and we have that

even if

remains infinitesimal.

Also this example shows how the choice of the space where to work changes
the solution of the problem and hence, as observed in Remarks
5.11

and
3.14
, the choice of a particular space depends on the phenomenon
which we want to describe.

Remark 5.13
.

We have assumed

and

5.5

Let

\partial_{t}u=A(x,\partial_{i})\left[u\right]

(119)

(120)

), (
119
), (

118
), (
119
), (
120
) translates in the following

u\in C^{1}(I,D_{A}^{\circ}\left(\Omega\right))

(121)

\partial_{t}^{\ast}u=A^{\ast}(x,D_{i})\left[u\right],\ t\in I^{\ast}

(122)

(123)

), (

Theorem 5.14

\square

Assume that

\square

5.6
Some examples of evolution problems

Example 1
: Let
\Omega
be a bounded open set and let us consider

\partial_{t}u=\nabla\cdot\left[k(u)\nabla u\right]

(126)

u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)

(127)

If

Let us translate this problem in the world of ultrafunctions; taking account

, we get:

u\in C^{1}(I^{\ast},V_{\text{{dir}}}^{\circ}\left(\Omega{{}^{\circ}}\right))

(128)

\partial_{t}^{\ast}u=D\cdot\left[k^{\ast}(u)Du\right]\ \ for\ \ x\in\Omega{{}^{\circ}}

(129)

u(0,x)=u_{0}^{\circ}(x)

(130)

So we can apply the theorems
5.15
and we get the existence of a unique
(local in time) solution. It is not difficult to get sufficient conditions
which guarantee the existence of a solution for every
t\in I^{\ast}.
For
example

Theorem 5.16
.

If the set

B=\left\{r\in\mathbb{R\ }|\ k(r)<0\right\}

(131)

is bonded, problem (
128
),(

),(

) has a global solution.

Proof
- We set

and with some abuse of

,

Then, we have that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\ast}\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u^{2}d_{\Omega}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}u\partial_{t}^{\ast}u\ d_{\Omega}x=2\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}uD\cdot\left[k^{\ast}(u)Du\right]d_{\Omega}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}k^{\ast}(u)\left|Du\right|^{2}d_{\Omega}x\leq 2\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}k^{-}(u)\left|Du\right|^{2}d_{\Omega}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left\|D\right\|^{2}\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}_{\Omega{{}^{\circ}}}k^{-}(u)\left|u\right|^{2}d_{\Omega}x

If we set

M=\ \underset{r\in\mathbb{R}}{\max}\ k^{-}(r)\left|r\right|^{2}

then, we have that

and this implies the existence of a global solution.

\square

In many applications of eq. (

)

its flow. Then, thanks to the generalized Gauss’ theorem

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}D\cdot\left[k^{\ast}(u)Du\right]d_{\Omega}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\oint}k^{\ast}(u)Du\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}\ \left|D\theta_{E}\right|\ dx

If we want to model a situation where the flow of

,
\Omega

\forall x\in\partial\Omega,\ \frac{du}{d\mathbf{n}}\left(x\right)=0;

\partial_{t}^{\ast}u=D\cdot\left[\mu_{\Omega}^{\circ}(x)k^{\ast}(x,u)Du\right]

In this case, we have the flow

6
A model for ultrafunctions

This section is devoted to prove Th.
6.23
namely to the construction a
space of fine ultrafunctions. The construction presented here is the
simplest which we have been able to find. Nevertheless it is quite involved
but we do not know if a substantially simpler one exists. The main
difficulty relies in the fact that
all
the properties of Def.
3.5
need to be satisfied simultaneously; in particular, properties (
37
) and (
40

Our model of fine ultrafunctions combines the theory of ultrafunctions with
the techniques related to step functions. Roughly speaking we can say that,
in this model, the fine ultrafunctions, as well as the standard continuous
functions, can be well approximated by step functions and this fact is a
cornerstone of our construction.

6.2
Hyperfinite step functions

The step functions are easy to handle ad hence they are largely used many
branches of mathematics and in particular in nonstandard analysis. Let us
describe the kind of step functions that will be considered in this paper.

Given an infinite hypercube

\left\{S_{a}\right\}_{a\in\Gamma}

\Gamma
is an hyperfinite set,

•

the index
a
is a point in
int(S_{a})
and if

then
a=r.

•

\forall a\in\Gamma,

\bar{\chi}_{S_{a}}\in V^{\ast}.

•

the
size
of the partitition, namely the number

\eta=\ \underset{a\in\Gamma}{\max}\ diam\left(S_{a}\right),

is infinitesimal; here
diam\left(S_{a}\right)
denotes the diameter of
S_{a}.

Definition 6.4
.

Given a partition
\left\{S_{a}\right\}_{a\in\Gamma},
a

u(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}u_{a}\chi_{S_{a}}(x).

where

is an internal function
namely a grid function.

The integral of a step function

is given by

\int^{\ast}u(x)dx=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}u(a)m(S_{a});

Given a fine partition
\left\{S_{a}\right\}_{a\in\Gamma},
any internal

u\in\mathfrak{F}(\mathbb{E}^{N},\mathbb{E})
can be approximated
by a step function
\breve{u}
by setting

\breve{u}(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}u(a)\chi_{S_{a}}(x).

(157)

The next theorem shows a very interesting property of the infinitesimal
partition in the contest of the Euclidean numbers.

.

7
Conclusive remarks

The definition
3.5
of fine ultrafunctions extends the notion of real
functions in such a way that:

•

(a) ”almost” all the partial differential equations (and other
functional problems) have a solution;

•

(b) some of the main properties of the smooth functions are preserved.

The assumptions included in definition
3.5
seems quite natural; however
they do not define a unique model and there is a lot of room to require
others properties that allow to prove more facts about the solutions of a
given problem.

Let’s illustrate this point with an example: let us consider equation (
148
); we know that it has a unique solution which preserves the energy;
however there are a lot of questions that are relevant for the physical
interpretation such as:

•

If
f(u)
grows more than
\left|u\right|^{\left(N+2\right)/(N-2)}
most likely, this solution has singular regions
S\subset\Gamma

where the density of energy is an infinite number; is there a precise
theorem? What can we say about the properties of
S?

•

In general the momentum is not ”exactly” preserved since the ”space”
\Gamma
is not invariant for translation (and hence we cannot apply the
Noether’s theorem); then the natural question is to know the initial
conditions for which the momentum is preserved and/or the initial conditions
for which the momentum is preserved up to an infinitesimal.

•

Under which assumptions the solutions converge to
0
as
t\rightarrow\infty?

•

And so on….

Some of these questions have an answer in the context of ultrafunctions
provided that you work enough on a single question. However there are
questions which do not have a YES/NO answer since there are models of
ultrafunctions in which the answer is YES and other in which the answer is
NO. A possible development of the theory is to add to the definition of
ultrafunctions other properties which allows a more detailed description of
the physical phenomenon described by the mathematical model. For example, in
Def.
3.5
we have not included
all
the properties of the
generalized derivative which can be deduced by the choice of
V{{}^{\circ}}
and Def. (
180
). The main difficulty, if you want to add a new
property to the ultrafunctions, is the proof of its consistency, namely the
construction of a model. For example it is easy to prove that the Leibniz
rule is not consistent with an algebra of functions which includes
idempotent functions (see the discussion in section
3.5
) but in
general, it is difficult to guess if a ”reasonable property” is consistent
with all the others (for example the identity
D_{i}D_{j}=D_{j}D_{i}
).

At this points, it is interesting compare the ultrafunction theory (and more
in general a theory which includes infinitesimals) and a traditional theory
based on real valued function spaces. In both cases there exist questions
which do not have a YES/NO answer because of the Goedel incompleteness
theorem, and, in both cases, we can add new axioms which allow to solve the
problem. The difficult issue is the proof of the consistency of these new
axioms. However, this issue is much easier in the world of Non-Archimedean
mathematics, since the mathematical universe is wider and there is more room
to construct a model.

Let us consider an example that clarifies this point. We do not know if the
Navier-Stokes equations have a smooth solution; however they have a unique
ultrafunction solution. Probably this fact is not relevant for an applied
mathematician or for an engineer since it does not help to discover new fact
relative to the motion of a real fluid. However, it is possible to add new
axioms which allow to prove properties of the solutions consistent with the
experiments and to end with a richer mathematical model. This model can be
used to get new practical results even if the problem of the existence of a
smooth solutions remains unsolved.

For this reasons we think that it is worthwhile to investigate the
potentialities of the Non-Archimedean mathematics.
