Topology of Discrete Quantum Feedback Control
Masaya Nakagawa, Masahito Ueda

Abstract
A general framework for analyzing the topology of quantum channels of single-particle systems is developed to find a class of genuinely dynamical topological phases that can be realized by means of discrete quantum feedback control. We provide a symmetry classification of quantum channels by identifying ten symmetry classes of discrete quantum feedback control with projective measurements. We construct various types of topological feedback control by using topological Maxwell demons that achieve robust feedback-controlled chiral or helical transport against noise and decoherence. Topological feedback control thus offers a versatile tool for creating and controlling nonequilibrium topological phases in open quantum systems that are distinct from non-Hermitian and Lindbladian systems and should provide a guiding principle for topology-based design of quantum feedback control.

Introduction

Topology plays a key role in the classification and characterization of diverse physical phenomena that are robust against local disturbances. In particular, topological phases of matter have been discussed in a wide variety of quantum and classical systems, which are characterized by global geometric structures of eigenstates rather than local order parameters

In view of versatile applicability of feedback control, we are naturally led to the following question: Can we utilize feedback control to create and manipulate nonequilibrium topological phases?
We address this problem in the following.

In this paper, we present a class of dynamical topological phases that can be realized with feedback control of quantum systems. Here, a unique role played by feedback control is that one can choose operations depending on measurement outcomes.
This flexibility enables versatile real-time control of topological phases that would be hard to achieve by unitary dynamics in which the driving protocol is predetermined and cannot depend on the state of a system under control.
Furthermore, feedback control allows active control of a quantum system, in contrast with passive control of open quantum systems by engineered dissipation.

We develop a general framework for analyzing the topology of quantum channels for discrete quantum feedback control. Mathematically, quantum channels are described by completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) maps, which provide a general form of time evolution in open quantum systems
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. Here, the time evolution of a system under feedback control is nonunitary due to measurement backaction. Consequently, the topology of CPTP maps is intrinsically non-Hermitian and beyond the theoretical framework of static (Hermitian) topological phases. We introduce topological invariants of quantum channels for single-particle systems on lattices under the general assumption of locality in measurement and feedback. For example, a nonzero winding number of the eigenspectrum of a quantum channel implies chiral transport due to feedback control, which may be regarded as a feedback-controlled counterpart of chiral edge transport in quantum Hall systems as schematically illustrated in Fig.
1
.

Quantum feedback control creates a class of nonequilibrium open quantum systems distinct from those described by non-Hermitian Hamiltonians

;
Kawabata et al. 2019a
;
Zhou and Lee 2019

Bardyn et al. 2013
;
Lieu et al. 2020
;
Kawabata et al. 2023
;
Sá et al. 2023
.

In particular, discrete quantum feedback control concerns discrete time evolution from an initial quantum state to a postfeedback quantum state. Such time evolution cannot, in general, be expressed by using a generator of continuous time evolution.
In fact, as shown in Sec.
III
, CPTP maps with local Kraus operators have highly degenerate zero eigenvalues, and therefore cannot be described by using any generator [see Eq. (
39

1

A non-Hermitian Hamiltonian can only describe the dynamics of pure states and requires postselection of particular measurement outcomes
Ashida et al. 2020
. The Lindblad quantum master equation can describe the dynamics of mixed states but does not refer to measurement outcomes
Wiseman and Milburn 2011
. In contrast, nonequilibrium topological phases created by quantum feedback control are realized by a nontrivial interplay between quantum measurement and feedback operations. We note that non-Hermitian topology has been applied to quantum channels in Ref.
Gong et al. 2018a
. However, its application beyond zero-dimensional systems is nontrivial and has remained elusive; in fact, the locality of Kraus operators, which is relevant in higher dimensions, is key for defining topological invariants, as discussed in Sec.
III
.

We present prototypical models of topological feedback control by using quantum Maxwell demons, which we shall refer to as topological Maxwell demons. In one dimension, a topological Maxwell demon causes feedback-assisted chiral transport due to topology. We find that the topological Maxwell demon creates topologically distinct phases by changing the duration of feedback control. We also show that feedback control can induce the non-Hermitian skin effect, which is a hallmark of dynamical topology unique to open systems
Lee 2016
;
Yao and Wang 2018
;
Gong et al. 2018a
;
Yokomizo and Murakami 2019
;
Okuma et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Okuma and Sato 2023
;
Lin et al. 2023
. The chiral transport caused by the topological Maxwell demon is protected by topology and hence robust against disorder, noise, and decoherence during the feedback process.

In Sec.

II
Discrete quantum feedback control

We consider discrete quantum feedback control in which a feedback cycle consists of quantum measurements and subsequent unitary operations that are conditioned on measurement outcomes. Let
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}
be the initial density matrix of a system. We first perform a quantum measurement described by a set of measurement operators
\{\hat{M}_{m}\}
subject to the completeness relation

\sum_{m}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{m}=\hat{I},

(1)

where the index
m
specifies a measurement outcome and
\hat{I}
is the identity operator
Wiseman and Milburn 2011
. The probability of an outcome
m
being obtained is given by
p_{m}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}]
, and the postmeasurement state corresponding to an outcome
m
is given by

\hat{\rho}_{m}=\frac{\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}}{p_{m}},

(2)

where the nonunitary state change is caused by measurement backaction.
Next, we let the system undergo unitary time evolution during time
\tau
, which is described by a unitary operator

\hat{U}_{m}=\mathcal{T}\exp\Bigl[-i\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\hat{H}_{m}(t)\Bigr],

(3)

where
\hat{H}_{m}(t)
is the feedback Hamiltonian that depends on the measurement outcome
m
and
\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator. We set
\hbar=1
throughout this paper.
If we take an ensemble average over measurement outcomes, then the final density matrix after one feedback loop is given by
Sagawa and Ueda 2008

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}p_{m}\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\hat{U}_{m}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{m}^{\dagger},

(4)

where
\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{m}=\hat{U}_{m}\hat{\rho}_{m}\hat{U}_{m}^{\dagger}
is the density matrix after feedback control with measurement outcome
m
.
Thus, a discrete quantum feedback control is described by a quantum channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\hat{U}_{m}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{m}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger},

(5)

which is a CPTP map
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
, where
\hat{K}_{m}:=\hat{U}_{m}\hat{M}_{m}
is the Kraus operator satisfying
\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{m}=\hat{I}
.

If a feedback cycle is repeated, the final density matrix is given by applying CPTP maps multiple times. Suppose that we repeat a measurement and a feedback operation
N
times. Let
(m_{1},m_{2},\cdots,m_{N})
be a sequence of measurement outcomes in this process.
The density matrix after the
n
th measurement is given by

\hat{\rho}_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{n}}=\frac{\hat{M}_{m_{n}}\hat{\rho}_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{n-1}}^{\prime}\hat{M}_{m_{n}}^{\dagger}}{p(m_{n}|m_{1},\cdots,m_{n-1})},

(6)

where

p(m_{n}|m_{1},\cdots,m_{n-1})=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{M}_{m_{n}}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{m_{n}}\hat{\rho}_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{n-1}}^{\prime}]

(7)

is the probability of an outcome
m_{n}
, conditioned on the past outcomes
m_{1},\cdots,m_{n-1}
.
After the
n
th measurement, we perform a feedback operation and obtain the postfeedback density matrix as

\hat{\rho}_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{n}}^{\prime}=\hat{U}_{m_{n}}\hat{\rho}_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{n}}\hat{U}_{m_{n}}^{\dagger}.

(8)

Then, the final density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}^{(N)}
, which is obtained as the averaged density matrix over all measurement outcomes, is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}^{(N)}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{N}}p(m_{N}|m_{1},\cdots,m_{N-1})p(m_{N-1}|m_{1},\cdots,m_{N-2})\cdots p(m_{2}|m_{1})p_{m_{1}}\hat{\rho}_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{N}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{N}}\hat{U}_{m_{N}}\hat{M}_{m_{N}}\cdots\hat{U}_{m_{1}}\hat{M}_{m_{1}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{M}_{m_{1}}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{m_{1}}^{\dagger}\cdots\hat{M}_{m_{N}}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{m_{N}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{N}(\hat{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}).

(9)

Thus, repeated feedback control is described by repeated applications of a CPTP map.
Equation (
9
) suggests an analogy between periodically driven (Floquet) quantum systems
Oka and Aoki 2009
;
Kitagawa et al. 2010a
;
Kitagawa et al. 2010b
;
Kitagawa et al. 2011
;
Lindner et al. 2011
;
Nathan and Rudner 2015
;
Roy and Harper 2017
;
Higashikawa et al. 2019
;
Oka and Kitamura 2019
;
Rudner and Lindner 2020
;
Harper et al. 2020
and feedback-controlled quantum systems: A unitary time-evolution operator for one period is repeatedly acted on a state vector in the former, while a CPTP map is repeatedly acted on a density matrix in the latter (see also Table
1
).

The above description can be generalized to the cases in which different measurements and feedback operations are performed during a single cycle. For example, suppose that we perform a second measurement described by measurement operators
\{\hat{M}_{m_{2}}^{\prime}\}
after the first measurement and the subsequent feedback operation. Then, we perform the second feedback control with unitary operators
\{\hat{U}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\prime}\}
, which may depend on the outcomes of the first and second measurements. The entire feedback cycle is described by a CPTP map

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\hat{\rho})=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}(\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\prime})^{\dagger},

(10)

where
\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\prime}=U_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\prime}\hat{M}_{m_{2}}^{\prime}\hat{U}_{m_{1}}\hat{M}_{m_{1}}
is the Kraus operator.
More general cases with nonunitary feedback operations will be discussed in Sec.
IX
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space of the system. Then, the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
is an element of the space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
, which is a Hilbert space with dimension
(\dim\mathcal{H})^{2}
. The Hilbert-Schmidt inner product of two operators
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is denoted by
\langle\hat{A},\hat{B}\rangle:=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{B}]
. A CPTP map
\mathcal{E}
[Eq. (
5
)] is regarded as an operator acting on the operator space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
(i.e.,
\mathcal{E}
is a superoperator). The adjoint superoperator
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
is defined by

\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\hat{\sigma}):=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}\hat{K}_{m},

(11)

which satisfies
\langle\hat{\sigma},\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})\rangle=\langle\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\hat{\sigma}),\hat{\rho}\rangle
.

We consider the eigenvalue problem of a CPTP map for feedback control:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R})=\xi_{n}\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L})=\xi_{n}^{*}\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L},

(13)

where
\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
denotes an eigenvalue, and
\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}
and
\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L}
are the corresponding right and left eigenoperators that satisfy the biorthogonal relation
\mathrm{Tr}[(\hat{\rho}_{m}^{L})^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}]=0
for
m\neq n
. In terms of the eigensystem of the CPTP map
\mathcal{E}
, the density matrix after a feedback loop in Eq. (
4
) is expanded as

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}})=\sum_{n}c_{n}\xi_{n}\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R},

(14)

where

\displaystyle c_{n}=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L},\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\rangle}{\langle\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L},\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}\rangle}

(15)

is an expansion coefficient that depends on the initial state
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}
. Here, for simplicity, we assume that the CPTP map is diagonalizable. If the CPTP map is not diagonalizable, we can expand the density matrix by using generalized eigenmodes
Ashida et al. 2020
.
Thus, the eigenvalues and the (generalized) eigenmodes of the CPTP map completely characterize the discrete quantum feedback control.

The eigensystem of a CPTP map has some general properties [see Fig.
1
(a) for a schematic illustration]. First, all the eigenvalues
\xi_{n}
of a CPTP map satisfy
|\xi_{n}|\leq 1
because of the contraction property of a CPTP map
Rivas and Huelga 2012
. An eigenmode
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{SS}}
with eigenvalue
+1
is a steady state of the dynamics in the sense that
\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{SS}})=\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{SS}}
. Second, since a CPTP map preserves the trace of the density matrix, that is,
\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})]=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}]
, all the right eigenmodes except for those corresponding to steady states are traceless:
\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}]=0\ (\xi_{n}\neq 1)
. Third, a CPTP map preserves the Hermiticity of an operator since

[\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})]^{\dagger}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}^{\dagger}).

(16)

It follows from Eqs. (
12
) and (
16
) that if
\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}
is an eigenmode of
\mathcal{E}
with eigenvalue
\xi_{n}
, then
(\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R})^{\dagger}
is an eigenmode with eigenvalue
\xi_{n}^{*}
since

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}((\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R})^{\dagger})=[\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R})]^{\dagger}=\xi_{n}^{*}(\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R})^{\dagger}.

(17)

Therefore, eigenvalues of a CPTP map are real or appear in complex-conjugate pairs.

III
Topological equivalence of quantum channels

In this section, we introduce the topology of quantum channels (CPTP maps) to investigate topological phases induced by feedback control.

III.1
Setup

We consider discrete quantum feedback control of a single-particle quantum system on a
d
-dimensional lattice.
Let

\Lambda:=\left\{\vec{R}_{\vec{i}}=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{d}i_{\lambda}\vec{a}_{\lambda};\ \vec{i}=(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}),i_{\lambda}=0,\cdots,L_{\lambda}-1\right\}

(18)

be a set of lattice sites, where
\vec{a}_{\lambda}\ (\lambda=1,\cdots,d)
denote the primitive lattice vectors. Each lattice site
\vec{R}_{\vec{i}}
is specified by a set of integers
\vec{i}=(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d})
. The total number of unit cells is given by
N_{\mathrm{cell}}:=L_{1}L_{2}\cdots L_{d}
.
The Hilbert space of the system is spanned by an orthonormal basis set
\{\ket{\vec{i},a}\}
(
\vec{i}=(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}),i_{\lambda}=0,\cdots,L_{\lambda}-1
, and
a=1,\cdots,D_{\mathrm{loc}}
), where
\ket{\vec{i},a}
denotes a quantum state at site
\vec{i}
, with the index
a
labeling additional degrees of freedom such as spin, orbit, sublattice, etc. The dimension of the Hilbert space in each unit cell is denoted by
D_{\mathrm{loc}}
, and thus the dimension of the entire Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of the system is given by
D=N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}
. (Note that we consider a one-body problem here.)
The density matrix of the system can be expanded in terms of the orthonormal basis set as

\hat{\rho}=\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b}\rho_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}.

(19)

We impose the periodic boundary condition (PBC)

\ket{\vec{i}+L_{\lambda}\vec{e}_{\lambda},a}=\ket{\vec{i},a}\ (\lambda=1,\cdots,d),

(20)

where
\vec{e}_{\lambda}
is a unit vector defined by
(\vec{e}_{\lambda})_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\delta_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}
, unless otherwise specified.

We perform feedback control of the system with a CPTP map (
5
). We assume that the Kraus operators
\hat{K}_{m}
do not change the particle number of the system. The Kraus operators are expanded by using the orthonormal basis as

\hat{K}_{m}=\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b}(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}.

(21)

Since a CPTP map is a superoperator acting on the operator space, it is convenient to vectorize the density matrix (
19
).
Here we invoke the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism, also known as the channel-state duality, which is a mathematical correspondence between quantum channels and bipartite states
Jamiołkowski 1972
;
Choi 1975
;
Jiang et al. 2013
;
Wilde 2013
. In the present case, there is an isomorphic map between an operator and a vector in a doubled Hilbert space (see Appendix
A.1
).
We vectorize the density matrix (
19
) as

\ket{\hat{\rho}}:=\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b}\rho_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}\ket{\vec{i},a}\otimes\ket{\vec{j},b}.

(22)

Correspondingly, there is an isomorphic mapping from a CPTP map

\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}

(23)

to a non-Hermitian and nonunitary operator

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\otimes\hat{K}_{m}^{*},

(24)

which we shall refer to as the matrix representation of
\mathcal{E}
(see Appendix
A.1
).
Throughout this paper, we use a symbol with a tilde to indicate an operator that acts on the doubled Hilbert space.

Topological phases of matter are often studied for translationally invariant systems, where the Bloch band theory allows the momentum-space representation of a Hamiltonian
Moessner and Moore 2021
. Analogously, here we consider translationally invariant feedback control. We assume that a CPTP map satisfies

\sum_{m}(\hat{T}_{\lambda}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{-1})\hat{\rho}(\hat{T}_{\lambda}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{-1})^{\dagger}=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}

(25)

for arbitrary
\hat{\rho}
, where
\hat{T}_{\lambda}\ (\lambda=1,\cdots,d)
is a translation operator:

\hat{T}_{\lambda}\ket{\vec{i},a}=\ket{\vec{i}+\vec{e}_{\lambda},a}.

(26)

In the matrix representation
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
of the CPTP map, the translational symmetry in Eq. (
25
) reads

\displaystyle(\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*})\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*})^{-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}.

(27)

We use the translational symmetry to obtain the momentum-space representation of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
as (see Appendix
A.2
for derivation)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{k}}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{\vec{k}}^{\dagger}X(\vec{k})\tilde{\bm{c}}_{\vec{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}\sum_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k})\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},b,d,\vec{\nu}},

(28)

where
\tilde{\bm{c}}_{\vec{k}}^{\dagger}=(\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger})_{a,c,\vec{\mu}}
with

\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\tilde{c}_{\vec{j},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

(29)

is a row vector of the Fourier transform of an auxiliary creation operator
\tilde{c}_{\vec{i},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}
defined as

\tilde{c}_{\vec{i},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}(\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\otimes\ket{\mathrm{vac}})=\ket{\vec{i},a}\otimes\ket{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},c},

(30)

\ket{\mathrm{vac}}
is the vacuum state of the system, and
X(\vec{k})
is a momentum-dependent
N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}\times N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}
matrix defined by

\displaystyle X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}\sum_{m}(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{j},a;\vec{j}^{\prime},b}(\hat{K}_{m})^{*}_{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu},c;\vec{j}^{\prime}+\vec{\nu},d}

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}.

(31)

Here, we note that the operator
\tilde{c}_{\vec{i},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}
has two additional indices
c
and
\vec{\mu}
compared with the original basis
\ket{\vec{i},a}
of the Hilbert space since
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
acts on the doubled Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. Physically, this point corresponds to the fact that the density matrix has bra and ket degrees of freedom.
The non-Hermitian matrix
X(\vec{k})
is a counterpart of the Bloch Hamiltonian in the band theory, and therefore we call it the Bloch matrix.
The mode expansion in Eq. (
14
) of the density matrix after feedback control can be expressed in terms of the eigensystem of the Bloch matrix (see Appendix
A.3
).

III.2
Topology of quantum channels with local Kraus operators

We are now in a position to discuss the topology of feedback control.
We consider a homotopy between CPTP maps, that is, two CPTP maps
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
are homotopic to each other if and only if there exists a continuous one-parameter family
\Xi_{s}
of CPTP maps (called a homotopy
Hatcher 2001
) that preserves the spectral gap and satisfies
\Xi_{0}=\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\Xi_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{1}
. Intuitively speaking, the existence of a homotopy between two CPTP maps means that they can continuously be deformed into each other.
Here, the spectral gap is either a point gap or a line gap according to the general discussion of topology of non-Hermitian operators
Gong et al. 2018a
;
Kawabata et al. 2019a
. Specifically, a point gap at
\xi=\xi_{\mathrm{PG}}
means that the CPTP map under consideration does not have an eigenvalue
\xi_{\mathrm{PG}}
. Similarly, a line gap for a line
l_{\mathrm{LG}}
means that a CPTP map does not have an eigenvalue on
l_{\mathrm{LG}}
. Here, we mostly focus on the case of a point gap and discuss the case of a line gap in Sec.
VII.5
.

In the momentum-space representation in Eq. (
28
), the eigenspectrum of a CPTP map can be calculated from the Bloch matrix defined in Eq. (
31
).
Thus, a homotopy between translationally invariant CPTP maps is obtained from that between their Bloch matrices.
The Bloch matrix
X(\vec{k})
defines a map from the Brillouin zone (i.e., a
d
-dimensional torus) to a space of non-Hermitian matrices. We can exploit this property to introduce a topological invariant for a CPTP map. If two CPTP maps (or their Bloch matrices) have different values of a topological invariant, they are not homotopic and cannot continuously be deformed into each other without closing the spectral gap (see Fig.
2
).
Thus, topological invariants can be used to homotopically classify CPTP maps.

Figure 2:
Eigenspectra
\{\xi_{n}^{(0)}(\vec{k})\}
and
\{\xi_{n}^{(1)}(\vec{k})\}
(shown by blue areas) of two translationally invariant CPTP maps
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
that can be calculated from their Bloch matrices defined on the Brillouin zone (the
d
-dimensional torus
T^{d}
). If the Bloch matrices have different values of a topological invariant, the two CPTP maps cannot continuously be deformed into each other without closing the spectral gap. In the figure, a point gap at
\xi_{\mathrm{PG}}
is depicted by a red square.

Here, we note that the Bloch matrix is an
N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}\times N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}
matrix. Since the size of the matrix diverges in the limit
N_{\mathrm{cell}}\to\infty
, the definition of a topological invariant of a Bloch matrix is nontrivial. We show that this problem can be circumvented by imposing locality on the Kraus operators. We assume that the Kraus operators are local in space and have a finite support. Physically, this assumption implies that the interaction between the system and the measurement apparatus (the feedback controller) is local. Let
\ell
be the diameter of the support of the Kraus operators, and let
R
be the range of locality of the Kraus operators. Specifically, we assume

(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}(\hat{K}_{m})^{*}_{\vec{i}^{\prime},c;\vec{j}^{\prime},d}=0\ \mathrm{for}\ |\vec{j}-\vec{j}^{\prime}|>\ell

(32)

and

(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}=0\ \mathrm{for}\ |\vec{i}-\vec{j}|>R.

(33)

We also assume that
\ell
and
R
are independent of the system size. Then, we have

(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}(\hat{K}_{m})^{*}_{\vec{i}^{\prime},c;\vec{j}^{\prime},d}=0\ \mathrm{for}\ |\vec{i}-\vec{i}^{\prime}|>2R+\ell,

(34)

since

\displaystyle|\vec{i}-\vec{i}^{\prime}|=

\displaystyle|\vec{i}-\vec{j}+\vec{j}-\vec{j}^{\prime}+\vec{j}^{\prime}-\vec{i}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\vec{i}-\vec{j}|+|\vec{j}-\vec{j}^{\prime}|+|\vec{i}^{\prime}-\vec{j}^{\prime}|.

(35)

The condition (
32
) leads to

X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k})=0\ \mathrm{for}\ |\vec{\nu}|>\ell.

(36)

Furthermore, from the relation (
34
), we obtain

X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k})=0\ \mathrm{for}\ |\vec{\mu}|>2R+\ell,

(37)

which indicates that the Bloch matrix
X(\vec{k})
can be truncated without changing its nonzero eigenvalues and can be replaced by a matrix
X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k})
with a finite dimension that does not increase when increasing the system size (see Fig.
3
).
Hence, the topology of a CPTP map can be diagnosed by that of its truncated Bloch matrix
X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k})
, which remains a finite-size matrix in the
N_{\mathrm{cell}}\to\infty
limit. Topological feedback control is thus characterized by a topological invariant for the truncated Bloch matrix.

Figure 3:
Schematic representation of the Bloch matrix
X(\vec{k})
which is an
N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}\times N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}
matrix. If the Kraus operators are local and have a finite support, the Bloch matrix can be truncated into a matrix
X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k})
with dimension
D_{\mathrm{trunc}}
that remains finite in the limit
N_{\mathrm{cell}}\to\infty
.

An important consequence of the locality of Kraus operators is that the CPTP map has highly degenerate zero eigenvalues. This result can be seen by noting that

\hat{K}_{m}\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}=0\ \mathrm{for}\ |\vec{i}-\vec{j}|>\ell,

(38)

which shows that
\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}
with
|\vec{i}-\vec{j}|>\ell
is an eigenoperator of the CPTP map with eigenvalue zero.
Thus, the truncation of a Bloch matrix and the highly degenerate zero eigenvalues can be understood as a consequence of local measurement, which projects out quantum coherence between distant sites.

The existence of zero eigenvalues of quantum channels implies a crucial difference between the dynamics described by those quantum channels and continuous time evolution described by the Lindblad quantum master equation.
In fact, a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
that has a zero eigenvalue cannot be expressed in terms of any generator
\mathcal{L}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}\neq e^{\mathcal{L}},

(39)

since the right-hand side cannot have a zero eigenvalue.
Thus, the topological classification of quantum channels cannot be reduced to that of the Lindbladians discussed in the literature
Bardyn et al. 2013
;
Lieu et al. 2020
;
Kawabata et al. 2023
;
Sá et al. 2023
.

If Kraus operators are not strictly local but have an exponentially decaying tail, the topology of a quantum channel may be discussed by approximating them with local ones because topology is insensitive to small deformations. Physically, this case should hold true unless rare measurement outcomes with extremely small probabilities due to an exponentially decaying tail of wavefunctions significantly alter the physics. While it is expected to be true for most cases, it would be interesting to find the cases where rare events due to the exponentially small tails of Kraus operators play a significant role in feedback control. We do not discuss those situations further in this paper.

III.3
Stable equivalence of quantum channels

In the definition of the equivalence relation in terms of a homotopy presented in the previous subsection, two CPTP maps acting on systems with different numbers of internal degrees of freedom are not homotopic to each other.
A more convenient classification of topological phases is obtained by introducing the notion of stable equivalence
Kitaev 2009
;
Roy and Harper 2017
. In the topological band theory, two band insulators are stably equivalent if they are continuously connected to each other after the addition of some trivial bands
Kitaev 2009
. Analogously, we define the stable equivalence of CPTP maps as follows. First, we generalize the quantum states at each unit cell as
\ket{\vec{j},a}
(
a=1,\cdots,D_{\mathrm{loc}},D_{\mathrm{loc}}+1,\cdots,D_{\mathrm{loc}}+n_{1}
) by adding
n_{1}
extra local degrees of freedom, which can physically be interpreted as ancillary sublattice sites. We assume that the Kraus operators
\hat{K}_{m}
act trivially on the extra degrees of freedom, i.e.,
\hat{K}_{m}\ket{\vec{j},a}=0
for
a=D_{\mathrm{loc}}+1,\cdots,D_{\mathrm{loc}}+n_{1}
. Next, we introduce a projective measurement of the ancillary degrees of freedom, which is described by

\mathcal{P}_{n_{1}}(\hat{\rho}):=\sum_{\vec{j}}\sum_{a=D_{\mathrm{loc}}+1}^{D_{\mathrm{loc}}+n_{1}}\hat{P}_{\vec{j},a}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{\vec{j},a},

(40)

where
\hat{P}_{\vec{j},a}:=\ket{\vec{j},a}\bra{\vec{j},a}
is the projection operator onto the
a
th internal degree of freedom at site
\vec{j}
. The projective measurement channel (
40
) plays the role of “trivial bands” in the sense that all of its nonzero eigenvalues are unity (see Appendix
C
). We note that the projection operators
\hat{P}_{\vec{j},a}
are local in space in accordance with the discussion in Sec.
III.2
. We define the direct sum of a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}
for the system and the projective measurement channel (
40
) for the extra bands by

(\mathcal{E}\oplus\mathcal{P}_{n_{1}})(\hat{\rho}):=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}+\sum_{\vec{j}}\sum_{a=D_{\mathrm{loc}}+1}^{D_{\mathrm{loc}}+n_{1}}\hat{P}_{\vec{j},a}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{\vec{j},a}.

(41)

Let
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
be CPTP maps. Then, the CPTP maps
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are said to be stably equivalent if and only if
\mathcal{E}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{P}_{n_{1}}
are homotopic to
\mathcal{E}_{2}\oplus\mathcal{P}_{n_{2}}
for two non-negative integers
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
(see Fig.
4
).

Figure 4:
Stable equivalence of CPTP maps. A CPTP map with the eigenspectrum shown by blue areas in the top-left figure is not homotopic to the other CPTP map with the eigenspectrum shown by blue and green areas in the bottom figure since the numbers of bands (eigenvalues) are different. However, they can be made homotopic if we add a trivial CPTP map with the eigenspectrum shown in the top-right figure so that the numbers of eigenvalues become equal to each other.

IV
Topological feedback control

IV.1
Chiral Maxwell demon

Having established the general framework of topology of quantum channels, here we present a prototypical example of topological quantum feedback control characterized by a topological invariant of the Bloch matrix of a quantum channel. Our model is a quantum counterpart of Maxwell’s demon in Ref.
Toyabe et al. 2010
(see also Ref.
Liu et al. 2023
). We consider a particle hopping on a one-dimensional (1D) lattice with
L
sites, where a quantum state at site
i
is denoted by
\ket{i}
. We first perform a projective measurement of the position of this particle with measurement operators
\hat{M}_{m}=\ket{m}\bra{m}

(m=1,\cdots,L)
. If the measurement outcome is
j
, we raise the potential at site
j-1
and perform feedback control with the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{j}=-J\sum_{i}(\ket{i}\bra{i+1}+\ket{i+1}\bra{i})+V\ket{j-1}\bra{j-1},

(42)

where
J\in\mathbb{R}
is the hopping amplitude and
V>0
is the height of the potential. The unitary operator for feedback control is given by
\hat{U}_{j}=\exp[-i\hat{H}_{j}\tau]
, where
\tau
is the duration of feedback control.
The potential barrier placed at site
j-1
prevents the particle from going to the left, thereby achieving unidirectional (chiral) transport of the particle to the right (see Fig.
5
).
Here, we do not need any external field to cause the transport in the system; instead, the information gained from the measurement is used to drive the particle unidirectionally.
Feedback control thus rectifies quantum fluctuations in measurement outcomes and converts them into a unidirectional transport in a manner similar to an information ratchet
Sagawa and Ueda 2010
.
We therefore refer to this model as the chiral Maxwell demon.

Figure 5:
Schematic illustration of the chiral Maxwell demon. A quantum-mechanical particle hops on a one-dimensional lattice with hopping amplitude
J
. We perform a projective measurement of the position of the particle. If we find the particle at site
j
, we raise the potential at site
j-1
to suppress the probability of the particle hopping to the left.

The eigenvalues
\xi_{n}(k)\ (n=0,\cdots,L-1)
of a quantum channel of this feedback control can be calculated by using the method described in Appendix
B
. They are given by

\xi_{0}(k)=\sum_{r}p(r)e^{-ikr}

(43)

and

\xi_{1}(k)=\xi_{2}(k)=\cdots=\xi_{L-1}(k)=0,

(44)

where

p(r)=p(j+r|j):=|(\hat{U}_{j})_{j+r,j}|^{2}

(45)

is the probability of finding a particle at site
j+r
after feedback control with measurement outcome
j
. This probability does not depend on
j
because of the translational invariance. The zero eigenvalues
\xi_{n\neq 0}(k)
are due to the projective measurement for which quantum coherence between different sites vanishes:
\hat{M}_{m}\ket{i}\bra{j}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}=0\ (i\neq j)
.

By using the eigenvalues, the topological invariant of a quantum channel is given by the winding number

\displaystyle w(\xi_{\mathrm{PG}})=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dk}{2\pi i}\partial_{k}\ln[\xi_{0}(k)-\xi_{\mathrm{PG}}]\in\mathbb{Z},

(46)

where
\xi_{\mathrm{PG}}
denotes the location of a point gap
Gong et al. 2018a
. Here, the eigenvalues
\xi_{n}(k)=0\ (n\neq 0)
do not contribute to the winding number [since
\partial_{k}\xi_{n}(k)=0
] and thus are excluded from Eq. (
46
). The winding number takes an integer value and it is invariant under continuous deformation of a feedback control protocol as long as the point gap at
\xi_{\mathrm{PG}}
is not closed. This case is consistent with the
\mathbb{Z}
classification of non-Hermitian matrices in 1D systems according to stable equivalence
Gong et al. 2018a
.

IV.2
Eigenspectrum of a quantum channel under the periodic and open boundary conditions

In Fig.
6
, we show the eigenspectrum of a quantum channel for the chiral Maxwell demon under the PBC. Besides the highly degenerate zero eigenvalues
\xi_{n\neq 0}(k)
, the loop structure formed by the eigenvalue
\xi_{0}(k)
is evident.
The winding number is
w(\xi_{\mathrm{PG}})=-1
for a point gap
\xi_{\mathrm{PG}}
inside the loop.
The eigenvalue
\xi_{0}(k)
is determined from the probability distribution of the position after feedback control as shown in Eq. (
43
). In fact, the eigenvalue
\xi_{0}(k)
is nothing but the characteristic function of the probability distribution
\{p(r)\}
. The chiral transport caused by feedback control increases the probability of a particle being found at site
r>0
and makes the probability distribution
\{p(r)\}
asymmetric with respect to
r>0
and
r<0
. Thus, the winding number of the quantum channel is closely related to the chiral transport achieved by the feedback in analogy to the chiral edge state in the quantum Hall effect and the Hatano-Nelson model for non-Hermitian topological phases
Gong et al. 2018a
;
Hatano and Nelson 1996
;
Hatano and Nelson 1997
;
Hatano and Nelson 1998
;
Lee et al. 2019
.

As seen from Fig.

(c) and

VIII.2
).

We note that the localization of left and right eigenmodes
\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L},\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}
at opposite edges implies that the overlap
\langle\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L},\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}\rangle
decreases exponentially as a function of the system size. From the eigenmode expansion (

becomes exponentially large, which significantly affects the relaxation dynamics of the system towards the steady state under repeated applications of a quantum channel
Mori and Shirai 2020
;
Haga et al. 2021
. Specifically, if the non-Hermitian skin effect takes place, the relaxation time under the OBC generically diverges in the infinite-size limit
Haga et al. 2021
.

\displaystyle w(\xi_{\mathrm{PG}}=0)=

We note that the mean displacement

IV.4
Robustness of chiral transport against disorder

The unidirectional transport induced by the chiral Maxwell demon is of a topological origin and therefore robust against external perturbations. Here, we demonstrate that the topological feedback control enhances the stability of particle transport. By way of illustration, we consider a task of transporting a particle from the leftmost site to the rightmost site in an
L

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{j}=

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle-J\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}(\ket{i}\bra{i+1}+\mathrm{H.c.})+\sum_{i=1}^{L}W_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle 0;

(54b)

In Fig.

V.1
Unitary symmetry

We first consider unitary symmetry of quantum channels. Similarly to the case of open quantum systems described by the Lindblad equation
Buča and Prosen 2012
;
Albert and Jiang 2014
, unitary symmetry of a quantum channel can be either weak symmetry or strong symmetry
de Groot et al. 2022
. A CPTP map
\mathcal{E}
has unitary symmetry if it commutes with a unitary superoperator
\mathcal{V}
, that is,

\mathcal{V}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{V}^{-1}=\mathcal{E}.

(56)

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
be the matrix representation of a CPTP map
\mathcal{E}
[see Eq. (
24
)]. Then, Eq. (
56
) is rewritten as

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}},

(57)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
is the matrix representation of
\mathcal{V}
which is given by a unitary operator acting on the doubled Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
.
We call unitary symmetry in Eqs. (
56
) and (
57
) weak symmetry
Buča and Prosen 2012
;
Albert and Jiang 2014
;
de Groot et al. 2022
.
If a CPTP map has weak symmetry, it can be block diagonalized according to the symmetry eigenvalues.
For example, translation symmetry in Eq. (
25
) is weak symmetry, and the argument
\vec{k}
of the Bloch matrix
X(\vec{k})
corresponds to the symmetry eigenvalue (see Appendix
A.2
).

Suppose that every Kraus operator of a CPTP map commutes with a unitary operator
\hat{V}
as
[\hat{K}_{m},\hat{V}]=0\ (\forall m)
. Then, the CPTP map satisfies Eq. (
57
) with
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}=\hat{V}\otimes\hat{I}
or
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}=\hat{I}\otimes\hat{V}^{*}
. Such symmetry is called strong symmetry
Buča and Prosen 2012
;
Albert and Jiang 2014
;
de Groot et al. 2022
, which gives a more stringent condition than weak symmetry. Strong symmetry is related to a conserved quantity of the dynamics. To see this relation, we consider the case of continuous symmetry and assume that every Kraus operator
\hat{K}_{m}
commutes with
\hat{V}_{\theta}=\exp[-i\theta\hat{A}]
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\hat{A}
is a Hermitian operator. This assumption implies
[\hat{K}_{m},\hat{A}]=0\ (\forall m)
. Then, for an arbitrary density matrix
\hat{\rho}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{A}\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})]=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{A}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{\rho}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{m}\Bigr)\hat{A}\hat{\rho}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{A}\hat{\rho}],

(58)

where we use
\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{m}=\hat{I}
in deriving the last equality. Thus, the observable
\hat{A}
is conserved under the CPTP map.

V.2
Modular conjugation symmetry

A CPTP map has an antiunitary symmetry due to its Hermiticity-preserving nature
Gong et al. 2018a
. To see this property, we define the modular conjugation
Liu et al. 2021
;
Kawabata et al. 2023
by an antiunitary operator
\tilde{J}
acting on the doubled Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
as

\tilde{J}(\rho_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}\ket{\vec{i},a}\otimes\ket{\vec{j},b}):=\rho_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}^{*}\ket{\vec{j},b}\otimes\ket{\vec{i},a}.

(59)

The modular conjugation operator
\tilde{J}
is a combination of a swap operation on states and the complex conjugation of their coefficients. The modular conjugation satisfies
\tilde{J}^{2}=1
and acts on the vectorized density matrix [see Eq. (
22
)] as

\tilde{J}\ket{\hat{\rho}}=\ket{\hat{\rho}^{\dagger}}.

(60)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{J}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\ket{\hat{\rho}}=

\displaystyle\tilde{J}\ket{\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ket{[\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})]^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ket{\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}^{\dagger})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\ket{\hat{\rho}^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\tilde{J}\ket{\hat{\rho}},

(61)

where we use the definition
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\ket{\hat{\rho}}=\ket{\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})}
of the matrix representation [see Eq. (
197
)], the property of the modular conjugation [Eq. (
60
)], and the Hermiticity-preserving property of a CPTP map [Eq. (
16
)].
Since Eq. (
61
) holds for arbitrary
\ket{\hat{\rho}}
, we obtain

\tilde{J}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\tilde{J}^{-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}},

(62)

which indicates that the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
has an antiunitary symmetry defined by the modular conjugation. Since a CPTP map must preserve the Hermiticity of the density matrix, any CPTP map has the modular conjugation symmetry given by Eq. (
62
).

V.3
Tenfold symmetry classification of feedback control with projective measurement

The topological classification of non-Hermitian matrices is performed on the basis of the BL symmetry classes
Bernard and LeClair 2002
, which are the non-Hermitian generalizations
Kawabata et al. 2019a
;
Zhou and Lee 2019
of the Altland-Zirnbauer (AZ) symmetry classes
Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
. Here, we discuss the BL symmetry classes of quantum channels for discrete quantum feedback control. In general, the symmetry of a CPTP map
\mathcal{E}
that belongs to a BL symmetry class has one of the following representations
Ashida et al. 2020

\displaystyle P\ \mathrm{symmetry}:\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{P}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{P}^{-1}=-\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{P}^{2}=\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(63a)

\displaystyle C\ \mathrm{symmetry}:\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{T}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}^{-1}=\epsilon_{C}\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}^{*}=\eta_{C}\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(63b)

\displaystyle K\ \mathrm{symmetry}:\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}^{-1}=\epsilon_{K}\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}^{*}=\eta_{K}\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(63c)

\displaystyle Q\ \mathrm{symmetry}:\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}^{-1}=\epsilon_{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}^{2}=\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(63d)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
is the matrix representation of
\mathcal{E}
acting on the doubled Hilbert space (see Sec.
III.1
),
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{X}\ (X=P,C,K,Q)
is a unitary operator on the doubled Hilbert space,
\epsilon_{X}=\pm 1\ (X=C,K,Q)
, and
\eta_{X}=\pm 1\ (X=C,K)
.
Here, we assume that a CPTP map does not have unitary symmetry; if a CPTP map has unitary symmetry, we first block diagonalize it according to the symmetry eigenvalues and consider the BL symmetry class of each block.
The combinations of the four types of symmetries, Eqs. (
63a
)-(
63d
), lead to 38 classes of non-Hermitian matrices
Kawabata et al. 2019a
.

Whereas general non-Hermitian matrices are classified in terms of the 38 BL symmetry classes
Kawabata et al. 2019a
, some of them are not compatible with CPTP maps for projective measurements. A CPTP map for a projective measurement is given by

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proj}}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{m}\hat{P}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{m},

(64)

where
\hat{P}_{m}
is a projection operator satisfying
\hat{P}_{m}^{\dagger}=\hat{P}_{m}
and
\hat{P}_{m}\hat{P}_{m^{\prime}}=\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\hat{P}_{m}
. A key property of the projective measurement channel (
64

C
). Physically, this property is related to the repeatability of projective measurements; that is, repeated projective measurements after the first projective measurement with outcome
m
always yield the same outcome
m

Nielsen and Chuang 2000
.
To identify the possible symmetry classes of the projective measurement channel (
64
), we note that a certain BL symmetry places a constraint on the eigenvalues of a CPTP map. Let
\xi
be an eigenvalue of a CPTP map
\mathcal{E}
. Then, if this CPTP map has

symmetry (
63a
) or
C
symmetry with
\epsilon_{C}=-1
,
-\xi
is also an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{E}

\mathcal{E}
has
K
(

\epsilon_{K}\xi^{*}
(
\epsilon_{Q}\xi^{*}

\mathcal{E}

Since a projective measurement channel (

-1

P

can be a symmetry of the projective measurement channel (see Fig.

Thus, the possible symmetries of a CPTP map for the projective measurement are as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{T}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}^{*}=\eta_{C}\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(65a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}^{*}=\eta_{K}\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(65b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}^{2}=\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(65c)

where

The present symmetry classification applies to the case with multipoint projective measurements. For example, let us consider the following CPTP map for feedback control with a two-point projective measurement:

(\hat{U}_{m}\hat{M}_{m})^{T}=\hat{M}_{m}^{T}\hat{U}_{m}^{T}

(\hat{U}_{m}\hat{M}_{m})^{\dagger}=\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{m}^{\dagger}

\hat{K}_{m}=\hat{M}_{m_{2}}\hat{U}_{m_{1}}\hat{M}_{m_{1}}
with
m=(m_{1},m_{2})
being a pair of measurement outcomes (see Sec.
II
).

VI.1
Class AI: Feedback without symmetry or with particle-hole symmetry

We begin with class AI, which includes only
K

\eta_{K}=+1

A CPTP map can have
K

\eta_{K}=+1

K

65b

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{S}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{S})^{-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}},

(71)

where
\tilde{S}
is the swap operator defined by

\tilde{S}(\ket{\vec{i},a}\otimes\ket{\vec{j},b})=\ket{\vec{j},b}\otimes\ket{\vec{i},a}.

(72)

The CPTP map can be block diagonalized by using the unitary symmetry (
71

Thus, if the CPTP map does not have any other symmetry, the block diagonalized CPTP map belongs to class AI.

VI.2

+
psH

and AI
+

The symmetry classes that include
K

\eta_{K}=+1

+
psH

+
psH
-
, where the last two classes include

C
and
Q

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\hat{P}_{m_{2}}\hat{U}_{m_{1}}\hat{P}_{m_{1}}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{m_{1}}\hat{U}_{m_{1}}^{\dagger}\hat{P}_{m_{2}},

(73)

where
m_{n}=(\vec{j}_{n},a_{n})\ (n=1,2)
denotes a measurement outcome for a projective measurement with the projection operator
\hat{P}_{m_{n}}:=\hat{P}_{\vec{j}_{n},a_{n}}=\ket{\vec{j}_{n},a_{n}}\bra{\vec{j}_{n},a_{n}}
, and

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}:=

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{m_{2}}\hat{U}_{m_{1}}\hat{P}_{m_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}})_{\vec{j}_{2},a_{2};\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}}\ket{\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}}\bra{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}}

(74)

is the Kraus operator.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\otimes\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{*}=

\displaystyle p(\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}|\vec{j}_{1},a_{1})

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}}\bra{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}}\otimes\ket{\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}}\bra{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}}

(75)

and

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{T}\otimes\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\dagger}=

\displaystyle p(\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}|\vec{j}_{1},a_{1})

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}}\bra{\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}}\otimes\ket{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}}\bra{\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}},

(76)

where
p(\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}|\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}):=|(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}})_{\vec{j}_{2},a_{2};\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}}|^{2}
is the probability of a measurement outcome
(\vec{j}_{2},a_{2})
being obtained in the projective measurement performed after feedback control associated with a measurement outcome
(\vec{j}_{1},a_{1})
. Thus, the conditions (
68a
) and (
68c
) for
C
and
Q
symmetries follow from symmetry of the transition probability
p(\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}|\vec{j}_{1},a_{1})
.

The Bloch matrix of the CPTP map (
73
) is given by

\displaystyle X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}(\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}})_{\vec{j},a;\vec{j}^{\prime},b}

\displaystyle\times(\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}})^{*}_{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu},c;\vec{j}^{\prime}+\vec{\nu},d}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\vec{\mu},\vec{0}}\delta_{\vec{\nu},\vec{0}}\delta_{a,c}\delta_{b,d}\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j}_{1},\vec{j}_{2}}p(\vec{j}_{2},a|\vec{j}_{1},b)

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}_{2}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}_{1}})}.

(77)

Thus, the Bloch matrix has the following structure:

X(\vec{k})=\begin{pmatrix}X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k})&O\\
O&O\end{pmatrix},

(78)

where

X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k})=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1,1,\vec{0};1,1,\vec{0}}(\vec{k})&X_{1,1,\vec{0};2,2,\vec{0}}(\vec{k})&\cdots&X_{1,1,\vec{0};D_{\mathrm{loc}},D_{\mathrm{loc}},\vec{0}}(k)\\
X_{2,2,\vec{0};1,1,\vec{0}}(\vec{k})&X_{2,2,\vec{0};2,2,\vec{0}}(\vec{k})&\cdots&X_{2,2,\vec{0};D_{\mathrm{loc}},D_{\mathrm{loc}},\vec{0}}(\vec{k})\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
X_{D_{\mathrm{loc}},D_{\mathrm{loc}},\vec{0};1,1,\vec{0}}(\vec{k})&X_{D_{\mathrm{loc}},D_{\mathrm{loc}},\vec{0};2,2,\vec{0}}(\vec{k})&\cdots&X_{D_{\mathrm{loc}},D_{\mathrm{loc}},\vec{0};D_{\mathrm{loc}},D_{\mathrm{loc}},\vec{0}}(\vec{k})\\
\end{pmatrix}

(79)

is the truncated Bloch matrix. Nonzero eigenvalues of the Bloch matrix are completely determined by those of the truncated Bloch matrix. The BL symmetry class of a given Bloch matrix is also determined from that of the corresponding truncated Bloch matrix.

By way of illustration, we consider a system with a spin-
1/2
particle, which corresponds to the case with
D_{\mathrm{loc}}=2
. The truncated Bloch matrix is given by

\displaystyle X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{\uparrow\uparrow}(\vec{k})&\xi_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\vec{k})\\
\xi_{\downarrow\uparrow}(\vec{k})&\xi_{\downarrow\downarrow}(\vec{k})\end{pmatrix},

(80)

where

\displaystyle\xi_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\vec{k}):=

\displaystyle X_{\sigma,\sigma,\vec{0};\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\vec{0}}(\vec{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j}_{1},\vec{j}_{2}}p(\vec{j}_{2},\sigma|\vec{j}_{1},\sigma^{\prime})e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}_{2}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}_{1}})}.

(81)

Let us examine the symmetry of the truncated Bloch matrix. First, we note that the truncated Bloch matrix always has an antiunitary symmetry (the modular conjugation symmetry),

X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{*}(-\vec{k})=X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k}),

(82)

due to the Hermiticity-preserving nature of the CPTP map (see Sec.
V.2
and Appendix
A.4
). This case corresponds to
K
symmetry (
65b
) with
\eta_{K}=+1
. Next, we consider
C
symmetry (
65a
). The simplest symmetry of this type with
\eta_{C}=+1
is given by

X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{T}(-\vec{k})=X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k}),

(83)

which is equivalent to the condition

\xi_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(-\vec{k})=\xi_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}(\vec{k}).

(84)

The condition (
84
) is satisfied if

\displaystyle p(\vec{j}_{2},\sigma|\vec{j}_{1},\sigma^{\prime})=p(\vec{j}_{1},\sigma^{\prime}|\vec{j}_{2},\sigma),

(85)

which can be interpreted as reciprocity in the feedback process. The truncated Bloch matrix (and hence the original Bloch matrix) with Eq. (
83
) belongs to the symmetry class AI
+
psH
+
in Table
2
. In the present case with Eqs. (
82
) and (
83
), the truncated Bloch matrix is Hermitian [
X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{\dagger}(\vec{k})=X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k})
], and therefore its eigenvalues are real. More specifically, the truncated Bloch matrix in a 1D system cannot have a nonzero winding number.

Another symmetry class AI
+
psH
-
possesses
C
symmetry with
\eta_{C}=-1
. An example of such symmetry is given by

(-i\sigma_{y})X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{T}(-\vec{k})(-i\sigma_{y})^{-1}=X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k}),

(86)

where
\sigma_{y}
is the Pauli matrix. This type of symmetry leads to the Kramers degeneracy in non-Hermitian systems
Kawabata et al. 2019a
. The symmetry (
86
) is equivalent to the following three conditions:

\displaystyle\xi_{\uparrow\uparrow}(-\vec{k})=

\displaystyle\xi_{\downarrow\downarrow}(\vec{k}),

(87a)

\displaystyle\xi_{\uparrow\downarrow}(-\vec{k})=

\displaystyle-\xi_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\vec{k}),

(87b)

\displaystyle\xi_{\downarrow\uparrow}(-\vec{k})=

\displaystyle-\xi_{\downarrow\uparrow}(\vec{k}).

(87c)

It follows from Eq. (
81
) that condition (
87a
) is satisfied if

\displaystyle p(\vec{j}_{2},\uparrow|\vec{j}_{1},\uparrow)=p(\vec{j}_{1},\downarrow|\vec{j}_{2},\downarrow),

(88)

which represents the reciprocity in which the two spin components flow in opposite directions.
In contrast, since
p(\vec{j}_{2},\sigma|\vec{j}_{1},\sigma^{\prime})\geq 0
, conditions (
87b
) and (
87c
) are satisfied if and only if

p(\vec{j}_{2},\uparrow|\vec{j}_{1},\downarrow)=p(\vec{j}_{2},\downarrow|\vec{j}_{1},\uparrow)=0\ (\forall\vec{j}_{1},\vec{j}_{2}).

(89)

Thus, the truncated Bloch matrix is written as

\displaystyle X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{\uparrow\uparrow}(\vec{k})&0\\
0&\xi_{\downarrow\downarrow}(\vec{k})\end{pmatrix}.

(90)

This matrix actually possesses a higher symmetry; the two spin sectors are completely decoupled, and the probability in each sector is separately conserved. The Bloch matrix cannot be an arbitrary non-Hermitian matrix; it must respect the complete positivity and the trace-preserving property of the original CPTP map. Thus, the CPTP properties impose nontrivial constraints on the symmetry of CPTP maps, which may also have an impact on the topological classification.

VI.3.1
Class A

Since a CPTP map always has modular conjugation symmetry, a CPTP map in a class without
K
symmetry requires unitary symmetry by which the Bloch matrix can be block diagonalized, as explained in Sec.
V
. Here, we explicitly construct such examples of feedback control with unitary symmetry.

Let
\{\hat{M}_{m}\}
be measurement operators. We assume that the measurement operators commute with an observable
\hat{C}
:
[\hat{M}_{m},\hat{C}]=0\ (\forall m)
. Such measurement operators describe a quantum nondemolition (QND) measurement for the observable
\hat{C}
in the sense that all the moments of
\hat{C}
before and after the measurement stay the same
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
, that is,

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{C}^{r}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{C}^{r}\hat{\rho}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{C}^{r}\hat{\rho}]

(91)

for arbitrary
\hat{\rho}
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
We further assume that the feedback Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{m}
commutes with
\hat{C}
, i.e.,
[\hat{H}_{m},\hat{C}]=0
so that
\hat{C}
is conserved during feedback control. Then, it follows that the Kraus operator
\hat{K}_{m}=\hat{U}_{m}\hat{M}_{m}
with
\hat{U}_{m}=\exp[-i\hat{H}_{m}\tau]
commutes with
\hat{C}
:
[\hat{K}_{m},\hat{C}]=0\ (\forall m)
.
Thus, the CPTP map for this feedback control has strong symmetry (see Sec.
V.1
).

Because of strong symmetry, the CPTP map for feedback control can be block diagonalized according to the symmetry eigenvalues for the ket and bra spaces. Let

\hat{C}=\sum_{c}c\hat{Q}_{c}

(92)

be the spectral decomposition of
\hat{C}
, where
c
is an eigenvalue of
\hat{C}
and
\hat{Q}_{c}
is the projection operator onto the corresponding eigenspace
\mathcal{H}_{c}
.
Then, the doubled Hilbert space is decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{c,c^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{c}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{c^{\prime}}.

(93)

Accordingly, the CPTP map can be decomposed into blocks that act on subspaces with different symmetry eigenvalues. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\otimes\hat{K}_{m}^{*}=\sum_{c,c^{\prime}}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c,c^{\prime}},

(94)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c,c^{\prime}}:=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{Q}_{c}\otimes\hat{K}_{m}^{*}\hat{Q}_{c^{\prime}}^{*}

(95)

acts nontrivially only on the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{c}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{c^{\prime}}
.

The BL symmetry classes are examined for each block diagonalized matrix
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c,c^{\prime}}
. A crucial observation here is that every block does not necessarily have the modular conjugation symmetry since

\tilde{J}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c,c^{\prime}}\tilde{J}^{-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c^{\prime},c}.

(96)

In particular,
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c,c^{\prime}}
with
c\neq c^{\prime}
does not possess modular conjugation symmetry, but it is related to
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c^{\prime},c}
by this symmetry.
Thus, it belongs to class A if it does not have any other symmetry.

VI.3.2
Class AI
†

The other classes (psH, AI
†
, and AII
†
) without
K
symmetry include
C
or
Q
symmetry. These symmetries are realized with feedback protocols with two-point measurements similar to those in Sec.
VII.2
. Specifically, we consider a spin-
1/2
particle with two sublattice degrees of freedom, where a quantum state at site
\vec{j}
with sublattice
a=A,B
and spin
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
is denoted by
\ket{\vec{j},a,\sigma}
. We perform a projective position measurement on this particle and assume that this measurement does not disturb the spin state of the particle. Thus, a measurement outcome is specified only by the site index
\vec{j}
and the sublattice index
a
, and the measurement operator is given by

\hat{M}_{\vec{j},a}=\ket{\vec{j},a,\uparrow}\bra{\vec{j},a,\uparrow}+\ket{\vec{j},a,\downarrow}\bra{\vec{j},a,\downarrow}.

(97)

The measurement operator satisfies
[\hat{M}_{\vec{j},a},\hat{S}^{z}]=0
, where

\hat{S}^{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\vec{i},a}(\ket{\vec{i},a,\uparrow}\bra{\vec{i},a,\uparrow}-\ket{\vec{i},a,\downarrow}\bra{\vec{i},a,\downarrow})

(98)

is the magnetization. A physical example of such a measurement is given by the position measurement of an atom with nuclear spin degrees of freedom, in which the measurement process does not alter the nuclear-spin states.

We consider a CPTP map

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\dagger},

(99)

where
\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}=\hat{M}_{m_{2}}\hat{U}_{m_{1}}\hat{M}_{m_{1}}
is the Kraus operator,
\hat{M}_{m}
is the measurement operator (
97
) corresponding to a measurement outcome
m=(\vec{j},a)
, and
\hat{U}_{m}
is a unitary operator for feedback operation. We assume that
[\hat{U}_{m},\hat{S}^{z}]=0
. As in the case of a single measurement in Sec.
VI.3.1
, the CPTP map can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\otimes\hat{K}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\uparrow\uparrow}+\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\uparrow\downarrow}+\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\downarrow\uparrow}+\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\downarrow\downarrow},

(100)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\sum_{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1},\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}}

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{\vec{j}_{2},a_{2},\sigma}\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}}\hat{M}_{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1},\sigma}

\displaystyle\otimes\hat{M}_{\vec{j}_{2},a_{2},\sigma^{\prime}}^{*}\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1}}^{*}\hat{M}_{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1},\sigma^{\prime}}^{*},

(101)

and

\hat{M}_{\vec{j},a,\sigma}:=\ket{\vec{j},a,\sigma}\bra{\vec{j},a,\sigma}.

(102)

Similarly to the analysis in Sec.
VI.2
, the Bloch matrix for
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
has a block-diagonal structure (

), and the truncated Bloch matrix is given by

X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})}(\vec{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{AA}^{(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})}(\vec{k})&\xi_{AB}^{(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})}(\vec{k})\\
\xi_{BA}^{(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})}(\vec{k})&\xi_{BB}^{(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})}(\vec{k})\end{pmatrix},

(103)

where

\displaystyle\xi_{ab}^{(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})}(\vec{k})=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j}_{1},\vec{j}_{2}}q^{(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})}(\vec{j}_{2},a|\vec{j}_{1},b)e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}_{1}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}_{2}})}

\displaystyle q^{(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})}(\vec{j}_{2},a|\vec{j}_{1},b)=(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{1},b})_{\vec{j}_{2},a,\sigma;\vec{j}_{1},b,\sigma}(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{1},b})_{\vec{j}_{2},a,\sigma^{\prime};\vec{j}_{1},b,\sigma^{\prime}}^{*}.

(105)

For simplicity, here we focus on the truncated Bloch matrix (
103
) with
\sigma=\uparrow
and
\sigma^{\prime}=\downarrow
. It has
C
symmetry with
\eta_{C}=+1
, i.e.,

[X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(-\vec{k})]^{T}=X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k})

(106)

if

\displaystyle\xi_{ab}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(-\vec{k})=\xi_{ba}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k}).

(107)

A condition for Eq. (
107
) is given by

\displaystyle q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{2},a|\vec{j}_{1},b)=q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{1},b|\vec{j}_{2},a).

(108)

This condition is interpreted as reciprocity in the feedback process. Since the truncated Bloch matrix
X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k})
does not have
K
symmetry, it belongs to symmetry class AI
†
.

.

\displaystyle\xi_{AA}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(-\vec{k})=

\displaystyle\xi_{AB}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(-\vec{k})=

\displaystyle-\xi_{AB}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k}),

\displaystyle q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{2},A|\vec{j}_{1},A)=

\displaystyle q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{1},B|\vec{j}_{2},B),

(111a)

\sigma_{x}[X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k})]^{\dagger}\sigma_{x}^{-1}=X_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k}),

(112)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle[\xi_{AA}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k})]^{*}=

\displaystyle\xi_{BB}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k}),

(113a)

\displaystyle[\xi_{AB}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k})]^{*}=

\displaystyle\xi_{AB}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k}),

(113b)

\displaystyle[\xi_{BA}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k})]^{*}=

\displaystyle\xi_{BA}^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{k}).

(113c)

Conditions (
113a
)-(
113c
) are satisfied if

\displaystyle[q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{2},A|\vec{j}_{1},A)]^{*}=

\displaystyle q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{1},B|\vec{j}_{2},B),

(114a)

\displaystyle[q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{2},A|\vec{j}_{1},B)]^{*}=

\displaystyle q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{1},A|\vec{j}_{2},B),

(114b)

\displaystyle[q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{2},B|\vec{j}_{1},A)]^{*}=

\displaystyle q^{(\uparrow\downarrow)}(\vec{j}_{1},B|\vec{j}_{2},A).

(114c)

In terms of the feedback unitary operators, sufficient conditions for Eqs. (
114a
)-(
114c
) are given by

\displaystyle(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{1},A})_{\vec{j}_{2},A,\sigma;\vec{j}_{1},A,\sigma}^{*}=

\displaystyle(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{2},B})_{\vec{j}_{1},B,\sigma;\vec{j}_{2},B,\sigma},

(115a)

\displaystyle(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{1},B})_{\vec{j}_{2},A,\sigma;\vec{j}_{1},B,\sigma}^{*}=

\displaystyle(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}_{2},B})_{\vec{j}_{1},A,\sigma;\vec{j}_{2},B,\sigma},

(115b)

VII.1
Su-Schrieffer-Heeger demon

First, we present a model of topological feedback control with
K
symmetry with
\eta_{K}=+1
that is distinct from the modular conjugation symmetry. This model provides a concrete example for the discussion presented in Sec.
VI.1
. We consider a 1D lattice with two sublattices. Each unit cell is labeled by an index
j
and each sublattice is labeled by
a=A,B
. We first perform a projective position measurement, which completely resolves the sublattice structure. A measurement outcome is specified by
m=(j,a)
, and the corresponding measurement operator is given by
\hat{M}_{m}=\ket{j,a}\bra{j,a}
. Next, we perform feedback control with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{a}\ (a=A,B)
, which depends only on the measurement outcome of the sublattice index. The feedback Hamiltonians are given by the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) model
Su et al. 1979
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{A}=

\displaystyle-J_{1}\sum_{j}(\ket{j,A}\bra{j,B}+\mathrm{H.c.})

\displaystyle-J_{2}\sum_{j}(\ket{j+1,A}\bra{j,B}+\mathrm{H.c.}),

(122)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{B}=

\displaystyle-J_{1}^{\prime}\sum_{j}(\ket{j,A}\bra{j,B}+\mathrm{H.c.})

\displaystyle-J_{2}^{\prime}\sum_{j}(\ket{j+1,A}\bra{j,B}+\mathrm{H.c.}),

(123)

where
J_{1},J_{2},J_{1}^{\prime},J_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
are the hopping amplitudes. See Fig.
12
for a schematic illustration of the feedback protocol. We note that the SSH model has time-reversal symmetry

\hat{H}_{a}^{*}=\hat{H}_{a},

(124a)

particle-hole symmetry

\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\hat{H}_{a}^{*}\hat{\Sigma}_{z}=-\hat{H}_{a},

(124b)

and chiral symmetry

\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\hat{H}_{a}\hat{\Sigma}_{z}=-\hat{H}_{a},

(124c)

where

\hat{\Sigma}_{z}=\sum_{j}(\ket{j,A}\bra{j,A}-\ket{j,B}\bra{j,B}).

(125)

Note that
\hat{\Sigma}_{z}^{2}=\hat{I}
.
Consequently, the unitary time-evolution operator
\hat{U}_{a}=\exp[-i\hat{H}_{a}\tau]
has the following symmetries (see Appendix
D
):

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{a}^{T}=\hat{U}_{a},

(126a)

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\hat{U}_{a}^{*}\hat{\Sigma}_{z}=\hat{U}_{a},

(126b)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\hat{U}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{\Sigma}_{z}=\hat{U}_{a},

(126c)

which follow from Eqs. (
124a
)-(
124c
), respectively.
Among the symmetries (
126a
)-(
126c
), only particle-hole symmetry (
126b
) leads to the symmetry of a quantum channel for this feedback control.
Since the measurement operator satisfies

\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\Sigma}_{z}=\hat{M}_{m},

(127)

particle-hole symmetry (
126b
) leads to
K
symmetry

(\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\otimes\hat{\Sigma}_{z}^{*})\sum_{m}(\hat{K}_{m}^{*}\otimes\hat{K}_{m})(\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\otimes\hat{\Sigma}_{z}^{*})=\sum_{m}(\hat{K}_{m}\otimes\hat{K}_{m}^{*})

(128)

of the CPTP map, where
\hat{K}_{m}=\hat{K}_{(j,a)}=\hat{U}_{a}\hat{M}_{m}
is the Kraus operator.

(129)

where
\tilde{S}
is the swap operator defined by Eq. (
72
). Hence, the CPTP map can be block diagonalized according to the symmetry eigenvalues of the unitary symmetry (
129
). The eigenvalues of
(\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\otimes\hat{\Sigma}_{z}^{*})\tilde{S}
are given by
\pm 1
. The eigenvectors for the eigenvalue
+1
are given by

\displaystyle\ket{j,A}\otimes\ket{j,A},

(130a)

\displaystyle\ket{j,B}\otimes\ket{j,B},

(130b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{j,A}\otimes\ket{j^{\prime},B}+\ket{j^{\prime},B}\otimes\ket{j,A}),

(130c)

and those for the eigenvalue
-1
are given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{j,A}\otimes\ket{j^{\prime},B}-\ket{j^{\prime},B}\otimes\ket{j,A}).

(131)

In each eigenspace of the operator
(\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\otimes\hat{\Sigma}_{z}^{*})\tilde{S}

62
) and particle-hole symmetry (
128
) are equivalent to each other since
(\hat{\Sigma}_{z}\otimes\hat{\Sigma}_{z}^{*})=\pm\tilde{S}

K
symmetry. Thus, each symmetry sector of the CPTP map belongs to class AI.

VII.2
Helical Maxwell demon

Next, we construct a model of symmetry-protected topological feedback control that belongs to class AI
+
psH
-
. We consider a spin-
1/2
particle on a 1D lattice with
L
sites. The Hilbert space of this system is spanned by a basis
\{\ket{j,\sigma}\}
(
j=1,\cdots,L
,
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
). We consider feedback control of this particle with a two-point measurement scheme (see Fig.
14
for a schematic illustration). We first perform a projective measurement of the position and spin with the projection operators
\hat{P}_{j,\sigma}=\ket{j,\sigma}\bra{j,\sigma}
. Let
m_{1}=(j_{1},\sigma_{1})
be an outcome of this measurement. If we obtain a measurement outcome
(j_{1},\uparrow)
, we raise a spin-independent potential at site
j_{1}-1
to prevent the particle from going to the left. If we obtain a measurement outcome
(j_{1},\downarrow)
, we raise a spin-independent potential at site
j_{1}+1
to prevent the particle from going to the right. The feedback Hamiltonians are given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{j_{1},\uparrow}=

\displaystyle-J\sum_{j}\sum_{\sigma}(\ket{j,\sigma}\bra{j+1,\sigma}+\ket{j+1,\sigma}\bra{j,\sigma})

\displaystyle+V\sum_{\sigma}\ket{j_{1}-1,\sigma}\bra{j_{1}-1,\sigma}

\displaystyle+h_{x}\sum_{j}(\ket{j,\uparrow}\bra{j,\downarrow}+\ket{j,\downarrow}\bra{j,\uparrow})

(132)

and

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{j_{1},\downarrow}=

\displaystyle-J\sum_{j}\sum_{\sigma}(\ket{j,\sigma}\bra{j+1,\sigma}+\ket{j+1,\sigma}\bra{j,\sigma})

\displaystyle+V\sum_{\sigma}\ket{j_{1}+1,\sigma}\bra{j_{1}+1,\sigma}

\displaystyle+h_{x}\sum_{j}(\ket{j,\uparrow}\bra{j,\downarrow}+\ket{j,\downarrow}\bra{j,\uparrow}),

(133)

where
J\in\mathbb{R}
is the hopping amplitude,
V>0
is the height of the potential, and
h_{x}
denotes the strength of a transverse magnetic field that mixes the spin components. Feedback control is described by a unitary operator
\hat{U}_{j_{1},\sigma_{1}}=\exp[-i\hat{H}_{j_{1},\sigma_{1}}\tau]
. After feedback control, we perform the second projective measurement with projection operators
\hat{P}_{j,\sigma}
. Let
m_{2}=(j_{2},\sigma_{2})
be an outcome of this second measurement. If
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{1}
, we do nothing. If
\sigma_{2}\neq\sigma_{1}

\pi
pulse of the magnetic field. Through this feedback control, a spin-up particle is transported to the right while a spin-down particle is transported to the left, which is analogous to helical edge states in the quantum spin Hall effect
Kane and Mele 2005a
;
Kane and Mele 2005b
;
Bernevig and Zhang 2006
;
Wu et al. 2006
;
König et al. 2007
.

VII.3
QND demon

Here, we present a model of topological feedback control with strong unitary symmetry by following the construction in Sec.
VI.3
. We consider a spin-
1/2
particle on a 1D lattice with two sublattices. A quantum state at site
j
with sublattice
a=A,B
and spin
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
is denoted by
\ket{j,a,\sigma}
. We perform a projective position measurement on this particle and assume that this measurement does not disturb the spin state of the particle. Thus, a measurement outcome is specified only by the site index
j
and the sublattice index
a
; therefore, the measurement operator is given by

\hat{M}_{j,a}=\ket{j,a,\uparrow}\bra{j,a,\uparrow}+\ket{j,a,\downarrow}\bra{j,a,\downarrow}.

(144)

The measurement operator satisfies
[\hat{M}_{j,a},\hat{S}^{z}]=0
, where

\hat{S}^{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,a}(\ket{i,a,\uparrow}\bra{i,a,\uparrow}-\ket{i,a,\downarrow}\bra{i,a,\downarrow})

(145)

is the magnetization.
Thus, the position measurement does not influence the spin degrees of freedom, and it serves as a QND measurement for
\hat{S}^{z}
(see Sec.
VI.3.1
).

If the measurement outcome is
m=(j,a)

\hat{H}_{m}

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{j,A}=

\displaystyle-J_{1}\sum_{i,\sigma}(\ket{i,A,\sigma}\bra{i,B,\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.})

\displaystyle-J_{2}\sum_{i,\sigma}(\ket{i+1,A,\sigma}\bra{i,B,\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.})

\displaystyle+V\sum_{\sigma}\ket{j-1,B,\sigma}\bra{j-1,B,\sigma}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}(h_{z}+h_{s})(\ket{i,A,\uparrow}\bra{i,A,\uparrow}-\ket{i,A,\downarrow}\bra{i,A,\downarrow})

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}(h_{z}-h_{s})(\ket{i,B,\uparrow}\bra{i,B,\uparrow}-\ket{i,B,\downarrow}\bra{i,B,\downarrow}),

(146)

and

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{j,B}=

\displaystyle-J_{1}\sum_{i,\sigma}(\ket{i,A,\sigma}\bra{i,B,\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.})

\displaystyle-J_{2}\sum_{i,\sigma}(\ket{i+1,A,\sigma}\bra{i,B,\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.})

\displaystyle+V\sum_{\sigma}\ket{j,A,\sigma}\bra{j,A,\sigma}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}(h_{z}+h_{s})(\ket{i,A,\uparrow}\bra{i,A,\uparrow}-\ket{i,A,\downarrow}\bra{i,A,\downarrow})

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}(h_{z}-h_{s})(\ket{i,B,\uparrow}\bra{i,B,\uparrow}-\ket{i,B,\downarrow}\bra{i,B,\downarrow}),

are the hopping amplitudes,

(

[\hat{H}_{m},\hat{S}^{z}]=0

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\otimes\hat{K}_{m}^{*}

\displaystyle=

(149)

and

(150)

VII.5
Chern demon

In the previous subsections, we have focused on the models of quantum feedback control that are characterized by point-gap topology. In this subsection, we present an example of a topological Maxwell demon that exhibits nontrivial line-gap topology. Our main idea is to simulate the Haldane model
Haldane 1988

1/2

\hat{M}_{\vec{j},a}=\ket{\vec{j},a,\uparrow}\bra{\vec{j},a,\uparrow}+\ket{\vec{j},a,\downarrow}\bra{\vec{j},a,\downarrow},

(166)

\displaystyle\hat{H}=

\displaystyle J_{1}\sum_{\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}\sum_{\langle\vec{i},\vec{j}\rangle}(\ket{\vec{i},A,\sigma}\bra{\vec{j},B,\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.})

\displaystyle+J_{2}\sum_{\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}\sum_{\langle\langle\vec{i},\vec{j}\rangle\rangle}\sum_{a=A,B}(\ket{\vec{i},a,\sigma}\bra{\vec{j},a,\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.}),

Here, special attention should be given to the physical difference between a Chern insulator and the Chern demon presented in this section. A Chern insulator is characterized by line-gap topology of a Hamiltonian, which is a generator of the dynamics of a system. Since the derivative of the dispersion relation with respect to momentum gives the group velocity, the gapless spectrum of edge states leads to chiral modes, which propagate unidirectionally along the edges. In contrast, the Chern demon is defined by line-gap topology of a quantum channel, which is a time-evolution operator itself rather than its generator. Thus, the physical meaning of the gapless edge-mode spectrum of the Chern demon is different from that of a Chern insulator. In particular, the boundary-localized modes of the Chern demon do not necessarily propagate along the edges of the system. This physical implication merits further investigation.

VIII
Topological phenomena arising from complete positivity and trace preservation

Although topological feedback control exploits non-Hermitian topology of quantum channels, it is crucially different from quantum systems with non-Hermitian Hamiltonians due to the fact that a quantum channel must satisfy complete positivity and trace preservation, i.e., it is a CPTP map
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. Here, we discuss how these properties lead to topological phenomena that are absent in conventional non-Hermitian systems.

VIII.1
Boundary-localized steady state

Complete positivity and trace preservation ensure that a quantum channel gives a map between density matrices, which leads to the existence of a steady state with the unit eigenvalue
Wolf 2012
. Consequently, the topological Maxwell demons discussed in Secs.
IV
and
VII
(except for those in Secs.
VII.4
and
VII.5
) exhibit boundary-localized steady states under the OBC. This feature is in stark contrast to topological phases described by non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, where the conservation of the norm of a state is not guaranteed and therefore a steady state does not exist, in general. Although those boundary-localized steady states in topological feedback control appear to be closely related to the non-Hermitian skin effect, we can construct a model that shows the non-Hermitian skin effect with no localized steady state (see Appendix
F
). In this sense, the emergence of boundary-localized steady states is a special feature of quantum channels and should be distinguished from the non-Hermitian skin effect. We note that a similar phenomenon is observed in classical stochastic processes where the probability conservation is satisfied, unlike general non-Hermitian systems
Sawada et al. 2024
.

VIII.2
Breakdown of conventional bulk-boundary correspondence in non-Hermitian skin effects

The bulk-boundary correspondence in non-Hermitian systems with nontrivial point-gap topology states that a nonzero winding number of the eigenspectrum under the PBC leads to boundary-localized skin modes under the OBC whose eigenvalues generically reside inside the region delimited by the PBC spectrum
Okuma et al. 2020
. However, eigenvalues of the boundary-localized steady states discussed in Sec.
VIII.1
must be unity by definition, and they do not belong to the internal region enclosed by the PBC spectrum (see, e.g., Figs.
6
,
8
, and
13
). More strikingly, it is possible that some eigenvalues under the OBC lie outside the region enclosed by the PBC spectrum (see the case of the QND demon shown in Fig.
16
). These facts indicate that the conventional bulk-boundary correspondence in non-Hermitian skin effects does not hold in topological feedback control.

The breakdown of the conventional bulk-boundary correspondence is attributed to the requirement of complete positivity and trace preservation for a quantum channel. The Hamiltonian under the OBC in a conventional non-Hermitian system is obtained by eliminating the matrix elements of the PBC Hamiltonian across the boundary of the system. However, such a straightforward implementation of the OBC for a quantum channel violates complete positivity and trace preservation. Instead, a quantum channel under the OBC should be defined as follows. Suppose that a quantum channel for feedback control under the PBC is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{PBC}}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{m}e^{-i\hat{H}_{m,\mathrm{PBC}}\tau}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}e^{i\hat{H}_{m,\mathrm{PBC}}\tau},

(171)

where
\hat{H}_{m,\mathrm{PBC}}
is a feedback Hamiltonian under the PBC. Then, the corresponding quantum channel under the OBC is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{OBC}}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{m}e^{-i\hat{H}_{m,\mathrm{OBC}}\tau}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}e^{i\hat{H}_{m,\mathrm{OBC}}\tau},

(172)

by using time evolution governed by the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{m,\mathrm{OBC}}
under the OBC so that
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{OBC}}
gives a CPTP map. By comparing Eqs. (
171
) and (
172
), one can see that the matrix elements of the quantum channel near the boundary are reconstructed to ensure complete positivity and trace preservation.

Since the OBC for a quantum channel is defined in a manner different from that for a Hamiltonian, the existing proofs of the bulk-boundary correspondence in non-Hermitian systems
Gong et al. 2018a
;
Okuma et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Borgnia et al. 2020
cannot simply be applied to quantum channels. The bulk-boundary correspondence in topological feedback control should be formulated so as to incorporate complete positivity and trace preservation.

VIII.3
Topological distinction between steady states and transient dynamics

The complete positivity also impacts the topological classification. As discussed in Sec.
V.2
, the complete positivity (or the Hermiticity preservation that follows) imposes the modular conjugation symmetry on a quantum channel. While some symmetry sectors of a quantum channel do not necessarily satisfy the modular conjugation symmetry (as shown in Sec.
VI.3
), a sector that includes a steady state must satisfy it since the steady-state density matrix must be Hermitian
Albert and Jiang 2014
. Thus, allowed symmetry classes for a steady-state sector of a quantum channel are restricted to those three classes (AI, AI
+
psH
+
, and AI
+
psH
-
), which include modular conjugation symmetry. Thus, the topological classification for boundary-localized steady states in Sec.
VIII.1
is more restrictive than the general topological classification of non-Hermitian systems due to the complete positivity.

If transient dynamics are considered, topological phenomena that are prohibited in steady states due to complete positivity become possible. An example is given by the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
demon in Sec.
VII.4
. As shown in Fig.
18
, a spin-diagonal sector
\mathcal{E}_{\uparrow\uparrow}
that includes a steady state is topologically trivial, whereas an off-diagonal sector
\mathcal{E}_{\uparrow\downarrow}
displays nontrivial point-gap topology owing to the absence of modular conjugation symmetry. The nontrivial topology in the off-diagonal sector manifests itself in the transient dynamics of the transverse components of the spin density, which vanish in the steady state (see Fig.
19
).
The topological distinction between the steady state and transient dynamics thus enriches topological phenomena in quantum feedback control.

IX
Dissipative feedback control

IX.1
Formalism

In reality, there exist many sources of noise and dissipation during feedback control.
The formalism presented so far can be generalized to such cases in which feedback operations are not unitary. Suppose that a feedback operation depending on a measurement outcome
m
is described by a CPTP map

\mathcal{F}_{m}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}\hat{\rho}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}^{\dagger},

(173)

where the Kraus operators
\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}
satisfy

\sum_{\alpha}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}=\hat{I}.

(174)

Then, given an initial density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}
, the density matrix after feedback control is

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}p_{m}\mathcal{F}_{m}\Bigl(\frac{1}{p_{m}}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\sum_{\alpha}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}^{\dagger},

(175)

where
p_{m}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}]
is the probability of outcome
m
for the measurement operator
\hat{M}_{m}
. Thus, feedback control is described by a quantum channel

\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{m,\alpha}\hat{K}_{m,\alpha}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m,\alpha}^{\dagger}

(176)

with Kraus operators

\hat{K}_{m,\alpha}:=\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}\hat{M}_{m}.

(177)

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{m,\alpha}\hat{K}_{m,\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{m,\alpha}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,\alpha}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}\hat{M}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{m}=\hat{I}

(178)

holds due to Eq. (
174
) and
\sum_{m}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{m}=\hat{I}
.

By repeating the analysis in Sec.
III.1
for the quantum channel (
176
), the eigenspectrum of the quantum channel is given by that of the Bloch matrix
X(\vec{k})
, whose matrix elements are given by

\displaystyle X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}\sum_{m,\alpha}(\hat{K}_{m,\alpha})_{\vec{j},a;\vec{j}^{\prime},b}

\displaystyle\times(\hat{K}_{m,\alpha})^{*}_{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu},c;\vec{j}^{\prime}+\vec{\nu},d}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}.

(179)

The difference from the case with unitary feedback is the existence of the additional index
\alpha
, which can be regarded as information that cannot be specified in the feedback process.

IX.2
Dissipative Maxwell demon

As an example of dissipative feedback control described by a CPTP map, Eq. (
173
), we consider a chiral Maxwell demon (see Sec.
IV
) subject to dissipation. We consider a spinless particle on a 1D lattice with
L
sites and perform a projective position measurement for this particle as in Sec.
IV
. We then perform a feedback operation governed by the Lindblad equation

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{m}(\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i[\hat{H}_{m},\hat{\rho}]+\sum_{j}\Bigl(\hat{L}_{m,j}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{m,j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{m,j}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{m,j},\hat{\rho}\}\Bigr),

(180)

where
\mathcal{L}_{m}
is the generator (Lindbladian) conditioned on the measurement outcome,
H_{m}
is the feedback Hamiltonian given in Sec.
IV.1
, and
\hat{L}_{m,j}
is the Lindblad operator acting on a local region around site
j
. Then, a CPTP map for the feedback operation is given by

\mathcal{F}_{m}=e^{\mathcal{L}_{m}\tau},

(181)

where
\tau
is the duration of feedback. Using the Lindblad equation (
180
), we can consider various dissipative effects on feedback control such as noise during feedback and a coupling to a reservoir. In particular, if the system is in contact with a heat bath, the topological Maxwell demon can rectify thermal fluctuations as well as quantum fluctuations to achieve unidirectional transport of a particle. However, we note that the Hamiltonian and the Lindblad operators in Eq. (
180
) should be chosen to be local to ensure the locality of the Kraus operators and to allow truncation of the Bloch matrix into a finite-dimensional matrix (see Sec.
III.2
). The locality-preserving Lindblad equation can be derived if the correlation time of the environment is sufficiently short
Shiraishi et al. 2024
.

Since the matrix elements of the Kraus operator
\hat{K}_{m,\alpha}=\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}\hat{M}_{m}
are written as

(\hat{K}_{m,\alpha})_{i,j}=(\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}\hat{M}_{m})_{i,j}=(\hat{F}_{m,\alpha})_{i,m}\delta_{j,m},

(182)

the Bloch matrix of the quantum channel is given by

\displaystyle X_{\mu,\nu}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\sum_{m,\alpha}(\hat{K}_{m,\alpha})_{j,j^{\prime}}(\hat{K}_{m,\alpha})^{*}_{j+\mu,j^{\prime}+\nu}e^{-ik(j-j^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\nu,0}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j}\sum_{m,\alpha}(\hat{F}_{m,\alpha})_{j,m}(\hat{F}_{m,\alpha})^{*}_{j+\mu,m}e^{-ik(j-m)}.

(183)

Thus, similarly to the case with unitary feedback control, eigenvalues of the Bloch matrix are given by

\displaystyle\xi_{0}(k)=

\displaystyle X_{0,0}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j}\sum_{m,\alpha}|(\hat{F}_{m,\alpha})_{j,m}|^{2}e^{-ik(j-m)},

(184)

\displaystyle\xi_{1}(k)=

\displaystyle\xi_{2}(k)=\cdots=\xi_{L-1}(k)=0.

(185)

Here, we note that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}|(\hat{F}_{m,\alpha})_{j,m}|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\bra{j}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}\ket{m}\bra{m}\hat{F}_{m,\alpha}^{\dagger}\ket{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bra{j}\mathcal{F}_{m}(\ket{m}\bra{m})\ket{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bra{j}\mathcal{F}_{m}\Bigl(\frac{1}{p_{m}}\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}\Bigr)\ket{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(j|m),

(186)

where
p(j|m)
is the probability of a particle being found at site
j
after feedback control with outcome
m
. Then, the eigenvalue
\xi_{0}(k)
is written as

\displaystyle\xi_{0}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j,m}p(j|m)e^{-ik(j-m)},

(187)

which takes the same form as in the case with unitary feedback control [see Eq. (
43
)]. Thus, regardless of whether feedback control is unitary or not, the eigenvalues of the quantum channel and its topology are completely determined by the probability distribution
p(j|m)
of the position after the feedback.

To demonstrate the robustness of the chiral Maxwell demon against dissipation during feedback control, we consider the Lindblad operators

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{m,j}^{(\mathrm{d})}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\ket{j}\bra{j},

(188)

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{m,j}^{(\mathrm{L})}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{L}}}\ket{j-1}\bra{j},

(189)

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{m,j}^{(\mathrm{R})}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{R}}}\ket{j+1}\bra{j},

(190)

which, respectively, describe dephasing, stochastic hopping to the left, and stochastic hopping to the right [see Fig.
21
(a) for a schematic illustration]. Figure
21
(b) shows the eigenspectrum of the quantum channel for the chiral Maxwell demon subject to dissipation. As can be seen from the figure, the loop structure of the eigenspectrum under the PBC is robust against dissipation, leading to the non-Hermitian skin effect under the OBC. This result indicates that the chiral Maxwell demon can rectify the noise induced by dissipation as well as quantum fluctuations induced by unitary evolution and achieve unidirectional transport that is robust against noise and decoherence.

IX.3
Feedback control with engineered dissipation

Dissipative feedback control not only shows the stability of topological feedback control against noise, but it can also be exploited to utilize dissipation as an essential tool for feedback control. Such a possibility can be envisaged since recent developments in quantum technologies with atoms, ions, and photons have enabled one to realize engineered dissipation
Müller et al. 2012
. For example, localized dissipation can be introduced in ultracold atoms, where the strength of dissipation is controlled by laser fields
Barontini et al. 2013
;
Corman et al. 2019
;
Huang et al. 2023
;
Tonielli et al. 2019
;
Dolgirev et al. 2020
. Motivated by the possibility of dissipation engineering, we propose a topological feedback control that is enabled by dissipation.

The setup of feedback control with engineered dissipation is as follows. We consider a spinless particle on a 1D lattice, perform a projective position measurement on it, and obtain a measurement outcome
m
. Next, we let the particle undergo dissipative time evolution under the Lindblad equation (
180
). Here, we assume that the Hamiltonian does not depend on the measurement outcome
m
and that it is given by a simple tight-binding model

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{m}=\hat{H}:=-J\sum_{j}(\ket{j}\bra{j+1}+\mathrm{H.c.})

(191)

with
J>0
being the hopping amplitude.
The dependence of the conditioned Lindblad equation (
180
) on the measurement outcome is encoded in the Lindblad operator, where engineered dissipation is described by

\hat{L}_{m,j-1}=\delta_{m,j}\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\ket{j-1}\bra{j-1}.

(192)

In other words, we introduce localized dissipation to the left side of the particle [see Fig.
22
(a) for a schematic illustration]. Here, we exploit the continuous quantum Zeno effect
Daley 2014
in the strong-dissipation regime
\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}\gg J
to suppress the hopping of the particle to the site that is subject to strong dissipation. Because of this feedback control of dissipation, hopping of a particle to the right is facilitated, and chiral transport is eventually achieved.

The eigenspectrum of a quantum channel for this dissipative feedback control is shown in Fig.
22
(b). The eigenspectrum under the PBC forms a loop, and that under the OBC displays the non-Hermitian skin effect. Here, the loop structure of the eigenspectrum under the PBC is realized due to the dissipation since the Hamiltonian (
191
) does not contain a wall potential. Thus, the topological feedback control and the associated chiral transport are achieved by dissipation.

X
Conclusion and outlook

In this paper, we have presented a class of dynamical topological phases realized with feedback control, which are characterized by the topology of quantum channels. The nontrivial point-gap topology of a quantum channel characterized by a winding number ensures robust chiral transport, which is analogous to the chiral edge current in the quantum Hall effect and leads to the emergence of the non-Hermitian skin effect under the open boundary condition. We have also provided a symmetry classification of quantum feedback control, identified ten classes compatible with projective measurements, and constructed examples of feedback control in each symmetry class. This general framework of symmetry and topology of quantum feedback control provides a guiding principle for designing stable feedback control of quantum systems since topology is insensitive to small disturbances. Furthermore, feedback control offers a unique way to control dynamical topological phases of matter distinct from other well-known classes such as periodically driven systems
Thouless 1983
;
Oka and Aoki 2009
;
Kitagawa et al. 2010a
;
Kitagawa et al. 2010b
;
Kitagawa et al. 2011
;
Lindner et al. 2011
;
Nathan and Rudner 2015
;
Roy and Harper 2017
;
Higashikawa et al. 2019
;
Oka and Kitamura 2019
;
Rudner and Lindner 2020
;
Harper et al. 2020
, non-Hermitian systems
Esaki et al. 2011
;
Gong et al. 2018a
;
Kawabata et al. 2019a
;
Zhou and Lee 2019
;
Bergholtz et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Okuma and Sato 2023
, and open quantum systems subject to engineered dissipation
Bardyn et al. 2013
;
Lieu et al. 2020
.

Topological feedback control is expected to be realized in various experimental platforms. For example, the past decade has witnessed remarkable progress in site-resolved measurement techniques and control of ultracold atoms in optical lattices
Bakr et al. 2009
;
Weitenberg et al. 2011
;
Gross and Bakr 2021
. More recently, atoms in optical tweezer arrays have emerged as highly controllable quantum simulators in which individual atoms can be measured and addressed in a programmable manner
Barredo et al. 2016
;
Bernien et al. 2017
;
Barredo et al. 2018
;
Ebadi et al. 2021
;
Bluvstein et al. 2022
;
Bluvstein et al. 2023
;
Browaeys and Lahaye 2020
. The high-precision position measurement and subsequent local control of a Hamiltonian for topological feedback control can be achieved by these state-of-the-art techniques. In addition to these promising platforms, quantum feedback control has widely been used in photonic systems
Sayrin et al. 2011
;
Vidrighin et al. 2016
, levitated nanoparticles
Kamba et al. 2021
;
Tebbenjohanns et al. 2021
;
Magrini et al. 2021
, and superconducting qubits
Vijay et al. 2012
;
Cottet et al. 2017
;
Naghiloo et al. 2018
;
Masuyama et al. 2018
. Given the rapid development in quantum control, topological feedback control in various experimental platforms will expand the scope of topological phases of matter.

We conclude this paper by discussing future perspectives and open problems.
First, while we have mostly focused on feedback control with projective measurements, the present framework for topology of quantum channels is also applicable to general quantum measurements; therefore, it is natural to ask whether nonprojective measurements can be harnessed to construct topological feedback control. In particular, since the symmetry classification in Sec.
V.3
is based on the spectral property of projective measurement channels, BL symmetry classes other than those in Table
2
are not prohibited for general quantum measurements. Since a quantum channel in a symmetry class beyond the tenfold way necessarily has a pair of eigenvalues
\pm 1
(if one assumes the existence of a steady state), its eigenvalue spectrum is reminiscent of that of dissipative time crystals
Gong et al. 2018a
;
Gong et al. 2018b
, where the eigenvalue
-1
implies oscillating components under repeated application of a quantum channel.

Second, as discussed in Sec.
VIII
, a crucial distinction between quantum channels and general non-Hermitian matrices is complete positivity and trace preservation, which should be satisfied by any time evolution of the density matrix of a quantum system
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. Although the topological classification of non-Hermitian matrices without the CPTP condition has been obtained
Kawabata et al. 2019a
;
Zhou and Lee 2019
, it is unclear whether the CPTP condition alters the topological classification. For example, in Sec.
VI.2
, we have shown that the CPTP condition places a nontrivial constraint on the Bloch matrix with transposition symmetry, which turns out to be characterized by an integer topological invariant rather than a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant. It merits further study to investigate the generality of this phenomenon. From a broader perspective, this problem may be related to a classification of time evolution of open quantum systems described by CPTP maps.

Last but not least, topological feedback control suggests a novel connection between topology and Maxwell’s demon. For example, an experimental realization of Maxwell’s demon in Ref.
Toyabe et al. 2010
demonstrated chiral transport of a particle due to feedback control, in close analogy with the chiral Maxwell demon in Sec.
IV
(see also Ref.
Liu et al. 2023
). Maxwell’s demon has attracted century-long interest in thermodynamics and statistical physics, and an information-theoretic understanding of the role of Maxwell’s demon has led to the integration of thermodynamics and information theory
Parrondo et al. 2015
. Thus, investigating the topology of feedback control may shed light on a hitherto unnoticed role of topology in thermodynamics.

Appendix A
Bloch theory of quantum channels

The Bloch theory lies at the heart of topological band theory
Moessner and Moore 2021
. However, how to generalize this theory to feedback-controlled quantum systems is nontrivial since feedback control involves not only unitary time evolution but also measurement processes. Here, we develop a Bloch theory of quantum channels described by CPTP maps. The following formalism is not limited to discrete quantum feedback control but can generally be applied to any translationally invariant quantum channels in single-particle systems.

A.1
CPTP map as a non-Hermitian operator

We begin by representing a CPTP map in terms of a non-Hermitian matrix. We consider a single-particle quantum system on a lattice (see Sec.
III.1
for details). We first vectorize the density matrix

\hat{\rho}=\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b}\rho_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}

(193)

by using the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism
Jamiołkowski 1972
;
Choi 1975
;
Jiang et al. 2013
;
Wilde 2013
as

\displaystyle\ket{\hat{\rho}}:=

\displaystyle(\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{I})\sqrt{D}\ket{\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b}\rho_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}\ket{\vec{i},a}\otimes\ket{\vec{j},b},

(194)

where
\{\ket{\vec{i},a}\}
is an orthonormal basis set of the
D
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of a single-particle system on a lattice (
D=N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}
; see Sec.
III.1
), and

\ket{\Omega}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\sum_{\vec{i}}\sum_{a}\ket{\vec{i},a}\otimes\ket{\vec{i},a}

(195)

is a maximally entangled state on a doubled Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. Consequently, we obtain a mapping from a CPTP map

\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}

(196)

to a non-Hermitian operator
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
acting on
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\ket{\hat{\rho}}:=

\displaystyle\ket{\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})\otimes\hat{I})\sqrt{D}\ket{\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j},\vec{l}}\sum_{a,b,c}\rho_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}\hat{K}_{m}\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}\ket{\vec{l},c}\otimes\ket{\vec{l},c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b}\rho_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}\hat{K}_{m}\ket{\vec{i},a}\otimes\hat{K}_{m}^{*}\ket{\vec{j},b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\otimes\hat{K}_{m}^{*}\Bigr)\ket{\hat{\rho}}.

(197)

Thus, the matrix representation of
\mathcal{E}
is

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\otimes\hat{K}_{m}^{*}.

(198)

The eigenvalues of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
are the same as those of
\mathcal{E}
since

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\ket{\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}}=

\displaystyle\ket{\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ket{\xi_{n}\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\xi_{n}\ket{\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}},

(199)

where we use Eq. (
194
) in deriving the last equality.

A.2
Momentum-space representation

Here, we consider a translationally invariant quantum channel satisfying

\sum_{m}(\hat{T}_{\lambda}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{-1})\hat{\rho}(\hat{T}_{\lambda}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{-1})^{\dagger}=\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}

(200)

for arbitrary
\hat{\rho}
, with
\hat{T}_{\lambda}\ (\lambda=1,\cdots,d)
being a translation operator:

\hat{T}_{\lambda}\ket{\vec{i},a}=\ket{\vec{i}+\vec{e}_{\lambda},a}.

(201)

In the matrix representation
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
of the CPTP map, the translational symmetry in Eq. (
200
) reads

\displaystyle(\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*})\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*})^{-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}.

(202)

In the context of open quantum systems, this symmetry can be regarded as weak symmetry of the CPTP map (see Sec.
V.1
)
Buča and Prosen 2012
;
Albert and Jiang 2014
. In fact, since the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
commutes with the tensor-product translation operator
\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*}
appearing in Eq. (
202
), the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
can be block diagonalized with respect to eigenvalues of
\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*}
. Eigenvectors of
\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*}
are constructed from the eigenstates

\ket{\vec{k},a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}\ket{\vec{j},a}

(203)

of the translation operator
\hat{T}_{\lambda}
with eigenvalue
e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{\lambda}}
as

\displaystyle(\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*})\ket{\vec{k},a}\otimes\ket{-\vec{k}^{\prime},b}=e^{-i(\vec{k}-\vec{k}^{\prime})\cdot\vec{a}_{\lambda}}\ket{\vec{k},a}\otimes\ket{-\vec{k}^{\prime},b}.

(204)

Thus, eigenvectors of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
are classified according to the eigenvalues of
\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*}
. Since the vector
\ket{\vec{k},a}\otimes\ket{-\vec{k}^{\prime},b}
corresponds to an operator
\ket{\vec{k},a}\bra{\vec{k}^{\prime},b}
through the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism [see Eqs. (
193
) and (
194
)],
\vec{k}-\vec{k}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
204
) is physically interpreted as the difference between crystal momenta of the ket and bra degrees of freedom of the density matrix.

By using the expression of the Kraus operator

\hat{K}_{m}=\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b}(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b},

(205)

the matrix representation
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
of the CPTP map is written as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}\otimes\hat{K}_{m}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j},\vec{i}^{\prime},\vec{j}^{\prime}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}(\hat{K}_{m})^{*}_{\vec{i}^{\prime},c;\vec{j}^{\prime},d}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}\otimes\ket{\vec{i}^{\prime},c}\bra{\vec{j}^{\prime},d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}\sum_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}t_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}\otimes\ket{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},c}\bra{\vec{j}+\vec{\nu},d},

(206)

where

t_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}:=\sum_{m}(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}(\hat{K}_{m})^{*}_{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},c;\vec{j}+\vec{\nu},d}.

(207)

For later convenience, we assume that components
\mu_{\lambda}
and
\nu_{\lambda}
in
\vec{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{d})
and
\vec{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{d})
take a value from
-L/2,-L/2+1,\cdots,L/2-1
[
-(L-1)/2,-(L-1)/2+1,\cdots,(L-1)/2
] for even (odd)
L
without loss of generality by using the periodic boundary condition (
20
).
The translational symmetry (
202
) exists if

t_{\vec{i}+\vec{e}_{\lambda},\vec{j}+\vec{e}_{\lambda}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}=t_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}},

(208)

where
\vec{e}_{\lambda}
is a unit vector defined by
(\vec{e}_{\lambda})_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\delta_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}
.
For notational simplicity, we introduce an auxiliary creation operator
\tilde{c}_{\vec{i},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}
as

\tilde{c}_{\vec{i},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}(\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\otimes\ket{\mathrm{vac}})=\ket{\vec{i},a}\otimes\ket{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},c},

(209)

where
\ket{\mathrm{vac}}
is the vacuum state of the system.
Then, the matrix representation of a translationally invariant CPTP map takes the form of a translationally invariant non-Hermitian tight-binding model as

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}\sum_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}t_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}\tilde{c}_{\vec{i},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}\tilde{c}_{\vec{j},b,d,\vec{\nu}}.

(210)

Here, we consider a single-particle system, although Eq. (
210
) is written like the second-quantized form.
We also note that the operator
\tilde{c}_{\vec{i},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}
has two additional indices
c
and
\vec{\mu}
compared with the original basis
\ket{\vec{i},a}
of the Hilbert space since
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
acts on the doubled Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. Physically, this point corresponds to the fact that the density matrix has bra and ket degrees of freedom.

Using the translational invariance, we obtain the momentum-space representation of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{k}}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{\vec{k}}^{\dagger}X(\vec{k})\tilde{\bm{c}}_{\vec{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}\sum_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k})\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},b,d,\vec{\nu}},

(211)

where
\tilde{\bm{c}}_{\vec{k}}=(\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}})_{a,c,\vec{\mu}}
is a column vector with

\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\tilde{c}_{\vec{j},a,c,\vec{\mu}}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

(212)

and

\displaystyle X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}t_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}e^{-i\vec{k}(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}\sum_{m}(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{j},a;\vec{j}^{\prime},b}(\hat{K}_{m})^{*}_{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu},c;\vec{j}^{\prime}+\vec{\nu},d}

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}.

(213)

The
N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}\times N_{\mathrm{cell}}D_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}
matrix
X(\vec{k})
is a counterpart of the Bloch Hamiltonian in band theory; therefore, we call it the Bloch matrix.

A.3
Mode expansion

Let us express the density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{f}}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}})
after feedback control in terms of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Bloch matrix
X(\vec{k})
. Let
v_{n}^{L}(\vec{k})
[
v_{n}^{R}(\vec{k})
] be a left (right) eigenvector of
X(\vec{k})
with eigenvalue
\xi_{n}(\vec{k})
. We assume that the Bloch matrix
X(\vec{k})
is diagonalizable and that the eigenvectors satisfy the biorthogonal condition
(v_{m}^{L}(\vec{k}),v_{n}^{R}(\vec{k}))=\delta_{m,n}
[here,
(u,v)
denotes the inner product of vectors
u
and
v
]
and the completeness relation
\sum_{n}v_{n}^{R}(\vec{k})(v_{n}^{L}(\vec{k}))^{\dagger}=1
. Then, the Bloch matrix is written as

\displaystyle X(\vec{k})=\sum_{n}\xi_{n}(\vec{k})v_{n}^{R}(\vec{k})(v_{n}^{L}(\vec{k}))^{\dagger},

(214)

the matrix element of which is given by

\displaystyle X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k})=\sum_{n}\xi_{n}(\vec{k})(v_{n}^{R}(\vec{k}))_{a,c,\vec{\mu}}(v_{n}^{L}(\vec{k}))^{*}_{b,d,\vec{\nu}}.

(215)

Substituting this result into Eq. (
211
), we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{n}\sum_{a,b,c,d}\sum_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}\xi_{n}(\vec{k})(v_{n}^{R}(\vec{k}))_{a,c,\vec{\mu}}(v_{n}^{L}(\vec{k}))^{*}_{b,d,\vec{\nu}}

\displaystyle\times\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},b,d,\vec{\nu}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{n}\xi_{n}(\vec{k})\bar{a}_{n}(\vec{k})a_{n}(\vec{k}),

(216)

where

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{n}(\vec{k}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{a,c}\sum_{\vec{\mu}}(v_{n}^{R}(\vec{k}))_{a,c,\vec{\mu}}\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger},

(217)

\displaystyle a_{n}(\vec{k}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{b,d}\sum_{\vec{\nu}}(v_{n}^{L}(\vec{k}))^{*}_{b,d,\vec{\nu}}\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},b,d,\vec{\nu}}.

(218)

From Eqs. (
209
) and (
212
), we find that the operator
\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}
creates a state

\tilde{c}_{\vec{k},a,c,\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}(\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\otimes\ket{\mathrm{vac}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}\ket{\vec{j},a}\otimes\ket{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu},c},

(219)

which is an eigenstate of
\hat{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\hat{T}_{\lambda}^{*}
with eigenvalue
e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{\lambda}}
. Thus, the right eigenvector of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
with eigenvalue
\xi_{n}(\vec{k})
is given by

\displaystyle\ket{\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}(\vec{k})}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\sum_{a,c}\sum_{\vec{\mu}}(v_{n}^{R}(\vec{k}))_{a,c,\vec{\mu}}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{j},a}\otimes\ket{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu},c},

(220)

and the corresponding left eigenvector is given by

\displaystyle\ket{\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L}(\vec{k})}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\sum_{b,d}\sum_{\vec{\nu}}(v_{n}^{L}(\vec{k}))_{b,d,\vec{\nu}}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{j},b}\otimes\ket{\vec{j}+\vec{\nu},d}.

(221)

Using the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism [see Eq. (
199
)], we obtain the right and left eigenoperators of the original CPTP map
\mathcal{E}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}(\vec{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\sum_{a,c}\sum_{\vec{\mu}}(v_{n}^{R}(\vec{k}))_{a,c,\vec{\mu}}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{j},a}\bra{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu},c},

(222)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L}(\vec{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\sum_{b,d}\sum_{\vec{\nu}}(v_{n}^{L}(\vec{k}))_{b,d,\vec{\nu}}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{j},b}\bra{\vec{j}+\vec{\nu},d},

(223)

respectively. In terms of the eigensystem of the CPTP map, the density matrix
\hat{\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}
after feedback control is expanded as [see Eq. (
14
)]

\displaystyle\hat{\rho_{\mathrm{f}}}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{n}\xi_{n}(\vec{k})\mathrm{Tr}[(\hat{\rho}_{n}^{L}(\vec{k}))^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}]\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}(\vec{k}).

(224)

Note that
\langle\hat{\rho}_{m}^{L}(\vec{k}),\hat{\rho}_{n}^{R}(\vec{k})\rangle=\delta_{mn}
.

A.4
Fundamental constraints on the Bloch matrix

Since a quantum channel must satisfy the complete positivity and the trace preservation, the Bloch matrix
X(\vec{k})
cannot be a general non-Hermitian matrix. We note that a superoperator satisfies the complete positivity if it has the Kraus representation (
5
) (see e.g., Ref.
Wilde 2013
).
The Kraus representation implies Hermiticity preservation, which leads to an antiunitary symmetry (
62
) of a quantum channel (see Sec.
V.2
). Using Eq. (
210
), the right-hand side of the symmetry condition (
62
) is written as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}\sum_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}t_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}\otimes\ket{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},c}\bra{\vec{j}+\vec{\nu},d},

(225)

and the left-hand side of Eq. (
62
) is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{J}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\tilde{J}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}\sum_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}(t_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}})^{*}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},c}\bra{\vec{j}+\vec{\nu},d}\otimes\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}\sum_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}(t_{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},\vec{j}+\vec{\nu}}^{c,a,-\vec{\mu};d,b,-\vec{\nu}})^{*}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{i},a}\bra{\vec{j},b}\otimes\ket{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},c}\bra{\vec{j}+\vec{\nu},d}.

(226)

Thus, the symmetry (
62
) holds if

(t_{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},\vec{j}+\vec{\nu}}^{c,a,-\vec{\mu};d,b,-\vec{\nu}})^{*}=t_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}.

(227)

We can check Eq. (
227
) directly from the definition (
207
) as

\displaystyle(t_{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},\vec{j}+\vec{\nu}}^{c,a,-\vec{\mu},d,b,-\vec{\nu}})^{*}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}(\hat{K}_{m})^{*}_{\vec{i}+\vec{\mu},c;\vec{j}+\vec{\nu},d}(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}.

(228)

In terms of the Bloch matrix, the relation (
227
) leads to

\displaystyle X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}t_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}^{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}(t_{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu},\vec{j}^{\prime}+\vec{\nu}}^{c,a,-\vec{\mu};d,b,-\vec{\nu}})^{*}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{l},\vec{l}^{\prime}}(t_{\vec{l},\vec{l}^{\prime}}^{c,a,-\vec{\mu};d,b,-\vec{\nu}})^{*}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{l}-\vec{\mu}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{l}^{\prime}-\vec{\nu}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{\mu}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{\nu}})}[X_{c,a,-\vec{\mu};d,b,-\vec{\nu}}(-\vec{k})]^{*},

(229)

where we use
\vec{R}_{\vec{l}-\vec{\mu}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{l}^{\prime}-\vec{\nu}}=\vec{R}_{\vec{l}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{l}^{\prime}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{\mu}}+\vec{R}_{\vec{\nu}}
in deriving the last equality.

Next, we consider the condition for trace preservation. The superoperator preserves the trace of the density matrix if

\sum_{m}\hat{K}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{m}=\hat{I}.

(230)

The condition (
230
) for the trace preservation is rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{i}}\sum_{a}\sum_{m}(\hat{K}_{m})^{*}_{\vec{i},a;\vec{j},b}(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{i},a;\vec{l},d}=\delta_{\vec{j},\vec{l}}\delta_{b,d},

(231)

and thus the hopping amplitudes and the Bloch matrix satisfy

\sum_{\vec{i}}\sum_{a}t_{\vec{i},\vec{j}}^{a,a,\vec{0};b,d,\vec{\nu}}=\delta_{\vec{\nu},\vec{0}}\delta_{b,d}

(232)

and

\sum_{a}X_{a,a,\vec{0};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k}=0)=\delta_{\vec{\nu},\vec{0}}\delta_{b,d},

(233)

respectively.
Equations (
229
) and (
233
) place fundamental constraints on the Bloch matrix.

Appendix B
Feedback control with projective measurement

Here we consider quantum feedback control with a projective measurement, where the measurement operators are given by
\hat{M}_{m}=\ket{\vec{j},a}\bra{\vec{j},a}
and a measurement outcome is denoted by
m=(\vec{j},a)
. In this case, the matrix elements of the Kraus operator
\hat{K}_{m}=\hat{U}_{m}\hat{M}_{m}
are written as

(\hat{K}_{m})_{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1};\vec{j}_{2},a_{2}}=(\hat{U}_{m})_{\vec{j}_{1},a_{1};\vec{j},a}\delta_{\vec{j}_{2},\vec{j}}\delta_{a_{2},a}.

(234)

Thus, the momentum-space representation of the CPTP map is given by

\displaystyle X_{a,c,\vec{\mu};b,d,\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{b,d}\delta_{\vec{\nu},\vec{0}}}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}^{\prime},b})_{\vec{j},a;\vec{j}^{\prime},b}(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}^{\prime},b})^{*}_{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu},c;\vec{j}^{\prime},b}

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})},

(235)

which indicates that the Bloch matrix can have nonzero matrix elements only in
D_{\mathrm{loc}}
columns. Therefore, nonzero eigenvalues of the Bloch matrix coincide with those of a
D_{\mathrm{loc}}\times D_{\mathrm{loc}}
matrix
X_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\vec{k}):=(X_{a,a,\vec{0};b,b,\vec{0}}(\vec{k}))
, which simplifies the spectral analysis of the CPTP map.

In particular, if a system has no internal degrees of freedom (i.e.,
D_{\mathrm{loc}}=1
), the analysis is further simplified. In this case, we can omit the indices for internal degrees of freedom, and the matrix elements of the Bloch matrix are written as

\displaystyle X_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k}):=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{\vec{\nu},\vec{0}}}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})_{\vec{j};\vec{j}^{\prime}}(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})^{*}_{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu};\vec{j}^{\prime}}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}.

(236)

Since the Bloch matrix has nonzero matrix elements only in one column, its eigenvalues
\xi_{\vec{\mu}}(\vec{k})
are given by its diagonal entries as

\displaystyle\xi_{\vec{0}}(\vec{k})=

\displaystyle X_{\vec{0},\vec{0}}(\vec{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}|(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}|^{2}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}p(\vec{j}|\vec{j}^{\prime})e^{-ik(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})},

(237)

\displaystyle\xi_{\vec{\mu}}(\vec{k})=

\displaystyle 0\ (\vec{\mu}\neq\vec{0}),

(238)

where

p(\vec{j}|\vec{j}^{\prime}):=|(\hat{U}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})_{\vec{j},\vec{j}^{\prime}}|^{2}

(239)

is the probability of finding a particle at site
\vec{j}
after feedback control with measurement outcome
m=\vec{j}^{\prime}
. Because of the translational invariance of the CPTP map, this probability should be a function of
\vec{r}=\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}}
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\xi_{\vec{0}}(\vec{k})=\sum_{\vec{r}}p(\vec{r})e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}},

(240)

where
p(\vec{r})=p(\vec{R}_{j}-\vec{R}_{j^{\prime}}):=p(\vec{j}|\vec{j}^{\prime})
. This result indicates that the nonzero eigenvalue of the CPTP map can be obtained from the characteristic function of the probability distribution
\{p(\vec{r})\}
of the displacement of a particle during feedback control. We note that
\xi_{\vec{0}}(\vec{k}=0)=\sum_{\vec{r}}p(\vec{r})=1
, which is consistent with the trace-preserving condition (
233
).

The Bloch matrix for the
D_{\mathrm{loc}}=1
case is diagonalizable if
X_{\vec{0},\vec{0}}(\vec{k})\neq 0
. If
X_{\vec{0},\vec{0}}(\vec{k})=0
, it cannot be diagonalized except for the trivial case with
X(\vec{k})=0
. Thus, the point at which
X_{\vec{0},\vec{0}}(\vec{k})=\xi_{\vec{0}}(\vec{k})=0
corresponds to an exceptional (nondiagonalizable) point
Ashida et al. 2020
. Specifically, if
X_{\vec{0},\vec{0}}(\vec{k})\neq 0
, the left eigenvector
v_{\vec{\nu}}^{L}(\vec{k})
and the right eigenvector
v_{\vec{\nu}}^{R}(\vec{k})
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\xi_{\vec{\nu}}(\vec{k})
are given by

\displaystyle(v_{\vec{0}}^{L}(\vec{k}))_{\vec{\mu}}=

\displaystyle\delta_{\vec{\mu},\vec{0}},

(241)

\displaystyle(v_{\vec{0}}^{R}(\vec{k}))_{\vec{\mu}}=

\displaystyle X_{\vec{\mu},\vec{0}}(\vec{k})/X_{\vec{0},\vec{0}}(\vec{k}),

(242)

\displaystyle(v_{\vec{\nu}}^{L}(\vec{k}))_{\vec{\mu}}=

\displaystyle-\delta_{\vec{\mu},\vec{0}}X_{\vec{\nu},\vec{0}}^{*}(\vec{k})/X_{\vec{0},\vec{0}}^{*}(\vec{k})+\delta_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}\ (\vec{\nu}\neq\vec{0}),

(243)

\displaystyle(v_{\vec{\nu}}^{R}(\vec{k}))_{\vec{\mu}}=

\displaystyle\delta_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}\ (\vec{\nu}\neq\vec{0}).

(244)

These eigenvectors satisfy the biorthogonal condition
(v_{\vec{\mu}}^{L}(\vec{k}),v_{\vec{\nu}}^{R}(\vec{k}))=\delta_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}
.
Substituting Eqs. (
241
)-(
244
) into the expansions (
222
)-(
224
), we obtain the density matrix after feedback control as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\sum_{\vec{\mu}}\xi_{0}(\vec{k})c(\vec{k})e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}(v_{\vec{0}}^{R}(\vec{k}))_{\vec{\mu}}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{j}}\bra{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\xi_{0}(\vec{k})c(\vec{k})e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}\ket{\vec{j}}\bra{\vec{j}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\sum_{\vec{\mu}\neq 0}X_{\vec{\mu},0}(\vec{k})c(\vec{k})e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

\displaystyle\times\ket{\vec{j}}\bra{\vec{j}+\vec{\mu}},

(245)

where the expansion coefficients

\displaystyle c(\vec{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\bra{\vec{j}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\ket{\vec{j}}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}p_{\vec{j}}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

(246)

are determined by the probability distribution
p_{\vec{j}}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{M}_{\vec{j}}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{\vec{j}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}]
of measurement outcomes.
The probability distribution of the position after feedback control is given by

\displaystyle\bra{\vec{j}}\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{i}})\ket{\vec{j}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}}\sum_{\vec{k}}\xi_{0}(\vec{k})c(\vec{k})e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{cell}}}\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{\vec{j}^{\prime}}\xi_{0}(\vec{k})p_{\vec{j}^{\prime}}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{R}_{\vec{j}}-\vec{R}_{\vec{j}^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{j}^{\prime}}p(\vec{j}|\vec{j}^{\prime})p_{\vec{j}^{\prime}}.

(247)

Appendix C
Eigenvalues of a quantum channel for projective measurement

Here, we show that eigenvalues of a quantum channel for a projective measurement are either zero or one. We consider a quantum channel

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proj}}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{m}\hat{P}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{m},

(248)

in which the Kraus operators are given by projective operators, i.e.,
\hat{P}_{m}^{\dagger}=\hat{P}_{m}
and
\hat{P}_{m}\hat{P}_{n}=\delta_{m,n}\hat{P}_{m}
.
The projective measurement channel (
248
) is positive semidefinite since

\displaystyle\langle\hat{A},\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proj}}(\hat{A})\rangle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{A}^{\dagger}\sum_{m}\hat{P}_{m}\hat{A}\hat{P}_{m}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{P}_{m}^{2}\hat{A}\hat{P}_{m}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{P}_{m}\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{P}_{m}\hat{P}_{m}\hat{A}\hat{P}_{m}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\langle\hat{P}_{m}\hat{A}\hat{P}_{m},\hat{P}_{m}\hat{A}\hat{P}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0

(249)

for an arbitrary operator
\hat{A}
. Thus, eigenvalues of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proj}}
are real and non-negative. These eigenvalues must be either zero or one since the measurement channel satisfies the idempotency condition:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{2}_{\mathrm{proj}}(\hat{\rho})=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\sum_{m^{\prime}}\hat{P}_{m}\hat{P}_{m^{\prime}}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{m^{\prime}}\hat{P}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\sum_{m^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\hat{P}_{m}\hat{\rho}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\hat{P}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\hat{P}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{proj}}(\hat{\rho}).

(250)

This completes the proof.

Appendix D
Correspondence between symmetry of a time-evolution operator and that of its generator

Suppose that a time-evolution operator has a generator. To investigate the symmetry of a time-evolution operator, it is helpful to consider the symmetry of its generator. Here, we summarize the correspondence between symmetry of a time-evolution operator and that of its generator to compare the former with the symmetry of quantum channels discussed in Sec.
V.3
.

In closed quantum systems, a time-evolution operator
\hat{U}=e^{-i\hat{H}t}
is generated by a Hermitian Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
. The Altland-Zirnbauer symmetries of a Hermitian Hamiltonian are written as

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{C}\hat{H}^{*}\hat{V}_{C}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\hat{H},\ \hat{V}_{C}\hat{V}_{C}^{*}=\pm\hat{I},

(251a)

\displaystyle-\hat{V}_{K}\hat{H}^{*}\hat{V}_{K}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\hat{H},\ \hat{V}_{K}\hat{V}_{K}^{*}=\pm\hat{I},

(251b)

\displaystyle-\hat{\Gamma}\hat{H}\hat{\Gamma}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\hat{H},\ \hat{\Gamma}^{2}=\hat{I},

(251c)

where
\hat{V}_{C},\hat{V}_{K},\hat{\Gamma}
are unitary operators
Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
. The corresponding symmetries of a unitary time-evolution operator derived from these symmetries of a Hamiltonian are written as

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{C}\hat{U}^{T}\hat{V}_{C}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\hat{U},\ \hat{V}_{C}\hat{V}_{C}^{*}=\pm\hat{I},

(252a)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{K}\hat{U}^{*}\hat{V}_{K}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\hat{U},\ \hat{V}_{K}\hat{V}_{K}^{*}=\pm\hat{I},

(252b)

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\Gamma}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\hat{U},\ \hat{\Gamma}^{2}=\hat{I}.

(252c)

A CPTP map of unitary time evolution is given by
\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger}
, which is mapped to a unitary operator
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=\hat{U}\otimes\hat{U}^{*}
acting on the doubled Hilbert space. It follows from Eqs. (
252a
)-(
252c
) that the corresponding symmetries of the CPTP map are given by

\displaystyle(\hat{V}_{C}\otimes\hat{V}_{C}^{*})\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{T}(\hat{V}_{C}\otimes\hat{V}_{C}^{*})^{-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}

(253a)

with
(\hat{V}_{C}\otimes\hat{V}_{C}^{*})(\hat{V}_{C}\otimes\hat{V}_{C}^{*})^{*}=+\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I}
,

\displaystyle(\hat{V}_{K}\otimes\hat{V}_{K}^{*})\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\hat{V}_{K}\otimes\hat{V}_{K}^{*})^{-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}

(253b)

with
(\hat{V}_{K}\otimes\hat{V}_{K}^{*})(\hat{V}_{K}\otimes\hat{V}_{K}^{*})^{*}=+\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I}
, and

\displaystyle(\hat{\Gamma}\otimes\hat{\Gamma}^{*})\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(\hat{\Gamma}\otimes\hat{\Gamma}^{*})^{-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}

(253c)

with
(\hat{\Gamma}\otimes\hat{\Gamma}^{*})^{2}=\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I}
.

Thus, these symmetries of unitary channels constitute a subset of symmetry classes with
\eta_{C}=\eta_{K}=+1
in Eqs. (
65a
)-(
65c
).

In Markovian open quantum systems, the generator (Lindbladian)
\mathcal{L}
of a CPTP map
\mathcal{E}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}
for time evolution features the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad form
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
. The matrix representation of a CPTP map through the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism (see Appendix
A.1
) is denoted by
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=e^{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}t}
. Here, we note that a Lindbladian cannot be a general non-Hermitian matrix since the real part of its eigenvalues must be nonpositive. Because of this constraint, only the following set of Bernard-LeClair symmetries are possible for a Lindbladian
Lieu et al. 2020
(see also Refs.
Kawabata et al. 2023
;
Sá et al. 2023
):

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{T}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}^{*}=\pm\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(254a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}^{*}=\pm\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(254b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}^{2}=\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(254c)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C},\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K},\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}
are unitary operators acting on the doubled Hilbert space.
The corresponding symmetries of a CPTP map are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{T}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{C}^{*}=\pm\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(255a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{K}^{*}=\pm\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I},

(255b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\ \tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}^{2}=\hat{I}\otimes\hat{I}.

(255c)

The symmetries (
255a
)-(
255c
) coincide with those of quantum channels in Eqs. (
65a
)-(
65c
).

Appendix E
Symmetry-preserving boundary condition for a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
demon

In this appendix, we describe how to implement a symmetry-preserving boundary condition for the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
demon in Sec.
VII.4
. The transition probability amplitudes for a feedback control in class AII
†
must satisfy Eqs. (
111a
)-(
111c
). Here we perform a feedback control near the boundary of the system to preserve these symmetry conditions. We consider a 1D lattice with
L
unit cells under the OBC, and the site
j=1
(
j=L
) denotes the left (right) boundary. Feedback control under the PBC satisfies the conditions (
111a
)-(
111c
). However, if we perform the same feedback under the OBC, the reflection of a particle at the boundary violates these conditions. A feedback protocol that preserves the symmetry at the boundary is given as follows. Let
m_{1}=(j_{1},a_{1})
be a measurement outcome of the first measurement. If
j_{1}=1
or
j_{1}=L
, we do nothing (i.e., the feedback Hamiltonian is set to zero:
\hat{H}_{1,a_{1}}=\hat{H}_{L,a_{1}}=0
). The transition amplitudes from the boundary sites therefore vanish.
If
j_{1}\neq 1,L
, we perform feedback control with the same Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{j_{1},a_{1}}
as that for the PBC case described in Sec.
VII.4
. Next, we perform the second projective measurement and obtain an outcome
m_{2}=(j_{2},a_{2})
. If
j_{2}=2,\cdots,L-1
, we perform the same additional feedback control as the PBC case shown in Eq. (
155
). If
j_{2}=1
or
j_{2}=L
, we perform a feedback operation with the unitary operator

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{\prime\prime}=\hat{S}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\exp[-i\theta_{m_{1},m_{2}}\hat{S}^{z}],

(256)

where

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{m_{1},m_{2}}=\sum_{\sigma}(\ket{j_{1},a_{1},\sigma}\bra{j_{2},a_{2},\sigma}+\ket{j_{2},a_{2},\sigma}\bra{j_{1},a_{1},\sigma})

(257)

is a swap operator. This unitary operation returns a particle back to the site before the first feedback control, thereby making the conditions (
111a
)-(
111c
) satisfied for the boundary sites. The phase factor
\theta_{m_{1},m_{2}}
is chosen so that the condition (
111a
) is satisfied for
j_{2}=j_{1}
.

Specifically, for the model in Sec.
VII.4
, the symmetry is preserved if the phase factors satisfy

\displaystyle p(1,A|2,A)e^{i\theta_{(2,A),(1,A)}}+p(1,B|2,A)e^{i\theta_{(2,A),(1,B)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(1,A|2,B)e^{i\theta_{(2,B),(1,A)}}+p(1,B|2,B)e^{i\theta_{(2,B),(1,B)}}

(258)

and

\displaystyle p(L,A|L-1,A)e^{i\theta_{(L-1,A),(L,A)}}

\displaystyle+p(L,B|L-1,A)e^{i\theta_{(L-1,A),(L,B)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(L,A|L-1,B)e^{i\theta_{(L-1,B),(L,A)}}

\displaystyle+p(L,B|L-1,B)e^{i\theta_{(L-1,B),(L,B)}},

(259)

where

p(j_{2},a_{2}|j_{1},a_{1})=|(\hat{U}_{j_{1},a_{1}})_{j_{2},a_{2},\sigma;j_{1},a_{1},\sigma}|^{2}.

(260)

The existence of a solution of Eqs. (
258
) and (
259
) depends on the details of the transition probability (
260
). For example, if

\displaystyle p(1,A|2,B)=p(1,B|2,A)

(261)

and

\displaystyle 2p(1,A|2,B)\geq|p(1,B|2,B)-p(1,A|2,A)|

(262)

are satisfied, a solution of Eq. (

\displaystyle\theta_{(2,A),(1,A)}=

\displaystyle\theta_{(2,B),(1,B)}=0,

(263)

\displaystyle\theta_{(2,A),(1,B)}=

\displaystyle\arccos\frac{p(1,B|2,B)-p(1,A|2,A)}{2p(1,A|2,B)},

(264)

\displaystyle\theta_{(2,B),(1,A)}=

\displaystyle\pi-\theta_{(2,A),(1,B)}.

(265)

A solution of Eq. (

Appendix F
Topological feedback control that shows the non-Hermitian skin effect but with no boundary-localized steady state

The models of topological feedback control presented in Secs.
IV
,
VII.1
-
VII.4
, and
IX
show the non-Hermitian skin effects and boundary-localized steady states due to nontrivial point-gap topology of quantum channels. While the former is widely observed in non-Hermitian topological phases
Gong et al. 2018a
;
Okuma et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Borgnia et al. 2020
;
Okuma and Sato 2023
;
Lin et al. 2023
, the latter does not exist in conventional non-Hermitian systems and thus is unique to topological quantum channels that preserve the positivity and trace of a density matrix (see Sec.
VIII.1
). Here, to distinguish these two topological phenomena, we present a model that shows the non-Hermitian skin effect but with no boundary-localized steady states. We consider a two-level atom on a 1D lattice with
L
sites. The internal states of the atom consist of a ground state (
g
) and an excited state (
e
). We perform a projective measurement of the position and the internal state of the atom described by the measurement operator
\hat{M}_{j,a}=\ket{j,a}\bra{j,a}
, where
\ket{j,a}
denotes a quantum state of the atom at site
j=1,\cdots,L
and internal state
a=g,e
. Next, depending on the measurement outcome
m=(j,a)
, we let the atom undergo time evolution under the Lindblad equation

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{m}(\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i[\hat{H}_{j,a},\hat{\rho}]+\sum_{i}\Bigl(\hat{L}_{i}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{i},\hat{\rho}\}\Bigr),

(266)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{j,a}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,a^{\prime}}J_{a^{\prime}}(\ket{i,a^{\prime}}\bra{i+1,a^{\prime}}+\ket{i+1,a^{\prime}}\bra{i,a^{\prime}})

\displaystyle+\delta_{a,e}V\ket{j-1,e}\bra{j-1,e}

(267)

is the feedback Hamiltonian with hopping amplitudes
J_{e},J_{g}\in\mathbb{R}
and potential height
V>0
, and
\hat{L}_{i}=\sqrt{\gamma}\ket{i,g}\bra{i,e}
is a Lindblad operator that describes spontaneous decay with rate
\gamma>0
from the excited state to the ground state. In other words, we impose a potential wall only if the internal state of the atom is detected to be the excited state. See Fig.
23
for a schematic illustration. The quantum channel for feedback control is thus given by (see also Sec.
IX
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{m}e^{\mathcal{L}_{m}\tau}(\hat{M}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{M}_{m}^{\dagger}),

(268)

where
\tau
is the duration of feedback control.

Figure
24
shows the eigenspectrum of the quantum channel (
268
). Here, a salient feature is that the band in the PBC spectrum that involves the steady state (the unit eigenvalue) does not form a loop and thus has a vanishing winding number. Physically, this feature occurs because the atom is in the internal ground state in the steady state and thus is not localized near the edges of the system. Meanwhile, the band located away from the steady state exhibits a loop structure. This topological structure leads to the non-Hermitian skin effect of an atom in the internal excited state, which has a finite lifetime due to spontaneous decay. Thus, the non-Hermitian skin effect in topological feedback control is not always accompanied by a boundary-localized steady state.
