Generating series for torsion-free bundles over singular curves: rationality, duality and modularity
Yifeng Huang, Ruofan Jiang

Abstract
. We consider two motivic generating functions defined on a variety, and reveal their tight connection. They essentially count torsion-free bundles and zero-dimensional sheaves. On a reduced singular curve, we show that the “Quot zeta function” encoding the motives of Quot schemes of 0-dimension quotients of a rank d d torsion-free bundle satisfies rationality and a Serre-duality-type functional equation. This is a high-rank generalization of known results on the motivic Hilbert zeta function (the d = 1 d=1 case) due to Bejleri, Ranganathan, and Vakil (rationality) and Yun (functional equation). Moreover, a stronger rationality result holds in the relative Grothendieck ring of varieties. Our methods involve a novel sublattice parametrization, a certain generalization of affine Grassmannians, and harmonic analysis over local fields. On a general variety, we prove that the “Cohen–Lenstra zeta function” encoding the motives of stacks of zero-dimensional sheaves can be obtained as a “rank → ∞ \to\infty ” limit of the Quot zeta functions. Combining these techniques, we prove explicit results for the planar singularities associated to the ( 2 , n ) (2,n) torus knots/links, namely, y 2 = x n y^{2}=x^{n} . The resulting formulas involve summations of Hall polynomials over partitions bounded by a box or a vertical strip, which are of independent interest from the perspectives of skew Cauchy identities for Hall–Littlewood polynomials, Rogers–Ramanujan identities, and modular forms.

When

The following lemma relates the global and local Quot zeta functions. Recall the notation in (

Lemma 2.1

Suppose

Z_{{\mathcal{E}}}(t)=Z_{U}(t)Z_{U}(\mathbb{L}t)\dots Z_{U}(\mathbb{L}^{d-1}t)\prod_{i=1}^{r}Z_{E_{i}}^{R_{i}}(t).

Proof.

For

4.
Parametrizing sublattices

The crux of the proof of Theorems

,
1.8

and clarify its connection with

.

Assume
R
satisfies
(*)
, recall the notation therein, and let

be the total fraction ring of

-lattice

such that

. For

-lattice in

is also called an

-lattice of rank

. When

is a finite field, the lattice zeta function of an

is defined by

\zeta^{R}_{M}(z)=\zeta_{M}(z):=\sum_{L\subseteq_{R}M}\lvert M/L\rvert^{-z},

where the sum extends over all
R
-sublattices of

. It is closely related to the Quot zeta function of

as an

. In this sense, even when

is infinite, the Quot zeta function of an

can also be called the

of

If

is a torsion-free module over

of rank

that is isomorphic to

and considering the embedding

depends only on the isomorphism class of

The lattice zeta function has been intensively studied

,

,

,

and is of interest in number theory. The usual method is to divide the set of sublattices of

into isomorphism classes, and express the contribution of each isomorphism class as an integral on

, see §

16
]
, even for simple examples such as the

5.3.
Constructible geometry

We set up the language for studying geometry up to stratifications. Recall that for a scheme
X
, a Zariski stratification (or simply, stratification) is a morphism
\breve{X}=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathbf{A}}X_{\alpha}\rightarrow X
that is bijective on field valued points such that
\mathbf{A}
is a finite set, and each
X_{\alpha}\rightarrow X
is a locally closed subscheme. A stratification is a categorical monomorphism in
\mathbf{Sch}
, a composition of stratifications is a stratification, and moreover, the pullback of a stratification
\breve{X}\rightarrow X
along a map
Y\rightarrow X
is a stratification of
Y
. These facts show that the set of stratifications satisfy the conditions RMS1
\sim
RMS3 of
[
56
, 04VC]
. Therefore, we can localize at the set of stratifications, and obtain a category where stratifications are isomorphisms, cf.
[
56
, 04VH]
. The resulting category is called the
category of constructible schemes
, denoted
\mathbf{CSch}
.
If
Z
is a scheme, the slice category
\mathbf{CSch}_{Z}:=(\mathbf{CSch}\downarrow Z)
is called the
category of constructible
Z
-schemes
.

A morphism in
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{CSch}}(X,Y)
will be called a
constructible morphism
and written as
f:X\xrightarrow{c}Y
. It is represented by a roof
X\leftarrow\breve{X}\xrightarrow{\breve{f}}Y
, where
\breve{X}\rightarrow X
is a stratification and
\breve{f}
is a morphism of schemes. When the context is clear, we will just write the roof as
\breve{f}:\breve{X}\rightarrow Y
. If we represent
f:X\xrightarrow{c}Y

resp.
g:Y\xrightarrow{c}Z
as
\breve{f}:\breve{X}\rightarrow Y

resp.
\breve{g}:\breve{Y}\rightarrow Z
, then the composition of
g\circ f
can be represented as
\breve{g}\circ p_{\breve{Y}}:\breve{X}\times_{Y}\breve{Y}\rightarrow Z
, where
p_{\breve{Y}}
is the projection
\breve{X}\times_{Y}\breve{Y}\rightarrow\breve{Y}
, which is the base change of
\breve{f}
along
\breve{Y}\rightarrow Y
. Two representations
\breve{X}\rightarrow Y
and
\breve{X}^{\prime}\rightarrow Y
are called equivalent, if they represent the same constructible morphism. More concretely, they are equivalent if they become the same after passing to a common refinement.

Remark 5.18

(Field valued points)
.

Let
F
be a field. There is a functor
\mathbf{Sch}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
by taking
F
-points. Since stratifications induces bijections on
F
-points, we can localize and obtain a functor
\mathbf{CSch}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
. This is called “taking field valued points of constructible schemes”. As a variant, we have a functor
\mathbf{CSch}\rightarrow\mathbf{Set}
sending a constructible scheme to its underlying set.

Remark 5.19

(Constructible subschemes)
.

A constructible morphism
f:X\xrightarrow{c}Y
in

\mathbf{CSch}
is called a
constructible subscheme

resp.
constructible immersion
, if we can represent it as a morphism
\breve{f}:\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathbf{A}}X_{\alpha}\rightarrow Y
, such that each
\breve{f}|_{X_{\alpha}}:X_{\alpha}\rightarrow Y
is a locally closed subscheme
resp.
immersion. When
f
is a constructible subscheme, we will also write
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\subseteq}}Y
.

If
X_{i}\xrightarrow{c}Y,i=1,2
are constructible subschemes such that
X_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\subseteq}}X_{2}
and
X_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\subseteq}}X_{1}
, we say that
X_{1}
is
constructibly identical
to
X_{2}
(as constructible subschemes of
Y
). In this case, we will write
X_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{=}}X_{2}
. For example, any stratification
\breve{X}\rightarrow X
is constructibly identical to
X
. Note that a constructible identity is necessarily a constructible isomorphism and is an equivalence relation. If
F
is a field, we will naturally identify the
F
-points of an
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\subseteq}}Y
as a subset of the
F
-points of
Y
. Then constructibly identical constructible subschemes of
Y
have the same
F
-points. Furthermore, their underlying sets are identical (as subsets of the underlying set
|Y|
of
Y
).

Conversely, it is easy to see that a constructible subscheme of
Y
is determined by its underlying set up to constructible identity. Moreover, a subset of
|Y|
which is a finite disjoint union of locally closed subspaces of
|Y|
(i.e., a constructible subset, at least when
Y
is Noetherian) can be upgraded to a constructible subscheme. Say, if
S
is a locally closed topological subspace which is the intersection of a closed set
F
and an open set

\sum_{r=0}^{d}\frac{\mathbb{L}^{d-r}}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{d-r}}c_{r}(t)=b_{d}(t)\in t^{d}{K_{0}(\mathrm{Stck}_{k})}[[t]].

From the elementary identity

(6.7)

\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{d-r}}-\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{d-r-1}}=\frac{\mathbb{L}^{d-r}}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{d-r}},\quad 0\leq r\leq d-1,

it follows that

(6.8)

b_{d}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{d}\frac{\mathbb{L}^{d-r}}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{d-r}}c_{r}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{d}\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{d-r}}a_{r}(t).

Recall that the topology on
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})[[\mathbb{L}^{-1}]][[t]]
is
t
-coefficientwise. We now show that each coefficient of
a_{d}(t)
converges in
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})[[\mathbb{L}^{-1}]]
. Fix

, and let

denote the

-coefficient of

. Since

, Equation (
6.8
) implies the following recurrence relation for

:

(6.9)

a_{d,N}=-\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{d-r}}a_{r,N}.

By setting
a_{d,N}=0
for

, we may rewrite (
6.9
) into an

matrix form

(6.10)

v_{d}=Mv_{d-1},\quad d\geq N+1,

where

(6.11)

M=-\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{1}}&\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{2}}&\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{3}}&\cdots\\
0&\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{1}}&\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{2}}&\cdots\\
0&0&\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{1}}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},\quad v_{d}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{d,N}\\
a_{d-1,N}\\
a_{d-2,N}\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}.

(Note that every entry in the matrix multiplication is just a finite sum.) It follows that

for all

. Observing that every entry of

has

at most
-1
in

in the sense of the dimension filtration, it follows that every entry of

has dimension at most
-(d-N)
. Therefore, every entry of

converges to

. In particular,
\lim_{d\to\infty}a_{d,N}=0
, so that
\lim_{d\to\infty}a_{d}(t)=0
.

By definition, the topology on
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})[[\mathbb{L}^{-1}]][[t]]
is nonarchimedean in the sense that for any sequence

, the sum
\sum_{d}x_{d}(t)
converges if and only if
\lim_{d\to\infty}x_{d}(t)=0
, and in this case any reordering of the sum converges to the same limit. Therefore in our case, the sum

converges. By the definitions of
c_{d}(t)
and
a_{d}(t)
, we have

(6.12)

\lim_{d\to\infty}Z_{R^{d}}(\mathbb{L}^{-d}t)=(\mathbb{L}^{-1};\mathbb{L}^{-1})_{\infty}\lim_{d\to\infty}c_{d}(t)=(\mathbb{L}^{-1};\mathbb{L}^{-1})_{\infty}c(t)\in K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})[[\mathbb{L}^{-1},t]],

proving the first assertion of Theorem
1.12
.

Finally, (
6.8
) and the definition of

imply

(6.13)

\widehat{Z}_{R}(t)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\sum_{r=0}^{d}\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{d-r}}a_{r}(t).

By
\lim_{d\to\infty}a_{d}(t)=0
and the nonarchimedean property, we may switch the summations:

(6.14)

\widehat{Z}_{R}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}a_{r}(t)\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\mathbb{L};\mathbb{L})_{l}}=c(t)(\mathbb{L}^{-1};\mathbb{L}^{-1})_{\infty}.

Comparing with (
6.12
), the last assertion of Theorem
1.12
then follows.
∎

6.3.7.
The proof

We say that a unipotent group scheme over a base

Lemma 6.9
.

Notation as in §
6.3.4
. There is a Zariski stratification

-split unipotent group scheme.

Proof.

Note that it suffices to show that

(b)

x_{i}y_{i}\in R^{\vee}
, so that
\tr_{K/Q}(D^{-1}x_{i}y_{i})\in Z
. Since
\chi
is trivial on
Z
, we have
\int_{x_{i}\in L^{\vee}}\chi(\tr_{K/Q}(D^{-1}x_{i}y_{i}))dx_{i}=\int_{x_{i}\in L^{\vee}}1\,dx_{i}=\mu(L^{\vee})
if
y_{i}\in L
.

It remains to show that
\int_{x_{i}\in L^{\vee}}\chi(\tr_{K/Q}(D^{-1}x_{i}y_{i}))dx_{i}=0
if
y_{i}\notin L
. We claim that

(7.16)

x_{i}\mapsto\chi(\tr_{K/Q}(D^{-1}x_{i}y_{i}))

is a nontrivial character on
L^{\vee}
; if this claim is proved, then the proof is complete because the integral of a nontrivial character is necessarily zero. We proceed by contradiction. Assume that
\chi(\tr_{K/Q}(D^{-1}x_{i}y_{i}))
is a trivial character on
x_{i}\in L^{\vee}
. Then the fractional ideal of
Z
in
Q
given by
\tr_{K/Q}(D^{-1}L^{\vee}y_{i})
is in the kernel of
\chi
. From our construction of
\chi
, we must have
\tr_{K/Q}(D^{-1}L^{\vee}y_{i})\subseteq Z
. Since
L^{\vee}y_{i}
is a fractional ideal of
R
in
K
, any
\alpha\in L^{\vee}y_{i}
satisfies
D^{-1}\alpha R\subseteq D^{-1}L^{\vee}y_{i}
, so that
\tr_{K/Q}(D^{-1}\alpha R)\subseteq Z
. By the characterization of
R^{\vee}
as a subset of
K
, we have
\alpha\in R^{\vee}
. It follows that
L^{\vee}y_{i}\in R^{\vee}
. By (
7.15
), we have
y_{i}\in L
, a contradiction.
∎

Before we prove Theorem
7.2
, we need one more observation.

Lemma 7.6
.

For any
R
-lattice
L
in
K^{d\times 1}
, we have
\mu^{\times}(\Aut L)=\mu^{\times}(\Aut((L^{\vee})^{T}))
.

Proof.

We claim that
\Aut((L^{\vee})^{T})=(\Aut L)^{T}
. If the claim is proved, since the transpose isomorphism
A^{\times}\to A^{\times}
preserves the measure
\mu^{\times}
, the lemma follows.

Noting that
\Hom_{R}(L,L)
is a subalgebra of
A
and
\Aut L=\Hom_{R}(L,L)^{\times}
, it suffices to prove that
\Hom_{R}((L^{\vee})^{T},(L^{\vee})^{T})=\Hom_{R}(L,L)^{T}
. Equivalently, we claim
x\in\Mat_{d}(K)
satisfies
xL\subseteq L
if and only if
x^{T}(L^{\vee})^{T}\subseteq(L^{\vee})^{T}
, which is itself equivalent to
L^{\vee}x\subseteq L^{\vee}
.

We recall from the definition of
L^{\vee}
and the double dual property that

(7.17)

L^{\vee}=\{u\in K^{1\times d}:uL\subseteq R^{\vee}\},\quad L=\{v\in K^{d\times 1}:L^{\vee}v\subseteq R^{\vee}\}.

It follows from the second equality that
xL\subseteq L
if and only if
L^{\vee}xL\subseteq R^{\vee}
, and it follows from the first equality that
L^{\vee}x\subseteq L^{\vee}
if and only if
L^{\vee}xL\subseteq R^{\vee}
. This proves the claim.
∎

7.3.
Proofs of Theorem
7.2
and Theorem
1.7

We now prove Theorem
7.2
and Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

Let
M=(R^{\vee})^{d\times 1}
and fix an
R
-lattice
L
in
V=K^{d\times 1}
. We remark that even though the zeta function
Z_{M}(t;L)
depends only on the isomorphism classes of
M,L
as
R
-modules, we need to fix the embeddings
M,L\subseteq V
throughout our proof. Consider the equation (
7.8
) with
\Phi=\mathbf{1}_{\Hom_{R}(L,M)}
and
\Psi=\mathbf{1}_{\Mat_{d}(\widetilde{R})}
, and substitute
z
with
z/d
. The strategy of the proof is to express both sides of the equation above in terms of the restricted lattice zeta function using (
7.9
) and (
7.10
).

We start with the left-hand side. By (
7.9
), we have

(7.18)

Z(\Phi;z/d)=\mu^{\times}(\Aut L)(M:L)^{z}\zeta_{M}(z;L).

Note that the normalization of every measure and the Fourier transform involved in (
7.9
) depends on
K,Q,\widetilde{R}
but not on
R
. In particular, we may apply (
7.9
) with
R=\widetilde{R}
and
L=M=\widetilde{R}^{d}
. It follows that

(7.19)

Z(\Psi;z/d)=\mu^{\times}(\GL_{d}(\widetilde{R}))\zeta^{\widetilde{R}}_{\widetilde{R}^{d}}(z;\widetilde{R}^{d}),

where the notation
\zeta^{\widetilde{R}}_{\widetilde{R}^{d}}
indicates that the lattice zeta function is with respect to the ground ring
\widetilde{R}
. Since every
\widetilde{R}
-lattice is isomorphic to
\widetilde{R}^{d}
, the function
\zeta^{\widetilde{R}}_{\widetilde{R}^{d}}(z;\widetilde{R}^{d})
is simply
\zeta^{\widetilde{R}}_{\widetilde{R}^{d}}(z)
, which by Solomon’s formula equals to
1/(q^{-z};q)_{d}^{s}
. Substituted into the above equation, it follows from the definition of
\mathit{NZ}_{M}(t;L)
that

(7.20)

\frac{Z(\Phi;z/d)}{Z(\Psi;z/d)}=\frac{\mu^{\times}(\Aut L)}{\mu^{\times}(\GL_{d}(\widetilde{R}))}(M:L)^{z}\mathit{NZ}_{M}(q^{-z};L).

We now consider the right-hand side of (
7.8
) with
z\mapsto z/d
. By (
7.10
), we have

(7.21)

Z(\widehat{\Phi};1-z/d)=\mu(L^{\vee})^{d}Z(\mathbf{1}_{L^{1\times d}};1-z/d).

By the definition of the zeta integral and the fact that matrix transposition
x\mapsto x^{T}
preserves the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{A}
and the measure
d^{\times}x
, we have

(7.22)

Z(\widehat{\Phi};1-z/d)=\mu(L^{\vee})^{d}Z(\mathbf{1}_{(L^{T})^{d\times 1}};1-z/d).

By the double dual property, we have
(L^{T})^{d\times 1}=\Hom_{R}((L^{\vee})^{T},M)
. It then follows from (
7.9
) that

(7.23)

Z(\widehat{\Phi};1-z/d)=\mu(L^{\vee})^{d}\mu^{\times}(\Aut((L^{\vee})^{T}))(M:(L^{\vee})^{T})^{d-z}\zeta_{M}(d-z;(L^{\vee})^{T}).

To compute
Z(\widehat{\Psi};1-z/d)
, we again consider
\Psi
as a special case of
\Phi
with
R=\widetilde{R}
and
L=M=\widetilde{R}^{d}
. Substituting this into the above equation and using the self-dualness of
\widetilde{R}
, the normalization
\mu(\widetilde{R})=1
and Solomons’ formula, we have

(7.24)

Z(\widehat{\Psi};1-z/d)=\mu^{\times}(\GL_{d}(\widetilde{R}))(q^{-(d-z)};q)_{d}^{-s}.

Combining the above equations and using the definition of
\mathit{NZ}_{M}(t;(L^{\vee})^{T})
, we have

(7.25)

\frac{Z(\widehat{\Phi};1-z/d)}{Z(\widehat{\Psi};1-z/d)}=\frac{\mu^{\times}(\Aut((L^{\vee})^{T}))}{\mu^{\times}(\GL_{d}(\widetilde{R}))}\mu(L^{\vee})^{d}(M:(L^{\vee})^{T})^{d-z}\mathit{NZ}_{M}(q^{-(d-z)};(L^{\vee})^{T}).

By Theorem
7.8
, the formulas (
7.20
) and (
7.25
) are equal. Equating them, applying Lemma
7.6
, removing the common factor
\mu^{\times}(\GL_{d}(\widetilde{R}))
, simplifying using the general property
(L_{1}:L_{2})=\mu(L_{1})/\mu(L_{2})
, and noting that
\mu(L^{\vee})=\mu((L^{\vee})^{T})
, we get

(7.26)

\frac{\mathit{NZ}_{M}(q^{-z};L)}{\mathit{NZ}_{M}(q^{-(d-z)};(L^{\vee})^{T})}=\mu(L)^{z}\mu(L^{\vee})^{z}\mu(M)^{d-2z}.

As the final step, we simplify the right-hand side of (
7.26
) using properties of duals. Recall the general property
(L_{1}^{\vee}:L_{2}^{\vee})=(L_{2}:L_{1})
. Substituting
L_{1}=L,L_{2}=(\widetilde{R})^{d\times 1}
and using the self-dualness
(\widetilde{R}^{d\times 1})^{\vee}=\widetilde{R}^{1\times d}
, the general property
(L_{1}:L_{2})=\mu(L_{1})/\mu(L_{2})
and the normalization
\mu(\widetilde{R})=1
, we have
\mu(L)\mu(L^{\vee})=1
. Using
M=(R^{\vee})^{d\times 1}
, we similarly have

(7.27)

\mu(M)=\mu(R^{\vee})^{d}=(R^{\vee}:\widetilde{R})^{d}=(\widetilde{R}:R)^{d}=q^{\delta d},

where we recall that
\delta=[\widetilde{R}:R]
is the Serre invariant. We thus get

(7.28)

\frac{\mathit{NZ}_{M}(q^{-z};L)}{\mathit{NZ}_{M}(q^{-(d-z)};(L^{\vee})^{T})}=q^{(d^{2}-2dz)\delta}.

Setting
t=q^{-z}
completes the proof of Theorem
7.2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Let
S
denote the set of isomorphism classes of

-lattices in

. Note that

In this section, we apply Proposition

8.1.
Boundary
R
-lattices

Define the set of
boundary
R
-lattices
for

to be

(8.1)

\partial_{\undertilde{M}}(M):=\{\undertilde{M}\subseteq L\subseteq_{R}M:\widetilde{R}L\cap M=L\}.

We note that lattices in
\partial_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
necessarily lie in

, unlike the lattices in
{\widetilde{\partial}}_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
; this turns out to be a convenient feature later. Nevertheless,
\partial_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
is in a canonical bijection with
{\widetilde{\partial}}_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
.

Lemma 8.1
.

The map
L\mapsto\widetilde{R}L
gives a bijection
\partial_{\undertilde{M}}(M)\to{\widetilde{\partial}}_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
with inverse
\widetilde{L}\mapsto\widetilde{L}\cap M
.

Proof.

If
L\in\partial_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
, then
\widetilde{R}L\in{\widetilde{\partial}}_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
because
L\in E_{R}(\widetilde{R}L;M)
. Therefore,
L\mapsto\widetilde{R}L
is well-defined. Conversely, if
\widetilde{L}\in{\widetilde{\partial}}_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
, assume
N\in E_{R}(\widetilde{L};M)
. Then we have
N\subseteq\widetilde{L}\cap M=:L
and
\widetilde{R}N=\widetilde{L}
. It follows that
\widetilde{R}L=\widetilde{L}
, so that
L\in\partial_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
. As a result,
\widetilde{L}\mapsto\widetilde{L}\cap M
is also well-defined.

It is clear that both maps are inverse to each other, finishing the proof.
∎

The alternative recipe to parametrize
R
-sublattices of
M
is as follows:

•

Choose
L_{b}\in\partial_{\undertilde{M}}(M)
;

•

Choose
\widetilde{N}
using the data at the right-hand side of (
4.5
) with
\widetilde{L}:=\widetilde{R}L_{b}
, and independently choose

.

When

, we get the following version of (

):

(8.2)

\lvert\mathit{NZ}^{R}_{M}(t)\rvert_{q}=\sum_{L_{b}\in\partial_{\undertilde{M}}(M)}\left\lparen\prod_{i=1}^{s}(t;q)_{\rk_{i}(\widetilde{R}L_{b}/\undertilde{M})}\right\rparen\sum_{L\in E_{R}(\widetilde{R}L_{b};L_{b})}t^{[L_{b}:L]}.

8.2.
Structure of
{R^{(2,n)}}

Fix

and a field

. Recall
{R^{(2,2m+1)}}:=k[[X,Y]]/(Y^{2}-X^{2m+1})

•

For

For

We crucially note that in both cases,

8.3.
Explicit parametrization of extension fibers

We now explicitly parametrize

for an arbitrary
\widetilde{R}
-lattice
\widetilde{L}
if

or
{R^{(2,2m)}}

and

Our general strategy is to reduce the above problem to a special case of Lemma

with

and

free (so the set

\widetilde{R}=R+TR,\text{ if }R={R^{(2,2m+1)}}

and

\widetilde{R}=R+e_{1}R=R+e_{2}R=e_{1}R+e_{2}R,\text{ if }R={R^{(2,2m)}}.

As a result, analogous equations hold if
\widetilde{R}
is replaced by

and

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
R={R^{(2,2m)}}
, and
\widetilde{L}
be an
\widetilde{R}
-lattice. Then an
R
-lattice
L\subseteq\widetilde{L}
satisfies
\widetilde{R}L=\widetilde{L}
if and only if
L+e_{i}\widetilde{L}=\widetilde{L}
for
i=1,2
.

Proof.

We note that the following decompositions are actually direct sums:

(8.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}L

\displaystyle=e_{1}L\oplus e_{2}L,

(8.8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}

\displaystyle=e_{1}\widetilde{L}\oplus e_{2}\widetilde{L}.

Hence,
\widetilde{R}L=\widetilde{L}
if and only if
e_{i}L=e_{i}\widetilde{L}
for

. Since multiplication by
e_{i}
is precisely the projection map from
\widetilde{L}
to
e_{i}\widetilde{L}
with respect to the decomposition (

), the condition
e_{i}L=e_{i}\widetilde{L}
is equivalent to

, where
(i,j)=(1,2)
or
(2,1)
.
∎

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
R={R^{(2,2m+1)}}
, and
\widetilde{L}
be an
\widetilde{R}
-lattice. Fix an
R
-lattice
F_{\widetilde{L}}\subseteq\widetilde{L}
such that
\widetilde{R}F_{\widetilde{L}}=\widetilde{L}
. Then an
R
-lattice
L\subseteq\widetilde{L}
satisfies
\widetilde{R}L=\widetilde{L}
if and only if
L+T\cdot F_{\widetilde{L}}=\widetilde{L}
.

Proof.

Assume
L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}=\widetilde{L}
. To prove
L+TL=\widetilde{L}
, since
T^{n}F_{\widetilde{L}}\subseteq L
for
n\gg 0
, it suffices to show that
\widetilde{L}=L+TL+T^{n}F_{\widetilde{L}}
for all
n\geq 1
. We prove it by induction on
n
. The base case
n=1
follows from the assumption. For the induction step, assume
n\geq 1
and
\widetilde{L}=L+TL+T^{n}F_{\widetilde{L}}
, then we have

(8.9)

\widetilde{L}=L+TL+T^{n}F_{\widetilde{L}}\subseteq L+TL+T^{n}\widetilde{L}=L+TL+T^{n}(L+TF_{\widetilde{L}})=L+TL+T^{n+1}F_{\widetilde{L}},

where the last equality follows from that
T^{n}L\subseteq L
if
n
is even and
T^{n}L\subseteq TL
if
n
is odd. (To see this, recall that
T^{2}\in R
and
L
is an
R
-lattice.)

Conversely, assume
L+TL=\widetilde{L}
. Then we have

(8.10)

\widetilde{L}=L+TL\subseteq L+T\widetilde{L}=L+T(F_{\widetilde{L}}+TF_{\widetilde{L}})=L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}+T^{2}F_{\widetilde{L}}.

Again, to prove
\widetilde{L}=L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}
, it suffices to prove that
\widetilde{L}=L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}+T^{2^{n}}F_{\widetilde{L}}
for
n\geq 1
. We have just proved the base case
n=1
. To proceed with induction, assume
n\geq 1
and
\widetilde{L}=L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}+T^{2^{n}}F_{\widetilde{L}}
, then we have

(8.11)

\begin{aligned} \widetilde{L}&=L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}+T^{2^{n}}F_{\widetilde{L}}\subseteq L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}+T^{2^{n}}\widetilde{L}\\
&=L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}+T^{2^{n}}(L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}+T^{2^{n}}F_{\widetilde{L}})=(L+T^{2^{n}}L)+(TF_{\widetilde{L}}+T^{2^{n}+1}F_{\widetilde{L}})+T^{2^{n+1}}F_{\widetilde{L}}\\
&=L+TF_{\widetilde{L}}+T^{2^{n+1}}F_{\widetilde{L}}.\end{aligned}\qed

From now on, if
R={R^{(2,2m+1)}}
, we fix a
free

R
-sublattice

of
\widetilde{L}
such that
\widetilde{R}F_{\widetilde{L}}=\widetilde{L}
. For instance, one may let

be the

-lattice generated by

, where

is an
\widetilde{R}
-basis of
\widetilde{L}
. As is foreshadowed in Lemma

.

We introduce some notation needed in our parametrization for

.

.

Define

,

Proposition 8.8

Assume the notation above. If

8.5.
The motivic version

To finish the proof of Theorems
1.8
and
1.10
, we need to show that parametrizations described above are motivic, i.e., they correspond to stratifications and fibrations. We just focus on the geometrizing Proposition
8.15
; the rest is similar.

The issue arises in padding (Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
), where we make a noncanonical choice for a new object for each previously chosen object that varies in a continuum. Thanks to Remark
5.40
, we can ignore this problem, i.e., it suffices to show that the family
\mathcal{Y}:=\{(L,L_{b}):L\in E_{R}(\widetilde{R}L_{b};L_{b}),L_{b}\in\partial_{{\mathfrak{c}}F}(F)\}
has rational motive (i.e., the motive is in
{\mathbb{Z}}[\mathbb{L}]
). Here the family depends on relative indices
[F:L_{b}]
and
[L_{b}:L]
that we implicitly fix.

Recall that we stratify the space of
L_{b}
’s according to the type
\lambda
of
F/L_{b}
. Denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
the locus of
L_{b}
with a fixed
\lambda
. It clearly has rational motive. Recall that we parametrize each
(L,L_{b})
by a triple
(L_{b},W_{L}^{\prime},\varphi_{L})
, notation as in Lemma
8.10
, where the space of possible
(W_{L}^{\prime},\varphi_{L})
’s depends (through
V_{1}
,
V_{2}
, and
W_{L_{b}}^{\prime}
) on
F_{b}
, a free
R
-lattice such that
\widetilde{R}F_{b}=\widetilde{L}_{b}
that we have to fix noncanonically in advance. But since
F_{b}
can be obtained as the
R
-linear span of any basis of
\widetilde{R}L_{b}
, there is a stratification
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda,i}
of
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
and a “coherent choice”
F_{b}
in family for each
L_{b}\in\mathcal{B}_{\lambda,i}
. Using these, we can build a stratification
\mathcal{Y}=\bigsqcup_{\lambda,\mu,i}\mathcal{Y}_{\lambda,\mu,i}
where
\mathcal{Y}_{\lambda,\mu,i}
consists of elements
(L_{b},W_{L}^{\prime},\varphi_{L})
of
\mathcal{Y}
such that
L_{b}\in\mathcal{B}_{\lambda,i}
and
W_{L}^{\prime}
has type
\mu
. One can easily identify
\mathcal{Y}_{\lambda,\mu,i}
with a trivial bundle over
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda,i}
whose fibers are isomorphic to the space of solutions to Lemma
8.10
as if
W_{L_{b}}^{\prime}
were a fixed
k[[X]]
-module of type
\mu
. The fiber clearly depends only on
\lambda,\mu
and has rational motive. Denote the fiber by
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\mu}
. Then

(8.22)

[\mathcal{Y}]=\sum_{\lambda,\mu,i}[\mathcal{B}_{\lambda,i}]\,[\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\mu}]=\sum_{\lambda,\mu}[\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}]\,[\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,\mu}],

which has rational motive. This completes the proof of Theorems
1.8
(up to Lemma
9.3
) and
1.10
.
\hfill\square

9.1.
Basic hypergeometric series

Our main tool to prove formal identities is the basic hypergeometric series (or
q
-hypergeometric series) and its transformations. We first recall the definition.

Definition 9.1
.

For
r,s\geq 0
, define

(9.1)

{}_{r}\phi_{s}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\\
b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\end{matrix};q,z\right]:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\lparen(-1)^{k}q^{\binom{k}{2}}\right\rparen^{s+1-r}\frac{(a_{1};q)_{k}\dots(a_{r};q)_{k}}{(q;q)_{k}(b_{1};q)_{k}\dots(b_{s};q)_{k}}z^{k}.

To apply the method of
q
-hypergeometric series, the first step is to convert the expression in question into a
q
-hypergeometric series. This often involves converting a
q
-Pochhammer symbol such as
(a;q)_{n\pm k}
,
(aq^{k};q)_{n}
, and
(a;q^{-1})_{k}
into the form
(A;q)_{k}
, where
A
does not depend on
k
. The process is straightforward once the goal is clear. Some useful identities are

(9.2)

\displaystyle(a;q)_{n-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(a;q)_{n}}{(aq^{n-1};q^{-1})_{k}},

(9.3)

\displaystyle(a;q)_{k}

\displaystyle=(-a)^{k}q^{\binom{k}{2}}(a^{-1};q^{-1})_{k}.

The second step is to apply transformation identities to the
q
-hypergeometric series we obtain. We will refer to
[
22
]
for standard identities. We state one here.

Lemma 9.2

(Cauchy,
[
22
, (II.5)]
)
.

As formal series in
a,z,q
, we have

(9.4)

{}_{1}\phi_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}a\\
az\end{matrix};q,z\right]:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}q^{\binom{k}{2}}\frac{(a;q)_{k}}{(q;q)_{k}(az;q)_{k}}z^{k}=\frac{(z;q)_{\infty}}{(az;q)_{\infty}}.

9.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.8

To simplify (
8.20
), we need the following identity, which appears to be new. We thank S. Ole Warnaar for sketching its proof.

Lemma 9.3
.

For
d,m\geq 0
and any partition
\mu\subseteq(m^{d})
, we have the following identity in
{\mathbb{Z}}[t,q]
:

(9.5)

\sum_{\lambda:\;\mu\subseteq\lambda\subseteq(m^{d})}g^{(m^{d})}_{\lambda}(q)\,g^{\lambda}_{\mu}(q)\,t^{\lvert\lambda\rvert}(t;q)_{d-\lambda_{m}^{\prime}}=g^{(m^{d})}_{\mu}(q)\,t^{\lvert\mu\rvert}.

Proof.

We denote
(q^{-1})_{n}:=(q^{-1};q^{-1})_{n}
to save space. Applying Theorem
3.4
and dividing both sides by
q^{-\sum_{i\geq 1}\mu_{i}^{\prime 2}}(q^{-1})_{d}
, the original identity is equivalent to

(9.6)

\sum_{\lambda:\;\mu\subseteq\lambda\subseteq(m^{d})}q^{-\sum_{i\geq 1}\lambda_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-\mu_{i}^{\prime})}\frac{(t;q)_{d-\lambda_{m}^{\prime}}(q^{d}t)^{\lvert\lambda\rvert}}{\prod_{i\geq 0}(q^{-1})_{\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-\lambda_{i+1}^{\prime}}}\prod_{i\geq 0}{\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-\mu_{i+1}^{\prime}\brack\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-\mu_{i}^{\prime}}_{q^{-1}}=\frac{(q^{d}t)^{\lvert\mu\rvert}}{\prod_{i\geq 0}(q^{-1})_{\mu_{i}^{\prime}-\mu_{i+1}^{\prime}}},

where
\lambda_{0}^{\prime}=\mu_{0}^{\prime}:=d
.

The strategy to prove (
9.6
) is as follows. We denote the left-hand side of (
9.6
) by
f_{\mu,m,d}(t,q)
. We first fix
\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}
and sum over
\lambda_{m}^{\prime}
from
\mu_{m}^{\prime}
to
\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}
. It turns out that the sum can be evaluated by Lemma
9.4
. We now sum the result over
\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}
with
d\geq\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}
and
\lambda_{i}^{\prime}\geq\mu_{i}^{\prime}
for
1\leq i\leq m-1
. It then turns out that this sum is a multiple of
f_{\overline{\mu},m-1,d}(t,q)
, where
\overline{\mu}
is the partition with
\overline{\mu}^{\prime}_{i}=\mu^{\prime}_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq m-1
and
\overline{\mu}^{\prime}_{m}=0
. The identity (
9.6
) then follows from induction on
m
.

We now carry out the computation in detail. If
m=0
, the identity reduces to
1=1
. Now assume
m\geq 1
. By extracting the factors that do not depend on
\lambda_{m}^{\prime}
, and substituting
k=\lambda_{m}^{\prime}-\mu_{m}^{\prime}
, the left-hand side of (
9.6
) equals

(9.7)

\begin{multlined}\frac{(q^{d}t)^{\mu^{\prime}_{m}}}{(q^{-1})_{\mu_{m}^{\prime}}}\sum_{\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\dots\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}}q^{-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\lambda_{i}^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-\mu_{i}^{\prime})}\frac{(q^{d}t)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\lambda_{i}^{\prime}}}{\prod_{i=0}^{m-2}(q^{-1})_{\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-\lambda_{i+1}^{\prime}}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}{\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-\mu_{i+1}^{\prime}\brack\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-\mu_{i}^{\prime}}_{q^{-1}}\\
\sum_{k=0}^{\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}-\mu_{m}^{\prime}}q^{-k(k+\mu_{m}^{\prime})}\frac{(t;q)_{d-\mu_{m}^{\prime}-k}(q^{d}t)^{k}}{(q^{-1})_{\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}-\mu_{m}^{\prime}-k}(q^{-1})_{k}}.\end{multlined}

We reorganize the inner sum of (
9.7
) into a
q^{-1}
-hypergeometric series as follows

(9.8)

\frac{(t;q)_{d-\mu_{m}^{\prime}}}{(q^{-1})_{\lambda^{\prime}_{m-1}-\mu_{m}^{\prime}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\lambda^{\prime}_{m-1}-\mu_{m}^{\prime}}(-1)^{k}q^{-\binom{k}{2}}\frac{(q^{\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}-\mu_{m}^{\prime}};q^{-1})_{k}}{(q^{-1})_{k}(q^{d-1-\mu_{m}^{\prime}}t;q^{-1})_{k}}(q^{d-1-\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}}t)^{k}.

Note that the upper bound of the sum of (
9.8
) may be replaced by
\infty
, since
(q^{\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}-\mu_{m}^{\prime}};q^{-1})_{k}=0
for
k>\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}-\mu_{m}^{\prime}
. Applying Lemma
9.4
with
a=q^{\lambda_{m-1}^{\prime}-\mu_{m}^{\prime}},z=q^{d-1-\mu_{m}^{\prime}}t
and reorganizing, we get

(9.9)

f_{\mu,m,d}(t,q)=(q^{d}t)^{\mu_{m}^{\prime}}q^{-\mu_{m}^{\prime 2}}{\mu_{m-1}^{\prime}\brack\mu_{m}^{\prime}}_{q^{-1}}f_{\overline{\mu},m-1,d}(t,q).

The desired identity then follows from induction on
m
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Remark 9.4
.

The identity (

) can be viewed as a bounded analogue of the skew Cauchy identity for Hall–Littlewood polynomials; we thank S. Ole Warnaar for pointing it out. Indeed, the

) reduces to the following identity

(t;z)_{d}\sum_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}(1,z,\dots,z^{d-1};z)\,Q^{\prime}_{\lambda/\nu}(t;z)=P_{\mu}(1,z,\dots,z^{d-1};z)

where

and

is the modified skew Hall–Littlewood polynomial (see e.g.
[

). This in turn follows from the skew Cauchy identity

\sum_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}(\underline{x};z)\,Q^{\prime}_{\lambda/\mu}(\underline{y};z)=P_{\mu}(\underline{x};z)\prod_{i,j}\frac{1}{1-x_{i}y_{j}}

by setting
\underline{x}=(1,z,\dots,z^{d-1})
and
\underline{y}=t
. Thus in some sense, we can view such a summation identity over partitions bounded by
(\infty^{d})
as a (skew) Cauchy type identity, and view the
(m^{d})
counterpart as its bounded analogue (while the
(m^{\infty})
counterpart compares to Rogers–Ramanujan identities in
[
33
]
).

Proposition 9.7
.

Assume the setting above, and let
R
be a complete local

-algebra of finite type with residue field

be any finitely generated module over

(9.17)

Proof.

We use the theorem of Białynicki-Birula
[
5
]
. Consider the natural action of the torus
(k^{\times})^{d}
on
E^{\oplus d}
, which induces an action on
\Quot_{E^{\oplus d},n}
for any
n\geq 0
. Pick a generic one-dimensional subtorus

in
(k^{\times})^{d}
that contains an element
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
with

distinct. We now determine the
T
-fixed points of
\Quot_{E^{\oplus d},n}
.

Let

, i.e.,

is an
R
-submodule of
k
-codimension

. For
1\leq i\leq d
, consider the coordinate projection
\pi_{i}:E^{\oplus d}\to E^{\oplus d}
by
\pi_{i}(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})=(0,\dots,f_{i},\dots,0)
. Define

. We claim that

is fixed by

if and only if

.

The “if” direction is trivial. For the “only if” direction, let

be an arbitrary element of

. Since

is fixed by
T
, we have
\lambda^{j}f=(\lambda_{i}^{j}f_{i})_{i}\in F
for

. Since

are distinct, the Vandermonde matrix

is invertible, so for each

, we can express
\pi_{i}(f)
as a

. It follows that

, and thus

.

As a result, by setting

, we have a locally closed decomposition

(\Quot_{E^{\oplus d},n})^{T}=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\dots,n_{d}\geq 0\\
n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}=n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\Quot_{E,n_{i}}.

By
[

]
, we have

. The desired result thus follows.
