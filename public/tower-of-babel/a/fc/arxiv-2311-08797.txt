Equivariant linear isometries operads over Abelian groups
Ethan MacBrough

Abstract
. N ∞ N_{\infty} -operads are an equivariant generalization of E ∞ E_{\infty} -operads introduced by Blumberg and Hill to study structural problems in equivariant stable homotopy theory. In the original paper introducing these objects, Blumberg and Hill raised the question of classifying N ∞ N_{\infty} -operads that are weakly equivalent to a particularly nice kind of N ∞ N_{\infty} -operad called a linear isometries operad. For some groups there is a known classification of linear isometries operads up to weak equivalence in terms of certain combinatorially defined objects called saturated transfer systems, but this classification is known to be invalid in general. Various authors have made incremental progress on understanding the domain of validity for this classification, but even among cyclic groups the validity is unknown in general. We determine the essentially all the finite Abelian groups for which the classification is valid using techniques from algebra and extremal combinatorics.

Introduction

Commutative ring spectra are objects representing (co)homology theories with a (graded-)commutative multiplication operation such as the cup product on singular cohomology. The basic datum of a commutative ring spectrum is a spectrum

Starting around the 1970s, homotopy theorists began realizing that generic commutative ring spectra can be quite pathological, and to prove stronger results one needs to keep track of the explicit homotopies realizing the axioms, as well as the homotopies between those homotopies, etc. This complicated homotopy data is most conveniently packaged into an operad, so that a structured ring spectrum becomes simply an algebra in spectra over some operad.

In the field of brave new algebra, the operadic approach to commutative ring spectra is mostly important since (1) algebras over an operad have excellent formal properties, enabling one to define powerful algebraic constructions such as localization, and (2) for most commutative ring spectra coming from nature, it’s easy to identify a nice operad acting on it. There’s also a less common but occasionally quite powerful application of operadic methods in the study of ring spectra, which might be called the “intertwining method”. Often the ring spectra we want to study have very complicated geometry, but admit the action of a rather concrete “model” operad whose geometry is relatively easy to understand, such as the little disks or linear isometries operads. The monadic action of the operad will then partially intertwine the simple geometry of the operad with the subtle geometry of the spectrum, and we can sometimes take advantage of this to extract non-trivial information about ring spectra. 2.
Technical machinery

Let
G
be a finite group. Recall that a
G
-universe is a representation
U
of
G
over
\mathbb{R}
such that every isotypic component of
U
is either trivial or has countably infinite dimension, and the isotypic component corresponding to the trivial character is non-trivial (i.e.,
U
contains a non-zero vector fixed by the action of
G
). Technically speaking a
G
-universe should also include the data of a
G
-invariant inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, but since
G
is finite every representation of
G
can be equipped with a
G
-invariant inner product and any two invariant inner products on a representation are connected by an equivariant isometry, so we can leave the inner product structure implicit.

Any
G
-universe
U
gives rise to a linear isometries operad
\mathcal{L}(U)
; the construction of
\mathcal{L}(U)
is explained in Blumberg and Hill
[
3
]
, but we don’t need to know this construction for the present work. All we need to know is that the transfer system
\Tr(U)
corresponding to
\mathcal{L}(U)
is defined by

\Tr(U)=\{K\to H:\Ind_{K}^{H}(\Res_{K}^{G}(U))\hookrightarrow\Res_{H}^{G}(U)\};

see Theorem 4.18 of
[
3
]
and Section 5.1 of
[
15
]
. Here
\Res
and
\Ind
denote the standard restriction and induction functors on group representations.

The existence of non-trivial automorphisms makes
G
-universes somewhat annoying to work with directly. Let
\widehat{G}_{\mathbb{R}}
be the set of isomorphism classes of irreducible real
G
-representations. Then we have a bijection between isomorphism classes of
G
-universes and subsets of
\widehat{G}_{\mathbb{R}}
containing the trivial representation
[\mathbb{R}]\in\widehat{G}_{\mathbb{R}}
. Indeed, given
T\subseteq\widehat{G}_{\mathbb{R}}
with
[\mathbb{R}]\in T
, we can construct a
G
-universe
U_{T}
by

U_{T}:=\bigoplus_{[V]\in T}V^{\infty}.

Conversely, given a
G
-universe
U
we can define
T_{U}\subseteq\widehat{G}_{\mathbb{R}}
to be the set of
[V]\in\widehat{G}_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
V\hookrightarrow U
. These operations form a mutually inverse pair of bijections. Subsets of
\widehat{G}_{\mathbb{R}}
are a bit more concrete than
G
-universes, but we can go a bit further following the underlying ideas from Section 5.2 of
[
15
]
. Indeed, complexification defines a bijection from isomorphism classes of irreducible real representations to
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-orbits of irreducible complex representations (see Section 5 of
[
5
]
). Thus we can instead represent
G
-universes as subsets
S\subseteq\widehat{G}
such that
[\mathbb{C}]\in S
and
S
is closed under the action of
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
.

Given a
G
-universe
U
, we let
S_{U}\subseteq\widehat{G}
be the corresponding subset. Tracing definitions, we can describe
S_{U}
more concretely as

S_{U}=\{[V]\in\widehat{G}:V\hookrightarrow U\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\}.

From this description and the definition of a universe it follows that given two
G
-universes
U,U^{\prime}
, there exists an embedding
U\hookrightarrow U^{\prime}
if and only if
S_{U}\subseteq S_{U^{\prime}}
.

We let
V_{G}=\mathcal{P}(\widehat{G})
be the lattice of subsets of
\widehat{G}
, considered as an equivariant lattice under the natural action of
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
. We also define the sublattice

\mathcal{U}_{G}\cong\mathcal{P}\left((\widehat{G}\setminus[\mathbb{C}])/\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})\right),

consisting of all subsets
S\in V_{G}
which contain
[\mathbb{C}]
and are closed under the action of
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
; i.e., the subsets that correspond to universes under the above equivalence. The above isomorphism shows that
\mathcal{U}_{G}
is itself a Boolean lattice.

Given a subgroup inclusion
H\leqslant G
we can define join-semilattice homomorphisms
I_{H}^{G}\colon V_{H}\to V_{G}
and
R_{H}^{G}\colon V_{G}\to V_{H}
as follows. Given
[V]\in\widehat{G}
, we define

R_{H}^{G}(\{[V]\})=R_{H}^{G}([V]):=\{[W]\in\widehat{H}:W\hookrightarrow V|_{H}\}.

Similarly, given
[W]\in\widehat{H}
we can define

I_{H}^{G}(\{[W]\})=I_{H}^{G}([W]):=\{[V]\in\widehat{G}:V\hookrightarrow W^{G}\}.

We then define
R_{H}^{G}
and
I_{H}^{G}
on arbitrary inputs as the unique join-preserving extensions, i.e. for instance

R_{H}^{G}(S)=\bigcup_{[V]\in S}R_{H}^{G}([V]).

These maps are both
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-equivariant and preserve membership in
\mathcal{U}
. Since induction and restriction both commute with complexification, it follows easily that under the equivalence between
G
-universes and elements of
\mathcal{U}_{G}
,
I_{H}^{G}\colon\mathcal{U}_{H}\to\mathcal{U}_{G}
corresponds to ordinary induction of representations and
R_{H}^{G}\colon\mathcal{U}_{G}\to\mathcal{U}_{H}
corresponds to restriction. We will typically write
R_{H}^{G}(\chi)
to mean
R_{H}^{G}(\{\chi\})
and similarly for
I_{H}^{G}(\chi)
.

Note that if
G
is Abelian then
I_{H}^{G}
is in fact a lattice homomorphism; indeed, by Frobenius reciprocity, given
S\in V_{H}
and
[V]\in\widehat{G}
, we have
[V]\in I_{H}^{G}(S)
if and only if
V|_{H}\hookrightarrow W
for some
[W]\in S
. But
G
Abelian implies
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(W)=1
, so
V|_{H}\hookrightarrow W
is equivalent to
[V|_{H}]=[W]
. Thus
I_{H}^{G}(S)
is the preimage of
S
under the restriction map
\widehat{G}\to\widehat{H}
. Since preimage maps always commute with all set operations, the claim follows. Note however that
R_{H}^{G}
is generally not a lattice homomorphism; for arbitrary
G
,
R_{H}^{G}
commutes with unions by construction, but
R_{H}^{G}
rarely commutes with intersections.

Now given some
U\in V_{G}
, we can define the transfer system

\Tr(U):=\{K\leqslant H:I_{K}^{H}R_{K}^{G}(U)\subseteq R_{H}^{G}(U)\}.

If
U\in\mathcal{U}_{G}
, then this is the same as the transfer system of the linear isometries operad for the
G
-universe corresponding to
U
. Thus we’ve reduced to the following problem:

Problem 2.1
.

Given a finite group
G
and a saturated transfer system
\mathcal{R}
on
G
, does there exist some
U\in\mathcal{U}_{G}
such that
\Tr(U)=\mathcal{R}
?

The definition of
\Tr(U)
suggests investigating the “diagram”
D_{U}(H):=R_{H}^{G}(U)
, since then we have

\Tr(U)=\Tr(D_{U}):=\{K\leqslant H:I_{K}^{H}(D_{U}(K))\subseteq D_{U}(H)\}.

Formally, a diagram
D
is just an assignment for each
H\leqslant G
of some
D(H)\in V_{H}
. Splitting the passage
U\mapsto\Tr(U)
into a two-step process
U\mapsto D_{U}\mapsto\Tr(D_{U})
is convenient since the passage
D\mapsto\Tr(D)
is much better-behaved than
U\mapsto\Tr(U)
. Indeed, since
I_{K}^{H}
is a lattice homomorphism it’s easily seen that given two diagrams
D,D^{\prime}
we have

\Tr(D\cap D^{\prime})\supseteq\Tr(D)\cap\Tr(D^{\prime}).

Thus given any saturated transfer system
\mathcal{R}
and a diagram
D
, there’s a unique minimal extension
\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I}\supseteq D
such that
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\Tr(\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I})
; in fact,
\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I}
can be constructed explicitly via the formula

\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I}(H)=\bigcup_{K\to H\in\mathcal{R}}I_{K}^{H}(D(K)).

Note that given
U,U^{\prime}\in V_{G}
, we do not necessarily have
D_{U\cap U^{\prime}}\supseteq D_{U}\cap D_{U^{\prime}}
since
R_{H}^{G}
does not commute with intersections. This is the root cause for the “laughably poor properties” of
U\mapsto\Tr(U)
(quoted from
[
15
]
).

One might reasonably hope that for suitably constrained
D
we can force
\Tr(\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I})=\mathcal{R}
. Indeed this hope can often be realized without too much manual labor. Unfortunately, even if
D=D_{U}
for some
U\in V_{G}
there’s no reason to expect
\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I}=D_{U^{\prime}}
for some
U^{\prime}\in V_{G}
, which at first glance seems to preclude using this as a technique for realizing transfer systems by linear isometries operads.

With a bit more consideration however, we can push this idea a bit further. Suppose for each
H\leqslant G
we choose some “lift”

J_{H}^{G}(\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I}(H))\in\mathcal{U}_{G}

such that

R_{H}^{G}J_{H}^{G}(\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I}(H))=\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I}(H).

This is always possible since
R_{H}^{G}
is surjective when
G
is Abelian. We can then define

U_{1}=\bigcup_{H\leqslant G}J_{H}^{G}(\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I}(H)).

By construction we now have
D_{U_{1}}\supseteq\langle D\rangle^{\mathcal{R}}_{I}
, but we can no longer guarantee
\Tr(D_{U_{1}})\supseteq\mathcal{R}
. But now we can iterate this process to construct a sequence of universes

U_{1}\subseteq U_{2}\subseteq\cdots.

Since
\mathcal{U}_{G}
is finite, this process must eventually stabilize at some
U_{n}
for which
\Tr(D_{U_{n}})\supseteq\mathcal{R}
.

Of course if we choose our lifts too arbitrarily then regardless of our initial choice of
D
we will likely end up trivially with
\Tr(D_{U_{n}})=(\leqslant)
. It turns out however that imposing some simple constraints on
J_{H}^{G}
allows us to obtain some control over
D_{U_{n}}
for all
n
, and with a bit of care this will allow us to force
\Tr(D_{U_{n}})=\mathcal{R}
. Of course it remains to show that we can construct a lifting mechanism satisfying these constraints; we show how to do this in
Section

3
.

2.1.
Sub-inductors

We now restrict
G
to be a finite Abelian group.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a finite Abelian group. A
sub-inductor

J=(J_{K}^{H})_{K\leqslant H\leqslant G}
is a collection of maps
J_{K}^{H}\colon V_{K}\to V_{H}
indexed by intervals
K\leqslant H
in
\Sub(G)
, subject to the following axioms:

(1)

For all
K\leqslant H
,
J_{K}^{H}
is an equivariant join-semilattice homomorphism.

(2)

For all
K\leqslant L\leqslant H
,

J_{L}^{H}\circ J_{K}^{L}=J_{K}^{H}.

(3)

For all
K\leqslant H
and all
U\in V_{K}
,

R_{K}^{H}J_{K}^{H}(U)=U.

(4)

For all
K,L\leqslant H
and all
U\in V_{L}
,

R_{K}^{H}J_{L}^{H}(U)\subseteq J_{K\wedge L}^{K}R_{K\wedge L}^{L}(U).

(5)

For all
K\leqslant H
,

1_{H}\in J_{K}^{H}(1_{K}).

If
J
is a sub-inductor on
G
, then the
residue
of
J
on a
H\leqslant G
is defined to be the set

\Res_{J}(H):=\bigcup_{K<H}J_{K}^{H}(\widehat{K}).

Note that when
H=\mathbf{1}
is the trivial subgroup of
G
, we always have
\Res_{J}(H)=\emptyset
. By equivariance and axiom
5
, if
H\neq\mathbf{1}
then
\Res_{J}(H)\in\mathcal{U}_{H}
.

Remark 2.3
.

If
J
is a sub-inductor, then it follows from axioms
1
and
3
that for all
K\leqslant H
and
U\in V_{K}
we have

J_{K}^{H}(U)=I_{K}^{H}(U)\cap J_{K}^{H}(\widehat{K}).

Thus since
V_{H}
is a Boolean (in particular, distributive) lattice and
I_{K}^{H}
is a lattice homomorphism, it follows that
J_{K}^{H}
is in fact a lattice homomorphism.

The canonical example of a sub-inductor is the standard induction map
I=(I_{K}^{H})_{K\leqslant H\leqslant G}
. Axiom
1
follows from additivity and the fact that complex conjugation commutes with induction. Axiom
3
follows from Frobenius reciprocity and surjectivity of
R_{K}^{H}
, and axiom
4
follows from Mackey’s formula. Axioms
2
and
5
are obvious. Note that for all non-trivial
\mathbf{1}\neq H\leqslant G
we have
\Res_{I}(H)=\widehat{H}
. In light of
2.12
, we want to construct sub-inductors with residues as small as possible.

Explicitly constructing sub-inductors other than
I
on more complicated groups can be quite challenging, but the following construction allows us to reduce the construction of sub-inductors on a general Abelian group to the case of Abelian
p
-groups.

Let
G,G^{\prime}
be two Abelian groups with
\gcd(|G|,|G^{\prime}|)=1
. Then every subgroup of
G\times G^{\prime}
is uniquely of the form
H\times H^{\prime}
for
H\leqslant G
and
H^{\prime}\leqslant G^{\prime}
. Furthermore every irreducible representation
\chi\in\widehat{G\times G^{\prime}}
is uniquely of the form
\chi=\tau\otimes\tau^{\prime}
for some
\tau\in\widehat{G}
and
\tau^{\prime}\in\widehat{G^{\prime}}
.

Let
J
(resp.
J^{\prime}
) be a sub-inductor of
G
(resp.
G^{\prime}
). Given a pair of subgroups
K\times K^{\prime}\leqslant H\times H^{\prime}
and a pair of irreducible representations
\tau\in\widehat{K},\tau^{\prime}\in\widehat{K^{\prime}}
, we define

(J\otimes J^{\prime})_{K\times K^{\prime}}^{H\times H^{\prime}}(\tau\otimes\tau^{\prime})=J_{K}^{H}(\tau)\otimes J_{K^{\prime}}^{H^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime})

and extend to
V_{K\times K^{\prime}}
by join-preservation.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
G,G^{\prime}
be two Abelian groups with
\gcd(|G|,|G^{\prime}|)=1
, and let
J
(resp.
J^{\prime}
) be a sub-inductor of
G
(resp.
G^{\prime}
). Then
J\otimes J^{\prime}
is a sub-inductor of
G\times G^{\prime}
and

\Res_{J\otimes J^{\prime}}(H\times H^{\prime})=\Res_{J}(H)\otimes\widehat{H^{\prime}}\cup\widehat{H}\otimes\Res_{J^{\prime}}(H^{\prime}).

Proof.

Axioms
2
and
5
are immediate from the construction, and axioms
3
and
4
follow from the fact that
R
commutes with tensor products. Equivariance also follows easily since
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
acts diagonally on a tensor product of representations, and
J\otimes J^{\prime}
preserves joins by construction.

For the final claim we can simply compute

\displaystyle\Res_{J\otimes J^{\prime}}(H\times H^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{K\times K^{\prime}<H\times H^{\prime}}(J\otimes J^{\prime})_{K\times K^{\prime}}^{H\times H^{\prime}}(\widehat{K\times K^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{K\times K^{\prime}<H\times H^{\prime}}J_{K}^{H}(\widehat{K})\otimes(J^{\prime})_{K^{\prime}}^{H^{\prime}}(\widehat{K^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{K<H}J_{K}^{H}(\widehat{K})\otimes\widehat{H^{\prime}}\cup\bigcup_{K^{\prime}<H^{\prime}}\widehat{H}\otimes(J^{\prime})_{K^{\prime}}^{H^{\prime}}(\widehat{K^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\Res_{J}(H)\otimes\widehat{H^{\prime}}\cup\widehat{H}\otimes\Res_{J^{\prime}}(H^{\prime}).

∎

2.2.
Diagrams

Definition 2.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group. A
diagram

D=(D(H))_{H\leqslant G}
on
G
is a collection of sets
D(H)\in V_{H}
indexed by all subgroups. The diagram
D
is
universal
if
D(H)\in\mathcal{U}_{H}
for all
H
.

A diagram
D
is
R
-stable
if
R_{K}^{H}(D(H))\subseteq D(K)
for all
K\leqslant H
. Let
J
be a sub-inductor and
\mathcal{R}
a transfer system on
G
. A diagram
D
is
(J,\mathcal{R})
-stable
if
J_{K}^{H}(D(K))\subseteq D(H)
for all
K\to H\in\mathcal{R}
. If
\mathcal{R}
is the maximal transfer system where
K\to H\in\mathcal{R}
iff
K\leqslant H
, then we will simply say
D
is
J
-stable.

Given any diagram
D
, there is a unique minimal
R
-stable diagram
\langle D\rangle_{R}
containing
D
, which we call the
R
-stabilization
of
D
. We can construct the
R
-stabilization explicitly via the formula

\langle D\rangle_{R}(H)=\bigcup_{K\geqslant H}R_{H}^{K}(D(K)).

Similarly, given a diagram
D
and any sub-inductor
J
and transfer system
\mathcal{R}
there is a unique minimal
R
-stable diagram
\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}
containing
D
, which we call the
(J,\mathcal{R})
-stabilization
of
D
. We can construct the
(J,\mathcal{R})
-stabilization explicitly via the formula

\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}(H)=\bigcup_{K\to H\in\mathcal{R}}J_{K}^{H}(D_{i}(K)).

If
\mathcal{R}
is the maximal transfer system we will simply write
\langle D\rangle_{J}
instead of
\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}
.

The action of
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
on
V_{H}
for all
H\leqslant G
induces an action of
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
on the set of diagrams. Note that since
R
and
J
are equivariant, for
\tau\in\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
we have

\langle\tau D\rangle_{R}=\tau\langle D\rangle_{R}

and similarly

\langle\tau D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}=\tau\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}.

In particular, if
D
is universal then any stabilization of
D
is also universal.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
D
be an
R
-stable diagram and let
J
be a sub-inductor and
\mathcal{R}
a transfer system. Then
\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}
is again
R
-stable.

Proof.

Let
L\leqslant H
. To show the claim we can simply compute

\displaystyle R_{L}^{H}(\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}(H))

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{K\to H\in\mathcal{R}}R_{L}^{H}J_{K}^{H}(D(K))

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{K\to H\in\mathcal{R}}J_{K\wedge L}^{L}R_{K\wedge L}^{K}(D(K))

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{K\to H\in\mathcal{R}}J_{K\wedge L}^{L}(D(K\wedge L))

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{M\to L\in\mathcal{R}}J_{M}^{L}(D(M))

\displaystyle=\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}(L),

∎

We now introduce two classes of subgroups
H\leqslant G
for which the transition from
D(H)
to
\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}(H)
is particularly well-controlled.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a transfer system on
G
. Then a subgroup
H\leqslant G
is
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant
if there exists no
K<H
such that
K\to H\in\mathcal{R}
. A subgroup
H\leqslant G
is
\mathcal{R}
-fibrant
if
K\to G\in\mathcal{R}
.

Remark 2.8
.

This nomenclature is inspired by connection between transfer systems and weak factorization systems identified by Franchere et al.
[
7
]
. Indeed, if
\mathcal{R}
is the set of fibrations of a model structure on the lattice
\Sub(G)
then clearly a subgroup is
\mathcal{R}
-fibrant if and only if it’s a fibrant object in the given model structure. Similarly if we think of
\mathcal{R}
as the set of acyclic fibrations in some model structure on
\Sub(G)
, then, although not quite as immediately obvious, one can show a subgroup is
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant if and only if it is a cofibrant object in the given model structure.

The reader need not be familiar with the theory of model categories to follow any of our arguments; we only use this connection to give these important classes of subgroups memorable names.

In our case, cofibrant subgroups are especially important since a saturated transfer system is characterized by its cofibrant subgroups.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
be transfer systems, and suppose
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is saturated. Then
\mathcal{R}\leqslant\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
if and only if every
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
-cofibrant subgroup is
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{R}\leqslant\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
then directly from the definition it’s easy to see that every
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
-cofibrant subgroup is
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant, regardless of whether or not
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is saturated.

Conversely, suppose
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is saturated and every
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
-cofibrant subgroup is
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant. Let
K\to H\in\mathcal{R}
; we need to show
K\to H\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. Let
L\leqslant H
be a minimal subgroup such that
L\to H\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. If
L^{\prime}<L
and
L^{\prime}\to L\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
then by transitivity we would have
L^{\prime}\to H\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
violating minimality of
L
; thus
L
must be
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
-cofibrant, and hence by assumption
L
is
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant. But by pullback-closure, since
K\to H\in\mathcal{R}
and
L\leqslant H
we must have
L\wedge K\to L\in\mathcal{R}
and hence
L\leqslant K
since
L
is
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant. But then since
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is saturated and
L\to H\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
by construction, this implies
K\to H\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
J
be a sub-inductor,
\mathcal{R}
a transfer system, and
D
a diagram. Then

(1)

For any
H\leqslant G
,

D(H)\subseteq\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}(H)\subseteq D(H)\cup\Res_{J}(H).

(2)

For any
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant
H\leqslant G
,

\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}(H)=D(H).

(3)

If
D
is
(J,\mathcal{R})
-stable, then for any
\mathcal{R}
-fibrant
H\leqslant G
,

D(H)\subseteq R_{H}^{G}(D(G)).

Proof.

The first two claims follow immediately from the explicit construction of
\langle D\rangle_{J}^{\mathcal{R}}
. The last claim follows from sub-inductor axiom
3
.
∎

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
J
be an arbitrary sub-inductor. Then for any
R
-stable and
J
-stable diagram
D
we have

D(H)=R_{H}^{G}(D(G)).

Proof.

Since every subgroup if fibrant with respect to the maximal transfer system, by part 3 of
2.10
, a
J
-stable diagram
D
satisfies

D(H)\subseteq R_{H}^{G}(D(G)).

The reverse inclusion holds by definition of
R
-stability.
∎

2.3.
Realizing saturated transfer systems

We can now present our main theorem on constructing
G
-universes with a given saturated transfer system.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
G
be a finite Abelian group, and let
\mathcal{R}
be a saturated transfer system on
G
. Suppose there exists a sub-inductor
J
and an
R
-stable
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant diagram
D
such that for all
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant subgroups
H
and all
K<H
,

I_{K}^{H}(D(K))\not\subseteq D(H)\cup\Res_{J}(H).

Then there exists a
G
-universe
U
such that
\Tr(U)=\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

By
we can assume
J
is transitive. Note that since by axiom
5
we have
1_{H}=J_{\mathbf{1}}^{H}(1)\in\Res_{J}(H)
for all
H\neq\mathbf{1}
, we can add
1_{H}
to
D(H)
without affecting our hypotheses. Thus we can also assume without loss of generality that
D
is universal.

Let
D_{0}=D
and define
D_{i}
inductively as follows. If
i
is odd, let

D_{i}=\langle D_{i-1}\rangle_{I}^{\mathcal{R}}.

If
i
is even, let

D_{i}=\langle D_{i-1}\rangle_{J}.

By equivariance, we have
D_{i}(H)\in\mathcal{U}_{H}
for all
H\leqslant G
. By
2.6
,
D_{i}
is
R
-stable for all
i
. Clearly
D_{i}\supseteq D_{i-1}
for all
i
, so since
\coprod_{H\leqslant G}\mathcal{U}_{H}
is finite there must be some
n
such that
D_{i}=D_{n}
for all
i\geqslant n
. Let
U
be the
G
-universe corresponding to
D_{n}(G)\in\mathcal{U}_{G}
. I claim
\Tr(U)=\mathcal{R}
.

First note that
D_{n}
must be
J
-stable and
(I,\mathcal{R})
-stable since
D_{n}=D_{n+1}=D_{n+2}
. By
2.11
, since
D_{n}
is
J
-stable and
R
-stable we have
D_{n}(H)=R_{H}^{G}(D_{n}(G))
. Thus

\Tr(U)=\Tr(D_{n}(G)).

Since
D_{n}
is
(I,\mathcal{R})
-stable we have by definition
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\Tr(U)
. To show the converse, it suffices by
2.9
to show that every
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant subgroup is also
\Tr(U)
-cofibrant.

So let
H
be
\mathcal{R}
-cofibrant, and suppose for contradiction there exists some
K<H
such that
K\to H\in\Tr(U)
. By part 2 of
2.10
, for
i
odd we know
D_{i}(H)=D_{i-1}(H)
, and by part 1 of
2.10
, for
i
even we know
D_{i}(H)=D_{i-1}(H)\cup\Res_{J}(H)
. Thus by an easy induction we can see

D_{n}(H)\subseteq D(H)\cup\Res_{J}(H).

On the other hand, we know
D_{n}(K)\supseteq D(K)
, so if
K\to H\in\Tr(U)=\Tr(D_{n})
then

I_{K}^{H}(D(K))\subseteq I_{K}^{H}(D_{n}(K))\subseteq D_{n}(H)\subseteq D(H)\cup\Res_{J}(H)

contradicting our hypothesis.
∎

3.
Constructing sub-inductors

Armed with
2.12
, we now know that to prove the saturation conjecture for
G
it suffices to construct a sub-inductor
J
and an
R
-stable invariant diagram
D
such that for all subgroups
H\in\Sub(G)
and all
K<H
,

I_{K}^{H}(D(K))\not\subseteq D(H)\cup\Res_{J}(H).

In this section we develop techniques to construct such a pair
(D,J)
. It turns out to be convenient to add a small extra constraint: for all
H\leqslant G
, we require

D(H)\not\subseteq\Res_{J}(H).

We call a pair
(D,J)
satisfying these constraints a
tight pair
. More formally:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a finite Abelian group. A
tight pair
is a pair
(D,J)
where
D
is an
R
-stable diagram and
J
is a sub-inductor, such that

(1)

For all
H\leqslant G
,
D(H)
is invariant under the action of
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
.

(2)

For all
K<H\leqslant G
,

I_{K}^{H}(D(K))\not\subseteq D(H)\cup\Res_{J}(H).

(3)

For all
H\leqslant G
,

D(H)\not\subseteq\Res_{J}(H).

Corollary 3.2

(Corollary to
2.12
)
.

If a finite Abelian group
G
has a tight pair, then every saturated transfer system on
G
can be realized by a linear isometries operad.

∎

Note that we don’t need constraint (3) in this corollary. The importance of constraint (3) is explained in the following subsection.

3.1.
Reduction to
p
-groups

One of the most powerful techniques in algebra is the technique of localization. The primary difficulty in solving the saturation conjecture for cyclic groups is that transfer systems don’t interact nicely with localization, in the sense that given a pair of groups
G,H
, even if we understand the transfer systems on
G
and
H
very well and
\gcd(|G|,|H|)=1
, understanding the transfer systems on
G\times H
is extremely difficult. Indeed, transfer systems on cyclic
p
-groups are quite easy to understand, but even saturated transfer systems on
C_{p^{n}}\times C_{q^{m}}
are very complicated.

An immediate benefit to working with tight pairs is that they localize. Recall from the discussion preceding
2.4
the notion of tensor product of sub-inductors.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
(resp.
G^{\prime}
) be a finite Abelian group equipped with a tight pair
(D,J)
(resp.
(D^{\prime},J^{\prime})
). Suppose
\gcd(|G|,|G^{\prime}|)=1
. Then
(D\otimes D^{\prime},J\otimes J^{\prime})
is a tight pair, where

D\otimes D^{\prime}(H\times H^{\prime}):=D(H)\otimes D(H^{\prime}).

Proof.

Clearly
D\otimes D^{\prime}
is
R
-stable and
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant. By
2.4
we know
J\otimes J^{\prime}
is a sub-inductor. For all
H\times H^{\prime}\leqslant G\times G^{\prime}
we have some
\chi\in D(H)\setminus\Res_{J}(H)
and
\chi^{\prime}\in D^{\prime}(H)\setminus\Res_{J^{\prime}}(H^{\prime})
, from which it follows by
2.4
that

\chi\otimes\chi^{\prime}\in D\otimes D^{\prime}(H\times H^{\prime})\setminus\Res_{J\otimes J^{\prime}}(H\times H^{\prime}).

Thus constraint (3) is satisfied.

Now suppose
K\times K^{\prime}<H\times H^{\prime}
. If
K<H
, choose

\chi\in I_{K}^{H}(D(K))\setminus\left(D(H)\cup\Res_{J}(H)\right).

Otherwise if
K=H
choose
\chi\in D(H)\setminus\Res_{J}(H)
. Define
\chi^{\prime}
similarly. Then certainly

\chi\otimes\chi^{\prime}\in I_{K\times K^{\prime}}^{H\times H^{\prime}}(D\otimes D^{\prime}(K\times K^{\prime}))\setminus\Res_{J\otimes J^{\prime}}(H\times H^{\prime}).

We also know either
\chi\notin D(H)
or
\chi^{\prime}\notin D(H^{\prime})
, so

\chi\otimes\chi^{\prime}\notin D\otimes D^{\prime}(H\times H^{\prime}).

Thus constraint (2) is satisfied.
∎

If
(D,J)
and
(D^{\prime},J^{\prime})
don’t satisfy constraint (3) then
3.3
can fail; indeed, if
G=C_{p}
for arbitrary prime
p
then one can easily construct a pair
(D,J)
satisfying all the requirements necessary to be a tight pair except for constraint (3), but it’s known that the saturation conjecture fails for groups of the form
C_{p}\times C_{q}
if
p\leqslant 3
. Localization is useful enough to warrant including constraint (3) in the definition of a tight pair even though it’s not necessary for
2.12
.

3.2.
Saturation conjecture for cyclic groups

3.3
allows us to completely bypass all of the difficulties in the direct approach to the saturation conjecture for cyclic groups. We first need a direct construction for cyclic
p
-groups. This requires a bit more technical work than directly proving the saturation conjecture for cyclic
p
-groups, but the ideas underlying the construction are essentially no more complicated than Rubin’s construction.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
G=C_{p^{n}}
be a cyclic
p
-group with
p\geqslant 5
. Then
G
has a tight pair
(D,J)
.

Proof.

The subgroups of
G
are totally ordered under inclusion, and we let
\mathbf{1}=H_{0}<H_{1}<\cdots<H_{n}=G
be the collection of all subgroups of
G
listed in order. For each
i
the restriction map

\Res_{H_{i-1}}^{H_{i}}\colon\widehat{H_{i}}\to\widehat{H_{i-1}}

is well-defined and surjective since
H_{i}
is Abelian, so we can find choose some section

s_{i}\colon\widehat{H_{i-1}}\to\widehat{H_{i}},

and we can assume
s_{i}
is chosen so that
s_{i}(1_{H_{i-1}})=1_{H_{i}}
. For each
\chi\in\widehat{H_{i-1}}
, let

J_{H_{i-1}}^{H_{i}}(\chi)=\{s_{i}(\chi),\overline{s_{i}(\overline{\chi})}\}.

We then extend
J_{H_{i-1}}^{H_{i}}
to a
\cup
-homorphism
V_{H_{i-1}}\to V_{H_{i}}
in the unique way. Finally, we take
J_{H_{i}}^{H_{i}}
to be the identity for all
i
, and for all
i<j
we define

J_{H_{i}}^{H_{j}}=J_{H_{j-1}}^{H_{j}}\circ\cdots\circ J_{H_{i}}^{H_{i+1}}.

We claim
J
constructed in this way is a sub-inductor. Axioms
1
,
2
, and
5
are essentially baked into the construction. Axiom
3
is immediate when
K=H_{i-1}
and
H=H_{i}
using the fact that restriction commutes with complex conjugation, and this then implies axiom
3
for arbitrary
K\leqslant H
by transitivity.

It turns out that when the subgroups of
G
are totally-ordered, axiom
4
is actually implied by the other axioms. Indeed, if
K,L\leqslant H
then either
K\leqslant L
or
K\geqslant L
. In the first case for any
U\in V_{L}
we have

R_{K}^{H}J_{L}^{H}(U)=R_{K}^{L}R_{L}^{H}J_{L}^{H}(U)=R_{K}^{L}(U)=J_{K\wedge L}^{K}R_{K\wedge L}^{L}(U).

In the second case we have

R_{K}^{H}J_{L}^{H}(U)=R_{K}^{H}J_{K}^{H}J_{L}^{K}(U)=J_{L}^{K}(U)=J_{K\wedge L}^{K}R_{K\wedge L}^{L}(U).

Note that for each
i>0
,

\Res_{J}(H_{i})=J_{H_{i-1}}^{H_{i}}(\widehat{H_{i-1}})

by transitivity, since for any
K<H_{i}
we have
K\leqslant H_{i-1}
. Furthermore, if
\chi\neq\tau\in\widehat{H_{i-1}}
then

I_{H_{i-1}}^{H_{i}}(\chi)\cap J_{H_{i-1}}^{H_{i}}(\tau)=\emptyset

by Frobenius reciprocity and axiom
3
. Thus for any
K\lessdot H
and any
\chi\in\widehat{K}
, we have

|I_{K}^{H}(\chi)\cap\Res_{J}(H)|=|J_{K}^{H}(\chi)|\leqslant 2.

In particular,

I_{K}^{H}(\chi)\setminus\Res_{J}(H)\neq\emptyset

since
|I_{K}^{H}(\chi)|=p>2
.

Now let
D(H_{i})=\{1_{H_{i}},\tau_{i},\overline{\tau_{i}}\}
where

\tau_{i}\in I_{H_{i-1}}^{H_{i}}(1_{H_{i-1}})\setminus\Res_{J}(H_{i})

for
i>0
, and
\tau_{0}\in\widehat{\mathbf{1}}
is the trivial representation. Then
D
is
R
-stable and
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant, and
D(H)\not\subseteq\Res_{J}(H)
. If
i<j
then

I_{H_{i}}^{H_{j}}(D(H_{i}))\supseteq I_{H_{i}}^{H_{j}}(\tau_{i}),

but

I_{H_{i}}^{H_{j}}(\tau_{i})\cap(D(H_{j})\cup\Res_{J}(H_{j}))\subseteq\{\tau_{j},\overline{\tau_{j}}\}\cup\left(I_{K}^{H}(\tau_{i})\cap\Res_{J}(H_{j})\right).

But

|I_{H_{i}}^{H_{j}}(\tau_{i})|=p^{j-i}

whereas

|\{\tau_{j},\overline{\tau_{j}}\}\cup\left(I_{K}^{H}(\tau_{i})\cap\Res_{J}(H_{j})\right)|\leqslant 2+2^{j-i}.

Since
p\geqslant 5
we have
p^{k}>2+2^{k}
for all
k\geqslant 1
, from which it follows

I_{H_{i}}^{H_{j}}(D(H_{i}))\not\subseteq D(H_{j})\cup\Res_{J}(H_{j}).

∎

By
3.3
and
3.2
this immediately solves the saturation problem for all cyclic groups.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
G
be a cyclic group of order coprime to
6
. Then every saturated transfer system on
G
can be realized by some linear isometries operad.

∎

3.3.
Saturation conjecture for rank two groups

The formalism of tight pairs is not only useful for their localization properties. It also turns out that the axioms a tight pair is required to satisfy are well-suited to powerful constructive techniques from extremal combinatorics. We first show a way to construct a sub-inductor from a diagram; this construction makes all of the sub-inductor axioms other than axiom
3
trivial and also makes bounding the residue trivial, but at the cost of making it harder to verify axiom
3
.

For a pair of subgroups
K\leqslant H
, let

(K,H]=\{L\leqslant H:L\not\leqslant K\}.

Note that for all
K,L\leqslant H
we have

(K\wedge L,L]\subseteq(K,H],

and for
K\leqslant L\leqslant H
we have

(K,H]=(K,L]\amalg(L,H].

Given a diagram
D
, let
J=J[D]
be defined by

J_{K}^{H}(U)=I_{K}^{H}(U)\setminus\bigcup_{L\in(K,H]}I_{L}^{H}(D(L)).

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
D
be a
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant diagram such that
1_{H}\notin D(H)
for all
H\leqslant G
, and let
J=J[D]
as above. If
J
satisfies sub-inductor axiom
3
, then
J
is a sub-inductor and for all
H\leqslant G
,

\Res_{J}(H)\cap D(H)=\emptyset.

Proof.

By assumption we know
J
satisfies axiom
3
. Axiom
1
follows since
J_{K}^{H}
is of the form

J_{K}^{H}(U)=I_{K}^{H}(U)\setminus S(K,H)

where

S(K,H)=\bigcup_{L\in(K,H]}I_{L}^{H}(D(L))

is
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant. Axiom
5
follows since
1_{H}\in I_{L}^{H}(D(L))
implies
1_{L}\in D(L)
. For
K\leqslant L\leqslant H
, since
(K,H]=(K,L]\cup(L,H]
we have

S(K,H)=S(L,H)\cup I_{L}^{H}(S(K,L)),

from which axiom
2
follows easily.

Now observe that by Frobenius reciprocity, given
K\leqslant H
and
U\in V_{K}
, we have
\chi\in J_{K}^{H}(U)
if and only if
\chi|_{K}\in U
and
\chi|_{M}\notin D(M)
for all
M\in(K,H]
. Let
K,L\leqslant H
and
U\in V_{K}
, and let
\chi\in J_{K}^{H}(U)
. To show
J
satisfies axiom
4
, we need to show

\chi|_{L}\in J_{K\wedge L}^{L}R_{K\wedge L}^{K}(U).

But

(\chi|_{L})|_{K\wedge L}=(\chi|_{K})|_{K\wedge L}\in R_{K\wedge L}^{K}(U),

and similarly for all
M\in(K\wedge L,L]\subseteq(K,H]
we have

(\chi|_{L})|_{M}=\chi|_{M}\notin D(M).

Thus
\chi|_{L}\in J_{K\wedge L}^{L}R_{K\wedge L}^{K}(U)
as claimed. Since
\chi
was arbitrary, this shows
J
satisfies axiom
4
.

Finally, let
K\leqslant L\leqslant H
and
U\in V_{K}
.

To see the claim about residues, note that for all
K<H
we have
H\in(K,H]
, so

D(H)=I_{H}^{H}(D(H))\subseteq\bigcup_{L\in(K,H]}I_{L}^{H}(D(L)).

Thus for all
K<H
we have

D(H)\cap J_{K}^{H}(\widehat{K})=\emptyset.

Since
V_{H}
is distributive, this implies

D(H)\cap\Res_{J}(H)=\emptyset.

∎

Definition 3.7
.

A
weak generating scheme
is a pair of diagrams
(A,T)
such that

(1)

For all
H\neq\mathbf{1}
,
A(H)
and
T(H)
are both
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant and non-empty, and

A(H)\cap T(H)=\emptyset.

(2)

For all
\mathbf{1}\neq K<H
we have

T(K)\cap R_{K}^{H}(A(H)\cup T(H))=\emptyset.

The most important part of this definition is that it says
T
is a
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant antichain with
|T(H)|\geqslant 2
for all
H\neq\mathbf{1}
. The constraints involving
A
should be thought of as a minor additional technical requirement.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
G
be a finite Abelian group, and let
(A,T)
be a weak generating scheme. Let
J=J[A\cup T]
and let

D=\langle A\cup T\rangle_{R}\setminus T,

i.e.
\chi\in D(H)
if and only if
\chi\notin T(H)
and there exists
L\geqslant H
such that
\chi\in R_{H}^{L}(A(L)\cup T(L))
.

If
J
satisfies sub-inductor axiom
3
, then
(D,J)
is a tight pair.

Proof.

By
3.6
we know
J
is a sub-inductor and

\left(A(H)\cup T(H)\right)\cap\Res_{J}(H)=\emptyset

for all
H\leqslant G
. Clearly
D
is
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant. For all
H\neq\mathbf{1}
we have

\emptyset\neq A(H)\subseteq D(H)\setminus\Res_{J}(H),

and for
H=\mathbf{1}
we have
1\in D(H)\setminus\Res_{J}(H)
. Thus for all
H
we have

D(H)\not\subseteq\Res_{J}(H).

Let
K<H
. Since
T(K)\cap R_{K}^{H}(T(H))=\emptyset
, we have
R_{K}^{H}(T(H))\subseteq D(K)
. But then

T(H)\subseteq I_{K}^{H}R_{K}^{H}(T(H))\subseteq I_{K}^{H}(D(K)),

so

T(H)\subseteq I_{K}^{H}(D(K))\setminus\left(D(H)\cup\Res_{J}(H)\right).

All that remains is to show
D
is
R
-stable. Let
K<H
and suppose
\chi\in D(H)
. Then by definition we have
\chi\in R_{H}^{L}(A(L)\cup T(L))
for some
L\geqslant H
. Since
(A,T)
is a weak generating scheme, this implies

\chi|_{K}\notin T(K)

and hence
\chi|_{K}\in D(K)
.
∎

3.6
and
3.8
are purely formal, but there doesn’t seem to be a good way of choosing a weak generating scheme such that formally guarantees axiom
3
holds. Instead we will use the probabilistic method from constructive extremal combinatorics to show the existence of some weak generating scheme for which axiom
3
holds. Note that axiom
4
automatically implies

R_{K}^{H}J_{K}^{H}(U)\subseteq U

for all
K\leqslant H
and
U\in V_{K}
, so to prove axiom
3
it suffices to show that for all
\chi\in\widehat{K}
and
H\geqslant K
we have

J_{K}^{H}(\chi)\neq\emptyset.

This formulation of the constraint is much easier to work with.

Before describing our randomized construction, it’s useful to extract a bit more notation. Let
G
be an Abelian
p
-group, and let
S_{G}
be the set of triples
(\chi,K,H)
where
K<H\leqslant G
and
\chi\in\widehat{K}
. Let
\operatorname{rk}(K,H)=\log_{p}([H:K])
. For
i>0
, let
S_{G}^{i}\subseteq S_{G}
be the subset of all
(\chi,K,H)\in S_{G}
such that
\operatorname{rk}(K,H)=i
.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
C\geqslant 0
. A diagram
D
is
C
-clustered
if for all
(\chi,K,H)\in S_{G}
we have

|J[D]_{K}^{H}(\chi)|\geqslant C^{\operatorname{rk}(K,H)}.

In fact to show that
D
is
C
-clustered it suffices to verify the condition for all
(\chi,K,H)\in S_{G}^{1}
:

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
G
be an Abelian
p
-group and
D
a diagram, and let

C_{1}=\min\{|J[D]_{K}^{H}(\chi)|:(\chi,K,H)\in S_{G}^{1}\}.

Then
D
is
C_{1}
-clustered.

Proof.

For
i\geqslant 1
, let

C_{i}=\min\{|J[D]_{K}^{H}(\chi)|:(\chi,K,H)\in S_{G}^{i}\}.

It suffices by induction to prove that for all
i+j=k
we have

C_{k}\geqslant C_{i}C_{j}.

Let
(\chi,K,H)\in S_{G}^{k}
be a minimizer, and let
K\leqslant L\leqslant H
be an arbitrary intermediate subgroup such that
\operatorname{rk}(K,L)=i
. By sub-inductor axioms
1
and
2
we have

J[D]_{K}^{H}(\chi)=\bigcup_{\xi\in J[D]_{K}^{L}(\chi)}J[D]_{L}^{H}(\xi),

and by axiom
4
this union is disjoint, so

C_{k}=|J[D]_{K}^{H}(\chi)|\geqslant|J[D]_{K}^{L}(\chi)|\min\{|J[D]_{L}^{H}(\xi)|\}\geqslant C_{i}C_{j}.

∎

Given a
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant
2
-clustered diagram
D
and a subset
T\subseteq\Sub(G)\setminus\{\mathbf{1}\}
, let
D^{T}\supseteq D
be the random diagram defined by

D^{T}(H)=\begin{cases}D(H)\cup\{\tau(H),\overline{\tau(H)}\}&H\in T\\
D(H)&H\notin T\end{cases}

where
\{\tau(H)\}_{H\in T}
is a collection of independent random variables sampled uniformly from

\tau(H)\in J[D]_{\mathbf{1}}^{H}(1)\setminus\{1_{H}\}.

Note that this is well-defined since
D
being
2
-clustered implies in particular that
|J[D]_{\mathbf{1}}^{H}(1)|\geqslant 2
for all
H\neq\mathbf{1}
.

We can immediately observe a few properties of this construction:

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
G
be an Abelian
p
-group,
D
a
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant
2
-clustered diagram, and
T\subseteq\Sub(G)\setminus\{\mathbf{1}\}
. Then

(1)

The diagram
D^{T}
is
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant and
1_{H}\notin D(H)
for all
H
.

(2)

For all
H\in T
,

(3)

For all
\mathbf{1}\neq K\leqslant H
,

R_{K}^{H}(D^{T}(H)\setminus D(H))\cap D(K)=\emptyset.

∎

We will construct a weak generating scheme inductively using the construction above. The following lemma is our main technical result, which shows that this construction preserves some level of clusteredness when
G
has rank two. We delay the proof until the end of this section.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
G
be a rank two Abelian
p
-group with
|G|=p^{n}
,
D
a
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant
C
-clustered diagram with

C>\max\{p^{1-1/n},10^{4}\},

and
T\subseteq\Sub(G)\setminus\{\mathbf{1}\}
. Let

\alpha=\sum_{H\in T}\frac{1}{|H|}.

Also let
\beta=p/C
,
\gamma=(\beta^{-n}-1/p)^{-1}
, and

\rho=e^{-15(\alpha+1)\beta\gamma}.

Suppose

p>\frac{\rho C^{1/4}}{5(3\log(2)-2)(\alpha+1)\gamma},

and let

C^{\prime}=\frac{\rho}{2}C.

Then
D^{T}
is
C^{\prime}
-clustered with probability at least

1-2p^{3n+3/4}e^{-\frac{\rho}{5}C^{1/4}}.

In particular, if

C>\left(5\log(2p^{3n+3/4})/\rho\right)^{4}

then with nonzero probability

is
C^{\prime}
-clustered.

We also need an estimate for rank two groups on the divisor sum
\alpha
featuring in
3.12
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
G
be a rank two Abelian
p
-group, and let
T_{i}\subseteq\Sub(G)
be the set of subgroups
H\leqslant G
such that
|H|=p^{i}
. Then for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sum_{H\in T_{i}}\frac{1}{|H|^{k}}\leqslant 2p^{i(1-k)}.

Furthermore, if
|G|=p^{n}
then

\sum_{\mathbf{1}\neq H\leqslant G}\frac{1}{|H|}\leqslant 2n,

and if
p\geqslant 3
then for all
k\geqslant 2
we have

\sum_{\mathbf{1}\neq H\leqslant G}\frac{1}{|H|^{k}}\leqslant 3p^{1-k}.

Proof.

By Theorem 3.3 of
[
16
]
, the number of subgroups
H\leqslant G
with
|H|=p^{i}
is at most
p^{i}(1-1/p)^{-1}\leqslant 2p^{i}
, so we easily compute

\sum_{H\in T_{i}}\frac{1}{|H|^{k}}\leqslant\frac{2p^{i}}{p^{ik}}=2p^{i(1-k)}.

Directly from this estimate we obtain

\sum_{\mathbf{1}\neq H\leqslant G}\frac{1}{|H|^{k}}\leqslant 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{i(1-k)}.

If
k\geqslant 2
then we can bound this by an infinite sum and apply the geometric series formula to obtain

\sum_{\mathbf{1}\neq H\leqslant G}\frac{1}{|H|^{k}}\leqslant\frac{2}{p^{k-1}-1}\leqslant 3p^{1-k},

where the last inequality uses
p\geqslant 3
. If
k=1
then
p^{i(k-1)}=1
for all

, so we have simply

\sum_{\mathbf{1}\neq H\leqslant G}\frac{1}{|H|^{k}}\leqslant 2n.

∎

Now let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and consider the sequence
b_{n,0},b_{n,1},...
defined by
b_{n,0}=1
and

b_{n,i+1}=\frac{e^{-90b_{n,i}^{-(n+1)}}}{2}b_{n,i}.

Clearly this sequence is well-defined, monotonically decreasing, and remains strictly positive forever.

Now let

again this is well-defined and

.

Also note that the function

x\mapsto\frac{(b_{n,n}x)^{1/4}}{5\log(2x^{3n+3/4})}=\Omega(x^{1/5})

is unbounded as

, so there is some

such that for all

we have

b_{n,n}x>\left(5\log(2x^{3n+3/4})e^{30(2n+1)b_{n,n}^{-(n+1)}}\right)^{4}.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
G
be a rank two Abelian

-group with

. If

Proof.

If

, so we assume

. For

be the set of subgroups

, and let
T_{n+1}=\Sub(G)\setminus\{\mathbf{1}\}
. For

let

\alpha_{i}=\sum_{H\in T_{i+1}}\frac{1}{|H|},

and let
C_{0},C_{1},...,C_{n+1}
be the sequence defined by
C_{0}=p
and

C_{i+1}=\frac{\rho_{i}}{2}C_{i},

where

\rho_{i}=e^{-15(\alpha_{i}+1)\beta_{i}\gamma_{i}},

with
\beta_{i}=p/C_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}=(\beta_{i}^{-n}-1/p)^{-1}
.

By
3.13
,
\alpha_{i}\leqslant 2
for

and
\alpha_{n}\leqslant 2n
. Let

and suppose by induction that

Then by definition,

\beta_{i}\leqslant b_{n,i}^{-1}.

Since

\gamma_{i}\leqslant(b_{n,i}^{n}-\frac{1}{2}b_{n,i}^{n})^{-1}=2b_{n,i}^{-n}.

Thus if

\rho_{i}\geqslant e^{-15\cdot 3\cdot b_{n,i}^{-1}\cdot 2b_{n,i}^{-n}}=e^{-90b_{n,i}^{-(n+1)}},

and if

\rho_{i}\geqslant e^{-15\cdot(2n+1)\cdot b_{n,n}^{-1}\cdot 2b_{n,n}^{-n}}=e^{-30(2n+1)b_{n,n}^{-(n+1)}}.

By the induction hypothesis and definition of

C_{i+1}\geqslant b_{n,i+1}p,

and

C_{n+1}\geqslant c_{n}p.

Note together with the bound on

C_{i}\geqslant C_{n+1}\geqslant p(p/2)^{-1/n}>p^{1-1/n},

and hence also

C_{i}>(2c_{n}^{-n})^{1-1/n}\geqslant(2b_{n,1}^{-n})^{1-1/n}\geqslant e^{90(n-1)}>10^{4}

. Furthermore, since

, for
i\leqslant n

C_{i}\geqslant b_{n,n}p>\left(5\log(2p^{3n+3/4})e^{30(2n+1)b_{n,n}^{-(n+1)}}\right)^{4}\geqslant\left(5\log(2p^{3n+3/4})/\rho_{i}\right)^{4}.

We now begin working towards the proof of
3.12
, but we first need to prove two sublemmas.

The following technical lemma is similar in spirit to the classical coupon collector’s problem. In the standard coupon collector’s problem one has a set
|X|=n
of “coupon types” and a sequence of independent uniformly distributed coupons
x_{1},x_{2},...\in X
, and one asks how many samples are required to collect at least one of each coupon type. Slightly more generally, one can ask how many samples are needed to collect a fixed fraction of all the coupon types, i.e. given
\varepsilon>0
what is the minimal
k
for which

|\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\}|=(1-\varepsilon)n.

One can show the expected number of samples needed is

\sum_{i=0}^{(1-\varepsilon)n-1}\frac{n}{n-i}\approx n\ln(1/\varepsilon),

and the exact number of samples needed is concentrated around the mean when
n
is large. Thus since
\ln(1/\varepsilon)
diverges as
\varepsilon
approaches zero, if we make
k=O(n)
samples then with high probability we can guarantee

|X\setminus\{x_{1},...,x_{k}\}|\geqslant\varepsilon|X|

for some
\varepsilon>0
depending only on
k/n
.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
X
be a set with

n=|X|\geqslant 10^{4},

and let
P_{1},P_{2},...,P_{m}
be a sequence of partitions of
X
such that each block in every
P_{i}
has size at most
2
, and suppose for all
i,j\leqslant m
, either
P_{i}=P_{j}
or for all
(B,B^{\prime})\in P_{i}\times P_{j}
we have
|B\cap B^{\prime}|\leqslant 1
. For each
i
suppose we’re given some
A_{i}\in P_{i}\amalg\{\star\}
, and suppose for each
i
we sample a block
b_{i}\in P_{i}\setminus\{A_{i}\}
independently and uniformly at random.

Then

\Pr\left(|X\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}|\leqslant\frac{e^{-5m/n}}{2}|X|\right)\leqslant n^{3/4}e^{-\frac{e^{-5m/n}}{5}n^{1/4}}.

Proof.

Our proof of this lemma is closely based on a math.stackexchange answer by Misha Lavrov
[
12
]
. We suggest the reader should read Lavrov’s argument in its simpler original context before attempting to understand the proof given below. Essentially all of the new arguments below are just there to ensure that the coins which land heads with probability
\sqrt{3/n}
only get flipped
O(\sqrt{n})
times; this fact is obvious in the original context but requires some significant work in our context.

Clearly the ordering of the
P_{i}
has no impact on the claim of the lemma, so we can freely reorder the
P_{i}
. We suppose
P_{1},...,P_{m^{\prime}}
each contain at least one block of size exactly two, and
P_{m^{\prime}+1},...,P_{m}
are discrete. We can also assume without loss of generality that for some sequence

0=i_{0}<i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}=m^{\prime},

we have
P_{i}=P_{j}
if and only if
i_{\ell-1}<i,j\leqslant i_{\ell}
for some
\ell\leqslant k
, and furthermore we can assume
i_{\ell}-i_{\ell-1}
is non-increasing with
\ell
.

Let

s=\min\{\lfloor n^{3/4}\rfloor,k\},

and let
G=(X,E)
be the undirected graph on
X
where
(x,y)\in E
if and only if
\{x,y\}\in P_{i}
for some
i\leqslant i_{s}
. Clearly
\deg_{G}(x)\leqslant s
for all
x\in X
. Thus by the Hajnal–Szemerédi theorem
[
11
]
, we can find an
equitable coloring
of
G
using at most
s+1
colors, by which we mean a vertex coloring such that the size of any two colored components differs by at most one. Note that either
s+1\leqslant n^{3/4}
or
s=\lfloor n^{3/4}\rfloor
, so in either case we have

(n^{1/4}-1)(s+1)\leqslant n.

Thus each colored component must have size at least
\lfloor n^{1/4}-1\rfloor
. Furthermore, note that if
s=k
then
(x,y)\in E
if and only if
\{x,y\}\in P_{i}
for some arbitrary
i\leqslant m
, so in this case if
x,y
share the same color then
\{x,y\}\notin P_{i}
for all
i\leqslant m
. On the other hand, if
s\neq k
then
s+1>n^{3/4}
implies

n^{1/4}(s+1)>n

and hence each colored component has size at most
\lceil n^{1/4}\rceil
.

Let
S\subseteq X
be a colored component and
x\in S
. If
s=k
then for
y\neq x
we have
(x,y)\in E
if and only if
\{x,y\}\in P_{i}
for some arbitrary
i\leqslant m
, so we have

\sum_{y\in S\setminus\{x\}}\#\{P_{i}:\{x,y\}\in P_{i}\}=0<\frac{m}{n}n^{1/2}.

On the other hand, if
s\neq k
then we must have
s+1>n^{3/4}
. For
y\neq x
there is at most one
t
such that
\{x,y\}\in P_{i_{t}}
, and if
y\in S
then
t>s
. Since
i_{\ell}-i_{\ell-1}
is non-increasing, for all
t>s
we have

i_{t}-i_{t-1}\leqslant\frac{1}{s+1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s+1}i_{\ell}-i_{\ell-1}=\frac{i_{s+1}}{s+1}\leqslant\frac{m}{s+1}<\frac{m}{n}n^{1/4},

and hence in this case we also have

\sum_{y\in S\setminus\{x\}}\#\{P_{i}:\{x,y\}\in P_{i}\}<(\lceil n^{1/4}\rceil-1)\frac{m}{n}n^{1/4}<\frac{m}{n}n^{1/2}.

Now let
P
be the partition of
X
into colored components, and let
P^{\prime}
be a refinement of
P
such that every block
B\in P^{\prime}
has size

n^{1/4}-2\leqslant|B|\leqslant\frac{n}{6}.

Existence of such a refinement is guaranteed by the fact that

\frac{n}{6}\geqslant 2(n^{1/4}-2).

Now fix some
S\in P^{\prime}
, and let
\ell=|S|
. For each
x\in S
, let
v(x)
be a counter initialized to zero. For each
i
, choose some ordering
P_{i}\setminus\{A_{i}\}=\{B_{1},...,B_{k_{i}}\}
, such that every block
B
with
B\cap S\neq\emptyset
comes before every block
B
with
B\cap S=\emptyset
. Note we have

\frac{n}{2}-1\leqslant k_{i}\leqslant n.

Consider the following alternative mechanism for generating
b_{i}
. Initialize
j=1
and iterate the following procedure until
j>k_{i}
:

(1)

If we’ve already chosen
b_{i}
, let
p=0
; otherwise set
p_{j}=\frac{1}{k_{i}+1-j}
.

(2)

If
B_{j}\cap S=\{x\}
, then increment
v(x)
with probability
3/n
. If
|B_{j}\cap S|=2
, then independently for each
x\in B_{j}\cap S
increment
v(x)
with probability
\sqrt{3/n}
.

(3)

If
B_{j}\cap S\neq\emptyset
and in the previous step we incremented
v(x)
for all
x\in B_{j}\cap S
, set
b_{i}=B_{j}
with probability
p_{j}n/3
. If
B_{j}\cap S=\emptyset
, set
b_{i}=B_{j}
with probability
p_{j}
.

(4)

Increment
j
.

The number of blocks
B\in P_{i}
such that
B\cap S\neq\emptyset
is at most
\ell\leqslant\frac{n}{6}
, so in step
3
if
B_{j}\cap S\neq\emptyset
then we always have

\frac{p_{j}n}{3}\leqslant\frac{n/3}{n/2-1+1-n/6}=1

and hence
p_{j}n/3
is indeed a well-defined probability.

Now from the above procedure one can see

\Pr(b_{i}=B_{j}|\text{$b_{i}$ not chosen before step $j$})=p_{j},

so

\Pr(b_{i}=B_{j})=p_{j}\prod_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j-1}(1-p_{j^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{k_{i}}.

Thus
b_{i}
sampled according to this procedure is uniformly distributed in
P_{i}\setminus\{A_{i}\}
. On the other hand, from the construction one can see that for all
x\in S
the number of
i
such that
x\in b_{i}
is at most
v(x)
. In particular,

Y\cap S\supseteq\{x\in S:v(x)=0\}.

Now for each
x\in S
and each
i\leqslant m
, there is at most one
j
for which
v(x)
can increment in the above process, namely the
j
corresponding to the unique block
B\in P_{i}
containing
x
. Furthermore, if
|B|=2
then
v(x)
can only increment if
B=\{x,y\}
with
y\in S
. By our previous argument we know

\sum_{y\in S\setminus\{x\}}\#\{P_{i}:\{x,y\}\in P_{i}\}<\frac{m}{n}n^{1/2}.

Thus
v(x)
is the sum of at most
m
Bernoulli random variables with parameter
3/n
and at most
\frac{m}{n}n^{1/2}
Bernoulli random variables with parameter
\sqrt{3/n}
. Thus

\Pr(v(x)=0)\geqslant\left(\left(1-\frac{3}{n}\right)^{n}\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{n^{1/2}}\right)^{n^{1/2}}\right)^{m/n}\approx e^{-\frac{m}{n}(3+\sqrt{3})}.

More precisely, using that

\left(1+\frac{x}{n}\right)^{n}\geqslant e^{x-(2-\sqrt{3})/2}

for
|x|\leqslant 3
when
n\geqslant 36
, we have

\Pr(v(x)=0)\geqslant e^{-\frac{m}{n}(3+\sqrt{3})}e^{-\frac{m}{n}(2-\sqrt{3})}=e^{-5m/n}.

Since
v(x)
and
v(y)
are independent for
x\neq y
, this shows

\#\{x\in S:v(x)=0\}

is bounded below by a binomial random variable with
\ell
trials and success probability
e^{-5m/n}
. Thus by a standard Chernoff bound we have

\Pr(|Y\cap S|\leqslant e^{-5m/n}|S|/2)\leqslant e^{-\frac{e^{-5m/n}}{4}|S|}\leqslant e^{-\frac{e^{-5m/n}}{5}n^{1/4}},

where in the last inequality we use
n\geqslant 10^{4}
to ensure
(n^{1/4}-2)/4\geqslant n^{1/4}/5
.

Repeating the above argument for all
S\in P^{\prime}
and applying the union bound now gives the claim of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
H
be an Abelian group of odd order and
K\lessdot H
, and let
\chi\in\widehat{K}
. Let
X=I_{K}^{L}(\chi)
, and for each
L\in(K,H]
let
P_{L}
be the partition of
X
defined by
\tau\equiv\tau^{\prime}\mod P_{L}
if and only if

\tau|_{L}\equiv\tau^{\prime}|_{L}\mod\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})

Let
S_{\chi}\subseteq(K,H]
be the subset of
L
for which
K\wedge L\leqslant\ker\chi
.

Then

(1)

If
M\leqslant L
then
P_{L}\leqslant P_{M}
.

(2)

If
M\leqslant L
and
L\in S_{\chi}
then
P_{L}=P_{M}
.

(3)

For all
L\in(K,H]
every block in
P_{L}
has cardinality at most two.

(4)

If
L\in(K,H]\setminus S_{\chi}
, then
P_{L}
is the discrete partition of
X
.

(5)

If
L\in S_{\chi}
, then
P_{L}
has a unique singleton block
\{1_{L}^{X}\}
and every other block has cardinality two.

(6)

If
M,L\in(K,H]
and
P_{M}\neq P_{L}
, then for every pair of blocks
(B,B^{\prime})\in P_{M}\times P_{L}
we have
|B\cap B^{\prime}|\leqslant 1
.

Proof.

Since restriction commutes with the action of
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
, if
M\leqslant L
and
\tau|_{L}\equiv\tau^{\prime}|_{L}\mod\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
then we must also have
\tau|_{M}\equiv\tau^{\prime}|_{M}\mod\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
, so claim (1) is immediate. Claim (2) is a corollary of claims (1), (3), and (5), since any partition satisfying the conclusion of claim (5) is maximal among partitions whose blocks have cardinality at most two.

Note that since
K\lessdot H
we have
K\vee L=H
for all
L\in(K,H]
, so a character
\tau\in\widehat{H}
is determined by its restriction to
K
and
L
. In particular, since
\tau|_{K}=\chi
for all
\tau\in X
, a character
\tau\in X
is determined by its restriction to
L
. Thus

R_{L}^{H}|_{X}\colon X\to\widehat{L}

is injective. Since
P_{L}
is the preimage under
R_{L}^{H}|_{X}
of the partition of
\widehat{L}
into
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-orbits, claim (3) follows immediately.

We prove claim (4) by contrapositive. Suppose
P_{L}
is not discrete, so there exists some
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}\in X
such that
\tau\equiv\tau^{\prime}\mod P_{L}
. Since
R_{L}^{H}|_{X}
is injective, we must have
\tau|_{L}=\overline{\tau^{\prime}}|_{L}
. But then

\chi|_{K\wedge L}=\tau|_{K\wedge L}=\overline{\tau^{\prime}}|_{K\wedge L}=\overline{\chi}|_{K\wedge L}.

Since
|K\wedge L|
is odd the only self-conjugate irreducible is
1_{K\wedge L}
, so we must have
\chi|_{K\wedge L}=1_{K\wedge L}
, or in other words
K\wedge L\leqslant\ker\chi
and hence by definition
L\in S_{\chi}
.

On the other hand, if
L\in S_{\chi}
then by Mackey’s formula

R_{L}^{H}(X)=I_{K\wedge L}^{L}(\chi|_{K\wedge L})=I_{K\wedge L}^{L}(1_{K\wedge L}).

Since induction commutes with the action of
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
, this implies
R_{L}^{H}(X)
is
\Gal(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
-invariant, so for every
\tau\in X
there exists some
\tau^{\prime}\in X
such that
\tau|_{L}=\overline{\tau^{\prime}}|_{L}
. If
\tau|_{L}\neq 1_{L}
then again using that
|L|
is odd we must have
\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau
. Thus letting
1_{L}^{X}\in X
be the unique preimage of

, claim (5) follows.

Finally, we again prove claim (6) by contrapositive. Suppose
M\neq L\in(K,H]
are such that there exist blocks
B\in P_{L}
and
B^{\prime}\in P_{L^{\prime}}
with
|B\cap B^{\prime}|\geqslant 2
. Then we necessarily must have
B=B^{\prime}=\{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\}
where
\tau|_{L}=\overline{\tau^{\prime}}|_{L}
and
\tau|_{L^{\prime}}=\overline{\tau^{\prime}}|_{L^{\prime}}
. But then
\tau|_{L\vee L^{\prime}}=\overline{\tau^{\prime}}|_{L\vee L^{\prime}}
, and hence
L\vee L^{\prime}\in S_{\chi}
by claim (4). Thus by claim (2) we have

P_{L}=P_{L\vee L^{\prime}}=P_{L^{\prime}}.

∎

Lemma 3.17
.

For any rank two Abelian
p
-group
G
with

,

\sum_{\mathbf{1}\neq H\leqslant G}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{|H|^{-k}}{k}\leqslant 1.

In particular, for any

\left|\sum_{H\in T}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{|H|}\right)-\frac{1}{|H|}\right|\leqslant 1.

Exchanging the order of summation and applying

Proof of
3.12
.

Fix some

Let

. For each

\displaystyle\Pr(\#\{L\in T^{\prime}:\tau(L)\in Y_{L}\}\geqslant k)

\displaystyle\leqslant\inf_{t>0}e^{-tk}\prod_{L\in T}\left(1+p_{L}(e^{t}-1)\right)

4.
Negative results in higher rank

Having obtained positive results on the saturation conjecture in ranks one and two, we now examine the saturation conjecture for higher rank Abelian groups. We will show that Abelian groups of rank three or more
never
satisfy the saturation conjecture, and in fact as the rank increases the ratio of unrealized to realized saturated transfer systems grows extremely quickly (faster than a quantity which is double-exponential in the rank squared).

4.1.
Failure of the saturation conjecture in rank three

Let
G=(C_{p})^{3}
for some (arbitrary) prime
p
. In this section we will identify an explicit saturated transfer system
\mathcal{R}
on
G
such that no linear isometries operad can realize
\mathcal{R}
.

Remark 4.1
.

Note that if
G
is any Abelian group of rank at least three then
(C_{p})^{3}\leqslant G
for some prime
p
. Then we can trivially extend
\mathcal{R}
to a saturated transfer system
\mathcal{R}^{G}
on
G
by defining
K\to H\in\mathcal{R}^{G}
if and only if
K=H
or
H\leqslant(C_{p})^{3}
and
K\to H\in\mathcal{R}
. If
U
were a
G
-universe such that
\Tr(U)=\mathcal{R}^{G}
, then
U|_{(C_{p})^{3}}
would be a
(C_{p})^{3}
-universe such that
\Tr(U|_{(C_{p})^{3}})=\mathcal{R}
, a contradiction. Thus in fact our results in this section imply

Theorem 4.2
.

If
G
is an Abelian group which is neither cyclic nor rank two (i.e., the size of a minimal generating set for
G
is at least three), then there exist saturated transfer systems on
G
that cannot be realized by linear isometries operads.

We can think of
G
as a three-dimensional vector space
V=G
over the field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with
p
elements. Fix a plane
H<V
and let
\mathcal{R}
be the saturated transfer system generated by
\{0\}\to H
. Explicitly, for
W^{\prime}\leqslant W\leqslant V
, we have
W^{\prime}\to W\in\mathcal{R}
if and only if either
W=W^{\prime}
or
W\leqslant H
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of lines
L<V
for which
L\not<H
; by construction we have
\{0\}\to L\notin\mathcal{R}
for all
L\in\mathcal{L}
. For each subspace
W\leqslant V
, we let
\pi_{W}\colon\widehat{V}\to\widehat{W}
be the restriction map. Note
\mathcal{L}
is exactly the set of points in the affine plane
\mathbb{P}(V)\setminus\mathbb{P}(H)
, so
|\mathcal{L}|=p^{2}
.

Let
U\in\mathcal{U}_{V}
. Suppose for contradiction that
\Tr(U)=\mathcal{R}
and let
X=\widehat{V}\setminus U
. Thus in particular, for each
\chi\in\widehat{H}
we have
\pi_{H}^{-1}(\chi)\not\subseteq X
, and for all
L\in\mathcal{L}
there exists some
\xi_{L}\in\widehat{L}
such that
\pi_{L}^{-1}(\xi_{L})\subseteq X
. We define

X_{L}=\pi_{L}^{-1}(\xi_{L})\subseteq X;

note we have
|X_{L}|=p^{2}
. Furthermore, given any
\chi\in\widehat{H}
, since
H\cap L=0
we can construct a unique character
\tau=\chi\otimes\xi_{L}\in\widehat{V}
such that
\pi_{H}(\tau)=\chi
and
\pi_{L}(\tau)=\xi_{L}
, so
\pi_{W}\colon X_{L}\to\widehat{H}
is a bijective covering.

For each
\tau\in\widehat{V}
we define the
covering index

c(\tau):=\#\{L\in\mathcal{L}:\tau\in X_{L}\}.

If
c(\tau)>0
, then by definition there exists some
L\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\tau\in X_{L}
, and hence in particular
\tau\in X
. Thus to derive a contradiction it suffices to find some
\chi\in\widehat{H}
such that every
\tau\in\pi_{H}^{-1}(\chi)
has positive covering index.

Since
\pi_{W}\colon X_{L}\to\widehat{H}
is bijective for each
L
, we know for each
\chi\in\widehat{H}
that

\sum_{\tau\in\pi_{H}^{-1}(\chi)}c(\tau)=|\mathcal{L}|=p^{2}.

Thus on each fiber of
\pi_{H}
, the average value of
c
is
p
. Thus if
c
has small variation on some fiber
\pi_{H}^{-1}(\chi)
then we can expect
c(\tau)
is close to
p
for all
\tau\in\pi_{H}^{-1}(\chi)
and hence in particular we should expect an upper bound on the variation to force
c(\tau)>0
for all such
\tau
. This suggests we should look for some
\chi\in\widehat{H}
that minimizes the variance

V(\chi):=-p^{2}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\tau\in\pi_{H}^{-1}(\chi)}c(\tau)^{2}.

If
L\neq L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
, then letting
H^{\prime}
be the plane generated by
L
and
L^{\prime}
, we can uniquely lift
\xi_{L}
and
\xi_{L^{\prime}}
to a character
\xi=\xi_{L}\otimes\xi_{L^{\prime}}\in\widehat{H^{\prime}}
, so
X_{L}\cap X_{L^{\prime}}=\pi_{H^{\prime}}^{-1}(\xi)
has cardinality
p
. Of course if
L=L^{\prime}
then
X_{L}\cap X_{L^{\prime}}=X_{L}
has cardinality
p^{2}
, so by double counting we compute

\displaystyle E[V(\chi)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{2}}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{H}}V(\chi)

\displaystyle=-p^{2}+\frac{1}{p^{3}}\sum_{\tau\in\widehat{V}}c(\tau)^{2}

\displaystyle=-p^{2}+\frac{1}{p^{3}}\sum_{L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}|X_{L}\cap X_{L^{\prime}}|

\displaystyle=-p^{2}+\frac{1}{p^{3}}\left((p^{4}-p^{2})p+p^{2}\cdot p^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=p-1.

Thus we can find some
\chi
with variance
V(\chi)\leqslant p-1
. Now suppose we choose
\tau\in\pi_{H}^{-1}(\chi)
uniformly at random and let
Y
be the random variable
Y=c(\tau)
. We have
E[Y]=p
and
V(Y)\leqslant p-1
. By Chebyshev’s inequality this implies

\operatorname{Pr}(Y=0)\leqslant\operatorname{Pr}(|Y-E[Y]|\geqslant p)\leqslant\frac{V(Y)}{p^{2}}<\frac{1}{p}.

But
Y
is derived deterministically from the uniform distribution on a set with cardinality
p
, so the probability of any event is a multiple of
\frac{1}{p}
. Thus we must in fact have
\operatorname{Pr}(Y=0)=0
, i.e.
c(\tau)>0
for all
\tau\in\pi_{H}^{-1}(\chi)
.

Remark 4.3
.

The specific
\mathcal{R}
investigated above was heuristically chosen to make the constraints on
U
as strong as possible. Intuitively, any specification of the form
0\to W\in\mathcal{R}
constrains
U
to be large, and this constraint is stronger when the dimension of
W
is larger; conversely, any specification of the form
0\to W\notin\mathcal{R}
constrains
U
to be small, and this constraint is stronger when the dimension of
W
is smaller. With our choice of
\mathcal{R}
we aimed to maximize the tension between these two opposing constraints.

4.2.
High rank Abelian groups

The preceding argument shows that if
G
has rank at least three then there exist saturated transfer systems that cannot be realized by linear isometries operads. But it’s still not clear whether these failure cases are exceptional or common. In this section we show by a simple counting argument that for
G=(C_{p})^{n}
with
n
large, almost all saturated transfer systems are not realized by linear isometries operads.

Of course the number of weak-equivalence classes of linear isometries operads is at most the number of isomorphism classes of
G
-universes, which is

|\mathcal{U}_{G}|\leqslant|V_{G}|=2^{|\widehat{G}|}=2^{p^{n}}.

On the other hand, we can find a lower bound for the number of saturated transfer systems as follows. For each
i\leqslant n
, let
\Sub(G)^{i}\subseteq\Sub(G)
be the set of subgroups of order
p^{i}
. For any
S\subseteq\Sub(G)
, define a poset endomorphism

f_{S}\colon\Sub(G)\to\Sub(G)

via the formula

f_{S}(H)=\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\leqslant H\\
K\in S\end{subarray}}K.

This endomorphism is clearly decreasing and idempotent, i.e.
f_{S}
is an
interior operator
. Note that for all
H\in S
we have
f_{S}(H)=H
; furthermore, if
S\subseteq\Sub(G)^{i}
for some
i
, then

S=\{H\in\Sub(G)^{i}:f_{S}(H)=H\}.

Thus every subset of
\Sub(G)^{i}
defines a unique interior operator. By
[
4
]
the set of interior operators are in bijection with the set of saturated transfer systems, so this shows that for all
i\leqslant n
, the number of saturated transfer systems is at least

2^{|\Sub(G)^{i}|}=2^{\binom{n}{i}_{p}},

where
\binom{n}{i}_{p}
is the
p
-Binomial coefficient

\binom{n}{i}_{p}=\frac{(p^{n}-1)(p^{n-1}-1)\cdots(p^{n-i+1})}{(p^{i}-1)(p^{i-1}-1)\cdots(p-1)};

this formula is easily derived by double-counting the number of complete flags in the
n
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector space
G
.

Now for simplicity take
n=2k
to be even. By the truncated geometric series formula,

\binom{n}{k}_{p}\geqslant p^{k(n-k)}=p^{n^{2}/4}.

Thus the number of saturated transfer systems is at least
2^{p^{n^{2}/4}}
. Comparing this to our prior upper bound of on the number of weak equivalence classes of linear isometries operads gives

\frac{\#\{\text{realized saturated transfer systems}\}}{\#\{\text{unrealized saturated transfer systems}\}}\leqslant 2^{-p^{n^{2}/4}+p^{n}}=2^{-p^{\Omega(n^{2})}}.
