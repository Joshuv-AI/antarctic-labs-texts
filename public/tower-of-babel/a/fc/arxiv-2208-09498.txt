Abstract
. We study a class of kinetic-type differential equations ∂ ϕ t / ∂ t + ϕ t = 𝒬 ^ ​ ϕ t \partial\phi_{t}/\partial t+\phi_{t}=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\phi_{t} , where 𝒬 ^ \widehat{\mathcal{Q}} is an inhomogeneous smoothing transform and, for every t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 , ϕ t \phi_{t} is the Fourier–Stieltjes transform of a probability measure. We show that under mild assumptions on 𝒬 ^ \widehat{\mathcal{Q}} the above differential equation possesses a unique solution and represent this solution as the characteristic function of a certain stochastic process associated with the continuous time branching random walk pertaining to 𝒬 ^ \widehat{\mathcal{Q}} . Establishing limit theorems for this process allows us to describe asymptotic properties of the solution, as t → ∞ t\to\infty .

1.
Introduction

The article is devoted to the study of generalized kinetic-type equations of the form

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\nu_{t}=\mathcal{Q}(\nu_{t})-\nu_{t},\\
\nu_{0}=\nu;\end{array}\right.

where
(\nu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a family of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
. The derivative with respect to the time parameter
t
is understood in the weak sense, that is,
\partial_{t}\nu_{t}
is a signed measure such that
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int g(y)\nu_{t}({\rm d}y)=\int g(y)\partial_{t}\nu_{t}({\rm d}y)
, for every bounded continuous
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{C}
. The operator
\mathcal{Q}
on the right-hand side is
a smoothing transform defined in the following fashion.
For a given random vector of coefficients
(N,C,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
taking values in
\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
and a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
, take a sequence of independent identically
distributed (i.i.d.) random variables
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
with common distribution
\nu
which are also independent of the vector
(N,C,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
. The image
\mathcal{Q}(\nu)
is then defined by

\mathcal{Q}(\nu)={\rm Law}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}Y_{k}+C\right),

where
{\rm Law}(X)
denotes the law of a random variable
X
.

The equations of the form (
1.1
) originate from the Boltzmann equation. In the Kac caricature of a Maxwell
gas
[
22
]
,
\nu_{t}
is the distribution of velocity of
a typical particle in a homogeneous gas exposed to
elastic binary collisions. This model corresponds to the choice of parameters
N=2
,
C=0
,
(A_{1},A_{2})=(\sin\Theta,\cos\Theta)
, with
\Theta
being a random variable with the uniform distribution on
[0,2\pi]
.
The term
\mathcal{Q}(\nu_{t})-\nu_{t}
on the right-hand side of (
1.1
) describes the change in the velocity after a binary collision. Observe that here
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are not positive, which is the standing assumption for the present work. However, it is known that one can reduce the Kac’s model to a model with positive coefficients and a symmetric initial condition; see, for example, footnote on p. 2 in
[
13
]
. The inelastic version of the caricature, that is, when
\mathbb{E}\left[A_{1}^{2}+A_{2}^{2}\right]\neq 1
, has been introduced in
[
28
]
and studied later in
[
6
]
. Equation (
1.1
) in the general form involving the smoothing transform appears for the first time in
[
5
]
, where the case
\mathbb{P}[C=0]=1
and
N=2
was investigated. In
[
5
]
it was also explained how various kinetic equations developed for describing market economy
[
24
,
25
]
fit into the above framework. The perturbed case
\mathbb{P}[C=0]<1
, to the best of our knowledge, was introduced and studied only in
[
7
]
in the binary case
N=2
.

The classical choice
N=2
is in general motivated by physical applications where it is natural to assume that every collision involves just two particles.
Nevertheless, the case of non-binary collisions, that is, when
\mathbb{P}[N=2]<1
, being of interest from a mathematical viewpoint, has also received some attention in the literature.
The case of deterministic
N\geq 2
has been treated in the papers
[
4
,
11
,
12
]
, whereas in the recent work
[
13
]
the variable
N
is also allowed to be random. In all aforementioned references (except
[
7
]
) it is assumed that
\mathbb{P}[C=0]=1
. Besides pure mathematical interest, a motivation for the analysis of kinetic equations of the form (
1.1
) in a general situation of random
N
and non-vanishing
C
arises in connection with models for wealth redistribution in econophysics. We hinge on approach and terminology borrowed from
[
7
,
10
,
13
]
and consider a class of models with indistinguishable trading agents. The
k
-th agent state is characterized by his current wealth
w_{k}\geq 0
.
Unlike in physical applications, it is natural to assume that interactions (trades) may involve a random number
N
of agents. Conditional on the event
\{N=n\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, an interaction has the form

w^{\ast}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{k,j}w_{j}+c_{k},\hskip 10.00002ptk=1,\ldots,n,

where
w_{k}
(respectively,
w^{\ast}_{k}
) is the pre-trade (respectively, post-trade) wealth of the
k
-the agent participating in the interaction. Here the coefficients
p_{k,j}
are assumed to be random and represent the redistribution of the wealth between agents, whereas
c_{k}
represents, say, taxation operated by an external subject. This model is described by (
1.1
) with the following distribution of parameters:

\mathbb{P}[(C,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n})\in\cdot|N=n]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}[(c_{k},p_{k,1},\ldots,p_{k,n})\in\cdot],\hskip 10.00002ptn=0,1,2,\ldots.

Our aim is to provide robust probabilistic tools to study asymptotic properties of the solutions to (
1.1
).
For convenience, we shall formulate and prove our main results in the terms of the characteristic functions of
(\nu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
which will be denoted via

\phi_{t}(\xi)=\phi(t,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi x}\>\nu_{t}({\rm d}x),\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}.

The relation (
1.1
) written in terms of
(\phi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
reads

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\phi_{t}(\xi)=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\phi_{t}(\xi)-\phi_{t}(\xi),\\
\phi_{0}(\xi)=\phi(\xi);\end{array}\right.

where
\phi(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi x}\>\nu({\rm d}x)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}
is the functional operator associated with the smoothing transform
\mathcal{Q}
. More precisely, for a
continuous function
\psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{C}
taking
values in the unit disk
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\>:\>\|z\|\leq 1\}
we
define the function
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{C}
via

(1.3)

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\psi(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\xi C}\>\prod_{k=1}^{N}\psi(A_{k}\xi)\right],\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}.

In what follows we denote by
R
a generic random variable with the distribution

\nu=\nu_{0}
, so
\phi(\xi)=\mathbb{E}e^{i\xi R}
.

We shall show below, see Theorem
2.1
, that under mild assumptions on
(N,C,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
, equation (
1.2
) for an arbitrary initial condition
\phi_{0}
possesses a unique solution in the class of functions

(1.4)

\Big\{\phi:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{C}\Big|\phi(t,\cdot)\text{ for every }t\geq 0\text{ is the characteristic function of a probability measure,}\\
\phi(\cdot,\xi)\in C^{(1)}([0,\infty)\text{ for every }\xi\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}.

Having settled uniqueness we then turn our attention to asymptotic analysis of this solution, as
t\to\infty
. Depending on a choice of the distribution of the vector of coefficients
(N,C,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
we consider the
two following scenarios.

In the first regime
\phi_{t}(\xi)\to\phi_{\infty}(\xi)
, as
t\to\infty
, for some non-degenerate limit function
\phi_{\infty}
and all
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then one expects that
\phi_{\infty}

satisfies the stationary version of (
1.2
) that is

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\phi_{\infty}(\xi)=\phi_{\infty}(\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}.

From the probabilistic point of view one would interpret
\phi_{\infty}
as the Fourier–Stieltjes transform a fixed point of the inhomogeneous smoothing transform
\mathcal{Q}
. As verified by
[
7
, Proposition 3]
in the case

\mathbb{P}[N=2]=1
and Theorem
2.6
of the present article
for general
N
,
\phi_{\infty}
is the characteristic function of a random variable
X
which satisfies

{\rm Law}(X)={\rm Law}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}X_{k}+C\right),

with
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
denoting independent copies of
X
which are also independent of the vector
(N,C,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
.

In the second scenario
\phi_{t}
has a nondegenerate pointwise limit only after a suitable rescaling. For some fixed
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, consider a rescaled function

w_{t}(\xi)=\phi_{t}\left((t+1)^{\beta}e^{\alpha t}\xi\right),\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that the factor
(t+1)^{\beta}
instead of a more natural and asymptotically equivalent
t^{\beta}
is taken for convenience, since in this case
w_{0}(\xi)=\phi_{0}(\xi)=\phi(\xi)
. For the sake of simplicity assume for the time
being that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x\nu_{t}({\rm d}x)<\infty
, for all
t\geq 0
. This condition ensures that the function
w_{t}(\xi)
is differentiable with respect to
\xi
for all
t\geq 0
, and equation (
1.2
) can be recast in
terms of
(w_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}w_{t}(\xi)-\xi\left(\alpha+\beta\frac{1}{t+1}\right)\partial_{\xi}w_{t}(\xi)=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{(t+1)^{\beta}e^{\alpha t}}w_{t}(\xi)-w_{t}(\xi),\\
w_{0}(\xi)=\phi(\xi).\end{array}\right.

Here
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}
, for
s\geq 0
, denotes a rescaled smoothing transform obtained by replacing
C
with
sC
, that is,

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}\psi(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\xi sC}\>\prod_{k=1}^{N}\psi(A_{k}\xi)\right],\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Suppose now that
w_{t}(\xi)\to w_{\infty}(\xi)
, as
t\to\infty
, for an appropriate choice of
\alpha
and
\beta
and a nondegenerate limit
w_{\infty}(\xi)
. Note that if
C\neq 0
this is possible only if

(t+1)^{\beta}e^{\alpha t}\to 0
, as
t\to\infty
.
Following
[
4
,
11
]
we can find a probabilistic interpretation of
w_{\infty}
.
Using the partial differential equation for
(w_{t})_{t\geq 0}
we see that
w_{\infty}
ought to satisfy

(1.5)

-\alpha\xi\>\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}w_{\infty}(\xi)=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}w_{\infty}(\xi)-w_{\infty}(\xi).

It is worth noting that this equation does not depend on the value of
\beta
. The probabilistic interpretation becomes evident after rewriting the above relation as

w_{\infty}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{1}\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}w_{\infty}(s^{-\alpha}\xi)\>{\rm d}s=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{N}w_{\infty}(L^{-\alpha}A_{k}\xi)\right],

where
L
is distributed uniformly on
(0,1)
and is independent of
(N,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
. As verified by our Theorems
2.8
and
2.9
,
w_{\infty}
is the characteristic function of a fixed point of the
modified
homogeneous
smoothing transform

\kappa\mapsto{\rm Law}\left(L^{-\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}X_{k}\right),

where
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of independent random variables with the common distribution
\kappa
, which are also independent of the vector
(N,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
. It is worth noting that the equation (
1.5
)
is homogeneous in the argument in the sense that if
w_{\infty}

satisfies (
1.5
), then any function of the form
\xi\mapsto w_{\infty}(c\xi)
also satisfies it, for
c\in\mathbb{R}
. In order to characterize
w_{\infty}
it is therefore important to find an appropriate boundary condition.
As it is verified by our main results the boundary condition for
w_{\infty}
, that is, the value of
\partial_{\xi}w_{\infty}(0)
depends on the interplay between the initial condition
\phi_{0}
, the expectation
\mathbb{E}[C]
and the generalized
Laplace transform
s\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{s}\right]
.

The main results of the present article show the convergence
w_{t}\to w_{\infty}
for an appropriate choice of
\alpha
and
\beta
. We achieve that by finding a stochastic process

W=(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that
\phi_{t}
is the characteristic function of
W_{t}
, for every fixed
t\geq 0
. The dynamics of such process can be guessed from the integral form of (
1.2
) which reads

\phi_{t}(\xi)=e^{-t}\phi_{0}(\xi)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-s}\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\phi_{t-s}(\xi){\rm d}s,\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0.

Upon plugging the definition of
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}
we can write, with
E
denoting a unit mean exponential random variable independent of everything else,

(1.6)

\phi_{t}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{E>t\}}\phi_{0}(\xi)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{E\leq t\}}e^{i\xi C}\>\prod_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{t-E}(A_{k}\xi)\right],\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, the initial position
W_{0}
of the process
W
must have the characteristic function
\phi_{0}
, and after unit mean exponential amount of time the process is replaced by a sum of
N
independent copies of itself rescaled by

A_{k}
’s and shifted by
C
.
Naturally, the independent copies follow the same dynamics. This in turn gives a hint that
W
should be driven by a continuous-time branching process. We provide an in-depth construction of the process
W
in
Section
2
very much in the spirit of
[
12
]
where the case
C=0
has been considered. Note, however, that even in this case our construction is new, since, unlike in
[
12
]
, we allow
N
to be random.

We finish the introduction with a brief
overview of yet another aspect of the analysis of kinetic-type equations, namely to the problem of the propagation of chaos. A system comprised of identical particles is called symmetric and chaotic with respect to some probability measure
\mu
if, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the first
k
components of the vector of their velocities converge in distribution to
\mu^{\otimes k}
, as the number of particles tends to infinity. A peculiar result proved by Kac in
[
22
]
is called
propagation of chaos
and states that if at time
0
a system of particles exposed to elastic binary collisions is symmetric and chaotic with respect to an absolutely continuous measure
\nu_{0}
, then at time
t
, for every
t>0
, it is again symmetric and chaotic with respect to
\nu_{t}
, the solution to (
1.1
). The question of whether a particular kinetic model exhibits such a phenomenon has become classical, see, for instance
[
19
,
27
,
29
]
and

[
15
,
17
]
for more recent results. Having mentioned that, we nevertheless shall not discuss these issues here in details, since they go beyond the scope of our paper.

The article is organized as follows. In Section
2
we formulate our assumptions and present the main results. In particular, we shall prove an existence and uniqueness result for the solutions to (
1.2
)
by constructing a process
W
which yields a probabilistic representation of the solution to (
1.2
). The corresponding proof is given in Section
3
. Many-to-one lemmas, recalled in
Section
4
, will be utilized for showing the convergence of the stochastic process
W
in Section
5
. This in turn allows us to give short proofs of our main results in Section
6
. Throughout the paper we use the following notation. The Dirac measure at
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
is denoted by
\delta_{x}
. Convergence (respectively, equality) in distribution is denoted by
\overset{d}{\to}
(respectively,
\overset{d}{=}
). The notation
\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}
stands for convergence in probability.

2.
Main results

We shall first provide some further necessary notation and formulate precisely our assumptions. Then we proceed by giving the probabilistic representation for solutions to (
1.2
) using a
continuous-time branching random walk. In particular, this result yields that such solution exists and is unique. Next, we shall formulate our main results on the asymptotic behaviour of
\phi_{t}
, as
t\to\infty
.

2.1.
Existence and uniqueness

As mentioned in the introduction,
\phi_{t}
solving (
1.2
) can be linked with a continuous-time branching process which we shall now describe.
In order to avoid an explosion in the branching process, and thus in
\phi_{t}
, we shall impose the classical non-explosion hypothesis on the distribution of
N
, see
[
21
, Theorem V.9.1]
:

(2.1)

\int^{1}_{1-\varepsilon}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s-\mathbb{E}[s^{N}]}=\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for all sufficiently small }\varepsilon>0.

Note that
\mathbb{E}[N]<\infty
is sufficient for (
2.1
). In what follows we also assume that, given
N
, the weights
A_{1},A_{2}
are a.s. positive and are not degenerate at
1
. More precisely, we suppose that for every
m\in\mathbb{N}

(2.2)

\mathbb{P}[A_{1}>0,A_{2}>0,\ldots,A_{m}>0|N=m]=1

and for some
m
such that
\mathbb{P}[N=m]>0
it holds

(2.3)

\mathbb{P}[A_{1}=1,A_{2}=1,\ldots,A_{m}=1|N=m]<1.

For the sake of transparency we shall further suppose that

(2.4)

\mathbb{E}[N]\in(1,\infty],

which is the standard supercriticality condition and which ensures that our branching process survives with a positive probability. Note that if
\mathbb{E}[N]\leq 1
then either
\mathbb{P}[N=1]=1
and the branching is degenerate, or
\mathbb{P}[N=1]<1
,

\mathbb{E}[N]\leq 1
and the process dies out with probability one. The equation (
1.1
) in both cases can be treated using our methods.

We now turn our attention to the probabilistic interpretation of the solution to (
1.2
) which we shall use to establish the existence and uniqueness result. We hinge on ideas presented in
[
12
]
.
More precisely we shall work with a (marked) continuous-time branching random walk
which can be described in the following fashion. At time
t=0
one particle is placed at the origin of the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
.
After a random amount of time, distributed according to the unit mean exponential law, the particle splits into a random number
N
of new particles which are placed at random points of
{\mathbb{R}}
given by a point process

\zeta=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{Z_{k}},\hskip 20.00003ptZ_{k}=\log A_{k}.

From here each particle reproduces in exactly the same way independently from other particles. In particular, the relative positions of particles with respect to their mother are distributed
according to a copy of
\zeta
. Denote by
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}\subseteq\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{N}^{n}
, where
\mathbb{N}^{0}:=\{\varnothing\}
, the (full) Galton-Watson tree of the underlying population with the Ulam-Harris labelling,
that is, we label the root with
\varnothing
and for each
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
we label its children with
xi
where
1\leq i\leq N(x)
, with
N(x)
denoting the number of children of
x
. For
x,y\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
, the notation
y\leq x
means that
y
belongs to the unique path connecting
\varnothing
and
x
or, equivalently,
y
is an ancestor of
x
. To model the dynamics of the process suppose that each vertex

x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is equipped with
(E(x),U(x),C(x),Z_{1}(x),Z_{2}(x),\ldots)
which is an independent copy of
(E,U,C,Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots)
, where
E
denotes a unit mean exponential random variable independent of

(U,C,Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots)
and
U
is a random variable independent of
(E,C,Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots)
with a law that will be specified later.
Suppose that the triple
(E(x),U(x),C(x))
is attached to the vertex
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
and
Z_{i}(x)
is attached to the edge between
x
and
xi
.
For every particle
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
we define its time of birth
B(x)
and the time of death
D(x)
by the formulas

B(\varnothing)=0,\hskip 20.00003ptB(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{|x|-1}E(x_{|k}),\hskip 10.00002pt|x|\geq 1,\hskip 10.00002ptD(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{|x|}E(x_{|k})=B(x)+E(x),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty},

where
x_{|k}
is the
k
-th vertex in the unique path from the root
\varnothing
to
x
and

|x|
denotes the length of this path. Note that
E(x)
is therefore interpreted as the lifetime of particle
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{T}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
the tree discovered up to time
t>0
, given by

\mathcal{T}_{t}=\{x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}\>:\>B(x)\leq t\}.

The set of particles alive at time
t
is given by

\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}=\{x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}\>:\>B(x)\leq t<D(x)\}.

The relative complement of
\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}
, that is

\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o}=\mathcal{T}_{t}\setminus(\partial\mathcal{T}_{t})=\{x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}\>:\>D(x)\leq t\},

represents the particles removed from the system by time
t
. Both collections
\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o}
will play a significant role in the forthcoming construction of the
probabilistic representation of the solutions to (
1.2
).

A continuous-time branching random walk is a branching process with a spatial component. The position
V(x)
of

x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is obtained by summing the edge weights along the unique path from the root
\varnothing
to
x
. Thus, for
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
,

V(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{|x|}Z_{x_{|k}}(x_{|k-1}).

Therefore, the positions of particles present in the system at time
t\geq 0
are given by the point process

\mathcal{Z}_{t}=\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}}\delta_{V(x)}.

The measure-valued process
\mathcal{Z}=(\mathcal{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is called a continuous time branching random walk.

Note that the variables
(U(x),C(x))_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}}
did not play a role so far in the construction of
\mathcal{Z}
. We shall now make use of them for two markings. The first one is given via an independent marking of

\mathcal{Z}
with independent copies of
U
, namely

2.2.
Asymptotics

We shall now explain how one can use the asymptotic behaviour of
\mathcal{W}_{t}
to show that
\phi_{t}
, appropriately scaled, possesses a non-trivial limit as
t\to\infty
.
It turns out that the underlying branching process, by a change of measure
argument, is related to a compound Poisson process. The asymptotic of the latter is described in terms of the function
\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[-1,+\infty]
defined by

\Phi(s)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{s}\right]-1,\hskip 10.00002pts\geq 0.

Let
\mathcal{D}=\left\{t>0\>:\>\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{t}\right]<\infty\right\}
and assume that
\mathcal{D}
is not empty. Note that
\Phi(0)=\mathbb{E}[N]-1
and, particularly,
\mathbb{E}[N]<\infty
iff
\Phi(0)<\infty
. Moreover, by the merit of Hölder’s inequality,
\mathcal{D}
is a convex set. The function
\Phi
is continuous and strictly convex on the open interval
{\rm int}(\mathcal{D})
.

The fundamental object in our context is the so-called additive martingale

(2.6)

M_{t}(\gamma)=e^{-\Phi(\gamma)t}\int e^{\gamma v}\mathcal{Z}_{t}({\rm d}v),\hskip 20.00003pt\gamma\in\mathcal{D}

If
\Phi(\gamma)>\gamma\Phi^{\prime}(\gamma)
, then under some mild moment assumptions, see Lemma
5.1
below,
M_{t}(\gamma)
converges almost surely and in
L_{1}
to a random variable

M_{\infty}(\gamma)
which is
almost surely positive on the set
\{|\mathcal{T}_{\infty}|=\infty\}
. If, on the other hand,
\Phi(\gamma)\leq\gamma\Phi^{\prime}(\gamma)
then
M_{t}(\gamma)
still has a nontrivial limit in a weaker sense (in probability or distribution)
after a polynomial rescaling. The martingale limits
M_{\infty}(\gamma)
will appear naturally in our arguments.

As
\mathcal{W}_{t}=\mathcal{X}_{R,t}+\mathcal{C}_{t}
it is convenient to express
W_{t}
as a sum of two terms
X_{R,t}
and
C_{t}
defined by the following identities. Put

(2.7)

X_{U,t}:=\int e^{v}u\>\mathcal{X}_{U,t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}u),\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,

where
U
denotes some random variable (we use here
U
instead of
R
, because this notation will be applied below in various contexts) and

(2.8)

C_{t}:=\int e^{v}c\>\mathcal{C}_{t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c),\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0.

In our settings we shall scale this processes using the so-called spectral function given by

\mu(s)=\Phi(s)/s,\hskip 10.00002pts\in\mathcal{D}.

Since
\Phi
is strictly convex, the spectral function
\mu
can possess at most one minimizer
\gamma^{*}
. If it exists, it is then the unique solution to the equation
\mu^{\prime}(\gamma^{*})=0
, or equivalently

{\Phi(\gamma^{*})}={\gamma^{*}}\Phi^{\prime}(\gamma^{*})
. We shall mainly consider values of
\gamma
smaller than
\gamma^{*}
. However, since we do not assume existence of
\gamma^{*}
, instead of referring to the inequality

\gamma<\gamma^{\ast}
, we shall use the condition
\mu^{\prime}(\gamma)<0
, or equivalently
\Phi(\gamma)>\gamma\Phi^{\prime}(\gamma)
. The latter condition will allow us to control the dynamics of

provided (roughly speaking) that the distribution of

lies in the domain of normal attraction of a stable law of index

.
More specifically (and similarly to
[

3.
Utilizing the branching property: proof of Theorem
2.1

In this section we shall demonstrate how one can use the branching property of continuous-time branching random walks to prove the existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
1.2
).
The key fact that we are going to use throughout this section is that given
\mathcal{T}_{t}
the collections

((E(x),U(x),C(x),Z_{1}(x),Z_{2}(x),\ldots))_{{x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}},x\geq y}
for
y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}
are conditionally i.i.d.

The process
Y_{t}=|\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}|
which counts the number of particles in
\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}
is usually called a continuous-time branching process or a Yule process, see
[
3
, Chapter III]
.
Note that, according to (
2.1
),
Y_{t}
is finite a.s. for each
t\geq 0
. Moreover, by the memoryless property of the exponential distribution,
Y=(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Markov process with the jump rates

(3.1)

\mathbb{P}\left[Y(t+{\rm d}t)=k\>|\>Y(t)=j\right]=j\mathbb{P}[N=k-j+1]{\rm d}t+o({\rm d}t)

for
k\geq j-1
.

We shall now state the branching property for
(\mathcal{X}_{U,t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\mathcal{C}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
that will be utilized in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. First, note that for
t,s>0
,

\mathcal{X}_{U,t+s}
can be written in the following fashion

\mathcal{X}_{U,t+s}=\sum_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}}\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t+s},y\leq x}\delta_{V(x)-V(y)+V(y)}\otimes\delta_{U(x)}.

Thus, if we denote for
y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}

\mathcal{X}^{(y)}_{U,s,s+t}=\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t+s},y\leq x}\delta_{V(x)-V(y)}\otimes\delta_{U(x)},

then the previous relation takes the form

(3.2)

\mathcal{X}_{U,t+s}(\cdot,\cdot)=\sum_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}}\mathcal{X}^{(y)}_{U,s,s+t}(\cdot-V(y),\cdot).

The key feature of this representation is the following property. For every fixed
s\geq 0
, given
\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}
, the point processes
(\mathcal{X}^{(y)}_{U,s,s+t}(\cdot,\cdot))_{t\geq 0}
,
y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}
are independent copies of
\mathcal{X}_{U,t}
. In other words,
\mathcal{X}_{U,t+s}
is a sum of
|\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}|
(conditionally) independent copies of
\mathcal{X}_{U,t}
appropriately shifted.
The process
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{C}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
also enjoys a version of the branching property. For
s,t\geq 0
it holds

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{t+s}=\mathcal{C}_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)+\sum_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}}\mathcal{C}^{(y)}_{s,s+t}(\cdot-V(y),\cdot)

with

\mathcal{C}^{(y)}_{s,s+t}=\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t+s},y\leq x}\delta_{V(x)-V(y)}\otimes\delta_{C(x)},\hskip 20.00003pty\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}.

Once again, for every fixed
s\geq 0
, given
\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}
,

(\mathcal{C}^{(y)}_{s,s+t})_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}}
are independent copies of
\mathcal{C}_{t}
.

Using (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) we can write, with
\mathcal{W}^{(y)}_{s,s+t}=\mathcal{X}^{(y)}_{R,s,s+t}+\mathcal{C}^{(y)}_{s,s+t}
,

(3.4)

\mathcal{W}_{t+s}(\cdot,\cdot)=\mathcal{C}_{s}(\cdot,\cdot)+\sum_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}}\mathcal{W}^{(y)}_{s,s+t}(\cdot-V(y),\cdot).

The notation introduced so far allows for a probabilistic representation of the solutions to (
1.2
).

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We intend to prove that the function

\psi_{t}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{i\xi W_{t}}\big],\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in{\mathbb{R}},

is the unique solution to equation (
1.2
). Recall also that
\phi_{0}
is the characteristic function of
R
.
First we prove that the function
\psi_{t}
satisfies equation (
1.2
). The argument follows the proof of
[
12
, Proposition 2.5]
with some slight changes. We first establish an auxiliary continuity estimate for
\psi_{t}
. Take
t>s
and recall that under our standing assumptions

Y_{s}
is a.s. finite. Using (
3.1
) we write

|\psi_{t}(\xi)-\psi_{s}(\xi)|\leq 2\mathbb{P}\left[\mbox{there are splits during $[s,t]$}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\big[1-e^{-(t-s)Y_{s}}\big]\to 0,\hskip 20.00003pts\to t,

by an appeal to the dominated convergence theorem. Now define the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
via

\mathcal{F}_{s}=\sigma\left((E(x),C(x),(Z_{k}(x))_{k\geq 0}\>:\>x\in\mathcal{T}_{s}\right)

and use (
3.4
) to write for
s<t
,

\psi_{t}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\exp\left\{i\xi W_{t}\right\}\right|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg.\exp\bigg\{i\xi C_{s}+\sum_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}}i\xi e^{V(y)}\int e^{v}z\mathcal{W}^{(y)}_{s,t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}z)\bigg\}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{s}\bigg]\bigg].

The measurability of
{C}_{s}
with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{s}
yields

(3.5)

\psi_{t}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[e^{i\xi C_{s}}\prod_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{s}}\psi_{t-s}\big(\xi e^{V(y)}\big)\bigg].

We shall use this formula to study left and right derivatives of
\psi_{t}
with respect to
t
.
For
h\in(0,t)
, by considering the number of splits that occur within the time interval
[0,h]
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[e^{i\xi C_{h}}\prod_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\psi_{t-h}\big(\xi e^{V(y)}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mbox{no splits during $[0,h]$}\right\}}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\bigg[e^{i\xi C_{h}}\prod_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\psi_{t-h}\big(\xi e^{V(y)}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mbox{one split during $[0,h]$}\right\}}\bigg]+o(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{t-h}(\xi)e^{-h}+\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\xi C}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\psi_{t-h}(\xi A_{k})\right]h+o(h).

Dividing both sides by
h
, sending
h\to 0^{+}
and using the continuity estimate established at the beginning of the proof yields

\partial^{-}_{t}\psi_{t}(\xi)+\psi_{t}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[e^{i\xi C}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\psi_{t}(\xi A_{k})\bigg]=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\psi_{t}(\xi).

If we now replace
t
with
t+h
, put
s=h
in (
3.5
), and then send
h\to 0^{+}
we conclude that
\partial^{+}_{t}\psi_{t}=\partial^{-}_{t}\psi_{t}
. Thus,
\psi
is indeed a solution of (
1.2
) as claimed.

To prove uniqueness of the solution we adopt to our settings the arguments provided in the proof of
[
13
, Proposition 1.4]
.
Let
\phi_{t}
be any solution to (
1.2
). Going back to (
1.6
) write

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[E(\varnothing)>t]\phi_{0}(\xi)+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{E(\varnothing)\leq t\}}\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\phi_{t-E(\varnothing)}(\xi)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t},|x|\leq 1}\phi_{0}\left(e^{V(x)}\xi\right)\prod_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o},\>|x|\leq 0}e^{i\xi e^{V(x)}C(x)}\prod_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o},\>|x|=1}\phi_{t-D(x)}\left(e^{V(x)}\xi\right)\right].

Iterating the above equation
n
times gives

\phi_{t}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t},|x|\leq n}\phi_{0}\left(e^{V(x)}\xi\right)\prod_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o},\>|x|\leq n-1}e^{i\xi e^{V(x)}C(x)}\prod_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o},\>|x|=n}\phi_{t-D(x)}\left(e^{V(x)}\xi\right)\right].

Under assumption (
2.1
),
\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o}
are both finite a.s. and therefore all three products under the expectation have a.s. finite limits as
n\to\infty
.
Furthermore, the limit of the third product is one.
An appeal to the dominated convergence theorem gives

\phi_{t}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o}}e^{i\xi e^{V(x)}C(x)}\prod_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}}\phi_{0}\left(e^{V(x)}\xi\right)\right],\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Recalling that
\phi_{0}
is the characteristic function of the random variable
R
we obtain

\phi_{t}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o}}e^{i\xi e^{V(x)}C(x)}\prod_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}}e^{i\xi e^{V(x)}R(x)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{i\xi C_{t}}e^{i\xi X_{R,t}}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{i\xi W_{t}}\big]=\psi_{t}(\xi)

for
t\geq 0,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The proof is complete.
∎

4.
Many-to-one lemmas

One of the most useful tools in the analysis of branching random walks are many-to-one lemmas. It turns out that one can express the expectation of a statistic, which depends on the entire collection of particles, in terms of a trajectory of one,
tagged, particle after an appropriate change of measure. The claims of these lemmas follow from general theory (see
[
20
, Theorem 8.2]
).
We shall however present the details for the readers convenience. For
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
denote

\lambda(\alpha)=\log\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{|x|=1}e^{\alpha V(x)}\bigg]=\log\big(\Phi(\alpha)+1\big)

and consider a standard zero-delayed random walk
S^{(\alpha)}=\big(S_{n}^{(\alpha)}\big)_{n\geq 0}
with increments having distribution defined by the formula

(4.1)

\mathbb{E}\big[h\big(S^{(\alpha)}_{1}\big)\big]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{|x|=1}e^{\alpha V(x)-\lambda(\alpha)}h(V(x))\bigg]

for any bounded measurable function
h:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Furthermore, let
\eta=(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a homogeneous Poisson process on
[0,\infty)
given via

\eta_{t}=\#\left\{k\geq 1\>:\>T_{k}\leq t\right\},\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,

where
T_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}E_{j}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
(E_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with the unit mean exponential distribution. Next lemma is the aforementioned many-to-one formula for Markov
branching process specialized to the continuous time random walk

\big(S_{\eta_{t}}^{(\alpha)}\big)_{t\geq 0}
, see
[
20
, Theorem 8.2]
. In what follows
S^{(\alpha)}
,
\eta
and
U
are assumed to be independent.

Lemma 4.1

(Many-to-one formula I)
.

Assume (
2.1
), (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and
\mathcal{D}\neq\varnothing
.
Under the introduced notation, for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
,
t\geq 0
and any measurable function
g\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
,

(4.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int g(v,x)\>\mathcal{X}_{U,t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}x)\right]=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\alpha S_{\eta_{t}}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)\eta_{t}}g\big(S_{\eta_{t}}^{(\alpha)},U\big)\Big].

Proof.

Define for
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\}
,

F_{n}(t,z)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}\big[\int g(z+v,x)\>\mathcal{X}_{U,t}^{n}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}x)\big],&t\geq 0,\\
\mathbb{E}\big[g(z,U)\big],&t<0,\end{cases}

where

\mathcal{X}_{U,t}^{n}=\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t},|x|<n}\delta_{V(x)}\otimes\delta_{U(x)},\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0.

Then
\mathcal{X}_{U,t}^{\infty}=\mathcal{X}_{U,t}
and thus
F_{\infty}(t,0)
is equal to the left-hand side of (
4.2
).
Note also that
F_{0}(t,z)=0
for
t\geq 0
.
Utilizing the branching property of
\mathcal{X}_{U,t}^{n}
and recalling (
4.1
) we obtain, for finite
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle F_{n}(t,z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\{E(\varnothing)\leq t\}}\sum_{|y|=1}F_{n-1}(t-E(\varnothing),z+V(y))+\mathbbm{1}_{\{E(\varnothing)>t\}}g(z,U)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\alpha S_{1}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{E_{1}\leq t\}}F_{n-1}\big(t-E_{1},z+S_{1}^{(\alpha)}\big)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{E_{1}>t\}}g(z,U)\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\big\{{-\alpha S_{\eta_{t}^{(1)}}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)\eta_{t}^{(1)}}\big\}F_{n-1}\big(t-T_{1},z+S_{\eta_{t}^{(1)}}^{(\alpha)}\big)\Big],

where

\eta_{t}^{(k)}=\#\left\{1\leq j\leq k\>:\>T_{j}\leq t\right\},\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002ptk\in\mathbb{N}.

Upon iterating
n
times the above formula for
F_{n}(t,z)
we deduce

\displaystyle F_{n}(t,z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\big\{{-\alpha S_{\eta_{t}^{(n)}}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)\eta_{t}^{(n)}}\big\}F_{0}\big(t-T_{n},z+S_{\eta_{t}^{(n)}}^{(\alpha)}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\big\{{-\alpha S_{\eta_{t}}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)\eta_{t}}\big\}g\big(z+S_{\eta_{t}}^{(\alpha)},U\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\{T_{n}>t\}}\Big].

Putting
z=0
in the above equality and sending
n\to\infty
yields (
4.2
) in view of the monotone convergence theorem.
∎

For
t\geq 0
and
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
define

(4.3)

\begin{split}Z_{t}(\gamma)&=\int e^{\gamma v}\mathcal{Z}_{t}({\rm d}v)=\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}}e^{\gamma V(x)}.\end{split}

A direct consequence of Lemma
4.1
is the following formula, valid for
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}
,

(4.4)

\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{t}(\gamma)\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int e^{\gamma v}\>\mathcal{Z}_{t}({\rm d}v)\right]=e^{t\Phi(\gamma)},\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0.

We mention in passing that the above formula combined with the branching property of
\mathcal{X}
stated in (
3.2
) can be used to check that
M_{t}(\gamma)
defined in (
2.6
) is indeed a martingale.
Differentiating both sides of the above equation with respect to
\gamma
yields that for
\gamma\in{\rm int}(\mathcal{D})
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\int ve^{\gamma v}\>\mathcal{Z}_{t}({\rm d}v)\right]=t\Phi^{\prime}(\gamma)e^{t\Phi(\gamma)}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}\left[\int v^{2}e^{\gamma v}\>\mathcal{Z}_{t}({\rm d}v)\right]=\left(t\Phi^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)+t^{2}(\Phi^{\prime}(\gamma))^{2}\right)e^{t\Phi(\gamma)}.

We shall also use a simple consequence of the Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality, see Theorem 10.3.2 in
[
14
]
). If
\mathbb{E}[U]=0
, then, for any
p\in[1,2]
and
\gamma>0
such that
p\gamma\in\mathcal{D}
,
there exists a universal constant
c_{p}>0
that depends only on
p
such that

(4.5)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[|X_{U,t}|^{p}]&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int e^{\gamma v}u\mathcal{X}_{U,t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}u)\right|^{p}\right]\leq c_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int e^{2\gamma v}u^{2}\mathcal{X}_{U,t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}u)\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\\
&\leq c_{p}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int e^{p\gamma v}|u|^{p}\mathcal{X}_{U,t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}u)\bigg]=c_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\int e^{p\gamma v}\mathcal{Z}_{t}({\rm d}v)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[|U|^{p}\right]=c_{p}e^{t\Phi(p\gamma)}\mathbb{E}\left[|U|^{p}\right].\end{split}

In the above chain of estimates the first one is the aforementioned Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality, the second one follows from subadditivity of
x\mapsto|x|^{p/2}
, for
p\in[1,2]
, and the final equality follows from (
4.4
).

Our next lemma is a less standard one. In view of similarities with the previous result we shall also refer to it as a many-to-one formula.

Lemma 4.2

(Many-to-one formula II)
.

Assume (
2.1
), (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and
\mathcal{D}\neq\varnothing
. For every
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
, every measurable function
g\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
and every
t\geq 0
,

(4.6)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int g(v,c)\>\mathcal{C}_{t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c)\right]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\eta_{t}-1}e^{-\alpha S_{k}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)k}g\big(S_{k}^{(\alpha)},C\big)\bigg],

where
C
is independent of
S^{(\alpha)}
and
\eta
on the right-hand side.

Proof.

We shall use the same procedure as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
Define, for
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\}
,

H_{n}(t,z)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}\left[\int g(z+v,c)\>\mathcal{C}_{t}^{n}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c)\right],&t\geq 0,\\
0,&t<0,\end{cases}

where

\mathcal{C}_{t}^{n}:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o},|x|<n}\delta_{V(x)}\otimes\delta_{C(x)},\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0.

In particular,
H_{0}(t,z)=0
. As in the previous lemma we shall use the branching property of
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{n}
. For a finite
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds

\displaystyle H_{n}(t,z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{|y|=1}H_{n-1}(t-E(\varnothing),z+V(y))+g(z,C)\mathbbm{1}_{\{E(\varnothing)\leq t\}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\alpha S_{1}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)}H_{n-1}\big(t-E_{1},z+S_{1}^{(\alpha)}\big)+\mathbbm{1}_{\{E_{1}\leq t\}}g(z,C)\Big].

Iterating the above equality yields

\displaystyle H_{n}(t,z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\big\{-\alpha S_{\eta_{t}^{(n)}}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)\eta_{t}^{(n)}\big\}H_{0}\big(t-T_{n},z+S_{n}^{(\alpha)}\big)+\sum_{k=0}^{\eta_{t}^{(n)}-1}e^{-\alpha S_{k}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)k}g\big(z+S_{k}^{(\alpha)},C\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\eta^{(n)}_{t}-1}e^{-\alpha S_{k}^{(\alpha)}+\lambda(\alpha)k}g\big(z+S_{k}^{(\alpha)},C\big)\bigg].

Taking
z=0
and sending
n\to\infty
yields the desired formula by an appeal to the monotone convergence theorem.
∎

Assume that
\mathbb{E}[|C|^{p}]<\infty
for some
p>0
. Then formula (
4.6
) entails

(4.7)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int e^{\gamma v}|c|^{p}\>\mathcal{C}_{t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|C|^{p}\right]\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{e^{t\Phi(\gamma)}-1}{\Phi(\gamma)},&\Phi(\gamma)\neq 0,\\
t,&\Phi(\gamma)=0,\end{cases}

for every
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}
. Recall that
C_{t}
is defined via (
2.8
) and note that, given
\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o}
and
(V(x))_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o}}
, the variables
(C(x))_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{o}}
are independent copies of
C
. Thus, if
\mathbb{E}[C]=0
, we can apply the Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality as we did in (
4.5
). This entails,
for
q\in[1,2]
,

(4.8)

\mathbb{E}\big[|C_{t}|^{q}\big]\leq c_{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\int e^{qv}|c|^{q}\>\mathcal{C}_{t}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c)\right].

5.
Limits of the associated stochastic processes: proofs of Theorems
2.3
and
2.4

Having represented the solution to (
1.2
) as the characteristic function of the process
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}

we shall analyse the asymptotic behaviour of the latter.
Recall the decomposition
W_{t}=C_{t}+X_{R,t}
with
X_{R,t}
and
C_{t}
given via (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), respectively.
The asymptotics of
(X_{R,t})_{t\geq 0}
is essentially determined by the condition
(H_{\gamma})
and the behaviour of the process
(M_{t}(\gamma))_{t\geq 0}
, defined in (
2.6
), which is well-known from the literature, since

M_{t}(\gamma)=e^{-\Phi(\gamma)t}\int e^{\gamma v}\mathcal{Z}_{t}({\rm d}v)=e^{-\Phi(\gamma)t}Z_{t}(\gamma)

forms a so-called continuous-time additive martingale. Since it is positive, it converges a.s. to a limit as
t\to\infty
. It turns out that the limit is nondegenerate if and only if
\mu^{\prime}(\gamma)<0
. Whenever
\mu^{\prime}(\gamma)\geq 0
, the martingale
M_{t}(\gamma)
converges to
0
a.s. and a polynomial correction term is necessary to obtain a nondegenerate limit for a rescaled
M_{t}(\gamma)
. We sum up this discussion in the following lemma, which follows from
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume (
2.1
), (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and (
2.4
). Suppose further that
\mathcal{D}\neq\varnothing
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{\gamma}\right)\log^{+}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{\gamma}\right)\right]<\infty,

for some
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}
. If
\mu^{\prime}(\gamma)<0
, then

\lim_{t\to+\infty}M_{t}(\gamma)=M_{\infty}(\gamma)\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{a.s. and in }L_{1}.

Furthermore,
\mathbb{P}[M_{\infty}(\gamma)>0\;|\;|\mathcal{T}_{\infty}|=\infty]=1
and also

(5.1)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\left(\max_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}}\gamma V(x)-\Phi(\gamma)t\right)=-\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.s.}

Note that (
5.1
) holds a.s. conditionally on the event
\{|\mathcal{T}_{\infty}|=\infty\}
. However, on the complementary event
\{|\mathcal{T}_{\infty}|<\infty\}
, the set
\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}
is empty for all large enough
t>0
, and (
5.1
) is still true if we interpret the maximum over the empty set as
-\infty
.

We aim now to prove Theorem
2.3
. To this end we apply a similar procedure as in the proofs of Proposition 3.2 in
[
12
]
and Theorem 2.3 in
[
18
]
. First, we fix some
\theta>0

and analyse the asymptotic behavior of the discrete skeleton
(C_{\theta n})_{n\geq 0}
. At the second step we pass to a continuous parameter by using an appropriate approximation argument. Note that for
\theta>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, branching property (
3.3
) yields

C_{\theta(n+1)}=C_{\theta n}+\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta n}}e^{V(x)}\int e^{v}c\>\mathcal{C}^{(x)}_{\theta n,\theta(n+1)}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c),

and
C_{\theta}^{(n)}(x):=\int e^{v}c\>\mathcal{C}^{(x)}_{\theta n,\theta(n+1)}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c)
,
x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta n}
, are conditionally independent copies of
C_{\theta}
. Thus, recalling (
4.3
),

(5.2)

\begin{split}C_{\theta(n+1)}&=C_{\theta n}+\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta n}}e^{V(x)}C_{\theta}^{(n)}(x)\\
&=C_{\theta n}+(\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta}])\cdot Z_{\theta n}(1)+\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta n}}e^{V(x)}\big(C_{\theta}^{(n)}(x)-\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta}]\big).\end{split}

Denote

\mathbb{X}_{k}:=\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta k}}e^{V(x)}\big(C_{\theta}^{(k)}(x)-\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta}]\big),

so that

(5.3)

C_{\theta(n+1)}=\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta}]\sum_{k=0}^{n}Z_{\theta k}(1)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{X}_{k}.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
in cases (a) and (b).

We start with decomposition (
5.3
). The first term on the right-hand side of (
5.3
) can be treated using Lemma
5.1
. Indeed, since
\mu^{\prime}(1)<0
,

(5.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}e^{-\theta n\Phi(1)}Z_{\theta n}(1)=\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{\theta n}(1)=M_{\infty}(1)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.s.}

To estimate the second term in (
5.3
), pick a constant
q\in(1,2]\cap{\rm int}(\mathcal{D})
such that
\mathbb{E}|C|^{q}<\infty
and
\mu^{\prime}(q)<0
, then
\mu(1)>\mu(q)
.
Notice that
\mathbb{X}_{k}
has the same distribution as
X_{U^{(k)},\theta k}
with the vertices marks
U^{(k)}(x)=C_{\theta}^{(k)}(x)-\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta}]
,
x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta k}
.
Inequalities (
4.7
), (
4.8
) yield

\mathbb{E}\big[\big|C_{\theta}^{(n)}(x)-\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta}]\big|^{q}\big]<c_{q,\theta},

for some constant
c_{q,\theta}
. Thus, (
4.5
) entails

\mathbb{E}[|\mathbb{X}_{n}|^{q}]\leq c_{q,\theta}e^{\theta n\Phi(q)}.

For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
we can write using Markov’s inequality

\mathbb{P}\left[\big|\mathbb{X}_{n}\big|>\varepsilon e^{n\theta\Phi(1)}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{-q}e^{-qn\theta\Phi(1)}\mathbb{E}[|\mathbb{X}_{n}|^{q}]\leq c_{q,\theta}\varepsilon^{-q}\cdot e^{qn\theta(\mu(q)-\mu(1))}.

A standard application of the Borel-Cantelli lemma entails

(5.5)

e^{-n\theta\Phi(1)}\mathbb{X}_{n}\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.s.}

as
n\to\infty
.
We now argue case by case.

Case
(a)
: If
\Phi(1)>0
, then
\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta}]=\mathbb{E}[C](e^{\theta\Phi(1)}-1)/\Phi(1)
by (
4.7
).
Using the Stolz–Cesáro lemma it is easy to check that the limit relation
e^{-\beta k}a_{k}\to a_{\infty}
,
k\to\infty
, for some sequence of real numbers
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\beta>0
and
a_{\infty}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, implies

e^{-\beta n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\to\frac{e^{\beta}}{e^{\beta}-1}a_{\infty},\hskip 20.00003ptn\to\infty.

Therefore, invoking (
5.3
) together with (
5.4
) and (
5.5
),

e^{-\theta n\Phi(1)}C_{\theta(n+1)}=e^{-\theta n\Phi(1)}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[C]\frac{e^{\theta\Phi(1)}-1}{\Phi(1)}Z_{\theta k}(1)+\mathbb{X}_{k}\bigg)\to\frac{e^{\theta\Phi(1)}\mathbb{E}[C]}{\Phi(1)}M_{\infty}(1)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.s.}

Case
(b)
: If
\Phi(1)=0
, then
\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta}]=\theta\mathbb{E}[C]
by (
4.7
). Moreover, since
Z_{\theta n}(1)\to M_{\infty}(1)
a.s. by (
5.4
) and
\mathbb{X}_{n}\to 0
a.s. by (
5.5
), another appeal to the Stolz–Cesáro lemma yields

\frac{1}{n\theta}C_{(n+1)\theta}\to\mathbb{E}[C]M_{\infty}(1)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.s.}

To conclude the proof of Theorem
2.3
in cases (a) and (b) it remains to pass from the discrete parameter
n\theta
to the continuous parameter
t
in both cases. Take
t>0
and for
\theta>0
let
n=\lfloor{t}/{\theta}\rfloor
. Utilizing (
3.3
), we conclude

\mathbb{E}[|C_{t}-C_{\theta n}|]=\mathbb{E}[|X_{C_{t-\theta n},\theta n}|].

Since, invoking (
4.7
),

\mathbb{E}[|C_{t-\theta n}|]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int e^{v}|c|\>\mathcal{C}_{\theta}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c)\right]\leq{\rm const}\cdot\theta\mathbb{E}[|C|]<\infty,

we can bound the
L_{1}
norm of
C_{t}-C_{\theta n}
as follows

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|C_{t}-C_{\theta n}\right|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{\theta n}(1)]\mathbb{E}|C_{t-\theta n}|\leq{\rm const}\cdot\theta e^{\theta n\Phi(1)}\mathbb{E}[|C|].

This implies

\lim_{\theta\to 0^{+}}\limsup_{t\to\infty}e^{-\theta n\Phi(1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|C_{t}-C_{\theta n}\right|\right]=0,

which secures our claim.
∎

Proofs of Theorem
2.3
in case (c) and of Theorem
2.4
in case (d).

Fix
\theta>0
. As in the previous part, we shall first show the convergence along the sequence
(\theta n)_{n\geq 0}
. We shall argue case by case.

Case
(d): If
\mathbb{E}[C]=0
, then (
5.2
) entails that
(C_{\theta n})_{n\geq 0}
is a martingale.
It is uniformly integrable since for
p\in(1,2]\cap\mathcal{D}
and
\Phi(p)<0
, invoking consecutively
(
5.2
) and (
4.5
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[|C_{\theta(n+1)}-C_{\theta n}|^{p}\right]=\mathbb{E}\big[|\mathbb{X}_{n}|^{p}\big]\leq c_{p}\mathbb{E}[|C_{\theta}|^{p}]e^{n\theta\Phi(p)}.

As a consequence, the martingale
(C_{\theta n})_{n\geq 0}
is bounded in
L_{p}
and whence uniformly integrable and convergent.

Case
(c):
Recall decomposition (
5.3
).
By an appeal to (
5.4
) the series
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}Z_{k\theta}(1)
is absolutely convergent a.s.
Moreover, the process
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{X}_{k}

forms a uniformly integrable martingale, which can be
verified in the same way as in case (d).

In both cases we have shown

C_{n\theta}\to C_{\infty}=\int e^{v}c\>\mathcal{C}_{\infty}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}}e^{V(x)}C(x).

The convergence
C_{t}\to C_{\infty}
,
t\to\infty
, can be checked in a similar fashion as in the proof of Theorem
2.3
.
∎

Recall that case (e) of Theorem
2.4
follows from the proof of part (e) in Theorem
2.6
given in the next section.

6.
Proof of Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
2.9

The convergence results obtained in the previous section allow us to prove Theorem
2.6
. We shall use an inhomogeneous version of Lemma 4.1 in
[
12
]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Assume that
(r_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an integer-valued stochastic process such that
r_{t}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\infty
, as
t\to\infty
. Suppose additionally that for every
t\geq 0
there is an array
(a_{k,t})_{k=1}^{r_{t}}
of a.s. positive random weights and a stochastic process
(b_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{r_{t}}a_{k,t}^{\gamma}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}a_{\infty},\hskip 10.00002pt\max_{k=1,\ldots,r_{t}}a_{k,t}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}0\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptb_{t}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}b_{\infty},\hskip 10.00002ptt\to\infty,

for some
\gamma\in(0,2]
, a nonnegative random variable
a_{\infty}
and a random variable
b_{\infty}
. Assume that
\nu_{0}
satisfies the condition
(H_{\gamma})
with the same
\gamma
as above. For a sequence
(R_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of i.i.d. random variables distributed according to
\nu_{0}
and which is independent of
(r_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
\left((a_{k,t})_{k=1}^{r_{t}}\>:t\geq 0\right)
and
(b_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, put

S_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{r_{t}}a_{k,t}R_{k}+b_{t}.

Then, as
t\to\infty
,

(6.1)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\xi S_{t}}\right]\to\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{g}_{\gamma}(\xi a_{\infty}^{1/\gamma})e^{i\xi b_{\infty}}\right],\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Proof.

Note that, for every fixed
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mathbb{E}\exp(i\xi S_{t})=\mathbb{E}\exp\left(i\xi b_{t}+\sum_{k=1}^{r_{t}}\log\phi_{0}(a_{k,t}\xi)\right),

where
\log\phi_{0}
is the principal branch of the logarithm of the characteristic function of
R_{1}
. In Lemma 4.1 in
[
12
]
it was proved that

\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r_{t}}\log\phi_{0}(a_{k,t}\xi)\right)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\hat{g}_{\gamma}(\xi a_{\infty}^{1/\gamma}),\hskip 10.00002ptt\to\infty.

Since
b_{t}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}b_{\infty}
,
t\to\infty
, we also have

\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r_{t}}\log\phi_{0}(a_{k,t}\xi)+i\xi b_{t}\right)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\hat{g}_{\gamma}(\xi a_{\infty}^{1/\gamma})e^{i\xi b_{\infty}},\hskip 10.00002ptt\to\infty.

By the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, which is applicable because

\left|\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r_{t}}\log\phi_{0}(a_{k,t}\xi)+i\xi b_{t}\right)\right|\leq 1,

we obtain (
6.1
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

The first three cases (A), (C) and (D) follow immediately from the above lemma. For the reader convenience we present here the proof of (C). By Lemma
5.1
and using that
\Phi(\gamma)=0
, we obtain

\sum_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}}e^{\gamma V(y)}\to M_{\infty}(\gamma)\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{a.s.}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\max_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}}e^{V(y)}\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{a.s.,}\hskip 20.00003ptt\to\infty.

Further, by Theorem
2.3
(c),
C_{t}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}C_{\infty}
,
t\to\infty
. Therefore, by Lemma
6.1
applied with
r_{t}=Y_{t}=|\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}|
,
a_{y,t}=e^{\gamma V(y)}
,
b_{t}=C_{t}
, it holds

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{i\xi W_{t}}\big]\to\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{g}_{\gamma}(\xi M_{\infty}^{1/\gamma}(\gamma))e^{i\xi C_{\infty}}\big],\hskip 10.00002ptt\to\infty.

Let us now consider case (E). Fix
\theta>0
and let us show that

(6.2)

e^{-\mu(2)\theta n}W_{\theta n}\overset{d}{\to}\sqrt{M_{\infty}(2)(\sigma_{0}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[C]^{2}/\Phi(2))}{\rm N},\hskip 10.00002ptn\to\infty,

where
{\rm N}
is a standard normal random variable which is independent of
M_{\infty}(2)
.

Fix
l\in\mathbb{N}
. Using the decomposition
W_{\theta n}=X_{R,\theta n}+C_{\theta n}
and the branching properties (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we can write

\displaystyle e^{-\mu(2)\theta n}W_{\theta n}

\displaystyle=e^{-\mu(2)\theta n}(X_{R,\theta n}+C_{\theta n})

\displaystyle=\left(e^{-\mu(2)\theta l}\sum_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta(n-l)}}e^{V(y)-\mu(2)\theta(n-l)}\left(X_{R,\theta l}^{(y)}+C_{\theta(n-l),\theta n}^{(y)}\right)\right)+e^{-\mu(2)\theta n}C_{\theta(n-l)},

where

C_{\theta(n-l),\theta n}^{(y)}:=\int e^{v}c\mathcal{C}^{(y)}_{\theta(n-l),\theta n}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptX_{R,\theta l}^{(y)}:=\int e^{v}r\mathcal{X}_{R,\theta l}^{(y)}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}r),\hskip 10.00002pty\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta(n-l)}.

Note that, for every
y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta(n-l)}
,
X_{R,\theta l}^{(y)}+C_{\theta(n-l),\theta n}^{(y)}
are independent copies of a random variable

W_{\theta,l}:=X_{R,\theta l}+C_{\theta l},

and are also independent of
\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta(n-l)}
and
(e^{V(y)})_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta(n-l)}}
. Since

\mathbb{E}[W_{\theta,l}]=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}[W_{\theta,l}^{2}]<\infty,

we can apply Lemma
6.1
with
\gamma=2
,
t=\theta(n-l)
,
a_{y,t}:=e^{V(y)-\mu(2)t}
,
r_{t}=|\partial\mathcal{T}_{t}|
,
a_{\infty}=M_{\infty}(2)
and
b_{t}=0
, to conclude that

\sum_{y\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta(n-l)}}e^{V(y)-\mu(2)\theta(n-l)}\left(X_{R,\theta l}^{(y)}+C_{\theta(n-l),\theta n}^{(y)}\right)\overset{d}{\to}\sqrt{M_{\infty}(2)\mathbb{E}[W_{\theta,l}]^{2}}{\rm N},\hskip 10.00002ptn\to\infty,

for every fixed
l\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, according to Theorem 3.2 in
[
9
]
, in order to deduce (
6.2
) it remains to prove that

(6.3)

\lim_{l\to\infty}e^{-\Phi(2)\theta l}\mathbb{E}[W_{\theta,l}^{2}]=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]/\Phi(2),

and that, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

(6.4)

\lim_{l\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big\{\big|e^{-\mu(2)\theta n}C_{\theta(n-l)}\big|>\varepsilon\big\}=0.

By Chebyshev’s inequality in order to prove (
6.4
) it suffices to check that

\lim_{l\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}e^{-\theta n\Phi(2)}\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta(n-l)}^{2}]=0.

But this follows from the chain of estimates

e^{-\theta n\Phi(2)}\mathbb{E}[C_{\theta(n-l)}^{2}]\overset{\eqref{eq:c3}}{\leq}c_{2}e^{-\theta n\Phi(2)}\mathbb{E}\left[\int e^{2v}c^{2}\mathcal{C}_{\theta(n-l)}({\rm d}v,{\rm d}c)\right]\overset{\eqref{eq:c_mixed_moment}}{=}c_{2}\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]e^{-\theta n\Phi(2)}\frac{e^{\theta(n-l)\Phi(2)}-1}{\Phi(2)},

since
\Phi(2)>0
. Limit relation (
6.3
) can be checked by direct calculations:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}W_{\theta,l}^{2}=\mathbb{E}(X_{R,\theta l}+C_{\theta l})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta l}}e^{V(x)}R(x)+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}^{o}_{\theta l}}e^{V(y)}C(y)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta l}}e^{V(x)}R(x)\right)^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}^{o}_{\theta l}}e^{V(y)}C(y)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[R^{2}]\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{x\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta l}}e^{2V(x)}\right]+\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}^{o}_{\theta l}}e^{2V(y)}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:c_mixed_moment}}{=}\sigma_{0}^{2}e^{\theta l\Phi(2)}+\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]\frac{e^{\theta l\Phi(2)}-1}{\Phi(2)}.

This completes the proof of (
6.3
) as well as of (
6.2
). Fix
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
and put
h_{\xi}(t):=\mathbb{E}\exp(i\xi e^{-\mu(2)t}W_{t})
. From (
6.2
) we
know that, for every fixed
\theta>0
, it holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}h_{\xi}(n\theta)=\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{-\xi^{2}\left(M_{\infty}(2)(\sigma_{0}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[C^{2}]/\Phi(2)\right)/2\right\}.

In order to conclude that the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}h_{\xi}(n\theta)=\lim_{t\to\infty,t\in{\mathbb{R}}}h_{\xi}(t)
it remains to apply the Croft-Kingman lemma, see Corollary 2 in
[
23
]
. The fact that
t\mapsto h_{\xi}(t)
is right-continuous follows from the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem and the fact that
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has a version with a.s. right-continuous paths.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

The result is a consequence of the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality (
4.5
). Let us provide the details in the first case when
(A)
holds,
\mathbb{E}[R]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[|R|^{1+\delta}]<\infty
for some
\delta>0
. Invoking Theorem
2.3
, it is sufficient to ensure that
e^{-\mu(1)t}X_{R,t}
converges
to 0 in probability. The latter follows from (
4.5
) applied with
\gamma=1
, because for any
\varepsilon>0
, choosing
p\in(1,1+\delta)\cap\mathcal{D}
such that
\mu(p)<\mu(1)
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\big[e^{-\mu(1)t}|X_{R,t}|>\varepsilon\big]\leq(\varepsilon e^{\mu(1)t})^{-p}\mathbb{E}[|X_{R,t}|^{p}]\overset{\eqref{eq:4:MZ}}{\leq}c_{p}\varepsilon^{-p}\mathbb{E}[|R|^{p}]\cdot e^{pt(\mu(p)-\mu(1))}\to 0,\hskip 10.00002ptt\to\infty.

All the remaining cases can be treated in exactly the same way.
∎
