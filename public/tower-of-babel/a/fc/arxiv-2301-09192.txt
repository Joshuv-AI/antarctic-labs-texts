Lower Bounds on Learning Pauli Channels with Individual Measurements
Omar Fawzi, Aadil Oufkir, Daniel Stilck França

Abstract
Understanding the noise affecting a quantum device is of fundamental importance for scaling quantum technologies. A particularly important class of noise models is that of Pauli channels, as randomized compiling techniques can effectively bring any quantum channel to this form and are significantly more structured than general quantum channels. In this paper, we show fundamental lower bounds on the sample complexity for learning Pauli channels in diamond norm. We consider strategies that may not use auxiliary systems entangled with the input to the unknown channel and have to perform a measurement before reusing the channel. For non-adaptive algorithms, we show a lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( 2 3 ​ n ​ ε − 2 ) \Omega(2^{3n}\varepsilon^{-2}) to learn an n n -qubit Pauli channel. In particular, this shows that the recently introduced learning procedure by [ 1 ] is essentially optimal. In the adaptive setting, we show a lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( 2 2.5 ​ n ​ ε − 2 ) \Omega(2^{2.5n}\varepsilon^{-2}) for ε = 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2 − n ) \varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(2^{-n}) , and a lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( 2 2 ​ n ​ ε − 2 ) \Omega(2^{2n}\varepsilon^{-2}) for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . This last lower bound holds even in a stronger model where in each step, before performing the measurement, the unknown channel may be used arbitrarily many times sequentially interspersed with unital operations.

A class of quantum channels that deserves particular attention is that of Pauli channels

In fact, the set of Pauli channels provides a simple and effective model of incoherent noise, admitting a representation in terms of a probability distribution corresponding to different Pauli errors and inheriting the rich structure of the Pauli matrices. In addition, it defines a physically relevant noise model (see e.g.,
[

) and the noise affecting a device can always be mapped into a Pauli channel by using randomized compiling
[
9

techniques without incurring a loss in fidelity. These properties make the problem of Pauli tomography, i.e., learning a Pauli channel, particularly relevant.

A popular and widely used technique to learn Pauli channels is
randomized benchmarking and its variations
[
1

10

11

12

The reason for its popularity is that it satisfies several desirable properties: It is robust to errors both in the initial state preparation and measurements (SPAM errors), it only uses simple input states and measurements and it does not use any auxiliary systems. The motivating question for this work is to ask whether this protocol is optimal given such properties and restrictions or if we can hope to find more efficient protocols. Thus, we derive lower bounds for protocols that learn Pauli channels that fit the setting of randomized benchmarking protocols, in the sense that we are allowed to apply a channel multiple times to an initial state and intersperse its use with unitaries (or more generally unital channels) before measuring the state. However, we are not going to consider protocols where we can perform entangled measurements on multiple outputs of the channel at the same time or perform measurements on auxiliary systems that are entangled with the system the Pauli channel acts on

We refer to this class of measurements as individual measurements. Although allowing for auxiliary systems that we can measure would lead to significantly more efficient and simple protocols as we will discuss later, in practical scenarios it is unclear if it is reasonable to assume we can noiselessly entangle the qubits of a noisy device we wish to characterize to another set of qubits, potentially of the same size as the original device. Thus, even though more complicated, our setup comes from a firm practical motivation.

A general lower bound on the number of steps required for Pauli channel tomography

In this section, we consider the problem of learning a Pauli quantum channel using individual measurements. Unlike the usual state tomography problem for which at each step the learner can only choose the measurement device, for quantum channels, the learner has additional choices. First, in every setting, the learner can choose the input quantum state at each step. This choice can be done in an adaptive fashion: the input quantum state at a given step can be chosen depending on the previous observations (and of course the previous input states and POVMs). Second, the learner has the ability to reuse the Pauli quantum channel as much as it wants before performing the measurement. This is specific to quantum process tomography too since for state tomography using individual measurements, once a measurement is performed, the post-measurement quantum state is usually useless. Finally, the learner can intertwine arbitrary unital quantum channels and the unknown Pauli quantum channel before measuring the output of this (possibly long) sequence of quantum channels.
We propose a lower bound on the number of steps required for the Pauli channel tomography problem in this general setting.

can depend on the previous observations
I_{1},\dots,I_{t-1}
(see Fig.
2
for an illustration). However, to not overload the expressions we do not add the subscript
I_{1},\dots,I_{t-1}
on
m_{t},\rho_{t},\mathcal{N}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{N}_{m_{t}-1}
or
\mathcal{M}_{t}
. By Born’s rule, performing a measurement on the output quantum state
\rho^{\text{output}}_{t}
using the POVM
\mathcal{M}_{t}=\{M^{t}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is equivalent to sampling from the probability distribution

\displaystyle x_{t}\sim\{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{\text{output}}_{t}M^{t}_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}.

The observations
(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
are used to construct a probability distribution
\hat{p}
on the set of Pauli operators
\mathds{P}_{n}
satisfying with a probability at least
2/3
:

\displaystyle\TV(p,\hat{p})\leq\varepsilon.

Note that unital operations cannot be used to prepare a new state and thus have a free step.
In fact, applying a unital operation after a noisy Pauli channel cannot prepare a rank-
1
state for example.
We propose the following lower bound on the number of steps
N
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The problem of Pauli channel tomography using ancilla-free individual measurements requires a number of steps satisfying:

\displaystyle N\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

This theorem shows that no matter how often the learner reuses the quantum Pauli channel intertwined with other unital quantum channels on each step, the global number of steps should be exponential in the number of qubits. This can be explained by the fact that a Pauli channel adds noise to the input state, so reapplying it makes the input state more noisy and can’t help to extract more information. Although, as we remark later, this lower bound is weaker in the dependency on the dimension
d
compared to the non-adaptive case, it has the particularity of not depending on the number of uses of the Pauli channel.

Proof.

We will break down the proof into several steps, which we outline below:

Construction of the family
\mathcal{F}

We start by describing a general construction of a big family
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{P}_{x}\}_{x\in[M]}
constituted of quantum Pauli channels satisfying for all
x\neq y\in[M]:\TV(p_{x},p_{y})\geq\varepsilon
, we say that the family
\mathcal{F}
is
\varepsilon
-separated. These quantum channels have the form for
x\in[M]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}p_{x}(P)P\rho P

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\left(\frac{1+4\alpha_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\right)P\rho P

(2)

where
\alpha_{x}(P)=\pm 1
are chosen randomly so that
\alpha_{x}(P)=-\alpha_{x}(\sigma(P))
for some perfect matching
\sigma
of
\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
. The latter condition ensures
\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha_{x}(P)=0
. Hence,
\mathcal{P}_{x}
is a valid quantum channel for
\varepsilon\leq 1/4
.

Suppose that we have already constructed an
\varepsilon
-separated family of Pauli quantum channels
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{P}_{x}\}_{x\in[M]}
of cardinality
M
. We show that we can add another element to this family as long as
M<e^{cd^{2}}
for some sufficiently small constant
c
. For this, we choose
\alpha(P)=-\alpha(\sigma(P))=\pm 1
with probability 1/2, independently for each edge
\{P,\sigma(P)\}
in the matching.
This
\alpha
leads to a quantum channel
\mathcal{P}(\rho)=\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\left(\frac{1+4\alpha(P)\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\right)P\rho P
.
Then, we control the probability that the corresponding Pauli quantum channel isn’t
\varepsilon
-far from the family
\mathcal{F}
. We denote the set of edges in the matching
\sigma
by
\mathds{P}_{n}/\sigma
.
By the union bound and Chernoff-Hoeffding inequality
[
28
]
:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\exists\mathcal{P}_{x}\in\mathcal{F}:\TV(p,p_{x})<\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x=1}^{M}\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}|p(P)-p_{x}(P)|<2\varepsilon\right)=\sum_{x=1}^{M}\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}4|\alpha(P)-\alpha_{x}(P)|<2d^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{M}\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathbb{1}_{\alpha(P)\neq\alpha_{x}(P)}<\frac{d^{2}}{4}\right)=\sum_{x=1}^{M}\mathds{P}\left(\frac{2}{d^{2}}\sum_{\{P,\sigma(P)\}\in\mathds{P}_{n}/\sigma}\mathbb{1}_{\alpha(P)\neq\alpha_{x}(P)}<\frac{1}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x=1}^{M}\exp(-2(d^{2}/2)(1/4)^{2})=M\exp(-d^{2}/16)

which is strictly smaller than
1
if
M<e^{d^{2}/16}
.
So far, we have proven the following lemma:

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists an
\varepsilon
-separated family
\mathcal{F}
of quantum Pauli channels of the form in (
3
) and size at least
e^{d^{2}/16}
.

Hence, we can use this family to encode a message
X\sim\unif\{[M]\}
to the sequence of outcomes produced by the learning algorithm
when provided with the quantum Pauli channel
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{X}
. More precisely, the learning algorithm
chooses its inputs states and performs individual measurements possibly after many uses of the channel
\mathcal{P}_{X}
intertwined with arbitrary unital quantum channels, and
observes a sequence of outcomes that will be transmitted to the decoder.
Upon receiving this sequence of outcomes, the decoder runs the data-processing part of the learning algorithm to produce a Pauli quantum channel
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
corresponding to a probability distribution
\hat{p}
satisfying, with a probability at least
2/3
,
\TV(\hat{p},p_{X})\leq\varepsilon/2
. Since the family of probability distributions
\{p_{x}\}_{x\in[M]}
is
\varepsilon
-separated, there is only one
\hat{X}
such that
\TV(\hat{p},p_{\hat{X}})\leq\varepsilon/2
.
Therefore a
1/3
-correct algorithm can decode with a probability of failure at most
1/3
. By Fano’s inequality, the encoder and decoder should share at least
\Omega(\log(M))\geq\Omega(d^{2})
nats of information.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
29
]
)
.

The mutual information between the index of the actual channel
X
and the estimated index
\hat{X}
is at least

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:\hat{X})\geq(2/3)\log(M)-\log(2)\geq\Omega(d^{2}).

Upper bound on the mutual information

We show that no algorithm can extract more than
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
nats of information at each step. For this, recall that
X
is the uniform random variable on the set
[M]
representing the encoder and denote by
I_{1},\dots,I_{N}
the sequence of outcomes produced by the data-acquisition part of the learning algorithm. The Data-Processing inequality implies:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:\hat{X})\leq\mathcal{I}(X:I_{1},\dots,I_{N}).

Recall the notation
I_{\leq k-1}\coloneqq(I_{1},\dots,I_{k-1})
for all
1\leq k\leq N
, the chain rule of mutual information gives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:I_{1},\dots,I_{N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{\leq k-1})

where
\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{\leq k-1})
denotes the conditional mutual information between
X
and
I_{k}
giving
I_{\leq k-1}
.
We claim that every conditional mutual information
\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{\leq k-1})
can be upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
. To prove this claim, we prove first a general upper bound on the conditional mutual information.

At step
t\in[N]
, the
1/3
-correct algorithm used by the decoder chooses the input state
\rho_{t}
, uses the unknown quantum Pauli channel
\mathcal{P}

m_{t}\geq 1
times, eventually intertwines the
\mathcal{P}
with unital quantum channels
\mathcal{N}_{1}^{t},\mathcal{N}_{2}^{t},\dots,\mathcal{N}_{m_{t}-1}^{t}
and finally measures the output with a POVM
\mathcal{M}_{t}=\{\lambda_{i}^{t}\left|\phi_{i}^{t}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\phi_{i}^{t}\right|\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}
where
\langle\phi_{i}^{t}|\phi_{i}^{t}\rangle=1
and
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{t}\left|\phi_{i}^{t}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\phi_{i}^{t}\right|=I
. Note that this implies
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{t}=d
. Observe that we can always reduce the measurement with a general POVM
\mathcal{M}
to the measurement with such a POVM by taking the projectors on the eigenvectors of each element of the POVM
\mathcal{M}
weighted by the corresponding eigenvalues.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})=\underbrace{\mathcal{P}\circ\mathcal{N}^{t}_{m_{t}-1}\circ\mathcal{P}\dots\mathcal{P}\circ\mathcal{N}^{t}_{1}\circ\mathcal{P}(\rho_{t})}_{\mathcal{P}\text{ is applied }m_{t}\text{ times}}
the quantum channel applied to the input quantum state
\rho_{t}
.
We denote by
q
the joint distribution of
(X,I_{1},\dots,I_{N})
:

\displaystyle q(x,i_{1},\dots,i_{N})=\frac{1}{M}\prod_{t=1}^{N}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle.

We use the usual notation of marginals by omitting the indices on which we marginalize. For instance, for all adaptive algorithms, for all
1\leq k\leq N
, we have:

\displaystyle q_{\leq k}(x,i_{1},\dots,i_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{k+1},\dots,i_{N}}\frac{1}{M}\prod_{t=1}^{N}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\!\frac{1}{M}\!\prod_{t=1}^{k}\!\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\!\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|\!\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})\!\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle\!\prod_{t=k+1}^{N}\!\sum_{i_{t}}\!\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\!\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|\!\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})\!\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\prod_{t=1}^{k}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle\prod_{t=k+1}^{N}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\prod_{t=1}^{k}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle.

We sometimes abuse the notation and use
q
instead of
q_{\leq k}
when it is clear from the context. In order to simplify the expressions, we introduce the notation

u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}=\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|d\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{k}}(\rho_{k})-\mathds{I}\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle
. Note that for adaptive strategies the vectors
\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle=\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{t}(i_{<k})\right\rangle
and the states
\rho_{k}=\rho_{k}(i_{<k})
depend on the previous observations
i_{<k}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
for all
k\in[N]
.
Then the general upper bound on the conditional mutual information is:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
1\leq k\leq N
and
u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}=\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}(i_{<k})\right|d\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{k}}(\rho_{k}(i_{<k}))-\mathds{I}\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}(i_{<k})\right\rangle
. We have for adaptive strategies:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{\leq k-1})\leq 3\mathds{E}_{x}\mathds{E}_{{i_{\leq k-1}}\sim q_{\leq k-1}}\Bigg[\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}\Bigg].

Moreover, for non-adaptive strategies
u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}=\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|d\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{k}}(\rho_{k})-\mathds{I}\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle
and:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{\leq k-1})\leq 3\mathds{E}_{x}\Bigg[\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}\Bigg].

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

We can remark that, for all
1\leq k\leq N
,
q(x,i_{\leq k})=\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}}{d}\right)q(x,i_{\leq k-1})
thus

\displaystyle\frac{q(x,i_{k}|i_{\leq k-1})}{q(x|i_{\leq k-1})q(i_{k}|i_{\leq k-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{q(x,i_{\leq k})q(i_{\leq k-1})}{q(x,i_{\leq k-1})q(i_{\leq k})}=\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}}{d}\right)q(x,i_{\leq k-1})q(i_{\leq k-1})}{q(x,i_{\leq k-1})\sum_{y}q(y,i_{\leq k})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}}{d}\right)q(i_{\leq k-1})}{\sum_{y}q(y,i_{\leq k})}=\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}}{d}\right)q(i_{\leq k-1})}{\sum_{y}q(y,i_{\leq k-1})\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,y}}{d}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})q(i_{\leq k-1})}{\sum_{y}q(y,i_{\leq k-1})(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})}=\frac{(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})}{\sum_{y}q(y|i_{\leq k-1})(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})}.

Therefore by Jensen’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{\leq k-1})

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{q(x,i_{k}|i_{\leq k-1})}{q(x|i_{\leq k-1})q(i_{k}|i_{\leq k-1})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})}{\sum_{y}q(y|i_{\leq k-1})(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\left(\log(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})-\sum_{y}q(y|i_{\leq k-1})\log(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\log(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})\right)-\sum_{y}\mathds{E}\left(q(y|i_{\leq k-1})\log(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})\right).

The first term can be upper bounded using the inequality
\log(1+x)\leq x
verified for all
x\in(-1,+\infty)
:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\log(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q}\log(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q}u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}=\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k}}u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}

because
\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}=\mathrm{Tr}(d\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})-\mathds{I})=0
.
The second term can be upper bounded using the inequality
-\log(1+x)\leq\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-x
verified for all
x\in(-1/2,+\infty)
:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(-\sum_{y}q(y|i_{\leq k-1})\log(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{y}\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q}q(y|i_{\leq k-1})\log(1+u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{y}\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}q(y|i_{\leq k-1})\sum_{i_{k}}\!\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(1\!+\!u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})\log(1\!+\!u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})

\displaystyle\leq\!\sum_{y}\!\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}\!q(y|i_{\leq k-1})\!\sum_{i_{k}}\!\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(1\!+\!u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})(\tfrac{1}{2}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})^{2}\!-\!u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y}\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}q(y|i_{\leq k-1})\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}((u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}+(u_{i_{k}}^{k,y})^{2})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{y}\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}q(y|i_{\leq k-1})\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}.

Since the conditional mutual is upper bounded by the sum of these two terms, the upper bound on the conditional mutual information follows.
∎

The following lemma permits to conclude the upper bound on the conditional mutual information and thus the upper bound on the mutual information.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
m\geq 1
,
\mathcal{N}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{N}_{m-1}
be unital quantum channels and
\mathcal{P}
be a Pauli quantum channel in the family
\mathcal{F}
. We have for all quantum states
\rho
and vectors
\left|\phi\right\rangle\in\mathbf{S}^{d}
:

\displaystyle|\left\langle\phi\right|d\mathcal{P}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{P}\dots\mathcal{P}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{P}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle-1|\leq(4\varepsilon)^{m}.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

For
x\in[M]
, we define the map
\mathcal{M}_{x}
satisfying the following equality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{x}(\rho)\coloneqq\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho)-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\frac{\mathds{I}}{d}=\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\frac{4\alpha_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{d^{2}}P\rho P,

where we have used the fact (see Lemma
A.2
) that for all
\rho
:

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}P\rho P=d\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\mathds{I}.

Note that
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{M}_{x}(\rho))=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho))-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\mathrm{Tr}(\frac{\mathds{I}}{d})=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)=0
.
Applying a unital quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
between two quantum channels
\mathcal{P}_{x}
can be seen as :

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{x}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{x}\mathcal{N}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\frac{\mathds{I}}{d}+\mathcal{M}_{x}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{x}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\frac{\mathds{I}}{d}\right)+\mathcal{N}\mathcal{M}_{x}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\frac{\mathds{I}}{d}+\mathcal{M}_{x}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\frac{\mathds{I}}{d}+\mathcal{N}\mathcal{M}_{x}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\frac{\mathds{I}}{d}+\mathcal{M}_{x}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{M}_{x}(\rho)

because
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{N}\mathcal{M}_{x}(\rho))=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{M}_{x}(\rho))=0
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{x}(\mathds{I})

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\frac{4\alpha_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\mathds{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}/\sigma}\frac{4\alpha_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\mathds{I}+\frac{4\alpha_{x}(\sigma(P))\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\mathds{I}=0.

(3)

By induction, we generalize the equality (
3
) to
m
applications of the Pauli channel
\mathcal{P}_{x}
:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathcal{P}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{P}_{x}\dots\mathcal{P}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho)}_{\mathcal{P}_{x}\text{ is applied }m\text{ times}}=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\frac{\mathds{I}}{d}+\underbrace{\mathcal{M}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{M}_{x}\dots\mathcal{M}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{M}_{x}(\rho)}_{\mathcal{M}_{x}\text{ is applied }m\text{ times}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi\right|d\mathcal{P}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{P}\dots\mathcal{P}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{P}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\phi\right|\mathds{I}+d\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{M}\dots\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{M}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=1+d\left\langle\phi\right|\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{M}\dots\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{M}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle.

On the other hand, for all vectors
\left|\phi\right\rangle\in\mathbf{S}^{d}
and Hermitian matrices
X=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|
we have:
|\left\langle\phi\right|X\left|\phi\right\rangle|=|\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|\langle\phi|\phi_{i}\rangle|^{2}|\leq\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}||\langle\phi|\phi_{i}\rangle|^{2}=\left\langle\phi\right||X|\left|\phi\right\rangle
therefore using Lemma
A.2
:

\displaystyle|\left\langle\phi\right|\mathcal{M}(X)\left|\phi\right\rangle|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\phi\right|\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{4\alpha(P)\varepsilon}{d^{2}}PXP\left|\phi\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}|\left\langle\phi\right|PXP\left|\phi\right\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left\langle\phi\right|P|X|P\left|\phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{4\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\left\langle\phi\right|d\mathrm{Tr}|X|\mathds{I}\left|\phi\right\rangle=\frac{4\varepsilon}{d}\mathrm{Tr}|X|,

(4)

moreover we can also obtain:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}|\mathcal{M}(X)|

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{4\alpha(P)\varepsilon}{d^{2}}PXP\right\|_{1}\leq\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{4\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\|PXP\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{4\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}|X|=4\varepsilon\mathrm{Tr}|X|,

(5)

and for a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}_{j}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}|\mathcal{N}_{j}(X)|

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{N}_{j}(X)\|_{1}=\left\|\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{N}_{j}(\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\left\|\lambda_{i}\mathcal{N}_{j}(\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|)\right\|_{1}=\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}|=\mathrm{Tr}|X|.

(6)

Therefore by induction we can prove:

\displaystyle|\left\langle\phi\right|d\mathcal{P}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{P}\dots\mathcal{P}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{P}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle-1|

\displaystyle=d|\left\langle\phi\right|\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{M}\dots\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{M}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq d\frac{4\varepsilon}{d}\mathrm{Tr}|\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{M}\dots\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{M}(\rho)|

\displaystyle\leq 4\varepsilon\mathrm{Tr}|\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{m-2}\dots\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{M}(\rho)|

\displaystyle\leq(4\varepsilon)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}|\mathcal{N}_{m-2}\dots\mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{M}(\rho)|

\displaystyle\leq\left(4\varepsilon\right)^{m}

(7)

where the first inequality follows from (
3
), the second inequality follows from (
3
) and the third inequality follows from (
3
).
∎

Now we can finally upper bound the mutual information between
X
and
(I_{1},\dots,I_{N})
:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\varepsilon\leq 1/4
.
The mutual information can be upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:I_{1},\dots,I_{N})=\mathcal{O}(N\varepsilon^{2}).

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

For all
1\leq t\leq N
, we remark that

u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}=\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|d\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})-\mathds{I}\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle=\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|d\mathcal{P}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{m_{t}-1}\mathcal{P}_{x}\dots\mathcal{P}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{P}(\rho_{t})\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle-1,

3.5

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:I_{t}|I_{\leq t-1})

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq t-1}}\sum_{i_{t}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}}{d}(u_{i_{t}}^{t,x})^{2}\leq 3\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}\sum_{i_{t}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}}{d}16\varepsilon^{2}=48\varepsilon^{2}

because
\sum_{i_{t}}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}=d
. Finally:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:I_{1},\dots,I_{N})=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathcal{I}(X:I_{t}|I_{\leq t-1})=\mathcal{O}(N\varepsilon^{2}).

This concludes the proof of Lemma

∎

Using Lemma

\displaystyle\Omega(d^{2})\leq\mathcal{I}(X:I_{1},\dots,I_{N})\leq\mathcal{O}(N\varepsilon^{2}),

.
∎

The main difference between non-adaptive and adaptive strategies is that the former should choose the set of inputs, number of repetition, unital channels applied in between and the measurement devices before starting the learning procedure so that they cannot depend on the actual observations of the algorithm.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a Pauli channel and let
N
be a sufficient number of steps to learn
\mathcal{P}
as defined in (
1

At step
t\in[N]
, a non-adaptive strategy with individual measurements has the ability to choose an input quantum state
\rho_{t}
, the number
m_{t}\geq 1

\mathcal{P}
, the unital quantum channels applied in between
\mathcal{N}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{N}_{m_{t}-1}
and the POVM
\mathcal{M}_{t}
for measuring the output quantum state
\rho^{\text{output}}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{output}}_{t}=\underbrace{\mathcal{P}\circ\mathcal{N}_{m_{t}-1}\circ\mathcal{P}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{P}\circ\mathcal{N}_{1}\circ\mathcal{P}(\rho_{t})}_{\mathcal{P}\text{ is applied }m_{t}\text{ times}}.

All these elements are chosen before starting the learning procedure (see Fig.

for an illustration). By Born’s rule, performing a measurement on the output quantum state
\rho^{\text{output}}_{t}
using the POVM
\mathcal{M}_{t}=\{M^{t}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is equivalent to sampling from the probability distribution

\displaystyle x_{t}\sim\{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{\text{output}}_{t}M^{t}_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}.

The observations
(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
are used to construct a probability distribution
\hat{p}
on the set of Pauli operators
\mathds{P}_{n}
satisfying with a probability at least
2/3

\displaystyle\TV(p,\hat{p})\leq\varepsilon.

Without loss of generality, we can choose the measurement devices of the form
\mathcal{M}_{t}=\{\lambda_{i}^{t}\left|\phi_{i}^{t}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\phi_{i}^{t}\right|\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}
where
\langle\phi_{i}^{t}|\phi_{i}^{t}\rangle=1
and
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\lambda_{i}^{t}=d
.
We prove the following lower bound on the total number of measurements and steps:

Theorem 4.2
.

The problem of Pauli channel tomography using non-adaptive ancilla-free individual measurements requires a total number of channel uses satisfying:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N}m_{t}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d^{4}}{\varepsilon^{6}}\right)

or a total number of steps satisfying:

\displaystyle N\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

At a first sight we can think that this theorem is not comparable to Theorem
3.2
since we give lower bounds on different parameters. However, if we ask the algorithm to only apply the channel once per step, we obtain an improved lower bound on the number of steps required for Pauli channel tomography using non-adaptive strategies. Moreover, it shows that the upper bound of
[
1
]
is almost optimal especially if we know that the additional uses of channels at each step are only required to make the algorithm resilient to errors in SPAM.
Finally, the optimal complexity
\Theta\left(\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
for Pauli channel tomography is surprising: We are ultimately interested in learning a classical distribution on
\mathds{P}_{n}\simeq[d^{2}]
in
\TV

\Theta\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
in the usual sampling access model, so our model is strictly weaker than the usual sampling access model. Furthermore, the quantum process tomography problem has an optimal copy complexity of
\tilde{\Theta}\left(\frac{d^{6}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathcal{I}(X:I_{t}|I_{\leq t-1})\leq 3\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq t-1}}\sum_{i_{t}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}}{d}(u_{i_{t}}^{t,x})^{2}.

Since now we consider non-adaptive algorithms, this upper bound can be simplified:

\displaystyle 3\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq t-1}}\sum_{i_{t}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}}{d}(u_{i_{t}}^{t,x})^{2}=3\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x=1}^{M}\sum_{i_{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}}{d}(u_{i_{t}}^{t,x})^{2}.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{t}}{d}\left(\left\langle\phi_{i}^{t}\right|d\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})\left|\phi_{i}^{t}\right\rangle-1\right)^{2}.

\displaystyle g(\alpha_{x})=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{t}}{d}\left(\left\langle\phi_{i}^{t}\right|d\mathcal{P}_{x}^{m_{t}}(\rho_{t})\left|\phi_{i}^{t}\right\rangle-1\right)^{2}

.

Proof of Lemma

Let

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\alpha}(\left\langle\phi\right|d\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})\left|\phi\right\rangle-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{4\alpha(P)\varepsilon}{d}\left\langle\phi\right|P\rho_{t}P^{\dagger}\left|\phi\right\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\sum_{P,Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{16\alpha(P)\alpha(Q)\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\left\langle\phi\right|P\rho_{t}P^{\dagger}\left|\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\phi\right|Q\rho_{t}Q^{\dagger}\left|\phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{16\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\left\langle\phi\right|P\rho_{t}P^{\dagger}\left|\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\phi\right|P\rho_{t}P^{\dagger}\left|\phi\right\rangle-\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{16\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\left\langle\phi\right|P\rho_{t}P^{\dagger}\left|\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\phi\right|\sigma(P)\rho_{t}\sigma(P)^{\dagger}\left|\phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{16\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\left\langle\phi\right|P\rho_{t}P^{\dagger}\left|\phi\right\rangle^{2}\leq\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{16\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\left\langle\phi\right|P\rho_{t}^{2}P^{\dagger}\left|\phi\right\rangle=\frac{16\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\left\langle\phi\right|d\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{t}^{2})\mathds{I}\left|\phi\right\rangle\leq\frac{16\varepsilon^{2}}{d},

where we used
\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\alpha(P)\alpha(Q)=0
if

,

and
the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\dots\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(\rho)}_{\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\text{ is applied }m\text{ times}}=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\frac{\mathds{I}}{d}+\underbrace{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\dots\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(\rho)}_{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\text{ is applied }m\text{ times}}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(\rho)-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\frac{\mathds{I}}{d}=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{4\alpha(P)\varepsilon}{d^{2}}P\rho P=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{4\alpha(P)\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\mathcal{N}_{P}(\rho)

where we use the notation for the unital quantum channel

for all

. So, using the notation
\mathcal{N}_{P_{m},m-1,\dots,1,P_{1}}=\mathcal{N}_{P_{m}}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{N}_{P_{m-1}}\dots\mathcal{N}_{P_{2}}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{N}_{P_{1}}
, we can develop the quantity we want to upper bound as follows:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\left\langle\phi\right|d\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{m}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle-1\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\left\langle\phi\right|\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\dots\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\Bigg(\sum_{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}}\frac{4\alpha(P_{1})\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\cdots\frac{4\alpha(P_{m})\varepsilon}{d^{2}}\cdot\left\langle\phi\right|\mathcal{N}_{P_{m}}\mathcal{N}_{m-1}\mathcal{N}_{P_{m-1}}\dots\mathcal{N}_{P_{2}}\mathcal{N}_{1}\mathcal{N}_{P_{1}}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle\Bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(4\varepsilon)^{2m}}{d^{4m-2}}\sum_{P,Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}^{m}}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\alpha(P_{1})\cdots\alpha(P_{m})\alpha(Q_{1})\cdots\alpha(Q_{m})\right)\cdot\left\langle\phi\right|\mathcal{N}_{P_{m},m-1,\dots,1,P_{1}}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\phi\right|\mathcal{N}_{Q_{m},m-1,\dots,1,Q_{1}}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle.

Q_{1}\notin\left(P_{1},\sigma(P_{1}),\dots,P_{m},\sigma(P_{m})\right)
and

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\alpha(P_{1})\cdots\alpha(P_{m})\alpha(Q_{1})\cdots\alpha(Q_{m})\right)=0,

(9)

as otherwise we can upper bound each term inside the sum by

and we count the number of these terms. Moreover we can gain a factor of

by using the properties of Pauli group for

\displaystyle\frac{(4\varepsilon)^{2m}}{d^{4m-2}}\sum_{P,Q:Q_{1}=P_{1}}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\alpha(P_{1})\cdots\alpha(P_{m})\alpha(Q_{1})\cdots\alpha(Q_{m})\right)\cdot\left\langle\phi\right|\mathcal{N}_{P_{m},m-1,\dots,1,P_{1}}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\phi\right|\mathcal{N}_{Q_{m},m-1,\dots,1,Q_{1}}(\rho)\left|\phi\right\rangle

\mathcal{P}
to the input
\rho_{t+1}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}
and then performing a measurement using the POVM
\mathcal{M}_{t+1}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}
(see Fig.
4
for an illustration). In this case, it observes
i_{t+1}\in\mathcal{I}_{t+1}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}
with a probability given by Born’s rule:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{t+1}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}\lambda_{i_{t+1}}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}\left|\phi_{i_{t+1}}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\phi_{i_{t+1}}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}\right|\right)=\lambda_{i_{t+1}}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}\left\langle\phi_{i_{t+1}}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}\right|\rho_{t+1}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}\left|\phi_{i_{t+1}}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}\right\rangle.

An adaptive strategy with limited adaptivity
N_{\text{ad}}
can only adapt on the last previous
N_{\text{ad}}
observations, that is for all
t\leq N
:

\displaystyle\rho_{t+1}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}

\displaystyle=\rho_{t+1}^{I_{t-N_{\text{ad}}+1},\dots,I_{t}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t+1}^{I_{1},\dots,I_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{t+1}^{I_{t-N_{\text{ad}}+1},\dots,I_{t}}.

We prove the following lower bound on the number of steps. Note that because of the assumption
m_{t}=1
for all steps
t
, the number of steps is the same as the number of channel uses.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\leq 1/(20d)
and
d\geq 80
. Adaptive strategies for the problem of Pauli channel tomography using one copy of the channel at each step and ancilla-free individual measurements require a number of steps
N
satisfying:

\displaystyle N\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d^{5/2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

Furthermore, any adaptive strategy with limited adaptivity
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
requires a number of steps
N
satisfying

\displaystyle N\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

(10)

In this theorem, we show that we can improve on the general lower bound of Theorem
3.2
by an exponential factor of number of qubits if the precision parameter
\varepsilon
is small enough. However, this lower bound could be as well not optimal so it remains either to improve it to match the non-adaptive upper bound of
[
1
]
or to propose an adaptive algorithm with a number of steps matching this lower bound. With the same proof, we can generalize this lower bound to adaptive algorithms with limited adaptivity. Any strategy that adapts on at most
\left\lceil\frac{H}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil
previous observations for the problem of Pauli channel tomography using individual measurements requires a number of steps
N\geq\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{d^{4}}{\sqrt{H}\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d^{5}}{H\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\}\right).
For instance, if the algorithm can only adapt its input state and measurement device on the previous
\left\lceil\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil
observations then it requires
N\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
steps to correctly approximate the unknown Pauli channel.
The remaining of this section is reserved to the proof of this theorem.

Construction of the family
\mathcal{F}

We start by constructing a family of Pauli quantum channels that is
\Omega(\varepsilon)
-separated. The elements of this family have the following form, for all
x\in\mathcal{F}=[M]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{1+2\tilde{\alpha}_{x}(P)\varepsilon d/\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}}{d^{2}}P\rho P=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}p_{x}(P)P\rho P

(11)

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{x}(P)=\alpha_{x}(P)-\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha_{x}(Q)
,
\alpha_{x}=\left(\alpha_{x}(P)\right)_{P}
and
p_{x}(P)=\frac{1+2\tilde{\alpha}_{x}(P)\varepsilon d/\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}}{d^{2}}
. For
x\in\mathcal{F}
,
\left(\alpha_{x}(P)\right)_{P}
are
d^{2}
random variables i.i.d. as
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. It is not difficult to check that
\{p_{x}\}_{x}
are valid probabilities for
\varepsilon\leq 1/4d
. Indeed, for all
P\in\mathds{P}_{n}
we have
|\tilde{\alpha_{x}}(P)|\leq 2\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}
so for
\varepsilon\leq 1/4d
we have
1+2\tilde{\alpha}_{x}(P)\varepsilon d/\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}\in[0,2]
thus
p_{x}(P)\in[0,2/d^{2}]\subset[0,1)
for
d\geq 2
.

We prove the existence of the family
\mathcal{F}
by showing that two randomly chosen Pauli channels are
\Omega(\varepsilon)
-far with high probability.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\beta
be a random variable independent and identically distributed as
\alpha
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})<\varepsilon/5\right)\leq{\exp(-cd^{2})}.

for a universal constant
c>0
.

If this claim is true, then a union bound permits to show the existence of the family with the property
M=\exp(\Omega(d^{2}))
. Let us prove first a lower bound on the expected
\TV
distance between
p_{\alpha}
and
p_{\beta}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\beta
be a random variable independent and identically distributed as
\alpha
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})\right)\geq\frac{7\varepsilon}{20}.

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

We start by writing:

\displaystyle\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left|\frac{\tilde{\alpha}(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\tilde{\beta}(P)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left|\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\beta(P)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|

(12)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\left|\frac{\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(Q)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\beta(Q)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|

(13)

where we use the triangle inequality.

To bound the expectation of (
13
), we use first the fact that
\|\alpha\|_{2}
is independent of
\left(\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\right)_{P}
to show that:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\left|\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\right|\right)\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\left|\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\right|\cdot\|\alpha\|_{2}\right)=\mathds{E}\left(\left|\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)\right|\right).

Then, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\left|\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)\right|\right)\leq\sqrt{\mathds{E}\left(\left(\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)\right)^{2}\right)}=d

and by the Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}\right)\geq\sqrt{\frac{{\left(\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right)^{3}}}{\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}^{4}\right)}}=\sqrt{\frac{(d^{2})^{3}}{d^{2}(d^{2}-1)+3d^{2}}}\geq\frac{d}{2}.

Finally, we can upper bound the expectation of (
13
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\left|\frac{\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(Q)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\beta(Q)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|\right)\leq 2\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\left|\frac{\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\right|\right)=\frac{2\varepsilon}{d}\frac{\mathds{E}\left(\left|\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)\right|\right)}{\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}\right)}\leq\frac{4\varepsilon}{d}.

(14)

We move to lower bound the expectation of (
12
). First, using the fact that
\|\alpha\|_{2}
is independent of
\left(\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\right)_{P}
we have:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\alpha(P)^{2}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}}\right)\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\mathds{E}\left(\alpha(P)^{2}\right)

which implies:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\left|\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\beta(P)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\alpha(P)^{2}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}}\right)-2\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\alpha(P)\beta(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}\|\beta\|_{2}}\right)=2\frac{\mathds{E}\left(\alpha(P)^{2}\right)}{\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}\right)}=\frac{2}{d^{2}}.

(15)

Similarly, using the fact that
\|\alpha\|_{2}
is independent of
\left(\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\right)_{P}
we have:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\alpha(P)^{4}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}^{4}}\right)\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}^{4}\right)=\mathds{E}\left(\alpha(P)^{4}\right).

This equality together with the Hölder’s inequality (or successive Cauchy-Schwarz inequality) imply:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\left|\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\beta(P)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|^{4}\right)\leq 16\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\alpha(P)^{4}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}^{4}}\right)=16\frac{\mathds{E}\left(\alpha(P)^{4}\right)}{\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}^{4}\right)}=\frac{48}{d^{2}(d^{2}-1)+3d^{2}}\leq\frac{48}{d^{4}}.

(16)

Using the inequalities (
15
) and (
16
) and the Hölder’s inequality we obtain the following lower bound on the expectation of (
12
):

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left|\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\beta(P)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|\right)=\varepsilon d\mathds{E}\left(\left|\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\beta(P)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon d\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(\left|\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\beta(P)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|^{2}\right)\right)^{3/2}}{\left(\mathds{E}\left(\left|\frac{\alpha(P)}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}-\frac{\beta(P)}{\|\beta\|_{2}}\right|^{4}\right)\right)^{1/2}}\geq\varepsilon d\sqrt{\frac{8/d^{6}}{48/d^{4}}}\geq\frac{2\varepsilon}{5}.

(17)

Therefore, using the inequalities (
12
), (
14
) and (
5
), the expected value of the
\TV
-distance satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})\right)\geq\frac{2\varepsilon}{5}-\frac{4\varepsilon}{d}\geq\frac{7\varepsilon}{20}\quad\text{ for }d\geq 80.

∎

Once we have a lower bound on the expected value of
\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})
, we can proceed to prove Lemma
5.2
.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

We want to show that the function
\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})
concentrates around its mean. Let
{(\gamma,\delta)}\in\left(\mathds{R}^{d^{2}}\right)^{2}
and
{(\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})}\in\left(\mathds{R}^{d^{2}}\right)^{2}
be two couples of vectors.
By the reverse triangle inequality we have:

\displaystyle|\TV(p_{\gamma},p_{\delta})-\TV(p_{\gamma^{\prime}},p_{\delta^{\prime}})|

\displaystyle\leq|\TV(p_{\gamma},p_{\delta})-\TV(p_{\gamma^{\prime}},p_{\delta})|+|\TV(p_{\gamma^{\prime}},p_{\delta})-\TV(p_{\gamma^{\prime}},p_{\delta^{\prime}})|

\displaystyle\leq\TV(p_{\gamma},p_{\gamma^{\prime}})+\TV(p_{\delta},p_{\delta^{\prime}}).

Define
E\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathds{R}^{d^{2}}:\|\gamma\|_{2}>d/4\}

and consider the case where
(\gamma,\delta)\in E^{2}
. Recall the definition for all
P\in\mathds{P}_{n}
:

\displaystyle p_{\gamma}(P)=\frac{1+2\tilde{\gamma}(P)\varepsilon d/\|\gamma\|_{2}}{d^{2}},

where
\tilde{\gamma}(P)=\gamma(P)-\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\gamma(Q).

We have by the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\TV(p_{\gamma},p_{\delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left|\frac{1+2\tilde{\gamma}(P)\varepsilon d/\|\gamma\|_{2}}{d^{2}}-\frac{1+2\tilde{\delta}(P)\varepsilon d/\|\delta\|_{2}}{d^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left|\frac{\gamma(P)}{\|\gamma\|_{2}}-\frac{\delta(P)}{\|\delta\|_{2}}\right|+\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\left|\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{\gamma(P)}{\|\gamma\|_{2}}-\frac{\delta(P)}{\|\delta\|_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left|\frac{\gamma(P)}{\|\gamma\|_{2}}-\frac{\gamma(P)}{\|\delta\|_{2}}\right|+\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left|\frac{\gamma(P)}{\|\delta\|_{2}}-\frac{\delta(P)}{\|\delta\|_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{d}\|\gamma\|_{1}\frac{\left|\|\gamma\|_{2}-\|\delta\|_{2}\right|}{\|\gamma\|_{2}\|\delta\|_{2}}+\frac{2\varepsilon}{d}\frac{\|\gamma-\delta\|_{1}}{\|\delta\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon\|\gamma\|_{2}\frac{\|\gamma-\delta\|_{2}}{\|\gamma\|_{2}\|\delta\|_{2}}+2\varepsilon\frac{\|\gamma-\delta\|_{2}}{\|\delta\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon\frac{4}{d}\|\gamma-\delta\|_{2}+2\varepsilon\|\gamma-\delta\|_{2}\frac{4}{d}=\frac{16\varepsilon}{d}\|\gamma-\delta\|_{2}.

Here we used that
\|\gamma\|_{1}\leq d\|\gamma\|_{2}
, as
\gamma
is a vector with
d^{2}
entries, and our assumption on the norms in the last inequality.
Hence, on the set
E^{2}
, by using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle|\TV(p_{\gamma},p_{\delta})-\TV(p_{\gamma^{\prime}},p_{\delta^{\prime}})|

\displaystyle\leq\TV(p_{\gamma},p_{\gamma^{\prime}})+\TV(p_{\delta},p_{\delta^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16\varepsilon}{d}\|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\|_{2}+\frac{16\varepsilon}{d}\|\delta-\delta^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}{d}\sqrt{\|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\delta-\delta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=:L\|(\gamma,\delta)-(\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\|_{2}.

Moreover, the function
(\gamma,\delta)\mapsto\TV(p_{\gamma},p_{\delta})
can be extended to an
L
-Lipschitz function with respect to the
2
-norm on the whole set
\mathds{R}^{d^{2}}\times\mathds{R}^{d^{2}}
using the following definition for every
(\gamma,\delta)\in\mathds{R}^{d^{2}}\times\mathds{R}^{d^{2}}
(Kirszbraun theorem, see App.
A.2
):

\displaystyle f(\gamma,\delta)=\inf_{(\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\in E^{2}}\left\{\TV(p_{\gamma^{\prime}},p_{\delta^{\prime}})+L\|(\gamma,\delta)-(\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\|_{2}\right\}.

Now consider
(\alpha,\beta)
as a couple of standard Gaussian vectors.
We can control the expected value of
f(\alpha,\beta)
using the lower bound on the expected value of
\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})
(Lemma
5.3
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(f(\alpha,\beta)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(f{\mathbb{1}_{E^{2}}}(\alpha,\beta)\right)+\mathds{E}\left(f{\mathbb{1}_{(E^{2})^{c}}}(\alpha,\beta)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{E}\left(f{\mathbb{1}_{E^{2}}}(\alpha,\beta)\right)\geq\frac{7\varepsilon}{20}-8\varepsilon{\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{32}\right)}\geq\frac{3\varepsilon}{10}

because when
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{1}_{E^{2}}
, we have
f(\alpha,\beta)=\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})
thus

\displaystyle|\mathds{E}\left(f{\mathbb{1}_{E^{2}}}(\alpha,\beta)\right)-\mathds{E}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})\right)|

\displaystyle={|\mathds{E}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta}){\mathbb{1}_{E^{2}}(\alpha,\beta)}\right)-\mathds{E}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})\right)|}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta}){\mathbb{1}_{(E^{2})^{c}}}(\alpha,\beta)\right)\leq 8\varepsilon\mathds{P}\left(E^{c}\right)\leq 8\varepsilon{\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{32}\right)}

where we have used the fact that
\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})\leq 4\varepsilon
and
\mathds{P}\left(E^{c}\right)=\mathds{P}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}\leq d/4\right)\leq{\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{32}\right)}
. Indeed, we can apply the concentration of Lipschitz functions of Gaussian random variables (see App.
A.3
) for the function
\alpha\rightarrow\|\alpha\|_{2}
which is
1
-Lipschitz by the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle|\|\alpha\|_{2}-\|\beta\|_{2}|\leq\|\alpha-\beta\|_{2}

and its expectation satisfies

\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}\right)\geq d/2
, thus:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(E^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}\leq d/4\right)=\mathds{P}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}-\mathds{E}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}\right)\leq-d/4\right)\leq{\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{32}\right)}.

We proceed with the same strategy for the function
f
which is
L
-Lipschitz where
L=\frac{16\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}{d}
. By the concentration of Lipschitz functions of Gaussian random variables (see App.
A.3
), we obtain for all
s\geq 0:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(|f(\alpha,\beta)-\mathds{E}\left(f(\alpha,\beta)\right)|>s\right)\leq{2\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}s^{2}}{2^{10}\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}

Then, we can deduce the upper bound on the probability:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})<\varepsilon/5\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})<\varepsilon/5,(\alpha,\beta)\in E^{2}\right)+\mathds{P}\left(\TV(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})<\varepsilon/5,(\alpha,\beta)\notin E^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(f(\alpha,\beta)<\varepsilon/5,(\alpha,\beta)\in E^{2}\right)+\mathds{P}\left((\alpha,\beta)\notin E^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(f(\alpha,\beta)-\mathds{E}\left(f\right)<-\varepsilon/10\right)+2\mathds{P}\left(\alpha\notin E\right)

\displaystyle\leq{6\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{2^{10}\cdot 10^{2}}\right)}\leq\exp(-{cd^{2}})

for a universal constant
c>0
.
∎

Hence we construct an
\varepsilon/5
-separated family
\mathcal{F}
of cardinality
\exp(\Omega(d^{2}))
. By changing
\varepsilon\leftrightarrow 5\varepsilon
in the definition of
\{\mathcal{P}_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{F}}
, the family becomes
\varepsilon
-separated for
\varepsilon\leq 1/(20d)
and
d\geq 80
.

Once the family
\mathcal{F}
is constructed, we can use it to encode a message in
[M]
to the sequence of outcomes produced by the learning algorithm
when provided with the quantum Pauli channel
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{x}
in the family
\mathcal{F}
.
More precisely, the learning algorithm
chooses its inputs states, performs adaptive individual measurements, and
observes a sequence of outcomes that will be transmitted to the decoder.
Upon receiving this sequence of outcomes, the decoder runs the data-processing part of the learning algorithm to learn the Pauli channel
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{x}
.
Therefore a
1/3
-correct algorithm can decode with a probability of failure at most
1/3
by finding the closest quantum Pauli channel in the family
\mathcal{F}
to the channel approximated by the algorithm. By Fano’s inequality, the encoder and decoder should share at least
\Omega(\log(M))\geq\Omega(d^{2})
nats of information. More precisely, if we denote by
X
the uniform random variable on the set
[M]
representing the message being encoded and
I_{1},\dots,I_{N}
the sequence of outcomes produced by the data-acquisition part of the learning algorithm, we have:

Lemma 5.4
.

The mutual information between the encoder and the outcomes produced by the learning algorithm is at least

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}(X:I_{1},\dots,I_{N})}\geq{(2/3)}\log(M)-\log(2)\geq\Omega(d^{2}).

This lemma is similar to Lemma
3.4
. However, the constructions we use to prove these lemmas as well as the type of algorithms (or the distributions of the outcomes) are quite different.

Upper bound on the mutual information

Since we have a lower bound on the mutual information, it remains to prove an upper bound depending on the number of steps
N
and the precision
\varepsilon
.
By upper bounding the mutual information between
X
and
I_{1},\dots,I_{N}
and using a contradiction argument, we prove Theorem
5.1
which we recall:

Theorem 5.5

(Restatement of Theorem
5.1
)
.

Let
\varepsilon\leq 1/(20d)
and
d\geq 80
. Adaptive strategies for the problem of Pauli channel tomography using one copy of the channel at each step and ancilla-free individual measurements require a number of steps
N
satisfying:

\displaystyle N\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d^{5/2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

Furthermore, any adaptive strategy with limited adaptivity
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
requires a number of steps
N
satisfying

\displaystyle N\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

For a random vector
Y\coloneqq(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{M})
, we use the notation
\mathds{E}_{x}(Y)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x=1}^{M}Y_{x}
. For
k\in[N]
and a random vector
Y
indexed by
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}
, we use the notation
\mathds{E}_{i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}(Y)=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\right)\cdot Y_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}
.
Recall that we can write the mutual information as:
\mathcal{I}(X:I_{1},\dots,I_{N})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{\leq k-1})
.
Fix
k\in[N]
, by Lemma
3.5
, we can upper bound the conditional mutual information:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{\leq k-1})\leq 3\mathds{E}_{x}\mathds{E}_{i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}\Bigg[\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}\Bigg],

(18)

where we use the notation

\displaystyle u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|\left(d\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho_{k})-\mathds{I}\right)\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|\left(\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{2\tilde{\alpha}_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}}P\rho_{k}P\right)\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{2\alpha_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}}\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|P\rho_{k}P\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle-\sum_{P,Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{2\alpha_{x}(Q)\varepsilon}{d^{2}\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}}\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|P\rho_{k}P\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{2\alpha_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}}\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|P\rho_{k}P\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle-\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{2\alpha_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{d\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}}.

Note that for adaptive strategies the vectors
\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle=\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})\right\rangle
and the states
\rho_{k}=\rho_{k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
depend on the previous observations
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
for all
k\in[N]
. Similarly, for a vector
\alpha=\left(\alpha(P)\right)_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}
we denote:

\displaystyle u_{i_{k}}^{k,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{2\alpha(P)\varepsilon}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|P\rho_{k}P\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle-\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{2\alpha(P)\varepsilon}{d\|\alpha\|_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)\varepsilon\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|P(\rho_{k}-\mathds{I}/d)P\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle.

We have
\sum_{i_{k}}\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}u_{i_{k}}^{k,x}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(d\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho_{k})-\mathds{I}\right)=0
as the Pauli channel
\mathcal{P}_{x}
is trace preserving.

Our goal is to bound the expectation in (
18
). Since
x\sim\unif[M]
and
(\alpha_{x})_{x}
are random variables i.i.d. as
\alpha
, we can see the RHS of (
18
) as an empirical mean.
Note that the cardinality of the constructed family
M=|\mathcal{F}|
is of order
\exp(\Omega(d^{2}))
, so every empirical mean
\mathds{E}_{x}g(\alpha_{x})
of a bounded function
g
can be approximated by the expected value
\mathds{E}g(\alpha)
with
\alpha
following the distribution explained in the construction, the difference will be, by Hoeffding’s inequality, of order
\exp(-\Omega(d^{2}))
so negligible.
More formally, in Proposition
B.1
, it is shown that there is a universal constant
C>0
such that with probability at least
9/10
we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x=1}^{M}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}}{d}\right)\right)\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\Bigg[\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}}{d}\right)\right)\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,\alpha})^{2}\Bigg]+N\varepsilon^{2}\exp(-Cd^{2}).

(19)

The proof relies on Hoeffding’s inequality applied on the random variable

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}}{d}\right)\right)\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}

that is bounded by
16N\varepsilon^{2}
(as
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i_{t}}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}(u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha})^{2}\leq 16\varepsilon^{2}
, see Lemma
5.6
). So the random variable

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}}{d}\right)\right)\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}

is essentially an empirical mean of i.i.d. bounded random variables.

Now since the inequality (
5
) holds with probability at least
9/10
, we can ask in our construction (
11
) that the random vectors
(\alpha_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{F}}
satisfy also the inequality (
5
). The existence of such family is guaranteed by the union bound as the total error probability is at most
1/10+{\exp(-cd^{2})}<1
(Lemma
5.2
).

Therefore, using the inequality (
5
), we obtain the upper bound on the mutual information:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{\leq k-1})

\displaystyle\leq 3\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{E}_{x,i\sim q_{\leq k-1}}\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}

\displaystyle=3\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x=1}^{M}\!\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\!\left(\!\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}}{d}\right)\!\right)\!\sum_{i_{k}}\!\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\Bigg[\!\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\!\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\!\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}}{d}\right)\!\right)\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,\alpha})^{2}\Bigg]+3N\varepsilon^{2}\exp(-Cd^{2})

\displaystyle=3\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{E}_{\leq k}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\Bigg[\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\left(1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}\right)\right)(u_{i_{k}}^{k,\alpha})^{2}\Bigg]+3N\varepsilon^{2}\exp(-Cd^{2})

where we use the notation
{\mathds{E}_{\leq k}[Y(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})]=\frac{1}{d^{k}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}{\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\right)}Y(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}
.
Observe that for non-adaptive strategies, we can simplify these large products using the fact
u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}
does not depend on
(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{t-1})
.
We obtain in this case an upper bound on the mutual information:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(X:I_{1},\dots,I_{N})\!

\displaystyle\leq\!3\!\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{E}_{k,\alpha}\!\Big[(u_{i_{k}}^{k,\alpha})^{2}\Big]\!+\!3N\varepsilon^{2}\exp(-Cd^{2}),

where we use the notation
{\mathds{E}_{k}[Y(i_{k})]=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i_{k}}\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}Y(i_{k})}
.
For this expression, using methods similar to the proof of Theorem
4.2
, one can obtain a bound of the form

\Omega(\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}})
as in Theorem
4.2
. However, for adaptive strategies, we can not simplify the terms

for
t<k
because
(u_{i_{k}}^{k,\alpha})^{2}
depends on the previous observations
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})

A.5
) to break the dependency between the variables in the last expectation. Recall that for all
t,i_{t}
,
\tilde{\rho_{t}}=\rho_{t}-\mathds{I}/d
and:

\displaystyle u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)\varepsilon\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|P\tilde{\rho}_{t}P\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle.

Using the fact that
\|\alpha\|_{2}

\alpha

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}\right)\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\left(1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}\right)\right)(u_{i_{k}}^{k,\alpha})^{2}\right)

(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\alpha(P)F(\alpha)\right)

(L1)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)\frac{1}{d}\sum_{R\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}(R\tilde{\rho_{t}})PRP\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)\frac{1}{d}\sum_{R\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}(R\tilde{\rho_{t}})(-1)^{R{\circ}P}R\right)^{2}

where we used that

. Now we expand the square, use

and Lemma

to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{t}}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}(u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}}\!\sum_{P,P^{\prime},R\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\!\alpha(P)\alpha(P^{\prime})\frac{1}{d}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(R\tilde{\rho_{t}})^{2}(-1)^{R{\circ}P}(-1)^{R{\circ}P^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}^{2}}\sum_{R\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\alpha(P)(-1)^{R{\circ}P}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{d}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(R\tilde{\rho_{t}})^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(M_{s,k}M_{t,k})=d\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{k}Q)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s}Q)\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{t}Q)\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|Q\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|Q\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{k-1}\frac{M_{s,k}}{(1+u_{i_{s}}^{s,\alpha})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,t<k\\
Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}\end{subarray}}\!d\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{k}Q)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s}Q)\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{t}Q)\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|\!Q\!\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle\left\langle\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right|\!Q\!\left|\phi_{i_{t}}^{t}\right\rangle}{(1+u_{i_{s}}^{s,\alpha})(1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha})}

\displaystyle=d\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{k}Q)^{2}\left(\sum_{s<k}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s}Q)\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|Q\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle}{(1+u_{i_{s}}^{s,\alpha})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4d\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\left(\sum_{s<k}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s}Q)\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|Q\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle}{(1+u_{i_{s}}^{s,\alpha})}\right)^{2}.

Note that this step is crucial because
\rho_{k}
depends on
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
so we need to avoid it in order to simplify with the expectations
\mathds{E}_{t}
for
t<k
. When we want to simplify the expectation

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\leq k-1}{\mathds{E}_{\alpha}}\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(\sum_{s=1}^{k-1}\frac{M_{s,k}}{(1+u_{i_{s}}^{s,\alpha})}\right)^{2}\right)\prod_{t\leq k-1}\left(1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4d\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathds{E}_{\leq k-1}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\Bigg(\sum_{s_{1}=1}^{k-1}\sum_{s_{2}=1}^{k-1}\bigg(\prod_{t\leq k-1}\left(1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}\right)\bigg)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s_{1}}Q)\left\langle\phi_{i_{s_{1}}}^{s_{1}}\right|Q\left|\phi_{i_{s_{1}}}^{s_{1}}\right\rangle}{(1+u_{i_{s_{1}}}^{s_{1},\alpha})}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s_{2}}Q)\left\langle\phi_{i_{s_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\right|Q\left|\phi_{i_{s_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\right\rangle}{(1+u_{i_{s_{2}}}^{s_{2},\alpha})}\Bigg),

we can see that if
s_{1}<s_{2}
(or
s_{1}>s_{2}
), we will get
0
because we can simplify the terms
(1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha})
in the product for
t>s_{2}
, the term
(1+u_{i_{s_{2}}}^{s_{2},\alpha})
is simplified with the denominator so we can take safely the expectation under
\mathds{E}_{s_{2}}
:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{s_{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s_{2}}Q)\left\langle\phi_{i_{s_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\right|Q\left|\phi_{i_{s_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i_{s_{2}}}\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s_{2}}Q)\lambda_{i_{s_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\left\langle\phi_{i_{s_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\right|Q\left|\phi_{i_{s_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\right\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s_{2}}Q)\mathrm{Tr}(Q)=0

because
\mathrm{Tr}(Q)=0
unless
Q=\mathds{I}
for which
\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s_{2}}Q)=\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s_{2}})=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{s_{2}}-\mathds{I}/d)=0
.
Therefore

(
L2
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\mathds{E}_{\leq k-1}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{k-1}\frac{M_{s,k}}{(1+u_{i_{s}}^{s,\alpha})}\right)^{2}\cdot\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}\right]\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\!\frac{8\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\!\sqrt{\!\mathds{E}_{<k}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\!\left[\!\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\!\left(\!\sum_{s<k}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s}Q)\!\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|\!Q\!\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle}{(1+u_{i_{s}}^{s,\alpha})}\!\right)^{2}\!\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}\!\right]\!\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\!\frac{32\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\!\sqrt{\sum_{s<k}\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathds{E}_{\leq s}\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{s}Q)^{2}\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|\!Q\!\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle^{2}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\Phi_{s}^{\alpha}\right)\!\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\!\frac{{64}\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\sqrt{\!\sum_{s<k}\!\mathds{E}_{\leq s}\!\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\!\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|\!Q\!\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|\!Q\!\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle\!\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\!\left(\Phi_{s}^{\alpha}\right)\!\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{=}\!\frac{{64}\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{s<k}\mathds{E}_{\leq s}\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|d\mathrm{Tr}(\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right|)\mathds{I}\left|\phi_{i_{s}}^{s}\right\rangle\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left(\Phi_{s}^{\alpha}\right)\!\Psi_{k}}

where in

we used

; in
(b)

; in

we used Lemma

We have proven so far, for all

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}\leq\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{d}+256\sqrt{k}\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{d^{2}}+\frac{{64}\varepsilon^{2}}{d\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{k}\sqrt{\Psi_{k}}.

(21)

The first term of the upper bound can be seen as a non-adaptive contribution. The second one can be thought as a geometric mean of the first and third terms. The last term represents essentially the contribution of the adaptivity. Our final stage of the proof is to use these recurrence inequalities to prove the lower bound by a contradiction argument.

Recall that
\Psi_{k}=\mathds{E}_{\leq k}\mathds{E}_{\alpha}\left[\left(u_{i_{k}}^{k,\alpha}\right)^{2}\prod_{t<k}\left(1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}\right)\right]
and
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{I}(X:I_{k}|I_{<k})\leq 3\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Psi_{k}+3N\varepsilon^{2}\exp(-Cd^{2})
.
We suppose that
N\leq c\frac{d^{5/2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}
for sufficiently small
c>0
. We know that from Lemma
5.4
and Lemma
3.5

\displaystyle c_{0}d^{2}\leq\mathcal{I}(X:Y)\leq 3\sum_{k\leq N}\Psi_{k}+3N\varepsilon^{2}\exp(-Cd^{2}).

So
\sum_{k\leq N}\Psi_{k}\geq c^{\prime}d^{2}
(for example
c^{\prime}=c_{0}/4
), on the other hand the inequality (
21
) implies:

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\Psi_{k}

\displaystyle\leq{\sum_{k}\left(\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{d}+256\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{d^{2}}\sqrt{k}+{64}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\sqrt{k}}{d\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{\Psi_{k}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{d}+256\frac{N\varepsilon^{3}}{d^{2}}\sqrt{N}+{64}\sum_{k}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\sqrt{k}}{d\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{c^{\prime}}d^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{k\leq N}\Psi_{k}}+256\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\sqrt{cd^{5/2}}+{64}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{d\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{\sum_{k}k}\sqrt{\sum_{k}\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{8}{\sqrt{c^{\prime}d}}+\frac{512}{\sqrt{c^{\prime}}d^{1/4}}+{64}\right)\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{d\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{\sum_{k}k}\sqrt{\sum_{k}\Psi_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{d\sqrt{d}}N\sqrt{\sum_{k}\Psi_{k}}

where in the third inequality we use
\sum_{k\leq N}\Psi_{k}\geq c^{\prime}d^{2}
,
N\leq c\frac{d^{5/2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}
and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and in the last inequality
C^{\prime}
is a universal constant. Therefore:

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\Psi_{k}\leq C^{\prime 2}\left(\frac{N^{2}\varepsilon^{4}}{d^{3}}\right)\leq C^{\prime 2}c^{2}d^{2}.

Hence

\displaystyle c_{0}d^{2}\leq\mathcal{I}(X:Y)\leq\sum_{k\leq N}3\Psi_{k}+3N\varepsilon^{2}\exp(-Cd^{2})\leq 6C^{\prime 2}c^{2}d^{2}

which gives the contradiction for
c\ll\sqrt{c_{0}}/C^{\prime}
. Finally we deduce
N\geq\Omega(d^{5/2}/\varepsilon^{2})
and we conclude the proof of the first lower bound of Theorem
5.1
.

If the adaptive algorithm can only adapt on the last
\mathcal{O}(H/\varepsilon^{2})
observations, the previous inequalities imply for all
1\leq k\leq N
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\Psi_{k}

\displaystyle\leq{\sum_{k}\left(\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{d}+256\sqrt{H}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}+\frac{{64}\varepsilon}{d\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{H}\sqrt{\Psi_{k}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq{\sum_{k}\left(\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{d}+256\sqrt{H}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}+\frac{({64}\varepsilon)^{2}H}{d^{3}}+\frac{\Psi_{k}}{2}\right)}

where we use AM-GM inequality, hence we deduce:

\displaystyle c_{0}d^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}(X:Y)\leq\sum_{k}3\Psi_{k}+3N\varepsilon^{2}\exp(-Cd^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{30\varepsilon^{2}N}{d}+6\cdot 256\sqrt{H}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}N}{d^{2}}+\frac{6\cdot({64}\varepsilon)^{2}HN}{d^{3}}

and finally we obtain:

\displaystyle N\geq\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{d^{4}}{\sqrt{H}\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d^{5}}{H\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\}\right).

For
H=\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
, this gives (
10
) and we conclude the proof of the second lower bound of Theorem
5.1
.
∎

6
Conclusion and open problems

We have provided lower bounds for Pauli channel tomography in the diamond norm using ancilla-free independent strategies for both adaptive and non-adaptive strategies. In particular, we have shown that the number of measurements should be at least
\Omega(d^{3}/\varepsilon^{2})
in the non-adaptive setting and
\Omega(d^{2.5}/\varepsilon^{2})
in the adaptive setting. We would like to finish with three interesting directions.
Finding the optimal complexity of Pauli channel tomography using adaptive individual measurements remains an open question. We conjecture this complexity to be
\Theta(d^{3}/\varepsilon^{2})
since we remark that in many situations the adaptive strategies cannot overcome the non-adaptive ones. Furthermore, we already obtained a
\Theta(d^{3}/\varepsilon^{2})
bound for adaptive strategies in the high precision and limited adaptivity regime, further evidence of this bound.
Moreover, since
[
21
]
established the optimal complexity for estimating the eigenvalues of a Pauli channel in the
l_{\infty}
-norm using ancilla-assisted non-adaptive independent strategies, it would be interesting to find the optimal complexity to learn a Pauli channel in the diamond norm when the algorithm can use
k
-qubit ancilla for
k\leq n
. Finally, it should be noted that all of the channel constructions used in this work have a very large spectral gap, i.e., are very noisy. It would be interesting to study the sample complexity of Pauli channel tomography in terms of the spectral gap as well.

Appendix A

A.1
Pauli group properties

In this section, we group some useful properties about the Pauli operators that we need for the proofs in this article.

Lemma A.1
.

We have for all
Q\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}(-1)^{P{\circ}Q}=d^{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}_{Q=\mathds{I}}.

Proof.

It is clear that for
Q=\mathds{I}
,
Q
commutes with every
P\in\mathds{P}_{n}
and thus the equality holds. Now, let
Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}\setminus\{\mathds{I}\}
and we write
Q=Q_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes Q_{n}
where for all
i\in[n]
,
Q_{i}\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}
is a Pauli matrix. By the same decomposition for
P\in\mathds{P}_{n}
, we can write:

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}(-1)^{P{\circ}Q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}}(-1)^{P_{1}{\circ}Q_{1}+_{2}\dots+_{2}P_{n}{\circ}Q_{n}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{P_{i}\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}}(-1)^{P_{i}{\circ}Q_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}4\mathbb{1}_{Q_{i}=\mathds{I}_{2}}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\mathbb{1}_{Q=\mathds{I}_{d}}

where we have used in the third equality the fact that every non identity Pauli matrix
Q_{i}
commutes only with the identity and itself (so it anti-commutes with the two other Pauli matrices) thus the sum
\sum_{P_{i}\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}}(-1)^{P_{i}{\circ}Q_{i}}=0
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

We have for all matrices
\rho
:

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}P\rho P=d\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\mathds{I}.

Proof.

Let
d=2^{n}
and
\rho\in\mathds{C}^{d\times d}
. It is known that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
forms an ortho-normal basis of
\mathds{C}^{d\times d}
for the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. Thus, we can write
\rho
in this basis:

\displaystyle\rho\!=\!\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\!\mathrm{Tr}\left(\!\frac{P}{\sqrt{d}}\rho\!\right)\!\frac{P}{\sqrt{d}}\!=\!\frac{1}{d}\!\sum_{P\in\{\mathds{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}\!\mathrm{Tr}\left(P\rho\right)\!P.

Therefore we can simplify the LHS by using the identity
PQ=(-1)^{P{\circ}Q}QP
for all
P,Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}P\rho P

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{P,Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}(Q\rho)PQP

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{P,Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}(Q\rho)(-1)^{P{\circ}Q}QPP

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}(Q\rho)Q\frac{1}{d}\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}(-1)^{P{\circ}Q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}(Q\rho)Q\cdot d\cdot\mathbb{1}_{Q=\mathds{I}}

\displaystyle=d\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\mathds{I},

where we have used Lemma
A.1
to obtain the fourth equality.
∎

In this section, we give the proof of an approximation used in the proof of Theorem

Proposition B.1
.

There is a universal constant
C>0
such that with probability at least
9/10
we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x=1}^{M}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}}{d}\right)\right)\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}

Let

x\in[M]

\displaystyle f_{k}(x)=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}}{d}\right)\right)\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}.

where we recall that the random variable

\displaystyle u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}=\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{2\alpha_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}}\left\langle\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right|P\rho_{k}P\left|\phi_{i_{k}}^{k}\right\rangle-\sum_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}\frac{2\alpha_{x}(P)\varepsilon}{d\|\alpha_{x}\|_{2}}.

\alpha_{x}(P)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

\alpha=\left(\alpha(P)\right)_{P\in\mathds{P}_{n}}

\displaystyle f_{k}(\alpha)\coloneqq\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,\alpha}}{d}\right)\right)\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,\alpha})^{2}.

Now define the random variables

\displaystyle F(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}f_{k}(x)\quad\text{and }\quad F(\alpha)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}f_{k}(\alpha).

We want to show a concentration of the random variable
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x=1}^{M}F(x)
around its mean
\mathds{E}_{\alpha}(F(\alpha))
. If the random variables
\left(F(x)\right)_{x}

\displaystyle f_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}}{d}\right)\right)\sum_{i_{k}}\frac{\lambda_{i_{k}}^{k}}{d}(u_{i_{k}}^{k,x})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{i_{t}}^{t}\left(\frac{1+u_{i_{t}}^{t,x}}{d}\right)\right)16\varepsilon^{2}=16\varepsilon^{2}

which implies an upper bound on the random variable

\displaystyle 0\leq F(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}f_{k}(x)\leq 16N\varepsilon^{2}.
