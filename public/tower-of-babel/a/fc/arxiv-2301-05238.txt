Quantum criticality under decoherence or weak measurement
Jong Yeon Lee, Chao-Ming Jian, Cenke Xu

Abstract
Decoherence inevitably happens when a quantum state is exposed to its environment, which can affect quantum critical points (QCP) in a nontrivial way. As was pointed out in recent literature on ( 1 + 1 ) ​ d (1+1)d conformal field theory (CFT) Garratt et al. 2022 , the effect of weak measurement can be mathematically mapped to the problem of boundary CFT. In this work, we focus on the ( 2 + 1 ) ​ d (2+1)d QCPs, whose boundary and defect effects have attracted enormous theoretical and numerical interests very recently. We focus on decoherence caused by weak measurements with and without postselecting the measurement outcomes. Our main results are: (1) for an O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N) Wilson-Fisher QCP under weak measurement with postselection, an observer would in general observe two different types of boundary/defect criticality with very different behaviors from the well-known Wilson-Fisher fixed points; in particular, it is possible to observe the recently proposed exotic “ extraordinary-log ” correlation. (2) An extra quantum phase transition can be driven by decoherence, if we consider quantities nonlinear with the decohered density matrix, such as the Renyi entropy. We demonstrate the connection between this transition to the information-theoretic transition driven by an error in the toric code model. (3) When there is no postselection, though correlation functions between local operators remain the same as the undecohered pure state, nonlocal operators such as the “ disorder operator ” would have qualitatively distinct behaviors; and we also show that the decoherence can lead to confinement.



I
Introduction

When a quantum state is exposed to an environment, it is being constantly probed and “measured”, forming entanglement with the degrees of freedom in the environment. If one is ignorant of the environment and the measurement outcome is lost, it amounts to tracing out the environment’s degrees of freedom and the original pure quantum state becomes a mixed state, which results in the loss of coherent quantum information. This process is referred to as quantum decoherence, and it is the bridge between the quantum mechanics that governs the microscopic nature, and our classical macroscopic world
Zurek 2003
. More generally, if a quantum state is weakly measured and the measurement outcome is still accessible, one can consider the effect of postselecting the measurement outcome and study process that is more general than decoherence. In recent years there has been a surge of progress in simulating quantum states of matter with nontrivial entanglement on platforms summarized as the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) technology
Preskill 2018
, including simulating exotic quantum many-body states such as topological order, spin liquids, and symmetry protected topological (SPT) states
Song et al. 2018
;
Satzinger et al. 2021
;
de Léséleuc et al. 2019
;
Keesling et al. 2019
;
Semeghini et al. 2021
, which have long been discussed in condensed-matter physics. In these platforms, decoherence can happen due to various reasons, which motivated recent inspection of the fate of SPT states under decoherence
Lee et al. 2022a
;
Zhu et al. 2022
;
de Groot et al. 2022
;
Lee et al. 2022b
(related studies motivated from other contexts were also conducted
Ma and Wang 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
).

Quantum criticality represents another class of quantum many-body states with peculiar and universal entanglement. Recently a class of
(1+1)d
conformal field theory (CFT), i.e., the Luttinger liquid under weak measurements has been studied, and it was pointed out that the effect of weak measurements can be mathematically mapped to the problem of the boundary of the CFT
Garratt et al. 2022
, which is a subject that was studied extensively in the past
Kane and Fisher 1992
. The same trick was used in the recent study of SPT states under decoherence
Lee et al. 2022b
, which exploited the observation that the wave function of the SPT states can be mapped to the partition function of the boundary states of the system after a space-time rotation
Xu and Senthil 2013
;
You et al. 2014
.

In this work, we focus on quantum critical points in
(2+1)d
. The connection between the decohered QCPs (or QCPs under weak measurement) in the bulk and the boundary criticality still holds, but the boundary criticality of
(2+1)d
QCPs is a subject that has only been carefully studied very recently, and it has attracted enormous interests from both the theoretical and numerical communities

(2+1)d

3d

Our current work will bridge these two directions that are under active studying, and we will demonstrate that a
(2+1)d
QCP under decoherence or weak measurements naturally exhibits the peculiar boundary criticality studied recently. This work is organized as follows:

In section
II
, we develop the general formalism of
analyzing decohered quantum critical states, including the general connection between the decohered or weakly measured bulk state and the boundary/defect criticality.

In section
III
we discuss the
(2+1)d

\mathrm{O}(N)
Wilson-Fisher quantum critical points under decoherence or weak measurements, and in section
III.1
we focus on the quantities linear with the density matrix, which can be observed directly in experiments. We demonstrate that weak measurements generally render the observed quantities rather different from the bulk QCP (with certain postselection that preserves the
\mathrm{O}(N)

In section
III.2
we discuss quantities nonlinear with the density matrix, which reveals a lot more structures of the mixed state density matrix of the critical state under decoherence. In particular, we discuss a quantum information phase transition that can be diagnosed through the 2nd Renyi entropy of the decohered system, along with other correlations defined in this section.

Here we would like to point out an important difference between the physical scenarios to be considered in section
III.1
and
III.2

III.1
we discuss physics under weak measurements on quantities such as energy density, and we allow a postselection on the measurement outcomes. Section

considers quantities nonlinear with the density matrix where postselection is not needed in this scenario; hence it can genuinely correspond to physics under decoherence due to coupling to the environment.

In section
III.4
we demonstrate that there is an explicit lattice model with an information transition driven by the strength of decoherence (or weak measurement) analogous to the decoherence-driven transition in section
III.2

If we forbid postselection, local correlation functions of the (locally) decohered density matrix would remain largely unchanged from the undecohered pure state. In recent years the nonlocal disorder operator has become a very important diagnosis of quantum states of matter. In section

II
general formalism

In order to discuss quantum states under decoherence, one approach is to first explicitly derive the ground-state wave function
|\Psi\rangle
in either exactly soluble lattice models or effective field theories, then construct the density matrix
\hat{\rho}^{D}
under decoherence
Lee et al. 2022b
. This approach relies on an explicit derivation of the ground-state wave function. The ground-state wave function can be derived for gapped states such as SPT phases
Lee et al. 2022a
;
Xu and Senthil 2013
;
You et al. 2014
, and also gapless phases with a Gaussian (free boson) Lagrangian, such as the
(1+1)d
Luttinger liquid
Garratt et al. 2022

(2+1)d
models
Ardonne et al. 2004
. But for general interacting theories, such as systems near the Wilson-Fisher quantum critical points, deriving the ground-state wave function is cumbersome. In this section, we follow a more general procedure to study interacting systems under decoherence.

Let us prepare an interacting quantum state which is the ground state of a Hamiltonian, whose pure state density matrix is given as
\hat{\rho}_{0}
. After the quantum state is prepared, we turn off the Hamiltonian and expose the system to
local
decoherence. For example, for a lattice model of qubits, a decohered density matrix may be represented as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{D}=\mathcal{E}[\hat{\rho}_{0}],\ \ \mathcal{E}=\prod_{{\bm{x}}}{\cal E}_{{\bm{x}}},

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{{\bm{x}}}[\hat{\rho}_{0}]=(1-p)\hat{\rho}_{0}+pZ_{{\bm{x}}}\hat{\rho}_{0}Z_{{\bm{x}}}.

(2)

where
\hat{\rho}^{D}
describes a mixed state (ensemble) and
{\cal E}
is given as the composition of local decoherence channels
{\cal E}_{\bm{x}}
. One interpretation of this decoherence channel is that, there is a certain probability for the environment to measure qubits in the
Z
basis (weak measurements) at each location
{\bm{x}}
, and the measurement outcomes are “lost”, which amounts to the dephasing noise in the study of quantum circuits. A generic decoherence channel maps a density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{0}
into
\hat{\rho}^{D}=\sum_{m}K_{m}\hat{\rho}_{0}K^{\dagger}_{m}
, where
\{K_{m}\}
is a set of Kraus operators satisfying the condition
\sum_{m}K^{\dagger}_{m}K_{m}=\openone
.
If we interpret a decoherence channel to be induced by weak measurements, we can consider a postselection followed by the channel where the resulting density matrix is given as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{D}_{P}\equiv\frac{{\cal P}[\hat{\rho}^{D}]}{\mathrm{tr}\{{\cal P}[\hat{\rho}^{D}]\}},\quad{\cal P}[\hat{\rho}^{D}]\equiv\sum_{m\in P}K_{m}\hat{\rho}_{0}K^{\dagger}_{m}

(3)

where
{\cal P}
is a generalized projection onto a subset of measurement outcomes
P

The most important factor that determines the form of

III
Decohered Wilson-Fisher critical point

In this section, we study two different scenarios for the system under weak measurements: In Sec.
III.1
, we postselect a set of measurement outcomes and show that the resulting correlation functions linear in the density matrix
\hat{\rho}^{D}_{P}
exhibit novel behaviors. In Sec.
III.2

\hat{\rho}^{D}
would exhibit an ordinary Wilson-Fisher behavior in this case, we show that quantities non-linear in
\hat{\rho}^{D}
still exhibit novel behaviors including the extraordinary-log criticality and information-theoretic phase transition.

III.4
Lattice Model and Doubled Hilbert Space

We have shown that a decoherence channel whose Kraus operators are symmetric under

can drive an Ising-type phase transition that spontaneously breaks the

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

As we will see, our crossed-Ising transition is closely related to a better known information transition in the context of topological surface codes.

In the following, we use another model to illustrate the essential physics of such a spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB) from
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{u}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{l}
to the diagonal
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

III.5
Correlation Functions and Physical Meanings

In the previous sections, we have defined several correlation functions or physical quantities which are nonlinear in decohered density matrices. Accordingly, these quantities are not directly accessible from experiments, raising questions about what they really mean physically.
In order to connect these quantities to experiments, we consider the signatures of the transition in the probability distribution of measurement outcomes.
More precisely, we will show that
\chi
in Eq. (
III.4
) corresponds to the sensitivity of the decohered mixed state against small perturbations. This, in turn, is related to the amount of information that can be obtained from measuring the mixed state.

To proceed, we define the notion of distance between two different density matrices as the following:

\displaystyle D_{\textrm{J}}(\rho,\sigma)\equiv\mathrm{tr}(\rho\log\rho-\rho\log\sigma+\sigma\log\sigma-\sigma\log\rho).

(48)

This quantity is the quantum generalization of the Jeffreys divergence
Jeffreys 1948
(or symmetrized quantum relative entropy), which quantifies the distance between two density matrices. It satisfies good properties to be a valid metric between mixed states:
(i)
non-negative,
(ii)
vanishing if and only if
\rho=\sigma
, and
(iii)
monotonically decreasing under the application of quantum channels.

In order to discuss the “sensitivity” of the decohered density matrix, we define an infinitesimal variation of the original density matrix under symmetry breaking “perturbation”, defined as the following quantum channel:

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\delta,v}:\rho\rightarrow(1-\delta)\rho+\delta Z_{v}\rho Z_{v}

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}\equiv{\cal M}_{\delta}[\rho],\quad{\cal M}_{\delta}\equiv\prod_{v}{\cal M}_{\delta,v}.

(49)

which amounts to the application of weak measurement in the
Z

\delta
also serves as a symmetry-breaking field in the doubled Hilbert space. Then,

quantifies the difference between the unperturbed and perturbed decohered density matrices. Accordingly, how fast the distance changes with
\delta
tells how sensitive the decohered system is against weak measurement in the
Z

Z

{\cal F}\equiv\partial_{\delta}^{2}D(\delta)\big|_{\delta=0}

.

\displaystyle\qquad-\log\mathrm{tr}(\rho\sigma^{n-1})-\log\mathrm{tr}(\sigma\rho^{n-1})\Big)

(50)

III.6

Under the Kramers-Wannier (KW) duality, the

V
summary and future directions

In this study, we examined the effects of decoherence and weak measurement on quantum critical points in
(2+1)d
space-time. We found that this problem is mathematically equivalent to the boundary or defect criticality of
(2+1)d
conformal field theories, which have been extensively researched in recent years. Our results indicate that when a QCP is exposed to decoherence or weak measurement, observers may observe peculiar behaviors, including the extraordinary-log correlation recently discovered in the context of boundary criticality. Additionally, as the strength of decoherence or weak measurement increases, the system can experience an information-theoretic transition that is captured by quantities nonlinear in the decohered density matrix. This transition is linked to spontaneous symmetry breaking when we consider quantities to the
n
-th power of the density matrix; in particular using the “doubled formalism”, we provided a specific example where the transition belongs to the
2d
Ising universality class for
n=2
. By KW duality, this transition is connected to the
error threshold
transition in the decohered toric code, which is another type of information-theoretic transition in quantum systems.

There are many related directions that are very much worth exploring in the future. We list two such directions as follows:

(1) Unconventional quantum criticality under decoherence:

It is known that in the world of quantum many-body systems, there are two types of quantum critical points: conventional and unconventional. Conventional QCPs correspond to quantum phase transitions between a disordered state that can be adiabatically connected to a direct product state, and a state that spontaneously breaks certain symmetry. This type of QCPs has classical analogs such as the Wilson-Fisher fixed points discussed in this work. Well-understood examples of such conventional QCP can be found in Ref.
Sachdev 2011
, including the QCP of the transverse field quantum Ising model, which is between the Ising ordered phase that spontaneously breaks the Ising symmetry, and a symmetric disordered phase that is adiabatically connected to a direct product state. For a two-dimensional quantum Ising model, the QCP is described by the
3D
Ising Wilson-Fisher fixed point. Another well-known example of such conventional QCP is the quantum phase transition between the superfluid phase and the Mott insulator phase in the Bose Hubbard model at integer filling
Fisher et al. 1989
, which belongs to the 3D XY Wilson-Fisher universality class if the model is built on a two-dimensional lattice.

On the other hand, unconventional QCPs are those that do not have a simple classical analog and may involve transitions between two ordered phases with different symmetries, or between an ordered phase and a topological order. The most well-known example of unconventional QCPs is the deconfined QCP
Senthil et al. 2004a
;
Senthil et al. 2004b
, which has many desirable phenomena such as deconfinement and a duality web as was summarized in Ref.
Wang et al. 2017
. There is another large class of unconventional QCPs which involve quantum disordered states that cannot be adiabatically connected to a direct product state, such as the topological orders. One early example of such quantum phase transitions can be found in Ref.
Wen and Wu 1993
. It is reasonable to expect that the unconventional QCPs under decoherence can also be mapped to certain boundary criticality problems, and it is going to be an unusual boundary criticality with unconventional QCP in the bulk. As we have already seen in the current work, decoherence may be at odds with deconfinement, as deconfinement is often signified by nonlocal operators such as the disordered operators or the Wilson loops, whose behavior can be strongly affected by decoherence. It would be interesting to study the fate of unconventional QCPs under decoherence in general in the future.

where
{\cal E}[\rho_{{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}]
is given as

\displaystyle\rho_{{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{{2N_{v}}}}\prod_{e}(1+s_{e}X_{e})Z_{e}^{(1-s_{e}s^{\prime}_{e})/2}

\displaystyle{\cal E}[\rho_{{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{{2N_{v}}}}\prod_{e}(1+s_{e}(1-2p)X_{e})Z_{e}^{(1-s_{e}s^{\prime}_{e})/2}

(82)

For
\mathrm{tr}({\cal E}[\rho_{{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}]\rho_{\textrm{tc}})
not to vanish,
\partial({\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime})=0
so that product of
Z_{e}
does not create anyons. In such a case, the product of
Z
-strings always commutes with a loop of
X
-strings along the bond.
In fact, we can show that

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{\textrm{tc}}|\prod_{e\in l}X_{e}\prod_{e}Z_{e}^{(1-s_{e}s_{e}^{\prime})/2}|\psi_{\textrm{tc}}\rangle=F(l,{\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime})\delta_{\partial l,0}\delta_{\partial({\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime}),0}

(83)

where
{\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime}
defines a non-trivial link configuration along the
dual link
whenever it takes a negative value.
For
\langle\bm{C}_{1,2}^{X,Y,Z}\rangle=c^{x,y,z}_{1,2}
we have

\displaystyle F(l,{\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime})=\langle\psi_{\textrm{tc}}|\bm{C}_{h(l)}^{X}\bm{C}_{h({\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime})}^{Z}|\psi_{\textrm{tc}}\rangle

(84)

where
h(l)\in\pi_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is the element of the homotopy group of the torus. For the simplest case where
\bm{C}_{1,2}^{X}=1
, we remark that
F(l,{\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime})
is non-zero if and only if
h({\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime})
is trivial.
Then, the overlap in Eq. (
81
) is given as

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\cal E}[\rho_{{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}]\rho_{\textrm{tc}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{{2N_{v}}}}\sum_{l}(1-2p)^{\absolutevalue{l}}\prod_{e\in l}s_{e}

\displaystyle\times\langle\psi_{\textrm{tc}}|\prod_{e\in l}X_{e}\prod_{e}Z_{e}^{(1-s_{e}s_{e}^{\prime})/2}|\psi_{\textrm{tc}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{h({\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime}),\bm{1}}}{2^{N_{v}}(2\cosh\beta)^{{2N_{v}}}}Z_{\textrm{RBIM}}[{\bm{s}},\beta]

(85)

where the summation is over all possible edge configuration
l
,
\tanh\beta=(1-2p)
, and

\displaystyle Z_{\textrm{RBIM}}[{\bm{s}},\beta]\equiv\sum_{{\bm{\sigma}}}\prod_{e=(v,v^{\prime})}e^{\beta s_{e}\sigma_{v}\sigma_{v^{\prime}}},

(86)

which turns out to be the partition function of an Ising model with random signs, specified by
\{s_{e}\}
, in the near-neighbor spin-spin interaction.
At
p=0
,
\beta^{-1}=0
, i.e., zero temperature limit, It has an interesting consequence: the matrix element
\langle\Omega_{\bm{s}}|{\cal E}[\rho_{\textrm{tc}}]|\Omega_{{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}\rangle
vanishes unless
{\bm{s}}
and
{\bm{s}}^{\prime}
belong to the same equivalence class, i.e.,
\partial({\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime})=0
. Furthermore, if
{\bm{s}}\sim{\bm{s}}^{\prime}
, then
Z_{\textrm{RBIM}}[{\bm{s}},\beta]=Z_{\textrm{RBIM}}[{\bm{s}}^{\prime},\beta]
. Accordingly,
{\cal E}[\rho_{\bm{s}}]
is block-diagonal. Therefore,
{\cal E}[\rho_{\textrm{tc}}]
decomposes as the following:

\displaystyle{\cal E}[\rho_{\textrm{tc}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}\rho_{{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}{\cal E}[\rho_{\textrm{tc}}])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N_{v}}(2\cosh\beta)^{{2N_{v}}}}\sum_{\bm{m}}Z_{\textrm{RBIM}}[{\bm{s}}_{\bm{m}},\beta]\,\rho_{{\bm{m}}}.

(87)

where the summation is taken over the equivalence class of
{\bm{s}}
, denoted by
{\bm{m}}
; the equivalence class is defined as
\partial({\bm{s}}\cdot{\bm{s}}^{\prime})=0
.
{\bm{s}}_{\bm{m}}
is the representative of the equivalence class
{\bm{m}}
, and
\rho_{\bm{m}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{\bm{m}}\equiv\sum_{{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\sim{\bm{s}}_{\bm{m}}}|\Omega_{\bm{s}}\rangle\langle\Omega_{{\bm{s}}^{\prime}}|.

(88)

Therefore, the decohered density matrix has the following block-diagonal structure (in
X
basis)

\displaystyle{\cal E}[\rho_{\textrm{tc}}]=\begin{bmatrix}\,\framebox{$\bm{B}_{1}$}&0&0&\,\,\cdots\,\\
0&\framebox{$\bm{B}_{2}$}&0&\,\,\cdots\\
0&0&\framebox{$\bm{B}_{3}$}&\,\,\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\,\,\ddots\end{bmatrix}

(89)

where each block is
2^{N_{v}-1}
by
2^{N_{v}-1}
dimensional matrix labeled by the equivalence class
{\bm{m}}
and its entries are all equal, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{i}\propto\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&\,\cdots\\
1&1&1&\,\cdots\\
1&1&1&\,\cdots\\
\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.75}{$\vdots$}}&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.75}{$\vdots$}}&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.75}{$\vdots$}}&\,\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.75}{$\ddots$}}\end{bmatrix}}_{\displaystyle 2^{N_{v}-1}}\left.\vphantom{\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&\,\cdots\\
1&1&1&\,\cdots\\
1&1&1&\,\cdots\\
\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.75}{$\vdots$}}&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.75}{$\vdots$}}&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.75}{$\vdots$}}&\,\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.75}{$\ddots$}}\end{bmatrix}}\right\}2^{N_{v}-1}=2^{N_{v}-1}|\phi_{\bm{m}}\rangle\langle\phi_{\bm{m}}|

(90)

where
|\phi_{\bm{m}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N_{v}-1}}}\sum_{{\bm{s}}\sim{\bm{s}}_{\bm{m}}}|\Omega_{\bm{s}}\rangle
. There are total
2^{N_{v}+1}
equivalence classes originated from
(N_{v}-1)
independent stabilizers
B_{p}
and two logical operators
\bm{C}^{X}_{i}
.

A.2
Entanglement Entropy

After reorganizing terms, we get

\displaystyle\rho_{\textrm{tc}}^{D}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{m}}p_{\bm{m}}|\phi_{\bm{m}}\rangle\langle\phi_{\bm{m}}|,\quad p_{\bm{m}}=\frac{Z_{\textrm{RBIM}}[{\bm{s}}_{\bm{m}},\beta]}{2\cdot(2\cosh\beta)^{{2N_{v}}}}.

(91)

Note that the entanglement entropy has a very interesting structure:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{\textrm{tc}}^{D}\ln\rho_{\textrm{tc}}^{D})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\bm{m}}p_{\bm{m}}\log p_{\bm{m}}

\displaystyle\propto-\sum_{\bm{m}}Z[{\bm{s}}_{\bm{m}},\beta]\log Z[{\bm{s}}_{\bm{m}},\beta]

(92)

which is nothing but a disorder averaged random bond Ising model’s free energy along the Nishimori line, whose transition point is located at
p_{c}=0.1094

Le Doussal and Harris 1988
. Therefore, there is an
intrinsic
phase transition of the entanglement entropy of the decohered toric code state at
p_{c}=0.1094
, which coincides with the decodability transition point obtained in Ref.
Dennis et al. 2002
.

A.3
Purity

Interestingly, the purity of the decohered density matrix maps to the partition function of the Ising model: (Here
C\equiv({2^{2}\cdot(2\cosh\beta)^{4N_{v}}})^{-1}
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}((\rho_{\textrm{tc}}^{D})^{2})=C\sum_{{\bm{m}}}Z^{2}_{\textrm{RBIM}}[{\bm{s}}_{\bm{m}},\beta]=C\sum_{\bm{s}}\frac{Z^{2}_{\textrm{RBIM}}[{\bm{s}},\beta]}{2^{N_{v}-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C(\cosh\beta)^{4N_{v}}}{2^{N_{v}-1}}\sum_{\bm{s}}\sum_{{\bm{\sigma}}}\sum_{{\bm{\sigma}}^{\prime}}\sum_{L_{1},L_{2}}(1-2p)^{|L_{1}|+|L_{2}|}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{e\in L_{1}}s_{e}\prod_{e^{\prime}\in L_{2}}s_{e^{\prime}}\prod_{i\in\partial L_{1}}\sigma_{i}\prod_{j\in\partial L_{2}}\sigma^{\prime}_{j}

(93)

where
L_{i}
is the link configuration defined on the square lattice. This expression can be further simplified by the following:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}((\rho_{\textrm{tc}}^{D})^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{4N_{v}+N_{v}+1}}\sum_{{\bm{\sigma}}}\sum_{{\bm{\sigma}}^{\prime}}\sum_{L_{1},L_{2}}(1-2p)^{|L_{1}|+|L_{2}|}

\displaystyle\qquad\times 2^{{2N_{v}}}\delta_{L_{1},L_{2}}\prod_{i\in\partial L_{1}}\sigma_{i}\prod_{j\in\partial L_{2}}\sigma^{\prime}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{4N_{v}+N_{v}+1}}\sum_{L_{1},L_{2}}(1-2p)^{|L_{1}|+|L_{2}|}\cdot 2^{{2N_{v}}}\delta_{L_{1},L_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times 2^{N_{v}}\delta_{\partial L_{1},0}\cdot 2^{N_{v}}\delta_{\partial L_{2},0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N_{v}+1}}\sum_{L}(1-2p)^{2\absolutevalue{L}}\delta_{\partial L,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{\textrm{FIM}}[\beta^{\prime}]}{2(2\cosh\beta^{\prime})^{{2N_{v}}}},\quad\tanh\beta^{\prime}=(1-2p)^{2}

(94)

where
Z_{\textrm{FIM}}[\beta^{\prime}]=Z_{\textrm{RBIM}}[\bm{1},\beta^{\prime}]
is the partition function of the ferromagnetic Ising model. In the last equality, we used that

\displaystyle\sum_{\partial\gamma=0}(\tanh\beta)^{|\gamma|}\prod_{e\in\gamma}s_{e}=\frac{Z_{\textrm{RBIM}}[s,\beta]}{2^{N_{v}}(\cosh\beta)^{{2N_{v}}}}.

(95)

Since the ferromagnetic 2d Ising model has a transition at
\beta=\ln(1+\sqrt{2})/2=0.441
, correlation functions in the doubled Hilbert space (in the next section) would exhibit a critical behavior at
p^{(2)}_{c}=0.178
.

A.4
Choi Isomorphism

One may study the decohered toric code state in the doubled Hilbert space under Choi isomorphism. The decohered density matrix maps into the following Choi state:

\displaystyle\|{\cal E}[\rho_{\textrm{tc}}]\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle=\sum_{i}|i\rangle\otimes({\cal E}[\rho_{\textrm{tc}}]|i\rangle)=\sum_{\bm{m}}p_{\bm{m}}|\phi_{\bm{m}}\rangle|\phi_{\bm{m}}\rangle

(96)

where we used Eq. (
91
).
The dephasing channel maps to the following Choi operator in the doubled Hilbert space:

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal E}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{e}(1-2p)^{1/2}e^{\tau Z_{e,u}Z_{e,l}},\quad\tanh\tau=\frac{p}{1-p}

(97)

which can be considered as an imaginary time evolution by an Ising Hamiltonian. Note that
\cosh\tau=(1-p)/\sqrt{1-2p}
and
\sinh\tau=p/\sqrt{1-2p}
. Then, we see that

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal E}}X_{e,u}\hat{{\cal E}}^{-1}=X_{e,u}e^{-2\tau Z_{e,u}Z_{e,l}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\hat{{\cal E}}B_{p}\hat{{\cal E}}^{-1}=B_{p}\prod_{e\in p}e^{-2\tau Z_{e,u}Z_{e,l}}

(98)

Now, consider the following parent Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{parent}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v}(1-A_{v})^{\dagger}(1-A_{v})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p}(1-B_{p})^{\dagger}(1-B_{p})

(99)

for some
\alpha,\beta>0
. Following the procedure in Ref.
Lee et al. 2022b
, we can show that the Choi state
\|{\cal E}[\rho_{\textrm{tc}}]\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle=\hat{{\cal E}}\|\rho_{\textrm{tc}}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle
is the ground state of the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal H}}^{D}

\displaystyle=\hat{{\cal H}}_{u}^{D}+\hat{{\cal H}}_{l}^{D}+\hat{{\cal H}}_{\textrm{int}}^{D}

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal H}}_{u}^{D}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{v}A_{v,u}-\sum_{p}\cosh(2\tau\sum_{e \in p} Z_{e,u}Z_{e,l})B_{p,u}

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal H}}_{\textrm{int}}^{D}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\prod_{e\in p}e^{-4\tau Z_{e,u}Z_{e,l}}

(100)

However, using that
\cosh(2 \tau\sum_{e \in p} Z_{e,u} Z_{e,l})\geq 0
and it commutes with
A_{v}
and
B_{p}
, we can simplify it as

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal H}}^{D}

\displaystyle=\hat{{\cal H}}_{u}^{D}+\hat{{\cal H}}_{l}^{D}+\hat{{\cal H}}_{\textrm{int}}^{D}

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal H}}_{u}^{D}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{v}A_{v,u}-\sum_{p}B_{p,u}

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal H}}_{\textrm{int}}^{D}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\prod_{e\in p}(\cosh 2\tau-Z_{e,u}Z_{e,l}\sinh 2\tau)

(101)

In this doubled system, the coupling
\hat{{\cal H}}_{\textrm{int}}^{D}
breaks two microscopic
magnetic
one-form symmetries into their diagonal subgroup, and we expect the phase transition from a doubled toric code order to a single toric code order. The transition should be captured by the condensation of
e
-anyons, which is diagnosed by non-vanishing expectation values of

\displaystyle\langle(\prod_{e\in\gamma^{\perp}}Z_{e,u})(\prod_{e\in\gamma^{\perp}}Z_{e,l})\rangle\sim\textrm{const},

(102)

where
\gamma^{\perp}
is the open string defined along the dual lattice.

A.5
Kramers-Wannier Duality

Under Kramers-Wannier duality, the toric code maps to the trivially disordered state,
|\Omega_{0}\rangle=|+\rangle^{\otimes N}
on the vertices of the dual lattice. The mapping is explicitly given as the following:

\displaystyle\prod_{e\ni v}X_{e}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow X_{v}

\displaystyle Z_{e\perp(v,v^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow Z_{v}Z_{v^{\prime}}

(103)

Note that in this dual model, the system has two 0-form
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetries even under decoherence. Also,
v
that labels the vertices in the dual lattice labels the plaquette in the original lattice. Since
\prod_{v}X_{v}
is dual to
\prod_{p}B_{p}=1
in the toric code, the mapped states must be symmetric under the 0-form
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry. The above relation makes it clear that the dephasing channel in Eq. (
79
) maps to the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetric decoherence channel in Eq. (
44
).

Now, applying the Kramers-Wannier duality on the doubled Hamiltonian in Eq. (
A.4
), we can obtain the Hamiltonian for the dual model in the doubled Hilbert space as the following:

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal H}}^{D}

\displaystyle=\hat{{\cal H}}_{u}^{D}+\hat{{\cal H}}_{l}^{D}+\hat{{\cal H}}_{\textrm{int}}^{D}

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal H}}_{u}^{D}

\displaystyle=-2\cosh 2\tau\sum_{v}X_{v}

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal H}}_{\textrm{int}}^{D}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{v}\prod_{v^{\prime}\in v}(\cosh 2\tau-Z_{v,u}Z_{v^{\prime},u}Z_{v,l}Z_{v^{\prime},l}\sinh 2\tau)

(104)

In this model, The transition should be captured by the development of an order parameter that breaks off-diagonal
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{u}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{l}
symmetry, which is diagnosed by non-vanishing expectation values of

\displaystyle\langle(Z_{v,u}Z_{v,l})(Z_{v^{\prime},u}Z_{v^{\prime},l})\rangle\sim\textrm{const}

(105)

for any well separated
v
and
v^{\prime}
.

A.6
Purity calculation

As stated in the main text, the (unnormalized) groundstate of the above Hamiltonian in the doubled Hilbert space is given as

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal E}}\|\rho_{0}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle=(1-p)^{2N_{v}}\sum_{\bm{l}}(\tanh\tau)^{\absolutevalue{\bl}}|\partial{\bm{l}}\rangle\otimes|\partial{\bm{l}}\rangle.

(106)

The norm of this wavefunction is given as

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\{(\rho^{D})^{2}\}

\displaystyle=\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\rho^{D}\|\rho^{D}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle\propto\sum_{{\bm{l}}_{1},{\bm{l}}_{2}}\delta_{\partial{\bm{l}}_{1},\partial{\bm{l}}_{2}}(\tanh\tau)^{|{\bm{l}}_{1}|+|{\bm{l}}_{2}|}

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{\{s_{v}\}}\Big[\prod_{e}\big(1+\tanh\tau\prod_{v\in\partial e}s_{v}\big)\Big]^{2}

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{\{s_{v}\}}\prod_{e}\big(1+\tanh 2\tau\prod_{v\in\partial e}s_{v}\big)

\displaystyle\propto Z_{\textrm{FIM}}[2\tau]

(107)

where we used the following identity:

\displaystyle\delta_{\partial{\bm{l}}_{1},\partial{\bm{l}}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N_{v}}}\sum_{s_{v}\in\{\pm 1\}}\prod_{v\in\partial{\bm{l}}_{1}}s_{v}\prod_{v^{\prime}\in\partial{\bm{l}}_{2}}s_{v^{\prime}}.

(108)

Note that at
p=p_{c}^{(2)}
,
2\tau
here agrees with
\beta^{\prime}
from Eq. (
A.3
), which establishes the self duality in the 2d Ising model.

Appendix B
Derivatives of the Distance

In this section, we calculate the second derivative of the distance defined in Eq. (
51
) for the decohered density matrix
\rho^{D}={\cal E}[|\Omega_{0}\rangle\langle\Omega_{0}|]
from Eq. (
44
).
The perturbation is defined by the following channel

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\delta,v}:\rho\rightarrow(1-\delta)\rho+\delta Z_{v}\rho Z_{v}

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}\equiv{\cal M}_{\delta}[\rho],\quad{\cal M}_{\delta}\equiv\prod_{v}{\cal M}_{\delta,v}.

(109)

By defining
h
such that
\tanh h=\delta/(1-\delta)
(
e^{h}=1/\sqrt{1-2\delta}
), the channel can be mapped to the following operator under Choi isomorphism:

\displaystyle\hat{\cal M}_{\delta}\equiv\prod_{v}(1-2\delta)^{1/2}e^{hZ_{v,u}Z_{v,l}}

(110)

Note that
\partial h/\partial\delta=1/(1-2\delta)
. Then, we can show that

\displaystyle\partial_{\delta}D^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\rho_{\delta}^{D}\|\partial_{\delta}\rho^{D}_{\delta}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle}{\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\rho_{\delta}^{D}\|\rho^{D}_{\delta}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle}-\frac{\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\rho^{D}\|\partial_{\delta}\rho^{D}_{\delta}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle}{\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\rho^{D}\|\rho^{D}_{\delta}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle}

\displaystyle\partial_{\delta}^{2}D^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\partial_{\delta}\rho_{\delta}^{D}\|\partial_{\delta}\rho^{D}_{\delta}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle}{\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\rho_{\delta}^{D}\|\rho^{D}_{\delta}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle}-\Bigg(\frac{\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\rho^{D}\|\partial_{\delta}\rho^{D}_{\delta}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle}{\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\rho^{D}\|\rho^{D}_{\delta}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle}\Bigg)^{2}

(111)

Let
O\equiv\sum_{v}Z_{v,u}Z_{v,l}
. Then, at
\delta\rightarrow 0
, we evaluate that

\displaystyle\partial_{\delta}{\cal M}_{\delta}\big|_{\delta\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=(O-L^{2})

(112)

Furthermore, exactly at
\delta=0
,
\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle O\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle\big|_{\delta=0}=0
due to the symmetric nature of the initial decohered density matrix under symmetric decoherence channel.
Then, plugging Eq. (
112
) into the Eq. (
B
), it is straightforward to show that the first derivative of the distance vanishes as expected, and the second derivative, depending on the order of limit, would be given as Eq. (
52
) or Eq. (
