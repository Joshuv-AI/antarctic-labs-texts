Analysis and numerical approximation of energy-variational solutions to the Ericksen–Leslie equations
Robert Lasarzik, Maximilian E.V. Reiter

Abstract
We define the concept of energy-variational solutions for the Ericksen–Leslie equations in three spatial dimensions. This solution concept is finer than dissipative solutions and satisfies the weak-strong uniqueness property. For a certain choice of the regularity weight, the existence of energy-variational solutions implies the existence of measure-valued solutions and for a different choice, we construct an energy-variational solution with the help of an implementable, structure-inheriting space-time discretization. Computational studies are performed in order to provide some evidence of the applicability of the proposed algorithm. MSC(2020): 35A35, 35Q35, 65M60, 76A15 Keywords: Existence, liquid crystal, Ericksen–Leslie, energy-variational solutions, numerical approximation, unit-norm constraint, mass-lumping, finite element method

Introduction

Liquid crystals comprise the structural properties of crystals within a fluid.
The fluid flow of the nematic phase of liquid crystals can be described by the Ericksen–Leslie system.
In this model, the material behaves like a liquid,
i.e.,
no positional order is present, but the molecules exhibit a long-range self-alignment along a direction (see Figure

In this way, liquid crystals sustain anisotropic dynamics such as the polarization of light or the transfer of heat, but at the same time offer the physical flexibility of a fluid, which makes these materials interesting for engineering and sciences

In comparison to measure-valued solutions, dissipative solutions have the advantage that they have less degrees of freedom and can be approximated by numerical schemes. Nevertheless, they form only a subset of measure-valued solutions and are thus not as precise. In the work at hand, we introduce energy-variational solutions (
cf.

[
30

), which have one degree of freedom more than dissipative solutions and can be argued to be as fine as measure-valued solutions, but as we will show, they can also be approximated by numerical schemes and have additional advantages.
This solution concept is useful where one might either not be able to derive weak solutions for physically relevant models or where weak solutions admit unphysical behaviour, like unphysical non-uniqueness
[
22

. The solution concept was first introduced in
[
29
]

We will prove that this concept is finer than the so-called dissipative solutions introduced by Lions
[
36

[

Additionally, in certain scenarios this concept is finer than the concept of measure-valued solutions.
Energy-variational solutions do not only fulfill the standard weak-strong uniqueness property of generalized solution concepts (
cf.

[
28

In
[
29

it was also argued that energy-variational solutions are amenable for different selection criteria.
The set of energy-variational solutions can be seen as a convex, weakly
∗
-closed superset of weak solutions but a subset of dissipative solutions and for a special choice of the regularity weight also a subset of measure-valued solutions. This may allow to introduce techniques from optimization theory in order to select the physically relevant solution maximizing the dissipation in every point-in-time
[
29
]
.

In this work, we define the concept of energy-variational solutions for the Ericksen–Leslie equations in three spatial dimensions.
This definition has some freedom, since it depends on the choice of a certain regularity weight
\mathcal{K}

For one choice of such a regularity weight, we prove the equivalence to a certain class of measure-valued solutions, and for another choice, we construct an energy-variational solution with the help of an implementable, structure-inheriting space-time discretization based on the finite element method.

Concerning the numerical approximation of the Ericksen–Leslie equations, a first study for the simplified system (

In their subsequent work

Even a relaxation from

A different approach is proposed in
[

For the simplified and penalized Ericksen–Leslie system also decoupling techniques and mixed methods are examined in

In
[

two numerical schemes for the simplified model are proposed. The first one uses the Ginzburg–Landau approximation for the unit-norm constraint and the second proposed scheme does not depend on a regularization parameter and fulfills the unit-norm constraint in the limit. However both schemes do not fulfill the unit-norm constraint at the discrete level exactly. We therefore use an approach from
[
4

by implementing a midpoint rule at the finite element level which solves the sphere constraint exactly at every node of the mesh. But we have to refine this approach by introducing a special projection (see Remark

The proposed scheme is the first numerical scheme implementing the main properties of the continuous system including the algebraic norm restriction at every node of the mesh

We think that the concept of energy-variational solutions is a strong tool for identifying limits of solutions to numerical schemes and can also serve as such in other related models.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{d}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla{\boldsymbol{d}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\,.

Remark

(Ericksen stress)
.

The main obstacle when passing to the limit in an approximation of the Ericksen–Leslie equations is often the so-called Ericksen stress, which can be seen as a kind of Korteweg stress coupling the elastic deformations of the director field to the evolution of the velocity field.
This term is the main reason that we have to consider a generalized solution framework different from usual weak solutions.
Via Lemma
2.6
we observe that both formulations

and

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{d}}\cdot[{\boldsymbol{d}}]_{x}^{T}

\displaystyle[{\boldsymbol{d}}]_{x}\Delta{\boldsymbol{d}}\text{d}x={}-\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{d}}\cdot(\Delta{\boldsymbol{d}}+|\nabla{\boldsymbol{d}}|^{2}{\boldsymbol{d}})\text{d}x

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\nabla{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}\nabla{\boldsymbol{d}}):\nabla\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\text{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot\nabla)|\nabla{\boldsymbol{d}}|^{2}-|\nabla{\boldsymbol{d}}|^{2}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\cdot\nabla)|{\boldsymbol{d}}|^{2}\text{d}x\,,

Remark

Remark

(Dissipative solutions)
.

Theorem
2.5
does not only show the weak-strong uniqueness of energy-variational solutions, it also shows that every energy-variational solution is a dissipative solution. A dissipative solution is defined via the relative-energy inequality (
10
),
i.e.,

({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{d}})
is called a dissipative solution, if it fulfills the relative energy-inequality

for all function
(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{d}}})
fulfilling the regularity properties (

). The concept of energy-variational solutions is thus strictly finer than the concept of dissipative solutions.

y_{2}\in Z_{h}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |(y_{1},y_{2})_{h}-(y_{1},y_{2})|&\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathcal{I}_{h}(y_{1}\cdot y_{2})-y_{1}\cdot y_{2}\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\lVert\mathcal{I}_{h}(y_{1}\cdot y_{2})-y_{1}\cdot y_{2}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}\\
&\leq Ch^{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}(y_{1}\cdot y_{2})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}\\
&\leq Ch^{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\lVert\nabla y_{1}:\nabla y_{2}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}\\
&\leq Ch^{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{h}}\left\lVert\nabla y_{1}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(K)}\left\lVert\nabla y_{2}\right\rVert_{L^{q}(K)}\\
&\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert\nabla y_{1}\right\rVert_{L^{p}\left(\Omega\right)}\left\lVert\nabla y_{2}\right\rVert_{L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)}\\
&\leq Ch\left\lVert y_{1}\right\rVert_{L^{p}\left(\Omega\right)}\left\lVert\nabla y_{2}\right\rVert_{L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)}.\end{aligned}

(24)

The first inequality is a triangle inequality, the second one uses Hölder’s inequality, the third bounds the interpolation error by Lemma
2.10
. Since
[Z_{h}]^{3}
consists of piecewise affine-linear functions the second derivative of a function in
[Z_{h}]^{3}
vanishes. This together with Hölder’s inequality, local and global inverse estimates (
cf.

2.8
) allow to conclude.
Note that the choice of exponents in the third step is necessary in order to make sure that we can indeed apply Lemma
2.10
.

In case that one function is not a finite element functions, we have to estimate the difference with the interpolated function,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |(\mathcal{I}_{h}y_{h},\mathcal{I}_{h}g)-(y_{h},g)|&=|(y_{h},\mathcal{I}_{h}g)-(y_{h},g)|\\
&\leq C\left\lVert y_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{p}\left(\Omega\right)}\left\lVert(\mathcal{I}_{h}-I)g\right\rVert_{L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)}\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert y_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{p}\left(\Omega\right)}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}g\right\rVert_{L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)}\,,\end{aligned}

(25)

and estimate the last factor on the right-hand side of (
24
) by

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\mathcal{I}_{h}g\right\rVert_{L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\nabla(\mathcal{I}_{h}-I)g\right\rVert_{L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)}+\left\lVert\nabla g\right\rVert_{L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\left\lVert\nabla^{2}g\right\rVert_{L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)}+\left\lVert\nabla g\right\rVert_{L^{q}\left(\Omega\right)}\leq C\left\lVert\nabla g\right\rVert_{W^{1,q}\left(\Omega\right)}.

∎

We introduce another interpolation operator
\interad:L^{1}(\Omega;\Rr^{3})\ra[Z_{h}]^{3}
by

\interad(g):=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{h}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}g(x)\phi_{z}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}\phi_{z}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}\phi_{z}\,.

(26)

We observe the identity

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\inter{f},g\right)&=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{h}}f(z)\phi_{z}(x)g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{h}}f(z)\int_{\Omega}\phi_{z}(x)g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\left(f,\interad g\right)_{h}\,.\end{split}

(27)

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
f\in\Cont(\overline{{\Omega}})
. Then it holds

\displaystyle\|f-\interad f\|_{\Cont(\overline{{\Omega}})}\to 0\text{ as }h\ra 0\,.

Proof.

Consider any
y\in\overline{{\Omega}}
. We obtain the pointwise estimate

\displaystyle f(y)-\interad f(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}f(y)\phi_{z}(y)-\sum_{z}\int_{\Omega}f(x)\phi_{z}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\phi_{z}(y)\frac{1}{\int_{\Omega}\phi_{z}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}\phi_{z}(y)\frac{\int_{\Omega}[f(y)-f(x)]\phi_{z}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}\phi_{z}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z}\phi_{z}(y)\max_{x\in supp(\phi_{z})}|f(x)-f(y)|

\displaystyle=\max_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{h}}\max_{x,s\in supp(\phi_{z})}|f(x)-f(s)|,

where the last term converges to
0
as
h\to 0
, since
g
is continuous.
Above, we used that
\sum_{z}\phi_{z}(y)=1
and that support of the shape functions
\phi_{z}
vanishes as
h\to 0
.
Since
y
was arbitrary the assertion follows.
∎

Further, we introduce the discrete Laplacian in a slightly different way than usual, since we further equip it with mass lumping. Then the discrete Laplacian
\Delta_{h}:H^{1}\left(\Omega\right)\to[Y_{h}]^{3}
of a function
f\in H^{1}\left(\Omega\right)
is defined via

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{h}f,b)_{h}

\displaystyle=(\nabla f,\nabla b)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }b\in Y_{h}.

(28)

Similarly, we define an adapted projection via
\mathcal{R}_{h}:L^{2}(\Omega;\Rr^{3})\ra[Z_{h}]^{3}
via

(\mathcal{R}_{h}f,b)_{h}=(f,b)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }b\in[Z_{h}]^{3}\,.

(29)

This choice allows to infer with (
22
) that

\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}f\right\rVert_{h}^{2}=(\mathcal{R}_{h}f,\mathcal{R}_{h}f)_{h}=(f,\mathcal{R}_{h}f)\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\mathcal{R}_{h}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}f\right\rVert_{h}\,,

(30)

which implies together with (
22
) that
\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}f\right\rVert_{h}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.

Remark

(Adapted discretization of convection term)
.

In comparison to the discretization in
[
4
]
, we use the above projection
\mathcal{R}_{h}
in the definition of the convection term in comparison to the
L^{2}
-projection
\mathcal{P}_{h}
. This change is needed in order to show the convergence to the finer concept of energy-variational solutions. In
[
4
]
only the convergence to dissipative solutions was shown, which is a bigger class of solutions. One key ingredient for this improvement are the above norm-equivalences.

3
Discrete system

In order to restrict ourselves to finite element spaces with a zero trace, we decompose the spatial approximation of our director into its interior and its non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition,
i.e.

\displaystyle d^{j}={d^{j}_{\circ}}+\mathcal{I}_{h}{{\boldsymbol{d}}_{0}},

(31)

with
\tr(d^{j}_{\circ})=0
and
{\boldsymbol{d}}_{0}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
.

Scheme
32
.

Let
(v^{0},d^{0})=(\mathcal{Q}_{h}v_{0},\mathcal{I}_{h}d_{0})
.
For
1\leq j\leq J
and
(v^{j-1},d^{j-1}_{\circ})\in U_{h}
, we want to find
(v^{j},d^{j}_{\circ})\in U_{h}
, such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\boldsymbol{d}_{t}v^{j},a)+((&v^{j-1}\cdot\nabla)v^{j},a)+\frac{1}{2}([\nabla\cdot v^{j-1}]v^{j},a)\\
&-([\mathcal{R}_{h}{\nabla d^{j-1}}]^{T}[d^{j-1/2}\times(d^{j-1/2}\times\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2})],a)_{h}+(T_{L}^{j},\nabla a)=0\,,\end{aligned}

(32a)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{d}_{t}d^{j},c)_{h}+(d^{j-1/2}\times[\mathcal{R}_{h}{\nabla d^{j-1}}v^{j}],d^{j-1/2}\times c)_{h}-((\nabla v^{j})_{skw}d^{j-1/2},c)_{h}\\
&{+}\lambda(d^{j-1/2}\times[(\nabla v^{j})_{sym}d^{j-1/2}],d^{j-1/2}\times c)_{h}{-}(d^{j-1/2}\times\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2},d^{j-1/2}\times c)_{h}=0,\end{aligned}

(32b)

for all
(a,c)\in U_{h}
. Hereby we discretize the Leslie stress tensor as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (T_{L}^{j},\nabla a)\coloneqq&(T_{D}^{j},\nabla a)+\lambda(d^{j-1/2}\times[(\nabla a)_{sym}d^{j-1/2}],d^{j-1/2}\times\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2})_{h}\\
&+((\nabla a)_{skw}\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2},d^{j-1/2})_{h},\end{aligned}

(33)

where
T^{j}_{D}
collects the dissipative terms of the discrete Leslie stress tensor again,
i.e.

\displaystyle(T_{D}^{j},\nabla a)\coloneqq

\displaystyle(\mu_{1}+\lambda^{2})((d^{j}\cdot(\nabla v^{j})_{sym}d^{j}),(d^{j}\cdot(\nabla a)_{sym}d^{j}))

\displaystyle+\mu_{4}\left((\nabla v^{j})_{sym},(\nabla a)_{sym}\right)+(\mu_{5}+\mu_{6}-\lambda^{2})\left((\nabla v^{j})_{sym}d^{j},(\nabla a)_{sym}d^{j}\right)\,.

Remark

(Features of the discrete system)
.

The mass lumping is applied on the director equation (
32b
) to guarantee the unit-norm restriction of
d^{j}
in every node.
In order to find a discrete energy law, the associated terms in the momentum equation (
32a
) and the discrete Laplacian are mass-lumped as well. Since the gradient of the director is piecewise constant and therefore not well-defined at the nodes of the mesh, we apply the mass-lumped
L^{2}
projection
\mathcal{R}_{h}
in the convection term. Note that this is computationally not too expensive, since it only effects the gradient of the past iterate.

Additionally, we note that solving the discrete system amounts in introducing two additional coupled equations, one to calculate the discrete Laplacian (
28
) and one to assure that the solution
v^{j}
fulfills the discrete vanishing divergence condition (
19
).

estimates

The proposed scheme is unconditionally solvable.

Proposition 3.1

(Existence of discrete solutions)
.

Let

,

Proof.

We show that the map

implicitly defined by the above scheme (
32
), whose zero is the next iterate in terms of

\displaystyle(\Pi(u),u)_{U_{h}}\geq 0

(34)

for all

for some constant

.
Where the dependency on
d^{j}
has to be interpreted via the discrete Greens function

associated to the discrete Laplacian and given by

\displaystyle\left(\nabla\mathcal{G}_{h}(w),\nabla b\right)=(w,b)_{h}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }b\in Y_{h}\,.

Noting that this operator is a continuous bijection due to Lax-Milgram, we observe that the associated mapping
\Pi
corresponding to our discrete scheme is actually well defined and continuous.
Then the existence of solutions to our discrete scheme (
32
) follows from a non-linear version of Brouwer’s fix-point theorem (
cf.

[
47
]
).
In particular, we evaluate

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\Pi(v^{j},&-\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2}),(v^{j},-\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2}))_{U_{h}}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{d}_{t}\left\lVert v^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{d}_{t}\left\lVert\nabla d^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\frac{k}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{d}_{t}v^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\\
&\quad+(\mu_{1}+\lambda^{2})\left\lVert d^{j}\cdot(\nabla v^{j})_{sym}d^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}+\mu_{4}\left\lVert(\nabla v^{j})_{sym}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\\

(35)

where we used the discrete identities

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{d}_{t}v^{j},v^{j})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{d}_{t}\left\lVert v^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\frac{k}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{d}_{t}v^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\,,\quad(-\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2},\boldsymbol{d}_{t}d^{j})_{h}=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{d}_{t}\left\lVert\nabla d^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(36)

Equation (
35
) is indeed positive, if

\displaystyle\left\lVert v^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla d^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\geq\left\lVert v^{j-1}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla d^{j-1}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}

holds, which only depends on
u^{j-1},k,h
. This is implied by choosing an appropriate norm, since we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert v^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla d^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla d^{j-1}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\geq\left\lVert v^{j}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\frac{2(c_{l}h)^{2}}{3}\left\lVert\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}

Note that the second term stems from a simple claculation obtained by applying the inverse estimate:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\leq c\left\lVert\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2}\right\rVert_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle=c(\nabla d^{j-1/2},\nabla(-\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2}))

\displaystyle\leq(c_{l}h)^{-1}\left\lVert\nabla d^{j-1/2}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}\left\lVert\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}.

∎

4
Computational studies

An efficient implementation of our discrete scheme (
32
) faces two challenges: The non-linearity in Scheme
32
does not allow to rely on well-known and effective solvers for the resulting linear systems. Secondly, all equations are coupled which leads to a high-dimensional mixed problem formulation.
Therefore, by fully linearizing and decoupling all equations we make use of an iterative fixed-point algorithm which was presented in
[
45
, ch. 5]
and follows the ideas of
[
43
, Algorithm
A_{1}
]
.
This approach can be described by the following scheme.

Scheme
51
.

(1) Let
(v^{j,0},d^{j,0}_{\circ})^{T}=(v^{j-1},d^{j-1}_{\circ})^{T}\in U_{h}
for
j>1
. For
j=1
let the initial values be given by
(v^{0},d^{0})=(\mathcal{Q}_{h}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{h}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{0})
.

(2) For
1\leq l\leq J
and
(v^{j,l-1},d^{j,l-1})\in U_{h}
, we want to find
(v^{j,l},d^{j,l}_{\circ})\in U_{h}
, such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\boldsymbol{d}_{t}&v^{j,l},a)+((v^{j-1}\cdot\nabla)v^{j,l},a)+\frac{1}{2}((\nabla\cdot v^{j-1})v^{j,l},a)+(T_{L}^{j,l},\nabla a)&\\
&-v_{el}A([\mathcal{R}_{h}{\nabla d^{j-1}}]^{T}[d^{j-1/2,l-1}\times(d^{j-1/2,l-1}\times\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2,l-1})],a)_{h}=0&\end{aligned}

(51a)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\boldsymbol{d}_{t}d^{j,l}&,c)_{h}+v_{el}(d^{j-1/2,l-1}\times[\mathcal{R}_{h}{\nabla d^{j-1}}v^{j,l-1}],d^{j-1/2,l}\times c)_{h}&\\
&-A(d^{j-1/2,l-1}\times\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2,l-1},d^{j-1/2,l}\times c)_{h}&\\
&-v_{el}((\nabla v^{j,l-1})_{skw}d^{j-1/2,l},c)_{h}&\\
&+v_{el}\lambda(d^{j-1/2,l-1}\times[(\nabla v^{j,l-1})_{sym}d^{j-1/2,l-1}],d^{j-1/2,l}\times c)_{h}=0,&\end{aligned}

(51b)

for all
(a,c)\in U_{h}
, where the constants
v_{el},A
describe the intensity of the coupling of the different physical properties.

(3) Stop if
\left\lVert v^{j,l}-v^{j,l-1}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}+\left\lVert d^{j,l}-d^{j,l-1}\right\rVert_{H^{1}\left(\Omega\right)}\leq\theta
, where
\theta
describes the tolerance of the fixpoint-solver.

For the above scheme, one would solve the discrete Laplace equation computationally after solving (
51a
) and (
51b
). This is possible since (
51b
) does not depend on
\Delta_{h}d^{j,l}
. Note that (
51a
) and (
51b
) could even be solved in parallel.
The Leslie stress tensor is thereby discretized as

\displaystyle T_{L}^{j,l}\coloneqq

\displaystyle v_{el}(\mu_{1}+\lambda^{2})(d^{j,l-1}\cdot(\nabla v^{j,l})_{sym}d^{j,l-1})(d^{j,l-1}\otimes d^{j,l-1})+\mu_{4}(\nabla v^{j,l})_{sym}

\displaystyle+v_{el}(\mu_{5}+\mu_{6}-\lambda^{2})(d^{j,l-1}\otimes(\nabla v^{j,l})_{sym}d^{j,l-1})_{sym}

\displaystyle+\lambda v_{el}A[d^{j-1/2,l-1}\otimes[d^{j-1/2,l-1}]_{x}^{T}[d^{j-1/2,l-1}]_{x}\cdot\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2,l-1}]_{sym}

\displaystyle+v_{el}A[d^{j-1/2,l-1}\otimes\Delta_{h}d^{j-1/2,l-1}]_{skw}\,.

Any fixed-point of scheme (
51
) solves the highly non-linear scheme (
32
).
By standard arguments, one can derive conditional convergence of our fixpoint algorithm, i.e.
u^{j,l}\to u^{j}\in U_{h}
as
l\to\infty
for a sufficiently small
k,h
. For more details, we refer to
[
45
]
.

Since we introduced the constants
v_{el},A
for the physical coupling of the quantities, the energy law changes slightly and we rescale the total energy to

\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{d}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{v}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{A}{2}\left\lVert\nabla{\boldsymbol{d}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}\,.
