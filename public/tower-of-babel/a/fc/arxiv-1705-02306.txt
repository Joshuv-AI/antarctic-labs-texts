Gradient of eigenvalues of Dirac operators and its applications


Abstract
For Dirac operators, which have discrete spectra, the concept of eigenvalues’ gradient is given and formulae for this gradients are obtained in terms of normalized eigenfunctions. It’s shown how the gradient is being used to describe isospectral operators or when finite number of spectral data is changed. Keywords : Dirac operator, Gradient of eigenvalue, Isospectral operators



Tigran Harutyunyan and Yuri Ashrafyan

Department of Mathematics and Mechanics, Yerevan State University, Yerevan, Armenia

\sigma_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\

y_{2}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle y_{1}(0)\cos\alpha+y_{2}(0)\sin\alpha=0,\quad\alpha\in\Big(-\cfrac{\pi}{2},\cfrac{\pi}{2}\Big],

(1.3)

\displaystyle y_{1}(\pi)\cos\beta+y_{2}(\pi)\sin\beta=0,\quad\beta\in\Big(-\cfrac{\pi}{2},\cfrac{\pi}{2}\Big],

(1.4)

B=\cfrac{1}{i}\sigma_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\\
\end{array}\right),

\end{array}\right).

By the same
L(p,q,\alpha,\beta)
we also denote a self-adjoint operator, generated by differential expression
\ell

L^{2}([0,\pi];{\mathbb{C}}^{2})

\displaystyle D=\Big\{y=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\\
\end{array}\right);y_{k}\in AC[0,\pi],(\ell y)_{k}\in L^{2}[0,\pi],k=1,2;

(1.5)

\displaystyle y_{1}(0)\cos\alpha+y_{2}(0)\sin\alpha=0,\ y_{1}(\pi)\cos\beta+y_{2}(\pi)\sin\beta=0\Big\}

(see, e.g.

The scalar product in

(f,g)=\int_{a}^{b}\langle f,g\rangle dx=\int_{a}^{b}[f_{1}(x)\bar{g}_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)\bar{g}_{2}(x)]dx
.

is purely discrete and consists of simple, real eigenvalues, which we denote by
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{n}(p,q,\alpha,\beta)=\lambda_{n}(\Omega,\alpha,\beta)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, to emphasize the dependence of
\lambda_{n}
on quantities
p,q
and
\alpha,\beta
.
It is also well known (see, e.g.
[

,
6
,
7
]
) that the eigenvalues form a sequence, unbounded below as well as above.
So we will enumerate it as
\lambda_{k}<\lambda_{k+1},k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\lambda_{k}>0
, when
k>0
and
\lambda_{k}<0
, when
k<0
, and the nearest to zero eigenvalue we will denote by
\lambda_{0}
.
If there are two nearest to zero eigenvalue, then by
\lambda_{0}
we will denote the negative one.
With this enumeration it is proved (see
[

,
6
,
7
]
), that the eigenvalues have the asymptotics:

{}\lambda_{n}(\Omega,\alpha,\beta)=n-\cfrac{\beta-\alpha}{\pi}+r_{n},\quad r_{n}=o(1),\quad n\rightarrow\pm\infty.

(1.6)

Let
y(x,\lambda)=\varphi(x,\lambda,\alpha,\Omega)
and
y(x,\lambda)=\psi(x,\lambda,\beta,\Omega)
are the solutions of the Cauchy problems

\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{c}\ell y=\lambda y\\
y(0,\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin\alpha\\
-\cos\alpha\\
\end{array}\right)\end{array},

(1.7)

\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{c}\ell y=\lambda y\\
y(\pi,\lambda)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin\beta\\
-\cos\beta\\
\end{array}\right)\end{array},

(1.8)

respectively.
Since the differential expression
\ell
is self-adjoint, the components
\varphi_{1}(x,\lambda)
,
\varphi_{2}(x,\lambda)
and
\psi_{1}(x,\lambda)
,
\psi_{2}(x,\lambda)
of the vector-functions
\varphi(x,\lambda)
and
\psi(x,\lambda)
can be chosen real-valued for real
\lambda

It is easy to see, that
\varphi_{n}(x,\Omega)=\varphi(x,\lambda_{n},\alpha,\Omega)
and
\psi_{n}(x,\Omega)=\psi(x,\lambda_{n},\beta,\Omega)

\lambda_{n}
.
By
a_{n}=a_{n}(\Omega,\alpha,\beta)
and
b_{n}=b_{n}(\Omega,\alpha,\beta)

\varphi_{n}(x,\Omega)
and
\psi_{n}(x,\Omega)

a_{n}=\|\varphi_{n}\|^{2}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}|\varphi_{n}(x,\Omega)|^{2}dx,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

b_{n}=\|\psi_{n}\|^{2}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}|\psi_{n}(x,\Omega)|^{2}dx,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

The numbers
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are called norming constants.
By
h_{n}(x,\Omega)
we will denote normalized eigenfunctions (i.e.
\|h_{n}(x)\|=1
) of operator
L(\Omega,\alpha,\beta)
:

h_{n}(x)=h_{n}(x,\Omega)=\cfrac{\varphi_{n}(x,\Omega)}{\sqrt{a_{n}(\Omega,\alpha)}},

and it can be taken also as

\hat{h}_{n}(x)=\hat{h}_{n}(x,\Omega)=\cfrac{\psi_{n}(x,\Omega)}{\sqrt{b_{n}(\Omega,\beta)}}.

It is easy to see, that
|h_{n}(0)|^{2}=\cfrac{1}{a_{n}}
and
|\hat{h}_{n}(\pi)|^{2}=\cfrac{1}{b_{n}}
.
Having a goal to describe the dependence of
\lambda_{n}
on quantities
p,q
and
\alpha,\beta
more precisely, we input a concept of eigenvalues’ gradient, by the following formula (compare with
[
8
]
)

grad\lambda_{n}=\left(\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}}{\partial\alpha},\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}}{\partial\beta},\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}}{\partial p(x)},\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}}{\partial q(x)}\right).

(1.9)

Definition 1
.

Let
g
is defined on
\left(a,b\right)
, where
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
.
The derivative of function
f
with respect to function
g
is called a function
\cfrac{\partial f}{\partial g(x)}
, which satisfies the equation

\cfrac{d}{d\epsilon}f(g+\epsilon v)\Big|_{\epsilon=0}=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\cfrac{\partial f}{\partial g(x)}v(x)dx,

for all
v\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(a,b\right)
.

We want to express the components of the eigenvalues’ gradient by normalized eigenfunctions of
L(p,q,\alpha,\beta)
problem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\lambda_{n}
and
h_{n}
are eigenvalues and normalized eigenfunctions of the
L(p,q,\alpha,\beta)
problem correspondingly.
Then there hold the relations:

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}(\alpha,\beta,p,q)}{\partial\alpha}=-|h_{n}(0)|^{2},

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}(\alpha,\beta,p,q)}{\partial\beta}=|h_{n}(\pi)|^{2},

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}(\alpha,\beta,p,q)}{\partial p(x)}=|h_{n_{1}}(x)|^{2}-|h_{n_{2}}(x)|^{2},

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}(\alpha,\beta,p,q)}{\partial q(x)}=2h_{n_{1}}(x)\cdot h_{n_{2}}(x).

Proof.

Let
h_{n}
is eigenfunction of problem
L(p,q,\alpha,\beta)
, and
\tilde{h}_{n}
is eigenfunction of problem
L(p,q,\alpha+\Delta\alpha,\beta)
.
Then

\ell h_{n}\equiv Bh^{\prime}_{n}(x)+\Omega(x)h_{n}(x)\equiv\lambda_{n}(\alpha)h_{n}(x),

(1.10)

h_{n_{1}}(0)\cos\alpha+h_{n_{2}}(0)\sin\alpha=0,

h_{n_{1}}(\pi)\cos\beta+h_{n_{2}}(\pi)\sin\beta=0.

\ell\tilde{h}_{n}\equiv B\tilde{h}^{\prime}_{n}(x)+\Omega(x)h_{n}(x)\equiv\lambda_{n}(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)\tilde{h}_{n}(x),

(1.11)

\tilde{h}_{n_{1}}(0)\cos(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)+\tilde{h}_{n_{2}}(0)\sin(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)=0,

\tilde{h}_{n_{1}}(\pi)\cos\beta+\tilde{h}_{n_{2}}(\pi)\sin\beta=0.

Multiply (
1.10
) by
\tilde{h}_{n}(x)
scalarly from the right, and (
1.11
) by
h_{n}(x)
from the left.
Taking into account the self-adjointness of
\Omega(x)

\left((h_{n},\Omega\tilde{h}_{n})=(\Omega h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n})\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big(Bh^{\prime}_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big)+\Big(\Omega h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big)=\lambda_{n}(\alpha)\Big(h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big),

\displaystyle\Big(h_{n},B\tilde{h}^{\prime}_{n}\Big)+\Big(\Omega h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big)=\lambda_{n}(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)\Big(h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big).

Subtracting from the second equation the first equation, we will get

\int_{0}^{\pi}\Big\langle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h_{n_{1}}\\
h_{n_{2}}\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{h}^{\prime}_{n_{2}}\\
-\tilde{h}^{\prime}_{n_{1}}\end{array}\right)\Big\rangle dx-\int_{0}^{\pi}\Big\langle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h^{\prime}_{n_{2}}\\
-h^{\prime}_{n_{1}}\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{h}_{n_{1}}\\
\tilde{h}_{n_{2}}\end{array}\right)\Big\rangle dx=

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\left[\lambda_{n}(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)-\lambda_{n}(\alpha)\right]\Big(h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big).

(1.12)

Taking into account, that in case of real potentials the components of the solutions can be taken real, thus the left side of the latter equation can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\Big[h_{n_{1}}(x)\tilde{h}^{\prime}_{n_{2}}(x)-h_{n_{2}}(x)\tilde{h}^{\prime}_{n_{1}}(x)-h^{\prime}_{n_{2}}(x)\tilde{h}_{n_{1}}(x)+h^{\prime}_{n_{1}}(x)\tilde{h}_{n_{2}}(x)\Big]dx=

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\cfrac{d}{dx}\Big[h_{n_{1}}(x)\tilde{h}_{n_{2}}(x)-h_{n_{2}}(x)\tilde{h}_{n_{1}}(x)\Big]dx=

\displaystyle=h_{n_{1}}(\pi)\tilde{h}_{n_{2}}(\pi)-h_{n_{2}}(\pi)\tilde{h}_{n_{1}}(\pi)-h_{n_{1}}(0)\tilde{h}_{n_{2}}(0)+h_{n_{2}}(0)\tilde{h}_{n_{1}}(0).

Since
h_{n}(x)=\cfrac{\varphi_{n}(x,\alpha)}{\sqrt{a_{n}(\alpha)}}
and
\tilde{h}_{n}(x)=\cfrac{\varphi_{n}(x,\alpha+\Delta\alpha)}{\sqrt{a_{n}(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)}}
,
then
h_{n}(0)=\cfrac{1}{\sqrt{a_{n}(\alpha)}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin\alpha\\
-\cos\alpha\end{array}\right)

and
\tilde{h}_{n}(0)=\cfrac{1}{\sqrt{a_{n}(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)\\
-\cos(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)\end{array}\right)
.
Thus the equation (
1.12
) can be rewritten as follows

-\cfrac{1}{\sqrt{a_{n}(\alpha)a_{n}(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)}}\sin\Delta\alpha=\left[\lambda_{n}(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)-\lambda_{n}(\alpha)\right]\Big(h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big).

From the latter, when
\Delta\alpha\rightarrow 0
, we obtain

\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}}{\partial\alpha}=-\cfrac{1}{a_{n}}=-|h_{n}(0)|^{2}.

(1.13)

Similarly we obtain

\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}}{\partial\beta}=\cfrac{1}{b_{n}}=|h_{n}(\pi)|^{2}.

(1.14)

To obtain the equality
\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}}{\partial p(x)}=|h_{n_{1}}(x)|^{2}-|h_{n_{2}}(x)|^{2}
, we write (
1.10
) in the form

Bh^{\prime}_{n}(x)+\left(\sigma_{2}p(x)+\sigma_{3}q(x)\right)h_{n}(x)\equiv\lambda_{n}(p)h_{n}(x)

(1.15)

and for (
1.11
) in the form

B\tilde{h}^{\prime}_{n}(x)+\left(\sigma_{2}\left[p(x)+\epsilon v(x)\right]+\sigma_{3}q(x)\right)\tilde{h}_{n}(x)\equiv\lambda_{n}(p+\epsilon v)\tilde{h}_{n}(x),

(1.16)

where
\tilde{h}_{n}
is normalized eigenfunction of the
L(p+\epsilon v,q,\alpha,\beta)
problem.
Multiply (
1.15
) by
\tilde{h}_{n}(x)
scalarly from the right, and (
1.16
) by
h_{n}(x)
from the left.
Taking into account, that
h_{n}
and
\tilde{h}_{n}
satisfy to the same boundary conditions, subtract equality (
1.15
) from (
1.16
), we obtain

\Big(h_{n},\sigma_{2}\left[p(x)+\epsilon v(x)\right]\tilde{h}_{n}\Big)-\Big(\sigma_{2}p(x)h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big)=\left[\lambda_{n}(p+\epsilon v)-\lambda_{n}(p)\right]\Big(h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big).

Form the latter it follows

\cfrac{\lambda_{n}(p+\epsilon v)-\lambda_{n}(p)}{\epsilon}\Big(h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\Big(h_{n_{1}}(x)\tilde{h}_{n_{1}}(x)-h_{n_{2}}(x)\tilde{h}_{n_{2}}(x)\Big)v(x)dx.

Tending
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, using the fact, that
\tilde{h}_{n}\rightarrow h_{n}
, when
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and the definition
1
, we obtain
\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}}{\partial p(x)}=|h_{n_{1}}(x)|^{2}-|h_{n_{2}}(x)|^{2}
.

Similarly we can obtain the equality
\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}}{\partial q(x)}=2h_{n_{1}}(x)\cdot h_{n_{2}}(x)
.

Theorem
1.1
is proved.
∎

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\lambda_{n}
and
h_{n}
are eigenvalues and normalized eigenfunctions of the
L(p,q,\alpha)
problem correspondingly.
Then there hold the relations:

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}(\alpha,p,q)}{\partial\alpha}=-|h_{n}(0)|^{2},

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}(\alpha,p,q)}{\partial p(x)}=|h_{n_{1}}(x)|^{2}-|h_{n_{2}}(x)|^{2},

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{n}(\alpha,p,q)}{\partial q(x)}=2h_{n_{1}}(x)\cdot h_{n_{2}}(x).

In case of real potentials the components of the solutions can be taken real.

and
\tilde{h}_{n}
are from

\{x_{k};\ x_{k}\rightarrow\infty,\ k\rightarrow\infty,\}

Write the equation (

) in the following form

Bh^{\prime}_{n}(x)+\left(\sigma_{2}p(x)+\sigma_{3}q(x)\right)h_{n}(x)\equiv\lambda_{n}(p)h_{n}(x)

and (

where
\tilde{h}_{n}
is normalized eigenfunction of the

Multiplying (

\tilde{h}_{n}(x)
from the right, and (

) by
h_{n}(x)
from the left.
Taking into account, that
h_{n}
and
\tilde{h}_{n}

1.17

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(h_{n_{1}}(x)\bar{\tilde{h}}_{n_{2}}(x)+h_{n_{2}}(x)\bar{\tilde{h}}_{n_{1}}(x)\Big)\epsilon v(x)dx=

(1.19)

\displaystyle=\left[\lambda_{n}(q+\epsilon v)-\lambda_{n}(q)\right]\Big(h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}\Big).

\alpha
and

[

]
).
But if we fix one of them, then the inverse problem can be solved uniquely (see

7
,

,
12
]
).
Therefore, usually is considered the problem

(see
[
4
,
7
,
11
,
13
]
).

It is also well-known, that for regular Dirac operators (the operators on finite interval with summable coefficients), we can not add or diminish the eigenvalues (because of obligatory asymptotics (
1.6
)), staying in the class of summable coefficients, but we can change the norming constants and describe the isospectral Dirac operators (see
[
11
,
13
]
).

The applications of eigenvalues’ gradient of describing operators, which isospectral with fixed operator

On the other hand, if we consider Dirac operator on half axis
(0,\infty)
(which has pure discrete spectra), we can add or diminish arbitrary finite number of eigenvalues or change norming constants, since in this case there are not obligatory asymptotics (see, e.g.

The applications of eigenvalues’ gradient in this case is given in section

2
Isospectrality on finite interval.

Let us consider the boundary-value problem
L(p,q,\alpha,0)
on
[0,\pi]
.
From the eigenvalues’ asymptotics (
1.6
) it follows:

{}\lambda_{n}(\Omega,\alpha,0)=n-\cfrac{\alpha}{\pi}+r_{n},\quad r_{n}=o(1),\quad n\rightarrow\pm\infty.

(2.1)

It is known (see
[
4
,
6
]
) that in the case of
\Omega\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}[0,\pi]
the norming constants have an asymptotic form:

a_{n}(\Omega)=\pi+c_{n},\quad\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}^{2}<\infty.

(2.2)

Definition 2
.

Two Dirac operators
L(\Omega,\alpha,0)
and
L(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\alpha},0)
are said to be isospectral,
if
\lambda_{n}(\Omega,\alpha,0)=\lambda_{n}(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\alpha},0)
, for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Omega,\tilde{\Omega}\in L^{1}_{\mathbb{R}}[0,\pi]
and
the operators
L(\Omega,\alpha,0)
and
L(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\alpha},0)
are isospectral.
Then
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the asymptotics (
2.1
):

\cfrac{\alpha}{\pi}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(n-\lambda_{n}(\Omega,\alpha,0))=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(n-\lambda_{n}(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\alpha},0))=\cfrac{\tilde{\alpha}}{\pi}.

∎

So, instead of isospectral operators

Our main goal is to give the description of the set

For Dirac operators the description of
