Abstract
. Barycentric coordinates provide solutions to the problem of expressing an element of a compact convex set as a convex combination of a finite number of extreme points of the set. Various approaches to this problem have arisen, in various contexts. The most general solution, namely the Gibbs coordinates based on entropy maximization, actually work in the broader setting of barycentric algebras, which constitute semilattice-ordered systems of convex sets. These coordinates involve exponential functions. For convex polytopes, Wachspress coordinates offer solutions which only involve rational functions. The current paper focuses primarily on convex polygons in the plane. After summarizing the Gibbs and Wachspress coordinates, we identify where they agree, and provide comparisons between them when they do not. Within a general formalism for analyzing coordinate systems, we then introduce a direct sparse geometric approach based on chordal decompositions of polygons, along with a symmetrized version which creates what we call cartographic coordinates. We present comparisons between cartographic coordinates based on distinct chordal decompositions, and comparisons of cartographic coordinates with Gibbs and Wachspress coordinates.

Each point of a simplex has a unique expression as a convex combination of vertices of the simplex (§

Handling these topics involves a mix of techniques: geometric, algebraic, and combinatorial.

1.3.
Notational conventions

The essence of algebra lies in the repeated concatenation of successive functions, where the output of one function is fed as an input to the next. In this context, Euler’s analytical notation as in the expression
\sin x
, where the argument is read after the function, clashes with the natural direction of reading from left to right.
3
3

3

Even within texts written in Semitic languages, mathematics is read in this direction.
Thus, while paying due respect to many traditional analytical notations, such as
H(\alpha)
for the entropy (
3.2
) and
Z(\beta)
for the partition function (
3.3
), our default option for function notation is
algebraic
or
diagrammatic
, with functions following their arguments (e.g.,

for the factorial function), or placed as a suffix (e.g.,
x^{2}
for the squaring function). A fortunate consequence of this convention in linear algebra is that vectors (to be transformed by multiplication with a matrix following) appear as rows or tuples, and do not require transposition to fit on a line.

The expression

is used to denote that a positive real number
x
is not only less than a real number
y
, but also very small by comparison. Logarithms that are presented without an explicit base are natural. For other notational conventions and definitions that are not otherwise specified explicitly (e.g., for group actions), readers are invited to consult

2.5.
The category of barycentric algebras

Since the composite of two barycentric homomorphisms

f\colon(A,I^{\circ})\to(A^{\prime},I^{\circ});x\mapsto x^{f}\mbox{ and }g\colon(A^{\prime},I^{\circ})\to(A^{\prime\prime},I^{\circ});y\mapsto y^{g}

is a barycentric algebra homomorphism
fg\colon(A,I^{\circ})\to(A^{\prime\prime},I^{\circ});x\mapsto x^{fg}
, the class
\mathbf{B}_{0}
of barycentric algebras forms (the object class of) a category
\mathbf{B}
of barycentric algebras, with the set
\mathbf{B}(A,A^{\prime})
of Definition
2.19
as the set of morphisms from
A
to
A^{\prime}
.

There is a forgetful functor
U\colon\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{Set}
from
\mathbf{B}
to the category
\mathbf{Set}
of sets, defined by the subset relationship
\mathbf{B}(A,A^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbf{Set}(A,A^{\prime})
. This functor has a left adjoint

(2.19)

B\colon\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{B}

which assigns the
free barycentric algebra

XB
to a set

, together with an insertion function (adjunction unit)
\eta_{X}\colon X\to XBU
. Thus, for each barycentric algebra
(A,I^{\circ})
and function

, there is a unique barycentric homomorphism
\overline{f}\colon XB\to A
such that
\eta_{X}\overline{f}=f
.

Given a set

2.5.3.
Products of barycentric algebras

For barycentric algebras
(A,I^{\circ})
and
(A^{\prime},I^{\circ})
, their
product

A\times A^{\prime}
is defined as the set
\set{(x,x^{\prime})}{x\in A\,,\ x^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}}
with componentwise structure

(x,x^{\prime})(y,y^{\prime})\underline{p}=\left(xy\,\underline{p},x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\,\underline{p}\right)

and

\pi\colon A\times A^{\prime}\to A;(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto x\,,\quad\pi^{\prime}\colon A\times A^{\prime}\to A;(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto x^{\prime}\,.

There is then a natural isomorphism

for each barycentric algebra

or

.

2.5.4.
Symmetric monoidal categories

The category

of real vector spaces and linear transformations carries the additonal structure

of a symmetric monoidal category

, p.180]
, with the one-dimensional real vector space

of vector spaces

of linear transformations from

interpreting bilinear functions

The category

of barycentric algebras carries a similar structure

,

]
. By analogy with (

defines a

of barycentric algebras

If

2.6.
A pocket dictionary

When a particular topic is analyzed from the standpoint of different fields, it becomes necessary to reconcile the differing and sometimes conflicting terminologies used in those fields. The basic assumption for this section is that a barycentric algebra

2.6.1.

As a convex set barycentric algebra, a finite-dimensional simplex

4.
Wachspress coordinates

4.1.
Volumetric coordinates in a simplex

The (unique) barycentric coordinates in a simplex may be given in terms of relative (hyper)volumes. These barycentric coordinates extend to affine coordinates for the entire affine space containing the simplex.

Consider a simplex of geometric dimension
n
that is spanned by the ordered set
\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{i-1},\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i+1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}
in
n
-dimensional Euclidean space. Here, the cyclic order is uniquely determined by topological orientation. The signed (hyper)volume of the simplex is

(4.1)

\tfrac{1}{n!}\det[\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{i-1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{i+1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}-\mathbf{v}_{0}]

or

(4.2)

\tfrac{1}{n!}\det[1\oplus\mathbf{v}_{0},1\oplus\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,1\oplus\mathbf{v}_{i-1},1\oplus\mathbf{v}_{i},1\oplus\mathbf{v}_{i+1},\dots,1\oplus\mathbf{v}_{n}]

in a more symmetric format that is related to homogeneous coordinates in projective geometry. (For the case
n=2
, see the subsequent section.)

Now, for a point
\mathbf{x}
of the simplex, define

(4.3)

p_{i}=\frac{\det[\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{i-1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{i+1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}-\mathbf{v}_{0}]}{\det[\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{i-1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{i+1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}-\mathbf{v}_{0}]}\,.

Then for
i>0
, the
p_{i}
are the (unique) barycentric coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
with respect to
\mathbf{v}_{i}
in the simplex, so that

(4.4)

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}

with

p_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\,.

The convex combination (
4.4
) based on (
4.3
) is said to give the
volumetric
coordinates for
\mathbf{x}
in terms of the vertices
\mathbf{v}_{i}
.

The formula (
4.4
) extends seamlessly to points
\mathbf{x}
that are outside the simplex. Here, certain of the numerators in (
4.3
) will become negative, and the expression (
4.4
) expresses
\mathbf{x}
as an affine combination of the vertices
\mathbf{v}_{i}
that is not a convex combination.

4.1.1.
Areal coordinates

The volumetric coordinates specialize, in the case of a triangle, to the classical
areal coordinates

[
27
]
. Here, it is convenient to set

(4.5)

A\left(\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\det[\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{2}-\mathbf{v}_{0}]=\frac{1}{2}\begin{vmatrix}1&v_{00}&v_{01}\\
1&v_{10}&v_{11}\\
1&v_{20}&v_{21}\\
\end{vmatrix}

for the signed area of a triangle in the Euclidean plane whose vertices are

\mathbf{v}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{00}&v_{01}\end{bmatrix},\mathbf{v}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{10}&v_{11}\end{bmatrix}\mbox{ and }\mathbf{v}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{20}&v_{21}\end{bmatrix}

in counterclockwise order,
specializing (

The following proposition recalls the convexity of the area

with respect to variation of the vertex

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider vectors

\mathbf{v}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{00}&v_{01}\end{bmatrix},\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{00}^{\prime}&v_{01}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix},\mathbf{v}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{10}&v_{11}\end{bmatrix}\mbox{ and }\mathbf{v}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{20}&v_{21}\end{bmatrix}

for each real number

Proof.

The result follows by consideration of the Laplace expansion

A\left(\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{vmatrix}v_{10}&v_{11}\\
v_{20}&v_{21}\end{vmatrix}-v_{00}\begin{vmatrix}1&v_{11}\\
1&v_{21}\end{vmatrix}+v_{01}\begin{vmatrix}1&v_{10}\\
1&v_{20}\end{vmatrix}\right)

of the determinant of (
4.5
) across its first row.
∎

5.
Comparing Wachspress and Gibbs coordinates

5.1.
When Wachspress and Gibbs coordinates coincide

Proposition 5.1
.

Consider a simplex as a free barycentric algebra
XB
on its set
X
of extreme points. Consider the insertion

(5.1)

j\colon X\hookrightarrow XB

as a valuation function. Then on
XB
, Wachspress coordinates agree with the Gibbs coordinates for the valuation function (
5.1
).

Definition 5.2
.

A polytope is said to be a
semisimplex
if, as a barycentric algebra, it is a direct product of simplices (free barycentric algebras, as in §
2.6.1
).

Remark 5.3
.

The only semisimplices in the plane are parallelograms and triangles. Recall from §
4.3.1
that all polygons are simple, so that notion of simplicity is not related to the semisimplicity of Definition
5.2
.

Theorem 5.4
.

[
35
, Th. 15.1]

For
i=1,2
, consider constituent systems represented by valuation functions
f_{i}:X_{i}\to A_{i}
, with respective entropy functions
H_{i}
. Let
f:X\to A
be the valuation function

f_{1}\times f_{2}:X_{1}\times X_{2}\to A_{1}\times A_{2}

of the independent combination of the constituent systems, with entropy function
H
. Then

H\big((\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\big)=H_{1}(\alpha_{1})+H_{2}(\alpha_{2})

for elements
\alpha_{i}
of
A_{i}
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Wachspress coordinates on semisimplices coincide with the Gibbs coordinates.

Proof.

The Wachspress coordinates on a semisimplex are given as products of the Wachspress coordinates on its simplicial direct factors
[
41
]
. (Note that Warren actually speaks of “tensor products” of simplices — compare §
2.6.5
.) Now by Proposition
5.1
, Wachspress coordinates maximize the entropy on simplices, since they coincide on a simplex with the entropy-maximizing Gibbs coordinates. By Theorem
5.4
, Wachspress coordinates also maximize entropy on semisimplices. It follows that they coincide there with the entropy-maximizing Gibbs coordinates.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

The individual symbolism

of

In summary, the bra-ket notation provides a clear distinction between the two roles that may be played by a vertex

7.
Chordal decompositions and cartographic coordinates

In this chapter, we present an alternative and more local approach to the coordinates of polytopes, namely
cartographic coordinates
. The key idea is to decompose the polytope into simplices known as
regions
, considering the resulting atlas of charts of the polytope, and directly taking the volumetric coordinates for the region within which a given point of the polytope lies. Any bias that is introduced by a particular decomposition may subsequently be removed by taking the average of a point’s coordinates in each of the decompositions appearing in the orbit of a symmetry group.

In the interest of keeping the discussion relatively brief and accessible, and to take advantage of special features that are available in the plane, we restrict to the case of polygons. Although cartographic coordinates are quite straightforward from the geometric point of view, a certain measure of algebraic, topological and combinatorial sophistication is required for their efficient handling. In particular, we draw on the formalism established in the preceding chapter.

7.2.
Region identification and orientation

A chordal decomposition

Our use of exterior algebra notation, here

We outline a recursive procedure for region identification and orientation, including the location of a given point within a region. The procedure is ultimately founded on the correspondence between chordal decompositions of polygons and rooted binary trees.

\textstyle{v_{i_{3}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{v_{i_{2}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Consider a polygon
\Pi

Definition 7.12

(a)
For

and

\bigodot_{\sigma\in\O}\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\ket{v}_{\sigma}=0

(7.14)

\bigodot_{\sigma\in S\cup\set{\tau}}\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\ket{v}_{\sigma}=\bigodot_{\sigma\in S}\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\ket{v}_{\sigma}+\mathds{1}_{\tau}\cdot\ket{v}_{\tau}-\mathds{1}_{\tau\cap\,\bigcup\set{\sigma}{\sigma\in S}}\cdot\ket{v}_{\tau}

recursively, using the pointwise product in the function algebra

.

(b)
Using the notation of (a), the formula

Lemma 7.13
.

Consider the equality

(7.16)

\bra{a}\bigodot_{\sigma\in S\cup\set{\tau}}\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\ket{v}_{\sigma}=\bra{a}\bigodot_{\sigma\in S}\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\ket{v}_{\sigma}

, and

(a)

If

, then (

) holds.

(b)

If

, then (
7.16
) holds.

(c)

7.16

By (

), we have

\displaystyle\bra{a}\bigodot_{\sigma\in S\cup\set{\tau}}\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\ket{v}_{\sigma}=\bra{a}\bigodot_{\sigma\in S}

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\ket{v}_{\sigma}

(7.17)

\displaystyle+\bra{a}\mathds{1}_{\tau}\cdot\braket{a|v}_{\tau}-\bra{a}\mathds{1}_{\tau\cap\,\bigcup\set{\sigma}{\sigma\in S}}\cdot\braket{a|v}_{\tau}\,.

Theorem 7.14
.

Let
\delta
be a chordal decomposition of
\Pi
.

(\mathrm{a})

The partition of unity property

\sum_{v\in V}\ket{v}_{\delta}=\mathds{1}_{\Pi}

holds.

(\mathrm{b})

With
a\in\Pi
, the barycentric or linear precision property

(7.18)

\bra{a}=\sum_{v\in V}\braket{a|v}_{\delta}\bra{v}

holds.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.23
, it suffices to prove (b), since (a) then follows by application of the barycentric homomorphism
\mathds{1}\colon\Pi\to I
to both sides of (b). Consider an element
a
of
\Pi
. There are various cases to consider, according to the location of
a
in the chordal decomposition
\delta
. In the remainder of this proof, “interior” refers to relative interiors.

Interiors of triangles:

If
a
lies in the relative interior of the oriented triangle
\tau=v_{i}\wedge v_{j}\wedge v_{k}
of the chordal decomposition, then by (
7.15
) and Lemma
7.13
(a),

(7.19)

\braket{a|v}_{\delta}=\bigodot_{\sigma\in\set{\rho\in T_{\delta}}{v\in\rho}}\bra{a}\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\braket{a|v}_{\sigma}=\braket{a|v}_{\tau}\,,

so
\braket{a|v}_{\delta}=0
if
v\notin\set{v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}}
. In this case, Proposition
7.4
yields (
7.18
) as an instance of the usual expression for areal coordinates in a triangle.

Interiors of boundary edges:

Suppose that
a
lies in the interior of a boundary edge
v_{i}\wedge v_{i+1}
. The boundary edge forms part of a unique triangle
\tau=v_{i}\wedge v_{i+1}\wedge v_{j}
of the chordal decomposition. Then, as with (
7.19
), we have
\braket{a|v}_{\delta}=0
if
v\notin\set{v_{i},v_{i+1},v_{j}}
, and Proposition
7.4
yields (
7.18
) as an instance of the usual expression for areal coordinates in a triangle.

Interiors of chords:

Suppose that
a
lies in the relative interior of the chord
v_{i}\wedge v_{j}
separating the oriented triangles
\tau_{1}=v_{i}\wedge v_{j}\wedge v_{k}
and
\tau_{2}=v_{l}\wedge v_{j}\wedge v_{i}
. Then by (
7.15
) and Lemma
7.13
(a),

\displaystyle\braket{a|v}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\bigodot_{\sigma\in\set{\rho\in T_{\delta}}{v\in\rho}}\bra{a}\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\braket{a|v}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\bra{a}\mathds{1}_{\tau_{1}}\cdot\braket{a|v}_{\tau_{1}}+\bra{a}\mathds{1}_{\tau_{2}}\cdot\braket{a|v}_{\tau_{2}}-\bra{a}\mathds{1}_{\tau_{1}\cap\tau_{2}}\cdot\braket{a|v}_{\tau_{2}}

\displaystyle=\braket{a|v}_{\tau_{1}}+\braket{a|v}_{\tau_{2}}-\braket{a|v}_{\tau_{2}}=\braket{a|v}_{\tau_{1}}\,,

so
\braket{a|v}_{\delta}=0
if

. In this case, (

) reduces to an instance of the usual expression for areal coordinates on a line segment.

Vertices:

Suppose that

of
\Pi
. Now, for any triangle

\delta

\braket{v_{i}|v_{j}}_{\sigma}=\delta_{ij}

\braket{v_{i}|v_{j}}_{\delta}=0

Now suppose that
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{r}
is the list of triangles within the chordal decomposition
\delta

. By Lemma

\braket{v_{i}|v_{i}}_{\delta}=\bra{v_{i}}\bigodot_{\sigma\in\set{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{r}}}\mathds{1}_{\sigma}\cdot\ket{v_{i}}_{\sigma}\,.

We then have

) also holds in this final case.

7.7.
Application of chordal and cartographic coordinates

Kim, U.-R.
et al.
used barycentric coordinates to compute the electrostatic potential that is created by a charged triangular plate
[
16
, (30)]
. Subsequently, the case of a charged polygonal plate is analyzed by means of the principle of superposition, based on a single triangular decomposition of the polygon
[
16
, §4]
. Special treatment is required for points at the edge of a triangular plate, and thus also for points on the boundaries of the triangles in the decomposition of a polygon
[
16
, p.14]
. The complications arise when one or more of the barycentric coordinates of a point of the polygon are zero.

For a convex polygon, the chordal and cartographic coordinates discussed above may be invoked to limit the occurrence of the troublesome zeros, which arise at points which do not lie in the interior of a triangle of a given decomposition. The case of the rectangle studied in
[
16
, pp.16–17]
takes a single chordal decomposition, where certain barycentric coordinates of a point on the diagonal are zero. Passing to the second chordal decomposition then eliminates the zeros from the barycentric coordinates of the points on the diagonal of the first decomposition, except for the point of intersection of the two diagonals.

For a given chordal decomposition of a convex polygon, the triangle/chord table (
7.11
) computed by our algorithm may be used to determine whether a given point of the interior of the polygon does or does not lie in the interior of a triangle of the chordal decomposition. To eliminate the troublesome zeroes for a point of a polygon with at least five sides, one must then select an appropriate chordal decomposition. For example, the barycenter of a regular hexagon does not lie inside a triangle of any chordal decomposition of type
1^{3}3
, but it does lie inside the central triangle of each of the two chordal decompositions of type
2^{3}
(compare Figure
6
, and §
8.2.5
below).

8.
Conclusion and future work

8.1.
Concluding remarks

We have applied the techniques of barycentric algebra to the problem of determining barycentric coordinates for elements of a polytope. Theorem
5.5
identifies a class of polytopes on which the Gibbs and Wachspress coordinates agree. The G–W discrepancy vector is introduced as a tool for comparing Gibbs and Wachspress coordinates on a given polygon.

On the basis of barycentric algebra, we offer a general framework for the study of barycentric coordinate systems on a polytope. Theorem
6.6
shows that the totality of all such coordinate systems forms a convex set. As a generalization of the G–W discrepancy vector, the
\lambda
–
\mu
discrepancy vector furnishes a tool for comparing any two barycentric coordinate systems
\lambda,\mu
on a given polygon.

We present two new barycentric coordinate systems for polygons: the sparse chordal coordinates and the symmetrical cartographic coordinates. An algorithm for their computation is based on the parsing tree for a non-coassociative coproduct on the edges of a triangulation of the polygon. The example of the unit square shows that the cartographic coordinates may yield distinct barycentric coordinates, even in a case where the Gibbs and Wachspress coordinates agree.

8.2.
Future work

Generally following the thread of the paper, we list some of the open problems that it raises, and some of the directions for future research that it might suggest.

8.2.1.
Projective geometry

As was mentioned in the Introduction, we have avoided any consideration of projective geometric aspects of Wachspress coordinates and the corresponding commutative algebra
[
15
,
17
]
. However, bearing in mind the barycentric algebraic approach to some of the incidence relation aspects of projective geometry
[
30
]
, it may be worth considering the polarity furnished by the Wachspress coordinates from this viewpoint.

8.2.2.
Other geometries

The polytopes and barycentric coordinates that were studied in this paper have implicitly used Euclidean geometry. Parts of the work might be extended to other geometries: spherical
[
2
,
4
]
, hyperbolic
[
3
]
, Wasserstein
[
1
]
, etc. In connection with the latter, compare
[
23
, §11]
,
[
24
]
.

8.2.3.
Continuous Wachspress coordinates

The standard approach to these coordinates was surveyed briefly in §
4.4
. It may prove fruitful to adopt an alternative approach based on barycentric algebras, making use of the modal theory in
[
29
, Ch. 3]
. This theory formalizes the approximation of a convex set by the polytopes it contains, or more generally realizing an arbitrary barycentric algebra as a colimit of its finitely generated subalgebras.

8.2.4.
Structure of the set of barycentric coordinate systems

Theorem
6.6
shows that the set
K_{\Pi}
of barycentric coordinate systems on a polytope
\Pi
is a convex set. What is the structure of this convex set? What are its extreme points?

8.2.5.
Chordal decompositions

The discussion at the end of §
7.7
identifies the need for a more detailed treatment of chordal decompositions of convex polygons having more than
4
vertices, with a view to avoiding the occurrence of zero as a barycentric coordinate of an interior point.

8.2.6.
Chordal and cartographic coordinates in higher dimensions

Chordal and cartographic coordinates, as considered in this paper, are limited to polygons. It is natural to consider their extension to higher dimensions. Here, no analogues of our parsing trees are available. As an initial approach, one may take an inductive decomposition of a polytope into simplices. Start at a particular vertex
v
, and consider the maximal proper walls that do not contain
v
, along with their simplicial decompositions. Then, create simplices in the new dimension by taking the cone from the vertex
v
to each such decomposed wall
[
34
, Th. 3.1]
. To “symmetrize” for the corresponding cartographic coordinates, one could then average over the full set of such decompositions obtained from each of the vertices
v
.

8.2.7.
Threshold convexity

We have not addressed numerical issues in this paper. One such issue is the important distinction between zero and non-zero coordinates. In dealing with this issue, it may be helpful to consider
threshold convexity
, a version of barycentric algebra effectively defaulting to zero or one for coordinates in weighted means whose closeness to one of the endpoints of the unit interval lies within the level of numerical noise
[
18
]
.
