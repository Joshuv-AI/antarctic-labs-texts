Bringing AI to the Edge: A Formal M&S Specification to Deploy Effective IoT Architectures
Román Cárdenas a , Patricia Arroba a , and José L. Risco-Martín b

Abstract
The Internet of Things is transforming our society, providing new services that improve the quality of life and resource management. These applications are based on ubiquitous networks of multiple distributed devices, with limited computing resources and power, capable of collecting and storing data from heterogeneous sources in real-time. To avoid network saturation and high delays, new architectures such as fog computing are emerging to bring computing infrastructure closer to data sources. Additionally, new data centers are needed to provide real-time Big Data and data analytics capabilities at the edge of the network, where energy efficiency needs to be considered to ensure a sustainable and effective deployment in areas of human activity. In this research, we present an IoT model based on the principles of Model-Based Systems Engineering defined using the Discrete Event System Specification formalism. The provided mathematical formalism covers the description of the entire architecture, from IoT devices to the processing units in edge data centers. Our work includes the location-awareness of user equipment, network, and computing infrastructures to optimize federated resource management in terms of delay and power consumption. We present an effective framework to assist the dimensioning and the dynamic operation of IoT data stream analytics applications, demonstrating our contributions through a driving assistance use case based on real traces and data.

1
Introduction

Emerging technological advances, such as smart cities,
personalized medicine, and driving assistance systems, among many other

Internet of Things (IoT)
applications, are enabling a transformation of our society by
applying
Artificial Intelligence (AI)
and
Machine Learning (ML)
algorithms to improve both people’s
quality of life and efficient resource management.
This technology is in full growth, and the deployment of 20.41 billion devices
connected to the internet is expected within the next few
years
(
Gartner. Press Releases (2017)
)
. Also, within the next decade,
McKinsey
(
Manyika et al. (2015)
)
estimates that the economic impact of the global

IoT
market will reach 11.1 trillion USD.

The fog computing paradigm has been designed to support these applications,
which require rapid mobility and intensive data processing in
real-time
(
Deng et al. (2016)
)
. Fog computing, as defined by Cisco
Systems
(
Saad (2018)
)
, aims to bring the cloud computing paradigm closer to
the edge of the access network, deploying
Edge Data Centers (EDCs)
that, together with 5G,
will provide improvements in latency and bandwidth congestion.

EDCs
, also considered as
Micro Data Centers (MDCs)
, are platforms
that provide computation, storage, and networking services to end nodes connected
to an access network
(
Aazam & Huh (2015)
)
. In contrast to the cloud,
EDCs
are
extensively distributed, and their resources are dimensioned accordingly to cover
the demand in small areas.
These edge infrastructures may support the massive Big Data requirements
to process and analyze vast amounts of heterogeneous data streams in
real-time to apply
incremental learning techniques (i.e., training current predictive models
with new data to improve the
Quality of Service (QoS)

(
Gepperth & Hammer (2016)
)
).
Solutions based on
Graphics Processing Unit (GPU)
clusters are one of the best
options to provide useful resources for
IoT
applications, as their
architecture allows the efficient training of computationally expensive

ML
algorithms such as
Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs)
, frequently used in data analytics,
with better performance than CPUs
(
Ananthanarayanan et al. (2017)
)
.

Currently, there is no effective deployment and operation of these new
generation
EDC
infrastructures, in terms of energy efficiency, due to
the challenge involved in adapting traditional data centers to the specific
needs in areas of human activity.
The state-of-the-art provides several models and simulators that assist in the
efficient deployment and operation of fog computing infrastructures. However,
the majority of them are software-based frameworks without a background of
mathematical formalism. On the other hand, formal modeling-based solutions are
focused on specific modules and do not cover the entire system, from
IoT
devices to processing units, which simplifies model management.
Moreover, the complex context of
IoT
, which involves the coordinated
management of user equipment, communication networks, processing units, and
mobility, could benefit from advanced
Modeling and Simulation (M&S)
methods. The solid
Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE)
principles ensure an incremental design that is logic, robust, and reliable.

In this paper, we propose an
M&S
framework for
IoT
scenarios.
The main contributions of this research are the following:

•

The model is entirely defined using the
Discrete Event System Specification (DEVS)
mathematical
formalism
(
Zeigler et al. (2000)
)
.
DEVS
provides several advantages, such
as completeness, verifiability, extensibility, and maintainability.

•

The approach for modeling the
IoT
applications is based on the

Function-as-a-Service (FaaS)
paradigm. This reactive approach enables better resource optimization
for scenarios with varying demand. As edge computing is a location-aware
technology, computation offloading services can improve their performance using

FaaS
patterns.

•

The model contains all the components that comprise the
IoT
scenario.
Our approach is able to capture interdependencies between elements of the system, providing a more accurate and reliable analysis of the scenario under
study.

•

Our work enables the optimization of the
EDCs
’ resource
management in a federated location-aware fashion, in terms of delay and power
consumption, considering the mobility of users and the placement of both
network and computing infrastructures. This mathematical formalism helps with
the energy and delay optimization of edge infrastructures for both the
deployment dimensioning and the dynamic operation in
IoT
data stream
applications.

•

We demonstrate the contributions of our research through a use
case, using real data for a driving assistance
IoT
application, from
the location and mobility of devices to processing units’ workload and power profiles.

2
Related Work

In the field of energy efficiency, the majority of
IoT
models based on
mathematical formalisms only cover specific system submodules. Models
formally describing
IoT
devices, communications networks, and access points
can be found in the research of Tom et al.
(
Tom et al. (2019)
)
, Zi et
al.
(
Zi et al. (2019)
)
and Sheth et al
(
Sheth & Dezfouli (2019)
)
, respectively.

Some models combine several submodules of the complete system.
Verma et al.
(
Verma et al. (2019)
)
provide models of IoT devices together with the
network, and Yu et al.
(
Yu et al. (2019)
)
design the
IoT
subsystems from the user
equipment to the access points. Research produced by Dong et
al.
(
Dong et al. (2019)
)
also includes the computing infrastructure at the edge level,
thus presenting a complete fog computing architecture. However, the model does
not provide user equipment mobility or handovers.

There are software tools for modeling and simulation of specific parts of
IoT
and edge computing scenarios. For instance, COOJA
(
Osterlind et al. (2006)
)
is a simulator for

Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs)
that focuses on the firmware of
IoT
devices and the communication
mechanisms between sensors. The FogNetSim++ simulator
(
Qayyum et al. (2018)
)
, built on top
of the OMNeT++ network simulator
(
Varga (2010)
)
, focuses on connectivity issues of fog
infrastructures, such as end-users mobility, handover processes, and fog communication
protocols. In contrast, the RelIoT simulator
(
Ergun et al. (2020)
)
, built on top of the ns-3
network simulator
(
Henderson et al. (2008)
)
, analyzes the reliability of fog nodes of edge computing
scenarios. Alternatively, FogTorch
(
Brogi & Forti (2017)
)
is a prototype Monte Carlo-based
simulator that provides an in-depth analysis of the
QoS
of
IoT
applications.

On the other hand, there are complete Fog infrastructure solutions that allow complex

IoT
applications to be simulated with a wide variety of degrees of freedom.
Among all the available tools, the most popular ones are those developed on top of the
CloudSim simulator
(
Calheiros et al. (2011)
)
. First, iFogSim
(
Gupta et al. (2017)
)
adds sensor and
actuator classes to model
IoT
devices. Then, IOTSim
(
Zeng et al. (2017)
)
focuses on
optimizing computation offloading services based on the MapReduce
algorithm
(
Dean & Ghemawat (2008)
)
. Finally, EdgeCloudSim
(
Sonmez et al. (2018)
)
includes simple
nomadic mobility models for end-users. However, all of these developments are
implemented through software simulations that are not supported by mathematical formalisms.

This paper presents the mathematical description and the integration of the
complete
IoT
system, from the user’s
IoT
devices to the processing
units of the edge data centers. Our models follow the principles of
MBSE
,
ensuring an incremental design that is logic, robust, and reliable. Our research
contributes to the state-of-the-art by providing location-awareness, 5G
capabilities, and federated management of the complete infrastructure for

IoT
applications following the
FaaS
model.

System Model and Problem Formulation

This section first describes a standard edge
computing-based
IoT
architecture. Then, we formulate the
problem and depict a broad vision of the implementation carried
out to represent the model.

3.1
System Model

We consider an edge computing system with
User Equipment (UE)
devices, as
shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Edge Computing Scenario.

Each
UE
executes one or more
IoT
services that acquire
data from their environment. Data processing is offloaded to

EDCs
.
EDCs
are located at the edge of the network,
which is called
Radio Access Network (RAN)
, and have powerful computing capabilities
to improve significantly the quality of computation experience
perceived by each
UE
.
Access Points (APs)
act as gateways and
establish wireless communication channels between
UE
and
the
RAN
. For enhancing the perceived quality of the signal,

UE
always connect to their closest
AP
. As
UE
nodes
can change their location, handovers from one
AP
to another
may be triggered.
APs
forward data packages sent by

UE
to the
EDCs
via
Point-to-Point (P2P)
optical fiber
communication links.

The
Internet Service Provider (ISP)
that owns the
RAN
performs different network
management-related tasks using
Core Network Functions (CNFs)
. Concretely, the

Software-Defined Network (SDN)
Control Function monitors the availability of all the

EDCs
in the
RAN
and activates or deactivates the

P2P
links between
EDCs
and
APs
to maximize

UE
’s perceived
QoS
while ensuring resource management
policies. The
SDN
Control Function introduces dynamic network
slicing features within the
RAN
in a location-aware
manner, activating the
P2P
links between
every
AP
and its closest
EDC
with enough available
computing resources.

The approach followed for modeling
IoT
applications’ behavior is
based on the
FaaS
paradigm. No resources are dedicated to any
service in the
EDC
. Only when a
UE
request to open a
new service (i.e., establish a communication link with the
EDC
to send service-related data) will the
EDC
earmark the required
computing resources for granting computation offloading while the
service is active.

4.1
Edge Data Centers (
\mathbf{EDC}
)

\mathrm{EDC}
models the behavior of the infrastructure deployed for enabling

UE

FaaS

are pools of resources.

that handles this request will reserve the requested

expected by the

will be met once the service starts. When the service is stopped by the
UE
,
used resources are available again for any new service request.

DEVS

For each direction of the communication,

)

\frac{C}{{B}_{XL}}(UE)=\log_{2}(1+SNR_{XL}(UE))

(5)

Modulation and Codification Schemes (MCSs)

is based on 5G, we used the

5G New Radio (NR)

(
3gpp38214 (2018)
)

contains 28 different
MCS
with spectral efficiencies
ranging from
0.2344
to
7.4063

\mathrm{bits}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Hz}}^{-1}

for uplink, and 29 for downlink with spectral efficiencies ranging
from
0.2344
to
5.5547

\mathrm{bits}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Hz}}^{-1}
.

Finally, the maximum spectral efficiency that fulfills
the Shannon-Hartley theorem is chosen from
\mathbf{MCS}_{UL}
and

\mathbf{MCS}_{DL}
:

\displaystyle\text{eff}_{XL}(UE)=\text{max}

\displaystyle\{MCS\in\mathbf{MCS}_{XL}\}

(6)

\displaystyle\text{st. }

\displaystyle MCS\leq\frac{C}{B}_{XL}(UE)

The set

gets assigned a bandwidth share.
Radio bandwidth assignation is inversely proportional to

For every connected

The scenario under study consists of an incremental learning

Advanced Driver Assistance System (ADAS)

for detecting potential hazardous situations.
Vehicles gather new data at a rate of 1

To do so,

inject in real-time the new images to

Each

Resource and power consumption models for the

(

)

.
GPUs
power consumption model is based on an

On the other hand, timing properties for the

In this way, adding new images to the training data set does not

will add a delay of 0.2 seconds when opening
or closing a session. Furthermore, when an

commands a

will be busy for
1 second before being able to perform any other activity (as to activate a

For simulating the scenarios, we used Mercury

Conclusions and Future Work

This research presents a formal
M&S
specification focused
on deploying effective
IoT
architectures supported by
computation offloading on the edge of the network.
Edge computing infrastructures will be key technology enablers
for boosting the performance of new
IoT
services that rely
on
AI
and
ML
algorithms, providing a sustainable
and scalable approach for applying incremental learning
methodologies and improving in real-time service performance.

System specification was carried out following an

approach together with the
DEVS
mathematical formalism.
The model describes a complete architecture of edge scenarios,
from
UE
to
PUs
, and provides information about
intercommunication between entities, latency perceived by

UE

Location awareness, an inherent characteristic of edge

dynamic infrastructure re-configuration.

Using complete, formal
M&S
tools like the one described in
this research assists in the decision-making process required
for the deployment of edge computing infrastructure.
We exemplify its benefits by presenting a use case based on
the incremental learning of
ADAS
applications. We explore
different resource management policies using simulation
tools compliant with the model under study.
Simulation results are discussed, and the most suitable
configurations are identified, taking into account both

and power consumption.

Appendix A
The Discrete Event System Specification

DEVS
is a general formalism for discrete event system modeling based on set theory
(
Zeigler et al. (2000)
)
. The
DEVS
formalism provides the framework for information modeling which gives several advantages to analyze and design complex systems: completeness, verifiability, extensibility, and maintainability. Once a system is described in terms of the
DEVS
theory, it can be easily implemented using an existing computational library. The parallel
DEVS
(PDEVS) approach was introduced, after 15 years, as a revision of Classic
DEVS
. Currently, PDEVS is the prevalent DEVS, implemented in many libraries. In our work, unless it is explicitly noted, the use of
DEVS
implies PDEVS.

DEVS
enables the representation of a system by three sets and five functions: input set
(X)
, output set
(Y)
, state set
(S)
, external transition function (
\delta_{\rm ext}
), internal transition function (
\delta_{\rm int}
), confluent function (
\delta_{\rm con}
), output function (
\lambda
), and time advance function (
ta
).

DEVS models are of two types: atomic and coupled. Atomic models are directly expressed in the DEVS formalism specified above. Atomic DEVS processes input events based on their model’s current state and condition, generates output events and transition to the next state. The coupled model is the aggregation/composition of two or more atomic and coupled models connected by explicit couplings. Particularly, an atomic model is defined by the following equation:

A=\langle X,Y,S,\delta_{\rm ext},\delta_{\rm int},\delta_{\rm con},\lambda,ta\rangle

(13)

where:

•

X
is the set of inputs described in terms of pairs port-value:
\left\{p\in IPorts,v\in X_{p}\right\}
.

•

Y
is the set of outputs, also described in terms of pairs port-value:
\left\{p\in OPorts,v\in Y_{p}\right\}
.

•

S
is the set of sequential states.

•

\delta_{\mathrm{ext}}:Q\times X^{b}\rightarrow S
is the external transition function. It is automatically executed when an external event arrives to one of the input ports, changing the current state if needed.

–

Q={(s,e)s\in S,0\leq e\leq ta(s)}
is the total state set, where
e
is the time elapsed since the last transition.

–

X^{b}
is the set of bags over elements in
X
.

•

\delta_{\mathrm{int}}:S\rightarrow S
is the internal transition function. It is executed right after the output (
\lambda
) function and is used to change the state
S
.

•

\delta_{\mathrm{con}}:Q\times X^{b}\rightarrow S
is the confluent function, subject to
\delta_{\mathrm{con}}(s,ta(s),\emptyset)=\delta_{\mathrm{int}}(s)
. This transition decides the next state in cases of collision between external and internal events, i.e., an external event is received and elapsed time equals time-advance. Typically,
\delta_{\mathrm{con}}(s,ta(s),x)=\delta_{\mathrm{ext}}(\delta_{\mathrm{int}}(s),0,x)
.

•

\lambda:S\rightarrow Y^{b}
is the output function.
Y^{b}
is the set of bags over elements in
Y
. When the time elapsed since the last output function is equal to
ta(s)
, then
\lambda
is automatically executed.

•

ta(s):S\rightarrow\Re_{0}^{+}\cup\infty
is the time advance function.

An atomic model’s state is
s\in S
at any given time
t
. If no external events
occur, its state remains in
s
for a period of time
ta(s)
(i.e., time advance
function). When the lifetime expires, the atomic model sends a set of output
events
Y^{b}\in Y
according to its output function
\lambda(s)
, and changes its
state to a new one given by the internal transition function
\delta_{int}(s)
.
If one or more input events
X^{b}\in X
occur before the expiration of
ta(s)
,
the model changes to a new state determined by the external transition function

\delta_{ext}(s,e,X^{b})
. The confluent transition function
\delta_{con}

determines the next state in the case of collisions when a model receives
external events at the same time of its internal transition.

The formal definition of a coupled model is described as:

M=\langle X,Y,C,EIC,EOC,IC\rangle

(14)

where:

•

X
is the set of inputs described in terms of pairs port-value:
\left\{p\in IPorts,v\in X_{p}\right\}
.

•

Y
is the set of outputs, also described in terms of pairs port-value:
\left\{p\in OPorts,v\in Y_{p}\right\}
.

•

C
is a set of DEVS component models (atomic or coupled). Note that
C
makes this definition recursive.

•

EIC
is the external input coupling relation, from external inputs of
M
to component inputs of
C
.

•

EOC
is the external output coupling relation, from component outputs of
C
to external outputs of
M
.

•

IC
is the internal coupling relation, from component outputs of
c_{i}\in C
to component outputs of
c_{j}\in C
, provided that
i\neq j
.

Given the recursive definition of
M
, a coupled model can itself be a part of a component in a larger coupled model system giving rise to a hierarchical
DEVS
model construction.
