Abstract
. In this paper, we study an interplay between local and global properties of spaces of minimal usco maps equipped with the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets. In particular, for each locally compact space X X and metric space Y Y , we characterize the space of minimal usco maps from X X to Y Y , satisfying one of the following properties: (i) compact, (ii) locally compact, (iii) σ \sigma -compact, (iv) locally σ \sigma -compact, (v) metrizable, (vi) ccc, (vii) locally ccc, where in the last two items we additionally assumed that Y Y is separable and non-discrete. Some of the aforementioned results complement ones of Ľubica Holá and Dušan Holý. Also, we obtain analogical characterizations for spaces of minimal cusco maps.

2.
Preliminaries

A
space
means a nonempty Hausdorff topological space. In what follows,
X,Y,Z
are spaces. The symbols
\overline{A}
and
\intr(A)
stand for the closure and the interior of the set
A
. Let
\partial A=\overline{A}\setminus A
be the boundary of
A
.
For a space
Y
, the family of all nonempty compact subsets of
Y
is denoted by
K(Y)
.

A map, or a multifunction, from
X
to
Y
is a function that assigns to each element of
X
a subset of
Y
. If
F
is a map from
X
to
Y
, shortly
F\colon X\to Y
, then its graph is the set
\{(x,y)\in X\times Y:y\in F(x)\}
. In this paper, we identify both maps and functions with their graphs. By
\overline{F}
we denote the closure of the graph of
F
in the Tychonoff product
X\times Y
.

A map
F
is called
upper semicontinuous
if for every

x\in X
and open set
V
containing
F(x)
, there exists an open set
U
such that
x\in U
and
F(U)\subseteq V
.
Following Christensen
[
5
]
, we say that a map
F\colon X\to Y
is
usco
if it is upper semicontinuous and
F(x)
is a nonempty compact subset of
Y
for each
x\in X
. Finally, a map
F\colon X\to Y
is said to be
minimal usco
if it is minimal with respect to the inclusion among all usco maps from
X
to
Y
. That is,
F
is a minimal usco map if
F
does not contain properly any other usco map from
X
to
Y
.
Using the Kuratowski-Zorn principle, we can guarantee that every usco map contains a minimal usco map, see
[
16
]
.
By
MU(X,Y)
we denote the family of all minimal usco maps from
X
to
Y
. Note that
MU(X,Y)\subseteq K(Y)^{X}
.

A convenient tool to determine whether a map is minimal usco uses notions of quasicontinuity and subcontinuity of its selections. A function
f\colon X\to Y
is
quasicontinuous
, if for every
x\in X
and neighborhoods
U
of
x
and
V
of
f(x)
, there is a nonempty open set
G\subseteq U
such that
f(G)\subseteq V
, see
[
29
]
. Let
A
be a dense subset of
X
, then a function
f\colon A\to Y
is said to be
densely defined
on
X
. Moreover,
f
is called
subcontinuous
on
X
, if for every net
(x_{i})\subseteq A
convergent to some
x\in X
, the net
(f(x_{i}))
has a cluster point in
Y
, see
[
25
]
.
We say that a function
f\colon A\to Y
is a
dense selection
of a map
F\colon X\to Y
, if
A
is dense in
X
and for every
x\in A
,
f(x)\in F(X)
.
The following theorem is one of several characterizations of minimal usco maps from
[
17
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
Y
be regular, and
F\colon X\to Y
. Then
F\in MU(X,Y)
, if and only if
F
possesses a dense selection
f\colon A\to Y
that is subcontinuous on
X
, quasicontinuous on
A
and
\overline{f}=F
.

We will use the following notation of special maps throughout this paper.

Definition 2.2
.

For an open
U\subseteq X
and
a,b\in Y
define
f^{a,b}_{U}\colon X\setminus\partial U\to Y
by

f^{a,b}_{U}(x)=\begin{cases}a,&\mbox{if }x\in U,\\
b,&\mbox{if }x\not\in\overline{U},\end{cases}

Note that the function
f^{a,b}_{U}
is densely defined on
X
, it is continuous on
X\setminus\partial U
and subcontinuous on
X
, thus by Theorem
2.1
the map
F^{a,b}_{U}=\overline{f^{a,b}_{U}}\in MU(X,Y)
.

By
H_{d}
we denote Hausdorff metric on
K(Y)
(see
[
1
]
for more details). Next, following
[
9
]
, we shall define the topology
\tau_{uc}
of
uniform convergence on compact sets
on
K(Y)^{X}
.

Definition 2.3
.

The topology
\tau_{uc}
on the set
K(Y)^{X}
is induced by the uniformity
\mathcal{U}_{uc}
, which has the following base

\{W[K,\varepsilon]:K\text{ is compact and }\varepsilon>0\},

where

W[K,\varepsilon]=\{(F,G)\in K(Y)^{X}\times K(Y)^{X}:H_{d}(F(x),G(x))<\varepsilon\text{, for every }x\in K\}.

The general
\tau_{uc}
-basic neighborhood of
F\in K(Y)^{X}
will be denoted by
W(F,K,\varepsilon)
, i.e.,
W(F,K,\varepsilon)=W[K,\varepsilon](F)
.

In this paper, the set
MU(X,Y)
will be, by default, equipped with the subspace topology inherited from
(K(Y)^{X},\tau_{uc})
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
(Y,d)
be a metric space. A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq K(Y)^{X}
is called

(i)

densely equicontinuous at

x\in X
, if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a finite family
\mathcal{U}
of open subsets of
X
such that
x\in\intr\overline{\bigcup\mathcal{U}}
and for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
U\in\mathcal{U}
,
\diam F(U)<\varepsilon
;

(ii)

densely equicontinuous
, if
\mathcal{F}
is densely equicontinuous at every
x\in X
;

(iii)

pointwise bounded
, if for every
x\in X
, the set
\bigcup\{F(x):F\in\mathcal{F}\}
is relatively compact, i.e., its closure in
Y
is compact.

The following analogue of Arzela–Ascoli theorem for minimal usco maps (see
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
) is crucial for this paper.

Theorem 2.5

(Holá–Holý)
.

Let
X
be a locally compact topological space and
(Y,d)
be a metric space. A subset
\mathcal{F}\subseteq MU(X,Y)
is compact if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is closed, densely equicontinuous, and pointwise bounded.

We shall use the following trivial fact a few times in sections
3
and
4
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
X
be a locally compact space and
K
a compact subset of
X
. Then there exists an open set
U\subseteq X
such that
K\subseteq U
and
\overline{U}
is compact.

Further
X
will always be a locally compact Hausdorff space, and
Y
a metric space equipped with a metric
d
. Note that under these assumptions, by
[
14
, Lemma 3.3]
,
MU(X,Y)
is a closed subset of
(K(Y)^{X},\tau_{uc})
.

For every
a\in Y
, denote a constant map with value
\{a\}
as
c_{a}
. The following lemmas are trivial, so we omit their proofs.

Lemma 2.7
.

The function
\phi\colon Y\rightarrow MU(X,Y)
defined by
\phi(a)=c_{a}
is an isometry. Moreover,
\phi(Y)
is closed in
MU(X,Y)
.

Lemma 2.8
.

If
X
is discrete, then
MU(X,Y)=Y^{X}
.

3.
Minimal usco maps

Recall that we, by default, assume that
X
is a locally compact space and
(Y,d)
a metric space. We start with the following simple facts necessary for the proofs of lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
, which are vital for this paper.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X
be such that
X=K\cup D
for some compact set
K
and discrete set
D
. Then there exists a compact open set
K^{\prime}\supseteq K
. In particular,
X
is a topological sum of a compact space
K^{\prime}
and a discrete space
X\setminus K^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
X
is locally compact, by Lemma
2.6
, there exists an open set
U\supseteq K
such that
\overline{U}
is compact. Fix any
x\in D\setminus U
. Since
D
is discrete and
K
is closed, there exists an open neighborhood
V
of
x
such that
V\cap K=\emptyset
and
V\cap D=\{x\}
. Thus the set
V\cap(X\setminus K)=\{x\}
is open in
X
, witnessing that
x\notin\overline{U}
. Since the point
x
was chosen arbitrarily, we get that
U=\overline{U}
. Hence
X
is a topological sum of a compact open set
U
and a discrete set
X\setminus U
, as required.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq K(Y)^{X}
is densely equicontinuous at
x\in X
, if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
and every open neighborhood
O
of
x
there is a finite family
\mathcal{U}
of open subsets of
X
such that

(i)

x\in\intr\overline{\bigcup\mathcal{U}}
;

(ii)

x\in\overline{U}
and
U\subseteq O
for every
U\in\mathcal{U}
;

(iii)

\diam F(U)<\varepsilon
for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
U\in\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

The implication
(\Leftarrow)
is trivial.

(\Rightarrow)
: Fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. There is a finite family
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\}
of open subsets of
X
such that
x\in\intr\overline{\bigcup\mathcal{U}}
and for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
U\in\mathcal{U}
,
\diam F(U)<\varepsilon
. Thus, there is an open neighborhood
V
of
x
such that
V\subseteq\overline{\bigcup\mathcal{U}}
, and also there is
U\in\mathcal{U}
such that
x\in\overline{U}
. Let
A=\{i\leq n:x\notin\overline{U_{i}}\}
and
B=\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus A
. Then for every open neighborhood
O
of
x
we have

x\in O\cap V\cap\bigcap_{i\in A}(X\setminus\overline{U_{i}})\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in B}O\cap\overline{U_{i}}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in B}\overline{O\cap U_{i}}

and thus the family
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\{O\cap U_{i}:i\in B\}
satisfies the required properties.
∎

We shall deal with the following special subset of
MU(X,Y)
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let

D_{2}(X,Y)=\{F\in MU(X,Y):|F(X)|\leq 2\}.

Lemma 3.4
.

The set
D_{2}(X,Y)
is closed in
MU(X,Y)
.

Proof.

Consider any
F\in MU(X,Y)
such that
|F(X)|>2
. Fix distinct
a,b,c\in F(X)
. Then there exists a finite subset
K\subseteq X
such that
\{a,b,c\}\subseteq F(K)
. Fix a positive real
\varepsilon
such that
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}\min\{d(a,b),d(a,c),d(b,c)\}
. At this point, it is easy to see that
W(F,K,\varepsilon)\cap D_{2}(X,Y)=\emptyset
. Hence the set
D_{2}(X,Y)
is closed.
∎

We say that a topological space is
non-trivial
if it has at least two points. Observe that
MU(X,Y)
is non-trivial if and only if
Y
is non-trivial.
Therefore, in the rest of the paper, we assume that
Y
is a non-trivial metric space.
The following two lemmas play a key role in this paper.

Lemma 3.5
.

If the subspace
D_{2}(X,Y)
of
MU(X,Y)
is locally
\sigma
-compact, then
X
is a topological sum of a compact space and a discrete space.
Moreover, if
Y
is non-discrete, then
X
is discrete.

Proof.

Let us first show that
X
is a topological sum of a compact space and a discrete space.
Fix any
a\in Y
and note that
c_{a}\in D_{2}(X,Y)
. Then there is a compact
K\subseteq X
and
\eta>0
such that

(1)

\overline{W(c_{a},K,\eta)}\cap D_{2}(X,Y)=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{F}_{n},

where for each
n\in\omega
the family
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is compact.

By Lemma
3.1
, it is enough to prove that
X\setminus K
is discrete.
Let
a,b\in Y
be two distinct points. Fix a positive
\varepsilon<d(a,b)
and an arbitrary
x\in X\setminus K
. By Theorem
2.5
, we have that the families
\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
n\in\omega
are densely equicontinuous at
x
.
By Lemma
3.2
, for every
n\in\omega
and open neighborhood
O
of
x
there is a finite family
\mathcal{U}_{n,O}
of open subsets of
X
such that
x\in\intr\overline{\bigcup\mathcal{U}_{n,O}}
; for every
U\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
,
U\subseteq O
; and for every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
U\in\mathcal{U}_{n,O}
,
\diam F(U)<\varepsilon
.

Claim
.

There is
n\in\omega
and an open neighborhood
O
of
x
such that the family
\mathcal{U}_{n,O}
consists of finite open sets.

Proof of the claim:

Seeking a contradiction, assume that for each
n\in\omega
and open neighborhood
O
of
x
the family
\mathcal{U}_{n,O}
contains an infinite element. We are going to construct a sequence of points and open sets by induction.
For
n=0
let
O_{0}
be an arbitrary open neighborhood of
x
such that
\overline{O_{0}}\cap X\setminus K=\emptyset
. By the assumption, there exists an infinite
U_{0}\in\mathcal{U}_{0,O_{0}}
. There are
y_{0},z_{0}\in U_{0}\subseteq O_{0}
such that
x,y_{0},z_{0}
are all distinct. Choose disjoint open sets
V_{0},W_{0}\subseteq U_{0}
such that
y_{0}\in V_{0}
,
z_{0}\in W_{0}
and
x\not\in\overline{V_{0}}\cup\overline{W_{0}}
.

Suppose that for all
j<n
we constructed open sets
O_{j},U_{j},V_{j},W_{j}
and points
y_{j}\in V_{j}
,
z_{j}\in W_{j}
such that
x\in O_{j}
and
x\not\in\overline{V_{j}}\cup\overline{W_{j}}
. Define an open neighborhood
O_{n}
of
x
by

O_{n}=O_{n-1}\cap(X\setminus\overline{V_{n-1}})\cap(X\setminus\overline{W_{n-1}}).

Choose an infinite set
U_{n}\in\mathcal{U}_{n,O_{n}}
. There are
y_{n},z_{n}\in U_{n}\subseteq O_{n}
such that
x,y_{n},z_{n}
are all distinct. Fix disjoint open sets
V_{n},W_{n}\subseteq U_{n}
such that
y_{n}\in V_{n}
,
z_{n}\in W_{n}
and
x\not\in\overline{V_{n}}\cup\overline{W_{n}}
.

By the construction, all of the sets in
\{V_{n},W_{n}:n\in\omega\}
are pairwise disjoint. Put
V=(X\setminus\overline{O_{0}})\cup\bigcup_{n\in\omega}V_{n}
. Since
K\subseteq X\setminus\overline{O_{0}}
, we get that
F^{a,b}_{V}\in W(c_{a},K,\eta)\cap D_{2}(X,Y)
. Observe that for every
n\in\omega
,
F^{a,b}_{V}(U_{n})=\{a,b\}
and
d(a,b)>\varepsilon
. So
F^{a,b}_{V}\notin\mathcal{F}_{n}
for each
n\in\omega
, which contradicts Equation
1
.
∎

The claim above implies that the set
\bigcup\mathcal{U}_{n,O}=\intr(\overline{\bigcup\mathcal{U}_{n,O}})
is a finite open neighborhood of
x
, witnessing that
x
is an isolated point. Since
x
was chosen arbitrarily, we obtain that
X\setminus K
is discrete.

Now, assume that
Y
is non-discrete. Let us show that
X
is discrete.
There is
a\in Y
such that for every
\eta>0
there exists
b\in Y
with
d(a,b)<\eta
. Then for every compact
K\subseteq X
and open
V\subseteq X
we have
F^{a,b}_{V}\in W(c_{a},K,\eta)\cap D_{2}(X,Y)
. We repeat the previous arguments with the following changes: now
x
is an arbitrary point of
X
(it can belong to
K
) and
O_{0}=X
. This way, we show that an arbitrary point of
X
possesses a finite open neighborhood, implying that
X
is discrete.
∎

The proof of the following lemma is similar to the one of Lemma

.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
D_{2}(X,Y)
is
\sigma
-compact, then

is discrete.

Proof.

Since the space
D_{2}(X,Y)
is
\sigma
-compact, there exist compact subsets
\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
n\in\omega
of
D_{2}(X,Y)
such that

D_{2}(X,Y)=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

Fix an arbitrary
x\in X
, distinct
a,b\in Y
and a positive
\varepsilon<d(a,b)
. By Theorem
2.5
, we have that the families
\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
n\in\omega
are densely equicontinuous at
x
.
By Lemma
3.2
, for every
n\in\omega
and open neighborhood
O
of
x
there is a finite family
\mathcal{U}_{n,O}
of open subsets of
X
such that
x\in\intr\overline{\bigcup\mathcal{U}_{n,O}}
; for every
U\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
,
U\subseteq O
; and for every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
and

,
\diam F(U)<\varepsilon
.
Similarly, as in the proof of Lemma

, it can be shown that there exists

and an open neighborhood

of

such that
\mathcal{U}_{n,O}
consists of finite open sets. It follows that the point

∎

The following theorem characterizes compactness of a space
MU(X,Y)
.

Theorem 3.7
.

The following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

MU(X,Y)
is compact;

(2)

D_{2}(X,Y)
is compact;

(3)

Y
is compact and
X
is discrete.

Proof.

Implication (1)
\Rightarrow
(2) follows from Lemma
3.4
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3): Note that
Y
, being homeomorphic to the closed subspace of
D_{2}(X,Y)
consisting of all constant maps, is compact. Discreteness of
X
follows from Lemma
3.6
.

The implication (3)
\Rightarrow
(1) follows from Lemma
2.8
.
∎

The following two technical lemmas will be useful.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
X
be a topological sum of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, then the space
MU(X,Y)
is homeomorphic to
MU(X_{1},Y)\times MU(X_{2},Y)
.

Proof.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, define
\varphi_{i}\colon MU(X,Y)\to MU(X_{i},Y)
by
\varphi_{i}(F)=F{\restriction}X_{i}
.
Let us define the map

\varphi\colon MU(X,Y)\to MU(X_{1},Y)\times MU(X_{2},Y)

by

\varphi(F)=(\varphi_{1}(F),\varphi_{2}(F)),\hbox{ for every }F\in MU(X,Y).

One can easily verify that
\varphi
is bijective with
\varphi^{-1}((F_{1},F_{2}))=F_{1}\cup F_{2}
.

Note that for every compact
K\subseteq X
we have that
K=K_{1}\cup K_{2}
, where
K_{i}=K\cap X_{i}\in K(X_{i})
.
We claim that for any
F\in MU(X,Y)
, the following holds:

\varphi_{1}(W(F,K,\varepsilon))=W(F{\restriction}X_{1},K_{1},\varepsilon).

The inclusion
\varphi_{1}(W(F,K,\varepsilon))\subseteq W(F{\restriction}X_{1},K_{1},\varepsilon)
is straightforward. For the reverse inclusion, observe that any
F_{1}\in W(F{\restriction}X_{1},K_{1},\varepsilon)
can be extended to
G\in W(F,K,\varepsilon)
, where
G=F_{1}\cup F{\restriction}X_{2}
.
Dually, one can check that

\varphi_{2}(W(F,K,\varepsilon))=W(F{\restriction}X_{2},K_{2},\varepsilon),

and thus

\varphi(W(F,K,\varepsilon))=W(F{\restriction}X_{1},K_{1},\varepsilon)\times W(F{\restriction}X_{2},K_{2},\varepsilon).

It is easy to see that
\varphi
is a homeomorphism.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
Y
be non-compact. If
MU(X,Y)
is locally
\sigma
-compact, then
X
is compact.

Proof.

Seeking a contradiction, assume that
X
is not compact. By lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
, we have that
X
is a topological sum of a compact set
K
and an infinite discrete set
D
. By Lemma
3.8
, we have that
MU(X,Y)
is homeomorphic to
MU(K,Y)\times MU(D,Y)
. In particular,
MU(D,Y)
can be embedded as a closed subset in
MU(X,Y)
. By Lemma
2.8
,
MU(D,Y)=Y^{D}
. Since
Y
is a non-compact metric space, it has a countably infinite closed discrete subset
B
. Since
D
is infinite, it contains a countable subset
C
. Thus, Baire space
\omega^{\omega}
is homeomorphic to a closed subset
B^{C}
of
Y^{D}
, i.e., it can be embedded as a closed subset of
MU(X,Y)
. Recall that
