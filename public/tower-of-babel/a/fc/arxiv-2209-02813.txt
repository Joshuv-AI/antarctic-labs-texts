Diluted-dispersed mass transfer within an AWE
N.Valle

Abstract
The goal of this document is describe the multiphase transfer processes describing the bubble dynamics of a water electrolyzer. The motivation is to describe the dilute-dispersed mass transfer within and Alkaline Water Electrolyzer. Special emphasis is put on the mathematical formulation. The presentation starts by posing the governing equations and their dimensionless counterpart. By filtering the equations, the two-fluid model is presented along with the need to sub-scale and wall models. To the later aim, boundary layer equations are introduced. By reviewing self-similiarity transformations, the analysis of Blasius, Ostrach and Sparrow is reviewed for Prandtl’s boundary layer equations; along with that of Leveque.

Boussinesq

While both density and viscosity are functions of the local supersaturation,

This is reasonable as far as the concentrations are close to saturation.
Consequently, we will consider them constant everywhere except for the

Nonetheless,

Note, however,

whereas for the liquid phase,
whose concentration is variable,

both contributes to the shrinking of the gas phase and its dilution.

3
Filtering

The solution of the aforementioned equations is know to
exhibit very fine spatio-temporal features.
These are mainly due to two main physical process:

(i)

the chaotic (turbulent) nature of the solution,
but also due to

(ii)

small-scale flow dynamics

However,
in most engineering applications we are interested in the large scale features.
Mathematically,
we can formulate these large scale features as a filtering of the solution.
We are then interested in obtaining filtered form of the governing equations,
which (ideally) is expressed in terms of the filtered variables only.

To do so, we introduce the filtering operator

\langle\cdot\rangle=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\cdot d\Omega

(36)

where
\Omega
could be a characteristic time, space or thermodynamic state,
giving rise to time- space- or ensemble-averaged filters.
After filtering,
one can perform a Reynolds decomposition of any variable
as an average value plus an oscillation from that average

u=\overline{u}+u^{\prime}

(37)

subject to
\langle u^{\prime}\rangle=0
.

By filtering the governing
equations
38
-
43
, we
obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\epsilon_{l}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\epsilon_{l}\tilde{U}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rho_{g}}{\rho_{l}}\frac{Sh_{b}}{Sc}\epsilon_{l}\overline{\zeta}\delta_{\Gamma}-\boxed{\nabla\cdot\overline{\phi_{l}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}

(38)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\epsilon_{g}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\epsilon_{g}\tilde{U}

\displaystyle=+\frac{Sh_{b}}{Sc}\epsilon_{g}\tilde{\zeta}\delta_{\Gamma}-\boxed{\nabla\cdot\overline{\phi_{g}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\epsilon_{l}\tilde{U}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\epsilon_{l}\tilde{U}\otimes\tilde{U}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\epsilon_{l}\overline{P}+\nabla\cdot\epsilon_{l}\tilde{S}+Gr\overline{\zeta}\hat{g}+\overline{M_{l}}-\boxed{\nabla\cdot\epsilon\overline{u^{\prime}\otimes u^{\prime}}}

(40)

\displaystyle\rho^{*}\frac{\partial\epsilon_{g}\tilde{U}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\epsilon_{g}\tilde{U}\otimes\tilde{U}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\epsilon_{g}\overline{P}+\mu^{*}\nabla\cdot\epsilon_{g}\tilde{S}+\rho^{*}Ar\hat{g}+\overline{M_{g}}-\boxed{\nabla\cdot\epsilon\overline{u^{\prime}\otimes u^{\prime}}}

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\epsilon_{l}\overline{\zeta}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\epsilon_{l}\overline{\zeta}\tilde{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Sc}\nabla^{2}\epsilon_{l}\overline{\zeta}+\frac{Da_{\Pi}}{Sc}\epsilon_{l}(\overline{\zeta}+1)+\frac{Sh_{b}}{Sc}\epsilon_{l}\overline{\zeta}-\boxed{\nabla\cdot\epsilon\overline{\zeta^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}

(42)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{g}\overline{\zeta}

\displaystyle=1

(43)

where we have introduced new terms (boxed) arising from the oscillatory
behavior of the solution.
These terms require a sub-grid scale model in order to close the relations.

Let’s analyze them in detail:

\overline{u^{\prime}\otimes u^{\prime}}

this terms is the
Reynolds stress tensor
and describes the
additional stress experienced by the flow due to the turbulent
oscillations.
The most common way to model these terms are the
eddy viscosity
models
, which assimilate them with an additional diffusion term.

\overline{\zeta^{\prime}u^{\prime}}

this terms is analogous to the turbulent dispersion force.
However, it has two contributions: the purely turbulent dispersion,
and the effect of the micromixing produced in the vicinity of the wall.
Assuming the Reynolds analogy, we can assume that the turbulent
dispersion is proportional to the eddy viscosity,
and so can be seen as the modification of the local Schmidt number.
Regarding the micromixing effects, we need to come up with an additional
closure term to model them.

3.1
Modeling of sub-scale terms

Mass transfer

We will assess here the contribution of sub-scale terms to the mass transfer
equation.
In particular, we will look at the filtered term of
equation (
43
)
\overline{\zeta^{\prime}\otimes u^{\prime}}
.
This term includes two different contributions which are very different in its
nature.

The first contribution is
micromixing
,
which is due to a perturbation within the mass transfer boundary layer.
When the solution is not locally isotropic within a computational domain
(e.g., near a wall)
filtering will result smear the solution,
introducing a non-physical damping of the solution.
In this cases,
we need to advance the resolution of the computation by so-called wall models.

The second contribution is
turbulent dispersion
due to the turbulent
oscillations of the system.
On the other hand,
even when the solution is oscillatory within the computational domain,
since non–linear terms
(e.g., convection)
do not commute with filtering,
result into additional terms due to the propagation of the oscillations.
Such additional terms need to be modeled by what we call sub-grid scale models.
This term can be modeled analogously to the Reynolds stress term reported before
by means of and eddy viscosity model.

Wall model

Since the typical size of mass transfer boundary layers is much smaller than the
hydrodynamic ones, since they scale as:

\frac{\delta}{\delta_{c}}=Sc^{-1/3}

(44)

Sc>>1
we obtain
\delta_{c}<<\delta
.
Since the resolution required to resolve
\delta

is already very high,
we can model the mass transfer boundary by means of a Sherwood-like expression
as:

\nabla\zeta\rvert_{w}=Sh_{w}c

(45)

which allows to work with a much coarser mesh.

4.1
Prandtl boundary layer equations

Ludwig’s Prandtl model for the boundary layer equations assumes a
2D,
incompressible,
stationary flow in the vicinity of a plate.
This results into the following set of equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=0

(47)

\displaystyle u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}+\nu\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial y^{2}}

(48)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=0

(49)

assume that the variations along the stream-wise direction are much smaller than
the variation in the wall-normal one.

Note that
equations (
47

49

three unknowns (
u

v
and
p

To simplify the equation a little bit further, we may assume that pressure
variation in the streamwise direction is either zero (as for the case of
boundary plates) or just hydrostatic (i.e.,
p=\rho gx
).

However, since the flow is incompressible, we can express velocities in terms of
a single streamfunction,
\Psi

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial y}

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}

This results in the following, single-variable PDE:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial y}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial x\partial y}-\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial y\partial y}

\displaystyle=\nu\frac{\partial^{3}\Psi}{\partial y^{2}y}+b

\displaystyle\left.u\right|_{y=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right|_{x\to\infty}=0

5
Self-similarity solutions

Equation (
51
) involves two variables (
x
and
y
),
which complicates its solution by analytical or numerical methods.

Self-similarity solutions are solutions to PDEs in terms of a self-similarity
variable,
\eta
which in general it is a function of both
x
and
y
.
In this way, we can say that the solutions of the system at any point in space
(i.e., in terms of
x
and
y
) are “similar” to the solution in terms of

\eta
.

This tool allows to reduce the number of variables, provided that a
self-similarity variable can be found or guessed.

Since most of the times we do not know the exact form of the self-similarity
function
a priori
,
we attempt generic functions for the self-similarity variables and functions,
substitute them in the formulation of the original PDE
and finally the generic function to make sure that the resulting PDE is a
function of the self-similarity variables solely.
We typically do so by assuming some sort of polynomial function on
x
and
y
.
The procedure is the following:

First,
we do assume a shape for the self-similarity variable
\eta
and the
self-similarity solution
\Psi
.
These functions can, in principle, be any analytic function.
As fluid dynamicists,
this is where our understanding of the physics of the problem,
funneled trough scaling arguments,
may lead to fine educated guesses on the shape of the equations.

On the one hand,
we assume that the solution is self-similar in the streamwise direction.
This can be hinted from the shape of the equations,
since it includes a purely convective term
(which has a purely transport character)
in the streamwise direction,
while diffusion
(which has a spreading character)
in the wall-normal direction.
Since we are looking at boundary layer flows,
we render
\eta=y/\delta
to represent the ratio of the wall-normal direction

y
to the thickness of the boundary layer
\delta
, being
\delta
some
function of the streamwise coordinate
x
.
We further hint,
that
\delta
is proportional to some power of
x
.
As such,
our candidate
\eta
looks like:

\eta=Ayx^{-s}

(57)

The velocity field is self-similiar in the streamwise direction.
However, since velocity is the derivative of the streamfunction,
\Psi
,
this implies that
\Psi
is a combination of
a self-similar function
f(\eta)

and some power of
x
.
This renders our candidate
f
as:

f(\eta)=B\Psi x^{-t}

(58)

The actual values of
s
and
t
is what gives
the scalings
of the
solution.

While some candidates may exist for
s
and
t
from experimental fittings,
we cannot say much on its value
a priori
.
As such,
we will then proceed to obtain a self-similarity solution for
equation (
51
).

We start by describing the following intermediate variables:

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{B}x^{t-s}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle-v

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}x^{t-1}\left(tf-s\eta f^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial x\partial y}

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{B}x^{t-s-1}\bigl((t-s)f^{\prime}-s\eta f^{\prime\prime}\bigr)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial y^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{2}}{B}x^{t-2s}f^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial y^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{3}\Psi}{\partial y^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{3}}{B}x^{t-3s}f^{\prime\prime\prime}

where
we have adopted the prime notation
(^{\prime})
to denote differentiation with respect
to
\eta

Since

\eta

and all differentiation is taken with respect to

This produces:

\displaystyle=0=\left.\frac{A}{B}x^{t-s}f^{\prime}\right|_{\eta=0}

\displaystyle\left.f^{\prime}\right|_{0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle=0=-\left.\frac{1}{B}x^{t-1}\left(tf-s\eta f^{\prime}\right)\right|_{\eta=0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle=0=\left.\frac{A}{B}x^{t-s}f^{\prime}\right|_{x\to\infty}

\displaystyle=0

6
Leveque equation

For the advection of passive scalars,
when
Sc>>1

(as it is typically the case in mass transfer)
the mass transfer boundary layer is much thinner than the hydrodynamic one.
This means that a linearization of the velocity profile is possible as:

w(x)\approx\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}x=w^{\prime}x

(78)

This was observed by Leveque, who proposed a new scaling as:

w^{\prime}x\frac{\partial c}{\partial z}=D\frac{\partial^{2}c}{\partial x^{2}}

(79)

where
w^{\prime}
and
D
can be functions of
z
only.

This last assumption is made in order to reuse Blasius-like expressions for

w^{\prime}
, which then evolves in the streamwise direction
z
.

6.1
Dirichlet boundary conditions

To which we can attempt a self-similarity transformation as:

\eta=Axf(z)^{-s}

(80)

where now
f(x)
can be any function of x.
On the other hand, the actual variable
c
is:

c=c(\eta)

(81)

By substituting these into the original equation (
79
), we obtain:

\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{c^{\prime}}=-s\frac{w^{\prime}}{D}f^{\prime}\frac{\eta^{2}}{A^{3}}f^{3s-1}

(82)

In order to satisfy self-similarity, we impose the following:

\displaystyle 3s-1

\displaystyle=0

(83)

\displaystyle\frac{w^{\prime}}{D}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=1

(84)

from where we obtain the final Leveque scaling:

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}

(85)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\int\frac{D}{w^{\prime}}dz

(86)

With this scaling,
equation (
79
) becomes:

\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{c^{\prime}}=-\frac{1}{A^{3}}\frac{1}{3}\eta^{2}\\

(87)

which is an eigenvalue problem.
We can reduce the order of the problem with realizing that

\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{c^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}ln(c^{\prime})

(88)

Solving for it we obtain:

c^{\prime}=Cexp\left(-\frac{1}{A^{3}}\frac{\eta^{3}}{9}\right)

(89)

From where we can see that, for convenience, we can impose
A
as:

A=\left(\frac{1}{9}\right)^{1/3}

(90)

such that equation (
89
)

c^{\prime}=Cexp\left(-\eta^{3}\right)

(91)

Once
A
is defined, the similarity is:

\eta=y\left(\frac{1}{9\int\frac{D}{w^{\prime}}dx}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}

(92)

The final expression for
c
is obtained by integration between
\eta
and

\infty
.

c_{\infty}-c(\eta)=C\int_{\eta}^{\infty}exp\left(-\eta^{3}\right)d\eta=C

(93)

6.2
Neumann boundary conditions

For Neumann boundary conditions:

\displaystyle D\frac{\partial c}{\partial y}\lvert_{y=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{j}{zF}

(94)

\displaystyle c\lvert_{y\to\infty}

\displaystyle=c_{sat}

(95)

To which we attempt a self-similiarity solution of the form:

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=Ayf(z)^{-s}

(96)

\displaystyle\theta(\eta)

\displaystyle=B\Delta cf(z)^{-u}

(97)

where
\Delta c=c-c_{s}at
.
We scale
\theta
as an arbitrary power of the shape function
f(x)
.
Substituting this scaling in the boundary conditions we obtain:

\displaystyle f^{u-s}\theta^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{j}{zFD}\frac{B}{A}

(98)

\displaystyle\theta(\infty)

\displaystyle=0

(99)

which requires
u-s=0
in order to obtain a self-similarity condition.
This means that
\eta
and
\theta
scale the same.

Introducing now the scaling in the Leveque equation (
79
),
we obtain the following ODE:

w^{\prime}\eta A^{-1}f^{s+u-1}f^{\prime}\left(u\theta-s\eta\theta^{\prime}\right)=DA^{2}f^{-2s}B^{-1}f^{u}\theta^{\prime\prime}

(100)

which can be rearranged as

\frac{w^{\prime}}{D}f^{\prime}f^{3s-1}\eta\left(u\theta-s\eta\theta^{\prime}\right)=A^{3}\theta^{\prime\prime}

(101)

from where we can impose the following self-similarity conditions:

\displaystyle\frac{w^{\prime}}{D}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=1

(102)

\displaystyle 3s-1

\displaystyle=0

(103)

Self-similarity requires then scaling and the shape function as

\displaystyle f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{D}{w^{\prime}}

(104)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}

(105)

which fully define the self-similarity.
The equation then takes the form

3A^{3}\theta^{\prime\prime}+\eta^{2}\theta^{\prime}-\eta\theta=0

(106)

where
A
can be chosen to simplify the system as

A=\left(\frac{1}{9}\right)^{1/3}

(107)

which renders the final form of the Leveque equation with Neumann boundary
conditions as:

\frac{1}{3}\theta^{\prime\prime}+\eta^{2}\theta^{\prime}-\eta\theta=0

(108)

which has an analytical solution if we attempt a solution like:

\theta(\eta)=\eta v(\eta)

(109)

which accepts an analytical solution of the form

\theta(\lambda)=CE_{\frac{4}{3}}\left(\lambda^{3}\right)=C\lambda\Gamma\left(-\frac{1}{3},\lambda^{3}\right)

(110)

where
E_{i}
is the exponential integral,
while
\Gamma
is the incomplete gamma function.
Taking its boundary conditions we obtain:

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)C

(111)

\displaystyle\theta(\infty)

\displaystyle=0

(112)

Thus, the final form of
\theta
becomes:

\theta(\eta)=\eta\frac{\Gamma\left(-\frac{1}{3},x^{3}\right)}{\Gamma\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)}=\eta Q\left(-\frac{1}{3},x^{3}\right)

(113)

where
Q
is the regularized upper incomplete gamma function.

The final expression for mass then becomes:

\Delta c_{wall}=\frac{q}{D}\theta(\eta)\left(9\int_{0}^{z}\frac{D}{w^{\prime}}dt\right)^{1/3}

(114)

which yields the expression for mass transfer coefficient as:

k_{f}=\frac{1}{\theta(0)}\left(9\int_{0}^{z}\frac{D}{w^{\prime}}dt\right)^{-1/3}

(115)

Where the final Sherwood number is:

Sh_{f}=\frac{k_{f}z}{D}=\frac{1}{\theta(0)}z\left(9\int_{0}^{z}\frac{D}{w^{\prime}}dt\right)^{-1/3}

(116)
