Further comments on the background of the problem, proof strategy, and related literature

It was observed long ago that an ill-posed differential equation can become well-posed when perturbed by random noise. Indeed, consider the reaction-diffusion equation

\partial_{t}u_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}u_{t}(x)+b(u_{t}(x)),\quad t\in(0,T],\,x\in D.

If the drift

[
GP93a
,
GP93b

showed that the corresponding stochastic equation
SHE(

0

;

This phenomenon has also been observed in other models: within the framework of rough path theory

[
CGL+24
]

An overview of the topic is given by Gess in
[

Equations with drifts less regular than those in

;

Thus, one can pose a natural question: how irregular can the drift

;

To show weak uniqueness we develop a new approach which combines stochastic sewing with certain ideas from ergodic theory (generalized couplings). First, let us recall that a powerful tool to establish the unique ergodicity of a Markov process is the coupling method pioneered by Doeblin in the 1930s
[
Doe38
]
; see also
[
Lig93
]
for a review of Doeblin’s contributions and their subsequent development. Assume that we are given a Markov transition kernel
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
and would like to show convergence of transition probabilities
P_{t}(x,\cdot)
and
P_{t}(y,\cdot)
, where
x
and
y
are in the state space of the process. The main idea is to construct two copies of the Markov process,
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, starting from
x
and
y
, respectively, and such that
\law(X_{t})=P_{t}(x,\cdot)
,
\law(Y_{t})=P_{t}(y,\cdot)
. Then, clearly,

d_{TV}(P_{t}(x,\cdot),P_{t}(y,\cdot))\leqslant\mathsf{P}(X_{t}\neq Y_{t}),

where
d_{TV}
stands for the total variation distance. Thus, if we can construct processes
X
and
Y
in such a way that the probability they do not couple by time
t
tends to
0
as
t\to\infty
, we get weak convergence of the transition probabilities and we can even bound the convergence rate. Of course, the main challenge now is how to construct processes
X
and
Y
to make
\mathsf{P}(X_{t}\neq Y_{t})
small. For SDEs driven by Brownian motion, this strategy was successfully implemented in
[
Ver88
]
, where exponential ergodicity was established. We refer to
[
EGZ19
]
for recent advancements.

While this strategy works quite well for finite-dimensional Markov processes, it typically fails when the state space is infinite-dimensional. The main reason is that the transition probabilities
P_{t}(x,\cdot)
and
P_{t}(y,\cdot)
are often orthogonal, resulting in zero probability for the processes
X
and
Y
to meet for any coupling. This phenomenon is discussed further in
[
HMS11
, Section 1]
and
[
Kul18
, Section 4.1]
and specific examples of processes with mutually orthogonal transition probabilities are provided also in
[
Sch05
]
(stochastic delay equation) and
[
BS20
, Theorem 4.8]
(stochastic heat equation).

To study such processes, Hairer, Mattingly, Scheutzow, and Bakhtin in a series of works in the 2000s
[
Hai02
,
Mat02
,
BM05
,
HMS11
]
developed a new approach called the generalized coupling method. The main idea is to “help” the process
Y
to become closer to the process
X
. Therefore, an intermediate process
\widetilde{Y}
is introduced, which starts at the same initial point as
Y
and receives an additional push towards
X
, for example, in the form
\lambda(X-\widetilde{Y})
for large
\lambda>0
. Consequently, the distance between
\widetilde{Y}
and
X
rapidly decreases. Of course, the pair
(X,\widetilde{Y})
is not a true coupling of
P_{t}(x,\cdot)
and
P_{t}(y,\cdot)
, hence the name “generalized coupling”. Nevertheless, it is often possible to show (usually with the help of Girsanov’s theorem) that the process
\widetilde{Y}
is not too far from
Y
in total variation distance, allowing one to conclude

W_{d}(P_{t}(x,\cdot),P_{t}(y,\cdot))\leqslant d_{TV}(\law(Y_{t}),\law(\widetilde{Y}_{t}))+\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt|\widetilde{Y}_{t}-X_{t}|,

where
W_{d}
denotes an appropriate Wasserstein distance. We address an interested reader to
[
Hai02
, Section 1.1]
, where the generalized coupling method is illustrated on a toy model. The latest developments of the method can be found in
[
GHMR17
,
BKS20
]
.

Kulik and Scheutzow
[
KS20
]
adapted this method to show weak uniqueness for stochastic delay equations with irregular drift. They take as
X
a weak solution to a stochastic equation with irregular drift and as
Y^{n}
a solution to a well-posed approximating equation. They introduce an intermediate process
\widetilde{Y}^{n}
, which serves a similar role as
\widetilde{Y}
above:
\widetilde{Y}^{n}
receives a kick towards
X
and becomes close to
X
for each
\omega
, yet it remains close to
Y^{n}
in law. A similar triangular inequality as above implies

W_{d}(\law(X_{t}),\law(Y^{n}_{t}))\leqslant d_{TV}(\law(Y^{n}_{t}),\law(\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t}))+\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt|\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t}-X_{t}|.

Since both terms on the right-hand side are small, we see that the process
Y^{n}_{t}
converges weakly to
X_{t}
. Since
X
was an arbitrary weak solution, weak uniqueness holds.
[
KS20
]
coins this approach “Control and Reimburse strategy”.

Han
[
Han22
,
Han24
]
uses the Kulik-Scheutzow method to get weak uniqueness for solutions to SHE with Hölder drift and diffusion coefficients, as well as for the stochastic wave equation with Hölder drift.

However let us stress that the strategy of
[
KS20
,
Han22
]
requires the drift to be a Hölder continuous function. The direct application of their method does not work if the drift is a Schwartz distribution or even a bounded function.

Thus, we see that neither the application of stochastic sewing alone nor the generalized coupling technique alone allows to obtain weak uniqueness results beyond the known pathwise uniqueness results for equations with distributional drift. The main novelty of this paper is that we show that a combination of these techniques allows to break this barrier and obtain weak uniqueness in the same regime where weak existence is known. In

Section

2.3

[
KS20

\displaystyle p_{t}^{Neu}(x,y):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(\Gamma(t,x-y+2n)+\Gamma(t,x+y+2n)),\quad t>0,\,x,y\in[0,1].

2.3
Overview of the proofs of main results

Weak uniqueness
. For the convenience of the reader we demonstrate the heuristics of our proof strategy on the following simplified example. Then we highlight the necessary changes which are needed in order to pass from this partially informal explanation to rigorous proofs of

[

Thus, we would like to show weak uniqueness for solutions to SDE

dX_{t}=b(X_{t})dt+dB_{t}^{H},\quad X_{0}=0,

(2.4)

where
b\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}
,
\alpha<0
. Assume for simplicity that
b
is a function. We fix a sequence
(b^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
of smooth functions converging to
b
in
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha-}
. Let
X^{n}
be the strong solution to
SDE(

)
.

Now consider
any

(X,B^{H})
to (
2.4
).
Our goal is to show that the sequence
(X^{n},B^{H})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
weakly converges to this solution
(X,B^{H})
. Since
(X,B^{H})
was an arbitrary solution to (
2.4
), this would imply that the sequence
(X^{n},B^{H})
converges in law to any weak solution of (

A naive direct approach, which fails here, would be simply to write

\|X_{t}-X^{n}_{t}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\Bigl\|\int_{0}^{t}(b(X_{r})-b^{n}(X^{n}_{r}))\,dr\Bigr\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)},

and then use stochastic sewing. Under the condition (
A

)

\sup_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}\|X_{t}-X^{n}_{t}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant Ct_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}\|X_{t}-X^{n}_{t}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{(\alpha+\frac{1}{2H})\wedge 1}+\text{good small terms}.

(2.5)

If
\alpha+\frac{1}{2H}>1
, one can remove the bad term
\sup_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}\|X_{t}-X^{n}_{t}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}
on the right-hand side for small

, and even get strong uniqueness of solutions to SDE (

), see
[
GG22
, Theorem 1.4]
,
[
CG16
, Theorem 1.13]
. However, we are interested in the regime where weak existence of (

[

, Theorem 8.2]
. In this regime, the exponent
\alpha+\frac{1}{2H}
is less than

and (
2.5
) does not imply that
\sup_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}\|X_{t}-X^{n}_{t}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}
is small. Therefore this direct approach does not work.
Nevertheless, as we demonstrate below, bound (
2.5
) is not entirely useless.

Therefore, we avoid comparing the solutions
X
and
X^{n}
directly. Instead, in the spirit of the “Control-and-Reimburse” strategy of
[
KS20
]
, we introduce now an auxiliary process
\widetilde{Y}^{n}
, which is defined on the same space as
X
and solves SDE

d\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t}=b^{n}(\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t})dt+\lambda(X_{t}-\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t})dt+dB_{t}^{H},

where the parameter
\lambda>1
will be fixed later. The main idea now is to show that the process
\widetilde{Y}^{n}
is close to
X^{n}
in law for
\lambda
not too large, and close to
X
in distance for
\lambda
not too small. Then by picking the best
\lambda
from these two opposite requirements, we derive that
X^{n}
and
X
are close to each other in law. By the triangle inequality,

W_{\|\cdot\|\wedge 1}(\law(X^{n}),\law(X))\leqslant d_{TV}(\law(X^{n}),\law(\widetilde{Y}^{n}))+\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt\|\widetilde{Y}^{n}-X\|_{\mathcal{C}([0,1])}.

(2.6)

We bound the first term of (
2.6
) in the same spirit as in
[
KS20
]
. The key difference between our approach and that in
[
KS20
]
lies in treating the second term of (
2.6
). This is precisely where stochastic sewing plays a key role.

Introduce a process
\widetilde{B}_{t}^{H}:=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(X_{r}-\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{r})dr+B_{t}^{H}
. Then
\widetilde{Y}^{n}
satisfies

d\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t}=b^{n}(\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t})dt+d\widetilde{B}_{t}^{H}.

Thus
(\widetilde{Y}^{n},\widetilde{B}^{H})
is a solution to
SDE(
0;b^{n}
)
.
Since
b^{n}
is a smooth function, weak uniqueness holds for this equation. Therefore, Girsanov’s theorem and Pinsker’s inequality yield (recall that
H\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
)

d_{TV}(\law(X^{n}),\law(\widetilde{Y}^{n}))\leqslant d_{TV}(\widetilde{B}^{H},B^{H})\leqslant C\lambda\bigl\|\|X-\widetilde{Y}^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}([0,1])}\bigr\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.

(2.7)

Next, to compare
\widetilde{Y}^{n}
and
X
, we benefit from the control term
\lambda(X-\widetilde{Y}^{n})
which pushes
\widetilde{Y}^{n}
towards
X
. After an easy calculation, we deduce for
t\in[0,1]

|X_{t}-\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t}|\leqslant\Bigl|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-r)}(b(X_{r})-b_{n}(\widetilde{Y}_{r}^{n}))\,dr\Bigr|.

(2.8)

Carefully bounding the right-hand side of the above inequality is absolutely crucial for the entire weak uniqueness proof.
First, let us review how
[
KS20
]
deals with bounds of a similar type, and then we explain how we improve their approach.

Assume additionally for a moment that
\alpha>0
. Taking supremum over
t\in[0,1]
in the above bound, estimating
|b(x)-b^{n}(y)|
as
\|b\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}}|x-y|^{\alpha}+\|b-b^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})}
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and using Young’s inequality, one gets
for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\|X-\widetilde{Y}^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}([0,1])}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\lambda^{-1}\|b-b^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})}+C\lambda^{-1}\|b\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}}\|X-\widetilde{Y}^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}([0,1])}^{\alpha}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\lambda^{-1}\|b-b^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})}+C\lambda^{-\varepsilon}\|b\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}}\|X-\widetilde{Y}^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}([0,1])}+C\lambda^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\|b\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}}.

Hence, for
\lambda
large enough the following inequaltiy holds:

\|X-\widetilde{Y}^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}([0,1])}\leqslant C\lambda^{-1}\|b-b^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})}+C\lambda^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\|b\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}}.

(2.10)

Combining this with the total variation bound (
2.7
) and substituting this into (
2.6
), one finally derives

W_{\|\cdot\|\wedge 1}(\law(X^{n}),\law(X))\leqslant C\|b-b^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})}+C\lambda^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\|b\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}}.

By taking the limit as
\lambda\to\infty
, one gets the desired weak convergence of
X^{n}
to an arbitrary weak solution
X
and thus weak uniqueness.

We see, however, that this approach does not work for
\alpha<0
, as neither of the terms on the right-hand side of the last inequality converges as
n,\lambda\to\infty
. Our main insight is to utilize stochastic sewing to bound (
2.8
) more carefully. Applying a modification of (
2.5
), we obtain (see
Corollary

3.7
)

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|X_{t}-\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{n}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\lambda^{-1-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})H}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|X_{t}-\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\text{good small terms}.

Of course, similar to (
2.5
), the term
\|X_{t}-\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}
appears with an exponent less than
1
, and we cannot eliminate it directly. However, this is not a problem anymore due to the presence of the additional factor
\lambda^{-1-(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})H}
, which we can make very small.
Comparing this with the way
[
KS20
]
treats the right-hand side of (
2.8
) (see (
2.9
) above), we see that a certain tradeoff takes place. The term
\|X_{t}-\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{n}\|
now has a much bigger exponent:
1/2
instead of
\alpha
(recall that
\alpha
is negative now). The price to pay is that the parameter
\lambda^{-1}
now has a smaller exponent
1+(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})H
instead of
1
. An additional disadvantage is that we are now unable to obtain a direct almost sure bound, but rather a weaker
L_{2}(\Omega)
bound. Nevertheless, this tradeoff is sufficient for us to close the buckling loop. By Young’s inequality

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|X_{t}-\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{n}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\lambda^{-2-(2\alpha-1)H}+C\lambda^{-\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|X_{t}-\widetilde{Y}^{n}_{t}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}+\text{good small terms},

which implies

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|X_{t}-\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{n}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\lambda^{-2-(2\alpha-1)H}+\text{good small terms}.

(2.11)

Recall that the Girsanov bound (
2.7
) includes an additional factor
\lambda
; therefore, in order to succeed, we must impose

\lambda\lambda^{-2-(2\alpha-1)H}\to 0,\qquad\text{as $\lambda\to\infty$},

which implies exactly our condition (
A
). Thus, substituting (
2.11
) into (
2.7
) and (
2.6
) and taking care of “good small terms”, we get the bound

W_{\|\cdot\|\wedge 1}(\law(X^{n}),\law(X))\leqslant C\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}-H(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})}+C\lambda^{2}\|b-b^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha-\varepsilon}},

which significantly improves
[
KS20
]
-type bound (
2.10
). We see that in our regime (
A
), the exponent of
\lambda
, that is,
-\frac{1}{2}-H(\alpha-\frac{1}{2})
, is negative. Therefore we can pick
\lambda:=\|b-b^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha-\varepsilon}}^{-1/3}
to get

W_{\|\cdot\|\wedge 1}(\law(X^{n}),\law(X))\leqslant C\|b-b^{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha-\varepsilon}}^{\rho}

for certain
\rho=\rho(\alpha,H)>0
. This yields the desired weak convergence of the fixed sequence
(X^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
to any weak solution
X
, establishing weak uniqueness. This completes the proof.

Now it remains to make this informal description of our proof strategy rigorous. This is the subject of the remaining part of the paper. The derivation of an analogue of (
2.8
) is done in
Lemma

4.4
. Bound (
2.7
) is justified in
Lemma

4.3
, and they are combined in
Lemma

4.1
. It is important to note that in our setting
b
is not function but rather a Schwartz distribution belonging to
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}
. As a result, the integral in the right-hand side of (
2.8
) is not well-defined, and even (
2.8
) does not hold. Therefore, one must consider an appropriate approximation.

The proof of weak uniqueness for SHE goes along the same lines; however, some modifications due to the infinite-dimensional nature of the problem are necessary. Most importantly, instead of the
\sup
distance
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{C}([0,1])}
, we have to work with the weighted in time and space norm defined in (
5.1
) and rely on the backward uniqueness-type results. This is the subject of
Section

5
.

Strong uniqueness for SDEs in case
d=1
. As before, we first explain the heuristics for the case when
b
is a function, and then proceed to the general case.

Let
d=1
, and let
(X^{1},B^{H})
,
(X^{2},B^{H})
be two weak solutions to SDE (
2.4
) defined on the same probability space with common noise. Consider a process
Y_{t}:=X^{1}_{t}\wedge X^{2}_{t}
. If we show that
Y
is also a weak solution to this equation, then by weak uniqueness we get for any bounded continuous strictly increasing function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057ptf(X^{1}_{t}\wedge X^{2}_{t})=\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057ptf(X^{1}_{t}),\quad t\geqslant 0,

which implies
X^{1}_{t}\wedge X^{2}_{t}=X^{1}_{t}
a.s. Similarly,
X^{1}_{t}\wedge X^{2}_{t}=X^{2}_{t}
a.s., and thus strong uniqueness holds.

To show that
Y
solves (
2.4
), we fix
\omega\in\Omega
. Let
A:=\{s\in[0,1]:X^{1}_{s}(\omega)>X^{2}_{s}(\omega)\}
. We see that
A
is an open set, and therefore it is a union of countably many mutually disjoint intervals
A=\cup(s_{k},t_{k})
. Further, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, by the continuity of
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
,

\displaystyle\int_{[s_{k},t_{k}]}b(X_{r}^{1})\,dr

\displaystyle=X^{1}_{t_{k}}-X^{1}_{s_{k}}-(B^{H}_{t_{k}}-B^{H}_{s_{k}})

\displaystyle=X^{2}_{t_{k}}-X^{2}_{s_{k}}-(B^{H}_{t_{k}}-B^{H}_{s_{k}})=\int_{[s_{k},t_{k}]}b(X_{r}^{2})\,dr.

This implies

\int_{A}b(X_{r}^{1})\,dr=\int_{A}b(X_{r}^{2})\,dr.

(2.12)

Let
t\in[0,1]
. Suppose without loss of generality that
X^{1}_{t}(\omega)\leqslant X^{2}_{t}(\omega)
.
Using (
2.12
) and that
t\notin A
by definition of
A
, we get

\displaystyle Y_{t}-B_{t}^{H}

\displaystyle=X^{1}_{t}-B_{t}^{H}=\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{r}^{1})\,dr=\int_{A}b(X_{r}^{1})\,dr+\int_{[0,t]\setminus A}b(X_{r}^{1})\,dr

\displaystyle=\int_{A}b(X_{r}^{2})\,dr+\int_{[0,t]\setminus A}b(X_{r}^{1})\,dr

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]}b(Y_{r})\,dr,

and thus
Y
solves (
2.4
).

Unfortunately, this argument becomes inapplicable when
b
is a distribution. Indeed, let
(b^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
be an arbitrary sequence of functions converging to
b
in
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha-}
. Then it is easy to show that for a certain subsequence
(n_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}

\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{[s_{k},t_{k}]}b^{n_{m}}(X_{r}^{1})\,dr=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{[s_{k},t_{k}]}b^{n_{m}}(X_{r}^{2})\,dr.

However, this does not imply that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{A}b^{n_{m}}(X_{r}^{1})\,dr
equals

\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{A}b^{n_{m}}(X_{r}^{2})\,dr
. Indeed, a sum of countably many numbers each converging to
0
does not necessarily converge to
0
.

Therefore, we fix additionally
K\in\mathbb{N}
and consider a set
A^{K}:=\cup_{k\leqslant K}(s_{k},t_{k})\subset A
. We introduce a process
Y^{K}_{t}:=X^{1}_{t}\1(t\in[0,1]\setminus A^{K})+X^{2}_{t}\1(t\in A^{K})
. Since
Y^{K}
differs from
X^{1}
(and hence from
X^{2}
) only on finitely many intervals, the above argument remains valid, and one can show that
Y^{K}
solves (
2.4
). Note, though, that
Y^{K}
is not necessarily adapted; therefore, we cannot conclude that
Y^{K}
is a weak solution to (
2.4
). Nevertheless, we have for any fixed
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\int_{0}^{t}b^{n_{m}}(Y_{r}^{K})\,dr\to Y^{K}_{t}-B_{t}^{H}\quad\text{as $m\to\infty$}.

(2.13)

By definition we have
Y^{K}_{t}\to Y_{t}
a.s. as
K\to\infty
. Since for any fixed
m
we have also

\int_{0}^{t}b^{n_{m}}(Y_{r}^{K})\,dr\to\int_{0}^{t}b^{n_{m}}(Y_{r})\,dr\quad\text{as $K\to\infty$},

we can conclude that

\int_{0}^{t}b^{n_{m}}(Y_{r})\,dr\to Y_{t}-B_{t}^{H}\quad\text{as $m\to\infty$},

as long as we can show that the convergence in (
2.13
) is uniform over
K
. This is not entirely trivial as the process
Y^{K}
is not adapted, and thus the stochastic sewing lemma is not applicable. Therefore, we rely here on the deterministic sewing lemma, which allows us to treat non-adaptive processes and nonlinear Young integral-type bounds. The key bound is established in
Lemma

3.16
, and this strategy is formalized in
Lemma

4.5
.

Preliminaries and auxiliary bounds

We introduce further necessary notation which will be used in the article. We denote by
\operatorname{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of all Lipschitz functions
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the usual norm and seminorm

[f]_{\operatorname{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|};\qquad\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+[f]_{\operatorname{Lip}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

For
0\leqslant S<T
let
\Delta_{[S,T]}
be the simplex

\Delta_{[S,T]}:=\{(s,t)\in[S,T]^{2}\colon s\leqslant t\}.

If
A_{\cdot,\cdot}
is a function
\Delta_{[S,T]}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
0\leqslant S\leqslant T
, then we put as standard

\delta A_{s,u,t}:=A_{s,t}-A_{s,u}-A_{u,t},\quad S\leqslant s\leqslant u\leqslant t\leqslant T.

If
f
is a function
[S,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
0\leqslant S\leqslant T
, then its
\ell
-variation,
\ell\in[1,\infty)
, is denoted as usual by

[f]_{\ell-\textrm{var};[S,T]}:=\bigl(\sup_{\Pi}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|f(t_{i+1})-f(t_{i})|^{\ell}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{\ell}},

where the supremum is taken over all partitions
\Pi=\{t_{0}=S,t_{1},\dots,t_{n}=T\}
of the interval
[S,T]
. The space of functions
f\colon[S,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite
\ell\in[1,\infty)
variation will be denoted by
\mathcal{C}^{\ell-\textrm{var}}([S,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For a partition
\Pi
as above we denote its diameter by
|\Pi|:=\max_{i}|t_{i+1}-t_{i}|
.

A continuous function
w\colon\Delta_{[S,T]}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called a
control
if
w(s,s)=0
for any
s\in[S,T]
and for any
(s,t)\in\Delta_{[S,T]}
,
u\in[s,t]
one has
w(s,u)+w(u,t)\leqslant w(s,t)
, see
[
FV10
, Definition 1.6]
. It is easy to see that the functions
w(s,t):=t-s
and
w(s,t):=[f]_{\ell-\textrm{var};[s,t]}^{\ell}
, where
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\ell-\textrm{var}}([S,T])
, are examples of controls.

If a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is given, then the conditional expectation given
\mathcal{F}_{t}
,
t\in[0,T]
, will be denoted by
\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{t}[\cdot]:=\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt[\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{t}]
.

As in
[
BDG21
,
ABLM24b
]
, we introduce the following family of norms and seminorms to analyze solutions of
SDE(
x;b
)
. If
f\colon[S,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a measurable function, then for

\gamma\in(0,1]
,
m\geqslant 1
we define

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}L_{m}([S,T])}:=\sup_{s\in[S,T]}\|f_{s}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)};\qquad[f]_{\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}L_{m}([S,T])}:=\sup_{(s,t)\in\Delta_{[S,T]}}\frac{\|f_{t}-f_{s}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}}{|t-s|^{\gamma}};

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}L_{m}([S,T])}=\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}L_{m}([S,T])}+[f]_{\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}L_{m}([S,T])}.

The space-time convolution of the heat kernel with the white noise will be denoted by
V
:

V_{t}(x):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}p_{t-r}(x,y)W(dr,dy),\quad t\geqslant 0,\,x\in D.

(3.2)

The following norms and seminorms will be important for the analysis of
SHE(
u_{0};b
)
. For a measurable function
f\colon[S,T]\times D\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma\in(0,1]
,

m\geqslant 1
we write

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|f\|_{{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}L_{m}({[S,T]})}}:=\sup_{t\in[S,T]}\sup_{x\in D}\|f(t,x)\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)};\\
&[f]_{{\mathcal{C}^{\gamma,0}L_{m}({[S,T]})}}:=\sup_{(s,t)\in\Delta_{[S,T]}}\sup_{x\in D}\frac{\|f_{t}(x)-P_{t-s}f_{s}(x)\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}}{|t-s|^{\gamma}};\\
&\|f\|_{{\mathcal{C}^{\gamma,0}L_{m}({[S,T]})}}:=\|f\|_{{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}L_{m}({[S,T]})}}+[f]_{{\mathcal{C}^{\gamma,0}L_{m}({[S,T]})}}.\end{split}

(3.3)

In the proofs we often use a standard heat kernel estimate

\|G_{t}^{d}f\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant Ct^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}},

(3.4)

where
\beta<0
,

and

, see, e.g.,
[
BDG21

.

Sewing lemmas

As mentioned earlier, our proofs frequently utilize both the deterministic and stochastic sewing lemmas. For the convenience of the reader, we recall them here.

We begin with the deterministic sewing lemma with controls.

Proposition 3.1

(Sewing lemma,
[
FZ18
, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

Let
0\leqslant S\leqslant T
. Let
A
be a function
\Delta_{[S,T]}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Assume that there exist a control
w
on the same simplex and a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that for every
(s,t)\in\Delta_{[S,T]}
,

one has

|\delta A_{s,u,t}|\leqslant w(s,t)^{1+\varepsilon}.

(3.5)

Further, suppose that there exists a process
\mathcal{A}\colon[S,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for any
t\in[S,T]
and any sequence of partitions
\Pi_{N}:=\{S=t^{N}_{0},...,t^{N}_{k(N)}=t\}
of
[S,t]
with
\lim_{N\to\infty}|\Pi_{N}|\to 0
one has

\sum_{i=0}^{k(N)-1}A_{t^{N}_{i},t_{i+1}^{N}}\to\mathcal{A}_{t}-\mathcal{A}_{S}\quad\text{ as $N\to\infty$}.

(3.6)

Then there exists a constant
C=C(\varepsilon)
independent of
S,T
,
w
such that for every
(s,t)\in\Delta_{S,T}
we have

|\mathcal{A}_{t}-\mathcal{A}_{s}|\leqslant|A_{s,t}|+Cw(s,t)^{1+\varepsilon}.

)
.

,
0\leqslant S\leqslant T
. Let

is

,

\displaystyle\|\hskip 0.6458pt\mathsf{E}\hskip 0.43057pt^{s}[\delta A_{s,u,t}]\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}\leqslant\Gamma_{2}|t-s|^{1+\varepsilon_{2}}.

Further, suppose that there exists a process

,
\Gamma_{i}
such that for every
(s,t)\in\Delta_{S,T}
we have

\|\mathcal{A}_{t}-\mathcal{A}_{s}\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\Gamma_{1}|t-s|^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon_{1}}+C\Gamma_{2}|t-s|^{1+\varepsilon_{2}}.

(3.11)

Then there exists

Proof.

Denote

,
t\in[0,1]
,

.
It is clear that for any

,

we have by Fatou’s lemma

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{t}(x)-S_{t-s}\varphi_{s}(x)\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\|A(t,x)-S_{t-s}A(s,x)\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{D}}s_{t-r}(x,y)\|\rho(r,y)\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}\,dydr

To estimate the first term in the right-hand side of the above inequality, we use

with

for

and

. Substituting this into (

) we get for any

,

Let

. Dividing both sides of the above inequality by

and taking supremum in the above inequality over all

\displaystyle\leqslant C(F+\|\rho\|_{{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}L_{m}({[0,1]})}})+CF\ell^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\kappa}{2}}[\varphi]_{{\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2},0}L_{m}({[s,t]})}}

where

. Since by assumptions of the lemma

, it follows from (

) that

Using (

), we deduce from

) that for any

such that

we have

If

). Otherwise, if

and define

and therefore by the above inequality and Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\sup_{z\in\mathbf{D}}\|\varphi_{t_{i+1}}(z)-S_{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}\varphi_{t_{i}}(z)\|_{L_{m}(\Omega)}

Weak uniqueness

Lemma 4.1

To obtain

Lemma 4.2

{\|\widetilde{Y}-B^{H}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1}L_{m}([0,1])}<\infty}

Define

We see that

Lemma 4.3

Proposition A.1
.
