Drazin-inverse and heat capacity for driven random walkers on the ring
Faezeh Khodabandehlou, Irene Maes

Abstract
We apply single and double tree-like representations of Markov jump processes on ℤ N {\mathbb{Z}}_{N} for obtaining their nonequilibrium heat capacity and for taking the diffusion limit N ↑ ∞ N\uparrow\infty . The main tool is a graphical representation of the Drazin-inverse of the backward generator. In that way, the combination of algebraic and graph-theoretical approaches enables an exact computation of an important nonequilibrium quantity (excess heat) for Markov jump processes.



I
Introduction

Markov jump processes offer an important modeling environment for nonequilibrium statistical mechanics. They arise from quantum processes for multilevel systems (beyond decoherence times) or provide coarse-grained descriptions of diffusion processes,
ja
. Nonequilibrium features such as maintaining steady currents, are possible by the presence of loops in the graph of possible configurations. The present paper focuses on such a loop-dynamics and how one can extract the thermal response.

Our constructive approach to exact computations in nonequilibrium calorimetry combines elements of linear algebra (pseudo-inverses), graph theory (matrix-forest theorem) and Markov jump processes. The purpose is to describe physically relevant properties of driven jump processes related to so called excess heat.
In that way, the paper takes up new challenges both in statistical mechanics and within the context of stochastic processes; see also
heatb
;
ner
.

More specifically, we are concerned with biased (or driven) random walks in an energy landscape defined on a ring. Without the driving, the stationary process is reversible with respect to the usual Gibbs distribution for that energy function. With the driving, we are motivated by nonequilibrium calorimetry for computing the heat capacity of such driven systems,
noncal
. As we will see, such a computation depends on getting the stationary distribution and the excess work (the so-called quasipotential). For both, we give algorithms following a graphical representation. It allows fast computation and visualization of the relevant physics, in particular, to find the nonequilibrium heat capacities.

In the next section, we define the quasipotential in the context of Markov jump processes, and we explain the main questions. In Section
III
, we show that the calculation of the quasipotential reduces to finding the Drazin-inverse of the backward generator. That Drazin-inverse coincides here with the limit of the resolvent, which, from the matrix-forest theorem
MForest1
;
MForest2
, gives rise to a graphical algorithm. That is explicitly achieved in Section
IV
with the main theorem in Section
IV.2
. In that way, we achieve a fast calculation of quasipotentials in Section
IV.3
; see also
simon
.
Combined with the matrix-tree theorem
kir
, it leads to an exact computation of the heat capacity in Section
V
. The explicit low-temperature behavior may or may not show a Third Law of Thermodynamics (vanishing of the heat capacity with temperature). Related to that, we discuss boundedness of the quasipotential in Section
VIII
.
Section
VI
presents the Kirchhoff formula, which allows a diffusion limit to recover the stationary distribution for the corresponding driven diffusion on the circle, as was already obtained in
davide
. The same is achieved for the quasipotential in Section
VII
, and in Section
VIII
we show how to use the graphical representation for obtaining bounds on the quasipotential. Conclusions are drawn in Section
IX
.

Appendix
A
is devoted to an explanation of the ideas in the algorithm and the computer code (which is publicly available in
code
). In the Appendix
B
we give an explicit proof that the index of the backward generator equals one.

II
Setup and main results

Consider a finite connected graph
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G}),\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G}))
, without self-edges, i.e., there is no edge that starts and ends in the same vertex.
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})
is the set of vertices and
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})
is the set of edges; put
n=|\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})|
and
m=|\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})|
. To every oriented edge
(x,y)
a weight
k(x,y)>0
is assigned.

A Markov jump process
X_{t}
uses the set of vertices as possible (physical) states and the weights on the graph become the transitions rates over the edges:
k(x,y)
is the rate of jumping from
x
to
y
and no jump is possible when no edge is present between the two vertices.

The backward generator
L
for the Markov process is the
n\times n
matrix with elements

L_{x,y}=\begin{cases}L_{x,y}=k(x,y)&\quad x\not=y\\
L_{x,x}=-\sum_{y}k(x,y)\end{cases}

(II.1)

for
x,y\in\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})
.
L
is directly related to the Laplacian matrix of the weighted directed graph; see also Appendix
B
and the many references in
hindustan
. For a weighted graph the Laplacian matrix is also called the Kirchhoff matrix; see e.g.
che2006
.

Every function
g
on
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})
is a column vector. The semigroup
S(t)=\exp(tL),t\geq 0
, satisfies

S(t)g(x)=\langle g(X_{t})\,|\,X_{0}=x\rangle

where
\langle\cdot\,|\,X_{0}=x\rangle
is the process expectation of the random walk
X_{t}
starting from
X_{0}=x
for
x\in\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})
.

The Master equation is

\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}=\rho_{t}L

for the time-dependent probability
\rho_{t}
, written as a row vector. There is a unique stationary distribution
\rho^{s}>0
, solution of
\rho^{s}L=0
, and, to settle the notation,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle g(X_{t})\,|\,X_{0}=x\rangle=\langle g\rangle^{s}:=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})}\rho^{s}(y)g(y)

where the convergence is exponentially fast, uniformly in
x
.

Take a source function
f
on
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})
with zero stationary expectation
\langle f\rangle^{s}=0
, and define
the quasipotential

V_{f}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}t\,S(t)f

(II.2)

which is exponentially converging. The main subject of the paper is to use an algorithm to find
V_{f}
. Note that equation (
II.2
) can be rewritten as
LV_{f}=-f
, which is a Poisson equation. In the literature on that Poisson equation, the requirement
\langle f\rangle^{s}=0
is called a
centrality condition
, and most applications are related to diffusion processes; see e.g.
poiss
. Here we deal with the Poisson equation in a discrete setting.

The following main results are obtained in the coming sections:

(1) identifying the quasipotential
V_{f}=-L_{D}^{-1}f
as obtainable from the Drazin-inverse
L_{D}^{-1}
of
L
working on
f,\langle f\rangle=0
;

(2) a graphical representation of the quasipotential
V
on the ring, and its efficient computation for different
\varepsilon,N,T
;

(3) from the graphical algorithm: exact computation of the nonequilibrium heat capacity
C
as function of temperature
T
(including
T\downarrow 0
) with parameters
N,\varepsilon
on the ring;

(4) diffusion limit of Kirchhoff formula and quasipotential on the ring.

III
The quasipotential is a pseudo-inverse

The mathematical challenges of Markov jump processes on finite connected graphs often relate to questions of linear algebra.

The backward generator in equation (
II.1
) is a singular matrix, which invites the study of its pseudo-inverses. One such pseudo-inverse is the Drazin inverse.

Consider a square matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
The
index
of
A
,
\text{ind}(A)
, is the smallest non-negative integer
k
such that
\text{rank}(A^{k})=\text{rank}(A^{k+1})
.

The Drazin inverse of
A
is the matrix
A_{D}^{-1}
satisfying the following three conditions
book
:

1.

A^{k}A_{D}^{-1}A=A^{k}
with
k=\text{ind}(A)
,

2.

A_{D}^{-1}AA_{D}^{-1}=A_{D}^{-1}
,

3.

AA_{D}^{-1}=A_{D}^{-1}A
.

The Drazin-inverse
A_{D}^{-1}
always exists and is unique,
book
.
We show here that the quasipotential (
II.2
) can be obtained as the Drazin-inverse of the backward generator
L
.

Proposition III.1
.

Let
V_{f}
be the quasipotential (
II.2
) for a function
f
having
\left\langle f\right\rangle^{s}=0
, then

V_{f}=-L_{D}^{-1}f.

Proof.

In Appendix
B
we discuss why
\text{ind}(L)=1
, and we give the explicit proof for the dynamics on a ring; see also
che2006
. As follows from Theorem 3.1 (2) in
Laurent
, that implies that

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(L+\lambda I)^{-1}L=L_{D}^{-1}\,L.

Because of the exponential convergence in (
II.2
),
\langle V_{f}\rangle^{s}=0
and

V_{f}(x)=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\dfrac{-1}{(L-\lambda I)}f(x)

where
\dfrac{1}{L-\lambda I},\lambda\in\mathbb{C},0<|\lambda|<\delta
for some
\delta>0
, is the resolvent-inverse of the backward generator
L
. In fact,
V_{f}
is the unique solution of the equation
LV=-f
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}[(L+\lambda I)^{-1}f]

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\lambda\to 0}[(L+\lambda I)^{-1}LV]

\displaystyle=-L_{D}^{-1}LV_{f}

\displaystyle=L_{D}^{-1}f.

∎

As a remark, if the index of a matrix is
1
, then the special case of the Drazin-inverse is known as the group inverse. Therefore,
the Drazin–inverse
L_{D}^{-1}
also equals the group inverse of
L
; see e.g. Theorem 2.2.1 in
book
.

IV
Quasipotential on a ring

Consider the ring
\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
with
N
vertices
{0,\frac{1}{N},\frac{2}{N},\dots,\frac{N-1}{N}}
. Moving in the clockwise direction takes
x
to
x+1/N
with driving
\varepsilon
. The reason for considering a mesh size
1/N
is to be able to explore as well the diffusion limit where
N\uparrow\infty
in what is to come.

A random walk
X_{t}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
has transition rates
k\left(x,x+\frac{1}{N}\right)
for the clockwise jumps
x\rightarrow x+\frac{1}{N}
and has transition rates
k\left(x,x-\frac{1}{N}\right)
for the counter-clockwise jumps
x\rightarrow x-\frac{1}{N}
; all other transitions are forbidden.

For an elementary example, we look at the simple random walker on the ring, where all the transition rates are equal to one and we take a source function
f
on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
with
\sum f(x)=0
. Then, the quasipotential
V=V_{f}
in equation (
II.2
) satisfies
\sum_{x}V(x)=0
and

V(x+1/N)+V(x-1/N)-2V(x)=-f(x)

(IV.1)

which is the standard discrete Poisson equation (for total charge equal to zero) with periodic boundary conditions, easily solved by Fourier transform.
To be explicit, for
N=3
, the solution is
V_{f}(x)=f(x)/3
.

IV.1
Single and double trees

We will give a graphical representation of the quasipotential (
II.2
) for a general (nearest neighbor) random walk dynamics on the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
. We need to recall some definitions.

A tree
in the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
(see Fig.
1
) is the ring with exactly one edge removed.
A double tree
in the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
(see Fig.
1
) is the ring with exactly two edges removed. Remark that a double tree consists of two connected parts and that one of the two parts is possibly only a vertex (if two edges with a common vertex are removed).

Figure 1:
A tree in
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
on the left side and double tree on the right side.

A rooted tree
in the ring (see Fig.
2
) is a tree in the ring where every edge has a direction and with a special vertex, called the root. The root is such that if we follow the directions from an arbitrary vertex on the ring, we end up in the vertex which is the root.

A double rooted tree
in the ring (see Fig.
2
) is a double tree in the ring where every edge has a direction and in each of the two parts of the double tree there is a special vertex called the root. This root is again such that if we start from an arbitrary vertex in one of the two parts and we follow the directions, we end up in the root of that part.

Figure 2:
A rooted tree with root in
r
on the left side and a double rooted tree on the right side. The double rooted tree is an element of
\mathcal{F}^{x\to y}
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of all rooted trees in the ring and
\mathcal{T}_{y}
be the set of all rooted trees with root in
y
. Define
\mathcal{F}^{x\to y}
to be the set of all double rooted trees in the ring such that
y
is one of the roots and
x
is in the same part as
y
(so there is a path from
x
to
y
, see Fig.
2
).

The weight of a rooted tree
T
resp. a double rooted tree
F
in the ring, noted by
w(T)
resp.
w(F)
is the product of all transition rates
k(z,u)
such that the edge
\left(z,u\right)
with direction
z\to u
belongs to the tree or the double tree (
u
is either
z+1/N
or
z-1/N
).
If
S
is some set of rooted trees or double rooted trees, we define
w(S)
to be the sum of all weights of the elements belonging to
S
. In particular

\displaystyle w(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}w(T)

(IV.2)

\displaystyle w(\mathcal{F}^{x\to y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to y}}w(F).

(IV.3)

IV.2
Graphical representation

We start by recalling the Kirchhoff formula (which is an application of the matrix-tree theorem).

Define
T_{x}^{y}
to be the tree in
\mathcal{T}_{x}
such that the edge between vertex
y
and
y+1/N
is removed. It follows that
w(\mathcal{T})=\sum_{x}w(\mathcal{T}_{x})
and
w(\mathcal{T}_{x})=\sum_{y}w(T^{y}_{x})
.

Using the matrix-tree theorem
kir
, the stationary probability distribution
\rho^{s}
,
\rho^{s}L=0
, appears as given by

\rho^{s}(x)=\frac{w(\mathcal{T}_{x})}{\sum_{y}w(\mathcal{T}_{y})}=\frac{\sum_{y}w(T_{x}^{y})}{w(\mathcal{T})}.

(IV.4)

We use it in Section
VI
for taking the diffusion limit of the stationary distribution.

For now and next, we move from the single tree to the double tree level.
Recall Proposition
III.1
for the quasipotential (
II.2
). For the random walk on the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
, we have the following representation.

Theorem IV.1
.

When
\left\langle f\right\rangle^{s}=0
,

V_{f}(x)=\dfrac{\sum_{y}w\left(\mathcal{F}^{x\rightarrow y}\right)}{w\left(\mathcal{T}\right)}f(y).

(IV.5)

Proof.

Define
g(x)=\dfrac{\sum_{y}w\left(\mathcal{F}^{x\rightarrow y}\right)}{w\left(\mathcal{T}\right)}f(y)
for all
x
.
From (
II.1
),

\displaystyle Lg\,(x)=k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})

\displaystyle(g(x+\frac{1}{N})-g(x))+k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})(g(x-\frac{1}{N})-g(x))

and hence,

\displaystyle w(\mathcal{T})Lg\,(x)=k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})\sum_{y}

\displaystyle\left(w(\mathcal{F}^{x+1/N\to y})-w(\mathcal{F}^{x\to y})\right)f(y)+

(IV.6)

\displaystyle k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})\sum_{y}\left(w(\mathcal{F}^{x-1/N\to y})-w(\mathcal{F}^{x\to y})\right)f(y).

We start with the case
y=x
in the sum. We have that
\mathcal{F}^{x+1/N\to x}\subset\mathcal{F}^{x\to x}
. If
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to x}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{x+1/N\to x}
, then the edge
(x,x+1/N)
does not belong to
F
. It follows that
k(x,x+1/N)w(F)=w(T)
for some
T\in\mathcal{T}
. Remark that this tree is not rooted in
x
. In the same way we have that
\mathcal{F}^{x-1/N\to x}\subset\mathcal{F}^{x\to x}
and if
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to x}\setminus\mathcal{F}^{x-1/N\to x}
, then the edge
(x,x-1/N)
does not belong to
F^{\prime}
. Therefore,
k(x,x-1/N)w(F)=w(T^{\prime})
for some
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
. Remark again that
T^{\prime}
is not rooted in
x
. The other way around, for any
T\in\mathcal{T}
that is not rooted in
x
, there exists
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to x}
such that either
w(T)=k(x,x+1/N)w(F)
or
w(T)=k(x,x-1/N)w(F)
. In this way we have a one-to-one correspondence between directed trees not rooted in
x
and directed double rooted trees in the ring. As a consequence,

\displaystyle k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})\big(

\displaystyle w(\mathcal{F}^{x+1/N\to x})-w(\mathcal{F}^{x\to x})\big)f(x)

\displaystyle+k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})\left(w(\mathcal{F}^{x-1/N\to x})-w(\mathcal{F}^{x\to x})\right)f(x)=-\sum_{T\notin\mathcal{T}_{x}}w(T)f(x).

Next, we consider what remains in the sum (
IV.6
), i.e.,

\displaystyle k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})\sum_{y\neq x}

\displaystyle\left(w(\mathcal{F}^{x+1/N\to y})-w(\mathcal{F}^{x\to y})\right)f(y)+

(IV.7)

\displaystyle k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})\sum_{y\neq x}\left(w(\mathcal{F}^{x-1/N\to y})-w(\mathcal{F}^{x\to y})\right)f(y).

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x+1/N\to y}
and
(x,x+1/N)\in F
. Then also
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to y}
. Similarly, if
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to y}
and
(x,x+1/N)\in F
, then
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x+1/N\to y}
. The same holds for
x-1/N
instead of
x+1/N
.

Now let
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x+1/N\to y}
and
(x,x+1/N)\notin F
. Suppose that
x
is no root in
F
. Then there is a unique
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to y}
with
(x,x-1/N)\notin F^{\prime}
such that
k(x,x+1/N)w(F)=k(x,x-1/N)w(F^{\prime})
. The same for
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x-1/N\to y}
and
(x,x-1/N)\notin F
such that
x
is not a root. Then there is a unique
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to y}
with
(x,x+1/N)\notin F^{\prime}
such that
k(x,x-1/N)w(F)=k(x,x-1/N)w(F^{\prime})
. We can also start with
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to y}
with
(x,x+1/N)\notin F
, then there is
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{x-1/N\to y}
without root in
x
such that
(x,x-1/N)\notin F^{\prime}
and
k(x,x-1/N)w(F^{\prime})=k(x,x+1/N)w(F)
. The same reasoning holds if we start with
F\in\mathcal{F}^{x\to y}
with
(x,x-1/N)\notin F
. We have established the one–to–one correspondence.

The only remaining terms in the sum (
IV.7
) are

\displaystyle k(x,x+1/N)\sum_{y\neq x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}^{x+1/N\to y}\\
(x,x+1/N)\notin F\\
x\text{ is root}\end{subarray}}w(F)+k(x,x-1/N)\sum_{y\neq x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}^{x-1/N\to y}\\
(x,x-1/N)\notin F\\
x\text{ is root}\end{subarray}}w(F).

This is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathcal{T}_{y}\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}w(T)f(y).

Altogether we have that (
IV.6
) is equal to

\displaystyle-\sum_{T\notin\mathcal{T}_{x}}w(T)f(x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathcal{T}_{y}\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}w(T)f(y)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{T\notin\mathcal{T}_{x}}w(T)f(x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathcal{T}_{y}\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}w(T)f(y)-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{x}}w(T)f(x)+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{x}}w(T)f(x)

\displaystyle=-w(\mathcal{T})f(x)+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{y}}w(T)f(y)

\displaystyle=-w(\mathcal{T})f(x).

The last equality follows from the assumption that

has vanishing stationary average, and the Kirchhoff formula (

).

We conclude that
w(\mathcal{T})Lg\,(x)=-w(\mathcal{T})f(x)
and thus

for all

-(L_{D}^{-1}f)(x)=g(x)=\dfrac{\sum_{y}w\left(\mathcal{F}^{x\rightarrow y}\right)}{w\left(\mathcal{T}\right)}f(y)
.
∎

IV.3
Computation of quasipotential

The graphical representation (
IV.5
) leads to an algorithm discussed in Appendix
A
for efficient computation of the quasipotential. The backward generator

Lg\,(x)=k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})\,\left(g(x+\frac{1}{N})-g(x)\right)+k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})\,\left(g(x-\frac{1}{N})-g(x)\right)

(IV.8)

on functions

\mathbb{Z}_{N}

Let
u
be an energy function defined on
[0,1]
and write
\beta=\frac{1}{T}
for the inverse of the absolute temperature
T

We use three types of transition rates:

k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})=e^{\beta(u(x)-u(x+\frac{1}{N}))}e^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}},\quad k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})=e^{\beta(u(x)-u(x-\frac{1}{N}))}e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}}

(IV.9)

(unbounded rates with drift amplitude
\frac{\varepsilon}{N}

k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})=e^{\frac{\beta}{2}(u(x)-u(x+\frac{1}{N}))}e^{\beta\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}},\quad k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})=e^{\frac{\beta}{2}(u(x)-u(x-\frac{1}{N}))}e^{-\beta\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}}

(IV.10)

(unbounded rates with drift amplitude
\beta\frac{\varepsilon}{N}
), and

k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})=\frac{e^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}}}{1+e^{-\beta(u(x)-u(x+\frac{1}{N}))}},\quad k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})=\frac{e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}}}{1+e^{-\beta(u(x)-u(x-\frac{1}{N}))}}

(IV.11)

\frac{\varepsilon}{N}

Diffusion limit from the Kirchhoff formula

Consider the Markov diffusion process
x_{t}\in S^{1}
(unit circle) defined via,

\textrm{d}x_{t}=[\varepsilon-u^{\prime}(x_{t})]\,\textrm{d}t+\sqrt{2T}\,\textrm{d}W_{t}

is standard Brownian motion, with prefactor containing the temperature
T

u
and constant driving
\varepsilon

N^{2}
while the mesh shrinks like

is the backward generator (
IV.8
) of the Markov process on

with transition rates
IV.10

, starting from the Kirchhoff-matrix tree theorem.

Recall the tree notions from Section

, and the Kirchhoff formula (
IV.4

That Kirchhoff formula has been applied in

to obtain the low-temperature asymptotics of

IV.10
). For those rates the amplitude

is divided by

, which is the correct diffusive scaling to reach (

that
T_{x}^{y}
denotes the tree in

such that the edge between vertex

From (

\displaystyle w(\mathcal{T}_{x})=\sum_{y}e^{\frac{\beta}{2}(u(y)-u(x))}\,e^{-\frac{\beta}{2N}\varepsilon a}\,e^{\frac{\beta}{2}(u(y+\frac{1}{N})-u(x))}\,e^{\frac{\beta}{2N}\varepsilon b}.

In the limit of large

The previous calculations can be extended to obtain the diffusion limit of the quasipotential (

Before doing that, we note that for the Markov diffusion process (

VIII
Boundedness of quasipotential

The previous formulæ (
VII
) and graphical representations allow evaluating the boundedness of the quasipotential. It suffices in fact to give a bound for the difference of quasipotentials over any edge. We treat here two (extreme) cases of transition rates.

We take the case of large
\varepsilon>0
in e.g. (
IV.11
), so that for simplicity we put
u(x)=0
at all vertices
x
, while

k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})=e^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}},\qquad k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})=e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}}.

(VIII.1)

Then, obviously, the maximal weight of rooted spanning trees is obtained when all edges are directed clockwise (in the same direction as the bias
\varepsilon
). There are
N
such rooted spanning trees, each with weight
e^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{N-1}{N}}
. On the other hand, the maximum weight of double–rooted trees (with
N-2
edges) is
e^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{N-2}{N}}
. Therefore, for every
\varepsilon
,
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
in (
VII
) are bounded,

\displaystyle V_{+}(x),\,V_{-}(x)\leq\sum_{y}\frac{N(N-1)e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{N-2}{N}}}{Ne^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{N-1}{N}}}f(y)\leq\,N^{2}\,e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}}\,\,f_{\text{max}},\quad f_{\text{max}}:=\max_{y}|f(y)|.

The
N^{2}
is often too big as a prefactor (as we did not take into account the centrality of
f
). For example, suppose that
f(x)=0
whenever
x\not=x^{*},x^{*}+1
, and
\Delta f:=|f(x^{*}+1)-f(x^{*})|
. Then,

V_{+}(x),\,V_{-}(x)\leq\frac{N(N-1)e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{N-2}{N}}}{Ne^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{N-1}{N}}}\Delta f\leq Ne^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1}{N}}\Delta f

and if for every
x
,
k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})=k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})=1
, then

\displaystyle V_{+}(x),\,V_{-}(x)\leq\frac{N^{2}\Delta f}{N^{2}}\leq\Delta f.

For going to the other extreme (looking back at the rates (
IV.11
) again), take
\varepsilon=0
and put for every
x\not=x^{*}
,
u(x)=0
while
u(x^{*})=u
. That is

k(x^{*},x^{*}+\frac{1}{N})=k(x^{*},x^{*}-\frac{1}{N})=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta u}},\qquad k(x,x+\frac{1}{N})=k(x,x-\frac{1}{N})=1.

In that case
w(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta u}}(N(N-1)+2)+(N-2)
and

\displaystyle V_{+}(x^{*}),\,V_{-}(x^{*})\leq\frac{N(N-1)}{\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta u}}(N(N-1)+2)+(N-2)}\,Nf_{\text{max}}\leq N(1+e^{-\beta u})\,\,f_{\text{max}}

and

\displaystyle V_{+}(x),\,V_{-}(x)\leq\frac{(N^{2}-1)\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta u}}+1}{\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta u}}(N(N-1)+2)+(N-2)}\,Nf_{\text{max}}\leq N\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta u}}\,\,f_{\text{max}}.

IX
Conclusions

The computation of
excesses
, the temporally–accumulated difference

\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}t\,\left[\langle g(X_{t})\,|\,X_{0}=x\rangle-\langle g\rangle\right]

(IX.1)

between finite–time expectations
\langle g(X_{t})\,|\,X_{0}=x\rangle

and their limit
\langle g\rangle=\lim_{t\uparrow\infty}\langle g(X_{t})\,|\,X_{0}=x\rangle
, is important for statistical mechanical
applications of stochastic processes
X_{t},t\geq 0
. In the present
paper, for
X_{t}
we have considered a driven random walker on a ring with
N

vertices, and we obtained a graphical representation (in terms of
combining weights of two trees) leading to efficient computation of
such a difference (
IX.1
), called the quasipotential. We have related that
quasipotential to the Drazin–inverse of the backward generator.

As an application to thermal physics and by choosing the appropriate
function
g
, we have computed the heat capacity of an ideal gas of
driven walkers. We have demonstrated that it is perfectly possible,
thanks to the developed algorithm, to study its low-temperature behavior
(satisfying an extended Nernst heat theorem in the case of bounded
transition rates) and its dependence on driving and on the size of the
ring. We have also shown that a continuum limit is possible,
giving an overdamped diffusion for which the quasipotential can be
given explicitly.

We expect that the combination of graph theory with nonequilibrium
statistical mechanics of Markov jump processes, as tried here, will give
rise to further interesting applications, in particular by extending
this work to metric graphs and to active (or persistent) random walkers.
