A Near-Linear Time Algorithm for the Chamfer Distance
Ainesh Bakshi, Piotr Indyk, Rajesh Jayaram, Sandeep Silwal, Erik Waingarten

Abstract
For any two point sets A , B ⊂ ℝ d A,B\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d} of size up to n n , the Chamfer distance from A A to B B is defined as CH ​ ( A , B ) = ∑ a ∈ A min b ∈ B ⁡ d X ​ ( a , b ) \texttt{CH}(A,B)=\sum_{a\in A}\min_{b\in B}d_{X}(a,b) , where d X d_{X} is the underlying distance measure (e.g., the Euclidean or Manhattan distance). The Chamfer distance is a popular measure of dissimilarity between point clouds, used in many machine learning, computer vision, and graphics applications, and admits a straightforward 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ n 2 ) \mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(dn^{2}\right) -time brute force algorithm. Further, the Chamfer distance is often used as a proxy for the more computationally demanding Earth-Mover (Optimal Transport) Distance. However, the quadratic dependence on n n in the running time makes the naive approach intractable for large datasets. We overcome this bottleneck and present the first ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximate algorithm for estimating the Chamfer distance with a near-linear running time. Specifically, our algorithm runs in time 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ d ​ log ⁡ ( n ) / ε 2 ) \mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(nd\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2}\right) and is implementable. Our experiments demonstrate that it is both accurate and fast on large high-dimensional datasets. We believe that our algorithm will open new avenues for analyzing large high-dimensional point clouds. We also give evidence that if the goal is to report a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximate mapping from A A to B B (as opposed to just its value), then any sub-quadratic time algorithm is unlikely to exist.

(Estimating Chamfer Distance in Nearly Linear Time)

Uniform vs Importance Sampling.

A natural algorithm for estimating
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
proceeds by
uniform sampling
: sample an
a\in A
uniformly at random and explicitly compute
\min_{b\in B}\|a-b\|_{1}
. In general, we can compute the estimator
\hat{z}
for
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
by averaging over
s
uniformly chosen samples, resulting in runtime
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(nds\right)
. It is easy to see that the resulting estimator is un-biased, i.e.
\E\left[\hat{z}\right]=\texttt{CH}(A,B)
. However, if a small constant fraction of elements in
A
contribute significantly to
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
, then
s=\Omega(n)
samples could be necessary to obtain, say, a
1\%
relative error estimate with constant probability. Since each sample requires a linear scan to find the nearest neighbor, this would result in a quadratic runtime.

While such an approach has good empirical performance for well-behaved datasets, it does not work for data sets where the distribution of the distances from points in
A
to their nearest neighbors in
B
is skewed. Further, it is computationally prohibitive to verify the quality of the approximation given by uniform sampling. Towards proving Theorem
2.1
, it is paramount to obtain an algorithm that works regardless of the structure of the input dataset.

A more nuanced approach is to perform
importance sampling
where we sample
a\in A
with probability proportional to its contribution to
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
. In particular, if we had access to a distribution,
\bm{D}_{a}
, over elements
a\in A
such that,
\min_{b\in B}\left\lVert a-b\right\rVert_{1}\leqslant\bm{D}_{a}\leqslant\lambda\min_{b\in B}\left\lVert a-b\right\rVert_{1}
, for some parameter
\lambda>1
, then sampling
O\left\lparen\lambda\right\rparen
samples results in an estimator
\hat{z}
that is within
1\%
relative error to the true answer with probability at least
99\%
. Formally, we consider the estimator defined in Algorithm
1
, where we assume access to
\texttt{CrudeNN}(A,B)
, a sub-routine which receives as input
A
and
B
and outputs estimates
\bm{D}_{a}\in\mathbbm{R}_{\geqslant 0}
for each
a\in A
which is guaranteed to be an upper bound for
\min_{b\in B}\|a-b\|_{1}
. Based on the values
\{\bm{D}_{a}\}_{a\in A}
we construct an importance sampling distribution
\mathcal{D}
supported on
A
. As a result, we obtain the following lemma:

Obtaining importance sampling probabilities.

It remains to show how to implement the
\texttt{CrudeNN}(A,B)
subroutine to obtain the distribution over elements in
A
which is a reasonable over-estimator of the true probabilities.
A natural first step is to consider performing an
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)
-approximate nearest neighbor search (NNS): for every
a^{\prime}\in A
, find
b^{\prime}\in B
satisfying
\|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}\|_{1}/\min_{b\in B}\|a^{\prime}-b\|_{1}=\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)
. This leads to the desired guarantees on
\{\bm{D}_{a}\}_{a\in A}
. Unfortunately, the state of the art algorithms for
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)
-approximate NNS, even under the
\ell_{1}
norm, posses extraneous
\mbox{poly}(\log n)
factors in the runtime, resulting in a significantly higher running time. These factors are even higher for the
\ell_{2}
norm.
Therefore, instead of performing a direct reduction to approximate NNS, we open up the approximate NNS black-box and give a simple algorithm which directly satisfies our desired guarantees on
\{\bm{D}_{a}\}_{a\in A}
.

To begin with, we assume that the aspect ratio of all pair-wise distances is bounded by a fixed polynomial,
\poly(n/\varepsilon)
(we defer the reduction from an arbitrary input to one with polynomially bounded aspect ratio to Lemma
A.3
).
We proceed via computing
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log(n/\varepsilon)\right)
different (randomized) partitions of the dataset
A\cup B
. The
i
-th partition, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log(n/\varepsilon)\right)
, can be written as
A\cup B=\cup_{j}\mathcal{P}^{i}_{j}
and approximately satisfies the property that points in
A\cup B
that are at distance at most
2^{i}
will be in the same partition
\mathcal{P}^{i}_{j}
with sufficiently large probability. To obtain these components, we use a family of
locality-sensitive hash functions
, whose formal properties are given in Definition
A.1
. Intuitively, these hash functions guarantee that:

1.

For each
a^{\prime}\in A
, its
true
nearest neighbor
b^{\prime}\in B
falls into the
same
component as
a^{\prime}
in the
i_{0}
-th partition, where
2^{i_{0}}=\Theta(\|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}\|_{1}
)
2
2

2

Recall we assumed all distances are between
1
and
\poly(n)
resulting in only
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)
different partitions
, and

2.

Every other extraneous
b\neq b^{\prime}
is
not
in the same component as
a^{\prime}
for each
i<i_{0}
.

It is easy to check that any hash function that satisfies the aforementioned guarantees yields a valid set of distances
\{\bm{D}_{a}\}_{a\in A}
as follows: for every
a^{\prime}\in A
, find the smallest
i_{0}
for which there exists a
b^{\prime}\in B
in the same component as
a^{\prime}
in the
i_{0}
-th partition. Then set
\bm{D}_{a^{\prime}}=\|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}\|_{1}
. Intuitively, the
b^{\prime}
we find for any fixed
a^{\prime}
in this procedure will have distance that is at least the closest neighbor in
B
and with good probability, it won’t be too much larger. A caveat here is that we cannot show the above guarantee holds for
2^{i_{0}}=\Theta(\|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}\|_{1})
. Instead, we obtain the slightly weaker guarantee that,
in the expectation
, the partition
b^{\prime}
lands in is a
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)
-approximation to the minimum distance, i.e.
2^{i_{0}}=\Theta(\log n\cdot\|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}\|_{1})
. Therefore, after running
\texttt{CrudeNN}(A,B)
, setting
\lambda=\log n
suffices for our
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(nd\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2}\right)
time algorithm. We formalize this argument in the following lemma:

(Oversampling with bounded Aspect Ratio)
.

Let
(X,d_{X})

Algorithm
2
,
\emph{\texttt{CrudeNN}}(A,B)
, outputs a list of (random) positive numbers

which satisfy the following two guarantees:

•

With probability
1
, every
a\in A
satisfies
\bm{D}_{a}\geqslant\min_{b\in B}d_{X}(a,b)
.

•

For every
a\in A
,
\mathop{\E}[\bm{D}_{a}]\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)\cdot\min_{b\in B}d_{X}(a,b)

2

\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(dn\log(n/\varepsilon)\right)

3.1
Experimental Setup

We use three different experimental setups, small scale, outlier, and large scale. They are designed to ‘stress test’ our algorithm, and relevant baselines, under vastly different parameter regimes. The datasets we use are summarized in Table

Small Scale

These experiments are motivated from common use cases of Chamfer distance in the computer vision and NLP domains. In our small scale experiments, we use two different datasets: (a) the ShapeNet dataset, a collection of point clouds of objects in three dimensions
[
6
]
. ShapeNet is a common benchmark dataset frequently used in computer graphics, computer vision, robotics and Chamfer distance is a widely used measure of similarity between different ShapeNet point clouds
[
6
]
.
(b) We create point clouds of words from text documents from
[
11
]
. Each point represents a word embedding obtained from the word-to-vec model of
[
14
]
in
\mathbbm{R}^{300}
applied to the Federalist Papers corpus. As mentioned earlier, a popular relaxation of the common Earth Mover Distance is exactly the (weighted) version of the Chamfer distance
[

,
1
]

Since ShapenNet is in three dimensions, we implement nearest neighbor queries using KD-trees to accelerate the brute force baseline as KD-trees can perform exact nearest neighbor search quickly in small dimensions. However, they have runtime exponential in dimension meaning they cannot be used for the text embedding dataset, for which we use a standard naive brute force computation. For both these datasets, we implement our algorithms using Python 3.9.7 on an M1 MacbookPro with 32GB of RAM. We also use an efficient implementation of KD trees in Python and use Numpy and Numba whenever relevant. Since the point clouds in the dataset have approximately the same

\texttt{CH}(A,B)+\texttt{CH}(B,A)

4
Lower Bound for Reporting the Alignment

We presented an algorithm that, in time
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(nd\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2}\right)
, produces a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation to
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
. It is natural to ask whether it is also possible to
report
a mapping
g:A\to B
whose cost
\sum_{a\in A}\|a-g(a)\|_{1}
is within a factor of
1+\varepsilon
from
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
. (Our algorithm uses on random sampling and thusdoes not give such a mapping). This section shows that, under a popular complexity-theoretic conjecture called the
Hitting Set Conjecture

[
22
]
, such an algorithm does not exists. For simplicity, we focus on the case when the underlying metric
d_{X}
is induced by the Manhattan distance, i.e.,
d_{X}(a,b)=\|a-b\|_{1}
. The argument is similar for the Euclidean distance, Euclidean distance squared, etc. To state our result formally, we first define the Hitting Set (HS) problem.

Definition 4.1

(Hitting Set (HS) problem)
.

The input to the problem consists of two sets of vectors
A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
, and the goal is to determine whether there exists some
a\in A
such that
a\cdot b\neq 0
for every
b\in B
. If such an
a\in A
exists, we say that
a

hits

B
.

It is easy to see that the Hitting Set problem can be solved in time
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(n^{2}d\right)
. The Hitting Set Conjecture
[
22
]
postulates that this running time is close to the optimal. Specifically:

Conjecture 4.2
.

Suppose
d=\Theta(\log^{2}n)
. Then for every constant
\delta>0
, no randomized algorithm can solve the Hitting Set problem in
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(n^{2-\delta}\right)
time.

Our result can be now phrased as follows.

Theorem 4.3

(Hardness for reporting a mapping)
.

Let
T(N,D,\varepsilon)
be the running time of an algorithm
ALG
that, given sets of
A",B"\subset\{0,1\}^{D}
of sizes at most
N
, reports a mapping
g:A"\to B"
with cost
(1+\varepsilon)\textup{{CH}(}A",B")
, for
D=\Theta(\log^{2}N)
and
\varepsilon=\frac{\Theta(1)}{D}
. Assuming the Hitting Set Conjecture, we have that
T(N,D,\varepsilon)
is at least
\Omega(N^{2-\delta})
for any constant
\delta>0
.

5
Conclusion

We present an efficient approximation algorithm for estimating the Chamfer distance up to a
1+\varepsilon
factor in time
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(nd\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2}\right)
. The result is complemented with a conditional lower bound which shows that reporting a Chamfer distance mapping of similar quality requires nearly quadratic time. Our algorithm is easy to implement in practice and compares favorably to brute force computation and uniform sampling. We envision our main tools of obtaining fast estimates of coarse nearest neighbor distances combined with importance sampling can have additional applications in the analysis of high-dimensional, large scale data.

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant 0}2^{-\Omega(k^{2})}\int_{\gamma:c\gamma_{a}}^{\infty}\max\left\{\exp\left(\ln(n)-c_{1}\cdot\frac{\gamma}{2^{k+2}\cdot\gamma_{a}}\right),1\right\}d\gamma

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant 0}2^{-\Omega(k^{2})}\left(\left(\alpha_{k}-c\cdot\gamma_{a}\right)^{+}+\int_{\gamma:\alpha_{k}}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\cdot\frac{\gamma}{2^{k+2}\gamma_{a}}\right)d\gamma\right),

where in the second inequality, we used the fact that the setting of
\alpha_{k}
, the additional
\ln(n)
factor in the exponent can be removed up to a factor of two. Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma:c\gamma_{a}}^{\infty}\mathop{\operatorname{\mathbf{Pr}}}\left[\bm{E}_{2}(\gamma)\right]d\gamma\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant 0}2^{-\Omega(k^{2})}\left(\gamma_{a}\cdot\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(2^{k}\log n\right)+\gamma_{a}\cdot\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(2^{k}\right)\right)=\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)\cdot\gamma_{a}.

Finally, the running time is dominated by the cost of evaluating
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log(n/\varepsilon)\right)
functions on
n
points in dimension
d
. Since each evaluation takes
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(d\right)
time, the bound follows.
∎

A.2
Locality-Sensitive Hashing at Every Scale

Lemma A.4

(Constructing a LSH at every scale)
.

For any
r\geqslant 0
and any
d\in\mathbbm{N}
, there exists a hash family
\mathcal{H}(r)
such that for any two points
x,y\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{\mathbf{Pr}}}_{\bm{h}\sim\mathcal{H}(r)}\left[\bm{h}(x)\neq\bm{h}(y)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\|x-y\|_{1}}{r}

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{\mathbf{Pr}}}_{\bm{h}\sim\mathcal{H}(r)}\left[\bm{h}(x)=\bm{h}(y)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{1}}{r}\right).

In addition, for any
\bm{h}\sim\mathcal{H}(r)
,
\bm{h}(x)
may be computed in
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(d\right)
time.

Proof.

The construction proceeds in the following way: in order to generate a function
\bm{h}\colon\mathbbm{R}^{d}\to\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
sampled from
\mathcal{H}(r)
,

•

We sample a random vector
\bm{z}\sim[0,r]^{d}
.

•

We let

\bm{h}(x)=\left(\left\lceil\frac{x_{1}+\bm{z}_{1}}{r}\right\rceil,\left\lceil\frac{x_{2}+\bm{z}_{2}}{r}\right\rceil,\dots,\left\lceil\frac{x_{d}+\bm{z}_{d}}{r}\right\rceil\right).

Fix
x,y\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
. If
\bm{h}(x)\neq\bm{h}(y)
, there exists some coordinate
k\in[d]
on which
\bm{h}(x)_{k}\neq\bm{h}(y)_{k}
. This occurs whenever (i)
|x_{k}-y_{k}|>r
, or (ii)
|x_{k}-y_{k}|\leqslant r
, but
\bm{z}_{k}
happens to fall within an interval of length
|x_{k}-y_{k}|
, thereby separating
x
from
y
. By a union bound,

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{\mathbf{Pr}}}_{\bm{h}\sim\mathcal{H}(r)}\left[\bm{h}(x)\neq\bm{h}(y)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{|x_{k}-y_{k}|}{r}=\frac{\|x-y\|_{1}}{r}.

On the other hand, in order for
\bm{h}(x)=\bm{h}(y)
, it must be the case that every
|x_{k}-y_{k}|\leqslant r
, and in addition, the threshold
\bm{z}_{k}
always avoids an interval of length
|x_{k}-y_{k}|
. The probability that this occurs is

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{\mathbf{Pr}}}_{\bm{h}\sim\mathcal{H}(r)}\left[\bm{h}(x)=\bm{h}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\max\left\{0,1-\frac{|x_{k}-y_{k}|}{r}\right\}\leqslant\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{|x_{k}-y_{k}|}{r}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{1}}{r}\right).

∎

Extending the above construction to
\ell_{2}
follows from embedding the points
A\cup B
into
\ell_{1}
via a standard construction.

Theorem A.5

(

13
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and define
\bm{T}:\mathbbm{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{k}

\bm{T}(x)_{i}=\frac{1}{\beta k}\sum_{j=1}^{d}Z_{ij}x_{j},\quad i=1,\ldots,k

where
\beta=\sqrt{2/\pi}
. Then for every vector

, we have

\operatorname{\mathbf{Pr}}[(1-\varepsilon)\|x\|_{2}\leqslant\|\bm{T}(x)\|_{1}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\|x\|_{2}]\geqslant 1-e^{c\varepsilon^{2}k},

where
c>0
is a constant.

The map
\bm{T}\colon\mathbbm{R}^{d}\to\mathbbm{R}^{k}
with
k=\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n/\varepsilon^{2}\right)
gives an embedding of
A\cup B
into
\ell_{1}^{k}
of distortion
(1\pm\varepsilon)
with high probability. Formally, with probability at least
1-1/n
over the draw of
\bm{T}
with
t=\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n/\varepsilon^{2}\right)
, every
a\in A
and
b\in B
satisfies

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\|a-b\|_{2}\leqslant\|\bm{T}(a)-\bm{T}(b)\|_{1}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\|a-b\|_{2}.

This embedding has the effect of reducing
\ell_{2}
to
\ell_{1}
without affecting the Chamfer distance of the mapped points by more than a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-factor. In addition, the embedding incurs an extra additive factor of
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(nd\log n/\varepsilon^{2}\right)
to the running time in order to perform the embedding for all points.

A.3
Reduction to
\poly(n/\varepsilon)
Aspect Ratio for
\ell_{p}
,
p\in[1,2]

In this section, we discuss how to reduce to the case of a
\poly(n/\varepsilon)
aspect ratio. The reduction proceeds by first obtaining a very crude estimate of
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
(which will be a
\poly(n)
-approximation), applying a locality-sensitive hash function in order to partition points of
A
and
B
which are significantly farther than
\poly(n)\cdot\texttt{CH}(A,B)
. Finally, we add
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)
coordinates and add random vector of length
\poly(\varepsilon/n)\cdot\texttt{CH}(A,B)
in order to guarantee that the minimum distance is at least
\poly(\varepsilon/n)\cdot\texttt{CH}(A,B)
without changing
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
significantly.

Proof of Lemma
A.3
.

Partitioning Given Very Crude Estimates.
In particular, suppose that with an
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(nd\right)+\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(n\log n\right)
time computation, we can achieve a value of
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbbm{R}_{\geqslant 0}
which satisfies

\texttt{CH}(A,B)\leqslant\bm{\eta}\leqslant c\cdot\texttt{CH}(A,B),

with high probability (which we will show how to do briefly with
c=\poly(n)
). Then, consider sampling
\bm{h}\sim\mathcal{H}(cn\cdot\bm{\eta})
and partitioning
A
and
B
into equivalence classes according to where they hash to under
\bm{h}
. The probability that two points at distance farther than
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)\cdot cn\cdot\bm{\eta}
collide under
\bm{h}
is small enough to union bound over at most
n^{2}
many possible pairs of vectors. In addition, the probability that there exists
a\in A
for which
b\in B
minimizing
\|a-b\|_{p}
satisfies
\bm{h}(a)\neq\bm{h}(b)
is at most
\texttt{CH}(A,B)/(cn\cdot\bm{\eta})\leqslant 1/n
. This latter inequality implies that computing the Chamfer distance of the corresponding parts in the partition and summing them is equivalent to computing
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
.

Getting Very Crude Estimates.
We now show how to obtain a
\poly(n)
-approximation to
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
in time
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(nd\right)+\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(n\log n\right)
for points in
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
with
\ell_{p}
distance. This is done via the
p
-stable sketch of Indyk
[
9
]
. In particular, we sample a vector
\bm{g}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
by independent
p
-stable random variables (for instance,
\bm{g}
is a standard Gaussian vector for
p=2
and a vector of independent Cauchy random variables for
p=1
). We may then compute the scalar random variables
\{\langle a,\bm{g}\rangle\}_{a\in A}
and
\{\langle b,\bm{g}\rangle\}_{b\in B}
, which give a projection onto a one-dimensional space. By
p
-stability, for any
a\in A
and
b\in B
, the distribution of
\langle a,\bm{g}\rangle-\langle b,\bm{g}\rangle
is exactly as
\|a-b\|_{p}\cdot\bm{g}^{\prime}
, where
\bm{g}^{\prime}
is an independent
p
-stable random variable. Hence, we will have that for every
a\in A
and
b\in B
,

\frac{\|a-b\|_{p}}{\poly(n)}\leqslant\left|\langle a,\bm{g}\rangle-\langle b,\bm{g}\rangle\right|\leqslant\|a-b\|_{p}\cdot\poly(n),

with probability
1-1/\poly(n)
and hence
\texttt{CH}(\{\langle a,\bm{g}\rangle\}_{a\in A},\{\langle b,\bm{g}\rangle\}_{b\in B})
, which is computable by 1-dimensional nearest neighbor search (i.e., repeatedly querying a binary search tree), gives a
\poly(n)
-approximation to
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
.

Adding Distance
Finally, we now note that
\bm{\eta}/c
gives us a lower bound on
\texttt{CH}(A,B)
. Suppose we append
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)
coordinates to each point and in those coordinates, we add a random vector of norm
\varepsilon\cdot\bm{\eta}/(cn)
. With high probability, every pair of points is now at distance at least
\varepsilon\cdot\bm{\eta}/(cn)
. In addition, the Chamfer distance between the new set of points increases by at most an additive
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\varepsilon\bm{\eta}/c\right)
, which is at most
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\varepsilon\right)\cdot\texttt{CH}(A,B)
, proving Lemma
A.3
.

∎
