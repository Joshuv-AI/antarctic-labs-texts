Introduction

Given a set system
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
with ground set
X
and a collection
{\mathcal{R}}\subset 2^{X}
of subsets of
X
, the
combinatorial
discrepancy
of the system is given by

{\rm disc}({\mathcal{R}}):=\min_{\chi:X\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}}\max_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}\left|\sum_{i\in S}\chi(i)\right|,

that is, the maximum imbalance over all sets in
{\mathcal{R}}
, minimized over all possible
2
-partitions of
X
.
Using the incidence matrix
A
of the system
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
, an equivalent linear algebraic formulation – the
vector balancing

problem can be stated as

{\rm disc}(A):=\min_{x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\|Ax\|_{\infty}.

For a given class of set systems, the problem of
discrepancy minimization
seeks to establish bounds on the
discrepancy of systems in the class, as a function of the size of the ground set, or other parameters. Besides its
inherent interest, discrepancy minimization has many applications in several areas of mathematics as well as computer
science and related subjects
[
13
,
30
]
.

Beck-Fiala and Komlos Conjectures

In their seminal work which initiated the use of the term “discrepancy” as well as the study of the discrepancy of set systems,
Beck and Fiala
[
10
]
showed that any set system where each element belongs to at most
t
sets, has discrepancy at most
2t-1
.
They conjectured that this bound could be improved to
O\left(\sqrt{t}\right)
. In linear algebraic terms, the closely related generalization
known as the
Komlos conjecture
, states that for a real matrix with
\ell_{2}
norm of each column at most
1
, the discrepancy is
O(1)
.
Despite significant progress in partial results and
understanding of discrepancy theory in the last
4
decades, these tantalizing conjectures have remained open.

Early and Recent Progress

Since the seminal results of Beck and Fiala, much progress has been made toward resolving
the Beck-Fiala and Komlos conjectures. Early breakthroughs in the area include those of Spencer

[
39
]
and Gluskin
[
22
]
who showed that the Komlos conjecture holds for
t=\Theta(n)
.
Later Srinivasan
[
40
]
and Banaszczyk
[
3
]
proved bounds for general
t
. The latter’s
bound of
5\sqrt{t\log n}
remains the best current result on the Komlos conjecture.

The above bounds were non-constructive and it was even conjectured that efficient algorithms to
construct colorings with the promised discrepancy bounds, did not exist. However in a major breakthrough, Bansal
[
4
]

gave an efficient algorithm to construct a coloring matching Spencer’s discrepancy bounds for
|{\mathcal{R}}|=\Theta(n)
.
This has been followed by a large number of recent results, both algorithmic and non-algorithmic, for upper and lower
bounds, as well as many applications and generalizations, see
[
28
,
5
,
6
,
16
,
27
,
37
,
7
]
.

Geometric Set Systems

The discrepancy of set systems with a geometric interpretation, such as for example of points and half-planes in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
or points and disks in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
has been of significant theoretical and practical importance, as it relates to several areas like computational geometry, statistical and
machine learning, algorithmic analysis, database theory, etc.
[
30
,
25
,
26
]
.
Questions such as Tuśnady’s problem have been studied since the
early years of discrepancy theory
[
9
,
11
]
. Other notable results include those of points and
projective planes (Spencer
[
1
]
), primal systems
of bounded VC dimension (Matoušek
[
29
]
), systems of bounded dual VC dimension, etc.(Matoušek-Welzl-Wernisch
[
32
]
).

More recently Ezra
[
21
]
proposed the notion of size-sensitive discrepancy, and further asked about the discrepancy of geometric set
systems with bounded degree. Using the packing and chaining framework of Dutta, Ezra and Ghosh
[
17
]
, together with the
partial coloring based algorithm of Lovett-Meka (or a similar algorithm), it is comparatively
straightforward to obtain bounds of
O\left(t^{1/4}\cdot\log n\right)
for points and half-planes in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. To some extent, this setting has been
considered in the unpublished work
[
20
]
,
but the bounds have an extra
\sqrt{\log\log t}\log n
factor (as in the above example) which stems from using
the partial coloring approach and a suboptimal assignment of parameters.

Moreover these bounds do not appear
in the published version.
To the best of our knowledge,
other than the above-mentioned unpublished drafts, there are no published or publicly available works which consider the question of
geometric set systems with bounded degree of elements. However interesting questions remain to be asked for such systems.
For example it seemed interesting to ask if the
O\left(t^{1/4}\log n\right)
bound for points and half-planes in the Beck-Fiala setting,
can be improved to

or better, using (for instance) the algorithmic frameworks of Bansal and others.

Set systems with low

or

and
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, points and (pseudo)disks in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and several others (see, for example

since dual systems of low union complexity also have low

where better bounds have been obtained for them for several structures of interest, such as unweighted and weighted epsilon nets, epsilon approximations,
relative approximations,
\epsilon

Our Contribution

We investigate the discrepancy of geometric set systems with linear or near-linear shallow cell complexity, and each element belonging
to at most
t
sets, where
t
is a given parameter independent of
n
. Our discrepancy bounds are stated below.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
be a set system with shallow cell complexity
\psi(m,k)=g(m)k^{c}
, where
g(m)=o(m^{{\varepsilon}})
for every
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
, is a non-decreasing
function of
m
and
c>0
is a constant independent of
n
and
k
, and each element of
X
belongs to at most
t
sets of

{\mathcal{R}}
. Then the discrepancy of
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
is at most

O\left(\sqrt{\left(\log n+\left(t^{c}g(n)\right)^{\frac{1}{1+c}}\right)\log n}\right).

In particular, for the following families of set systems with degree at most
t
, the discrepancy is bounded as below.

1.

Points and half-planes in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:
O\left(\sqrt{(\log n+t^{1/2})\log n}\right)
.

2.

Points and homothets of a convex body in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:
O\left(\sqrt{(\log n+t^{1/2})\log n}\right)
.

3.

Points and disks in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:
O\left(\sqrt{(\log n+t^{1/2})\log n}\right)
.

4.

Points and pseudodisks in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:
O\left(\sqrt{(\log n+t^{1/2})\log n}\right)
.

5.

Points and
\alpha
-fat triangles in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:
O\left(\sqrt{(\log n+(t\cdot\log^{*}n)^{1/2})\log n}\right)
.

6.

Points and objects with linear union complexity:
O\left(\sqrt{(\log n+t^{1/2})\log n}\right)
.

7.

Points and locally
\gamma
-fat semi-algebraic objects in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with bounded description complexity:

O\left(\sqrt{(\log n+t^{1/2}\cdot 2^{O(\log^{*}n)})\log n}\right)
.

8.

Points and half-spaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
:
O\left(\sqrt{(\log n+t^{2/3})\log n}\right)
.

9.

Points and orthants in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
:
O\left(\sqrt{(\log n+t^{1/2})\log n}\right)

These bounds match or improve the known, as well as the conjectured general bounds for systems of bounded degree.
For instance, for set systems of points and half-planes in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we get the bound of

O\left(\left(\left(\log n+\sqrt{t}\right)\log n\right)^{1/2}\right),

which is
O\left(\sqrt{t}\right)
for
t=\Omega(\log n)
.
For
t=\omega\left(\log^{2}n\right)

\sqrt{\log n}=o(t^{1/4})
, so the
O\left(t^{1/4}\sqrt{\log n}\right)
bound improves upon the conjectured

O\left(\sqrt{t}\right)
bound of Beck and Fiala. Thus, they give much better bounds even without using the random walk based framework and intricate analysis of
many recent discrepancy minimization algorithms e.g.
[
28
,
6
,
38
]
.

While our discrepancy and crossing number bounds are not difficult to obtain, we believe they are important, as

shallow cell complexity (without the degree bound), which are known to be tight

It should be noted that merely using low shallow complexity cannot improve the bound on the crossing number,

Matchings (or spanning paths or trees) with low crossing number are of wide interest, as they are used in a number of

2
Preliminaries

The
projection
of a set system
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
on to a subset
Y\subset X
of the ground set is denoted by

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mid Y}:=\left\{R\cap Y\;\mid\;R\in{\mathcal{R}}\right\}
.

A
primal
set system has a ground set
P
of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and subsets given by all possible intersections
with a class
{\cal G}
of geometric objects in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, that is the set system
(P,{\cal G}_{\mid P})
. A
dual
set system given
a (finite) collection
{\cal G}
of geometric objects is given by
({\cal G},{\cal G}_{\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, that is, equivalence classes of points of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
under
intersection with objects in
{\cal G}
.

The notion of
shallow cell complexity
has found many applications
in Computational Geometry, including improved bounds on
{\varepsilon}
-nets and related structures (see e.g.
[
35
]
).

Definition 5

(Shallow-cell Complexity)
.

A set system
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
has
shallow-cell complexity

\psi(\cdot,\cdot)
if for any
Y\subseteq X
,
the number of subsets in
{\mathcal{R}}|_{Y}
of size
l

is at most
|Y|\cdot\psi(|Y|,l)
.

In this paper, we shall throughout work with set systems whose shallow cell complexity
is given by

\displaystyle\psi(l,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle l^{c_{1}}g(l)k^{c},

(1)

where
c_{1},c>0
are constants independent of
l,k
, and
g(l)=o(l^{{\varepsilon}})
for every
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1]
.

Definition 6

(Union Complexity)
.

A set of
m
geometric objects
{\mathcal{O}}
has
union complexity

f(m)
if the number of faces of all dimensions in the union of the members
of
{\mathcal{O}}
, is at most
f(m)
.

It is known (see e.g.
[
35
]
) that set systems formed by intersections of points with objects having union complexity

f(.)
, have shallow cell complexity at most
f(m/k)\cdot k^{2}
.

A central lemma for set systems of bounded shallow cell complexity, is the Shallow Packing Lemma
of
[
17
,
33
]
. To state the lemma, first we need
to define
shallow packings
.

Definition 7

(Shallow Packing)
.

Let
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
be a set system, and
\delta
and
k
be positive integers.
A subset of ranges
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
is a
k
-shallow
\delta
-packing
or

(k,\delta)
-packing
, if any pair of ranges in
{\mathcal{P}}
have symmetric difference
greater than
\delta
, and for all
R\in{\mathcal{P}}
we have
|R\cap X|\leq k
.

Now follows the Shallow Packing Lemma of Mustafa
[
33
]
, which gives optimal bounds for
shallow packings of set systems, in terms of their shallow cell complexity.

Theorem 8

(Shallow Packing Lemma, Mustafa
[
33
]
)
.

Let
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
be a set system with
|X|=n
and shallow cell complexity
\varphi_{{\mathcal{R}}}
. If
the VC dimension of
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
is at most
d_{0}
, and
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
is a
k
-shallow
\delta
-packing then

\frac{24d_{0}n}{\delta}\cdot\varphi_{{\mathcal{R}}}\left(\frac{4d_{0}n}{\delta},\frac{12d_{0}k}{\delta}\right)\leq C_{d_{0}}\frac{n}{\delta}\psi\left(\frac{n}{\delta},\frac{k}{\delta}\right),

where
C_{d_{0}}
is a constant independent of
n
and
\delta
.

Finally, we define the notions of
crossing number
and partitions with low crossing number, which are central to this paper.

Definition 9

(Crossing Number; Partitions with Low Crossing Number)
.

A range
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
is
crossed
by a pair of elements of
X
,
\{x,y\}
if
|\{x,y\}\cap X|=1
. Given a partition of the universe
X
into
pairs
\Pi=(\{x_{i},y_{i}\})_{i=1}^{n/2}
with
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}\cap\{x_{j},y_{j}\}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
, and
\bigcup_{i\geq 1}\{x_{i},y_{i}\}=X
, the
crossing
number
of
\Pi
is the maximum number of pairs of
\Pi
which cross any given range of
{\mathcal{R}}
.

3
Well-Behaved Dual Systems

In this section, we prove our theorems and corollaries. We begin with the proof of Theorem
1
. The proof follows immediately from the
statements of Theorem
3
and Corollary
4
, with the following additional lemma,
which gives a bound on the discrepancy of an arbitrary set system, in terms of the
crossing number
of a perfect matching of the elements.
The first statement in the lemma is an easy observation, while the second statement is well-known (see e.g. Chapter 5
[
30
]
).

Lemma 10
.

Let
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
be a set system,
|X|
even, with degree bounded by
t
and having a matching with crossing number at most
N
. Then the discrepancy of
(X,{\mathcal{R}})
is at most

O\left(\min\left\{N,\sqrt{N\cdot\log|{\mathcal{R}}|}\right\}\right)=O\left(\sqrt{N\cdot\log|{\mathcal{R}}|}\right).

Proof.

It is easy to see that the discrepancy is at most
N
: just take an arbitrary coloring where each vertex of matched pair of vertices, is given opposite colors. The discrepancy
of a set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
is at most the number of pairs that have exactly one vertex in
S
. This is at most the crossing number, i.e.
N
.

To see that the discrepancy is at most
O\left(\sqrt{N\log|{\mathcal{R}}|}\right)
, consider a random coloring which ensure that in every matched pair, opposite colors are assigned to the vertices
in the pair. Now for any set
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
, the expected discrepancy of
S
is zero, and the variance of the discrepancy of
S
is at most the number of pairs which cross
S
, i.e. at most the
crossing number
N
. Now applying Chernoff bounds, allowing for a maximum deviation of
O\left(\sqrt{N\log|{\mathcal{R}}|}\right)
, and taking a union bound over all sets of
{\mathcal{R}}
, we
get the statement of the lemma.
∎

For the proof of Theorem
3
, we shall need the following lemma, which guarantees the existence of a pair
of points which cross a small number of ranges.

Lemma 11

(Short Edge Lemma)
.

Assume the setting of Theorem

. Then for any set or multiset of ranges

,
there exist points

is crossed by at most

maximum value satisfying

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{d_{0}}\frac{|T|}{\delta}\cdot\psi\left(\frac{|T|}{\delta},\frac{t}{\delta}\right),

where

is a constant that depends only on

.

Proof.

For each point

Let

Then for any pair of points

\displaystyle n

where

O\left(\left(n^{c_{1}}t^{c}g(n)\right)^{\frac{1}{1+c_{1}+c}}\right).

In particular, if
c_{1}=0
, then the crossing number
is at most

O\left(\left(t^{c}g(n)\right)^{\frac{1}{1+c}}\right).

Proof.

We shall construct the matching by picking pairs of points sequentially, in a greedy manner. Each time, we shall
choose a pair of points that crosses the least number of ranges. However, instead of looking at the number of ranges crossed
by a given pair, we shall do a weighted counting, in which we shall assign a weight to each range and look to choose the pair
that minimizes the
weight
of the ranges that it crosses. The weight of a range
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
will be a function of the number of pairs
already selected, which cross
S
. This will force the selection of edges which do not cross the ranges that have already been
crossed several times, thus our strategy will tend to favour low crossing number with respect to the already selected edges.

Initially, we assign each range a weight of
1
. After the
i
-th pair has been chosen, for a given range
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
let
c_{S}(i)
denote the number
of already selected pairs, which cross
i
. Then the weight of
S
is given by
w_{i}(S)=2^{c_{S}(i)}
. The weight of any subset of ranges
{\cal S}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
is the sum
of the weights of the ranges in
{\cal S}
.
At each step, we greedily pick a pair that crosses the least number of weighted ranges of
{\mathcal{R}}
, where the weights have been updated at the end
of the previous step. At the end of the
i
-th step, let
T_{i}
denote the multiset obtained
{\mathcal{R}}
by replacing each range
S\in{\mathcal{R}}

with
w_{i}(S)
copies of
S
. Note that
w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})=|T_{i}|
, where the cardinality of
T_{i}
refers to the weighted cardinality.

By the Short Edge Lemma
11
, we have

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{|T_{i}|^{1+c_{1}}}{\delta^{1+c_{1}+c}}g(|T_{i}|/\delta)t^{c}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})^{1+c_{1}}}{\delta^{1+c_{1}+c}}g(w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}))t^{c}.

Therefore, there exists a pair of points in

X\setminus\{x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\ldots,x_{i},y_{i}\},

which cross at most
\delta
ranges,
where
\delta
is the maximum value satisfying

\delta\leq\left(\frac{|T_{i}|^{1+c_{1}}}{n}g(|T|)t^{c}\right)^{1/(1+c_{1}+c)}.

We choose this pair
\{x_{i+1},y_{i+1}\}
to be the
(i+1)
-st edge of the matching.
Let
{\mathcal{R}}_{i+1}
denote the ranges of
{\mathcal{R}}
that are crossed by the pair
\{x_{i+1},y_{i+1}\}
.
The weight of the system
{\mathcal{R}}
after the
(i+1)
-st step, will therefore be

\displaystyle w_{i+1}({\mathcal{R}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})-w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}_{i+1})+w_{i+1}({\mathcal{R}}_{i+1}).

Now since the crossing number of the ranges in
{\mathcal{R}}
increased by
1
after choosing
\{x,y\}
, therefore by the
definition of the weight function, their weights double after the
(i+1)
-st step, that is,

w_{i+1}({\mathcal{R}}_{i+1})=2w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}_{i+1}).

Thus we get

\displaystyle w_{i+1}({\mathcal{R}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})-w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}_{i+1})+2w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}_{i+1})

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})\left(1+\frac{w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}_{i+1})}{w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})}\right).

Noting that
|{\mathcal{R}}_{i+1}|\leq\delta
by our choice of the pair
\{x_{i+1},y_{i+1}\}
, we get

\displaystyle w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}_{i+1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\cdot\left(w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})^{1+c_{1}}t^{c}\cdot\left(\frac{g(w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}))}{n-2i}\right)\right)^{1/(1+c_{1}+c)}.

Now substituting in the recurrence relation (
2
) for the weight of
{\mathcal{R}}
, gives

\displaystyle w_{i+1}({\mathcal{R}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})\left(1+\frac{w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}_{i+1})}{w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})\left(1+\left(\frac{t^{c}}{(n-2i)}\cdot\frac{g(w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}))}{w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})^{c}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+c_{1}+c}}\right).

Since by definition
g(n)=o(n^{{\varepsilon}})
for every
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1]
and we assumed
c
is constant, therefore the ratio
\frac{g(w_{i}({\mathcal{R}}))}{w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})^{c}}
is
maximized when
w_{i}({\mathcal{R}})
is the minimum, i.e.
w_{0}({\mathcal{R}})
, or
|{\mathcal{R}}|
. Further, by our assumption that
|{\mathcal{R}}|\geq n
, the ratio becomes at most

\frac{g(n)}{n^{c}}
. Thus we get

\displaystyle w_{n/2}({\mathcal{R}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle w_{0}({\mathcal{R}})\prod_{i=1}^{n/2-1}\left(1+\left(\frac{t^{c}g(n))}{(n-2i)n^{c}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+c_{1}+c}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{R}}|\prod_{i=1}^{n/2-1}\left(1+\left(\frac{t^{c}g(n)}{(n-2i)n^{c}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+c_{1}+c}}\right).

Taking logarithms with respect to both sides, we get

\displaystyle\log w_{n/2}({\mathcal{R}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\log|{\mathcal{R}}|+O\left(\left(\left(\frac{t}{n}\right)^{c}g(n)\right)^{\frac{1}{1+c_{1}+c}}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2-1}\frac{1}{(n-2i)^{1/(1+c_{1}+c)}}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\log|{\mathcal{R}}|+O\left(\left(t^{c}g(n)\right)^{\frac{1}{1+c_{1}+c}}n^{c_{1}/(1+c_{1}+c)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log|{\mathcal{R}}|+O\left(\left(n^{c_{1}}t^{c}g(n)\right)^{\frac{1}{1+c_{1}+c}}\right).

Here in the second inequality above, we have used the fact that

\sum_{i=1}^{n/2-1}\frac{1}{(n-2i)^{\frac{1}{1+c_{1}+c}}}\leq n^{1-\frac{1}{1+c_{1}+c}}.

Finally, let
c_{\max}:=\max_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}c_{S}(n/2)
denote the maximum number of crossings over all sets
S\in{\mathcal{R}}
.
We have

c_{\max}=\max_{S\in{\mathcal{R}}}\log w_{n/2}(S)\leq\log w_{n/2}({\mathcal{R}}).

By Equation (
3
), this is at most

\log|{\mathcal{R}}|+O\left(\left(n^{c_{1}}t^{c}g(n)\right)^{1/(1+c_{1}+c)}\right).

Since the VC dimension of the system is bounded (in fact, substituting
k=n
in the
shallow cell complexity, we see that the VC dimension is at most
2+c_{1}+c
), we get that
\log|{\mathcal{R}}|=O(\log n)
, since the number of ranges
is polynomial in the size of the universe
X
.

This completes the proof of the theorem.

4
Conclusion

We have shown improved discrepancy bounds in set systems with near-linear shallow cell complexity, under the condition that each point belongs to a bounded number
of sets. Though algorithmic considerations are not the focus of our paper, these are now briefly discussed. In order to obtain a low-discrepancy coloring, we need
to compute a matching with low crossing number. Such algorithms have been studied by several authors e.g.
[
42
,
43
,
23
,
15
]
.
For our purposes, it suffices to use the original algorithm of Welzl, which uses at most
O(n^{3}t)
time in our case. We note that the faster algorithms of Csikos and
Mustafa
[
15
]

as well as Har-Peled
[
23
]
give matchings with slightly worse bounds on the crossing number (by a logarithmic factor in
n
), which results in a corresponding increase in the
discrepancy.
