Wakimoto construction for double loop algebras and ζ \zeta -function regularisation
Tommaso Franzini

Abstract
. The Feigin-Frenkel homomorphism underpinning the Wakimoto construction realises an affine Lie algebra at critical level in terms of the β ​ γ \upbeta\upgamma -system of free fields. It was recently shown that much of the construction also goes through for double loop algebras. However, certain divergent sums appear. In this paper we show that, suggestively, these sums vanish when one performs ζ \zeta -function regularisation.



1.
Introduction

Free field realisations are a powerful tool in chiral conformal field theory; they embed intricate symmetry algebras into simpler algebras of the modes of free fields.
An important example is the free field realisation of an affine Lie algebra in terms of a Heisenberg algebra, first proposed by Wakimoto
[
Wak86
]
in the case of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
, and later generalised to any affine Lie algebra by Feigin and Frenkel in the untwisted case
[
FF90a
]
and by Szczesny in the twisted one
[
Szc02
]
. This construction can be used to define Wakimoto modules, which are of central importance in the study of integrable systems as they play a fundamental role in the description of the spectra of quantum Gaudin models of finite type
[
FFN94
]
and in the computation of correlation functions in the WZW model
[
FF90b
]
.

For any simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of finite type, this realisation can be mathematically formulated as a vertex algebra homomorphism between the vacuum Verma module at critical level over the corresponding untwisted affine algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
and the Fock space for the
\upbeta\upgamma
-system of free fields
[
FBZ04
,
Fre07
]
, as we will now recall.

1.1.
Free field realisation for finite-type Lie algebras

For any finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}_{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{+}
there is a Lie algebra homomorphism

(1)

\displaystyle\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\Der\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})

realising the Lie algebra as differential operators on the algebra of polynomial functions
\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
on the unipotent group
U=\exp(\n_+)\simeq\mathfrak{n}_{+}
. Choosing coordinates
X^{\alpha}
labelled by the positive roots,
\alpha\in\Delta_{+}
, then
\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})=\mathbb{C}[X^{\alpha}]_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}
.
For example, for
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
one has the well-known realisation

(2)

\displaystyle E\mapsto D\qquad H\mapsto-2XD\qquad F\mapsto-X^{2}D,

where
E,F
and
H
are the Chevalley-Serre generators and
X
,
D:=\partial_{X}
are the generator of the Weyl algebra with commutation relations
[D,X]=1
.

The main fact underpinning the Wakimoto construction is that this homomorphism can be promoted to a homomorphism of vertex algebras, as follows.
First, consider the affine algebra

(3)

\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}\simeq_{\mathbb{C}}\mathfrak{g}[t,t^{-1}]\oplus\mathbb{C}\mathsf{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}\mathsf{d},

where
\mathsf{k}
is the central element and
\mathsf{d}
is the derivation in the homogeneous gradation,
i.e.
\mathsf{d}=t\partial_{t}
.
It has
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{+}:=\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathbb{C}[t]\oplus\mathbb{C}\mathsf{k}
as a subalgebra. We define
\mathbb{C}\ket{0}^{k}
the one-dimensional representation of
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{+}
in which
\mathfrak{g}[t]
acts trivially and the central element acts as multiplication by
k\in\mathbb{C}
, called the
level
of the representation. The vacuum Verma module at level
k
is the induced module

(4)

\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},k}=U(\widehat{\mathfrak{g}})\otimes_{U(\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{+})}\mathbb{C}\ket{0}^{k}

This space has the structure of a vertex algebra (cfr.
[
FBZ04
, Theorem 2.4.5]
).

On the other hand, one can define the Fock module for the
\upbeta\upgamma
-system on
\mathfrak{n}_{+}
,
\mathsf{M}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
(see
section

3.1
for the details on its construction).
Denoting with
\Omega_{\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})}
the space of 1-forms, one has
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}\simeq\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},k}[1]
,
\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})\simeq\mathsf{M}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})[0]
and
\Der\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})\oplus\Omega_{\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})}\simeq\mathsf{M}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})[1]
. It is possible to construct the following map of vector spaces

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},k}[\leq 1]\to\mathsf{M}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})[\leq 1],

identifying the corresponding vacuum vectors and mapping

(6)

\displaystyle A[-1]\ket{0}^{k}\mapsto\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}P_{A}^{\alpha}(\upgamma[0])\upbeta_{\alpha}[-1]\ket{0}.

Crucially, this map is not a map of vertex algebras, as it does not preserve non-negative products.
This problem can be solved by adding extra terms, as shown in
[
FF90a
,
Fre07
]
. Formally, there exists a linear map

(7)

\displaystyle\phi:\mathfrak{g}\to\Omega_{\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})},

such that

(8)

\displaystyle\rho+\phi:\mathfrak{g}\to\Der\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})\oplus\Omega_{\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})}

can be lifted to a map of vertex algebras,

(9)

\displaystyle\theta:\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},-h^{\vee}}\to\mathsf{M}(\mathfrak{n}_{+}),

where, crucially, the level has to be set to the critical level
-h^{\vee}
,
h^{\vee}
being the dual Coxeter number of
\mathfrak{g}
.

In the case of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
,
eq.

2
is lifted to the vertex algebra map

(10)

\begin{gathered}E[-1]\ket{0}\mapsto\upbeta[-1]\ket{0}\qquad H[-1]\ket{0}\mapsto-2\upgamma[0]\upbeta[-1]\ket{0}\\
F[-1]\ket{0}\mapsto-\upgamma[0]\upgamma[0]\upbeta[-1]\ket{0}-2\upgamma[-1]\ket{0}.\end{gathered}

1.2.
Generalisation to the affine case

It is natural to ask whether this construction generalises to the case where
\mathfrak{g}
itself is an untwisted affine algebra.
Perhaps surprisingly, as shown in
[
You21
]
, much of it does, as follows.

The algebra
\mathfrak{g}
still admits a triangular decomposition, where the various subalgebras are now infinite-dimensional and in general not nilpotent, namely
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathfrak{n}_{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{+}
. We denote by
\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}\cong\mathring{\mathfrak{n}}_{-}\oplus\mathring{\mathfrak{h}}\oplus\mathring{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
the corresponding underlying finite-type Lie algebra, with
\mathring{\Delta}
its space of roots.
A basis of
\mathfrak{g}
is given by

(11)

\displaystyle\{\mathsf{k},\mathsf{d}\}\cup\{J_{a,n}\}_{a\in\mathcal{I};n\in\mathbb{Z}},

where
J_{a,n}:=J_{a}\otimes t^{n}
and
\mathcal{I}:=(\mathring{\Delta}\setminus\{0\})\cup\{1,\dots,\rank\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}\}
. We define the index set
\mathsf{A}:=\{(a,0)\}_{a\in\mathring{\Delta}_{+}}\cup(\mathcal{I}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1})
, so that a basis for
\mathfrak{n}_{+}
is given by
\{J_{a,n}\}_{(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}}
.

Following
[
Kum02
]
, one can define the pro-nilpotent algebra
\widetilde{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}=\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
.
More explicitly, this space can be defined as the inverse limit of a system of nilpotent Lie algebras
\mathfrak{n}_{+}/\mathfrak{n}_{\geq k}
, where

(12)

\mathfrak{n}_{\geq k}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\Delta_{+}\\
\text{ht}(\alpha)\geq k\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}

is the ideal containing elements with degree higher than
k
. Here
ht
denotes the grade of a root in the homogeneous gradation,
i.e.
\text{ht}(\delta n+\alpha)=n
, for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\delta\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is the imaginary root, defined as the sum of all roots multiplied by their corresponding Dynkin label.
Therefore, elements of this completion are possibly infinite sums of the form
\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}x^{\alpha}
, with
x^{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
, provided they truncate to finite sum modulo
\mathfrak{n}_{\geq k}
for any
k
.

Via the exponential map, one can similarly define the pro-group
U
as the inverse limit of the groups
\exp(\n_+/\n_{\geq k})
, where the multiplicative structure is given by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula and whose elements are infinite products of the form
\prod_{(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}}\exp(x^{a,n}J_{a,n})
with
x^{a,n}\in\mathbb{C}
, provided they truncate to finite ones modulo terms
\exp(\n_+/\n_{\geq k})
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

One introduces a set of coordinates
X^{a,n}:U\to\mathbb{C}
on
U
, such that for
g=\prod_{(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}}\exp(x^{a,n}J_{a,n})
,
X^{a,n}(g)=x^{a,n}
, which define the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra of polynomial functions on
U
,

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})=\mathbb{C}[X^{a,n}]_{(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}}.

In this setting, the Weyl algebra
\mathcal{H}
is the free unital
\mathbb{C}
-algebra generated by
X^{a,n}
and
D_{a,n}
, quotiented by the relations

(14)

\displaystyle[X^{a,n},X^{b,m}]=0=[D_{a,n},D_{b,m}],\qquad[D_{a,m},X^{b,n}]=\delta^{b}_{a}\delta^{n}_{m}.

The space of derivations on
\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
,
\Der\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
, is a subalgebra of the Weyl algebra
\mathcal{H}
with elements of the form
\sum_{(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}}P^{a,n}(X)D_{a,n}
, where
P^{a,n}(X)\in\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
, where only a finite number of terms is non-zero; the respective completions
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
and
\widetilde{\Der}\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
, are those algebras where this last restriction is lifted.

There is a continuous homomorphism of Lie algebras

\displaystyle\varrho:\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\widetilde{\Der}\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})

(15)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}}P^{a,n}_{A}(X)D_{a,n}

The case of
\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
has been explicitly worked out in
[
You21
]
; as a novel example, consider the Cartan-Weyl basis for
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
given by
\{E_{\pm\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathring{\Delta}_{+}}
where
\mathring{\Delta}_{+}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\}
, together with the Cartan generators
\{H_{i}\}_{i=1,2}
. An explicit matrix representation is given in terms of
3\times 3
matrices, by the identification
E_{\alpha_{1}}\mapsto\mathsf{e}_{12}
,
E_{\alpha_{2}}\mapsto\mathsf{e}_{23}
,
E_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\mapsto\mathsf{e}_{13}
,
E_{-\alpha_{1}}\mapsto\mathsf{e}_{21}
,
E_{-\alpha_{2}}\mapsto\mathsf{e}_{32}
,
E_{-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}\mapsto\mathsf{e}_{31}
and
H_{1}\mapsto\mathsf{e}_{11}-\mathsf{e}_{22}
,
H_{2}\mapsto\mathsf{e}_{22}-\mathsf{e}_{33}
, where
\mathsf{e}_{ij}
is the matrix with a 1 in position
(i,j)
and zero elsewhere.

One finds for example

\displaystyle\varrho(J_{\alpha_{1},1})=

\displaystyle D_{{\alpha_{1}},1}-\sum_{k\geq 2}X^{{\alpha_{2}},k-1}D_{{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}},k}-\sum_{k\geq 3}X^{{-\alpha_{1}},k-1}D_{1,k}

(16)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\geq 3}X^{{-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}},k-1}D_{{\alpha_{2}},k}+2\sum_{k\geq 3}X^{{1},k-1}D_{{\alpha_{1}},k}-\sum_{k\geq 3}X^{{2},k-1}D_{{\alpha_{1}}\,k}

\displaystyle+(-X^{{-\alpha_{2}},2}X^{{\alpha_{2}},1}+\dots)D_{{\alpha_{1}},4}+(-X^{{-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}},2}X^{{\alpha_{2}},1}+\dots)D_{1,4}

\displaystyle+(-X^{{-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}},2}X^{{\alpha_{2}},1}+\dots)D_{2,4}+(X^{1,2}X^{{\alpha_{2}},1}+\dots)D_{{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}},4}+\dots

In the appendix we present more examples of this realisation.

1.3.
The widening gap subalgebra

Let us now briefly comment on the general structure of the terms in
16
. For each element
J_{a,n}\in\mathfrak{g}
one finds that

(17)

\displaystyle\varrho(J_{a,n})=f^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1029pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.50208pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{c}}}_{{{a}{b}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.57375pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.57375pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1205pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.51463pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}\sum_{k\geq N}X^{b,k-n}D_{c,k}+\sum_{(b,m)\in\mathsf{A}}R_{J_{a,n}}^{b,m}(X)D_{b,m}.

for some
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
depending on
a,b,c
and
n
. Here, the first term is a quadratic infinite sum in the generators and will be of central importance in the sections below. Indeed, this kind of sums will be the main source of problems when trying to lift the homomorphism of Lie algebras to one of vertex algebras. The second term is part of a subalgebra of
\widetilde{\Der}\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
, of derivations of
\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
with
widening gap
,
\bDer\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
. We will give a precise definition in
section

3.4
, but roughly speaking these are those possibly infinite sums where the loop degree of each
X
factor in the polynomial
R
grows “slower” than the loop degree of the corresponding
D
, creating a gap between them that eventually widens.
For example in the case of
\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}}_{3}
, the polynomial part of
R_{J_{{\alpha_{1}},0}}^{{\alpha_{1}},7}(X)D_{{\alpha_{1}},7}
is

\displaystyle R_{J_{{\alpha_{1}},0}}^{{\alpha_{1}},7}(X)=

\displaystyle X^{{-\alpha_{2}},2}X^{{-\alpha_{1}},2}X^{{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}},3}+4X^{{1},3}(X^{{1},2})^{2}

\displaystyle-4X^{{2},2}X^{{1},3}X^{{1},2}+(X^{{2},2})^{2}X^{{1},3}

\displaystyle-4(X^{{1},1})^{2}X^{{-\alpha_{1}},2}X^{{\alpha_{1}},3}-(2X^{{1},1})^{2}X^{{-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}},2}X^{{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}},3}

(18)

\displaystyle-8X^{{1},3}X^{{1},2}(X^{{1},1})^{2}+\dots

and one sees that each of these terms is a product of monomials with loop degree strictly less than
\lfloor 7/2\rfloor
; this is part of a pattern, and the gap,

grows unboundedly with
n
. We define the map

\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}
.

,

is part of the coordinate system on

, while if

1.4.
Vertex algebras and splitting map

Following the finite-type construction, the next natural step would be to consider the corresponding vertex algebras and repeat the same construction as above.
As before, one can define the vacuum Verma module over the central extension by
K
of the loop algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathbb{C}[s,s^{-1}]
, which naturally carries the structure of a vertex algebra. We denote it by
\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},K}
, where
\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},K}[1]\simeq\mathfrak{g}
.

Similarly to the finite-type construction, one also defines the Fock module for the
\upbeta\upgamma
-system on
\mathfrak{n}_{+}
,
\mathsf{M}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
, which has the structure of a vertex algebra.
Having in mind the idea of embedding expressions like the one in
16
, we need to enlarge this space by allowing infinite sums, hence we have to work in a certain completion of this space,
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
.

As before, from (
15
) one can introduce a map of vector spaces

(21)

\displaystyle\vartheta:\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},K}\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}(\mathfrak{n}_{+}),

from the vacuum Verma module over the double loop algebra of
\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}
at level
K
.
As in the finite-type case it turns out that non-negative products are not preserved.
Nevertheless, the remarkable result from
[
You21
]
, is that also in the affine setting there exists an analogue of the splitting map (
7
), namely

(22)

\varphi:\mathfrak{g}\to\Omega_{\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})},

where
\Omega_{\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})}
is the space of 1-forms. It maps

(23)

\displaystyle J_{a,n}\mapsto\sum_{(b,m)\in\mathsf{A}}Q_{J_{a,n};b,m}(X)dX^{b,m}.

It has the property that the map

(24)

\displaystyle\varrho+\varphi:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\widetilde{\Der}\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})\oplus\Omega_{\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})},

can be lifted to a linear map

(25)

\displaystyle\vartheta:\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},0}\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}(\mathfrak{n}_{+}),

from the vacuum Verma module over the
loop
algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
,
i.e.
at zero level, so that the zeroth products of generators are preserved (cfr.
[
You21
, Theorem 33]
):

(26)

\displaystyle\vartheta(J_{a,n}[-1]\ket{0})_{(0)}\vartheta(J_{b,m}[-1]\ket{0})=\vartheta(J_{a,n}[-1]\ket{0}_{(0)}J_{b,m}[-1]\ket{0}).

Unfortunately, the space
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}(\mathfrak{n}_{+})
is not a vertex algebra. For example, the would-be first products may result in ill-defined divergent sums. Consider the state
a=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{I},k\geq 0}\upgamma^{a,k}[0]\upbeta_{a,k}[-1]\ket{0}
; the first product of this element with itself would be

\displaystyle a_{(1)}a

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{I},k\geq 0}\upgamma^{a,k}[0]\upbeta_{a,k}[-1]\ket{0}_{(1)}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{I},j\geq 0}\upgamma^{b,j}[0]\upbeta_{b,j}[-1]\ket{0}

1.5.
Main result

On encountering such divergent sums, it is a standard trick in physics to attempt to cure them via
\zeta
-function regularisation. The main result of this paper is to prove the following observation first made in
[
You21
]
. Let
\reg
denote the
\zeta
-function regularisation procedure, which we define in a moment. Then, we have the following

Theorem
.

For all
J_{a,n}[-1]\ket{0},J_{b,m}[-1]\ket{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},0}
one has

(28)

\textup{{reg}}[\vartheta(J_{a,n}[-1]\ket{0})_{(1)}\vartheta(J_{b,m}[-1]\ket{0})]=0.

In other words, after zeta-function regularisation, the divergent first products are not merely finite but, strikingly, are actually
all
zero on the nose.
One should caveat that this statement does not immediately lead to a homomorphism of vertex algebras, essentially because the map
\reg
does not respect associativity. However, the seemingly rather intricate cancellations involved are certainly highly suggestive.

In more detail, the
\zeta
-function regularisation procedure we employ works as follows.
We define a new vertex Lie algebra over the ring of rational function
\mathbb{C}(z)
, whose products depend on a formal parameter
z
.
This parameter
z
plays the role of a
regulator
: this will enable us to identify the ill-defined quantities and ultimately to “renormalise” them. The map

(29)

\displaystyle\reg:\mathbb{C}(z)\to\mathbb{C},

is defined through a series of steps: one first maps
z\mapsto e^{y}
and then expands for small
y
. Then, after some manipulations of the resulting series, one extracts the constant term.
To see how it works, consider the following example

(30)

\begin{split}&\frac{z^{2}}{1-z^{2}}\leadsto\frac{e^{2y}}{1-e^{2y}}\\
&\qquad\leadsto\frac{(1+2y+2y^{2}+\dots)}{1-1-2y-2y^{2}-\dots}=-\frac{1}{2y}\frac{(1+2y+\dots)}{(1+y+\dots)}\\
&\qquad\qquad\leadsto-\frac{1}{2y}(1+2y+\dots)(1-y+\dots)=-\frac{1}{2y}-\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{O}(y)\leadsto-\frac{1}{2}\end{split}

This is equivalent to removing the singular term and taking the standard limit
y\to 0
(
z\to 1
).

In

In

Finally, in

2.
Vertex (Lie) algebras

2.1.
Definition and properties

Definition 2.1
.

Given a graded vector space
\mathscr{V}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{V}^{(k)}
over a field
\mathbb{K}
, a vector
\ket{0}\in\mathscr{V}
called the
vacuum
, a map
T\in\End(\mathscr{V})
called
translation
, and a map called the
state-field correspondence

Y:\mathscr{V}\to\End\mathscr{V}[[x,x^{-1}]]
,
a\mapsto Y(a,x):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{(k)}x^{-k-1}
, with
a_{(k)}\in\End\mathscr{V}
of degree
\deg(a)-k-1
,
i.e.
a_{(k)}\mathscr{V}^{(n)}\subset\mathscr{V}^{(n+\deg(a)-k-1)}
, a
vertex algebra
is the quadruple
(\mathscr{V},\ket{0},T,Y(\bullet,x))
satisfying the following axioms

i)

Vacuum axiom:
Y(\ket{0},x)=\id_{\mathscr{V}}
;

ii)

Creation axiom: for all
a\in\mathscr{V}
,
Y(a,x)\ket{0}\in\mathscr{V}[[x]]
and
Y(a,x)\ket{0}\Big|_{x=0}=a
;

iii)

Translation axiom:
T\ket{0}=0
and for all
a\in\mathscr{V}
,
[T,Y(a,x)]=\partial_{x}Y(a,x)
;

iv)

Borcherds’ identities: for all
a,b\in\mathscr{V}
,

\displaystyle\res_{x-y}\iota_{y,x-y}

\displaystyle f(x,y)Y(Y(a,x-y)b,y)=

(31)

\displaystyle\res_{x}\iota_{x,y}f(x,y)Y(a,x)Y(b,y)-\res_{x}\iota_{y,x}f(x,y)Y(b,y)Y(a,x),

where
f(x,y)
is a rational function with poles at most at
x=0
,
y=0
or
x-y=0
and
\iota_{x,y}
denote the formal power series expansion in the domain
\absolutevalue{x}>\absolutevalue{y}
.

Given a state
a\in\mathscr{V}
, the field
Y(a,x)
can be interpreted as the generating function of an infinite number of products, called the
n
-th products
,
\mathscr{V}\times\mathscr{V}\to\mathscr{V}
,
(a,b)\mapsto a_{(n)}b
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, such that
a_{(n)}b=0
for sufficiently large
n
, where
a_{(n)}\in\End\mathscr{V}
is called the
n
-th mode of
a
.
It is possible to rephrase the axioms in terms of modes as follows

i’)

Vacuum axiom: for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\ket{0}_{(n)}a=\delta_{n,-1}a
;

ii’)

Creation axiom: for all
a\in\mathscr{V}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a_{(n)}\ket{0}=0
and
a_{(-1)}\ket{0}=a
;

iii’)

Translation axiom: for all
a\in\mathscr{V}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
[T,a_{(n)}]=-na_{(n-1)}
;

iv’)

Borcherds’ identities: for all
a,b\in\mathscr{V}
,
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{m}{j}(a_{(n+j)}b)_{(m+k-j)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{n}{j}

\displaystyle\Big((-1)^{j}a_{(m+n-j)}(b_{(k+j)})

(32)

\displaystyle\qquad-(-1)^{j+n}b_{(n+k-j)}(a_{(m+j)})\Big).

where for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\binom{m}{k}=\frac{1}{k!}m(m-1)\dots(m-k+1)
for all
k>0
and
\binom{m}{0}=1
.

Setting
n=0
in
32
, we find the so-called
commutator formula
for the modes,

(33)

\displaystyle[a_{(m)},b_{(k)}]=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(a_{(j)}b)_{(m+k-j)}.

Expressing this in terms of state-field map, one finds the
locality formula
(see
[
Kac01
, Theorem 2.3]
),

(34)

\displaystyle(x-y)^{N}[Y(a,x),Y(b,y)]=0,\qquad\text{for $N$ big enough}.

Similarly, setting
m=0
one obtains the
associativity formula
, which can be seen as a recursive formula for the composition of modes,

(35)

\displaystyle(a_{(n)}b)_{(k)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{n}{j}

\displaystyle\Big((-1)^{j}a_{(n-j)}(b_{(k+j)})-(-1)^{j+n}b_{(n+k-j)}(a_{(j)})\Big).

In particular, setting
n=-1
one gets

(36)

Y(a_{(-1)}b,y)=Y(a,y)_{+}Y(b,y)+Y(b,y)Y(a,y)_{-},

where

(37)

\displaystyle Y(a,x)_{+}:=\sum_{k<0}a_{(k)}x^{-k-1},\qquad Y(a,x)_{-}:=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{(k)}x^{-k-1}.

This can be generalised to define the so-called
normal ordering of fields
,

(38)

\nodot Y(a,x)Y(b,y)\nodot:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{m<0}a_{{m}}b_{{n}}x^{-m-1}+\sum_{m\geq 0}b_{(n)}a_{(m)}x^{-m-1}\right)y^{-n-1}.

where the effect of the ordering is to move positive modes to the right, which in a field theory context can be interpreted as “annihilation operators act first”.
This operation is right-associative,
i.e.
\nodot Y(a,x)Y(b,y)Y(c,z)\nodot=\nodot Y(a,x)\nodot Y(b,y)Y(b,z)\nodot\nodot
.

From these axioms, it is possible to show that
Y(a,x)b=e^{xT}Y(b,-x)a
for all
a,b\in\mathscr{V}
. In the language of modes, this gives rise to the so-called
skew-symmetry formula
for the products,

(39)

a_{(n)}b=-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+j}}{k!}T^{j}(b_{(n+j)}a).

For example, it turns out that the zeroth product is skew-symmetric modulo translations, while the first product is symmetric modulo translations.

We now proceed to define vertex Lie algebras.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a graded vector space
\mathscr{L}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{L}^{(k)}
over a field
\mathbb{K}
, a vector
\ket{0}\in\mathscr{L}
called the
vacuum
, a map
T\in\End(\mathscr{L})
called
translation
, and a map
Y_{-}:\mathscr{L}\to\End(\mathscr{L})t^{-1}\mathbb{C}[[t^{-1}]]
,
a\mapsto Y(a,x):=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{(k)}x^{-k-1}
, with
a_{(k)}\in\End(\mathscr{L})
of degree
\deg(a)-k-1
,
i.e.
a_{(k)}\mathscr{L}^{(n)}\subset\mathscr{L}^{(n+\deg(a)-k-1)}
, a
vertex Lie algebra
is the quadruple
(\mathscr{L},\ket{0},T,Y_{-}(\bullet,x))
satisfying the following axioms

i)

a_{(n)}\ket{0}=0
for
n
large enough;

ii)

Translation axiom:
(Ta)_{(n)}b=-na_{(n-1)}b
;

iii)

Skew-symmetry axioms:
a_{(n)}b=-\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{n+k}}{k!}T^{k}(b_{(n+k)}a)
;

iv)

Borcherds’ identities: for all
a,b\in\mathscr{L}
,

(40)

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{m}{j}(a_{(j)}b)_{(m+n-j)}c=a_{(m)}b_{(n)}c-b_{(n)}a_{(m)}c

It is clear that the polar part of a vertex algebra, obtained by forgetting about all terms with positive powers
x^{n}
,
n\geq 0
, in the state-field map
Y(\bullet,x)
, gives rise to a vertex Lie algebra.

To any vertex Lie algebra, one can associate a Lie algebra which is the Lie algebra of its modes, namely

(41)

\displaystyle\Lie(\mathscr{L})=\mathscr{L}\otimes\mathbb{C}((t))/\imaginary(T\otimes 1+\id\otimes\partial_{t}).

with commutation relations given by

(42)

\displaystyle[A_{[m]},B_{[n]}]=\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{m}{k}(A_{(n)}B)_{[m+n-k]},

where by
A_{[n]}
we identify the image of
A\in\mathscr{L}
in
\Lie(\mathscr{L})
.
It is also possible to define the left-adjoint functor to the one sending a vertex algebra to its polar part (for more detail cfr.
[
FBZ04
]
). The resulting space is the
universal enveloping vertex algebra

(43)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(\mathscr{L}):=U(\Lie(\mathscr{L}))\otimes_{U(\Lie(\mathscr{L}))_{+}}\mathbb{C},

which has the structure of a vertex algebra.

3.
The vertex Lie algebra
\widehat{\mathsf{M}}_{z}[\leq 1]

3.1.
Heisenberg algebra and Fock module

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an affine Kac-Moody algebra with triangular decomposition
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{n}_{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{+}
, whose underlying finite algebra is denoted by
\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Let
\mathsf{A}=\{(a,0)\}_{a\in\mathring{\Delta}_{+}}\cup(\mathcal{I}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1})
be an index set indexing a basis of
\mathfrak{n}_{+}
, where
\mathring{\Delta}_{+}
is the set of positive roots of
\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\mathcal{I}=(\mathring{\Delta}\setminus\{0\})\cup\{1,\dots,\rank\mathring{\mathfrak{g}}\}
.

Let
z
be a formal parameter. We denote by
\mathbb{C}[z]
, the polynomial ring in
z
with complex coefficients. Given
M,N\in\mathbb{Z}
and
(a,m)\in\mathsf{A}
, consider the free unital associative algebra
\mathsf{H}_{z}
generated by
\upbeta_{a,m}[M]
,
\upgamma^{a,m}[N]
and
\bsf{1}
quotiented by the ideal generated by the commutation relations

(44)

\begin{gathered},\upbeta_{b,n}[N]]=0,\qquad\qquad[\upgamma^{a,m}[M],\upgamma^{b,n}[N]]=0,\\
[\upbeta_{a,m}[M],\upgamma^{b,n}[N]]=z^{m}\delta_{N+M,0}\delta^{b}_{a}\delta^{n}_{m}\bsf{1}.\end{gathered}

This algebra can be seen as a “deformation” of the
Heisenberg algebra

\mathsf{H}
, that can be recovered by taking the limit
z\to 1
. We introduce the parameter
z
with the role of
regulator
: its meaning will be clear in the following sections.

In this section, unless otherwise stated, we work over the ring
\mathbb{C}[[z]]
of formal power series. As we will see, in certain special cases we can work over
\mathbb{C}[z]
or
\mathbb{C}(z)
.

This algebra represents a system of free fields as it can be decomposed into
\mathsf{H}_{z}\cong\mathsf{H}_{z}^{+}\otimes\mathsf{H}_{z}^{-}
, where

(45)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{z}^{-}\simeq_{\mathbb{C}}\{\upgamma^{a,m}[N],\upbeta_{a,m}[N-1]\}_{(a,m)\in\mathsf{A};N\leq 0},

(46)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{z}^{+}\simeq_{\mathbb{C}}\{\upgamma^{a,m}[N],\upbeta_{a,m}[N-1]\}_{(a,m)\in\mathsf{A};N>0},

are called the
creation
and
annihilation
subalgebras, respectively.

Introducing a vacuum vector
\ket{0}
, we call
\mathsf{M}_{z}
the induced
\mathsf{H}_{z}
-module annihilated by
\mathsf{H}_{z}^{+}
, on which
\bsf{1}\ket{0}=\ket{0}
.

Denoting by
Q
the root lattice of
\mathfrak{g}
, there is the
\mathbb{Z}\times Q
gradation of
\mathsf{H}_{z}
and consequently of
\mathsf{M}_{z}
, in which
\upbeta_{a,n}[N]
has grade
(N,\alpha)
and
\upgamma^{a,n}[N]
has grade
(N,-\alpha)
, whenever
J_{a,n}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
and
\ket{0}
has degree
(0,0)
.
The natural depth gradation of
\mathsf{M}_{z}
is given by

(47)

\mathsf{M}_{z}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{M}_{z}[i]

and a corresponding filtration
\mathsf{M}_{z}[\leq\!K]=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{K}\mathsf{M}_{z}[i]
for
K\geq 0
.

More explicitly, the space
\mathsf{M}_{z}[0]
is spanned by elements of the form
R(\upgamma[0])\ket{0}
, while
\mathsf{M}_{z}[1]
by finite linear combinations of elements of the form

(48)

P(\upgamma[0])\upbeta_{a,m}[-1]\ket{0}+Q(\upgamma[0])\upgamma^{a,m}[-1]\ket{0}

where
P,Q,R
are polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[\upgamma^{a,n}[0]]_{(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}}
.

3.2.
Vertex algebra structure on
\mathsf{M}_{z}

3.4.
States with widening gap

We now restrict the space

, by excluding all those sums that give rise to infinite power series in

This follows by extending the idea of widening gap introduced in

to this setting.

A family of polynomials
\{P^{a,n}\}_{(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}}
in
\mathbb{C}[\upgamma^{b,m}]_{(b,m)\in\mathsf{A}}
has widening gap if for all
K\geq 1
, there exists a
\overline{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, such that for all

(59)

(60)

\sum_{(a_{i},m_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}\in\mathsf{A}}P^{a_{1},m_{1}}(\upgamma)\cdots P^{a_{n},m_{n}}(\upgamma)\upbeta_{a_{1},m_{1}}[-N_{1}]\cdots\upbeta_{a_{n},m_{n}}[-N_{n}]\ket{0}

,

and the polynomials

have widening gap.
By construction, we have that
\mathsf{M}_{z}\subset\overline{\mathsf{M}}_{z}
, since any finite sum has obviously a widening gap.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given a collection of polynomials with widening gap
\{P^{b,m}(\upgamma)\}_{(b,m)\in\mathsf{A}}
,

\upbeta_{a,n}[N]P^{b,m}(\upgamma)\ket{0}=z^{n}\frac{\partial P^{b,m}(\upgamma)}{\partial\upgamma^{a,n}[-N]}\ket{0}

is again a collection of polynomials with widening gap, with
(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}
and
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

The following statement characterises the space of states with widening gap

Lemma 3.2
.

The space
({\overline{\mathsf{M}}_{z}}[\leq\!1],\ket{0},T,Y(\bullet,z))
is a vertex Lie algebra.

The proof is essentially the same of
[

, with the addition of the regulator which is this setting does not produce any particular difference. As a matter of fact, when taking the limit

To finally prove the statement, one has to ensure that the vertex Lie algebra axioms are still satisfied after regarding infinite sums as expansions of rational functions. This is the case for vacuum, translation and skew-symmetry as they are all equality involving single products. More subtle is the case for Borcherds’ identities, since nested products appear. However, as pointed out above, resummation is only needed when computing the first products of quadratic states.

In

Then, in the section above we have introduced the space of main interest for this work, the vertex Lie algebra

4.1.
\zeta
-function regularisation

For any given expression in
\mathbb{C}(z)
, we introduce the following regularisation procedure

(75)

\reg:\mathbb{C}(z)\to\mathbb{C}

defined as follows

i)

perform the transformation
z\to e^{y}
;

ii)

power expand the resulting term for small values of
y
;

iii)

regard the result as the ratio of Laurent series, which is again a Laurent series;

iv)

remove the singular terms and perform the limit
y\to 0
,
i.e.
z\to 1
to remove the regulator. This is equivalent to extracting the constant term of the series obtained.

As an example, consider the following

(76)

\displaystyle\frac{z^{3}}{1-z^{2}}\leadsto\frac{e^{3y}}{1-e^{2y}}

(77)

\displaystyle\qquad\leadsto\frac{(1+3y+9y^{2}/2+\dots)}{1-1-2y-2y^{2}-\dots}=-\frac{1}{2y}\frac{(1+3y+\dots)}{(1+y+\dots)}

(78)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leadsto-\frac{1}{2y}(1+3y+\dots)(1-y+\dots)=-\frac{1}{2y}-1+\mathcal{O}(y)\leadsto-1

where each arrow corresponds to one of the steps above. Therefore we would write

(79)

\reg\left[\frac{z^{3}}{1-z^{2}}\right]=-1.

In particular, for any polynomial or rational function which is regular at
z=1
, this procedure is equivalent to the evaluation
z=1
. For this reason, roughly speaking, we can regard this procedure as a “renormalised” version of the limiting procedure
z\to 1
.

. They can be mapped into

, using the vertex algebra map

eq.

. Acting on both sides with the map

eq.

106

(123)

p\left(\uptheta(J_{a,n}[-1]\ket{0})_{(1)}\uptheta(J_{b,m}[-1]\ket{0})\right)=0.

Recall from the doubling construction summarised in

that the image of

is obtained by glueing together a positive and a negative copy of the Lie algebra homomorphism (

). For this reason, there are no overlapping terms,
i.e.

\displaystyle p_{0}\circ\uptheta(x)=0\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{V}_{0}^{\mathfrak{g},0}.

Moreover, for all

\displaystyle p_{+}\circ\uptheta(x)=\vartheta(x),\qquad p_{-}\circ\uptheta(x)=\tau\circ\vartheta\circ\sigma(x)

Keeping this in mind, using the result of

on

\textup{{reg}}[\vartheta(x)_{(1)}\vartheta(y)]+\textup{{reg}}[\tau\circ\vartheta\circ\sigma(x)_{(1)}\tau\circ\vartheta\circ\sigma(y)]=0.

The first term will produce either a term proportional to the vacuum, when two quadratic semi-infinite sums are contracted together or when
(\upbeta_{a,n}[-1]\ket{0})_{(1)}=\upbeta_{a,n}[1]
is contracted with a single
\upgamma^{a,n}[-1]\ket{0}
, or terms of the form

, with

of degree
>0
. Similarly, the second term, being the “negative copy" coming from the glueing procedure, will produce either terms proportional to the vacuum or terms of the form
Q(\upgamma)\ket{0}
, with
Q\in\mathbb{C}[\upgamma^{a,n}]_{(a,n)\in\mathsf{A}_{-}}
of degree
>0
.
It follows that the two contributions have to be independently zero, exception made for terms of the form
\mathbb{C}(z)\ket{0}
.

By direct calculation one finds that this product is proportional to the Killing form, and therefore it is non-trivial only in two cases:
(a,n)=(E_{\alpha},n)
,
(b,m)=(E_{-\alpha},-n)
for some
\alpha_{+}\in\mathring{\Delta}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
(a,n)=(H_{i},n)
,

, where

, is an orthogonal basis for the Cartan subalgebra.

explicitly and use the symmetry property of the

In the second case, we similarly obtain

where in the last term we have used the symmetry of the
(1)
product.

By definition, the effect of the map
\tau
is to “mirror" the elements in
{\widehat{\mathsf{M}}_{z}}[\leq\!1]
, to get a realisation of the algebra as derivations on
\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{n}_{-})
. This means that whenever we have a non-trivial contraction between

, we also have it between
\tau(a)
and
\tau(b)
, with the only difference that
z
is replaced with
z^{-1}
,
i.e.

(129)

\tau(a)_{(1)}\tau(b)=\varpi(a_{(1)}b)

where
\varpi
is the inversion map
z\mapsto z^{-1}
.

By
lemma

5.2
, we conclude that after regularisation, the two terms are equal and therefore they must be both independently zero.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper we rigorously proved an observation made in
[
You21
]
.
To do that, we first defined the space
\widehat{\mathsf{M}}_{z}
, which has the structure of a vertex Lie algebra, whose products depend on a regulation parameter
z
. It is known that the unregulated limit
z\to 1
gives rise to ill-defined quantities. For this reason, we introduced a suitable regularisation procedure, thanks to which these products were “renormalised”.
Using this, we showed that the regularised first products on the image of the map
\vartheta
are not just well-defined, but remarkably they are zero on the nose.
