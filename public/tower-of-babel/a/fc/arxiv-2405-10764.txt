Abstract
Hysteresis in the pressure-saturation relation in unsaturated porous media, which is due to surface tension on the liquid-gas interface, exhibits strong degeneracy in the resulting mass balance equation. Solutions to such degenerate equations have been recently constructed by the method of convexification even if the permeability coefficient depends on the hysteretic saturation. The model is extended here to the case that the solid matrix material is viscoelastic and that the system is coupled with a gravity driven moisture flux. The existence of a solution is proved by compact anisotropic embedding involving Orlicz spaces with respect to the time variable. Keywords: hysteresis, degenerate equation, porous media, viscoelasticity, gravity effects 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 47J40, 35K65, 74F10, 76S05



1  Introduction

We pursue here the study started in
[
12
,
13
]
of degenerate diffusion in unsaturated porous media filled with liquid and gas. Here we additionally take into account the deformations of the solid matrix produced by the penetrating humidity, and the effects of gravity on fluid diffusion as a generalization of the Richards equation (see
[
24
]
). To be precise, our main modeling assumptions are the following:

(a)

The pressure-saturation relation exhibits hysteresis;

(b)

The solid skeleton is viscoelastic;

(c)

The permeability of the material depends on the moisture content;

(d)

A gravity-driven moisture flux takes part in the process.

Our model is inspired by the experimental evidence about hysteresis in hydrogeology in, e.g.,
[
2
,
16
,
20
]
. Figure
1
is taken from
[
20
]
and shows rate-independent hysteresis between the logarithm soil suction
\psi
, which is a decreasing function of the pressure, and the volumetric water content
\theta
which, up to a linear transformation, can be identified with the saturation. The main characteristic of the hysteresis relation in Figure
1
is that at turning points where the pressure changes the orientation from decreasing to increasing or vice versa, the starting slope is horizontal, which makes the diffusion problem degenerate.

Figure 1:
Typical experimental hysteresis dependence in porous media between the logarithm soil suction
\psi
and the volumetric water content
\theta
.

Mathematical investigation of diffusion problems with hysteresis goes back to A. Visintin’s pioneering monograph
[
25
]
presenting basic methods of solving PDEs with hysteresis. However, two problems have remained unsolved until recently: the degeneracy at turning points and the dependence of the diffusion coefficient on the saturation. We have proved in
[
12
]
that the degeneracy in the case of constant permeability can be overcome using a method developed there to convexify the hysteresis relation. In
[
13
]
, the convexification combined with a compact embedding theorem for anisotropic Orlicz spaces was shown to give a sufficient argument for the solvability also in the case of saturation-dependent permeability. Let us also mention some previous results on hysteresis dependence of the permeability coefficient in the non-degenerate case under additional regularization in time or space
[
4
,
5
,
9
,
26
]
. To our knowledge, only E. El Behi-Gornostaeva addressed in her thesis
[
8
]
the full problem of hysteresis-dependent saturation without regularization in the non-degenerate case and proposed a method for the existence proof.

In this paper we extend the problem to the case of deformable porous media under gravity effects and full degeneracy of the saturation dependence. The process is driven by the mass conservation principle and the quasistatic mechanical equilibrium equation in the small deformations regime. We further simplify the system in order to make it mathematically tractable by assuming that shear stresses are negligible. The gravity term and the viscoelasticity of the deformable solid skeleton represent a new degree of complexity related to the fact that no obvious a priori upper bound for the solutions is available here. We show that such an estimate can be obtained using a hysteresis variant of the Moser iteration technique under an additional assumption on the admissible Preisach operators
G
, namely, that the Preisach density
\phi(x,r,v)
in (
3.6
) of
G
decays sufficiently slowly at infinity, see Hypothesis
3.6
. The proof of the existence theorem is then carried out by a convexity and compactness argument developed in
[
13
]
.

The structure of the present paper is the following. In Section
2
we set up the mathematical model for the phenomenon and show that it is consistent with the physical principles of mass and energy conservation. In Section
3
we recall the definitions of the main concepts including convexifiable Preisach operators, and state the main existence Theorem
3.7
. In Section
4
we propose a time discretization scheme with time step
\tau>0
and derive estimates independent of
\tau
. A uniform upper bound for the time-discrete approximation is established in Section
5
via Moser-type iterations. In Section
6
we show that, similarly to
[
13
]
, we can derive the convexity estimate (
6.26
) for the time derivative of the pressure. As shown in the last Section
7
, this is sufficient to let the time step
\tau
tend to
0
and to prove that the limit is the desired solution.

The fluid diffusion in a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
filled with a deformable unsaturated porous medium is driven by the liquid mass conservation principle

\rho\theta_{t}=-\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{q}

(2.1)

with constant mass density
\rho
, and by the mechanical equilibrium equation for the solid

\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=0\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \Omega,\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}(x)=0\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \partial\Omega,

(2.2)

where
\boldsymbol{n}(x)
denotes the unit outward normal vector to
\Omega
at a point
x\in\partial\Omega
.
We write (
2.2
) in variational form

\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\nabla_{s}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(2.3)

where
\nabla_{s}
denotes the symmetric gradient.

We assume that the deformations are small, so that
\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}
is the relative local volume change, and that shear stresses can be neglected, that is,

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\mu\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}+\gamma\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}-p)\boldsymbol{\delta},

(2.4)

where
\mu>0
is a constant bulk elasticity modulus,
\gamma>0
is a bulk viscosity modulus, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{ij})
,
i,j=1,2,3
is the Kronecker symbol. Then (
2.3
) can be written as

\int_{\Omega}(\mu\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}+\gamma\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}-p)\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\xi}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}).

(2.5)

Let
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be arbitrary, and let
W
be the solution to the problem

-\Delta W=h,\qquad W=0\ \mbox{ on }\ \partial\Omega.

(2.6)

Putting
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\nabla W
in (
2.5
), we get the mechanical equilibrium equation in the form

\mu\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}+\gamma\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}-p=0

(2.7)

a.e. in
\Omega
.

Assume now that the relative liquid mass flux
\boldsymbol{q}-\rho\boldsymbol{u}_{t}
is proportional to the liquid pressure gradient
\nabla p
, that is,

\boldsymbol{q}-\rho\boldsymbol{u}_{t}=-\kappa\nabla p,

(2.8)

with proportionality factor
\kappa=\kappa(x,\theta)>0
depending on
x\in\Omega
and
\theta
. The liquid pressure
p
can be decomposed into two components

p=p_{c}+p_{h},

(2.9)

where
p_{c}
is the capillary pressure and
p_{h}
is the hydrostatic pressure. For the hydrostatic pressure we assume the classical relation

p_{h}=\rho g_{0}\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot(x-x_{0}),

(2.10)

where
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is the unit vector in the gravity direction and
x_{0}
is a referential point.

We introduce the dimensionless normalized capillary pressure

u=\frac{p_{c}}{p_{0}}

(2.11)

and follow the modeling hypotheses of
[
2
,
24
]
which consists in representing the
p_{c}\mapsto\theta
hysteresis relation by the formula

\theta=G[u],

(2.12)

where
G
is a Preisach operator defined below in Section
3
.

On the boundary
\partial\Omega
of
\Omega
we assume that the unit outward normal
\boldsymbol{n}(x)
is defined almost everywhere and that the normal component of the relative flux
\boldsymbol{q}-\rho\boldsymbol{u}_{t}
in (
2.8
) is proportional to the difference of pressures
p
inside and
p^{*}
outside the body, that is,

-\kappa(x,\theta)\nabla p\cdot\boldsymbol{n}(x)=b^{*}(x)(p-p^{*})\ \ \mbox{ on }\ \partial\Omega.

(2.13)

The weak formulation of the mass balance Eq (
2.1
) then reads

\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho(\theta_{t}+\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}_{t})y+\kappa(x,\theta)\nabla p\cdot\nabla y\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)(p-p^{*})y\,\mathrm{d}s(x)=0

(2.14)

for every test function
y\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Assume for the moment that there is no mass exchange with the exterior corresponding to the choice
b^{*}(x)=0
in (
2.13
), that is,

-\kappa(x,\theta)\nabla p\cdot\boldsymbol{n}(x)=0

(2.15)

on
\partial\Omega
. Putting
y=1
in (
2.14
), we get

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\rho(\theta+\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u})(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(2.16)

The interpretation of (
2.16
) is mass conservation. The term
\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x
describes the evolution of the volume represented by
\Omega
, and
\rho\int_{\Omega}(\theta+\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u})(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x
is the total water mass in
\Omega
at time
t
. Naturally, if the volume increases and mass is conserved, then the saturation decreases and vice versa.

Consider now the energy balance. Under the boundary condition (
2.15
), no power is supplied to the system, and the total energy of the system should therefore decrease because all phenomena like viscosity, diffusion, and hysteresis dissipate energy. We put
y=p/\rho
in (
2.14
) and obtain using (
2.15
) that

\int_{\Omega}\big(\theta_{t}+\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\big)p\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\kappa(x,\theta)}{\rho}|\nabla p|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(2.17)

For a Preisach operator
G
in (
2.12
) there exists a Preisach potential operator
V
and a dissipation operator
D
such that

G[u]_{t}u=V[u]_{t}+|D[u]_{t}|\ \mathrm{\ a.\,e.}

(2.18)

Using (
2.7
) and (
2.9
)–(
2.11
), we can rewrite (

) in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\left(p_{0}V[u]+\rho g_{0}\theta\,\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot(x-x_{0})+\frac{\mu}{2}|\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\Omega}\left(p_{0}|D[u]_{t}|+\frac{\kappa(x,\theta)}{\rho}|\nabla p|^{2}+\gamma|\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(2.19)

The energetic interpretation of (
2.19
) is the following: The term

\int_{\Omega}\left(p_{0}|D[u]_{t}|+\frac{\kappa(x,\theta)}{\rho}|\nabla p|^{2}+\gamma|\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

(2.20)

is the dissipation rate which is positive in agreement with the principles of thermodynamics, while

\int_{\Omega}\left(p_{0}V[u]+\rho g_{0}\theta\,\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot(x-x_{0})+\frac{\mu}{2}|\mathrm{\,div\,}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

(2.21)

is the potential energy of the system which is therefore decreasing as expected.

Putting

, from (
2.7
) and (
2.9
)–(
2.14
) we get the following system, consisting of a PDE coupled with an ODE, for the unknowns

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left((G[u]+v)_{t}y+\frac{\kappa(x,G[u])p_{0}}{\rho}\left(\nabla u+\frac{\rho g_{0}}{p_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\cdot\nabla y\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{p_{0}b^{*}(x)}{\rho}(u-u^{*})y\,\mathrm{d}s(x)=0,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\gamma v_{t}+\mu v-p_{0}u=0,

(2.23)

for every
y\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, where we set
u^{*}=(p^{*}-p_{h})/p_{0}
.

The existence proof for (
2.22
)-(
2.23
) is independent of the exact values of the physical constants. We therefore normalize all constants to

for simplicity and consider the system

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big((G[u]+v)_{t}y+\kappa(x,G[u])

\displaystyle\left(\nabla u+\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\cdot\nabla y\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)(u-u^{*})y\,\mathrm{d}s(x)=0,

(2.24)

\displaystyle v_{t}+v=u,

(2.25)

for every
y\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, coupled with the initial conditions

u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad v(x,0)=v_{0}(x)\ \mathrm{\ a.\,e.}\mbox{ in }\ \Omega.

(2.26)

3  Statement of the problem

We study here Problem (
2.24
)–(
2.26
) in a bounded Lipschitzian domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and time interval
(0,T)
, where
N\in\mathbb{N}
can be arbitrary. Recall first the definition of the Preisach operator introduced in
[
21
]
in the variational setting of
[
18
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\lambda\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,\infty))
be a given function with the following properties:

\displaystyle\exists\Lambda>0:\ \lambda(x,r)=0\ \mathrm{\ for\ }r\geq\Lambda,\forall x\in\Omega,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \exists\bar{\lambda}>0:\ |\lambda(x_{1},r_{1})-\lambda(x_{2},r_{2})|&\leq\Big(\bar{\lambda}\,|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|r_{1}-r_{2}|\Big)\ \forall r_{1},r_{2}\in(0,\infty),\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\Omega.\end{aligned}

(3.2)

For a given
r>0
, we call the play operator with threshold
r
and initial memory
\lambda
the mapping which with a given function
u\in L^{1}(\Omega;W^{1,1}(0,T))
associates the solution
\xi^{r}\in L^{1}(\Omega;W^{1,1}(0,T))
of the variational inequality

|u(x,t)-\xi^{r}(x,t)|\leq r,\quad\xi^{r}_{t}(x,t)(u(x,t)-\xi^{r}(x,t)-z)\geq 0\ \mathrm{\ a.\,e.}\ \forall z\in[-r,r],

(3.3)

with initial condition

\xi^{r}(x,0)=\lambda(x,r)\ \mathrm{\ a.\,e.},

(3.4)

and we denote

\xi^{r}(x,t)=\mathfrak{p}_{r}[\lambda,u](x,t).

(3.5)

Given a measurable function
\phi:\Omega\times(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
and a constant
\bar{G}\in\mathbb{R}
, the Preisach operator
G
is defined as a mapping
G:L^{2}(\Omega;W^{1,1}(0,T))\to L^{2}(\Omega;W^{1,1}(0,T))
by the formula

G[u](x,t)=\bar{G}+\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\xi^{r}(x,t)}\phi(x,r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r.

(3.6)

The Preisach operator is said to be regular if the density function
\phi
of
G
in (
3.6
) belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})
, and there exist constants
\phi_{1},\bar{\phi}>0
and a decreasing function
\phi_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
U>0
, all
x,x_{1},x_{2}\in\Omega
, and a.e.
(r,v)\in(0,U)\times(-U,U)
we have

\displaystyle 0<\phi_{0}(U)<\phi(x,r,v)<\phi_{1},

(3.7)

\displaystyle|\phi(x_{1},r,v)-\phi(x_{2},r,v)|\leq\bar{\phi}\,|x_{1}-x_{2}|.

(3.8)

In applications, the natural physical condition
\theta=G[u]\in[0,1]
is satisfied for each input function
u
if and only if
\bar{G}\in(0,1)
and

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(x,r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r\leq 1-\bar{G},\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(x,r,-v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r\leq\bar{G},

(3.9)

for a.e.
x\in\Omega
. However, for the existence result in Theorem
3.7
only (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) are substantial.

Let us mention the following classical result (see
[
18
, Proposition II.3.11]
).

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G
be a regular Preisach operator in the sense of Definition
3.1
. Then it can be extended to a Lipschitz continuous mapping
G:L^{p}(\Omega;C[0,T])\to L^{p}(\Omega;C[0,T])
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
.

The Preisach operator is rate-independent. Hence, for input functions
u(x,t)
which are monotone in a time interval
t\in(a,b)
, a regular Preisach operator
G
can be represented by a superposition operator
G[u](x,t)=B(x,u(x,t))
with an increasing function
u\mapsto B(x,u)
called a
Preisach branch
. Indeed, the branches may be different at different points
x
and different intervals
(a,b)
. The branches corresponding to increasing inputs are said to be
ascending
(the so-called wetting curves in the context of porous media), the branches corresponding to decreasing inputs are said to be
descending
(drying curves).

Definition 3.3
.

Let
U>0
be given. A Preisach operator is said to be uniformly counterclockwise convex on
[-U,U]
if for all inputs
u
such that
|u(x,t)|\leq U
a.e., all ascending branches are uniformly convex and all descending branches are uniformly concave in the sense that there exists
\beta>0
such that for every
u\in(-U,U)
we have the implications

u_{t}>0\implies\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial u^{2}}\geq\beta,\qquad u_{t}<0\implies\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial u^{2}}\leq-\beta

in the sense of distributions, see
[
12
]
.

A regular Preisach operator
G
is called convexifiable if for every
U>0
there exist a uniformly counterclockwise convex Preisach operator
P
on
[-U,U]
, positive constants
g_{*}(U),g^{*}(U),\bar{g}(U)
, and a twice continuously differentiable mapping
g:[-U,U]\to[-U,U]
such that

g(0)=0,\quad 0<g_{*}(U)\leq g^{\prime}(u)\leq g^{*}(U),\quad|g^{\prime\prime}(u)|\leq\bar{g}(U)\ \ \forall u\in[-U,U],

(3.10)

and
G=P\circ g
.

A typical example of a uniformly counterclockwise convex operator is the so-called
Prandtl-Ishlinskii operator
characterized by positive density functions
\phi(x,r)
independent of
v
, see
[
18
, Section 4.2]
. Operators of the form
P\circ g
with a Prandtl-Ishlinskii operator
P
and an increasing function
g
are often used in control engineering because of their explicit inversion formulas, see
[
3
,
6
,
19
]
. They are called the
generalized Prandtl-Ishlinskii operators
(GPI) and represent an important subclass of Preisach operators. Note also that for every Preisach operator
P
and every Lipschitz continuous increasing function
g
, the superposition operator
G=P\circ g
is also a Preisach operator, and there exists an explicit formula for its density, see
[
17
, Proposition 2.3]
. Another class of convexifiable Preisach operators is shown in
[
12
, Proposition 1.3]
.

.

\displaystyle\ \mathrm{\ a.\,e.},

]
, we have to assume a polynomial decay of the Preisach density function at infinity that will allow us to perform a hysteresis variant of the Moser iterations in Section
5
.

Hypothesis 3.6
.

The Preisach density
\phi:\Omega\times(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
in (
3.6
) is such that

\exists m>0\ \exists\phi_{0}>0:\phi(x,r,v)\geq\phi_{0}\max\{1,r+|v|\}^{-m}\ \mathrm{\ a.\,e.}\ \mathrm{\ for\ }\ (r,v)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}.

(3.16)

Condition (
3.16
) is indeed compatible with (
3.9
) if
m>2
, as

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\max\{1,r+v\}^{-m}\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{r}^{\infty}\max\{1,z\}^{-m}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{m-2},

so that (
3.9
) is satisfied if for example
\bar{G}=1/2
and
\phi_{0}\leq 1-2/m
.

Our main existence result reads as follows.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let Hypotheses
3.4
–
3.6
hold, and let
G
be a convexifiable Preisach operator in the sense of Definition
3.3
. Then there exists a solution
(u,v)
to Problem (
2.24
)–(
2.26
) such that
u,v\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
,
\nabla u\in L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{N})
,

and
\theta_{t}=G[u]_{t}
belong to the Orlicz space
L^{\Phi_{log}}(\Omega\times(0,T))
generated by the function
\Phi_{log}(v)=v\log(1+v)
, and
v_{t}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
.

Basic properties of Orlicz spaces are summarized in
[
13
, Section 5]
. For a more comprehensive discussion, we refer the interested reader to the monographs
[

,
22
]
.

4  Time discretization

We proceed as in
[
12
]
, choose a discretization parameter
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the time step
\tau=T/n
, and replace system (
2.24
)-(
2.25
) with the following time-discrete counterpart for the unknowns
\{u_{i},v_{i}:i=1,\dots,n\}
:

\displaystyle\hskip-256.0748pt\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\Big((G[u]_{i}-G[u]_{i-1})+(v_{i}-v_{i-1})\Big)y+\kappa(x,G[u]_{i})\big(\nabla u_{i}+\boldsymbol{\nu}\big)\cdot\nabla y\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36967pt+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)(u_{i}-u^{*}_{i})y\,\mathrm{d}s(x)

\displaystyle=0,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}(v_{i}-v_{i-1})+v_{i}

\displaystyle=u_{i},

(4.2)

for every test function
y\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
, where
u^{*}_{i}(x)=u^{*}(x,i\tau)
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
are as in (
2.26
). Here, the time-discrete Preisach operator
G[u]_{i}
is defined for an input sequence
\{u_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\}
by a formula of the form (
3.6
), namely,

G[u]_{i}(x)=\bar{G}+\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\xi^{r}_{i}(x)}\phi(x,r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r,

(4.3)

where
\xi^{r}_{i}
denotes the output of the time-discrete play operator

\xi^{r}_{i}(x)=\mathfrak{p}_{r}[\lambda,u]_{i}(x)

(4.4)

defined as the solution operator of the variational inequality

|u_{i}(x)-\xi^{r}_{i}(x)|\leq r,\quad(\xi^{r}_{i}(x)-\xi^{r}_{i-1}(x))(u_{i}(x)-\xi^{r}_{i}(x)-z)\geq 0,\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{N},\ \ \forall z\in[-r,r],

(4.5)

with a given initial condition

\xi^{r}_{0}(x)=\lambda(x,r)\ \mathrm{\ a.\,e.}

(4.6)

similarly as in (
3.3
)-(
3.4
). Note that the discrete variational inequality (
4.5
) can be interpreted as weak formulation of (
3.3
) for piecewise constant inputs in terms of the Kurzweil integral, and details can be found in
[
10
, Section 2]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

System (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) with initial conditions
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
as in (
2.26
) admits at least one solution
\{u_{i},v_{i}\}\subset W^{1,2}(\Omega)
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction, and assume that the sequences
\{u_{i},v_{i}\}
have already been constructed for
i=1,\dots,j-1
. Note that there is no hysteresis in the passage from
u_{j-1}
to
u_{j}
, so that there exists a function
G_{j}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, continuous and nondecreasing in the second variable, such that
G[u]_{j}=G_{j}(x,u_{j})
. Furthermore, by (
4.2
) we have

v_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{1+\tau}v_{j-1}(x)+\frac{\tau}{1+\tau}u_{j}(x).

(4.7)

Hence, (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) can be interpreted as a quasilinear elliptic equation for the unknown
u\coloneqq u_{j}
of the form

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Bigg(\bigg(\frac{1}{\tau}G_{j}(x,u)+\frac{1}{1+\tau}u\bigg)y+\kappa(x,G_{j}(x,u))(\nabla u+\boldsymbol{\nu})\cdot\nabla y\Bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x

(4.8)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)(u-u^{*}_{j})y\,\mathrm{d}s(x)=\int_{\Omega}a_{j}y\,\mathrm{d}x

for every
y\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
with given functions
a_{j}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Let
\{e_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset W^{1,2}(\Omega)
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
. It is convenient to choose eigenfunctions of the following problem, which is compatible with boundary conditions (
2.13
), namely

\kappa_{0}\int_{\Omega}\nabla e_{k}\cdot\nabla y\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)e_{k}y\,\mathrm{d}s(x)=\mu_{k}\int_{\Omega}e_{k}y\,\mathrm{d}x

with eigenvalues
0<\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}\leq\dots
. For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
we look for coefficients
u_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,m
, such that the function

u^{(m)}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}e_{k}(x)

satisfies the identity

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Bigg(\bigg(\frac{1}{\tau}G_{j}(x,u^{(m)})+\frac{1}{1+\tau}u^{(m)}\bigg)e_{k}\,\mathrm{d}x+\kappa(x,G_{j}(x,u^{(m)}))(\nabla u^{(m)}+\boldsymbol{\nu})\cdot\nabla e_{k}\Bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x

(4.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)(u^{(m)}-u^{*}_{j})e_{k}\,\mathrm{d}s(x)-\int_{\Omega}a_{j}e_{k}\,\mathrm{d}x=0

for every
k=1,\dots,m
. The existence of a solution to (
4.9
) can be proved in a classical way using the Brouwer degree theory. An introduction to topological methods for solving nonlinear partial differential equations can be found in
[
11
, Chapter V]
, but we will mainly refer to
[
14
]
, where detailed proofs are also provided.

We begin by noting that we can define a continuous mapping
T:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
associating to
\boldsymbol{u}\coloneqq(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})
the left-hand side of (
4.9
) for
k=1,\dots,m
. Let
\gamma\in[0,1]
, and consider the homotopy

\displaystyle T_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u})_{k}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\gamma}{\tau}G_{j}(x,u^{(m)})+\frac{1}{1+\tau}u^{(m)}\bigg)e_{k}\,\mathrm{d}x

(4.10)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\Big((1-\gamma)\kappa_{0}+\gamma\kappa(x,G_{j}(x,u^{(m)}))\Big)(\nabla u^{(m)}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\nu})\cdot\nabla e_{k}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)(u^{(m)}-\gamma u^{*}_{j})e_{k}\,\mathrm{d}s(x)-\gamma\int_{\Omega}a_{j}e_{k}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Testing (
4.10
) by
u_{k}
and summing up over
k=1,\dots,m
, by Hypothesis
3.4
we obtain

\displaystyle T_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\gamma}{\tau}G_{j}(x,u^{(m)})u^{(m)}+\frac{1}{1+\tau}|u^{(m)}|^{2}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\Big((1-\gamma)\kappa_{0}+\gamma\kappa(x,G_{j}(x,u^{(m)}))\Big)\big(|\nabla u^{(m)}|^{2}+\gamma\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\nabla u^{(m)}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)\big(|u^{(m)}|^{2}-\gamma u^{*}_{j}u^{(m)}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s(x)-\gamma\int_{\Omega}a_{j}u^{(m)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{1}{1+\tau}|u^{(m)}|^{2}+\kappa_{0}|\nabla u^{(m)}|^{2}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)|u^{(m)}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(x)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{1}{\tau}|G_{j}(x,u^{(m)})|+|a_{j}|\bigg)|u^{(m)}|\,\mathrm{d}x-(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1})\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{(m)}|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)|u_{j}^{*}||u^{(m)}|\,\mathrm{d}s(x).

Hence, by Young’s inequality and again Hypothesis
3.4
, we see that
T_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}>0
outside the ball
B^{(m)}_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
with a sufficiently large radius
R
independent of
\gamma
and
m
. Therefore, the equation
T_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{u})=0
has no solution on
\partial B^{(m)}_{R}
, so that by
[
14
, Theorem 3]
the Brouwer degree
d(T_{\gamma},B^{(m)}_{R},0)
is a constant for
\gamma\in[0,1]
. The mapping
T_{0}
is linear, hence its degree is odd. In particular,
d(T_{1},B^{(m)}_{R},0)=d(T_{0},B^{(m)}_{R},0)\neq 0
, which implies (see
[
14
, Theorem 2]
) that the equation
T_{1}(\boldsymbol{u})=0
, equivalently, (
4.9
), has at least one solution in
B^{(m)}_{R}
.

Testing (
4.9
) by
u_{k}
, summing up over
k=1,\dots,m
, and employing Hypothesis
3.4
we see that the sequence
\{u^{(m)}\}
is uniformly bounded in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
, hence it is compact in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Let
u
be the limit of any convergent subsequence of
\{u^{(m)}\}
. Passing to the limit in (
4.9
) as
m\to\infty
, we check that (
4.8
) holds with
y=e_{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since the system
\{e_{k}\}
is complete in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, (
4.8
) holds for
u=u_{j}
, and recalling (
4.7
) we conclude that system (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) has a solution.
∎

5  Uniform upper bounds

The situation is different from the case without gravity studied in
[
13
]
, where the upper bound could be derived in an elementary way from Hilpert’s inequality following
[
15
]
. Here, we propose a hysteresis variant of the Moser iteration procedure which is new to our knowledge. The price we pay is that we have to restrict the class of admissible Preisach densities
\phi(x,r,v)
assuming that they have a sufficiently slow decay at infinity, see Hypothesis
3.6
.

We first test (
4.1
) by
y=u_{i}
and get, using (
4.2
),

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\Big((G[u]_{i}-G[u]_{i-1})u_{i}+(v_{i}-v_{i-1})v_{i}\Big)+\kappa(x,G[u]_{i})\big(\nabla u_{i}+\boldsymbol{\nu}\big)\cdot\nabla u_{i}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36967pt+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|v_{i}-v_{i-1}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)(u_{i}-u^{*}_{i})u_{i}\,\mathrm{d}s(x)=0

(5.1)

for all

. Let us define the functions

\psi(x,r,\xi)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\xi}\phi(x,r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v,\quad\Psi(x,r,\xi)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\xi}v\phi(x,r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v.

(5.2)

In terms of the sequence
\xi^{r}_{i}(x)=\mathfrak{p}_{r}[\lambda,u]_{i}
we have

(5.3)

Choosing in (

)

and using the fact that the function

(5.4)

and (

) yields, by Hypothesis
3.4
and the inequality
(v_{i}-v_{i-1})v_{i}\geq(v_{i}^{2}-v_{i-1}^{2})/2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Psi(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i})-\Psi(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i-1})\big)\,\mathrm{d}r+\frac{v_{i}^{2}-v_{i-1}^{2}}{2}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x

(5.5)

\displaystyle+\kappa_{0}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)|u_{i}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(x)\leq C

, with a constant

independent of
\tau
. Summing up over

and exploiting the fact that, by the definition of

in (

) and the assumptions on
\phi

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi(x,r,\xi^{r}_{0}(x))\,\mathrm{d}r=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\int_{0}^{\lambda(x,r)}v\phi(x,r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r\leq\frac{\phi_{1}}{2}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\lambda^{2}(x,r)\,\mathrm{d}r\leq C,

and we are assuming

\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\int_{\Omega}|v_{i}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\tau\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)|u_{i}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(x)\right)\leq C

C>0

For parameters
R>1
and

to be specified later, we define the function

U_{k,R}(u)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u|u|^{2k}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ |u|\leq R,\\[5.69054pt]
(2k+1)R^{2k}u-2kR^{2k+1}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ u>R,\\[5.69054pt]
(2k+1)R^{2k}u+2kR^{2k+1}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ u<-R.\end{array}\right.

The function
U_{k,R}
is odd, increasing, continuously differentiable, and its derivative has the form

U_{k,R}^{\prime}(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(2k+1)|u|^{2k}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ |u|\leq R,\\[5.69054pt]
(2k+1)R^{2k}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ |u|>R.\end{array}\right.

(5.9)

We test (

) with

, which is an admissible test function, and we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}(G[u]_{i}{-}G[u]_{i-1})U_{k,R}(u_{i})+(v_{i}{-}v_{i-1})U_{k,R}(u_{i})+U_{k,R}^{\prime}(u_{i})\kappa(x,G[u]_{i})\big(\nabla u_{i}{+}\boldsymbol{\nu}\big)\cdot\nabla u_{i}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36967pt+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)(u_{i}-u^{*}_{i})U_{k,R}(u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}s(x)=0.

For every
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and for every nondecreasing function

\alpha\big(h(\alpha+\beta)-h(\beta)\big)\geq 0.

(5.11)

Formula (
5.11
) for

,

together with (

(v_{i}-v_{i-1})U_{k,R}(u_{i})\geq(v_{i}-v_{i-1})U_{k,R}(v_{i})\geq W_{k,R}(v_{i})-W_{k,R}(v_{i-1}),

(5.12)

where we put

The next term in (

U_{k,R}^{\prime}(u_{i})\kappa(x,G[u]_{i})\big(\nabla u_{i}+\boldsymbol{\nu}\big)\cdot\nabla u_{i}\geq aU_{k,R}^{\prime}(u_{i})|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}-bU_{k,R}^{\prime}(u_{i})

R
and

\frac{|U_{k,R}(u)|^{(2k+2)/(2k+1)}}{uU_{k,R}(u)}=\frac{|U_{k,R}(u)|^{1/(2k+1)}}{|u|}\leq 1.

Since

is bounded by Hypothesis

to check that there exists a constant

and

for all

.

Finally, in order to estimate the time-discrete hysteresis term in (

\Psi_{k,R}(x,r,\xi)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\xi}U_{k,R}(v)\phi(x,r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v.

Similarly as in (

\displaystyle(\psi(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i})-\psi(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i-1}))U_{k,R}(u_{i})

\displaystyle\geq(\psi(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i})-\psi(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i-1}))U_{k,R}(\xi^{r}_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\Psi_{k,R}(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i})-\Psi_{k,R}(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i-1}).

Summing up in (

) over

and using the above estimates (

), (

), (
5.16
), and (

), we get, using again an estimate of the initial step at

similar to (

) and the assumption

,

\displaystyle\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi_{k,R}(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i})\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}x+a\tau\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\int_{\Omega}U_{k,R}^{\prime}(u_{i})|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)u_{i}U_{k,R}(u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}s(x)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq C\Big(C^{2k}+\tau\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}U_{k,R}^{\prime}(u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)

(5.19)

with constants
a>0
and
C>0
independent of
\tau
,
k
, and
R
.

For
u\in\mathbb{R}
put

V_{k,R}(u)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u|u|^{k}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ |u|\leq R,\\[5.69054pt]
(k+1)R^{k}u-kR^{k+1}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ u>R,\\[5.69054pt]
(k+1)R^{k}u+kR^{k+1}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ u<-R.\end{array}\right.

We have

\displaystyle|\nabla V_{k,R}(u_{i})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(k+1)^{2}|u_{i}|^{2k}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ |u_{i}|<R,\\[5.69054pt]
(k+1)^{2}R^{2k}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ |u_{i}|\geq R,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle|V_{k,R}(u_{i})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|u_{i}|^{2k+2}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ |u_{i}|<R,\\[5.69054pt]

Then

\displaystyle U_{k,R}^{\prime}(u_{i})|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k+1}{(k+1)^{2}}\big|\nabla V_{k,R}(u_{i})\big|^{2},

(5.25)

\displaystyle u_{i}U_{k,R}(u_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2k+1}{(k+1)^{2}}\big|V_{k,R}(u_{i})\big|^{2}.

We define an equivalent norm of an element

\|w\|_{1,2}\coloneqq\Big(a\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b^{*}(x)|w|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(x)\Big)^{1/2}

)-(

\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi_{k,R}(x,r,\xi^{r}_{i})\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\tau}{k+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|V_{k,R}(u_{i})\|_{1,2}^{2}\leq C\Big(C^{2k}+\tau\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}U_{k,R}^{\prime}(u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x\Big).

(5.27)

We define

With this notation, by (

Let us now compare the

-norm of

with the

-norm of

on the left-hand side
of (

|\nabla u_{i}^{(k,R)}|^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}k^{2}|u_{i}|^{2k-2}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}&\mathrm{\ for\ }\ |u_{i}|<R\\[5.69054pt]

and, by Young’s inequality with conjugate exponents

and

8  Conclusions

In this paper, we have investigated a model for unsaturated flow in a viscoelastic porous medium exhibiting degenerate hysteresis in the pressure-saturation relationship, coupled with gravity-driven moisture flux. This extends previous work on degenerate diffusion in porous media with hysteresis by incorporating the effects of solid matrix deformation and gravity, which introduce significant challenges in the analysis. The key difficulty lies in obtaining a priori bounds for the solutions, which we address by employing a hysteresis variant of the Moser iteration technique. Specifically, we require a condition on the Preisach density, namely, that it decays sufficiently slowly at infinity (Hypothesis
3.6
). This condition allows us to establish a uniform upper bound for the solutions. Building upon this estimate, we leverage the convexity and compactness arguments developed in
[
13
]
to prove the existence of a weak solution to the coupled system. Our approach involves a time discretization scheme, the derivation of estimates independent of the time step, and a passage to the limit.

The results presented here contribute to a deeper understanding of flow phenomena in deformable porous media, relevant to various applications in hydrogeology and related fields. Future work could explore the impact of more complex constitutive models for the solid matrix, as well as the development of efficient numerical methods for the computation of solutions.
