Online combinatorial linear optimization via a Frank-Wolfe based metarounding algorithm
Ryotaro Mitsuboshi, Kohei Hatano, Eiji Takimoto

Abstract
Metarounding is an approach to convert an approximation algorithm for linear optimization over some combinatorial classes to an online linear optimization algorithm for the same class. We propose a new metarounding algorithm under a natural assumption that a relax-based approximation algorithm exists for the combinatorial class. Our algorithm is much more efficient in both theoretical and practical aspects. Keywords Metarounding ⋅ \cdot Boosting ⋅ \cdot Frank-Wolfe

Introduction

Online decision-making problems using combinatorial objects arise in many applications, e.g.,
routing, advertisements, and resource allocation tasks.
Examples of combinatorial objects include
permutations
[
13
]
,

k
-sets
[
25
]
,
paths
[
11
]
,
matching
[
4
,
13
]
,
spanning trees
[
19
]

and so on.
Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{N}^{n}_{+}
be a combinatorial class, i.e., a set of combinatorial objects,
where each combinatorial object is represented as a positive vector.
More formally, online combinatorial linear optimization over a combinatorial class
\mathcal{C}

is defined as a repeated game between
a player and an environment. The protocol is the following:
For each round
t=1,2,\dots
,
(i) the player chooses
\bm{c}_{t}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Then, (ii) the environment reveals a loss vector
\bm{\ell}_{t}\in[0,1]^{n}
and
(iii) the player incurs the loss
\bm{c}_{t}\cdot\bm{\ell}_{t}
.

The player’s goal is to minimize the
\alpha
-regret defined as

R_{T}(\alpha)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{c}_{t}\cdot\bm{\ell}_{t}-\alpha\min_{\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{c}\cdot\bm{\ell}_{t},

for as small
\alpha\geq 1
as possible.

There are two approaches for online combinatorial linear optimization over
\mathcal{C}
.
The first approach is to design algorithms for individual classes
[
4
,
13
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
.
The second approach is so called offline-to-online conversion,
which uses an offline approximation algorithm over
\mathcal{C}

to construct an online algorithm over
\mathcal{C}

[
15
,
10
,
9
]
.
An offline approximation algorithm takes
\bm{\ell}
as input
and then outputs a vector
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying

\bm{c}\cdot\bm{\ell}\leq\alpha\min_{\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\bm{c}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\ell}
.
The offline-to-online conversion approach is generic in that
once we design an efficient conversion algorithm,
we can just use known approximation algorithms for different combinatorial classes.
For example, Garber proposed an algorithm
that calls the sub-routine
O(\ln T)
times for each round
t

and achieves
R_{T}(\alpha)=O(T^{2/3})

[
10
]
.
The paper also proposed
an algorithm that calls the sub-routine
O(\sqrt{T}\ln T)

per round and achieves
R_{T}(\alpha)=O(\sqrt{T})
.
The algorithm needs to know the number of total rounds
T
and
the approximation ratio
\alpha
a priori.
In addition, the number of sub-routine calls depends on
T
, so
the conversion via the sub-routine may slow for a huge
T
.

Another offline-to-online conversion approach is to use metarounding
[
2
]
,
which uses a relaxation-based approximation algorithm.
In fact, for many combinatorial classes
\mathcal{C}
,
there are relaxation-based approximation algorithms.
Thus, the requirement is natural.
Metarounding converts a vector
\bm{x}\in\mathop{\rm relax}(\mathcal{C})
into
a combinatorial vector
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}
,
where
\mathop{\rm relax}(\mathcal{C})
is
a convex set satisfying
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathop{\rm relax}(\mathcal{C})
.
The relax-based approximation algorithms takes
\bm{\ell}
as input
and then outputs a vector
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying

\bm{c}\cdot\bm{\ell}\leq\alpha\min_{\bm{p}\in\mathop{\rm relax}(\mathcal{C})}\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\ell}
. The only difference between relaxation-based approximation
and the approximation algorithm is
the right-hand-side of the condition.
Using the relaxation-based approximation algorithm as sub-routine,
metarounding algorithms aim to output a vector
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}
randomly
to satisfy

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{c}}[\bm{c}]\cdot\bm{\ell}\leq\alpha\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\ell}
for all
\bm{\ell}
.
Thus, combining the metarounding algorithm
with an arbitrary online linear optimization algorithm
over
\mathop{\rm relax}(\mathcal{C})
,
one can achieve

R_{T}(\alpha)=\alpha(1\text{-regret of the OLO algorithm})

[
9
]
.
Thus, once an efficient metarounding algorithm is obtained,
we can fuse it to an OLO algorithm to obtain a better regret bound.

iterations, where
D_{\infty}^{2}
is
the maximal value of a combinatorial vector
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}

and
M
is the input-dependent constant.

This paper proposes a new metarounding algorithm
that finds a distribution
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{C}}

over
\mathcal{C}
that satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{c}\sim\bm{\lambda}}[\bm{c}]\cdot\bm{\ell}\leq(\alpha+\epsilon)\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\ell}

This guarantee is

Our algorithm is designed based on

\displaystyle f(\bm{x})=\max_{i\in[k]}x_{i}\quad\iff\quad f^{\star}(\bm{y})=I_{\mathcal{P}^{k}}(\bm{y})=\begin{cases}0&\bm{y}\in\mathcal{P}^{k},\\

3.1
Margin optimization boosting algorithms

Boosting is a common machine learning technique that
combines multiple low-accuracy predictors to yield a highly accurate one.
Since the predictors to be combined are chosen from
a set of exponentially many predictors,
one cannot compute the combined predictor directory.
The main idea of boosting is to collect the most promising predictors
one by one until some condition is satisfied.

Gradient Boosting Machines
[
8
]
,
such as XGBoost
[
5
]
and LightGBM
[
16
]
, are
popular algorithms that minimize some loss functions.
For binary classification,
it is well known that a predictor
that maximizes the quantity so-called “margin”
instead of minimizing loss functions
is known to have smaller losses for unseen data
[
20
]
.
Thus, some algorithms,
such as LPBoost
[
6
]
,
SoftBoost
[
23
]
, and
ERLPBoost
[
24
]
,
are proposed to optimize the margin.

First, we revisit the approach by Fujita et al.
[
9
]
.
In

\displaystyle\min_{\beta,\bm{\lambda}}\;\beta\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda_{\bm{c}}c_{i}\leq\beta x_{i},\quad\forall i\in[n],

(2)

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{C}}.

The Lagrange dual problem for Problem (

\displaystyle\max_{\gamma,\bm{\ell}}\;\gamma\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\bm{\ell}\cdot\bm{c}\geq\gamma,\quad\forall\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C},

(3)

\displaystyle\bm{\ell}\cdot\bm{x}=1,\quad\bm{\ell}\geq\bm{0}.

Problem (
3
) has

so

uses the column-generation approach
like soft margin boosting algorithms;
That is, starting from
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}=\emptyset

they solve a problem under the constraints in (

)
over
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
and then
append a new combinatorial vector
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}

to

one by one until some conditions are met.
One can see that Problem (

as so-called “Edge minimization” in boosting
(See, e.g.,

]

In fact, setting

With this insight,

is proposed based on a boosting algorithm
named SoftBoost
[

]
, but the resulting guarantee is

We want to use the state-of-the-art soft margin optimization
named ERLPBoost
[
24

to solve Problem (

),
which uses a normalized relative entropy regularizer,
but there are some issues.
First,

ERLPBoost succeeded because its feasible region is probability simplex,
while Problem (

One naive idea is to use unnormalized relative entropy
instead of the normalized one.
This approach results in a similar result to Fujita et al.
since the variable

With these issues, we use a different approach,
a Frank-Wolfe-based approach similar to
the one in Mitsuboshi et al.
[

First, we convert Problem (

to an equivalent bounded problem and then use an ERLPBoost-like problem.
The resulting algorithm is a generalization of the soft margin boosting
in the following sense:
Our algorithm becomes ERLPBoost if we set
\bm{x}=\bm{1}
.

The regularization

\displaystyle\max_{\gamma,\bm{\ell}}\;\gamma-\frac{1}{\eta}\Delta(\bar{\bm{\ell}})\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad\bar{\bm{\ell}}\cdot\bar{\bm{c}}\geq\gamma,\quad\forall\bar{\bm{c}}\in\bar{\mathcal{C}},\quad\bar{\bm{\ell}}\in\mathcal{P}^{n}.

Lemma 1
.

Let

Proof.

Recall that

\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k}=\nabla H^{\star}(\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k})=\arg\max_{\bar{\bm{\ell}}\in\mathcal{P}^{n}}\bar{\bm{\ell}}^{\top}\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k}-\frac{1}{\eta}\Delta(\bar{\bm{\ell}}).

By definition of

\displaystyle H^{\star}(\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k})=\;

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k}^{\top}\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k}-\frac{1}{\eta}\Delta(\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k})

\displaystyle\geq\;

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\ell}}^{\top}\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k}-\frac{1}{\eta}\Delta(\bar{\bm{\ell}})\geq\bm{\ell}^{\top}C^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k}-\frac{1}{\eta}\ln n\geq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{c}\sim\bm{\lambda}_{k}}\left[\bm{\ell}\cdot\bm{c}\right]-\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

On the other hand,
by the assumption of the

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[k]}\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{j}\cdot\bar{\bm{c}}_{j+1}=\max_{j\in[k]}\bm{\ell}_{j}\cdot\bm{c}_{j+1}\leq

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[k]}\alpha\min_{\bm{p}\in\mathop{\rm relax}(\mathcal{C})}\bm{\ell}_{j}\cdot\bm{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha\max_{j\in[k]}\bm{\ell}_{j}\cdot\bm{x}=\alpha\max_{j\in[k]}\|\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{j}\|_{1}=\alpha,

where the second inequality comes from

\bm{x}\in\mathop{\rm relax}(\mathcal{C})

Combining the above relations, we have

\epsilon_{k}=H^{\star}(\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k})-\max_{j\in[k]}\bar{\bm{c}}_{j+1}\cdot\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{j}\geq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{c}\sim\bm{\lambda}_{k}}\left[\bm{\ell}\cdot\bm{c}\right]-\frac{\epsilon}{2}-\alpha,\qquad\forall\bm{\ell}\in[0,M]^{n}.

Thus,
\epsilon_{k}\leq\epsilon/2

for all
\bm{\ell}\in[0,M]^{n}

Lemma 2

For all

\displaystyle\forall k\geq 1,\quad\epsilon_{k+1}\leq\left(1-\mu_{k}\right)\epsilon_{k}+2\eta\mu_{k}^{2}M^{2}D_{\infty}^{2}.

(9)

Proof.

First of all,
\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}
is chosen to optimize

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}^{k+1}}H^{\star}(\bar{C}^{(k+1)}\bm{\lambda}),\qquad\bar{C}^{(k+1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{c}}_{1}&\bar{\bm{c}}_{2}&\dots&\bar{\bm{c}}_{k+1}\end{bmatrix}\in[0,MD_{\infty}]^{n\times(k+1)},

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{k+1}\geq\;

\displaystyle H^{\star}(\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k})-H^{\star}(\bar{C}^{(k+1)}\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle\geq\;

\displaystyle-\mu_{k}\nabla H^{\star}(\bar{C}^{(k+1)}\bm{\lambda}_{k})^{\top}\bar{C}^{(k+1)}\left(\bm{e}_{\bar{\bm{c}}_{k+1}}-\bm{\lambda}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{2}\mu_{k}^{2}\|\bar{C}^{(k+1)}(\bm{e}_{\bar{\bm{c}}_{k+1}}-\bm{\lambda}_{k})\|_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\;

\displaystyle-\mu_{k}\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k}\bar{C}^{(k+1)}\left(\bm{e}_{\bar{\bm{c}}_{k+1}}-\bm{\lambda}_{k}\right)-2\eta\mu_{k}^{2}M^{2}D_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle-\mu_{k}\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k}\cdot\bar{\bm{c}}_{k+1}+\mu_{k}\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k}^{\top}\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k}-2\eta\mu_{k}^{2}M^{2}D_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\;

\displaystyle-\mu_{k}\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k}\cdot\bar{\bm{c}}_{k+1}+\mu_{k}\left[\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k}^{\top}\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k}-\frac{1}{\eta}\Delta(\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k})\right]-2\eta\mu_{k}^{2}M^{2}D_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle-\mu_{k}\bar{\bm{\ell}}_{k}\cdot\bar{\bm{c}}_{k+1}+\mu_{k}H^{\star}(\bar{C}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}_{k})-2\eta\mu_{k}^{2}M^{2}D_{\infty}^{2}
