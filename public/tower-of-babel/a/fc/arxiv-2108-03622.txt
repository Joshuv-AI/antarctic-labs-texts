Rigidity of the round cylinders in Ricci shrinkers
Yu Li, and, Bing Wang

Abstract
In this paper, we prove that the round cylinders are rigid in the space of Ricci shrinkers. Namely, any Ricci shrinker that is sufficiently close to S n − 1 × ℝ S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R} in the pointed-Gromov-Hausdorff topology must itself be isometric to S n − 1 × ℝ S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R} .

Introduction

A Ricci shrinker is a triple
(M^{n},g,f)
of smooth manifold
M^{n}
, Riemannian metric
g
and a smooth function
f
satisfying

\displaystyle Rc+\text{Hess}\,f=\frac{1}{2}g,

where the potential function
f
is normalized so that

\displaystyle R+|\nabla f|^{2}

\displaystyle=f.

(1.1)

The Ricci shrinkers play essential roles in studying the singularities of the Ricci flow. For example, it was proved by Enders-Müller-Topping
[
26
]
that any proper blowup sequence from a type-I Ricci flow converges smoothly to a nontrivial Ricci shrinker.

Due to its importance, the classification of Ricci shrinkers has attracted extensive attentions.
In dimension
2
or
3
, we know that
\mathbb{R}^{2},S^{2},\mathbb{R}^{3},S^{3},S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, and their quotients make the complete list of all Ricci shrinkers (e.g.,
[
32
]
[
54
]
[
57
]
[
13
]
).
In higher dimensions, there exist many nontrivial, non-product Ricci shrinkers (e.g.,
[
37
]
[
11
]
[
27
]
), and the classification of Ricci shrinkers is only achieved when extra assumptions are assumed.
Such assumptions include non-negativity of curvatures (e.g.,
[
52
]
[
46
]
[
55
]
[
45
]
[
48
]
), restriction of the Weyl curvatures (e.g.,
[
59
]
[
50
]
[
12
]
[
23
]
),
restriction of asymptotic behavior at infinity (e.g.,
[
40
]
[
41
]
), Kähler conditions (e.g.,
[
56
]
[
22
]
[
20
]
), and others.
In general, much less is known if no extra assumptions are assumed.

We can consider all Ricci shrinkers as one moduli space
\mathcal{M}
equipped with pointed-Gromov-Hausdorff topology. On each Ricci shrinker,
f
always achieves its minimum value (cf.
[
14
]
) at some point
p
, which can be assigned as a base point.
A natural question is: which Ricci shrinker is rigid in
\mathcal{M}
, in the sense that there is no nearby (in the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff distance) Ricci shrinker other than itself? For instance, it follows from
[
35
]
and the weak-compactness theory developed in
[
44
]
that the spherical space form
S^{n}/\Gamma
is rigid since any Ricci shrinker is a self-similar Ricci flow solution. Other rigid examples include
\mathbb{CP}^{2n}
proved by Kröncke
[
42
]
and
S^{2}\times S^{2}
proved by Sun-Zhu
[
66
]
very recently.
Their proofs depend on a delicate local structure theory of
\mathcal{M}
(cf.
[
62
]
[
36
]
), which is not available for non-compact Ricci shrinkers.
This makes the rigidity problem for non-compact Ricci shrinkers much more involved.
Up to now, the only non-compact Ricci shrinkers known to be rigid are the Gaussian solitons
(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{E})
, whose rigidity is proved through an entropy-gap argument (cf.
[
69
]
[
70
]
[
48
]
).

Inspired by the fundamental work of Colding-Ilmanen-Minicozzi
[
21
]
on mean curvature flow, and our earlier research on 4-dimensional Ricci shrinkers
[
47
]
, we are interested in figuring out whether the generalized cylinders
S^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k},2\leq k\leq n-1

are rigid in the moduli space of Ricci shrinkers. In this article, we confirm the rigidity of the round cylinders
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, i.e., the cases
k=n-1
.

Theorem 1.1

(Main Theorem)
.

There exists a small constant
\hat{\epsilon}=\hat{\epsilon}(n)>0
satisfying the following property.

Suppose
(M^{n},p,g,f)
is a Ricci shrinker such that

\displaystyle d_{PGH}\left\{(M^{n},p,g),(S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R},p_{c},g_{c})\right\}<\hat{\epsilon},

(1.2)

then
(M,g)
is isometric to
(S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R},g_{c})
. Here
d_{PGH}
means the pointed-Gromov-Hausdorff distance,
p
is a minimum point of
f
and
p_{c}
is a fixed point of
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies heavily on the symmetry improvement technique of S. Brendle et al. (cf.
[

]
[

]
[

]
[

]
[

]
), the classification result of B. Kotschwar
[

]
and the weak-compactness theory developed by H. Li, S. Huang and the authors (
[

]

[

]

[

).

Outline of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The proof consists of four steps.

Step 1. The base point
p
is in an
\epsilon
-neck.

Condition (
1.2
) means that the base point
p
has a neighborhood very close to the standard cylinder in the Gromov-Hausdorff topology, which is very rough.
However, by the weak-compactness theory developed in
[
44
]
, we can improve the Gromov-Hausdorff topology to the
C^{\infty}
-Cheeger-Gromov topology (cf. Proposition 7.4 of
[
44
]
).
Therefore, by choosing
\hat{\epsilon}
sufficiently small, we are able to show (cf. Proposition
2.6
) that
p
is actually in the center of an evolving normalized
\epsilon
-neck (cf. Definition
2.5
).
We may understand that the point
p
has
\epsilon
-symmetry with respect to the model space
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Step 2. Each point
x\in M
is in an
\epsilon
-neck or a region
\epsilon
-close to the Bryant soliton, after proper rescaling.

This step is the technical core of this paper. We characterize the
\epsilon
-symmetry in terms of curvature and potential function estimates and show that these estimates are almost preserved along the flow line of
\nabla f
.
Roughly speaking, if the level set of
f
is uniformly away from being critical (i.e.,
\nabla f=0
), then each point locates in an
\epsilon
-neck. Moreover, the critical points are isolated, and the region is very close to the Bryant soliton near the critical point of
f
. Therefore, each point “far away” from the critical set is in an
\epsilon
-neck, and each point “near” critical set is in a region
\epsilon
-close to the Bryant soliton. These descriptions can be made precise, which is the key new ingredient of this argument.

Step 3.
(M,g)
is rotationally symmetric.

This step utilizes the celebrated symmetry improvement technique developed by S. Brendle et al. (cf. Appendix
B
for details). Suppose
\epsilon
is sufficiently small and every point on
(M,g)
has
\epsilon
-symmetry (with respect to either the cylinder or Bryant soliton), then every point has
\frac{\epsilon}{2}
-symmetry.
Consequently, this process will run forever and yield that
every point
x\in M
has
2^{-k}\epsilon
-symmetry for each positive integer
k
. Therefore,
(M,g)
is rotationally symmetric.

Step 4.
(M,g)
is isometric to
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Since
(M,g)
is rotationally symmetric already, the classification result of Kotschwar
[
38
]
applies.
We know that
(M,g)
is isometric to
S^{n}
,
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By condition (
1.2
), we conclude that
(M,g)
must be isometric to
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
.
∎

From the discussion above, it is clear that the most difficult part of the proof is to analyze precisely the propagation of the
\epsilon
-symmetry along the flow line of
\nabla f
.
Due to this propagation, if there is a neck region far away from the base point and close to the round cylinder, then the behavior of the far-end can be described precisely.
Since
f\sim\frac{d^{2}(p,\cdot)}{4}
, the value of
f
indicates the distance to the base point.
For each pair
t\leq s
, we define
\Sigma(t,s)
to be a connected component of the level set

\displaystyle\{x\in M\,\mid t\leq f(x)\leq s\}.

(1.3)

In the particular case
t=s
, we define

\displaystyle\Sigma(t)\coloneqq\Sigma(t,t)=\{x\in M\,\mid f(x)=t\}.

(1.4)

If a point locates in a cylinder-like neighborhood, we say it is the center of
\epsilon
-neck.
Alternatively, if a point locates in a neighborhood close to a steady soliton with mild singularities, we say it is the center of
\epsilon
-steady soliton conifold.
Note that the conifold here is the one introduced by Chen-Wang (cf. Definition 1.2 of
[
24
]
) to denote the Riemannian space with mild singularities.
For the exact definitions of the aforementioned concepts, see Definition
2.5
, Definition
4.3
and Definition
A.1
.
With these terminologies, we can precisely describe the propagation of the
\epsilon
-symmetry in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(Propagation of almost symmetry)
.

For any positive constants
n,A,B,\epsilon
and
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
, there exist positive constants
\sigma=\sigma(n,\delta_{0},A,B)
,
L=L(n,\delta_{0},A,B)
and
\eta=\eta(n,\delta_{0},A,B,\epsilon)
satisfying the following property.

Let
(M^{n},g,f)
be a Ricci shrinker with

\displaystyle\begin{cases}|\nabla^{i}Rm|\leq BR^{\frac{i}{2}+1},\quad\forall\,0\leq i\leq 4\quad&\text{on}\quad\Sigma(t_{0},s_{0});\\
R\leq\epsilon_{1}f\quad&\text{on}\quad\Sigma(t_{0},s_{0});\\
|\overset{\circ}{Rm_{\Sigma}}|\leq\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}R\quad&\text{on}\quad\Sigma(t_{0});\\
\left|R-\frac{n-1}{2}\right|\leq\epsilon^{\prime}_{1}\quad&\text{on}\quad\Sigma(t_{0});\\
(1-\delta_{0})s_{0}\geq t_{0}\geq n\epsilon_{1}^{-1};\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}(g)\geq-A;\\
\Sigma(t_{0})\text{ is diffeomorphic to }S^{n-1}.\end{cases}

(1.5)

If
\epsilon_{1}\leq\sigma
and
\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}\leq\eta
, then one of the following statements holds.

(a).

There exists an end
E
with
\partial E=\Sigma(t_{0})
such that any point in
\Sigma(\eta^{-1}t_{0},\infty)\subset E
is the center of an evolving normalized
\epsilon
-neck. Moreover,
E
is asymptotic to the round cylinder with rate
O(r^{-\tau(n)})
.

(b).

There exists an end
E
with
\partial E=\Sigma(t_{0})
such that any point in
\Sigma(\eta^{-1}t_{0},\infty)\subset E
is the center of an evolving
\epsilon
-neck. Moreover,
E
is asymptotic to a regular cone with cross section diffeomorphic to
S^{n-1}
.

(c).

There exists a compact set
E
with
\partial E=\Sigma(t_{0})
, a number
s\geq s_{0}
and a point
q\in\Sigma(s)
such that
R(q)=\sigma s
. Moreover, any point in
\Sigma(\eta^{-1}t_{0},s)\subset E
is the center of an evolving
\epsilon
-neck and any point in the cap
D:=E\backslash\Sigma(t_{0},s)
is the center of an
\epsilon
-steady soliton conifold. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\emph{diam}_{g}D\leq\frac{L}{\sqrt{s}},\quad\sup_{D}|f-s|\leq L\quad\text{and}\quad L^{-1}s\leq\inf_{D}R\leq\sup_{D}R\leq s+L.

(1.6)

Note that (
1.5
) on
\Sigma(t_{0},s_{0})
is satisfied if the region is very close to the cylinder, where the constant
\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}
can be chosen as zero. Since the function
f
contains no critical point on the region
\Sigma(t_{0},s_{0})
, one can follow Munteanu-Wang
[
53
]
to define the tangential curvature operator
Rm_{\Sigma}
restricted on each level set of
f
and the term
\overset{\circ}{Rm_{\Sigma}}
is the “traceless” part of
Rm_{\Sigma}
(cf. (
2.6
), (
2.7
)). If
R/f
is small, the value of
|\overset{\circ}{Rm_{\Sigma}}|
measures how close
Rm_{\Sigma}
resembles the curvature operator of the standard
S^{n-1}
. Theorem
1.2
implies that if a Ricci shrinker has a neck region sufficiently close to the cylinder in some weak sense, one can move forward along the direction of
\nabla f
to determine the future geometric property.
If we regard a Ricci shrinker as a Ricci flow solution, the control of the geometric behavior at spatial infinity amounts to that near the singular time
t=1
.
Recall that the traditional pseudolocality on Ricci shrinkers (cf.
[
48
, Corollary
10.6
]
) means that the almost Euclidean property is preserved by the flow until time
t=1
.
Theorem
1.2
claims instead that the
\epsilon
-symmetric property is almost preserved by the flow until time
t=1
. Therefore, Theorem
1.2
can be understood as a generalized form of pseudolocality theorem on Ricci shrinkers.
See Figure
1
for rough intuition.
We emphasize here that the global bound of the curvature is not needed. With some extra assumptions like the boundedness of the curvature, Munteanu-Wang
[
53
, Theorem
1.6
]
has already obtained case (a) in Theorem
1.2
.

We briefly discuss the proof of Theorem
1.2
. We first show on the neck region satisfying (
1.5
), the estimate
|\overset{\circ}{Rm_{\Sigma}}|\leq\epsilon_{0}R
holds on
\Sigma(t_{0},s_{0})
, where
\epsilon_{0}
is a constant depending only on
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon^{\prime}_{1}
. Since
f\geq t_{0}
is large and
R/f\leq\epsilon_{1}
is small,
all normal directional part of
Rm
is small and insignificant. Moreover, we can show that the scalar curvature
R
is almost constant along any level set
\Sigma
of
f
contained in the neck region. From the elliptic equation of
R
, one obtains an ODE by examining the behavior of
R
along the gradient flow of
f
.

One can extend the neck region from
\Sigma(t_{0},s_{0})
to
\Sigma(t_{0},s)
with
s
to be maximal so that (
1.5
) still holds. Then we show that
s
is finite only if there exists a point
q\in\Sigma(s)
with
R(q)=\epsilon_{1}f(q)
.
Here, we need to use the pseudolocality for Ricci shrinkers proved in
[
48
]
to show that any point
x\in\Sigma(\eta^{-1}t_{0},s)
is the center of an evolving
\epsilon
-neck.
Depending on the behavior of
R
along the gradient flow of
f
, only three cases can happen, as described in Theorem
1.2
.
If
R
stays close to
\frac{n-1}{2}
, then
s=\infty
and we obtain the case (a). If at some point in the neck
R
is smaller than
\frac{n-1}{2}
by some detectable small number, then
R
will be quadratically decaying along the flow. In this case,
s=\infty
and we obtain the case (b). If at some point in the neck
R
is bigger than
\frac{n-1}{2}
by some detectable small number, then
R
will be increasing along the flow until it reaches the boundary
\Sigma(s)
. If this happens,
s<\infty
and we obtain case (c).
In this case, it is important to show that there exists a compact cap
D
with
\partial D=\Sigma(s)
. The reason is that the behavior of the Ricci shrinker near the boundary
\Sigma(s)
is modeled on a Ricci steady soliton conifold (cf. Definition
A.1
) at a proper scale.
This can be proved through a compactness argument (cf.
[
44
]
[
34
]
). Since each model steady soliton conifold has only one end (cf. Theorem
A.22
), the cap region
D
is compact. Further analysis implies the estimates in (
1.6
).

Now we continue to discuss Step 2 in the outline proof of Theorem
1.1
in more detail.
First, one notices that any Ricci shrinker close to the cylinder satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1.2
for two neck regions. By extending those two neck regions, there are three possibilities.

(1).

The Ricci shrinker has two ends so that each point is the center of an evolving
\epsilon
-neck.

(2).

The Ricci shrinker has exactly one end so that outside a compact cap
D
, any point is the center of an evolving
\epsilon
-neck.

(3).

The Ricci shrinker is compact and it consists of two caps
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
such that outside those two caps, any point is the center of an evolving
\epsilon
-neck.

In order to realize Step 2 in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, the last key ingredient needed is the observation that the cap is modeled on the Bryant soliton in cases (2), (3) above. This fact follows from the classification result (cf. Theorem
4.7
) of any asymptotically cylindrical Ricci steady soliton conifold with the curvature condition PIC2,
which is a generalization of the celebrated work of S. Brendle
[
3
]
. On the other hand, if one considers the Ricci flow associated with a Ricci shrinker close to the cylinder, we show that locally the Ricci flow almost preserves the nonnegativity of some curvature conditions (cf. Theorem
5.4
) defined by S. Brendle.
By analyzing the geometry along the gradient flow of
f
, one can show that the blowup limit near the cap region must have the PIC2 condition.
Consequently, the blowup limit must be a steady soliton conifold with PIC2 condition, which can be classified and can only be the Bryant soliton.
Therefore, the cap regions are modeled on Bryant solitons.
In conclusion, for a Ricci shrinker sufficiently close to a cylinder, each point lies in a region either
\epsilon
-close to the cylinder or the Bryant soliton.
Roughly speaking, this means that the shrinker itself is
\epsilon
-symmetric (cf. Definition
B.1
, Definition
B.3
or Definition
B.4
for precise definitions).
Therefore, we have collected sufficient technical preparation to apply the symmetry improvement argument of S. Brendle et al.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss some basic properties for Ricci shrinkers and the associated Ricci flows. In Section 3, we obtain all the necessary technical estimates on the neck region.
Section 4 focuses on the cap region and shows that it can be modeled on a Ricci steady soliton conifold. As a conclusion of the estimates in Section 3 and Section 4, we prove Theorem
1.2
at the end of Section 4.
In Section 5, we prove Theorem
1.1
.
In the last section, we discuss possible generalizations of our theorems.

Lemma 2.1

(
[

]

[
33
]
)

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(d(x,p)-5n\right)^{2}_{+}\leq f(x)\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(d(x,p)+\sqrt{2n}\right)^{2}

for all

, where

.

For any Ricci shrinker

with the normalization (

), the entropy is defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\mu}(g)\coloneqq\log\int\frac{e^{-f}}{(4\pi)^{n/2}}\,dV.

We remark that

[

]

[

]

\displaystyle R_{ik}f_{k}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{i}R=\nabla_{k}R_{ki},

(2.1)

\displaystyle R_{ijkl}f_{l}=

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}R_{ik}-\nabla_{i}R_{jk}=\nabla_{l}R_{ijkl},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}R=

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}R_{ij}=

\displaystyle R_{ij}-2R_{ikjl}R_{kl}.

Here, the weighted Laplacian

We have the following estimates of the curvature along the normal direction. The proof follows from direct calculations based on (

Proposition 2.2
.

For any Ricci shrinker
(M^{n},g,f)
such that
R\leq\frac{3}{4}f
on a level set
\Sigma
of
f
, the following estimates hold on
\Sigma
.

\displaystyle|R_{an}|\leq

\displaystyle Cf^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\nabla R|,

\displaystyle|R_{nn}|\leq

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}\left(R+|Rc|^{2}+|\nabla^{2}R|\right),

\displaystyle|R_{abcn}|\leq

\displaystyle Cf^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\nabla Rc|,

\displaystyle|R_{ancn}|\leq

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}\left(|Rc|+|\nabla^{2}Rc|+|Rc|\,|Rm|\right),

where
C=C(n)>0
.

Proof.

From (
1.1
) and our assumption on
R
, we have
|\nabla f|\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{f}
. We compute on
\Sigma
,

\displaystyle|R_{an}|=\frac{|Rc(e_{a},\nabla f)|}{|\nabla f|}\leq Cf^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\langle\nabla R,e_{a}\rangle|\leq Cf^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\nabla R|.

from (
2.1
). Moreover, it follows from (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) that

\displaystyle|R_{nn}|=

\displaystyle|\nabla f|^{-2}|Rc(\nabla f,\nabla f)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}|\langle\nabla R,\nabla f\rangle|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}\left|\Delta R-R+2|Rc|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}\left(R+|Rc|^{2}+|\nabla^{2}R|\right).

Similarly, we compute from (
2.2
),

\displaystyle|R_{abcn}|\leq|\nabla f|^{-1}|R_{abcl}f_{l}|\leq Cf^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\nabla Rc|.

For the last inequality, we have

\displaystyle|R_{ancn}|\leq

\displaystyle|\nabla f|^{-2}|R_{akcl}f_{k}f_{l}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}|(\nabla_{k}R_{ac}-\nabla_{a}R_{kc})f_{k}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}|\nabla_{k}R_{ac}f_{k}-\nabla_{a}(R_{kc}f_{k})+R_{kc}f_{ka}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}|\Delta R_{ac}-R_{ac}+2R_{akcl}R_{kl}-\nabla^{2}_{ac}R/2+R_{kc}(g_{ka}/2-R_{ka})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}|2R_{akcl}R_{kl}-R_{kc}R_{ka}-R_{ac}/2+\Delta R_{ac}-\nabla^{2}_{ac}R/2|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cf^{-1}\left(|Rc|+|\nabla^{2}Rc|+|Rc|\,|Rm|\right).

In sum, the proof is complete.

Ricci flow associated with a Ricci shrinker

Recall that any Ricci shrinker
(M^{n},g,f)
can be regarded as a self-similar solution of the Ricci flow. Let
{\psi^{t}}:M\to M
be a family of diffeomorphisms generated by
X(t)=\dfrac{1}{1-t}\nabla f
, and
\psi^{0}=\text{id}
.
In other words, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\psi^{t}}(x)=\frac{1}{1-t}\nabla f\left({\psi^{t}}(x)\right).

(2.12)

It is well known that the rescaled pull-back metric
g(t)\coloneqq(1-t)(\psi^{t})^{*}g
satisfies the Ricci flow equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g=-2Rc(g(t))

for any
-\infty<t<1
. In particular,
g(0)=g
. For any Ricci shrinker, the associated Ricci flow is implicitly understood.

Example 2.4

(Round cylinder)
.

The round cylinder
(S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R},g_{c})
is a Ricci shrinker where the scalar curvature is identically
\dfrac{n-1}{2}
and the potential function
f_{c}=\dfrac{z^{2}}{4}+\dfrac{n-1}{2}
, where
z
is the coordinate in the
\mathbb{R}
-factor. The associated Ricci flow is
\{(S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R},g_{c}(t)),\,-\infty<t<1\}
, where

\displaystyle g_{c}(t)=2(n-2)(1-t)g_{S^{n-1}}+dz\otimes dz,\quad g_{c}(0)=g_{c}.

In conclusion, for each large

∎

Now we are able to finish the proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem

:

It follows from the combination of Theorem

that each ancient Ricci flow solution

Applying (

Appendix A
Steady soliton conifold

Following Chen-Wang
[
24
]
, we introduce the concept of the steady soliton conifold and prove many fundamental properties.

Definition A.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}(n)
be the collection of geodesic spaces
(X^{n},p,d,g,f)
with the following properties.

1.

f
is a locally Lipschitz function defined on
X
and
f(p)=0
.

2.

X
has a disjoint regular-singular decomposition
X=\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{R}
is the regular part,
\mathcal{S}
is the singular part.
A point is called regular if it has a neighborhood that is isometric to a totally geodesic convex domain of some smooth Riemannian manifold. A point is called singular
if it is not regular.

3.

(\mathcal{R},g)
is an open (possibly incomplete) Riemannian manifold of dimension
n
with
R\geq 0
and satisfies the Ricci steady soliton equation

\displaystyle Rc_{f}:=Rc+\emph{Hess}\,f=0.

(A.1)

4.

\mathcal{R}
is geodesically convex, i.e., for any pair of points
x,y\in\mathcal{R}
, there exists a shortest geodesic in
\mathcal{R}
connecting
x
and
y
.

5.

\dim_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{S}\leq n-4
, where
\dim_{\mathcal{M}}
means Minkowski dimension.

6.

Every tangent space of
x\in\mathcal{S}
is a metric cone of Hausdorff dimension
n
. Moreover, if
Y
is a tangent cone of
x
, then the unit ball

centered at vertex

must satisfy

\displaystyle|B(\hat{x},1)|\leq(1-\delta_{0})\omega_{n}

for some uniform positive number
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(n)>0
. Here the volume is the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure and

is the volume of the unit ball in

.

Note that

is called a (Riemannian) conifold if all the properties in the above definition except equation (

) are satisfied (cf. Definition 1.2 of
[
24
]
).
The geodesic space
(X,d)
is the completion of
(\mathcal{R},g)
. The equation (

By taking the divergence of (

), we obtain

on

for a constant

. If

and

is a constant. From the equation

on

, we conclude that

on
\mathcal{R}
. In this case,
(X,d)
is a Ricci-flat Riemannian conifold and is called a

and hence

\displaystyle R+|\nabla f|^{2}=1

(A.2)

on

. In this case,

is called a

Ricci steady soliton. In the following, we derive some geometric and analytic properties of
(X,d)\in\mathfrak{S}(n)

[

It follows from the Weitzenböck formula that on
\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle 0=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{f}|\nabla b|^{2}=|\text{Hess}\,b|^{2}+Rc_{f}(\nabla b,\nabla b)+\langle\nabla\Delta_{f}b,\nabla b\rangle=|\text{Hess}\,b|^{2}.

Therefore,

and one can prove as

that the gradient flow of

preserves the regular point. Now the conclusion follows easily.
∎

For any

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}(u,v):=\int_{\mathcal{R}}\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle\,d\mu.

It can be proved (see

\displaystyle P_{t}(u)(y)=\int u(x)p(t,x,y)\,d\mu(x),\quad\forall u\in L^{2}(X,\mu),\,t>0.

Here,

is called the heat kernel with respect to

. Notice the

can be regarded as the unique generator concerning

and the domain of
\Delta_{f}

We have the following estimates which follow from

Theorem A.14
.

(Heat kernel estimates)
There exists a unique heat kernel

with respect to the Dirichlet form
\mathscr{E}
.

1.

p(t,x,y)
is stochastically complete. That is,

\displaystyle\int p(t,x,y)\,d\mu(x)=1.

2.

For any

C_{5}=C_{5}(n,L)>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{C_{5}^{-1}}{|B(x,\sqrt{t})|_{\mu}}\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,y)}{C_{5}^{-1}t}\right)\leq p(t,x,y)\leq\frac{C_{5}}{|B(x,\sqrt{t})|_{\mu}}\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,y)}{C_{5}t}\right)

for any

and

.

Next, we have the following Bakry-Émery condition.

Proposition A.15
.

(Bakry-Émery condition)
Let
(X,p,d,f)\in\mathfrak{S}(n)
. Then for any
u\in\Sigma(\Delta_{f})
with
\Delta_{f}u\in N^{1,2}(X,\mu)
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla u|^{2}\Delta_{f}v\,d\mu\geq\int v\langle\nabla u,\nabla\Delta_{f}u\rangle\,d\mu.

Proof.

If
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{R})
, then it follows from the Weitzenböck formula that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{f}|\nabla u|^{2}=|\text{Hess}\,u|^{2}+Rc_{f}(\nabla u,\nabla u)+\langle\nabla u,\nabla\Delta_{f}u\rangle\geq\langle\nabla u,\nabla\Delta_{f}u\rangle.

Then for any
v\in L^{\infty}\cap\Sigma(\Delta_{f})
with
v\geq 0
and
\Delta_{f}v\in L^{\infty}
, it is clear from the integration by parts that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla u|^{2}\Delta_{f}v\,d\mu\geq\int v\langle\nabla u,\nabla\Delta_{f}u\rangle\,d\mu.

Now the general case follows by approximating
u
by functions in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{R})
as Proposition
A.5
.

Now we recall

Definition A.19
.

The first eigenvalue fo

\displaystyle\lambda:=\inf_{u\in N^{1,2}(X,\mu)}\frac{\mathscr{E}(u,u)}{\int u^{2}\,d\mu}=\inf_{u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{R})}\frac{\int|\nabla u|^{2}\,d\mu}{\int u^{2}\,d\mu}.

Notice that the last equality holds from Proposition

. For any non-compact Ricci steady soliton conifold, we have the following theorem, which is a generalization of

, Proposition

.

Theorem A.20
.

Let
(X,p,d,f)\in\mathfrak{S}(n)
be non-compact. Then
\lambda=\frac{1}{4}
.

Proof.

First we show that
\lambda\leq 1/4
. This can be done by considering the test function
u(x):=e^{-\frac{1}{4}(1+\epsilon)d(p,x)}\phi(x)
, where
\phi
is a cutoff function with

on

Since the volume

for any

Next we compute on

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}e^{\frac{f}{2}}=\left(-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{4}\right)e^{\frac{f}{2}}\leq-\frac{1}{4}e^{\frac{f}{2}}.

Since

is locally bounded, the above equation also holds on

in the weak sense. Therefore, we have

by the same argument of
[

]
.

\displaystyle G(x,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x,y)\,dt.

Next, we recall the following definition.

Definition A.21
.

Let
(X,p,d,f)\in\mathfrak{S}(n)
be non-compact and
E
an end of

.
E
is said to be parabolic if it does not admit a positive

satisfying

\displaystyle h=1

on

and

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to E(\infty)}h(x)<1,

where

denotes the infinity of

. Otherwise,

It is clear from the existence of a positive Green’s function that

has at least one nonparabolic end.

Now we can follow the same proof as in
[
51
, Theorem
4.1
]
to prove the following result.

Theorem A.22
.

Let
(X,p,d,f)\in\mathfrak{S}(n)
be non-compact and nontrivial. Then
X
has only one end.

Proof.

We assume
X
has at least two ends and derive a contradiction. We first show
X
has at most one nonparabolic end. Indeed, if
X
has two nonparabolic end, one can follow the arguments of
[
43
, Theorem
21.1,21.3
]
to construct a positive, bounded, nonconstant, harmonic function
u
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}(u,u)=\int|\nabla u|^{2}\,d\mu<\infty.

However, this contradicts Corollary
A.18
. Hence,
X
has exactly one nonparabolic end
E
. Suppose
X
has more than one end, then we can choose
F
as a parabolic end.
For simplicity, we may assume that
X
has exactly two ends
E
and
F
.

Claim 1:

F
contains a geodesic ray
\gamma\subset F\cap\mathcal{R}
.

Actually, we fix a point
q\in\mathcal{R}\cap F

and a sequence of points
y_{i}\in\mathcal{R}\cap F
. Let
\alpha_{i}\subset\mathcal{R}
be a shortest geodesic connecting
q
and
y_{i}
, whose existence is guaranteed by item 4 of Definition A.1. Then

\alpha_{i}
converges to a geodesic
\gamma\subset F
, naturally parametrized by arc length, such that
\gamma(0)=q
. Since
q
is regular point, there exists a small constant
\epsilon
such that

\gamma([0,\epsilon])\subset\mathcal{R}
. In particular, at each point
y\in\gamma([0,\epsilon])
, each tangent space at
y
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By volume comparison and heat kernel estimate(cf.
[
34
, Theorem 4.5]
), for each sequence
r_{\alpha}\to 0^{+}
, the unit balls in tangent spaces associated to this sequence and points
y=\gamma(t)
varies continuously in Gromov-Hausdorff topology for
t\in[0.5\epsilon,\infty)
.
In particular, the volume of unit balls in tangent spaces is then a continuous function of
t
. Thus, by the gap property(Item 6 of Definition A.1), it is a constant independent of
t
. This means that for each
t\in[0.5\epsilon,\infty)
, a tangent space of
\gamma(t)
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, it follows from definition that
\gamma(t)
is a regular point, i.e.,
\gamma(t)\in\mathcal{R}
for any
t\geq 0
.
We define the Busemann function

\displaystyle\beta(x)=\lim_{t\to\infty}(t-d(x,\gamma(t))).

It follows from the Laplacian comparison that

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}\beta\geq-1,\quad|\nabla\beta|=1,

which in turn implies that

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}e^{\beta}\geq 0.

(A.7)

Claim 2:
Fix
p\in X
, for all
L>0
large enough, we have

\displaystyle|B(p,L)\cap E|_{\mu}\leq Ce^{L}.

(A.8)

On the end
E
, the function
d(p,\cdot)+\beta
is uniformly bounded. Therefore, it suffices to show that for some large
s>0
, we have

\displaystyle|\{s<-\beta<t\}\cap E|_{\mu}\leq Ce^{t},\quad\forall\;t>s.

(A.9)

Actually, by perturbing distance function and the high co-dimension assumption of
\mathcal{S}
(cf.
[
24
, Corollary B.3]
), for each small
\delta>0
, we can find an almost “tubular neighborhood”
T_{\delta}
of
\mathcal{S}
.
The distance from any point in
\partial T_{\delta}
to
\mathcal{S}
is comparable to
\delta
,
and the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
\partial T_{\delta}\cap K
is bounded by
C(K)\delta^{2}
for dimensional reason, where
K
is any compact set. Applying integration by parts away from
T_{\delta}
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{\{s<-\beta<t\}\backslash T_{\delta}}(\Delta_{f}e^{\beta})e^{-f}=\int_{\{-\beta=s\}\cap(E\backslash T_{\delta})}e^{\beta}e^{-f}-\int_{\{-\beta=t\}\cap(E\backslash T_{\delta})}e^{\beta}e^{-f}+\int_{\partial T_{\delta}\cap E}\langle\nabla\beta,\vec{n}\rangle e^{\beta}e^{-f},

where
\vec{n}
is the outward unit normal vector of
\partial T_{\delta}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle|\{\beta=-t\}\cap(E\backslash T_{\delta})|_{\mu}\leq e^{t-s}|\{\beta=-s\}\cap E|_{\mu}+C(E,s,t)\delta^{2}e^{t},

where
\mu
is the induced measure on the hypersurfaces. Integrating the above inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle|\{s<-\beta<t\}\cap(E\backslash T_{\delta})|_{\mu}\leq e^{t-s}|\{\beta=-s\}\cap E|_{\mu}+C(E,s,t)\delta^{2}e^{t}.

Letting
\delta\to 0
, we arrive at (
A.9
).
By choosing proper cutoff functions, integration by parts(cf.
[
24
, Proposition 2.17]
,
[
49
, Theorem 1.4]
) then implies that

\displaystyle|F\backslash B(p,L)|_{\mu}\leq Ce^{-L}.

(A.10)

Then we verbatim follow the proof in
[
51
]
. Define a cut-off function
\phi
with support in
B(p,2L)
such that
\phi=1
on
B(p,L)
and
|\nabla\phi|\leq CL^{-1}
. Using the fact
\lambda_{1}(\Delta_{f})=\frac{1}{4}
, and the volume estimate (
A.9
) and (
A.10
), integration by parts implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{X}\left\{\Delta_{f}e^{\beta}\right\}\phi^{2}e^{-f}=\int_{X}e^{\frac{1}{2}\beta}\left(\Delta_{f}e^{\frac{1}{2}\beta}+\frac{1}{4}e^{\frac{1}{2}\beta}\right)\phi^{2}e^{-f}\leq\int_{X}|\nabla\phi|^{2}e^{\beta}e^{-f}\leq\frac{C}{L}\to 0.

It then follows from the combination of (
A.7
) and the above inequality that on
\mathcal{R}
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}e^{\beta}=0,\quad\Delta_{f}\beta=-1\quad\text{and}\quad|\nabla\beta|=1.

It follows from Proposition
A.13
that
(X,d)
is isometric to
(Y\times\mathbb{R},d^{\prime}\times d_{g_{E}})
, where
(Y,d^{\prime})
is a Ricci steady soliton conifold. Since we assume
X
has at least two ends,
Y
must be compact. Therefore, it follows from Proposition
A.11
that
Rc_{Y}
and hence
Rc_{X}
are identically
0
on
\mathcal{R}
.

In sum, we obtain a contradiction, and the original conclusion holds.

∎

d_{g(\bar{t})}(z_{1},\bar{x})\geq\Lambda_{1}R(z_{1},\bar{t})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and
d_{g(\bar{t})}(z_{2},\bar{x})\geq\Lambda_{1}R(z_{2},\bar{t})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, then

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(x,t)\leq\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}R(x,t),

for any
(x,t)\in B_{g(\bar{t})}(\bar{x},\bar{L}R(\bar{x},\bar{t})^{-\frac{1}{2}})\times[\bar{t}-\bar{L}R(\bar{x},\bar{t})^{-1},\bar{t}]
, where
\bar{L}
is the constant in Theorem
B.2
.

Definition B.6

(
\epsilon
-symmetry of type-C)
.

The Ricci flow
(M^{n},g(t))_{t\leq\bar{t}}
with Assumption
1
and Assumption
3
is called
\epsilon
-symmetric of type-C at time
\bar{t}
if
M
is diffeomorphic to
S^{n}
and there exists a compact domain
D\subset M
and a family of time-independent vector fields
\mathcal{U}=\{U^{(a)}:1\leq a\leq{n\choose 2}\}
which are defined on an open subset containing
D
such that the following statements hold:

•

The domain
D
is a disjoint union of two domains
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, each of which is diffeomorphic to
B^{n}
.

•

\lambda_{1}(x,\bar{t})<\theta_{0}R(\bar{x},\bar{t})
for all points
x\in M\setminus D
.

•

\lambda_{1}(x,\bar{t})>\frac{1}{2}\theta_{0}R(x,\bar{t})
for all points
x\in D
.

•

\partial D_{1}
and
\partial D_{2}
are leaves of Hamilton’s CMC foliation of
(M,g(\bar{t}))
.

•

For each
x\in M\setminus D
, the point
(x,\bar{t})
is
\epsilon
-symmetric in the sense of Definition
B.1
.

•

The Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{U^{(a)}}(g(t))
satisfies for each
k\in\{1,2\}
the estimate

\sup_{D_{k}\times[\bar{t}-\rho_{k}^{2},\bar{t}]}\sum_{l=0}^{2}\sum_{a=1}^{{n\choose 2}}\rho_{k}^{2l}\big|D^{l}(\mathcal{L}_{U^{(a)}}(g(t)))|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2},

where
\rho_{k}^{-2}:=\sup_{x\in D_{k}}R(x,\bar{t})
.

•

For each
k\in\{1,2\}
, the following property holds.
If
\Sigma\subset D_{k}
is a leaf of Hamilton’s CMC foliation of
(M,g(\bar{t}))
that has distance at most
50\,r_{\mathrm{neck}}(\partial D_{k})
from
\partial D_{k}
, then

\sup_{\Sigma}\sum_{a=1}^{{n\choose 2}}\rho_{k}^{-2}|\langle U^{(a)},\nu\rangle|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2},

where
\nu
is the unit normal vector to
\Sigma
in
(M,g(\bar{t}))
and
r_{\mathrm{neck}}(\partial D_{k})
is defined by the identity
\mathrm{area}_{g(\bar{t})}(\partial D_{k})=\mathrm{area}_{g_{S^{n-1}}}(S^{n-1})r_{\mathrm{neck}}(\partial D_{k})^{n-1}
.

\bullet

For each
k\in\{1,2\}
, the following property holds.
If
\Sigma\subset D_{k}
is a leaf of Hamilton’s CMC foliation of
(M,g(\bar{t}))
that has distance at most
50\,r_{\mathrm{neck}}(\partial D_{k})
from
\partial D_{k}
, then

\sum_{a,b=1}^{{n\choose 2}}\bigg|\delta_{ab}-\mathrm{area}_{g(\bar{t})}(\Sigma)^{-\frac{n+1}{n-1}}\int_{\Sigma}\langle U^{(a)},U^{(b)}\rangle_{g(\bar{t})}\,d\mu_{g(\bar{t})}\bigg|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.
