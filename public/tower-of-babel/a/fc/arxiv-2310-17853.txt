Rahman polynomials


Abstract
Two very closely related Rahman polynomials are constructed explicitly as the left eigenvectors of certain multi-dimensional discrete time Markov chain operators K n ( i ) ​ ( 𝒙 , 𝒚 , N ) K_{n}^{(i)}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}};N) , i = 1 , 2 i=1,2 . They are convolutions of an n + 1 n+1 -nomial distribution W n ​ ( 𝒙 , N ) W_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}};N) and an n n -tuple of binomial distributions ∏ i W 1 ​ ( x i , N ) \prod_{i}W_{1}(x_{i};N) . The one for the original Rahman polynomials is K n ( 1 ) ​ ( 𝒙 , 𝒚 , N ) = ∑ 𝒛 W n ​ ( 𝒙 − 𝒛 , N − ∑ i z i ) ​ ∏ i W 1 ​ ( z i , y i ) K_{n}^{(1)}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}};N)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{z}};N-\sum_{i}z_{i})\prod_{i}W_{1}(z_{i};y_{i}) . The closely related one is K n ( 2 ) ​ ( 𝒙 , 𝒚 , N ) = ∑ 𝒛 W n ​ ( 𝒙 − 𝒛 , N − ∑ i y i ) ​ ∏ i W 1 ​ ( z i , y i ) K_{n}^{(2)}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}};N)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}W_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{z}};N-\sum_{i}y_{i})\prod_{i}W_{1}(z_{i};y_{i}) . The original Markov chain was introduced and discussed by Hoare, Rahman and Grünbaum as a multivariable version of the known soluble single variable one. The new one is a generalisation of that of Odake and myself. The anticipated solubility of the model gave Rahman polynomials the prospect of the first multivariate hypergeometric function of Aomoto-Gelfand type connected with solvable dynamics. The promise is now realised. The n 2 n^{2} system parameters { u i ​ j } \{u_{i\,j}\} of the Rahman polynomials are completely determined. These u i ​ j u_{i\,j} ’s are irrational functions of the original system parameters, the probabilities of the multinomial and binomial distributions.

Let us start with a brief review of the general framework of

n
-variables.

2.1
Discrete time Markov chains of
n
-variables

The main actor is the transition matrix
\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
which governs the
evolution of the probability distribution function
\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell)
over
\Xi
, a finite set,

\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell+1)=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{y}};\ell),\quad\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell)\geq 0,\quad\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell)=1,\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

at time step
\ell
to
\ell+1
.
That is,
\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
specifies
the one step transition probability from point

{\boldsymbol{y}}
to
{\boldsymbol{x}}
which belong to a finite set
\Xi
, a subset of the non-negative integer lattice
in
n
-dimensions
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})>0,\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\quad{\boldsymbol{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}),\quad\qquad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Xi\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n},

satisfying

\displaystyle\text{\bf conservation of probability:}\quad\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=1,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\Longrightarrow\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell+1)=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell)=1.

Let us impose the condition that
\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell)
does not explode and
approaches to a
stationary
distribution
\pi_{S}
at large
\ell
;

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell)=\pi_{S}({\boldsymbol{x}}).

The existence of the stationary distribution is guaranteed
when
\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})

satisfies the following
reversibility
or
detailed balance
condition
with a certain positive
reversible distribution

\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})
,

reversible:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{x}})\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\quad\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})>0,\quad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Xi.

(2.2)

Deviding the above reversibility condition by
\sqrt{\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{y}})}
,
one obtains a real symmetric matrix
\mathcal{T}_{n}
;

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})}}\,\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\sqrt{\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{y}})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{y}})}}\,\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{x}})\sqrt{\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})}

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{x}}),\qquad{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Xi.

(2.4)

In other words, the transition matrix
\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
is related to a
real positive symmetric matrix
\mathcal{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
by a similarity transformation
in terms of
\sqrt{\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})}
.
This means the following

Proposition 2.1

The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
are real and the range of the spectrum is

-1<\text{The eigenvalues of }\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\leq 1,

(2.5)

and the eigenvector of the maximal eigenvalue 1 is the
the reversible distribution

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{x}})=\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}}).

(2.6)

The maximal eigenvalue is simple.
These are due to Perron’s theorem on positive matrices.
As
\ell
becomes large, all the components of
\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell)

belonging to the eigenvalues other than 1 diminish and

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{P}({\boldsymbol{x}};\ell)=\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}}).

In other words, the reversible distribution is stationary.

The above maximal eigenvector relation (
2.6
) also means the following

Remark 2.2

A constant is the left eigenvector of
\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
with the maximal
eigenvalue 1;

v_{\boldsymbol{0}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\equiv 1,\quad\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})v_{\boldsymbol{0}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=1=v_{\boldsymbol{0}}({\boldsymbol{y}}).

By generalising the
Remark
2.2
, one arrives at the following

Theorem 2.3

The complete set of left eigenvectors
\{v_{\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\}
of

\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
,

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})v_{\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\kappa({\boldsymbol{m}})v_{\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{y}}),\quad-1<\kappa({\boldsymbol{m}})\leq 1=\kappa({\boldsymbol{0}}),\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\Xi^{d},

(2.7)

provides the complete sets of eigenvectors of
\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})

and
\mathcal{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
;

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})v_{\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\kappa({\boldsymbol{m}})v_{\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\Xi^{d},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Xi}\mathcal{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})v_{\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\sqrt{\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{y}})}=\kappa({\boldsymbol{m}})v_{\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\sqrt{\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})},\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\Xi^{d}.

(2.9)

Here

is the dual set of
\Xi
and
\#(\Xi^{d})=\#(\Xi)
.
The left eigenvectors are orthogonal to each other with the orthogonality weight
function
\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})
;

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Xi}v_{\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{x}})v_{{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\pi_{R}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0,\quad{\boldsymbol{m}}\neq{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime},

(2.10)

since
\mathcal{K}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
has some parameters and for generic values of the parameters,
the eigenvalues are not degenerate.
