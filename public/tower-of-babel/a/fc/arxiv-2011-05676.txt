A ( 2 + ε ) (2+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for preemptive weighted flow time on a single machine
Lars Rohwedder, Andreas Wiese

Abstract
Weighted flow time is a fundamental and very well-studied objective function in scheduling. In this paper, we study the setting of a single machine with preemptions. The input consists of a set of jobs, characterized by their processing times, release times, and weights and we want to compute a (possibly preemptive) schedule for them. The objective is to minimize the sum of the weighted flow times of the jobs, where the flow time of a job is the time between its release date and its completion time. It had been a long-standing open problem to find a polynomial time O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -approximation algorithm for this setting. In a recent break-through result, Batra, Garg, and Kumar (FOCS 2018) found such an algorithm if the input data are polynomially bounded integers, and Feige, Kulkarni, and Li (SODA 2019) presented a black-box reduction to this setting. The resulting approximation ratio is a (not explicitly stated) constant which is at least 10.000 10.000 . In this paper we improve this ratio to 2 + ε 2+\varepsilon . The algorithm by Batra, Garg, and Kumar (FOCS 2018) reduces the problem to Demand MultiCut on trees and solves the resulting instances via LP-rounding and a dynamic program. Instead, we first reduce the problem to a (different) geometric problem while losing only a factor 1 + ε 1+\varepsilon , and then solve its resulting instances up to a factor of 2 + ε 2+\varepsilon by a dynamic program. In particular, our reduction ensures certain structural properties, thanks to which we do not need LP-rounding methods. We believe that our result makes substantial progress towards finding a PTAS for weighted flow time on a single machine.

Introduction

Weighted flow time is a fundamental and well studied objective in
the scheduling literature, e.g.,
[
BGK18
,
FKL19
,
BP04
,
BP03
,
BP14
,
KTW99
,
AT18
,
CK02
,
CKZ01
]
.
We are given
a set of jobs
J
where each job
j\in J
is characterized by a
release time
r_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, a processing time
p_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, and a
weight
w_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
. In a computed schedule, the flowtime
F_{j}

of a job

is the difference between its completion time and its
release date
r_{j}
. The goal is to minimize
\sum_{j\in J}w_{j}F_{j}
.

In this paper, we study the setting of a single machines in which
we allow to preempt jobs (and resume them later). Note that without
preemptions the problem cannot even be approximated with a factor
of
O(n^{1/2-\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0

[
KTW99
]
.
It is known by the work of Chekuri and Khanna
[
CK02
]

that for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation
in quasi-polynomial time (QPTAS), assuming quasi-polynomially bounded
input data. In contrast to this, it had been a long-standing
important open problem whether a constant factor approximation can
be computed in
polynomial
time
[
SW99
]
.
In a breakthrough result, Batra,
Garg, and Kumar
[
BGK18
]
presented such an
algorithm with pseudopolynomial running time. While for many
scheduling problems one can assume the input data to be polynomially
bounded via straight-forward rounding of the input etc., this is
not the case for weighted flow time. However, Feige, Kulkarni, and
Li
[
FKL19
]
gave a non-trivial black-box reduction to
this setting which completely settles the mentioned long-standing
open question (and also yields a QPTAS for arbitrary input data).

The algorithm in
[
BGK18
]
first reduces a
given problem instance to a clean graph problem, the
Demand
MultiCut problem on trees
. This reduction loses a factor of
32

in the approximation ratio. Then, the authors present an approximation
algorithm for the resulting instance of
Demand MultiCut
.
To this end, they split it into two subinstances and solve the first
one by rounding a linear program (LP) and the second one with a dynamic
program (DP). Their approximation ratio for the first subinstance
is
24+8\beta
where
\beta=O(1)
is the approximation ratio of
an algorithm by Chan, Grant, Könemann, and Sharpe
[
CGKS12
]

(which is invoked as a subroutine); the constant
\beta
is not explicitly
stated in
[
CGKS12
]
. The DP for the second
subinstance crucially exploits the hierarchical structure given by
the tree. Its approximation ratio is a constant which is not explicitly
stated in
[
BGK18
]
, but is at least
512
.
Hence, the overall approximation ratio is at least
32\cdot(536+8\beta)\geq 10.000
.
While one could try to optimize this constant, it is not
clear how to avoid to lose substantial factors in several parts of
the algorithm, e.g., the factor
32
in the reduction to
Demand
MultiCut
, further constant factors when solving the two subinstances
mentioned above, and also the dependence on
\beta
.

1.1
Our contribution

In this paper, we present a polynomial time
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation
algorithm for weighted flow time on a single machine. We first reduce
the problem to a geometric problem (rather than
Demand MultiCut
).
Then we solve the resulting instance of this problem by a dynamic
program. Our reduction is almost loss-less, i.e., it loses only a
factor of
1+\varepsilon
, and our DP has an approximation ratio
of only
2+\varepsilon
which leads to an approximation ratio of

2+\varepsilon
overall.

In our geometric problem, the input consists of a set of non-overlapping
axis-parallel rectangles of unit height and a set of rays that are
all vertical and oriented downwards, see Figure
1
.
Each rectangle has a cost and a capacity, each ray has a demand. The
goal is to select rectangles of minimium total cost such that for
each ray, the total capacity of the selected rectangles intersecting
it is at least the demand of the ray. For technical reasons there
are some local dependencies between rectangles, that is, some rectangles
can only be selected when another rectangle of the same
size directly to its left is selected as well.

In the instances obtained by our reduction, the rectangles are arranged
in a hierarchical structure given by a hierarchical decomposition
of the
x
-axis. More precisely, the projection of each rectangle
to the
x
-axis concides with a cell of this hierarchical decomposition.
Moreover, when we traverse each ray from its respective initial point
on, the widths of the rectangles hit by the ray are monotone (non-increasing).
This hierarchical structure is crucial for our dynamic program (similarly
to the tree-structure in
[
BGK18
]
). In particular,
we manage to obtain this important structure while losing only a factor
of
1+\varepsilon
in the reduction.

The hatched rectangles form an example solution.
The capacities and costs of the rectangles and the demands

Importantly, in contrast to
[
BGK18
]

we can solve our instances of this geometric problem by dynamic programming
only, and do not require the LP rounding algorithm from
[
CGKS12
]

or a similar procedure (with additional constant factor losses). The
intuitive reason is that, translated to our geometric visualization,
the instances of
Demand MultiCut
described in
[
BGK18
]

introduce vertical line segments, rather than rays, and the algorithm
in
[
BGK18
]
needs LP-rounding for a certain
type of (intuitively short) line segments, which we can completely
avoid. In our DP, we translate some ideas from
[
BGK18
]

to our geometric problem. However, our routine is significantly more
involved than the DP in
[
BGK18
]
due to the
higher complexity of our geometric problem (compared to
Demand
MultiCut on trees
), and since it is designed to optimize the
approximation ratio of
2+\varepsilon
incurred by it.

Our algorithm has pseudo-polynomial running time. With the black-box
reduction in
[
FKL19
, Section 4]
we turn
it into a polynomial time algorithm, while losing only a factor of

1+\varepsilon
.

Theorem 1
.

There is a polynomial time
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm
for the problem of minimizing weighted flow time on a single machine
in the preemptive setting.

We believe that our result is a crucial step forward in the search
of a PTAS for weighted flow time on a single machine. In particular,
a possible approach for constructing a PTAS could be to use our reduction
to the geometric problem above and develop a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation
algorithm for the resulting instances.

Reduction to geometric problem

We start with some standard transformations to simplify
the instance of weighted flow time. We assume w.l.o.g. that
1/\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}

and
\min_{j}r_{j}=0
. Moreover, we can assume that
\max_{j}r_{j}\leq\sum_{j}p_{j}

since otherwise we can split the given instance into independent subinstances.
Recall that
P
is defined as the ratio
\max_{j}p_{j}/\min_{j}p_{j}

By scaling the input values and rounding,
we can also assume that
\min_{j}p_{j}=1
,

\max_{j}p_{j}=P

1\leq w_{j}\leq O_{\varepsilon}(n^{2}P)

We define

Hence, we can assume

Then the problem is modeled by the following integer program

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in J\\

\displaystyle x_{j,t}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall j\in J,t\in\{r_{j},\dotsc,T\}

Given a feasible schedule, one can easily obtain a feasible solution
to (IP) with the same cost following the intuition for the variables

Also, one can show that any feasible solution to (IP) can be translated

It is not clear how to approximate (IP) directly in polynomial time
and how to make use of the visualization above for this. Therefore,
we will give a randomized reduction of (IP) to a different (and in
particular more structured) integer program (IP2) with the following
relation.

1.

Any solution for (IP) can be transformed into a solution for (IP2)
such that the objective value increases at most by a factor
1+O(\varepsilon)

in expectation.

2.

Any solution for (IP2) can be transformed into a solution for (IP)
with the same objective value.

In particular, in (IP2) we will define rectangles for each
job
j
which intuitively result from merging certain sets of
adjacent squares of
j
. Morever, these rectangles will be aligned
with a hierarchical grid which will help us later to compute a cheap
solution to (IP2) by a dynamic program.

Hierarchical grid.

Our hierarchical grid has
O_{\varepsilon}(\log T)=O_{\varepsilon}(\log nP)

levels. Each grid cell
C
of some level
\ell
corresponds to some
interval
[t_{1},t_{2})
with
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. We define

\mathrm{beg}(C):=t_{1}
,
\mathrm{end}(C):=t_{2}
, and
\mathrm{len}(C):=t_{2}-t_{1}
.
Each cell
C
has
K:=(2/\varepsilon)^{1/\varepsilon}
children
cells of level
\ell+1
, unless
\ell
is the maximum level
\ell_{\max}

of the hierarchy in which case
C
does not have any children cells.
There will be exactly one grid cell of level
0
. The grid is parametrized
by two random variables
\mathrm{off}_{x},\mathrm{off}_{y}
. Intuitively, we give the
grid a horizontal shift with some random offset
\mathrm{off}_{x}
. Also, we
choose the size of the unique cell of level
0
randomly via an offset

\mathrm{off}_{y}
.

Formally, we define
\ell_{\max}
to be the minimal value
k

such that
K^{k-2}\geq T
which will ensure that later the grid cells

C
of level
\ell_{\max}
satisfy that
\mathrm{len}(C)\in[1,K)
. We
choose both
\mathrm{off}_{y}\in\{(2/\varepsilon)^{0},(2/\varepsilon)^{1},\dotsc,(2/\varepsilon)^{1/\varepsilon-1}\}

and
\mathrm{off}_{x}\in\{-K^{\ell_{\max}-1}+1,-K^{\ell_{\max}-1}+2,\dotsc,0\}

uniformly at random. We define that the unique grid cell of
level
0
corresponds to the interval
[\mathrm{off}_{x},\mathrm{off}_{x}+\mathrm{off}_{y}K^{\ell_{\max}})

which contains
[0,T)
(since
T\leq K^{\ell_{\max}-2}\leq K^{\ell_{\max}}-K^{\ell_{\max}-1}\leq\mathrm{off}_{y}K^{\ell_{\max}}+\mathrm{off}_{x}
).
Thus, we can assume w.l.o.g. that no job is processed outside
[\mathrm{off}_{x},\mathrm{off}_{x}+\mathrm{off}_{y}K^{\ell_{\max}})
.
Inductively, for each grid cell
C
with
\mathrm{len}(C)\geq K\cdot\mathrm{off}_{y}

of some level
\ell
, we introduce
K
child grid cells of level

\ell+1
, one for each interval

\left[\mathrm{beg}(C)+\frac{i}{K}\mathrm{len}(C),\ \mathrm{beg}(C)+\frac{i+1}{K}\mathrm{len}(C)\right),\quad i=0,\dotsc,K-1.

By construction, the interval of each grid cell of level
\ell
has
length
\mathrm{off}_{y}\cdot K^{\ell_{\max}-\ell}
. Denote by
\mathcal{C}
the set
of all grid cells (of all levels). For each
C\in\mathcal{C}
, denote by
\ell(C)

its level. It follows that
\ell_{\max}=\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\ell(C)
.

Segments of jobs.

For each job
j
we want to define a set of
O_{\varepsilon}(\log nP)

segments
\mathrm{Seg}(j)
which form a partition of
[r_{j},T)
,
see Figure
3
. We will associate each segment

S\in\mathrm{Seg}(j)
with some grid cell
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
S\subseteq C

and denote by
\mathrm{Seg}(j,C)
the segments in
\mathrm{Seg}(j)
associated
with
C
. We will ensure that all segments in
\mathrm{Seg}(j,C)
are aligned
with the grid cells of level
\ell(C)+2
and in particular all have
the same size. We will also ensure that for each
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
the union of the segments in
\mathrm{Seg}(j,C)
forms an interval that is

right-aligned with
C
, i.e., it holds that
\bigcup_{S\in\mathrm{Seg}(j,C)}S=[s,\mathrm{end}(C))

for some
s\in C
.

Formally, consider a job
j
. We construct a sequence of cells
C_{\ell_{\max}},C_{\ell_{\max}-1},\dotsc,C_{0}

in levels
\ell_{\max},\ell_{\max}-1,\dotsc,0
such that the union
of these cells contains
[r_{j},T)
. The cells are chosen as follows.
Cell
C_{\ell_{\max}}
is identical to the cell of of level
\ell_{\max}

that contains
r_{j}
. Suppose we have chosen cells
C_{\ell_{\max}},\dotsc,C_{k}
.
Then we define
C_{k-1}
as the cell of level
k-1

that contains
\mathrm{end}(C_{k})
(see Figure
3
); observe
that this implies
\mathrm{end}(C_{k})<\mathrm{end}(C_{k-1})
. For each
k\in\{\ell_{\max}-1,\dotsc,1\}

consider the interval
[\mathrm{end}(C_{k+1}),\mathrm{end}(C_{k}))
, and
[r_{j},\mathrm{end}(C_{\ell_{\max}}))

for
k=\ell_{\max}
. The length of this interval must be an integer
multiple of
\mathrm{len}(C_{k+1})
,
or 1 if
k=\ell_{\max}
. We subdivide this interval into segments
of length
\mathrm{len}(C_{k+2})
each if
k\leq\ell_{\max}-2
, and
of length 1 each if
k>\ell_{\max}-2
.
We define that these segments form the set
\mathrm{Seg}(j,C_{k})
.
It follows by construction that each of them coincides with a cell
of level
k+2
if
k\leq\ell_{\max}-2
, and otherwise has length 1.

For each job
j
we define
\mathrm{Seg}(j):=\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Seg}(j,C)
.
In the following lemma, we will prove some properties of these segments.
For this, for each job
j
let
F_{j}^{*}
denote the flowtime of

j
in
\mathrm{OPT}

\mathrm{Seg}(j,C_{j}^{*})

.

For each job

,

Proof.

By construction the first four properties follow immediately. The non-trivial property we need to show
is the fifth.
First we will show that probability at least

1-3\varepsilon
it holds that

F_{j}^{*}\notin[\varepsilon/2\cdot\mathrm{off}_{y}K^{k},2/\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{off}_{y}K^{k}]\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(1)

Note that if (
1
) is not true for
k
then
F_{j}^{*}

and
\mathrm{off}_{y}K^{k}
differ by a factor of at most
2/\varepsilon
.
In other words, it suffices to show that with the mentioned probability
we have

|\log_{2/\varepsilon}F_{j}^{*}-\log_{2/\varepsilon}(\mathrm{off}_{y}K^{k})|>1\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Notice that
\log_{2/\varepsilon}(\mathrm{off}_{y}K^{k})\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence,
the statement above is implied by

\displaystyle\lfloor\log_{2/\varepsilon}F_{j}^{*}\rfloor

\displaystyle\neq\log_{2/\varepsilon}(\mathrm{off}_{y})+k/\varepsilon-1,

\displaystyle\lfloor\log_{2/\varepsilon}F_{j}^{*}\rfloor

\displaystyle\neq\log_{2/\varepsilon}(\mathrm{off}_{y})+k/\varepsilon,\phantom{-1}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\lfloor\log_{2/\varepsilon}F_{j}^{*}\rfloor

Lemma 9

Given a family

We build a dynamic programming table that contains an entry for each

for

and

\mathcal{R}(I)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(Q)

If there is no such

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

We check for each

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

a special symbol indicating that the choice is infeasible.

Now let

be an inner node and let again

be the path from

. Let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\in\chi_{Q}
. In the following we describe
how to compute the table entry for
(v,\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
. Let
u_{1},\dotsc,u_{K}

be the children of
v
and
Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{K}
the extension of

Q
to each child. For each

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{i}\in\chi_{Q_{i}}

be the set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{i}
with

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{i}\cap\mathcal{R}(Q)\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

(6)

for which the set
\mathcal{S}_{i}
stored in

.
As an overall solution

that minimizes

. We define

by memoization:

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\in\chi_{\{r\}}
,

\mathcal{S}_{i}

,

is the

, and

\mathcal{S}_{\{r\}}^{*}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.
Likewise, we set

. Each
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is
derived from selections at the children of

. In the same way
we recursively define

on each path

. Indeed,

forms a consistent solution:

is

.
By construction we have that

Moreover, by Equation (

and thus (

) of Definition

) of Definition

.
To this end let

. We need

be any extension of

ending in a leaf. Then also

be the cheapest consistent solution. We show inductively that for

Q
from the root to a vertex

.
It follows that
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is of minimal cost, because

is at most the cost of the entry computed for

If

Now assume that

,

.
Since the rectangle sets in both sides contain the same rectanges

The rectangles at entry

\mathcal{S}_{i}

over all entries

Hence,

c(\mathcal{S})\leq c(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{S}_{K})\\
\leq c(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q})+c(\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\supseteq Q_{1}}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{R}(Q))+\cdots+c(\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\supseteq Q_{K}}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{R}(Q))\leq c(\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\supseteq Q}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}).

Quasi-polynomial
size consistent solution

In this section, we prove a weaker variant of Lemma
10

which already introduces several of our key techniques and leads to
a quasi-polynomial
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation. More precisely,
in this section we relax the condition in Lemma
10

on the size of each set
\chi_{Q}
with
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
to
|\chi_{Q}|\leq(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))}

and also the running time to
(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))}
.

For each rectangle
R
we define a
density

\rho_{R}
which
approximately describes its cost-efficiency
c_{R}/p_{R}
. Instead
of using this ratio directly, we define
\rho_{R}:=(1+\varepsilon)^{k}

for the value

with
(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\leq c_{R}/p_{R}<(1+\varepsilon)^{k+1}
.
In this way,
\rho_{R}
differs from
c_{R}/p_{R}
only by a small
factor of
1+\varepsilon
, but we ensure that there are only
O_{\varepsilon}(\log nP)

different densities overall. Recall that we defined the set
\mathcal{R}(j,C)

for combinations of a job
j
and cell
C\in\mathcal{C}
(which contains
all rectangles in
\mathcal{R}(C)
corresponding to
j
). By construction,
almost all of these rectangles have the same cost
c_{R}
,
apart from the leftmost rectangle in
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
whose cost might be
higher. Thus, we can describe the densities of the rectangles
in
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
by only two values that we denote by
\rho_{j,C},\rho^{\prime}_{j,C}
.
Formally, let

\mathcal{R}(j,C)

\rho_{j,C}:=\rho_{R}

and

\rho{}_{j,C}:=\infty
.
Using these values

, all rectangles have the
same capacity, and

.

.

There are at most
O_{\varepsilon}(\log(nP))

\tau

. Moreover,

Consider a type

.
Moreover, the costs of different rectangles

Recall, the rectangles in
\mathcal{R}(j,C)

Let

\mathrm{len}(C)/K^{2}

. The latter applies

Since this is at least

Let
\mathcal{R}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{R}

j
such that
R\in\mathcal{R}(j,C)
and

\mathcal{R}^{*}
contains exactly the first
s^{\prime}
rectangles from
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
.
We define
B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime}):=\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}^{*}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})}c_{R}
.

We define now our solution
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. For a cell
C
and a type

\tau
the solution
\bigcup_{s^{\prime}}\mathcal{R}^{*}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})
can be very
complicated. Instead, we construct an algorithm GreedySelect which,
intuitively, computes a simple solution of total cost at most
(2+\varepsilon)\sum_{s^{\prime}}B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})

that covers as much of each ray
L(I)
as the rectangles in
\bigcup_{s^{\prime}}\mathcal{R}^{*}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})
.
For computing it, we need to know only
\{B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})\}_{s^{\prime}}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
will consist of the union of all these simple solutions
for all cells
C\in\mathcal{Q}
and types
\tau
and for each path
Q\in\mathcal{Q}

we will simply define
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}:=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}(Q)
. Then there are only

(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))}
options for
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}
since
it depends only on the
O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))
budgets
\{B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})\}_{C,s^{\prime}:C\in Q}
.

Procedure GreedySelect.

Formally, the input of GreedySelect consists of a cell
C
, a type

\tau=(\rho,\rho^{\prime},s)
, and for each
s^{\prime}\in\{1,\dotsc,s\}
a budget

B(s^{\prime})
(for the purpose of this section we can think this value
as
B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})
). It selects rectangles of total cost at
most
(2+\varepsilon)\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})
from the sets in
\left\{\mathcal{R}(j,C)\right\}_{j}

that are of type
\tau
. We will denote by GreedySelect
(C,\tau,\left\{(B(s^{\prime})\right\}_{s^{\prime}})

the computed rectangles.

Note that for two jobs
j,j^{\prime}
for which
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
and
\mathcal{R}(j^{\prime},C)

are of the same type
\tau
, the rectangles in these two sets look
identical, up to a vertical shift (and they might have different costs).
We first define a fractional
solution greedily. For each job
j
for which
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
is of type

\tau
and each
r\in\{1,\dotsc,s\}
we define a value
x_{j,r}

which denotes the fractional extent to which we select the
r
-th
rectangle in
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
. Initially, we define
x_{j,r}=0
for each
such variable.
For each
s^{\prime}=s,s-1,\dotsc,1
we start a phase in which we consider
the jobs for which there is a
\tilde{s}\geq s^{\prime}
such that buying
the first
\tilde{s}
rectangles in
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
costs at most
B(\tilde{s})
.
Observe that through the phases more and more jobs satisfy this condition
and hence if a job is available in one phase then it will also be
available in all future phases. We sort the corresponding jobs decreasingly
by
\prec
(so in particular non-increasingly by their release dates)
and we consider them in this order. Note that in our graphical
visualization this orders the jobs from bottom to top. When we consider
a job
j
, for all
r\leq s^{\prime}
we increase
x_{j,r}
simultaneously
by the same amount until either
x_{j,r}=1
for each such
r
or
we paid exactly
(1+\varepsilon)B_{s^{\prime}}
in this phase (fractionally).
Hence, the fractional cost is
(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})
by construction.

We chose our ordering for the jobs since our rays are vertical
and downwards oriented and, hence, if a rectangle is located further
down, it intersects with more rays whose demands it helps to satisfy.
In particular, here we crucially exploit that for each interval
I

the corresponding object
L(I)
is a vertical ray, rather than e.g.,
a line segment. Using this, we will show that if
B(s^{\prime})\geq B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})

for each
s^{\prime}
then our fractional solution
\left\{x_{j,r}\right\}_{j,r}

covers as much from each ray
I\in\mathcal{I}
as the rectangles in
\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}(C)

of type
\tau
. In the output of GreedySelect we select for each
job
j
and each
r\in\{1,\dotsc,s\}
the
r
-th rectangle in
\mathcal{R}(j,C)

if
x_{j,r}>0
, i.e., intuitively we round up each variable
x_{j,r}

with
x_{j,r}>0
. We will show that for each
r
there is at most
one job
j
such that
0<x_{j,r}<1
and hence we pay additionally
at most
\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})
due to the rounding.

Lemma 14
.

Suppose that for each
s^{\prime}
it holds that

B(s^{\prime})\geq B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})
. Then for each interval
I\in\mathcal{I}

it holds that

p(\mathrm{GreedySelect}(C,\tau,\left\{(B(s^{\prime})\right\}_{s^{\prime}})\cap\mathcal{R}(I))\geq p\left(\bigcup_{s^{\prime}}\mathcal{R}^{*}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{R}(I)\right)

and additionally
c(\mathrm{GreedySelect}(C,\tau,\left\{(B(s^{\prime})\right\}_{s^{\prime}}))\leq(2+\varepsilon)\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})
.

Proof.

First, we claim that already the fractional solution covers as much
from each
I\in\mathcal{I}
as
\mathcal{R}^{*}
. To this end, let
I\in\mathcal{I}
and
r\in\mathbb{N}

such that
L(I)
intersects the
r
-th rectangle of each job of
type
\tau
in
\mathcal{R}(C)
. We want to show that the fractional solution
covers
L(I)
at least as much as
\mathcal{R}^{*}
.

We say that a job
j
is of
kind
k

if
k
is the largest
value
k^{\prime}
such that buying the first
k^{\prime}
rectangles of
\mathcal{R}(j,C)

costs at most
B(k^{\prime})
and one of the first
k^{\prime}
rectangles of
\mathcal{R}(j,C)

intersects
L(I)
. Observe that some jobs are of no kind at all,
however, such segments are not selected in
\mathcal{R}^{*}
(since
B(s^{\prime})\geq B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})

for each
s^{\prime}
) or do not intersect
L(I)
. In other words, it suffices
to show that GreedySelect covers
L(I)
at least as much as
\mathcal{R}^{*}

with rectangles that are of some kind
k
. Intuitively, if
j
is
of kind
k
then
k
is the earliest round in which we might have
selected rectangles of
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
.

Let
\hat{s}^{\prime}
be the minimal value
s^{\prime}
such that after iteration

s^{\prime}
(which refers to the value of
s^{\prime}
in this iteration; recall
that these values decrease through the iterations) the algorithm has
selected (possibly partially in previous iterations) the first
s^{\prime}\geq r

rectangles of all jobs of kind
k\geq s^{\prime}
. Regarding rectangles of
kinds
k
with
r\leq k<\hat{s}^{\prime}
, we know that in each iteration

k<\hat{s}^{\prime}
GreedySelect spends by a factor of
1+\varepsilon
more
budget than
\mathcal{R}^{*}
(since
B(k)\geq B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,k)
). Since
we consider rectangles
R
of the same type
\tau
, their ratios

c_{R}/p_{R}
can only differ by a factor of
(1+\varepsilon)
. In
particular, the rectangles of all kinds
k<\hat{s}^{\prime}
selected by
the algorithm have a total (fractional) size that is at least as large
as the corresponding rectangles in
\mathcal{R}^{*}
. Also, we sort the corresponding
jobs decreasingly by
\prec
and the rays
L(I)
are vertical and
downward oriented. This implies that
p(\mathrm{GreedySelect}(C,\tau,\left\{(B(s^{\prime})\right\}_{s^{\prime}})\cap\mathcal{R}(I))\geq p\left(\bigcup_{s^{\prime}}\mathcal{R}^{*}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{R}(I)\right)

for each
I\in\mathcal{I}
.

By construction, the cost of the fractional solution is at most
(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})
.
We argue that the cost increases by at most another
\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})

when we round up the fractional solution. To this end, we claim that
in the fractional solution for each kind
k
and each
r\leq k
there
can be at most one job
j
of kind
k
such that for its
r
-th
rectangle it holds that
0<x_{j,r}<1
. The claim is clearly true
before the first iteration. Suppose that it is true after the iteration
that corresponds to some value
k^{\prime}
. Suppose that in the next iteration
corresponding to
k^{\prime}-1
a value
x_{j,r}
is increased such that
before
x_{j,r}=0
. Let
k
be the kind of the corresponding job

j
. Assume by contradiction that there is some other variable
x_{j^{\prime},r}

with
0<x_{j^{\prime},r}<1
corresponding to some other job
j^{\prime}
of kind

k
. If
j\prec j^{\prime}
then the algorithm would have increased
x_{j^{\prime},r}

instead of
x_{j,r}
in this iteration
k^{\prime}-1
. If
j^{\prime}\prec j
then
in the previous iteration in which
x_{j^{\prime},r}
was increased, it would
have increased
x_{j,r}
instead. Also, note that if a job
j
is
of kind
k
then always
x_{j,r}=0
for each
r>k
.

When we round up the fractional solution, then for each kind
k

and each
r\leq k
we round up at most one variable
x_{j,r}
for
a job
j
of kind
k
. The total cost of rounding up all these rectangles
for this kind
k
is bounded by
B(k)
. Hence, the total cost of
rounding up is bounded by
\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})
which yields a total cost
of
(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})+\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})
as claimed.
∎

Definition of consistent solution.

As mentioned above, we define the set of all rectangles
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
in our solution by

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{C}\bigcup_{\tau}\mathrm{GreedySelect}(C,\tau,\left\{B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})\right\}_{s^{\prime}})
.
For each path
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
we define
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}:=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cap\bigcup_{C\in Q}\mathcal{R}(Q)

and observe that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}
can be computed with GreedySelect once
we know all
O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))
budgets
\left\{B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})\right\}_{\tau,s^{\prime},C:C\in Q}
.
Each of them is an integer, bounded by
O_{\varepsilon}(nT\cdot\max_{j}w_{j})\leq O_{\varepsilon}(n^{4}P^{2})
,
which yields only
(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))}
possibilities
overall. We define
\chi_{Q}
to contain each of these possibilities.
This proves Lemma
10
if we relax
the condition on the size of each set
\chi_{Q}
to
|\chi_{Q}|\leq(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))}

and allow a running time of
(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))}
.

5
Polynomial size
consistent solution

In this section we prove Lemma
10

(without any relaxations of its statement). We start by defining the
solution
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
and the sets
\left\{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
.
Afterwards, we define the family
\left\{\chi_{Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
.

In the approach in Section
4

we guessed the values
\{B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})\}_{C,\tau,s^{\prime}}
and recovered
an approximate solution using only them. For a path
Q
it seems
unlikely to be able to guess all
O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))
values

B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})
corresponding to cells
C\in Q
(or sufficiently
strong approximate variants of them) in polynomial time. However,
consider the values
\{B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)\}_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
, where
B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)=\sum_{\tau,s^{\prime}}B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime}).

Note that for each path
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
only the
O_{\varepsilon}(\log(nP))

values in
\{B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)\}_{C\in Q}
are relevant. Our first step
is to construct suitable substitutes for these quantities that can
be guessed efficiently. Whenever we say that we “guess a value
x

in time
O(f(n))
” for some function
f
we mean that in time

O(f(n))
we can compute a set (which hence has size
O(f(n))
)
that contains
x
. In the proof of the following lemma we use smoothing
techniques due to
[
BGK18
]
.

Lemma 15
.

There are values
\left\{B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\right\}_{C\in\mathcal{C}}

with the following properties

•

B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\geq B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)
for all
C
,

•

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)
,

•

for each path
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
we can guess in time
(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}

all values
\left\{B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\right\}_{C\in Q}
.

Proof.

We set

B^{\prime}(C)=B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)+\hskip-20.00003pt\sum_{C^{\prime}:v_{C^{\prime}}\text{ is ancesor of }v_{C}}\hskip-20.00003ptB^{\mathrm{opt}}(C^{\prime})\cdot\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{K}\right)^{\mathrm{dist}(v_{C},v_{C^{\prime}})}.

Then for each
C\in\mathcal{C}
we set
B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)=(1+\varepsilon)^{k}

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
with
(1+\varepsilon)^{k-1}<B^{\prime}(C)\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}
.
By the construction it is obvious that
B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\geq B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)

for all
C\in\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, note that each cell
C
has at most

K^{i}
descendants
C^{\prime}
with
\mathrm{dist}(C,C^{\prime})=i
. Hence

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}B^{\prime}(C)\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}K^{i}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{K}\right)^{i}\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{i}\leq(1+2\varepsilon)\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C).

Finally,

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}B^{\prime}(C)\leq(1+4\varepsilon)\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C).

Let
\{C_{1},\dotsc,C_{k}\}=Q\in\mathcal{Q}
with
C_{1}
being the root.
Guessing
B^{\mathrm{round}}(C_{i})
is equivalent to guessing
\lfloor\log_{1+\varepsilon}(B^{\prime}(C_{i}))\rfloor
.
Notice that we have
B^{\prime}(C_{i})\geq\varepsilon B^{\prime}(C_{i-1})
for all

i=2,\dotsc,k
. It follows that

\log_{1+\varepsilon}(B^{\prime}(C_{i}))\geq\log_{1+\varepsilon}(B^{\prime}(C_{i-1}))+\log_{1+\varepsilon}(\varepsilon)\geq\log_{1+\varepsilon}(B^{\prime}(C_{i-1}))-O_{\varepsilon}(1).

In other words, we want to guess values
q_{1},\dotsc,q_{k}=O_{\varepsilon}(\log(nP))

with
k=O_{\varepsilon}(\log(nP))
such that for some
c\leq O_{\varepsilon}(1)
it holds that

q_{i}>q_{i-1}-c
for all
i
.
By transforming
p_{i}=q_{i}+i\cdot c
we obtain the problem of
guessing values
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{k}=O_{\varepsilon}(\log(nP))
with

k=O_{\varepsilon}(\log(nP))
such that
p_{i}>p_{i-1}
. This can
be done in time
2^{O_{\varepsilon}(\log(nP))}=(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}
:
We guess for all values
v\in\{1,2,\dotsc,O_{\varepsilon}(\log(nP))\}

whether
p_{i}=v
for some
i
. After guessing, the first such
v

must be
p_{1}
, the second must be
p_{2}
, etc., because
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{k}
.
Hence the values of
p_{1},p_{2},\dotsc,p_{k}
are fully determined.
∎

Informally speaking, by this lemma we can assume that our algorithm
knows all values
\left\{B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\right\}_{C\in Q}

when we consider a path
Q
.

Next, we divide all rectangles into small and large according to their
cost compared to the total budget
B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)
of their
cell
C
. Let
\delta>0
be a constant (depending only on
\varepsilon
)
to be defined later. Consider a cell
C
and a job
j
with
\mathcal{R}(j,C)\neq\emptyset
.
We say that
j

is large for
C
if for the leftmost rectangle

R\in\mathcal{R}(j,C)
it holds that
c_{R}>\delta\cdot B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)
,
and
j

is small for
C
if
c_{R}\leq\delta\cdot B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)
.
We define
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{large}}:=\bigcup_{C}\bigcup_{j:j\,\mathrm{is\,large\,for\,}C}\mathcal{R}(j,C)

and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}:=\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{large}}
.
Note that since the leftmost rectangle is always the most expensive one,
for every small job
j
, all rectangles
R\in\mathcal{R}(j,C)
satisfy
c_{R}\leq\delta B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)
.

Intuitively, we will prove Lemma
10

separately for
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{large}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}
and argue afterwards that this yields
the complete proof of Lemma
10
.
More precisely, for these sets we will provide families
\left\{\chi_{\mathrm{large},Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
,

\left\{\chi_{\mathrm{small},Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
for which there exist consistent
solutions that dominate
\mathcal{R}^{*}

on
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{large}},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}
according to
the next definition.

Definition 16
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{subset}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}

and let
\left\{\chi_{Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
be a family with

\chi_{Q}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{R}(Q)}
for each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. We say
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
is a

solution for
\left\{\chi_{Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}

that dominates

\mathcal{R}^{*}
on
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{subset}}
if

1.

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
for each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,

2.

for each
I\in\mathcal{I},Q\in\mathcal{Q}
with
\mathcal{R}(I)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(Q)
, we have that

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{subset}}
covers as much of
I
as
\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{subset}}
,
i.e.,
p(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{subset}}\cap\mathcal{R}(I))\geq p(\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{subset}}\cap\mathcal{R}(I))

3.

for any two paths

with

we have that

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}\cap\mathcal{R}(Q^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q^{\prime}}
.

We define
c(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})
to be the

of
(\mathcal{R}^{\prime},\left\{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}})
.

Notice that in the definition of the cost, we do not take the intersection
with
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{subset}}
. The reason is intuitively that

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{large}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}

Hence, when we select a set

then we need to pay for all

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{large}}

or
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}
. We will prove the following two lemmas in Sections
5.1

and
5.2
.

Lemma 17
.

In time
(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}
we can

\left\{\chi_{\mathrm{small},Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
with
\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}|\chi_{\mathrm{small},Q}|\leq(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}

that that dominates
\mathcal{R}^{*}
on
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}
and has cost at most

.

Lemma 18
.

In time
(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon,\delta}(1)}

can compute a family
\left\{\chi_{\mathrm{large},Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
with

\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}|\chi_{\mathrm{large},Q}|\leq(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon,\delta}(1)}

that dominates
\mathcal{R}^{*}

.

Together these lemmas imply Lemma

.

Proof of Lemma
10
.

We define
\delta:=\varepsilon/K^{8}
. We first compute the families

\left\{\chi_{\mathrm{large},Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
,
\left\{\chi_{\mathrm{small},Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}

in time
(nP)^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}
according to Lemmas
18

and

. For each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
we define that
\chi_{Q}

contains the set
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{large},Q}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small},Q}
for each combination

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{large},Q}\in\chi_{\mathrm{large},Q}
and a set

.
Then

and

imply that for

.

5.1
Consistent solution for small rectangles

This section is dedicated to proving Lemma
17
.
Recall that in the quasi-polynomial construction, for defining a set

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}
we guessed the
O_{\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(nP))
values
\{B^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})\}_{C,\tau,s^{\prime}:C\in Q}
.
Since in this section we focus on small rectangles, let
B_{\mathrm{small}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,\tau,s^{\prime})

denote the budgets that
\mathcal{R}^{*}
spends on buying the first
s^{\prime}

rectangles in
\mathcal{R}(C)
of type
\tau
. In our polynomial time procedure
we want to guess only
O_{\varepsilon}(\log(nP))
of them for each path

Q
. The strategy for this is to increase the budgets slightly. Intuitively,
in each cell
C
we want to spend more budget than
\mathcal{R}^{*}
on rectangles
from sets
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
with good densities
\rho_{j,C},\rho^{\prime}_{j,C}

and hence select more such rectangles. It will turn out that these
additional rectangles cover as much of each line segment
L(I)
as
the rectangles from sets
\mathcal{R}(j^{\prime},C^{\prime})
with
bad
cost-efficiencies

\rho_{j^{\prime},C^{\prime}},\rho^{\prime}_{j^{\prime},C^{\prime}}
where
v_{C^{\prime}}
is an ancestor of
v_{C}
.
Therefore, in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{Q}
we do not need rectangles from the sets
\mathcal{R}(C^{\prime},\tau,s^{\prime})

for of types

14
one can show that the cost for each

C
,
\tau
, and
s
that GreedySelectSmall spends is at most

\min\{(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{s^{\prime}}B_{S}(s^{\prime})+K^{4}\delta B^{\mathrm{round}}(C),(2+\varepsilon)\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})\},

where

are the budgets passed to GreedySelectSmall, since
the fractional solution computed by GreedySelectSmall has a cost of

(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{s^{\prime}}B(s^{\prime})
and afterwards we round up at most
K^{4}

variables corresponding to at most
K^{2}
small jobs, which incurs
an additional cost of at most

.
This implies

\displaystyle c(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C,s,s^{\prime}}\bigg(\sum_{(\rho,\rho^{\prime})<_{L}\gamma(C,s,s^{\prime})}\hskip-20.00003ptB^{\mathrm{actual}}(C,(\rho,\rho^{\prime},s),s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(1+\varepsilon)(B_{\mathrm{small}}^{\mathrm{round}}(C,(\gamma(C,s,s^{\prime}),s),s^{\prime})+B^{\mathrm{add}}(C,s,s^{\prime}))+K^{4}\delta B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(2+\varepsilon)\hskip-20.00003pt\sum_{(\rho,\rho^{\prime})>_{L}\gamma(C,s,s^{\prime})}\hskip-20.00003ptB_{\mathrm{small}}^{\mathrm{round}}(C,(\rho,\rho^{\prime},s),s^{\prime})\bigg).

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C,s,s^{\prime}}\bigg(\sum_{(\rho,\rho^{\prime})<_{L}\gamma(C,s,s^{\prime})}B^{\mathrm{actual}}(C,(\rho,\rho^{\prime},s),s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(2+\varepsilon)\sum_{(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\geq_{L}\gamma(C,s,s^{\prime})}B_{\mathrm{small}}^{\mathrm{round}}(C,(\rho,\rho^{\prime},s),s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(1+\varepsilon)B^{\mathrm{add}}(C,s,s^{\prime})+K^{4}\delta B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C,s,s^{\prime}}\bigg(\sum_{(\rho,\rho^{\prime})<_{L}\gamma(C,s,s^{\prime})}B_{\mathrm{small}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,(\rho,\rho^{\prime},s),s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(1+\varepsilon)(2+\varepsilon)\sum_{(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\geq_{L}\gamma(C,s,s^{\prime})}\hskip-20.00003ptB_{\mathrm{small}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(C,(\rho,\rho^{\prime},s),s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(2+\varepsilon)B^{\mathrm{add}}(C,s,s^{\prime})+(K^{4}\delta+\varepsilon/K^{4})B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\bigg).

\displaystyle\leq(2+4\varepsilon)c(\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}})+2\varepsilon(2+\varepsilon)\sum_{C}B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)+(K^{4}\delta+\varepsilon/K^{4})\sum_{C,s,s^{\prime}}B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)

\displaystyle\leq(2+4\varepsilon)c(\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}})+(K^{8}\delta+6\varepsilon)\sum_{C}B^{\mathrm{round}}(C)

\displaystyle\leq(2+4\varepsilon)c(\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}})+(K^{8}\delta+6\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon)c(\mathcal{R}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq(2+O(\varepsilon))c(\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}})+O(K^{8}\delta+\varepsilon)c(\mathcal{R}^{*}).\qed

5.1.3
Definition of solutions
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small},Q}

We now define the solutions
\left\{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small},Q}\right\}_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
.
Let
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Let

be the cells in

Q

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small},Q}
contains all rectangles in

that we will use in order to satisfy the demand of intervals

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small}}\cap\mathcal{R}(Q)

from which we selected additional rectangles

will ensure that

We start with the rectangles in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small}}\cap\mathcal{R}(Q)
and omit some
of them in the following. Consider some cell

, which is not one of the two bottom-most

we

with

in
\mathcal{R}(j,C)
intersects with

\mathcal{R}(j,C)
and this set can contain
only only rectangle that intersects with

Consider a job

\mathcal{R}(j,C)\cap\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small}}

\mathcal{R}(j,C)

We do this for each job
j

Next, we will omit rectangles of high densities under certain circumstances;
intuitively, because they have become obsolute due to other rectangles

For every
C_{j}
with

as the lexicographically minimal density pair
\gamma(C_{j},s,r(s,C,Q))

over all

C_{i}
that is
a ancestor of of
C_{j}
, i.e.,
i<j
. Recall that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small}}

has spent an additional budget of

compared to the optimal solution on rectangles in

of

as in

Proposition 22
.

For each

.
Also, for any two paths

Lemma 23

Let
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
and

\mathcal{R}(I)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(Q)

p(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small},Q}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}\cap\mathcal{R}(I))\geq d(R^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}\cap\mathcal{R}(I)).

Proof.

Consider the solution
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small}}
and let
C\in Q
.
We have by Lemma
14
(with straightforward adaption to GreedySelectSmall) for each type
\tau

p(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small}}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}(C,\tau)\cap\mathcal{R}(I))\geq p(\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}(C,\tau)\cap\mathcal{R}(I)),

where
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}(C,\tau)
are the small rectangles of type
\tau
in
\mathcal{R}(C)
.
In particular, if we sum over all types, the solution
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small}}
covers
L(I)
at least
as much as
\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{small}}
.
If
L(I)
fully traverses
C
(the ray begins above
C
) we have, in fact, a slightly stronger relation.
Let
({\rho}^{-},{\rho^{\prime}}^{-})=\sigma^{-}(C,Q)
.
If
\rho^{-}<\infty
then there is some

such that in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{small}}

we payed an extra budget of

for buying the first

\sigma^{+}(C_{i},Q)

Lemma 24

p_{2}

∎

and values

5.2
Consistent solution for large rectangles

In this section we prove Lemma

for each cell

First, for each cell

to be the smallest multiple of

a solution

at most
2\cdot B_{\mathrm{large}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(C)
on rectangles of jobs that are
large for

. Then, for each path

we define a solution

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{large},Q}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{large}}

Type groups.

First, we form groups of job types. We say that two types

,

(\bar{\rho},\bar{\rho}^{\prime},\bar{s})
are in the same group if
\rho/\rho^{\prime}=\bar{\rho}/\bar{\rho}^{\prime}

and

. The intuition is that jobs of the same type behave
similarly w.r.t.
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
; therefore, we will consider
the jobs of each group separately. This is possible since there are
only constantly many groups.

Lemma 27
.

There are at most
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
different groups.

Proof.

Consider a job

Lemma 28
.

It holds that
c(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{large}})\leq 2c(\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{large}})+3\varepsilon\cdot c(\mathcal{R}^{*})
.

Proof.

Consider a cell
C
and a group
g
. Let
J(g)
denote the set
of all jobs
j
such that
\mathcal{R}(C,j)
is in group
g
. Let

be the jobs from

for which
\mathcal{R}(j,C)\cap\mathcal{R}^{*}\neq\emptyset
.
For each job
j_{k^{\prime}}\in\{j_{1},\dotsc,j_{k}\}
we bought the first
s^{\prime}(j_{k^{\prime}})

rectangles of two jobs for which buying the first
s^{\prime}(j_{k^{\prime}})
rectangles
costs at most
B_{\mathrm{large},g,k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{round}}(C)
. This
yields a total cost of at most
2\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}B_{\mathrm{large},g,k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{round}}(C)
.
For each
k^{\prime}\in\{1,\dotsc,k\}
we have that
B_{\mathrm{large},g,k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\leq c\left(\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}(C,j_{k^{\prime}})\right)+\varepsilon\delta\cdot B_{\mathrm{large}}^{\mathrm{round}}(C)

Across all groups, there can be at most
1/\delta
jobs
j
that
are large for

and which satisfy
\mathcal{R}(j,C)\cap\mathcal{R}^{*}\neq\emptyset
.
Thus, if we define

to be the value

that
corresponds to the job

, we obtain that

\displaystyle c(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{large}}\cap\mathcal{R}(C))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{g}\sum_{j_{k^{\prime}}\in J^{*}(g)}2\cdot B_{\mathrm{large},g,j_{k^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{round}}(C)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{g}\sum_{j_{k^{\prime}}\in J^{*}(g)}2\left(c\left(\mathcal{R}^{*}\cap\mathcal{R}(C,j_{k^{\prime}})\right)+\varepsilon\delta\cdot B_{\mathrm{large}}^{\mathrm{round}}(C)\right)

\displaystyle=

A.1
Simplification of input instance

First we establish that

We scale all values

and

by the same factor so that

This preserves the approximation rate of a solution (if it is scaled accordingly), but
the values

Since the optimum is at least

To obtain bounded weights we scale each

Now remove all jobs
