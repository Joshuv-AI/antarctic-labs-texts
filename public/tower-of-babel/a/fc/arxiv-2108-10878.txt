Refinements to the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions
Jesse Thorner, Asif Zaman

Abstract
. We prove a version of the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions that is uniform enough to deduce the Siegel–Walfisz theorem, Hoheisel’s asymptotic for intervals of length x 1 − δ x^{1-\delta} , a Brun–Titchmarsh bound, and Linnik’s bound on the least prime in an arithmetic progression as corollaries. Our proof uses the Vinogradov–Korobov zero-free region, a log-free zero density estimate, and the Deuring–Heilbronn zero repulsion phenomenon. Improvements exist when the modulus is sufficiently powerful.



1.
Introduction and statement of results

1.1.
Main result

Let
q\geq 2
. The prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions states that as
x\to\infty
, the primes
p\leq x
equidistribute in the
\varphi(q)
residue classes
a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})
with
\gcd(a,q)=1
. Under the generalized Riemann hypothesis (GRH), primes in intervals of length
h
equidistribute in residue classes modulo
q
once
h/\varphi(q)\geq\sqrt{x}(\log x)^{2}
; that is,

(1.1)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x-h<p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})\end{subarray}}\log p=\frac{h}{\varphi(q)}+O(\sqrt{x}(\log x)^{2}),\hskip 18.49988ptx\geq q.

Unconditional progress towards (
1.1
) has classical origins; the latest benchmarks are described in
Section

1.2
. Each such result usually addresses a specific feature: long intervals (e.g.,
h=x
), short intervals (e.g.,
h=x^{1-\delta}
), small moduli (e.g.,
q\leq(\log x)^{100}
), or large moduli (e.g.,
q\leq x^{\delta}
). We prove a version of the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions that interpolates these benchmarks, giving the most uniform progress towards (
1.1
) that current results on Dirichlet
L
-functions permit. A major obstacle to uniformity is the possible existence of real zeros of Dirichlet
L
-functions near
s=1
. It follows from work of McCurley
[
10
]
that
\prod_{\chi\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})}L(s,\chi)\neq 0
in the region
\mathrm{Re}(s)\geq 1-1/(13\log(q(|\mathrm{Im}(s)|+3)))
apart from at most one real zero, say
\beta_{1}
. If
\beta_{1}
exists, then it is simple, and there exists a unique nontrivial real Dirichlet character
\chi_{1}\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})
such that
L(\beta_{1},\chi_{1})=0
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
q\geq 2
and
a
be coprime integers and
4\leq h\leq x
. Define
\lambda
and
\theta
by

\lambda=\lambda(x,q,a,h):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\int_{x-h}^{x}(1-\chi_{1}(a)t^{\beta_{1}-1})dt&\mbox{if $\beta_{1}$ exists,}\\
1&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad\theta:=\begin{cases}32/37&\mbox{if $\beta_{1}$ exists,}\\
7/12&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For all
0<\varepsilon<1-\theta
, there is a constant
=\Cr{main}(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
\lambda h/\varphi(q)\geq x^{\theta+\varepsilon}
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x-h<p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})\end{subarray}}\log p=\frac{\lambda h}{\varphi(q)}\Big[1+O_{\varepsilon}\Big(\exp\Big(\frac{-\Cr{main}\log x}{\log q+(\log\frac{x}{h})^{\frac{2}{3}}(\log^{+}\log\frac{x}{h})^{\frac{1}{3}}+(\log x)^{\frac{2}{5}}(\log\log x)^{\frac{1}{5}}}\Big)\Big)\Big].

The implied constant and
\Cr{main}
are effectively computable, and
\log^{+}(u)=\max\{0,\log u\}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Let
A\geq 1
. If
\beta_{1}
exists and
q\leq(\log x)^{A}
, then
Theorem

1.1
holds with
\theta=\frac{7}{12}
, but at a cost:
\Cr{main}
will depend ineffectively on
A
. We justify this in
Section

4
using Siegel’s theorem
[
2
, Ch. 21]
: For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
b_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

(1.2)

1-\beta_{1}\geq b_{\varepsilon}q^{-\varepsilon}.

The constant
b_{\varepsilon}
is effectively computable when
\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}

[
13
, Thm 3]
.

Remark 1.3
.

If there exists a constant
>0
, independent of
q
, such that
\beta_{1}\leq 1-\frac{\Cr{relative}}{\log q}
, then one may take
\lambda=1
and
\theta=\frac{7}{12}
in
Theorem

1.1
. The implied constant and
\Cr{main}
will depend effectively on
\Cr{relative}
. We justify these assertions in
Section

4
.

If
\beta_{1}
does not exist, then
\lambda=1
and
\frac{h}{\varphi(q)}
is the main term in
Theorem

1.1
. If
\beta_{1}
exists, then the notion of a “main term” and an “error term” in
Theorem

1.1
depends on how one views the role of
\beta_{1}
. We suggest the perspective of viewing

(1.3)

\frac{\lambda h}{\varphi(q)}=\frac{h}{\varphi(q)}-\frac{\chi_{1}(a)}{\varphi(q)}\frac{x^{\beta_{1}}-(x-h)^{\beta_{1}}}{\beta_{1}}

as the sum of a “primary main term” and a “secondary main term”, leaving

(1.4)

\frac{\lambda h}{\varphi(q)}\exp\Big(\frac{-\Cr{main}\log x}{\log q+(\log\frac{x}{h})^{\frac{2}{3}}(\log^{+}\log\frac{x}{h})^{\frac{1}{3}}+(\log x)^{\frac{2}{5}}(\log\log x)^{\frac{1}{5}}}\Big)

as an “error term”. A similar perspective is implicit in Linnik’s work on the least prime
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})

[
9
]
. In light of our piecewise definition of
\lambda
, this perspective accommodates all ranges of
q
and
h
under our consideration, regardless of whether
\beta_{1}
exists.

If
\beta_{1}
exists, then there exists
\xi\in(x-h,x)
such that

(1.5)

\lambda=1-\chi_{1}(a)\xi^{\beta_{1}-1}.

Furthermore, if there exists
A\geq 1
such that
q\leq(\log x)^{A}
, then (
1.2
) with
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{3A}
shows that

(1.6)

\lambda=1+O(\exp(-b_{\frac{1}{3A}}(\log x)^{2/3})).

The
O
-term in (
1.6
) is readily absorbed by (
1.4
). When
q
is larger, we allow the contribution from
\beta_{1}
in (
1.3
) to function as a “secondary main term” rather than attempt to push the contribution into any sort of “error term”. This permits
Theorem

1.1
to effectively count primes in arithmetic progressions modulo
q
as long as the expected number of primes congruent to
a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})
in the interval
(x-h,x]
, namely
\lambda h/\varphi(q)
, exceeds
x^{\theta+\varepsilon}
. If, in addition, one has
(\log x)/(\log q)\to\infty
, then the expected number of primes becomes an effective asymptotic count, even if
\beta_{1}
exists. The point is that (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) are each scaled by
\lambda
, so the existence of
\beta_{1}
proportionally affects both quantities. This differs from traditional presentations of the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions (e.g.,
[
2
]
) where the contribution from
\beta_{1}
is viewed as an “error term” instead of a “secondary main term.”

Even if
\beta_{1}
exists, we can always effectively estimate
\lambda
using the effective upper bound
\beta_{1}\leq 1-b_{1/2}q^{-1/2}
from (
1.2
) as well as the bounds

(1.7)

\tfrac{1}{8}\min\{1,(1-\beta_{1})\log x\}<\lambda<2,

which we prove in
Section

4
.
Therefore, even in the most restrictive case, for all
0<\varepsilon<1-\theta
, there exists an effectively computable constant
=\Cr{range_{c}onst}(\varepsilon)>0
such that the condition
\lambda h/\varphi(q)\geq x^{\theta+\varepsilon}
in
Theorem

1.1
is satisfied when
x\geq\Cr{range_{c}onst}
and

(1.8)

h\geq x^{1-\delta_{1}},\qquad q\leq x^{\delta_{2}},\qquad\delta_{1}+\frac{3}{2}\delta_{2}\leq 1-\theta-\varepsilon.

If
\beta_{1}
does not exist, then the
\frac{3}{2}
can be replaced by
1
.

Taking
h=x
and
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{555}
in
Theorem

1.1
, we arrive at the following result using (
1.8
).

Corollary 1.4
.

There exists a constant
>0
such that if
q\geq 2
and
a
are coprime integers,
x\geq q^{12}
, and
\lambda=\lambda(x,q,a,x)
is as in
Theorem

1.1
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})\end{subarray}}\log p=\dfrac{\lambda x}{\varphi(q)}\Big[1+O\Big(\exp\Big(-\Cr{cor_{l}ong_{2}}\frac{\log x}{\log q}\Big)+\exp\Big(-\Cr{cor_{l}ong_{2}}\frac{(\log x)^{3/5}}{(\log\log x)^{1/5}}\Big)\Big)\Big].

The implied constant and
\Cr{cor_{l}ong_{2}}
are absolute and effectively computable.

Remark 1.5
.

If
\beta_{1}
exists, then
\lambda(x,q,a,x)=1-\chi_{1}(a)x^{\beta_{1}-1}/\beta_{1}
.

1.2.
Connections with classical results

To display the uniformity of
Theorem

1.1
, we deduce the Siegel–Walfisz theorem with the best known error term, Hoheisel’s asymptotic prime number theorem for short intervals with the best known error term, a Brun–Titchmarsh type bound for short intervals, and Linnik’s bound on the least prime in an arithmetic progression as corollaries of
Theorem

1.1
, the ineffective bound (
1.6
), and the effective bound (
1.7
). While all of these results can be proved individually, the point here is that
Theorem

1.1
provides a single asymptotic result that is uniform enough to deduce all of them. These deductions, which require no information on
\beta_{1}
except for (
1.2
) and the effective computability of
b_{1/2}
, showcase the flexibility of
Theorem

1.1
in handling any combination of large, medium, or small moduli with short, medium, or long intervals.

If
A\geq 1
, and
q\leq(\log x)^{A}
, then (
1.6
) and
Theorem

1.1
with
h=x
imply that there exists an ineffective constant
c_{A}>0
such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})\end{subarray}}\log p=\frac{x}{\varphi(q)}\Big(1+O\Big(\exp\Big(-c_{A}\frac{(\log x)^{3/5}}{(\log\log x)^{1/5}}\Big)\Big)\Big).

(See also Koukoulopoulos
[
8
, Thm 1.1]
.) If
\varepsilon>0
and one adds the condition that
h\geq x^{7/12+\varepsilon}(\log x)^{A}
, then (
1.6
),
Theorem

1.1
, and
Remark

1.2
recover the strongest known version of Hoheisel’s short interval prime number theorem
[
4
]
for arithmetic progressions to moduli
q\leq(\log x)^{A}
: There exists an ineffective constant
c_{A,\varepsilon}>0
such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x-h<p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})\end{subarray}}\log p=\frac{h}{\varphi(q)}\Big(1+O_{\varepsilon}\Big(\exp\Big(-c_{A,\varepsilon}\frac{(\log x)^{1/3}}{(\log\log x)^{1/3}}\Big)\Big)\Big).

Consider now the situation where
q
is large. Applying
Theorem

1.1
with
\varepsilon=\frac{3}{110}
, we find that if
\lambda h/\varphi(q)\geq x^{4/5}
and
(\log x)/(\log q)\to\infty
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x-h<p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})\end{subarray}}\log p\sim\frac{\lambda h}{\varphi(q)}.

If
\beta_{1}
exists, then
\lambda
satisfies (
1.7
). Consequently, for all fixed
\delta>0
, there exists an effectively computable constant
=\Cr{BT1}(\delta)>0
such that if
x\geq q^{\Cr{BT1}}
, then

\frac{h}{\varphi(q)}(1-\beta_{1})\log x\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x-h<p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})\end{subarray}}\log p\leq(2+\delta)\frac{h}{\varphi(q)}.

When
\beta_{1}
does not exist, the
(1-\beta_{1})\log x
factor is deleted, and
2+\delta
becomes
1+\delta
. The upper bound is a weak form of the Brun–Titchmarsh theorem for short intervals (cf.
[
12
, Theorem 1.2]
). Since
\beta_{1}<1
, the lower bound implies that there exists a prime
p\leq q^{\Cr{BT1}}
such that
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})
, as Linnik proved
[
9
]
.

2.
Proof of
Theorem

1.1

Let
q\geq 2
,
T\geq 1
and
0\leq\sigma\leq 1
. For all Dirichlet characters
\chi\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})
, we define

N_{\chi}(\sigma,T):=|\{\rho=\beta+i\gamma\colon\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R},~\beta\geq\sigma,~|\gamma|\leq T,~L(\rho,\chi)=0\}|.

If
\beta_{1}
exists, then we define

N_{\chi_{1}}^{*}(\sigma,T):=|\{\rho=\beta+i\gamma\colon\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R},~\beta\geq\sigma,~|\gamma|\leq T,~\rho\neq\beta_{1}~L(\rho,\chi)=0\}|.

Let

N_{q}(\sigma,T):=\sum_{\chi\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})}N_{\chi}(\sigma,T).

If
\beta_{1}
exists, then let

N_{q}^{*}(\sigma,T):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})\\
\chi\neq\chi_{1}\end{subarray}}N_{\chi}(\sigma,T)+N_{\chi_{1}}^{*}(\sigma,T).

Along with the standard theory of Dirichlet
L
-functions
[
2
]
, we use a log-free zero density estimate that encodes the phenomenon that if
\beta_{1}
exists, then
\beta_{1}
repels all other zeros of
\prod_{\chi\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})}L(s,\chi)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
T\geq 1
,
q\geq 2
, and
0\leq\sigma\leq 1
. We have the unconditional bound

(2.1)

N_{q}(\sigma,T)\ll_{\varepsilon}(qT)^{(\frac{12}{5}+\varepsilon)(1-\sigma)}.

If
\beta_{1}
exists and
\nu(U):=\min\{1,(1-\beta_{1})\log U\}
, then

(2.2)

N_{q}^{*}(\sigma,T)\ll_{\varepsilon}\nu(qT)(qT)^{(\frac{37}{5}+\varepsilon)(1-\sigma)}.

Remark 2.2
.

Bombieri’s original proof in
[
1
, Ch. 6]
of the existence of an absolute and effectively computable constant
>0
such that
N_{q}^{*}(\sigma,T)\ll\nu(qT)(qT)^{\Cr{bomb}(1-\sigma)}
is fairly complicated. In an earlier version of this manuscript, we had pushed Bombieri’s strategy to its limit, proving that one can take
\Cr{bomb}
to be slightly smaller than
\frac{75}{4}
. The idea behind the simple proof below, which has the added benefit of producing stronger numerical exponents, was later communicated to us by Heath-Brown.

Proof.

It is well-known that (
2.1
) follows from the work of Huxley
[
5
]
and Jutila
[
7
]
. To deduce (
2.2
), let
0<\varepsilon<1
and let
\nu_{\varepsilon}
an effectively computable and sufficiently small positive constant depending only on
\varepsilon
. If
\nu(qT)>\nu_{\varepsilon}
, then the conclusion follows from (
2.1
) by inflating the implied constant if necessary. Therefore, we assume that
\nu(qT)\leq\nu_{\varepsilon}
, in which case our effective lower bound for
\beta_{1}
implies that
qT
is sufficiently large (depending only on
\varepsilon
). The version of the Deuring–Heilbronn phenomenon proved by Jutila
[
7
, Thm 2]
implies that if
\sigma>\frac{5}{6}
and

(2.3)

\nu(qT)<\frac{1}{8}(qT)^{-(2+\varepsilon)(1-\sigma)/(6\sigma-5)}(6\sigma-5),

then
N^{*}_{q}(\sigma,T)=0
. Hence, (
2.2
) holds under these assumptions. For the remaining cases, our starting point is a trivial consequence from (
2.1
), namely

N_{q}^{*}(\sigma,T)\leq N_{q}(\sigma,T)\ll_{\varepsilon}\nu(qT)^{-1}\nu(qT)(qT)^{(\frac{12}{5}+\varepsilon)(1-\sigma)}.

Since we can rescale
\varepsilon
accordingly, it therefore suffices to show
\nu(qT)\gg q^{-(5+3\varepsilon)(1-\sigma)}
to complete the proof of (
2.2
). If
\sigma\leq\frac{9}{10}
, then our effective lower bound for
\beta_{1}
implies that
\nu(qT)\gg q^{-1/2}\geq q^{-5(1-\sigma)}
, as required. Otherwise, if
\sigma>\frac{9}{10}
and (
2.3
) does not hold, then

\nu(qT)\gg(qT)^{-(2+\varepsilon)(1-\sigma)\frac{10}{4}}\gg(qT)^{-(5+3\varepsilon)(1-\sigma)},

as required. This proves (
2.2
).
∎

Using
Theorem

2.1
, we prove a highly uniform version of the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions that depends only on a zero-free region for Dirichlet
L
-functions.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
q\geq 2
and
a
be coprime integers, and let
4\leq h\leq x
. Let
\lambda
and
\theta
be as in
Theorem

1.1
. Let
0<\varepsilon<1-\theta
, and let
\delta:[1,\infty)\to[0,1]
be a function such that for all
T\geq e
, the product
\prod_{\chi\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})}L(s,\chi)
does not vanish in the region

\{s\in\mathbb{C}\colon\mathrm{Re}(s)\geq 1-\delta(T),~|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leq T\}

except possibly for
\beta_{1}
, if it exists. If
\lambda h/\varphi(q)\geq x^{\theta+\varepsilon}
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x-h<p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\,(\mathrm{mod}{\,q})\end{subarray}}\log p=\frac{\lambda h}{\varphi(q)}\Big\{1+O_{\varepsilon}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{x}{h}}\frac{\log x}{\log q}\sup\Big\{\frac{x^{-\varepsilon^{2}\delta(t)}}{\sqrt{t}}\colon\frac{ex}{h}\leq t\leq\frac{x^{(1-\theta)(1-\varepsilon)}}{q}\Big\}+x^{-\varepsilon\theta/2}\Big)\Big\}.

The implied constant is effectively computable and depends at most on
\varepsilon
.

Remark 2.4
.

If
\beta_{1}
exists (resp. does not exist) and the exponent
\frac{22}{5}
(resp.
\frac{12}{5}
) in
Theorem

2.1
is improved to some constant
>0
, then
Theorem

2.3
holds with
\theta=1-\frac{1}{\Cr{improved_{Z}DE}}
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

We use the Vinogradov–Korobov zero-free region for Dirichlet
L
-functions
[
11
, Ch. 9]
in
Theorem

2.3
. In particular, there exists an absolute and effectively computable constant
>0
such that for
t\geq e
,

(2.4)

\delta(t)\geq\Cr{ZFR-VK}/(\log q+(\log t)^{2/3}(\log^{+}\log t)^{1/3}).

Since
e^{-\frac{1}{u+v}}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2\max\{u,v\}}}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2u}}+e^{-\frac{1}{2v}}
for
u,v>0
, it follows that

\dfrac{x^{-\varepsilon^{2}\delta(t)}}{\sqrt{t}}\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Cr{ZFR-VK}\log x}{2\log q}-\frac{1}{2}\log t\Big)+\exp\Big(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Cr{ZFR-VK}\log x}{2(\log t)^{2/3}(\log^{+}\log t)^{1/3}}-\frac{1}{2}\log t\Big)

for
t\geq e
. We maximize these two exponentials over
t\in[\frac{ex}{h},\infty)
. The first exponential is maximized at the leftmost endpoint
t=ex/h
. For the second exponential, notice
\frac{1}{2}\log t
and
\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Cr{ZFR-VK}\log x}{2(\log t)^{2/3}(\log^{+}\log t)^{1/3}}
are the same order of magnitude when
\log t\asymp(\log x)^{3/5}(\log\log x)^{-1/5}
. Thus, the second exponential is maximized on the interval
[\frac{ex}{h},\infty)
for a value of
t=t_{0}
such that either
t_{0}=ex/h
, or
\log t_{0}\asymp_{\varepsilon}(\log x)^{3/5}(\log\log x)^{-1/5}
and
t_{0}\geq ex/h
. This implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq ex/h}\Big\{\dfrac{x^{-\varepsilon^{2}\delta(t)}}{\sqrt{t}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{h}{x}}\exp\Big(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Cr{ZFR-VK}\log x}{2\log q}\Big)+\sqrt{\frac{h}{x}}\exp\Big(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Cr{ZFR-VK}\log x}{2(\log t_{0})^{2/3}(\log^{+}\log t_{0})^{1/3}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{h}{x}}\exp\Big(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Cr{ZFR-VK}\log x}{2\log q+2(\log t_{0})^{2/3}(\log^{+}\log t_{0})^{1/3}}\Big),

since
e^{-\frac{1}{u}}+e^{-\frac{1}{v}}\leq 2e^{-\frac{1}{u+v}}
for
u,v>0
.
Therefore, by the conditions on
t_{0}
, there exists an effectively computable constant
\Cr{main}=\Cr{main}({\varepsilon})>0
, depending at most on
\varepsilon
, such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{x}{h}}\frac{\log x}{\log q}\sup\Big\{\frac{x^{-\varepsilon^{2}\delta(t)}}{\sqrt{t}}\colon\frac{ex}{h}\leq t\leq\frac{x^{(1-\theta)(1-\varepsilon)}}{q}\Big\}+x^{-\varepsilon\theta/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\log x}{\log q}\exp\Big(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Cr{ZFR-VK}\log x}{2\log q+2(\log t_{0})^{2/3}(\log^{+}\log t_{0})^{1/3}}\Big)+x^{-\varepsilon\theta/2}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}\exp\Big(-\frac{\Cr{main}\log x}{\log q+(\log\frac{x}{h})^{2/3}(\log^{+}\log\frac{x}{h})^{1/3}+(\log x)^{2/5}(\log\log x)^{1/5}}\Big).

Inserting this estimate in the conclusion of
Theorem

2.3
proves
Theorem

1.1
.
∎

. The zero-free region only influences quality of the

-term.

3.1.

be the squarefree part of

, let
\lambda
and
