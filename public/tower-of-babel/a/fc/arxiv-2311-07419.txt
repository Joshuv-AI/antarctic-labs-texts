Diaconis-Ylvisaker prior penalized likelihood for p / n → κ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p/n\to\kappa\in(0,1) logistic regression
Philipp Sterzinger

Abstract
We characterise the behaviour of the maximum Diaconis-Ylvisaker prior penalized likelihood estimator in high-dimensional logistic regression, where the number of covariates is a fraction κ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \kappa\in(0,1) of the number of observations n n , as n → ∞ n\to\infty . We derive the estimator’s aggregate asymptotic behaviour when covariates are independent normal random variables with mean zero and variance 1 / n 1/n , and the vector of regression coefficients has length γ ​ n \gamma\sqrt{n} , asymptotically. From this foundation, we devise adjusted Z Z -statistics, penalized likelihood ratio statistics, and aggregate asymptotic results with arbitrary covariate covariance. In the process, we fill in gaps in previous literature by formulating a Lipschitz-smooth approximate message passing recursion, to formally transfer the asymptotic results from approximate message passing to logistic regression. While the maximum likelihood estimate asymptotically exists only for a narrow range of ( κ , γ ) (\kappa,\gamma) values, the maximum Diaconis-Ylvisaker prior penalized likelihood estimate not only exists always but is also directly computable using maximum likelihood routines. Thus, our asymptotic results also hold for ( κ , γ ) (\kappa,\gamma) values where results for maximum likelihood are not attainable, with no overhead in implementation or computation. We study the estimator’s shrinkage properties and compare it to logistic ridge regression and demonstrate our theoretical findings with simulations. Keywords: infinite estimates, phase transition, conjugate prior, approximate message passing, logistic ridge regression



1
Introduction

1.1
Logistic regression

Logistic regression is arguably one of the most widely used models in
statistical practice to associate binary responses with a sequence of
covariates, for either inference on covariate effects or prediction. A
logistic regression model assumes that conditionally on covariate
vectors
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}
,
\bm{x}_{j}\in\Re^{p}
, the responses

y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
,
y_{j}\in\{0,1\}
are realizations of independent
Bernoulli random variables such that

\displaystyle\Pr(y_{j}=1|\bm{x}_{j})=\zeta^{\prime}\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)\quad(j=1,\ldots,n)\,,

(2)

for some unknown vector of regression coefficients

\bm{\beta}_{0}\in\Re^{p}
, and where
\zeta^{\prime}(x)=1/({1+e^{-x}})
is the
derivative of the cumulant transform
\zeta(x)=\log(1+e^{x})
. The
log-likelihood of the logistic regression model is given by

\ell(\bm{\beta};\bm{y},\bm{X})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\{y_{j}\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}-\zeta\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)\right\}\,,

(3)

where
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
and
\bm{X}
is the
n\times p

matrix with
j
th row
\bm{x}_{j}
. The maximum likelihood (ML) estimator

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny ML}}
is simply the maximiser of (
3
), and is the
standard choice for estimating the signal
\bm{\beta}_{0}
. Despite ML
being a standard choice for estimating logistic regression models,
existence of the ML estimate is not guaranteed for all configurations
of responses and covariates, as was first shown in

Albert and
Anderson 1984
. Specifically, the ML estimate does not
exist – colloquially, its components take on infinite values – if
and only if there exists a separating hyperplane in
\Re^{p}
that
perfectly discriminates among the covariates
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}

according to the values of the corresponding outcomes
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}

(except, perhaps, from observations falling onto that separating
hyperplane). This phenomenon has been termed (quasi-) complete
separation by
Albert and
Anderson 1984
.
Candès and
Sur 2020

showed that the nonexistence of the ML estimate becomes particularly
problematic in high dimensions, where, under assumptions about the
distribution of
\bm{x}_{j}
, existence is asymptotically restricted to a
narrow region of the limits
p/n\to\kappa\in(0,1)
and

\var(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0})\to\gamma^{2}
.

1.2
Maximum penalized likelihood estimation

There are several penalized likelihood (MPL) estimation methods that
can remedy the occurrence of infinite ML estimates. These methods
maximise the penalized log-likelihood

\ell^{*}(\bm{\beta};\bm{y},\bm{X})=\ell(\bm{\beta};\bm{y},\bm{X})+\log p(\bm{\beta})\,,

(4)

where the penalty
\log p(\bm{\beta})
is chosen to ensure that the MPL
estimate exists. Popular penalties include simple separable functions

\log p(\bm{\beta})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}f(\bm{\beta}_{j})
that do not depend on the
observed data, as is the case for LASSO
(see
Hastie
et al. 2015
, Section 3.2 and
Section 3.6.1)
or ridge regression

(
Cessie and
Houwelingen 1992
)
, and certain penalties, which may
depend on the covariates
\bm{X}
. A notable penalty that depends on

\bm{X}
is the logarithm of the Jeffreys’ invariant prior, where

\log p(\bm{\beta};\bm{X})=\log\det(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{W}(\bm{\beta})\bm{X})/2
, for
diagonal matrix
\bm{W}(\bm{\beta})
with
j
th diagonal entry

w_{jj}=\zeta^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta})(1-\zeta^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta})

(
Kosmidis and
Firth 2021
)
. Another penalty that depends on
\bm{X}
is
the logarithm of the conjugate prior of

Diaconis and
Ylvisaker 1979
, henceforth DY prior, which is the
subject of investigation in this paper.

Aside from guaranteed existence, the maximum Jeffreys’ prior penalized
likelihood (mJPL) and the maximum Diaconis-Ylvisaker prior penalized
likelihood (mDYPL) estimators are also equivariant under linear and
general transformations of the parameters, respectively. In the
classical fixed-
p
setting, the asymptotic properties of mJPL and
mDYPL estimators are also well understood. Under mild regularity
conditions, the mJPL estimator has asymptotically smaller bias than
what the ML estimator typically has, and both the mJPL and mDYPL
estimators preserve the asymptotic normality and Cramér-Rao
efficiency that is typically expected of the ML
estimator.
Kosmidis and
Firth 2021
and
Rigon and
Aliverti 2023

provide details for the Jeffreys’ and DY prior, respectively.

1.4
Related work

In a pair of influential works,
Sur and
Candès 2019
and

Candès and
Sur 2020
show that, in the high dimensional asymptotic
regime where
p/n\to\kappa\in(0,1)
, the ML estimate does not
exist for a wide range of
\kappa
and signal strength

\var(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0})\to\gamma^{2}
, with probability
approaching one. Even for configurations where the ML estimate exists,
it is shown to exhibit bias in an aggregate sense as well as inflated
standard errors.
Zhao
et al. 2022
generalise these results for
Gaussian design matrices with arbitrary covariate covariance and
provide results about the asymptotic distribution of the ML
estimator’s coordinates as well as the likelihood ratio test
statistic, using a stochastic representation of the ML
estimator.
In a related vein,
Salehi
et al. 2019
establish aggregate
asymptotic results for maximum penalized likelihood estimators with
separable, convex penalty functions, focusing on the regime where

p/n\to\kappa\in(0,\infty)
. The results of

Salehi
et al. 2019
are, though, specific to aggregate
asymptotics with no consideration of inference.

1.5
Our contribution

This paper establishes the asymptotic behaviour of the mDYPL estimator
in high dimensional logistic regression with Gaussian covariates and

(\kappa,\gamma)
values beyond the phase transition curve of

Candès and
Sur 2020
using techniques from the approximate message
passing (AMP) literature. From a statistical perspective, we
accurately describe the asymptotic behaviour of the mDYPL estimator
and investigate the impact of the choice of its hyperparameters on its
performance. Within the realm of AMP, we are able to transfer the
typical AMP results to a MPL estimation problem with nonseparable
penalty function and provide a smooth AMP recursion along with an
approximation argument that is essential to formally employ AMP
results in logistic regression.

Following the arguments in
Sur and
Candès 2019
, we derive the
AMP-typical aggregate asymptotic behaviour of the mDYPL estimator,
which includes the aggregate bias and variance
in the asymptotic regime where

\bm{x}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{p},n^{-1}\bm{I}_{p})
,

p/n\to\kappa\in(0,1)
and

\var(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0})\to\gamma^{2}
. In contrast to similar
such results about the ML estimator, our results are no longer
confined to the narrow region of
(\kappa,\gamma)
values below the
phase transition curve, but apply whenever a solution to a system of
nonlinear equations is attainable. We found such solutions to exist
for
(\kappa,\gamma)
values well beyond the region where the ML
estimate exists asymptotically. Due to its equivariance under linear
transformations of the covariates, the mDYPL estimator admits a
stochastic representation analogous to what is established for the ML
estimator in
Zhao
et al. 2022
. In conjunction with our aggregate
asymptotic results in the setting of
Sur and
Candès 2019
, we can
generalise the characterisation of the aggregate asymptotic behaviour
of the mDYPL to arbitrary covariate covariances. Further, we derive
the asymptotic distribution of the mDYPL estimator’s coordinates and
of the DY prior penalized likelihood ratio statistic in the high
dimensional setting with arbitrary covariate covariances. Our results
also generalize those in
Zhao
et al. 2022
in that they reduce to
the corresponding results for the ML estimator when one of the DY
prior hyperparameters is set to zero.

By rewriting the DY prior penalized log-likelihood as a logistic
regression log-likelihood with transformed responses, this paper is,
to the best of our knowledge, the first to establish asymptotic
results for maximum penalized likelihood estimators with nonseparable
penalty functions in high dimensional logistic regression. This paves
the way for similar results for other penalty functions which reduce
to a perturbation of the responses.

On a technical note, the AMP machinery employed to derive our
asymptotic results hinges on a recursion, which can be thought of as
an approximation to the solution of the first order condition of the
MPL estimation problem at hand. Formally, this recursion is required
to be Lipschitz-smooth in its arguments, which is not the case for
logistic regression. The reason for this lies in the nature of binary
responses, which are modelled as a step function

Y_{j}=\mathds{1}\left\{\bar{\varepsilon}_{j}<\zeta^{\prime}\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)\right\}
for
\bar{\varepsilon}_{j}\sim U(0,1)
. Clearly, such a step function cannot be Lipschitz-smooth in
its arguments
\bar{\varepsilon}_{j},\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}
. We thus
introduce a smooth AMP recursion, which approximates
Y_{i}
by a smooth
function

{}_{\epsilon}Y_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left\{1-\frac{\bar{\varepsilon}_{j}-\zeta^{\prime}\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)}{\left|\bar{\varepsilon}_{j}-\zeta^{\prime}\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)\right|+\epsilon}\right\}\,,

(5)

for
\epsilon>0
. We link this smooth recursion to the non-smooth
recursion of interest to formally transfer the asymptotic results from
the AMP literature to logistic regression. This step has hitherto been
missing in the analysis of the ML estimator in high dimensional
logistic regression in
Sur and
Candès 2019
, as noted in

Feng
et al. 2022
. Details are provided in
Appendix
of the Supplementary Material document.

2
Maximum Diaconis-Ylvisaker prior penalized likelihood

Given hyperparameters
\tau>0
and
\bm{\beta}_{P}\in\Re^{p}
, the log of
the DY prior for the logistic regression model is given by

\log p(\bm{\beta};\bm{X})=\tau\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\{\zeta^{\prime}\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{P}\right)\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}-\zeta\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)\right\}+C\,,

(6)

where
C
is a normalising constant that does not depend on

\bm{\beta}
. In (
6
),
\bm{\beta}_{P}
is the mode of the DY
prior, and
\tau
controls the variability of the prior
distribution. The reader is referred to

Diaconis and
Ylvisaker 1979
for further details on the
hyperparameters and a discussion of the prior from a Bayesian
viewpoint. The DY prior penalized log-likelihood, without the
normalising constant is given by

\ell^{*}(\bm{\beta};\bm{y},\bm{X})=(\tau+1)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\{\left(\frac{1}{\tau+1}y_{j}+\frac{\tau}{\tau+1}\zeta^{\prime}\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{P}\right)\right)\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}-\zeta\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)\right\}\,.

(7)

In contrast to the ML estimator, the maximiser of
(
7
), henceforth denoted by
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
, is unique and
exists for all data configurations
\{\bm{y},\bm{X}\}

(see, for
example,
Rigon and
Aliverti 2023
, Theorem 1)
. Straightforward algebra
reveals that
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
is the maximiser of a logistic regression
log-likelihood with pseudo-responses

\bm{y}^{*}=~(y_{1}^{*},\ldots,y_{n}^{*})^{\top}
,

y_{j}^{*}=\alpha y_{j}+(1-\alpha)\zeta^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{P})
, for

\alpha=1/(1+\tau)\in(0,1)
. In particular, it holds that

(\tau+1)\ell(\bm{\beta};\bm{y}^{*},\bm{X})=\ell^{*}(\bm{\beta};\bm{y},\bm{X})
.
Hence, mDYPL estimates can be computed using standard ML routines for
logistic regression. Furthermore, the mDYPL estimator
converges to the ML estimator for
\alpha\to 1
(
\tau\to 0
), and
to the prior mode
\bm{\beta}_{P}
for
\alpha\to 0
(
\tau\to\infty
). For this reason, we refer to
\alpha
as shrinkage
parameter. It is well known that in the fixed
p
asymptotic regime,
the ML estimator of a logistic regression model is biased away from

\bm{0}_{p}

(
Cordeiro and
McCullagh 1991
)
, and recent empirical evidence
suggests that this continues to hold in high dimensions (see

Zhao
et al. 2022
,
Sur and
Candès 2019
). Hence, it seems
natural to shrink the mDYPL estimator away from the ML estimator
towards zero by setting
\bm{\beta}_{P}=\bm{0}_{p}
.
In what follows, all results we derive hold for

\bm{\beta}_{P}=\bm{0}_{p}
and, unless otherwise specified, for any
choice of
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Generalising the results to
arbitrary choices for
\bm{\beta}_{P}
is left for future
research. Furthermore, the dependence of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
on
\alpha

is typically suppressed. Whenever that dependence is relevant
we write
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}(\alpha)
.

3
Asymptotic behaviour of the mDYPL estimator

We now characterise the asymptotic behaviour of the mDYPL estimator in
high dimensional logistic regression with Gaussian covariates, for
arbitrary
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Central to the developments is the
aggregate behaviour of the mDYPL estimator, that is the behaviour of

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\psi(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-{\mu}_{*}\bm{\beta}_{0,j},\bm{\beta}_{0,j})\,,

(8)

as
n\to\infty
, for some scalar
{\mu}_{*}
, which we define in
Section
3.1
, and where
\psi:\Re^{2}\to\Re
is a
pseudo-Lipschitz function of order two. As a starting point, we derive
the almost sure limit of (
8
) in the regime of

Sur and
Candès 2019
where

\bm{x}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{p},n^{-1}\bm{I}_{p})
,

\var\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)\to\gamma^{2}
. Based on this
result, the work of
Zhao
et al. 2022
can be extended to derive
the aggregate behaviour of the mDYPL estimator for arbitrary covariate
covariance
\bm{x}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{p},\bm{\Sigma})
and

\var\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)=\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{0}\to\gamma^{2}
, given some mild regularity conditions on

\bm{\Sigma}
.

3.1
Aggregate behaviour

Consider
a model matrix
\bm{X}
with independent rows

\bm{x}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{p},n^{-1}\bm{I}_{p})
, and predictors

\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}
with variance

\var(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0})\to\gamma^{2}
. The asymptotic behaviour of

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
is analysed using an appropriately defined generalised AMP
recursion (see
Feng
et al. 2022
and references therein for a
comprehensive overview of AMP), whose iterates have a known asymptotic
distribution as
p/n\to\kappa\in(0,1)
, with a stationary point
that coincides with the mDYPL estimator. To employ the AMP theory,
some regularity conditions on the asymptotic behaviour of the signal
are required. It is assumed that there exists a random variable

\bar{\beta}\sim\pi_{\bar{\beta}}
with finite second moment, such
that the empirical distribution of the entries of
\bm{\beta}_{0}
converges
weakly to the distribution
\pi_{\bar{\beta}}
and

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}^{2}/p\to\expect(\bar{\beta}^{2})
.

The asymptotic behaviour of the mDYPL estimator is governed by the
solution
({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*},{\sigma}_{*})
to the system of nonlinear equations in
three variables
(\mu,b,\sigma)
:

\displaystyle\expect\left[2\zeta^{\prime}(Z)Z\left\{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}-\zeta^{\prime}\left(\textrm{prox}_{b\zeta}\left(Z_{*}+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}b\right)\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=0

(9)

\displaystyle 1-\kappa-\expect\left[\frac{2\zeta^{\prime}(Z)}{1+b\zeta^{\prime\prime}(\textrm{prox}_{b\zeta}\left(Z_{*}+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}b\right))}\right]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}-\frac{b^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\expect\left[2\zeta^{\prime}(Z)\left\{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}-\zeta^{\prime}\left(\textrm{prox}_{b\zeta}\left(Z_{*}+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}b\right)\right)\right\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=0\,,

which depends on the shrinkage parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Here,

Z\sim N(0,\gamma^{2})
,
Z_{*}=\mu Z+\kappa^{1/2}\sigma G
for

G\sim N(0,1)
which is independent of
Z
,
\zeta^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)
is the
second derivative of
\zeta(\cdot)
, and

\textrm{prox}_{{b}_{*}\zeta}\left(x\right)=\arg\min_{u}\left\{{b}_{*}\zeta(u)+\frac{1}{2}(x-u)^{2}\right\}
denotes the proximal operator. The system of
equations in (
9
) defines the stationary points to
the state evolution of the underlying AMP recursion, which can also be
derived as the first order conditions of a related auxiliary
optimization problem to MPL estimation (see, for example,

Salehi
et al. 2019
).

Denote by
\bm{J}(\mu,b,\sigma)
the Jacobian matrix of the LHS of
(
9
) with respect to
(\mu,b,\sigma)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
(\alpha,\kappa,\gamma)
are such that

\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{1/2})
almost surely and that
(
9
) admits a solution
({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*},{\sigma}_{*})
such
that
\bm{J}({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*}{\sigma}_{*})
is nonsingular. Then for any
function
\psi:\Re^{2}\to\Re
that is pseudo-Lipschitz of order 2,

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-{\mu}_{*}\bm{\beta}_{0,j},\bm{\beta}_{0,j})\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\expect\left[\psi({\sigma}_{*}G,\bar{\beta})\right],\quad\textrm{as }n\to\infty\,,

(10)

where
G\sim N(0,1)
is independent of
\bar{\beta}\sim\pi_{\bar{\beta}}
.

For appropriate choices of
\psi(t,u)
, one gets the typical
asymptotic results for centred AMP recursions:

(i)

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-{\mu}_{*}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\right)\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0
, for
\psi(t,u)=t

(ii)

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-{\mu}_{*}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\right)^{2}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}{\sigma}_{*}^{2}
, for
\psi(t,u)=t^{2}

(iii)

\frac{\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{p}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}{\sigma}_{*}^{2}+(1-{\mu}_{*})^{2}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\kappa}
, for
\psi(t,u)=(t-(1-{\mu}_{*})u)^{2}

(iv)

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-{\mu}_{*}\bm{\beta}_{0,j})\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0
, for
\psi(t,u)=tu

Statements (i) and (ii) characterise what is termed bias and variance
respectively in
Sur and
Candès 2019
, (iii) gives an aggregate
measure of the MSE, and (iv) shows that the recentred estimator is
asymptotically uncorrelated from the signal
\bm{\beta}_{0}
in an aggregate
sense.

Since we are typically interested in signal recovery, we can also
obtain corresponding results for the rescaled mDYPL estimator

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}/{\mu}_{*}
. In particular,

(vi)

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\frac{1}{{\mu}_{*}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\right)\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0
, for
\psi(t,u)=\frac{t}{{\mu}_{*}}

(vii)

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\frac{1}{{\mu}_{*}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\right)^{2}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{{\sigma}_{*}^{2}}{{\mu}_{*}^{2}}
, for
\psi(t,u)=\left(\frac{t}{{\mu}_{*}}\right)^{2}

(viii)

, for
\psi(t,u)=\frac{tu}{{\mu}_{*}}

If the empirical distribution of the components of

\bar{\beta}\sim\pi_{\bar{\beta}}

\beta\in\mathcal{B}

(ix)

\frac{1}{|\{j:\bm{\beta}_{0,j}=\beta\}|}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\right)\mathds{1}\{\bm{\beta}_{0,j}=\beta\}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}({\mu}_{*}-1)\beta
, for
\psi(t,u)=(t-(1-{\mu}_{*})u)\mathds{1}\{u=\beta\}/\Pr\left(\bar{\beta}=~\beta\right)

(x)

\frac{1}{|\{j:\bm{\beta}_{0,j}=\beta\}|}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\frac{1}{{\mu}_{*}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\right)\mathds{1}\{\bm{\beta}_{0,j}=\beta\}\overset{\textrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0
, for
\psi(t,u)=\frac{t}{{\mu}_{*}}\mathds{1}\{u=\beta\}/\Pr\left(\bar{\beta}=~\beta\right)

While the choices of

Remarks

The existence of a solution
({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*},{\sigma}_{*})
to
(
9
) is required because the underlying AMP recursion
is specifically designed such that a fixed point corresponds to a
maximum of
\ell(\bm{\beta};\bm{y}^{*},\bm{X})
(see
Section
of the Supplementary Material
document). Obtaining theoretical guarantees about the existence of a
solution
({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*},{\sigma}_{*})
for arbitrary configurations of

(\kappa,\gamma)
is far from trivial. That assumption is typically
verified numerically for specific settings of interest (see for
example the supplementary material of

Sur and
Candès 2019
or

Salehi
et al. 2019
). In the numerical analyses in
the current paper, we are able to obtain a solution

({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*},{\sigma}_{*})
for all
(\alpha,\kappa,\gamma)
that we
consider. The additional assumption that
\bm{J}({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*},{\sigma}_{*})
is
nonsingular arises from the smoothing argument that is necessary to
formally transfer the asymptotic results from the AMP literature to
mDYPL estimation. In particular, the limiting step at which we let our
smooth approximation converge to the AMP recursion of interest,
requires some well-behavedness of the corresponding system of
equations so that its solution converges to
({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*},{\sigma}_{*})
. We
guarantee that this is the case by requiring the nonsingularity of

\bm{J}({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*},{\sigma}_{*})
and using the Implicit Function
Theorem. The assumption of nonsingularity of

\bm{J}({\mu}_{*},{b}_{*},{\sigma}_{*})
is not present in
Sur and
Candès 2019
,
as the smoothing approximation step through
(
5
) is missing in their analysis. The
almost sure boundedness of the squared
\ell_{2}
-norm of

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}/\sqrt{n}
is required to asymptotically link the mDYPL
estimator with the iterates of a AMP recursion whose exact asymptotic
distribution is known. Theorem
3.2
gives
sufficient conditions under which such boundedness holds almost
surely.

Theorem 3.2
.

In the setting of Theorem
3.1
, and if

(\kappa,\gamma)
are such that the ML estimate exists with
probability approaching one, then almost surely

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}(\alpha)\|_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}=\mathcal{O}(1)\,,

(11)

for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Furthermore, for any
(\kappa,\gamma)
, there exists
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
such that (
11
) holds almost surely for all
0<\alpha<\alpha_{0}
.

The first part of Theorem
3.2
states that
whenever the ML estimate exists asymptotically, and is

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
almost surely (see Theorem 4 of the
supplementary material of
Sur and
Candès 2019
), so is

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
.
Heuristically, almost sure boundedness of the mDYPL estimator holds
since the ML estimator is bounded almost surely in this instance and

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
is shrunk away from
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny ML}}
towards the origin.

The second part of Theorem
3.2
states that
the almost sure boundedness of
\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n}

continues to hold for any
(\kappa,\gamma)
pair for an interval of
values of the shrinkage parameter
\alpha
, even when the ML estimate
does not exist asymptotically. This is established by showing that
eventually, for
\alpha
small enough

\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\beta}\in\Re^{p}:\\
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{2}>c\sqrt{n}\end{subarray}}{\sup}\,\log p(\bm{\beta};\bm{X})<-(\tau+1)n\log(2)=\ell(\bm{0}_{p};\bm{y}^{*},\bm{X})\,,

(12)

with very high probability. In words, the log of the DY prior penalty
is dominated by
\ell(\bm{0}_{p};\bm{y}^{*},\bm{X})
outside a sphere of radius

c\sqrt{n}
. Thus, continuity of
\ell(\bm{\beta};\bm{y}^{*},\bm{X})

establishes that the global maximiser must then lie within said
sphere. It is unclear at this point whether the threshold
\alpha_{0}

is an artefact of our proof strategy and whether or how this threshold
relates to the existence of a solution to (
9
).

The numerous simulation studies we performed suggest that

\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
even in settings

(\alpha,\kappa,\gamma)
where we are unable to find a numerical
solution to (
9
). Figure
3
shows
the average squared
\ell_{2}
-norm of

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}\left(\{1+\kappa\}^{-1}\right)
for the setting of
Figure

versus
n\in\{200,400,\ldots,8000\}
,
over
50
repetitions for each combination of
n
,
\kappa
, and

\gamma
. We find no empirical evidence against the boundedness of

\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n}
. Further simulation experiments in the
same setting for more extreme values

\alpha\in\{0.9,0.95,0.99,0.999\}
are given in
Section
of the Supplementary Material
document, and result in strong evidence that the mDYPL estimator is of
order
\sqrt{n}
.

3.2
Arbitrary covariate covariance

We now relax the assumption of uncorrelated Gaussian covariates and
instead assume that
\bm{X}
has rows that are i.i.d. draws from

\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{p},\bm{\Sigma})
,
\bm{\Sigma}\in\Re^{p\times p}
. In what
follows, let
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\top}
, where
\bm{L}
denotes a
Cholesky factorization of
\bm{\Sigma}
, and denote the minimum and
maximum eigenvalues of
\bm{\Sigma}
by
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})
and

\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma})
, respectively. As before, we require that

\var{(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0})}=\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{0}\to\gamma^{2}\in\Re
and that
p/n\to\kappa\in(0,1)
.

The derivations in
Zhao
et al. 2022
enable us to relate the
mDYPL estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
from covariates with covariance

\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\top}
and signal
\bm{\beta}_{0}
to the mDYPL
estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
with
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{p},\bm{I}_{p})
covariates and
signal
\bm{\theta}_{0}=\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}
via

\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}=\bm{L}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
; these relations are a consequence of
the equivariance properties of maximum likelihood estimators under
linear transformations of covariates
(
Zehna 1966
, see, for
example,)
and immediately extend to the mDYPL estimator.
In conjunction with Theorem
3.1
, we can
then get the following asymptotic result about the aggregate behaviour
of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume that
(\alpha,\kappa,\gamma)
are such that the conditions of
Theorem
3.1
are met. Let
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
be
the mDYPL estimator for a logistic regression model with
i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{p},\bm{\Sigma})
covariates, with

\underset{n\to\infty}{\limsup}\,\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma})/\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})<\infty
, and signal
\bm{\beta}_{0}
. Then for
any
t\in\Re
,

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\left\{\sqrt{n}\tau_{j}\frac{\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-{\mu}_{*}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}}{{\sigma}_{*}}\leq t\right\}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\Phi(t)\,,

(13)

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the CDF of the standard normal distribution,

\mathds{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function and

\tau_{j}=\var\left(\bm{x}_{ij}|\bm{x}_{i-j}\right)
is the conditional
variance of the
j
th predictor given all others. Further, if

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\delta_{\sqrt{n}\tau_{j}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\pi_{\bar{\beta}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}n\tau_{j}^{2}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\expect(\bar{\beta}^{2})\,,

(14)

for some distribution
\pi_{\bar{\beta}}
with finite second moment
and
\bar{\beta}\sim\pi_{\bar{\beta}}
, where
\delta_{(\cdot)}

is the Dirac delta function, then for any pseudo-Lipschitz function

\psi:\Re^{2}\to\Re
of order 2,

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\psi\left(\sqrt{n}\tau_{j}\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-{\mu}_{*}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\right),\sqrt{n}\tau_{j}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\right)\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\expect\left[\psi({\sigma}_{*}G,\bar{\beta})\right],\quad\textrm{as }n\to\infty\,,

(15)

for
G\sim N(0,1)
independent of
\bar{\beta}
.

3.3
Inference

The aggregate behaviour of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
in
Theorem
3.1
is the basis to extend the
arguments in
Zhao
et al. 2022
and

Sur and
Candès 2019
to mDYPL, and derive the asymptotic
distribution of adjusted
Z
-statistics and of DY prior penalized
likelihood ratio test statistics.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that

(\alpha,\kappa,\gamma)
are such that the conditions of
Theorem
3.1
are met. Let

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
be the mDYPL estimator for a logistic regression model
with
i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{p},\bm{\Sigma})
covariates and signal

\bm{\beta}_{0}
. Then for any regression coefficient such that
\sqrt{n}\tau_{j}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}=\mathcal{O}(1)
,

\sqrt{n}\tau_{j}\frac{\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}_{j}-{\mu}_{*}\bm{\beta}_{0,j}}{{\sigma}_{*}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\,,

(16)

where
\tau_{j}=\var(\bm{x}_{ij}|\bm{x}_{i-j})
.

Remark

Following the proof of
Zhao
et al. 2022
, the
rotational invariance of normal random variables immediately
extends Theorem
3.4
to linear combinations of
parameters. Specifically, for any sequence of unit vectors
\bm{v}\in\Re^{p}
such that
\tau(\bm{v})\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
, it holds that

\sqrt{n}\tau(\bm{v})\frac{\bm{v}^{\top}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}-{\mu}_{*}\bm{\beta}_{0})}{{\sigma}_{*}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\,,

(17)

where
\tau(\bm{v})=\var(\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}|\bm{P}_{\bm{v}^{\perp}}\bm{v})=(\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{v})^{-1}
and

\bm{P}_{\bm{v}^{\perp}}
is the projection onto the subspace orthogonal to

\bm{v}
. Most notably, this extends Theorem
3.4
to
arbitrarily large, but fixed, collections of parameters.

We now turn to the characterisation of the DY prior penalized
likelihood ratio statistic. The discussion in

Zhao

shows that it is sufficient to

distribution of the likelihood ratio statistic is derived in

Candès 2019

approximation arguments. If the ML estimator is replaced by the mDYPL

Candès 2019

Candès 2019

distribution of the DY prior penalized likelihood ratio statistic,

.

, respectively. The
same holds for the empirical coordinatewise bias, which is
approximately
({\mu}_{*}-1)\bm{\beta}_{0,j}
. The mid subtable of

1
reports results for scenario (b). As expected from
Figure
5
, both coordinatewise bias and RMSE are
substantially larger than they are in scenario (a). The mDYPL
estimator underestimates the nonzero components of the regression
coefficient by about half their absolute value, while the aggregate
RMSE is now
22.82
in contrast to
2.25
in scenario (a). Finally,
the bottom subtable of Table

reports results for scenario
(c). There is substantial aggregate bias due to the shrinkage of the
mDYPL estimator towards zero, and in concordance with our theory, that
aggregate bias is no longer zero but close to

({\mu}_{*}-1)\approx-0.187
.

Overall, setting
\alpha=1/(1+\kappa)
is attractive because we
are able to find solutions to equations (

) for a
wide range of
(\kappa,\gamma)
values. However, it is inadequate as
a means of bias reduction in the high-dimensional setting without the
rescaling of the mDYPL estimator predicted by
Theorem

.

, and

. For each

(\kappa,\gamma)
combination, we identify the
\alpha
value that
minimises
{\sigma}_{*}/{\mu}_{*}
among the

values for which we

Sur

We use the setting in
Salehi
et al. 2019
. Let
\{\bm{y},\bm{X}\}
be
data from a logistic regression model with covariates

\bm{x}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{p},p^{-1}\bm{I}_{p})
and signal
\bm{\beta}_{0}
,
the entries of which are realizations of independent random variables
with distribution
\pi_{\bar{\beta}}
, such that the signal strength

\|\bm{\beta}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}/p
is equal to
\gamma^{2}
, as

p/n\to\kappa\in(0,1)
. The ridge logistic regression estimator
is

\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny R}}=\arg\underset{\bm{\beta}\in\Re^{p}}{\max}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\{y_{j}\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}-\zeta\left(\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right)\right\}-\frac{\lambda}{2p}\sum_{j=1}\bm{\beta}_{j}^{2}\,,

et al. 2019

locally Lipschitz function
\psi:\Re^{2}\to\Re

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\psi\left(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny R}}_{j}-\bar{\mu}\bm{\beta}_{0,j},\bm{\beta}_{0,j}\right)\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\expect\left[\psi\left(\bar{\sigma}G,\bar{\beta}\right)\right]\,,

(21)

where

,
\bar{\beta}\sim\pi_{\bar{\beta}}

independent of
Z
and
\bar{\mu},\bar{\sigma}
are part of the
solution
(\bar{\mu},\bar{b},\bar{\sigma})

(\mu,b,\sigma)

\displaystyle\expect\left[2\zeta^{\prime\prime}\left(-\gamma Z_{1}\right)\textrm{prox}_{b\zeta}\left({\mu}\gamma Z_{1}+{\sigma}Z_{2}\right)\right]+{\mu}\kappa

\displaystyle=0

(22)

\displaystyle 1-\kappa+{b}\lambda-\expect\left[\frac{2\zeta^{\prime}\left(-\gamma Z_{1}\right)}{1+{b}\zeta^{\prime\prime}\left(\textrm{prox}_{{b}\zeta}\left({\mu}\gamma Z_{1}+{\sigma}Z_{2}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle{\sigma}^{2}\kappa-\expect\left[2\zeta^{\prime}\left(-\gamma Z_{1}\right)\left\{{\mu}\gamma Z_{1}+{\sigma}Z_{2}-\textrm{prox}_{{b}\zeta}\left({\mu}\gamma Z_{1}+{\sigma}Z_{2}\right)\right\}^{2}\right]

5
Concluding remarks

We have described the asymptotic behaviour of the mDYPL estimator for
high dimensional logistic regression under Gaussian covariates and
arbitrary covariate covariance. We generalize previous results about
aggregate asymptotics and inference for the ML estimator, which are
confined to a limited range of
(\kappa,\gamma)
values below the
phase transition curve of
Candès and
Sur 2020
, to general

(\kappa,\gamma)
values. We show that the adaptive shrinkage factor

\alpha=1/(1+\kappa)
is appealing in that it admits a solution to
(
9
) for a wide range of
(\kappa,\gamma)
values, so
that the mDYPL estimator can be adequately scaled for signal recovery
and inference in high dimensions.
We also illustrate that the shrinkage factor
\alpha
of the DY prior
can be tuned, so that the mDYPL estimator has desirable, or optimal
asymptotic properties. For example, by tuning
\alpha
, the rescaled
mDYPL estimator has been found to achieve a minimum MSE that is
comparable to the rescaled ridge estimator of

Salehi
et al. 2019
, with the added benefit of being accompanied
by inferential procedures with provable asymptotic properties.

On a technical note, Appendix
provides a rigorous
approximation argument to apply the AMP framework to the logistic
regression model, and which has hitherto been missing from recent
literature, as noted in
Feng
et al. 2022
. Although a side
contribution, this may be of separate interest for the development of
AMP-instances where the Lipschitz assumption fails. Finally, we manage
to establish asymptotic results for MPL estimators with penalty
functions that are not separable, which paves the way for similar such
results for other penalty functions which reduce to a perturbation of
the responses, most notably the mJPL estimator.

Overall, the developments in the current paper provide a solid
foundation for the recovery of estimation and inferential performance
in binomial-response models with
p/n\to\kappa\in(0,1)
, with
an intercept and nongaussian covariates, and link functions other than
the logistic, irrespective of the existence of the ML estimate, the
dimension of the covariates and the signal strength. For example,
when the logistic regression model has an intercept,

Zhao
et al. 2022
and the associated empirical
supporting evidence are useful when studying the behaviour of the
mDYPL estimator in that setting.
Furthermore, the empirical evidence provided in

Zhao
et al. 2022
suggests that the asymptotic theory
we developed can be extended to a large family of distributions with
sufficiently light tails. Translating results in the AMP literature
about generalisations to nongaussian designs (see, for example,

Bayati
et al. 2015
and
Chen and
Lam 2021
) to logistic
regression estimated using mDYPL seems fruitful in that direction. The
signal strength can also be efficiently estimated using recent
developments in
Yadlowsky
et al. 2021
.

In our work we assumed the existence of a solution to equations
(
9
) for specific values of

(\alpha,\kappa,\gamma)
. Recent works such as

Sur and
Candès 2019
and
Salehi
et al. 2019
also rely in
similar such assumptions. Despite that we have been able to obtain
solutions for a wide range of
(\alpha,\kappa,\gamma)
,
characterising the existence of solutions to (
9
)
across the
(\kappa,\gamma)
plane would be a useful development.

Theorem
3.2
shows that
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}
is of order

\sqrt{n}
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
when the ML estimate exists,
and that for any
(\kappa,\gamma)
, there exists a range of shrinkage
factors for which the same holds. Evidently, that result is a partial
characterisation of the the boundedness of
\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\textrm{\tiny DY}}\|_{2}
for any

(\kappa,\gamma)
. However, we provide strong empirical evidence that
the mDYPL estimator is bounded even in cases where we are not able to
obtain a solution to (
9
).
