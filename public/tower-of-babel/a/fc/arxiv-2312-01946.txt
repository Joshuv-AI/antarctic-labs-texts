The Evaluation of Graphs on Surfaces for State-Sum Models with Defects
Julian FARNSTEINER and Christoph SCHWEIGERT

Abstract
The evaluation of graphs on 2-spheres is a central ingredient of the Turaev–Viro construction of three-dimensional topological field theories. In this article, we introduce a class of graphs, called extruded graphs, that is relevant for the Turaev–Viro construction with general defect configurations involving defects of various dimensions. We define the evaluation of extruded graphs and show that it is invariant under a set of moves. This ensures the computability and uniqueness of our evaluation.

Introduction

Topological field theories have mediated many relations between topology and representation theory: they also have applications in
the theory of topological phases of matter, in quantum computing and for the
construction of correlators of 2-dimensional conformal field theories.
As a consequence, topological field theories, in particular in three dimensions,
have been intensely
studied in the last few decades. Various constructions of topological field
theories have been developed; the state-sum construction combines the virtues
of providing fully local theories, together explicit tools for concrete computations.

It is by now well accepted that topological field theories should be studied
on stratified manifolds where strata of various codimensions can be endowed with representation-theoretic
decorations. Such strata, also called defects,
are central for applications to representation theory; they also
provide insight in
symmetries and dualities of the topological field theories; for an early
study in the case of 3-dimensional Dijkgraaf–Witten theories see, for example,
[

In the context of state-sum constructions, defects have recently been studied

of skeleton; it is a natural candidate for the Turaev–Viro invariant.

The vertex evaluation is thus a central ingredient of the Turaev–Viro
construction. A Turaev–Viro construction for a general defect 3-manifold
needs such an evaluation for extruded graphs.
The main result of this paper is such an evaluation, along with the proof that
it has all the properties that make it algorithmically computable.
This work does not contain a proof that a Turaev–Viro theory can be built using our evaluation.
In particular, proving the independence of the invariant for 3-manifolds from the choice of skeleton involves combinatorial geometry for stratified manifolds for which the authors are not experts.
Some more discussion on the assembly into a state-sum model can be found in
[

We expect the resulting theory to be in agreement with the one presented recently in
[
7

.

To define such a vertex evaluation one could
envisage the following (standard) strategy: exhibit a series of moves that reduces the
extruded graph to a standard graph on the sphere. A good candidate for such a standard graph would
be a loop on the sphere with a marked point labeled by an endomorphism. The
standard graph could then
be evaluated by a trace. This strategy has a crucial problem: one has to show that
any combination of moves that simplifies a graph gives a trace with the same numerical value.
This requires a careful analysis of relations between different moves. In our situation, this
is a very difficult problem, since the combinatorics of extruded graphs is involved and, to our knowledge, has not been developed.

Hence, in this paper, we use a rather different strategy: we
define
an
evaluation for
all
extruded graphs and then
prove theorems stating that a certain set of natural moves does not change the value
of the evaluation. The main idea of our definition is as follows: the block space
\mathrm{T}_{v}

in which the label
\varphi
for a vertex
v
is chosen comes by definition

[
20
]
as a subspace
of a
pre-block space

\mathrm{T}^{\mathrm{p}}_{v}
. We show that the labels we have chosen
for the state-sum variables and the invariant tensors
combine to an element in the pre-block space
\mathrm{T}^{\mathrm{p}}
. We then use the spherical structure on the fusion categories
to exhibit
\mathrm{T}
not only as a subspace, but as a retract of
\mathrm{T}^{\mathrm{p}}
. This defines
the evaluation. We then introduce a set of moves and
prove
that
the moves leave the evaluation
invariant. The set of moves allows us to reduce the graph so that in the end,
we evaluate a trace. This ensures that our evaluation procedure
can be explicitly implemented in practice.

For the practitioner, the content of this article can thus be reduced
to the following statements: there is a well-defined evaluation procedure for extruded graphs, and using the
moves described in Section
4.1
, the evaluation of any extruded graph can be reduced to the computation
of a trace on a bimodule category.

This paper is organized as follows:
Section

We will mainly work with
\mathbb{K}

When not stated otherwise, all linear categories are also assumed finite and semisimple.

A linear category

Lemma 2.2

(

)
.

The trace of a

2.2
Monoidal categories

We treat all monoidal categories as strict.
For the monoidal product of two objects
a
and
b
, we simply write
ab
, with the exception of the tensor product
\otimes
of vector spaces.
Reversing the product of a monoidal category
\mathcal{A}
leads to another monoidal category, denoted
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{rev}}
.

The monoidal categories we consider are pivotal, i.e., they are a rigid and a monoidal isomorphism from the bidual functor to the identity functor has been chosen
[
14
, Definition 4.7.7]
.
Thanks to the coherence theorem
[
27
, Theorem 2.2]
, we treat the pivotal structure as strict as well.
We will therefore not distinguish between left and right dualities, and denote by
a^{*}
the dual of an object
a
in a pivotal category.
We occasionally make use of the fact that a pivotal structure on a category
\mathcal{A}
gives rise to a distinguished monoidal equivalence

\overline{\mathcal{A}}\cong{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{rev}}
,
which is the identity on objects and sends morphisms
\overline{f}
to the dual morphism
f^{*}
.
We denote the evaluation and coevaluation maps by

{\mathrm{ev}}_{a}\colon\ aa^{*}\to\mathds{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{}_{a}{\mathrm{coev}}\colon\ \mathds{1}\to a^{*}a.

(2.1)

Instead of distinguishing between left and right evaluation and coevaluation, we stick to the morphisms (
2.1
) and use

{\mathrm{ev}}_{a^{*}}\colon\ a^{*}a\to\mathds{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{}_{a^{*}}{\mathrm{coev}}\colon\ \mathds{1}\to aa^{*}

for the other duality.

Both the left and the right pivotal trace endow a given pivotal fusion category
\mathcal{A}
with the structure of a Calabi–Yau category.
If the two traces agree, the category
\mathcal{A}
is called
spherical
, and it carries the structure of a Calabi–Yau category in a canonical way.

2.5
Traced bimodule categories

Given a monoidal category
\mathcal{A}
, we consider
left
\mathcal{A}
-module categories

\mathcal{M}
.
This is a category equipped with an (exact)
action functor

\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
, denoted
(a,m)\mapsto am
, together with coherence data and axioms.
For details, we refer to
[
14
]
.

We are interested only in finite and semisimple module categories over fusion categories.
In particular, all of our module categories are exact.

There are the obvious related notions of right module categories and bimodule categories.
A left
\mathcal{A}
-module category can be equivalently described as a right
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{rev}}
-module category.
An
\mathcal{A}
-
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule category is the same as a left
\mathcal{A}\btimes{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{rev}}
-module category.
Lastly, every linear category is canonically equipped with the structure of a left or right
\mathrm{vect}
-module category. Hence, a left
\mathcal{A}
-module category can also be seen as an
\mathcal{A}
-
\mathrm{vect}
-bimodule category.

We will make tacit use of these identifications.
To keep in mind which type of module category a particular category is, we sometimes write, for instance,
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{B}}=\mathcal{M}
for an
\mathcal{A}
-
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule category, and similar for left and right module categories.

For every fusion category
\mathcal{A}
, we can consider the
regular bimodule category

{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}
, whose action functors are given by the monoidal product.

Because we work over
finite
tensor categories, the bimodule categories we consider have internal Homs with respect to each action.
This means that given objects
m,n
in a bimodule category
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{B}}
, there are
internal Hom-objects

{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{m},{n}]\in\mathcal{A}
and
{}_{\mathcal{B}}[{m},{n}]\in\mathcal{B}
, characterized by the existence of isomorphisms

{{}_{\mathcal{A}}}\langle a,{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{m},{n}]\rangle\cong{{}_{\mathcal{M}}}\langle am,n\rangle
,
{{}_{\mathcal{B}}}\langle b,{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{m},{n}]\rangle\cong{{}_{\mathcal{M}}}\langle bm,n\rangle
.

Pivotal structures on monoidal categories lead to a unique module structure on the opposite of a module category:
For a bimodule category
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{B}}
over pivotal categories
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, the opposite category
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
is canonically a
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule category, with actions defined by
b\overline{m}a\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\overline{a^{*}mb^{*}}
.

If a bimodule category
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{B}}
over pivotal categories
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
also carries the structure of a Calabi–Yau category, it is natural to impose the following consistency condition:
Let
{a\in\mathcal{A}}
,
{b\in\mathcal{B}}
, and
m\in\mathcal{M}
be objects, and let
f\colon amb\to amb
be an endomorphism in
\mathcal{M}
.
The morphism

\displaystyle\mathrm{ptr}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=({\mathrm{ev}}_{a^{*}}m{\mathrm{ev}}_{b})\circ(a^{*}fb^{*})\circ({}_{a}{\mathrm{coev}}m{}_{b^{*}}{\mathrm{coev}}),

(2.6)

which is an endomorphism of
m
, is called the
partial trace
of
f
with respect to
a
and
b
.
The consistency condition between the Calabi–Yau structure and the module actions on
\mathcal{M}
mentioned above is

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{M}}(f)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathrm{ptr}(f)).

(2.7)

Equation (
2.7
) can be illustrated in a string diagram

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{tracedef_a_1}}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{M}}\underbrace{\left(\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{tracedef_b_1}}\right)}_{\mathrm{ptr}(f)}.

Following
[
29
]
, we call a Calabi–Yau structure on a bimodule category over pivotal fusion categories which satisfies the partial trace property (
2.7
) a
bimodule trace
.
The corresponding notion for a one-sided module category is called a
module trace
.
We refer to a (bi-)module category equipped with a (bi-)module trace as a
traced
(
bi-
)
module category
.
The proof of the next lemma is left to the reader.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a pivotal fusion category. The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

\mathcal{A}
is a spherical fusion category.

(2)

The regular bimodule category
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}
admits a bimodule trace.

If either statement is true, then the bimodule trace on
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}
is unique up to a normalization factor.
If in addition, the bimodule trace is normalized such that the dimension of the monoidal unit is
1
, then the bimodule trace is equal to the
(
left or right
)
pivotal trace.

Remark 2.4
.

Given two
\mathcal{A}
-module categories
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, the product category
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}
can be equipped with the structure of an
\mathcal{A}
-module category by simultaneous action on the components, which is denoted
\mathcal{M}\boxplus\mathcal{N}
.
This module category is called the direct sum of
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
.
A module category
\mathcal{M}
is called
indecomposable
if the existence of an equivalence of module categories
{\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{N}\boxplus\mathcal{K}}
implies that either
\mathcal{N}
or
\mathcal{K}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{M}
, with the other being trivial.
The structure of a bimodule trace on an indecomposable bimodule category is, if it exists, unique up to a scalar
[
29
, Proposition 4.4]
.
The question whether or not a (bi-)module category admits a bimodule trace can be stated as an eigenvalue problem
[
29
, Propositions 5.4 and 5.7]
.

Remark 2.5
.

The relevance of the structure of a bimodule trace for TFTs can also be understood from the perspective of homotopy fixed points:
It is expected that spherical fusion categories are
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-fixed points in the homotopy category of fusion categories
[
13
]
.
We expect traced bimodule categories to be the corresponding
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-fixed point morphisms.

A module trace also provides a relation between the (pivotal) dimension of an internal Hom object, and the dimensions of the objects in the module category.

Lemma 2.6
.

Given a left
\mathcal{A}
-module category
\mathcal{M}
over a spherical fusion category
\mathcal{A}
with a module trace, the dimension of the internal Hom for simple objects
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{M}
can be computed as follows

\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{\mathbf{m}},{\mathbf{n}}]}=\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}}{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{n}}.

Proof.

Following
[
29
, Section 5]
, we call the square matrix
Q
with entries

Q_{\mathbf{nm}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{\mathbf{m}},{\mathbf{n}}]}

for (representatives of isoclasses of) simple objects
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{M}
the
dimension matrix
of
\mathcal{M}
.
From
[
29
, Proposition 5.7]
, we know that
Q
is of rank 1 with only non-zero entries.
This implies that
Q
is of the form
Q_{\mathbf{nm}}=\xi_{\mathbf{n}}\zeta_{\mathbf{m}}
for non-zero scalars
\xi_{\mathbf{n}},\zeta_{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathbb{K}
.

Moreover, the module trace on
\mathcal{M}
causes the dual of the internal Hom to behave much like the dual of the Hom-space

{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{m},{n}]^{*}={}_{\mathcal{A}}[{n},{m}]

(2.8)

for all objects
m,n\in\mathcal{M}
.
This is easy to check using the Yoneda lemma; for
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle a,{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{n},{m}]\rangle&\cong\langle an,m\rangle\cong\langle m,an\rangle^{*}\cong\langle a^{*}m,n\rangle^{*}\\
&\cong\langle a^{*},{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{m},{n}]\rangle^{*}\cong\langle{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{m},{n}],a^{*}\rangle\cong\langle a,{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{m},{n}]^{*}\rangle.\end{aligned}

Due to (
2.8
), the dimension matrix
Q
is symmetric, which means that
\xi_{\mathbf{m}}=\zeta_{\mathbf{m}}
for all simple representatives
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}
.

The dimension vector is an eigenvector of the dimension matrix
Q
with eigenvalue
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}
, see
[
29
, Proposition 5.4]

\sum_{\mathbf{m}}Q_{\mathbf{nm}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{m}}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\xi_{\mathbf{n}}\xi_{\mathbf{m}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{m}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{n}}.

As
\xi_{\mathbf{n}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}
are non-zero, this is equivalent to the following:

\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\xi_{\mathbf{m}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{m}}}{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{n}}}{\xi_{\mathbf{n}}}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\lambda,

where we introduced a non-zero scalar
\lambda
that does not depend on
\mathbf{n}
.
Substituting
\xi_{\mathbf{m}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{m}}}{\lambda}
, we find

\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{m}}}{\lambda\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}}=\lambda,\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}}}{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}}=\lambda^{2}.

Finally, we obtain the statement of the lemma

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{{}_{\mathcal{A}}[{\mathbf{m}},{\mathbf{n}}]}

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathbf{nm}}=\xi_{\mathbf{n}}\xi_{\mathbf{m}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{n}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{m}}}{\lambda^{2}}=\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}}{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbf{n}}.

∎

2.6
Centers of bimodule categories

Let
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{A}}
be an
\mathcal{A}
-
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule category.
Given an object
m\in\mathcal{M}
, a family of isomorphisms
\mathrm{br}^{z}_{a}\colon am\to ma
for each object
a\in\mathcal{A}
, satisfying the conditions
\mathrm{br}^{z}_{ab}=(\mathrm{br}^{z}_{a}b)\circ(a\mathrm{br}^{z}_{b})
and
{\mathrm{br}^{z}_{\mathds{1}}=\mathrm{id}_{m}}
is called a
balancing
for
m
.
The linear category
Z_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})
, whose objects are pairs
{z=(m,\mathrm{br}^{z})}
, where
m\in\mathcal{M}
and
\mathrm{br}^{z}
is a balancing for
m
, and whose morphisms
f\colon z\to z^{\prime}=(m^{\prime},\mathrm{br}^{z^{\prime}})
are morphisms
f\colon m\to m^{\prime}
satisfying the condition that
\mathrm{br}^{z^{\prime}}_{a}\circ(af)=(fa)\circ\mathrm{br}^{z}_{a}
, is called the
center
of
\mathcal{M}

We denote the forgetful functor
z\mapsto m
, which forgets the balancing, by
U\colon Z_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})\to\mathcal{M}
.
If
\mathcal{M}
is the regular bimodule category
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}
, the center
Z_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A})
is the Drinfeld center of
\mathcal{A}
.

Given objects
z\in Z_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we define a particular pair of morphisms in
\mathcal{M}

{\mathrm{brev}_{z}}_{a}\colon\ aU(z)a^{*}\to U(z)\qquad\text{and}\qquad{}_{a}{\mathrm{cobrev}_{z}}\colon\ U(z)\to a^{*}U(z)a,

to be called
braided evaluation
and
braided coevaluation
.
They can be defined in two equivalent ways, which are the left- and right-hand sides of the equations below

\displaystyle{\mathrm{brev}_{z}}_{a}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(U(z){\mathrm{ev}}_{a})\circ(\mathrm{br}^{z}_{a}a^{*})=({\mathrm{ev}}_{a}U(z))\circ\big(a(\mathrm{br}^{z}_{a^{*}})^{-1}\big),

\displaystyle{}_{a}{\mathrm{cobrev}_{z}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\big((\mathrm{br}^{z}_{a^{*}}\big)^{-1}a)\circ(U(z){}_{a}{\mathrm{coev}})=(a^{*}\mathrm{br}^{z}_{a})\circ({}_{a}{\mathrm{coev}}U(z)).

(2.10)

In diagrammatic notation, we represent the braided evaluation and coevaluation as

{\mathrm{brev}_{z}}_{a}=\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{pic_brev}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{}_{a}{\mathrm{cobrev}_{z}}=\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{pic_cobrev}},

(2.11)

where we introduced the convention that objects of the center are drawn as red lines.
Pictures such as (
2.11
) containing objects in the center
Z_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})
of a bimodule category
\mathcal{M}
are always diagrams in
\mathcal{M}
, not
Z_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})
.
When evaluating such diagrams, the forgetful functor has to be applied to all objects labeling red lines, and crossings between red and black lines need to be replaced by balancings. Unlike in the diagrammatic calculus for a braided monoidal category, there are no over- and under-crossings that need to be distinguished.

Remark 2.8
.

Any linear category
\mathcal{X}
can be seen as a
\mathrm{vect}
-
\mathrm{vect}
-bimodule category.
With respect to these
\mathrm{vect}
-actions, every object
x\in\mathcal{X}
admits a balancing, and all balancings are isomorphic. Hence, there is an equivalence of categories
\mathcal{X}\cong Z_{\mathrm{vect}}(\mathcal{X})
.

Remark 2.9
.

An alternative model for the center is given by the category of bimodule functors and bimodule natural transformations
\mathrm{Fun}_{{\mathcal{A}}|{\mathcal{A}}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{M}})
, see
[
15
, Section 2.8]
: There is a canonical equivalence of categories

\mathrm{Fun}_{{\mathcal{A}}|{\mathcal{A}}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{M}})\cong Z_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})\colon F\mapsto F(\mathds{1})
.

2.7
Balanced functors and their diagrams

The center of a module category
\mathcal{M}
over a monoidal category
\mathcal{A}

can also be characterized by a universal property:
A
balanced functor
consists of a functor
F\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{X}
, equipped with a family of isomorphisms

\gamma_{a,m}\colon F(am)\to F(ma)
, natural in
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}
, which moreover satisfies
\gamma_{ab,m}=\gamma_{b,ma}\circ\gamma_{a,bm}
.
Balanced functors form a category
\mathrm{Fun}^{\gamma}({\mathcal{M}},{\mathcal{X}})
, whose morphisms are balanced natural transformations, i.e., those natural transformations
\eta\colon F\Rightarrow G
which are compatible with the balancings
\gamma^{F}
and
\gamma^{G}
of
F
and
G
in that
\gamma_{a,m}^{G}\circ\eta_{am}=\eta_{ma}\circ\gamma_{a,m}^{F}
.
Up to equivalence, the center
Z_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})
is the unique category such that there is an equivalence of functor categories

\mathrm{Fun}^{\gamma}({\mathcal{M}},{\mathcal{X}})\cong\mathrm{Fun}({Z_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})},{\mathcal{X}})
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a (left) module category over any monoidal category
\mathcal{A}
, and let
\mathcal{N}
be a right
\mathcal{A}
-module category.
The category
\mathcal{N}\btimes\mathcal{M}
is an
\mathcal{A}
-
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule category. The balanced functors out of categories of this form are of particular interest to us.
We usually denote the balancing of a functor
F\colon\mathcal{N}\btimes\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{X}
into a category
\mathcal{X}
as
\gamma_{n,a,m}\colon F(na,m)\to F(n,am)
.

The prototypical example of such a balanced functor is the Hom-functor of a module category
\mathcal{M}
over a rigid monoidal category
\mathcal{A}
:
Let
\mathcal{N}=\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, which is a right
\mathcal{A}
-module category with action
\overline{m}a\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\overline{a^{*}m}
, where
a^{*}
here denotes the
right
dual.
Then the evaluation morphism defines isomorphisms

\bigl\langle\overline{m}a,m^{\prime}\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle\overline{a^{*}m},m^{\prime}\bigr\rangle\cong\bigl\langle\overline{m},am^{\prime}\bigr\rangle,

which assemble into a balancing of the Hom-functor.
The Hom-functor of a bimodule category
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{B}}
has even more structure, as it is balanced with respect to the
\mathcal{A}\btimes{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{rev}}
-action on
\mathcal{M}
.
In general, if
\mathcal{N}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule category and
\mathcal{M}
is an
\mathcal{A}
-
\mathcal{B}
-bimodule category, then we say a functor

F\colon\ {}_{\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{A}}\btimes{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{B}}\to\mathcal{X}

is
bi-balanced
, if it is equipped with a balancing between the right
\mathcal{A}\btimes{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{rev}}
-action on
\mathcal{N}
and the left
\mathcal{A}\btimes{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathrm{rev}}
-action on
\mathcal{M}
.

We also call functors

G\colon\ {}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{A}}\btimes{}_{\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{B}}\to\mathcal{X},

bi-balanced, if they are equipped with two balancings, one for
\mathcal{M}
and one for
\mathcal{N}
, which are required to commute.
In the ambiguous case
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
, a functor can be bi-balanced in either way. We introduce the following terminology

\displaystyle\qquad\text{``disconnected'' balancings},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{``connected'' balancings}.

(2.13)

We next develop a graphical calculus for bi-balanced functors of the “connected” type that is tailored to our purposes.
In particular, given a bi-balanced functor
S\colon\overline{\mathcal{N}}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{X}
, we will be able to pictorially represent those morphisms in
\mathcal{X}
which are built from

•

morphisms of the form
S(f,g)
where
f
and
g
are morphisms in
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}
, respectively; and

•

the balancing isomorphisms of
S
, and their inverses.

Our diagrams specialize to the diagrams we introduced in Section
2.3
for the choice of the Hom-functor as bi-balanced functor
S
.

For morphisms
m_{1}\xrightarrow{f}m_{2}\xrightarrow{g}{m_{3}}
in
\mathcal{M}
and
n_{2}\xrightarrow{h}n_{1}
in
\mathcal{N}
, we write

\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{pic_funcdiag_profun_2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=S\left(h,\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{pic_strdiag_gf}}\right)=S(h,g\circ f),

which represents a morphism
S(n_{1},m_{1})\to S(n_{2},m_{3})
in
\mathcal{X}
.
Given objects
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
m\in\mathcal{M}
, and
{n\in\mathcal{N}}
, we draw the following diagram to represent the left balancing of
S

\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{pic_funcdiag_bal}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\gamma_{n,a,m}\colon\ S(a^{*}n,m)\to S(n,am).

(2.14)

As usual, the juxtaposition of a strands labeled by
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}
stands for the object
{am\in\mathcal{M}}
.
For bi-balanced functors, bending around strands is allowed both on the left and on the right-hand side of diagrams.
To illustrate this, consider the following picture, which represents a morphism
S(a^{*}n,mb^{*})\to S\big(nb,m^{\prime}\big)
in
\mathcal{X}

\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{pic_funcdiag_bibal_ex}}=S(f,nb)\circ\gamma_{n,b,am}^{\text{right}-1}\circ\gamma_{n,a,mb}^{\text{left}}.

(2.15)

If we consider the special case where
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{B}={}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}
is the regular bimodule category, and
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{vect}
, we can choose
S
to be the Hom-functor of
\mathcal{A}
, which is a bi-balanced functor.
Diagrams such as (
2.15
) then specialize to the diagrams we encountered in Section
2.3
, with the modification that the previously empty double-box is now labeled with the name of a functor, in this case Hom.
The reason why these diagrams really specialize to the diagrams from Section
2.3
for the Hom-functor is that “bending around strands” as in (
2.14
) encodes the Hom-functor’s balancing, which is defined using a coevaluation; on the other hand, the coevaluation is also represented by bending around a strand in the diagrams from Section
2.3
, which are derived from string diagrams.
The Hom-functor of a module category
\mathcal{M}
over
\mathcal{A}
also comes with a linear map
\bigl\langle m,m^{\prime}\bigr\rangle\to\bigl\langle am,am^{\prime}\bigr\rangle
for each triple of objects
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
, which is part of the data of module action functor.
If
\mathcal{A}
is a rigid and pivotal, this linear map can be expressed using the balancing of the Hom-functor, together with either an evaluation or a coevaluation.
This is true of any balanced functor
S\colon\overline{\mathcal{N}}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{X}
over a pivotal category
\mathcal{A}
:
The two expressions

\gamma_{an,a,m}\circ S({}_{a}{\mathrm{coev}}n,m)=\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\includegraphics[scale]{pic_funcdiag_sidestrand}}=\gamma^{-1}_{n,a^{*},am}\circ S(n,{\mathrm{ev}}_{a^{*}}m)

are equal.

Of course, diagrams for bi-balanced functors can be composed, and the composition operation is the same we already encountered in (
2.3
): inserting the pre-composed diagram into the double-box of the post-composed diagram.
In this way, our diagrams are similar to other approaches to graphical calculi, such as the “corollas” appearing, for example, in
[
21
, for the composition see Example 2.14]
.

We leave it to the reader to convince themself that the evaluation of diagrams for bi-balanced functors is well defined and that isotopic diagrams evaluate to equal morphisms.
In any case, the diagrams throughout this work are meant as illustrations that can, at any point, be translated into standard notation.

Let now
{}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{A}}

\mathcal{A}
-

Proof of Lemma

.

Remark 3.2
.

We briefly discuss some differences to

Here, all manifolds are oriented as opposed to 2-framed.
To compensate this loss of topological structure, we need additional algebraic structure:
The tensor categories considered in

We also use a spherical structure on the bimodule categories.
Furthermore, all categories we consider are semisimple: by

We do not discuss the question whether a similar evaluation can be defined for extruded graphs

Remark 3.3
.

The terminology

In Definition

An alternative view is three-dimensional, by considering the cylinder over the surface.

From the nodes, perpendicular circular

These

From this perspective, nodes are no longer labeled.
Note that a special case of ray categories are Drinfeld centers which are braided and thus categorically inherently three-dimensional objects.
Further discussion can be found in
[

Mathematically, both perspectives are completely equivalent; they are simply different ways of thinking about the same data.

Moves are certain transformations of an extruded graph, changing both its topological and algebraic data.
They make the abstractly defined evaluation procedure algorithmically computable.
