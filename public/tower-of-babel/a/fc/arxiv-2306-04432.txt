A note on the non-existence of functors
E. Dror Farjoun, S. O. Ivanov, A. Krasilnikov, A. Zaikovskii

Abstract
. We consider several types of non-existence theorems for functors. For example, there are no nontrivial functors from the category of groups (or the category of pointed sets, or vector spaces) to any small category. Another type of questions that we consider are questions about nonexistence of subfunctors and quotients of the identity functor on the category of groups (or abelian groups). For example, there is no a natural non-trivial way to define an abelian subgroup of a group, or a perfect quotient group of a group. As an auxiliary result we prove that, for any non-trivial subfunctor F F of the identity functor on the category of groups, any group can be embedded into a simple group that lies in the essential image of F . F. The paper concludes with a few questions regarding the non-existence of certain (co-)augmented functors in the ∞ \infty -category of spaces.



1.
Introduction

It is natural to expect that many large and “rich” categories should have only trivial functors to any small category. A typical example of a statement proven below is that any functor from the category of groups to the category of finite groups is constant. We show that any functor from a strongly connected (i.e. all hom-sets are non-empty) category
\mathpzc{C}
with small products to a small category
\mathpzc{D}
is a constant functor. Moreover, we prove a version of this statement that depends on a cardinal.

Theorem
.

If there is a cardinal
\kappa
such that
\mathpzc{C}
is a strongly connected category that has products indexed by sets of cardinality
\leq\kappa,
and
\mathpzc{D}
is an essentially small category whose hom-sets have cardinality
\leq\kappa,
then any functor from
\mathpzc{C}
to
\mathpzc{D}
is constant.

Interesting examples of consequences of this theorem are given by the following statements. Any functor from the category
of countable groups to the category of finitely generated groups is constant. For any two cardinals
\kappa
and
\kappa^{\prime}
such that
\kappa\geq\max(2^{\kappa^{\prime}},\aleph_{0}),
any functor from the category of pointed sets of cardinality at most
\kappa
to the category of pointed sets of cardinality at most
\kappa^{\prime}
is constant.

In a slightly different vein, we consider the impossibility of certain subfunctors and quotients of the identity functor of the category of groups, and the category of abelian groups. For example, the abelianization of a group
G\mapsto G/[G,G]
is a quotient of the identity functor on the category of groups that takes values in the subcategory of abelian groups.
So, there is a natural quotient of any group which is abelian. Is it possible to find a natural abelian subgroup in a group
F(G)\subseteq G
in a non-trivial way?
The center of a group comes to mind, but it is not a functor. We show that this is impossible, there is no a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor on the category of groups taking values in the subcategory of abelian groups.
In order to prove this statement, we study the essential image of a sub-functor of the identity functor.
The essential image
\mathcal{IM}(F)\subseteq{\sf Ob}(\mathpzc{D})
of a functor
F:\mathpzc{C}\to\mathpzc{D}
is defined as the class of objects isomorphic to
Fc
for some
c.
It is well known that any group can be embedded into a simple group
[
11
]
. The following theorem can be interpreted as a strengthening of this statement.

Theorem
.

Let
F
be a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor on the category of groups, and
G
be a group. Then there exists a simple group
S
such that
G\subseteq S
and
S=FS\in\mathcal{IM}(F).

This theorem implies that for any proper variety of groups
\mathcal{V}
and any augmented functor on the category of groups
F:{\sf Gr}\to{\sf Gr}
with augmentation
\varepsilon:F\to{\rm Id},
the augmentation
\varepsilon
is trivial.

A similar question to the question about a natural abelian subgroup of a group is the question about a natural perfect quotient of a group. There is a natural perfect subgroup of any group called perfect core. Is there a natural perfect quotient? We answer this question in the negative. Moreover, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem
.

Let
Q
be a non-trivial quotient of the identity functor on the category of groups. Then
\mathcal{IM}(Q)
contains either a non-trivial finite cyclic group, or all free abelian groups.

In particular, we obtain that, for any natural quotient
G\twoheadrightarrow QG
of a group with
QG
always a free group,
QG
is trivial. Similarly if
QG
is always either trivial or a non-abelian group then
QG
is identically the trivial group. We also prove some similar statements about the category of abelian groups.

Note that the subcategory of abelian groups is reflective in the category of groups. There are many other examples of reflective subcategories of groups: nilpotent groups of class
n
, metabelian groups,
n
-Burnside groups, uniquely divisible groups,
HR
-local groups of Bousfield (for more examples see
[
1
]
). We show that there is no a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor in the category of groups having values in these subcategories.

Theorem
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a proper reflective subcategory of the category of groups. Then there is no a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor on the category of groups having values in
\mathcal{R}.

However, the dual statement does not hold (Example
3.15
), and the analogue of this statement for the category of abelian groups does not hold as well (Example
3.21
).

Our work was partly motivated by questions from homotopy theory. It is natural to expect that there should be analogous non-existence propositions in the
\infty
-category of spaces. Next, we will propose some “non-existence” questions from homotopy theory. We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{\geq 1}_{*}
the
\infty
-category of pointed connected spaces, and by
\mathcal{S}p
the
\infty
-category of spectra. The following question is a natural analogue of the result about the non-existence of a non-trivial natural abelian subgroup in a group.

Question 1
.

For a functor
F:\mathcal{S}^{\geq 1}_{*}\to\mathcal{S}p
from the
\infty
-category of spaces to spectra, is every natural transformation to the identity functor
\Omega^{\infty}FX\to X
null-homotopic?

Question 2
.

Let
F:\mathcal{S}^{\geq 1}_{*}\to\mathcal{S}^{\geq 1}_{*}
be a augmented functor from the
\infty
-category of pointed connected spaces to itself and
n\geq 1
be an integer such that the map
\pi_{n}(FX)\to\pi_{n}(X)
is trivial for all
X
. Does it follow that the augmentation

FX\to X
naturally and uniquely factors homotopically through the
(n+1)
-th stage of the Whitehead tower

Or, more technically, the
n
-connected cover is the terminal augmented functor,
FX\to X,
with
\pi_{n}(FX)\to\pi_{n}(X)
is the zero map (note that here the zero map is assumed only for one

). In a slogan, “if

kills one homotopy group, it also kills all the lower ones”.

Similar results about coaugmented functors can be found in
[
6
]
. From each of the above implication one deduces immediately that, on the
\infty
-category of spaces, there are only trivial natural transformations
F\to{\rm Id}
such that and for all
X
one has
FX\cong K(A,n)
for some
n\geq 1.
Note that the last space has the structure of abelian group, thus this implication is a direct analog of Theorem
3.11
below.

Functors from large to small categories

The following non-existence results are quick implications of the above lemma.
A category
\mathpzc{C}
is called
strongly connected
if for any two objects
c,c^{\prime}\in\mathpzc{C}
the hom-set
\mathpzc{C}(c,c^{\prime})
is not empty.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\kappa
be a cardinal,

\mathpzc{C},\mathpzc{D}
be categories with products indexed by sets of cardinality
\kappa,
and let
F:\mathpzc{C}\to\mathpzc{D}
be a functor. Assume that
|\mathpzc{D}(F(c^{\prime}),F(c))|\leq\kappa
for any two objects
c^{\prime},c
of
\mathpzc{C}.
Then the following holds

(1)

for any two parallel morphisms
f,g:c^{\prime}\to c
of
\mathpzc{C}
we have
F(f)=F(g);

(2)

if
\mathpzc{C}
is strongly connected, then
F
is isomorphic to a constant functor.

Proof.

(1) Assume the contrary that there exists morphisms
f,g:c^{\prime}\to c
such that
F(f)\neq F(g).
Take a set
A
such that
|A|=\kappa
and consider a constant family
{\bf c}=(c_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in A},
where
c_{\alpha}=c.
Since the image of the map
\mathpzc{C}(c^{\prime},c_{\alpha})\to\mathpzc{D}(F(c^{\prime}),F(c_{\alpha}))
has at least two elements, the cardinality of the image of the map
\prod\mathpzc{C}(c^{\prime},c_{\alpha})\to\prod\mathpzc{D}(F(c^{\prime}),F(c_{\alpha}))
has cardinality at least
2^{\kappa}>\kappa.
On the other hand, by Lemma
2.1
the map
\prod\mathpzc{C}(c^{\prime},c_{\alpha})\to\prod\mathpzc{D}(F(c^{\prime}),F(c_{\alpha}))
factors through the set
\mathpzc{D}(F(c^{\prime}),F\left(\prod c_{\alpha}\right))
whose cardinality
\leq\kappa
by the assumption, which is impossible.

(2) Since
\mathpzc{C}
is strongly connected, it is sufficient to prove that for any morphism
f:c^{\prime}\to c
the morphism
F(f)
is an isomorphism. Take a morphism
g:c\to c^{\prime}.
By (1) we obtain
F(g)F(f)=F(gf)=F({\sf id}_{c^{\prime}})={\sf id}_{F(c^{\prime})}
and
F(f)F(g)=F(fg)=F({\sf id}_{c})={\sf id}_{F(c)}.
Then
F(f)
is an isomorphism.
∎

A category
\mathpzc{D}
is called essentially small, if it is equivalent to a small category.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mathpzc{C},\mathpzc{D}
be categories and
F:\mathpzc{C}\to\mathpzc{D}
be a functor. Assume that
\mathpzc{D}
is essentially small category and there exists a cardinal
\kappa
such that

\mathpzc{C}
has products indexed by any set of cardinality
\kappa
while
|\mathpzc{D}(d^{\prime},d)|\leq\kappa
for all objects
d^{\prime},d
of
\mathpzc{D}.
Then the following holds

(1)

for any two parallel arrows
f,g:c^{\prime}\to c
we have
F(f)=F(g);

(2)

if
\mathpzc{C}
is strongly connected, then
F
is isomorphic to a constant functor.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
\mathpzc{D}
is small.
Consider the category of presheaves
\hat{\mathpzc}{D}={\sf Funct}(\mathpzc{D}^{op},{\sf Sets})
and the Yoneda embedding
Y:\mathpzc{D}\to\hat{\mathpzc}{D}.
By the Yoneda lemma we obtain that

|\hat{\mathpzc}{D}(Y(d^{\prime}),Y(d))|=|\mathpzc{D}(d^{\prime},d)|\leq\kappa

for any objects
d^{\prime},d
of
\mathpzc{D}.
We also know that
\hat{\mathpzc}{D}
has all small products. Then we can apply Proposition
2.2
to the functor
YF:\mathpzc{C}\to\hat{\mathpzc}{D}.
The assertion follows from the fact that

is fully faithful.

Example 2.8
.

Denote by

the category, whose objects are the ordinals and morphisms are canonical embeddings. So

is a “big poset,” in fact a “big well ordered set.” Then

Therefore, the assumption of being strongly connected in Corollary

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
{\sf Gr}^{\sf count}
be the category of countable groups and
{\sf Gr}^{\sf f.g.}
be the category of finitely generated groups. Any functor
{\sf Gr}^{\sf count}\to{\sf Gr}^{\sf f.g.}
is isomorphic to a constant functor.

Proof.

Countable free product of countable groups is countable. Hence
{\sf Gr}^{\sf count}
has countable coproducts. The hom-sets between finitely generated groups are countable, because any homomorphism is uniquely defined by its values of generators. Therefore, if we take
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
and
\mathpzc{C}={\sf Gr}^{\sf count},
and
\mathpzc{D}={\sf Gr}^{\sf f.g.}
and use the dual version of the Proposition

{\sf Gr}^{\sf count}\to{\sf Gr}^{\sf f.g.}

\kappa

Let

Assume that

Example 2.11

(Functors from sets)
.

The category of all sets

Here for a natural number

3.
Subfunctors and quotients of the identity functor

3.1.
Subfunctors of the identity functor of the category of groups

Throughout this subsection, we denote by
F
a given sub-functor of the identity functor on the category of groups

(3.1)

F\subseteq\mathrm{Id}_{\sf Gr}.

A subgroup
H
of a group
G
is called
characteristic
(denoted by
H\triangleleft_{\sf ch}G
) if for any automorphism
\varphi:G\to G
we have
\varphi(H)\subseteq H.
Any characteristic subgroup is normal because it is invariant with respect to inner automorphisms. An advantage of characteristic subgroups over normal subgroups is that
K\triangleleft_{\sf ch}H
and

H\triangleleft_{\sf ch}G
implies
K\triangleleft_{\sf ch}G.
Note that the injectivity assumption on
F
namely,
FG\subseteq G,
implies directly that
F
preserves injectivity of maps.

Lemma 3.1
.

(1)

F(G)\triangleleft_{\sf ch}G
for any
G;

(2)

for any
H\triangleleft_{\sf ch}F(G)
we have

F(G)/H\subseteq F(G/H);

(3)

for any family of groups
(G_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in A}
the standard assembly map is injective:

\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{\alpha\in A}F(G_{\alpha})\subseteq F(\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{\alpha\in A}G_{\alpha}).

(4)

for any family of groups
(G_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in A}
the standard assembly map is injective:

F(\prod_{\alpha\in A}G_{\alpha})\subseteq\prod_{\alpha\in A}F(G_{\alpha}).

Proof.

(1) For any automorphism
\varphi
of
G
the map
F(\varphi)
is an automorphism of
F(G).
Since
F(\varphi)
is a restriction of
\varphi
to a subgroup, we obtain
\varphi(F(G))\subseteq F(G).

(2) Since
H\triangleleft_{\sf ch}F(G)
and
F(G)\triangleleft_{\sf ch}G,
we obtain that
H
is normal in
G.
Hence we can consider the projection
p:G\to G/H.
By the naturality of
F
we obtain that
F(G/H)
contains
p(F(G))=F(G)/H.

(3) The group
\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{\alpha\in A}F(G_{\alpha})
is the subgroup of
\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{\alpha\in A}G_{\alpha}
generated by the images of
F(G_{\alpha}).
The assertion follows.

(4) For any projection
p_{\beta}:\prod_{\alpha\in A}G_{\alpha}\to G_{\beta}
we have
p_{\beta}(F(\prod_{\alpha\in A}G_{\alpha}))\subseteq F(G_{\beta}).
The assertion follows.
∎

By the essential image
\mathcal{IM}(F)
of a functor
F:\mathpzc{C}\to\mathpzc{D}
we mean the class of objects isomorphic to an object of the form

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
F
be a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor on the category of groups, and
G
be a group. Then there exists a simple group
S
such that
G\subseteq S
and
S=FS\in\mathcal{IM}(F).

Proof.

Assume the contrary, that there is a group
B
such that for any simple group
S
containing
B,
we have
F(S)\neq S.
Since
F(S)
is a normal subgroup (Lemma
3.1
), we have
F(S)=1.

Every group can be embedded into a simple group
[
11
]
. Given a group
G,

take the product
G\times B
and consider a simple group
S_{G}
containing
G\times B.
Then we have
F(S_{G})=1.
Combining this with the inclusion
F(G)\subseteq F(S_{G}),
which is implied by functoriality, we obtain
F(G)=1
for any
G,
contradicting the assumption.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

This implies, in particular, that for any functor
F
as above, any group
G
can be embedded in a group of the form
FH,
for some group
H.

Next, let us consider augmented functors
FG\to G,
taking values in any
proper reflective subcategory
of groups. A full subcategory
\mathpzc{D}
of a category
\mathpzc{C}
is called reflective, if for any object of
c
of
\mathpzc{C}
there is a universal arrow
c\to d
to an object of
\mathpzc{D}.
In other words, a full subcategory
\mathpzc{D}\subseteq\mathpzc{C}
is reflective, if the functor of embedding
\mathpzc{D}\hookrightarrow\mathpzc{C}
has a left adjoint functor
L:\mathpzc{C}\to\mathpzc{D}
called reflector. We say that a reflective subcategory
\mathpzc{D}
is proper, if it is isomorphism-closed and not equal to
\mathpzc{C}.

Example 3.6

(Varieties of groups)
.

A variety of groups is a class of all groups whose elements satisfy a fixed system of identity relations. It is also well known that a variety of groups can be defined as a full subcategory of the category of groups that is closed under small products, taking images and taking subgroups. A variety is called proper if it is not equal to the category of all groups. Examples of proper varieties of groups are abelian groups, nilpotent groups of class
n
, metabelian groups,
n
-Burnside groups (i.e. groups
G
satisfying
g^{n}=1
for all elements
g\in G
). A proper variety of groups is a proper reflective subcategory in the category of groups.

Example 3.7

(
f
-local groups)
.

Let
f:A\to B
be a homomorphism of groups. We say that a group
G
is
f
-local, if
f
induces a bijection
f^{*}:{\sf Hom}(B,G)\overset{\cong}{\to}{\sf Hom}(A,G)
(see
[
5
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
1
]
). A homomorphism
f:A\to B
is called local, if
B
is
f
-local. For any local homomorphism
f,
the full subcategory of
f
-local groups
\mathcal{R}_{f}
is reflective
[
4
, Cor.1.7]
. Moreover, the reflector
L_{f}
satisfies the property
L_{f}A\cong B.
In particular, if
f
is not an isomorphism,
\mathcal{R}_{f}
is a proper reflective subcategory.

Example 3.8

(Uniquely divisible groups)
.

A group
G
is called uniquely divisible, if for any
g\in G
and any integer
n
there exists a unique
h\in G
such that
h^{n}=g.
Equivalently, uniquely divisible groups can be defined as groups local with respect to the homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}.
In particular, we obtain that the class of uniquely divisible groups forms a proper reflective subcategory.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a proper reflective subcategory of the category of groups with a reflector
L.
Then the kernel of the unit

(3.2)

KG={\sf Ker}(\eta_{G}:G\to LG)

is a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor.

Proof.

Let us assume that
KG
is trivial and prove that
\eta_{G}
is an isomorphism. Since epimorphisms in the category of groups are surjective homomorphisms, it is sufficient to prove that
\eta_{G}
is an epimorphism. Take two homomorphisms
i,i^{\prime}:LG\to A
such that
i\eta_{G}=i^{\prime}\eta_{G}.
By the universal property of
LG,
we obtain that the equations
\eta_{A}i\eta_{G}=\eta_{A}i^{\prime}\eta_{G}
imply
\eta_{A}i=\eta_{A}i^{\prime}.
Then using that
\eta_{A}
is a monomorphism, we obtain
i=i^{\prime}.

∎

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a proper reflective subcategory of the category of groups. Then there is no a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor on the category of groups having values in
\mathcal{R}.

Proof.

Assume that
F
is a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor and prove that its values do not lie in
\mathcal{R}.
Let
L
be the reflector for
\mathcal{R}
and
KG
be the kernel of
\eta_{G}:G\to LG.
By Lemma
3.9
,
K
is also a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor. Since
F
and
K
are non-trivial, there exist groups
G
and
G^{\prime}
such that
FG
and
KG^{\prime}
are nontrivial.
Take a simple group
S
containing the product
G\times G^{\prime}.
The inclusion
G\subseteq S
induces an inclusion
FG\subseteq FS.
Therefore
FS
is non-trivial, and hence,
FS=S.
Similarly we obtain
KS=S.
The equation
KS=S
implies that
S\notin\mathcal{R}.
Therefore
FS\notin\mathcal{R},
which finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a proper variety of groups (for example, the variety of abelian groups) and
F:{\sf Gr}\to{\sf Gr}
be an augmented functor

(3.3)

\varepsilon:F\longrightarrow\mathrm{Id}_{\sf Gr}

taking values in
\mathcal{V}.
Then
\varepsilon
is trivial.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{V}
is a variety, the image
\mathrm{Im}(\varepsilon)\subseteq\mathrm{Id}_{\sf Gr}
also takes values in
\mathcal{V}.
Then the result follows from Theorem
3.10
and the fact that
\mathcal{V}
is a proper reflective subcategory.
∎

Corollary 3.12
.

There is no a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor on the category of groups having values in uniquely divisible groups.

3.2.
Quotients of the identity functor of the category of groups

Let us now briefly consider conditions that any quotient of the identity functor on groups
G\twoheadrightarrow Q(G)
must satisfy.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
Q
be a non-trivial quotient of the identity functor on the category of groups. Then
\mathcal{IM}(Q)
contains either a non-trivial finite cyclic group, or all free abelian groups.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{IM}(Q)
does not contain a non-trivial finite cyclic group and show that all free abelian groups are in
\mathcal{IM}(Q).
Set
F(G)=\mathrm{Ker}(G\to Q(G)).
If
F(\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z},
then by Corollary
3.3
,
Q
is trivial.
Assume that
F(\mathbb{Z})\neq\mathbb{Z}.
Using that non-trivial finite cyclic groups are not in
\mathcal{IM}(Q),
we obtain
F(\mathbb{Z})=0.
Since
F(\mathbb{Z})=0,
Corollary
3.2
(2) implies that
F(\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus X})=0
for any set
X,
and
Q(\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus X})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus X}.

∎

Corollary 3.14
.

There is no a non-trivial quotient of the identity functor on the category of groups taking values in the subcategory of perfect groups (or free groups, or non-abelian groups).

Example 3.15

(Quotient of the identity functor having values in a coreflective subcategory)
.

Denote by
\mathcal{T}
the full subcategory of the category of groups generated by elements of finite order. This subcategory is coreflective. Indeed for any group
G,
we can consider the subgroup
tG\subseteq G
generated by elements of finite order, and the inclusion
tG\hookrightarrow G
is the universal arrow from a group generated by torsion elements. On the other hand, we can consider the quotient
QG
of a group
G
by the normal subgroup generated by squares of all elements. Therefore
QG
is a non-trivial quotient of the identity functor having values in the coreflective subcategory
\mathcal{T}.

Example 3.16

(Natural embedding into a perfect group)
.

Although, there is no non-trivial quotient of the identity functor taking values in the category of perfect groups, there is a natural embedding

(3.4)

G\hookrightarrow C(G),

where
C(G)
is an acyclic (in particular, perfect) group. There are two different constructions of such a functor
C(G):
one of them uses the group of bijections
\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Q}
with “compact support”
[
8
, §3.1]
; and another one uses the universal binate tower of groups
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
.

3.3.
The category of abelian groups

In this subsection we will study subfunctors and quotients of the identity functor on the category of abelian groups. We will denote by
\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty})
the
p
-quasicyclic group, which can be defined as
\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty})=\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z},
where
\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
is the subgroup of
\mathbb{Q}
generated by elements
1/p^{n}.

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
F
be a non-vanishing, proper subfunctor of the identity functor on the category of abelian groups. Then
\mathcal{IM}(F)
contains either a non-trivial finite cyclic group or the
p
-quasicyclic group for some prime
p.

Proof.

Assume the contrary, that the
\mathcal{IM}(F)
contains no non-trivial finite cyclic groups or a
p
-quasicyclic group.
Any abelian group
G
can be embedded into a divisible abelian group
G\subseteq D.
Any divisible abelian group
D
is a direct sum of groups isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty}).
Since a direct sum is embedded into the product, we obtain that any abelian group
G
can be embedded into
a product of copies of groups
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty}).
In particular
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}\cong\bigoplus_{p}\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty})\subseteq\prod_{p}\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty}).
The group
\mathbb{Q}
can be embedded into the product
(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{\mathbb{N}}
by sending
q
to
(q/1,q/2,q/3,\dots).
Therefore, any abelian group
G
can be embedded into a product of the
p
-quasicyclic groups

(3.5)

G\subseteq\prod_{\alpha\in A}\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty}_{\alpha}).

Since any proper subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty})
is a finite cyclic group, the assumption implies
F(\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty}))=0.
Similarly by Lemma
3.1
, for any family of abelian groups
(G_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in A}
we have

(3.6)

F(\prod_{\alpha\in A}G_{\alpha})\subseteq\prod_{\alpha\in A}F(G_{\alpha}).

Since
F
being, a sub-functor of the identity, it preserve injectivity of maps, and hence it follows that

(3.7)

F(G)\subseteq F(\prod_{\alpha\in A}\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty}_{\alpha}))\subseteq\prod_{\alpha\in A}F(\mathbb{Z}(p^{\infty}_{\alpha}))=0.

This contradicts the non-vanishing assumption on
F.

∎

Corollary 3.18
.

There is no a non-trivial subfunctor of the identity functor on the category of abelian groups taking values in the subcategory of
torsion-free abelian groups.

Theorem 3.19
.

Let
Q
be a non-trivial quotient of the identity functor on the category of abelian groups. Then
\mathcal{IM}(Q)
either contains a non-trivial finite cyclic group, or all free abelian groups.

Proof.

For a non-trivial quotient of the identity functor
Q(G)=G/F(G)
in the category of abelian groups
Q:{\sf Ab}\to{\sf Ab}
, we can construct a non-trivial quotient of the identity functor on the category of groups
\widetilde{Q}:{\sf Gr}\to{\sf Gr}
by the formula
\widetilde{Q}(G)=Q(G/[G,G]).
Then the assertion follows from Theorem
3.13
.
∎

Corollary 3.20
.

There is no a non-trivial quotient of the identity functor on the category of abelian groups taking values in the subcategory of divisible groups.

Example 3.21
