Flows on Metric Graphs with General Boundary Conditions
Klaus-Jochen Engel, Marjeta Kramar Fijavž

Abstract
. In this note we study the generation of C 0 C_{0} -semigroups by first order differential operators on L p ​ ( ℝ + , ℂ ℓ ) × L p ​ ( [ 0 , 1 ] , ℂ m ) \mathchoice{\mathrm{L}^{p}\bigl({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\bigr)}{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell})}\times\mathchoice{\mathrm{L}^{p}\bigl([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m}\bigr)}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})} with general boundary conditions. In many cases we are able to characterize the generation property in terms of the invertibility of a matrix associated to the boundary conditions. The abstract results are used to study well-posedness of transport equations on non-compact metric graphs.

1.
Introduction

This is a companion paper of
[
18
]
where we studied linear wave and diffusion equations on metric graphs subject to general boundary conditions.
The aim of the present work is to apply the same approach used there to examine first order equations modeling flows on metric graphs.
This leads to the abstract Cauchy problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=Gx(t),&t\geq 0,\\
x(0)=x_{0},\end{cases}

for a first-order differential operator
G\colon D(G)\subset X\to X
on the Banach space

X=\mathchoice{\mathrm{L}^{p}\bigl({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\bigr)}{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell})}\times\mathchoice{\mathrm{L}^{p}\bigl([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m}\bigr)}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}.

Here,
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and the interval
[0,1]
represent the unbounded, respectively normalized bounded edges of the graph while its structure is encoded in the boundary conditions appearing in the definition of the domain
D(G)
.

In the simplest case, all edges are bounded, hence we have
\ell=0
, i.e.,
X=\mathchoice{\mathrm{L}^{p}\bigl([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m}\bigr)}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}
and

(1.2)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=c({\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}})\cdot\tfrac{d}{ds},

\displaystyle D(G)

\displaystyle=\bigl\{f\in\mathchoice{\mathrm{W}^{1,p}\bigl([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m}\bigr)}{\mathrm{W}^{1,p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{W}^{1,p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{W}^{1,p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}:V_{0}f(0)=V_{1}f(1)\bigr\},

where
c(s)=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1}(s),\ldots,\lambda_{m}(s))

for non-vanishing, Lipschitz continuous functions
\lambda_{k}({\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}})
,
k=1,\ldots,m
, and suitable scalar “boundary” matrices
V_{0},V_{1}\in\mathrm{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C})
.

It is well known, for the details we refer to
[
19
, Sect. II.6]
, that the problem (
1.1
) is well-posed if and only if
G
generates a strongly continuous semigroup on
X
.

Our results now give sufficient conditions, which in many important cases are also necessary, in terms of the invertibility of a linear map, for a first-order differential operator
G
with general boundary conditions to be a generator, hence for the well-posedness of the associated problem (
1.1
). For example, by
Subsection 2.4

the operator
G
in (
1.2
) is a generator if and only if

(1.3)

\det\bigl(V_{1}P_{+}+V_{0}P_{-})\neq 0,

where
P_{\pm}
denote the spectral projections associated to the positive/negative eigenvalues of
c({\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}})
. In particular, condition (
1.3
) characterizes the well-posedness of the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) for
G
given by (
1.2
).

The semigroup approach to linear transport equations (or flows) on networks was initiated in
[
28
]
and further pursued by many authors, see e.g.
[
30
,
16
,
9
,
31
,
6
,
8
,
5
,
7
]
.
In all these works finite compact graphs (i.e., with finitely many edges that are all compact intervals) are considered,
c({\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}})
are constant and diagonal matrices, and the boundary conditions rely on the structure of the graph, representing conservation of the mass, so-called Kirchhoff conditions. In
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
the same approach was applied to study flows on infinite but compact graphs.

The setting has been generalized in
[
27
,
17
]
to so-called difference operators, that are operators of the form (
1.2
) where
c({\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}})
was considered to be a constant, not necessarily diagonal, self-adjoint matrix.
We note that our problem can also be formulated as a first order boundary system or a so-called port-Hamiltonian system. In this framework
generation results are shown in Hilbert space setting by a suitable basis transformation and then carried over to
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-spaces, see for example
[
32
,
24
]
.
These methods were also applied in
[
23
,
25
]
to first order equations on some classes of non-compact graphs.

In comparison to the above cited works our approach presents several novelties: it allows to

•

consider operators on
\mathchoice{\mathrm{L}^{p}\bigl({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\bigr)}{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C}^{\ell})}\times\mathchoice{\mathrm{L}^{p}\bigl([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m}\bigr)}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}{\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,1],\mathbb{C}^{m})}
with applications to
non
-compact graphs,

•

treat very general (also non-diagonal) “velocity matrices”
c({\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}},{\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}})
, cf. (
2.1
),

•

treat very general (also non-local) boundary conditions, cf. (
2.3
),

•

treat all cases of
p\in[1,+\infty)
simultaneously without using interpolation arguments,

•

obtain necessary conditions for the generator property, hence the well-posedness, for important classes of boundary conditions.

Our reasoning is based on similarity transformations and a perturbation result for boundary perturbations of domains of generators from
[
1
, Sect. 4.3]
.

This paper is organized as follows. In
Section 2
, after introducing our general setup in
Subsection 2.1
, we state and prove in
Subsection 2.2
the main generation result,
Theorem 2.4
.
In
Subsection 2.3
we supplement this result in
Theorem 2.12
by a necessary condition for
G
to be a generator.
If the boundary operators defining the domain
D(G)
are, modulo bounded perturbations, given by point evaluations, this gives simple generation conditions which are presented in
Subsection 2.4
. In the compact case
\ell=0
this reduces in
Subsection 2.4
to a very simple necessary and sufficient determinant condition like (
1.3
).
In
Section 3
we then show how our results apply to transport processes on metric graphs. In particular, we reproduce and generalize many existing results which demonstrates the versatility of our approach.
Finally, in the appendix we recall our approach to boundary perturbations from
[
1
,
18
]
and present in
Theorem A.6
a new necessary condition for a perturbed generator to be again a generator.

3.
Application to Flows in Networks

In this section we use our abstract results to show the well-posedness of transport equations on networks. We will consider initial-boundary value problems as in (
1.1
) where the structure of the network will be encoded in the domain
D(G)
.

Consider a finite metric graph (network) with
n
vertices
\mathsf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{v}_{n}
,
m

internal
edges
\mathsf{e}^{i}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{e}^{i}_{m}
, which we parametrize on the unit interval
[0,1]
, and
\ell

external
edges
\mathsf{e}^{e}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{e}^{e}_{\ell}
, parametrized on the half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. A graph without external edges is called
compact
.
For simplicity, the graph is assumed to be connected and without vertices of degree 1.

Along the edges of this metric graph some material is transported. This flow process is described by a system of linear transport equations

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\,u^{e}_{k}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{e}_{k}(s)\cdot\frac{d}{ds}\,u^{e}_{k}(t,s),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\ s>0,

\displaystyle k=1,\dots,\ell,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\,u^{i}_{j}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{i}_{j}(s)\cdot\frac{d}{ds}\,u^{i}_{j}(t,s),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\ s\in(0,1),

\displaystyle j=1,\dots,m,

for some uniformly bounded functions
\lambda^{e}_{k}({\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}})\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}_{+}},\mathbb{C})
,
\lambda^{i}_{j}({\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}})\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}([0,1],\mathbb{C}
.
Here,
u^{e}_{k}
,
u^{i}_{j}
represent the flow density and
\lambda^{e}_{k},\lambda^{i}_{j}
the flow velocity on the edge
\mathsf{e}^{e}_{k},\mathsf{e}^{i}_{j}
, respectively.
In the vertices of the graph the material gets redistributed according to some prescribed rules which translate to boundary conditions of our problem. We shall assume that these conditions respect the network structure, that is only the values at common endpoints can interfere.

While the underlying combinatorial graph is a priori not directed, the parametrization orients the edges of the corresponding metric graph.
The direction of the flow on a given edge is determined by the sign of the appropriate velocity coefficient: a negative coefficient yields a flow from the endpoint
0
to the endpoint
1
on the corresponding edge and the opposite holds in the case of a positive coefficient. For a directed graph we always assume that the direction of the edges corresponds to the direction of the flow.
Let us start with some simple examples.

3.1.
Compact Graphs

Starting with
[
28
]
, there is a series of articles dealing with transport processes of type (
3.1
) on compact graphs, cf.
[
30
,
6
,
5
]
.
In all these works
the semigroup approach is used, which is summarized in
[
15
]
and
[
8
, Ch. 18]
. Note, that in most of these papers the velocities are taken positive and the flow process is directed on the edges from the endpoint 1 to the endpoint 0. Moreover, the “boundary space”
{\partial X}
in these works relates to the vertices of the graph and equals
\mathbb{C}^{n}
while we take
{\partial X}=\mathbb{C}^{m}
that complies with the number of the boundary conditions needed (see the discussion preceding (
2.5
)).

The most natural boundary conditions in this context are
Kirchhoff conditions
expressing the conservation of mass, that is, the sum of the incoming flow in every vertex equals the sum of the outgoing flow from that vertex.
These conditions are complemented by certain transmission rules that prescribe the proportions of the mass flowing into each of the outgoing edges. As observed in
[
6
, Thm. 2.1]
, one needs to restrict to the case without sinks in the graph; that is, we have to assume that from every vertex the material is flowing out on at least one edge. In this case, the boundary conditions appearing in the description of the domain of the generator can be rewritten in the form

(3.2)

f(0)=\mathbb{B}f(1),

where
\mathbb{B}
is the so-called (weighted, transposed)
adjacency matrix of the line graph
, i.e. an
m\times m
matrix incorporating the structure of the graph, see
[
8
, Prop. 18.2]
for more details. By
Subsection 2.4
, the network transport problem (
3.1
) with boundary conditions of the form (
3.2
) is well-posed with no extra assumptions on the matrix
\mathbb{B}
.

Our results cover all situations where the boundary conditions can be written as in (
3.2
) which, apart from simple transport equation with constant coefficients like in
[
28
,
6
,
5
,
8
]
, include also non-constant coefficients cases as in
[
30
,
4
]
or transport equations with scattering, see
[
31
,
15
]
. Yet another example of this kind are transmission line networks with non-conservative junction conditions as studied in
[
12
]
. Since every edge has exactly one starting and one ending vertex, the local boundary conditions

[
12
, (3.3)]
can be equivalently written as (
3.2
) where the entries of
\mathbb{B}
correspond to appropriate entries of locally given scattering matrices. Again, no additional assumption on
\mathbb{B}
is needed in order to obtain well-posedness. In
[
12
]
only balanced graphs are considered (i.e., graphs with the same number of incoming and outgoing edges in each vertex) and invertibility of the scattering matrices is assumed, however, the focus there is not on well-posedness but on spectral properties of the generator.

We point out that
Theorem 2.4
and its corollaries for the compact case, i.e.,

Subsection 2.2
and
Subsection 2.4
, enable to tackle problems with much more general boundary conditions as studied in the references mentioned above. Moreover, one can apply the spectral theory developed in
[
3
]
to characterize the spectrum of the generator in terms of the graph matrix
\mathbb{B}
, see, e.g.
Appendix A
and
[
8
, Cor. 18.13]
.

3.2.
Non-compact Graphs

To our knowledge, first-order equations on non-compact graphs have not attained much attention in the literature.
In
[
20
]
the action of momentum operator
-\imath\hbar\tfrac{d}{ds}
on non-compact metric (or quantum) graphs has been considered in the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-setting, the conditions yielding a self-adjoint generator were presented, and its spectral properties studied. Further,
[
25
]
is devoted to port-Hamiltonian systems corresponding to a class of hyperbolic partial differential equations of first order on the semi-axis and the main result is the characterization of the boundary conditions yielding
C_{0}
-semigroup generation. On the other hand, there has been some more work done in the case of second-order equations, see
[
18
]
and the references therein.

Let us first show how to write the boundary conditions also in the non-compact case in the form resembling (
3.2
).
Starting with the problem on a compact graph as in
Subsection 2.4
and assuming that all the velocities are negative, the generation condition becomes the invertibility of
V_{0}
. For invertible
V_{0}
, we can write the boundary conditions as

V_{0}f(0)=V_{1}f(1)\iff f(0)=V_{0}^{-1}V_{1}f(1),

hence, by taking
\mathbb{B}:=V_{0}^{-1}V_{1}
, we obtain (
3.2
). We now proceed similarly in the non-compact case, with the setting as in
Subsection 2.4
and taking all the internal velocities to be negative. We can write the boundary conditions in
D(G)
as

\bigl(V_{0}^{e},V_{0}^{i}\bigr){f^{e}(0)\choose f^{i}(0)}=V_{1}^{i}f^{i}(1)\iff{f^{e}(0)\choose f^{i}(0)}=\bigl(V_{0}^{e},V_{0}^{i}\bigr)^{+}V_{1}^{i}f^{i}(1)

where
A^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of matrix
A
. Note, that the generation condition in this case yields that the matrix

\bigl(V_{0}^{e},V_{0}^{i}\bigr)
has full rank (
=q
) and the pseudoinverse is the right inverse of this matrix. Taking
\mathbb{B}:=\bigl(V_{0}^{e},V_{0}^{i}\bigr)^{+}V_{1}^{i}
we obtain the desired form of the conditions. Thus, by
Subsection 2.4
we can cover a wide variety of boundary conditions.

-Semigroups

In this appendix we first briefly recall a perturbation result from
[
18
, App. A]
which easily follows
from
[

and is our main tool to prove

.
Further, in

.

To explain the general setup we consider

•

two Banach spaces
X
and
\partial X
, called “state” and “boundary” spaces, respectively;

•

a closed, densely defined ‘‘maximal’’ operator
1
1

1

A_{m}:D(A_{m})\subseteq X\to X
;

•

the Banach space
[D(A_{m})]:=(D(A_{m}),\|{\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}\|_{A_{m}})
where
\|f\|_{A_{m}}:=\|f\|+\|A_{m}f\|
is the graph norm;

•

two “boundary” operators
L,\Phi\in\mathcal{L}([D(A_{m})],\partial X)
.

Then, define two restrictions
A,\,G\subset A_{m}
by

\displaystyle D(A):

\displaystyle=\bigl\{f\in D(A_{m}):Lf=0\bigr\}=\ker(L),

\displaystyle D(G):

\displaystyle=\bigl\{f\in D(A_{m}):\Phi f=0\bigr\}=\ker(\Phi).

Hence, one can consider
G
with boundary condition
\Phi f=0
as a perturbation of the operator
A
with abstract “Dirichlet type” boundary condition
Lf=0
.
In order to proceed we make throughout this appendix the following

Assumption A.1
.

\hyper@anchor

(i)

The operator

-semigroup

on

By combining

Theorem A.2

Assume that there exist

,

,

, and a strongly continuous family
(\mathcal{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,t_{0}],{\partial X}),X)
such that the following assertions hold.

(i)

For every
v\in\mathrm{W}^{2,p}_{0}([0,t_{0}],{\partial X})
the function

x\colon[0,t_{0}]\to X,\quad x(t):=\mathcal{B}_{t}v

is a classical solution of the boundary control problem

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=A_{m}x(t),&0\leq t\leq t_{0},\\

For all

\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\bigl\|\Phi\,T(s)x\bigr\|_{\partial X}^{p}\,ds\leq M\cdot\|x\|_{X}^{p}.

(iii)

For all

,

\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\bigl\|\Phi\mathcal{B}_{t}v\bigr\|_{\partial X}^{p}\,dt\leq M\cdot\|v\|_{p}^{p}.

(iv)

\mathcal{Q}_{t_{0}}
is invertible, where

is given by

(\mathcal{Q}_{t_{0}}v)({\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}})=\Phi{\mathcal{B}}_{{\raisebox{1.3pt}{{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}v\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathrm{W}_{0}^{2,p}([0,t_{0}],{\partial X}).

Then

While the previous result provides a sufficient condition for
G\subset A_{m}
with domain

to be the generator of a

-semigroup our next aim is to give a necessary one. To this end we first need the following result from in

.

Lemma A.4
.

For each
\lambda\in\rho(A)
the restricted operator
L|_{\ker(\lambda-A_{m})}
is invertible and

L_{\lambda}:=(L|_{\ker(\lambda-A_{m})})^{-1}:\partial X\to\ker(\lambda-A_{m})\subseteq X

is bounded.

Using these so-called abstract Dirichlet operators
L_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}({\partial X},X)
one can characterize the spectrum of
G
, cf.
[
3
, Cor. 3.13]
. Note that
L_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}({\partial X},[D(A_{m})])
, hence
\Phi L_{\lambda}:{\partial X}\to{\partial X}
is bounded for every
\lambda\in\rho(A)
.

Lemma A.5
.

For every
\lambda\in\rho(A)
one has

\lambda\in\rho(G)\quad\iff\quad\Phi L_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}({\partial X})\text{ is invertible}

Since for
\lambda\in\rho(A)
the norms
\|\cdot\|_{[D(A_{m})]}
and
\|\cdot\|_{X}
restricted to
\operatorname{rg}(L_{\lambda})=\ker(\lambda-A_{m})
are equivalent, the restriction
L:\bigl(\ker(\lambda-A_{m}),\|\cdot\|_{X}\bigr)\to{\partial X}
is bounded. Hence,

(A.1)

\|L\|_{\lambda}:=\|L\|_{\mathcal{L}((\ker(\lambda-A_{m}),\|\cdot\|_{X}),{\partial X})}<+\infty

is well-defined.
Now we are well-prepared to give a necessary condition for
G
to be a generator on
X
.

Theorem A.6
.

Suppose that
G\subset A_{m}
with domain
D(G)=\ker(\Phi)
is a generator of a
C_{0}
-semigroup where

\Phi=\Phi_{0}+V\cdot L
for
\Phi_{0}\in\mathcal{L}([D(A_{m})],\partial X)
and a non-invertible operator
V\in\mathcal{L}({\partial X})
. If
\dim({\partial X})<\infty
then

(A.2)

\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}\|L\|_{\lambda}\cdot\|\Phi_{0}L_{\lambda}\|=+\infty.

Proof.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in\rho(A)\cap\rho(G)
. Then, by
Appendix A
, the Dirichlet operators
L_{\lambda}
,
L_{\mu}
exist and by
Appendix A
the operator
\Phi L_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}({\partial X})
is invertible. A simple computation then shows that

R(\lambda,G)L_{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{\lambda-\mu}\bigl(L_{\mu}-L_{\lambda}(\Phi L_{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi L_{\mu}\bigr).

Using that
\lambda R(\lambda,G)f\to f
as
\lambda\to+\infty
for every
f\in X
we conclude that for all
x\in{\partial X}

\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}L_{\lambda}(\Phi L_{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi L_{\mu}x=0.

Since
\Phi L_{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}({\partial X})
is invertible and the unit ball in
{\partial X}
is compact this implies

(A.3)

\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}\bigl\|L_{\lambda}(\Phi L_{\lambda})^{-1}\bigr\|=0.

Next, using that
LL_{\lambda}=Id_{{\partial X}}
from the decomposition
\Phi=\Phi_{0}+V\cdot L
we obtain

V=\Phi L_{\lambda}-\Phi_{0}L_{\lambda}=\Phi L_{\lambda}\cdot\bigl(Id-(\Phi L_{\lambda})^{-1}\cdot\Phi_{0}L_{\lambda}\bigr)\in\mathcal{L}({\partial X})

which by assumption is not invertible. Therefore,

(A.4)

\|\Phi_{0}L_{\lambda}\|\geq\bigl\|(\Phi L_{\lambda})^{-1}\bigr\|^{-1}.

Combining this with (
A.1
) gives

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{0}L_{\lambda}\|^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|(\Phi L_{\lambda})^{-1}\bigr\|

\displaystyle=\bigl\|LL_{\lambda}(\Phi L_{\lambda})^{-1}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{L}(X)}

\displaystyle\leq\|L\|_{\lambda}\cdot\bigl\|L_{\lambda}(\Phi L_{\lambda})^{-1}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{L}({\partial X},X)}.

By (
A.3
) we infer

\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}\|L\|_{\lambda}^{-1}\cdot\|\Phi_{0}L_{\lambda}\|^{-1}=0

which implies (
A.2
).
∎

Note that in the previous proof we didn’t make use of the fact that
