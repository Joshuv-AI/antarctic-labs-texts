HyDRA : Hypergradient Data Relevance Analysis for Interpreting Deep Neural Networks
Yuanyuan Chen, Boyang Li, Han Yu, Pengcheng Wu, Chunyan Miao

Abstract
The behaviors of deep neural networks (DNNs) are notoriously resistant to human interpretations. In this paper, we propose Hypergradient Data Relevance Analysis, or HyDRA , which interprets the predictions made by DNNs as effects of their training data. Existing approaches generally estimate data contributions around the final model parameters and ignore how the training data shape the optimization trajectory. By unrolling the hypergradient of test loss w.r.t. the weights of training data, HyDRA assesses the contribution of training data toward test data points throughout the training trajectory. In order to accelerate computation, we remove the Hessian from the calculation and prove that, under moderate conditions, the approximation error is bounded. Corroborating this theoretical claim, empirical results indicate the error is indeed small. In addition, we quantitatively demonstrate that HyDRA outperforms influence functions in accurately estimating data contribution and detecting noisy data labels. The source code is available at https://github.com/cyyever/aaai_hydra.



Introduction

What makes neural networks do exactly what they do?
This is a crucial question that lingers in the minds of machine learning researchers and practitioners. The underpinnings of deep neural networks (DNNs), including non-convex objective functions, stochastic optimization, and over parameterization, may implicitly regularize the network and improve generalization (e.g.,
LiandLiang 2018
;
Arora,Cohen,andHazan 2018
), but also hinder the interpretation of the network behaviors and the training process.
The black-box nature of DNNs can render their predictions untrustworthy in the eyes of the general public and prohibit wide adoption. Conversely, good interpretability can enhance the training, debugging, and auditing of DNNs.

The focus of the present paper is to understand DNNs by attributing their predictions to the training data. That is, how training data influence network predictions on test data. However, as the training data are involved in the entirety of the complex training process, accurately capturing their influence on the final network is a challenge.

The pioneering work of
(
KohandLiang 2017
)
proposes influence functions (IF), which measure the contribution of a training sample
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
to a test sample
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test}}
as the change in the loss of
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test}}
when the weight of training data
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
is changed marginally.

This technique enables a number of applications (e.g.,
AlaaandVanDer Schaar 2020
), but suffers from two drawbacks. First, it only measures the contribution around the final model parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}
and ignores the possibility that a change in the weights of training data may lead to a local optimum different from
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}
with a drastically different test loss.
Second, IF relies on the inverse Hessian on the whole training data, which is computationally expensive to approximate.
(
Hara,Nitanda,andMaehara 2019
)
analyzed the entire trajectory but still rely on Hessians.

In this paper, we propose Hypergradient for Data Relevance Analysis (
HyDRA
) to address the aforementioned shortcomings. By unrolling the test loss gradient with respect to data weights through all training steps,
HyDRA
accounts for how changes in data weights shape the whole training process. As optimization utilizes the gradient on the same data repeatedly, even marginal changes in the data weights can accumulate and eventually shift the point of convergence. Figure
1
illustrates such a scenario where the local analysis of IF and the whole-of-trajectory analysis of
HyDRA
find separate local minima and contribution values. Further, to simplify computation, we propose an approximation method that eliminates the cumbersome Hessian or its inverse and establishes an analytical upper bound on the approximation error.

Empirically, we verified that the approximation indeed results in small error and is closer to the whole-of-trajectory Hessian-aware measurements than IF.
The approximation method of
HyDRA
achieved high correlation with the Hessian-aware measurements, whereas the local analysis of IF added
11
% to
16
% to the approximation error. In the particular experiment we conducted, the removal of Hessian reduces wall-clock running time by a factor of
971
on
2
TitanX Pascal GPUs.
We also compared
HyDRA
and IF in their ability to identify data points with erroneous labels and found that
HyDRA
had provided superior detection performance.

HyDRA
: Hypergradient for Data Relevance Analysis

We first introduce some preliminaries. We aim to learn a function
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
. The training dataset contains
N
data points and is denoted as
D_{\text{train}}=\{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}{}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. The function
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is learned by minimizing both the empirical risk
\mathcal{R}
and the regularization term
f_{\text{reg}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta})&=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\in D_{\text{train}}}\left(1/N+\epsilon_{i}\right)\,\ell(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta}),\\
\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})&=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\lambda f_{\text{reg}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\end{split}

(1)

where
\ell
is the per-sample loss such as cross entropy, and
\lambda
is a regularization coefficient. In this paper, we adopt
L^{2}
regularization
f_{\text{reg}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
The sample loss weight
\epsilon_{i}
is set to zero during training; its function will be explained shortly.
See table
1
for the notations used in this paper.

Similar to the training dataset, the test dataset with
M
data points is denoted as
D_{\text{test}}=\{\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test},i}\}{}_{i=1}^{M}
with
D_{\text{train}}\cap D_{\text{test}}=\varnothing
.
The test loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}
is a measurement of model generalizability:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test}}\in D_{\text{test}}}\ell(\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}).

We usually optimize the loss function using vanilla Gradient Descent (GD) or GD with momentum.
Vanilla GD iteratively updates the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
as

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}-\eta\boldsymbol{g}_{t-1},

where
\eta
is the learning rate at step t. In GD with momentum, we first update the momentum
\boldsymbol{v}_{t}
, followed by updating
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
.

\boldsymbol{v}_{t}=p\boldsymbol{v}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{g}_{t-1},

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}-\eta\boldsymbol{v}_{t},

where
p
is a constant in interval
(0,1)
that determines the strength of the accumulated momentum. We repeat the optimization for
T
steps and arrive at the final network parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}
. The stochastic versions of the two algorithms simply replace
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
with the gradients on mini-batches of training data.

Recall that every training sample
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
is associated with the data weight
1/N+\epsilon_{i}
in the training loss (equation
1
). Thus, we can remove the
i{\text{th}}
sample from the training by setting
\epsilon_{i}
to
-1/N
. The resulting change in the test loss can be estimated with a first-order Taylor expansion. We define the contribution of the
i{\text{th}}{}
training data point on model performance,
\mathcal{C}(i)
, as the estimated change (which is negligible with a sufficiently large training dataset), as

\mathcal{C}(i)\coloneq-\frac{1}{N}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}\Bigr|_{\epsilon_{i}=0}.

(2)

If removal of the data sample
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
from the training data increases test loss, the sample would have a positive contribution. Otherwise, the sample hurts generalization performance and makes a negative contribution.

We can measure the contribution to any portion of the test set simply by replacing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}
in equation (
2
) with the loss on the data points in question. For example, the contribution by the
i{\text{th}}
training sample on model performance regarding the
j{\text{th}}
test sample,
\mathcal{C}(i,j)
, is computed as

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}(i,j)&\coloneq-\frac{1}{N}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ell(\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test},j},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}\Bigr|_{\epsilon_{i}=0}.\end{split}

In equation (
2
),
\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}
does not directly depend on
\epsilon_{i}
and only through
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, we can write the total derivative as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}.

(3)

\left.{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}\middle/{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right.
can be exactly computed using backpropagation. However, computing
\left.{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}}\middle/{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}\right.
, or in other words
\nabla_{T,i}
, is not so straightforward as
\epsilon_{i}
is involved in the entire optimization process. In the following, we give the recurrence equations for
\nabla_{T,i}
in the whole-batch gradient descent.

In vanilla gradient descent,
\nabla_{t,i}
can be computed recurrently as a function of
\nabla_{t-1,i}
as follows:

\begin{split}\nabla_{t,i}&=\nabla_{t-1,i}-\eta\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{g}_{t-1}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}\\
&=\nabla_{t-1,i}-\eta\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{t-1}}\nabla_{t-1,i}\\
&\quad-\eta\lambda\nabla_{t-1,i}-\eta\boldsymbol{g}_{t-1,i},\end{split}

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{g}_{t,i}
denotes the gradient of
i{\text{th}}
sample.
Note that since
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
is a function of both
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
and
\epsilon_{i}
, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{g}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}=\boldsymbol{H}_{t}\nabla_{t,i}+\boldsymbol{g}_{t-1,i}
, which introduces the Hessian.

Similarly, for gradient descent with momentum,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}

\displaystyle=p\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}_{t-1}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}+\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{t-1}}\nabla_{t-1,i}

(5)

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\nabla_{t-1,i}+\boldsymbol{g}_{t-1,i},

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla_{t,i}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{t-1,i}-\eta\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}.

(7)

In both cases we have the initial conditions

\displaystyle\nabla_{0,i}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\text{\, and \,}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}=\boldsymbol{0}.

By recurrently computing
\nabla_{t,i}
through the entire optimization trajectory,
HyDRA
holistically measures the contribution of
i{\text{th}}{}
data point to the neural network.

Fast Approximation

We propose a fast approximation technique, which sets the Hessian
\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{t-1}}
in equation (
4
) and equation (
5
) to zero with an analytical bound on the approximation error. In the data contribution equation (
3
), the term
\left.{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}\middle/{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right.
can always be computed exactly, so this approximation only affects the
\nabla_{T,i}
term. Algorithm
1
shows the mini-batch version of the approximate algorithm for computing
\nabla_{T,i}
.

The Hessian-vector product can be approximated in
O({\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert}_{2})
time using a method akin to finite difference
(
Pearlmutter 1994
)
. Nevertheless, the computation is slow as it requires (usually two) additional backpropagations and is susceptible to truncation and discretization errors just like finite difference. Consequently, removing the Hessian provides desirable simplification to the algorithm.

With the moderate conditions below, we can show that applying the proposed approximation results in bounded difference between the true
\nabla_{t,i}
and its approximation
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t,i}
. Additionally, if we let the learning rate decay exponentially, the difference vanishes after sufficient training iterations.

1
.

The training loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}{}
is twice differentiable.

2
.

The optimization process converges, that is,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}.

3
.

The empirical risk function
\mathcal{R}
has Lipschitz-continuous gradients with Lipschitz constant
L
. Formally, there exists a constant
L
such that

{\Big\lVert\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\Big\rVert}_{2}\leq L{\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\rVert}_{2},\forall\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\in\mathcal{H}.

4
.

The sequence of learning rates
\eta
is non-increasing and lower-bounded by 0. That is,

\eta_{t}\geq\eta_{t+1}>0,\forall t.

5
.

The sequence of products of learning rate
\eta
and regularization coefficient
\lambda
satisfies

0<\eta_{t}\lambda<1,\forall{t}
.

6
.

The sequence
\nabla_{t,i}
is ultimately bounded by a constant
P
.

1
.

Under the above conditions and vanilla GD, the norm of the approximation error is bounded by

{\lVert\nabla_{t,i}-\widetilde{\nabla}_{t,i}\rVert}_{2}<LP\frac{\eta_{1}}{\eta_{t}\lambda}.

2
.

Under the above conditions, vanilla GD, and an exponential decay schedule for the learning rate
\eta
, the approximation error diminishes when
t
tends to infinity

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\lVert\nabla_{t,i}-\widetilde{\nabla}_{t,i}\rVert}_{2}=0.

We can also relax the Lipschitz-continuity
3
and derive similar results. The proof details are in the supplemental material. In the experiments, we empirically verified these theoretical results and found that the approximation indeed leads to small errors.

Time and Space Complexity

It takes
O({\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert}_{2})
time and space to compute the parameter gradients. Using the approach in
(
Pearlmutter 1994
)
, Hessian-vector products can be computed in
O({\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert}_{2})
time and space.
We also need
O({\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert}_{2})
extra space to store previous
\nabla_{t-1,i}
,
\left.{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}_{t-1}}\middle/{\mathrm{d}\epsilon_{i}}\right.
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{t-1,i}
.
Therefore, we need a total of
O(T{\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert}_{2})
time and
O({\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert}_{2})
space to trace a single training sample.
Tracing all training samples simultaneously requires
O(T{\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert}_{2}\lvert D_{\text{train}}\rvert)
time and
O({\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert}_{2}\lvert D_{\text{train}}\rvert)
space.

Related Work

Influence function.

As a precursor to our work,
(
KohandLiang 2017
)
measured the influence of a training data point
\boldsymbol{z}
on a test data point
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test}}
via the obtained parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}
as
\operatorname{IF}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test}})

\operatorname{IF}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test}})\coloneq-\left(\frac{\partial\ell(\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{test}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{H}_{T}^{-1}\frac{\partial\ell(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

(8)

The difficulty of equation (
8
) lies in computing the inverse of Hessian
\boldsymbol{H}_{T}^{-1}
, for which the naive approach is infeasible due to the size of modern DNNs. Koh & Liang introduced two methods for this purpose.

The first method assumes that
\boldsymbol{H}
is positive definite,
\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}
can then be formulated as the optimal vector
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{*}
that minimizes the quadratic form
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\phi}+{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}
using conjugate gradient.

The second method to find
\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}
is to iterate the following until convergence,

\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{v}+\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\frac{\partial\ell(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v},

(9)

where the training data point
\boldsymbol{z}
is randomly sampled in each iteration and the initial
\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}
is set to
\boldsymbol{v}
.
In practice, we use the Monte Carlo expectation from multiple runs.
Both methods adopt the approximate Hessian-vector product
(
Pearlmutter 1994
)
.

Although these techniques render the time and space complexity manageable, dealing with the inverse Hessian is still tedious and slow. Numerical considerations such as damping and scaling factors require careful treatment. Errors can be introduced by the variance of the Monte Carlo expectation and the errors in the Hessian-vector product. In comparison,
HyDRA
offers a simplified method that removes the Hessian and a theoretical upper bound for the introduced error.

Hypergradient-based optimization.

Hypergradient is the gradient of the test loss with respect to the hyperparameters
(
Bengio 1999
;
Domke 2012
;
Franceschiet al. 2017
;
Franceschiet al. 2018
;
Lorraine,Vicol,andDuvenaud 2020
)
.
With hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, model parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and test loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}
, the hyperparameter optimization problem can be defined as the bi-level optimization

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{*}&=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{test}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})),\\
\text{s.t. }\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})&=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\theta}).\end{split}

Note that we write
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
to underscore the fact that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
influences the optimization of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
. Thus, in order to compute the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}))
w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, we must find the derivative
\left.{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})}\middle/{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\right.
, which may be computed by unrolling
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
throughout the training trajectory or for only a few steps as an approximation.

Hypergradient enables gradient-based optimization of hyperparameters, such as learning rates
(
Doniniet al. 2020
;
Metzet al. 2019
)
, network architectures
(
Liu,Simonyan,andYang 2019
)
, or data augmentation
(
Linet al. 2019
)
.

(
Maclaurin,Duvenaud,andAdams 2015
)
and
(
Wanget al. 2018
)
distilled a large training dataset into
10
-
100
data points.
(
Bohdal,Yang,andHospedales 2020
)
distilled data labels instead of input features.

(
MehraandHamm 2019
)
used penalty functions to avoid hypergradient in the bi-level optimization.

A key difference between
HyDRA
and other works on hypergradient-based optimization is that we use the hypergradient
\nabla_{t,i}
to assign credit to training data rather than to optimize
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. As a result,
HyDRA
is less vulnerable to the accumulated inaccuracies in the hypergradient estimates over time. We take advantage of this by omitting the Hessian with bounded error.

Inter-class contribution.

In the next experiment, we inspected the contribution from the training data of one class to the test data of another class. We partitioned the training dataset into
C
subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{C}
according to the annotated class, where
C
is the number of classes. Similarly, we partitioned the test set into
C
subsets
T_{1},\ldots,T_{C}
. The contribution
\mathcal{C}(S_{i},T_{j})
from a training class
S_{i}
to a test class
T_{j}
is the average contribution over all possible pairs of samples

\mathcal{C}(S_{i},T_{j})\coloneq\frac{1}{\lvert S_{i}\rvert\lvert T_{j}\rvert}\sum_{a\in S_{i}}\sum_{b\in T_{j}}\mathcal{C}(a,b).

We plot the inter-class contribution matrices for MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and CIFAR-10 in figure
2
. The rows represent training data classes and the columns represent test data classes.
As normalization, we divide every matrix entry by
\sqrt{\text{column sum}\times\text{row sum}}
.

In all three datasets, the maximum contribution values for each row and column appear on the diagonal, indicating that training data always produce the most reduction in test loss for their own class. In MNIST, we observe symmetrically low contribution between the digit pairs (3, 5), (2, 7), and (4, 9). As the two classes in each pair have similar appearances, there is competition between them. Lowering the test loss of one class will likely increase the test loss of the other class.
Similar symmetry exists for the pairs (Pullover, Coat), (T-shirt, Shirt), and (Sneaker, Ankle boot) in Fashion-MNIST, and between Automobile and Truck in CIFAR-10. We find these to be consistent with intuition.

Conclusion

We propose
HyDRA
, a technique for estimating the contribution of training data by differentiating the test loss against training data weights through time. To simplify computation, we provide a Hessian-free approximation to the exact derivative and establish an analytical upper bound for the approximation error. In the experiments, we compare
HyDRA
to influence functions
(
KohandLiang 2017
)
, and confirm that
HyDRA
provide more accurate estimates for data contribution, which facilitates the identification of label noise in the training data. With
HyDRA
, the AI research community can be equipped with an effective and computationally efficient tool to interpret the influence of training samples to DNN predictions.

Broader Impact

The deployment of machine learning in critical areas such as law enforcement and human resources has raised concerns regarding potential bias and prejudice of such algorithms
(
Frazieret al. 2019
;
Poyiadziet al. 2020
)
. In many cases, the apparent bias is not due to algorithmic design, but to existing stereotypes inadvertently captured by training data
(
Mehrabiet al. 2019
;
BryantandHoward 2019
)
. Therefore, the ability to trace an algorithmic prediction back to training data samples could help mitigate and eventually eliminate model bias in machine learning. We caution that the elimination of bias requires systemic efforts that extend far beyond pure algorithmic research. Having said that, we believe that this work could play a positive role in that direction.

Appendix E
Approximation Error Analysis

In this section, we will analyze the approximation error introduced by dropping the Hessian term in the case of vanilla GD.
After dropping the Hessian term, the recurrent update equation becomes
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t,i}=\nabla_{t-1,i}-\eta\lambda\nabla_{t-1,i}-\eta\boldsymbol{g}_{t-1,i}
.
We are interested in bounding the norm of the approximation error, which is defined below.

1
.

The approximation error at the
t{\text{th}}
iteration is defined as

\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\coloneq\nabla_{t,i}-\widetilde{\nabla}_{t,i}
,
where
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t,i}
is the approximation of
\nabla_{t,i}
.

Before the analysis, we also need some moderate conditions about the optimization process.

1
.

The training loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}{}
is twice differentiable.

2
.

The optimization process converges, that is,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
.

Bounds with Lipschitz Continuity

First, we bound the error under several moderate conditions regarding the optimization process. In the next subsection, we show how these conditions can be further relaxed.

3
.

The empirical risk function
\mathcal{R}
has Lipschitz-continuous gradients with Lipschitz constant
L
. Formally, there exists a constant
L
such that

{\Big\lVert\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\Big\rVert}_{2}\leq L{\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\rVert}_{2},\forall\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\in\mathcal{H}.

The Lipschitz continuity is a regular condition, which is implied by, for example, that
\mathcal{R}
is twice differentiable and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
takes value from a compact hypothesis space. The latter is likely true since
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
tends to be close to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
. It is worth noting that this condition constrains the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{t}}
to the range
[-L,L]
.

4
.

The learning rate sequence
\eta
is non-increasing and lower-bounded by 0. That is,

\eta_{t}\geq\eta_{t+1}>0,\forall t.

Since
\eta_{t}
and
\lambda
are both typically quite small, we assume their product is also small.

5
.

The sequence of products of learning rate
\eta
and regularization coefficient
\lambda
satisfies

0<\eta_{t}\lambda<1,\forall{t}
.

Finally, the sequence
\nabla_{t,i}
should be bounded, or efforts to estimate it would end in vain.

6
.

The sequence
\nabla_{t,i}
is ultimately bounded by a constant
P
. Without loss of generality,

{\lVert\nabla_{t,i}\rVert}_{2}<P,\forall i.

1
.

With conditions
1
-
6
, the norm of the approximation error is bounded by

{\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rVert}_{2}<LP\frac{\eta_{1}}{\eta_{t}\lambda}.

Proof.

First, we have the recursive formula:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{0}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(10)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{t}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta\lambda)\boldsymbol{e}_{t-1}-\eta\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{t-1}}\nabla_{t-1,i}.

(11)

After solving it, we get

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t}(-\eta_{j}){(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}.

(12)

By the triangle inequality,

{\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rVert}_{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}{\lVert\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}\rVert}_{2}.

We then have

\begin{split}&\sum_{j=1}^{t}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}{\lVert\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}\rVert}_{2}\\
\leq&\,\sum_{j=1}^{t}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}{\lVert L\nabla_{j-1,i}\rVert}_{2}\\
=&\,\sum_{j=1}^{t}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}LP\\
\leq&\,LP\eta_{1}\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}{(1-\eta_{t}\lambda)}^{j}\\
=&\,LP\eta_{1}\frac{1-(1-\eta_{t}\lambda)^{t}}{\eta_{t}\lambda}\\
<&\,LP\frac{\eta_{1}}{\eta_{t}\lambda}.\end{split}

As such, we obtain the desired inequality. ∎

Furthermore, if we know that the learning rate decays sufficiently exponentially, the approximation error diminishes when
t
tends to infinity.

7
.

The learning rate sequence
\eta
decays exponentially at rate
c
, which is less than
1-\eta_{1}\lambda
. That is,

\eta_{t+1}=c\eta_{t},\;\forall t,

0<c<1-\eta_{1}\lambda.

2
.

With conditions
1
-
7
and the learning rate schedule in condition
7
, the approximation error diminishes when
t
tends to infinity

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rVert}_{2}=0.

Proof.

Under the specific learning rate schedule, we have

\begin{split}{\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\rVert}_{2}&\leq LP\sum_{j=1}^{t}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}\\
&=LP\sum_{j=1}^{t}\eta_{1}c^{j-1}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}\\
&\leq LP\frac{\eta_{1}}{c}{(1-\eta_{t}\lambda)}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(\frac{c}{1-\eta_{1}\lambda}\right)^{j}\\
&=LP\frac{\eta_{1}}{c}{(1-\eta_{t}\lambda)}^{t}\frac{\frac{c}{1-\eta_{1}\lambda}-(\frac{c}{1-\eta_{1}\lambda})^{t+1}}{1-\frac{c}{1-\eta_{1}\lambda}}.\end{split}

When
t\to\infty
,
{(1-\eta_{t}\lambda)}^{t}
and
(c/(1-\eta_{1}\lambda))^{t+1}
go to zero, and the claim follows. ∎

Bounds with Relaxed Conditions

The condition
3
in the previous section may appear to be too restrictive. In this subsection, we replace it with a more relaxed condition and show that the error is still upper-bounded in the limit.

8
.

The Hessian sequence
\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}
converges as
t\to\infty

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{t}}={\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*},

(13)

where
{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*}
is the Hessian of the empirical risk
\mathcal{R}
at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
.

Since
\boldsymbol{\theta}
converges, it makes sense to assume that Hessian converges, too.

1
.

The eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}
converge to the eigenvalues of
{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*}
.

By this corollary, we can find an index
N
such that
\lvert\kappa_{\text{max}}{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{t}}}-\kappa_{\text{max}}{{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*}}\rvert<\delta,\forall t\geq N
, given an arbitrarily small
\delta
, where
\kappa_{\text{max}}
is the eigenvalue with maximal absolute value. Now, we fix such an infinitesimal
\delta_{\text{eigen}}
and the corresponding index
N_{\text{eigen}}
.

Finally, since
\eta_{t}
and
\lambda
are both typically quite small, we assume their product is eventually small.

3
.

With conditions
1
-
6
, and
8
, and let
t_{0}=N_{\text{eigen}}+1
be the start of the tail portion of the optimization, we can upper bound the error’s norm as

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t,t_{0}}\rVert}_{2}<(\kappa_{\text{max}}{{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*}}+\delta_{\text{eigen}})P\frac{\eta_{t_{0}}}{\eta_{t}\lambda}.

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{t,t_{0}}
is a shorthand for a two-part sum that constitute
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
,

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}&=\boldsymbol{e}_{t,t_{0}}\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{t_{0}-1}(-\eta_{j}){(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=t_{0}}^{t}(-\eta_{j}){(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}.\end{split}

Proof.
Again, we have the recursive formula:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{0}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(14)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{t}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta\lambda)\boldsymbol{e}_{t-1}-\eta\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{t-1}}\nabla_{t-1,i}.

(15)

After solving it, we get

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t}(-\eta_{j}){(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}.

(16)

Now if
t\geq t_{0}
, then by the triangle inequality,

\begin{split}{\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t,t_{0}}\rVert}_{2}&\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t_{0}-1}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}{\lVert\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}\rVert}_{2}\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=t_{0}}^{t}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}{\lVert\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}\rVert}_{2}.\end{split}

Consider the second part first, we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{j=t_{0}}^{t}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}{\lVert\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}\rVert}_{2}\\
<&\sum_{j=t_{0}}^{t}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}(\kappa_{\text{max}}{{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*}}+\delta_{\text{eigen}}){\lVert\nabla_{j-1,i}\rVert}_{2}\\
\leq&(\kappa_{\text{max}}{{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*}}+\delta_{\text{eigen}})P\sum_{j=t_{0}}^{t}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}\\
\leq&(\kappa_{\text{max}}{{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*}}+\delta_{\text{eigen}})P\eta_{t_{0}}\sum_{j=t_{0}}^{t}{(1-\eta_{t}\lambda)}^{t-j}\\
=&(\kappa_{\text{max}}{{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*}}+\delta_{\text{eigen}})P\eta_{t_{0}}\frac{1-(1-\eta_{t}\lambda){}^{t-t_{0}}}{\eta_{t}\lambda}\\
<&(\kappa_{\text{max}}{{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er}}}^{*}}+\delta_{\text{eigen}})P\frac{\eta_{t_{0}}}{\eta_{t}\lambda}.\end{split}

Note that the first part of the right-hand side goes to
0
as
t\to\infty
. In other words, for any small
\delta_{1}>0
, there exists a number
N_{1}>t_{0}
, such that for all
t\geq N_{1}
,

\sum_{j=1}^{t_{0}-1}\eta_{j}{(1-\eta_{j}\lambda)}^{t-j}{\lVert\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{er},{j-1}}\nabla_{j-1,i}\rVert}_{2}<\delta_{1}.

Taken together, for any infinitesimal
\delta_{1}>0
, there exist an index
N_{1}
