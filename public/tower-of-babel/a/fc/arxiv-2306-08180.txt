Abstract
. Generalized Abel equations have been employed in the recent literature to invert Radon transforms which arise in a number of important imaging applications, including Compton Scatter Tomography (CST), Ultrasound Reflection Tomography (URT), and X-ray CT. In this paper, we present novel injectivity results and inversion methods for Generalized Abel operators. We apply our theory to a new Radon transform, ℛ j \mathcal{R}_{j} , of interest in URT, which integrates a square integrable function of compact support, f f , over ellipsoid and hyperboloid surfaces with centers on a plane. Using our newly established theory on generalized Abel equations, we show that ℛ j \mathcal{R}_{j} is injective and provide an inversion method based on Neumann series. In addition, using algebraic methods, we present image phantom reconstructions from ℛ j ​ f \mathcal{R}_{j}f data with added pseudo random noise.

We introduce novel inversion methods for a class of generalized Abel operators. We show, under certain conditions on the operator kernel, that the generalized Abel equations have unique square integrable solution. We apply our theory to ellipsoid and hyperboloid Radon transforms,
\mathcal{R}_{j}
, which integrate square integrable functions of compact support over ellipsoids and hyperboloids of revolution with centers on a plane in
n
-dimensional space. The stability of
\mathcal{R}_{j}
is analyzed in
[
12
, example 3.4]
, from a microlocal perspective. Inversion formulae are not provided, however. We aim to address this here. We prove that
\mathcal{R}_{j}
is injective, and provide an inversion method based on Neumann series. Specifically, after Fourier decomposition, we show that
\mathcal{R}_{j}
can be reduced to a set of generalized Abel operators. The operator kernels are then shown to satisfy the specific conditions of our theorems to invert
\mathcal{R}_{j}
. We also introduce a novel generalized Radon transform,
\mathcal{R}
, which defines the integrals of a square integrable function over the surfaces of revolution of continuous curves, which are more general than ellipses and hyperbola. In addition to our theory on
\mathcal{R}_{j}
, we prove injectivity results for
\mathcal{R}
.

3.
Volterra equations

In this section, we review weakly singular Volterra equations and prove our first main result which provides uniqueness of solution conditions for a class of generalized Abel equations.

3.1.
Weakly singular Volterra equations

In this section, we review some results from the literature on weakly singular Volterra equations. We have the theorem

6

Theorem 3.1
.

Set
j=0
or
j=1

f\in L^{2}([a,b])
, where
-\infty<a<b<\infty

\beta\in[0,1)

K\in L^{2}(T_{j})

the following conditions on

be satisfied:

(1)

where

and

is known for all

Proof.

The case

follows directly from theory on standard Volterra equations of the first kind. We now consider the case
\beta\in(0,1)

D_{0}(r)=[a,r]

\begin{split}\int_{D_{j}(r)}\frac{g(p)}{\left[(-1)^{j}(r-p)\right]^{1-\beta}}\mathrm{d}p&=\int_{D_{j}(r)}\int_{B_{j}(p)}\frac{K(p,\omega)}{\left[(-1)^{j}(p-\omega)\right]^{\beta}\left[(-1)^{j}(r-p)\right]^{1-\beta}}f(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}p\\
&=\int_{D_{j}(r)}L_{\beta}(r,\omega)f(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega,\end{split}

where

L_{\beta}(r,\omega)=(-1)^{j}\int_{\omega}^{r}\frac{K(p,\omega)}{\left[(-1)^{j}(p-\omega)\right]^{\beta}\left[(-1)^{j}(r-p)\right]^{1-\beta}}\mathrm{d}p.

We now substitute

, and

(3.3)

\begin{split}L_{\beta}(r,\omega)&=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{K(\omega+j(r-\omega)+(-1)^{j}(r-\omega)t,\omega)}{t^{\beta}(1-t)^{1-\beta}}\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

With this, we have

L_{\beta}(r,r)=\frac{\pi K(r,r)}{\sin(\pi\beta)}=c_{\beta}(r),

where
\left|c_{\beta}(r)\right|\geq\epsilon
, for all
r\in[a,b]
, for some
\epsilon>0
, by conditions (1) and (2). That is,

is bounded away from zero on

. Further, we have

(3.4)

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\beta}}{\mathrm{d}r}(r,\omega)\right|&=\left|\int_{0}^{1}(-1)^{j}t\cdot\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}K}{\mathrm{d}p}(\omega+j(r-\omega)+(-1)^{j}(r-\omega)t,\omega)}{t^{\beta}(1-t)^{1-\beta}}\mathrm{d}t\right|\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{1}t\cdot\frac{\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}K}{\mathrm{d}p}(\omega+j(r-\omega)+(-1)^{j}(r-\omega)t,\omega)\right|}{t^{\beta}(1-t)^{1-\beta}}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\frac{\pi M}{\sin(\pi\beta)},\end{split}

for
(r,w)\in T_{j}
, where
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}K}{\mathrm{d}p}\right|\leq M
on
T_{j}
, by condition (1), noting that
T_{j}
is compact.

Now

(3.5)

\begin{split}h(r)&=\frac{1}{c_{\beta}(r)}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\int_{D_{j}(r)}\frac{g(p)}{\left[(-1)^{j}(r-p)\right]^{1-\beta}}\mathrm{d}p\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{c_{\beta}(r)}\int_{D_{j}(r)}\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\beta}}{\mathrm{d}r}(r,\omega)f(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega+(-1)^{j}f(r)\end{split}

we can convert (
3.2
) into a Volterra equation of the second kind with bounded kernel and solve uniquely for
f
by Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Theorem 4.1

We split the proof into two parts. First, we prove the

The

-
For this part of the proof, we consider the Radon transform

\mathcal{R}_{j}f(t,y_{1})=\int_{\Omega_{j}}\frac{\sqrt{t+\left(\frac{1}{s}+(-1)^{j+1}\right)x_{1}^{2}}}{\sqrt{t+(-1)^{j+1}x_{1}^{2}}}f\left(x_{1}+y_{1},\sqrt{\frac{t+(-1)^{j+1}x_{1}^{2}}{s}}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{1},

,

and
\Omega^{+}_{1}=[0,\infty)

\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{j}f}(t,\xi)=\int_{B_{j}}\cos\left(\xi\sqrt{(-1)^{j}(t-sx_{2}^{2})}\right)\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\frac{st}{t-sx_{2}^{2}}+\left(1+(-1)^{j+1}s\right)}\hat{f}\left(\xi,x_{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{2},

. Substituting

\begin{split}&\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{j}f}(p,\xi)=\\
&\int_{B_{j}}\cos\left(\xi\sqrt{s}\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\right)}\right)\sqrt{(-1)^{j}s\frac{p^{2}}{p^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}+\left(1+(-1)^{j+1}s\right)}\hat{f}\left(\xi,x_{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{2}\\

a weakly singular Volterra equation. Now we can apply Theorem
3.1
to recover

. To see this, let

with

(4.10)

\begin{split}&H_{j}(\xi,p,x_{2})=\\
&\left[\cos\left(s\xi\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\right)}\right)\right]\left[\sqrt{s\frac{p^{2}}{p+x_{2}}+(-1)^{j}\left(1+(-1)^{j+1}s\right)(p-x_{2})}\right]\\

Let

be defined as in Theorem
3.1
. We have

H_{j}(\xi,p,p)=\sqrt{\frac{s}{2}}\cdot p\neq 0,

for
p\in[a,b]
,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}g_{j}\in C(T_{j})
, and

(4.11)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}h_{j}(\xi,p,x_{2})&=\frac{(-1)^{j+1}\xi p\sqrt{s}}{\sqrt{(-1)^{j}(p^{2}-x_{2}^{2})}}\sin\left(\xi\sqrt{s}\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\right)}\right)\\
&=(-1)^{j+1}ps\xi^{2}\sinc\left(\xi\sqrt{s}\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\right)}\right).\end{split}

Thus,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}h_{j}(\xi,\cdot,\cdot)\in C(T_{j})
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}H_{j}(\xi,\cdot,\cdot)\in C(T_{j})
, for any fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, and for

3.1

The

-

and

Substituting

gives

(4.14)

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{j}f}(p,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})&=s^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\int_{B_{j}}\left[(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)\right]^{\frac{n-3}{2}}\sqrt{(-1)^{j}(p^{2}-\omega^{2})+s\omega^{2}}\\
&\times\left[\int_{S^{n-2}}e^{i\sqrt{s}\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)}(\Theta\cdot{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\mathrm{d}S^{n-2}\right]\hat{f}\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}},\omega\right)\mathrm{d}\omega\\
&=s^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\int_{B_{j}}\left[(-1)^{j}\left(p-\omega\right)\right]^{\alpha}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}K_{i}(p,\omega)\right]\hat{f}\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}},\omega\right)\mathrm{d}\omega,\end{split}

a generalized Abel equation, where
\alpha=\frac{n-3}{2}\geq 0
,
B_{0}=[0,p]
,
B_{1}=[p,b]
, and
p=\sqrt{t/s}
. The kernels
K_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3
, are defined

(4.15)

\begin{split}K_{1}(p,\omega)&=\int_{S^{n-2}}e^{i\sqrt{s}\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)}(\Theta\cdot{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\mathrm{d}S^{n-2}\\
&=\alpha_{n}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{i\sqrt{s}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)}\cos\varphi_{n-2}}\sin^{n-3}(\varphi_{n-2})\mathrm{d}\varphi_{n-2}\\
&=\alpha_{n}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos\left(\sqrt{s}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)}\cos\varphi_{n-2}\right)\sin^{n-3}(\varphi_{n-2})\mathrm{d}\varphi_{n-2},\end{split}

where
\alpha_{3}=2
,
\alpha_{4}=2\pi
, and

(4.16)

\begin{split}\alpha_{n}&=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[\int_{[0,\pi]^{n-4}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-4}\sin^{i}(\varphi_{i})\mathrm{d}\varphi_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}\varphi_{n-4}\right]\mathrm{d}\varphi_{n-3}\\
&=2\pi\int_{[0,\pi]^{n-4}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-4}\sin^{i}(\varphi_{i})\mathrm{d}\varphi_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}\varphi_{n-4},\end{split}

for
n\geq 5
, where
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n-2}

, which parametrize the unit
(n-2)
-sphere. The kernel
K_{2}
is defined by

(4.17)

K_{2}(p,\omega)=(p+\omega)^{\alpha},

and

(4.18)

\begin{split}K_{3}(p,\omega)&=\sqrt{(-1)^{j}(p^{2}-\omega^{2})+s\omega^{2}}.\end{split}

Now,

K(p,p)=\left[\int_{S^{n-2}}\mathrm{d}S^{n-2}\right](2p)^{\alpha}\left(p\sqrt{s}\right)=\left(2^{\alpha}A_{n-2}\sqrt{s}\right)p^{\alpha+1}\neq 0,\ \text{for all}\ p\in[a,b],

where
A_{n-2}
is the surface area of
S^{n-2}
. Let
T_{j}
be defined as is Theorem
3.1
. Then
K^{(i)}_{2},K^{(i)}_{3}\in C(T_{j})
, for any
i\geq 0
.

We have
K_{1}\in C(T_{j})
, and

(4.19)

\begin{split}&K^{(1)}_{1}(p,\omega)=\\
&\frac{\alpha_{n}\sqrt{s}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|p}{(-1)^{j+1}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin\left(\sqrt{s}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)}\cos\varphi_{n-2}\right)}{\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)}}\cos\varphi_{n-2}\sin^{n-3}(\varphi_{n-2})\mathrm{d}\varphi_{n-2}\\
&=\frac{\alpha_{n}s|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|^{2}p}{(-1)^{j+1}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sinc\left(\sqrt{s}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)}\cos\varphi_{n-2}\right)\cos^{2}\varphi_{n-2}\sin^{n-3}(\varphi_{n-2})\mathrm{d}\varphi_{n-2}.\end{split}

Define
J\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
as

J(u)=\alpha_{n}(-1)^{j+1}s|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sinc\left(\sqrt{s}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|u\cos\varphi_{n-2}\right)\cos^{2}\varphi_{n-2}\sin^{n-3}(\varphi_{n-2})\mathrm{d}\varphi_{n-2}.

Then,

G_{1,1}(p,\omega)=K^{(1)}_{1}(p,\omega)=p\cdot J\left(\sqrt{(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)}\right)\in C(T_{j}).

Letting

, we have, by Theorem
2.3

(4.20)

\begin{split}\left(J\circ g\right)^{(i)}(p,\omega)&=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{i}}{\mathrm{d}p^{i}}\left(J\circ g\right)(p,\omega)\\
&=\sum_{\gamma\in\Pi}J^{(|\gamma|)}\left(g(p,\omega)\right)\cdot\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}g^{(|\tau|)}(p,\omega),\end{split}

where
\Pi
is the set of partitions of
\{1,\ldots,i\}
, and
i\geq 1

We have

(4.21)

g^{(q)}(p,\omega)=P_{j,q}(p,\omega)\left[(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}-q},

for

P_{j,q}(p,\omega)=(p^{2}-\omega^{2})P^{(1)}_{j,q-1}(p,\omega)-\left(2q-3\right)p\cdot P_{j,q-1}(p,\omega),\ \ \text{for}\ \ j\geq 2,

with
P_{j,1}(p,\omega)=(-1)^{j}p
, and
P_{j,0}(p,\omega)=1
.
It follows that, for
\gamma\in\Pi

(4.22)

\begin{split}\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}g^{(|\tau|)}(p,\omega)&=\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}P_{j,|\tau|}(p,\omega)\left[(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}-|\tau|}\\
&=\left[(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)\right]^{\frac{|\gamma|}{2}-i}\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}P_{j,|\tau|}(p,\omega).\end{split}

Now, (
4.20
) becomes

(4.23)

\begin{split}&\left(J\circ g\right)^{(i)}(p,\omega)=\\
&\ \ \ \ \frac{1}{\left[(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)\right]^{i}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Pi}J^{(|\gamma|)}\left(g(p,\omega)\right)\cdot\left[(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)\right]^{\frac{|\gamma|}{2}}\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}P_{j,|\tau|}(p,\omega).\end{split}

Hence,

H_{i,q}(p,\omega)=\left[(-1)^{j}\left(p^{2}-\omega^{2}\right)\right]^{i}\left(J\circ g\right)^{(q)}(p,\omega)\in C(T_{j}),

for all
1\leq q\leq i
.

Now, using Theorem

(4.24)

K^{(i)}_{1}(p,\omega)=p\cdot\left(J\circ g\right)^{(i-1)}(p,\omega)+(i-1)\left(J\circ g\right)^{(i-2)}(p,\omega),

(4.25)

\begin{split}G_{1,i}(p,\omega)&=\left[(-1)^{j}(p-\omega)\right]^{i-1}K_{1}^{(i)}(p,\omega)\\

∎

Remark 4.2
.

Let
f\in L^{2}_{c}\left([-c,c]^{n-1}\times[a,b]\right)
, where
c>0
, and
0<a<b
, and let us consider the special case when
s=1
and
j=0
. In this case,
\mathcal{R}_{0}f(t,\mathbf{y})
defines the integrals of
f
over spheres, radius
\sqrt{t}
, center
\mathbf{y}
. By Theorem
4.1
, to reconstruct
f
uniquely from
\mathcal{R}_{0}f
when
s=1
, it is sufficient to know
\mathcal{R}_{0}f(t,\mathbf{y})
for
\mathbf{y}\in\left[-(c+b),(c+b)\right]^{n-1}
, and for
\sqrt{t}\in[a,b]
. We have thus derived an inversion method for the spherical Radon transform with centers on a plane in
n
-dimensions, which uses only the sphere centers on a finite volume cube, and radii on the bound
[a,b]
. As discussed in the introduction, the formulae of
[
24
]
uses all sphere centers and radii, and in
[
26
]
, the authors present support theorems, but no inversion method. In
[
32
]
, the authors present an inversion formula for
\mathcal{R}_{0}
in the
s=1
case, which applies to distributions,
f
, and only uses limited sphere radii and centers. However, their inversion formulae only apply to the continuous part of
f
. Theorem
4.1
applies to
L^{2}_{c}
functions. Thus, Theorem
4.1
derives important inversion methods for the spherical Radon transform, and for more general ellipsoid and hyperboloid transforms.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{+}_{j}\left(t,y_{1}\right)=\mathcal{C}_{j}\left(t,y_{1}\right)
, and let
\mathcal{C}^{-}_{j}\left(t,y_{1}\right)
be the reflection of
\mathcal{C}^{+}_{j}\left(t,y_{1}\right)
in
\{x_{2}=0\}
. Let
\mathcal{S}^{+}_{j}(t,\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{S}_{j}(t,\mathbf{y})
and
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{j}(t,\mathbf{y})
be the reflections of one another in
\{x_{n}=0\}
. Then, we define

(4.26)

\mathcal{E}_{j}f(t,y_{1})=\int_{\mathcal{C}^{-}_{j}\left(t,y_{1}\right)}f\mathrm{d}\mathcal{C}^{-}_{j}+\int_{\mathcal{C}^{+}_{j}\left(t,y_{1}\right)}f\mathrm{d}\mathcal{C}^{+}_{j},

when
n=2
, and

(4.27)

\mathcal{E}_{j}f(t,\mathbf{y})=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{-}_{j}(t,\mathbf{y})}f\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}^{-}_{j}+\int_{\mathcal{S}^{+}_{j}(t,\mathbf{y})}f\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}^{+}_{j},

when
n\geq 3
. When
s>1
,
\mathcal{E}_{0}f
defines the integrals of
f
over two-sided elliptic arcs of revolution. When
0<s\leq 1
,
\mathcal{E}_{0}f
defines the integrals of
f
over ellipsoids of revolution.
\mathcal{E}_{1}
defines integrals over two-sheeted hyperboloids. We now have the following corollary.

Corollary 4.3
.

The null space of
\mathcal{E}_{j}
, on domain
L^{2}_{c}\left(\Delta_{a,b}\right)
, where
0<a<b<\infty
and
\Delta_{a,b}=\{a\leq x_{n}\leq b\}\cup\{-b\leq x_{n}\leq-a\}
, is completed composed of odd functions in
x_{n}

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{2}_{c}\left(\Delta_{a,b}\right)
be such that
\mathcal{E}_{j}f=0
. Let
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
, where
f_{i}=f
on
\{(-1)^{i}x_{n}<0\}
, and
f_{i}=0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{(-1)^{i}x_{n}<0\}
. Then,
\mathcal{E}_{j}f=\mathcal{R}_{j}\left(f_{1}+\tilde{f_{2}}\right)=0
, where
\tilde{f_{2}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},x_{n})=f_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},-x_{n})
, and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}\right)
. Thus, by Theorem
4.1
,
f_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},x_{n})=-f_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},-x_{n})
, and
f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},x_{n})=-f_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},-x_{n})+f_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},x_{n})
is odd in
x_{n}
.
∎

Discussion 4.4
.

In
[
12
]
, the authors analyze the stability of
\mathcal{E}_{j}
from a microlocal perspective. They show that
\mathcal{E}_{j}
is a Fourier Integral Operator (FIO) which satisfies the Bolker condition, if
f
is supported on one side of
\{x_{n}=0\}
. Thus, for such
f
, we would not expect to see any additional (unwanted) image singularities in the reconstruction.

Let us now break down some of the key instabilities in the inversion steps needed to solve the Volterra equations (
4.9
) and (
4.14
), which are from the proof of Theorem
4.1
. When
n
is even, we require
m=n/2
differentiation steps and one Abel operation (which removes the singularity as in (
3.2
)) to solve (
4.9
) and (
4.14
). The Abel operator is order
1/2
smoothing on Sobolev scale, so in total this means that, roughly speaking,
(n-1)/2
differentiation steps are needed to invert (
4.9
) and (
4.14
), when
n
is even. When
n
is odd, no Abel operation is needed, as there is no singularity on the kernel diagonal, and, as in the even case,
(n-1)/2
differentiation steps are needed to invert (
4.14
). The remaining inversion operations, i.e., Fourier transformation, change of variables, and inverting a second type Volterra equation with bounded kernel, do not require any further differentiation steps, and are more stable. As the dimension increases, so too does the order of smoothing and the ill-posedness, and the increase in smoothing appears to grow at the same rate as the classical hyperplane Radon transform, which is also order
(n-1)/2
smoothing on Sobolev scale
[
29
, Theorem 5.1]
. This discussion does not provide proof of this, however, and Sobolev space theory should be explored to address this. We leave this for future work as it is beyond the scope of this paper.

4.2.
A generalization

In this subsection, we present a generalization of the results of Theorem
4.1
. Let
f\in L^{2}_{c}\left(\{a\leq x_{n}\leq b\}\right)
, where
0\leq a<b
, Let
r=r(p,\omega)\in C\left(T_{0}\right)
, where
T_{0}=\{(p,\omega):a\leq p\leq b,a\leq\omega\leq p\}
. Then, we define the generalized Radon transform, for
n\geq 2
,

(4.28)

\mathcal{R}f(p,\mathbf{y})=\int_{S^{n-2}}\int_{a}^{p}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}\omega}\right)^{2}}r(p,\omega)^{n-2}f\left(r(p,\omega)\Theta+\mathbf{y},\omega\right)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}S^{n-2}.

When
n=2
,
\Theta=\pm 1
, and the integral
\int_{S^{n-2}}=\sum_{\Theta=\pm 1}
becomes a sum over two points, and
\mathcal{R}f
defines the integral of
f
over the curve
\mathcal{C}=\{\left(r(p,\omega)+y_{2},\omega\right):\omega\in[0,p]\}
, and its reflection in the line
\{(y_{2},x_{2}):x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}
. When
n\geq 3
,
\mathcal{R}f
defines the integrals of
f
over the surfaces of revolution of
\mathcal{C}
, with central axis
\{(\mathbf{y},x_{n}):x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

We now have the following theorem, which is a generalization of Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
f\in L^{2}_{c}\left(\{a\leq x_{n}\leq b\}\right)
, where
0\leq a<b
. Let
r\in C\left(T_{0}\right)
, be of the form

(4.29)

r(p,\omega)=(p-w)^{\frac{q}{2}}\cdot\nu(p,\omega),

where
q\geq 1
, is an integer,
\nu\in C^{\infty}\left(T_{0}\right)
, and
T_{0}=\{(p,\omega):a\leq p\leq b,a\leq\omega\leq p\}
. Further assume that
\nu(p,p)\neq 0
, for
p\in[a,b]
. Then, (
4.28
) can be solved uniquely for
f
, if
\mathcal{R}f(p,\mathbf{y})
is known for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, and
p\in[a,b]
.

Remark 4.6
.

Note, for bounded
\nu
, we only need the
\mathbf{y}
in some compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, as
f
is compactly supported.

Proof.

Taking the Fourier transform in
\mathbf{y}
on both sides of (
4.28
) yields

(4.30)

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathcal{R}f}(p,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})&=\int_{a}^{p}(p-\omega)^{\alpha}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{3}K_{i}(p,\omega)\right]\cdot\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{\xi}},\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega\\
&=\int_{a}^{p}(p-\omega)^{\alpha}K(p,\omega)\cdot\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{\xi}},\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega,\end{split}

a set of generalized Abel equations.

Let
\beta=q/2
. We define

\begin{cases}\alpha=(n-3)/2\geq-1/2,&\text{if $q=1$}.\\
\alpha=\beta(n-2)\geq 0,&\text{if $q\geq 2$}.\end{cases}

As
\alpha>-1
, we aim to apply Corollary
3.5
to solve (
4.30
). The kernels
K_{i}
are defined

(4.31)

K_{1}(p,\omega)=\begin{cases}2\cos\left(|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|(p-\omega)^{\beta}\nu(p,\omega)\right),&\text{if $n=2$}.\\
\alpha_{n}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos\left(|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|(p-\omega)^{\beta}\nu(p,\omega)\cos\varphi\right)\sin^{n-3}\varphi\mathrm{d}\varphi,&\text{if $n\geq 3$},\end{cases}

where the
\alpha_{n}
are as defined in Theorem
4.1
,
K_{2}(p,\omega)=\nu(p,\omega)^{n-2}
, and

(4.32)

K_{3}(p,\omega)=\begin{cases}\left((p-\omega)+\left((p-\omega)\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\omega}(p,\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\nu(p,\omega)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},&\text{if $q=1$}.\\
\left(1+(p-\omega)^{q-2}\left((p-\omega)\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\omega}(p,\omega)-\beta\nu(p,\omega)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},&\text{if $q\geq 2$}.\end{cases}

Let

g(p,\omega)=(p-\omega)+\left((p-\omega)\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\omega}(p,\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\nu(p,\omega)\right)^{2}\in C^{\infty}(T_{0}).

When
p=\omega
,
g(p,p)=\frac{1}{4}\nu(p,p)^{2}>0
, for
p\in[a,b]
, by assumption. When
p>\omega
and
(p,\omega)\in T_{0}
, then clearly
g(p,\omega)>0
. Thus,
g>0
everywhere on
T_{0}
, and must also be bounded away from zero on
T_{0}
, by smoothness of
g
and compactness of
T_{0}
. Therefore,
K_{3}(p,\omega)=\sqrt{g(p,\omega)}\in C^{\infty}\left(T_{0}\right)
, when
q=1
. If
q\geq 2
, then
K_{3}
is clearly smooth on
T_{0}
. Further,
K_{2}\in C^{\infty}\left(T_{0}\right)
, since
\nu\in C^{\infty}\left(T_{0}\right)
, by assumption.

Now, we have

(4.33)

K(p,p)=\begin{cases}\left|\nu(p,p)\right|,&\text{if $q=1$ and $n=2$}.\\
\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n-3}\varphi\mathrm{d}\varphi\right)\cdot\left|\nu(p,p)\right|\cdot\nu(p,p)^{n-2},&\text{if $q=1$ and $n\geq 3$}.\\
2\mu(p),&\text{if $q\geq 2$ and $n=2$}.\\
\alpha_{n}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n-3}\varphi\mathrm{d}\varphi\right)\cdot\mu(p)\cdot\nu(p,p)^{n-2},&\text{if $q\geq 2$ and $n\geq 3$},\end{cases}

where

(4.34)

\mu(p)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{1+\left(\beta\nu(p,p)\right)^{2}},&\text{if $q=2$}.\\
1,&\text{if $q\geq 3$}.\end{cases}

Therefore,
K(p,p)\neq 0
, for
p\in[a,b]
, by assumption that
\nu(p,p)\neq 0
, for
p\in[a,b]
. It remains to be shown that
K_{1}
satisfies the conditions of Corollary
3.5
.

Let
g_{1}(p,\omega)=|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|(p-\omega)^{\beta}\nu(p,\omega)\in C(T_{0})
. Let
n=2
. Then, we have

(4.35)

\frac{\mathrm{d}K_{1}}{\mathrm{d}p}(p,\omega)=2g_{2}(p,\omega)\sinc\left(g_{1}(p,\omega)\right)\in C(T_{0}),

where

(4.36)

\begin{split}g_{2}(p,\omega)&=-g_{1}(p,\omega)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}g_{1}}{\mathrm{d}p}(p,\omega)\\
&=-|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|^{2}(p-\omega)^{2\beta-1}\nu(p,\omega)\left[\beta\nu(p,\omega)+(p-\omega)\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}p}(p,\omega)\right],\end{split}

which is smooth on
T_{0}
. We can now apply Theorem
3.1
to prove the result in the
n=2
case.

Let
n\geq 3
. Then,

(4.37)

\frac{\mathrm{d}K_{1}}{\mathrm{d}p}(p,\omega)=\alpha_{n}\cdot g_{2}(p,\omega)\int_{0}^{\pi}\sinc\left(g_{1}(p,\omega)\cos\varphi\right)\sigma(\varphi)\mathrm{d}\varphi\in C(T_{0}),

where
\sigma(\varphi)=\cos^{2}(\varphi)\sin^{n-3}(\varphi)
. Let
J\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be defined

J(u)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sinc\left(u\cos\varphi\right)\sigma(\varphi)\mathrm{d}\varphi.

To finish the proof, we aim to show

(4.38)

h_{k}(p,\omega)=(p-\omega)^{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}p^{k}}J\left(g_{1}(p,\omega)\right)\in C(T_{0}),

for any
k\geq 1
. When
q
is even, (
4.38
) clearly holds. We now focus on the case when
q
is odd. Using Theorem
2.3
and Theorem
2.2
, we have, for

,

(4.39)

\begin{split}h_{k}(p,\omega)&=(p-\omega)^{k}\cdot\sum_{\gamma\in\Pi}J^{(|\gamma|)}\left(g_{1}(p,\omega)\right)\cdot\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}g^{(|\tau|)}_{1}(p,\omega)\\
&=\sum_{\gamma\in\Pi}J^{(|\gamma|)}\left(g_{1}(p,\omega)\right)\cdot(p-\omega)^{k}\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{|\tau|}c_{i,|\tau|}(p-\omega)^{\beta-(|\tau|-i)}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{i}\nu}{\mathrm{d}p^{i}}(p,\omega)\right]\\
&=\sum_{\gamma\in\Pi}J^{(|\gamma|)}\left(g_{1}(p,\omega)\right)\cdot\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}(p-\omega)^{|\tau|}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{|\tau|}c_{i,|\tau|}(p-\omega)^{\beta-(|\tau|-i)}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{i}\nu}{\mathrm{d}p^{i}}(p,\omega)\right]\\
&=\sum_{\gamma\in\Pi}J^{(|\gamma|)}\left(g_{1}(p,\omega)\right)\cdot\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{|\tau|}c_{i,|\tau|}(p-\omega)^{(\beta+i)}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{i}\nu}{\mathrm{d}p^{i}}(p,\omega)\right],\end{split}

where
\Pi

,

denotes derivative
|\gamma|
of

,
c_{|\tau|,|\tau|}=|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|
, and

(4.40)

c_{i,|\tau|}=|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\cdot\begin{pmatrix}|\tau|\\
i\end{pmatrix}\left(\prod_{l=0}^{|\tau|-1-i}(\beta-l)\right),

for
0\leq i\leq|\tau|-1
. On the third line of (

), we use the fact that

k=\sum_{\tau\in\gamma}|\tau|\implies(p-\omega)^{k}=\prod_{\tau\in\gamma}(p-\omega)^{|\tau|}.

In the exponent in the sum on the fourth line of (

),
\beta+i\geq 1/2,

since

, and

. This finishes the proof.

5.
Image reconstructions

In this section, we present image reconstructions from
\mathcal{E}_{j}f
data in
n=2
dimensions, where
f
is a simulated image phantom. To reconstruct
f
, we use algebraic methods. Specifically, let
A_{j}
denote the discretized form of
\mathcal{E}_{j}
, let
\mathbf{u}
be the discretized and vectorized form of
f
on a uniform grid, and let
\mathbf{b}
denote the measured data. Then, we aim to find

(5.1)

\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda G(\mathbf{x}),

where
G
is a regularization function (e.g., Total Variation (TV) or Tikhonov), and
\mathcal{X}
is our solution space (e.g., non-negative functions).

5.1.
Data simulation

We discretize
f
on an
m\times m
uniform grid, with
m
odd,

\left\{(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{1}=-m/2+\frac{m}{m-1}(i-1),x_{2}=-m/2+\frac{m}{m-1}(j-1),1\leq i,j\leq m-1\right\},

and
\mathcal{E}_{j}f(t,y_{1})
is sampled for
y_{1}\in[-m,m]
with
2m
steps at even intervals, and for
t=sp^{2}
, where
p\in\{1,\ldots\frac{m+1}{2}\}
, and
s=2
is fixed. We choose
s=2
so the ellipse foci lie on
\{x_{2}=0\}
, and this is motivated by applications in URT. For example, the ellipse foci could represent sound wave emitters and receivers.

We simulate data with noise in the following way

(5.2)

\mathbf{b}=A_{\epsilon}\mathbf{x}+\gamma\times\frac{\|A_{\epsilon}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{l}}\mathbf{\eta},

where
l
is the length of
A_{\epsilon}\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{\eta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a set of draws from a standard Gaussian, and
\gamma
is a parameter which controls the noise level.
A_{\epsilon}
is a perturbed
A
, which is used to avoid inverse crime. Specifically,
A_{\epsilon}
is generated by multiplying every non-zero element of
A
by
u\sim U(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)
, where
U(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)
is the uniform distribution on
[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]
. Thus, we perturb the weights of
A
uniformly and at random, and, on top of that, we add additional Gaussian noise to the perturbed data.

Throughout this section, we fix
m=257
,
\epsilon=0.05
, and we vary
\gamma\in[0.01,0.05]
, i.e., between
1\%
and
5\%
added Gaussian noise. Note, the
(p,y_{1})
sampling described above is sufficient to satisfy the conditions of Theorem
4.1
, and we can recover
f
, with compact support on
[-m/2,m/2]\times[0,m/2]
, uniquely from
\mathcal{E}_{j}f
.

Reconstruction methods

In this section, we discuss our methods. We consider the imaging methods listed below:

(1)

Conjugate Gradient Least Squares (CGLS) with Tikhonov regularization
[

(2)

[

G(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{\|\nabla\mathbf{x}\|^{2}_{2}+\beta^{2}},

where
\beta>0

\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m^{2}}

The objective (

CGLS with Tikhonov regularization, while fast and efficient, offers modest regularization, and is included in our simulations mainly to highlight some of the artifacts we expect to see if the solution is not sufficiently regularized. TV is included to show the effectiveness of a stronger regularizer in removing the artifacts.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented novel injectivity results and inversion methods for a class of generalized Abel operators. In Theorem
3.2
, we provided sufficient conditions on the Abel operator kernel for existence and uniqueness of solution in
L^{2}([a,b])
. In Theorem
4.1
, we applied our theory to ellipsoid and hyperboloid Radon transforms,
\mathcal{R}_{j}
, which have applications to URT when
j=0
. We proved injectivity of
\mathcal{R}_{j}
, and provided an inversion method based on Neumann series. Specifically, we showed, after Fourier transformation, that
\mathcal{R}_{j}
could be reduced to a set of generalized Abel operators. The operator kernels were then shown to satisfy the conditions of Theorem
3.2
to prove injectivity of
\mathcal{R}_{j}
. We generalized the results of Theorem
4.1
in section
4.2
, and showed that
f
could be recovered uniquely from its integrals over the surfaces of revolution of a more general set of continuous curves. In section
5
, we presented simulated reconstructions of image phantoms in two-dimensions from ellipse and hyperbola integral data. We compared Tikhonov and TV regularization methods. Strong artifacts were observed in the Tikhonov reconstructions due to data limitations, and Tikhonov regularization was not sufficient to suppress the noise. TV faired much better, and successfully suppressed the more significant artifacts observed in the Tikhonov reconstructions.

In discussion
4.4
, we outlined the smoothing properties of
\mathcal{R}_{j}
, and discussed the inversion instabilities. In further work, we aim to formalize these ideas and derive stability estimates on Sobolev scale.

\hat{f}(\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in S^{n-1}}f\left(p({\boldsymbol{\omega}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right)\mathrm{d}\Omega,

where
\omega_{n}
denotes the volume of
S^{n-1}
,
\mathrm{d}\Omega
is the standard measure on
S^{n-1}
, and
\mathbf{y}=2p{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
.
\hat{f}(\mathbf{y})
is the mean value of
f
over the sphere with center
\mathbf{y}/2
and radius
|\mathbf{y}|/2
. We use the notation of
[
9
]
in this appendix. In
[
9
]
, the authors present inversion formulae for
\hat{f}
, and investigate its applications to the Darboux equation.

After spherical harmonic decomposition of
\hat{f}
, in
[
9
, Theorem 1]
, the authors reduce
\hat{f}
to a set of Volterra equations

(A.2)

\hat{f}_{l}(s)=\left(\frac{2}{s}\right)^{n-1}\frac{\omega_{n-1}}{\omega_{n}C_{l}^{\gamma}(1)}\int_{0}^{s}C_{l}^{\gamma}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)f_{l}(r)r^{2\gamma}\left(1-\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{2}\right)^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}r,

where
\gamma=\frac{n-2}{2}
, and
C_{l}^{\gamma}
is a Gegenbauer polynomial of degree
l
. The equations (
A.2
) are to be solved for
f_{l}
, for every
l\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Equation (
A.2
) can be converted to a generalized Abel equation by multiplying both sides by
s^{2\gamma-1}

(A.3)

\begin{split}\frac{s^{n-1}}{\lambda_{n}}\hat{f}_{l}(s)&=\int_{0}^{s}\left(s-r\right)^{\alpha}\left[r^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\left(s+r\right)^{\alpha}C_{l}^{\gamma}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)\right]f_{l}(r)\mathrm{d}r\\
&=\int_{0}^{s}\left(s-r\right)^{\alpha}K_{l}(s,r)f_{l}(r)\mathrm{d}r\end{split}

where
\lambda_{n}=\frac{2\omega_{n-1}}{\omega_{n}C_{l}^{\gamma}(1)}\neq 0
, and
\alpha=(n-3)/2
. In
[
9
, Theorem 1]
, the authors solve (
A.2
) using a generalization of Cormack’s formula
[
10
]
, under the assumption that the
f_{l}
are smooth. We provide an
L^{2}
solution to (
A.2
) in the following theorem.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
f_{l}\in L^{2}([a,b])
, with
0<a<b<\infty
. Then (
A.2
) can solved uniquely for
f_{l}
if
\hat{f}_{l}
is known for
s\in[a,b]
.

Proof.

Let
T_{0}
be defined as in Theorem
3.1
. By (
A.3
), it is sufficient to show that
K_{l}
satisfies the conditions of Corollary
3.4
are satisfied. We have

K_{l}(s,s)=2^{\alpha}s^{2\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}C_{l}^{\gamma}(1)\neq 0,

for
s\in[a,b]
. Let

g_{1}(s,r)=r^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\left(s+r\right)^{\alpha},

and

g_{2}(s,r)=C_{l}^{\gamma}\left(\frac{r}{s}\right).

Then,
g_{1}\in C^{\infty}(T_{0})
. Further, since
g_{2}
is the composition of a polynomial and
r/s
, which is smooth on
T_{0}
,
g_{2}\in C^{\infty}(T_{0})
. Thus,
K_{l}\in C^{\infty}(T_{0})
, and the conditions of Corollary
3.4
are satisfied. This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark A.2
.

In
[
9
, Theorem 1]
, an inversion formula is provided for (
A.2
) for smooth functions, which cannot have singularities (edges). In many imaging applications, the
f
of interest have sharp edges, and thus a solution in
L^{2}
is desired. Theorem
A.1
provides such a solution. It is noted that equation
A.3
, given the smoothness of
K_{l}
, could also be solved for
L^{2}
functions using the arguments of
[
4
, pages 515 and 516]
. This analysis and Theorem
A.1
are included as it gives another concrete example, in addition to Theorem
4.1
and the generalizations of section
4.2
, of the applications of generalized Abel equations and Theorem
3.2
to Radon transforms.
