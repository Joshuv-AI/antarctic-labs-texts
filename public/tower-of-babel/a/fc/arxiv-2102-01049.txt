(Un-)bounded transition fronts for the parabolic Anderson model and the randomized F-KPP equation
Jiří ČernýAlexander Drewitz, Lars Schmitz

Abstract
We investigate the uniform boundedness of the fronts of the solutions to the randomized Fisher-KPP equation and to its linearization, the parabolic Anderson model. It has been known that for the standard (i.e. deterministic) Fisher-KPP equation, as well as for the special case of a randomized Fisher-KPP equation with so-called ignition type nonlinearity, one has a uniformly bounded (in time) transition front. Here, we show that this property of having a uniformly bounded transition front fails to hold for the general randomized Fisher-KPP equation. Nevertheless, we establish that this property does hold true for the parabolic Anderson model.

Introduction

We consider the random partial differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}w_{t}(t,x)&=\frac{1}{2}w_{xx}(t,x)+\xi(x,\omega)\,F(w(t,x)),\qquad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R},\ \omega\in\Omega,\\
w(0,\cdot)&=\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,0]}.\end{split}

(F-KPP)

In our specific setting,
(\xi(x))_{x\in\mathbb{R}}=(\xi(x,\omega))_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
,

\omega\in\Omega
, is a stochastic process on a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
fulfilling suitable mixing and sample path regularity
conditions (see Section
2
), and the non-linearity

is
generated by the probability generating function belonging to branching
Brownian motion, see condition (
PROB
) below
(
2.1
).

The investigation of (

) for the homogeneous case
\xi\equiv 1

has a long history, dating back to the seminal works of Fisher

[
Fis37
]
and Kolmogorov, Petrovskii and Piscunov

[
KPP37
]
. The equation has found a plethora of applications,
such as describing the dynamics of a randomly mating diploid population
in a one-dimensional habitat, or also to model flame propagation, see

[
AW75
]
.

It is well-known, see
[

, Theorem 14]
, that in the
homogeneous case
\xi\equiv 1
the solution

of (

)
converges to a

. More precisely, there
exists a function
{(0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto m(t)}
such that

\displaystyle w(t,m(t)+\cdot\,)\displaystyle\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{t\rightarrow\infty}g\quad\text{uniformly,}

(1.1)

for some function

with
g(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{x\rightarrow-\infty}0
and

g(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{x\rightarrow\infty}1
, and which is unique up to spatial translations.
In this context, the function

is usually referred to as the

position of the wave
. The convergence in

(
F-KPP
) for the case
\xi\equiv 1
is bounded, i.e. for every

\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
there exist

,
such that for all

\inf_{x\leq\underline{x}}\,w(t,x+m(t))\geq 1-\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{x\geq\overline{x}}\,w(t,x+m(t))\leq\varepsilon.

Therefore, a question arising naturally in our context is whether a

) is observed in the
setting of a random nonlinearity in (
F-KPP
) as well. It turns out
that in the investigation of this question, for a variety of reasons the
linearization of (
F-KPP

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{t}(t,x)&=\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}(t,x)+\xi(x,\omega)\,u(t,x),\qquad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R},\ \omega\in\Omega,\\
u(0,x)&=u_{0}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\end{split}

plays an important role as well.

2
Model and results

We will assume
\xi=(\xi(x))_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
to be a stochastic process
on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
having Hölder continuous
paths. I.e., there exists
\alpha=\alpha(\xi)>0
and

, such that

\left|\xi(x)-\xi(y)\right|\leq C\,|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

(HÖL)

We will consider throughout the standard model of
\Omega
being the
space of Hölder continuous functions and
\mathcal{F}
to be the
\sigma
-algebra
generated by point evaluations.
Furthermore, we assume the following

•

\xi
is
uniformly bounded away from
0

\infty

0<\mathsf{ei}:=\essinf_{\omega}\xi(x,\omega)<\esssup_{\omega}\xi(x,\omega)=:\mathsf{es}<\infty\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R};

(BDD)

•

\xi
is

: For every

(\xi(x))_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\text{ and }(\xi(x+h))_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\text{ have the same distribution;}

(STAT)

•

\xi
fulfills a
\psi
-mixing
condition: Let

\mathcal{F}_{x}:=\sigma(\xi(z):z\leq x)
and
\mathcal{F}^{y}:=\sigma(\xi(z):z\geq y)
,

x,y\in\mathbb{R},
and assume that there exists a continuous,
non-increasing function
\psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, such that for

\mathcal{F}_{j}
-measurable

and

\mathcal{F}^{k}
-measurable

we have

\begin{split}\big|\mathbb{E}\big[X-\mathbb{E}[X]\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{k}\big]\big|&\leq\mathbb{E}[|X|]\cdot\psi(k-j),\\
\big|\mathbb{E}\big[Y-\mathbb{E}[Y]\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{j}\big]\big|&\leq\mathbb{E}[|Y|]\cdot\psi({k-j}),\quad\text{and}\\
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}&\psi(k)<\infty.\end{split}

(MIX)

Note that (
MIX
) implies the ergodicity of
\xi
with respect to
the shift operator
\theta_{y}
acting on
\Omega
via

\xi(\cdot)\circ\theta_{y}=\xi(\cdot+y)
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

In order to specify the initial conditions for (
PAM
) under
consideration, for
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
and
C^{\prime}>1
consider the condition

\begin{split}&\delta^{\prime}\mathds{1}_{[-\delta^{\prime},0]}\leq u_{0}\leq C^{\prime}\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,0]},\end{split}

(PAM-INI)

and we define the class of initial conditions to (
PAM
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PAM}}:=\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PAM}}(\delta^{\prime},C^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\big\{u_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)\text{ measurable}:\ u_{0}\text{ fulfills \eqref{eq:PAM_initial} for }\delta^{\prime}\text{ and }C^{\prime}\big\}.

In order to describe the admissible non-linearities for (
F-KPP
),
let
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an arbitrary sequence of reals in
[0,1]
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}=1,\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp_{k}=2,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2}p_{k}=:m_{2}<\infty.

Then define
F:\ [0,1]\to[0,1]

F(u):=(1-u)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}(1-u)^{k},\quad u\in[0,1].

(PROB)

In passing, we note that

. The reason for considering this type
of non-linearity is its suitability for being investigated using
techniques from branching processes. In particular, the solutions to
(
F-KPP
) can then be expressed as a functionals of a branching
Brownian motion, see Proposition
5.1
.

On top of the above, we need a further technical condition to be
fulfilled. In order to be able to formulate it, note that
Lemma
A.2
states the existence of a critical velocity

v_{c}\geq 0
and Proposition
A.3
that of another
velocity
v_{0}>0;
here, the former pertains to the characteristics of
the Lyapunov exponent while, under suitable assumptions, the latter
essentially is the speed of the front of the solutions to (
PAM
)
and (
F-KPP
). In order for our approach to be effective, we need to
perform a change of measure that requires

v_{0}>v_{c}

(VEL)

to be fulfilled.
For the time being, we content ourselves with referring to Section
2.1
, where we argue that there do exist potentials fulfilling (
VEL
), alongside all other conditions required for our results to hold. For further details and a more profound discussion of condition (
VEL
), as well as for examples of potentials which do or do not entail (
VEL
) to be satisfied, we refer to
[
DS21
]
.

In order to investigate the position of the front, we introduce for

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
M>0
and
t\geq 0
the quantities

\begin{split}m^{\varepsilon}(t)&:=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,w(t,x)\geq\varepsilon\},\\
m^{\varepsilon,-}(t)&:=\inf\{x\geq 0\,:\,w(t,x)\leq\varepsilon\},\\
\overline{m}^{M}(t)&:=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,u(t,x)\geq M\},\\
\overline{m}^{M,-}(t)&:=\inf\{x\geq 0\,:\,u(t,x)\leq M\}.\end{split}

(2.2)

Note that all these quantities are random variables (and their
distributions depend on the initial conditions of the respective
equations).

Definition 2.1
.

The solution to (
F-KPP
) is said to have a
uniformly
bounded transition front
if for each
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})

there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\infty)
such that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
for all
t
large enough we have

m^{\varepsilon}(t)-m^{1-\varepsilon,-}(t)\leq C_{\varepsilon}.

The solution to (
PAM
) is said to have a
uniformly
bounded transition front
if for all
\varepsilon,M\in(0,\infty)

with
\varepsilon<M
, there exists a constant

C_{\varepsilon,M}\in(0,\infty)
such that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., for all
t

large enough,

\overline{m}^{\varepsilon}(t)-\overline{m}^{M,-}(t)\leq C_{\varepsilon,M}.

(2.3)

We can now state our two main results. The first one is for the solution
to (
PAM
) and states that its transition front stays bounded
uniformly in time.

Theorem 2.2
.

If (
HÖL
),
(
BDD
), (
STAT
), (
MIX
) and (
VEL
) are fulfilled, the solution to (
PAM
) has a
uniformly bounded transition front. Furthermore, for
\delta^{\prime},C^{\prime}>0

fixed, the corresponding constant
C_{\varepsilon,M}
in
(
2.3
) is independent of

u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PAM}}(\delta^{\prime},C^{\prime})
.

Our second, and more important, main result states that an analogous
statement is in general
not
true for the solution to (
F-KPP
).

Theorem 2.3
.

There exist potentials
\xi
fulfilling (
HÖL
),
(
BDD
), (
STAT
) and (
MIX
) such that the
transition front of the solution to (
F-KPP
) is not uniformly
bounded in time. More precisely, such
\xi
can be chosen so that for
any
\delta\in(0,1)
and any
\varepsilon>0
we find a sequence

(x_{n},t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
as well as a function

\varphi\in\Theta(\ln n)
such that

(a)

x_{n},t_{n}\to\infty
as

, and
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Theta(n)
,

(b)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

This means that, at least along a subsequence of times, the interval of

w\approx 0
) to locally stable (

), grows at least

While the previous result will be derived using probabilistic techniques,
we will enhance it employing analytic techniques to show that the

2.3
is true even for some “negative

\varepsilon
”.
In particular, this entails the non-monotonicity of the solution in space.

Theorem 2.4
.

There exist potentials
\xi
fulfilling (
HÖL
),
(
BDD
), (
STAT
) and (
MIX
), some
\varepsilon>0

small enough, and sequences
(t_{n}^{\prime})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,

(l_{n}^{\prime})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(r_{n}^{\prime})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

[0,\infty)

l_{n}^{\prime}<r_{n}^{\prime}

,
r_{n}-l_{n}\in\Theta(\ln n)

. In particular, it might seem surprising given

2.1
Discussion and previous results

As already explained in the Introduction, the homogeneous case of

]

Freidlin and Gärtner, see e.g.
[

]
as well as

3.1
below), which
characterizes the solution to the linearization (
PAM
), also
played an important role in the derivation.

In the setting described in the Introduction, second order corrections to
the position
m^{\varepsilon}(t)
of the front are obtained in

[
DS21
]
, where it has been shown that the suitably centered and
rescaled front fulfills an invariance principle. Again, the proof takes
advantage of analyzing (
PAM
) first. Let us note here that in

[
Nol11b
]
, a corresponding invariance principle has been derived for
non-linearities that are either ignition type or bistable; note however,
that – as will be explained below – on a logarithmic in time scale
these fronts behave quite differently from the fronts to (
F-KPP
)
in our context. For a different and due technical reasons restricted set
of initial conditions, Nolen
[
Nol11a
]
has derived a central limit
theorem for the position of the front of the solution to (
F-KPP
)
by analytic means. The initial condition

of
[
Nol11a
]
is
required to depend on the randomness of the environment.

When it comes to the boundedness of transition fronts, Nolen and Ryzhik

[
NR09
]
consider the setting of a stationary, ergodic and bounded

\xi.
The nonlinearity
F
is assumed to be of ignition-type. I.e.,
there exists
\theta\in(0,1)
such that

F(w)=0\text{ for all }w\notin(\theta_{0},1),\ F(w)>0\text{ for all }w\in(\theta_{0},1),\text{ and }F^{\prime}(1)<0.

(2.5)

They find that the solution to (
F-KPP
) has a uniformly bounded
transition front, see
[
NR09
, Proposition 2.3]
. Our main result

2.3
entails that condition
(
2.5
) is crucial here, since otherwise one cannot
expect uniformly bounded transition fronts.

Also note that in

[
DS21
, Theorem
]

it has been shown that in the setting of the
current article, the front of the solution to
(
F-KPP
) lags behind the front of the solution to (

)
at most logarithmically in

. More precisely, for

\varepsilon\in(0,1),

\overline{m}^{\varepsilon}(t)-m^{\varepsilon}(t)\in O(\ln t),\quad t\to\infty.

Therefore, it immediately arises the question whether this upper

provides the following
partial affirmative answer: There exists an increasing sequence
(t_{n})

of times with
t_{n}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}=\infty
and a sequence
(x_{n})
of reals such
that
\overline{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}(t_{n})-x_{n}\geq c_{0}\log t_{n}
such that for all

w(t_{n},x_{n})<\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{ and (by definition) }\quad u(t_{n},\overline{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}(t_{n}))=\frac{1}{2}.

As in the homogeneous context, there are profound and interesting links
to branching processes (in random environment). In
[
ČD20
]
, in the
setting of discrete space, invariance principles have been derived for
the position of the front of the PAM as well as the
position of the maximum of BRWRE. Furthermore, it has been shown that the
distance between these two quantities is in

In this context, a subtle but important difference to the homogeneous

) and the maximum of

below for the precise definition) do exhibit a slightly

]

for the law of the maximum of branching random walk in random environment

]
to the setting of

), nor can we directly obtain a respective non-tightness

).
Furthermore, it is trivial that the distribution function

of the maximum of a BBMRE at time

which is the solution to (

,
is non-increasing in space. This again is in stark contrast to

, which states that this is not the case

As already alluded to above, Theorem

as well as Theorems

) behaves

). In this

requires condition

) to be fulfilled, while the potential

is constructed in

) from the sole assumption

(

While from a
PDE point of view we lack the experience as well as a good enough control
of the fronts that would enable us to explain this phenomenon, it becomes
more tractable from a probabilistic point of view. Indeed, we will see

, that the solution to (
PAM
) can
be represented in terms of expectations of a Brownian motion in random

Here,

On the other hand, regarding the solution to (

An important tool for the investigation of the solutions to (

3.2
Branching Brownian motion in random environment

A key tool for proving Theorems

and
2.4
is the correspondence between the solution to
(

) and branching Brownian motion in random environment, cf. Proposition
5.1
below. Branching Brownian motion in random
environment
\xi
(BBMRE) started at
x\in\mathbb{R}
is defined as
follows: Conditionally on the realization of
\xi
, we place one
particle at
x
at time
0
. As time evolves, all particles move
independently according to standard Brownian motion. In addition, and
independently of everything else, while at
y
, a particle splits at rate

\xi(y)
.
Once a particle splits, this particle is removed and, randomly and
independently from everything else with probability
p_{k}
, replaced by
k

new particles that are put at the position
y
of the removed particle.
These
k
new particles evolve independently according to the same
diffusion-branching mechanism as the remaining particles. This defines

branching Brownian motion in the branching environment

\xi
with
offspring distribution
(p_{k})
. For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\xi
,

\texttt{E}_{x}^{\xi}
denotes the corresponding expectation of the probability
measure
\mathtt{P}_{x}^{\xi}
of a BBMRE, starting in
x
.

If the respective BBMRE is evident from the context, we
use
N(t)
to denote the set of particles alive at time
t
in this
BBMRE. For any particle
Y\in N(t)
, we denote by
(Y_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}

the trajectory of itself and its ancestors up to time
t
. We will also
call
(Y_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
the
genealogy
of
Y
. For
t\geq 0
and

x\in\mathbb{R}
, we define

\begin{split}N^{\geq}(t,x):=\{Y\in N(t)\,:Y_{t}\geq x\}\quad\text{ and }\quad N^{\leq}(t,x):=\{Y\in N(t)\,:Y_{t}\leq x\}\end{split}

(3.3)

as the number of particles in the process at time
t
which are located
to the right or to the left of
x
. Furthermore, in a slight abuse of
notation, we also use
N
to denote an entire BBMRE process.

To complete the list of notation, for a stochastic process

X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and some Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R},
we denote

H_{B}(X):=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}\in B\}
and set
H_{x}(X):=H_{\{x\}}(X)
,

x\in\mathbb{R}
. For a particle
Y\in N(t)
of a BBMRE, we set

H_{B}(Y)=\inf\{s\in[0,t]:Y_{s}\in B\}
, where
(Y_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is the
genealogy of
Y
and as usual
\inf\emptyset=\infty.

5
Unbounded transition front for randomized F-KPP equation

In this section we show our main results about the transition front for
the solution to (
F-KPP
), Theorems
2.3

and
2.4
.
The proofs are based on the following branching process representation of
the solution.

Proposition 5.1

(
[
DS21
, Proposition
]
)
.

Let
\xi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a non-negative bounded function
satisfying (
HÖL
),
F
as in (
PROB
),
and let
f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a function which can be
pointwise approximated by an increasing sequence of continuous functions. Then the function

w(t,x):=1-{\tt E}^{\xi}_{x}\Big[\prod_{Y\in N(t)}f(Y_{t})\Big]

solves the equation

w_{t}=\frac{1}{2}w_{xx}+\xi(x)F(w)

with initial condition
w(0,\cdot)=1-f
.
In particular,

w(t,x)=\mathtt{P}^{\xi}_{x}(N^{\leq}(t,0)\neq\emptyset)

(5.1)

solves this
equation with

f=\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}
, i.e.
w(0,\cdot)=\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,0]}
.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that Proposition
5.1
slightly differs from the
usual McKean representation in homogeneous branching environment.
More precisely, for
\xi\equiv c
being a constant function and

w(0,\cdot)=\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,0]}
, the canonical representation is given by

w(t,x)=\mathtt{P}^{c}_{0}(N^{\geq}(t,x)\neq\emptyset)
. This representation
follows from Proposition
5.1
using the symmetry

\mathtt{P}^{c}_{x}(N^{\leq}(t,0)\neq\emptyset)=\mathtt{P}^{c}_{0}(N^{\geq}(t,x)\neq\emptyset)

which is a consequence of the reflection symmetry of the Brownian
motion and the homogeneity of the environment. However, this identity
fails to hold if
\xi
is non-homogeneous.

5.1
The potential

We start the proof of Theorem
2.3
by constructing a
suitable potential
\xi
, for which we then show the unboundedness of
the transition front of the solution to (

). We fix two
positive finite constants
\mathsf{es}
and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{es}}{\mathsf{ei}}>2.

(5.2)

We further let

Let furthermore

Observe that the map

Lemma 5.3
.

There is a constant

such that

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. there exists a (random) increasing sequence

The proof is an easy application of the Borel-Cantelli lemma.

) and
(

\{Y_{t}:Y\in\mathtt{RM}(t)\}\subset(m,R)
. In particular, in
combination with (

is always larger at the position of an r-mirrored particle than at the
position of the corresponding l-mirrored particle:

If
Y\in\LM(t)
and
Y^{\prime}=\mu_{t}(Y)
, then

\xi(Y_{t})\leq\xi(Y^{\prime}_{t})
.

(5.11)

We can now describe the dynamics of
N_{l}
,
N_{r}
and of the types under
the coupling
\mathtt{Q}_{l,r}^{\xi}
. At time

, there is one (l-mirrored)
particle at position

in

and one (r-mirrored) particle at

this determines the bijection
\mu_{0}
uniquely.
Every particle in
N_{l}
(resp.
N_{r}
) performs Brownian motion,
independently of the other particles in
N_{l}
(resp.
N_{r}
). The
corresponding mirrored and coupled particles are required to satisfy

) and (

) respectively, which is
possible, since the law of Brownian motion is invariant by reflection;
besides these two conditions the motion of particles in
N_{l}
is
independent of the motion of particles in
N_{r}
.

(a)

Y

(resp.

(resp. r-coupled particle,

(resp.

(resp.

At time

At time

. It is replaced by
k
new particles, with

p_{k}
, independently of everything else. The type of

branches with rate
\xi(Y^{\prime}_{t})
at time

, as it should.

(a)

If an l-mirrored particle hits

, that is

and

, then, by consequence of

(b)

If an l-mirrored particle

(c)

If an l-mirrored particle hits

and

To show that the coupling succeeds, i.e. that (

)
holds, it is sufficient to show that with probability at least

there are no l-mirrored and bad particles after time

.
In this vein, we define two good events:

i.e., on

no particle from

enters

before time
t
, and

\mathcal{G}_{2}(t):=\big\{N_{r}^{\leq}(t,L)\neq\emptyset\big\};

(5.13)

i.e., there is a (necessarily free, if
\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)
occurs as
well) particle to the left of
L
at time
t
. We now need the
following lemma which ensures that we can find
t
such that those
events are typical.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
t^{\prime}<1
such that for all
n

large enough, with
t=t^{\prime}\varphi(n)/\sqrt{2\mathsf{ei}}
,

\mathtt{Q}_{l,r}^{\xi}\big(\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)\cap\mathcal{G}_{2}(t)\big)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(5.14)

We postpone the proof of this lemma and complete the proof of
Proposition
5.4
first. Let
t
be as in
Lemma
5.5
. We claim that

\{N_{l}(t)\subset N_{r}(t)\}\supset\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)\cap\mathcal{G}_{2}(t).

(5.15)

If we show this, then the claim of Proposition
5.4

follows with
\Cr{const_{c}oupling}=t/\ln n=t^{\prime}c_{0}/\sqrt{2\mathsf{ei}}
.

To prove (
5.15
), recall first that bad particles can only be created
if an l-mirrored particle hits
L
. As a consequence,

on
\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)
there cannot be any bad particles at time
t
.

(5.16)

Next, we show that

on

\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)\cap\mathcal{G}_{2}(t)
there are no l-mirrored
particles at time
t

(5.17)

either. To this end define

\mathcal{R}(t)=\inf\{Y^{\prime}_{t}:Y\in\Free(t)\}
to be the position of the
leftmost free particle, and
\mathcal{L}(t)=\sup\{Y_{t}:Y\in\LM(t)\}
to be
the position of the rightmost l-mirrored particle, with the convention

\inf\emptyset=+\infty
,
\sup\emptyset=-\infty;
in the remaining
cases, a.s., the infimum and supremum are attained, since
\Free(t)

and
\LM(t)
are a.s. finite sets). Let

\tau:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\mathcal{L}(t)>\mathcal{R}(t)\}.

We claim that
\tau=\infty
,
\mathtt{Q}_{l,r}^{\xi}
-a.s. Indeed, we first
note that
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
are right-continuous. In addition, the
only jumps that
\mathcal{L}
has are downward jumps. They occur a.s. iff
the rightmost l-mirrored particle changes its type due to (A)–(C). (If
one of (A)–(C) occurs, then a.s. there is only one l-mirrored
particle at position
\mathcal{L}(t)
. At branching events,
\mathcal{L}
is
unchanged, as l-mirrored particles are created only at positions
where l-mirrored particles are already present, see (a)). Similarly,
with the exception of the first jump from
+\infty
, the only jumps
that the function
\mathcal{R}
has are upwards jumps, occurring a.s. iff the
leftmost free particle becomes r-coupled due to (B). Therefore, it
follows that a.s.
\tau\geq\inf\{t\geq 0:\mathcal{L}(t)=\mathcal{R}(t)\}
.
However, the event
\{\exists t\in[0,\infty):\mathcal{L}(t)=\mathcal{R}(t)\}

cannot occur by the construction of the coupling, since if an
l-mirrored and a free particle meet, then at this instant they become
l-/r-coupled immediately. Hence,
\tau=\infty
almost surely, as claimed.

Assume now that
\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)\cap\mathcal{G}_{2}(t)
occurs. At time

t
, there is thus a particle from
N_{r}
and no particle from
N_{l}
to
the left of
L
. From the construction, this particle is neither
r-coupled (since on
\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)
there is no corresponding
l-coupled particle there), nor r-mirrored (as all r-mirrored
particles are always in
(m,R)
). Therefore, it must be free
and thus
\mathcal{R}(t)<L
. Since
\tau=\infty
a.s.,

\mathcal{L}(t)<L
as well. However, by construction, l-mirrored
particles are always located in
(L,m)
, and thus

\mathcal{L}(t)<L
implies
\mathcal{L}(t)=-\infty
, that is

\LM(t)=\emptyset,
establishing (
5.17
).

All in all, from the above it follows that on

\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)\cap\mathcal{G}_{2}(t),
(
5.16
) as well as
(
5.17
) hold true, i.e., there do not exist any
l-mirrored or bad particles at time
t
. Hence, all particles in

N_{l}(t)
are necessarily l-coupled, which proves (
5.15
).
This completes the proof of Proposition
5.4
.
∎

.

Proof of Lemma
5.5
.

We first estimate the probability of
\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)
as a function of

t\in[0,\varphi(n)/\sqrt{2\mathsf{ei}}]
. To this end we write
\mathcal{N}(t)
for the
number of particles from
N_{l}
that hit
L
before
t;
here, we only count
the first hit of
L
by any particle. That is, we disregard possible
successive hits of
L
by the same particle, and also the fact that this
particle could branch between the hitting of
L
and the time
t
, and
thus produce more particles at time
t
that hit
L
. The expectation
of
\mathcal{N}(t)
can be written as

\mathtt{E}_{l}^{\xi}[\mathcal{N}(t)]=E_{l}\Big[e^{\int_{0}^{H_{L}}\xi(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s};H_{L}<t\Big]\leq E_{l}\Big[e^{\int_{0}^{H_{L}}\tilde{\xi}(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s};H_{L}<t\Big],

(5.18)

where the potential
\tilde{\xi}
is given by
\tilde{\xi}(x)=\mathsf{es}
if

x\geq x_{n}
, and
\tilde{\xi}(x)=\mathsf{ei}
if
x<x_{n}
. To estimate the
right-hand side, note that there are two possible scenarios for a
particle to hit
L
. Either, it stays all the time in the interval

(L,x_{n})
where the potential equals
\mathsf{ei}
and hits
L
(i.e., it
displaces by altogether at least
l-L\geq(1-5\delta_{1})\varphi(n)
). Or, it
spends some
s
units of time in the interval
[x_{n},\infty)
, where the
potential is
\mathsf{es}
, but then it should displace by at least

(x_{n}-l)+(x_{n}-L)\geq(1+4\delta_{1})\varphi(n)
in
t-s
units of
time. Ignoring prefactors which are sub-exponential in

\varphi(n)
and using standard Gaussian tail bounds, we thus arrive at the
following upper bound:

\begin{split}\mathtt{E}_{l}^{\xi}[\mathcal{N}(t)]&\lesssim\exp\Big\{t\mathsf{ei}-\frac{(1-5\delta_{1})^{2}\varphi(n)^{2}}{2t}\Big\}+\sup_{s\leq t}\exp\Big\{(t-s)\mathsf{ei}+s\mathsf{es}-\frac{(1+4\delta_{1})^{2}\varphi(n)^{2}}{2(t-s)}\Big\}\\
&=\exp\Big\{\sigma(n)\Big(t^{\prime}-\frac{(1-5\delta_{1})^{2}}{t^{\prime}}\Big)\Big\}+\sup_{s^{\prime}<t^{\prime}}\exp\Big\{\sigma(n)\Big(t^{\prime}+s^{\prime}\,\frac{\mathsf{es}-\mathsf{ei}}{\mathsf{ei}}-\frac{(1+4\delta_{1})^{2}}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}\Big)\Big\},\end{split}

(5.19)

where we introduced

\sigma(n)=\varphi(n)\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{ei}}{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad t^{\prime}=\frac{t\,\mathsf{ei}}{\sigma(n)}

(5.20)

in order to put the various terms on the same scale. Using Markov’s
inequality, to show that
\mathtt{P}_{l}^{\xi}(\mathcal{G}_{1}(t)^{c})\to 0,
it is
sufficient to show that both summands on the right-hand side of
(
5.19
) tend to
0.
For this to be the case for the first one,
it is sufficient to require

t^{\prime}<(1-5\delta_{1}).

(5.21)

Before dealing with the second term (which we will do below
(
5.24
)), we turn our attention to the event
\mathcal{G}_{2}(t)
.

To control the probability of the event
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, we need two claims.

Claim 5.6
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
t_{0}<\infty
such that for all
n
large enough,

\mathtt{P}_{r}^{\xi}\Big(\big|\big\{Y\in N_{r}(t):Y_{t}\in[x_{n}+\delta_{1}\varphi(n),x_{n}+2\delta_{1}\varphi(n)]\big\}\big|\geq e^{(1-\delta_{2})\mathsf{es}\,t}\Big)\geq 1-\varepsilon/2,\qquad\text{for all $t\geq t_{0}$.}

(5.22)

In order not to hinder the flow of reading, we postpone the proof
of Claim
5.6
to the end of the proof of

5.5

Claim 5.7
.

Let
t=t^{\prime}\varphi(n)/\sqrt{2\mathsf{ei}}

t^{\prime}<1

. Then for

Proof.

Obviously

.
Moreover, by the large deviation lower bound from
[
CR88

, for
every

and
\eta>0
, if

is sufficiently large,
then

\mathtt{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{ei}}(N^{\leq}(t,-vt)\neq\emptyset)\geq\exp\{t(\mathsf{ei}-v^{2}/2-\eta)\}.

Using this estimate with

,
and by rewriting it using the notation introduced in

∎

Using these two claims, we have that for any

The second summand on the right-hand side converges to
0
as

if

\begin{split}&\exp\Big\{\sigma(n)\Big(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}-\frac{(1+2\delta_{1})^{2}}{t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}}-\eta\Big)\Big\}\cdot\exp\{(1-\delta_{2})\mathsf{es}\,s\}\\

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\Big[E_{1}[H_{0}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{B_{r}\in[0,2]\,\forall r\in[0,H_{0}]\}}]\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{\xi(x)=\mathsf{es}\,\forall x\in[0,2]\}}\Big]\geq c>0,

which finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎
