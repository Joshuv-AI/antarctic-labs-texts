Thermal Area Law in Long-Range Interacting Systems
Donghoon Kim, Tomotaka Kuwahara, Keiji Saito

Abstract
The area law of the bipartite information measure characterizes one of the most fundamental aspects of quantum many-body physics. In thermal equilibrium, the area law for the mutual information universally holds at arbitrary temperatures as long as the systems have short-range interactions. In systems with power-law decaying interactions, r − α r^{-\alpha} ( r r : distance), conditions for the thermal area law are elusive. In this work, we aim to clarify the optimal condition α > α c \alpha>\alpha_{c} such that the thermal area law universally holds. A standard approach to considering the conditions is to focus on the magnitude of the boundary interaction between two subsystems. However, we find here that the thermal area law is more robust than this conventional argument suggests. We show the optimal threshold for the thermal area law by α c = ( D + 1 ) / 2 \alpha_{c}=(D+1)/2 ( D D : the spatial dimension of the lattice), assuming a power-law decay of the clustering for the bipartite correlations. Remarkably, this condition encompasses even the thermodynamically unstable regimes α < D \alpha<D . We verify this condition numerically, finding that it is qualitatively accurate for both integrable and nonintegrable systems. Unconditional proof of the thermal area law is possible by developing the power-law clustering theorem for α > D \alpha>D above a threshold temperature. Furthermore, the numerical calculation for the logarithmic negativity shows that the same criterion α > ( D + 1 ) / 2 \alpha>(D+1)/2 applies to the thermal area law for quantum entanglement.



Introduction.—

Quantum correlation and entanglement play pivotal roles in understanding quantum many-body systems from an information-theoretic standpoint.
They help in identifying the exotic quantum phases
Kitaev and Preskill 2006
;
Levin and Wen 2006
;
Chen et al. 2010
and serve as crucial resources in quantum information processing
Horodecki et al. 2009
. One hallmark of quantum many-body systems is the area law for the ground state
Eisert et al. 2010
, which posits that the correlation and entanglement between two subsystems are constrained by the surface area of their interface. This principle has undergone extensive verification through both analytical and numerical approaches in a wide array of scenarios
Vidal et al. 2003
;
Audenaert et al. 2002
;
Plenio et al. 2005
;
Gioev and Klich 2006
;
Hastings 2007
;
Masanes 2009
;
Arad et al. 2012
;
Arad et al. 2013
;
Brandão and Horodecki 2013
;
Kuwahara and Saito 2020a
;
Anshu et al. 2022
. The area law not only elucidates the inherent complexities of quantum systems but also significantly enhances their simulatability using classical computers
Landau et al. 2015
;
Arad et al. 2017
.

The quantum Gibbs state describing thermal equilibrium obeys an analogous principle, known as the thermal area law, as long as the systems have short-range interactions
Wolf et al. 2008
;
Sherman et al. 2016
;
Berta et al. 2018
;
Kuwahara et al. 2021
;
Lemm and Siebert 2023
.
The thermal area law for the mutual information is written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(A:B)=\mathcal{O}(\beta|\partial A|),

(1)

where
\mathcal{I}
represents the mutual information of the Gibbs state between two subsystems
A
and
B
at inverse temperature
\beta
.
Here,
|\partial A|
denotes the size of the boundary between
A
and
B
.
The thermal area law is not only relevant to the mutual information but also extends to a wider range of bipartite information measures
Sherman et al. 2016
;
Berta et al. 2018
;
Kuwahara et al. 2021
. We note that a recent study has further clarified that genuine quantum correlations for the Gibbs state also cannot be as large as the system size for short-range interacting systems
Kuwahara and Saito 2022
.

In contrast to short-range interactions, the general aspects of locality in long-range interacting systems remain less understood. Long-range interactions here refer to interactions that decay as
r^{-\alpha}
with distance
r

Defenu et al. 2023
, which are ubiquitous in nature such as in atomic, molecular, and optical systems
Yan et al. 2013
;
Jurcevic et al. 2014
;
Richerme et al. 2014
;
Islam et al. 2013
;
Bernien et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2017
.
These interactions lead to nontrivial phenomena absent in short-range systems
Koffel et al. 2012
;
Vodola et al. 2014
;
Gong et al. 2016
;
Kozin and Kyriienko 2019
;
Kuwahara and Saito 2020b
;
Tran et al. 2021a
;
Tran et al. 2021b
;
Minato et al. 2022
;
Tamaki and Saito 2020
.
Key unresolved issues include (i) identifying the critical threshold
\alpha_{\rm c}
above which
any systems have the thermal area law, (ii) understanding the underlying physics, and (iii) distinguishing between classical and quantum correlations.
This paper addresses these questions as fundamental physics.
In a practical aspect, this direction may lead to finding an efficiency-guaranteed algorithm for simulating the long-range quantum Gibbs state.
The mutual information is bounded by the interaction Hamiltonian as shown in Ref.
Wolf et al. 2008
. A simple argument on the operator norm
Kuwahara and Saito 2020a
;
Minato et al. 2022
leads to that the thermal area law (
1
) still remains valid for the condition
\alpha>D+1
. At first glance, this may lead to an intuition that the optimal condition for the thermal area law is given by
\alpha>D+1
(or
\alpha_{c}=D+1
).
Indeed, the threshold given by the finite boundary interaction recurs across various contexts in physics, indicating that physical properties undergo qualitative changes at this threshold in various many-body systems
Dyson 1969
;
Kosterlitz 1976
;
Araki 1975
;
Kishimoto 1976
;
Gong et al. 2017
;
Minato et al. 2022
.

However, in this work, we discover that the underlying physics of the thermal area law is influenced by a different characteristic:
the clustering property
, which is expected to remain robust in any noncritical phases.
Note that the clustering property has been widely adopted as a basic and natural assumption in discussing various quantum properties such as entanglement area law
Brandão and Horodecki 2013
, quantum Gibbs sampling
Kastoryano and Brandão 2016
;
Brandão and Kastoryano 2019
, the eigenstate thermalization
Brandao and Cramer 2015
;
Kuwahara and Saito 2020c
, etc.
Consequently, we show that the thermal area law for the mutual information possesses a greater resilience than traditionally anticipated. Utilizing the power-law clustering properties in bipartite correlations, we analytically find that the sufficient condition for the thermal area law in the mutual information is given by
\alpha>(D+1)/2
. Regarding the critical threshold
\alpha_{c}
above which any systems satisfy the thermal area law, our numerical analysis concludes that
\alpha_{c}=(D+1)/2
, since below
\alpha_{c}
we provide explicit examples violating the area law.
Remarkably, this condition encompasses
\alpha<D
, where the thermodynamic extensivity is violated
Defenu et al. 2023
. In this regime, most analytical techniques break down, and universal behaviors remain largely unexplored.
While the numerical investigation suggests that the clustering property robustly holds even for thermodynamic nonextensive regimes, we demonstrate that the power-law clustering theorem can be rigorously derived for
\alpha>D
by identifying a suitable temperature regime. Consequently, the thermal area law unconditionally holds for
\alpha>D
. Our key results are depicted schematically in Fig.
1
.

In addition, we investigate whether differences exist between quantum and classical correlations in the context of the thermal area law. By employing the logarithmic negativity proposed by Shapourian, Shiozaki, and Ryu for bilinear fermionic systems
Shapourian et al. 2017
, we demonstrate that this criterion equally governs the thermal area law for quantum entanglement.

Setup.—

We consider a quantum system with
N
qudits or fermions on the
D
-dimensional lattice. Let
\Lambda
be the set of all sites on the lattice of
N

in the

\displaystyle H=\sum_{Z:|Z|\leq k}h_{Z}\,,

(2)

where

is the local Hamiltonian on a subset

with site number
|Z|
.
We consider the long-range interactions satisfying the following condition:

\displaystyle J_{i,i^{\prime}}:=\sum_{Z:Z\ni\{i,i^{\prime}\}}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq{g\over(1+d_{i,i^{\prime}})^{\alpha}}\,.

(3)

Here,
J_{i,i^{\prime}}
is the maximum norm of the local Hamiltonian including the sites
i
and
i^{\prime}
, and
g
is some constant. The symbol
d_{i,i^{\prime}}
stands for the distance between the sites
i
and
i^{\prime}
. The parameter
\alpha
is the index of power-law decay in the interaction. We will provide examples of the Hamiltonian in Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
) later. Note that the Hamiltonian does not contain the Kac factor for the thermodynamically nonextensive regime
\alpha<D
, since such a factor does not exist in realistic situations

;
Neyenhuis et al. 2017
.

The quantum Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
at inverse temperature
\beta
is defined as
\rho_{\beta}:=e^{-\beta H}/{\rm tr}(e^{-\beta H})
. The mutual information of
\rho_{\beta}
between two regimes
A
and
B
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B):=S(\rho_{\beta}^{A})+S(\rho_{\beta}^{B})-S(\rho_{\beta}^{AB}),

(4)

where
\rho_{\beta}^{X}
is the reduced density matrix of
\rho_{\beta}
on the subset
X\subseteq\Lambda
, and
S(\rho_{\beta}^{X}):=-{\rm tr}(\rho_{\beta}^{X}\log\rho_{\beta}^{X})
is the von Neumann entropy.
We define the correlation function of two observables
O_{A}
and
O_{B}
as

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{A},O_{B}):={\rm tr}(\rho_{\beta}O_{A}O_{B})-{\rm tr}(\rho_{\beta}O_{A})\cdot{\rm tr}(\rho_{\beta}O_{B}).

(5)

Main theorem.—

We find that the thermal area law of the mutual information holds above some power-law threshold with the assumption of clustering.

Theorem 1:

Let us assume that the correlation function of the quantum Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
between two arbitrary operators
O_{i}
and
O_{i^{\prime}}
supported on the sites
i
and
i^{\prime}
satisfies the following power-law clustering property:

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{i},O_{i^{\prime}})\right|\leq\frac{C}{d_{i,i^{\prime}}^{\alpha}}\|O_{i}\|\cdot\|O_{i^{\prime}}\|,

(6)

where
C
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant. Then, for the bipartition
A,B
of
D
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda

(A\cup B=\Lambda)
and
\alpha>(D+1)/2
, the mutual information is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)\leq\text{const.}\beta|\partial A|\,.

(7)

We postpone the sketch of proof to the end of the paper (the details are in the Supplemental Material (SM)
Sup
). The theorem ensures that the thermal area law holds for the regime
\alpha>(D+1)/2
. This condition means that the thermal area law is much more robust than expected from a simple argument on the norm of the boundary interaction, which leads to the weaker condition of
\alpha>D+1
.
Remarkably, the condition partially includes the thermodynamically nonextensive regime, extending up to interaction decays of
r^{-1}
(1D),
r^{-3/2}
(2D), and
r^{-2}
(3D).

Power-law clustering and unconditional thermal area law.—

Theorem 1 suggests that the crucial physics behind the thermal area law is not only from the magnitude of the boundary interaction but also from the clustering property. Note that the clustering property is expected to be one of the robust physical properties in noncritical thermal phases, rendering it an inherently natural assumption
Brandão and Horodecki 2013
. We here demonstrate that the power-law clustering property can be rigorously proven for
\alpha>D
above a temperature threshold as shown in the following Theorem 2.

Theorem 2:

Under the general Hamiltonian (
2
) with (
3
) for the regime
\alpha>D
, the following clustering property holds for the temperatures above
\beta_{c}^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|O_{X}\|\cdot\|O_{Y}\|{|X||Y|\,e^{{(|X|+|Y|)/k}}\over d_{X,Y}^{\alpha}}\,,

(8)

where
d_{X,Y}=\min_{i\in X,j\in Y}d_{i,j}
and
|X|
and
|Y|
are numbers of sites in the regions
X
and
Y
, respectively.
C
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant and the threshold temperature is given by
\beta_{c}=1/(8ugk)
with
u=\sum_{j\in\Lambda}1/(1+d_{i,j})^{\alpha}
.

The proof is based on the cluster expansion technique. The details in the proof are provided in SM
Sup
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result to establish the power-law clustering theorem in quantum long-range interacting systems.

From this statement, the clustering property in Theorem 1 is not an assumption but rigorously holds above a threshold temperature as long as the thermodynamically extensive regime (i.e.,
\alpha>D
) is considered. In particular, in 1D systems, the clustering property is believed to hold at arbitrary temperatures
Araki 1969
;
Kimura and Kuwahara 2024
. Moreover, we stress that even in the thermodynamically nonextensive regime (
\alpha<D
), numerical calculations for the two models below show the validity of the clustering property.

Proof sketch of main theorem.—

We here provide the outline of the proof of the main theorem.
For the sake of simplicity, we focus on the simple case where
h_{Z}
is
h_{i,j}
that acts only on two different sites
i,j\in\Lambda
. The proof for the general case is in SM
Sup
.

The first step is to leverage the remarkable inequality demonstrated in Ref.
Wolf et al. 2008
.
Consider the total Hamiltonian
H=H_{A}+H_{B}+H_{\partial A}
, where
H_{A}
,
H_{B}
are supported only on regions
A
and
B
respectively, and
H_{\partial A}
represents the interaction between these regions.
Reference
Wolf et al. 2008
utilized the Gibbs variational principle, expressed as

\displaystyle F(\rho)-F(\rho_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\beta^{-1}D(\rho||\rho_{\beta})\geq 0\,,

(14)

where
F(\rho):={\rm tr}(H\rho)+\beta^{-1}{\rm tr}[\rho\log(\rho)]
is the nonequilibrium free energy and
D(\rho||\sigma)={\rm tr}(\rho\log\rho)-{\rm tr}(\rho\log\sigma)
is the quantum relative entropy.
By substituting
\rho=\rho_{\beta}^{A}\otimes\rho_{\beta}^{B}
into this inequality, Ref.
Wolf et al. 2008
established a bound on the mutual information:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)

\displaystyle\leq\beta\,\mathrm{tr}\left[(\rho_{\beta}^{A}\otimes\rho_{\beta}^{B}-\rho_{\beta}^{AB})H_{\partial A}\right].

(15)

The second crucial step is to reduce quantum complexity by utilizing the clustering property.
We can represent the boundary interaction as
H_{\partial A}=\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}h_{i,j}
, and each operator
h_{i,j}
can be expanded as
h_{i,j}=\sum_{s=1}^{d_{0}^{4}}h_{i}^{(s)}\otimes h_{j}^{(s)}
, with
h_{i}^{(s)}
exclusively supported on site
i
and
h_{j}^{(s)}
on
j
.
Here,
d_{0}
denotes the Hilbert space dimension of a single site. The operators
h_{i}^{(s)}\otimes h_{j}^{(s)}
can be chosen to be Hermitian and mutually orthogonal with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.
Inserting this decomposition into Eq. (
15
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)

\displaystyle\leq\beta\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}\sum_{s=1}^{d_{0}^{4}}\left|\mathrm{Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(h_{i}^{(s)},h_{j}^{(s)})\right|\,.

(16)

The assumption of the clustering property (
6
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)\leq C\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}\sum_{s=1}^{d_{0}^{4}}\beta\|h_{i}^{(s)}\|\cdot\|h_{j}^{(s)}\|d_{i,j}^{-\alpha}.

(17)

By the condition (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\max_{s}\left(\|h_{i}^{(s)}\|\cdot\|h_{j}^{(s)}\|\right)\leq d_{0}J_{i,j}\leq\frac{d_{0}g}{d_{i,j}^{\alpha}}.

(18)

Combining Eq. (
17
) and Eq. (
18
), we finally obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)\leq\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}\frac{\beta d_{0}^{5}gC}{d_{i,j}^{2\alpha}}.

(19)

It can be proven that the summation of the distance
\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}d_{i,j}^{-2\alpha}
is upper bounded by the boundary area when
2\alpha>D+1
. Therefore, inequality (
19
) leads to the desired inequality (
7
).

Summary and outlook.—

We consider the validity of the thermal area law in the systems with long-range interactions
r^{-\alpha}
. Under the assumption of the clustering property, we derive the critical threshold
\alpha_{\rm c}:=(D+1)/2
above which any systems obey the thermal area law of the mutual information (See Fig.
1
). Remarkably, the regime covers the thermodynamically nonextensive regime.
This criteria potentially may allow for an efficient representation of quantum Gibbs states exhibiting a power-law decay up to
r^{-\alpha_{\rm c}}
.
Given this criterion, it is a crucial future problem
to develop an efficiency-guaranteed algorithm for simulating long-range interacting systems at finite temperatures, as well as constructing tensor network states with polynomial bond dimensions
Molnar et al. 2015
;
Kliesch et al. 2014
.
Furthermore, experimental verification of the thermal area law in long-range interacting systems appears feasible using the TFD state. A trapped ion quantum computer enables the preparation of the TFD state at finite temperatures, as outlined in Ref.
Zhu et al. 2020
. Subsequent measurements of the Rényi entanglement entropy of this state, utilizing the methodology described in Ref.
Islam et al. 2015
, could potentially demonstrate its conformity to the thermal area law when
\alpha>(D+1)/2
.

S.I
Setup

We consider a quantum system, where we use
\Lambda
to represent the set of sites.
Given an arbitrary subset
X
in
\Lambda
,
X\subseteq\Lambda
, we denote the cardinality of the set
X
, i.e., the number of sites contained in
X
, by
|X|
.
For any two subsets
X,Y
in
\Lambda
,
X,Y\subseteq\Lambda
, we define the distance between the two sets,
d_{X,Y}
, as the shortest path length of the graph that connects
X
and
Y
. We remark that when
X
intersects with
Y
,
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
, the distance between the sets
X
and
Y
is zero:
d_{X,Y}=0
.
We denote the complementary set of
X
as
X^{\rm c}
, namely
X^{\rm c}:=\Lambda\setminus X
, and the surface subset of
X
as
\partial X
, i.e.
\partial X:=\{i\in X|d_{i,X^{\rm c}}=1\}
, respectively.
Also, for an arbitrary operator
O
, we denote the support of
O
by
{\rm Supp}(O)
.
We often describe the support of the operator explicitly by adding an Index of lower right subscript such as
O_{X}
with
{\rm Supp}(O_{X})=X
.

We consider a
k
-local Hamiltonian
H
with long-range interactions:

\displaystyle H=\sum_{Z:|Z|\leq k}h_{Z}\,,

(S.1)

where
Z
is a subset indicating the interacting sites, and
h_{Z}
is the local Hamiltonian. Note that
|Z|
is the number of the interacting sites, and hence
h_{Z}
means
Z
-body interaction. We assume that the interaction form satisfies the following property

\displaystyle J_{i,i^{\prime}}:=\sum_{Z:Z\ni\{i,i^{\prime}\}}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq{g\over(1+d_{i,i^{\prime}})^{\alpha}}.

(S.2)

Here,
J_{i,i^{\prime}}
is the maximum operator norm of the local Hamiltonians containing the sites
i
and
i^{\prime}
.

We consider the quantum Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
with a fixed inverse temperature
\beta
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}:=e^{-\beta H}/Z,\quad Z={\rm tr}(e^{-\beta H}).

(S.3)

The mutual information of
\rho_{\beta}
between two subsets
A
and
B
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B):=S(\rho_{\beta}^{A})+S(\rho_{\beta}^{B})-S(\rho_{\beta}^{AB}),

(S.4)

where
\rho_{\beta}^{X}
is the density matrix of
\rho_{\beta}
for the regime
X\in\{A,B,AB\}
and
S(\rho_{\beta}^{X}):=-{\rm tr}(\rho_{\beta}^{X}\log\rho_{\beta}^{X})
is the von Neumann entropy.
The standard correlation function of the density matrix
\rho
between two observables
O_{A}
and
O_{B}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho}(O_{A},O_{B}):={\rm tr}(\rho O_{A}O_{B})-{\rm tr}(\rho O_{A})\cdot{\rm tr}(\rho O_{B}).

(S.5)

S.II
Proof of Main Theorem in a General Setting

Theorem 1
.

Let us assume that the correlation function of the quantum Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
between two arbitrary operators
O_{X}
and
O_{Y}
supported on the subsets
X
and
Y
in
\Lambda
satisfies the following power-law clustering property:

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\right|\leq\frac{C}{d_{X,Y}^{\alpha}}\|O_{X}\|\cdot\|O_{Y}\|,

(S.6)

where
C
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant.
Then, for the bipartition
A,B
of
D
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda
(
A\cup B=\Lambda
) and
\alpha>(D+1)/2
, the mutual information is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)\leq\beta\cdot C\cdot|\partial A|\cdot\text{const}.

(S.7)

Proof.

We partition the lattice
\Lambda
into two subsets
A,B\subset\Lambda
and write the Hamiltonian as
H=H_{A}+H_{B}+H_{\partial A}
. Here,
H_{A}
,
H_{B}
are the Hamiltonian supported only on
A,B
, respectively, and
H_{\partial A}
is the interaction between
A
and
B
, i.e.,

\displaystyle H_{\partial A}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z\cap A\neq\emptyset\\
Z\cap B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}h_{Z}.

(S.8)

Using the non-negativity of the quantum relative entropy
D(\rho\|\sigma):={\rm tr}(\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma))
yields

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\rho_{\beta})=\beta{\rm tr}(\rho H)-S(\rho)+\log Z=\beta F_{\beta}(\rho)-\beta F_{\beta}(\rho_{\beta})\geq 0,

(S.9)

for an arbitrary density matrix
\rho
. Here,
F_{\beta}(\rho)
is the free energy defined as

\displaystyle F_{\beta}(\rho):={\rm tr}(\rho H)-\beta^{-1}S(\rho),

(S.10)

and it becomes
F_{\beta}(\rho_{\beta})=-\beta^{-1}\log Z
at equilibrium. Substituting
\rho=\rho_{\beta}^{A}\otimes\rho_{\beta}^{B}
and using
\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)=S(\rho_{\beta}^{A}\otimes\rho_{\beta}^{B})-S(\rho_{\beta}^{AB})
and
{\rm tr}((H_{A}+H_{B})\rho_{\beta}^{A}\otimes\rho_{\beta}^{B})={\rm tr}((H_{A}+H_{B})\rho_{\beta}^{AB})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)\leq\beta\cdot{\rm tr}((\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}-\rho_{AB})H_{\partial A}).

(S.11)

For the
k
-local Hamiltonian, each local term
h_{Z}
can be expanded by a sum of the tensor products of two operators supported on
Z_{A}=Z\cap A
and
Z_{B}=Z\cap B
:

\displaystyle h_{Z}=\sum_{s=1}^{d_{0}^{2k}}h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\otimes h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)}.

(S.12)

Here,
d_{0}
denotes the Hilbert space dimension of a single site. The operators
h_{i}^{(s)}\otimes h_{j}^{(s)}
can be chosen to be Hermitian and orthogonal to each other with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, i.e.
{\rm tr}\left((h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\otimes h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)})^{\dagger}(h_{Z_{A}}^{(s^{\prime})}\otimes h_{Z_{B}}^{(s^{\prime})})\right)\propto\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}
.
Equations (
S.8
), (
S.11
) and (
S.12
) lead to

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z\cap A\neq\emptyset\\
Z\cap B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=1}^{d_{0}^{2k}}\beta\cdot{\rm tr}\left((\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}-\rho_{AB})h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\otimes h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)}\right)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z\cap A\neq\emptyset\\
Z\cap B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=1}^{d_{0}^{2k}}\beta\cdot\left|\mathrm{Cor}_{\rho_{AB}}(h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)},h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)})\right|.

(S.13)

The assumption of the clustering property gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z\cap A\neq\emptyset\\
Z\cap B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=1}^{d_{0}^{2k}}\beta\cdot\|h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\|\|h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)}\|\frac{C}{d_{Z_{A},Z_{B}}^{\alpha}}

(S.14)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z_{A}\ni i\\
Z_{B}\ni j\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=1}^{d_{0}^{2k}}\beta\cdot\|h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\|\|h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)}\|\frac{C}{d_{i,j}^{\alpha}}

(S.15)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z_{A}\ni i\\
Z_{B}\ni j\end{subarray}}\beta\cdot d_{0}^{2k}\max_{s}\|h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\|\|h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)}\|\frac{C}{d_{i,j}^{\alpha}}.

(S.16)

In the second inequality, we have used the fact that
d_{Z_{A},Z_{B}}^{-\alpha}
can be expressed as
d_{i,j}^{-\alpha}
for some
i\in Z_{A}
and
j\in Z_{B}
. In the third inequality, we have used
\sum_{s=1}^{d_{0}^{2k}}\|h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\|\|h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)}\|\leq d_{0}^{2k}\max_{s}\|h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\|\|h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)}\|
.
Note that

\displaystyle\max_{s}\|h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\|\|h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)}\|\leq\sqrt{{\rm tr}(h_{Z}^{\dagger}h_{Z})}\leq d_{0}^{k/2}\|h_{Z}\|.

(S.17)

By the condition Eq. (
S.2
) of the long-range interaction, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z_{A}\ni i\\
Z_{B}\ni j\end{subarray}}\max_{s}\|h_{Z_{A}}^{(s)}\|\|h_{Z_{B}}^{(s)}\|\leq d_{0}^{k/2}\sum_{Z:Z\ni\{i,j\}}\|h_{Z}\|\leq\frac{d_{0}^{k/2}g}{d_{i,j}^{\alpha}}.

(S.18)

Combining Eq. (
S.16
) and Eq. (
S.18
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)\leq\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}\frac{\beta d_{0}^{5k/2}gC}{d_{i,j}^{2\alpha}}.

(S.19)

It was proven that
\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B}d_{i,j}^{-2\alpha}
is upper bounded by the boundary area
|\partial A|
when
2\alpha>D+1

Minato et al. 2022
. With Eq. (
S.19
), we finally obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rho_{\beta}}(A:B)\leq\beta\cdot C\cdot|\partial A|\cdot\text{const},

(S.20)

for
2\alpha>D+1
.
∎

S.III
Power-Law Clustering Theorem for
\alpha>D

We consider the regime
\alpha>D
, so that the extensivity property holds as

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{H_{L}}\right\|\leq g|L|,\quad\widehat{H_{L}}=\sum_{Z:Z\cap L\neq\emptyset}h_{Z}=H-H_{L^{\rm c}}

(S.21)

for an arbitrary subset
L\subseteq\Lambda
.
Then, the clustering theorem for the bipartite correlations is rigorously proven as

Theorem 2
.

The following relation holds for long-range interacting systems for the temperatures above
\beta_{c}^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|O_{X}\|\cdot\|O_{Y}\|{|X||Y|\,e^{{(|X|+|Y|)/k}}\over d_{X,Y}^{\alpha}}\,,

(S.22)

where
C
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant and the threshold temperature is given by
\beta_{c}=W(1/ue)/(2gk)
with the Lambert
W
function (i.e., the inverse function of
xe^{x}
) and
u=\sum_{j\in\Lambda}1/(1+d_{i,j})^{\alpha}
.
We can lower-bound
\beta_{c}
as
\beta_{c}\geq 1/(8ugk)
by using

W(1/ue)\leq 1/(1+ue)\leq 1/(4u),

where we use
u
is always larger than
1
from the definition.

For the proof of the clustering theorem, we utilize the cluster expansion
Kuwahara et al. 2020
;
Kuwahara and Saito 2020d
;
Kliesch et al. 2014
.
First of all, we describe the correlation function
{\rm Cor}_{\rho}(O_{X},O_{Y})
can be written as

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})=\frac{1}{{\cal Z}^{2}}{\rm tr}\left(e^{-\beta H^{(+)}}O_{X}^{(0)}O_{Y}^{(1)}\right),

(S.23)

where we define the operators
O^{(+)}
,
O^{(0)}
and
O^{(1)}
as

\displaystyle O^{(+)}=O\otimes\hat{1}+\hat{1}\otimes O,\quad O^{(0)}=O\otimes\hat{1},\quad O^{(1)}=O\otimes\hat{1}-\hat{1}\otimes O\,,

(S.24)

by considering a copy of the original Hilbert space. The quantity
{\cal Z}
is a normalization factor
{\cal Z}^{2}={\rm tr}(e^{-\beta H^{(+)}})
(
=[{\rm tr}(e^{-\beta H})]^{2}
in the original Hilbert space). It is simple to observe that the following lemma holds:

Lemma 1
.

For an arbitrary set of operators
\{O_{Z_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, the following relation holds:

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(O_{Z_{1}}^{(+)}O_{Z_{2}}^{(+)}\cdots O_{Z_{m}}^{(+)}O_{X}^{(0)}O_{Y}^{(1)}\right)=0

(S.25)

when the subsets
\{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}\}
cannot connect the subsets
X
and
Y
[see Fig.
5
(a)], i.e.,

\displaystyle\left(X\cup Z_{i_{1}}\cup Z_{i_{2}}\cup\cdots\cup Z_{i_{s}}\right)\cap\left(Y\cup Z_{i_{s+1}}\cup Z_{i_{s+2}}\cup\cdots\cup Z_{i_{m}}\right)=\emptyset

(S.26)

with
\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m}\}=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Without loss of generality, we let
\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{s}\}=\{1,2,\ldots,s\}
and
\{1,2,\ldots,s\}\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m}\}=\{s+1,s+2,\ldots,m\}
.
We then denote

\displaystyle O_{Z_{1}}^{(+)}O_{Z_{2}}^{(+)}\cdots O_{Z_{s}}^{(+)}=W_{1},\quad O_{Z_{s+1}}^{(+)}O_{Z_{s+2}}^{(+)}\cdots O_{Z_{m}}^{(+)}=W_{2},

(S.27)

with

\displaystyle{\rm Supp}(X\cup W_{1})\cap{\rm Supp}(Y\cup W_{2})=\emptyset,

(S.28)

where the second equation is equivalent to the condition (
S.26
).
We then decompose as

{\rm tr}\left(O^{(+)}_{Z_{1}}O^{(+)}_{Z_{2}}\ldots O^{(+)}_{Z_{m}}O^{(0)}_{X}O^{(1)}_{Y}\right)={\rm tr}\left(W_{1}O^{(0)}_{X}\right){\rm tr}\left(W_{2}O^{(1)}_{Y}\right).

(S.29)

Here, the operators
O^{(+)}_{Z_{i_{k}}}=O_{Z_{i_{k}}}\otimes\hat{1}+\hat{1}\otimes O_{Z_{i_{k}}}
are symmetric for the exchange of the two Hilbert space while the operator
O^{(1)}_{Y}=O_{Y}\otimes\hat{1}-\hat{1}\otimes O_{Y}
is antisymmetric, which yields
{\rm tr}\left(W_{2}O^{(1)}_{Y}\right)=0
.
We thus prove the main equation (
S.25
).

\square

[ End of Proof of Lemma
1
]

By using Lemma
1
, the function
{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})
can be expanded as

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})=\frac{1}{{\cal Z}^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}:{\rm connected}}\frac{(-\beta)^{m}}{m!}{\rm tr}\left(h_{Z_{1}}^{(+)}h_{Z_{2}}^{(+)}\cdots h_{Z_{m}}^{(+)}O_{X}^{(0)}O_{Y}^{(1)}\right),

(S.30)

where the summation
\sum_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}:{\rm connected}}
is taken over the collections
\{X,Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m},Y\}
that connect
X
and
Y
. See Fig.
5
(b). Note that
\{Z_{s}\}_{s=1}^{m}
can be the same, e.g.,
Z_{1}=Z_{2}
. Let us define the operator
\rho_{\rm cl}
as

\displaystyle\rho_{{\rm cl}}=\frac{1}{{\cal Z}^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}:{\rm connected}}\frac{(-\beta)^{m}}{m!}h_{Z_{1}}^{(+)}h_{Z_{2}}^{(+)}\cdots h_{Z_{m}}^{(+)}.

(S.31)

Using the operator
\rho_{{\rm cl}}
, the correlation
{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})
has the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\right|\leq 2\left\|O_{X}\right\|\cdot\left\|O_{Y}\right\|\cdot\left\|\rho_{{\rm cl}}\right\|_{1}

(S.32)

To count the summation of
\sum_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}:{\rm connected}}
, we first decompose as

\displaystyle\{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}\}=w_{\rm cl}\oplus w_{\rm cl}^{\rm c},\quad w_{\rm cl}^{\rm c}:=\{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}\}\setminus w_{\rm cl},

(S.33)

where
w_{\rm cl}
is taken such that
w_{\rm cl}\oplus\{X,Y\}
are connected to each other (See Fig.
5

\displaystyle Z\cap Z^{\prime}=\emptyset\quad\textrm{for}\quad Z\in w_{\rm cl},\quad Z^{\prime}\in w^{\rm c}_{\rm cl}.

(S.34)

We define all the sets of
w_{\rm cl}
and
w_{\rm cl}^{\rm c}
as
\mathcal{G}_{\rm cl}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\rm c}_{\rm cl}
, respectively. We also define the total support of the
w_{\rm cl}
and
w_{\rm cl}^{\rm c}
as
V_{w_{\rm cl}}
and
V_{w_{\rm cl}^{\rm c}}
, respectively. We then obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm cl}

\displaystyle={1\over{\cal Z}^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}{(-\beta)^{m}\over m!}\sum_{s=0}^{m}{m!\over(m-s)!s!}\sum_{\{Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{s}\}=w_{\rm cl}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm cl}}\!\!\!\!\tilde{h}(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{s})\sum_{w_{\rm cl}^{\rm c}\in\mathcal{G}^{\rm c}_{\rm cl}}\sum_{Z_{s+1},\cdots,Z_{m}\atop\in w_{\rm cl}^{\rm c}}\!\!\!\!\!\!h^{(+)}(Z_{s+1})h^{(+)}(Z_{s+2})\cdots h^{(+)}(Z_{m})

(S.35)

Here, we have defined

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{P}h^{(+)}(Z_{P_{1}})\cdots h^{(+)}(Z_{P_{s}})\,,

(S.36)

where

Z
. In particular, when

From the expression (
S.35

\displaystyle\|{\rho_{{\rm cl}}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq{1\over{\cal Z}^{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}{\beta^{s}\over s!}\sum_{w_{\rm cl}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm cl}}\|\tilde{h}(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{s})\|\,{\rm tr}\Bigl[e^{-\beta H^{(+)}_{V_{w^{\rm c}_{\rm cl}}}}\Bigr]\leq\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}{\beta^{s}\over s!}\sum_{w_{\rm cl}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm cl}}e^{2\beta g|V_{w_{\rm cl}}|}\|\tilde{h}(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{s})\|\,.

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\Bigl[e^{-\beta H^{(+)}_{V_{w^{\rm c}_{\rm cl}}}}\Bigr]

S.21

w_{\rm cl}^{\rm r}

and

. See Fig.

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}(\beta e^{2\beta gk})^{s}\sum_{s^{\prime}=1}^{s}\sum_{w_{\rm cl}^{\rm s}}{\|\tilde{h}(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{s^{\prime}})\|^{\prime}\over s^{\prime}!}\sum_{w_{\rm cl}^{\rm r}}{\|\tilde{h}(Z_{s^{\prime}+1},\cdots,Z_{s})\|^{\prime}\over(s-s^{\prime})!}\,,

(S.39)

where

\displaystyle\|\tilde{h}(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{s^{\prime}})\|^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{P}\|h_{Z_{P_{1}}}^{(+)}\|\cdots\|h_{Z_{P_{s^{\prime}}}}^{(+)}\|\leq 2^{s^{\prime}}\sum_{P}\|h_{Z_{P_{1}}}\|\cdots\|h_{Z_{P_{s^{\prime}}}}\|\,.

(S.40)

Similarly to (
S.40
),
\|\tilde{h}(Z_{s^{\prime}+1},\cdots,Z_{s})\|^{\prime}
is defined. Below, we evaluate the terms on
w_{\rm cl}^{\rm s}
and
w_{\rm cl}^{\rm r}
in (
S.39
). To this end, the following lemma is useful.

Lemma 2
.

For
\alpha>D
,
the quantity
J_{i,j}
defined in (
S.2

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\Lambda}J_{i,j}J_{j,k}\leq{g^{2}u\over(1+d_{i,k})^{\alpha}}\,,

(S.41)

where

. The iterative use of (
S.41
) leads to
\left[{\bm{J}}^{\ell}\right]_{i,k}\leq{g^{\ell}u^{\ell-1}/(1+d_{i,k})^{\alpha}}
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\Lambda}J_{i,j}J_{j,k}

\displaystyle=g^{2}\sum_{j\in\Lambda}{1\over(1+d_{i,j})^{\alpha}}{1\over(1+d_{j,k})^{\alpha}}\leq{g^{2}\over(1+d_{i,k})^{\alpha}}\sum_{j\in\Lambda}{(2+d_{i,j}+d_{j,k})^{\alpha}\over(1+d_{i,j})^{\alpha}}{1\over(1+d_{j,k})^{\alpha}}.

(S.42)

Note
(x+y)^{\alpha}\leq 2^{\alpha-1}(x^{\alpha}+y^{\alpha})
for
\alpha>1
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\Lambda}J_{i,j}J_{j,k}

\displaystyle\leq{g^{2}2^{\alpha-1}\over(1+d_{i,k})^{\alpha}}\sum_{j\in\Lambda}{1\over(1+d_{i,j})^{\alpha}}+{1\over(1+d_{j,k})^{\alpha}}={g^{2}u\over(1+d_{i,k})^{\alpha}}.

(S.43)

The finiteness of
u
is guaranteed by the condition
\alpha>D
.

2
]

Let us first consider the terms on
w_{\rm cl}^{\rm s}
in (
S.39

\displaystyle\sum_{w_{\rm cl}^{\rm s}}{\|\tilde{h}(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{s^{\prime}})\|^{\prime}\over s^{\prime}!}

S.VI
Numerical Investigation of Clustering Properties in 2D Long-Range Interacting Spin Systems

In this section, we present numerical analyses of the clustering properties of correlation functions in two-dimensional long-range interacting spin systems. These results extend the findings for bilinear fermion systems discussed in the main text, providing further evidence that clustering properties universally hold even in nonintegrable systems. In particular, we show that both the long-range Heisenberg model and the long-range XX model exhibit clustering properties consistently in the regime
\alpha<D
.

We consider a rectangular lattice of dimensions
L_{x}\times L_{y}
, where the lattice sites are indexed by integer coordinates
\bm{r}=(x,y)
with
1\leq x\leq L_{x}
and
1\leq y\leq L_{y}
. The distance between two lattice sites
\bm{r}_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1})
and
\bm{r}_{2}=(x_{2},y_{2})
is given by
|\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2}|=\sqrt{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}+(y_{1}-y_{2})^{2}}
.

We study the 2D long-range Heisenberg model characterized by the Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{Hei}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2}\\
\bm{r}_{1}\neq\bm{r}_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2|\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2}|^{\alpha}}\bm{S}_{\bm{r}_{1}}\cdot\bm{S}_{\bm{r}_{2}},

(S.52)

where
\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}=(S_{\mathbf{r}}^{x},S_{\mathbf{r}}^{y},S_{\mathbf{r}}^{z})
represents the Pauli spin vector at lattice site
\bm{r}
.

We calculate the two-point correlation function
\langle\bm{S}_{\bm{i}}\cdot\bm{S}_{\bm{i}+\bm{r}}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{\beta}\,\bm{S}_{\bm{i}}\cdot\bm{S}_{\bm{i}+\bm{r}})
for the Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}=e^{-\beta H_{\mathrm{Hei}}}/\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta H_{\mathrm{Hei}}})
, between two sites
\bm{i}
and
\bm{i}+\bm{r}
. The results are illustrated in Fig.
8
.

Two types of geometries are considered. The first is a rectangular lattice with dimensions
L_{x}=16
and
L_{y}=5
. In Fig.

\beta=0.1
by fixing
\bm{i}=(3,3)
and varying
\bm{r}=(r,0)
for

S.VII
Numerical Detail of Shapourian-Shiozaki-Ryu Negativity

We consider
n
spinless fermionic particles with the basis states
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle=c^{\dagger}|0\rangle
. The determinant of the
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{X}=(x_{i,j})
is defined as

\displaystyle\det\mathbf{X}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)x_{1,\sigma(1)}\cdots x_{n,\sigma(n)},

(S.54)

and the Pfaffian of the
2n\times 2n
skew-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{Y}=(y_{i,j})
, i.e.
\mathbf{Y}^{\mathrm{T}}=-\mathbf{Y}
, is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pf}[\mathbf{Y}]=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{2n}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)y_{\sigma(1)\sigma(2)}\cdots y_{\sigma(2n-1),\sigma(2n)}.

(S.55)

Here,
S_{n}
and
\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)
denotes the symmetric group and the sign of the permutation
\sigma
, respectively.
For an arbitrary
2n\times 2n
matrix
\mathbf{Z}
, we use the following expression

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}=\begin{pmatrix}[\mathbf{Z}]^{(1,1)}&[\mathbf{Z}]^{(1,2)}\\
[\mathbf{Z}]^{(2,1)}&[\mathbf{Z}]^{(2,2)}\end{pmatrix}

(S.56)

to describe the
n\times n
block matrix
[\mathbf{Z}]^{(a,b)}
with
a
-th row and
b
-th column (
a,b\in\{1,2\}
). We denote

\mathds{1}_{n}
as an
n\times n
identity matrix.

We use the Grassmann variables
\{\xi_{j},\overline{\xi}_{j}\}
to describe the fermionic systems with the following notation:

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\equiv(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n}),

(S.57)

\displaystyle(\xi,\overline{\xi})

\displaystyle\equiv(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n},\overline{\xi}_{1},\cdots,\overline{\xi}_{n}),

(S.58)

with the differentials

\displaystyle d\xi

\displaystyle\equiv d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1},

(S.59)

\displaystyle d\overline{\xi}d\xi

\displaystyle\equiv d\overline{\xi}_{n}\cdots d\overline{\xi}_{1}d\xi_{n}\cdots d\xi_{1},

(S.60)

\displaystyle d(\overline{\xi},\xi)

\displaystyle\equiv d\overline{\xi}_{1}d\xi_{1}\cdots d\overline{\xi}_{n}d\xi_{n}=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}d\overline{\xi}d\xi.

(S.61)

We define the state with the Grassmann variable as

\displaystyle|\xi_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv|0\rangle_{j}-\xi_{j}|1\rangle_{j},

(S.62)

\displaystyle|\overline{\xi}_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\langle 0|_{j}-\overline{\xi}_{j}\langle 1|_{j},

(S.63)

\displaystyle|\xi\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}|

\displaystyle=|\xi_{1}\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}_{1}|\otimes\cdots\otimes|\xi_{n}\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}_{n}|.

(S.64)

The definition naturally leads to
\langle\overline{\xi}_{j}|\xi_{j}\rangle=e^{\overline{\xi}_{j}\xi_{j}}
. We also use the notation
(\chi,\overline{\chi})
to describe another Grassmann variables.

In terms of the Grassmann variables, the trace of an operator
O
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[O]=\int\langle-\overline{\xi}|O|\xi\rangle e^{-\sum_{j}\overline{\xi}_{j}\xi_{j}}\,d(\overline{\xi},\xi).

(S.65)

We consider the following form of the density operator:

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{Z_{\rho}}\int d\overline{\xi}d\xi\,|\xi\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}|e^{(\xi,\overline{\xi})\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Gamma}(\xi,\overline{\xi})^{\mathrm{T}}+\sum_{j}\overline{\xi}_{j}\xi_{j}}.

(S.66)

Imposing
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho]=1
with Eqs. (
S.65
) and (
S.66
), we have
Z_{\rho}=\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]
. Therefore, the density matrix is written as

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]}\int d\overline{\xi}d\xi\,|\xi\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}|e^{(\xi,\overline{\xi})\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Gamma}(\xi,\overline{\xi})^{\mathrm{T}}+\sum_{j}\overline{\xi}_{j}\xi_{j}}.

(S.67)

Using Eq.(
S.65
), we can relate the two-point correlations functions of the fermionic creation and annihilation operators to the matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
by

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}[\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}]^{(1,1)}&[\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}]^{(1,2)}\\
[\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}]^{(2,1)}&[\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}]^{(2,2)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\langle c_{j}c_{i}\rangle_{\rho}&-\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}\rangle_{\rho}\\
\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}\rangle_{\rho}&\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rho}\end{pmatrix}.

(S.68)

In other words, we identify the density matrix with the covaraince matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
.

Now we move onto the Shapourian-Shiozaki-Ryu (SSR) negativity. We partition the total system
\Lambda
into
A
and
B
with
|A|=n_{A}
and
|B|=n_{B}
. To define the SSR negativity between
A
and
B
, we need the partial time-reversal transformation
R_{A}
on the subsystem
A
. The time-reversal transformation maps
|\xi\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}|
to
|i\overline{\xi}\rangle\langle i\xi|
in the coherent basis. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle(|\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in A},\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in B}\rangle\langle\{\overline{\chi}_{j}\}_{j\in A},\{\overline{\chi}_{j}\}_{j\in B}|)^{R_{A}}=|\{i\overline{\chi}_{j}\}_{j\in A},\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in B}\rangle\langle\{i\xi_{j}\}_{j\in A},\{\overline{\chi}_{j}\}_{j\in B}|,

(S.69)

and therefore the density matrix after the partial time-reversal transformation becomes

\displaystyle\rho^{R_{A}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]}\int d\overline{\xi}\,d\xi\,|i\overline{\xi}_{A},\xi_{B}\rangle\langle i\xi_{A},\overline{\xi}_{B}|e^{(\xi,\overline{\xi})\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Gamma}(\xi,\overline{\xi})^{\mathrm{T}}+\sum_{j}\overline{\xi}_{j}\xi_{j}}.

(S.70)

Now, we rearrange the Grassmann variables. Let the vector
(\xi^{\prime},\overline{\xi}^{\prime})\equiv(\xi_{A}^{\prime},\xi_{B}^{\prime},\overline{\xi}_{A}^{\prime},\overline{\xi}_{B}^{\prime})=(-i\overline{\xi}_{A},\xi_{B},-i\xi_{A},\overline{\xi}_{B})
, then we represent it by introducing the matrix
\mathbf{T}
:

\displaystyle(\xi^{\prime},\overline{\xi}^{\prime})^{\mathrm{T}}=\mathbf{T}(\xi,\overline{\xi})^{\mathrm{T}},\qquad\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-i\mathds{1}_{n_{A}}&0\\
0&\mathds{1}_{n_{B}}&0&0\\
-i\mathds{1}_{n_{A}}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&\mathds{1}_{n_{B}}\end{pmatrix}.

(S.71)

Here,
\mathds{1}_{n_{A}}
(
\mathds{1}_{n_{B}}
) is an
n_{A}\times n_{A}
(
n_{B}\times n_{B}
) identity matrix.
With this matrix, a partial transformed density matrix is rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\rho^{R_{A}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]}\int d\overline{\xi}d\xi\,|\xi\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}|e^{(\xi,\overline{\xi})\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}(\xi,\overline{\xi})^{\mathrm{T}}},

(S.72)

with a newly defined matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{T}\Bigg[\bm{\Gamma}+\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathds{1}_{n}\\
\mathds{1}_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}\Bigg]\mathbf{T}.

(S.73)

The next step is to consider the product
(\rho^{R_{A}})^{\dagger}\rho^{R_{A}}
. We introduce the matrix
\mathbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
for the operator
(\rho^{R_{A}})^{\dagger}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{\prime\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}([\mathbf{S}^{\prime}]^{(2,2)})^{\dagger}&([\mathbf{S}^{\prime}]^{(1,2)})^{\dagger}\\
([\mathbf{S}^{\prime}]^{(2,1)})^{\dagger}&([\mathbf{S}^{\prime}]^{(1,1)})^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}.

(S.74)

This leads to

\displaystyle(\rho^{R_{A}})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]}\int d\overline{\xi}d\xi\,|\xi\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}|e^{(\xi,\overline{\xi})\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{S}^{\prime\prime})(\xi,\overline{\xi})^{\mathrm{T}}}.

(S.75)

After a straightforward computation, we find the following expression for the product
(\rho^{R_{A}})^{\dagger}\rho^{R_{A}}
:

\displaystyle(\rho^{R_{A}})^{\dagger}\rho^{R_{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n^{2}}\mathrm{Pf}[\mathbf{B}]}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]^{2}}\int d\overline{\xi}d\xi\,|\xi\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}|e^{(\xi,\overline{\xi})\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}(\xi,\overline{\xi})^{\mathrm{T}}+\sum_{j}\overline{\xi}_{j}\xi_{j}},

(S.76)

with introducing two matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}[\mathbf{S}^{\prime}]^{(1,1)}&-\mathds{1}_{n}\\
\mathds{1}_{n}&[\mathbf{S}^{\prime\prime}]^{(2,2)}\end{pmatrix},

(S.77)

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}0&[\mathbf{S}^{\prime\prime}]^{(1,2)}\\
[\mathbf{S}^{\prime}]^{(2,1)}&0\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0&[\mathbf{S}^{\prime}]^{(1,2)}\\
[\mathbf{S}^{\prime\prime}]^{(2,1)}&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}[\mathbf{S}^{\prime\prime}]^{(1,1)}&0\\
0&[\mathbf{S}^{\prime}]^{(2,2)}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathds{1}_{n}\\
\mathds{1}_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(S.78)

Then the SSR negativity is

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{SSR}}=\log\|\rho^{R_{A}}\|_{1}=\log\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\sqrt{(\rho^{R_{A}})^{\dagger}\rho^{R_{A}}}\Big].

(S.79)

S.VII.1
Numerical Procedure

Based on the above calculations, we present the following procedure to numerically calculate the SSR negativity.

1.

We first compute the covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
from Eq. (
S.68
). Then we calculate

\mathbf{S}^{\prime},\mathbf{S}^{\prime\prime},
and
\mathbf{B}
from Eqs. (
S.73
), (
S.74
), and (
S.77
) to construct
\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}
by Eq. (
S.78
).

2.

Note the relation

\displaystyle(\rho^{R_{A}})^{\dagger}\rho^{R_{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n^{2}}\mathrm{Pf}[\mathbf{B}]\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}]}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]^{2}}\rho^{\prime\prime},

(S.80)

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}]}\int d\overline{\xi}d\xi\,|\xi\rangle\langle\overline{\xi}|e^{(\xi,\overline{\xi})\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}(\xi,\overline{\xi})^{\mathrm{T}}+\sum_{j}\overline{\xi}_{j}\xi_{j}}.

(S.81)

From
\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}
, using Eq. (
S.68
) again, we define the covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}
of
\rho^{\prime\prime}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\begin{pmatrix}\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}&\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}\\
\langle c_{i}c_{j}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}&\langle c_{i}c_{j}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}[(\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime})^{-1}]^{(2,1)}&\Big([(\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime})^{-1}]^{(2,2)}\Big)^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\Big([(\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime})^{-1}]^{(1,1)}\Big)^{\mathrm{T}}&\mathds{1}_{n}+[(\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime})^{-1}]^{(1,2)}\end{pmatrix}.

(S.82)

3.

We choose the unitary transformation
U
to define another fermionic annihilation operators
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\cdots,d_{n})
:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{d}^{\dagger}\\
\mathbf{d}\end{pmatrix}=U\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{c}^{\dagger}\\
\mathbf{c}\end{pmatrix},

(S.83)

so that it diagonalizes
\rho^{\prime\prime}
as

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime\prime}=\frac{e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}d_{i}^{\dagger}d_{i}}}{Z^{\prime\prime}},

(S.84)

where
Z^{\prime\prime}=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho^{\prime\prime}]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+e^{-\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}})
. Equation. (
S.84
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\langle d_{i}^{\dagger}d_{j}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}&\langle d_{i}^{\dagger}d_{j}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}\\
\langle d_{i}d_{j}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}&\langle d_{i}d_{j}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{e^{\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}}+1}\delta_{i,j}&0\\
0&\frac{e^{\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}}}{e^{\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}}+1}\delta_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}=U\begin{pmatrix}\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}&\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}\\
\langle c_{i}c_{j}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}&\langle c_{i}c_{j}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}\end{pmatrix}U^{\dagger}.

(S.85)

It means that we can obtain
\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}
by diagonalizing the covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}\mathrm{diag}(\langle d_{i}^{\dagger}d_{i}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}},\langle d_{i}d_{i}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}})U,

(S.86)

\displaystyle\langle d_{i}^{\dagger}d_{i}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}}+1}.

(S.87)

4.

From the following identity

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\sqrt{(\rho^{R_{A}})^{\dagger}\rho^{R_{A}}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(-1)^{n^{2}}\mathrm{Pf}[\mathbf{B}]\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}]}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]^{2}}}\mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{\rho^{\prime\prime}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Pf}[\mathbf{B}]\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}]}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]^{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1+e^{-\frac{\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}}{2}}}{\sqrt{1+e^{-\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}}}},

(S.88)

we get the following expression of the SSR negativity with computable quantities:

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{SSR}}

\displaystyle=\log\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\sqrt{(\rho^{R_{A}})^{\dagger}\rho^{R_{A}}}\Big]

(S.89)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\Bigg[\frac{(-1)^{n^{2}}\mathrm{Pf}[\mathbf{B}]\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}]}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]^{2}}\Bigg]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg[\log\Big(1+e^{-\frac{\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}}{2}}\Big)-\frac{1}{2}\log(1+e^{-\epsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}})\Bigg]

(S.90)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\Bigg[\frac{(-1)^{n^{2}}\mathrm{Pf}[\mathbf{B}]\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}]}{\mathrm{Pf}[\bm{\Gamma}]^{2}}\Bigg]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Big(\sqrt{\langle d_{i}^{\dagger}d_{i}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}}+\sqrt{\langle d_{i}d_{i}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}}\Big).

(S.91)
