Abstract
. We consider Kolmogorov-Fokker-Planck equations with unbounded drift terms which are only measurable in time and locally Hölder continuous in space. In particular, we extend the parametrix method to this setting and we prove existence and uniqueness of measure solutions to the associated Cauchy problem, as well as the equivalence with the corresponding stochastic formulation.

35Q84, 35A08, 60H10, 60H30

1.
Introduction

The aim of this work is to prove existence and uniqueness for measure solutions to the linear Cauchy problem with measure data

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}\mu_{t}+\sigma\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}-\mathrm{div}_{v}(F(t,x,v)\mu_{t})\quad&(t,x,v)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},\\
\mu_{0}=\bar{\mu}\quad&(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where

T,\sigma>0
(possibly
T=\infty
) and
F:[0,T]\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
is a suitable measurable vector field. The assumptions on
F
will be presented below and indeed, achieving a well-posedness theory under such assumptions is the main scope of the paper.

1.1.
Presentation of the problem

Our analysis is focused on three fundamental aspects related to (
1.1
). First of all, we are interested in understanding under which conditions problem (
1.1
) is well-posed. Furthermore, we aim to investigate what are the possible minimal assumptions ensuring the uniqueness of this solution (in a sense later on specified). Lastly, since we are interested in solutions with finite first order moment (hence, in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
), we
focus our attention on models where the drift term
F(t,z)
is possibly unbounded, and which satisfies a suitable growth condition alongside with a local smoothness assumption. Eventually, the equivalence of (
1.1
), in the sense of Theorem
1.4
below, with a second-order system of SDEs will be the output of our analysis and the starting point for possible applications. These applications, not contained in the present paper, will be shortly presented at the end of this introduction and constitute the scope of further investigation.

By duality, the problem of finding a unique solution to (
1.1
) is connected to the problem of proving the existence of a solution for the dual Kolmogorov equation

\sigma\Delta_{v}g(t,z)+\partial_{t}g(t,z)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}g(t,z)+F(t,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}g(t,z)=\Psi(t,z),\ (t,z)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{1+2d},

(1.2)

where
z=(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
,
\Psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and
\sigma>0
is the constant appearing in (
1.1
). This field of research has an interest of its own, and this problem is classically attacked by proving the existence of a fundamental solution for the operator associated to (
1.2
) via an adaptation of the Levy parametrix method, see for instance the works
[
19
,
11
]
focused on proving the existence of a classical solution to the Cauchy problem associated with (
1.2
). Recently, there has been a proliferation of new results extending those for the classical case to Lie solutions (see Section
3
) considering diffusion matrices and drift terms with bounded coefficients, only measurable in time and locally Hölder continuous in space, see
[
15
,
7
]
. Nevertheless, to our knowledge the case of unbounded
F
has never been considered before in this context and for this reason also the technical results of Section
3
are a novelty of this work.

Recalling that
z=(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
and comparing our analysis to the existing literature on the dual equation (
1.2
), it is immediately clear that the regularity assumptions for
F
are anisotropic in the sense that, for any fixed
t\in(0,T)
, the drift term
F
is assumed to be
\alpha
-Hölder continuous in
v
, and
\frac{\alpha}{3}
-Hölder continuous in
x
(see assumption
(A_{1})
). This is due to the underlying non-Euclidean geometry associated with the operator and later on introduced in Section
2
.

In conclusion, our aim is to develop an existence and uniqueness theory for problems of the type (
1.1
) with possibly unbounded drift
F
subject to a growth condition and not necessarily Lipschitz regular in
z
(in the usual Euclidean sense). Moreover, our analysis provides us with new insights for the study of Kolmogorov equations (
1.2
) since it extends recent results for the existence of a fundamental solution to the case of unbounded drift terms, that is still not present in the already existing literature.

1.2.
Statement of main results

In this work, we are interested in studying the following type of solutions.

Definition 1.1
.

A flow of probability measures
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
, where
\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
is the
1
-Wasserstein space, is a solution of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}\mu_{t}+\sigma\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}-\mathrm{div}_{v}(F(t,z)\mu_{t})\quad&(t,x,v)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},\\
\mu_{0}=\bar{\mu}\quad&(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d},\end{cases}

(1.3)

if and only if for all
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and for all
t\in[0,T]
it holds

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\psi\,d\mu_{t}-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\psi\,d\overline{\mu}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi+F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi+\sigma\Delta_{v}\psi\right)\,d\mu_{s}\,ds.

(1.4)

If
T=\infty
, we say that
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a global solution of (
1.3
), otherwise we call it a local solution.

Remark 1.2
.

By a density argument, we infer that if
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a solution of (
1.3
), then (
1.4
) holds for any
\psi\in{\sf A}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})=\{\psi\in C^{1}_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}):\ \Delta_{v}\psi\in C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})\}
.

In particular, we are interested in proving the existence of a global solution for (
1.3
) under minimal assumptions, and then we will tackle the uniqueness of such a solution.
To prove that (
1.3
) admits at least a local solution, we have to make the following
minimal
assumptions on the vector field
F
.

(A_{0})

F:[0,+\infty)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
is a Carathéodory map, i.e. it is measurable in the first variable and continuous in the second one.

(A_{1})

For any
T>0
there exists a constant
C(F)>0
such that
F(t,z)\leq C(F)(1+|z|_{B}^{\beta})
for all
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
, with
\beta\in(0,1)
.

(A_{2})

For any
T>0
and any compact set
K\subset{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
there exist two constants
L>0
and
\alpha\in(\beta,1]
such that

|F(t,z_{1})-F(t,z_{2})|\leq L\left\|z_{1}-z_{2}\right\|_{\rm B}^{\alpha},\ \forall\,z_{1},z_{2}\in K,\ \forall\,t\in[0,T].

Notice that Assumption
(A_{2})
makes explicit use of the Lie structure we will introduce in Section
2
.

To get the existence result, we will use the theory of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and for this reason we fix a probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,{\sf P})
. Let us recall the following definition.

Definition 1.3
.

Consider the SDE

\begin{cases}dX(t)=V(t)\,dt&t\in[0,T]\\
dV(t)=F(t,X(t),V(t))\,dt+\sqrt{2\sigma}\,dB(t)&t\in[0,T]\\
X(0)=X_{0},\quad V(0)=V_{0}\end{cases}

(1.5)

where
B
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\Sigma,{\sf P})
,
F
satisfies Assumptions
(A_{0})
,
(A_{1})
and
(A_{2})
,
\sigma>0
,
(X_{0},V_{0})\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and
T>0
(possibly
T=\infty
). We say that (
1.5
) admits a strong solution if there exists a stochastic process
(X,V)
such that:

(i)

\int_{0}^{t}V(s)\,ds
and
\int_{0}^{t}F(s,X(s),V(s))\,ds
are well-defined for any
t\in[0,T]
;

(ii)

For any
t\in[0,T]
we have

V(t)=V_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F(s,X(s),V(s))\,ds+\sqrt{2\sigma}B(t),\qquad X(t)=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}V(s)\,ds.

The process
Z=(X,V)
is the strong solution of (
1.5
). We say that uniqueness in law holds for the SDE (
1.5
) if for any two solutions
Z_{1},Z_{2}
one has
{\rm Law}(Z_{1}(t))={\rm Law}(Z_{2}(t))
for any
t\in[0,T]
. Furthermore, we say that pathwise uniqueness holds if
Z_{1}=Z_{2}
almost surely (a.s.).

On the other hand, uniqueness of the solution of (
1.4
) is shown by means of a duality approach based on the results of Section
3
. The following global existence and uniqueness theorem holds true.

Theorem 1.4
.

Under Assumptions
(A_{0})
,
(A_{1})
and
(A_{2})
, for any
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and
T>0
, the equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}\mu_{t}+\sigma\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}-\mathrm{div}_{v}(F(t,z)\mu_{t})\quad&(t,x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{+}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},\\
\mu_{0}=\bar{\mu}\quad&(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d},\end{cases}

(1.6)

admits exactly one global solution
{\bm{\mu}}:=\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}}\in C({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
, which can be characterized as follows. Let
B
be a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion and
(X_{0},V_{0})\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
be independent of
B
and such that
{\rm Law}(X_{0},V_{0})=\mu_{0}
, then there exists a stochastic process
(X,V)\in L^{1}(\Omega;C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
for all
T>0
such that
{\bm{\mu}}=Law(X,V)
and
(X,V)
is the pathwise unique strong solution to

\begin{cases}dX(t)=V(t)\,dt&t\in{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}\\
dV(t)=F(t,X(t),V(t))\,dt+\sqrt{2\sigma}\,dB(t)&t\in{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}\\
X(0)=X_{0},\quad V(0)=V_{0}.\end{cases}

(1.7)

Remark 1.5
.

In particular, we can say that for any
T>0
the unique solution of (
1.6
) is the flow of probability measures obtained by applying the evaluation map on a measure
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{1}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
.

It is also worth noticing that if
\sigma=0
, then Assumptions
(A_{0})
,
(A_{1})
and
(A_{2})
do not guarantee the uniqueness of the solution for the system of Ordinary Differential Equations (
1.7
). From this point of view, Theorem
1.4
can be seen as a regularization by noise result, in a degenerate noise setting (see
[
12
]
and references therein).

1.3.
Further directions

The well-posedness results descibed above are in the present paper recovered for the case of a linear Kolmogorov equation. Indeed, they constitute in our opinion the starting point of a broader investigation, concerning particle systems of the second order whose prototypical agent is driven by a McKean-Vlasov SDE, or by a Vlasov-Fokker-Plank PDE. In this case the drift field depends on the solution
\mu
itself in a nonlocal way, a typical example being the choice

F(t,\mu,z)=H(t,z)\ast\mu_{t}\,.

(1.8)

A future objective is extending the results obtained here to such a case, which is relevant from the point of view of the applications to mean-field control problems for multi-agent systems. The (simpler) case of first order systems has been addressed, also with well-posedness results for a class of optimal control problems, in a number of recent papers (we may refer for instance to
[
5
,
17
]
, where also applications are presented.)

Even if in literature boundedness and global Lipschitz continuity for the kernel
H
in (
1.8
) are often assumed, also in the nonlinear case we strive to obtain results under weaker assumptions (growth conditions at infinity in place of global boundedness, local Hölder continuity etc.), exactly in the spirit of the present paper. Moreover, we remark, as it can also be inferred by related first-order cases as in
[
5
,
1
]
, that the equivalence with a stochastic formulation is particularly useful for uniqueness issues, fixed point arguments, and stability estimates on the solutions which allow one to prove well-posedness of the related optimal control problems. This research program will be carried out in some forthcoming papers.

Plan of the work

In Section
2
we introduce the necessary notation and some preliminary results useful for our work. Section
3
is devoted to

), through an extension of the parametrix method for the unbouded drift case. The proof of our main results is exploited in Section

2.
Useful notation and preliminary results

We denote by
\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
the space of
\mathfrak{B}
-valued random variables (i.e. Borel-measurable functions
X:\Omega\to\mathfrak{B}
). The law of a random variable
X\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
is defined as
{\rm Law}(X)=X\sharp{\sf P}
, where
\sharp
denotes the pushforward, i.e. for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{B})

{\rm Law}(X)(A)={\sf P}(X^{-1}(A)).

We denote by
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
the subspace of
\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
of random variables
X
such that

{\sf E}[(d_{\mathfrak{B}}(X,x_{0}))^{p}]<\infty,\ \forall x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B},

where
{\sf E}
is the expected value.
In this setting, we can define a coupling of two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B})
as any random variable
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B}\times\mathfrak{B})
such that
{\rm Law}(X)=\mu
and
{\rm Law}(Y)=\nu
(see
[
21
, Definition 1.1]
).

For any
T>0
, we denote by
C([0,T];\mathfrak{B})
the space of continuous functions
f:[0,T]\to\mathfrak{B}
equipped with the uniform distance, i.e.

d_{\infty}(f,g)=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}d_{\mathfrak{B}}(f(t),g(t)).

Clearly,
C([0,T];\mathfrak{B})
is a complete separable metric space.

2.1.
Flows of probability measures

For reader’s convenience, in the following we will use the notation
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
to also denote continuous flows of probability measures, with
\bm{\mu}=\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
. We write
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];\mathfrak{B}))
to state that there exists a Borel probability measure (that we still denote
\bm{\mu}
) on
C([0,T];\mathfrak{B})
such that
\mu_{t}={\rm ev}_{t}\sharp\mu
. With this identification, we can say that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];\mathfrak{B}))\subset C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
. Furthermore, for any
T_{1}>T_{2}
and any
\bm{\mu}=\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T_{1}]}\in C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
we still denote
\bm{\mu}=\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T_{2}]}
(i.e. truncating the flow at
T_{2}
), so that through this choice we have
C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))\subset C([0,T_{2}];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
.

We will denote by
C([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B}))
the space of narrowly continuous flows of probability measures, i.e. such that for any
t_{0}\in[0,T]
and any
f\in C_{\rm b}(\mathfrak{B})
it holds

\lim_{t\to t_{0}}\int_{\mathfrak{B}}f(x)d\mu_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathfrak{B}}f(x)d\mu_{t_{0}}(x),

see
[
2
, Section 5.1]
.

2.2.
Random operators

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a Banach space. A function
\mathcal{T}:\Omega\times\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{B}
is said to be a random operator if, for any
x\in\mathfrak{B}
,
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,x)\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
. We say that a random operator is continuous if, for any
\omega\in\Omega
,
\mathcal{T}(\omega,\cdot):\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{B}
is continuous. A random operator is compact if, for any
\omega\in\Omega
,
\mathcal{T}(\omega,\cdot):\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{B}
is a compact operator. A random fixed point of random operator
\mathcal{T}
is a random variable
X\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
such that for any
\omega\in\Omega
we have
\mathcal{T}(\omega,X(\omega))=X(\omega)
, in shorthand notation
\mathcal{T}X=X
. We recall here the Bharucha-Reid fixed point theorem (see
[
13
, Corollary 2.2]
), which is a random version of Schauder’s fixed point theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{X}
be a closed convex subset of
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathcal{T}:\Omega\times\mathfrak{X}\to\mathfrak{X}
a continuous compact random operator. Then there exists at least one random fixed point.

2.3.
Hölder spaces

In the following, we denote the elements of
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
either by
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
or by
(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
.
As pointed out for the first time in
[
14
]
, the correct
geometrical framework for the analysis of our problem is a Lie group. For this reason, we endow
\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}
with the group law

(t,z)\mapsto(t_{0},z_{0})\circ(t,z)=(t_{0}+t,x_{0}+x+tv_{0},v_{0}+v),

(2.1)

for every
(t_{0},z_{0})=(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0}),(t,z)=(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}
.
Then
\mathbb{K}:=(\mathbb{R}^{1+2d},\circ)
is a Lie group with identity element
e:=(0,0,0)
and inverse defined by

(t,z)^{-1}:=(-t,-x+tv,-v),\qquad\forall\,(t,z)=(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}.

Additionally, we introduce a suitable dilation group
\{\Phi_{r}\}_{r>0}
defined as

\Phi_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{1+2d},\quad\Phi_{r}(t,z):=(r^{2}t,r^{3}x,rv),\quad\forall r>0,

(2.2)

which respects the intrinsic scaling of problem (
1.6
).
Hence, from now on we shall consider the homogeneous norm in
\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}

associated to the group of dilations
\Phi_{r}
defined in (
2.2
) and given by

\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{K}}:\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}\to[0,\infty),\quad\|(t,z)\|_{\mathbb{K}}:=|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|z\|_{\rm B}:=|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{\frac{1}{3}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}|v_{i}|.

(2.3)

Starting from the traslation (
2.1
) and dilation (
2.2
) group, we are allowed to introduce a left-invariant
1
-homogeneous quasi-distance on
\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
defined as

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{K}}(t,z;\tau,\zeta)=\|(\tau,\zeta)^{-1}\circ(t,z)\|_{\mathbb{K}}.

The quasi-distance
d
is globally equivalent to the control distance of the group and, being a quasi-distance, there exists a structural constant
k>0
such that

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{K}}(t,z;\tau,\zeta)\leq k\left(d_{\mathbb{K}}(t,z;s,y)+d_{\mathbb{K}}(s,y;\tau,\zeta)\right)\qquad\,\,\forall(\tau,\zeta),(t,z),(s,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+2d};

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{K}}(t,z;\tau,\zeta)\leq k\,d_{\mathbb{K}}(\tau,\zeta;t,z)\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\forall(\tau,\zeta)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}.

Furthermore, we are allowed to introduce the associated
d_{\mathbb{K}}
-balls defined as

{\sf B}_{r}(t_{0},z_{0}):=\{(t,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}:d_{\mathbb{K}}(t_{0},z_{0};t,z)<r\},

and, thanks to Remark 1.1 of
[
7
]
, we observe

\displaystyle|{\sf B}_{r}(\tau,\zeta)|=|{\sf B}_{r}(0)|=\omega r^{2+4d},

where
\omega:=|{\sf B}_{1}(0)|>0
and
2+4d
is the
homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}
.

Finally, we recall the definition of anisotropic Hölder spaces
C^{\alpha}_{\rm B}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in(0,3]
, as given in
[
15
, Definition
1.1
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
. We define

\displaystyle C^{\alpha}_{\rm B}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})

\displaystyle:=\left\{\psi_{p,M}\in C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})\,:\,\sup_{z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}|\psi(z)|+\sup_{z_{1},z_{2}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\frac{|\psi(z_{2})-\psi(z_{1})|}{\|z_{2}-z_{1}\|_{\rm B}^{\alpha}}<\infty\right\}.

(2.4)

For
\alpha\in(1,2]
, we define

as the space of functions

(i)

.

2.4.
Lie differentiability

It is known (see for instance
[
15
]
) that we cannot expect the solutions of (
1.6
) to be regular separately in the
t
and
x
variables. In order to handle this regularity issue, we need to exploit the Lie structure introduced in the previous subsection. In the following, we denote by
\mathbb{O}_{d}
and
\mathbb{I}_{d}
respectively the
d\times d
null and identity matrices. We then introduce the
2d\times 2d
constant matrix
B
and the exponential of the group

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{O}_{d}\end{pmatrix},\qquad e^{\tau B}=\mathbb{I}_{2d}+\tau B=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{d}&\tau\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\forall\tau>0.

(2.5)

Remark 2.3
.

We adopt the ordering
(x,v)
in place of
(v,x)
used in
[
15
]
. For this reason,
B
is assumed to be upper triangular instead of lower triangular.

For
f\in C([0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
we define the Lie derivative in
(t,z)\in[0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
as

Yf(t,z):=\lim_{s\to t}\frac{f(s,e^{(s-t)B}z)-f(t,z)}{s-t},

(2.6)

provided that the limit exists. In such a case, we say that
f
is Lie differentiable in
(t,z)
.

Remark 2.4
.

It is readily seen that
Yf=(v\cdot\nabla_{x}+\partial_{t})f
when
f\in C^{1}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
: indeed, we just have to observe that
s\mapsto(s,e^{(s-t)B}z)
is the integral curve of the
drift term

v\cdot\nabla_{x}+\partial_{t}
.

Thanks to the previous remark, we can also introduce a notion of Lie differentiability which is analogous to absolute continuity in the Euclidean setting, as introduced in
[
15
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
I\subseteq[0,+\infty)
be an interval. We denote by
{\rm AC}_{Y}(I;C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
the space of continuous functions
f:I\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
such that
f(t,\cdot)\in C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
for any
t\in I
and there exists a function
Yf\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(I;C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
with the property that for any
s,t\in I

f(s,e^{(s-t)B}z)=f(t,z)+\int_{t}^{s}Yf(\tau,e^{(\tau-t)B}z)d\tau,

(2.7)

where we recall the definition of Bochner-Lebesgue space
L^{1}_{\rm loc}(I;C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
as the space of all measurable functions
g:I\to C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
such that for any compact
K\subset I
it holds

\int_{K}\left\|g(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}dt<\infty.

In such a case,
Yf
is the a.e. Lie derivative of
f
. Clearly, if
I=[0,T]
, then
Yf\in L^{1}(I;C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
.

Remark 2.6
.

Notice that we still use the notation
Yf
in the previous definition. Indeed, if
f\in{\rm AC}_{Y}(I;C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
, then the limit in (
2.6
) exists for a.e.
(t,z)\in I\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
and coincides with the a.e. Lie derivative
Yf
. In the usual Euclidean case, this is similar to the request that
f
is absolutely continuous. However, due to the fact that we are implicitly considering
f:I\to C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and the Banach space
C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
does not satisfy the Radon-Nykodim property (see
[
4
, Definition 1.2.5 and Example 1.2.8.a]
), we would be asking for a stronger property that implies absolute continuity of
C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
-valued functions in the Euclidean setting (see
[
4
, Propositions 1.2.2 and 1.2.3]
). From this point of view, the space
{\rm AC}_{Y}(I;C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
does not characterize
absolute continuous functions with respect to the Lie derivative
Y
, but actually, according to
[
4
, Proposition 1.2.3]
,
C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
-valued absolute continuous functions (on the integral curve of
Y
) that are a.e. Lie differentiable.

We may also consider Hölder spaces with respect to the integral curves of
Y
, as done in
[
15
, Definition 1.2]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
T>0
. We denote by
C_{Y}^{\alpha}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
the space of functions
f:(0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
such that

\left\|f\right\|_{C_{Y}^{\alpha}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,s\in(0,T),\ z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\\
t\not=s\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(s,e^{(s-t)B}z)-f(t,z)|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}<\infty.

Combining the Hölder regularity with respect to
Y
with the anisotropic Hölder spaces previously introduced in this section, we provide the following definition of intrinsic Hölder spaces, as done in
[
15
, Definition 1.3]
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,3]
and
T>0
. We define the intrinsic Hölder space
C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
as follows

(i)

If
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then

C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})=L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\cap C_{Y}^{\alpha/2}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}),

where we recall the definition of the Bochner-Lebesgue space
L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
as the space of measurable functions
f:(0,T)\to C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
such that

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))}:=\esssup_{t\in(0,T)}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}<\infty.

Furthermore, we define

\left\|f\right\|_{C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}:=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))}+\left\|f\right\|_{C_{Y}^{\alpha/2}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}.

(ii)

If
\alpha\in(1,2]
, then
f\in C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
if and only if
f\in L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\cap C_{Y}^{\alpha/2}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and
\nabla_{v}f\in C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha-1}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
, where
\nabla_{v}f
is well-defined since
f(t,\cdot)\in C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
for a.a.
t\in(0,T)
. Furthermore, we define

\left\|f\right\|_{C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}:=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))}+\left\|\nabla_{v}f\right\|_{C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha-1}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}+\left\|f\right\|_{C_{Y}^{\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{2}}}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}.

(iii)

If
\alpha\in(2,3]
, then
f\in C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
if and only if
f\in L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
,
\nabla_{v}f\in C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha-1}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and
Yf\in L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha-2}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
. Furthermore, we define

\left\|f\right\|_{C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}:=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))}+\left\|\nabla_{v}f\right\|_{C_{{\rm B},Y}^{\alpha-1}((0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}+\left\|Yf\right\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);C_{\rm B}^{\alpha-2}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))}.

3.
Existence results for the dual Kolmogorov equation

In this section we prove some auxiliary results regarding existence and uniqueness of strong
Lie solutions to a generalized version of the adjoint problem associated to (
1.6
), that is

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u(t,z)=\Psi(t,z)&(t,z)\in[0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\\
u(T,z)=g(z)&z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
T>0
,
\Psi:[0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
and
g:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
are suitable measurable functions.
In particular, the adjoint Kolmogorov operator
\mathcal{L}
is defined as

\mathcal{L}=\mu\Delta_{v}+Y+F(t,z)\cdot\nabla_{v},\ (t,z)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{1+2d},

where we recall that the Lie derivative
Y
is defined in (
2.6
),
z=(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
and
\mu>0
.
For simplicity, let us introduce the notation

\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}u(t,z)=\mu\Delta_{v}+F(t,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u(t,z)-\Psi(t,z),\ (t,z)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{1+2d},

so that the equation can be rewritten as
Yu=-\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}u
.

Definition 3.1
.

A continuous function
u:[0,T]\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is said to be a strong Lie solution of (
3.1
) if and only if

(i)

\partial_{v_{i}}u,\partial^{2}_{v_{i}\,v_{j}}u\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}([0,T);C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
for
i,j=1,\dots,d
;

(ii)

for any
(t,z)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
we have

u(t,z)=g(z)+\int_{t}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}u(\tau,e^{(\tau-t)B}z)d\tau.

Remark 3.2
.

Such definition is equivalent to the one given in
[
15
, Definition 1.5]
once the final condition
u(T,\cdot)\equiv 0
is prescribed. Furthermore, if
u
is a strong Lie solution of (
3.1
), then
Yu
is defined a.e. and we have
Yu\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}([0,T);C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
, i.e.
u\in{\rm AC}_{Y}([0,T);C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
, see Remark
2.6
. Finally, observe that
u
satisfies (
3.1
) pointwise for a.e.
(t,z)\in(0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
and then it is a classical pointwise solution if
\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}u
is continuous.

As already stated in the introduction of this work, the problem of proving the existence and uniqueness of a solution to (
3.1
) originated from

[
19
]
and its study under mild assumptions for
F
has been of great interest for
the community in recent years, see for instance the recent works
[
7
,
15
]
. Still, to the best of our knowledge, there is no available result considering assumptions

(A_{0})
,
(A_{1})
and
(A_{2})
for the drift
F
, even in the case of constant coefficients for the matrix
A
.
In order to reach our goal, we implement an extension of the parametrix method given in
[
15
]
adapting an idea already
considered in
[
10
]
for parabolic equations to the hypoelliptic setting.
For this purpose, we define the principal part operator
\mathcal{K}
of
\mathcal{L}
as

\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}=\lambda\Delta_{v}+Y,\ (t,z)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{1+2d}.

This is a constant coefficients operator, sharing the same transport term of the operator
\mathcal{L}
and with no lower order terms.
Furthermore,
it is homogeneous with respect to the group dilations
\Phi_{r}
, i.e. if
f
is a solution of
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}f\equiv 0
,
then the function
f_{r}(t,z)=f(r^{2}t,r^{3}x,rv)
satisfies the same equation on a rescaled domain.
Analogously,
f_{(t_{0},z_{0})}(t,z)=f((t_{0},z_{0})\circ(t,z))
still satisfies
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}f_{(t_{0},z_{0})}=0
.
Additionally, if we define
V_{i,\lambda}:=\sqrt{\lambda}\partial_{v_{i}}
, we can rewrite

\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i,\lambda}^{2}+Y.

In particular
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}
satisfies the Hörmander condition, i.e. the Lie algebra generated by
V_{i,\lambda},[V_{i,\lambda},Y]
for
i=1,\dots,d
,
denoted by
{\sf Lie}\{V_{i,\lambda},[V_{i,\lambda},Y],\ i=1,\dots,d\}
, is of full rank
1+2d
, where the definition of Lie brackets is
[X,Y]=XY-YX
.

Now, we are in a position to recall the definition of fundamental solution of the operator
\mathcal{L}
according to
[
15
, Definition 1.6]
.

Definition 3.3
.

A fundamental solution of
\mathcal{L}
on
[0,T]\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
is a function
p=p(s,z;t,y)
defined for
0\leq s<t\leq T
and
z,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
such that:

(1)

p(\cdot,\cdot;t,y)
is a solution of the equation
\mathcal{A}_{\Psi}u+Yu=0
on
(0,T)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
in the sense of Definition
3.1
;

(2)

for every
g\in C_{\rm b}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
we have

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,z)\to(t,y)\\
s<t\end{subarray}}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}p(s,z;t,\eta)g(\eta)\,d\eta=g(y),

i.e. the family of measures
\{p(s,z;t,\eta)d\eta\}_{(s,z)\in[0,t)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}
converges narrowly to
\delta_{y}
as
(s,z)\to(t,y)
.

Analogously, for
s<t
and
z,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
one defines the fundamental solution of
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}
, that from now on will be denoted as
{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}(s,z;t,y)
.
More specifically, given
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}
is a constant coefficient operator, we are able to explicitly write the expression of its fundamental solution
{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}
. Indeed, if we define the covariant matrix associated to
B
, see (
2.5
), as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{0}^{t}e^{\tau B}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{O}_{d}\\
\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}e^{\tau B^{\ast}}d\tau=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{t^{3}}{3}\mathbb{I}_{d}&\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}&\displaystyle t\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix},

then one can explicitly compute its determinant and its inverse as

{\sf det}(\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}(t))=\frac{\lambda^{d}t^{4d}}{(12)^{d}},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}^{-1}(t)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{d}}\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{12}{t^{3}}\mathbb{I}_{d}&\displaystyle-\frac{6}{t^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{d}\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{6}{t^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{d}&\displaystyle\frac{4}{t}\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix},

so that the fundamental solution
{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}
can be expressed as

{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}(s,z;t,y)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(y-e^{(t-s)B}z)^{\sf t}\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}^{-1}(t-s)(y-e^{(t-s)B}z)\right)}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{2d}{\sf det}(\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}(t-s))}},

(3.2)

where with
t
we denote the transpose of a matrix (or vector). In particular, for any fixed
s,t\in[0,T]
with
s<t
and
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
, we have

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}(s,z;t,y)\,dy=1.

(3.3)

Furthermore, it is not difficult to check that

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}(s,z;t,y)\,dz=1.

(3.4)

Indeed, if we write the integral explicitly and we use the change of variables
\zeta=-e^{(t-s)B}z
(whose absolute value of the Jacobian determinant is
1
), we have

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}(s,z;t,y)\,dz=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(y+\zeta)^{\sf t}\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}^{-1}(t-s)(y+\zeta)\right)}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{2d}{\sf det}(\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}(t-s))}}\,d\zeta\\
=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}(s,-e^{-(t-s)B}y;t,\zeta)d\zeta=1.

We also recall the following estimates on
{\bm{P}}^{\mu}
, that are given in
[
15
, Proposition A1]
.

Proposition 3.4
.

For all
\delta,\lambda,T>0
there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
0\leq s<t\leq T
it holds:

\left|\partial_{v_{i}}{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}(s,z;t,y)\right|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{t-s}}{\bm{P}}^{\lambda+\delta}(s,z;t,y).

(3.5)

In
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
the authors proved the existence of a fundamental solution for
\mathcal{L}
under boundedness assumptions for
F
, together with some Gaussian bounds in terms of
{\bm{P}}^{\lambda}
for suitable
\lambda>0
.
Here, our first aim is to extend this result to our set of
assumptions
(A_{0})
,
(A_{1})
and
(A_{2})
, i.e. with a
locally

\alpha
-Hölder continuous
F
having a

growth of order
\beta<\alpha
,
specifically getting rid of the boundedness assumption imposed by
[
15
, Assumption 1.3]
.
Given our results are a refinement of the ones proposed in
[
15
]
obtained via a methodology similar to the one of

[

]
, here we will only report the core computations required to achieve our results, while everything else will be left to the reader under the guidance of
[

]
and
[

]

Theorem 3.5
.

Under Assumptions
(A_{0})
,
(A_{1})
and
(A_{2})
, the operator

has a fundamental solution
p(s,z;t,y)
,

and the constants

, such that

\displaystyle p(s,z;t,y)\leq C\,{\bm{P}}^{\sigma+\varepsilon}(s,z;t,y)

(3.6)

\displaystyle|\partial_{v_{i}}p(s,z;t,y)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{t-s}}{\bm{P}}^{\sigma+\varepsilon}(s,z;t,y)

(3.7)

\displaystyle|\partial^{2}_{v_{i}v_{j}}p(t,z;\mathcal{T},y)|\leq\frac{C}{t-s}{\bm{P}}^{\sigma+\varepsilon}(s,z;t,y)

for any

with

for any

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}p(s,z;t,y)dy=1.

(3.10)

Remark 3.7

Clearly, the result holds true also when considering a more general diffusion governed by a symmetric matrix

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.5

The proof of this result is a variation of both
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
and it is based on the Levy parametrix method, which presents many technicalities.
For this reason, we will only present the proof of this result with unbounded coefficients for our specific case, but bear in mind it is possible to extend it to general Kolmogorov operators with unbounded coefficients.

Following the notation of
[
15
]
, we set our kernel
{\bm{P}}^{\sigma}(s,z;t,y)
as the
parametrix
, whose explicit expression is the one
introduced in (
3.2
). Once the parametrix function is introduced, the Levy parametrix method prescribes that a fundamental solution to
\mathcal{L}
is of the form

\displaystyle p(s,z;t,y)={\bf P}^{\sigma}(s,z;t,y)+\int\limits_{s}^{t}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}{\bf P}^{\sigma}(s,z;\tau,\eta)\varphi(\tau,\eta;t,y)d\eta d\tau,

(3.11)

where
\varphi
is an unknown function. Now, if we assume
p
is indeed the fundamental solution to
\mathcal{L}
, then we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{L}p(s,z;t,y)=\mathcal{L}{\bf P}^{\sigma}(s,z;t,y)+\mathcal{L}\int\limits_{s}^{t}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}{\bf P}^{\sigma}(s,z;\tau,\eta)\varphi(\tau,\eta;t,y)d\eta d\tau.

Now we rewrite
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}+F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}+Y
and we recall that
(\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}+Y)\mathbf{P}^{\sigma}=0
. Hence
\varphi
is the solution of the Volterra equation

\displaystyle\varphi(s,z;t,y)=F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}{\bf P}^{\sigma}(s,z;t,y)+\int\limits_{s}^{t}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}{\bf P}^{\sigma}(s,z;\tau,\eta)\varphi(\tau,\eta;t,y)d\eta d\tau,

which can be determined through an iterative procedure that leads to the following Neumann series representation

\displaystyle\varphi(s,z;t,y)=\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\varphi_{k}(s,z;t,y),

(3.12)

where

\begin{cases}\varphi_{1}(s,z;t,y)=F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}{\bm{P}}^{\sigma}(s,z;t,y),\\[20.0pt]
\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(s,z;t,y)=\int\limits_{s}^{t}\int\limits_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}{\bm{P}}^{\sigma}(s,z;t,y)\,\varphi_{k}(\tau,\eta;t,y)d\eta d\tau,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

(3.13)

Now, for the sake of brevity, we assume
\Psi\equiv 0
bearing in mind that nothing in the proof would change if this was not the case, and we would have to proceed
exactly as in
[
15
]
to estimate the terms related to it.
In order to complete the proof of our result, we have to show that:

(1)

the series (
3.12
) is uniformly convergent on compact subsets of
D_{T}:=\{(s,z;t,y)\,:\,0<s<t<T\}

(see forthcoming Subsection
3.1.1
); moreover, upper bounds and Hölder estimates for
\varphi
hold,

\displaystyle\|z\|_{B}^{\beta}

\displaystyle|v|^{\beta}

Let us fix a compact set

(3.48)

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

We first prove uniqueness of the solutions and then we move to the existence statement. To do this, we need to better understand the relation between the solutions of (
3.45
) and the ones of (
1.6
) in
[0,T]
. This is explained in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
hold. For any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
, let
u:[0,T]\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
be the unique bounded strong Lie solution of (
3.45
), that exists by Corollary
3.14
, and let
\mu
be any solution of (
1.6
). Then

t\in[0,T]\mapsto\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u(t,z)d\mu_{t}(z)

is Lipschitz continuous and for any
t\in[0,T]
it holds

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u(t,z)d\mu_{t}(z)=-\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\psi(z)d\mu_{s}(z)ds.

(4.1)

Proof.

Let
u
be the bounded strong Lie solution of (
3.45
) and extend it by setting
u(t,z)=0
for any
t>T
and
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
let
u_{\varepsilon}=\rho_{\varepsilon}\,\ast\,u
. Notice that
u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}\left(\left(\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2},+\infty\right)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\right)
and that, by (
3.49
) and Lemma
A.2
,

\left\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\left(\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2},\infty\right)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\right)}+\left\|\nabla_{x}u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\left(\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2},\infty\right)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\right)}\\
+\left\|\Delta_{v}u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\left(\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2},\infty\right)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\right)}\leq C\left\|\psi\right\|_{L^{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}.

(4.2)

Furthermore, by (
3.48
) and the fact that the convolution commutes with
\nabla_{v}
, we get

\left\|\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\left(\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2},\infty\right)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\right)}\leq C_{u}C_{\beta}(\psi)

(4.3)

for any fixed
\beta\in(0,\alpha)
. We underline that this bound is independent of
\varepsilon>0
. In particular, this means that for fixed
s\in\left(\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2},T\right]
,
u_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\in{\sf A}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
with bounds that are independent of
s
.

Let us now prove that, for fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

t\in\left(\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2},T\right]\mapsto\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)d\mu_{t}

is Lipschitz-continuous, with Lipschitz constant depending on
\varepsilon>0
.

In order to do this, we rewrite the difference as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}&u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)d\mu_{t}-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)d\mu_{s}\\
&=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}(u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)-u_{\varepsilon}(s,z))d\mu_{t}+\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)d(\mu_{t}-\mu_{s})\\
&=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(h,z)d\mu_{t}(z)\,dh+\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)d(\mu_{s}-\mu_{t})(z),\end{split}

(4.4)

where the first integral on the right-hand side is well-posed by (
4.2
). Furthermore, since
u_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\in{\sf A}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
we can use (
1.4
) to rewrite the second integral on the right-hand side of (
4.4
) as follows

\displaystyle\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)d\mu_{t}-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)d\mu_{s}=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(h,z)d\mu_{t}(z)\,dh

(4.5)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)+F(t,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)\right)\,d\mu_{h}(z)\,dh.

Taking the absolute value on both sides and using the aforementioned bounds, we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)d\mu_{t}-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)d\mu_{s}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\int_{s}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(h,z)|d\mu_{t}(z)\,dh

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left|v\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)+F(t,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)\right|d\mu_{h}(z)\,dh

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C\left(|t-s|+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}|v|d\mu_{h}(z)dh\right)\leq C|t-s|

where we used that
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
and thus its first moments are uniformly bounded. Furthermore, using again (
4.5
), it is clear that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\,t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)d\mu_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)+F(t,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)\right)d\mu_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(Yu_{\varepsilon}(t,z)+F(t,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)\right)d\mu_{t}(z).

Then, integrating over
[t,T]
for
t>\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(T,z)\,d\mu_{T}(z)-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u_{\varepsilon}(t,z)\,d\mu_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(Yu_{\varepsilon}(s,z)+F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)\right)d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds.

First of all, we observe that as
\varepsilon\to 0
the left-hand side converges to

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u(T,z)\,d\mu_{T}(z)-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u(t,z)\,d\mu_{t}(z)=-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u(t,z)\,d\mu_{t}(z)

by the dominated convergence theorem, since by definition and (
3.48
)

\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\left(\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2},+\infty\right)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\right)}\leq\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}((0,+\infty)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}\leq C_{u}C_{\beta}(\psi).

For the right hand side, we rewrite

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(Yu_{\varepsilon}(s,z)+F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)\right)d\mu_{s}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(Yu_{\varepsilon}(s,z)+(\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast(F\cdot\nabla_{v}u))(s,z)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)\right)d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)-(\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast(F\cdot\nabla_{v}u))(s,z)\right)d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast\left(Yu+F\cdot\nabla_{v}u+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}u\right)\right)(s,z)d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)-(\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast(F\cdot\nabla_{v}u))(s,z)\right)d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast\psi)(s,z)d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{\varepsilon}(s,z)\,d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds-\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast(F\cdot\nabla_{v}u)(s,z)\,d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds,

where in the second last equality we used Lemma
A.4
while in the last one we used Remark
3.2
. It is clear that, by the dominated convergence theorem,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast\psi)(s,z)d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\psi(z)d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds,

hence we only need to prove that the second and third integral converge to the same value. To do this, one has simply to apply Lemma
A.3
with
m=d
to the bounded Caratheodory function
\nabla_{v}u
and the sublinear one
F
, by
(A_{2})
, in the second integral, and with
m=1
to the bounded Caratheodory function
1
and the sublinear one
F\cdot\nabla_{v}u
in the third one. Notice that we need estimate (
3.48
) to guarantee that
\nabla_{v}u
is bounded (and
F\cdot\nabla_{v}u
is sublinear by
(A_{2})
) and then the lemma can be applied. The Lipschitz property and the fact that (
4.1
) holds up to
t=0
follow immediately. ∎

Now we are ready to prove the following uniqueness result.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
, Equation (
1.6
) admits at most one solution.

Proof.

Let
\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}\in C({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
be two solutions of (
1.6
) and consider
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
. By Lemma
4.1
, for any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
, we have

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}u(t,z)d\mu^{j}_{t}(z)=-\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\psi(z)d\mu^{j}_{s}(z)ds,\ j=1,2,

where
u
is the unique bounded strong Lie solution of (
3.45
). Subtracting term by term and evaluating at
0
, we get

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\psi(z)d(\mu^{1}_{s}-\mu^{2}_{s})(z)ds=0,

whence the thesis by arbitrariness of
\psi
. This clearly applies also to global solutions, by arguing with an arbitrary
T>0
. ∎

To prove the existence of a solution, we need, instead, to relate the strong solutions of (
1.7
) with the ones of (
1.6
). This is established in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
hold and let
(X,V)\in L^{1}(\Omega;C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
be a strong solution of (
1.7
). Then the flow of probability measures
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{1}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
, where
\bm{\mu}={\rm Law}(X,V)
, solves (
1.6
).

Proof.

Let
Z=(X,V)
be a strong solution of (
1.7
) and
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
. By Itô’s formula (see
[
20
, Theorem IV.3.3]
) we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi(Z(t))&=\psi(Z(0))\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\left(V(s)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi(Z(s))+F(s,Z(s))\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi(Z(s))+\sigma\Delta_{v}\psi(Z(s))\right)ds\\
&+\sqrt{2\sigma}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{v}\psi(Z(s))\,dB(s)\end{split}

(4.6)

Since
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
, it is clear that
\nabla_{v}\psi
is bounded and then (see
[
16
, Theorem 3.2.1]
)

{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{v}\psi(Z(s))\,dB(s)\right]=0.

(4.7)

Furthermore, observe that, given
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and Assumption
(A_{1})
,

\left|V(s)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi(Z(s))+F(s,Z(s))\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi(Z(s))+\sigma\Delta_{v}\psi(Z(s))\right|\leq C(1+\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}|Z(s)|),

where
C
is a constant depending on
\psi
,
C(F)
and
\sigma
. Since it is clear that

{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left|V(s)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi(Z(s))+F(s,Z(s))\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi(Z(s))+\sigma\Delta_{v}\psi(Z(s))\right|\,ds\right]\\
\leq CT\left(1+{\sf E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}|Z(s)|\right]\right)<\infty,

we can use Fubini’s theorem to get

{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left(V(s)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi(Z(s))+F(s,Z(s))\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi(Z(s))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}\psi(Z(s))\right)\,ds\right]\\
=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi(z)+F(s,z)\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi(z)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{v}\psi(z)\right)\,d\mu_{s}(z)\,ds.

(4.8)

Finally, taking the expectation on both sides of (
4.6
) and using (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), we get (
1.4
).
∎

Now we are finally ready to prove the main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
(X_{0},V_{0})\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
be independent of
B
and such that
{\rm Law}(X_{0},V_{0})=\mu_{0}
. To prove that (
1.7
) admits a strong solution, we need to consider two cases:

Case 1:
F
is bounded.
Assume that
F\in L^{\infty}([0,+\infty)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
.
Fix
T>0
and let
\mathcal{T}:\Omega\times C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})\to C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
be defined as follows: for any
\omega\in\Omega
and
(X,V)\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
, we set

\mathcal{T}(\omega,X,V)=(\mathcal{T}_{X}(\omega,X,V),\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X,V))

where for any
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{X}(\omega,X,V)(t)

\displaystyle=X_{0}(\omega)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X,V)(s)\,ds

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X,V)(t)

\displaystyle=V_{0}(\omega)+\int_{0}^{t}F(s,X(s),V(s))\,ds+\sqrt{2\sigma}B(\omega,t).

For fixed
(X,V)\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
,
\mathcal{T}_{V}(\cdot,X,V)
is sum of measurable functions of
\omega\in\Omega
while and
\mathcal{T}_{X}(\cdot,X,V)
is the sum of a measurable function of
\omega
with the Riemann integral of a continuous stochastic process, hence
\mathcal{T}
is a random operator. Now we want to prove that
\mathcal{T}
is a continuous and compact random operator.

We first prove that
\mathcal{T}
is a continuous random operator. To do this, fix
\omega\in\Omega
and consider any sequence
(X_{n},V_{n})\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
converging towards
(X,V)\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
. Then we have for any
t\in[0,T]

\left|\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X,V)(t)-\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X_{n},V_{n})(t)\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}|F(s,X(s),V(s))-F(s,X_{n}(s),V_{n}(s))|\,ds.

Taking the supremum over
[0,T]
, we get

\left\|\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X,V)-\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X_{n},V_{n})\right\|_{L^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq\int_{0}^{T}|F(s,X(s),V(s))-F(s,X_{n}(s),V_{n}(s))|\,ds.

Recalling that
F
is bounded and continuous in the second variable by Assumption
(A_{0})
, we can use the dominated convergence theorem to achieve

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left\|\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X,V)-\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X_{n},V_{n})\right\|_{L^{\infty}[0,T]}=0.

Once this is done, it is also clear that
\mathcal{T}_{X}(\omega,X_{n},V_{n})
converges towards
\mathcal{T}_{X}(\omega,X,V)
. This proves the continuity of
\mathcal{T}(\omega,\cdot,\cdot)
for any
\omega\in\Omega
.

Next, we need to prove that
\mathcal{T}
is a compact random operator. To do this we will show that for fixed
\omega\in\Omega
the range of
\mathcal{T}(\omega,\cdot,\cdot)
is composed of equibounded and equi-Hölder functions. Indeed, in such a case, by the Ascoli-Arzelá theorem we know that
\mathcal{T}
is compact random operator.

So, first,
we prove that for any
\omega\in\Omega
there exists a constant
M(\omega)
such that

\left\|\mathcal{T}(\omega,X,V)\right\|_{L^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq M(\omega),\ \quad\forall(X,V)\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}).

(4.9)

Indeed, we set

M_{B}(\omega):=\max_{t\in[0,T]}|B(\omega,t)|

(4.10)

and observing that for any
t\in[0,T]
and any
(X,V)\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
we have

|\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X,V)(t)|\leq|V_{0}(\omega)|+T\left\|F\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}+\sqrt{2\sigma}M_{B}(\omega)=:M_{V}(\omega).

Furthermore,

|\mathcal{T}_{X}(\omega,X,V)(t)|\leq|X_{0}(\omega)|+TM_{V}(\omega)=:M_{X}(\omega).

Hence (
4.9
) holds with
M(\omega)=\sqrt{(M_{V}(\omega))^{2}+(M_{X}(\omega))^{2}}
.

Now we prove the equi-Hölder condition, i.e. we show that for any
\omega\in\Omega
and for any
\gamma<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
there exists a constant
L_{\gamma}(\omega)
such that, for all
(X,V)\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and
t,s\in[0,T]
,

|\mathcal{T}(\omega,X,V)(t)-\mathcal{T}(\omega,X,V)(s)|\leq L_{\gamma}(\omega)|t-s|^{\gamma}.

(4.11)

To do this, let

L_{B,\gamma}(\omega)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,s\in[0,T]\\
t\not=s\end{subarray}}\frac{|B(\omega,t)-B(\omega,s)|}{|t-s|^{\gamma}},

(4.12)

which is finite since
B
is locally Hölder continuous of order
\gamma<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
(see
[
20
, Theorem I.2.2]
). Hence, for any
t,s\in[0,T]
and
(X,V)\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})

|\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X,V)(t)-\mathcal{T}_{V}(\omega,X,V)(s)|\leq\left\|F\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}|t-s|+\sqrt{2\sigma}L_{B,\gamma}(\omega)|t-s|^{\gamma}.

We set

L_{V,\gamma}(\omega)=\sqrt{2\sigma}L_{B,\gamma}(\omega)+\left\|F\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}T^{1-\gamma}.

Next, observe that

|\mathcal{T}_{X}(\omega,X,V)(t)-\mathcal{T}_{X}(\omega,X,V)(s)|\leq M_{V}(\omega)|t-s|\leq M_{V}(\omega)T^{1-\gamma}|t-s|^{\gamma}=:L_{X,\gamma}(\omega)|t-s|^{\gamma}.

We obtain (
4.11
) by setting
L_{\gamma}(\omega)=\sqrt{(L_{V,\gamma}(\omega))^{2}+(L_{X,\gamma}(\omega))^{2}}
.

Once we established that
\mathcal{T}
is a continuous and compact random operator, by the Bharucha-Reid Fixed Point Theorem
2.1
, we know that there exists a stochastic process
(X,V)\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
such that
\mathcal{T}(X,V)=(X,V)
, i.e.
(X,V)
is a solution of (
1.7
).

Now set
\bm{\mu}={\rm Law}(X,V)
. Let us show that
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{1}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
. To do this, recall that

V(t)=V_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F(s,X(s),V(s))\,ds+\sqrt{2\sigma}B(t)

and then

|V(t)|\leq|V_{0}|+\left\|F\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}T+\sqrt{2\sigma}M_{B}.

(4.13)

By Doob’s maximal inequality (see
[
20
, Theorem II.1.7]
) we have

{\sf E}\left[M_{B}\right]\leq\sqrt{{\sf E}\left[M_{B}^{2}\right]}\leq 2\sqrt{T},

(4.14)

hence, taking the supremum over
[0,T]
and the expectation in (
4.13
), we get

{\sf E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|V(t)|\right]\leq M_{1}(\mu_{0})+\left\|F\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})}T+\sqrt{8\sigma T}=:H_{1,V}(\mu_{0},T,F).

(4.15)

Furthermore, we have

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X(t)|\leq|X_{0}|+T\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|V(t)|

so that, taking the expectation,

{\sf E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X(t)|\right]\leq M_{1}(\mu_{0})+TH_{1,V}(\mu_{0},T,F)=:H_{1,X}(\mu_{0},T,F).

(4.16)

Setting
H_{1}(\mu_{0},T,F)=H_{1,X}(\mu_{0},T,F)+H_{1,V}(\mu_{0},T,F)
we have

{\sf E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|(X(t),V(t))|\right]\leq{\sf E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X(t)|+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|V(t)|\right]\leq H_{1}(\mu_{0},T,F),

(4.17)

that proves
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{1}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
. By Proposition
4.3
we know that
\bm{\mu}
solves (
1.6
). In particular, by Proposition
4.2
,
\bm{\mu}
is the unique solution of (
1.6
).

Hence, in particular, if we consider two solutions
(X_{1},V_{1})
and
(X_{2},V_{2})
with laws respectively
\bm{\mu}^{1}
and
\bm{\mu}^{2}
, then both flows of probability measures belong to
C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
and solve (
1.6
), hence
\bm{\mu}^{1}=\bm{\mu}^{2}
and uniqueness in law holds. Since we have both strong existence and uniqueness in law, by a well-known result by Cherny (see
[
9

), we get that the strong solution of (

) is pathwise unique. Once we established existence of local solutions and pathwise uniqueness, the fact that

is arbitrary guarantees that the solution is global.

Case 2:
F
is unbounded.
Consider a sequence of cut-off functions
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
with support on the ball

of
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}

of strong solutions of (

) with nonlinearity

. Define the sequence of stopping times

First, let us prove that this sequence of stopping times is almost surely non-decreasing. To do this, consider

and define the events

E_{m}:=\{(X_{0},V_{0})\in B_{m}\},\qquad E_{m}^{c}:=\Omega\setminus E_{m}.

Consider then

First observe that on

, clearly,
\tau_{m}=0
. Hence

{\sf P}(\tau_{m}\leq\tau_{n}|E_{m}^{c})=1.

We only need to prove that

(4.18)

To do this, consider the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\sf P}(\cdot\mid E_{m}))
. Then, since

is still a Brownian motion in this probability space,
(X_{m},V_{m})
and
(X_{n},V_{n})
are still strong solutions of (
1.7
) with nonlinearity
F_{m}
and
F_{n}
. By continuity we know that
\tau_{m},\tau_{n}>0
almost surely. Furthermore, for

(X_{m},V_{m})
and
(X_{n},V_{n})
both satisfy (

F

. Hence, by pathwise uniqueness, the two solutions must coincide up to
\min\{\tau_{m},\tau_{n}\}
. However, by continuity, we get

|(X_{n}(\tau_{m}),V_{n}(\tau_{m}))|=|(X_{m}(\tau_{m}),V_{m}(\tau_{m}))|=m
, that implies
(X_{n}(\tau_{m}),V_{n}(\tau_{m}))\in B_{n}
. Thus
\tau_{n}\geq\tau_{m}
a.s. given
E_{m}
and then (
4.18
) follows.

Since
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is non-decreasing, we can consider the stopping time
\tau_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{n}
. Now we want to prove that
\tau_{\infty}=\infty
a.s. To do this, fix

and observe that

\displaystyle|V_{n}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq|V_{0}|+\int_{0}^{t}|F(s,Z_{n}(s))||\eta_{n}(Z_{n}(s))|ds+\sqrt{2\sigma}|B(t)|

\displaystyle\leq|V_{0}|+C(F)t+C(F)\int_{0}^{t}|X_{n}(s)|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}ds+C(F)\int_{0}^{t}|V_{n}(s)|^{\beta}ds+\sqrt{2\sigma}|B(t)|.

Since

to the first integrand and with
p=\frac{1}{\beta}
to the second integrand. This leads to

\displaystyle|V_{n}(t)|\leq|V_{0}|+C(F)\left(3-\frac{4\beta}{3}\right)t+C(F)\beta\int_{0}^{t}(|X_{n}(s)|+|V_{n}(s)|)ds+\sqrt{2\sigma}\max_{0\leq s\leq t}|B(s)|.

hence

\displaystyle|Z_{n}(t)|\leq C\left(|Z_{0}|+t+\int_{0}^{t}|Z_{n}(s)|ds+\max_{0\leq s\leq t}|B(s)|\right),

where the constant

only depends on

\beta
and
\sigma

\displaystyle|Z_{n}(t)|\leq C\left(|Z_{0}|+t+\max_{0\leq s\leq t}|B(s)|\right)e^{Ct}.

In particular, for

,

\displaystyle n\leq C\left(|Z_{0}|+\tau_{n}+\max_{0\leq s\leq\tau_{n}}|B(s)|\right)e^{C\tau_{n}}.

Now assume by contradiction that
{\sf P}(\tau_{\infty}<\infty)>0
and consider any
\omega

and

\omega

, we achieve

\displaystyle\infty\leq C\left(|Z_{0}|+\tau_{\infty}(\omega)+\max_{0\leq s\leq\tau_{\infty}(\omega)}|B(s,\omega)|\right)e^{C\tau_{\infty}(\omega)}<\infty,

that is absurd. Hence
\tau_{\infty}=\infty
almost surely. Set also, for convenience,
\tau_{0}=0
. Now define, for any
t\geq 0

N(t)=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ t\leq\tau_{n}\},

that is well-defined since
\tau_{n}\to\infty
almost surely. Recall that, by pathwise uniqueness, we have
(X_{m}(t),V_{m}(t))=(X_{n}(t),V_{n}(t))
for

whenever

. Hence, the process

X(t)=X_{N(t)}(t),\ V(t)=V_{N(t)}(t),\ \forall\,t\geq 0

is well-defined. It remains to prove that
(X,V)
is a solution to (
1.7

we have

\displaystyle V(t)

\displaystyle=V_{N(t)}(t)=V_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F_{N(t)}(s,Z_{N(t)}(s))ds+\sigma B(t)

\displaystyle=V_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F(s,Z_{N(s)}(s))ds+\sigma B(t)

\displaystyle=V_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F(s,Z(s))ds+\sigma B(t).

This proves that
(X,V)
is the unique strong solution of (
1.7

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Z(t)|\leq C\left(|Z_{0}|+T+M_{B}\right)e^{CT}

and taking the expectation we have

{\sf E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Z(t)|\right]\leq C\left({\sf E}[|Z_{0}|]+T+\sqrt{T}\right)e^{CT}<\infty.

Hence, if we set
\bm{\mu}:={\rm Law}(X,V)
, we have
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{1}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
. By Proposition
4.3

\bm{\mu}

1.6

∎
