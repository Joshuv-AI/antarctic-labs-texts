Metrics for Bayesian Optimal Experiment Design under Model Misspecification
Tommie A. Catanach & Niladri Das

Abstract
The conventional approach to Bayesian decision-theoretic experiment design involves searching over possible experiments to select a design that maximizes the expected value of a specified utility function. The expectation is over the joint distribution of all unknown variables implied by the statistical model that will be used to analyze the collected data. The utility function defines the objective of the experiment where a common utility function is the information gain. This article introduces an expanded framework for this process, where we go beyond the traditional Expected Information Gain criteria and introduce the Expected General Information Gain which measures robustness to the model discrepancy and Expected Discriminatory Information as a criterion to quantify how well an experiment can detect model discrepancy. The functionality of the framework is showcased through its application to a scenario involving a linearized spring mass damper system and an F-16 model where the model discrepancy is taken into account while doing Bayesian optimal experiment design.



I
Introduction

For science and engineering systems there are often many choices of experiments to run or data to collect in order to infer information. Each of these choices has different costs in terms of time, money, or other constraints. One common solution to this problem stems from the field of Bayesian optimal experimental design (BOED). This approach uses the rigor of the Bayesian paradigm and information theory to formalize the design of experiments and treats it as an optimization problem. Concretely, the aim is to maximize a utility function that captures the worth of a particular experimental design. This utility function, typically the Expected Information Gain (EIG), depends on the posterior distribution sampled over many hypothetical realizations of plausible datasets from the experiment. However, for real applications, where there is the model discrepancy, EIG might not be the only relevant measure of information we should consider.

In this work, we consider two additional criteria that measure notions of the robustness of the design. The first criterion, Expected Generalized Information Gain (EGIG), captures the expected information gained (or lost) when an experimenter uses a model with discrepancy. The second criterion, Expected Discriminatory Information (EDI) reflects whether the information gained from an experiment would be sufficient to discriminate between the model and an alternative. The EGIG-based design seeks to mitigate discrepancy while the EDI-based seeks to only detect it. With these criteria, we aim to correct pathological issues in BOED and advance the BOED literature, which has a relatively few works concerning the robustness of BOED.

II
Modeling and Key Concepts

II-A

System Description

Because of the difficulty of BOED, we will study this problem in the context of simplified models, specifically stationary discrete-time linear processes driven by Gaussian noise. We define the state vector as
\bm{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{A}\bm{x}_{t-1}+\bm{\eta}_{t},\quad\quad(t=1,2,...).

(1)

\bm{A}
is an
n\times n
transition matrix and
\bm{\eta}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{Q})
is the process noise where
\bm{Q}\succeq 0
. We assume
\bm{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0})
. For simplicity, unless specified we will take
\bm{\mu_{0}}=0
.

The observation equation is

\bm{y}_{t}=\bm{H}\bm{x}_{t}+\bm{v}_{t},

(2)

where the measurements are
\bm{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
,
\bm{H}
is the measurement matrix and
\bm{v}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{R})
where

\bm{R}\succ\bm{0}
. The random vectors
\{\bm{x}_{0},\bm{\eta}_{1},...,\bm{\eta}_{t},\bm{v}_{1},...,\bm{v}_{t}\}
are assumed to all be independent.

From this general case, we will study two simplifications. First, we consider a system without dynamics (or equivalently a single time step of the system), corresponding:
\bm{y}=\bm{H}\bm{x}+\bm{v}
. Second, we will study the system after it has converged to its stationary distribution, assuming that
\bm{A}
is asymptotically stable. In this case, if
t
is sufficiently large, we have that
\bm{x}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{L})
, where
\bm{\Sigma_{L}}
is the solution to the discrete Lyapunov equation
\bm{\Sigma_{L}}=\bm{A}\bm{\Sigma_{L}}\bm{A^{T}}+\bm{Q}
.

II-B

Bayesian Inference

In Bayesian inference to rigorously update our beliefs about
\bm{X}
with observation data
\bm{Y}
, we apply Bayes’ theorem,

p\left(\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y}\right)=\frac{p\left(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X}\right)p\left(\bm{X}\right)}{p\left(\bm{Y}\right)}.

(3)

The prior
p\left(\bm{X}\right)
reflects our initial beliefs about
\bm{X}
while
p\left(\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y}\right)
is our posterior (after observations) belief. The likelihood,
p\left(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X}\right)
is the probability of observing
\bm{Y}
given a state
\bm{X}
, while
p\left(\bm{Y}\right)
is the overall probability of observing the data given our prior (called the evidence). Often we are interested in measuring how informative is the data. To do this we measure our change in belief, i.e. the information gain, using the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence,

\text{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[p\left(\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y}\right)||p\left(\bm{X}\right)\right]=\int p\left(\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y}\right)\log\frac{p\left(\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y}\right)}{p\left(\bm{X}\right)}d\bm{X}

(4)

For the Gaussian case where
p\left(\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mu_{1}},\bm{\Sigma_{1}}\right)
and
p\left(\bm{X}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mu_{0}},\bm{\Sigma_{0}}\right)
the KL divergence is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\text{Tr}\left[\bm{\Sigma_{0}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma_{1}}\right]-n+\left(\bm{\mu_{1}}-\bm{\mu_{0}}\right)^{T}\bm{\Sigma_{0}}^{-1}\left(\bm{\mu_{1}}-\bm{\mu_{0}}\right)+\log\frac{\mid\bm{\Sigma_{0}}\mid}{\mid\bm{\Sigma_{1}}\mid}).

(5)

The KL divergence can be generalized using a more expressive, yet still information theoretically valid, measure of information
[
4
]
defined over three distributions:
r(\bm{X})
,
p(\bm{X})
, and
q(\bm{X})
given by

\mathcal{I}_{r(\bm{X})}[p(\bm{X})\mid\mid q(\bm{X})]=\int r\left(\bm{X}\right)\log\frac{p\left(\bm{X}\right)}{q\left(\bm{X}\right)}d\bm{X}.

(6)

The interpretation of this form of information is that we want to measure a change in belief (e.g. information gained or lost) when updating from

q\left(\bm{X}\right)
to
p\left(\bm{X}\right)
in the view of
r\left(\bm{X}\right)
. The view defines our reference frame for assessing changes in information. Typically, both
r\left(\bm{X}\right)
and
p\left(\bm{X}\right)
would be the posterior and
q\left(\bm{X}\right)
the prior, recovering the KL divergence. However, in the case where there is a model discrepancy,
r\left(\bm{X}\right)
could be the unknown posterior from the true model, while
p\left(\bm{X}\right)
could the inferred posterior from the model with discrepancy. Therefore, we could measure whether inference with the model discrepancy is still getting close to the correct result. We note that unlike the KL divergence this measure can be negative meaning that
q\left(\bm{X}\right)
provides more information about
r\left(\bm{X}\right)
than
p\left(\bm{X}\right)
does.

For the case where,
r(\bm{X})
,
p(\bm{X})
, and
q(\bm{X})
are all described by multivariate Gaussians,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{r(\bm{X})}[p(\bm{X})\mid\mid q(\bm{X})]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\text{Tr}[(\bm{\Sigma_{q}}^{-1}-\bm{\Sigma_{p}}^{-1})\bm{\Sigma_{r}}]-\left(\bm{\mu_{r}}-\bm{\mu_{p}}\right)^{T}\bm{\Sigma_{p}}^{-1}\left(\bm{\mu_{r}}-\bm{\mu_{p}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\bm{\mu_{r}}-\bm{\mu_{q}}\right)^{T}\bm{\Sigma_{q}}^{-1}\left(\bm{\mu_{r}}-\bm{\mu_{q}}\right)+\log\frac{\mid\bm{\Sigma_{q}}\mid}{\mid\bm{\Sigma_{p}}\mid}\bigg).

(7)

This uses the fact that Eq.
6
can be expressed as the difference of two KL divergences and employing Eq.
5
.

II-C

Bayesian Filtering

For a Markov process where the state
\bm{x_{t}}
only depends on
\bm{x_{t-1}}
and the observation
\bm{y_{t}}
only depends on
\bm{x_{t}}
we can simplify the inference problem for the state
\bm{x_{t}}
given a time series of observations
\bm{Y_{t}}=\{\bm{y_{0}}\dots\bm{y_{t}}\}
as

p\left(\bm{x_{t}}\mid\bm{Y_{t}}\right)=\frac{p\left(\bm{y_{t}}\mid\bm{x_{t}}\right)p\left(\bm{x_{t}}\mid\bm{Y_{t-1}}\right)}{p\left(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{Y_{t-1}}\right)}.

(8)

Using this, the Bayesian filter for the system described by Eq.(
1
)-(
2
), is the Kalman filter,

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}\bm{\mu}_{t-1|t-1}

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}\bm{\Sigma}_{t-1|t-1}\bm{A}^{T}+\bm{Q}

(10)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t|t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\bm{K}_{t}(\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})

(11)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t}

\displaystyle=(\bm{I}-\bm{K}_{t}\bm{H})\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}.

(12)

where
\bm{K}_{t}=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\bm{H}^{T}\bm{S_{t}}^{-1}
is the Kalman gain matrix and
\bm{S_{t}}=\bm{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\bm{H}^{T}+\bm{R}
is the predictive uncertainty.
Considering a single time step, the
a-priori
estimator of
\bm{x}_{t}
is
\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}
with covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}
. The
a-posteriori
estimator of
\bm{x}_{t}
is
\bm{\mu}_{t|t}
with covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t}
. Therefore, the prior, posterior, and evidence are

\displaystyle p(\bm{x}_{t})

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1},\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}),

(13)

\displaystyle p(\bm{x}_{t}\ |\ \bm{y}_{t},\bm{d})

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t},\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t}),

(14)

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}_{t}\ |\ \bm{d})

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1},\bm{S_{t}}).

(15)

As we can see from Eq.
9
-
12
, only the means
\bm{\mu}
depend on the observations
\bm{y}
. Thus, when
\bm{A}
is asymptotically stable we can find the stationary distribution of
\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t}
. Here we define
\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\rightarrow\bm{\Gamma}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t}\rightarrow\bm{\Sigma}_{D}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
We do this by first using the discrete time algebraic Riccati equation (DARE) given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}=\bm{A}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{A}^{T}+\bm{Q}-\bm{A}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{H}^{T}(\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{H}^{T}+\bm{R})^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{A}^{T}

(16)

and then solve for
\bm{\Sigma}_{D}
via

\bm{\Sigma}_{D}=\bm{\Gamma}-\bm{\Gamma}\bm{H}^{T}(\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{H}^{T}+\bm{R})^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}.

(17)

II-D

Bayesian Optimal Experimental Design

In BOED, the first step to modeling the problem is to define a utility function
U(\bm{d})
that gives the value of performing an experiment at
\bm{d}\in\mathcal{D}
. The set
\bm{d}\in\mathcal{D}
spans the space of possible designs. In Bayesian design, the utility is a function of the posterior distribution
p(\bm{X}\ |\ \bm{d},\bm{Y})
. The utility function is maximized to find the optimal design
\bm{d}^{*}
, i.e.
\bm{d}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{d}\in\mathcal{D}}U(\bm{d})
.

The choice of the utility function
U(\bm{d})
is crucial, as different functions will usually lead to different optimal designs
[
8
]
. One of the most principled choices often used in BOED is the mutual information. This is the information gained about
\bm{X}
by taking measurements,
\bm{Y}
, according to design
\bm{d}
. This is just the KL divergence from the prior to posterior,
\text{D}_{\text{KL}}[p(\bm{X}\ |\ \bm{Y},\bm{d})||p(\bm{X})]
, Eq.
4
.

However at the point of choosing
\bm{d}
, we do not have the measurements. Thus, in order to access the effectiveness of the design
\bm{d}
, we take the expected KL divergence over plausible data sets
p(\bm{Y}\ |\bm{d})
. This utility function is known as the Expected Information Gain (EIG) and is defined as,

\displaystyle\text{EIG}(\bm{d})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{Y}\ |\bm{d})}[\text{D}_{\text{KL}}[p(\bm{X}\ |\ \bm{Y},\bm{d})||p(\bm{X})]]

\displaystyle=\int p(\bm{X},\bm{Y}\ |\ \bm{d})\log\frac{p(\bm{X}\ |\ \bm{Y},\bm{d})}{p(\bm{X})}d\bm{X}d\bm{Y}.

(18)

Bayesian Optimal Experimental Design Criteria

III-A

Expected Information Gain

For the linear Gaussian model given by Eq.(
1

2

Single Step Update
: First for the case of a single update step (or equivalently when no dynamics are present) we begin by substituting the values from Eq.
9
-
12
into the Gaussian KL divergence expression, Eq.
5
. Rearranging terms with the matrix inversion lemma and cyclic property of the trace, the information gain from the prior to the posterior is

\displaystyle\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(p(\bm{x}_{t}\ |\ \bm{y}_{t},\bm{d})||p(\bm{x}_{t}))=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\text{log}|\bm{I}+\bm{H}^{T}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}|

\displaystyle-\text{tr}[\bm{S_{t}}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\bm{H}^{T}]

\displaystyle+(\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})^{T}\bm{S_{t}}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\bm{H}^{T}\bm{S_{t}}^{-1}(\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})\Big]

(19)

Only the last term depends on
\bm{y}_{t}
, so for the EIG we just need to find the expectation of the quadratic term, which is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{y}_{t}|\bm{d})}[(\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})^{T}\bm{S_{t}}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\bm{H}^{T}\bm{S_{t}}^{-1}(\bm{y}_{t}-

\displaystyle\quad\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[\bm{S_{t}}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\bm{H}^{T}\bm{S_{t}}^{-1}\text{Cov}(\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[\bm{S_{t}}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\bm{H}^{T}].

(20)

Here we recall Eq.
15
so
\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}
has mean
\bm{0}
and covariance
\bm{S}_{t}
. Therefore, noting the cancellation of the trace terms, the EIG of the single step of the Kalman filter is

\displaystyle\text{EIG}(\bm{d})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{y}_{t}\ |\ \bm{d})}[\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(p(\bm{x}_{t}\ |\ \bm{y}_{t},\bm{d})||p(\bm{x}_{t}))]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{|\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}|}{|\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\text{log}|\bm{I}+\bm{H}^{T}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}|\Big].

(21)

Infinite Horizon
: We may also be interested in assessing the EIG about a state
\bm{x}_{t}
when the system and filters have converged to their stationary distributions. For this we define our prior knowledge about
\bm{x}_{t}
as solution to the Lyapunov equation e.g.
p(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{L})
when
t
is sufficiently large to be in the asymptotic regime. Similarly, when we have a sufficiently large set of observations,
\bm{Y}_{t}
, we know the posterior belief about
\bm{x}_{t}
will have the form
p(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{Y}_{t},\bm{d})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t}),\bm{\Sigma}_{D})
. Here we express
\bm{\mu}_{t}
as a function of
\bm{Y}_{t}
to emphasize that
\bm{\mu}_{t}
is a random variable defined by
\bm{Y}_{t}
. Therefore, the information gain from observing the
\bm{Y}_{t}
is

\displaystyle\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(p(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{Y}_{t},\bm{d})||p(\bm{x}_{t}))=

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{Tr}\left[\bm{\Sigma}_{L}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{D}\right]-n+\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{L}^{-1}\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t})+\log\frac{\mid\bm{\Sigma}_{L}\mid}{\mid\bm{\Sigma}_{D}\mid}\right).

Again, the only term that depends on the observations is the quadratic term. Therefore, to compute the EIG we first derive the expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{Y}_{t}|\bm{d})}

\displaystyle[\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{L}^{-1}\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t})]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[\bm{\Sigma}_{L}^{-1}\text{Cov}(\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t})\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t})^{T})]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[\bm{\Sigma}_{L}^{-1}(\bm{\Sigma}_{L}-\bm{\Sigma}_{D})]=n-\text{Tr}[\bm{\Sigma}_{L}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{D}].

(23)

Here we use that
\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{Y}_{t}|\bm{d})}[\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t})]=\bm{0}
and
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t})\bm{\mu}_{t}(\bm{Y}_{t})^{T}]=\bm{\Sigma}_{L}-\bm{\Sigma}_{D}
. This is shown as Eq.
57
in Appendix
VI-A
.

Therefore, taking the expectation of Eq.
22
over
\bm{Y}_{t}
and substituting in the result of Eq.
23
which cancels the trace terms, we find similarly to Eq.
21
that

\displaystyle\text{EIG}(\bm{d})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{Y}_{t}\ |\ \bm{d})}[\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(p(\bm{x}_{t}\ |\ \bm{Y}_{t},\bm{d})||p(\bm{x}_{t}))]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{|\bm{\Sigma}_{L}|}{|\bm{\Sigma}_{D}|},\text{ as }t\rightarrow\infty.

(24)

III-B

Expected Generalized Information Gain

Using the generalized measure of information in eq.
6
, we can assess how much information is expected to be gained or lost by an experiment
d
when there is a model discrepancy. We define the true model as
\mathcal{M}^{*}
and the model with discrepancy as
\mathcal{M}
, both of which have the same unknown states,
\bm{X}
which we seek to infer. This expectation is taken over data that is generated according to
p(\bm{Y}\ |\bm{d},\mathcal{M}^{*})
. This leads to the Expected Generalized Information Gain (EGIG) given by,

\displaystyle\text{EGIG}(\bm{d},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}^{*})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{Y}\ |\bm{d},\mathcal{M}^{*})}\bigg[\mathcal{I}_{p(\bm{X}\ |\ \bm{Y},\bm{d},\mathcal{M}^{*})}[p(\bm{X}\ |\ \bm{Y},\bm{d},\mathcal{M})||p(\bm{X}\mid\mathcal{M})]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int p(\bm{X},\bm{Y}\ |\ \bm{d},\mathcal{M}^{*})\log\frac{p(\bm{X}\ |\ \bm{Y},\bm{d},\mathcal{M})}{p(\bm{X}\mid\mathcal{M})}d\bm{X}d\bm{Y}

(25)

\displaystyle=\int p(\bm{X},\bm{Y}\ |\ \bm{d},\mathcal{M}^{*})\log\frac{p(\bm{Y}\ |\ \bm{X},\bm{d},\mathcal{M})}{p(\bm{Y}\mid\mathcal{M})}d\bm{X}d\bm{Y}

(26)

Notes that Eq.
26
is a simple rearrangement using Bayes’ theorem, which can be easier to compute for some problems.

Typically we do not know the model
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, so in practice we should either define a set of plausible models we want to be robust to or we can assess the sensitivity to perturbations away from
\mathcal{M}
by computing derivatives of the EGIG using either automatic differentiation or numerical derivatives.

In the context of inferring
\bm{x}_{t}
with a system defined by Eq.

-
2

and the model we use for inference as
\mathcal{M}=\{\bm{A},\bm{H},\bm{Q},\bm{R}\}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\Bigg(\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}^{*}\\

\displaystyle(\bm{d},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M^{*}})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t}|\mathcal{M}^{*})}\left[\log\frac{p(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{y}_{t},d,\mathcal{M})}{p(\bm{x}_{t}\mid d,\mathcal{M})}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}[(\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})^{T}\bm{S}_{t}^{-1}(\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{H}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})]\bigg).

(30)

Using Eq.

-

\displaystyle(\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{H}\bm{x}_{t})\sim

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}((\bm{H}^{*}-\bm{H})\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}^{*},

V
Conclusion

Maximizing the value of data for inference and prediction requires the careful selection of experimental conditions by modeling the experiment. These models are prone to misspecifications. We propose an information theoretic framework that extends the notion of Expected Information Gain (EIG), typically used in Bayesian experiment design, to address the model mismatch issue. The proposed the Expected Generalized Information Gain (EGIG) captures the information gained or loss with respect to a true model, when the experiment is designed based on a model with discrepancy. On the other hand the proposed Expected Discriminatory Information (EDI) discriminates between models based upon the data generated, which further aids in the model refinement. These three metrics are complementary as EIG emphasizes data efficient experiments, EGIG emphasizes experiments that lead to results that are robust to model discrepancy, and EDI emphasizes experiments that would detect modeling failures.
