Vanishing criteria for Ceresa cycles
Jef Laga, Ari Shnidman

Abstract
. We prove cohomological vanishing criteria for the Ceresa cycle of a curve C C embedded in its Jacobian J J . Namely: ( A ) (A) If H 3 ⁡ ( J ) Aut ⁡ ( C ) = 0 \HH^{3}(J)^{{\rm Aut}(C)}=0 , then the Ceresa cycle is torsion modulo rational equivalence; ( B ) (B) If H 0 ⁡ ( J , Ω J 3 ) Aut ⁡ ( C ) = 0 \HH^{0}(J,\Omega_{J}^{3})^{{\rm Aut}(C)}=0 , then the Ceresa cycle is torsion modulo algebraic equivalence, with criterion ( B ) (B) conditional on the Hodge conjecture. We then use these criteria to study the simplest family of curves where ( B ) (B) holds but ( A ) (A) does not, namely the family of Picard curves C : y 3 = x 4 + a ​ x 2 + b ​ x + c C\colon y^{3}=x^{4}+ax^{2}+bx+c . Criterion ( B ) (B) and work of Schoen combine to show that the Ceresa cycle of a Picard curve is torsion in the Griffiths group. 1.
Introduction

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field and
C
a smooth, projective, and connected curve over
k
of genus
g\geq 2
with Jacobian variety
J
.
Let
e
be a degree-
1
divisor of
C
and let
\iota_{e}\colon C\hookrightarrow J
be the Abel-Jacobi map based at
e
.
We study the torsion behaviour of the Ceresa cycle

(1.1)

\displaystyle\kappa_{C,e}:=[\iota_{e}(C)]-(-1)^{*}[\iota_{e}(C)]\in{\rm CH}_{1}(J)

in the Chow group modulo rational equivalence.
If
\kappa_{C,e}
is torsion, then
(2g-2)e=K_{C}
in
{\rm CH}_{0}(C)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
, where
K_{C}
is the canonical divisor class.
Moreover if
(2g-2)e
is canonical, then the image of
\kappa_{C,e}
in
{\rm CH}_{1}(J)\otimes{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
is independent of
e
and we denote it by
\kappa(C)
, see §
2.7
for these claims.
Thus, the class
\kappa(C)
vanishes if and only if
\kappa_{C,e}
is torsion for some degree-
1
divisor
e
.

We also consider the image
\bar{\kappa}(C)
of
\kappa(C)
in the Griffiths group
{\rm Gr}_{1}(J)\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
of homologically trivial
1
-cycles modulo algebraic equivalence.
When
g=2
, or more generally when
C
is hyperelliptic, it is easy to see that
\kappa(C)=0
.
On the other hand, Ceresa famously showed that
\bar{\kappa}(C)\neq 0
for a very general curve
C
over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
of genus
g\geq 3

[
14
]
.

The vanishing of the Ceresa cycle is interesting for various reasons. For example,
\kappa(C)=0
if and only if the Chow motive
\mathfrak{h}(C)
has a multiplicative Chow-Künneth decomposition (by
[
22
, Proposition 3.1]
and Proposition
2.12
).
Moreover
\bar{\kappa}(C)=0
if and only if the tautological subring modulo algebraic equivalence is generated by a theta divisor (by
[
6
, Corollary 3.4]
), in which case Poincaré’s formula
[C]=\frac{\Theta^{g-1}}{(g-1)!}
holds modulo algebraic equivalence.
More generally, the Ceresa cycle over
\mathcal{M}_{g}
serves as a testing ground for the study of homologically trivial algebraic cycles in codimension greater than 1.

1.1.
Vanishing criteria

We prove cohomological vanishing criteria for Ceresa cycles of curves with nontrivial automorphisms. Let
\HH^{*}(-)
be a Weil cohomology functor, such as
\ell
-adic cohomology with
\ell\neq{\rm char\hskip 2.84526pt}(k)
or singular cohomology when
k={\rm\mathbb{C}}
. Note that the finite group
{\rm Aut}(C)
acts on
\HH^{*}(J)
, by functoriality. Cupping with the principal polarization gives an injection
\HH^{1}(J)(-1)\hookrightarrow\HH^{3}(J)
, allowing us to define the primitive cohomology
\HH^{3}(J)_{\mathrm{prim}}:=\HH^{3}(J)/\HH^{1}(J)(-1)
.

Theorem A
.

If
\HH^{3}(J)_{\mathrm{prim}}^{{\rm Aut}(C)}=0
, then
\kappa(C)=0
.

This improves on a recent result of Qiu and Zhang stating that if
(\HH^{1}(C)^{\otimes 3})^{{\rm Aut}(C)}=0
, then
\kappa(C)=0

[
43
]
.
1
1

1

This formulation is equivalent to theirs, using
[
51
, Theorem 1.5.5]
.

By contrast, Theorem
A
requires only the weaker condition that the subrepresentation
\HH^{3}(J)_{\mathrm{prim}}\subset\HH^{3}(J)\simeq\bigwedge^{3}\HH^{1}(C)\subset\HH^{1}(C)^{\otimes 3}
has no nontrivial
{\rm Aut}(C)
-fixed points. If the quotient
C/{\rm Aut}(C)
has genus
0
, then our hypothesis is equivalent to
\HH^{3}(J)^{{\rm Aut}(C)}=0
.

Our proof of Theorem
A
is inspired by Beauville’s proof that for the curve
y^{3}=x^{4}+x
, the image of
\kappa(C)
under the complex Abel-Jacobi map vanishes
[
7
]
. To achieve a vanishing result in the Chow group, we work directly with the rational Chow motive
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)
and make crucial use of the finite-dimensionality results of Kimura
[
29
]
.

Our second result is a vanishing criterion for
\overline{\kappa}(C)
, however the result is conditional on the Hodge conjecture. In particular, we assume for the rest of the introduction that
k
has characteristic
0
.

Theorem B
.

Assume the Hodge conjecture for abelian varieties. If
\HH^{0}(J,\Omega_{J}^{3})^{{\rm Aut}(C)}=0
, then
\bar{\kappa}(C)=0
.

More precisely, we require the Hodge conjecture for
J\times A
, where
A
is the abelian variety described in Proposition
3.3
.
Note that
\HH^{0}(J,\Omega_{J}^{3})\simeq\bigwedge^{3}\HH^{0}(C,\Omega_{C}^{1})
, so the conditions of Theorems
A
and
B
both depend only on the abstract representation
(G,V)=({\rm Aut}(C),\HH^{0}(C,\Omega_{C}^{1}))
.

The proof of Theorem
B
is in the same spirit as that of Theorem
A
. The Hodge conjecture is used to show that the motive
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(J)^{{\rm Aut}(C)}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A)(-1)
, from which the algebraic triviality of
\kappa(C)
follows.

1.2.
Picard curves

The condition
\HH^{3}(J)_{\mathrm{prim}}^{{\rm Aut}(C)}=0
in Theorem
A
is only rarely satisfied, e.g. it holds for exactly two plane quartic curves over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
(see Theorem
E
below).
The condition
\HH^{0}(J,\Omega_{J}^{3})^{{\rm Aut}(C)}=0
of Theorem
B
is satisfied more often; e.g. it holds for all Picard curves
y^{3}=x^{4}+ax^{2}+bx+c
.
For such curves, it turns out that we require a nontrivial case of the Hodge conjecture to apply Theorem
B
, namely that a certain Weil class on an abelian fourfold is algebraic.
Fortunately, Schoen has proved the Hodge conjecture in our specific situation
[
46
]
. (See also recent work of Markman
[
36
]
.)
This leads to an unconditional proof of the vanishing of
\bar{\kappa}(C)
. By further analyzing
\kappa(C)
, we prove the following precise characterization of the vanishing of
\kappa(C)
in the Picard family:

Theorem C
.

Let
C_{f}
be a smooth projective Picard curve with model

(1.2)

\displaystyle C_{f}\colon y^{3}=f(x)=x^{4}+ax^{2}+bx+c

for some
a,b,c\in k
. Consider the point
P_{f}=(a^{2}+12c,72ac-2a^{3}-27b^{2})
on the elliptic curve

E_{f}\colon y^{2}=4x^{3}-27\cdot{\rm disc}(f).

Then
\bar{\kappa}(C_{f})=0
, and
\kappa(C_{f})=0
if and only if
P_{f}\in E_{f}(k)
is torsion.

Here,
{\rm disc}(f)
is the discriminant of
f
and the coordinates of
P_{f}
are the usual
I
- and
J
-invariants of the binary quartic form
z^{4}f(x/z)
; see §
4.1
.
The fact that
P_{f}
defines a point on
E_{f}
follows from a classical relation between the invariants of a binary quartic. Picard curves have a unique point
\infty
at infinity, for which
(2g-2)\infty=4\infty
is canonical. For a more precise version of Theorem
C
relating the torsion orders of
\kappa_{C_{f},\infty}
and
P_{f}
, see §
4.2
.

Theorem
C
generalizes the results of
[
31
]
concerning
bielliptic
Picard curves, i.e. those with
b=0
in (
1.2
).
There we exploited the explicit geometry of the bielliptic cover to characterize the vanishing locus of
\kappa(C)
.
Here we must proceed more indirectly: using Schoen’s work on the Hodge conjecture, we first show that the vanishing of
\kappa(C)
is equivalent to the vanishing of its Abel–Jacobi image in the intermediate Jacobian.
Using the Shioda-Tate formula and considerations of isotrivial families of elliptic curves, we are able to bootstrap the calculations of
[
31
]
to determine the Abel-Jacobi image up to multiple and obtain the characterization of Theorem
C
.

In §
3
, we give several examples of higher genus families of curves
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow B
for which Theorem
B
applies. It would be interesting to completely characterize the vanishing of
\kappa(C)
in these families as well, and to find more such families.
The general proof strategy would follow that of Theorem
C
: using the Hodge conjecture, one shows that the vanishing of
\kappa(\mathcal{C}_{b})
is equivalent to the vanishing of
\sigma(b)
, where
\sigma
is a section of a certain abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow B
.
(The analytification of
\sigma
is the normal function associated to the Ceresa cycle over
B
, which lands in the
{\rm Aut}(C)
-fixed points of the intermediate Jacobian.) One then tries to identify
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow B
and
\sigma
explicitly.

1.3.
Vanishing loci in genus
3

Let
\mathcal{M}_{g}
be the coarse moduli space of genus-
g
curves
C
, seen as a variety over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
.
Let
V_{g}^{\mathrm{rat}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{g}
be the locus of curves with
\kappa(C)=0
.
Let
V_{g}^{\mathrm{alg}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{g}
be the analogous subset for
\bar{\kappa}(C)
.
The subsets
V_{g}^{\mathrm{rat}}
and
V_{g}^{\mathrm{alg}}
are each a countable union of closed algebraic subvarieties (Lemma
5.1
). We have
V_{g}^{\mathrm{rat}}\subset V_{g}^{\mathrm{alg}}
, and Ceresa’s result
[
14
]
shows that
V_{g}^{\mathrm{alg}}\neq\mathcal{M}_{g}
for all
g\geq 3
.
What can be said about the irreducible components of
V_{g}^{\mathrm{rat}}
and
V_{g}^{\mathrm{alg}}
and their dimensions? For a fixed
g
, are there only finitely many components?
Collino and Pirola showed that
V_{3}^{\mathrm{rat}}
does not contain subvarieties of dimension
\geq 4
that are not themselves contained in the hyperelliptic locus
[
16
, Corollary 4.3.4]
.
On the other hand, Theorem
C
shows that
V_{3}^{\mathrm{alg}}
contains the
2
-dimensional Picard locus.
Turning to
V_{3}^{\mathrm{rat}}
, we answer several open question about its geometry and arithmetic by analyzing the torsion locus of the section
P_{f}
of
E_{f}
:

Theorem D
.

Let
X\subset\mathcal{M}_{3}
be the Picard locus.

(i)

(X\cap V_{3}^{\mathrm{rat}})\times_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}\mathbb{C}
is a countably infinite disjoint union of rational curves. In particular, there exist one-parameter families of plane quartic curves in
V_{3}^{\mathrm{rat}}
,
and
V_{3}^{\mathrm{rat}}
contains infinitely many positive-dimensional components.

(ii)

If
K
is a number field and
C\in(X\cap V_{3}^{\mathrm{rat}})(K)
, then the order of
\kappa_{C,\infty}
in
{\rm CH}_{1}(J)
is bounded above by a quantity depending only on
[K\colon{\rm\mathbb{Q}}]
.

An example of a one-parameter family as in
(i)
is the family
C_{t}\colon y^{3}=x^{4}-12x^{2}+tx-12
.
Note that we have previously shown in
[
31
, Theorem 1.3]
that the torsion orders of the classes
\kappa_{C,e}
, where
C
has genus
3
and
4e=K_{C}
, are unbounded.

Recall that there is a stratification of the non-hyperelliptic locus of
\mathcal{M}_{3}
by locally closed subvarieties
X_{G}
, indexed by certain finite groups
G
, with the property that a non-hyperelliptic curve
[C]\in\mathcal{M}_{3}({\rm\mathbb{C}})
lies in
X_{G}
if and only if
{\rm Aut}(C)\simeq G
. It turns out that the isomorphism class of the
{\rm Aut}(C)
-representation
\HH^{0}(C,\Omega_{C}^{1})
does not depend on the choice of curve
[C]
in
X_{G}({\rm\mathbb{C}})
.
Our final theorem determines exactly which
X_{G}
are contained in
V_{3}^{\mathrm{rat}}
or
V_{3}^{\mathrm{alg}}
.

Theorem E
.

Let
G
be a finite group isomorphic to the automorphism group of a plane quartic.
Then
X_{G}\subset V_{3}^{\mathrm{rat}}
if and only if
G=C_{9}
or
G_{48}
, and
X_{G}\subset V_{3}^{\mathrm{alg}}
if and only if
X_{G}
is contained in the Picard locus, in other words if and only if
G=C_{3},C_{6},C_{9}
or
G_{48}
.

Here,
G_{48}
is the group with GAP label
(48,33)
. The strata
X_{C_{9}}
and
X_{G_{48}}
are single closed points, represented by the curves
y^{3}=x^{4}+x
and
y^{3}=x^{4}+1
respectively.

Theorem
E
shows that
X_{G}\subset V_{3}^{\mathrm{rat}}
if and only if
\HH^{3}(J)^{G}=0
for every (equivalently, some) curve in
X_{G}
, and that
X_{G}\subset V_{3}^{\mathrm{alg}}
if and only if
\HH^{0}(J,\Omega_{J}^{3})^{G}=0
for every curve in
X_{G}
. Thus, for
g=3
, the criteria of Theorems
A
and
B
exactly single out the strata
X_{G}
where
\kappa(C)
or
\bar{\kappa}(C)
identically vanishes.
Does this continue to hold in higher genus? Are there non-hyperelliptic curves of arbitrarily large genus for which Theorem
A
or
B
applies?

We also use Theorem
B
to find the first examples of positive dimensional components of
V_{g}^{\mathrm{alg}}
with generic point satisfying
{\rm Aut}(C)=\{1\}
.

\kappa(C)=0
and

.
This uses the following fact: if

dominates another curve

, then
\overline{\kappa}(C)=0
implies
\overline{\kappa}(D)=0
. For example, the genus 6 family

C_{t}\colon y^{9}=\left(\frac{x+1}{x-1}\right)\left(\frac{x+t}{x-t}\right)^{3},

admits a

by the fact above, and satisfies

(again conditional on the Hodge conjecture) with trivial generic automorphism group; see Theorem

. Thus, even in genus 3, the implications of Theorems

and

are not yet fully understood, since it is hard to know which curves admit high genus covers that satisfy our vanishing criteria. We should remark that, as far as we know, there are no known examples of curves
C
with
\overline{\kappa}(C)=0
,
{\rm Aut}(C)=\{1\}
, and
{\rm End}(J)={\rm\mathbb{Z}}
; see
[

for data confirming this for many quartic plane curves. The curves

are no exceptions as generically we have

Finally, Gao and Zhang have recently proven a Northcott property for the Beilinson-Bloch height of the Ceresa cycle on

and (equivalently) the modified diagonal cycle
\Delta(C)
on

[

. More precisely, for each

, there exists an open dense subset

such that for any

and

, the number of

and with

U_{g}
, or equivalently, its complement
Z_{g}^{\mathrm{slim}}:=\mathcal{M}_{g}\setminus U_{g}
. The Northcott property implies that any positive dimensional component of
V_{g}^{\mathrm{rat}}
is contained in
Z_{g}^{\mathrm{slim}}
, but

may be strictly larger than the union of the positive dimensional components of

Notation and background

2.1.
Chow groups

Let
k
be field. A variety is by definition a separated scheme of finite type over
k
. We say a variety is nice if it is smooth, projective and geometrically integral.
If
X
is a smooth and geometrically integral variety and
p\in\{0,\dots,\dim X\}
, let
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)
denote the Chow group (with
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
-coefficients) of codimension
p
cycles modulo rational equivalence.
If
Z\subset X
is a closed subscheme of codimension
p
, we denote its class in
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)
by
[Z]
(using
[
49
,
Tag 02QS
]
if
Z
is not integral).

If
X
is additionally projective, then
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)
has a filtration by subgroups

\displaystyle{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\mathrm{alg}}\subset{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}\subset{\rm CH}^{p}(X),

where
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\mathrm{alg}}
is the subgroup of algebraically trivial cycles (in the sense of
[
2
, §3.1]
) and
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}
the subgroup of homologically trivial cycles (with respect to a fixed Weil cohomology theory for nice varieties over
k
).
The Griffiths group is by definition
{\rm Gr}^{p}(X)={\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\mathrm{hom}}/{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\mathrm{alg}}
.
We occasionally write
{\rm CH}_{p}(X)={\rm CH}^{\dim X-p}(X)
and
{\rm Gr}_{p}(X)={\rm Gr}^{\dim X-p}(X)
.
If
R
is a ring, we write
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{R}={\rm CH}^{p}(X)\otimes_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}}R
and
{\rm Gr}^{p}(X)_{R}={\rm Gr}^{p}(X)\otimes_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}}R
.

2.2.
Base change, specialization and families

We state three lemmas concerning operations on cycles.
These seem to be standard, but we could not locate proofs in the literature.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X/k
be a nice variety and
K/k
a (not necessarily finite) extension of fields.

(1)

The base change maps
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\rm\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{K})_{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
and
{\rm Gr}^{p}(X)_{\rm\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow{\rm Gr}^{p}(X_{K})_{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
are injective.

(2)

If in addition
k
is algebraically closed, the base change maps
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{K})
and
{\rm Gr}^{p}(X)\rightarrow{\rm Gr}^{p}(X_{K})
are injective.

Proof.

The proof is an adaptation of
[
12

We first prove
(2)
for
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{K})

Suppose

has trivial image in
{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{K})
.
Then
\alpha
already has trivial image in
{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{K^{\prime}})

is a subfield that is finitely generated over
k

{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{K})
can be defined over such a subfield.
By spreading out, we can find a smooth integral variety

with function field

such that
\alpha
has trivial image in
{\rm CH}^{p}(X\times_{k}U)

Since

is algebraically closed, there exists a
k
-point

.
Pulling back along

defines a left-inverse
{\rm CH}^{p}(X\times_{k}U)\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{p}(X)
to the map
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{p}(X\times_{k}U)
.
It follows that
\alpha
is trivial in
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)

The argument for
{\rm Gr}^{p}(X)

We now prove
(1)
for

{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{k^{\prime}})\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{p}(X)
, when precomposed with the base change map, is multiplication by
[k^{\prime}:k]
.
The second follows from Part
(2)
.
The case of
{\rm Gr}^{p}(X)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
is again analogous.

Lemma 2.2
.

In the above notation,

(C,t_{0},t_{1},Z)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow{\rm Spec}(R)
be an integral, projective, flat, proper and regular model of
C
, which exists by
[
34
, Proposition 10.1.8]
.
Let
\mathrm{Hilb}_{X/R}\rightarrow{\rm Spec}(R)
be the Hilbert scheme of the projective morphism
X\rightarrow{\rm Spec}(R)
.
The closed subscheme
Z
determines a
K
-morphism
\alpha\colon C\rightarrow\mathrm{Hilb}_{X/R}
.
Since
C
is connected, the image of
\alpha
is contained in an open and closed subscheme
\mathrm{Hilb}_{X/R}^{\phi}
with fixed Hilbert polynomial.
The surface
\mathcal{C}
is regular,
\mathrm{Hilb}^{\phi}_{X/R}\rightarrow{\rm Spec}(R)
is projective
[
3
, Corollary (2.8)]
and we may view
\alpha
as a rational map
\mathcal{C}\dashrightarrow\mathrm{Hilb}_{X/R}^{\phi}
.
Therefore by
[
34
, Theorem 9.2.7]
there exists a birational map
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, obtained by successively blowing up closed points in the special fiber, and an
R
-morphism
\tilde{\alpha}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Hilb}^{\phi}_{X/R}
with generic fiber
\alpha
.
In other words, there exists a closed subscheme
\mathcal{Z}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}\times_{R}X
, flat over
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
, whose generic fiber equals
Z
.

The
K
-points
t_{i}\in C(K)
extend to
R
-points
\tilde{t}_{i}\colon{\rm Spec}(R)\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
whose reductions
\bar{t}_{i}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{k}(k)
land in the smooth locus of
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
.
The closed subschemes
\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{t}_{i}}\subset X
are flat over
R
.
Therefore
\mathrm{sp}([Z_{t_{0}}]-[Z_{t_{1}}])=[\mathcal{Z}_{\bar{t}_{0}}]-[\mathcal{Z}_{\bar{t}_{1}}]
.

By the Zariski connectedness theorem, the special fiber
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
is connected.
Consequently, by resolving irreducible components of
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
, we can connect
\bar{t}_{0}
to
\bar{t}_{1}
by a sequence of nice curves: there exists a finite extension
k^{\prime}/k
, a collection of nice curves
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
over
k^{\prime}
, and for each
1\leq i\leq n
a pair of points
s_{i,1},s_{i,2}\in D_{i}(k^{\prime})
and a morphism
\varphi_{i}\colon D_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{k^{\prime}}
such that
\varphi_{1}(s_{1,1})=\bar{t}_{1}
,
\varphi_{i}(s_{i,2})=\varphi_{i+1}(s_{i+1,1})
for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
\varphi_{n}(s_{n,2})=\bar{t}_{2}
.
Letting
W^{(i)}
be the pullback of
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
along
\varphi_{i}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{sp}([Z_{t_{0}}]-[Z_{t_{1}}])_{k^{\prime}}=([W^{(1)}_{s_{1,1}}]-[W^{(1)}_{s_{1,2}}])+\cdots+([W^{(n)}_{s_{n,1}}]-[W^{(n)}_{s_{n,2}}]).

Since each term
([W^{(i)}_{s_{i,1}}]-[W^{(i)}_{s_{i,2}}])
lies in
{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{k^{\prime}})_{\mathrm{alg}}\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
by definition of algebraic triviality, the same is true for their sum.
Taking the pushforward along
X_{k^{\prime}}\rightarrow X_{k}
, we see that
[k^{\prime}:k]\cdot\mathrm{sp}([Z_{t_{0}}]-[Z_{t_{1}}])\in{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{k})_{\mathrm{alg}}
, as desired.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The proof shows that if
K
is perfect and

is algebraically closed, then
\mathrm{sp}
even sends
{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{K})_{\mathrm{alg}}
to

.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X\rightarrow S
be a smooth proper morphism of smooth varieties over a field
k
.
Let
\alpha
be a codimension
p
cycle on
X
.
Then the locus of points

such that the (Gysin) fiber
\alpha_{s}\in{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{s})_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
is zero (resp. lies in
{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{s})_{\mathrm{alg},{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
) is a countable union of closed algebraic subvarieties of

.

Proof.

There exists a countable subfield
k_{0}\subset k
, a smooth proper morphism
X_{0}\rightarrow S_{0}
of varieties over
k_{0}
and a cycle
\alpha_{0}
on

whose base change to

are
X\rightarrow S
and

respectively.
Since

is countable,

has only countably many closed subschemes.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be the collection of integral closed subschemes

such that

2.3.
Chow motives

We recall a few relevant facts about the category
\mathsf{Mot}(k)
of (pure, contravariant) Chow motives
M
over
k
with
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-coefficients; see
[
47
]
or
[
42
, §2]
for basic definitions.

Denote the Lefschetz motive by
\mathbb{L}
, its
n
th tensor power by
\mathbb{L}^{n}
and write
M(n)=M\otimes\mathbb{L}^{n}
.
Following
[
42
, §2.5]
, the Chow group in codimension
p
of
M
is by definition
{\rm CH}^{p}(M)={\rm Hom}_{\mathsf{Mot}(k)}(\mathbb{L}^{p},M)
.
If
M=\mathfrak{h}(X)
where
X
is smooth projective over
k
, then
{\rm CH}^{p}(M)={\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
. (Beware that we need to take
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-coefficients on the right hand side.)
If
C
is a nice curve over
k
and
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{h}(C)\rightarrow M(p-1)
a morphism in
\mathsf{Mot}(k)
, we obtain a homomorphism of abelian groups
{\rm CH}^{1}(\varphi)\colon{\rm CH}^{1}(C)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{p}(M)
.
We define
{\rm CH}^{p}(M)_{\mathrm{alg}}
to be the union of the images of
{\rm CH}^{1}(\varphi)
ranging over all pairs
(C,\varphi)
as above.
This coincides with
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\mathrm{alg}}\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
when
M=\mathfrak{h}(X)
, see
[
2
, Corollary 3.13]
.

Finally, we define motives of fixed points.
If
G
is a finite group acting on a motive
M
, write
M^{G}
for the submotive cut out by the idempotent
\frac{1}{\#G}\sum_{g\in G}g_{*}\in{\rm End}(M)
.
We have
{\rm CH}^{p}(M^{G})={\rm CH}^{p}(M)^{G}
.
If
G
acts on a nice variety
X
, then
G
acts on
\mathfrak{h}(X)
and
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)
, and
{\rm CH}^{p}(\mathfrak{h}(X)^{G})={\rm CH}^{p}(X)^{G}\otimes{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
.

2.4.
Chow–Künneth decomposition for abelian varieties

Let
A/k
be a
g
-dimensional abelian variety.
Deninger and Murre
[
19
, §3]
have constructed a canonical Chow–Künneth decomposition

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}(A)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{2g}\mathfrak{h}^{i}(A),

uniquely characterized by the following property: if
(n)\colon A\rightarrow A
denotes the multiplication-by-
n
, then
(n)^{*}
acts on
\mathfrak{h}^{i}(A)
via
n^{i}
for every integer
n
.
On the other hand, Beauville
[
5
]
has shown that there exists a direct sum decomposition
{\rm CH}^{p}(A)_{\rm\mathbb{Q}}=\bigoplus_{s=p-g}^{p}{\rm CH}^{p}_{(s)}(A)
, where

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\rm CH}^{p}_{(s)}(A)=\{\alpha\in{\rm CH}^{p}(A)_{\rm\mathbb{Q}}\colon(n)^{*}\alpha=n^{2p-s}\alpha\quad\forall n\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}\}.

The two decompositions are linked by the formula
{\rm CH}^{p}(\mathfrak{h}^{i}(A))={\rm CH}^{p}_{(2p-i)}(A)
.
Beauville conjectured that
{\rm CH}^{p}_{(s)}(A)=0
when
s<0
, and he proved it when
p\in\{0,1,g-2,g-1,g\}

[
5
, Proposition 3(a)]
.

Example 2.5
.

For
p=1
, the Beauville decomposition
{\rm CH}^{1}(A)_{\rm\mathbb{Q}}={\rm CH}^{1}_{(0)}(A)\oplus{\rm CH}^{1}_{(1)}(A)
is the decomposition of a divisor class into symmetric and anti-symmetric classes.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
A/k
is an abelian variety, then
{\rm CH}^{p}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A))_{\mathrm{alg}}={\rm CH}^{p}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A))
for all
p\geq 0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
(1), we may assume
k
is algebraically closed.
The only nonzero Chow group of
\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A)
is
{\rm CH}^{1}(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A))={\rm CH}^{1}_{(1)}(A)
, the set of anti-symmetric elements of
{\rm CH}^{1}(A)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
.
The lemma follows from the fact that
{\rm CH}^{1}_{(1)}(A)={\rm CH}^{1}(A)_{\mathrm{hom}}\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
and that homological and algebraic equivalence coincide for codimension-
1
cycles.
∎

2.5.
The Lefschetz decomposition for abelian varieties

Let
A/k
be an abelian variety with polarization
\lambda\colon A\rightarrow A^{\vee}
.
Let
\ell\in{\rm CH}^{1}_{(0)}(A)
be the unique class satisfying
2\ell=(1,\lambda)^{*}\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}\in{\rm Pic}(A\times A^{\vee})
is the Poincaré bundle.
Künnemann has shown
[
30
, Theorem 5.2]
that intersecting with
\ell^{g-i}
induces an isomorphism
\mathfrak{h}^{i}(A)\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}^{2g-i}(A)(g-i)
for all
0\leq i\leq g
.
This induces a Lefschetz decomposition of the Chow–Künneth components
\mathfrak{h}^{i}(A)
, see
[
30
, Theorem 5.1]
.
We are chiefly interested in the components
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(A)
and
\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}(A)
when
g\geq 2
; in that case the Lefschetz decomposition has the form
\mathfrak{h}^{3}(A)=\mathfrak{h}^{3}_{\mathrm{prim}}(A)\oplus\ell\cdot\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A)
and
\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}(A)=\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}_{\mathrm{prim}}(A)\oplus\ell^{g-2}\cdot\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A)
.
It has the property that the isomorphism
\ell^{g-3}\colon\mathfrak{h}^{3}(A)\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}(A)(g-3)
is a direct sum of isomorphisms
\mathfrak{h}^{3}_{\mathrm{prim}}(A)\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}_{\mathrm{prim}}(A)
and
\ell\cdot\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A)\rightarrow\ell^{g-2}\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A)
.
Taking Chow groups, we get a decomposition
{\rm CH}^{g-1}(\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}(A))={\rm CH}^{g-1}(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathrm{prim}}^{2g-3}(A))\oplus{\rm CH}^{g-1}(\ell^{g-2}\cdot\mathfrak{h}^{1}(A))
.

2.6.
Beauville components of
C

Consider a nice curve
C
of genus
g\geq 2
over
k
with Jacobian
J
.
Let
e
be a degree-
1
divisor on
C
and embed
C
in
J
using the Abel–Jacobi map based at
e
, sending
x\in C
to the divisor class of
x-e
.
Decompose
[C]=[C]_{0}+\cdots+[C]_{g-1}
with
[C]_{s}\in{\rm CH}_{(s)}^{g-1}(J)
.
In this subsection we analyze the component
[C]_{1}
more closely, whose vanishing is equivalent to the vanishing of
\kappa(C)
.
Proposition
2.7
can be viewed as a determination of the ‘imprimitive part’ of
[C]_{1}
.

For
\alpha\in{\rm CH}_{p}(J)
and
\beta\in{\rm CH}_{q}(J)
, the Pontryagin product
\alpha\star\beta
is the pushforward of
\alpha\times\beta
under the addition map
J\times J\rightarrow J
. If
n
is a positive integer let
\alpha^{\star n}
be the
n
-fold Pontryagin product of
\alpha
with itself.
Let
K_{C}\in{\rm CH}_{0}(C)
denote the canonical divisor class and let
x_{e}\in J(k)
be the point corresponding to the degree-
0
divisor class
[(2g-2)e]-K_{C}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

We have an equality in
{\rm CH}_{(1)}^{1}(J)
:

(2.3)

\displaystyle(2g-2)\cdot[C]_{0}^{\star(g-2)}\star[C]_{1}=[C]^{\star(g-1)}\star([0]-[x_{e}]).

Here we view
[0]-[x_{e}]
as an element of
{\rm CH}_{0}(J)
.

Proof.

If
D
is a degree
g-1
divisor class on
C
, let
\Theta_{D}
be the image of the map
{\rm Sym}^{g-1}(C)\rightarrow J
defined by
x\mapsto[x]-D
.
Then
[C]^{\star(g-1)}=(g-1)![\Theta_{(g-1)e}]
.
By Riemann–Roch,

(-1)_{*}[\Theta_{(g-1)e}]=[\Theta_{K_{C}-(g-1)e}]=[x_{e}]\star[\Theta_{(g-1)e}].

Combining the last two sentences shows that
(-1)_{*}([C]^{\star(g-1)})=[x_{e}]\star[C]^{\star(g-1)}
.

Since Pontryagin product sends
{\rm CH}^{g-p}_{(s)}(J)\times{\rm CH}^{g-q}_{(t)}(J)
to
{\rm CH}^{g-p-q}_{(s+t)}(J)
and since
{\rm CH}^{1}_{(s)}(J)\neq 0
only if
s\in\{0,1\}
, we calculate that
[C]^{\star(g-1)}=[C]_{0}^{\star(g-1)}+(g-1)\cdot[C]_{0}^{\star(g-2)}\star[C]_{1}
.
Applying
(-1)_{*}
and
[x_{e}]\star
to the previous identity, we obtain

\displaystyle(-1)_{*}[C]^{\star(g-1)}

\displaystyle=[C]_{0}^{\star(g-1)}-(g-1)\cdot[C]_{0}^{\star(g-2)}\star[C]_{1},

\displaystyle[x_{e}]\star[C]^{\star(g-1)}

\displaystyle=[x_{e}]\star[C]_{0}^{\star(g-1)}+(g-1)\cdot[C]_{0}^{\star(g-2)}\star[C]_{1}.

Note that
[x_{e}]\star
acts trivially on
{\rm CH}^{1}_{(1)}(J)
since
([x_{e}]-[0])\in\oplus_{s\geq 1}{\rm CH}^{g}_{(s)}(J)
by the explicit description of the Beauville decomposition for zero-cycles
[
5
, bottom of p. 649]
.
Equating the right hand sides of the two centered equations proves that
(2g-2)\cdot[C]_{0}^{\star(g-2)}\star[C]_{1}=[C]_{0}^{\star(g-1)}\star([0]-[x_{e}])
.
Since
[C]_{s}^{\star(g-1)}\star([0]-[x_{e}])\in\oplus_{t\geq s+1}{\rm CH}^{1}_{(t)}(J)=\{0\}
for all
s\geq 1
, it follows that
[C]_{0}^{\star(g-1)}\star([0]-[x_{e}])=[C]^{\star(g-1)}\star([0]-[x_{e}])
, concluding the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Suppose that
[C]_{1}=0
in
{\rm CH}_{1}(J)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
.
Then
(2g-2)e=K_{C}
in
{\rm CH}_{0}(C)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
.

Proof.

If
[C]_{1}=0
, then
[C]^{\star(g-1)}\star([0]-[x_{e}])=0
by (
2.3
).
On the other hand,
[C]^{\star(g-1)}=(g-1)![\Theta_{(g-1)e}]
is multiple of a theta divisor, in the notation of the proof of Proposition
2.7
.
Since
\Theta_{(g-1)e}
defines a principal polarization, the map
x\mapsto[\Theta_{(g-1)e+x}]-[\Theta_{(g-1)e}]=[\Theta_{(g-1)e}]\star([x]-[0])
induces an isomorphism
\varphi\colon J(k)\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{1}(J)_{\hom}
.
Since
\varphi(x_{e})
is torsion, it follows that
x_{e}\in J(k)
is itself torsion, as desired.
∎

The principal polarization defines an ample class
\ell\in{\rm CH}_{(1)}^{1}(J)
, which induces a Lefschetz decomposition
\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}(J)=\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}_{\mathrm{prim}}(J)\oplus\ell^{g-2}\cdot\mathfrak{h}^{1}(J)
as in §
2.5
.

Corollary 2.9
.

Suppose that
(2g-2)e=K_{C}
in
{\rm CH}_{0}(C)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
.
Then
[C]_{1}\in{\rm CH}^{g-1}(\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}_{\mathrm{prim}}(J))
.

Proof.

By definition and the discussion in §
2.4
,
[C]_{1}\in{\rm CH}_{(1)}^{g-1}(J)={\rm CH}^{g-1}(\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}(J))
.
Since
x_{e}
is torsion,
(n)_{*}([0]-[x_{e}])=0
for some integer
n\geq 1
.
The decomposition (
2.2
) implies that
[0]=[x_{e}]
in
{\rm CH}_{0}(J)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
.
Therefore (
2.3
) shows that
[C]_{0}^{\star(g-2)}\star[C]_{1}=0
.
Using properties of the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-action on
{\rm CH}^{*}(J)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
(in the sense of
[
37
, §1.3]
), this implies that
\ell\cdot[C]_{1}=0
.
Since
{\rm CH}^{g-1}(\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}_{\mathrm{prim}}(J))
equals the kernel of
\ell\cdot(-)\colon{\rm CH}^{g-1}(\mathfrak{h}^{2g-3}(J))\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{g-1}(\mathfrak{h}^{2g-1}(J)(1))

∎

Let

.

Proof.

).

.

Lemma 2.11
.

If

are degree-

divisors such that

is torsion, then

.

Suppose

has order

{\rm CH}_{0}(C)
.
Then

, hence
(n)_{*}([\iota_{e}(C)]-[\iota_{e^{\prime}}(C)])=0
in

.
On the other hand, the decomposition (

) shows that

Therefore

Hence
\kappa_{C,e}-\kappa_{C,e^{\prime}}
is torsion too.
∎

Let
\kappa(C)
be the image of
\kappa_{C,e}
in
{\rm CH}_{1}(J)_{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
for any choice of degree-
1
divisor

on

such that
(2g-2)e=K_{C}
in

.

shows that this class is independent of the choice of

.
(If

be the image of

in
{\rm Gr}_{1}(J)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
.
Since all degree-

is also the image of

in

in

.

Suppose now that

in

.
Since

.

.

In the above notation,

in

if and only if

for all

.

.

Proposition

shows that the vanishing of

.
The next lemma isolates a summand of the Chow group that contains

.
Recall that if

In the above notation,

The proof of Theorem

Proposition 3.3

Let

Proof.

Let

The polarization on

We now show that the Hodge conjecture for

By the Hodge conjecture, there exist morphisms of motives

Since

Hence

Using the Lefschetz isomorphism

4.
Picard curves

4.1.
Generalities

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero.
A Picard curve over
k
is by definition a nice curve with an affine model
y^{3}=f(x)=x^{4}+ax^{2}+bx+c
for some
a,b,c\in k
.
Conversely, given such a polynomial
f(x)\in k[x]
of nonzero discriminant, the projective closure of
y^{3}=f(x)
in
{\rm\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
is a nice curve denoted by
C_{f}
.
It has a unique point at infinity
P_{\infty}
, which is
k
-rational.

For every third root of unity
\omega\in\bar{k}
, the map
(x,y)\mapsto(x,\omega y)
defines an automorphism of
C_{f,\bar{k}}
. We view
\mu_{3}
as a subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(C_{f,\bar{k}})
in this way. Then
\mu_{3}
also acts on the Jacobian
J_{f,\bar{k}}
by taking images of divisors.

The discriminant of
f
has the following expression:

(4.1)

\displaystyle{\rm disc}(f)=-4a^{3}b^{2}-27b^{4}+16a^{4}c+144ab^{2}c-128a^{2}c^{2}+256c^{3}.

We view
f
as the dehomogenization
F(x,1)
of the quartic form
F(X,Z)=X^{4}+aX^{2}Z^{2}+bXZ^{3}+cZ^{4}
, and we
define
I(f)
and
J(f)
to be the usual
I
- and
J
-invariants attached to
F
, as in
[
10
, §2]
. Their explicit formulae in our case are:

\displaystyle I(f)

\displaystyle=a^{2}+12c,

\displaystyle J(f)

\displaystyle=72ac-2a^{3}-27b^{2}.

The 19th century invariant theorists observed the identity
J(f)^{2}=4I(f)^{3}-27\cdot{\rm disc}(f)
, which can be verified by direct computation. Therefore
P_{f}:=(I(f),J(f))
is a
k
-point on the elliptic curve

E_{f}\colon y^{2}=4x^{3}-27\cdot{\rm disc}(f).

4.2.
Ceresa vanishing criteria

Since
(2g-2)P_{\infty}=4P_{\infty}
is canonical, we may use
P_{\infty}
to embed
C_{f}
in its Jacobian
J_{f}
and define the Ceresa cycle
\kappa_{C_{f},P_{\infty}}\in{\rm CH}_{1}(J_{f})
as in the introduction; we denote it by
\kappa_{f}
for simplicity.
Recall that
\kappa(C_{f})
denotes the image of
\kappa_{f}
in
{\rm CH}_{1}(J_{f})_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
and
\bar{\kappa}(C_{f})
its image in
{\rm Gr}_{1}(J_{f})_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
.
Theorem
C
follows from the following slightly stronger theorems, whose proofs will take up the rest of this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists an integer
N\geq 1

(
depending neither on
f
nor
k)
such that
N\cdot\kappa_{f}\in{\rm CH}^{2}(J_{f})_{\mathrm{alg}}
for every Picard curve
C_{f}
over every algebraically closed field
k
(of characteristic zero).

Theorem 4.2
.

The Ceresa cycle
\kappa_{f}\in{\rm CH}_{1}(J)
is torsion if and only if
P_{f}\in E_{f}(k)
is torsion.
Moreover, if
k
is algebraically closed, there exists an integer
M\geq 1
with the following property: if
C_{f}
is a Picard curve and
\kappa_{f}
is torsion, then
\ord(\kappa_{f})
divides
M\cdot\ord(P_{f})
and
\ord(P_{f})
divides
M\cdot\ord(\kappa_{f})
.

Theorem
4.1
will be proven in §
4.4
, and Theorem
4.2
will be proven in §
4.7
.
A standard argument using Lemma
2.1
shows that we may assume
k={\rm\mathbb{C}}
.
So in the remainder of §
4
, all varieties will be over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
, and cohomology will be singular cohomology.

Remark 4.3

Theorem
4.2
generalizes
[

, which considered the special case where

. There, we exploited the bielliptic cover to show that the Ceresa cycle maps via a correspondence to a multiple of the point

on the elliptic curve

, and one checks using the explicit formula for

[

Is it always the case that

4.3.
Multilinear algebra

Our first goal (Proposition
4.7
) is to explicitly identify the abelian variety

of Proposition
3.3
for Picard curves.

Write
\mathcal{O}={\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\omega]
for the ring of Eisenstein integers with
\omega^{2}+\omega+1=0
and let
K={\rm\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-3})
be its fraction field.
Let
C
be a Picard curve over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
with Jacobian variety
J
.
The
\mu_{3}
-action on
C
extends to an embedding
\mathcal{O}\subset{\rm End}(J)
.
Using this action, the singular cohomology group
\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Z}})
is a free
\mathcal{O}
-module of rank
3
, and
\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
is a
3
-dimensional
K
-vector space.
The next lemma says that the criterion of Theorem
B
is always satisfied for Picard curves.

Lemma 4.5
.

\HH^{0}(J,\Omega_{J}^{3})^{\mu_{3}}=0
and the Hodge structure
\HH^{3}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})^{\mu_{3}}
is of type
(1,2)+(2,1)
.

Proof.

Since
\HH^{0}(J,\Omega_{J}^{3})\simeq\bigwedge^{3}\HH^{0}(C,\Omega_{C}^{1})
, the first claim follows from a calculation with differentials (Example
3.4
). The second claim follows from the Hodge decomposition for
\HH^{3}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.
∎

We may view
\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
either as a
K
-vector space or
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space; when we perform tensor operations, we will add the subscript
K
when we view it as a
K
-vector space, and add no subscript otherwise.
For example, cup product induces an isomorphism
\bigwedge^{3}\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})\simeq\HH^{3}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
, and we will use this identification without further mention.

The universal property of exterior powers induces a canonical
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-linear surjection
\bigwedge^{3}\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})\rightarrow\bigwedge^{3}_{K}\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.
It is well known (see
[
39
, Lemma 12(i)]
or
[
18
, Lemma 4.3]
) that this map admits a canonical splitting, which we use to view
\bigwedge_{K}^{3}\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
as a direct summand of
\HH^{3}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.

Lemma 4.6
.

We have
\HH^{3}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})^{\mu_{3}}=\bigwedge_{K}^{3}\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
inside
\HH^{3}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.
Moreover
\dim_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}\HH^{3}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})^{\mu_{3}}=2
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the statements after tensoring with
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
.
Let
g\in\mu_{3}\subset{\rm Aut}(C)
be a nontrivial element.
The action of
g
on
\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})
has eigenvalues (with multiplicity)
\omega,\omega,\omega,\omega^{2},\omega^{2},\omega^{2}
so we can write
\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{C}})=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
where
V_{i}
is the
\omega^{i}
-eigenspace.
Since a three element subset of these eigenvalues have product
1
if and only if they are all equal,
\HH^{3}(J;{\rm\mathbb{C}})^{\mu_{3}}=(\bigwedge^{3}V_{1})\oplus(\bigwedge^{3}V_{2})
.
On the other hand, an argument similar to the proof of
[
18
, Proposition 4.4]
shows
(\bigwedge^{3}_{K}\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}}))\otimes_{K}{\rm\mathbb{C}}=\bigwedge^{3}_{K\otimes{\rm\mathbb{C}}}\HH^{1}(J;{\rm\mathbb{C}})=(\bigwedge^{3}V_{1})\oplus(\bigwedge^{3}V_{2})
, proving the equality.
The explicit description of this subspace shows that it is
2
-dimensional over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
.
∎

Let
E
be the elliptic curve with Weierstrass equation
y^{2}=x^{3}+1
.

Proposition 4.7
.

There is an isomorphism of Hodge structures
\HH^{3}(J;{\rm\mathbb{Q}})^{\mu_{3}}\simeq\HH^{1}(E,{\rm\mathbb{Q}})(-1)
.

Proof.

Since there exists a unique elliptic curve up to isogeny with endomorphism algebra
K
, there exists a unique Hodge structure of dimension

4.5.
The Abel–Jacobi map

If
X
is a smooth variety over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
, we will use the notion of an integral (respectively rational) variation of (pure) Hodge structures over the complex manifold
X({\rm\mathbb{C}})
, called a
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
-VHS (respectively
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-VHS) for short; see
[
50
, §5.3.1]
for definitions.
If
V
is a Hodge structure of odd weight
2k-1
, its intermediate Jacobian
\mathrm{J}(V)
is the complex torus

\displaystyle(V\otimes_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}}{\rm\mathbb{C}})/(F^{k}+V_{\tau}),

where
F^{k}\subset V\otimes_{{\rm\mathbb{Z}}}{\rm\mathbb{C}}
is a part of the descending Hodge filtration and
V_{\tau}
denotes the quotient of
V
by its torsion subgroup.
More generally, if
\mathbb{V}
is a
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
-VHS of weight
2k-1
over
X({\rm\mathbb{C}})
, we can define its intermediate Jacobian
\mathrm{J}(\mathbb{V})\rightarrow X({\rm\mathbb{C}})
, a relative complex torus whose fibers over points

are the classical intermediate Jacobians
\mathrm{J}(\mathbb{V}_{x})
, see
[
50
, §7.1.1]
.
If
\mathbb{V},\mathbb{W}
are two
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
-VHS of odd weight, then
\mathrm{J}(\mathbb{V}(p))=\mathrm{J}(\mathbb{V})
for all
p\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
and a morphism
\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{W}
of
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
-VHS induces a homomorphism of (relative) complex tori
\mathrm{J}(\mathbb{V})\rightarrow\mathrm{J}(\mathbb{W})
.

If
X/{\rm\mathbb{C}}
is a nice variety and

, we write
\mathrm{J}^{p}(X)=\mathrm{J}(\HH^{2p-1}(X({\rm\mathbb{C}});{\rm\mathbb{Z}}))
.
In this situation there is an Abel–Jacobi map

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{AJ}^{p}_{X}\colon{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\hom}\rightarrow\mathrm{J}^{p}(X),

defined in
[
50
, §7.2.1]
.
Moreover, if
S/{\rm\mathbb{C}}
is a smooth variety,
\pi\colon X\rightarrow S
a smooth projective morphism with geometrically integral fibres and
p\in{\rm\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, then
R^{p}\pi_{*}{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
(pushforward of the constant sheaf in the analytic topology) has the structure of a
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
-VHS over
S({\rm\mathbb{C}})
.
If
Z
is a codimension
p
cycle on
X
all of whose components are flat over
S
, and such that
Z_{s}\in{\rm CH}^{p}(X_{s})_{\hom}
for every
s\in S({\rm\mathbb{C}})
, then Griffiths has shown that there exists a holomorphic section
{\rm AJ}(Z)
of the relative complex torus
\mathrm{J}(R^{p}\pi_{*}{\rm\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow S({\rm\mathbb{C}})
with the property that
{\rm AJ}(Z)_{s}={\rm AJ}_{X}^{p}(Z_{s})
for all
s\in S({\rm\mathbb{C}})
; this is called the normal function associated to

.

We record the fact that Abel–Jacobi maps are compatible with correspondences.
Let
X,Y
be nice varieties over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
and let
\gamma\in{\rm CH}^{r+\dim(X)}(X\times Y)
be a correspondence of degree
r
.
This induces for every
p\geq 0
a homomorphism
\gamma_{*}\colon{\rm CH}^{p}(X)\rightarrow{\rm CH}^{p+r}(Y)
via the formula
\alpha\mapsto\pi_{Y,*}(\pi_{X}^{*}(\alpha)\cdot\gamma)
, where
\pi_{X}\colon X\times Y\rightarrow X
and
\pi_{Y}\colon X\times Y\rightarrow Y
denote the projections.
The same formula defines morphism of Hodge structures
\HH^{p}(X;{\rm\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow\HH^{p+2r}(Y)(r)
for every
p
, hence a homomorphism of complex tori
\gamma_{*}\colon\mathrm{J}^{p}(X)\rightarrow\mathrm{J}^{p+r}(Y)
.

Lemma 4.11
.

In the above notation,
\gamma_{*}
sends
{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\hom}
to
{\rm CH}^{p+r}(Y)_{\hom}
.
Moreover for every
\alpha\in{\rm CH}^{p}(X)_{\hom}
,
\gamma_{*}({\rm AJ}_{X}^{p}(\alpha))={\rm AJ}_{Y}^{p+r}(\gamma_{*}(\alpha))
.

Proof.

The first sentence follows from the compatibility of the cycle class map with correspondences
[
50
, Proposition 9.21]
.
To prove the compatibility of the Abel–Jacobi map with correspondences, it suffices to prove the compatibility with pullbacks, pushforwards and intersection product. The case of pullback is elementary, using the definition of
{\rm AJ}^{p}_{X}
in terms of extensions of Hodge structures, see
[
15
, §2.2]
.
The case of pushforward follows from that of pullback and Poincare duality.
Finally, compatibility with intersection product follows from
[
50
, Proposition 9.23]
.
∎

{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
