Parity Games of Bounded Tree-Depth
Konrad Staniszewski

Abstract
The exact complexity of solving parity games is a major open problem. Several authors have searched for efficient algorithms over specific classes of graphs. In particular, Obdržálek showed that for graphs of bounded tree-width or clique-width, the problem is in P \mathrm{P} , which was later improved by Ganardi, who showed that it is even in LOGCFL \mathrm{LOGCFL} (with an additional assumption for clique-width case). Here we extend this line of research by showing that for graphs of bounded tree-depth the problem of solving parity games is in logspace uniform AC 0 {\text{AC}^{0}} . We achieve this by first considering a parameter that we obtain from a modification of clique-width, which we call shallow clique-width. We subsequently provide a suitable reduction.

Introduction

Parity games are two-player games played on directed graphs with ranked vertices

G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V,rank:V\rightarrow\mathbb{N})
. Player
\mathrm{X}
makes a move at vertices from

V_{\mathrm{X}}
by moving a token along one of the outgoing edges.
By making moves, players form a sequence of vertices called a play.
A play may be finite or not, but it must be
exhaustive. That is, whenever there is a possibility to make a move, then a move is made.
Player
\mathrm{E}
wins a play
\pi
if it is either finite and ends at a vertex from
V_{\mathrm{O}}
or is
infinite and the parity of the highest rank that occurs infinitely often is even,
otherwise player
\mathrm{O}
wins.

Parity games play an important role in system verification and synthesis
[
23
]
.
However, the exact complexity of solving parity games is still an open problem.
The fastest known algorithms work in time
n^{\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(1+\frac{d}{\log(n)}\right)\right)}

[
22
,
21
]
, where
n
is the size of the
graph and
d
is the number of different ranks.
The problem was also tackled from the point of view of parameterized complexity.
Obdržálek showed that in graphs with bounded tree-width
[
26
]
or clique-width
[
27
]

parity games can be solved in polynomial time.
The result for tree-width was later improved to
{\text{NC}^{2}}
by Fearnley and Schewe
[
13
]
and to
\mathrm{LOGCFL}
by Ganardi
[
16
]
.
The result for clique-width was improved by Ganardi to
\mathrm{LOGCFL}
under an additional assumption that the
k
-expression is given
[
16
]
.
It was also shown that parity games admit a polynomial-time algorithm on graphs with bounded entanglement
[
4
]
, DAG-width
[
3
]
, or Kelly-width
[
20
]

(for Kelly-width, decomposition is assumed to be a part of the input). For more about these classes and relations between them see
[
17
]
.
Calude, Jain, Khoussainov, Li, and Stephan in their breakthrough work about solving parity games in quasipolynomial time
[
5
]

showed that the problem is fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized by the number of ranks.

In this work, we show that solving parity games on graphs of bounded tree-depth is in logspace uniform
{\text{AC}^{0}}

1
1

1

A problem is said to be in logspace uniform
{\text{AC}^{0}}
if there is a constant
k
, a polynomial
p
, and a deterministic Turing machine that given an input size
N
, generates in
space
\mathcal{O}(\log(N))
the description of a circuit of size
\mathcal{O}(p(N))
and depth at most
k
that solves the problem instances of size
N
.
The circuit can be viewed as a directed graph with vertices of in-degree
0
denoted as input, out-degree
0
as output, and other vertices
labeled with boolean operations (
\wedge
,
\vee
or
\neg
; both
\wedge
and
\vee
can take an arbitrary number of arguments).
Edges show computation flow. In such a graph, vertices are usually called gates.
For a more detailed definition see
[
2
]
.
.
We start by introducing a parameter, which we call shallow clique-width, in
\cref
sec:def_not.
In
\cref
sec:tools, we introduce tools that are subsequently used in
\cref
sec:circ_for_scw to create an
{\text{AC}^{0}}
algorithm for parity games on
graphs of bounded shallow clique-width.
In
\cref
sec:circ_td, we show how to efficiently reduce the problem of solving parity games on graphs of bounded tree-depth
to solving parity games on graphs of bounded shallow clique-width.
Our result improves over the ones of
Obdržálek and Ganardi when considering graphs of bounded tree-depth.
That is because logspace uniform
{\text{AC}^{0}}
is a strict subclass of
\mathrm{LOGCFL}
(parity is not in
{\text{AC}^{0}}

[
14
,
1
]
),
and graphs of bounded tree-depth have bounded tree-width.
In addition to this, our intermediate result for shallow clique-width is an example of an
{\text{AC}^{0}}
algorithm for solving parity games
on a class of graphs that are not sparse (assuming an appropriate graph description is provided).

Related Work

The relation between uniform
{\text{AC}^{0}}
and tree-depth was studied in
[
10
,
11
]
.
Elberfeld, Jakoby, and Tantau showed that
{\mathrm{MSO}}
definable problems are in uniform
{\text{AC}^{0}}
when
considering structures whose Gaifman graphs have bounded tree-depth
[
11
]
.
Elberfeld, Grohe, and Tantau showed that on graphs of bounded tree-depth
{\mathrm{FO}}
,
{\mathrm{MSO}}
, and
{\mathrm{GSO}}
(guarded second-order logic)
have the same expressive power
[
10
]
.
The question of whether the winning regions of parity games are definable in
{\mathrm{MSO}}
is unsolved for the case when the number of ranks is unbounded.
Dawar and Grädel showed that they are not definable in
\mu
-calculus (a fragment of
{\mathrm{MSO}}
),
but can be defined in
{\mathrm{GSO}}
if we use a quasi-order over vertices (having a higher rank) along with a unary predicate
\mathrm{OddRank}

[
8
]
.
However, this along with
[
10
,
11
]
does not place parity games on graphs of bounded tree-depth in uniform
{\text{AC}^{0}}

since the tree-depth of the Gaifman graphs of the structures considered in
[
8
]
is unbounded due to the aforementioned quasi-order.

We use a dynamic programming approach along the graph decomposition similar to the one of Obdržálek
[
27
]
.
However, dealing with
{\text{AC}^{0}}
requires more technical care. In particular, we need to provide definitions of operations that are computable in logspace uniform
{\text{AC}^{0}}
.
Another difference is that we don’t restrict our attention to
t
-strategies but to a potentially larger set of positional strategies. We also provide the efficient reduction
from solving parity games on graphs of bounded tree-depth to solving parity games on graphs of bounded shallow clique-width.

Definitions and Notation

2.1
Basic Notation

We use
g={{f}[y\mapsto c]}
to define
g
that coincides with
f
except that
g(y)=c
.
To define a partial function that coincides with
f
but is undefined on
y
we write
{{f}[y\mapsto\text{undefined}]}
.
By
{\mathrm{Dom}(f)}
we denote the set of elements for which
f
is defined.
Given a function
f:A\rightarrow B
and finite or infinite sequence
s=a_{0},a_{1},\ldots
of elements of
A
,
we denote application of
f

to
s
by
f(s)=f(a_{0}),f(a_{1}),\ldots
.

2.2
Arenas, Games, Colorings

An
arena
is a directed graph
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V)
with vertices partitioned between players
\mathrm{E}
and
\mathrm{O}
.
In a parity game
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V,rank:V\rightarrow\mathbb{N})
, the behavior of player
\mathrm{X}
is modeled by a partial function

\rho_{\mathrm{X}}:V^{*}\times V_{\mathrm{X}}\rightharpoonup V

called
strategy
,
which given the sequence of visited vertices and the vertex that the token is on, gives
the next vertex to move the token to.
Every strategy must be exhaustive and correct – whenever there is a possibility of player movement,
then the function must be defined,
and the token can only be moved along existing edges.
A strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{X}}
is
positional
if the result depends only on the
vertex the token is on (i.e. for a vertex
v\in V_{\mathrm{X}}
and a prefix of a play
\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi}
we have
\rho_{\mathrm{X}}(\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi},v)=\rho_{\mathrm{X}}(v)
).
A play
\pi
is consistent with
\rho_{\mathrm{X}}
if whenever it is the turn of player
\mathrm{X}
,
then the move is made according to
\rho_{\mathrm{X}}
.
A strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{X}}
is winning for player
\mathrm{X}
from a
vertex
v
if all plays starting at
v
and consistent with
\rho_{\mathrm{X}}
are winning for player
\mathrm{X}
.
It is known that parity games are globally positionally determined
[
12
]
. That is for
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E,rank)

there exists a partition of
V
into the winning regions
{W_{\mathrm{E}}}
and
{W_{\mathrm{O}}}
such that

\mathrm{E}
has a positional strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
that wins from every vertex in
{W_{\mathrm{E}}}
, and

\mathrm{O}
has a positional strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{O}}
that wins from every vertex in
{W_{\mathrm{O}}}
.

We define
\Pi_{G}(\rho_{\mathrm{X}},v)
as the set of plays in the arena
G
that start at the vertex

v
and are consistent with the strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{X}}
for player
\mathrm{X}
,
whereas by
\overset{\rightarrow}{\Pi}_{G}(\rho_{\mathrm{X}},v,w)

we define their prefixes that end at
w
(i.e.
\overset{\rightarrow}{\Pi}_{G}(\rho_{\mathrm{X}},v,w)=\{\pi[\ldots i]:\pi[i]=w\wedge\pi\in\Pi_{G}(\rho_{\mathrm{X}},v)\}
).
To distinguish a play from its prefix, we denote plays using the symbol
\pi
and prefixes of plays using

\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi}
.

We say that
Color:V\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}}{}
is a player-aware coloring of vertices from

G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E)
if vertices from
V_{X}
are colored using colors from
C_{\mathrm{X}}
.
We will sometimes write a parity game along with player-aware coloring of its arena and say that

G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime}=V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{O}},E^{\prime},rank^{\prime},Color^{\prime})
is a subgame of
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E,rank,Color)

if and only if
V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{E}}\subseteq V_{\mathrm{E}}
,
V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{O}}\subseteq V_{\mathrm{O}}
,
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
,

rank^{\prime}
is
rank
with domain restricted to
V^{\prime}
and
Color^{\prime}
is
Color
with domain restricted to
V^{\prime}
.

2.3
Shallow Clique-Width for Arenas

Here we define shallow clique-width for arenas, which
can be viewed as clique-width
[
6
,
7
]
with unions of arbitrary number of components (instead of only binary ones)
and restricted term height.

Definition 1
.

A
tree-model

\mathcal{TM}=(T,C=C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}},Color,D)
consists of a rooted tree
T
, with all root-to-leaf paths having the same length, a set of colors
C=C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}}
, a coloring

Color
of leaves of
T
, and a function
D
that assigns to each internal node of
T
a subset of
C\times C
.

For a tree-model
\mathcal{TM}=(T,C=C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}},Color,D)
and a node
l
of
T
, by
\mathcal{TM}_{l}
we mean a
tree-model
(T_{l},C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}},Color^{\prime},D^{\prime})
where
T_{l}
is the subtree of
T

rooted at
l
,
Color^{\prime}
is
Color
with domain restricted to leaves of
T_{l}
, and
D^{\prime}
is
D
with domain
restricted to internal nodes of
T_{l}
.

Definition 2
.

We define an arena
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V)
induced by
\mathcal{TM}=(T,C=C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}},Color,D)
in the following way.

•

If
T
consists of one node
v
and
Color(v)\in C_{\mathrm{X}}
, then it induces edgeless
G
with
V_{\mathrm{X}}=\{v\}
.

•

Otherwise, let
l
be
T
’s root and let
l_{1},\ldots l_{m}
be children of
l
.
The arena induced by
\mathcal{TM}
is created by taking union of arenas induced by

\mathcal{TM}_{l_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{TM}_{l_{m}}
, and
adding a directed edge from every vertex colored
c
to every vertex colored
c^{\prime}
whenever

{\langle c,c^{\prime}\rangle}\in D(l)
.

For an example of a tree-model and the induced arena see
\cref
fig:ef_tm_example.

Definition 3
.

The
shallow clique-width
of an arena
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V)
is the smallest
k

such that there exists a tree-model
\mathcal{TM}=(T,C=C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}},Color,D)
with

|C|\leq k
and
T
of height at most
k
such that the induced arena is
G
.

One might observe that shallow clique-width is similar to the notion of shrub-depth
[
19
,
18
]
.
The main difference, however,
is that for a shallow clique-width’s tree-model
\mathcal{TM}=(T,C=C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}},Color,D)
and an internal node
l
of
T
a pair
{\langle c,c^{\prime}\rangle}\in D(l)

affects all pairs
{\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle}
of leaves in the subtree of
T
rooted at
l
such that
Color(v)=c
and
Color(v^{\prime})=c^{\prime}
,
whereas in shrub-depth it is additionally restricted to only those for which the lowest common ancestor in
T
is
l
.

It is worth mentioning that if a class of graphs has bounded shallow clique-width, then it
has bounded clique-width and shrub-depth.
The former observation follows from the definition of clique-width,
the latter one from the fact that in the shallow clique-width’s tree-model there are at most
2^{|C\times C|}
sets
D(l)

at each level of the tree-model. So at the expense of an increase in the number of colors, we can get rid
of these sets and describe connections using only the distance to the lowest common ancestor and colors.

2.4
Tree-Depth

Tree-depth was originally defined for undirected graphs
[
24
]
.
Here we adapt this definition to arenas by simply forgetting the orientation of edges.

Definition 4
.

A forest of rooted trees
\mathcal{F}
is an
elimination forest
of an arena
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V)

if vertices of
\mathcal{F}
are vertices of
G
and

{\langle v,w\rangle}\in E
implies that
v
and
w
are in the same tree
T
in
\mathcal{F}

and either
v
is an ancestor of
w
or
w
is an ancestor of
v
in
T
(see
\cref
fig:ef_tm_example).
Tree-depth of an arena
G
is the smallest height
of an elimination forest of
G
.

Lemma 5
.

[
25
, Chapter 6.2]

If an arena
G
has an elimination forest of height
k
, then any simple path in
G

has length at most
2^{k+1}-2
.

The lemma above can be proven by induction on the height of the elimination forest.

One can think of graphs of bounded tree-depth as graphs of bounded tree-width
[
9
, Chapter 12]
with an
additional constraint on the height of the tree-decomposition. More precisely, if
a graph
G
has a tree-decomposition of height
h
where each bag contains at most
k
vertices,
then it has tree-depth at most
k(h+1)

[
25
, Chapter 6.4]
(to obtain the elimination forest from the tree-decomposition, we leave each vertex of
G
in the bag that is closest to
the root of the tree-decomposition and then replace each bag in the tree-decomposition with a path of its elements).
What is more, if a graph has tree-depth
k
, then it admits a tree-decomposition of height bounded by
k+1
where each bag contains at most
k+2
vertices
(to obtain the tree-decomposition, we take the elimination forest
\mathcal{F}
,
make it a tree
T
by choosing a root
r
of an arbitrary tree in
\mathcal{F}
and connecting other roots to
r
,
and define bags for vertices of
T
as sets of vertices on vertex-
r
paths).

Shallow clique-width is more general than tree-depth.
First, for an arena
G
of tree-depth
k
one can use
2(k+1)3^{k+1}
colors to create a tree-model of height
k+1

that induces
G
. This is because we can take some elimination forest
\mathcal{F}
of
G
of height
k
and color each vertex according to its player-membership in
G
, and depth and connections to ancestors in
\mathcal{F}

(for each vertex
v
and its ancestor
w
we have that either there is a directed edge from
v
to
w
, to
v
from
w
, or
v
and
w
are not connected in
G
).
On the other hand, arenas where each vertex has a directed edge to every vertex have small shallow clique-width, but large tree-depth.

The major advantage of tree-depth over shallow clique-width is that given an arena
G
of tree-depth at most
k
, we know how to efficiently find
an elimination forest of
G
of height at most
k
(details in
\cref
sec:EX).
It is also worth noting that despite the simplicity of tree-depth, some problems are
{W[1]\text{-}hard}
when parameterized by it
[
15
, Lemma 2, Proposition 5]
.

As mentioned before, in this work, we take a detour by first proving that solving parity games played on arenas of bounded
shallow clique-width is in logspace uniform
{\text{AC}^{0}}
(assuming an appropriate graph description is provided) and then providing a reduction.
We decided to do so as thinking about vertices of the same color as the ones that will be treated similarly
turned out to be conceptually simpler from our perspective. What is more, such an approach gave us a
logspace uniform family of
{\text{AC}^{0}}
-circuits for a class of graphs that are not sparse.

3
Tools

Now we proceed with tools for constructing a logspace uniform family of circuits for
parity games played on arenas with
bounded shallow clique-width.
The approach presented here is similar to the one used in
[
27
]
for creating a polynomial-time algorithm for parity games
played on arenas of bounded clique-width.
Here we also operate on some succinct information characterizing strategies that we call enforcements.
Enforcements are an adaptation of Obdržálek’s borders
[
26
]
from tree-width to shallow clique-width.
The main idea behind borders is to store for a separating set
S
, a strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
, and a vertex
v
information about the worst arrivals at vertices
of
S

that opponent can achieve when starting from
v
and playing against
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
,
whereas in enforcements we are interested in knowing what the worst arrivals at vertices of a specific color can be.
Our adaptation of borders is analogous to the one considered in
[
27
]
.

We
additionally extend and modify this approach
to obtain a logspace uniform family of constant-depth circuits.

3.1
Parity Games With Stop

Definition 6
.

We define a
stop parity game
as a parity game where player
\mathrm{E}
has an additional
move called
{\scriptstyle{STOP}}
at his positions.
Executing this move ends the game in a draw.

Observation 7
.

In an arena
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V)
with vertices ranked by
rank:V\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
,
winning regions for player
\mathrm{E}
in both stop and normal parity games
are equal. What is more if a strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
is winning in either of the games,
then it is winning in the other one, as
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
cannot be forced to use
{\scriptstyle{STOP}}
.

Definition 8
.

We say that a strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
is
safe from

v
in a stop parity game
G
,
if all plays that start at
v
and are consistent with
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
are not losing for player
\mathrm{E}
.

Stop parity games will turn out to be useful for solving parity games on arenas that can be extended.

3.2
Enforcements

Definition 9
.

Let
\mathrm{elem}(s)
denote the set of vertices that appear in the sequence
s
.
For a game
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E,rank)
, we define a function

mr_{G}(\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi})=\max(\mathrm{elem}(rank(\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi})))
.
That is for a prefix of a play, return the maximum rank of its vertex.

Definition 10
.

We define a total order
\prec
on natural numbers as
p\prec q\Leftrightarrow(-1)^{p}p<(-1)^{q}q
and

p\preceq q\Leftrightarrow(p\prec q\vee p=q)
. That is
\ldots\prec 5\prec 3\prec 1\prec 0\prec 2\prec 4\prec 6\prec\ldots
.

Definition 11
.

For a stop parity game
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E,rank)
, a strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
and
v\in V

we define a partial function

\displaystyle\mathrm{venf}^{G}_{\rho_{\mathrm{E}},v}(w)=\begin{cases}{\min_{\preceq}}{\{mr_{G}(\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi}):\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi}\in\overset{\rightarrow}{\Pi}_{G}(\rho_{\mathrm{E}},v,w)\wedge\rho_{\mathrm{E}}(\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi})={\scriptstyle{STOP}}}\}&\text{if }w\in V_{\mathrm{E}}.\\
{\min_{\preceq}}{\{mr_{G}(\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi}):\overset{\rightarrow}{\pi}\in\overset{\rightarrow}{\Pi}_{G}(\rho_{\mathrm{E}},v,w)\}}&\text{if }w\in V_{\mathrm{O}}\end{cases}

Where
{\min_{\preceq}}(A)
is a partial function that for a finite subset of natural numbers
A

gives the smallest element of
A
according to
\preceq
and is undefined for
A=\emptyset
.

Intuitively for
w\in V_{\mathrm{O}}
the operation above returns the worst possible arrival at
w
that player
\mathrm{O}
can force when playing
against the strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
and for
w\in V_{\mathrm{E}}
the worst arrival at
w
such that
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}

will decide to stop the game.

Definition 12
.

We naturally adapt the definition above to games
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E,rank)
with player-aware coloring
Color:V\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}}{}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{enf}^{G}_{\rho_{\mathrm{E}},v}(c)={\min_{\preceq}}{\{\mathrm{venf}^{G}_{\rho_{\mathrm{E}},v}(w):w\in{\mathrm{Dom}(\mathrm{venf}^{G}_{\rho_{\mathrm{E}},v})}\wedge Color(w)=c\}}

An
enforcement
is any partial function from
C
to
\{0,\ldots,\max(rank(V))\}
.
From now on by

enforcement from
v

of a strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
in
G

we will mean

\mathrm{enf}^{G}_{\rho_{\mathrm{E}},v}
. Note that this is always an enforcement
and that
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
can have different enforcements from different vertices (see
\cref
fig:enf_example).

Operations on Enforcements

Clearly, modifications of a strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}

can result in modifications of enforcements of this strategy.
What is more, some of these modifications can be done directly on the enforcements without knowing
the original strategy.
In the further parts of this work, we will be interested in
modifications that adapt strategies of player
\mathrm{E}
from an arena
G

to an arena
G^{\prime}
, that was made from
G
by addition of directed edges
between vertices of specified colors.
It will usually suffice to remember enforcements of a
strategy instead of the strategy itself and to perform respective operations
on enforcements. Below we introduce some useful operations on enforcements.

The first operation
corresponds to a modification of a strategy
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
to the strategy
\rho^{\prime}_{\mathrm{E}}

that behaves like
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
, but each time
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
decides to
{\scriptstyle{STOP}}
at some vertex of a specified color

c

\rho^{\prime}_{\mathrm{E}}
uses the option to move to the vertex from which the game was started. Moreover, we want to ensure that
\rho^{\prime}_{\mathrm{E}}

will never close a cycle with the highest rank being odd by doing so.

Definition 15
.

For
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V,rank:V\rightarrow\mathbb{N},Color:V\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}})
,
enforcement
P
and color
c
we define

\displaystyle\mathrm{loop}(P,c)=\begin{cases}P&\text{if }c\in C_{\mathrm{E}}\wedge c\not\in{\mathrm{Dom}(P)}\\
{{P}[c\mapsto\text{undefined}]}&\text{if }c\in C_{\mathrm{E}}\wedge P(c)\text{ is even }\end{cases}

Please note that
\mathrm{loop}(P,c)
is undefined if
c\in C_{\mathrm{O}}
or

P(c)
is odd.
Let
P
be the enforcement of
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
from some vertex
v
.
Let
c
be any element of
C_{\mathrm{E}}
and assume that from
each vertex of color
c
there is an edge to
v
.
Now, the first case in the definition of
\mathrm{loop}(P,c)
corresponds
to the situation when
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
will never stop at a vertex of color
c

when starting the game from
v
. Whereas the second one to the situation
when
\rho_{\mathrm{E}}
can be forced to stop at a vertex of color
c
, but the prefix
of the play that will lead here will always have even max rank.

Definition 16
.

For
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V,rank:V\rightarrow\mathbb{N},Color:V\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}})
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
,

c
and enforcements
P
,
Q
, we define two partial functions

\displaystyle\mathrm{lift}(Q,r)(c)

\displaystyle=\max(Q(c),r)\;\text{if }c\in{\mathrm{Dom}(Q)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{\overset{\scalebox{2}[1]{$\sim$}}{\min}}(P,Q)(c)

\displaystyle={\min_{\preceq}}(P(c),Q(c))

In this writing, we allow one of
{\min_{\preceq}}(P(c),Q(c))
arguments to be undefined

4
Circuit Family for Shallow Clique-Width

Now we will show how for

(and therefore time

the circuit
\mathbb{SCW}_{\langle n,d,k\rangle}

of depth
{\mathcal{O}(k^{3})}
, that given the specific representation of a parity game
of shallow clique-width at most

4.1
Overview

The circuit will input parity game’s

It is worth noting that the approach presented in this section fails for parity games of bounded shrub-depth.
In short, that is because it makes use of the fact that in shallow clique-width,
we specify connections using colors, whereas in shrub-depth, we
additionally condition connections on the lowest common ancestor in the tree-model.
To be more precise, when constructing an arena bottom-up from shallow clique-width’s tree-model

\mathcal{TM}=(T,C=C_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus C_{\mathrm{O}},Color,D)

during the processing of an internal vertex
l
of
T

for

of

to each leaf of

of color

is the lowest common ancestor of the vertices to be connected. This means that in shrub-depth a vertex is
distinguished not only by its color but also by the information which subtree of

has the vertex as a leaf.
Simple attempts to mitigate that either increase the depth of the circuit to be logarithmic or
the number of possible enforcements to be exponential in the number of vertices.

\mathrm{U}_{\mathrm{E}}^{s,t}

and
\mathrm{U}_{\mathrm{O}}^{s,t}
are up-closed.
Note also that
\mathbb{U}^{\langle s,t\rangle}
has constant depth and can be computed in space
\mathcal{O}(\log(n)+\log(d)k)
.
This is because
\mathcal{O}{(\log(n))}
bits suffice to remember a constant number of vertices.
What is more, as each enforcement is a partial function from the set of
k
colors to the set of
d
ranks, so to encode one

\mathcal{O}(\log(d)k)
bits suffice, and we consider at most four of them simultaneously.

Lemma 24
.

Given that
\mathbb{U}^{\langle s,t\rangle}
inputs a valid set of enforcements for
each vertex
v
of a subgame
G^{\prime}
of game
G
then it outputs a valid set of enforcements for
each vertex
v
of a game
G^{\prime\prime}
created from
G^{\prime}
by addition of directed edge from every vertex of
color
s
to every vertex of color
t
.

A simple case analysis suffices to prove this lemma when
s
belongs to player
\mathrm{O}
.
When
s
belongs to player
\mathrm{E}
one can separately prove soundness (again by simple case analysis)
and completeness following the presented intuition.
As these proofs are rather long and tedious
they have been postponed to
\cref
sec:ski_lem_uev.

4.3.4
Conditional Update Gadget

For each pair of colors
{\langle s,t\rangle}
we define
\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{\langle s,t\rangle}

that inputs the bits that
\mathbb{U}^{\langle s,t\rangle}
inputs with one additional bit
that is
{\mathrm{true}}
if and only if the update defined by
\mathbb{U}^{\langle s,t\rangle}

should be performed (i.e. whether enforcements should be updated or copied).
We define
\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{\langle s,t\rangle}
to be a simple combination
of
\mathbb{U}^{\langle s,t\rangle}
and
\mathrm{COMBINE}^{2,n(d+1)^{k}}
.

4.3.5
Processing Gadget

Here we define a gadget of depth
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
that stacks operations of conditional update gadgets.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k^{2}}
be pairs of colors in some fixed order.
We define the gadget
\mathbb{P}
as a sequence of gadgets
\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{p_{1}},\ldots\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{p_{k^{2}}}

where
\mathit{ENF}_{v}^{\mathrm{in}}
part of the input for gadget
\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{p_{i}}
comes from the output of
\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{p_{i-1}}

for
i>1
and rest of the input of
\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{p_{j}}
for
j>0
comes from the input
of
\mathbb{P}
. In particular for
\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{p_{j}}
the bit that tells whether to perform the update comes from
the pack of
k^{2}
bits (denoted as
{DB}^{\mathrm{in}}
) that
\mathbb{P}
inputs aside from
\mathit{ECOLOR}{}
and

COLOR_{v}
,
\mathit{ENF}_{v}^{\mathrm{in}}
,
{A}^{\mathrm{in}}[v]
for each
v
. We define the output of
\mathbb{P}
to be the output of
\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{p_{k^{2}}}

and
{A}^{\mathrm{out}}[v]={A}^{\mathrm{in}}[v]
for each
v
.

Note that
\mathbb{P}
defined above has depth
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
and can be computed in space

\mathcal{O}(\log(n)+\log(d)k)
, as each of the conditional update gadgets can be computed in this space, and we can
use
\mathcal{O}(\log(k))
bits to encode for which
\mathbb{U}_{cond}^{p_{j}}
we are performing the computation.

Let
l
be an internal node of
T
from the given tree-model and let
l_{1},\ldots,l_{m}
be children of
l
. Gadget
\mathbb{P}
will be later
used to calculate valid enforcement sets for a subgame of
G
played on an arena induced by
\mathcal{TM}_{l}

from valid enforcement sets for a subgame of
G
played on an union of arenas induced by
\mathcal{TM}_{l_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{TM}_{l_{m}}
.

4.4
Circuit Construction

The construction of the circuit will proceed in layers, with each layer output being
n
big packs of bits,
each containing
\mathit{ENF}_{v}^{\mathrm{out}}
and

for each vertex

The output of each layer will be treated as the input for the next one.
The output of
l

\mathit{LPACK}_{l}
.
To start the construction off and create layer

, we use
\mathrm{INIT}_{v}
gadget for each vertex

.
To create parts for layer

we need to use
\mathbb{P}
for each node
{\langle l,i\rangle}

We do so by setting
\mathbb{P}
’s

whereas
\mathit{ENF}_{v}^{\mathrm{in}}
and

bits using

with input being
n
big bit packs from
\mathit{LPACK}_{l-1}
and
\mathit{COND}[j]=\mathit{CHILD}_{l,i}[j]

(here
\mathrm{COMBINE}^{n,n((d+1)^{k}+1)}
is used to take a disjoint union of arenas).
The output of
\mathbb{P}
here constitutes to
\mathit{LPACK}_{l}
.

To produce partition of
V
into winning regions
we first gather calculated results using
\mathrm{COMBINE}^{n,n((d+1)^{k}+1)}
gadget with input being set to

n
big bit packs from
\mathit{LPACK}_{k}
and
\mathit{COND}[j]={\mathrm{true}}
, and then
create for each vertex

gadget that checks whether there is an enforcement

such that

is
{\mathrm{true}}
and for each

{\mathrm{false}}
.

.

There exists a Turing machine that given

, and

5
Circuit Family for Tree-Depth

Now we will show how for
n,d>1
, and
k\geq 0
compute in space

\mathcal{O}(\log(n)+\log(d)k)
circuit
\mathbb{TD}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}

of depth being at most
{\mathcal{O}(k^{3})}
, that
given an
n
-vertex parity game with all ranks being less that
d
(we also require
d\leq 2n
)
and elimination forest of height at most
k
, outputs the winner for each vertex.
We achieve this by creating circuit
\mathbb{RED}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}
that changes input representation
to the one accepted by
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}{}
, and then getting the winners
from the output of
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}{}

using circuit
\mathbb{GET}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}
.
Note that along with the construction of the circuit
\mathbb{RED}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}

we show a more efficient reduction that does not have an exponential blow-up
in
k
(in contrast to the simple one presented in
\cref
ssec:td_intro).
Such improvement is not necessary for the main result of this paper, but it makes
the construction more computationally tractable.
In the end, we show how to get rid of the requirement for the elimination forest.

5.1
Circuit Input

Circuit
\mathbb{RED}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}
inputs a game
G=(V=V_{\mathrm{E}}\uplus V_{\mathrm{O}},E\subseteq V\times V,rank:V\rightarrow\mathbb{N})

and elimination forest
\mathcal{F}
as the following sequence of bits.

•

For each vertex
v\in V
:

–

d
bits that one-hot encode

(denoted as
\mathit{RANK}_{v}
),

–

one bit that is true if and only if
v
is a vertex
of player
\mathrm{E}
(denoted as
\mathit{EB}[v]
),

–

k+1
bits that one-hot encode the depth of
v
in
\mathcal{F}

(denoted as
\mathit{DEPTH}_{v}
; bit
\mathit{DEPTH}_{v}[x]
is set to true if
v
has depth
x
; root has depth
0
),

–

n-1
bits that encode the parent of
v
in
\mathcal{F}

(denoted as
\mathit{PARENT}_{v}
;
\mathit{PARENT}_{v}[w]
is
{\mathrm{true}}
if
w
is a parent of
v
in
\mathcal{F}
;
each non-root vertex has exactly one parent).

•

For each pair of vertices
v,w\in V
, a bit that is set to true if and only if
there is a directed edge from
v
to
w
(denoted as
\mathit{EDG}[v,w]
).

Checking whether
\mathcal{F}
is a valid forest can be done similarly as in
\cref

For ancestor-descendant relationship one can compute in space
\mathcal{O}(\log(n)+\log(k))
a gadget of depth
\mathcal{O}(\log(k))

that at level
i
checks for every

whether there is a simple path from

to

that goes towards the root
and has length at most

.

5.2
Reduction Circuit

First of all, we do not aim to create exactly the same game as input for
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}
,
but a modified game from which we could deduce winning regions in the original one.
The main idea here is to split each vertex in a number of vertices such that
when we merge them back, then we get the original arena.
Let
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be all vertices of
G
, with each vertex
v\in V

we associate
2n
copies of
v
with the same player ownership:

v\rightarrow{{{v}_{v_{1}}^{{\circ}{}}}},\ldots,{{{v}_{v_{n}}^{{\circ}{}}}},{{{v}_{v_{1}}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}},\ldots,{{{v}_{v_{n}}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}
.
The first
n
of these copies
are denoted as
{{{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}
, whereas the last
n
as
{{{v}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}
.
Each of them corresponds to one additional vertex from the input of
\mathbb{RED}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}

(the copy among the first
n
copies of
v
that corresponds to
w
is denoted as
{{{v}_{w}^{{\circ}{}}}}
,
whereas the copy among the last
n
copies that corresponds to
w
is denoted as
{{{v}_{w}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}
).
Intuitively for a vertex
v\in V
, copies from
{{{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}
and
{{{v}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}

will be used to distribute the original connections from and to
v
. More precisely,
vertices from
{{{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}
will allow to choose a connection from
v
, whereas vertices from
{{{v}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}

to execute it, and if there will be a connection from
v
to
w
in
G
, then
we will create a connection from some
{{{v}_{v^{\prime}}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}
to some
{{{w}_{w^{\prime}}^{{\circ}{}}}}
.

We set the rank of
copies from
{{{v}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}
to
rank(v)+2
, whereas
the rank of copies from
{{{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}
to
1
if
v
belongs to player
\mathrm{E}
, and to

0
if
v
belongs to player
\mathrm{O}
(as
{{{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}
will be used to choose a connection we don’t want
a player to put off the decision indefinitely).
Let
x
be the depth of
v
in
\mathcal{F}
.
We color copies
{{{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}
using color
{\langle{\circ},\mathit{EB}[v],x\rangle}
,
whereas copies from
{{{v}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}
using
{\langle{\rightarrow},\mathit{EB}[v],x\rangle}
.
The copies created here are leaves of the tree-model that
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}
will take as input.
Note that for all operations presented above we can compute in space
\mathcal{O}(\log(n)+\log(k))
gadget of constant depth,
as we need
\mathcal{O}(\log(n))
bits to represent a copy of a vertex and rank and
\mathcal{O}(\log(k))
to represent a color.

Before we go any further, we introduce some gadgets. For simplicity, we will assume that
vertex is its own ancestor.

•

\mathrm{CHILDLESS}_{v}
– that is
{\mathrm{true}}
if and only if
v
has no children in
\mathcal{F}
.

•

\mathrm{ANEQ}_{v}^{w}
– that is
{\mathrm{true}}
if and only if
w
is an ancestor of
v
in
\mathcal{F}
.
Note that it can be done by a gadget with
\mathcal{O}(\log(k))
layers computable in space
\mathcal{O}(\log(n)+\log(k))
that in layer

i
considers paths from vertices towards root of length at most
2^{i}
.

•

\mathrm{ANEQC}_{v}^{x\rightarrow x^{\prime}}
–
that is true if and only there are two ancestor
w
and
w^{\prime}
of
v
of depth
x
and
x^{\prime}
respectively such that there is an an edge from
w
to
w^{\prime}
.

Now we will describe how we compute the part of
\mathbb{RED}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}
that outputs the rest of the tree-model.
The main thing that we will do here is that for each vertex
v
that is a leaf in the given
elimination forest we will gather copies of ancestors that correspond to
v
, add connections using the copies and join
the copies along the way creating a tree-model similar in shape to the given elimination forest.
The computation will consist of
k+1
major steps, and
3k+3
simple steps.
Major steps will create layers of the tree-model (see
\cref
sec:ac0_shrub_circ_in; layers are numbered starting from the layer of leaves, which has number
0
), whereas simple steps will extend the height of the tree-model to
match the specification for
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}{}
.
As in the description of the tree-model for
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}{}
we use graph where number of nodes in every layer is the same and in our case equals number of copies of vertices,
so we will use copies of vertices to number nodes from a specific layer. The tree-model that we
will produce here may not use all of the leaves
(there may be vertices at the bottom that will be left isolated),
we get around it by relaxing the correctness of
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}{}
input
so that it will allow vertices from layer
0
without parents (note that they will be ignored in the further parts of the circuit).
As the steps presented below are rather technical we encourage to refer to
\cref

First the major steps, for
l=1
, for each vertex
v
of the input game that is childless in
\mathcal{F}
,
we gather and initialize copies of ancestors of
v
corresponding to
v
.
That is for each

and

we set

\displaystyle\mathit{CHILD}_{l,{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}}[{{{w}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ANEQ}_{v}^{w}\wedge\mathrm{CHILDLESS}_{v}

\displaystyle\mathit{CHILD}_{l,{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}}[{{{w}_{v}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ANEQ}_{v}^{w}\wedge\mathrm{CHILDLESS}_{v}

\displaystyle{{DB}}_{l,{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}}[{\langle{\langle{\circ},p,x\rangle},{\langle{\rightarrow},p,x\rangle}\rangle}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{CHILDLESS}_{v}

Then we add all connections between gathered copies, that is for

v\in V
,
p,p^{\prime}\in\{{\mathrm{true}},{\mathrm{false}}\}
and
x,x^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
we set

\displaystyle{{DB}}_{l,{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}}[{\langle{\langle{\rightarrow},p,x\rangle},{\langle{\circ},p^{\prime},x^{\prime}\rangle}\rangle}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{CHILDLESS}_{v}\wedge\mathrm{ANEQC}_{v}^{x\rightarrow x^{\prime}}

Now let
1<l\leq k+1
.
Here we create parts that drag low depth vertices up in the tree-model and merge gathered copies.
That is for
p,p^{\prime}\in\{{\mathrm{true}},{\mathrm{false}}\}
,
x\in\{0,\ldots,k-l+1\}
,
w\not=v
we set:

\displaystyle\mathit{CHILD}_{l,{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}}[{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{CHILDLESS}_{v}\wedge\bigvee_{t\in\{0,\ldots,k-l+1\}}{\mathit{DEPTH}_{v}[t]}

\displaystyle\mathit{CHILD}_{l,{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}}[{{{w}_{w}^{{\circ}{}}}}]

\displaystyle=\mathit{DEPTH}_{v}[k-l+1]\wedge\mathit{PARENT}_{w}[v]

\displaystyle{{DB}}_{l,{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}}[{\langle{\langle{\circ},p,k-l+1\rangle},{\langle{\circ},p,k-l+1\rangle}\rangle}]

\displaystyle=\mathit{DEPTH}_{v}[k-l+1]

For simple steps, we extend the height of the tree-model to match specification for
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}
.
Let
v
be some fixed vertex in
V
and
w
any vertex from
V
, and let
k+3\leq l\leq 4k+4
, we set

\mathit{CHILD}_{k+2,{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}}[{{{w}_{w}^{{\circ}{}}}}]
to
\mathit{DEPTH}_{w}[0]
whereas
\mathit{CHILD}_{l,{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}}[{{{v}_{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}]
to
{\mathrm{true}}
.

In both major and simple steps, we set unmentioned bits to
{\mathrm{false}}
.

5.3
Output Circuit

Now we will proceed with the definition of
\mathbb{GET}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}
.
To get the winning region of
\mathrm{E}
we need to check
for each vertex
v\in V_{\mathrm{E}}
whether there is a copy of
{{{v}_{w}^{{\circ}{}}}}
such that
\mathrm{E}
wins from

{{{v}_{w}^{{\circ}{}}}}
and for each
v\in V_{\mathrm{O}}
whether
\mathrm{E}
wins from all copies of
v
.
It suffices as note that we have constructed input for
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}{}
such that
when we contract all
{{{v}^{{\circ}{}}}}
and
{{{v}^{{\rightarrow}{}}}}
into one vertex of rank

then we get the original game.

{OUT}^{\mathbb{GET}{}}[v]=(\mathit{EB}[v]\wedge\bigvee_{w\in V}{{OUT}[{{{v}_{w}^{{\circ}{}}}}]})\vee(\neg\mathit{EB}[v]\wedge\bigwedge_{w\in V}{{OUT}[{{{v}_{w}^{{\circ}{}}}}]})

where

denotes the output bits of
{\mathbb{SCW}_{{\langle 2n^{2},d+2,4k+4\rangle}}}{}
. Note that
\mathbb{GET}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}
can be computed in space

\mathcal{O}(\log(n))
as
\mathcal{O}(\log(n))
bits suffice to handle a constant number of vertices.

.

There exists a Turing machine that given

, and

,

’s vertices into winning regions of

and

.

5.4
Computing Elimination Forest

The following is an easy consequence of the result from
[
11
, Theorem 1]
.

Lemma 28
.

For any
k\geq 0
there exists a logspace uniform family of
{\text{AC}^{0}}
-circuits
that inputs a graph and checks whether its tree-depth is
k
.

Now observe that we can get an elimination forest for an arena of tree-depth at most
k

by a circuit that forgets about the orientation of edges and performs
k
steps.
At step
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
it considers a graph of tree-depth at most
k-i+1
and checks
which vertices should be removed to make it a graph of tree-depth at most
k-i
.
It does so by taking for each vertex
v
its connected component,
removing
v
from it and
checking the tree-depth of the component with
v
removed using a circuit from
\cref
theorem:treedepth_circ.
In case there are many candidates the circuit picks the first one.
From
\cref
theorem:treedepth_circ and
\cref
lemma:treedepth_short_paths,
for fixed
k
this circuit can have constant depth and be computed in logarithmic space
(to compute connected components it suffices to check for paths of length at most
2^{k+1}-2

what can be done similarly as in
\mathrm{ANEQ}_{v}^{w}
).
So from the construction above and
\cref
thm:td_given_solve_log we have the following.

Corollary 29
.

For any
k\geq 0
there exists a logspace uniform family of
{\text{AC}^{0}}
-circuits
that solves parity games played on arenas of tree-depth at most
k
.

6
Conclusions and Further Work

We have shown that solving parity games played on arenas of bounded tree-depth or shallow clique-width is in logspace uniform
{\text{AC}^{0}}
,
assuming that the suitable tree-model is provided for the latter case.
Speaking of uniformity, one can
assign appropriate binary identifiers to gates such that for any
k
, the problem:
“Given circuit parameters
n
,
d
in binary and binary identifiers of two gates
A
,
B
decide whether in
\mathbb{TD}_{{\langle n,d,k\rangle}}
both
A
and
B
are present, and
the output of
B
constitutes to the
input of
A
”
can be solved by a deterministic Turing machine that works in time polylogarithmic in
n
and uses space logarithmic in
n
.
However, going lower seems to require more technical care.
A natural next step in research would be
