The Normal Distributions Indistinguishability Spectrum and its Application to Privacy-Preserving Machine Learning
Yun Lu, Malik Magdon-Ismail, Yu Wei, Vassilis Zikas

Abstract
Differential Privacy (DP) (and its variants) is the most common method for machine learning (ML) on privacy-sensitive data. In big data analytics, one often uses randomized sketching/aggregation algorithms to make processing high-dimensional data tractable. Intuitively, such ML algorithms should provide some inherent privacy, yet most existing DP mechanisms do not leverage or under-utilize this inherent randomness, resulting in potentially redundant noising. The motivating question of our work is: (How) can we improve the utility of DP mechanisms for randomized ML queries, by leveraging the randomness of the query itself? Towards a (positive) answer, our key contribution is (proving) what we call the Normal Distributions Indistinguishability Spectrum Theorem (in short, NDIS Theorem), a theoretical result with several practical implications. In a nutshell, NDIS is a closed-form analytic computation for the ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -indistinguishability-spectrum (in short, ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -IS) of two arbitrary (multi-dimensional) normal distributions N 1 {N_{1}} and N 2 {N_{2}} , i.e., the optimal δ \delta (for any given ε \varepsilon ) such that N 1 {N_{1}} and N 2 {N_{2}} are ( ε , δ \varepsilon,\delta )-close according to the DP distance. The importance of the NDIS theorem lies in that (1) it yields efficient estimators for the above IS, and (2) it allows us to analyze DP-mechanisms with normally-distributed outputs, as well as more general mechanisms by leveraging their behavior on large inputs. We apply the NDIS theorem to derive DP mechanisms for queries with normally-distributed outputs—i.e., Gaussian Random Projections (RP)—and for more general queries—i.e., Ordinary Least Squares (OLS). Both RP and OLS are highly relevant in data analytics. Compared to existing techniques, our new DP mechanisms achieve superior privacy/utility trade-offs for any privacy budget ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) by leveraging the randomness of the underlying algorithms. We then apply the NDIS theorem to a data-driven DP notion—in particular relative DP introduced by Lu et al. [S&P 2024]. Here, as we show, our method identifies, for the first time in any such data-driven notion, the range of ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) for which no additional noising is needed. The superiority of our resulting mechanisms is proven analytically and confirmed by extensive empirical results.

II-A

Showcasing NDIS: The Gaussian Mechanism

To ease into our paradigm of using the NDIS theorem for analyzing and designing a DP mechanism, we re-analyze the well-studied Gaussian mechanism (see
[
4
,
1

5
]

-DP. To do so, first, we consider two arbitrary fixed neighboring outputs

\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(D^{\prime})
of the Gaussian mechanism. By applying the NDIS theorem to these outputs, we find that the IS
\delta_{\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(D),\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(D^{\prime})}(\varepsilon)
is uniquely determined by, and increases with
\frac{{\left\|f(D)-f(D^{\prime})\right\|}_{2}}{\sigma}
. Here,

represents the sensitivity of the query

between the neighboring input

and

, and

is the standard deviation of the Gaussian noise

Next, to find the desired

, we identify the neighboring input
D
and

that maximizes the sensitivity of the query

. We then set
\sigma
to be the smallest value such that the IS
\delta_{\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(D),\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(D^{\prime})}(\varepsilon)
is less than or equal to
\delta
for every fixed value of
\varepsilon

satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. This is because, for our chosen

\delta_{\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(D),\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(D^{\prime})}(\varepsilon)\leq\delta
holds for all neighboring input pair
D,D^{\prime}
, which directly implies that
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP by definition.

We note the above does not establish a new result for the Gaussian mechanism—
[
5
]
already proved the tight bound—and therefore we do not consider it a key contribution of our paper (in fact, we have moved relevant details to Appendix
C
to leave space for our novel technical contributions, discussed below). Nonetheless, we see value in discussing our analysis here, as it provides a blueprint for using the NDIS to design DP mechanisms

and analyzing their privacy: first, compute the IS for an arbitrary neighboring output pair of

II-B

(Gaussian) Random Projection

Random Projection (RP) is a method used in ML for dimensionality reduction, in order to allow learning on huge data. RP projects (i.e., sketches) a high-dimensional database

Informally, RP achieve some intuitive level of privacy by itself—first shown by Blocki

Applying NDIS to Gaussian RP.
First, we plug in the (Gaussian) output distribution of RP to NDIS.

In a nutshell, our DP mechanism (Alg.

II-C

Ordinary Least Squares

Ordinary least squares (OLS), a.k.a.

Relative DP Mechanisms

Last but not least, in
Section

VII

relative DP
is a notion introduced by Lu

We note that, despite being a relaxed notion of privacy (compared to DP), relative DP has a number of advantages, in particular when considered in connection to the NDIS theorem: First, as shown in
[
3
]
it allows for (black-box) IS estimator with concrete error guarantees; this can be used to empirically confirm the privacy of heuristic approaches (such as the last ALS-based mechanism discussed above). Most importantly, it allows for far more noise-frugal mechanisms, which depending on the input dataset and the required parameters
\varepsilon
and
\delta

Thus, it serves as a perfect way to demonstrate how our NDIS theorem answers our key question of how to leverage inherent randomness of ML algorithms for improving utility while achieving a (guaranteed) level of privacy. As discussed below, it also sheds light on the importance of leverage score as a privacy-relevant quantity.

Our DP mechanisms for RP and OLS above, can be easily turned to (more noise-frugal) relative
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanisms. The idea is the following: Recall that for any given
(\varepsilon,\delta)
pair, the NDIS theorem can compute an associated leverage

that allows us to get

-DP for all input databases with (records that have) worst case leverage
\bar{s}

-DP. Otherwise, as in the standard DP case, we will noise/regularize the input to lower the above leverage down to
\bar{s}

The above is true (and empirically validated) for all the mechanisms we discussed in the DP sections (the RP and the two OLS ones) adapted for the above relative leverage. [
3

Remark 1

(Estimating leverage for a set of databases)
.

The above brings up the question of the complexity of computing/estimating leverage for a set of databases. First, we observe that the LSV and norm of records in a database can be used to upper-bound leverage; hence upper-bounding leverage is no harder than computing these parameters used in previous works. We also note that previous works focused on LSV (which can be lower bounded) rather than the sensitivity of linear regression—it is not hard to verify that depending on the input data, the sensitivity may be unbounded, leading to unbounded noise. In contrast,

VIII
Conclusion

We presented the Normal Distributions Indistinguishability Spectrum (NDIS) Theorem, a powerful theoretical tool with evident applications in devising noise-frugal Differential Private mechanisms for randomized ML. We demonstrated the applicability of NDIS to private machine learning (ML) by deriving DP mechanisms for ML queries with normally-distributed outputs—i.e., Gaussian Random Projection (RP)—and for more general queries—i.e., Ordinary Least Squares (OLS). Our DP mechanisms enjoy superior performance compared to other RP-based DP mechanisms. Our proposed relative DP mechanisms enjoy “free” privacy guarantee in a wide range of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
thanks to the tight analysis by using NDIS. We believe the NDIS theorem has a broad application in analyzing and designing DP mechanisms with normally-distributed outputs as well as more general mechanisms satisfying the assumptions of the central limit theorem. Additionally, developing variants of the NDIS theorem for other variants of differential privacy (e.g., Rényi DP) is an interesting avenue for future research.

Appendix G
Large-Sample Distribution Theory

Definition 10

(Almost Sure Convergence
[
12
]
, Def D.3)
.

Let
x_{n}
be a sequence of random variables indexed by the sample size. The random variable
x_{n}
converges almost surely to the constant
c
if and only if

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\texttt{lim}}x_{n}=c\right]=1,

Also, it is written as

\displaystyle x_{n}\xrightarrow{a.s.}x.

Almost sure convergence states that the probability of observing a sequence that does not converge to
c
ultimately vanishes. Intuitively, it states that once the sequence
x_{n}
becomes close to
c
, it stays close to
c
.

Theorem 8

(Kolmogorov’s Law of Large Numbers
[
12
]
, Thm D.8)
.

Let
x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}
be random variables independently and identically distributed to a distribution with finite mean
\mu
and finite variance. Then,

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{n}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu,

where

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}.

Definition 11

(Convergence in Distribution
[
12
]
, Def D.9)
.

Let
x_{n}
be a sequence of random variables indexed by the sample size with cumulative density function (CDF)
F_{n}(x_{n})
, and let
x
be random variable with CDF
F(x).
If

\displaystyle\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\texttt{lim}}{\left|F_{n}(x_{n})-F(x)\right|}=0,

at all continuity points of
F(x)
, then
x_{n}
converges in distribution to
x.

Definition 12

(Limiting Distribution
[
12
]
, Def D.10)
.

Let
x_{n}
be a sequence of random variables indexed by the sample size with cumulative density function (CDF)
F_{n}(x_{n})
, and let
x
be random variable with CDF
F(x).

If
x_{n}
converges in distribution to
x,
then
F(x)
is the limiting distribution of
x_{n}.
Also, it is written as

\displaystyle x_{n}\xrightarrow{dist}x.

Theorem 9

(Multivariate Lindeberg–Levy Central Limit Theorem
[
12
]
, Thm D.18A)
.

Let
x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}
be random variables independently distributed to a multivariate distribution with finite mean vector
\mu
and finite positive definite covariance matrix

then

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\bar{x}_{n}-\mu)\xrightarrow{dist}\mathcal{N}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{0}},Q),

where

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}.
