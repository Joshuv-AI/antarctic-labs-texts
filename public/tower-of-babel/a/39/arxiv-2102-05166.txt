Discrete Bessel and Mathieu functions


Abstract
The two-dimensional Helmholtz equation separates in elliptic coordinates based on two distinct foci, a limit case of which includes polar coordinate systems when the two foci coalesce. This equation is invariant under the Euclidean group of translations and orthogonal transformations; we replace the latter by the discrete dihedral group of N N discrete rotations and reflections. The separation of variables in polar and elliptic coordinates is then used to define discrete Bessel and Mathieu functions, as approximants to the well-known continuous Bessel and Mathieu functions, as N N -point Fourier transforms approximate the Fourier transform over the circle, with integrals replaced by finite sums. We find that these ‘discrete’ functions approximate the numerical values of their continuous counterparts very closely and preserve some key special function relations.

Introduction

The role of the Euclidean group of translations, reflections and rotations
in the determination of the coordinate systems that separate the solutions
of the two-dimensional Helmholtz equation is well known from the
work by Willard Miller Jr.
[
1
, Ch. 1]
. This symmetry accounts
for their separability in four coordinate systems: Cartesian, polar, parabolic
and elliptic. Only the elliptic system is generic; when the two foci coalesce,
this system becomes the polar one with angular and radial coordinates;
when one focus departs to infinity the system becomes parabolic;
and when both foci do, it becomes Cartesian.

The polar decomposition was used by Biagetti et al.
[
2
]

to first introduce a discrete version of Bessel functions based on an
expansion of plane waves into a finite number of polar components
—that was not quite complete. This was properly completed in Ref.
[
3
]
,
defining
discrete Bessel functions

B_{n}^{{\scriptscriptstyle N}}(\rho)
, which
approximate the usual
continuous
Bessel functions

J_{n}(\rho)
by replacing Fourier series over a circle
{\cal S}^{1}
by
the finite Fourier transform on
N
equidistant points on that circle,

\theta_{m}=2\pi m/N,\qquad m\in\{0,1,\ldots,N{-}1\}=:{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}},

(1)

where
m
is counted modulo
N
.
It was found that these discrete functions approximated very closely
(of the order
10^{-16}
) the corresponding continuous ones over a
region, roughly
0\leq n+\rho<N
.

Several authors have introduced functions that approximate
the well-known continuous Bessel functions
J_{n}(\rho)
, for the purpose
of reducing computation time, or to provide new classes
of solutions to difference equations that will share some of
their salient properties
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
Our approach follows the well known approximation
afforded by the
N
-point finite Fourier transform to the
integral Fourier transform over the circle.
This is done for polar and elliptic coordinates, and introduces
both ‘discrete’ Bessel and Mathieu functions. These functions,
we should emphasize, differ from those proposed in the works cited
above, which are also distinct in definition and purpose among
themselves. By construction it will follow that under
N\to\infty
,
these discrete functions become the continuous ones, although this
limit requires further mathematical precision, as it may involve
Gibbs-type oscillation phenomena that we cannot address here.

In Sect.
2
we present this discretization method
and a resumé of the results in Ref.
[
3
]
for Bessel
functions, to note that the discrete functions thus defined
approximate the continuous ones remarkably well.
In Sect.
3
of the present paper we
apply the strategy of replacing harmonic analysis on
{\cal S}^{1}

by
{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
to define discrete approximants to
the Mathieu functions of first and second kind in the elliptic
coordinate system. All relations are backed by
numerical verification. In the concluding Sect.
4

we provide some further connections and preliminary conclusions.

2
Continuous and discrete Bessel functions

The Helmholtz equation for wavefields
f(x,y)
of (fixed) real
wavenumber
\kappa\in{\cal R}
, is

(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}+\kappa^{2})f(x,y)=0,

(2)

with
\partial_{z}\equiv\partial/\partial_{z}
and
(x,y)\in{\cal R}^{2}
.
In this section we follow the well known case of polar coordinates,

x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad r\in[0,\infty),\quad\theta\in(-\pi,\pi]={\cal S}^{1}.

(3)

A key assumption is a Hilbert space structure for
the solutions
f(x,y)
by which one can write them as
the two-dimensional Fourier transform,

f(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\!\!\!\int_{{\cal R}^{2}}{\rm d}\kappa_{x}\,{\rm d}\kappa_{y}\,\exp{\rm i}(x\kappa_{x}+y\kappa_{y})\,\widetilde{f}(\kappa_{x},\kappa_{y}).

(4)

The Helmholtz equation (
2
) is then correspondingly transformed to
a conjugate space
(\kappa_{x},\kappa_{y})\in{\cal R}^{2}
where it reads

(\kappa^{2}-\kappa_{x}^{2}-\kappa_{y}^{2})\widetilde{f}(\kappa_{x},\kappa_{y})=0
,
which we can also refer to polar coordinates
\kappa_{x}=\kappa\cos\phi
,

\kappa_{y}=\kappa\sin\phi
, with the surface element

{\rm d}\kappa_{x}\,{\rm d}\kappa_{y}=\kappa\,{\rm d}\kappa\,{\rm d}\phi
.
The solutions to the Fourier-transformed Helmholtz equation are thus
reduced by a Dirac
\delta
-distributions in the radius

[
1
, Ch. 1]
, as

\widetilde{f}(\kappa_{x},\kappa_{y})=\sqrt{2\pi}\kappa^{-1}\delta(\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa})\,{f}_{\!\circ}(\phi)
,
with
{f}_{\!\circ}(\phi)
a function on the
\phi
-circle
{\cal S}^{1}
of
radius
\widetilde{\kappa}
, that we write again
\kappa
, understanding that it
is the fixed wavenumber.
The Helmholtz solutions (
4
) thus acquire the single-integral form

f(x,y)=\frac{1}{\surd 2\pi}\int_{{\cal S}^{1}}{\rm d}\phi\,\exp{\rm i}\kappa(x\cos\phi+y\sin\phi)\,{f}_{\!\circ}(\phi),

(5)

with the Hilbert space structure based on the inner product
of functions
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{\!\circ}(\phi)
and
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{\!\circ}(\phi)
on the circle,

(f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{\!\circ},f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{\!\circ})_{\circ}:=\int_{{\cal S}^{1}}{\rm d}\phi\,f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{\!\circ}(\phi)^{*}f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{\!\circ}(\phi).

(6)

It is here that we reduce the continuous circle Fourier transform to the
N
-point
discrete Fourier transform, from
{{\cal S}^{1}}
to
{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
,
replacing integrals by summations and the continuous variable
\phi\in{\cal S}^{1}

with
\phi_{m}\in{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
, as

\int_{{\cal S}^{1}}{\rm d}\phi\,{F}_{\!\circ}(\phi)\leftrightarrow\sum_{m\in{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}}{F}(\phi_{m}),\qquad{2\pi\leftrightarrow N,\atop\phi_{m}=2\pi m/N,}

(7)

for
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,N{-}1\}
counted modulo
N
; the set of
N
discrete
angles
\phi_{m}
are thus equidistant by
2\pi/N
.
The functions
f(\phi_{m})\equiv f_{m}
can be interpreted as sample points of a
continuous function, or as the index for the list of components
of an
N
-cyclic vector. In either case, the inner product of two
discrete functions
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{n}
and
f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{n}
is naturally

(f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}},f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}})_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}:=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f_{n}^{{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}*}\,f^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{n},

(8)

and it is clear that the
N\to\infty
limit will lead back from the
discrete to the continuum, with the approximations and limits familiar
from Fourier theory.

The Helmholtz equation (
2
) in polar coordinates,
multiplied by
r^{2}
,

(r^{2}\partial^{2}_{r}+r\partial_{r}+\partial^{2}_{\phi}+\kappa^{2})f(r,\phi)=0,

(9)

shows that solutions can be factored into a
function of the radius times a function of the angle
as
f(r,\phi)=R(r)\,\Phi(\phi)
, while (
5
)
implies that solutions
\Phi(\phi)
for the angular
factor will determine a corresponding radial factor

R(r)
. An orthonormal and complete set of
eigenfunctions of
\partial^{2}_{\phi}
over the circle

\phi\in{\cal S}^{1}
is the set of phases
\Phi_{n}(\phi):=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\exp({\rm i}n\phi)
, with integer

n\in\{0,\pm 1,\ldots\}
, and inner products

(\Phi_{n},\Phi_{n^{\prime}})_{\circ}=\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}
.
When the domain of these functions is restricted
from
\phi\in{\cal S}^{1}
to

\phi_{m}\in{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
as in (
1
),
we retain the subset of
N
functions
on the
N
points in
{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
,
given by

\Phi^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\phi_{m}):=\frac{1}{\surd N}\exp({\rm i}n\phi_{m})=\frac{1}{\surd N}\exp\bigg(\frac{2\pi{\rm i}mn}{N}\bigg)=\Phi^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n\pm N}(\phi_{m}),

(10)

labeled by the
cyclic
subset
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N{-}1\}
,
that are also orthonormal under the common inner product
(
8
) for discrete functions on
{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
,
and complete:

(\Phi^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n},\Phi^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n^{\prime}})_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}=\delta_{n,n^{\prime}},\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\!\Phi^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\phi_{m})^{*}\,\Phi^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\phi_{m^{\prime}})=\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}.

(11)

Returning to (
5
) with
(x,y)
in the polar coordinates
(r,\theta)

of (
3
), and taking for
{f}_{\circ}(\phi_{m})
the basis functions
(
10
) on the discrete points of
{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
, we write the

N
solutions to the discretized Helmholtz equation, labeled
by cyclical
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N{-}1\}
, as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{}\!\!\!\!\!f_{n}(r,\theta_{k})&=&\!\!\!\displaystyle\frac{1}{\surd N}\sum_{m\in{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}}\!\!\!\exp[{\rm i}\kappa r(\cos\theta_{k}\cos\phi_{m}+\sin\theta_{k}\sin\phi_{m})]\,\Phi^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\phi_{m})\\
&=&\!\!\!\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in{\cal S}^{1}_{(N)}}\!\!\!\exp[{\rm i}\kappa r\cos(\theta_{k}-\phi_{m})]\exp({\rm i}n\phi_{m})\\
&=&\!\!\!\displaystyle\frac{e^{{\rm i}n(\theta_{k}+\pi/2)}}{N}\sum_{m\in{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}}\!\!\!\exp({\rm i}\kappa r\sin\varphi_{m})\exp(-{\rm i}n\varphi_{m}),\end{array}

(12)

having replaced
\varphi_{m}:=\theta_{k}-\phi_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\pi
in the summation
over the
N
discrete points on the circle.

Following Miller
[
1
, p. 29]
, the phase in front of
(
12
),
e^{{\rm i}n\theta_{n}(\theta_{k}+\pi/2)}={\rm i}^{n}e^{2\pi{\rm i}nk/N}={\rm i}^{n}\sqrt{N}\Phi^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\theta_{k})
, is extracted
to write the functions as

f_{n}(r,\theta_{k})={\rm i}^{n}\sqrt{N}\,B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\kappa r)\,\Phi^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\theta_{k}),

(13)

where the radial factor
B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\rho)
,
\rho:=\kappa r
, are the

discrete Bessel functions
. From (
12
) these functions are
seen to be
real
and their parities, using coefficients

\{c_{n},s_{n}\}:=\{1,0\}
for
n
even or
\{0,1\}
for
n
odd,
can be written as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\rho)&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in{\cal S}^{1}_{N}}\!\!\!\exp({\rm i}\rho\sin\varphi_{m})\,[c_{n}\cos(n\varphi_{m})-{\rm i}s_{n}\sin(n\varphi_{m})]\\
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in{\cal S}^{1}_{N}}\!\!\!\exp({\rm i}\rho\sin\varphi_{m})\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\cos n\varphi_{m},&n\hbox{ even},\\
-{\rm i}\sin n\varphi_{m},&n\hbox{ odd}.\end{array}\right.\end{array}

(14)

The distinction between even and odd cases of
n
,
as done in
[
3
]
, is subtle but important to obtain
the correct result for all
n
’s (
cf
.
[
2
, Eq. (9)]
).
It results in the parity and cyclicity properties

B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\rho)=B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n\pm N}(\rho)=(-1)^{n}B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{-n}(\rho)=(-1)^{n}B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(-\rho),\quad B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(0)=\delta_{n,0},

(15)

which also hold for the continuous Bessel functions

J_{n}(\rho)
of integer order
[
8
]
.

A plane wave of wavenumber
\kappa
along the
y
-axis
in a Helmholtz medium that allows
only
N
equidistant directions of propagation on the circle,
can be obtained from (
14
) using the completeness
relation (
11
) to expand the middle term
and write

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\exp({\rm i}\rho\sin\varphi_{m})=B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{0}(\rho)&+&\!\!\!\displaystyle 2\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{2n}(\rho)\cos(2n\varphi_{m})\\
&+&\!\!\!\displaystyle 2{\rm i}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}B^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{2n+1}(\rho)\sin((2n{+}1)\varphi_{m}),\end{array}

(16)

showing how the discrete Bessel functions can take the
place of the continuous ones,
cf.

[
7
, Eq. KU120(13)]
.

In Ref.
[
3
]
we proved analytically, and verified
numerically, that the following expressions for the discrete
Bessel functions are exact analogues of those valid for
continuous Bessel functions. Corresponding to

[
7
, WA44]
for odd
N=:2j+1
, in Ref.
[
3
]
we
proved the linear relations involving the even and odd-

discrete Bessel functions,

\displaystyle B_{0}(\rho)+\sum_{n=1}^{j}B_{2n}(\rho)\cos(2n\varphi_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\rho\sin\varphi_{m}),

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{j}B_{2n+1}(\rho)\sin((2n{+}1)\varphi_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sin(\rho\sin\varphi_{m}).

(18)

The quadratic formulas
[
9
, §7.6.2, Eq. (6)]

associated to the name of Graf, were
shown in Ref.
[
10
]
to derive from the rotation of
spherical harmonics through Wigner-

These relations, of group-theoretical origin, retain their

\sum_{n=-2j}^{2j}B_{n}(\rho)\,B_{n^{\prime}-n}(\rho^{\prime})=B_{n^{\prime}}(\rho+\rho^{\prime}),

(19)

keeping in mind the parity property (
15
)
for the negative

-indices in the sum

, numbered by

\rho

\rho_{k}=k\in\{0,1,\ldots,N{-}1\}
.

3
Discrete Mathieu functions

Elliptic coordinates on the plane generalize the previous
polar coordinates (
3
); they are defined in terms of
Cartesian coordinates through

x=\cosh\varrho\,\cos\psi,\quad y=\sinh\varrho\,\sin\psi,\qquad\varrho\in[0,\infty),\quad\psi\in(-\pi,\pi]={\cal S}^{1}.

(20)

where
(\varrho,\psi)
are analogues of the previous polar
coordinates
(r,\phi)
for which we retain the names as ‘radial’
and ‘angular’ variables. For fixed
\varrho
or for fixed
\psi
,
the locus of points
(x,y)\in{\cal R}^{2}
that satisfy

{x^{2}}/{\cosh^{2}\!\varrho}+{y^{2}}/{\sinh^{2}\!\varrho}=1,\qquad{x^{2}}/{\cos^{2}\!\psi}-{y^{2}}/{\sin^{2}\!\psi}=1,

(21)

draw families of confocal ellipses or hyperbolas respectively.
At
\varrho=0
,
\psi\in{\cal S}^{1}
draws twice the line between
the two foci
(x,y)=(\pm 1,0)
for
\psi=(0,\pi)
.
The major and minor semi-axes of the ellipses are

\cosh\varrho
and
\sinh\varrho
respectively, so their
eccentricities are
1/\cosh\varrho
, that tend to
circles when
\varrho\to\infty
. On the other hand, for fixed

\psi\in{\cal S}^{1}
in each of the four quadrants, since

\varrho\geq 0
, only one of the four arms of the hyperbola is
traversed. Thus we expect
four
parity cases out
of the two reflections, across the
x
and
y
axes.
Compare this with the case of polar coordinates where

r\geq 0
but all reflection axes are equivalent, so
(-1)^{n}

in (
14
) provides the two Bessel parity cases.

Set of equally-spaced discrete points on ellipses
(

) of the ‘angular’ coordinates

\{\psi_{m}\}\in{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
for
N=21
,

.

The Helmholtz differential equation (
2
), written in the
elliptic coordinates (
20
), is clearly separable,

[(\partial^{2}_{\varrho}+\kappa^{2}\cosh^{2}\!\varrho)+(\partial^{2}_{\psi}-\kappa^{2}\cos^{2}\!\psi)]\,f(\varrho,\psi)=0,

(22)

so that solutions can be written in the product form

f(\varrho,\psi)\sim P(\varrho)\,\Psi(\psi)
.
Dividing by
f
, one obtains two coupled equations
in
\varrho
and
\psi
, the latter is an eigenvalue
equation in the angular coordinate,

(\partial^{2}_{\psi}-2q\cos 2\psi)\,\Psi(\psi,q)=\nu\,\Psi(\psi,q),\qquad q:={\textstyle\frac{1}{4}}\kappa^{2},

(23)

known as the Mathieu differential equation. The angular
coordinate
\psi
is periodic and a well-known solution
method consists in expanding solutions of (
23
)
in the Fourier basis
\sim\exp({\rm i}n\psi)
over all integer
n
.
This defines the Mathieu functions of the first kind

{\rm ce}_{n}(\psi,q)
and
{\rm se}_{n}(\psi,q)
with
integer
n

[
11
]
, characterized by a parity
index
p\in\{0,1\}
for even and odd cases
[
7
, Eqs. 8.61]
,
and distinct for even and odd indices. In a two-line expression
all cases can be written as

\bigg[{{\rm ce}_{2n+p}(\psi,q)\atop{\rm se}_{2n+p}(\psi,q)}\bigg]=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\bigg[{A^{2n+p}_{2s+p}\cos((2s{+}p)\psi)\atop B^{2n+p}_{2s+p}\sin((2s{+}p)\psi)}\bigg].

(24)

The parities are even
{\rm ce}_{n}(-\psi,q)={\rm ce}_{n}(\psi,q)
,
odd
{\rm se}_{n}(-\psi,q)=-{\rm se}_{n}(\psi,q)
, and
{\rm se}_{0}(\psi,q)\equiv 0
.
The coefficients
A^{n}_{s},\,B^{n}_{s}
are found introducing this expansion into
(
23
) to find recursion relations
[
7
, Eqs. 8.62]
that lead
to efficient numerical computation. For use below, we write
them using Fourier series as

\bigg[{A^{n}_{s}\atop B^{n}_{s}}\bigg]=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\cal S}^{1}}{\rm d}\psi\,\bigg[{\cos(s\psi)\,{\rm ce}_{n}(\psi,q)\atop\sin(s\psi)\,{\rm se}_{n}(\psi,q)}\bigg],

(25)

for
n\neq 0
, while
A^{n}_{0}=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal S}^{1}}}{\rm d}\psi\,{\rm ce}_{n}(\psi,q)
, and

B^{n}_{0}\equiv 0
.
The Mathieu functions (
24
) are orthogonal under
the inner product (
6
) over the circle,

({\rm ce}_{m},{\rm ce}_{n})_{\circ}=\pi\delta_{m,n}
,

({\rm se}_{m},{\rm se}_{n})_{\circ}=\pi\delta_{m,n}

for
n\neq 0
—zero otherwise, and

({\rm ce}_{m},{\rm se}_{n})_{\circ}=0
.

We now restrict the range of the angular coordinate

\psi
from
{\cal S}^{1}
to
{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
, shown
for the elliptic coordinates in Fig.
2
,
in correspondence with the previous discrete phase functions
in the Bessel case (
10
), and thus defining the ‘angular’

discrete Mathieu functions
of the
first type over
\psi_{m}\in{\cal S}^{1}_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}
as

\bigg[{{\rm ce}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{2n+p}(\psi_{m},q)\atop{\rm se}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{2n+p}(\psi_{m},q)}\bigg]:=\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\bigg[{a^{2n+p}_{2s+p}\cos((2s{+}p)\psi_{m})\atop b^{2n+p}_{2s+p}\sin((2s{+}p)\psi_{m})}\bigg],

(26)

with coefficients
a^{n}_{s},\,b^{n}_{s}
. The finite
N
-point
Fourier transform approximates them through the
replacement (
7
) to
the functions and coefficients
A^{n}_{s},\,B^{n}_{s}

of the continuous case in (
25
), as

\bigg[{a^{n}_{s}\atop b^{n}_{s}}\bigg]:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\bigg[{\cos(s\psi_{m})\,{\rm ce}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\psi_{m},q)\atop\sin(s\psi_{m})\,{\rm se}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n}(\psi_{m},q)}\bigg]\simeq\frac{1}{2}\bigg[{A^{n}_{s}\atop B^{n}_{s}}\bigg],

(27)

for
s\neq 0
, while
a_{0}^{n}=A_{0}^{n}
,
b^{n}_{0}=0
, and also
b^{0}_{s}=0
.

The last relation in (

) is an approximate equality, the
validity of which is contingent upon the numerical computation

In favor of the thus defined discrete Mathieu functions, we note

({\rm ce}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n},{\rm ce}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n^{\prime}})_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}N\delta_{n,{n^{\prime}}},\!\quad({\rm se}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n},{\rm se}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{{n^{\prime}}\neq 0})_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}N\delta_{n,{n^{\prime}}},\!\quad({\rm ce}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n},{\rm se}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}_{n^{\prime}})_{{\scriptscriptstyle(N)}}=0.

(28)

4
Concluding remarks

The expansion of Helmholtz plane waves in
series of radial Bessel and angular trigonometric
functions has its discrete analogue in Eq. (
16
), which tells us that the wavefield
due to a finite number
N
of plane waves at
equidistant direction angles can be expanded
in discrete Bessel radial functions and
corresponding trigonometric angular functions.
A similar statement will hold when the wavefield
is expanded in discrete Mathieu functions with
the phases determined by the points on an ellipse
as depicted in Fig.
2
.
Conceivably such fields can be produced in
resonant two-dimensional micro-cavities fed by
a number of activation channels.

We recognize that the full treatment and
exploration of properties for the discrete
Bessel and Mathieu function presented here
is not exhaustive, but that it should be sufficient
to indicate that the approximation method
consisting in the replacement of a continuous
closed subgroup of the symmetry group of a
partial differential equation by a finite
discrete group is definitely of interest.
In the present case of two
dimensions, the orthogonal group was reduced
to the dihedral group. In three dimensions,
the symmetry Euclidean symmetry group could
reduce its three-dimensional rotation subgroup
by any of its polyhedral subgroups, whose
functions may serve to describe wavefields
with a corresponding subset of
wave propagation directions. Here we have presented
a set of exact relations, others whose
approximation closeness was estimated through
numerical computation, and others that have
been only suggested by that approach, and for
which we expect to present further results from
ongoing work.
