Map-making Strategies For Next Generation CMB Polarization Experiments
Simon Biquard

Abstract
I briefly review some of the most common map-making strategies for experiments targeting the polarization of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB), in light of the anticipated volumes of data collected by next generation observatories such as Simons Observatory and CMB-S4. Then, I focus on the pair-differencing approach, used for example in the POLARBEAR and BICEP collaborations. Using simulations including a number of systematic effects, I evaluate the impact of correlated and unpolarized signals such as coming from the atmosphere, which is a major contaminant for ground-based experiments.

Review of map-making flavors

Map-making
is the reconstruction of the observed sky from the time-ordered data (TOD) collected by a telescope.
It compresses the volume of data by many orders of magnitude, while trying to preserve cosmological information.

The usual data model assumed for the map-making problem is:

\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{n}

(1)

where
\mathbf{d}
is the (calibrated) TOD,
\mathbf{P}
is the pointing matrix (which encodes the scanning and orientation of the telescope),
\mathbf{s}
is the true sky map, and
\mathbf{n}
is the noise vector.

Map-making is just a
linear operation
,
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{d}
, mapping the TOD to an estimator
\mathbf{m}
of the true sky map.
In general, we can write
\mathbf{L}=\pab{\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W_{1}}\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W_{2}}
, and the specific choices of
\mathbf{W_{1}}
and
\mathbf{W_{2}}

2
The pair differencing approach

We assume that the atmospheric signal and the ground pickup are
unpolarized
.
Then, for dual-polarization detectors
A
and
B
, we can introduce a difference time stream:

\mathbf{d}_{A/B}=I\pm P\quad\longrightarrow\quad\mathbf{d}_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\pab{\mathbf{d}_{A}-\mathbf{d}_{B}}=P

(2)

which is in principle free of atmospheric and total intensity sky signal.
We use it to reconstruct the Stokes
Q
and
U
maps.

This approach removes unwanted, unpolarized signals, e.g., atmosphere, ground pickup, etc.; is numerically efficient, avoiding long-time noise correlations due to the atmosphere, and can produce maps with nearly optimal noise properties.
These advantages can only be exploited if the unwanted signals in both detectors are indeed
identical
. This may not be the case due to, e.g., (i) miscalibration of the detector gains (ii) bandpass differences (iii) different beam shapes.
We here evaluate the impact of such effects.

3
Preliminary results and outlook

Our setup uses the Time-Ordered Astrophysics Scalable Tools (TOAST) package for simulating data and the MAPPRAISER library
[
4
]
for producing the sky maps.
We simulate Simons Observatory Small Aperture Telescope-like data with rotating half wave plate (HWP) and include the atmospheric noise.
In Fig.
1
, we show a sample of our results displaying the
residual spectra
of the difference between the reconstructed and input sky maps for a number of cases and compare them with targeted signals.

We conclude
tentatively
that in the studied cases, the HWP helps mitigating the impact of the included systematics leading to an overall satisfactory performance of the method.
The on-going work focuses on (1) refining simulations by adding bandpass and beam differences; (2) inclusion of realistic gain calibration error distributions, and, (3) performing a likelihood analysis to estimate biases and their impact on the the tensor-to-scalar ratio.
