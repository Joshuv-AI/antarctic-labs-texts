Introduction and Main Results

The present paper is part of an ongoing project whose main objective is the investigation of the phase diagram and phases of the unitary ensemble of random matrices
with a general complex potential

(1)

V(z;\bm{t})=\frac{z^{2p}}{2p}+\sum_{j=1}^{2p-1}\frac{t_{j}z^{j}}{j},\qquad t_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}},\quad p\in{\mathbb{N}},

in the complex space of the vector of the parameters

\bm{t}=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{2p-1})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2p-1}.

The unitary ensemble under consideration is defined as the complex measure on the space of
n\times n
Hermitian random matrices,

(2)

\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}}e^{-n\mathrm{Tr}\,V(M;\bm{t})}\textrm{d}M,

where

(3)

\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}(\bm{t})=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}e^{-n\mathrm{Tr}\,V(M;\bm{t})}\textrm{d}M

is the
partition function
. As well known (see, e.g.,
[
6
]
), the ensemble of eigenvalues of
M
,

Me_{k}=z_{k}e_{k},\qquad\;k=1,\ldots,n,

is given by the probability distribution

(4)

\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\bm{t})}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod^{n}_{j=1}\exp\left[-nV(z_{j};\bm{t})\right]\textrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\textrm{d}z_{n},

where

(5)

\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\bm{t})=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\cdots\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod^{n}_{j=1}\exp\left[-nV(z_{j};\bm{t})\right]\textrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\textrm{d}z_{n},

is the
eigenvalue partition function
. The partition functions

\mathcal{Z}_{n}
and

\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}
are related by the formula,

(6)

\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\bm{t})}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}(\bm{t})}=\frac{1}{\pi^{n(n-1)/2}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}k!.

Formulae (
5
), (
6
) are well known for real polynomial potentials
V(z)
of even degree
(see, e.g.,
[
6
]
), and their proof for a complex
V(z)
goes through without any change.

By Heine’s formula (see e.g.
[
23
]
) the multiple integral in (
5
) is, up to a multiplicative constant, the determinant of the Hankel matrix
H_{n}[w]:=\{w_{j+k}\}_{k,j=0,\ldots,n}
, where
w(x;\bm{t})\equiv\exp[-nV(x;\bm{t})]
and
w_{\ell}
is the
\ell
-th moment of the weight
w(x;\bm{t})
. Correspondingly, one can also consider the system of monic orthogonal polynomials
\{P_{n}(z;\bm{t})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}
satisfying

(7)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}P_{n}(z;\bm{t})z^{k}w(z;\bm{t})dz=0\ ,\quad\mbox{for }\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1.

The connection of this system of orthogonal polynomials and the partition function (
5
) can be seen as follows: the orthogonal polynomial of degree
n
exists and is unique if the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\bm{t})
, or the
n\times n
Hankel determinant
\det H_{n}[w]
, is nonzero. Indeed, the existence follows from the explicit formula

(8)

P_{n}(z;\bm{t})\equiv P_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{\det H_{n}[w]}\det\begin{pmatrix}w_{0}&w_{1}&\cdots&w_{n-1}&w_{n}\\
w_{1}&w_{2}&\cdots&w_{n}&w_{n+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\reflectbox{$\ddots$}&\vdots&\vdots\\
w_{n-1}&w_{n}&\cdots&w_{2n-2}&w_{2n-1}\\
1&z&\cdots&z^{n-1}&z^{n}\end{pmatrix},

and the uniqueness follows from the fact that the linear system to find the coefficients of
P_{n}(z;\bm{t})\equiv z^{n}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_{j}(\bm{t})z^{j}
, is of the form
H_{n}[w]\bm{a}=\bm{b}
, and thus can be inverted if the Hankel determinant is nonzero.

It is well known that the normalized counting measure for the zeros of these orthogonal polynomials weakly converges to the associated
equilibrium measure

\nu_{\rm eq}
(See e.g.
[
10

and references therein). For a review of the definitions and properties of the equilibrium measure in the cases where the external field is real and complex see §

The properties of the equilibrium measure when the external field is real have been studied extensively over the last two decades or so (see e.g.

the contour of integration for orthogonal polynomials in the case where the external field is real, as the solution of the associated extremal problem for the equilibrium measure automatically ensures that the real line is the correct contour of integration.

In this work we are considering polynomials defined by a "complex orthogonality condition", of the form (
7

w(z;\bm{t})\equiv e^{-nV(z;\bm{t})}

[
2
,
3

14
]
), and each equivalence class yields a different sequence of orthogonal polynomials.

Even though for each choice of the equivalence class of contours, our method would work, for the sake of simplicity of exposition, we will restrict ourselves as follows:
We will assume that the external field is a polynomial of degree 2p (see (
1
)), and we will choose the class of contours of integration that are all in the same equivalence class as the real axis.

As opposed to the case of a real measure on the real axis defining more classical polynomials all of whose zeros are real, the case of complex orthogonality produces polynomials whose zeros exhibit more complicated behavior. In fact, as the degree of the polynomials tends to infinity, the zeros accumulate on nontrivial curves in the complex plane.

In order to carry out an asymptotic analysis of the orthogonal polynomials with complex weights, a new problem arises which is the effective selection of a contour of integration for which subsequent analysis is possible. It turns out that the effective selection of the contour of integration determines within it the accumulation set of the zeros of the orthogonal polynomials, which is the support of the equilibrium measure (suitably generalized to the complex case).

The problem of determining this important set in the plane, which is later used as a portion of the contour of integration, is actually connected to a classical energy problem dating back at least to Gauss - the energy of a continuum of particles in the presence of an external field that experiences a repelling force whose potential is logarithmic. The set is determined by considering, for each member
\Gamma
of the class of admissible contours
\mathcal{T}
, the energy minimization problem on
\Gamma
, and then selecting a contour
\Gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{T}
that maximizes this minimum energy. In other words,
\Gamma_{0}

(9)

\underset{\Gamma\in\mathcal{T}}{\mathrm{max}}\left\{\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\textrm{supp}\,(\nu)\subset\Gamma\\

The admissible sectors (in which the admissible equivalence classes of contours could approach

\nu

Equilibrium Measure and Quadratic Differentials

2.1.
Equilibrium measure for orthogonal polynomials associated with real external fields.

Let

V(x;\bm{t})=\frac{x^{2p}}{2p}+\sum_{j=1}^{2p-1}\frac{t_{j}z^{j}}{j},

\mathscr{M}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}):=\left\{\nu:\nu\geq 0,\ \int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\textrm{d}\nu=1\right\}.

6

(4)

\rho_{V}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\,h(x)R_{+}^{1/2}(x),\quad R(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{q}(x-a_{k})(x-b_{k}),

2.5.
Quadratic Differentials

In this subsection we briefly remind some definitions and basic facts about quadratic differentials from
[

]
. The zeros and poles of

-arc is called a
\theta
-trajectory.
The above equation implies that a
\theta
-arc can only contain regular points of

, because at the critical points

is not defined. For a meromorphic quadratic differential, there is only one

-arc passing through each regular point.

We will refer to a

-trajectory ( resp.

-trajectory) which is incident with a critical point as a

(resp.

). If

of

of the quadratic differential

(see §5 of
[
22
]
). A Jordan curve

composed of open

(also referred to as a

-polygon.
\Sigma
is called a

, if at least one of its end points is a singular point.

Now we can state the

: for a meromorphic quadratic differential

\texttt{ord}(z)=n

\Gamma(b_{q},\infty)

since by definition

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{a_{1}}\left\{\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{+}-\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{-}\right\}\overline{\textrm{d}z}=0\ .

So we must consider

\displaystyle\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{q}}\left\{\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{+}-\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{-}\right\}\overline{\textrm{d}z}\ .

There are two types of integrals: those across bands and those across gaps.

For

\Gamma(a_{j},b_{j})
, the quantity

has a jump discontinuity across the contour:

. So we have

\displaystyle\int_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}\left\{\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{+}-\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{-}\right\}\overline{\textrm{d}z}=\int_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}\overline{B_{+}(z)}\left(u_{+}(z)+u_{-}(z)\right)\overline{\textrm{d}z}\ ,

\displaystyle u_{+}(z)+u_{-}(z)=-2\sum_{k=j}^{q-1}\int_{b_{k}}^{a_{k+1}}B(s)\textrm{d}s-2\int_{b_{q}}^{+\infty}B(s)\textrm{d}s,\qquad\mbox{ for all }z\in\Gamma(a_{j},b_{j})\ ,

\displaystyle\int_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}\overline{B_{+}(z)}\ \overline{\textrm{d}z}=\overline{\int_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}B_{+}(z)\textrm{d}z}=-\int_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}B_{+}(z)\textrm{d}z\ ,

where the last equality follows from (

\displaystyle\int_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}\left\{\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{+}-\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{-}\right\}\overline{\textrm{d}z}=\left(2\sum_{k=j}^{q-1}\int_{b_{k}}^{a_{k+1}}B(s)\textrm{d}s+2\int_{b_{q}}^{+\infty}B(s)\textrm{d}s\right)\int_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}B_{+}(z)\textrm{d}z\ .

For

and

\displaystyle u_{+}(z)-u_{-}(z)=-2\sum_{k=j+1}^{q}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}B_{+}(s)\textrm{d}s,\ \qquad\mbox{ for all }z\in\Gamma(b_{j},a_{j+1})\ .

And so we find

\displaystyle\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}\left\{\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{+}-\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{-}\right\}\overline{\textrm{d}z}=\left(-2\sum_{k=j+1}^{q}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}B_{+}(s)\textrm{d}s\right)\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}\overline{B(z)}\ \overline{\textrm{d}z}\ .

Note that from (

\displaystyle\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}\overline{B(z)}\ \overline{\textrm{d}z}=-\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}B(z)\textrm{d}z\ .

Therefore

(143)

\displaystyle\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}\left\{\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{+}-\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{-}\right\}\overline{\textrm{d}z}=\left(2\sum_{k=j+1}^{q}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}B_{+}(s)\textrm{d}s\right)\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}B(z)\textrm{d}z\ .

Using (
138

(144)

\begin{split}&\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{q}}\left\{\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{+}-\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{-}\right\}\overline{\textrm{d}z}=\\
&\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left(2\sum_{k=j}^{q}\int_{b_{k}}^{a_{k+1}}B(s)\textrm{d}s\right)\int_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}B_{+}(z)\textrm{d}z+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\left(2\sum_{k=j+1}^{q}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}B_{+}(s)\textrm{d}s\right)\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}B(z)\textrm{d}z\ .\end{split}

Note that in (

(145)

\begin{split}&\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{q}}\left\{\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{+}-\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{-}\right\}\overline{\textrm{d}z}=\\
&2\sum_{k=1}^{q}\int_{b_{k}}^{a_{k+1}}B(s)\textrm{d}s\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}B_{+}(z)\textrm{d}z+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\left(2\sum_{k=j+1}^{q}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}B_{+}(s)\textrm{d}s\right)\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}B(z)\textrm{d}z\ .\end{split}

Exchanging indices of summation in the first term on the r.h.s. of (

(146)

\begin{split}&\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{q}}\left\{\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{+}-\left[u(z)\overline{B(z)}\right]_{-}\right\}\overline{\textrm{d}z}=\\
&2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}B(s)\textrm{d}s\sum_{k=1}^{j}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}B(z)\textrm{d}z+\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\left(2\sum_{k=j+1}^{q}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}B(s)\textrm{d}s\right)\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}B(z)\textrm{d}z=\\
&2\sum_{j=1}^{q-1}\left[\int_{b_{j}}^{a_{j+1}}B(s)\textrm{d}s\left(\sum_{k=1}^{q}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}B(z)\textrm{d}z\right)\right]+\int_{b_{q}}^{+\infty}B(s)\textrm{d}s\left(\sum_{k=1}^{q}\int_{a_{k}}^{b_{k}}B(z)\textrm{d}z\right)=0\ .\end{split}

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
3

The end-points
a_{j}(\bm{t}),b_{j}(\bm{t})\in\mathscr{J}^{(q)}_{\bm{t}}
deform continuously with respect to
\bm{t}
as shown in Theorem
2
, due to nonsingularity of the Jacobian matrix at a regular
q
-cut point. Notice that the critical graph of the quadratic differential
Q(z;\bm{t})\textrm{d}z^{2}
is intrinsic to the polynomial
Q
and does not depend on the particular branch chosen for its natural parameter
\eta_{q}(z;\bm{t})
, for example the one chosen in (
56
). For the purposes of this proof, for each fixed
\bm{t}
, unlike our choice in (
56
), we choose the branch
\tilde{\eta}_{q}(z;\bm{t})
whose branch cut has no intersections with the critical graph
\mathscr{J}^{(q)}_{\bm{t}}
and we can characterize the critical graph of
Q(z;\bm{t})\textrm{d}z^{2}
as the totality of solutions to

(158)

\Re\tilde{\eta}_{q}(z;\bm{t})=0.

Recalling (
56
) with the choice of branch discussed above, notice that

(159)

\frac{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{q}}{\partial z}(z^{*};\bm{t}^{*})=-\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\left(z^{*}-z_{\ell}(\bm{t}^{*})\right)\left[\prod_{j=1}^{q}\left(z^{*}-a_{j}(\bm{t}^{*})\right)\left(z^{*}-b_{j}(\bm{t}^{*})\right)\right]^{1/2}\neq 0,

where
z^{*}\in\mathscr{J}^{(q)}_{\bm{t}^{*}}\setminus\left\{a_{j}(\bm{t}^{*}),b_{j}(\bm{t}^{*})\right\}^{q}_{j=1}
does not lie on the branch cut chosen to define
\tilde{\eta}_{q}
. Since
z^{*}
is not on the branch cut, there is a small neighborhood of
z^{*}
in which
\tilde{\eta}_{q}(z,\bm{t}^{*})
is analytic. By Cauchy-Riemann equations, from (
159
) we conclude that at least one of the quantities
\frac{\partial\Re\tilde{\eta}_{q}}{\partial x}(z^{*};\bm{t}^{*})
or
\frac{\partial\Re\tilde{\eta}_{q}}{\partial y}(z^{*};\bm{t}^{*})
is non zero,
z=x+\textrm{i}y
. Without loss of generality, let us assume that

(160)

\frac{\partial\Re\tilde{\eta}_{q}}{\partial x}(z^{*};\bm{t}^{*})\neq 0.

Now, think of the left hand side of (
158
) as a map

(161)

\Re\tilde{\eta}_{q}\left(x,\bm{w}\right):{\mathbb{R}}\times W\to{\mathbb{R}},

where
z=x+\textrm{i}y
, and an element
\bm{w}\in W\simeq{\mathbb{R}}^{4p-1}
represents the variable
y
and the real and imaginary parts of the parameters in the external field:

\bm{w}=\left(y,\Re t_{1},\Im,t_{1},\cdots,\Re t_{2p-1},\Im t_{2p-1}\right)^{T}.

Now, by the real-analytic Implicit Function Theorem
[
12
]
, we know that there exists a neighborhood
U
of

\bm{w}^{*}\equiv\left(y^{*},\Re t^{*}_{1},\Im,t^{*}_{1},\cdots,\Re t^{*}_{2p-1},\Im t^{*}_{2p-1}\right)

and a real-analytic map
\Psi:U\to{\mathbb{R}}
, with

\Psi\left(\bm{w}^{*}\right)=x^{*}

and

\Re\tilde{\eta}_{q}(\Psi(\bm{w}),\bm{w})=0.

That is to say that for any
y
in a small enough neighborhood of
y^{*}
and for any
\bm{t}\equiv\left(\Re t_{1},\Im,t_{1},\cdots,\Re t_{2p-1},\Im t_{2p-1}\right)^{T}
in a small enough neighborhood of
\bm{t}^{*}
, there is an
x\equiv x(y,\bm{t})
such that
z=x+iy
lies on the critical graph
\mathscr{J}^{(q)}_{\bm{t}}
. The real-analyticity of
\Psi
, in particular, ensures that
\mathscr{J}^{(q)}_{\bm{t}}
deforms continuously with respect to
\bm{t}
. This finishes the proof of Theorem
3
.

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
1

Let us start with the following two lemmas.

Lemma 10
.

The points
z_{j}(\bm{t})
,
j=1,\cdots,r
, depend continuously on
\bm{t}
.

Proof.

The right hand side of (
80
) clearly depends continuously on
\bm{t}
(since the
\bm{t}
-dependence in
R
is through the end-points which do depend continuously on
\bm{t}
). So the zeros of
h(z;\bm{t})
, being
z_{\ell}(\bm{t})
,
\ell=1,\cdots,r
, depend continuously on
\bm{t}
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

There are no singular finite geodesic polygons with one or two vertices associated with the quadratic differential
Q(z)\textrm{d}z^{2}
given by (
52
).

Proof.

The proof follows immediately from the Teichmüller’s lemma and the fact that
Q
is a polynomial.
∎

Now we prove Theorem
1
. Let
\bm{t}^{*}
be a regular
q
-cut point. We show that there exists a small enough neighborhood of
\bm{t}^{*}
in which all the requirements of Definition
4
hold simultaneously. We prove this in the following two mutually exclusive cases:

(a)

when none of the points
z_{\ell}(\bm{t}^{*})
lie on
\mathscr{J}^{(q)}_{\bm{t}^{*}}\setminus J^{(q)}_{\bm{t}^{*}}
, and

(b)

when one or more of the points
z_{\ell}(\bm{t}^{*})
lie on
\mathscr{J}^{(q)}_{\bm{t}^{*}}\setminus J^{(q)}_{\bm{t}^{*}}
.

Let us first consider the case (a) above. So we are at a regular
q
-cut point
\bm{t}^{*}
where we know that

(162)

A_{\ell}(\bm{t}^{*})\neq 0,\qquad\ell=1,\cdots,r,

where

(163)

A_{\ell}(\bm{t}):=\Re\eta_{q}\left(z_{\ell}(\bm{t});\bm{t}\right).

For
\varepsilon>0
, let
D_{\varepsilon}(\bm{t}^{*})
denote the open set of all points
\bm{t}
such that

|\Re t_{k}-\Re t^{*}_{k}|<\varepsilon,\quad\text{and}\quad|\Im t_{k}-\Im t^{*}_{k}|<\varepsilon,\qquad\mbox{for}\qquad k=1,\cdots,2p-1.

Since the functions
A_{\ell}(\bm{t})
are continuous functions of
\bm{t}
, for each
\ell=1,\cdots,r
there exists
\varepsilon_{\ell}>0
such that for all
\bm{t}\in D_{\varepsilon_{\ell}}(\bm{t}^{*})
the inequalities
A_{\ell}(\bm{t})\neq 0
hold for each
\ell=1,\cdots,r
. Let
\varepsilon:=\underset{1\leq\ell\leq r}{\min}\varepsilon_{\ell}
. The claim is that for all
\bm{t}\in\Omega=:\Omega_{0}\cap D_{\varepsilon}(\bm{t}^{*})
(see the proof of Theorem
2
to recall the open set
\Omega_{0}
) all requirements of Definition
4
hold. It is obvious that the second requirement of Definition
4
holds by the choice of
\varepsilon
. Suppose that condition (3) of Definition
4
does not hold for some
\tilde{\bm{t}}\in\Omega
. Let
K(\bm{t}^{*})
denote the infinite geodesic polygon which hosts the complementary contour
\Gamma_{\bm{t}}(b_{q}(\bm{t}),+\infty)
as required by condition (3) of Definition
4
. Due to Theorem
3
this is only possible if

(a-i)

one or more points on the boundaries
\ell^{(b_{q})}_{2}
and
\ell^{(b_{q})}_{3}
of the infinite geodesic polygon
K(\bm{t}^{*})
continuously deform (as
\bm{t}^{*}
deforms to
\tilde{\bm{t}}
) to coalesce together and block the access of a complementary contour from

to

, or

(a-ii)

if there are one or more humps in

(see Remark

), one or more points on the boundaries
\ell^{(b_{q})}_{2}
or
\ell^{(b_{q})}_{3}
of the infinite geodesic polygon

continuously deform (as
\bm{t}^{*}
deforms to
\tilde{\bm{t}}
) to coalesce with the hump(s) and block the access of a complementary contour from

to

Notice that if there are

in

or
p<q
which means there are no humps at all, then the only possibility to block the access from

to

. Notice that

can not be

and
