Constructive proofs for some semilinear PDEs on H 2 ​ ( e | x | 2 / 4 , ℝ d ) H^{2}(e^{|x|^{2}/4},\mathbb{R}^{d})
Maxime Breden Hugo Chu

Abstract
We develop computer-assisted tools to study semilinear equations of the form − Δ ​ u − x 2 ⋅ ∇ u = f ⁡ ( x , u , ∇ u ) , x ∈ ℝ d . -\Delta u-\frac{x}{2}\cdot\nabla{u}=f(x,u,\nabla u),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}. Such equations appear naturally in several contexts, and in particular when looking for self-similar solutions of parabolic PDEs. We develop a general methodology, allowing us not only to prove the existence of solutions, but also to describe them very precisely. We introduce a spectral approach based on an eigenbasis of ℒ := − Δ − x 2 ⋅ ∇ \mathcal{L}:=-\Delta-\frac{x}{2}\cdot\nabla in spherical coordinates, together with a quadrature rule allowing to deal with nonlinearities, in order to get accurate approximate solutions. We then use a Newton–Kantorovich argument, in an appropriate weighted Sobolev space, to prove the existence of a nearby exact solution. We apply our approach to nonlinear heat equations, to nonlinear Schrödinger equations and to a generalised viscous Burgers equation, and obtain both radial and non-radial self-similar profiles.

Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) are fundamental in the modelling of many physical and biological systems, and the computation of their solutions is often well understood on bounded domains.
However, certain PDEs can lose some key features such as particular solutions or conservation laws when restricted to a bounded domain. Furthermore, many problems, such as the ones studied in this work, are more natural to consider on an unbounded domain, either because their structure leads to simpler proofs or because they automatically give rise to localised solutions whose decay is intrinsically prescribed by the equation.

Among those, diffusion problems (both linear and nonlinear), many of which are natural to consider on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, have a simple treatment on bounded domains based on standard Sobolev spaces but which fails to extend directly on unbounded domains. Many alternative techniques have been developed or applied to the study of diffusions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such as variational formulations
[
22
,
33
,
60
]
, Lyapunov (including energy/entropy) techniques and shooting methods for radial solutions
[
12
,
44
]
. In particular, one popular approach is to recover some estimates which hold in the bounded case such as the Poincaré inequality by working on weighted Sobolev spaces instead.

Slightly more recently, a new family of tools has emerged, namely computer-assisted proofs (CAPs), which are complementary to the classical techniques. One of the benefits of CAPs is that the existence results they provide always come hand in hand with a quantitative description of the obtained solutions. Another advantage is that CAPs typically generalise in a straightforward way to systems, which is not necessarily the case for some of the aforementioned techniques.
Given a PDE, abstractly written as
F(u)=0
, and a numerically obtained approximate solution
\bar{u}
, a common feature of many CAPs is to consider an associated Newton-like operator of the form

\displaystyle T(u)=u-AF(u),

(1)

where
A
is a well-chosen approximation of
DF(\bar{u})^{-1}
, and then to prove that
T
is a contraction on a small neighbourhood of
\bar{u}
, in a suitable norm
[
56
]
.

Following the seminal papers of Nakao
[
40
]
and Plum
[
45
]
, many authors contributed to the further development and popularisation of computer-assisted proofs for elliptic PDEs (see e.g.
[
4
,
20
,
26
,
42
,
50
,
57
,
59
,
62
,
64
]
), but the vast majority of these works can only be applied to problems on bounded domains. Until recently, the main exception was the technique developed by Plum, which takes
A
equal to
DF(\bar{u})^{-1}
and relies on eigenvalues bounds obtained via the so-called homotopy method in order to control
\|DF(\bar{u})^{-1}\|
. This approach can handle a variety of problems on unbounded domains, see
[
61
]
for a recent example and
[
41
, Part II]
for a more general presentation. Regarding other CAP techniques on unbounded domains, but for more specific problems, we also mention
[
37
]
which studies the spectrum of coupled harmonic oscillators, and
[
38
]
which deals with eigenvalue exclusion for localised perturbations of a one-dimensional periodic Schrödinger operator. Lately, other general CAP methodologies were developed for (systems of) PDEs on unbounded domains, first specifically for radial solutions
[
54
,
55
]
, and then for more general solutions in
[
14
,
15
]
. In these recent approaches, one goes back to a problem on a bounded domain, either by compactifying the domain, or by dealing separately with the solutions at infinity, or by introducing a periodised problem whose solutions approximate those of the original problem. Such techniques are well-suited to study localised solutions of equations having only bounded coefficients.

In this work, we study elliptic PDEs of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u:=-\Delta u-\frac{x}{2}\cdot\nabla{u}=f(x,u,\nabla u),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2)

and develop new computer-assisted tools for such equations. Equations of the form (
2
) have already been studied extensively, as they naturally appear in different contexts such as the ones we present below, or when studying self-similarity in various problems (see
[
2
,
6
,
19
]
or
[
31
]
in the case of the Navier–Stokes equations).

As a first example, consider the nonlinear heat equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v=\Delta v-\varepsilon|v|^{p-1}v,\qquad\varepsilon=\pm 1.

(3)

If
u
solves (
2
) with
f(x,u,\nabla u)=f(u)=u/(p-1)-\varepsilon|u|^{p-1}u
, i.e.

\mathcal{L}u=\frac{u}{p-1}-\varepsilon|u|^{p-1}u,

(4)

then
v(t,x)=t^{-1/(p-1)}u(x/\sqrt{t})
is a self-similar solution of (
3

One typically seeks “physical” solutions, having Gaussian decay or as critical points of some energy functional. In the radial case, a thorough description of solutions can then be achieved via shooting or ODE methods (see
[

,
63
]
and references therein). Alternatively, a variational approach
[

,
60
]
identifies such solutions by working directly on weighted Sobolev spaces.

As a second example, notice that, if

for an arbitrary function

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u=e^{-|x|^{2}/8}\left(-\Delta\varphi+\left(\frac{|x|^{2}}{16}+\frac{d}{4}\right)\varphi\right),

therefore equation (
2
) is also connected to Schrödinger equations. Indeed, consider for instance the critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation

Using the aforementioned change of unknown

\displaystyle f(x,u,\nabla u)=f(x,u)=\left(\frac{d}{4}-\frac{\omega}{2}\right)u+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}e^{|x|^{2}/(2d)}|u|^{4/d}u.

In this work, we develop a computer-assisted approach based on (

Remark 3
.

While the radial solutions we obtain were already known to exist thanks to previous works
[
12
,
44
,
60
]
, our results also provide a precise description of these solutions. Furthermore, our methods can also deal with non-radial solutions, without relying on a perturbation argument. The only other work we are aware of where non-radial solutions are found is
[
22
, Remark 3.11]
, which relies on local bifurcation theory and therefore only holds for

Remark 4
.

Beyond the results themselves, we believe part of the interest of this work lies in the computer-assisted tools that are developed in order to handle equations of the form (
2
), and in the underlying ideas which may prove useful for CAPs, and more largely for numerical analysis, in a broader context.

•

Our CAPs are based on a spectral expansion of the solution, which is a common approach on bounded domains, but has proven challenging on unbounded domains. This is made possible by departing from the usual paradigm which consists in choosing the basis only in terms of the leading order differential operator (i.e.
\Delta
in (
2
)), so that this operator becomes easy to invert by hand. Indeed, the basis we use makes
\mathcal{L}:=-\left(\Delta+\frac{x}{2}\cdot\nabla\right)
diagonal (and therefore trivial to invert), but the operator
\Delta
alone does not have a well-behaved inverse with respect to this basis.

•

Extracting some compactness is usually key for CAPs based on (
1
), and can be especially challenging on unbounded domains. Here, compactness is retrieved by working with appropriately weighted Sobolev spaces (see Section
2.2
).

•

We use spherical coordinate basis functions of the Hilbert space
H^{2}(\mu)
which are the counterparts of the spherical harmonics in the Hermite–Laguerre family and allow us to handle our problems globally and with a unified treatment. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first occurrence of the use of these bases to solve partial differential equations, even from a purely numerical perspective. Indeed, this differs from the previous literature (both theoretical
[
22
]
and computational
[
25
]
)
on this family of problems which have exhibited (multivariate) Hermite functions as an eigenbasis for
\mathcal{L}
. While at first glance, these bases might appear tricky to work with (especially in nonlinear problems), we demonstrate that they are well-suited to our problems. We show that they can be handled efficiently to both provide a spectral method to find numerical solutions to problems of the form (
2
) and then validate these solutions rigorously. On the one hand, these bases allow us to handle non-radial solutions to radially symmetric equations. On the other hand, in the case of radial solutions, these bases allow an approach based on the analysis of the operator
\mathcal{L}
while exploiting the dimensionality reduction given by radial equations.

•

As already mentioned, spectral expansions are common in CAPs for nonlinear equations, but only when they are associated to a Banach algebra structure which facilitates the control of nonlinearities. The basis used in this work does not enjoy this property, but we still manage to handle some nonlinear terms, thanks to a combination of rigorous quadrature and Sobolev embeddings, in a fashion which is more reminiscent of CAPs based on finite elements
[
41
, Part I]
.

•

Still regarding nonlinear terms, let us mention that we also handle non-polynomial nonlinearities, namely a fractional power
p
in (
3
). The existing CAP techniques based on spectral methods are more convenient to use when only polynomial nonlinearities occur, and therefore a common approach in the presence of non-polynomial terms is to first reformulate the equation in a polynomial one, typically adding several new unknowns and associated equations in the process
[
28
,
34
,
36
]
. For a different approach allowing to directly treat some non-polynomial terms without adding extra equations and unknowns, but relying heavily on a Banach algebra structure, we refer to
[
10
]
. One can also approximate smooth non-polynomial terms using interpolation and computable error bounds
[
3
,
23
,
58
]
, but once again, the Banach algebra structure is then typically used to control perturbations around a given approximate solution. In the current work, we directly deal with the fractional power in a somewhat ad-hoc manner that also does not necessitate the introduction of extra equations and unknowns, and works without a Banach algebra.

Our approach can also handle some equations featuring first-order derivatives, when
f
in (
2
) really does depend on
\nabla u
. As an example, we also study self-similar solutions of a generalised viscous Burgers equation on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(see, e.g.
[
2
,

]

\partial_{t}v+v^{2}\partial_{x}v=\partial_{xx}v,

(8)

of the form

v(t,x)=t^{-1/4}u\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{t}}\right).

Then, one has to find a profile

which solves an equation akin to (
2
) with
f(x,u,\partial_{x}u)=u/4-u^{2}\partial_{x}u
:

\displaystyle\partial_{xx}u+\frac{x}{2}\partial_{x}u+\frac{u}{4}-u^{2}\partial_{x}u=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

(9)

Finally, let us conclude the introduction by emphasising that there have been previous occurrences of CAPs of somewhat different nature for self-similar solutions, and that this topic has been attracting a lot of attention recently for fluid flows
[
13
,
16
]
.

Functional analytic preliminaries

Properties of the operator
\mathcal{L}

One of the main ideas of this work is to make use of the operator

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{x}{2}\cdot\nabla-\Delta,

to play the role analogous to that of the Laplacian
-\Delta
as it is usually employed for computer-assisted proofs on a bounded domain. Indeed, consider the weighted space
L^{2}(\mu)
, with scalar product

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x)v(x)\mu(x)\mathrm{d}x,

where
\mu(x)=e^{|x|^{2}/4}/Z
,
Z=2^{d-1}\omega_{d-1}
where
\omega_{d-1}=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)
denotes the surface area of the
(d-1)
-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(this choice of normalisation is made in order to match the usual convention in numerical analysis).
By integration by parts, we obtain the Dirichlet form

\displaystyle\langle\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle=\langle u,\mathcal{L}v\rangle,\qquad\mbox{for all $u\in H^{1}(\mu),v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L})=H^{2}(\mu)$,}

(10)

which yields that
\mathcal{L}
is a positive self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\mu)
, and can be analysed in the same “Hilbertian” fashion as
-\Delta
is in a first course on elliptic PDEs, as will be seen below. We refer to
[
32
]
for more properties of
\mathcal{L}
, and note that the above Dirichlet form structure is also often exploited in the analysis of the generators of reversible diffusions (see for instance
[
43
]
, we explore the numerical analysis of more general equations of the form
V^{\prime}(x)\partial_{x}u-\partial_{xx}u=f(x,u)
on the weighted Sobolev space
H^{1}(e^{-V})
in
[
8
]
).

In the previous literature on the operator
\mathcal{L}

[
22
,
25
]
, its eigenbasis is usually given in cartesian coordinates in terms of Hermite functions

\mathcal{L}\Psi_{\beta}=-\left(\frac{x}{2}\cdot\nabla+\Delta\right)\Psi_{\beta}=\frac{d+|\beta|}{2}\Psi_{\beta},\qquad\mbox{where }\Psi_{\beta}(x)=e^{-|x|^{2}/4}\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}H_{\beta_{i}}(x_{i}/2),\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

(11)

where
H_{n}
denotes the Hermite polynomial of degree
n
. This basis might be suitable for some problems, but since the equations treated in this work are radially symmetric, we will instead use bases in spherical coordinates (except for
d=1
).

Definition 5

(Spherical coordinate basis)
.

For
d\in\{2,3\}
, define the following orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(\mu)
.

•

For
d=2
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

,
x_{1}=r\cos\vartheta
,
x_{2}=r\sin\vartheta
,

\psi_{l,m}(r,\vartheta)=e^{-r^{2}/4}(r/2)^{|l|}L_{m}^{(|l|)}(r^{2}/4)\begin{cases}\cos l\vartheta\qquad&\mbox{if $l\geq 0$}\\
-\sin l\vartheta\qquad&\mbox{if $l<0$,}\end{cases}

where
L^{(\alpha)}_{m}
denotes the generalised Laguerre polynomial of degree

and parameter

(see
[
1

).

•

For

,

,

,

,
x_{1}=r\sin\vartheta\cos\phi
,

,

\psi_{k,l,m}(r,\vartheta,\phi)=e^{-r^{2}/4}(r/2)^{l}L_{m}^{(l+1/2)}(r^{2}/4)P^{k}_{l}(\cos\vartheta)\begin{cases}\cos k\phi\qquad&\mbox{if $k\geq 0$}\\

The construction of the bases given in Definition

Lemma 8
.

Let
f
be a hyperspherical function on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
of order
l\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f=-l(l+d-2)f,

where
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Then
e^{-r^{2}/4}(r/2)^{l}L_{m}^{(l+d/2-1)}(r^{2}/4)f

with eigenvalue
(d+l)/2+m

\mathbb{R}^{d}

Proof.

This follows from separation of the radial variable and the spherical variables and by identifying the Laguerre differential equation after the change of variable

.
∎

In order to obtain the bases of Definition

, it then suffices to have orthogonal bases of the eigenspaces of
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}

[

Note that as a consequence, this directly generalises the known corresponding bases for the harmonic Schrödinger operator and for the generator of the radially symmetric Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Furthermore, the linear relationship between bivariate Hermite and Laguerre polynomials observed in
[

]

Corollary 9

(Radial eigenbasis)
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\alpha=d/2-1
and let
\psi_{n}(r):=L^{(\alpha)}_{n}(r^{2}/4)e^{-r^{2}/4}
, then

\mathcal{L}\psi_{n}=-\left(\frac{r}{2}+\frac{d-1}{r}\right)\frac{\partial\psi_{n}}{\partial r}-\frac{\partial^{2}\psi_{n}}{\partial r^{2}}=\left(\frac{d}{2}+n\right)\psi_{n}.

Note that with these bases, we can handle radial equations globally without treating either the behaviour at infinity or at the origin separately (see for instance
[
55
]
for an example of CAP where the behaviours at the origin and at infinity are handled separately).

Spaces, norms and Poincaré inequality

One of the main challenges of computer-assisted proofs on unbounded domains is that the embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is not compact anymore for
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as no Poincaré inequality even holds. Observe that in our setting with weighted spaces, the eigenbasis of
\mathcal{L}
together with (
10
) immediately implies a genuine Poincaré inequality

\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}\langle\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\qquad\mbox{for all $u\in H^{1}(\mu)$},

(12)

since
d/2
is the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
. We can therefore consider on
H^{1}(\mu)
the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{1}(\mu)}=\langle\nabla\cdot,\nabla\cdot\rangle
and its associated norm, which is equivalent to the usual
H^{1}(\mu)
-norm. Similarly, on the space
H^{2}(\mu)
we consider the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{2}(\mu)}=\langle\mathcal{L}\cdot,\mathcal{L}\cdot\rangle
and associated norm, which is also equivalent to the usual
H^{2}(\mu)
-norm (see Lemma
16
).

Notice that these choices of norms yield, in a similar fashion to (
12
),

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mu)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}\|u\|_{H^{2}(\mu)}\qquad\mbox{for all $u\in H^{2}(\mu)$}.

(13)

Notation 10
.

We denote by
\|u\|_{\infty}
the essential supremum of
|u|
, i.e. for a measure
\nu
on
\Omega
,

\|u\|_{\infty}:=\inf\left\{M\mid|u(x)|\leq M\textrm{ for $\nu$-a.e. $x\in\Omega$}\right\}.

Our notation does not depend on
\nu
, as all the measures we will be using are equivalent (i.e. they have the same null sets). Finally, we adopt the usual convention that

is said to be continuous if it has a continuous representative.

Another important feature of our setup is that that

is well-defined (say via the Lax–Milgram theorem

, using (

Remark 11
.

The fixed-point theorem

As is common in the CAP literature, our approach will rely on a fixed-point theorem allowing for the
a posteriori validation
of an appropriate numerical solution
\bar{u}
, that is, a proof of the existence of a solution
u^{\star}
to

\mathcal{L}u=f(x,u,\nabla u),

(22)

in an explicit neighbourhood of
\bar{u}
in a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
. This is achieved by constructing a well-chosen map
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
with solutions of Eq. (
22
) as its fixed points and which is contracting in a neighbourhood of
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{X}
. In order to actually prove that
T
is a contraction, we will use the following statements (see
[
41
,
53
,
62
]
and the references therein for variants).

Theorem 20
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})
be a Banach space, let
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-map and let
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{X}
be such that there exists a constant
Y\geq 0
and an increasing continuous function
\mathcal{Z}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\displaystyle\|T(\bar{u})-\bar{u}\|_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\leq Y,

(23)

\displaystyle\|DT(u)\|_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{Z}(\|u-\bar{u}\|_{\mathcal{X}})\qquad\mbox{for all $u\in\mathcal{X}$.}

(24)

If there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle Y+\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathcal{Z}(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle<\delta,

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\delta)<1,

(26)

then
T
has a unique fixed point
u^{\star}
in
\bar{B}(\bar{u},\delta)
.

Proof.

See for instance
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]

∎

If
T
is polynomial in

of degree

24

Let

\displaystyle\leq Y,

(27)

\displaystyle\|D^{k}T(\bar{u})h^{k}\|_{\mathcal{X}}

(28)

Introducing the radii polynomials

\displaystyle P(\delta)

\displaystyle=Y-\delta+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{Z_{k}}{k!}\delta^{k},

\displaystyle Q(\delta)

\displaystyle=-1+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{Z_{k}}{(k-1)!}\delta^{k-1},

.

Proof.

See for instance
[

, Corollary 4.4]
.
∎

We now explain how to construct suitable operator
T
in order to study solutions of equation (
22
).
Provided
g:u\mapsto f(\cdot,u,\nabla u)
maps
H^{2}(\mu)
to
L^{2}(\mu)
, our general problem

\mathcal{L}u=f(x,u,\nabla u)

can be reformulated as finding a zero of the map
F:H^{2}(\mu)\to H^{2}(\mu)
, where

F(u)=u-\mathcal{L}^{-1}f(x,u,\nabla u).

Note that if
Dg(\bar{u})
in fact maps
H^{2}(\mu)
to
H^{1}(\mu)
, then
DF(\bar{u})
is a compact perturbation of the identity. In particular, and this is a key aspect common to many such computer-assisted proofs, we can expect to find an accurate approximate inverse of
DF(\bar{u})
which is also a compact perturbation of the identity. This motivates the introduction of the linear map
A:H^{2}(\mu)\to H^{2}(\mu)
such that
P_{n}AP_{n}=A_{n}
is an approximate inverse of
P_{n}DF(\bar{u})P_{n}
computed numerically, and where
P_{n}A(I-P_{n})=(I-P_{n})AP_{n}=0
and
(I-P_{n})A(I-P_{n})=I-P_{n}
. That is, using the block decomposition associated with the orthogonal projection
P_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{n}&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix},

and we expect such an
A
to be accurate approximate inverse of
DF(\bar{u})
if
n
is large enough. This statement will be made quantitative in Section
4.1.2
, by leveraging the explicit compactness estimate (
15
), which allows us to obtain a computable upper bound for
\left\|I-ADF(\bar{u})\right\|_{H^{2}(\mu),H^{2}(\mu)}
.

We therefore consider the quasi-Newton operator
T:H^{2}(\mu)\longrightarrow H^{2}(\mu)
with

T(u)=u-AF(u),

which we expect to be contracting near
\bar{u}
.
Provided
A
is invertible (this will be an automatic consequence of verifying assumption (
26
)), zeros of
F
are indeed in one-to-one correspondence with fixed points of
T
. In Sections
4
to
6
, we derive suitable estimates
Y
and
\mathcal{Z}
(or
Z_{k}
) allowing us to apply Theorem
20
or Corollary
21
to this fixed-point operator
T
for the different equations mentioned in the introduction.

Remark 22
.

One could choose to perform a computer-assisted proof in
H^{1}(\mu)
instead of
H^{2}(\mu)
, but the class of treatable problems would be more restricted and this would not directly yield
L^{\infty}
-estimates on the solution in dimension
d\in\{2,3\}
.

Handling nonlinearities via quadrature rules

To derive the bounds
Y
and
\mathcal{Z}
needed for our CAP (See Theorem
20
), we will need to rigorously compute quantities like
\|\bar{u}^{p}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
. In the more classical case of Fourier series,
\bar{u}
is typically a finite Fourier series (i.e. a trigonometric polynomial), and
\bar{u}^{p}
is then still a finite Fourier series (provided
p
is an integer), whose integral or norm is therefore straightforward to compute exactly. In contrast, in our setting, whenever
u\neq 0
and
v\neq 0
belong to
P_{n}(H^{2}(\mu))
, the product
uv
does not belong to
P_{n^{\prime}}(H^{2}(\mu))
for any
n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. However, we can still compute exactly
\|uv\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
(or
\|\bar{u}^{p}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
), and we explain how this can be done in this section.

For clarity, we focus here on the case of the radial equation

\mathcal{L}u=u/(p-1)-\varepsilon u^{p},\quad\varepsilon=\pm 1,\ p\in\mathbb{N},

giving rise to self-similar solutions of the semilinear heat equation, but all the constructions can be adapted to the other examples. We denote by
\{\hat{\psi}_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
the normalisation of the orthogonal basis
\{\psi_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
(see Corollary
9
) with respect the
L^{2}(\mu)
-inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, i.e.

\hat{\psi}_{m}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{m!}{\Gamma(m+\alpha+1)}}L^{(\alpha)}_{m}(r^{2}/4)e^{-r^{2}/4},\qquad\alpha=\frac{d}{2}-1.

We will solve our problem with respect to this basis and look for our numerical approximation
\bar{u}
in
\mathrm{Span}\{\hat{\psi}_{m}\}_{m=0}^{n}
(which is the set of radial functions in
P_{n}(H^{2}(\mu))
). We can then write
\bar{u}
in terms of its “Fourier” coefficients:

\bar{u}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\bar{u}_{m}\hat{\psi}_{m}\qquad\bar{\mathbf{u}}=(\bar{u}_{0},\ldots,\bar{u}_{n}).

(29)

Since our computer-assisted proof will mostly rely on a Hilbertian approach, one of the main challenges is to be able to compute many projections and integrals (eventually rigorously) on unbounded domains. Such endeavour might appear naive given the lack of simple error control on Gauss–Laguerre quadrature and the notorious difficulties of working with this family of bases
[
24
]
. Furthermore, we would ideally like to solve (via gradient descent or Newton’s method) the Galerkin problem

\left\langle\mathcal{L}\bar{u}-\bar{u}/(p-1)+\varepsilon\bar{u}^{p},\hat{\psi}_{j}\right\rangle=0\qquad\mbox{for all $j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}$},

(30)

without further approximating the problem. We show that this can in fact be achieved relatively efficiently for a nonlinearity of moderate degree by simply adapting the Gauss–Laguerre quadrature. Consider more generally the problem of evaluating (eventually rigorously) integrals of the form

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\prod_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}(x)\mu(x)\mathrm{d}x,

for
f_{k}\in\mathrm{Span}\{\hat{\psi}_{m}\}_{m=0}^{n}
. For instance,
\|\bar{u}^{p}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
can be written in this form, but also the coefficients of
P_{n}(\bar{u}^{p})
given by
\langle\bar{u}^{p},\hat{\psi}_{m}\rangle
for
m=0,\ldots,n
. Since we are in the radial setting, this boils down to evaluating integrals of the form

\int_{0}^{\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}(r)(r/2)^{d-1}e^{r^{2}/4}\mathrm{d}r,

(31)

with
f_{k}(r)=p_{k}(r^{2}/4)e^{-r^{2}/4}
for some polynomial
p_{k}
of degree at most
n
. Note that all calculations can be thought to be done with respect to the variable
z=r^{2}/4
.

Remark 23
.

When we have to deal with non-radial solutions, we also use spherical coordinates, and the projections and integrals with respect to the spherical variables only involve sines and cosines and thus do not require a quadrature. This is another advantage of using spherical coordinates instead of cartesian ones (i.e., of using the bases of Definition
5
instead of (
11
)), for which nontrivial quadratures would be required in every direction.

We now show how to exactly compute integrals of the form (
31
).

Lemma 24
.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
be polynomials of degree at most
n
and let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
2N-1\geq mn
. Then, for
\alpha>-1
and
\beta>0

\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{\alpha}e^{-\beta z}\prod_{k=1}^{m}p_{k}(z)\mathrm{d}z=\beta^{-\alpha-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}\prod_{k=1}^{m}p_{k}\left(\frac{z_{i}}{\beta}\right),

(32)

where
z_{i}
denotes the
i
th
root of
L^{(\alpha)}_{N}
and

W_{i}=\frac{\Gamma(N+\alpha+1)z_{i}}{N!(N+1)^{2}[L^{(\alpha)}_{N+1}(z_{i})]^{2}}\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.

Proof.

This follows from using the chain rule for the change of variables
z\mapsto z/\beta
and the
N
th
Gauss–Laguerre quadrature (see
[
24
, Formula 4.1]
) which is exact for polynomials of degree
2N-1
or less.
∎

In our implementation,
p_{k}
will usually be written as

p_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a^{k}_{j}L^{(\alpha)}_{j}/\|\psi_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

Thus, the above quadrature rule would be written as

\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{\alpha}e^{-\beta z}\prod_{k=1}^{m}p_{k}(z)\mathrm{d}z=\beta^{-\alpha-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n}a^{k}_{j}L^{(\alpha)}_{j}\left(\frac{z_{i}}{\beta}\right)=\beta^{-\alpha-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}\prod_{k=1}^{m}(V\mathbf{a}^{k})_{i},

where
\mathbf{a}^{k}=(a^{k}_{j})_{0\leq j\leq n}
and
V=(V_{ij})=(L^{(\alpha)}_{j}(z_{i}/\beta)/\|\psi_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)})
is a (pseudo)-Vandermonde matrix. However, this matrix has very large entries so that (
32
) as written needs to be computed in high precision arithmetic. When (
32
) needs to be evaluated fast and accurately, it proves better to use a regularised Vandermonde matrix
\bar{V}=\mathrm{Diag}(\beta^{-\alpha-1}W)^{1/m}V

so that

\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{\alpha}e^{-\beta z}\prod_{k=1}^{m}p_{k}(z)\mathrm{d}z=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{m}(\bar{V}\mathbf{a}^{k})_{i}.

In practice,
\bar{V}
can be precomputed once (using high precision if needed but then possibly stored in lower precision) and after this is done both the rigorous and non-rigorous computations can be carried out in a manageable amount of time.

Remark 25
.

In practice,
\bar{V}
proves much better behaved than
V
and easier to work with. It could be interesting to further investigate the properties of
\bar{V}
.

Example 26
.

In both the rigorous and non-rigorous parts, one needs to compute the matrix

G_{ij}=\langle\hat{\psi}_{i},\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{j}\rangle

which can be written as
G=\bar{V}^{T}\mathrm{Diag}(\bar{V}\bar{\mathbf{u}})^{p-1}\bar{V}
for
m=p+1
and
\beta=p
(and
\bar{\mathbf{u}}
as in (
29
)).

Note that here the nonlinearity in the Galerkin problem is not approximated by its so-called
pseudospectral
projection (usually obtained by collocation) found in the previous related literature
[
27
,
25
]
.

Like the validation procedure, the quadrature is implemented rigorously in
Julia
using the package
IntervalArithmetic.jl

[
46
]
. Our implementation also relies on approximating the roots of
L^{(\alpha)}_{N}
with high precision, using initial guesses given by
FastGaussQuadrature.jl

[
52
]
and refining them using Newton’s method in
BigFloat
arithmetic. The roots are then enclosed rigorously by combining the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra and the Intermediate Value Theorem (recall that the roots of orthogonal polynomials are always simple and real).
V
is then computed by recursion using the three-term recurrence relation for orthogonal polynomials. Since some of the
z_{i}
have large magnitude, this calculation quickly leads to large error accumulation, hence the need to enclose the
z_{i}
with high precision. While this quadrature might appear expensive, in our experience for problems with
N\leq 2000
, the enclosure of the roots together with the computation of
\bar{V}
only took a few minutes on a standard laptop.

Putting quadrature aside, the overall computational cost of the computer-assisted proofs presented in this paper is very similar to that of more standard proofs using Fourier series: the main limitations are memory constraints and matrix-matrix multiplications in interval arithmetic. The cost of computing the quadrature, i.e., the matrix
\bar{V}
, only becomes noticeable if
N
has to be taken somewhat large. Indeed, increasing
N
requires increasing the precision for the calculation of the quadrature rule, and more specifically for rigorously evaluating the polynomials
L_{j}^{(\alpha)}
, inevitably increasing computational time. Among all the computer-assisted proofs presented in this paper, only for the one of Theorem
42
did the quadrature have a predominant cost.

Remark 27
.

Hermite and Laguerre bases are far less popular than Fourier and Chebyshev for computer-assisted proofs, or in numerical analysis for this matter. One reason is the lack of simple and global error control on interpolation and quadrature. However, polynomials are well-controlled in the bulk, and the tails of
\hat{\psi}_{m}
can be controlled via the following estimate of Szegö
[
1
, formula 22.14.13]

|\hat{\psi}_{m}(r)|\leq\hat{\psi}_{m}(0)e^{-r^{2}/8}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(m+\alpha+1)}{m!}}e^{-r^{2}/8},

(33)

if
\alpha=d/2-1\geq 0
, i.e., if
d\geq 2
. In particular, this estimate in theory allows for integration with rigorous error control, say by bluntly integrating in the bulk via interval arithmetic. Since our method will only rely on integration, it might handle more general nonlinearities. Note that this estimate immediately provides
L^{\infty}
-bounds on finite linear combinations of the
\hat{\psi}_{m}
’s.

The semilinear heat equation

In this section, we are interested in the semilinear heat equation

\partial_{t}v=\Delta v-\varepsilon|v|^{p-1}v\qquad\mbox{on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

(34)

with
p>1
. It is well-known that if
u
solves the nonlinear elliptic problem

-\Delta u-\frac{x}{2}\cdot\nabla{u}-\frac{u}{p-1}+\varepsilon|u|^{p-1}u=0,

(35)

then
v(t,x)=t^{-1/(p-1)}u(x/\sqrt{t})
is a self-similar solution of (
34
). These solutions are relevant when studying the asymptotic behaviour of solutions to Eq. (
34
). Indeed, provided that
v(0,\cdot)\in H^{1}(\mu)
, they appear as (possibly unstable) “omega-limit points”
[
32
]
of
v(t,\cdot)
in the sense that there exists a solution
u
to Eq. (
35
) and
t_{n}\rightarrow\infty
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|t_{n}^{1/(p-1)}v(t_{n},\cdot)-u\left(\frac{\cdot}{\sqrt{t_{n}}}\right)\right\|_{\infty}=0.

Let us first focus on positive radial solutions of Eq. (
35
) (we will later show in Section
4.3
that the solutions we obtain are indeed positive)
which have been the subject of
[
12
,
44
]
, so that Eq. (
35
) becomes

\mathcal{L}u=-\partial_{r}^{2}u-\left(\frac{d-1}{r}+\frac{r}{2}\right)\partial_{r}u=\frac{u}{p-1}-\varepsilon u^{p}.

(36)

Furthermore, let us assume first for simplicity that
p\in\mathbb{N}
. An example with a non-integer power
p
will be presented in Section
4.4
, and non-radial solutions will be studied in Section
4.5
.

Suitable bounds for the validation

In this section, we apply the general strategy outlined in Section
2.4
to Eq. (
36
), and derive computable estimates

and

satisfying the assumptions of Corollary

.
Since we are first looking for radial solutions, we restrict ourselves to the Hilbert space

Throughout this section, we then assume
\bar{u}
is an element of

. In particular,

This quantity will appear in several of the estimates to come.

In order to study Eq. (

\displaystyle F:\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{H},

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\longmapsto u-\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(\frac{u}{p-1}-\varepsilon u^{p}\right),

(37)

and

\begin{cases}DT(\bar{u})h&=h-ADF(\bar{u})h=h-A(h-\mathcal{L}^{-1}(h/(p-1)-\varepsilon p\bar{u}^{p-1}h))\\
D^{k}T(\bar{u})h^{k}&=-\varepsilon\dfrac{p!}{(p-k)!}A\mathcal{L}^{-1}(\bar{u}^{p-k}h^{k})\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad 1<k\leq p.\end{cases}

Recall Notation

and Remark

4.1.1
The bound
Y

It suffices to estimate

\displaystyle\|T(\bar{u})-\bar{u}\|^{2}_{H^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|AF(\bar{u})\|_{H^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|P_{n}(AF(\bar{u}))\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(AF(\bar{u}))\|_{H^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|A_{n}(P_{n}F(\bar{u}))\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}F(\bar{u})\|^{2}_{H^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|A_{n}(P_{n}F(\bar{u}))\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\mathcal{L}^{-1}(\bar{u}^{p})\|^{2}_{H^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|A_{n}(P_{n}F(\bar{u}))\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\left(\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}\bar{u}^{p}\|^{2}_{H^{2}}-\|P_{n}\mathcal{L}^{-1}(\bar{u}^{p})\|^{2}_{H^{2}}\right).

Thus, we may choose

Y^{2}:=\|\mathcal{L}A_{n}(P_{n}F(\bar{u}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\left(\|\bar{u}^{p}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\|P_{n}(\bar{u}^{p})\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right).

Note that all the terms in
Y
can be evaluated rigorously by means of a quadrature rule (see Lemma
24
) and the output is expected to be small provided the truncation level
n
is large enough. In particular, the first term can practically be made arbitrarily small in the non-rigorous computation of
\bar{u}
.

Remark 29
.

Here and in many subsequent places, we slightly abuse notation, in the sense that the number
Y
that we will actually compute is only an upper bound of the quantity
Y
defined above. Indeed, we use interval arithmetic to control rounding errors, and in later estimates we also only upper-bound quantities like
\left\|\bar{u}\right\|_{\infty}
rather than trying to compute them
exactly
.

4.1.2
The bound
Z_{1}

We choose to compute
Z_{1}
by treating finite and infinite dimensional parts separately, and therefore take

Z_{1}:=\left\|\left(\begin{matrix}Z^{11}&Z^{12}\\
Z^{21}&Z^{22}\end{matrix}\right)\right\|_{2}

where the
Z^{ij}
are real numbers satisfying

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\|P_{n}DT(\bar{u})P_{n}\|_{H^{2},H^{2}}&\leq Z^{11},\\
\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}DT(\bar{u})P_{n}\|_{H^{2},H^{2}}&\leq Z^{21},\\
\|P_{n}DT(\bar{u})P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2},H^{2}}&\leq Z^{12},\\
\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}DT(\bar{u})P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2},H^{2}}&\leq Z^{22}.\\
\end{cases}

(38)

Indeed, for
h\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|h\|_{H^{2}}=1
, write
h=h_{n}+h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
such that
h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
,
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\in\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
. Then,

\displaystyle\|DT(\bar{u})h\|_{H^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\|P_{n}DT(\bar{u})h\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}DT(\bar{u})h\|_{H^{2}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\left(\|P_{n}DT(\bar{u})h_{n}\|_{H^{2}}+\|P_{n}DT(\bar{u})h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2}}\right)^{2}+\left(\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}DT(\bar{u})h_{n}\|_{H^{2}}+\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}DT(\bar{u})h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\left(Z^{11}\|h_{n}\|_{H^{2}}+Z^{12}\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2}}\right)^{2}+\left(Z^{21}\|h_{n}\|_{H^{2}}+Z^{22}\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}Z^{11}&Z^{12}\\
Z^{21}&Z^{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\|h_{n}\|_{H^{2}}\\
\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq Z_{1}\qquad\mbox{since $\|h_{n}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}=1$.}

In the following subsections, we derive computable bounds
Z^{ij}

38
).

4.1.3
The bound
Z^{11}

For
h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
,

P_{n}DT(\bar{u})h_{n}=P_{n}(I-ADF(\bar{u}))h_{n}=(I_{n}-A_{n}P_{n}DF(\bar{u})P_{n})h_{n}

by construction of
A

\|P_{n}DT(\bar{u})P_{n}\|_{H^{2},H^{2}}=\|I_{n}-A_{n}P_{n}DF(\bar{u})P_{n}\|_{H^{2},H^{2}}=\|\mathcal{L}(I_{n}-A_{n}P_{n}DF(\bar{u})P_{n})\mathcal{L}^{-1}\|_{L^{2},L^{2}}=:Z^{11},

which is expected to be small because
A_{n}\approx(P_{n}DF(\bar{u})P_{n})^{-1}
. Using the isometric isomorphism between
L^{2}(\mu)
and
\ell^{2}
, this quantity
Z^{11}
is in fact nothing but the
2
-norm of an
(n+1)\times(n+1)

M\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}

\|M\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\|M\|_{1}\|M\|_{\infty}}
. While this estimate is not sharp in general, it is good enough for our purpose, as we only need to compute

either when

is very close to being zero (and we only need an estimate which is below

), or where
\|M\|_{2}
is not the crucial estimate.

4.1.4
The bound
Z^{21}

For
h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, we get that

\displaystyle P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}DT(\bar{u})h_{n}

\displaystyle=-P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}DF(\bar{u})h_{n}

\displaystyle=P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\mathcal{L}^{-1}(h_{n}/(p-1)-\varepsilon p\bar{u}^{p-1}h_{n})

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon pP_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\mathcal{L}^{-1}(\bar{u}^{p-1}h_{n}),

and thus,

\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}DT(\bar{u})h_{n}\|_{H^{2}}=p\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}h_{n})\|_{L^{2}}.

Then, writing

h_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}a_{m}\hat{\psi}_{m},

we get

P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}h_{n})=\sum_{m=0}^{n}a_{m}P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{m}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}h_{n})\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{n}\|a_{m}P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{m})\|_{L^{2}}\qquad\mbox{by Minkowski inequality}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{n}|a_{m}\lambda_{m}|\|\lambda^{-1}_{m}P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{m})\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n}\lambda^{-2}_{m}\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{m})\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n}\lambda_{m}^{2}a_{m}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\qquad\mbox{by Cauchy--Schwarz inequality}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n}\lambda^{-2}_{m}\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{m})\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\|h_{n}\|_{H^{2}}.

Introducing the vector

with

w_{m}:=\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{m})\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\|\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\|P_{n}(\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{m})\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right)^{1/2},

and identifying

Note that

is expected to be small, especially for small

\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}w\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\lambda^{-2}_{m}w_{m}^{2}
small.

4.1.5
The bound
Z^{12}

Now, for
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\in\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}

\displaystyle P_{n}DT(\bar{u})h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}

\displaystyle=-P_{n}ADF(\bar{u})h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2}}}{\lambda_{n+1}}\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{p-1}\hat{\psi}_{m})\|_{L^{2}}\qquad\mbox{by\penalty\ \eqref{eq:projection_error_L2H2}}.

\|P_{n}DT(\bar{u})P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\|_{H^{2},H^{2}}\leq{\frac{p}{\lambda_{n+1}}\left\|\,\left|A_{n}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\right|w\right\|_{H^{2}}=\frac{p}{\lambda_{n+1}}\left\|\,\left|\mathcal{L}A_{n}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\right|w\right\|_{L^{2}}}=:Z^{12},

where for

For
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\in\mathcal{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}

4.1.7
The bounds
Z_{k}

Proving positivity a posteriori

Similarly to the previous literature on the semilinear heat equation, we also wish to verify the (strict) positivity of our radial solutions in this work. Note that even if
\bar{u}
were proven to be strictly positive, for any
\delta>0
,
B(\bar{u},\delta)
necessarily contains functions that are eventually negative at
+\infty
, e.g.
\bar{u}-(\delta/2)\hat{\psi}_{m}/\|\hat{\psi}_{m}\|_{H^{2}(\mu)}
for large enough
m
. Thus, the positivity of
u^{\star}
cannot be deduced directly. Since
\bar{u}
decays faster than our
L^{\infty}
-control, a direct comparison of
\bar{u}
and
u^{\star}
is only sufficient in a neighbourhood of zero. We thus resort to a maximum principle argument to show the (strict) positivity of
u^{\star}
away from zero with the lemma below.

Lemma 31
.

Let
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
two
\mathcal{C}^{0}
functions and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function such that

\displaystyle-\Delta\varphi+b\cdot\nabla\varphi+c\varphi=0.

(42)

Assume there exists
r_{0}>0
such that
c(x)>0
for all
|x|\geq r_{0}
. Assume further that
\varphi(x)>0
for all
|x|\leq r_{0}
, and that
\varphi(x)\underset{|x|\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
. Then,
\varphi(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

This statement is essentially due to the maximum principle, which can be used even though we are on an unbounded domain because we already control the behaviour of the solution at infinity.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and assume that
\varphi
is nonpositive somewhere. Then, since
\varphi(x)\underset{|x|\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
, there exists
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\varphi(x_{0})=\min_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi\leq 0
, and we must have
|x_{0}|>r_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{U}
be a connected open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
containing
x_{0}
, such that
|x|\geq r_{0}
for all
x\in\mathcal{U}
, and such that there exists
x
in
\partial\mathcal{U}
with
|x|=r_{0}
. Since
\varphi
reaches its (nonpositive) minimum over
\bar{\mathcal{U}}
at the interior point
x_{0}
, while
c
is positive in
\mathcal{U}
, the strong maximum principle yields that
\varphi
must be constant on
\bar{\mathcal{U}}
. In particular,
\varphi
is nonpositive on
\partial\mathcal{U}
, which contradicts the fact that the
\varphi(x)
is positive for all
|x|\leq r_{0}
.
∎

Note that with
\varepsilon=-1
in (
36
), we are in the “bad-sign” case of the semilinear heat equation, so that the strong maximum principle and the above lemma cannot be applied directly to equation (

An example with a fractional exponent

In the case

s
times more where the degree of the nonlinearity is

Now, analysing the function

An example of a non-radial solution

Let us now turn to the problem of finding (real-valued) non-radial solutions to problem (
35
). We limit ourselves to the case
\varepsilon=-1,d=2,p=3
here (though, such construction can be generalised), so that the problem remains polynomial while allowing for solutions that are not necessarily positive. In polar coordinates, the equation for
u(r,\vartheta)=e^{-r^{2}/8}\varphi(r\cos\vartheta,r\sin\vartheta)
reads

\mathcal{L}u=\frac{1}{2}u+u^{3},

(46)

where

\mathcal{L}u=-\frac{r}{2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}-\Delta=-\frac{r}{2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}-\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\right)-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\vartheta^{2}}.

Observe that since the equation itself is radially symmetric, if
u:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution to Eq. (
46
), then so is
u(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot+\vartheta_{0})
for any phase
\vartheta_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
. Thus, our approach which relies on a contraction principle, and hence requires local uniqueness, cannot possibly work directly on
H^{2}(\mu)
. By treating the problem in polar coordinates, instead of using cartesian coordinates with the Hermite basis (
11
), we get a natural way to handle the translation invariance in the angular variable.

We thus restrict the problem to an appropriate subspace, namely

\mathcal{H}=\overline{\mathrm{Span}\left\{\psi_{k,m}\,\mid\,k\in\mathbb{N},m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}^{H^{2}}\subset H^{2}(\mu),

where
\psi_{k,m}
are as in Definition
5
, and, as before, we denote by
\hat{\psi}_{k,m}
the normalisation of
\psi_{k,m}
with respect to the
L^{2}(\mu)
-inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Since we restricted ourselves to nonnegative
k
’s,
\mathcal{H}
is nothing but the space of
H^{2}(\mu)
-functions which are even with respect to
\vartheta
.
Solving for Eq. (
46
) in this space addresses the above symmetry “issue” and it is straightforward to check that

\displaystyle F:\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\longmapsto u-\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(\frac{u}{2}+u^{3}\right)

(47)

is well-defined and compact. In this respect, our strategy goes along the same line as the one employed in
[
5
]
to find non-radial solutions of analogous semilinear equations on the disc. In the most general setting, the above-defined space
\mathcal{H}
shall be used, but one can simplify the proof further if the solution has a zero projection onto certain frequencies. In our example, we will perform our computer-assisted proof in the subspace

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}=\overline{\mathrm{Span}\left\{\psi_{2k+1,m}\,\mid\,k\in\mathbb{N},m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}^{H^{2}}\subset\mathcal{H}\subset H^{2}(\mu).

Working on such subspaces can also automatically discard other non-zero solutions which are not of interest such as radial ones. Here, we have that

\mathcal{L}\psi_{2k+1,m}=\lambda_{k+m}\psi_{2k+1,m},\qquad\lambda_{j}:=\frac{3}{2}+j,

and the
\psi_{2k+1,m}
for
k,m\in\mathbb{N}
form a Hilbert basis of
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
. Note that the definition of
\lambda_{j}
in that context is slightly different from the one introduced in Notation
28
. In particular, this change reflects the fact that the first eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
restricted to
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
is strictly larger than
d/2=1
, which slightly improves the Poincaré inequalities (
12
) and (
13
) on
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
.

The estimates for the radii polynomials coefficients are essentially the same as in Section
4.1
, with
P_{n}
now denoting the projection onto
\mathrm{Span}\left\{\psi_{2k+1,m}\,\mid\,k\in\mathbb{N},m\in\mathbb{N}\text{ with }k+m\leq n\right\}
, and

w_{k,m}:=\|P_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\bar{u}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{2k+1,m})\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\left(\|\bar{u}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{2k+1,m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}-\|P_{n}(\bar{u}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{2k+1,m})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right)^{1/2}.

The computation of integrals described in Section
3
is adapted as follows:

•

the integral is first reformulated as a sum of integrals of product of functions, separable in
r
and
\vartheta
components so as to apply Fubini’s Theorem,

•

the integrals with respect to
\vartheta
are treated via the multiplication formula for cosines and orthogonality,

•

the integrals with respect to

are of the form (
32
) after the change of variable

.

As for the

-bounds on
\hat{\psi}_{2k+1,m}

|\hat{\psi}_{2k+1,m}(r,\vartheta)|\leq|\hat{\psi}_{2k+1,m}(r,0)|

by enclosing its zeros (which must all be real), this time using
PolynomialRoots.jl
, the Julia implementation of
[

]
.

An example with first order terms

In this final section, we consider a toy problem whose interest lies in the fact that it leads to an equation of the form (
2
) with a first-order term, i.e. a right-hand side of the form
f(x,u,\nabla u)
with an actual dependence on
\nabla u
. We focus on a generalised viscous Burgers equation on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with Neumann boundary condition

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v+v^{2}\partial_{x}v=\partial_{xx}v,\\
\partial_{x}v(t,0)=0\qquad\qquad\quad\mbox{for all $t>0.$}\end{cases}

(53)

Then, under the similarity transformation

v(t,x)=t^{-1/4}u\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{t}}\right),

v
solves Eq. (
53
) for
t>0
if
u
solves

\mathcal{L}u-\frac{u}{4}+u^{2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=0,\qquad x\geq 0.

(54)

Similar problems have been studied in
[
2
,
49
]
and the references therein.
This equation can be approached with our method, as
u\in H^{2}(\mu)
implies that
u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
and thus
u^{2}\partial_{x}u\in L^{2}(\mu)
.

Remark 40
.

We deal with a one-dimensional example here for simplicity, but terms like
(u\cdot\nabla)u
could in principle also be handled in dimension
d\in\{2,3\}
, as
u\in H^{2}(\mu)
is then still enough to guarantee that
(u\cdot\nabla)u\in L^{2}(\mu)
since
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

In order to enforce the Neumann boundary condition, we symmetrise the problem and work on the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{\hat{\psi}_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}}^{H^{2}}=\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{e^{-x^{2}/4}\hat{H}_{2m}(x/2)\}_{m=0}^{\infty}}^{H^{2}},

i.e. we make use of “even half-Hermite series” where
\hat{H}_{n}=H_{n}/\sqrt{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}}
denotes a normalised Hermite polynomial. Note that working on
\mathcal{H}
is equivalent to working on
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},e^{x^{2}/4})
with the additional Neumann boundary condition (see also
[
60
, Section 3]
). Furthermore, for clarity, in what follows, all elements of
L^{2}(\mu)
that we will consider should be treated as functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
having an even extension on
\mathbb{R}
. In particular, for such functions
u
and
v
, we have that

\langle u,v\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}uv\mu(x)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}uve^{x^{2}/4}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}uve^{x^{2}/4}\mathrm{d}x.

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}
