A Novel Exact Analytical Expression for the Magnetic Field of a Solenoid
Mostafa Behtouei 1 , Luigi Faillace 1 , Bruno Spataro 1 , Alessandro Variola 1 and Mauro Migliorati 2,3

Abstract
In this paper we present the analytical calculations to derive the magnetic field of a solenoid by solving exactly a fractional integral with the use of a novel method. Starting from the Biot-Savart law, we consider a coil of negligible thickness with a stationary electric current. We derive the expressions of the on and off-axes magnetic field components. The results have been compared to some simplified and known analytical formulae as well as to a commercial numerical code showing a good agreement.



I
Introduction

The evaluation of the magnetic field in a solenoid is important in many fields of applied physics. For example, it is used in particle accelerators to counteract the effect of beam blow-up, due to space charge, in high brilliance photoinjectors
[
1
,
2
]
as well as for high gradient accelerating structures
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. Moreover the use of magnetic correctors and steering devices are largely used in linear accelerators to align the trajectory of the charged beam
[
6
]
.

The basic laws of current-carrying coils, representing a solenoid, have been established experimentally by Biot and Savart and then developed by Ampere
[
7
,
8
]
. The Biot-Savart law is a consequence of Maxwell’s equations. The procedure to determine the magnetic field is as follows:

Magnetic fields generated by steady current can be obtained from the curl of the vector potential,

\bf B={\bf\nabla}\times A

(1)

Assuming the Maxwell’s equations, choosing the Coulomb gauge,
{\bf\nabla\cdot A}
=0 and implying the steady state condition, gives

\nabla^{2}{\bf A}=\mu_{0}{\bf J}

(2)

This is Poisson’s equation and the solution is

{\bf A}(\mathrm{p})=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int\frac{{\bf J}(\mathrm{p^{\prime}})}{|{\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}}|}d^{3}\mathrm{p^{\prime}}

(3)

where
{\bf p}
is the observation point and
{\bf p^{\prime}}
is the integration variable. From Eq. (
1
) we finally obtain the Biot-Savart law for a wire of finite thickness

{\bf B}(\mathrm{p})=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int\frac{{\bf J}(\mathrm{p^{\prime}})\times({\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}})}{|{\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}}|^{3}}d^{3}\mathrm{p^{\prime}}

(4)

where
d^{3}\mathrm{p^{\prime}}
is the infinitesimal volume element. For a thin wire with an infinitesimal element,
d\mathrm{p^{\prime}}
, this reduces to

{\bf B}(\mathrm{p})=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int\frac{{\bf I}\times({\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}})}{|{\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}}|^{3}}d\mathrm{p^{\prime}}.

(5)

In the past some methods have been investigated in order to solve the above integrals to have analytic expressions of the magnetic fields produced by a thin wire, a 2D surface element or, in a general form, by a current density. The authors of
[
9
]
proposed an analytical solution starting from the numerical approach developed by Caspi
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
and Leleux
[
13
]
, and they described the magnetic potential near the axis of a multipole with the inclusion of the magnetic dipole. However, the problem for the magnetic field components far from the axis needed the evaluation of a large number of terms for the expansion coefficients. The authors of
[
14
]
have derived an analytical expression for the magnetic field of a cylinder of finite length with a uniform, transverse magnetization by using the assumptions that there are no free currents and that the magnetic field could be expressed as the gradient of a magnetostatic scalar potential. The final solution of the magnetic field components has been written in terms of the complete elliptic integrals of the first, second and third kind.

The problem that all authors were facing
[
9
,
12
,
14
]
, was that the final integral in cylindrical coordinates of the magnetic field components or the vector potential become a fractional integral of the order 3/2 and 1/2, respectively. In both cases we have to face a branch line. In this paper we propose a solution of the problem by cutting the branch line in order to have an analytic function inside the integral instead of multi-valued operation (obtained in the case of branch line). This approach can also be used for calculating the radial force and its derivatives caused by the centrifugal space charge. The author of
[
15
]
has derived an equation for the derivatives of the radial force which is related to the centrifugal space charge using the elliptical integral.

In the next section we will obtain an equation for the magnetic field generated by a single coil of radius
R
and we will calculate analytically the field amplitude on and off axis in a point at an arbitrary distance
r
from the axis of the coil.

II
Derivation of magnetic induction generated by a wire carrying current (circular current loop)

{\bf p^{\prime}}=(R\ \cos\alpha,R\ \sin\alpha,z_{0})

(6)

In the special case of a constant current
I

{\bf B}(\mathrm{p})=\frac{\mu I}{4\pi}\int\ \frac{d{\bm{\sigma}}\times({\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}})}{|{\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}}|^{3}}

with,

d{\bm{\sigma}}=R\ d\alpha\ {\bm{\tau}}

where

R
and

{\bm{\tau}}=-\hat{i}\sin\alpha+\hat{j}\cos\alpha

with
\hat{i}
and
\hat{i}
the unit vectors along
x
and

d{\bm{\sigma}}=R\ d\alpha\ (-\hat{i}\sin\alpha+\hat{j}\cos\alpha)

Subtracting the two vectors of the Eqs. (
6
) and (

{\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}}=\hat{i}(r\cos\gamma-R\cos\alpha)+\hat{j}(r\sin\gamma-R\sin\alpha)+\hat{k}(z-z_{0})

and, replacing Eq. (

{\bf B}(\mathrm{p})=\frac{\mu_{0}IR}{4\pi}\int\frac{{\bm{\tau}}\times({\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}})}{|{\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}}|^{3}}\ d\alpha

where cross product of
{\bm{\tau}}

{\bm{\tau}}\times({\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}})=\begin{vmatrix}\hat{i}&\hat{j}&\hat{k}\\

=\hat{i}\ (z-z_{0})\ \cos\alpha+\hat{j}\ (z-z_{0})\ \sin\alpha+\hat{k}\ (R-r\ \cos(\gamma-\alpha))

To solve the integral of Eq. (
13
) we can calculate
|{\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}}|^{2}
as follows:

|{\bf p}-{\bf p^{\prime}}|^{2}=r^{2}+R^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}-2rR\cos(\gamma-\alpha)

(15)

Substituting Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) into the Eq. (
13
) we obtain:

{\bf B}(\mathrm{p})=\frac{\mu_{0}{I}R}{4\pi}\\
\int\frac{(\hat{i}\cos\alpha+\hat{j}\sin\alpha)(z-z_{0})+\vec{k}\ (R-r\ \cos(\gamma-\alpha))}{[r^{2}+R^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}-2rR\cos(\gamma-\alpha)]^{3/2}}\ d\alpha

(16)

Writing the components of the magnetic field along the x, y and z coordinates gives:

\mathrm{B}_{x}=\frac{\mu_{0}IR(z-z_{0})}{4\pi}\\
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\cos\alpha}{[r^{2}+R^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}-2rR\cos(\gamma-\alpha)]^{3/2}}\ d\alpha

(17)

\mathrm{B}_{y}=\frac{\mu_{0}IR(z-z_{0})}{4\pi}\\
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin\alpha}{[r^{2}+R^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}-2rR\cos(\gamma-\alpha)]^{3/2}}\ d\alpha

(18)

\mathrm{B}_{z}=\frac{\mu_{0}IR}{4\pi}\\
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{(R-r\cos(\gamma-\alpha))}{[r^{2}+R^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}-2rR\ \cos(\gamma-\alpha)]^{3/2}}\ d\alpha

(19)

Let D be the boundary of
C^{3}
, that is,

\mathrm{D}=\{{\bf p}=\{x,y,z\}\in C^{3}|\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}<R\}

(20)

We apply the above boundary because we are working on a cylindrical shape type of solenoid and the magnetic field outside of the solenoid is not considered.

The basic differential laws of magnetostatics are given by:

\nabla\times{\bf B}({\bf p})=\frac{4\pi}{c}\ {\bf j},\ \ {\bf p}\in D

(21)

\nabla\cdot{\bf B}({\bf p})=0,\ \ {\bf p}\in D

(22)

To verify Eq. (
22
) we rewrite the magnetic field vector in cylindrical coordinates:

\mathrm{B}_{r}={\bf B}\ \cdot\ {\bf r}=B_{x}\ \cos\gamma+B_{y}\ \sin\gamma

(23)

\mathrm{B}_{\gamma}={\bf B}\ \cdot\ {\bm{\tau}}=-B_{x}\ \sin\gamma+B_{y}\ \cos\gamma

(24)

where,

{\bm{\tau}}=(-\sin\gamma,\ \cos\gamma,\ 0)

(25)

{\bf r}=(\cos\gamma,\ \sin\gamma,\ 0)

(26)

By substituting Eqs. (
17
), (
18
) into the Eqs. (
23
), (
24
), we obtain the components of magnetic field in cylindrical coordinates as follows,

\mathrm{B}_{r}=\frac{\mu_{0}IR(z-z_{0})}{4\pi}\\
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\cos(\gamma-\alpha)}{[r^{2}+R^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}-2rR\cos(\gamma-\alpha)]^{3/2}}\ d\alpha

(27)

\mathrm{B}_{\gamma}=\frac{\mu_{0}IR(z-z_{0})}{4\pi}\\
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin(\alpha-\gamma)}{[r^{2}+R^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}-2rR\cos(\gamma-\alpha)]^{3/2}}\ d\alpha

(28)

\mathrm{B}_{z}=\frac{\mu_{0}IR}{4\pi}\\
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{(R-r\ \cos(\gamma-\alpha))}{[r^{2}+R^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}-2rR\ \cos(\gamma-\alpha)]^{3/2}}\ d\alpha

(29)

It is possible to verify that Eq. (
22
) is satisfied and the Eq. (
28
), due to symmetry considerations, is always zero.
We rewrite Eqs. (
27
) and (
29
) with the definition of a parameter
\xi
as:

\xi(r,R,z)=\frac{2rR}{r^{2}+R^{2}+(z-z_{0})^{2}}

(30)

Considering dimensionless parameters
\eta=\frac{r}{R}
,
M_{z}=\frac{z}{R}
and putting
z_{0}=0
, the above equation becomes:

\xi(R,z,\eta)=\frac{2\eta}{1+\eta^{2}+M_{z}^{2}}

(31)

After some manipulations and replacing the above equation we obtain that the radial and longitudinal components of the magnetic field are:

\mathrm{B}_{r}=\frac{\mu_{0}IM_{z}}{4\sqrt{2}\pi R}(\frac{\xi}{\eta})^{3/2}\ I_{2}(\xi)

(32)

\mathrm{B}_{z}=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{4\sqrt{2}\pi R}(\frac{\xi}{\eta})^{3/2}\ (I_{1}(\xi)-\eta\ I_{2}(\xi))

(33)

where,

I_{1}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\psi}{[1-\xi\ \cos(\psi)]^{3/2}}

(34)

I_{2}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(\psi)}{[1-\xi\ \cos(\psi)]^{3/2}}\ d\psi

(35)

and
\psi=\gamma-\alpha
(we put the reference at
\gamma=0
). In the next section, we first investigate the statement of the problem, then we introduce a method to solve the above integrals.

III
Statement of the Problem

As we have mentioned in the previous section, we will first report here the current theorems used in literature for solving the fractional integrals.

Riemann-Liouville fractional integral:

Definition: Let Re
\nu>0
and f be piecewise continuous and integrable on (0,
\infty
). Then for
z>0
we define

D_{z-z_{0}}^{-\alpha}f(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{0})^{-\alpha+1}}dz

(36)

when
z=z_{0}
, this is called Riemann-Liouville fractional integral of the function
f
of order
\alpha
.

The Weyl fractional derivatives is used when
z_{0}
takes on a singular value from
-\infty
to
\infty
, and it can be expressed as,

D_{z^{-\infty}}^{-\alpha}f(z)=\frac{(-1)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{z_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{f(z)^{n}}{(z-z_{0})^{-\alpha-1}}dz

(37)

and

D_{z^{+\infty}}^{-\alpha}f(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\int_{-\infty}^{z_{0}}\frac{f(z)^{n}}{(z-z_{0})^{-\alpha-1}}dz.

(38)

The Caputo fractional derivative
[
16
]
is used in order to solve the differential equations without defining the fractional order initial conditions. Caputo’s definition is as follows.

\small{D_{z-z_{0}}^{-\alpha}f(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\frac{f(z)^{n}}{(z-z_{0})^{\alpha+1+n}}dz,\ \ n-1<\alpha<n.}

(39)

Notice that all above theorems have a limitation for
\alpha
, and we need a theorem which is defined for all values of
\alpha
. The authors of
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
have studied this kind of integral. The procedure is as follows.

First we recall the Cauchy’s integral formula:

D^{n}f(z)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{2\pi i}\oint_{C}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{0})^{n+1}}dz

(40)

Let the contour of integration be
\gamma(z_{0},z^{+})
in which the branch line for
(z-z_{0})^{-\alpha-1}
starts from the position z and end at the fixed point
z_{0}
. The above equation is equivalent to the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral when Re
(\alpha)<0
. We divide the contour
\gamma(z_{0},z^{+})
into three contours (see Fig. 2),

\gamma(z_{0},z^{+})=\gamma_{1}(z_{0}\rightarrow z)\ U\ \gamma_{2}(O)\ U\ \gamma_{3}(z\rightarrow z_{0})

(41)

where,

\gamma_{1}(z\rightarrow z_{0})
: line segment from z to
z_{0}
;

\gamma_{2}(O)
: small circle centered at
z_{0}
;

\gamma_{3}(z_{0}\rightarrow z)
: line segment from
z_{0}
to z.

Fig. 2:
contour of integration

Then the Cauchy’s integral formula becomes:

D^{n}f(z)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma(z_{0},z)}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{0})^{n+1}}dz=I_{\gamma_{1}}+I_{\gamma_{2}}+I_{\gamma_{3}}

(42)

I_{\gamma_{1}},I_{\gamma_{2}},I_{\gamma_{3}}
denote the integrals over the mentioned contours
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
. Then, the line in which the branch occurs can be written,

\frac{1}{(z-z_{0})^{\alpha+1}}=e^{(-\alpha-1)(ln|z-z_{0}|+i(\theta-\pi))}\ \ \ on\ \ \gamma_{1}

(43)

\frac{1}{(z-z_{0})^{\alpha+1}}=0\ \ \ on\ \ \gamma_{2}

(44)

\frac{1}{(z-z_{0})^{\alpha+1}}=e^{(-\alpha-1)(ln|z-z_{0}|+i(\theta+\pi))}\ \ \ on\ \ \gamma_{3}

(45)

It should be noted that the integral tends to zero on
\gamma_{2}
as the contour’s radius goes to zero. Substituting the above equations inside of Eq. (
11
) we obtain

{}^{\gamma(z,z^{+})}D_{z-z_{0}}^{n}f(z)=\small{\frac{(e^{i\pi\alpha}+e^{-i\pi\alpha})\ \Gamma(\alpha+1)}{2\pi i}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{0})^{n+1}}dz}

(46)

or

{}^{\gamma(z,z^{+})}D_{z-z_{0}}^{n}f(z)=\frac{sin(\pi\alpha)\ \Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\pi}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{0})^{n+1}}dz

(47)

Notice that above equation is valid for all values of
\alpha
and we can use it for our case as we see in the next section.

IV
Fractional Integral’s Solution and the Final Solution

To solve the integrals
34
and
35
we transform the equations into the complex plane with
z=e^{-i\psi}
and use the modified Cauchy’s integral formula. It should be noted that to use the Cauchy’s integral formula, one should deal with the analytic function of the integral. However, in our case, we have multi-valued operation.

It should be noted that when z goes to
z_{0}
, it creates a branch line along to the branch point
z_{0}
. To eliminate the branch line and turn the multi-valued function into an analytic function we divide the contour into three contours in order to use the Cauchy’s integral formula [see Appendix]. Such a problem requires an entirely new solution, but the same principle can work also in our case. Finally we obtain

I_{1}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{d\psi}{[1-\xi\ \cos(\psi)]^{3/2}}\\
=\frac{\pi}{(1+\xi)^{3/2}}\ _{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})

(48)

I_{2}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(\psi)}{[1-\xi\ \cos(\psi)]^{3/2}}\ d\psi\\
=\frac{-\pi}{(1+\xi)^{3/2}}\ [\ _{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})-\ _{2}F_{1}(\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2};2;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})\ ]

(49)

Where
\ {}_{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})
and
\ {}_{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})
are the ordinary hypergeometric functions having a general form of the kind

\ {}_{p}F_{q}(a_{1},..,a_{p};b_{1},..,b_{q};z)=\Sigma_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{n}...(b_{p})}{(b_{1})_{n}...(b_{p})_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}

(50)

where
(a_{p})_{n},(b_{q})_{n}
are the rising factorial or Pochhammer symbol with

(a)_{0}=1

(51)

and

(a)_{n}=a(a+1)(a+2)...(a+n-1),\ \ \ \ n=1,2,..

(52)

Finally axial and radial magnetic field components are:

\mathrm{B}_{r}=\frac{\mu_{0}I\ M_{z}}{2R}(\frac{\xi}{2\eta(1+\xi)})^{3/2}\\
[\ _{2}F_{1}(\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2};2;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})\ -\ _{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})]

(53)

\mathrm{B}_{z}=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2R}(\frac{\xi}{2\eta(1+\xi)})^{3/2}\\
[(1+\eta)\ _{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})-\eta\ _{2}F_{1}(\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2};2;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})\ ]

(54)

A simple derivation of the axial magnetic field component can be obtained to evaluate the field on the axis of the coil:

\mathrm{B}_{z}=\frac{\mu_{0}IR^{2}}{2(R^{2}+z^{2})^{3/2}}

(55)

This is a classic equation which can be found in many physics textbooks and papers
[
8
,
9
,
19
]
. For the radial on-axis field component (
\eta
=0), both hypergeometric functions of the radial magnetic field components become 1 and cancel each other giving:

\mathrm{B}_{r}=0

(56)

The authors of
[
9
]
described the magnetic potential near the axis of a multipole and they have obtained the following equations for the magnetic field components:

\mathrm{B}_{r}(r,z)=2\Sigma_{p=0}^{\infty}p\ G_{02p}(z)r^{2p-1}

(57)

\mathrm{B}_{z}(r,z)=\Sigma_{p=0}^{\infty}\ G_{02p+1}(z)r^{2p}

(58)

where,

G_{02p}(z)=\frac{\mu_{0}I_{c}}{R^{2p}}\Sigma_{k=0}^{\infty}F_{0,2p,2k+1}\ f_{2k+1}(z)

(59)

G_{02p+1}(z)=\frac{\mu_{0}I_{c}}{R^{2p}}\Sigma_{k=0}^{\infty}F_{0,2p,2k+1}\ g_{2k+1}(z)

(60)

in the above equations we have the following definitions,

f_{h}(t)=(\frac{z}{\sqrt{R^{2}+z^{2}}})^{h},\ \ \ \ (h=0,1,...\infty)

(61)

g_{2k+1}(t)=\frac{df_{2k+1}}{dt}=\frac{(2k+1)R^{2}}{(R^{2}+z^{2})^{3/2}}f_{2k}(t).

(62)

F_{0,2p,2k+1}=\frac{(-1)^{p}}{4^{p}(p!)^{2}}(\mathrm{M}^{p}\mathrm{F}_{00})_{2k+1}

(63)

where M stands for the second derivative of functions
f_{2k+1}
and
\mathrm{F_{00}}
is reported in
[
9
]
. Taking the first two terms of the magnetic field components we obtain:

\mathrm{B}_{r}(r,z)=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{4}[\frac{3R^{2}z}{(R^{2}+z^{2})^{5/2}}]r

(64)

\mathrm{B}_{z}(r,z)=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2}\ [\frac{R^{2}}{(R^{2}+z^{2})^{3/2}}-\frac{3R^{2}\ r^{2}}{2(R^{2}+z^{2})^{5/2}}]

(65)

which coincide with Eqs.
53
and
54
when we consider the first two terms in the expansion of the hypergeometric series
\ {}_{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})
and
\ {}_{2}F_{1}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;\frac{2\xi}{1+\xi})
.

By assuming
(r^{2}+z^{2})^{1/2}\gg R
and neglecting the higher order terms of R in the denominator of the Eqs.
53
and
54
and finally taking only few terms in the expansion of the hypergeometric functions, the axial and radial magnetic field components can be written as

\mathrm{B}_{r}(r,z)=\frac{\mu_{0}m}{4\pi}\frac{3rz}{(r^{2}+z^{2})^{5/2}}

(66)

\mathrm{B}_{z}(r,z)=\frac{\mu_{0}m}{4\pi}(\frac{3z^{2}}{(r^{2}+z^{2})^{5/2}}-\frac{1}{(r^{2}+z^{2})^{3/2}})

(67)

where
m
stands for the magnetic moment of the coil,
m=IS
, with
S
its cross section. These are well known expressions of the magnetic field of a small coil (magnetic dipole) at distance much greater than the radius of the loop
[
20
]
.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we reported the derivation of the on and off-axes magnetic field components of a solenoid with negligible thickness by means of the hypergeometric functions
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
. The results have been compared with some known analytical expressions in some simple cases and with the numerical code CST Particle Studio. We obtained a very good agreement. The method described in this paper can be used to obtain the magnetic field of correctors in a LINAC or for the magnetic focusing system of a klystron.

[Solution of the Fractional Integral]

We begin with Cauchy’s integral formula:

D^{n}f(z)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{2\pi i}\oint_{C}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{0})^{n+1}}dz

(68)

Let the contour of integration be
\gamma(z_{0},z^{+})
. The branch line for
(z-z_{0})^{-\alpha-1}
starts from the position z and ends at the fixed point
z_{0}
. The above equation is equivalent to the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral when Re
(\alpha)<0
. We divide the contour
\gamma(z_{0},z^{+})
into three contours (see Fig. 6),

\gamma(z_{0},z^{+})=\gamma_{1}(z_{0}\rightarrow z)\ U\ \gamma_{2}(O)\ U\ \gamma_{3}(z\rightarrow z_{0})

(69)

where,

\gamma_{1}(z\rightarrow z_{0})
: line segment from z to
z_{0}
;

\gamma_{2}(O)
: small circle centered at
z_{0}
;

\gamma_{3}(z_{0}\rightarrow z)
: line segment from
z_{0}
to z.

Fig. 6:
contour of integration

Then the Cauchy’s integral formula becomes:

D^{n}f(z)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma(z_{0},z)}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{0})^{n+1}}dz=I_{\gamma_{1}}+I_{\gamma_{2}}+I_{\gamma_{3}}

(70)

I_{\gamma_{1}},I_{\gamma_{2}},I_{\gamma_{3}}
denote the integrals over the mentioned contours
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
. Then, the line in which the branch occurs can be written as

\frac{1}{(z-z_{0})^{\alpha+1}}=e^{(-\alpha-1)(ln|z-z_{0}|+i(\theta-\pi))}\ \ \ on\ \ \gamma_{1}

(71)

\frac{1}{(z-z_{0})^{\alpha+1}}=0\ \ \ on\ \ \gamma_{2}

(72)

\frac{1}{(z-z_{0})^{\alpha+1}}=e^{(-\alpha-1)(ln|z-z_{0}|+i(\theta+\pi))}\ \ \ on\ \ \gamma_{3}

(73)

It should be noted that the integral tends to zero on
\gamma_{2}
as the contour’s radius goes to zero. Substituting the above equations inside of Eq. (
11

(74)

{}^{\gamma(z,z^{+})}D_{z-z_{0}}^{n}f(z)=\frac{sin(\pi\alpha)\ \Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\pi}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{0})^{n+1}}dz.

(75)

Notice that above equation is valid for all values of
\alpha

\cos(\psi)

\cos\psi=\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}
