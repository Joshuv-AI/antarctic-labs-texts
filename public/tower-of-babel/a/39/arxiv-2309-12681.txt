Tight and Efficient Gradient Bounds for Parameterized Quantum Circuits
Alistair Letcher, Stefan Woerner, Christa Zoufal

Abstract
The training of a parameterized model largely depends on the landscape of the underlying loss function. In particular, vanishing gradients (also known as barren plateaus ) are a central bottleneck in the scalability of variational quantum algorithms (VQAs), and are known to arise in various ways, from circuit depth and hardware noise to global observables. However, a caveat of most existing gradient bound results is the requirement of t t -design circuit assumptions that are typically not satisfied in practice. In this work, we loosen these assumptions altogether and derive tight upper and lower bounds on loss and gradient concentration for a large class of parameterized quantum circuits and arbitrary observables, which are significantly stronger than prior work. Moreover, we show that these bounds, as well as the variance of the loss itself, can be estimated efficiently and classically – providing practical tools to study the loss landscapes of VQA models, including verifying whether or not a circuit/observable induces barren plateaus. In particular, our results can readily be leveraged to rule out barren plateaus for a realistic class of ansätze and mixed observables, namely, observables containing a non-vanishing local term. This insight has direct implications for hybrid Quantum Generative Adversarial Networks (qGANs), a generative model that can be reformulated as a VQA with an observable composed of local and global terms. We prove that designing the discriminator appropriately leads to 1-local weights that stay constant in the number of qubits, regardless of discriminator depth. Combined with our first contribution, this implies that qGANs with appropriately chosen generators do not suffer from barren plateaus even at scale – making them a promising candidate for applications in generative quantum machine learning. We demonstrate this result by training a qGAN to learn a 2D mixture of Gaussian distributions with up to 16 qubits, and provide numerical evidence that global contributions to the gradient, while initially exponentially small, may kick in substantially over the course of training.

2.3
Locality

To describe whether a given observable induces barren plateaus, various notions of
locality
have been introduced
[
2
,
8
,
22
]

Our central contribution, Theorem
1
, provides bounds for

However, in order to exclude barren plateaus for specific circuit-observable pairs, as in Corollary
1

In this work, we say that

if it acts non-trivially on a constant number

otherwise. This is referred to as

This notion of locality can be refined by decomposing

and
\alpha\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}
, where a

is defined by
P_{\alpha}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{\alpha_{i}}
and
\sigma_{\alpha_{i}}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}
are the Pauli matrices. We can then define

to be

-local
if it contains exactly
k

\absolutevalue{\al}=k
in the

pseudo-norm (
\absolutevalue{\al}\coloneqq\absolutevalue{ \{ \al_i \neq 0 \} }
). We can then define an observable to be

if it contains

local term

with a non-vanishing coefficient

. Note that we do not require observables to have a polynomial number of terms or a polynomially bounded spectral norm, as often assumed in the literature. These distinctions are illustrated, with examples, in Figure

2.4
Related Work

Most existing results focus on barren plateaus in circuits that satisfy certain
t
-design assumptions, either exactly
[

,
2
,

,
7
,

In particular,

As this work will show, relaxing these assumptions leads to more practical bounds with slightly different implications.

By proving that Pauli strings make independent contributions to the gradient,

]
have extended the

[

For Periodic Structure ansätze,
[
65
]
have conjectured that the scaling of the gradient variance is inversely proportional to the dimension of a dynamical Lie algebra associated to the input state. This has recently been proven independently by
[

]
and
[
67
]

for observables that are in the Lie algebra associated with the ansatz. While both works provide interesting and novel perspectives on the origins of barren plateaus, they differ from our contributions in requiring circuits to form approximate 2-designs.

[
64
]
have analyzed the practical usefulness of the Hardware Efficient Ansatz (HEA), and identify a “Goldilocks scenario where shallow HEAs could achieve a quantum speedup: QML tasks with data satisfying an area law of entanglement”. This is encouraging, and we hope our contributions can help refine their insights by loosening their
t
-design assumptions.

[
13
]
have ruled out barren plateaus for quantum convolutional networks and tree tensor networks, but these classes of circuits do not apply to problems that are naturally induced by global observables, as they require tracing out almost all qubits before measurement. They also show that a class of hardware-efficient ansätze induces barren plateaus for sufficient depth – but do not provide lower bounds in order to rule them out for shallow circuits.

Finally,

]
and

have proposed initialization schemes that rule out barren plateaus without

-design assumptions. However, the results of

applies to global observables, but only provides non-trivial bounds when the gradient at the origin is sufficiently large. Our work instead focuses on providing tight bounds for generic uniform initialization, a larger class of circuits, and

3
Results

The central contribution of this section is Theorem
1
, where we provide tight upper and lower bounds for loss and gradient concentration, for
arbitrary
observables, and
without any t-design assumptions
. The result is stated for product mixed states to avoid overloading notation and highlight its practical interpretability, but we provide a proof of the generalized result for arbitrary mixed states in Appendix

3.1
Main Theorem

We introduce the circuit class in Definition
1
below, along with notions of light-cones and orthogonality required to state our main result in Definitions
2
and
3
.

Definition 1

(Circuit class)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be the family of parameterized circuits
U(\theta)
of the form

U(\theta)=\prod_{k=0}^{K}W_{k}R_{k}(\theta)\,,

where
W_{k}
are Clifford gates;
R_{k}(\theta)=\prod_{l}R_{P_{kl}}(\theta_{kl})
are products of rotation gates generated by arbitrary Pauli strings
P_{kl}
, acting on
any number of qubits
, such that
R_{0}
begins with two layers of orthogonal single-qubit rotations (see Figure
2
); and parameters
\theta_{kl}
are independent and initialized uniformly over
[-\pi,\pi]
.

Moreover, for any circuit
U(\theta)\in\mathcal{U}
, any initial state
\rho
and any observable
H
, we write

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\Tr(U(\T) \rho U^\dagger(\T) H)

for the induced loss, and similarly,
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
for the loss induced by any Pauli observable
P_{\alpha}
.

Importantly, none of our assumptions restrict the expressivity of the circuit class. In the case of initial orthogonal layers, any parameterized circuit
U(\theta)
can be written as a circuit
U^{\prime}(\theta,\psi)
with two initial orthogonal layers parameterized by
\psi
, by setting
\psi=0
. The same applies to parameters being independent, which can evolve to be (almost) equal over the course of training. Similarly, non-Clifford gates can be approximated using a sequence of parameterized rotation gates with appropriate parameters; specifically, adding
T\propto R_{Z}(\pi/4)
to the Clifford group provides a set of universal quantum gates.

Moreover, these assumptions are necessary to avoid edge cases inducing vanishing gradients. For example, a RealAmplitudes circuit
[
69
]
, which does not have two initial orthogonal layers, paired with a 1-local
Y
-observable, may have
uniformly zero gradients
. One could loosen the assumption by imposing further restrictions on the
observable
or the
input state
, but these are typically imposed by the problem at hand – it is thus preferable to impose the weakest possible assumptions on the
ansatz
. We discuss this in full detail in Appendix
C
.

Definition 2

(Light-cones)
.

Provided any
m
-parameter circuit
U(\theta)
from Definition
1
, and any
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]^{m}
, the
light-cone

{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}
is defined as the number of qubits on which
U^{\dagger}(\theta)P_{\alpha}U(\theta)
acts non-trivially.

By loosening
t
-design assumptions, our results (below) show that gradient concentration is not determined by locality, circuit depth or entanglement
per se
, but by the
interaction
between the observable and circuit, which is captured by the size of light-cones. This has the
a priori
surprising consequence that even a
global
observable does not always induce barren plateaus, as further detailed below. For a more intuitive and visual illustration of how these light-cones arise, and how these notions improve on the bounds in prior work, please refer to Appendix
B
.

Definition 3

(Orthogonality)
.

If
\rho=\otimes\rho_{i}
is a product mixed state, we define a measure of orthogonality

\Omega(\rho)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}\\
\forall i\,:\,\alpha_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}\end{subarray}}\Tr(P_\al\rho)^{2}=\prod_{i}\,\sum_{\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}}\Tr(\sig_{\la_i} \rho_i)^{2}

which quantifies the ‘portion’ of
\rho
which is orthogonal to the first layer of rotations
\otimes_{i}R_{\sigma_{\nu_{i}}}
.

As shown in Appendix
C
, an initial state
\rho
that is aligned with an eigenvector of the first-layer rotations may produce uniformly zero gradients – motivating us to capture the notion of orthogonality
\Omega(\rho)
provided above. In the special case where
\rho=\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}
is the zero initial state, and the first layer of the circuit does not include
Z
-rotations, the initial state is fully orthogonal to the first layer, and we find
\Omega(\rho)=1
.

In Part
(G)
of Appendix
A
, we generalize the theorem given below to all mixed states by defining the generalized
\alpha
-orthogonality
\Omega(\rho,\alpha)
to be a sum over weights
\Tr(P_\la\rho)^{2}
for which
P_{\lambda}
is orthogonal to the first layer, on each qubit line where
P_{\alpha}
does not commute with the second layer.
However, unlike product states, this measure is no longer
\alpha
-independent, resulting in bounds that are more general but less intuitive and practical. They can, nonetheless, still be estimated efficiently using Monte Carlo sampling if the coefficients
\Tr(P_\la\rho)
are known.

Theorem 1
.

For any
m
-parameter circuit
U(\theta)\in\mathcal{U}
, any observable
H=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}
, and any mixed state
\rho
, each Pauli term makes an independent contribution to the loss and gradient variances:

\displaystyle\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{2}\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\,,

\displaystyle\Var_{\theta}\big[\nabla\mathcal{L}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{2}\Var_{\theta}\big[\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\,.

Moreover, writing
\mathcal{D}
for the discrete uniform distribution over
\{0,\pi/2\}^{m}
, each non-trivial term (
\alpha\neq 0
) can be reduced to a
discrete
expectation, namely,

\norm{\, \Var_\T\big[ \nabla\cL_\al\big] \,}_{\infty}=\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]=\E_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\alpha}\big]\,.

Finally, for product mixed states
\rho
(generalized to all states in Appendix
A
), the loss variance is bounded by

\displaystyle\Omega(\rho)\,\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}}\ \right]\leq\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\leq\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}}\ \right]\,,

and there are circuit-observable pairs that saturate these inequalities. In particular, combined with the previous equation, at least one partial derivative
\partial_{k}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
satisfies the lower bound, and all of them satisfy the upper bound.

Notably, our reduction of a continuous variance to a discrete expectation implies that one can efficiently, and classically, estimate the variance of the loss, since
U(\theta)
is a Clifford circuit for parameters in
\{0,\pi/2\}
. This implies, in turn, that one can classically check whether a given circuit-observable pair will suffer from barren plateaus, hence enabling
classical ansatz design
.
Our bounds can similarly be estimated efficiently, and are significantly tighter than those of existing work, as illustrated in Figure
3

B

\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]
does not make the bound evaluation redundant, because the variance of the bound estimation scales favorably compared with that of the loss.

An immediate consequence of Theorem
1
is that we can rule out barren plateaus provided
at least one
term
P_{\alpha}
with non-vanishing
c_{\alpha}
has logarithmically small light-cones. Given any mixed (or local) observable, one generic approach to achieve this is to construct a circuit with logarithmic depth and a local form of entanglement, since light-cones typically grow with each layer of entangling gates.
To make this as concrete as possible, we explicitly rule out barren plateaus for the standard class of EfficientSU2 circuits
[
68
]
in Corollary
1
below. It should be noted that while this particular circuit may be simulated efficiently for a local observable, this is not necessarily possible in the mixed observable case. Furthermore, the setting can easily be extended to a larger class of circuits with shallow depth and
O(1)
-local entanglement, by varying the proof in Appendix
A
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
H
be any mixed observable on
n
qubits,
\rho
the zero initial state, and
U(\theta)
an EfficientSU2 circuit of logarithmic depth
O(\log n)
, pairwise entanglement, and rotation layers
(R_{Y},R_{Z})
. Then the corresponding loss does not suffer from barren plateaus, namely,

\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}\big]\in\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\poly(n)}\right)\,.

For completeness, this corollary is illustrated numerically in Figure
1

, where we provide explicit examples of local and mixed observables which induce only polynomially vanishing gradients – as opposed to the exponentially vanishing gradients of a global observable. Though the example is simplistic, the fact that mixed observables do not induce barren plateaus has a number of applications, and in particular, allows us to guarantee non-vanishing initial gradients for qGANs in Section
3.2
. It is also worth emphasizing that while global gradients vanish exponentially
on average
, this does not imply we ought to use local approximations of the loss, because global terms can contribute significantly later in training, as displayed in the training of qGANs (Fig.
4
a
).

Extensions.
Corollary
1
is stated for algebraically mixed observables, but can easily be extended with appropriate variations of the proof. We state two such possibilities below, which are in line with the corollaries 1 and 4 of
[
7
]
, proven for circuits forming 2-designs.

1.

For 1D-lattice circuits, if
H
contains a
topologically

k
-local Pauli string with
k\in O(\log n)
, then the lower bound is still polynomial. (Recall that an observable is topologically
k
-local if it acts non-trivially on
k

neighbouring
qubits in the 1D lattice.)

2.

If
H
is
low-bodied

[
22
]
, i.e. contains
an
algebraically

k
-local Pauli string with
k\in O(\log n)
, then the lower bound is super-polynomial but sub-exponential, namely,
\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}\big]\in\Omega(1/\poly(n^{\log n}))
.

Globality.

As already mentioned, Theorem
1
implies that even a
global
observable may not necessarily induce barren plateaus. Although apparently at odds with
[
2
]
, this is no real surprise, since global and local observables are only separated by a change of basis. Consider for example a circuit with two initial rotation layers, followed by a single linear layer of CNOT gates. The global observable
X^{\otimes{n}}
propagates through this entanglement layer by cancelling itself out on all qubit lines except the last, producing a light-cone of size 1 and a gradient variance lower-bounded by
1/4
,
for any number of qubits
. Despite the artificiality of this construction, it may be helpful in designing ansätze for problem-specific observables, and demonstrates that relaxing
t
-design assumptions allows us to capture finer observable-circuit interaction. Notably, this aligns with the recent insights on barren plateaus using symmetry groups and dynamical Lie algebras; see
[
66
,
67
,
70
]
.

In the following section, we apply Theorem
1
to prove that a class of qGANs do not suffer from barren plateaus regardless of discriminator depth, providing a promising test-bed for generative quantum machine learning.

3.2
Quantum GANs

Generative QML models learn to encode the probability distribution underlying given data by employing quantum resources, which has been shown to facilitate efficient (but approximate) quantum data loading
[
29
,
71
]
. However, generative quantum models
[
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
,
76
]

that are based on
explicit
loss functions such as the Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
77
]
–which require explicit access to the model probability distribution–inherently suffer from observable-induced barren plateaus
[
78
,
22
]
.
It is therefore desirable to work with
implicit
loss functions, which are functions of samples underlying the model probability distribution, instead.
For example,
[
22
]
have suggested the use of implicit loss functions which are approximately local, such as the Maximum Mean Discrepancy loss with a particular class of Gaussian kernels.
However, the trainability of models with this type of loss function has so far only been proven for tensor product ansätze.
Orthogonally, local formulations and approximations of the actual loss functions have been suggested as potential remedy
[
16
,
22
,
10
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. Although this approach can guarantee non-vanishing gradients, it may however lead to spurious local minima
[
27
,
28
]
.

On the other hand, hybrid qGANs
[
29
,
31
,
42
,
32
]
, also based on implicit loss functions, can be reformulated as a ground state problem with respect to a global observable. We focus on this setting, which falls outside the scope of trainability guarantees proven by existing work.

A generative adversarial network consists of two opposing models, a generator and a discriminator. The goal of the generator is to generate data samples that are similar to a training data set and the goal of the discriminator is to correctly classify data samples as true (from the training data set) or fake (stemming from the generator).
We consider hybrid qGANs where the generator is a parameterized
quantum
circuit
G_{\theta}
, while the discriminator is a
classical
neural network
D_{\phi}
– resulting in a hybrid qGAN. The generator’s goal is to encode a
discrete
distribution
p_{\theta}
matching the original distribution
p_{\text{true}}
underlying the training data, while the discriminator attempts to distinguish between them.

Definition 4

(Discriminator Class)
.

We consider discriminators
D_{\phi}:\mathbb{B}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
in the class
\mathcal{N}
of fully-connected neural networks with
L
hidden layers, leaky-ReLU activation functions
[
79
]
and an output activation
F(x)=\log(\sig(x))
, for min-max GANs, or
F(x)=x
, for Wasserstein GANs. For each layer
l
, we write
m_{l}
for the number of neurons (width),
\sigma_{l}
for the standard deviation of initial weights (all parameters except biases), and
\gamma_{l}
for the leaky-ReLU parameter. In particular, the neural net has
m_{0}=n
inputs and
m_{L+1}=1
output.
We assume that discriminator weights and biases are initialized following any i.i.d. symmetric distributions, such as state-of-the-art Kaiming or Xavier initializations
[
80
,
81
]
. If the training data
x\in X
is not initially binary, we assume that it can be transformed by some mapping
T:X\to\mathbb{B}^{n}
before being fed into the discriminator.

Importantly, note that that leaky-ReLU is the typical choice of activation for state-of-the-art GANs
[
82
,
83
,
84
]
.

Definition 5

(Generator Class)
.

We consider generators
G_{\theta}
in the class
\mathcal{U}
of parameterized quantum circuits from Definition
1
. The generator action is written as

G_{\theta}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}}\sqrt{p_{\theta}(x)}e^{iq_{\theta}(x)}\ket{x}\,,

noting that the phase factor
e^{iq_{\theta}(x)}
has no impact on the learnt distribution, and can therefore be neglected.

The generator and discriminator losses are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{G}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\E_{x\sim p_{\theta}}\big[F(D_{\phi}(x))\big]\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{D}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\E_{x\sim p_{\text{true}}}\big[F(D_{\phi}(x))\big]+\E_{x\sim p_{\theta}}\big[\tilde{F}(D_{\phi}(x))\big]\,,

for some analytic functions
F,\tilde{F}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, encompassing both min-max
[
85
]
and Wasserstein
[
86
]
GANs. While the (classical) discriminator gradients may be computed with standard automatic differentiation techniques, the (quantum) generator gradients require further considerations.
The latter reads
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{G}(\theta,\phi)=\sum_{x}\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)F(D_{\phi}(x))\,,

where the individual gradients
\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)
are subject to observable-induced barren plateaus
[
2
]
because
\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)=\nabla_{\theta}\bra{0}G_{\theta}^{\dagger}OG_{\theta}\ket{0}
, where
O=\ket{x}\bra{x}
is a global projector.

However, this intuition is misleading: the generator loss can be rewritten as
\mathcal{L}_{G}(\theta,\phi)=\bra{0}G_{\theta}^{\dagger}H_{\phi}G_{\theta}\ket{0}
, with a diagonal observable
H_{\phi}=\sum_{x}F(D_{\phi}(x))\ket{x}\bra{x}
that depends only on the discriminator, and can be expanded into the
Z
-Pauli basis to obtain
H_{\phi}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}(\phi)Z_{\alpha}
, where, using Equation (
14
),

c_{\alpha}(\phi)=\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x}{(-1)}^{\alpha\cdot x}D_{\phi}(x)\right)\,.

(2)

Since these weights are independent from
\theta
, we can moreover split the loss into a weighted sum that includes local and global terms
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}(\theta)=\bra{0}G_{\theta}^{\dagger}Z_{\alpha}G_{\theta}\ket{0}
which are, in turn, independent from
\phi
:

\mathcal{L}_{G}(\theta,\phi)=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}(\phi)\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}(\theta)\,.

(3)

Hence, following Theorem
1
,
H_{\phi}
will not necessarily induce a barren plateau if local weights
c_{\alpha}(\phi)
are sufficiently large for local contributions to the gradient to be non-vanishing. The central contribution of this section, Theorem
2
, is precisely to prove that this holds for all 1-local weights, for any discriminator from Definition
4
.

Theorem 2
.

Consider any discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\phi}\in\mathcal{N}
, and the corresponding weights
c_{\alpha}(\phi)
defined by Equation (
2
). Then, for any 1-local weight (namely
\absolutevalue{\al}=1
), we have

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\Big[c_{\alpha}(\phi)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma_{L+1}^{2}}{16}\ \prod_{l=1}^{L}\frac{m_{l}\sigma_{l}^{2}(1+\gamma_{l})^{2}}{4}\,.

In particular, initialising parameters such that
m_{l}\sigma^{2}_{l}\geq 4
for each
l
, the bound reduces to
\E_{\phi}\big[c_{\alpha}(\phi)^{2}\big]\geq\sigma^{2}_{L+1}/16
, which is
constant
both in the number of qubits
n
and the discriminator depth
L
.

Theorem
2
guarantees that the observable
H_{\phi}
induced by a discriminator
D_{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}
has 1-local weights that stay
constant
in the number of qubits, provided the initial variance of parameters is inversely proportional to layer width. This coincides with the state-of-the-art approaches to initialisation in classical ML, guaranteeing that back-propagated gradients have a maintained variance across layers
[
80
,
81
]
. Combining these insights with our previous results, we can immediately rule out barren plateaus for qGANs, provided the generator has logarithmic depth. We state this explicitly for the class of EfficientSU2 circuits in Corollary
2
below, which, as already discussed, can easily be extended to other circuit classes with shallow depth and
O(1)
-local entangling layers.

Corollary 2

(Absence of barren plateaus in qGANs)
.

Let
\rho
the zero initial state,
G_{\theta}
be an EfficientSU2 circuit of logarithmic depth
O(\log n)
, pairwise entanglement, and rotation layers
(R_{Y},R_{Z})
, and
D_{\phi}\in\mathcal{N}
be a discriminator satisfying
m_{l}\sigma_{l}^{2}\geq 4
for each layer
l
. Then the induced loss function given by Equation (
3
) does not suffer from barren plateaus, namely,

\Var_{\theta,\phi}\big[\mathcal{L}_{G}\big]\in\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\poly(n)}\right)\,.

This guarantees that initialising parameters following Definitions
4
and
5
, namely, uniformly for the generator and following any i.i.d. symmetric distribution for the discriminator, produces non-exponentially-vanishing loss and gradients
[
63
]
. One might also ask whether the (classical) discriminator has non-vanishing gradients – but this has extensively been studied in classical machine learning, and using a leaky-ReLU activation function is known to mitigate the problem significantly
[
87
]
.

As already emphasized, note that the absence of barren plateaus is a necessary condition for trainability, but in no way guarantees that training will lead to an optimal solution. Nonetheless, the fact that Pauli terms contribute independently (Theorem
1
) proves that local approximations of the loss function can only harm the training process – by increasing initial concentration of gradients and potentially leading to undesirable local minima. Moreover, while initially exponentially small, global terms may kick in over the course of training, as displayed numerically in the following section.

3.3.4
Limitations

It is useful to notice that our 16-qubit experiments consistently led to ‘spiky’ generator distributions, as shown in Figure
4
(b). Indeed, while the ground distribution is a
continuous
Gaussian mixture, the generator encodes this into amplitudes

x
ranges over a
discrete
set
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Without introducing an inductive bias that forces ‘adjacent’ inputs
x
to have similar amplitudes, the generator is unfortunately blind to the underlying continuity, attempting to match the Gaussian mixture as if it were discrete. This may explain the resulting spikiness, and calls for further work on inductive biases and the optimization landscape of qGANs more generally.

4
Conclusion and Outlook

This paper extends prior work on barren plateaus, by lifting
t
-design assumptions and providing tight upper and lower bounds on gradient concentration for a large class of parameterized quantum circuits, and arbitrary observables (Theorem
1
). Notably, we also present a method for classically estimating these bounds and the actual loss (gradient) variance efficiently, providing a practical tool for studying variational loss landscapes and designing ansätze.
In particular, our result emphasizes that gradient concentration is not exclusively determined by observable locality, circuit depth, and entanglement, but instead, strongly relies on the observable-ansatz
interaction
, formulated in terms of light-cones. One fortunate consequence is that
mixed
observables do
not
necessarily induce barren plateaus (Corollary
1
), and even
global
observables may have non-vanishing gradients if the circuit is chosen wisely.
Interesting directions for future work would be to extend our proof techniques to additional circuit classes, such as Hamiltonian variational ansätze, as well as investigate the simulability of our circuit class, following
[
94
]
.

Moreover, we leverage our results to show that qGANs are an auspicious class of GQML algorithms by proving that the corresponding observable is mixed under reasonable conditions, and hence, does not induce barren plateaus (Theorem
2
and Corollary
2
).
We illustrate these theoretical results with numerical experiments, where we train a qGAN to learn Gaussian mixtures with up to
16
qubits, and provide evidence that global contributions kick in during training.
We nonetheless emphasise that non-vanishing gradients, though necessary, are not a sufficient condition in the over-arching goal of reaching a global (or even a local) minimum of the loss.
In particular, while our work rules out barren plateaus for qGANs with shallow generators, future work calls for a deeper understanding of VQA optimization landscapes, and the potential introduction of an inductive bias to help qGANs efficiently learn continuous distributions, as suggested by our
16
-qubit experiments.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that our results highlight a central difference between quantum barren plateaus and the classical vanishing gradient problem for neural networks, where it is only the
depth
of the circuit which causes gradients to vanish, by composition of activation functions across layers
[
87
,
95
]
. Instead, Theorem
1
shows that quantum gradient concentration depends on how many qubits are ‘hit’ by the observable (the
width
of the light-cone), and thus, whether the sphere on which they live induces a concentration of measure phenomenon
[
1
]
. Depth of the circuit, in itself,
does not
induce vanishing gradients – it only does so indirectly because each layer typically entangles more and more qubits together, thus broadening the light-cone. Another way of making this point is that high
local
expressivity (high-depth on a local part of the circuit) does not induce barren plateaus – whereas the analogous situation for classical neural networks
would
.

Proof of Theorem
1

Theorem
1
.

For any
m
-parameter circuit
U(\theta)\in\mathcal{U}
, any observable
H=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}
, and any mixed state
\rho
, each Pauli term makes an independent contribution to the loss and gradient variances:

\displaystyle\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{2}\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\,,

\displaystyle\Var_{\theta}\big[\nabla\mathcal{L}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{2}\Var_{\theta}\big[\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\,.

Moreover, writing
\mathcal{D}
for the discrete uniform distribution over
\{0,\pi/2\}^{m}
, each non-trivial term (
\alpha\neq 0
) can be reduced to a
discrete
expectation, namely,

\norm{\, \Var_\T\big[ \nabla\cL_\al\big] \,}_{\infty}=\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]=\E_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\alpha}\big]\,.

Finally, for product mixed states
\rho
(generalized in Part
(G)
of the proof), the loss variance is bounded by

\displaystyle\Omega(\rho)\,\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}}\ \right]\leq\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\leq\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}}\ \right]\,,

and there are circuit-observable pairs that saturate these inequalities. In particular, combined with the previous equation, at least one partial derivative
\partial_{k}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
satisfies the lower bound, and all of them satisfy the upper bound.

Proof.

To avoid overloading indices, we make the following notational changes. We write

P(\theta)\coloneqq R_{P}(\theta)=\exp(-iP\T/2)

for the rotation gate induced by a Pauli string
P
and a parameter
\theta
. By introducing identity Clifford gates
W_{k}
in order to separate each parameterized rotation into its own layer
R_{k}
, and adjusting the value of
K
accordingly, we rewrite the circuit with relabelled parameters
{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta,\phi,\omega)
as

U({\bm{\theta}})=A_{K}(\theta)B(\phi)C(\omega)\,,

(4)

where

A_{K}(\theta)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}W_{k}P_{k}(\theta_{k})\qquad,\qquad B(\phi)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{\mu_{i}}(\phi_{i})\qquad,\qquad C(\omega)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{\nu_{i}}(\omega_{i})\,,

so that
\sigma_{\mu_{i}},\sigma_{\nu_{i}}
are the two layers of orthogonal single-qubit rotations, i.e.
\mu,\nu\in\{1,2,3\}^{n}
and
\mu_{i}\neq\nu_{i}
for each
i
. We will write
\E
without subscript to denote the expectation over all parameters
\E_{{\bm{\theta}}}
. To begin, recall that decomposing the observable
H=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}
leads to a decomposition of the loss

\mathcal{L}=\Tr\left(U^{\dagger}HU\rho\right)=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\Tr\left(\rho U^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}U\right)\eqqcolon c_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\,,

and correspondingly, a decomposition of partial derivatives
\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}=c_{\alpha}\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
for each parameter
\tau\in\{\theta,\phi,\omega\}
. Finally, we also decompose the initial state
\rho=\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}
into the Pauli basis.

(A) Vanishing expectation.
In order to prove that Pauli terms contribute independently to loss and gradient
variances
, we first prove that loss and gradient
expectations
vanish. To begin, by linearity, notice that the expectations

\displaystyle\E\big[\mathcal{L}\big]=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\E\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\E\big[\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}\big]=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\E\big[\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]

decompose across Pauli strings. For all
\alpha\neq 0
, we prove that
\E\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]=\E\big[\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]=0
by induction on the number of layers
K
. For the base case (
K=0
), the loss splits across qubits (indexed by
i
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}\Tr\left(U^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}UP_{\lambda}\right)=\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}\prod_{i}\Tr\big(\sig_{\nu_i}(-\TTT_i) \sig_{\mu_i}(-\TT_i) \sig_{\al_i} \sig_{\mu_i}(\TT_i) \sig_{\nu_i}(\TTT_i) \sig_{\la_i} \big)\eqqcolon\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}\prod_{i}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha\lambda}^{i}\,.

(5)

By assumption that
\alpha\neq 0
, there exists
j
such that
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
. If
\alpha_{j}\neq\mu_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha\lambda}^{j}

\displaystyle=\Tr\big(\sig_{\nu_j}(-\TTT_j) \sig_{\mu_j}(-2\TT_j) \sig_{\al_j} \sig_{\nu_j}(\TTT_j) \sig_{\la_j} \big)

\displaystyle=\cos(\TT_j)\Tr\big(\sig_{\nu_j}(-\TTT_j) \sig_{\al_j} \sig_{\nu_j}(\TTT_j) \sig_{\la_j} \big)+i\sin(\TT_j)\Tr\big(\sig_{\nu_j}(-\TTT_j) \sig_{\mu_j} \sig_{\al_j} \sig_{\nu_j}(\TTT_j) \sig_{\la_j} \big)\,.

Both terms vanish on expectation over
\phi_{j}
. On the other hand, if
\alpha_{j}=\mu_{j}
, orthogonality of
\mu,\nu

\alpha_{j}\neq\nu_{j}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha\lambda}^{j}=\Tr\big(\sig_{\nu_j}(-2\TTT_j) \sig_{\al_j} \sig_{\la_j} \big)=\cos(\TTT_j)\Tr\big( \sig_{\al_j} \sig_{\la_j} \big)+i\sin(\TTT_j)\Tr\big( \sig_{\nu_j} \sig_{\al_j} \sig_{\la_j} \big)\,.

This also vanishes on expectation over
\omega_{j}
. By independence between parameters, we obtain
\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\right]=0
, concluding the base case. For the induction step, writing
U_{K}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=\Tr\left(U_{K}^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}U_{K}\rho\right)=\Tr\left(U_{K-1}^{\dagger}P_{K}(-\theta_{K})W_{K}^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}W_{K}P_{K}(\theta_{K})U_{K-1}\rho\right)\,.

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\right]=\Tr\left(U_{K-1}^{\dagger}P_{\gamma}U_{K-1}\rho\right)=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=\cos\theta_{K}\Tr\left(U_{K-1}^{\dagger}P_{\gamma}U_{K-1}\rho\right)+i\sin\theta_{K}\Tr\left(U_{K-1}^{\dagger}P_{K}P_{\gamma}U_{K-1}\rho\right)\,,

which vanishes on expectation over
\theta_{K}

\E\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]=0

\alpha\neq 0

\E\big[\mathcal{L}\big]=c_{0}

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\right]=\E\left[\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\right]=0

\alpha_{j}\neq\beta_{j}

\alpha_{j}=0

. We therefore obtain

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha\lambda}^{j}\mathcal{L}_{\beta\lambda^{\prime}}^{j}\right]=\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha\lambda}^{j}\E_{\phi_{j}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\beta\lambda^{\prime}}^{j}\right]\right]=0\,.

It remains only to deal with the final case, where
\alpha_{j}\neq\beta_{j}\neq\mu_{j}
. By Lemma
1
, integrating over
\phi_{j}
yields

\displaystyle\E_{\phi_{j}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha\lambda}^{j}\mathcal{L}_{\beta\lambda^{\prime}}^{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(\sigma_{\nu_{j}}(-\omega_{j})\sigma_{\alpha_{j}}\sigma_{\nu_{j}}(\omega_{j})\sigma_{\lambda_{j}}\right)\Tr\left(\sigma_{\nu_{j}}(-\omega_{j})\sigma_{\beta_{j}}\sigma_{\nu_{j}}(\omega_{j})\sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(\sigma_{\nu_{j}}(-\omega_{j})i\sigma_{\mu_{j}}\sigma_{\alpha_{j}}\sigma_{\nu_{j}}(\omega_{j})\sigma_{\lambda_{j}}\right)\Tr\left(\sigma_{\nu_{j}}(-\omega_{j})i\sigma_{\mu_{j}}\sigma_{\beta_{j}}\sigma_{\nu_{j}}(\omega_{j})\sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}_{j}}\right)\,.

Now
\alpha_{j}\neq\beta_{j}\neq\mu_{j}
combined with
\mu_{j}\neq\nu_{j}
implies (wlog) that
\alpha_{j}=\nu_{j}
and
\beta_{j}\neq\nu_{j}
. The first term thus reduces to

\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(\sigma_{\alpha_{j}}\sigma_{\lambda_{j}}\right)\Big(\cos(\TTT_j)\Tr\left(\sigma_{\beta_{j}}\sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}_{j}}\right)+i\sin(\TTT_j)\Tr\left(\sigma_{\nu_{j}}\sigma_{\beta_{j}}\sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}_{j}}\right)\Big)\,,

which vanishes on expectation over
\omega_{j}
. Similarly, the second term vanishes because
\alpha_{j}\neq\beta_{j}\neq\mu_{j}
combined with
\mu_{j}\neq\nu_{j}
implies
i\sigma_{\mu_{j}}\sigma_{\alpha_{j}}\neq\pm\sigma_{\nu_{j}}
and
i\sigma_{\mu_{j}}\sigma_{\beta_{j}}=\pm\sigma_{\nu_{j}}
. We thus conclude
\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha\lambda}^{j}\mathcal{L}_{\beta\lambda^{\prime}}^{j}\right]=0
for all
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}
and hence
\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\right]=0
, completing the base case. For the induction step, as in the previous section, let
U_{K}
be a circuit with
K\geq 1
layers and let
\gamma,\delta
be such that
W_{K}^{\dagger}P_{\alpha/\beta}W_{K}=P_{\gamma/\delta}
. If
P_{K}
commutes with
P_{\gamma}
and
P_{\delta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}=\Tr(U_{K-1}^\dagger P_\ga U_{K-1}\rho)\Tr(U_{K-1}^\dagger P_\del U_{K-1} \rho)\,,

which vanishes by the induction hypothesis. (Note that orthogonality of
P_{\alpha},P_{\beta}
implies orthogonality of
P_{\gamma},P_{\delta}
.) If
P_{K}
commutes with
P_{\gamma}
but not with
P_{\delta}
(without loss of generality), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}=\cos\theta_{K}\Tr( U_{K-1}^\dagger P_\ga U_{K-1} \rho)\Tr(U_{K-1}^\dagger P_\del U_{K-1} \rho)+\sin\theta_{K}\Tr(U_{K-1}^\dagger P_\ga U_{K-1} \rho)\Tr(U_{K-1}^\dagger i P_K P_\del U_{K-1}\rho)\,,

which vanishes on expectation over
\theta_{K}
. Finally, if
P_{K}
commutes with neither, we obtain, by Lemma
1
,

\displaystyle\E_{\theta_{K}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(U_{K-1}^\dagger P_\ga U_{K-1} \rho)\Tr( U_{K-1}^\dagger P_\del U_{K-1} \rho)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Tr(U_{K-1}^\dagger iP_K P_\ga U_{K-1} \rho)\Tr(U_{K-1}^\dagger i P_K P_\del U_{K-1} \rho)\,.

Since
P_{\gamma}
and
P_{\delta}
are orthogonal,
iP_{K}P_{\gamma}
and
iP_{K}P_{\delta}
are also orthogonal, so both terms vanish by the induction hypothesis. This completes the induction, giving
\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\right]=0
. The proof for partial derivatives similarly follows by induction, or can be obtained using the parameter-shift rule

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}+e_{\tau}\pi/2)-\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}-e_{\tau}\pi/2)}{2}\,,

where
e_{\tau}
is the unit vector along the
\tau
direction, along with the standard trigonometric identities identities

\cos(x \pm\pi/2)=\mp\sin(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sin(x \pm\pi/2)=\pm\cos(x)\,.

From the vanishing expectations obtained in part
(A)
, we can now decompose the variance of the loss as

\Var\big[\mathcal{L}\big]=\E\Big[\left(\mathcal{L}-c_{0}\right)^{2}\Big]=\E\left[\bigg(\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}c_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\bigg)^{2}\right]=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\neq 0}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\right]=\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}c_{\alpha}^{2}\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{2}\Var\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\,.

Similarly, for gradients, we have, for each parameter
\tau
,

\Var\big[\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}\big]=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{2}\E\left[\left(\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{2}\Var\big[\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]

In other words, we have proven that each Pauli term
P_{\alpha}
contributes independently to loss and gradient variances.

(C) Variance of the gradients.
Note that a lower bound could be produced by invoking the converse of
[
63
, Theorem 1]
, but the bound would be loose. Instead, we directly show that

\norm{\, \Var_\T\big[ \nabla\cL_\al\big] \,}_{\infty}\coloneqq\max_{\tau}\Var\big[\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]=\Var\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\,.

For convenience, and by the equivalence obtained in part
(B)
above, we work with squared-expectations rather than variances. To begin, for any parameter
\tau
, we can invoke the parameter-shift rule to obtain

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big(\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}}+e_{\tau}\pi/2)^{2}\right]+\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}}-e_{\tau}\pi/2)^{2}\right]-2\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}}+e_{\tau}\pi/2)\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}}-e_{\tau}\pi/2)\right]\Big)\,.

The first two terms can be equated with
\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}})^{2}\right]
by changing variables
{\bm{\theta}}\to{\bm{\theta}}\pm e_{\tau}\pi/2
and recalling that
\mathcal{L}
is periodic over each parameter with period
2\pi
, implying invariance over the uniform distribution. The third term can also be upper-bounded by
\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}})^{2}\right]
by following the same induction leading to Equation (
6
). We thus obtain

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\Big(\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}})^{2}\right]+\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}})^{2}\right]+2\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}})^{2}\right]\Big)=\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}})^{2}\right]

for all
\tau
, and therefore
\max_{\tau}\E\left[\left(\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]
. To prove equality, consider any parameter
\tau
whose index we denote by
k
, and decompose
U=U_{\tau_{+}}U_{\tau_{-}}
, where
U_{\tau_{-}}
and
U_{\tau_{+}}
are the left- and right-hand sides of the parameterized gate
P_{k}(\tau)
, where
U_{\tau_{-}}
includes the gate itself. In particular, partial derivatives can conveniently be written as

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=\frac{i}{2}\Tr(U_{\tau_-}^\dagger\Big[ P_k, U_{\tau_+}^\dagger P_\al U_{\tau_+} \Big] U_{\tau_-} \rho)\,.

We now prove that equality is reached for the last (right-most) parameter
\tau
for which
P_{k}(\theta)
does not commute with
U_{\tau_{+}}^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}U_{\tau_{+}}
. (For example, if the circuit ends with a parameterized
R_{Z}
gate and
P_{\alpha}=X/Y
on the corresponding qubit line,
\tau
will be this final parameter. However, if
P_{\alpha}=I/Z
, then the final gate will have no impact and
\tau
will be an earlier parameter.) Since all parameters beyond
P_{k}(\tau)
are now defined to have no bearing on the loss, they can be set to
0
. By defining the Pauli string
P_{\gamma}\coloneqq U_{\tau_{+}}^{\dagger}(0)P_{\alpha}U_{\tau_{+}}(0)
, thanks to each
W_{k}
being Clifford, the loss reduces to

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=\Tr(U_{\tau_-}^\dagger P_\ga U_{\tau_-} \rho)\,.

Similarly, the partial derivative with respect to
\tau
reduces to

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=\frac{i}{2}\Tr(U_{\tau_-}^\dagger\Big[ P_k, P_\ga\Big] U_{\tau_-} \rho)\,.

Since
P_{k}
is defined not to commute with
P_{\gamma}
, we now have

\frac{i}{2}\big[P_{k},P_{\gamma}\big]=iP_{k}P_{\gamma}=P_{k}(-\pi/2)P_{\gamma}P_{k}(\pi/2)\,,

which implies that the partial derivative with respect to
\tau
is simply a shifted version of the loss:

\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}})=\Tr(U_{\tau_-}^\dagger(\bT-e_\tau\pi/2) P_\ga U_{\tau_-}(\bT+e_\tau\pi/2) \rho)=\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}({\bm{\theta}}+e_{\tau}\pi/2)\,.

Finally, by changing variables
{\bm{\theta}}\to{\bm{\theta}}+e_{\tau}\pi/2
and invoking the periodicity of
\mathcal{L}
once more, we obtain

\E\big[\big(\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big)^{2}\big]=\E\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\big]

for this particular parameter
\tau
, and therefore
\max_{\tau}\E\left[\left(\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right]=\E\left[\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\alpha}\right]
. Rewriting this as a variance, we conclude

\norm{\, \Var\big[ \nabla\cL_\al\big] \,}_{\infty}=\max_{\tau}\Var\big[\partial_{\tau}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]=\Var\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\,.

(D) Variance of the loss (exact).
To begin, let us write
\mathcal{D}
for the discrete uniform distribution over
\{0,\pi/2\}^{m}
. We prove, by induction on the number of parameters
m
, that

\E_{\theta}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]=\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{m}\sum_{\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{m}}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}(\theta)\,,

(6)

The base case holds trivially since there are no parameters to integrate over. For the induction step, let
U_{m}
be a circuit with
m\geq 1
parameters, and
\theta^{\prime}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m})
. Using the circuit structure from Definition
1
, we can write

U_{m}(\theta)=U_{m-1}(\theta^{\prime})A_{m}(\theta_{m})\coloneqq U_{m-1}(\theta^{\prime})W_{m}P_{m}(\theta_{m})\,,

where
W_{m}
is a Clifford gate (possibly the identity), and
P_{m}
is an arbitrary Pauli string.
Now let
\gamma
be such that
P_{\gamma}=W_{m}^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}W_{m}
. If
P_{m}
commutes with
P_{\gamma}
, we have
P_{m}(\theta)P_{\gamma}=P_{\gamma}P_{m}(\theta)
and in particular,

A_{m}^{\dagger}(0)P_{\alpha}A_{m}(0)+A_{m}^{\dagger}(\pi/2)P_{\alpha}A_{m}(\pi/2)=2P_{\gamma}\,.

This implies that we can rewrite

\displaystyle\E_{\theta}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[\Tr( U_{m-1}^\dagger P_\ga U_{m-1} \rho)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\theta_{m}\in\{0,\pi/2\}}\E_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[\Tr( U_{m-1}^\dagger A_m^\dagger(\T_m) P_\al A_m(\T_m) U_{m-1} \rho)^{2}\right]\,.

Similarly, if
P_{m}
does not commute with
P_{\gamma}
, we have
P_{m}(\theta)P_{\gamma}=P_{\gamma}P_{m}(-\theta)
and thus

A_{m}^{\dagger}(0)P_{\alpha}A_{m}(0)+A_{m}^{\dagger}(\pi/2)P_{\alpha}A_{m}(\pi/2)=P_{\gamma}+P_{m}(-\pi)P_{\gamma}=P_{\gamma}+iP_{m}P_{\gamma}\,.

Using Lemma
1
to integrate over
\theta_{m}
, we can again rewrite the expectation in the same form:

\displaystyle\E_{\theta}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\theta}\left[\Tr( U_{m-1}^\dagger P_m(-2\T_m) P_\ga U_{m-1} \rho)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\theta_{m}}\left[\cos^{2}\theta_{m}\right]\E_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[\Tr( U_{m-1}^\dagger P_\ga U_{m-1} \rho)^{2}\right]+\E_{\theta_{m}}\left[\sin^{2}\theta_{m}\right]\E_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[\Tr( U_{m-1}^\dagger i P_m P_\ga U_{m-1} \rho)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\theta_{m}\in\{0,\pi/2\}}\E_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[\Tr( U_{m-1}^\dagger A_m^\dagger(\T_m) P_\al A_m(\T_m) U_{m-1} \rho)^{2}\right]\,.

By the induction hypothesis, since
A_{m}^{\dagger}(\theta_{m})P_{\alpha}A_{m}(\theta_{m})m
is a Pauli string for each
\theta_{m}\in\{0,\pi/2\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\E_{\theta}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\theta_{m}\in\{0,\pi/2\}}\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{m-1}}\Tr( U_{m-1}^\dagger(\T') A_m^\dagger(\T_m) P_\al A_m(\T_m) U_{m-1}(\T') \rho)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{m}\sum_{\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{m}}\Tr( U_m^\dagger(\T) P_\al U_m(\T) \rho)^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{m}\sum_{\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{m}}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}(\theta,\phi,\omega)\,.

This concludes the induction.

(E) Variance of the loss (bounds).
Recall, from Equation (
4
), that we can decompose the circuit as
U_{K}=A_{K}(\theta)B(\phi)C(\omega)
, with
A_{K}(\theta)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}W_{k}P_{k}(\theta_{k})
. In particular, for any fixed
\phi,\omega
, Equation (
6
) gives

\E_{\theta}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}(\theta,\phi,\omega)\right]=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\sum_{\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{K}}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}(\theta,\phi,\omega)\,,

(7)

where
K=m-2n
. Now notice that
P_{k}(\theta_{k})
are Clifford gates for all
\theta_{k}\in\{0,\pi/2\}
, implying that

P_{\alpha(\theta)}\coloneqq A_{K}^{\dagger}(\theta)P_{\alpha}A_{K}(\theta)

is a Pauli string for all
\theta\sim\mathcal{D}
, whose index we denote by
\alpha(\theta)
. Assuming
\rho=\otimes\rho_{i}
is a product mixed state, Equation (
7
) now decomposes into the following product:

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\sum_{\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{K}}\ \prod_{i}\ \E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]

(8)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\sum_{\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{K}}\ \prod_{i}\ \E\left[\Tr(\sig_{\nu_i}(-\TTT_i) \sig_{\mu_i}(-\TT_i) \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \sig_{\mu_i}(\TT_i) \sig_{\nu_i}(\TTT_i) \rho_i)^{2}\right]\,.

If
\alpha_{i}(\theta)=\mu_{i}
, the
i
th term reduces to

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]=\E\left[\Tr(\sig_{\nu_i}(-\TTT_i) \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \sig_{\nu_i}(\TTT_i) \rho_i)^{2}\right]\,,

and, by assumption that
\alpha_{i}(\theta)=\mu_{i}\neq\nu_{i}
, integrating over
\omega_{i}
using Lemma
1
yields

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\sig_{\al_i(\T)} \rho_i)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Tr(i \sig_{\nu_i} \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \rho_i)^{2}\,.

(9)

In particular, by decomposing
\rho_{i}
into the Pauli basis, we can notice that

\sum_{j\neq 0}\Tr(\sig_j \rho_i)^{2}=2\Tr(\rho^2_i)-1\leq 1\,,

(10)

and hence,

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\neq 0}\Tr(\sig_j \rho_i)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\,.

Otherwise, if
\alpha_{i}(\theta)\neq 0,\mu_{i}
, integrating over
\phi_{i}
using Lemma
1
yields

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\E_{\omega_{i}}\left[\Tr(\sig_{\nu_i}(-\TTT_i) \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \sig_{\nu_i}(\TTT_i) \rho_i)^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\E_{\omega_{i}}\left[\Tr(\sig_{\nu_i}(-\TTT_i) i \sig_{\mu_i} \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \sig_{\nu_i}(\TTT_i) \rho_i)^{2}\right]\,.

Now notice that
\alpha_{i}(\theta)\neq\mu_{i}\neq\nu_{i}
implies
\sigma_{\alpha_{i}(\theta)}=\sigma_{\nu_{i}}
and
i\sigma_{\mu_{i}}\sigma_{\alpha_{i}}\neq\sigma_{\nu_{i}}
or vice-versa. In the former case (wlog), the first term simplifies without integration, while integrating over
\omega_{i}
in the second term yields

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr( \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \rho_i)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Tr(i \sig_{\mu_i} \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \rho_i)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Tr(i \sig_{\nu_i} i \sig_{\mu_i} \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \rho_i)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr( \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \rho_i)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Tr(i \sig_{\mu_i} \sig_{\al_i(\T)} \rho_i)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Tr(\sig_{\mu_i} \rho_i)^{2}\,.

(11)

Again, the fact that all three Pauli terms are distinct implies

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\neq 0}\Tr(\sig_j \rho_i)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\,.

We thus obtain
\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all
\alpha_{i}(\theta)\neq 0
, and taking the product over all qubits yields

\prod_{i}\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al(\T)}}

in the usual
L_{0}
pseudo-norm
\absolutevalue{\al}\coloneqq\absolutevalue{ \{ \al_i \neq 0 \} }
. Now recall that the light-cone
\Delta_{\alpha}^{\bm{\theta}}
denotes the number of qubits on which
U^{\dagger}({\bm{\theta}})P_{\alpha}U({\bm{\theta}})
acts non-trivially. Since the first two layers
B(\phi),C(\omega)
only contain single-qubit rotations, which cannot alter light-cones, this is equivalent to defining
\Delta_{\alpha}^{\bm{\theta}}={\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}
as the number of qubits on which
A_{K}^{\dagger}(\theta)P_{\alpha}A_{K}(\theta)
acts non-trivially.
In particular, for all
\theta\sim\mathcal{D}
, we have

P_{\alpha(\theta)}\coloneqq A_{K}^{\dagger}(\theta)P_{\alpha}A_{K}(\theta)

and thus
{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}=\absolutevalue{\al(\T)}
. Re-invoking Equation (
8
), we thus conclude

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\sum_{\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{K}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}=\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\ \right]\,.

(12)

For the lower bound, let us define the orthogonality between
\rho
and the first layer of rotations
\otimes_{i}R_{\sigma_{\nu_{i}}}
as

\Omega(\rho)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}\\
\forall i\,:\,\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}\end{subarray}}\Tr(P_\la\rho)^{2}\,.

Now notice that for any
\alpha(\theta)\neq 0
, equations (
9
) and (
11
) both yield a lower bound

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}\Tr(\sig_{\be_i} \rho_i)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Tr(\sig_{\ga_i} \rho_i)^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}}\Tr(\sig_{\la_i} \rho_i)^{2}\,,

where
\beta_{i},\gamma_{i}
are indices such that
\beta_{i}\neq\gamma_{i}\neq\nu_{i}\neq 0
.

On the other hand, if
\alpha_{i}(\theta)=0
, we decompose
\rho_{i}=\sum_{j}c_{ij}\sigma_{j}
into the Pauli basis and notice that
\Tr(\rho_i)=1=2c_{0}
implies

\Tr(\rho_i^2)\geq 2\Tr(\rho_i^2)-1=\sum_{\lambda_{i}\neq 0}\Tr(\sig_{\la_i} \rho_i)^{2}\geq\sum_{\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}}\Tr(\sig_{\la_i} \rho_i)^{2}\,.

Invoking Equation (
10
), we therefore have

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{i}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]=\Tr(\rho_i)^{2}\geq\sum_{\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}}\Tr(\sig_{\la_i} \rho_i)^{2}\,.

Taking the product over all qubits, we obtain

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}(\theta,\phi,\omega)^{2}\right]\geq\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al(\T)}}\,\prod_{i}\,\sum_{\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}}\Tr(\sig_{\la_i} \rho_i)^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\Omega(\rho)

and thus, using Equation (
8
) once more,

\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\sum_{\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{K}}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\Omega(\rho)=\Omega(\rho)\,\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\ \right]\,.

Rewriting our upper and lower bounds in terms of variances, we obtain, for any
\alpha\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\Omega(\rho)\,\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}}\ \right]\leq\Var\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\leq\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}}\ \right]\,.

In the special case where
\rho=\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}
is the zero initial state and the first rotations are not
Z
-rotations, note that
\rho
is pure and fully orthogonal to the first layer, so that
\Omega(\rho)=1
.

(F) Tightness of bounds.
Our concentration bounds can be attained by first setting
K=0
, where the circuit is no more than two layers of orthogonal single-qubit rotations:

U(\phi,\omega)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{\mu_{i}}(\phi_{i})\sigma_{\nu_{i}}(\omega_{i})\,.

In this case, single-qubit rotations do not alter light-cones, hence
{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}=\absolutevalue{\al}
for all
\theta\sim\mathcal{D}
and the bounds reduce to

\displaystyle\Omega(\rho)\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al}}\leq\Var\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al}}\,.

The upper bound is now reached by pairing this circuit with any Pauli string
P_{\alpha}
such that
\alpha_{i}=\mu_{i}
for all
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
. Similarly, the lower bound is reached whenever
\rho
is a pure product state and
\alpha_{i}\neq\mu_{i}
for all
i
.

(G) Extension to arbitrary mixed states.
We extend our result to arbitrary initial states
\rho
, by decomposing
\rho=\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}
into the Pauli basis and defining a generalized
\alpha
-orthogonality measure
\Omega(\rho,\alpha)
as

\Omega(\rho,\alpha)\coloneqq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda(\alpha)}\Tr\big(P_\la\rho\big)^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda(\alpha)}4^{n}d_{\lambda}^{2}\,,

where

\Lambda(\alpha)=\{\lambda\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}\mid\lambda_{i}=0\ \ \forall\alpha_{i}=0\quad\land\quad\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}\ \ \forall\alpha_{i}=\mu_{i}\quad\land\quad\lambda_{i}\neq 0\ \ \text{otherwise}\}\,.

Intuitively,
\Omega(\rho,\alpha)
measures the ‘portion’ of
\rho
which is orthogonal to the first layer, on each qubit line where
P_{\alpha}
does not commute with the second layer (namely
\alpha_{i}\neq 0,\mu_{i}
), by summing over the respective coefficients
d_{\lambda}^{2}
.
We generalize our bounds by first proving that for any mixed state
\rho
, and any
\alpha\neq 0
,

\Omega(\rho,\alpha)\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al}}\ \leq\ \E_{\phi,\omega}\left[\Tr(C^\dagger B^\dagger P_\al B C \rho)^{2}\right]\ \leq\ \Omega(\rho,\alpha)\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al}}\,.

To begin, we let
r=\absolutevalue{ \{ \al_i \neq 0, \mu_i \}}
be the number of qubit lines on which
P_{\alpha}
does not commute with the second layer, and integrate over corresponding parameters
\phi_{i}
using Equation (
6
) to obtain

\E_{\phi,\omega}\left[\Tr\left(C^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}BC\rho\right)^{2}\right]=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{r}\sum_{\phi\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{r}}\E_{\omega}\left[\Tr\left(C^{\dagger}P_{\alpha(\phi)}C\rho\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(13)

where
\alpha(\phi)
is the index of the Pauli string

P_{\alpha(\phi)}=\bigotimes_{\alpha_{i}=0,\mu_{i}}\sigma_{\alpha_{i}}\bigotimes_{\alpha_{i}\neq 0,\mu_{i}}\sigma_{\mu_{i}}(-\phi_{i})\sigma_{\alpha_{i}}\sigma_{\mu_{i}}(\phi_{i})\,.

Let us now fix any
\beta\coloneqq\alpha(\phi)
. As above, we let
t=\absolutevalue{ \{\be_i \neq 0, \nu_i\} }
be the number of qubit lines on which
P_{\beta}
does not commute with the first layer, and define
\beta(\omega)
as the index of the Pauli string

P_{\beta}=\bigotimes_{\beta_{i}=0,\nu_{i}}\sigma_{\beta_{i}}\bigotimes_{\beta_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}}\sigma_{\nu_{i}}(-\omega_{i})\sigma_{\beta_{i}}\sigma_{\nu_{i}}(\omega_{i})

for
\omega\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{t}
.
Similarly, we now integrate over parameters
\omega_{i}
using Equation (
6
) to obtain

\displaystyle\E_{\omega}\left[\Tr(C^\dagger P_{\be} C \rho)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t}\sum_{\omega\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{t}}\left(\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}\Tr( P_\be(\TTT) P_\la)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t}\sum_{\omega\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{t}}\left(\sum_{\lambda}2^{n}d_{\lambda}\delta(\beta(\omega)=\lambda)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t}\sum_{\omega\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{t}}\sum_{\lambda}4^{n}d_{\lambda}^{2}\delta(\beta(\omega)=\lambda)\,.

In order to pick out the non-zero coefficients in this sum over
\omega
, we notice that
\beta_{i}(0)\neq\beta_{i}(\pi/2)\neq\nu_{i}\neq 0
for all
\beta_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}
, and thus define a finer-grained notion of orthogonality

\Gamma(\beta)=\{\lambda\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}\mid\lambda_{i}=\beta_{i}\ \ \forall\beta_{i}=0,\nu_{i}\quad\land\quad\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}\ \ \text{otherwise}\}

to conclude

\displaystyle\E_{\omega}\left[\Tr(C^\dagger P_{\be} C \rho)^{2}\right]=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t}\sum_{\lambda\in\Gamma(\beta)}4^{n}d_{\lambda}^{2}\,.

Returning to Equation (
13
), and recalling that
r=\absolutevalue{\al}-\absolutevalue{\al_i = \mu_i}
and
t=\absolutevalue{\al}-\absolutevalue{\al(\phi)_i = \nu_i}
, we obtain

\E_{\phi,\omega}\left[\Tr\left(C^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}BC\rho\right)^{2}\right]=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2\absolutevalue{\al}-\absolutevalue{\al_i = \mu_i}}\sum_{\phi\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{r}}2^{\absolutevalue{\al_i(\phi) = \nu_i}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Gamma(\alpha(\phi))}4^{n}d_{\lambda}^{2}\,.

Now, by orthogonality of the first two layers, let
\eta_{i}
be the indices such that
0\neq\eta_{i}\neq\mu_{i}\neq\nu_{i}
for each
i
. In particular, for any
\alpha_{i}\neq 0,\mu_{i}
, we either have
\alpha_{i}=\nu_{i}
or
\alpha_{i}=\eta_{i}
, and in turn,
\sigma_{\mu_{i}}(-\phi_{i})\sigma_{\alpha_{i}}\sigma_{\mu_{i}}(\phi_{i})
must take values
\sigma_{\nu_{i}}
and
\sigma_{\eta_{i}}
for
\phi_{i}=0
and
\phi_{i}=\pi/2
respectively (relabeling parameters if necessary). By construction, we therefore have

\Gamma(\alpha(\phi))=\{\lambda\mid\lambda_{i}=0\ \ \forall\alpha_{i}=0\quad\land\quad\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}\ \ \forall\alpha_{i}=\mu_{i}\quad\land\quad\lambda_{i}=\nu_{i}\ \ \forall\phi_{i}=0\quad\land\quad\lambda_{i}\neq 0,\nu_{i}\ \ \forall\phi_{i}=\pi/2\}

and thus

\bigcup_{\phi\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{r}}\Gamma(\alpha(\phi))=\Lambda(\alpha)\,.

For the lower bound, this implies

\displaystyle\E_{\phi,\omega}\left[\Tr\left(C^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}BC\rho\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2\absolutevalue{\al}-\absolutevalue{\al_i = \mu_i}}\sum_{\phi\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{r}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Gamma(\alpha(\phi))}4^{n}d_{\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2\absolutevalue{\al}-\absolutevalue{\al_i = \mu_i}}\sum_{\phi\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{r}}\sum_{\lambda\in\cup\Gamma(\alpha(\phi))}4^{n}d_{\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al}}\Omega(\alpha,\rho)\,.

For the upper bound, we first use
\Gamma(\alpha(\phi))\subseteq\Lambda(\alpha)
and
\absolutevalue{\al_i(\phi) = \nu_i}=\absolutevalue{\phi_i = 0}
in order to obtain

\displaystyle\E_{\phi,\omega}\left[\Tr\left(C^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}BC\rho\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2\absolutevalue{\al}-\absolutevalue{\al_i = \mu_i}}\sum_{\phi\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{r}}2^{\absolutevalue{\phi_i = 0}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda(\alpha)}4^{n}d_{\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Omega(\rho,\alpha)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al}+r}\sum_{\phi\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{r}}2^{\absolutevalue{\phi_i = 0}}\,.

We now use a standard binomial expansion, along with the fact that
r\leq\absolutevalue{\al}
, to conclude

\displaystyle\E_{\phi,\omega}\left[\Tr\left(C^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}BC\rho\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Omega(\alpha,\rho)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al}+r}\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom{r}{k}2^{k}

\displaystyle=\Omega(\alpha,\rho)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al}+r}(2+1)^{r}

\displaystyle\leq\Omega(\alpha,\rho)\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\absolutevalue{\al}}\,.

Returning to the full loss using Equation (
7
), we have

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\right]=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\sum_{\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}^{K}}\E_{\phi,\omega}\left[\Tr(C^\dagger B^\dagger P_{\al(\T)} B C \rho)^{2}\right]\,,

allowing us to conclude, writing
\Omega_{\alpha}^{\theta}=\Omega(\rho,\alpha(\theta))
,

\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \Omega_{\alpha}^{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\ \right]\ \leq\ \Var\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\ \leq\ \E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \Omega_{\alpha}^{\theta}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\ \right]\,.

Again, the bounds can be estimated classically and efficiently, by Monte Carlo sampling
\theta\sim\mathcal{D}
, provided the coefficients
d_{\lambda}
of the initial state
\rho=\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}
are known.
∎

Corollary
1
.

Let
H
be any mixed observable on
n
qubits,
\rho
the zero initial state, and
U(\theta)
an EfficientSU2 circuit of logarithmic depth
O(\log n)
, pairwise entanglement, and rotation layers
(R_{Y},R_{Z})
. Then the corresponding loss does not suffer from barren plateaus, namely,

\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}\big]\in\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\poly(n)}\right)\,.

Proof (Sketch).

First note that
\rho=\outerproduct{0}{0}^{n}
is orthogonal to the first layer of
R_{Y}
rotations, implying
\Omega(\rho)=1
. Now let
U(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}E
be the EfficientSU2 circuit, where
R_{i}
is a product of single-qubit rotations and
E
is a pairwise entangling layer. Now recall that
{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}
is the number of qubits on which
U(\theta)^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}U(\theta)
acts non-trivially. Notice that
E
can at most extend the light-cone of a Pauli matrix
\sigma
by 2 qubits upwards and downwards, hence broadening the light-cone of a
k
-local string
P_{\alpha}
by at most 4 for each of the
k
non-identity terms. Meanwhile, the single-qubit rotations in
R_{i}
cannot alter light-cones, so it follows by induction on the depth
d
that

{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\leq k+4kd

for any
k
-local
P_{\alpha}
and any
\theta
. In particular, applying Theorem
1
, we obtain

\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\big]\ \geq\ \E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}}\ \right]\geq\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{k+4kd}\,.

Now by assumption,
H
contains a
k
-local string
P_{\alpha}
with
k\in O(1)
and
c_{\alpha}\in\Omega(1/\poly(n))
, which implies, for any logarithmic depth
d\in O(\log n)
, that
k+4kd\in O(\log n)
. Invoking the first part of Theorem
1
, the contribution of this term to the variance is independent, and vanishes at most polynomially vanishing in
n
, namely,

\Var_{\theta}\big[\mathcal{L}\big]\ \geq\ c_{\alpha}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{k+4kd}\in\ \ \Omega\left(\frac{1}{\poly(n)}\right)\,.\qed

Lemma 1
.

For any random variable
\theta
uniformly distributed over
[-\pi,\pi]
, we have

\E_{\theta}\left[\sin\theta\right]=\E_{\theta}\left[\cos\theta\right]=\E_{\theta}\left[\sin\theta\cos\theta\right]dx=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\E_{\theta}\left[\sin^{2}\theta\right]=\E_{\theta}\left[\cos^{2}\theta\right]=\frac{1}{2}\,.

Proof.

For the left-hand-side, we have

\E_{\theta}\left[\sin\theta\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin\theta d\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big[-\cos\theta\Big]_{-\pi}^{\pi}=0\,.

The analogous argument holds for
\cos\theta
and
\sin\theta\cos\theta=\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta
. For the right-hand-side, we invoke the standard identity
\cos^{2}\theta/\sin^{2}\theta=(1\pm\cos 2\theta)/2
to obtain

\E_{\theta}\left[\sin^{2}\theta\right]=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(1-\cos 2\theta)d\theta=\frac{1}{4\pi}\Big[\theta-\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta\Big]_{-\pi}^{\pi}=\frac{1}{2}\,.

The analogous argument holds for
\cos^{2}\theta
.

Appendix B
Light-Cones and Efficient Evaluation

efficiently and classically
, and numerically demonstrate how they compare favorably with existing bounds for local 2-designs.

Light-cones.
For a parameterized circuit
U(\theta)
and a Pauli string
P_{\alpha}
, recall that the light-cone
{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}=\Delta_{\alpha}(\theta)
is the number of qubits on which
U(\theta)^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}U(\theta)
acts non-trivially. In Figure
5
below, we visually illustrate the light-cones arising from different values of
\theta\sim\mathcal{D}
, for a specific circuit
U(\theta)
and Pauli observable
P_{\alpha}=ZII

\theta_{6}
. As shown in the figure, this parameter gives rise to light-cones of different sizes, namely,
\Delta_{\alpha}(0)=1

\Delta_{\alpha}(\pi/2)=3

1

\rho=\outerproduct{0}{0}

{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\text{full}}}
in their bounds, namely, the number of qubits on which
U(\theta)^{\dagger}P_{\alpha}U(\theta)

all
values of
\theta

Variance bounds for estimators.
For completeness, we end this appendix with an analysis of our estimators for the lower bound, as well as the variance of the loss itself. First, we look at the variance when estimating
g\coloneqq\E_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\alpha}\big]
via Monte Carlo sampling. As previously noted, since
U(\theta)
is a Clifford circuit for
\theta\sim\mathcal{D}
, the variable
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\alpha}
takes values in
\{0,1\}
over
\mathcal{D}
. In other words, estimating
g
corresponds to sampling from a Bernoulli variable
Y
with success probability
g
. The variance immediately follows as
\Var_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\alpha}\right]=g-g^{2}
.

Second, let us consider the lower bound
h\coloneqq\E_{\mathcal{D}}[\ \left(1/4\right)^{{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}}\ ]\leq g
. Since the light-cone
{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}
takes values in
\{0,\ldots,n\}
for
n
qubits, we approximate this by a Binomial distribution
X
with parameters
n,p
. Now the Moment Generating Function (MGF) of a (scaled) Binomial random variable
cX
, for a scaling factor
c\in\mathbb{R}
, is given by
[
96
]

\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\ e^{cXt}\ \right]=(1-p+pe^{ct})^{n}\,.

Thus, setting
t=1
and
c=\ln(1/4)
gives us
h=(1-3/4p)^{n}
.
Similarly, the variance can be written via the MGF as

\Var\left[(1/4)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\right]=(1-15/16p)^{n}-h^{2}\,.

For small
x
, we can approximate
e^{x}\approx 1+x
to write

1-15/16x\approx e^{-15/16x}=e^{(5/4)(-3/4x)}\approx(1-3/4x)^{5/4}\,.

Motivated by this relationship, one easily finds that for all
p\in[0,1]
and
n\geq 1
, we have

(1-15/16p)^{n}\leq(1-3/4p)^{5n/4}=h^{5/4}\,,

which implies
\Var[\,(1/4)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\,]\leq h^{5/4}-h^{2}
. This quantity is monotonically increasing for all
0\leq h\leq\frac{5^{4/3}}{16}\approx 0.53
, hence

\Var\left[(1/4)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\right]\leq g^{5/4}-g^{2}

for all
g\leq 0.53
. Thus, the lower bound has a variance of
O(g^{5/4})
while the loss variance has a variance of
O(g)
. For small
g
, this can lead to orders of magnitudes of reduction in the number of samples required to achieve a certain target accuracy. Analogously, the variance of the upper bound can be bounded by
\Var[\,(1/2)^{\Delta_{\alpha}^{\theta}}\,]\leq g^{3/2}-g^{2}
. However, the variance of the upper bound is not maximized when the expectation value equals
g
, so this scaling is only illustrative. Fig.

Appendix C
Justification of Circuit Assumptions

Here, we provide a justification of the circuit assumptions from Definition
1
, by providing explicit examples that violate Theorem
1
whenever any assumption is loosened – which may be informative in designing circuits skillfully.

Orthogonal layers.
As described in Section
3.1
, the absence of two initial orthogonal layers can induce uniformly zero gradients. A trivial example is to take a circuit consisting only of
R_{Y}
rotation gates, and any
Y
-observable, which thus commutes with the circuit entirely. While seemingly artificial, this also applies to the commonly used class of RealAmplitudes circuits
[
69
]
, which can produce a zero loss when paired with a 1-local
Y

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}HU

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}\Big(X\otimes Z\Big)\Big(R_{X}(\theta_{2})\otimes R_{X}(\theta_{3})\Big)\text{CNOT}\Big(R_{Y}(\theta_{0})\otimes R_{Y}(\theta_{1})\Big)

Appendix D
Equivalence Between
k
-Degree Polynomials and
k
-Local Diagonal Observables

In this appendix, we provide a bijection between binary polynomials
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
k
, and diagonal Hermitian observables
H
whose Pauli terms are at most (algebraically)
k
-local. This implies that generic polynomial binary optimization may include arbitrary global terms – but Theorem
1
guarantees that a fixed polynomial may
not
induce barren plateaus, provided it includes a monomial of small degree!

Context.
The goal of unconstrained black-box binary optimization
[
37
,
39
]
is to minimize a function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which we can evaluate, but whose closed-form expression is unknown. Importantly, this problem can be formulated as a VQA, with a diagonal observable given by
H=\sum_{x}f\left(x\right)\ket{x}\bra{x}
, where the sum ranges over bitstrings
x
of length
n
. To decompose this into the Pauli basis, write
Z_{\alpha}\coloneqq\bigotimes Z^{\alpha_{i}}
for any
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, with
Z^{0}=I
and
Z^{1}=Z
. Then,

H=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}Z_{\alpha}\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x}{(-1)}^{\alpha\cdot x}f(x)\right)Z_{\alpha}\,.

(14)

Since each coefficient
c_{\alpha}
comes with an exponentially small pre-factor
1/2^{n}
, it is not obvious whether this observable is ‘mainly’ local or global. The prevailing approach has been to check this empirically, as illustrated by
[
37
]
, who show that a number of application-specific observables can be fitted to
2
-local models with high fidelity.

However, Theorem
1
implies that whether or not the observable is ‘close’ to a local one is, in fact, beside the point: all that is necessary to exclude barren plateaus is for
some
local coefficient
c_{\alpha}
to be non-vanishing,
irrespective
of global terms. Moreover, these coefficients can easily (and simultaneously) be estimated by Monte Carlo sampling of the black box
f(x)
, whose convergence rate for
N
samples is
O(\sqrt{N})
. Crucially, this is
independent
from the number of qubits
n
. If at least one local coefficient is found to be non-vanishing, Corollary
1
readily guarantees initial trainability for typical shallow circuits. If not, this knowledge could significantly help us design the ansatz. For instance, if a global coefficient is found to be non-vanishing, one may add an appropriate entangling layer, acting as a basis transformation, in order to transform a global term to a local one.

A special case of this setting is when
f
is not a black box but an explicit polynomial
f(x)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}}c_{I}x_{I}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is a set of multi-indices,
c_{I}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
x_{I}=\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}
. A well-known instance is quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO)
[
97
]
, where
f
is required to be
quadratic
. In this case, the corresponding VQA formulation is given by a 2-local observable, which will not induce barren plateaus. In the following proposition, we generalize this correspondence by producing a bijective mapping between polynomials of degree
k
and
k
-local observables, implying that generic polynomial optimization may include arbitrary global terms. Thankfully, Theorem
1
guarantees that such observables will
not
induce barren plateaus provided they include a non-vanishing local term, which broadens the spectrum of applications from QUBO to arbitrary polynomial binary optimization.

Proposition 1
.

For fixed
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{k}
be the set of polynomials
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
k
, and let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the set of diagonal Hermitian matrices
H\in M_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C})
whose Pauli decomposition
H=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}Z_{\alpha}
satisfies
\absolutevalue{\al}\leq k
for all
c_{\alpha}\neq 0
. Then
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and
\mathcal{H}_{k}
are isomorphic. More specifically, the following map
T:\mathcal{F}_{k}\to\mathcal{H}_{k}
is bijective:

T(f)=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}f(x)\ket{x}\bra{x}\,.

Proof.

First notice that both sets are finite-dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{R}
, since the set of
diagonal
Hermitian matrices is in fact real. Writing
x_{\alpha}=\prod_{i}x^{\alpha_{i}}
as the monomial corresponding to any
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, a basis for
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is the set of monomials
\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\absolutevalue{\al}\leq k}
. Similarly, a basis for
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is the set of Pauli strings
\{P_{\alpha}\}_{\absolutevalue{\al}\leq k}
. It follows that
\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k}
are isomorphic, since finite-dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{R}
of equal dimension are unique up to isomorphism.

To prove that
T
is bijective, the only non-trivial step is to show that it is well-defined, i.e.,
T(f)\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
. Since
T
is linear, it suffices to show this holds for any monomial
f(x)=x_{\alpha}
with
\absolutevalue{\al}\leq k
. Using the decomposition of projectors into the Pauli basis from Equation (
14
), we have

T(x_{\alpha})=\sum_{\beta}c_{\beta}P_{\beta}\coloneqq\sum_{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x}(-1)^{\beta\cdot x}x_{\alpha}\right)P_{\beta}\,.

We must now prove that
c_{\beta}=0
for any
\absolutevalue{\be}>k
. For any such
\beta
, since
\absolutevalue{\al}\leq k
, we must have
\beta_{i}=1
and
\alpha_{i}=0
for some index
i
. Writing
x^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
for the bitstrings corresponding to
x,\alpha,\beta
with the
i
th term omitted, we find

c_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x^{\prime}}(-1)^{\beta^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime}}\sum_{x_{i}}(-1)^{x_{i}}x_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x^{\prime}}(-1)^{\beta^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime}}x^{\prime}_{\alpha^{\prime}}\sum_{x_{i}}(-1)^{x_{i}}=0\,.

We therefore conclude
T(x_{\alpha})\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Now that the mapping is well-defined, it remains to show bijectivity. But since
\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathcal{H}_{k}
have already been shown to be isomorphic, this follows from injectivity. The latter trivially holds, since
T(f)=0
implies
f(x)=0
for all
x
, by virtue of
\ket{x}\bra{x}
being a basis for
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, and hence
f=0
.
∎

Remark.
Any binary function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
can be written as a polynomial of degree
n
, by inductively extending the following one-variable identity
f(x)=f(0)(1-x)+f(1)x
. It immediately follows that Proposition
1
extends to all binary functions which can be written as a sum of
k
-
variable
functions, rather than
k
-
degree
monomials. This may be particularly relevant for
black-box
binary optimization, where
f
is arbitrary.

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem
2

Theorem
2
.

Consider any discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\phi}\in\mathcal{N}
, and the corresponding weights
c_{\alpha}(\phi)
defined by Equation (
2
). Then, for any 1-local weight (namely
\absolutevalue{\al}=1
), we have

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\Big[c_{\alpha}(\phi)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma_{L+1}^{2}}{16}\ \prod_{l=1}^{L}\frac{m_{l}\sigma_{l}^{2}(1+\gamma_{l})^{2}}{4}\,.

In particular, initialising parameters such that
m_{l}\sigma^{2}_{l}\geq 4
for each
l
, the bound reduces to
\E_{\phi}\big[c_{\alpha}(\phi)^{2}\big]\geq\sigma^{2}_{L+1}/16
, which is
constant
both in the number of qubits
n
and the discriminator depth
L
.

Proof.

Consider a fixed discriminator
D_{\phi}\in\mathcal{N}
, and denote its leaky-ReLU activation function by
R(x)=\max(x,\gamma x)
(with parameter
\gamma
), as well as its weight / bias matrices by
A^{l}
/
B^{l}
, for each layer
l
, together forming its parameter set
\phi
. We inductively define the output of the
l
th layer as
D^{0}(x)=x
for the input,

D^{l}(x)=R\left(A^{l}D^{l-1}(x)+B^{l}\right)

for the hidden layers
l:1\to L
, and

D^{L+1}(x)=A^{L+1}D^{L}(x)+B^{L+1}

for the output (no leaky ReLU). A discriminator with
L
hidden layers can thus be expressed as

D_{\phi}(x)=F\left(D^{L+1}(x)\right)\,,

where the output activation
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by
F(x)=\log(\sig(x))
for min-max GANs and
F(x)=x
for Wasserstein GANs. Finally, we write
m_{0},\ldots,m_{L+1}
for the width of each layer, so that
A^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}\times m_{l-1}}
and
B^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}}
for each
l
. Now recall the definition of
c_{\alpha}
from Equation (
14
):

c_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{(-1)}^{\alpha\cdot x}D_{\phi}(x)\,.

Our goal is to provide a lower bound for

\E_{\phi}\Big[c_{\alpha}(\phi)^{2}\Big]=\E_{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}(x)\cdot D_{\phi}(y)\right]\,.

Induction on the number of layers.
Ignoring the output activation, we first prove that

\E_{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)\cdot D_{\phi}^{L}(y)\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}\prod_{l=1}^{L}\frac{m_{l}\sigma_{l}^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}}{4}\,,

(15)

by induction on
L
. Let
k
be the unique index such that
\alpha_{k}=1
. For the base case, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{0}(x)\cdot D_{\phi}^{0}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}x\cdot y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}\cdot y^{\prime}+1)-(x^{\prime}\cdot y^{\prime})-(x^{\prime}\cdot y^{\prime})+(x^{\prime}\cdot y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}1=\frac{1}{4}

where
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}^{n-1}
are defined as
x,y\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
with the
k
th component removed. Now for any fixed
l:1\to L
, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{l}(x)\cdot D_{\phi}^{l}(y)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{l}}\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}R\left(A^{l}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)+B^{l}\right)_{i}R\left(A^{l}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)+B^{l}\right)_{i}\right]\,.

To reduce this further, let us fix
i
and simplify notation by defining

\displaystyle f(x)\coloneqq\left(A^{l}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)+B^{l}\right)_{i}=\sum_{j}A^{l}_{ij}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)_{j}+B^{l}_{i}\,.

Expanding the leaky-ReLU as

R(x)=\max(x,\gamma x)=x(H(x)+\gamma H(-x))\,,

where
H(x)
is the Heaviside step function, the goal is now to simplify the expression

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}R(f(x))R(f(y))\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}f(x)f(y)\Big[H(f(x))+\gamma H(-f(x))\Big]\Big[H(f(y))+\gamma H(-f(y))\Big]\right]\,.

Let us first make note of the trivial fact that

xH(x)=\frac{1}{2}(x+\absolutevalue{x})\,,

and extend this decomposition in two dimensions to obtain the less trivial identities

\displaystyle xy\Big[H(x)H(y)+H(-x)H(-y)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(xy+\absolutevalue{xy}\Big)\,,

\displaystyle xy\Big[H(x)H(-y)+H(-x)H(y)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(xy-\absolutevalue{xy}\Big)\,.

Unfortunately, these equations alone will produce cross-terms whose expectations do not have closed-form solutions. Thankfully, we can sidestep this entirely by summoning the symmetry of parameter distributions, which imply

\E_{\phi}\left[f(x)f(y)H(f(x))H(f(y))\right]=\E_{\phi}\left[f(x)f(y)H(-f(x))H(-f(y))\right]\,.

Combining these two sets of equations, we obtain

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}f(x)f(y)\Big[H(f(x))+\gamma H(-f(x))\Big]\Big[H(f(y))+\gamma H(-f(y))\Big]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\gamma^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}f(x)f(y)\Big[H(f(x))H(f(y))+H(-f(x))H(-f(y))\Big]\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}f(x)f(y)\Big[H(f(x))H(-f(y))+H(-f(x))H(f(y))\Big]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\gamma^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\Big[f(x)f(y)+\big|f(x)f(y)\big|\Big]\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\gamma}{2}

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\Big[f(x)f(y)-\big|f(x)f(y)\big|\Big]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\gamma)^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}f(x)f(y)\right]+\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{4}\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\big|f(x)f(y)\big|\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\gamma)^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}f(x)f(y)\right]+\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{4}\E_{\phi}\left[\left(\sum_{x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}\big|f(x)\big|\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1+\gamma)^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}f(x)f(y)\right]\,.

We now use the fact that parameters are independent (and symmetrically distributed), along with the fact that any term where
x
or
y
does not appear must vanish in the signed sum, to obtain

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}f(x)f(y)\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\left(A^{l}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)+B^{l}\right)_{i}\left(A^{l}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(y)+B^{l}\right)_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\Big(A^{l}_{ij}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)_{j}+B^{l}_{i}\Big)\Big(A^{l}_{ik}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)_{k}+B^{l}_{k}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\E_{\phi}\left[A^{l}_{ij}A^{l}_{ik}\right]\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)_{j}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sigma_{l}^{2}\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)_{j}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma_{l}^{2}\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)\cdot D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)\right]\,.

This holds for each
i
, so we obtain

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{l}(x)\cdot D_{\phi}^{l}(y)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma_{l}^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{l}}\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)\cdot D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{l}\sigma_{l}^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}}{4}\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)\cdot D_{\phi}^{l-1}(x)\right]\,.

The induction is complete, so Equation (
15
) holds.

(Output activation.)
It now remains to assimilate the output activation
F
. For both min-max and Wasserstein GANs, we can decompose
F
into odd and even parts and obtain

F(x)=F_{\text{odd}}(x)+F_{\text{even}}(x)=F^{\prime}(0)x+F_{\text{even}}(x)\,,

where
F^{\prime}(0)=1/2
for min-max GANs and
F^{\prime}(0)=1
for Wasserstein GANs. It follows that

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}(x)D_{\phi}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}F\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)+B^{L+1}\right)F\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)+B^{L+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(0)^{2}

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)+B^{L+1}\right)\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)+B^{L+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}F_{\text{even}}\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)+B^{L+1}\right)F_{\text{even}}\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)+B^{L+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+F^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)+B^{L+1}\right)F_{\text{even}}\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)+B^{L+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+F^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}F_{\text{even}}\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)+B^{L+1}\right)\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)+B^{L+1}\right)\right]\,.

The second term is non-negative since it is the expectation of a squared quantity, while the third and fourth terms vanish by symmetry of the distributions on
A^{L+1}
and
B^{L+1}
. More precisely, a change of variables
(A^{L+1},B^{L+1})\to(-A^{L+1},-B^{L+1})
is invariant under expectation, and gives

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\left(-A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)-B^{L+1}\right)F_{\text{even}}\left(-A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)-B^{L+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\left[\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)+B^{L+1}\right)F_{\text{even}}\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)+B^{L+1}\right)\right]

since
F_{\text{even}}
is even. The third term is therefore equal to its opposite, and vanishes. Similarly for the fourth term. It remains only to expand the remaining first term:

\displaystyle\E_{\phi}\Big[c_{\alpha}(\phi)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\E_{\phi}\left[F^{\prime}(0)^{2}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}(x)D_{\phi}(y)\right]

\displaystyle\geq F^{\prime}(0)^{2}\E_{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)+B^{L+1}\right)\left(A^{L+1}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)+B^{L+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(0)^{2}\sum_{j,k}\E_{\phi}\left[A^{L+1}_{j}A^{L+1}_{k}\right]\E_{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)_{j}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(0)^{2}\sum_{j}\sigma_{L+1}^{2}\E_{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)_{j}D_{\phi}^{L}(y)_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(0)^{2}\sigma_{L+1}^{2}\E_{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot x}(-1)^{\alpha\cdot y}D_{\phi}^{L}(x)\cdot D_{\phi}^{L}(y)\right]\,.

Using Equation (
15
), and remembering that
F^{\prime}(0)\geq 1/2
, the proof is drawn to a close:

\E_{\phi}\Big[c_{\alpha}(\phi)^{2}\Big]\geq\frac{\sigma_{L+1}^{2}}{16}\prod_{l=1}^{L}\frac{m_{l}\sigma_{l}^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}}{4}\,.\qed

Appendix F
Robustness of Experiments

In this section, we check the statistical significance and robustness of our numerical experiments from Section
3.3
.
On one hand, we verify that the results are representative of typical training behavior, i.e., consistent over various random seeds that determine initial parameters and sampled batches from the true and generated distributions. On the other, we check whether our results are robust under inexact gradients. For this purpose, we run the same training setup as in Section
3.3
, but average the results over 10 random seeds, and estimate the generator gradients using Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA)
[
98
]
. This algorithm provides an unbiased estimator for the full gradient, which we average over 10 perturbation vectors, and is typically used in real hardware experiments thanks to its constant cost with respect to the number of parameters.

The results presented in Figure
12
(a) show the average evolution of the relative entropy over 500 training iterations, along with shaded standard deviations.
Overall, results are relatively robust, though the relative entropy converges more convincingly for 6 qubits than for 16 qubits. This behavior is reflected in the average entropy converging towards smaller values, but also in the decreasing standard deviation.
For 16 qubits, while the average entropy also decreases uniformly throughout training, the standard deviation stays more or less constant at later stages of training.
As discussed in the main text, this suggests that the model struggles to encode the underlying continuous distribution, possibly due to the lack of an inductive bias.
Finally, Figure
12
(b) illustrates the average of the learned PDFs for the various random seeds. While the
6-qubit PDF is rather close to the target, the 16-qubit PDF is particularly spiky – even in the averaged setting. This is in alignment with the relative entropy failing to converge to zero, and calls for further work to improve the capacity of quantum generators to encode continuous distributions effectively.
