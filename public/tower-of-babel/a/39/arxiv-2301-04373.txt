Inf-sup condition and locking: Understanding and circumventing. Stokes, Laplacian, bi-Laplacian, Kirchhoff–Love and Mindlin–Reissner locking type, boundary conditions
Gilles Leborgne

Abstract
The inf-sup condition, also called the Ladyzhenskaya–Babuška–Brezzi (LBB) condition, ensures the well-posedness of a saddle point problem, relative to a partial differential equation. Discretization by the finite element method gives the discrete problem which must satisfy the discrete inf-sup condition. But, depending on the choice of finite elements, the discrete condition may fail. This paper attempts to explain why it fails from an engineer’s perspective, and reviews current methods to work around this failure. The last part recalls the mathematical bases.

The inf-sup condition, what is that ?

1.1
The constrained problem under concern

Let
(V,(\cdot,\cdot)_{V})
be a Hilbert space and
(Q,||.||_{Q})
be a Banach space.
Let
V^{\prime}={\cal L}(V;\mathbb{R})
and

(spaces of the linear continuous forms).
Let

and

be linear continuous forms,
and let

and

be bilinear continuous forms.

The problem under concern is: Find
(u,p)\in V\times Q
s.t.

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil& $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&a(u,v)+b(v,p)&=\langle f,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V},\quad\forall v\in V,\cr&b(u,q)&=\langle g,q\rangle_{Q^{\prime},Q},\quad\forall q\in Q.\crcr}}\,\right.

(1.1)

E.g., with
\Omega
an open regular bounded set in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

and
{L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)}\simeq{L^{2}(\Omega)}/\mathbb{R}
(that is the space of

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&({\rm grad}{\vec{u}},{\rm grad}{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}-(p,{\rm div}{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}=\langle{\vec{f}},{\vec{v}}\rangle_{{H^{-1}},{H^{1}_{0}}},\quad\forall{\vec{v}}\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{n}\cr&-({\rm div}{\vec{u}},q)_{L^{2}}=0,\quad\forall q\in{L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)}.\crcr}}\,\right.

(1.2)

Let
A\in{\cal L}(V;V^{\prime})
,
B\in{\cal L}(V;Q^{\prime})
and
B^{t}\in{\cal L}(Q,V^{\prime})

be the associated linear continuous mapping (bounded operators)

\langle Au,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=a(u,v),\quad\langle Bv,p\rangle_{Q^{\prime},Q}=b(v,p)=\langle B^{t}p,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}.

(1.3)

Then (

) also reads

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil& $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&Au+B^{t}p&=f\in V^{\prime},\cr&Bu&=g\in Q^{\prime},\crcr}}\,\right.

(1.4)

the equation

being the constraint. E.g., find
({\vec{u}},p)\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{n}\times{L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)}
s.t.

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil& $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&{-}\Delta{\vec{u}}+{\vec{\rm grad}}p&={\vec{f}},\cr&{\rm div}{\vec{u}}&=0.\crcr}}\,\right.

(1.5)

-continuous finite elements for both the velocity and pressure.

1.4
Where is the problem?

E.g., with (

) and continuous

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&({\vec{\rm grad}}{\vec{u}}_{h},{\vec{\rm grad}}{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}}-(p_{h},{\rm div}{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}}=({\vec{f}},{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}},\quad\forall{\vec{v}}_{h}\in{V_{h}},\cr&-({\rm div}{\vec{u}}_{h},q_{h})_{L^{2}}-\sum_{K=1}^{n_{K}}h_{K}^{2}({\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h}{-}{\Pi_{V_{h}}\!}{\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h},{\vec{\rm grad}}q_{h})_{L^{2}(K)}=0,\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h},\cr}}\,\right.

(3.14)

since
({\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h}{-}{\Pi_{V_{h}}\!}{\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h},{\vec{w}}_{h})=0
for
{\vec{w}}_{h}\in{V_{h}}
(definition of
\Pi_{V_{h}}
).

Computation:
we have to compute a new unknown
{\vec{z}}_{h}={\Pi_{V_{h}}\!}{\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h}\in{V_{h}}
(luckily very cheap for
P_{1}
finite elements):
Find
{\vec{u}}_{h},{\vec{z}}_{h}\in{V_{h}}
et
p_{h}\in Q_{h}
s.t.

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&({\vec{\rm grad}}{\vec{u}}_{h},{\vec{\rm grad}}{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}}-(p_{h},{\rm div}{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}}=({\vec{f}},{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}},\quad\forall{\vec{v}}_{h}\in{V_{h}},\cr&-({\rm div}{\vec{u}}_{h},q_{h})_{L^{2}}-\sum_{K=1}^{n_{K}}h_{K}^{2}({\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h},{\vec{\rm grad}}q_{h})_{L^{2}}+h^{2}({\vec{z}}_{h},{\vec{\rm grad}}q_{h})_{L^{2}}=0,\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h},\cr&({\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h},{\vec{z}}_{h}^{\prime})_{L^{2}(K)}-({\vec{z}}_{h},{\vec{z}}_{h}^{\prime})_{L^{2}(K)}=0,\quad\forall{\vec{z}}_{h}^{\prime}\in{V_{h}},\;\forall K.\cr}}\,\right.

(3.15)

E.g.with
P_{1}
finite elements, the
({\vec{z}}_{h},{\vec{z}}_{h}^{\prime})_{L^{2}}
associated matrix
can be made diagonal thanks to the “mass lumping” technique:
Thus the last equation (in
{\vec{z}}_{h}^{\prime}
) gives
{\vec{z}}_{h}
explicitly as a function of
{\vec{\rm grad}}_{h}p_{h}

(order 1 precision).

Remark 3.3

The associated Lagrangian, cf. (
2.8
), is now:

L_{h}({\vec{v}}_{h},p_{h})={1\over 2}||{\rm grad}{\vec{v}}_{h}||^{2}_{L^{2}}-(p_{h},{\rm div}{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}}-(f,v_{h})_{L^{2}}-{1\over 2}\sum_{K=1}^{n_{K}}h_{K}^{2}||{\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h}{-}{\Pi_{V_{h}}\!}{\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h}||^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}.

(3.16)

3.7
Brezzi and Pitkäranta’s method

A previous method proposed by Brezzi and Pitkäranta
[
11
]

consists in penalizing the initial problem with the Laplacian of the pressure
(to “control the oscillations” of
p_{h}
):
Find
{\vec{u}}_{h}\in V_{h}
and
p_{h}\in Q_{h}
s.t.

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&({\vec{\rm grad}}{\vec{u}}_{h},{\vec{\rm grad}}{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}}-(p_{h},{\rm div}{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}}=({\vec{f}},{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}},\quad\forall{\vec{v}}_{h}\in V_{h},\cr&-({\rm div}{\vec{u}}_{h},q_{h})_{L^{2}}-\varepsilon\sum_{K=1}^{n_{K}}h_{K}^{2}({\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h},{\vec{\rm grad}}q_{h})_{L^{2}(K)}=0,\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h},\cr}}\,\right.

(3.17)

with some
\varepsilon>0
.
We however get a spurious limit condition
{\partial p\over\partial n}=0
independent of
\varepsilon
(by integration by parts).
(This spurious limit condition is lessen with (
3.14
).)

Remark 3.4

The associated Lagrangian is now:

L_{h}({\vec{v}}_{h},p_{h})={1\over 2}||{\rm grad}{\vec{v}}_{h}||^{2}_{L^{2}}-(p_{h},{\rm div}{\vec{v}}_{h})_{L^{2}}-(f,v_{h})_{L^{2}}-{1\over 2}\varepsilon\sum_{K=1}^{n_{K}}h_{K}^{2}||{\vec{\rm grad}}p_{h}||^{2}_{L^{2}(K)},

(3.18)

to compare with (
3.16
).

3.8
Hughes, Franca and Balestra’s method

Hughes, Franca and Balestra
[
24
]
proposed a
“Galerkin Least-squares” method: The pressure is stabilized “with the solution”.
The problem reads, with the associated Lagrangian,

L({\vec{v}},p)={1\over 2}||{\rm grad}{\vec{v}}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(p,{\rm div}{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(f,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-{\varepsilon\over 2}\sum_{K=1}^{n_{K}}h_{K}^{2}||-\Delta u{+}{\vec{\rm grad}}p{-}f||^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}.

(3.19)

(For
P_{1}
finite elements, this method is similar to Brezzi and Pitkäranta’s method.)

Here
\varepsilon
has to be small enough not to destroy the coercivity in
u
,
see the term
({\vec{\rm grad}}u,{\vec{\rm grad}}v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\varepsilon\sum_{k}h^{2}(\Delta u,\Delta v)_{L^{2}(K)}
,
the control being done thanks to the inverse inequality (quasi-uniform mesh)

||\Delta u_{h}||_{L^{2}(K)}\leq Ch||{\vec{\rm grad}}u_{h}||_{L^{2}(K)},\quad\forall u_{h}\in V_{h}.

(So
0<\varepsilon<{1\over\sqrt{C}}
.)

3.9
Douglas and Wang’s method

To avoid the eventual destruction of the coercivity for
{\vec{u}}
,
Douglas et Wang consider

\underbrace{({\rm grad}{\vec{u}},{\rm grad}{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}-(p,{\rm div}{\vec{v}})+(q,{\rm div}{\vec{u}})+\varepsilon\sum_{K=1}^{n_{K}}h_{K}(-\Delta{\vec{u}}+{\vec{\rm grad}}p-{\vec{f}},-\Delta{\vec{v}}+{\vec{\rm grad}}q)_{L^{2}(K)}}_{c(({\vec{u}},p),({\vec{v}},q))}=\underbrace{(f,v)_{L^{2}}}_{\ell({\vec{v}},q)}.

(3.20)

This preserves the stability since
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive, but the symmetry is lost.
So this method is adapted to the generalization of the Stokes equations to the Navier–Stokes equations.

4
Laplacian (harmonic problem)

The linear spaces needed are described in §
9
.

4.1
A dirichlet problem

Let
f\in{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
.
Problem: Find
p\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
s.t.

-\Delta p=f.

(4.1)

That is,

({\vec{\rm grad}}p,{\vec{\rm grad}}q)_{L^{2}}=\langle f,q\rangle,\quad\forall q\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.

(4.2)

The associated minimum problem is:
Find the minimum of
J(p)=\min_{q\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}}J(q)
,
where

J(q):={1\over 2}||{\vec{\rm grad}}q||_{L^{2}}^{2}-\langle f,q\rangle.

(4.3)

To get
{\vec{\rm grad}}p
during the computation, introduce

{\vec{u}}={\vec{\rm grad}}p\;\in{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\hbox{and then}\quad-{\rm div}{\vec{u}}=f.

(4.4)

And (
4.1
) becomes: Find
({\vec{u}},p)\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
s.t.

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&({\vec{u}},{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}-({\vec{\rm grad}}p,{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}=0,\quad\forall{\vec{v}}\in{L^{2}(\Omega)},\cr&-({\vec{u}},{\vec{\rm grad}}q)_{L^{2}}=-\langle f,q\rangle_{{H^{-1}},{H^{1}_{0}}},\quad\forall q\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.\crcr}}\,\right.

(4.5)

And
p
is now the Lagrangian multiplier for the constraint
{\rm div}{\vec{u}}=-f
, cf. the integration by parts.

And if
({\vec{u}},p)\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
is a solution, then

{\vec{u}}={\vec{\rm grad}}p\in{{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{n}}
in
\Omega
, and

{\rm div}{\vec{u}}=-f
in
{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
.
So
\Delta p=f
in
{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
with
p\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
: This is (
4.1
).

With

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&a({\vec{u}},{\vec{v}})=({\vec{u}},{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}\quad\hbox{sur }{{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{n}}\times{{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{n}},\cr&b({\vec{v}},q)=-({\vec{v}},{\vec{\rm grad}}q)_{L^{2}}\quad\hbox{sur }{{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{n}}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)},\crcr}}\,\right.

(4.6)

(
4.5
) has the appearance of (
1.1
) with
V={{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{n}}
and
Q={H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
.

Here
b({\vec{v}},p)=\langle B{\vec{v}},p\rangle_{{H^{-1}},{H^{1}(\Omega)}}=(B^{t}p,{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
so
B={\rm div}:\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr{{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{n}}&\rightarrow{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\cr{\vec{v}}&\rightarrow B{\vec{v}}={\rm div}({\vec{v}})\crcr}}\,\right\}

and
B^{t}=-{\vec{\rm grad}}:\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}&\rightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)}\cr p&\rightarrow B^{t}p=-{\vec{\rm grad}}p\crcr}}\,\right\}
.

Thus
{\rm Ker}(B)={\rm Ker}({\rm div})=\{{\vec{v}}\in{{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{n}}:{\rm div}{\vec{v}}=0\}
,
and thanks to the Helmholtz decomposition (
9.31
)

{{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{n}}={\vec{\rm grad}}({H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)})\oplus^{\perp_{L^{2}}}{\rm Ker}({\rm div})
,
the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}}
coercive on
{\rm Ker}(B)
.

And
B^{t}
is surjective since
B
is, cf. (
13.5
)
and the closed range theorem
11.2
.

Thus (
4.5
) is well-posed, see theorem
12.1
.

4.2
A Neumann problem

Let
f\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
Problem: Find
p\in{H^{1}(\Omega)}
s.t.

-\Delta p=f,\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\partial p\over\partial n}_{|\Gamma}=0.

(4.7)

That is,

({\vec{\rm grad}}p,{\vec{\rm grad}}q)_{L^{2}}=(f,q)_{L^{2}},\quad\forall q\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.

(4.8)

The associated minimum problem is:
Find the minimum of
J(p)=\min_{q\in{H^{1}(\Omega)}}J(q)
,
where

J(q):={1\over 2}||{\vec{\rm grad}}q||_{L^{2}}^{2}-(f,q)_{L^{2}}.

(4.9)

The mixed associated problem is: Find
({\vec{u}},p)\in{H^{\rm div}(\Omega)}\times{L^{2}(\Omega)}
s.t.

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&({\vec{u}},{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}+(p,{\rm div}{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}=0,\quad\forall{\vec{v}}\in{H^{\rm div}(\Omega)},\cr&({\rm div}{\vec{u}},q)_{L^{2}}=(f,q)_{L^{2}},\quad\forall q\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\crcr}}\,\right.

(4.10)

Indeed, if
({\vec{u}},p)\in{H^{\rm div}(\Omega)}\times{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is a solution, then

{\rm div}{\vec{u}}=f\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
{\vec{u}}=-{\vec{\rm grad}}p\in{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
, thus
-\Delta p=f\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
with
{\partial p\over\partial n}_{|\Gamma}=0
(since
{\rm Im}(\gamma_{n})={H^{-{1\over 2}}(\Gamma)}
).

With

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&a({\vec{u}},{\vec{v}})=({\vec{u}},{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}\quad\hbox{sur }{H^{\rm div}(\Omega)}\times{H^{\rm div}(\Omega)},\cr&b({\vec{v}},q)=({\rm div}{\vec{v}},q)_{L^{2}}\quad\hbox{sur }{H^{\rm div}(\Omega)}\times{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\crcr}}\,\right.

(4.11)

(
4.5
) has the appearance of (
1.1
) with
V={H^{\rm div}(\Omega)}
and
Q={L^{2}(\Omega)}
.

Here
B:{\vec{v}}\in{H^{\rm div}(\Omega)}\rightarrow B{\vec{v}}={\rm div}{\vec{v}}\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is surjective, cf. (
13.2
),
and
a(\cdot,\cdot)
est
{H^{\rm div}}
-coercive on
{\rm Ker}(B)=\{{\vec{v}}\in{H^{\rm div}(\Omega)}:{\rm div}{\vec{v}}=0\}
.
And
{\rm Im}(B)
being closed (since it is surjective), so is
{\rm Im}(B^{t})
(closed range theorem
11.2
).
Thus (
4.10
) is well-posed, see theorem
12.1
.

5
Bilaplacian (biharmonic problem)

The linear spaces needed are described in §

.

5.1
Problem

We look here at the Dirichlet problem: If

Weak form: Find

s.t.

(\Delta p,\Delta q)_{L^{2}}=\langle f,q\rangle_{{H^{-2}},{H^{2}_{0}}},\quad\forall q\in{H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)}.

(5.2)

The Lax–Milgram theorem indicates that (
5.2
) is well-posed.

The associated minimum problem is:
Find the minimum of
J(p)=\min_{q\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}}J(q)
,
where

J(q):={1\over 2}||\Delta q||_{L^{2}}^{2}-\langle f,q\rangle_{{H^{-2}},{H^{2}_{0}}}.

(5.3)

5.2
Introduction of
\Delta p

(Not conclusive.)
A function in
\in{H^{2}(\Omega)}
, s.t.
\Delta p
and
\Delta q

cf. the

Argyris finite elements. Let

\phi=\Delta p

(5.4)

Then problem (
5.1
) is rewritten as:
Find

s.t.

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&\phi=\Delta p,\cr&\Delta\phi=f.\crcr}}\,\right.

(5.5)

And the weak form is, if
f\in{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
:
Find
(\phi,p)\in{H^{1}(\Omega)}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
s.t.

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&(\phi,\psi)_{L^{2}}+({\vec{\rm grad}}p,{\vec{\rm grad}}\psi)_{L^{2}}=0,\quad\forall\psi\in{H^{1}(\Omega)},\cr&({\vec{\rm grad}}\phi,{\vec{\rm grad}}q)_{L^{2}}=-\langle f,q\rangle_{{H^{-1}},{H^{1}_{0}}},\quad\forall q\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.\crcr}}\,\right.

(5.6)

Indeed, if
(\phi,p)\in{H^{1}(\Omega)}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}

),
then
\phi-\Delta p=0
(thus
\Delta p\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}

{\partial p\over\partial n}_{|\Gamma}=0
.
And
p\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
with
\Delta\phi=f\in{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
, thus
\Delta^{2}p=f
.

With

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&a(\phi,\psi)=(\phi,\psi)_{L^{2}}\quad\hbox{sur }{H^{1}(\Omega)}\times{H^{1}(\Omega)},\cr&b(\phi,v)=({\vec{\rm grad}}\phi,{\vec{\rm grad}}v)_{L^{2}}\quad\hbox{sur }{H^{1}(\Omega)}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)},\crcr}}\,\right.

(5.7)

(
4.5
) has the appearance of (
5.6
) with

and
Q={H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
.

Here

.

Thus

Apply Lax–Milgram theorem for

6
Locking

The locking phenomenon appears when the coercivity of the approximated problem increases much faster than
the coercivity of the continuous problem.
Thus the numerical solution is close to zero, which is absurd in general.

Let
\Omega
be bounded open set in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.

6.1
A typical situation

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
so that
\lambda>>1
(a “large” given real).
We look for
{\vec{u}}\in{{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{n}}
and
p\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}

that minimize

M({\vec{v}},q)={1\over 2}||{\rm grad}{\vec{v}}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+{\lambda\over 2}||{\vec{v}}-{\vec{\rm grad}}q||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-({\vec{f}},{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(g,q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(6.1)

Example 6.1

For the Mindlin–Reissner problem,

||{\rm grad}{\vec{v}}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is replaced by
|a({\vec{v}},{\vec{v}})|

where
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is a bilinear form that is continuous and coercive on
{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
.

Let

X={H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{n}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}

(6.2)

provided with the inner product associated to the norm

||({\vec{v}},q)||_{X}=(||{\rm grad}{\vec{v}}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+||{\vec{\rm grad}}q||_{L^{2}}^{2})^{1\over 2}

(6.3)

so that
X
is a Hilbert space.

A solution
({\vec{u}},p)\in X
realizing the min of
M
satisfies

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil& \qquad$\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&({\rm grad}{\vec{u}},{\rm grad}{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}+\lambda({\vec{u}}-{\vec{\rm grad}}p,{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}=({\vec{f}},{\vec{v}}),&\forall{\vec{v}}\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{n},\cr&\lambda({\vec{u}}-{\vec{\rm grad}}p,{\vec{\rm grad}}q)_{L^{2}}=(g,q),&\forall q\in{H^{1}(\Omega)}.\cr}}\,\right.

(6.4)

Let

\Phi(({\vec{u}},p),({\vec{v}},q))=({\rm grad}{\vec{u}},{\rm grad}{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}+\lambda({\vec{u}}-{\vec{\rm grad}}p,{\vec{v}}-{\vec{\rm grad}}q)_{L^{2}}.

(6.5)

Thus (
6.4
) reads: Find
({\vec{u}},p)\in X
s.t.

\Phi(({\vec{u}},p),({\vec{v}},q))=({\vec{f}},{\vec{v}})_{L^{2}}+(g,q)_{L^{2}},\quad\forall({\vec{v}},q)\in X.

(6.6)

Proposition 6.2

The bilinear form
\Phi:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is coercive and continuous on
X
:
with the Poincaré inequality (
9.28
) we have

\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr&\exists\alpha_{\Phi}>0,\quad\forall({\vec{v}},q)\in X,\quad\Phi(({\vec{v}},q),({\vec{v}},q))\geq\alpha_{\Phi}||({\vec{v}},q)||_{X}^{2},\quad\hbox{et}\quad\alpha_{\Phi}\mathop{\sim}_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}{1\over c_{\Omega}},\cr&\exists C>0,\quad\forall({\vec{u}},p),({\vec{v}},q)\in X,\quad\Phi(({\vec{u}},p),({\vec{v}},q))\leq C||({\vec{u}},p)||_{X}||({\vec{v}},q)||_{X},\quad\hbox{et}\quad C\mathop{=}_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}O(\lambda).\crcr}}\,\right.

(6.7)

And problem (
6.6
) is well posed.

Proof.
Bi-linearity. Since
\Phi
is symmetric (trivial), that is
\Phi(({\vec{u}},p),({\vec{v}},q))=\Phi(({\vec{v}},q),({\vec{u}},p))
,
we have to prove that

\Phi(({\vec{u}}_{1},p_{1})+\alpha({\vec{u}}_{2},p_{2}),({\vec{v}},q))=\Phi(({\vec{u}}_{1},p_{1}),({\vec{v}},q))+\alpha\Phi(({\vec{u}}_{2},p_{2}),({\vec{v}},q))
: trivial.

Coercivity.
If
\kappa>0
then, with (
9.28
) :

\displaystyle{\Phi(({\vec{v}},q),({\vec{v}},q))=}

\displaystyle{{}||{\rm grad}{\vec{v}}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lambda||{\vec{v}}-{\vec{\rm grad}}q||_{L^{2}}^{2}}

\displaystyle{\geq}

\displaystyle{{}[(1{-}\kappa)||{\rm grad}{\vec{v}}||_{L^{2}}+c_{\Omega}\kappa||{\vec{v}}||_{L^{2}}^{2}]+\lambda[||{\vec{v}}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+||{\vec{\rm grad}}q||_{L^{2}}^{2}-2||{\vec{v}}||_{L^{2}}||q||_{L^{2}}].}

Let
x=||{\vec{v}}||_{L^{2}}
and
y=||{\vec{\rm grad}}q||_{L^{2}}
. We have

c_{\Omega}\kappa x^{2}+\lambda(x-y)^{2}\geq{\lambda c_{\Omega}\kappa\over\lambda+c_{\Omega}\kappa}y^{2},

with
c_{\rm max}={\lambda c_{\Omega}\kappa\over\lambda+c_{\Omega}\kappa}
the largest constant possible
(
c_{\rm max}
is largest
c
s.t.
c_{\Omega}\kappa x^{2}+\lambda(x-y)^{2}\geq cy^{2}
: easy check).
Thus

\Phi(({\vec{v}},q),({\vec{v}},q))\geq(1{-}\kappa)||{\rm grad}{\vec{v}}||_{L^{2}}+{\lambda c_{\Omega}\kappa\over\lambda+c_{\Omega}\kappa}||{\vec{\rm grad}}q||_{L^{2}}.

And the “best”
\alpha_{\Phi}
(the largest possible)
is obtained by choosing
\kappa
s.t.
1{-}\kappa={\lambda c_{\Omega}\kappa\over\lambda+c_{\Omega}\kappa}
, i.e.
\kappa

solution of
\kappa^{2}+b\kappa-{\lambda\over c_{\Omega}}=0
where
b=\lambda{c_{\Omega}+1\over c_{\Omega}}-1
.
The discriminant is
b^{2}+4{\lambda\over c_{\Omega}}
, and the positive root is

\kappa={b\over 2}(-1+\sqrt{1+4{\lambda\over b^{2}c_{\Omega}}})
.
And for
\lambda>>1
, we have
b\simeq\lambda
, thus

4{\lambda\over b^{2}c_{\Omega}}\simeq 4{1\over\lambda c_{\Omega}}
, thus

-1+\sqrt{1+4{\lambda\over b^{2}c_{\Omega}}}\simeq{2\over\lambda c_{\Omega}}
, so

\kappa\simeq{1\over c_{\Omega}}
in the vicinity of
\lambda=\infty
.

Continuity: Easy check.

Remark 6.3

For the associated numerical approximation with finite elements,
it
\lambda
is “large” then difficulties are expected, since
C=O(\lambda)
.
Indeed, the conditioning of the associated matrix is
\simeq{C\over\alpha_{\Phi}}=0(\lambda)
,
and this conditioning explodes with
\lambda
.
However, a bad choice of the discrete spaces leads to a much faster explosion than expected,
see proposition
6.5
.

The open mapping theorem

8.1
Notations

If
E
and
F
are linear spaces, a map
T:E\rightarrow F
is linear iff

T(x_{1}+\lambda x_{2})=T(x_{1})+\lambda T(x_{2})
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in E
and all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
;
And then
T(x)
is denoted
T.x
or
Tx
.

Let
(E,||.||_{E})
be a normed space.
Let
B_{E}(x,\rho)=\{x^{\prime}\in E;||x^{\prime}-x||_{E}<\rho\}
the ball of radius
\rho>0
centered at
x\in E
.

If
(E,||.||_{E})
and
(F,||.||_{F})
are two normed spaces,
if
T:\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr E&\rightarrow F\cr x&\rightarrow T(x)\crcr}}\,\right\}

is a linear map, then
T
is said to be continuous (or bounded) iff

\exists c>0,\quad\forall x\in E,\quad||T.x||_{F}\leq c\,||x||_{E}.\quad\hbox{and then}\quad||T||:=\sup_{x\in B_{E}(0,1)}||T.x||_{F}.

(8.1)

In the sequel, the space
E
and
F
will be Banach spaces (complete for the norm in use).

Let
{\cal L}(E;F)
be the set of linear continuous mapping from
E
to
F
.
Then

||.||_{{\cal L}(E;F)}:\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr{\cal L}(E;F)&\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cr T&\rightarrow||T||_{{\cal L}(E;F)}:=\sup_{x\in B_{E}(0,1)}||T.x||_{F}{\;\mathop{=}^{\hbox{\footnotesize denoted}}\;}||T||\crcr}}\,\right.

(8.2)

define a norm in
{\cal L}(E;F)
(easy check),
and
({\cal L}(E;F),||.||)
is a Banach space
(check: If
(T_{n})_{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is a Cauchy sequence, that is
||T_{n}-T_{m}||\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n,m\rightarrow\infty}0
,
then, for any
x\in E
,
||(T_{n}-T_{m})(x)||_{F}\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n,m\rightarrow\infty}0
,
thus
(T_{n}(x))_{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
F
complete, thus converge to a
y_{x}\in F
;
Then define
T:x\in E\rightarrow T(x)=y_{x}
: It is easy to check that
T
is linear and continuous
with
||T-T_{n}||\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\rightarrow\infty}0
.)

Let
E^{\prime}:={\cal L}(E;\mathbb{R})
, called the dual of
E
(the set of linear continuous real valued functions,

\mathbb{R}
being provided with its usual norm).
For
\ell\in E^{\prime}
and
x\in E
denote:

\ell(x)=\ell.x=\langle\ell,x\rangle_{E^{\prime},E}\;\;\in\mathbb{R}.

(8.3)

So, cf. (
8.1
),

||\ell||_{E^{\prime}}=\sup_{x\in B_{E}(0,1)}|\ell.x|=\sup_{x\in B_{E}(0,1)}|\langle\ell,x\rangle_{E^{\prime},E}|

(8.4)

defines a norm in
E^{\prime}
s.t.
(E^{\prime},||.||_{E^{\prime}})
is a Banach space.

If
T\in{\cal L}(E;F)
(linear and continuous) then its adjoint is the
linear map
T^{\prime}:F^{\prime}\rightarrow E^{\prime}
characterized by:

T^{\prime}:\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr F^{\prime}&\rightarrow E^{\prime}\cr\ell&\rightarrow T^{\prime}(\ell){\;\mathop{=}^{\hbox{\footnotesize denoted}}\;}T^{\prime}.\ell,\quad\hbox{where}\quad\langle T^{\prime}.\ell,x\rangle_{E^{\prime},E}:=\langle\ell,T.x\rangle_{F^{\prime},F},\quad\forall x\in E.\crcr}}\,\right.

(8.5)

Proposition 8.1

T^{\prime}
is continuous with

||T^{\prime}||=||T||.

(8.6)

Thus
T^{\prime}\in{\cal L}(F^{\prime},E^{\prime})
.

Proof.
||T^{\prime}.\ell||_{E^{\prime}}=\sup_{||x||_{E}\leq 1}|\langle T^{\prime}.\ell,x\rangle_{E^{\prime},E}|=\sup_{||x||_{E}\leq 1}|\langle\ell,T.x\rangle_{F^{\prime},F}|\leq\sup_{||x||_{E}\leq 1}||\ell||_{F^{\prime}}||T.x||_{F}=\sup_{||x||_{E}\leq 1}||T||\,||x||_{E}||\ell||_{F^{\prime}}=||T||\,||\ell||_{F^{\prime}}
, thus
||T^{\prime}||\leq||T||
;
And similarly
||T.x||_{F}\leq||T^{\prime}||\,||\ell||_{F^{\prime}}
, thus
||T||\leq||T^{\prime}||
.

{E^{\prime\prime}}=({E^{\prime}})^{\prime}={\cal L}(E^{\prime};\mathbb{R})
is a Banach space (since
\mathbb{R}
is complete).
Let

J:\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr E&\rightarrow E^{\prime\prime}={\cal L}(E^{\prime};\mathbb{R})\cr x&\rightarrow J(x),\quad\hbox{where}\quad J(x)(\ell):=\ell.x,\;\forall x\in E.\crcr}}\,\right.

(8.7)

J
is linear (trivial),
is continuous, with
||J||=\sup_{x\in B_{E}(0,1)}|J(x)|=\sup_{x\in B_{E}(0,1)}(\sup_{\ell\in B_{E^{\prime}}(0,1)}|J(x)(\ell)|)=\sup_{x\in B_{E}(0,1)}(\sup_{\ell\in B_{E^{\prime}}(0,1)}|\ell.x|)=\sup_{x\in B_{E}(0,1)}(||x||_{E})=1
,
and injective (one-to-one) since
J(x)=0
implies
\ell.x=0
for all
\ell\in E^{\prime}
that implies
x=0
.
Thus
J
is a “canonical injection”.

Thus
J(E)={\rm Im}(E)
, the range or image of
E
by
J
, can be identified to a subspace of
E^{\prime\prime}
.

Definition 8.2

A Banach space
E
is reflexive iff
J
is bijective (= one-to-one and onto),
and then is identified with
E
, denoted
E^{\prime\prime}\simeq E
, and
J(x)
is denoted
x
.

(Remark: A Hilbert space is always reflexive,
and a reflexive Banach space “almost” behaves like a Hilbert space for computation purposes
(with the use of the bracket
\langle.,.\rangle_{E^{\prime},E}
similar to the use of a inner product).
There are however some substantial differences: e.g. in a reflexive Banach space there exist closed subspaces without any complement, whereas in a Hilbert space any closed subspace has a complement (even an orthogonal one);
And this causes some theoretical difficulties treated in the sequel.)

8.2
The open mapping theorem

Theorem 8.3
(Open mapping theorem)

Let
E
and
F
be Banach spaces.
If
T\in{\cal L}(E;F)
(linear and continuous) is surjective (= onto,
i.e.
{\rm Im}(T)=F
), then

\exists\gamma>0\quad\hbox{s{.}t{.}}\quad T(B_{E}(0,1))\supset B_{F}(0,\gamma).

(8.8)

That is, if
T
is linear continuous and surjective,
then any open set in
E
is transformed by
T
into an open set in
F
.
So
T(B_{E}(0,1))
is not “flat” (it contains an open set).

And the converse is true: if (
8.8
) then
T
is surjective.

Proof.
See Brézis
[
8
]
. Steps :
1-
T
being onto, we have
\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}^{*}}}\overline{T(B_{E}(0,n))}=F
,
and Baire’s Theorem gives the existence of a closed space
\overline{T(B_{E}(0,n))}

containing an open set;
2- The linearity of
T
then implies that
\overline{T(B_{E}(0,1))}
contains an open set
B_{F}(0,2\gamma)

for some
\gamma>0
.
3- And
T
being continuous and
E
being complete we get

T(B_{E}(0,1))\supset B_{F}(0,\gamma)
.

Converse:
T(B_{E}(0,1))\supset B_{F}(0,\gamma)
, and
T
is linear, so
T(E)=F
.

Corollary 8.4

If
T\in{\cal L}(E;F)
is bijective, i.e. injective (= one-to-one) and surjective (= onto),
then the linear map
T^{-1}:F\rightarrow E
is continuous, that is,

\exists\gamma>0,\;\forall y\in F,\quad||T^{-1}.y||_{E}\leq{1\over\gamma}||y||_{F}.

(8.9)

Thus

\exists\gamma>0,\;\forall x\in E,\quad||T.x||_{F}\geq\gamma||x||_{E}.

(8.10)

Proof.
Then
T
bijective gives
T^{-1}(B_{F}(0,\gamma))\subset B_{E}(0,1)
.
And
T^{-1}
is linear since
T
is,
thus
T^{-1}(B_{F}(0,1))\subset B_{E}(0,{1\over\gamma})
.
So
y\in B_{F}(0,1)
gives
||T^{-1}.y||_{E}\leq{1\over\gamma}||y||_{F}
, i.e. (
8.9
).
Then
y=T.x
gives (
8.10
) (bijectivity).

Remark 8.5

If
T
is bijective between Banach spaces,
then the problem: For
b\in F
find
x\in E
s.t.
T.x=b
is well-posed,
that is, has a unique solution
x=T^{-1}.b
s.t.
\exists c>0
(independent of
b
),
||x||_{E}\leq c||b||_{F}

(the inverse
T^{-1}
is continuous).
Indeed, the bijectivity of
T
gives a unique solution
x=T^{-1}.b
,
and (
8.9
) gives
||x||_{E}=||T^{-1}.b||_{E}\leq{1\over\gamma}||b||_{F}
.

Remark 8.6

A linear continuous bijective mapping between two infinite dimensional Banach spaces behaves like
in finite dimension,
e.g. like
T:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by its matrix
\pmatrix{-2&0\cr 0&3}
.
Here
||T||=3
,

T^{-1}=\pmatrix{-{1\over 2}&0\cr 0&{1\over 3}}
,
and

.

Let

8.3
Quotient space
E/{\rm Ker}(T)
, and open mapping theorem

Let
E
and
F
be banach spaces.
Let
T\in{\cal L}(E;F)
(linear and continuous).
Then
K={\rm Ker}(T)=T^{-1}(\{0\})
(the kernel of
T
) is linear subspace that is closed (since
T
is continuous).

Consider the relation in
E
defined by:
x\sim y
iff
x-y\in K
.
This is an equivalence relation (easy check).
Let
{E\!/\!K}=\{Z\subset E:\exists x\in E,\;Z=x+K\}=\{x+K:x\in E\}
be the set of the equivalence classes (quotient space).
An element of
{E\!/\!K}
is denoted
{\dot{x}}=x+K
.
In particular
\dot{0}=K
.

The (usual) operators
+
in
{E\!/\!K}
and
.
on
{E\!/\!K}
are defined by, if
{\dot{x}}=x+K
,
{\dot{y}}=y+K
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

{\dot{x}}+{\dot{y}}=x+y+K,\quad\hbox{and}\quad\lambda.{\dot{x}}=\lambda x+K,

(8.13)

definition independent of the
x^{\prime}\in{\dot{x}}
and
y^{\prime}\in{\dot{y}}
(easy check).
Then
({E\!/\!K},+,.)
is a linear space (easy check) with
\dot{0}
the zero in
{E\!/\!K}
.

Lemma 8.9

The canonical map
\pi:\left\{\hbox{}\,\vbox{\halign{\strut\hfil$\displaystyle{#}$ & $\displaystyle{{}#}$\hfil\cr E&\rightarrow{E\!/\!K}\cr x&\rightarrow\pi(x):=x+K={\dot{x}}\cr}}\,\right\}
is linear and surjective.

Proof.
Linearity:
\pi(x+\lambda y)=(x+\lambda y)+K=x+K+\lambda y+\lambda K=\pi(x)+\lambda\pi(y)
since
K
is a linear space (so
K=K+\lambda K
).

Surjectivity: If
{\dot{x}}\in{E\!/\!K}
then
\exists x\in E
s.t.
{\dot{x}}=x+K=\pi(x)
(definition of
{E\!/\!K}
).

For
{\dot{x}}\in{E\!/\!K}
, define
||.||_{E\!/\!K}:{E\!/\!K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

||{\dot{x}}||_{E\!/\!K}=||\pi(x)||_{E\!/\!K}:=\inf_{x_{0}\in K}||x+x_{0}||_{E}{\;\mathop{=}^{\hbox{\footnotesize denoted}}\;}||x||_{E\!/\!K}.

(8.14)

Lemma 8.10

||.||_{E\!/\!K}
is a norm in
{E\!/\!K}
, and
({E\!/\!K},||.||_{E\!/\!K})

And
\pi
is continuous with
||\pi||\leq 1
.

Proof.
||{\dot{x}}||_{E\!/\!K}=0

\Leftrightarrow

\inf_{x_{0}\in K}||x+x_{0}||_{E}=0

\Leftrightarrow

||x||_{E}\leq 0
since
0\in K

\Leftrightarrow

x=0

\Leftrightarrow

\pi(x)=0
(since

is linear)

\Leftrightarrow

{\dot{x}}=K=\dot{0}
.

||\lambda{\dot{x}}||_{E\!/\!K}=\inf_{x_{0}\in K}||\lambda x+x_{0}||_{E}=\inf_{x_{0}\in K}||\lambda x+\lambda x_{0}||_{E}=\inf_{x_{0}\in K}|\lambda|\,||x+x_{0}||_{E}|\lambda|\,||{\dot{x}}||_{E\!/\!K}

.

Let

||\pi(x)||_{E\!/\!K}=\min_{x_{0}\in K}||x+x_{0}||_{E}

{\vec{u}}_{i}^{T}.{\vec{u}}_{j}={{\vec{v}}_{i}^{T}.(B^{T}.B{\vec{v}}_{j})\over\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}=\lambda_{j}{{\vec{v}}_{i}^{T}.{\vec{v}}_{j}\over\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}=\delta_{ij}
,

Let

be the
m*m

Corollary A.4

Let
k\leq r{-}1
and
B_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}{\vec{u}}_{i}.{\vec{v}}_{i}^{T}
.
Then

\displaystyle\min_{Z:{\rm Rang}Z=k}||B-Z||=\sigma_{k+1}=||B-B_{k}||,

where

||Z||=\sup_{{\vec{x}}\neq\vec{0}}{||Z.{\vec{x}}||_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\over||{\vec{x}}||_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
is the usual norm.

This gives a numerical measure of the rank of
B
:
If
\sigma_{k+1}
is of the precision order of the computer,
then the numerical rank o

is

Proof.
We get

(easy check).

Thus
U^{T}.(B-B_{k}).V={\rm diag}_{m,n}(0,...,0,\sigma_{k+1},...,\sigma_{r},0,...)
,
thus
||B-B_{k}||=\sigma_{k+1}
, and in particular
\displaystyle\min_{Z:{\rm Rang}Z=k}||B-Z||\leq\sigma_{k+1}
.

Let
Z
be a
m*n
matrix with rank
k
. Thus
\dim{\rm Ker}Z=n-k

Let

. So
\dim(E)\geq 1

(intersection of a dimension

space with a dimension

\mathbb{R}^{n}
).

Let
{\vec{x}}\in E

||{\vec{x}}||_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}=1

||(B{-}Z).{\vec{x}}||_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}=||B.{\vec{x}}||_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}=||\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sigma_{i}({\vec{v}}_{i}^{T}.{\vec{x}}){\vec{u}}_{i}||^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sigma_{i}^{2}({\vec{v}}_{i}^{T}.{\vec{x}})^{2}
,

\displaystyle\min_{Z:{\rm Rang}Z=k}||B-Z||\geq\sigma_{k+1}

Application: The discrete inf-sup condition

Example of
{\rm div}{\vec{u}}=0
, corresponding to
B
a rectangular
m*n
matrix
(computation of
b({\vec{v}}_{h},q_{h})=0
).

Here
B^{T}
stands for
[B_{h}]
, cf. (
1.12
),
and we compute the singular values of
B^{T}
,
i.e. the eigenvalues of
\pmatrix{0&B^{T}\cr B&0}
. We get:

Proposition B.1

Let
\sigma_{r}>0
be the smallest positive eigenvalue of
B
.
We have (value of the inf-sup constant)

\inf_{q_{h}\in Q_{h}}\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}{b(v_{h},q_{h})\over||v_{h}||_{V}||q_{h}||_{Q}}=\sigma_{r}.

Proof.
B=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}{\vec{u}}_{i}.{\vec{v}}_{i}^{T}
gives

B.{\vec{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}({\vec{v}}_{i}^{T}.{\vec{x}})\,{\vec{u}}_{i}
, so

{\vec{y}}^{T}.B.{\vec{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}({\vec{v}}_{i}^{T}.{\vec{x}})\,({\vec{y}}^{T}.{\vec{u}}_{i})
.

Let
{\vec{x}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x^{j}{\vec{v}}_{j}

and
{\vec{y}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}y^{k}{\vec{u}}_{k}
. Thus

{\vec{y}}^{T}.B.{\vec{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}x^{i}\,y^{i}
.

Thus for
||x||=1
, and
{\vec{y}}
being fixed with
||{\vec{y}}||=1
,
the sup is given by
x^{i}={\sigma_{i}y^{i}\over(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2})^{1\over 2}}
,
and gives
{\vec{y}}^{T}.B.{\vec{x}}={1\over(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2})^{1\over 2}}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2}=(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2})^{1\over 2}
.
Thus the inf for
{\vec{y}}
is given with
{\vec{y}}={\vec{u}}_{r}
, and gives
{\vec{y}}^{T}.B.{\vec{x}}=\sigma_{r}
.
