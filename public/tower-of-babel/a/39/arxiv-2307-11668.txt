An Efficient Interior-Point Method for Online Convex Optimization
Elad Hazan, Nimrod Megiddo

Abstract
A new algorithm for regret minimization in online convex optimization is described. The regret of the algorithm after T T time periods is O ⁡ ( T ​ log ⁡ T ) O(\sqrt{T\log T}) - which is the minimum possible up to a logarithmic term. In addition, the new algorithm is adaptive, in the sense that the regret bounds hold not only for the time periods 1 , … , T 1,\ldots,T but also for every sub-interval s , s + 1 , … , t s,s+1,\ldots,t . The running time of the algorithm matches that of newly introduced interior point algorithms for regret minimization: in n n -dimensional space, during each iteration the new algorithm essentially solves a system of linear equations of order n n , rather than solving some constrained convex optimization problem in n n dimensions and possibly many constraints.

Introduction.

In this paper we present an interior point algorithm for the general online learning framework of online convex optimization with computational and prediction advantages compared to the state of the art.
The setting of online convex optimization (OCO) is a widely considered framework for online learning capable of modeling a host of prediction problems such as portfolio selection, online routing, prediction from expert advice and more

1

we refer the reader to the book and survey
[

.

In online convex optimization a decision maker makes a sequence of choices, which are represented as points in a convex set in Euclidean space. After revealing her decision, the adversary presents the decision maker with a cost function that determines the cost of the decision maker in this particular round. The goal of the decision maker is to minimize regret, or the difference in cost between her aggregate loss and the loss of the best fixed decision in hindsight.

The literature of online learning extensively covers polynomial-time algorithms for OCO. Essentially all known algorithms for OCO are variants of the online gradient descent algorithm
[
18
]
and its generalizations to mirror-decent
[
4
,
16
,
10
]
with various Bregman regularizations. The structure of such algorithms is similar: a gradient step is taken with respect to the previous cost function followed by a projection to the convex decision set, as depicted in figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Gradient-based vs. new Interior point algorithm

The computation of projections into convex sets can be carried out in polynomial time under reasonable assumptions
2
2

2

i.e. under assumption that the underlying convex decision set is equipped with a membership oracle, see
[
8
]
for more details.
. However, in many scenarios the computation of projections are computationally prohibitive and constitute the most time consuming operation in OCO algorithms. In this paper we present an interior point algorithm for online convex optimization that never leaves the underlying convex set without sacrificing the near-optimal regret guarantees of existing gradient-based methods.

Our interior point algorithm, depicted in Figure
1
, is also an iterative algorithm. Every iteration it moves in a direction based on the gradient of the previous cost function. However, instead of moving exactly in the direction of the gradient, which may take us outside of the decision set, the new algorithm first
linearly-transforms the gradient according to a self-concordant barrier function of the decision set
. The resulting direction and new point are guaranteed to be inside the decision set.
Thus, the need for projections is completely removed, and replaced by a linear transformation which is much more efficient to compute.

1.1
Formal problem statement

Online convex optimization.

In the
Online Convex Optimization problem

[
18
]
, an adversary
picks a sequence of convex functions
f_{t}:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
,
t=1,2,\ldots,T
,
where
\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is convex, compact and bounded.
At stage
t
, the player has to pick an
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{K}
without knowing the function
f_{t}
.
The player then incurs a cost of
f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
.
The setting in the paper is that after choosing
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, the player is informed of the entire
function
f_{t}
over
\mathcal{K}
.
The total cost to the player is
\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
. Online Convex Optimization encompasses, for example, experts algorithms

[
14
]
with arbitrary convex loss function,
the problem of universal portfolio optimization
[
6
]
, online routing
[
3
]
and more. The reader is referred to
[
8
]
for a survey of recent applications in machine learning.

Regret minimization.

Suppose the minimum cost over all possible single choices is attained at some

\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(\mathbf{x})
.
In this case the
regret
resulting from the choices
(\mathbf{x}_{1};f_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T};f_{T})
is defined as

R(\mathbf{x}^{*})=R(\mathbf{x}_{1};f_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T};f_{T})~\equiv~\mbox{$\sum_{t=1}^{T}$}\left[f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-f_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{*})\right]~.

The problem of regret minimization calls for choosing the points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}
so as to minimize
R
, subject to the
condition that, when
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
is chosen, only
\mathbf{x}_{1},f_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t},f_{t}
are known.
It is known that, in the worst case, the minimum possible regret is
\Omega(\sqrt{T})
(see e.g.
[
5
]
or
[
9
]
footnote on page 7.).

Computational efficiency.

Until recently, known algorithms that attain minimum possible regret
require that in each stage the algorithm solve some constrained convex optimization problem over
\mathcal{K}
,
which can be prohibitive in some practical applications.
In particular, if
\mathcal{K}
is a convex polyhedron, the best known worst-case bound
3
3

3

See, for example,
[
17
]
.
on the number of
iterations of an optimization algorithm is
O(\sqrt{n}\,L)
, where
L
is the number of bits in the description of
\mathcal{K}
,
and each iteration requires solving a linear system of order
n
.

Recently,
[
2
]
introduce a novel interior point method which is much more efficient. Building upon their work, we propose a new method for constructing an almost-minimum-regret algorithm, which requires in each stage only solving
a system of linear equations of order
n
, rather than solving an optimization problem over
\mathcal{K}
.
Thus, this method improves the running time at least by a factor

\sqrt{n}
, and much more than that when
\mathcal{K}
is more complicated, for example, a convex polyhedron with many facets.
In contrast to the algorithm of
[
2
]
, our algorithm is provably “adaptive” in the sense that its regret is almost the minimum possible not only over the
stages
1,\ldots,T
but also over every sub-interval of stages
s,s+1,\ldots,t
. In addition, it does not require an initial computation of the analytic center of the convex set.

1.2
Previous Algorithms

There are numerous algorithms for Online Convex Optimization, some of which attain the minimum possible regret of
O(\sqrt{T})
.
Most of these algorithms can be classified into the following two classes:
(
i
)
link-function
algorithms, developed in
[
7
,
13
]
,
and
(
ii
)
regularized follow-the-leader
algorithms (see
[
8
]
for bibliography).

Follow-The-Regularized-Leader algorithms.

The intuitive “Follow-The-Leader”

(FTL) algorithm picks for
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
a minimizer
of the function
F_{t}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\sum_{s=1}^{t}f_{s}(\mathbf{x})
over
\mathcal{K}
.
It is folklore that the regret of FTL is not optimal
4
4

4

This can be seen by taking
\mathcal{K}=[-1,1]
and
f_{t}
to alternate between
f_{t}(x)=x
or
f_{t}=-x
. FTL will alternate between
-1
and
1
, always suffering maximal loss. In contrast - staying with either point in the long run attains half the loss of FTL. See also
[
8
]
page 6.
.
This fact suggested the more general “Follow-The-Regularized-Leader” (FTRL) algorithm that picks

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
as a minimizer of the function
F_{t}(\mathbf{x})+\rho(\mathbf{x})
over
\mathcal{K}
,
where
\rho(\mathbf{x})
is a certain function that serves as a “regularizer”.
Different variants of the method correspond to different choices of
\rho(\mathbf{x})
.
The FTRL approach led to the resolution of some prediction problems, notably the resolution of
the value of bandit information in
[
2
]
.
One advantage of the FTRL approach is its relatively intuitive analysis due to Kalai and Vempala
[
12
]
.
On the negative side, FTRL algorithms are known to be “non-adaptive”, in the sense that the
regret over a general sub-interval
s,s+1,\ldots,t
may be linear in
t-s
rather
than
O(\sqrt{t-s})

[
11
]
. Furthermore, the running time of the algorithm in each stage may not
be practical because the algorithm has to solve
some optimization problem over
\mathcal{K}
.

The “link function” methodology.

In contrast to the intuitive FTRL methodology, which relies on the entire past of history of the play,
link-function algorithms, as developed in
[
7
,
13
]
, use less information and proceed “incrementally”. They are also known as “primal-dual” or “online mirrored descent” algorithms, in analogy to their offline counterparts - mirrored descent algorithms for convex optimization
[
1
]
.
Perhaps the easiest algorithm to describe is Zinkevich’s
[
18
]
online gradient descent, which picks
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
to be
the orthogonal projection of the point
\mathbf{y}_{t+1}\equiv\mathbf{x}_{t}-\eta\nabla f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
into
\mathcal{K}
.
Of course,
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
is the point in
\mathcal{K}
nearest
\mathbf{y}_{t+1}
, hence its computation can be costly, for example, if
\mathcal{K}
has many facets.
On the other hand, link-function algorithms are adaptive (in the sense explained above)
and are usually more efficiently computable than FTRL algorithms in case projections turn out to be easy.

Merging the two approaches.

The main idea in the new algorithm is to follow the incremental-update approach, but make sure it
never requires projections from the exterior of
\mathcal{K}
into
\mathcal{K}
(hence the name “interior point”).
This is accomplished by moving from
\mathbf{x}_{t}
to
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
in a direction that is obtained from the
gradient of
\nabla f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
by a linear transformation (like in Newton’s method),
which depends on
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbf{x}_{t}
.
The assumption is that
\mathcal{K}
is specified by means of a self-concordant barrier function (see below).
This idea was introduced to learning theory by Abernethy, Hazan and Rakhlin
[
2
]
, who used these barriers as regularizers.
Our technique can be interpreted as
using the barrier function as a link function rather than a regularizer
.

1.3
The new contribution compared to previous work

The contribution of this paper is the design and analysis of a new algorithm for online
convex optimization. The regret of the algorithm is almost the minimum possible.
It is adaptive and requires only solving one system of linear equations of order
n
per stage.
In comparison to previous work, most other minimum-regret algorithms require, in the worst case, solving a complete optimization problem each iteration.
The one exception which matches this running time is
[
2
, Algorithm 2]
.
It has minimum regret and its computation complexity is close to that of our algorithm.
However,
[
2
, Algorithm 2]
is not adaptive. In addition, it requires computing of the so-called analytic center of
\mathcal{K}
for the starting point
\mathbf{x}_{1}
, which requires solving a nontrivial optimization problem, and as originally defined works only for linear cost functions (although this is correctable by taking the gradient approximation of the loss functions).
We prove that an efficient interior point algorithm can be derived via the “link function” methodology together with tools from interior point methods. Previously, such results were proven only from the “follow the leader” methodology.

Algorithm and regret bounds

3.1
The algorithm and its performance.

We assume in this section that when the player has to pick the next point
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
,
the player recalls
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and knows
\nabla f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
and
\nabla^{2}\beta(\mathbf{x}_{t})
.
Interior-point algorithms for optimization typically utilize the Newton direction.
In the case of minimizing a function of the form

F_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})\equiv f(\mathbf{x})-\mu\cdot\beta(\mathbf{x})
,
while at a point
\mathbf{x}
, the newton direction would be

\mathbf{n}=-[\nabla^{2}F_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})]^{-1}\nabla(F_{\mu})(\mathbf{x})
.
However, for minimum-regret online optimization, it turns out that the following direction is
useful:

\mathbf{n}_{t}=-[\nabla^{2}\beta(\mathbf{x}_{t})]^{-1}\nabla f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})~,

i.e., the gradient factor is determined by the previous objective function
f_{t}
,
while the Hessian factor is determined by the barrier function
\beta
. This is reminiscent to the online gradient descent algorithm of
[
18
]
, in which only the gradient of the last cost function is used, although we also use the linear transformation defined by the barrier. The standard Newton direction is not used since our objective in online convex optimization is to minimize regret rather than reach a global optimum.

Thus, when our algorithm is employed, the player picks

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}+\eta\,\mathbf{n}_{t}
,
where
0<\eta<1
is a scalar whose value depends on
T
; it tends to zero as
T
tends to infinity.
Denote

\mathbf{g}_{t}=\nabla f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

and

\mathbf{H}_{t}=\nabla^{2}\beta(\mathbf{x}_{t})
.
Hence,

\mathbf{n}_{t}=-\mathbf{H}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{g}_{t}
.

3.3
A bound on the gradients.

As with any first-order method, the smoothness of the objective function, or alternatively an upper bound on the gradient size, plays a role in its convergence rate. The convexity and boundedness properties of the loss functions also affect regret minimization, and thus we wish to express our bound on the regret with
respect to bounds on the gradients of the functions selected by the adversary.
Thus, we denote

G=\max\left\{\|\nabla f_{t}(\mathbf{x})\|\,:\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K},\ t=1,\ldots,T\right\}~.

Since the player does not know the function
f_{t}
at the time of picking
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, and
that choice depends on
G
, we simply assume that the adversary is restricted to
choosing only functions
f
such that
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|\leq G
for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}
.

We note that standard techniques, in particular the “doubling trick”, can be used,
without harming our asymptotic regret bounds, to eliminate the requirement
that the algorithm knows an upper bound
G
a priori. Roughly speaking, this simple technique assumes an a-priory fixed upper bound on
G
. If during the repeated game this bound is exceeded, then the assumed upper bound is doubled. It can be shown that the overall regret forms a geometric series, and this way our results can be extended to the case that
G
is not known in advance.
