Abstract
Despite the popularity of conditional cash transfers in low- and middle-income countries, evidence on their long-term effects remains scarce. This study assesses the impact of Ecuador’s Human Development Grant on the formal sector labour market outcomes of children in eligible households. This grant, one of the first of its kind, is characterized by weak enforcement of its eligibility criteria. Using a regression discontinuity design, we find that the programme had no overall impact on formal employment rates and labour income earned in the formal sector around 15 years after exposure, and thus not affecting the intergenerational transmission of poverty. We only document a positive effect on the non-mestizo population (mainly consisting of indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorians). Keywords: conditional cash transfers, long-term effects, formal labour market, employment, labour income. JEL classification: I38, J21. J32, J25, J46.

Introduction

In 1997, Santiago Levy, Mexico’s deputy finance minister, sought to overhaul the government’s anti-poverty approach. Existing programmes provided assistance to the poor mainly in the form of food subsidies. Levy argued that these programmes were ineffective and inefficient. Levy based his reasoning on a central tenet of Economics that states that direct cash transfers are more effective in improving the welfare of the poor than subsidies for specific consumer goods. On this basis, Levy proposed the first conditional cash transfer (CCT) programme,
Progresa
, later renamed
Oportunidades
. The most salient feature of CCTs is that, to be eligible for direct cash payments, recipients must meet requirements related to child school attendance, medical check-ups ot the like.

Since Mexico launched
Progresa
, many countries at different levels of development on all five continents have introduced these types of programmes. CCTs have three objectives. In the short term, they seek to alleviate the financial constraints of the poor. In the medium term, their main objective is to improve the human capital (health and education) of the poor. Finally, they aim to reduce poverty in the long run by improving labour market participation and earnings. Therefore, the most distinctive long-term feature of CCTs is their objective to improve the economic outcomes of the next generation, thereby preventing the transmission of poverty to the next cohort.

Paradoxically, while numerous studies highlight the short-term benefits of CCTs, particularly for school enrolment, evidence on their long-term impact remains limited and inconclusive. Furthermore, some voices have warned against the common practice—and the potential bias associated with it—of rarely conducting long-term follow-ups on social interventions when their short-run effects are small
(
Leight 2022a
)
, as has sometimes been the case with CCT programmes.

This paper examines the impact of one of the pioneering programmes of its kind, the Human Development Grant (HDG) launched in 2003 in Ecuador—where enforcement of the grant’s eligibility criteria has historically been very weak—, on the future work outcomes of young adults eligible for the programme when they were children. Combining information from poverty censuses (the specific databases used to administer the programme) and social security records in a regression discontinuity design (RDD), we estimate the local intention-to-treat (ITT) effect of the HDG on the employment probability and the labour income earned in the formal economy in 2024 by eligible individuals in 2008/2009. Therefore, we assess the impact of being a potential recipient of the programme approximately 15 years after of eventual eligibility.

Overall, our findings suggest that the HDG does not appear to be fulfilling its long-term objectives. A child who was eligible for HDG in 2008/2009 had no different outcomes in the formal labour market (in terms of employment or earnings) 15 years later than an ineligible one. To understand the reasons for this non-positive outcome, we refer to previous studies documenting the grant’s relatively limited contribution to human capital formation and discuss possible explanations, emphasising the limited capacity of the formal economy to absorb workers. We only observe a positive effect on both outcomes in the non-mestizos, predominantly made up of indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorians. This impact is most likely due to these populations starting from a much more disadvantaged position, and we refer to several previous studies documenting an income effect of the HDG only among the most deprived groups.

We contribute to the literature in two different ways. First and most importantly, our work adds to the limited number of existing studies on the long-term impact of CCTs
(
Molinaán et al. 2019a
)
. Second, given the weak enforcement of requirements to receive the subsidy, it provides additional evidence to weigh in the debate on the relevance of conditionality in these programmes
(
Baird et al. 2013a
;
Baird et al. 2014a
)
.

Background and related literature

Conditional cash transfers have represented the spearhead of anti-poverty policies in Latin America and the Caribbean and other low- and middle-income regions over the last two decades. After the pioneering Mexican and Brazilian experiences, the HDG, in its current version, was rolled out as one of the first programmes of this kind operating in the hemisphere
(
Cecchini & Madariaga 2011a
;
Rawlings 2005a
;
Villatoro 2005a
)
. Similarly to most benefits of this kind, in addition to targeting socially disadvantaged households, the payment of a monthly monetary sum is conditional on families meeting certain criteria related to children’s school enrolment and attendance of medical check-ups. Nevertheless, as we explain in the next section, one idiosyncratic feature of the Ecuadorian programme is that the enforcement of those requirements has been (at best) quite weak.

These programmes aim to improve the lives of households experiencing deprivation and to reduce future poverty. The conditions attached to the benefits are aimed at encouraging human capital accumulation—particularly among children—to prevent the intergenerational transmission of poverty
(
Cecchini & Madariaga 2011a
;
Fiszbein et al. 2009a
)
. According to
Fiszbein et al. 2009a
, the rationale for imposing eligibility conditions follows from two different considerations. The first is the assumption that parental investment in children’s human capital would be suboptimal in the absence of such requirements, mainly because of the existence of positive externalities related to education and health and to information problems (e.g., about the returns to human capital), principal–agent problems (incomplete altruism or even conflicts between the two parents) or behavioural elements (excessively high discount rates on the part of parents). Political economy considerations are relevant as well. Demanding that poor households meet certain criteria to access the targeted benefits aims to foster greater acceptance of such programmes among taxpayers than might be the case if the cash transfers were unconditional.

The literature that assesses the performance of these types of programmes is notably abundant
\parentext
see, among many others,
Baird et al. 2014a
and
Parker et al. 2007a
. The case of the Ecuadorian programme has received much attention from the research community. In addition to establishing its relevance for poverty alleviation
(
Fiszbein et al. 2009a
;
Ordóñez et al. 2015a
;
World Bank 2018a
)
, this body of work has highlighted the positive impact of the programme on school enrolment—see
Ponce 2023a
for a survey—and a reduction in child labour
(
Martínez & Rosero 2012a
;
Edmonds & Schady 2012a
;
Schultz 2004a
)
. Overall, the grant does not appear to have significantly affected child development
(
Paxson & Schady 2010a
;
Ponce & Bedi 2010a
;
Fernald & Hidrobo 2011a
)
, health
(
Fernald & Hidrobo 2011a
)
or adult labour supply
(
Bosch & Schady 2019a
)
.

According to the discussion of
Barrientos 2012a
on the overall benefits of social protection, cash transfers of this kind can curb the intergenerational transmission of poverty through several channels. By alleviating credit constraints, they can contribute to human capital formation
(
Baird et al. 2013a
;
Baird et al. 2014a
;
Parker et al. 2007a
)
. Furthermore, they may foster investment in durable assets that result in future income streams
(
Martínez 2005a
;
Maluccio 2010a
;
Gertler et al. 2012a
;
Blattman et al. 2020a
;
Gelders & Bailey-Athias 2019a
)
. Relatedly, in some cases, mainly depending on the recipient (e.g., if only mothers receive the grant), social protection benefits can alter household resource allocation such that families spend more on goods and services that specifically serve children’s interests
(
Attanasio et al. 2010a
;
Attanasio & Lechene 2014a
;
Angelucci & Attanasio 2013a
;
Bergolo & Galván 2018a
;
Macours et al. 2012a
;
Schady & Rosero 2008a
)
.

The number of studies on the long-term impact of these programmes is quite limited
(
Molinaán et al. 2019a
)
but offers some grounds for optimism. Recent works taking advantage of experimental or quasi-experimental designs find that child eligibility for a conditional cash transfer improves educational attainment and labour market outcomes at a later age in Mexico
(
Kugler & Rojas 2018a
;
Parker & Vogl 2023a
;
Araujo & Macours 2021a
)
, Brazil
(
Laguinge et al. 2024a
)
, Honduras
(
Molinaán et al. 2020a
)
, Nicaragua
(
Barham et al. 2024a
)
and Colombia
(
García et al. 2012a
)
. Furthermore, this literature suggests additional benefits in the latter two countries (e.g., positive effects on cognitive development and health behaviours)
(
Barham et al. 2013a
;
García et al. 2012a
)
.

Three of these works
(
Barham et al. 2024a
;
Kugler & Rojas 2018a
;
García et al. 2012a
)
specifically refer to the impact on labour market formalization, the main issue on which we provide evidence. Moreover, the results of these studies are not totally conclusive. In some studies, the results are only significant for some population groups and their findings are contradictory. For instance,
Barham et al. 2024a
report only a positive impact on men, while other studies find the opposite, only a relevant effect on women
(
Parker & Vogl 2023a
;
García et al. 2012a
)
, or fail to find any significant effect on labour market outcomes
(
Filmer & Schady 2014a
;
Araujo et al. 2018a
)
.

Using data from a randomised controlled trial and a RDD,
Araujo et al. 2018a
study the impact of the Ecuadorian HDG on test scores, educational attainment and work outcomes at a horizon of approximately ten years among children who lived in households eligible for the transfer. They report much more nuanced results than those for other Latin American countries. Their findings indicate that early-childhood exposure did not improve test scores 10 years later, a positive effect on secondary school completion and a positive impact, albeit non-robust, on female employment. A related work
(
Mideros & Gassmann 2021a
)
evaluates social mobility in terms of a composite welfare index (based on a set of family assets and characteristics) among Ecuadorian households, leveraging the administrative registry used for targeting the grant (which, by its own design, suffers from high attrition rates, well above 50%). Employing a difference-in-differences strategy, this paper concludes that HDG eligibility enhances absolute and relative social mobility. Note that this study explores how a household’s own socio-economic status evolves over time rather than whether the grant actually pushes the next generation forward by improving its well-being in adulthood.

Our work aims to contribute to this still limited body of literature by providing additional evidence on the Ecuadorian case. Specifically, we focus on the likelihood of working in the formal economy and on labour income earned in this sector (unconditional on labour force participation) in February 2024, among individuals who were eligible for the HDG in 2008/2009..

It is worth highlighting the existence of substantial differences between the second analysis carried out by
Araujo et al. 2018a
—based on a RDD and centred on educational attainment and labour market outcomes—and ours, which justifies this research. First, while
Araujo et al. 2018a
centre their analysis on the 19–25 age group, our research focuses on individuals aged 26–31, who are much more likely to have completed their transition from school to work (and, therefore, the risks associated with underestimating the programme’s positive effects on employability are lower). For example, in the first quarter of 2024, the proportion of employed individuals aged 19–25 and 26–31 was roughly 28% and 10%, respectively
(
National Institute of Statistics and Censuses of Ecuador 2025c
)
. In fact,
Molinaán et al. 2019a
argue that the lack of impact identified in pioneering studies of the long-term effects of CCTs is due to the focus on individuals who were too young.

Second, the focus of these authors is on a different outcome (the probability of employment at a given point in time, in 2008/2009) to that considered here. In this respect, given Ecuador’s low unemployment rate of 3.5% in 2024
(
ILO 2025a
)
, it is reasonable to surmise that any eventual improvements in labour market outcomes will be in terms of job quality rather than quantity, primarily in the form of a reduction in informality, which affected to almost 70% of workers in that year.

Third, the identification strategy in
Araujo et al. 2018a
relies on eligibility for the HDG according to a poverty census from 2002/2003 (and the authors centres on outcomes in 2008/2009). We concentrate on a different time frame (from 2008/2009 to roughly 15 years later), making use of the same sort of cadastre from 2008/2009, which aimed not only to update the registry of potential beneficiaries but also to significantly improve the targeting of the grant
(
Fabara 2009a
;
Ministerio de Inclusión Económica y Social 2019a
)
. Such changes could also enhance the performance of the HDG. Also, the HDG became much more generous over time. The HDG amount was set at
15\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
US$] per month from 2003 to 2007, rose to
30\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
US$] in 2008 and then to
35\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
US$] between 2009 and 2011. Since 2012 it has stood at
50\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
US$] and from 2018 it has varied according to the number of children, reaching up to
150\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
US$]
(
ECLAC 2025a
)
.

Last but not least, their analysis is based on the merging of two poverty censuses using the identity card number (
cédula de identidad
) of generally an adult woman (who is typically the mother of the children who entitled the household to receive the HDG). In principle, this strategy has two limitations. In the first place, from 2002/2003 to 2008/2009, they can only track around 55% of the households that were present in the 2008/2009 poverty census. This approach can result in non-random sample attrition. For families that believed they were suitable candidates for receiving welfare payments, ensuring their presence in the poverty census (which households could voluntarily participate in, even if they did not receive a visit from government interviewers) was clearly urgent. However, it is reasonable to suspect that those whose economic situation had improved over time had fewer incentives to remain present in the cadastre.

Therefore, in principle, if the programme has any effect on economic success, an exploration that relies on tracking individuals across different waves of the poverty census could underestimate the effect of eligibility on socio-economic outcomes (i.e., it would be less likely to observe those individuals exhibiting better economic performance over time).
In contrast, as explained the Subsection
3.2
, in our analysis, we are able to recover the card identity number of more than 80% of the children whom we aim to follow in 2008/2009, who can potentially appear in (and be linked to) social security records around 15 years later.

In the second place,
Araujo et al. 2018a
are only able to examine the outcomes of young adults that continued to live in the household in which they were raised (and not for those who started their own household or moved into a different one). It is unlikely that the outcomes of those young adults aged 19–25 years old that are still living with their mothers can be representative of the entire population of interest. They must, in principle, have had much lower labour market attainment than those who had already left the family home.

Our study also contributes to the existing literature on the relevance of the conditionality associated with these types of benefits
(
Baird et al. 2013a
;
Baird et al. 2014a
)
. As we explain in Section
3
, the enforcement of conditionality for the Ecuadorian HDG is weak, in contrast to that for other programmes of this kind in the region. Although national authorities have always publicly announced the requirements in terms of school enrolment and medical check-ups, they have never monitored whether households actually meet them. Consequently, the HDG cannot be described as a strict
conditional
transfer. Some authors argue that the programme actually offers something between a conditional and unconditional benefit
(
Schady & Araujo 2008a
)
.

When assessing children’s future performance in the formal labour market as young adults, we think it is useful to first describe the characteristics of this in Ecuador. The definition of formality used here follows the
“legalistic”
or
“social protection”
approach
(
Gasparini & Tornarolli 2009a
)
. Formal workers are those affiliated with social security and consequently enjoy rights to certain social benefits, such as contributory old-age, survivors’ and disability pensions, maternity and sickness benefits, unemployment insurance and health care
(
Social Security Administration 2020a
)
. Informality is a multidimensional phenomenon associated with different explanatory factors (economic structure, lack of law enforcement, poor public services and burdensome regulatory frameworks). Whether participation in the informal economy is a voluntary decision is also a subject of debate
(
Loayza et al. 2009a
;
Maloney 2004a
;
Cimoli et al. 2006a
;
Portes & Schauffler 1993a
;
La & Shleifer 2014a
;
Biles 2009a
)
.

Informality in the Ecuadorian labour market is pervasive and almost endemic. This segment accounted for 72% and 69% of total employment in 2008 and 2024, respectively
(
ILO 2025a
)
. Regardless of the factors behind the existence of this sector, informal workers in Ecuador have on average much worse social outcomes than those in the formal labour market, such as lower earnings, higher poverty rates and worse future career prospects
(
Canelas 2019a
;
Matano et al. 2020a
;
Maurizio & Vásquez 2019a
;
Maurizio & Monsalvo 2021a
;
Maurizio et al. 2023a
)
. In addition, available evidence suggests that informality may result in negative externalities, too. A larger informal sector not only results in higher inequality but also negatively impacts tax collection
(
Boitano & Abanto 2019a
)
, the health status of the population
(
Utzet et al. 2021a
)
, pension coverage
(
Daude et al. 2015a
)
and levels of political participation
(
Baker & Dorr 2022a
)
. Therefore, working in the formal sector in Ecuador is undoubtedly a positive socio-economic outcome that is of unquestionable interest to policymakers.

Research design

Institutional setting

The HDG was introduced in Ecuador in 2003 as a reformulation of the Solidarity Grant, a poorly targeted earlier social benefit introduced in 1998 to provide a safety net for socially disadvantaged families. This grant aimed to compensate these households for the removal of subsidies on gas, petrol and electricity, which was part of the liberalisation and adjustment policies adopted by the country in the late 1990s. The HDG programme aspires to alleviate poverty in the short term and foster human capital formation in order to prevent the intergenerational transmission of poverty. Specifically, it targets vulnerable families with children under the age of 16.

The redesign of the programme in 2003 intended to improve its targeting. In particular, the Ecuadorian government—with technical assistance from universities—created an ad hoc poverty census to identify the most vulnerable population more accurately (in Spanish,
Sistema de Identificación y Selección de Beneficiarios de Programas Sociales
[SELBEN]). The data collection on households’ socio-economic and demographic characteristics relied on visits, public calls and the option for households to sign up for the registry on a voluntary basis and request that the government evaluate their eligibility for inclusion.

The national authorities initially developed an eligibility index (from 0 to 100, from the lowest to the highest well-being level) based on a principal component analysis of 27 household variables. Households in the first two quintiles of the index with children below 16 years old were eligible for the grant.

In principle, the programme imposes two conditions on grant recipients. The first requirement relates to education: children aged six to 18 must be enrolled in school and maintain monthly attendance of at least 80%. The second condition is that infants and children up to five years old attend a series of medical check-ups (one preventive check-up every two months for children under one year old and and every six months for children between one and five years old). Unlike other CCTs that operate in Latin America and the Caribbean, this programme has not set up any enforcement mechanism to verify that beneficiaries meet these conditions. Consequently, the Ecuadorian authorities do not suspend the benefits if families fail to comply with the requirements, meaning that the transfer is unconditional in practice. Although the Ecuadorian government does not verify compliance, families do commit in writing to satisfying the conditions, and the authorities have always publicly emphasised the need to meet the mentioned requirements.

Theoretically, disregarding the non-enforcement of conditionality, loss of eligibility (due to the presence of children or under the SELBEN index) implies benefit withdrawal. In practice, government employees regularly visit households to update the poverty census. This process might result in the suspension of the HDG. Regrettably, information on how often this occurs is scarce. For instance, the government maintains the grant as long as households have children under 18 years old. Loss of eligibility under this criterion should be easy to monitor, but updates to eligibility status can take months or years to be implemented in practice. In addition, Ecuadorian authorities tend to withdraw the grants from a large numbers of households at once rather than continuously on a case-by-case basis.

At the outset of the programme, the government committed to renewing the registry approximately every five years and, to this end, set up the so-called Social Registry 2008/2009. Administered by the Social Registry Unit, a public agency under the auspices of the Ministry of Economic and Social Inclusion of Ecuador, the Social Registry operated similarly to the SELBEN and was aimed to improve the targeting of the HDG. Since the 2008/2009 wave, the National Institute of Statistics and Censuses of Ecuador has been responsible for data collection. Using non-linear component analysis and 30 household variables, the Social Registry Unit developed a Social Registry Index, rescaled from 0 to 100, to determine eligibility for different social benefits. The government set the cut-off point at 36.5987 points. Along with the rules related to the presence of children under 16 years old, this criterion determined eligibility for the grant from August 2009 to August 2014, when the new Social Registry 2013/2014 came into force. Updating the database meant that HDG payments ceased for more than
200,000
households
(
Buser et al. 2017a
)
.

On top of being the flagship social programme of successive Ecuadorian governments for more than two decades, the HDG has also become one of the most important benefits of its kind in Latin America and the Caribbean. In 2022, it represented approximately 1% of the GDP and reached 7.6% of the population (more than 12% in 2011). The basic amount of the HDG was
11.5\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
$] per month from 2003 to 2007 (and up to
15\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
$] until 2006 and
30\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
$] in 2007 for the the 20% poorest households),
30\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
$] in 2008, and
35\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
$] in the period 2009–2011 and has been
50\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
$] since 2012
(
ECLAC 2025a
)
. The size of the transfer was considerable, bearing in mind that the average national per capita income and the average per capita income of the first two quintiles in 2008 (our departure point) were
166\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
$] and
32\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
$] per month, respectively
(
Centro de Estudios Distributivos, Laborales y Sociales & World Bank 2025a
)
. Whenever possible, the recipients are mothers, who can withdraw the benefit from private banks. In a recent change, eligible mothers can now receive the payment directly into their bank account.

Data

Our analysis leverages data from two different sources. The first is the Social Registry 2008/2009
(
Social Registry Unit 2025a
)
. As explained above, this is a cadastre administered by the Ministry of Economic and Social Inclusion that contains static socio-economic and demographic information of Ecuadorian households. It enables public institutions to determine the eligibility of beneficiaries of social benefits and achieve appropriate targeting according to the procedure described above.

The Social Registry database includes the Social Registry Index (and the component variables needed for its calculation) that governs receipt of the HDG. From August 2009 onwards, households were eligible if they scored fewer than 36.5987 points on the index. The Social Registry 2008/2009 index—hereafter referred to as the poverty index—is therefore the running variable in our analysis. Furthermore, we have access to information on the actual receipt of the HDG in 2010 according to administrative sources.

We merge these data from the Social Registry with information from the
Ecuadorian Social Security Institute 2025a
, our second data source, for February 2024. It provides data on monthly labour income reported to social security. This administrative registry allows us to track the formal sector labour market performance in that month of all individuals with a valid identity card number included in the Social Registry in 2008/2009.

With these data, we are able to identify the HDG eligibility status of children aged 15 years or younger in 2008/2009, along with their subsequent participation in, and labour income from, the formal sector once they are 26 years old and above in February 2024. In practice, these individuals were aged 10–15 at the time they could have become recipients, and 26–31 when their position in the labour market was evaluated.

2

4.2% of the individuals in our sample were interviewed in 2007, which explain why our sample contains a few individuals with 10 years old in 2008/2009 and 31 years old in 2024. The age intervals used for the various analyses throughout the paper aim to ensure that the age groups are of a similar size, despite including different numbers of years.
Our analysis focuses on those young individuals who lived in households (included in the Social Registry 2008/2009) whose poverty index at that time ranged from 30.5 to 42.5 points.

For younger individuals, high labour market participation is not necessarily a positive socio-economic outcome. Early employment may simply indicate that a young person has left the education system prematurely. Therefore, The school-to-work transition should not play a major role in our analysis. The standard age for completing higher education is 21 and, for instance, in the first quarter of 2024, roughly 73% of individuals within the age range 26–31 were employed and only around 10% of them were in education (nearly 57% of whom were already in work).

The merging of all the databases is based on the identity card number of the children. We assume that those individuals with a valid personal identity card number that were not found in the Social Security records did not participated in formal labour activities, with no formal employment and null formal labour income. Unfortunately, there is a relevant proportion of individuals (18.6%) for which the identity card number is missing. This situation can be due either to the fact that it was simply not collected by the cadastre administrators, or to the circumstance that a relevant share of children did not have a personal identity card in 2008/2009 (which was not compulsory for children at that time).

Moreover, the share of children without a personal identity card number changes significantly at the cut-off point, being higher above (25.4%) than below the threshold (13%). This circumstance obeys to two different reasons. First, the authorities put much more emphasis collecting detailed information from individuals below the cut-off (the actual potential recipients of the benefit) than from those above the cut-off. The latter group received much less attention from the Ecuadorian authorities as they were not relevant for administering the programme. Second, the grant administrators encouraged the members of the households receiving the benefit to obtain a personal identity card if they did not have one.

In principle, if the probability of not having a valid identity card number should be independent of future labour market outcomes (and the reasons for the availability of this variable suggest that it should be independent of what happens to individuals around 15 years later), the presence of missing values should not be a major problem and complete case analysis might be a reasonable option. However, the much higher incidence of not having a valid personal identity number above the threshold introduces a discontinuity in the density of the poverty index at the cut-off. We further comment on this issue in the next section.

To overcome this problem, we impute both the participation in the formal labour market and the earnings obtained in it using the hot deck method. In a nutshell, this technique consists in defining strata based on a set of categorical variables, for each respondent with a missing value of the variable(s) (the recipient), finding a respondent the (donor) within the same stratum and taking its values for the variables with missing values
(
Andridge & Little 2010a
;
Yan 2008a
)
. Some advantages of the hot deck method are efficiency gains compared to the complete case analysis, that it is less sensitive to model misspecification than approaches based on parametric models and that only plausible values can be imputed. We implement a random version of hot deck, where missing data are imputed stochastically using the approximate Bayesian bootstrap method proposed by
Rubin & Schenker 1986a
.

Our imputation makes use of a set of variables comprising covariates that are likely to affect the outcome (employment in each month) or that are potentially associated with non-response. Specifically, we consider the following variables: position in relation to the cut-off, gender, age, ethnicity (mestizos and non-mestizos), household head education, household head marital status , household size, area of residence and province. To define the strata, we include all these variables as binary categorical variables.
3
3

3

In particular, the cells are defined by the following categories: below vs. above the cut-off, males vs. females, 10–13 years old and 14–15 years old, mestizos vs. non-mestizos, household heads with up to six years of education vs. more than six years of schooling, single vs. married or in union household heads marital status, household size of less than five vs. five or more people, urban vs. rural area of residence and province dummies.
This technique produces unbiased estimates if the missing data are either missing completely at random or missing at random (the probability that of a line being missing varies only with respect to the categorical variables used for imputation). Using this method, we are able to impute virtually 100% of the observations—all but 33 out of
42.845
—with a missing identity card number. Hereafter, our calculations are based on this sample with imputed labour market outcomes.

Moreover, we present the results of our main analyses using both complete cases (i.e., children with an identity card number) and applying a multivariate imputation procedure as a robustness check. The latter approach involves two imputation equations. The first is a
logit
model in which formal employment in February 2024 is modelled as a function of the same set of variables used in the hot deck procedure, plus age squared. The second one is an ordinary least squares regression in which positive wages are regressed on the same covariates. The assumptions required to obtain unbiased estimates are the same as those underlying the hot deck method. Unlike the hot deck procedure, this approach does not require the use of binary categorical variables; it allows for the inclusion of continuous variables and canton dummies, rather than province dummies, which cannot be used in the hot deck method due to excessively small cell sizes at the canton level.

Empirical strategy

We make use of a sharp RDD to evaluate the long-run impact of the HDG. We focus on the ITT effect, using a research design that exploits the discontinuity in eligibility at the cut-off point in the Social Registry 2008/2009 index (36.5987). Most of our analyses rely on non-parametric local polynomial estimation methods using a data-driven mean squared error (MSE) optimal bandwidth
(
Calonico et al. 2019c
;
Calonico et al. 2019b
;
Cattaneo et al. 2020b
;
Cattaneo & Titiunik 2022a
)
. Our rationale for choosing this method is that global high-order polynomials (i.e., a parametric approach) lead to noisy estimates, sensitivity to the polynomial degree and poor coverage of confidence intervals
(
Gelman & Imbens 2019a
)
.

Our application of this method unfolds as follows
(
Cattaneo et al. 2020b
)
. First, we select a polynomial of order
p
and a kernel
K(\cdot)
. The standard practice is to choose
p=1
(local linear regression). We also employ
p=2
(local quadratic regression) as a robustness check. Second, we choose a bandwidth. We select a bandwidth
h
that minimizes the asymptotic MSE of the point estimator for our baseline analysis
(
Calonico et al. 2019b
)
. We also consider in our robustness checks an alternative optimal bandwidth that minimises the coverage error rate (CER) of the robust bias-corrected confidence interval.

Third, we choose a kernel function to weigh the observations in the interval of interest around the cut-off point (
c
). We select a triangular kernel,
K(u)=(1-\lvert u\rvert)\mathbb{1}(\lvert u\rvert\leq 1)
, which assigns non-negative weights to each transformed observation (centred around the cut-off and then divided by the selected bandwidth) based on the distance between the observation’s score and the cut-off. This kernel function is MSE-optimal.

Fourth, we estimate a weighted least squares regression, with the weights given by the kernel function,
K\left(\frac{X_{i}-c}{h}\right)
, of the labour market outcome variable for individual
i
at time
t
(
Y_{it}
)—an employment indicator that takes the value of one if the person is employed in the formal sector or the labour income in US$ obtained in the formal economy (unconditional on labour force participation)—on an intercept, the treatment indicator
T_{i}
(which takes the value one if the poverty index in 2008/2009 is equal to or lower than the cut-off point and zero otherwise), the polynomial on the re-centred forcing variable (
X_{i}-c
), the polynomial on the re-centred running variable interacted with the treatment indicator and the control covariates of interest (
Z_{i}
), all of them at their 2008/2009 values.

Formally, we estimate the parameter capturing the ITT effect (
\hat{\tau}
) through the following equation:

\hat{Y}_{it}=\hat{\alpha}+\hat{\tau}T_{i}+\hat{\mu}_{+}(X_{i}-c)+\hat{\mu}_{-}(X_{i}-c)T_{i}+Z_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\Theta}

(1)

We assess the precision of this point estimate using robust confidence intervals (more conservative than conventional ones)
(
Calonico et al. 2014a
)
. Within this framework, the inclusion of controls primarily aims to improve the efficiency of the estimates, rather than correcting unexpected imbalances (which would necessitate invoking parametric assumptions on the regression functions to enable extrapolation or redefining the parameter of interest)
(
Cattaneo et al. 2020b
)
.

Note that if a household was below the relevant threshold, this implies that not only it must have received the grant when the new targeting rule came into force, but also it was also very likely to have been paid for several months (or even years) until its eligibility status changed (and this was noticed by the Ecuadorian authorities), or until the Social Registry Unit updated the cadastre. Therefore, if a household was below the cut-off implies that it may have received the HDG for several years. According to the administrative registry of payments for 2010 (the only year for which we have access to this type of information), the targeting of the grant (relative to the database information, for households with an index value between 30.5 and 42.5 points) was excellent, with very low undercoverage (2.6% of households below the threshold did not receive the grant in 2010) and leakage rates (only 9.6% of families above the cut-off received payments).
4
4

4

Obviously, our figures refer only to the households in the Social Registry. Considering the entire population that is theoretically eligible for the grant, although the take-up rates of the programme are high (65% in the two poorest quintiles in 2013), travel costs, personal identity stigma and dissatisfaction with the government pose significant barriers to claiming the HDG
(
Rinehart & McGuire 2017a
)
.
Therefore, our estimate not only captures the (intrinsically interesting) ITT effect, but also provides a reasonable lower bound on the local average treatment effect.

Furthermore, we estimate the impact of receiving the grant in 2010 using a fuzzy discontinuity design strategy, where we instrument the payment with the discontinuity in take-up rates around the cut-off in the Social Registry Index 2008/2009. This approach is not free from limitations: the discontinuity in the cut-off is very likely to be correlated with receipt of the HDG not only in 2010 but also in subsequent years (for which we lack access to the relevant data). Consequently, we do not consider this strategy as capable of offering a better understanding of the long-run effects of the grants. Conversely, this approach is not superior to our exercise of estimating the ITT effect, so we present it merely as a reassuring robustness check, which yields numerically identical effects.

The source of identification of the ITT effect is the reduction in households’ likelihood of being paid the HDG upon crossing the eligibility threshold in the Social Registry Index 2008/2009. In other words, the policy application is as good as randomised in the neighbourhood of the cut-off if the research design satisfies certain conditions. The first condition is that there must be no manipulation of the forcing variable by families. It is virtually impossible for families to alter their position relative to the threshold, since the Ecuadorian authorities determined the value of the threshold only some time after constructing the entire database. In any case, we perform a manipulation test of the density discontinuity based on the local polynomial density methods proposed by
Cattaneo et al. 2018a
;
Cattaneo et al. 2020c
. The results of this test do not allow us to reject the null hypothesis of no manipulation (Figure
1
).

The second condition is that there must be no correlation between an observation being below the cut-off point and the factors affecting the labour market outcome. We assess whether there is any discontinuity in the average values of the observable covariates (measured approximately 15 years prior to the realisation of the outcome) using the specification outlined above. We find no evidence of discontinuities in these predetermined characteristics at the relevant threshold of the index (Table
2
). Therefore, we have no reason to anticipate any discontinuity in the corresponding unobservable factors at the cut-off. We repeat the same battery of tests, this time including only individuals with valid identity card numbers, and find that we cannot reject the null hypothesis of continuity at the 5% level for any of the considered covariates. This suggests that selection into the sample of people with valid identity card numbers based on unobservables is unlikely to be relevant and that our imputation procedures can perform well.

Regarding the complete case sample, we find a clear jump in the probability of having a missing identity card number and a discontinuity in the probability density at the cut-off point (see Figure
A1
and
A2
). It is important to note, however, that this latter finding can scarcely be interpreted as evidence of manipulation. Manipulation is highly unlikely by design in our setting. First, the authorities collected all information on household characteristics through interviews. Second, they constructed a poverty index using a non-linear principal component analysis approach. Finally, they chose as the threshold the value that excluded the bottom 40% of households in terms of socio-economic status. Thus, the information on households was collected prior to the determination of the cut-off, precluding any strategic behaviour by respondents.

A common threat to identification in longitudinal studies examining the impact of public policies on labour market outcomes is selective migration. While our dataset does not allow us to directly observe this phenomenon, we can offer several arguments suggesting that it is unlikely to significantly bias our results.

First, assuming that the HDG had no effect on migration, and given that we do not reject continuity in observable characteristics around the cut-off, it is plausible to expect continuity in unobservable factors as well, including geographic mobility. Second, the use of Social Security records, which constitute a nationwide administrative register, enables us to account for internal migration. Although we cannot identify who has moved, employment spells remain recorded as long as individuals are affiliated with the national system.

Third, potential bias would arise if HDG eligibility influenced international migration. The direction of such bias depends on whether the effect of the programme on migration is positive or negative. If the grant encouraged migration abroad, our RDD estimates would underestimate the programme’s impact, as emigrants cannot be linked to Social Security data and are thus implicitly assumed to have zero employment or earnings. Conversely, if the transfer discouraged migration, our estimates would be biased upwards. Although direct evidence for Ecuador is lacking, recent studies suggest that conditional cash transfers (CCTs) may increase geographic mobility
(
Araujo & Macours 2021a
;
Parker & Vogl 2023a
;
Barham et al. 2024a
)
, although the literature remains inconclusive
(see, e.g.
Angelucci 2015a
)
.

Finally, given the importance of migration in Ecuador, it is worth addressing this issue more thoroughly despite the limitations of our data. According to
Olivié et al. 2008a
, Ecuadorian emigrants who send remittances—which is the subgroup for whom data are available—tend to be more educated and originate from the wealthier regions of the country. In contrast, the HDG targets the bottom two quintiles of the poverty index, meaning that our cut-off distinguishes the poorest 40% from the rest of the population. Migration is therefore likely to be more prevalent among socio-economic groups above the threshold. Since our identification strategy relies on non-parametric local polynomial methods estimated in a narrow window around the cut-off, selective migration should not pose a major concern for the validity of our analysis.

We present our estimation results in three steps. First, we discuss the impact of a household being below the index cut-off (i.e., the ITT effect) on future labour market outcomes, presenting both a graphical illustration of the impact of the discontinuity and the econometric results. Second, we look at whether the effects differ across different groups of children. Third, we show the results of an extensive battery of robustness checks.

Heterogeneity in the effects of the programme

In order to study the impact of the HDG on different types of individuals, we stratify the sample according to several relevant dimensions, as measured by the Social Registry in 2008/2009 at the time of data collection. Table 4 and 5 show the effects of the HDG by gender, ethnicity, age in February 2024, the education level of the household head and area of residence (urban or rural).

Most of the results are consistent with the overall findings presented above. HDG eligibility during childhood did not seem to improve labour market outcomes for any group of young adults, except for the non-mestizo population, which is mainly composed of indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian individuals. These are minorities that have historically experienced more severe social disadvantages than the average population in terms of education, employment, and poverty. We also reject the hypothesis that the differences between mestizo and non-mestizo outcomes are equal to zero. The estimated effects are substantial: HDG eligibility in childhood increased the probability of working in the formal economy for non-mestizos by 4.5 percentage points (0.1 standard deviations) and raises formal labour income by over
25\text{\,}\mathrm{[}
US$] per month (0.08 standard deviations).

Interestingly, we find no difference by age. In principle, one might expect individuals exposed to the HDG earlier in life to have better outcomes, or older individuals to perform better as they are more likely to have completed the transition from education to work.

Discussion

This subsection discusses potential explanations for our findings and their possible implications. Our overall null results are consistent with those reported by
Araujo et al. 2018a
. Using data from the 2002/2003 and 2013/2014 waves of the Social Registry, these authors analyse the impact on employment rates in 2013/2014 of children who had lived in eligible households in 2002/2003. They find only negligible effects of the HDG on the probability of employment, with a modest positive impact for women in some specifications.

First, regarding the external validity of our findings, we should bear in mind that we estimate only the local ITT effects of the subsidy. In other words, our results are only relevant for households around the specific cut-off point in 2008/2009. One should not extrapolate them to families with a significantly lower socio-economic status. In fact, previous work has revealed that children living in the country’s poorest households experience the largest short-term impacts (in terms of school enrolment and health) from the HDG
(
Oosterbeek et al. 2008a
;
Paxson & Schady 2010a
;
Schady & Araujo 2008a
)
.

.

A second explanation for the limited overall results of the HDG in our setting relates to the role of both the quantity and quality of education. As discussed in Section
2
, human capital formation is the most obvious channel through which the HDG may affect long-term socio-economic outcomes. In this respect,
Schady & Araujo 2008a
find positive short-run effects of the grant on contemporaneous school enrolment of children aged 6--16.
7
7

7

Oosterbeek et al. 2008a
find a positive effect on school enrolment only for the first quintile of the poverty index and no impact around the cut-off separating the first two quintiles from the rest of the rest of the sample.
Similarly,
Araujo et al. 2018a
report a positive impact of having benefited from the grant on the probability of young adults (aged 19 to 25) having completed secondary education around the 2002/2003 Social Registry cut-off, 10 years after receipt.

While the HDG appears to exert a positive effect on the quantity of education, the existing literature is more sceptical about its impact on educational quality. In particular, using a research design similar to ours,
Ponce & Bedi 2010a
find no effect of either eligibility or receipt of the HDG on mathematics or language test scores for children aged between 5 and 16 years in the vicinity of the cut-off point between 2004 and 2005. Leveraging a randomised evaluation,
Araujo et al. 2018a
follow children who were five years of age or younger at baseline into late childhood to test for programme effects ten years later. In the same fashion, they find that early exposure to the HDG did not improve test scores. This lack of impact on the quality of education received by eligible children might explain the absence of effects on young adults’ labour market outcomes.

Third, there are other channels through which the HDG could potentially affect future labour market outcomes and for which the findings are mixed. For instance, the evidence regarding the impact of the grant on cognitive development is conflicting
(
Fernald & Hidrobo 2011a
;
Paxson & Schady 2010a
;
Ponce & Bedi 2010a
)
. With respect to health, another dimension of human capital formation, the literature on the HDG is also not particularly optimistic. It identifies modest positive effects on haemoglobin levels and receipt of deworming treatments and nutritional supplements for some segments of children only
(
Fernald & Hidrobo 2011a
;
Paxson & Schady 2010a
)
.
Buser et al. 2017a
find that losing the HDG after seven years of receipt negatively impacts children’s nutritional status, but, perhaps surprisingly, they report no positive effect of gaining access to the grant.A recent non-experimental study using county-level panel data suggests that expanding the HDG substantially reduced under-five mortality, particularly from poverty-related diseases such as malnutrition, diarrhoeal diseases, and lower respiratory tract infections
(
Moncayo et al. 2019a
)
.

Fourth, one potential factor influencing our results is the nature and dynamics of the informal sector in Ecuador. If the dualistic view of informality prevails, an increase in workers’ educational attainment does not necessarily translate into an expansion of formal employment. According to this perspective, the formal and informal sectors operate in a largely disconnected manner and exhibit significantly different productivity levels. Informality would be a by-product of the lack of dynamism in the formal economy—the engine of economic growth. This formal economy’s inability to absorb more workers could be due to various factors, ranging from international trade specialisation
(
Cimoli et al. 2006a
)
to the scarcity of highly educated entrepreneurs
(
La & Shleifer 2014a
)
, which would act as structural barriers to reducing informality.
8
8

8

The most orthodox view of informality—that employment in this sector is a voluntary choice made by rational economic agents who opt for it due to the burdensome regulations and requirements of the formal economy—could also partly account for these null results. This perspective associates informality with high inter-temporal discount rates, which would explain why some workers choose to be out of social security systems in exchange for higher take-home pay
(
Perry et al. 2007a
)
. Specifically, high discount rates, which are transmitted intergenerationally
(
Knowles & Postlewaite 2023a
)
, were assumed to be one of the characteristics of the potential beneficiaries of CCTs by their architects. Nonetheless, the plausibility of this explanation is limited by the clearly disadvantaged nature of the informal sector in Ecuador.

In a related argument,
Pritchett 2001a
asserts that increased education does not lead to higher wages if the demand for educated labour is stagnant. This circumstance could arise either because the skill intensity of the economy is not growing or because sectoral shifts—which educated workers are more adaptable to, as they are better able to manage imbalances—are not occurring quickly enough. This reasoning could apply not only to wages but more also generally to the creation of higher-quality jobs in the formal sector.

Fifth, a possible explanation relates to the fact that some young individuals may not have completed the education-to-work transition yet. Almost 28% of individuals aged 19 to 25 years were enrolled in education in the first quarter of 2024
(
National Institute of Statistics and Censuses of Ecuador 2025e
)
, with the proportion even lower among those in the first income quintiles. This figure stood at roughly 10% for those aged between 26 and 31 years, of whom more than a half were employed. Therefore, this factor—which has been cited to explain the null results of some previous studies focusing on younger individuals
(
Molinaán et al. 2019a
)
—should not play a substantial role in our context. Indeed, the age range considered in our study is quite similar to that used in recent research reporting positive effects
(
Araujo & Macours 2021a
;
Kugler & Rojas 2018a
;
Laguinge et al. 2024a
;
Parker & Vogl 2023a
)

Ultimately, the programme’s limited impact on human capital accumulation, ranging from school enrolment to health-related outcomes, may be due, at least in part, to the Ecuadorian authorities’ weak enforcement of conditionalities. Although the programme set out a series of requirements, these were not consistently implemented or enforced. This illustrates the blurred line between unconditional and conditional cash transfers, as noted by
Baird et al. 2013a
. The way these programmes work in practice often depends on more than just what is written on paper. Outcomes can vary depending on how clearly the rules are communicated to recipients, how strongly the authorities emphasise the importance of schooling, how closely the conditions are monitored and whether people believe there are real consequences for not meeting the requirements.

In this regard, it is more appropriate to conceptualise transfer programmes along a spectrum—from fully unconditional transfers to strictly conditional schemes. Existing empirical evidence suggests that conditionality exerts a causal influence on the short-term efficacy of cash transfers
(
Baird et al. 2013a
)
. By extension, it is plausible that long-term effects are similarly contingent upon the degree of enforcement.
9
9

9

According to the classification framework proposed by
Baird et al. 2013a
, Ecuador’s HDG ranks relatively low in terms of conditionality when compared to other CCT programmes in Latin America and the Caribbean, several of which have been associated with positive long-term impacts. The authors distinguish seven programme types based on the presence and enforcement of educational conditions: (0) UCTs unrelated to children or education; (1) UCTs targeting children with an explicit educational objective; (2) UCTs implemented within an educational framework; (3) CCTs featuring explicit conditions or educational encouragement, but lacking monitoring or enforcement; (4) CCTs with explicit conditions that are imperfectly monitored and minimally enforced; (5) CCTs with monitoring and enforcement of enrolment conditions; and (6) CCTs with monitoring and enforcement of school attendance requirements. Within this typology, the Ecuadorian HDG is classified as a type 3 programme—characterised by formal conditions or educational encouragement without any meaningful monitoring or enforcement mechanisms. In contrast, countries within the region that have exhibited more substantial long-term impacts in prior studies are generally those with higher conditionality scores. For instance, Honduras’
Programa de Asignación Familiar II
is rated at level 5; Nicaragua’s
Red de Protección Social
at level 6; and Mexico’s
Progresa
at level 4. Interestingly, Brazil’s
Bolsa Escola
—which has demonstrated significant long-term effects on labour market outcomes—is placed in the same category as the HDG (Type 3), while its successor,
Bolsa Familia
, receives a higher score of 4. Meanwhile, Colombia’s
Familias en Acción
, despite being assigned the highest conditionality score (6), has shown relatively limited long-term impact, underscoring the complexity of the relationship between programme design and outcomes.
Although no experimental or quasi-experimental studies have been conducted specifically in the Ecuadorian context, prior research indicates that the observed positive effects on educational enrolment are largely confined to households that perceive the government as effectively monitoring compliance
(
Schady & Araujo 2008a
)
.

As mentioned above, we find robust positive effects on social security affiliation among non-mestizo population, mainly composed of indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian populations. We discuss several hypothetical explanations for these findings.

First, while prior research find more short-run benefits of the programme in rural areas
\parentext
see, for instance,
Fernald & Hidrobo 2011a
and
Paxson & Schady 2010a
, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study examines of short-term outcomes by stratifying its results by ethnicity. The eventual existence of above-average positive effects in the short term could also account for the long-run benefits identified in this research.

Second, as is well known, indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian people have been historically excluded from the development process in Ecuador
(
Larrea & Montenegro 2006a
;
Rangel 2008a
)
. Poverty levels among these groups are substantially higher than the national average. Similarly, education and health indicators are considerably below the national average. In terms of employment, informality is more prevalent among these populations. For example, in 2008, social security affiliation among the mestizo population aged 26–31 was 43%, compared to only 34% among the non-mestizo individuals within the same age range
(
National Institute of Statistics and Censuses of Ecuador 2025d
)
. In this context, the positive impact on their labour market outcomes in the formal economy could be explained by the fact that these groups, mainly consisting of indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorians, start from a much more disadvantaged positions than mestizos. The HDG may therefore be more likely to alleviate credit and liquidity constraints among this ethnic group.

Although evidence for Ecuador is lacking, in this line,
Bando et al. 2005a
argue that the worse starting point of indigenous groups in terms of school enrolment and child labour helps to explain how Mexico’s
Progresa
contributes to narrowing the ethnic gap in these areas.The Ecuadorian economy may therefore have a greater capacity to absorb these populations into the formal sector; improvements in education and health brought about by the HDG may be particularly effective in enabling these groups to participate more fully in the labour market.

In a related argument, families in marginalised communities might have had lower initial expectations regarding children’s futures. CCTs could shift parental attitudes towards long-term investments in education and reduce intergenerational poverty traps, with stronger impacts among groups for whom these traps are deeper
(
Attanasio & Kaufmann 2014a
;
Behrman et al. 2011a
)
.

Last, given that conditionality is arguably weak, our analysis of the Ecuadorian case can likely only assess the income effect of the CCT programme. In this context, the income effect of the HDG may be particularly effective for these population groups, owing to their historically low levels of social development. For instance, as mentioned above,
Paxson & Schady 2010a
find that the HDG had a positive effect on health indicators exclusively among extremely poor children in rural areas, with no significant effects at the aggregate level. Similarly,
Oosterbeek et al. 2008a
observe that, in the case of education, the income effect tends to be strongest among the poorest and diminishes as income rises, becoming negligible for families around the second quintile of the poverty index. It is plausible that a comparable pattern applies to historically excluded groups relative to the rest of the population.

Conclusion

Despite having become flagship social programmes in several countries, empirical evidence on the impact of CCTs on long-term outcomes—those more aligned with their core objectives than the short-term effects—remains scarce. By targeting poor households with children and promoting human capital formation, policymakers expect these social benefits to improve young adults’ income-generating capacity and, in turn, enhance the well-being of future generations.

This paper has addressed this gap by providing a rigorous assessment of the effect of Ecuador’s HDG on the future outcomes in the formal labour market of eligible children. Focusing on individuals aged 26–31, around 15 years after their initial eligibility, we have found that the overall impact of the transfer is null; i.e., it does not mitigate the intergenerational transmission of poverty by increasing future formal employment or wages. These findings are not at odds with those of
Araujo et al. 2018a
, who evaluate other long-term outcomes such as test scores and employment. Interestingly, however, we have identified a robust positive effect only among the non-mestizo population, mainly composed of indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian individuals.

We have discussed several potential explanations for these findings, including the lack of robust evidence on the HDG’s effects beyond school enrolment, the potential limitations of weak conditionality enforcement and the limited capacity of the formal economy to absorb workers. We have interpreted the positive effect among non-mestizo groups as resulting from their more disadvantaged starting point, enabling them to benefit more from the income effect of the HDG—consistent with prior evidence suggesting that positive impacts are concentrated among the most vulnerable groups.
