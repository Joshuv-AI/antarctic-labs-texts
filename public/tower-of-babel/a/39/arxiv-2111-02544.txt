Polygon Placement Revisited: (Degree of Freedom + 1)-SUM Hardness and an Improvement via Offline Dynamic Rectangle Union
Marvin Künnemann, André Nusser

Abstract
We revisit a classical problem in computational geometry: Determine the largest copy of a simple polygon P P that can be placed into the simple polygon Q Q . Despite significant effort studying a number of settings, known algorithms require high polynomial running times, even for the interesting case when either P P or Q Q have constant size. (Barequet and Har-Peled, 2001) give a conditional lower bound of n 2 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{2-o(1)} under the 3SUM conjecture when P P and Q Q are (convex) polygons with Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) vertices each. This leaves open whether we can establish (1) hardness beyond quadratic time and (2) any superlinear bound for constant-sized P P or Q Q . In this paper, we affirmatively answer these questions under the higher-order k k SUM conjecture, proving natural hardness results that increase with each degree of freedom (scaling, x x -translation, y y -translation, rotation): • (scaling, x x -translation:) Finding the largest copy of P P that can be x x -translated into Q Q requires time n 2 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{2-o(1)} under the 3SUM conjecture, even for orthogonal (rectilinear) polygons P , Q P,Q with O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) and n n vertices, respectively. • (scaling, x x -translation, y y -translation:) Finding the largest copy of P P that can be arbitrarily translated into Q Q requires time n 2 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{2-o(1)} under the 4SUM conjecture, even for orthogonal polygons P , Q P,Q with O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) and n n vertices, respectively. This establishes the same lower bound under the assumption that Subset Sum cannot be solved in time O ⁡ ( ( 2 − ε ) n / 2 ) O((2-\varepsilon)^{n/2}) for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . • The above lower bounds are almost tight when one of the polygons is of constant size: Using an offline dynamic algorithm for maintaining the area of a union of rectangles due to Overmars and Yap, we obtain an 𝒪 ~ ​ ( ( p ​ q ) 2.5 ) \tilde{\cal O}((pq)^{2.5}) -time algorithm for orthogonal polygons P , Q P,Q with p p and q q vertices, respectively. This matches the lower bounds up to an n 1 / 2 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{1/2+o(1)} -factor when P , Q P,Q have O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) and n n vertices. • (scaling, x x -translation, y y -translation, rotation:) Finally, finding the largest copy of P P that can be arbitrarily rotated and translated into Q Q requires time n 3 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{3-o(1)} under the 5SUM conjecture. As in our reductions, each degree of freedom determines one summand in a k k SUM instance, these lower bounds appear likely to be best possible under k k SUM. We are not aware of any other such natural ( degree of freedom + 1 ) (\text{degree of freedom}+1) -SUM hardness for a geometric optimization problem. Finally, we prove an additional tight OV hardness of the translations-only case.

1.1
Our Results and Technical Overview

To state our results, we first give an intuitive definition of
k
SUM and Orthogonal Vectors (OV), see Section
2
for details. In the
k
SUM problem, we are given
k
sets of
n

k
integers — one from each set — that sum to zero. This problem is hypothesized not to be solvable in time
{\cal O}(n^{\lceil k/2\rceil-\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
.
In the Orthogonal Vectors (OV) problem, we are given two sets of
n
vectors in
\{0,1\}^{d}
and we ask whether there is a vector from the first set that is orthogonal to a vector from the second set.
This problem is hypothesized to not be solvable in time
{\cal O}(n^{2-\epsilon}\mathrm{poly}(d))
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Throughout this section, let
p
and
q
denote the number of vertices of the input polygons
P
and
Q
, respectively.
As a simple first result, we obtain that fixed-size polygon placement under translations has a quadratic-time hardness not only under the 3SUM Hypothesis (as shown in
[
BH01
]

[
Wil05
]
. This lower bound also applies to unbalanced cases of the problem, where

and

Theorem 1.1
.

Assuming the OV Hypothesis, there is no
{\cal O}((pq)^{1-\varepsilon})
-time algorithm for any
\varepsilon>0
for finding an
x
-translation of an orthogonal polygon
P
that fits into an orthogonal polygon
Q
. This holds even restricted to
p=\Theta(q^{\alpha})
for an arbitrary
\alpha>0
.

This lower bound tightly matches a corresponding
{\cal O}(pq\log(pq))
-time algorithm for orthogonal polygons
P
and
Q
due to Hernandez-Barrera
[
Her96
]
. Besides yielding a lower bound of
(pq)^{1-o(1)}
for every relationship between
p
and
q
, this also immediately proves the same hardness when we allow
any polynomial-space
preprocessing of
P
(or
Q
) by a recent result
[
AVW21
]
.
In the remainder of this section, we turn to the cases where scaling is allowed. We first give a surprisingly simple 3SUM lower bound for translations in a single dimension and scaling.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assuming the 3SUM Hypothesis, there is no
{\cal O}(q^{2-\varepsilon})
-time algorithm for any

Specifically, we give quadratic-time hardness already when

We reduce from this problem by using a polygon

The corresponding polygon

Theorem 1.3

Assuming the 4SUM Hypothesis, there is no

In the figure on the left, we illustrate the polygon

We show that our conditional lower bounds are not far from optimal for orthogonal polygons when

denote the polygon

\tilde{\cal O}(q^{2.5})
-time algorithm for constant

Theorem 1.4
.

For polygons
P,Q
, let
\lambda^{*}
denote the largest scaling factor
\lambda
such that
\lambda P
can be placed into
Q
under translations.
Given any orthogonal simple polygons
P
and

with
p
and
q
vertices, respectively, we can compute
\lambda^{*}
in time
{\cal O}((pq)^{2.5}2^{{\cal O}(\log^{*}pq)})
.

Interestingly, the above algorithm is obtained using an offline dynamic algorithm for maintaining the union of
n
rectangles in amortized
\tilde{\cal O}(\sqrt{n})
time, which led to algorithms for Klee’s measure problem (see
[
OY91
,
Cha10
]
). The basic idea is to cover
P
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus Q
by rectangles
P_{1},\dots,P_{p^{\prime}}
and
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{q^{\prime}}
, respectively, where
p^{\prime}={\cal O}(p)
and
q^{\prime}={\cal O}(q)
. Let
R_{i,j}
denote the set of translations of
P
such that
P_{i}
intersects the interior of
Q_{j}
, yielding a set of
forbidden

lies outside of

are rectangles as well.
Consequently,
P
can be placed into
Q
under translation

if and only if
\tau
is not contained in any of the forbidden regions, i.e., it is not contained in
\bigcup_{i,j}R_{i,j}
.

For a scaled copy
\lambda P
, we view the set of
R_{i,j}
’s as a dynamically changing set of (open) rectangles, parameterized by the scaling factor
\lambda
. While these rectangles may completely change with every change in
\lambda
, we may find a suitable representation in rank space that incurs only few, i.e.,
{\cal O}((pq)^{2})
, combinatorial changes. More specifically, we show how to reduce the problem of determining the largest
\lambda
such that
\bigcup_{i,j}R_{i,j}(\lambda)
does not cover all possible translations to the problem of determining the first point at which a dynamically changing set of rectangles contains a hole, where updates are specified in advance. Using a data structure with amortized
\tilde{\cal O}(\sqrt{n})
-time updates, we obtain a
\tilde{\cal O}((pq)^{2.5})
-time algorithm over all
{\cal O}((pq)^{2})
updates. We give the proof in Section
7
.

The Remaining Degree of Freedom: Rotation.

Finally, we take into account rotation as an additional degree of freedom. Specifically, for the setting of scaling, translation, and rotation, we obtain cubic-time hardness based on the 5SUM Hypothesis.

Theorem 1.5
.

Assuming the 5SUM Hypothesis, there is no
{\cal O}((p+q)^{3-\varepsilon})
-time algorithm for any
\varepsilon>0
for finding a largest copy of a simple polygon
P
that can be rotated and translated to fit into a simple polygon
Q
.

This result concludes our line of
(\text{degree of freedom}+1)
-SUM hardness results for the problem. For each degree of freedom, we show how it can be used to determine one summand in a corresponding
k
SUM instance, where the remaining summand is uniquely determined by the previous choices. We are not aware of any other geometric optimization problem for which such a natural
(\text{degree of freedom}+1)
-SUM hardness is known.

The 5SUM lower bound of Theorem
1.5
is perhaps the most interesting technical contribution of the paper. A natural approach to adapt the 4SUM construction would be to arrange many rotated copies of the four prongs of
P
into a wheel-like structure (see the figure on the right). In such a construction, the rotation can choose a group
i
of four prongs to be tested against set gadgets situated at the bottom, left, top, and right. Which information can we encode into the choice of the group? A central idea of our approach is that, instead of letting the prongs have a uniform distance of approximately 1, we use different distances for the corresponding
x
- and the
y
-directions, say a distance of
1
for the vertical prongs and a distance of
\mu_{i}
for the horizontal prongs. With such a construction, one might hope to reduce from the following problem:

\exists a_{1}\in A_{1},a_{2}\in A_{2},b_{1}\in B_{1},b_{2}\in B_{2},\mu\in M:b_{2}-b_{1}=\mu(a_{2}-a_{1}).

While this problem has a similar structure to 5SUM, it is unclear whether it is indeed 5SUM-hard: the combination of additive and multiplicative structures might make this problem simpler. It turns out, however, that we can reduce from 5SUM into our geometric problem directly, by exploiting that our gadgets can be made robust against small perturbations (we need to ensure a certain robustness anyway to deal with slight possible variations of the desired translations, scalings, and rotations, which poses a minor technical complication in our proofs). More specifically, the idea is to use that, when choosing
\mu=1+\varepsilon
for small values of
\varepsilon
, we can approximate
\mu(a_{2}-a_{1})=(1+\varepsilon)(a_{2}-a_{1})\approx a_{2}-a_{1}+\varepsilon
. To exploit this trick, consider the following (equivalent) formulation of 5SUM:

\exists a_{1}\in A_{1},a_{2}\in A_{2},b_{1}\in B_{1},b_{2}\in B_{2},c\in C:b_{2}-b_{1}=a_{2}-a_{1}+c,

where
A_{1},A_{2},B_{1},B_{2},C
are sets of
n
integers in
\{-U,\dots,U\}
with
U=n^{5}
. The idea is to set
\mu_{i}=1+\frac{c_{i}}{M}
where
c_{i}
is the

M
is a large value to be determined later. We need to adapt the placement of the set gadgets slightly: the bottom and top set gadgets, representing
A_{1},A_{2}
, should be spaced at a horizontal distance of essentially
M
. Likewise, we place the left and right set gadgets, representing
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, at a vertical distance of essentially
M
. Intuitively, we can place
\lambda P
(for large enough
\lambda
) into
Q
if and only if the following conditions hold:

–

The rotation chooses a group and thus a corresponding factor
\mu_{i}=1+\frac{c}{M}
for some
c\in C
.

–

The
x
-translation chooses some
a_{1}\in A_{1}
whose rectangle contains the bottom prong of the chosen group.

–

The
y
-translation chooses some
b_{1}\in B_{1}
whose rectangle contains the left prong of the chosen group.

–

The scaling
\lambda
is approximately equal to
M+a_{2}-a_{1}
for some
a_{2}\in A_{2}
whose rectangle contains the top prong of the chosen group.

–

Finally, the right prong of the chosen group must be contained in the rectangle of some
b_{2}\in B_{2}
determined by
M+b_{2}-b_{1}\approx\lambda\mu\approx(M+a_{2}-a_{1})(1+\frac{c}{M})=M+a_{2}-a_{1}+c+\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})c}{M}
. By choosing
M
large enough (e.g.,
M=U^{3}
) we can ensure that the approximation error
\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})c}{M}=o(1)
is negligible. We obtain equivalence with
b_{2}-b_{1}=a_{2}-a_{1}+c
.

In Section
5
, we give the proof of the theorem, showing how to also overcome further technical complications, such as possible deviations from the intended translations, scalings, and rotations (e.g., we need to enforce that a rotation is chosen that enforces prongs intended for
A_{1},A_{2}
are not rotated to intersect with
B_{1},B_{2}
).

Approximation Hardness.

It turns out that the above hardness results also give strong hardness of approximation. We detail this for the case of scaling and x-translations in Section
4
. The same arguments can be used to obtain similar statements for our other settings.

1.2
Outlook and Open Problems

We provide insights into the fine-grained complexity of the polygon placement problem, by showing
(\text{degree of freedom}+1)
-SUM hardness. For the cases without rotations and constant-size
P
, we show that the optimal running time is between
q^{2-o(1)}
(assuming the 4SUM Hypothesis) and
\tilde{\cal O}(q^{2.5})
, by an improvement via offline dynamic algorithms. Can we close this gap? We believe that an algorithmic improvement is more likely than a higher lower bound.

Similar challenges remain for more general cases: Can we generalize some of our algorithmic ideas for orthogonal polygons to general simple polygons? On the hardness side, it is an interesting challenge to prove even higher, i.e., super-cubic, conditional lower bounds when
p=\Theta(q)
. However, proving higher bounds from the
k
SUM Hypothesis appears rather unlikely, as in our constructions, each degree of freedom naturally chooses a corresponding summand in a given
k
SUM instance.
Finally, can we use ideas from this paper to give hardness also for convex
P
and/or
Q
? Barequet and Har-Peled
[
BH01
]
could adapt their lower bounds for the fixed-size case under translation to convex
P
and
Q
in a quite natural way. It is unclear how this could be done for our lower bounds, so that proving stronger lower bounds for convex restrictions remains an interesting open problem.

We also give a simple lower bound based on the Orthogonal Vectors (OV) problem. In this problem, we are given two sets

Correctness.

We show that this construction gives a Polygon Placement instance equivalent to the given 5SUM’ instance. We split the proof into two lemmas, one for each direction.
For the next two proofs, recall that for any
x,y,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, we use
x\in y+[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
to mean
x\in[y-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

If there are
a_{1}\in A_{1},a_{2}\in A_{2},b_{1}\in B_{1},b_{2}\in B_{2}
and
c\in C
such that
b_{2}-b_{1}=a_{2}-a_{1}+c
, then there is a scaling factor
\lambda\geq M-2U
such that
\lambda P
can be rotated and translated to be contained in
Q
.

Proof.

Let
a_{1}\in A_{1},a_{2}\in A_{2},b_{1}\in B_{1},b_{2}\in B_{2}
and
i^{*}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
be such that
b_{2}-b_{1}=a_{2}-a_{1}+c_{i^{*}}
where
c_{i^{*}}
is the
i^{*}
-th element of
C
. We claim that we can place
\lambda P
with scaling factor
\lambda=M+a_{2}-a_{1}
by translating its origin by
\tau=(a_{1},b_{1})
and rotating the polygon by an angle of
-2\pi\frac{i^{*}-1}{4n}
around its origin. Note that this implies
\lambda\in[M-2U,M+2U]
.

We first observe that under this scaling, rotation and translation,
P_{0}
fits into
Q_{0}
: We can conservatively approximate
P_{0}
and
Q_{0}
by their circumscribed circle and inscribed circle, respectively, whose radii are the circumradius
R_{P_{0}}
and the apothem
a_{Q_{0}}
, respectively. Since their side lengths are
4\lambda
and
10M
, respectively, we notice by Fact
2.1
and
\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}U
that

R_{P_{0}}+\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert_{2}\leq 4\lambda n+\sqrt{2}U\leq 4(M+2U)n+\sqrt{2}U\leq 5Mn\leq a_{Q_{0}},

where the second-to-last inequality follows from
M=U^{2}n\geq 8Un+\sqrt{2}U
for sufficiently large
n
, as
U=n^{5}
. Thus, when shifting an arbitrarily rotated
\lambda P
by
\tau
,
P_{0}
will remain in the inscribed circle of
Q_{0}
.

It remains to show that each prong
P_{j}^{(i)}
fits into
Q
under this placement. We first consider the most important case of
i=i^{*}
. Note that by the chosen rotation, we have that
P_{1}^{(i^{*})},P_{2}^{(i^{*})},P_{3}^{(i^{*})}
and
P_{4}^{(i^{*})}
are the bottom horizontal, right vertical, top horizontal and left vertical prongs of
\lambda P
. We claim that the part of
P_{1}^{(i^{*})}
that leaves
Q_{0}
is contained in the prong in
S(A_{1})
representing
a_{1}
. Indeed, note that the center segment of
P_{1}^{(i^{*})}
intersects the bottom segment of
Q_{0}
at an
\mathrm{x}
-coordinate of
\tau_{\mathrm{x}}=a_{1}
.
As
\lambda\leq M+2U\leq 2M
, we have that
P_{1}^{(i^{*})}
is a prong of width
2\lambda\varepsilon\leq 4M\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{200n}\leq 2\delta
and length
\lambda L\leq 2ML=L^{\prime}
. Hence, this part fits fully into the width-
2\delta
, length-
L^{\prime}
prong of
S(A_{1})
centered at
\mathrm{x}
-coordinate
a_{1}
.
Likewise, the center segment of prong
P_{4}^{(i^{*})}
intersects the left vertical segment at a
\mathrm{y}
-coordinate of
\tau_{\mathrm{y}}=b_{1}
, and thus the prong
P_{4}^{(i^{*})}
is fully contained in the union of
Q_{0}
and the prong representing
b_{1}
in
S(B_{1})
(attached at
\mathrm{y}
-coordinates
b_{1}+[-\delta,\delta]
).
For prong
P_{3}^{(i^{*})}
, we observe that its center segment intersects the top horizontal segment of
Q_{0}
at
\mathrm{x}
-coordinate
\tau_{\mathrm{x}}+\lambda=a_{1}+(M+a_{2}-a_{1})=M+a_{2}
. Thus,
P_{3}^{(i^{*})}
is fully contained in the union of
Q_{0}
and the prong representing
a_{2}
in
S(A_{2})
(attached at
\mathrm{x}
-coordinates
M+a_{2}+[-\delta,\delta]
). Finally, the center segment of prong
P_{2}^{(i^{*})}
intersects the right vertical segment of
Q_{0}
at a
\mathrm{y}
-coordinate of

\tau_{y}+\lambda\left(1+\frac{c_{i^{*}}}{M}\right)=b_{1}+(M+a_{2}-a_{1})\left(1+\frac{c_{i^{*}}}{M}\right)=b_{1}+M+a_{2}-a_{1}+c_{i^{*}}+\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})c_{i^{*}}}{M}.

Using that
b_{1}+a_{2}-a_{1}+c_{i^{*}}=b_{2}
by assumption, and that
\left|\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})c_{i^{*}}}{M}\right|\leq\frac{2U^{2}}{M}=\frac{2}{n}
, we obtain that the prong
P_{2}^{(i^{*})}
intersects the right vertical segment of
Q_{0}
at a
\mathrm{y}
-coordinate of
M+b_{2}+[-(\lambda\varepsilon+\frac{2}{n}),\lambda\varepsilon+\frac{2}{n}]
. By noting that
\lambda\varepsilon+\frac{2}{n}\leq 2M\varepsilon+\frac{2}{n}=\frac{1}{400n}+\frac{2}{n}\leq\frac{1}{400}=\delta
for sufficiently large
n
, this range is fully contained in the range
M+b_{2}+[-\delta,\delta]
given by the prong representing
b_{2}
in
S(B_{2})
, which thus contains the relevant part of
P_{2}^{(i^{*})}
. We conclude that each prong of the
i^{*}
-th group fits into
Q_{0}
.

It remains to argue that each prong
P_{j}^{(i)}
with
i\neq i^{*}
fits into
Q_{0}
. This claim is essentially trivial: Imagine a rotation of coordinate axes such that
P_{j}^{(i)}
is a vertical prong. It is easy to see that
P_{j}^{(i)}
intersects the bottom segment of
Q_{0}
at
\mathrm{x}
-coordinates in
[-2M,2M]
. The corresponding
4M\times L^{\prime}
rectangle below
Q_{0}
is contained in the trivial gadget, and thus can contain the part of
P_{j}^{(i)}
leaving
Q_{0}
. This concludes the lemma.
∎

We now show the converse of Lemma
5.1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

If there is a scaling factor
\lambda\geq M-2U
such that
\lambda P
can be rotated and translated to be contained in
Q
, then there are
a_{1}\in A_{1},a_{2}\in A_{2},b_{1}\in B_{1},b_{2}\in B_{2}
and
c\in C
such that
b_{2}-b_{1}=a_{2}-a_{1}+c
.

Proof.

Assume that there is a scaling factor
\lambda\geq M-2U
such that
\lambda P
can be placed into
Q
(under rotation and translation) and fix such a placement of
\lambda P
. Specifically, let
\lambda\geq M-2U
be the scaling factor,
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the translation of the center of
\lambda P
, and let
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
denote the rotation angle of
\lambda P
around its center (in counter clockwise direction). In the remainder of this proof, we show that the following sequence of statements holds:

(1)

The rotation angle
\alpha
is in
-2\pi\cdot\frac{i}{4n}+[-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}]
for some
i\in\{0,\dots,4n-1\}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}\coloneqq\frac{\delta}{Mn}
. Furthermore, there is some prong
P^{(i^{*})}_{j^{*}}
that intersects the set gadget
S(A_{1})
.

(2)

We have
j^{*}\in\{1,3\}
and there are
a_{1}\in A_{1},a_{2}\in A_{2}
such that the
x
-translation
\tau_{\mathrm{x}}
is within
a_{1}+[-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}}]
(if
j^{*}=1
) or within
M+a_{2}+[-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}}]
(if
j^{*}=3
) and the scaling factor
\lambda
is within
M+a_{2}-a_{1}+[-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{scale}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{scale}}]
, where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}}\coloneqq 21\delta
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{scale}}\coloneqq 43\delta
.

(3)

If
j^{*}=1
, then there is some
b_{1}\in B_{1}
such that the
y
-translation
\tau_{\mathrm{y}}
is within
b_{1}+[-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}}]
. If
j^{*}=3
, there is some
b_{2}\in B_{2}
such that the
y
-translation
\tau_{\mathrm{y}}
is within
M+b_{2}+[-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}}]
.

(4)

Finally, there is a corresponding
b_{2}\in B_{2}
(if
j^{*}=1
) or
b_{1}\in B_{1}
(if
j^{*}=3
) such that
b_{2}-b_{1}=a_{2}-a_{1}+c_{i*}
.

After showing the above statements, we can then deduce that any placement of
\lambda P
in
Q
where
\lambda\geq M-U
uniquely defines
a_{1}\in A_{1},a_{2}\in A_{2},b_{1}\in B_{1},b_{2}\in B_{2},c\in C
such that
b_{2}-b_{1}=a_{2}-a_{1}+c
.

We first collect general useful facts: By
M=U^{2}n
, we have
M-2U=M(1-2/(Un))\geq 0.9M
for sufficiently large
n
, which we will use as a lower bound for
\lambda
. A trivial upper bound is given by
\lambda\leq 3M
, as otherwise already the scaled
P_{0}
(of side length more than
12M
) cannot fit into
Q
(of side length
10M
). By Fact
2.1
, the regular
4n
-gon
Q_{0}
with side length
10M
has a circumradius
R\leq 10Mn
. Thus, the diameter of
Q_{0}
is bounded by
2R\leq 20Mn
, which we will frequently exploit.

To prove (1), note that since
\lambda L\geq 0.9ML=45Mn>20Mn\geq 2R
, each prong of
\lambda P
(each of length
\lambda L
) cannot be fully contained in
Q_{0}
, but must intersect either a set gadget or a trivial gadget. In fact, by the structure of
P
and
Q
, every prong
P_{j}^{(i)}
of
\lambda P
must intersect a
unique
(set or trivial) gadget of
Q
. In particular, let
P_{j^{*}}^{(i^{*})}
denote the prong of
\lambda P
intersecting
S(A_{1})
. We will show that the rotation angle is very close to
-2\pi\cdot\frac{(j^{*}-1)n+i^{*}-1}{4n}
: Let
\beta\coloneqq\alpha+2\pi\cdot\frac{(j^{*}-1)n+i^{*}-1}{4n}
be the difference of
\alpha
and this angle. Observe that the center segment of
P_{j^{*}}^{(i^{*})}
has an intersection of length at least
\lambda L-2R\geq 45Mn-20Mn=25Mn
inside a prong of
S(A_{1})
and cannot traverse a horizontal distance of more than
2\delta
(as any prong of
S(A_{1})
has width
2\delta
). Since under rotation angle
\alpha
,
P_{j^{*}}^{(i^{*})}
traverses a horizontal distance of at least
25Mn\cdot|\sin(\beta)|
, we must have that
|\sin(\beta)|\leq\frac{2\delta}{25Mn}
, and thus by Fact
2.1
,
|\beta|\leq(\pi/2)\cdot|\sin(\beta)|\leq 2\cdot\frac{2\delta}{25Mn}\leq\frac{\delta}{Mn}
, concluding the proof of (1).

To prove (2), let
P_{\mathrm{top}}
denote the prong opposite of
P_{j^{*}}^{(i^{*})}
. Note that if
j^{*}\in\{2,4\}
then
P_{\mathrm{top}}
is to the left of
S(A_{1})
, and hence cannot intersect
S(A_{2})
(the unique gadget that
P_{\mathrm{top}}
must intersect), as
P_{\mathrm{top}}
is located to the right of
S(A_{1})
. Thus, we must have
j^{*}\in\{1,3\}
. If
j^{*}=1
, then for
P_{j^{*}}^{(i^{*})}
to intersect some prong of
S(A_{1})
, the translation
\tau_{x}
must be such that the center segment of
P_{j^{*}}^{(i^{*})}
intersects the bottom horizontal segment of
Q_{0}
at some prong of
S(A_{1})
, i.e., at an
\mathrm{x}
-coordinate in
a_{1}+[-\delta,\delta]
for some
a_{1}\in A_{1}
. Note that the center line of
P_{j^{*}}^{(i^{*})}
runs through the origin of
P
and that any segment of
Q_{0}
is at distance at most
2R
from the translated center of
\lambda P
. Thus, by the Line Rotation Lemma
2.2
(setting
\Delta=0,Y=2R,\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}
), the center segment intersects
Q_{0}
’s bottom segment at an
\mathrm{x}
-coordinate in
\tau_{\mathrm{x}}+[-2R\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}},2R\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}]
. For
a_{1}+[-\delta,\delta]
and
\tau_{x}+[-2R\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}},2R\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}]

\tau_{\mathrm{x}}
must be in
a_{1}+[-(\delta+2R\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}),\delta+2R\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}]

2R\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}\leq\frac{20Mn\delta}{Mn}=20\delta
, we obtain that
\tau_{\mathrm{x}}
is indeed in
a_{1}+[-21\delta,21\delta]
. For the scaling factor, recall that the center segment of the opposite prong
P_{\mathrm{top}}
must intersect some prong of
S(A_{2})
, i.e., at some
\mathrm{x}
-coordinate in
M+a_{2}+[-\delta,\delta]
for some
a_{2}\in A_{2}
. By the Line Rotation Lemma
2.2
(setting
\Delta=\lambda,Y=2R,\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}
), the center segment intersects the top horizontal segment of
Q_{0}
at an
\mathrm{x}
-coordinate in
\tau_{\mathrm{x}}+[\lambda-2R\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}},\lambda(1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}^{2})+2R\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}]
. Since
\tau_{\mathrm{x}}
is in
a_{1}+[-21\delta,21\delta]

\lambda\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}^{2}\leq 3M\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}^{2}=\frac{3\delta^{2}}{Mn^{2}}\leq\delta

is in
M+a_{2}-a_{1}+[-43\delta,43\delta]
.

If
j^{*}=3
, i.e.,
P
is as before, but rotated by
\pi
, the calculations are analogous, but choose first
a_{2}
, then
a_{1}
: For
P_{\mathrm{top}}
to intersect

for some
a_{2}\in A_{2}
. Correspondingly, for

, the scaling factor

must be in

for some

.

To prove (3), let
P_{\mathrm{left}}
(

) denote the prongs in the

-th group following (preceding)

, respectively. If

, then the intersection of the center line of
