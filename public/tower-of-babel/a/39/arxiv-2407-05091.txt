Theory of Rashba splitting in quantum-well states
Mitsuaki Kawamura, Taisuke Ozaki

Abstract
We present a theory pertaining to the asymptotic behavior of Rashba energy splitting in a quantum-well state (QWS). First, unlike previous studies, we derive k -linear Rashba term from a first-principles Hamiltonian in a physically convincing manner. The k -dependent in-plane intrinsic magnetic-field term originates from the spin–orbit interaction and hybridized s s - p z p_{z} orbital, whereas a steep nucleus potential realizes the linearity for the k of the effective magnetic field. Next, we analyze the Rashba effect of a QWS using a one-dimensional tight-binding model developed based on the bottom-up approach that is aforementioned. The Rashba-splitting behavior of this system is captured from the density at the interface. The density can be expressed analytically as a function of the monolayer number and well depth. Finally, we apply our formula to the QWS of a few-monolayers Ag on an Au(111) surface to validate the theory based on a realistic system. Our tight-binding analysis qualitatively fits the first-principles result using only two fitting parameters and predicts the optimal condition for achieving a large Rashba splitting.



I
Introduction

In a system featuring significant spin–orbit coupling (SOC) and broken inversion symmetry, the spin degeneracy can be resolved using the Bloch wavenumber (
k
)-dependent intrinsic Zeeman-like Hamiltonian as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\textbf{k}}^{\textrm{R}}=\alpha_{\textrm{R}}(\textbf{e}_{z}\times\textbf{k})\cdot\hat{s}.

(1)

This phenomenon is known as the Rashba effect
Bychkov and Rashba 1984
, which was first directly observed on an Au(111) surface via angle-resolved photoelectron spectroscopy (ARPES)
LaShell et al. 1996
.
After this discovery, the Rashba effect was observed on other metallic surfaces, such as Bi
Koroteev et al. 2004
, Ag, and Cu
Yaji et al. 2018
.
This effect can be applied to a spin-current source and detector in spintronics devices
Sánchez et al. 2013
.
One of the leading research topics pertaining to Rashba-splitting materials is improving splitting to realize a higher spin–charge current conversion ratio.
A long-range ordered
(\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{3})R
30°surface alloy comprising
1/3
of a monolayer (ML) Bi on an Ag(111) surface significantly enhances this effect
Ast et al. 2007
, and a bulk BiTeI
Ishizaka et al. 2011
has a larger splitting.
Nanostructure systems are another exciting platform to observe the Rashba effect.
In InGaAs/InAlAs
Nitta et al. 1997
or InGaAs/InP
Engels et al. 1997
interfaces, we can modulate the magnitude of energy splitting by adjusting the gate voltage.
Similarly, the layer number of the quantum-well state (QWS) system is a tunable parameter that governs the Rashba effect
Cercellier et al. 2004
;
Cercellier et al. 2006
;
Noguchi et al. 2021
.
Moreover, in the QWS system, another degree of freedom, namely, multiple localization modes, appears depending on the thickness of the confinement area
Forster et al. 2011
;
Chiang 2000
.
Therefore, we can widely and flexibly adjust Rashba energy splitting in the QWS system.

To design a material system by leveraging the Rashba effect, theoretical and computational schemes are effective as they allow the consideration of synthesized and hypothetical materials.
First-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT)
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
;
Kohn and Sham 1965
, including SOC
MacDonald et al. 1980
, can well reproduce the spin-splitting energy band of Rashba systems such as the Au(111) surface state
Bihlmayer et al. 2006
and Bi surface alloy
Bihlmayer et al. 2007
;
Yamaguchi et al. 2017
.
Although the energy band from DFT may not necessarily correspond to the ARPES spectrum, the extremely weak electronic correlation of these materials enables a quantitative comparison of the theoretical and experimental results.
The QWS system of few-monolayers Ag on Au(111) surfaces is an example of such a weakly correlated Rashba system, for which we reproduced the energy band and Rashba-splitting parameter quantitatively in a previous study
Noguchi et al. 2021
.
Based on further analysis, we revealed that the envelope function of a QWS at the Ag/Au boundary offers a significant contribution to the energy splitting.
Our theoretical background is based on the result of an earlier study
Nagano et al. 2009
, which derives the
k
-dependent energy splitting
\Delta\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}}^{\textrm{SOC}}
as

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}}^{\textrm{SOC}}=|\textbf{k}|\int d^{3}r2\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}|\psi_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})|^{2}

(2)

by assuming the two-dimensional free electronic wavefunction
\psi_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})=e^{i{k_{x}x+k_{y}y}}u(z)
.
However, as the wavefunction of a realistic system, such as surface alloy and the QWS, is different from that of two-dimensional free electronic state, the applicability of the earlier study is unclear, and we must address the discrepancy between the atomic SOC and the Rashba interaction term.
Additionally, if we can formulate such a bottom-up derivation, then a precise theoretical model for capturing the wide-ranging behavior of the QWS system can be derived.

This study aims to systematically derive a
k
-dependent Rashba splitting formulation for a realistic system.
Additionally, we derive the minimum model for the Rashba effect of the QWS to understand its nature and predict the optimal condition to achieve a large Rashba splitting.
This paper is organized as follows:
The derivation of Rashba energy splitting and a simplified model of the QWS along with its analytical solution are presented in Sec.
II
.
In Sec.
III
, an example of Rashba splitting in a QWS, namely few-monolayer Ag on an Au (111) surface, is presented.
Additionally, a comparison between our analytical result and the result from first-principles calculation is presented.
Finally, we present our summary in Sec.
IV
.

II
Theory

In this section, we explain the basic theory of the Rashba effect in a surface system and then derive a minimum model for the QWS system.
Finally, the model is solved analytically using Green’s function.

II.1
SOC and Rashba splitting

The SOC term in a Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\textrm{SOC}}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=2\{\nabla V({\textbf{r}})\times\hat{\textbf{p}}\}\cdot\textbf{s}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}},

(3)

where
\textbf{s}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
are the Pauli matrices times
1/2
,
\sigma(\sigma^{\prime})=\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
,
V({\textbf{r}})
is the nuclear potential, and
\hat{\textbf{p}}\equiv-i\nabla
is the momentum operator.
The contribution of the Hamiltonian above to the orbital eigenenergy is computed using spinor eigenfunctions
(\psi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\uparrow},\psi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\downarrow})
at wavenumber
k
and band
n
as follows:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}\pm}^{\textrm{SOC}}=2\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\int d^{3}r\psi^{\pm*}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}(\textbf{r})\{\nabla V({\textbf{r}})\times\hat{\textbf{p}}\}\cdot\textbf{s}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\psi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{r}).

(4)

Here, we consider the energies of two states, indexed as
+
and
-
, separately.
By assuming the absence of the space dependence of the spin in a single orbital, the Bloch spinor wavefunction
\psi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}
is written as

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}\psi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\uparrow}(\textbf{r})\\
\psi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\downarrow}(\textbf{r})\\
\end{matrix}\right)=e^{i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{r}}u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})\left(\begin{matrix}\chi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\uparrow}\\
\chi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\downarrow}\\
\end{matrix}\right),

(5)

where
\chi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}
is the space-independent spin portion of the wavefunction, and
u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})
is the periodic portion of the Bloch wavefunction and is the solution to the following equation:

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{(\nabla+i\textbf{k})^{2}}{2}+V(\textbf{r})\right)u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})=\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}}^{0}u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r}).

(6)

By inserting the Bloch wavefunction in Eq. (
5
), the SOC portion of the orbital energy expressed in Eq. (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}\pm}^{\textrm{SOC}}

\displaystyle=\textbf{s}^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}}\cdot 2\int d^{3}r\nabla V({\textbf{r}})\times\{\textbf{k}|u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})|^{2}-iu^{*}_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})\nabla u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})\},

(7)

where each Bloch state has a
k
-dependent spin moment expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\textbf{s}^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\chi^{\pm*}_{\textbf{k}\sigma}\textbf{s}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\chi^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}\sigma^{\prime}}.

(8)

Equation (
7
) indicates a Zeeman-like term, which causes energy splitting between two spin states that are parallel and anti-parallel to the intrinsic magnetic field.
To analyze the
k
-dependence of the SOC energy in the vicinity of
\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}
, we expand the periodic portion of the wavefunction
u_{\textbf{k}}
as follows:

\displaystyle u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})

\displaystyle=u_{\textbf{0}}(\textbf{r})+\sum_{\beta=\{x,y\}}k_{\beta}\left.\frac{\partial u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial k_{\beta}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}=\{x,y\}}k_{\beta}k_{\beta^{\prime}}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial k_{\beta}\partial k_{\beta^{\prime}}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}+O(k^{3}),

(9)

where the first and second derivatives of the wavefunction with respect to
k
are obtained by solving the Sternheimer Eqs.
Sternheimer 1954

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2}+V(\textbf{r})-\varepsilon_{\textbf{0}}^{0}\right)\left.\frac{\partial u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial k_{\beta}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}=i\frac{\partial u_{\textbf{0}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial r_{\beta}},

(10)

and

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2}+V(\textbf{r})-\varepsilon_{\textbf{0}}^{0}\right)\left.\frac{\partial^{2}u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial k_{\beta}\partial k_{\beta^{\prime}}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}}^{0}}{\partial k_{\beta}\partial k_{\beta^{\prime}}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}+\delta_{\beta\beta^{\prime}}\right)u_{\textbf{0}}(\textbf{r})+i\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{\beta}}\left.\frac{\partial u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial k_{\beta^{\prime}}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{\beta^{\prime}}}\left.\frac{\partial u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial k_{\beta}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}\right),

(11)

respectively.
As
u_{\textbf{0}}
is the solution of the eigen-equation presented in Eq. (
6
) at
\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}
, we can set
u_{\textbf{0}}
as a real function.
Consequently,
\partial u_{\textbf{k}}/\partial k_{\beta}|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}
and
\partial^{2}u_{\textbf{k}}/(\partial k_{\beta}\partial k_{\beta^{\prime}})|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}
become a pure imaginary and a real function, respectively.
Therefore, the complex conjugate of the wavefunction becomes

\displaystyle u^{*}_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})

\displaystyle=u_{\textbf{0}}(\textbf{r})-\sum_{\beta=\{x,y\}}k_{\beta}\left.\frac{\partial u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial k_{\beta}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}=\{x,y\}}k_{\beta}k_{\beta^{\prime}}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial k_{\beta}\partial k_{\beta^{\prime}}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}+O(k^{3}).

(12)

By inserting Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
) into the SOC-energy term in Eq. (
7
) and applying partial integration and antisymmetry to the outer product, the zeroth- and second-order terms for
k
vanish, and the energy is written as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}\pm}^{\textrm{SOC}}=\textbf{s}^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}}\cdot 2\int d^{3}r\nabla V({\textbf{r}})\times\left\{\textbf{k}u^{2}_{\textbf{0}}(\textbf{r})+2i\sum_{\beta=\{x,y\}}k_{\beta}\left.\frac{\partial u_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial k_{\beta}}\right|_{\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}}\nabla u_{\textbf{0}}(\textbf{r})\right\}+O(k^{3}).

(13)

The dominant region of this integral is near the nuclear position where the absolute value of
\nabla V
becomes large.
If
u_{\textbf{0}}
comprises orbitals such as
s
,
p_{z}
, and
d_{3z^{2}-r^{2}}
, which are symmetric in the
x-y
plane, then
\partial u_{\textbf{0}}/\partial r_{x(y)}
is zero at the nuclear position.
Based on Eq. (
10
),
\partial u_{\textbf{0}}/\partial k_{x(y)}
becomes small in that region, and the integral in Eq. (
13
) is dominated by the first term.
In Sec.
III
, we show that this orbital symmetry is realized in the Ag/Au QWS system.

The system exhibits two- or three-fold symmetry on the

If we consider a system that exhibits two- or three-fold symmetry on the
x-y
plane and no inversion symmetry on the
z
-axis [See Fig.
1
(a)], then only the
z
-component of
\nabla V
remains in the integral and Eq. (
13
) becomes

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}\pm}^{\textrm{SOC}}\approx 2(\textbf{e}_{z}\times\textbf{k})\cdot\textbf{s}^{\pm}_{\textbf{k}}\int d^{3}r\frac{\partial V(\textbf{r})}{\partial z}u^{2}_{\textbf{0}}(\textbf{r})

(14)

in the vicinity of
\textbf{k}=\textbf{0}
.
The energy difference between two-spin states depicted in Fig.
1
(b) is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\left|\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}+}^{\textrm{SOC}}-\varepsilon_{\textbf{k}-}^{\textrm{SOC}}\right|

\displaystyle=2\alpha_{\textbf{R}}\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}},

(15)

where we use
|\textbf{s}_{\textbf{k}}|=1/2
and define the following Rashba-splitting parameter

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{R}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int d^{3}r\frac{\partial V(\textbf{r})}{\partial z}u^{2}_{\textbf{0}}(\textbf{r}).

(16)

The applicability of Eq. (
16
) is not limited to the two-dimensional free electronic system, in contrast to an earlier study
Nagano et al. 2009
, even though the same formulation is derived.
Next, we discuss the Rashba-splitting parameter
\alpha_{\textrm{R}}
.

Let us consider the schematic illustration of the QWS shown in Fig.
2
(a).
If the considered electronic state is energetically isolated, then the wavefunction can be approximated as single-band state smoothly modulated along the
z
-axis, i.e.,
u_{\textbf{0}}(\textbf{r})\approx\varphi_{\textrm{at}}(\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}_{i})u_{\textrm{env}}(z)
, in the vicinity of the
i
-th nucleus, where
\varphi_{\textrm{at}}
is the atomic orbital and
u_{\textrm{env}}
is the envelope function [See Fig.
2
(b)].
Because
V(\textbf{r})
has a large gradient in the vicinity of the nuclei, the integrand of Eq. (
16
) can be depicted schematically as shown in Fig.
2
(c).
Subsequently, the Rashba parameter becomes

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{R}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i=-\infty}^{N_{\textrm{well}}}\int d^{3}r\frac{\partial V_{\textrm{at}}(\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}_{i})}{\partial z}|\varphi_{\textrm{at}}(\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}_{i})|^{2}|u_{\textrm{env}}(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i=-\infty}^{N_{\textrm{well}}}\Delta\alpha_{\textrm{R},i},

(17)

where
V_{\textrm{at}}
and
\textbf{r}_{i}
are the potential and position of the
i
-th nucleus, respectively;

N_{\textrm{well}}
is the number of monolayers in the well region.
The relatively smooth envelope function can be approximated around the
i
-th nucleus as
|u_{\textrm{env}}(z)|^{2}=|u_{\textrm{env}}(z_{i})|^{2}+d|u_{\textrm{env}}|^{2}/dz|_{z=z_{i}}(z-z_{i})
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\Delta\alpha_{\textrm{R},i}

\displaystyle\approx|u_{\textrm{env}}(z_{i})|^{2}\int d^{3}r\frac{\partial V_{\textrm{at}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial z}|\varphi_{\textrm{at}}(\textbf{r})|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{d|u_{\textrm{env}}|^{2}}{dz}\right|_{z=z_{i}}\int d^{3}r\frac{\partial V_{\textrm{at}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial z}|\varphi_{\textrm{at}}(\textbf{r})|^{2}z

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d|u_{\textrm{env}}|^{2}}{dz}\right|_{z=z_{i}}\langle\delta V\rangle_{i},

(18)

where

\displaystyle\langle\delta V\rangle_{i}\equiv\int d^{3}r\frac{\partial V_{\textrm{at}}(\textbf{r})}{\partial z}|\varphi_{\textrm{at}}(\textbf{r})|^{2}z.

(19)

The first term on the first line of Eq. (
18
) vanishes because of the symmetries of
V_{\textrm{at}}
and
\varphi_{\textrm{at}}
.

\Delta\alpha_{\textrm{R},i}=0
when the gradient of the envelope function is 0 [right panel of Fig.
2
(c)], whereas
\Delta\alpha_{\textrm{R},i}
is positive (negative) when the envelope function increases (decreases) as shown in the left panel of Fig.
2
(c).
Substituting Eq. (
18
) into Eq. (
17
) yields the total Rashba parameter as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{R}}=\sum_{i=-\infty}^{N_{\textrm{well}}}\left.\frac{d|u_{\textrm{env}}|^{2}}{dz}\right|_{z=z_{i}}\langle\delta V\rangle_{i}.

(20)

In the structure shown in Fig.
2
(b), because the averaged gradient of the potential
\langle\delta V\rangle_{i}
in this summation assumes only two values, i.e., the averages for the substrate (
\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{sub}}
) and well (
\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{well}}
), we can shift the averaged gradients of the potential outside
\sum
as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{R}}

\displaystyle\approx\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{sub}}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{0}\left.\frac{d|u_{\textrm{env}}(z)|^{2}}{dz}\right|_{z=z_{i}}

\displaystyle+\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{well}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\textrm{well}}}\left.\frac{d|u_{\textrm{env}}(z)|^{2}}{dz}\right|_{z=z_{i}}.

(21)

If the envelope function
|u_{\textrm{env}}(z)|^{2}
varies gradually, then these summations can be approximated with integrals as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{R}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{sub}}}{c_{\textrm{sub}}}\int_{-\infty}^{z_{\textrm{b}}}dz\frac{d|u_{\textrm{env}}(z)|^{2}}{dz}

\displaystyle+\frac{\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{well}}}{c_{\textrm{well}}}\int_{z_{\textrm{b}}}^{z_{\textrm{b}}+c_{\textrm{well}}N_{\textrm{well}}}dz\frac{d|u_{\textrm{env}}(z)|^{2}}{dz}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{sub}}}{c_{\textrm{sub}}}-\frac{\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{well}}}{c_{\textrm{well}}}\right)|u_{\textrm{env}}(z_{\textrm{b}})|^{2},

(22)

where
c_{\textrm{sub}}
(
c_{\textrm{well}}
) is the layer distance at the substrate (well) and
z_{\textrm{b}}
is the boundary of the substrate and well.
Furthermore, we leverage the fact that the envelope function vanishes in the deep position of the substrate (
z=-\infty
) and on the surface (
z=z_{\textrm{b}}+c_{\textrm{well}}N_{\textrm{well}}
).
This formula shows that
\alpha_{\textrm{R}}
is determined by the charge density at the interface regardless of the detailed structure inside the well, provided that the atomic species used for the film is known.
Additionally, the difference in the potential gradients
\partial V/\partial z
of the two atoms significantly enhances the Rashba effect.

II.3
Tight-binding model for QWS and analytical solution

(a) One-dimensional tight-binding model for QWS.
(b) Schematic illustration of density of states for isolated semi-infinite substrate (left) and finite-length well region (right).
If these separated systems hybridize, then localized states are formed in the well region (c).
In this case, the fourth QWS originating from the state with the energy of

In this section, we estimate the
|u_{\textrm{env}}(z_{\textrm{b}})|^{2}
of a QWS as the wavefunction of the simplified one-dimensional tight-binding model shown in Fig.
3
(a).
Subsequently, the envelope function at each atomic site
u_{\textrm{env}}(z_{i})
is approximated as a coefficient for the local basis of the tight-binding model as

\displaystyle u_{\textrm{env}}(z_{i})\approx\langle i|u_{n}\rangle,

(23)

where
|i\rangle
is the local basis at site
i
and
|u_{n}\rangle
is the
n
-th QWS.
We focus on the square of this quantity at the interfacial substrate site, namely
i=0
, and compute this quantity as a residue of Green’s function at a pole of a QWS as

\displaystyle|u_{\textrm{env}}(z_{\textrm{b}})|^{2}\approx|\langle 0|u_{n}\rangle|^{2}=\textrm{Res}_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}}[G_{00}(\varepsilon)],

(24)

where the pole
\varepsilon_{n}
corresponds to the eigenenergy of the
n
-th QWS.
Subsequently, Green’s function of the interfacial substrate can be written as a single-site Dyson’s equation [see Fig.
3
(a)]

\displaystyle G_{00}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon-\Sigma^{\textrm{s}}_{\textrm{sub}}-\Sigma^{\textrm{s}}_{\textrm{well}}},

(25)

where

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\textrm{s}}_{\textrm{sub}}(\varepsilon)=t^{2}G_{\textrm{sub}}^{\textrm{s}}(\varepsilon)

(26)

and

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\textrm{s}}_{\textrm{well}}(\varepsilon)=t^{2}G_{\textrm{well}}^{\textrm{s}}(\varepsilon)

(27)

are the local self-energies originating from the two neighboring surfaces.

G_{\textrm{sub}}^{\textrm{s}}
and
G_{\textrm{well}}^{\textrm{s}}
are the surface Green’s functions at the substrate and well, respectively.

G_{\textrm{sub}}^{\textrm{s}}
is computed using the recursion method
Haydock et al. 1975
as an onsite edge Green’s function of semi-infinite chain

\displaystyle G_{\textrm{sub}}^{\textrm{s}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}-4t^{2}}}{2t^{2}}.

(28)

Meanwhile,
G_{\textrm{well}}^{\textrm{s}}
becomes

\displaystyle G_{\textrm{well}}^{\textrm{s}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{\left\langle 1\left|\phi_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}\right.\right\rangle\left\langle\left.\phi_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}\right|1\right\rangle}{\varepsilon-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}}.

(29)

Here,
\left|\phi_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}\right\rangle
and
E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}
are eigenvectors and eigenvalues for a finite-length chain whose Hamiltonian is expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}-d&-t&&&&\\
-t&-d&-t&&&\\
&-t&-d&-t&&\\
&&-t&\ddots\ &-t&\\
&&&-t&-d&-t\\
&&&&-t&-d\end{pmatrix},

(30)

where the size of this matrix is the number of the well layers
N_{\textrm{well}}
.
This matrix is known as the tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix, which can be diagonalized analytically
Noschese et al. 2013
;
Kulkarni et al. 1999
as

\displaystyle E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}

\displaystyle=-d-2t\cos(n\omega),

(31)

\displaystyle\left|\phi_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N_{\textrm{well}}+1}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\sin(n\omega),\sin(2n\omega),\cdots,\sin(nN_{\textrm{well}}\omega)\right)^{T},

(32)

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{N_{\textrm{well}}+1}.

(33)

Next, we focus on the
n
-th QWS, whose energy is close to
E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}
.
To stabilize this state, the following conditions must be satisfied:

\displaystyle E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}<-2t,\quad N_{\textrm{well}}\geq n.

(34)

When we focus on the energy
E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}
, we are only required to consider the corresponding contribution in Eq. (
29
).
Subsequently, for
\varepsilon=E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}+i\gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\textrm{s}}_{\textrm{well}}(\varepsilon)\approx\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}}\frac{2}{N_{\textrm{well}}+1}\sin^{2}(n\omega).

(35)

Next, we consider
\Sigma_{\textrm{sub}}(\varepsilon)
expressed as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\textrm{s}}_{\textrm{sub}}(\varepsilon)=t^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}-4t^{2}}}{2t^{2}}\right)

(36)

By performing the Taylor expansion around
\varepsilon-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}
, we have

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\textrm{s}}_{\textrm{sub}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}+\sqrt{E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})2}-4t^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}}{2\sqrt{E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})2}-4t^{2}}}\right)(\varepsilon-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})})

\displaystyle+O\left[\left(\varepsilon-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}\right)^{2}\right]

(37)

Using Eqs. (
35
) and (
37
),
G(\varepsilon)
in Eq. (
25
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle G(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\varepsilon-\frac{e_{2}}{\varepsilon-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}}+e_{1}+e_{0}(\varepsilon-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})})},

(38)

\displaystyle e_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}+\frac{E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}}{2\sqrt{E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})2}-4t^{2}}},

(39)

\displaystyle e_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}+\sqrt{E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})2}-4t^{2}}\right),

(40)

\displaystyle e_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{2t^{2}}{N_{\textrm{well}}+1}\sin^{2}(n\omega).

(41)

Based on this formula of Green’s function, we can compute the pole associated to with the QWS as follows:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}

\displaystyle+\frac{e_{1}-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}\pm\sqrt{4e_{2}(1-e_{0})+(e_{1}-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})})^{2}}}{2(1-e_{0})}.

(42)

The minus sign is relevant to satisfy the condition
\varepsilon_{n}=E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})}
when
t=0
and
d>0
.
The corresponding residue is

\displaystyle\textrm{Res}_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}}[G_{00}(\varepsilon)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{4e_{2}(1-e_{0})+(e_{1}-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})})^{2}}-(e_{1}-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})})}{2(1-e_{0})\sqrt{4e_{2}(1-e_{0})+(e_{1}-E_{n}^{(\textrm{well})})^{2}}}.

(43)

By combining Eqs. (
22
) and (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{R}}\approx\alpha_{\textrm{R}}^{\delta V}\textrm{Res}_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}}[G_{00}(\varepsilon)],

(44)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{R}}^{\delta V}\equiv\frac{\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{sub}}}{c_{\textrm{sub}}}-\frac{\langle\delta V\rangle_{\textrm{well}}}{c_{\textrm{well}}}

(45)

governs the strength of the SOC in the Rashba effect, whereas
\textrm{Res}_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}}[G_{00}(\varepsilon)]
governs the shape of the electronic state.
Based on the discussion above, the only free parameter in Eq. (
43
) is the ratio between the onsite potential
d
and the hopping integral
t
.
Therefore, in this analysis, only two parameters (
\alpha_{\textrm{R}}^{\delta V}
and
d/t
) govern the Rashba splitting.

Next, we explain the numerical condition for the first-principles calculation.
The slab models of Ag/Au(111) were constructed as follows:

The basis set was configured as follows: Two, two, two, and one radial wavefunctions with a cutoff radius of 7.0 a.u. were used for the s-, p-, d-, and f-orbitals of the Ag atom (Ag7.0-s2p2d2f1), respectively, and four, three, two, and one radial wavefunctions with a cutoff radius of 7.0 a.u. for the corresponding orbitals of the Au atom (Au7.0-s4p3d2f1).

IV
Summary

We formulated a theory to obtain the
k
-linear Rashba splitting energy systematically from the SOC Hamiltonian by employing reasonable assumptions, such as broken inversion symmetry, two- or three-fold symmetry around the
z
-axis, and a steep nuclear potential.
Subsequently, we derived a minimum model that captures the Rashba effect in a QWS using a one-dimensional tight-binding model and Green’s function.
Our theory agreed qualitatively with the first-principles result of a realistic Ag/Au(111) system using only two fitting parameters.
Furthermore, we predicted the optimal condition to obtain a large Rashba splitting.
This result can be utilized to design new material systems for realizing efficient spintronics devices.
Moreover, our theoretical framework can be extended to, for example, more complex nanostructures such as three-step QWSs.
