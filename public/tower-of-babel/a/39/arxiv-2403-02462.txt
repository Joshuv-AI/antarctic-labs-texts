Edge states for tight–binding operators with soft walls
Camilo Gómez Araya David Gontier Hanne Van Den Bosch

Abstract
. We study one– and two–dimensional periodic tight-binding models in the presence of a potential that grows to infinity in one direction, hence preventing the particles to escape in this direction (the soft wall). We prove that a spectral flow appears in these edge models, as the wall is shifted with respect to the lattice. We identity this flow with the number of Bloch bands. This provides a lower bound for the number of edge states appearing in such models. For the two-dimensional case, we compute the spectral flow for edges that have any rational orientation with respect to the lattice. The results are illustrated by applying them to the one-dimensional SSH chain and the Wallace model for graphene. © 2025 by the authors. 1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to study
edge states
in terminated periodic structures described by tight–binding (TB) Hamiltonians. Such TB operators are extensively studied in condensed matter, as they provide simple models which correctly reproduce the physics of more complex ones (represented
e.g.
by Schrödinger operators acting on the continuum). This research is motivated by models from solid state physics such as the one-dimensional Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) chain
[
34
]
and the two-dimensional Wallace model for graphene
[
36
]
. Indeed, it is known for these models that edge states may appear when these periodic systems are restricted to a halfspace. For the Wallace model for instance, the presence of these edge states depends strongly on the direction of the cut. Notable examples are the
zigzag cut
, where a flat band of edge modes appear, and the
armchair cut
, where no edge states appear, see
[
1
,
29
,
8
,
13
,
11
,
12
]
.
Even in the simple one–dimensional SSH model,
an edge state appears or disappears depending on which of the nonequivalent
bonds
is cut.
In these one-dimensional systems, the topological versus
trivial
nature of experimentally observed edge states is still a topic of hot debate (see e.g.
[
30
]
for theoretical considerations and
[
5
]
for recent experimental results) ever since the first reports of Majorana fermions in superconducting wires
[
6
,
28
]
.

In the present article, we give a framework to study the appearance of edge modes in general TB models arising in a wide variety of physical situations, such as graphene sheets, superconducting wires or optical lattices. However, instead of working with
hard truncations
(Dirichlet boundary conditions), as it is usually done in numerical simulations, we are interested in edge modes that arise from the lattice termination by a
soft wall
, that is, a continuous potential barrier that impedes the propagation in one halfspace. The main reason is that one cannot translate properly a
hard cut
in TB models. To illustrate the problem, imagine a TB problem on the line
\mathbb{Z}
, with the presence of a hard wall whose position is parametrized by
t\in\mathbb{R}
, with the convention that one
deletes
all sites on the left of
t
. Then, when
t
varies in an interval of the form
(n-1,n)
, nothing changes, but when
t
crosses the site
n
, the corresponding Hilbert space suddenly switches from
\ell^{2}(\{n,n+1,\cdots\})
to
\ell^{2}(\{n+1,n+2,\cdots\})
. The corresponding operators are not continuous in
t
, which makes this case difficult to study. Note that, for some experimental realizations of periodic systems, confinement by a smooth potential instead of a sharp lattice termination is easily achieved. Numerical results on the effect of soft confinement in different configurations can be found for instance in
[
4
,
16
,
17
]
.

We prove the appearance of a spectral flow in the gaps of the essential spectrum as the soft wall is shifted, and relate this flow with a number of Bloch bands (see Theorem
1.1
). In particular, our result implies the presence of edge states for
many
values of
t
. Surprisingly, this spectral flow is independent of the shape of the wall. However, in the case where the soft wall varies slowly with respect to the lattice constant, we can prove that edge states always appear, for
all
values of
t
, regardless of the topological properties of the material (see Theorem
1.3
below).

When studying the analogue of this problem for models set on the continuum, that is for Schrödinger operators with periodic potentials in
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, one can use the spectral flow to study the effect of hard truncations, modelled by a Dirichlet boundary condition along a line parametrized by a translation parameter
t
(the cut). These models have been studied in
[
24
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
10
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
, where it is proved that a spectral flow appears as the cut is translated. In addition, this spectral flow can be computed explicitly, and is related to a number of Bloch bands of the initial bulk operator. The results of the present paper are the discrete analogue of these, although the techniques of proof are rather different.

In the two–dimensional case, we also prove that numerous edge modes must appear when one cuts such materials with a commensurate angle having large numerators/denominators. Actually, following the lines of
[
19
]
, one could prove that
all bulk gaps are filled with edge spectrum
in the incommensurate case (although we do not provide a full proof here, as it is similar to the one in
[
19
]
).

Let us give a short description of our main result in the one–dimensional setting. We consider a general TB bulk operator
H
acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
, of the convolution form

\forall\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N}),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad(H\Psi)_{n}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}h(m)\Psi_{n-m}=:(h*\Psi)_{n}

where for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
h(n)
is an
N\times N
matrix such that
h^{*}(n)=h(-n)
. We assume throughout that
\sum_{n}\left\|h_{n}\right\|_{\rm op}<\infty
, which guarantees the boundedness of the operator by Young’s inequality (see Lemma
2.1
below). Strictly speaking, the model is set on
a_{0}\mathbb{Z}
with
a_{0}>0
the length of the unit cell. In what follows, we take
a_{0}=1
. By taking a Fourier transform, the spectrum of
H
is purely essential, of the form

\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}:=\sigma(H)=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\sigma(H_{k}),\quad\text{with}\quad H_{k}:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}h(m){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}km}.

The map
k\mapsto H_{k}
is
2\pi
–periodic and analytic, and takes values in
{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
, the set of Hermitian
N\times N
matrices. Let
\lambda_{1,k}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N,k}
be the eigenvalues of
H_{k}
ranked in increasing order. By standard perturbation theory
[
27
]
, for all
1\leq j\leq N
, the map
k\mapsto\lambda_{j,k}
is continuous and
2\pi
–periodic. The
j
-th
Bloch band
is the image of this map, namely the interval
\bigcup_{k}\{\lambda_{j,k}\}
. The connected components of
\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
are the
gaps
in the essential spectrum. For an energy
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, we denote by
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
the number of Bloch bands below
E
. It is the unique integer such that

\forall k\in[-\pi,\pi],\qquad\lambda_{{\mathcal{N}}(E),k}<E<\lambda_{{\mathcal{N}}(E)+1,k},

with the convention that
\lambda_{0,k}=-\infty
and
\lambda_{N+1,k}=+\infty
. Occasionally, when comparing several bulk operators, we will also write
{\mathcal{N}}_{H}(E)
.

We now perturb the bulk Hamiltonian by adding the wall potential. We fix a Lipschitz continuous function
w:\mathbb{R}\to{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
satisfying

(1)

\forall v\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus\{0\},\qquad\lim_{x\to-\infty}\langle v,w(x)v\rangle=+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{x\to+\infty}\langle v,w(x)v\rangle=0.

The first limit states that the lowest eigenvalue of
w(x)
diverges to
+\infty
as
x\to-\infty
, and the second that all entries of the matrix
w
converge to
0
as
x\to+\infty
.
From
w
, we define the wall operator
W(t)
acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
as the multiplication operator

(2)

\forall\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N}),\qquad(W(t)\Psi)_{n}:=w(n-t)\Psi_{n}.

The parameter
t\in\mathbb{R}
indicates a shift in the position of the wall.
Our goal is to study the family of
edge
operators
t\mapsto H^{\sharp}(t)
defined by

H^{\sharp}(t):=H+W(t),\quad\text{specifically}\quad\left(H^{\sharp}(t)\Psi\right)_{n}=(h*\Psi)_{n}+w(n-t)\Psi_{n}.

It is not difficult to see that the family
t\mapsto H^{\sharp}(t)
is translation equivariant, in the sense that
H^{\sharp}(t+1)=\tau_{1}^{*}H^{\sharp}(t)\tau_{1}
, where
\tau_{1}
is the usual translation operator
(\tau_{1}\Psi)_{n}:=\Psi_{n-1}
. We prove in Proposition
2.4
below that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
H^{\sharp}(t)
is self-adjoint with a domain independent of
t
, and that the essential spectrum of
H^{\sharp}(t)
is also independent of
t
. However, some extra eigenvalues may appear in the essential gaps as
t
moves. For
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, we denote by

{\mathrm{Sf}}(H^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1])

the
spectral flow
of
t\mapsto H^{\sharp}(t)
at energy
E
as
t
increases from
0
to
1
. It counts the net number of eigenvalues of
t\mapsto H^{\sharp}(t)
crossing the energy
E
downwards (see Appendix
A
for a precise definition, following
[
2
,
31
,
9
]
). Let us state our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
be a Lipschitz function satisfying (
1
).
For all
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, the energy
E
is in the essential gaps of all operators
H^{\sharp}(t)
, and

{\mathrm{Sf}}(H^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1])=-{\mathcal{N}}(E),

where we recall that
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
is the number of Bloch bands below
E
for the bulk–operator.

In particular, the spectral flow is independent of the precise expression for the soft wall. A nonzero spectral flow implies that, at least for some values of
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the set
\sigma_{\rm{edge}}(t):=\sigma(H^{\sharp}(t))\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, sometimes called the
edge spectrum
, is non empty. It only consists of eigenvalues. The corresponding eigenvectors are called
edge
states, and describe localized modes which are blocked by the wall on the left, and which cannot propagate in the medium on the right.

Let us briefly explain the idea of the proof with the help of the graphical representation in Figure
1
. First, we show that the spectral flow for any soft wall is equal to the spectral flow for a simplified
steep wall
model that essentially behaves as a step potential with value
\Sigma\gg E
(Figure
1
.1). Next, we show that the spectral flow decouples in a left and right contribution by summing a compact perturbation that erases some bonds (Figure
1
.2). Here, the right-hand side of the picture does not contribute to the spectral flow at energy
E
.
A similar procedure can be performed on the
dislocated model
shown in Figure
1
.3. In this model, as
t
increases by one, one site decouples from the otherwise periodic chain. A suitable compact perturbation yields a cut dislocated model shown in Figure
1
.4.
As before, the left-hand-side does not contribute to the spectral flow while the right-hand-sides coincides with that of the steep wall model.
This allows us to show that the spectral flow for any soft wall model equals the spectral flow of the related dislocated model.
Then, the spectral flow for the dislocated model can be computed exactly, by interpreting it as the limit of finite chains with periodic boundary conditions.

Figure 1
.
Schematic illustration of the models in the proof. In the first figure, as
t
varies from
0
to
1
, the wall is pushed one cell to the right. We prove that all spectral flows coincide for these four scenarios, and explicitly compute the third one.

Remark 1.2
.

The result is the discrete analogue to the one in
[
20
]
, up to the minus sign. This is due to the fact that in the present article, the wall is moving to the right when
t
increases, which is the direction opposite to the one in
[
20
]
. In
[
19
,
20
]
(see also
[
21
]
), one of us gave an interpretation of this result, that we called the
Grand Hilbert Hotel
, that might be useful. It is similar to the charge pumping denomination of Thouless
[
35
]
. Basically, we may think of each Bloch band as a hotel floor with an infinite number of rooms, each room occupied by one guest (or fermion).
As
t
goes from
0
to
1
, the wall is pushed on the right, deleting the leftmost rooms. A particle from the lowest band moves up to the second band, giving a spectral flow of
-1
in the gap above the first band. Two particles cross the gap above the second band: one from the deleted leftmost room, and one to accommodate the new arrival from the first band, and so on.

In practice, one is often interested in the spectrum of
H^{\sharp}(t=t_{0})
, that is for a single value of
t
. We have the following (see Section
2.3
for the proof).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
be a soft wall which is
\nu
–Lipschitz, for some
\nu>0
. If
E
belongs to an essential gap of
H
, then for all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
the operator
H^{\sharp}(t_{0})
has at least
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
eigenvalues in each interval of the form
(\lambda,\lambda+\nu]
in this gap.

Remark 1.4
.

Recall that we took the length scale of the chain to be
a_{0}=1
, so
\lambda
and
\nu
have the same units. To restore a length scale and work instead on
a_{0}\mathbb{Z}
, it is convenient to define the operator
W
in (
2
) by
(W(t)\Psi)_{R}:=w(R-ta_{0})\Psi_{R}
for
R\in a_{0}\mathbb{Z}
, so that
t\mapsto H(t)
is translation equivariant with period
1
. If
w(\cdot)
is
\nu
-Lipschitz, then
W(\cdot)
and
H(\cdot)
are
\nu a_{0}
–Lipschitz, and there are
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
eigenvalues in each interval of the form
(\lambda,\lambda+\nu a_{0}]
.

This theorem states that the density of eigenvalues in the gap containing
E
is at least
{\mathcal{N}}(E)/\nu
. If
\nu
is small, the potential
W
increases slowly to
+\infty
on the left. It somehow acts as a local energy shift. It is therefore not so surprising that many eigenvalues appear in all gaps in this situation. On the other hand, if
\nu
is very large, (imagine a situation where one approximates a hard cut by a sequence of soft wall models with
\nu\to\infty
) then the spectral flow does not give information on the spectrum of
H^{\sharp}(t_{0})
: it may or may not contain extra eigenvalues. Figure
4
below illustrates this situation for the SSH model.

Before we go on, let us recall that there are several ways to derive TB models from the ones set on the continuum. One approach, called the
strong binding limit
, is given in
[
14
,
15
,
32
]
, and consists in studying the limit of Schrödinger operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with a periodic potential, when the
wells
of the potential approach a delta-function centered on each atom. In this limit (similar to the semi-classical one), the wave-function
\Psi
is replaced by a discrete function giving the amplitude of
\Psi
associated to each atom. Another way to derive TB models is through the use of Wannier functions. In this case, one finds a countable set of functions
(w_{i})
which are well-localized around the atoms
({\mathbf{x}}_{i})
, and which span the lowest part of the spectrum of the Hamiltonian. A wave–function
\Psi(x)
is then approximated by the sum
\sum c_{i}w_{i}(x)
. Thanks to the localization of
w_{i}
, the value
c_{i}w_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})
can be interpreted as the value
\Psi({\mathbf{x}}_{i})
. The hopping transition between atoms
i
and
j
are then
t_{ij}=\langle w_{i},Hw_{j}\rangle
. We refer to
[
33
,
22
]
for more details.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we prove some basic properties of one-dimensional Hamiltonians with and without soft walls. We explain how to reduce the problem to the somewhat easier case of periodic
Jacobi
operators and prove Theorem
1.1
assuming it holds for Jacobi operators. As an illustration of the general results we discuss the SSH chain in Section
2.4
and present numerical simulations of its spectrum. In Section
2.3
, we give the proof of Theorem
1.3
, again assuming the version of Theorem
1.1
for Jacobi operators.

The computation of the spectral flow for periodic Jacobi operators takes up the entire Section
3
. This is the main technical part of the paper.

In Section
4
, we explain how to extend our result in the two–dimensional setting. The main observation is that a 2d model, even cut by a (commensurate) soft wall, is still periodic in the direction along the wall. After a Fourier transform in this direction, we are left with a family of one-dimensional models, indexed by the momentum in the direction along the wall. We prove in Theorem
4.5
that the greater the incommensurability of the cut, the more abundant the edge states. In Section
4.3
, we specify to the Wallace model for graphene to illustrate the concepts and give numerical results on the edge states.

Finally, we provide in Appendix
A
a precise definition of the Spectral Flow adapted to our setting, and establish some of its properties that are used in the body of the paper.

2.
Spectral flows in one-dimensional models

In this section, we prove our results in the one–dimensional setting.

2.1.
Generalities for bulk and edge Hamiltonians

2.1.1.
Bulk periodic Hamiltonians

We work in this section in the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
, with operators that are periodic, i.e., commute with the shift operator
\tau_{1}
defined by
(\tau_{1}\Psi)_{n}:=\Psi_{n-1}
. Such operators appear in TB models for a periodic chain of atoms, and
N
represents the number of particles in one unit cell. The corresponding Hamiltonian can be written as a discrete convolution in the form

(3)

(H\Psi)_{n}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}h(m)\Psi_{n-m}=(h*\Psi)_{n}.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
h\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N\times N})
and satisfies the symmetry condition
h(n)^{*}=h(-n)
, then
H
is a bounded self-adjoint operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
.

Proof.

The symmetry condition
h(n)^{*}=h(-n)
ensures that
H
is a symmetric operator. In addition, the fact that
h
is summable implies that
H
is a bounded operator. Indeed, by the discrete Young inequality for convolutions, we have

\forall\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N}),\quad\|H\Psi\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|h*\psi\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\|h\|_{\ell^{1}}\|\psi\|_{\ell^{2}},\quad\text{with}\quad\|h\|_{\ell^{1}}:=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\|h(n)\|_{{\rm{op}}}.

So
H
is a bounded symmetric operator, hence self-adjoint.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Throughout this section, we will use the term
periodic
to refer to 1-periodic models. If a Hamiltonian is periodic with period
K
for some integer
K>1
, we can identify
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
with
\ell^{2}(K\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{K\,N})
hence with
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{K\,N})
, and obtain a 1-periodic model with larger
blocks
. This effectively changes the length–scale
a_{0}
into
\widetilde{a}_{0}:=Ka_{0}
. See Subsection
2.4
for an example of this.

We define the unitary Fourier transform by
{\mathcal{F}}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})\to L^{2}([-\pi,\pi],\mathbb{C}^{N})
by

(4)

\left({\mathcal{F}}[\Psi]\right)(k):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\Psi_{m}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}km},\quad\text{so that}\quad\Psi_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}{\mathcal{F}}[\Psi](k){\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}kn}{\mathrm{d}}k,

and note that for any
u\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi],\mathbb{C}^{N})
,

(5)

\left({\mathcal{F}}H{\mathcal{F}}^{*}u\right)(k)=H_{k}u(k),\quad\text{with}\qquad H_{k}:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}h(m){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}km}.

Since
h(n)=h^{*}(-n)
, the matrix
H_{k}
is Hermitian for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\lambda_{1,k}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N,k}
be the eigenvalues of
H_{k}
ranked in increasing order. Since the map
k\mapsto H_{k}
is
2\pi
–periodic and analytic, the maps
k\mapsto\lambda_{j,k}
are
2\pi
–periodic and continuous (we loose analyticity when eigenvalues intersect). In what follows, we set
\Omega^{*}:=(-\pi,\pi]
. The spectral theorem then shows that

(6)

\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}:=\sigma(H)=\bigcup_{k\in\Omega^{*}}\sigma\left(H_{k}\right)=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\bigcup_{k\in\Omega^{*}}\{\lambda_{j,k}\}.

The spectrum of
H
consists of
N

Bloch bands
, which are the images of
k\mapsto\lambda_{j,k}
. Each Bloch band is a closed interval by continuity and periodicity of this map. The intervals in the complement of this spectrum are called
gaps
, or
bulk gaps
.

2.1.2.
Soft wall operators

Our goal is to study such periodic Hamiltonians, in the presence of a soft wall. Let us specify the class of walls that we use in this article.

Definition 2.3
.

An operator
W
acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
is a soft wall operator if it is of the form

\forall n\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad(W\Psi)_{n}=w(n)\Psi_{n},

where
w:\mathbb{R}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
is
\nu
–Lipschitz for some
\nu>0
, and satisfies (
1
), i.e. has the limits

\forall v\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus\{0\},\qquad\lim_{x\to-\infty}\langle v,w(x)v\rangle=+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{x\to+\infty}\langle v,w(x)v\rangle=0.

The first limit implies that for all
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, there is
x_{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}
so that

\forall x\leq x_{\Sigma},\qquad w(x)\geq\Sigma.

Since
w
is Lipschitz, it is continuous. Also, since it goes from
+\infty
to
0
, it is bounded from below. The shifted, or translated, soft wall operator is

(W(t)\Psi)_{n}:=w(n-t)\Psi_{n}.

It is an unbounded self-adjoint operator, with domain

{\mathcal{D}}(W(t)):=\left\{\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N}),\quad W(t)\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})\right\}.

Note that
\left\|W(t)-W(0)\right\|_{{\rm{op}}}=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|w(n-t)-w(n)\right\|_{{\rm{op}}}\leq\nu|t|
, so this domain is independent of
t
and we will denote it simply by
{\mathcal{D}}(W)
. Let
\mu_{1,x}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{N,x}
be the eigenvalues of
w(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
w
is
\nu
–Lipschitz, all the curves
x\mapsto\mu_{1,x}
are
\nu
–Lipschitz as well. In addition, all these curves go from
+\infty
to
0
on
\mathbb{R}
. As
W(t)
is block diagonal with block elements
w(n-t)
, we directly deduce that

(7)

\sigma(W(t))=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sigma(w(n-t))=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\{\mu_{j,n-t}\},\quad\text{and that}\quad\sigma_{\rm ess}(W_{t})=\{0\}.

The spectrum is purely discrete, composed of eigenvalues. They are all of finite multiplicities (except maybe
0
), and the only accumulation point is
0
. In addition, the map
t\mapsto W(t)
is translation equivariant, in the sense that

W(t+1)=\tau_{1}^{*}W(t)\tau_{1},\qquad\text{with}\quad(\tau_{1}\Psi)_{n}:=\Psi_{n-1}.

In particular, the spectrum is
1
–periodic (which can be read directly from (
7
)). Figure
2
illustrates the situation.

Figure 2
.
Left: the function
w(x):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+1}-x\right)
, with
N=1
. Right: the spectrum of the corresponding operator
W(t)
, as a function of
t
(the
n=0
case is in red). As
t
increases, the wall moves to the right. Here,
t\mapsto W(t)
is operator increasing.

We define the
edge
operator
H^{\sharp}(t):=H+W(t)
. Let us record some properties of this operator.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
be a Lipschitz function satisfying (
1
).

(1)

For all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the operator
H^{\sharp}(t)
is self-adjoint on the constant domain
{\mathcal{D}}(W)
.

(2)

The map
t\mapsto H^{\sharp}(t)
is norm–resolvent continuous and translation equivariant, in the sense that
H^{\sharp}(t+1)=\tau_{1}^{*}H^{\sharp}(t)\tau_{1}
, where
\tau_{1}
is the usual translation operator
(\tau_{1}\Psi)_{n}:=\Psi_{n-1}
;

(3)

The essential spectrum
\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H^{\sharp}(t))
is independent of
t
, and equals
\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

For the first point, the bulk operator
H
is bounded and self-adjoint, hence is a bounded perturbation of the self-adjoint operator
W(t)
. Thus,
H^{\sharp}(t)
is self-adjoint on
{\mathcal{D}}(W)
.

For the second point, note that
H
is independent of
t
while
the map
t\mapsto W(t)
is norm–resolvent continuous. Indeed, the operator
({\mathrm{i}}-W(t))^{-1}
is block diagonal with block elements
({\mathrm{i}}-w(n-t))^{-1}
, and we have the bound

\displaystyle\left\|\dfrac{1}{{\mathrm{i}}-w(n-t)}-\dfrac{1}{{\mathrm{i}}-w(n-t^{\prime})}\right\|_{\rm{op}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\dfrac{1}{{\mathrm{i}}-w(n-t)}\left[w(n-t)-w(n-t^{\prime})\right]\dfrac{1}{{\mathrm{i}}-w(n-t^{\prime})}\right\|_{\rm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\nu|t^{\prime}-t|.

The translation equivariance follows from the translation equivariance of
W(t)
and the periodicity of
H
. Since
\tau_{1}
is unitary, it implies that the spectrum of
H^{\sharp}(t)
is 1-periodic in
t
.

It remains to prove the third point, and compute the essential spectrum of
H^{\sharp}(t)
. We omit the argument
t
for shortness. We identify

\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
with
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{-},\mathbb{C}^{N})\oplus\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{C}^{N})

and define the restrictions
\Pi_{L}
from
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
onto
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{-},\mathbb{C}^{N})
and
\Pi_{R}
from
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
onto
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
We write

\displaystyle H^{\sharp}=H^{\sharp}_{L}\oplus H^{\sharp}_{R}+K,\quad\text{with}\quad H^{\sharp}_{L}=\Pi_{L}H^{\sharp}\Pi_{L}^{*},\quad H^{\sharp}_{R}=\Pi_{R}H^{\sharp}\Pi_{R}^{*}

and

\displaystyle K=\Pi_{R}H^{\sharp}\Pi_{L}^{*}+\Pi_{L}H^{\sharp}\Pi_{R}^{*}=\Pi_{R}H\Pi_{L}^{*}+\Pi_{L}H\Pi_{R}^{*},

where the last equality comes from the fact that
W
is diagonal.
First, we claim that
K
is a compact operator. Indeed, for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce the finite range one–periodic Hamiltonian

(8)

H_{\ell}\Psi:=h_{\ell}*\Psi,\quad\text{with kernel}\quad h_{\ell}(n)=h(n){\mathds{1}}(|n|\leq\ell).

Since
h_{\ell}
converges to
h
in
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N\times N})
, we have
\|H-H_{\ell}\|_{\rm op}\to 0
as
\ell\to\infty
by Young’s inequality (see the proof of Lemma
2.1
). So we also have
\|K-K_{\ell}\|_{\rm op}\to 0
, with
K_{\ell}:=\Pi_{R}H_{\ell}\Pi_{L}^{*}+\Pi_{L}H_{\ell}\Pi_{R}^{*}
. In addition,
K_{\ell}
is finite rank for all
\ell>0
, so
K
is compact, as stated.

Since
K
is compact, it does not affect the essential spectrum, so
\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H^{\sharp})=\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H^{\sharp}_{L})\bigcup\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H^{\sharp}_{R})
. Let us identify the essential spectra of
H^{\sharp}_{L}
and
H^{\sharp}_{R}
.
To the right of the wall,
W
is bounded and decays to zero. Thus,
\Pi_{R}W\Pi_{R}^{*}
is compact and

\sigma_{{\rm{ess}}}(H^{\sharp}_{R})=\sigma_{{\rm{ess}}}(H_{R}).

We have
\sigma_{{\rm{ess}}}(H_{R})=\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
: the inclusion
\sigma_{{\rm{ess}}}(H_{R})\subset\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
can be shown directly by extending a singular Weyl sequence for
H_{R}
by zero to the left. For the opposite inclusion, consider a singular Weyl sequence
(\Psi^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
for
H
and fix a translation
R_{k}
such that
\left\|\Pi_{L}\Psi^{k}_{R_{k}}\right\|\leq 1/k
, where
\Psi_{R}(n):=\Psi(n-R)
is the translation of
\Psi
by
R
. Then
\Pi_{R}\Psi^{k}_{R_{k}}
is a singular Weyl sequence for
H_{R}
.

To the left of the wall,
W_{L}
has compact resolvent, since it has eigenvalues of finite multiplicity and without accumulation points. Since
H
is bounded,

H_{L}(W_{L}-i)^{-1}
is compact and
\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H^{\sharp}_{L})=\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(W_{L})=\emptyset
.
∎

2.2.
Computation of the spectral flow: reduction to the Jacobi case

We now compute the spectral flow of
t\mapsto H^{\sharp}(t)
. Recall that, for
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, the spectral flow
{\mathrm{Sf}}(H^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1])
counts the net number of eigenvalue branches that cross
E
downwards, as
t
varies between
0
and
1
. A precise definition together with some properties can be found in Appendix
A
. The following example illustrates the concept.

Example 2.1
.

For the soft wall operator
t\mapsto W(t)
, we have

\forall E>0,\qquad{\mathrm{Sf}}(W(t),E,[0,1])=-N,\qquad\text{while}\qquad\forall E<0,\qquad{\mathrm{Sf}}(W(t),E,[0,1])=0.

This can be seen directly from Figure
2
. This case fits in the framework of Theorem
1.1
with
H=0
, since in this case the
N

bands
consist of the singleton
0
.

We will take advantage of the robustness of the spectral flow and reduce the problem to simpler models. For instance, we will first prove the results for
1
–periodic Jacobi operators (for which
h(n)=0
for
|n|\geq 2
), and then extend it to all
1
–periodic Hamiltonians by an approximation argument. The proof that the spectral flow equals
-{\mathcal{N}}(E)
for Jacobi operator is postponed to the next Section. In this section, we explain how to generalize the result from Jacobi operators to any periodic Hamiltonian.

A
Jacobi operator
is an operator
H
acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
of the form

(9)

\forall\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N}),\qquad(H\Psi)_{n}:=a_{n-1}^{*}\Psi_{n-1}+b_{n}\Psi_{n}+a_{n}\Psi_{n+1},

where
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are two families of complex–valued
N\times N
matrices, with
b_{n}=b_{n}^{*}
. We will often represent such operators with a block matrix of the form

H=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c}\ddots&\ddots&&&&\\
\ddots&b_{-2}&a_{-2}&&&\\
&a_{-2}^{*}&b_{-1}&a_{-1}&&\\
\hline\cr&&a_{-1}^{*}&b_{0}&a_{0}&\\
&&&a_{0}^{*}&b_{1}&\ddots\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right).

We used the canonical basis of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
, and the two straight lines indicate the decomposition
\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}^{-}\cup\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
\mathbb{Z}^{-}:=\{n\in\mathbb{Z},\ n<0\}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\{n\in\mathbb{Z},\ n\geq 0\}
. A Jacobi operator is
K
–
periodic
if
a_{n+K}=a_{n}
and
b_{n+K}=b_{n}
. It is
1
–periodic, or simply periodic, if
b_{n}=b
and
a_{n}=a
are independent of
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Periodic Jacobi operators are of the convolution form (
3
) with

h(-1)=a,\quad h(0)=b,\quad h(1)=a^{*}.

In this simple case, the bulk spectrum (
6
) is given by

(10)

\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}:=\sigma(H)=\bigcup_{k\in\Omega^{*}}\sigma\left(H_{k}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad H_{k}:=a^{*}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}k}+b+a{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k}.

We will establish a version of Theorem
1.1
in the special case of Jacobi operators.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
H
be a Jacobi operator and
w:\mathbb{R}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
be a Lipschitz function satisfying (
1
). Define the operator family
H^{\sharp}(t)
as in Section
2.1.2
.
For all
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, the energy
E
is in the essential gaps of all operators
H^{\sharp}(t)
, and

{\mathrm{Sf}}(H^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1])=-{\mathcal{N}}(E).

The proof of this Proposition is technically the most involved part of the paper. Its full proof is the topic of Section
3
. For now, let us explain how to deduce Theorem
1.1
from it, that is how to go from the periodic Jacobi case to the general
1
–periodic Hamiltonians.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
as a corollary of Proposition
2.5
.

First, we explain how the case of finite-range interactions can be reduced to the Jacobi case, using
supercells
, and next we show that convolution by
\ell^{1}
-kernels can be approximated by finite rank kernels.

Step 1 : From
1
–periodic Jacobi operators to finite range interactions

Assume indeed that the periodic Hamiltonian
H
has a kernel
h
with finite range, say
h(n)=0
for all
n>\ell
. Then we can write
H
as a Jacobi operator acting on
\ell^{2}(\ell\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{\ell N})
, by defining the supercell kernel
\widetilde{h}
with

\widetilde{h}(-1)=\widetilde{a},\quad\widetilde{h}(0)=\widetilde{b},\quad\widetilde{h}(1)=\widetilde{a}^{*}.

where
\widetilde{b}
and
\widetilde{a}
are matrices of size
(\ell N)\times(\ell N)
, given by

\widetilde{b}:=\begin{pmatrix}h(0)&h(-1)&\cdots&h(-\ell+1)\\
h(1)&h(0)&\cdots&h(-\ell+2)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
h(\ell-1)&\cdots&h(1)&h(0)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{a}:=\begin{pmatrix}h(-\ell)&0&\cdots&0\\
h(-\ell+1)&h(-\ell)&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
h(-1)&h(-2)&\cdots&h(-\ell)\end{pmatrix}.

We denote by
\widetilde{H}
the corresponding operator acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{\ell N})
. Then
\sigma(H)=\sigma(\widetilde{H})
, and each Bloch band of
H
becomes a set of
\ell
bands for
\widetilde{H}
. In particular,
{\mathcal{N}}_{\widetilde{H}}(E)=\ell{\mathcal{N}}_{H}(E)
.

For the supercell soft wall, we set

\widetilde{w}(x):=\begin{pmatrix}w(\ell x)&0&\cdots&0\\
0&w(\ell x+1)&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&w(\ell x+\ell-1)\end{pmatrix},

and define
\widetilde{W}(t)
by the construction in Subsection
2.1.2
.
The operator
\widetilde{H}^{\sharp}(t):=\widetilde{H}+\widetilde{W}(t)
is a periodic Jacobi operator with the same spectrum as the original
H^{\sharp}(\ell t)
. As
t
increases from
0
to
1
, the parameter
\ell t
varies from
0
to
\ell
. In particular, the spectral flow satisfies

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(\widetilde{H}^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1]\right)={\mathrm{Sf}}\left(H^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,\ell]\right)=\ell\,{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(H^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1]\right).

On the other hand, Proposition
2.5
gives

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(\widetilde{H}^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=-{\mathcal{N}}_{\widetilde{H}}(E)=-\ell{\mathcal{N}}_{H}(E),

which proves Theorem
1.1
in the case of finite range kernels.

Step 2 : From finite range to integrable kernels

Now consider a general kernel
h
in
\ell^{1}
. For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the periodic Hamiltonian
H_{\ell}
with finite range potential

h_{\ell}(n):=h(n){\mathds{1}}(|n|\leq\ell).

This kernel has already been introduced in (
8
). Recall that
\|H-H_{\ell}\|_{\rm op}\to 0
as
\ell\to\infty
by Young’s inequality. Note also that
\|H^{\sharp}(t)-H_{\ell}^{\sharp}(t)\|_{\rm op}=\|H-H_{\ell}\|_{\rm op}
is independent of
t
, hence goes to
0
uniformly in
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, the spectrum of
H_{\ell}^{\sharp}(t)
approaches uniformly the spectrum of
H^{\sharp}(t)
.

Let us fix
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
and set

\varepsilon:=\operatorname{dist}(E,\sigma_{\rm{bulk}})>0.

The previous point shows that there exists
\ell_{0}
such that, for all
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
, we have
\|H-H_{\ell}\|_{\rm op}\leq\varepsilon/2
. For
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
, we obtain that
E
is not the essential spectrum of
H_{\ell}^{\sharp}(t)
for all
t
, hence the spectral flow
{\mathrm{Sf}}\left({H}^{\sharp}_{\ell}(t),[0,1],E\right)
is well-defined. In addition, we have, with obvious notations,
{\mathcal{N}}_{H_{\ell}}(E)={\mathcal{N}}_{H}(E)
for
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
.

We can now apply the robustness of the spectral flow with respect to small bounded perturbation, see Lemma
A.9
in the Appendix, and get that

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(H^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(H^{\sharp}_{\ell}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=-{\mathcal{N}}(E),

where we used that Theorem
1.1
holds for finite range interactions.
∎

2.3.
The spectrum at fixed
t_{0}
, proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
, which states that for all
t_{0}
and all
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, there are at least
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
eigenvalues in each interval of size
\nu
in the gap where
E
lies.

Proof.

To start, recall all eigenvalues branches are
\nu
-Lipschitz. Unfortunately, we could not find a reference for this well-known result, so let us give a short proof of this fact, and prove the following: Let
t\mapsto A(t)
be a continuous family of self-adjoint operators which is
\nu
-Lipschitz, in the sense
\|A(t)-A(s)\|_{\rm{op}}\leq\nu|t-x|
(this implies that they all have the same domain), then all isolated branches of eigenvalues are also
\nu
-Lipschitz.

In the case where
A(t)
is matrix valued (finite dimensional case), we first note that for all normalized
\Psi
, the map
t\mapsto\langle\Psi,A(t)\Psi\rangle
is
\nu
-Lipschitz. Indeed, we have

\left|\langle\Psi,A(t)\Psi\rangle-\langle\Psi,A(s)\Psi\rangle\right|=\left|\langle\Psi,A(t)-A(s),\Psi\rangle\right|\leq\|A(t)-A(s)\|_{\rm{op}}\|\Psi\|^{2}\leq\nu|t-s|.

Since the
j
-th lowest eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}(t)
is defined as a min–max combination of such
\nu
–Lipschitz functions, we conclude that all maps
t\mapsto\lambda_{j}(t)
are
\nu
–Lipschitz.

We now turn to the general case. Let
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\lambda_{0}:=\lambda(t_{0})
be an isolated eigenvalue of
A(t_{0})
, and let
m=\dim{\mathrm{Ker}}(A(t_{0})-\lambda_{0})
be its multiplicity. By continuity of the spectrum
[
27
]
, there is
\varepsilon>0
and
\eta>0
so that for all
t\in(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0}+\varepsilon)
the interval
(\lambda_{0}-\eta,\lambda_{0}+\eta)
contains exactly
m
eigenvalues of
A(t)
. We label the smallest eigenvalue as
\lambda_{1}(t)
and
\lambda_{m}(t)
for the largest one. In addition, by taking
\varepsilon
and
\eta
small enough, we may further assume that

\begin{cases}{\rm dist}\left(\lambda_{0}-\eta,\sigma(A(t))\right)={\rm dist}\left(\lambda_{0}-\eta,\lambda_{1}(t)\right)=\lambda_{1}(t)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta),\\
{\rm dist}\left(\lambda_{0}+\eta,\sigma(A(t))\right)={\rm dist}\left(\lambda_{0}+\eta,\lambda_{m}(t)\right)=(\lambda_{0}+\eta)-\lambda_{m}(t).\end{cases}

In particular, since
A(t)
is self-adjoint, we have
\|\left(A(t)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)\right)^{-1}\|_{\rm{op}}=\left(\lambda_{1}(t)-(\lambda_{0}+\eta)\right)^{-1}
. Let us prove that
t\mapsto\lambda_{1}(t)
is
\nu
–Lipschitz on the interval
(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0}+\varepsilon)
. We have

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{1}(t)-\lambda_{1}(s)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\lambda_{1}(t)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)\right|\cdot\left|\lambda_{1}(s)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)\right|\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(s)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(t)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)}\right|,

and the last term is also

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\frac{1}{A(s)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)}\right\|_{\rm{op}}-\left\|\frac{1}{A(t)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)}\right\|_{\rm{op}}\right|\leq\left\|\frac{1}{A(s)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)}-\frac{1}{A(t)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)}\right\|_{\rm{op}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\frac{1}{A(s)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)}\right\|_{\rm{op}}\left\|\frac{1}{A(t)-(\lambda_{0}-\eta)}\right\|_{\rm{op}}\left\|A(t)-A(s)\right\|_{\rm{op}}

We conclude that
\left|\lambda_{1}(t)-\lambda_{1}(s)\right|\leq\nu|t-s|
. The proof that
\lambda_{m}(s)
is
\nu
–Lipschitz on the interval
(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0}+\varepsilon)
is similar. Finally, for any local continuous branch of eigenvalue
\lambda(\cdot)
with
\lambda(t_{0})=\lambda_{0}
, we have

\lambda_{1}(t)-\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda(t)-\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{m}(t)-\lambda_{0},\quad\text{so}\quad\left|\lambda(t)-\lambda(t_{0})\right|\leq\nu|t-t_{0}|.

We therefore proved that for all
t_{0}
, there is
\varepsilon>0
so that any continuous branch
\lambda(\cdot)
of isolated eigenvalue satisfies
|\lambda(t)-\lambda(t_{0})|\leq\nu|t-t_{0}|
for all
t\in(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0}+\varepsilon)
. By compactness of
[t,s]
for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that
\lambda(\cdot)
is globally
\nu
–Lipschitz.

Now, let
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
with
{\mathcal{N}}(E)>0
. Since there is a nonzero spectral flow,
H^{\sharp}(t)
has eigenvalues for at least some values of
t
. By shifting
t
if necessary, we may assume
t=0
. We label this eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}(0)
, and denote by
\lambda_{0}(t)
a corresponding continuous branch. We use this as a starting point to label the eigenvalue curves
\lambda_{k}(t)
for
t\in[0,1]
such that, for all
t\in[0,1]

\lambda_{k}(t)\leq\lambda_{k+1}(t),\text{ where }k\in\mathbb{Z},

and with the convention that
\lambda_{k}(t)
equals the edge of the essential spectrum if there exists no
k
-th eigenvalue at this value of
t
. Since the spectrum is periodic, we have
\lambda_{k}(1)=\lambda_{j}(0)
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. The spectral flow equals
-{\mathcal{N}}(E)
, so there are exactly
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
eigenvalues that cross
\lambda_{k}(1)
upwards. We deduce that we actually have

\lambda_{k}(1)=\lambda_{k+{\mathcal{N}}(E)}(0)
for all values of
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\lambda_{k}(0)
is an eigenvalue of
H^{\sharp}(0)
.
Then, the Lipschitz continuity of
t\mapsto\lambda_{k}(t)
gives

\lambda_{k+{\mathcal{N}}(E)}(0)-\lambda_{k}(0)=\lambda_{k}(1)-\lambda_{k}(0)\leq\nu.

Hence, there are at least
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
eigenvalues for
H^{\sharp}(0)
in each interval of length
\nu
in the gap.
If
\nu
is smaller than the width of the gap, the same argument shows that there is edge spectrum for any
t_{0}\in[0,1]
, and we can repeat the counting argument for this
t_{0}
.

∎

2.4.
Example : the SSH model

Before going to the technicalities of the proof, let us illustrate our Theorem in the case of the Su–Schrieffer–Heeger (SHH) model
[
34
]
.

2.4.1.
Presentation of the model, and basic facts

Figure 3
.
Left: illustration of the SSH model. Right: its band structure for different values of
\delta:=||J_{1}|-|J_{2}||
.

The SSH model is a tight-binding Hamiltonian describing a polyacetylene chain, a simple polymer where the distance between neighboring carbon-hydrogen (CH) pairs takes alternating values. We denote by
d_{1}>0
(resp.
J_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
) the distance (resp. hopping amplitude) between the
(2n)
-th (CH) pair and the
(2n+1)
-th one, and by
d_{2}=1-d_{1}>0
(resp.
J_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
) the ones between
(2n+1)
-th and the
(2n+2)
-th. If
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
, then the distances alternate, hence so are the hopping parameters, see Figure
3
. In the tight-binding approximation (and for an idealized infinite chain), the Hamiltonian takes the form of a
2
–periodic Jacobi operator acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C})
with alternating hopping strengths
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
, that is

\begin{pmatrix}\ddots&\ddots&&&&\\
\ddots&0&J_{1}&&&\\
&J_{1}^{*}&0&J_{2}&&\\
&&J_{2}^{*}&0&J_{1}&\\
&&&J_{1}^{*}&0&\ddots\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

It most cases, we take
J_{1},J_{2}
real numbers.
Complex values of
J_{1},J_{2}
happen to appear in the case of the Wallace model for graphene with zigzag cuts.
In this case, the phases can be removed by defining a
gauge transformation

U=e^{iA}
where
A
is the multiplication operator by

A_{n}=\begin{cases}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\phi_{n}&n\geq 1\\
0&n=0\\
-\sum_{j=n}^{-1}\phi_{n}&n\leq-1\end{cases}\quad\text{ with }\phi_{n}=\begin{cases}\arg{J_{1}}&n\text{ odd}\\
\arg{J_{2}}&n\text{ even }\end{cases}.

The SSH Hamiltonian becomes a
1
-periodic Jacobi operator
H
if we take
N=2
and the
2\times 2
blocks

(11)

b=\begin{pmatrix}0&J_{1}\\
J^{*}_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad a=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
J_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}.

According to (
10
), the bulk spectrum can be computed from

H_{k}=b+a{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k}+a^{*}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}k}=\begin{pmatrix}0&J_{1}+J_{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}k}\\
J_{1}+J_{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k}&0\end{pmatrix},

whose spectrum is
\pm|J_{1}+J_{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k}|
. Note that, as
k
varies on
\mathbb{R}
, the complex number
J_{1}+J_{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k}
describes a circle with center
J_{1}
, and radius
|J_{2}|
. With this in mind, we conclude that

\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}:=\sigma(H)=\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H)=[-W,-\delta]\cup[\delta,W],\quad W:=|J_{1}|+|J_{2}|,\quad\delta:=\left||J_{1}|-|J_{2}|\right|.

In particular, is
|J_{1}|\neq|J_{2}|
, there is a gap of size
2\delta>0
around the origin, separating the two Bloch bands. In this gap, we have
{\mathcal{N}}(E)=1
.

Remark 2.6

(Hard truncation for SSH)
.

For the hard cut Hamiltonian

H^{\sharp}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&J_{1}&&\\
J_{1}^{*}&0&J_{2}&\\
&J_{2}^{*}&0&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

seen as an operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0},\mathbb{C})
(without supercell), then any
\psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0},\mathbb{C})
solution to
H^{\sharp}\psi=0
solves
J_{1}\psi_{1}=0
and
\psi_{n+2}=\frac{-J_{1}^{*}}{J_{2}}\psi_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
. We easily deduce that
\psi_{2n+1}=0
, and that
\psi
is non null and square integrable at
+\infty
iff
|J_{1}|<|J_{2}|
. So
0\in\sigma\left(H^{\sharp}\right)
iff
|J_{1}|<|J_{2}|
. When the hard cut is translated, and crosses one atom, the role of
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are exchanged. So, depending on the location of the hard cut, the eigenvalue
0
is a (protected) eigenvalue, or is not an eigenvalue.

2.4.2.
Numerical simulations for the SSH chain

We now compute numerically the spectral flow in the SSH model, in the presence of a shifted soft wall. For the soft wall, we take

w(x)=\begin{pmatrix}w_{1}(x)&0\\
0&w_{1}(x+d_{1})\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{with}\quad w_{1}(x):=\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{for}\quad x\geq 0\\
-\nu x&\quad\text{for}\quad x\leq 0\end{cases},

which corresponds to the evaluation of
w_{1}
at the location of each of the two atoms in the first unit cell. Note that
w
satisfy the assumption of a soft wall, and is
\nu
–Lipschitz.

To compute the edge spectrum of the edge operator, we choose the following simple procedure: we restrict
H^{\sharp}(t)
to the the box
[-L,L]
(with
2L+1
unit cells), so we consider the finite matrices

H^{\sharp}_{L}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}b&a&&&\\
a^{*}&b&a&&\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&a^{*}&b&a\\
&&&a^{*}&b\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}W_{t}(-L)&&&\\
&W_{t}(-L+1)&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&W_{t}(L)\end{pmatrix}.

Then, for each eigenpair
(\lambda,u)
with
\|u\|_{\ell^{2}_{L}}=1
, we check if the mass of
u
is localized near the origin, or, more specifically, not localized at the boundary of our simulation box. This procedure discards the spurious edge modes due to the truncation. We detect that
u
is a
true
edge mode whenever
\|u{\mathds{1}}(-L/2\leq n\leq L/2)\|_{\ell^{2}_{L}}\geq 3/4
.

For our numerical simulations, we took
d_{1}=1/4
(so
d_{2}=3/4
) for the distances between the atoms,
J_{1}=3/2
and
J_{2}=1/2
for the hopping parameters,
L=100
, and we took different values of
\nu\in[0.5,1,5,10]
. The results are displayed in Figure
4
. In each of these figures, we observe a spectral flow of
-1
in the middle gap, and of
-2
above the second Bloch band, in accordance with Theorem
1.1
. The flow of eigenvalues becomes steeper and steeper as
\nu
increases, as predicted in Theorem
1.3
. Note that our soft wall
w
is continuous, but not continuously differentiable. This explains the
kinks
in the Right figures (
\nu=5
and
\nu=10
), which happens when the kink of the wall touches the second atom located at
x_{2}=1/4
. Note also that since we took
x\mapsto w_{1}
non-increasing, the maps
t\mapsto W(t)
and
t\mapsto H^{\sharp}(t)
are operator non-decreasing. So all eigenvalue branches are non-decreasing, as can be checked on these pictures.

Finally, the first two simulations correspond to values of
\nu
smaller than the size of the gap (
2|J_{1}-J_{2}|=2
) and we can observe that the lower bound on the number of eigenvalues from Theorem
4.2
is almost achieved here.

,

. When the Lipschitz constant increases, the curves become steeper.

3.
Computation of spectral flows in the Jacobi case

It remains to prove Proposition
2.5
, that is to prove that the spectral flow is
-{\mathcal{N}}(E)
in the case of periodic Jacobi operators. In order to do so, we will use the robustness of the spectral flow to make successive modifications to the operators.
In Subsections
3.1
, the Jacobi operator
H^{\sharp}(t)
will be shown to have the same spectral flow as a
cut
model
H^{\Sigma}_{\rm{cut}}(t)
, which, itself, has a same spectral flow equal to a dislocated model
\widetilde{H}(t)
, see Subsection
3.2
. This last spectral flow can be computed explicitly, and turns out to be equal to
-{\mathcal{N}}(E)
.

Before we start, we emphasize that several techniques are available to study Jacobi operators, such as the ones involving
transfer matrices
. If
H
is a Jacobi operator, then the equation
H\Psi=E\Psi
implies that

\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{n+1}\\
\Psi_{n}\end{pmatrix}=T(E)\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{n}\\
\Psi_{n-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{with}\quad T(E):=\begin{pmatrix}-a^{-1}(b-E)&-a^{-1}a^{*}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

so that we can study the properties of
\Psi
by studying the transfer matrix
T(E)
. However, this matrix is not always well-defined (in the previous formulation, we need
a
to be invertible for instance). Actually, we were not able to recover our results using the formalism of transfer matrices, even in the case where
a
is invertible. Our proof rather relies on a direct study of the full operator, and a variational argument.

3.1.
Reduction to the cut case

We first deform the potential
w
to a simpler form. In what follows, we fix a Jacobi operator
H\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
with kernel
h
having diagonal entries
b
and off-diagonal entries
a
. Let us fix
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, and consider some
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}
large enough so that

\Sigma\geq E+4C_{a,b}+1,\qquad\text{with}\quad C_{a,b}:=\max\{\|a\|_{\rm{op}},\|b\|_{\rm{op}}\}.

Since
w(x)\to+\infty
as
x\to-\infty
, there is
x_{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}
so that
w(x)\geq\Sigma
for all
x<x_{\Sigma}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
x_{\Sigma}\leq-1
. We consider the modified potential (here and in what follows,
\Sigma
is shorthand for
\Sigma\mathbb{I}_{N}
)

(12)

w_{\Sigma}(x):=\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if}\quad x\geq 0,\\
-x(\Sigma-b)&\quad\text{if}\quad x\in[-1,0],\\
(\Sigma-b)&\quad\text{if}\quad x\in[x_{\Sigma},-1],\\
(x-x_{\Sigma}+1)(\Sigma-b)+(x_{\Sigma}-x)w(x)&\quad\text{if}\quad x\in[x_{\Sigma}-1,x_{\Sigma}],\\
w(x)&\quad\text{if}\quad x\leq x_{\Sigma}-1.\end{cases}

We also denote by
W_{\Sigma}(t)
the corresponding modified wall operator. The key properties of
w_{\Sigma}
and
W_{\Sigma}
are summarized in the following straightforward Lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

The potential
w_{\Sigma}
is Lipschitz (hence continuous), is linear on
[-1,0]
and satisfies

\forall x>0,\quad w_{\Sigma}(x)=0,\quad\text{while}\quad\forall x<-1,\quad w_{\Sigma}(x)\geq E+3C_{a,b}+1.

Proof.

The first points are obvious. If
x<-1
, we note that
(\Sigma-b)\geq\Sigma-\|b\|_{\rm{op}}\geq E+3C_{a,b}+1
and that, if
x<x_{\Sigma}\leq-1
, we also have
w(x)\geq\Sigma\geq E+3C_{a,b}+1
. The last inequality follows since
w_{\Sigma}
is a combination of these two matrices for
x<-1
.
∎

The difference of potentials
(w-w_{\Sigma})
is continuous, vanishes for
x<x_{\Sigma}
, and goes to
0
as
x\to+\infty
. In particular, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
(W-W_{\Sigma})(t)
is compact, and the map
t\mapsto(W-W_{\Sigma})(t)
is continuous and translation equivariant.

Let
H^{\Sigma}(t):=H+W_{\Sigma}(t)
be the corresponding edge Hamiltonian. We have

H^{\Sigma}(t)-H^{\sharp}(t)=W_{\Sigma}(t)-W(t),

which is compact for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In addition, both operators
H^{\Sigma}
and
H^{\sharp}
are translation equivariant. By robustness of the spectral flow with respect to compact operators, see Lemma
A.11
, we deduce that

(13)

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(H^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1]\right)={\mathrm{Sf}}\left(H^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1]\right).

In what follows, we study the operator
H^{\Sigma}(t)
. The main advantage of this new potential
w_{\Sigma}
is that it is piece-wise linear for
x\in[-1,0]
.

Our goal is now to compute the spectral flow of
t\mapsto H^{\Sigma}(t)
. For
t\in[0,1]
we define the cut operator

K(t):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c}\ddots&\ddots&&&&\\
\ddots&0&(1-t)a&&&\\
&(1-t)a^{*}&0&a&&\\
\hline\cr&&a^{*}&0&ta&\\
&&&ta^{*}&0&\ddots\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right),

where all undisplayed matrix elements are
0
. This operator is piece-wise linear, of the form
K(t)=tK(1)+(1-t)K(0)
, with

K(0)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c}\ddots&\ddots&&&&\\
\ddots&0&a&&&\\
&a^{*}&0&a&&\\
\hline\cr&&a^{*}&0&0&\\
&&&0&0&\ddots\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad K(1)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c}\ddots&\ddots&&&&\\
\ddots&0&0&&&\\
&0&0&a&&\\
\hline\cr&&a^{*}&0&a&\\
&&&a^{*}&0&\ddots\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right).

Note that
K(1)=\tau_{1}K(0)\tau_{1}^{*}
, so the map
t\mapsto K(t)
can be extended by translation equivariance, and this extension is continuous. For simplicity, in what follows, we will restrict the study to
t\in[0,1]
.

We now define the cut Jacobi operator

H_{\rm{cut}}^{\Sigma}(t):=H^{\Sigma}(t)-K(t),\quad\text{so that}\quad H^{\Sigma}(t)=H^{\Sigma}_{\rm{cut}}(t)+K(t).

Since
t\mapsto H^{\Sigma}(t)
and
t\mapsto K(t)
are continuous and translation equivariant, so is
t\mapsto H_{\rm{cut}}^{\Sigma}(t)
. So, using again the robustness of the spectral flow with respect to compact, translation equivariant operators, see Lemma
A.11
, we find that

{\mathrm{Sf}}(H^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}(H^{\Sigma}_{\rm{cut}}(t),E,[0,1]).

Also, by construction of the cut operator, for all
t\in[0,1]
,
H_{\rm{cut}}^{\Sigma}(t)
is block diagonal with respect to the decomposition
\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}^{-}\cup\mathbb{Z}^{+}
(note that this is no longer the case for
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,1]
). We write
H_{\rm{cut}}^{\Sigma}(t)=H^{\Sigma}_{L}(t)\oplus H^{\Sigma}_{R}(t)
.
In an analogous way, we decompose the bulk operator as
H_{\rm cut}(t)=H_{L}(t)\oplus H_{R}(t)
and the wall operator as
W(t)=W_{L}(t)\oplus W_{R}(t)
, so that
H^{\Sigma}_{L}(t)=H_{L}(t)+W_{L}(t)
and
H^{\Sigma}_{R}(t)=H_{R}(t)+W_{R}(t)
.

These left and right operators are not periodic nor translation equivariant in
t
. Still, one can define the spectral flow as the net number of eigenvalues crossing the energy
E
downwards. Since these operators are no longer translation equivariant, a priori this spectral flow could depend on the value of
E
in the essential gap (see Appendix
A
). In any case, since
H_{\rm{cut}}^{\Sigma}(t)
is block diagonal, we have

{\mathrm{Sf}}(H^{\Sigma}_{\rm{cut}}(t),E,[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}(H_{L}^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1])+{\mathrm{Sf}}(H_{R}^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1]).

Lemma 3.2
.

We have

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(H_{L}^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=0
and thus

{\mathrm{Sf}}(H^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}(H_{R}^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1]).

Proof.

We write
H^{\Sigma}_{L}(t)=H_{L}(t)+W_{L}(t)
. Explicitly, the matrix representing
H_{L}(t)
is given by (we keep the straight lines to emphasize that this matrix in only infinite in one direction)

H_{L}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c c | }\ddots&\ddots&&\\
\ddots&b&a&\\
&a^{*}&b&ta\\
&&ta^{*}&b\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\right).

Note that
H_{L}(t)
is a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{-},\mathbb{C}^{N})
, with uniform bound

\forall t\in[0,1],\qquad\|H_{L}(t)\|_{{\rm{op}}}\leq 3C_{a,b}.

Indeed, we have, using that
0\leq t\leq 1
, and with
\Psi_{0}:=0
, that

\|H_{L}(t)\Psi\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq\sum_{n=-\infty}^{-1}(\|a^{*}\|_{\rm op}\|\Psi_{n-1}\|+\|b\|_{\rm op}\|\Psi_{n}\|+\|a\|_{\rm op}\|\Psi_{n+1}\|)^{2}\leq C_{a,b}^{2}9\sum_{n=-\infty}^{-1}\|\Psi_{n}\|^{2}=9C_{a,b}^{2}\|\Psi\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

On the other hand,
W_{L}(t)
is block diagonal with block elements involving
w_{\Sigma}(n-t)
. Since
n\leq-1
and
t\in[0,1]
, we have
n-t\leq-1
, so
W_{L}(t)\geq\Sigma-\|b\|_{\rm{op}}
. Hence,

H^{\Sigma}_{L}(t)\geq W_{L}(t)-\|H_{L}(t)\|_{\rm{op}}\geq\Sigma-\|b\|_{\rm{op}}-3C_{a,b}\geq E+1,

by our choice of
\Sigma
in (
12
).
As a consequence,
E
is never in the spectrum of
H^{\Sigma}_{L}(t)
. This proves the lemma.
∎

We now focus on the right part. Due to our specific piece-wise linear choices for
w_{\Sigma}
and for the cut operator
K(t)
, we obtain that
H^{\Sigma}_{R}(t)
is also piece-wise linear, of the form
H^{\Sigma}_{R}(t)=(1-t)H^{\Sigma}_{R}(0)+tH^{\Sigma}_{R}(1)
, with

H^{\Sigma}_{R}(0)=\left(\begin{array}[]{| c c c c }\hline\cr b&a&&\\
a^{*}&b&a&\\
&a^{*}&b&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right),\qquad\text{and}\qquad H^{\Sigma}_{R}(1)=\left(\begin{array}[]{| c c c c }\hline\cr\Sigma&0&&\\
0&b&a&\\
&a^{*}&b&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right).

As noted before, the half-line operators are no longer translation equivariant: the presence of the boundary at
0
makes the model at
t=1
not unitarily equivalent to the one at
t=0
. The key observation is that the relevant part of the spectrum is still periodic, since
H^{\Sigma}_{R}(1)
is a direct sum of
\Sigma
with (a shifted version of)
H^{\Sigma}_{R}(0)
. We will study such families of operators in the next section.

3.2.
Dislocated Jacobi operators

In order to study the right–half Jacobi operator
H^{\Sigma}_{R}(t)
, we study yet another
dislocated
model. The idea to study this model comes from Hempel and Kohlmann in
[
24
,
23
,
25
]
, and was used in
[
18
,
20
]
to compute spectral flows for Schrödinger operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In this setting, the dislocated model is modelled by a Schrödinger operator of the form
H_{t}:=-\partial_{xx}^{2}+V_{t}(x)
with the dislocated potential

V_{t}(x)=V(x){\mathds{1}}(x<0)+V(x-t){\mathds{1}}(x\geq 0),

where
V
is
1
–periodic. The map
t\mapsto H_{t}
is norm–resolvent continuous and
1
-periodic, and we recover periodic models at the endpoints. As mentioned in the introduction, a difficulty with the discrete model is that it is not possible to create such a continuous shift. Our workaround is to introduce a translation equivariant dislocated model
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t)
.

The operator
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t)
acts on the full space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
and is defined for
t\in[0,1]
, by

\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c c c}\ddots&\ddots&&&&&\\
\ddots&b&a&&&&&\\
&a^{*}&b&(1-t)a&ta&&&\\
\hline\cr&&(1-t)a^{*}&(1-t)b+t\Sigma&(1-t)a&&&\\
&&ta^{*}&(1-t)a^{*}&b&a&\\
&&&&a^{*}&b&\ddots&\\
&&&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right).

This operator is constructed to interpolate linearly between
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t=0)
and
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t=1)
, with

\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(0):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c }\ddots&\ddots&&&\\
\ddots&b&a&&&\\
&a^{*}&b&a&&\\
\hline\cr&&a^{*}&b&a&\\
&&&a^{*}&b&\ddots\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(1):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c c c}\ddots&\ddots&&&&&\\
\ddots&b&a&&&&&\\
&a^{*}&b&0&a&&&\\
\hline\cr&&0&\Sigma&0&&&\\
&&a^{*}&0&b&a&\\
&&&&a^{*}&b&\ddots&\\
&&&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right).

See also Figure
1
.3. The operator
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(0)
is exactly the periodic Jacobi operator
H
, and its spectrum is
\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
. For the operator
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(1)
, we note the following. Let us write
\mathbb{Z}=\{0\}\cup(\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})
, and
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})=\mathbb{C}^{N}\oplus\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\mathbb{C}^{N})
. With respect to this decomposition, we write

\forall\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N}),\qquad\Psi=\{\Psi_{0}\}\oplus\Phi,\quad\text{with}\quad\Phi=(\cdots,\Psi_{-2},\Psi_{-1},\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2},\cdots).

Then the operator
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(1)
acts as

\forall\Psi=\{\Psi_{0}\}\oplus\Phi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N}),\qquad\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(1)\left[\{\Psi_{0}\}\oplus\Phi\right]=\{\Sigma\Psi_{0}\}\oplus H\Phi.

In what follows, we will write

(14)

\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(1)=\Sigma\,\widetilde{\oplus}\,\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(0),

for such a decomposition. In particular, we have

\sigma\left(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(1)\right)=\sigma\left(H\right)\bigcup\{\Sigma\}=\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}\bigcup\{\Sigma\}.

In addition, the extra eigenvalue
\Sigma
is of multiplicity
N
. Since the energy
E
is not in the spectrum of
H
, and since
\Sigma>E
, it is not in the spectrum of
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(0)
nor
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(1)
.

Finally, since
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t)
is a compact perturbation of
H
(only a finite number of matrix elements are modified), we have

\sigma_{\rm{ess}}\left(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t)\right)=\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H)=\sigma_{\rm{bulk}},

so the essential spectrum is independent of
t\in[0,1]
. Again, some eigenvalues may appear in these essential gaps. Since
E
is in such an essential gap, the spectral flow
{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1]\right)
is well-defined. Actually, we prove that it equals the one of the right–cut model introduced in the previous section.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left({H}_{R}^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1]\right)={\mathrm{Sf}}\left(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1]\right).

Proof.

We consider yet another cut operator
\widetilde{K}(t)
, defined by
\widetilde{K}(t)=(1-t)\widetilde{K}(0)+t\widetilde{K}(1)
, with

\widetilde{K}(0):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c |c c c }\ddots&\ddots&&\\
\ddots&0&a&0&\\
\hline\cr&a^{*}&0&\ddots&\\
&0&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad\widetilde{K}(1):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c |c c c }\ddots&\ddots&&\\
\ddots&0&0&a&\\
\hline\cr&0&0&\ddots&\\
&a^{*}&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right),

where all undisplayed elements are null. Note that
\widetilde{K}(1)=0\,\widetilde{\oplus}\,\widetilde{K}(0)
, hence that
(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}-s\widetilde{K})(1)=\Sigma\,\widetilde{\oplus}\,(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}-s\widetilde{K})(0)
for all
s\in[0,1]
. Therefore, by robustness of the spectral flow under compact perturbations, we find that (see Lemma
A.11
below)

{\mathrm{Sf}}(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t)-\widetilde{K}(t),E,[0,1]).

The operator
\widetilde{H}(t)-\widetilde{K}(t)
is block diagonal, of the form
\widetilde{H}_{L}^{\Sigma}(t)\oplus\widetilde{H}_{R}^{\Sigma}(t)
. This time, the left part is constant, independent of
t
, hence has a null spectral flow. For the right part, we recover the half Jacobi matrix
H_{R}^{\Sigma}(t)
.
∎

3.3.
Computation of the spectral flow for the dislocated model

The main result of this section is an exact computation of the spectral flow in the dislocated case.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=-{\mathcal{N}}(E).

This result was first proved in
[
24
,
23
,
25
]
in the case of Schrödinger operators acting on the continuum. The proof presented here is similar, but more complete, and uses solely tools from spectral theory.

3.3.1.
The finite periodic dislocated model

First, we introduce a finite dimensional version of our dislocated operator. This is the point in the proof where working with periodic Jacobi operators instead of general periodic Hamiltonians becomes easier to write and study. For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\Omega_{\ell}
the set of
\ell
integers centered around
0
. Explicitly,

\Omega_{\ell}:=\left\{-\left\lfloor\frac{\ell-1}{2}\right\rfloor,-\left\lfloor\frac{\ell-1}{2}\right\rfloor+1,\cdots,-1,0,1,\cdots,\left\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\right\rfloor-1,\left\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\right\rfloor\right\},

so
\Omega_{1}=\{0\}
,
\Omega_{2}=\{0,1\}
,
\Omega_{3}=\{-1,0,1\}
, and so on. In what follows, we identify
\Omega_{\ell}
with the torus
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
, but we chose the labelling of
\Omega_{\ell}
to display our matrices. For such
\ell
with
\ell\geq 5
, we denote by
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}_{\ell}(t)
a periodic version of the dislocated Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(t)
. This operator acts on
\ell^{2}(\Omega_{\ell},\mathbb{C}^{N})
. In the canonical basis, this is the operator (the bars in the matrix still denote the separation between strictly negative and positive indices)

\widetilde{H}_{\ell}^{\Sigma}(t):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c c c}\ddots&\ddots&&&&&a^{*}\\
\ddots&b&a&&&&&\\
&a^{*}&b&(1-t)a&ta&&&\\
\hline\cr&&(1-t)a^{*}&(1-t)b+t\Sigma&(1-t)a&&&\\
&&ta^{*}&(1-t)a^{*}&b&a&\\
&&&&a^{*}&b&\ddots&\\
a&&&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right).

Note the presence of
a^{*}
(resp.
a
) in the upper–right (resp. lower–left) corner. Again, this family of matrices is linear in
t
, and interpolates between
\widetilde{H}_{\ell}^{\Sigma}(t=0)
and
\widetilde{H}_{\ell}^{\Sigma}(t=1)
, with

\widetilde{H}_{\ell}^{\Sigma}(0):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c }\ddots&\ddots&&&&a^{*}\\
\ddots&b&a&&&\\
&a^{*}&b&a&&\\
\hline\cr&&a^{*}&b&a&\\
&&&a^{*}&b&\ddots\\
a&&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad\widetilde{H}_{\ell}^{\Sigma}(1):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c |c c c c c}\ddots&\ddots&&&&&a^{*}\\
\ddots&b&a&&&&&\\
&a^{*}&b&0&a&&&\\
\hline\cr&&0&\Sigma&0&&&\\
&&a^{*}&0&b&a&\\
&&&&a^{*}&b&\ddots&\\
a&&&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right).

At
t=0
, the matrix
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}_{\ell}(0)
is a finite version of the periodic Jacobi matrix
H
. We will write
H^{\Sigma}_{\ell}(0)=:H_{\ell}
below to emphasize this point. Its spectrum can be directly computed using Fourier series. Using plane waves with periodicity
\ell
, we find (compare with (
10
))

\sigma(H_{\ell})=\bigcup_{k\in\Omega_{\ell}^{*}}\sigma\left(a^{*}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}k}+b+a{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k}\right),

with
\Omega_{\ell}^{*}:=\frac{2\pi}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}\cap\Omega^{*}
where we recall that
\Omega^{*}=(-\pi,\pi]
. In particular, we have
\sigma(H_{\ell})\subset\sigma(H)
, and the energy
E
is not in the spectrum of
H_{\ell}
. Actually, for each of the
N
Bloch bands
\{\lambda_{j,k}\}_{k\in\Omega^{*}}
of
H
, we can associate the
\ell
eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j,k}\}_{k\in\Omega^{*}_{\ell}}
of
H_{\ell}
.

At
t=1
, apart from the presence
\Sigma
in the middle, we also recognize a periodic model, and we have a relation of the form (compare with (
14
))

\widetilde{H}_{\ell}^{\Sigma}(1)=\Sigma\,\widetilde{\oplus}\,H_{\ell-1},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\sigma\left(\widetilde{H}_{\ell}^{\Sigma}(1)\right)=\sigma\left(H_{\ell-1}\right)\bigcup\{\Sigma\},

where
\Sigma
is an eigenvalue of multiplicity
N
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
\ell\geq 5
, we have
{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}_{\ell}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=-{\mathcal{N}}(E)
.

Proof.

The map
t\mapsto\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}_{L}(t)
is continuous (even linear) in
t
. So the branches of eigenvalues of these matrices are also continuous in
t
. At
t=0
, there are
\ell{\mathcal{N}}(E)
eigenvalues of
\widetilde{H}_{\ell}^{\Sigma}(0)
below the energy
E
, and at
t=1
, there are
(\ell-1){\mathcal{N}}(E)
eigenvalues of
\widetilde{H}_{\ell}^{\Sigma}(1)
below
E
. This difference implies that the net number of eigenvalues crossing
E
when
t
increases to
1
equals
-{\mathcal{N}}(E)

∎

Let us recall our analogy of the Grand Hilbert Hotel given in Remark
1.2
. In this finite periodic case, there are only a finite number of rooms per floor. The difference between the initial and final number of rooms counts, without ambiguity, the number of eigenvalues that cross from one band to the next.

3.3.2.
From the finite dislocated model to the infinite dislocated model

In the previous section, we proved that the spectral flow of the supercell dislocated model
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}_{\ell}
is
-{\mathcal{N}}(E)
for all
\ell\geq 5
. In this section, we justify that one may take the limit
\ell\to\infty
and deduce that spectral flow is also
-{\mathcal{N}}(E)
for the full dislocated model
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}
.
Throughout this subsection, we drop the superscript
\Sigma
, and the
tilde
notation for clarity: we will simply write
H(t)
and
H_{\ell}(t)
for the dislocated models acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{N})
and
\ell^{2}(\Omega_{\ell},\mathbb{C}^{N})
respectively. Recall that at
t=0
, the operators
H(0)
and
H_{\ell}(0)
are periodic, and that

\sigma(H_{\ell}(0))\subset\sigma_{\rm{bulk}},\quad\sigma(H(0))=\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}.

In order to relate the operators
H_{\ell}
and
H
that act on different Hilbert spaces, we introduce
i_{\ell}:{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}\to{\mathcal{H}}
for the extension by zero. Explicitly,

\forall\Psi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell},\ \forall n\in\mathbb{Z},\quad(i_{\ell}\Psi)_{n}=\begin{cases}\Psi_{n}&\quad\text{if}\quad n\in\Omega_{\ell},\\
0&\quad\text{else}\end{cases},

whose adjoint is the restriction operator
i_{\ell}^{*}:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
given by

\forall\Psi\in{\mathcal{H}},\ \forall n\in\Omega_{\ell},\quad(i_{\ell}^{*}\Psi)_{n}=\Psi_{n}.

We also introduce a smooth cutoff function
\chi:\mathbb{R}\mapsto[0,1]
such that

\chi(x)=1\quad\forall x\in[-1/4,1/4],\qquad\chi(x)=0\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1/3,1/3),

and define
\chi_{\ell}
as the multiplication operator by
\chi(\cdot/\ell)
in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
. Note that this function is supported in
[-\ell/3,\ell/3]\subset\Omega_{\ell}
. We will use the following identities throughout this section.

Lemma 3.6
.

For all
\ell\geq 12
and
t\in[0,1]
we have,

(1)

H(t)i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}=i_{\ell}H_{\ell}(t)\chi_{\ell}
from
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
to
{\mathcal{H}}
, and
\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}H(t)=\chi_{\ell}H_{\ell}(t)i_{\ell}^{*}
from
{\mathcal{H}}
to
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
,

(2)

For any multiplication operator
\phi
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
with support in
\Omega_{\ell}\setminus[-\ell/4,\ell/4]
, we have
H_{\ell}(t)\phi=H_{\ell}(0)\phi
as operators from
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
to
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
.

Similarly, For any multiplication operator
\widetilde{\phi}
on
{\mathcal{H}}
with support in
\mathbb{Z}\setminus[-\ell/4,\ell/4]
, we have
H(t)\widetilde{\phi}=H(0)\widetilde{\phi}
as operators from
{\mathcal{H}}
to
{\mathcal{H}}
.

(3)

The operator
R_{\ell}:=\left[H_{\ell},\chi_{\ell}\right]=H_{\ell}(t)\chi_{\ell}-\chi_{\ell}H_{\ell}(t)
acting on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
is independent of
t
, and satisfies
\|R_{\ell}\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}\leq\frac{C}{\ell}
.

The first point states that we can replace
{H}(t)
with
{H}_{\ell}(t)
in the support of
\chi_{\ell}
(the cut-off function erases the boundary conditions). The second point states that outside the support of the cut-off function, the operators are independent of
t\in[0,1]
. The last point says that one can commute the Hamiltonians with the cut-off operator, up to an error of order
O(\ell^{-1})
.

Proof.

For the first point, we note that for all
\Psi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
, the function
\chi_{\ell}\Psi
has support in
[-\ell/3,\ell/3]
. Whenever
\ell\geq 9
, this implies that
H_{\ell}(t)\chi_{\ell}\Psi
has support in
[-\ell/3-1,\ell/3+1]
, hence
H_{\ell}(t)\chi_{\ell}\Psi=H(t)i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}\Psi
.
The second relation is the adjoint of the first.

For the second point, we note that
H_{\ell}(t)=H_{\ell}(0)+M_{\ell}(t)
, where
M_{\ell}(t):=i_{\ell}M(t)i_{\ell}^{*}
, and where
M(t)
is a finite rank operator with nonzero matrix elements only in the entries
\{-1,0,1\}
. So
M_{\ell}(t)\phi=0
whenever
\ell>4
, since we assumed that
\phi=0
in
\Omega_{\ell}\cap[-\ell/4,\ell/4]\supset\{-1,0,1\}
. The argument for
\widetilde{\phi}
is identical.

For the third point, since
\chi_{\ell}
is equal to the identity in the support of
M
for all
\ell>8
, we have

M_{\ell}(t)\chi_{\ell}=\chi_{\ell}M_{\ell}(t)
. In particular,
[H_{\ell}(t),\chi_{\ell}]=[H_{\ell}(0),\chi_{\ell}]
is independent of
t
. Next, we find that

\left[H_{\ell}(0)\chi_{\ell}-\chi_{\ell}H_{\ell}(0)\right]_{ij}=\begin{cases}a\ (\chi_{\ell}(n+1)-\chi_{\ell}(n))&\quad\text{if}\quad(i,j)=(n,n+1)\\
a^{*}(\chi_{\ell}(n-1)-\chi_{\ell}(n))&\quad\text{if}\quad(i,j)=(n,n-1)\\
0&\quad\text{else}\end{cases}.

Note that
|\chi_{\ell}(n+1)-\chi_{\ell}(n)|\leq\frac{C}{\ell}
. So

R_{\ell}:=H_{\ell}(t)\chi_{\ell}-\chi_{\ell}H_{\ell}(t)=H_{\ell}(0)\chi_{\ell}-\chi_{\ell}H_{\ell}(0)\quad\text{satisfies}\quad\|R_{\ell}\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}\leq 2\|a\|_{\rm op}\frac{C}{\ell}.\qed

First, we prove that any complex number in the resolvent set of
H(t)
is in the resolvent set of
H_{\ell}(t)
for sufficiently large values of
\ell
.

Lemma 3.7
.

There is a constant
C
, such that, for all
t\in[0,1]
and all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying

\operatorname{dist}(\lambda,\sigma({H}(t))):=\varepsilon>0,

we have

\operatorname{dist}(\lambda,\sigma({H}_{\ell}(t)))\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\text{ for all }\ell\geq\max(12,C/\varepsilon).

Recall that, for a self-adjoint operator
A
, we have
\operatorname{dist}(\lambda,\sigma(A))=\|(\lambda-A)^{-1}\|_{\rm op}^{-1}
.

Proof.

Since the essential spectrum of
H(t)
coincides with
\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, we find that
\operatorname{dist}(\lambda,\sigma_{\rm{bulk}})\geq\varepsilon
.
Recall that
\sigma(H_{\ell}(0))\subset\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, so in particular
H_{\ell}(0)-\lambda
is invertible with
\|(H_{\ell}(0)-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\leq\varepsilon^{-1}
. The same bound holds for
(H(t)-\lambda)^{-1}
.
We combine both resolvents to construct an approximate inverse for
H_{\ell}(t)-\lambda
.
Indeed, for any
\ell\geq 9
, the identities from Lemma
3.6
give

(15)

\left(H_{\ell}(t)-z\right)\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}=\chi_{\ell}\left(H_{\ell}(t)-z\right)i_{\ell}^{*}+R_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}=\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}\left(H(t)-z\right)+R_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}

as operators from
{\mathcal{H}}
to
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
. Together with the fact that
i_{\ell}^{*}i_{\ell}={\mathds{1}}_{\ell}:={\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}}
, we get the following identity, as operators from
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
to
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
,

\displaystyle(H_{\ell}(t)-\lambda)\left\{\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}(H(t)-\lambda)^{-1}i_{\ell}+({\mathds{1}}_{\ell}-\chi_{\ell})(H_{\ell}(0)-\lambda)^{-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}i_{\ell}+R_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}(H(t)-\lambda)^{-1}i_{\ell}+({\mathds{1}}_{\ell}-\chi_{\ell})-R_{\ell}(H_{\ell}(0)-\lambda)^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad={\mathds{1}}_{\ell}+R_{\ell}\left\{i_{\ell}^{*}(H(t)-\lambda)^{-1}i_{\ell}-(H_{\ell}(0)-\lambda)^{-1}\right\}:={\mathds{1}}_{\ell}+\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(t).

We can bound
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(t)
by combining the bounds on the resolvents and on
R_{\ell}
from Lemma
3.6
to obtain

\left\|\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(t)\right\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}\leq\frac{2C}{\varepsilon\ell},

Note that the bound is independent of
t\in[0,1]
. Then, for
\ell\geq C^{\prime}:=\max\{12,\frac{4C}{\varepsilon}\}
, we have
\|{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}(t)\|_{\rm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. We conclude that
H_{\ell}(t)-\lambda
is invertible with

\displaystyle\|(H_{\ell}(t)-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}(H(t)-\lambda)^{-1}i_{\ell}+({\mathds{1}}_{\ell}-\chi_{\ell})(H_{\ell}(0)-\lambda)^{-1}\right\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}\,\|({\mathds{1}}_{\ell}+\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(t))^{-1}\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{1}{1-\left\|{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}(t)\right\|_{\rm{op}}}\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon}.

This is the desired result.
∎

To complete the proof, we have to show that the spectral projectors of
H_{\ell}(t)
in the gaps of
\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
converge to spectral projectors of
H(t)
. We record a convenient property of projections.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
P
be a projector of rank
m
, and
A
be a bounded operator such that
\|P-A\|_{\rm{op}}<1
. Then
\mathrm{rank}(A)\geq m
.

Proof.

We have
\|P-PAP\|_{\rm{op}}<1
, so, seen as maps from
E:=\mathrm{Ran}(P)
to itself, we have
\|\mathbb{I}_{m}-A|_{E}\|<1
, which proves that
A|_{E}
is full rank on
E
. So
\mathrm{rank}(A)\geq m
.
∎

In what follows, for
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
, we denote by
P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(A)
the spectral projector of the self-adjoint operator
A
on the open interval
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. We recall that if
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are not in the spectrum of
A
, then this spectral projector can be written as the Cauchy contour integral

P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(A)=\dfrac{1}{2{\mathrm{i}}\pi}\oint_{{\mathscr{C}}}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}z}{z-A},

where
{\mathscr{C}}
is any simple, positively oriented contour in
\mathbb{C}
enclosing the interval
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
and no other portions of the real line. We can take for instance
{\mathscr{C}}
the positively oriented circle with center
\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})
and radius
\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Fix
t
and let
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
which are not eigenvalues of
H(t)
. Then, for
\ell
large enough, we have

\mathrm{rank}\left(P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H_{\ell}(t))\right)=\mathrm{rank}\left(P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H(t))\right).

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.7
, the idea is to localize the resolvent of
H_{\ell}(t)
using the cut-off functions
\chi_{\ell}
. On the support of
\chi_{\ell}
, we compare it with the resolvent of
H(t)
, and outside this support, we compare it with
H_{\ell}(0)
(independent of
t
). Since the interval
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is contained in a gap of
\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, the latter part does not contribute to the spectral projection.

To start, let
{\mathscr{C}}
be the positively oriented circle as above, which encloses the interval
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. Note that

\forall z\in{\mathscr{C}},\quad\operatorname{dist}(z,\sigma(H(t)))\geq\max\{\operatorname{dist}(\lambda_{1},\sigma(H(t))),\operatorname{dist}(\lambda_{2},\sigma(H(t)))\}=:\varepsilon>0.

So, by Lemma
3.7
, for
\ell\geq\frac{C}{\varepsilon}
, this contour is also included in the resolvent set of
H_{\ell}(t)
, and we have the bound

\forall z\in{\mathscr{C}},\quad\|(z-H_{\ell}(t))^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon},\quad\text{and}\quad\|(z-H(t))^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.

We use the localization function
\chi_{\ell}
and
\phi_{\ell}:=\sqrt{1-\chi_{\ell}^{2}}
, such that
\chi_{\ell}^{2}+\phi_{\ell}^{2}=1
. The support of
\chi_{\ell}
is in
[-\ell/3,\ell/3]
, the support of
\phi_{\ell}
is in the complement of
(-\ell/4,\ell/4)
. The third point of Lemma
3.6
(for
\chi_{\ell}
, but the proof is similar for
\phi_{\ell}
) shows that there is a constant
C
so that

(16)

R_{\ell}:=[H_{\ell}(t),\chi_{\ell}]\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\ell}^{\phi}:=[H_{\ell}(t),\phi_{\ell}]\quad\text{satisfy}\quad\left\|R_{\ell}\right\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}\leq\frac{C}{\ell},\quad\left\|R_{\ell}^{\phi}\right\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}\leq\frac{C}{\ell}.

Consider
z\in{\mathscr{C}}
, and recall (
15
) which states that

\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}(H(t)-z)=(H_{\ell}(t)-z)\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}-R_{\ell}.

Composing this identity with
(H_{\ell}(t)-z)^{-1}
on the left and
(H(t)-z)^{-1}i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}
on the right gives

(17)

(H_{\ell}(t)-z)^{-1}\chi_{\ell}^{2}=\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}(H(t)-z)^{-1}i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}+{\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}_{\ell}(z),

with
\|{\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}_{\ell}(z)\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}\leq\frac{8C}{\varepsilon^{2}\ell}
.
On the other hand, we have
H_{\ell}(t)\phi_{\ell}=H_{\ell}(0)\phi_{\ell}
as operators on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
(see Lemma
3.6
(2)), hence, taking adjoints,
\phi_{\ell}H_{\ell}(t)=\phi_{\ell}H_{\ell}(0)
. Writing

\phi_{\ell}(H_{\ell}(0)-z)=\phi_{\ell}(H_{\ell}(t)-z)=(H_{\ell}(t)-z)\phi_{\ell}-R_{\ell}^{\phi}

and multiplying by
(H_{\ell}(t)-z)^{-1}
on the left and
(H_{\ell}(0)-z)^{-1}\phi_{\ell}
on the right gives

(18)

(H_{\ell}(t)-z)^{-1}\phi_{\ell}^{2}=\phi_{\ell}(H_{\ell}(0)-z)^{-1}\phi_{\ell}+{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}_{\ell}(z),

again with
\|{\mathcal{R}}^{(2)}_{\ell}(z)\|_{{\rm{op}}}\leq\frac{8C}{\varepsilon^{2}\ell}
. Summing (
17
) and (
18
)
gives

\displaystyle(H_{\ell}(t)-z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(H_{\ell}(t)-z)^{-1}(\chi_{\ell}^{2}+\phi_{\ell}^{2})

\displaystyle=\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}(H(t)-z)^{-1}i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}+\phi_{\ell}(H_{\ell}(0)-z)^{-1}\phi_{\ell}+({\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}^{(1)}+{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}^{(2)})(z).

Inserting this identity in the Cauchy formula gives

\displaystyle P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H_{\ell}(t))=\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H(t))i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}+\phi_{\ell}P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H_{\ell}(0))\phi_{\ell}+\frac{1}{2{\mathrm{i}}\pi}\oint_{\mathscr{C}}({\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}^{(1)}+{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}^{(2)})(z){\mathrm{d}}z.

Since
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
lies in a gap of
\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
, the second term is actually zero. The last term can be bounded by
4C|{\mathscr{C}}|/(\pi\varepsilon^{2}\ell)
. Thus, we can find
\ell_{0}
sufficiently large such that for all
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
, we have

\left\|P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H_{\ell}(t))-\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H(t))i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}\right\|_{\rm{op}}<1.

From Lemma
3.8
, we conclude that

\mathrm{rank}(P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H(t)))\geq\mathrm{rank}\left(\chi_{\ell}P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H(t))\chi_{\ell}\right)\geq\mathrm{rank}\left(P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H_{\ell}(t))\right).

For the opposite inequality, we have to define the cut-off function
\widetilde{\phi}_{\ell}
such that

\widetilde{\phi}_{\ell}^{2}+i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}^{2}i^{*}_{\ell}={\mathds{1}}_{\mathcal{H}}
.
Multiplying (
15
) on the left by
i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}(H_{\ell}(t)-z)^{-1}
and on the right by
(H(t)-z)^{-1}

gives the identity

i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}^{2}i_{\ell}^{*}(H(t)-z)^{-1}=i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}(H_{\ell}(t)-z)^{-1}\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}+\widetilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{(1)}_{\ell}(z),

with
\|\widetilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{(1)}_{\ell}(z)\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}\leq\frac{8C}{\varepsilon^{2}\ell}
. The analogue of (
18
) reads

\widetilde{\phi}_{\ell}^{2}(H(t)-z)^{-1}=\widetilde{\phi}_{\ell}(H(0)-z)^{-1}\widetilde{\phi}_{\ell}+\widetilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{(2)}_{\ell}(z).

with
\|\widetilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{(2)}_{\ell}(z)\|_{{\rm{op}},\ell}\leq\frac{8C}{\varepsilon^{2}\ell}
. We then apply the Cauchy formula to

\displaystyle(H(t)-z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}(H_{\ell}(t)-z)^{-1}\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}+\widetilde{\phi}_{\ell}(H(0)-z)^{-1}\widetilde{\phi}_{\ell}+(\widetilde{{\mathcal{R}}}_{\ell}^{(1)}+\widetilde{{\mathcal{R}}}_{\ell}^{(2)})(z),

and note that the second term vanishes as before.
Thus, Lemma
3.8
gives for all sufficiently large values of
\ell

\mathrm{rank}(P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H_{\ell}(t)))\geq\mathrm{rank}\left(i_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H_{\ell}(t))\chi_{\ell}i_{\ell}^{*}\right)\geq\mathrm{rank}\left(P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(H(t))\right),

and completes the proof.
∎

We now have all the ingredients to complete the proof of Lemma
3.4
on the spectral flow of
t\mapsto H(t)
.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Choose a partition
0=t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{M}=1
and positive numbers
(a_{i})_{1\leq i\leq M}
so that, for all
1\leq i\leq M
, the projection
P_{(E-a_{i},E+a_{i})}\left(H(t)\right)
is finite rank and
E\pm a_{i}
belong to the resolvent set of
H(t)
for all
t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]
. We recall in Appendix
A
that the spectral flow of
t\mapsto{H}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Sf}}\left({H}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\mathrm{rank}\,P_{(E+a_{i},E+a_{i+1})}(H(t_{i})),

with the convention that
\mathrm{rank}\,P_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}(A)=-\mathrm{rank}\,P_{(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1})}(A)
if
\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{1}
. We used here that

\dim\,{\mathrm{Ker}}(H(1)-E)=\dim\,{\mathrm{Ker}}(H(0)-E),

which comes from the fact that
H(1)=\Sigma\,\widetilde{\oplus}\,H(0)
, so that there are no boundary terms

The hypothesis on
E\pm a_{i}
and the continuity of the eigenvalues as a function of
t
, shows that there is
\varepsilon_{i}>0
such that

\forall t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}],\quad\operatorname{dist}(E\pm a_{i},\sigma(H(t)))\geq\varepsilon_{i}.

By Lemma
3.7
, with
\varepsilon=\min_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
there is
\ell_{0}
such that, for all
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
, we have

\forall t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}],\quad\operatorname{dist}(E\pm a_{i},\sigma(H_{\ell}(t)))\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

Thus, the partition given by
\{t_{i}\}_{i=0,\cdots,M}
and the corresponding values
a_{i}
are suitable to compute the spectral flow of
H_{\ell}
for all
\ell>\ell_{0}
.
By applying Lemma
3.9
, we find a sufficiently large
\ell
, such that

\mathrm{Ran}\left(P_{(E+a_{i},E+a_{i+1})}\left(H(t_{i})\right)\right)=\mathrm{Ran}\left(P_{(E+a_{i},E+a_{i+1})}\left(H_{\ell}(t_{i})\right)\right)\text{ for }i=1,\cdots,M-1,

and thus, by using Lemma
3.5
,

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left({H}(t),E,[0,1]\right)={\mathrm{Sf}}\left({H}_{\ell}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=-{\mathcal{N}}(E).\qed

4.
Application for two–dimensional materials

In this section, we apply the previous theory to the case of two–dimensional materials in the tight–binding approximation. Our goal is to describe the spectrum of such materials in the presence of a soft wall. In the one-dimensional case, we could without loss of generality fix the unit cell to be
[0,1)
and the corresponding Fourier variable belongs to
[-\pi,\pi)
. To describe general two-dimensional models, we need the full terminology of solid state physics.

4.1.
Reduction to the one-dimensional case

4.1.1.
Bravais lattices and bulk operators in tight-binding models

Let us first fix some notation. We consider a
\mathbb{L}
–periodic crystal, where
\mathbb{L}
is a Bravais lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, of the form

\mathbb{L}={\mathbf{a}}_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\mathbb{Z},\qquad{\mathbf{a}}_{1},{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad\det({\mathbf{a}}_{1},{\mathbf{a}}_{2})\neq 0.

We denote by
\Gamma
a fundamental cell of the lattice,
i.e.
a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that the disjoint union of its
\mathbb{L}
–translations cover
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:
\bigsqcup_{{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L}}\Gamma+{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A typical choice is
\Gamma={\mathbf{a}}_{1}[0,1)\oplus{\mathbf{a}}_{2}[0,1)
, but any choice works. We denote by
M\in\mathbb{N}
the number of atoms in each unit cell
\Gamma
. The location of these atoms are
({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{x}}_{M})
with
{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\in\Gamma
.

The wave-function is an element
\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{C}^{M})
parametrized as

\Psi=\left(\psi_{\mathbf{R}}\right)_{{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L}},\quad\text{where}\quad\forall{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L},\quad\psi_{{\mathbf{R}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{(1)}_{{\mathbf{R}}}&\psi^{(2)}_{{\mathbf{R}}}&\cdots&\psi^{(M)}_{{\mathbf{R}}}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\quad\in\mathbb{C}^{M}.

As noted in the introduction, one interpretation of this notation is that
\psi_{{\mathbf{R}}}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{C}
is the amplitude of the electron on the atom located at
{\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
. The discrete translation invariance of the perfect, infinite crystal translates in the convolution form of the two–dimensional tight–binding Hamiltonian
{\mathsf{H}}
(we use straight letters for two–dimensional operators), of the form

(19)

\forall\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{C}^{M}),\quad\forall{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L},\quad({\mathsf{H}}\Psi)_{{\mathbf{R}}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}}{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}-{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}},

where
{\mathsf{h}}:\mathbb{L}\to{\mathcal{M}}_{M}(\mathbb{C})
is a family of
M\times M
matrices satisfying
h(-{\mathbf{R}})=h({\mathbf{R}})^{*}
. We assume that
h\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{C}^{M\times M})
, which implies as before that
{\mathsf{H}}
is a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{C}^{M})
.

Since
\sf H
is a convolution operator, it is diagonal in Fourier basis.
As usual in solid state physics, we introduce a pair of reciprocal lattice vectors
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}
,
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}
, that are defined by the relations
\langle{\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{*},{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
. These vectors generate the reciprocal lattice
\mathbb{L}^{*}
, and the natural domain for the Fourier variable is the Brillouin zone
\Gamma^{*}:=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{L}^{*}
(seen as a torus). Reasoning as in Section
2.1.1
, we find that
{\mathcal{F}}{\mathsf{H}}{\mathcal{F}}^{*}=\int_{\Gamma^{*}}^{\oplus}{\sf H}_{\mathbf{k}}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathbf{k}}
, where

(20)

\forall{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad{\sf H}_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L}}{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{R}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}\qquad\in{\mathcal{S}}_{M}(\mathbb{C}).

The map
{\mathbf{k}}\mapsto{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}
is analytic (it is the sum of a uniformly absolutely convergent series of analytic functions) and
\mathbb{L}^{*}
–periodic, so it is enough to describe
{\sf H}_{\mathbf{k}}
on the Brillouin zone
{\mathbf{k}}\in\Gamma^{*}
. We denote by
\lambda_{1,{\mathbf{k}}}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{M,{\mathbf{k}}}
the eigenvalues of
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}
, ranked in increasing order. The
j
-th (two–dimensional)
Bloch band
is the interval
\bigcup_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\{\lambda_{j,{\mathbf{k}}}\}
, and the spectrum of
{\mathsf{H}}
is the union of these
M
Bloch bands:

\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}=\sigma\left({\sf H}\right)=\bigcup_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\sigma({\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}})=\bigcup_{j=1}^{M}\bigcup_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\Gamma^{*}}\{\lambda_{j,{\mathbf{k}}}\}.

In the next section, we will add a wall which is constant along the
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
–direction (see Section
4.2
below for the case of general angles). This implies that the model with the wall is still periodic in the
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
–direction. In particular, we may perform a partial Fourier transform in this direction. It is useful at this point to introduce the one–dimensional lattices

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{1}:={\mathbf{a}}_{1}\mathbb{Z},\quad\mathbb{L}_{2}:={\mathbf{a}}_{2}\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{L}_{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}^{*}_{1}:={\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\mathbb{Z},\quad\mathbb{L}^{*}_{2}:={\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\mathbb{L}^{*}=\mathbb{L}^{*}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{L}^{*}_{2}.

We also denote by
\Gamma^{*}_{2}:=({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{L}^{*}_{2}
the Brillouin zone of the
\mathbb{L}_{2}
lattice, seen as a torus/line in the two–dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, in the sense that we keep the orientation of this line along the
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
–direction. We write
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in\Gamma_{2}^{*}
for the Fourier variable in this direction. In the literature, it is often identified with the one–dimensional real torus/line, with the identification
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}=k_{2}\frac{{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}}{|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}|}
where
k_{2}=|{\mathbf{k}}_{2}|\in\mathbb{R}/|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}|\mathbb{Z}
.

With these conventions, the partial Fourier transform is
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{C}^{M})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{L}_{1}\times\Gamma^{*}_{2},\mathbb{C}^{M})
defined by

(21)

\forall({\mathbf{R}}_{1},{\mathbf{k}}_{2})\in\mathbb{L}_{1}\times\Gamma^{*}_{2},\qquad\left({\mathcal{F}}_{2}[\Psi]\right)({\mathbf{R}}_{1},{\mathbf{k}}_{2}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Gamma_{2}|}}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}_{2}\in\mathbb{L}_{2}}\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}_{1}+{\mathbf{R}}_{2}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}_{2}}.

We obtain that
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}{\mathsf{H}}{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{*}=\int_{\Gamma^{*}_{2}}^{\oplus}H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathbf{k}}_{2}
, where, for all
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in\Gamma^{*}_{2}
,
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}
is an operator acting on the one–dimensional lattice
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L}_{1},\mathbb{C}^{M})
, of the form

\forall\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L}_{1},\mathbb{C}^{M}),\quad\forall{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\in\mathbb{L}_{1},\qquad\left(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}\Psi\right)_{{\mathbf{R}}_{1}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}_{1}}h_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}({\mathbf{R}}_{1}^{\prime})\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}_{1}-{\mathbf{R}}_{1}^{\prime}},

where the kernel
h_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}
is

(22)

\forall{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\in\mathbb{L}_{1},\qquad h_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}({\mathbf{R}}_{1}):=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}_{2}\in\mathbb{L}_{2}}{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}_{1}+{\mathbf{R}}_{2}){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}_{2}}.

Note that the series is convergent and the resulting one-dimensional kernel satisfies the hypothesis of the previous section, since
{\mathsf{h}}\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{L})
implies
h_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{L}_{1})
by Fubini’s Theorem.

In what follows, we fix
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in\Gamma_{2}^{*}
, and study the one-dimensional periodic operator
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}
, and its soft wall counterpart
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}^{\sharp}(t)
. The spectrum of
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}
is purely essential, composed of
M
(one-dimensional) Bloch bands. It is the union of the spectra of
\left(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}\right)_{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}}
, defined as (compare with (
5
))

\forall{\mathbf{k}}_{1}\in\Gamma_{1}^{*},\quad\left(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}\right)_{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}}:=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\in\mathbb{L}_{1}}h_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}({\mathbf{R}}_{1}){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}_{1}}.

Together with (
22
), we get that

\left(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}\right)_{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\in\mathbb{L}_{1}}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}_{2}\in\mathbb{L}_{2}}{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}_{1}+{\mathbf{R}}_{2}){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}_{2}-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}_{1}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L}}{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}}={\mathsf{H}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}+{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}.

We deduce that

(23)

\sigma\left(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}\right)=\bigcup_{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}\in\Gamma^{*}_{1}}\sigma\left(h_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}({\mathbf{k}}_{1})\right)=\bigcup_{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}\in\Gamma^{*}_{1}}\sigma\left({\mathsf{H}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}+{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}\right)=\bigcup_{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}\in{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\mathbb{R}}\sigma\left({\mathsf{H}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{1}+{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}\right).

One should think of this formula as follows: the spectrum of
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}
is the
projection
of the two–dimensional bands (the spectra of
{\mathsf{H}}_{{\mathbf{k}}}
) on the
\Gamma^{*}_{2}\approx{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\mathbb{R}
line, where the projection is along the
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}
–direction. Note that
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}
is the direction orthogonal to the
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
vector (which will be the direction of the wall). We extend this formula to the case of general angles below.

4.1.2.
Soft walls parallel to a lattice vector

We now add a soft wall potential. We will choose our wall aligned with the
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
vector (see Section
4.2
below for other
conmensurate
directions). To do so, we introduce the normalized vector
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}:=\tfrac{{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}}{|{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}|}
. This vector satisfies
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{2}=0
and
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{1}=\frac{2\pi}{|{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}|}
. We consider a two–dimensional soft wall potential
w:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to{\mathcal{S}}_{M}(\mathbb{C})
of the form

\forall{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad w({\mathbf{x}})=w_{*}({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}),

where
w_{*}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{M}(\mathbb{C})
is a one–dimensional Lipschitz soft wall potential as in Section
2.1.2
. Note that
w({\mathbf{x}}+\lambda{\mathbf{a}}_{2})=w({\mathbf{x}})
, so this wall is constant along the
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
direction. In addition, since
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}
is normalized, this potential has the same Lipschitz constant as
w_{1}
. To introduce a dislocation parameter
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we set (see also Remark
1.4
)

(24)

w_{t}({\mathbf{x}}):=w({\mathbf{x}}-t{\mathbf{a}}_{1})=w_{*}\left({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot\left({\mathbf{x}}-t{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right)\right)=w_{*}\left({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}-t\frac{2\pi}{\|{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\|}\right).

The corresponding operator
W(t)
acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{C}^{M})
is the block–diagonal operator

\forall\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{C}^{M}),\quad\forall{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L},\quad(W(t)\Psi)_{{\mathbf{R}}}=w_{t}({\mathbf{R}})\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}}.

For a physical crystal subject to a scalar potential
v_{*}
which is constant in the
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
-direction, one would consider a diagonal potential of the form

(25)

w_{t}({\mathbf{R}})={\rm diag}\left(v_{*}({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot\left({\mathbf{R}}-t{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right)),\cdots,v_{*}({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot\left({\mathbf{R}}-t{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+{\mathbf{x}}_{M}\right)\right),

where we recall that
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{x}}_{M}
are the positions of the atoms with respect to the center of the unit cell. Note that if
{\mathbf{R}}=n{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+m{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
, then
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}=n{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{1}
.

Finally, the soft wall model is the operator

{\mathsf{H}}^{\sharp}(t)={\mathsf{H}}+W(t),\quad\text{with domain}\quad{\mathcal{D}}(W)\subset\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{M}).

This operator is periodic in the
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
–direction. After the partial Fourier transform in this direction, we obtain that
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}{\mathsf{H}}^{\sharp}_{t}{\mathcal{F}}_{2}=\int_{\Gamma^{*}_{2}}^{\oplus}H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}^{\sharp}(t){\mathrm{d}}{\mathbf{k}}_{2}
where
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}^{\sharp}(t)
acts on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{M})
with

H^{\sharp}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t)=H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}+W(t).

We recover the soft wall described in Section
2.1.2
: the normalization for
t
in (
24
) has been chosen so that when
t
moves from
0
to
1
, the wall shifts by the vector
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}
. We can now apply the previous results to this case.

4.1.3.
Edge spectrum in the two–dimensional setting

For an energy
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}:=\sigma({\mathsf{H}})
, we denote by
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
the number of (two–dimensional) Bloch bands of the two–dimensional bulk operator
{\mathsf{H}}
below
E
. It is the only integer for which

\forall{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\lambda_{{\mathcal{N}}(E),{\mathbf{k}}}<E<\lambda_{{\mathcal{N}}(E)+1,{\mathbf{k}}},

where we recall that
\lambda_{1,{\mathbf{k}}}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{M,{\mathbf{k}}}
are the eigenvalues of the
M\times M
matrix
{\mathsf{H}}({\mathbf{k}})
defined in (
20
). Similarly, for
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in\Gamma^{*}_{2}
, and for an energy
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}})
, we denote by
{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(E)
the number of (one–dimensional) Bloch bands of
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}
below
E
. It is the integer for which

\forall{\mathbf{k}}_{1}\in{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\mathbb{R},\qquad\lambda_{{\mathcal{N}}(E),{\mathbf{k}}_{1}+{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}<E<\lambda_{{\mathcal{N}}(E)+1,{\mathbf{k}}_{1}+{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}.

If
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{bulk}}
is in the essential gap of the full two–dimensional operator
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}
, then, we have
{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(E)={\mathcal{N}}(E)
for all
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in\Gamma^{*}_{2}
. However, it may happen that an energy
E
belongs to the essential gap of
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}
for
some
values of
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}
, and is not in an essential gap of the full operator
{\mathsf{H}}
. In this case,
{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(E)
may be well-defined, but not
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
. This happens for instance in the Wallace model for graphene (see Section
4.3
below).

We can now state our result in the two–dimensional case. It is a straightforward application of Proposition
2.4
and Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
w_{1}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{M}
is a Lipschitz soft wall. Then, with the previous notation,

(1)

For all
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in\Gamma^{*}_{2}
, and all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the operator
H^{\sharp}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t)
is a well-defined self-adjoint operator whose domain is independent of
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}
and
t
and given by

{\mathcal{D}}:=\left\{\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L}_{1},\mathbb{C}^{M}),\quad W_{t=0}\Psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L}_{1},\mathbb{C}^{M})\right\}.

(2)

The map
\Gamma^{*}_{2}\times\mathbb{R}\ni({\mathbf{k}}_{2},t)\mapsto H^{\sharp}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t)
is norm-resolvent continuous and translation equivariant in
t
.

(3)

For all fixed
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in\Gamma^{*}_{2}
, the spectrum
t\mapsto\sigma(H^{\sharp}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t))
is
1
-periodic in
t
. The essential spectrum
\sigma_{\rm ess}(H^{\sharp}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t))
is independent of
t
, and equals
\sigma(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}})
described in (
23
).

(4)

For all fixed
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in\Gamma^{*}_{2}
, and for all
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}})
, we have

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}^{\sharp}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=-{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(E).

Concerning the spectrum at fixed
t=t_{0}
, we can apply Theorem
1.3
. We first make the observation that if
w(\cdot)
is
\nu
–Lipschitz, then, for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the map
t\mapsto w_{t}({\mathbf{x}})
is
\nu\frac{|\Gamma|}{\|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|}
–Lipschitz. Indeed, we have, using the classical identity
\|{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\|=2\pi\|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\||\Gamma|^{-1}
, that

\left|\widetilde{w}_{t}({\mathbf{x}})-\widetilde{w}_{t^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}})\right|=\left|w\left({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}-t\frac{2\pi}{\|{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\|}\right)-w\left({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}-t^{\prime}\frac{2\pi}{\|{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\|}\right)\right|\leq\nu\frac{2\pi}{\|{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\|}|t-t^{\prime}|=\nu\frac{|\Gamma|}{\|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|}|t-t^{\prime}|.

In particular, the maps
t\mapsto W_{t}
and
t\mapsto H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}^{\sharp}(t)
are also
\nu\frac{|\Gamma|}{\|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|}
–Lipschitz. We obtain the following corrolary of Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
be a soft wall which is
\nu
–Lipschitz, for some
\nu>0
. Then, for all
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in\Gamma^{*}_{2}
, all energy
E
belonging to an essential gap of width
g(E)\geq\nu|\Gamma|\|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|^{-1}
of
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}
, and all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, the operator
H^{\sharp}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t_{0})
has at least
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
eigenvalues in each interval of the form
(\lambda,\lambda+\nu|\Gamma|\|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|^{-1}]
in this gap.

In particular, there are at least
\left\lfloor\dfrac{g(E)\|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|}{\nu|\Gamma|}\right\rfloor{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(E)
eigenvalues in this gap.

We skip its proof, as it is similar to the one of Theorem
1.3
(see also Remark
1.4
). This time, the density of eigenvalues is lower bounded by
\frac{\nu|\Gamma|}{{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(E)\|{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|}
.

4.2.
Walls in other directions

We now give one possible way to extend our result to walls which are rotated by any commensurable angle. In the case of incommensurate angles, one can follow
[
19
]
to prove that
all essential bulk gaps are filled with edge spectrum
, for all values of
t
.

We will show that a wall along any commensurable angle can be cast in the previous framework by increasing the size of the unit cell.
For two co-prime integers
n,m
with
n\neq 0
and
m\neq 0
, we define the new vectors

(26)

\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1}:=n{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{2}:=n{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+m{\mathbf{a}}_{2}.

In this section, the wall will be aligned with the vector
\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}
, and move along the
\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{1}
–direction (which is also the
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}
–direction). Varying
n
and
m
allows to describe all commensurable directions for the wall, except for the
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
–directions (which were studied in the previous section).

These two vectors generate a new lattice
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}
, which is a sublattice of the original
\mathbb{L}
. We denote by
\widetilde{\Gamma}
a corresponding unit cell (supercell). The number of atoms per unit cell is now

\widetilde{M}=|n\,m|M.

We consider the tight-binding Hamiltonian
\widetilde{{\mathsf{H}}}
as the representation of the original
{\mathsf{H}}
with
\widetilde{\Gamma}
as unit cell. Note that the new kernel
\widetilde{{\mathsf{h}}}
is now a function from
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}
to
\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{M}\times\widetilde{M}}
.

4.2.1.
Bulk spectrum

The spectrum of the bulk Hamiltonian is identical in both frameworks, but written in the Fourier decomposition for the
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}
lattice, it consists of
|n\,m|
-times more bands on a
|n\,m|
-times smaller Brillouin zone: a single band for the original Hamiltonian
{\mathsf{H}}
corresponds to
|n\,m|
bands of the
supercell Hamiltonian

\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}
.
To make things concrete, we write
L=|nm|
and label the
L
points of
\mathbb{L}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}
as
{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{y}}_{L}
. We then define
{\mathcal{S}}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{C}^{M})\mapsto\ell^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbb{L}},(\mathbb{C}^{M})^{L})
by

\bigl({\mathcal{S}}\Psi\bigr)({\mathbf{R}})=\left(\Psi({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{y}}_{1}),\cdots,\Psi({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{y}}_{L})\right)^{\top}

With this transformation,

\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}:={\mathcal{S}}{\mathsf{H}}{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
acts as

\bigl(\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}\Phi\bigr)({\mathbf{R}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}}\widetilde{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})\Phi_{{\mathbf{R}}-{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}}\quad\text{ where }\quad\widetilde{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})_{ij}={\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{y}}_{i}+{\mathbf{y}}_{j}).

Then, a computation shows that the dual lattice of
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}
is
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}:=\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{1}^{*}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}^{*}\mathbb{Z}
, with

(27)

\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}=\frac{{\mathbf{a}}^{*}_{1}}{n}-\frac{{\mathbf{a}}^{*}_{2}}{m}\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}=\frac{{\mathbf{a}}^{*}_{2}}{m}.

This time, it is the dual lattice
\mathbb{L}^{*}
which is a sublattice of
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}
. The corresponding Brillouin zone
\widetilde{\Gamma^{*}}:=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}
has an area
|\widetilde{\Gamma^{*}}|=\frac{1}{|n\,m|}|\Gamma^{*}|
. The operator
\widetilde{{\mathsf{H}}}
is again a convolution, and we denote by
\widetilde{{\mathsf{H}}}({{\mathbf{k}}})
the corresponding Fourier fibers of the operators. The map
{\mathbf{k}}\mapsto\widetilde{{\mathsf{H}}}({\mathbf{k}})
is smooth and
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}
–periodic. We describe this relation more precisely in this short Lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have

\sigma\left(\widetilde{{\mathsf{H}}}({\mathbf{k}})\right)=\bigcup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{K}}}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}\cap\Gamma^{*}}\sigma\left({\mathsf{H}}({\mathbf{k}}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{K}}})\right)=\bigcup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{K}}}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}}\sigma\left({\mathsf{H}}({\mathbf{k}}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{K}}})\right).

Proof.

This result is classical but we provide a simple proof.
Recall the definition

\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}}\widetilde{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{R}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}\qquad\in{\mathcal{S}}_{LM}(\mathbb{C}).

We fix
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(or in the Brillouin zone
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}
) and compute its spectrum. To this end, for some
u\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
and
{\mathbf{K}}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}
, we define

u_{{\mathbf{K}}}:=\left({\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{y}}_{1}\cdot({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}})}u,\cdots,{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{y}}_{L}\cdot({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}})}u\right)^{\top},

and compute, taking advantage of the periodicity of
\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}
,

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{{\mathbf{K}}}\right)_{i}=\left(\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}}}u_{{\mathbf{K}}}\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{R}}\cdot({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}})}\left(\widetilde{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}})u_{{\mathbf{K}}}\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{R}}\cdot({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}})}\sum_{j=1}^{L}{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}-{\mathbf{y}}_{i}+{\mathbf{y}}_{j}){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\cdot({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}})}u.

Relabeling
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}:={\mathbf{R}}-{\mathbf{y}}_{i}+{\mathbf{y}}_{j}
, we note that this variable runs over all sites of the original lattice
\mathbb{L}
, so we obtain finally

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{{\mathbf{K}}}\right)_{i}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\cdot({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}})}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\cdot({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}})}{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})u={\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\cdot({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}})}{\mathsf{H}}_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}}}u.

If
u
is an eigenvector of
{\mathsf{H}}_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{K}}}
, then
u_{{\mathbf{K}}}
is an eigenvector of
\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}
with the same eigenvalue, for all
{\mathbf{K}}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}
. In addition, the eigenvectors
u_{\mathbf{K}}
are linearly independent for
{\mathbf{K}}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}/\mathbb{L}^{*}
, so we have obtained the
LM
eigenvalues of the supercell Hamiltonian
\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}({\mathbf{k}})
, and the result follows.
∎

When we perform a partial Fourier transform of
\widetilde{{\mathsf{H}}}
in the
\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}
–direction, and obtain the new family
\widetilde{H}_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}}
with
\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}^{*}_{2}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

We have

\sigma\left(\widetilde{H}_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}}\right)=\bigcup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{1}\in\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{1}^{*}\mathbb{R}}\sigma\left({\mathsf{H}}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{1}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2})\right).

In other words, the spectrum of
\widetilde{H}_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}}
is again the projection of the spectra of initial operator
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}
along the
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}
–direction (which is the direction orthogonal to the wall). It is therefore quite easy to compute the (essential) spectrum of the one–dimensional bulk operator
\widetilde{H}_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}}
for any angle: it is a projection of the two–dimensional Bloch bands of the bulk operator
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}
(which is independent of the angle) along a particular direction. Figure
6
illustrates this phenomenon for the Wallace model.

Proof.

As in (
23
), we find that

\sigma\left(\widetilde{H}_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}}\right)=\bigcup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{1}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}^{*}}\sigma\left(\widetilde{{\mathsf{H}}}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{1}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2})\right).

Together with Lemma
4.3
, we deduce that

\sigma\left(\widetilde{H}_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}}\right)=\bigcup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{K}}}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{*}}\bigcup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{1}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}^{*}}\sigma\left({\mathsf{H}}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{1}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{K}}})\right)=\bigcup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{1}\in\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{1}^{*}\mathbb{R}}\bigcup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{K}}}_{2}\in\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}\sigma\left({\mathsf{H}}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{1}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{K}}}_{2}^{*})\right).

It remains to prove that the union in
\widetilde{{\mathbf{K}}}_{2}^{*}
can be dropped. Recall that we assume that
n
and
m
are coprime. Let
(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
so that
pn+qm=1
. We have, using (
27
),

{\mathsf{H}}\left({\mathbf{k}}-p\,n\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{1}^{*}\right)={\mathsf{H}}\left({\mathbf{k}}-p{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}+\frac{pn}{m}{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)={\mathsf{H}}\left({\mathbf{k}}-p{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}-q{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}+\frac{1}{m}{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)={\mathsf{H}}\left({\mathbf{k}}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}^{*}\right),

where we used the
\mathbb{L}
–periodicity of
{\mathbf{k}}\mapsto{\mathsf{H}}({\mathbf{k}})
in the last equality. This proves that any integer shift in the
\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}^{*}
–direction can be implemented as another shift in the
\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{1}^{*}
–direction. The result follows.
∎

4.2.2.
Spectral flow for the edge Hamiltonian with general angles

We denote by
\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}^{\sharp}(t)
the tight-binding model with a soft wall defined as in the previous section, but now with
\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
. Applying Theorem
4.2
to this case results in

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
be a soft wall which is
\nu
–Lipschitz, for some
\nu>0
. If
E
belongs to an essential gap of width
g(E)\geq\nu
, then for all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
the operator
\widetilde{H}^{\sharp}(t_{0})_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}}
has at least
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
eigenvalues in each interval of the form
(\lambda,\lambda+\nu|\Gamma|/\|\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|]
in this gap.

Note that the density of edge states is lower bounded by
{\mathcal{N}}(E)\|\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|/(\nu|\Gamma|)
. When the cut becomes close to incommensurate, in the sense that
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|
becomes large, then there are numerous edge states for each value of
\widetilde{{\mathbf{k}}}_{2}
.

Proof.

According to Theorem
4.1
,

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left({\widetilde{H}^{\sharp}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t),E,[0,1]}\right)=-{\mathcal{N}}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}}(E).

Each Bloch band in the supercell framework corresponds to
L
Bloch bands of the original Hamiltonian, so we have
{\mathcal{N}}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}}(E)=L{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathsf{H}}}(E)
. On the other hand, we claim that the spectrum of
\widetilde{H}^{\sharp}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t)
is periodic in
t
with period
1/L
. This can be seen as follows.

Recall that
n,m
are coprimes, and let
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
pn+qm=1
as before. Then, we have
\frac{1}{nm}=\frac{p}{n}+\frac{q}{m}
. This gives,

\widetilde{w}_{\frac{1}{nm}}({\mathbf{x}})=w_{*}\left(\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot\left({\mathbf{x}}-\frac{1}{nm}\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{1}\right)\right)=\widetilde{w}\left(\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot\left({\mathbf{x}}-\frac{1}{nm}\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{1}+\frac{1}{m}\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}\right)\right)=\widetilde{w}\left(\widetilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{2}^{\perp}\cdot\left({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\right)\right),

so
\widetilde{w}_{\frac{1}{nm}}({\mathbf{x}})=\widetilde{w}_{0}({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2})
. After a shift of the wall by
t=\frac{1}{nm}
, we recover the initial model, translated by
-{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
. This proves our claim.
We conclude that

{\mathrm{Sf}}\left({\widetilde{H}^{\sharp}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t),E,\left[0,\frac{1}{L}\right]}\right)=\frac{1}{L}{\mathrm{Sf}}\left(\widetilde{H}^{\sharp}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t),E,[0,1]\right)=-{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathsf{H}}}(E).

Recalling that the eigenvalue branches for
t\mapsto\widetilde{\mathsf{H}}^{\sharp}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(t)
are Lipschitz with constant
\nu|\widetilde{\Gamma}|/\|\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|=\nu L\Gamma/\|\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\|
and mimicking the proof of Theorem
1.3
gives the result.
∎

4.3.
Example : the Wallace model for graphene

We now apply the previous results in the case of graphene, within the Wallace approximation. We consider three different angles, and compute the edge spectral flow in these three cases.

4.3.1.
The Wallace model

Figure 5
.
Our convention for the zigzag direction (left), armchair direction with
(n,m)=(-1,1)
(middle), and another angle with
(n,m)=(2,-1)
(right). The shaded cell represents
\Gamma

\widetilde{\Gamma}

Let us start by describing the bulk spectrum of graphene. Graphene has a honeycomb lattice structure, which is the triangular Bravais lattice, with two atoms per unit cell. As basis vectors, we may choose

\mathbb{L}={\mathbf{a}}_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{with}\quad{\mathbf{a}}_{1}=\frac{a_{0}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\sqrt{3}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{a}}_{2}=\frac{a_{0}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\sqrt{3}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{(zig--zag convention)},

where
a_{0}=\sqrt{3}d_{0}
is the graphene lattice constant and
d_{0}
is the distance between neighboring carbon atoms. For unit cell we take
\Gamma=[0,1){\mathbf{a}}_{1}+[0,1){\mathbf{a}}_{2}
as shown in Figure
5
and there are
M=2
carbon atoms, located at

{\mathbf{x}}_{1}=\frac{1}{3}({\mathbf{a}}_{1}+2{\mathbf{a}}_{2})
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}=\frac{1}{3}(2{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2})
. The Wallace Hamiltonian only contains hopping between nearest neighbours, with

\begin{cases}({\mathsf{H}}\Psi)_{{\mathbf{R}}}^{(1)}&={\mathsf{t}}_{0}\left(\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}}^{(2)}+\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}-{\mathbf{a}}_{1}}^{(2)}+\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}}^{(2)}\right),\\
({\mathsf{H}}\Psi)_{{\mathbf{R}}}^{(2)}&={\mathsf{t}}_{0}\left(\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}}^{(1)}+\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{a}}_{1}}^{(1)}+\Psi_{{\mathbf{R}}-{\mathbf{a}}_{2}}^{(1)}\right).\end{cases}

Here,
{\mathsf{t}}_{0}
is the carbon-carbon hopping amplitude and since it just sets the energy scale, we will take
{\mathsf{t}}_{0}=1
throughout this Section. There are only five non-zero matrices
{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{R}})
, given by

{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{0}})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{a}}_{1})={\mathsf{h}}(-{\mathbf{a}}_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\mathsf{h}}(-{\mathbf{a}}_{1})={\mathsf{h}}({\mathbf{a}}_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

We find that the Bloch fibers are given by

{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1+{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{1}}+{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{2}}\\
c.c.&0\end{pmatrix}.

The map
{\mathbf{k}}\mapsto{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}
is
\mathbb{L}^{*}
–periodic, with
\mathbb{L}^{*}={\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\mathbb{Z}\oplus{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\mathbb{Z}
, with

{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}a_{0}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{3}\\
-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}a_{0}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{3}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}
are
\pm|1+{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{1}}+{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{2}}|
. Thus, the two bands are mirror images that meet at the so called Dirac points
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbf{K}}+\mathbb{L}^{*}
or
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathbb{L}^{*}
,
{\mathbf{K}}:=\frac{1}{3}({\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*})
and
{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}:=\frac{2}{3}({\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*})
. These are the only points where the gap closes.

4.3.2.
Zigzag orientation

With this choice for the lattice vector
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
, and assuming as before that the wall is constant along the
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
–direction, we can see that the associated halfspace model would corresponds to the zig-zag or bearded cut (see the left panel of Fig.
5

After a partial Fourier transform in the
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}

b=h_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}({\mathbf{0}})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1+{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{2}}\\
c.c.&0\end{pmatrix},\quad a^{*}=h_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}({\mathbf{a}}_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

We recover the SSH matrices studied in Section

and

For shortness, define
r({\mathbf{k}}_{2})=|1+{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{2}}|=|2\sin\left(\frac{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{2}}{2}\right)|

The essential spectrum is just

\sigma(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}})=\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}})=[-|1+r({\mathbf{k}}_{2})|,-|1-r({\mathbf{k}}_{2})|]\cup[|1-r({\mathbf{k}}_{2}),|1+r({\mathbf{k}}_{2})|],

The gap of the one-dimensional operator
H_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}
closes only for
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}=\frac{1}{3}{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}+\mathbb{L}_{2}^{*}
and
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}=\frac{2}{3}{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}+\mathbb{L}_{2}^{*}
, corresponding to the projections of the
{\mathbf{K}}
and
{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}
points on the line
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\mathbb{R}
, along the
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}
–direction. This can be seen in the first panel of Figure
6
. Apart from these two points, we have
{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathbf{k}}_{2}}(E=0)=1
. Numerical simulations of the edge modes are presented in Figures
7
and
8
.

Note that
r({\mathbf{k}}_{2})<1
if and only if
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}\in(-1/3,1/3){\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}
modulo
\mathbb{L}_{2}^{*}
. In some sense, the Dirac cones
\pm\frac{1}{3}{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}
separate the two phases
|J_{1}|<|J_{2}|
and
|J_{2}|<|J_{1}|
of the SSH model. According to Remark
2.6
, this explains in particular why a hard cut would create a line of edge modes in the gap
(-1/3,1/3){\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}
or in the gap
(1/3,2/3){\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}

\mathbb{L}_{2}^{*}

Also note that the spectrum is symmetric around zero, and that for
{\mathbf{k}}_{2}={\mathbf{a}}_{2}/2
(at the edge of the first Brillouin zone) the chain decouples and the essential spectrum is just
\{-1,1\}
.

Appendix A
The Spectral Flow

The notion of spectral flow has been introduced in
[
2
,
3
]
, and studied extensively in the case of continuous path of symmetric Fredholm operators (see also
[
31
]
and the recent book
[
9
]
for a complete picture). Recall that for a bounded self-adjoint operator
A
, the operator
A-E
is Fredholm iff
E
is not in the essential spectrum of
A
. In the unbounded case, we can work instead with the family
B_{t}:=f(A_{t}-E)
, where
f
is a bounded increasing function with
f(\lambda)=\lambda
around
\lambda=0
. The goal of this Appendix is to give a self–contained version of the Spectral flow, using only tools from spectral theory, and which is enough for our purpose.

A.1.
Norm resolvent topology

In what follows, we fix
{\mathcal{H}}
a separable Hilbert space. We define the norm-resolvent metric on the set of (possibly unbounded) self-adjoint operators
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{H}})
acting on
{\mathcal{H}}
by

d(A,B):=\left\|{\mathcal{R}}({\mathrm{i}},A)-{\mathcal{R}}({\mathrm{i}},B)\right\|_{\rm op},

where we set
{\mathcal{R}}(z,A):=(z-A)^{-1}
.
This endows
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{H}})
with the norm-resolvent topology. Recall that the point
{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathbb{C}
does not play any special role, and that, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, the corresponding metric with
z
instead of
{\mathrm{i}}
defines the same topology. More specifically, we have the following.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
A
be a self-adjoint operator, and let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(A)
. Then there is
\varepsilon>0
and
C\geq 0
so that, for all self-adjoint operators
B
with
d(A,B)<\varepsilon
, we have
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(B)
, and
\|(z-B)^{-1}-(z-A)^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\leq Cd(A,B)
.

We refer to
[
27
, Remark IV.3.13]
for the proof, see in particular
[
27
, Eqn. IV (3.10)]
.

Lemma A.2
.

If
A
is self-adjoint and
H
is bounded self-adjoint, then the map
\lambda\mapsto A+\lambda H
is continuous for the norm-resolvent metric. In particular
\mathbb{R}\ni\lambda\mapsto A-\lambda
is continuous.

Proof.

Let us prove continuity at
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

\frac{1}{{\mathrm{i}}-(A+\lambda H)}-\frac{1}{{\mathrm{i}}-(A+\lambda_{0}H)}=(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\frac{1}{{\mathrm{i}}-(A+\lambda H)}H\frac{1}{{\mathrm{i}}-(A+\lambda_{0}H)}.

Since
A
and
A+\lambda H
are self-adjoint, we have
\|({\mathrm{i}}-A)^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\leq 1
(and similarly for
A+\lambda H
), so
d(A+\lambda_{0}H,A+\lambda H)\leq|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|\cdot\|H\|_{\rm op}
, which implies continuity.
∎

A.2.
Definition of the Spectral Flow

We consider a family
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto A_{t}
of self-adjoint operators on
{\mathcal{H}}
, continuous for this topology. We denote by
\sigma(\{A_{t}\})
and
\sigma_{\rm ess}(\{A_{t}\})
the spectrum and essential spectrum of the family
\{A_{t}\}
as the closure of the union (over
t
) of the spectra and essential spectra. Namely,

\sigma\left(\{A_{t}\}\right):=\overline{\bigcup_{t\in[0,1]}\sigma(A_{t})}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{\rm ess}\left(\{A_{t}\}\right):=\overline{\bigcup_{t\in[0,1]}\sigma_{\rm ess}(A_{t})}.

Both
\sigma(\{A_{t}\})
and
\sigma_{\rm ess}(\{A_{t}\})
are closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. Our goal is to define the spectral flow
{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,t\in[0,1])
for any
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm ess}\left(\{A_{t}\}\right)
. It will correspond to the net number of branches of eigenvalues of
A_{t}
crossing the energy
E
downwards.

Recall that for any self-adjoint operator
A
, if
(E-g,E+g)\cap\sigma_{{\rm{ess}}}(A)=\emptyset
, then there is
0\leq a\leq g
so that both numbers
E-a
and
E+a
are not in the spectrum of
A
(if
E\notin\sigma(A)
, one can simply take
a=0
). The spectral projector
P_{(E-a,E+a)}(A)
is then of finite rank, and given by the Cauchy integral

P_{(E-a,E+a)}(A)=P_{[E-a,E+a]}(A)=\frac{1}{2{\mathrm{i}}\pi}\oint_{{\mathscr{C}}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}z}{z-A},

where
{\mathscr{C}}
is the positively oriented complex circle of center
E
and radius
a
. We recall the following classical result. We skip the proof.

Lemma A.3
.

With the same notation as before, there is
\varepsilon>0
so that the map
B\mapsto P_{(E-a,E+a)}(B)
is continuous on

\{B\in{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{H}}),\ d(A,B)<\varepsilon\}
. In particular, we have
\mathrm{rank}\,P_{(E-a,E+a)}(B)=\mathrm{rank}\,P_{(E-a,E+a)}(A)
for all such
B
.

We can now define the spectral flow of
\{A_{t}\}
at the energy
E
. For all
t\in[0,1]
, we let
a(t)\geq 0
be so that
\{E\pm a(t)\}
are not in the spectrum of
A_{t}
. By Lemma
A.3
, there is
\varepsilon(t)>0
so that the result of the Lemma holds. Set

U(t):=\left\{t^{\prime}\in[0,1],\quad d(A_{t},A_{t^{\prime}})<\varepsilon(t)\right\}.

By continuity of the map
t\mapsto A_{t}
, the set
U(t)
is an open set of
[0,1]
, and the union of all
U(t)
covers the compact set
[0,1]
. By classical arguments, we can find a subdivision
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{M}=1
and real non-negative numbers
a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{M}\geq 0
, which we call the
widths
, so that

(29)

\forall 1\leq i\leq M,\quad[t_{i-1},t_{i}]\ni t\mapsto P_{(E-a_{i},E+a_{i})}(A_{t})\quad\text{is continuous on $[t_{i-1},t_{i}]$}.

In other words, the spectrum of
A_{t}
does not cross the levels
\{E\pm a_{i}\}
for
t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]
. Notice in particular that
E+a_{i}
and
E+a_{i+1}
do not belong to the spectrum of
A_{i}
. The
spectral flow
of
\{A_{t}\}
is defined by

{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,t\in[0,1]):=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathrm{rank}\,P_{[E,E+a_{i})}(A_{t_{i-1}})-\mathrm{rank}\,P_{[E,E+a_{i})}(A_{t_{i}}).

Note that
E
may be an eigenvalue of
A_{t_{i}}
here, so that
P_{[E,E+a_{i}]}
is the spectral projection of
A_{t_{i}}
,
including
the eigenspace
{\rm Ker}(A_{i}-E)
. It is unclear with this definition that the spectral flow has nice topological properties. However, after relabelling the sums, we get the equivalent formula

(30)

\boxed{{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,t\in[0,1])=\dim{\mathrm{Ker}}(A_{0}-E)-\dim{\mathrm{Ker}}(A_{1}-E)+\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\mathrm{rank}\,P_{(E+a_{i},E+a_{i+1})}(A_{t_{i}}),}

with the convention that
\mathrm{rank}\,{P}_{(a,b)}(A)=-\mathrm{rank}P_{(b,a)}(A)
if
b<a
. Note that due to the boundary terms, the spectral flows depends on the value of
E
in the gap. However, if we restrict the spectral flow to operators
A_{t}
satisfying
\dim{\mathrm{Ker}}(A_{0}-E)=\dim{\mathrm{Ker}}(A_{1}-E)
, then we will prove below that the spectral flow is independent of
E
in the gap. This happens in particular if
A_{1}
is unitarily equivalent to
A_{0}
.

It is a classical result (see
[
31
]
) that the spectral flow is a well-defined quantity, which is independent of the choice of the
(t_{i})
and the
(a_{i})
. The only condition is that the family
(t_{i},a_{i})
satisfies (
29
). The following example illustrates that the spectral flow counts the number of branches of eigenvalue going downwards.

Example A.1
.

Assume that the spectrum of
A_{t}
exhibits a single decreasing branch of eigenvalue
\lambda(t)
crossing downwards the energy
E
at
t_{*}\in(0,1)
. For the subdivision, take
M=3
,
t_{0}=1
,
t_{1}=t_{*}-\varepsilon
,
t_{2}=t_{*}+\varepsilon
and
t_{3}=1
, and set
a_{1}=0
,
a_{2}=a>0
,
a_{3}=0
, where
a>\max\{\lambda(t_{1})-\lambda(t_{*}),\lambda(t_{*}-\lambda(t_{2}))\}
. Then we get

\mathrm{rank}\,P_{(E+a_{1},E+a_{2})}(A_{t_{1}})=1,\quad\mathrm{rank}\,P_{(E+a_{2},E+a_{3})}(A_{t_{2}})=0,

so the spectral flow equals
1
in this case. See also Fig.
9
.

Figure 9
.
The subdivision
(t_{i})
and corresponding widths
(a_{i})
. In this example, the spectral flow is
1
at
E=0
. The spectral flow is a signed sum of branches of eigenvalues crossing the red horizontal segments (sign
+
if the arrow is upwards, sign
-
otherwise).

A.3.
Stability of the spectral flow

The Spectral flow is stable in the following sense.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
t\mapsto A_{t}
be a norm-resolvent continuous family of self-adjoint operators, and
E\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm ess}(\{A_{t}\})
. There is
\varepsilon>0
so that, for any norm-resolvent continuous family
(B_{t})
of self-adjoint operators satisfying
d(A_{t},B_{t})<\varepsilon
for all
t\in[0,1]
and
B_{0}=A_{0}
,
B_{1}=A_{1}
, it holds
{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}(B_{t},E,[0,1])
.

We emphasize that we require the paths
(A_{t})
and
(B_{t})
to have the same endpoints here. This is due to the boundary terms in (
30
). The proof follows the exact same lines as in
[
31
, Proposition 3]
, so we do not repeat it here.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
(s,t)\mapsto(A_{t}^{(s)})
be a norm-resolvent continuous family of self-adjoint operators, such that the endpoints
A_{0}^{(s)}=A_{0}
and
A_{1}^{(s)}=A_{1}
are independent of
s
. Assume in addition that
{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t}^{(s)},E,t\in[0,1])
is well-defined for all
s
. Then this spectral flow is independent of
s
.

This result follows by taking a suitable subdivision in the homotopy parameter
s
and applying the previous Lemma. The bottomline is that the only obstruction for homotopic invariance is that the spectral flow at
E
becomes ill-defined, which happens when the essential gap closes.

Recall Weyl’s theorem, which states that if
A
is self-adjoint and
K
is compact symmetric, then
A+sK
is self-adjoint, and
\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(A+sK)=\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(A)
for all
s
: the essential gaps are stable under compact perturbations, and therefore never close. Applying the previous lemma to
A_{t}^{(s)}:=A_{t}+sK
gives the following result.

Lemma A.6
.

Let
(K_{t})
be a continuous family of symmetric compact operators with
K_{0}=K_{1}=0
. Then
{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t}+K_{t},E,[0,1])
.

There is no assumption on the smallness of the family
(K_{t})
in the compact case. Finally, we record the following useful result.

Lemma A.7
.

If
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a strictly increasing function, then

{\mathrm{Sf}}(f(A_{t}),f(E),[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,[0,1]).

Here,
f(A_{t})
is defined via the spectral calculus. We skip the proof, as it is straightforward. In the case where
(A_{t})
are uniformly bounded from below by some
C
, one can take
f_{1}(x):=(C-x)^{-1}
and
f_{2}(x)=x-(C-E)^{-1}
, and deduce that

{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}\left((C-A_{t})^{-1},(C-E)^{-1},[0,1]\right)={\mathrm{Sf}}\left((C-A_{t})^{-1}-(C-E)^{-1},0,[0,1]\right).

The advantage of the right-hand side is that the family
t\mapsto(C-A_{t})^{-1}-(C-E)^{-1}
is a norm continuous family of
bounded
symmetric operators, for which
0
is not in the essential spectrum. So this family is Fredholm self-adjoint, and we are back to the
usual
theory.

A.4.
The translation equivariant case

In the previous section, the spectral flow depends on the energy
E
in the gap. This is due to the presence of boundary terms in (
30
).

For
I\subset\mathbb{R}
an interval of
\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
the set of norm-resolvent continuous paths of self-adjoint operator
(A_{t})
satisfying that

\forall E\in I,\quad\dim{\mathrm{Ker}}(A_{0}-E)=\dim{\mathrm{Ker}}(A_{1}-E).

Typical examples are:

•

If
\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto(A_{t})
is
1
–periodic in
t
, that is
A_{t+1}=A_{t}
, then
(A_{t})
is in
{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
with
I=\mathbb{R}
.

•

If
A_{1}
is unitary equivalent to
A_{0}
, say
A_{1}=UA_{0}U^{*}
, then
(A_{t})
is in
{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
with
I=\mathbb{R}
.

•

If
A_{1}=\Sigma\oplus A_{0}
(as in Section
3.2
), then
(A_{t})
is in
{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
with
I=(-\infty,\Sigma)
or
I=(\Sigma,+\infty)
.

In this case, the boundary terms in (
30
) cancels, and we obtain the simpler formula

\boxed{{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,t\in[0,1])=\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\mathrm{rank}\,P_{(E+a_{i},E+a_{i+1})}(A_{t_{i}})}\quad\text{for}\quad A\in{\mathcal{C}}_{I},\quad\text{and}\quad E\in I.

Now, the results of the previous Lemma holds, without the endpoints constraint. Mimicking the proof of Lemma
A.4
gives the following.

Lemma A.8
.

Let
A_{t}
be a continuous operator family in
{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
, and let
E\in I\setminus\sigma_{{\rm{ess}}}(\{A_{t}\})
. There is
\varepsilon>0
so that, for any norm-resolvent continuous family
(B_{t})
of self-adjoint operators satisfying
d(A_{t},B_{t})<\varepsilon
for all
t\in[0,1]
and such that
A_{t}+B_{t}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
, we have
{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}(B_{t},E,[0,1])
.

From this stability Lemma, we easily deduce the counterpart of Lemma
A.5
.

Lemma A.9
.

Let
(s,t)\mapsto(A_{t}^{(s)})
be a norm-resolvent continuous family of self-adjoint operators, such that, for all
s
, the family
t\mapsto A_{t}^{(s)}
is in
{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
. Let
E\in I
, and assume that
{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t}^{(s)},E,t\in[0,1])
is well-defined for all
s
. Then this spectral flow is independent of
s
.

In particular, using Lemma
A.2
stating that
\lambda\mapsto A-\lambda
is continuous, we get the following.

Lemma A.10
.

Let
(A_{t})\in{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
, and let
g\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(\{A_{t}\})
be an essential gap of the family
(A_{t})
, so that
g\subset I
. Then the spectral flow
{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,[0,A])
is independent of
E
in the gap
g
.

Finally, we state the counterpart of Lemma
A.6
.

Lemma A.11
.

Let
(K_{t})
be a continuous family of symmetric compact operators such that, for all
s\in[0,1]
, we have
(A_{t}+sK_{t})\in{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
. Then, for
E\in I
, we have
{\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t},E,[0,1])={\mathrm{Sf}}(A_{t}+K_{t},E,[0,1])
.
