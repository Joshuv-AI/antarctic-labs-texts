\mathrm{Herm}_{m}\times\mathrm{Mat}_{n,m}
under the map

\Pi
. In other words, the natural identification of the spaces

(\mathrm{Herm}_{m}\times\mathrm{Mat}_{n,m})/\mathrm{U}(m)\longleftrightarrow(\Xi_{m}\times\mathrm{Mat}_{n,m})/\mathbb{T}^{m}

is measure preserving.

We came to the following problem.
Consider the space

\mathrm{Herm}_{n}\times\mathrm{Mat}_{n,m}\times\Xi_{m}

equipped with
a measure

\tau_{m+n}\left|\det\left(1+i\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&M\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|^{-2n-2m}dw_{m}(M)\,d\dot{A}\,d\dot{B}.

(2.8)

We must watch behavior of this measure under transformations 2)-4)
described in Lemma

2.1
.

2.4. The taking of a characteristic function.

Next, we write the characteristic function of the matrix

i\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&M\end{pmatrix}
,

\varphi(s)=iA-sB(1-isM)^{-1}B^{*}=iA-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{sb_{k}b_{k}^{*}}{1-is\mu_{k}},

(2.9)

where
b_{k}
are columns of the matrix
B
.

We see that poles of
\varphi(s)
are located at points

s_{k}=\frac{1}{i\mu_{k}}
, and residues at the poles are rank 1 matrices given by

\mathop{\mathrm{Res}}\nolimits_{s=1/{i\mu_{k}}}\varphi(s)=-\frac{b_{k}b_{k}^{*}}{\mu_{k}^{2}}.

Notice that

\varphi(0)=iA
, and the matrix
b_{k}b_{k}^{*}
remembers the vector
b_{k}
up to a phase.
Therefore, there is no need to introduce new coordinates,
the matrices
A
,
B
,
M
can be automatically reconstructed from
the function

\varphi(s)
.

Notice that in a general position all the vectors

b_{k}
are non-zero.

2.5. The second Cayley transform.

Next, we take the function

\psi(s)=-1+2(1+\varphi(s))^{-1}.

Notice that for a pure imaginary

s
the matrix

\psi(s)
is unitary (since the matrix
\varphi(s)
is anti-Hermitian).
Denote

U:=\psi(0)
. Clearly,

iA=-1+2(1+U)^{-1}.

Next, at points

s=1/i\mu_{k}
a matrix
(1+\varphi(s))^{-1}

is degenerate, wherefore
(-1)
is an eigenvalue of
\psi(s)
.
Let us write Taylor expansions of functions

1+\varphi(s)
and
(1+\varphi(s))^{-1}

at a point
s=1/i\mu_{k}
,

1+\varphi(s)=-\frac{b_{k}b_{k}^{*}}{\mu_{k}^{2}\left(s-\frac{1}{i\mu_{k}}\right)}+V+\dots,\qquad(1+\varphi(s))^{-1}=W+\left(s-\frac{1}{i\mu_{k}}\right)Y+\dots,

where
V
,
W
,
Y
are certain matrices. Then

(1+\varphi(s))^{-1}(1+\varphi(s))=1=-\frac{Wb_{k}b_{k}^{*}}{\mu_{k}^{2}\left(s-\frac{1}{i\mu_{k}}\right)}+(WV-\mu_{k}^{-2}Yb_{k}b_{k}^{*})+...

Therefore,

\displaystyle Wb_{k}b_{k}^{*}=0;

\displaystyle WV-\mu_{k}^{-2}Yb_{k}b_{k}^{*}=1.

(2.10)

We have

Wb_{k}=0
. Otherwise, multiplying the vector column
Wb_{k}
by a non-zero row
b_{k}

we get a nonzero matrix. Next, we write the equality of matrix elements

\langle(\dots)b_{k},b_{k}\rangle
for both sides of (
2.10
),

\bigl\langle(WV-\mu_{k}^{-2}Yb_{k}b_{k}^{*})b_{k},b_{k}\bigr\rangle=\langle b_{k},b_{k}\rangle.

(2.11)

Notice that

\langle WVb_{k},b_{k}\rangle=\langle Vb_{k},W^{*}b_{k}\rangle=\langle Vb_{k},0\rangle=0.

Indeed, the matrix

W=(\psi(s)+1)/2
is normal, therefore
\ker W=\ker W^{*}
.

Next,

(Yb_{k}b_{k}^{*})b_{k}=Yb_{k}(b_{k}^{*}b_{k})=\langle b_{k},b_{k}\rangle\,Yb_{k}.

Hence equation
(
2.11
) transforms to

\langle Yb_{k},b_{k}\rangle=-2\mu_{k}^{2},

or, equivalently, to

\langle\psi^{\prime}(1/i\mu_{k}),b_{k}\rangle=-\mu_{k}^{2}.

(2.12)

In particular, we see that vectors

b_{k}
can be reconstructed up to a phase from the function

\psi(s)
.

2.6. The linear fractional change of the argument.

It remains to perform the last step, i.e., passing to the function

\chi(t)=\psi\Bigl(\frac{t+1}{t-1}\Bigr).

Let us define new parameters of conjugacy classes:

1) points
t_{k}
of the unit circle defined from the condition:

t_{k}:=\frac{1+i\mu_{k}}{1-i\mu_{k}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\frac{t_{k}-1}{t_{k}+1}=i\mu_{k};

2) a matrix
U\in\mathrm{U}(n)
,

U:=-1+2(1+iA^{-1})^{-1};

3) vectors
c_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,

c_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}e^{i\theta_{k}}(1+t_{k})b_{k},

(2.13)

where a phase factor

e^{i\theta_{k}}
is fixed by the condition
c_{k}^{1}>0

(where
c_{k}^{1}
is the first coordinate).

Clearly,

\bullet

U=\chi(-1)
;

\bullet

t_{k}
are the points, where
\chi(t)
has an eigenvalue
-1
;

\bullet

c_{k}
are normalized solutions of the equation
\chi(t_{k})v_{k}=-v_{k}
.

Compose a diagonal matrix

T
with entries
t_{j}
;
compose a matrix
C
of size
n\times m
from the columns

c_{k}
.

The condition (
2.12
) can be written as

\langle\chi^{\prime}(t_{k})b_{k},b_{k}\rangle=-\frac{4}{(t_{k}+1)^{2}}=-\frac{4t_{k}^{-1}}{|t_{k}+1|^{2}}

or

\langle\chi^{\prime}(t_{k})c_{k},c_{k}\rangle=-t_{k}^{-1}.

Now we can watch what is happened with the measure
(
2.8
) under this change.
Simultaneously, we pass to quotient by the action
of
\mathbb{T}^{m}
, recall that in
Subsect.
2
this was leaved for a future.

a) The Lebesgue measure
d\dot{B}
after change (
2.13
) and passing
to quotient by the action the torus transforms to

2^{2m(n-1)}\cdot(2\pi)^{m}\prod_{k=1}^{m}|1+t_{k}|^{-2n}\prod_{k=1}^{m}c_{1k}\,dc_{1k}\,\prod_{1\leqslant l\leqslant n,2\leqslant k\leqslant n}d\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits c_{kl}^{\prime}\,\,d\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits c_{kl}^{\prime}.

(2.14)

b) The measure
dw^{m}
, see (
2.5
), transforms to

2^{n(n-1)/2}(2\pi)^{m}\cdot 2^{m(m+1)}\prod_{k=1}^{m}|1+t_{k}|^{-2m}\prod_{1\leqslant k<l\leqslant m}|t_{k}-t_{l}|^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\frac{dt_{k}}{it_{k}}.

(2.15)

c) It remains to examine the factor

\tau_{m+n}\left|\det\left(1+i\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&M\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|^{-2n-2m}\,d\dot{A}.

First, we transform it to the form

\tau_{m+n}\left|\det\left(1+i\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&M\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|^{-2n-2m}\,d\dot{A}=\\
=\frac{\tau_{m+n}}{\tau_{n}}\left|\det\left(1+i\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&M\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|^{-2n-2m}|\det(1+iA)|^{2n}\times\\
\times\tau_{n}|\det(1+iA)|^{-2n}d\dot{A}=\\
=\frac{\tau_{m+n}}{\tau_{n}}\left|\det\left(1+i\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&M\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|^{-2n-2m}|\det(1+iA)|^{2n}\,d\sigma_{n}(U),

(2.16)

here we applied the Hua formula
(
2.3
) for the Haar measure.

Next, we must transform two determinants in this formula,

\det(1+iA)=\det\bigl(1-1+2(1+U)^{-1}\bigr)=2^{n}\det(1+U)^{-1}.

\det\left(1+i\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&M\end{pmatrix}\right)=\det\begin{pmatrix}1+iA&iB\\
iB^{*}&1+iM\end{pmatrix}=\\
=\det\begin{pmatrix}2(1+U)^{-1}&2iC(1+T)^{-1}\\
2i(1+T^{-1})C^{*}&1+(T+1)^{-1}(T-1)\end{pmatrix}

The right lower block equals to

2T(1+T)^{-1}=2(1+T^{-1})^{-1}
, therefore, we get

2^{m+n}\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&(1+T^{-1})^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\det\begin{pmatrix}(1+U)^{-1}&iC\\
iC^{*}&1+T\end{pmatrix}\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&(1+T)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=\\
=2^{m+n}\prod_{k=1}^{m}|1+t_{k}|^{-2}\cdot\det(1+U)^{-1}\det\bigr(1+T+C^{*}(1+U)C\bigl).

In the last pass, we applied the formula for a determinant of a block matrix,

\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=\det a\,\det(d-ca^{-1}b).

(2.17)

Collecting all the formulas together, we come to Theorem

1.2
.

\square

1.1
does not need a separate proof,

of double coset space to another.
