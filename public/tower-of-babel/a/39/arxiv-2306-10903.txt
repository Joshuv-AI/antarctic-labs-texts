Dynamics and Quantum Optimal Transport: Three lectures on quantum entropy and quantum Markov semigroups
Eric Carlen

Abstract
This document presents the contents of three lectures delivered by the author at the Erdős Center School “Optimal Transport on Quantum Structures”, Septemer 19-23, 2022 in Budapest, Hungary. It presents a fairly self contained account of an active topic of current research, and this account should be accessible to most graduate students, as befits lectures for a school. The main results are known, but there a number of new proofs and some new results.



1
Lecture One: Quantum Entropy Inequalities

1.1
Introduction

The following notation will be used throughout these lectures.
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
denotes the
n\times n
complex matrices,

M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
the positive semidefinite
n\times n
complex matrices, and

M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
the positive definite
n\times n
complex matrices. Sometimes it is more convenient to refer instead to bounded operators on a
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, in which case we use the corresponding notations
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
\mathcal{B}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{B}^{++}(\mathcal{H})
. To keep the proofs simple, we shall always assume that
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional.
This is the heart of the matter and is the case most relevant to applications in quantum information theory. However, many of the results do extend easily to infinite dimensions.
In particular, for the tools necessary to generalize the content of the first lecture to a fairly general von Neumann algebra setting, see Araki’s paper
[
5
]
.
Araki’s paper also makes an explicit connection with the matrix algebra case treated here,

A
density matrix

\rho
is an element of
M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
with unit trace; i.e.,
{\rm Tr}[\rho]=1
.
The set of all
invertible density matrices
is denoted
\mathfrak{S}
, and its closure, the set of all density matrices is
\overline{{\mathfrak{S}}}
. For a quantum observable represented by a self-adjoint operator
A
, the expected value of the measurement of
A
with the system in the state
\rho
is
{\rm Tr}[\rho A]
. For a density matrix
\rho
, the
von Neumann entropy

S(\rho)

is the quantity

S(\rho):=-{\rm Tr}[\rho\log\rho]\ .

Given two density matrices
\rho
and
\sigma
, the

Umegaki relative entropy of
\rho
with respect to
\sigma

[
84
]
is the quantity

D(\rho||\sigma):={\rm Tr}[\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)]\ .

(1.1)

This is one of several generalizations of the classical formula for the relative entropy of two strictly positive probability distribution
p
and
q
on
\{1,\dots,n\}
, namely

D(p||q)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}\log\left(\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}\right)\ .

There are various natural interpretations of “logarithm of
\rho
divided by
\sigma
” for two density matrices
\rho,\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}
. One, the choice made by Umegaki, is
\log\rho-\log\sigma
.
Another natural choice is

{\displaystyle\log\left(\rho^{1/2}\sigma^{-1}\rho^{1/2}\right)}
.
Making this other choice yields the
Belavkin-Stasewski relative entropy

[
7
]

D_{BS}(\rho||\sigma):={\rm Tr}\left[\rho\log\left(\rho^{1/2}\sigma^{-1}\rho^{1/2}\right)\right]\ .

There are many other generalizations of the classical formula, some of which lead to meaningful relative entropy functionals that share many key
properties of the classical relative entropy functional, and others that do not.
For more on this, see
[
37
,
15
]
.

Having pointed out the there are other interesting functionals that merit the name “quantum relative entropy”, the rest of these lectures focus on the Umegaki relative entropy defined in (
1.1
), and when we refer to quantum relative entropy in what follows, we always mean the Umegaki relative entropy, which has a direct physical meaning, as we now explain.

Loosely speaking, the number of experiments required to distinguish the state
\rho
from
\sigma
with an error probability of size

\epsilon
is
-\log\epsilon/D(\rho||\sigma)
, in a complete analogy with “hypothesis testing” in classical mathematical statistics. See
[
47
,
50
,
69
,
70
]
for more information and the introduction to
[
13
]
for a brief resume.
In this sense, the relative entropy is a physically relevant measure of the “divergence” of
\rho
from
\sigma
. While the relative entropy is not a metric –
D(\rho||\sigma)
is not even symmetric in
\rho
and
\sigma
– it does dominate the square of a natural metric, as expressed by the quantum Pinsker inequality

D(\rho||\sigma)\geq\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[|\rho-\sigma|]^{2}\ .

(1.2)

The evolution of quantum states, i.e., density matrices, in non-equilibrium quantum statistical mechanics is given by
quantum Markov
Semigroups
, which are semigroups of
quantum operations
, as we now explain.

Let
\Phi:M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
be a linear transformation.
\Phi
is
positive
when
\Phi(A)\geq 0
for all
A\geq 0
.
\Phi
is
2
-positive if the block matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi(A)&\Phi(B)\\
\Phi(C)&\Phi(D)\end{array}\right]\geq 0
whenever
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}\right]\geq 0
. For each integer
k>2
, the condition of
k
-positivity is defined in the analogous manner, and
\Phi
is
completely
positive if it is
k
positive for all
k

[
80
]
. For example, it is easy to see that for any
m\times n
matrix
V
,
the map
\Phi:X\mapsto V^{*}XV
is completely positive, and it follows that for any set
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{\ell}\}
of
m\times n
matrices,

\Phi:X\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}V_{j}^{*}XV_{j}\ .

(1.3)

is completely positive. By a theorem of Kraus
[
54
]
and Choi
[
25
,
27
]
, every completely positive map
\Phi
from
M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
to
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
has this form, and in fact, one can always find such a representation with
\ell\leq mn
. The map
\Phi
is
unital
if it takes the identity to the identity; i.e.,
\Phi(I)=I
. The map
\Phi
is
trace preserving
if
{\rm Tr}[\Phi(X)]={\rm Tr}[X]
for all
X
. For each
n
, we equip
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle:={\rm Tr}[X^{*}Y]
, making it a Hilbert space. For any linear transformation
\Phi:M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, we write
\Phi^{\dagger}
to denote the adjoint with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. It is easy to see that if
\Phi
is given by (
1.3
), then

\Phi^{\dagger}:X\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}V_{j}XV_{j}^{*}\ .

In any case, whether
\Phi
is completely positive or not,
\Phi
is unital if and only if
\Phi^{\dagger}
is trace preserving.

Unital completely positive maps
\Phi
and their completely positive trace preserving duals
\Phi^{\dagger}
play a fundamental role in quantum information theory and the quantum theory of open systems
[
32
]
. Since the state of a finite dimensional quantum system may be identified with a density matrix

\rho\in\overline{{\mathfrak{S}}}
, a linear map
\Phi^{\dagger}
that updates the state of a quantum system must at the very least preserve positivity and the trace. But this is not the only requirement. The condition of complete positivity arises through the consideration of a system relative to its environment, and hence it is completely positive unital maps that update the observables in the time evolution, and completely positive trace preserving maps that update the states under a partial measurement of a system
[
54
]
. Completely positive trace preserving maps maps
\Phi^{\dagger}
are known as
quantum operations
, and they give the
Schödinger picture
of the evolution of a system, with the states being updated. In the dual
Heisenberg picture
, the state is unchanged, but the observables are updated by
\Phi
.
In the literature on quantum Markov semigroups, it is fairly
standard to use
\Phi
to refer to the Heisenberg picture and
\Phi^{\dagger}
to refer to the Schrödinger picture. We shall generally adhere to this convention here.

The following example is fundamental: Let
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
and identify
{\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}^{mn}
with the
m
-fold direct sum of
m
copies of
{\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}^{n}
. We may then think of matrices in
M_{mn}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
as
m\times m
block matrices each of whose entries is in
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. Then, suppressing the
n
dependence, for each
m
we define

\Psi_{m}(X)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}X&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&X\end{array}\right]\ ,

where the matrix on the right is the
m\times m
block diagonal matrix each of whose diagonal entries is
X
. This is evidently completely positive and unital. Its adjoint,
\Psi_{m}^{\dagger}
, is therefore trace preserving and completely positive. It is easy to see that

\Psi^{\dagger}_{m}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}X_{1,1}&\cdots&X_{1,m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
X_{m,1}&\cdots&X_{m,m}\end{array}\right]\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j,j}\ ,

and this operation is known as the
partial trace
.
Some of the theorems that follow involve negative powers. When
\Phi:M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
is unital and completely positive,
\Phi(A)\in M_{m}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
whenever
A\in M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. However, the image of
M_{m}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
under the completely positive trace preserving map
\Phi^{\dagger}
may lie entirely in
M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\backslash M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. For example, take
n=2m
and think of elements of
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
as
2\times 2
block matrices with entries in
M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. Then define

\Phi\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}\right]\right)=A\qquad{\rm so\ that}\qquad\Phi^{\dagger}(A)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]\ .

A fundamental property of the quantum relative entropy is that it decreases under any quantum operation. This is the
Data Processing Inequality
(DPI) proved by Lindblad
[
61
]
building on earlier work
[
59
,
60
]
, and it says that for any two density matrices
\rho
and
\sigma
, and any quantum operation
\Phi^{\dagger}
,

D(\Phi^{\dagger}\rho||\Phi^{\dagger}\sigma)\leq D(\rho||\sigma)\ .

(1.4)

Roughly speaking, performing any quantum operation on
\rho
and
\sigma
can only make then harder to distinguish. This inequality is one of the cornerstones of quantum information theory.

We now turn to semigroups of quantum operations, and make our first connection relating them and quantum entropy.

1.1 DEFINITION
.

A
Quantum Markov Semigroup
(QMS) is a semigroup
\{\mathscr{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
of completely positive unital maps on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. Its dual

\{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a semigroup of completely positive trace preserving maps. Such semigroups are called
Quantum Dynamical Semigroups
.

For any QMS
\{\mathscr{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, and any
t>0
since
\mathscr{P}_{t}{\mathds{1}}={\mathds{1}}
,
1
is an eigenvalue of each
\mathscr{P}_{t}
, and hence each
\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}
.
Therefore, there will be at least one invariant density matrix
\sigma
for the dynamical semigroup
\{\mathscr{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
; i.e., a density matrix

\sigma
such that
\mathscr{P}_{t}\sigma=\sigma
for all
t\geq 0
. We will generally be interested in the case in which there is exactly one such state
\sigma
; i.e., the case in which the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
1
of
\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}
is
1
. In this case, the QMS is said to be
ergodic
.

Consider such a quantum dynamical semigroup
\{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\}_{t\geq 0}
with invariant state
\sigma
. Then by the invariance of
\sigma
and the DPI, for any density matrix
\rho
.

D(\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho||\sigma)=D(\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho||\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\sigma)\leq D(\rho||\sigma)\ .

That is, in complete generality, the function

t\mapsto D(\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho||\sigma)

is monotone decreasing. The following list of questions motivate the discussion that follows:

(1)
Under what circumstances do we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}D(\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho||\sigma)=0
, and when this is the case, when is the convergence exponential and at what exponential rate? (Note that by the Pinsker Inequality (
1.2
), when
D(\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho||\sigma)
decreases at an exponential rate, then
{\rm Tr}[|\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho-\sigma|]
decreases at one-half this exponential rate.)

(2)
Under what circumstances can we write the evolution
\rho\mapsto\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho
as gradient for for the relative entropy with respect to some metric on the space
\mathfrak{S}
of invertible density matrices?

(3)
When it is possible to write the evolution
\rho\mapsto\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho
as gradient for the relative entropy with respect to some metric on the space
\mathfrak{S}
of invertible density matrices,
under what circumstances can we explicitly find such a metric on
\mathfrak{S}
and relate the rate at which
\lim_{t\to\infty}D(\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho||\sigma)=0
to geometric properties of the metric?

Throughout this investigation, a number of inequalities pertaining to quantum entropy play a fundamental role. We begin the discussion proper with a self-contained presentation of these, with complete proofs.

1.2
Convexity theorems and monotonicity theorems

The DPI (
1.4
) is a prototypical monotonicty theorem: It says that the relative entropy is monotone decreasing under the application of any completely positive trace preserving map. This is closely connected with the fact that

(\rho,\sigma)\mapsto D(\rho||\sigma)

is convex; this is the
joint convexity of the relative entropy
. To see that this is a simple consequence of the DPI, consider the map
\Phi:M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{2n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
be defined by

\Phi(H)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}H&0\\
0&H\end{array}\right]\quad{\rm and\ hence}\quad\Phi^{\dagger}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Y_{1}&0\\
0&Y_{2}\end{array}\right]\right)=Y_{1}+Y_{2}\ .

These are evidently completely positive and
\Phi^{\dagger}
is evidently trace preserving. Now consider two pairs of density matrices
\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\}
and
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}
and define

\rho:=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho_{1}&0\\
0&\rho_{2}\end{array}\right]\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\sigma:=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma_{1}&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}\end{array}\right]\ .

Then the DPI says

D(\rho||\sigma)\geq D\left(\frac{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}{2}\big|\big|\frac{\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}}{2}\right)\ ,

but clearly

D(\rho||\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}D(\rho_{1}||\sigma_{1})+\frac{1}{2}D(\rho_{2}||\sigma_{2})\ .

Therefore

\frac{1}{2}D(\rho_{1}||\sigma_{1})+\frac{1}{2}D(\rho_{2}||\sigma_{2})\geq D\left(\frac{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}{2}\big|\big|\frac{\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}}{2}\right)\ .

By continuity, midpoint convexity implies convexity in general, and this proves that the joint convexity is a consequence of the DPI.

It turns out that the DPI is also a consequence of the joint convexity of the relative entropy. Historically, this is the way the DPI was first proved
[
61
]
:
The joint convexity of the relative entropy is a simple consequence of the Lieb Concavity Theorem
[
57
]
, and from here, Lindblad used the
structure theory of completely positive maps given by the Stinespring Dilation Theorem to prove the DPI. See
[
12
]
for more information.

Many well-known concavity or convexity theorems for matrices have an equivalent expression as a monotonicity theorem, just as we
have explained in regard to the joint convexity of the relative entropy and the DPI. It turns out that the monotonicity formulations have a number of advantages:

(1)
It is often simpler to give a direct proof of the monotonicity theorem than a direct proof of the convexity/concavity theorem but the former always implies the latter via consideration of the partial trace.

(2)
The monotonicity theorem often has direct physical significance, as in the case of the DPI.

(3)
The monotonicity theorem often holds for a wider class of positive maps than quantum operations. A case in point is the DPI which
was recently shown by Müller-Hermes and Reeb
[
68
]
to be valid for all positive trace preserving maps.

The fundamental monotonicity inequalities that we will prove here are the monotonicity versions of the first three convexity theorems in a fundamental 1973 paper of Lieb
[
57
]
:

1.2 THEOREM
.

For all completely positive unital maps
\Phi:M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, all

0\leq t\leq 1
, all
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, all
X,Y\in M_{m}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, all
K\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,

{\rm Tr}[\Phi(K^{*})Y^{1-t}\Phi(K)X^{t}]\leq{\rm Tr}[K^{*}\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)^{1-t}K\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{t}]\ .

(1.5)

1.3 THEOREM
.

For all completely positive unital maps
\Phi:M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,
all
0\leq t\leq 1
, all
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, all

X,Y\in M_{m}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
and all
K\in M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
such that
\Phi^{\dagger}(X),\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)\in M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,

{\rm Tr}[\Phi^{\dagger}(K^{*})\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)^{t-1}\Phi^{\dagger}(K)\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{-t}]\leq{\rm Tr}[K^{*}Y^{t-1}KX^{-t}]\ .

(1.6)

1.4 THEOREM
.

For all completely positive unital maps
\Phi:M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, all
0\leq t\leq 1
, all
m,n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, all
X,Y\in M_{m}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, all
K\in M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,

{\rm Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\dagger}(K^{*})\frac{1}{sI+\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)}\Phi^{\dagger}(K)\frac{1}{sI+\Phi^{\dagger}(X)}{\rm d}s\right]\leq{\rm Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}K^{*}\frac{1}{sI+Y}K\frac{1}{sI+X}{\rm d}s\right]\ .

(1.7)

Theorem
1.2
was first proved by Uhlmann
[
83
]
in a more general – and definitive – form. He did not need to assume that
\Phi
is completely positive or unital, but only that it satisfies the inequality

\Phi(K^{*}K)\geq\Phi(K)^{*}\Phi(K)\ .

This is known the
Schwarz inequality
in operator algebras, and as we will explain below, it holds for all unital completely positive maps
[
26
]
, but also for a wider class of positive maps.
In the more restricted form stated here, all that we will need, Theorem
1.2
is equivalent to the celebrated Lieb Concavity Theorem, Theorem
1.5
below, as we shall explain.

First, let us see how Theorem
1.2
yields the DPI. Take
K=1
. Since
\Phi
is unital, (
1.7
) reduces to

{\rm Tr}[Y^{1-t}X^{t}]\leq{\rm Tr}[\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)^{1-t}\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{t}]\ ,

Since
\Phi^{\dagger}
is trace preserving,
{\rm Tr}[Y]={\rm Tr}[\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)]
, and hence for all
t>0
,

\frac{{\rm Tr}[Y^{1-t}X^{t}]-{\rm Tr}[Y]}{t}\leq\frac{{\rm Tr}[\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)^{1-t}\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{t}]-{\rm Tr}[\Phi(Y)]}{t}\ .

Taking the limit
t\downarrow 0
, we obtain

{\rm Tr}[Y(\log X-\log Y)]\leq{\rm Tr}[\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)(\log\Phi^{\dagger}(X)-\log\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)]\ ,

and this is the same as
D(\Phi^{\dagger}(X)||\Phi^{\dagger}(Y))\leq D(X||Y)
.

We next explain how Theorem
1.2
yields the Lieb Concavity Theorem:

1.5 THEOREM

(Lieb, 1973)
.

For
0\leq t\leq 1
, and any fixed
K\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, the function

(X,Y)\mapsto{\rm Tr}[K^{*}Y^{1-t}KX^{t}]

(1.8)

is jointly concave on
M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\times M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
.

Proof.

Let
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
be positive definite in
M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, and define

X=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X_{1}&0\\
0&X_{2}\end{array}\right]\qquad{\rm and}\qquad Y=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Y_{1}&0\\
0&Y_{2}\end{array}\right]\ .

As before, define
\Phi:M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{2m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
by
\Phi(K)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}K&0\\
0&K\end{array}\right]
for all
K\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,
and note that
\Psi
is completely positive and unital. Then

Y^{1-t}\Phi(K)X^{t}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Y_{1}^{1-t}KX_{1}^{t}&0\\
0&Y_{2}^{1-t}KX_{2}^{t}\end{array}\right]

so that
{\rm Tr}[\Phi(K^{*})Y^{1-t}\Phi(K)X^{t}]={\rm Tr}[K^{*}Y_{1}^{1-t}KX_{1}^{t}]+{\rm Tr}[K^{*}Y_{2}^{1-t}KX_{2}^{t}]
. On the other hand,

{\rm Tr}[K^{*}\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)^{1-t}K\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{t}]={\rm Tr}[K^{*}(Y_{1}+Y_{2})^{1-t}K(X_{1}+X_{2})^{t}]\ .

Then by (
1.5
)

{\rm Tr}[K^{*}Y_{1}^{1-t}KX_{1}^{t}]+{\rm Tr}[K^{*}Y_{2}^{1-t}KX_{2}^{t}]\leq{\rm Tr}[K^{*}(Y_{1}+Y_{2})^{1-t}K(X_{1}+X_{2})^{t}]\ .

Since
(X,Y)\mapsto{\rm Tr}[K^{*}Y^{1-t}KX^{t}]
is homogeneous of degree one, this is equivalent to (
1.8
).
∎

In the exact same way, one deduces from Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
that the following are true:

1.6 THEOREM

(Lieb, 1973)
.

For all
0\leq t\leq 1
,

(X,Y,K)\mapsto{\rm Tr}[K^{*}Y^{-1+t}KX^{-t}]

is jointly convex on
M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\times M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\times M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
.

1.7 THEOREM

(Lieb, 1973)
.

(X,Y,K)\mapsto{\rm Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}K^{*}\frac{1}{sI+Y}K\frac{1}{sI+X}{\rm d}s\right]\

is jointly convex on
M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\times M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\times M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
.

An argument of the type Lindblad made to deduce the DPI from the joint convexity of the relative entropy can be used to derive Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
and
1.4
from Theorems
1.5
,
1.6
and
1.7
respectively; see
[
12
]
. However, we do not need this here: It is simpler to directly prove the monotonicity version.

In
[
57
]
, Lieb proved that the Theorems Theorems
1.5
,
1.6
and
1.7
are all equivalent to one another in the sense that once one has proved any of them, the others follows by simple arguments. The analog of this equivalence for the monotonicity versions is much simpler.

Indeed, Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.6
are structurally different in that one is a concavity statement in the two variables
X
and
Y
, the other is a convexity statement in the three variables
X
,
Y
and
K
. However, for fixed
X
and
Y
, the inequalities in the monotonicity version are both comparisons of two quadratic forms in the variable
K
. The Legendre transform provides a duality between quadratic forms that then relates Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
, as will be shown at the end of this lecture. In fact, it will be shown that all three of the fundamental monotonicity theorems are equivalent.
This equivalence by duality will be significant for the third lecture.

However, before proving Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, we explain their relevance to the questions posed here. Recall that we are interested in metrics on the space
\mathfrak{S}
of invertible density matrices. Metrics that have a certain monotonicity property paly a key role in what follows.

In the Introduction, it was noted that Theorems 2 and 3 of
[
57
]
provided the answers to questions that were not asked until many years later, and that when they were finally asked,
it was not recognized
that the answers could be found in
[
57
]
. The questions concerned
monotone metrics
on the space of non-degenerate density matrices.

1.3
Monotone metrics

Let
\rho(t)
be a differentiable path in the space
\mathfrak{S}
of invertible
n\times n
density matrices; i.e., elements of

M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
with unit trace. Then the derivative
\rho^{\prime}(t)=K(t)
is a self-adjoint operator with
{\rm Tr}[K(t)]=0
. We may think of

\mathfrak{S}
as a differentiable manifold, and it is then of interest to equip it with various Riemannian metrics that have the property that the
distance between
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}
,
d(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
, decreases under the application of
any quantum operation; i.e.,

d(\Phi^{\dagger}(\rho_{1}),\Phi^{\dagger}(\rho_{1}))\leq d(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\ .

(1.9)

In such a metric, any quantum operation performed on the states can only make it harder to distinguish between them. See the Introduction to
[
13
]
for further discussion of the problem of distinguishing between states, which is basic to quantum communication.

We may identify the tangent space to
\mathfrak{S}
at each point
\rho
with the space of traceless self-adjoint
n\times n
matrices. If we denote the quadratic form that specifies a Riemannian metric at
\rho
by
\gamma_{\rho}(K,K)
, then the contractive property (
1.9
) for such a metric is equivalent to

\gamma_{\Phi^{\dagger}(\rho)}(\Phi^{\dagger}(K),\Phi^{\dagger}(K))\leq\gamma_{\rho}(K,K)\ .

It is then natural to ask whether such metrics exist, and if so, what they might be. The same question had been raised and answered in the classical case in which the analog of
\mathfrak{S}
is the set
\mathfrak{S}_{c}
of
n
dimensional strictly positive probability vectors

p:=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
with each
p_{j}>0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}=1
. At each
p\in\mathfrak{S}_{c}
,
we may identify the tangent space with the subspace of
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
consisting of vectors
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})

with

\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}=0
. The analogs of quantum operations are the
row stochastic matrices

P
; i.e., elements of

M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}})
with non-negative and the property that for each
j
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i,j}=1
. In this setting,
it is natural to ask for metrics
\gamma
on
\mathfrak{S}_{c}
with the property that

\gamma_{Pp}(Pk,Pk)\leq\gamma_{p}(k,k)

for all stochastic
P
at each
p\in\mathfrak{S}_{c}
and for each tangent vector
k
. In 1982, Cencov
[
22
]
, building on earlier work of Fisher
[
41
]
, proved that the unique such metric, up to a constant multiple, is the
Fisher Information

\gamma_{p}(k,k)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{k_{j}^{2}}{p_{j}}\ .

A decade later, Cencov together with Morozova
[
66
]
, took up the quantum problem. In the non-commutative setting, there are many possible
ways to “divide by” a non-degenerate density matrix
\rho
. Morozova and Cencov came up with several conjectures, all eventually shown to be correct,
that certain explicit metrics were in fact monotone metrics, although they did not resolve any of these conjectures. An account of their work can be found in
[
76
]
.

However, had they known of Theorems 2 and 3 of Lieb’s paper, and then recognized them as monotonicity theorems, they would have had positive solutions to the most important of their conjectures: These theorems, written in the monotonicity forms (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) show that for each
0<t<1

\gamma_{\rho}^{(t)}(K,K):={\rm Tr}[K\rho^{t-1}K\rho^{-t}]

(1.10)

and

\widehat{\gamma}_{\rho}(K,K):={\rm Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}K\frac{1}{s+\rho}K\frac{1}{s+\rho}{\rm d}s\right]

(1.11)

are monotone metrics. As we have already observed in a Legendre transform argument, the right hand sides are always positive for non-zero
K
, so that these do define Riemannian metrics, and then the monotonicity is provided by (
1.6
) and (
1.7
). Note that when
\rho
and
K
commute, we have

\gamma_{\rho}^{(t)}(K,K)=\widehat{\gamma}_{\rho}(K,K)={\rm Tr}\left[\frac{K^{2}}{\rho}\right]\ ,

as one might expect.
Thus these functionals studied by Lieb in 1973 are both natural quantum generalizations of the Fisher Information metric
.
However, none of the early writers on the subject made the connection with Lieb’s theorems, and his paper
[
57
]
is not cited in
[
76
]
which gives the first explicit proof of the fact that (
1.10
) and (
1.11
) do in fact define monotone metrics as had been conjectured by Morozova and Cencov.

The inequality (
1.11
) can be viewed as an entropy inequality. To see this recall the integral representation for the logarithm of
X\in M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
:

\log(X)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda+1}-\frac{1}{\lambda+X}\right){\rm d}\lambda\ .

(1.12)

From here one easily deduces that the right hand side of (
1.11
) is the negative of the Hessian of the entropy
S(\rho)
. That is,

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s\partial t}{\rm Tr}[(\rho+sK+tK)\log(\rho+sK+tK)]\bigg|_{s=0,t=0}={\rm Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}K\frac{1}{s+\rho}K\frac{1}{s+\rho}{\rm d}s\right]\ ,

as one can verify using the integral representation for the logarithm (
1.12
). Lesniewski and Ruskai
[
56
]
showed that all monotone metrics arise in this manner from one of the
quasi entropies
that had been introduced by Petz
[
74
,
75
]
.

1.4
The Lieb-Ruskai Monotonicity Theorem

The Lieb-Ruskai Monotonicity Theorem is a fundamental result proved in
[
58
]
. It asserts the monotonicity of an operator function in two variables under completely positive maps.

1.8 THEOREM

(Lieb and Ruskai 1974)
.

Let
\Phi:M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
be completely positive. Then for all
K\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, and all
X\in M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})

\Phi\left(K^{*}\frac{1}{X}K\right)\geq\Phi(K)^{*}\frac{1}{\Phi(X)}\Phi(K)\ .

Some years later, Choi
[
28
]
proved a generalization. To state this in a convenient form, recall that for a matrix
X\in M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, the
Moore-Penrose
generalized inverse of
X
, denote
X^{+}
, is the operator obtained from
X
by replacing all
non-zero
eigenvalues of
X
by their inverses and leaving the kernel as it is.

1.9 THEOREM

(Choi 1980)
.

A map
\Phi:M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
is
2
-positive if and only if for all
K\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
and all
X\in M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
such that

{\rm ker}(X)\subset{\rm ker}(K^{*})
,

\Phi\left(K^{*}X^{+}K\right)\geq\Phi(K)^{*}\Phi(X)^{+}\Phi(K)\ .

(1.13)

Apart from the relaxation of the condition that
X\in M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, which Choi did not relax, this is an extension of Theorem
1.8
since every completely positive map is
2
positive, but the converse is not true; see, e.g.,
[
25
]
. Choi’s proof uses a special case of the following well-known lemma on Schur complements.

1.10 LEMMA
.

For
X,Y\in M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
and
K\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
the following are equivalent:

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&K\\
K^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]\geq 0\ .

{\rm ker}(X)\subseteq{\rm ker}(K^{*})\quad{\rm and}\quad Y\geq K^{*}X^{+}K\ .

{\rm ker}(Y)\subseteq{\rm ker}(K)\quad{\rm and}\quad X\geq KY^{+}K^{*}\ .

Proof.

First suppose that
X\in M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. For any
v\in{\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}^{n}
,

\left\langle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-X^{-1}Kv\\
v\end{array}\right)\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&K\\
K^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-X^{-1}Kv\\
v\end{array}\right)\right\rangle=\langle v,Yv\rangle-\langle v,K^{*}X^{-1}Kv\rangle\ .

On the other hand,

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&K\\
K^{*}&K^{*}X^{-1}K\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X^{1/2}&0\\
K^{*}X^{-1/2}&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X^{1/2}&X^{-1/2}K\\
0&0\end{array}\right]\geq 0\ .

Now suppose only that
X\in M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. Then for all
\epsilon>0
,
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X+\epsilon{\mathds{1}}&K\\
K^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]\geq 0
and

X+\epsilon{\mathds{1}}\in M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. Hence what we have just proved shows that

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&K\\
K^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]\geq 0\quad\iff\quad Y\geq K^{*}(X+\epsilon{\mathds{1}})^{-1}K\qquad{\rm for\ all}\ \epsilon>0\ .

(1.14)

Evidently, (
1.14
) holds for all
\epsilon>0
if and only if
{\rm ker}(X)\subseteq{\rm ker}(K^{*})
, and in this case

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}K^{*}(X+\epsilon{\mathds{1}})^{-1}K=K^{*}X^{+}K\ .

Therefore, relaxing the condition that
X\in M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
to
X\in M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
yields

{\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&K\\
K^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]\geq 0}
if and only if
{\rm ker}(X)\subseteq{\rm ker}(K^{*})
and
Y\geq K^{*}X^{+}K
.

The upper left and lower right corners of
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&K\\
K^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]
are on an equal footing and therefore it is also true that

{\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&K\\
K^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]\geq 0}
if and only if
{\rm ker}(Y)\subseteq{\rm ker}(K)
and
X\geq KY^{+}K^{*}
.
∎

We next prove Theorem
1.9
in full generality, without any assumptions of invertibility; see
[
19
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is
2
-positive, and
{\rm ker}(X)\subset{\rm ker}(K^{*})
. By Lemma
1.10

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&K\\
K^{*}&K^{*}X^{+}K\end{array}\right]\geq 0

and then since
\Phi
is
2
-positive,
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi(X)&\Phi(K)\\
\Phi(K)^{*}&\Phi(K^{*}X^{+}K)\end{array}\right]\geq 0
. By Lemma
1.10

once again, (
1.13
) is valid.

Next, suppose that (
1.13
) is valid whenever
{\rm ker}(X)\subset{\rm ker}(K^{*})
. We first claim that in this case,

{\rm ker}(\Phi(X))\subseteq{\rm ker}(\Phi(K)^{*})
. To see this,
taking
X={\mathds{1}}
, and
K=L^{*}
for any
L\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, we see that
\Phi
satisfies

\Phi(LL^{*})\geq\Phi(L^{*})^{*}\Phi({\mathds{1}})^{+}\Phi(L^{*})\geq 0

(1.15)

for all
L\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. In particular,
\Phi
is positive, and
\Phi(L^{*})=\Phi(L)^{*}
. For any positive matrix
A
, we have for some
\lambda>0
,
0\leq A\leq\lambda{\mathds{1}}
, and hence

0\leq\Phi(A)\leq\lambda\Phi({\mathds{1}})
. Hence
{\rm ran}(\Phi(A))\subseteq{\rm ran}(\Phi({\mathds{1}}))
. Since any
L^{*}\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
can be written as a linear combination of
4
non-negative matrices, it follows that
{\rm ran}(\Phi(L^{*}))\subseteq{\rm ran}(\Phi({\mathds{1}}))
for all
L\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, and consequently it follows from (
1.15
) that

{\rm ker}(\Phi(LL^{*}))\subseteq{\rm ker}(\Phi(L^{*}))\qquad{\rm for\ all}\quad L\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\ .

(1.16)

Finally, since
{\rm ker}(X)\subset{\rm ker}(K^{*})
,
X\geq\lambda KK^{*}
for some
\lambda>0
, and then since
\Phi
is positive and moreover
satisfies (
1.15
),
\Phi(X)\geq\lambda\Phi(KK^{*})
, and then by (
1.16
),

{\rm ker}(\Phi(X))\subseteq{\rm ker}(\Phi(KK^{*}))\subseteq{\rm ker}(\Phi(K)^{*})\ .

Now suppose that

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&K\\
K^{*}&Y\end{array}\right]\geq 0
. By Lemma
1.10
,
{\rm ker}(X)\subset{\rm ker}(K^{*})
and
Y\geq K^{*}X^{+}K
. By (
1.13
) and the positivity of
\Phi
,

\Phi(Y)\geq\Phi\left(K^{*}X^{+}K\right)\geq\Phi(K)^{*}\Phi(X)^{+}\Phi(K)\ ,

and we have just seen that
{\rm ker}(\Phi(X))\subseteq{\rm ker}(\Phi(K)^{*})
.
By Lemma
1.10
again,

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi(X)&\Phi(K)\\
\Phi(K)^{*}&\Phi(Y)\end{array}\right]\geq\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi(X)&\Phi(K)\\
\Phi(K)^{*}&\Phi(K^{*}X^{-1}K)\end{array}\right]\geq 0\ ,

and hence
\Phi
is
2
-positive.
∎

Theorem
1.9
has many consequences. The first is the following immediate corollary:

1.11 COROLLARY
.

Let
\Phi:M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
be
2
-positive and unital.
Then for all
K\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,

\Phi(K^{*}K)\geq\Phi(K)^{*}\Phi(K)\ .

(1.17)

Kadison
[
52
]
had proved the inequality (
1.17
) for all
self-adjoint

K
and all positive
\Phi
, and referred to (
1.17
) as a Schwarz inequality.
Unital maps that satisfy (
1.17
) are known as
Schwarz maps
.
(The terminology is nearly, but not completely, standard. Petz
[
75
, p. 62]
calls any map satisfying (
1.17
) a Schwarz map.)
Choi proved in
[
28
, Appendix A]
that there exist Schwarz maps that are not
2
-positive; e.g., the
map
\Phi
on
M_{2}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
given by

\Phi(X)=\frac{1}{2}X^{T}+\frac{1}{4}{\rm Tr}[X]I\ ,

where
X^{T}
is the transpose of
X
. His construction was further developed in
[
82
]
and
[
49
, Example 3.6]
.

Corollary
1.11
is the source of many monotonicity inequalities.
However, in all proofs to come, we only need (
1.13
) (with
\Phi^{\dagger}
in place of
\Phi
for later convenience) in the
tracial
form:

{\rm Tr}\left[\Phi^{\dagger}\left(K^{*}X^{+}K\right)\right]\geq{\rm Tr}\left[\Phi^{\dagger}(K)^{*}\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{+}\Phi^{\dagger}(K)\right]\quad{\rm whenever}\quad{\rm ker}(X)\subseteq{\rm ker}(K^{*}),

(1.18)

which, when
\Phi^{\dagger}
is quantum operation, and hence trace preserving, is the same as

{\rm Tr}\left[K^{*}X^{+}K\right]\geq{\rm Tr}\left[\Phi^{\dagger}(K)^{*}\Phi^{\dagger}(X)^{+}\Phi^{\dagger}(K)\right]\quad{\rm whenever}\quad{\rm ker}(X)\subseteq{\rm ker}(K^{*})\ ,

(1.19)

One may expect that (
1.18
) is valid for a wider class of maps
\Phi^{\dagger}
than
2
-positive maps, and this is the case. It was recently proved
[
19
]
by myself and Alexander Müller-Hermes that (
1.19
) is satisfied if and only if
\Phi
satisfies the Schwarz inequality, which while
(
1.18
) is satisfied if and only if
\Phi
is a generalized Schwarz map, as defined in
[
19
]
. However, it is the fact that (

) is valid whenever
\Phi
is
2
-positive and trace preserving that we shall need here. In fact, we will only need to know that this inequality is valid for all quantum operations
\Phi^{\dagger}
. In short, we we shall see, (

) is in some sense “the mother of all monotonicity and convexity theorems for trace functions”.

1.5
Operator monotonicity

A function
f:(0,\infty)\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
is said to be
operator monotone increasing
in case for all
A,B\in M_{n}^{++}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, any
n
,

A\geq B
implies
f(A)\geq f(B)
, and
f
is said to be
operator monotone decreasing
if
-f
is operator monotone increasing.

This inverse function
f(x)=x^{-1}
is operator monotone decreasing. To see this, let
A,B>0
, and define
X:=(A+B)^{-1/2}A(A+B)^{-1/2}
and
Y:=(A+B)^{-1/2}B(A+B)^{-1/2}
.
Evidently,
A\geq B\iff X\geq Y
, and
Y^{-1}\geq X^{-1}\iff B^{-1}\geq A^{-1}
. Since
X+Y=1
,
X
and
Y
commute, and then by the spectral theorem
X\geq Y
implies
Y^{-1}\ \geq X^{-1}
simply because
f(x)=x^{-1}
is monotone decreasing in
x
.

This example is the source of many other examples: For instance, let
0<t<1
. Then there is the integral representation

x^{t}=\frac{\sin(\pi t)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{t}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\lambda+x}\right){\rm d}{\lambda}=\frac{\sin(\pi t)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{t}\frac{x}{\lambda+x}\frac{{\rm d}{\lambda}}{\lambda}\ ,

and now it follows from what we have just proved that
f(x)=x^{t}
is operator monotone increasing for all
0<t<1
. By a theorem of Löwner
[
63
]
, every operator monotone increasing functions has an integral representation of this general form (see below), and this is the deep part of the theory. Simon’s book
[
78
]
contains a beautiful account, with many proofs, some new, of Löwner’s Theorem. However, for the specific examples that arise in this paper, elementary arguments suffice, such as the ones provided just above.

Nonetheless, we quote Löwner’s Theorem
[
63
]
as stated in Ando and Hiai
[
4
]
; see also
[
3
]
and
[
78
]
:

1.12 THEOREM

(Löwner’s theorem)
.

For
x,\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, define the function
\varphi(x,\lambda)
by

\varphi(x,\lambda):=(1+\lambda)\frac{x}{\lambda+x}\ ,

and notice that for each
x
,
\varphi(x,\lambda)
is a bounded function of
\lambda
, so that for any finite positive Borel measure
\mu
on
(0,\infty)
, all
\beta\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
and all
\gamma\geq 0
,

h(x):=\beta+\gamma x+\int_{(0,\infty)}\varphi(x,\lambda){\rm d}\mu\ ,

is a well defined function on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}
. The mapping
(\beta,\gamma,\mu)\mapsto h
is an affine isomorphism onto the class of operator monotone increasing functions.

Note that

\frac{x}{\lambda+x}=1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+x}\ .

By the opening remarks on the inverse function, it is clear that for each
\lambda
,
\varphi(\lambda,x)
is concave and monotone increasing in
x
, not only as a function of a real variable, but also in the operator sense. Thus all operator monotone functions
h
that are real valued on
(0,\infty)
are also operator concave, and thus
-h
is operator convex, and monotone decreasing.

1.6
Proof of the fundamental monotonicity theorems

As we have indicated in the introduction, Theorem
1.3
is equivalent to Theorem
1.2
by a duality argument, and
Theorem
1.4
is equivalent to an integrated form of Theorem
1.2
. This equivalence was pointed out by Hiai and Petz
[
48
]
who made
use of it to give a beautiful proof of these theorems and more. We give a development of their ideas starting from a duality theorem that not only clarifies the
origins of the equivalence that they discovered, but also allows the relaxation of some of the invertibility and non-degeneracy assumptions in their work.

Theorem
1.2
, Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
can all be viewed as inequalities between a pair of quadratic forms on the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{n}
consisting of
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
equipped with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. We now explain a basic duality between possibly degenerate quadratic forms on a finite dimensional Hilbert space that we shall apply

Let
A
and
B
be positive definite operators on some finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
; that is,
A,B\in\mathcal{B}^{++}(\mathcal{H})
.
Then

A\leq B\quad\iff\quad B^{-1}\leq A^{-1}\ .

(1.20)

One way to see this is to use the readily verified fact that for all
X\in\mathcal{B}^{++}(\mathcal{H})
, the Legendre transform of the quadratic form
v\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\langle v,Xv\rangle
is
w\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\langle w,X^{-1}w\rangle
.
That is,

\sup_{v\in\mathcal{H}}\{\Re\langle v,w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle v,Xv\rangle\ \}=\frac{1}{2}\langle w,X^{-1}w\rangle\ .

(1.21)

If the inequality on the left in (
1.20
) is valid, then for all
v,w\in\mathcal{H}
,

\Re\langle v,w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle v,Bv\rangle\leq\Re\langle v,w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle v,Av\rangle\ ,

and then taking the supremum over
v
and using (
1.21
), one obtain the inequality on the right in (
1.20
). The same sort of argument,
or an appeal to the fact that the Legendre transform is involutive, shows that the inequality on the right implies the inequality on the left, thus proving (
1.20
).

There is a variant of (
1.21
) that is valid for
X\in\mathcal{B}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
and not only for
X\in\mathcal{B}^{++}(\mathcal{H})
. Let
P
denote the orthogonal projection onto
{\rm ran}(X)
, the range of of
X
.
Since
X\in\mathcal{B}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
,
X|_{{\rm ran}(X)}
is invertible and

P\left(X|_{{\rm ran}(X)}\right)^{-1}P=X^{+}\ ,

(1.22)

where
X^{+}
is the generalized inverse of
X
. Then for all
w\in{\rm ran}(X)
, and all
v\in\mathcal{H}
,

\Re\langle v,w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle v,Xv\rangle=\Re\langle Pv,w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle Pv,XPv\rangle\ ,

and therefore, using (
1.21
) and (
1.22
),

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in\mathcal{H}}\{\ \Re\langle v,w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle v,Xv\rangle\ \}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\rm ran}(X)}\{\Re\langle x,w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle x,X|_{{\rm ran}(X)}x\rangle

(1.23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle w,X^{+}w\rangle\ .

In the next theorem, we apply these ideas to compare positive operators on two different Hilbert spaces given a linear map form one to the other.

1.13 THEOREM
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
be two finite dimensional Hilbert spaces, and let
T
be a linear transformation from
\mathcal{H}
into
\mathcal{K}
. Let
A\in\mathcal{B}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
and
B\in\mathcal{B}^{+}(\mathcal{K})
. Suppose that

{\rm ran}(T^{*})\subseteq{\rm ran}(A)\quad{\rm and}\quad{\rm ran}(T)\subseteq{\rm ran}(B)\ .

(1.24)

Then

\langle v,T^{*}BTv\rangle\leq\langle v,Av\rangle\qquad{\rm for\ all}\quad v\in\mathcal{H}

(1.25)

if and only if

\langle w,TA^{+}T^{*}w\rangle\leq\langle w,B^{+}w\rangle\qquad{\rm for\ all}\quad w\in\mathcal{K}\ .

(1.26)

Proof.

Suppose that (
1.25
) is valid. Then for all
v\in\mathcal{H}
and all
w\in\mathcal{K}
,

\Re\langle v,T^{*}w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle v,Av\rangle\ \leq\ \Re\langle v,T^{*}w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle v,T^{*}BTv\rangle\ .

(1.27)

Let
P
denote the orthogonal projection onto
{\rm ran}(A)
. By (
1.24
),
T^{*}w=PT^{*}w
and therefore by (
1.23
),

\sup_{v\in\mathcal{H}}\{\Re\langle v,T^{*}w\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle v,Av\rangle\ \}=\frac{1}{2}\langle T^{*}w,A^{+}T^{*}w\rangle\ .

(1.28)

Next, let
Q
denote the orthogonal projection onto the range of
B
, and consider the right side of (
1.27
),

1.14 LEMMA
.

Let
\Phi:M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\to M_{m}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})

{\rm ran}(\Phi^{\dagger})\subseteq{\rm ran}(L_{\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)})

Proof.

The statement about

{\rm ker}(R_{\Phi^{\dagger}(X)})\subset{\rm ker}(\Phi)
, To prove this, let
K\in{\rm ker}(R_{\Phi^{\dagger}(X)})
We must show that
\Phi(K)=0
, and evidently this is the case if and only if
\langle Y,\Phi(K)\rangle=0
for all
Y\in M_{m}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. Since for
Y\in M_{m}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})

\langle Y,\Phi(K)\rangle={\rm Tr}[Y\Phi(K)]={\rm Tr}[\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)K]={\rm Tr}[K\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)]\ ,

we must show that
{\rm Tr}[K\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)]=0

Since

\lambda>0
so that
X\geq\lambda Y
. Then since

is positive,

\Phi^{\dagger}(X)\geq\lambda\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)

Since

0=K\Phi^{\dagger}(X)K^{*}\geq\lambda K\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)K^{*}=\lambda\left(K\left(\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)\right)^{1/2}\right)\left(K\left(\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)\right)^{1/2}\right)^{*}\ .

Therefore,
K\left(\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)\right)^{1/2}=0
, and consequently

{\rm Tr}[\Phi^{\dagger}(Y)K]=0
as was to be shown. This proves the statemeent about

Lecture Two: Reversible QMS as Gradient flow for Relative Entropy

2.1
Gradient flow on
\mathfrak{S}

Recall that
{\mathfrak{S}}
, the space of strictly positive density matrices, is a relatively open subset of
\{A\in M_{n}^{+}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})\ :{\rm Tr}[A]=1\}
, and we may identify the tangent space
T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}

at each point
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}
with

\mathcal{V}:=\{A\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}):A=A^{*}\quad{\rm and}\quad{\rm Tr}[A]=0\}\ .

(2.1)

The cotangent space
T_{\rho}^{\dagger}{\mathfrak{S}}
may also be identified with
\mathcal{V}
through the duality pairing
\langle{A,B}\rangle={\rm Tr}[AB]
for
A,B\in\mathcal{V}
.

Let
\{g_{\rho}\}_{\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}}
be a Riemannian metric on
{\mathfrak{S}}
, i.e., a collection of positive definite bilinear forms
g_{\rho}:T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}\times T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
depending smoothly on
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}
.
Consider the associated operator
\mathscr{G}_{\rho}:T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}\to T_{\rho}^{\dagger}{\mathfrak{S}}
defined by

\langle{A,\mathscr{G}_{\rho}B}\rangle=g_{\rho}(A,B)

for
A,B\in T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}
.
Clearly,
\mathscr{G}_{\rho}
is invertible and self-adjoint with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product.
Define
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}:T_{\rho}^{\dagger}{\mathfrak{S}}\to T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}
by
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}=(\mathscr{G}_{\rho})^{-1}
, so that

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(A,B)=\langle{A,\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{-1}B}\rangle\ .

(2.2)

The contravariant metric tensor consists of the collection of positive definite bilinear forms
g^{\dagger}_{\rho}:T_{\rho}^{\dagger}{\mathfrak{S}}\times T_{\rho}^{\dagger}{\mathfrak{S}}\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
depending smoothly on
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}
given by

\displaystyle g_{\rho}^{\dagger}(A,B)=\langle{A,\mathcal{K}_{\rho}B}\rangle\ .

Equipping
T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}
and
T_{\rho}^{\dagger}{\mathfrak{S}}
with these inner products, making them real Hilbert spaces,

\mathscr{G}_{\rho}:T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}\to T_{\rho}^{\dagger}{\mathfrak{S}}
is an isometry. We could have started with the the inner product on
T_{\rho}^{\dagger}{\mathfrak{S}}
specified by the positive operator
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
, and then defined
\mathscr{G}_{\rho}
to be its inverse. In fact, this is often the most convenient way to specify the metrics with which we shall work.

For a smooth functional
\mathcal{F}:{\mathfrak{S}}\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
and
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}
, its
differential

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}(\rho)\in T_{\rho}^{\dagger}{\mathfrak{S}}
is defined by

\langle{A,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}(\rho)}\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathcal{F}(\rho+\varepsilon A)-\mathcal{F}(\rho))

for
A\in T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}
. Note that this is independent of the Riemannian metric
g_{\rho}
.
However, the
gradient

\nabla_{g}\mathcal{F}(\rho)\in T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}
depends on the Riemannian metric through the duality formula that defines it, namely

g_{\rho}(A,\nabla_{g}\mathcal{F}(\rho))=\langle{A,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}(\rho)}\rangle

for
A\in T_{\rho}{\mathfrak{S}}
. It follows that
\mathscr{G}_{\rho}\nabla_{g}\mathcal{F}(\rho)=\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}(\rho)
, or equivalently

\displaystyle\nabla_{g}\mathcal{F}(\rho)=\mathcal{K}_{\rho}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}(\rho)\ .

Given a smooth function
t\mapsto\rho(t)
with values in
{\mathfrak{S}}
, at each
t
,

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))=:\partial_{t}\rho(t)\in T_{\rho(t)}{\mathfrak{S}}\ .

The gradient flow equation
\partial_{t}\rho(t)=-\nabla_{g}\mathcal{F}(\rho(t))
takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(t)(t)=-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}(\rho)\ .

Now consider a QMS
(\mathscr{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with generator
\mathscr{L}
so that
\mathscr{P}_{t}=e^{t\mathscr{L}}
. Suppose that
\sigma
is the unique invariant state for this semigroup. Given
\rho_{0}\in{\mathfrak{S}}
, define the curve

\rho(t):=\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho_{0}\ .

Then by the DPI,
t\mapsto D(\rho(t)||\sigma)
is monotone decreasing. This is an obvious necessary condition for the evolution specified by

(\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger})_{t\geq 0}
to be gradient flow for the function
\mathcal{F}(\rho):=D(\rho||\sigma)
on
{\mathfrak{S}}
. There is however a further necessary condition: each

\mathscr{P}_{t}
must be self adjoint with respect to an inner product associated with the entropy.

Recall that

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s\partial t}{\rm Tr}[(\rho+sA+tB)\log(\rho+sA+tB)]\bigg|_{s=0,t=0}={\rm Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}A\frac{1}{\lambda+\rho}B\frac{1}{\lambda+\rho}{\rm d}\lambda\right]\ ,

Define
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}
by

\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(A):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda+\rho}A\frac{1}{\lambda+\rho}{\rm d}\lambda=\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}s}\log(\rho+sA)\bigg|_{s=0}

(2.3)

so that

\langle A,\mathcal{D}_{\rho}B\rangle={\rm Tr}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}A\frac{1}{\lambda+\rho}B\frac{1}{\lambda+\rho}{\rm d}\lambda\right]\ .

In this case it is easy to compute
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}
:

\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(A)=\int_{0}^{1}\rho^{1-t}A\rho^{t}{\rm d}t=\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}s}\exp(\log\rho+sA)\bigg|_{s=0}\ .

We then have the following non-degenerate quadratic forms:

\langle A,\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(B)\rangle\quad{\rm and}\quad\langle A,\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\rho}(B)\rangle\ .

In this geometric context, these quatratic forms are defined in the space
{\mathcal{V}}
defined in (
2.1
), but there is the obvious way to extend them to all of
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, and this is useful to do. In fact, the extension of the inner prodcut
\langle A,\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\rho}(B)\rangle
all of
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
has a name: It is the

Boguliobov–Kubo–Mori
(BKM) inner product
[
55
]
specified by
\rho
which is given by

\langle A,B\rangle_{{\rm BKM}}:=\langle A,\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\rho}(B)\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}{\rm Tr}\left[A^{*}\rho^{1-t}B\rho^{t}\right]{\rm d}t\ .

(2.4)

The following theorem was proved by Maas and myself
[
18
]
, and it is a quantum version of a result proved in the Markov chain setting by Dietert
[
36
]
.

2.1 THEOREM
.

Let
(\mathscr{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an ergodic QMS with generator
\mathscr{L}
and invariant state
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
.
If there exists a continuously differentiable Riemannian metric
(g_{\rho})
on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
such that the quantum master equation
\partial\rho=\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}\rho
is the gradient flow equation for
\Ent_{\sigma}
with respect to
(g_{\rho})
, then each
\mathscr{P}_{t}
is self-adjoint with respect to the BKM inner product associated to
\sigma
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Suppose that there is such a metric, and let operator
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
be defined in terms of it by (
2.2
).
Since
\mathrm{d}D(\rho||\sigma)=\log\rho-\log\sigma
, the gradient flow equation
\partial_{t}\rho=-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}\mathrm{d}\Ent_{\sigma}(\rho)
is

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}\rho=-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\ .

Applying this identity to
\rho_{\varepsilon}=\sigma+\varepsilon A
for
A\in{\mathord{\cal A}}_{0}
, and differentiating at
\varepsilon=0
, we obtain using (
2.3
)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}A=-\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda+\sigma}A\frac{1}{\lambda+\sigma}{\rm d}\lambda\right)=-\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}(A)\ ,

or equivalently,

\mathscr{L}A=-\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda+\sigma}\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}(A)\frac{1}{\lambda+\sigma}{\rm d}\lambda\right)=-\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}(A)\ ,

Consequently, for
A,B\in{\mathcal{V}}
, with
{\mathcal{V}}
given by (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\langle{\mathscr{L}A,B}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\rm BKM}(\sigma)}=-{\rm Tr}[A^{*}\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}B]\ .

As the operator
\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}
is self-adjoint with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt scalar product, this implies the result.
∎

A converse has very recently been proved by Brooks and Maas
[
10
]
: The condition of BKM self adjointness is sufficient as well as necessary.
At this stage, the construction of the Riemannian metric is somewhat implicit, and it is not presently clear in what sense it might be a transport
metric, or how it might be used to address the questions raised in the first lecture.

However, for a physically important sub-class of QMS semigroups for which each
\mathscr{P}_{t}
is self-adjoint with respect to the
BKM inner product for the invariant state, one can construct the metric very explicitly, and use this construction to prove
inequalities governing the rate of approach to the invariant state, among other things. The basis of this construction is a structure theorem for
the generators of this class of processes. In the next section, we discuss structure theorems for various classes of
QMS generators, focusing on results we shall use to construct our metrics.

2.2
Inner products on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
associated to
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}
and the structure of QMS Generators

Work of Lindblad
[
62
]
and Gorini, Kossakowski and Sudarshan
[
46
]
has provided a structure theorem for the set of QMS generators. It turns out that if one imposes certain
self-adjointness conditions on the QMS, the structure theorem becomes much more specific. We have already seen in Theorem
2.1

that if
\mathscr{P}_{t}
is a QMS such that
\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}
is given by
gradient flow for the relative entropy functional
D(\rho||\sigma)
, where
\sigma
is the invariant state for
\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}
, then
\mathscr{P}_{t}
must be self adjoint for the
BKM inner product defined in (
2.4
). Unfortunately, there does not presently exist a useful structure theorem for the set of BKM self-adjoint QMS generators,
but for the smaller class of GNS self-adoint QMS generators, introduced below, there is such a structure theorem due to Alicki
[
1
]
.

We now discuss various inner products on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
determined by a state
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}
, and we closely follow
[
2
]
. Let
\mathcal{P}[0,1]
denote the set of probability measures on the interval
[0,1]
. Notice that for each
s\in[0,1]
,

{\rm Tr}[B^{*}\sigma^{1-s}A\sigma^{s}]={\rm Tr}[(\sigma^{(1-s)/2}B\sigma^{s/2})^{*}\sigma^{(1-s)/2}A\sigma^{s/2}]\ ,

and the right hand side is strictly positive when
B=A\neq 0
.

2.2 DEFINITION
.

For each
m\in\mathcal{P}[0,1]
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{m}
denotes the inner product on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
given by

\langle B,A\rangle_{m}={\rm Tr}[B^{\ast}\mathcal{M}_{m}(A)]\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{M}_{m}(A)=\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{s}A\sigma^{1-s}\;\mathrm{d}m(s)\ .

(2.5)

The
Gelfand-Naimark-Segal
(GNS) inner product corresponds to
m=\delta_{0}
, the point mass at
s=0
. Other cases are known by name. Taking
m=\delta_{1/2}
yields the
Kubo-Martin-Schwinger
(KMS) inner product

\langle B,A\rangle_{{\rm KMS}}={\rm Tr}[B^{*}\sigma^{1/2}A\sigma^{1/2}]\ .

Taking
m
to be uniform on
[0,1]
yields the Bogoliubov-Kubo-Mori inner product

\langle B,A\rangle_{{\rm BKM}}
already defined in (
2.4
).

For
\sigma=n^{-1}{\mathds{1}}
, the normalized identity, all choices of
m
reduce to the normalized Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.

Throughout what follows,
\mathfrak{H}
always denotes the Hilbert space
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
equipped with this inner product,

\langle B,A\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}=\frac{1}{n}{\rm Tr}[B^{*}A]\ .

Let
\mathcal{L}(M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}))
denote the linear operators on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, or, what is the same thing in this finite dimensional setting, on
\mathfrak{H}
. A dagger is always used to denote the adjoint with respect to the inner product on
\mathfrak{H}
, That is, for
\Phi\in\mathcal{L}(M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}))
,
\Phi^{\dagger}
is defined by

\langle\Phi^{\dagger}(B),A\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}=\langle B,\Phi(A)\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}

for all
A,B
, which is consistent with our previous notation since the normalization has no effect on the adjoint.

We can also make
\mathcal{L}(M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}))
into a Hilbert space by equipping it with the normalized Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.
Throughout what follows, this Hilbert space is denoted by
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
. The following formula for the inner product in

\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
is often useful. Let
\{F_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
be any orthonormal basis for
\mathfrak{H}
. Then for
\Phi
and
\Psi\in\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}
,

\langle\Psi,\Phi\rangle_{\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle\Psi(F_{i,j}),\Phi(F_{i,j})\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\ .

To each
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
, there corresponds the
modular operator

\Delta_{\sigma}
on
\mathfrak{H}
defined by

\Delta_{\sigma}A=\sigma A\sigma^{-1}\ .

Consider any orthonormal basis
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\}
of
{\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}^{N}
consisting of eigenvectors of
\sigma
, so that for
1\leq j\leq n
,
\sigma u_{j}=\lambda_{j}u_{j}
. For
1\leq i,j\leq n
, define

E_{i,j}=\sqrt{n}|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{j}|
, so that
\{E_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathfrak{H}}
. A simple computation shows that for each
i,j
,

\Delta_{\sigma}E_{i,j}=\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}^{-1}E_{i,j}\ .

Thus
\Delta_{\sigma}
is diagonalized, with positive diagonal entries, by an orthonormal basis in
\mathfrak{H}
. It follows that
\Delta_{\sigma}
is a positive operator on
\mathfrak{H}
.
Using the Spectral Theorem, we may then define
\Delta_{\sigma}^{s}
for all
s
. This provides another way to write the operator
{\mathcal{M}}_{m}
defined in (
2.5
):

{\mathcal{M}}_{m}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\Delta_{\sigma}^{s}{\rm d}m\right)R_{\sigma}

where
R_{\sigma}
denotes right multiplication by
\sigma
, also a positive operator on
\mathfrak{H}
that commutes with
\Delta_{\sigma}
,
and hence with
\int_{0}^{1}\Delta_{\sigma}^{s}{\rm d}m
.

This may well look familiar. If we define
f_{m}(x)=\int x^{s}{\rm d}m
, then

{\mathcal{M}}_{m}={\mathbb{J}}_{f}(\sigma,\sigma)

where
{\mathbb{J}}_{f}(X,Y)
is the operator introduced in connection with the Hiai-Petz method for proving trace inequalities.

As is well-known, both the set of completely positive maps and the set of QMS generators are convex cones. For completely positive maps this is obvious. For the case of QMS generators, consider a QMS
\{\mathscr{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
with generator

\mathscr{L}:=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}(\mathscr{P}_{t}-I)

Note that
\mathscr{L}{\mathds{1}}=0
, and
\mathscr{P}_{t}=e^{t\mathscr{L}}
. If
\mathscr{L}_{1}
and
\mathscr{L}_{2}
are two generators of completely positive semigroups, then

e^{t(\mathscr{L}_{1}+\mathscr{L}_{2})}=\lim_{k\to\infty}(e^{(t/k)\mathscr{L}_{1}}e^{(t/k)\mathscr{L}_{2}})^{k}

(2.6)

is completely positive, and hence the set of generators of completely positive semigroups is closed under addition, and also evidently under multiplication
by non-negative real numbers. Since if
\mathscr{L}_{j}{\mathds{1}}=0
for
j=1,2
, then
(\mathscr{L}_{1}+\mathscr{L}_{2}){\mathds{1}}=0
, it follows easily that the set of all
QMS semigroup generators is a convex cone. However, it is not a pointed cone: Let
H\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
be self-adjoint, and define
\mathscr{L}(A)=i[H,A]
.
Then
e^{t\mathscr{L}}(A)=e^{itH}Ae^{-itH}
is a QMS, and evidently both
\mathscr{L}
and
-\mathscr{L}
are QMS generators.

There are some obvious examples of semigroups of completely positive operators on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
. For instance if
\Phi
is completely positive, so it
e^{t\Phi}
.
Likewise, for any
G\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, so is each
A\mapsto e^{-tG^{*}}Ae^{-tG}
, which has the generator

\mathscr{L}(A)=-G^{*}A-AG\ .

By what we have noted above, for any completely positive map
\Phi
on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
and any
G\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,

\mathscr{L}(A):=\Phi(A)-G^{*}A-AG

(2.7)

generates a semigroup of completely positive operators. It is a QMS generator if and only if
\mathscr{L}({\mathds{1}})=0
, and this in turn is the case if and only if

\Phi({\mathds{1}})=G^{*}+G
.

Write
G:=K+iH
where
H
and
K
are self adjoint. Then

\mathscr{L}(A)=\Phi(A)-\frac{1}{2}(\Phi({\mathds{1}})A+A\Phi({\mathds{1}}))+i[H,A]\ .

(2.8)

It turns out that this class of examples is all that there is: By a 1976 theorem
of Lindblad
[
62
]
and Gorini, Kossakowski and Sudarshan
[
46
]
, every QMS generator
\mathscr{L}
on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
has this exact form.
Moreover, since every completely positive map
\Phi
has a Kraus representation

\Phi(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}V_{j}^{*}AV_{j}\

(2.9)

for some
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{m}\}\subset M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,
(
2.8
) can be rewritten as

\mathscr{L}A=i[H,A]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}V_{j}^{*}[A,V_{j}]+[V_{j}^{*},A]V_{j}\ ,

(2.10)

and consequently,

\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}\rho=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{j=1}^{m}[V_{j},\rho V_{j}^{*}]+[V_{j}\rho,V_{j}^{*}]

(All of this will be proved in the next section of this lecture.)

The terms
\Phi
and
G
in the decomposition of
\mathscr{L}
in (
2.7
) are not uniquely determined by
\mathscr{L}
. Indeed, consider any CP map
\Phi
with the Kraus representation (
2.9
). Then for any choice of complex numbers
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}
, define
W_{j}:=V_{j}+c_{j}{\mathds{1}}
, and

\Psi(A):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}W_{j}^{*}AW_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}|c_{j}|^{2}A\ .

Then a simple computation shows that

\Phi(A)=\Psi(A)-G^{*}A-AG\qquad{\rm where}\qquad G=\sum_{j=1}c_{j}W_{j}\ .

Consequently, in the Lindblad form (
2.10
), the “Hamiltonian part”
i[H,A]
is not uniquely determined by
\mathscr{L}
.

The situation is better if we impose some sort of self adjointness requirement on
\mathscr{L}
. However, for general
m
, and for
\mathscr{L}
in the form (
2.7
),
it is possible for
\mathscr{L}
to be self adjoint but not
\Phi
, and vice-versa – even for any choice of the non-unique decomposition.
Hence in general,
the problem of determining the structure of the cone of completely positiver maps that are self adjoint with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

is different from the problem of determining the structure of the cone QMS generators the are self adjoint with respect to some
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{m}
.
There is, however, an important family of cases in which there is a canonical decomposition into self adjoint components.
This is when
m=\delta_{s}
, the point mass at
s\in[0,1]
, which includes the GNS and KMS inner products, but not the BKM inner product.

2.3
Structure of QMS generators

In this section we begin to explain the structure theory for various classes of QMS generators. We begin with some useful tools that are used in the proof of the
structure theorems, but are more widely useful. Again in this section we closely follow
[
2
]
.

Recall that
{\mathfrak{H}}
denotes
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
equipped with the normalized Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. Let
\mathcal{L}({\mathfrak{H}})
denote the space of linear transformation on
{\mathfrak{H}}
. We make

\mathcal{L}({\mathfrak{H}})
into a Hilbert space
\widehat{{\mathfrak{H}}}
by equipping
\mathcal{L}({\mathfrak{H}})
with its normalized Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. There is a very useful construction that takes two orthonormal bases for
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and constructs from them an orthonormal basis for
\widehat{{\mathfrak{H}}}
. Expanding
\Phi\in\mathcal{L}({\mathfrak{H}})
in bases constructed this way turns out to reveal significan properties of
\Phi
, as we now explain.

It is convenient to index orthonormal bases of
{\mathfrak{H}}
by ordered pairs

(i,j)\in\{1,\dots n\}\times\{1,\dots n\}=:\mathcal{J}_{n}\ .

We use lower case greek letters to denote elements of the index set. For
\alpha=(i,j)\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
,
\alpha^{\prime}:=(j,i)
.

For
F,G\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
, define the operator
\#(F\otimes G)
on
{\mathfrak{H}}
by

\#(F\otimes G)X:=FXG\ .

Simple computations show that

\langle\#(F_{1}\otimes G_{1}),\#(F_{2}\otimes G_{2})\rangle_{\widehat{{\mathfrak{H}}}}=\langle F_{1},F_{2}\rangle_{{\mathfrak{H}}}\langle G_{1},G_{2}\rangle_{{\mathfrak{H}}}\ .

Therefore, whenever
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
and
\{G_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
are two orthonormal bases of
{\mathfrak{H}}
,
\{\#(F_{\alpha}\otimes G_{\beta})\}_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
is an orthonormal basis of
\widehat{{\mathfrak{H}}}
.

Now fix any orthonormal basis
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
of
{\mathfrak{H}}
.
Then
\{F^{*}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
is also an orthonormal basis of
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and hence
\{\#(F_{\alpha}^{*}\otimes F_{\beta})\}_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
is an orthonormal basis of
\widehat{{\mathfrak{H}}}
. Thus,
every linear operator
\Phi
on
{\mathfrak{H}}
has an expansion

\Phi=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}(c_{\Phi})_{\alpha,\beta}\#(F_{\alpha}^{*}\otimes F_{\beta})\ ,

(2.11)

where

(c_{\Phi})_{\alpha,\beta}=\langle\#(F_{\alpha}^{*}\otimes F_{\beta}),\Phi\rangle_{\widehat{{\mathfrak{H}}}}\ .

(2.12)

In particular, the coefficients
(c_{\Phi})_{\alpha,\beta}
are uniquely determined.

2.3 DEFINITION

(Characteristic matrix)
.

Given
\Phi\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathfrak{H}})
, and an orthonormal basis
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
of
{\mathfrak{H}}
,
its
characteristic matrix
for this orthonormal basis is the
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix
C_{\Phi}
whose
(\alpha,\beta)
th entry is
[C_{\Phi}]_{\alpha,\beta}
as specified in
(
2.12
).

The following is a convenient formulation of a theorem of Choi
[
27
]
:

2.4 THEOREM
.

A linear operator
\Phi
on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
is completely positive if and only if for every orthonormal basis
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
of
{\mathfrak{H}}
, the corresponding characteristic matrix
C_{\Phi}
is positive semi-definite.

Proof.

The right side of (
2.12
) can be computed using the matrix unit basis
\{E_{k,\ell}\}
to compute the normalized trace:

[C_{\Phi}]_{\alpha,\beta}=\langle\#(F_{\alpha}^{*}\otimes F_{\beta}),\Phi\rangle_{\widehat{{\mathfrak{H}}}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq k,\ell\leq n}{\rm Tr}[E_{k,\ell}^{*}F_{\alpha}\Phi(E_{k,\ell})F_{\beta}^{*}]\ .

Let
(z_{1},\dots,z_{n^{2}})\in{\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}^{n^{2}}
and define
G:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}z_{\alpha}F_{\alpha}^{*}
. Then

\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\overline{z}_{\alpha}(c_{\Phi})_{\alpha,\beta}z_{\beta}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq k,\ell\leq n}{\rm Tr}[E_{\ell,k}G^{*}\Phi(E_{k,\ell})G]\ .

(2.13)

Let
[E_{i,j}]
denote the block matrix whose
i,j
th entry is
E_{i,j}
. Then it is easy to see that
n^{-1/2}[E_{i,j}]
is an orthogonal projection, and in particular, positive.
Now suppose that
\Phi
is completely positive. Then the block matrix
[G^{*}\Phi(E_{i,j})G]
whose
i,j
th entry is
G^{*}\Phi(E_{i,j})G
is positive. The right side of (
2.13
) is then
the trace (on the direct sum of
n
copies of
{\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}^{n}
) of the product of positive
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrices, and as such it is positive. Thus, whenever
\Phi
is completely positive,
C_{\Phi}
is positive semi-definite.

On the other hand, suppose that
C_{\Phi}
is positive semi-definite. Let
\Lambda
be a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the eigenvalues of
C_{\Phi}
, and let
U
be a unitary such that

C_{\Phi}=U^{*}\Lambda U
.
Then by (
2.11
), for any
X\in{\mathfrak{H}}
,

\Phi(X)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}U^{*}_{\alpha,\gamma}\lambda_{k}U_{\gamma,\beta}F_{\alpha}^{*}XF_{\beta}=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}V_{\gamma}^{*}XV_{\gamma}\ ,

where
{\displaystyle V_{\gamma}:=\sqrt{\lambda_{\gamma}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}U_{\gamma,\alpha}F_{\alpha}}
.
This shows that whenever
C_{\Phi}
is positive semi-definite,
\Phi
is completely positive, and provides a Kraus representation of it.
∎

So far, we have not required any special properties of the orthonormal bases
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
of
{\mathfrak{H}}
that we used. To characterize QMS generators, it will be necessary to choose bases that have several useful properties:

2.5 DEFINITION

(symmetric, unital and matrix unit bases)
.

An orthonormal basis
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
of
{\mathfrak{H}}
is
symmetric
in case

{\rm For\ all}\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}\ ,\quad F_{\alpha}^{*}=F_{\alpha^{\prime}}\ .

It is
unital
in case it is symmetric and moreover

F_{(1,1)}={\mathds{1}}\ .

It is the
matrix unit
basis corresponding to an orthonormal basis
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\}
of
{\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}^{n}
in case

F_{(i,j)}=\sqrt{n}|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{j}|\ .

Note that a matrix unit basis is symmetric.

One reason unital bases are useful is the following: Let us compute the characteristic matrix of the identity transformation for a unital basis
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
. Note that

{\rm Tr}[F_{\alpha}]={\rm Tr}[F_{(1,1)}^{*}F_{\alpha}]=n\delta_{(1,1),\alpha}\ .

Therefore, proceeding as in the proof of Lemma
2.4
,

(C_{I})_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq k,\ell\leq n}{\rm Tr}[E_{k,\ell}^{*}F_{\alpha}E_{k,\ell}F_{\beta}^{*}]=\\
\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\rm Tr}[F_{\alpha}]{\rm Tr}[F_{\beta}]=\delta_{\alpha,(1,1)}\delta_{\beta,(1,1)}\ .

(2.14)

That is,
C_{I}
is zero except in the upper left entry, where it has the value
1
. This fact will be useful below.

2.6 DEFINITION

(Reduced characteristic matrix)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a Hermitian operator on
{\mathfrak{H}}
such that
\mathscr{L}{\mathds{1}}=0
, and let
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
be a unital orthonormal basis.
Let
C_{\mathscr{L}}
be the characteristic matrix of
\mathscr{L}
with respect to this basis. The
reduced characteristic matrix

R_{\mathscr{L}}
of
\mathscr{L}
is the
(n^{2}-1)\times(n^{2}-1)
matrix obtained by deleting the first row and column of
C_{\mathscr{L}}
.

The following lemma is proved in
[
46
]
.

2.7 LEMMA
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a Hermitian operator on
{\mathfrak{H}}
such that
\mathscr{L}{\mathds{1}}=0
, and let
\{F_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
be a unital orthonormal basis. Let
R_{\mathscr{L}}
be the reduced characteristic matrix of
\mathscr{L}
with respect to this basis. Then
e^{t\mathscr{L}}
is completely positive for all
t>0
if and only if
R_{\mathscr{L}}
is positive semi-definite,
and in this case there is a completely positive operator
\Phi\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathfrak{H}})
and a
G\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
such that for all
A\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,

\mathscr{L}(A)=\Phi(A)-G^{*}A-AG\ .

(2.15)

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathscr{P}_{t}:=e^{t\mathscr{L}}
is completely positive for each
t>0
. By (
2.14
),
R_{I}
, the reduced characteristic matrix of the identity, is
0
.
Then since

R_{t^{-1}(\mathscr{P}_{t}-I)}=t^{-1}R_{\mathscr{P}_{t}}-t^{-1}R_{I}=t^{-1}R_{\mathscr{P}_{t}}\ ,

the reduced characteristic matrix of
t^{-1}(\mathscr{P}_{t}-I)
coincides with the reduced characteristic matrix of

t^{-1}\mathscr{P}_{t}
, and by Lemma
2.4
this is positive. Taking the limit
t\to 0
, we conclude that the reduced characteristic matrix of
\mathscr{L}
is positive.

Conversely, suppose that the reduced characteristic matrix of
\mathscr{L}
is positive.
Since
F_{(1,1)}={\mathds{1}}
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(A)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}[C_{\mathscr{L}}]_{\alpha,\beta}F_{\alpha}^{*}AF_{\beta}=-G^{*}A-AG+\sum_{\alpha,\beta}[R_{\mathscr{L}}]_{\alpha,\beta}F_{\alpha}^{*}AF_{\beta}

where

G:=-\frac{1}{2}(c_{\mathscr{L}})_{(1,1),(1,1)}{\mathds{1}}-\sum_{\beta}(c_{\mathscr{L}})_{(1,1),\beta}F_{\beta}\ .

By Theorem
2.4
, if we define
\Phi(A):=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}[R_{\mathscr{L}}]_{\alpha,\beta}F_{\alpha}^{*}AF_{\beta}
, then

\Phi
is completely positive. This shows that for all
A\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,
\mathscr{L}(A)=\Phi(A)-G^{*}A-AG
whenever
R_{\mathscr{L}}
is positive semidefinite. Then by the argument around (
2.6
),

e^{t\mathscr{L}}
is completely positive for all
t>0
, and
\mathscr{L}
has the form specified in (
2.15
).
∎

2.8 THEOREM

(Structure of QMS generators)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a QMS generator on

. Then there is a completely positive map
\Phi\in{\mathcal{L}}(M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}))
and a self adjoint

H\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})

such that for all

\mathscr{L}(A)=\Phi(A)-\frac{1}{2}(\Phi({\mathds{1}})A+A\Phi({\mathds{1}})+i[H,A]\ .

Proof.

By Lemma
2.7
, there is a completely positive operator
\Phi\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathfrak{H}})
and a
G\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
such that for all

,

\mathscr{L}(A)=\Phi(A)-G^{*}A-AG
. Since
\mathscr{L}
is a QMS generator,
\mathscr{L}{\mathds{1}}=0
, and hence

. Therefore,

for some self

Then

G^{*}A+AG=\frac{1}{2}\Phi({\mathds{1}})A+A\frac{1}{2}\Phi({\mathds{1}})-iHA+iAH=\frac{1}{2}\Phi({\mathds{1}})A+A\frac{1}{2}\Phi({\mathds{1}})-i[H,A]\ .

∎

2.4
Structure of generators satisfying detailed balance

A classical Markov chain on the state space
\{1,\dots,n\}
with transition matrix
P
and invariant measure
\mu
satisfies the
detailed balance condition
in case for all
1\leq i,j\leq n

\mu(i)P_{i,j}=\mu(j)P_{j,i}

(2.16)

which means that in the steady state, a transition from
i
to
j
has exactly the same probability as a transition from
j
to
i
. If one is watching a film of the process, it is impossible to know if the film is running backwards or forwards. For this reason, the detailed balance condition is sometimes referred to as
microscopic reversibility
. If we equip the space of functions on the state space
\{1,\dots,n\}
with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
defined by

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu(j)\overline{f(j)}g(j)\ ,

and define
Pf(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{i,j}f(j)
, then given (
2.16
)

\langle f,Pg\rangle_{\mu}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mu(i)P_{i,j}\overline{f(i)}g(j)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mu(j)P_{i,j}\overline{f(i)}g(j)=\langle Pf,g\rangle_{\mu}\ .

Thus, (
2.16
) implies that
P
is self adjoint with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
, and a little thought shows that the argument reverses to that in fact the self adjointness of
P
with respect to inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
characterizes the detailed balance condition.

This simple observation opens the way to defining the detailed balance condition for QMS semigroups
\mathscr{P}_{t}
with invariant state
\sigma
. Actually, it opens many ways, since, as we have seen, there are many choices for the inner product associated to
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}
.

There is an extensive discussion of quantum detailed balance in the the physics literature, and in particular on how it related to reversibility. For early papers from the 1970’s,
see

[
3
,
6
,
21
,
53
,
79
]
. In these papers various different proposals are discussed, and recent work
[
38
,
39
,
40
,
64
,
81
]
has added to the variety of
proposals. Indeed, the “right” choice of the inner product depends on the physical question being asked, and as emphasized already in the early work of Agarwal
[
6
]
, the connection with reversibility may involve other operations. In any case, two choices that have been considered to be particularly relevant in various physical contexts are the GNS and KMS inner products.

As will be seen here, the following choice is natural for our context:

2.10 DEFINITION
.

The generator
\mathscr{L}
of a QMS satisfies
detailed balance condition with respect to
\sigma
in case
\mathscr{L}
is self-adjoint with
regard the the GNS inner product associated to
\sigma
.

2.11 Remark
.

Whenever
\mathscr{L}
satisfies detailed balance condition with respect to
\sigma
,
\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}\sigma=0
since for any
A
,

0=\langle A,\mathscr{L}{\mathds{1}}\rangle_{{\text{G}NS}}=\langle\mathscr{L}A,{\mathds{1}}\rangle_{{\text{G}NS}}={\rm Tr}[(\mathscr{L}A)^{*}\sigma]={\rm Tr}[A^{*}\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}\sigma]\ .

Note that the GNS inner product is the inner product determined by
m_{0}=\delta_{0}
.
The key fact that makes an explicit structure theorem possible in this case is due to Alicki
[
1
]
:

2.12 LEMMA
.

Let
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}
be a non-degenerate density matrix, and let
s\in[0,1]
,
s\neq 1/2
.
Let
\mathcal{K}
be any operator on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
that is self-adjoint with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\delta_{s}}
and also preserves self-adjointness.
Then
\mathcal{K}
commutes with the modular operator
\Delta_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

For any
A,B\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{K}\Delta_{\sigma}^{2s-1}(A),B\rangle_{\delta_{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\sigma^{s}(\mathcal{K}(\sigma^{2s-1}A\sigma^{1-2s}))^{*}\sigma^{1-s}B]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\sigma^{s}(\sigma^{2s-1}A\sigma^{1-2s})^{*}\sigma^{1-s}\mathcal{K}(B)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\sigma^{1-s}A^{*}\sigma^{s}\mathcal{K}(B)]={\rm Tr}[\sigma^{s}(\mathcal{K}(B^{*}))^{*}\sigma^{1-s}A^{*}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\sigma^{s}B\sigma^{1-s}\mathcal{K}(A^{*})]={\rm Tr}[\sigma^{1-s}(\mathcal{K}(A))^{*}\sigma^{s}B]=\langle\mathcal{K}(A),B\rangle_{\delta_{1-s}}\ .

By a simple computation, for all
s,t\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\langle\Delta_{\sigma}^{t}(A),B\rangle_{\delta_{s}}={\rm Tr}[\sigma^{s}(\sigma^{t}A\sigma^{-t})^{*}\sigma^{1-s}B]={\rm Tr}[\sigma^{s-t}A^{*}\sigma^{1-s+t}B]=\langle A,B\rangle_{\delta_{s-t}}\ .

Since
s-(2s-1)=1-s
,
\langle\mathcal{K}(A),B\rangle_{\delta_{1-s}}=\langle\Delta_{\sigma}^{2s-1}(\mathcal{K}(A)),B\rangle_{\delta_{s}}
.
As
B
is arbitrary,
\Delta_{\sigma}^{2s-1}\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}\Delta_{\sigma}^{2s-1}
.
Since
\mathcal{K}
commutes with
\Delta_{\sigma}^{2s-1}
, it commutes with

f(\Delta_{\sigma}^{2s-1})
for every function
f
.
∎

In particular, if
\mathscr{L}
satisfies detailed balance, then

\mathscr{L}A=\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}(\sigma A\sigma^{-1}))\sigma\ .

We now consider a unital orthonormal basis for
{\mathfrak{H}}
consisting of eigenvectors of
\Delta_{\sigma}
.
This is possible since
\Delta_{\sigma}
is a positive operator. The eigenvectors are of the form
|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{j}|
where

\sigma u_{j}=\lambda_{j}u_{j}

and then

\Delta_{\sigma}(|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{j}|)=e^{\omega_{(i,j)}}|u_{i}\rangle\langle u_{j}|\quad{\rm where}\quad\omega_{i,j}:=\log\lambda_{i}-\log\lambda_{j}\ .

Computing the characteristic matrix
C_{\alpha,\beta}
for such a basis we find

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta}C_{\alpha,\beta}F_{\alpha}^{*}AF_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}A=\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}(\sigma A\sigma^{-1}))\sigma=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}C_{\alpha,\beta}\sigma^{-1}F_{\alpha}^{*}\sigma A\sigma^{-1}F_{\beta}\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta}C_{\alpha,\beta}e^{\omega_{\beta}-\omega_{\alpha}}F_{\alpha}^{*}AF_{\beta}\ .

In other words,

e^{\omega_{\alpha}}C_{\alpha,\beta}=C_{\alpha,\beta}e^{\omega_{\beta}}\ .

(2.17)

Since the characteristc matrix is self adjoint, (
2.17
) implies that

\omega_{\alpha}\neq\omega_{\beta}\quad\Rightarrow\quad C_{\alpha,\beta}=0\ .

Therefore with an ordering of the indices
\alpha
so that

\alpha\geq\beta\iff\omega_{\alpha}\geq\omega_{\beta}
, the matrix

C_{\alpha,\beta}
is block-diagonal. Now one need only recall how the Kraus operator in the representation of
\mathscr{L}
are determined by the eigenvectors of the reduced density matrix of
\mathscr{L}
, and one has Alicki’s Thoerem
[
1
]
:

2.13 THEOREM

(Structure of Lindblad operators with detailed balance)
.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{t}=e^{t\mathscr{L}}
be a quantum Markov semigroup on
B(\mathscr{H})
satisfying detailed balance with respect to
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}
.
Then the generator
\mathscr{L}
and its adjoint
\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}
have the form

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\mathscr{L}_{j}\ ,\qquad\mathscr{L}_{j}(A)=V_{j}^{*}[A,V_{j}]+[V_{j}^{*},A]V_{j}\ ,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\mathscr{L}_{j}^{\dagger}\ ,\qquad\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}_{j}(\rho)=[V_{j},\rho V_{j}^{*}]+[V_{j}\rho,V_{j}^{*}]\ ,

(2.19)

where
\mathcal{J}
is a finite index set, the operators
V_{j}\in B(\mathscr{H})
satisfy
\{V_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}=\{V_{j}^{*}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
, and
\omega_{j}\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}V_{j}

\displaystyle=e^{-\omega_{j}}V_{j}\quad\text{for all }j\in\mathcal{J}\ .

(2.20)

For
j\in\mathcal{J}
, let
j^{*}\in\mathcal{J}
be an index such that
V_{j^{*}}=V_{j}^{*}
. It follows from (
2.20
) that

\displaystyle\omega_{j^{*}}=-\omega_{j}\ .

Moreover, if we define
H=-\log\sigma
, (
2.20
) is equivalent to the commutator identity

[V_{j},H]=-\omega_{j}V_{j}
.
Furthermore, in our finite-dimensional context, the identity

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}^{t}V_{j}=e^{-\omega_{j}t}V_{j}

is valid for some
t\neq 0
in
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
if and only if it is valid for all
t\in{\mathord{\mathbb{C}}}
.

There is also a well developed structure theorem for generators of QMS semigroups that are self-adjoint with respect to the KMS inner product; see
[
2
,
40
]
. Unfortunately,
there does not yet exist such a theorem for the set of QMS generators that are self-adjoint with respect to the BKM inner product, which strictly includes
[
2
]
the set of those that
are self-adjoint for the GNS inner product considered here. By Theorem
2.1
, this would be the most natural class of QMS to consider. However, the metrics to be constructed in the
remainder of this lecture make use of the structure theorem, Theorem
2.13
, and its analog for the BKM case is presently lacking.

2.5
Gradient flow for the relative entropy

Consider a generator
\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}
written in the form (
2.19
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\mathscr{L}_{j}^{\dagger}\ ,\qquad\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}_{j}(\rho)=[V_{j},\rho V_{j}^{*}]+[V_{j}\rho,V_{j}^{*}]\ ,

(2.21)

where
\{V_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
is a finite set of eigenvectors of
\Delta_{\sigma}
such that
\{V_{j}^{*}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}=\{V_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
, and where
\Delta_{\sigma}V_{j}=e^{-\omega_{j}}V_{j}
for some
\omega_{j}\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
.
As before, we use the notation
\partial_{j}A:=[V_{j},A]
.

The next lemma will be a chain rule for the logarithm. It gives us a non-commuative analog and extension of the formula

\nabla\rho(x)=\rho(x)\nabla\log\rho(x)\ ,

which behind the fact that one recovers the usual linear heat equation from the gradient flow of the entropy with respect to the
2
-Wasserstein metric.
The relevant non-commutative analog of multiplication by
\rho
is somewhat involved, but is also something we have discussed.
Define

f_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}x^{s}{\rm d}s=\frac{1-x}{\log x}

on
(0,\infty)
.

Recall the operator introduced by Hiai and Petz:

{\mathbb{J}}_{f}(X,Y):=f(R_{X}L_{Y}^{+})L_{Y},

for any
Y,X\geq 0
and any operator monotone function
f
on
(0,\infty)
.
Taking
f
to be
f_{0}
, we find

{\mathbb{J}}_{f_{0}}(X,Y)(A)=\int_{0}^{1}Y^{1-s}AX^{s}{\rm d}s\ .

Our formulas will involve

{\mathbb{J}}_{f_{0}}(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho,e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)(A)=\int_{0}^{1}\big(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho\big)^{1-s}A\big(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho\big)^{s}\;\mathrm{d}s\ .

(2.22)

Evidently, if
A
commutes with
\rho
,

{\mathbb{J}}_{f_{0}}(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho,e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)(A)=e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho A\ .

That is,
{\mathbb{J}}_{f}(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho,e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)
is nothing other than a non-commutative version of multiplication by
e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho
.
Its inverse, which we have encountered in the first lecture, is

{\mathbb{J}}_{f_{0}}^{-1}(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho,e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)(A)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda+e^{\omega/2}\rho}A\frac{1}{\lambda+e^{-\omega/2}\rho}\;\mathrm{d}\lambda\ .

We are now ready to state our fundamental lemma
[
17
]
which may be regarded as a non-commutative chain rule for the logarithm.

2.14 LEMMA

(Chain rule for the logarithm)
.

For all
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
and
j\in\mathcal{J}
we have

\displaystyle e^{-\omega_{j}/2}V_{j}\rho-e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho V_{j}

\displaystyle=\left[{\mathbb{J}}_{f_{0}}(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho,e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)\right]\partial_{j}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\ .

Proof.

Since
\Delta_{\sigma}V_{j}=e^{-\omega_{j}}V_{j}
for all
j
,
\sigma^{s}V_{j}\sigma^{-s}=\Delta^{s}_{\sigma}V_{j}=e^{-s\omega_{j}}V_{j}
. Differentitating at
s=0
,

[V_{j},\log\sigma]=\omega_{j}V_{j}\ .

\displaystyle\partial_{j}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)

\displaystyle=[V_{j},\log\rho]-[V_{j},\log\sigma]

\displaystyle=V_{j}\log\rho-\log\rho V_{j}-\omega_{j}V_{j}

\displaystyle=V_{j}\log(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)-\log(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho)V_{j}\ .

Therefore,

\int_{0}^{1}\big(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho\big)^{1-s}[\partial_{j}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)]\big(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho\big)^{s}\;\mathrm{d}s=\\
\int_{0}^{1}\big(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho\big)^{1-s}[V_{j}\log(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)-\log(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho)V_{j}]\big(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho\big)^{s}\;\mathrm{d}s\ .

However,

\big(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho\big)^{1-s}[V_{j}\log(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)-\log(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho)V_{j}]\big(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)^{s}=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho)^{1-s}V_{j}(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho)^{s}\ .

The claim now follows from (
2.22
) and the integration of the total derivative.
∎

For
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}
we define the operator
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}:{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathcal{V}}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}A:=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\partial_{j}^{\dagger}\big({\mathbb{J}}_{f_{0}}(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho,e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)(\partial_{j}A)\big)\ .

(2.23)

Since
{\rm Tr}(A^{*}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}B)=\overline{{\rm Tr}(B^{*}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}A)}
for
A,B\in{\mathcal{V}}
, it follows that
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
is a non-negative self-adjoint operator on
{\mathfrak{H}}
for each
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
.
Recall that
{\mathcal{V}}
is the traceless self adjoint matrices in
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
which we have identified with the tangent and cotangent spaces to
{\mathfrak{S}}
.
Assuming that
\mathscr{P}_{t}
is ergodic, the operator
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}:{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathcal{V}}
is invertible for each
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
, as we explain below. Since
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
depends smoothly on
\rho
, it follows that
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
induces a Riemannian metric on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
defined by (
2.2
).

2.15 DEFINITION
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a QMS generator satisfying the detailed balance condition with respect to
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}
. For each
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}
, let the operator
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
be defined by (
2.23
). Then, provided
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
is invertible for all
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}
,
the Riemannian metric on
{\mathfrak{S}}
induced by
\mathscr{L}
is given by

g_{\rho}(\dot{\rho},\dot{\rho})=\langle\dot{\rho},\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{-1}\dot{\rho}\rangle=\langle(\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{-1}\dot{\rho}),\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{-1}\dot{\rho})\rangle\ .

(2.24)

The following result
[
17
]
shows that the Kolmogorov forward equation
\partial_{t}\rho=\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}\rho
can be formulated as the gradient flow equation for
\Ent_{\sigma}
. A different construction of such a metric was given at the same time by Mielke and Mittenzweig
[
65
]

2.16 PROPOSITION
.

For
\rho\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
we have the identity

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}\rho=-\mathcal{K}_{\rho}\mathrm{d}D(\rho||\sigma)\ ,

hence the gradient flow equation of
\Ent_{\sigma}
with respect to the Riemannian metric induced by
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}
is given by the solution of the equation
\partial_{t}\rho=\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}\rho
.

Proof.

Using the chain rule from Lemma
2.14
, and the fact that
\{V_{j}\}=\{V_{j}^{*}\}
and
\omega_{j^{*}}=-\omega_{j}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\rho}\mathrm{d}D(\rho||\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\partial_{j}^{\dagger}\big({\mathbb{J}}_{f_{0}}(e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho,e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho)\partial_{j}(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\partial_{j}^{\dagger}\big(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}V_{j}\rho-e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho V_{j}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\Big(\partial_{j}^{\dagger}\big(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}V_{j}\rho-e^{\omega_{j}/2}\rho V_{j}\big)+\partial_{j}\big(e^{\omega_{j}/2}V_{j}^{*}\rho-e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\rho V_{j}^{*}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\Big([V_{j},\rho V_{j}^{*}]+[V_{j}\rho,V_{j}^{*}]\Big)+e^{\omega_{j}/2}\Big([V_{j}^{*},\rho V_{j}]+[V_{j}^{*}\rho,V_{j}]\Big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\Big([V_{j},\rho V_{j}^{*}]+[V_{j}\rho,V_{j}^{*}]\Big)=-\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}\rho\ ,

which is the desired identity.
∎

The gradient flow structure given in Proposition
2.16
is a non-commutative analogue of the Kantorovich gradient flow structure established by Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto
[
51
]
for the
Kolmogorov forward equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(x,t)=\Delta\rho(x,t)-\nabla\cdot(\rho(x,t)\nabla\log\sigma(x))\ .

Their gradient flow structure is formally given in terms of the operator
K_{\rho}
defined by

\displaystyle K_{\rho}\psi=-\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla\psi)\ ,

for probability densities
\rho
on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
and suitable functions
\psi:{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
in analogy with (
2.23
).
As the differential of the relative entropy
\Ent_{\sigma}(\rho)=\int_{{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\rho(x)\log\frac{\rho(x)}{\sigma(x)}\;\mathrm{d}x
is given by
\mathrm{d}\Ent_{\sigma}(\rho)=1+\log\frac{\rho}{\sigma}
, we have

\displaystyle K_{\rho}\mathrm{d}\Ent_{\sigma}(\rho)=-\Delta\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla\log\sigma)\ ,

which is the commutative counterpart of Proposition
2.16
.

2.6
Non commutative differential structure

To bring out the analogy made at the end of he previous section, we introduce a non-commmutative gradient and divergence associated to
\mathscr{L}
, as specified by
\sigma
and

3
Applications of the Gradient Flow Structure

3.1
Geodesic convexity and relaxation to equilibrium

In this section we develop the advantages of having written the evolution equations as gradient flow for the relative entropy.
We draw on work of Otto and Westdickenberg
[
72
]
and Daneri and Savaré
[
30
]
.

Let
(\mathscr{M},g)
be any smooth, finite-dimensional Riemannian manifold.
For
x,y
in
\mathscr{M}
, the Riemannian distance
d_{g}(x,y)
between
x
and
y
is given by minimizing an action integral of paths
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathscr{M}
running from
x
to
y
:

d_{g}^{2}(x,y)=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}(s)\|^{2}_{g(\gamma(s))}\;\mathrm{d}s\ :\ \gamma(0)=x,\ \gamma(1)=y\right\}\ ,

where

\|\dot{\gamma}(s)\|^{2}_{g(\gamma(s))}=g_{\gamma(s)}(\dot{\gamma}(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))\ .

(If the infimum is achieved, any minimizer
\gamma
will be a geodesic.)
If
F
is a smooth function on
\mathscr{M}
,
let
{\rm grad}_{g}F
denote its Riemannian gradient. Consider the semigroup
S_{t}

of transformations on
\mathscr{M}

given by solving
\dot{\gamma}(t)=-{\rm grad}_{g}F(\gamma(t))
; we assume that nice global solutions exist, which will be the case in our application. The semigroup
S_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, is gradient flow for
F
.

For
\lambda\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
, the function
F
is
\lambda
-convex in case whenever
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathscr{M}
is a distance minimizing geodesic, then for all
s\in(0,1)
,

\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}s^{2}}F(\gamma(s))\geq\lambda g(\dot{\gamma}(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))\ .

It is a standard result that whenever
F
is
\lambda
-convex, the gradient flow for
F
is
\lambda
-contracting in the sense that for all
x,y\in\mathscr{M}
and
t>0
,

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}d_{g}^{2}(S_{t}(x),S_{t}(y))\leq-2\lambda d_{g}^{2}(S_{t}(x),S_{t}(y))\ .

We apply these ideas here using an approach to geodesic convexity developed by Otto and Westdickenberg
[
72
]
.
Let
\{\gamma(s)\}_{s\in[0,1]}
be any smooth path in
\mathscr{M}
with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(1)=y
.
They use the gradient flow transformation
S_{t}
to define a one-parameter family of paths
\gamma^{t}:[0,1]\to\mathscr{M}
,
t\geq 0
defined by

\gamma^{t}(s)=S_{t}\gamma(s)\ .

Since
\gamma^{t}
is admissible for the variational problem that defines
d_{g}(S_{t}(x),S_{t}(y))
, it is immediate that for each
t\geq 0
,

d_{g}^{2}(S_{t}(x),S_{t}(y))\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\gamma^{t}(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\gamma^{t}(s))}\;\mathrm{d}s\ .

(3.1)

In the present smooth setting it is shown in
[
30
, (2.8) – (2.11)]
that if for all smooth curves

\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathscr{M}
,

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\bigg|_{0+}\left(\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\gamma^{t}(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\gamma^{t}(s))}\right)\leq-2\lambda\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\gamma^{0}(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\gamma^{0}(s))}\ ,

(3.2)

for all
s\in(0,1)
, then
F
is geodesically
\lambda
-convex.

To see the connection between (
3.2
) and the contraction property, suppose that
x
and
y
are connected by a minimal geodesic
\gamma
so that

d_{g}^{2}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\gamma(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\gamma(s))}\;\mathrm{d}s\ .

(If
x
and
y
are sufficiently close, this is the case.)
Then (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) combine to yield

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\bigg|_{0+}d_{g}^{2}(S_{t}(x),S_{t}(y))\leq-2\lambda d_{g}^{2}(x,y)\ ,

and then, provided that
S_{t}(x)
and
S_{t}(y)
continue to be connected by a minimal geodesic for all
t
, the semigroup property of
S_{t}
yields the exponential
\lambda
-contractivity of the flow:

d_{g}(S_{t}(x),S_{t}(y))\leq e^{-\lambda t}d_{g}(x,y)\ .

(3.3)

The local argument in
[
30
]
proves the geodesic
\lambda
-convexity of
F
when (
3.2
) is valid for all smooth paths in
\mathscr{M}
, and thus leads to (
3.3
) without any assumptions of geodesic completeness.

When
F
is
\lambda
-convex for some
\lambda>0
, so that (
3.2
) and hence also (
3.3
) are satisfied, then,
F
has at most one fixed point
x_{0}
in
\mathscr{M}
, which is necessarily a strict minimizer of
F
on
\mathscr{M}
.
Subtract a constant to obtain
F(x_{0})=0
. Then under the geodesic
\lambda
-convexity of
F
, one has

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}F(S_{t}(x))\leq-2\lambda F(S_{t}(x))\ ,

(3.4)

and this
F(S_{t}(x))
decreases exponentially fast to its minimum value.

There is useful direct route from (
3.2
) to (
3.4
).
If
\gamma(t)
is given by the gradient flow of
F
through
\gamma(t)=S_{t}(x)
, then

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}F(\gamma(t))=-\|{\rm grad}_{g}F(\gamma(t))\|^{2}_{g(\gamma(t))}\ .

(3.5)

Define the
energy function

E
associated to
F
by

E(x)=\|{\rm grad}_{g}F(x)\|^{2}_{g(x)}\ .

(3.6)

Then (
3.2
) applied with
\gamma^{t}(s)=S_{s+t}(x)

together with the semigroup property yields

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}E(S_{t}(x))\leq-2\lambda E(S_{t}(x))\ .

Hence (
3.2
) leads directly to

E(S_{t}(x))\leq e^{-2\lambda t}E(x)\ .

(3.7)

Then, as in the original work of Bakry and Emery, (
3.7
) together with (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) yield

F(x)-F(S_{T}(x))=\int_{0}^{T}E(S_{t}(x)){\rm d}t\leq\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2\lambda t}E(x){\rm d}t\ .

Then taking
T\to\infty
yields

F(x)\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda}E(x)\ .

(3.8)

In our setting, when
F
is a relative entropy function, (
3.8
) will be a

generalized logarithmic Sobolev inequality
.
Form here one readily deduces

F(S_{t}(x))\leq e^{-2\lambda t}F(x)\ .

(3.9)

Thus, to prove geodesic convexity of
F
, and what is more directly significant for our applications, the inequalities (
3.3
) and (
3.9
), it suffices to prove (
3.2
), which is a

first derivative estimate
. In contrast, a proof of
\lambda
-convexity of
F
by direct calculation of the Hessian of
F
followed by an estimate of its least eigenvalue requires the computation of second covariant derivatives which can be difficult depending on the metric. Otto and Westdickenberg developed their approach having in mind applications to an infinite-dimensional setting (such as that of
[
71
]
) in which second covariant derivatives are difficult to work with rigorously. It turns out
that their approach is also quite fruitful in our finite-dimensional setting.

3.2
Connection with the Brenier-Benamou Formula

The Brenier-Benamou formula
[
8
]
for the
2
-Kantorovich distance between two probability densities
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
is:

\displaystyle W_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\int|\nabla\psi_{t}(x)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\;\mathrm{d}t\ :\ \partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\dive(\rho_{t}\nabla\psi_{t})=0\ ,\rho_{t}|_{t=0,1}=\rho_{0,1}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\int\frac{|{\bf p}_{t}(x)|^{2}}{\rho_{t}(x)}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}t\ :\ \partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\dive{\bf p}_{t}=0\ ,\rho_{t}|_{t=0,1}=\rho_{0,1}\bigg\}\ .

The second expression for
W_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}
is the Brenier-Benamou formula. The first expression is used in their work to make a connection with the Wasserstein distance. The equality of the two formulas is a consequence of a minimizing property of gradients: Among all time dependent vector fields
{\bf p}_{t}(x)
such that

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\dive(\rho_{t}{\bf p}_{t})=0\ ,

(3.10)

the one that minimizes
{\displaystyle\int|\nabla\psi_{t}(x)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)}
is a gradient; i.e., it is of the form
{\bf p}_{t}=\nabla\psi_{t}
. To prove this, one simply needs to show that the set of
{\bf p}_{t}
satisfying (
3.10
) is non-empty, and then by convexity there is a unique minimizer. Since the difference between any two such
{\bf p}_{t}
has zero divergence, a simple integration by parts in the Euler-Lagrange equation provides the proof.
Therefore, in the second minimization formula, in searching for minimizers,
{\bf p}_{t}
, we may restrict our attention to those of the from
{\bf p}_{t}=\rho\nabla\psi
.

It is useful to compare this with our metrics on
{\mathfrak{S}}
that are determined by a QMS generator
\mathscr{L}
satisfying the detailed balance condition.
In our case, for a smooth curve
\rho(t)
in
{\mathfrak{S}}
, we have from Theorem
2.19
that

g_{\rho}(\dot{\rho},\dot{\rho})=\langle\dot{\rho},\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{-1}\dot{\rho}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}=\langle(\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{-1}\dot{\rho}),\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{-1}\dot{\rho})\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\ .

That is, writing

\dot{\rho}(t)=:\mathcal{K}_{\rho(t)}B(t)
,
g_{\rho}(\dot{\rho},\dot{\rho})=\langle B,\mathcal{K}_{\rho}B\rangle
.

Therefore

\displaystyle d_{g}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=

\displaystyle\inf\bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\langle{B_{t},\mathcal{K}_{\rho_{t}}B_{t}}\rangle\;\mathrm{d}t\ :\ \dot{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{K}_{\rho_{t}}B_{t}\ ,\rho_{t}|_{t=0}=\rho_{0}\ ,\rho_{t}|_{t=1}=\rho_{1}\bigg\}\ .

Recall that
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(B)=-\dive({\mathbb{M}}_{\rho}\nabla B)
where
{\mathbb{M}}_{\rho}
is defined in (
2.26
).
Making the change of variables,

\widehat{{\bf A}}_{t}:=\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}\nabla B_{t}\ ,

(3.11)

we have from
\dot{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{K}_{\rho_{t}}B_{t}
and the definition (
2.24
) of the metric
g_{\rho}
,

g_{\rho}(\dot{\rho},\dot{\rho})=\langle B_{t},\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(B_{t})\rangle=\langle\widehat{{\bf A}}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}^{-1}\widehat{{\bf A}}_{t}\rangle\ .

In complete analogy with the classical case, if we minimize
\langle{\bf A}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}{\bf A}_{t}\rangle
over all
{\bf A}_{t}
satisfying
\dot{\rho}_{t}+\dive{\bf A}_{t}=0
, the minimizer
{\bf A}_{t}
has the form specified in (
3.11
); that is, it is the gradient of some
B
as shown in
[
17
]
. The proof is essentially the same as in the classical case, if anything, it is even simpler in our finite dimensional setting:

3.1 LEMMA
.

g_{\rho}(\dot{\rho},\dot{\rho})=\min\left\{\ \langle{\bf A}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}^{-1}{\bf A}_{t}\rangle\ :\ \dot{\rho}(t)+\dive({\bf A}_{t})=0\ \right\}.

Proof.

Let
{\bf A}_{t}
be such that
\dot{\rho}=-\dive({\bf A}_{t})
, and let
{\bf X}_{t}:={\bf A}_{t}-\widehat{{\bf A}}_{t}
. Since
\dot{\rho}(t)=-\dive(\widehat{{\bf A}}_{t})
,
\dive({\bf X}_{t})=0
.
Then

\displaystyle\langle{\bf A}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}^{-1}{\bf A}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{{\bf A}}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}^{-1}\widehat{{\bf A}}_{t}\rangle+\langle{\bf X}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}^{-1}{\bf X}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\Re\langle{\bf X}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}^{-1}{\widehat{\bf A}}_{t}\rangle\ .

However, by (
3.11
),

\langle{\bf X}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}^{-1}{\widehat{\bf A}}_{t}\rangle=\langle{\bf X}_{t},\nabla B\rangle=\langle\dive({\bf X}_{t}),B\rangle=0\ ,

and
\langle{\bf X}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}^{-1}{\bf X}_{t}\rangle\geq 0
with equality if and only if
{\bf A}_{t}=\widehat{\bf A}_{t}
.
∎

Therefore we obtain the following variational formula for our metric:

\displaystyle d_{g}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=

\displaystyle\inf\bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\langle{{\bf A}_{t},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(t)}^{-1}{\bf A}_{t}}\rangle\;\mathrm{d}t\ :\ \dot{\rho}_{t}+\dive{\bf A}_{t}=0\ ,\rho_{t}|_{t=0}=\rho_{0}\ ,\rho_{t}|_{t=1}=\rho_{1}\bigg\}

This is the direct analog of the Brenier-Benamou formula,
it gives us the justification for referring to the metrics on
{\mathfrak{S}}
that we are using as non-commutative mass transport metrics. More important, just as in the classical setting, the
“relaxed” form of the constraint
\dot{\rho}_{t}+\dive{\bf A}_{t}=0
is very helpful in applications.

3.3
Geodesic convexity using intertwining relations

In this section, let
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}
, and let
\{\mathscr{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}=\{e^{t\mathscr{L}}\}_{t\geq 0}
be an ergodic QMS that satisfies the
detailed balance condition with respect to
\sigma
.
Let
\mathscr{L}
be given in the standard form specified in Theorem
2.13
so that the data specifying

\mathscr{L}
are the sets
\{V_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
and
\{\omega_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
.
Let
\nabla:{\mathfrak{H}}\to{\mathfrak{H}}_{\mathcal{J}}
and
\dive:{\mathfrak{H}}_{\mathcal{J}}\to{\mathfrak{H}}
be the associated non-commutative gradient and divergence (as opposed to the associated Riemannian gradient and divergence).

Let
\rho:[0,1]\to{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
be a smooth path in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
, and define the one-parameter family of paths,
\rho^{t}(s)
,
(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,\infty)
by

\rho^{t}(s)=\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)\ .

By what has been explained at the beginning of this lecture, if we can prove that

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\bigg|_{0+}\left(\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\rho^{t}(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\rho^{t}(s))}\right)\leq-2\lambda\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\rho^{0}(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\rho^{0}(s))}

(3.12)

for all smooth
\rho:[0,1]\to\mathscr{M}
and all
s\in(0,1)
, we will have
proved the geodesic convexity of the relative entropy functional, and
consequently, we shall have proved

D(\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho||\sigma)\leq e^{-2\lambda t}D(\rho||\sigma)\ .

Note that (
3.12
) is equivalent to proving

\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\rho^{t}(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\rho^{t}(s))}\leq e^{-2\lambda t}\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\rho^{0}(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\rho^{0}(s))}

(3.13)

By Lemma
3.1
, for any
{\bf C}_{s}
such that

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\rho^{t}(s)+{\bf C}_{s}=0\ ,

\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\rho^{t}(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\rho^{t}(s))}\leq\langle{\bf C}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\rho^{t}(s)}^{-1}{\bf C}_{s}\rangle\ .

On the other hand, if we define
\widehat{{\bf A}}_{s}:=\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}\nabla B_{s}
where
B_{s}:=\mathcal{K}_{\rho}(s)^{-1}\dot{\rho}(s)
, then again by Lemma
3.1
,

\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\rho^{0}(s)\right\|^{2}_{g(\rho^{0}(s))}=\langle\widehat{{\bf A}}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}^{-1}\widehat{{\bf A}}_{s}\rangle\ .

There is a natural choice for
{\bf C}_{s}
: Since
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\rho^{0}(s)=-\dive(\widehat{{\bf A}}_{s})
, applying
\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}
to both sides yields

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\rho^{t}(s)=\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\left(\dive(\widehat{{\bf A}}_{s})\right)\ .

We now construct an “intertwining operator”
\mathscr{Q}_{t}
on
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\mathcal{J}}
such that

\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ\dive=\dive\circ\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}\ .

With this in hand, we may take
{\bf C}_{s}:=\dive\left(\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{{\bf A}}_{s}\right)
. Then, whenever we can prove that

\langle\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s}\rangle\leq e^{-2\lambda t}\langle\widehat{{\bf A}}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}^{-1}\widehat{{\bf A}}_{s}\rangle\ ,

(3.14)

we shall have proved (
3.13
). We shall be able to do this in a number of interesting cases. First, we construct the map
\mathscr{Q}_{t}
.

3.2 LEMMA
.

For
t>0
, define the linear transformation
\mathscr{Q}_{t}:{\mathfrak{G}}_{\mathcal{J}}\to{\mathfrak{G}}_{\mathcal{J}}
by

\mathscr{Q}_{t}:=\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}\circ\mathscr{L}_{0}^{+}\circ\dive

(3.15)

where
\mathscr{L}_{0}^{+}
is the generalized inverse of the map
\mathscr{L}_{0}
defined in (
2.25
).
Then for all traceless
A
,

\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}A=\mathscr{Q}_{t}\nabla A\ ,

(3.16)

and consequently

\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ\dive=\dive\circ\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}\

as operators on
{\mathfrak{H}}_{\mathcal{J}}
. Furthermore, for all
s,t>0
,

\mathscr{Q}_{s}\mathscr{Q}_{t}=\mathscr{Q}_{s+t}\ .

(3.17)

Proof.

A simple computation verifies (
3.16
). Then for any
{\bf C}\in{\mathfrak{H}}_{\mathcal{J}}
,

\langle{\bf C},\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}A\rangle_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{\mathcal{J}}}=\langle{\bf C},\mathscr{Q}_{t}\nabla A\rangle_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{\mathcal{J}}}=\langle\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}{\bf C},\nabla A\rangle_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{\mathcal{J}}}\ .

Integration by parts yields

\langle\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}(\dive{\bf C}),A\rangle_{{\mathfrak{H}}}=\langle\dive(\mathscr{Q}_{t}({\bf C}),A\rangle

for all traceless
A
, and then since divergences are traceless and
\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}
is trace preserving, the restriction on the trace of
A
is superfluous.
Finally, by the definition

\mathscr{Q}_{s}\mathscr{Q}_{t}=\nabla\mathscr{P}_{s}\circ\mathscr{L}_{0}^{+}\mathscr{L}_{0}\mathscr{P}_{t}\circ\mathscr{L}_{0}^{+}\circ\dive=\nabla\mathscr{P}_{s+t}\circ\mathscr{L}_{0}^{+}\circ\dive=\mathscr{Q}_{s+t}\

since
\mathscr{P}_{t}
preserves the kernel of
\mathscr{L}_{0}
, and this proves (
3.17
).
∎

3.3 DEFINITION
.

The inequality

\langle\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}{\bf X},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}{\bf X}\rangle\leq e^{-2\lambda t}\langle{{\bf X}},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}^{-1}{{\bf X}}\rangle\ ,

(3.18)

when it holds for some
\lambda\geq 0
, and all
{\bf X}\in{\mathfrak{H}}
, is called the
action dissipation inequality
.

Evidently when (
3.18
) is valid for general
{\bf X}
, the specific case (
3.14
) is valid.
We now present a simple sufficient condition for (
3.18
) that can
be able verified in a number of interesting examples, as was done in
[
17
]
.

In some case,
\mathscr{Q}_{t}
takes a particularly simple form such that (
3.14
) is evidently satisfied.

3.4 THEOREM
.

Suppose that for some numbers
a_{j}
,
j\in\mathcal{J}
,

\displaystyle[\partial_{j},\mathscr{L}]=-a_{j}\partial_{j}\

(3.19)

for each
j\in\mathcal{J}
. Then with
\mathscr{Q}_{t}:{\mathfrak{G}}_{\mathcal{J}}\to{\mathfrak{G}}_{\mathcal{J}}
defined by (
3.15
),

\mathscr{Q}_{t}(A_{1},\dots,A_{|\mathcal{J}|})=(e^{-ta_{1}}\mathscr{P}_{t}A_{1},\dots,e^{-ta_{|\mathcal{J}|}}\mathscr{P}_{t}A_{|\mathcal{J}|})\ .

(3.20)

Then with
\lambda:=\min\{\ a_{j}\ :\ j\in\mathcal{J}\ \}
, the action dissipation inequality (
3.18
) is satisfied.

Proof.

Let
A\in{\mathfrak{H}}
,
{\rm Tr}[A]=0
, and define
A_{j}(t)=\partial_{j}\mathscr{P}_{t}A
. Then
A_{j}(0)=\partial_{j}A_{j}
and

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}A_{j}(t)=\partial_{j}\mathscr{L}\mathscr{P}_{t}A=\mathscr{L}\partial_{j}\mathscr{P}_{t}A-a_{j}\partial_{j}\mathscr{P}_{t}A=[\mathscr{L}-a_{j}{\mathds{1}}]A_{j}(t)\ .

It follows that
t\mapsto e^{ta_{j}}A_{j}(t)
is the unique solution of

{\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}X(t)=\mathscr{L}X(t)}
with

X(0)=\partial_{j}A
, which is of course
\mathscr{P}_{t}\partial_{j}A
. Therefore,

\partial_{j}\mathscr{P}_{t}A=e^{-ta_{j}}\mathscr{P}_{t}\partial_{j}A
, and this proves that

\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}A=(e^{-ta_{1}}\mathscr{P}_{t}\partial_{1}A,\dots,e^{-ta_{|\mathcal{J}|}}\mathscr{P}_{t}\partial_{|\mathcal{J}|}A)\ .

(3.21)

Then since
{\rm Tr}[A]=0
,
\mathscr{L}_{0}^{+}\circ\dive(\nabla A)=A
, and then (
3.21
) yields

\mathscr{Q}_{t}\nabla A=\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}\mathscr{L}_{0}^{+}\circ\dive(\nabla A)=(e^{-ta_{1}}\mathscr{P}_{t}\partial_{1}A,\dots,e^{-ta_{|\mathcal{J}|}}\mathscr{P}_{t}\partial_{|\mathcal{J}|}A)\ ,

and this proves (
3.20
). This in turn yields

\langle\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s}\rangle=e^{-2\lambda t}\langle\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s}\rangle\ ,

and then by Theorem
1.4

\langle\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s}\rangle\leq\langle\widehat{\bf A}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}^{-1}\widehat{\bf A}_{s}\rangle

Combining the last two inequalities yields (
3.18
) .
∎

It was shown in
[
17
]
that for both the Fermi and Bose Quantum Mehler Semgroups one has that

\mathscr{Q}_{t}{\bf A}=(e^{-\lambda t}\mathscr{P}_{t}A_{1},\dots,e^{-\lambda t}\mathscr{P}_{t}A_{|\mathcal{J}|})\ .

(3.22)

for some
\lambda>0
depending on the “temperature” parameter in the semigroup, and on whether the semigroup is the Fermi or Bose version. See
[
17
]
for details. Here we simply note that whenever (
3.22
) is satisfied,

\langle\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s}\rangle=e^{-2\lambda t}\langle\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s}\rangle\ ,

and then by Theorem
1.4

\langle\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\widehat{\bf A}_{s}\rangle\leq\langle\widehat{\bf A}_{s},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}^{-1}\widehat{\bf A}_{s}\rangle

Therefore,
whenever (
3.22
) is valid, so is (
3.14
) and hence (
3.13
).
Altogether we have proved:

3.5 THEOREM
.

Let
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{+}
, and let
\mathscr{P}_{t}=e^{t\mathscr{L}}
be an ergodic QMS that satisfies the detailed balance condition with respect to
\sigma
.
Let
\nabla
and
\dive
denote the associated non-commutative gradient and divergence.
Suppose that for some
\lambda>0
, the action dissipation inequality (
3.18
) is satisfied.

Then the relative entropy with respect to
\sigma
is geodesicaly
\lambda
-convex for the Riemannian metric
g_{\rho}
determined by
\mathscr{L}
.
Moreover, the exponential convergence estimate

\displaystyle D(\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho||\sigma)\leq e^{-2\lambda t}D(\rho||\sigma)

holds, as well as the generalized logarithmic Sobolev inequality

\displaystyle D(\rho||\sigma)\leq-\frac{1}{2\lambda}{\rm Tr}\big[\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}(\rho)\big(\log\rho-\log\sigma\big)\big]\ .

(3.23)

In
[
17
]
, Theorem
3.5
was shown to apply
for both the Fermi and Bose Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups
\mathscr{P}_{t}
, with the action dissipation inequality being proved using Theorem
3.4
.
Actually, in these cases, there is a
Mehler type formula for

\mathscr{P}_{t}
from which the intertwining can be readily checked, as shown in
[
17
]
. In the Fermi case,
this can be found in formulas (4.1) and (4.2) of
[
14
]
, and the formula in the Bose case is a simple adaptation of this.

3.4
Dual from of the action dissipation inequality

There is a dual form of the action dissipation inequality (
3.18
),

\langle\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}{\bf X},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}{\bf X}\rangle\leq e^{-2\lambda t}\langle{{\bf X}},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}^{-1}{{\bf X}}\rangle\ ,

that is particularly useful in applications.

Suppose (
3.18
) is valid. Then for arbitrary
{\bf Y}\in M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla Y,\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}{\bf X}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}{\bf X},\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}^{-1}\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}{\bf X}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\langle\nabla Y,\mathscr{Q}_{t}^{\dagger}{\bf X}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\lambda t}\langle{{\bf X}},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}^{-1}{{\bf X}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{Q}_{t}\nabla Y,{\bf X}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\lambda t}\langle{{\bf X}},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}^{-1}{{\bf X}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}Y,{\bf X}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\lambda t}\langle{{\bf X}},\mathbb{M}_{\rho(s)}^{-1}{{\bf X}}\rangle\ .

Taking the supremum over
{\bf X}
, so that we are taking the Legendre transforms of two quadratic functions we obtain

\langle\nabla Y,\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}\nabla Y\rangle\geq e^{2\lambda t}\langle\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}Y,\mathbb{M}_{\rho}\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}Y\rangle\ .

(3.24)

Since the Legendre transform is involutive, a standard argument shows that (
3.24
) is actually equivalent to the action dissipation inequality (
3.18
).

The inequality (
3.24
) is called the
gradient estimate
by Wirth and Zhang
[
87
]
. Actually it is the “dimension free” case
GE(\lambda,\infty)
of a family of inequalities
GE(\lambda,N)
depending on “curvature” and “dimension”. They show, using a calculation of the Hessian of the relative entropy from
[
18
]
that (
3.24
) holds if and only if the relative entropy is
\lambda
geodesically convex. While the line of argument presented here may suggest that
that the entropy dissipation inequality is only a sufficient condition for
\lambda
-geodesic convexity of the relative entropy, it is actually necessary as well. This was proved in
[
18
,
31
]
. The equivalence by duality of (
3.14
) and (
3.24
) somewhat simplifies the proof that either condition is equivalent to geodesic convexity of the relative entropy. However, the main point to make as the end of these lectures approaches is that there is much additional information in the papers
[
18
,
31
,
87
]
.

Wirth and Zhang also show using concavity of operator means that whenever the semigroup
\mathscr{P}_{t}
satisfies the intertwining relation (
3.22
), then the gradient estimate (
3.24
) is satisfied. We gave a simple proof using the inequalities considered here: Assuming (
3.22
),

e^{2\lambda t}\langle\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}Y,\mathbb{M}_{\rho}\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}Y\rangle=\langle\mathscr{P}_{t}\nabla Y,\mathbb{M}_{\rho}\mathscr{P}_{t}\nabla Y\rangle

and then by Theorem
1.2
, Uhlmann’s monotonicity version of the Lieb Concavity Theorem,

\langle\mathscr{P}_{t}\nabla Y,\mathbb{M}_{\rho}\mathscr{P}_{t}\nabla Y\rangle\leq\langle\nabla Y,\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}\nabla Y\rangle\ .

3.5
Another route to the action dissipation inequality

Theorem
3.4
is not the only route to proving the action dissipation inequality. We close with a simple example due to Wirth and Zhang
[
87
]
. The
completely depolarizing channel
is the QMS on
M_{n}({\mathord{\mathbb{C}}})
given by

\mathscr{L}(X)=\frac{1}{n}{\rm Tr}[X]{\mathds{1}}-X\ .

(3.25)

Since
\Phi(X):=\frac{1}{n}{\rm Tr}[X]{\mathds{1}}
defined a completely positive unital and trace preserving map,
\mathscr{P}_{t}=e^{t\mathscr{L}}
is a QMS satisfying the detailed balance condition with respect to
\tau
, the normalized trace.

Using (
3.25
) in the right side of (
3.23
) with
\sigma=\tau
, we obtain

-{\rm Tr}\big[\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}(\rho)\big(\log\rho-\log\tau\big)\big]=-{\rm Tr}[(\tau-\rho)\big(\log\rho-\log\tau\big)\big]=D(\rho||\tau)+D(\tau||\rho)\ ,

as noted in
[
67
]
. Since
D(\tau||\rho)\geq 0
, we have the generalized logarithmic Sobolev inequality

D(\rho||\tau)\leq-{\rm Tr}\big[\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}(\rho)\big(\log\rho-\log\tau\big)\big]\ .

(3.26)

This inequality turns out to be the best possible inequality that holds
independent of the dimension
:
In high dimension there exist
\rho\neq\tau
such that
D(\tau||\rho)
is an arbitrarily small multiple of
D(\rho||\tau)
;
[
67
]
.

It is easy to write the generator explicitly in terms of a set
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{2n-1}\}
of operators defining that various partial derivatives, but we shall not need this. Also now that since for
\tau
, the modular operator is the identity, all of the Bohr frequencies
\omega
are zero. Hence
\mathbb{M}_{\rho}
simplifies to

\mathbb{M}_{\rho}{\bf A}=\int_{0}^{1}\rho^{1-s}{\bf A}\rho^{s}{\rm d}s\ .

It is also easy to write down a formula for
\mathscr{P}_{t}
in this simple case:

\mathscr{P}_{t}A=e^{-t}A+(1-e^{-t})\frac{1}{n}{\rm Tr}[A]{\mathds{1}}\ .

Since
\nabla{\mathds{1}}=0
, and since each component of
\nabla A
is traceless,

\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}A=e^{-t}\nabla A=\mathscr{P}_{t}\nabla A\ .

(3.27)

Equivalntly, for each
j
,

[\partial_{j},\mathscr{L}]=0

so that (
3.20
) is satisfied, but for
a_{j}=0
. Hence Theorem
3.4
only tells us that the action dissipation inequality (
3.24
) is satisfied
for
\lambda=0
, which is useless.

However,
as Wirth and Zhang showed,
one can still establish a non-trivial form of (
3.24
): First observe that since
\Phi
, and hence each
\mathscr{P}_{t}
is self adjoint, so that
\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}=\mathscr{P}_{t}
,

\langle\nabla Y,\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}\nabla Y\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}{\rm Tr}[(\mathscr{P}_{t})^{s}\rho(\nabla Y)^{*}(\mathscr{P}_{t})^{1-s}\rho(\nabla Y)]{\rm d}s

Then by the Lieb Concavity Theorem, for each
s

{\rm Tr}[(\mathscr{P}_{t}\rho)^{s}(\nabla Y)^{*}(\mathscr{P}_{t}\rho)^{1-s}(\nabla Y)]\geq e^{-t}{\rm Tr}[\rho^{s}(\nabla Y)^{*}\rho^{1-s}(\nabla Y)]\ ,

where we have simply discarded the positive term multiplying
(1-e^{-t})
. Therefore,

\langle\nabla Y,\mathbb{M}_{\mathscr{P}_{t}^{\dagger}\rho(s)}\nabla Y\rangle\geq e^{-t}\langle\nabla Y,\mathbb{M}_{\rho}\nabla Y\rangle\ .

However, by (
3.27
),

e^{-t}\langle\nabla Y,\mathbb{M}_{\rho}\nabla Y\rangle=e^{t}\langle e^{-t}\nabla Y,\mathbb{M}_{\rho}e^{-t}\nabla Y\rangle=e^{t}\langle\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}Y,\mathbb{M}_{\rho}\nabla\mathscr{P}_{t}Y\rangle\ .

Therefore, (
3.24
) is satisfied with
\lambda=1/2
. Then by Theorem
3.5
, we recover (
3.26
). For the dimension dependent best constant in the entropy production inequality for the depolarizing channel, see
[
67
]
. The simple treatment
here does not yield the best constant in low dimensions, but using the curvature-dimension conditions investigated in
[
86
,
87
]
, one can do better. It remain to be seen if the method presented here can be adapted to treat other problems such as those considered in
[
9
]
.
