Ordinary Differential Equation Models and their Computation Methods
Jaeyong Lee

Abstract
In this article, I introduce the differential equation model and review their frequentist and Bayesian computation methods. A numerical example of the FitzHugh-Nagumo model is given.



1
Introduction

I spent a Sabbatical year in the Statistical and Applied Mathematical Sciences Institute in 2010. During the Sabbatical year, I attended a talk given by James Ramsay. He gave a talk on the differential equation model and an example of modeling underground water levels in some place in Canada. The water level data can be seen as a function of time and its plot was not smooth at all. It was wiggly and has big jumps. The water level was mostly affected by the rainfall, but with some time lags.
He showed the fitted curves and predictions. I was very surprised that his fitted curve as well as the predictions follow the observed data very closely. I would not be surprised by the fitted curve following the data closely if the model has thousands of parameters, but his estimate follows the data with just a handful of parameters. At the talk I imagined myself analyzing the same data set without the differential equation model. I thought about many complicated nonparametric models, but I could not think of a statistical model which would resemble the fitted curve he produce on his slide. This is how I got drawn to the topic of the differential equation model.

The differential equation is a primary mathematical model that describes dynamical systems which are first developed by Henri Poincaré to explain celestial bodies. Differential equations are widely used in many different areas such as the epidemic model, climate model, economic model, and chemical engineering, to name just a few. The statistics community however largely neglected the differential equation modeling until recently.

The differential equation model is a statistical model that consists of two equations:

observation equation:

\displaystyle\displaystyle y(t_{i})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=x(t_{i})+\epsilon(t_{i}),~i=1,2,\ldots,n,

(1)

differential equation:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dx(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=f(x(t),t;\theta),

(2)

where
\displaystyle 0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{n}<T
are time points where observations
\displaystyle y(t)
are observed and
\displaystyle\epsilon(t_{i})
’s are observational error typically assumed to follow
\displaystyle N(0,\Sigma)
with positive definite matrix
\displaystyle\Sigma
. The error variance
\displaystyle\Sigma
is assume typically a diagonal matrix
\displaystyle\Sigma=diag(\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{p}^{2})
. Here
\displaystyle y(t)
is a
\displaystyle p
-dimensional vector that are observable and
\displaystyle x(t)
is also a
\displaystyle p
-dimensional vector function that satisfies the differential equation (
2
) and it is the mean function of the regression model (
1
). The function
\displaystyle f
is smooth and uniquely determines
\displaystyle x
when the initial value of
\displaystyle x
,
\displaystyle x_{0}=x(0)
is given. Note that the differential equation model (
1
) and (
2
) is just a nonlinear regression model where the mean function of the regression model is expressed as a solution of a differential equation. It also resembles the state-space model. One can view that the differential equation model as the state-space model whose state equation is expressed as a differential equation.

Here I give an example of the differential equation model. The SIR model is the differential equation model for disease spread in a small community. It consists of differential equations for three curves, susceptible (S), infected (I), and recovered (R):

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dS(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\beta\frac{1}{N}I(t)S(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dI(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta\frac{1}{N}I(t)S(t)-\gamma I(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dR(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma I(t),

where
\displaystyle t
is time in
\displaystyle[0,T]
, for some
\displaystyle T>0
,
\displaystyle N
is the total population of the community, and
\displaystyle S(t)
,
\displaystyle I(t)
and
\displaystyle R(t)
are
the numbers of the susceptibles, the infected and the recovered at time
\displaystyle t
, respectively. There are three equations in the model, but fundamental quantities are two terms. One is
\displaystyle\beta\frac{1}{N}I(t)S(t)
which can be interpreted as the number of infected people in a unit time. The parameter
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}=\beta/N
is the probability that one infected and one susceptible meet and the susceptible gets infected during a unit time and
\displaystyle\beta
is the number of people in the population or among
\displaystyle N
susceptibles who get infected by one infected during a unit time. Some authors prefer
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}
and use

\frac{dS(t)}{dt}=-\beta^{\prime}I(t)S(t).

Another fundamental quantity is
\displaystyle\gamma I(t)
which is the number of recovered during a unit time. The parameter
\displaystyle\gamma
is the recovery rate of the infected during a unit time. For the SIR model, we assume the population size
\displaystyle N
remains constant over the time period
\displaystyle[0,T]
and

S(t)+I(t)+R(t)=N,~\forall t.

Thus, essentially we need only two equations.

In the differential equation model, we assume that we do not observe
\displaystyle S(t)
,
\displaystyle I(t)
and
\displaystyle R(t)
directly, but observe them with errors, i.e. we observe
\displaystyle S^{*}(t_{i})
and
\displaystyle I^{*}(t_{i})
at
\displaystyle 0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{n}<T
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle S^{*}(t_{i})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle S(t_{i})+\epsilon_{1}(t_{i}),

\displaystyle\displaystyle I^{*}(t_{i})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(t_{i})+\epsilon_{2}(t_{i}),

where
\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}(t)
and
\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}(t)
are errors.

Frequentist Methods

2.1
Explicit Numerical Integration of Differential Equation

The parameter of the differential equation is estimated with the least squares method, i.e.,

\hat{\theta}:=argmin_{\theta}||y_{i}-x(t_{i})||^{2}.

In the course of the optimization, whenever a value of
\displaystyle x(t)
is required, a numerical solver is invoked.

This method performs well when the sample size and the number of parameters are small. But its computation can be prohibitive when the model gets larger. When the numerical solver is the 4th order Runge-Kutta algorithm, the estimator is asymptotically efficient (Xue et al. 2010, Bhaumik and Ghosal 2017).

Two-step methods

?

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=(y(t_{1}),\ldots,y(t_{n}))

\hat{\theta}:=argmin_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}||\frac{d\hat{x}(t_{i})}{dt}-f(\hat{x}(t_{i}),t_{i},\theta)||^{2}.

(3)

There are variations in the first and the second steps with different nonparametric function estimation methods and different norms. This method is not asymptotically efficient but computationally fast.

Bayesian Methods

In this section, the Bayesian estimation methods for the differential equation model are reviewed.
There are three groups of parameters in the differential equation model. Two are obvious and one is not. The two groups of obvious parameters are the parameter in the differential equation (
2
),
\displaystyle\theta
, and the observational error variance
\displaystyle\Sigma
. Under some smoothness conditions, the differential equation together with initial value of
\displaystyle x
,
\displaystyle x_{0}:=x(0)
, uniquely determine
\displaystyle x
. Thus, one needs to include the initial value of
\displaystyle x
,
\displaystyle x_{0}:=x(0)
, which is not so obvious at a glance. To complete the Bayesian model, we need to put a prior on
\displaystyle\theta,\Sigma
and
\displaystyle x_{0}
. We will denote the prior

(\theta,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2},x_{0})\sim\pi(\theta)\pi(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\pi(x_{0}).

3.1
Bayesian Method with Explicit Integration of Differential Equation

The first approach was the approach taken in

\pi(\theta,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2},x_{0}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\propto\pi(\theta,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2},x_{0})\times p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\sigma},x_{0}),

where

p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\sigma},x_{0})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}|2\pi\Sigma|^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(y_{i}-x(t_{i};\theta,x_{0}))^{\prime}\Sigma^{-1}(y_{i}-x(t_{i};\theta,x_{0}))},

\displaystyle x(t)
is denoted as
\displaystyle x(t;\theta,x_{0})
to show the dependence on
\displaystyle\theta
and
\displaystyle x_{0}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(x(t_{1}),\ldots,x(t_{n}))
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=(y(t_{1}),y(t_{2}),\ldots,y(t_{n}))
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{p})
. To compute the posterior, one can apply the Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm such as Metropolis-Hastings sampler or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. The advantage of this method is that it computes the exact posterior while other methods employ certain approximations to ease the posterior computation.
The difficulties with this approach are twofold. First, the analytic solution of the differential equation (
2

Second, the differential equation can be chaotic and a small variation in the parameter may result in completely different curves. Thus, estimation of the parameter can be very sensitive and it affects at times poor performances of the estimator.

\displaystyle x
is evaluated using mathematical formulas of the Euler method or the 4th order Runge-Kutta method. This method gives accurate parameter estimates with fast computing time with a moderate dimension sizes of
\displaystyle\theta
. But when the dimension of
\displaystyle\theta
is high, the computation can be intensive.

?

3.3
Gaussain Process Approaches

The key fact used in the Gaussian process approach is the fact that when
\displaystyle x
follows a Gaussian process,
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle\dot{x}
are jointly a Gaussian process. This contradicts the mathematical fact that when
\displaystyle x
is a function,
\displaystyle x
determines
\displaystyle\dot{x}
. Nevertheless, it gives a methodological advantage in ordinary differential equation (ODE) models.
There are two approaches in this category: adaptive gradient matching (AGM) method (Calderhead et al. 2009; Dondelinger et al. 2013)
??
and Gaussian process ODE (GPODE) method
[
wang2014gaussian
]

Suppose
\displaystyle x\sim GP(0,C_{\phi})

\displaystyle 0
and covariance function
\displaystyle C_{\phi}
with parameter
\displaystyle\phi

In the AGM method, the posterior is obtained by

\pi(\theta,\boldsymbol{\sigma},\phi|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\sigma},x_{0})\times\pi(x|\theta,\phi,\gamma)\times\pi(\theta,\phi,\gamma,\boldsymbol{\sigma}),

where
\displaystyle\pi(\theta,\phi,\gamma,\boldsymbol{\sigma})
is the prior
\displaystyle(\theta,\phi,\gamma,\boldsymbol{\sigma})
and
\displaystyle\gamma
is the additional error variance introduced to
\displaystyle\dot{x}
in (
6
).
The prior
\displaystyle\pi(x|\theta,\phi,\gamma)
is constructed by

\pi(x|\theta,\phi,\gamma)=\int\pi(x,\dot{x}|\theta,\phi,\gamma)d\dot{x}

(4)

where

\pi(x,\dot{x}|\theta,\phi,\gamma)\propto GP(x,|0,C_{\phi})GP(\dot{x}|x,\phi)\prod_{i=1}^{n}N(\dot{x}(t_{i})|f(t_{i},x(t_{i}),\theta),\gamma I).

(5)

Note that integration (
4

\displaystyle GP(\dot{x}|x,\phi)

\displaystyle x

\dot{x}(t_{i})=f(x(t_{i}),t_{i};\theta)+\nu_{i},~\nu_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}N(0,\gamma).

(6)

Note that this is not a legitimate derivation following probability laws, because the distributions of
\displaystyle\dot{x}

In the GPODE method, the posterior is obtained as

\pi(\theta,\phi,\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\propto p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f},\phi,\boldsymbol{\sigma})\times GP(x|0,C_{\phi}),

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=(f(t_{i},x(t_{i}),\theta))_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f},\phi,\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}|\dot{\mathbf{x}},\phi,\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}
replaced by
\displaystyle\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}|\dot{\mathbf{x}},\phi,\boldsymbol{\sigma})

Conveniently, this integration also can be done explicitly under the normal error assumption.

3.4
Two-Step Approaches

?

\displaystyle x

y_{j}=x(t_{j})+\epsilon(t_{j}),j=1,2,\ldots,n

\displaystyle x(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}b_{j}(t)

\displaystyle b_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\beta_{j}

\displaystyle x(t)
or
\displaystyle\beta_{j}

In the second step, one sample
\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle\theta

\theta^{*}:=argmin_{\theta}\int||\dot{x}(t)-f(x(t),\theta)||^{2}w(t)dt

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle\theta
is approximated by collecting
\displaystyle\theta^{*}

?

Two step approaches are not generally asymptotically efficient. But
?

\displaystyle\theta^{*}

\theta^{*}:=argmin_{\theta}\int||x(t)-x^{*}_{\theta}(t)||^{2}dt,

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle x^{*}_{\theta}(t)
