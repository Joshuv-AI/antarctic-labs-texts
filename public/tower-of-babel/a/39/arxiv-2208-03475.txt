Peng : A program for transport properties of low-density binary gas mixtures Journal: Computer Physics Communications
Yu Zhai, You Li, Hui Li, Frederick R. W. McCourt

Abstract
The fundamental properties of molecules bridge experiment and theory. Transport properties (diffusion, thermal diffusion, thermal conductivity and viscosity) of binary mixtures are measurable in experiments, and well-defined in theory, but difficult to compute with high accuracy. In addition to high-accuracy inter-molecular potential energy curves (PECs), a reliable and high-order solution program that compute the properties based on the PECs is required. In this work, we present a computer program called Peng that performs the collision integration numerically, and solves the Boltzmann equation in Chapman–Enskog fashion. The program has been devised to perform both parts of the solution procedure to arbitrary order, so that no hard-coded limitation will prevent a user from computing at higher precision, except the amount of RAM and the required computational time. Peng is well-designed in an Object-Oriented Programming (OOP) fashion, which make the program clear and easy to modify. In addition to the end-user oriented program, Peng is also compiled as a dynamic shared library that may readily be extended and embedded in users’ programs.

Nature of problem (approx. 50-250 words):

Nowadays, quantum chemistry provides high-accuracy intermolecular
interactions potential energy curves (PECs),
and more and more accurate thermophysical properties of dilute gases
can be measured experimentally.
It is meaningful to build a bridge between thermophysical properties
and PECs, so that people can refine the PECs and learn more about the nature
of dynamics of gases.
For a dilute binary gas mixture,
the theory, the Boltzmann equation,
and its solution has been available for a long time,
but fewer numerical solution packages are available for the public.
An easy-to-use and easy-to-extend software package is required.

Solution method (approx. 50-250 words):

To complete the work,
we have created a program that computes the collision integrals (
\Omega^{(\ell,s)}
)
for a set of temperatures, following which
the thermophysical properties are computed using the Chapman–Enskog solutions
of the Boltzmann equation.
Both parts of the program are written in C++.
The program is easy to use,
and a well-designed framework is provided thanks to the Object-oriented design.
Users with programming experience can readily extend the present work.

Additional comments including restrictions and unusual features (approx. 50-250 words):

In this version, the program is limited
to the computation of the properties of mixtures of structureless atoms.
For polyatomic molecules, additional work need to be done.

There has been some

(1) The programs were composed in an old-fashioned style,

Of course, the drawbacks of the old programs are caused by historical reasons:

Lack of objected oriented program (OOP) paradigm in computational physics,

What makes it worse is that

Thus, it can serve as a platform for comparing different algorithms.

the central components are the collision integrals
\Omega_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell,s)}
,
which are determined by classical collisions between atom pairs.
The numerical computation of
\Omega_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell,s)}

is often performed in reduced units,
where lengths are given as multiples of the collision radius
\sigma

and energies are multiples of the well depth
\varepsilon
of the PEC,

e.g.
, the reduced PEC is
V^{\star}(r^{\star})=\varepsilon V(\sigma r^{\star})
,
in which the star (
⋆
) indicates a dimensionless reduced quantity.
Clearly,
we can have all the quantities involved in classic collision in
the “star” format by using the reduced units introduced above.

To compute
\Omega_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell,r)}
numerically, three integration must be carried out.
The scattering angle
\chi
is
a function of initial kinetic energy of the collision
E^{\star}
and the collision parameter
b^{\star}
, and is given by

\begin{split}\chi(E^{\star},b^{\star})&=\pi-2b^{\star}\int_{r_{\mathrm{m}}}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{r^{\star}}}{r^{{\star}2}F(r^{\star})},\\
&\text{with}\quad b^{\star}\in[0,+\infty),E^{\star}\in[0,+\infty).\end{split}

(65)

with

F(r^{\star})=\left[1-\frac{V^{\star}(r^{\star})}{E^{\star}}-\frac{b^{{\star}2}}{r^{{\star}2}}\right]^{1/2}.

(66)

The cross section
Q^{(\ell){\star}}
is a function of the initial kinetic energy
E^{\star}
,

Q^{(\ell){\star}}(E^{\star})=\cfrac{2}{1-\frac{1+(-1)^{\ell}}{2(1+\ell)}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\cos^{\ell}\chi(E^{\star},b^{\star})\right)b^{\star}\differential{b^{\star}}.

(67)

The collision integral
\Omega^{{\star}(\ell,s)}
is function of the reduced temperature
T^{\star}

\Omega^{\star(\ell,s)}(T^{\star})=\frac{1}{(s+1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}x^{s+1}Q^{{\star}(\ell)}(xT^{\star})\differential{x},

(68)

and
\Omega^{(\ell,s)}(T)
can be obtained using the expression

\Omega^{(\ell,s)}(T)=\frac{\frac{1}{2}(s+1)!\left[1-\frac{1+(-1)^{\ell}}{2(1+\ell)}\right]}{\sqrt{2\pi\mu/kT}}\Omega^{\star(\ell,s)}(T^{\star}).

(69)

Different numerical integration schemes can be implemented for these three integrals.
Also, different schemes can be combined freely.

Although it is often taught in books about OOP

Alternatively, readers may think such objects abstraction of computers of specific usage.
For those readers who are familiar with procedural programming,

, Fortran 77 users,

3.2
Algorithms implemented in
Peng

To give the end users a usable software,
and to test the framework,
we have supplied some concrete classes for integration.
Following Ref.
[
6
]
,

\chi
is rewritten as a function of
r_{\mathrm{m}}
,

\chi(E^{\star},r^{\star}_{\mathrm{m}})=\chi\Big(E^{\star},b^{\star}=r^{\star}_{\mathrm{m}}\sqrt{\frac{E^{\star}-V^{\star}(r^{\star}_{\mathrm{m}})}{E^{\star}}}\Big),

(70)

and the integration in
[r_{\mathrm{m}},\infty)
is mapped to
(0,1)

by
r\to\frac{r_{\mathrm{m}}}{r}
,
and the Chebyshev–Gauss (CG) quadrature rule
[
21
]
is used.
Note that CG rule is for
(-1,1)
.
To use it under this circumstance,
we assume the integrand of the
chi
quadrature is an even function,
and we only take the positive half.
The CG quadrature rule is based on Chebyshev nodes,
which are nested.
In our implementation,
3^{n}
(
n=1,2,\ldots
) nodes are used,
and the integration algorithm is written in class
CGQuadrature
.

The cross-section
Q^{(\ell)\star}
is

\begin{split}Q^{(\ell)\star}(E^{\star})=&\cfrac{2}{1-\frac{1+(-1)^{\ell}}{2(1+\ell)}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\cos^{\ell}\chi\right)b^{\star}\differential{b^{\star}}\\
=&\cfrac{1}{1-\frac{1+(-1)^{\ell}}{2(1+\ell)}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\cos^{\ell}\chi\right)\differential(b^{\star 2})\\
=&\cfrac{1}{1-\frac{1+(-1)^{\ell}}{2(1+\ell)}}\left[\int_{r^{\star}_{\mathrm{E}}}^{r^{\star}_{\mathrm{O^{\prime}}}}\!\!+\!\!\int_{r^{\star}_{\mathrm{O}}}^{\infty}\right]\left(1-\cos^{\ell}\chi\right)\\
&\times\differential\left(r^{\star 2}_{\mathrm{m}}{\frac{E^{\star}-V^{\star}(r^{\star}_{\mathrm{m}})}{E^{\star}}}\right).\end{split}

(71)

Due to orbiting
[
6
,
1
,
7
,
5
]
,
the integration must be divided into two parts when the energy is less
than
E^{\star}_{\mathrm{C}}
, corresponding to
r^{\star}_{\mathrm{C}}
,
which is the minimum of the function

y(r^{\star})=V^{\star}(r^{\star})+\tfrac{1}{2}r^{\star}\derivative{V^{\star}(r^{\star})}{r^{\star}}.

(72)

r^{\prime}_{\mathrm{E}}
is the smallest for a specific
E^{\star}
,
corresponding to
b^{\star}=0
.

r^{\star}_{\mathrm{O}}
and
r^{\star}_{\mathrm{O}^{\prime}}
are roots of

V^{\star}(r^{\star})+\frac{b^{{\star}2}E^{\star}}{r^{{\star}2}}-E^{\star}=0.

(73)

When
E^{\star}
is larger than
E^{\star}_{\mathrm{C}}
,

r^{\star}_{\mathrm{O}}=r^{\star}_{\mathrm{O^{\prime}}}=r^{\star}_{\mathrm{C}}
.
The two integration may be carried out numerically using the CG quadrature rule,
and have already been written in class
QCG
.
Note that when orbiting happens,
i.e.,
|\chi|
is large (in the present implement,
|\chi|>20\pi
),
it is very difficult to obtain an accurate scattering angle numerically.
Following Ref.
[
5
]
,

\cos^{l}(\chi)
is replaced by the average value

\cos^{\ell}(\chi)=\begin{cases}0\quad&(\ell\text{ is odd})\\
\frac{(\ell-1)!!}{\ell!!}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{\ell}{2}+\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{\ell}{2}+1)\sqrt{\pi}}\quad&(\ell\text{ is even})\end{cases}.

(74)

To compute
\Omega^{(\ell,s)\star}
,
generalized Gauss–Laguerre (GL) quadrature rule
[
21
]
is used,
because the formula for
\Omega^{(\ell,s)\star}
is in the format of the GL rule,
of which the grids and weight
are given by an automatic quadrature rule generator
[
22
]
.
Note that in generalized GL rule, different quadrature grids are used when
s
changes.
The computed
\Omega^{(\ell,s)\star}(T^{\star})
,
implemented in
OmegaGL
,
is then called by class
AtomPair

to obtain
\Omega^{(\ell,s)}(T)
.

We notice that with our algorithms,
the computational wall time is slightly longer than those of Refs.
[
5
,
7
,
6
]
.
This is mainly because we did not take the interpolation approximation of
Q
.
In the reference implementations,
Q
used by
\Omega
is obtained from interpolation.
However, we find that this made the final results unstable if
transport properties at higher temperature are required,
in which case the interpolation pivots may be changed.
Of course, this problem can be solved by using a nested interpolation grids
with spline, for example.
However, we do have more powerful computers now,
so direct computation of
Q
is affordable.
Should the user require such an interpolation,
one could embed our
QCG
class in their implementation of
QImpl
,
or write their own.

All other details are written as inline comments in
Peng
’s source code,
and the HTML format documentation can be generated using Doxygen
[
23
]
.
Developers can follow these documents should they wish to add new algorithms.
Beside the syntax check provided by compilers,
we use Valgrind
[
24
]
to check
that
Peng
does not suffer from problems from memory leakage or uninitialized variables.

6
Summary and Outlook

We presented
Peng
,
an extensible C++ library that compute the collision integrals between
atom pairs,
and transport properties
(diffusion, thermal diffusion, thermal conductivity, and viscosity)
based on the Chapman–Enskog solution of Boltzmann equation.

When designing the program,
extensibility is kept in mind.
The main three steps of computing classical collision integrals,
angle of deflection
\chi
, cross-section
Q
,
and collision integral
\Omega

can be implemented as concrete class of virtual classes

ChiImpl
,
QImpl
, and
OmegaImpl
.
Although we provide usable implementations based on Chebyshev–Gauss
and Gauss–Laguerre quadrature rules,
however, we will implement more algorithms,
and contributions from the community are welcomed.
Users can also only use the
TransportProperties
class
to use with their own integration code with minimal effort.

Peng
gives the user the ability of computing
thermophysical properties,
and the method developers a platform to test their algorithms.

Of course, the present version of
Peng
can only compute
classical collision integrals,
and can only deal with monatomic gas binary mixtures,
although these functions are rare in computer programs
publicly available.
In the future, more functions,

e.g.
, computing quantum collision integrals
[
1
,
31
]
,
dealing with polyatomic gases
[
32
]
,
and more flexible quadrature rules
(including doing the integration on multi-dimension sparse grids)
can be added in newer version.

B.1
Scalars, vectors, and tensors

In this work, following the tradition of
[
9
]

a
) to represent a scalar,
and bold italic font (
\bm{a}
) to denote a vector.

E.g.
, velocity
\bm{c}=(u,v,w)
is a vector, whose
x
,
y
, and
z
components are
u
,
v
, and
w
.
So a function
f
of
\bm{c}
would be of
u
,
v
, and
w
.
If we want to get the gradient of
f
, it would be

\frac{\partial}{\bm{\partial}\bm{c}}f=\left(\partialderivative{f}{u},\partialderivative{f}{v},\partialderivative{f}{w}\right).

(75)

The scalar (inner) product of two vectors are written as
\bm{a}.\bm{b}
,
which gives a scalar equals the sum of the products of each pair of the compoments of
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
.
The divergence of a function
f
can be, therefore, denoted by

\frac{\partial}{\bm{\partial}\bm{c}}.f=\partialderivative{f}{u}+\partialderivative{f}{v}+\partialderivative{f}{w}.

(76)

If the dot does not present,
\bm{a}\bm{b}
is a second order tensor (matrix),
which is denoted by bold Sans Serif font (
\bm{\mathsf{w}}
)

\bm{\mathsf{w}}=\bm{a}\bm{b}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{x}b_{x}&a_{x}b_{y}&a_{x}b_{z}\\
a_{y}b_{x}&a_{y}b_{y}&a_{y}b_{z}\\
a_{z}b_{x}&a_{z}b_{y}&a_{z}b_{z}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}w_{xx}&w_{xy}&w_{xz}\\
w_{yx}&w_{yy}&w_{yz}\\
w_{zx}&w_{zy}&w_{zz}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(77)

The divergence of a tensor is the sum of its diagonal elements, it is easy to turn a tensor as non-divergence,
and is denoted by
\accentset{0}{\bm{\mathsf{w}}}

\accentset{0}{\bm{\mathsf{w}}}=\bm{\mathsf{w}}-\frac{1}{3}(w_{xx}+w_{yy}+w_{zz})\bm{\mathsf{U}}

(78)

for a tensor in

space,
where
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
is the unitary matrix.
If the rows and columns of a tensor
\bm{\mathsf{w}}
is interchanged, we have the transposed one
\overline{\bm{\mathsf{w}}}
.
The symmetricalized
\bm{\mathsf{w}}
is then

\overline{\overline{\bm{\mathsf{w}}}}=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\mathsf{w}}+\overline{\bm{\mathsf{w}}}).

(79)

This symbol, can be combined with the non-divergence symbol, as
\accentset{0}{\overline{\overline{\bm{\mathsf{w}}}}}
.

Simple product of tensors is still a tensor, whose elements are

(\bm{\mathsf{w}}.\bm{\mathsf{w}}^{\prime})_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{\gamma}w_{\alpha\gamma}w_{\gamma\beta}^{\prime},

(80)

while the double, or scalar product is

\bm{\mathsf{w}}:\bm{\mathsf{w}}^{\prime}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}w_{\alpha\beta}w_{\beta\alpha}^{\prime}.

(81)

B.2
{\partial_{r}}/{\partial t}

The

\frac{\partial_{r}F}{\partial t}=\sum_{\lambda\in\{n,\bm{c},T\}}\partialderivative{F}{\lambda}\frac{\partial_{r}\lambda}{\partial t}.

We define

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{r}n}{\partial t}=

0&(r>0)\end{cases},

(83)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{r}\bm{c}}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\left(\bm{c}.\frac{\partial}{\bm{\partial}\bm{r}}\right)\bm{c}+\bm{F}-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\bm{\partial}\bm{r}}.\bm{\mathsf{p}}^{(0)}&(r=0)\\
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\bm{\partial}\bm{r}}.\bm{\mathsf{p}}^{(r)}&(r>0)\end{cases},

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{r}T}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\bm{c}.\frac{\partial T}{\bm{\partial}\bm{r}}-\frac{2}{3k_{\mathrm{B}}n}\left(\bm{\mathsf{p}}^{(0)}:\frac{\partial}{\bm{\partial}\bm{r}}\bm{c}+\frac{\partial}{\bm{\partial}\bm{r}}.\bm{q}^{(0)}\right)&(r=0)\\

(85)

B.3
Integrals related to

Again, we write the Maxwellian velocity distribution function,

i.e.
, the distribution function for a uniform and steady gas (Eq. (

))

f_{i}^{(0)}=n_{i}\left(\frac{m_{i}}{2\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right)^{3/2}\exp{-m_i(\vect{C}_i)^2/2\kB T}.

Let

,
G
,

,

, … be functions of velocity (we will label them with subscripts to specify the variable),

I

\begin{split}n_{0}^{2}I_{0}(F)&\equiv\iiint f_{0}^{(0)}f^{(0)}(F_{0}+F-F_{0}^{\prime}-F^{\prime})gb\differential{b}\differential{\epsilon}\differential{\vect{c}},\\

(86)

is only partly integrated, thus

and

\bm{c}_{0}
,

are defined as

[F,G]_{0}=[G,F]_{0}\equiv\int G_{0}I_{0}(F)\differential{\vect{c}_0}.

(87)

For

\bm{c}_{0}
and

and
K

,

\begin{split}[F_{0}+G_{1},H_{0}+K_{1}]_{01}\equiv&\int F_{0}I_{12}(H_{0}+K_{1})\differential{\vect{c}_0}+\int G_{1}I_{21}(H_{0}+K_{1})\differential{\vect{c}_1}\\
=&[H_{0}+K_{1},F_{0}+G_{1}]_{01}.\end{split}

(88)

\bm{c}_{0}

\begin{split}n^{2}\{F,G\}=&n^{2}\{G,F\}\\
