Arbitrary-order pressure-robust DDR and VEM methods for the Stokes problem on polyhedral meshes
Lourenço Beirão da Veiga, Franco Dassi, Daniele A. Di Pietro, Jérôme Droniou

Abstract
This paper contains two major contributions. First we derive, following the discrete de Rham (DDR) and Virtual Element (VEM) paradigms, pressure-robust methods for the Stokes equations that support arbitrary orders and polyhedral meshes. Unlike other methods presented in the literature, pressure-robustness is achieved here without resorting to an 𝑯 ⁡ ( div ) \boldsymbol{H}(\DIV) -conforming construction on a submesh, but rather projecting the volumetric force onto the discrete 𝑯 ⁡ ( 𝐜𝐮𝐫𝐥 ) \boldsymbol{H}(\CURL) space. The cancellation of the pressure error contribution stems from key commutation properties of the underlying DDR and VEM complexes. The pressure-robust error estimates in h k + 1 h^{k+1} (with h h denoting the meshsize and k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 the polynomial degree of the DDR or VEM complex) are proven theoretically and supported by a panel of three-dimensional numerical tests. The second major contribution of the paper is an in-depth study of the relations between the DDR and VEM approaches. We show, in particular, that a complex developed following one paradigm admits a reformulation in the other, and that couples of related DDR and VEM complexes satisfy commuting diagram properties with the degrees of freedom maps. Key words. Stokes problem, pressure-robustness, discrete de Rham method, Virtual Element method, compatible discretisations, polyhedral methods MSC2010. 65N12, 65N30, 65N99, 76D07

Introduction

Denote by
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
an open connected polyhedral domain.
For the sake of simplicity, we assume that
\Omega
has trivial topology, i.e., there is no tunnel crossing it and it does not enclose any void.
Given a volumetric force
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the Stokes problem for a homogeneous Newtonian fluid with unit viscosity reads:

\left\{~\begin{aligned} &\text{Find the velocity $\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and the pressure $p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ such that}\\
&\CURL(\CURL\boldsymbol{u})+\GRAD p=\boldsymbol{f}\hskip 11.49994pt\text{in $\Omega$},\\
&\DIV\boldsymbol{u}=0\quad\text{in $\Omega$},\\
&\CURL\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}\hskip 11.49994pt\text{on $\partial\Omega$},\\
&\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega$},\\
&\int_{\Omega}p=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

Notice that, in the momentum balance equation, we have used the vector calculus identity
-\LAPL\boldsymbol{u}=\CURL(\CURL\boldsymbol{u})-\GRAD(\DIV\boldsymbol{u})
along with the fact that
\DIV\boldsymbol{u}=0
to reformulate the viscous term as the curl of the vorticity.
The trivial topology assumption is made to simplify the exposition: for domains crossed by tunnels (i.e., for which the first Betti number is non-zero), one additionally has to enforce the orthogonality of the velocity to 1-harmonic forms for well-posedness.
We do not delve further into this topic here and refer to, e.g.,
[
4
, Chapter 4]
for additional details.
We also consider homogeneous natural boundary conditions only for the sake of simplicity: the extension to non-homogeneous and/or essential boundary conditions is possible (see, e.g., the discussion in
[
18
]
concerning the formulation corresponding to Eq. (1) therein).
We also notice that the extension of the method to more standard boundary conditions is possible by modifying the space for the velocity (e.g., for wall boundary conditions, it suffices to consider the subspace of velocities with vanishing tangential components on boundary edges and faces – these components being naturally available in our discrete spaces).
The details are postponed to a future work.
We finally point out that the two-dimensional case can be recovered as described in
[
24
, Remark 10]
, and the resulting scheme has analogous robustness properties as the ones discussed below for the three-dimensional case.

We are interested in the weak formulation of problem (
1.1
) described hereafter.
Assume
\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and denote by
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{H}(\CURL;\Omega)
the spaces of functions that are square-integrable over
\Omega
along with their gradient and curl, respectively.
Additionally letting
L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\coloneq\left\{q\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\int_{\Omega}q=0\right\}
, the weak formulation of problem (
1.1
) reads:

\left\{~\begin{aligned} &\text{Find $\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{H}(\CURL;\Omega)$ and $p\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)$ such that}\\
&\int_{\Omega}\CURL\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\CURL\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}\GRAD p\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\hskip 11.49994pt\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}(\CURL;\Omega)\\
&\int_{\Omega}\GRAD q\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\hskip 11.49994pt\forall q\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega).\end{aligned}\right.

(1.2)

Problem (
1.2
) admits a unique solution which, if regular enough, satisfies (
1.1
) almost everywhere.
It is a simple matter to check that changing the irrotational component of the body force
\boldsymbol{f}
only affects the pressure
p
, leaving the velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
unaltered.
When considering numerical approximations, the failure to reproduce this property at the discrete level can have a sizeable impact on the quality of the numerical solution
[
40
,
38
]
.
This can happen, e.g., when the Coriolis force is taken into account (in two dimensions, this force is always irrotational).
A numerical example where large unphysical oscillations occur due to the lack of pressure-robustness is provided in
[
24
]
; see, in particular, Fig. 4 therein.
Numerical schemes that behave robustly with respect to the magnitude of the irrotational part of the body force are often referred to as
pressure-robust
.
From the analysis standpoint, such methods guarantee velocity error estimates that are independent of the pressure.

The issue of pressure-robustness for finite element discretizations on standard (conforming) meshes has been addressed in several works.
A two-dimensional finite element pair on standard triangular meshes which is conforming, inf-sup stable, and delivers
\boldsymbol{H}(\DIV;\Omega)
-conforming approximations of the velocity has been developed in
[
36
]
using as a starting point the Stokes complex; see also
[
42
]
for an extension to quadrilateral elements.

\boldsymbol{H}(\DIV;\Omega)
-conforming velocity approximations naturally lead to pressure-robustness.
A related strategy to recover this property for a variety of numerical schemes is outlined in
[
39
]
(see also
[
41
]
), where the authors suggest a modification of the right-hand side involving the projection of the test function onto an
\boldsymbol{H}(\DIV;\Omega)
-conforming space.
This strategy has been applied to the design of pressure-robust Hybrid High-Order (HHO) discretizations of the Stokes problem on conforming simplicial meshes in
[
33
]
; see also
[
31
, Section 8.6]
and
[
24
]
(along with the precursor works
[
34
,
19
]
) concerning the extension to the full Navier–Stokes equations.
In
[
12
,
13
,
11
]
, the authors proposed a family of Virtual Element schemes for general polytopal meshes such that the virtual velocity is divergence-free and enjoys error bounds that do not depend directly on the pressure; although this represents an improvement with respect to standard inf-sup stable methods, the scheme is not fully pressure-robust since the velocity error depends indirectly on the pressure through a higher order loading term. We can designate such schemes as
asymptotically pressure robust
, meaning that the terms involving the pressure in the right-hand side of the error estimates are of higher-order than the dominating error component.

Adapting the strategy of
[
39
]
to general polytopal meshes can be problematic owing to the difficulty of devising discrete spaces that are both
\boldsymbol{H}(\DIV;\Omega)
-conforming
and
fully computable
.

\boldsymbol{H}(\DIV;\Omega)
-conforming virtual spaces, e.g., fulfill the first requirement but not the second; as a result, when used in the design of numerical schemes, they only lead to
asymptotic pressure robustness
.
One possibility then consists in constructing
\boldsymbol{H}(\DIV;\Omega)
-conforming spaces based on a matching and shape regular simplicial submesh, as recently proposed in
[
37
]
; see also
[
25
]
concerning the application of a similar strategy to HHO methods.
While this approach leads to fully pressure-robust methods, it hinges on a construction that can be computationally expensive, particularly in dimension 3 and/or in the presence of faces and edges that are orders of magnitude smaller than the parent element.

In this work we explore a different strategy based on a
compatible
approach, that is, we replace the spaces that appear in the weak formulation (
1.2
) with finite-dimensional counterparts that form an exact complex when connected by (discrete counterparts of) the usual vector calculus operators.
Pressure-robustness is then obtained projecting the body force
\boldsymbol{f}
onto the discrete
\boldsymbol{H}(\CURL;\Omega)
space and leveraging a commutativity property involving the interpolators on the discrete counterparts of
\boldsymbol{H}(\CURL;\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Omega)
and the (discrete analog of) the gradient operator.
A similar strategy has been considered in
[
18
]
in the context of Compatible Discrete Operators, leading to a pressure-robust, low-order method on general polytopal meshes.
We also mention here
[
26
, Remark 23]
on a related approach for HHO methods on standard meshes.
Two different design paradigms are considered: the discrete de Rham (DDR) approach of
[
28
]
(see also
[
32
,
29
]
), where both the spaces and differential operators are replaced by discrete analogs, and the Virtual Element Method (VEM) of
[
7
,
5
]
(see also
[
8
,
6
]
), where compatible and conforming (but not fully computable) spaces are exploited to design a numerical scheme through computable projections.
In both cases, we obtain fully pressure-robust schemes that, when complexes of degree
k\geq 0
are used as starting points, converge as
h^{k+1}
(with
h
denoting, as usual, the meshsize) in the graph norm.
The key feature of both schemes is that they achieve pressure-robustness on general polytopal meshes
without
resorting to a matching simplicial submesh.

This work also contains a second important contribution, namely the construction of bridges between the DDR and VEM approaches.
Specifically, we recast the spaces and local constructions of each paradigm into the other, thus enabling an in-depth comparison.
On one hand, this shows differences in the choice (and polynomial degree) of certain degrees of freedom;
on the other hand, it reveals that the reduction of the number of unknowns is obtained through different strategies in the two methods (serendipity for VEM, a systematic use of enhancement for DDR).
The links established in the present work can serve as a starting point for cross-fertilization of these approaches.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we establish the discrete setting.
In Sections
3
and
4
we state, respectively, the DDR and VEM schemes along with the corresponding pressure-robust error estimates.
A numerical study of the methods is performed in Section
5
, where we also verify in practice the pressure-robustness property.
Bridges between the DDR and VEM schemes are built in Section
6
.
Finally, Section
7
contains the proofs of the main results.

Setting

2.1
Mesh and orientation of mesh entities

For any measurable set

, we denote by
h_{Y}\coloneq\sup\{|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}|\,:\,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in Y\}

sharing

points out of

For any edge

Denote by

a face such that

and let
\boldsymbol{n}_{FE}
be the unit vector normal to

lying in the plane of

such that

We let

Differential operators on faces and tangential trace

For any mesh face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
, we denote by
\GRAD_{F}
and

.
Moreover, for any

\VROT_{F}r\coloneq\varrho_{-\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}}(\GRAD_{F}r)\hskip 11.49994pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 11.49994pt\ROT_{F}\boldsymbol{z}=\DIV_{F}(\varrho_{-\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{z}),

3.1
Spaces

We define the following spaces for the velocity and the pressure:

\displaystyle\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\CURL,h}^{k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\Big\{\begin{aligned} \underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}&=\big((\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}},T},\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}},T}^{\rm c})_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}},(\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}},F},\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}},F}^{\rm c})_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}},(v_{E})_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{h}}\big)\,:\,\\

Remark 5

(Pressure robustness)
.

It can be easily checked that the substitution
\boldsymbol{f}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{f}+\GRAD\psi
with
\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
in (
1.1
) results in
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\leftarrow(\boldsymbol{u},p+\psi)
, showing that the velocity field is not affected by the irrotational component of the source term at the continuous level.
By (
3.16
), a similar property holds at the discrete level: the substitution
\boldsymbol{f}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{f}+\GRAD\psi
with
\psi\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
(the additional regularity being required by the presence of the interpolator in the definition (
3.15
) of
\ell_{h}
) results in
(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\underline{p}_{h})\leftarrow(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\underline{p}_{h}+\underline{I}_{\GRAD,h}^{k}(\psi+C))
where
C
is a constant which ensures that
(\underline{I}_{\GRAD,h}^{k}(\psi+C),\underline{I}_{\GRAD,h}^{k}1)_{\GRAD,h}=0
.
This property has the important consequence that the right-hand side of the error estimate (
3.19
) is independent of the pressure, and is therefore not affected by the substitution
\boldsymbol{f}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{f}+\GRAD\psi
, showing that the DDR scheme (
3.14
) is
pressure robust

[
39
]
.
This property is obtained here on general meshes, for arbitrary polynomial degrees, and without resorting to submeshing.

4
VEM scheme

The second scheme we present in this paper is based on a set of Virtual Element spaces that form an exact sequence. Since the spaces are a simple modification of those presented in

[
5
]
, we will provide only a brief description and refer to the above paper for a deeper overview.
Throughout the rest of this section, the integer
k\geq 0
will denote the polynomial degree of the sequence.

4.1
Local spaces on faces

We first introduce the local
edge
and
nodal
spaces on faces, minimal modifications of those introduced in
[
7
,
5
]
. These spaces can be seen as a generalization to polygons of Nédélec elements of the first kind. Let
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
denote a mesh face.
In order to describe directly the serendipity version of the spaces (which, when compared to the standard version, requires a more complex construction but is more computationally efficient), we let

\beta_{F}\coloneq k+1-\eta_{F}\,,

(4.1)

where
\eta_{F}
is an integer, equal to or smaller than the number of straight lines necessary to cover the boundary of
F
. A safe (but possibly not optimal) choice is
\eta_{F}=3
, yielding
\beta_{F}=k-2
. Higher values of
\beta_{F}
will lead to a more efficient scheme but may not always be feasible depending on the face geometry.
In what follows, we always assume
\eta_{F}\geq 1
so that
\beta_{F}\leq k
; note that in the case
\beta_{F}=k
, which represents the plain non-serendipity VEM, the conditions in the spaces (
4.3
), (
4.8
) vanish and one does not need to define the serendipity projectors (
4.2
), (
4.7
).
In the case
\beta_{F}\geq 0
we assume that faces are convex (such condition is not needed if
\beta_{F}<0
). This convexity condition simplifies the development of serendipity spaces; for the treatment of non-convex faces also in the case
\beta_{F}\geq 0
we refer to
[
9
]
. An alternative option would be instead to use an “enhancement”, in the spirit of
[
1
]
, that is a simpler approach but leads to a less significant reduction in the number of degrees of freedom.

4.1.1
Edge space on faces

We start by defining a projector
{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{S,F}^{{\rm e}}:{\cal S}^{{\rm e}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(F)
, where
{\cal S}^{{\rm e}}
is a set of sufficiently regular functions
F\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, as follows:
For all
\boldsymbol{v}\in{\cal S}^{{\rm e}}
,

\displaystyle\mbox{$\int_{\partial F}[(\boldsymbol{v}-{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{S,F}^{{\rm e}}\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{\partial F}][\GRAD_{F}p\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{\partial F}]\>=0\hskip 11.49994pt\forall{p}\in\mathcal{P}^{k+1}(F)$},

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\mbox{$\int_{\partial F}(\boldsymbol{v}-{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{S,F}^{{\rm e}}\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{\partial F}\>=0$},

(4.2b)

\displaystyle\mbox{if $k>1$: \ $\int_{F}\ROT_{F}(\boldsymbol{v}-{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{{\rm e}}_{S,F}\boldsymbol{v})p\>=0\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{k-1}(F)$},

(4.2c)

\displaystyle\mbox{if $\beta_{F}\geq 0$: \ $\int_{F}(\boldsymbol{v}-{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{{\rm e}}_{S,F}\boldsymbol{v})\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F}\>p\>=0\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}}(F)$},

(4.2d)

where
(\boldsymbol{t}_{\partial F})_{|E}\coloneq\omega_{FE}\boldsymbol{t}_{E}
for all
E\in\mathcal{E}_{F}
. Note that, if
\eta_{F}
is chosen smaller than the number of straight lines necessary to cover the boundary of
F
, the above conditions are either to be intended in the least square sense or the integral in the first condition to be taken on a suitable subset of
\partial F
.
The (serendipity) edge space on
F
is then defined as:

SV_{k}^{{\rm e}}(F)\coloneq\Big\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(F)\,:\,\DIV_{F}\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(F),\,\ROT_{F}\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(F),\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{E}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(E)\quad\forall E\in\mathcal{E}_{F},\\
\int_{F}(\boldsymbol{v}-{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{S,F}^{{\rm e}}\boldsymbol{v})\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F}\,p\>=0\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}|k}(F)\Big\},

(4.3)

where
\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}|k}(F)
is any space such that

\mathcal{P}^{k}(F)=\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}}(F)\oplus\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}|k}(F)\,.

The following operators constitute (once bases for the corresponding polynomial test spaces are chosen) a unisolvent set of degrees of freedom (DoFs) for
SV_{k}^{{\rm e}}(F)
:

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{on each edge $E\in\mathcal{E}_{F}$, $\int_{E}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{E})p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(E)$,}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{if $\beta_{F}\geq 0$: \ }\int_{F}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F})\>p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}}(F),

(4.5)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{if $k>0$: \ }\int_{F}\ROT_{F}\boldsymbol{v}~p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{k}(F),

(4.6)

where we recall that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F}=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{c}_{F}
, and that
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{k}(F)
was defined in (
2.1
).
Following
[
5
, Eq. (3.6)]
, we can compute the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projector
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},F}^{k+1}{}:SV^{{\rm e}}_{k}(F)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k+1}(F)
using only the DoFs on
V^{{\rm e}}_{k}(F)
(that is, without the need of actually reconstructing the functions of
V^{{\rm e}}_{k}(F)
).

Remark 6

(Edge serendipity operator)
.

One could modify the serendipity operator into

{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{S,F}^{{\rm e}}:{\cal S}^{{\rm e}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(F)+{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F}^{\perp}\,\mathcal{P}^{k}(F)
,
by increasing the test functions in (
4.2c
) to
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{k}(F)
. This would guarantee
that the Nédélec space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(F)+{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F}^{\perp}\,\mathcal{P}^{k}(F)
is contained in
SV_{k}^{{\rm e}}(F)
,
and thus that the full Nédélec space of the first kind is contained
in
{V}^{{\rm e}}_{k}(T)
, see (
4.13
) below.
Such a change would however not improve the interpolation properties of the space with respect to Lemma
19
below.

4.1.2
Nodal space on faces

For the construction of the nodal serendipity space on faces we proceed as before.
Let
\Pi^{{\rm n}}_{S,F}:{\cal S}^{{\rm n}}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{k+1}(F)
, with
{\cal S}^{{\rm n}}
a space of sufficiently regular functions
F\to\mathbb{R}
,
be a projector defined by: For all
q\in{\cal S}^{{\rm n}}
,

\displaystyle\mbox{$\int_{\partial F}[\GRAD_{F}(q-\Pi^{{\rm n}}_{S,F}q)\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{\partial F}][\GRAD_{F}p\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{\partial F}]\>=0\hskip 11.49994pt\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{k+1}(F)$},

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mbox{$\int_{\partial F}(q-\Pi_{S,F}^{{\rm n}}q)({\boldsymbol{x}}_{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\partial F})\>=0$},

\displaystyle\mbox{if $\beta_{F}\geq 0$: \, $\int_{F}\GRAD_{F}(q-\Pi^{{\rm n}}_{S,F}q)\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F}\,p\>=0\hskip 11.49994pt\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}}(F)$},

where, for each
E\in\mathcal{E}_{F}
,
(\boldsymbol{n}_{\partial F})_{|E}\coloneq{\omega_{FE}}\boldsymbol{n}_{FE}
. The same observation as in (
4.2d
) applies.
The (serendipity) nodal space of order
k+1
on the face
F
is then defined as:

\displaystyle SV_{k+1}^{{\rm n}}(F)\coloneq\Big\{q\in H^{1}(F):

\displaystyle q_{|E}\in\mathcal{P}^{k+1}(E)\quad\forall E\subset\mathcal{E}_{F},\>\Delta_{F}q\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(F),

(4.8)

\displaystyle\int_{F}(\GRAD_{F}q-\GRAD_{F}\Pi_{S,F}^{{\rm n}}q)\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F}\,p\>=0\hskip 11.49994pt\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}|k}(F)\Big\}.

Note that the above conditions easily imply that functions in
SV_{k+1}^{{\rm n}}(F)
are continuous on the boundary of
F
.
The DoFs in
SV_{k+1}^{{\rm n}}(F)
are

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{for each vertex $\nu\in\mathcal{V}_{F}$, the value $q(\boldsymbol{c}_{\nu})$, }

(4.9)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{if $k\geq 1$: \ for each edge $E\in\mathcal{E}_{F}$, $\int_{E}q\,p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(E)$},

(4.10)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{if $\beta_{F}\geq 0$:}\ \int_{F}(\GRAD_{F}q\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F})\>p\>\hskip 11.49994pt\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}}(F).

(4.11)

Notice that, if
F
is a triangle, the space
SV_{k+1}^{{\rm n}}(F)
corresponds to the standard polynomial Finite Element space of degree
k+1
. We do not discuss here projectors in the space
SV_{k+1}^{{\rm n}}(F)
, since these will not be needed in the following.

4.2

L^{2}

Let
T
denote a mesh element of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, which we assume to have convex faces.
We introduce the following nodal, edge, and face (local) three-dimensional spaces, which, again, are minimal modifications of those in
[

(to which we refer for the proofs of the properties hereafter stated):

\displaystyle V^{{\rm n}}_{k+1}(T)

\displaystyle\coloneq\Big\{q\in H^{1}(T)\>:q_{|F}\in SV^{{\rm n}}_{k+1}(F)\quad\forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{T},\>\,\Delta\,q\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(T)\Big\},

(4.12)

\displaystyle V^{{\rm e}}_{k}(T)

\displaystyle\coloneq\Big\{\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(T)\,:\DIV\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(T),\>\CURL(\CURL\boldsymbol{v})\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(T),\\

(4.13)

where, as before,
\boldsymbol{v}_{{\rm t},F}
denotes the tangential trace of
\boldsymbol{v}
over
F

\!{V}^{{\rm f}}_{k}(T)\!\coloneq\!\Big\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(T)\,:\,{\DIV\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}}(T),\,\CURL\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(T),\>\boldsymbol{w}_{|F}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(F)\hskip 11.49994pt\forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}\Big\}.

(4.14)

The following linear maps form a set of DoFs for
V^{{\rm n}}_{k+1}(T)
:

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{for each vertex $\nu\in\mathcal{V}_{T}$, the nodal value $q(\boldsymbol{c}_{\nu}),$ }

(4.15)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{if $k\geq 1$: \ for each edge $E\in\mathcal{E}_{T}$, $\int_{E}q\,p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(E),$}

(4.16)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{for each face $F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}$ with $\beta_{F}\geq 0$, $\int_{F}(\GRAD_{F}q\cdot\boldsymbol{x}_{F})\>p\>\hskip 11.49994pt\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}}(F)$,}

(4.17)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{$\int_{T}(\GRAD q\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})\>p\>\hskip 11.49994pt\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(T).$}

(4.18)

In
V^{{\rm e}}_{k}(T)
, the DoFs are

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{for each edge $E\in\mathcal{E}_{T}$, $\int_{E}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{E})p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(E),$ }

(4.19)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{for each face $F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}$ with $\beta_{F}\geq 0$, $\int_{F}({\boldsymbol{v}_{{\rm t},F}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{F})\>p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}}(F),$}

(4.20)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{if $k>0$: \ for each face $F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}$, $\int_{F}\ROT_{F}{\boldsymbol{v}_{{\rm t},F}}\,p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{k}(F)$},

(4.21)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{$\int_{T}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}_{T})p\,\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(T)$},

(4.22)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{ $\int_{T}(\CURL\boldsymbol{v})\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\times\boldsymbol{p})\,\>\hskip 11.49994pt\forall\boldsymbol{p}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(T)$},

(4.23)

where we recall that
\boldsymbol{x}_{T}=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{c}_{T}
.
Finally, for
V^{{\rm f}}_{k}(T)
we have the DoFs

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{for any face $F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}$, $\int_{F}(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F})p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(F),$}

(4.24)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{if $k>0$: \ $\int_{T}(\DIV\boldsymbol{w})p\>\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{k}(T),$ }

(4.25)

\displaystyle\bullet\mbox{$\int_{T}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\times\boldsymbol{p})\>\quad\forall\boldsymbol{p}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(T)$}.

(4.26)

Many of the above DoFs could equivalently be written using the polynomial subspaces (
2.2
)-(
2.3
) instead of using an explicit expression. We prefer here to conform to the standard notation used in the VEM literature; a bridge between the two approaches will be built in Section
6
.
We also notice that, on tetrahedra, the spaces above have a higher number of internal DoFs than in the corresponding finite element case; one could reduce such number by applying an enhancement approach, see Remark
9
.
However, here we will make no effort to reduce this number, as it is assumed that, in practice, they could be eliminated by static condensation (since they are internal to the elements).

As shown in
[
5
, Proposition 3.7]
, from the above DoFs we can compute (in particular) the following
L^{2}
-orthogonal projections of virtual functions on polynomial spaces:
from
V^{{\rm e}}_{k}(T)
to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(T)
and
from
V^{{\rm f}}_{k}(T)
to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(T)
(actually, notice that we could also compute richer projections from
V^{{\rm f}}_{k}(T)
to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k+1}(T)
, but this will not be used in the following).
These projections are used to define the following scalar products for edge and face spaces mimicking the
\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(T)
product:
For
\bullet\in\{{\rm e},{\rm f}\}
, we let

[\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}]_{V^{\bullet}_{k}(T)}\coloneq\int_{T}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},T}^{k}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},T}^{k}}\boldsymbol{w}+s^{T}((I-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},T}^{k}})\boldsymbol{v},(I-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},T}^{k}})\boldsymbol{w})\qquad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\in V^{\bullet}_{k}(T)\times V^{\bullet}_{k}(T),

where the symmetric and computable bilinear form
s^{T}(\cdot,\cdot)
can be taken, for instance, as

s^{T}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})={h_{T}^{3}}\sum_{i}({\rm dof}_{i}\{\boldsymbol{v}\})\>({\rm dof}_{i}\{\boldsymbol{w}\})\qquad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\in V^{\bullet}_{k}(T)\times V^{\bullet}_{k}(T),

(4.27)

where we assume that all DoFs
{\rm dof}_{i}
are scaled in order to behave (with respect to element size changes) as nodal evaluations.
It is immediate to check that, by construction,

[\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{p}]_{V^{\bullet}_{k}(T)}=\int_{T}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}\>\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in V^{\bullet}_{k}(T),\;\forall\boldsymbol{p}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(T).

(4.28)

Remark 7

(Alternative stabilisation)
.

Another choice of stabilisation in
V^{\rm e}_{k}(T)
is

s^{T}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{T}}h_{T}^{2}\int_{E}\Pi_{\mathcal{P},E}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{E})\Pi_{\mathcal{P},E}^{k}(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{E})+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}}h_{T}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},F}^{k+1}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\rm t},F}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},F}^{k+1}\boldsymbol{v}_{{\rm t},F}

while, in
V^{\rm f}_{k}(T)
, we can take

s^{T}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{T}}h_{T}\int_{F}\Pi_{\mathcal{P},F}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F})\Pi_{\mathcal{P},F}^{k}(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F})+\int_{T}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},T}^{{\rm c},k+1}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},T}^{{\rm c},k+1}\boldsymbol{w}.

These stabilisations are inspired by the ones considered in the DDR setting and, following the ideas in
[
28
, Lemma 5]
and using discrete inverse inequalities, it can easily be checked that they yield coercive and consistent
\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(T)
-like inner products.

Remark 8

(Alternative choices of degrees of freedom)
.

An integration by parts easily shows that the DoFs (
4.17
) could be replaced by

\int_{F}\!q\,p\>
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}^{\beta_{F}}(F)
, and analogously the set (
4.18
) by

\int_{T}\!qp\>
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(T)
.
Knowledge on one set of DoFs implies knowledge on the other set, and vice-versa. Similarly, the set of DoFs (
4.23
) could be replaced by

\int_{T}\!\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\CURL(\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\times\boldsymbol{p})\>
for all
\boldsymbol{p}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(T)
and the set (
4.25
)
by
\int_{T}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\GRAD p\>
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{k}(T)
.
The advantage of the current choice is a more direct expression of the differential operators in terms of DoFs. The advantage of the alternative choice would be the reduced regularity needed in order to compute the DoF-interpolant of a generic function.

Remark 9

(Enhancement)
.

Note that one could apply an “enhancement” approach (in the elements volume), in order to reduce the number of internal DoFs. The enhancement idea, first introduced in
[
1
]
, is to adopt a slightly different definition of the VEM spaces in order to reduce the number of DoFs without sacrificing accuracy or computability.
For the spaces presented here, a particularly interesting form of enhancement can be obtained in the spirit of the DDR approach, see Section
6.3
.

4.3
Global spaces

The global spaces are constructed by standard degrees of freedom gluing, as in classical Finite Elements. The scalar nodal space is conforming in
H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
, the edge space in
\boldsymbol{H}(\CURL;\Omega)
, and the face space in
\boldsymbol{H}(\DIV;\Omega)
.
We set

\displaystyle V^{{\rm n}}_{k+1,0}

\displaystyle\coloneq\Big\{q\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\mbox{ such that }q_{|T}\in V^{{\rm n}}_{k+1}(T)\quad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\Big\},

(4.29)

\displaystyle V^{{\rm e}}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\Big\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}(\CURL;\Omega)\mbox{ such that }\boldsymbol{v}_{|T}\in V^{{\rm e}}_{k}(T)\hskip 11.49994pt\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\Big\},

(4.30)

\displaystyle V^{{\rm f}}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneq\Big\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{H}(\DIV;\Omega)\mbox{ such that }\boldsymbol{w}_{|T}\in V^{{\rm f}}_{k}(T)\quad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\Big\}.

(4.31)

The global DoFs can be trivially derived from the local ones (for instance, the space
V^{{\rm n}}_{k+1,0}
has one DoF per vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
k
DoFs per edge, etc.).
For
\bullet\in\{{\rm e},{\rm f}\}
, the global scalar products are given by

[\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}]_{V^{\bullet}_{k}}\coloneq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}[\boldsymbol{v}_{|T},\boldsymbol{w}_{|T}]_{V^{\bullet}_{k}(T)}\qquad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\in V^{\bullet}_{k}\times V^{\bullet}_{k}.

Remark 10

(Virtual exact sequence)
.

The Virtual Element spaces defined above form an exact sequence:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V^{{\rm n}}_{k+1,0}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\DIV}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

where the space

collects broken polynomial functions on

.

It is important to point out that the inclusions above can be also computed in practice, in the following sense.

]
for a deeper overview on such aspects.

4.4
Discrete problem and convergence

We are now able to present the VEM discretization of problem (

). Assuming that

Remark 11

(Pressure robustness)
.

Whenever the loading
\boldsymbol{f}
is a gradient, i.e.
\boldsymbol{f}=\GRAD\psi
for some sufficiently regular
\psi
, the commuting diagram property recalled at the end of the previous section gives

\boldsymbol{f}_{I}={\cal I}^{{\rm e}}_{k}(\GRAD\psi)=\GRAD{\cal I}^{{\rm n}}_{k+1}(\psi)

where the operator
{\cal I}^{{\rm n}}_{k+1}
denotes the natural DoF interpolator in
V^{{\rm n}}_{k+1}
(which consists in the space
V^{\rm n}_{k+1,0}
without the zero average condition).
Therefore,
reasoning as in Remark
5
, it can be checked that irrotational perturbations of the loading term have no influence on the velocity, and that the method is
pressure robust
in the sense of
[
39
]
.
This will also be reflected in the convergence result of Theorem
12
below, where the right-hand side of the error estimate does not depend on the pressure.
Such property is obtained here on general meshes, for arbitrary polynomial degrees, and without resorting to submeshing.

6
Bridging the VEM and DDR approaches

In this section we bridge the VEM and DDR approaches. The bridge VEM
\to
DDR consists in constructing, for each virtual space
V^{\bullet}_{l}
,
(\bullet,l)\in\{({\rm n},k+1),({\rm e},k),({\rm f},k)\}
, a fully discrete space
\underline{V}^{\bullet}_{l}
of vectors of polynomials, with the DoFs (interpreted as providing polynomials through the moments they describe) creating isomorphisms
V^{\bullet}_{l}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\approx}}{{\to}}\underline{V}^{\bullet}_{l}
; we also construct discrete operators, between the various
\underline{V}^{\bullet}_{l}
spaces, that commute with DoF maps and the corresponding operators between the virtual spaces
V^{\bullet}_{l}
.
Similarly, to create the bridge DDR
\to
VEM, we construct for each DDR space
\underline{X}_{\bullet}^{k}
,
\bullet\in\{\GRAD,\CURL,\DIV\}
, a space of virtual functions
X_{\bullet}^{k}
with DoFs that create isomorphisms
X_{\bullet}^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\approx}}{{\to}}\underline{X}_{\bullet}^{k}
, and such that the discrete DDR operators commute, through these isomorphisms, with the corresponding continuous differential operators between the virtual spaces.

6.3
Comparison and further developments

Due to the use of the serendipity approach on element faces, the Virtual Element Method has fewer DoFs for certain DoF sets. On the other hand, thanks to the systematic adoption of what, in the VEM language, would be called an enhancement approach, the DDR has fewer DoFs in other instances, such as element volumes.
Enhancement and serendipity have a similar goal, which is to use some DoFs that allow the computation of a suitable projection operator in order to “enslave” some other DoFs and thus reduce the local space dimension. They however have some important technical differences:

•

enhancement incorporates information made available by differential operators reconstructions into a potential of higher order than the projection directly computable from the DoFs;

•

serendipity hinges on the fact that fixing the value of a polynomial on the boundary of an element fixes also its value inside the element, at least for elements with a sufficiently large number of edges/faces (or otherwise with the help of some internal moments).

This difference is reflected in the fact that serendipity is a geometry-dependent approach (as can be noticed recalling the definition of the parameter
\eta_{F}
in Section
4.1
), while higher-order reconstructions/enhancement are independent of the element shape (but potentially less efficient as they “enslave” a smaller number of DoFs).

Thanks to the bridges developed above, such DoF-reduction strategies can be easily combined
resulting in a more efficient and highly competitive construction that can be interpreted both as DDR and VEM, as detailed in the recent work
[
30
]
.
Drawing the details of such construction is beyond the scope of the present article; nevertheless, to shed some more light onto the idea, we show very briefly the following short example of cross-fertilization.
Consider the Virtual Element space
V^{{\rm f}}_{k}(T)
. It can be checked that lowering by one the degree
k
appearing in the DoF set (
4.26
) (that is, testing only on
\boldsymbol{p}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k-1}(T)
) would still allow to compute the
L^{2}
-projection

{\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},T}^{k}}:V^{{\rm f}}_{k}(T)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{k}(T)
. Therefore one could use such projection to introduce an “enhancement” following the spirit in
[
1
]
and mimicking the analogous construction in the DDR approach:
decrease by one the degree
k
in (
4.26
) and introduce the corresponding constraint in the definition of the space
V^{{\rm f}}_{k}(T)
.
Analogously, the serendipity idea from VEM could be injected in the DDR setting, allowing to lower the DoF count on faces.
For instance, one could introduce in the spaces
\underline{X}_{\GRAD,T}^{k}
and
\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\CURL,T}^{k}
a construction leveraging serendipity operators in order to fix the values of certain polynomial components on faces.
