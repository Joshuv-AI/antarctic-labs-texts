Tsallis Entropy Regularization for Linear Quadratic Regulator and Kullback–Leibler control
Yota Hashizume, Koshi Oishi, Shota Yamanaka, Kenji Kashima, Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract
Shannon entropy regularization is widely adopted in optimal control due to its ability to promote exploration and enhance robustness, e.g., maximum entropy reinforcement learning known as Soft Actor-Critic. The aim of this paper is to show that formulations based on Tsallis entropy, which is a one-parameter extension of Shannon entropy, retain many of the structural and computational advantages of Shannon-entropy-based approaches while offering additional benefits. In particular, we derive a closed-form solution for the linear quadratic regulator and an efficient computational method for the Kullback–Leibler control problem. We also demonstrate its usefulness in balancing between exploration and sparsity of the obtained control law.



I
Introduction

The incorporation of Shannon entropy of a control policy into the objective function of reinforcement learning,
a technique known as maximum entropy reinforcement learning, has been applied in approaches like Soft Actor-Critic
[
1
]
.
This method finds practical applications in various real-world scenarios, notably in fields like robotics.
An important feature of using entropy regularization is that the optimal policy is stochastic which is advantageous for exploration and
improves robustness
[
2
]
.

Another example is privacy protection, where input randomization induced by entropy regularization makes it difficult for an eavesdropper to estimate the system state
[
3
]
.

Given these practical benefits, there is extensive research on optimal control utilizing entropy regularization.
When using entropy regularization,
the probability density function of the optimal control policy takes positive values for all input values.
Due to this property, entropy regularization is not suitable for problems where you
want to reduce the number of possible input values.

Regularization using Tsallis entropy allows us to obtain
a high-entropy but sparse control law.
The sparsity we aim for in this paper is different from sparsity as used in the context of sparse control
[
4
,
5
]
. The latter mainly refers to the length of time the input
value is
0
, whereas the sparsity here refers to the small support of the
probability distribution of the control law. In other words, increasing the
sparsity in this sense means increasing the number of input values for which the
probability of realization is
0
;
See Section
V-C
for a practical example.

In
[
6
]
, Tsallis entropy,
which originates from Tsallis statistical mechanics
[
7
]
, is used to regularize optimal transport problems to obtain high-entropy, but sparse solutions.
When regularization is performed using Shannon entropy, the optimal control law is
farthest from sparsity.
On the other hand, the support of a deterministic control law is a singleton set,
so it can be said to be the sparsest probability control law,
but in this case, the entropy is
0
.
Regularization using Tsallis entropy allows us to obtain a control law that lies
between these two extreme properties.

This allows the control input range to be limited in physical implementations (while retaining the benefit achieved by randomization), which is of clear practical importance; see Fig.
3
. In the context of privacy protection, unbounded noise, which is unnecessary
in most practical situations except for concealing outliers
[
3
]
, can be avoided by employing Tsallis
entropy regularization.
As another example, in logistics and transportation systems, balancing exploration and sparsity allows traffic to avoid excessive concentration on a single route for robustness, while at the same time limiting the number of active routes; see Fig.
4
.

In the context of reinforced learning, regularization using Tsallis entropy has been studied both theoretically and in applications
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
One of the major issues in Tsallis entropy regularized reinforcement learning is that even if the state value function or action value function can be estimated,
calculating the
q
-potential required for normalizing the policy is generally difficult; See Section
III-B
below.
Previous studies have taken the following approaches to address this problem:

•

[
8
]
limited the entropic index
q
, which is the degree of freedom in Tsallis entropy, to
q=0
for analytical calculation

•

[
9
]
performed calculations by using Taylor expansion and first-order approximation

•

[
10
]
avoided the calculation of
q
-potential by approximating the policy as a normal distribution

As mentioned above, in general MDP settings, this problem is solved by performing approximate calculations unless the entropic index
q
is limited.

In this study, we propose a method to obtain the optimal control law without approximation by performing Tsallis entropy regularization
in the range of
0\leq q<1
for Kullback–Leibler (KL) control and Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR), which are common in the context of control and are easier to solve than MDP.
Through numerical examples, we verify that the optimal control policies achieve
high entropy while maintaining sparsity, demonstrating the usefulness of TROC. A preliminary version, in which Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
are proven, is uploaded
[
11
]
.

III
Tsallis Entropy Regularized Optimal Control Problem

III-A

Problem formulation

Consider a discrete-time system with the state
x_{k}\in\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the control input
u_{k}\in\mathbb{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Conditional state transition probability distribution of
x_{k+1}
under

x_{k}=\bm{x},u_{k}=\bm{u}
, is denoted as
\varphi_{x_{k+1}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}\mid x_{k}=\bm{x},u_{k}=\bm{u})
. Also,
\varphi_{x_{0}}(\bm{x})
denotes the
initial distribution. Under this setting, we formulate Tsallis Entropy Regularized
Optimal Control Problem (TROC).

Problem 1

(TROC)
.

Let the terminal time be
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
Find the stochastic state feedback control policy
\pi_{k}(\bm{u}\mid\bm{x})=\varphi_{u\mid x}(\bm{u}\mid x_{k}=\bm{x}),k=0,\ldots,T-1
, that minimizes the
cost function
J
given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}J(&\left\{\pi_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{T-1}):=\mathbb{E}\left[l_{T}(x_{T})\right]\\
&+\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[l_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})-\lambda\mathcal{H}_{q}(\pi_{k}(u_{k}\mid x_{k}))\right],\end{split}

(13)

where
\lambda>0
and
\mathcal{H}_{q}
is the conditioned deformed
q
-entropy

\mathcal{H}_{q}(\pi(u\mid\bm{x})):=-\frac{1}{2-q}\left(\int\pi(u\mid\bm{x})\log_{q}\pi(u\mid\bm{x})du-1\right).

(14)

The objective of Problem
1
is to balance the traditional cost minimization
(i.e., the sum of running costs
l_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})
and the terminal cost

l_{T}(x_{T})
) and the maximization of the deformed
q
-entropy of the control
policy, which encourages exploration in the control policy. The parameter
\lambda
controls the trade-off between
these objectives.

III-B

Bellman equation for TROC

In this section, we introduce the Bellman equation for TROC in a general setting based on
[
10
]
.
The state-value function
V^{*}(i,\bm{x})

\displaystyle\begin{split}&V^{*}(i,\bm{x})\\
&:=\min_{\{\pi_{k}\}_{k=i}^{T-1}}\mathbb{E}[l_{T}(x_{T})]+\sum_{k=i}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[l_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})-\lambda\mathcal{H}_{q}(\pi_{k}(u_{k}\mid x_{k}))]\end{split}

(15)

and state-input value function

\displaystyle Q^{*}_{k}(\bm{x},\bm{u}):=l_{k}(\bm{x},\bm{u})+\mathbb{E}[V^{*}(k+1,x_{k+1})\mid x_{k}=\bm{x},u_{k}=\bm{u}].

(16)

Then, we obtain the following

Proposition 3

(Bellman Equation for TROC)
.

For Problem

\displaystyle V^{*}(k,\bm{x})=\min_{\pi_{k}}\mathbb{E}[Q_{k}^{*}(\bm{x},u)]-\lambda\mathcal{H}_{q}(\pi(u\mid\bm{x})),

(17)

and the optimal control policy is given by

\varphi_{u\mid x}^{*}(\bm{u}\mid\bm{x}):=\exp_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}Q^{*}_{k}(\bm{x},\bm{u})+C_{k}(\bm{x})\right)

(18)

where

determined by normalization constraint

\int_{{\mathbb{U}}}\varphi^{*}_{u|x}(u|\bm{x})du=1.

(19)

is called
q

In the case of the Shannon entropy regularized optimal control problem
[
18
]
, that is,
when

, the Bellman equation (

\displaystyle V(k,\bm{x})=-\lambda\log\int\exp\left(-\frac{Q_{k}(\bm{x},u)}{\lambda}\right)du

(20)

and

, and the optimal control policy is given by the soft-max function

which is positive for all

On the contrary, when

is not a special value, the distribution in (

which is called ent-max
[

It should be emphasized that the ent-max control law for

III-C

Robustness of Tsallis entropy regularized control

In this section, we discuss the robustness of the optimal control policy obtained
by solving the Tsallis entropy regularized control problem.
It is known that the maximum entropy optimal control problem is robust to cost function variations
in the sense that it is equivalent to the following min-max problem
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Proposition 4
.

For cost functions
l_{k}
, the optimal control policies of the min-max problem and the maximum entropy optimal control problem is the same;

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{\pi_{k}}\max_{\left\{\tilde{l}_{k}\right\}\in\tilde{L}(\left\{l_{k}\right\};\varepsilon)}\sum_{k=0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[\tilde{l}_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})\right]\\
&\quad=\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{\pi_{k}}\sum_{k=0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[l_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})\right]-\lambda\mathcal{H}(\pi_{k}(u_{k}\mid x_{k}))\end{split}

(22)

\tilde{L}(\left\{l_{k}\right\};\varepsilon)
is defined as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\tilde{L}(\left\{l_{k}\right\};\varepsilon)\\
&:=\left\{\left\{\tilde{l}_{k}\right\}\middle|{}^{\forall}k\ \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[\lambda\log\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u_{k}=1}^{N}\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta l_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})}{\lambda}\right)\right)\right]\leq\varepsilon\right\}\end{split}

(23)

with
\Delta l_{k}(x_{k},u_{k}):=l_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})-\tilde{l}_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})
.

This theorem shows that solving the maximum entropy optimal control problem is equivalent to solving the min-max problem with the robust set
\tilde{L}(\left\{l_{k}\right\};\varepsilon)
.

A similar theorem holds for the Tsallis entropy regularized optimal control problem.
As preparation, we first define the
q
-expectation.

Definition 5
.

For
0<q<2
, the
q
-expectation
\mathbb{E}_{p}^{q}[\cdot]
for a probability density function

\mathbb{R}
is defined as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}^{q}[f(x)]:=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x)^{q}f(x)dx}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x)^{q}dx}.

(24)

This can similarly be defined for probability density functions over finite sets.

Remark 2
.

The
q
-expectation for a probability density function

is
equal to the standard expectation for the probability density function
\frac{p(x)^{q}}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x)^{q}dx}
.
This distribution is called the escort distribution of

When
q=1
, the escort distribution is equivalent to the original distribution.
When
0<q<1
, the shape of the escort distribution becomes flatter compared to the original distribution.
This means that in this case, events with low probabilities are given greater weight than those in the standard expectation.
Conversely, when
1<q<2
, the weight for events with low probabilities is smaller, and the weight for events with high probabilities is larger. (See Fig.
2
.)

Fig. 2
:
Escort distribution for standard normal distribution

by replacing the expectation in Theorem

IV-A

Riccati equations

The Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) is an optimal control problem for linear

We consider the state

V-A

Kullback-Leibler control

Kullback-Leibler (KL) control is a control problem having costs described in terms of the KL divergence, enabling efficient numerical solutions to nonlinear optimal
control problems
[
22
]
. Since KL control can also be interpreted as an entropy-regularized
optimal control problem, it can be extended to the Tsallis entropy framework by
replacing KL divergence with
q
-KL divergence.
The advantage over MDP setting is that there is no need to estimate the input-value function.

In this section, we assume states are defined on a finite set
\mathbb{X}=\{1,...,n\}
, and control inputs are given by a transition matrix

P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. That is, if at time
k
, the state is distributed
according to the probability vector
\varphi_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then at time
k+1
,
the state distributes according to
\varphi_{k+1}=P^{\pi}\varphi_{k}
with input
P
.
Under this setting, we formulate the
q
-KL control problem as follows:

Problem 2

(
q
-KL Control Problem)
.

Consider a Markov process

\varphi_{k}^{\pi}
with transition matrix
P_{k}^{\pi}
. For the initial distribution

\varphi_{0}
, state stage cost
l\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, transition matrix
P^{0}
,
terminal time
T\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, and
\lambda>0
, find the transition
matrices
P_{k}^{\pi},k=0,...,T-1
that minimize the cost function
J

given by

\displaystyle J(\pi):=l^{\top}\varphi_{T}^{\pi}+\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\left(l^{\top}\varphi_{k}^{\pi}+\lambda D_{q}\left({P_{k}^{\pi}\varphi_{k}^{\pi}}\middle\|{P^{0}\varphi_{k}^{\pi}}\right)\right).

(55)

Here,
D_{q}\left({\varphi}\middle\|{\psi}\right)
is the
q
-KL divergence in the discrete case,

\displaystyle D_{q}\left({\varphi}\middle\|{\psi}\right):=\frac{1}{2-q}\left(\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}\log_{q}\frac{\varphi_{i}}{\psi_{i}}-1\right).

(56)

According to the objective function (
55
),
the goal of Problem
2
is to minimize the cost associated with the state at each time point,
while also minimizing the
q
-KL divergence between the state transition matrix
P_{k}^{\pi}

conditioned on the state and the given transition matrix
P^{0}
.
Since
P_{0}
represents the transition probabilities in the absence of control,
the cost of changing the transition matrix from
P_{0}
to
P_{k}^{\pi}
is expressed using
q
-KL divergence.
In particular, similar to the conventional KL divergence,

\displaystyle\textup{supp}({\varphi})\subset\textup{supp}({\psi})

(57)

is needed to make
D_{q}\left({\varphi}\middle\|{\psi}\right)
finite.
This implies that only transitions that can occur without control can be realized.

Similar to KL control, there is no need to estimate the
input-value function for the
q
-KL control setting

Theorem 3
.

For Problem
2
, the optimal control policy
P_{k}^{*}
is given by

(P_{k}^{*})_{ij}:=P^{0}_{ij}\exp_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{*}(k+1)_{i}+C_{k}(j)\right)

(58)

where
C_{k}(j)
and
V^{*}
are determined by
\sum_{i}(P_{k}^{*})_{ij}=1
and

\displaystyle V^{*}(T)_{j}

\displaystyle=l_{j}

(59)

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{*}(k)_{j}&=l_{j}+\left\{\lambda D_{q}\left({(P_{k}^{*})_{:j}}\middle\|{(P^{0})_{:j}}\right)+V^{*}(k+1)^{\top}(P_{k}^{*})_{:j}\right\}\\
&\hskip 140.0pt(k=0,...,T-1),\end{split}

(60)

where
(P)_{:j}
denotes the
j
-th column of
P
.

Proof:

We show
V^{*}
is the optimal state-value function. Similar to (
17
), let us consider

\displaystyle V(k)_{j}=l_{j}+\min_{\pi}\left\{\lambda D_{q}\left({(P_{k}^{\pi})_{:j}}\middle\|{(P^{0})_{:j}}\right)+V(k+1)^{\top}(P_{k}^{\pi})_{:j}\right\}.

(61)

Following the same reasoning as the proof of Lemma
1
, we can find the minimizer.
The KKT conditions are given by

\displaystyle V(k+1)_{i}+\lambda\log_{q}\frac{(P_{k}^{\pi})_{ij}}{P^{0}_{ij}}-C_{k}(j)^{\prime}=0,

(62)

\displaystyle C_{j}(k)^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i}(P_{k}^{\pi})_{ij}-1\right)=0.

(63)

From (
62

P_{k}^{*}
in (
58
).
∎

V-B

Efficient calculation based on parallelization and network sparsity

For the Shannon-entropy regularized KL-control case, i.e.,
q=1
, (
58
) reduces to

(P_{k}^{*})_{ij}\propto P^{0}_{ij}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{*}(k+1)_{i}\right)

(64)

for each
(j,k)
.
In contrast, no closed-form expression is available for the Tsallis-entropy case (
q\neq 1
).
Instead, we propose a scalable numerical procedure.

To compute the optimal control policy in Theorem
3
, for
k=T-1,\ T-2,\ \ldots,0
, we need to

•

solve
F_{jk}(C_{k}(j))=0
, where

F_{jk}(C):=\sum_{i}P^{0}_{ij}\,\exp_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{*}(k+1)_{i}+C\right)-1,

(65)

•

obtain
(V^{*}(k))_{j}
by (
60
),

for
j=1,\ldots,n
.
It should be emphasized that both of them are closed with respect to
j
. This separable structure allows for straightforward parallel computation. For instance, one can employ the Newton iteration

C_{k}(j)^{(t+1)}=C_{k}(j)^{(t)}-\beta\frac{F_{jk}(C_{k}(j)^{(t)})}{F_{jk}^{\prime}(C_{k}(j)^{(t)})}

(66)

with damping factor
\beta\in(0,1)
to enhance global convergence.

Moreover, in many practical applications the underlying network is sparse, in the sense that many entries of
P^{0}
are zero.
Such sparsity can be exploited to reduce memory requirements and to accelerate computations, for example by adopting the sparse matrix data structures provided in SciPy
[
24
]
.
Furthermore, since the system dynamics
P^{0}
are time-invariant (i.e., independent of
k
), the sparsity structure needs to be constructed only once and reused for all time steps.

VI
Conclusion

In this study, we formulated the KL control problem and linear quadratic regulator
problem with Tsallis entropy regularization term for the case of entropic index

0\leq q<1
, and proposed a method to obtain the optimal control law without
approximation.

Through numerical experiments, we demonstrated the utility of this approach for obtaining solutions
that are both high in entropy and sparse.
We extended existing results regarding the robustness of problems similar to TROC and demonstrated the dependence on
\varepsilon
that occurs only when
q\neq 1
.
Robustness of TROC is currently under investigation.

This work represents a first step toward constructing a Tsallis-entropy-based framework, and many aspects remain to be investigated. For example, the rigorous connection between Problem 1 and Theorem 1 remains open.
As another example, since Tsallis entropy has been used to regularize optimal transport problems in
[
6
]
, its control-theoretic counterpart, namely, density control
[
26
,
20
,
21
]
, appears to be a promising research direction (the Sinkhorn iteration cannot be directly applied).

Appendix A
Tsallis entropy regularized optimization

We prove two lemmas on optimization problems with a Tsallis entropy regularization term.

Lemma 1
.

For a real-valued function
Q
and
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\varphi^{*}(u):=\exp_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}Q(u)+C\right)

(70)

where
C
is a constant determined by
\int\varphi^{*}(u)du=1

is a unique minimizer of

\displaystyle J(\varphi):=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\varphi}\left[Q(u)\right]-\lambda\mathcal{H}_{q}(\varphi).

(71)

Proof:

We derive the optimal solution without the non-negativity constraint
\varphi(u)\geq 0

and show that the solution satisfies the non-negativity property.
Euler-Lagrange equation for the optimization problem is

Q(u)-\lambda\log_{q}(\varphi(u))-C^{\prime}=0,

(72)

where
C^{\prime}
is Lagrangian multiplier.
From (
72
) and (
3
), we obtain (
70
).
Then,
\varphi^{*}
is the unique minimizer of (
71
) by the concavity of Tsallis entropy
\mathcal{H}_{q}
, and it is non-negative.
∎

In particular, when
Q(u)
is a quadratic form, the optimal solution becomes a
q
-Gaussian.

Lemma 2
.

For a positive-definite matrix
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

\displaystyle Q(u):=(u-\mu)^{\top}R(u-\mu).

(73)

Then,
\varphi^{*}
in (
70
) is given by
q
-Gaussian
N_{q}(\mu,\Sigma)
with

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}:=\frac{(n+4)-(n+2)q}{\lambda}\eta R,

(74)

\displaystyle\eta:=\left\{\det(R)^{-1/2}\left(\frac{\pi\lambda}{1-q}\right)^{n/2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2-q}{1-q}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2-q}{1-q}+\frac{n}{2}\right)}\right\}^{\frac{2(1-q)}{(n+2)-nq}}.

(75)

Proof:

Since Tsallis entropy is translation invariant, we can assume
\mu=0
without loss of generality. Then,

\displaystyle\varphi^{*}(u)

\displaystyle=\exp_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}u^{\top}Ru+C\right)

(76)

\displaystyle=\exp_{q}(C)\exp_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda\exp_{q}(C)^{1-q}}u^{\top}Ru\right).

(77)

Thus,
\varphi^{*}
is
N_{q}(0,\Sigma)
with

\Sigma^{-1}:=\frac{(n+4)-(n+2)q}{\lambda\exp_{q}(C)^{1-q}}R.

(78)

From the normalization condition,

\exp_{q}(C)
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\exp_{q}(C)^{-1}\\
&=\det(R)^{-1/2}\left(\frac{\pi\lambda\exp_{q}(C)^{1-q}}{1-q}\right)^{n/2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2-q}{1-q}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2-q}{1-q}+\frac{n}{2}\right)}.\end{split}

Finally,
\eta:=\exp_{q}(C)^{-(1-q)}
is equivalent to (

).
∎

Appendix B
Upper bound for support of
q
-LQR optimal state trajectory

Here, we derive the region that contains the optimal state trajectory of
q
-LQR given an initial state
x_{0}
.
From Theorem
2
, the optimal state trajectory follows the dynamics:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(A_{k}+B_{k}K_{k})x_{k}+B_{k}w_{k}

(79)

\displaystyle w_{k}

\displaystyle\sim N_{q}(0,\Sigma_{k}),

(80)

Therefore, the expected value of the state follows the dynamics
\mathbb{E}[x_{k+1}]=(A_{k}+B_{k}K_{k})\mathbb{E}[x_{k}]
. Hereafter, we denote the expected value of the state as
\bar{x}_{k}:=\mathbb{E}[x_{k}]
.

Under these conditions, the following proposition holds:

Proposition 5
.

Let
\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{T}
be the optimal state trajectory of
q
-LQR, and let the initial state
x_{0}
be given. Then the support of the state distribution can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\textup{supp}({\varphi_{x_{k}}})\subset\left\{x_{k}\middle|\ \|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}\leq r_{k}^{2}\right\},

(81)

where
r_{k}>0
.

Proof:

Hereafter, let
\lambda_{\textup{max}}(A)
and
\lambda_{\textup{min}}(A)
denote the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix
A
, respectively.

From
w_{k}^{\top}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}w_{k}\geq\lambda_{\textup{min}}(\Sigma_{k}^{-1})\|w_{k}\|^{2}

and (
12
), the support of the distribution of
w_{k}
satisfies:

\textup{supp}({\varphi_{w_{k}}})\subset\left\{w_{k}\middle|\ \|w_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{n+4-(n+2)q}{(1-q)\lambda_{\textup{min}}(\Sigma_{k}^{-1})}\right\}.

(82)

Therefore, assuming
x_{k}
satisfies (
81
), the following holds:

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}

(83)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|G_{k}(x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k})\|^{2}+\|B_{k}w_{k}\|^{2}

(84)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lambda_{\textup{max}}(G_{k}^{\top}G_{k})\|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}+\lambda_{\textup{max}}(B_{k}^{\top}B_{k})\|w_{k}\|^{2}

(85)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lambda_{\textup{max}}(G_{k}^{\top}G_{k})r_{k}^{2}+\lambda_{\textup{max}}(B_{k}^{\top}B_{k})\frac{n+4-(n+2)q}{(1-q)\lambda_{\textup{min}}(\Sigma_{k}^{-1})},

(86)

where
G_{k}:=A_{k}+B_{k}K_{k}
.
Since it is trivial that
x_{0}
satisfies (
81
), the proof is complete.
∎
