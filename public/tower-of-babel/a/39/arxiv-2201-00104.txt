On Product Sets of Arithmetic Progressions
Yunkun Zhou

Abstract

This confirms a conjecture of Elekes and Ruzsa from about two decades ago. If instead 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is relaxed to be a subset of a finite arithmetic progression in integers with positive constant density, we prove that | 𝒜 ⋅ 𝒜 | ≥ | 𝒜 | 2 ( log ⁡ | 𝒜 | ) 2 ​ log ⁡ 2 − 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) . |\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}|\geq\frac{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}{(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{2\log 2-1+o(1)}}. This solves the typical case of another conjecture of Elekes and Ruzsa on the size of the product set of a set 𝒜 \mathcal{A} whose sumset is of size O ⁡ ( | 𝒜 | ) O(|\mathcal{A}|) . Introduction

The celebrated Erdős multiplication table problem asks to estimate how many numbers can be represented as the product of two positive integers which are at most
N
. It is a classical result
[
10
]
that there are
o(N^{2})
such numbers, which can be proved by simply considering the typical number of prime factors of an integer
n\leq N
. Erdős
[
8
]
first determined the answer up to a
(\log N)^{o(1)}
factor:

\Big|[N]\cdot[N]\Big|=\frac{N^{2}}{(\log N)^{2\theta+o(1)}},

(1.1)

where
[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
\theta=1-\frac{1+\log\log 4}{\log 4}
(which implies that
2\theta=1-\frac{1+\log\log 2}{\log 2}
), and
\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}
is defined to be
\{aa^{\prime}:a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\}
for any set
\mathcal{A}
.
The finer-order term was improved later by Tenenbaum
[
32
]
. Remarkably, Ford
[
12
]
has determined the quantity up to a constant factor:

\Big|[N]\cdot[N]\Big|\asymp\frac{N^{2}}{(\log N)^{2\theta}(\log\log N)^{3/2}}.

(1.2)

There is also work on generalizations of the Erdős multiplication table problem to higher dimensions: see
[
16
]
for references.

In this paper, we are interested the same problem but with
[N]
replaced by an arbitrary arithmetic progression
\mathcal{A}
of
N
integers. In some cases the size of the product set can be as large as the trivial upper bound
\binom{N+1}{2}
, which is of order
N^{2}
. One example that achieves this bound is
\{1+kd:k\in[N]\}
where
d>2N
. It is interesting to know whether the size of the product set of any arithmetic progression with given length
N
can be substantially smaller than the bound in (
1.1
). Elekes and Ruzsa
[
7
]
conjectured that it cannot. In this paper, we prove their conjecture.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be a finite arithmetic progression and
\theta=1-\frac{1+\log\log 4}{\log 4}
. Then

|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}|\geq|\mathcal{A}|^{2}(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{-2\theta-o(1)}.

The strongest lower bound we can prove is
|\mathcal{A}|^{2}(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{-2\theta}(\log\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{-7-o(1)}
(Theorem
7.1
), which is sharp up to a power of
\log\log|A|
.

We also prove an asymmetric version of the conjecture, which strengthens Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be two finite arithmetic progressions with lengths
2\leq|\mathcal{B}|\leq|\mathcal{A}|
and
\theta=1-\frac{1+\log\log 4}{\log 4}
. Then

|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}|\geq{|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|}{(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{-\theta-o(1)}(\log|\mathcal{B}|)^{-\theta}},

where the
o(1)
terms go to zero as
|\mathcal{A}|
tends to infinity.

Remark
.

The two theorems above are sharp up to a
(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{o(1)}
factor. The proof we present gives the error term in the form
\exp(O(\sqrt{\log\log|\mathcal{A}|}\log\log\log|\mathcal{A}|))
. In fact, a stronger bound up to a
(\log\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{O(1)}
factor can be obtained. We leave details of the proof to the stronger error term to Appendix
A
.

A particular structural property of arithmetic progressions
\mathcal{A}
is that it has small sumset. The celebrated sum-product conjecture
[
9
]
states that for any
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
,

\max\{|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|,|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}|\}\gg|\mathcal{A}|^{2-o(1)},

(1.3)

where
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}=\{a+a^{\prime}:a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\}
and
\ll
is to denote up to a multiplicative constant.
There has been much progress towards this conjecture (see
[
23
]
for the current record), though it is still wide open. One extremal case of the sum-product conjecture is the case where one of the sumset and product set is very small, in which, the conjecture is known to be true. Indeed,
Chang
[
4
]
proved that

|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}|\ll|\mathcal{A}|\implies|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|\gg|\mathcal{A}|^{2},

which is sharp up to a multiplicative constant factor. See
[
19
,
22
]
for discussions in other settings.

We are interested in the other extremal case where
|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|/|\mathcal{A}|
is bounded, which seems to be much more challenging. In contrast to Chang’s result
[
4
]
, even if the doubling number (i.e.
|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|/|\mathcal{A}|
) is at most
2
, the product set can still be smaller than
|\mathcal{A}|^{2}
by a polylogarithmic factor (see (
1.2
)).
Nathanson and Tenenbaum proved
[
20
]
that if
|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|<3|\mathcal{A}|-4
, then
|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}|\gg|\mathcal{A}|^{2}/(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{2}
.
This result was later generalized by Chang
[
3
]
to
h
-fold product sets. Finally, Elekes and Ruzsa
[
7
]
proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.3

(Elekes and Ruzsa
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be a finite set. Then the following holds.

|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|\ll|\mathcal{A}|\implies|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}|\geq|\mathcal{A}|^{2}(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{1-2\log 2-o(1)}.

Elekes and Ruzsa
[
7
]
showed that if
|\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}|\ll|\mathcal{A}|
, then
|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}|\gg|\mathcal{A}|^{2}(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{-1}
when
\mathcal{A}
is a finite set of integers.
As a corollary of Solymosi’s remarkable result
[
28
]
, the same implication holds when
\mathcal{A}
is a finite set of reals. We remark that their proofs did not use specific properties of the integers, and we improve their bounds by taking the arithmetic information of integers into account. Indeed, the
2\log 2-1
exponent has a natural arithmetic interpretation. By the Sathe-Selberg formula
[
25
]
, the number of
n\leq N
with
\Omega(n)=(2+o(1))\log\log N
is about
N/(\log N)^{2\log 2-1+o(1)}
, where
\Omega(n)
is the number of prime factors of
n
. Further we claim that the bound in Conjecture
1.3
is optimal up to
o(1)
factor in the exponent. One simple example is to take
\mathcal{A}\subset[N]
containing set of all numbers
n
with
\Omega(n)=(1+o(1))\log\log N
. By the Hardy-Ramanujan theorem one has
|\mathcal{A}|=(1+o(1))N
. Since
A\cdot A
is contained in the set of integers with
\Omega(n)=(2+o(1))\log\log N
, combining this with the previously mentioned application of Sathe-Selberg’s formula, the claim follows.

We believe that to get the conjectured exponent
2\log 2-1
, one has to take the arithmetic information into account. We also remark that for similar arithmetic reasons, the constant
2\log 2-1
appears in recent work
[
5
,
18
,
29
]
.

By the Freiman-Ruzsa theorem
[
15
,
24
]
, every set with constant doubling number is a dense subset of a generalized arithmetic progression of constant dimension. A natural case is that
\mathcal{A}
is a dense subset of an arithmetic progression. In fact, in some sense this is also the typical case
[
1
,
2
]
.
In this paper, we prove that the Elekes-Ruzsa conjecture is true in this case.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1]
and
\mathcal{A}
be a subset of an arithmetic progression
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
with
|\mathcal{A}|\geq\delta|\mathcal{P}|
, then

|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}|\gg_{\delta}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{1-2\log 2-o(1)}.

We remark that Pomerance and Sárközy
[
21
]
proved a special case of Theorem
1.4

(\mathcal{P}=[N])
.
We also prove a more general asymmetric version.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1]
and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be two subsets of two finite arithmetic progressions
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, and
|\mathcal{A}|\geq\delta|\mathcal{P}_{1}|
,
|\mathcal{B}|\geq\delta|\mathcal{P}_{2}|
, then

|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}|\gg_{\delta}|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{\frac{1}{2}-\log 2-o(1)}(\log|\mathcal{B}|)^{\frac{1}{2}-\log 2-o(1)}.

Although Theorem
1.4
does not solve Conjecture
1.3
completely, it can be used to prove the case for doubling number up to
4
(in terms of difference set). In this case, Eberhard, Green and Manners
[
6
, Theorem 6.4]
proved that
\mathcal{A}
must be a subset of an arithmetic progression with positive density, and thus the following corollary can be deduced from Theorem
1.4
immediately.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be a finite set with

|A-A|\leq(4-\varepsilon)|A|
, then

|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{A}|\gg_{\varepsilon}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{1-2\log 2-o(1)}.

In Theorem
1.5
, one can relax the condition that
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
are dense subsets of arithmetic progressions
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}
, to the condition that
\mathcal{A}
intersects an arithmetic progression
\mathcal{P}_{1}
(with sizes
|\mathcal{P}_{1}|=\Theta(|\mathcal{A}|)
) with positive density, and a similar condition holds for
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
. Such
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
may have large sumsets.

Our Theorem
1.5
also easily implies the following observation. Consider the restricted sumsets

\mathcal{A}+_{G}\mathcal{B}:=\{a+b:(a,b)\in G)\},\quad|G|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|,

where
\varepsilon
is a small absolute constant. If the restricted sumset is small, then one can get similar lower bounds on
|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}|
as in Theorem
1.5
. The proof simply consists of using the structural results in
[
17
,
26
]
to conclude that such set
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
must overlap with some arithmetic progressions
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}
heavily, and then applying Theorem
1.5
to conclude the proof. We leave the details for the interested reader.

We prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.5
by bounding the multiplicative energy from above.

Definition 1.7

(Multiplicative energy)
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be two finite subsets of integers. The multiplicative energy between
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
is defined as

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=\left|\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}:a_{1}b_{1}=a_{2}b_{2}\}\right|.

When
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
, we write
E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}):=E_{\times}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A})
, which is called the multiplicative energy of
\mathcal{A}
.

The first main theorem is about the multiplicative energy of any finite arithmetic progression.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
be a finite arithmetic progression. Then there exists a subset
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
with size
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\geq|\mathcal{A}|(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{-\theta-o(1)}
such that
E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\ll|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|^{2}.

A stronger version in terms of the
o(1)
term is achieved in Appendix
A
. The second main theorem is about the multiplicative energy of a dense subset of any finite arithmetic progression.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1]
and let
\mathcal{A}
be a subset of a finite arithmetic progression
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
such that
|\mathcal{A}|\geq\delta|\mathcal{P}|
. Then there exists a subset
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
with size
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\gg_{\delta}|\mathcal{A}|
such that

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\ll_{\delta}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{2\log 2-1+o(1)}.

1.1
Proof ideas

Our main focus will be on Theorem
1.8
and Theorem
1.9
, and all the rest of our results will follow from them fairly straightforwardly (See Section
2
).

Theorem
1.8
and Theorem
1.9
are well understood when the arithmetic progression is just the first
N
integers (See
[
31
,
8
,
13
]
for the special case of Theorem
1.8
and
[
21
]
for the special case of Theorem
1.9
.) The classical proofs of both special cases involve estimates on
\pi_{k}(x)
(the number of integers up to
x
with
k
distinct prime factors) and tools like Shiu’s Theorem on bounding mean values of multiplicative functions. The original arguments cannot be directly generalized because these tools are not applicable to general arithmetic progressions. Our main novelty is the deduction of the general case in the case where the parameters of the arithmetic progression lie in a certain range such that all classical tools are applicable. The deduction is completed by proving the desired upper bounds for multiplicative energies in the other cases via elementary counting arguments. After the deduction steps, we carefully employ and adapt the method developed in
[
13
,
8
,
12
,
14
]
to conclude the proof of both Theorem
1.8
and Theorem
1.9
.

2
Reduction to multiplicative energy estimates

In this section, we show that Theorems
1.8
and
1.9
imply all the other main results.
The implications follow from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (see Lemma
2.1
). The proof of Lemma
2.1
is standard (e.g., see
[
30
]
Corollary 2.10 for an additive version): we include it here for completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be nonempty sets of nonzero integers. Then we have

1.

|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}|\geq\frac{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{B}|^{2}}{E_{\times}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}.

2.

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq\sqrt{E_{\times}(\mathcal{A})E_{\times}(\mathcal{B})}
.

Proof.

For any two sets of integers
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
, we define

r_{\mathcal{X}\cdot\mathcal{Y}}(m):=|(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}:xy=m|,

and similarly define define ratio representation functions
r_{\mathcal{X}/\mathcal{Y}}(m)
for
m\in\mathbb{Q}
.
By Definition
1.7
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}|\cdot E_{\times}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}}1\cdot\sum_{m\in\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}}r_{\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}}(m)^{2}\geq\left(\sum_{m\in\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}}r_{\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}}(m)\right)^{2}=|\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{B}|^{2}.

This gives (
1
). By Definition
1.7
,
and noting that
a_{1}a_{2}=a_{1}^{\prime}a_{2}^{\prime}
is equivalent to

\frac{a_{1}}{a_{1}^{\prime}}=\frac{a_{2}^{\prime}}{a_{2}}
, we have

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A})=\left|\{(a_{1},a_{2};a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}^{4}:a_{1}a_{2}=a_{1}^{\prime}a_{2}^{\prime}\}\right|=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Q}}r_{\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}}(m)^{2}.

We have a symmetric equation for
\mathcal{B}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A})E_{\times}(\mathcal{B})=\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Q}}r_{\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}}(m)^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Q}}r_{\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{B}}(m)^{2}\right)\geq\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Q}}r_{\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{A}}(m)r_{\mathcal{B}/\mathcal{B}}(m)\right)^{2}.

The right hand side is the square of the following counting

\left|\{(a_{1},a_{2};b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}:a_{1}/a_{2}=b_{1}/b_{2}\}\right|=E_{\times}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}).

The equality is because
a_{1}/a_{2}=b_{1}/b_{2}
is equivalent to
a_{1}b_{2}=a_{2}b_{1}
. Hence we have (
2
).
∎

Assuming Theorems
1.8
and
1.9
, we prove all the theorems regarding product sets (Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.4
and
1.5
) via Lemma
2.1
. Before we proceed to the proof, we remark that the condition in Lemma
2.1
that
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
do not contain
0
is a mild technical restriction, as deleting one single element changes the size of product sets by at most
O(|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|)
, and changes multiplicative energy by at most
O(|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|)
, which are negligible for our purpose.

Proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
assuming Theorem
1.8
.

By Theorem
1.8
, there exist subsets
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
with sizes
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\gg|\mathcal{A}|(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{-\theta-o(1)},|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|\gg|\mathcal{B}|(\log|\mathcal{B}|)^{-\theta-o(1)},

such that
E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\ll|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|^{2},E_{\times}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\ll|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|^{2}.

These energy bounds give

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\leq\sqrt{E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})E_{\times}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})}\ll|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}||\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|,

where the first inequality is due to (
2
) of Lemma
2.1
. By (
1
) of Lemma
2.1
we conclude that

|\mathcal{A}\cdot\mathcal{B}|\geq|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cdot\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|\geq\frac{|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|^{2}|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|^{2}}{E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})}\geq\frac{|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|}{(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{\theta+o(1)}(\log|\mathcal{B}|)^{\theta+o(1)}}.

We thereby prove Theorem
1.2
. Taking
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
in Theorem
1.2
gives Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
assuming Theorem
1.9
.

By applying Theorem
1.9
to
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, we get subsets
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
with sizes
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\gg_{\delta}|\mathcal{A}|,|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|\gg_{\delta}|\mathcal{B}|,

and energy bounds

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\ll_{\delta}|\mathcal{A}|^{2}(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{2\log 2-1+o(1)},\quad E_{\times}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\ll_{\delta}|\mathcal{B}|^{2}(\log|\mathcal{B}|)^{2\log 2-1+o(1)}.

By applying (
2
) of Lemma
2.1
we get

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\ll_{\delta}|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{B}|(\log|\mathcal{A}|)^{\log 2-\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}(\log|\mathcal{B}|)^{\log 2-\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}.

Now Theorem
1.5
follows by (
1
) of Lemma
2.1
.
Taking
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
in Theorem
1.5
gives Theorem
1.4
.
∎

3
Proof of Theorem
1.9
: reduction to the essential case

In this section, we want to reduce Theorem
1.9
to the following case, which is proved in the Section
4
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1]
. Let
\mathcal{P}=\{a+id:0\leq i<L\}
be an arithmetic progression with common difference
d
and length
L
, with
L,d>0
,
L\log L>a\geq dL
, and
\operatorname{gcd}(a,d)=1
. If
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
is a subset of size at least
\delta L
containing only square-free elements and
L
is sufficiently large with respect to
\delta
, then there exists a subset
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
with
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\gg|\mathcal{A}|
such that

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\ll L^{2}(\log L)^{2\log 2-1+o(1)}.

(3.1)

We start by giving an upper bound on the multiplicative energy in some special cases. When
\operatorname{gcd}(a,d)=1
, we have the following observation on the multiplicative energy.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{a+id:0\leq i<L\}
be an arithmetic progression with
\operatorname{gcd}(a,d)=1
and
a>0,d>0
. For any
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
, we have
E_{\times}(\mathcal{A})\leq 2|\mathcal{A}|^{2}+4\frac{L^{3}}{a}(1+\log L).

Proof.

The number of solutions
(a_{1},a_{2};a_{3},a_{4})\in\mathcal{A}^{4}

\frac{x_{1}L}{a}<1
then there is no valid tuple in
X
.

Similarly, as
x_{1}y_{i}\in[a,a+dL)
, we know that
y_{i}\in[\frac{a}{x_{1}},\frac{a}{x_{1}}+\frac{dL}{x_{1}})
. As
y_{1}<y_{2}
and they are congruent mod
d
, we know that there is no valid choice for
(y_{1},y_{2})
if
\frac{L}{x_{1}}<1
. Otherwise if
\frac{L}{x_{1}}\geq 1
, then the number of choices is at most

\frac{1}{2}\left\lceil\frac{L}{x_{1}}\right\rceil\cdot\left(\left\lceil\frac{L}{x_{1}}\right\rceil-1\right)\leq\frac{L^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}}.

Therefore, for each
x_{1}
, the number of choices for
(x_{2};y_{1},y_{2})
is at most
\frac{L^{3}}{ax_{1}}
.

Let
X_{1}
be the set of such tuples with
x_{1}\leq\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}
. We first estimate
|X_{1}|
. As we sum
\frac{L^{3}}{ax_{1}}
over
1\leq x_{1}\leq\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}
, we conclude that
|X_{1}|\leq\frac{L^{3}}{a}\left(\log\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}+1\right)\leq L^{2}\left(2\frac{L}{a}+2\frac{L}{a}\log\frac{a}{L}\right)\leq 4L^{2}.

It remains to estimate the size of
X_{2}=X\setminus X_{1}
. It consists of tuples with
x_{1}>\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}
. Note that
x_{1}^{2}\leq x_{1}y_{1}<a+dL
, we know that
\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}<x_{1}<\sqrt{a+dL}
.
For each fixed
x_{1}=x\in(\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}},\sqrt{a+dL})
, we consider tuples
(x_{1},x_{2};y_{1},y_{2})\in X_{2}
. As argued above, we know that
x_{2}
is an element in
(x_{1},x_{1}dL/a)
with
x_{2}\equiv x_{1}\pmod{d}
, and
y_{1},y_{2}
are elements in
[\frac{a}{x_{1}},\frac{a}{x_{1}}+\frac{dL}{x_{1}})
with
y_{1}\equiv y_{2}\equiv ax_{1}^{-1}\pmod{d}
.

Next we use the fact that all elements in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
having at most
T
distinct prime factors and are square-free, which implies that
\omega(x_{i})+\omega(y_{j})=\omega(x_{i}y_{j})\leq T
for
i,j\in\{1,2\}
. Therefore for each
(x_{1},x_{2};y_{1},y_{2})\in X_{2}
, we have

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{\omega(x_{1}y_{1})+\omega(x_{1}y_{2})+\omega(x_{2}y_{1})+\omega(x_{2}y_{2})-4T}\geq 1.

Using the above bound and that
\omega(x_{i}y_{j})=\omega(x_{i})+\omega(y_{j})
since
x_{i}y_{j}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is square-free, we have

\begin{split}|X_{2}|&\leq\sum_{(x_{1},x_{2};y_{1},y_{2})\in X_{2}}2^{-\omega(x_{1})-\omega(x_{2})-\omega(y_{1})-\omega(y_{2})+2T}\\
&\leq 2^{2T}\sum_{\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}<x_{1}<\sqrt{a+dL}}2^{-\omega(x_{1})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}<x_{2}<x_{1}\frac{a+dL}{a}\\
x_{2}\equiv x_{1}\;(\mathrm{mod}\;d)\end{subarray}}2^{-\omega(x_{2})}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{a}{x_{1}}\leq y<\frac{a+dL}{x_{1}}\\
y\equiv ax_{1}^{-1}\;(\mathrm{mod}\;d)\end{subarray}}2^{-\omega(y)}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(4.4)

We now apply Theorem
4.2
to two factors on the right hand side of (
4.4
). Note that for
x^{\prime}=x_{1}\frac{a+dL}{a}
and
y^{\prime}=x_{1}dL/a
, we have
y^{\prime}>\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}\frac{dL}{a}=\frac{ea}{L}d\geq ed^{2}>d^{2}
(where we use the assumption that

) and
x^{\prime}>y^{\prime}
. Finally we have
(y^{\prime})^{2}=x_{1}^{2}d^{2}L^{2}/a^{2}>ex_{1}d^{2}\geq ex_{1}>x^{\prime}
. Therefore parameters
(x,y,d,f)=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},k,2^{-\omega(\cdot)})

4.2
with
\alpha=\beta=1/2

. By Theorem
4.3
, we have
\sum_{p\leq x^{\prime},p\nmid d}f(p)/p\leq\frac{\log\log x^{\prime}+C}{2}
for

, and further

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}<x_{2}<x_{1}\frac{a+dL}{a}\\
x_{2}\equiv x_{1}\;(\mathrm{mod}\;d)\end{subarray}}2^{-\omega(x_{2})}\ll\frac{x_{1}\frac{dL}{a}}{\phi(d)}\left(\log\frac{x_{1}(a+dL)}{a}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{x_{1}dL}{a\phi(d)}(\log x_{1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.5)

Similarly as
x_{1}<\sqrt{a+dL}\leq\sqrt{2L\log L}<L^{\frac{2}{3}}
for
L
sufficiently large, one can verify that for
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(\frac{a+dL}{x_{1}},\frac{dL}{x_{1}})
, we have
(y^{\prime})^{2}=d^{2}\frac{L^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}}>d^{2}L^{\frac{2}{3}}>d^{4}
,
x^{\prime}>y^{\prime}
, and
\frac{(y^{\prime})^{2}}{x^{\prime}}=\frac{d^{2}L^{2}}{x_{1}(a+dL)}>\frac{d^{2}L^{2}}{(a+dL)^{3/2}}>1
. Hence by Theorem
4.2
with the same
(\alpha,\beta,A_{1},A_{2})
and by the same estimate due to Theorem
4.3
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{a}{x_{1}}\leq y<\frac{a+dL}{x_{1}}\\
y\equiv ax_{1}^{-1}\;(\mathrm{mod}\;d)\end{subarray}}2^{-\omega(y)}\ll\frac{\frac{dL}{x_{1}}}{\phi(d)}\left(\log\frac{a+dL}{x_{1}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sqrt{2}\frac{dL}{x_{1}\phi(d)}(\log(a+dL))^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.6)

Here in the second inequality we use
\frac{a+dL}{x_{1}}\geq\sqrt{a+dL}
. Put (
4.5
) and (
4.6

\begin{split}|X_{2}|&\ll 2^{2T}\sum_{\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}<x_{1}<\sqrt{a+dL}}2^{-\omega(x_{1})}\cdot\frac{d^{3}L^{3}}{x_{1}a\phi(d)^{3}}(\log x_{1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\log(a+dL))^{-1}\\
&=\frac{d^{3}L^{3}}{a\phi(d)^{3}}2^{2T}(\log(a+dL))^{-1}\sum_{\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}<x_{1}<\sqrt{a+dL}}2^{-\omega(x_{1})}\frac{(\log x_{1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{x_{1}}.\end{split}

(4.7)

Here the implicit constant is absolute. To estimate the second line of (

for

\sum_{e^{k}<x_{1}<e^{k+1}}2^{-\omega(x_{1})}\frac{(\log x_{1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{x_{1}}\leq\sum_{e^{k}<x_{1}<e^{k+1}}2^{-\omega(x_{1})}\frac{k^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{e^{k}}\ll e^{k}(k+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{k^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{e^{k}}\ll k^{-1},

with

\sum_{\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}<x_{1}<\sqrt{a+dL}}2^{-\omega(x_{1})}\frac{(\log x_{1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{x_{1}}\ll\sum_{k=\lfloor\log\frac{ea^{2}}{L^{2}}\rfloor}^{\lfloor\log\sqrt{a+dL}\rfloor}k^{-1}\leq 2\log\log(a+dL).

Noting that

Put (

4.9

Here the implicit constant factor is absolute. It follows that

E_{\times}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\leq 2|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|^{2}+8(|X_{1}|+|X_{2}|)\ll L^{2}\left(1+\frac{L}{a}(\log L)^{2\log 2-1+\epsilon}\right).

Recall that

∎

5
Elements with a fixed number of primes factors in arithmetic progressions

In this section, we make some preparations for the proof of Theorem
1.8
in Section
6
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arithmetic progression satisfying
0<dL\leq a\leq L\sqrt{\log L}
. For any positive integer
n
, we denote the sequence of distinct prime factors of
n
by
p_{1}(n)<p_{2}(n)<\cdots<p_{\omega(n)}(n)
. For parameters
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{A};\alpha,\beta):=\{n\in\mathcal{A}:n\textrm{ square-free},\;\omega(n)=k,\;\log\log p_{j}(n)\geq\alpha j-\beta\;\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\;1\leq j\leq k\}

(5.1)

and let
N_{k}(\mathcal{A};\alpha,\beta)=|\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{A};\alpha,\beta)|
. Our goal is to give a lower bound on
N_{k}(\mathcal{A};\alpha,\beta)
. This is a natural generalization of
[
8
,
11
,
31
]
where the object studied is the special arithmetic progression
\mathcal{A}=[N]
. Our proof uses ideas from
[
8
,
31
]
and in Appendix
A
we give a stronger estimate following
[
14
]
.

First we estimate the number of primes in an arithmetic progression using the following theorem of Siegel and Walfisz. For a finite
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, let
\pi(\mathcal{A})
be the number of primes in
\mathcal{A}
.

Theorem 5.1

(Siegel-Walfisz, see Section II.8. in
[
32
]
)
.

Let
\pi(x;q,a)
be the number of primes up to
x
with
p\equiv a\pmod{q}
. Then for any
A>0
,
uniformly for
(a,q)=1
and
1\leq q\leq(\log x)^{A}
, we have

\pi(x;q,a)=\frac{x}{\phi(q)\log x}+O\left(\frac{x}{\phi(q)(\log x)^{2}}\right).

(5.2)

Lemma 5.2
.

If
\mathcal{A}=\{a+id:0\leq k<L\}
satisfies that
L
is sufficiently large, and
0<dL<a<10L\sqrt{\log L}
and
\operatorname{gcd}(a,d)=1
, then
\pi(\mathcal{A})\geq\frac{dL}{2\phi(d)\log L}
.

Proof.

First note that we can write
\pi(\mathcal{A})=\pi(a+dL-1;d,a)-\pi(a-1;d,a)
. From our assumption we know that
d<10\sqrt{\log L}
and
a+dL>a>L
, so we may take
A=1
in Theorem
5.1
and
L>e^{100}
such that
1\leq d<\log(a-1)<\log(a+dL-1)
. Hence we apply (
5.2
) to have

\pi(\mathcal{A})=\frac{a+dL-1}{\phi(d)\log(a+dL-1)}-\frac{a-1}{\phi(d)\log(a-1)}+O\left(\frac{a-1}{\phi(d)(\log(a-1))^{2}}+\frac{a+dL-1}{\phi(d)(\log(a+dL-1))^{2}}\right).

To estimate the main term, noticing that
a-1\leq a+dL-1\leq 2a-2
, we have

\frac{a+dL-1}{\phi(d)\log(a+dL-1)}=\frac{a+dL-1}{\phi(d)\log(a-1)}+O\left(\frac{a+dL-1}{\phi(d)(\log(a-1))^{2}}\right).

This shows that

\pi(\mathcal{A})=\frac{dL}{\phi(d)\log(a-1)}+O\left(\frac{a-1}{\phi(q)(\log(a-1))^{2}}+\frac{a+dL-1}{\phi(d)(\log(a+dL-1))^{2}}+\frac{a+dL-1}{\phi(d)(\log(a-1))^{2}}\right).

Clearly
\frac{a+dL-1}{\phi(d)(\log(a-1))^{2}}
is the largest among the three summands in the error term. As
L\leq dL<a\leq 10L\sqrt{\log L}
, we can bound the error terms by

\frac{a+dL-1}{\phi(d)(\log(a-1))^{2}}\leq\frac{20dL\sqrt{\log L}}{\phi(d)(\log L)^{2}}=O\left(\frac{dL}{\phi(d)(\log L)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right).

As
L
is sufficiently large, we may assume that the error term is at most
\frac{1}{6}\frac{dL}{\phi(d)\log L}
. Also note that
a-1\leq 10L\sqrt{\log L}\leq L^{\frac{3}{2}}
when
L
is sufficiently large. This gives the desired estimate

\pi(\mathcal{A})\geq\frac{2}{3}\frac{dL}{\phi(d)\log L}-\frac{1}{6}\frac{dL}{\phi(d)\log L}=\frac{dL}{2\phi(d)\log L}.

∎

Using this, we can estimate
N_{k}(\mathcal{A};\alpha,\beta)
with a specific choice of the parameters motivated by
[
8
]
.

Proposition 5.3
.

If
\mathcal{A}=\{a+id:0\leq i<L\}
is an arithmetic progression satisfying that
L
is sufficiently large,
0<dL\leq a\leq L\sqrt{\log L}
, and
\operatorname{gcd}(a,d)=1
, then for
k=\left\lfloor\frac{\log\log L}{\log 4}-5\sqrt{\log\log L}\right\rfloor-4
,

N_{k}(\mathcal{A};\log 4,1)\gg{L}{(\log L)^{-\theta-o(1)}}.

Proof.

To obtain a lower bound, we count elements
n\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{A};\log 4,1)
with
p_{1}(n)p_{2}(n)\cdots p_{k-1}(n)<\sqrt{a}
.
Once we fixed such a choice of
(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k-1})
with
\log\log p_{j}\geq j\log 4-1
for all
1\leq j\leq k-1
, we count the number of
p_{k}
with
p_{k}>p_{k-1}
and
p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
. Let
q=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k-1}
. Clearly if
qp\in\mathcal{A}
for some prime
p
, then
p\geq\frac{a}{q}>\sqrt{a}>q>p_{k-1}
. Moreover we have

\log\log p>\log\log\sqrt{L}=\log\log L-\log 2>k\log 4-1,

so any such choice of prime
p
would give a valid choice for
p_{k}
as defined in (
5.1
).

For any such fixed choice of
q
, if
\operatorname{gcd}(q,d)>1
, then any element in
\mathcal{A}
is not a multiple of
q
, so there is no such choice of
p_{k}
.
If
\operatorname{gcd}(q,d)=1
, then
q|a+id
is equivalent to
i\equiv-ad^{-1}\pmod{q}
and there is an unique such
i=i_{0}
with
0\leq i_{0}<q
. Then the multiples of
q
in
\mathcal{A}
are of the form

\left\{a+(i_{0}+k^{\prime}q)d:0\leq k^{\prime}<\frac{L-i_{0}}{q}\right\}\supseteq\{a+(i_{0}+k^{\prime}q)d:0\leq k^{\prime}<\lfloor L/q\rfloor\},

and the inclusion follows from
\frac{L-i_{0}}{q}>\lfloor L/q\rfloor-1
.
Hence any element
p\in\mathcal{A}_{q}:=\{a^{\prime}+k^{\prime}d:0\leq k^{\prime}<L^{\prime}\}
with
a^{\prime}=\frac{a+i_{0}d}{q}
and
L^{\prime}=\lfloor L/q\rfloor
would satisfy
pq\in\mathcal{A}
. It is sufficient to estimate the number of primes from
\mathcal{A}_{q}
, which itself is an arithmetic progression. As
\operatorname{gcd}(a^{\prime},d)\leq\operatorname{gcd}(a+i_{0}d,d)=\operatorname{gcd}(a,d)=1
, we have
\operatorname{gcd}(a^{\prime},d)=1
. Moreover, we know that
dL^{\prime}\leq\frac{dL}{q}<\frac{a}{q}\leq a^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}<10L^{\prime}\sqrt{\log L^{\prime}}
when
L
is sufficiently large. Note that
L^{\prime}\geq\frac{L}{q}-1>\frac{L^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(\log L)^{\frac{1}{4}}}-1
is also sufficiently large. By invoking Lemma
5.2
, the number of choices for
p_{k}
is at least

\pi(\mathcal{A}_{q})\geq\frac{dL^{\prime}}{2\phi(d)\log L^{\prime}}\geq\frac{dL/q}{4\phi(d)\log L}.

(5.3)

Summing (
5.3
) up over all possible
(p_{1},\dots,p_{k-1})
,
we have

N_{k}(\mathcal{A};\log 4,1)\geq\frac{dL}{4\phi(d)\log L}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k-1},p_{1}\cdots p_{k-1}<\sqrt{a}\\
p_{j}\nmid d,\log\log p_{j}\geq j\log 4-1\;\forall\;1\leq j\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k-1}}.

(5.4)

Since
L\leq a\leq L^{2}
, the condition in Lemma
5.4
is satisfied with our choice of

,
and it implies that

\begin{split}N_{k}(\mathcal{A};\log 4,1)&\geq\frac{dL}{4\phi(d)\log L}(e\log 4)^{k-1}(\log\sqrt{a})^{-o(1)}\geq\frac{L}{(\log L)^{\theta+o(1)}},\end{split}

where we use that
\frac{d}{\phi(d)}\geq 1
,
k=(1+o(1))\frac{\log\log L}{\log 4}
, and the constant
\frac{1}{4}
is absorbed in the

term.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
x
large enough,
k\leq\frac{\log\log x}{\log 4}-5\sqrt{\log\log x}
,
d=O(\log x)
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k},p_{1}\cdots p_{k}<x\\
p_{j}\nmid d,\log\log p_{j}\geq j\log 4\;\forall\;1\leq j\leq k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}\geq(e\log 4)^{k}(\log x)^{-o(1)}.

(5.5)

Proof.

Let
h=\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{\log\log x}}{\log 4}\right\rfloor
, and
J=\lfloor k/h\rfloor=\sqrt{\log\log x}-O(1)
. For each
j\geq 1
, we define

I_{j}:=\left[e^{e^{j\sqrt{\log\log x}}},e^{e^{(j+1)\sqrt{\log\log x}}}\right).

By Theorem
4.3
, we know that
\sum_{p\in I_{j}}1/p=\sqrt{\log\log x}+O(1)
. Therefore
\sum_{p\in P_{j}}1/p=\sqrt{\log\log x}-O(\log\log\log x)
where
P_{j}
is the set of primes in
I_{j}
which are not factors of
d
, and the

O(\log\log\log x)
term bounds the contribution from those primes which are factors of
d
.
In the summation in (
5.5
), we consider only the tuples
(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})
with
h
primes in
P_{j}
for each
1\leq j\leq J
and
(k-Jh)
primes in
P_{J+1}
. Noting that
k-Jh\leq h\leq e^{h}\leq e^{\sqrt{\log\log x}}
, a straightforward computation shows that

p_{1}\cdots p_{k}\leq\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}he^{(j+1)\sqrt{\log\log x}}+(k-Jh)e^{(J+2)\sqrt{\log\log x}}\right)<e^{e^{(J+4)\sqrt{\log\log x}}}<x

where in the last step we use that
J+4<\sqrt{\log\log x}
for
x
large. Hence, any such tuple would satisfy
p_{1}\cdots p_{k}<x
. Moreover, we know that
\log\log p_{j}\geq\lceil j/h\rceil\sqrt{\log\log x}>j\log 4
. Therefore any such tuple contributes to a summand on the left hand side of (

). Then the left hand side of (
5.5
) is at least

\prod_{j=1}^{J}\left(\sum_{p_{1}<\cdots<p_{h}\in P_{j}}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{h}}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k-Jh}\in P_{J+1}}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k-Jh}}\right).

(5.6)

Factors in (
5.6
) are of the same form. We estimate each of them as follows. For
1\leq t\leq h
and
j\geq 1
,

\sum_{p_{1}<\cdots<p_{t}\in P_{j}}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{t}}=\frac{1}{t!}\sum_{p_{1}\in P_{j}}\frac{1}{p_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{2}\in P_{j}\\
p_{2}\neq p_{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{2}}\cdots\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{t}\in P_{j}\\
p_{t}\neq p_{1},\dots,p_{t-1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{t}}\geq\frac{1}{t!}\left(\sum_{p\in P_{j}}\frac{1}{p}-\frac{t}{e^{e^{j\sqrt{\log\log x}}}}\right)^{t}.

(5.7)

Note that
t\leq h\leq\sqrt{\log\log x}/\log 4\leq e^{e^{j\sqrt{\log\log x}}}
, so the term inside the parenthesis on the right hand side of (

) is
\sqrt{\log\log x}-O(\log\log\log x)
. We shall apply Stirling’s formula and obtain that, when

is sufficiently large, the right hand side of (

\begin{split}\textrm{RHS of }\eqref{eqn:sum-of-re-each}&\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log\log x}-O(\log\log\log x)-1}{t/e}\right)^{t}\cdot(\log\log x)^{-1/4-o(1)}\\
&\geq\left(e\log 4-O\left(\log\log\log x/\sqrt{\log\log x}\right)\right)^{t}\cdot(\log\log x)^{-1/4-o(1)}\\
&\geq(e\log 4)^{t}\cdot(\log\log x)^{-C}\end{split}

for some absolute constant

.
Applying this bound to (

) with

and

, we have

\begin{split}\textrm{LHS of }\eqref{eqn:sum-of-reciprocal}\geq\eqref{eqn:sum-of-re-1}&\geq(e\log 4)^{Jh}(\log\log x)^{-CJ}\cdot(e\log 4)^{k-Jt}(\log\log x)^{-C}\\
&=(e\log 4)^{k}\cdot(\log\log x)^{-O(\sqrt{\log\log x})}\end{split}

Thus, noting that the
(\log\log x)^{-O(\sqrt{\log\log x})}=(\log x)^{-o(1)}

q
, if
\operatorname{gcd}(q,d)>1
, then any element in
\mathcal{A}
is not a multiple of
q
, so there is no such choice of
p_{k}
.

If
\operatorname{gcd}(q,d)=1
, we know that
q|a+id
is equivalent to
i\equiv-ad^{-1}\pmod{q}
. There is a unique such
i_{0}
with
0\leq i_{0}<q
. Then the multiples of
q
in
\mathcal{A}
are of the form

\left\{a+(i_{0}+k^{\prime}q)d:0\leq k^{\prime}<\frac{L-i_{0}}{q}\right\}\supseteq\{a+(i_{0}+k^{\prime}q)d:0\leq k^{\prime}<\lfloor L/q\rfloor\}.

The inclusion is because
\frac{L-i_{0}}{q}>\lfloor L/q\rfloor-1
.
Hence any element
p\in\mathcal{A}_{q}:=\{a^{\prime}+k^{\prime}d:0\leq k^{\prime}<L^{\prime}\}
where
a^{\prime}=\frac{a+i_{0}d}{q}
and
L^{\prime}=\lfloor L/q\rfloor
would satisfy
pq\in\mathcal{A}
. It is sufficient to estimate the number of primes from
\mathcal{A}_{q}
, which itself is an arithmetic progression. As
\operatorname{gcd}(a^{\prime},d)\leq\operatorname{gcd}(a+i_{0}d,d)=\operatorname{gcd}(a,d)=1
, we have
\operatorname{gcd}(a^{\prime},d)=1
. Moreover, we know that
dL^{\prime}\leq\frac{dL}{q}<\frac{a}{q}\leq a^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}<10L^{\prime}\sqrt{\log L^{\prime}}
when
L
is sufficiently large. We also have that
L^{\prime}\geq\frac{L}{q}-1>\frac{L^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(\log L)^{\frac{1}{4}}}-1
is also sufficiently large. Hence by Lemma
5.2
, the number of choices for
p_{k}
is at least

\pi(\mathcal{A}_{q})\geq\frac{dL^{\prime}}{2\phi(d)\log L^{\prime}}\geq\frac{dL/q}{4\phi(d)\log L}.

(A.2)

Hence by summing (
A.2
) up, we have

N_{k}(\mathcal{A};\log 4,\beta)\geq\frac{dL}{4\phi(d)\log L}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k-1},p_{1}\cdots p_{k-1}<\sqrt{a}\\
p_{j}\nmid d,\log\log p_{j}\geq j\log 4-\beta\;\forall\;1\leq j\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k-1}}.

(A.3)

To estimate the sum on the right hand side of (
A.3
), we use Lemma
A.4
. Let
\gamma^{\prime},C^{\prime}
be the constants in Lemma
A.4
, and let
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime},d^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})=(\sqrt{a},k-1,d,\beta)
. We verify that the assumptions are met. First, as
x^{\prime}\geq\sqrt{a}\geq\sqrt{L}
, we may assume that
x^{\prime}
is sufficiently large by setting
L
large enough. As
L
is sufficiently large, we have
L^{2}\geq a\geq L
, so
L\geq x^{\prime}\geq\sqrt{L}
. Hence
\log\log x^{\prime}=\log\log L+O(1)
, and

k^{\prime}=k-1=\frac{\log\log L}{\log 4}+O(1)=\frac{\log\log x^{\prime}}{\log 4}+O(1).

Also we have
d\leq 10\sqrt{\log L}\leq 10\sqrt{\log(x^{\prime})^{2}}=O(\log x^{\prime})
. By choosing
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
, we have
\beta^{\prime}=\beta=\gamma\log\log\log\log L\geq\gamma\log\log\log\log x^{\prime}
. Therefore we may apply Lemma
A.4
with
a
being polynomial in
L
to get that there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that, by recalling the definition of
\theta
,

\begin{split}N_{k}(\mathcal{A};\log 4,\beta)&\gg\frac{dL}{\phi(d)\log L}\cdot(\log L)^{\frac{1+\log\log 4}{\log 4}}(\log\log L)^{-3/2}(\log\log\log L)^{-C_{0}}\\
&\gg\frac{L}{(\log L)^{\theta}}(\log\log L)^{-3/2}(\log\log\log L)^{-C_{0}},\end{split}

where in the last inequality we use that
\frac{d}{\phi(d)}\geq 1
.
∎

Theorem A.2

(Theorem 1 in
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be independent uniform random variables on
[0,1]
and
\mathbf{1}_{E}
be the indicator function for event
E
. Uniformly for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
u,w>0
, we have

\Pr\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}\leq t}\leq(n+w-u)t+u\;\forall\;t\in[0,1]\right)=1-e^{-\frac{2uw}{n}}+O\left(\frac{u+w}{n}\right).

(A.4)

Corollary A.3
.

There exists a sufficiently large constant
C
such that the following holds. Uniformly for
\beta\geq C\alpha>0
and
C\leq\frac{N-\alpha n+\beta}{\alpha}
, we have

\vol\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):\begin{matrix}0\leq x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}\leq N,\\
x_{j}\geq\alpha j-\beta\;\forall\;1\leq j\leq n\end{matrix}\right\}\leq 3\frac{N^{n}}{n!}\frac{\beta(N-\alpha n+\beta)}{n\alpha^{2}}.

Moreover, if we further have that
\frac{\beta(N-\alpha n+\beta)}{n\alpha^{2}}\leq 1
, then

\vol\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):\begin{matrix}0\leq x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}\leq N,\\
x_{j}\geq\alpha j-\beta\;\forall\;1\leq j\leq n\end{matrix}\right\}\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{N^{n}}{n!}\frac{\beta(N-\alpha n+\beta)}{n\alpha^{2}}.

Proof.

We may shrink the region by
N
along each dimension. Now
(N,\alpha,\beta)=(1,\frac{\alpha}{N},\frac{\beta}{N})
. As the volume of
\{x_{i}=x_{j}\}
is zero, we may assume that they are all distinct without affecting the volume. Then we know that the volume of the desired region is
\frac{1}{n!}
of the volume of the following region:

R^{\prime}:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in[0,1]^{n}:\textrm{the $j$-th smallest element is at least $(\alpha j-\beta)/N$ for all $1\leq j\leq n$}\}.

As
\vol\{x_{j}=(\alpha j-\beta)/N\}=0
for each
j
, we can rewrite
R^{\prime}
as

R^{\prime\prime}:=\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in[0,1]^{n}:\textrm{the number of elements $\leq t$ is at most $\frac{Nt+\beta}{\alpha}$ for all $t\in[0,1]$}\right\}

without affecting the volume, i.e.
\vol(R^{\prime\prime})=\vol(R^{\prime})
. Compare it with Theorem
A.2
. Choose
u=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\geq C
and
w=\frac{N-\alpha n+\beta}{\alpha}\in[C,n/u]
. We denote the left hand side of (
A.4
) by
Q_{n}(u,w)
. We have
\vol(R^{\prime})=\vol(R^{\prime\prime})=Q_{n}(u,w)
. By Theorem
A.3
, there exists an absolute constant
C_{0}
such that

\left|Q_{n}(u,w)-\left(1-e^{-\frac{2uw}{n}}\right)\right|\leq C_{0}\frac{u+w}{n}.

Pick
C>8C_{0}
. Then
C_{0}\frac{u+w}{n}\leq\frac{1}{4}\frac{uw}{n}
. Since
1-x\leq e^{-x}
, we conclude that

\vol(R^{\prime})=Q_{n}(u,w)\leq 1-e^{-\frac{2uw}{n}}+C_{0}\frac{u+w}{n}\leq\frac{2uw}{n}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{uw}{n}<3\frac{uw}{n}=3\frac{\beta(N-\alpha n+\beta)}{n\alpha^{2}}.

With the extra condition that
\frac{\beta(N-\alpha n+\beta)}{n\alpha^{2}}\leq
, we have
\frac{uw}{n}\leq 1
. Also note that for
x\in[0,2]
,
e^{-x}\leq 1-\frac{x}{4}
. Therefore we conclude that, as
\frac{2uw}{n}\in[0,2]
,

\vol(R^{\prime})=Q_{n}(u,w)\geq\frac{uw}{2n}-C_{0}\frac{u+w}{n}\geq\frac{uw}{4n}=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\beta(N-\alpha n+\beta)}{n\alpha^{2}}.

Putting back the factor
\frac{N^{n}}{n!}
, we get the desired statements.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

There exist constants
\gamma
and
C_{0}>0
so that for any
x
large enough,
k=\frac{\log\log x}{\log 4}+O(1)
,
d=O(\log x)
, and
\beta\geq\gamma\log\log\log\log x
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k},p_{1}\cdots p_{k}<x\\
p_{j}\nmid d,\log\log p_{j}\geq j\log 4-\beta\;\forall\;1\leq j\leq k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}\geq(\log x)^{\frac{1+\log\log 4}{\log 4}}(\log\log x)^{-3/2}(\log\log\log x)^{-C_{0}}

(A.5)

First let us explain the intuition behind the right hand side of (
A.5
). If we replace the conditions by that
p_{j}<x
and
p_{j}\nmid d
for all
1\leq j\leq k
, then the sum would be

\left(\sum_{p<x,p\nmid d}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{k}=(\log\log x-O(\log\log\log d))^{k}=(\log\log x)^{k}(\log\log d)^{-O(1)}=(\log\log x)^{k-o(1)}.

Here we repeat each term on the left hand side of (
A.5
) by exactly
k!
times. Thus we should expect a formula of the form
\frac{(\log\log x)^{k-o(1)}}{k!}
. Now we aim to show that, with these extra conditions on the choices of
(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})
, the sum stays roughly the same.

Proof.

Motivated by
[
11
]
, we partition primes in
\mathcal{P}=\{p:p\nmid d\}
into consecutive parts. Fix
\lambda_{0}=1.9
, and for
j\geq 1
let
\lambda_{j}
to be the largest prime such that

\sum_{p\leq\lambda_{j}:p\nmid d}\frac{1}{p}\leq j.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{j}=\{p:\lambda_{j-1}<p\leq\lambda_{j},p\nmid d\}
. First we can estimate the size of
\lambda_{j}
. By maximality of
\lambda_{j}
we have

j-1<j-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}<\sum_{p\leq\lambda_{j}:p\nmid d}\frac{1}{p}\leq\sum_{p\leq\lambda_{j}}\frac{1}{p}

(A.6)

Meanwhile note that
\sum_{p|d}\frac{1}{p}=O(\log\log\omega(d))=O(\log\log\log\log x)
, so we have

\sum_{p\leq\lambda_{j}}\frac{1}{p}\leq\sum_{p\leq\lambda_{j}:p\nmid d}\frac{1}{p}+\sum_{p|d}\frac{1}{p}\leq j+O(\log\log\log\log x).

By Merten’s estimate (e.g. see Theorem 1.10 in
[
32
]
), we have

\sum_{p\leq\lambda_{j}}\frac{1}{p}=\log\log\lambda_{j}+O(1).

Thus we conclude that for some absolute constant
C>0
,

j-C<\log\log\lambda_{j}<j+C\log\log\log\log x.

Moreover, we know that by (
A.6
) we have

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{j}}\frac{1}{p}=\sum_{p\leq\lambda_{j}:p\nmid d}\frac{1}{p}-\sum_{p\leq\lambda_{j-1}:p\nmid d}\frac{1}{p}\geq j-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}-(j-1)=1-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}.

(A.7)

Fix positive integers
t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\dots\leq t_{k}
.

\mathcal{P}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k}):=\{(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\in\mathcal{P}^{k}:p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k},\quad p_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{t_{j}},\quad\forall 1\leq j\leq k\}.

We would like to reduce the summation in (
A.5
) over
(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})
into summation over
(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})
. Thus we would like to impose some restrictions on
(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})
so that the conditions
p_{1}\cdots p_{k}<x
,
p_{j}\nmid d
, and
\log\log p_{j}\geq j\log 4-\beta
are all satisfied. Clearly
p_{j}\nmid d
is satisfied for all
j
. Note that we have

\log\log p_{j}\geq\log\log\lambda_{t_{j}-1}\geq t_{j}-1-C,

so the condition
\log\log p_{j}\geq j\log 4-\beta
is satisfied if we require that
t_{j}\geq j\log 4-(\beta-1-C)
. Meanwhile, we also need
p_{1}\cdots p_{k}<x
. Note that we have

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log p_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log\lambda_{t_{j}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{t_{j}+C\log\log\log\log x}.

Hence if we define
M=\log\log x-C\log\log\log\log x
, then
p_{1}\cdots p_{k}<x
if

\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{t_{j}}\leq e^{M}.

(A.8)

Now we impose the restriction that
t_{j}
’s are not concentrated at small values or large values: if many
t_{j}
’s are small, then it is hard to take distinct
p_{j}
’s from the same
\mathcal{P}_{t}
; if many of them are large then (
A.8
) is disobeyed. Let
T
be a sufficiently large constant to be determined. We choose our
t_{j}
so that they only take values in
[T+1,M-T]
, and
t_{mT+1}\geq T+m+1
and
t_{k-mT}\leq M-T-m
for all positive integers
m<\frac{k}{T}
. Let
b_{t}
be the number of
1\leq j\leq k
with
t_{j}=T+t
for
1\leq t\leq M-2T
. As
t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{k}
, the numbers
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
are uniquely determined by
b_{1},\dots,b_{M-2T}
. Moreover, by our previous restriction, we know that
b_{t}\leq\min(Tt,T(M-2T-t+1),k)
for all
1\leq t\leq M-2T
. First we argue that by choosing
T
sufficiently large, (
A.8
) is satisfied. Indeed,

\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{t_{j}}=\sum_{t=1}^{M-2T}b_{t}e^{T+t}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{M-2T}T(M-2T-t+1)e^{T+t}=\sum_{s=1}^{M-2T}Tse^{M-T+1-s}<Te^{M-T+1}

(A.9)

which is less than
e^{M}
if we take
T>2
. Now we have

\begin{split}\sum_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\in\mathcal{P}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}&=\prod_{t=1}^{M-2T}\frac{1}{b_{t}!}\left(\sum_{p_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{t+T}}\frac{1}{p_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{t+T}\\
p_{2}\neq p_{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{2}}\cdots\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{b_{t}}\in\mathcal{P}_{t+T}\\
p_{b_{t}}\neq p_{1},\dots,p_{b_{t}-1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{b_{t}}}\right)\\
&\geq\prod_{t=1}^{M-2T}\frac{1}{b_{t}!}\left(\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{t+T}}\frac{1}{p}-\frac{b_{t}-1}{\lambda_{t+T-1}}\right)^{b_{t}}\\
[\mbox{by }\eqref{eqn:P_j-lower}]\quad&\geq\prod_{t=1}^{M-2T}\frac{1}{b_{t}!}\left(1-\frac{b_{t}}{\lambda_{t+T-1}}\right)^{b_{t}}\\
[\mbox{by }b_{t}\leq tT]\quad&\geq\prod_{t=1}^{M-2T}\frac{1}{b_{t}!}\left(1-\frac{tT}{\exp\exp(t+T-1-C)}\right)^{tT}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\prod_{t=1}^{M-2T}\frac{1}{b_{t}!}\end{split}

(A.10)

where the last inequality is derived by picking
T
sufficiently large. Omitting the coefficient
1/2
, (
A.10
) is the volume of the region

R(t_{1},\dots,t_{k}):=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\begin{matrix}0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{k}\leq M-2T,\\
t_{j}-T-1\leq x_{j}\leq t_{j}-T\;\forall\;1\leq j\leq k\end{matrix}\right\}.

Recall that
t_{j}
satisfies the following conditions:
t_{j}\geq j\log 4-(\beta-1-C)
for any
1\leq j\leq k
, and
t_{k-mT}\geq T+m+1
and
t_{k-mT}\leq M-T-m
for all positive integers
m<\frac{k}{T}
. Therefore we conclude that the union of
R(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})
over these choices of
(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})
contains the following region

R:=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\begin{matrix}0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{k}\leq M-2T,\\
x_{j}\geq j\log 4-(\beta-1-C)\;\forall\;1\leq j\leq k,\\
x_{mT+1}\geq T+m,x_{k-mT}\leq M-T-m\;\forall\;1\leq m<k/T\end{matrix}\right\}.

For simplicity let
\beta^{\prime}=\beta-1-C
. Let us define

R_{0}:=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{k}\leq M-2T,x_{j}\geq j\log 4-\beta^{\prime}\;\forall\;1\leq j\leq k\right\},

and for
1\leq m<\frac{k}{T}
,

V_{1}(m)=\vol(R_{0}\cap\{x_{mT+1}<m\});\quad V_{2}(m)=\vol(R_{0}\cap\{x_{k-mT}>M-2T-m\}).

Then we have
R=R_{0}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{1\leq m<k/T}V_{1}(m)\cup V_{2}(m)\right)
, so

\vol(R)\geq\vol(R_{0})-\sum_{1\leq m<k/T}V_{1}(m)-\sum_{1\leq m<k/T}V_{2}(m).

(A.11)

First we estimate
\vol(R_{0})
. By Corollary
A.3
, we may set
(\alpha,\beta,N,n)=(\log 4,\beta^{\prime},M-2T,k)
. When
\gamma>C
and
x
is sufficiently large, we have
\beta^{\prime}>\log 4>0
and

(M-2T)-k\log 4+\beta^{\prime}=(\gamma-C)\log\log\log\log x+O(1).

(A.12)

Hence the assumptions of Corollary
A.3
are met, so

\vol(R_{0})\gg\frac{(M-2T)^{k}}{k!}\frac{(\log\log\log\log x)^{2}}{k}.

For each
V_{1}(m)
, we notice that the first
mT+1
coordinates contribute volume at most
\frac{m^{mT+1}}{(mT+1)!}
, so

V_{1}(m)\leq\frac{m^{mT+1}}{(mT+1)!}\cdot\vol\{0\leq x_{mT+2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{k}\leq M-2T:x_{j}\geq\alpha j-\beta^{\prime}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ mT+2\leq j\leq k\}.

By changing the variables, we shall rewrite as
V_{1}(m)\leq\frac{m^{mT+1}}{(mT+1)!}S_{1}(m)
where

S_{1}(m):=\vol\left\{\begin{matrix}0\leq y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq\cdots\leq y_{k-mT-1}\leq M-2T:\\
y_{j}\geq(j+mT+1)\log 4-\beta^{\prime}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ 1\leq j\leq k-mT-1\end{matrix}\right\}.

Similarly for
V_{2}(m)
we have
V_{2}(m)\leq\frac{m^{mT+1}}{(mT+1)!}S_{2}(m)
where

S_{2}(m):=\vol\left\{\begin{matrix}0\leq y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq\cdots\leq y_{k-mT-1}\leq M-2T:y_{j}\geq j\log 4-\beta^{\prime}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ 1\leq j\leq k-mT-1\end{matrix}\right\}.

By comparing the definition of
S_{1}(m)
and
S_{2}(m)
, we see that
(j+mT+1)\log 4-\beta^{\prime}>j\log 4-\beta^{\prime}
, so the region of
S_{1}(m)
is a subset of that of
S_{2}(m)
. Therefore
S_{1}(m)\leq S_{2}(m)
. We next give an upper bound on
S_{2}(m)
.
Since
\beta\geq\gamma\log\log\log\log x
, the conditions in Corollary
A.3
is satisfied once
x
is sufficiently large (which is true as long as
L
is large enough). By applying Corollary
A.3

we have that

S_{2}(m)\leq\frac{(M-2T)^{k-1-mT}}{(k-1-mT)!}\cdot\frac{\beta^{\prime}(M-2T-\log 4(k-1-mT)+\beta^{\prime})}{(k-1-mT)\alpha^{2}}.

By using (
A.12
), the upper bound can be simplified as

\leq\frac{(M-2T)^{k-1-mT}}{(k-1-mT)!}\cdot\frac{\beta^{\prime}(\gamma-C)\log\log\log\log x+\beta^{\prime}(mT+O(1))}{k-1-mT}.

We next use the binomial identity
\binom{k}{mT+1}=\frac{k!}{(mT+1)!(k-1-mT)!}
to get upper bound

\frac{(M-2T)^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{(mT+1)!}{(M-2T)^{mT+1}}\cdot\binom{k}{mT+1}\cdot\frac{(\log\log\log\log x+mT)\log\log\log\log x}{k-1-mT},

and now we proceed by estimating binomial coefficients to get

S_{2}(m)\leq\frac{(M-2T)^{k}}{k!}\cdot(mT+1)!\left(\frac{k}{e(M-2T)mT}\right)^{mT+1}\cdot(\log\log\log\log x+mT)\log\log\log\log x.

For
i=1,2
, as
V_{i}(m)\leq\frac{m^{mT+1}}{(mT+1)!}S_{i}(m)\leq\frac{m^{mT+1}}{(mT+1)!}S_{2}(m)
, we have

\begin{split}V_{i}(m)&\leq\frac{(M-2T)^{k}}{k!}\cdot\left(\frac{k}{e(M-2T)T}\right)^{mT+1}\cdot(\log\log\log\log x+mT)\log\log\log\log x\\
&\leq\frac{(M-2T)^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{1}{T^{mT+1}}\cdot(\log\log\log\log x)^{2}(mT)^{2}.\end{split}

We next sum up all
V_{i}(m)
over
m<k/T
, and compare the sum with
\vol(R_{0})
. One has

\frac{1}{\vol(R_{0})}\sum_{1\leq m<k/T}V_{i}(m)\leq\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{(mT)^{2}}{T^{mT+1}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{T}}

for any
T\geq 8
. Thus, by choosing
T=8
, we conclude that for
i=1,2
,

\sum_{m\leq k/T}V_{i}(m)<\frac{1}{4}\vol(R_{0}).

Plugging these two bounds in (
A.11
), we get

\vol(R)\geq\frac{1}{2}\vol(R_{0})\gg\frac{(M-16)^{k}}{k!}\frac{(\log\log\log\log x)^{2}}{k}.

Recall that
M=\log\log x-C\log\log\log\log x
. By using Stirling formula, there exists some positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\begin{split}\vol(R)&\gg\left(\frac{e\log 4\log\log x-C_{1}\log\log\log\log x}{\log\log x}\right)^{\frac{\log\log x}{\log 4}}\frac{(\log\log\log\log x)^{2}}{(\log\log x)^{3/2}}\\
&\gg(\log x)^{\frac{1+\log\log 4}{\log 4}}\cdot\frac{(\log\log\log x)^{C_{2}}(\log\log\log\log x)^{2}}{(\log\log x)^{3/2}},\end{split}

which completes the proof.
∎
