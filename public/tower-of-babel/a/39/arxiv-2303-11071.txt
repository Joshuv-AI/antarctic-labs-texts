On Kripke, Vietoris and Hausdorff Polynomial Functors
Jiří Adámek, Stefan Milius, Lawrence S. Moss

Abstract
The Vietoris space of compact subsets of a given Hausdorff space yields an endofunctor 𝒱 \mathscr{V} on the category of Hausdorff spaces. Vietoris polynomial endofunctors on that category are built from 𝒱 \mathscr{V} , the identity and constant functors by forming products, coproducts and compositions. These functors are known to have terminal coalgebras and we deduce that they also have initial algebras. We present an analogous class of endofunctors on the category of extended metric spaces, using in lieu of 𝒱 \mathscr{V} the Hausdorff functor ℋ \mathcal{H} . We prove that the ensuing Hausdorff polynomial functors have terminal coalgebras and initial algebras. Whereas the canonical constructions of terminal coalgebras for Vietoris polynomial functors takes ω \omega steps, one needs ω + ω \omega+\omega steps in general for Hausdorff ones. We also give a new proof that the closed set functor on metric spaces has no fixed points.

Introduction

This paper presents results on terminal coalgebras and initial
algebras for certain endofunctors on the categories
\mathsf{Haus}
of
Hausdorff topological spaces and
\mathsf{Met}
of extended metric spaces.
These results are based on the terminal coalgebra construction first
presented by Adámek
[
2
]
(in dual form) and independently by
Barr
[
9
]
. Given an endofunctor
F
, iterate
F
on the unique
morphism
!\colon F1\to 1
to obtain the following

\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
-chain

1\xleftarrow{~!~}F1\xleftarrow{~F!~}FF1\xleftarrow{~FF!~}FFF1\xleftarrow{~FFF!~}\cdots

(1)

Assume that the limit exists, and denote it
by
V_{\omega}
and the limit cone by
\ell_{n}\colon V_{\omega}\to F^{n}1
(
n<\omega
).
We obtain a unique morphism
m\colon FV_{\omega}\to V_{\omega}
such that
for all
n\in\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
we have

F\ell_{n}=\big(FV_{\omega}\xrightarrow{~m~}V_{\omega}\xrightarrow{~\ell_{n+1}~}F^{n+1}1\big).

(2)

If
F
preserves the limit
V_{\omega}
, then
m
is an isomorphism.
Its inverse yields the terminal coalgebra
m^{-1}\colon V_{\omega}\to FV_{\omega}
; shortly
\nu F=V_{\omega}
, and we say
that the terminal coalgebra is
obtained in
\omega
steps
.

This technique of
finitary iteration
is the most basic and
prominent construction of terminal coalgebras. However finite
iteration requires that the limit in (
1
) exists and
also that it is preserved by the functor. It does
not
apply on

\mathsf{Set}
to the finite power set functor
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
. For that functor

FV_{\omega}\not\cong V_{\omega}
. However, a modification of finite
iteration does apply, as shown by Worrell
[
26
]
. One
makes a
second infinite iteration
, iterating
F
on the
morphism
m\colon FV_{\omega}\to V_{\omega}
rather than on

!\colon F1\to 1
, obtaining a chain

V_{\omega}\xleftarrow{~m~}V_{\omega+1}\xleftarrow{~Fm~}V_{\omega+2}\xleftarrow{~FFm~}\cdots

(3)

Its limit is denoted by

V_{\omega+\omega}=\lim_{n<\omega}V_{\omega+n}
with the limit
cone
\bar{l}_{n}\colon V_{\omega+\omega}\to V_{\omega+n}
, for

n<\omega
. Worrell’s insight was that this second limit,

V_{\omega+\omega}
, is preserved by every finitary functor. We prove
that this also works for functors built from
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
using product,
coproduct, and composition (which may be non-finitary).

These are
the
Kripke polynomial functors
mentioned in our title.

We are interested in other settings where terminal coalgebras may be
built using either the limit in (
1
) or the limit in
(
3
). We study fixed points of naturally occurring
endofunctors on Hausdorff spaces and metric spaces, endofunctors built
from the Vietoris and Hausdorff functors and several other natural
constructions.

In the category
\mathsf{Top}
a good analogy of
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
is the
Vietoris
functor

\mathscr{V}
assigning to every space
X
the space of all compact
subsets equipped with the Vietoris topology (
section 4
).
Hofmann et al.
[
12
]
define
Vietoris polynomial
functors
as those endofunctors on
\mathsf{Top}
built from
\mathscr{V}
, the constant
functors, and the identity functor, using product, coproduct, and
composition. We study this on the subcategory
\mathsf{Haus}
of Hausdorff
spaces and use that
\mathscr{V}\colon\mathsf{Haus}\to\mathsf{Haus}
preserves limits of

\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
-chains, a fact for which we present a new proof. This implies that for
Vietoris polynomial
functors
(defined as above but with
\mathscr{V}
in lieu of
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}})
, the
terminal coalgebra exists and is the limit
of (
1
). The existence of initial algebras follows.

We also present a result for the category
\mathsf{Met}
of metric spaces and
nonexpanding maps. The role of the Vietoris functor is played by the

Hausdorff functor

\mathcal{H}
assigning to every space
X
the space

\mathcal{H}X
of all compact subsets with the Hausdorff metric.

Other contributions.

In addition to the aforementioned results we show results obtained by
either varying the category or the endofunctor. For example, consider
again the Hausdorff polynomial functors. Whenever
F
is such a
functor and the constants involved in its construction are complete
spaces,
\nu F
again turns out to be complete. Analogous results hold
for compact spaces, or ultrametric spaces. Finally, we present a proof
of the description of
\nu\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
and
V_{\omega}
for
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}

exhibited by Worrell
[
26
]
(the latter without a proof).

We simplify a proof of a known negative result: the variation of
\mathcal{H}
obtained
by moving from compact sets to closed sets has no fixed points.

Related work.

Our work is more general and hence improves results of
Abramsky
[
1
]
, Hofmann et al.
[
12
]
, and
Worrell
[
26
]
.

There are numerous results about the existence and construction of
terminal coalgebras in the literature. At several places we discuss
other possible approaches to our results.

Preliminaries

We review a few preliminary points. We assume that readers are
familiar with basic notions of category theory as well as algebras and

We denote by
\mathsf{Set}
the category of
sets and functions,

.

Consider an

-chain

X_{0}\xleftarrow{~f_{0}~}X_{1}\xleftarrow{~f_{1}~}X_{2}\xleftarrow{~f_{2}~}\cdots

(4)

1.

In

(x_{n})_{n<\omega}
,

that are

f_{n}(x_{n+1})=x_{n}
for every

\ell_{n}\colon L\to X_{n}

\ell_{n}((x_{i}))=x_{n}
.

2.

In
\mathsf{Top}
, the limit is again carried
by the same set

as in
\mathsf{Set}
, and the limit projections

\ell_{n}^{-1}(U)
, for

.

3.

In
\mathsf{Met}
, the limit is again carried by the

, and the same limit projections

.

Smooth Monomorphisms.

In addition to terminal coalgebras, we also study initial algebras for the functors
of interest in this paper. For this, we call on a general result which allows one
to infer the existence of the initial algebra for an endofunctor
F
from the existence
of a terminal coalgebra for
F
(or in fact of any algebra with monic
structure).

For a class
\mathcal{M}
of monomorphisms we denote by
\Sub_{\mathcal{M}}(A)
the
collection of subobjects of
A
represented by monomorphisms from

\mathcal{M}
. To say that this is a dcpo means that it is a set which (when
ordered by factorization in the usual way) is a poset having directed joins.

Definition 2

(
[
4
, Def. 3.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a class of monomorphisms closed under isomorphisms and
composition.

1.

We say that an object
A
has
smooth
\mathcal{M}
-subobjects
provided that
\Sub_{\mathcal{M}}(A)
is a dcpo with
bottom
\bot
, where the least element
and directed joins are given by colimits of the corresponding
diagrams of subobjects.

2.

The class
\mathcal{M}
is
smooth
if every
object of
\mathscr{A}
has smooth
\mathcal{M}
-subobjects.

A category has
smooth monomorphisms
if the class of all
monomorphisms is smooth.

Example 3
.

1.

The categories
\mathsf{Set}
and
\mathsf{Top}
have smooth
monomorphisms, and so does the full subcategory of Hausdorff
spaces. This is easy to see.

2.

The category
\mathsf{Met}
also has smooth monomorphisms (these are the injective
non-expanding maps)
[
4
, Lemma A.1]
.

The full subcategory
\mathsf{CMS}
of complete metric spaces does not have
smooth monomorphisms. However, strong monomorphisms (isometric
embeddings) are smooth in both
\mathsf{Met}
and
\mathsf{CMS}

[
4
, Lemma A.2]
.

3.

Strong monomorphisms (subspace embeddings) in
\mathsf{Top}
are not
smooth
[
3
, Ex. 3.5]
.

Theorem 4

(
[
4
, Cor. 4.4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a smooth class of monomorphisms. If an endofunctor
F

preserving
\mathcal{M}
has a terminal coalgebra, then it has an initial algebra.

Note that loc. cit. states more: given any algebra

m\colon FA\rightarrowtail A
where
m
lies in
\mathcal{M}
, the initial algebra
exists and is a subalgebra of
(A,m)
.

Kripke Polynomial Functors

We turn to the first collection of functors mentioned in the title of
this paper: the Kripke polynomial functors on
\mathsf{Set}
. The name stems
from
Kripke structures
used in modal logic. Our definition below is a
slight generalization of the (finite) Kripke polynomial functors
presented by Jacobs
[
13
, Def. 2.2.1]
. (Kripke polynomial
functors using the full power-set functor were originally introduced
by Rößiger
[
21
]
.) We admit arbitrary products in lieu of just arbitrary
exponents.

Definition 5
.

The
Kripke polynomial functors

F
are the set functors built
from the finite power-set functor, constant functors and the
identity functor, by using product, coproduct and composition. In
other words, Kripke polynomial functors are built according to the
following grammar:

\textstyle F::=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}\mid A\mid\mathsf{Id}\mid\prod_{i\in I}F_{i}\mid\coprod_{i\in I}F_{i}\mid FF,

where
A
ranges over all sets (and is interpreted as a constant
functor) and
I
is an arbitrary index set.

Remark 6
.

The constant functors could be omitted from the grammar since they
are obtainable from the rest of the grammar. The constant functor
with value
1
is the empty product. For each set
A
, the constant
functor with value
A
is then a coproduct:
A=\coprod_{a\in A}1
.

Example 7
.

The Kripke polynomial functor
FX=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}(A\times X)
is the type
functor of finitely branching labelled transition systems with a set

A
of actions.

Remark 8
.

An endofunctor is
finitary
if it preserves directed colimits.
Worrell
[
26
]
proved that for every finitary set functor
the terminal coalgebra is obtained in
\omega+\omega
steps. We
prove a version of Worrell’s result but for Kripke polynomial
functors.

There are Kripke polynomial set functors which are not finitary. One
example of such a functor is
F(X)=X^{\mathds{N}}
, where
\mathds{N}
is the
set of natural numbers. There are also finitary set functors which
are not Kripke polynomial functors. One example (see
Appendix C
)
is the functor assigning to a set
X
the set of
nonempty

finite subsets of
X
.

Our proof below uses ideas from Worrell’s work
[
26
]
.

Theorem 9
.

Every Kripke polynomial functor
F
has a terminal coalgebra
obtained in
\omega+\omega
steps:
\nu F=V_{\omega+\omega}
.

Proof.

1.

We first observe that
F
preserves
monomorphisms and intersections of monomorphisms. This is clear
for constant functors and for
\mathsf{Id}
, and it
is easy to see for

\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
. Moreover, these properties are clearly preserved by
product, coproduct and composition.

2.

Let
(X_{n})_{n<\omega}
be an

\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
-chain in
\mathsf{Set}
. Then the canonical morphism

m\colon F(\lim X_{n})\to\lim FX_{n}
is monic. This is obvious for
constant functors and
\mathsf{Id}
. Let us check it for
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
.
Denote the limit projections by
\ell_{n}\colon\lim X_{n}\to X_{n}

and
p_{n}\colon\lim\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}X_{n}\to\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}X_{n}
(
n<\omega
); the
canonical morphism
m
is unique such that

p_{n}\cdot m=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}\ell_{n}
. Now given
S\neq T
in

\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}(\lim X_{n})
, without loss of generality we can pick

x\in T\setminus S
. Using that the
\ell_{n}
are jointly monic,
for every
s\in S
we can choose
n<\omega
such that

\ell_{n}(x)\neq\ell_{n}(s)
. Since
S
is finite, this choice can
be performed independently of
s\in S
. Thus

\ell_{n}(x)\not\in\ell_{n}[S]
, and hence

\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}\ell_{n}(T)\neq\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}(S)
. Thus,
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}\ell_{n}
is a jointly
monic family. Since
p_{n}\cdot m=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}\ell_{n}
, we see that
m

is monic.

3.

An induction on Kripke polynomial functors

F
now shows that
m\colon V_{\omega+1}\to V_{\omega}
is monic.
We have seen this for the base case functors in

Item 2
. The desired property that
m
is monic is
preserved by products, coproducts and composition. In particular,
for a composition
FG
note that the canonical morphism for
FG

is the composition

FG(\lim X_{n})\xrightarrow{~Fm~}F(\lim GX_{n})\xrightarrow{~m^{\prime}~}\lim FGX_{n},

where
m
is the canonical morphism for
G
w.r.t. the given

\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
-chain and
m^{\prime}
the one for
F
and the

\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
-chain
(GX_{n})_{n<\omega}
. So this morphism

m^{\prime}\cdot Fm
is monic since
both
m
and
m^{\prime}
are so and
F
preserves monomorphisms by

Item 1
.

4.

Since
F
preserves monomorphisms, we see
that
Fm
,
FFm
etc. are monic. We obtain a decreasing chain of
subobjects
V_{\omega+n}\rightarrowtail V_{\omega}
. Therefore, the
limit
V_{\omega+\omega}=\lim_{n<\omega}V_{\omega+n}
is
simply the intersection of these subobjects. From

Item 1
we know that
F
preserves this limit. It
follows that
\nu F=V_{\omega+\omega}
, as desired.∎

Every Kripke polynomial functor

Vietoris Polynomial Functors

Hofmann et
al.
[
12
]
proved that Vietoris polynomial functors on the category

\mathsf{Haus}
of Hausdorff spaces have terminal coalgebras obtained in

\omega
steps. Our proof is slightly different from theirs
because we wish to avoid a result stated by
Zenor
[
27
]

whose proof is incomplete.

Recall that a
base
of a topology is a collection
\mathcal{B}

of open sets such that every open set is a union of members of

\mathcal{B}
. A
subbase
is a collection of open sets whose
finite intersections form a base. For every collection
\mathcal{B}

of subsets of the space, there is a smallest topology for which

\mathcal{B}
is a (sub)base, the family of unions of finite
intersections from
\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 12
.

1.

Let
X
be a topological space. We denote by
\mathscr{V}X
the space of
compact subsets of
X
equipped with the “hit-and-miss”
topology. This topology has as a subbase all sets of the following forms:

\displaystyle U^{\Diamond}

\displaystyle=\{R\in VX:R\cap U\neq\emptyset\}

\displaystyle\text{($R$ hits $U$)},

(5)

\displaystyle U^{\Box}

\displaystyle=\{R\in VX:R\subseteq U\}

\displaystyle\text{($R$ misses $X\setminus U$),}

where
U
ranges over the open sets of
X
. We call
\mathscr{V}X
the
Vietoris space of

X
, also known as the
hyperspace
of
X
.

2.

Recalling that the image of a compact set
under a continuous function is also compact,
for a continuous function
f\colon X\to Y
we put
\mathscr{V}f(A)=f[A]
for every compact subset
A
of
X
.

Remark 13
.

1.

For a compact Hausdorff space
X
, Vietoris
[
25
]

defined
\mathscr{V}X
to consist of all
closed
subsets of
X
. These
are the same as the compact subsets in this case. In the
coalgebraic literature,

has also mostly been studied for spaces

which are compact Hausdorff. However,
the “classic Vietoris space” (using closed subsets) does not
yield a functor on

(see Hofmann et

[
12

). Hofmann et
al.
[
12

call the functor

in

.

2.

Michael
[

proved that

is Hausdorff iff so is
\mathscr{V}X
.

3.

Vietoris
[
25
]

originally proved that for a compact Hausdorff space
X
(the
classic Vietoris space)
\mathscr{V}X
is compact Hausdorff, too.

4.

A
Stone space
is a compact Hausdorff space
having a base of clopen sets.
If
X
is a Stone
space, so is
\mathscr{V}X
;
see
[

or
[

Proposition 14
.

For every continuous function
f\colon X\to Y
and every open

U\subseteq Y
,
(f^{-1}(U))^{\Diamond}=(\mathscr{V}f)^{-1}(U^{\Diamond})
,
and
(f^{-1}(U))^{\Box}=(\mathscr{V}f)^{-1}(U^{\Box})
.

Proof.

Let
R\in\mathscr{V}X
. Observe that

R\cap f^{-1}(U)\neq\emptyset\iff f[R]\cap U\neq\emptyset\iff f[R]\in U^{\Diamond}\iff R\in(\mathscr{V}f)^{-1}(U^{\Diamond}).

This proves our first assertion for all

R\subseteq f^{-1}(U)\iff f[R]\subseteq U\iff f[R]\in U^{\Box}\iff R\in(\mathscr{V}f)^{-1}(U^{\Box}).

Corollary 15
.

The mappings

.

1.

The full subcategories

2.

If an

3.

If

\mathcal{B}
which is closed under finite unions, then the sets

and

already form a

\mathscr{V}X
.
Indeed, given a set

of open subsets of

(\bigcup\mathcal{S})^{\Diamond}=\bigcup\{U^{\Diamond}:U\in\mathcal{S}\}
. Moreover, it is easy to see that

\textstyle(\bigcup\mathcal{S})^{\Box}=\bigcup\{\big(\bigcup\mathcal{F}\big)^{\Box}:\text{$\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{S}$ finite}\};

“

” is trivial, and for “
\subseteq

. Hence, if

consists of

, then

due to

is a union of sets of the form

for

.

Proposition 18
.

The functor
\mathscr{V}\colon\mathsf{Haus}\to\mathsf{Haus}
preserves limits of

\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
-chains.

Proof.

Consider an
\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
-chain as in (
4
). Let

M=\lim\mathscr{V}X_{n}
, with limit cone
r_{n}\colon M\to\mathscr{V}X_{n}
. Let

m\colon\mathscr{V}L\to M
be the unique continuous map such that

\mathscr{V}\ell_{n}=r_{n}\cdot m
for all
n<\omega
. We shall prove that
m

is a bijection and then that its inverse is continuous, which proves
that
m
is an isomorphism.

1.

Injectivity of
m
follows from the
fact that
\mathscr{V}\ell_{n}

the same. An easy induction then shows that every Hausdorff
polynomial functor is accessible, so that the Limit Theorem can be
applied. However, our elementary proof shows that the terminal
coalgebra chain converges in
\omega+\omega
steps. The proof of
Makkai and Paré’s Limit Theorem does not yield such a bound.

6
Variation: the Closed Subset Functor on
\mathsf{Met}

We have been concerned with the Hausdorff functor taking a metric
space
M
to the space of its nonempty compact subsets. For two
variations, let us consider
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}\colon\mathsf{Met}\to\mathsf{Met}
taking
M
to the
set of its
closed
subsets, and its subfunctor

\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}\colon\mathsf{Met}\to\mathsf{Met}
taking
M
to the set of its nonempty
closed subsets. Both
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}M
and
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}M
are given the
Hausdorff metric. For a non-expanding map
f\colon X\to Y
, the
non-expanding map
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}f\colon\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}X\to\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}Y
sends a
closed subset
S
of
X
to the closure of
f[S]
. This makes

\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}
and
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}
functors. Due to the empty set,
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}
is a
closer analog of
\mathcal{H}
. It is natural to ask whether the positive results of
Section
5
hold for these functors
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}
and

\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}
. As proved by van Breugel
[
23
, Prop. 8]
, the
functor
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}
has no terminal coalgebra. Turning to
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}
,
this functor has an initial algebra given by the empty metric space
and a terminal coalgebra carried by a singleton metric space. But

\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}
has no other fixed points (see van Breugel et
al.
[
24
, Cor. 5]
), where an object
X
is a
fixed
point
of an endofunctor
F
if
FX\cong X
. We provide below a
different, shorter proof.

Remark 45
.

1.

A subset
X
of a metric space is
\delta
-discrete
if
whenever
x\neq y
are elements of
X
,
d(x,y)\geq\delta
.
Every subset of a
\delta
-discrete set is

\delta
-discrete, and every such set is closed. Moreover, if

C
and
D
are different subsets of a
\delta
-discrete set,
then
\bar{d}(C,D)\geq\delta
.

2.

A subset
S
of an ordinal
i
is
cofinal
if for all
j<i

there is some
k\in S
with
j\leq k<i
. If
S
is not cofinal,
then its complement
i\setminus S
must be so. (But it is possible
that both
S
and
i\setminus S
are cofinal in
i
.)

Theorem 46
.

There is no isometric embedding
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}M\to M
when
|M|\geq 2
.

Proof.

Suppose towards a contradiction that
\iota\colon\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}M\to M

were an isometric embedding where
|M|\geq 2
. If all distances in

M
are
0
or
\infty
, then
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}M
is the nonempty power set
of
M
. In this case, our result follows from the fact that for

|M|\geq 2
,
M
has more nonempty subsets than
elements.
Thus we fix distinct points
a,b\in M
of finite
distance, and put
\delta=d(a,b)/2
. Let

A=\{x\in M:d(x,a)\leq\delta\}
, and let
B=M\setminus A
.
(In case
d(a,b)=\infty
, we need to adjust this by setting

\delta=\infty
, and
B
to be the points whose distance to
a
is
finite. But we shall not present the argument in this case.)

We proceed to define an ordinal-indexed sequence of elements

x_{i}\in M
. We also prove that each set
S_{i}=\{x_{j}:j<i\}

is
\delta
-discrete, and we put

X_{i}=\begin{cases}A&\text{if $\{j<i:x_{j}\in A\}$ is cofinal in $i$}\\
B&\text{else}.\end{cases}

For
i=0
, put
x_{0}=\iota(\{a,b\})
. Given an ordinal
i>0
,
we put

x_{i}=\iota(X_{i}\cap S_{i}).

Being nonempty (since
i>0
) and

\delta
-discrete,
X_{i}\cap S_{i}
lies in
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}M
.

The remainder of our proof consists of showing that for every ordinal
i
:

d(x_{j},x_{k})\geq\delta\qquad\text{for $0\leq j<k\leq i$}.

We proceed by transfinite induction.
Assume that our claim holds for every
k<i
and then prove it for

i
. The base case
i=0
is trivial. For
i>0
, note first that it
follows from the induction hypothesis that
S_{i}
is
\delta
-discrete.

Hence, we need only verify that
d(x_{j},x_{i})\geq\delta
when

0\leq j<i
. We argue the case
X_{i}=A
; when
X_{i}=B
, the
argument is similar, mutatis mutandis. For
j=0
, recall that

x_{0}=\iota(\{a,b\})
and
x_{i}=\iota(A\cap S_{i})
. Since
b
has
distance at least
\delta
from every element of
A
, we obtain

\bar{d}(\{a,b\},A\cap S_{i})\geq\delta
. As
\iota
is an
isometric embedding, this distance is also
d(x_{0},x_{i})
. Now let
j>0
.
Since we have
X_{i}=A
, let
k
be such that
j\leq k<i
and
x_{k}\in A
.
Now either
x_{j}=\iota(A\cap S_{j})
or else

x_{j}=\iota(B\cap S_{j})
.

In the first case, note that
x_{k}\in A\cap S_{i}
since
k<i
, and

x_{k}\notin S_{j}
by the definition of
S_{j}
since
k\geq j
. So

A\cap S_{j}
and
A\cap S_{i}
are different nonempty subsets of the

\delta
-discrete set
S_{i}
. Hence, the distance between these sets
is at least
\delta
, and therefore we have

d(x_{j},x_{i})\geq\delta
.

In the second case,
B\cap S_{j}
is a nonempty subset of
B
, and
thus again it not equal to
A\cap S_{i}
. So again we see that

d(x_{j},x_{i})=\bar{d}(B\cap S_{j},A\cap S_{i})\geq\delta
.

We now obtain the desired contradiction since
(x_{i})
is an
ordinal-indexed sequence of pairwise distinct elements of
M
.
∎

Corollary 47
.

1.

The functor
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}\colon\mathsf{Met}\to\mathsf{Met}
has no fixed points
except the empty set and the singletons.

2.

The functor
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}\colon\mathsf{Met}\to\mathsf{Met}
admits
no isometric embedding
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}M\to M
, whence has no fixed point.

Proof.

The first item is immediate from
Theorem 46
. For the second
one, observe that the inclusion map
e\colon\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}M\to\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}M

is an isometric embedding. Assuming that there were an isometric
embedding
\iota\colon\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}M\to M
, we see that
M
cannot be
empty (since
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}M
is nonempty) or a singleton (since then

|\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}M|=2
). Hence
|M|\geq 2
. Moreover, we obtain an
isometric embedding
\iota\cdot e\colon\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}^{\prime}M\to M
,
contradicting
Theorem 46
.∎

7
Summary

We have investigated versions of the finite power set functor for the
categories
\mathsf{Haus}
and
\mathsf{Met}
. Our main results are that the Vietoris
functor
\mathscr{V}
, and indeed all Vietoris polynomial functors, have
terminal coalgebras obtained in
\omega
steps of the
terminal-coalgebra chain. The same holds for the Hausdorff polynomial
functors on
\mathsf{Met}
, but the iteration takes
\omega+\omega
steps and
so the underlying reasons are different.

Our work on the Kripke and Hausdorff polynomial functors highlights a
technique which we feel could be of wider interest. To prove that a
terminal coalgebra exists in a situation where the limit of the

\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
-chain (
1
) is not preserved by the
functor, one could try to find preservation properties which imply
that the limit of the
\omega^{\mathsf{op}}
-chain
(V_{\omega+n})_{n}
was
preserved. In
\mathsf{Set}
, we used finitarity and preservation of
monomorphisms and intersections, and in
\mathsf{Met}
we used preservation of
intersections, isometric embeddings, and isometric cones.

We have also seen that for the functor
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{cl}}
on
\mathsf{Met}
, there is no
fixed point and hence no terminal coalgebra. We leave open the
question of whether every Vietoris polynomial functor on
\mathsf{Top}
has a
terminal coalgebra.

Appendix A
Trees and the Limit of the Terminal-Coalgebra Chain for
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}

We give the description of
V_{\omega}

for
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}
due to Worrell
[
26
]
.
We provide a full
exposition to the results which Worrell stated without proof.

A
tree
is a directed graph
t
with a distinguished node

\mbox{\sf root}(t)
from which every other node can be reached by a unique
directed path.
Every tree in our sense must have a root, so there is no
empty tree. All of our trees are
unordered
. We always
identify isomorphic trees.

Definition 48
.

1.

We use the notation
t_{x}
for the subtree of
t
rooted in the
node
x
of
t
.

2.

A tree
t
is
extensional
if for every node
x

distinct children
y
and
z
of
x
give different (that is,
non-isomorphic) subtrees
t_{y}
and
t_{z}
.

3.

A
graph bisimulation
between two trees
t
and
u
is a
relation between the nodes of
t
and the nodes of
u
with the
property that whenever
x
and
y
are related: (a) every child of
x

is related to some child of
y
, and (b) every child of
y
is
related to some child of
x
.

4.

A
tree bisimulation
between two trees
t
and
u
is a
graph bisimulation
R
with the additional properties that that

(a)

The nodes
\mbox{\sf root}(t)
and
\mbox{\sf root}(u)

are related; the roots are not
related to other nodes;
and

(b)

whenever two nodes are related, their parents are also related.

5.

Two trees are
tree bisimilar
if there is a tree
bisimulation between them.

6.

A tree
t
is
strongly extensional

if every tree bisimulation on it is a
subset of the diagonal

\Delta_{t}=\{(x,x):x\in t\}.

In other words,
t
is strongly extensional iff distinct children

x
and
y
of the same node define subtrees
t_{x}
and
t_{y}
which
are
not
tree bisimilar.

Remark 49
.

1.

Every composition and every union of
tree bisimulations is again a tree bisimulation. In addition, the
opposite relation of every tree bisimulation is a tree bisimulation:
if
R
is a tree bisimulation from
t
to
u
, then
R^{\mathsf{op}}
is a
tree bisimulation from
u
to
t
. Consequently, the largest tree
bisimulation on every tree is an equivalence relation.

2.

A subtree
s
of a strongly extensional tree
t
is strongly
extensional. Indeed,
if
R
is a
tree bisimulation on
s
,
then
R\cup\Delta_{t}
is a tree bisimulation on
t
.
Since
R\cup\Delta_{t}\subseteq\Delta_{t}
, we have
R\subseteq\Delta_{s}
.

Lemma 50
.

If
t
and
u
are strongly extensional and related by a tree
bisimulation, then we have
t=u
.

Proof.

Let
R
be a tree bisimulation between
t
and

u
. By
49
,
R^{\mathsf{op}}\cdot R
is a tree
bisimulation on
t
, whence
R^{\mathsf{op}}\cdot R\subseteq\Delta_{t}
by
strong extensionality.
But every node of
t
is related to at least
one node of
u
(use induction on the depth of nodes) implying that
R^{\mathsf{op}}\cdot R=\Delta_{t}
. Similarly,
R\cdot R^{\mathsf{op}}=\Delta_{u}
. Thus,
R
(is a
function and it) is an isomorphism of trees, and we identify such
trees.
∎

Notation 51
.

1.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be the class of trees. We define maps

\partial_{n}\colon\mathcal{T}\to V_{n}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{n}1
as follows:
\partial_{0}

is the unique map to
1
, and given the map
\partial_{n}
and a tree

t
, we put

\partial_{n+1}(t)=\{\partial_{n}(t_{x}):\text{$x$ is a child of
the root of $t$}\}.

On the right we have a subset of
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{n}1
, and this is an element
of
\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{n+1}1
.

2.

The trees
t
and
u
are
Barr equivalent
provided that

\partial_{n}t=\partial_{n}u
for all
n
. We write
t\approx u
in
this case.

3.

For every tree
t
, we define maps

\rho^{t}_{n}\colon t\to V_{n}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{n}1
in the following
way:
\rho^{t}_{0}
is the unique map
t\to 1
, and for all
nodes
x
of
t
,

\rho^{t}_{n+1}(x)=\{\rho^{t}_{n}(y):\mbox{$y$ is a
child of $x$ in $t$}\}
. This family of maps
\rho^{t}_{n}

is a cone: we have

\rho^{t}_{n}=v_{m,n}\cdot\rho^{t}_{m}
for every
connecting map
v_{m,n}\colon\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{m}1\to\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{n}1
,

m\geq n
. Hence, there is a unique map

\rho^{t}_{\omega}\colon t\to V_{\omega}
such that

\ell_{n}\cdot\rho^{t}_{\omega}=\rho^{t}_{n}
for all
n
.

Remark 52
.

Note that
V_{n}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}^{n}1
may be described as the set of all
extensional trees of height at most
n
. Indeed,
1
is described as
the singleton set consisting of the root-only tree, and every finite
set of extensional trees in
V_{n+1}=\mathscr{P}_{\mathsf{f}}V_{n}
is represented by
the extensional tree obtained by tree-tupling the trees from the given set.

Remark 53
.

1.

If

\rho^{t}_{n+1}(a)=\rho^{t}_{n+1}(b)
, then for all
children
a^{\prime}
of
a
, there is some child
b^{\prime}
of
b
and

\rho^{t}_{n}(a^{\prime})=\rho^{t}_{n}(b^{\prime})
. This is easy
to see from the definition of
\rho^{t}_{n+1}
.

2.

For all trees
t
,

\rho^{t}_{i}(\mbox{\sf root}(t))=\partial_{i}(t).

Furthermore, let
b\colon t\to\mathcal{T}
be given
by
b(x)=t_{x}
. Then

\rho^{t}_{i}=\partial_{i}\cdot b
.

Definition 54
.

Let
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,
be a sequence of
nodes in a tree
t
, and let
y
also be a node in
t
. We write

\lim x_{n}=y

to mean
that for every
n
there is some
m
such that

\rho^{t}_{n}(x_{p})=\rho^{t}_{n}(y)
whenever
p\geq m
.

A tree

is
compactly branching
if for all nodes
x
of

t
, the set of children of
x
is
sequentially compact
:
for every sequence of
(y_{n})
of children of
x

there is
a subsequence
(w_{n})
of
(y_{n})
and
some child
z
of
x
such that
\lim{w_{n}}={z}
.
