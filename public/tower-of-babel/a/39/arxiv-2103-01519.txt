Hessian Eigenspectra of More Realistic Nonlinear Models
Zhenyu Liao, Michael W. Mahoney

Abstract
Given an optimization problem, the Hessian matrix and its eigenspectrum can be used in many ways, ranging from designing more efficient second-order algorithms to performing model analysis and regression diagnostics. When nonlinear models and non-convex problems are considered, strong simplifying assumptions are often made to make Hessian spectral analysis more tractable. This leads to the question of how relevant the conclusions of such analyses are for more realistic nonlinear models. In this paper, we exploit deterministic equivalent techniques from random matrix theory to make a precise characterization of the Hessian eigenspectra for a broad family of nonlinear models, including models that generalize the classical generalized linear models, without relying on strong simplifying assumptions used previously. We show that, depending on the data properties, the nonlinear response model, and the loss function, the Hessian can have qualitatively different spectral behaviors: of bounded or unbounded support, with single- or multi-bulk, and with isolated eigenvalues on the left- or right-hand side of the bulk. By focusing on such a simple but nontrivial nonlinear model, our analysis takes a step forward to unveil the theoretical origin of many visually striking features observed in more complex machine learning models.

Introduction

The Hessian matrix is ubiquitous in applied mathematics, statistics, and machine learning (ML).
Given a (loss) function
L(\mathbf{w})
with some parameters
\mathbf{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, the Hessian
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
is defined as the second derivative of the objective function with respect to the model parameter, i.e.,
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w})=\partial L(\mathbf{w})/(\partial\mathbf{w}\cdot\partial\mathbf{w}^{\sf T})
.
When a ML model is being trained, it is common to parameterize that model by
\mathbf{w}
, and then train that model by minimizing some (smooth) loss function
L(\mathbf{w})
, with associated Hessian matrix
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w})
, by backpropagating the error to improve
\mathbf{w}

[
40
,
100
]
.
Alternatively, once a ML model is trained, the Hessian matrix
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w})
(and the related Fisher information matrix
[
89
,
91
]
) can be examined to identify outliers, perform diagnostics, and/or engage in other sorts of uncertainty quantification and model validation
[
44
,
102
,
85
]
.

For convex problems, the Hessian
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{w})
provides detailed information on how to adjust the gradient to achieve improved convergence, e.g., as in Newton-like methods.
For non-convex problems, the properties of the local loss “landscape” around a given point
\mathbf{w}
in the parameter space is of central significance
[
27
,
56
,
21
,
61
,
101
,
103
,
102
]
.
In this case, most obviously, the signs of the smallest and largest Hessian eigenvalue can be used to test whether a given
\mathbf{w}
is a local maximum, local minimum, or a saddle point (i.e., the second-derivative test).
More subtly, the Hessian eigenvalue distribution characterizes the local curvature of the loss function and provides direct access to, for instance, the fraction of negative Hessian eigenvalues.
This quantity determines the number of (local) descent directions, a quantity that is directly connected to the rates of convergence of various optimization algorithms
[
49
,
23
]
, as well as the large-scale structure of the configuration space
[
13
,
4
]
.

For theoretical analysis of neural network (NN) models, Hessian eigenspectra are often assumed to follow well-known random matrix distributions such the Marc̆enko–Pastur law
[
69
]
or the Wigner’s semicircle law
[
93
]
.
This enables one to use Random Matrix Theory (RMT), but it involves (for NNs, at least) making relatively strong simplifying assumptions
[
80
,
81
,
24
]
.
A somewhat more realistic setup involves using a so-called
spiked model
(or a spiked covariance model)
[
8
,
11
,
63
]
.
In this case, the matrix follows a
signal-plus-noise
model and consists of
full rank
random noise matrix and
low rank
statistical information structure. The “signal” eigenvalues are generally larger than the noisy “bulk” eigenvalues; and the maximum eigenvalues, when isolated from the bulk, are referred to as the “spikes.”
A substantial theory-practice gap exists, however.
In both toy examples
[
41
]
, as well as state-of-the-art NN models
[
101
,
103
,
102
,
100
,
86
,
30
]
, the strong simplifying assumptions are far from realistic or satisfactory.
(A similar theory-practice gap has been observed for other NN matrices to which RMT has been applied, perhaps most notably weight matrices
[
70
,
71
]
.)
A more precise understanding of the Hessian eigenspectra (and its dependence on input data structure, the underlying response model and model parameters, as well as the loss function) for more realistic ML models is needed.

1.1
Our approach

In this article, we address these issues, in a setting that is simple enough to be analytically tractable but complex enough to shed light on much more realistic large-scale models.
We consider a family of generalized generalized linear models (G-GLMs) that extends the popular generalized linear model (GLM)
[
29
,
44
]
; and we show that, even for such simple models, the key simplifying assumptions used in previous theoretical analyses of Hessian eigenspectra, can be very inexact.
In particular, apart from a few special cases (including linear least squares and the logistic regression model in the case of homogeneous features), most Hessians of G-GLM are
not
close to the Marc̆enko–Pastur and/or the semicircle law.
Instead, the corresponding Hessian depends in a complicated way on the interplay between the input feature structure, the underlying response model, and the loss function.
These tradeoffs can be precisely characterized by the proposed analysis in the G-GLM model we consider.

The G-GLM describes a generalized linear relation between the input feature
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and the corresponding response
y_{i}
, in the sense that there exists some parameters
\mathbf{w}_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
such that for given
\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}_{i}
, the response
y_{i}
is independently drawn from

y_{i}\sim f(y\mid\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}_{i}),

(1)

for some conditional density function
f(\cdot\mid\cdot)
.
This setup extends GLMs such as the logistic model

\mathbb{P}(y=1\mid\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x})=(1+e^{-\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}})^{-1},\quad y\in\{-1,1\},

(2)

and it covers a large family of models in many applications in statistics and ML.
Other examples of G-GLM models include:

•

the (noisy) nonlinear factor model
[

]
, where

•

•

the single-layer NN model

For a given training set

\textstyle\min_{\mathbf{w}}L(\mathbf{w})=\min_{\mathbf{w}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(y_{i},\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}_{i}),

In many applications, however, the optimization problem in (

Organization of the paper.

We discuss related previous efforts and preliminaries in Section

Related work

Random matrix theory.

Random matrices of the type (
4

[

,

]
.
This model is of the form
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{Z}^{\sf T}\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}

Our results generalize this, in the sense that we allow the matrix

This is of direct interest for the Hessian matrix of the G-GLM.

and

(Theorem

This is directly related to the popular spectral initialization method used in non-convex optimization

Statistical physicists are interested in the energy “landscape” of random functions, by means of studying the Hessian of such functions

Similar techniques were then proposed to provide a “good” initialization for gradient descent in non-convex problems such as phase retrieval

2.2
Spiked random matrix model

Define the
empirical spectral measure
of a random matrix
\mathbf{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}

\mu_{\mathbf{M}}\equiv\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{M})}

(5)

as the normalized counting measure of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{M})
of
\mathbf{M}
. If the random
\mu_{\mathbf{M}}
converges to a (non-random) probability measure
\mu
as
p\to\infty
, we say
\mu
is the
limiting spectral measure
of the random matrix
\mathbf{M}
. Examples of such limiting laws include the popular Marc̆enko-Pastur law
[
69
]
and Wigner’s semicircle law
[
93
]
.

In many applications, instead of the (limiting) eigenvalue distribution, one may be more interested in the behavior of some particular eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs. For a standard (noise-like) random matrix
\mathbf{M}
, the term “
spiked random matrix model
”
[
51
,
11
,
6
]
is used to refer to the
low rank
update of
\mathbf{M}
, for instance the additive perturbation of the type
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{A}
for some (deterministic)
\mathbf{A}
such that
\rank(\mathbf{A})=K
is fixed as
p\to\infty
. This type of random matrix model plays a fundamental role in the analysis of principle component analysis (PCA)
[
52
,
53
]
, sparse PCA
[
2
,
68
,
15
]
and Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA)
[
47
,
10
,
54
]
in high dimensions. As a concrete example, for
\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{I}_{p})
we can write
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]=\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\sf T}+\mathbf{Z}
for
\mathbf{Z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times n}
having i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, so that the sample covariance matrix
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\sf T}+\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\sf T}+\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Z}^{\sf T}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\sf T}
follows a spiked model that is an (at most) rank-two additive perturbation of the standard Wishart random matrix
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\sf T}
. The spiked model is particularly popular and useful in charactering signal-plus-noise type behavior in the features, by focusing on the isolated eigenvalues (i.e., the “spikes”) outside the main eigenspectrum bulk.
Precisely, if the signal strength
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|
is larger than a certain threshold (that depends on the dimension ratio
p/n
), an informative eigenvalue
\hat{\lambda}
of
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}
can be observed to “jump out” from the noisy main bulk, with the associated eigenvector
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
“aligned” to
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, in the sense that the “cosine-similarly”
\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\mu}/\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|\gg 0
as
n,p\to\infty
.

variance (so that
\|\mathbf{v}\|=O(1)
with high probability), we have, for
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|=O(1)
that
\mathbf{v}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\mu}\to 0
as
p\to\infty
; that is, a random vector is
almost orthogonal
to any deterministic or
independent
random vector in high dimensions.

This type of
phase transition
behavior has been extensively studied in the RMT literature
[
8
,
11
,
63
]
. In this article, we show two
different
types of spikes and their corresponding phase transitions: the first well-known spike due to data signal
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, which follows the classical BPP phase transition
[
8
]
; and a second less-well-known spike, which arises from the underlying (nonlinear) response model (
1
), even in the absence of any signal, and which follows a very different phase transition behavior. This is discussed at length with numerical evaluations in Section
4
.

2.3
Deterministic equivalent

In this work, a unified analysis framework is proposed to assess simultaneously the limiting spectral measure and the behavior of the (possible) isolated eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs of the Hessian-type matrix in (
4
). This analysis is based on the so-called deterministic equivalent technique
[
43
,
25
]
that provides asymptotic characterization of the (expectation of the) resolvent
\mathbf{Q}(z)\equiv(\mathbf{H}-z\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}
of the random Hessian matrix
\mathbf{H}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
. With additional concentration of measure arguments
[
60
,
76
]
, the limiting spectral measure, the isolated eigenvalue location, as well as the eigenvector projection onto a given deterministic vector, while all being random, become asymptotically deterministic and fully accessible via the analysis of
\mathbf{Q}(z)
in the
n,p\to\infty
limit. More precisely, we have the following.

1.

The normalized trace
\frac{1}{p}\tr\mathbf{Q}(z)
characterizes the Stieltjes transform
m_{\mu}(z)=\int(t-z)^{-1}\mu(dt)
of the limiting spectral measure
\mu
of
\mathbf{H}
, from which
\mu
can be recovered, for
a,b
continuity points of
\mu
, via
\mu([a,b])=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{a}^{b}\Im[m_{\mu}(x+\imath\varepsilon)]\,dx
.

2.

Solving the determinant equation
\det(\mathbf{H}-\lambda\mathbf{I}_{p})=0
for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
outside the limiting spectral support
\supp(\mu)
(that is, not in the bulk eigenspectrum) reveals the asymptotic location of the isolated eigenvalue
\hat{\lambda}
of
\mathbf{H}
.

3.

For
(\hat{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{u}})
an eigenvalue-eigenvector pair of interest of
\mathbf{H}
, with Cauchy’s integral formula we have
|\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}(z)\mathbf{w}\,dz
, for a deterministic vector
\mathbf{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and
\Gamma_{\lambda}
a positively oriented contour surrounding
only

\hat{\lambda}
.

As such, for
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
a deterministic equivalent of
\mathbf{Q}(z)
, that is,

\mathbf{Q}(z)\leftrightarrow\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z),\quad\text{with~}\frac{1}{p}\tr\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{Q}(z)-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z))\to 0,\text{~and~}\mathbf{a}^{\sf T}(\mathbf{Q}(z)-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z))\mathbf{b}\to 0,

(6)

almost surely as
n,p\to\infty
, for
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of bounded (Euclidean and spectral) norms, the limiting spectral measure (via the associated Stieltjes transform) and the behavior of isolated eigenpairs are directly accessible via the study of
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
.

4
Empirical Evaluation and Discussion

In this section, we provide further discussion on the consequences of Theorems
1
,
2
and
3
, together with numerical evaluations.
The implications of Theorem
1
on the (limiting) Hessian eigenvalue distribution is discussed in Section
4.1
.
Then, we discuss the consequences of Theorems
2
and
3
on possible isolated eigenpairs, and we show that two fundamentally different phase transition behaviors occur.
Recall that classical spiked models, extensively studied in RMT literature
[
8
,
11
,
63
]
, exhibit the following: (i) the isolated spike appears due to the presence of some statistical “signal” in the data; and (ii) a “monotonic” phase transition behavior, as a function of the signal strength, can be characterized.
Here, we show that, in addition to this classical “signal” spike, another type of spike may arise due to the underlying G-GLM model itself (i.e.,
\mathbf{w}_{*}
and
\mathbf{w}
), and it exhibits a very different transition behavior.
In particular,
in Section
4.2
, we discuss eigenvalue spikes due to data signal; and
in Section
4.3
, we discuss eigenvalue spikes due to model structure, even when there is no signal in the data.

However, it must be pointed out that this “visual approximation” by Marc̆enko-Pastur law is
not robust
, in the sense that it “visually” holds only for (yet still is formally different from) the case of (i) logistic model with (ii) logistic loss and (iii) identity covariance
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
.
Any change in the response model (e.g., with the phase retrieval model in Figure
1(c)
), the choice of loss function (e.g., with the exponential loss in Figure
2
-right), or going beyond the simple identity covariance (that models very homogeneous features) setting, as in Figure
3
, would induce a Hessian spectrum that is
very
different from the Marc̆enko-Pastur law.
In this vein, our Theorem
1
goes beyond such a “loose” Marc̆enko-Pastur approximation.
It provides a first example toward the understanding of Hessians of deep NNs and other much more realistic models that exploit nonlinear transformations (such as activation function) and feature statistics.

In the proofs of Theorem
1
–
4
, we explicitly used the fact that, for jointly Gaussian random variables, uncorrelatedness implies independence, and therefore our main results (on the limiting Hessian eigenvalue distribution and the possible isolated eigenpairs) are proven to hold only for Gaussian features. On the other hand, universality phenomena commonly arise in random matrix theory, and it has been proven for various models that the limiting spectral measure
[
88
]
, the spectrum edges
[
12
,
99
]
, and even involved functionals of the eigenvalues
[
7
]

do not
depend on the distribution of the random matrix, so long as its entries are independent and have zero mean, unit variance, and finite higher, say fourth, order moment.
6
6

6

Counterexample exists, though, for instance it has been shown in
[
31
,
66
]
that, even in the presence of signal, nonlinear random matrices may produce “noisy spikes” (that are purely random and carry no information about the data signal and) that, in particular, depends on the fourth moment of the distribution.

Figure

.
We conjecture that Theorem

4.2
Isolated eigenvalues: Spike due to data signal

To study the spike due to data “signal”
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, as well as its phase transition behavior, we focus here on the case
\mathbf{w}_{*}=\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{0}
. This, in the case of logistic model (
2
), gives rise to a much simpler form of limiting eigenspectrum (per Theorem
1
) and possible isolated eigenpairs (per Theorem
2
and
3
), as summarized in the following corollary, the proof of which is given in Appendix
B.3
.

Corollary 1

(Spike due to data signal: logistic model)
.

Consider the logistic model in (
2
) with logistic loss, for
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_{*}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
, we have
g=1/4
and the limiting Hessian eigenvalue distribution is the Marc̆enko-Pastur law, but rescaled by a factor of
1/4
.
Moreover, it follows from Theorem
2
and
3
that there is
at most one
isolated eigenpair
(\hat{\lambda}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
of
\mathbf{H}

and it satisfies

\hat{\lambda}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}~{\xrightarrow{a.s.}}~\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\frac{1}{4}(1+\rho+c\cdot\frac{\rho+1}{\rho})&\rho>\sqrt{c},\\
\frac{1}{4}(1+\sqrt{c})^{2}&\rho\leq\sqrt{c};\end{array}\right.

(13)

and

\frac{|\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}}~{\xrightarrow{a.s.}}~\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha=\frac{\rho^{2}-c}{\rho^{2}+c\rho}&\rho>\sqrt{c},\\
0&\rho\leq\sqrt{c};\end{array}\right.

(14)

with the signal strength
\rho=\lim\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}
and
c=\lim p/n
.

The isolated eigenvalue-eigenvector behavior described in Corollary
1
follows the classical phase transition
[
9
,
8
,
77
]
.
In particular, it exhibits the following properties: (i) the isolated eigenvalue always appears on the right-hand side of the main (Marc̆enko-Pastur) bulk; and (ii) the eigenvalue location and associated eigenvector alignment is “monotonic” with respect to the signal strength
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}
.
By “monotonic,” we mean that, for a fixed dimension ratio
c
, the largest Hessian eigenvalue is bound to become ‘isolated” once
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}
exceeds
\sqrt{c}
, and also that its value, as well as the eigenvector alignment, increase monotonically as
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}
grows.
This behavior is confirmed in Figure
6
.

As we shall see below in Section
4.3
, this behavior is
not
exhibited for a spike due to the model parameter
\mathbf{w}
.

4.3
Isolated eigenvalues: Spike due to model structure

To study the spike due to the underlying model structure (i.e.,
\mathbf{w}_{*}
and
\mathbf{w}
), as well as its phase transition behavior, we position ourselves in the situation where
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{0}

B.4
.

Corollary 2

(Spike due to model: logistic model)
.

Consider the logistic model in (
2
) with logistic loss,
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
, then the Stieltjes transform

satisfies
m(z)=1/({\mathbb{E}}[f(r,z)]-z)
for

and

that
depends
on
\mathbf{w}
but
not
on
\mathbf{w}_{*}
. Moreover, there is
at most one
isolated eigenvalue
\hat{\lambda}_{\mathbf{w}}
of
\mathbf{H}
that is due to
\mathbf{w}
and satisfies
\hat{\lambda}_{\mathbf{w}}-\lambda_{\mathbf{w}}~{\xrightarrow{a.s.}}~0
with
\lambda_{\mathbf{w}}
solution to
0=\det\mathbf{G}(\lambda_{\mathbf{w}})=1+m(\lambda_{\mathbf{w}})\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[f(r,\lambda_{\mathbf{w}})(r^{2}-\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2})]}{\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}}

The situation here (for a spike due to model) is more subtle than for the spike due to data signal (discussed in Section

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we precisely characterized the Hessian eigenspectra of the nonlinear G-GLMs, in the high dimensional regime where both feature dimension and sample size are large and comparable.
By focusing on this more realistic, yet still tractable, family of models, our results provide theoretical justifications for many empirical observations in large-scale machine learning models such as deep neural networks.
The deterministic equivalent technique that we employ is very powerful, and we expect that it will enable analysis of a range of more realistic machine learning models.
Of particular future interest would be to use this technique to understand heavy-tailed structure in the correlations of state-of-the-art-neural network models
[
70
,
71
]
, and the connections with recent results on heavy-tailed behavior in stochastic optimization algorithms that are used to train these models
[
46
,
42
]
.

\mathbf{v}=O_{\|\cdot\|}(u_{n})
, as its Euclidean norm satisfying

A.1
Proof of Theorem
4

The proof of the deterministic equivalent (recall from Section
2.3
) result of the type
\mathbf{Q}(z)\leftrightarrow{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}(z)]
generally comes in two steps:

1.

establish the convergence in mean, that is, to show that the proposed
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
is an asymptotic approximation for the expected resolvent
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}(z)]
in the sense that

\|\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}(z)]\|\to 0,

(16)

as
n,p\to\infty
; and

2.

show that the random quantities
\frac{1}{p}\tr\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}(z)
and
\mathbf{a}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}(z)\mathbf{b}
of interest concentrate around their expectations in the sense that

\frac{1}{p}\tr\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{Q}(z)-{\mathbb{E}}\mathbf{Q}(z))\to 0,\quad\text{~and~}\mathbf{a}^{\sf T}(\mathbf{Q}(z)-{\mathbb{E}}\mathbf{Q}(z))\mathbf{b}\to 0,

(17)

almost surely as
n,p\to\infty
. This can be done with concentration of measure arguments
[
60
]
or, in the Gaussian case, the Nash–Poincaré inequality to bound the variance (see for example
[
76
, Proposition 2.4]
) and then applying Markov’s inequality and the Borel Cantelli lemma.

Here we will focus on the first part of the proof and show for
\mathbf{Q}(z)=(\mathbf{H}-z\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}
,
\mathbf{H}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
defined in (
4
) that

\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}(z)]-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)\|\to 0,\quad\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)\equiv\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g}{1+g\cdot\delta(z)}(\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\sf T})\right]-z\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1},

(18)

as
n,p\to\infty
, for random vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and
g=\ell^{\prime\prime}(y,\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{z})
for
\mathbf{z}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{p})
as in (
8
),
y
defined in (
1
),
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}
the projection onto the subspace spanned by the columns of
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}[\mathbf{w}_{*},~\mathbf{w}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times 2}
so that
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}=\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}
for
\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{U}
invertible, and
\delta(z)
the unique solution to (
7
).

Before diving into the detailed proof of Theorem
4
, we will illustrate the main idea leading to Theorem
4
, notably the form of
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
in (
18
), in the simple setting of
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
. As discussed above in Section
2.1
, the main technical difficulty lies in the fact that the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}=\diag\{\ell^{\prime\prime}(y_{i},\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
depends on the
\mathbf{x}_{i}
’s in a rather involved nonlinear manner per (
1
). Nonetheless, note that standard Gaussian vector
\mathbf{x}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{p})
can decomposed as

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{x}^{\perp}\equiv\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{x}^{\perp},\quad\text{for}\quad\mathbf{x}^{\perp}\equiv(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{x},

(19)

the sum of its projection onto the subspace spanned by the columns of
\mathbf{U}=[\mathbf{w}_{*},~\mathbf{w}]
(recall that
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
and assume for the moment that
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{*}
are linearly independent) and the complementary subspace. Due to the orthogonal invariance of standard multivariate Gaussian, we have that both
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\perp}=(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{x}
are Gaussian, and they are
independent
, for example by checking that
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\sf T}(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})]=\mathbf{0}
.

Since
\mathbf{D}
depends on
\mathbf{X}
only through
\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{X}
, we have that
\mathbf{D}
is
independent
of
\mathbf{X}^{\perp}\equiv[\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\perp},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\perp}]=(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times n}
so that

\mathbf{H}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}=\frac{1}{n}(\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{X}^{\perp})\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{X}^{\perp})^{\sf T}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^{\perp}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{X}^{\perp})^{\sf T}+\mathbf{A}

(20)

for some
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
of low rank (since
\rank(\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\leq 2
). As a consequence, our object of interest
\mathbf{H}
is shown to follow a spiked random matrix model as discussed in Section
2.2
, with the main bulk
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^{\perp}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{X}^{\perp})^{\sf T}
now more accessible due to the independence between
\mathbf{X}^{\perp}
and
\mathbf{D}
.
Moreover, since
\mathbf{X}^{\perp}
is also a low rank update of the original
\mathbf{X}
, the above derivation shows that, asymptotically, the limiting spectral measure of
\mathbf{H}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}
in (
4
) is the same as that of
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^{\perp}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{X}^{\perp})^{\sf T}
and thus the same as if
\mathbf{D}
was
independent
of
\mathbf{X}
.
While the analysis of limiting eigenvalue distribution of
\mathbf{H}
is largely simplified based on the argument above, the characterization of the low rank matrix
\mathbf{A}
requires more effort, so as to determine the behavior of the possible isolated eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs of
\mathbf{H}
.

The idea above can be generalized, under Assumption
1
, to arbitrary profile of the statistical mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and covariance
\mathbf{C}
, leading to Theorem
4
, the proof of which is given as follows.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

For mathematical convenience, for the resolvent
\mathbf{Q}(z)=(\mathbf{H}-z\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}
, we will take
z<0
outside the spectral support of
\mathbf{H}
in what follows. Since
\mathbf{Q}(z)
and
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
from the theorem statement are complex analytic functions for

(matrix-valued Stieltjes transforms are analytic

{\mathbb{R}}^{-}
is equivalent to obtaining the result on all of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
.

Following the orthogonal decomposition idea in (
19
), for
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}[\mathbf{w}_{*},~\mathbf{w}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times 2}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{C})
we have
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{p})
and

\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{z}_{i}+\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp},\quad\text{~for~}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}\equiv(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{z}_{i}.

(21)

Note that (i)
\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}=\mathbf{0}
, (ii)
\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{z}_{i}
is independent of
\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}
due to the Gaussianity of
\mathbf{z}_{i}
and

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}]=\mathbf{0},\quad{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp})^{\sf T}]=\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}.

(22)

With these results, we now move on to the proof of (
18
). Recall the definition
\mathbf{Q}=(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}-z\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}
with
\mathbf{D}=\diag\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and

g_{i}\equiv\ell^{\prime\prime}(y_{i},\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}_{i}),

(23)

we first introduce the following intermediate deterministic quantities

\theta\equiv\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]>0,\quad\mathbf{M}_{\theta}\equiv{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}}{1+g_{1}\cdot\theta}\right],\quad\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}\equiv\left(\mathbf{M}_{\theta}-z\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1},

(24)

where we denote, for i.i.d.
\mathbf{x}_{i},g_{i}
and without loss of generality,
\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\equiv(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\neq 1}g_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}-z\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
the “leave-one-out” version of
\mathbf{Q}
that is
independent
of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
and
g_{1}
. Our proof strategy is to show that

\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}]-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}\|\to 0,\quad\|\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\|\to 0,

and thus the conclusion of (
18
).

With the resolvent identity
\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\mathbf{B}^{-1}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{B}^{-1}
for invertible
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
, we write,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{Q}\left(\mathbf{M}_{\theta}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}\right)\right]\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}={\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}]\mathbf{M}_{\theta}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}[g_{i}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}]\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}]\mathbf{M}_{\theta}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{i}\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}}{1+g_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}}\right]\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta},

where we used the Sherman–Morrison formula.

Since the random variable
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
is expected to concentrate around its expectation
\theta=\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]
, we write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{i}\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}}{1+g_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{i}\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}}{1+g_{i}\cdot\theta}\right]+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}(\theta-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i})}{(1+g_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i})(1+g_{i}\cdot\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]\mathbf{M}_{\theta}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}(\theta-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i})}{1+g_{i}\cdot\theta}\right]

\displaystyle\equiv{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]\mathbf{M}_{\theta}+\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}]

for
\mathbf{D}_{1}\equiv\diag\{\frac{g_{i}}{1+g_{i}\cdot\theta}(\theta-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathbf{D}=\diag\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where we used the fact that
\mathbf{Q}_{-i}
is independent of both
g_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. As such,

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}]={\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]\mathbf{M}_{\theta}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}-\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}]\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}.

(25)

For the first right-hand side term in (
25
), again by the resolvent identity

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}-\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}-\mathbf{Q}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\frac{1}{n}g_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\frac{g_{i}\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}}{1+g_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\frac{g_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\sf T}}{1+g_{i}\cdot\theta}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\right]+\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\frac{g_{1}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\sf T}(\theta-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1})}{(1+g_{1}\cdot\theta)(1+g_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1})}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\mathbf{M}_{\theta}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]+\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{Q}\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\right]

where we used again the Sherman–Morrison formula. The first term is easily seen to be of order
O(n^{-1})
in spectral norm (with
\|\mathbf{Q}(z)\|\leq\dist(z,\supp\mu_{\mathbf{H}})^{-1}
and
\|\mathbf{M}_{\theta}\|=O(1)
under Assumption
1
). For the second term, note that with the additional
1/n
in front, it suffices to bound the spectral norm of the second right-hand side term in (
25
). Since
\|\mathbf{Q}\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X}^{\sf T}\|\leq 1
, it thus remains to control

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{i}\left|\theta-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right|\right],

(26)

that appears in
\mathbf{D}_{1}
. To this end, note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\theta-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\right|^{4}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}-\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}+\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{Q}_{-1}-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}])\right|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}-\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\right|^{4}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{Q}_{-1}-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}])\right|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle+6\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}-\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\right|^{2}\cdot\left|\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{Q}_{-1}-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}])\right|^{2}\right]+O(n^{-4})=O(n^{-2}),

with
\alpha\equiv\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]
by definition, where we used, for the last line,
[
5
, Lemma B.26]
, the Burkholder inequality (see e.g.,
[
5
, Lemma 2.13]
), as well as the fact that
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\boldsymbol{\mu}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\|=O(1)
under Assumption
1
.

As a result, by Markov’s inequality
\mathbb{P}(X>t)\leq{\mathbb{E}}[X^{k}]/t^{k}
for every
k
(for
X,t>0
) and the union bound, we have that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{i}\left|\theta-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{C^{\prime}n^{-1/4}}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i}\left|\theta-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right|>t\right)\,dt

\displaystyle+n\int_{C^{\prime}n^{-1/4}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\theta-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}_{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\right|>t\right)\,dt\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{4}}

holds for some constant
C,C^{\prime}>0
that depend on
p,n
only via the ratio
p/n
.

Putting all together, we obtain the first approximation

\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}]-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}\|=O(n^{-\frac{1}{4}}),\quad\text{~for~}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}\equiv\left(\mathbf{M}_{\theta}-z\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1},\quad\mathbf{M}_{\theta}\equiv{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}}{1+g_{1}\cdot\theta}\right],

(27)

with
\theta\equiv\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]>0
.

Note in passing we also proved that
\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]\|=O(n^{-1})
.

It thus remains to establish the second approximation by showing

\|\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\|\to 0,

(28)

as
n,p\to\infty
, with
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}=(\mathbf{M}-z\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}
and
\mathbf{M}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g}{1+g\cdot\delta(z)}(\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\sf T})\right]
as defined in (
18
). To this end, we first compute the expectation in
\mathbf{M}_{\theta}
. Note that
g_{i}=\ell^{\prime\prime}(y_{i},\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}_{i})
depends on
\mathbf{x}_{i}

only
via the projection
\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{z}_{i}
under (
1
). We thus decompose
\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\sf T}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{z}_{i})(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{z}_{i})^{\sf T}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{z}_{i}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp})(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{z}_{i}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp})^{\sf T}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\sf T}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp})^{\sf T}\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp})^{\sf T}\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\sf T},

where we denote
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{z}_{i}
. As such

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\theta}

\displaystyle\equiv{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}}{1+g_{1}\cdot\theta}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{1}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{\sf T}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp})^{\sf T}\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}})}{1+g_{1}\cdot\theta}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g_{1}}{1+g_{1}\cdot\theta}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{\sf T}+\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right]

where we used the crucial fact that
\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}
is independent of
g_{i},\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{z}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
due to the Gaussianity of
\mathbf{z}_{i}
, with
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}]=\mathbf{0}
and
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp}(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\perp})^{\sf T}]=\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}
from (
22
). As a result, we have

\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}_{\theta}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g^{2}(\delta-\theta)}{(1+g\cdot\delta)(1+g\cdot\theta)}\cdot(\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\sf T})\right]

(29)

for
\delta=\delta(z)=\frac{1}{p}\tr\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)
as defined in (
7
).

Further note that

\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}=\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}_{\theta}\right)\bar{\mathbf{Q}}

so that with
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)=\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g\cdot\mathbf{C}}{1+g\delta(z)}\right]-z\mathbf{I}-p\right)^{-1}
as defined in (
7
),

\displaystyle|\delta-\theta|=\left|\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}})\right|+\left|\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta})\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}])\right|+\left|\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]\right|

where we used the fact that
\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}]-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}\|=O(n^{-\frac{1}{4}})
and
\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}_{-1}]\|=O(n^{-1})
to bound the third and fourth right-hand side term, respectively, and the following lemma to bound the first right-hand side term.

Lemma 1

(
[
87
, Lemma 2.6]
)
.

For
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
symmetric,
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
,

\tr\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{M}+\tau\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\sf T}-z\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}-\tr\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{M}-z\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{A}\|}{|\Im[z]|}.

The inequality further extends to the case of symmetric and positive semidefinite
\mathbf{A}
, with
\tau>0
and
z<0
so long that the inverses are well-defined, by replacing the right-hand-side
|\Im[z]|
by
|z|
.

We thus obtain from (
29
) that

\displaystyle|\delta-\theta|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{n}\tr\mathbf{C}(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta})\right|+O(n^{-\frac{1}{4}})

\displaystyle=|\delta-\theta|\cdot\frac{1}{n}\tr\left(\mathbf{C}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g^{2}}{(1+g\cdot\delta)(1+g\cdot\delta)}(\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{U}})\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\sf T})\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\right)+O(n^{-\frac{1}{4}})

so that

|\delta-\theta|=O(n^{-\frac{1}{4}}),\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{\theta}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\|=O(n^{-\frac{1}{4}}).

(30)

This concludes the proof of Theorem
4
.
∎

(31)

with

\mathbf{U}^{+}\mathbf{z}&\mathbf{U}^{+}\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{U}^{+}\mathbf{z})^{\sf T}-(\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{U})^{+}\end{bmatrix}\right]

for
\mathbf{U}^{+}

.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
2

For
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
defined in Theorem
4
, we have, with the Woodbury identity that

\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)=\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g\cdot\mathbf{C}}{1+g\cdot\delta(z)}\right]-z\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\right)^{-1}=\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}_{b}(z)+\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\right)^{-1}

with
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)\equiv\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g\cdot\mathbf{C}}{1+g\cdot\delta(z)}\right]-z\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}
that characterizes the main bulk,
\mathbf{V}\equiv[\boldsymbol{\mu},~\mathbf{C}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{U}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times 3}
and

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(z)\equiv{\mathbb{E}}\frac{g}{1+g\cdot\delta(z)}\begin{bmatrix}1&(\mathbf{U}^{+}\mathbf{z})^{\sf T}\\
\mathbf{U}^{+}\mathbf{z}&\mathbf{U}^{+}\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{U}^{+}\mathbf{z})^{\sf T}-(\mathbf{U}^{\sf T}\mathbf{U})^{+}\end{bmatrix}.

(33)

Recall from Theorem
1
that
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
is an eigenvalue within the main bulk if the Stieltjes transform is not defined

m(z)=\frac{1}{p}\tr\left({\mathbb{E}}_{g}\frac{g\cdot\mathbf{C}}{1+g\cdot\delta(z)}-z\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{p}\tr\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z).

To characterize the possible isolated eigenvalues (i.e., the “spikes”), we aim to find
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
is not defined but
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)
is (and thus, outside the main bulk), or equivalently

\det\bar{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}(z)=0,\quad\text{such that $\det\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}^{-1}(z)\neq 0$ }

by solving for
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\det\bar{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}(z)=\det\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}_{b}(z)+\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\right)=\det\bar{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}_{b}(z)\cdot\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z))

\displaystyle=\det(\mathbf{I}_{2K+1}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)\mathbf{V})

with Sylvester’s identity. This concludes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

A.4
Proof of Theorem
3

Similar to the behavior of the isolated eigenvalues studied in Theorem
2
, the corresponding eigenvector can be characterized via the Cauchy’s integration technique.
More precisely, from Theorem
4
, recall that
\mathbf{V}\equiv[\boldsymbol{\mu},~\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w}_{*},~\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times 3}
captures the low rank structure of the Hessian matrix
\mathbf{H}
.
Thus, we can characterize the projection of a given isolated eigenvector
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
onto the subspace spanned by the columns of
\mathbf{V}
(that are assumed to be linearly independent for simplicity of presentation). To this end, we need to evaluate, for
\lambda
the asymptotic approximation given in Theorem
2
of an isolated eigenvalue
\hat{\lambda}
of
\mathbf{H}
with corresponding eigenvector
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
, the following matrix

\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{u}}\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\sf T}\mathbf{V}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}(z)\mathbf{V}\,dz=-\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)\mathbf{V}\,dz+o(1)

(34)

where
\Gamma_{\lambda}
is a positively oriented contour surrounding the isolated eigenvalue only.

Since the columns of
\mathbf{V}
, denoted
\mathbf{v}_{a}
for
a\in\{1,2,3\}
, are assumed to be linearly independent, one can write

\hat{\mathbf{u}}=\sum_{a=1}^{3}\theta_{a}\mathbf{v}_{a}+\boldsymbol{\omega}_{a}

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
is a random vector of zero mean and orthogonal to
\mathbf{v}_{a}
. As a consequence, the projection in (
34
) allows to retrieve the coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
via

\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{u}}\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\sf T}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\sf T}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\mathbf{V}+o(1)

(35)

with a central limit theorem argument.

We now present the proof of Theorem
3
as follows.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
(\hat{\lambda},\hat{\mathbf{u}})
be an isolated eigenvalue-eigenvector pair of
\mathbf{H}
, and let
\lambda
denote the asymptotic approximation given in Theorem
2
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{u}}\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\sf T}\mathbf{V}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\mathbf{Q}(z)\mathbf{V}\,dz=-\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}(z)\mathbf{V}\,dz+o(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}_{b}(z)+\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(z)\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{V}\,dz+o(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi\imath}\oint_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{I}_{3}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(z)\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)\mathbf{V}\right)^{-1}\,dz+o(1)

with Woodbury identity, where we recall
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)\equiv\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g\cdot\mathbf{C}}{1+g\cdot\delta(z)}\right]-z\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}
defined in (
7
) that characterizes the main bulk. With Cauchy’s residue theorem, it remains to evaluate the associated residue and thus the values of
z
lying within
\Gamma_{\lambda}
such that
\mathbf{I}_{3}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)\mathbf{V}
is singular. As a consequence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{u}}\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{\sf T}\mathbf{V}=-{\rm Res}\left(\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)\mathbf{V}\cdot\left(\mathbf{I}_{3}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(z)\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)\mathbf{V}\right)^{-1}\right)+o(1)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(\lambda)\mathbf{V}\cdot\lim_{z\to\lambda}\left(\mathbf{I}_{3}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(z)\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(z)\mathbf{V}\right)^{-1}+o(1)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(\lambda)\mathbf{V}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{v}_{r,\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{v}_{l,\mathbf{G}}^{\sf T}}{\mathbf{v}_{l,\mathbf{G}}^{\sf T}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}(\lambda)\mathbf{v}_{r,\mathbf{G}}}+o(1)\equiv-\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{b}(\lambda)\mathbf{V}\cdot\Xi(\lambda)+o(1)

with
\mathbf{G}(z)
defined in (

),
\mathbf{v}_{l,\mathbf{G}},\mathbf{v}_{r,\mathbf{G}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
the left and right eigenvectors of
\mathbf{G}(\lambda)
associated with eigenvalue zero, and
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}(z)
the derivative of
\mathbf{G}(z)
with respect to

.

This concludes the proof of Theorem

.
∎

Note in particular that

B.1
Logistic model

Consider the logistic model as in (

)

\mathbb{P}(y=1\mid\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x})=\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x})=1-\mathbb{P}(y=-1\mid\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}),\quad\sigma(t)=(1+e^{-t})^{-1}

(36)

with the logistic loss

and

, we have for

B.2
Phase retrieval model

Consider the noiseless phase retrieval model for which
y=(\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x})^{2}
with square loss
\ell(y,h)=(y-h^{2})^{2}/4
and
h=\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w})
, we have for
g\equiv\ell^{\prime\prime}(y,h)
defined in (
8
) that

g=\frac{\partial^{2}\ell(y,h)}{\partial h^{2}}=3h^{2}-y

(39)

so that for any fixed
\delta(z)
,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{g}{1+g\cdot\delta(z)}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{3h^{2}-y}{1+(3h^{2}-y)\delta(z)}\right]

(40)

where we note that
3h^{2}-y=\mathbf{x}^{\sf T}\Delta\mathbf{x}
with
\Delta\equiv 3\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\sf T}-\mathbf{w}_{*}\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
so that
3h^{2}-y
is a weighted sum of (at most) two independent non-central chi-square random variable. (Recall that, for
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{I}_{p})
,
\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
follows a non-central
\chi^{2}
with
p
degree of freedom and denotes
\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\sim\chi^{2}(p,\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2})
.) In particular, for
\mathbf{w}=\alpha\mathbf{w}_{*}
with
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we obtain
\Delta=(3\alpha^{2}-1)\mathbf{w}_{*}\mathbf{w}_{*}^{\sf T}
and
3h^{2}-y
is thus a (non-centered) chi-square random variable.
