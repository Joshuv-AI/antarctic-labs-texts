Approximating Gromov-Hausdorff Distance in Euclidean Space
Sushovan Majhi, Jeffrey Vitter, Carola Wenk

Abstract
The Gromov-Hausdorff distance ( d G ​ H ) (d_{GH}) proves to be a useful distance measure between shapes. In order to approximate d G ​ H d_{GH} for compact subsets X , Y ⊂ ℝ d X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d} , we look into its relationship with d H , i ​ s ​ o d_{H,iso} , the infimum Hausdorff distance under Euclidean isometries. As already known for dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , the d H , i ​ s ​ o d_{H,iso} cannot be bounded above by a constant factor times d G ​ H d_{GH} . For d = 1 d=1 , however, we prove that d H , i ​ s ​ o ≤ 5 4 ​ d G ​ H d_{H,iso}\leq\frac{5}{4}d_{GH} . We also show that the bound is tight. In effect, this gives rise to an O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log{n}) -time algorithm to approximate d G ​ H d_{GH} with an approximation factor of ( 1 + 1 4 ) \left(1+\frac{1}{4}\right) .

Introduction

This paper grew out of our effort to
compute the Gromov-Hausdorff distance between Euclidean subsets. The
Gromov-Hausdorff distance between two abstract metric spaces was first
introduced by M. Gromov in ICM 1979 (see Berger
[
1
]
).
The notion, although it emerged in the context of Riemannian metrics, proves to
be a natural distance measure between any two (compact) metric spaces. Only in
the last decade the Gromov-Hausdorff distance has received much attention from
the researchers in the more applied fields. In shape recognition
and comparison, shapes are regarded as metric spaces that are deformable under a
class of transformations. Depending on the application in question, a suitable
class of transformations is chosen, then the dissimilarity between the shapes
are defined by a suitable notion of
distance measure or error
that is
invariant under the desired class of transformations. For comparing Euclidean
shapes under Euclidean isometry, the use of Gromov-Hausdorff distance is
proposed and discussed in

[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

In this paper, we are primarily motivated by the questions pertaining to the
computation of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance, particularly between Euclidean
subsets. Although the distance measure puts the Euclidean shape matching on a
robust theoretical foundation
[
2
,
3
]
,
the question of computing the Gromov-Hausdorff distance, or even an
approximation thereof, still remains elusive. In the recent years, some efforts
have been made to address such computational aspects. Most notably, the authors
of
[
6
]
show an NP-hardness result for approximating
the Gromov-Hausdorff distance between metric trees. For Euclidean subsets,
however, the question of a polynomial-time algorithm is still open.
In
[
5
]
, the authors show that computing the
Gromov-Hausdorff distance is related to various NP-hard problems and study a
variant of the distance measure.

The authors of
[
7
]
introduce the additive
distortion—the one used in Gromov-Hausdorff distance—and consider the
problem of minimizing the distortion of bijective functions between sets of the
same cardinality. For the real-line case (
d=1
), the authors demonstrate a
polynomial-time
2
-approximation algorithm
[
7
, Theorem
6]
. An open problem is also posed in

[
7
]
: are there polynomial-time approximations to
find the distortion-minimizing bijection withing a factor less than
2
?
Although the context is similar, we consider minimizing the distortion of
correspondences between sets of possibly different cardinalities. As we see in
Definition
1.5
, a correspondence between two sets is a more general relation than
a function.

All these computational concerns provoke the natural curiosity about the genuine
computational hardness of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance between Euclidean
subsets. Let
d\geq 1
and
X,Y\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be compact sets equipped with the
standard Euclidean metric, and let
d_{GH}(X,Y)
denote their Gromov-Hausdorff
distance. One then wonders:

(i)

Is there an algorithm to compute
d_{GH}(X,Y)

exactly

in polynomial-time?

(ii)

If not, can we find a polynomial-time approximation algorithm
for
d_{GH}(X,Y)
, possibly with a reasonably small approximation factor?

(iii)

If not, is it NP-hard to approximate
d_{GH}(X,Y)
, like the metric
graph case?

The above questions motivate our investigation into the computation of
d_{GH}

in Euclidean spaces.

Background and Related Work

The notion of Gromov-Hausdorff distance is closely related to the notion of
Hausdorff distance. Let
(Z,d_{Z})
be any metric space. We first give a formal
definition of the directed Hausdorff distance between any two subsets of
Z
.

Definition 1.1

(Directed Hausdorff Distance)
.

For any two
compact subsets
X,Y
of a metric space
(Z,d_{Z})
, the
directed
Hausdorff distance
from
X
to
Y
, denoted
\overrightarrow{d}_{H}^{Z}(X,Y)
, is
defined by

\max_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}d_{Z}(x,y).

Unfortunately, the directed Hausdorff distance is not symmetric. To retain
symmetry, the
Hausdorff distance
is defined in the following way:

Definition 1.2

(Hausdorff Distance)
.

For any two compact subsets

X,Y
of a metric space
(Z,d_{Z})
, their
Hausdorff distance
, denoted by

d_{H}^{Z}(X,Y)
, is defined by

\max\left\{\overrightarrow{d}_{H}^{Z}(X,Y),\overrightarrow{d}_{H}^{Z}(Y,X)\right\}.

To keep our notations simple, we drop the superscript when it is
understood that
Z
is taken to be
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
X,Y
are Euclidean subsets
equipped with the standard Euclidean metric
\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert
. The
d_{H}
can be
computed in
O(n\log{n})
-time for finite point sets with at most
n
points or

n
line segments; see
[
8
]
.

We follow Gromov’s book (
[
9
]
) to define the
Gromov-Hausdorff distance. The primary definition uses the concept of an
isometry or distance-preserving map between metric spaces, which we define
first.

Definition 1.3

(Isometry)
.

A map
f:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is called an
isometry
if

d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})=d_{Y}(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2})).

We immediately note that an isometry
f
is injective, and that

f:X\to f(X)
is a homeomorphism.

We are now in a place to define the Gromov-Hausdorff distance formally. Unlike
the Hausdorff distance, the Gromov-Hausdorff distance is defined between two
abstract metric spaces
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
that may not share a common
ambient space. We start with the following formal definition:

Definition 1.4

(Gromov-Hausdorff Distance
[
9
]
)
.

The
Gromov-Hausdorff distance
, denoted by
d_{GH}(X,Y)
, between two
metric spaces
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
is defined to be

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:X\to Z\\
g:Y\to Z\\
Z\end{subarray}}d_{H}^{Z}(f(X),g(Y)),

where the infimum is taken over all isometries
f:X\to Z
,
g:Y\to Z

and metric spaces
(Z,d_{Z})
.

The definition of Gromov-Hausdorff distance may not seem very natural at first
glance—it deserves a bit of explanation. As mentioned earlier, the definition
works for abstract metric spaces
X
and
Y
, without requiring them to be
embedded in a common ambient metric space. In order to anatomize Definition
1.4
, we
first observe that the maps
f,g
are embedding
X
and
Y
, respectively, into
a common metric space
(Z,d_{Z})
. Since
f,g
are isometries, the subsets

f(X),g(Y)
of
Z
are isometric to
X
and
Y
, respectively. As
f(X)

and
g(Y)
are subsets of
Z
, their Hausdorff distance
d^{Z}_{H}(f(X),g(Y))
can
now be considered. The Gromov-Hausdorff distance is defined to minimize (if the
minimum exists) this
d^{Z}_{H}(f(X),g(Y))
, subject to all isometries
f,g
and
ambient metric space
(Z,d_{Z})
. As a consequence, Gromov-Hausdorff distance is a
distance measure between abstract metric spaces
X
and
Y
that is also
invariant under any isometric transformations of
X
or
Y
. A detour to

[
5
,
9
,
10
]
is suggested
for a detailed treatment of the definitions and properties of Gromov-Hausdorff
distance.

In order to present an equivalent definition of the Gromov-Hausdorff
distance that is computationally viable, we first define the notion of
a correspondence.

Definition 1.5

(Correspondence)
.

A
correspondence

{\cal C}

between any two (non-empty) sets
X
and
Y
is defined to be a subset

{\cal C}\subseteq X\times Y
with the following two properties:

i)

for any
x\in X
, there exists a
y\in Y
such that
(x,y)\in{\cal C}
, and

ii)

for any
y\in Y
, there exists an
x\in X
such that
(x,y)\in{\cal C}
.

A correspondence
{\cal C}
is a special
relation
that assigns all points of
both
X
and
Y
a corresponding point. If the sets
X
and
Y
in the
Definition
1.5
are equipped with metrics
d_{X}
and
d_{Y}
, respectively, we can
also define the distortion of the correspondence
{\cal C}
.

Definition 1.6

((Additive) Distortion of
Correspondence)
.

Let
{\cal C}
be a
correspondence between two metric spaces
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
, then its

distortion
, denoted
Dist({\cal C})
, is defined to be

\sup_{(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in{\cal C}}\left\lvert d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})-d_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})\right\rvert

The distortion
Dist({\cal C})
is sometimes called the
additive
distortion as
opposed to the
multiplicative
distortion; see
[
11
]
for
a definition. In the context of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance, the distortion of
a correspondence measures how much the two metrics are distorted by the
{\cal C}
. In
the extreme case, when
{\cal C}
is an isometry, its distortion becomes zero. For
non-empty sets
X,Y
, we denote by
{\cal C}(X,Y)
the set of all correspondences
between
X
and
Y
. An alternative definition of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance
is given in Lemma
1.7
; see
[
10
]
for a proof.

Lemma 1.7
.

For any two compact metric spaces
(X,d_{X})

and
(Y,d_{Y})
, the following holds:

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf\limits_{{\cal C}\in{\cal C}(X,Y)}Dist({\cal C}).

This combinatorial formulation unveils the genuine complexity entailed in the
computation of Gromov-Hausdorff distance. For two finite metric spaces
X,Y

containing at most
n
points, the computation takes
O(2^{n})
-time by
enumerating all possible correspondences between the points of
X
and
Y
.

Our Contribution

Our main contribution in this work is to provide a satisfactory answer to the
quest of understanding the relation between
d_{H,iso}
(see Definition
2.1
) and

d_{GH}
when
X,Y
are compact subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
equipped with the standard
Euclidean metric.

In Theorem
3.2
, we show that

d_{H,iso}(X,Y)\leq\frac{5}{4}d_{GH}(X,Y)

for
any compact
X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
. For subsets of the real line, it is tempting to
believe that
d_{GH}=d_{H,iso}
. We show in Theorem
3.10
that this is, in fact,
not true by showing that the bound
\frac{5}{4}
in Theorem
3.2
is tight. Since

d_{H,iso}(X,Y)
can be computed in
O(n\log{n})
-time (
[
12
]
), we
provide an
O(n\log{n})
-time algorithm to approximate
d_{GH}(X,Y)
for finite

X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
with an approximation factor of
(1+\frac{1}{4})
.

Gromov-Hausdorff vs Hausdorff Distance under Isometry

With the basic definitions now at our disposal, we make our readers acquainted
with a related notion
d_{H,iso}(X,Y)
here, and list a few of its relevant
consequences. In this section, we also introduce the concept of nearest neighbor
correspondences, present some of their properties, and then illuminate the trail
that has led us to the pinnacle of our findings of Section
3
.

2.1
Comparing
d_{GH}
with
d_{H,iso}

For any dimension
d\geq 1
, a
Euclidean isometry

T:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is defined to be a map that preserves the distance,
i.e.,

\left\lvert T(x_{1})-T(x_{2})\right\rvert=\left\lvert x_{1}-x_{2}\right\rvert\mbox{ for all }x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

When
d=1
, the map
T
can only afford to be a translation or a reflection
(flip). In
d=2
, a Euclidean isometry is characterized by a combination of a
translation, a rotation by an angle, and a reflection about the origin. For more
about Euclidean isometries, see
[
13
]
. We denote by
{\cal E}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

the set of all isometries of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Definition 2.1

(Hausdorff under Isometry)
.

For any two compact subsets
X,Y
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we define

d_{H,iso}(X,Y)=\inf\limits_{T\in\mathcal{E}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}d_{H}(X,T(Y)).

We immediately note that
d_{H,iso}
induces a pseudo-metric on the set of
compact subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
; if
d_{H,iso}(X,Y)=0
, then
X
is
congruent

to
Y
.

Remark 2.2
.

If
X,Y
are subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
with at most
n

points, the authors of
[
12
]
prove that their
d_{H,iso}(X,Y)

can be computed in
O(n\log{n})
-time.

The
d_{H,iso}(X,Y)
minimizes the Hausdorff distance over only Euclidean
isometries; whereas
d_{GH}(X,Y)
considers minimizing over
all

isometries and
all
embeddings for
X
and
Y
—not just Euclidean. The
observation quickly yields the following inequality:

d_{GH}(X,Y)\leq d_{H,iso}(X,Y).

It is most natural to wonder if they are, in fact, equal. To our
disappointment, we can contrive the following configuration in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to show
the contrary in Example
2.3
.

Example 2.3

(
d_{GH}<d_{H,iso}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
)
.

2.2
Nearest Neighbor Correspondence

To conclude our discussion of this section, we lastly present our line of
investigation into Hausdorff correspondences. As noted previously, in
Example
2.3
and Example
2.4
,
d_{GH}(X,Y)\neq d_{H,iso}(X,Y)
in general.
However, we take the analysis one step further, and explore in Theorem
2.8

that there does not exist a Euclidean isometry
T
such that the Hausdorff
correspondence between
X
and
T(Y)
is an optimal correspondence that
minimizes the distortion.

Among many possible correspondences, the following correspondence is
particularly interesting when considering two Euclidean subsets.

Definition 2.5

(Nearest Neighbor Correspondence)
.

For any two compact subsets
X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, we define the
nearest
neighbor correspondence

{\cal C}_{NN}
to be the relation defined by the nearest
neighbors of points of
X
and
Y
. More precisely,

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{NN}=\{(x,y)\in X\times Y\mid(x\text{ is a nearest neighbor of }y\text{ in }X)

\displaystyle\text{ or }(y\text{ is a nearest neighbor of }x\text{ in }Y)\}.

Since
X
and
Y
are compact, the nearest neighbors exist. Hence, the
induced relation is a correspondence. Now follows an important structural
property concerning crossings of a correspondence.

Definition 2.6

(Crossing)
.

For a correspondence

{\cal C}\in{\cal C}(X,Y)
, we say a pair of edges
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
is a

crossing
if they cross in the usual sense, i.e., either of the following
happens
x_{1}<x_{2}
but
y_{1}>y_{2}
or
x_{1}>x_{2}
but
y_{1}<y_{2}
; see Figure
2
.

Lemma 2.7

(Crossing)
.

For any two compact
X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
, the nearest neighbor correspondence

{\cal C}_{NN}(X,Y)
is free of crossings.

Proof.

Let us consider two edges
e=(x,y)
and
e^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in
{\cal C}_{NN}(X,Y)

with
x<x^{\prime}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
y
is a nearest
neighbor of
x
. In order to show that
e
cannot cross
e^{\prime}
, we assume the
contrary:
y^{\prime}<y
. Now, we consider the following cases based on the position
of
y
with respect to
x
. In each case we show that neither
y^{\prime}
is a
nearest neighbor of
x^{\prime}
nor
x^{\prime}
is a nearest neighbor of
y^{\prime}
. This would
contradict the fact that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\cal C}(X,Y)
.

Case 1
(
y\leq x
):

In this case, a nearest neighbor of
x^{\prime}
cannot be smaller than
y
. Hence,

y^{\prime}
cannot be its nearest neighbor. Also for
y^{\prime}<y
, its nearest neighbor
has to be also smaller than
x
, hence
x^{\prime}
cannot be its nearest neighbor
either.

Case 2
(
x<y
):

A nearest neighbor of
x^{\prime}
cannot be smaller than
y
. Hence,
y^{\prime}
cannot be
its nearest neighbor. Since
y^{\prime}<y
, we have
y^{\prime}\leq x
in this case.
Therefore, a nearest neighbor
y^{\prime}
has to be smaller than
x
. So,
x^{\prime}

cannot be its nearest neighbor.

∎

We wrap up this section with our final result of this section in the
following theorem.

Theorem 2.8
.

For
d\geq 1
, there exist compact subsets
X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

{\cal C}_{NN}(X,T(Y))
is not an optimal correspondence for any
Euclidean isometry
T\in\mathcal{E}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Proof.

For
d\geq 2
, we refer the readers to Example
2.3
and Figure
1
. We
also note here that there does not exist any non-trivial Euclidean isometry

T
such that
{\cal C}_{opt}
becomes the nearest neighbor correspondence.

In the case
d=1
, we use
X,Y
from Example
2.4
and Figure
3
.
Here,
{\cal C}_{opt}
must have crossings—even when one considers the reflection
of
Y
. By Lemma
2.7
,
{\cal C}_{opt}
cannot be produced by any nearest
neighbor correspondence.
∎

3
Approximating Gromov-Hausdorff Distance in
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}

This section is devoted to our main result (Theorem
3.2
) on approximating the
Gromov-Hausdorff distance between subsets of the real line. Unless stated
otherwise, in this section we always assume that
X,Y
are compact subsets
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
and both are equipped with the standard Euclidean metric denoted by

\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert
.

Definition 3.1

(Standard Configuration)
.

Let
{\cal C}
be any correspondence between two compact subsets
X,Y
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
.
There exist
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in{\cal C}
such that
\big\lvert\left\lvert x-x^{\prime}\right\rvert-\left\lvert y-y^{\prime}\right\rvert\big\rvert=D
,
where
D
is the distortion of
{\cal C}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
that
x\leq x^{\prime}
and
\left\lvert x-x^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert y-y^{\prime}\right\rvert
. Then, there exists an

{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
-isometry such that, when applied on
Y
,
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
are
arranged as in Figure
2
(b). For the proofs that follow, we assume this
standard configuration for any given correspondence
{\cal C}
, and denote by

\{(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\}
a fixed pair that has this property.

We have already noted that
d_{GH}(X,Y)\leq d_{H,iso}(X,Y)
for any compact

X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
; see
[
4
]
. Together with this,
Theorem
3.2
thus gives us the approximation algorithm for
d_{GH}
with an
approximation factor of
\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\right)
. Later in Theorem
3.10
, we
also show that the upper bound of Theorem
3.2
is tight.

Theorem 3.2

(Approximation of Gromov-Hausdorff Distance)
.

For any two compact
X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
we have

d_{H,iso}(X,Y)\leq\frac{5}{4}d_{GH}(X,Y).

Proof.

In order to prove the result, it suffices to show that for any correspondence

{\cal C}\in{\cal C}(X,Y)
with
Dist({\cal C})=D
, there exists a Euclidean isometry

T\in\mathcal{E}({\mathbb{R}}^{1})
such that

d_{H}(X,T(Y))\leq\frac{5D}{8}.

Depending on the crossing (Definition
2.6
)
behavior, we classify a given correspondence into three main types: no double
crossing (Definition
3.3
), wide crossing (Definition
3.6
), and no
wide crossing, and we divide the proof for each type into Theorem
3.4
,
Theorem
3.8
, and Theorem
3.9
, respectively.
∎

3.1
No Double Crossings

We start with the definition of a double crossing edge.

Definition 3.3
.

An edge in a correspondence

{\cal C}\in{\cal C}(X,Y)
is said to be a
double crossing
if it crosses both the
edges
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and
(x,y)
as defined in Definition
3.1
; see Figure
8
.

In the following theorem, we consider the case when there is no double
crossing edge in
{\cal C}
.

Theorem 3.4

(No Double Crossing)
.

For a correspondence
{\cal C}\in{\cal C}(X,Y)
without any double crossing, there exists
a value
\Delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

d_{H}(X,Y+\Delta)\leq\frac{5D}{8},

where
D=Dist({\cal C})
.

Proof.

In the trivial case, when
d_{H}(X,Y)\leq\frac{5D}{8}
, we take
\Delta=0
. We
now assume the non-trivial case that
d_{H}(X,Y)>\frac{5D}{8}
. As shown in
Figure
4
, we define the following two subsets of
X
:

\begin{split}A&=\left\{a\in X\cap\left(x+D,\infty\right)\mid\exists~b\in Y\cap[y^{\prime},y]\mbox{ with }(a,b)\in{\cal C}\right\},\\
A^{\prime}&=\left\{a\in X\cap\left(-\infty,x^{\prime}-D\right)\mid\exists~b\in Y\cap[y^{\prime},y]\mbox{ with }(a,b)\in{\cal C}\right\}.\end{split}

(2)

Figure 4
:
The no double crossing case is shown. The sets
A,A^{\prime}
and
B,B^{\prime}

are shown as subsets of the thick, blue intervals in the top and bottom.

We also define the following two subsets of
Y
:

\begin{split}B&=\left\{b\in Y\cap\left(y^{\prime}+D,y-D\right)\mid\exists~a\in X\cap(x,\infty)\mbox{ with }(a,b)\in{\cal C}\right\},\\
B^{\prime}&=\left\{b\in Y\cap\left(y^{\prime}+D,y-D\right)\mid\exists~a\in X\cap(-\infty,x^{\prime})\mbox{ with }(a,b)\in{\cal C}\right\}.\end{split}

(3)

As shown in Lemma
A.3
, not all pairs of the above defined sets can be
non-empty together. The assumption that
d_{H}(X,Y)>\frac{5D}{8}
implies, again
from Lemma
A.3
, that one of the above sets must be non-empty. Without any
loss of generality, it then suffices to study only the following three unique
cases:

1.

A\neq\emptyset,B=\emptyset

2.

A=\emptyset,B\neq\emptyset

3.

A\neq\emptyset,B\neq\emptyset
.

For each of the cases, we show that a positive number
\Delta
can be chosen
such that
d_{H}(X,Y+\Delta)\leq\frac{5D}{8}
.

Case 1
(
A\neq\emptyset,B=\emptyset
):

We denote
p_{0}=\max{A}
and
\varepsilon=p_{0}-x-D
. We also let
q_{0}\in Y\cap[y^{\prime},y]

such that
(p_{0},q_{0})\in{\cal C}
; let
\varepsilon^{\prime}=(y-q_{0})
; see Figure
5
. From
the assumption that
d_{H}(X,Y)>\frac{5D}{8}
, we first note that

\varepsilon>\frac{D}{8}
. We also argue that

\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq D-\varepsilon,\text{ and }\varepsilon\leq\frac{D}{2}.

(4)

Figure 5
:
No double crossing Case (1) is shown. The set
A
is a subset
of the thick, blue interval in the top.

To see (
4
), we start by observing that
(p_{0}-x^{\prime})\geq(q_{0}-y^{\prime})
. From
the observation together with the distortion of the pair
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and

(p_{0},q_{0})
, we get

D\geq\left\lvert(p_{0}-x^{\prime})-(q_{0}-y^{\prime})\right\rvert=(p_{0}-x^{\prime})-(q_{0}-y^{\prime})=(p_{0}-q_{0})-(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})=\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}.

So,
\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq D-\varepsilon
. In particular,
\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq D
. So, from the distortion
of the pair
(x,y)
and
(p_{0},q_{0})
, we also get

D\geq\left\lvert(p_{0}-x)-(y-q_{0})\right\rvert=\left\lvert(D+\varepsilon)-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right\rvert=D+\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}.

This implies
that
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
. As a result, we also have

\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon+\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\varepsilon)\leq\frac{D}{2}.

We now show that
\overrightarrow{d}_{H}(X,Y+\Delta)\leq\frac{5D}{8}
, for any
translation amount
\Delta
such that

\begin{cases}\Delta=\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8},\text{ when }\frac{D}{8}<\varepsilon\leq\frac{D}{4}\\
\Delta\in\left[\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8},\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{8}\right],\text{ when }\frac{D}{4}<\varepsilon\leq\frac{D}{2}.\end{cases}

(5)

Due to (
4
) and the fact that
\varepsilon>\frac{D}{8}
, we immediately note
from (
5
) that

0<\Delta\leq\frac{5D}{8}.

In order to cover

X
, we consider the following (possibly overlapping) intervals to cover the
real line:

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(-\infty,y^{\prime}-\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta\right),{\cal I}_{2}=\left[y^{\prime}-\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta,y^{\prime}+\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta\right],

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(y^{\prime}+\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta,q_{0}-\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta\right),{\cal I}_{4}=\left[q_{0}-\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta,q_{0}+\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta\right],

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{5}

\displaystyle=\left[y-\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta,y+\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta\right],\text{ and }{\cal I}_{6}=\left(y+\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta,\infty\right).

Since
y-q_{0}=\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq D
from (
4
), the intervals
{\cal I}_{4}
and
{\cal I}_{5}

intersect. So, the union of the intervals is the entire real line. For an
arbitrary point
a\in X
from any of the above intervals, we show that there
exists a point
b\in Y
such that
\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
. The
intervals
{\cal I}_{2}
,
{\cal I}_{4}
, and
{\cal I}_{5}
are
covered
by
y^{\prime}
,
q_{0}
, and

y
, respectively. So, we present our argument only for
{\cal I}_{1},{\cal I}_{3}
, and

{\cal I}_{6}
.

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal I}_{1}}
)

Let
a\in{\cal I}_{1}\cap X
and
b\in Y
such that

(a,b)\in{\cal C}
. From
\Delta\leq\frac{5D}{8}
, it follows that
a<y^{\prime}
. We first
note that
(a,b)
cannot cross
(x,y)
, since there is no double crossing. We
next argue that
(a,b)
does not cross
(p_{0},q_{0})
, either. We assume the
contrary that
(a,b)
crosses
(p_{0},q_{0})
, consequently
b\in[q_{0},y]
. So, we
get
(b-q_{0})\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
. And, the distortion of the pair
(a,b)
and

(p_{0},q_{0})
yields the following contradiction:

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\geq\big\lvert\left\lvert p_{0}-a\right\rvert-\left\lvert q_{0}-b\right\rvert\big\rvert=\left\lvert(x+D+\varepsilon-a)-(b-q_{0})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert(x-a)+D+\varepsilon-(b-q_{0})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=(x-a)+D+\varepsilon-(b-q_{0}),\text{ since }(b-q_{0})\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq D\text{ from (\ref{eqn:eps})}

\displaystyle=[(x-x^{\prime})+(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})+(y^{\prime}-a)]+D+\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})+(y^{\prime}-a)+D+\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime},\text{ since }(x-x^{\prime})\geq 0

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{2}+(y^{\prime}-a)+D+\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{D}{2}+\left(\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)+D+\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime},\text{ since }a\in{\cal I}_{1}

\displaystyle=D+\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}+\frac{9D}{8}-\Delta\right)

\displaystyle>D,\text{ since the second term is positive by Lemma~\ref{lem:eps}}.

Now that we have
(a,b)
not crossing
(p_{0},q_{0})
, we show that

\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
. If
b\geq a
, then from the distortion
bound of
D
for the pair of (non-crossing) edges
(a,b)
and
(p_{0},q_{0})
, we
get
b-a\leq\frac{D}{2}-(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime})
. From (
5
), it is evident
that
\Delta\leq\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}
. So,

\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert=\Delta+(b-a)\leq\Delta+\left[\frac{D}{2}-(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime})\right]\leq\frac{D}{2}.

If
b\leq a
, then from the distortion bound of
D
for the
pair of (non-crossing) edges
(a,b)
and
(x,y)
, we have

a-b\leq\frac{D}{2}
. Since we have already observed
that
\Delta\leq\frac{5D}{8}
, we also have

\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
. In either case, we conclude that

\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
.

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal I}_{3}}
)

We note here that the distance between the
endpoints of
{\cal I}_{3}
is

\displaystyle\left(q_{0}-\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta\right)-\left(y^{\prime}+\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta\right)

\displaystyle=(q_{0}-y^{\prime})-\frac{10D}{8}

\displaystyle=(y-\varepsilon^{\prime})-y^{\prime}-\frac{10D}{8}

\displaystyle=(y-y^{\prime})-\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{10D}{8}

\displaystyle=(x-x^{\prime}+D)-\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{10D}{8}

\displaystyle=(x-x^{\prime})-\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\frac{D}{4}\right)

So,
{\cal I}_{3}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
x-x^{\prime}>\varepsilon^{\prime}+\frac{D}{4}
. We also note
similarly that
{\cal I}_{3}\subseteq[x^{\prime},x]
. If
{\cal I}_{3}
is empty, there is nothing
show. Let us assume that it is non-empty.

Let
a\in{\cal I}_{3}\cap X
and
b\in Y
such that
(a,b)\in{\cal C}
. Since
a\in[x^{\prime},x]
,
the edge
(a,b)
cannot cross
(x,y)
or
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
because of
Lemma
A.1
. Moreover,
\left\lvert b-a\right\rvert\leq\frac{D}{2}
. We now argue that

(a,b)
does not cross
(p_{0},q_{0})
either. We assume the contrary that the
edge
(a,b)
crosses
(p_{0},q_{0})
, i.e.,
q_{0}<b\leq y
. So, we get the
following contradiction:

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\geq\big\lvert\left\lvert p_{0}-a\right\rvert-\left\lvert q_{0}-b\right\rvert\big\rvert=\left\lvert(x+D+\varepsilon-a)-(b-q_{0})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert(x-a)+D+\varepsilon-(b-q_{0})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=(x-a)+D+\varepsilon-(b-q_{0}),\text{ since }(b-q_{0})\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq D\text{ from (\ref{eqn:eps})}

\displaystyle=\left(y-\frac{D}{2}\right)-a+D+\varepsilon-(b-q_{0})

\displaystyle=(q_{0}+\varepsilon^{\prime})-\frac{D}{2}-a+D+\varepsilon-(b-q_{0})

\displaystyle=2q_{0}+\varepsilon^{\prime}+\frac{D}{2}-a+\varepsilon-b

\displaystyle\geq 2q_{0}+\varepsilon^{\prime}+(b-a)-a+\varepsilon-b,\text{ since }\frac{D}{2}\geq(b-a)

\displaystyle=2(q_{0}-a)+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle>2\left(\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime},\text{ since }a\in{\cal I}_{3}

\displaystyle=D+\left[\frac{D}{4}-2\Delta+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle\geq D,\text{ since the second term is non-negative by Lemma~\ref{lem:eps-1}}.

Hence,
(a,b)
does not cross
(p_{0},q_{0})
. Therefore, by arguing similar to

{\cal I}_{1}
we conclude
\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
.

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal I}_{6}}
)

For
a\in{\cal I}_{6}\cap X
, we get

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle>y+\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta=\left(p_{0}-\varepsilon-\frac{D}{2}\right)+\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta

\displaystyle=p_{0}+\frac{D}{8}-\varepsilon+\Delta

\displaystyle\geq p_{0},\text{ since }\Delta\geq\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8}\text{ by (\ref{eqn:delta-1})}

\displaystyle=\max A.

By the definition of the set
A
, an edge
(a,b)\in{\cal C}
cannot cross
(x,y)
.
For this reason, it cannot cross
(p_{0},q_{0})
either. The rest of the argument
presented for
{\cal I}_{1}
then goes through. Therefore,

\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
by arguing similar to
{\cal I}_{1}
.

In order to show the other direction, i.e.,

\overrightarrow{d_{H}}(Y+\Delta,X)\leq\frac{5D}{8}
, we argue that there exists
a translation amount
\Delta
satisfying (
5
). To that end, we
consider the following (possibly overlapping) partition of the real line into
intervals:

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(-\infty,x-\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right),{\cal J}_{2}=\left[x-\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta,x+\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right],

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(x+\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta,p_{0}-\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right),{\cal J}_{4}=\left[p_{0}-\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta,p_{0}+\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right],\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{5}

\displaystyle=\left(p_{0}+\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta,\infty\right).

For an arbitrary point
b\in Y
from any of the above intervals, we now show
that there exists a point
a
in
X
such that

\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
. The intervals
{\cal J}_{2}
and
{\cal J}_{4}
are

covered
by
x
and
p_{0}
, respectively. We present our argument only
for
{\cal J}_{1},{\cal J}_{3}
, and
{\cal J}_{5}
. While the argument for
{\cal J}_{1}
and
{\cal J}_{5}
goes
through for any
\Delta
satisfying (
5
), the interval
{\cal J}_{3}
may
require a more particular choice of
\Delta
.

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal J}_{1}}
)

For any
b\in{\cal J}_{1}\cap Y
with an edge

(a,b)\in{\cal C}
, we argue that the edge cannot cross
(p_{0},q_{0})
. Since

\Delta>0
, we note that either
b\in[y^{\prime},y-D)
or
b<y^{\prime}
. If
b\in[y^{\prime},y-D]
,
the edge
(a,b)
cannot cross
(x,y)
, since
B=\emptyset
. If
b<y^{\prime}
, the
edge
(a,b)
cannot cross
(x,y)
, as no double crossing is allowed. Now,

b<y-D\leq q_{0}
implies that the edge
(a,b)
cannot cross
(p_{0},q_{0})
without
first crossing
(x,y)
. The rest of the argument presented for
{\cal I}_{1}
then
goes through. Therefore,
\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
by arguing
similar to
{\cal I}_{1}
.

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal J}_{3}}
)

We observe that
{\cal J}_{3}\neq\emptyset
if and only if

\varepsilon>\frac{D}{4}
. There is nothing to show if
{\cal J}_{3}
is empty. So, we assume
that
\varepsilon>\frac{D}{4}
, and claim that there must exist a
\Delta
in

\left[\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8},\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{8}\right]
, hence satisfying
(
5
), such that for any
b\in Y\cap{\cal J}_{3}
there exists
a\in X

with
\left\lvert a-(b+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
.

We argue by contradiction. Let us assume that no such
\Delta
exists in given
the interval, i.e., for all

\Delta\in\left[\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8},\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{8}\right]
the following
subset of
{\cal J}_{3}
is non-empty:

E(\Delta)=\left\{b\in{\cal J}_{3}\mid X\cap\left[b-\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta,b+\frac{5D}{8}+\Delta\right]=\emptyset\right\}.

Let us also define the following subsets of
Y
:

Y_{\leq}=\left\{b\in Y\mid\text{there exists an edge }(a,b)\in{\cal C}\text{ with }a\leq b\right\}

and

Y_{\geq}=\left\{b\in Y\mid\text{there exists an edge }(a,b)\in{\cal C}\text{ with }a\geq b\right\}.

We note from Lemma
A.6
that
E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\leq}
and
E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\geq}
cannot be both non-empty for any given
\Delta
. Therefore,

E(\Delta)\neq\emptyset
for all
\Delta
implies

i)

either
E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\leq}
is empty and
E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\geq}

non-empty for all
\Delta
, or

ii)

E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\leq}
is non-empty and
E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\geq}
empty
for all
\Delta
.

In order to arrive at a contradiction, we now show, however, that

E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\geq}
is empty when
\Delta=\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8}
, whereas

E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\leq}
is empty when
\Delta=\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{8}
.

When
\Delta=\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8}
, we show that
E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\geq}=\emptyset
.
Consider any
b\in{\cal J}_{3}\cap Y_{\geq}
and an edge
(a,b)\in{\cal C}
such that
a\geq b
. Due to the distortion bound of
D
for the pair of edges
(x,y)
and

(a,b)
, we have
(a-x)\leq D+(y-b)
. So,

\displaystyle a-(b+\Delta)

\displaystyle\leq(x+D+y-b)-(b+\Delta)

\displaystyle\leq x+D+y-2b-\Delta

\displaystyle<x+D+y-2\left(x+\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)-\Delta,\text{ since }b\in{\cal J}_{3}

\displaystyle=D+(y-x)-\frac{10D}{8}+\Delta

\displaystyle=D+\frac{D}{2}-\frac{10D}{8}+\Delta

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{4}+\left(\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\frac{D}{8}\leq\frac{5D}{8},\text{ since }\varepsilon\leq\frac{D}{2}\text{ from (\ref{eqn:eps})}.

So,
b\notin E(\Delta)
. Therefore,
E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\geq}=\emptyset
for

\Delta=\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8}
.

We show now that
E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\leq}=\emptyset
when

\Delta=\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{8}
. To see this, consider
b\in{\cal J}_{3}\cap Y_{\leq}

and an edge
(a,b)\in{\cal C}
such that
a\leq b
. Due to the distortion bound of

D
for the pair of edges
(p_{0},q_{0})
and
(a,b)
, we have
(p_{0}-a)\leq D+(b-q_{0})
. So,

\displaystyle(b+\Delta)-a

\displaystyle\leq(b+\Delta)+D+(b-q_{0})-p_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta+D+2b-q_{0}-p_{0}

\displaystyle<\Delta+D+2\left(p_{0}-\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)-q_{0}-p_{0},\text{ since }b\in{\cal J}_{3}

\displaystyle=(p_{0}-q_{0})-\frac{2D}{8}-\Delta

\displaystyle=\left(\varepsilon+\frac{D}{2}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)-\frac{2D}{8}-\left(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{8}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{5D}{8}.

So,
b\notin E(\Delta)
. Therefore,
E(\Delta)\cap Y_{\leq}=\emptyset
for

\Delta=\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{8}
. This is a contradiction. Hence, there must
exist
\Delta
satisfying (
5
).

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal J}_{5}}
)

Similarly for
b\in{\cal J}_{5}\cap Y
, an edge

(a,b)\in{\cal C}
cannot cross
(x,y)
due to Lemma
A.2
. Moreover, it
cannot cross
(p_{0},q_{0})
. If it did, i.e.,
a\in[x,p_{0}]
, the distortion of
the pair would exceed
D
:

\displaystyle\left\lvert(b-q_{0})-(p_{0}-a)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq(b-q_{0})-p_{0}+\left(b-\frac{D}{2}\right),\text{ as }(b-a)\leq\frac{D}{2},\text{ from Lemma~\ref{lem:standard-1}}

\displaystyle=(b-q_{0})+(b-p_{0})-\frac{D}{2}=[(b-p_{0})+(p_{0}-q_{0})]+(b-p_{0})-\frac{D}{2}

\displaystyle=(p_{0}-q_{0})+2(b-p_{0})-\frac{D}{2}=\left(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}+\frac{D}{2}\right)+2(b-p_{0})-\frac{D}{2}

\displaystyle>\left(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}+\frac{D}{2}\right)+2\left(\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)-\frac{D}{2},\text{ as }b\in{\cal J}_{5}

3.2
Double Crossings

Now, we undertake the task of finding a suitable isometry/alignment when there
is a double crossing in
{\cal C}
. In this case, we may have to consider flipping
Y

to construct such an isometry. We always flip
Y
about the midpoint of
x

and
x^{\prime}
. After flipping, the image of
Y
is denoted by
\widetilde{Y}
and the
image of any
b\in Y
by
\widetilde{b}
. We first present two technical lemmas.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(p,q)\in{\cal C}
be a double crossing; see Figure
8
. If we denote

h=(x-x^{\prime})
,
\varepsilon_{1}=(p-x)
, and
\varepsilon_{2}=(y^{\prime}-q)
, then we have the following:

i)

\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\geq h
,

ii)

\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\leq D-h
,

iii)

h\leq\frac{D}{2}
,
and

iv)

\left\lvert p-\widetilde{q}\right\rvert\leq\frac{D}{2}-h
, where
\widetilde{q}
denotes
the reflection of
q
about the midpoint of
x
and
x^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Figure 8
:
A double crossing
(p,q)
is shown.

i)

Let us assume the contrary, i.e.,
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}+h
. Then, the
distortion for the pairs
(x,y)
and
(p,q)
becomes

\left\lvert\varepsilon_{2}+D+h-\varepsilon_{1}\right\rvert=\varepsilon_{2}+h+D-\varepsilon_{1}>D.

This contradicts the fact
that the distortion of
{\cal C}
is
D
. Therefore, we conclude that

\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\geq h
.

ii)

Since from (i) we have
\varepsilon_{1}\geq\varepsilon_{2}
, from the distortion for the
pairs
(p,q)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, we have

h+\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\leq D.

(8)

So,
\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\leq D-h
.

iii)

From (ii) we have
\varepsilon_{2}+D\geq\varepsilon_{1}
. Hence, the distortion for the
pairs
(p,q)
and
(x,y)
implies

\varepsilon_{2}+D+h-\varepsilon_{1}\leq D.

(9)

Adding (
8
) and (
9
), we get
2h\leq D
. Hence,

h\leq\frac{D}{2}
.

iv)

If
p>\widetilde{q}
, then

p-\widetilde{q}=\varepsilon_{1}-\frac{D}{2}-\varepsilon_{2}\leq(D-h)-\frac{D}{2}-\varepsilon_{2}\leq\frac{D}{2}-h.

Otherwise,

\widetilde{q}-p=\frac{D}{2}+\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}\leq\frac{D}{2}-(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})\leq\frac{D}{2}-h.

Therefore,
\left\lvert p-\widetilde{q}\right\rvert\leq\frac{D}{2}-h
.

∎

In our pursuit of constructing the right isometry, we first define a wide
(double) crossing. We show in Theorem
3.8
, that we need to flip
Y
in
the presence of such a wide crossing.

Definition 3.6

(Wide Crossing)
.

A double crossing edge

is called a

if either

p<\begin{cases}\min A^{\prime},&\text{ if }A^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\\
x^{\prime}-\frac{D}{2},&\text{ if }A\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\max A>y+\frac{3D}{4}\\

p>\begin{cases}\max A,&\text{ if }A\neq\emptyset\\
x+\frac{D}{2},&\text{ if }A^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\min A<y^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{4}\\

first in the following technical lemma.

from Lemma
3.5
, the intervals

{\cal J}_{3}
and
{\cal J}_{4}
intersect. Since
p_{1}-x=\eta_{1}^{\prime}\leq D
from (
14
),
the intervals
{\cal J}_{4}
and
{\cal J}_{5}
intersect. As a result, the union of the
intervals is the entire real line. For an arbitrary point

\widetilde{b}\in\widetilde{Y}
from any of the above intervals, we show that
there exists a point
a\in X
such that

\left\lvert a-(\widetilde{b}+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
. We present the argument only
for
{\cal J}_{1}
and
{\cal J}_{6}
.

\boldsymbol{({\cal J}_{1})}

For any
\widetilde{b}\in\widetilde{Y}\cap{\cal J}_{1}
, we
first argue that
\widetilde{b}<\widetilde{q_{1}}
. When
\eta_{1}<\frac{D}{8}
,
this is definitely true. When
\eta_{1}\geq\frac{D}{8}
, then (
14
)
yields
h\leq\frac{3D}{8}
. So,

\displaystyle\widetilde{b}-\widetilde{q_{1}}

\displaystyle<\left(p_{2}-\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)-\widetilde{q_{1}}=(x^{\prime}-\eta_{2}^{\prime})-\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta-(\widetilde{y}-\eta_{1})

\displaystyle=(x^{\prime}-\widetilde{y})-\frac{5D}{8}+(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}^{\prime}-\Delta)=\frac{D}{2}-\frac{5D}{8}+(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}^{\prime}-\Delta)

\displaystyle=\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}^{\prime}-\frac{D}{8}-\Delta=\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}^{\prime}-\frac{D}{8}-\left(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}-\frac{D}{8}\right),\text{ from (\ref{eqn:delta-2-wide})}

\displaystyle=\eta_{2}-\eta_{2}^{\prime}\leq 0,\text{ from (\ref{eqn:eta-2})}.

So,
\widetilde{b}<\widetilde{q_{1}}
. Consequently,
b>q_{1}
. Since
q_{1}=\max B_{1}
, for any edge
(a,b)
, we must have
a<x
by the definition of the set

B_{1}
; see (
13
). Therefore,
(a,b)
crosses
(p_{1},q_{1})
. So after
flipping
Y
, the edge
(a,\widetilde{b})
does not cross

(p_{1},\widetilde{q_{1}})
. The rest of the argument presented for
{\cal I}_{1}
then
goes through. Hence arguing similar to
{\cal I}_{1}
, we have

\left\lvert a-(\widetilde{b}+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
.

\boldsymbol{({\cal J}_{6})}

For
\widetilde{b}\in\widetilde{Y}\cap{\cal J}_{6}
, we
argue that
\widetilde{b}>\widetilde{q_{2}}
. We have

\displaystyle\widetilde{b}-\widetilde{q_{2}}

\displaystyle>\left(p_{1}+\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)-\widetilde{q_{2}}=(x+\eta_{1}^{\prime})+\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta-(\widetilde{y^{\prime}}+\eta_{2})

\displaystyle=(x-\widetilde{y^{\prime}})+\frac{5D}{8}+(\eta_{1}^{\prime}-\eta_{2}-\Delta)=-\frac{D}{2}+\frac{5D}{8}+(\eta_{1}^{\prime}-\eta_{2}-\Delta)

\displaystyle=\eta_{1}^{\prime}-\eta_{2}+\frac{D}{8}-\Delta\geq\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}+\frac{D}{8}-\Delta,\text{ as }\eta_{1}^{\prime}\geq\eta_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}+\frac{D}{8}-\left(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}-\frac{D}{8}\right),\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}+\frac{D}{8}-\left(h-\frac{3D}{8}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{D}{4},\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}+\frac{D}{2}-h\right\}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\text{ since }\eta_{1}\geq\eta_{2}\text{ as assumed, and }h\leq\frac{D}{2}.

So,
\widetilde{b}>\widetilde{q_{2}}
. Consequently,
b<q_{2}
. Since
q_{2}=\min B_{2}
, for edge
(a,b)
, we have
a>x^{\prime}
by the definition of the set
B_{2}
; see
(
16
). On the other hand, we have
a\notin[x^{\prime},x]
due to
Lemma
A.1
. As a result,
a>x
. Furthermore, we must have
a>p_{1}
. If
not, i.e.,
a\in[x,p_{1}]
, then the distortion of the pair of edges

(p_{1},\widetilde{q_{1}})
and
(a,\widetilde{b})
exceeds
D
:

\displaystyle(\widetilde{b}-\widetilde{q_{1}})-(p_{1}-a)

\displaystyle=[(\widetilde{b}-p_{1})+(p_{1}-\widetilde{q_{1}})]-(p_{1}-a)

\displaystyle=\left[(\widetilde{b}-p_{1})+\eta_{1}^{\prime}+h+\frac{D}{2}+\eta_{1}\right]-(p_{1}-a)

\displaystyle\geq\left[(\widetilde{b}-p_{1})+\eta_{1}^{\prime}+h+\frac{D}{2}+\eta_{1}\right]-\eta_{1}^{\prime},\text{ since }a\in(x,p_{1})

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{b}-p_{1})+h+\frac{D}{2}+\eta_{1}

\displaystyle>\left(\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)+h+\frac{D}{2}+\eta_{1},\text{ since }\widetilde{b}\in{\cal J}_{5}

\displaystyle=D+\frac{D}{8}+h+\eta_{1}-\Delta\geq D,\text{ for any }\Delta\text{ satisfying }(\ref{eqn:delta-2-wide}).

Therefore,
a>p_{1}
and the edge
(a,\widetilde{b})
does not cross

(p_{1},\widetilde{q_{1}})
. Hence arguing similar to
{\cal I}_{1}
, we have

\left\lvert a-(\widetilde{b}+\Delta)\right\rvert\leq\frac{5D}{8}
.
This completes the proof for wide crossing.
∎

In order to complete our analysis of various types of correspondences, we show
now that a flip is not required if there is no wide crossing in
{\cal C}
.

Theorem 3.9

(No Wide Crossing)
.

Let
{\cal C}
be a
correspondence between two compact sets
X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}
with distortion
D
. If
there are double crossings but not wide, then there exists a value
\Delta\in{\mathbb{R}}

such that

d_{H}(X,Y+\Delta)\leq\frac{5D}{8}.

Proof.

We assume that there are double crossings in
{\cal C}
, but none of them are wide;
see Figure
12
.

Figure 12
:
No wide crossing exists, but there are double
crossings.

Since there are double crossings, at least one of the following two subsets of

Y
is non-empty:

\begin{split}C_{1}&=\left\{b\in Y\cap\left(-\infty,y^{\prime}\right)\mid\exists~a\in X\cap(x,\infty)\mbox{ with }(a,b)\in{\cal C}\right\},\\
C_{2}&=\left\{b\in Y\cap\left(y,\infty\right)\mid\exists~a\in X\cap(-\infty,x^{\prime})\mbox{ with }(a,b)\in{\cal C}\right\}.\\
\end{split}

(21)

We also define
q_{1}=\min C_{1}
,
q_{2}=\max C_{2}
, and edges

(p_{1},q_{1}),(p_{2},q_{2})\in{\cal C}
. Let us now denote
\xi_{1}=y^{\prime}-q_{1}
and
\xi_{2}=q_{2}-y
.
If
d_{H}(X,Y)\leq\frac{5D}{8}
, we take
\Delta=0
. Let us assume the non-trivial
case that
d_{H}(X,Y)>\frac{5D}{8}
. Since wide crossings are not allowed, we have
from Definition
3.6
that
p_{2}\geq x^{\prime}-D,p_{1}\leq p_{0}
and from Lemma
3.5

that
h\leq\frac{D}{2}
. Consequently, the assumption that

d_{H}(X,Y)>\frac{5D}{8}
implies either
\varepsilon>\frac{D}{8}
,
\xi_{1}>\frac{D}{8}
,
or
\xi_{2}>\frac{D}{8}
. Without any loss of generality, we also assume that

\xi_{1}\geq\xi_{2}
so that the translation of
Y
occurs from left to right.
We now consider the following two cases.

Case 1
(
\varepsilon\geq\xi_{1}
):

This case is equivalent to Case (
1
) of Theorem
3.4
. In Case (
1
) of
Theorem
3.4
, the intervals
{\cal I}_{1},{\cal I}_{6}
and
{\cal J}_{1},{\cal J}_{5}
use the fact that
there are no double crossings. We show here that arguments presented for the
intervals still work in the presence of (non-wide) double crossings.

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal I}_{1}}
)

For any
a\in{\cal I}_{1}\cap X
with an edge
(a,b)
, we
show that
(a,b)
cannot cross
(x,y)
. And, the rest of the argument for

{\cal I}_{1}
goes through. For any
a\in{\cal I}_{1}\cap X
, we note that
a
satisfies
(
10
), the condition of a wide crossing. When
\varepsilon\leq\frac{D}{4}
,
we have

\displaystyle x^{\prime}-a

\displaystyle>\frac{D}{2}+\left(\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)=D-\Delta+\frac{D}{8}=D-\left(\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8}\right)+\frac{D}{8},\text{ from (\ref{eqn:delta-1})}

\displaystyle=D+\left(\frac{D}{4}-\varepsilon\right)\geq D,\text{ since }\varepsilon\leq\frac{D}{4}.

When
\varepsilon>\frac{D}{4}
, we have

x^{\prime}-a>\frac{D}{2}+\left(\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)\geq\frac{D}{2},\text{ as
}\Delta\leq\frac{5D}{8}.

Consequently, an edge
(a,b)\in{\cal C}
cannot be a
double crossing, otherwise it would become a wide crossing. Hence,
(a,b)

cannot cross
(x,y)
.

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal I}_{6}}
)

For any
\in{\cal I}_{6}\cap X
with an edge
(a,b)
, we
argue that
(a,b)
cannot cross
(x,y)
. And, the rest of the argument for

{\cal I}_{6}
goes through. We have already noted there that
a>\max A
. Hence,

(a,b)
cannot cross
(x,y)
, otherwise it becomes a wide crossing.

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal J}_{1}}
)

Since
h\leq\frac{D}{2}
from Lemma
3.5
, here
we can instead consider

{\cal J}_{1}=\left(-\infty,x^{\prime}-\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right).

For any
b\in{\cal J}_{1}\cap Y

with an edge
(a,b)
, we argue that
(a,b)
cannot cross
(x,y)
. Hence, the
rest of the argument for
{\cal J}_{1}
presented in Case (
1
) of Theorem
3.4

goes through. We note that
b<q_{1}
, since

\Delta\geq\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8}\geq\xi_{1}-\frac{D}{8}
. From the definition of the
set
C_{1}
, the edge
(a,b)
cannot be a double crossing.

(
\boldsymbol{{\cal J}_{5}}
)

For any
b\in{\cal J}_{5}\cap Y
with an edge
(a,b)
, we
argue that
(a,b)
cannot cross
(p_{0},q_{0})
, i.e.,
a>p_{0}
. And, the rest of
the argument for
{\cal J}_{5}
goes through. Due to Lemma
A.2
, we can have
either
a<x^{\prime}
,
a\in[x,p_{0}]
, or
a>p_{0}
. We arrive at a contradiction when
assumed the first two.

We first assume that
a<x^{\prime}
, i.e.,
(a,b)
is a double crossing. This yields a
contradiction, as
(a,b)
becomes a wide crossing. When
\varepsilon\leq\frac{D}{4}
,
we have

\displaystyle x^{\prime}-a

\displaystyle\geq b-y,\text{ from Lemma~\ref{lem:cross}}

\displaystyle>\frac{D}{2}+\varepsilon+\left(\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)\geq\frac{D}{2}+\varepsilon+\frac{5D}{8}-\left(\varepsilon-\frac{D}{8}\right)\geq D.

When
\varepsilon>\frac{D}{4}
, we have

\displaystyle x^{\prime}-a

\displaystyle\geq b-y>\frac{D}{2}+\varepsilon+\left(\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{D}{2}+\varepsilon+\frac{5D}{8}-\left(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{8}\right),\text{ from (\ref{eqn:delta-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{3D}{2}-\varepsilon^{\prime}\geq\frac{D}{2},\text{ since }\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq D\text{ from (\ref{eqn:eps})}.

So,
(a,b)
cannot be a double crossing.

We now assume that
a\in[x,p_{0}]
to arrive at the contradiction that the
distortion of
(a,b)
and
(p_{0},q_{0})
exceeds
D
:

\displaystyle(b-q_{0})-(p_{0}-a)

\displaystyle=[(b-p_{0})+(p_{0}-q_{0})]-(p_{0}-a)

\displaystyle>\left[\left(\frac{5D}{8}-\Delta\right)+\left(\varepsilon+\frac{D}{2}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\right]-(p_{0}-a)

\displaystyle\geq\left[\frac{9D}{8}+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\left(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}-\frac{3D}{8}\right)\right]-(p_{0}-a),\text{ from (\ref{eqn:delta-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{12D}{8}-(p_{0}-a)\geq\frac{12D}{8}-(b-a),\text{ as }b\geq p_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{12D}{8}-\frac{D}{2},\text{ from Lemma~\ref{lem:standard-1}}

\displaystyle\geq D.

Hence,
a>p_{0}
, i.e.,
(a,b)
cannot cross
(p_{0},q_{0})
.

Case 2
(
\varepsilon\geq\xi_{1}
):

This case is equivalent to Case (
2
) of Theorem
3.4
, and the argument
is analogous.

This concludes the proof.

We conclude this section by showing in Theorem
3.10
that the bound of
Theorem

is a tight upper bound in the following sense:

Theorem 3.10

(Tightness of the Bound)
.

For any
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{4}
and
\delta>0
, there exist compact
X,Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}

with
d_{GH}(X,Y)=\delta

and

d_{H,iso}(X,Y)=\left(\frac{5}{4}-\varepsilon\right)\delta.

Proof.

It suffices to assume that
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{4(2k+1)}
for some

k\in\mathbb{N}
. We now take (sorted)

X=\{x^{\prime},x,x_{k},x_{k-1},\cdots,x_{1},x_{0}\}\mbox{ and
}Y=\{y^{\prime},y_{0},y_{1},\cdots,y_{k-1},y_{k},y\},

with distances as shown in
Figure
13
. As a result, we also have
(y_{i}-x)=4i\varepsilon\delta
and

(x_{k}-y_{i})=2\delta+4(k-i+1)\varepsilon\delta
,
\forall i\in\{0,1,2,\cdots,k\}
.

and

Y
about the

x^{\prime}

by

To prove our claim that
d_{H,iso}(X,Y)=\left(\frac{5}{4}-\varepsilon\right)\delta
, we
consider translating both
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
, the reflection of
Y
about
the midpoint of
x
and
x^{\prime}
.

When translating
\widetilde{Y}
, we note that the smallest Hausdorff distance
of
\frac{3\delta}{2}
is achieved for a translation of
\widetilde{Y}
by an
amount of
\frac{\delta}{2}
to the right. For this amount of translation,

\widetilde{y^{\prime}}

and
x_{0}
, where
\widetilde{y^{\prime}}

is the reflection of
y^{\prime}
about the midpoint of
x
and
x^{\prime}
. And all the other

are at distance at least

\left(50\delta-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)
from
x
.

Now, we consider translating
Y

\Delta\in{\mathbb{R}}

that

If

4
Conclusions and Future Work

In our investigation, we focus on approximating Gromov-Hausdorff distance by the
Hausdorff distance for subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
. The use of
d_{H,iso}
yields an
approximation algorithm with a tight factor. We do not know, however, if other
algorithms can be devised with a better approximation factor. We believe that
the problem of computing the Gromov-Hausdorff distance or even approximating it
by a factor less than
\frac{5}{4}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
is NP-hard. The question of a
polynomial-time approximation algorithm for subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is still open for

d\geq 2
.
