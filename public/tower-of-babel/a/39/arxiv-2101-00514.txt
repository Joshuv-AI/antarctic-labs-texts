Envelopes for multivariate linear regression with linearly constrained coefficients
R. Dennis Cook, Liliana Forzani, Lan Liu

Abstract
A constrained multivariate linear model is a multivariate linear model with the columns of its coefficient matrix constrained to lie in a known subspace. This class of models includes those typically used to study growth curves and longitudinal data. Envelope methods have been proposed to improve estimation efficiency in the class of unconstrained multivariate linear models, but have not yet been developed for constrained models that we develop in this article. We first compare the standard envelope estimator based on an unconstrained multivariate model with the standard estimator arising from a constrained multivariate model in terms of bias and efficiency. Then, to further improve efficiency, we propose a novel envelope estimator based on a constrained multivariate model. Novel envelope-based testing methods are also proposed. We provide support for our proposals by simulations and by studying the classical dental data and data from the China Health and Nutrition Survey and a study of probiotic capacity to reduced Salmonella infection . Key Words: Growth curves, envelope models, repeated measures

Envelope estimator stemming from Model (

Consider a subspace
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{r}
that satisfies the two conditions (i)
{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathbf{Y}}\mid{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{x}}_{1}\sim{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathbf{Y}}\mid{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{x}}_{2}
for all relevant
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
and (ii)
{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathbf{Y}}\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\;\,{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathbf{Y}}\mid{\mathbf{X}}
. Condition (i) insures that the marginal distribution of
{\mathbf{Q}}_{S}{\mathbf{Y}}
does not depend on
{\mathbf{X}}
, while statement (ii) insures that, given
{\mathbf{X}}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathbf{Y}}
cannot provide material information via an association with
{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathbf{Y}}

{\mathbf{X}}
on the distribution of
{\mathbf{Y}}
is concentrated solely in
{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathbf{Y}}

{\mathbf{Q}}_{S}{\mathbf{Y}}
that are unaffected by changes in
{\mathbf{X}}
and in doing so produce downstream gains in estimative and predictive efficiency.

In terms of model (
1.1

{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}
and condition (ii) holds if and only if
{\mathcal{S}}
is a reducing subspace of
\Sigma

{\mathcal{S}}
must decompose
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}={\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}+{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}

\Sigma
that contains
{\mathcal{B}}
, which is called the
\Sigma

{\mathcal{B}}
and is represented as
{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Sigma$}}}({\mathcal{B}})

(
Cook et al., 2010

Given the envelope dimension

2.3
Potential bias in
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\mathrm{cm}}

Assuming that
{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
,
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{\mathrm{cm}}
and
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\mathrm{cm}}
are unbiased estimators of
\alpha
and
\beta
. However, if

then both
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{\mathrm{cm}}
and
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\mathrm{cm}}
are biased, which could materially affect the estimators:
E(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{\mathrm{cm}})=({\mathbf{U}}^{T}{\mathbf{U}})^{-1}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}
and

is
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}-{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathbf{U}}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}={\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{U}}}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}

\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\mathrm{cm}}
. Simulation results that illustrate the potential bias effects are discussed in Section
4.2
. Otherwise, we assume that
{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
in the remainder of this article unless indicated otherwise.

2.4
Comparison of asymptotic variances of

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}^{T}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{0}^{T}.

2.4.2
{\mathcal{U}}\supseteq{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Sigma$}}}({\mathcal{B}})

Assuming that

Proposition 2.3

Assume that

1.

If
{\mathbf{c}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Sigma$}}}({\mathcal{B}})
then
\mathrm{avar}(\sqrt{n}{\mathbf{c}}^{T}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\mathrm{cm}})=\mathrm{avar}(\sqrt{n}{\mathbf{c}}^{T}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\mathrm{em}})

2.

If
{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathrm{span}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{02})
then
\mathrm{avar}(\sqrt{n}{\mathbf{c}}^{T}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\mathrm{cm}})\leq\mathrm{avar}(\sqrt{n}{\mathbf{c}}^{T}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\mathrm{em}})

3.

If
{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathrm{span}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{01})

The central message of this lemma is that the difference between the asymptotic covariance matrices for the estimators

Although the above derivation is under two simple cases where

and the envelope space

but if the basis
{\mathbf{U}}
in the constrained model is incorrect or if the constrained model is excessively parameterized, then envelopes can be much more efficient. This motivated us to incorporate envelopes into the constrained model so that we can further improve efficiency if constraints are reasonably well modeled for the data

3.2
Enveloping
\beta

Estimation of

\beta
under the constrained model (

), it does affect the envelope estimator of
\beta
. This raises the issue of selecting a good basis for the purpose of estimating

Consider re-parameterizing
{\mathbf{U}}
as
{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}
and
\alpha
as
{\mathbf{V}}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}
for some positive definite matrix
{\mathbf{V}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
, giving
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}={\mathbf{U}}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}=({\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{V}}^{-1})({\mathbf{V}}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$})
. We could use either

to estimate

In this section, we show that the envelope estimator of
\beta

The following two propositions provide technical tools for demonstrating that the maximum likelihood envelope estimator of

is simply

when

is semi-orthogonal, where
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{\mathrm{ecm}}
is the envelope estimator of
\alpha
under the constrained model (
1.2
).

Proposition 3.2

(a) Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
be a reducing subspace of the symmetric matrix
{\mathbf{M}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
, and let
{\mathbf{V}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times k}
be a semi-orthogonal matrix. Then
{\mathbf{V}}{\mathcal{S}}
is a reducing subspace of
{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{V}}^{T}
.
(b) Let
{\mathcal{D}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
be a reducing subspace of
{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{V}}^{T}
. Then
{\mathbf{V}}^{T}{\mathcal{D}}
is a reducing subspace of
{\mathbf{M}}
.

Proposition 3.3

Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathbf{M}}}({\mathcal{S}})\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
be the smallest reducing subspace of the symmetric matrix
{\mathbf{M}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
that contains
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, and let
{\mathbf{V}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times k}
be a semi-orthogonal matrix. Then
{\mathbf{V}}{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathbf{M}}}({\mathcal{S}})
is the smallest reducing subspace of
{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{V}}^{T}
that contains
{\mathbf{V}}{\mathcal{S}}
; that is,
{\mathbf{V}}{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathbf{M}}}({\mathcal{S}})={\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{V}}^{T}}({\mathbf{V}}{\mathcal{S}})
.

These two propositions show that the results of Section
3.1
can be used straightforwardly to get the envelope estimator of
{\mathbf{U}}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}
when
{\mathbf{U}}
is semi-orthognonal. The standard maximum likelihood estimator of
{\mathbf{U}}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}
is just
{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{\mathrm{cm}}
with asymptotic covariance matrix
{\mathbf{U}}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{D|S}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}

3.1
, we seek the maximum likelihood estimator of
{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathbf{U}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Sigma$}}_{D|S}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}}({\mathbf{U}}{\mathcal{A}})

3.3
is equal to
{\mathbf{U}}{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Sigma$}}_{D|S}}({\mathcal{A}})

From Proposition
3.3
, the maximum likelihood estimator of
{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathbf{U}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Sigma$}}_{D|S}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}}({\mathbf{U}}{\mathcal{A}})

{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{{\mathcal{E}}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\Sigma$}}_{D|S}}({\mathcal{A}})
, which implies that envelope estimator of
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}={\mathbf{U}}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}

\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{\mathrm{ecm}}={\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{\mathrm{ecm}}
with asymptotic variance
{\mathbf{U}}\mathrm{avar}(\sqrt{n}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{\mathrm{ecm}}){\mathbf{U}}^{T}
. Propositions
3.2
and
3.3
also suggest how to proceed when re-prameterizing as

\mathbf{O}
is an orthognal matrix and
{\mathbf{U}}
is not necessarily orthogonal. In that case the envelope estimator of
\mathbf{O}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}
is simply
\mathbf{O}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{\mathrm{ecm}}
, and so the envelope estimator of
\beta
is invariant under orthogonal re-paramterization of the kind used here.

Thus, to consider constrained model envelope under a linear transformation of
{\mathbf{U}}
, it suffices to consider a re-scaling transformation. That is, we consider
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}={\mathbf{U}}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}=({\mathbf{U}}\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}^{-1})(\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$})

\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}=\mathrm{diag}(1,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k})

in their development of a scaled version of envelope model (

Without loss of generality, we cast our discussion of scaling in the context of conditional model (
2.3
). We suppose that there is a scaling of the response
{\mathbf{Y}}_{D}
so that the scaled response
\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}{\mathbf{Y}}_{D}
follows an envelope model in
\Lambda
\alpha
with the envelope

(\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}_{0})
denote an orthogonal matrix. Then we can parameterize

and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{D|S}\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}^{T}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Y}}_{Di}\mid({\mathbf{X}}_{i},{\mathbf{Y}}_{Si})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{0}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}{\mathbf{X}}_{i}+\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{D|S}{\mathbf{Y}}_{Si}+{\mathbf{e}}_{D|Si},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{D|S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}^{T}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}_{0}\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}_{0}^{T})\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}^{-1}.

After maximizing the log likelihood over all parameters except

3.3.1
Evaluating the choice of
{\mathbf{U}}
by testing rows of
\alpha

Having selected the dimension
u
of the envelope, we may also want to test if
{\mathbf{U}}
is over specified. This can be achieved by testing if individual rows of
\alpha
are equal to
0
. For instance, if
\mathbf{U}^{T}(t)=(1,t,t^{2},t^{3})
we might wish to test if the cubic term is necessary by testing if the last row of
\alpha
is
0

Consider a test that the last
k_{2}\leq k-u
rows of the
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}
in model (
3.1
) all equal
0
. Following
Su et al., 2016
and
Zhu and Su, 2019
, this hypothesis can be tested by conformably partitioning in (
3.1
)
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{1}^{T},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{2}^{T})^{T}
with
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{j}\times u},\;j=1,2
, and then testing if
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{2}=0
, so under the null hypothesis

\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}=((\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})^{T},0)^{T}
. The restriction
k_{2}\leq k-u
on the number of rows tested arises because the rank of
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{1}
must equal
u
under both the null and alternative hypothesis. If
k_{2}=k-u
then without loss of generality we can take
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{1}=\mathbf{I}_{u}
.

When
k_{2}<k-u
, the maximum likelihood estimator of
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{1}
can be found by following the steps leading to (
3.2
) and then introducing the restriction that
{\mathbf{G}}=({\mathbf{G}}_{1}^{T},0)^{T}
. Partition
{\mathbf{Y}}_{D}=({\mathbf{Y}}_{D_{1}}^{T},{\mathbf{Y}}_{D_{2}}^{T})^{T}
to conform to the partitioning of
\phi
. Then

\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}}_{1}=\arg\min_{{\mathbf{G}}_{1}}\log|{\mathbf{G}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{S}}_{D_{1}|({\mathbf{X}},S)}{\mathbf{G}}_{1}|+\log|{\mathbf{G}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{S}}_{D_{1}|(D_{2},S)}^{-1}{\mathbf{G}}_{1}|,

(3.7)

where the minimum is computed over all semi-orthogonal matrices
{\mathbf{G}}_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{1}\times u}
,
{\mathbf{S}}_{D_{1}|(D_{2},S)}
is the sample residual covariance matrix of the regression of

{\mathbf{Y}}_{D_{1}}
on
({\mathbf{Y}}_{D_{2}},{\mathbf{Y}}_{S})
with an intercept, and
{\mathbf{S}}_{D_{1}|({\mathbf{X}},S)}
is the sample residual covariance matrix of the regression of

{\mathbf{Y}}_{D_{1}}
on
({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}_{S})
with an intercept. When

, we must have
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{1}=\mathbf{I}_{u}
and no estimate of
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{1}

The likelihood ratio test statistic is computed as twice the difference between the log likelihood under the null hypothesis,

\hat{L}_{u,k_{2}}=c-\frac{n}{2}\left\{\log|\mathbf{T}_{S}|+\log|{\mathbf{S}}_{D_{2}|S}|+\log|{\mathbf{S}}_{D_{1}|(D_{2},S})|+\log|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{S}}_{D_{1}|({\mathbf{X}},S)}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}}_{1}|+\log|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}}_{1}^{T}{\mathbf{S}}_{D_{1}|(D_{2},S)}^{-1}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}}_{1}|\right\},

and the fully maximized log likelihood (
3.3
). Here

6
Discussion

The primary computational step for all of the envelope methods described herein involves finding
\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}=\arg\min_{{\mathbf{G}}\in{\mathcal{G}}}\log|{\mathbf{G}}^{T}{\mathbf{M}}_{1}{\mathbf{G}}|+\log|{\mathbf{G}}^{T}{\mathbf{M}}_{2}{\mathbf{G}}|
over a class
{\mathcal{G}}
of semi-orthogonal matrices, where the inner product matrices
{\mathbf{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{M}}_{2}
depend on the application. The R package Renvlp by M. Lee and Z. Su contains a routine for minimizing objective functions of this form. Computations are straightforward once
\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}
has been found. Renvlp also implements specialized methodology for data analysis under envelope model (
1.4
) and partial envelope model. The associated routines can be modified for the models described herein. Description of and links to packages for envelope methods are available at z.umn.edu/envelopes.

We relegated discussion of certain well-established aspects of envelope methodology to the Supplement. Non-normality and the bootstrap are discussed in Section
and methods for selecting the envelope dimension are reviewed in Section
. Enveloping for
(\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$})
jointly is discuss in Section
and finally a brief discussions of envelopes and Rao’s simple structure is in Section
