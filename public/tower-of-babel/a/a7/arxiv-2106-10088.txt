Conservative iterative methods for implicit discretizations of conservation laws
Philipp Birken, Viktor Linders

Abstract
Conservation properties of iterative methods applied to implicit finite volume discretizations of nonlinear conservation laws are analyzed. It is shown that any consistent multistep or Runge-Kutta method is globally conservative. Further, it is shown that Newton’s method, Krylov subspace methods and pseudo-time iterations are globally conservative while the Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel methods are not in general. If pseudo-time iterations using an explicit Runge-Kutta method are applied to a locally conservative discretization, then the resulting scheme is also locally conservative. However, the corresponding numerical flux can be inconsistent with the conservation law. We prove an extension of the Lax-Wendroff theorem, which reveals that numerical solutions based on these methods converge to weak solutions of a modified conservation law where the flux function is multiplied by a particular constant. This constant depends on the choice of Runge-Kutta method but is independent of both the conservation law and the discretization. Consistency is maintained by ensuring that this constant equals unity and a strategy for achieving this is presented. Experiments show that this strategy improves the convergence rate of the pseudo-time iterations.

Introduction

Conservation laws arise ubiquitously in the modelling of physical phenomena and their discretizations have been the subject of intense study; see e.g.
[
17
, Chapter 1]
and
[
18
, Chapter 1]
. They derive their name from the fact that they describe the conservation of some quantities of interest over time. In computational fluid dynamics (CFD), these quantities are usually mass, momentum and energy. For convenience we always refer to the conserved quantity as the ”mass” in this paper.

Many numerical schemes have been designed to mimic mass conservation. Throughout, we refer to such schemes as
globally conservative
. As special cases,
locally conservative
schemes dictate that local variations in the mass propagate from one computational cell to neighboring ones without ”skipping” any cells (typically referred to as ”conservative schemes” in the literature). In particular, finite volume methods are designed in part upon this principle, although many other schemes possess the same property
[
13
]
. A major result on the topic is the Lax-Wendroff theorem, see e.g.
[
16
]
, which provides sufficient conditions for numerical solutions of convergent and locally conservative discretizations to converge to weak solutions of the corresponding conservation law.

For stiff problems, e.g. the simulation of wall bounded viscous flows,
[
23
]
, implicit time stepping methods are used. Such discretizations result in systems of linear or nonlinear equations. In the context of fluid flow simulations, these systems are sparse and very large. Their solutions are approximated using iterative methods, see e.g.
[
2
,
1
,
12
,
6
,
10
,
11
,
8
,
5
,
7
,
4
]
and the references therein.

It is natural to ask whether such approximate solutions satisfy the conservation properties upon which the discretizations are based, and in particular, whether a Lax-Wendroff type result is available. It is typically claimed that this is not the case, e.g. in
[
15
]
, where a version of a Newton-Jacobi iteration was noted to violate global conservation. In this paper we set out to answer this question in greater detail for some well-known (families of) iterative methods. We consider conservative implicit space-time discretizations whose solutions in each time step are approximated by a fixed number of iterations with different iterative methods.

After introducing relevant concepts and definitions in section
2
, we investigate the global conservation of iterative methods in section
3
. It is shown that Newton’s method, Krylov subspace methods and pseudo-time iterations using Runge-Kutta (RK) methods are globally conservative if the initial guess has correct mass. However, the Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel methods in general are not. In section
4
we focus on pseudo-time iterations using explicit RK methods. We show that such methods are locally conservative. By fixing the number of iterations and considering the limit of infinitesimal space-time increments, an extension of the Lax-Wendroff theorem is given. It turns out that the numerical solution in general converges to a weak solution of a modified conservation law for which the flux function is multiplied by a particular constant. An expression for this modification constant is given that only depends on the choice of Runge-Kutta method and the selected pseudo-time steps. A technique for ensuring that the constant equals one is presented, thereby ensuring consistency of the resulting scheme. Experiments indicate that the new technique improvess the convergence rate of the pseudo-time iterations. Numerical tests corroborate these findings and further suggest that the results hold for systems of conservation laws in multiple dimensions. Conclusions are drawn in section
5
.

Throughout the paper we separate quantities that belong to a spatial discretization from those that do not. Any vector living on a grid with
m
cells is denoted by a lower case bold letter, e.g.
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Similarly, any matrix operating on such a vector is represented by a bold upper case letter, e.g.
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. In Section
4
, we consider
s
-stage Runge-Kutta methods. Vector quantities spanning these stages are denoted by bold and underlined lower case letters, e.g.
\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Matrices operating on the stages are represented by bold and underlined upper case letters e.g.
\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
.

2
Conservation and conservative discretizations

In this paper we consider numerical methods for conservation laws, meaning partial differential equations of the form

\displaystyle u_{t}+\nabla\cdot f(u)

\displaystyle=0,\quad t\in(t_{0},t_{e}],\quad x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1)

\displaystyle u(x,t_{0})

\displaystyle=u_{0}(x),

possibly with additional boundary conditions. Here, the
k
th component
u_{k}
of the vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
represents the concentration of a quantity, i.e. the total amount of that quantity in a domain is given by
\int_{\Omega}u_{k}\text{d}x
. The conservation law states that this amount is only changed by flow across the boundary of the domain. Assuming that the net flow across the boundary is zero, we thus have for all
t\in(t_{0},t_{e}]
that

\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)\text{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}(x)\text{d}x.

(2)

In this paper we focus on scalar conservation laws in 1D, i.e. the case when
d=q=1
in (
1
). Numerical experiments in section
4
suggest that the results of the paper can be generalized to systems in multiple dimensions, however this task is deferred for future work. Further, we restrict our analysis to the Cauchy problem for (
1
), or the problem with periodic boundary conditions. Thus, the conservation law of interest becomes

\displaystyle u_{t}+f_{x}

\displaystyle=0,\quad t\in(t_{0},t_{e}],\quad x\in\Omega,

(3)

\displaystyle u(x,t_{0})

\displaystyle=u_{0}(x),

where either
\Omega=(-\infty,\infty)
or
\Omega=(a,b]
and
u(a)=u(b)
. Which case is considered will be clear from context.

A very successful line of research in computational fluid dynamics is to construct numerical methods that respect (
2
) on a discrete level. Herein, we predominantly consider finite volume methods that utilize the implicit Euler method as temporal discretization. Let the computational grid be given by
(x_{i},t_{n})=(i\Delta x,n\Delta t)
with
n\geq 0
. This grid may be either infinite or finite and periodic in space, i.e.
x_{i+m}=x_{i}
for some positive integer
m
. We consider discretizations on the form

\frac{u_{i}^{n+1}-u_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-\hat{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\right)=0.

(4)

Here,
u_{i}^{n}
approximates the solution
u(x,t_{n})
of (
3
) in the computational cell
\Omega_{i}
. The notation
\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})
is used as a placeholder for a generic numerical flux of the form
\hat{f}(u_{i-p}^{n+1},\dots,u_{i+q}^{n+1})
, where
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
determine the bandwidth of the stencil. Multiplying (
4
) by
\Delta x
and summing over all
i
reveals that

\Delta x\sum_{i}u_{i}^{n+1}=\Delta x\sum_{i}u_{i}^{n}.

(5)

Comparing this with (
2
) shows that the finite volume scheme discretely mimics conservation of mass.

2.1
Local conservation

It is the telescoping nature of the spatial terms in (
4
) that leads to the preceeding result. In the finite volume community, this property is typically referred to simply as conservation. However, to distinguish it from other mass conservative schemes, we will henceforth refer to this property as
local conservation
:

Definition 1
.

A discretization of (
1
) that can be written in the form (
4
) is said to be locally conservative.

Local conservation plays a central role in the Lax-Wendroff theorem, which considers the Cauchy problem for (
3
). First, the notion of
consistency
must be specified:

Definition 2
.

A numerical flux
\hat{f}
is consistent with
f
if it is Lipschitz continuous in each argument and if
\hat{f}(u,\dots,u)=f(u)
.

A numerical flux function is
inconsistent
if it fails to satisfy Definition
2
. We will see examples of this in Section
4
.

The Lax-Wendroff theorem applies to the Cauchy problem for (
3
) and considers locally conservative discretizations with consistent numerical flux. If the numerical solution of such a scheme converges to a function
u
in the limit of vanishing
\Delta x
and
\Delta t
, the theorem provides sufficient conditions for
u
to be a weak solution of the conservation law (
1
)
[
17
, Chapter 12]
. More precisely, consider a sequence of grids
(\Delta x_{\ell},\Delta t_{\ell})
such that
\Delta x_{\ell},\Delta t_{\ell}\rightarrow 0
as
\ell\rightarrow\infty
. Let
\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t)
denote the piecewise constant function that takes the solution value
u_{i}^{n}
in
(x_{i},x_{i+1}]\times(t_{n-1},t_{n}]
on the
\ell
th grid. We make the following assumptions:

Assumption 1
.

1.

There is a function
u(x,t)
such that over every bounded set
\Omega=[a,b]\times[0,T]
in
x
-
t
space,

\|\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t)-u(x,t)\|_{1,\Omega}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad\ell\rightarrow\infty.

2.

For each
T
there is a constant
R>0
such that

TV(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(\cdot,t))<R\quad\text{for all}\quad 0\leq t\leq T,\quad\ell=1,2,\dots

The first of these assumptions asserts that the numerical solution is convergent in the
L^{1}
-norm with limit
u
. The second one states that the total variation of the numerical solution remains bounded independently of the grid. The Lax-Wendroff theorem may now be stated as follows:

Theorem 1

(Lax-Wendroff)
.

Consider the locally conservative discretization (
4
), suppose that the numerical flux
\hat{f}
is consistent and that Assumption
1
is satisfied. Then,
u(x,t)
is a weak solution of (
1
).

The theorem and its assumptions require that the linear or nonlinear systems arising in (
4
) are solved exactly. However, in practice these systems will be solved approximately using iterative methods. The natural question that now arises is: Do iterative solvers maintain the convergence to weak solutions?

2.2
Global conservation

To answer this question we must establish if iterative methods preserve local conservation. However, local conservation is a special case of the more general notion of
global conservation
. Let us therefore consider a finite and periodic grid. Discretizing (
3
) in space on a mesh with cells
\Omega_{i}
of volume
|\Omega_{i}|
, we arrive at an initial value problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}),\quad t\in(t_{0},t_{e}],

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},

with
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}(t),\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Definition 3
.

A discretization
\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}=(\hat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}),\dots,\hat{f}_{m}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}))^{\top}
is globally conservative if

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|\hat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})=0.

Global conservation implies that (
2
) is fulfilled on a semidiscrete level by (
6
):

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}(t_{0}),\quad\forall t\in[t_{0},t_{e}].

Suppose next that a time integration method is used to solve (
6
).

Definition 4
.

A time integration method applied to the conservative semidiscretization (
6
) is globally conservative if

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{n+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{n}

holds for each time step
n=0,1,\dots

Note that the conservation property (
5
) derived for the locally conservative finite volume discretization (
4
) is a special case of Definition
4
, where
\Omega_{i}=\Delta x
for every
i
. Global conservation is thus necessary for local conservation, which in turn is necessary for the Lax-Wendroff Theorem.

In the CFD community, one hears from time to time the claim that implicit methods are not conservative. However, this is not true: All consistent linear multistep methods are globally conservative. To see this, consider a generic
s
-step linear multistep method

\sum_{j=0}^{s}a_{j}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n+j}=\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{s}b_{j}\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n+j}),

(7)

where
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
are given method dependent coefficients. Suppose that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{n+j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{0}
for
j=0,\dots,s-1
. Multiplying (
7
) by
|\Omega_{i}|
and summing over all cells gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|a_{s}u_{i}^{n+s}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{n+j}+\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{s}b_{j}\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|\hat{f}_{i}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n+j})}_{=0}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{0}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{j}.

Consistent multistep methods satisfy
a_{s}=1
and
a_{0}+\dots+a_{s-1}=-1
. Hence, global conservation is ensured.

Similarly, all Runge-Kutta (RK) methods are globally conservative. Consider an
s
-stage RK method

\displaystyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{j},

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{l=1}^{s}a_{j,l}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{l}\right),\quad j=1,\dots,s,

where
a_{j,l}
and
b_{j}
are given by the method. Since
\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}
is globally conservative it follows from Definition
3
that the mass of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{j}
is zero and therefore that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{n+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|k_{j_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{n}.

Hence, RK methods are globally conservative.

Exponential integrators have been proven to be conservative as well
[
9
]
, essentially due to the following useful lemma:

Lemma 2
.

For any
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the Jacobian
\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{\prime}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})=\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}{\partial\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
of the globally conservative discretization
\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})
satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|\left(\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{\prime}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}\right)_{i}=0.

(9)

Proof.

See
[
27
]
.
∎

In the remainder we also need the following result:

Lemma 3
.

Let
\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{\prime}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be the Jacobian of a conservative discretization
\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})
and let
\alpha
be any scalar. The solution
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}
of a linear system of the form

\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}+\alpha\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{\prime}\right)\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}},

(10)

satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|x_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|b_{i}.

Proof.

Multiplying the
i
th element of (
10
) by
|\Omega_{i}|
and summing over all
i
gives

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|b_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|\left(\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}+\alpha\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{\prime}\right)\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|x_{i}+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|\left(\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{\prime}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)_{i}.

By Lemma
2
the final term on the right-hand side is zero.
∎

3
Globally conservative iterative solvers

We begin by asking: Do iterative solvers respect global conservation? Since global conservation is necessary for local conservation, which in turn is used in Theorem
1
, a negative answer will immediately exclude the possibility to extend the Lax-Wendroff theorem to incorporate a particular iterative method.

Discretizing (
6
) in time using the implicit Euler method results in a nonlinear equation system of the form

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}-\alpha\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}.

(11)

We concern ourselves with a set of iterates
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
with limit
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n+1}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

Definition 5
.

An iterative method with iterates
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
is said to be globally conservative if

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{(k+1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{n},\qquad k=0,1,\dots

In the following subsections we investigate the conservation properties of some of the most common iterative methods.

3.1
Newton’s Method

Newton’s method finds an approximate solution to (
11
) by solving the sequence of linear systems

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}\right)\right)\Delta\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}+\alpha\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}\right),

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}+\Delta\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},

for
k=0,1,\dots
Since
\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}
is conservative it has zero mass by Definition
3
. If
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}
have the same mass, the entire right-hand side of the first equation in (
12
) has zero mass. Hence, by Lemma
3
, the increment
\Delta\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
also has zero mass. Thus, the mass of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}
is the same as that of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
, which by assumption is the same as that of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}
. Hence, Newton’s method is globally conservative.

In large scale practical applications, each linear system in (
12
) is solved approximately using other iterative methods. In the following, we consider a generic linear system

(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}},

(13)

that may either represent a Newton iteration or a linear discretization of a conservation law. In line with Lemma
2
, it is assumed that the mass of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}
is zero for any vector
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

3.2
Stationary linear methods

Any iterative method for solving (
13
) that can be written in the form

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{M}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{N}}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}

(14)

for some matrices
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{M}}
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{N}}
, is termed a stationary linear method. Well known examples include the Richardson, Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel iterations.

3.2.1
Richardson iteration

For the Richardson iteration,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{M}}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\theta(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{N}}^{-1}=\theta\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}
, where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
is a fixed parameter. With some minor rearrangements, the iteration can thus be expressed as

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}+\theta(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}-(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}).

Multiplying the
i
th element by
|\Omega_{i}|
and summing over
i
results in

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{(k+1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{(k)}+\theta\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|(b_{i}-u_{i}^{(k)})+\alpha\theta\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)})_{i}.

By assumption, the matrix-vector product
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
has zero mass, hence the final term vanishes. Further, if
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
has the same mass as
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}
, then the second term on the right-hand side vanishes. By induction it follows that the Richardson iteration is globally conservative.

3.2.2
Jacobi iteration

Consider again the linear system (
13
). With the matrix decomposition
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{L}}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}})
, where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}
,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{L}}
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}}
are the diagonal, lower and upper triangular parts of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
respectively, the Jacobi iteration is obtained by setting
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{M}}=\alpha(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}})^{-1}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{L}}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}})
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{N}}^{-1}=(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}})^{-1}
. Inserting this into (
14
), multiplying by
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}
, then adding and subtracting
\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
, gives after some rearrangement

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}+\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)})+\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}.

Multiplying the
i
th element by
|\Omega_{i}|
and summing over
i
results in

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{(k+1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|b_{i}+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|a_{i,i}(u_{i}^{(k+1)}-u_{i}^{(k)})+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)})_{i}.

(15)

The final term vanishes by the assumption. However, the second term on the right-hand side is in general non-zero and thus introduces a conservation error

\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|a_{i,i}(u_{i}^{(k+1)}-u_{i}^{(k)}).

The Jacobi method is therefore not globally conservative in general.

However, consider the special case when the diagonal
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}
of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a scalar multiple of the identity matrix, say
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}=a\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}
. Then
a_{i,i}=a
for
i=1,\dots,m
. If
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
has the same mass as
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}
we find, after rearrangement of (
15
), that

(1-\alpha a)\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{(k+1)}=(1-\alpha a)\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|b_{i}.

Thus,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}
has correct mass, at least as long as
\alpha a\neq 1
. Hence, the Jacobi iteration is conservative in this special case.

3.2.3
Gauss-Seidel iteration

The Gauss-Seidel iteration is defined by
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{M}}=\alpha(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{L}}))^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{N}}^{-1}=(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{L}}))^{-1}
. Inserting this into (
14
), multiplying by
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{D}}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{L}})
, then adding and subtracting
\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}
gives

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)})+\alpha A\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}.

Following the same procedure as previously, we multiply the
i
th element by
|\Omega_{i}|
and sum over
i
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|b_{i}+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|\sum_{j=i+1}^{m}a_{i,j}(u_{j}^{(k+1)}-u_{j}^{(k)})

\displaystyle+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)})_{i}.

By assumption, the final term vanishes. However, the second term on the right-hand side is in general non-zero. Thus, the Gauss-Seidel method is not globally conservative and the conservation error is given by

\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|\sum_{j=i+1}^{m}a_{i,j}(u_{j}^{(k+1)}-u_{j}^{(k)}).

However, note that for problems where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
is lower triangular the Gauss-Seidel iteration is indeed conservative. In fact, for such problems the method is by definition exact.

3.3
Krylov subspace methods

Given an initial guess
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(0)}
for the solution of the linear system (
13
), the
k
th iteration of a Krylov subspace method belongs to the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(0)}+\text{span}\left\{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)},(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)},\dots,(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})^{k-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(0)}+\text{span}\left\{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)},\dots,\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{k-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)}\right\},

where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}-(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(0)}
is the initial residual. Examples of Krylov subspace methods are GMRES, CG and BICGSTAB
[
24
, Chapters 6–7]
.

We may express the
k
th iteration
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}
as

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(0)}+\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}c_{l}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{l}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)},

for some coefficients
c_{l},\,l=0,\dots,k-1
that depend on, and are chosen by the method. It follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|u_{i}^{(0)}+c_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|r_{i}^{(0)}+\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}c_{l}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Omega_{i}|(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{l}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)})_{i}.

The final term vanishes by assumption. Note that the mass of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(0)}
is zero if the mass of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(0)}
equals that of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}
. Thus, the second term on the right-hand side also vanishes. All Krylov subspace methods are therefore conservative if the initial guess is chosen to have the same mass as the right-hand side.

Typically a preconditioner
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{P}}^{-1}
will pre- or post-multiply
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
in Krylov subspace methods. We will not delve into this subject here but merely observe that if
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{P}}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}
have the same mass for any choice of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}
, then the above analysis applies without modification.

3.4
Multigrid methods

Multigrid methods combine two methods, namely a smoother and a coarse grid correction (CGC). The idea is to separate the residual into high and low frequency components. As a smoother, an iterative method is applied, designed to effectively damp the high frequency components.

The role of the CGC is to remove the low frequency components of the residual. The residual is mapped to a coarser grid using a restriction operator,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{R}}
. Smoothing is then applied and a prolongation operator
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{P}}
is used to reconstruct the residual on the fine grid. Finally, the fine grid solution is updated by adding the correction to the previous iterate. The procedure can be applied to a hirearchy of grids, thereby resulting in a multigrid method. If we consider only two grid levels and assume that the system is solved exactly on the coarser one, then the coarse grid correction for the linear problem (
13
) has the form

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k+1)}=(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{P}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})_{\ell-1}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{R}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})_{\ell})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(k)}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{P}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}-\alpha\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})_{\ell-1}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{R}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}.

(16)

Here, the notation
(\cdot)_{\ell}
and
(\cdot)_{\ell-1}
denote matrices operating on the fine and the coarse grid respectively.

The easiest way to make a multigrid method globally conservative is to choose its components to be globally conservative. The smoother can be any of the globally conservative iterations discussed so far. By Lemma
3
, the inverted matrices in (
16
) are mass conserving. Thus, it remains to choose globally conservative restriction and prolongation operators
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{R}}
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{P}}
. This is achieved using agglomeration. The restriction is performed by agglomerating a number of neighboring cells by constructing a volume weighted average of fine grid values. Conversely, the prolongation is done by injecting coarse grid values at multiple fine grid points. In fact, this is the standard choice of multigrid method in CFD since it results in faster convergence than other alternatives. For details, see
[
3
]
.

3.5
Pseudo-time iterations

Pseudo-time iterations are obtained by adding a pseudo-time derivative term to the algebraic equations (
11
) (or (
13
)),

\frac{\partial\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial\tau}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}-\alpha\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n},\qquad\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}(0)=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}.

(17)

Here,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}
is initial data that must be provided, e.g.
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}
. The idea is that the system eventually should reach a steady state as
\tau\rightarrow\infty
where the pseudo-time derivative vanishes
[
14
]
, resuting in a solution to the original nonlinear system (
11
). Any time integration method can in principle be applied to the initial value problem (
17
). Explicit RK methods are globally conservative by construction. Implicit methods result in new systems of equations whose solutions are once again approximated using iterative methods
[
5
]
. Whether the resulting approximation is conservative depends on the choice of method. Any one of the conservative methods discussed so far can be applied in principle. We will return to pseudo-time iterations in Section
4
, where more details are provided.

4
Local conservation of pseudo-time iterations

Having covered global conservation in the previous section, we now switch focus to local conservation. The Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel methods cannot be locally conservative since they are not globally conservative in general. For the other methods considered so far, the question remains open.

In what follows, we restrict our attention to the Cauchy problem for (
3
). We focus on pseudo-time iterations, show that these methods are locally conservative and prove an extension of the Lax-Wendroff theorem. Noting that the Richardson iteration is equivalent to pseudo-time iterations with explicit Euler, this method is also covered.

4.1
Pseudo-time iterations in locally conservative form

We once again consider the finite volume method (
4
) where the implicit Euler method is used as time discretization. In order to apply pseudo-time iterations to this scheme, we introduce a pseudo-time derivative,

\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\tau}+g_{i}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})=0,\qquad u_{i}(0)=u_{0_{i}},\qquad i=\dots,-1,0,1,\dots

where the nonlinear function
g_{i}
is given by

g_{i}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})=\frac{u_{i}-u_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})-\hat{f}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})\right).

(20)

Several different methods are available for iterating in pseudo-time
[
28
,
5
]
. Herein, we use an explicit
s
-stage Runge-Kutta method (ERK). Let
(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}},\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}},\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{c}}})
denote the coefficient matrix and vectors of the ERK method. We denote the
k
th pseudo-time iterate by
u_{i}^{(k)}
. The subsequent iterate
u_{i}^{(k+1)}
is computed from
u_{i}^{(k)}
as

u_{i}^{(k+1)}=u_{i}^{(k)}-\Delta\tau_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}g_{i}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{(k)}\right),

(21)

where the stage vectors
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{(k)}
,
j=1,\dots,s
have elements

U_{j_{\iota}}^{(k)}=u_{\iota}^{(k)}-\Delta\tau_{k}\sum_{l=1}^{j-1}a_{j,l}g_{\iota}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}}_{l}^{(k)}\right),\quad\iota=i-p,\dots,i+q.

(22)

As previously,
p
and
q
determine the bandwidth of the finite volume stencil.

In the remainder we always use a fixed number
N
of iterations. A step in physical time is taken by setting
u_{i}^{n+1}=u_{i}^{(N)}
. Throughout,
u_{i}^{(0)}=u_{i}^{n}
is chosen as initial iterate.

Recall that the
stability function

\phi(z)
of an RK method
(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}},\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}},\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{c}}})
is given by (see e.g.
[
31
, Chapter IV.3]
)

\phi(z)=1+z\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}-z\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}},

(23)

where
\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}
is the
s\times s
identity matrix. The
stability region
of the RK method is defined as the subset of the complex plane for which
|\phi(z)|<1
. The proof of the following lemma is rather lengthy and is therefore deferred to Appendix
A
.

Lemma 4
.

For each pseudo-time iteration
k=0,\dots,N-1
, let
\mu_{k}=\Delta\tau_{k}/\Delta t
and set
u_{i}^{(0)}=u_{i}^{n}
. Then, for any
N\geq 1
, the elements of the pseudo-time iterate
u_{i}^{(N)}
satisfy the relation

\frac{u_{i}^{(N)}-u_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}-\hat{h}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}\right)=0,\qquad i=\dots,-1,0,1,\dots

(24)

where the flux
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}
is given by

\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right)\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}

(25)

and
\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}=\left(\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}^{(k)}\right),\dots,\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{U}}_{s}^{(k)}\right)\right)^{\top}
.

Remark 1
.

The product in (
25
) is empty when
k=N-1
. To handle this case we use the convention

\prod_{l=N}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})=1.

It follows immediately from Lemma
4
that the locally conservative nature of the discretization (
4
) is preserved by the pseudo-time iterations, albeit with the new numerical flux
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}
in place of
\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
. The question with which flux
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}
is consistent, is answered in the following Theorem:

Theorem 5
.

Let
N
be given and let
\mu_{k}=\Delta\tau_{k}/\Delta t
for
k=0,\dots,N-1
. Set
u_{i}^{(0)}=u_{i}^{n}
and terminate the pseudo-time iteration after
N
steps. Then the resulting scheme can be written in the locally conservative form

\frac{u_{i}^{n+1}-u_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}-\hat{h}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}\right)=0,\qquad i=\dots,-1,0,1,\dots

(26)

where
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}
, given by (
25
), is a numerical flux consistent with the flux
c(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N-1})f
, with

c(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N-1})=1-\prod_{l=0}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l}).

(27)

Proof.

Firstly, the locally conservative form (
26
) follows from setting
u_{i}^{n+1}=u_{i}^{(N)}
in (
24
). Secondly we recall that the numerical flux
\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
depends on
p+q+1
parameters, e.g.
\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{w}})=\hat{f}(w_{i-p},\dots,w_{i+q})
. Inspecting the flux
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}
we see that it is additionally dependent on
u_{i}^{n}
so that we may write
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{w}})=\hat{h}^{N}(w_{i-p},\dots,w_{i+q};u_{i}^{n})
. To establish the consistency we must therefore show that
\hat{h}^{N}(u,\dots,u;u)=cf(u)
. To do this, we first note from (
20
) that
g_{i}(u,\dots,u;u)=0
due to the consistency of
\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
. Using the fact that
\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is lower triangular for any ERK method, it follows from (
21
) and (
22
) that
U_{j_{\iota}}^{(k)}=u_{i}^{(k)}=u
for every
j
,
k
and
\iota=i-p,\dots,i+q
. Consequently, the vector
\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}
becomes

\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}^{k}(u,\dots,u;u)=\left(\hat{f}(u,\dots,u),\dots,\hat{f}(u,\dots,u)\right)=\hat{f}(u,\dots,u)\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}=f(u)\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}},

where the consistency of
\hat{f}
has been used in the final equality. Inserting this into the flux function
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}
in (
25
) and using (
23
) gives

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right]f(u)

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}[1-\phi(-\mu_{k})]\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right]f(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left[\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})-\prod_{l=k}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right]f(u)

\displaystyle=\left[1-\prod_{l=0}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right]f(u)

\displaystyle=c(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N-1})f(u),

where we have utilized the fact that the sum in the third equality is telescoping. Finally, note that
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}
is formed by a linear combination of evaluations of
\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
and is therefore Lipschitz continuous.
∎

The fact that
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}
is inconsistent with
f
when
c\neq 1
is a manifestation of the error associated with the pseudo-time iterations. This has important implications on the convergence of the resulting scheme. What follows is an extension of the Lax-Wendroff theorem (see
[
17
, Chapter 12]
) pertaining to ERK pseudo-time iterations:

Theorem 6
.

Consider a sequence of grids
(\Delta x_{\ell},\Delta t_{\ell})
such that
\Delta x_{\ell},\Delta t_{\ell}\rightarrow 0
as
\ell\rightarrow\infty
. Fix
N
independently of
\ell
, set
u_{i}^{(0)}=u_{i}^{n}
and terminate the pseudo-time iterations after
N
steps. Let
\Delta\tau_{k,\ell}/\Delta t_{\ell}=\mu_{k,\ell}=\mu_{k}
be constants independent of
\ell
for each
k=0,\dots,N-1
. Suppose that the numerical flux
\hat{f}
in (
4
) is consistent with
f
and that Assumption
1
is satisfied. Then,
u(x,t)
is a weak solution of the conservation law

u_{t}+c(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N-1})f_{x}=0,\qquad c(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N-1})=1-\prod_{l=0}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l}).

(28)

Proof.

We follow the proof of the Lax-Wendroff theorem given in
[
17
, Chapter 12]
with changes and details added where necessary. Throughout the proof we let
\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t)
denote the piecewise constant function that takes the solution value
u_{i}^{n}
in
(x_{i},x_{i+1}]\times(t_{n-1},t_{n}]
on the
\ell
th grid. Similarly, for
j=1,\dots,s
we let
\mathcal{U}_{j_{\ell}}^{(k)}(x,t)
be the piecewise constant function that takes the value
U_{j_{i}}^{(k)}
in
(x_{i},x_{i+1}]\times(t_{n-1},t_{n}]
.

The discretization (
26
) can equivalently be expressed as

\Delta x_{\ell}\left[\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n+1})-\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n})\right]+\Delta t_{\ell}\left[\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}-\hat{h}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}\right]=0.

(29)

Let
\varphi\in C_{0}^{1}
be a compactly supported test function. Multiply (
29
) by
\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})
and sum over all
n
and
i
. Due to the compact support of
\varphi
, these sums can be extended arbitrarily beyond the bounds of
x
and
t
, hence we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta x_{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})\left(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n+1})-\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n})\right)

(30)

\displaystyle+\Delta t_{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})\left[\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}-\hat{h}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}\right]=0.

At this point we will use summation by parts on the sums in (
30
). Recall that for two sequences
(a_{i})
and
(b_{i})
, the summation by parts formula can be expressed as

\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{j}(b_{j}-b_{j-1})=a_{M}b_{M}-a_{1}b_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{M}(a_{j+1}-a_{j})b_{j}.

Applied to the
n
-sum in the first term and to the
i
-sum in the second term in (
30
), this results in

\displaystyle\Delta x_{\ell}\Delta t_{\ell}

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\right.\left(\frac{\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})-\varphi(x_{i},t_{n-1})}{\Delta t_{\ell}}\right)\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n})

(31)

\displaystyle+\left.\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\varphi(x_{i+1},t_{n})-\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})}{\Delta x_{\ell}}\right)\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}\right]

\displaystyle=-\Delta x_{\ell}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x_{i},0)\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},0).

Here we have used the fact that
\varphi
has compact support in order to eliminate all boundary terms except the one at
n=0
.

We now let
\ell\rightarrow\infty
and investigate the convergence of the terms arising in (
31
). The first and third terms are identical to those in the proof of the Lax-Wendroff theorem
[
17
, Chapter 12]
. Thus, it can immediately be concluded that the first term converges to

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{t}u(x,t)\text{d}x\text{d}t

and the third one to

-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x,0)u(x,0)\text{d}x.

It remains to investigate the second term in (
31
).

Expanding
\hat{h}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(N)}
using (
25
) yields for the second term in (
31
)

\displaystyle\Delta x_{\ell}

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\varphi(x_{i+1},t_{n})-\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})}{\Delta x_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right)\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}.

To this expression, add and subtract

\displaystyle\Delta x_{\ell}

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\varphi(x_{i+1},t_{n})-\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})}{\Delta x_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right)f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n}))\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}

in order to obtain

\displaystyle\Delta x_{\ell}

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\varphi(x_{i+1},t_{n})-\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})}{\Delta x_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right)\right]f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n}))

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Delta x_{\ell}

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\varphi(x_{i+1},t_{n})-\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})}{\Delta x_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right)\left(\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}-f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n}))\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\right).

The bracketed part of this expression evaluates to
c(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N-1})
, as in the proof of Theorem

c(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N-1})\Delta x_{\ell}\Delta t_{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\varphi(x_{i+1},t_{n})-\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})}{\Delta x_{\ell}}\right)f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n})).

Apart from the factor

, which is independent of
\ell

[

, Chapter 12]
and converges to

c(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N-1})\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{x}f\text{d}x\text{d}t.

It remains to show that

\displaystyle\Delta x_{\ell}

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\varphi(x_{i+1},t_{n})-\varphi(x_{i},t_{n})}{\Delta x_{\ell}}\right)

(32)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right)\left(\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}-f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x_{i},t_{n}))\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\right)

vanishes in the limit
\ell\rightarrow\infty
. Since
\mathcal{U}_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{U}_{j_{\ell}}^{(k)}
are both constant in

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\varphi_{x}(x,t_{n})

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right)\left(\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}(x,t)-f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t))\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\right).

Here,

is placeholder notation for the vector whose

To establish that this term indeed vanishes, it suffices to show that

\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\right)\left(\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}(x,t)-f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t))\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\right)\right|

(33)

tends to zero for almost every

. By the Cauchy-Schwarz and triangle inequalities, (

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\top}(\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{I}}}+\mu_{k}\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}})^{-1}\|\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{N-1}|\phi(-\mu_{l})|\right)\left\|\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}(x,t)-f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t))\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\right\|,

(34)

where the Euclidean norm in

is used. The only term in (

and it therefore suffices to show that this term vanishes for almost every

Recall that
f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell})=\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{\ell})
by consistency. A standard norm inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{f}}}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{(k)}(x,t)-f(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t))\underline{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{s}\max_{1\leq j\leq s}

\displaystyle\left|\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathcal{U}_{j_{\ell}}^{(k)}(x-p\Delta x,t),\dots,\mathcal{U}_{j_{\ell}}^{(k)}(x+q\Delta x,t)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t),\dots,\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{s}\max_{1\leq j\leq s}

\displaystyle\left\{\left|\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathcal{U}_{j_{\ell}}^{(k)}(x-p\Delta x,t),\dots,\mathcal{U}_{j_{\ell}}^{(k)}(x+q\Delta x,t)\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x-p\Delta x,t),\dots,\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x+q\Delta x,t))\right|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left|\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x-p\Delta x,t),\dots,\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x+q\Delta x,t))\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\left.\hat{f}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t),\dots,\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t))\right|\right\}.

Since

is Lipschitz continuous in each argument there is a constant

such that the final expression is bounded by

\displaystyle L\sqrt{s}\max_{1\leq j\leq s}\max_{p\leq r\leq q}\left|\mathcal{U}_{j_{\ell}}^{(k)}(x+r\Delta x,t)-\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x+r\Delta x,t)\right|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle L\sqrt{s}\max_{p\leq r\leq q}\left|\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x+r\Delta x,t)-\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(x,t)\right|.

The second of these terms appear in the proof of the Lax-Wendroff theorem
[

, Chapter 12]
and vanishes in the limit for almost every

due to the bounded total variation of
\mathcal{U}_{\ell}
. Further, from (

) and (

) and the fact that
u_{i}^{(0)}=u_{i}^{n}
it follows that for every
j=1,\dots,s
and
k\geq 0
,
U_{j_{\iota}}^{(k)}=u_{\iota}^{n}
for each
\iota=i-p,\dots,i+q
in the limit of vanishing

. Consequently,
\left|\mathcal{U}_{j_{\ell}}^{(k)}(\cdot,t)-\mathcal{U}_{\ell}(\cdot,t)\right|
vanishes identically as
\ell\rightarrow\infty
.

A few remarks about Theorem
6
are in place: First, we demand from a useful iterative method that it converges to the correct solution as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, the pseudo-time steps should be chosen in a way that ensures that
c\rightarrow 1
, or equivalently,

\prod_{l=0}^{N-1}\phi(-\mu_{l})\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad N\rightarrow\infty.

(35)

The simplest way to do this is to choose each pseudo-time step so that
|\phi(-\mu_{l})|<1
, i.e. to stay within the stability region of the RK method.

Secondly, observe that if
\phi(-\mu_{l})=0
for any
l
, then
c=1
irrespective of how many further iterations that are carried out. For some RK methods such a root exists while for others it does not. For instance, the explicit Euler method has stability function
\phi(-\mu)=1-\mu
, hence
\mu=1
is a root of
\phi
. On the other hand, Heun’s method has stability function
\phi(-\mu)=1-\mu+\mu^{2}/2>0
for all
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and therefore does not have any real roots. A strategy is thus to choose a RK method with a root, begin the pseudo-time iterations with a step that corresponds to this root, then resort to a conventional method for choosing the remaining pseudo-time steps. The initial iteration will not change the limit as
N\rightarrow\infty
if the remaining pseudo-time steps are selected within the stability region.

5
Summary and Conclusions

In this paper we have studied conservation properties of a selection of iterative methods applied to 1D scalar conservation laws. The fact that conservation is a design principle behind many numerical schemes motivates such a study. We have established that Newton’s method, the Richardson iteration, Krylov subspace methods, coarse grid corrections using agglomeration, as well as ERK pseudo-time iterations preserve the global conservation of a given scheme if the initial guess has correct mass. However, the Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel methods do not, unless the linear system being solved possesses particular properties.

The stronger requirement of local conservation has been investigated for ERK pseudo-time iterations. We have shown that local conservation is preserved for finite volume schemes that employ the implicit Euler method in time. However, the resulting modified numerical flux may be inconsistent with the governing conservation law. An extension of the Lax-Wendroff theorem shows that this inconsistency leads to convergence to a weak solution of a conservation law modified by a particular constant. We give an exact expression for the modification constant, which depends only on the stability function of the ERK method and the pseudo-time steps. Depending on the problem solved, this modification can alter both the propagation speed and amplitude of the numerical solution if the constant differs from unity. We present a strategy for ensuring that the constant equals one and show numerically that the strategy results in faster convergence. Experiments suggest that the results hold even for systems of conservation laws in multiple dimensions.
