Smooth Non-Stationary Bandits
Su Jia, Qian Xie, Nathan Kallus, Peter I. Frazier

Abstract
In many applications of online decision making, the environment is non-stationary and it is therefore crucial to use bandit algorithms that handle changes. Most existing approaches are designed to protect against non-smooth changes, constrained only by total variation or Lipschitzness over time. However, in practice, environments often change smoothly , so such algorithms may incur higher-than-necessary regret. We study a non-stationary bandits problem where each arm’s mean reward sequence can be embedded into a β \beta -Hölder function, i.e., a function that is ( β − 1 ) (\beta-1) -times Lipschitz-continuously differentiable. The non-stationarity becomes more smooth as β \beta increases. When β = 1 \beta=1 , this corresponds to the non-smooth regime, where Besbes et al. 2014 established a minimax regret of Θ ~ ​ ( T 2 / 3 ) \tilde{\Theta}(T^{2/3}) . We show the first separation between the smooth (i.e., β ≥ 2 \beta\geq 2 ) and non-smooth (i.e., β = 1 \beta=1 ) regimes by presenting a policy with O ~ ​ ( k 4 / 5 ​ T 3 / 5 ) \tilde{O}(k^{4/5}T^{3/5}) regret on any k k -armed, 2 2 -Hölder instance. We complement this result by showing that the minimax regret on the β \beta -Hölder family of instances is Ω ⁡ ( T ( β + 1 ) / ( 2 ​ β + 1 ) ) \Omega(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta+1)}) for any integer β ≥ 1 \beta\geq 1 . This matches our upper bound for β = 2 \beta=2 up to logarithmic factors. Furthermore, we validated the effectiveness of our policy through a comprehensive numerical study using real-world click-through rate data.



Keywords:

multi-armed bandits; Hölder Class; non-stationarity; online learning; smoothness

1
Introduction

In numerous decision-making problems characterized by uncertainty and limited feedback on the outcomes, an agent must judiciously leverage past observations to make informed future decisions.

The
Multi-armed Bandits
(MAB) framework provides a paradigm for addressing this issue, as it encapsulates the fundamental trade-off between (i) exploration, i.e., acquiring new information, and (ii) exploitation, i.e., generating rewards based on available information.

Further complicating this challenge is the temporal evolution of the underlying environment, a characteristic prevalent in many real-world problems in operations and marketing.
This motivates the study of the non-stationary bandits problem.
In the standard model, the adversary is limited by how much the mean reward is allowed to change
in total
over time
(
Besbes et al. 2014
)
.
Formally, the
total variation
is defined as
\sum_{t=1}^{T}|r_{a}(t)-r_{a}(t+1)|
, where
r_{a}(t)
is the mean reward of arm
a
in round
t
, and
T
is the total number of rounds.

The problem within this framework has been extensively studied.
Besbes et al. 2014
introduced a policy that periodically “restarts” the learning process by discarding all prior observations.
With total variation
V
, this policy achieves
\tilde{O}\left(V^{1/3}T^{2/3}\right)
regret (i.e., the loss relative to the optimal policy if the mean rewards were known, as formally defined in Section
2
); see their Theorem 2.
Moreover, this result is best possible.
For any
V
, any policy suffers
\Omega\left(V^{1/3}T^{2/3}\right)
regret on some instance with total variation
V
; see their Theorem 1.

However, the total variation model may be too pessimistic, as it allows the adversary to shock the mean reward
instantaneously
.
Can we achieve lower regret by requiring the mean rewards to change
continuously
?
More precisely, suppose that the sequence
\{r_{a}(t)\}_{t\in[T]}
of mean rewards can be
embedded
into an
L
-Lipschitz function, in the sense that there exists an
L
-Lipschitz function
f_{a}:[0,1]\rightarrow\real
such that for all
t\in[T]
we have

\displaystyle r_{a}(t)=f_{a}\left(\frac{t}{T}\right).

(1)

Note that by Lipschitzness of
f_{a}
, we have

V\leq L
, so we immediately obtain an
\tilde{O}(L^{1/3}T^{2/3})
regret bound.
The above question can then be formalized as:

Q1:
Can we improve this
\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})
bound by capitalizing on Lipschitz continuity?

The answer is negative. In fact, although
Besbes et al. 2014
proved the
\Omega\left(V^{1/3}T^{2/3}\right)
lower bound using
discontinuous
reward functions, an alternate proof can be given using Lipschitz continuous reward functions; see our Theorem
4.4
.
In other words, the impact of continuity is negligible compared to the constraints imposed by the total variation budget.

But there is more to the story. The Lipschitz non-stationarity model may still be overly pessimistic, since it allows the adversary to instantly shock the
rate of change
in the mean reward (formally, the derivative of
f_{a}
in eqn. (
1
)).
However, in many applications, the underlying environment changes in a
smooth
manner, such as gradual changes in temperature, economic factors, and demand for seasonal products, just to name a few.
Furthermore, the capacity to abruptly change slope plays a pivotal role in the lower-bound proof under the Lipschitz model.
This suggests that the optimal regret bound may be improved with a suitable smoothness assumption.

To formalize the notion of smooth non-stationarity, we borrow a standard concept, the
Hölder class
, from nonparametric statistics.
For any integer
\beta\geq 1
, a function is
\beta
-Hölder
if the first
(\beta-1)
derivatives exist and are Lipschitz (the formal definition is deferred to Section
2
).
An instance is
\beta
-Hölder if the reward function
r_{a}(\cdot)
of each arm can be embedded into a
\beta
-Hölder function
f_{a}(\cdot)
on
[0,1]
, as formally specified by eqn. (
1
).

In particular, for
\beta=1
, this recovers the Lipschitz model discussed above.
Setting
\beta\geq 2
, we constrain the adversary more than in the previous literature, since the sequence of mean rewards can be embedded into a
differentiable
function.
This motivates the central question of this work:

Q2:
Can we break the minimax regret bound,
\tilde{\Theta}(T^{2/3})
, for the non-smooth setting (i.e.,
\beta=1
), under smooth non-stationarity (i.e.,
\beta\geq 2
)?

We provide an affirmative answer by showing an
\tilde{O}(T^{3/5})
upper bound on the minimax regret for
\beta=2
.
Moreover, this bound is
nearly optimal
: We show that for any integer
\beta\geq 1
, the minimax regret is
\Omega(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta+1)})
for
\beta
-Hölder instances.
In particular, for
\beta=2
, our upper and lower bounds match up to logarithmic factors.

1.1
Our Contributions

Our contributions can be categorized into the following three parts.

1.

A novel formulation.
Our first contribution is to introduce
smoothness
in non-stationary online models.
We quantify the smoothness of an arrival sequence using a standard concept,
Hölder smoothness
, from non-parametric statistics.
Loosely, a non-stationary bandits instance is
\beta
-Hölder if the mean reward sequence can be embedded into a
\beta
-Hölder function (formally defined in Section
2
).
Our formulation provides a unified framework for existing non-stationary models: Special cases have been studied for
\beta=1

(
Besbes et al. 2014
)
and
\beta\in(0,1)

(
Manegueu et al. 2021
)
.

2.

Upper bounds.
We present a simple policy and analyze its regret for the settings
\beta=1,2
.
Specifically, we show the following results.

(a)

A simple derivative-free policy:
We propose a simple policy, dubbed the
Budgeted Exploration
(BE) policy, which “restarts” periodically by discarding all historical observations.
Surprisingly, our policy does
not
use any derivative information.

(b)

Upper bound for
\beta=1
: an alternate proof.

Theorem 1 in
Besbes et al. 2014
implies that their policy (which is different from ours) has
\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})
regret for
\beta=1
.
We show that our BE policy has
\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})
regret; see our Theorem
5.2
.

(c)

Regret analysis via a potential function argument:
We analyze the regret of the BE policy using a
potential function
argument, a common technique in competitive analysis but less so in MAB.
Informally, consider an adversary who aims to produce a mean-reward sequence to fool the learner and hence forge a high regret.
We argue that if the regret is high in some interval, then the adversary loses a
proportionally
large amount of energy (in terms of
T
) and is therefore less powerful in the future.
Therefore, we can bound the total regret in terms of the initial energy of the adversary.

(d)

Separation between smooth and non-smooth settings:
We show that our BE policy has
\tilde{O}(k^{4/5}T^{3/5})
regret when
\beta=2
; see Theorem
6.2
.
We emphasize that this is the first
separation
between the smooth (i.e.,
\beta\geq 2
) and non-smooth (i.e.,
\beta=1
) regimes for non-stationary bandits:
For
\beta=1
, the minimax regret is
\Omega(T^{2/3})
(see (b)), which is higher than our upper bound for
\beta=2
.

3.

Lower bounds.
We provide an
\Omega(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta+1)})
lower bound on the minimax regret that is valid for any integer
\beta\geq 1
; see Theorem
4.4
.
This result holds significance due to the following.

(a)

Minimax optimality.
For
\beta=2
, this lower bound matches our
\tilde{O}(T^{3/5})
upper bound for
\beta=2
up to logarithmic factors.

(b)

Lower bound for
\beta=1
: an alternative proof.

Besbes et al. 2014
showed that for any total variation budget
V
, any policy has
\Omega(V^{1/3}T^{2/3})
regret; see their Theorem 1.
Our Theorem
4.4
implies an alternative proof for this result by setting
\beta=1
.
Moreover, the construction in
Besbes et al. 2014
employs mean-reward sequences with “jumps”.
In contrast, our proof uses mean-reward sequences that change continuously.
This suggests a surprising insight: Unlike between the smooth and non-smooth settings, there is
no
separation between the continuous and discontinuous settings.

(c)

Conjecture: smooth non-stationary bandits is almost as easy as stationary bandits?

Our general lower bound leads us to the conjecture that the lower bounds can be matched for
\beta\geq 3
.
Assuming it is true, then the minimax regret
T^{1/2+O(1/\beta)}
tends to
\sqrt{T}
, as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, almost matching that of
stationary
bandits.
In other words, this would lead to a rather surprising insight: Non-stationary bandits with high smoothness is almost as manageable as stationary bandits.

2
Formulation

We now formally define our non-stationary bandit model.
We are given a finite set
[k]
of arms and a known finite time horizon
[T]
. (For any integer
n\geq 1
we write
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
.)
For each arm
a\in[k]
, let
(Z_{a}^{t})_{t\in[T]}
be independent
\sigma
-subgaussian random variables that represent the realized rewards.
For simplicity, we assume
\sigma=1
; our results can be extended to any
\sigma>0
straightforwardly.
With this simplification, we can fully specify an
instance
by the
reward function

r_{a}(t):=\mathbb{E}[Z_{a}^{t}]
of the arms.
As is standard in the prevailing literature, we assume that the mean rewards are bounded.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{a}^{t}]\in[-1,1]
for all
a\in[k]
and
t\in[T]
.

In each round
t\in[T]
, the learner selects an arm
A_{t}
and receives an observable
reward

Y_{t}:=Z_{A_{t}}^{t}
.
Based on the past rewards observed, the learner selects the next arm
A_{t+1}
, with the goal of maximizing the cumulative rewards.
This decision-making process is called a
policy
. Formally, a policy
(A_{t})_{t\in[T]}
is a stochastic process such that
A_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable for each
t\in[T]
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{Z_{a}^{s}:a\in[k],1\leq s\leq t-1\}
.

An important special case is the
one-armed
setting, which is often used to highlight the essential ideas in non-stationary bandits (see, e.g., Section 35.3 of
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
).
There are two arms: a
static arm
, whose reward function is a constant,
0
, and a
changing arm
.
The core challenge is to learn the sign of the mean reward of the changing arm.

2.1
The Hölder Class and Smooth Non-stationary Bandits

For every arm
a
, the reward function
r_{a}(t)
is specified over the
discrete
domain
\{1,\dots,T\}
, making it unclear how to characterize the smoothness of non-stationarity in this context.
To do this, we first define the smoothness of a function defined on a
continuous
domain.

Definition 2.1

(Hölder Class)
.

For any
L>0
and integer
\beta\geq 1
, we say a function
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\real
is
\beta
-Hölder
and write
f\in\Sigma(\beta,L)
if (i)
f
is (
\beta-1
)-times differentiable, and (ii)
f^{(\beta-1)}
and
f
are
L
-Lipschitz.

For concreteness, observe that
f\in\Sigma(1,L)
if and only if
f
is
L
-Lipschitz, and that
f\in\Sigma(2,L)
if and only if
f
is differentiable and
f^{\prime},f
are
L
-Lipschitz.

A bandit instance is
\beta
-Hölder if its reward function can be
embedded
into a
\beta
-Hölder function in the following sense.

Definition 2.2

(Smooth Non-stationary Instance)
.

We say that a sequence
\{r(t)\}
can be
embedded
in a function
\mu:[0,1]\rightarrow\real
if for any
t\in[T]
, it holds that

r(t)=\mu\left(\frac{t}{T}\right).

A non-stationary bandit instance
r=\{r_{a}(t)\}
is
\beta
-Hölder
, if the reward function of every arm
a\in[k]
can be embedded in some function
\mu_{a}\in\Sigma(\beta,L)
.

For example, in the one-armed case, consider
r_{1}(t)=f(t/T)
for
f(x)=|x-\frac{1}{2}|
.
This instance is
1
-Hölder but
not

2
-Hölder.
In fact, for any differentiable function
g(x)
with
r_{1}(t)=g(t/T)
, the derivative
g^{\prime}(x)
changes from
-1
to
+1
in an interval of length
O(1/T)
near
x=\frac{1}{2}
.
Consequently,
|g^{\prime\prime}(x)|=\Omega(T)
, but the definition of
2
-Hölder requires
g^{\prime\prime}
be bounded by an
absolute
constant.

We emphasize that in Definition
2.2
,
\mu_{a}
is defined on the
normalized
time scale
[0,1]
while
r_{a}
is defined on
[T]=\{1,\dots,T\}
.
To avoid confusion, we will use “
x
” for the
[0,1]
scale and “
t
” for the
[T]
scale.
This work focuses on characterizing the minimax regret of the Hölder family, formally defined as follows.

Definition 2.3

(The Hölder Family)
.

For any integer
k\geq 2
,
\beta\geq 1
, we denote by
\Sigma_{k}(\beta,L)
the family of
k
-armed,
\beta
-Hölder instances.

We use
\Sigma_{1}(\beta,L)
to denote the family of one-armed instances (although these instances actually involve two arms).
Next, we will define the metric for assessing a policy.

2.2
The Regret

The problem is trivial if
r_{a}(\cdot)
’s were known.
In each round
t
, the optimal policy chooses
a_{t}^{*}\in\arg\max_{a\in\{0,1\}}r_{a}(t)
and collects, in expectation, a reward of
r^{*}(t)=\max_{a\in\{0,1\}}\{r_{a}(t)\}
.
When
r_{a}(\cdot)
’s are unknown, the policy needs to learn the reward functions.
Differently from stationary stochastic bandits or adversary bandits - where a policy is compared against the best fixed arm - the benchmark in the non-stationary setting may select different arms over time.
The
dynamic regret
is the difference between this benchmark and the expected total rewards of the policy.

Definition 2.4

(Regret)
.

The
dynamic regret
(or simply
regret
) of a policy
A
for an instance
r=\{r_{a}(t)\}
is

\textstyle\mathrm{Reg}(A,r)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r^{*}(t)-Z_{A_{t}}^{t}\right)\right].

For a family
\cal F
of instances, the
worst-case regret
of
A
is
\max_{r\in\cal F}\mathrm{Reg}(A,r)
.
The
minimax regret
of
\cal F
is the minimum achievable worst-case regret among all policies, formally defined as
\inf_{A}\sup_{r\in\cal F}{\rm Reg}(A,r)
, where “inf” is over all policies.

Compared to static regret, dynamic regret better captures the loss due to not knowing the underlying reward function and therefore serves as a better performance metric compared to against the best fixed arm.
Moreover, it is easy to verify that any bound on the dynamic regret carries over to static regret, but not vice versa.
Furthermore, in practical scenarios, such as those involving seasonal patterns, dynamic regret tends to be more meaningful than static regret.
For example, suppose that the reward function is given by
f(t)=\cos(2\pi t)
.
Then, any fixed-arm policy has
0
static regret and
\Omega(T)
dynamic regret.

It is crucial for the reader not to confuse
Hölder
class with
Hölder continuity
.
A function
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\real
is
\alpha
-Hölder-continuous
if
|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|\leq|x-x^{\prime}|^{\alpha}
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in[0,1]
.
This is only meaningful for
\alpha\leq 1
, as otherwise the function must be a constant.
In its most general form, for any
\beta>0
, the
\beta
-Hölder class consists of all functions
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\real
such that (i)
f
is
\ell:=\lfloor\beta\rfloor
times differentiable and (ii)
f^{(\ell)}
is
(\beta-\ell)
-Hölder-continuous; see, e.g., Definition 1.2 of
Tsybakov 2004
.
To highlight the key ideas, we focus on integer
\beta
’s, although the results can be extended to non-integer
\beta
’s straightforwardly.

Manegueu et al. 2021
considered
Hölder-continuous
non-stationary mean reward and considered embedding the mean reward sequence into a function defined on a continuous domain, as specified in our eqn. (
1
); see their Section 5.3.
Although they referred to their problem as “switching smooth expected rewards”, the slope of the mean reward may change abruptly, and hence the instances they considered are not really smooth.

From a more unified point of view,
Manegueu et al. 2021
considered
\beta<1
,
Besbes et al. 2014
considered
\beta=1
, and we are the first to consider
\beta>1
, which is our main contribution.
The leap from
\beta=1
to
\beta>1
is substantially
more
challenging than from
\beta=1
to
\beta<1
.
The former must leverage derivatives, whereas the latter can still work with the functions themselves while better tracking general
\beta<1
exponents in arguments similar to the case of
\beta=1
.

3
Related Work

We classify the related papers based on the following four characteristics: model-free non-stationarity, model-based non-stationarity, smoothness in bandits, and monotonicity in exploration.

Model-free Non-stationarity.

Recent multi-armed bandit literature has recognized the importance of
non-stationarity.
We say that a model is
model-free
if the non-stationarity is not defined by a statistical temporal model.

Auer et al. 2002
considered an extremely general model where the reward function can be
arbitrarily
non-stationary.
However, their
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret bound is not comparable to ours, since they compare against the best fixed arm in hindsight (i.e., static regret).
It should be noted that this frame can also capture a slightly more general benchmark called
tracking regret
, where we compare against the best policy that changes arms a certain number of times.
It can be shown that for any integer
m>0
, the EXP4 policy has
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{Tmk})
regret against any policy that changes arm
m
times; see eqn. (31.2) in
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
.

Variants of the UCB algorithm have been investigated for non-stationary environments.
The sliding-window UCB algorithm was introduced without analysis by
Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006
.

Garivier and Moulines 2011
analyzed this algorithm assuming that the mean rewards remain constant during periods and change at an unknown time (“breakpoint”).
This approach has also been extended to linear bandits
(
Cheung et al. 2019
)
and MDP
(
Gajane et al. 2018
)
.

Previous work has considered several aspects: contextual information
(
Luo et al. 2018
;
Russac et al. 2019
)
; uncertainty in the number of changes

(
Auer et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2019
)
; and Bayesian prior information
(
Trovo et al. 2020
)
, to name a few.
However, these papers make no smoothness assumptions on the non-stationarity.

In addition, the “smoothly changing setting” in
Trovo et al. 2020
considers Lipschitz reward functions that may be
non-differentiable
, and is therefore incomparable with our work.

Zhao et al. 2020
considered online convex optimization with “smooth” functions
f_{t}(x)
.
They assumed that in each round the arriving loss function has a bounded gradient in the
action

x
(instead of in the time
t
), which is different from our focus.

Model-based Non-stationarity.

There is a substantial literature that studies the dynamic aspect of the MAB problem by modeling the evolution of the reward function as a stochastic process.
Motivated by task scheduling,
Gittens 1979
considered a Bayesian setting in which the state of the selected arm evolves in a Markovian fashion and presented an optimal policy, based on the famous
Gittens index
.
Even more related is the
restless bandits
problem
(
Whittle 1988
)
, where the state of each arm evolves regardless of whether it is selected, but we only observe the state and reward of the selected arm.
This problem is rather challenging, since we need to learn the evolution of
all
the Markov chains.

Several other papers explore more specialized stochastic models to account for non-stationarity.

Slivkins and Upfal 2008
assumed that the reward function is drawn from a known reflected Brownian motion with variance
\sigma^{2}
, and showed an optimal regret of
\tilde{O}\left(k\sigma^{2}\right)
.
Other evolution models include other Gaussian processes
(
Grünewälder et al. 2010
)
and discrete Markov chains
(
Zhou et al. 2021
)
.

Smoothness in Bandits.

Model smoothness and the Hölder class are often studied in non-parametric statistics; see, e.g.,
Györfi et al. 2002
and
Tsybakov 2004
.
In the MAB literature,
\beta
-Hölder smoothness has been considered in contextual bandits, where the mean reward is assumed to have a smooth dependence on the context.

Rigollet and Zeevi 2010
and
Perchet and Rigollet 2013
primarily concentrated on cases where
\beta\leq 1,
while
Hu et al. 2020
and
Gur et al. 2022
directed their attention towards scenarios where
\beta\geq 1.

The key difference from our problem is
monotonicity
in the direction for exploration: Exploration can be done in any direction in the context space, but this is not true for the time axis.
Existing policies generally do not preserve monotonicity.
For example, Algorithm 1 in
Hu et al. 2020
maintains a hierarchical tree and zooms in on regions that are worth further exploration.
This idea is apparently infeasible in our setting.

Monotonicity in Exploration.

The
one-armed
version of our problem can be viewed as scanning the reward function monotonically at
uniform
speed, and making a decision on whether to pull arm
1
in each round.
This is related to a line of work in which the learner is required to explore a domain
monotonically
, with the goal of stopping near the
mode
(i.e., maximum) of the unknown reward function
(
Chen 2021
;
Jia et al. 2021
;
Jia et al. 2022
;
Salem et al. 2021
)
.

This problem can be viewed as the “dual” of our problem, where the learner controls the speed of scanning but must pull the changing arm in every round.
In this line of work, the impact of smoothness has been considered.
The minimax regret when
\beta=1
is
\tilde{\Theta}(T^{3/4})

(
Chen 2021
)
and
\tilde{\Theta}(T^{5/7})
when
\beta=2

(
Jia et al. 2021
)
, under the extra assumption that the reward function is unimodal.
We reiterate that these results are not applicable to our problem, as we do not have control over the scanning speed.

4
Lower Bounds

In this section, we construct a
\beta
-Hölder family of one-armed instances for any given integer
\beta\geq 1
, and show that every policy has
\Omega(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta+1)})
regret on it.
Each curve is defined as a
smooth
concatenation of several basic curves, in a way that prevents the learner from anticipating future evolution of the curve, even with access to past information.

4.1
Building a Bowl

We will partition the normalized time horizon
[0,1]
into
epochs
of length
\delta
.
Each curve in our family is a smooth concatenation of more basic functions, each being either a constant curve or a
bowl-shaped curve
(or a simple
bowl
).
A bowl consists of two
sides
, each of width
\Theta(\delta)
, and a flat part (i.e., constant) connecting them that has width
\Theta(\delta)
.
Specifically, each side of a bowl has
vanishing
derivatives up to order
(\beta-1)
at the endpoints.
This property enables us to concatenate a bowl
smoothly
with another bowl or with the constant curve, satisfying the requirement for the Hölder class.
Furthermore, each bowl has a maximum height
\Omega(\delta^{\beta})
.
We will use this property to make a policy suffer
\Omega(\delta^{\beta})
regret on each epoch.
We state these properties formally below.

Proposition 4.1

(Side of the Bowl)
.

For any fixed integer
\beta\geq 1
, there exists a family
\{g_{\varepsilon}\}
of
(\beta-1)
-times continuously differentiable functions where
g_{\varepsilon}
is defined on
[0,\varepsilon]
, with

(i) vanishing derivatives:

4.3
The Main Lower Bound

Now we are ready to state the lower bound for the one-armed case.
Recall that
m=1/(6\delta)
.
With some foresight, we choose
\delta=\Theta(T^{-1/(2\beta+1)})
.
We slightly abuse the notation by writing
{\rm Reg}(A,v)
the regret of a policy
A
when the mean reward of the changing arm is given by
r_{1}(t)=\mu_{v}(t/T)
.

Theorem 4.4

(Main Lower bound)
.

For any integer
\beta\geq 1
and policy
A
, there exists
v\in\{{\rm r},{\rm b}\}^{m}
such that

\mathrm{Reg}(A,v)>\frac{1}{24}\cdot 2^{-\beta}\left(C_{\beta}\right)^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}\cdot T^{\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta+1}}.

We outline the analysis and defer the formal proof to Appendix
A
.
First, observe that due to the smooth concatenation, the learner is unable to predict whether in the upcoming epoch the curve is bowl-shaped or constant.
Thus, we can view the problem as
m
separate instances, each having
6\delta T
rounds, and hence it suffices to lower bound the regret on each epoch.

To do this, fix an epoch
[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
.
As a key step, observe that for any point in

[x_{j}+2\delta,x_{j}+4\delta]
, the optimal arm is arm
0
if
r_{1}
follows the blue curve, and arm
1
otherwise.
We show that any policy has at least
1/2
probability of choosing the wrong arm in each of these rounds.

We now make this statement formal. Consider two instances that are identical up to the
(j-1)^{\mathrm{st}}
epoch.
For any prefix
u\in\{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{b}\}^{j-1}
and color
\chi\in\{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{b}\}
, we will consider the instances
\mu_{u\oplus\mathrm{r}}
and
\mu_{u\oplus\mathrm{b}}
where
\oplus
denotes the vector concatenation.
Recall that
t_{j}=x_{j}T
.

Lemma 4.5

(Likely to Select a Wrong Arm)
.

For any round
t
in the
j
-th epoch
[t_{j-1},t_{j}]
, prefix
u\in\{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{b}\}^{j-1}
and policy
A=(A_{s})_{s\in[T]}
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}_{u\oplus{\rm b}}[A_{t}=1]+\mathbb{P}_{u\oplus{\rm r}}[A_{t}=0]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

To show this, recall that the red and blue curves differ by
O(\delta^{\beta})
, and that an epoch comprises only
\delta T
rounds.
Thus, any policy “collects”
O(\delta^{\beta})^{2}\cdot\delta T=O(1)
Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence within an epoch.
Therefore, by Pinsker’s inequality, the red and blue curves cannot be distinguished, in the sense that the probability of any event differs by at most
1/2
under (the probability measure induced by) these two instances.
The desired inequality follows by applying this to the event that
A_{t}=1
.
We defer the details to Appendix
B
.

Since there are
\delta T
rounds in an epoch, the expected regret on an epoch is
\Omega(\delta^{\beta})\cdot 6\delta T=\Omega(\delta^{\beta+1}T)
.
Summing over all
1/\delta
epochs, the total regret is
\delta^{-1}\cdot\Omega(\delta^{\beta+1}T)=\Omega(\delta^{\beta}T)=\Omega(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta+1)})
.

In this section, we illustrate the key ideas behind our analysis in the

The mean rewards of arm

Furthermore, there exists an unknown function

changing

for all

.
The key is to determine the

of the mean reward of the changing arm.
We will suppress the subscripts and write
\mu(t)=\mu_{1}(t)
and

.

A natural policy is to estimate the rate of change in the reward rate and make decisions based on the predicted trend.
Surprisingly, our policy, which achieves minimax optimal regret, does
not
use any derivative information.
Instead, this policy initiates a “restart” at appropriate intervals, disregarding all previous observations.
We will provide a detailed description of this policy next.

5.1
The Budgeted Exploration Policy

As a rule of thumb in the non-stationary bandits, a reasonable policy should strike a balance between two trade-offs.
The first is
exploration vs. exploitation
, i.e., balancing between information acquisition versus capitalizing on existing information.
The second is
remembering vs. forgetting
, i.e., discarding old information at a judicious rate to account for non-stationarity.

To manage the second trade-off, we propose a
Budgeted Exploration (BE)
policy that “restarts” periodically, as detailed in Algorithm
1
.
The policy is specified by two parameters: the
exploration budget

B\geq 1
and the epoch size
\Delta\in(0,1)
.
It partitions the normalized timescale
[0,1]
into
epochs

[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
where
x_{i}=i\Delta
for each
i=0,\dots,\Delta^{-1}-1
.
Equivalently, it partitions
\{1,\dots,T\}
into epochs
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
where
t_{i}=x_{i}T
.
(For simplicity, we assume that
\Delta^{-1}
is an integer. Apparently, this is not essential for the asymptotic results.)
In each epoch, the policy selects the changing arm from the start of the epoch until either (i) the epoch ends or (ii) the budget
B
runs out, whereupon the policy selects the static arm in all remaining rounds in the epoch.

Unlike the Rexp3 algorithm in
Besbes et al. 2014
, which uses the EXP3 algorithm as a subroutine to handle exploration-exploitation, we employ a much simpler approach.
We continue to select arm
1
until its cumulative reward in this epoch is lower than
-B
.
This ensures that the average regret on each epoch is not too large.
This idea can also be generalized to the
multi
-armed setting by imposing a budget on the
difference
in the rewards of the empirically best arm.

5.2
Non-smooth Case:
\beta=1

Before delving deep into our focus, the

Proposition 5.1

(Generic Upper Bound,
\beta=1,k=1
)
.

Suppose

. Then

\mathrm{Reg}\left(\mathrm{BE}(\Delta,B),\Sigma_{1}(1,L)\right)\leq\Delta^{-1}(1+L\Delta^{2}T+B).

By selecting
\Delta=L^{-2/3}T^{-1/3}\log^{1/3}T\text{ and }B=L^{-1/3}T^{1/3}\log^{2/3}T,

1
-Hölder family.
This result, combined with Theorem

, characterizes the minimax regret for the
\beta=1

).

Theorem 5.2

(Upper Bound,
\beta=1,k=1
)
.

For any

, there exists some
B=\tilde{O}(T^{1/3})
and
\Delta=\tilde{O}(T^{-1/3})
such that

\mathrm{Reg}\left(\mathrm{BE}(B,\Delta),\Sigma_{1}\left({1,L}\right)\right)=O\left(L^{1/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}T\right).

Note that any function in
\Sigma(1,L)
has total variation

on
[0,1]
.
Therefore, the above is implied by the
\tilde{O}(V^{1/3}T^{2/3})

However, we analyze this result to help lay the foundation for the analysis in the
\beta=2

Our analysis proceeds by analyzing the regret on three types of epoch.
Observe that in each epoch, the function
\mu
either (i) is always positive, (ii) is always negative, or (iii) intersects the time axis, i.e.,
\mu(x)=0
for some
x
.
We refer to these three types of epochs as
positive
,
negative
and
crossing
epochs. We should mention that in a crossing epoch,
\mu
may not necessarily “cross” the time axis but merely “touches” it; however, this distinction is not essential to our analysis.

We upper bound the expected regret incurred in each type of epoch as follows:

1.

First consider a positive epoch.
By concentration bounds, we can show that the budget is unlikely to be depleted, and hence with high probability the policy always chooses the correct arm.

2.

Now consider a negative epoch.
Suppose the budget runs out in the
\tau
-th round after the epoch starts.
By standard concentration bounds, we can show that with a high probability the policy eliminates the correct arm
and therefore the expected regret after time
\tau
is
0
.
On the other hand, note that the total regret until time
\tau-1
is at most
B
.
By boundedness of the mean reward, we conclude that the regret until time
\tau
is
B+O(1)
.

3.

Finally, consider a crossing epoch.
Since
\mu
is
L
-Lipschitz, we have
\mu(x)=O(L\Delta)
on this epoch, where we recall that
\Delta\in[0,1]
is the length of an epoch (on the
normalized
time scale).
Thus, the total regret on this epoch is
O(L\Delta)\cdot\Delta T=O(L\Delta^{2}T)
.

Combining the analysis in the above three cases, we conclude that the regret on
every
epoch is
O(1+B+L\Delta^{2}T)
.
Proposition
5.1
follows immediately since there are
\Delta^{-1}
epochs.
We formalize the above analysis in Lemma
E.2
,
Lemma
E.3
and Lemma
E.4
respectively in Appendix
E
.

5.3
An
T^{3/5}
Upper Bound for
\beta=2

Based on the above analysis framework, we next show an
\tilde{O}(T^{3/5})
upper bound on the minimax regret for
2
-Hölder instances.
Again, we will show a generic bound first.

Proposition 5.3

(Generic Upper Bound,
\beta=2
,

)
.

Suppose

, then

\mathrm{Reg}\left(\mathrm{BE}(\Delta,B),\Sigma_{1}(2,L)\right)\leq 2\Delta^{-1}(1+B+L\Delta^{3}T).

By choosing

\Delta=L^{-2/5}T^{-1/5}\log^{1/5}T\text{ and }B=L^{-1/5}T^{2/5}\log^{3/5}T
, we obtain the following main result of this section.

Theorem 5.4

(Optimal Regret Bound,
\beta=2
)
.

For any
L>0
, there exist some
B=\tilde{O}(T^{2/5})
and
\Delta=\tilde{O}(T^{-1/5})
such that

\mathrm{Reg}\left(\mathrm{BE}(B,\Delta),\Sigma_{1}\left({2,L}\right)\right)=O\left(L^{1/5}T^{3/5}\log^{2/5}T\right).

Remarkably, this upper bound provides the first
separation
between the smooth
(\beta\geq 2)
and non-smooth
(\beta=1)
regimes
(
Besbes et al. 2014
)
.
Our analysis for
\beta=2
follows the framework of the
\beta=1
case.
The analysis for positive and negative epochs remains valid, since they do
not
rely on the smoothness of
\mu
.
We will refer to these two types of epochs as
non-crossing epochs
, as
\mu
does not cross the
x
-axis.

The main difference lies in the regret analysis for crossing epochs.
We will leverage the smoothness to argue that the regret on those epochs is low
on average
.
We distinguish between two types of crossing epoch, depending on whether they contain a zero of
\mu^{\prime}
.

Definition 5.5

(Stationary Points and Epochs)
.

A point
s\in[0,1]
is
stationary
if
\mu^{\prime}(s)=0
.
An epoch
[x_{j},x_{j+1}]

is
stationary
if it contains a stationary point, and is
non-stationary
otherwise.

The regret analysis on a stationary epoch is relatively easier.
By Lipschitzness of
\mu^{\prime}
and Newton-Leibniz theorem, on such an epoch we have
|\mu^{\prime}|\leq L\Delta
.
Thus, for small
\Delta\in(0,1)
, the reward function would not vary much.
In other words, the instance is almost stationary in this epoch and, therefore, easy to handle.

The key step is to bound the regret on
non-stationary
crossing epochs.
Imagine an adversary trying to fool the learner.
Knowing that the learner is using the BE policy, the adversary first chooses
\mu_{1}<0
at the beginning of an epoch, so that when the exploration budget runs out, the static arm has a higher mean reward.
The learner will then choose arm
0
for exploitation.
After the learner stops exploring, the adversary bends
\mu_{1}
upward to make it positive.
Consequently, the learner selects the wrong arm in the exploitation phase.

However, we next show that the adversary can not employ this tactic too often, due to the smoothness requirement.
We show that to generate a very high regret on a crossing epoch
i
, the nearest stationary point
x
must be
proportionally
far away.
This enables us to
amortize
the regret on epoch
i
to the epochs between
x
and crossing epoch
i
.

Lemma 5.6

(Key Lemma: Regret on Crossing Epochs)
.

Let
j
be a crossing epoch, and
j+\ell
be a stationary epoch (
\ell
may be negative or
0
).
Moreover, suppose every epoch between them is non-stationary, i.e., epoch
i
is non-stationary whenever
(j+\ell-i)\cdot(j-i)<0
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}R_{t}\right]\leq 2L\cdot(|\ell|+1)\cdot\Delta^{3}T.

We briefly explain the high-level idea.
Denote by
\|f\|_{\infty}
the supremum of the absolute value of a function
f
on epoch
j
.
Since
\mu^{\prime}
is Lipschitz and vanishes at some point in epoch
j+\ell
, we deduce that
\|\mu^{\prime}\|_{\infty}=O(L\cdot\ell\Delta)
.
Moreover, since
\mu
has a crossing in epoch
j
, we have
\|\mu\|_{\infty}=O(\|\mu^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\cdot\Delta)
.
Therefore, the total regret is bounded by
\Delta T\cdot\|\mu\|_{\infty}=O(L\cdot\ell\Delta^{3}T)
.

With this lemma, we next derive our main regret bound and defer the details to Appendix
F
.

Proof Sketch of Proposition
5.3
.

We show that the
average
regret per epoch is
O(L\Delta^{3}T)
.
Let us index the stationary epochs as
1\leq s_{1}<\dots<s_{n}\leq\Delta^{-1}
.
Crucially, we observe that for any
j
, there is at most one crossing epoch between
s_{j}
and
s_{j+1}
.
If it does exist, we denote by
i_{\mathrm{x}}
the epoch that contains it.
Then, by Lemma
5.6
, the regret on this epoch satisfies

R[i_{\mathrm{x}}]\leq 2L\cdot(|i_{\mathrm{x}}-s_{j}|+1)\cdot\Delta^{3}T\leq 2L\cdot|s_{j+1}-s_{j}|\cdot\Delta^{3}T.

Since the other epochs are non-crossing, the regret on each of them is
O(B)
.
Thus, the total regret on epochs
s_{j}
through
s_{j+1}
can be bounded as

\sum_{s_{j}\leq i<s_{j+1}}R[i]\leq(s_{j+1}-s_{i})\cdot O(L\Delta^{3}T+B).

Equivalently, the average regret on each epoch is
O(L\Delta^{3}T+B)
.

\square

As a caveat, the above proof assumes that there is at least one stationary point.
We finally handle the corner case where
\mu
has no stationary point.

Proposition 5.7

(Corner Case: No Stationary Point)
.

Suppose
\mu^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in[0,1]
.
Then, for any
B>0
and
\Delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\mathrm{Reg}\left(\mathrm{BE}(B,\Delta),\mu\right)\leq L\Delta^{2}T+(B+1)\Delta^{-1}.

5.7

.

The Budgeted Exploration Policy, Finite-Armed Case

The main result of this section is a regret bound that is (i) minimax nearly optimal in
T
and (ii) sublinear in
k
.
As in the one-armed case, we start with a regret bound for arbitrary
B,\Delta
.

Proposition 6.1

(Generic Upper Bound,
\beta=2
,
k\geq 2
)
.

Suppose
B^{2}\geq k^{-1}\Delta T\log T\log k
.
Then for any finite
k
, Lipschitz constant
L>0
, we have

{\rm Reg}\left({\rm BE}(B,\Delta),\ \Sigma_{k}(2,L)\right)\leq 2\Delta^{-1}\left(1+kB+k^{2}L\Delta^{3}T\right).

It is important to note that, in contrast to the bound outlined in Proposition
5.3
, we have introduced an additional factor of
k
before
B
and
k^{2}
before
L\Delta^{3}T
.
By choosing

\Delta=L^{-2/5}k^{-3/5}T^{-1/5}\cdot\log^{1/5}T\cdot\log^{1/5}k\quad\text{and}\quad B=L^{-1/5}\sqrt{k^{-1}\Delta T\log T\log k},

we immediately obtain the following.

Theorem 6.2

(Upper Bound,
\beta=2
,
k\geq 1
)
.

For any finite
k\geq 1
, Lipschitz constant
L>0
, there exists some
\Delta=\tilde{O}(k^{-3/5}T^{-1/5})
and
B=\tilde{O}(k^{-1/2}T^{2/5})
such that

{\rm Reg}\left({\rm BE}(B,\Delta),\Sigma_{k}(2,L)\right)=O\left(k^{4/5}T^{3/5}\cdot\log^{2/5}k\cdot\log^{2/5}T\right).

Our analysis employs a
potential function
argument, a common technique in competitive analysis.
Imagine an adversary who constructs the reward function to fool the learner.
The adversary starts with a certain amount of
potential
.
We argue that every time the adversary forges an epoch with high regret, the potential decreases
proportionally
to the regret on this epoch.
Consequently, the adversary has less power in the future.
Therefore, we can bound the regret in terms of the
initial
potential.

The critical step in this argument is to find an appropriate definition of potential.
The pivotal insight is derived from the key lemma in the one-armed case (Lemma
5.6
):
The only way for the adversary to generate a
\omega(T^{2/5})
regret in an epoch is to create a crossing with a high derivative.
(We write
f(x)=\omega(g(x))
if
g(x)=o(f(x))
.)

To generalize this idea to the multi-armed setting, we re-define a
crossing
as a point where the
best
arm changes.
Furthermore, we define a
fast crossing
as a crossing where the derivatives of the two arms involved differ by
\Omega(\Delta)
.

We classify the epochs into three types: (i) non-crossing epochs, in which the best arm never changes;
(ii) slow-crossing epochs, which contain a crossing but no fast crossings; and (iii) fast-crossing epochs, which contain at least one fast crossing.

The regret analysis for the first two types of epoch is similar to the one-armed setting.
To analyze the regret in the third type, we define the
energy
at a fast crossing as the difference in
\mu^{\prime}
between the two arms involved.
Repeating the argument in Lemma
5.6
for all
ordered
pairs of arms, we show that, for any instance, the total energy is at most
k(k-1)L
.
By choosing this to be the initial potential, we can bound the total regret on all type-(iii) epochs as
O(k^{2}L\Delta^{2}T)
.
We defer the details to Appendix
I
.

7.1
Additional Benchmark: Rexp3 policy

Apart from the BE-NS policy, we consider another benchmark, Rexp3, proposed by
Besbes et al. 2014
. To explain this policy, we first provide some background.
In the
adversarial bandits
problem, the reward functions are chosen by an adversary sequentially.
A natural approach is the
multiplicative weights algorithm
(MWA).
In simple terms, it maintains a weight for each arm and randomly draws an arm in each round with a probability proportional to the weight.

However, to update the weights, we need to assume
full information
feedback, i.e., we observe the rewards of all arms regardless of which arm is selected.
To operate under
bandit feedback
, where we only observe the reward of the selected arm, the EXP3 algorithm was introduced.
This policy “simulates” MWA using unbiased reward estimators and achieves
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
expected regret against the best arm in hindsight.

To incorporate the forgetting-vs-remembering trade-off,
Besbes et al. 2014
proposed the “restart” version of the EXP3 algorithm, dubbed
Rexp3
.
This algorithm partitions the horizon into epochs (or
batches
, as in their work) of length
\Delta_{T}
, and
ignores all previous observations at the beginning of each epoch.
Moreover, for robustness in the performance, they integrated this policy with the
\varepsilon
-greedy algorithm.
The policy randomly uniformly explores an arm in each round with a fixed probability
\gamma
.
This policy achieves
\tilde{O}(V^{1/3}T^{2/3})
regret where
V
is the total variation of the mean rewards; see their Theorem 1.

8
Conclusions and Future Directions

In this paper, we presented
smoothly-varying
non-stationary bandits and demonstrated the first separation between the smooth and non-smooth settings.
We present a derivative-free policy that achieves
\tilde{O}(T^{3/5})
regret in the smooth setting, which is asymptotically lower than the
\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})
minimax regret for the non-smooth regime.
Moreover, we show that this upper bound is nearly optimal by establishing an
\Omega(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta+1)})
lower bound for the minimax regret on the
\beta
-Hölder family for every integer
\beta\geq 1
.
Finally, we conjecture that the lower bounds can be matched for every integer
\beta\geq 3
but this remains open. If this is true, it means that as smoothness increases, we can obtain regret that approaches the optimal
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret of
stationary
bandits, since
\tilde{O}(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta+1)})=T^{1/2+O(1/\beta)}
.

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
4.5

First, we introduce some standard concepts and tools.
For simplicity, for any instance
\mu:[0,1]\rightarrow\real
, let
(Z_{\mu}^{t})_{t\in[T]}
be the reward vector under this instance.

Definition B.1

(KL-Divergence)
.

Let
X,Y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be random vectors, specified by probability mass functions
f_{X},f_{Y}:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
.
The
Kullback-Leibler divergence
(or KL divergence) is defined as

\mathrm{KL}(X,Y)=\sum_{v\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f_{X}(v)\log\frac{f_{X}(v)}{f_{Y}(v)}.

We show that at any time, the KL-divergence of the two random variables is on the order of the squared difference of their means.

Lemma B.2

(Bounding the KL-Divergence)
.

For
i=1,2
, suppose random variable
Z_{i}
takes value on
\{\pm 1\}
and has mean
r_{i}
.
Then, when
|r_{2}|\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\mathrm{KL}(Z_{1},Z_{2})\leq\frac{4}{3}\left(r_{1}-r_{2}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Proof.
By definition, we can write
Z_{i}=2X_{i}-1
where
Z_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ber}\left(\frac{r_{i}+1}{2}\right)
for
i=1,2
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(Z_{1},Z_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{KL}(X_{1},X_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{1}+1}{2}\ln\frac{r_{1}+1}{r_{2}+1}+\frac{1-r_{1}}{2}\ln\frac{1-r_{1}}{1-r_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{1}+1}{2}\frac{r_{1}-r_{2}}{r_{2}+1}+\frac{1-r_{1}}{2}\frac{r_{2}-r_{1}}{1-r_{2}}

Appendix D
Preliminaries for the Upper Bound

Before presenting the proofs of Theorems

We will focus on bounding the regret conditional on the following

Loosely, this is the event that the rewards in all sufficiently large time intervals obey Hoeffding’s inequality.

Definition D.1

(Clean Event)
.

For any arm
a
and rounds
t,t^{\prime}\in[T]

\mathcal{C}_{a}^{t,t^{\prime}}=\left\{\sum_{s=t}^{t^{\prime}}\left(Z_{a,s}-\mu_{a}(s)\right)\leq\sqrt{6\log T\cdot(t^{\prime}-t)}\right\}.

We define the

\mathcal{C}=\bigcap_{a,t,t^{\prime}}\mathcal{C}_{a}^{t,t^{\prime}}
where the intersection is over all arms

.

To show the event

occurs with high probability, we use the following basic concentration bound, whose proof can be found in, e.g.,
Vershynin 2018
.

Theorem D.2

(Concentration Bounds)
.

Let
Z_{1},...,Z_{m}
be independent random variables supported on
[-1,1]
and
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}
.
Then for any
\delta>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]|>\delta)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2m}\right).

Lemma D.3

(Clean Event Occurs w.h.p.)
.

For any
T
, it holds that
\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{C}}]\leq T^{-1}
.

Proof.

Proof. By Hoeffding’s inequality

\square

\sum_{s_{j}\leq i<s_{j+1}}R[i]\leq(s_{j+1}-s_{i})\cdot(2L\Delta^{3}T+B+1).

The above clearly also holds when there is no crossing between
s_{j}
and
s_{j+1}
.
In fact, in this case, the
2L\Delta^{3}T
term disappears.
Summing over
j=1,\dots,n
, we have

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{1/\Delta}R[i]

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{s_{j}\leq i<s_{j+1}}R[i]

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(s_{j+1}-s_{i})\cdot(2L\Delta^{3}T+B+1)

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq 2n\cdot(L\Delta^{3}T+B),

and the desired bound follows by noticing that
n\leq\frac{1}{\Delta}
.

\square

Appendix I
Proof of Proposition
6.1
: An
\tilde{O}(T^{3/5})
Bound for the Multi-armed Case

We provide a formal proof of the upper bound for the general finite-armed setting with
\beta=2
.

I.1
Preliminaries

To formalize the ideas outlined in Section
6
, we extend the notion of
crossing
by redefining it as points at which the best arm changes.
We will specifically pay attention to crossings where the overtaker’s speed is substantially faster than that of the arm being overtaken, formally defined as follows.

Definition I.1

(Crossing and Fast Crossing)
.

We say that an arm
\alpha

overtakes
another arm
\beta
, if there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
\varepsilon\in[-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}]
, we have
\mu_{\alpha}(x+\varepsilon)>\mu_{\beta}(x+\varepsilon)
if and only if
\varepsilon>0
.
For any
x\in[0,1]
, denote by
a^{*}(x)\in[k]
the arm with the highest mean reward at
x
(where we break ties arbitrarily).
We say that
x
is a
crossing
, if an arm
a
overtakes
a^{*}(x)
at
x
.
Furthermore,
x
is a
fast-crossing
if
\mu^{\prime}_{a}(x)-\mu^{\prime}_{a^{*}(x)}(x)\geq\Delta.

Our regret analysis employs a
potential function argument
, which is quite common in competitive analysis for online algorithms.
The adversary starts with a certain amount of
potential
.
We will show in Section
I.2
that the regret is low on a (i)
non-crossing
epoch, i.e., an epochs that contains no crossing, and (ii)
stationary crossing
epoch, i.e., an epoch that contains a crossing but not a fast crossing.

Therefore, to generate a high regret on an epoch, the adversary needs to forge a fast crossing.
As the key step in the analysis, we argue that this consumes a high energy (formally defined in Section
I.4
) and results in a large reduction of the potential. Consequently, the adversary has less power in the future.
Subsequently, we will fix and suppress the epoch length
\Delta\in[0,1]
.

For the analysis, we modify the definition of
clean event
as follows.

Definition I.2

(Clean Event)
.

For any arm
a
and rounds
t,t^{\prime}\in[T]
, consider the event

\mathcal{C}_{a}^{t,t^{\prime}}=\left\{\sum_{s=t}^{t^{\prime}}\left(Z_{a}^{s}-\mu_{a}(s)\right)\leq\sqrt{\frac{6\log k}{k}\cdot\log T\cdot(t^{\prime}-t)}\right\}.

We define the
clean event
as
\mathcal{C}=\bigcap_{a,t,t^{\prime}}\mathcal{C}_{a}^{t,t^{\prime}}
where the intersection is over all arms
a
and pairs of
t,t^{\prime}
with
t^{\prime}>t+2\log T
.

Compared to the one-armed case, in the above definition, we have an extra
\frac{\log k}{k}
factor.
Intuitively, the term
\log k
is introduced for applying the union bound on the
k
arms, and the term
1/k
occurs since each arm is only guaranteed to be selected
t/k
times after
t
rounds.
Next, we show that
\cal C
occurs with high probability.

Lemma I.3

(Clean Event Occurs w.h.p.)
.

For any
k,T\geq 1
, we have
\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{C}}]\leq k^{-1}T^{-2}.

Proof.

Proof.
By Hoeffding’s inequality
[
Vershynin 2018
, Theorem 2.2.6]
,
for any
1\leq t\leq t^{\prime}\leq T
with
t^{\prime}-t\geq 2\log T
, taking

\delta=\sqrt{\frac{6\log k}{k}\log T\cdot(t^{\prime}-t)},

we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{\mathcal{C}_{a}^{t,t^{\prime}}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(t^{\prime}-t)/k}\cdot 6\log T\cdot\frac{t^{\prime}-t}{k}\right)=k^{-1}T^{-3}.

There are at most
T^{2}
combinations of
t,t^{\prime}
, so by the union bound, we conclude that

\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{C}}]=\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcup_{a,t,t^{\prime}}\overline{\mathcal{C}_{a}^{t,t^{\prime}}}\right]\leq\sum_{a,t,t^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{\mathcal{C}_{a}^{t,t^{\prime}}}\right)\leq k^{-1}T^{-1}.

\square

I.2
Non-crossing epochs

In this section bound the regret on non-crossing epochs.

Lemma I.4

(Regret on a Non-crossing Epoch)
.

Suppose that
R
the regret of the BE policy
{\rm BE}(B,\Delta)
on a non-crossing epoch and
B^{2}\geq k^{-1}\Delta T\log T\log k
, then

\mathbb{E}[R]\leq 1+kB.

Proof.

Proof. By definition of
C
, the cumulative reward after
s
rounds (counting from the start of the epoch) deviates from the mean by at most
\sqrt{k^{-1}s\log T\log k}
.
Since
s\leq\Delta T
, this quantity is at most
\sqrt{k^{-1}\Delta T\log T\log k}
.
Thus, if
B\geq\sqrt{k^{-1}\Delta T\log T\log k}
, the BE policy will never eliminate the best arm.
In other words, if all but one arm are eliminated at some time, then the future regret in this epoch is
0
.
On the other hand, regardless of where
C
occurs, the regret incurred by each arm is bounded by
(B+1)
by Lemma
E.3
.
Therefore, we can bound the regret on this epoch as

\mathbb{E}[R]\leq\mathbb{E}[R|\bar{C}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\bar{C}]+\mathbb{E}[R|C]\cdot\mathbb{P}[C]\leq\Delta T\cdot T^{-3}+(k-1)\cdot(B+1)\leq 1+kB.

\square

I.3
Regret on Stationary Epochs

We next bound the regret on stationary epochs.
Recall that a crossing epoch is stationary if it does not contain a fast crossing.
We will categorize the arms into two groups: those that have
ever
been the best arm at some point during this epoch and the rest.
We formalize this notion as follows. Recall that
r_{a}(t)=\mu_{a}(t/T)
for all
t\in[T]
, where
\mu_{a}:[0,1]\rightarrow[-1,1]
is a
2
-Hölder function.

Definition I.5

(Arms That Have Ever Been the Best)
.

For any epoch
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]\subseteq[0,1]
, we define

A^{*}_{i}=\left\{a\in[k]:\quad\exists x\in[x_{i},x_{i+1}]\text{ such that }\mu_{a}(x)\geq\mu_{a^{\prime}}(x),\ \forall a^{\prime}\in[k]\right\}

We first show that, on any stationary epoch
i
, the mean rewards of the arms in
A^{*}=A^{*}_{i}
do not differ much.
To see this, observe that, by definition, every
a\in A^{*}
“overtakes” the best arm at some point.
Moreover, when this happens, the difference in the derivatives differs by at most
L\Delta
.
Since there can be at most
k
crossings in an epoch, the derivatives of any two arms can only differ by
kL\Delta
on this epoch.
We summarize this observation as the following lemma.

Lemma I.6

(Arms in
A^{*}
Have Similar Rewards)
.

Let
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
be a stationary epoch.
Then for any
a,b\in A^{*}_{i}
and any
x\in[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
, we have

|\mu_{a}(x)-\mu_{b}(x)|\leq kL\Delta^{2}.

We will show that on the clean event, if all but one arm are eliminated at some point, then the remaining arm must be in
A^{*}
.
Consequently, the regret in the exploitation phase is low.
Formally, we have the following bound.
For convenience, we switch to the non-normalized time scale, where we recall that
t_{i}=x_{i}\Delta
.

Lemma I.7

(Regret on Stationary Crossing Epochs)
.

Suppose
B^{2}\geq k^{-1}\Delta T\log T\log k
.
Let
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]\subseteq[0,T]
be a stationary epoch. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}R_{t}\right]\leq kB+kL\Delta^{3}T.

Proof.

Proof. Let
S
be the time when all but one arms are eliminated and write
A^{*}=A^{*}_{i}
.
First, we observe that on the clean event
C
, any arm in
A^{*}
can never be eliminated for “losing” to an arm in
(A^{*})^{c}
.
In fact, this is because the width of the confidence interval scales as
\sqrt{t}
, and we assumed that
B^{2}\geq k^{-1}\Delta T\log T\log k
.

We use the above to bound the regret after time
S
.
By Lemma
I.6
, for any
a,b\in A^{*}
we have
|\mu_{a}(x)-\mu_{b}(x)|\leq kL\Delta^{2}
for all
x
.
In particular, at any time
x
, let
\mu^{*}(x)=\max_{a\in[k]}\mu_{a}(x)
, then

|\mu_{a}(x)-\mu^{*}(x)|\leq kL\Delta^{2}.

Thus, the regret after
S
can be bounded as

\sum_{t=S}^{t_{i+1}}R_{t}\leq L\Delta^{2}\cdot\Delta T=kL\Delta^{3}T.

On the other hand, the regret before
S
is bounded by
kB
.
Therefore, the total regret on this epoch is bounded as

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}R_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=t_{i}}^{S}R_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=S}^{t_{i}+\Delta^{-1}T}R_{t}\right]\leq kB+kL\Delta^{3}T.

\square

I.4
Regret on Fast Crossing Epochs

We next show that each epoch has at most one fast crossing for each ordered pair of arms.

Lemma I.8

(At Most One Fast Crossing Per Epoch)
.

For any pair
\alpha,\beta
of arms and any epoch, there is at most one fast crossing where
\alpha
overtakes
\beta
.

Proof.

Proof.
Suppose
\alpha
overtakes
\beta
at some
x_{0}\in[0,1]
and
\mu_{\alpha}^{\prime}(x)-\mu_{\beta}^{\prime}(x)\geq L\Delta
. Denote by
g(x):=\mu_{\alpha}(x_{0}+x)-\mu_{\beta}(x_{0}+x).

Then,
g(0)=0
and
g^{\prime}(0)\geq L\Delta
.
By the defintion of Hölder class,
g^{\prime}
is
L
-Lipschitz, so for any
x>0
we have

g^{\prime}(x)\geq g^{\prime}(0)-Lx\geq L\Delta-Lx.

Thus, for any
x>0
, it holds that

g(x)=\int_{0}^{x}g^{\prime}(s)\ ds\geq\int_{0}^{x}L\Delta-Ls\ ds=L\Delta x-\frac{L}{2}x^{2}.

Therefore, for any
0\leq x\leq 2\Delta
, we have

g(x)=\mu_{\alpha}(x)-\mu_{\beta}(x)\geq 0.

In other words, the next time
\alpha
overtakes
\beta
occurs at least
\Delta/L
time later. Therefore, there is at most one fast crossing where
\alpha
overtakes
\beta
.
\square

Note that the above is consistent with the definition for
k=1
. We next introduce the notion of energy, which captures the adversary’s ability to cause high regret in the neighborhood of a crossing.

Definition I.9

(Energy)
.

Suppose
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
are all fast crossings, and at
x_{i}
, arm
\alpha(i)
overtakes
\beta(i)
.
Define the
energy
of the
i
-th fast crossing by

\kappa_{i}:=|\mu^{\prime}_{\alpha(i)}(x_{i})-\mu^{\prime}_{\beta(i)}(x_{i})|.

We now show that the total potential is bounded by a constant (in
T
).

Lemma I.10

(Conservation of Energy)
.

Under the notations in Definition
I.9
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\kappa_{i}\leq(k-1)kL.

Proof.

Proof. For a fixed (ordered) pair of arms
a,b
, denote by
x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{\ell}}
the subsequence of fast crossings
where
a
overtakes
b
.
For simplicity, we write
x_{j}=x_{i_{j}}
.
Observe that between any two crossings
x_{i},x_{i+1}
, there exists at least one stationary point, which we denote by
s_{i}
.
By Lipschitzness of
\mu^{\prime}
, we have

\kappa_{i}=|\mu^{\prime}(x_{i})-\mu^{\prime}(s_{i})|\leq L|s_{i}-x_{i}|.

Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\kappa_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}L|s_{i}-x_{i}|\leq L,

where the last inequality follows since the intervals
\{(x_{i},s_{i}):i\in[\ell]\}
are disjoint.
Since there are
k(k-1)
distinct ordered pairs of arms, the claimed inequality follows.

\square

Finally, we connect the total energy to the regret. Its proof is similar to that of Lemma
5.6
, so we omit it here.

Lemma I.11

(Regret is Proportional to the Energy)
.

Under the notations in Definition
I.9
, denote by
R[i]
the expected regret of the policy
{\rm BE}(B,\Delta)
on the epoch containing
x_{i}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}R[i]\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\kappa_{i}\Delta^{2}T.

Again, we emphasize by Lemma
I.8
, each epoch contains only one fast crossing, and thus
R[i]
and
R[j]
correspond to distinct epochs if
i\neq j
.
We now combine all this and prove the main upper bound.

I.5
Proof of Proposition
6.1

Denote by
E_{\rm nx},E_{\rm st},E_{\rm fx}\subseteq\{1,\dots,\Delta^{-1}\}
the subset of non-crossing epochs, stationary crossing epochs and non-stationary crossing epochs (recall that such epochs are exactly crossing epochs with a fast crossing).
From Lemma
I.4
, the total regret on each in
E_{\rm nx}
is at most
1+kB
.
By Lemma
I.7
, the regret on each epoch in
E_{\rm st}
is bounded by
kB+kL\Delta^{3}T
.
Therefore,

\sum_{j\in E_{\rm st}}R[j]\leq|E_{\rm st}|\cdot(kB+kL\Delta^{3}T).

Combining Lemma
I.10
and Lemma
I.11
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in E_{\rm fx}}R[i]\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\kappa_{i}\Delta^{2}T<k^{2}L\Delta^{2}T.

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{1/\Delta}R[i]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in E_{\rm nx}}R[i]+\sum_{i\in E_{\rm st}}R[i]+\sum_{i\in E_{\rm fx}}R[i]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Delta^{-1}(1+kB)+|E_{\rm st}|\cdot(kB+kL\Delta^{3}T)+k^{2}L\Delta^{2}T

\displaystyle\leq 2\Delta^{-1}(1+kB)+2Lk^{2}\Delta^{2}T,

where the last inequality follows since
|E_{\rm st}|\leq 1/\Delta
.
\square
