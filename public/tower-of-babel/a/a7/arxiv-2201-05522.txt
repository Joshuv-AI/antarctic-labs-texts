On zonal steady solutions to the 2D Euler equations on the rotating unit sphere
Marc Nualart

Abstract
The present paper studies the structure of the set of stationary solutions to the incompressible Euler equations on the rotating unit sphere that are near two basic zonal flows: the zonal Rossby-Haurwitz solution of degree 2 and the zonal rigid rotation Y 1 0 Y_{1}^{0} along the polar axis. We construct a new family of non-zonal steady solutions arbitrarily close in analytic regularity to the second degree zonal Rossby-Haurwitz stream function, for any given rotation of the sphere. This shows that any non-linear inviscid damping to a zonal flow cannot be expected for solutions near this Rossby-Haurwitz solution. On the other hand, we prove that, under suitable conditions on the rotation of the sphere, any stationary solution close enough to the rigid rotation zonal flow Y 1 0 Y_{1}^{0} must itself be zonal, witnessing some sort of rigidity inherited from the equation, the geometry of the sphere and the base flow. Nevertheless, when the conditions on the rotation of the sphere fail, the set of solutions is much richer and we are able to prove the existence of both explicit stationary and travelling wave non-zonal solutions bifurcating from Y 1 0 Y_{1}^{0} , in the same spirit as those emanating from the zonal Rossby-Haurwitz solution of degree 2.



1
Introduction

In this paper we consider the incompressible Euler equation on the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
rotating around the polar axis with angular velocity
\widetilde{\gamma}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In vorticity formulation it reads

(1)

\partial_{t}{\Omega}+U\cdot\nabla({\Omega}-2\widetilde{\gamma}\cos\theta)=0,\quad U=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi,\quad{\Delta}\Psi={\Omega},

where
U
denotes the divergence-free velocity field of the fluid tangent to the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, and
{\Omega}
and
\Psi
are its associated vorticity and stream-function, respectively. The term
-U\cdot\nabla(2\widetilde{\gamma}\cos\theta)
accounts for the Coriolis force due to the rotation of the sphere. Here, we parametrize the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
by the usual spherical coordinate system

x=\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\quad y=\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\quad z=\cos\theta,

where
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
are the colatitude and longitude, respectively. See Section
2
below for a more precise definition of the differential operators terms in (
1
) and the choice of the coordinate chart. The stream functions of stationary solutions to (
1
) satisfy

(2)

\nabla^{\perp}\Psi\cdot\nabla(\Delta\Psi-2\widetilde{\gamma}\cos\theta)=0.

In this direction, any solution
\Psi
to

(3)

{\Delta}\Psi-2\widetilde{\gamma}\cos\theta=F(\Psi),

for some
F\in C^{1}
automatically satisfies (
2
) and is thus a stationary solution to the Euler equation.

The eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
{\Delta}
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
are the spherical harmonics and are such that
{\Delta}\Psi=-\lambda_{n}\Psi
, with
\lambda_{n}=n(n+1)
being the corresponding eigenvalues (see Section
2
). In particular, we denote by
Y_{n}^{0}=Y_{n}^{0}(\theta)
the spherical harmonic that is a zonal function (i.e., a function that only depends on the colatitude
\theta
) and satisfies
{\Delta}Y_{n}^{0}=-\lambda_{n}Y_{n}^{0}
.

Moreover, we introduce the zonal Rossby-Haurwitz stream function of degree
n
, which is given by

\Psi_{n}=\beta Y_{n}^{0}+\frac{2\widetilde{\gamma}}{n(n+1)-2}\cos\theta,\quad\beta\neq 0.

Together with the choice
F(\Psi)=-n(n+1)\Psi
, it solves (
3
) and therefore it is an explicit steady solution to the Euler equation. The Rossby-Haurwitz flows on the rotating sphere are regarded as the direct analogues of the Kolmogorov flows on the torus, since they are, modulo rotation corrections, generated by the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, or spherical laplacian. Moreover, the flows produced by the Rossby-Haurwitz stream-functions are of interest from the meteorological viewpoint, since they are usually present in the atmospheres of the earth and the outer planets of the solar system, see
[
16
,
21
]
. Finally, note that zonal stream functions, of the form
\Psi=\Psi(\theta)
, solve (
2
) for all
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
, their associated velocity being
U=-\partial_{\theta}\Psi(\theta)\textbf{e}_{\varphi}
. As such,
Y_{n}^{0}
solves (
2
) for any rotation.

This paper is devoted to the study of stationary solutions that are close to the steady configurations presented above. More precisely, we will focus on two zonal base flows:

•

The rigid rotation
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}=\alpha\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}}\cos\theta
, of amplitude
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

•

The zonal Rossby-Haurwitz stream function of second degree
\Psi_{*}:=\Psi_{2}=\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}}{2}\cos\theta
.

Our choice of these two zonal flows is mainly motivated by the fact that the rigid rotation is the simplest flow motion one can have on a rotating sphere. In order to study the analogues of the Kolmogorov flow on the sphere, since the rigid rotation is itself a spherical harmonic on the first shelve of the eigenvalues, we consider the zonal spherical harmonic belonging to the second shelve of eigenvalues, thus the choice of
\Psi_{*}
.
In the sequel, taking
\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{3}}\widetilde{\gamma}
, we write the zonal Rossby-Haurwitz stream function of second degree as
\Psi_{*}=\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
, while now (
1
) becomes

(4)

\partial_{t}{\Omega}+U\cdot\nabla({\Omega}-4\gamma Y_{1}^{0})=0,\quad U=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi,\quad{\Delta}\Psi={\Omega}.

and (
3
) is now written as

(5)

{\Delta}\Psi-4\gamma Y_{1}^{0}=F(\Psi).

In this paper we investigate the structure of the set of stationary states near the rigid rotation and the zonal Rossby-Haurwitz stream function of second degree because these steady solutions may constitute possible end-state configurations for the long-time dynamics of perturbations of the rigid rotation and the zonal Rossby-Haurwitz solution. These sets of steady states close to the background zonal flows
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
and
\Psi_{*}
depend on the rescaled angular velocity
\gamma
and the amplitude parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
, respectively. Thus, our analysis keeps track of these quantities to understand up to which degree they influence the solutions.

In this direction, our first main result shows the existence of real analytic steady non-trivial (not a linear combination of spherical harmonics of eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}=6
) and non-zonal solutions to the
\gamma
-rotating Euler equations (
4
) which are arbitrarily close to the stream function
\Psi_{*}=\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
, for
(\beta,\gamma)\neq(0,0)
, in the space of real analytic functions
C^{\omega}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
endowed with the Gevrey norm

\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}^{2}:=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{|m|\leq n}\mu_{n}^{2}e^{2\lambda\mu_{n}^{1/2}}|u_{n}^{m}|^{2},

where
\lambda_{n}=n(n+1)
and
\mu_{n}=\lambda_{n}+1
. See Section
2.3
for more details on this norm.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\beta,\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}>0
, there exist
c_{\gamma,\beta}>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
there exist analytic functions
\Psi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
and
F_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{R}})
such that

{\Delta}\Psi_{\varepsilon}-4\gamma Y_{1}^{0}=F_{\varepsilon}(\Psi_{\varepsilon})

and

\|\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}-\Psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=O(\varepsilon),

with

\begin{split}\langle\Psi_{\varepsilon},Y_{6}^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}&=-\beta^{2}\frac{1}{36}\frac{45}{11\pi\sqrt{182}}c_{\gamma,\beta}\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3}),\\
\langle\Psi_{\varepsilon},Y_{4}^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}&=-\frac{1}{14}\left(\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{154\pi}(11\gamma^{2}-5\beta^{2})-\beta^{2}\frac{1}{7\pi}\frac{30\gamma^{2}}{7\gamma^{2}+15\beta^{2}}\right)c_{\gamma,\beta}\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3}).\end{split}

In particular,
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
is non-zonal and non-trivial.

The above problem is radically different when one considers the rigid rotation
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
as the base zonal flow. Indeed, the next result shows that, possibly up to a discrete set of rotations, any sufficiently smooth travelling wave solution to the Euler equation sufficiently close to
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
must be zonal.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
a=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}}
and let
\alpha,c,\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\alpha\neq 0
and

(6)

2a(\alpha+2\gamma)\neq n(n+1)(\alpha a-c),\quad\text{for all }n\geq 1.

Then, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that any travelling wave solution to the Euler Equation on the
\gamma
-rotating sphere of the form
U=U(\theta,\varphi-ct)
with associated vorticity
{\Omega}
satisfying

\|{\Omega}+2\alpha Y_{1}^{0}\|_{H^{4}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}

must be zonal, that is,
U=U(\theta)\textbf{e}_{\varphi}
and
{\Omega}={\Omega}(\theta)
.

On the other hand, we exhibit two examples for which condition (
6
) fails and the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
above is not true. Firstly, note that the condition does not hold for the wave velocity
c=0
, and amplitudes
\alpha=\gamma
, coinciding with the
\gamma
-rotation of the sphere. For this choice of parameters we have the following result, which is a direct consequence of Theorem
1.1
for
\beta=0
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, there exist
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
there exist analytic non-zonal and non-trivial steady solutions
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
to the
\gamma
-rotating Euler equations (
4
) such that

\|\gamma Y_{1}^{0}-\Psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=O(\varepsilon).

Secondly, let us remark that the Euler equations for an inviscid incompressible fluid in a non-rotating sphere in vorticity form are

(7)

\partial_{t}\overline{{\Omega}}+\overline{U}\cdot\nabla\overline{{\Omega}}=0,\quad\overline{U}=\nabla^{\perp}\overline{\Psi},\quad{\Delta}\overline{\Psi}=\overline{{\Omega}}.

Here, we denote the vorticity by
\overline{{\Omega}}
to distinguish it from the vorticity
{\Omega}
that solves the Euler equations in a rotating sphere (
1
), and similarly for the velocity
\overline{U}
and stream-function
\overline{\Psi}
. By close inspection of (
1
) and (
7
), one can see that steady state solutions
{\Omega}(\theta,\varphi)
of the Euler equations (
1
) on a sphere rotating with velocity
\widetilde{\gamma}
correspond to travelling wave solutions to the Euler equations in a sphere at rest (
7
) of the form

(8)

\overline{{\Omega}}(t,\theta,\varphi)=-2\widetilde{\gamma}\cos\theta+{\Omega}(\theta,\varphi-\widetilde{\gamma}t).

Since
2\widetilde{\gamma}\cos\theta=4\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
and
{\Delta}Y_{1}^{0}=-2Y_{1}^{0}
, we also have the following relation for the stream-functions
{\Delta}\overline{\Psi}=\overline{{\Omega}}
and
{\Delta}\Psi={\Omega}
, that is,

(9)

\overline{\Psi}(t,\theta,\varphi)=2{\gamma}Y_{1}^{0}+\Psi(\theta,\varphi-\widetilde{\gamma}t).

From this observation, we find a second example for which condition (
6
) is not true and one can obtain non-trivial non-zonal solutions. For the choice of wave velocities
c=\widetilde{\gamma}
and amplitudes
\alpha=3\gamma
, with the relation
\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{3}}\widetilde{\gamma}
, we have the following.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, there exist
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
there exist analytic non-zonal and non-trivial travelling wave solutions
\overline{\Psi}_{\varepsilon}
to the non-rotating Euler equation (
7
) such that

\|3\gamma Y_{1}^{0}-\overline{\Psi}_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=O(\varepsilon).

Indeed, from (
8
) and (
9
), choosing
\overline{\Psi}_{\varepsilon}=2{\gamma}Y_{1}^{0}+\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\varphi-\widetilde{\gamma}t)
, where
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\varphi)
is the non-zonal steady solution to the rotating Euler equations (
4
) given by Theorem
1.1
for
\beta=0
, it is clear that
\overline{{\Omega}}_{\varepsilon}={\Delta}\overline{\Psi}_{\varepsilon}
is a non-trivial (non-zonal) travelling wave solution to the Euler equations (
7
) such that
\overline{\Psi}_{\varepsilon}
is
\varepsilon
-close to
3\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
in the analytic Gevrey space
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
.

We finish our discussion by presenting a setting for which even if condition (
6
) fails, the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
may still hold if one is willing to assume further conditions on the solution. This is the purpose of the following result.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that any steady solution to the non-rotating Euler Equations (
7
) whose vorticity
\overline{{\Omega}}
satisfies

\|\overline{{\Omega}}+2\alpha Y_{1}^{0}\|_{H^{4}}\leq\varepsilon_{0},\quad\langle\overline{{\Omega}},Y_{1}^{m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=0,\text{ for }|m|=1,

must be zonal, that is,
\overline{U}=\overline{U}(\theta)\textbf{e}_{\varphi}
and
\overline{{\Omega}}=\overline{{\Omega}}(\theta)
.

1.1
Perspectives

Below we present a short literature review on properties of steady solutions to the Euler equations set in both a rotating sphere and planar domains. Afterwards, relate the results of this paper with the current state-of-the-art of the field.

1.1.1
Euler equations on a 2D flat domain

In 1907, Orr
[
37
]
discovered a mixing mechanism that damps the non-shear component of inviscid Euler solutions close to the Couette flow in a channel. This mixing mechanism, currently known as
vorticity mixing
, produces a key effects on the dynamics of the flow. The mixing of the vorticity produces
inviscid damping
, a phenomenon for which the velocity is damped. Later, this vorticity mixing was also seen to be present in rotating flat domains such as
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
or
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
.

In the past few years there has been a huge development on the theory of inviscid damping. Linear results for flows near Couette, stricly monotone, radial and Kolmogorov flows can be found in
[
2
,
15
,
25
,
28
,
43
,
44
,
46
,
47
]
. However, the complete dynamics are described by the full nonlinear problems, which are considerably much harder. See
[
4
,
3
,
23
,
31
,
17
]
for non-linear inviscid damping results for solutions near some shear and radial flows.

The study on the local structure of steady solutions has also attracted recent attention, see
[
10
,
11
,
24
,
35
]
. The domain in which the motion takes place actually plays an important role in the geometrical properties of the stationary solutions. For example, Hamel and Nadirashvili proved that in a strip where the velocity has no stagnation points, the flow must be shear, see
[
19
]
and the references therein for this and similar results in the half-plane and radial domains. These results confer the idea that certain steady solutions adapt to the geometry and symmetries of the domain they occupy. Further statements in this direction are given in
[
13
,
18
]
. Similarly, a current line of research investigates if steady solutions nearby some background stationary state inherit geometrical properties of this background state. In the case where the fluid domain is a channel, this motivates the following definition: We say that a background shear flow
u
is

•

Rigid
, if all steady solutions sufficiently near
u
are themselves shear flows.

•

Flexible
if, on the contrary, there exists steady solutions arbitrarily close to
u
that are not shear flows.

These definitions highly depend on the metric used to measure the distances. Moreover, whether the flow is rigid or flexible has quite substantial dynamical consequences. For instance, flexibility of a shear flow
u
in a certain metric space automatically rules out the possibility of non-linear inviscid damping towards a nearby shear flow for all initial perturbations arbitrarily close to the base shear flow in that space. Indeed, one could take as an initial condition the non-shear stationary solution. Results on this geometrical property are available for some basic shear flows and highlight the role of the regularity: Lin and Zeng answered the rigidity/flexibility dichotomy for the Couette flow in the periodic channel in
[
29
]
, Castro and Lear proved similar results for Couette in a periodic strip in
[
8
]
and partial answers for the Poiseuille and Kolmogorov flows were obtained by Coti Zelati, Elgindi and Widmayer in
[
14
]
.

In
[
29
]
, the authors proved that in the periodic channel steady solutions whose vorticity is sufficiently close to that of Couette in
H^{s}
with
s>3/2
must be shear. They also constructed non-shear steady solutions whose vorticity is arbitrarily close to the Couette flow vorticity in
H^{s}
, with
s<3/2
, implying that inviscid damping to a shear flow is not true in low regularity. In
[
8
]
, the authors obtained a new family of non-trivial (non-shear) and smooth travelling waves for the 2D Euler equation in a periodic strip, with the associated vorticity being arbitrarily close to Couette in
H^{s}
, with
s<3/2
, also denying the possibility of non-linear inviscid damping back to a shear flow.

In
[
14
]
the authors showed rigidity of the Poiseuille flow in the periodic channel for solutions close in vorticity in
H^{5+}
regularity and rigidity of the Kolmogorov flow on the rectangular torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}_{\delta}=[0,2\pi\delta)\times[0,2\pi)
for solution near the Kolmogorov vorticity in
H^{3+}
regularity. However, the situation for the Kolmogorv flow on the square torus was shown to be completely different: the authors constructed non-trivial steady solutions (non-shear and not in the kernel of the linearized Euler equations) to the Euler equations arbitrarily near the Kolmogorov flow in analytic regularity via a fixed point argument. This, in turn, implies that the linear inviscid damping for solutions near the Kolmogorov flow on
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
obtained in
[
43
]
cannot be extended to the non-linear level, no matter the regularity of the initial perturbation.

1.1.2
Euler equations on a rotating sphere

The Euler equations on the two dimensional sphere are used to model geophysical fluid dynamics. Some of its applications include meteorological predictions and the study of the motion of the atmosphere of Earth and other planets of the Solar system. A complete introduction to the theory of solutions to Navier-Stokes and Euler equations on the two dimensional unit sphere can be found in
[
41
]
and the references therein. See also
[
38
]
and the references therein for a discussion regarding the Navier-Stokes and Euler equations on compact Riemannian manifolds.

Just like shear flows in the flat Euclidean setting, zonal flows are basic for understanding the long time dynamics of the equations. In this direction, non-linear Lyapunov stability for a class of stationary flows was shown in
[
7
]
and non-linear structural stability of solutions belonging to the second eigenspace of the Laplace-Beltrami operator was obtained in
[
45
]
. In
[
9
]
the authors proved that finite-time averages of solutions stay close to a subspace of zonal flows, the initial data being arbitrarily far away from that subspace, further supporting the idea that zonal flows are possible end-states for general initial configurations. These results were later extended in
[
42
]
to rotationally symmetric surfaces.

More recently, Constantin and Germain studied the Euler equation on a rotating sphere in
[
12
]
. There, they obtained that any solution
\Psi
to (
3
) for some
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
must be zonal if the corresponding non-linearity
F
is such that
F^{\prime}>-6
, and they further remark that this conditions is sharp by considering the Rossby-Haurwitz solution of degree 2. The non-zonal solutions constructed in Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.3
provide another non-trivial (they are not a Rossby-Haurwitz stream function) explicit example of this sharpness.

1.1.3
Contributions of the paper and further insights

Up to our knowledge, our results are the first to show geometrical properties on stationary solutions near zonal Rossby-Haurwitz stream functions of degree 2, the analogue of the Kolmogorov flow in the planar case. Indeed, Theorem
1.2
is a spherical version of the rigidity of the Kolmogorov flow in
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}_{\delta}
, while Theorem
1.1
is in the same spirit as the flexibility of the Kolmogorov flow in
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
.

A direct consequence of this flexibility is that inviscid damping towards a zonal flow for solutions to the Euler equation on the rotating sphere whose stream function is close to the Rossby-Haurwitz solution
\Psi_{*}=\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
, for
\beta\neq 0
, is not true, independently of the regularity assumptions and of the rotation
\gamma
. It is also worth remarking here that the steady solutions obtained in Theorem
1.1
(and Corollary
1.3
) do not contradict the results in
[
9
]
mentioned above for large rotations
\gamma
because, despite being non-zonal, their time-averages (that is, themselves) are
\varepsilon
close to the zonal flows
\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
and
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
, respectively.

While Theorem
1.2
and the series of results Corollary
1.3
, Corollary
1.4
and Corollary
1.5
provide some description of the set of steady solutions to the Euler equations on a (rotating) sphere which are close to the rigid rotation generated by the stream-function
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
, the complete picture is far from being understood. Precisely, for the Euler equations on a sphere at rest, the set of solutions close to the rigid rotation
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
is very rich: On one hand, from Corollary
1.4
there are non-trivial (non-zonal) travelling wave solutions arbitrarily close to the rigid rotation, while on the other hand, from Corollary
1.5
all steady states orthogonal to
Y_{1}^{m}
for
|m|=1
and sufficiently close to the rigid rotation are zonal. This plethora of phenomena hint at an even richer long-time behaviour of general perturbations of the rigid rotation under the dynamics of the Euler equations on a non-rotating sphere.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce essential concepts for the development of the manuscript. We begin by defining the main differential geometric notions, such as the set of spherical coordinates we will use and the differential operators we will consider throughout the paper. Afterwards, we give the precise definition and properties of the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, the so-called spherical harmonics and finally we provide the definition of Sobolev and Gevrey spaces on the sphere.

2.1
Basic definitions on differential geometry

We usually parametrize the two dimensional unit sphere by the standard colatitude-longitude spherical coordinates

(\theta,\varphi)\mapsto(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta),\quad(\theta,\varphi)\in(0,\pi)\times(0,2\pi),

which covers
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
except for the half circle
\{\theta\in(0,\pi),\,\varphi=0\}
and so we complement it with the chart

(10)

(\widetilde{\theta},\widetilde{\varphi})\mapsto(-\sin\widetilde{\theta}\cos\widetilde{\varphi},-\cos\widetilde{\theta},-\sin\widetilde{\theta}\sin\widetilde{\varphi}),\quad(\widetilde{\theta},\widetilde{\varphi})\in(0,\pi)\times(0,2\pi),

which removes the equatorial half circle
\{\theta=0,\,\varphi\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2})\}
, to obtain a smooth atlas for
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
. In the sequel we mainly work with the usual colatitude-longitude parametrization and we explicitly remark when we work with the other chart. The Riemannian metric of
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
in the colatitude-longitude chart is given by

g(\theta,\varphi)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sin^{2}\theta\end{pmatrix},

from which one obtains that the vectors

\textbf{e}_{\theta}=\partial_{\theta},\quad\textbf{e}_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi},

form an orthonormal basis of the tangent space
T_{p}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
at
p\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\setminus\{N,\,S\}
, where
N
and
S
denote the North and South poles of the sphere, respectively. Given a scalar function
f:{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, the differential operators gradient and Laplace-Beltrami are given by

\begin{split}\nabla f=\partial_{\theta}f\textbf{e}_{\theta}+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}f\textbf{e}_{\varphi},\quad{\Delta}f&=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}(\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}f)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\partial^{2}_{\varphi}f.\end{split}

We further define the
-\frac{\pi}{2}
-rotation
J
in the tangent space given by
J\textbf{e}_{\theta}=-\textbf{e}_{\varphi}
and
J\textbf{e}_{\varphi}=\textbf{e}_{\theta}
, from which we set
\nabla^{\perp}f:=J\nabla f
. Similarly, the divergence and rotational operators for a vector field
\textbf{u}=u_{\theta}\textbf{e}_{\theta}+u_{\varphi}\textbf{e}_{\varphi}
are defined by

\begin{split}\text{div}(\textbf{u})&=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}(\sin\theta u_{\theta})+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}u_{\varphi},\quad\text{curl}(\textbf{u})=-\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}(\sin\theta u_{\varphi})+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}u_{\theta}.\end{split}

Additionally, the differential volume form is
\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}=\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi
. We refer the reader to
[
9
,
27
,
41
]
for a comprehensive treatment on the details of the differential geometric viewpoint of the Euler equation and
[
32
]
for the deduction of the Euler equations in vorticity formulation posed in the two dimensional unit sphere.

2.2
Spherical Harmonics

The real spherical harmonics of degree
n\geq 0
are the eigenfunctions of the negative spherical Laplace-Beltrami operator
-{\Delta}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}=n(n+1)
, see
[
1
,
34
,
41
]
. Each eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
has multiplicity
2n+1
for each
n\geq 0
and the corresponding real spherical harmonics are given by

Y_{n}^{m}=Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\varphi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}(-1)^{m}\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{2n+1}{4\pi}\dfrac{(n-m)!}{(n+m)!}}P_{n}^{|m|}(\cos\theta)\sin(|m|\varphi),&\text{if }m<0,\\[5.0pt]
\sqrt{\dfrac{2n+1}{4\pi}}P_{n}(\cos\theta),&\text{if }m=0,\\[5.0pt]
(-1)^{m}\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{2n+1}{4\pi}\dfrac{(n-m)!}{(n+m)!}}P_{n}^{m}(\cos\theta)\cos(m\varphi),&\text{if }m>0,\end{array}\right.

where
m=0,\pm 1,\dots,\pm n
. For
n\geq 0
,
P_{n}
are the Legendre polynomials defined by

P_{n}(s)=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}s^{n}}(s^{2}-1)^{n},\quad s\in(-1,1),

which are solutions to the eigenvalue problem

(11)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left((1-s^{2})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right)P_{n}(s)=-n(n+1)P_{n}(s).

For
n>0
and
0<|m|\leq n
the associated Legendre functions
P_{n}^{m}
are given by

P_{n}^{m}(s)=(-1)^{m}(1-s^{2})^{m/2}\dfrac{d^{m}}{ds^{m}}P_{n}(s).

The set of all spherical harmonics
\{Y_{n}^{m}:n\geq 0,\,|m|\leq n\}
forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
. That is, any
u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
can be written as

u=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{|m|\leq n}u_{n}^{m}Y_{n}^{m},\quad u_{n}^{m}=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}u\,Y_{n}^{m}\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{S}}^{2},

where the convergence is in the
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
sense. For each
n\geq 0
we define the linear subspace
\textbf{Y}_{n}=\text{span}\{Y_{n}^{m}:|m|\leq n\}
, any
u\in\textbf{Y}_{n}
is such that
{\Delta}u=-\lambda_{n}u
. In the sequel, we will mainly work with

Y_{1}^{0}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}}\cos\theta,\quad Y_{2}^{0}(\theta)=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}(3\cos^{2}\theta-1),\quad Y_{2}^{2}(\theta,\varphi)=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{15}{\pi}}\sin^{2}\theta\cos(2\varphi).

2.3
Function spaces on the unit sphere

Let
A=-{\Delta}+1
and
\mu_{n}:=\lambda_{n}+1
, for all
n\geq 0
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we define the inhomogeneous Sobolev space
H^{k}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
using the domain of definition of
A^{k/2}
, see
[
6
,
41
]
. More precisely, we set

H^{k}({\mathbb{S}}^{2}):=\left\{u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\,:\,u=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{|m|\leq n}u_{n}^{m}Y_{n}^{m},\;\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{|m|\leq n}\mu_{n}^{k}|u_{n}^{m}|^{2}<\infty\right\}

together with the norm

\|u\|_{H^{k}}^{2}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{|m|\leq n}\mu_{n}^{k}|u_{n}^{m}|^{2}.

We further define the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{k}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
as the completion of
C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
, the space of smooth functions with zero average on the sphere, with respect to the norm

\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}:=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{|m|\leq n}\lambda_{n}^{k}|u_{n}^{m}|^{2}.

We also introduce the analytic Gevrey class of functions
{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
, for
\lambda>0
given by the domain of
A^{k/2}e^{\lambda A^{1/2}}
. Indeed,

{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{S}}^{2}):=\left\{u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\,:\,u=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{|m|\leq n}u_{n}^{m}Y_{n}^{m},\;\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{|m|\leq n}\mu_{n}^{k}e^{2\lambda\mu_{n}^{1/2}}|u_{n}^{m}|^{2}<\infty\right\},

and for
u\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}
we consider the norm

(12)

\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}}^{2}:=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{|m|\leq n}\mu_{n}^{k}e^{2\lambda\mu_{n}^{1/2}}|u_{n}^{m}|^{2}.

Note that the space of real analytic functions is such that
C^{\omega}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})=\bigcup_{\lambda>0}{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}
, for any
k\geq 0
, see
[
26
]
. Moreover, we also have the following.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
6
]
)
.

For
\lambda\geq 0
and
k>3/2
, the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}
is a topological algebra. More precisely, if
u,v\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}
, then
uv\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}
and

(13)

\|uv\|_{{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}}\leq C_{k}\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}}\|v\|_{{\mathcal{G}}^{k/2}_{\lambda}},

with
C_{k}>0
depending only on
k
.

In the sequel, we take
k=2
and denote
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}:={\mathcal{G}}^{1}_{\lambda}
, a topological algebra thanks to the above lemma. Finally, for any
u,v\in L^{2}
we consider the usual scalar product

\langle u,v\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}uv\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}.

3
Non-zonal stationary solutions near Rossby-Haurwitz

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We begin by showing an analogous statement for stream functions belonging to
H^{2}
, see Theorem
3.1
below. Afterwards, we will use elliptic regularity theory to improve the regularity of the constructed steady solutions up to real analytic, see Theorem
3.2
below and section
3.2
. We follow the strategy presented in
[
14
]
to prove the existence of non-trivial non-shear solutions to the Euler equation arbitrarily close to the Kolmogorov flow on the square torus.

The Euler equation on a rotating sphere (
4
) for a small steady perturbation
{\omega}
around the vorticity
{\Omega}_{*}=-6\beta Y_{2}^{0}-2\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
associated to the Rossby-Haurwitz stream function
\Psi_{*}=\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
reads

\begin{split}0&=\partial_{t}{\omega}+\frac{1}{2}\left(3\beta\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}\cos\theta+\gamma\sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}}\right)\left(1+6{\Delta}^{-1}\right)\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}+u\cdot\nabla{\omega},\end{split}

from which we define the linearized operator

{\mathcal{L}}{\omega}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(3\beta\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}\cos\theta+\gamma\sqrt{\frac{3}{\pi}}\right)\left(1+6{\Delta}^{-1}\right)\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}.

A close inspection shows that the kernel of
{\mathcal{L}}
is formed by zonal flows and eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator whose eigenvalue is
-6
, that is, the subspace
\textbf{Y}_{2}
. This motivates our definition of non-trivial solutions as those that are not in the kernel of the linearized operator. Indeed,
\Psi_{\varepsilon}=\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}+\varepsilon\textbf{Y}_{2}
is already a non-zonal stationary solution
\varepsilon
close to the Rossby-Haurwitz stream function, and so we are interested in more general non-zonal solutions.

The following result shows the existence of steady non-trivial and non-zonal solutions to the rotating Euler equation on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
arbitrarily close to
\beta Y_{1}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
in
H^{2}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For all
\beta,\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}>0
, there exist
c_{\gamma,\beta}>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
there exist functions
\Psi_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
and
F_{\varepsilon}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
for which

{\Delta}\Psi_{\varepsilon}-4\gamma Y_{1}^{0}=F_{\varepsilon}(\Psi_{\varepsilon})

and

\|\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}-\Psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{2}}=O(\varepsilon),

with

\begin{split}\langle\Psi_{\varepsilon},Y_{6}^{2}\rangle&=-\beta^{2}\frac{1}{36}\frac{45}{11\pi\sqrt{182}}c_{\gamma,\beta}\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3}),\\
\langle\Psi_{\varepsilon},Y_{4}^{2}\rangle&=-\frac{1}{14}\left(\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{154\pi}(11\gamma^{2}-5\beta^{2})-\beta^{2}\frac{1}{7\pi}\frac{30\gamma^{2}}{7\gamma^{2}+15\beta^{2}}\right)c_{\gamma,\beta}\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3}).\end{split}

On the other hand, the next result asserts that the functions obtained above are smoother than just
H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
, in fact they are real analytic. Furthermore, the following result shows that these new functions
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
are arbitrarily close to
\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
in the Gevrey class of functions
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}
, for some
\lambda>0
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The solution
\Psi_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}
given by Theorem
3.1
is real analytic, that is,
\Psi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
. Moreover, there exists
\lambda>0
and
M>0
, both independent of
\varepsilon>0
such that

\|\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}-\Psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq M\varepsilon,\text{ for all }0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We show Theorem
3.1
in the following subsections. We begin in
3.1.1
by setting up the basis that will lead to a contraction argument in
3.1.2
. Thereafter, in
3.1.3
we use the explicit expression of our functions to deduce the presence of non-trivial modes. This guarantees that the associated flows are non-zonal and neither in
\textbf{Y}_{2}
.

3.1.1
Setup of the argument

The existence of a stationary state near
\Psi_{*}=\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
relies on constructing the solution
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
perturbatively from the stream function
\Psi_{*}
and is based on the fact that
\Psi_{*}
satisfies
\Delta\Psi_{*}-4\gamma Y_{1}^{0}=F_{*}(\Psi_{*})
with
F_{*}(s)=-6s
. It is then natural to make the ansatz

\Psi_{\varepsilon}=\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon\psi,\quad F_{\varepsilon}=F_{*}+\varepsilon f,

which produces a nonlinear elliptic equation for
\psi
, with
f
to be determined as well,

{\Delta}\psi+6\psi=f(\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon\psi).

Note that the operator
{\Delta}+6
is, in general, not invertible, and while this constitutes some difficulties, it also permits to introduce via
\psi
elements of the kernel
\ker{\mathcal{L}}
, for instance,
Y_{2}^{2}
, which gives the following equation to be solved

(14)

{\Delta}\psi+6\psi=f(\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon\psi+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}),

with
\psi\perp\ker({\Delta}+6)
. We choose
f
as a cubic polynomial
f=f(z)=Az+Bz^{2}+c_{\gamma,\beta}z^{3}
with coefficients
A,B\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
c_{\gamma,\beta}>0
, where
A,B
will be determined as functionals of
\psi
and
\varepsilon>0
and
c_{\gamma,\beta}
will be chosen later on. We obtain

(15)

\begin{split}{\Delta}\psi+6\psi=&A\Psi_{*}+B\Psi_{*}^{2}+c_{\gamma,\beta}\Psi_{*}^{3}\\
&+\varepsilon(\psi+Y_{2}^{2})\big(A+2B\Psi_{*}+3c_{\gamma,\beta}\Psi_{*}^{2}\big)\\
&+R(B,c_{\gamma,\beta},\psi,\varepsilon),\end{split}

where

R(B,c_{\gamma,\beta},\psi,\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{2}(\psi+Y_{2}^{2})^{2}\big(B+3c_{\gamma,\beta}\Psi_{*}\big)+\varepsilon^{3}c_{\gamma,\beta}(\psi+Y_{2}^{2})^{3}.

In order to remark the dependence of the polynomial
f
on the coefficients
A,\,B
and
c_{\gamma,\beta}
, we denote
f=f(A,B,c_{\gamma,\beta};z)
. A necessary condition to have solutions to the semilinear elliptic problem (
14
) is that the right hand side of (
15
) must be orthogonal to the kernel
\ker({\Delta}+6)=\textbf{Y}_{2}
, so
f(A,B,c_{\gamma,\beta};\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi)
must satisfy five orthogonality conditions.

We can reduce the number of orthogonality conditions by exploiting the symmetries of the spherical harmonics and the polynomial nature of the nonlinearity
f
. Hence, if we assume that
\psi
is an even function in
\varphi
, that is,
\langle\psi,Y_{n}^{-|m|}\rangle=0
, for all
n\geq 1
and all
0<|m|\leq n
, it is then straightforward to see that
f(A,B,c_{\gamma,\beta};\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi)
is also even in
\varphi
for all
A,B,c_{\gamma,\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, so that the two orthogonality conditions regarding
Y_{2}^{-2}
and
Y_{2}^{-1}
are automatically satisfied.

If we also further assume that
\langle\psi,Y_{n}^{2k+1}\rangle=0
, for all
n\geq 0
and all
k\geq 0
such that
2k+1\leq n
, this in turn implies that
f(A,B,c_{\gamma,\beta};\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi)
is orthogonal to
Y_{2}^{1}
. Indeed, any multiplication of spherical harmonics of the form
Y_{n_{1}}^{2m_{1}}Y_{n_{2}}^{2m_{2}}
, for
0\leq 2m_{i}\leq n_{i}
cannot generate spherical harmonics of the form
Y_{n_{3}}^{2m_{3}+1}
, for any
0\leq 2m_{3}+1\leq n_{3}
. The other two orthogonality conditions which remain to be considered are

(16)

\langle f(A,B,c_{\gamma,\beta};\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi),Y_{2}^{0}\rangle=\langle f(A,B,c_{\gamma,\beta};\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi),Y_{2}^{2}\rangle=0.

These equations are restrictions for the coefficients
A=A(\psi;\varepsilon)
and
B=B(\psi;\varepsilon)
. Using the given expression for
f
, we find that

(17)

\begin{split}0=\beta A(\psi;\varepsilon)&+\frac{7\gamma^{2}+5\beta^{2}}{7\sqrt{5\pi}}B(\psi;\varepsilon)+3\beta\frac{11\gamma^{2}+5\beta^{2}}{28\pi}c_{\gamma,\beta}\\
&+\varepsilon\left\langle(2B(\psi;\varepsilon)\Psi_{*}+3c_{\gamma,\beta}\Psi_{*}^{2})\psi,Y_{2}^{0}\right\rangle+\langle R,Y_{2}^{0}\rangle,\\
0=A(\psi;\varepsilon)&-\left(\beta\frac{2}{7}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}-2\langle\Psi_{*}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)B(\psi;\varepsilon)+3\left(\frac{3\gamma^{2}+5\beta^{2}}{28\pi}+\langle\Psi_{*}^{2}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)c_{\gamma,\beta}\\
&+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\langle R,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle.\end{split}

From here we can easily obtain

(18)

\begin{split}0&=\left(\frac{7\gamma^{2}+15\beta^{2}}{35}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}-2\beta\langle\Psi_{*}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)B(\psi;\varepsilon)+\beta\left(\frac{6\gamma^{2}}{7\pi}-3\langle\Psi_{*}^{2}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)c_{\gamma,\beta}\\
&+\varepsilon\left\langle(2B(\psi;\varepsilon)\Psi_{*}+3c_{\gamma,\beta}\Psi_{*}^{2})\psi,Y_{2}^{0}\right\rangle+\langle R,Y_{2}^{0}\rangle-\beta\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\langle R,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle.\end{split}

Together with the above assumptions on the orthogonality of
\psi
with certain families of spherical harmonics and in order to be able to solve for
B(\psi;\varepsilon)
in (
18
), we define the function space
X
we will work in by

(19)

\begin{split}X&:=\left\{\psi\in H^{2}:\psi\perp Y_{2}^{m},Y_{n}^{-|k|},Y_{n}^{2k-1},\quad|m|\leq 2,\,n>0,\,k>0,\right.\\
&\qquad\left.|\langle\psi,\Psi_{*}Y_{2}^{2}\rangle|\leq\frac{1}{8}\frac{\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2}}{\sqrt{5\pi}},\quad|\langle\psi,\Psi_{*}^{2}Y_{2}^{2}\rangle|\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2}}{3},\quad\|\psi\|_{H^{2}}\leq 150(1+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}\right\}.\end{split}

The following lemma determines
A(\psi;\varepsilon)
and
B(\psi;\varepsilon)
and state some of their properties.

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that for
\psi\in X
and for
0\leq\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{1}
the relations (
17
) recursively define real sequences
(a_{j}(\psi))_{j\geq 0}
and
(b_{j}(\psi))_{j\geq 0}
both depending on
c_{\gamma,\beta}
and such that

(20)

A(\psi;\varepsilon):=\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{j}(\psi)\varepsilon^{j},\qquad B(\psi;\varepsilon):=\sum_{j\geq 0}b_{j}(\psi)\varepsilon^{j}

are well-defined, uniformly bounded for
\psi\in X
and satisfy (
17
). Moreover, the maps

\psi\mapsto a_{j}(\psi),\qquad\psi\mapsto b_{j}(\psi),\quad j\geq 0,

are Lipschitz continuous on
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
with constants
L_{j}\leq L^{j}
, for some
L>0
, and the maps

\psi\mapsto a_{0}(\psi),\qquad\psi\mapsto b_{j}(\psi)

are Lipschitz continuous on
\dot{H}^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
, with

\begin{split}|a_{0}(\psi_{1})-a_{0}(\psi_{2})|&<\frac{1}{2}(1+|\beta|)(\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})c_{\gamma,\beta}\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}},\\
|b_{0}(\psi_{1})-b_{0}(\psi_{2})|&<\frac{2}{25}|\beta|c_{\gamma,\beta}\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}.\end{split}

Remark 3.4
.

The Lipschitz continuity of the coefficients
a_{0}(\psi)
and
b_{0}(\psi)
for
\psi\in X\subset H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
is key for proving the contraction argument for the subspace
X
that will guarantee the existence and uniqueness of solution.

Proof.

From (
18
) one can write,

(21)

\begin{split}\left(\frac{7\gamma^{2}+15\beta^{2}}{35}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}-2\beta\langle\Psi_{*}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)B(\psi;\varepsilon)&=-\beta\left(\frac{6\gamma^{2}}{7\pi}-3\langle\Psi_{*}^{2}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)c_{\gamma,\beta}\\
&-\varepsilon\left\langle(2B(\psi;\varepsilon)\Psi_{*}+3c_{\gamma,\beta}\Psi_{*}^{2})\psi,Y_{2}^{0}\right\rangle\\
&-\langle R,Y_{2}^{0}\rangle+\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\langle R,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle.\end{split}

Expanding here
B(\psi,\varepsilon)
in series as
B(\psi;\varepsilon):=\sum_{j\geq 0}b_{j}(\psi)\varepsilon^{j}
and comparing coefficients in
\varepsilon
one shows that
b_{j}(\psi)
can be inductively defined from a linear combination of
b_{j-2}(\psi)
and
b_{j-1}(\psi)
. The coefficients of this linear combination are
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
inner products of
\psi
against spherical harmonics. These inner products are uniformly bounded because
\psi\in X
, see (
19
). Therefore, one can show by induction that there exists
M
sufficiently large such that
|b_{j}(\psi)|\leq M^{j}
and the series expansion for
B(\psi,\varepsilon)
converges for
0\leq\varepsilon<M^{-1}
. The same holds for
A(\psi,\varepsilon):=\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{j}(\psi)\varepsilon^{j}
because we can use

A(\psi;\varepsilon)=\left(\beta\frac{2}{7}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}-2\langle\Psi_{*}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)B(\psi;\varepsilon)-3\left(\frac{3\gamma^{2}+5\beta^{2}}{28\pi}+\langle\Psi_{*}^{2}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)c_{\gamma,\beta}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\langle R,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle.

to find
a_{j}(\psi),\,j\geq 0
, directly from
B(\psi,\varepsilon)
, with

(22)

a_{0}(\psi)=\left(\beta\frac{2}{7}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}-2\langle\Psi_{*}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)b_{0}(\psi)-3\left(\frac{3\gamma^{2}+5\beta^{2}}{28\pi}+\langle\Psi_{*}^{2}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)c_{\gamma,\beta}.

The maps
\psi\mapsto a_{j}(\psi)
and
\psi\mapsto b_{j}(\psi)
for
j\geq 0
are Lipschitz. For
j\geq 1
it follows from the recursive construction of the coefficients, while for
j=0
we observe that

(23)

b_{0}(\psi)=B(\psi;0)=-\beta c_{\gamma,\beta}\frac{\left(\frac{6\gamma^{2}}{7\pi}-3\langle\Psi_{*}^{2}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle\right)}{\frac{7\gamma^{2}+15\beta^{2}}{35}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}-2\beta\langle\Psi_{*}\psi,Y_{2}^{2}\rangle}=:-\beta c_{\gamma,\beta}\frac{n(\psi)}{d(\psi)},

which is well-defined because
\psi\in X
implies
d(\psi)\neq 0
, see (
19
). We compute

\Psi_{*}Y_{2}^{2}=(\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0})Y_{2}^{2}=\beta\frac{1}{14}\sqrt{\frac{15}{\pi}}Y_{4}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{7\pi}}Y_{3}^{2}-\beta\frac{1}{7}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}Y_{2}^{2}

and

\begin{split}\Psi_{*}^{2}Y_{2}^{2}&=(\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0})^{2}Y_{2}^{2}\\
&=\beta^{2}\frac{15}{11\pi\sqrt{182}}Y_{6}^{2}+\beta\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{15}{77}}Y_{5}^{2}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{154\pi}(11\gamma^{2}-5\beta^{2})Y_{4}^{2}+\frac{3\gamma^{2}+5\beta^{2}}{28\pi}Y_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Recalling that
\langle Y_{2}^{m},\psi\rangle=0
and
\|Y_{n}^{m}\|_{\dot{H}^{-2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=\frac{1}{n(n+1)}
, we have

\begin{split}|n(\psi_{1})-n(\psi_{2})|&\leq 3\left(\beta^{2}\frac{15}{11\pi\sqrt{182}}\frac{1}{42}+|\beta|\frac{|\gamma|}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{15}{77}}\frac{1}{30}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{154\pi}(11\gamma^{2}+5\beta^{2})\frac{1}{20}\right)\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}\\
&<\frac{3}{250}(\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2})\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}},\end{split}

and

\begin{split}|d(\psi_{1})-d(\psi_{2})|&\leq 2|\beta|\left(|\beta|\frac{1}{14}\sqrt{\frac{15}{\pi}}\frac{1}{20}+\frac{|\gamma|}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{7\pi}}\frac{1}{12}\right)\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}\\
&<\frac{6}{125}(\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2})\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}.\end{split}

Then,

\begin{split}|b_{0}(\psi_{1})-b_{0}(\psi_{2})|&=|\beta|c_{\gamma,\beta}\left|\frac{n(\psi_{1})d(\psi_{2})-n(\psi_{2})d(\psi_{1})}{d(\psi_{1})d(\psi_{2})}\right|\\
&\leq|\beta|c_{\gamma,\beta}\left|\frac{n(\psi_{1})-n(\psi_{2})}{d(\psi_{1})}\right|+|\beta|c_{\gamma,\beta}\left|\frac{n(\psi_{2})\left(d(\psi_{2})-d(\psi_{1})\right)}{d(\psi_{1})d(\psi_{2})}\right|,\end{split}

and we deduce that the
\dot{H}^{2}
(and also the
H^{2}
) Lipschitz constant of
b_{0}
is bounded by

\begin{split}|\beta|&c_{\gamma,\beta}\frac{\left(\frac{7\gamma^{2}+15\beta^{2}}{35}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2}}{\sqrt{5\pi}}\right)\frac{3}{250}(\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2})+\left(\frac{6\gamma^{2}}{7\pi}+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2}\right)\frac{6}{125}(\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2})}{\left(\frac{7\gamma^{2}+15\beta^{2}}{35}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2}}{\sqrt{5\pi}}\right)^{2}}<\frac{2}{25}|\beta|c_{\gamma,\beta}.\end{split}

For the Lipschitz constant of
a_{0}
we have

\begin{split}|a_{0}(\psi_{1})-a_{0}(\psi_{2})|&\leq\left(\frac{2}{7}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}|\beta|+2|\langle\Psi_{*}\psi_{1},Y_{2}^{2}\rangle|\right)|b_{0}(\psi_{1})-b_{0}(\psi_{2})|\\
&+2|\langle\Psi_{*}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}),Y_{2}^{2}\rangle||b_{0}(\psi_{2})|+3c_{\gamma,\beta}|\langle\Psi_{*}^{2}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}),Y_{2}^{2}\rangle|,\end{split}

thanks to (
22
). From (
23
) we can bound

|b_{0}(\psi)|\leq\frac{\frac{6\gamma^{2}}{7\pi}+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2}}{\frac{7\gamma^{2}+15\beta^{2}}{35}\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2}}{\sqrt{5\pi}}}|\beta|c_{\gamma,\beta}<8|\beta|c_{\gamma,\beta},

and we also have

\begin{split}2|\langle\Psi_{*}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}),Y_{2}^{2}\rangle|&\leq\frac{4}{125}(|\beta|+|\gamma|)\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}},\\

3.1.2
Contraction Mapping

The solutions to (
15

X
. Therefore, we define

K_{\varepsilon}:X\rightarrow H^{2},\quad\psi\mapsto K_{\varepsilon}(\psi),

where
K_{\varepsilon}(\psi)
solves
({\Delta}+6)K_{\varepsilon}=f(A(\psi;\varepsilon),B(\psi;\varepsilon),c_{\gamma,\beta};\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi)
in the orthogonal complement of
\textbf{Y}_{2}
, where
A(\psi;\varepsilon)
and
B(\psi;\varepsilon)
are defined as above, guaranteeing that the necessary orthogonality conditions are satisfied.

Proposition 3.6
.

K_{\varepsilon}
defines a contraction on
(X,\|\cdot\|_{H^{2}})
, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough.

Proof.

Let
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
for which by Lemma
3.3
the coefficients
A(\psi;\varepsilon)
and
B(\psi;\varepsilon)
are well-defined. We first show that
K_{\varepsilon}
maps
X
into itself, see (
19
) for the definition of
X
. By construction, since
\psi
is orthogonal to
Y_{n}^{-|k|}
and
Y_{n}^{2k-1}
for all
n,k>0
and
f
is a cubic polynomial, it is clear that
K_{\varepsilon}(\psi)
will be orthogonal to
Y_{n}^{-|k|}
and
Y_{n}^{2k-1}
for all
n,k>0
, too. Moreover, it is straightforward to see that

|\langle K_{\varepsilon}(\psi),\Psi_{*}Y_{2}^{2}\rangle|+|\langle K_{\varepsilon}(\psi),\Psi_{*}^{2}Y_{2}^{2}\rangle|\lesssim\varepsilon,

since there are no non-zonal spherical harmonics at order 0 in
\varepsilon
in the expansion of the nonlinearity
f(A(\psi;\varepsilon),B(\psi;\varepsilon),c_{\gamma,\beta};\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi)
in orders of
\varepsilon
. Now, since
\psi\in X
we have
\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\psi\|_{H^{2}}\leq 150(1+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}
. Together with the bounds (
24
) and
\|\Psi_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq(|\beta|+|\gamma|)
, one can easily prove that

\begin{split}|f(A(\psi;\varepsilon),&B(\psi;\varepsilon),c_{\gamma,\beta};\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi)|\\
&\leq|A(\psi;\varepsilon)|\|\Psi_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}+|B(\psi;\varepsilon)|\|\Psi_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+|c_{\gamma,\beta}|\|\Psi_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{3}+O(\varepsilon)\\
&\leq 5(1+|\beta|)(\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})(|\beta|+|\gamma|)c_{\gamma,\beta}+8(1+|\beta|)(|\beta|+|\gamma|)^{2}c_{\gamma,\beta}\\
&\quad+(|\beta|+|\gamma|)^{3}c_{\gamma,\beta}+O(\varepsilon)\\
&\leq 17(1+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}+O(\varepsilon),\end{split}

which in turn gives

\|f(A(\psi;\varepsilon),B(\psi;\varepsilon);\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi)\|_{L^{2}}\leq 65(1+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2},

for
\varepsilon>0
small enough. For

\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}:\psi\mapsto f(A(\psi;\varepsilon),B(\psi;\varepsilon),c_{\gamma,\beta};\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi).

we have that
K_{\varepsilon}(\psi)=({\Delta}+6)^{-1}\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}(\psi)
and since
\langle\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}(\psi),Y_{2}^{m}\rangle=0
for all
0\leq|m|\leq 2
,

\|K_{\varepsilon}(\psi)\|_{H^{2}}\leq\frac{13}{6}\|\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}(\psi)\|_{L^{2}}\leq 150(1+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}.

Thus, for
\varepsilon
small enough we conclude that
K_{\varepsilon}(X)\subset X
. We finish the proof of the proposition by showing that
K_{\varepsilon}
is indeed a contraction mapping. For this, let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in X
and define
G_{j}=\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\psi_{j}
for
j=1,\,2
. Observe that

\begin{split}\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}(\psi_{1})-\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}(\psi_{2})&=f(A(\psi_{1};\varepsilon),B(\psi_{1};\varepsilon),c_{\gamma,\beta};G_{1})-f(A(\psi_{2};\varepsilon),B(\psi_{2};\varepsilon),c_{\gamma,\beta};G_{2})\\
&=(A(\psi_{1};\varepsilon)-A(\psi_{2};\varepsilon))\Psi_{*}+(B(\psi_{1};\varepsilon)-B(\psi_{2};\varepsilon))\Psi_{*}^{2}+O(\varepsilon)\\
&=(a_{0}(\psi_{1})-a_{0}(\psi_{2}))\Psi_{*}+(b_{0}(\psi_{1})-b_{0}(\psi_{2}))\Psi_{*}^{2}+O(\varepsilon)\end{split}

Thus, up to terms of order
\varepsilon
we use the Lipschitz constants for
a_{0}(\psi)
and
b_{0}(\psi)
in
\dot{H}^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
previously found and we bound

\begin{split}\|(a_{0}(\psi_{1})&-a_{0}(\psi_{2}))\Psi_{*}+(b_{0}(\psi_{1})-b_{0}(\psi_{2}))\Psi_{*}^{2}\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+|\beta|)(\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})\|\Psi_{*}\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{2}{25}|\beta|\|\Psi_{*}^{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right)c_{\gamma,\beta}\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}.\end{split}

Note that
\|\Psi_{*}\|_{L^{2}}=|\beta|+|\gamma|
and
\|\Psi_{*}^{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{5}{4}(\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})
. Then,

\begin{split}\|(a_{0}(\psi_{1})&-a_{0}(\psi_{2}))\Psi_{*}+(b_{0}(\psi_{1})-b_{0}(\psi_{2}))\Psi_{*}^{2}\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+|\beta|)(\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})(|\beta|+|\gamma|)+\frac{2}{25}|\beta|\frac{5}{4}(\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})\right)c_{\gamma,\beta}\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}\\
&<(1+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}c_{\gamma,\beta}\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}.\end{split}

Now, take
c_{\gamma,\beta}:=\frac{1}{2}(1+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})^{-2}<\frac{1}{2}
from which we deduce that

\|K_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{1})-K_{\varepsilon}(\psi_{2})\|_{H^{2}}\leq\|\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}(\psi_{1})-\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}(\psi_{2})\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+O(\varepsilon)\right)\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{H^{2}},

hence obtaining a contraction for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.
∎

3.2
Analytic Regularity

To show Theorem
3.2
, we will demonstrate that the solution
\psi_{\varepsilon}
constructed in Theorem
3.1
is a real analytic function whose
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}
norm is uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
for some
\lambda>0
. This is the statement of Proposition
3.10
below. Since the stream function
\Psi_{\varepsilon}=\Psi_{*}+\varepsilon\psi_{\varepsilon}
, this will directly yield that

\|\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}-\Psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq M\varepsilon.

Before Proposition
3.10
, we recall the operator
A=-{\Delta}+1
and we first show a more generic result concerning analytic regularity and
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}
bounds for solutions to semilinear elliptic equations with analytic coefficients on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

For
0\leq n\leq N
let
a_{n}\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
be analytic functions such that

(30)

\|a_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq C_{1}e^{C_{2}\lambda},\quad 0\leq n\leq N,

for some
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and for all
\lambda\geq 0
. Let
u\in H^{2}
solve the semilinear partial differential equation

(31)

Au=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}u^{n}.

Then,
u\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
and there exists
\lambda_{*}>0
depending only on
\|u\|_{H^{2}},\,N,\,C_{1},\,C_{2}
such that

\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq 4\|u\|_{H^{2}},

for all
0\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{*}
.

Proof.

We begin with the analyticity of
u
, for which we first prove that
u\in C^{\infty}
, it is smooth. Let
k\geq 2
and let
C_{k}
be the algebra constant of
H^{k}
. Then,

\|u\|_{H^{k+2}}=\|Au\|_{H^{k}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|a_{n}u^{n}\|_{H^{k}}\leq N\left(\max_{n=0,...,N}\|a_{n}\|_{H^{k}}\right)\left(1+(C_{k}\|u\|_{H^{k}})^{N}\right).

Since
u\in H^{2}
, the usual bootstrap argument derived from the above inequality yields that
u\in H^{k}
, for all
k\geq 0
, from which we deduce that
u\in C^{\infty}
, it is smooth.

Now, for any point
p\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
distinct from the north and south poles, we find that
u
is smooth and solves

-\left(\partial_{\theta\theta}+\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\partial_{\varphi\varphi}\right)u(\theta,\varphi)+u(\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}(\theta,\varphi)u^{n}(\theta,\varphi).

in local coordinates
(\theta,\phi)
in a small enough neighbourhood of
p\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
such that it does not intersect the north and south poles. Together with (
30
), the above equation shows that
u
is a smooth solution to a semilinear elliptic partial differential equation with analytic coefficients in an open neighbourhood of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for which classic results yield that the solution is in fact real analytic in that neighbourhood (see
[
5
,
20
]
for modern proofs). For the poles, we use the coordinate system (
10
) to remove the artificial singularities appearing in the expression of the Laplacian of
u
in local coordinates. Since
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
is compact, a classical partition of unity and covering argument shows that
u
is real analytic in the whole of
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
and thus
\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}<\infty
, for all
0\leq\lambda<\lambda_{1}
, for some
\lambda_{1}>0
. We now find
\varepsilon
-independent estimates on
\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}
. In particular, from the Gevrey norm definition (
12
), we have that

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{d\lambda}\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}^{2}=\left\langle A^{1/2}Ae^{\lambda A^{1/2}}u,Ae^{\lambda A^{1/2}}u\right\rangle\leq\|A^{1/2}Ae^{\lambda A^{1/2}}u\|_{L^{2}}\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}},

from which we deduce that

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq\|A^{1/2}Ae^{\lambda A^{1/2}}u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|Ae^{\lambda A^{1/2}}Au\|_{L^{2}}=\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}u^{n}\right\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|a_{n}u^{n}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}.

Now, let
C>1
be the algebra constant of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}
. Then, for all
0\leq n\leq N
we have that

\|a_{n}u^{n}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq\|a_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}(C\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}})^{n}\leq C^{N}C_{1}e^{C_{2}\lambda}(1+\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}^{N}),

where we have used the uniform bounds in (
30
). Therefore,

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq NC^{N}C_{1}e^{C_{2}\lambda}(1+\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}^{N}),

multiplying both sides by
\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}^{N-1}
and using
\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}^{N-1}\leq 1+\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}^{N}
gives

\frac{d}{d\lambda}(1+\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}^{N})\leq N^{2}C^{N}C_{1}e^{C_{2}\lambda}(1+\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}^{N})^{2}.

Solving the differential inequality and recalling that
\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{0}}=\|u\|_{H^{2}}
, we obtain

\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq\left(\frac{\|u\|_{H^{2}}^{N}+(1+\|u\|_{H^{2}}^{N})N^{2}C^{N}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\left(e^{C_{2}\lambda}-1\right)}{1-(1+\|u\|_{H^{2}}^{N})N^{2}C^{N}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\left(e^{C_{2}\lambda}-1\right)}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}.

Finally, let
\lambda_{*}>0
be such that
(1+\|u\|_{H^{2}}^{N})N^{2}C^{N}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\left(e^{C_{2}\lambda_{*}}-1\right)=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},\|u\|_{H^{2}}^{N}\right\}
, so that

\|u\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq 4\|u\|_{H^{2}},

for all
0\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{*}
.
∎

With the above Lemma at hand, we are now able to prove the following result and finish the section.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
small enough and let
\psi_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}
be the fixed point of
K_{\varepsilon}
. Then,
\psi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\omega}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
and there exists
\lambda>0
independent of
\varepsilon>0
such that

\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq 400(1+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
as in the statement of Theorem
3.1
. Then,
\psi_{\varepsilon}
solves

{\Delta}\psi_{\varepsilon}+6\psi_{\varepsilon}=f(\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0}+\varepsilon\psi_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon Y_{2}^{2}),

where
f(s)=As+Bs^{2}+c_{\gamma,\beta}s^{3}
, with
A=A(\psi_{\varepsilon};\varepsilon)
and
B=B(\psi_{\varepsilon};\varepsilon)
being fixed coefficients, uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon>0
for
\varepsilon
small enough, by virtue of Lemma
3.5
. Writing
f
as a polynomial in
\psi_{\varepsilon}
one obtains uniformly bounded
c_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for
0\leq i,j\leq 3
such that

{\Delta}\psi_{\varepsilon}+6\psi_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{n=0}^{3}\left(\sum_{i+j=3-n}c_{i,j}(\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0})^{i}\varepsilon^{j}(Y_{2}^{2})^{j}\right)\varepsilon^{n}\psi_{\varepsilon}^{n},

from which we deduce that

A\psi_{\varepsilon}=7\psi_{\varepsilon}-\sum_{n=0}^{3}\left(\sum_{i+j=3-n}c_{i,j}(\beta Y_{2}^{0}+\gamma Y_{1}^{0})^{i}\varepsilon^{j}(Y_{2}^{2})^{j}\right)\varepsilon^{n}\psi_{\varepsilon}^{n}=:\sum_{n=0}^{3}a_{n}u^{n}.

Now, each
a_{n}
is a finite combination of spherical harmonics, which renders the analyticity of the coefficients. Moreover, for all
m\geq 0
and
0\leq l\leq m
, one has

\|Y_{m}^{l}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}=\|Ae^{\lambda A^{1/2}}Y_{m}^{l}\|_{L^{2}}=(m^{2}+m+1)e^{\lambda(m^{2}+m+1)^{1/2}},

which yields

\|a_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq C_{1}e^{C_{2}\lambda},\quad 0\leq n\leq 3,

for some
C_{1}>0
and
C_{2}>0
large enough. Consequently, we can apply Lemma
3.9
to obtain the analyticity of
\psi_{\varepsilon}
and the Gevrey bound
\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq 4\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{2}}
, for all
0\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{*}
. Since
\psi_{\varepsilon}\in X
, we conclude that
\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}}\leq 400(1+\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})^{2}
, for all
0\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{*}
. The proof is finished.
∎

4
Stationary Structures near rigid rotation

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.2
, which concerns the rigidity of the base zonal flow
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
. Afterwards, we discuss the effectiveness of condition (
6
) to geometrically describe the set of solutions near
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
.

4.1
Rigidity

The idea of the proof is to obtain a coercive estimate for the linearised operator related to the Euler equation on the rotating sphere about
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
and to simultaneously control the non-linear term, obtaining a contradiction if the associated vorticity is both non-zonal and sufficiently close to the rigid rotation vorticity
-2\alpha Y_{1}^{0}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

A general longitudinal travelling wave solution
U(\theta,\varphi,t)
to the 2D Euler equation on the sphere is of the form

U(\theta,\varphi,t)=U_{\theta}(\theta,\varphi-ct)\textbf{e}_{\theta}+U_{\varphi}(\theta,\varphi-ct)\textbf{e}_{\varphi},

for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and its associated vorticity is given by

{\Omega}(\theta,\varphi-ct)=-\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}(\sin\theta U_{\varphi}(\theta,\varphi-ct))+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}U_{\theta}(\theta,\varphi-ct).

We begin by setting

(32)

{\Omega}=-2\alpha Y_{1}^{0}+\widetilde{{\omega}},\quad\widetilde{{\omega}}={\omega}+\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0},\quad\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\widetilde{{\omega}}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi.

Note that

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}{\Omega}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(-\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}(\sin\theta U_{\varphi})+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}U_{\theta}\right)\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=0,

and further observe
\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\omega}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=0
. Hence, since
{\Omega}
is spherically average free we define
\Psi:={\Delta}^{-1}{\Omega}
, with
\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\Psi\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}=0
. We also deduce that both
\widetilde{\omega}
and
{\omega}
are spherically average free, so that we can further define

\Psi=\alpha Y_{1}^{0}+\widetilde{\psi},\quad\widetilde{\psi}=\psi+\widetilde{\psi}_{0},\quad\widetilde{\psi}_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\widetilde{\psi}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi,

where
\widetilde{\psi}={\Delta}^{-1}\widetilde{{\omega}}
and
\psi={\Delta}^{-1}{\omega}
are such that
\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\widetilde{\psi}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}=0
and
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=0
. Additionally, we set
\widetilde{u}=u+\widetilde{u}_{0}
, where
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
and
\widetilde{u}_{0}=\nabla^{\perp}\widetilde{\psi}_{0}
, respectively.

In particular, all functions we are considering are average free on the sphere, so that the
\|\cdot\|_{H^{k}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{H}^{k}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}
norms are equivalent. Moreover, we note that the smallness assumption of Theorem
1.2
now reads

(33)

\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{H^{4}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}

Writing the Euler equation on the rotating sphere in vorticity form we obtain

\begin{split}0&=\partial_{t}{\Omega}+U\cdot\nabla({\Omega}-4\gamma Y_{1}^{0})\\
&=(\alpha a-c)\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}+a(2\alpha+4\gamma)\partial_{\varphi}\psi+u\cdot\nabla{\omega}+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}\psi\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}-\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{\psi}_{0}\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}.\\
\end{split}

Let us further define the linear operator
{\mathcal{L}}{\omega}:=(a\alpha-c)\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}+a(2\alpha+4\gamma)\partial_{\varphi}\psi
. Inspecting
{\mathcal{L}}{\omega}
in spherical harmonics one sees that the choice of
c
and
\gamma
in (
6
) ensures the existence of some constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(c,\gamma)>0
such that

\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{1}\|{\mathcal{L}}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}.

On the other hand, the Sobolev embedding yield

\|u\cdot\nabla{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}=\|\nabla^{\perp}\psi\cdot\nabla{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{\dot{H}^{3}}\|{\omega}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}=\|{\omega}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2},

while the interpolating inequality between Sobolev spaces and Poincaré inequality provides

\|{\omega}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\lesssim\|{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\|{\omega}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}\lesssim\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\|{\omega}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}.

Next, we show that
\left\|\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{\dot{H}^{4}}
. Indeed, for
\theta\in\left(\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)
we have that
\left|\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\right|\lesssim 1
and using the definition of
\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}
and the Sobolev embedding we can easily estimate

\left|\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}\right|\lesssim\|\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\nabla\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{\dot{H}^{3}}.

Similarly, for
\theta\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\cup\left(\frac{3\pi}{4},\pi\right]
we have that
\left|\frac{1}{\cos\theta}\right|\lesssim 1
and we bound

\begin{split}\left|\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}\right|\lesssim\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\right|&=\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}({\Delta}\widetilde{{\omega}}-\partial^{2}_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}})\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\right|\\
&\leq\|{\Delta}\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{C^{2}}\\
&\lesssim\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{\dot{H}^{4}}\end{split}

Therefore, we easily estimate

\left\|\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}\psi\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\partial_{\varphi}\psi\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{\dot{H}^{4}}\|\partial_{\varphi}\psi\|_{L^{2}},

and in the same manner we also bound

\left\|\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{\psi}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|\widetilde{\psi}\|_{\dot{H}^{4}}\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}=\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}.

Now, we have that

\begin{split}\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|{\mathcal{L}}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}&\leq\|u\cdot\nabla{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}\psi\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{{\omega}}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{\psi}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\lesssim\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\|{\omega}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}+\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{\dot{H}^{4}}\|\partial_{\varphi}\psi\|_{L^{2}}+\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}.\end{split}

We further observe that
\|{\omega}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}\leq 2\|\widetilde{\omega}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}
and
\|\partial_{\varphi}\psi\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}
. Therefore, there exists a constant
C>1
such that

\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\|\widetilde{{\omega}}\|_{\dot{H}^{4}}.

Choosing
\varepsilon_{0}=\frac{1}{2C}
shows that the above inequality is satisfied only if
\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}=0
. Hence,
{\Omega}
is a zonal function and the associated velocity field
U
is a zonal flow, namely
U=U(\theta)\textbf{e}_{\varphi}
.
∎

We finish this subsection by proving Corollary
1.5
.

Proof of Corollary
1.5
.

The result follows swiftly from the proof of Theorem
1.2
above. In the setting for which there is no rotation and the solution is assumed to be steady (which corresponds to
\gamma=0
and
c=0
), condition (
6
) fails for
n=1
. Still, in this case the linear operator
{\mathcal{L}}{\omega}:=a\alpha(1+2{\Delta}^{-1})\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}
admits the coercive estimate

\|\partial_{\varphi}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{3}a\alpha\|{\mathcal{L}}{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}

as long as
{\omega}
(equivalently
{\Omega}
as defined in (
32
)) is orthogonal to
Y_{1}^{m}
, for
|m|=1
. With this estimate at hand, one can replicate the proof of Theorem
1.2
to obtain the desired result.
∎

4.2
On the sharpness of Theorem
1.2

There exist pairs
(c,\alpha)
for which relation (
6
) does not hold. For example,
c=0
and
\alpha=\gamma
is one of them. For this choice, the linearised operator acting on the vorticity arising from the Euler equations on the rotating sphere around the flow given by the stream-function
\alpha Y_{1}^{0}=\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
has a non-trivial kernel formed by zonal flows and eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator of eigenvalue
-6
. The steady non-trivial and non-zonal solutions to the Euler equations (
4
) arbitrarily close to
\gamma Y_{1}^{0}
obtained in Corollary
1.3
are an easy consequence of Theorem
1.1
for
\beta=0
and provide an example for which the spectral condition (
6
) fails and the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
does not hold.

The pair
c=\eta
and
\alpha=3\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{3}}\eta
, for any
\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
gives a choice of values for which (
6
) fails for the Euler equations on a non-rotating sphere, that is,
\gamma=0
. The non-trivial travelling wave solutions obtained in Corollary
1.4
are a further example in which the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
is not true.

Another setting for which we have equality in (
6
) is when
c=0
and
\gamma=0
. In this case the linearised operator around
Y_{1}^{0}
(we can take
\alpha=1
for simplicity) has a non-trivial kernel formed by zonal flows and eigenfunctions of the laplacian of eigenvalue
-2
. However, as we have seen from Corollary
1.5
above, the conclusion of Theorem (
1.2
) is still valid if one assumes them to be orthogonal to
Y_{1}^{m}
, for
|m|=1
. Alternatively, dropping this assumption, one would be tempted to follow the strategy presented for the proof of Theorem
1.1
in Section
3
and consider, for instance,

\Psi_{\varepsilon}=Y_{1}^{0}+\varepsilon\psi,\quad F_{\varepsilon}(s)=-2s+\varepsilon f(s),

which yields the non-linear elliptic equation

{\Delta}\psi+2\psi=f(Y_{1}^{0}+\varepsilon\psi),

and try to construct non-zonal non-trivial solutions by introducing
Y_{1}^{1}
through the kernel, thus obtaining

{\Delta}\psi+2\psi=f(Y_{1}^{0}+\varepsilon\psi+\varepsilon Y_{1}^{1}).

As before, one would need to construct both
f
and
\psi
simultaneously. However, some problems arise. Firstly, one cannot expect
f
to remain automatically orthogonal to
Y_{1}^{1}
any more, and hence an additional compatibility condition would be in place.

In that case, looking for some general
f=f(Y_{1}^{0}+\varepsilon\psi+\varepsilon Y_{1}^{1})
with
f\in C^{1}
and expanding in orders of
\varepsilon
, one would have

f=f(Y_{1}^{0})+\varepsilon(\psi+Y_{1}^{1})f^{\prime}(Y_{1}^{0})+O(\varepsilon^{2})

and in this case the compatibility conditions would read

\langle f,Y_{1}^{0}\rangle=\langle f,Y_{1}^{1}\rangle=0.

These two compatibility conditions are related to each other
due to an intrinsic property of the spherical harmonics that can be traced back directly to the Legendre polynomials. Indeed, one can prove that for all
n\geq 1
there exists
C>0
only depending on
n
such that

\langle f(Y_{n}^{0}),Y_{n}^{0}\rangle=C\langle f^{\prime}(Y_{n}^{0})Y_{n}^{1},Y_{n}^{1}\rangle.

Indeed, note that from (
11
) and the definitions of the spherical harmonics and the associated Legendre polynomials,

\begin{split}\langle f(Y_{n}^{0}),Y_{n}^{0}\rangle&=\pi\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(Y_{n}^{0})Y_{n}^{0}(\theta)\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=\pi C\int_{0}^{\pi}f(Y_{n}^{0})P_{n}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=\pi C\int_{0}^{\pi}f(Y_{n}^{0})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}\left(\sin^{2}\theta\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}P_{n}(\cos\theta)\right)\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=\pi C\int_{0}^{\pi}f(Y_{n}^{0})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\left(\sin^{2}\theta\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}P_{n}(\cos\theta)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.\end{split}

Integrating by parts and recognising
Y_{n}^{1}
, we obtain

\begin{split}\langle f(Y_{n}^{0}),Y_{n}^{0}\rangle&=-\pi C\int_{0}^{\pi}f^{\prime}(Y_{n}^{0})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}Y_{n}^{0}(\theta)\left(\sin^{2}\theta\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}P_{n}(\cos\theta)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=\pi C\int_{0}^{\pi}f^{\prime}(Y_{n}^{0})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}P_{n}(\cos\theta)\left(\sin^{2}\theta\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\cos\theta}P_{n}(\cos\theta)\right)\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=C\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f^{\prime}(Y_{n}^{0})(P_{n}^{1}(\cos\theta))^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\\
&=C\langle f^{\prime}(Y_{n}^{0})Y_{n}^{1},Y_{n}^{1}\rangle.\end{split}

This has a non-trivial consequence, since then the compatibility conditions read

\begin{split}0=\langle f,Y_{1}^{0}\rangle&=\langle f(Y_{1}^{0}),Y_{1}^{0}\rangle+O(\varepsilon),\\
0=\langle f,Y_{1}^{1}\rangle&=\langle f^{\prime}(Y_{1}^{0})Y_{1}^{1},Y_{1}^{1}\rangle+\langle f^{\prime}(Y_{1}^{0})\psi,Y_{1}^{1}\rangle+O(\varepsilon)\\
&=C\langle f(Y_{1}^{0}),Y_{1}^{0}\rangle+\langle f^{\prime}(Y_{1}^{0})\psi,Y_{1}^{1}\rangle+O(\varepsilon).\end{split}

The two conditions can therefore be written as

0=\langle f(Y_{1}^{0}),Y_{1}^{0}\rangle+O(\varepsilon),\quad 0=\langle f^{\prime}(Y_{1}^{0})\psi,Y_{1}^{1}\rangle+O(\varepsilon).

Any choice of polynomial
f
or, more generally, any linear combination of functions, shows that we cannot start a completely determined recursive definition of the coefficients because we lack information on
\langle f^{\prime}(Y_{1}^{0})\psi,Y_{1}^{1}\rangle
. For this reason, we cannot apply our strategy to construct stationary non-trivial non-zonal solutions to the non-rotating Euler equation arbitrarily close to
Y_{1}^{0}
.

Eventually, our method is doomed to fail. Indeed, assuming that
{\Delta}\Psi_{\varepsilon}=F_{\varepsilon}(\Psi_{\varepsilon})
is a stationary non-trivial non-zonal solution with
\Psi\in H^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
, our analytic regularity results would imply that
f(\Psi_{\varepsilon})
is uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
and thus the non-linearity
F_{\varepsilon}
would be such that
F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}>-6
. However, using
[
12
, Theorem 4]
, any solution
{\Delta}\Psi=G(\Psi)
with
G^{\prime}>-6
must be zonal, which yields a contradiction.

The key difference with Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.3
above is that in these cases we have more elements in the kernel, namely we can introduce
Y_{2}^{2}
via
{\Delta}+6
, for this choice we obtain two completely determined compatibility conditions and the non-linearity
F_{\varepsilon}
is no longer such that
F_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}>-6
.
