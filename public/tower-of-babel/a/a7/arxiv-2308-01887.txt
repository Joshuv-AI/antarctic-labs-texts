Athena 2.0: Discourse and User Modeling in Open Domain Dialogue
Omkar Patil, Lena Reed, Kevin K. Bowden, Juraj Juraska, Wen Cui, Vrindavan Harrison

Abstract
Conversational agents are consistently growing in popularity and many people interact with them every day. While many conversational agents act as personal assistants, they can have many different goals. Some are task-oriented, such as providing customer support for a bank or making a reservation. Others are designed to be empathetic and to form emotional connections with the user. The Alexa Prize Challenge aims to create a socialbot, which allows the user to engage in coherent conversations, on a range of popular topics that will interest the user. Here we describe Athena 2.0, UCSC’s conversational agent for Amazon’s Socialbot Grand Challenge 4. Athena 2.0 utilizes a novel knowledge-grounded discourse model that tracks the entity links that Athena introduces into the dialogue, and uses them to constrain named-entity recognition and linking, and coreference resolution. Athena 2.0 also relies on a user model to personalize topic selection and other aspects of the conversation to individual users.

3.1
Discourse Model

The Athena discourse model is designed to track the topics under discussion, selected named entities from the last few user and system utterances, speaker information, and anaphoric expressions. The number of entities to track is a parameter of the model, and is used to control the size of the “centered” entities
[
3
,
53
]
. The information in the discourse model is then used in downstream tasks such as named entity recognition (NER), named entity linking (NEL), and system response generation.

The discourse model facilitates information sharing by storing its data in a state table that is accessible to all Athena modules. This year’s focus on the discourse model required changes to the Response Generators (RGs), which are responsible for creating the system response using a variety of strategies described in Section
5
.

Previously, the various Athena RGs did not share information in a well defined way, which led to overly short conversations and abrupt changes in topic. The current RG did not have direct knowledge of the entities introduced by previous RGs. This year, when the RGs introduce an entity in the system utterance, the entity and its knowledge graph ID are recorded in the discourse model. Then downstream RGs can then rely on the discourse model to identify any system generated entities and their IDs, rather than having the NER/NEL detect them in the system utterance.

An important aspect of the discourse model is that it tracks if entities are introduced through a user utterance vs. a system response. This distinction is important for the confidence estimates given to the entities. The discourse model also contains information pertaining to the entity type (e.g. person, song, movie), which is used by the coreference model to resolve pronouns. By introducing the discourse model and incorporating these different elements, Athena can produce conversations that focus on entities that reflect the user’s interest.

3.2
Named Entity Recognition and Linking

The named entity recognition (NER) and named entity linking (NEL) modules are used to understand entity mentions in users’ utterances. They are critical for the Knowledge-Graph RGs, which use the entity information to retrieve facts from WikiData. NER and NEL also provide entity information for downstream tasks, and allow the response generators to produce coherent responses. NER and NEL in open domain conversations expose various challenges, such as the lack of annotated open-domain conversational data, and the inaccurate recognition of popular entities with existing tools such as Spotlight DBPedia
[
37
]
and our previous Athena 1.0 NER/NEL module
[
18
]
. The creation of annotated data is especially difficult since the rules of the competition prohibit the use of Mechanical Turk on user data. To cope with these challenges, we mined templates from real-user conversations, created
\sim
1M instances of synthetic training data with context, leveraged a pre-trained BERT model
[
8
]
, and developed a context-aware NER/NEL system. The NER/NEL in Athena 2.0 achieves a 15% improvement over the NER/NEL in Athena 1.0, and a 30% improvement over Spotlight, when tested with Athena’s real conversations.

Popularity Metric.
We developed gazetteers by retrieving
\sim
950K Wikidata entities for the entity types needed to support Athena’s popular topics such as Movies, TV, Music, Sports, Books, Board Games and Video Games. To help generate the synthetic data, and provide features to the ranking algorithm, we also stored popularity scores based on English Wikipedia page views, aggregated over a three month period. We used Amazon’s Elastic Search to create an index for these gazetteers for effective retrieval.
The NER/NEL use popularity to disambiguate between two entities with the same name when there is no other distinguishing factor present. While the process of gathering page view counts is simple for most cases, niche genres required additional steps to generate a popularity score due to the way their Wikipedia and Wikidata pages are structured.

BERT-Based NER and NEL.
We first fine-tune a BERT model for mention-detection with the generated synthetic data, including dialogue context. 3.3
Entity-Based Coreference Resolution

Coreference resolution relies on a discourse model. Once pronouns are resolved to their antecedents, we can link them to entities in the KG, and store this information in the discourse model. This allows both further coreference resolution and named entity recognition and linking to access a more complete discourse model. We start with topics that we have KG RGs for (as described in Section
5.2

rely on entities from the knowledge graph. From our logs, we are able to tell that the most frequent pronouns used include "he, she, him, her, they" and their derivatives, as well the pronoun "it". Therefore, our focus is currently only on resolution of these specific pronouns, rather than a general mentions resolution. To date, we have developed and experimented with several different models for coreference resolution: one rule-based model and two neural models.
The benefits of a rule-based system include ease of implementation and efficiency. This approach is very lightweight compared to a neural model. An analysis of data pulled from live traffic shows that the majority of anaphoric pronouns can be resolved with only one previous turn of context. Some contexts result in near 100% confidence in the prediction, and therefore can be captured with a small number of rules. The rule-based model uses the entities mentioned in the last utterance, as well as the type of the entity and the type of pronouns. A drawback of a rule-based system is that it is heavily dependent on the quality of information that is present in the discourse model. However, conversation flows and user utterances can be unpredictable, and anaphora can be present in many different forms. For example, when pronouns refer to an entity within the user’s current utterance, it causes complications because the entity has not yet been added to the discourse model (Figure

This is because the NLU pipeline runs coreference resolution before Named Entity Recognition and Linking (NER/NEL) for the current utterance, which allows NER/NEL to take advantage of the updated context provided by coreference engine. If NER/NEL identifies the correct named entity, then that entity will be added to the discourse model. However, neural models, such as AllenNLP’s coreference model

Dialogue Management

Open-domain conversational dialogue management is a challenging task due to the many different possibilities for valid responses to a user’s utterance given the context . Unlike task-oriented dialogues, where the dialogue manager (DM) can optimize a clear set of objectives, open-domain dialogues do not have a clear metric of appropriateness

Athena’s DM uses a shallow hierarchy based on a top down decomposition into a number of subcomponents, each with its own responsibility. RG-DM Interface.

The interface between the dialogue manager and response generators is a contract-based approach. The dialogue manager passes a set of response conditions to the response generators. The conditions act as a contract that the response generators must meet in order for their response to be considered for the current turn. This approach gives our dialogue system the flexibility to take advantage of a wide variety of response generator types, such as indexing and retrieval RGs, generative response models
[
19
,
39
]
,
and call flows that resemble finite state automata.
The DM-RG interface allows for backwards compatibility, e.g. a previous version of Athena supported a legacy system by treating it as an RG. In addition, completely swapping out all of Athena’s RGs would require little modification to Athena’s dialogue manager, consisting of updates to the response ranking function.

RGs can return multiple response candidates at each turn. In fact, this is encouraged in case some responses get removed during a quality filtering stage. If an RG does not have anything of substance to say at a given point in conversation, then it returns a null response (rather than saying something along the lines of “I don’t know what that is”). Furthermore, RGs are required to return the response parts as labeled separate segments.

Action Manager.

Situations commonly arise where the dialogue system needs to facilitate the conversation by performing some functional speech act. These situations are topic independent and may occur at any point in dialogue. The job of the Action Manager is to recognize these situations and decide on an appropriate course of action for the system to take. We use the set of nine system action types in Table
6
. The
converse
action denotes the case where a functional speech act should not be performed. The Action manager uses a heuristic rule method based on NLU features, such as dialogue act, keyword matching, and regular expressions, to decide the next system action.

Constraint Manager.

Once the system action has been decided, the Constraint Manager is responsible for generating a set of constraints that describe the next system utterance. The constraints specify a topic of discussion, as well as potential entities to mention. Also, the constraints contain a flag that signals if the dialogue manager is initiating discussion on a new topic this turn. For example, the constraints:
{ Topic = harry_potter, Entity Mention = J.K_Rowling, Dialogue_act = opinion_question }
,
describe the following system utterance:
“
What do you think about J.K. Rowling? Do you know much about her?
”

Currently, the constraint generation is based on heuristic rules. The constraint manager is designed using a flexible topic-agnostic approach based on general principles of entity/topical coherence. In short, the general strategy of the constraint manager is to talk about the same entities or concepts as the user. The constraint manager can specify soft or hard constraints.

In the process of generating constraints for the next turn, the Constraint Manager asks some internal questions: What is the current topic? Is it a continuation from the last turn’s topic or has the user changed it to something new? Are we still on the same topic, but have shifted to a new sub-topic, or focused on a new entity? These questions are answered by another module: the Topic Manager sub-module, described below.

While generating constraints, the DM has the ability to initiate a topic change action. Two of the most common reasons for changing the topic during this phase are if the DM loses track of the current topic, or does not know appropriate constraints to produce for current conversational context.

Topic Manager.

The Topic Manager refers to a collection of modules and sub-modules that perform several tasks related to topic representation, topic detection, and topic state representation. Athena’s dialogue manager uses a topic state to track topical information related to the current conversation so far. In addition to the current topic, the topic state includes a distribution over turns spent on each topic so far, and a topic history containing the sequence of topics visited. It contains lists of entities mentioned by both the user and the system. Only some of Athena’s RGs supply responses with annotated entity mentions. Flow-RGs that have a KG RG also have a dictionary that can return Wikidata IDs for
any entity mentions, in order to support the Discourse Model and coreference across RGs.

Individual topics are represented using Python classes. Each class stores information related to that topic such as its name, referential expressions associated with the topic, and subtopics that fall within its general domain, e.g., basketball is a subtopic of sports. The arrangement of topics is designed to reflect Athena’s conversation structures and content.

Other topics are handled by retrieval based methods, or by search, such as by using EVI, a database of facts stored in an Elastic Search index, and other response indices that are based on user’s frequent utterances and offer a few turns of conversation on that topic.

The topic detector module is also part of the Topic Manager.
The topic detector searches the user utterance for topic name mentions and key words associated with each topic. Then, a combination of features, such as the dialogue act, topic key-words, and Amazon’ Topic classifier are used to recognize and detect situations where the user is trying to invoke some new topic. We categorize user topic invocations into two types. In the first type, the user explicitly invokes the topic by name, e.g., "let’s talk about sports". In the second type, the user might only refer to some entity related to Athena’s internal topic, e.g., "what do you think about Kobe Bryant?" falls within the sports topic class. Athena’s dialogue manager requires a discrete topic class to be assigned to each conversation turn. Therefore, correctly identifying entities and linking them to their associated topic is paramount.

Initiative Manager.

In some situations the DM may decide to change the topic of discussion. The Initiative Manager comes into play after the decision has been made to leave the current topic. It decides what new topic to initiate, or whether to give the user the initiative. We have repeatedly experimented with different approaches to allowing the user more initiative.

In SGC4, we increased the frequency of our menu-prompt that offers the user a selection of three topics that haven’t been discussed yet, to help the user take initiative in the conversation. Figure

RG Dispatcher.

The RG dispatcher decides which RGs to run given the action and constraints. In most cases Athena has two or three RGs that can contribute to conversations within a given topic. All RGs capable of meeting the constraints are selected. Also, some RGs run every turn, such as RGs for responding to “red” questions, RGs that contain Athena’s persona responses, and question-answering RGs such as EVI.

The decision making in the response dispatcher is an automatic process mainly achieved through look up tables. We construct a registry by requiring each RG to register (at time of instantiation) the action types and topics that they are capable of responding to. Then, during execution, the registry determines which RGs to run based on the outputs of the Action Manager and Constraint Manager.

Response Pool Builder.

The response pool builder is the DM module that interfaces with the response generators. This module takes the list of RGs output by the RG Dispatcher and calls on them to populate a preliminary response pool. This preliminary response pool is refined with filters for profanity and repetition and then gets sent back to the DM. Before passing the response candidates to Cobot’s offensive speech classifier, we mask words and phrases that we observed to cause responses to be flagged falsely as offensive, such as “king”, “president”, or “saturated”.

Response Ranker.

The goal of the response ranker is to find the best response that also matches the constraints output by the Constraint Manager.
Athena’s response ranker is a BERT-based neural response ranker which was trained on hand annotated Alexa Prize conversation data. The annotators were given two turns each of user and Athena’s conversation context along with a pool of candidate responses, and asked to rank the candidate responses. We have annotated additional data and retrained this response ranker three times during SGC4.

Fallback Strategy.

Several circumstances can lead to the response pool being empty. For instance, a system crash, the RGs could not meet the dialogue manager constraints, or all the responses were removed from the response pool during the filtering stage. The purpose of the Fallback Strategy module is to take over and provide a graceful way of moving the conversation forward. Our most frequent fallback approach is to initiate conversation on a new previously unvisited topic, but after several system initiatives, Athena will use a fallback strategy that gives the user the initiative by prompting for an unvisited topic. The Fallback Strategy selects a template utterance from a collection of utterances crafted specially for this circumstance.

Response Builder.

The Response Builder module assembles the response parts and performs a cleaning post-processing step, before sending the response to the TTS engine.

5.1
Flow-Based Response Generators

Though lacking somewhat in robustness, and requiring a great deal of handcrafting, call-flows are still the most reliable way to generate contextually appropriate responses in a dialogue system. With a graph of nodes between which the RG moves depending on what the user replies, and with a set of system response templates for each node to select from, flow-based RGs capture a larger and more specific context, and are thus able to be more context-aware and fluent, when compared to other RG types, such as retrieval-based or neural. This RG design naturally has a rather limited support for user initiative, which we make up for with other RGs in Athena and by ensuring the responses from different RGs get smoothly interwoven across multiple turns, as well as within a single turn.

Our Flow-RG framework was created for Athena 1.0 to supports the design of dialogue flows and systematically communicate information from the Flow RGs to the DM. Flow-RG functions to modularize and streamline the process of implementing flow-based RGs, and to impose a common structure and constraints on RGs across all topics. Each call-flow (typically one for a topic) can comprise multiple independent
miniflows
(corresponding to subtopics or specific subjects), and Flow-RG ensures the transitions between them are smooth and natural, regardless of which order they are executed in. Since flow-based RGs tend to lean more on system initiative, the framework also ensures that interweaving with other types of RGs (such as for question answering), and the subsequent flow resumption, work seamlessly, as described and illustrated in Section
5.1.1
.

Flow-RG enforces each next turn in the flow graph to be conditioned on the dialogue act(s) of the current user utterance, which should reduce the chance of Athena’s subsequent response ignoring the user’s intent, which can be anything from expressing an opinion, to requesting information, to merely acknowledging Athena’s response in the previous turn
[
18
]
. To make the conditioning more robust, we now allow
preconditions
based on other aspects of the user utterance to be specified as well, such as a named entity or a particular set of keywords present in the utterance, or the sentiment of the utterance. Another feature we introduced in Flow-RG this year are
universal templated miniflows
, which only need a data file to be provided in order for them to be enabled for a given topic. Two examples of these are the hypothetical and would-you-rather question miniflows introduced in the majority of Athena’s flow-based RGs (see Section
5.1.3
).

One specific Flow-RG that has been important to our system is our Introduction. The goal of the Knowledge Graph-based Response Generators (KG RGs) was to

Qualitative Evaluation.

The Topical Chat corpus is a written chat corpus between two humans while the DD-NRG model is intended to operate in a real-time spoken conversation. We therefore set up an evaluation task where human raters were asked to examine Athena’s response in a particular dialogue context, and then annotate DD-NRG’s response for the following attributes:

Makes Sense:
Is the DD-NRG response sensible and not just gibberish?

Coherent to Dialogue Context:
Is the DD-NRG response consistent with the previous dialogue context and user’s current utterance. This is in line with the competition’s goal of maintaining a coherent conversation with the user.

Takes Conversation Forward:
Does the DD-NRG response help keep the conversation going or makes it end abruptly. We hypothesize that ending the response with a question would help the DD-NRG maintain the flow of the conversation.

Better than current Athena response:
Is the DD-NRG response better than Athena’s current response. If the response is similar or better, it’s marked as positive, indicating that the DD-NRG response can match the quality of other hand crafted responses from Athena.

5.4.2
Neural Response Generation from Meaning Representations

Another way we make use of neural models in Athena is for generating responses from meaning representations
[
45
,
39
,
19
,
27
,
26
]
. This approach relies on the content and the dialogue act (DA) to be provided as input, but offers more control over the generated response in return. It is therefore compatible with the Flow-RG framework, and can be used to generate whole responses, as well as just their segments (combined with template-based segments), at any point in the flow-graph. Flow-RG prepares the input meaning representation (MR) for the model – essentially a dictionary of attributes and their values, which constitute the desired content to mention, along with the DA type indication – at any given node in the flow graph, depending on the context and the user model.

This hybrid neural-and-template design drastically reduces the amount of effort put into writing response templates for variable content (typically content that can be looked up in a knowledge base), yet it takes advantage of a relatively small number of handcrafted templates to achieve natural and context-aware acknowledgments and transitions, which the data-to-text model is not trained to produce. The handcrafted templates are mostly short and do not contain any slots to fill with information. Note that a response being composed of a mixture of neural-generated and template-based segments does not change anything about how Flow-RG ultimately combines them into a final response.

User Model

Creating a user model, and modifying the dialogue system’s responses
based on that user model, should increase the user’s feelings of agency, and create more engaging conversations

[
29
,
40
]
. We build a user
model incrementally over multiple conversations with each user.

The user model relies on the NLU pipeline, several handcrafted regular expressions, and annotated data. The user model tracks general information across conversations, such as the user’s name, whether or not they’ve self-identified as a youth, and their interests. We also track topic-specific information, such as their pet’s names, their weekend hobbies, and their favorite dinosaur.

We primarily use the user model when determining which topics to present the user with, either in a menu of options or by explicitly taking the initiative. The user model
promotes topics that it believes users are most likely interested in hearing
about without needing to explicitly ask for their input as well as, within a topic, tailoring
our responses based on how the user responds.

To increase the user’s feelings of agency
[
17
]
, we adapt Athena’s responses to the values stored in the user model. For example, if Athena is taking the initiative when picking the next topic and earlier the user said
I like eating pasta

. Here, Athena shows that she remembers the user, and initiates a new topic that is likely to be relevant to the user’s interests.

We further use this information to better handle repeat user conversation. For example, we use the user’s name to personalize our greeting, to avoid topics that we’ve already explored, and remember their previous preferences. In live A/B testing we have seen a statistically significant improvement in user rating when comparing our personalized topic selection strategy against our default topic selection strategy.

8
Conclusion

Here we describe a conversational agent for spoken dialogue named Athena,
which competed as an Alexa Prize Socialbot in 2020/2021. We interpret user input
using various methods, such as coreference resolution, NER/NEL and user modeling. We
tune our responses based on these features to create responses which fit the
user’s interests.

One of the strengths of Athena is its ability to incorporate responses from a wide variety of resources, such as real-time KG walks, as well as a modular conversation flow framework, that allows RGs to be interweaved. We greatly improved our NER/NEL system that incorporates a large knowledge base of entities and methods for generating a huge corpus of synthetic training examples, as well as utilizing annotated data from real user conversations. We developed a discourse model and a coreference resolution module which integrates our use of the Wikidata KG across multiple response generators, allowing them to maintain context and understand what has been discussed. We also experimented with several neural methods to generate responses and analyzed the quality of these outputs. Our analysis of Athena’s conversations collected during the semi-finals phase suggests that Athena 2.0 is greatly improved over Athena 1.0, with many of our response generators engaging the users to a greater depth. But obviously, with a challenge like the Alexa Prize Challenge, there is always future work to be done. In future work, we hope to further improve our representation of context, and further explore the use of neural response generation in order to reduce the amount of handcrafting in conversational agents.
