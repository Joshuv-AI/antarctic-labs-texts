1
Introduction and main results

For
0<r
we consider the function

f_{r}(x):=\frac{\log(1+rx)}{\log(1+x)},\quad x>0.

(1)

It extends by continuity to the interval
(\max(-1,-1/r),0]
with the value
f_{r}(0)=r
.
Furthermore,
\lim_{x\to\infty}f_{r}(x)=1
.

In a study of the fractal dimension of certain sets in number theory coming from the number of multinomial coefficients congruent to a given residue modulo a prime, David Bradley is led to the conjecture, that
f_{r}
is a Bernstein function when
0<r<1
, see
[
4
]
. We prove that this is true as part of the following result:

Theorem 1
.

The function
f_{r}
is a complete Bernstein function
when
0<r<1
and a Stieltjes function when
r>1
.

Bernstein functions are by definition
C^{\infty}
-functions
f:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
satisfying

(-1)^{n-1}f^{(n)}(x)\geq 0,\quad x>0,n=1,2,\ldots.

(2)

In particular, Bernstein functions are non-negative, increasing and concave. To verify the inequalities (
2
) for
f_{r}
seems a complicated task, because one has to differentiate a quotient. We prove instead the stronger statement, that
f_{r}
belongs to the subclass
\mathcal{CBF}
of complete Bernstein functions studied in
[
8
]
. Our proof uses complex analysis.

Bernstein functions have a long history, and they play an important role in probability theory and potential theory, see
[
8
]
and
[
1
]
.

We also need the class
{\mathcal{S}}_{\lambda}
of generalized Stieltjes functions of order
\lambda>0
defined as the functions
f:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
having a representation of the form

f(x)=c+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\mu(t)}{(x+t)^{\lambda}},

(3)

where
c\geq 0
, and
\mu
is a positive measure on
[0,\infty)
such that the integral in (
3
) converges for
x>0
. When
\lambda=1
the functions in

are just called Stieltjes functions. A function in the class
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
clearly extends to a holomorphic function in the cut plane
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
. For more information on these functions see
[
7
]
. We mention in particular that
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{S}_{2}
.

Complete Bernstein functions are characterized by six equivalent conditions given in Theorem 6.2 of
[
8
]
. We shall establish condition (iv), stating that they are Pick functions with certain properties. For the convenience of the reader we have included some key results about Pick functions in Section 2.

We denote by
\Log
the principal branch of the logarithm, which is holomorphic in the cut plane
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
and defined by

\Log(z)=\log|z|+i\Arg(z),

where

is the principal argument taking its values in

The expression

We first give more details about the case

2
Preliminaries about Pick functions

Let
\mathbb{H}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\Im z>0\}
denote the open upper half-plane.

A holomorphic function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
is called a Pick function if its imaginary part
\Im f(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

For a Pick function
f
,
\Im f
is a non-negative harmonic function, and it has the mean value property. It is therefore either identically zero or strictly positive on
\mathbb{H}
. This means that a Pick function is either a real constant function or it maps
\mathbb{H}
into itself. Pick functions are treated in
[
5
]
,
[
8
]
and in the Appendix to
[
9
]
.

A Pick function
f
has the representation

f(z)=\alpha+\beta z+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{t-z}-\frac{t}{1+t^{2}}\right)\,d\mu(t),\quad z\in\mathbb{H},

(10)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\beta\geq 0
and
\mu
is a positive measure on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying the integrability condition
\int(1+t^{2})^{-1}\,d\mu(t)<\infty
. The triple
(\alpha,\beta,\mu)
is uniquely determined by
f
as

\alpha=\Re f(i),\quad\beta=\lim_{y\to\infty}f(iy)/iy,\quad\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}(1/\pi)\Im f(x+iy)\,dx=\mu,

(11)

where the last convergence is in the vague topology for measures meaning that

\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\pi}\int\Im f(x+iy)g(x)\,dx=\int g\,d\mu

(12)

for all continuous functions
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
with compact support. To see this we note that using convolution we have

\frac{1}{\pi}\int\Im f(x+iy)g(x)\,dx=\frac{\beta y}{\pi}\int g(x)\,dx+\int P_{y}\star g(t)\,d\mu(t),

where

P_{y}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}},\quad y>0,x\in\mathbb{R}

is the Poisson kernel for the upper half-plane.

Equation (
12
) now follows because
\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}P_{y}\star g(t)=g(t)
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
(and even uniformly in
t
). Furthermore, we can apply Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem. To see the latter, if
g
has
compact support contained in the interval
[a,b]
and supremum norm
C:=||g||_{\infty}
,
we find for
0<y<1,t\in\mathbb{R}
,

|P_{y}\star g(t)|\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{y|g(x)|}{(t-x)^{2}+y^{2}}\,dx\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}C,&t\in\mathbb{R},\\
\frac{C(b-a)}{\pi(a-t)^{2}},&t<a,\\
\frac{C(b-a)}{\pi(t-b)^{2}},&t>b,\end{array}\right.

so
|P_{y}\star g(t)|
has a
\mu
-integrable majorant because of the assumption
\int(1+t^{2})^{-1}\,d\mu(t)<\infty
.

This implies in particular, that if there is an open interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}(1/\pi)\Im f(t+iy)=\varphi(t),\quad t\in I

(13)

and the convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
I
, then the restriction of
\mu
to the interval
I
has the continuous density
\varphi
with respect to Lebesgue measure.

The measure
\mu
has mass
m\geq 0
at a point
a\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if

\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}y\Im f(a+iy)=m.

(14)

In fact,

y\Im f(a+iy)=\beta y^{2}+\int\frac{y^{2}}{(t-a)^{2}+y^{2}}\,d\mu(t)\to\mu(\{a\})

for
y\to 0^{+}
because

\frac{y^{2}}{(t-a)^{2}+y^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{1+(t-a)^{2}},\quad 0<y<1,t\in\mathbb{R},

and therefore
2(1+(t-a)^{2})^{-1}
is an integrable majorant with respect to
\mu
.

For a Stieltjes function
f
given by (
3
) with
\lambda=1
, we get

-(1/\pi)\Im f(-x+iy)=P_{y}\star\mu(x),

and we get similar results for Stieltjes functions as for Pick functions.

Let

(i)
\liminf_{z\to t,z\in\mathbb{H}}u(z)\geq 0

Proof of Theorem
2
.

From the expression

f_{r}(z)=\frac{\log|1+rz|+i\Arg(1+rz)}{\log|1+z|+i\Arg(1+z)},\quad z\in\mathbb{H},

we see that

\mathbb{H}

The harmonic function

\Im f_{r}(z)=\frac{\log|1+z|\Arg(1+rz)-\log|1+rz|\Arg(1+z)}{\log^{2}|1+z|+\Arg^{2}(1+z)}.

It does not seem possible to see directly that this expression is positive, but it will follow from Proposition

We claim that for

\varphi_{r}(t):=\lim_{z\to t,z\in\mathbb{H}}(1/\pi)\Im f_{r}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0,&t\geq-1,\\
\frac{-\log(1+rt)}{\log^{2}(-t-1)+\pi^{2}},&-1/r<t<-1,\\
\infty,&t=-1/r,\\
\frac{\log((-t-1)/(-1-rt))}{\log^{2}(-t-1)+\pi^{2}},&t<-1/r.\end{array}\right.

For this we use that
\Arg(z)\in(0,\pi)
for

and

\lim_{z\to t,z\in\mathbb{H}}\Arg(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0,&t>0,\\

The convergence to
\varphi_{r}(t)
is uniform for
t
in compact subsets of the open intervals
(-\infty,-1/r),(-1/r,-1),(-1,\infty)
, and the expressions of
\varphi_{r}(t)
to the right are non-negative for all

.

We have shown that the conditions of Proposition

are satisfied for

, and the
\liminf

\Im f_{r}(z)>0
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.
This shows that
f_{r}
is a Pick function with a representation

f_{r}(z)=\Re f_{r}(i)+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{t-z}-\frac{t}{1+t^{2}}\right)\,d\mu_{r}(t),

(16)

see Section 2, because

\beta_{r}:=\lim_{y\to\infty}\frac{f_{r}(iy)}{iy}=0,

as is easily seen. We have

\alpha_{r}:=\Re f_{r}(i)=\frac{\log 2\log(1+r^{2})+\pi\arctan(r)}{\log^{2}(2)+\pi^{2}/4}>0.

The measure
\mu_{r}
has the density
\varphi_{r}
in the open intervals
(-\infty,-1/r),(-1/r,-1)
and
(-1,\infty)
because of (
13
), and there are no masses at the points
-1/r,-1
by (
14
). Replacing

by
-t
we finally get

f_{r}(z)=\alpha_{r}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{t}{1+t^{2}}-\frac{1}{t+z}\right)\varphi_{r}(-t)\,dt.

In the notation of formula (6.8) in
[
8
]
we have
\varphi_{r}(-t)\,dt=(1+t^{2})d\rho(t)
, and (
4
) follows with
\sigma_{r}(t)=\varphi_{r}(-t)/t
. Putting

in (

) we get

r=\alpha_{r}-\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi_{r}(-t)}{t(1+t^{2})}\,dt=\alpha_{r}-\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{r}(t)}{1+t^{2}}\,dt,

and the second formula in (
7
) follows.

The formula in Theorem
2
for
\sigma_{r}
shows that it is integrable over the interval
(1,\infty)
. From formula (

f_{r}(z)=r+\int_{1}^{\infty}\sigma_{r}(t)\,dt-\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{t\sigma_{r}(t)}{z+t}\,dt,

4
A convolution equation

The Laplace transform of a positive measure
\mu
on
[0,\infty)
is the function

\mathcal{L}\mu(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}\,d\mu(t),\quad x>0,

provided that the integral is finite for all
x>0
.
If
\mu
has a continuous density
f:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with respect to
Lebesgue measure
m
on
(0,\infty)
, i.e.,
d\mu=fdm
, we write
\mathcal{L}f=\mathcal{L}\mu
.

With this in mind we have

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}\mu)(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-yx}\mathcal{L}\mu(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\mu(t)}{y+t},\quad y>0.

Note that the family of functions
f_{r},r>0
satisfies

f_{r}(x)f_{1/r}(rx)=1,\quad r,x>0.

(23)

Assume now that
0<r<1
and rewrite (
23
) as

\frac{f_{r}(x)}{x}f_{1/r}(rx)=\frac{1}{x}=\mathcal{L}m(x)=\mathcal{L}1(x),\quad x>0.

(24)

Using the Laplace transformation, Equation (
6
) can be written

\frac{f_{r}(x)}{x}=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}\mu_{r})(x)=\mathcal{L}g_{r}(x),\quad g_{r}(s)=r+\mathcal{L}\sigma_{r}(s),

(25)

and Equation (
8
) can be written

f_{1/r}(rx)=1+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega_{1/r}(rt)}{x+t}\,dt=1+\mathcal{L}h_{r}(x),

(26)

where

h_{r}(s)=\mathcal{L}\omega_{1/r}(rt)(s)=\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-st}\omega_{1/r}(rt)\,dt,\quad s>0.

Using well-known properties of Laplace transformation,
we see that (
24
) is equivalent to the convolution equation

g_{r}\star(\delta_{0}+h_{r})(x)=1,\quad x>0

or

g_{r}(x)+\int_{0}^{x}g_{r}(s)h_{r}(x-s)\,ds=1,\quad x>0.

5
A question answered in the negative

Since
f(x)=\log(1+x)
is a complete Bernstein function, it is a natural question if the following extension of Theorem 1 holds:

f\in\mathcal{CBF}\implies f(rx)/f(x)\in\mathcal{CBF}\;\mbox{when}\,0<r<1.

We shall see that the answer to this question is negative.

First notice that if such an implication holds, then necessarily
f(0)=0
, for otherwise letting
r\to 0
we would obtain
1/f(x)\in\mathcal{CBF}
, and under the assumption
f\in\mathcal{CBF}
this is only possible, when
f
is constant.

A counterexample to the implication is obtained, when

f(x)=x/(x+1)+x/(x+3)
which is a complete Bernstein function. A small calculation shows that

\frac{f(x/4)}{f(x)}=\frac{(x+1)(x+3)(x+8)}{(x+2)(x+4)(x+12)}=1-\frac{3/10}{x+2}-\frac{3/4}{x+4}-\frac{99/20}{x+12}.

Since we have negative coefficients in the partial fraction decomposition, the conclusion is that the function
f(x/4)/f(x)\notin\mathcal{CBF}
.
