Abstract
. Rational word languages can be defined by several equivalent means: finite state automata, rational expressions, finite congruences, or monadic second-order (MSO) logic. The robust subclass of aperiodic languages is defined by: counter-free automata, star-free expressions, aperiodic (finite) congruences, or first-order (FO) logic. In particular, their algebraic characterization by aperiodic congruences allows to decide whether a regular language is aperiodic. We lift this decidability result to rational transductions, i.e., word-to-word functions defined by finite state transducers. In this context, logical and algebraic characterizations have also been proposed. Our main result is that one can decide if a rational transduction (given as a transducer) is in a given decidable congruence class. We also establish a transfer result from logic-algebra equivalences over languages to equivalences over transductions. As a consequence, it is decidable if a rational transduction is first-order definable, and we show that this problem is PSPACE-complete.

Logical and algebraic characterizations of rational languages

The theory of rational languages over finite words is mature and rich with results that stem from their many different characterizations, such as: finite automata, rational expressions, monadic second-order logic (

One of the main features of the algebraic approach is the existence, for any rational language, of a canonical object: the

(also known as Myhill-Nerode congruence) which is minimal in a strong sense that it is coarser than any congruence recognizing the language, and is related to the minimal automaton of a language.
Furthermore, many correspondences between some

of

and

(see

[

]
) have been established. Congruence varieties
are sets of congruences of finite index with good closure properties
including closure under coarser congruences

which means that the syntactic congruence carries, in some sense, the intrinsic algebraic properties of a language.
The most prominent of the aforementioned results, which was obtained together by Schützenberger
[

and McNaughton and Papert
[

Since then many other such correspondences have been found (again, see

The strength of these results is that they give, through the syntactic congruence, an

Rational transductions

Transductions are (partial) functions from finite words to finite words.
At the computational level they are realized by transducers, which are automata given together with an output function mapping transitions to finite words.
Although transducers have been studied for almost as long as automata, far less is known about them. A more recent result of
[
EH01
]
has sparked some renewed interest in their logical aspect.
This result states that transductions definable by MSO-transducers (
MSOT
), a logical model of transducers defined by Courcelle in the general context of graph transductions
[
CE12
]
, exactly characterize the transductions realized by two-way transducers.
Another model of one-way transducers with registers, called
streaming string transducers
, has also been shown to capture the same class of transductions called
regular transductions

[
AC10
]
.
The first steps of a logic-algebra relationship for transductions have been made by
[
FKT14
]
and
[
CD15
]
, where first-order transducers (
FOT
) are shown to be expressively equivalent to streaming string transducers (resp. two-way transducers) with an aperiodic transition congruence.
These characterizations are however not effective, and the class of regular functions still lacks a canonical object similar to the syntactic congruence, which would yield a way to decide if a given transducer realizes a transduction definable in first-order logic, for example.

Rational transductions
are the transductions realized by
one-way transducers, and they also coincide with transductions
definable by rational expressions over the product of two free monoids
(with component-wise concatenation and Kleene star)
[
BB79
]
.
In this paper, we define subclasses of rational transductions by
restricting the class of
transition congruence
of the
transducers which define them. The transition congruence of a
transducer is just defined as the transition congruence of its
underlying automaton, ignoring the output. The reason for choosing such
a definition is because we obtain nice correspondences between
classes of transition congruences of transducers and logical fragments
of MSO-transducers. First of all, the rational transductions are
exactly captured by a natural restriction of
MSOT
, called

order-preserving

MSOT

[
Boj14
,
Fil15
]
. Its first-order fragment was shown to
coincide with rational transduction definable by a one-way transducer
with aperiodic transition congruence in
[
Boj14
]
. Other
correspondences, for instance with
FO
{}^{2}{[<]}
, are shown in this paper.

Let us point out that the characterization of first-order rational
functions as the functions defined by aperiodic transducers
[
Boj14
]

is not effective since it does not
provide a way to decide if a given transduction is definable in
first-order logic. In
[
CG14
]
a different kind of
characterization of (non-functional) transductions over a unary alphabet is given, in
terms of rational expressions rather than in terms of transition
congruence.

A first result on the existence of a canonical machine for
transductions is given in
[
Cho03
]
, where the author defines a
construction of a syntactic congruence for sequential transducers,

i.e.
transducers with a deterministic underlying (input) automaton, which define a strict subclass of rational transductions.
This syntactic congruence, like for languages, is minimal in a
stronger sense than just yielding a sequential transducer with the
minimal number of states: it is minimal in the algebraic sense that it
is coarser than any transition congruence of a sequential transducer
realizing the transduction.
A second such result is the existence of a canonical machine for rational functions, shown in
[
RS91
]
.
However, as the authors mentioned in this article, this canonical machine is not minimal in any of the ways described above.
In the present paper we refine the approach of
[
RS91
]
in order to obtain minimal machines in terms of both number of states and of transition congruence.
This approach is based on a computational model of transductions called

A bimachine is a deterministic model of transductions, introduced in
[
Sch61
]
and further studied (and named) in
[
Eil74
]

In contrast to considering restrictions of the transition congruence
of the underlying automata of transducers, a different way of defining subclasses of rational transductions,
taking into account the outputs, has been proposed in
[
CKLP15
]

and
[
CCP17
]
. It is based on the natural notion of continuity,

i.e.
, preserving a class of languages by inverse image. In
[
CKLP15
]
, the authors were able to effectively
characterize sequential transductions definable by AC
0
circuits. In
[
CCP17
]
the authors show how to decide if a transduction
is continuous with respect to many usual varieties and they also
compare the two notions of continuity and realizability.

1.1.1.
Words and languages

An

\Sigma
is a finite set of symbols called

, a

over
\Sigma
is an element of the free monoid
\Sigma^{*}
, whose neutral element, the

\epsilon
.
The

of a word

is denoted by
|w|
with
|\epsilon|=0
.
For a non-empty word
w
and two positions
1\leq i\leq j\leq|w|
we denote by

the
i
th letter of
w
and by
w[i:j]
the factor of
w
beginning with the
i
th letter and ending with the
j
th letter of
w
.
For two words
u,v
, we write

if
u
is a prefix of
v
and in this case we denote by
u^{-1}v
the unique word
v^{\prime}
such that
uv^{\prime}=v
.
The
longest common prefix
of two words
u,v
is denoted by
u\wedge v
and the
prefix distance
between
u
and

is defined as
\parallel\!\!{u,v}\!\!\parallel=|u|+|v|-2|u\wedge v|
.
A

is a set of words and

denotes the longest common prefix of all the words in

with the convention that
\bigwedge\varnothing=\epsilon
.

1.1.2.
Finite automata

A

(or simply automaton) over an alphabet
\Sigma
is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=\left(Q,\Delta,I,F\right)
where
Q
is a finite set of
states
,
\Delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q
is the

, and
I,F\subseteq Q
denote the set of
initial states
and the set of
final states
, respectively.
A

of

over a word

is a word
r=q_{0}\ldots q_{|w|}
over

such that
(q_{i},w[i+1],q_{i+1})\in\Delta
for all

.
The run

is
accepting
if
q_{0}\in I
and
q_{|w|}\in F
.
A word
w
is
accepted
by
\mathcal{A}
if there exists an accepting run over it, and the language
recognized
by
\mathcal{A}
is the set of words accepted by
\mathcal{A}
and is denoted by
\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket
.
We will use the notation
p\xrightarrow{w}_{\mathcal{A}}q
(or just

when it is clear from context) to denote that there exists a run
r
of
\mathcal{A}
over
w
such that
r[1]=p
and
r[|r|]=q
.
An automaton
\mathcal{A}
is called

if its set of initial states is a singleton and for any two transitions
(p,\sigma,q_{1}),(p,\sigma,q_{2})\in\Delta
it holds that
q_{1}=q_{2}
.
An automaton is

if any word has at most one accepting run over it.
We call an automaton

if for any
p\in Q,\sigma\in\Sigma
there exists
q\in Q
such that
(p,\sigma,q)\in\Delta
.
A state
q
of an automaton
\mathcal{A}
is said to be
accessible
if there exists a word
w
and an initial state

, such that
q_{0}\xrightarrow{w}_{\mathcal{A}}q
. We say by extension that the automaton
\mathcal{A}
is

Finally, a language is called

1.2.
Algebraic characterization of rational languages

1.2.1.
Congruences

It is well known that rational languages are equivalently recognized by congruences of finite index. Let us define these notions.
Let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on
\Sigma^{*}
.
The equivalence class of a word
w
is denoted by
[w]_{\sim}
(or just
[w]
when it is clear from context).
We say that
\sim
has finite index if the quotient
\Sigma^{*}/_{\sim}=\left\{[w]_{\sim}\mid w\in\Sigma^{*}\right\}
is finite.
Let
\sim_{1}
,
\sim_{2}
be two equivalence relations, we say that
\sim_{1}
is
finer
than
\sim_{2}
(or that
\sim_{2}
is
coarser
than
\sim_{1}
) if any equivalence class of
\sim_{2}
is a union of equivalence classes of
\sim_{1}
, or equivalently for any two words
u,v
, if
u\sim_{1}v
then
u\sim_{2}v
. We write
{\sim_{1}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{2}}
to denote that
\sim_{1}
is finer than
\sim_{2}
.

An equivalence relation
\sim
over
\Sigma^{*}
is a
right congruence
(resp. a
left congruence
) if for any words
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
and letter
\sigma\in\Sigma
we have
u\sim v\Rightarrow u\sigma\sim v\sigma
(resp.
u\sim v\Rightarrow\sigma u\sim\sigma v
).
A
congruence
is defined as both a left and a right congruence.
When
\sim
is a congruence, the quotient
\Sigma^{*}/_{\sim}
is naturally endowed with a monoid structure, with multiplication
[u]_{\sim}\cdot[v]_{\sim}=[uv]_{\sim}
and identity
[\epsilon]_{\sim}
.
We say that a congruence
\sim

recognizes
a language
L
if
L
is a union of equivalence classes of
\sim
.
Let us note that the intersection of two right (resp. left) congruences
\sim_{1},\sim_{2}
over the same alphabet is also a right (resp. left) congruence which we denote by
\sim_{1}\sqcap\sim_{2}
.

{exa}

Important examples of congruences which we use
throughout the article are: the
syntactic congruence

\equiv_{L}
of a language
L
and the
transition congruence

\approx_{\mathcal{A}}
of an automaton
\mathcal{A}
with set of states
Q
. When

\mathcal{A}
is deterministic with initial state
q_{0}
, we also define

\sim_{\mathcal{A}}
, the
right transition congruence
of
\mathcal{A}
. These
relations are defined as follows, with
u,v
ranging over all words:

\begin{array}[]{rclclrcl}u&\equiv_{L}&v&\Leftrightarrow&(\forall x,y\in\Sigma^{*},&xuy\in L&\Leftrightarrow&xvy\in L)\\
u&\approx_{\mathcal{A}}&v&\Leftrightarrow&(\forall p,q\in Q,&p\xrightarrow{u}_{\mathcal{A}}q&\Leftrightarrow&p\xrightarrow{v}_{\mathcal{A}}q)\\
u&\sim_{\mathcal{A}}&v&\Leftrightarrow&(\forall q\in Q,&q_{0}\xrightarrow{u}_{\mathcal{A}}q&\Leftrightarrow&q_{0}\xrightarrow{v}_{\mathcal{A}}q)\end{array}

If
\mathcal{A}
recognizes a language
L
, then its transition congruence
\approx_{\mathcal{A}}
recognizes the same language.
The relation
\equiv_{L}
recognizes the language
L
and is the coarsest among congruences which do so. From the two previous remarks, this well-known fact follows: a language is rational if and only if its syntactic congruence has finite index.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a complete and deterministic automaton, then an equivalence class
[w]_{\sim_{\mathcal{A}}}
can be identified with the state of
\mathcal{A}
reached by reading the word
w
. In the following we will often make this identification, implicitly assuming that
\mathcal{A}
is complete (an automaton can be made complete in
PTime
), and will write
[w]_{\mathcal{A}}
rather than
[w]_{\sim_{\mathcal{A}}}
to simplify notations. Furthermore, for two deterministic automata
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}
such that
{\sim_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}}
, we will say by extension that
\mathcal{A}_{1}
is finer than
\mathcal{A}_{2}
and write
\mathcal{A}_{1}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{A}_{2}
. For example the minimal automaton of a language
L
is the coarsest deterministic automaton recognizing
L

(up to isomorphism).

1.2.2.
Congruence classes

A
congruence class
(class for short)
C
associates to any finite alphabet
\Sigma
, a set
\textbf{C}(\Sigma)
of congruences of finite index over
\Sigma
, such that
\textbf{C}(\Sigma)
is (1) closed under intersection and (2) closed under taking coarser congruences.
Note that we will often abuse the notation and write
C
instead of
\textbf{C}(\Sigma)
.

An automaton is called a
C
-automaton
if its transition congruence is in
C
.
A language is a
C
-language
if it is recognized by a
C
-automaton.
Note that since a class is stable by taking coarser congruences, a language is a
C
-language if and only if its syntactic congruence is in
C
. Let
\mathcal{L}(\textbf{C})
denote the set of
C
-languages.

Remark 1
.

Recognizability by a congruence of finite index is equivalent to the notion of recognizability by a
stamp
(
i.e.
, a surjective morphism from a free monoid to a finite monoid).
Indeed, a congruence
\sim
over an alphabet
\Sigma
yields a natural (surjective) morphism
[\cdot]_{\sim}:\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}/_{\sim}
which of course recognizes the same language.
Conversely, given a morphism
\mu:\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow M
with
M
a finite monoid, one can define the congruence
\sim_{M}
by
u\sim_{M}v
if
\mu(u)=\mu(v)
.
In
[
PS05
]
, the authors define
\mathcal{C}
-varieties of stamps (a generalization of the notion of monoid varieties), which are in particular congruence classes as we define them in this paper.
We choose to consider this notion of congruence classes simply because our results hold in this more general framework.

Note that in
[
FGL16b
]
we had chosen the term of
congruence variety
instead of
congruence class
which was ill-suited, as was kindly pointed out by Jean-Éric Pin, since the term variety stems from an equational theory, which does not exist in a context as general as the one of congruence classes.

1.2.3.
Definability problem and decidable classes

Given a set of languages
\mathcal{V}
, the
\mathcal{V}
-definability
problem asks whether a language
L
, given by an automaton, belongs to
\mathcal{V}
.

A congruence of finite index over an alphabet
\Sigma
can be given by
a morphism
\mu:\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow M
, with
M
a finite monoid (two
words are equivalent if they have the same image by
\mu

The morphism
\mu
can itself be given explicitly by a function

The
C
-membership problem
asks if a congruence of finite
index, given as a morphism, is in
C
.
In particular, the
\mathcal{L}(\textbf{C})
-definability problem reduces to the
C
-membership problem through the syntactic congruence.
A class is called
decidable
if its membership problem is decidable.

{exas}

We give several examples of decidable congruence classes.

•

The set
\mathbf{F}

•

A congruence

•

A central example of congruence class in this paper is the class

of

A congruence
\sim
is aperiodic if there exists an integer

such that for any word

.
Again, one can easily check that this property is stable by intersection and taking coarser congruences.
It is shown in
[

]
that the aperiodicity problem for a language, given as an automaton, is

-complete.
Moreover, even if the given automaton is deterministic, this problem remains

-hard
[
CH91
]
.

•

Let us also mention the congruence class
\mathbf{DA}
which was shown to recognize the languages definable in first-order logic with two variables
[

]
. A congruence of finite index
\sim
is in
\mathbf{DA}
if there exists an integer

such that for any words

,
{(uvw)}^{n}v(uvw^{n})\sim{(uvw)}^{n}
.

3.
Bimachines

Bimachines are a model of computation as expressive as (functional) transducers, that was introduced by
[
Sch61
]
and further studied (and named) by
[
Eil74
]
.
One of the main features of bimachines is their completely deterministic nature.
In order to express all the rational transductions a bimachine needs, as its name suggests, two automata:
A right automaton, which reads words deterministically from right to left and a left automaton (which is just a deterministic automaton).
The roles of the two automata are completely symmetrical, however the right automaton can be seen intuitively as a regular look-ahead for the left automaton.

Using a result from
[
RS91
]
we show that for a given bimachine, one can minimize the left automaton with respect to the right one and
vice versa
.
We give a
PTime
algorithm for bimachine minimization (in the vein of Moore’s DFA minimization algorithm), but underline the fact that a given transduction does not have a unique minimal bimachine, in general.

Finally, for a rational transduction
f
, we describe the canonical bimachine from
[
RS91
]
(
i.e.
, it does not depend on the description of
f
), which relies on the existence of a canonical right automaton.
Intuitively, this right automaton represents the “minimal” look-ahead information needed to realize the transduction sequentially.

3.1.
Bimachines and transductions

3.1.1.
Right automaton

Formally a
right automaton
over an alphabet
\Sigma
is an automaton
\mathcal{R}=(Q,\Delta,I,F)
such that
I
is a singleton and its transitions are backward deterministic, meaning that for any two transitions
(p_{1},\sigma,q),(p_{2},\sigma,q)\in\Delta
it holds that
p_{1}=p_{2}
.
The only difference with the classical notion of automaton lies in the definition of accepting runs.
A run
r
over a right automaton is called
accepting
if
r[1]
is final and
r[|r|]
is initial.
Therefore a right automaton can be thought of as reading words from right to left, deterministically.
A run
r
of
\mathcal{R}
over the word
w
will be denoted by
r[1]\xleftarrow{w}_{\mathcal{R}}r[|r|]
to emphasize that
\mathcal{R}
is a right automaton, and a transition
(p,\sigma,q)
will be depicted by an arrow from
q
to
p
.

3.1.2.
Left congruence

The
left transition congruence
associated with a right automaton
\mathcal{R}=(Q,\Delta,\left\{r_{0}\right\},F)
is defined by
u\sim_{\mathcal{R}}v
if
\forall r\in Q,\ r\xleftarrow{u}_{\mathcal{R}}r_{0}\Leftrightarrow r\xleftarrow{v}_{\mathcal{R}}r_{0}
.
Exactly like for left automata, we assume that
\mathcal{R}
is (co-)complete and identify
[u]_{\sim_{\mathcal{R}}}
(often denoted by
[u]_{\mathcal{R}}
) with the unique state
r\in Q
such that
r\xleftarrow{u}_{\mathcal{R}}r_{0}
. We also say that a right automaton
\mathcal{R}_{1}
is finer than
\mathcal{R}_{2}
(denoted by
\mathcal{R}_{1}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{2}
) if
{\sim_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}
.

3.1.3.
Bimachine

A
bimachine
over an alphabet
\Sigma
is a tuple
\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho\right)
where
\mathcal{L}=\left(Q_{\mathcal{L}},\Delta_{\mathcal{L}},\left\{l_{0}\right\},F_{\mathcal{L}}\right)
is a left (
i.e.
, deterministic) automaton,
\mathcal{R}=\left(Q_{\mathcal{R}},\Delta_{\mathcal{R}},\left\{r_{0}\right\},F_{\mathcal{R}}\right)
is a right automaton,
\omega:Q_{\mathcal{L}}\times\Sigma\times Q_{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
is the
output function
,
\lambda:F_{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
is the
left final function
and
\rho:F_{\mathcal{L}}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
is the
right final function
.
The two automata
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
are required to recognize the same language.

We extend naturally the function
\omega
to
Q_{\mathcal{L}}\times\Sigma^{*}\times Q_{\mathcal{R}}
as follows:
for all
l\in Q_{\mathcal{L}},r\in Q_{\mathcal{R}}
,
\omega(l,\epsilon,r)=\epsilon
and for all
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
,
l^{\prime}\in Q_{\mathcal{L}},r^{\prime}\in Q_{\mathcal{R}}
such that
l\xrightarrow{u}_{\mathcal{L}}l^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime}\xleftarrow{v}_{\mathcal{R}}r
then
\omega(l,uv,r)=\omega(l,u,r^{\prime})\omega(l^{\prime},v,r)
.

We define
\llbracket\mathcal{B}\rrbracket
the transduction
realized
by
\mathcal{B}
such that
\text{dom}(\llbracket\mathcal{B}\rrbracket)=\llbracket\mathcal{L}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{R}\rrbracket
, and for any word
u\in\llbracket\mathcal{L}\rrbracket
if
l
and
r
are the final states of the runs over
u
of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
respectively, then
\llbracket\mathcal{B}\rrbracket(u)=\lambda(r)\omega(l_{0},u,r_{0})\rho(l)
.

3.2.
Bimachine minimization

Sequential transducers can be minimized by producing the outputs as soon as possible, and this can be done in
PTime
(see
[
Cho03
]
and Section
2.1
).
For transducers in general, no such procedure is known.
Nevertheless, it was shown in
[
RS91
]
that, for a bimachine, the left
automaton can be minimized when the right automaton is fixed.
This is called left minimization, and it is done in the same “as soon as possible”
spirit but using the look-ahead information given by the right
automaton. Symmetrically, one can minimize the right automaton when
the left automaton is fixed (called right minimization).
By applying left minimization followed by right
minimization, we obtain a minimal bimachine.
We describe this minimization, generalize some properties from
[
RS91
]
, and provide a
PTime
algorithm for bimachine
minimization.

For
i=1,2
, let
\mathcal{B}_{i}
be a bimachine realizing
f
with
\mathcal{L}_{i}
and

\mathcal{R}_{i}
as left and right automata. We say that
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is finer
than
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{1}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{B}_{2}
, if
\mathcal{L}_{1}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}_{2}
and
\mathcal{R}_{1}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{2}
.
If
\mathcal{B}_{1}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{B}_{2}
,
\mathcal{B}_{2}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\llbracket\mathcal{B}_{1}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{B}_{2}\rrbracket
then we say that
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are equal up to state renaming and output shifting and we denote it by
\mathcal{B}_{1}\mathrel{\boxminus}\mathcal{B}_{2}
.
A bimachine
\mathcal{B}
realizing a
function
f
is
minimal
if for any other bimachine
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
realizing

f
, either
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{B}
or
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is incomparable with
\mathcal{B}
.
In other words, a bimachine is minimal if there does not exist a strictly coarser equivalent bimachine.
If
M
is a set of bimachines, we denote by
\text{Min}(M)\subseteq M

the set of minimal bimachines among
M
.

3.2.1.
Left and right minimization

Let
f
be a transduction realized by a bimachine
\mathcal{B}
with a right automaton
\mathcal{R}
.
We describe the construction of a bimachine

\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{B})=\left(\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{R}),\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho\right)

which realizes
f
and has the minimal left automaton among all
bimachines realizing
f
with right automaton

\mathcal{R}
. We will often write
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B})
and
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
when the transduction is clear from context.
Intuitively,
\mathcal{B}
can be seen as a sequential transducer processing
input words the positions of which are annotated by the class (in the
transition congruence of
\mathcal{R}
) of the suffix from that
position. Then, left minimization can be seen as minimizing this
sequential transducer. While it is a good intuition behind the
construction, the explicit annotation is however not necessary and we rather give a direct construction that
does not modify the input words.

We first define a family of new transductions, for any words
u,w
let:

\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)=\bigwedge\left\{f(uv)|\ v\in[w]_{\mathcal{R}}\cap u^{-1}\text{dom}(f)\right\}

The word
\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)
is the longest possible output from reading
u
, with the look-ahead information that the suffix is in
[w]_{\mathcal{R}}
.
The states of
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
will be the classes of the right congruence
\sim_{L}
defined for any words
u,v
by
u\sim_{L}v
if:

\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}\forall w\in\Sigma^{*},\ uw\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow vw\in\text{dom}(f)\text{ and,}\\
\text{if }uw\in\text{dom}(f),\text{ then }{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(uw)={\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(vw)\end{array}\right.

We define the automaton
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})=\left(Q,\Delta,\left\{l_{0}\right\},F\right)
from the congruence
\sim_{L}
:

•

Q=\Sigma^{*}/_{\sim_{L}}

•

\Delta=\left\{([w]_{\sim_{L}},\sigma,[w\sigma]_{\sim_{L}})|\ w\in\Sigma^{*},\sigma\in\Sigma\right\}

•

l_{0}=[\epsilon]_{\sim_{L}}

•

F=\left\{[w]_{\sim_{L}}|\ w\in\text{dom}(f)\right\}

Now we can define the outputs of the bimachine:

•

\omega([u]_{\sim_{L}},\sigma,[w]_{\mathcal{R}})={\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)

•

\lambda([w]_{\mathcal{R}})=\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(\epsilon)
for
w\in\text{dom}(f)

•

\rho([u]_{\sim_{L}})={\widehat{f}_{[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(u)
for
u\in\text{dom}(f)

Since we take a definition of
\sim_{L}
slightly different from
[
RS91
]
, we show, using the same idea as in
[
Cho03
]
for sequential transductions, that
\sim_{L}
is
indeed a right congruence and that the outputs of the bimachine are
well-defined.

Lemma 6
.

\sim_{L}
is a right congruence and the output functions of

\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{B})
are well-defined.

Proof 3.1
.

Let
u\sim_{L}v
,
\sigma
be a letter and let
w
be a word in
{(u\sigma)}^{-1}\text{dom}(f)
.
We want to show that
{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)}^{-1}f(u\sigma w)={\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v\sigma)}^{-1}f(v\sigma w)
.
We know that
{\widehat{f}_{[z]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(uz)={\widehat{f}_{[z]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(vz)
for
z\in u^{-1}\text{dom}(f)
, and let us denote this word by
g(z)
.
Then
f(u\sigma w)=\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)g(\sigma w)
and we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)}^{-1}f(u\sigma w)&=&\left(\bigwedge_{z\in[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}f(u\sigma z)\right)^{-1}f(u\sigma w)\\
&=&\left(\bigwedge_{z\in[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)g(\sigma z)\right)^{-1}f(u\sigma w)\\
&=&\left(\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)\bigwedge_{z\in[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}g(\sigma z)\right)^{-1}f(u\sigma w)\\
&=&\left(\bigwedge_{z\in[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}g(\sigma z)\right)^{-1}{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(u\sigma w)\\
&=&\left(\bigwedge_{z\in[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}g(\sigma z)\right)^{-1}{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(v\sigma w)\\
&=&{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v\sigma)}^{-1}f(v\sigma w)\end{array}

Hence
u\sigma\sim_{L}v\sigma
and
\sim_{L}
is indeed a right
congruence.

We only have left to show that the output functions

\omega,\lambda,\rho
are
well-defined
i.e.
, they do not depend on the choices of
u
and

w
. First, it is rather immediate by definition of
\widehat{f}_{[w]}
that this function does not depend on
w
, and therefore by
definition of
\omega
and
\lambda
, these two functions do not
depend on the choice of
w
. Now, let us show that
\omega
does not
depend on the choice of
u
either,
i.e.
,
{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)={\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v\sigma)
for
u\sim_{L}v
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)&=&{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)\bigwedge_{z\in[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}g(\sigma z)\\
&=&\bigwedge_{z\in[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}g(\sigma z)\\
&=&{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v\sigma)\end{array}

Finally, by applying the definition of
\sim_{L}
to
w=\epsilon
, one
immediately gets
\rho([u]_{\sim_{L}})=\rho([v]_{\sim_{L}})
for
u,v
such that
u\sim_{L}v
and
u,v\in\text{dom}(f)
.

Symmetrically, one can define
\mathit{Right}_{f}(\mathcal{L})
and

\mathit{Right}_{f}(\mathcal{B})
.
The correctness and effectiveness of these constructions was shown in
[
RS91
]
:

Lemma 7

(
[
RS91
]
)
.

\mathit{Right}_{f}(\mathcal{B})
and
\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{B})
are equivalent to

\mathcal{B}
, and are both computable.

The following proposition was shown in the case of total transductions in
[
RS91
]
.
In our setting (
i.e.
, when both automata recognize the domain) we are able to extend it to arbitrary transductions.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a bimachine with automata
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
.
Then
\mathcal{L}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
and
\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L})
.

Proof 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho\right)
be a bimachine realizing
f
.
We will show that
\mathcal{L}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
and the proof for the right automaton is symmetrical.
Let
u\sim_{\mathcal{L}}v
for two words
u,v
, we want to show that
u\sim_{L}v
(using the same notation as above).
Since
\mathcal{L}
recognizes
\text{dom}(f)
, we know that for any word
w
,
uw\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow vw\in\text{dom}(f)
.
We only need to show that for any word
w\in u^{-1}\text{dom}(f)
,
{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(uw)={\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(vw)
.
Let
w\in u^{-1}\text{dom}(f)
, we have that
f(uw)=\lambda([uw]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([\epsilon]_{\mathcal{L}},u,[w]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},w,[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([uw]_{\mathcal{L}})
.
We obtain
\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)=\lambda([uw]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([\epsilon]_{\mathcal{L}},u,[w]_{\mathcal{R}})\alpha([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[w]_{\mathcal{R}})
where:

\alpha([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[w]_{\mathcal{R}})=\bigwedge\left\{\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},z,[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([uz]_{\mathcal{L}})|\ z\sim_{\mathcal{R}}w\right\}

Finally we have:

\displaystyle{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(uw)

\displaystyle={\alpha([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[w]_{\mathcal{R}})}^{-1}\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},w,[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([uw]_{\mathcal{L}})

\displaystyle={\alpha([v]_{\mathcal{L}},[w]_{\mathcal{R}})}^{-1}\omega([v]_{\mathcal{L}},w,[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([vw]_{\mathcal{L}})

\displaystyle={\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(vw)

The following result is an immediate consequence of the latter proposition:

Corollary 9
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho)
be a bimachine. Then

\begin{array}[]{llllll}\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B})&\in&\text{Min}\{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\mathcal{R},\omega^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\mid\llbracket\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{B}\rrbracket\}\\
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{B})&\in&\text{Min}\{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\mid\llbracket\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{B}\rrbracket\}\end{array}

Proof 3.3
.

By definition,
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B})
has
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
and

\mathcal{R}
as left and right automata respectively. Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be a
bimachine with
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{R}
as left and right automata
such that
\llbracket\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{B}\rrbracket
. By
Proposition
8
, one gets
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
. The result for
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{B})
is shown symmetrically.

3.2.2.
Bimachine minimization

By applying left and right minimization successively, we show that we
obtain a minimal bimachine. According to
Lemma
7
, these two steps are effective, and we
call this procedure
bimachine minimization
.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a bimachine realizing a transduction
f
. Then
\mathit{Left}(\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{B}))

and
\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
are minimal bimachines realizing

f
. Moreover,
\mathcal{B}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Left}(\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{B}))
and
\mathcal{B}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
.

Proof 3.4
.

We only show the result for
\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
, the other
result being proved symmetrically. Let
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho)
.
Suppose that
\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
is not minimal and let

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
be a bimachine realizing
f
such that

\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))\sqsubseteq\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. Then,

\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
has
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
as left
automaton, and
\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}))\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
as right
automaton.

By Proposition
8
, we have

\mathcal{L}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
and
\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}))
, hence

\mathcal{L}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\qquad\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}))\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}

Since
\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
, we can restrict the right automaton of

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{R}

and obtain a bimachine realizing
f
with

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
as left automaton, and
\mathcal{R}
as right automaton. This
bimachine just ignores the extra information given by
\mathcal{R}
, by
setting its output function to
([u]_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}})\mapsto\omega^{\prime}([u]_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}})
. If
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is strictly coarser than

\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
, this contradicts the minimality of

\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
among bimachines realizing
f
with
\mathcal{R}
as
right automaton (Corollary
9
).

Now, suppose that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is strictly coarser than

\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}))
. We also show a contradiction, by a
similar argument. Since
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, we can
restrict the left automaton of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
to
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
(the
output function ignores the finer information given by

\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
) and obtain a bimachine realizing
f
with

\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
as left automaton, and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
as right
automaton. This yields a contradiction since
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is strictly
finer than
\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}))
and

\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}))
is the minimal automaton among
bimachines realizing
f
with
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
as right
automaton.

3.2.3.
Minimizing bimachines in
PTime

According to
Lemma
7
(shown in
[
RS91
]
), we know that

\mathit{Left}(\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{B}))
and
\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
are
computable, for any bimachine
\mathcal{B}
. We show here that it can be done
in
PTime
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a bimachine. One can compute
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B})
and
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{B})
in
PTime
.

Proof 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho\right)
be a bimachine realizing a transduction
f
, with automata
\mathcal{L}=\left(Q_{\mathcal{L}},\Delta_{\mathcal{L}},\left\{l_{0}\right\},F_{\mathcal{L}}\right)
and
\mathcal{R}=\left(Q_{\mathcal{R}},\Delta_{\mathcal{R}},\left\{r_{0}\right\},F_{\mathcal{R}}\right)
.
The algorithm to obtain
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B})
works in two steps: (1) make the outputs earliest, (2) minimize the state space of the left automaton.

Step 1:

We construct
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}\right)
, a bimachine with the same automata but with the earliest outputs, given the automaton
\mathcal{R}
.

\omega^{\prime}([u]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}})={\widehat{f}_{[\sigma v]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[v]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)\quad\lambda^{\prime}([v]_{\mathcal{R}})=\widehat{f}_{[v]_{\mathcal{R}}}(\epsilon)\quad\rho^{\prime}([u]_{\mathcal{L}})={\widehat{f}_{[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(u)

These values can be computed in polynomial time, we use the same idea described in
[
Cho03
, Section 5]
, which can be seen as the special case when the right automaton is trivial, and we give a sketch of the procedure.
We first remark that for any words
u,v

\widehat{f}_{[v]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)=\lambda([uv]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([\epsilon]_{\mathcal{L}},u,[v]_{\mathcal{R}})\alpha([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[v]_{\mathcal{R}})
with:

\alpha([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[v]_{\mathcal{R}})=\bigwedge\left\{w|\ \exists x\in[v]_{\mathcal{R}},\ \omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},x,[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([ux]_{\mathcal{R}})=w\right\}

As in
[
Cho03
]
, in order to compute the values
\alpha([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[v]_{\mathcal{R}})
, we consider the directed graph of the automaton
\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{R}
, with the edges
(([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[\sigma v]_{\mathcal{R}}),([u\sigma]_{\mathcal{L}},[v]_{\mathcal{R}}))
labelled by the outputs of
\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}})
.
In order to account for the outputs of final functions, we add two vertices, a source
s
pointing to the initial states,
i.e.
, states
([\epsilon]_{\mathcal{L}},[v]_{\mathcal{R}})
for
v\in\text{dom}(f)
, with an edge labelled by
\lambda([v]_{\mathcal{R}})
, and a target
t
pointing to states
([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})
with an edge labelled by
\rho([u]_{\mathcal{L}})
.
The value
\alpha([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[v]_{\mathcal{R}})
is obtained as the longest common prefix of the labels of all the paths starting at
([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[v]_{\mathcal{R}})
and ending in
t
.
According to
[
Cho03
]
these values can be computed in polynomial time.

Step 2:

Now we describe a minimization algorithm in the vein of Moore’s minimization algorithm of DFAs.
The idea is to compute an equivalence relation over the state space of
\mathcal{L}
, by successive refinements.
The only difference with Moore’s algorithm is that the initial equivalence relation must be compatible with the output functions.
More precisely, if we identify states of
\mathcal{L}
with classes of
\sim_{\mathcal{L}}
:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}[u]_{\mathcal{L}}\sim_{0}[v]_{\mathcal{L}}&\text{if}&[u]_{\mathcal{L}}\in F_{\mathcal{L}}\Leftrightarrow[v]_{\mathcal{L}}\in F_{\mathcal{L}},\ \rho^{\prime}([u]_{\mathcal{L}})=\rho^{\prime}([v]_{\mathcal{L}}),\\
&&\text{and }\forall w,\sigma,\ \omega^{\prime}([u]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[w]_{\mathcal{R}})=\omega^{\prime}([v]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[w]_{\mathcal{R}})\\
[u]_{\mathcal{L}}\sim_{i+1}[v]_{\mathcal{L}}&\text{if}&[u]_{\mathcal{L}}\sim_{i}[v]_{\mathcal{L}}\text{ and }\forall\sigma,\ [u\sigma]_{\mathcal{L}}\sim_{i}[v\sigma]_{\mathcal{L}}\end{array}

Since
{\sim_{i+1}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{i}}
for any
i
, this sequence of relations converges in at most
|\mathcal{L}|
steps to a relation we denote by
\sim_{*}
.
Moreover,
\sim_{0}
can be computed in
PTime
and each
\sim_{i+1}
can also be computed in
PTime
from
\sim_{i}
.
The relation
\sim_{*}
can be naturally extended to words by
u\sim_{*}v
if
[u]_{\mathcal{L}}\sim_{*}[v]_{\mathcal{L}}
.

We want to show that the algorithm is correct meaning that
{\sim_{*}}={\sim_{L}}
, using the same notation as in
3.2.1
.
We show by induction on
i\geq 0
that
{\sim_{L}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{i}}
, and thus
{\sim_{L}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{*}}
.
Let
u\sim_{L}v
, we have that
u\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow v\in\text{dom}(f)
,
{\widehat{f}_{[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(u)={\widehat{f}_{[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(v)
, and for all
w,\sigma
we have
{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)={\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v\sigma)
, hence
u\sim_{0}v
.
Now let us assume that
{\sim_{L}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{i}}
for some integer
i
.
Let
u\sim_{L}v
, we have
u\sim_{i}v
, and for all
\sigma
,
u\sigma\sim_{L}v\sigma
hence
u\sigma\sim_{i}v\sigma
and
u\sim_{i+1}v
.

Conversely, let us show by induction on
i
that if
u\sim_{i}v
then for any
w\in\Sigma^{i}
,
uw\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow vw\in\text{dom}(f)
and
{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(uw)={\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(vw)
when
uw\in\text{dom}(f)
.
If
u\sim_{0}v
then
u\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow v\in\text{dom}(f)
and when
u\in\text{dom}(f)
then
{\widehat{f}_{[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(u)={\widehat{f}_{[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(v)
by definition of
\sim_{0}
.
Let us assume that the proposition holds at some rank
i
, and let
u\sim_{i+1}v
.
Let
w=\sigma w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{i+1}
. We have by assumption that
u\sigma\sim_{i}v\sigma
which means that
u\sigma w^{\prime}\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow v\sigma w^{\prime}\in\text{dom}(f)
.
Furthermore, if
u\sigma w^{\prime}\in\text{dom}(f)
then
{\widehat{f}_{[w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)}^{-1}f(u\sigma w^{\prime})={\widehat{f}_{[w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v\sigma)}^{-1}f(v\sigma w^{\prime})
since
u\sigma\sim_{i}v\sigma
.
Since in particular,
u\sim_{0}v
then
{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)={\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v\sigma)
.
Finally we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(u\sigma w^{\prime})&=&\left({\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)\right){\widehat{f}_{[w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u\sigma)}^{-1}f(u\sigma w^{\prime})\\
&=&\left({\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v\sigma)\right){\widehat{f}_{[w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v\sigma)}^{-1}f(v\sigma w^{\prime})\\
&=&{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w^{\prime}]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(v\sigma w^{\prime})\end{array}

Hence
u\sim_{i+1}v
which concludes the induction.
Since
{\sim_{*}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{i}}
for any
i
, we have that if
u\sim_{*}v
then for any
w\in\Sigma^{*}
,
uw\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow vw\in\text{dom}(f)
and
{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(uw)={\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(vw)
when
uw\in\text{dom}(f)
. In particular we have shown that
{\sim_{*}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{L}}
and finally
{\sim_{*}}={\sim_{L}}
.

As a result of applying twice this procedure successively, one obtains
that bimachine minimization is in
PTime
.

Corollary 12
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a bimachine. The minimal bimachines
\mathit{Left}(\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{B}))
and

\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
can be computed in
PTime
.

3.3.
Canonical bimachine

We have seen how to minimize the left automaton of a bimachine with
respect to a fixed right automaton. This minimization is canonical in
the following sense: as long as two bimachines
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}
realize the same
transduction and have the same right automaton, the left minimization
produces the same bimachine
\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{B}_{1})=\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{B}_{2})
. We describe here a
canonical way of defining a right automaton
\mathcal{R}_{f}
, which
yields, by left minimisation, a canonical bimachine denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{f}
. This
canonical bimachine has been initially defined in
[
RS91
]
. We recall its construction and exhibit some of
its useful properties.

3.3.1.
Canonical right automaton

The contribution of
[
RS91
]
relies on the existence of a canonical left congruence which yields a canonical right automaton.
This canonical right automaton can be thought of as the minimal amount of look-ahead information needed to realize the transduction.

The
left congruence
of a transduction
f
is defined by

u\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}v
if for any word
w
,
wu\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow wv\in\text{dom}(f)
and
\sup\left\{\parallel\!\!{f(wu),f(wv)}\!\!\parallel|\ wu\in\text{dom}(f)\right\}<\infty
.
Intuitively, this congruence says that the two suffixes
u

and
v
have the same effect with respect to membership to the domain,
and that
f(wu)
and
f(wv)
are equal up to a suffix the length of
which is bounded by some constant depending only on
u
and
v
.
For a rational transduction, this congruence has always finite index.
The converse does not hold, however a transduction is rational if and only its left congruence has finite index and it preserves rational languages by inverse image as it was shown in
[
RS91
]
.
For the rest of this section,
[w]_{\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}}
will be denoted by
[w]
.
The
canonical right automaton
of
f
is defined naturally from
f
:
\mathcal{R}_{f}=\left(\Sigma^{*}/_{\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}},\Delta,\left\{[\epsilon]\right\},F\right)
with
\Delta=\left\{\left([\sigma w],\sigma,[w]\right)|\ w\in\Sigma^{*},\sigma\in\Sigma\right\}
and
F=\left\{[w]|\ w\in\text{dom}(f)\right\}
.

We define symmetrically
\mathrel{\rightharpoonup}_{f}
the
right congruence
of a transduction
f
, and the associated left automaton
\mathcal{L}_{f}
.

The automaton
\mathcal{R}_{f}
(resp.
\mathcal{L}_{f}
) is minimal in the sense that any bimachine realizing the same function must have a finer right (resp. left) automaton.

Proposition 13
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho\right)
be a bimachine realizing
f
.
Then
\mathcal{L}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}_{f}
and
\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{f}
.

Proof 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho\right)
be a bimachine realizing
f
.
We only show that
\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{f}
, the proof for the left automaton being symmetrical.
Let
u\sim_{\mathcal{R}}v
for two words
u,v
, we want to show that
u\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}v
.
We know that
\mathcal{R}
recognizes
\text{dom}(f)
, hence for any word
w
,
wu\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow wv\in\text{dom}(f)
.
Let
w
be a word such that
wu\in\text{dom}(f)
.
Since
[u]_{\mathcal{R}}=[v]_{\mathcal{R}}
we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f(wu)&=&\lambda([wu]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([\epsilon]_{\mathcal{L}},w,[u]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([w]_{\mathcal{L}},u,[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([wu]_{\mathcal{L}})\\
f(wv)&=&\lambda([wu]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([\epsilon]_{\mathcal{L}},w,[u]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([w]_{\mathcal{L}},v,[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([wv]_{\mathcal{L}})\end{array}

Hence we can bound the distance between
f(wu)
and
f(wv)
regardless of
w
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\parallel\!\!{f(wu),f(wv)}\!\!\parallel&\leq&|\omega([w]_{\mathcal{L}},u,[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([wu]_{\mathcal{L}})|+|\omega([w]_{\mathcal{L}},v,[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([wv]_{\mathcal{L}})|\\
&\leq&k(|u|+|v|+2)\end{array}

where
k
is the maximum length of a word in the ranges of
\omega
and
\rho
(considering
\omega
over single letters).
Hence we have shown that
u\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}v
which concludes the proof.

Let us show a very similar proposition which will help establish the equivalence between
C
-transducers and
C
-bimachines.

Proposition 14
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a transducer, with underlying automaton
\mathcal{A}
, realizing a transduction
f
.
Then
{\approx_{\mathcal{A}}}\sqsubseteq{\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}}
and
{\approx_{\mathcal{A}}}\sqsubseteq{\mathrel{\rightharpoonup}_{f}}
.

Proof 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=\left(\mathcal{A},\mathpzc{o},\mathpzc{i},\mathpzc{t}\right)
be a transducer realizing
f
with
\mathcal{A}=\left(Q,\Delta,I,F\right)
its underlying automaton.
We will show that
{\approx_{\mathcal{A}}}\sqsubseteq{\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}}
, the proof for the right congruence being symmetrical.
Let
u\approx_{\mathcal{A}}v
, we have to show that
u\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}v
.
Since
\mathcal{A}
recognizes the domain of
f
, we have for any word
w
that
wu\in\text{dom}(f)\Leftrightarrow wv\in\text{dom}(f)
.
Let
w\in u^{-1}\text{dom}(f)
, we want to show that
\parallel\!\!{f(wu),f(wv)}\!\!\parallel
does not depend on
w
.
Let
q_{I}\xrightarrow{w|x}_{\mathcal{T}}q\xrightarrow{u|y}_{\mathcal{T}}q_{F}
denote an accepting run of

over

Then there is an accepting run

Let

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\parallel\!\!{f(wu),f(wv)}\!\!\parallel&=&\parallel\!\!{ixyt,ixzt}\!\!\parallel\\

&\leq&k(|u|+|v|+2)\end{array}

where

\mathpzc{o}

.

3.3.2.
The canonical bimachine

We can now define the canonical bimachine from
[
RS91
]
.
Note that for a given transduction
f
, realized by a bimachine
\mathcal{B}
, the left minimization of
\mathcal{B}
only depends on the right automaton of the bimachine.
Hence we can define the
canonical bimachine
associated with
f
by
\mathcal{B}_{f}=\left(\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{R}_{f}),\mathcal{R}_{f},\omega_{f},\lambda_{f},\rho_{f}\right)
with:

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\omega_{f}([u]_{\sim_{L}},\sigma,[w]_{\mathcal{R}_{f}})&=&{\widehat{f}_{[\sigma w]_{\mathcal{R}_{f}}}(u)}^{-1}\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}_{f}}}(u\sigma)&\\
\lambda_{f}([w]_{\mathcal{R}_{f}})&=&\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}_{f}}}(\epsilon)&\text{for }w\in\text{dom}(f)\\
\rho_{f}([u]_{\sim_{L}})&=&{\widehat{f}_{[\epsilon]_{\mathcal{R}_{f}}}(u)}^{-1}f(u)&\text{for }u\in\text{dom}(f)\end{array}

This bimachine is called canonical because its definition does not depend on the description of
f
.
Furthermore, as it was shown in
[
RS91
]
, this machine is computable from a transducer (or a bimachine) realizing
f
.
Again by symmetry, there is actually a second canonical bimachine with
\mathcal{L}_{f}
and
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f})
as its automata.
Note that the canonical bimachine is, in particular, minimal: right minimization of the canonical bimachine cannot yield a coarser right automaton than
\mathcal{R}_{f}
(Proposition
13
).

Algebraic characterization of rational transductions

The purpose of this section is to give a characterization of

C

C
is a decidable
class of congruences. First, we show that
C
-transductions, which
by definition are the transductions realized by (functional but possibly ambiguous)
C
-transducers,
also correspond to the transductions realized by the bimachines whose
left and right automata are both
C

C

straightforward as it may seem and relies on bimachine minimization.
Indeed,
unambiguous

equivalent to
C
-bimachines, as it was stated in
[
RS95
]

of functional transducers (except for
\textbf{C}=\mathbf{A}

[
FGL16b
]
). In other words, we (non-trivially) strengthen the known
correspondence between functional transducers and unambiguous
transducers
[
Eil74
]
to
C
-transducers, which by
[
RS95
]
implies that (functional)
C
-transducers and

C
-bimachines coincide.

The canonical bimachine defined in the previous section,
while being canonical, cannot be used to test
C
-rationality in general:

4.1.

Here we show in two steps that

.
The

of a bimachine
\mathcal{B}
with automata
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
is defined as
{\approx_{\mathcal{B}}}={\approx_{\mathcal{L}}\sqcap\approx_{\mathcal{R}}}
.
Intuitively two words are equivalent with respect to
\mathcal{B}
if they are equivalent with respect to both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a bimachine realizing
f
, then there exists an unambiguous transducer realizing
f
with underlying automaton
\mathcal{A}
such that
{\approx_{\mathcal{B}}}\sqsubseteq{\approx_{\mathcal{A}}}
.

Proof 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho\right)
be a bimachine with automata
\mathcal{L}=\left(L,\Delta_{\mathcal{L}},\left\{l_{0}\right\},F_{\mathcal{L}}\right)
and
\mathcal{R}=\left(R,\Delta_{\mathcal{R}},\left\{r_{0}\right\},F_{\mathcal{R}}\right)
.
We construct a transducer
\mathcal{T}=\left(\mathcal{A},\mathpzc{o},\mathpzc{i},\mathpzc{t}\right)

Let us define
\mathcal{A}
as the product of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
:

•

•

Since
\mathcal{L}
and

both recognize

,

also recognizes
\text{dom}(f)
and for any word

.
Now we can define the outputs of

•

for

•

for

The transducer
\mathcal{T}
is equivalent to

by construction, so we only have left to show that

.
Let

.

.

.

.

\mathcal{T}=\left(\mathcal{A},\mathpzc{o},\mathpzc{i},\mathpzc{t}\right)

\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}),\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho\right)

Let
u,v
be two words and let us assume that

,

and
{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(uw)={\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(vw)
if

The first condition is fulfilled, since
\mathcal{A}

Let
\left\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\right\}

For
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,k\right\}
let
I\xrightarrow{u|x_{i}}_{\mathcal{T}}p_{i}
and

denote the runs over
u
and
v
, respectively, where the outputs

are uniquely defined because
\mathcal{T}
realises a function.
For any word
z
in
[w]_{\mathcal{R}}
, and any

there is a run on
z
from
p_{i}
to a final state, since

and

are equivalent for
\mathcal{R}
.
For

we define
\alpha_{i}=\bigwedge\left\{\alpha\mathpzc{t}(q)|\ p_{i}\xrightarrow{z|\alpha}_{\mathcal{T}}q,\ q\in F,\ z\in[w]_{\mathcal{R}}\right\}
.
By its definition we have for any

\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)=x_{i}\alpha_{i}

\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)=y_{i}\alpha_{i}

In particular for

Finally we obtain:

\displaystyle{\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(u)}^{-1}f(uw)

\displaystyle={(x_{1}\alpha_{1})}^{-1}x_{1}\alpha_{1}\beta

\displaystyle=\beta

\displaystyle={(y_{1}\alpha_{1})}^{-1}y_{1}\alpha_{1}\beta

\displaystyle={\widehat{f}_{[w]_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)}^{-1}f(vw)

A direct consequence of Propositions

and
17
is that

-bimachines characterize

-transductions.

Corollary 18
.

Let

be a congruence class.
A transduction is
C
-rational if and only if it can be realized by a

-bimachine.

Remark 19
.

Going from a bimachine to an unambiguous transducer is done in

.
However, as shown in the proof of Proposition

, going from a transducer to a bimachine yields, in general, an exponentially larger bimachine and is thus in

.

4.2.
Bounding minimal bimachines

In this subsection we show
that any minimal bimachine realizing a transduction
f

is bounded in size by some constant that depends only on
f
. It is
based on properties of the canonical bimachine and implies in particular that the
number of minimal bimachines is finite (up to equivalence under
\mathrel{\boxminus}
, see Subsection
3.2
),
a result that was left open in
[
RS91
]
.

The following proposition intuitively shows that the more information
you put in the right automaton of a bimachine, the less information
you need in the left automaton, and symmetrically.

Proposition 20
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
be bimachines realizing a transduction
f

with automata
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2},\mathcal{R}_{2}
,
respectively. The following two implications hold true:

\mathcal{R}_{1}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{2}\implies\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{2})\sqsubseteq\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{1})
and

\mathcal{L}_{1}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}_{2}\implies\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{2})\sqsubseteq\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{1})
.

Proof 4.4
.

We only show the result for right automata.
The idea of the proof is that
\mathcal{R}_{1}
gives more information than
\mathcal{R}_{2}
, hence
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
must compute more information than
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{1})
in order to realize
f
.
We want to show that there exists a bimachine realizing
f
with automata
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
and
\mathcal{R}_{1}
.
This will show according to Proposition
8
that
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{2})\sqsubseteq\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{1})
.
We can assume that
\mathcal{R}_{1}
is accessible without loss of generality.
Identifying states of
\mathcal{R}_{1}
with equivalence classes of
\sim_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
, we have a well defined function
\pi:\Sigma^{*}/_{\sim_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}/_{\sim_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}}
since
\mathcal{R}_{1}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{2}
.
Hence if we consider
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}_{2})=(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{2}),\mathcal{R}_{2},\omega_{2},\lambda_{2},\rho_{2})
, then we can define
\mathcal{B}=(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{2}),\mathcal{R}_{1},\omega,\lambda_{2},\rho_{2}\circ\pi)
where
\omega([w]_{\mathcal{R}_{1}},\sigma,l)=\omega_{2}(\pi([w]_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}),\sigma,l)
.
By construction
\mathcal{B}
realizes
f
, which concludes the proof.

We now have all the ingredients necessary to bound the size of the
minimal bimachines.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho)
be a bimachine
realizing a transduction
f
. If
\mathcal{B}
is minimal, then

\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}
and
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}
.

Proof 4.5
.

We will show the lemma for
\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
.
Assume that
\mathcal{B}
is minimal. Consider the bimachine

\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
. According to
Proposition
10
, we have

\mathcal{B}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
but since
\mathcal{B}
is minimal, it
implies that the left and right automata of
\mathcal{B}
and

\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
are the same, up to renaming of their
states. The only possible difference between
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
lies in
their output functions. Therefore, we can assume that

\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
for
some output functions
\omega^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}
,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{L}=\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})
and
\mathcal{R}=\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}))
.

Intuitively, since
\mathcal{R}_{f}
contains the minimum information needed for a right automaton,
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})
contains the maximum information needed for a left automaton, and any additional information should be removed by minimizing.
We first apply the left minimization and obtain
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})
.
According to Proposition
13
, we have

\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{f}
which implies, by
Proposition
20
, that

\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})\sqsubseteq\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})=\mathcal{L}
.
We then use the same reasoning for the second minimization:
According to Proposition
13
, we have

\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}_{f}
and by
Proposition
20
, we get

\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f})\sqsubseteq\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}))=\mathcal{R}
.

Theorem 22
.

Let
f
be a transduction. The set of minimal bimachines realizing

f
is finite (up to
\mathrel{\boxminus}
).
Moreover, if
f
is given by a transducer or a bimachine, one can
compute a set of representatives of each class of minimal
bimachines.

Proof 4.6
.

The first statement is a direct consequence of
Lemma
21
, since there are
finitely many left and right automata
coarser than
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})
and
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f})
.

Now, to compute a set of representatives, we compute the set

X=\{\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))\mid\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho),\llbracket\mathcal{B}\rrbracket=f\text{ and }\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{f}\}

Before proving that this set is finite and is indeed a set of
representatives, let us explain how to compute it. First, by
[
RS91
]
, the
canonical bimachine
\mathcal{B}_{f}=(\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{R}_{f}),\mathcal{R}_{f},\omega_{f},\lambda_{f},\rho_{f})
(defined in Section
3.3
) is computable if
f
is given
by a transducer or a bimachine. By symmetry, so is the canonical
bimachine with left and right automata
\mathcal{L}_{f}
and

\mathit{Right}_{f}(\mathcal{L}_{f})
respectively. Then, the computation of
X

is done as follows:

(1)

compute the canonical bimachine
\mathcal{B}_{f}=(\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{R}_{f}),\mathcal{R}_{f},\omega_{f},\lambda_{f},\rho_{f})
,

(2)

pick a right automaton
\mathcal{R}
such that

\mathit{Right}_{f}(\mathcal{L}_{f})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{f}
(there are
finitely many up to state renaming) and
\llbracket\mathcal{R}\rrbracket=\text{dom}(f)
,

(3)

let
\mathcal{B}=(\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{R}_{f}),\mathcal{R},\omega^{\prime}_{f},\lambda^{\prime}_{f},\rho_{f})
where the output functions
\omega^{\prime}_{f}
and

\lambda^{\prime}_{f}
are defined by

\omega^{\prime}_{f}([u]_{\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{R}_{f})},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}})=\omega_{f}([u]_{\mathit{Left}_{f}(\mathcal{R}_{f})},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}_{f}})
and
\lambda^{\prime}_{f}([v]_{\mathcal{R}})=\lambda_{f}([v]_{\mathcal{R}_{f}})
.
It is well-defined since
\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{f}
,

(4)

compute
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))

(Theorem
11
) and add
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
to
X
,

(5)

go back to step 2 as long as there is a right
automaton
\mathcal{R}
still left to pick.

It remains to prove that
X
is finite, and
that
X
is a set of representatives. To show that
X
is finite,
it suffices to remark that given two bimachines
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and

\mathcal{B}_{2}
with the same right automaton
\mathcal{R}
, and defining the
same transduction, we have

\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}_{1}))=\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}_{2}))
. It is
direct by definition of the operation
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B})

which ignores the left automaton of
\mathcal{B}
as well as its output
functions, and only depends on its right automaton and
f
(and
symmetrically for the operation
\mathit{Right}(.)
). Then, finiteness
is due to the fact that only right automata
\mathcal{R}
such that

\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{f}
are considered, and
there are finitely many of them.

Finally, we show that
X
is a set of representatives, i.e., for
any minimal bimachine
\mathcal{B}
, there exists
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\in X
such that

\mathcal{B}\mathrel{\boxminus}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. By Lemma
21
and
Proposition
13
, if
\mathcal{R}
is the right automaton
of
\mathcal{B}
, then
\mathit{Right}_{f}(\mathcal{L}_{f})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{R}_{f}
. Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))
. Then clearly

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\in X
. By Proposition
10
we have

\mathcal{B}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Right}(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{B}))=\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, and by minimality
of
\mathcal{B}
, one obtains that
\mathcal{B}\mathrel{\boxminus}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.

4.3.
Characterization of
C
-rationality and decision

4.3.1.
Exhaustive search

If
C
is a decidable class of congruences, then Theorem
22

implies that
C
-rationality is decidable. Indeed, it suffices
to compute a set of representatives of the minimal bimachines
realizing a transduction
f
(given for instance by a transducer),
and then to test whether one of them is in
C
.

Lemma 23
.

Let
C
be a congruence class and let
f
be a transduction.
Then
f
is a
C
-transduction if and only if one of its minimal bimachines is a
C
-bimachine.

Proof 4.7
.

If a minimal bimachine of
f
is a
C
-bimachine, then
f
is a
C
-transduction according to Corollary
18
.
Conversely, let us assume that
f
is realized by a
C
-bimachine.
Then according to Proposition
10

\mathit{Left}(\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{B}))
is a minimal bimachine realizing
f
and coarser than
\mathcal{B}
.
Hence
\mathit{Left}(\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{B}))
is a minimal
C
-bimachine.

Theorem 24
.

Let
C
be a decidable congruence class.
Then, given a transducer, one can decide if it realizes a
C
-transduction.

Proof 4.8
.

According to Theorem
22
we can compute the minimal bimachines of
f
, and from Lemma
23
we only need to check if one of the minimal bimachines is a
C
-bimachine.

4.3.2.
Alternative characterization

In this section we consider a slightly different characterization which only requires to compute one minimal bimachine to check
C
-rationality.

Let
C
be a congruence class and let
f
be a transduction.
We define, if it exists,
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
as the finest
C
-automaton coarser than

\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})
and finer than
\mathcal{L}_{f}
. Let us define the right congruence

\sim_{f}^{\textbf{C}}=\bigsqcap\left\{\sim_{\mathcal{L}}\mid\ {\mathcal{L}}\ \text{ is a
}\textbf{C}\text{-automaton s.t. }{{\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})}}\sqsubseteq{\mathcal{L}}\right\}
. If
{\sim_{f}^{\textbf{C}}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{\mathcal{L}_{f}}}
then we define
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
as the left automaton associated with
\sim_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
and recognizing
\text{dom}(f)
.
We obtain the following characterization of
C
-rationality:

Lemma 25
.

Let
C
be a congruence class and let
f
be a transduction.
Then
f
is a
C
-transduction if and only if the two following conditions are satisfied:

•

{\sim_{f}^{\textbf{C}}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{\mathcal{L}_{f}}}

•

\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}})
is a
C
-automaton

Proof 4.9
.

Let
f
be a transduction.
We know from Lemma
23
that
f
is a

C
-transduction if and only if it is realized by a
minimal
C
-bimachine. Now, let us assume that
f
is realized by a
minimal
C
-bimachine
\mathcal{B}
with left and right automata
\mathcal{L}
and

\mathcal{R}
respectively.
We want to show that
{\sim_{f}^{\textbf{C}}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{\mathcal{L}_{f}}}

and
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}})
is a
C
-automaton. By
Lemma
21
we know that
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}
. By definition of
\sim_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
, it is finer than
\sim_{\mathcal{L}}
since
\mathcal{L}
is a

C
-automaton coarser than
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})
. By
Proposition
13
, we have
\mathcal{L}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}_{f}
and
hence
{\sim_{f}^{\textbf{C}}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{\mathcal{L}_{f}}}
. It remains to show that

\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}})
is a
C
-automaton. First, there is a
bimachine
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
with left automaton
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
which realizes
f
,
obtained by substituting the left automaton
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{B}
by

\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
(recall that
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}
) and changing
the output function so that the extra information given by

\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
is just ignored. Then, by minimizing the right
automaton of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
(whose right automaton is
\mathcal{R}
), one obtains

\mathcal{R}\sqsubseteq\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}})
. Since
\mathcal{R}
is a

C
-automaton, so is
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}})
.

Conversely, suppose that
{\sim_{f}^{\textbf{C}}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{\mathcal{L}_{f}}}
and

\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}})
is a
C
-automaton. We show the existence
of a
C
-bimachine realizing
f
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{f}=(\mathcal{L}_{f},\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}),\omega_{f},\lambda_{f},\rho_{f})
be a
canonical bimachine associated with
f
. We can turn this bimachine
into a
C
-bimachine. First, since
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{L}_{f}
,
one can substitute in
\mathcal{B}_{f}
the left automaton
\mathcal{L}_{f}
by

\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
, change its outputs so that they ignore the extra
information given by
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
, and obtain a bimachine realizing

f
with
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
as left automaton. By applying once the right
minimization on this new bimachine, one obtains a
C
-bimachine realizing

f
with
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
and
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}})
as left and
right automata respectively.

Note that we could define
\mathcal{R}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
symmetrically and have a similar characterization.

Now we define a bimachine
\mathcal{B}_{f,\textbf{C}}
, which will be a
C
-bimachine if and only if

is a
C
-transduction.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{f,\textbf{C}}=\mathcal{L}_{f}\sqcap\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
, if
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}}
exists, and
\mathcal{L}_{f,\textbf{C}}=\mathcal{L}_{f}
otherwise.
Then
\mathcal{B}_{f,\textbf{C}}
is the bimachine obtained with left automaton
\mathcal{L}_{f,\textbf{C}}
, and right automaton
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f,\textbf{C}})
which is well defined since by definition,

.

Theorem 26
.

Let
C
be a congruence class and let
f
be a transduction.
Then
f
is a
C
-transduction if and only if the bimachine
\mathcal{B}_{f,\textbf{C}}
is a
C
-bimachine.

Proof 4.10
.

The proof mainly relies on the previous lemma.
First, if
\mathcal{B}_{f,\textbf{C}}
is a

-transduction, since it realizes

by definition, then
f
is a
C
-transduction by Corollary

.
Conversely, if
f
a
C
-transduction then by Lemma

, we have both:

•

{\sim_{f}^{\textbf{C}}}\sqsubseteq{\sim_{\mathcal{L}_{f}}}

•

\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}})
is a

-automaton

Hence

which is a

-automaton and
\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f,\textbf{C}})=\mathit{Right}(\mathcal{L}_{f}^{\textbf{C}})

.

Among all the minimal bimachines one has to test in order to check whether a transduction is

5.
Characterization of aperiodic transductions

In the aperiodic case (that is, for
\textbf{C}=\mathbf{A}
),
we give a stronger characterization than for arbitrary congruence classes, namely that any minimal bimachine of an aperiodic transduction is aperiodic.
This gives us a
PSpace
algorithm for deciding aperiodicity of a transduction given by a bimachine.

5.1.
Characterization of aperiodicity

Here we show that the canonical bimachine of an aperiodic transduction is aperiodic.
As it was shown above in Proposition
13
, the canonical right automaton of an aperiodic transduction is always aperiodic.
The difficulty lies in showing that the left automaton of the canonical bimachine is also aperiodic, for an aperiodic transduction.
It relies on the decomposition of a transduction as the composition of a sequential and a
right sequential
transduction, which is a characterization of rational transductions
[
EM65
]
.
A right sequential transduction is simply a transduction realized by a transducer with an underlying right automaton, which can be seen as a sequential transducer but reading and writing from right to left.

Let us define
label_{\mathcal{R}}
the
labelling transduction
of a right automaton
\mathcal{R}
over the alphabet
\Sigma
.
Let
Q
be the set of states of
\mathcal{R}
and let
\Sigma_{\mathcal{R}}=\left\{\sigma_{q}|\ \sigma\in\Sigma,\ q\in Q\right\}
.
We define
label_{\mathcal{R}}:\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
as the transduction realized by the right-sequential transducer
\mathcal{T}=\left(\mathcal{R},\mathpzc{o},\bar{\epsilon},\bar{\epsilon}\right)
where
\bar{\epsilon}
is the constant function which maps any element to
\epsilon
and
\mathpzc{o}(p,\sigma,q)=\sigma_{q}
.
We also define
\ell_{f}
, the unique transduction such that

f=\ell_{f}\circ label_{\mathcal{R}_{f}}
and
whose domain is
\{label_{\mathcal{R}}(u)\ |\ u\in\text{dom}(f)\}
.

Proposition 28
.

\ell_{f}
is a sequential transduction.

Proof 5.1
.

This proposition is a corollary of the existence of the canonical bimachine
\mathcal{B}_{f}=\left(\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f}),\mathcal{R}_{f},\mathpzc{o},\lambda,\rho\right)
.
Intuitively, the right automaton does a right to left pass on the input word, annotating each letter by the current state.
Then the left automaton can produce the outputs deterministically, over this enriched alphabet.
Let
\sim_{L}
denote the right congruence of
\mathit{Left}(\mathcal{R}_{f})
, let
[u]
denote
[u]_{\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}}
as in
3.2.1
and let
{[u]}_{L}
be used for
{[u]}_{\sim_{L}}
.
We define
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}=\left(\mathcal{A}_{\ell},\mathpzc{o}_{\ell},\bar{\epsilon},\mathpzc{t}_{\ell}\right)
with
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}=\left(Q_{\ell},\delta_{\ell},\left\{q_{0}\right\},F_{\ell}\right)
a sequential transducer realizing
\ell_{f}
.

•

Q_{\ell}=\left\{([u]_{L},[v])\right\}\uplus\left\{q_{0}\right\}

•

\delta_{\ell}=\left\{(([u]_{L},[\sigma v]),\sigma_{[v]},([u\sigma]_{L},[v]))\right\}\cup\left\{(q_{0},\sigma_{[v]},([\sigma]_{L},[v]))\right\}

•

F_{\ell}=\left\{([u]_{L},[\epsilon])|\ u\in\text{dom}(f)\right\}
and also
q_{0}\in F_{\ell}
if
\epsilon\in\text{dom}(f)

•

\mathpzc{o}_{\ell}(([u]_{L},[\sigma v]),\sigma_{[v]},([u\sigma]_{L},[v]))=\omega([u]_{L},\sigma,[v])

and
\mathpzc{o}_{\ell}(q_{0},\sigma_{[v]},([\sigma]_{L},[v]))=\lambda([\sigma v])\omega([\epsilon],\sigma,[v])

•

\mathpzc{t}([u]_{L},[\epsilon])=\rho([u]_{L})
and
\mathpzc{t}(q_{0})=\lambda([\epsilon])\rho([\epsilon]_{L})
if
\epsilon\in\text{dom}(f)

Hence
\ell_{f}
is a sequential transduction.

Now we have to show that
\ell_{f}
is also an aperiodic transduction whenever
f
is.

Proposition 29
.

If
f
is an aperiodic transduction, then
\ell_{f}
is also aperiodic.

Proof 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be an aperiodic transducer realizing
f
with an underlying automaton
\mathcal{A}=\left(Q,\Delta,I,F\right)
.
We define
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, a transducer with underlying automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\left(Q^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime},I^{\prime},F^{\prime}\right)
defined as the product of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{R}_{f}
:

•

Q^{\prime}=Q\times(\Sigma/_{\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}})

•

\Delta^{\prime}=\left\{((p,[\sigma u]),\sigma,(q,[u]))|\ (p,\sigma,q)\in\Delta\right\}

•

I^{\prime}=I\times(\text{dom}(f)/_{\mathrel{\leftharpoonup}_{f}})

•

F^{\prime}=F\times\left\{[\epsilon]\right\}

The outputs of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
are exactly the same as the ones of
\mathcal{T}
, ignoring the extra information given by
\mathcal{R}_{f}
.
The transducer
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}

6.
Logical transducers

The theory of rational languages is rich with results connecting automata, logics and algebra and some of those results have been successfully lifted to transductions:

MSO
-transducers (
MSOTs
), a logical model of transducers introduced by Courcelle
[
CE12
]
, have been shown to be equivalent to deterministic two-way transducers
[
EH01
]
as well as a deterministic one-way model of transducers with registers
[
AC10
]
.
More recently, an equivalence between first-order transducers and transducers with an aperiodic transition monoid has been shown
[
FKT14
,
CD15
]
.

However,
MSOTs
are far more expressive than one-way transducers as they can express functions that do not preserve the order of the input word, like
mirror
, which returns the mirror of a word, or
copy
, which copies an input word twice.
A natural restriction on
MSOTs
, called
order-preserving

MSOT
, has been shown to express exactly the rational functions
[
Boj14
,
Fil15
]
.
In the following we will only consider order-preserving
MSOTs
and simply call them
MSOTs
.

Many links have been shown between logical fragments of
MSO
and algebraic varieties of monoids (see
e.g.

[
Str94
,
PS05
]
) and the strength of those links is that they often give equational descriptions of the corresponding language varieties, and thus give a way to decide if a language is definable in some given logical fragment.
Our goal is to provide a framework to attempt to lift some of these results from languages to transductions.
We give sufficient conditions on
\mathcal{F}
, a logical fragment of
MSO
, to decide if a transduction, given by an
MSOT
, can be realized by an
\mathcal{F}
-transducer.
In particular we show that it is decidable if a transduction, given by an
MSOT
, can be realized by an
FOT
.

6.1.3.

In the model of Courcelle, originally introduced in the more general context of graph transductions

Since we consider order-preserving word transductions, we only need to define the unary predicates of the output structure and those are defined by formulas with one free variable.
Models for such formulas are words with a marked position, called

Furthermore, we choose a similar but slightly different approach from formulas with a free variable and consider formulas with a constant, which can be seen as a variable which is never quantified upon.
For

A

\mathcal{F}
-transductions (as for instance
\mathbf{A}
-transductions and
FO
[<]
-transductions).
In this section we give sufficient conditions under which the equivalence holds.
For that purpose, we introduce an intermediate logical model of transductions, based on pairs of formulas, which is
ad hoc
to bimachines.
Hence, the equivalence between this new formalism and
C
-transductions is straightforward, and the only remaining task is to give sufficient conditions under which this formalism is equivalent with
\mathcal{F}
-transductions.
The conditions we give are,
a priori
, not necessary, however we try and give conditions that are as general as possible, so that they can be applied to most known logical fragments of
MSO
.

6.2.1.
Pairs of formulas

For a logical fragment
\mathcal{F}
, we define 2-
\mathcal{F}
formulas over
\Sigma
as follows, where
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
denote closed
\mathcal{F}
-formulas and
\Gamma
denotes a subset of
\Sigma
:

F::=F\vee F\mid{(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})}_{\Gamma}

For words
u,v
and a letter
\sigma
, we define
(u\sigma v,|u|+1)\models F
by induction on 2-formulas:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}(u\sigma v,|u|+1)\models{(\phi,\phi^{\prime})}_{\Gamma}&\mathrm{if}&u\models\phi\ \mathrm{and}\ v\models\phi^{\prime}\ \mathrm{and}\ \sigma\in\Gamma\\
(u\sigma v,|u|+1)\models F_{1}\vee F_{2}&\mathrm{if}&(u\sigma v,|u|+1)\models F_{1}\ \mathrm{or}\ (u\sigma v,|u|+1)\models F_{2}\end{array}

From this we can define the
\wedge
operator:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{(\phi_{1},\phi^{\prime}_{1})}_{\Gamma_{1}}\wedge{(\phi_{2},\phi^{\prime}_{2})}_{\Gamma_{2}}&:=&{(\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2},\phi^{\prime}_{1}\wedge\phi^{\prime}_{2})}_{\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}}\\
(F_{1}\vee F_{2})\wedge F&:=&(F_{1}\wedge F)\vee(F_{2}\wedge F)\\
F\wedge(F_{1}\vee F_{2})&:=&(F\wedge F_{1})\vee(F\wedge F_{2})\end{array}

Similarly we can define the
\neg
operator:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\neg{(\phi,\phi^{\prime})}_{\Gamma}&:=&{(\top,\top)}_{\Sigma\setminus\Gamma}\vee{(\neg\phi,\top)}_{\Sigma}\vee{(\top,\neg\phi^{\prime})}_{\Sigma}\\
\neg(F_{1}\vee F_{2})&:=&\neg F_{1}\wedge\neg F_{2}\end{array}

{exa}

Let us define the pointed language of Example
6.1.3
by pairs of formulas:

\left(\exists x\ \alpha(x),\top\right)_{\left\{\gamma\right\}}\vee\left(\top,\exists x\ \beta(x)\right)_{\left\{\gamma\right\}}

From this alternative formalism of pairs of formulas we can define
logical 2-transducers
which are defined exactly as logical transducers except that
MSO
c
formulas are replaced by 2-
MSO
formulas.
Given a logical fragment
\mathcal{F}
, we say that 2-
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{c}}
are
equivalent
if they define the same pointed languages.

6.2.2.
Bimachines and logical transducers

The logical formalism of pairs of formulas is
ad hoc
to bimachines, thus we obtain the following unsurprising result:

Lemma 38
.

Let
C
be a congruence class equivalent to a fragment
\mathcal{F}
.
A transduction is definable by a
C
-bimachine without final outputs if and only if it is definable by a 2-
\mathcal{F}
-transducer.

Proof 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\bar{\epsilon},\bar{\epsilon}\right)
be a
C
-bimachine without final outputs realizing a transduction
f
.
Let us define a 2-
\mathcal{F}
-transducer
\mathcal{T}=\left(K,\phi_{\text{dom}},\left(\psi_{v}\right)_{v\in K}\right)
realizing
f
.
Since
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
are
C
-automata, we know that for any word
w
, there exist
\mathcal{F}
-formulas
\phi_{{[w]}_{\mathcal{L}}}
and
\phi_{{[w]}_{\mathcal{R}}}
which respectively recognize the languages
{[w]}_{\mathcal{L}}
and
{[w]}_{\mathcal{R}}
.
Then we can define
\phi_{\text{dom}}=\bigvee_{u\in\text{dom}(f)}\phi_{{[u]}_{\mathcal{L}}}
, since
\mathcal{L}
recognizes
\text{dom}(f)
.
Let
K=\left\{v\ |\ \exists u,\sigma,w\text{ s.t. }\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[w]_{\mathcal{R}})=v\right\}
,
then for
v\in K
, we define:

\psi_{v}=\bigvee_{\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[w]_{\mathcal{L}})=v}\left(\phi_{{[u]}_{\mathcal{L}}},\phi_{{[w]}_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)_{\left\{\sigma\right\}}

By construction,
\mathcal{T}
realizes
f
.

Conversely let
\mathcal{T}=\left(K,\phi_{\text{dom}},\left(\psi_{v}\right)_{v\in K}\right)
be a 2-
\mathcal{F}
-transducer realizing a transduction
f
.
We define
\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\bar{\epsilon},\bar{\epsilon}\right)
, a
C
-bimachine without final outputs realizing
f
.
Given an
\mathcal{F}
-sentence
\phi
, let
\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\phi}
denote respectively a left and a right
C
-automaton recognizing
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket
.
Let
v\in K
, and
\psi_{v}=\bigvee_{i=1,\ldots,n_{v}}{(\theta_{v}^{i},\chi_{v}^{i})}_{\Gamma_{v}^{i}}
, then we define:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\phi_{\text{dom}}}\times\prod_{v\in K\ i=1,\ldots,n_{v}}\mathcal{L}_{\theta_{v}^{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{\phi_{\text{dom}}}\times\prod_{v\in K\ i=1,\ldots,n_{v}}\mathcal{R}_{\chi_{v}^{i}}

Finally, the outputs of
\mathcal{B}
are defined by
\omega({[u]}_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,{[w]}_{\mathcal{R}})=v
if there exists
v\in K
such that
u\models\theta_{v}^{i}
,
\sigma\in\Gamma_{v}^{i}
and
w\models\chi_{v}^{i}
for some
i=1,\ldots,n_{v}
.
Otherwise, it means that
u\sigma w
does not belong to the domain of
f
and we can set
\omega({[u]}_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,{[w]}_{\mathcal{R}})=\epsilon
.
Again,
\mathcal{B}
realizes
f
by construction.

The previous lemma does not capture the entire class of
C
-transductions since we restrict ourselves to
C
-bimachines without final outputs. In order to circumvent this issue, we define the notion of an
\epsilon
-isolating congruence class, a class
C
for which the restriction over final output does not reduce the expressiveness of
C
-bimachines.
The
empty word congruence
over an alphabet
\Sigma
is the congruence such that for any words
u,v
, we have
u\sim v
if
u=\epsilon\Leftrightarrow v=\epsilon
. A congruence class which for any given alphabet contains the corresponding empty word congruence, is called
\epsilon
-isolating
.

Proposition 39
.

Let
C
be an
\epsilon
-isolating congruence class.
Then any
C
-transduction can be realized by a
C
-bimachine without final outputs.

Proof 6.2
.

Let
f:\Sigma^{+}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
be a
C
-transduction, and let
\mathcal{B}=\left(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\omega,\lambda,\rho\right)
be a
C
-bimachine realizing
f
.
We denote by
\sim_{\epsilon}
the empty word congruence over
\Sigma
, and by
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon}
the left and right automata associated with
\sim_{\epsilon}
, respectively.
Since
{\sim_{\epsilon}}\in\textbf{C}
by hypothesis,
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon}
are
C
-automata.
We define
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\left(\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon},\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon},\omega^{\prime},\overline{\epsilon},\overline{\epsilon}\right)
with

\omega^{\prime}(([u]_{\mathcal{L}},[u]_{\epsilon}),\sigma,([v]_{\mathcal{R}},[v]_{\epsilon}))=\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}})
if
u,v\neq\epsilon
,
\lambda([v]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}})
if
u=\epsilon
and
v\neq\epsilon
,
\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([u]_{\mathcal{L}})
if
u\neq\epsilon
and
v=\epsilon
, and
\lambda([v]_{\mathcal{R}})\omega([u]_{\mathcal{L}},\sigma,[v]_{\mathcal{R}})\rho([u]_{\mathcal{L}})
if

By construction
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
realizes

, and since
{({\approx_{\mathcal{B}}}\sqcap{\sim_{\epsilon}})}\sqsubseteq{\approx_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}}
,

is indeed a

-bimachine.

Corollary 40
.

Let
C
be an
\epsilon
-isolating congruence class equivalent to a fragment
\mathcal{F}
.
Then a transduction is a
C
-transduction if and only if it is definable by a 2-
\mathcal{F}
-transducer.

Proof 6.3
.

According to Proposition
39
, we have that
C
-bimachines without final outputs exactly characterize
C
-transductions.
Thus, from Lemma

we have the equivalence between
C
-transductions and 2-
\mathcal{F}
-transductions.

From the previous corollary we can immediately deduce:

Corollary 41
.

Let
C
be an
\epsilon
-isolating congruence class equivalent to a fragment
\mathcal{F}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{c}}
and 2-
\mathcal{F}
define the same pointed languages.
Then a transduction is a

-transduction if and only if it is an
\mathcal{F}
-transduction.

6.2.3.
From
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{c}}
to 2-
\mathcal{F}
and back

When
\mathcal{F}
is a fragment equivalent to some congruence class
C
, logical transductions defined with 2-
\mathcal{F}
formulas trivially have the same expressive power as
C
-bimachines.
In this section we shall give some sufficient properties on a logical fragment
\mathcal{F}
under which
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{c}}
-formulas and 2-
\mathcal{F}
-formulas are equivalent,
i.e.
, define the same pointed languages.
For this we introduce a third logical formalism for pointed words:

A pointed word over an alphabet
\Sigma
can alternatively be seen a logical structure over
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma\uplus\dot{\Sigma}}=\left\{{(\sigma(x))}_{\sigma\in\Sigma},{(\dot{\sigma}(x))}_{\sigma\in\Sigma},x<y\right\}
,
i.e.
, a word over an extended alphabet which contains a pointed copy of each letter.
Note that the number of pointed positions is not necessarily one.
However we will see in the next example that restricting to one pointed position can be enforced by a formula
\phi_{\text{pointed}}
.

{exa}

The language defined by the formula in Example
6.1.3
is:

\left\{u\alpha u^{\prime}\dot{\gamma}v|\ u,u^{\prime},v\in\Sigma^{*}\right\}\cup\left\{u\dot{\gamma}v\beta v^{\prime}|\ u,v,v^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}\right\}

We can define this language by an
MSO
-sentence over the extended alphabet
\Sigma\uplus\dot{\Sigma}
.
First we define the formula:

\phi_{\text{pointed}}:=\left(\exists x\ \dot{\Sigma}(x)\right)\wedge\left(\forall x,y\ \dot{\Sigma}(x)\wedge\dot{\Sigma}(y)\rightarrow x=y\right)

The formula
\phi_{\text{pointed}}
specifies that exactly one position holds a pointed letter, with predicate
\dot{\Sigma}(x)
being a shortcut for
\bigvee_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\dot{\sigma}(x)
.
Finally we obtain the formula:

\phi_{\text{pointed}}\wedge\exists c\ \dot{\gamma}(c)\wedge\left(\left(\exists x\ x<c\wedge\alpha(x)\right)\vee\left(\exists x\ x>c\wedge\beta(x)\right)\right)

This is just the formula of Example
6.1.3
where
\mathsf{c}
is replaced by the variable
c
which is existentially quantified, and holds a pointed letter.

Our goal here is to show that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{c}}
-formulas and 2-
\mathcal{F}
-formulas define the same pointed languages under the following assumption (1)–(3). Assumption (4) is used to show that a corresponding congruence class would be
\epsilon
-isolating.

(1)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{c}}
-formulas over an alphabet
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{F}
-formulas over the extended alphabet
\Sigma\uplus\dot{\Sigma}
define the same pointed languages.

(2)

A language over the alphabet
\Sigma
is definable by an
\mathcal{F}
-formula over
\Sigma
if and only if it is definable by an
\mathcal{F}
-formula over a larger alphabet
\Sigma\cup\Gamma
.

(3)

\mathcal{F}
-languages are closed under
pointed concatenation
, meaning that for any two
\mathcal{F}
-languages
L_{1},L_{2}
over an alphabet
\Sigma
and a fresh symbol
\sharp
,
L_{1}\cdot\sharp\cdot L_{2}
is an
\mathcal{F}
-language over
\Sigma\uplus\left\{\sharp\right\}
.

(4)

\left\{\epsilon\right\}
is an
\mathcal{F}
-language.

These assumptions may seem quite strong and not necessary, and they are, however they cover several well studied logical fragments of
MSO
, as
shown in Section
6.3
.

Lemma 43
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a fragment equivalent to some congruence class
C
.
Under assumption (2), any
\mathcal{F}
-definable pointed language is 2-
\mathcal{F}
-definable.

Proof 6.4
.

Let
\phi
be an
\mathcal{F}
-formula recognizing a pointed language over the extended alphabet.
Since
\mathcal{F}
is equivalent to the congruence class
C
, let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\textbf{C}(\Sigma\uplus\dot{\Sigma})
-automaton, with transition relation
\Delta
, recognizing

.
Then we define

\psi=\bigvee_{(p,\dot{\sigma},q)\in\Delta}{(\phi_{I}^{p},\phi_{q}^{F})}_{\left\{\sigma\right\}}

where
\phi_{I}^{p},\phi_{q}^{F}

Lemma 44
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a fragment equivalent to some congruence class
C
.
Under assumption (2)–(3), any 2-
\mathcal{F}
-definable pointed language is
\mathcal{F}

Proof 6.5
.

Let us show that under assumption (3), a 2-

Let

Up to decomposing

.
According to assumption (3), there exists an
\mathcal{F}
-formula
\chi_{i}
such that

Under assumption (2), these can be seen as

-languages over

Since

Hence, there exists an
\mathcal{F}
-formula
\chi

Proposition 45
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a fragment equivalent to some congruence class

Under assumptions (1)–(3), the logics

.

According to Lemmas

We give sufficient conditions under which a congruence class corresponding to a logical fragment is

Proposition 46
.

Let

be a congruence class equivalent to some fragment

Then

is

Proof 6.7
.

According to (4),

Thus for any alphabet

Let

[<]
,

Conclusion

We have shown that the problem of deciding whether a bimachine realizes an aperiodic transduction is
PSpace
-complete. A question which remains open is whether the problem is still in
PSpace
if the input is given as a transducer instead of a bimachine.
Our main result is that any rational transduction can be completely characterized by a finite set of minimal bimachines, and we have introduced a logical formalism which allows to lift some known equivalences from rational languages to rational transductions.

One possible direction of investigation would be to formalize in algebraic terms sufficient, and possibly necessary, conditions under which a logic-algebra equivalence for languages can be lifted to transductions.
It would also be interesting to investigate the cases which are precisely not covered by our approach such as the logics
MSO
[=]
or
FO
[+1]
.
Another direction would be to try to lift the present results further to regular functions,
i.e.
, functions realized by two-way transducers.
Indeed a characterization of a regular transduction in terms of minimal congruences would for instance yield a way to decide if a regular function is first-order definable. In
[
Boj14
]
such a characterization is given, but with the stronger
origin semantics
, where every output position originates from an input position, which means that two transducers which realize the same function but produce outputs at different positions will not be considered equivalent under this semantics. Obtaining a similar characterization in origin-free semantics is a very ambitious objective and for now there is no known canonical way to define a regular function.
