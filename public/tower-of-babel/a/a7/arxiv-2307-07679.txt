Sharp Convergence Rates for Matching Pursuit
Jason M. Klusowski, Jonathan W. Siegel

Abstract
We study the fundamental limits of matching pursuit, or the pure greedy algorithm, for approximating a target function f f by a linear combination f n f_{n} of n n elements from a dictionary. When the target function is contained in the variation space corresponding to the dictionary, many impressive works over the past few decades have obtained upper and lower bounds on the error ‖ f − f n ‖ \|f-f_{n}\| of matching pursuit, but they do not match. The main contribution of this paper is to close this gap and obtain a sharp characterization of the decay rate, n − α n^{-\alpha} , of matching pursuit. Specifically, we construct a worst case dictionary which shows that the existing best upper bound cannot be significantly improved. It turns out that, unlike other greedy algorithm variants which converge at the optimal rate n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} , the convergence rate n − α n^{-\alpha} is suboptimal. Here, α ≈ 0.182 \alpha\approx 0.182 is determined by the solution to a certain non-linear equation.



1
Introduction

Matching pursuit
[
20
]
is a widely used algorithm in signal processing that approximates a target signal by selecting a sparse linear combination of elements from a given dictionary.

Over the years, matching pursuit has garnered significant attention due to its effectiveness in capturing essential features of a signal with a parsimonious representation, offering reduced storage requirements, efficient signal reconstruction, and enhanced interpretability of the underlying signal structure. Because of this, its applications span various domains, including image, video, and audio processing and compression
[
2
,
22
]
.

While previous works have explored the convergence properties of matching pursuit, several open questions and challenges remain. In particular, the relationship between the characteristics of the target signal, the chosen dictionary, and the convergence rate warrants further investigation.
The main objective of this paper is to provide a comprehensive analysis of the convergence properties of matching pursuit. Understanding the convergence rate is crucial for assessing the algorithm’s efficiency and determining the number of iterations required to achieve a desired level of approximation accuracy.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and
\mathbb{D}\subset H
be a symmetric collection of unit vectors, i.e.,
\|d\|=1
for
d\in\mathbb{D}
and
d\in\mathbb{D}
implies
-d\in\mathbb{D}
, called a dictionary. Non-linear dictionary approximation methods, which attempt to approximate a target function
f
by a sparse linear combination

f\approx\sum_{n=1}^{s}a_{n}d_{n},

(2)

where both the sequence of dictionary elements
d_{i}\in\mathbb{D}
and the coefficients
a_{i}
depend upon the function
f
to be approximated, are common method in machine learning and signal processing. Such methods aim to generate an approximation of the form (
2
) with a small number of terms
s
, and include gradient boosting
[
11
]
,
L^{2}
-boosting
[
4
]
, basis pursuit
[
5
]
, and matching pursuit
[
20
]
.

In this work, we consider matching pursuit
[
20
]
, which is a classical method for algorithmically generating a convergent non-linear dictionary expansion of the form

f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}d_{n}.

(3)

Matching pursuit is also known as the pure greedy algorithm
[
9
]
, and is given by

f_{0}=0,~d_{n}\in\argmax_{d\in\mathbb{D}}\langle r_{n-1},d\rangle,~f_{n}=f_{n-1}+\langle r_{n-1},d_{n}\rangle d_{n},

(4)

where
r_{n}=f-f_{n}
is the residual at step
n
. We remark that the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
here is the inner product of the Hilbert space
H
, which is typically the
L_{2}
inner product with respect to a probability distribution
\mu
in practical applications. An equivalent way of writing this method, which explains the name pure greedy algorithm, is

(a_{n},d_{n})=\argmin_{a\in\mathbb{R},d\in\mathbb{D}}\left\|f-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}d_{i}+ad\right)\right\|=\argmin_{a\in\mathbb{R},d\in\mathbb{D}}\left\|r_{n-1}-ad\right\|.

(5)

In each step we add the single term which minimizes the error the most, hence the name pure greedy algorithm. In other words, we fit a single term to the residual in each step.

An important generalization of this algorithm is the pure greedy algorithm with shrinkage
0<s\leq 1
, see
[
32
, p. 375]
, given by

f_{0}=0,~d_{n}\in\argmax_{d\in\mathbb{D}}\langle r_{n-1},d\rangle,~f_{n}=f_{n-1}+s\langle r_{n-1},d_{n}\rangle d_{n}.

(6)

Here we scale down the greedy term by a factor
s
, called the shrinkage factor, in each step.

Finding the optimal term
d_{n}
in (
6
) could potentially involve solving a high-dimensional, non-convex optimization problem. To address this computational issue, gradient tree boosting
[
11
]
(i.e.,
L^{2}
boosting) features an additional step in which
\langle r_{n-1},d\rangle
is itself greedily optimized. Here the dictionary consists of normalized piecewise constant functions (or decision trees), which are fit to the residuals
r_{n-1}
via the
CART algorithm

[
3
]
. While CART is usually not motivated as a greedy way to optimize the inner product
\langle r_{n-1},d\rangle
over a collection of normalized piecewise constant functions, it can be equivalently formulated as such. Except for decision stumps (or depth-one trees) which involve only a 2-dimensional optimization, understanding the optimization gap for this greedy heuristic (CART) in general is currently an open problem, one that we do not address in the present paper. That is, we assume herein that the optimization problem is solved
exactly
in (
6
).

Despite the practical success and great interest generated by the method of matching pursuit, the precise convergence properties of the algorithm (
4
) for general dictionaries
\mathbb{D}
have not been precisely determined. To describe this problem, we introduce the variation norm
[
15
,
6
]
with respect the the dictionary
\mathbb{D}
, defined by

\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}=\inf\{t>0:~f\in tB_{1}(\mathbb{D})\},

(7)

where the set
B_{1}(\mathbb{D})
is the closed convex hull of
\mathbb{D}
, i.e.,

B_{1}(\mathbb{D})=\overline{\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}d_{i},~a_{i}\geq 0,~\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}=1,~d_{i}\in\mathbb{D}\right\}}.

(8)

This norm is a common measure of complexity when studying non-linear dictionary approximation, since it is well-defined and useful for any abstract dictionary
\mathbb{D}\subset H

[
6
,
32
]
. Results for the variation space
\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})
can also often be extended to interpolation spaces which allow an even wider class of target functions to be analyzed
[
1
]
.

For some context, let us give a few examples of dictionaries
\mathbb{D}
of interest and their corresponding variation spaces. For specific dictionaries of interest, the variation spaces have often been characterized and other descriptions are available. For example, the variation space
\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})
for the dictionary

\mathbb{D}=\{e^{(x-c)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})},~c\in\mathbb{R},~\sigma>0\}

(9)

of Gaussian bumps is equivalent to the Besov space the Besov space
B^{1}_{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
, which consists of function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-1}\omega_{2}(f,t)_{1}\frac{dt}{t}<\infty,

(10)

where
\omega_{2}(f,t)_{1}
is the second order modulus of smoothness in
L_{1}
given by

\omega_{2}(f,t)_{1}=\sup_{0<h<t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x+h)-2f(x)+f(x-h)|dx.

This equivalence was proved in
[
21
]
. For more information on Besov spaces, we refer to
[
7
,
8
]
.

Another example are the spaces
\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})
for the dictionaries of ridge functions

\mathbb{D}=\{\sigma(\omega\cdot x+b),~\omega\in S^{d-1},~b\in\mathbb{R}\},

(11)

which correspond to shallow neural networks have been characterized and intensively studied
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
30
,
28
,
13
,
10
]
. Here
\sigma=\max(0,x)^{k}
is the popular ReLU
k
activation function.

Typically the convergence of abstract greedy algorithms is studied on the variation space
\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})

[
32
]
. For instance, it is shown in
[
9
]
that the pure greedy algorithm (
4
) satisfies

\|f-f_{n}\|\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}n^{-1/6}.

(12)

This was subsequently improved by Konyagin and Temylakov
[
14
]
to

\|f-f_{n}\|\leq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}n^{-11/62},

(13)

and finally by Sil’nichenko
[
31
]
to

\|f-f_{n}\|\leq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}n^{-\alpha},

(14)

where
\alpha=\gamma/(2(2+\gamma))\approx 0.182
and
\gamma>1
is a root of the non-linear equation

(1+\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2+\gamma}}\left(1+\frac{1}{1+\gamma}\right)-1-\frac{1}{\gamma}=0.

(15)

For the pure greedy algorithm with shrinkage (
6
) the method of Sil’nichenko implies that

\|f-f_{n}\|\leq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}n^{-\alpha},

(16)

where
\alpha=\gamma/(2(2+\gamma))
and
\gamma>1
is a root of the non-linear equation

(1+\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2+\gamma}}\left(1+\frac{1}{1+\gamma}\right)-1-\frac{2-s}{\gamma}=0.

(17)

As
s\rightarrow 0
, the exponent
\alpha\approx 0.305
(c.f., the exponent

2/7\approx 0.285
obtained in
[
23
]
). We remark that for
s=0
, the pure greedy algorithm is stationary. This manifests itself in the fact that although the exponent
\alpha
approaches
0.305
, the constant
C
in the upper bound approaches
\infty
as
s\rightarrow 0
.

On the other hand, for functions
f\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})
it is known
[
27
,
12
]
that for each
n
there exists an approximation

f_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}d_{i},

(18)

such that
\|f-f_{n}\|\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}n^{-1/2}
, and that the exponent
n^{-1/2}
is optimal for all dictionaries and
f\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})

[
15
]
.

Thus, in general the best we could hope for in the convergence of matching pursuit is a convergence rate like
n^{-1/2}
, which is attained by other greedy algorithms such as the orthogonal or relaxed greedy algorithm
[
9
,
12
]
(in fact, the orthogonal greedy algorithm has been shown to converge even faster for compact dictionaries
[
29
,
16
]
). Remarkably, the convergence of matching pursuit is strictly worse. Specifically, it was shown in
[
19
]
that there exists a dictionary
\mathbb{D}\subset H
and an
f\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})
such that the iterates of the pure greedy algorithm (
4
) satisfy

\|f-f_{n}\|\geq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}n^{-0.27}.

(19)

This estimate was finally improved in
[
18
]
to

\|f-f_{n}\|\geq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}n^{-0.1898}.

(20)

Comparing the estimates (
14
) and (
20
) we see that there is a still a significant, though small, gap between the best upper and lower bounds. The goal of this work is to close this gap nearly completely. We show the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\gamma>1
be the root of the equation (
15
). Then for every
\alpha>\gamma/(2(2+\gamma))
, there exists a dictionary
\mathbb{D}\subset H
and a function
f\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})
such that the iterates of the pure greedy algorithm (i.e., matching pursuit) (
4
) satisfy

\|f-f_{n}\|\geq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}n^{-\alpha}.

(21)

Combined with the upper bound (
14
), this gives a precise characterization of the exponent in the convergence rate of matching pursuit
and therefore makes significant progress towards solving an open problem posed in
[
32
]
, that is, to find the order of decay of the pure greedy algorithm. Additionally, this shows that the rate of convergence with shrinkage
s<1
is strictly better than for
s=1
, i.e., that
any
amount of shrinkage improves the algorithm in the worst case, lending theoretical support to the empirical observation that some shrinkage in gradient tree boosting can result in significant performance improvements.

Finally, we remark that although the pure greedy algorithm is significantly worse than the relaxed or orthogonal greedy algorithm in the worst case over the variation space
\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})
, it achieves the same rate of convergence on some interpolation spaces between the variation space and the space
H
, see
[
17
]
for details.

Although we do not give all of the details, our method shows that the rate obtained by Sil’nichenko (
16
) is optimal also for shrinkage
s_{0}<s<1
for a numerically computable value
s_{0}
. However, for sufficiently small shrinkage
s
our method breaks down and it is an open problem to determine the rate of convergence as
s\rightarrow 0
.

2
Construction of the Worst-Case Dictionary

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
1
. This is based upon an extension and optimization of the constructions developed in
[
19
,
18
]
.

2.1
Basic Construction

We let
H=\ell^{2}
and denote by
\{e_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
its standard basis. Let
0<\beta<\frac{1}{2}
. We will attempt to construct a realization of the pure greedy algorithm (
4
), i.e., a dictionary
\mathbb{D}\subset H
and an initial iterate
f\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})
, for which the convergence rate is bounded below by

\|f-f_{n}\|\geq Cn^{-\frac{1}{2}+\beta}.

We will show that this construction succeeds as long as

\left(\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\right)^{\beta}\left(\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{1-2\beta}\right)<1.

(22)

Let us begin by verifying that this implies Theorem
1
. It suffices to show that with equality in (
22
), we have

\alpha=\frac{1}{2}-\beta=\frac{\gamma}{(2(2+\gamma))},

(23)

where
\gamma
is the root of (
15
). Solving the relation (
23
) for
\gamma
in terms of
\beta
we get

\gamma=\frac{1-2\beta}{\beta}.

(24)

Plugging this into (
15
), we readily see that (
15
) is equivalent to equality in (
22
).

Let us proceed with the lower bound construction. It will be convenient in what follows to work with the residual form of the pure greedy algorithm, given by

r_{0}=f,~d_{n}\in\argmin_{d\in\mathbb{D}}\langle r_{n-1},d\rangle,~r_{n}=r_{n-1}-\langle r_{n-1},d_{n}\rangle d_{n}.

(25)

The construction, which is quite technical and follows the methods developed in
[
19
,
18
]
, will depend upon four fundamental parameters in addition to
\beta
: an integer
K>1
, an integer
N>1
, a real number
\epsilon>0
, and a function
\phi\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
.

Define two sequences of elements
\{r_{n}\}_{n=K-1}^{\infty}\subset H
and
\{d_{n}\}_{n=K}^{\infty}\subset H
inductively by

\begin{split}r_{K-1}&=-K^{-1/2+\beta}\frac{1}{\sqrt{K-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}e_{i},\\
d_{n}&=\gamma_{n}r_{n-1}+h_{n}+\xi_{n}e_{n},\\
r_{n}&=r_{n-1}-q_{n}d_{n},\end{split}

where
h_{n}\in H
is defined by (recall that
\phi
is one of our parameters)

h_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{n}\phi\left(\frac{i}{n}\right)e_{i}.

(26)

Here
\gamma_{n},\xi_{n},\alpha_{n}
and
q_{n}
are numbers which are chosen so that the following three conditions are satisfied:

\|r_{n}\|=(n+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}+\beta},~q_{n}=\langle r_{n-1},d_{n}\rangle,~\text{and}~\|d_{n}\|=1.

(27)

Then, we set (recall that
N
and
\epsilon
are parameters)

\begin{split}f&=r_{N},\\
\tilde{d}_{N}&=\epsilon\frac{r_{N}}{\|r_{N}\|}+\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}}d_{N},\\
\mathbb{D}&=\{-\tilde{d}_{N},\tilde{d}_{N}\}\cup\{-d_{n},d_{n}\}_{n\geq N}.\end{split}

From (
2.1
) and (
27
), we see that
\langle r_{N},d_{N}\rangle=\langle r_{N-1}-q_{N}d_{N},d_{N}\rangle=0
.
This implies that
\|\tilde{d}_{N}\|=1
.
It is also clear that
f\in\text{span}(\tilde{d}_{N},d_{N})
, so that
\|f\|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D})}<\infty
.

The idea is that the iteration in (
2.1
) should follow the execution of the pure greedy algorithm applied to the dictionary
\mathbb{D}
with initial residual
f
(note that for notational convenience we begin indexing at
N
instead of at
0
). In order to do this, we need to show that our parameters can be chosen appropriately so that the conditions in (
27
) can always be satisfied at every iteration, and so that the inner product inequalities

|\langle r_{n-1},\tilde{d}_{N}\rangle|<q_{n}~\text{and}~|\langle r_{n-1},d_{k}\rangle|<q_{n}

(28)

hold for every
n>N
and
k\geq N
with
k\neq n
. This is divided into the following two technical results.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that the smooth function
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfies the following conditions:

•

For some
0<\delta<1
, we have
\phi(x)=0
for
x\in[0,\delta]
.

•

\phi
satisfies the following integral equality:

\left(\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)\left(1+\int_{x}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right)dx\right)=\frac{\beta}{1-2\beta}.

(29)

•

\phi
satisfies the following two integral inequalities:

\sup_{a\in[0,1]}\left|\int_{0}^{a}(\phi^{\prime}(x)x-(\beta-1)\phi(x))\left(1+\int_{a^{-1}x}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right)dx\right|<1.

(30)

\sup_{a\in[0,1]}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\left((\beta-1)+(\beta-1)\int_{x}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}+\phi(x)\right)\left(\int_{ax}^{x}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right)dx+\int_{a}^{1}\phi(x)\frac{dx}{x}\right|<1.

(31)

Then for sufficiently large
N,M>1
and sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, the conditions (
27
) can always be satisfied for every
n\geq K
and the conditions (
28
) can be satisfied for every
n>N
and
k\geq N
with
k\neq n
(by making appropriate choices of
\gamma_{n},\xi_{n},\alpha_{n}
and
q_{n}
). Thus, the iteration (
2.1
) defines a realization of the pure greedy algorithm with dictionary
\mathbb{D}
and initial residual
f
.

This Proposition is proved in Section
3
and borrows may of the ideas from
[
18
]
. The method in
[
18
]
can essentially be viewed as choosing
\phi
to be a smoothed version of

c\chi_{[\tau,1]}(x):=\begin{cases}c&x\geq\tau\\
0&x<\tau,\end{cases}

(32)

where
c
is chosen to satisfy (
29
), and then optimizing in
\tau
and
\beta
. By generalizing to an arbitrary smooth function
\phi
we are able to improve upon this analysis. That we can close the gap between existing upper and lower bounds and determine the sharp exponent in the rate of convergence this way is contained in the following Proposition.

Proposition 2
.

For any
0<\beta<\frac{1}{2}
satisfying the condition (
22
), there exists a smooth function
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfying the conditions of Proposition
1
.

This Proposition is proved in Section
4
. Combining these results, we obtain Theorem
1
.

3
Proof of Proposition
1

We begin by determining conditions on the values
\gamma_{n},\xi_{n},\alpha_{n}
and
q_{n}
which are required to ensure that (
27
) is satisfied.
We calculate
q_{n}
by noting that

\|r_{n}\|^{2}=\|r_{n-1}\|^{2}-q_{n}^{2},

(33)

so that

q_{n}^{2}=\|r_{n-1}\|^{2}-\|r_{n}\|^{2}=n^{-1+2\beta}-(n+1)^{-1+2\beta}.

(34)

We are free to choose
\gamma_{n}
, and following the argument in
[
18
]
we make the choice
\gamma_{n}=(q_{n}^{-1}-q_{n-1}^{-1})
.
The conditions (
27
) and the construction (
2.1
) imply that

q_{n}=\langle r_{n-1},d_{n}\rangle=\gamma_{n}\|r_{n-1}\|^{2}+\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle=\gamma_{n}n^{-1+2\beta}+\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle,

(35)

which means that
\alpha_{n}
must be chosen so that
h_{n}
satisfies

\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle=q_{n}-\gamma_{n}n^{-1+2\beta}.

(36)

Next, since
h_{n}\in\text{span}(e_{1},...,e_{n-1})
, we see by induction that
d_{n},r_{n}\in\text{span}(e_{1},...,e_{n})
. Using the construction (
2.1
) this implies that

1=\|d_{n}\|^{2}=\|\gamma_{n}r_{n-1}+h_{n}\|^{2}+\xi_{n}^{2}.

(37)

This means that
h_{n}
must also satisfy

\|\gamma_{n}r_{n-1}+h_{n}\|\leq 1.

(38)

Finally, we solve equation (
37
) for
\xi_{n}
and choose
\xi_{n}=\sqrt{1-\|\gamma_{n}r_{n-1}+h_{n}\|^{2}}
to be the positive root.

3.1
Inner Product Formulas

Next, we derive formulas for the inner products appearing in (
28
), following
[
18
,
19
]
. Consider first the case
k<n
. We will proceed by induction on
m>k
. Combining the last two equations in (
2.1
) we see that

r_{m}=(1-q_{m}\gamma_{m})r_{m-1}-q_{m}h_{m}-q_{m}\xi_{m}e_{m}.

(39)

Plugging the choice
\gamma_{m}=q_{m}^{-1}-q_{m-1}^{-1}
into this, we get

r_{m}=\frac{q_{m}}{q_{m-1}}r_{m-1}-q_{m}h_{m}-q_{m}\xi_{m}e_{m}.

(40)

Taking the inner product with
d_{k}
, noting that
d_{k}\in\text{span}(e_{1},...,e_{k})
, and that
m>k
by assumption, we get

\langle r_{m},d_{k}\rangle=\frac{q_{m}}{q_{m-1}}\langle r_{m-1},d_{k}\rangle-q_{m}\langle h_{m},d_{k}\rangle.

(41)

Using (
27
) and the last line in (
2.1
), we see that by construction
\langle r_{k},d_{k}\rangle=0
. From this and equation (
41
) we see by induction on
m
that

\langle r_{n-1},d_{k}\rangle=-q_{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=k+1}^{n-1}\langle h_{i},d_{k}\rangle\right),

(42)

for
k<n
.

The case of
\langle r_{n-1},\tilde{d}_{N}\rangle
is a bit more complicated. We first note that since
\langle r_{N},d_{N}\rangle=0
the formula (
2.1
) implies that

\langle f,\tilde{d}_{N}\rangle=\epsilon\|r_{N}\|.

(43)

Taking the inner product of (
40
) with
\tilde{d}_{N}
, we see that

\langle r_{m},\tilde{d}_{N}\rangle=\frac{q_{m}}{q_{m-1}}\langle r_{m-1},\tilde{d}_{N}\rangle-q_{m}\langle h_{m},\tilde{d}_{N}\rangle.

(44)

Combining the base case (
43
) and the inductive step (
44
), we get

\langle r_{n-1},\tilde{d}_{N}\rangle=-q_{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=N+1}^{n-1}\langle h_{i},\tilde{d}_{N}\rangle-\epsilon\frac{\|r_{N}\|}{q_{N}}\right).

(45)

Next, we consider the case
k>n
. In this case the relations (
2.1
) give, for
k>n

\langle r_{n-1},d_{k}\rangle=\gamma_{k}\langle r_{n-1},r_{k-1}\rangle+\langle r_{n-1},h_{k}\rangle,

(46)

since
r_{n-1}\in\text{span}(e_{1},...,e_{n-1})
. Further, we see that

\langle r_{n-1},r_{k-1}\rangle=\langle r_{n-1},r_{k-2}\rangle-q_{k-1}\langle r_{n-1},d_{k-1}\rangle.

(47)

Shifting the indices in (
46
) we also get

\langle r_{n-1},d_{k-1}\rangle=\gamma_{k-1}\langle r_{n-1},r_{k-2}\rangle+\langle r_{n-1},h_{k-1}\rangle.

(48)

Solving this equation for
\langle r_{n-1},r_{k-2}\rangle
gives

\langle r_{n-1},r_{k-2}\rangle=\gamma_{k-1}^{-1}(\langle r_{n-1},d_{k-1}\rangle-\langle r_{n-1},h_{k-1}\rangle),

(49)

and plugging that back into equation (
47
) gives

\langle r_{n-1},r_{k-1}\rangle=(\gamma_{k-1}^{-1}-q_{k-1})\langle r_{n-1},d_{k-1}\rangle-\gamma_{k-1}^{-1}\langle r_{n-1},h_{k-1}\rangle.

(50)

Finally, plugging this into (
46
), we get that

\langle r_{n-1},d_{k}\rangle=(\gamma_{k-1}^{-1}-q_{k-1})\gamma_{k}\langle r_{n-1},d_{k-1}\rangle+\left\langle r_{n-1},h_{k}-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\gamma_{k-1}}h_{k-1}\right\rangle.

(51)

3.2
Parameter Asymptotics

The estimates required to prove Proposition
1
are quite technical and require the comparison of a variety of sums with corresponding integrals. For this purpose, we will need asymptotic formulas for some of the parameters introduced so far. These asymptotics were first derived in
[
18
,
19
]
.

We have from (
34
) that

q_{n}^{2}=(1-2\beta)n^{-2+2\beta}(1+c/n+O(n^{-2})),

(52)

for a constant
c
(the exact value of
c
will not be important for us). This implies that

q_{n}=\sqrt{1-2\beta}n^{\beta-1}(1-2\beta)n^{-2+2\beta}(1+c^{\prime}/n+O(n^{-2})),

(53)

for a (different) constant
c^{\prime}
.
From this, we obtain the following asymptotics for
\gamma_{n}
,

\gamma_{n}=(q_{n}^{-1}-q_{n-1}^{-1})=\frac{(1-\beta)}{\sqrt{1-2\beta}}n^{-\beta}(1+O(n^{-1})).

(54)

These two formulas give us the asymptotics of the right hand side of (
36
)

q_{n}-\gamma_{n}n^{-1+2\beta}=\left(\sqrt{1-2\beta}-\frac{(1-\beta)}{\sqrt{1-2\beta}}\right)n^{\beta-1}(1+O(n^{-1}))=\frac{-\beta}{\sqrt{1-2\beta}}n^{\beta-1}(1+O(n^{-1})).

(55)

Finally, we will also need the asymptotics for the inductive factor in (
51
)

(\gamma_{m-1}^{-1}-q_{m-1})\gamma_{m}=\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{m-1}}(1-\gamma_{m-1}q_{m-1})=\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{m-1}}\frac{q_{m-1}}{q_{m-2}}.

(56)

The asymptotics for
\gamma_{m}
(
54
) and the asymptotics for
q_{n}
(
53
) imply that

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{m-1}}=\left(1-\frac{\beta}{m}+o(m^{-1})\right),~\frac{q_{m-1}}{q_{m-2}}=\left(1+\frac{\beta-1}{m}+o(m^{-1})\right).

(57)

Multiplying these, we get

(\gamma_{m-1}^{-1}-q_{m-1})\gamma_{m}=(1-(1+o_{m}(1))m^{-1}).

(58)

Note that here and in the following, we use the notation
o_{k}(1)
to denote a quantity depending upon the parameter
k
whose limit is
0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
We also adopt the convention that the constants in big-
O
expressions and the rate of decay to
0
in little-
o
expressions will be uniform in any lower case indices
n,m,k,
or
l
which do not appear in the argument (or the subscript if the argument is
1
), but may depend upon other parameters of the construction. This modified big-
O
notation is necessary to simplify the comparisons between sums and integrals in the following.

Finally, we recall the basic fact, which we will use without reference in the following, that (using this convention)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}o_{k}(1)=o_{n}(1).

(59)

3.3
Estimates for the Conditions (
27
)

In this Section, we show that for sufficiently large
K
, the parameters
\xi_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
can be chosen to satisfy the conditions (
27
). For this, we will need the following formula for the coefficients of
r_{n}
, which will also be useful later.

Lemma 1
.

The following formula holds for
K\leq k\leq n

\langle r_{n},e_{k}\rangle=-q_{n}\left(\xi_{k}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\langle h_{j},e_{k}\rangle\right).

(60)

Furthermore, for
0<k\leq K-1\leq n
, we have

\langle r_{n},e_{k}\rangle=-q_{n}\left(\frac{K^{-1/2+\beta}}{q_{K-1}\sqrt{K-1}}+\sum_{j=K}^{n}\langle h_{j},e_{k}\rangle\right).

(61)

Since we also have
\langle r_{n},e_{k}\rangle=0
for
k>n
, this lemma gives the components of all of the iterates
r_{n}
.

Proof.

We prove this formula for any fixed
k>0
by induction on
n
, with the base case being
n=\max(K-1,k)
. The base case follows in the case where
n=k\geq K
from

\langle r_{k},e_{k}\rangle=\langle r_{k-1}-q_{k}d_{k},e_{k}\rangle=-q_{k}\langle d_{k},e_{k}\rangle=-q_{k}\xi_{k},

(62)

since
r_{k-1},h_{k}\in\text{span}(e_{1},...,e_{n-1})
. When
k\leq K-1
, we use the definition of
r_{K-1}
to get (
61
) in the case
n=K-1
.

The inductive step follows from (
40
) since

\langle r_{n},e_{k}\rangle=\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n-1}}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle-q_{n}\langle h_{n},e_{k}\rangle.

(63)

Using the inductive hypothesis, this gives

\langle r_{n},e_{k}\rangle=-\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n-1}}q_{n-1}\left(\xi_{k}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}\langle h_{j},e_{k}\rangle\right)-q_{n}\langle h_{n},e_{k}\rangle=-q_{n}\left(\xi_{k}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\langle h_{j},e_{k}\rangle\right),

(64)

when
k\geq K
, and similarly when
k\leq K-1
.

∎

Using this, we prove the following.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a non-zero
C^{\infty}
function. Then for sufficiently large
K
, equations (
26
) and (
36
) are solvable for
\alpha_{n}
. In addition,
\alpha_{n}
is non-negative and the resulting value of
h_{n}
satisfies (
38
), so that
\xi_{n}
is defined via (
37
). Finally, the construction satisfies

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\xi_{n}=1.

(65)

Proof.

Note that since
\phi\neq 0
is non-negative, we have

\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)dx=C_{\phi}>0.

(66)

We begin by calculating the inner product
\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle
for
n\geq K
using Lemma
1
and the definition of
h_{n}
in (
26
). We get

\begin{split}\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\langle h_{n},e_{k}\rangle=&-\frac{q_{n-1}\alpha_{n}}{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=\max(k+1,K)}^{n-1}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{j}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\phi\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)\right.\\
&+\left.\frac{K^{-\frac{1}{2}+\beta}}{q_{K-1}\sqrt{K-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)+\sum_{k=K}^{n-1}\xi_{k}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\right).\\
&\end{split}

For sufficiently large
K
, we now show by induction on
n\geq K
that we can solve (
26
) and (
36
) for
\alpha_{n}\geq 0
, and that the resulting
h_{n}
will satisfy (
36
) and
\|\gamma_{n}r_{n-1}+h_{n}\|\leq\sqrt{3}/2
, which implies that for the
\xi_{n}
satisfying (
37
) we will have
\xi_{n}\geq 1/2
.

So assume inductively that for all
K\leq k<n
we have
\xi_{k}\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
\alpha_{k}\geq 0
. (Note that in the base case
n=K
there is nothing to assume.) Since
\phi\geq 0
, we use the inductive assumption on
\alpha_{k}
to remove the initial double sum on the right hand side of (
3.3
) and get the inequality

\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle\leq-\frac{q_{n-1}\alpha_{n}}{n}\left(\frac{K^{-\frac{1}{2}+\beta}}{q_{K-1}\sqrt{K-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)+\sum_{k=K}^{n-1}\xi_{k}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\right).

(67)

Using the asymptotics (
53
), (
54
) and (
55
), and comparing the Riemann sum in the previous equation with an integral, a straightforward calculation shows that
K
can be chosen sufficiently large so that
0\leq\alpha_{n}\leq C
(for an appropriate constant
C
) and

\|\gamma_{n}r_{n-1}+h_{n}\|\leq\gamma_{n}\|r_{n-1}\|+\|h_{n}\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},

(68)

which implies that
\xi_{n}\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover, since
\alpha_{n}\leq C
we readily see that

\|\gamma_{n}r_{n-1}+h_{n}\|\leq\gamma_{n}\|r_{n-1}\|+\|h_{n}\|\leq\gamma_{n}\|r_{n-1}\|+\alpha_{n}\left[\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\phi(x)\right]n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rightarrow 0,

(69)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, which implies that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\xi_{n}=1
.

∎

3.4
Estimates for the Inner Product Conditions

In the following analysis, we assume that
K
has been chosen sufficiently large so that the conclusion of Proposition
3
holds, and turn to proving that the conditions (
28
) are satisfied under the assumptions of Proposition
1
for sufficiently large
N
and small
\epsilon
.

For this, we will need the following estimate relating sums to integrals of
\phi
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a
C^{\infty}
function such that for some
\delta>0
, we have
\phi(x)=0
on
[0,\delta]
. Let
n\geq l\geq k
, then we have

\sum_{j=l}^{n-1}\frac{1}{j}\phi\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)=\int_{\frac{k}{n}}^{\frac{k}{l}}\phi(x)\frac{dx}{x}+O(k^{-1}).

(70)

Here the constant in
O(k^{-1})
only depends upon
\phi
and not upon
n
and
l
.

Proof.

Consider the sequence of
n-l
points

x_{0}:=\frac{k}{n}<x_{1}:=\frac{k}{n-1}<\cdots<x_{n-l-1}:=\frac{k}{l+1}<x_{n-l}:=\frac{k}{l}.

(71)

This sequence of points forms a partition
\Delta
of the interval
[\frac{k}{n},\frac{k}{l}]
and the gap (or mesh/norm) of
\Delta
satisfies

|\Delta|:=\sup_{i}|x_{i+1}-x_{i}|\leq\frac{k}{l}-\frac{k}{l+1}=\frac{k}{l(l+1)}<\frac{1}{k}.

(72)

Moreover, we have the following estimate

\log\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)-\log\left(\frac{k}{j+1}\right)=\log\left(\frac{j+1}{j}\right)=\log\left(1+\frac{1}{j}\right)=\frac{1}{j}+O\left(\frac{1}{j^{2}}\right).

(73)

Since
\phi
is bounded, we obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{j=l}^{n-1}\frac{1}{j}\phi\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)&=\sum_{j=1}^{n-l}\phi\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)\left[\log\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)-\log\left(\frac{k}{j+1}\right)\right]+O\left(\sum_{j=l}^{n-1}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\right)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n-l}\phi(x_{i})[\log(x_{i})-\log(x_{i-1})]+O(k^{-1}).\end{split}

Finally, since
\phi
is smooth and
\log(x)
is increasing and bounded on
[\delta,1]
, we obtain, by comparing with the Riemann-Stieltjes integral and using that the mesh
\Delta
satisfies
|\Delta|<k^{-1}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n-l}\phi(x_{i})[\log(x_{i})-\log(x_{i-1})]=\int_{\frac{k}{n}}^{\frac{k}{l}}\phi(x)d\log(x)+O(k^{-1})=\int_{\frac{k}{n}}^{\frac{k}{l}}\phi(x)\frac{dx}{x}+O(k^{-1}).

(74)

∎

Similar to the argument given in
[
18
]
, the next step is to carefully study the sequence
\alpha_{n}
. We also give an asymptotic formula for the coefficients of
r_{n}
.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that
\phi
satisfies the condition (
29
) in Proposition
1
.
Then, in the preceding construction, with
K
chosen large enough such that the conclusion of Proposition
3
holds, the sequence
\alpha_{n}
in (
26
) will satisfy

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}=1.

(75)

More precisely, this convergence will be such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\alpha_{n+1}}{\alpha_{n}}=1+o(n^{-1}).

(76)

In addition, under these conditions as
k\rightarrow\infty
we have the following asymptotics for the coefficients of
r_{n}

\langle r_{n},e_{k}\rangle=-q_{n}\left(1+\int_{\frac{k}{n}}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}+o_{k}(1)\right),

(77)

for any
n\geq k
. (Recall that by our convention
o_{k}(1)
goes to
0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
uniformly in
n
.)

Proof.

The proof is quite similar to the proof of Lemma 2 in
[
18
]
. Suppose that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}\geq\alpha

(78)

for some
\alpha\geq 0
.

Using the formula (
3.3
), we see that

\begin{split}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}{q_{n-1}\alpha_{n}}\geq&\left(\alpha\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{}{n}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=\max(k+1,K)}^{n-1}\frac{1}{j}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\phi\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)\right]\right.\\
&+\left.\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{K^{-\frac{1}{2}+\beta}}{q_{K-1}\sqrt{K-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)+\sum_{k=K}^{n-1}\xi_{k}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\right]\right).\end{split}

We proceed to evaluate the limits in the above equation. Using the fact that
\phi
is Riemann integrable, that
K
is fixed and that
\xi_{k}\rightarrow 1
as
k\rightarrow\infty
, we see that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{K^{-\frac{1}{2}+\beta}}{q_{K-1}\sqrt{K-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)+\sum_{k=K}^{n-1}\xi_{k}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)dx.

(79)

Further, applying Lemma
2
to
\phi
, we see that

\sum_{j=\max(k+1,K)}^{n-1}\frac{1}{j}\phi\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)=\int_{\frac{k}{n}}^{\min\left(1,\frac{k}{K}\right)}\phi\left(z\right)\frac{dz}{z}+O(k^{-1}).

(80)

From this, by comparing a Riemann sum with an integral, we see that

\begin{split}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=\max(k+1,K)}^{n-1}\frac{1}{j}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\phi\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)\right]&=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\left[\int_{\frac{k}{n}}^{\min\left(1,\frac{k}{K}\right)}\phi\left(z\right)\frac{dz}{z}\right]+O\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k^{-1}\right)\right]\\
&=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\left[\int_{\frac{k}{n}}^{\min\left(1,\frac{k}{K}\right)}\phi\left(z\right)\frac{dz}{z}\right]+O\left(\frac{\log{n}}{n}\right)\right]\\
&=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\left[\int_{\frac{k}{n}}^{1}\phi\left(z\right)\frac{dz}{z}\right]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\int_{\frac{k}{K}}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right]\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)\left(\int_{x}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right)dx,\end{split}

since for large enough
n
,
\phi(k/n)=0
for
k=1,...,K
(recall that
\phi
is assumed to vanish on
[0,\delta]
for some
\delta
).

Plugging this into (
3.4
), we get

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}{q_{n-1}\alpha_{n}}\geq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)\left(1+\alpha\int_{x}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right)dx\right).

(81)

Utilizing the asymptotics (
53
) and (
55
), we obtain that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}{q_{n-1}}=\frac{\beta}{1-2\beta}.

(82)

Together with (
81
), this implies that (assuming that (
78
) holds)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}\leq\left[\frac{\beta}{1-2\beta}\right]\left(\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)\left(1+\alpha\int_{x}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right)dx\right)^{-1}.

(83)

In an entirely analogous manner, we prove that
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}\leq\alpha
(for any
\alpha\geq 0
) implies that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}\geq\left[\frac{\beta}{1-2\beta}\right]\left(\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)\left(1+\alpha\int_{x}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right)dx\right)^{-1}.

(84)

We proceed to use the same fixed point argument from
[
18
]
to complete the proof. Define
F_{\phi,s}
by

F_{\phi,s}(\alpha)=\left[\frac{\beta}{1-2\beta}\right]\left(\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)\left(1+\alpha\int_{x}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right)dx\right)^{-1}.

(85)

Proposition
3
implies that
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}\geq 0
. Thus, setting
\pi_{0}=0
and
\pi_{n}=F_{\phi,s}(\pi_{n-1})
, we see inductively that (
83
) implies that

\limsup_{m\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{m}\leq\pi_{2n-1},

(86)

while (
84
) implies that

\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{m}\geq\pi_{2n}.

(87)

Thus (
75
) will be proved if we can show that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{n}=F_{\phi,s}^{n}(0)=1
(here the exponent represents function composition).

Lemma 3
.

The function
F_{\phi,s}^{n}
defined in (
85
) satisfies
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{\phi,s}^{n}(0)=1
.

Proof.

Rewrite
F_{\phi,s}
as

F_{\phi,s}(\alpha)=\frac{A}{B+C\alpha},

(88)

where
A=\frac{\beta}{1-2\beta}>0
,
B=\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)dx>0
, and
C=\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)\left(\int_{x}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}\right)dx>0
.

The assumption (
29
) implies that
F_{\phi,s}(1)=1
, i.e., that
A=B+C
. We wish to show that iterating the map
F_{\phi,s}
converges to this fixed point. This follows from the following simple calculation

|F^{2}_{\phi,s}(\alpha)-1|=\left|\frac{C^{2}}{C^{2}+B^{2}+BC\alpha+BC}(\alpha-1)\right|\leq\frac{C^{2}}{C^{2}+B^{2}}|\alpha-1|,

(89)

since
\frac{C^{2}}{C^{2}+B^{2}}<1
.
∎

Lemma
3
completes the proof of (
75
).

Next, we prove the asymptotic formula (
77
). We may assume that
k>K
and use Lemma
1
and the fact that
\xi_{k}\rightarrow 1
(see Proposition
3
) to get

\langle r_{n},e_{k}\rangle=-q_{n}\left(1+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\langle h_{j},e_{k}\rangle+o_{k}(1)\right).

(90)

Using the definition of
h_{n}
(
26
), we get

\langle r_{n},e_{k}\rangle=-q_{n}\left(1+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{j}\phi\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)+o_{k}(1)\right)=-q_{n}\left(1+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\frac{1+o_{k}(1)}{j}\phi\left(\frac{k}{j}\right)+o_{k}(1)\right).

(91)

Using Lemma
2
and noting that
\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\phi(z)}{z}dz<\infty
(since
\phi=0
on
[0,\delta]
), we get

\langle r_{n},e_{k}\rangle=-q_{n}\left(1+\int_{\frac{k}{n}}^{1}\phi(z)\frac{dz}{z}+o_{k}(1)\right),

(92)

as desired.

Finally, we will prove (

). For this, we note that (
36
), combined with the asymptotics (
55
) implies that

\frac{\langle r_{n},h_{n+1}\rangle}{\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}=1+\frac{\beta-1}{n}+o(n^{-1}).

(93)

Using the formula (
40
) to rewrite this as

\frac{\langle r_{n},h_{n+1}\rangle}{\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}=\frac{\langle\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n-1}}r_{n-1}-q_{n}h_{n}-q_{n}\xi_{n}e_{n},h_{n+1}\rangle}{\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}=\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n-1}}\frac{\langle r_{n-1},h_{n+1}\rangle}{\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}-q_{n}\frac{\langle h_{n}+\xi_{n}e_{n},h_{n+1}\rangle}{\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}.

(94)

Using the asymptotics (
53
) and (

), we see that

\frac{q_{n}}{\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}=c^{\prime}+O(n^{-1}),

(95)

for a constant
c^{\prime}
. In addition, using the definition of
h_{n}
, the fact that
\xi_{n}\rightarrow 1
, and that
\phi
is smooth, we see that

\langle h_{n}+\xi_{n}e_{n},h_{n+1}\rangle=\frac{1}{n+1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\phi\left(\frac{i}{n}\right)\phi\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right)+\phi\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)+o_{n}(1)\right)=c^{\prime\prime}n^{-1}+o(n^{-1}),

(96)

for a constant
c^{\prime\prime}=\phi(1)+\int_{0}^{1}\phi^{2}(x)dx
.
This means that the last term in (

) is

q_{n}\frac{\langle h_{n}+\xi_{n}e_{n},h_{n+1}\rangle}{\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle}=cn^{-1}+o(n^{-1}),

(97)

for a constant
c
.

Now, we calculate, using the definition of
h_{n}
,

\langle r_{n-1},h_{n}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\langle h_{n},e_{k}\rangle=\frac{\alpha_{n}}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right).

(98)

Similarly, we obtain

\langle r_{n-1},h_{n+1}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\langle h_{n},e_{k}\rangle=\frac{\alpha_{n+1}}{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\phi\left(\frac{k}{n+1}\right).

(99)

Next, we observe that

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\phi\left(\frac{k}{n+1}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\left[\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)-\phi\left(\frac{k}{n+1}\right)\right].

(100)

Using that
\phi
is smooth, we see that

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\left[\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)-\phi\left(\frac{k}{n+1}\right)\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\langle r_{n-1},e_{k}\rangle\left[\frac{k}{n(n+1)}\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)+O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{n^{4}}\right)\right].

(101)

Applying the asymptotics (
77

158
) becomes

\beta(1-\beta)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}<1,

(161)

and (
159
) becomes

\left(\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\right)^{\beta}\left(\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{1-2\beta}\right)<1.

(162)

We see that (
161
) holds for any
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
since the left hand side is
<1
and the right hand side is
\geq 1
. Thus the only condition on
\beta
which must be satisfied is (
162
), which is exactly the relation (
22
).

The only step left in the proof of Theorem
1
is to show that the equation (
149
) can be solved for
f
when
F
is given by (
155
). Differentiating (
149
) with respect to
a
, we obtain the following integral differential equation on
[\tau,1]
:

f(a)+a^{-1}\int_{\tau}^{a}f(x)f(a^{-1}x)dx=G(a):=F^{\prime}(a)=c\tau^{-\beta}a^{\beta-1}.

(163)

We will use the following Lemma, based on the Schauder fixed point theorem, to solve this equation.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
G(x)
is continuously differentiable and satisfies the bound

R_{G}:=\sup_{a\in[\tau,1]}a^{-2}\int_{\tau}^{a}G(a^{-1}x)dx<1.

(164)

In addition, assume that there exist functions
0\leq g_{1}(x)\leq g_{2}(x)\leq G(x)
on
[\tau,1]
such that

G(a)-a^{-1}\int_{\tau}^{a}g_{1}(x)g_{1}(a^{-1}x)dx\leq g_{2}(a),

(165)

and

G(a)-a^{-1}\int_{\tau}^{a}g_{2}(x)g_{2}(a^{-1}x)dx\geq g_{1}(a).

(166)

Then the equation (
163
) has a continuously differentiable solution
f\geq 0
.

Proof.

Consider the map
T_{G}:C([\tau,1])\rightarrow C([\tau,1])
defined by

T_{G}(f)(a)=G(a)-a^{-1}\int_{\tau}^{a}f(x)f(a^{-1}x)dx.

(167)

For a fixed
M
to be determined later, define the set

S_{M}:=\{f\in C^{1}([\tau,1]),~g_{1}(x)\leq f(x)\leq g_{2}(x),~|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq M\}\subset C([\tau,1]).

(168)

By the Arzela-Ascoli theorem,
S_{M}
is a compact subset of
C([\tau,1])
. We will show that for sufficiently large
M
,
T_{G}
maps
S_{M}
into itself. Indeed, if
g_{1}(x)\leq f(x)\leq g_{2}(x)
, then we clearly have (since
g_{1},g_{2}
and thus
f
are non-negative)

T_{G}(f)(a)=G(a)-a^{-1}\int_{\tau}^{a}f(x)f(a^{-1}x)dx\leq G(a)-a^{-1}\int_{\tau}^{a}g_{1}(x)g_{1}(a^{-1}x)dx\leq g_{2}(a)

(169)

and

T_{G}(f)(a)=G(a)-a^{-1}\int_{\tau}^{a}f(x)f(a^{-1}x)dx\geq G(a)-a^{-1}\int_{\tau}^{a}g_{2}(x)g_{2}(a^{-1}x)dx\geq g_{1}(a),

(170)

by assumption on
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. Further, taking derivates, we see that

T_{G}(f)^{\prime}(a)=G^{\prime}(a)+a^{-2}\int_{\tau}^{a}f(x)f(a^{-1}x)dx-a^{-1}f(a)f(1)+a^{-3}\int_{\tau}^{a}f(x)f^{\prime}(a^{-1}x)dx.

(171)

We now integrate the last integral above by parts to obtain

\begin{split}a^{-3}\int_{\tau}^{a}f(x)f^{\prime}(a^{-1}x)dx&=a^{-2}\int_{\tau}^{a}f(x)\frac{d}{dx}f(a^{-1}x)dx\\
&=a^{-2}(f(a)f(1)-f(\tau)f(a^{-1}\tau))-a^{-2}\int_{\tau}^{a}f^{\prime}(x)f(a^{-1}x)dx.\end{split}

Since
G
is fixed,
f\leq g_{2}\leq G
and
|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq M
by assumption, collecting the preceding equations gives a bound of

|T_{G}(f)^{\prime}(a)|\leq K+R_{G}M,

(172)

where
K
is a constant only depending upon
G
and not upon the bound
M
. If
R_{G}<1
, we can choose
M>K(1-R_{G})^{-1}
to guarantee that
|T_{G}(f)^{\prime}(a)|\leq M
as well. This proves that
T_{G}
maps
S_{M}
to itself. The proof is now completed by invoking the Schauder fixed point theorem.
∎

Finally, we verify the assumptions of Lemma
4
for the function
G
in (
163
). A straightforward calculation gives that the integral in (
164
) is maximized when
a=\min(1,\tau(1+\beta)^{1/\beta})
and its value is given by

\frac{ca^{-(\beta+1)}}{\beta}\left(\left(\frac{a}{\tau}\right)^{\beta}-1\right).

(173)

Plugging in the values
\beta=\beta^{*}
and
\tau=\tau^{*}
which give equality in equations (
162
) and (
160
) for
s=1
, a straightforward numerical calculation gives that
R_{G}<1
. Since this value is continuous in
\beta
and
\tau
, the same holds for close enough
\beta
and
\tau
.

Finally, we verify the existence of the functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
in Lemma
4
. We consider the modified map

\tilde{T}_{G}(f)(a)=\max\left(0,G(a)-a^{-1}\int_{\tau}^{a}f(x)f(a^{-1}x)dx\right).

(174)

Starting with the function
f_{0}=G
, we iterate to obtain the sequence
f_{k}=\tilde{T}_{G}(f_{k-1})
. The map
\tilde{T}_{G}
is monotone, so that
f_{0}\geq f_{2}\geq f_{4}\cdots
and
f_{1}\leq f_{3}\leq f_{5}\cdots
. Consequently, if
f_{2n+1}>0
for some
n
, then
g_{1}=f_{2n+1}
and
g_{2}=f_{2n}
will satisfy the conditions of Lemma
4
. This follows since by the monotonicity of
\tilde{T}_{G}
,
f\leq f_{2n}
implies that
\tilde{T}_{G}(f)\geq f_{2n+1}
and
f\geq f_{2n+1}
implies that
\tilde{T}_{G}(f)\leq f_{2n+2}\leq f_{2n}
. Finally,
\tilde{T}_{G}(f)\geq f_{2n+1}>0
implies that
\tilde{T}_{G}
and
T_{G}
coincide, which shows that the conditions of the Lemma are satisfied.
