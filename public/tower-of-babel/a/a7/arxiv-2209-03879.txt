Extensions of representation stable categories
Joe Moeller

Abstract
. A category of FI type is one which is sufficiently similar to finite sets and injections so as to admit nice representation stability results. Several common examples admit a Grothendieck fibration to finite sets and injections. We begin by carefully reviewing the theory of fibrations of categories with motivating examples relevant to algebra and representation theory. We classify which functors between FI type categories are fibrations, and thus obtain sufficient conditions for an FI type category to be the result of a Grothendieck construction.



1.
Introduction

One strategy when studying representations of a family of groups is to find a category where the automorphism groups are the groups of interest. A functor from this category into some some category of modules amounts to a family of representations of the groups equipped with some extra data. The seminal instance is that of the symmetric groups and the category of finite sets and injective functions, denoted
\mathsf{FI}
. Church–Ellenberg–Farb–Nagpal showed that over a noetherian ring
k
, any submodule of a finitely generated
\mathsf{FI}
-module is finitely generated
[
CEFN14
]
. The implication of this finite generation property for the family of
S_{n}
representations underlying an
\mathsf{FI}
-module is that beyond a certain point, all the data of higher-indexed representations are completely determined by the lower ones.
Quod est superius est sicut quod inferius.

For each finite group
G
, there is a category
\mathsf{FI}_{G}
which enjoys many of the same properties as
\mathsf{FI}
. Just as in
\mathsf{FI}
, the objects of
\mathsf{FI}_{G}
are finite sets, but the morphisms are injections where each element of the source is decorated with an element of
G
.

. The automorphism groups here are precisely the wreath products

. Sam and Snowden defined this category and proved that its category of modules over a noetherian ring is noetherian
[

]
.

It can be seen without immense effort, once one is brought to ask the question, that the evident projection functor
\mathsf{FI}_{G}\to\mathsf{FI}
is a
fibration
. A
fibration of categories
is a functor
\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{X}
with a lifting property which allows one to formally pull back objects and arrows of
\mathcal{A}
along arrows in
\mathcal{X}
. There is an equivalence between such fibrations and weak functors
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
valued in categories, functors, and natural transformations. The details of this classical equivalence can be found in
[
JY21
]
. Such a weak functor is known as an
\mathcal{X}
-indexed category
.
The reverse process, constructing a category with a fibration to
\mathcal{X}
from an
\mathcal{X}
-indexed category, is called the
Grothendieck construction
.
The following vague, but natural, question arises.

Question 1.1
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is a category which is known to have nice representation theoretic properties, under which conditions does the Grothendieck construction of a weak functor
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
inherit those desired properties from
\mathcal{X}
?

The vagueness in this question lies precisely in what is meant by “nice representation theoretic properties”. Progress has been made to extract the essential categorical features of
\mathsf{FI}
that allow for its apparent nice representation theoretic properties. Gan and Li
[
GL15
]
have shown that under some simple combinatorial conditions, over a field of characteristic 0, finitely generated modules of an EI category (where every endomorphism is invertible) with objects essentially parameterized by
\mathcal{N}
are noetherian. Gadish imposed some further categorical conditions, but was able to lift the demand for parameterization by
\mathcal{N}
, the motivating case being modules over
\mathsf{FI}^{n}
. In so doing, a noetherian result was obtained for a wider class of categories, designated
\mathsf{FI}
-type categories

[
Gad17
]
. This paper seeks to provide an answer to
1.1
by providing necessary and sufficient conditions on an
\mathcal{X}
-indexed category such that the Grothendieck construction produces an
\mathsf{FI}
-type category, provided that
\mathcal{X}
itself is of
\mathsf{FI}
type.

Presently, we prove the following theorem in order to provide an answer to one instance of Question
1.1
.

Theorem

(
4.6
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an
\mathsf{FI}
-type category, and let
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be an indexed category. Then the Grothendieck construction applied to
\mathcal{M}
produces an
\mathsf{FI}
-type category and the related fibration preserves pullbacks and weak pushouts if

(1)

the fibers are
\mathsf{FI}

(2)

and object

in
\mathcal{M}x
is invertible

(3)

the inclusions
\mathcal{M}x\hookrightarrow\textstyle\int\hskip-5.05942pt\mathcal{M}

(4)

\mathcal{M}
is weakly reversible

for all objects

and morphisms

in
\mathcal{X}
.

We do not expect the terms ‘weak pushout’ or ‘weakly reversible’ to be familiar to the reader, but are introduced within the paper where appropriate.

Section

2.
Grothendieck fibrations

A
Grothendieck fibration
is a functor
P\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{X}
which essentially allows you to divide the category
\mathcal{A}
into subcategories
\mathcal{A}_{x}
called
fibers
over the objects
x
of
\mathcal{X}
, with
pullback
functors between them corresponding to the maps in
\mathcal{X}
. Such structures naturally appear in fields as widely varying as algebraic geometry
[
Vis05
]
, logic and theoretical computer science
[
Jac99
]
, and homotopy theory and higher category theory
[
Shu08
]
. They have been studied extensively by category theorists, especially in the context of topos theory
[
Gra66
,
Her94
,
Bor94
,
Joh02
,
Str20
,
JY21
]
.

The
Grothendieck construction
produces such a fibration from a family of categories and functors indexed by the objects of the base category
\mathcal{X}
. In fact, any fibration is equivalent to one produced by this construction. In this way, there is an equivalence between fibrations and indexed categories. This offers two different perspectives of the same data, often allowing different tools and results to be used.

In this section, we recall the basic theory of indexed categories, Grothendieck fibrations, their equivalence via the Grothendieck construction, and examples found in algebra and representation theory. The full generality of this theory demands the use of 2-categorical concepts and language. There are however 1-categorical versions of everything, which we do our best to include whenever possible. We encourage the unfamiliar reader to look to these first as they read through.
Unabridged detail can be found in Johnson and Yau’s book
[
JY21
]
for all 2-categorical concepts we use here, including 2-categories, pseudofunctors, pseudonatural transformations, and modifications. We will review an abbreviated form of this information for the reader’s convenience.

2.1.
Indexed Categories

We will use
\mathsf{Cat}
to refer either to the category of categories and functors, or the 2-category of the same and natural transformations. We do our best to make the distinction clear either explicitly or by context.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a category. A
strict
\mathcal{X}
-indexed category
is a functor
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
. An
\mathcal{X}
-indexed functor
is a natural transformation
\alpha\colon\mathcal{M}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}
. Let
\mathsf{ICat}_{s}(\mathcal{X})
denote the functor category
[\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathsf{Cat}]
.

Example 2.1

(Group representations)
.

Let
\mathsf{FinGrp}
denote the category of finite groups and group homomorphisms. Define the functor
\mathsf{Rep}\colon\mathsf{FinGrp}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
as follows. To a finite group
G
assign the category of representations of
G
,
\mathsf{Rep}(G)
. To a group homomorphism
\phi\colon G\to H
assign the functor
\phi^{*}\colon\mathsf{Rep}(H)\to\mathsf{Rep}(G)
given by pulling back along
\phi
.

Example 2.2

(Ring modules)
.

Let
\mathsf{Ring}
denote the category of rings and ring homomorphisms. Define the functor
\mathsf{Mod}\colon\mathsf{Ring}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
as follows. To a ring
R
it assigns the category of
R
-modules,
R\mathsf{Mod}
. To a ring homomorphism
\phi\colon R\to S
assign the functor
\phi^{\ast}\colon S\mathsf{Mod}\to R\mathsf{Mod}
given by pulling back along
\phi
, restriction of scalars.

Example 2.3

(Group action as a functor)
.

Groups can be thought of as one-object groupoids, and group homomorphisms as functors between these. Let
G
and
H
be groups and
A\colon G\to\mathsf{Aut}(H)
a right action of
G
on

which sends the unique object of
G
to
H
itself, and every element of
G
to the automorphisms specified by the action. By composing with the inclusion functor, we see this as an indexed category
G^{\mathrm{op}}\xrightarrow{A}\mathsf{Grp}\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Cat}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be 2-categories. A
pseudofunctor

F\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
consists of an object function
\mathrm{ob}\mathcal{C}\to\mathrm{ob}\mathcal{C}
just as functors do, but now the assignment on morphisms is a functor
\mathcal{C}(c,c^{\prime})\to\mathcal{D}(Fc,Fc^{\prime})
(thus including a function on the 2-morphisms which is associative and unital). The composition and unit preservation laws at the level of 1-morphisms are now weakened from equations to specified invertible 2-morphisms
\mu_{f,g}\colon F(f)\circ F(g)\Rightarrow F(f\circ g)
natural in
f
and
g
, and
\eta_{c}\colon id_{Fc}\Rightarrow F(id_{c})
natural in
c
. We call these the
compositor
and
unitor
maps respectively. Further, these maps must themselves satisfy some equations, which can be found in Johnson and Yau’s book
[
JY21
]
.

There is a suitable generalization of natural transformations, called
pseudonatural transformations
. The naturality condition, instead of being the requirement for certain squares to commute, is extra structure in the form of invertible 2-morphisms, which again must satisfy some equations. Unlike with natural transformations, there is also a fitting notion of map
between
pseudonatural transformations, called a
modification
. Just as a natural transformation assigns a morphism in the target category to each object of the source category, a modification assigns a 2-morphism in the target category to each object of the source category. Again, full detail can be found
op cit
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a category, thought of as a locally discrete 2-category. An
\mathcal{X}
-indexed category
is a pseudofunctor
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
. An
\mathcal{X}
-indexed functor
is a pseudonatural transformation
\alpha\colon\mathcal{M}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}
. A
\mathcal{X}
-indexed natural transformation
is a modification. Let
\mathsf{ICat}(\mathcal{X})
denote the 2-category
[\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathsf{Cat}]_{ps}
of pseudofunctors, pseudonatural transformations, and modifications.

2.2.

2.3.
Fibrations

Example 4.10

(Block permutations)
.

Let

be given by

on objects, and for an injection

5.2.
Monomorphisms

Nothing interesting is needed for the condition of every map being a monomorphism. If the base has the property, then the total category will have the property if and only if the fibers all have the property. Going from total to fibers follows from the fact that a monomorphism in a category remains a monomorphism in all subcategories. The converse is a direct check.

Lemma 5.2

(Every map is a monomorphism)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a category where every map is a monomorphism, and
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be an indexed category. Then every map in
\textstyle\int\hskip-5.05942pt\mathcal{M}
is a monomorphism if and only if each map in the category
\mathcal{M}x
is a monomorphism for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be such that every map in
\mathcal{M}x
is a monomorphism. Let
(f,k)\colon(x,a)\to(y,b)
and
(g_{1},\ell_{1}),(g_{2},\ell_{2})\colon(z,c)\to(x,a)
be such that
(f,k)\circ(g_{1},\ell_{1})=(f,k)\circ(g_{2},\ell_{2})
. Then we get
f\circ g_{1}=f\circ g_{2}
, and since every map in
\mathcal{X}
is a monomorphism, we get
g_{1}=g_{2}
. We also get
\mathcal{M}g_{1}(k)\circ\ell_{1}=\mathcal{M}g_{2}(k)\circ\ell_{2}=\mathcal{M}g_{1}(k)\circ\ell_{2}
, and since every map in
\mathcal{M}z
is a monomorphism, we have
\ell_{1}=\ell_{2}
. Thus
(f,k)
is a monomorphism.

To prove the converse, consider instead a fibration
P\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{X}
such that every map in
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{X}
is a monomorphism. Let
f\colon a\to b
and
g_{1},g_{2}\colon c\to a
be maps in
\mathcal{A}_{x}
such that
f\circ g_{1}=f\circ g_{2}
. Since these are maps in
\mathcal{A}
,
f
is a monomorphism, and we get
g_{1}=g_{2}
.
∎

5.3.
Endomorphisms are invertible

A category where every endomorphism is an isomorphism is called an
EI category
. This condition is equivalent to the condition that every morphism between isomorphic objects is invertible.
5.3
gives sufficient conditions for the Grothendieck construction of an indexed category
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
with an EI base category to be an EI category.
5.6
is the converse, though phrased in the language of fibrations. So these conditions are both necessary and sufficient.

5.4.
Increasing

We say that a category is
increasing
if each object only has finitely many isomorphism classes of objects which map into it.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an increasing category, and
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
an indexed category. Then
\textstyle\int\hskip-5.05942pt\mathcal{M}
is increasing if and only if each fiber is increasing.

Proof.

Assume that each fiber is increasing. To have
(x,a)\leq(y,b)
, we must have a map
(f,k)\colon(x,a)\to(y,b)
, which consists of a map
f\colon x\to y
and a map

. For a fixed

, there are finitely many choices for
x
, and finitely many choices for
a
. Thus
\textstyle\int\hskip-5.05942pt\mathcal{M}
is increasing.

Let

be a fibration where
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{X}
are increasing. Let
x
be an object in
\mathcal{X}
, and

in
\mathcal{A}_{x}
, i.e. there is a map

in
\mathcal{A}_{x}
. Since

is a map in
\mathcal{A}
, there are finitely many choices for

, and so the choices are the choices within
\mathcal{A}

, which is still finite.
∎

5.7.
Weak pushouts

Given a fibration of categories with weak pushouts, where the functor preserves weak pushouts, it is straightforward to show that the fibers actually have weak pushouts. Include your diagram in the fiber into the total category, take weak pushout in the total category, and then factor the maps into their vertical parts. The resulting maps end up having equal codomain, and they form the legs of the desired square.

The other direction is more difficult. In the strong scenario where we have left adjoints to each reindexing functor, we can adjoint the fiber components of the legs, and pushforward into the fiber over the weak pushout of the base components. Then we can take weak pushout there, and adjoint back to get maps of the right type. This is a special case of a construction that works for any colimit. However, such left adjoints do not exist even in the case of
\mathsf{FI}_{G}
. Indeed, an adjunction between
G^{n}
and
G^{m}
would would be an isomorphism. This is the only subsection in this section of the paper with only sufficient conditions, not necessary and sufficient.

Definition 5.10
.

We say that an indexed category
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
is
weakly reversible
if for each map
f\colon x\to y
in
\mathcal{X}
, there is a weak pushout preserving functor
f_{!}\colon\mathcal{M}x\to\mathcal{M}y
such that
f_{!}f^{*}
is identity on objects, along with a natural transformation
\eta^{f}\colon id_{\mathcal{M}x}\Rightarrow f^{*}f_{!}
.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be a locally reversible indexed category with
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{M}x
having weak pushouts for each

. Then
\textstyle\int\hskip-5.05942pt\mathcal{M}
has weak pushouts.

Proof.

Let
(z,c)\xleftarrow{(g,\ell)}(x,a)\xrightarrow{(f,k)}(y,b)
be a diagram in

. Take the weak pushout of the base components of this diagram in
\mathcal{X}
. Let

6.
Future work and open questions

This section contains the questions related to the above work which I found interesting, but could not answer for one reason or another. I have neither the time nor expertise to answer all of them myself. I encourage any reader with an answer to contact me.

The symmetric groups have double covers, which are (mostly non-split) group extensions. Taken together, they form a double cover of
\mathsf{FB}
. So this should extend to a double cover of
\mathsf{FI}
. Should such a category exist, it ought to admit a fibration over
\mathsf{FI}
, in which case the present work would inform us of its representation stability properties, thus giving stability results for families of representations of the double covers of the symmetric groups.

We should be able to use a pointwise formula for Kan extensions of pseudofunctors to define an induction-restriction adjunction for indexed categories
[
Nun18
]
. This adjunction would then transfer to extensions of
\mathsf{FI}
-type categories.

A question suggested to me by Wee Liang Gan is if you start with an indexed category over a locally noetherian category (i.e. all finitely generated modules over a noetherian ring are noetherian), does the Grothendieck construction produce another locally noetherian category?

Nir Gadish pointed out to me that one of the main motivations to think about categories of
\mathsf{FI}
-type was to generalize character polynomials, and it would be natural to explore how the ring of character polynomials changes under the Grothendieck construction. For example, in the case of
\mathsf{FI}
, the ring of character polynomials is the polynomial ring in infinitely many variables. Then how would that change when passing from
\mathsf{FI}
to
\mathsf{FI}_{G}
and to non-split extensions?

In
[
SS17
]
, Sam and Snowden define a property for functors called “property (F)”, and show that if
\Phi\colon C\to C^{\prime}
has this property, and
\mathsf{Rep}_{k}(C)
is Noetherian, then
\mathsf{Rep}_{k}(C^{\prime})
is also Noetherian. A naïve attempt to use this in the present context would be to investigate when a fibration has property (F). However, note that a fibration is generally going to map from a category we know less about to one we know more about, so this is the wrong direction. Instead, it is more interesting to investigate when a fibration with a
\mathsf{FI}
-type base category admits a property (F) section.
