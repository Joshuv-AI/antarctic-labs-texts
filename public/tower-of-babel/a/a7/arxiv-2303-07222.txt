Reconciling rough volatility with jumps
Eduardo Abi Jaber, Nathan De Carvalho

Abstract
We reconcile rough volatility models and jump models using a class of reversionary Heston models with fast mean reversions and large vol-of-vols. Starting from hyper-rough Heston models with a Hurst index H ∈ ( − 1 / 2 , 1 / 2 ) H\in(-1/2,1/2) , we derive a Markovian approximating class of one dimensional reversionary Heston-type models . Such proxies encode a trade-off between an exploding vol-of-vol and a fast mean-reversion speed controlled by a reversionary time-scale ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 and an unconstrained parameter H ∈ ℝ H\in\mathbb{R} . Sending ϵ \epsilon to 0 0 yields convergence of the reversionary Heston model towards different explicit asymptotic regimes based on the value of the parameter H H . In particular, for H ≤ − 1 / 2 H\leq-1/2 , the reversionary Heston model converges to a class of Lévy jump processes of Normal Inverse Gaussian type. Numerical illustrations show that the reversionary Heston model is capable of generating at-the-money skews similar to the ones generated by rough, hyper-rough and jump models.

Introduction

Since the 1987 financial crash, financial option markets have exhibited a notable implied volatility skew, especially for short-term maturities. This skew reflects the market’s expectation of significant price movements on very short time scales in the underlying asset, which poses a challenge to traditional continuous models based on standard Brownian motion. To address this issue, the literature has developed several classes of models that capture the skewness in implied volatilities. Three prominent approaches are:

•

conventional one-factor stochastic volatility models boosted with large mean-reversion speed and vol-of-vol. This class of models have been justified by several empirical studies that have identified the presence of very fast mean-reversion in the S
\&
P volatility time series
[
10
,
17
,
27
,
28
]
and by the fact that they are able to correct conventional models to reproduce the behavior of the at-the-money skew for short maturities
[
39
]
;

•

jump diffusion models, especially the class of affine jump-diffusions for which valuation problems become (semi-)explicit using Fourier inversion techniques, see
[
20
]
. Such class of models incorporates occasional and large jumps to explain the skew observed implicitly on option markets, see
[
19
]
, and
[
12
]
for an empirical analysis of the impact of adding jumps to stochastic volatility diffusion on the implied volatility surface;

•

rough volatility models, where the volatility process is driven by variants of the Riemann-Liouville fractional Brownian motion

W_{t}^{H}=\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(H+1/2\right)}\int_{0}^{t}\left(t-s\right)^{H-1/2}dW_{s},\quad t\geq 0,

(0.1)

So far, in the mathematical finance community, jump diffusion models and rough volatility models have often been treated as distinct approaches, and, in some cases, they have even been opposed to each other, see for instance

The above suggests that rough volatility and jump models may not be that different after all. Our main motivation is to establish for the first time in the literature a connection between rough volatility and jump models through conventional volatility models with fast mean-reverting regimes.

From rough Heston to reversionary Heston

1.1
Rough and hyper-rough Heston

Let
\left(W,W^{\perp}\right)
be a two-dimensional Brownian motion defined on a filtered probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\left(\mathscr{F}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{Q}\right)
which satisfies the usual conditions, where
\mathbb{Q}
is the risk-neutral probability. Set
B:=\rho W+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}W^{\perp}
with
\rho\in\left[-1,1\right]
. We take as a starting point a stochastic volatility model for an underlying asset
P
in terms of a time-changed Brownian motion:

dP_{t}=P_{t}dB_{\bar{U}_{t}},\quad P_{0}>0,

(1.1)

for some non-decreasing continuous process
\bar{U}
. If
\bar{U}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}U_{s}ds
, then
U
would correspond to the spot variance and
\bar{U}
plays the role of the integrated variance. The hyper-rough Volterra Heston model introduced in
[
36
]
and studied further in
[
2
, Section 7]
assumes that the dynamics of the integrated variance is of the form

\bar{U}_{t}=\bar{G}_{0}(t)+\xi\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}(t-s)W_{\bar{U}_{s}}ds,

(1.2)

for a suitable continuous function
\bar{G}_{0}
, and
\xi>0
, and
K_{H}
is the fractional kernel

K_{H}(t)=t^{H-1/2},\quad t>0,

(1.3)

for
H\in(-1/2,1/2]
. The lower bound
H>-1/2
ensures the
L^{1}_{loc}
integrability of the kernel
K_{H}
so that the stochastic convolution appearing in (
1.2
) is well-defined. Furthermore if the kernel happens to be in
L^{2}_{loc}
, the following lemma ensures the existence of a spot variance process.

Lemma 1.1

(Existence of spot variance)
.

Let
K\in L^{2}_{loc}
and
g_{0}\in L^{1}_{loc}
. Assume there exists a non-decreasing adapted process
\bar{U}
and a Brownian motion

such that

\bar{U}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}g_{0}(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)W_{\bar{U}_{s}}ds,

(1.4)

with
\sup_{t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\bar{U}_{t}\right|\right]<\infty
, for all
T>0
. Then,
\bar{U}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}U_{s}ds
, where
U
is a non-negative weak solution to the following stochastic Volterra equation

U_{t}=g_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)\sqrt{U_{s}}dW_{s},\quad\mathbb{Q}\otimes dt-a.e.

(1.5)

Conversely, assume there exists a non-negative weak solution
U
to the stochastic Volterra equation (
1.5
) such that
\sup_{t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[U^{2}_{t}\right]<\infty
, for all
T>0
, then
\bar{U}
solves (
1.4
).

Proof.

This is obtained by an application of stochastic Fubini’s theorem, see
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
.
∎

Going back to the fractional case, if we restrict
H

\left(0,1/2\right]
, then we have
K_{H}\in L^{2}_{loc}
. For

\displaystyle dP_{t}=P_{t}\sqrt{U_{t}}dB_{t},

(1.6)

\displaystyle U_{t}=g_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}(t-s)\xi\sqrt{U_{s}}dW_{s},

(1.7)

for some initial input curve

. Two notable specifications of such admissible input curves are given by

\begin{cases}g_{0}\text{ continuous and non-decreasing with }g_{0}\geq 0,\\

(1.8)

Moreover, for

the sample paths of the spot variance

is continuous but no longer absolutely continuous. Indeed, in this case, one can show that the trajectories of

[

A key advantage of rough and hyper-rough Heston models is the semi-explicit knowledge of the characteristic function of the log-price modulo a deterministic Riccati Volterra convolution equation, as they belong to the class of Affine Volterra processes
[
6
,
2
]
. More precisely,
for any

satisfying

\Re(u_{1})=0,\quad\Re(u_{2})\leq 0,

the joint Fourier–Laplace transform of

is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(u_{1}\log P_{T}+u_{2}\bar{U}_{T}\right)\right]=\exp\left(u_{1}\log P_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}R(\psi_{H}(T-s))d\bar{G}_{0}(s)\right),

for all
T\geq 0
, where
\psi_{H}
is the continuous solution to the following fractional Riccati–Volterra equation

\displaystyle\psi_{H}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}(t-s)R(\psi_{H}(s))ds,\quad t\geq 0,

\displaystyle R(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(u_{1}^{2}-u_{1})+u_{2}+\rho\xi u_{1}x+\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}x^{2},

(1.9)

see
[
2
, Section 7]
.
This allows for fast pricing and calibration via Fourier inversion techniques. Compared to the conventional Heston model where the characteristic function is explicit, the solution to the Riccati Volterra equation is not explicitly known and must be approximated numerically. Several numerical schemes have been proposed including the Adams scheme in
[
21
]
, the multi-factorial approximation of rough volatility models in
[
5
]
or the hybrid scheme based on fractional power series expansion in
[
16
]
.

1.2
Deriving reversionary Heston as a proxy:
\epsilon
-shifting the singularity

In both regimes, rough and hyper-rough, with the exception of
H=\frac{1}{2}
, the model is non-Markovian, non-semimartingale with singular kernels. From a practitioner standpoint, it is therefore natural to look for Markovian approximations by suitable smoothing of the singularity of the fractional kernel (
1.3
) sitting at the origin.
In this section, we show how we can build a Markovian semi-martingale proxy of hyper-rough models. This is achieved using a two-step procedure.

First step: recover semimartingality
by smoothing out the singularity of the fractional kernel
K_{H}
.
We fix
\epsilon>0
, and we consider the shifted fractional kernel

K_{H,\epsilon}(t):=\left(t+\epsilon\right)^{H-\frac{1}{2}},\quad t>0,

(1.10)

and the corresponding ‘integrated variance’
\bar{U}^{\epsilon}
given by

\bar{U}^{\epsilon}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}g_{0}^{\epsilon}(s)ds+\xi\int_{0}^{t}K_{H,\epsilon}(t-s)W_{\bar{U}^{\epsilon}_{s}}ds,

(1.11)

with

g_{0}^{\epsilon}(t)=U_{0}+\theta\int_{0}^{t}K_{H,\epsilon}(s)ds.

Note that now
K_{H,\epsilon}
is in
L^{2}_{loc}
for any value of
H
, so that an application of Lemma
1.1
yields that
\bar{U}^{\epsilon}=\int_{0}^{\cdot}U^{\epsilon}_{s}ds
where the spot variance
U^{\epsilon}
solves the equation

\displaystyle dP^{\epsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=P^{\epsilon}_{t}\sqrt{U^{\epsilon}_{t}}dB_{t}

(1.12)

\displaystyle U^{\epsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=U_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}K_{H,\epsilon}(t-s)\theta ds+\int_{0}^{t}K_{H,\epsilon}(t-s)\xi\sqrt{U^{\epsilon}_{s}}dW_{s}.

(1.13)

Moreover, since
K_{H,\epsilon}
is continuously differentiable on
[0,T]
, denoting by
K^{\prime}_{H,\epsilon}
its derivative, we get that
U^{\epsilon}
is a semimartingale with the following dynamics

\displaystyle dU_{t}^{\epsilon}=\left(K_{H,\epsilon}(0)\theta+\int_{0}^{t}K^{\prime}_{H,\epsilon}(t-s)dZ_{s}^{\epsilon}\right)dt+K_{H,\epsilon}(0)\xi\sqrt{U_{t}^{\epsilon}}dW_{t},

(1.14)

with

\displaystyle dZ^{\epsilon}_{t}=\theta dt+\xi\sqrt{U_{t}^{\epsilon}}dW_{t}.

(1.15)

Second step: recover a Markovian proxy thanks to the resolvent of the first kind.
The only non-Markovian term in (
1.14
) is the term
\int_{0}^{t}K^{\prime}_{H,\epsilon}(t-s)dZ_{s}^{\epsilon}
appearing in the drift. Using the resolvent of the first kind of
K_{H,\epsilon}
we will re-express this term in terms of a functional of the past of the process
U^{\epsilon}
. For a kernel
K
, a resolvent of the first kind is a measure
L
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
of locally bounded variation such that

\int_{[0,t]}K(t-s)L(ds)=1,\quad t\geq 0,

(1.16)

see
[
33
, Definition 5.5.1]
. A resolvent of the first kind does not always exist. We will make use of the notations
(f*g)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(t-s)g(s)ds
and
(f*L)(t)=\int_{[0,t]}f(t-s)L(ds)
.

Lemma 1.2
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and
H\in(-1/2,1/2)
. The kernel
K_{H,\epsilon}
admits a resolvent of the first kind
L_{\epsilon}
of the form

L_{\epsilon}(dt)=\frac{\delta_{0}(dt)}{K_{H,\epsilon}(0)}+\ell_{\epsilon}(t)dt,

(1.17)

with
\ell_{\epsilon}
a locally integrable function.
Moreover, the function
(K_{H,\epsilon}^{\prime}*L_{\epsilon})
is continuously differentiable and it holds, for all

, that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}K^{\prime}_{H,\epsilon}(t-s)dZ_{s}^{\epsilon}=-\left(\frac{1}{2}-H\right)\epsilon^{-1}\left(U_{t}^{\epsilon}-U_{0}\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(K_{H,\epsilon}^{\prime}*L_{\epsilon}\right)^{\prime}(t-s)\left(U_{s}^{\epsilon}-U_{0}\right)ds.

(1.18)

Proof.

First, the existence of the resolvent is justified as follows. Given
H\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})

is a positive completely monotone kernel
1

1

(K^{\prime}_{H,\epsilon}*L_{\epsilon})(t)=\frac{K^{\prime}_{H,\epsilon}(0)}{K_{H,\epsilon}(0)}+(K^{\prime}_{H,\epsilon}*\ell_{\epsilon})(t).

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}K^{\prime}_{H,\epsilon}(t-s)dZ_{s}^{\epsilon}=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}K_{H,\epsilon}(t-s)dZ_{s}^{\epsilon}+\left((K_{H,\epsilon}^{\prime}*L_{\epsilon})^{\prime}*(K_{H,\epsilon}*dZ_{t}^{\epsilon})\right)_{t},

(1.21)

0.2
)–(
0.3
) where the parameter
H
becomes unconstrained. In the following section, we illustrate numerically the fact that such reversionary Heston model can be seen as a proxy of rough and hyper-rough Heston models.

Remark 1.3
.

Such proxy approximation can directly be applied to the Riemann-Liouville fractional Brownian motion defined in (
0.1
) to get the proxy:

dW_{t}^{H,\epsilon}=-\left(\frac{1}{2}-H\right)\epsilon^{-1}W_{t}^{H,\epsilon}dt+\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}dW_{t},\quad W_{0}^{H,\epsilon}=0,

(1.22)

whose solution is explicitly given by

W_{t}^{H,\epsilon}=\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}-H\right)\epsilon^{-1}(t-s)}dW_{s},

(1.23)

which is a mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process as long as
H<\frac{1}{2}
, while the value
H=\frac{1}{2}
yields back the standard Brownian motion.

First, such Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process has been recently used to construct the Quintic stochastic volatility model
[
8
]
to achieve remarkable joint fits of SPX and VIX implied volatilies, outperforming even its rough and path-dependent counterparts as shown empirically in
[
7
]
.

Furthermore, notice that the case
H=0
degenerates into the fast scale volatility factor from
Fouque et al. 2003a
, with
m=0
,
\nu=1
and their time-scale is twice the reversionary time-scale
\epsilon
, and whose auto-correlation under the invariant distribution is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[W_{t}^{0,\epsilon}W_{s}^{0,\epsilon}\right]=e^{-\frac{\left|t-s\right|}{2\epsilon}}.

Consequently, the reversionary time-scale
\epsilon
sets the speed of decay of the auto-correlation function of
W^{H,\epsilon}
.

2
The joint characteristic functional of reversionary Heston

The following theorem provides the joint conditional characteristic functional of the log-price
\log S^{\epsilon}
and the integrated variance
\bar{V}^{\epsilon}:=\int_{0}^{\cdot}V^{\epsilon}_{s}ds
in the model (
0.2
)–(
0.3
) in terms of a solution to a system of time-dependent Riccati ordinary differential equations.

Theorem 2.1

(Joint characteristic functional)
.

Let
f,g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
be measurable and bounded functions such that

\Re g+\frac{1}{2}\left((\Re f)^{2}-\Re f\right)\leq 0.

(2.1)

Then, the joint conditional characteristic functional of
(\log S^{\epsilon},\bar{V}^{\epsilon})
is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}f({T-s})d\log S^{\epsilon}_{s}+\int_{t}^{T}g({T-s})d\bar{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}\right)\right|\mathscr{F}_{t}\right]=\exp\left(\phi_{\epsilon}\left(T-t\right)+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\psi_{\epsilon}\left(T-t\right)V^{\epsilon}_{t}\right),\quad t\leq T,

(2.2)

where
(\phi_{\epsilon},\psi_{\epsilon})
is the solution to the following system of time-dependent Riccati equations

\displaystyle\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\theta+\epsilon^{-H-\frac{1}{2}}V_{0}\right)\psi_{\epsilon}(t),\quad\phi_{\epsilon}(0)=0,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\psi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\psi_{\epsilon}^{2}(t)+\left(\rho\xi\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}f(t)-\epsilon^{-1}\right)\psi_{\epsilon}(t)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\left(g({t})+\frac{f^{2}({t})-f({t})}{2}\right),\qquad\psi_{\epsilon}(0)=0.

(2.4)

Proof.

The proof is given in Section
2.2
below.
∎

Before proving the result, we note that in the case
f
and
g
are constant, one recovers the usual formula for the characteristic function of the
Heston 1993
model, where the solution
(\phi_{\epsilon},\psi_{\varepsilon})
of (
2.3
)-(
2.4
) is explicit as stated in the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2

(Explicit marginals)
.

Let
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
and set

f(t)=iu
and
g(t)=iv
, for all
t\in[0,T]
. Then, the solution
(\phi_{\epsilon},\psi_{\epsilon})
to the Riccati equations (
2.3
)-(
2.4
) is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\phi_{\epsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}-H}\theta+\epsilon^{-1-2H}V_{0}\right)\xi^{-2}\left(\left(1-i\rho\epsilon^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\xi u-d\right)t-2\epsilon\ln\left(\frac{1-ge^{-\epsilon^{-1}td}}{1-g}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{\epsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{-H-\frac{1}{2}}\xi^{-2}\left(1-i\rho\epsilon^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\xi u-d\right)\frac{1-e^{-\epsilon^{-1}td}}{1-ge^{-\epsilon^{-1}td}},

with

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle:=\frac{1-i\rho\epsilon^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\xi u-d}{1-i\rho\epsilon^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\xi u+d},\quad d:=\sqrt{\left(1-i\rho\epsilon^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\xi u\right)^{2}-2\left(\epsilon^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\xi\right)^{2}\left(iv-\frac{u^{2}+iu}{2}\right)},\quad\Re d>0.

(2.5)

Consequently, the conditional joint characteristic function of
(\log S_{T}^{\epsilon},\int_{t}^{T}V^{\epsilon}_{s}ds)
is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\exp\left(iu\log{S^{\epsilon}_{T}}+iv\int_{t}^{T}{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}ds\right)\right|\mathscr{F}_{t}\right]=\exp\left(iu\log S^{\epsilon}_{t}+\phi_{\epsilon}\left(T-t\right)+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\psi_{\epsilon}\left(T-t\right)V^{\epsilon}_{t}\right),\quad t\leq T.

(2.6)

Proof.

For the explicit derivation of the formulas, see for example
[
31
, Chapter 2]
. An application of Theorem
2.1
yields the result.
∎

\phi_{\epsilon}

2.1
Time-dependent Riccati ODEs: existence and uniqueness

In this section, we consider a generic class of time-dependent Riccati equations that encompass
equation (
2.4
), in the form

{\psi^{\prime}}(t)=a(t)\psi^{2}(t)+b(t)\psi(t)+c(t),\quad\psi(0)=u_{0},\quad t\leq T,

(2.7)

with
u_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
and
a,b,c:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
three measurable and bounded functions. We say that
\psi:[0,t^{*}]\to\mathbb{C}
for some
t^{*}\in\left(0,T\right]
is a local extended solution to (
2.7
) with some initial condition
\psi(0)=u_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
if, almost everywhere on
[0,t^{*}]
, it is continuously differentiable and satisfies the relations in (
2.7
). The extended solution is global if
t^{*}=T
.

The presence of the squared non-linearity in (
2.7
) precludes the application of the celebrated Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem and can lead to explosive solutions in finite time. Compared to the related literature on similar Riccati equations
[
24
, Lemma 2.3 and Section B]
, we provide a concise and simplified proof for the existence and uniqueness of a global extended solution to the Riccati equation (
2.7
) using a variation of constant formula under the following assumption on the coefficients
(a,b,c)
and the initial condition
u_{0}
:

\Im(a(t))=0,\quad a(t)>0,\quad\Re\left(c(t)\right)+\frac{\Im\left(b(t)\right)^{2}}{4a(t)}\leq 0,\quad\Re(u_{0})\leq 0,\quad t\leq T.

(2.8)

The following theorem gives the existence and uniqueness of a solution to the Riccati equation (
2.7
).

Theorem 2.4

(Existence and uniqueness for the Riccati)
.

Let
u_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(u_{0})\leq 0
and
a,b,c:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
be measurable and bounded functions satisfying (
2.8
). Then, there exists a unique extended solution
\psi:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
to the Riccati equation (
2.7
) such that

\displaystyle\Re(\psi(t))\leq 0,\quad t\leq T,

(2.9)

and

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leq T}|\psi(t)|<\infty.

(2.10)

Proof.

For the existence part, we proceed in two steps. First, we start by arguing the existence of a local solution using
Carathéodory’s theorem. For this we rely on
[
33
, Chapter 12, Section 2]
, using the notations therein (see equation (1.7) for example), we consider the integral equation

\psi(t)=\psi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}g\left(t,s,\psi(s)\right)ds,\quad t\geq 0,

(2.11)

where the operator
g
is defined by

g(t,s,\psi(s)):=a(s)\psi(s)^{2}+b(s)\psi(s)+c(s).

Let
D
be an open, connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{C}
that contains
\left(0,\psi(0)\right)
, and
C\left(D,\mathbb{C}\right)
be the set of continuous applications valued from
D
to
\mathbb{C}
. Define

T_{\infty}:=\sup\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}|C_{\psi(0),D}\left([0,T),\mathbb{C}\right)\neq\emptyset\right\},

where

C_{\psi(0),D}\left([0,T),\mathbb{C}\right):=\left\{\phi\in C\left(D,\mathbb{C}\right)|\phi(0)=\psi(0)\text{ and }\left(t,\phi(t)\right)\in D\text{ for }t\in[0,T)\right\}.

An application of
[
33
, Theorem 2.6]
yields the existence of a unique non-continuable solution to (
2.11
) which means that
\left(t,\psi(t)\right)\in D
on the interval
[0,T_{\infty})
and that either
T_{\infty}=T
or
\underset{t\rightarrow T_{\infty}}{\text{lim}}|\psi(t)|=\infty
. Indeed, the assumptions (i) to (v) of
[
33
, Theorem 2.6]
are readily satisfied by boundedness and integrability of
a
,
b
and
c
and the fact that
g
does not depend on
t
and satisfies the Carathéodory conditions.

Second, we argue that

\sup_{t\leq T_{\infty}}|\psi(t)|<\infty,

(2.12)

which would then yield
T_{\infty}=T
and the existence of a global solution
\psi
. Let
t<T_{\infty}
. We start by showing that
\Re(\psi(t))\leq 0
. Indeed, taking real parts in (
2.7
),
\psi_{\text{\mathbb{r}}}:=\Re\left(\psi\right)
satisfies the following equation on
[0,T_{\infty})
:

\psi_{\text{\mathbb{r}}}^{\prime}(s)=\left\{a(s)\Re\left(\psi(s)\right)+\Re\left(b(s)\right)\right\}\psi_{\text{\mathbb{r}}}(s)+d(s),

where
d(s)=-a(s)\left(\Im\left(\psi(s)\right)+\frac{\Im\left(b(s)\right)}{2a(s)}\right)^{2}+\Re\left(c(s)\right)+\frac{\Im^{2}\left(b(s)\right)}{4a(s)}\leq 0
thanks to condition (
2.8
), after a completion of squares. The variation of constant for
\psi_{\text{\mathbb{r}}}
then yields

\psi_{\text{\mathbb{r}}}(t)=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\left(a(u)\Re\left(\psi(u)\right)+\Re\left(b(u)\right)\right)du}\Re(u_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}d(s)e^{\int_{s}^{t}\left(a(u)\Re\left(\psi(u)\right)+\Re\left(b(u)\right)\right)du}ds\leq 0,

since the exponential is positive and
d(s)\leq 0
, and
\Re(u_{0})\leq 0
by assumption. This shows that
\Re(\psi)\leq 0,
on
[0,T_{\infty})
. Finally, an application of a similar variation of constants formula on equation (
2.7
) leads to

\psi(t)=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\left(a(u)\left(\psi(u)\right)+\Re\left(b(u)\right)\right)du}u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}\left(a(u)\left(\psi(u)\right)+\left(b(u)\right)\right)du}c(s)ds,

so that taking the module together with the triangle inequality and the fact that
\Re(\psi)\leq 0
on
[0,T_{\infty})
, yields

\displaystyle|\psi(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\left|e^{\int_{0}^{t}\left(a(u)\psi(u)+\left(b(u)\right)\right)du}\right||u_{0}|+\int_{0}^{t}\left|e^{\int_{s}^{t}\left(a(u)\psi(u)+\left(b(u)\right)\right)du}\right||c(s)|ds

\displaystyle=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\left(a(u)\Re\left(\psi(u)\right)+\Re\left(b(u)\right)\right)du}|u_{0}|+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}\left(a(u)\Re\left(\psi(u)\right)+\Re\left(b(u)\right)\right)du}|c(s)|ds

\displaystyle\leq e^{\int_{0}^{t}\Re\left(b(u)\right)du}|u_{0}|+\int_{0}^{T}e^{\int_{s}^{t}\Re\left(b(u)\right)du}|c(s)|ds

\displaystyle\leq C\left(|u_{0}|+\int_{0}^{T}|c(s)|ds\right),

where
C=\sup_{s,s^{\prime}\in[0,T]^{2}}e^{\int_{s}^{s^{\prime}}\Re(b(u))du}
does not depend on
t

and is finite by boundedness of
b
.
This shows (
2.12
) as needed. Combining the above we obtain the existence of a solution
\psi
on
[0,T]
satisfying (
2.9
) and (
2.10
).

To argue uniqueness, assume there are two such extended solutions
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
that satisfy (
2.7
). Then,

\left(\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}\right)^{\prime}(t)=\left(a(t)\left(\psi_{2}+\psi_{1}\right)(t)+b(t)\right)\left(\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}\right)(t),\quad\left(\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}\right)(0)=0,\quad t\leq T,

which yields

\left|\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}\right|(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|a(s)\left(\psi_{2}+\psi_{1}\right)(s)+b(s)\right|\left|\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}\right|(s)ds\leq c\int_{0}^{t}\left|\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}\right|(s)ds,\quad t\leq T,

for some
c>0
by boundedeness of
(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},a,b)
using (
2.12
), so that
the uniqueness is obtained from Gronwall’s lemma.

∎

\psi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(t)=a_{\epsilon}\psi_{\epsilon}^{2}(t)+b_{\epsilon}(t)\psi_{\epsilon}(t)+c_{\epsilon}(t),\quad\psi_{\epsilon}(0)=0,\quad t\leq T,

where we defined

Since condition (

\Re\left(c_{\epsilon}\right)+\frac{\Im\left(b_{\epsilon}\right)^{2}}{4a_{\epsilon}}=\epsilon^{H-1/2}\left(\Re g+\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\Re f\right)^{2}-\Re f\right)+\left(\rho^{2}-1\right)\left(\Im f\right)^{2}\right)\leq 0,

The function

We now prove the expression for the charateristic functional (

). Define the following process

\displaystyle M_{t}

\displaystyle=\exp(U_{t}),

\displaystyle U_{t}

\displaystyle=\phi_{\epsilon}\left(T-t\right)+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\psi_{\epsilon}\left(T-t\right)V^{\epsilon}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}f(T-s)d\log S^{\epsilon}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}g(T-s)d\bar{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}.

M_{T}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(T-s)d\log S^{\epsilon}_{s}+\int_{0}^{T}g(T-s)d\bar{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}\right),

which yields (

). We now argue that

is a martingale. We first show that

read

\displaystyle dM_{t}=M_{t}\left(dU_{t}+\frac{1}{2}d\langle U\rangle_{t}\right),

which is equal to

from the Riccati equations (

) and (

). This shows that

is a local martingale.

\Re(\psi_{\epsilon})\leq 0
which implies

, so that

\displaystyle\Re(U_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\Re(f(T-s))d\log S^{\epsilon}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\Re(g(T-s))d\bar{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\Re(g(T-s))-\frac{1}{2}\Re(f(T-s))\right){V}_{s}^{\epsilon}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\Re(f(T-s))\sqrt{{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}}dB_{s}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\Re(f(T-s))^{2}{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\Re(f(T-s))\sqrt{{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}}dB_{s}=:\tilde{U}_{t},

where the last inequality follows from (
2.1
). It follows that

\displaystyle|M_{t}|=\exp(\Re(U_{t}))\leq\exp(\tilde{U}_{t}),

where the process
\exp(\tilde{U})
is a true martingale, see
[
6
, Lemma 7.3]
. This shows that
M
is a true martingale, being a local martingale bounded by a true martingale, see
[
35
, Lemma 1.4]
, which concludes the proof.

3
From reversionary Heston to jump processes

In this section, we establish the convergence of the log-price and the integrated variance
(\log S^{\epsilon},\bar{V}^{\epsilon})
in the reversionary Heston model (
0.2
)-(
0.3
) towards a Lévy jump process
(X,Y)
, as
\epsilon
goes to
0
. More precisely, the limit
(X,Y)
belongs to the class of Normal Inverse Gaussian - Inverse Gaussian (NIG-IG) processes defined as follows.

Definition 3.1

(NIG-IG process)
.

Fix
\alpha\geq|\beta|\geq 0
,
\delta,\lambda>0
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. We say that
\left(X_{t},Y_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a Normal Inverse Gaussian - Inverse Gaussian (NIG-IG) process with parameters
\left(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\lambda\right)
if it is a two-dimensional homogeneous Lévy process with càdlàg sample paths, starting from
(X_{0},Y_{0})=(0,0)
almost surely, with Lévy exponent
\eta
defined by

\eta(u,v):=\left[i\mu u+\delta\left(\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-2i\lambda v-\left(\beta+iu\right)^{2}}\right)\right],\quad u,v\in\mathbb{R},

(3.1)

i.e. the joint characteristic function is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(iuX_{t}+ivY_{t}\right)\right]=\exp\left(\eta(u,v)t\right),\quad u,v\in\mathbb{R},\quad t\leq T.

(3.2)

In order to justify the existence of such a class of Lévy processes, one needs to justify that
\eta
given in (
3.1
) is indeed the logarithm of a characteristic function associated to an infinitely divisible distribution, see
[
42
, Corollary 11.6]
.
This is the object of the following lemma, which also provides the link with first-hitting times and subordinated processes.

Lemma 3.2

(Representation using subordination)
.

Let
\alpha\geq|\beta|\geq 0
,
\delta,\lambda>0
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
(\widetilde{W},\widetilde{W}^{\perp})
be a two dimensional Brownian motion. Let
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be the first hitting-time process defined as

\Lambda_{t}:=\inf\left\{s\geq 0:\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}s+\widetilde{W}_{s}\geq\delta t\right\},\quad t\in\left[0,T\right],

(3.3)

and define
Z
as the following shifted subordinated process

\displaystyle Z_{t}=\mu t+\beta\Lambda_{t}+\widetilde{W}^{\perp}_{\Lambda_{t}},\quad t\in\left[0,T\right].

(3.4)

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(iuZ_{t}+iv\lambda\Lambda_{t}\right)\right]=\exp(\eta(u,v)t),\quad u,v\in\mathbb{R},\quad t\in\left[0,T\right].

(3.5)

In particular,
\eta
given by (
3.1
) is the logarithm of the characteristic function of the joint random variable
(Z_{1},\lambda\Lambda_{1})
which is infinitely divisible.

Proof.

Fix
t\in\left[0,T\right]
. By construction, it is well-known that
\Lambda_{t}
has an Inverse Gaussian distribution if
\alpha>\left|\beta\right|
with parameters
\textit{IG}\left(\frac{\delta t}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}},\delta^{2}t\right)
, and in the drift-free case
\alpha=\left|\beta\right|
,
\Lambda_{t}
follows a Lévy distribution with parameters
\textit{Lévy}\left(0,\delta^{2}t\right)
(see
[
13
]
and Definition
A.3
in the Appendix). Now conditional on
\Lambda_{t}
,
Z_{t}
is Gaussian with parameters
\mathscr{N}\left(\mu t+\beta\Lambda_{t},\Lambda_{t}\right)
and using the tower property of conditional expectation, we get for the first case that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(iuZ_{t}+iv\lambda\Lambda_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\exp\left(iuZ_{t}\right)\right|\Lambda_{t}\right]\exp\left(iv\lambda\Lambda_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(iu\left(\mu t+\beta\Lambda_{t}\right)-\frac{\Lambda_{t}u^{2}}{2}+iv\lambda\Lambda_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(iu\mu t\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\left(iu\beta-\frac{u^{2}}{2}+iv\lambda\right)\Lambda_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(iu\mu t+\delta t\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}\left(iu\beta-\frac{u^{2}}{2}+iv\lambda\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\text{i.e. }\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(iuZ_{t}+iv\lambda\Lambda_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(iu\mu t+\delta t\left(\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-2i\lambda v-\left(\beta+iu\right)^{2}}\right)\right)=\exp(\eta(u,v)t),\quad u,v\in\mathbb{R},

where we used Definition
A.3
to get the fourth equality, noting that
\Re\left(i\left(u\beta+v\lambda\right)-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\right)\leq 0
. Similar computations yield the result for the case
\alpha=\left|\beta\right|
. Furthermore, we will say that that the random variable
(Z_{1},\lambda\Lambda_{1})
follows a NIG-IG distribution with parameters
(\alpha,\beta,\mu,\delta,\lambda)
(see Definition
A.3
in the Appendix). Such distribution is infinitely divisible because if
\left(X_{1},Y_{1}\right),\cdots,\left(X_{m},Y_{m}\right)
are independent NIG-IG random variables with common parameters
\left(\alpha,\beta,\lambda\right)
and individual
\left(\mu_{i},\delta_{i}\right)
, for
i=1,\cdots,m
, then
\left(X,Y\right):=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\right)
is again NIG-IG-distributed with parameters
\left(\alpha,\beta,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{i},\lambda\right)
.
∎

The appellation NIG-IG for the couple
(X,Y)
in Definition
3.1
is justified as follows:

•

Y
is an Inverse Gaussian process first derived by
Schrödinger 1915
which can be checked either by recovering the Inverse Gaussian distribution with parameters
\textit{IG}\left(\frac{\lambda\delta}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}},\lambda\delta^{2}\right)
after setting
u=0
in (
3.2
); or by using the representation as a first passage-time in (
3.3
). It is worth pointing that,
for
\alpha=|\beta|
, one recovers the well-known Lévy distribution for the first-passage of a Brownian motion with parameters
\textit{Lévy}\left(0,\lambda\delta^{2}\right)
. The
Lévy
distribution can be seen as a special case of the
Inverse Gaussian
distribution.

•

X
is the celebrated Normal Inverse Gaussian process of
Barndorff-Nielsen 1997
, with parameters
\textit{NIG}\left(\alpha,\beta,\mu,\delta\right)
, which can be checked by setting
v=0
in (
3.2
) or by using the representation as subordinated Brownian motion with an Inverse Gaussian subordinator as in (
3.4
).

In addition, we allow in Definition
3.1
the parameter
\alpha
to be equal to
\infty
in the following sense:

Remark 3.3

(Normal process)
.

Considering the set of parameters

(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\lambda)=\left(\alpha,0,\sigma^{2}\alpha,\mu,1\right),

a second order Taylor expansion, as
\alpha\to\infty
, of the square root yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(iuX_{t}+ivY_{t}\right)\right]=\exp\left(\left[i\mu u-\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{u^{2}}{2}+iv\right)\right]t\right),\quad u,v\in\mathbb{R},\quad t\leq T,

which is equivalent to the normal-deterministic process defined by

\left(X_{t},Y_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,T]}=\left(\mu t+\sigma\widetilde{W}_{t},\sigma^{2}t\right)_{t\in[0,T]}\overset{d}{=}\left(\mu t+\widetilde{W}_{\sigma^{2}t},\sigma^{2}t\right)_{t\in[0,T]},

where
\widetilde{W}
is an
\mathscr{F}
-adapted Brownian motion. We will consider that such (degenerate) process is a particular case of Definition
3.1
with parameters denoted by

\left.\left(\alpha,0,\sigma^{2}\alpha,\mu,1\right)\right|_{\alpha\to\infty}.

We are now in place to state our main convergence theorem. Theorem
3.4
provides the convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions of the joint process
(\log S^{\epsilon},\bar{V}^{\epsilon})
through the study of the limiting behavior of the characteristic functional given in Theorem
2.1
. Interestingly, the limiting behavior disentangles three different asymptotic regimes based on the values of
H
that can be seen intuitively on the level of the Riccati equation (
2.4
) as follows. Applying the variation of constants on
\psi_{\epsilon}
from equations (
2.13
) and (
2.14
), we get:

\displaystyle\psi_{\epsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{H+1/2}\int_{0}^{t}K_{\epsilon}(t-s)F(s,\psi_{\epsilon}(s))ds,\quad t\leq T,

(3.6)

\displaystyle F(s,u)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}u^{2}+\rho\xi f(s)u+g(s)+\frac{f^{2}(s)-f(s)}{2},

(3.7)

with
K_{\epsilon}
the kernel defined by

K_{\epsilon}(t)={\epsilon}^{-1}e^{-{\epsilon}^{-1}t},\quad t\geq 0.

(3.8)

Assuming that
\psi_{\epsilon}
converges to some
\psi_{0}
and observing that
K_{\epsilon}
plays the role of the Dirac delta as
\epsilon\to 0
, one expects
\int_{0}^{t}K_{\epsilon}(t-s)F(s,\psi_{\epsilon}(s))ds\to F(t,\psi_{0}(t))
in (
3.6
), the pre-factor
\epsilon^{H+1/2}
suggests then three different limiting regimes with respect to
H
that can be characterized through the functions
F
and
\psi_{0}
:

\begin{cases}\psi_{0}(t)=0,&\mbox{if }H>-1/2,\\
\psi_{0}(t)=F(t,\psi_{0}(t)),\quad\Re(\psi_{0}(t))\leq 0,&\mbox{if }H=-1/2,\\
0=F(t,\psi_{0}(t)),\quad\quad\,\,\;\,\Re(\psi_{0}(t))\leq 0,&\mbox{if }H<-1/2.\end{cases}

(3.9)

The function
\psi_{0}
in (
3.9
) is even explicitly given by

\psi_{0}(t)=\begin{cases}0,\quad&\mbox{if }H>-1/2,\\
\xi^{-2}\left(1-\rho\xi f(t)-\sqrt{\left(1-\rho\xi f(t)\right)^{2}-2\xi^{2}\left(g(t)+\frac{f^{2}(t)-f(t)}{2}\right)}\right),&\mbox{if }H=-1/2,\\
-\xi^{-1}\left(\rho f(t)+\sqrt{f(t)\left(1-\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)f(t)\right)-2g(t)}\right),&\mbox{if }H<-1/2,\end{cases}

(3.10)

see Lemma
A.1
below. Furthermore, the convergence of the integrated variance process is strengthened to a functional weak convergence on the Skorokhod space
D
of real-valued càdlàg paths on
[0,T]
endowed with the strong
M_{1}
topology, see Section
3.3.1
below. Such topology is weaker and less restrictive than the commonly used uniform or
J_{1}
topologies which share the property that a jump in a limiting
process can only be approximated by jumps of comparable size at the same time or, respectively, at nearby times. On the contrary, the
M_{1}
topology of
Skorokhod 1956
captures approximations of unmatched jumps, which in our case, will allow us to prove the convergence of the stochastic process
\bar{V}^{\epsilon}
with continuous sample trajectories towards a Lévy process with càdlàg sample trajectories. The statement is now made rigorous in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.4

(Convergence towards NIG-IG processes)
.

Let
f,g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
be bounded and measurable such that
\Re f=\Re g=0
and such that
\psi_{0}
defined in (
3.10
) has bounded variations. Then, based on the value of
H
, we obtain different explicit asymptotic formulas for the characteristic functional given in Theorem
2.1
:

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(T-s)d\log{S^{\epsilon}_{s}}+\int_{0}^{T}g(T-s)d\bar{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}\right)\right]=\exp\left(\phi_{0}(T)\right),

(3.11)

with

\phi_{0}(T):=\begin{cases}V_{0}\int_{0}^{T}h(s)ds,\quad&\mbox{if }H>-1/2,\\
\left(\theta+V_{0}\right)\xi^{-2}\left(T-\int_{0}^{T}\left(\rho\xi f(s)+\sqrt{\left(1-\rho\xi f(s)\right)^{2}-2\xi^{2}h(s)}\right)ds\right),&\mbox{if }H=-1/2,\\
-\theta\xi^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\rho f(s)+\sqrt{\rho^{2}f^{2}(s)-2h(s)}\right)ds,\quad&\mbox{if }H<-1/2,\end{cases}

(3.12)

where

.
In particular for
\rho\in(-1,1)
, as
\epsilon\to 0
, the finite-dimensional distributions of the joint process
(\log\frac{S^{\epsilon}}{S_{0}},\bar{V}^{\epsilon})

in the sense of Definition

with the following parameters depending on the value of

:

(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\mu,\lambda):=\begin{cases}\left.\left(\alpha,0,V_{0}\alpha,-\frac{V_{0}}{2},1\right)\right|_{\alpha\to\infty},\quad&\mbox{if }H>-1/2,\\
\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sqrt{(\xi-2\rho)^{2}+4(1-\rho^{2})}}{\xi(1-\rho^{2})},-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\xi-2\rho}{\xi\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)},\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}(\theta+V_{0})\xi^{-1},-\rho\left(\theta+V_{0}\right)\xi^{-1},\frac{1}{1-\rho^{2}}\right),&\mbox{if }H=-1/2,\\
\left(\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^{2})},-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^{2})},\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\theta\xi^{-1},-\rho\theta\xi^{-1},\frac{1}{1-\rho^{2}}\right),\quad&\mbox{if }H<-1/2,\end{cases}

(3.13)

where
\theta

S_{0}
,
\xi
and
V_{0}
are the same from (

)-(
0.3
). Furthermore, the process
\bar{V}^{\epsilon}
converges weakly towards
Y
on the space
(D,SM_{1})
, as
\epsilon\to 0
.

Proof.

The convergence of the characteristic functional in (
3.11
) is established in Section

(Lemmas
3.7
and
3.8
). This implies the convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions of

(\log S^{\epsilon},\bar{V}^{\epsilon})
as detailed in Section

on

.
∎

Remark 3.5

(An interesting interpretation)
.

The interpretation of the convergence results becomes even more interesting when combined with Section

3.1
Convergence of the joint characteristic functional

In this section we prove the convergence of the characteristic functional of
(\log{\frac{S^{\epsilon}}{S_{0}}},\bar{V}^{\epsilon})
as
\epsilon

0
stated in Theorem

. For this, we fix

f,g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
bounded and measurable such that
\Re f=\Re g=0
. We note that (
2.1
) is trivially satisfied so that an application of Theorem
2.1
, with

, yields that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(T-s)d\log S^{\epsilon}_{s}+\int_{0}^{T}g(T-s)d\bar{V}_{s}^{\epsilon}\right)\right]=\exp\left(\phi_{\epsilon}(T)+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\psi_{\epsilon}(T)V_{0}\right),

(3.17)

where
(\phi_{\epsilon},\psi_{\epsilon})

)-(

).
We start by showing that the second term in the exponential

goes to

for any value of

in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

For
\epsilon>0
, let
\psi_{\epsilon}
be a solution the time-dependent Riccati ODE (
2.4
) such that
\Re(\psi^{\epsilon})\leq 0
with
f,g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
bounded and measurable functions such that

. Then,

\left|\psi_{\epsilon}(t)\right|\leq C\epsilon^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\left(1-e^{-\epsilon^{-1}t}\right),\quad t\leq T,

(3.18)

for some constant
C
independent of
\epsilon
. In particular, we have the uniform convergence

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\sup_{t\leq T}\epsilon^{1/2-H}|\psi_{\epsilon}(t)|=0,\quad H\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.19)

Proof.

The variation of constants applied to the differential equation (
2.13
) yields:

\psi_{\epsilon}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}c_{\epsilon}(s)e^{\int_{s}^{t}\left(a_{\epsilon}\psi_{\epsilon}(u)+b_{\epsilon}(u)\right)du}ds,

with
a_{\epsilon},b_{\epsilon},c_{\epsilon}
defined as in (

). Given that

and

are both bounded on

, we fix

such that
\left|c_{\epsilon}(u)\right|\leq C\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}},u\leq T
. Note that
\Re b_{\epsilon}=-\epsilon^{-1}
, having also
\Re\left(\psi_{\epsilon}\right)\leq 0
and

, we get consequently by linearity when taking the real part

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{\epsilon}(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{a_{\epsilon}\int_{s}^{t}\Re\left(\psi_{\epsilon}(u)\right)du}e^{\Re b_{\epsilon}(t-s)}ds

(3.20)

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\epsilon^{-1}(t-s)}ds

(3.21)

which yields the desired upper bound on the solution when integrating explicitly the exponential. The convergence result follows immediately.
∎

The first term
\phi_{\epsilon}
, however, yields different limits based on the value of

\phi_{\epsilon}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\theta+\epsilon^{-H-\frac{1}{2}}V_{0}\right)\psi_{\epsilon}(s)ds,

for different regimes of

.

Lemma 3.8
.

We have the convergence

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\phi_{\epsilon}(T)=\phi_{0}(T),

(3.22)

where
\phi_{0}(T)
is given by (
3.12
).

Proof.

Case
H>-1/2
.

In this case, the solution
\psi_{\epsilon}
converges uniformly to zero from the upper bound given in Lemma
3.7
which, combined with the expression of

in (

), yields

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}F(s,\psi_{\epsilon}(s))=F(s,0)=g(s)+\frac{f^{2}(s)-f(s)}{2},\quad s\leq T.

(3.23)

Furthermore, integrating the variation of constants expression (
3.6
) leads to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\epsilon^{-H-1/2}\psi_{\epsilon}(u)du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left\{\int_{0}^{u}K_{\epsilon}(u-s)F(s,\psi_{\epsilon}(s))ds\right\}du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left\{\int_{s}^{t}K_{\epsilon}(u-s)du\right\}F(s,\psi_{\epsilon}(s))ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(1-e^{-\epsilon^{-1}(t-s)}\right)F(s,\psi_{\epsilon}(s))ds,

where we used Fubini for the second equality as the integrated quantity is bounded and measurable. Now, given the function
s\mapsto\left(1-e^{-\epsilon^{-1}(t-s)}\right)F(s,\psi_{\epsilon}(s))
is uniformly bounded in

Fix
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
. Since, for all
\epsilon>0
,
\bar{V}^{\epsilon}
is almost surely non-decreasing and non-negative, we have that, for all
\omega\in\Omega

\displaystyle||\bar{V}^{\epsilon}(\omega)||

\displaystyle=\bar{V}_{T}^{\epsilon}(\omega),

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(\bar{V}^{\epsilon}(\omega),\delta)

\displaystyle=0\vee\bar{V}^{\epsilon}_{\delta}(\omega)\vee\left(\bar{V}^{\epsilon}_{T}(\omega)-\bar{V}^{\epsilon}_{T-\delta}(\omega)\right),\quad\delta>0.

This yields that, for a threshold
c>0
big enough, the probability in condition
(i)
on the measures
\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}:=\mathbb{P}_{\left(\bar{V}_{.}^{\epsilon}\right)^{-1}}
reduces to

\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}\left(\left\{x\in D,||x||>c\right\}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\omega:||\bar{V}_{.}^{\epsilon}(\omega)||>c\right\}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{V}_{T}^{\epsilon}>c\right)\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{V}_{T}^{\epsilon}>c\right)<\bar{\varepsilon},

where the last inequality is satisfied by tightness of the family
\left(\bar{V}_{T}^{\epsilon}\right)_{\epsilon>0}
of random variables in
\mathbb{R}
which is obtained as a direct consequence of Lévy’s continuity theorem, recall that
\left(\bar{V}_{T}^{\epsilon}\right)_{\epsilon>0}
has been shown to converge in Section
3.1
. This yields
(i)
. In addition, regarding the second condition
(ii)
, set an arbitrary
\eta>0
, and take
\delta
small enough such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}\left(\left\{x\in D:w^{\prime}(x,\delta)\geq\eta\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(0\vee\left|\bar{V}_{\delta}^{\epsilon}\right|\vee\left|\bar{V}_{T}^{\epsilon}-\bar{V}_{T-\delta}^{\epsilon}\right|\geq\eta\right)\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\mathbb{P}\left(0\vee\left|\bar{V}_{\delta}^{\epsilon}\right|\vee\left|\bar{V}_{T}^{\epsilon}-\bar{V}_{T-\delta}^{\epsilon}\right|\geq\eta\right)<\bar{\varepsilon},

where the last but one inequality holds by tightness of the family
\left(\bar{V}_{\delta}^{\epsilon},\bar{V}_{T-\delta}^{\epsilon}\right)_{\epsilon>0}
while the last one is justified by stochastic continuity of
\bar{V}^{\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
and of its limit
Y
.

Remark 3.13
.

Since
\log S^{\epsilon}=-\frac{1}{2}\bar{V}^{\epsilon}+\rho W_{\bar{V}^{\epsilon}}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}W^{\perp}_{\bar{V}^{\epsilon}}

Lemma A.1
.

(Uniqueness of the complex root with a non-positive real part)
Take
\xi>0
. For all
f
,
g
bounded and measurable with
\Re f=\Re g=0
,
t\in\left[0,T\right]
and
\rho\in\left[-1,1\right]
, both polynomials

\displaystyle P(X)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}X^{2}-\left(1-\rho\xi f(t)\right)X+g(t)+\frac{f^{2}(t)-f(t)}{2}

\displaystyle Q(X)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}X^{2}+\rho\xi f(t)X+g(t)+\frac{f^{2}(t)-f(t)}{2}

admit exactly two roots with respective real parts of strict opposite signs if
\left(f(t),g(t)\right)\neq\left(0,0\right)
, and if
\left(f(t),g(t)\right)=\left(0,0\right)
, then the polynomial
P
has roots
0
and
\frac{2}{\xi^{2}}
, while
Q
has
0
as a double root.

Proof.

Let us detail the proof for
P
, similar arguments will apply to
Q
. By d’Alembert-Gauss theorem, the polynomial
P
admits exactly two roots expressed as:

\left\{\xi^{-2}\left(1-\rho\xi f(t)\pm\sqrt{\left(1-\rho\xi f(t)\right)^{2}-2\xi^{2}\left(g(t)+\frac{f^{2}(t)-f(t)}{2}\right)}\right)\right\},

where we take the principal square root in the expression above, i.e.with non-negative real-part. Consequently, the roots have real parts

\left\{\xi^{-2}\left(1\pm\Re\left(\sqrt{\left(1-\rho\xi f(t)\right)^{2}-2\xi^{2}\left(g(t)+\frac{f^{2}(t)-f(t)}{2}\right)}\right)\right)\right\},

so that it remains to show
\left|\Re\left(\sqrt{\left(1-\rho\xi f(t)\right)^{2}-2\xi^{2}\left(g(t)+\frac{f^{2}(t)-f(t)}{2}\right)}\right)\right|>1
.

Denote
\delta=a+ib
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\delta^{2}=\left(1-\rho\xi f(t)\right)^{2}-2\xi^{2}\left(g(t)+\frac{f^{2}(t)-f(t)}{2}\right)
, then it follows that
a
and
b
satisfy

\begin{cases}a^{2}-b^{2}&=1+\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)\left(\xi\Im f(t)\right)^{2},\\
ab&=-\left(\rho\xi\Im f(t)+\xi^{2}\left(\Im g(t)-\frac{\Im f(t)}{2}\right)\right).\end{cases}

(A.1)

If
\rho\neq\pm 1
, then the result is immediate from the first inequality in (
A.1
), while if
\rho=\pm 1
, then
|a|=\sqrt{1+b^{2}}
and
b
cannot be zero, otherwise
\delta^{2}=a^{2}=1-2i\left(\rho\xi\Im f(t)+\xi^{2}\left(\Im g(t)-\frac{\Im f(t)}{2}\right)\right)
which cannot be the case, since
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
\left(\Im f(t),\Im g(t)\right)\neq\left(0,0\right)
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f
and
g
be bounded measurable functions such that
\Re f=\Re g=0
. Then there exists a finite positive constant
C
such that the ratio

\gamma(s):=\frac{|\psi_{0}(s)|}{-\Re\psi_{0}(s)}\leq C,\forall s\in E,

where the set
E
is given by

E:=\left\{s\in[0,T],\left(f(s),g(s)\right)\neq\left(0,0\right)\right\},

and
\psi_{0}
is given in (
3.10
) in the case
H<-1/2
.

Proof.

We start by explicitly computing the real and imaginary parts of
\psi_{0}
in the case
H<-1/2
, whose expression is given anew by

\psi_{0}(s)=-\xi^{-1}\left(\rho f(s)+\sqrt{f(s)\left(1-\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)f(s)\right)-2g(s)}\right),\quad s\in[0,T].

Set the real functions
a
and
b
such that, for any
s\in[0,T]

a(s)+ib(s)=\sqrt{f(s)\left(1-\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)f(s)\right)-2g(s)},

square the above equality, identify the real and imaginary parts, find
a
unambiguously on
E
(which imposes
a\neq 0
) as a root to a fourth-degree polynomial knowing the square roots in (
3.9
) are principal (i.e. with positive real parts), then deduce the associated
b
, such that

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)\left(\Im f\right)^{2}+\sqrt{\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{2}\left(\Im f\right)^{4}+\left(\Im f-2\Im g\right)^{2}}\right)},

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\Im f-\Im g}{a}.

Consequently, we get explicitly on
E

\psi_{0}=\Re\psi_{0}+i\Im\psi_{0},

with

\displaystyle\Re\psi_{0}

\displaystyle=-\xi^{-1}a,

\displaystyle\Im\psi_{0}

\displaystyle=-\xi^{-1}\left(\rho\Im f+\frac{\frac{1}{2}\Im f-\Im g}{a}\right).

Rewrite
\gamma
as

\gamma=\sqrt{1+\tilde{\gamma}^{2}},\quad\tilde{\gamma}:=\frac{\Im\psi_{0}}{\Re\psi_{0}},

and we can readily discard the case
\rho=0
and
\Im f-2\Im g=0
, since
\Im\psi_{0}=0
in that case and the ratio simplifies into
1
which yields the result. Assume from now on that
\rho\neq 0
or
\Im f-2\Im g\neq 0
. We can write

\tilde{\gamma}=\mathbb{A}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)+\mathbb{B}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right),

with

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\rho\Im f}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)\left(\Im f\right)^{2}+\sqrt{\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{2}\left(\Im f\right)^{4}+\left(\Im f-2\Im g\right)^{2}}\right)}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\Im f-\Im g}{\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)\left(\Im f\right)^{2}+\sqrt{\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{2}\left(\Im f\right)^{4}+\left(\Im f-2\Im g\right)^{2}}\right)},

so that there are three remaining cases for which it is sufficient to show that both
\mathbb{A}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)
and
\mathbb{B}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)
are bounded to conclude the proof.

•

Case
\rho\neq 0
and
\Im f-2\Im g=0
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)

\displaystyle=0,

are both bounded, recall
\rho\in(-1,1)
.

•

Case
\rho=0
and
\Im f-2\Im g\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\Im f-\Im g}{\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\Im f\right)^{2}+\sqrt{\left(\Im f\right)^{4}+\left(\Im f-2\Im g\right)^{2}}\right)},

and
\mathbb{B}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)
is bounded since
\left(x,y\right)\mapsto\mathbb{B}\left(x,y\right)
is continuous on any compact set of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\left\{(0,0)\right\}
(recall both
\Im f
and
\Im g
are bounded) and has a finite limit at
\left(0,0\right)
, valued
1
, indeed

\mathbb{B}\left(x,y\right)=\frac{1}{\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)\frac{x^{2}}{x-2y}+\sqrt{\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{2}\frac{x^{4}}{\left(x-2y\right)^{2}}+1}}\underset{\underset{x-2y\neq 0}{\left(x,y\right)\to\left(0,0\right)}}{\longrightarrow}1.

•

Case
\rho\neq 0
and
\Im f-2\Im g\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho\Im f}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)\left(\Im f\right)^{2}+\sqrt{\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{2}\left(\Im f\right)^{4}+\left(\Im f-2\Im g\right)^{2}}\right)}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}\left(\Im f,\Im g\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\Im f-\Im g}{\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)\left(\Im f\right)^{2}+\sqrt{\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{2}\left(\Im f\right)^{4}+\left(\Im f-2\Im g\right)^{2}}\right)},

are both bounded by continuity of
\left(x,y\right)\mapsto\mathbb{A}\left(x,y\right)
and
\left(x,y\right)\mapsto\mathbb{B}\left(x,y\right)

on any compact set of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\left\{(0,0)\right\}
(recall both
\Im f
and
\Im g
are bounded) and both functions have a finite limit at
\left(0,0\right)
, valued
0
and
1
respectively, obtained with similar arguments as in the previous case.
