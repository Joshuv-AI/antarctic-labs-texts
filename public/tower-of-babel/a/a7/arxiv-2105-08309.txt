Time and Query-Optimal Quantum Algorithms Based on Decision Trees
Salman Beigi, Leila Taghavi, Artin Tajdini

Abstract
It has recently been shown that starting with a classical query algorithm (decision tree) and a guessing algorithm that tries to predict the query answers, we can design a quantum algorithm with query complexity O ⁡ ( G ​ T ) O(\sqrt{GT}) where T T is the query complexity of the classical algorithm (depth of the decision tree) and G G is the maximum number of wrong answers by the guessing algorithm [ LL16 , BT20 ] . In this paper we show that, given some constraints on the classical algorithms, this quantum algorithm can be implemented in time O ~ ​ ( G ​ T ) \tilde{O}(\sqrt{GT}) . Our algorithm is based on non-binary span programs and their efficient implementation. We conclude that various graph-theoretic problems including bipartiteness, cycle detection and topological sort can be solved in time O ⁡ ( n 3 / 2 ​ log 2 ​ n ) O(n^{3/2}\log^{2}n) and with O ⁡ ( n 3 / 2 ) O(n^{3/2}) quantum queries. Moreover, finding a maximal matching can be solved with O ⁡ ( n 3 / 2 ) O(n^{3/2}) quantum queries in time O ⁡ ( n 3 / 2 ​ log 2 ​ n ) O(n^{3/2}\log^{2}n) , and maximum bipartite matching can be solved in time O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log 2 ​ n ) O(n^{2}\log^{2}n) .

In fact, this spectral gap is small due to the fundamental reason that the spectral gap of the Laplacian is upper bounded by the weights of the cuts in the graph, and the weights of certain cuts in our decision tree have to remain small since they are related to a parameter of the span program called

1.1
Our proof strategy

As mentioned above,
the bound on the query complexity in Theorem
1
in
[
BT20
]
in the case of binary input alphabet (
\ell=2
), is based on span programs.
Our first step to prove Theorem
1
is to generalize the construction of
[
BT20
]
and convert a decision tree along with a guessing algorithm into a non-binary span program with complexity
O(\sqrt{\log(\ell)GT})
.
We note that this bound is an improvement over the immediate bound of
O(\log(\ell)\sqrt{GT})
that is obtained by turning an arbitrary decision tree to a binary one while blowing its depth by a
\log(\ell)
factor.

Our second step is to
compile
a wide class of non-binary span programs including the above ones, into a quantum algorithm with the same query complexity.
3
3

3

In
[
BT19
]
the fact that non-binary span programs characterize quantum query complexity is proved using the dual adversary bound, and not by directly compiling to a quantum algorithm.

This algorithm, similar to other span program based algorithms, consists of a sequence of alternative applications of two reflections. One of these reflections is a reflection through the indices of the
available input vectors
. The other one is a reflection through the kernel of a matrix
M
, which is a matrix that encapsulates the structure of the span program.

The third step of the proof is to implement the above algorithm time-efficiently. That is, to implement the aforementioned reflections time-efficiently. The first reflection is easily implementable using two quantum queries. Next, to implement reflection through
\ker(M)
, we need to take a detour. We first, by making some modifications on the starting decision tree, adjust the span program as well as the matrix
M
so that its kernel takes a tractable structure. We
explicitly compute
a basis for
\ker(M)
, and argue that the reflection through
\ker(M)
can be implemented in time
O(\log n)
.

Let us give some details about the structure of
\ker(M)
. Following
[
BT20
]
we think of the guessing algorithm as a coloring of edges of the decision tree by two colors: black and red. For any vertex of the decision tree, its outgoing edges correspond to outputs of the query at that vertex. We color the edge associated to the output of the guessing algorithm in black, and the other edge in red. Therefore, visiting a red edge means the guessing algorithm made a mistake. Next, the decision tree is adjusted in such a way that
\ker(M)
consists of a vector for each edge. Vectors associated to red edges become orthogonal to all other vectors, while vectors associated to black edges have a non-trivial overlap with those of neighboring black edges. Thus, any
black path
in the decision tree gives rise to a sequence of vectors in the kernel any two consecutive of which overlap. Next, we observe that by adding an extra black edge in the modified decision graph, and turning a black path to a
black cycle
, we can convert those vectors to orthogonal ones via
Fourier transform
.

Theorem
1
starts with two classical algorithms, namely the classical query and the guessing algorithms. We need these classical algorithms to satisfy some properties in order to obtain Theorem
1
. One immediate property is that given a vertex of the decision tree, we need to be able to locally construct the decision tree together with the coloring of the edges around that vertex at low cost. A less trivial property imposed by the above discussion about the structure of
\ker(M)
, is that we need to be able to
traverse along black paths
at low cost. This property is sometimes highly non-trivial and should be verified in applications. Moreover, the classical query algorithm requires some post-processing in order to compute the output after making all necessary queries. We need the post-processing of the classical query algorithm to have a low cost comparing to
O(\sqrt{GT})
. Finally, to implement our quantum algorithm in Theorem 1 we need access to QRAM. We give more details on these issues and assumptions in Section
4
, where we also give the formal statement of our result.

Related works

Assume that we have a time-efficient quantum algorithm computing a function

We note that we can give a proof of Theorem

[

Preliminaries

To compute a function
f:D_{f}\to[m]
, with domain
D_{f}\subseteq[\ell]^{n}
, in the query model, it is assumed that access to the input
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in D_{f}
of
f
is provided by queries to its coordinates.
In a classical query algorithm, we ask the value of some coordinate and based on its answer, decide to query another coordinate; at the end we output the result.

Such a classical query algorithm can be modeled by a
decision tree

\mathcal{T}
whose internal vertices are associated with queries, i.e., indices
1\leq j\leq n
, and whose edges correspond to answers to queries, i.e., elements of
[\ell]
. At each vertex the algorithm queries the associated index, and moves to the next vertex via the edge whose label equals the answer to that query. The algorithm ends once we reach a leaf of the tree. The leaves are labeled by elements of
[m]
, the output set of the function, and determine the output of the algorithm. The query complexity of the algorithm is the maximum number of queries in the algorithm over all
x\in D_{f}
, which is equal to the depth of the decision tree.

When the input alphabet is non-binary (
\ell>2
), it is sometimes useful to label outgoing edges of a vertex of the decision tree
\mathcal{T}
not by elements of
[\ell]
, but with subsets of
[\ell]
that form a
partition
. The point is that sometimes the query algorithm is ignorant of the exact value of a query answers, and depends only on a subset to which the query answer belongs. We refer to
[
BT20
]
for more details and explicit examples on this.

In the quantum case, queries can be made in superposition. Such a query to an input
x
can be modeled by the unitary
O_{x}
:

O_{x}|j,p\rangle=|j,(x_{j}+p)\mod\ell\rangle,

where the first register contains the query index
1\leq j\leq n
, and the second register stores the value of
x_{j}
in a reversible manner. Therefore, a quantum query algorithm for computing
f(x)
starts with a computational basis state (that is input independent), applies a unitary of the form

U_{k}O_{x}\ldots U_{2}O_{x}U_{1},

where
U_{i}
’s are input independent (yet they depend on
f
itself), and ends
by a measurement in the computational basis that determines the outcome of the algorithm. We say that an algorithm computes
f
, if for every
x\in D_{f}\subseteq[\ell]^{n}
the algorithm outputs
f(x)
with probability at least
2/3
.

The query complexity of such an algorithm is the number of queries, i.e., the number of
O_{x}
’s in the sequence of unitaries.
The time complexity of this algorithm equals the sum of the time complexity of implementing individual unitaries where the time complexity of implementing
O_{x}
is assumed to be
1
.

2.1
Non-binary span program

A
non-binary span program
(NBSP) evaluating a function
f:D_{f}\rightarrow[m]
with
D_{f}\subseteq[\ell]^{n}
consists of:

•

a finite-dimensional inner product space of the form

H=H_{1}\oplus H_{2}\oplus\ldots H_{n}\oplus H_{\rm free}\oplus H_{\rm forbid},

where each
H_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq n
can be written as

H_{j}=H_{j,0}+\cdots+H_{j,\ell-1}
. Also,
H_{\rm free}
/
H_{\rm forbid}
is the space of the vectors that are
always available/unavailable
. These two extra vector spaces are sometimes useful in the implementation of the algorithm associated to a span program.

•

a finite-dimensional inner product vector space
\mathcal{V}
,

•

m
non-zero target vectors
|t_{0}\rangle,|t_{1}\rangle,\ldots,|t_{m-1}\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
,

•

a linear operator
A:H\to\mathcal{V}
.

Given these data, for any
x\in D_{f}
, let

H(x):=\bigoplus_{1\leq j\leq n}H_{j,x_{j}}\oplus H_{\rm free}\subseteq H.

Then
AH(x)
, i.e., the image of
H(x)
under
A
, is called the
space of available vectors
for
x
.
We say that the span program evaluates
f
if for every
x\in D_{f}
there exists

•

a positive witness
\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\in H(x)
such that
A\left|w_{x}\right\rangle=\left|t_{f(x)}\right\rangle
and

•

a negative witness
\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|AH(x)=0
and
\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|t_{\beta}\rangle=1-\delta_{f(x),\beta}
for all
\beta\in[m]
.

Here, the first condition guarantees that
\left|t_{f(x)}\right\rangle
belongs to the space of available vectors, and the second condition says that
\left|t_{\beta}\right\rangle
for
\beta\neq f(x)
does not belong to the space of available vectors.

We say that the span program evaluates the function
f
if for every
x\in D_{f}
,
\left|t_{\alpha}\right\rangle
belongs to the span of the available vectors
I(x)
if and only if
\alpha=f(x)
.

The positive and negative complexities of the NBSP together with the collections
w
and
\bar{w}
of positive and negative witnesses are defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{wsize}^{+}(w,\bar{w}):=\max_{x\in D_{f}}~\|\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{wsize}^{-}(w,\bar{w}):=\max_{x\in D_{f}}~\|A^{\dagger}\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle\|^{2}.

Then the complexity of the NBSP is equal to

\displaystyle\mathrm{wsize}(P,w,\bar{w})=\sqrt{\mathrm{wsize}^{-}(P,w,\bar{w})\,\cdot\,\mathrm{wsize}^{+}(P,w,\bar{w})}.

(1)

We use the non-binary span program of

. These are a restricted class of NBSPs, so here we mention their differences. Suppose that for each

Then, we may denote the image of each of these subsets under the map

. We note that, in this case,

are determined by the index sets of those orthonormal bases, which are the same sets that index vectors in

for every
1\leq j\leq n
and
q\in[\ell]
, as well as
I_{\rm free},I_{\rm forbid}\subset\mathcal{V}
.
Then, the set of input vectors
I\subseteq\mathcal{V}
is defined by

I=I_{\rm free}\cup I_{\rm forbid}\cup\Bigg(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\bigcup_{q\in[\ell]}I_{j,q}\Big),

and for every

the set of
available vectors

I(x)
is defined by

I(x)=I_{\rm free}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}I_{j,x_{j}}.

Target vectors, and negative/positive witnesses remain the same.
Note that in this case, the linear operator

can be thought of as a

.

3
From decision trees to quantum algorithms

In this section we first show how we can convert a
non-binary span program with a special structure to a quantum query algorithm. We then prove how we can convert a classical query algorithm that computes a function
f
and a guessing algorithm that predicts queries to its input, into a non-binary span program. This non-binary span program has the required special structure.

3.1
From the span program to a quantum algorithm

In order to use span programs for proving Theorem
1
we need to describe the quantum algorithm associated to a non-binary span program.

Theorem 7
.

Consider a non-binary span program for a function
f:D_{f}\to[m]
with
D_{f}\subseteq[\ell]^{n}
.
Suppose that the target vectors of this span program are of the form

\left|t_{\alpha}\right\rangle=\left|z_{0}\right\rangle-\left|z_{\alpha}\right\rangle,\qquad 1\leq\alpha\leq m,

for some vectors
\left|z_{1}\right\rangle,\dots,\left|z_{m}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
, and the negative witness
\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle
for every
x
satisfies

\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|z_{0}\rangle=1,\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|z_{\alpha}\rangle=\delta_{f(x),\alpha},\qquad 1\leq\alpha\leq m.

Let
\epsilon>0
be a constant, and
{\rm{wsize}}^{+},{\rm{wsize}}^{-}
be the positive and negative witness sizes of the span program.
Let
A^{\prime}
be a matrix with columns
\frac{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}{\sqrt{{\rm{wsize}}^{+}}}\left|z_{0}\right\rangle,\frac{-\sqrt{2}\epsilon}{\sqrt{{\rm{wsize}}^{+}}}\left|z_{1}\right\rangle,\ldots,\frac{-\sqrt{2}\epsilon}{\sqrt{{\rm{wsize}}^{+}}}\left|z_{m}\right\rangle
, i.e.,

\displaystyle A^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}{\sqrt{{\rm{wsize}}^{+}}}\begin{bmatrix}\left|z_{0}\right\rangle&-\left|z_{1}\right\rangle&\ldots&-\left|z_{m}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}{\sqrt{{\rm{wsize}}^{+}}}\Big(\left|z_{0}\right\rangle\left\langle e_{z_{0}}\right|-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\left|z_{\alpha}\right\rangle\left\langle e_{z_{\alpha}}\right|\Big),

where
e_{z_{j}}
’s are indices of the columns of
A^{\prime}
.
Also, let
M=[A^{\prime},A]
be a column-padded matrix, with first the columns of
A^{\prime}
and then the columns of
A
. Equivalently, define

M:{\rm span}\{\left|e_{z_{0}}\right\rangle,\left|e_{z_{1}}\right\rangle,\dots,\left|e_{z_{m}}\right\rangle\}\oplus H\to\mathcal{V}
by
M=A^{\prime}\oplus A
.
Let
\Lambda
be the orthogonal projection on the kernel of
M
, and let

\Pi_{x}=\sum_{j\in\{0,\ldots,m\}}\left|j\right\rangle\left\langle j\right|+\Pi^{\prime}_{x},

where
\Pi^{\prime}_{x}
denotes projection on
AH(x)
.
Then, there is a bounded error quantum algorithm computing
f(x)
based on repeated applications of the reflections
2\Lambda-I
and
2\Pi_{x}-I
for
O(W)
times, where
W=\sqrt{{\rm{wsize}}^{+}{\rm{wsize}}^{-}}
is the complexity of the span program.

The proof of this theorem given in Appendix
A
, is based on ideas from
[
LMR+11
]
.

In the statement of Theorem
7
we restrict the structure of the target vectors and negative witnesses. We note that any non-binary span program can be transformed to a
canonical form

[
BT19
]
whose target vectors and negative witnesses do have this structure. Thus, this theorem can be applied on any span program after transforming it to a canonical one.

3.2
From a decision tree to a span program

In order to use Theorem
7
towards the proof of Theorem
1
, we need to first construct a non-binary span program based on the setup of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 8
.

Assume that we have a classical algorithm for a function
f:D_{f}\to[m]
with
D_{f}\subseteq[\ell]^{n}
whose query complexity is
T
. Furthermore, assume that we have a guessing algorithm that predicts the values of queried bits, making at most
G
mistakes. Then there exists a non-binary span program computing the function
f
with query complexity
O(\sqrt{GT})
.

We note that this result in the special case of
\ell=2
is already proven in
[
BT20
]
. Furthermore, the existence of such a non-binary span program in the general case can already be proven using the known bound of
O(\sqrt{GT})
on the quantum query complexity, and the fact that NBSPs characterize quantum query complexity up to a constant factor. Nevertheless, here in order to use Theorem
7
to prove Theorem
1
, we need the explicit structure of the span program given by Theorem
8
.

Let
f:D_{f}\to[m]
with
D_{f}\subseteq[\ell]^{n}
be an arbitrary function. Also, let
\mathcal{T}
be a decision tree for
f
with depth
T
. This means that
internal vertices of
\mathcal{T}
are indexed by
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and outgoing edges are labeled by elements (or in general subsets in partitions) of
[\ell]
. Moreover, leaves are indexed by elements of
[m]
. Following
[
BT20
]
, we use two colors, black and red, to present the behavior of the guessing algorithm. At each vertex with associated index
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the guessing algorithm predicts a value for
x_{j}
. We color the outgoing edge of that vertex whose label equals (contains) the predicted value in black. We color the rest of the outgoing edges in red. We call such a coloring of edges of a decision tree a
G-coloring
. Note that, in a G-coloring, each vertex has exactly one black outgoing edge. Also note that, by the assumption in the theorem, in each path from the root to a leaf of
\mathcal{T}
, there are at most
G
red edges.

Here, for simplicity of presentation and the fact that to prove Theorem
1
, the non-binary span program with binary inputs is sufficient, we prove Theorem
8
in the special case of
\ell=2
.
This proof, taken from
[
BT20
]
is based on a span program with orthogonal inputs. The proof of this theorem in the general case is given in Appendix
B
.

Proof.

To prove the theorem we present a non-binary span program with complexity
O(\sqrt{GT})
. Our span program however, is not for
f
but for the function
\tilde{f}
which sends any
x\in D_{f}
to an associated leaf of the decision tree
\mathcal{T}
. Observe that in the classical algorithm we start with the root and follow edges of
\mathcal{T}
labeled by
x_{j}
’s until we reach a leaf which we denote by
\tilde{f}(x)
. As the classical algorithm essentially finds
\tilde{f}(x)
(from which
f(x)
can be obtained), to prove the theorem we just need to design a span program for
\tilde{f}
.

To present this span program first we need to develop some notation. Let
V=V_{\rm{in}}\cup V_{\rm leaf}
be the vertex set of
\mathcal{T}
, where
V_{\rm{in}}
denotes the set of internal vertices, and
V_{\rm leaf}
the leaves of
\mathcal{T}
. Then, for every vertex
v\in V_{\rm{in}}
, its associated index is denoted by
J(v)
, i.e.,
J(v)
is the index
1\leq j\leq n
that is queried by the classical algorithm at node
v
. The two outgoing edges of
v
are indexed by elements of
\{0,1\}
and connect
v
to two other vertices. We denote these vertices by
N(v,0)
and
N(v,1)
. We also represent the G-coloring of edges of
\mathcal{T}
by a function
C(v,q)\in\{{\rm{black}},{\rm{red}}\}
which denotes the color of the outgoing edge of
v
with label
q
.

We can now describe our non-binary span program:

•

the vector space
\mathcal{V}
is
|V|
-dimensional with the orthonormal basis

\{\left|v\right\rangle\,:\,v\in V\},

•

the input vectors are

I_{j,q}=\Big\{\sqrt{W_{C(v,q)}}\big(\left|v\right\rangle-\left|{N(v,q)}\right\rangle\big)\,:\,v\in V\text{ s.t. }J(v)=j\Big\},

where
W_{{\rm{black}}}
and
W_{{\rm{red}}}
are positive real numbers to be determined,

•

the target vectors are indexed by leaves
z
of the tree:

\left|t_{z}\right\rangle=\left|z_{0}\right\rangle-\left|z\right\rangle,

where as before
z_{0}\in V
is the root of
\mathcal{T}
.

For every
x\in D_{f}
let
P_{x}=P_{\tilde{f}(x)}
be the path from the root of the decision tree to the leaf
\tilde{f}(x)
. Thus the target vector
\big|t_{\tilde{f}(x)}\big\rangle
equals

\big|t_{\tilde{f}(x)}\big\rangle=\left|z_{0}\right\rangle-\big|\tilde{f}(x)\big\rangle=\sum_{v\in P_{x}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{W_{C\big(v,x_{J(v)}\big)}}}\left\{\sqrt{W_{C\big(v,x_{J(v)}\big)}}\left(\left|v\right\rangle-\left|N(v,x_{J(v)})\right\rangle\right)\right\},

where the vectors in the braces are all available for
x
. Since by assumption the number of red edges along the path
P_{x}
is at most
G
and the number of all edges of
P_{x}
is at most
T
, the positive complexity is bounded by

{\rm{wsize}}^{+}\leq\frac{1}{W_{{\rm{red}}}}G+\frac{1}{W_{{\rm{black}}}}T.

We let the negative witness for
x
be

\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle=\sum_{v\in P_{x}}\left|v\right\rangle.

It is easy to verify that
\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle
is orthogonal to all available vectors, and that
\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|t_{u}\rangle=\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|z_{0}\rangle=1
for all
u\neq\tilde{f}(x)
. Thus
\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle
is a valid negative witness. Moreover, an input vector of the form

\sqrt{W_{C(v,q)}}\big(\left|v\right\rangle-\left|{N(v,q)}\right\rangle\big),

contributes in the negative witness size only if its corresponding edge
(v,N(v,q))
leaves the path
P_{x}
, i.e., they have only the vertex
v
in common. In this case the contribution would be equal to
W_{C(v,q)}
, the weight of that edge. The number of such red (black) edges equals the number of black (red) edges in
P_{x}
, which is bounded by
T
(
G
). Therefore, the negative witness size is bouned by

{\rm{wsize}}^{-}\leq W_{{\rm{black}}}G+W_{{\rm{red}}}T\big.

Now letting
W_{\rm{black}}=\frac{1}{G}
and
W_{\rm{red}}=\frac{1}{T}
, we have
{\rm{wsize}}^{+}\leq 2GT
and
{\rm{wsize}}^{-}\leq 2
.
Therefore, the quantum query complexity of
\tilde{f}
, and hence also
f
are bounded by
O(\sqrt{GT})
.
∎

4
Time-efficient implementation

This section is dedicated to the proof of our main result that is formally stated in Theorem
9
below. To prove our result, we start with the query-efficient algorithm provided by
Theorem
7
and Theorem
8
and try to implement it time-efficiently.

In order to obtain a time-efficient quantum algorithm, in addition to the query complexity of the underlying classical algorithms that we start with, their time complexity also matter. Indeed, we need the cost of certain operations in the classical algorithms that build the decision tree and the appropriate coloring of its edges to be bounded.

The state space of our proposed quantum algorithm described by Theorem
7
is essentially the edge set (or the vertex set) of the decision tree
\mathcal{T}
. Indeed, in the classical algorithm also each vertex of the decision tree corresponds to a
state
of the algorithm. Thus the state space of our quantum algorithm is essentially the same as the state space of the underlying classical algorithm. However, in order to implement the algorithm time-efficiently we need to keep track of the correspondence between the state space of the algorithm and vertices of the decision tree. This correspondence is provided by some local operations mentioned above. Thus, in the following we assume that the following classical subroutines have bounded time complexity:

•

Local subroutine:
We assume that there is a subroutine
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{Local}}}
that given a state of the classical algorithm (a vertex of the decision tree) outputs the local structure of the decision tree in a neighborhood of that vertex. In particular given a vertex,
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{Local}}
returns the followings:

–

The type of the vertex i.e. root, leaf, or internal vertex

–

The parent vertex, list of children vertices and the color of the edges connecting them

–

The query associated to the vertex and the query answer associated with each of the neighboring edges

•

BlackPath subroutine:
Note that any vertex of the decision tree, except leaves connected to a red edge, belongs to a path of black edges; see Figures
1
for an illustrative example. We assume that there is a subroutine
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{BlackPath}}}
that given a state of the classical algorithm (a vertex of the decision tree), returns the followings about the black path containing that vertex:

–

The
length
of the black path.

–

Given additional input
k
, the
k
-th vertex on the black path.

•

PostProcess subroutine:
The classical algorithm after making all the queries and moving from the root of the decision tree to a leaf, makes some (classical) post-processing in order to compute the final output of the function. We assume that this is done by a subroutine denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{PostProcess}}}
.

As discussed above the necessity of subroutines
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{Local}}},\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{PostProcess}}}
is apparent. However, the necessity of
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{BlackPath}}}
will become clear later.

We note that
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{BlackPath}}}
, gives global information about the structure of the decision tree beyond local information. Nevertheless, recall that black edges correspond to making correct guesses of query answers. Then, we expect that updating the state of the algorithm as we move along a black path is not costly.

Finally, before giving the formal statement of our main result, we need to clarify a few points regarding our setup for quantum algorithms:

-

In our quantum algorithms we assume that
all one-qubit gates
as well as the CNOT gate are available. We also assume the full connectivity of the qubits, meaning that CNOT can be applied on any pair of qubits.

-

The time (or circuit) complexity of algorithms are measured in terms of the number of gates and the number of queries.

-

More importantly, we assume accessibility to quantum RAM (QRAM).

Theorem 9
.

Assume that we have a classical algorithm that computes a function
f:D_{f}\to[m]
with
D_{f}\subseteq[\ell]^{n}
whose query complexity is
T
. Furthermore, assume that we have

•

a guessing algorithm that predicts the values of queried bits that makes at most
G
mistakes,

•

classical subroutines
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{Local}},\mathcal{A}_{\rm{BlackPath}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{PostProcess}}}
described above that can be implemented in time
C_{\rm{Local}},C_{\rm{BlackPath}}
and
C_{{\rm{PostProcess}}}
respectively.

Then, having access to QRAM, there is a quantum algorithm computing the function
f
with query complexity
O(\sqrt{\log(\ell)GT+\log^{2}(\ell)G^{2}})
and time (circuit) complexity

O\left(\big(C_{\rm{Local}}+C_{\rm{BlackPath}}+\log^{2}n\big)\sqrt{\log{(\ell)}GT+\log^{2}(\ell)G^{2}}+C_{{\rm{PostProcess}}}\right).

In particular, if
\log(\ell)G=O(T)
, that is usually the case in applications, the query complexity equals
O(\sqrt{\log(\ell)GT})
and the. time complexity is

O\left(\big(C_{\rm{Local}}+C_{\rm{BlackPath}}+\log^{2}n\big)\sqrt{\log{(\ell)}GT}+C_{{\rm{PostProcess}}}\right).

Note that a decision tree may have an exponential number of edges, so the state space of the classical algorithm may have at least linear size. That is, to encode the state of the algorithm we may need at least
\Omega(n)
bits. If this encoding is not done properly, to update the state of the algorithm in each step, we need to update all these
\Omega(n)
bits, and the cost of implementing subroutines
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{Local}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{BlackPath}}
becomes at least
\Omega(n)
. To reduce this cost, we need to encode the state of the algorithm deliberately in such a way that we do not need to read the entire encoding of a state in each update.

Our proof of Theorem
9
is based on the algorithm provided by Theorem
7
.
This algorithm consists of repeated applications of the unitary
U=R_{\Lambda}R_{\Pi}
, where
R_{\Pi}=2\Pi-I
and
R_{\Lambda}=2\Lambda-I
are specified by Theorem
8
. Thus to prove our result we need to implement both
R_{\Pi}
and
R_{\Lambda}
in time
O(C_{\rm{Local}}+C_{\rm{BlackPath}}+\log^{2}n)
.
Implementation of
R_{\Pi}
is straightforward and will be taken care of later. However, time-efficient implementation of
R_{\Lambda}
is more involved.

Recall that
R_{\Lambda}
is the reflection through the kernel of
M
. Thus the implementation of
R_{\Lambda}
amounts to the inspection of the structure of
\ker(M)
. To this end, in the following we start with the easy case of
\ell=2
and
G=1
. We observe that by slightly changing the underlying span program (while keeping its complexity of the same order),
\ker(M)
takes a nice structure. Then employing the Fourier transform, we would be able to implement
R_{\Lambda}
in time
O(C_{\rm{Local}}+C_{\rm{BlackPath}}+\log^{2}n)
.

In this appendix we show that given a non-binary decision tree
\mathcal{T}
of depth
T
together with a coloring of its edges, we can convert it to a non-binary span program (NBSP) with complexity
O(\sqrt{GT})
, where
G
is the maximum number of red edges from the root to leaves of
\mathcal{T}
.

We start by defining a non-binary decision tree for functions with non-binary input from
[
BT20
]
.

Definition 13

(Generalized decision tree and G-coloring)
.

A generalized decision tree
\mathcal{T}
is a rooted directed tree such that each internal vertex
v
(including the root) of
\mathcal{T}
corresponds to a query index
1\leq J(v)\leq n
. Outgoing edges of
v
are labeled by subsets of
[\ell]
that form a partition of
[\ell]
.
Leaves of
\mathcal{T}
are labeled with elements of
[m]
.
We say that
\mathcal{T}
decides a function
f:D_{f}\to[m]
with
D_{f}\subseteq[\ell]^{n}
if for every
x\in D_{f}
, by starting from the root of
\mathcal{T}
and following edges labeled by
Q_{v}(x_{J(v)})
we reach a leaf with label
m=f(x)
.
As in the case of binary functions, a G-coloring of a generalized decision tree
\mathcal{T}
is a coloring of its edges by two colors black and red, in such a way that any vertex of
\mathcal{T}
has exactly one outgoing edge with black color.

We emphasize that in a generalized decision tree, some query outcomes may be grouped into a single edge if decisions made by the algorithm after this query are independent of which particular query outcome within the group occurred. Therefore labels of edges are subsets of
[\ell]
. See
[
BT20
]
for more details on this.

We now construct a NBSP based on a generalized decision tree
\mathcal{T}
.
We note that, as in the binary case, instead of
f
it suffices to construct an NBSP associated to the function
\tilde{f}
that outputs a leaf of
\mathcal{T}
corresponding to each

.

•

The vector space
\mathcal{V}
is
|{V}|
-dimensional with the orthonormal basis
\{\left|v\right\rangle\,:\,v\in{V}\}
, where
V
is the vertex set of
\mathcal{T}
.

•

To define the vector space
H
we first define an auxiliary space
\widehat{H}
with the orthonormal basis

\big\{\left|v,{\rm{black}}\right\rangle,\,\left|v,{\rm{red}}\right\rangle,\left|v^{\#}\right\rangle:\,v\in V_{\rm{in}}\big\}\cup\big\{\left|v\right\rangle\,:\,v\in V\big\}.

where
V_{\rm{in}}
is the set of internal vertices of
\mathcal{T}
which correspond to query indices.

Then let the input vector space
H_{j,q}\subseteq\widehat{H}
be

H_{j,q}={\rm span}\Big\{\left|v,C(v,q)\right\rangle-\left|N(v,q)\right\rangle\,:\,v\in V_{\rm{in}},\,J(v)=j\Big\},

where by
N(v,q)
we mean the child of vertex
v
in
\mathcal{T}
whose label
contains

q\in[\ell]
, and
C(v,q)
is the color of the associated edge.
Then, let
H_{j}=H_{j,0}+\cdots+H_{j,(\ell-1)}.

•

H_{\rm free}={\rm span}\left\{\left|v^{\#}\right\rangle:v\in{V}_{{\rm{in}}}\right\}
.

•

H_{\rm forbid}={\rm span}\big\{\left|v,{\rm{red}}\right\rangle:v\in{V}_{\rm{in}}\big\}
.

•

Then let
H=H_{1}+\cdots+H_{n}+H_{\rm free}+H_{\rm forbid}

that is a subspace of
\widehat{H}
. Indeed,
H
equals the span of the following vectors:

-

\left|v,{\rm{black}}\right\rangle-\left|b_{v}\right\rangle
for
v\in{V}_{{\rm{in}}}
where
b_{v}
is the
unique black child
of
v

-

\left|v,{\rm{red}}\right\rangle
for
v\in{V}_{{\rm{in}}}

-

\left|v\right\rangle
for
v\in V^{r}

-

\left|v^{\#}\right\rangle
for
v\in{V}_{{\rm{in}}}

Here, by
V^{r}
we mean the set of vertices of
\mathcal{T}
that have a
red parent edge
.

•

The target vectors are indexed by leaves
z\in V_{\rm{leaf}}
of the decision tree:

\left|t_{z}\right\rangle=\left|v_{0}\right\rangle-\left|z\right\rangle,

where
v_{0}
is the root of
\mathcal{T}
.

•

Define the operator
\widehat{A}:\widehat{H}\to\mathcal{V}
by

\displaystyle\widehat{A}\left|v,c\right\rangle=\sqrt{W_{c}}\sum_{u\in N(v)}\left|u\right\rangle

\displaystyle\forall v\in{V}_{\rm{in}},\;c\in\{{\rm{red}},{\rm{black}}\}

\displaystyle\widehat{A}\left|v\right\rangle=\sqrt{W_{{\rm{black}}}}\left|v\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\forall v\in{V}^{b}

\displaystyle\widehat{A}\left|v\right\rangle=\sqrt{W_{{\rm{red}}}}\left|v\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\forall v\in{V}^{r}

\displaystyle\widehat{A}\left|v^{\#}\right\rangle=\left|v\right\rangle-\sum_{u\in N(v)}\left|u\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\forall v\in{V}_{\rm{in}}.

Here
N(v)
is the set of children of
v
,
W_{\rm{black}},W_{\rm{red}}
are two parameters to be determined, and
V^{b}
is the set of vertices of
\mathcal{T}
whose parent edge is black.
Then let
A:=\widehat{A}\big|_{H}:H\to\mathcal{V}
.

Now we show that this is a valid span program and compute its complexity. For any
x\in D_{f}
let
z_{\tilde{f}(x)}
be the leaf associated to
\tilde{f}(x)
, and let
P(x)
be the path from the root
z_{0}
to the leaf
z_{\tilde{f}(x)}
. Then the positive witness
\left|w_{x}\right\rangle\in H(x)
is

\displaystyle\left|w_{x}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in P(x)\cap V_{\rm{in}}}\left|v^{\#}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{W_{C(v,x_{J(v)})}}}\Big(\left|v,C(v,x_{J(v)})\right\rangle-\left|N(v,x_{J(v)})\right\rangle\Big).

Thus the positive complexity is bounded by

{\rm{wsize}}^{+}\leq T+\frac{2}{W_{\rm{black}}}T+\frac{2}{W_{\rm{red}}}G.

The negative witness equals
\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle=\sum_{v\in P(x)}\left|v\right\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
. We note that
\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|t_{\alpha}\rangle=1-\delta_{f(x),\alpha}
for any
\alpha
. Moreover, it is not hard to verify that
\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle
is orthogonal to
AH(x)
. Thus
\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle
is a valid negative witness. Next, we compute the negative complexity.
For a vertex
v\in V_{{\rm{in}}}
let

H_{v}:={\rm span}\Big\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\left|v,{\rm{black}}\right\rangle-\left|b_{v})\right\rangle,\left|v,{\rm{red}}\right\rangle\Big\}\cup\big\{\left|u\right\rangle\,:\,u\in N(v)\cap V^{r}\big\}.

These subspaces are orthogonal to each other and to
H_{{\rm free}}
. Indeed, we have

H=\bigoplus_{v\in V_{\rm{in}}}H_{v}\oplus H_{\rm free}.

Note that as a valid negative witness,
\left|\bar{w}_{x}\right\rangle
is orthogonal to
AH_{\rm free}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V_{\rm{in}}}\left\|\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\big|_{H_{v}}\right\|^{2}.

Next, we note that if
v\notin P(x)
, then
\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\big|_{H_{v}}=0
.
Otherwise, if
v\in P(x)
, there are two cases:

1.

If
C(v,x_{J(v)})={\rm{black}}
, then

\displaystyle\left\|\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\big|_{H_{v}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big|{\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\Big(\left|v,{\rm{black}}\right\rangle-\left|N(v,{x_{J(v)}})\right\rangle\Big)}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big|{\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\left|v,{\rm{red}}\right\rangle}\Big|^{2}+\sum_{u\in N(V)\cap V^{r}}\Big|{\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\left|u\right\rangle}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=0+W_{\rm{red}}+0.

2.

If
C(v,x_{J(v)})={\rm{red}}
, then

\displaystyle\left\|\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\big|_{H_{v}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big|{\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\Big(\left|v,{\rm{black}}\right\rangle-\left|b_{v}\right\rangle\Big)}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big|{\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\left|v,{\rm{red}}\right\rangle}\Big|^{2}+\sum_{u\in N(V)\cap V^{r}}\Big|{\left\langle\bar{w}_{x}\right|A\left|u\right\rangle}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}W_{\rm{black}}+W_{\rm{red}}+W_{\rm{red}}.

Putting these together, we obtain

{\rm{wsize}}^{-}_{x}\leq W_{\rm{red}}T+(W_{\rm{black}}/2+2W_{\rm{red}})G.

Now letting
W_{\rm{black}}=\frac{1}{G}
and
W_{\rm{red}}=\frac{1}{T}
and using
G\leq T
, we get
{\rm{wsize}}^{+}=O(GT)
and
{\rm{wsize}}^{-}=O(1)
. This means that the total complexity is
O(\sqrt{GT})
as desired.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
9
for generalized non-binary decision trees

In this appendix we give the proof of Theorem
9
in its most general form: for
\ell>2
and for generalized non-binary decision trees.
The main difference of a decision tree and a non-binary decision tree is the degree of its internal vertices. We refer to Appendix
B
for a detailed definition of generalized non-binary decision trees.

Figure 6
:
Converting a non-binary decision tree to a binary one. A vertex of the decision tree with four children (a), can be replaced with a depth-2 subtree (b). This approach although works increases the depth of the tree by a
\log(\ell)
factor. In (c) we keep the black edge of (a), i.e., the edge with label
D
, untouched while replacing the other three children with a binary tree. This process changes the depth of the tree from
T
to
O\big(T+\log(\ell)G\big)
.

If we use a span program with the same structure as what we defined in Theorem
8
for a function
f:D_{f}\to[m]
with
D_{f}\in[\ell]^{n}
, we get a
O(\sqrt{\ell-1})
blow up in the complexity of the span program. An alternating solution is to use the non-binary span program that we defined in Appendix
B
, and to convert it to a quantum algorithm and try to implement it time-efficiently. It turns out that the construction of the kernel of the matrix
M
for the NBSP of Appendix
B
is quite complicated, and we do not know how to implement the reflection through its kernel time-efficiently (even after modifying the decision tree).
Thus, our strategy to handle the case of
\ell>2
is to turn the non-binary decision tree to a binary one at the cost of a
\sqrt{\log(\ell)}
blow up in the query complexity.

One may suggest that we can encode every symbol of the input using
\log(\ell)
binary bits and treat the function as it has a binary input (see Figure
6
b). Although this approach does work, it scales the query and time complexities by
\log(\ell)
.
Here, to get an improved scaling
we take a different approach. When converting a non-binary decision tree to a binary one, for any internal vertex
v
, we keep its outgoing black edge untouched, yet we replace the outgoing red edges by a binary tree of depth
\log\ell
(see Figure
6
c). This construction converts any red edge into a path of length
\log(\ell)
in the resulting binary tree.
This decision tree has depth
T^{\prime}=T+G\log(\ell)
. Moreover, there are at most
G^{\prime}=G\log(\ell)
red edges in any path from its root to any of its leaves. So the query complexity of the resulting algorithm is
O(\sqrt{G^{\prime}T^{\prime}})=O(\sqrt{\log(\ell)GT+\log^{2}(\ell)G^{2}})
.
Therefore, assuming that
\log(\ell)G=O(T)
, that is usually the case in applications, we only get a
\sqrt{\log\ell}
blow up in its query complexity.
Then using Theorem
9
in the binary case, we obtain the result for arbitrary
\ell
.
Here, we should also mention that modifying the decision tree as above, imposes modifications in the subroutines
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{Local}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{BlackPath}}
. These modifications are
local
and prescribed as illustrated in the example of Figure
6
. Moreover, they increase the time complexity of the subroutines
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{Local}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{BlackPath}}
associated to the modified tree, by a constant additive term. This is because, e.g., for
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{Local}}}
, given any vertex of the modified tree, whose address is assumed to be given in terms of its parent vertex in the original tree, we can query
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{Local}}}
of the original tree to find the children of its parent vertex in the original tree, and then compute the children in the modified tree according to Figure
6
. The subroutine
\mathcal{A}_{{\rm{BlackPath}}}
is also updated for the modified tree similarly.
