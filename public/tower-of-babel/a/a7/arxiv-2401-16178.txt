Bézier curves and the Takagi function Journal: Bulletin des sciences mathématiques
Lenka Ptáčková, Franco Vivaldi

Abstract
We consider Bézier curves with complex parameters, and we determine explicitly the affine iterated function system (IFS) corresponding to the de Casteljau subdivision algorithm, together with the complex parametric domain over which such an IFS has a unique global connected attractor. For a specific family of complex parameters having vanishing imaginary part, we prove that the Takagi fractal curve is the attractor, under suitable scaling.

Introduction

We consider connections between a subdivision scheme
of geometric modelling, the de Casteljau algorithm for
Bézier curves, and iterated function systems of fractal theory.
We obtain an explicit representation of this algorithm as a pair of affine
maps with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[t]
, the ring of polynomials in
t
with
integer coefficients. Here
t
is the parameter of the Bézier curve,
and fractals are generated by allowing
t
to assume complex values.
After determining the range of
t
-values for which the IFS has a unique
global connected attractor, we establish rigorously the appearance of
the Takagi fractal curve in a specific asymptotic regime.

The Takagi curve is the graph of a continuous and nowhere differentiable
function, the Takagi function, introduced by Teiji Takagi in 1903, and
subsequently rediscovered several times, e.g., by Hildebrandt in 1933
or de Rham in 1957
[
18
]
.
The Takagi function appears in many areas of mathematics, including analysis, probability
theory, combinatorics, and number theory —see the surveys

[
1
,
18
]
and references therein.

In geometric modelling, the recursive construction of fractals has
proved valuable for generating realistic (i.e., typically
non-smooth) forms. This approach originated from the work of Barnsley
and co-workers on fractal image compression
[
3
]
,
using the machinery of iterated function systems; these are dynamical
systems consisting of collections of contraction mappings

[
2
, section 3.7]
.
This idea later appeared in the context of subdivision schemes,
such as Bézier and B-spline curves, which are widely used in
geometric modelling, computer graphics, and
computer aided design, due to the simplicity of their construction.

The first such application is due to R. Goldman
[
11
]
,
who presents a constructive procedure that builds an IFS based on the
de Casteljau subdivision algorithm, and shows that Bézier curves are
attractors of such an IFS.

Schaefer et al.
[
23
]
extend the idea by showing that
curves and surfaces generated by several well-known subdivision algorithms
are also attractors, fixed points of IFSs.
They demonstrate how any curve generated by an arbitrary stationary subdivision scheme or subdivision surfaces without extraordinary

vertices can be represented by an IFS.
Furthermore, they provide a general paradigm for introducing control points and subdivision rules for arbitrary fractals generated by an IFS consisting of affine transformations, such as the Sierpinski gasket or the Koch curve. Moving the control points of these fractals induces transformations that are affine (but not necessarily conformal).

Tsianos and Goldman
[
24
]
study the opposite direction:
they show how to apply the de Casteljau subdivision algorithm and several standard knot insertion procedures for B-splines to build fractal shapes.
They do so by allowing the parameters, or knots, to be complex numbers.
Then they construct IFSs for these extended versions of classical subdivision
algorithms, where the control points and the parameters are taken from
the complex plane. They prove that starting with any control polygon,
the IFS for Bézier curves with a given complex parameter converges
uniformly to a limiting curve.
Furthermore, they show that every fractal in the plane generated by a
conformal IFS can be reproduced by Bézier subdivision in the complex
plane
[
24
, Corollary 3.3]
.
They can thus generate fractals with control points; however,
in contrast to
[
23
]
, adjusting the control points
induces transformations that are conformal but not necessarily affine.

Some well-known subdivision schemes can also produce fractal curves and
surfaces. For example, the four-point interpolating subdivision scheme
introduced by Dyn et al.
[
7
]
is defined using a tension parameter

\omega
, and the smoothness of the limit curves depends on this parameter.
Thus while the limit curve is almost
C^{2}
-continuous for some
values of
\omega
, for other values one obtains
fractal curves and surfaces, as shown in
[
13
]
.

Connections between splines and fractals also emerge if one allows the order
of a polynomial B-spline to be a real or complex number, leading to
fractional and complex B-splines, respectively —see
[
10
,
22
]
and references therein.
The complexification of the order is also available for the

exponential
B-splines, defined as convolution products of
exponential functions, see
[
21
,
22
]
and
references therein.
However, according to
[
22
]
, neither the classical nor
extended polynomial and exponential B-splines provide appropriate
approximations of functions that exhibit self–similar or fractal
behaviour. In these cases, one needs to resort to fractal interpolation
and approximation techniques.
The extension of polynomial B-splines to self–similar or fractal
functions was presented in
[
20
]
(see also

[
22
, Section 5]
).

2
Iterated function systems

We provide some background on iterated function systems
—see
[
2
]
. For basic dynamical systems
terminology, see also
[
16
]
.

An iterated function system is a dynamical system whose
‘points’ are subsets of a set,
\mathbb{C}^{m}
in the present setting.
The appropriate space for such a system is a
complete metric space
,
namely a pair
(X,d)
, where
X
is a set, and
d
is a metric on
X
with respect to which all Cauchy sequences
in
X
converge to a limit in
X
.

Further, a mapping
f
of
(X,d)
to itself is a

contraction mapping
if there is a non-negative real
number
s<1
such that for all
x,y\in X

d(f(x),f(y))\leqslant s\cdot d(x,y)\qquad 0\leqslant s<1.

The smallest such number
s
is called the
contractivity factor

for
f
.

The relevance of contraction mappings in our context is given by
the
Banach fixed point theorem

[
17
, Section 5.1]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

A contraction mapping of a complete metric space has a unique fixed point.
Such a fixed point is an attractor, whose basin of attraction is the whole
space.

Fractals are compact sets, and we are interested in the set
\mathcal{H}(X)

of all compact subsets of a set
X
. If
(X,d)
is a complete metric space,
one defines a metric
d_{H}
on
\mathcal{H}(X)
—the
Haussdorf
metric
— which turns it into a complete metric space.

This is done as follows.
Given
x\in X
and
B\in\mathcal{H}(X)
, we first define the distance
from
x
to
B
as
d(x,B)=\min\{d(x,y)\,|\,y\in B\}
.
Then, given
A\in\mathcal{H}(X)
we define the distance from
A
to
B
as

d(A,B)=\max\{d(x,B)\,|\,x\in A\}.

(1)

Finally, by making the above symmetric, one obtains the Hausdorff metric

d_{H}
on
\mathcal{H}(X)
:

\mathrm{d}_{H}(A,B)=\max\{d(A,B),d(B,A)\}.

(2)

The metric space
(\mathcal{H}(X),\mathrm{d}_{H})
is complete

[
2
, Section 7]
, and we
now define a contraction mapping on
\mathcal{H}(X)
.

Definition
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and let

f_{k}:X\rightarrow X,\;k=1,\dots N
be a collection of
contraction mappings with respective contractivity factors
s_{k}
.
The dynamical system

F:\mathcal{H}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(X)\hskip 30.0ptF(A)=\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}f_{k}(A)

(3)

is called a (hyperbolic)
iterated function system (IFS)
on
X
.

Then we have
[
15
]
:

Theorem 2.2

(Hutchinson)
.

The transformation
F
given in (
3
) is a contraction mapping
on the complete metric space
(\mathcal{H}(X),\mathrm{d}_{H})
with contractivity
factor
s=\max\{s_{k}\;|\;k=1,\dots,N\}
.

From the above and Banach theorem
2.1
,
we conclude that
F
has its unique fixed point
A^{\ast}\in\mathcal{H}(X)
,
which obeys

A^{\ast}=F(A^{\ast})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{k}(A^{\ast}),

and can be obtained as the limit

A^{\ast}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F^{n}(B)\enskip\text{for any}\enskip B\in\mathcal{H}(X).

The above fixed point will be called the
attractor of the IFS
.

Many well-known fractals are generated by affine
iterated function systems. We consider maps of the form

f:\mathbb{C}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}\hskip 30.0ptf(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b},

(4)

where
\mathbf{A}
is an
m\times m
regular matrix and
\mathbf{b}

is an
m
-dimensional vector.

3
IFS for subdivision curves

Subdivision curves are defined recursively. The process starts with a
set of control points
\mathbf{P}
as input, a refinement scheme is applied
to them, generating as output a new refined set of control points. The subdivision curve is the limit of this recursive
process.

In
[
23
,
24
]
a procedure is presented
to generate an IFS for binary subdivision curves, including uniform
B-spline curves and Bézier curves.
We provide an alternative representation of the IFS of Bézier curves,
and prove that in a suitable range of complex parameters this IFS is
contractive and therefore has a unique fixed point, the
global attractor. We also establish that in the same parametric
domain the attractor is connected.

We first review some basic facts concerning Bézier curves.

3.1
Bézier Curves and the de Casteljau subdivision algorithm

Bézier curves were popularized in 1962 by the French engineer Pierre Bézier, who worked for Renault. They can be expressed explicitly as parametric curves using the Bernstein basis polynomials.
Alternatively, points on a Bézier curve can be constructed by the de Casteljau algorithm, which was introduced by Paul de Casteljau already in 1959. He worked for Citroën and his work was kept a secret by Citroën for a long time
[
9
]
. P. de Casteljau and P. Bézier had different approaches and they introduced Bézier curves independently, but P. Bézier could publish his work first.

The de Casteljau algorithm generalises to polynomial curves of arbitrary degree
the construction of a parabola by repeated linear interpolation
[
8
]
.
It is defined as follows:
Let
\textbf{p}_{0},\dots,\textbf{p}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
set of control points and
t\in[0,1]
. We set

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{0}(t)&=&\mathbf{p}_{i}\\
\mathbf{p}_{i}^{k}(t)&=&(1-t)\mathbf{p}_{i}^{k-1}(t)+t\mathbf{p}_{i+1}^{k-1}(t)\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}k&=&1,\dots,n\\
i&=&0,\dots,n-k.\end{array}\right.\end{array}\right.

(5)

Then
\mathbf{p}_{0}^{n}(t)
is the point with parameter value
t
on the Bézier curve of degree
n
.

The de Casteljau algorithm can be viewed as a subdivision scheme, a method for finding new control points
\textbf{l}_{0}(t),\dots,\textbf{l}_{n}(t)
and
\textbf{r}_{0}(t),\dots,\textbf{r}_{n}(t)
from the original control points
\textbf{p}_{0},\dots,\textbf{p}_{n}
.
These new control points, which represent the original Bézier curve
restricted to the parameter intervals
[0,t]
and
[t,1]
, respectively,
are computed from the formulae

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textbf{l}_{i}(t)={\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i}B_{j}^{i}(t)\textbf{p}_{j}},&i=0,\dots,n,\\
\textbf{r}_{i}(t)={\displaystyle\sum_{j=i}^{n}B_{n-j}^{n-i}(t)\textbf{p}_{i+n-j}},&i=0,\dots,n,\end{array}

(6)

where
B_{j}^{n}(t)
are Bernstein basis polynomials
B^{n}_{j}(t)=\binom{n}{j}t^{j}(1-t)^{n-j}
.

The de Casteljau algorithm (
6
) can be rewritten
into the following matrix form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{l}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{l}_{n}\\
\end{array}\right)&=&\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}B^{0}_{0}(t)&0&\ldots&0\\
B^{1}_{0}(t)&B_{1}^{1}(t)&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
B^{n}_{0}(t)&B^{n}_{1}(t)&\ldots&B^{n}_{n}(t)\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{p}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{p}_{n}\\
\end{array}\right)=\textbf{L}(t)\cdot\textbf{P}\\
&&\\
\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{r}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{r}_{n}\\
\end{array}\right)&=&\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}B^{n}_{0}(t)&B^{n}_{1}(t)&\ldots&B^{n}_{n}(t)\\
0&B_{0}^{n-1}(t)&\ldots&B_{n-1}^{n-1}(t)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&B_{0}^{0}(t)\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{p}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{p}_{n}\\
\end{array}\right)=\textbf{R}(t)\cdot\textbf{P}\end{array}

(7)

The matrices
\textbf{L}(t)
,
\textbf{R}(t)
represent the left and right
subdivision scheme for Bézier curves. Starting with the original control
points and applying these matrices repeatedly generates a sequence of
control polygons that converge to the original Bézier curve.

The matrices
\textbf{L}(t)
and
\textbf{R}(t)
are invertible,
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}=t^{i}
and
\lambda_{i}=(1-t)^{i}
, respectively,
for
i=0,\dots,n
.
The eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{0}=1
is

\mathbf{v}_{0}=(1,1,\dots,1)^{\top}
, since both matrices are row-stochastic.

3.2
IFS for Bézier curves with complex parameter

The de Casteljau algorithm (section
3.1
)
provides a subdivision scheme for Bézier curves with real parameter
t
.
In order to generate fractals, we now extend the algorithm to the case of complex
parameter
t\in\mathbb{C}
, and then transform the matrices

\mathbf{L}(t)
and
\mathbf{R}(t)
in
\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
into an IFS
consisting of two affine maps
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
of
\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)}
.

This extension is achieved via a conjugacy that displays the
n
-dimensional
linear part
\mathbf{A}_{k}
and translation vector
\mathbf{b}_{k}
of

f_{k}
as sub-matrices —cf. equation (
4
).
This may be done in four equivalent ways:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathrm{I}&\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{A}_{k}&\mathbf{b}_{k}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\end{array}\right)&\mathrm{II}&\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{A}_{k}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{b}_{k}&1\end{array}\right)\\
\vskip 10.0pt\cr\mathrm{III}&\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\mathbf{b}_{k}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{A}_{k}\end{array}\right)&\mathrm{IV}&\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{b}_{k}&\mathbf{A}_{k}\end{array}\right)\end{array}\qquad k=0,1.

(8)

These matrices, which we shall call
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
,
k=0,1
,
are distinguished by the location of a row, or column,
of the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}_{n+1}
.
These matrices act on the right (I and IV) or on the left
(II and III), on column or row vectors that feature a 1
—the homogeneous component—
in the last (I and II) or the first entry (III and IV).

The equivalence of these matrices is achieved by a combination of
a reflection
(i,j)\leftrightarrow(n-i,n-j)
and/or a transposition.
The former is performed by the matrix with ones on the
secondary diagonal (which coincides with its own inverse), while
Theorem 1 of
[
25
]
guarantees the existence of
a (symmetric) transposition similarity.

A general procedure for constructing a similarity between
the de Casteljau matrices (
7
), or more general
subdivision matrices, and the matrices

\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
of type II is developed in
[
23
]
:

\mathbf{M}^{(0)}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\;\mathbf{L}(t)\mathbf{P},\qquad\mathbf{M}^{(1)}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\;\mathbf{R}(t)\mathbf{P},

(9)

where
\mathbf{P}
is a square matrix created from any column vector
whose last two entries differ (for instance, the vector
of control points
(\mathbf{p}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)}
)
by adding columns from identity matrix and a last column of ones
corresponding to the homogenous component of the coordinates.
The matrix
\mathbf{P}
, which performs a change of basis in

\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
, is invertible by construction.
We illustrate the effect of different choices of
\mathbf{P}

on the attractor of IFS in Figure
1
.

f_{0}
is the first row of matrix

of (

), i.e.,
(-1+i,1,1)
, while the fixed point of
f_{1}
is
(2+i,0,1)
— the last row of
\mathbf{P}
.

Because the elements in a row of
\mathbf{L}(t)
(and similarly for

\mathbf{R}(t)
) sum up to 1, and the last column of
\mathbf{P}
is a
column of ones, the last column of
\mathbf{L}(t)\mathbf{P}
is also a
column of ones.
Further, the last column of
\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{L}(t)\mathbf{P}
is the
last column of the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}_{n+1}
because the last
column of
\mathbf{P}
is a column of ones and

\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{I}
.
Thus the matrices
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
are both of type II
in (
8
).

The great generality of the above construction is accompanied by a
lack of specific information about the submatrices
\mathbf{A}_{k}

and the translation vector
\mathbf{b}_{k}
in
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
.
Below (theorem
3.2
) we establish when the above affine IFS
has a unique global connected attractor.
For this purpose we must show that all infinite products of
\mathbf{A}_{0}

and
\mathbf{A}_{1}
converge to the zero matrix, or equivalently, that their

joint spectral radius
is less than one.
Recall that the joint spectral radius of a set of matrices

\mathcal{A}
is defined as

\rho(\mathcal{A}):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup\{||\mathbf{A}_{i_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{A}_{i_{n}}||^{\frac{1}{n}}:\mathbf{A}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\},

where
||\cdot||
is any matrix norm.

In the 90’s Daubechies and Lagarias
[
6
]
defined the
generalized spectral radius
, which was proved to be equal to the joint spectral radius for a bounded set of matrices
[
5
]
.
The
generalized spectral radius
is defined as:

\rho(\mathcal{A}):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup\{\rho(\mathbf{A}_{i_{1}}\dots\mathbf{A}_{i_{n}})^{\frac{1}{n}},\mathbf{A}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\}.

We shall employ the following lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\mathbf{A}_{0},\mathbf{A}_{1}
can be simultaneously upper-triangularized,
i.e., if there
exists an invertible matrix
\mathbf{S}
such that
\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{S}

are both upper-triangular, then
\rho(\mathbf{A}_{0},\mathbf{A}_{1})=max\{\rho(\mathbf{A}_{0}),\rho(\mathbf{A}_{1})\}
.

Proof.

The result follows immediately from examination of the diagonal entries of the product
of two upper-triangular matrices. See
[
12
, Lemma 4.7]
.
∎

Remark
.

In
[
4
]
it is shown that a necessary and sufficient
condition for a compact affine IFS in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to have a unique attractor
is that
\rho(\mathcal{A})<1
, where
\mathcal{A}
is the set of matrices
corresponding to the linear part of the set of affine transformations
the IFS consists of.
This statement can be extended verbatim to our case of compact affine
IFS in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

The next result, which is crucial to our analysis, exhibits
a conjugacy that transforms
\mathbf{L}(t)
into a diagonal matrix
and
\mathbf{R}(t)
into an upper-triangular matrix, both of type
III in (
8
); these matrices are determined explicitly.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
n\geqslant 0
, let
\mathbf{S}
be the matrix of eigenvectors
of the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix
\mathbf{L}(t)
.
Then
\mathbf{S}
is invertible, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{L}(t)\mathbf{S})_{i,j}&=&\displaystyle\binom{0}{i-j}t^{i}\\
\vskip 10.0pt\cr\displaystyle(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{R}(t)\mathbf{S})_{i,j}&=&\displaystyle\binom{n-i}{n-j}t^{j-i}(1-t)^{i}\end{array}\hskip 30.0pti,j=0,\ldots,n.

(10)

Proof.

As noted above [see remarks after eq. (
7
)],
the
(n+1)
-dimensional matrix
\mathbf{L}(t)
over

\mathbb{Z}[t]
has the
(n+1)
distinct eigenvalues
t^{i}
,
i=0,\ldots,n
.
Thus
\mathbf{L}(t)
is diagonalizable via the similarity
\mathbf{S}
, and
considering that
\binom{0}{i-j}=\delta_{i,j}
, the Kronecker delta,
the first formula in (
10
) is proved.

Next we show that
\mathbf{S}_{i,j}=\binom{i}{j}
, by computing the
eigenvectors of
\mathbf{L}(t)
.
We shall use the identity
[
14
, p. 174]

\binom{i}{k}\binom{k}{j}=\binom{i}{j}\binom{i-j}{k-j}\qquad i,j,k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(11)

Keeping in mind the expression for
\mathbf{L}(t)
in (
7
),
and that
\mathbf{L}(t)_{i,k}=0
for
k<0
and
k>n
,
we compute the
i
-th component of the
j
-th eigenvector by summing over
all integers
k
. We obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k}B^{i}_{k}\binom{k}{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\binom{i}{k}\binom{k}{j}t^{k}(1-t)^{i-k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{i}{j}\sum_{k}\binom{i-j}{k-j}t^{(k-j)+j}(1-t)^{(i-j)-(k-j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{j}\binom{i}{j}\sum_{m}\binom{n}{m}t^{m}(1-t)^{n-m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{j}\binom{i}{j}(t+1-t)^{n}=t^{j}\binom{i}{j},

where the summation variable
m=k-j
is unrestricted because
k
is.
This computation shows that the
j
th column vector of
\mathbf{S}
has components

\binom{i}{j},i=0,\ldots,n
, as desired.

Next we show that
\mathbf{S}^{-1}_{i,j}=(-1)^{i+j}\binom{i}{j}
, i.e.,

\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{I}
.
Again, Using (
11
) and summing over

\big(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{S}\big)_{i,j}=\sum_{k}(-1)^{i+k}\binom{i}{k}\binom{k}{j}=\binom{i}{j}(-1)^{i}\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\binom{i-j}{k-j}.

If
i<j

\binom{i}{j}=0
, and the rightmost expression vanishes.
The same happens if

the binomial theorem, we have

\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\binom{i-j}{k-j}=(-1)^{j}\sum_{m}\binom{i-j}{m}(-1)^{m}=(-1)^{j}(-1+1)^{i-j}=0.

Finally, for

\big(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{S}\big)_{i,i}=\binom{i}{i}(-1)^{2i}=1
, as desired.

It remains to show that

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(n+1)}_{i+1,j+1}+\hat{T}^{(n+1)}_{i,j+1}

\displaystyle t^{j-i}(1-t)^{i}\left[\binom{n-i}{n-j}(1-t)+\binom{n+1-i}{n-j}t\right]

The Takagi curve

The Takagi curve is a graph of the Takagi (or Blancmange) function.
Some of its remarkable properties are described in the surveys

[
1

18

. The Takagi function is defined by

\mathrm{T}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma(2^{n}x)}{2^{n}}\qquad x\in[0,1]

where
\sigma(x)=\min_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|x-n|
, that is,

\sigma(x)
is the distance from
x
to the nearest integer.

The Takagi curve has infinite length over any nonempty open interval in

[0,1]

[
18
, Theorem 11.4]
, and it is self-similar in the
sense that it satisfies a dyadic self-similarity equation
[
18
, Theorem 4.1]
.
However, if we adopt Mandelbrot’s definition of a fractal as a set whose
Hausdorff dimension is strictly greater than its topological dimension

[
19
, p. 15]
, then the Takagi curve is not a fractal
since its topological dimension and its Haussdorff dimension
(as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) are both equal to 1
[
18
, Theorem 11.2]
.

have
f_{d_{n+1}}(0)=0
, that is, the first iterate of
the map doesn’t change the initial point. Hence,
from (
30
) we have
Z_{n+1}^{(d)}=Z_{n}^{(d)}
, so that

a_{k,n+1}^{(d)}=a_{k,n}^{(d)}\quad k=0,1,\ldots\hskip 30.0pt(d_{n+1}=0),

(34)

in accordance with (
31
) and (
32
).

If
d_{n+1}=1
, then we have
f_{d_{n+1}}(0)=t

and from (
29
) we have

Z_{n+1}^{(d)}=f_{n+1}^{(d)}(0)=f_{n}^{(d)}(t)=U_{n}^{(d)}t+Z_{n}^{(d)}.

(35)

Letting
W_{n}^{(d)}=U_{n}^{(d)}t
with
t=1/2+\mathrm{i}\beta
and
using the binomial theorem, we find

\displaystyle W_{n}^{(d)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1/2-\mathrm{i}\beta)^{u_{n}}(1/2+\mathrm{i}\beta)^{n-u_{n}+1}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k}\binom{u_{n}}{k}(-1)^{k}(\mathrm{i}\beta)^{k}\frac{1}{2^{u_{n}-k}}\right)\times\left(\sum_{\ell}\binom{n-u_{n}+1}{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\beta)^{\ell}\frac{1}{2^{n-u_{n}+1-\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\binom{u_{n}}{k}\sum_{\ell}\binom{n-u_{n}+1}{\ell}\frac{1}{2^{n+1-k-\ell}}(\mathrm{i}\beta)^{k+\ell}.

Letting
k+\ell=m
in (
36
) we obtain

w_{m,n}=\frac{\mathrm{i}^{m}}{2^{n+1-m}}\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\binom{u_{n}}{k}\binom{n+1-u_{n}}{m-k}.

For
k<0
(
k>m
) the first (second) binomial coefficient is zero,
so we may restrict the range of summation to
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
,
which completes the proof of the recursion formulae.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(a_{k,n}^{(d)})
be the coefficients of
Z_{n}^{(d)}
, as in
equation (
31
). Then for all
d
and
k
the following limit exists

a_{k}^{(d)}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{k,n}^{(d)},\qquad\mbox{with the bound }\qquad|a_{k}^{(d)}|<2^{k+1}

(37)

independent on
d
. Thus the polynomials
Z_{n}^{(d)}
converge coefficientwise
to a convergent power series:

Z^{(d)}(\beta):=\lim_{n\to\infty}Z_{n}^{(d)}(\beta),\qquad|\beta|<\frac{1}{2}.

(38)

Proof.

Using the identities
[
14
, pp 174,199]

\sum_{k}\binom{r}{k}\binom{s}{m-k}=\binom{r+s}{m},\hskip 30.0pt\frac{z^{m}}{(1-z)^{m+1}}=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\binom{k}{m}z^{k}\quad(m\geqslant 0),

and formula (
33
) we obtain the following uniform bound
(to lighten up the notation, we write
a_{k,n}
for
a_{k,n}^{(d)}
):

\displaystyle|a_{m,n+1}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|a_{m,n+1}-a_{m,0}|=\left|\sum_{j=0}^{n}(a_{m,j+1}-a_{m,j})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}\bigl|a_{m,j+1}-a_{m,j}\bigr|=\sum_{j=0}^{n}|w_{m,j}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{2^{m}}{2^{j+1}}\sum_{k}\binom{u_{j}}{k}\binom{j+1-u_{j}}{m-k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}\binom{j+1}{m}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}\binom{j+1}{m}=2^{m+1}.

This derivation shows that, for all
d\in\Omega
, the series

\sum_{j}w_{m,j}
converges absolutely, which implies
that, as
n\to\infty
, the sequence of polynomials
Z_{n}^{(d)}

converges coefficientwise to a power series; the latter in turn
converges for
|\beta|<1/2
for all
d
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
Z^{(d)}(\beta)
be as in (
38
). Then, for every
\alpha\in[0,1]
:

\mathbf{v}(\alpha):=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}Z^{(\pi^{-1}(\alpha))}(\beta)}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\right|_{0}=2\mathrm{i}\mathrm{T}(\alpha),

(40)

where
\pi
is defined in (
23
) and
\mathrm{T}
is the Takagi function.

Proof.

For any
n\geqslant 1
, from equation (
31
) we have

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{n}^{(d)}(\beta)}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\right|_{0}=\mathrm{i}a_{1,n}^{(d)},

so we have to compute the limits
a_{0}^{(d)}
and
a_{1}^{(d)}
as in
(
37
).
We determine the first coefficient
a_{0,n}
of the
polynomial
Z_{n}^{(d)}(\beta)
.

If
\beta=0
, the IFS (
20
) is represented as the pair
of planar maps

F_{0}(x,y)=\bigl(\frac{1}{2}x\,,\,\frac{1}{2}y\bigr)\hskip 30.0ptF_{1}(x,y)=\bigl(\frac{1}{2}x+\frac{1}{2}\,,\,\frac{1}{2}y\bigr).

(41)

The attractor
\mathcal{A}(0)
is the unit interval, over which the
map is seen to be the inverse of the doubling map
x\mapsto 2x\,({\rm mod\ }1)
.
Let
d(n)=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n})
be the sequence of
the first
n
digits of
d
, and
consider expression (
29
) for the finite
sequence
\overleftarrow{d}\hskip-1.0pt(n)
. Since
\beta=0
, the final point
will be
Z_{n}^{(d)}(0)=a_{0,n}
, where
a_{0,n}

is a rational number.

Consider now the orbit of the point
z=0
under the sequence of maps

f^{(k)}
determined according to the expression (
28
).
This sequence is
\overleftarrow{d}\hskip-1.0pt(n)
, and the final point is

Z_{n}^{(d)}(0)=a_{n,0}^{(d)}
, from (
29
).
This point is the initial point of an orbit of the inverse map
—the doubling map– which corresponds to the reverse sequence
d(n)
.
It is well-known that the sequence
d(n)
is the sequence of binary
digits of the initial point, and therefore we have

a_{0,n}=r_{n}^{(d)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{d_{k}}{2^{k}}\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}[1/2].

(42)

In other words, the dyadic rational
r_{n}^{(d)}
is the initial point of an
orbit of the doubling map, which terminates at the fixed point
0

in
n
steps.
Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, from (
26
) we find
that
a_{0}^{(d)}=x^{(d)}
.

Next we determine
a_{1,n}
, using induction on
n
. For
n=1
we have

f^{(0)}(0)=0
and
f^{(1)}(0)=1/2+\mathrm{i}\beta=a_{0,1}+\mathrm{i}\beta a_{1,1}
,
so in either case we have
a_{1,1}=2\mathrm{T}(a_{0,1})
. Assume now that this
is true for all binary sequences of length
n
:
a_{1,n}=2\mathrm{T}(a_{0,n})
.

We consider the successive digit
d_{n+1}
. If
d_{n+1}=0
we have

a_{1,n+1}=a_{1,n}
from (
34
), and there is nothing to prove.
If
d_{n+1}=1
, then from (
32
) and (
33
) with

m=1
we find

a_{1,n+1}=-\mathrm{i}w_{1,n}+a_{1,n}.

(43)

We compute

-\mathrm{i}w_{1,n}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}[(n+1-u_{n})-u_{n}]=\frac{n+1-2u_{n}}{2^{n}}.

From the above, equation (
43
), the induction hypothesis,
and lemma
4.3
with

, we have

a_{1,n+1}=a_{1,n}+\frac{n+1-2u_{n}}{2^{n}}=2\mathrm{T}(a_{0,n})+\frac{n+1-2u_{n}}{2^{n}}=2\mathrm{T}\bigl(a_{0,n}+\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\bigr),

which completes the induction.
Taking the limit, we have, for any

\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{1,n+1}^{(d)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}2\mathrm{T}\bigl(a_{0,n}^{(d)}+\frac{d_{n+1}}{2^{n+1}}\bigr)=2\mathrm{T}(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{0,n+1}^{(d)})=2\mathrm{T}(x^{(d)}),

the penultimate step being justified by the continuity of
\mathrm{T}
.
The proof is complete.

The quantity

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}(\beta)\in\mathbb{C}
be the attractor of the
de Casteljau IFS (
20
) for the Bézier curve with control
points
0
and
1
and complex subdivision parameter
t=\frac{1}{2}+i\beta
,
and let
\mathrm{g}
be the scaling map (
21
).
Then

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\mathrm{d}_{H}(\mathrm{g}\big(\mathcal{A}(\beta),(2\beta)^{-1}\big),\mathcal{T})=0,

(45)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the graph of the Takagi function and
\mathrm{d}_{H}
is the
Hausdorff distance (
2
).

Proof.

We shall provide two proofs.

From lemma
4.2
, we can represent the attractor of
the IFS as

\mathcal{A}(\beta)=\bigcup_{d\in\Omega}Z^{(d)}(\beta)=\bigcup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}Z^{(\pi^{-1}(\alpha))}(\beta)\qquad|\beta|<1/2.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{*}(\beta)=\mathrm{g}(\mathcal{A}(\beta),(2\beta)^{-1})
, and
write
Z(\alpha,\beta)
for
Z^{(\pi^{-1}(\alpha)}(\beta)
.
We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{*}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\alpha}\mathrm{g}(Z(\alpha,\beta),(2\beta)^{-1})=\bigcup_{\alpha}\alpha+\mathrm{g}(Z(\alpha,\beta)-\alpha,(2\beta)^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\alpha}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}(\alpha)+O_{\alpha}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}+\bigcup_{\alpha}O_{\alpha}(\beta),

where
|O_{\alpha}(\beta)|\leqslant C\beta
, for some constant

C=C(\alpha)=C_{x}(\alpha)+\mathrm{i}C_{y}(\alpha)
. (Here ‘
+
’ represents
the Minkowski sum of sets.)
We have

\mathrm{d}_{H}(\mathcal{A}^{*}(\beta),\mathcal{T})\leqslant\sup_{\alpha}C(\alpha)\beta.

Using
31
and (
37
) we estimate:

\displaystyle|C(\alpha)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle|C_{x}(\alpha)|+|C_{y}(\alpha)|\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\bigl(|a_{2k}|+\frac{1}{2}|a_{2k+1}|\bigr)\beta^{2k-2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geqslant 1}(2^{2k+1}+2^{2k+1})\beta^{2k-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\sum_{k\geqslant 0}(4\beta^{2})^{k}=\frac{4}{1-4\beta^{2}},\qquad|\beta|<\frac{1}{2}.

Thus, for
|\beta|<1/2
we have

\mathrm{d}_{H}(\mathcal{A}^{*}(\beta),\mathcal{T})\leqslant\frac{4\beta}{1-4\beta^{2}}\to 0,

as claimed.

For an alternative proof, we let
\beta\in(0,\sqrt{3}/2)
, in which range
the IFS
\{f^{(0)},f^{(1)}\}
is hyperbolic
[see comment after eq. (
20
)].
For any
d\in\Omega
, we define the
following sequence of complex numbers:

\overleftarrow{Z}\hskip-1.0pt_{0}^{(d)}(\beta)=0\qquad\overleftarrow{Z}\hskip-1.0pt_{n}^{(d)}(\beta)=f^{(d_{n})}\circ f^{(d_{n-1})}\circ\cdots\circ f^{(d_{1})}(0),\quad n\geqslant 1.

(46)

[Note that in general
\overleftarrow{Z}\hskip-1.0pt_{n}^{(d)}\not=Z_{n}^{(\overleftarrow{d}\hskip-1.0pt)}
, cf. (
24
).]
Since
0
belongs to
\mathcal{A}(\beta)
for any
\beta
, so do all the

\overleftarrow{Z}\hskip-1.0pt_{n}^{(d)}
and
Z_{n}^{(d)}
. It follows that the two sets

\Gamma_{n}(\beta)=\bigcup_{d\in\Omega}Z_{n}^{(d)}(\beta)\hskip 30.0pt\overleftarrow{\Gamma}\hskip-1.0pt_{n}^{(d)}(\beta)=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}\overleftarrow{Z}\hskip-1.0pt_{k}^{(d)}(\beta)

are finite subsets of
\mathcal{A}(\beta)
.
Since all binary sequences of length
n
are represented in
\Gamma_{n}
,
for any
d
,
n
, and
\beta
in the range specified above we obtain the
chain of inclusions

\overleftarrow{\Gamma}\hskip-1.0pt_{n}^{(d)}(\beta)\subset\Gamma_{n}(\beta)\subset\mathcal{A}(\beta).

(47)

From the hyperbolicity of the IFS and

[
2
, theorem 2, p. 365]
it then follows
that for almost all
d\in\Omega
[with respect to the Lebesgue
measure, see comment following eq. (
23
)]) we have

\overline{\lim_{n\to\infty}\overleftarrow{\Gamma}\hskip-1.0pt_{n}^{(d)}(\beta)}=\mathcal{A}(\beta),

where the overbar denotes the closure.
From (
47
) we then have

\overline{\lim_{n\to\infty}\Gamma_{n}(\beta)}=\mathcal{A}(\beta)\qquad\beta\in(0,\sqrt{3}/2).

(48)

From lemma
4.4
, we have

\mathrm{g}(Z_{n}^{(d)}(\beta),(2\beta)^{-1})=r_{n}^{(d)}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{T}(r_{n}^{(d)})+O(\beta^{2}),\qquad\beta\to 0,

(49)

where the constant in

depends on

We obtain

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{g}\big(\Gamma_{n}(\beta),(2\beta)^{-1}\big),\mathcal{T})=0,

where the (asymmetric) distance
\mathrm{d}
is defined in (
1

gives a set of points of
\mathcal{A}^{*}

which are dense in

. Since
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is closed, their closure

is contained in
\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

Remark
.

Theorem
4.1
does not imply that
\mathcal{A}^{*}

(which is connected from theorem
3.2
)
is topologically equivalent to
\mathcal{T}
for all sufficiently small
\beta
.
Indeed while there is a unique parametric curve
Z^{(d)}(\beta)
through each point
of
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}(0)
, the union of all curves does not constitute a fiber bundle
over
\mathcal{B}
because the intersections of curves visible in figure

6
can be shown to occur arbitrarily close to
\mathcal{B}
,
for instance, near
x=1/2
.

In closing, we show (informally) that the Takagi function also appears
in the general case of a Bézier curve of degree
m\geqslant 2
,
as one component of the vector field.
We use the lower-triangular representation (
18
)
of the de Casteljau matrices.
Let
\{f^{(0)},f^{(1)}\}
be the corresponding
affine IFS on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, and let
\mathcal{\bar{A}}(\beta)

be the attractor for
t=1/2+\mathrm{i}\beta
.
For any sequence
d\in\Omega
we form the matrix product

\mathbf{M}_{n}(d)
as in (
27
).
Then the vector

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
Z_{n,1}^{(d)}\\
\vdots\\
Z_{n,m}^{(d)}\end{array}\right)=\mathbf{M}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{array}\right),

(50)

whose entries are polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[1/2,\mathrm{i}\beta]
, belongs to

\mathcal{\bar{A}}(\beta)
for any
\beta
, since so does the
vector
(1,0^{m})^{\top}
, being the fixed point
of
f_{0}
in homogeneous coordinates.

Consider now the first component of this vector.
From the lower triangular form of
\mathbf{M}^{(0)}
and
\mathbf{M}^{(1)}

it is straighforward to show that its evolution is determined by the
pair of maps

f^{(0,m)}(x)=tx\qquad f^{(1,m)}(x)=(1-t)x+mt\hskip 30.0ptt=\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}\beta.

(51)

This system is conjugate to the system (
20
) by the bijection

x\mapsto mx
.
Hence for
\beta=0
the smooth Bézier curve
\mathcal{B}_{1}(0)
is the interval
with end-points 0 and
m
, and the map
\pi_{m}
which sends the
symbolic binary address
d
to the corresponding point on

\mathcal{B}_{1}
(namely the constant coefficient of
Z_{n,1}^{(d)}
), is given
by
\pi_{m}=m\pi
, where
\pi
is defined in (
23
).
In the limit as
n\to\infty
the first (affine) component of the vector
(
50
) is equal to
mZ^{(d)}
, and hence the
corresponding component of the vector field is given by

x=\pi_{m}(d)=m\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{d_{k}}{2^{k}},\hskip 30.0pt\mathbf{v}_{m}(x)=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}Z^{(\pi_{m}^{-1}(x))}(\beta)}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\right|_{0}=2m\mathrm{i}\mathrm{T}(x/m),

(52)

where
\mathrm{T}

Defining the scaling function as

\mathrm{g}(z,\alpha,\beta)=\alpha\operatorname{Re}(z)+\beta\,\mathrm{i}\,\operatorname{Im}(z)

the limit (
45

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\mathrm{d}_{H}(\mathrm{g}\big(\mathcal{A}(\beta),1/m,(2m\beta)^{-1}\big),\mathcal{T})=0,
