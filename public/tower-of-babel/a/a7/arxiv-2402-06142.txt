Developing a Theoretical Model for the Resummation of Infrared Effects in the Post-Reconstruction Power Spectrum
Naonori Sugiyama

Abstract
Since galaxy distribution reconstruction effectively reduces non-Gaussian terms in the power spectrum covariance matrix, it has attracted interest not only for Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) signals but also for various cosmological signal analyses. To this end, this paper presents a novel theoretical model that addresses infrared (IR) effects in the post-reconstruction galaxy power spectrum, including 1-loop corrections. In particular, we discuss the importance of incorporating non-perturbative effects arising from IR contributions into the displacement vector 𝒔 \bm{s} used for reconstruction. Consequently, post-reconstruction nonlinear damping of BAO can be described by a single two-dimensional Gaussian function. This is a phenomenon not observed when 𝒔 \bm{s} is considered to at a linear order in the Zel’dovich approximation. Furthermore, we confirm that the cross-power spectrum of the pre- and post-reconstruction density fluctuations lacks IR effect cancellations, and shows an exponential decay in both the cross-power spectrum and the associated shot-noise term.



I
Introduction

The reconstruction of galaxy distributions
Eisenstein et al. 2007a
has traditionally been a focus of research to amplify baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) signals
Sunyaev and Zeldovich 1970
;
Peebles and Yu 1970
. However, recent findings indicate that these reconstruction techniques can effectively reduce non-Gaussian terms in the covariance matrix of cosmological
n
-point statistics. As a result, their application has expanded beyond BAO signals to encompass a variety of cosmological signals. In particular, there is a growing interest in improving constraints on cosmological parameters through post-reconstruction two-point statistics
Hikage et al. 2020a
;
Wang et al. 2022
. In addition, applications in three-point statistics have been reported to strengthen constraints on primordial non-Gaussianity
Shirasaki et al. 2021
.

Despite the potential of reconstruction techniques, their practical applications, particularly beyond BAO signal analysis, are still emerging. This slow advancement is due to a limited understanding of the nonlinear characteristics of reconstructed density fluctuations. The examination of pre-reconstruction two-point correlation functions and power spectra primarily employs advanced theoretical models, which extend beyond Standard Perturbation Theory (SPT) (e.g.,
Bernardeau et al. 2002
), to precisely extract small-scale information. For instance, sophisticated theoretical models such as the Convolution Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (CLPT)
Carlson et al. 2013
;
Wang et al. 2014
, the Renormalized Perturbation Theory (RPT)
Crocce and Scoccimarro 2006
, the TNS model
Taruya et al. 2010
, and the Effective Field Theory of Large-scale Structure (EFT of LSS)
Baumann et al. 2012
;
Carrasco et al. 2012
have been applied to analyze actual galaxy survey datasets, such as the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey Data Release 12 (BOSS DR12) galaxy data
(
Eisenstein et al. 2011
;
Bolton et al. 2012
;
Dawson et al. 2013
;
Alam et al. 2015
)
. On the other hand, theoretical research into post-reconstruction density fluctuations has been limited to 1-loop calculations in SPT
Schmittfull et al. 2015a
;
Hikage et al. 2017
;
Hikage et al. 2020b
and methods based on the Zel’dovich approximation
Padmanabhan et al. 2009
;
Seo et al. 2016
;
White 2015
;
Chen et al. 2019
. See also
Ota et al. 2021
;
Ota et al. 2023
for perturbation theory approaches to iterative reconstruction methods
Zhu et al. 2017
;
Zhu et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2017
;
Schmittfull et al. 2017
;
Hada and Eisenstein 2018
;
Hada and Eisenstein 2019
;
Seo et al. 2022
.

In this study, we primarily investigate the infrared (IR) effect on the power spectrum of galaxies. This effect possesses a unique property that allows for non-perturbative analysis, making it crucial for advancing theories beyond SPT. For the pre-reconstruction scenario, several IR effects are found, and we summarize them below. When taking the IR limit and considering only the nonlinearities arising from IR effects, all IR nonlinear effects are negated in the calculations of the power spectrum, leaving only the linear power spectrum
(
Jain and Bertschinger 1996
;
Scoccimarro and Frieman 1996
;
Kehagias and Riotto 2013
;
Peloso and Pietroni 2013
;
Sugiyama and Futamase 2013
;
Sugiyama and Spergel 2014
;
Blas et al. 2013
;
Blas et al. 2016a
;
Lewandowski and Senatore 2017
)
. This IR cancellation holds true when evaluating all density fluctuations at equal times. However, assessing the correlation of fluctuations at distinct times yields the consistency relation for the LSS, which uniquely connects higher-order statistics to their lower-order counterparts
(
Peloso and Pietroni 2013
;
Kehagias and Riotto 2013
;
Creminelli et al. 2013
)
. Moreover, proper consideration of the IR effect can shed light on the nonlinear decay of the BAO signal
(
Zeldovich 1970
;
Eisenstein et al. 2007b
;
Crocce and Scoccimarro 2008
;
Matsubara 2008a
;
Sugiyama and Spergel 2014
;
Senatore and Zaldarriaga 2015
;
Baldauf et al. 2015
;
Blas et al. 2016b
;
Senatore and Trevisan 2018
;
Ivanov and Sibiryakov 2018
;
Lewandowski and Senatore 2020
;
Sugiyama et al. 2021
)
.

This paper aims to comprehensively elucidate the impact of non-perturbative IR effects on the BAO signal and the overall power spectrum shape after reconstruction. In this context, we present a new IR-resummed model of the post-reconstruction power spectrum, including 1-loop corrections. Concurrently, we explore its relation to established theories that address partial IR effects after reconstruction, such as the 1-loop correction in SPT and the Zel’dovich approximation. We further demonstrate the fundamental significance of the IR effect in the cross-power spectrum between the pre- and post-reconstruction density fluctuations. The proposed model should be directly applicable to observational galaxy data as it incorporates Redshift Space Distortions (RSDs) (e.g.,
Kaiser 1987
) and galaxy bias effects (e.g.,
Desjacques et al. 2018
).

The outline of this paper is as follows. Section

reviews the non-perturbative treatment of infrared (IR) effects on pre-reconstruction density fluctuations. Section

IV
calculates the cross-power spectrum of density fluctuations before and after reconstruction, highlighting the importance of nonlinear IR effects. Section

A

II.1
Infrared (IR) effects on dark matter density fluctuations

Consider a density field function
\rho(\bm{x})
defined over three-dimensional spatial coordinates
\bm{x}
. Let
\delta(\bm{x})
represent the deviation from the mean density field
\bar{\rho}
. This relation can be expressed as
\rho(\bm{x})=\bar{\rho}\left(1+\delta(\bm{x})\right)
. The Fourier transform of
\delta(\bm{x})
can be represented as
\widetilde{\delta}(\bm{k})=\int d^{3}xe^{-i\bm{x}\cdot\bm{k}}\delta(\bm{x})
, where the tilde denotes a Fourier-transformed quantity.

Let us begin by considering the density fluctuations of dark matter, denoted
\delta_{\rm m}(\bm{x})
, wherein the subscript “m” represents “matter”. In perturbation theory, an
n
th-order dark matter density fluctuation in Fourier space, denoted
\widetilde{\delta}^{\,[n]}_{{\rm m}}(\bm{k})
, can be represented by the convolution integral of
n
wave vectors (
\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n}
) with an associated
n
th-order kernel function
F_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n})
. Hence, the
n
th-order density fluctuation is expressed as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}^{\,[n]}_{{\rm m}}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}p_{1}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\cdots\frac{d^{3}p_{n}}{(2\pi)^{3}}(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{[1,n]})

(1)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle F_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n})\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p}_{1})\cdots\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p}_{n})\;,

where
\bm{p}_{[1,n]}=\bm{p}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{p}_{n}
,
\widetilde{\delta}^{\,[1]}_{\rm m}
is the linear (first-order) dark matter density fluctuation in Fourier space, and
\delta_{\rm D}
is the three-dimensional delta function.

II.1.1
Second-order fluctuations

For example, the second-order kernel function is given by

\displaystyle F_{2}(\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})=\frac{17}{21}+\frac{1}{2}\mu_{12}\left(\frac{p_{1}}{p_{2}}+\frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}}\right)+\frac{2}{7}\left(\mu_{12}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\;,

(2)

where
p_{n}=|\bm{p}_{n}|
and
\mu_{12}=\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2}
with
\hat{p}_{n}=\bm{p}_{n}/p_{n}
. Substituting Eq. (
2
) into Eq. (
1
) and performing the inverse Fourier transform, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm m}^{[2]}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{17}{21}\big[\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\big]^{2}-\bm{\Psi}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\cdot\nabla\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x})

(3)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{2}{7}\left[\left(\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}{\partial^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\right)\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\right]^{2}\;.

On the right-hand side of Eq. (
3
), the three terms from left to right are, respectively, named the “nonlinear growth term,” “shift term,” and “tidal term”
Schmittfull et al. 2015b
;
Schmittfull et al. 2015a
. The linear displacement vector, associated with the shift term, is defined by the linear dark matter density fluctuation as

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}^{[1]}(\bm{x})=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\frac{i\bm{k}}{k^{2}}\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{k})\;.

(4)

In the IR limit, where one of the wave vectors in
F_{2}(\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})
is much smaller than the other (specifically, when
p_{2}\to 0
), the following relation holds:

\displaystyle F_{2}(\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})\xrightarrow[p_{2}\to 0]{}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\bm{p}_{1}\cdot\bm{p}_{2}}{p_{2}^{2}}\right)\;.

(5)

When substituting Eq. (
5
) into Eq. (
1
), the following approximation is considered simultaneously:

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{1}-\bm{p}_{2})\xrightarrow[p_{2}\to 0]{}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{1})\;.

(6)

Consequently, in the IR limit, we derive

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm m}^{[2]}(\bm{x})\xrightarrow[\rm IR\,limit]{}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}\cdot\nabla\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\;,

(7)

where

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}=\bm{\Psi}^{[1]}(\bm{x}=\bm{0})\;.

(8)

In deriving Eq. (
7
), we also accounted for the condition
p_{1}\to 0
and introduced a prefactor of
2
. Comparison with Eq. (
3
) reveals that the IR limit solution involves isolating only the shift term, and furthermore, the displacement vector contained in the shift term is evaluated at the origin
\bm{x}=\bm{0}
.

Decomposing the second-order fluctuation solution into terms manifesting in the IR limit and the others, the solution can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm m}^{[2]}=\delta^{[2]}_{\rm(S)m}(\bm{x})-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}\cdot\nabla\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\;,

(9)

where the subscript
(\rm S)
denotes the contribution from short-wavelength modes, and
\delta_{\rm(S)m}^{[2]}(\bm{x})
is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm(S)m}^{[2]}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{17}{21}\left[\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\right]^{2}-\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(\rm S)}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\cdot\nabla\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x})

(10)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{2}{7}\left[\left(\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}{\partial^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\right)\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\right]^{2}\;.

In the equation above, we defined the displacement vector contributing from short-wavelength modes as

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}^{[1]}(\bm{x})=\bm{\Psi}^{[1]}(\bm{x})-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}\;,

(11)

where
\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}^{[1]}(\bm{x}=\bm{0})=\bm{0}
.

II.1.2
Non-perturbative treatment

In the IR limit of the
n
th-order kernel function
F_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n})
, one of the
n
wave numbers contributing to the scale of interest
k=|\bm{p}_{[1,n]}|
possesses a magnitude substantially smaller than the other
(n-1)
wave numbers. For a single dark matter fluid in General Relativity (GR), the recursion relation allows the determination of solutions for
F_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n})
up to an infinite order (e.g., see
Bernardeau et al. 2002
). Using mathematical induction on this recursion relation, the relation below can be proven
Sugiyama and Futamase 2013
:

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274ptF_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n-1},\bm{p})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[p\to 0]{}\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\bm{p}_{[1,n-1]}\cdot\bm{p}}{p^{2}}\right)

(12)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\times\hskip 5.69046ptF_{n-1}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n-1})\;.

More generally, for
r
wave numbers approaching zero,

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092ptF_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n-r},\bm{p}_{n-r+1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n})\xrightarrow[p_{n-r+1},\dots,p_{n}\to 0]{}

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt\frac{(n-r)!}{n!}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{r}\left(\frac{\bm{p}_{[1,n-r]}\cdot\bm{p}_{n-r+\alpha}}{p_{n-r+\alpha}^{2}}\right)F_{n-r}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n-r})\;.

(13)

In this context, we decompose the linear dark matter density fluctuation into long-wavelength modes, with wave numbers approaching zero, and short-wavelength modes which are independent of the former:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p})=\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p}\to 0)+\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm(S)m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p})\;.

(14)

Substituting Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
) into Eq. (
1
) and using the approximation

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{[1,n]})\xrightarrow[p_{n-r+1},\dots,p_{n}\to 0]{}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{[1,n-r]})\;,

(15)

the dark matter density fluctuation can be formally expressed as
Sugiyama and Spergel 2014

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm m}(\bm{x})=\delta_{\rm(S)m}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]})\;,

(16)

where the
n
th order of
\delta_{\rm(S)m}(\bm{x})
is given by

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\delta^{[n]}_{{\rm(S)m}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}p_{1}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\cdots\frac{d^{3}p_{n}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,e^{i(\bm{p}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{p}_{n})\cdot\bm{x}}

(17)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle F_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n})\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm(S)m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p}_{1})\cdots\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm(S)m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p}_{n})\;.

Thus, the IR limit effect in a single dark matter fluid can be interpreted as a result of a coordinate transformation due to the displacement vector at the origin.

The IR limit is an operation that extracts nonlinear contributions from large scales (small wave numbers). In other words, the wave number
k
of interest is considerably larger than the wave numbers contributed in the IR limit. Therefore, historically, the IR limit solution has also been referred to as the high-
k
(
k\to\infty
) limit solution (e.g., see
Bernardeau et al. 2008
). Conversely, the ultraviolet (UV) limit corresponds to the low-
k
(
k\to 0
) limit and is treated by, e.g., EFT of LSS
Baumann et al. 2012
;
Carrasco et al. 2012
.

In summary, this paper makes three approximations related to the IR limit:

1.

As illustrated in Eqs. (
5
) and (
13
), for the kernel function
F_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n})
that characterizes nonlinear effects, we employ the approximate kernel function when one or multiple wave numbers approach zero. This means that the dominant effect in the IR limit appears as the coordinate transformation of the short-wavelength density fluctuation
\delta_{\rm(S)}
due to
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
as in Eq. (
16
).

2.

As presented in Eqs. (
6
) and (
15
), within the delta function
\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{[1,n]})
that characterizes the mode-coupling integral with multiple wave vectors, we assume no mode coupling occurs between the IR effect and other components when one or more wave numbers approach zero. This implies that the displacement vector is evaluated at the origin in Eq. (
8
), and furthermore, indicates no correlation between
\delta_{\rm(S)}
and
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
, as will be discussed in Section
II.3
.

3.

The nonlinear effects of
\delta_{\rm(S)m}
are truncated at a finite perturbation order. Any higher-order perturbative effects beyond this are treated as IR effects through
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
.

The clearest example in the IR limit occurs when
\delta_{\rm(S)m}(\bm{x})\sim\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x})
, approximating the nonlinear dark matter density fluctuation as

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm m}(\bm{x})\xrightarrow[\rm IR\,limit]{}\delta_{\rm m}^{[1]}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}).

(18)

The conclusions of this paper will be mainly based on this simplest case in Sections
III
and
IV
. When higher-order correction terms up to the 1-loop order are to be considered, they will be explained on there as appropriate.

II.2
Extensions to RSD and bias effects

In cases affected by RSD and bias effects, such as observed galaxies, the use of Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (LPT) is advantageous. This is because LPT naturally accommodates displacement vectors. Within LPT, RSD effects are integrated into the displacement vector. Bias effects, on the other hand, can be treated using the equation
(
Matsubara 2008b
)

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm g}(\bm{x},\hat{n})

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q\left[1+\delta_{\rm bias}(\bm{q})\right]\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\bm{\Psi}_{\rm red}(\bm{q},\hat{n})\right),

where
\delta_{\rm g}
represents the galaxy density fluctuation,
\delta_{\rm bias}
is the density fluctuation including the bias effect in Lagrangian space,
\bm{\Psi}_{\rm red}
is the displacement vector including the RSD effect, and
\hat{n}
is a unit vector directed along the line of sight (LOS). The relation between
\bm{\Psi}
and
\bm{\Psi}_{\rm red}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}_{\rm red}=\bm{\Psi}+\frac{\dot{\bm{\Psi}}\cdot\hat{n}}{aH}\hat{n},

(20)

where
\dot{\bm{\Psi}}(\bm{q})
is the time derivative of
\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})
, and
a
and
H
are the scale factor and the Hubble parameter, respectively.

The
n
th order of
\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})
in Fourier space is represented as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\Psi}}^{\,[n]}_{{\rm m}}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\,\int\frac{d^{3}p_{1}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\cdots\frac{d^{3}p_{n}}{(2\pi)^{3}}(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{[1,n]})

(21)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n})\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p}_{1})\cdots\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p}_{n}),

where the nonlinear kernel vector functions up to the second order are given by

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{1}(\bm{p}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{p}_{1}}{p_{1}^{2}},

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{2}(\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{14}\frac{\bm{p}_{[1,2]}}{|\bm{p}_{[1,2]}|^{2}}\left(1-\mu_{12}^{2}\right)^{2}.

(22)

Note that the second-order kernel vector function
\bm{L}_{2}(\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})
does not include a shift term but consists of a combination of a growth term and a tidal term, unlike
F_{2}(\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})
.

The displacement vector is decomposed into two parts: the value at the origin
\bm{q}=\bm{0}
and the other values. Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}_{\rm red}(\bm{q},\hat{n})=\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}(\hat{n})+\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)red}(\bm{q},\hat{n}).

(23)

Here,
\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)red}=\bm{\Psi}_{\rm red}(\bm{q})-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}
is termed as the short-wavelength mode of
\bm{\Psi}_{\rm red}
. Consequently,
\delta_{\rm g}
can be formally rewritten as

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm g}(\bm{x},\hat{n})=\delta_{\rm(S)g}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}(\hat{n}),\hat{n}),

(24)

where

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm(S)g}(\bm{x},\hat{n})

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\hskip-2.84544ptd^{3}q\left[1+\delta_{\rm bias}(\bm{q})\right]\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)red}(\bm{q},\hat{n})\right),

In the above derivation, we used
1=\int d^{3}q\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{x}-\bm{q})=\int d^{3}q\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}(\hat{n}))
. In Fourier space, it becomes

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm g}(\bm{k},\hat{n})=e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}(\hat{n})}\,\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm(S)g}(\bm{k},\hat{n}).

(26)

Note that Eq. (
24
) is simply a rewrite of Eq. (
19
) and, unlike Eq. (
16
), is not the result of solving the equation to perturbative infinite order in the IR limit. To consider Eq. (
24
) as an extension of the solution of Eq. (
16
) that takes into account the bias effect, the RSD effect, and even nonlinear effects on the displacement vector, one must make an assumption. That is,
in the IR limit, all contributions from IR modes appear as the coordinate transformation through
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}
. This is expected to be true for a single dark matter fluid in GR. However, in Appendix
A
, we introduce Degenerate Higher-Order Scalar-Tensor (DHOST) theories (for reviews, see e.g.
Langlois 2019
;
Kobayashi 2019
), a type of modified gravity theory, as an example of how a simple rewrite of Eq. (
19
) into Eq. (
24
) cannot extract only the IR effect through
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}
. Also, while this assumption is considered valid for the standard bias model in GR (e.g., see
Desjacques et al. 2018
), there is a possibility that it may be violated by other bias effects in the context of modified gravity theories
Sugiyama et al. 2023
;
Sugiyama et al. 2023
and multicomponent fluids
Yoo et al. 2011
.

II.2.1
Perturbative expansion

In Fourier space, the
n
th-order term of the galaxy density fluctuation can be expressed as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm g}^{\,[n]}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}p_{1}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\cdots\frac{d^{3}p_{n}}{(2\pi)^{3}}(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{p}_{[1,n]})

(27)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle Z_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n},\hat{n})\,\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p}_{1})\cdots\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{p}_{n}).

Here,
Z_{n}(\bm{p}_{1},\dots,\bm{p}_{n})
represents nonlinear kernel functions that incorporate both the bias effect and the RSD effect.

Approximating both
\delta^{(\rm S)}(\bm{x})
and
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}
as linear, we then obtain
(
Sugiyama et al. 2021
)

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm g}(\bm{x},\hat{n})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rm IR\,limit]{}

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm g}^{[1]}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}^{[1]}(\hat{n}),\hat{n}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm g}(\bm{k},\hat{n})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\rm IR\,limit]{}

\displaystyle e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm red}^{[1]}(\hat{n})}\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm g}^{\,[1]}(\bm{k},\hat{n}).

(28)

The linear galaxy density fluctuation is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm g}^{\,[1]}(\bm{k},\hat{n})=Z^{[1]}(\bm{k},\hat{n})\widetilde{\delta}^{\,[1]}_{\rm m}(\bm{k}),

(29)

where
Z_{1}(\bm{k},\hat{n})
is the first-order kernel function

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}\sigma^{2}_{0}(r)+2\left(\frac{3\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{j}-\delta_{ij}}{2}\right)\sigma^{2}_{2}(r)\;,

where

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\ell}(r)=\frac{1}{3}i^{\ell}\int\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}j_{\ell}(pr)P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(p)\;.

(68)

To simplify the calculation, we only consider the isotropic component of the
{\cal A}
function and ignore the
\sigma_{2}
term. Furthermore, the 2PCF of the wiggle part in Eq. (
65
) has a peak around
r_{\rm BAO}\sim 110\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}
and is zero at other scales, so we fix the scale dependence that appears in the
\sigma_{0}(r)
function to
r=r_{\rm BAO}
. With these approximations, the
{\cal A}
function becomes

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt{\cal A}(\bm{k})\approx\exp\left(\frac{k^{2}(1-\mu^{2})\sigma_{0}^{2}(r_{\rm BAO})+k^{2}\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}_{0,\,\parallel}(r_{\rm BAO})}{2}\right),

(69)

where
\sigma^{2}_{0,\,\parallel}=(1+f)^{2}\sigma_{0}^{2}
. Note that the
\bm{r}
-dependence has been omitted here. Then, we finally obtain

\displaystyle P(\bm{k})|_{\rm w}\to{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}^{2}(\bm{k})\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm w}(k)\;.

(70)

The function that expresses the nonlinear damping of the BAO signal is defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal D}(\bm{k}){\cal A}(\bm{k})={\cal D}_{\rm BAO}(\bm{k})\;,

(71)

and its specific form is given by

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}(\bm{k})=\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}(1-\mu^{2})\sigma_{{\rm BAO},\,\perp}^{2}+k^{2}\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}_{{\rm BAO},\,\parallel}}{2}\right),

(72)

where the radial and transverse components of smoothing factors are calculated as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{{\rm BAO},\,\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\int\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\left(1-j_{0}(pr_{\rm BAO})\right)P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(p),

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{{\rm BAO},\,\parallel}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+f)^{2}\,\sigma^{2}_{{\rm BAO},\,\perp}.

(73)

Substituting Eqs. (
63
) and (
70
) into Eq. (
62
), we obtain the IR-resummed power spectrum model at the linear level:

\displaystyle P(\bm{k})=\big[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\big]^{2}\left[{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}^{2}(\bm{k})P_{\rm w}(k)+P_{\rm nw}(k)\right]\;.

(74)

This form is consistent with the template model proposed by
Eisenstein et al. 2007b
, which has been widely used for BAO analyses.

When the decomposition of the nonlinear power spectrum into the wiggle and no-wiggle parts is applied to the propagator and mode-coupling term in Eq. (
48
), we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684ptG^{2}(\bm{k})P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\to{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}\left[P_{\rm w}(k)+P_{\rm nw}(k)\right]\;,

(75)

and

\displaystyle P_{\rm MC}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left[{\cal D}^{-2}(\bm{k})-1\right]\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm nw}(k)

(76)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left[{\cal A}^{2}(\bm{k})-1\right]\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm w}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\big]^{2}\left[{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}^{2}(\bm{k})P_{\rm w}(k)+P_{\rm nw}(k)\right]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}\left[P_{\rm w}(k)+P_{\rm nw}(k)\right]\;.

These expressions show that the nonlinear damping effect of BAO arises from the mode-coupling term, and the contribution from the propagator term cancels out with the second term of the mode-coupling term.

II.5.2
1-loop level

We turn to the 1-loop order correction terms. In the IR limit, the nonlinear galaxy power spectrum is described as

\displaystyle P(\bm{k})\to{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k}){\cal D}^{-2}(\bm{k})P_{\rm(S)}(\bm{k})\;,

(77)

where
P_{\rm(S)}
is consists only of the short-wavelength density fluctuations,
\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm(S)}
. At the 1-loop level, we derive the short-wavelength power spectra from Eq. (
31
) as

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptP_{\rm(S)}^{[22]}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{[22]}(\bm{k})-P_{\rm IR}^{[22]}(\bm{k})\;,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{[22]}(\bm{k})-\ln{\cal D}^{-2}(\bm{k})\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\;,

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptP_{\rm(S)}^{[13]}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{[13]}(\bm{k})-P_{\rm IR}^{[13]}(\bm{k})\;,

(78)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{[13]}(\bm{k})-\ln{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\;,

where we used the uncorrelation between
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
and
\delta_{\rm(S)}
. Compared to the propagator including the 1-loop correction term in Eq. (
59
), the propagator and the mode-coupling term at the 1-loop level are shown as

\displaystyle G^{2}(\bm{k})P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)

(79)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left[\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)+P_{\rm(S)}^{[13]}(\bm{k})\right]\;,

and

\displaystyle P_{\rm MC}(\bm{k})

(80)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left({\cal D}^{-2}(\bm{k})-1\right)\left[\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)+P_{\rm(S)}^{[13]}(\bm{k})\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle P_{\rm(S)}^{[22]}(\bm{k})\;.

When Eq. (
79
) and Eq. (
80
) are added, they yield the 1-loop power spectrum in SPT.

To describe the nonlinear damping of the BAO signal, we recalculate the mode-coupling term in Eq. (
80
). First, given that
\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)+P_{\rm(S)}^{[13]}(\bm{k})\propto P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)
, we can directly apply the computational techniques used to derive the linear IR resummation model in Eq. (
76
) to the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
80
). Therefore,

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left({\cal D}^{-2}(\bm{k})-1\right)\left[\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)+P_{\rm(S)}^{[13]}(\bm{k})\right]

(81)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[{\rm IR-resummed}]{}

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left({\cal D}^{-2}(\bm{k})-1\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}\left(1-\ln{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\right)P_{\rm nw}(k)+P_{\rm nw}^{[13]}(\bm{k})\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\left({\cal A}^{2}(\bm{k})-1\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}\left(1-\ln{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\right)P_{\rm w}(k)+P_{\rm w}^{[13]}(\bm{k})\right]\;,

where

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptP_{\rm nw}^{[13]}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(P_{\rm nw}(k)/P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\right)P^{[13]}(\bm{k})\;,

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptP_{\rm w}^{[13]}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(P_{\rm w}(k)/P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\right)P^{[13]}(\bm{k})\;.

(82)

Furthermore, in the IR limit,
P_{{\rm(S)}}^{[22]}
can be explicitly written as

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptP_{\rm(S)}^{[22]}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k}){\cal D}^{-2}(\bm{k})

(83)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[P_{22}(\bm{k})-\ln{\cal D}^{-2}(\bm{k})[Z_{1}(\bm{k})]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\right]\;.

In this context, since
P_{22}(\bm{k})
appropriately includes the wiggle part within the mode-coupling integral, no further manipulation is required. For the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
83
), when considering the wiggle part,
{\cal D}^{-2}
originating from the mode-coupling integral should be replaced by
{\cal A}^{2}
. Conversely, for the no-wiggle part,
{\cal D}^{-2}
remains unchanged. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\rm(S)}^{[22]}(\bm{k})\xrightarrow[{\rm IR-resummed}]{}

\displaystyle P_{22}(\bm{k})-\ln{\cal D}^{-2}(\bm{k})[Z_{1}(\bm{k})]^{2}P_{\rm nw}(k)

\displaystyle-{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k}){\cal A}^{2}(\bm{k})\left(\ln{\cal A}^{2}(\bm{k})\right)[Z_{1}(\bm{k})]^{2}P_{\rm w}(k)\;.

(84)

Substituting Eqs. (
81
) and (
84
) into Eq. (
80
) and then combining with Eq. (
79
), we obtain the IR-resummed power spectrum model at the 1-loop level:

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptP(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}^{2}(\bm{k})

(85)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}\left(1-\ln{\cal D}^{2}_{\rm BAO}(\bm{k})\right)P_{\rm w}(k)+P_{\rm w}^{[13]}(\bm{k})\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm nw}(k)+P^{[13]}_{\rm nw}(\bm{k})+P^{[22]}(\bm{k})\;.

) at

in real space. The upper panel shows the result using
{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}
(magenta solid line), the linear theory (black dashed line), and the approximation where

.

II.6
BAO signal in the mode coupling term

The IR-resummed power spectrum model, which is obtained by decomposing the linear power spectrum into the wiggle and no-wiggle parts, as in Eqs. (
74
) and E(
85
), was derived by
Sugiyama and Spergel 2014
based on the results of numerical experiments showing that the BAO signal in the mode-coupling term was negligibly small. The model derived there was one in which all
{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}(\bm{k})
appearing in Eqs. (
74
) and (
85
) was replaced with
{\cal D}(\bm{k})
.

Subsequent studies
Baldauf et al. 2015
;
Vlah et al. 2016
;
Senatore and Zaldarriaga 2015
;
Blas et al. 2016b
;
Senatore and Trevisan 2018
;
Ivanov and Sibiryakov 2018
;
Lewandowski and Senatore 2020
introduced considerations regarding the wiggle part included in the mode-coupling term. It was understood that the wiggle part within the propagator term cancels out with a part of the wiggle part in the mode-coupling term, ultimately leading to the conclusion that the nonlinear damping of the BAO signal is explained by the wiggle part in the mode-coupling term. Consequently, the nonlinear damping of the BAO signal is now described by
{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}
, as shown in Eqs. (
74
) and (
85
), rather than by
{\cal D}
. However, in the context of the
\Lambda
CDM model, the numerical difference between
{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}
and
{\cal D}
is known to be minimal
Blas et al. 2016b
.

Figure
1
numerically demonstrates the nonlinear effects on the BAO signal in real space, assuming
f=0
. This figure plots the ratio of the wiggle part to the no-wiggle part in the linear IR-resummed model given by Eq. (
74
) at
z=0
(magenta solid line). For comparison, the linear prediction (black dashed line) and the approximation where
{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}
is replaced by
{\cal D}
as provided by
Sugiyama and Spergel 2014
(blue dotted line) are also plotted. The lower panel of this figure shows the difference between the result from Eq. (
74
) and the approximation where
{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}
is replaced by
{\cal D}
, defined as
{\rm Diff.}=\left[{\cal D}^{2}_{\rm BAO}-{\cal D}^{2}\right]P_{\rm w}/P_{\rm nw}
.

From this figure, we can see that even if we approximate
{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}\approx{\cal D}
and ignore the wiggles in the mode-coupling term in Eq. (
76
), the numerical effect on the power spectrum is about
0.2\%
. This is the result for the case of
z=0
, where the nonlinear effect is at its maximum, and for actual observations at redshifts
z=0.2-0.2
, this difference will be even smaller.

Therefore, in terms of constructing a model that can realistically explain observations, the approximation
{\cal D}_{\rm BAO}\approx{\cal D}
is valid. In this case, the mode-coupling term in Eq. (
76
) consists only of the no-wiggle part as

\displaystyle P_{\rm MC}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(1-{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\right)[Z_{1}(\bm{k})]^{2}P_{\rm nw}(k)\;,

(86)

leading to the linear IR-resummed model

\displaystyle P(\bm{k})=\big[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\big]^{2}\left[{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})P_{\rm w}(k)+P_{\rm nw}(k)\right]\;.

(87)

Similarly, at the 1-loop level, the following model is also approximately satisfied
Sugiyama and Spergel 2014
:

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptP(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})

(88)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}\left(1-\ln{\cal D}^{2}(\bm{k})\right)P_{\rm w}(k)+P_{\rm w}^{[13]}(\bm{k})\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[Z_{1}(\bm{k})\right]^{2}P_{\rm nw}(k)+P^{[13]}_{\rm nw}(\bm{k})+P^{[22]}(\bm{k})\;.

III
Post-Reconstruction Case

We apply the method for constructing the IR-resummed power spectrum model from the pre-reconstruction to the post-reconstruction case.

III.1
Post-reconstruction density fluctuations

To reconstruct the galaxy distribution, the displacement vector for reconstruction is calculated from the observed galaxy density fluctuations as follows
Eisenstein et al. 2007a
:

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt\bm{s}(\bm{x})=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{x}}\left(\frac{i\bm{p}}{p^{2}}\right)\left(-\frac{W_{\rm G}(pR_{\rm s})}{b_{1,\rm fid}}\right)\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm obs}(\bm{p}),

(89)

where
b_{1,\rm fid}
is an input fiducial linear bias parameter for reconstruction,
W_{\rm G}(pR_{\rm s})=\exp\left(-p^{2}R_{\rm s}^{2}/2\right)
is a Gaussian filter function, and
R_{\rm s}
is an input smoothing scale. This
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
is derived from the observed galaxy fluctuation
\delta_{\rm obs}(\bm{x})
. By substituting
\delta_{\rm obs}
with the nonlinear density fluctuation
\delta
in Eq. (
19
), which includes the bias and RSD effects, we can make a theoretical prediction of
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
.

The reconstruction scheme uses
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
to move each of the galaxy data particles and the corresponding random particles. Such an operation is expressed as

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\left(1+\delta(\bm{x})\right)\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{s}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{s}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\right).

(90)

Here,
\delta_{\rm d}(\bm{x})
is the density fluctuation of the reconstructed galaxy data particles, and
\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})
is the density fluctuation of the reconstructed random particles. The observed post-reconstruction galaxy density fluctuation is then given by
Sugiyama et al. 2021
;
Shirasaki et al. 2021

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm d}(\bm{x})-\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\delta(\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{s}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\right).

III.2
IR effects on the post-reconstruction density fluctuation

The displacement vector for reconstruction,
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
, is derived from the nonlinear density fluctuation, which includes the shift term that should be considered in the IR limit [e.g., see Eq. (
2
)]. This characteristic of
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
is unique, contrasting with the displacement vector
\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})
, where the shift term is excluded [e.g., Eq. (
22
)]. In this subsection, we focus on examining the non-perturbative behavior of the shift term within
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
and demonstrate its impact on the post-reconstruction density fluctuation.

Following the approach introduced in Section
II
, we decompose the galaxy density fluctuation
\delta(\bm{x})
into two components: the IR effect represented by
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
, and the short-wavelength density fluctuation
\delta_{\rm(S)}(\bm{x})
. By substituting Eq. (
24
) and Eq. (
26
) into Eq. (
89
) and Eq. (
91
), we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\delta_{\rm(S)}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}})\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}})\right)

(92)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x^{\prime\prime}\delta_{\rm(S)}(\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}-\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}-\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})\right).

In the second line, we used the coordinate transformation:
\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}=\bm{x}^{\prime}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
. Here,
\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{x})
is defined as

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{x})=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{x}}\left(\frac{i\bm{p}}{p^{2}}\right)\left(-\frac{W_{\rm G}(pR_{\rm s})}{b_{1,\rm fid}}\right)\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{p}).

(93)

In the IR limit, the short-wavelength displacement vector for reconstruction,
\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}
, no longer receives contributions from the shift term, leading to its analogy with the displacement vector
\bm{\Psi}
. As demonstrated in Eqs. (
23
) and (
24
), the IR effect within the nonlinear density fluctuation is represented by
\bm{\Psi}
at the origin. Similar to
\bm{\Psi}
, by decomposing
\bm{s}_{\rm S}
into its origin value and other components, we can elucidate the IR effect within the post-reconstruction density fluctuation:

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{x})=\overline{\bm{s}}+\bm{s}_{\rm(SS)}(\bm{x}),

(94)

where
\overline{\bm{s}}=\bm{s}_{\rm S}(\bm{x}=\bm{0})
. Finally,
\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x})
can be formally rewritten as

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x})=\delta_{\rm(S)rec}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec}),

(95)

where

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec}=\overline{\bm{\Psi}}+\overline{\bm{s}},

(96)

and

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\delta_{\rm(S)rec}(\bm{x})=\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\delta_{\rm(S)}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{s}_{\rm(SS)}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\right).

(97)

This result shows that the IR effect manifests in the post-reconstruction density fluctuation as the coordinate transformation via
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec}
, like the pre-reconstruction case in Eq. (
24
). Consequently, the arguments applicable to the pre-reconstruction IR effect can similarly be applied to the post-reconstruction context.

III.3
Lagrangian space

We can also perform the calculation of density fluctuations after reconstruction in Lagrangian space. To facilitate a comparison with the Zel’dovich approximation, set to be discussed in Section
III.5
, we provide proof for Eq. (
95
) in Lagrangian space.

In Lagrangian space,
\delta_{\rm d}(\bm{x})
and
\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})
are each expressed as

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q\left(1+\delta_{\rm bias}(\bm{q})\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})-\bm{s}(\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q}))\right),

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\bm{s}(\bm{q})\right).

(98)

Similar to Eq. (
92
), they can be rewritten as

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q\left(1+\delta_{\rm bias}(\bm{q})\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}-\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q})-\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}))\right),

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}})\right)

(99)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q^{\prime}\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}^{\prime}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}-\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}^{\prime})\right),

where
\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q})=\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
in Eq. (
23
). For
\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})
, we used the coordinate transformation:
\bm{q}^{\prime}=\bm{q}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
.

Note that
\bm{s}(\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q}))
within
\delta_{\rm d}(\bm{x})
does not include the contribution from the shift term in the IR limit. This is because the coordinate transformation, represented by
\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})
, cancels out the IR effect of
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
. As a result, it can be expressed only in terms of short-wavelength modes as
\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}))
.

Furthermore, we decompose
\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}
into the value at the origin
\bm{q}=\bm{0}
and other parts:

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{s}}+\bm{s}_{\rm(SS)}(\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}))

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{s}}+\bm{s}_{\rm(SS)}(\bm{q}),

(100)

where we used
\bm{\Psi}_{\rm S}(\bm{q}=\bm{0})=\bm{0}
.

Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm(S)d}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec})-\delta_{\rm(S)s}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec})

(101)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm(S)rec}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec}),

where

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm(S)d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q\left(1+\delta_{\rm bias}(\bm{q})\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q})-\bm{s}_{\rm(SS)}(\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}_{\rm(S)}(\bm{q}))\right),

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm(S)s}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\bm{s}_{\rm(SS)}(\bm{q})\right).

(102)

III.4
IR cancellation after reconstruction

We can show that the IR cancellation occurs even after reconstruction:

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x})\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle\delta_{\rm(S)rec}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec})\delta_{\rm(S)rec}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec})\big\rangle

(103)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\big\langle\delta_{\rm(S)rec}(\bm{x})\delta_{\rm(S)rec}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\big\rangle\;.

Approximating both
\delta_{\rm(S)rec}(\bm{x})
and
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec}
as linear, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x})\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\big\langle\delta^{[1]}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}_{\rm rec})\delta^{[1]}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}_{\rm rec})\big\rangle

(104)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle\delta^{[1]}(\bm{x})\delta^{[1]}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\big\rangle\;.

Under this approximation, the propagator term and the mode-coupling term are, respectively, represented as

\displaystyle G^{2}(\bm{k})P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\to{\cal D}_{\rm rec}^{2}(\bm{k})[Z_{1}(\bm{k})]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\;,

(105)

and

\displaystyle P_{\rm MC}(\bm{k})\to(1-{\cal D}_{\rm rec}^{2}(\bm{k}))[Z_{1}(\bm{k})]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\;.

\displaystyle P_{\rm rec}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\rm rec}^{2}(\bm{k})[Z_{1}(\bm{k})]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)

(107)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(1-{\cal D}_{\rm rec}^{2}(\bm{k}))[Z_{1}(\bm{k})]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[Z_{1}(\bm{k})]^{2}P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(k)\;.

Here,
{\cal D}_{\rm rec}(\bm{k})
is the two-dimensional exponentially decaying function after reconstruction, given by

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\rm rec}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\left(-i\bm{k}\cdot\overline{\bm{\Psi}}_{\rm rec}^{[1]}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\rm c}\right),

(108)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}(1-\mu^{2})\sigma^{2}_{\rm rec,\perp}+k^{2}\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\rm rec,\parallel}}{2}\right).

The radial and transverse components of the smoothing factors are further decomposed into

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm rec,\perp}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{\perp}^{2}+\sigma_{\rm ps,\perp}^{2}+\sigma_{\rm ss,\perp}^{2}\;,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm rec,\parallel}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{\parallel}^{2}+\sigma_{\rm ps,\parallel}^{2}+\sigma_{\rm ss,\parallel}^{2}\;,

(109)

where
\sigma_{\perp}^{2}
and
\sigma_{\parallel}^{2}
are given in Eq. (

). The subscript “ps” means the correlation between
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
and
\overline{\bm{s}}
, and “ss” denotes the auto-correlation of
\overline{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\rm ps,\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\int_{k_{\rm min}}^{k_{\rm max}}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\left(-\frac{W_{\rm G}(pR_{\rm s})}{b_{1,\rm fid}}\right)\left[2\left(b_{1}+\frac{f}{5}\right)\right]P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(p)\;,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\int_{k_{\rm min}}^{k_{\rm max}}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\left(-\frac{W_{\rm G}(pR_{\rm s})}{b_{1,\rm fid}}\right)\Bigg[2\left(1+f\right)\left(b_{1}+\frac{3}{5}f\right)\Bigg]P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(p)\;,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\rm ss,\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\int_{k_{\rm min}}^{k_{\rm max}}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\left(-\frac{W_{\rm G}(pR_{\rm s})}{b_{1,\rm fid}}\right)^{2}\left[\left(b_{1}^{2}+\frac{2}{5}b_{1}f+\frac{3}{35}f^{2}\right)P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(p)+\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\right]\;,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\rm ss,\parallel}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\int_{k_{\rm min}}^{k_{\rm max}}\frac{dp}{2\pi^{2}}\left(-\frac{W_{\rm G}(pR_{\rm s})}{b_{1,\rm fid}}\right)^{2}\Bigg[\left(b_{1}^{2}+\frac{42}{35}b_{1}f+\frac{3}{7}f^{2}\right)P_{\rm m}^{[11]}(p)+\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\Bigg]\;.

Here, we summarize the key considerations when performing the above calculations:

1.

The integral range should depend on the scale at which
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
is measured.

2.

Since
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
is derived from the power spectrum of the measured galaxy density fluctuations, we should consider the shot-noise term
1/\bar{n}
in
\sigma_{\rm ss,\perp}^{2}
and
\sigma_{\rm ss,\parallel}^{2}

III.5
Comparisons with the Zel’dovich approximation

Previous studies
Padmanabhan et al. 2009
;
Seo et al. 2016
;
White 2015
;
Chen et al. 2019
have often used a simple linear approximation for
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
, known as the Zel’dovich approximation for reconstruction. This approximation, however, does not account for nonlinear IR effects in
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
. In this subsection, we present a detailed comparison between the Zel’dovich approximation and our findings in Section
III.4
.

In the Zel’dovich approximation, Eq. (
98
) becomes

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q\left(1+\delta_{\rm bias}^{[1]}(\bm{q})\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\bm{\Psi}^{[1]}(\bm{q})-\bm{s}^{[1]}(\bm{q})\right)\;,

\displaystyle 1+\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\int d^{3}q\delta_{\rm D}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{q}-\bm{s}^{[1]}(\bm{q})\right)\;.

(111)

In the IR limit, they are represented as

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm d}^{[1]}\left(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}-\overline{\bm{s}}^{[1]}\right)\;,

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm s}^{[1]}\left(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{s}}^{[1]}\right)\;.

(112)

We then derive

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x})\to\delta_{\rm d}^{[1]}\left(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}-\overline{\bm{s}}^{[1]}\right)-\delta_{\rm s}^{[1]}\left(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{s}}^{[1]}\right)\;.

(113)

The key difference of this equation from Eq. (
101
) is how
\bm{s}(\bm{q})
is treated within
\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})
. In Eq. (
101
), non-perturbative IR effects are incorporated into
\bm{s}(\bm{q})
, revealing both
\overline{\bm{\Psi}}
and
\overline{\bm{s}}
effects in
\delta_{\rm s}(\bm{x})
. In contrast, the Zel’dovich approximation assumes a linear
\bm{s}(\bm{q})
, resulting in only the
\overline{\bm{s}}
effect.

In Fourier space, Eq. (
113
) becomes

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm rec}(\bm{k})

(114)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\Big[e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\left(\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]}+\overline{\bm{s}}^{[1]}\right)}Z_{{\rm d},1}(\bm{k})-e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\overline{\bm{s}}^{[1]}}Z_{{\rm s},1}(\bm{k})\Big]\widetilde{\delta}_{\rm m}^{\,[1]}(\bm{k})\;,

where

\displaystyle Z_{{\rm d},1}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1+\left(-\frac{W_{\rm G}(kR_{\rm s})}{b_{1,\rm fid}}\right)\right]Z_{1}(\bm{k})\;,

\displaystyle Z_{{\rm s},1}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{W_{\rm G}(kR_{\rm s})}{b_{1,\rm fid}}\right)\,Z_{1}(\bm{k})\;.

(115)

\displaystyle G(\bm{k})={\cal D}_{\rm rec}(\bm{k})Z_{{\rm d},1}(\bm{k})-{\cal D}_{\rm ss}(\bm{k})Z_{{\rm s},1}(\bm{k})\;,

(116)

with

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\rm ss}(\bm{k})=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\left(-i\bm{k}\cdot\overline{\bm{s}}^{[1]}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\rm c}\right)\;.

(117)

Equation (
116
) shows that the propagator in the Zel’dovich approximation comprises two Gaussian decay functions:
{\cal D}_{\rm rec}(\bm{k})
and
{\cal D}_{\rm ss}(\bm{k})

105

{\cal D}_{\rm rec}(\bm{k})
appears. The specific expression for

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\rm ss}(\bm{k})=\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}(1-\mu^{2})\sigma^{2}_{\rm ss,\perp}+k^{2}\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\rm ss,\parallel}}{2}\right),

(118)

where the radial and transverse components of the smoothing factors are given in Eq. (
110
).

We can also demonstrate that Eq. (
113
) breaks the IR cancellation:

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt\langle\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x})\delta_{\rm rec}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{\rm d}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\delta_{\rm d}^{[1]}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\rangle

(119)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{\rm d}^{[1]}(\bm{x}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]})\delta^{[1]}_{\rm s}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{\rm s}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\delta^{[1]}_{\rm d}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\overline{\bm{\Psi}}^{[1]})\rangle

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{\rm s}^{[1]}(\bm{x})\delta^{[1]}_{\rm s}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\rangle\;.

and

Hence, breaking the IR cancellation leads to an exponential decay of the power spectrum. This finding deviates from our result where the IR cancellation is satisfied even after reconstruction.

IV.1
Shot noise in the cross-power spectrum

The shot-noise term in the power spectrum arises when the same particle is counted multiple times during power spectrum calculations. Usually, the shot-noise term is absent in the cross-power spectrum of different density fluctuations. However, in the case of the cross-power spectrum between the pre- and post-reconstruction density fluctuations, the same galaxies can be identified, making the shot-noise term significant.

In the discrete picture, the number density in Fourier space is given by

\displaystyle n(\bm{k})=\sum_{i}^{N_{\rm g}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{i}},

(140)

where
\bm{x}_{i}
denotes the position of the
i
th galaxy and
N_{\rm g}
represents the total number of galaxies. The auto power spectrum in this discrete picture is expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{V}{N^{2}_{\rm g}}|n(\bm{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V}{N^{2}_{\rm g}}\sum_{i\neq j}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j})}+\frac{V}{N^{2}_{\rm g}}\sum_{i=j}^{N_{\rm g}}

(141)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(\bm{k})+\frac{1}{\bar{n}},

where
V
denotes the survey volume. The first and second terms on the right-hand side correspond to the power spectrum and the shot-noise term, respectively,

\displaystyle P(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V}{N^{2}_{\rm g}}\sum_{i\neq j}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j})},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V}{N^{2}_{\rm g}}\sum_{i=j}^{N_{\rm g}},

(142)

where
\bar{n}=N_{\rm g}/V
is the mean number density.

Let
n_{\rm rec}(\bm{k})
be the post-reconstruction density field in Fourier space. It can be expressed in the discrete picture as

\displaystyle n_{\rm rec}(\bm{k})=\sum_{i}^{N_{\rm g}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}_{{\rm rec},i}},

(143)

where

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{{\rm rec},i}=\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{s}(\bm{x}_{i}).

(144)

Therefore, the shot-noise term in the cross-power spectrum is given by

\displaystyle\frac{V}{N^{2}_{\rm g}}\sum_{i=j}^{N_{\rm g}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\left(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{{\rm rec},i}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V}{N^{2}_{\rm g}}\sum_{i=j}^{N_{\rm g}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{s}(\bm{x}_{i})}

(145)

Calculating the ensemble average of the above equation and taking the continuous limit, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{V}{N^{2}_{\rm g}}\sum_{i=j}^{N_{\rm g}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{s}(\bm{x}_{i})}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\rm g}}\int d^{3}x\left\langle e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{s}(\bm{x})}\right\rangle

(146)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\left\langle e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{s}(\bm{x}=\bm{0})}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\left\langle e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\overline{\bm{s}}^{[1]}}\right\rangle.

In the final line, we made an approximation of the linear
\bm{s}(\bm{x})
. Finally, we derive

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{V}{N_{\rm g}^{2}}n(\bm{k})n_{\rm rec}^{*}(\bm{k})\right\rangle=P_{\rm cross}(\bm{k})+{\cal D}_{\rm ss}(\bm{k})\left(\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\right).

(147)

This result indicates that the shot-noise term is proportional to
{\cal D}_{\rm ss}(\bm{k})
and exponentially decays, as is
P_{\rm cross}(\bm{k})
.

V
Conclusions

In this paper, we present a new theoretical model addressing the resummation of infrared (IR) effects in the power spectrum of galaxy density fluctuations after reconstruction. Our model accurately describes the nonlinear damping of the post-reconstruction BAO signal, while including the 1-loop correction term in SPT. The first main results are summarized in Eqs. (
126
) and (
127
).

We point out in Section
III.2
that it is crucial to consider the nonlinear IR effects contained within the displacement vector for reconstruction
\bm{s}
(
89
). As a result, the IR effects on the post-reconstruction density fluctuation can be described as a coordinate transformation of the density fluctuations (
95
), like the pre-reconstruction case (
24
). This result leads to the cancellation of the IR effects on the power spectrum in the IR limit even after reconstruction. Furthermore, the nonlinear behavior of BAO is characterized by the two-dimensional Gaussian decaying function of BAO, supporting the theoretical foundation of the commonly used single two-dimensional Gaussian decay function in post-reconstruction power spectrum and 2PCF analyses
(
Alam et al. 2017
;
Hinton et al. 2017
;
Alam et al. 2021
;
Moon et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

We compare our findings regarding the post-reconstruction infrared (IR) effects with those obtained using the Zel’dovich approximation and SPT in Section
III.5
and Section
III.6
. Notably, in the Zel’dovich approximation, which linearly approximates
\bm{s}
, IR cancellation does not occur, and two Gaussian damping functions are required to describe the nonlinear BAO effects. Conversely, in the post-reconstruction SPT, IR cancellation is observed in the IR limit, corroborating our results.

We further clarify the behavior of the cross-power spectrum between pre- and post-reconstruction density fluctuations, noting its overall exponential decay due to the absence of IR cancellation. This observation also applies to the corresponding shot-noise term. While these phenomena have been observed in previous simulation studies
Wang et al. 2022
, our work thus provides a mathematical foundation for these observations. Additionally, as our second main result, we present a model, as shown in Eq. (
138
), to elucidate the nonlinear effects of BAO on the cross-power spectrum.

In conclusion, our work presents a novel direction in developing theoretical models for the post-reconstruction power spectrum beyond standard perturbation theory. The IR-resummed models presented in this paper, which consider the galaxy bias and RSD effects, are directly applicable to the analysis of real galaxy data.
