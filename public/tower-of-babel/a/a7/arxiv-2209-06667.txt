The potential roles of transacylation in intracellular lipolysis and related QSSA approximations
Ján Eliaš, Klemens Fellner, Peter Hofer, Monika Oberer, Renate Schreiber, Rudolf Zechner

Abstract
Fatty acids (FAs) are crucial energy metabolites, signalling molecules, and membrane building blocks for a wide range of organisms. Adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) is the first and presumingly most crucial regulator of FA release from triacylglycerols (TGs) stored within cytosolic lipid droplets. However, besides the function of releasing FAs by hydrolysing TGs into diacylglycerols (DGs), ATGL also promotes the transacylation reaction of two DG molecules into one TG and one monoacylglycerol molecule. To date, it is unknown whether DG transacylation is a coincidental byproduct of ATGL-mediated lipolysis or whether it is physiologically relevant. Experimental evidence is scarce since both, hydrolysis and transacylation, rely on the same active site of ATGL and always occur in parallel in an ensemble of molecules. This paper illustrates the potential roles of transacylation. It shows that, depending on the kinetic parameters but also on the state of the hydrolytic machinery, transacylation can increase or decrease downstream products up to 50% and more. We provide an extensive asymptotic analysis including quasi-steady-state approximations (QSSA) with higher order correction terms and provide numerical simulation. We also argue that when assessing the validity of QSSAs one should include parameter sensitivity derivatives. Our results suggest that the transacylation function of ATGL is of biological relevance by providing feedback options and altogether stability to the lipolytic machinery in adipocytes. Keywords: Lipolysis; Enzyme reaction; Transacylation; Michaelis-Menten; Quasi steady state approximation; Simulation 2020 MSC : 34C60, 37N25, 92C40, 92C45.

Introduction

Fatty acids (FAs) are crucial for ATP production, synthesis of biological membranes, thermogenesis, and signal transduction
[
3

25

Animals and humans store FAs in the form of water-insoluble triacylglycerols (TGs, one species thereof is triolein, TO) within cytosolic lipid droplets (LDs) of specialised fat cells called adipocytes, but also in other cell types. From a biochemical perspective, a TG molecule is composed of three FAs esterified to a glycerol backbone. LD-associated TGs are degraded step-wise by an enzymatic cascade of lipid hydrolases commonly designated as ”lipases” in a process called intracellular lipolysis.

Distinct lipases are responsible for each lipolytic step
[
4
,
24
]
. The initial step in the degradation of TGs is catalysed by adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) releasing one FA and one diacylglycerol (DG, one species thereof is diolein, DO) molecule
[
19

. In the second lipolytic step, hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) hydrolyses DGs into FAs and monoacylglycerols (MGs; one species thereof is monoolein, MO)
[
18
]
. Finally, monoglyceride lipase (MGL) hydrolyses MGs into FAs and glycerol, both of which can be secreted from the adipocytes into the bloodstream to supply remote organs.

To ensure an adequate response to changes in the metabolic state of an organism, lipolysis is fine-tuned by a large number of molecular factors regulating the three lipases
[
8
]
. One of these factors is comparative gene identification-58 (CGI-58) which accelerates lipolysis by increasing ATGL activity
[
14
]
. Despite literally thousands of studies on the physiological role of lipolysis and its complex regulation, a quantitative description of the intricate reaction processes of TG hydrolysis is, however, still missing.

Given the outstanding metabolic importance of lipolysis, it is remarkable that ATGL (and possibly HSL
[
26
]
) not only catalyse the hydrolysis reaction but in parallel also a transacylation reaction, which transfers a FA from one DG onto a second DG to generate TG and MG
[

In fact, measurement of ATGL activity is far from being trivial due its hydrolase and transacylase activity occurring simultaneously. Chemical inhibition of ATGL

For example, as soon as a TG molecule formed by DG transacylation is hydrolysed to DG, then the effective outcome (one FA and MG were formed) is identical to the outcome of a DG hydrolysis event, thereby masking DG transacylase activity.

The importance and potential metabolic function of transacylation
is unknown.
By its function, the transacylation reaction provides feedback towards the TG pool
as well as an alternative in creating MG alongside DG hydrolysis.
One may believe that transacylation plays a minor to no role in TG metabolism.

Specifically, we postulate Michaelis-Menten (MM) kinetics for TG and DG hydrolysis and

[

Secondly, using MAL as reaction rate model for an enzymatic transacylation reaction

mechanism of transacylation becomes better understood.

From a mathematical viewpoint, the stoichiometry of DG hydrolysis (involving one DG molecule) is different to DG transacylation (involving two DG molecules), which implies different nonlinearities of the respective reaction rates. Hence, the relative contributions
of DG hydrolysis and DG transacylation
depend on the DG concentration, which in return depends on
the kinetics producing and processing DGs: We are faced with a complex nonlinear system behaviour.
Finally, we also emphasise that we believe that the analysis of this paper can be extended to general
superlinear and monotone increasing models for DG transacylation rates.

The results of this article are summarised as follows:

In Section

4
Model reduction and QSSA prerequisites

The most important equations in system (
2.2
)
is the closed subsystems for TG and DG concentrations:

\displaystyle\dot{s}

\displaystyle=-m_{1}(s)+V\kappa q^{2},

\displaystyle s(0)=1,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\dot{q}

\displaystyle=L\bigl[m_{1}(s)-Vd(q)\bigr],

\displaystyle d(q):=m_{2}(q)+2\kappa q^{2}.

(4.2)

Here, we have scaled (w.l.o.g.) the initial TG concentration to one and introduce the function
d(q)
as a short hand for the sum of the DG hydrolysis and DG transacylation reaction rates in eq. (
4.2
).

In this section, we investigate under which conditions system
(
4.1
)–(
4.2
) can be reduced to a simpler model system by means of a quasi-steady-state-approximation (QSSA). QSSAs exploit that some quantities change slowly in time compared to typical time scales of the overall dynamics. Then, a zero order QSSA sets the time derivative of these quantities to zero, thus treating it quasi like for a steady state. From the viewpoint of asymptotic analysis, QSSAs are singularly perturbed ODE problems, see e.g.
[
9
,
12
]
and the many references therein.
Hence, approximating slowly changing quantities leads to initial layers (except in the special case where the initial data of the slowly changing quantities already matches their QSSA values), which can be resolved, for instance, by matched asymptotics.
More accurate approximations can be obtained by calculating higher order correction terms. Note that (
4.1
) can’t qualify for a QSSA since the positive feedback provided by DG transacylation is at most half of TG hydrolysis. Even if DG hydrolysis could be neglected, then the feedback DG transacylation and subsequent TG hydrolysis provides is only like TG
\to
DG
\to
1/2 TG
\to
1/2 DG …, see Figure
2
.

However, eq. (
4.2
) features a gain and two loss terms (TG hydrolysis vs. DG hydrolysis and DG transacylation), where the loss terms
are monotone increasing in
q
. Hence, the dynamics of (
4.2
) tends to balance gain and loss terms. If this happens sufficiently fast, a QSSA is expected to serve as a simplified approximative model replacing
(
4.2
) by an algebraic equation (and possible higher order correction terms).

As a first example, we divide eq. (
4.2
) by
L
and assume that
L
is a large parameter. Then, the small parameter
\varepsilon={L}^{-1}

suggests a QSSA
\varepsilon\dot{q}\simeq 0
whenever
L
is sufficiently large. In the following, we will see that also the parameters

V
and
\kappa
yield QSSAs provided they are sufficiently large. In these two cases, one rescales
q\to\varepsilon q
in
(
4.2
) with either
\varepsilon=V^{-1}
or
\varepsilon=\kappa^{-1/2}
, which
lead again to a left hand side
\varepsilon\dot{q}\simeq 0
.

In all three cases, system (
4.1
)–(
4.2
) can be approximated by a zero order QSSA model system by setting
\varepsilon\dot{q}=0
and replacing the differential equation (
4.2
) by the algebraic equation

\frac{m_{1}(s)}{V}=d(\tilde{q})=2\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}+m_{2}(\tilde{q}),\qquad\text{with}\quad m_{2}(\tilde{q})=\frac{\tilde{q}}{1+\tilde{q}},

(4.3)

where the function
\tilde{q}=\tilde{q}(s)
is the unique positive solution to (
4.3
) (recall the monotonicity in
\tilde{q}
) as long as it exists. In particular,
\tilde{q}
is not defined when
\kappa=0
and
\frac{m_{1}(s)}{V}\geq 1
which contradicts the bound
m_{2}(\tilde{q})<1

for all
\tilde{q}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Note, that the explicit solution to the third order polynomial (
4.3
) by Cardano’s formula is of little practical use
and that we will make use of
\tilde{q}
either implicitly or approximatively.

The following Lemma
4.1
states a parameter condition which imply
\tilde{q}\leq 1/2
and provides a useful approximation formula of
\tilde{q}
in this case.
Note that in practical examples, we expect QSSA levels
\tilde{q}
to be rather small, in particular if
V
and/or
\kappa
are large enough.

Lemma 4.1

(Condition ensuring
\tilde{q}\leq\frac{1}{2}
and explicit approximation formula)
.

Assume that the parameters
(V,\kappa)
satisfy
the condition

V\geq\frac{4}{1+2\kappa}\qquad\iff\qquad\kappa\geq\frac{2}{V}-\frac{1}{2}.

(4.4)

For instance, suppose
V\geq 4
and
\kappa\geq 0
.

Then,
\tilde{q}\leq\frac{1}{2}
holds for all
s\leq 1
and
K>0
and we can approximate (within 10%)

\tilde{q}\sim\frac{1}{V}\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+4(2\kappa-1)/V}+1}=\begin{cases}O(\frac{1}{V})&\text{if }V\gg\kappa,\\
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\kappa V})}&\text{if }V\ll\kappa.\end{cases}

(4.5)

Proof.

Since
d(\tilde{q})
is strictly monotone increasing in
\tilde{q}
, the QSSA equation (
4.3
), i.e.

d(\tilde{q})=2\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{q}}{1+\tilde{q}}=\frac{m_{1}(s)}{V}=:I.

has a unique positive solution
\tilde{q}
for every input value
I>0
.
The explicit solution by Cardano’s formula is too cumbersome for practical use. Instead, we derive an approximation formula for sufficiently small
\tilde{q}
, i.e. inputs

I
. In particular, since
d(\tilde{q})
is monotone increasing, a sufficient condition for
\tilde{q}\leq\frac{1}{2}
is obtained from estimating
\frac{m_{1}}{V}\leq\frac{1}{V}
for all
s\geq 0
and
K>0
:

\frac{1}{V}\leq d\Bigl(\tilde{q}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{\kappa}{2}+\frac{1}{3},\qquad\iff\qquad\kappa\geq\frac{2}{V}-\frac{2}{3}.

Next, for sufficiently small
\tilde{q}
(to be determined), we can rewrite

d(\tilde{q})=2\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}+\tilde{q}-\tilde{q}^{2}+\underbrace{\frac{\tilde{q}^{3}}{1+\tilde{q}}}_{=\epsilon=O(\tilde{q}^{3})}=I

(4.6)

and the last term on the left hand side will be assumed of lower order. A formal asymptotic expansion
\tilde{q}=\tilde{q}_{0}+\epsilon\tilde{q}_{1}
yields

\tilde{q}_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{1+4I(2\kappa-1)}-1}{2(2\kappa-1)}=\frac{2I}{\sqrt{1+4I(2\kappa-1)}+1},\qquad\tilde{q}_{1}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4I(2\kappa-1)}}.

In order to ensure the approximation
\tilde{q}_{0}
to be real
in the case
when
0\leq 2\kappa<1
,
it is sufficient to impose the constraint

\frac{1}{V}\leq\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{1-2\kappa}\in\Bigl[\frac{1}{4},\infty\Bigr)\quad\text{for}\quad\kappa\in\Bigl[0,\frac{1}{2}\Bigr).

(4.7)

From (
4.6
), we calculate that

\tilde{q}_{0}\leq\frac{1}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\epsilon(\tilde{q}_{0})=\frac{\tilde{q}_{0}^{3}}{1+\tilde{q}_{0}}\leq\frac{1}{12}.

Thus,
\epsilon(\tilde{q})=\epsilon(\tilde{q}_{0})+O(\epsilon)
is
(at least) of order
O(10^{-1})
. On the other hand, straightforward calculation shows that
\tilde{q}_{0}\leq\frac{1}{2}
is equivalent to
I\leq(1+2\kappa)/4
. Since
m_{1}(s)\leq\tfrac{1}{V}
, a sufficient condition independent of
s
and
K
is

\frac{1}{V}\leq\frac{1+2\kappa}{4}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\tilde{q}_{0}\leq\frac{1}{2},

which is (
4.4
) and also implies (
4.7
) and comletes the proof.
∎

4.1
Conditions for QSSA

A QSSA which approximates
\varepsilon\dot{q}\simeq 0
is expected to feature an initial layer
whenever the initial data
q(0)
do not equal the initial values of the approximation
\tilde{q}(s(0))
, see e.g.
[
9
]
. Indeed, we expect

\dot{q}\sim O(\varepsilon^{-1})
during the initial layer except for initial data
q(0)
, which are already
O(\varepsilon)
-close to

\tilde{q}(s(0))
.

Here, it is natural to consider initial data
q(0)=0
, i.e. that there is initially no DG, which implies that there is an initial layer behaviour.
(Other initial data which are not
O(\varepsilon)
-close to
\tilde{q}(s(0))
can be treated the same.)
For a QSSA to be a useful and valid approximation, the initial layer must be short compared to the dynamics which is of
interest.

In the following, we derive necessary conditions on the parameters as well as bounds on the shortness of the
initial layer.
The proximity of
q(t)
to
\tilde{q}(s(t))

is – in our opinion – best studied after rewriting (
4.2
) as perturbation
\pi
around the QSSA variable
\tilde{q}(s(t))
, i.e.

q(t)=\tilde{q}(s(t))+\pi(t).

(4.8)

By using the identities
q^{2}=\tilde{q}^{2}+\pi(2\tilde{q}+\pi)
and

m_{2}(q)=m_{2}(\tilde{q})+\frac{\pi}{(1+\tilde{q})(1+\tilde{q}+\pi)}
,
we rephrase the right hand side of (
4.2
) as

\dot{q}=-LV\pi\underbrace{\left[2\kappa(2\tilde{q}+\pi)+\frac{1}{(1+\tilde{q})(1+\tilde{q}+\pi)}\right]}_{>0}.

(4.9)

Note that the squared bracket on the r.h.s. is always strictly positive
(since
q=\pi+\tilde{q}\geq 0
). Hence, the equality
\mathrm{sign}(\dot{q})=-\mathrm{sign}(\pi)
follows, which is a useful observation.

We would like to estimate the duration of the initial layer in the following.
First, we derive the evolution equation of

. By taking the derivate of (

\tilde{q}
short for
\tilde{q}(s)

\dot{\tilde{q}}=\dot{\tilde{q}}(s)=\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)}{Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q})}\dot{s},

where we use the formulas:

m_{1}^{\prime}(s)=\frac{K}{(K+s)^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d^{\prime}(q)=4\kappa{q}+m^{\prime}_{2}(q)>0,\quad m_{2}^{\prime}(q)=\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}},

(4.10)

By recalling
\dot{q}=\dot{\tilde{q}}+\dot{\pi}
, we obtain

\dot{\pi}=-LV\pi\left[2\kappa(2\tilde{q}+\pi)+\frac{1}{(1+\tilde{q})(1+\tilde{q}+\pi)}\right]-\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)}{Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q})}\dot{s},

(4.11)

The time scales of eq. (
4.11
) are key to understanding necessary conditions for a QSSA.
The first term in (
4.11
) acts as a stabilising terms and aims to relax
\pi(t)
towards zero (since the square bracket is positive).
On the other hand, the second term is always positive (as
\dot{s}<0

\tilde{q}
due to the decay of

\dot{s}\ll 1

The eigenvalue
-LVd^{\prime}(\tilde{q})<0
characterises the linear stability of the QSSA
\tilde{q}(s)
provided that the decay of
s
is negligible.

Remark 4.1
.

The fast timescale of classical QSSA for a single substrate - single enzyme reaction is often calculated from the eigenvalue
LVd^{\prime}(\tilde{q})

[

First, we consider (

(4.12)

Secondly, at

,

and

4.11

\dot{\pi}|_{t=t_{m}}=\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}}{Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}_{m})}\left[m_{1}-V\kappa\tilde{q}_{m}^{2}\right]=\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}}{Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}_{m})}\left[\frac{m_{1}}{2}+\frac{Vm_{2}(\tilde{q}_{m})}{2}\right],\qquad\tilde{q}_{m}:=\tilde{q}(s(t_{m})),

(4.13)

where the last equality holds due to
\dot{q}(t_{m})=0
, i.e.
m_{1}=Vd(q)
and
q(t_{m})=\tilde{q}_{m}
.

Thirdly, we consider the halfway-up timepoint when
\pi=-\frac{\tilde{q}(s)}{2}
holds in
[0,t_{m}]
. Evaluating
\dot{\pi}
yields

\displaystyle\dot{\pi}|_{\pi=-\frac{\tilde{q}(s)}{2}}

\displaystyle=LV\underbrace{\left[\frac{3}{2}\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}+\frac{1}{(2+\tilde{q})}\frac{\tilde{q}}{(1+\tilde{q})}\right]}_{\frac{1}{(2+\tilde{q})}\frac{m_{1}}{V}\leq\cdots\leq\frac{3}{4}\frac{m_{1}}{V}\hskip 8.19447pt\ }+\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}}{Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q})}\!\!\!\underbrace{\left[{m_{1}}-V\kappa{q}^{2}\right]}_{\frac{m_{1}}{2}+\frac{Vm_{2}}{2}\leq\cdots\leq m_{1}\hskip 16.38895pt}

where the estimates of first term used that
m_{1}=Vd(\tilde{q})
and the lower bound in the second term follows
from
\dot{q}>0
impllying
2V\kappa q^{2}<m_{1}-Vm_{2}
.
Note also that
\frac{1}{2+\tilde{q}}\lesssim\frac{1}{2}
for small values of
\tilde{q}
, which we expect for large values of
V
and
\kappa
. Hence, we observe that

\dot{\pi}|_{\pi=-\frac{\tilde{q}(s)}{2}}\simeq\frac{\dot{\pi}|_{t=0}+\dot{\pi}|_{t=t_{m}}}{2}

We therefore take this average as a typical value of
\dot{\pi}

throughout the initial layer and use it as an estimation
T
of the duration
t_{m}
of the initial layer:

\tilde{q}(s(t_{m}))=T\frac{\dot{\pi}|_{t=0}+\dot{\pi}|_{t=t_{m}}}{2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad T:=\frac{2\tilde{q}(s(t_{m}))}{\dot{\pi}|_{t=0}+\dot{\pi}|_{t=t_{m}}},

(4.14)

(and where of course
T\simeq t_{m}
should hold for a good estimation
T
).

In the following, we estimate
T
in terms of the parameters. First, by recalling (
4.3
), we observe that

V\tilde{q}d^{\prime}(\tilde{q})=V\underbrace{\left[4\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{q}}{(1+\tilde{q})^{2}}\right]}_{\frac{1}{(1+\tilde{q})}\frac{m_{1}}{V}\leq\cdots\leq 2\frac{m_{1}}{V}\hskip 8.19447pt\ }=\theta(\tilde{q})m_{1},\qquad\text{where}\quad\theta(\tilde{q})\in\left[\frac{1}{1+\tilde{q}},2\right],\quad\forall\tilde{q}\geq 0,

and moreover,
\theta\in[\tfrac{1}{2},2]
for
\tilde{q}\leq 1
. Next, we rewrite
T
as

T=\frac{1}{T_{1}^{-1}+T_{2}^{-1}+T_{3}^{-1}}

(4.15)

and identify the terms
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
in (
4.12
) and
T_{3}
in (
4.13
) using the notations
\tilde{q}_{0}:=\tilde{q}(s(0))=\tilde{q}(1)
and
q_{m}:=\tilde{q}(s(t_{m}))
:

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{m_{1}(s(0))}\frac{\tilde{q}_{m}}{L}=2(K+1)\frac{\tilde{q}_{m}}{L},

(4.16)

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{m_{1}^{\prime}(s(0))}\frac{\tilde{q}_{m}}{\tilde{q}_{0}}\frac{V\tilde{q}_{0}d^{\prime}(\tilde{q}_{0})}{m_{1}(s(0))}=\frac{2(K+1)^{2}}{K}\frac{\tilde{q}_{m}}{\tilde{q}_{0}}\theta,\qquad\theta(\tilde{q})\in\left[\frac{1}{1+\tilde{q}},2\right],

(4.17)

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{m_{1}^{\prime}(s(t_{m}))}\frac{\tilde{q}_{m}Vd^{\prime}(V\tilde{q}_{m})}{{m_{1}}-V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}_{m}}=\frac{2(K+s(t_{m}))^{2}}{K}\frac{\theta}{\psi},\qquad\psi\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1],

(4.18)

where we have used for
T_{3}
in (
4.13
) that
\tilde{q}_{m}=\tilde{q}(t_{m})=q(t_{m})
and
{m_{1}}-V\kappa{q}^{2}=\psi m_{1}
for some
\psi\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1]
.

In particular, we will estimate (
4.17
) and (
4.18
) as

T_{2}=\frac{\tilde{q}_{m}}{\tilde{q}_{0}}\frac{2(K+1)^{2}}{K}\theta\leq\frac{4(K+1)^{2}}{K},\qquad T_{3}=\frac{2(K+s(t_{m}))^{2}}{K}\frac{\theta}{\psi}\leq\frac{8(K+1)^{2}}{K},

since
\tilde{q}_{m}\leq\tilde{q}_{0}
,
\theta\leq 2
and
\psi^{-1}\leq 2
.
We then obtain the upper bound

T\leq\frac{2(K+1)^{2}}{\frac{L}{\tilde{q}_{m}}(K+1)+\frac{3}{4}K}.

(4.19)

The time scale
T
has to be compared a time scale representing the decay of the substrate
s

\frac{m_{1}}{2}\leq-\dot{s}\leq m_{1}
holds during the initial layer (due to
\dot{q}>0
implying
2V\kappa q^{2}<m_{1}-Vm_{2}
), we choose the Michaelis Menten model
\dot{s}=-\frac{3}{4}m_{1}(s)
to provide a reference time of the decay of

. Straightforward integration with

yields

K\ln(s(t))+s(t)=1-\frac{3}{4}t.

As comparison for the times scale of the initial layer, we introduce the time
T_{90\%}
when
s(T_{90\%})=\tfrac{9}{10}
,
which means that at time
T_{90\%}
about 90% of the TG substrate is still to be processed. We calculate

T_{90\%}=\frac{2}{15}+\frac{4}{3}K\ln\Bigl(\frac{10}{9}\Bigr)\simeq\frac{2}{15}(1+K).

(4.20)

As a condition on the estimation
T
of the duration of the initial layer, we impose

\frac{2(K+1)^{2}}{\frac{L}{\tilde{q}_{m}}(K+1)+\frac{3}{4}K}=T\leq T_{90\%}=\frac{2}{15}(1+K)\iff\frac{L}{\tilde{q}_{m}}\geq 15-\frac{3}{4}\frac{K}{K+1}.

(4.21)

We state the following Proposition:

Proposition 4.2
.

Denote by
t_{m}
the time point when

is maximal, thus satisfying
q(t_{m})=\tilde{q}(s(t_{m}))=:\tilde{q}_{m}

holds. Then,

can be considered suitable for a QSSA in the sense that after the estimation

of the duration of the initial layer least

The weaker, yet simplified condition

(4.23)

.

Both conditions (
4.22
) and (

to be sufficiently small at the end time
t_{m}
of the initial layer. Smallness of

is implied
from

(or both) being sufficiently large, cf. (

) and Lemma
4.1
.

By inserting (
4.23

4.3
) and using

,
we obtain the explicit conditions

\kappa\geq\frac{15}{2L}\left(\frac{15}{LV}-\frac{1}{1+\frac{L}{15}}\right),\quad\text{or}\quad V\geq\frac{1}{\frac{2\kappa L^{2}}{15^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+\frac{15}{L}}}

(4.24)

Altogether, eqs. (
4.23
) and (

) underline that we can expect a QSSA of the

,
V
and/or

Looking back to numerical example Figure

Remark 4.3

We point out that

5
Asymptotic expansions

In this section, we perform asymptotic expansions of the non-dimensionalised full model system (
2.2
), which we recall for the convenience of the reader

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\dot{s}=-m_{1}(s)+V\kappa q^{2},\\[2.84526pt]
&\frac{1}{L}\dot{q}=\left[m_{1}(s)-V\left(m_{2}(q)+2\kappa q^{2}\right)\right],\\[2.84526pt]
&\dot{p}=V\left(m_{2}(q)+\kappa q^{2}\right),\\[2.84526pt]
&\dot{f}=m_{1}(s)+Vm_{2}(q),\end{aligned}\end{cases}\quad\text{where}\quad\begin{cases}m_{1}(s)=\frac{s}{K+s},\\[2.84526pt]
m_{2}(q)=\frac{q}{1+q},\\[2.84526pt]
K=\frac{K_{1}}{s_{0}},L=\frac{s_{0}}{K_{2}},\\[2.84526pt]
V=\frac{V_{2}}{V_{1}},\kappa=\frac{\sigma K_{2}^{2}}{V_{2}},\end{cases}

(5.1)

We will consider the three cases which allow a QSSA: sufficiently large
L
,
V
and
\kappa
.
In the case of large
L
, the left hand side of
q
equation in (
5.1
) features the small parameter
\varepsilon=\tfrac{1}{L}
. By setting
\varepsilon=0
, the
q
equation
reduces to the algebraic equation (
4.3
). The corresponding zero order QSSA model system reads as

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\dot{\tilde{s}}=-m_{1}(\tilde{s})+V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2},\\[2.84526pt]
&\dot{\tilde{p}}=V\left(m_{2}(\tilde{q})+\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}\right),\\[2.84526pt]
&\dot{\tilde{f}}=m_{1}(\tilde{s})+Vm_{2}(\tilde{q}),\end{aligned}\end{cases}\quad\text{where $\tilde{q}$ solves uniquely }\quad\frac{m_{1}(\tilde{s})}{V}=2\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}+m_{2}(\tilde{q}).

(5.2)

We point out that the QSSA
\tilde{q}(\tilde{s}(t))
from (
5.2
) is not the
same as
\tilde{q}(s(t))
with
s(t)
solving the full system (
5.1
), which we have studied in the previous Section
4.1
. However, the feedback from
\tilde{q}(\tilde{s}(t))
to the evolution of
\tilde{s}
in
(
5.2
) is rather weak (recall
\tfrac{m_{1}}{2}\leq-\dot{s}\leq m_{1}
) and was therefore neglected for the sake of clarity
in the derivation the conditions of Proposition
4.2
, which are anyway not sharp.

In the following, we improve the the approximative quality of the zero order QSSA system (
5.2
) by deriving higher order corrections. We will first consider the case of large
L
and later the cases of large
V
and
\kappa
.
We will see that all these cases lead to somewhat different approximative systems and explain the difference in terms of the interpretation of the parameters. In the case of large
V
, we will even provided second order corrections, since
it has been suggested that
V\sim 3
is a realistic value, which would make second order terms necessary.

5.1
First order asymptotic expansion for
L
large

The parameter
L=\tfrac{s_{0}}{K_{2}}
represents to ratio of initial TG substrate to the MM constant of DG hydrolysis.
One could think that
s_{0}
should be significantly larger than a realistic value
K_{2}
, which would be in favour of considering
L
large. However, this is not so clear since ATGL and HSL are assumed bound to the surface of lipid droplets, the TG substrate which is actually available might need to be also near the surface. So effectively, the available
s_{0}
might not be larger than
K_{2}
.

With
\varepsilon=\tfrac{1}{L}
being a small parameter, we rewrite the
q
equation of (
5.1
) as

\varepsilon\dot{q}=m_{1}(s)-Vd(q),\qquad\text{where}\quad d(q)=2\kappa q^{2}+\frac{q}{1+q}.

(5.3)

Inserting the asymptotic expansion
q=q_{0}+\varepsilon q_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
into (
5.3
) and comparing coefficients yields in order

\varepsilon^{0}
yields

m_{1}(s)=Vd(q_{0}),\qquad\Rightarrow\quad q_{0}(t)=\tilde{q}(s(t)),

(5.4)

which is the solution of the algebraic QSSA equation (
4.3
).
In order
\varepsilon^{1}
, we obtain

\dot{q}_{0}=-Vd^{\prime}(q_{0})q_{1},\qquad\Rightarrow\quad q_{1}(t)=-\frac{\dot{\tilde{q}}}{Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q})}=-\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)\dot{s}}{(Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}))^{2}},

(5.5)

where we have calculated
\dot{\tilde{q}}
from differentiating
m_{1}(s)=Vd(\tilde{q})
.
In order to obtain an expression of
q_{1}
in terms of
s
only, we insert the
s
equation of (
5.1
) into
the right hand side of (
5.5
), we have with
\tilde{q}=\tilde{q}(s(t))

q_{1}=-\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)}{(Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}))^{2}}\left(-m_{1}(s)+V\kappa q^{2}\right)=-\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)}{(Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}))^{2}}\left(-m_{1}(s)+V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}\right)+O(\varepsilon).

(5.6)

Hence, we have as first order QSSA

q=\tilde{q}(s(t))-\frac{1}{L}\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)(-m_{1}(s)+V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2})}{(Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}))^{2}}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{L^{2}}\Bigr).

(5.7)

We are now able to derive the first order approximative system.
Inserting eqs. (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) into the
s
equation of full system (
5.1
) yields

\dot{s}=-m_{1}(s)+V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}-2\varepsilon\tilde{q}\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)\dot{s}}{(Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}))^{2}}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

and, therefore

\dot{s}=\left(1-2\varepsilon\tilde{q}\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)}{(Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}))^{2}}\right)\left(-m_{1}(s)+V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}\right)+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(5.8)

Next, we insert eqs. (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) into the
p
equation of (
5.1
):

\dot{p}=V\kappa{q}^{2}+V\frac{q}{1+q}=V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}+V\frac{\tilde{q}}{1+\tilde{q}}+\varepsilon V\left(2\kappa\tilde{q}+\frac{1}{(1+\tilde{q})^{2}}\right)q_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(5.9)

where
q_{1}
is given by (
5.6
).
Finally, inserting eqs. (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) into the
f
equation of (
5.1
) yields

\dot{f}=m_{1}(s)+V\frac{\tilde{q}}{1+\tilde{q}}+\varepsilon V\frac{1}{(1+\tilde{q})^{2}}q_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(5.10)

where
q_{1}
is given by (
5.6
). Altogether, we obtain as first order QSSA model system

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\dot{s}=\left(1-2\varepsilon\tilde{q}\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)}{(Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}))^{2}}\right)\left(-m_{1}(s)+V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}\right),\\[0.0pt]
&\dot{p}=V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2}+V\frac{\tilde{q}}{1+\tilde{q}}+\varepsilon V\left(2\kappa\tilde{q}+\frac{1}{(1+\tilde{q})^{2}}\right)q_{1},\\[0.0pt]
&\dot{f}=m_{1}(s)+V\frac{\tilde{q}}{1+\tilde{q}}+\varepsilon V\frac{1}{(1+\tilde{q})^{2}}q_{1},\end{aligned}\end{cases}\text{where}\ \begin{cases}m_{1}(s)=\frac{s}{K+s},\\[2.84526pt]
\tilde{q}=\tilde{q}(s(t)),\\[2.84526pt]
q_{1}=-\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)(-m_{1}(s)+V\kappa\tilde{q}^{2})}{(Vd^{\prime}(\tilde{q}))^{2}},\\[2.84526pt]
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{L}.\end{cases}

(5.11)

.

In the first order reduced model system (

5.2
First and higher order asymptotic expansion for
V
large

We consider the case
V
large and
V_{2}\gg V_{1}
holds. In this parametric regime, DG hydrolysis, that is the
loss term
m_{2}
in the
q
equation in system (
5.1
), is much faster than the inflow term
m_{1}
from TG hydrolysis. Hence,
q
is expected to be
O(\varepsilon)
and ought to be rescaled accordingly, i.e.
q=\varepsilon Q
with
\varepsilon=1/V
. Hence, we obtain from non-dimensionalised system (
5.1
) the following rescaled model

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\dot{s}=-m_{1}(s)+\varepsilon\kappa Q^{2},\\[2.84526pt]
&\varepsilon\dot{Q}=L\bigl[m_{1}(s)-\bigl(M_{2}(Q)+2\varepsilon\kappa Q^{2}\bigr)\bigr],\\[2.84526pt]
&\dot{p}=m_{2}(Q)+\varepsilon\kappa Q^{2},\\[2.84526pt]
&\dot{f}=m_{1}(s)+M_{2}(Q),\end{aligned}\end{cases}\text{where}\ \begin{cases}m_{1}(s)=\frac{s}{K+s},\\[2.84526pt]
M_{2}(Q)=\frac{Q}{1+\varepsilon Q}=Q-\varepsilon Q^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\\[2.84526pt]
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{V}.\end{cases}

(5.12)

Note that with
q=O(\varepsilon)
, DG hydrolysis is in zero order approximation a linear term (since there is little substrate) and that DG transacylation is a term of order
O(\varepsilon)
. As a consequence, the asymptotic expansion in the case

V
large is simpler and more explicit than in the previous case of
L
large.
We insert the ansatz
Q=Q_{0}+\varepsilon Q_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
into the
Q
equation in (
5.12
)
and compare the coefficients to obtain

Q_{0}(t)=m_{1}(s(t)),\qquad Q_{1}=-\frac{\dot{Q}_{0}}{L}+\left[1-2\kappa\right]Q_{0}^{2}=-\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}}{L}\dot{s}+\left[1-2\kappa\right]m_{1}^{2}(s).

(5.13)

Inserting (
5.13
) into (
5.12
) yields the first order QSSA system

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\dot{s}=-m_{1}(s)+\varepsilon\kappa m_{1}^{2}(s),\\[2.84526pt]
&\dot{p}=m_{1}(s)+\varepsilon\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)m_{1}(s)}{L}-\varepsilon\kappa m_{1}^{2}(s),\\[2.84526pt]
&\dot{f}=2m_{1}(s)+\varepsilon\frac{m_{1}^{\prime}(s)m_{1}(s)}{L}-2\varepsilon\kappa m_{1}^{2}(s),\end{aligned}\end{cases}\quad\text{where}\quad\begin{cases}m_{1}(s)=\frac{s}{K+s},\\[2.84526pt]
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{V}.\end{cases}

(5.14)

Note, that solutions to (
5.14
) conserves total glycerol and total FAs only up to the zero order terms. In first order, the conservation laws do not hold due to the term
\varepsilon m_{1}^{\prime}m_{1}(s)
in the equations for
\dot{p}
and
\dot{f}
, which stems form the first term in
Q_{1}
and is hence proportional to
\dot{s}
and a results of the asymptotic expansion.
The
s
equation in (
5.14
) is scalar like in system (
5.11
) and has the added benefit of not requiring to solve for
\tilde{q}
.

5.3
First order asymptotic expansion of

:
\kappa
large

We consider the case
\kappa

holds and DG transacylation, which is a loss term in the

Like in the case of
V

with
\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{\kappa}
. By accordingly rescaling the non-dimensionalised system (
5.1

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\dot{s}=-m_{1}(s)+VQ^{2},\\[2.84526pt]

6
Conclusion and outlook

Intracellular lipolysis is a central catabolic pathway involved in a variety of cellular processes including energy production, signal transduction, and lipid remodelling. The main lipolytic enzymes (i.e. ATGL, HSL, and MGL) have been functionally characterised in great detail. Over the last decades, gain- and loss-of-function studies in different model organisms corroborated their crucial roles in neutral lipid catabolism
[
4
,
19
]
. Nevertheless, lipolysis is far from being completely understood. We are convinced that the classical view of lipolysis as a linear process involving the consecutive action of ATGL, HSL, and MGL is too simplistic. On the one hand, published data suggest that lipolysis is an enzymatically redundant process (ATGL not only hydrolyses TGs but also DGs
[
27
]
,
vice versa
HSL not only hydrolyses DGs but also TGs and MGs
[
2
]
). On the other hand, three independent studies convincingly showed that ATGL also exhibits DG transacylation activity at least
in vitro

[
10
,
26
,
13
]
, indicating that intracellular lipolysis is a non-linear process. The
in vivo
relevance of DG transacylation is still elusive though.

Kinetic parameters of model system (
2.2
) are currently not available. In particular, the parameters
\kappa
and
V
for DG transacylation and DG hydrolysis are unknown since these two functions can not be distinguished by any current experimental protocol. In a work in progress,
we are developing mathematical approaches to identify kinetic parameters from experimental data using minimisation of tracking functional as well as Bayesian estimators.
First preliminary results of parameter identification (yet for more specific mathematical models than system (
2.2
)) suggest values of
\kappa
and
V
, which put the system dynamics somewhere near the middle of the parametric plots Figs.
4
and

5
. A plausible guess suggests
V
somewhat larger that one and DG transacylation on average maybe 20% of DG hydrolysis, but with a strong dependence on the substrate and an increase up to 50% in some experiments.

For these parameter values, the effects of DG transacylation as shown in Figs.
5
are rather neutral: A slight delay in substrate processing time but negligible effects on the time of MG and FA production compared to the system without DG transacylation.
This might be a reason why some researchers believe that DG transacylation does not play a significant role
in vivo
:
Not because it is not active, but because it behaves rather neutrally.

However, if the hydrolytic machinery is shifted away from these
conditions, DG transacylation will play a more significant role. First, if
DG hydrolysis is lacking, DG transacylation serves as a failsafe mechanism by producing MGs and even indirectly FAs
when TGs formed are hydrolysed further to DGs.
However, this failsafe mechanism comes at a price:
If DG hydrolysis is present, a highly effective DG transacylation
will compete with DG hydrolysis. This will cause downstream delays by forcing the lipolysis cascade TG
\to
DG
\to
MG rather into a semi-open loop of TG
\to
DG
\to
1/2 TG
\to
1/2 DG
\to
1/4 TG etc, see e.g.
Fig
5
and it’s illustration of the downstream delays.

It seems highly remarkable that our current preliminary guess on parameter values places the kinetics of the lipolytic machinery in the middle of these two options: failsafe mechanism versus DG substrate competition. One can speculate that the lipolytic machinery evolved to operate at a flexible state rather than at the maybe more efficient state without DG transacylation. We interpret our observations that there is biological purpose behind both DG hydrolysis and DG transacylation constituting the lipolysis machinery.

As a second biological purpose, we can speculate that various effects of DG transacylation have the ability to homogenise the process of
lipid hydrolysis over the surface of lipid droplets.
We recall that the ratio of DG transacylation to DG hydrolysis depends on the DG concentration levels and that DG transacylation becomes dominate for sufficiently large concentrations of DGs.
The enzyme localisation on the surface of LDs can be temporally and spatially heterogeneous
[
1
,
5
,
21
]
. Local absence of HSL would lead to
local DG accumulation as DGs can’t leave the LD. It can by speculated that DG transacylation may serve as a regulator of spatial heterogeneities of
DG concentrations on the surface of the lipid droplet by processing local surpluses (and without having to wait for diffusion to spread DGs).
Hence, DG transacylation would contribute to an overall more homogeneous and controlled downstream processing,
which is certainly an important physiological factor. However, ATGL-mediated DG transacylation averaged over the entire surface of the lipid droplet cannot fully compensate for complete HSL deficiency
in vivo
, since HSL knockout mice accumulate DGs in adipose and non-adipose tissues
[
7
]
.

There are many potential directions of future research. From a mathematical perspective, a rigorous proof
of the convergence of the fast-slow ODE systems to the limiting QSSA is work in progress.
The proof requires a sufficiently sharp perturbation argument
for nonlinear, non-autonomous ODE systems. Unfortunately, estimations of the fundamental matrix of non-autonomous systems like in
[
22
, Chapter 3]
are currently insufficient, which we strongly consider to be a technical artefact rather than the results of the
system behaviour. As a by-pass, we are currently trying to find a suitable change of variables, which might allow to improve the estimates on
the non-autonomous semigroup.

From a modelling and interdisciplinary perspective, a central goal is the identification of the lipolytic kinetic parameters from experimental data.
In particular, we aim to understand the dynamics of TG breakdown in adipocytes via cell-based assays and mice experiments.
However, we will have to work with a limited number of data points both in adipocyte cell assays and even less in mice experiments. We expect that reduced QSSA models like presented in Section
5
will be important in order to
fit parameter from
in vivo
data.

There is, however, an additional mathematical question concerning the use of QSSAs. As part of the process of
mathematical modelling, a sensitivity analysis of the parameter is an important step.
In particular, we are studying parameter sensitivity derivatives and how they evolve along solutions of the system.
Parameter sensitivity derivatives provided very useful insights into the qualitative behaviour of the system and also into how challenging the inverse problem of identifying parameters from measurements will be.

In the spirit of this paper, which is to discuss the role of DG transacylation, the following system considers the sensitivity derivatives with respect to the parameter
