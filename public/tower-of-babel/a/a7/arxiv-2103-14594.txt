On the Local-Global Conjecture for Commutator Traces
Brooke Logan Ogrodnik

Abstract
We study the trace set of the commutator subgroup of Γ ⁡ ( 2 ) , \Gamma(2), a type of Local-Global problem about thin groups. We determine the local obstructions and then use the correspondence between binary quadratic forms and hyperbolic matrices to find some global obstructions. We then develop a probabilistic argument for the existence of sufficiently large admissible traces by modeling the elements as non-backtracking random walks in a 2-dimensional lattice via their homology class and word length. Finally, we investigate the number of commutators needed to represent matrices in the commutator subgroup. This is done using an algorithm of Goldstein and Turner along with utilizing properties of level k k -Markoff type surfaces. We conjecture that any trace in the commutator subgroup of Γ ⁡ ( 2 ) \Gamma(2) can be represented with either 1 or 2 commutators.

Introduction

The questions addressed in this paper are motivated by a conjecture of McMullen
[
McM
]

Conjecture 1.1
.

The set
\{[\overline{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}}]\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})\mid a_{i}\leq 2\}
has exponential growth as

In 2018, Bourgain and Kontorovich reformulated this conjecture into a Local-Global question about thin (semi) groups
[

]
. Before we show the reformulation, we give the general definition of admissibility and critical exponents.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a sub-semigroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
F
a polynomial with respect to the entries of the matrices such that
F(\Gamma)\subset\mathbb{Z}
. An integer

is defined as

if for all

,
n\in F(\Gamma)\bmod{q}
.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a sub-semigroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}).
Then its
critical exponent
is defined to be the abscissa of convergence of its Poincaré series,
\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert}^{-2s}.

Using the observation that
[\overline{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}}]
is fixed by
\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\cdots\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, the conjecture by McMullen can be reformulated to that of a Local-Global problem in the following way. Let

be a subset of the integers. Define

\Gamma_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left<\begin{pmatrix}a&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\mid a\in A\right>^{+}\cap\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}),

(where
<g_{i}>^{+}
is the semigroup generated by the elements

) choose

, and look at the trace set
\mathscr{T}_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma)\mid\gamma\in\Gamma_{A}\}
(
\mathscr{T}_{A}=F(\Gamma)
). If

then it is easy to see that every

is admissible.

Conjecture 1.4

(Local-Global with Multiplicity
[
BK18
]
)
.

Let
A
be an alphabet for which the critical exponent,

\delta_{A}
, exceeds 1/2. Then the set
\mathscr{T}_{A}
of traces contains every sufficiently large admissible integer. Moreover,
the multiplicity for admissible
t\in[X,2X]
is at least

\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{A}\mid\operatorname{Tr}(M)=t\text{ and }\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert<X\}>X^{2\delta_{A}-1-o(1)}.

Conjecture
1.4
would then imply McMullen’s Conjecture
1.1
(see
[
BK18
]
). Despite this reformulation of the problem, relatively little progress has been made. In this paper we consider a related problem in a particular group that has more structure. The group is defined as follows.

Take
\Gamma(2)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\gamma\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\mid\gamma\equiv I\bmod{2}\}
, the level-2 principal congruence subgroup of the modular group
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and let
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
be its commutator subgroup. Our main objective, analogous to McMullen’s conjecture, is to study the trace set of
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}.
This is interesting since the trace is related to the length of closed geodesics in the trivial homology. First, we determine the admissible values.

Theorem 1.5

(Admissibility Theorem)
.

The set of admissible values for the trace set of
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
are all
t
such that
t\bmod{256}\in\{2,18,66,146\}
and
t\bmod{9}\in\{0,2,3,6,7\}.

Conjecture 1.6
.

For all sufficiently large admissible
t
, there exists
\gamma\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
such that
\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma)=t.

This conjecture asks for less than the what Conjecture
1.4
would ask in the analogous setting since we are only asking if matrices with trace
t
exist, and not about multiplicity. We are not able to fully solve this and so in these pages we will prove some theorems about the structure of the group and give both empirical and probabilistic arguments toward the conjecture.

It is not even known that a positive proportion of the traces arise. One’s first instinct may be to use the relationships between level structures of the Markoff surface and 1-commutators, elements of the form
[X,Y]
with
X,Y\in\Gamma(2)
(see Section
7
), as studied by Ghosh and Sarnak
[
GS17
]
. That is, perhaps the traces of 1-commutators already produce a positive proportion. However, this direction seems just as, if not more, difficult since one would need to exhibit matrices in
\Gamma(2)
with very special properties. This question about 1-commutators then opens an auxiliary direction of inquiry where one asks, for
\gamma
in the commutator subgroup of
\Gamma(2)^{\prime},
what is the commutator width of
\gamma
? I.e. the minimal number of commutators needed to represent
\gamma.
There are cases of different groups where such a question is undecidable
[
BBN59
]
, however Wicks showed that it is possible to compute the width in free groups
[
Wic62
]
. We use the algorithm of Goldstein and Turner
[
GT79
]
to do so effectively and study the distribution of widths which lead to the formulation of Conjecture
1.14
.

Another question one might ask is, are there admissible values that do not arise as traces? We explain a constructive algorithm (Section
5
) that will tell us if a given value is or is not a trace of a matrix in the commutator subgroup of
\Gamma(2)
modeling Gauss’s reduction theory of Binary Quadratic Forms. In doing this, we identify what the failures are in this context and it seems reasonable to believe that these are all of them.

Lemma 1.7
.

The only admissible values,
t
, such that
|t|<10^{6}
and
t
is not the trace of an element in
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
is when
t\in\{-1006,-1726,-2558,-4718\}.

Conjecture 1.8
.

The only admissible values,
t
, such that
t
is not the trace of an element in
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
is when
t\in\{-1006,-1726,-2558,-4718\}.

Note that while lengths of closed geodesics correspond to
\pm\operatorname{Tr}(g),
the positive values of these local-global failures are not admissible since in our group if

is admissible,
-t
is not.

In Section

we model representatives of the
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-conjugacy classes of

Preliminaries

In the previous section we defined
H=<\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}>
and its commutator subgroup,
H^{\prime}
. The following lemma contains statements that were either directly or indirectly mentioned already and the proofs are elementary and thus omitted.

Lemma 2.1
.

The following hold:

1.

H
and
H^{\prime}
are free groups

2.

H/H^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}

3.

H^{\prime}
is an infinitely generated group

4.

H^{\prime}=\left<[\mathcal{A}^{m},\mathcal{B}^{n}]\mid n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\right>.

5.

For all
g\in H,
there exists
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
h\in H^{\prime}
such that
g=\mathcal{A}^{m}\mathcal{B}^{n}h

6.

\Gamma(2)=\pm H
and
H^{\prime}=\Gamma(2)^{\prime}.

Take
g=\mathcal{A}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{A}^{m_{k}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{k}}\in H
where

. Then a

will be defined as the following set of points:

\left\{(0,0),(m_{1},0),(m_{1},n_{1}),(m_{1}+m_{2},n_{1}),\ldots,\left(\sum m_{i},\sum n_{i}\right)\right\}.

Then let
\pi(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\sum m_{i},\sum n_{i})
be the homology class that

falls in.

Definition 2.2
.

Take
g\in H
with
g=\mathcal{A}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{A}^{m_{k}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{k}}
where
m_{i},n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
m_{1}
and
n_{k}
are the only values allowed to be equal to 0. Then the

is
\ell(g)=\sum|m_{i}|+|n_{i}|.
We further define the
narrow length
for

to be
\ell_{N}(g)=k.

Definition 2.3
.

For any element
g\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
,

can be expressed as a product of
k
-commutators,
g=[g_{1},h_{1}]\cdots[g_{k},h_{k}]
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}.
The least such

is called the

of

.

Clearly,
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\triangleleft\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
since
\Gamma(2)\triangleleft\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}).
Thus for any
\Gamma

\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\leq\Gamma\leq\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\triangleleft\Gamma.

Lemma 2.4
.

\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\triangleleft L\triangleleft\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and furthermore,
g\in L
implies
\operatorname{Tr}(g)\equiv 2\bmod{16}.

Proof.

We can easily calculate the group
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{8}
. We know that
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
is generated by
[\mathcal{A}^{m},\mathcal{B}^{n}]
and since we are looking modulo 8 we just need to check what these generators are when

and we find that it is
\{I,5I\}
. Thus
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\leq L
and a normal subgroup by the previous statement. One can show that
g\in\Gamma(4)
implies that
\operatorname{Tr}(g)\equiv 2\bmod{16}
and, since
L\subset\Gamma(4)
, the same holds in

Furthermore
L\triangleleft\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
given that
L
is a congruence subgroup.
∎

This lemma gives us the first insight to the local obstructions of the trace set, i.e. that any trace in the commutator subgroup must, at the very least, be congruent to 2 modulo 16.

2.1
Local-Global

A result of Epstein
[
Eps87
]
(see also Sharp
[
Sha04
]
), which extended the work of Phillips and Sarnak
[
PS87
]
, gives us the following (in crude form) for
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
:

Theorem 2.5
.

The critical exponent for
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
is
\delta=1.
Equivalently,

\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\mid\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert<X\}=X^{2+o(1)}

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is any Archimedian norm.

Given that
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
is the commutator subgroup of
H
, we know it is an infinite index subgroup of
\Gamma(2)
(by Lemma
2.1
) and thus an infinite index subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and is also Zariski dense (Corollary
3.3
), so it is
thin
.

Definition 2.6
.

An integer
n
is
admissible
if for all
q\geq 1
,
n\in\operatorname{Tr}(\Gamma(2)^{\prime})\bmod{q}
.

We will now state another version of the Local-Global conjecture as well as a lemma demonstrating the bounds that we get for free due to the structure of the group. Similar to Conjecture
1.4
, we define the multiplicity as
\operatorname{mult}_{X}(t)=\{\gamma\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\mid\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert<X\textit{ and has trace }t\}.

Conjecture 2.7
.

If
t\asymp X
is admissible then,
\operatorname{mult}_{X}(t)>X^{1-o(1)}.

Almost for free, one gets just shy of positive proportion of traces as seen in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

\#\{|t|<X\mid\exists\gamma\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime},\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert<X,\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma)=t\}>X^{1-o(1)}.

Proof.

First we note that
\operatorname{mult}_{X}(t)\leq X^{1+o(1)}.
This comes from the fact that in order to construct
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
, there are
\ll X
choices for
a
, and once we choose
a
, the value
d=t-a
is determined. Finally there are
X^{o(1)}
possibilities for
b
and
c
as divisors of
ad-1
.

Given Theorem
2.5
, we know that

\sum_{|t|<X}\operatorname{mult}_{X}(t)=X^{2+o(1)}

and combined with the multiplicity bound above, we use Cauchy-Schwarz to see that

\displaystyle X^{2+o(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|t|<X}\operatorname{mult}_{X}(t)\cdot 1_{\operatorname{mult}_{X}(t)\neq 0}

And so the number of distinct traces less than
X
is greater than
X^{1-o(1)}
.
∎

We will note a few lemmas that will help in proving what

The first lemma is a consequence of Hensel’s Lemma and the argument is standard.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
k

-I\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(k)^{\prime}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
(k,2)=1,

\operatorname{SL}_{2}(k)=\Gamma(2)\bmod{k}
and
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(k)^{\prime}=\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{k}
.

Proof.

First, we note that
\Gamma(2)\leq\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. So what remains to be shown is
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(k)\leq\Gamma(2)\bmod{k}
. Now by assumption
k
is not divisible by 2. Thus there exists
\alpha
such that
2\alpha\equiv 1\bmod{k}
. This easily gives us the next two congruences,
C\equiv\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}\bmod{k}\text{ and }D\equiv\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}\bmod{k}.
Since
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is generated by
C,D,-I
and we just saw that
C,D,-I\in\Gamma(2)\mod k
we get that
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(k)=\Gamma(2)\mod k
. Thus
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(k)^{\prime}=\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\mod k
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

For
(k,6)=1,

C,D\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(k)^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
q\equiv-3\bmod{k}
,
u\equiv q^{-1}\bmod{k}
,
n\equiv 1-u\bmod{k}
, and
m\equiv-2n^{-1}\bmod{k}
. First, we should justify that
u
and
m
actually exist. Given that
k
is not divisible by 3 we know that
q
is invertible and thus
u
exists. Since
(k,2)=1
, there exists a

such that

which gives us
(1-u)3t\equiv 3t+t(-3)u\equiv 3t+t(-3)q^{-1}\equiv 4t\equiv 1\bmod{k}
and so

is also invertible which gives us that

Next, we take a specific element in the commutator subgroup and replace

and

[C^{n},D^{m}][C^{u},D^{q}]\equiv[C^{n},D^{-2n^{-1}}][C^{q^{-1}},D^{q}]\equiv\begin{pmatrix}9+2nq&-3q^{-1}\\
12n^{-1}+3q&-4(nq)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\bmod{k}.

Modular arithmetic gives us then that
[C^{n},D^{m}][C^{u},D^{q}]\equiv C\bmod{k}
. For the same reason we see that
D=C^{T}\equiv([C^{n},D^{m}][C^{u},D^{q}])^{T}=[C^{-q},D^{-u}][C^{-m},D^{-n}].
And so

∎

By Lemma

Indeed, call the left-hand set

Write

c_{1}&d_{1}\end{pmatrix}+p^{l}\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&b_{2}\\
c_{2}&d_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bmod{p^{l+1}}.

Let

be such that

Then the set of possible values of

is a three dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}

given that

must satisfy

Proof that
1\Rightarrow 2

\displaystyle|\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{p^{l+1}}|

\displaystyle=\#\{\gamma\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p^{l+1})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\gamma\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{p^{n_{p}}}\}\textit{ by Assuming 1}

\displaystyle=p^{3}\cdot\#\{\gamma\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p^{l})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\gamma\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{p^{n_{p}}}\}\textit{ by Fact (ii)}

\displaystyle=p^{3}\cdot|\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{p^{l}}|\textit{ by Assuming 1 again}

Proof that
2\Rightarrow 1
This statement is equivalent to showing that the size of the two groups are the same since Fact (i) told us the one is a subgroup of the other. We will prove this via induction. For
l=n_{p},
clearly 1 holds by definition. Now assume that it holds for some

Then,

\displaystyle|\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{p^{l+1}}|

\displaystyle=p^{3}\cdot|\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{p^{l}}|\textit{ by Assuming 2}

\displaystyle=p^{3}\cdot\#\{\gamma\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p^{l})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\gamma\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{p^{n_{p}}}\}\textit{ by inductive step}

\displaystyle=\#\{\gamma\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(p^{l+1})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\gamma\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{p^{n_{p}}}\}\textit{ by Fact (ii)}.

∎

First, we will state and some general group theoretic properties (that together will be similar to the classic Goursat’s Lemma) which will help in piecing the previous three portions of this section together. For the remainder of this section, given a subgroup of

.

Let

Lemma 3.16
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\{(\gamma\bmod{2^{m}3^{n}},\gamma\bmod{k})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\gamma\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}\bmod{2^{m}3^{n}k}\}

\displaystyle K

(1)

5.1
Algorithm for Existence

We will first briefly recall some definitions and properties of Binary Quadratic Forms so that we might use the correspondence between them and hyperbolic matrices to find representatives of matrices with trace
t
from each
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
conjugacy class. These definitions come mainly from
Applications of Thin Orbits

[
Kon16
]
and Section 8 of
Zetafunktionen und quadratische Körper

[
Zag81
]
.

A binary quadratic form is
f(x,y)=Ax^{2}+Bxy+Cy^{2}
with
A,B,C\in\mathbb{Z}
which we can also write in shorthand notation as
[A,B,C].
A binary quadratic form’s
discriminant
is
D=B^{2}-4AC
.

Definition 5.3
.

Two binary quadratic forms
f
and
g
are
(narrowly) equivalent
if there exists
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
ad-bc=1
and
g(x,y)=f(ax+by,cx+dy).

A root of a binary quadratic form
f(x,1)=Ax^{2}+Bx+C
with
A\neq 0
and discriminant
D
is
\theta_{f}=\frac{-B+\sqrt{D}}{2A}
when
A\neq 0
. We look at the continued fraction expansion of
\theta_{f}
such that
-1<\overline{\theta_{f}}<0
and
1<\theta_{f}
, the continued fraction expansion is

[\overline{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}}]=a_{1}+\frac{1}{a_{2}+\frac{1}{\cdots+\frac{1}{a_{k}+\frac{1}{\theta_{f}}}}}=\theta_{f}.

Now let
f
and
g
be (narrowly) equivalent binary quadratic forms and
\theta_{f}
and
\theta_{g}
be their roots. Then we know there exists
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
f(ax+by,cx+dy)=g(x,y).
Note that
\gamma^{-1}\theta_{f}=\theta_{g}.

It is for this reason that we many times judge if two binary quadratic forms are (narrowly) equivalent via looking at their continued fraction expansion and, furthermore, we just need to know if the continued fractional expansion of
\theta_{f}
is that of
\theta_{g}
after some even number of shifts i.e. are equivalent with respect to
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}).

Definition 5.4
.

We call a binary quadratic form
[A,B,C]

primitive
if
\gcd(A,B,C)=1.

Definition 5.5
.

We define
the narrow class number of
D
,
h_{n}(D)
, to be the number of (narrowly) inequivalent primitive binary quadratic forms of discriminant
D
.

One can use Gauss’s reduction algorithm to construct representatives of the equivalency classes of binary quadratic forms. However, this algorithm may find multiple representatives of the same class. So this algorithm gives us a finite list of binary quatric forms but it is only an upper bound for the (narrow) class number. Thus, there is one last step to ensure that the binary quadratic forms are in different classes. This is done via computing the continued fraction expansion of
\theta_{f}
where
f
ranges over the different forms found in Gauss’s Algorithm. If one wanted to find the (narrow) class number, they would count the remaining forms that are also primitive. We then use this class number,
h_{n}(D)
, to answer questions about the traces in
L
and
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
t\neq 2
be congruent to
2\bmod{16}
and
D=(t^{2}-4)/64
. Then there is a bijection between binary quadratic forms of
[A,B,C]
with discriminant
D
and hyperbolic matrices with fixed trace
t
in
L
.

Proof.

Let
t\equiv 2\bmod{16}
and
D>0
such that
t^{2}-64D=4.
Now take,
[A,B,C]
, a binary quadratic form (not necessarily primitive) of discriminant
D
. Then we can define the following map:

[A,B,C]\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\frac{t-8B}{2}&-8C\\
8A&\frac{t+8B}{2}\end{pmatrix}\in L.

If
\begin{pmatrix}4a+1&8b\\
8c&4d+1\end{pmatrix}\in L,
then
[c,(d-a)/2,-b]\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\frac{t-4(d-a)}{2}&8b\\
8c&\frac{t+4(d-a)}{2}\end{pmatrix}.
However note that,

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-a)^{2}}{4}+4bc=\frac{(4(a+d)+2)^{2}-4}{64}.

Thus
t=4(a+d)+2
which means that
\frac{t-4(d-a)}{2}=4a+1
and
\frac{t+4(d-a)}{2}=4d+1
and so the defined mapping is onto.
∎

Lemma 6.3

For

6.2
Choosing “Good” Representatives

Now that we know these properties of word length and homology we will extend them to conjugacy classes.

Definition 6.10
.

For
g\in L

\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
conjugacy class and its

\ell([g])=\min_{h\in[g]}\ell(h)\textit{ and }\ell([g]_{H})=\min_{h\in[g]_{H}}\ell(h).

Notice here that for any

\ell([g]_{H})=\ell(h)
via Corollary
6.5
and also, by definition,
\ell([g])\leq\ell([g]_{H}).

Lemma 6.11
.

If
\ell(g)=\ell([g]),
then
\ell(g^{t})=\ell([g^{t}]).

Proof.

Suppose not, that is suppose there exists
h\in[g^{t}]
such that
\ell(h)<\ell(g^{t}).
Then, since

for some
\gamma\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}),

h^{t}=(\gamma^{-1})^{t}g\gamma^{t}
which implies that
h^{t}\in[g],
but then we get a contradiction since, by Lemma
6.3
,
\ell(g)\leq\ell(h^{t})=\ell(h)<\ell(g^{t}).

∎

The idea is now to modify the representatives of the conjugacy classes (produced by the algorithm described in Section
5.1
) to force the following three conditions on the representatives chosen:

1.

g\in\Delta\cap L

2.

\ell(g)=\ell([g]),

3.

If
g
is a representative of the conjugacy class and
g^{t}\not\in[g],
then
g^{t}
is the chosen representative for
[g^{t}]
.

We then define

is this “good” representative (in the sense that we get from the modified list of representatives). This representation is chosen so that the word length is as short as possible. Later on in this section, and the next, we will observe that having a small word length is helpful and grouping elements in terms of their
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
conjugacy classes accomplishes this.

Let

and

Then, we can define a mapping,

\Delta,

We don’t choose to do this even though

If

then for some

By definition of

Note that

So Corollary

∎

6.4
Looking at a random walk

For
g\in\Delta
where
g=\mathcal{A}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{A}^{m_{k}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{k}}
, a walk is the set of points,

\left\{(0,0),(m_{1},0),(m_{1},n_{1}),(m_{1}+m_{2},n_{1}),\ldots,\left(\sum m_{i},\sum n_{i}\right)\right\}.

Then the walk ends at
\pi(g)
and the length of the walk is
\ell(g)
(see Figure
8
). It is for this reason that we can model our set of hyperbolic conjugacy classes as non-backtracking random walks in the two dimensional lattice and look for when walks end at the origin (points such that
\pi(g)=(0,0)
and are thus in
H^{\prime}
).

Figure 8:
The walk on the left is for the matrix
\mathcal{A}^{1}\mathcal{B}^{3}\mathcal{A}^{2}\mathcal{B}^{2}\mathcal{A}^{3}\mathcal{B}^{1}.
The walk on the right hand side is for the matrix
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}^{2}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{B}^{-2}\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{B}^{-1}.

Now we will look at the probability of a non-backtracking random walk in 2 dimensions ending at the origin. We noted that the
h(t)
representatives of hyperbolic conjugacy classes appear to have an average word length on the order of
\log(|t|)
. It is known that the probability of a random walk on a 2d lattice of length
2N
ending at the origin is asymptotically
(\pi N)^{-1}.
This calculation can be done by first noting that the total number of walks is
4^{2N}
and the number of walks that end at the origin is
\sum\binom{2N}{2k}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2N-2k}{N-k}.
The latter value comes from noticing that, in order to return to the origin, the total amount of times one travels up is equal to the total number of times one travels down and the total number of times one travels left must equal the times traveling right. Using identities of binomial coefficients this probability reduces to
\frac{\binom{2N}{N}^{2}}{4^{2N}}
and then Stirling’s Approximation gives the asymptotic
(\pi N)^{-1}.
The probability of a non-backtracking walk of length
2N
ending at the origin is then also asymptotically
(c\pi N)^{-1}
for some
c

[
OEIS
]
. If the model accurately predicts the homology class, then for large enough admissible
t
, the size of
h^{\prime}(t)
should be of the order
h(t)/\log(|t|)
.

7
Commutator Width

We might ask ourselves if
g\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
can it be written as a 1-commutator. We can further generalize this question to ask, what is the minimal number of commutators needed to write
g\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}.
We call this value the commutator width of
g
. Given that
\gamma[h,k]\gamma^{-1}=[\gamma h\gamma^{-1},\gamma k\gamma^{-1}],
it is clear that
g
has the same width as any other element in its
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
conjugacy class. So the question reduces, as usual, to answering the question in
\Delta\cap\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
.

For certain
g
, this question is not hard to answer as can be seen in Lemma
7.1
below.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
g\in\Delta\cap\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
and let
g
have narrow length equal to 2 or 3. Then
g
and
g^{-1}
can both be written as a 1-commutator in
\Gamma(2)^{\prime}.

Proof.

Suppose
g
has narrow length 2. Then, let

g=\mathcal{A}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{1}}\mathcal{A}^{m_{2}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{2}}=\mathcal{A}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{1}}\mathcal{A}^{-m_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{-n_{1}}=[\mathcal{A}^{m_{1}},\mathcal{B}^{n_{1}}].

Suppose instead that
g
has narrow length 3. Then,

g=\mathcal{A}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{1}}\mathcal{A}^{m_{2}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{2}}\mathcal{A}^{m_{3}}\mathcal{B}^{n_{3}}=[\mathcal{A}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{B}^{-n_{2}},\mathcal{B}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\mathcal{A}^{m_{2}}].

Since
g
must be a 1-commutator,
g=[X,Y]
for some
X,Y\in H
which then implies that
g^{-1}
is also a 1-commutator given that
g^{-1}=[X,Y]^{-1}=[Y,X].

∎

But the commutator widths are not always this easy to see.

.

For an admissible value,

7.2
Connection to the Markoff Equation

Now, one might ask why we are interested in the commutator width. This interest stemmed from the fact that there is a relationship between 1-commutators and solutions to the level
k
Markoff-type equations. We will change the wording of some of the theorems stated in Ghosh and Sarnak’s paper to fit our purposes. For more information on this topic we recommend
[
GS17
]
.

Take the Markoff-type equation for fixed
k,

x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}=k
and let
M_{k}
be the set of integer solutions to this equation. Such things tie together with 1-commutators using Fricke’s Identity:

\operatorname{Tr}(A)^{2}+\operatorname{Tr}(B)^{2}+\operatorname{Tr}(AB)^{2}-\operatorname{Tr}(A)\operatorname{Tr}(B)\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=\operatorname{Tr}([A,B])+2.

(5)

We already know that not all of the commutators are 1-commutators (see the summary of the search results) and the algorithm discussed in the previous section by Goldstein and Turner
[
GT79
]
appears to find a representative pretty quickly with commutator width 2 (which was the inspiration for Conjecture
7.4
). The goal of this section is to be able to improve the algorithm by using facts about level
k
Markoff-type solutions to speed up differentiating between 1 and greater than 1 commutator widths.

Definition 7.5
.

A
Vieta Involution
of
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
is defined to be
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}x_{2}-x_{3}).

Definition 7.6
.

An element
x
is defined to be
\hat{\Gamma}
-equivalent
to
u
if a combination of permutations, flipping 2 signs and Vieta Involutions on
x
gives
u
.

Lemma 7.7

(see
[
GS17
]
)
.

Let
(a,b,c)
be an integer solution to the level
k
Markoff-type equation,
x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}=k,
and suppose there exists matrices
A,B\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\operatorname{Tr}(A)=a,

\operatorname{Tr}(B)=b,
and
\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=c.
Then any
\hat{\Gamma}
-equivalent solution,
(x,y,z)
, to
(a,b,c)
also has matrices in
X,Y\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(Z)
such that
\operatorname{Tr}(X)=x
,
\operatorname{Tr}(Y)=y
,
\operatorname{Tr}(XY)=z
and
[X,Y]
is in the same conjugacy class as either
[A,B]
or
[A,B]^{-1}.

Proof.

First we take another one of Fricke’s identities for
A,B\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
:

\operatorname{Tr}(A)\operatorname{Tr}(B)=\operatorname{Tr}(AB)+\operatorname{Tr}(AB^{-1}).

Let
(a,b,c)\in M_{k}
and
A,B\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\operatorname{Tr}(A)=a
,
\operatorname{Tr}(B)=b
and
\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=c
. Therefore we can think of
(a,b,c)
as being mapped to
[A,B].
We can see that any
\hat{\Gamma}
- equivalent solution can be mapped to either a matrix in the conjugacy class of
[A,B]
or its inverse via the following calculations:

\displaystyle(b,a,c)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{Tr}(B),\operatorname{Tr}(A),\operatorname{Tr}(AB))=(\operatorname{Tr}(B),\operatorname{Tr}(A),\operatorname{Tr}(BA))\mapsto[B,A]=[A,B]^{-1}

\displaystyle(a,c,b)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{Tr}(A),\operatorname{Tr}(AB),\operatorname{Tr}(B))=(\operatorname{Tr}(A^{-1}),\operatorname{Tr}(AB),\operatorname{Tr}(B))\mapsto[A^{-1},AB]=[A,B]^{-1}

\displaystyle(-a,b,-c)

\displaystyle=(-\operatorname{Tr}(A),\operatorname{Tr}(B),-\operatorname{Tr}(AB))\mapsto[-A,B]=[A,B]

\displaystyle(a,b,a\cdot b-c)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{Tr}(A),\operatorname{Tr}(B),\operatorname{Tr}(A)\operatorname{Tr}(B)-\operatorname{Tr}(AB))=(\operatorname{Tr}(A),\operatorname{Tr}(B^{-1}),\operatorname{Tr}(AB^{-1}))

\displaystyle\mapsto[A,B^{-1}]=B^{-1}[A,B]^{-1}B.

∎

The previous lemma is true for all of

. For

Lemma 7.12
.

Let
g\in\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
with
\operatorname{Tr}(g)=t
and
t\neq 16a^{2}+2
. Then
g
is a 1-commutator if only if there exists matrices
A,B\in H
such that
[A,B]=g
or
[A,B]^{-1}=g
and

(\operatorname{sgn}(-t)\operatorname{Tr}(A),\operatorname{Tr}(B),\operatorname{Tr}(AB))\in\mathcal{F}_{t+2}^{\operatorname{sgn}(t)}(\mathbb{Z}).

Proof.

The one direction of this proof is obvious. Now suppose
g
is a 1-commutator. Then there exists matrices
X,Y\in H
such that
[X,Y]=g.
By Fricke’s Identity (
5
), we may conclude that
(\operatorname{Tr}(X),\operatorname{Tr}(Y),\operatorname{Tr}(XY))\in M_{t+2}
and Theorem
7.11
gives us the existence of some
(a,b,c)
such that
(\operatorname{sgn}(-t)a,b,c)\in\mathcal{F}_{t+2}^{\operatorname{sgn}(t)}(\mathbb{Z})
and
(a,b,c)
is
\hat{\Gamma}
-equivalent to
(\operatorname{Tr}(X),\operatorname{Tr}(Y),\operatorname{Tr}(XY)).
By Corollary
7.9
we know that there then exists
\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in H
such that
\operatorname{Tr}(\tilde{X})=a,\operatorname{Tr}(\tilde{Y})=b,\operatorname{Tr}(\tilde{X}\tilde{Y})=c.
Furthermore there exists
h\in H
such that either
[\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}]=h[X,Y]h^{-1}
or
[\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}]=h[X,Y]^{-1}h^{-1}.
Letting
[A,B]=h^{-1}[\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}]h,
the lemma is completed.
∎

This lemma will help us in two ways. First, when asking what the commutator width of
g\in\Delta\cap\Gamma(2)^{\prime}
with
t=\operatorname{Tr}(g)
and
t
not of the form
16a^{2}+2
, we find
\mathcal{F}_{t+2}^{\operatorname{sgn}(t)}(\mathbb{Z}).
If it is empty, the smallest width it can have is two and thus we can abort Goldstein and Turner’s algorithm if we find a representative that has commutator width 2 given that two is now the best case scenario. Secondly, if
\ell(g)
is large (and we know that it has a lower bound of

and either

If Mathematica found a solution, then
g
has width 1 and if it proves that there is no solution, then the smallest the commutator width can be is two.
