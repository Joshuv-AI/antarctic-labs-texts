Contrastive Language Video Time Pre-training
Hengyue Liu, Kyle Min, Hector A. Valdez, Subarna Tripathi

Abstract
We introduce L A V I T I , a novel approach to learning language, video, and temporal representations in long-form videos via contrastive learning. Different from pre-training on video-text pairs like EgoVLP, L A V I T I aims to align language, video, and temporal features by extracting meaningful moments in untrimmed videos. Our model employs a set of learnable moment queries to decode clip-level visual, language, and temporal features. In addition to vision and language alignment, we introduce relative temporal embeddings (TE) to represent timestamps in videos, which enables contrastive learning of time. Significantly different from traditional approaches, the prediction of a particular timestamp is transformed by computing the similarity score between the predicted TE and all TEs. Furthermore, existing approaches for video understanding are mainly designed for short videos due to high computational complexity and memory footprint. Our method can be trained on the Ego4D dataset with only 8 NVIDIA RTX-3090 GPUs in a day. We validated our method on CharadesEgo action recognition, achieving state-of-the-art results.

3.1
Feature Extraction

Different from spatio-temporal transformers
Bertasius et al. 2021
, which can only utilize a fixed number of frames, our method adopts a post-temporal information injection strategy to enable long-form temporal reasoning.
We use the CLIP vision encoder,
e.g
.
, ViT
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
, to extract the
[cls]
token from each frame independently.
The visual features of a video can be represented as a sequence of visual vectors
{\mathbf{V}}=\{{\textnormal{v}}_{1},{\textnormal{v}}_{2},\dots,{\textnormal{v}}_{T}\}
,
{\textnormal{v}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C}
, where
T
is the number of frames in the video, and
C
is the channel dimension of the frame features.
We then save the extracted frame features to storage, so that we can perform off-line pre-training without accessing video data.
This significantly reduces the computation burden, and we can train on whole untrimmed videos rather than short-term video sequences.
We then employ a non-overlap 1D convolution on
{\mathbf{V}}
to generate
T^{\prime}
number of visual tokens with a feature dimension of
d
, denoted by
{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{T^{\prime}\times d}
.
For the pre-training purpose, the frozen text encoder is used to create the language embeddings of all narrations by extracting the
[eos]
tokens.
Given
M
number of narrations in a video, we extract a set of language vectors
{\mathbf{L}}=[{\textnormal{l}}_{1},{\textnormal{l}}_{2},\dots,{\textnormal{l}}_{M}]
,
{\textnormal{l}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C}
, and each vector is L2-normalized.

3.2
Temporal Embedding and Encoder

To inject temporal information, we create learnable temporal embeddings
{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{0}\times d}
, representing all the timestamps of a video.

{\mathbf{T}}
is initialized as 1D positional embeddings following
Vaswani et al. 2017
, where the cosine similarity between adjacent embeddings is larger distant ones.
As
T^{\prime}\neq T_{0}
, we interpolate
{\mathbf{T}}
to the same length of
T^{\prime}
as
{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}
, where each embedding can represent a particular timestamp relatively to the video length.
The visual tokens
{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}
are added with temporal embeddings
{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}
as the video features
{\mathbf{M}}={\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}
, which is fed into a standard transformer encoder.

3.3
Decoder

Instead of aligning a video clip
[cls]
token with the corresponding text embeddings of the description of the clip (
e.g
.
, a narration), we view video-language pre-training on untrimmed videos as a direct set prediction problem.
Following DETR
Carion et al. 2020
, we create fixed-size learnable queries
{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
, where the video features
{\mathbf{M}}
serve as keys and values to the decoder.
Denote the output embeddings of the decoder as
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}
, they are projected and L2-normalized into visual embeddings
{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}
and temporal embeddings
{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2\times d}
via feed-forward networks (FFNs), respectively.
The output of the decoder is denoted by
{\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime}=\{({\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}_{i},{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}_{s_{i}},{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}_{e_{i}})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where temporal embeddings corresponded to the start and end timestamps
(s_{i},e_{i})
of detected moments.

3.4
Match and Alignment

Since the narrations in the Ego4D dataset are annotated with a single timestamp rather than an interval, we augment each narration with a start and end timestamp.
Different from EgoVLP, a narration’s start (or end) timestamp is determined by its previous and later narrations.
For a narration with timestamp
t_{j}
, we uniformly sample a start and end timestamp
(s_{j},e_{j})
as

\displaystyle s_{j}=\texttt{Uniform}(t_{j-1},t_{j})

(1)

\displaystyle e_{j}=\texttt{Uniform}(t_{j},t_{j+1}).

For each narration, we can sample its corresponding temporal embeddings
{\mathbf{T}}_{s_{j}}
and
{\mathbf{T}}_{e_{j}}
.
Let the ground truth moments be
{\mathbf{Y}}=\{({\mathbf{L}}_{j},{\mathbf{T}}_{s_{j}},{\mathbf{T}}_{e_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{M}
, we perform the bipartite matching between
{\mathbf{Y}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime}
via Hungarian algorithm.
Concretely, we compute 3 pairwise cosine similarities
<\{{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},\{{\mathbf{L}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}>
,
<\{{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}_{s_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N},\{{\mathbf{T}}_{s_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{M}>
, and
<\{{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}_{e_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N},\{{\mathbf{L}}_{e_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{M}>
.
Each similarity matrix is followed by the Sigmoid activation, which allows for multi-label matching as there exist the same narrations or timestamps within videos.
The final cost is the negation of the element-wise product of the 3 similarity matrices.
After finding the matched predictions and groundtruth, following SigLIP
Zhai et al. 2023
, we use Sigmoid contrastive loss to align language, vision and time of moments.
For unmatched predictions, we push their similarities with groundtruth to be -1 (equivalent to all labels of 0 in binary cross-entropy).

Experiments

4.1
Implementation Details

We use the Ego4D
Grauman et al. 2022
dataset for pre-training.
Each untrimmed video is divided into chunks of 600 seconds following
Lin et al. 2022
for efficient data access.
Our codebase is adopted from OpenCLIP
Ilharco, Gabriel and Wortsman, Mitchell and Wightman, Ross and Gordon, Cade and Carlini, Nicholas and Taori, Rohan and Dave, Achal and Shankar, Vaishaal and Namkoong, Hongseok and Miller, John and Hajishirzi, Hannaneh and Farhadi, Ali and Schmidt, Ludwig
and LAVILA
Zhao et al. 2022
.
We use the CLIP vision encoder with ViT-H-14 backbone pre-trained on DFN-5B
Fang et al. 2023
, and standard CLIP text encoder
Radford et al. 2021
.
The visual embeddings are pre-extracted and stored locally with a stride of 5, namely we uniformly sample and compute the embeddings of 6 frames per second.
We train the model with 8 NVIDIA RTX-3090 GPUs for 20 epochs with a batch size of 256, and a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-4}
using the Adam optimizer
Kingma and Ba 2014
.
It takes approximately 20 minutes to train 1 epoch.
The 1D convolution layer has a kernel size of 7 with a stride of 7, and the output number of channels
d=512
.
Both the transformer encoder and decoder has a stack of 6 layers, with 8 attention heads and each head has a feature dimension of 64.

Conclusions

We devise a novel approach to learning language, video, and temporal representations in long-form videos via contrastive learning, termed as
L

A

V

I

T

I

. Unlike existing methods,
L

A
