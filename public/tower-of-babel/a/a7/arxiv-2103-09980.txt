Limit theorems for moment processes of beta Dyson’s Brownian motions and beta Laguerre processes
Fumihiko Nakano, Hoang Dung Trinh, Khanh Duy Trinh

Abstract
In the regime where the parameter beta is proportional to the reciprocal of the system size, it is known that the empirical distribution of Gaussian beta ensembles (resp. beta Laguerre ensembles) converges weakly to a probability measure of associated Hermite polynomials (resp. associated Laguerre polynomials), almost surely. Gaussian fluctuations around the limit have been known as well. This paper aims to study a dynamical version of those results. More precisely, we study beta Dyson’s Brownian motions and beta Laguerre processes and establish LLNs and CLTs for their moment processes in the same regime. Keywords: Dyson’s Brownian motion ; beta Laguerre process ; the moment method ; associated Hermite polynomials ; associated Laguerre polynomials AMS Subject Classification: Primary 60B20 ; Secondary 60H05

Introduction

Gaussian beta ensembles, with a parameter
\beta>0
, are one of the most studied random matrix models. They are generalizations of Gaussian orthogonal/unitary/symplectic ensembles in terms of the joint density of eigenvalues. A nice tridiagonal matrix model for them was constructed in
[
14
]
. Based on that random matrix model, results on the limiting behavior of the empirical distribution, the edge scaling limit, the bulk scaling limit and characteristic polynomials have been established
[
5
,
15
,
17
,
18
,
30
,
34
,
46
]
. It is worth mentioning that the joint density itself is good enough to solve problems such as the convergence to a limit, fluctuations around the limit and large deviation principles
[
3
,
26
,
38
]
.

When studying the limiting behavior of quantities of Gaussian beta ensembles as the system size
N
tends to infinity, the parameter
\beta
is usually assumed to be fixed. The case where
\beta
varies as a function of
N
has been considered recently. For example, in the regime where
\beta N\to 2c
, where
c\in(0,\infty)
is some constant, with probability one, the empirical distribution of the eigenvalues converges weakly to a probability measure of associated Hermite polynomials interpolating a Gaussian distribution and the semi-circle distribution
[
1
,
6
,
32
]
. In the same regime, the bulk scaling limit is a homogeneous Poisson point process
[
6
,
32
,
33
]
and the edge scaling limit is the Gumbel distribution
[
29
]
.

The aim of this paper is to study a dynamical version of the limiting behavior of the empirical distribution in the regime where
\beta N\to 2c\in(0,\infty)
. Let us first introduce related results. We concern with (scaled) Gaussian beta ensembles of ordered eigenvalues whose joint density is of the form

\frac{1}{Z_{N,\beta}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}|^{\beta}\prod_{l=1}^{N}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{l}^{2}}{2}},\quad(\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{N}),

(1)

where
Z_{N,\beta}
is the normalizing constant. Let

L_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}},

be the empirical distribution, where
\delta_{\lambda}
denotes the Dirac measure. Then as
N\to\infty
with
\beta N\to 2c\in(0,\infty)
, the sequence
\{L_{N}\}
converges weakly to a probability measure
\nu_{c}
, almost surely
[
6
,
32
,
43
]
. The density of
\nu_{c}
, for
c>0
, is given by

\nu_{c}(x)=\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}|\hat{f}_{c}(x)|^{2}},\quad\hat{f}_{c}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{c}{\Gamma(c)}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{c-1}e^{-t^{2}/2}e^{ixt}dt.

(2)

We remark here that the family
\nu_{c}
, for
c>-1
, appeared in
[
4
]
as probability measures of associated Hermite polynomials. The convergence means that for any bounded continuous function
f
,

\langle L_{N},f\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\lambda_{i})\to\langle\nu_{c},f\rangle\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty,\quad\text{almost surely.}

Here
\langle\mu,f\rangle
denotes the integral
\int f(x)d\mu(x)
for a probability measure
\mu
and an integrable function
f
. The above still holds when
f
is a continuous function with some growth condition at infinity (e.g. polynomial growth). We refer to those convergences as the law of large numbers (LLN).
Fluctuations around the limit were already established
[
32
,
43
]
. For a continuously differentiable function
f
whose derivative
f^{\prime}
is of polynomial growth, the following central limit theorem (CLT) holds. As
N\to\infty
,

\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle L_{N},f\rangle-{\mathbb{E}}[\langle L_{N},f\rangle]\Big)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{f,c}^{2}),

(3)

where
\sigma_{f,c}^{2}\geq 0
is the limiting variance and ‘
\overset{d}{\to}
’ denotes the convergence in distribution. CLTs are first established for polynomial test functions by a martingale approach based on the tridiagonal random matrix model. Then extending from polynomials to such differentiable functions is done by a standard method in random matrix theory. The limiting variance
\sigma_{f,c}^{2}
when the test function
f(x)=x^{n}
has been calculated by using stochastic analysis
[
39
]
and loop equations
[
22
]
.

Our object in this paper is the so-called beta Dyson’s Brownian motions defined to be the strong solution of the following system of stochastic differential equations (SDEs)

\begin{cases}d\lambda_{i}(t)=db_{i}(t)+\dfrac{\beta}{2}\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}dt,\\
\lambda_{i}(0)=0,\end{cases}i=1,\dots,N,

(4)

together with the constraint that

(\lambda_{1}(t),\lambda_{2}(t),\dots,\lambda_{N}(t))\in\Omega_{G},(t>0).

Here
\{b_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent standard Brownian motions and

\Omega_{G}=\{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}:\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}\}.

For
\beta\geq 1
, the above system of SDEs has a unique strong solution and
\lambda_{1}(t),\dots,\lambda_{N}(t)
never collide for
t>0
(see
[
3
, §4.3]
, for example). Dyson
[
20
]
showed that the eigenvalue process of symmetric (resp. Hermitian) matrix-valued processes of independent standard real (resp. complex) Brownian motions solve the above SDEs with
\beta=1
(resp.
\beta=2
).
When
0<\beta<1
, the above SDEs were treated in
[
11
]
by using multivalued SDEs.
In this case, almost surely, the set of
t
such that
\lambda_{i}(t)=\lambda_{j}(t)
for some
i\neq j
has Lebesgue measure zero. The process
\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}
can also be explained as a type A radial Dunkl process, a Markov process on
\Omega_{G}
with generator

L[f]({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}({\mathbf{x}})+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{j<i}\frac{1}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}\right)({\mathbf{x}}),\quad{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\Omega_{G},

for suitable
f\in C^{2}(\Omega_{G})
. It turns out that (see
[
37
]
) for
t>0
,
\{\lambda_{i}(t)/\sqrt{t}\}
is distributed according to the Gaussian beta ensemble (
1
).

We study the limiting behavior of the empirical measure process

\mu_{t}^{(N)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(t)},

through its moment processes

S_{n}^{(N)}(t)=\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},x^{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(t)^{n},\quad(n=0,1,2,\dots).

For fixed
T>0
, regard
S_{n}^{(N)}
as random elements on the space
C([0,T])
of continuous functions on
[0,T]
endowed with the supremum norm, we establish the LLN and CLT for each moment process in the regime where
\beta N\to 2c
. Precise statements are stated in Theorems
2.1
,
2.7
and
2.11
. Since the Gaussian beta ensemble (
1
) is the distribution of
\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}
at time
t=1
, results on the process level imply the following CLT.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\{p_{n}\}
be associated Hermite polynomials (orthogonal with respect to
\nu_{c}
) which are defined recursively as

\displaystyle p_{0}=1,\quad p_{1}=xp_{0}=x,

\displaystyle p_{n+1}=xp_{n}-(n+c)p_{n-1},\quad(n\geq 1).

Let
P_{n}
be a primitive of
p_{n}
, that is,
P_{n}^{\prime}=p_{n}
. Then as
N\to\infty
with
\beta N=2c
,

\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle L_{N},P_{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},P_{n}\rangle\Big)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{P_{n},c}^{2}),

where

\sigma_{P_{n},c}^{2}=\frac{1}{n+1}\int p_{n}(x)p_{n}(x)d\nu_{c}(x)=\frac{1}{n+1}(c+1)\cdots(c+n).

Moreover, for any
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\{\sqrt{N}(\langle L_{N},P_{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},P_{n}\rangle)\}_{n=0}^{M}
jointly converge in distribution to independent Gaussian random variables.

The above theorem is included in the statement of Theorem
2.11
.
This result provides more information than the CLT (
3
) mentioned above. Such type of CLT statements concerning with orthogonal polynomials has already been known for the three classical beta ensembles (Gaussian beta ensembles, beta Laguerre ensembles and beta Jacobi ensembles) with fixed
\beta

[
10
,
16
]
. This paper uses an idea of choosing orthogonal polynomials from
[
10
]
but detailed arguments are totally different.
We establish analogous results on the Laguerre case as well. The Jacobi case which requires more works will be written in a forthcoming paper.

The paper is organized as follows. LLNs and CLTs for moment processes of the eigenvalue process (
4
) (the Gaussian case) are established in Section 2. Then Section 3 deals with the Laguerre case by using similar arguments.

2
Gaussian case

In this section, we study the limiting behavior of the empirical measure process
\mu_{t}^{(N)}
of beta Dyson’s Brownian motions (
4
) when
\beta=2c/N
, where
c\in(0,\infty)
is a given positive constant. In case
\beta
is fixed, the LLN and CLT for the empirical measure process of more general eigenvalue processes related to beta ensembles have been studied
[
36
,
44
,
45
]
. In our considering regime, the LLN for
\mu_{t}^{(N)}
is a special case of general results in
[
11
]
. However, we are going to re-prove the LLN for
\mu_{t}^{(N)}
by a moment method developed in
[
42
]
in the study of beta Laguerre processes.

For
f=f(t,x)\in C^{2}([0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}})
, we write
\partial_{t}f=\partial f/\partial t,f^{\prime}=\partial f/\partial x
and
f^{\prime\prime}=\partial^{2}f/\partial x^{2}
. Then by Itô’s formula,

\displaystyle d\langle\mu^{(N)}_{t},f\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}df(t,\lambda_{i}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{\prime}(t,\lambda_{i}(t))d\lambda_{i}(t)+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{\prime\prime}(t,\lambda_{i}(t))dt+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{t}f(t,\lambda_{i}(t))dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{\prime}(t,\lambda_{i}(t))db_{i}(t)+\frac{c}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{f^{\prime}(t,\lambda_{i}(t))}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}dt

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}+\partial_{t}f\rangle dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{\prime}(t,\lambda_{i}(t))db_{i}(t)+\frac{c}{2}\iint\frac{f^{\prime}(t,x)-f^{\prime}(t,y)}{x-y}d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(x)d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(y)dt

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}+\partial_{t}f\rangle dt-\frac{c}{2N}\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f^{\prime\prime}\rangle dt.

(5)

To be more precise, the above formula holds when
\lambda_{1}(t),\dots,\lambda_{N}(t)
are all distinct, which occurs almost surely for almost every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The form (
5
) is a starting point to deal with many problems in the study of stochastic processes related to beta ensembles.

2.1
Law of large numbers

Denote by
S_{n}^{(N)}(t)=\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},x^{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(t)^{n}
the
n
th moment process of
\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}
.
The formula (
5
) for
f=x^{n}
reads

\displaystyle dS_{n}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(t)^{n-1}db_{i}(t)+\frac{cn}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}S_{j}^{(N)}(t)S_{n-2-j}^{(N)}(t)dt

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)S_{n-2}^{(N)}(t)dt-\frac{c}{2N}n(n-1)S_{n-2}^{(N)}(t)dt,

or in the integral form

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{i}(u)^{n-1}db_{i}(u)+\frac{cn}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\int_{0}^{t}S_{j}^{(N)}(u)S_{n-2-j}^{(N)}(u)du

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)\int_{0}^{t}S_{n-2}^{(N)}(u)du-\frac{c}{2N}n(n-1)\int_{0}^{t}S_{n-2}^{(N)}(u)du,\quad(n\geq 1).

(6)

Let
T>0
be fixed and let
C([0,T])
be the space of continuous functions on
[0,T]
endowed with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. We regard
S^{(N)}_{n}
as a
C([0,T])
-valued random element. The idea now is to use induction to show the convergence of the
n
th moment process
S_{n}^{(N)}
in the space
C([0,T])
. This idea has been used in
[
42
]
to establish the LLN for the empirical process of beta Laguerre processes. A sequence
\{X^{(N)}\}_{N}
of
C([0,T])
-valued random elements is said to converge in probability to a non-random element
x\in C([0,T])
if for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\|X^{(N)}-x\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon)=0.

We denote the convergence in probability by ‘
\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}
’.
Our result is as follows.

Theorem 2.1
.

There is a probability measure-valued process
(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
such that for any polynomial
p(x)
, as
N\to\infty
,

\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},p\rangle\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}\langle\mu_{t},p\rangle.

The convergence still holds when
f(t,x)
is a polynomial in
t
and
x
, that is, as
N\to\infty
,

\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle.

Here for a function
f(t,x)
of two variables
t
and
x
, the integral
\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle
is taken over
x
. Moreover,
\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle
is differentiable (as a function of
t
) and the following relation holds

\partial_{t}\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle=\frac{c}{2}\iint\frac{f^{\prime}(t,x)-f^{\prime}(t,y)}{x-y}d\mu_{t}(x)d\mu_{t}(y)+\langle\mu_{t},\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}+\partial_{t}f\rangle.

(7)

Here note that we also use the partial derivation notation
\partial_{t}\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle
to denote the derivative with respect to
t
, although the function
\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle
depends only on
t
.

Remark 2.2
.

The limiting process
(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
will be identified later in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. It turns out that the probability measure
\mu_{t}
is determined by moments. Thus, the convergence of all moment processes implies that the sequence of empirical measure processes
(\mu_{t}^{(N)})_{0\leq t\leq T}
converges to
(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
in probability in the space of continuous probability measure-valued processes endowed with the topology of uniform convergence. Refer to
[
42
]
for the derivation.

We prove Theorem
2.1
through several lemmas. We begin with the martingale part.

Lemma 2.3
.

For each
n=1,2,\dots
, denote by

M_{n}^{(N)}(t)=\frac{n}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{i}(u)^{n-1}db_{i}(u)

the martingale part in
S_{n}^{(N)}
. Then

M_{n}^{(N)}\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}0\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty.

Proof.

Note that
\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(u)^{2k}]du<\infty
, for any
k=1,2,\dots
, because we know exactly the joint distribution of
\{\lambda_{i}(u)\}
. Thus,
M_{n}^{(N)}
is a martingale with the quadratic variation

[M_{n}^{(N)}](t)=\frac{n^{2}}{N}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(u)^{2n-2}}{N}du=\frac{n^{2}}{N}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mu_{u}^{(N)},x^{2(n-1)}\rangle du.

Using Doob’s martingale inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|M_{n}^{(N)}\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|M_{n}^{(N)}(t)|\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[M_{n}^{(N)}(T)^{2}]}{\varepsilon^{2}}=\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}N}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},x^{2(n-1)}\rangle]dt.

Recall that
\{\lambda_{i}(t)/\sqrt{t}\}
is distributed as the Gaussian beta ensemble with parameter
\beta=2c/N
. It follows that
{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},x^{2(n-1)}\rangle]=t^{n-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\langle L_{N},x^{2(n-1)}\rangle]
with
L_{N}
being the empirical distribution defined in the introduction. In this regime, the sequence
\{{\mathbb{E}}[\langle L_{N},x^{2(n-1)}\rangle]\}_{N}
is easily shown to be bounded (for example, see
[
32
,
33
]
). Then the integral in the above estimate is bounded (as
N\to\infty
), implying the desired result. The lemma is proved.
∎

Next, we introduce the following fundamental results whose proof is standard and thus is omitted. They will be used to derive the convergence of
S_{n}^{(N)}
by induction.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\{X^{(N)}\}_{N}
and
\{Y^{(N)}\}_{N}
be two sequences of
C([0,T])
-valued random elements.
Assume that
X^{(N)}
and
Y^{(N)}
converge in probability to non-random limits
x
and
y
, respectively. Then the following hold.

(i)

As
N\to\infty
,

a(t)X^{(N)}(t)+b(t)Y^{(N)}(t)\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}a(t)x(t)+b(t)y(t),

where
a,b\in C([0,T])
.

(ii)

As
N\to\infty
,

X^{(N)}(t)Y^{(N)}(t)\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}x(t)y(t).

(iii)

As
N\to\infty
,

\int_{0}^{t}X^{(N)}(u)du\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}\int_{0}^{t}x(u)du.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

For
n=0,1
, it is clear that

S_{0}^{(N)}(t)\equiv 1,\quad S_{1}^{(N)}(t)=M_{1}^{(N)}(t)\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}0.

Now we show by induction that each moment process converges in probability to a non-random limit in
C([0,T])
as
N\to\infty
. Indeed, assume that for any
l\leq n-1
, the
l
th moment process
S_{l}^{(N)}
converges in probability to
m_{l}(t)\in C([0,T])
as
N\to\infty
. We need to show that
S_{n}^{(N)}
also converges. Observe that the martingale part and the last term in the equation (
6
) converge to zero. The remaining terms converge by induction assumption with the help of Lemma
2.4
. Combining all those observations, we deduce that

S_{n}^{(N)}(t)\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}m_{n}(t)\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty,

where

m_{n}(t)
is defined as

m_{n}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)m_{n-2}(u)+\frac{cn}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}m_{j}(u)m_{n-2-j}(u)\bigg)du.

(8)

Note that
m_{1}(t)\equiv 0
. It then follows that the limiting moment process
m_{n}(t)
is zero,
m_{n}(t)\equiv 0
, for any odd
n
. By induction, the equation (
8
) implies first that
m_{n}(t)
is a continuous function, for any
n\geq 0
. Then taking the derivative of the integral, we obtain a differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)m_{n-2}(t)+\frac{cn}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}m_{j}(t)m_{n-2-j}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)m_{n-2}(t)+\frac{cn}{2}\sum_{j=0,j:\text{even}}^{n-2}m_{j}(t)m_{n-2-j}(t)

(9)

with initial condition
m_{n}(0)=0\ (n\geq 1)
.
By induction again, we deduce that
m_{n}(t)
is of the form

m_{n}(t)=u_{n}t^{n/2},

where
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is a sequence of real numbers satisfying a self-convolutive recurrence

\begin{cases}u_{2n}=(2n-1)u_{2n-2}+c\sum_{j=0,j:\text{even}}^{2n-2}u_{j}u_{2n-2-j},\quad(u_{0}=1),\\
u_{2n+1}=0.\end{cases}

(10)

It turns out that
\{u_{n}\}
are moments of the probability measure
\nu_{c}
of associated Hermite polynomials
[
19
]
,

u_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n}\nu_{c}(x)dx.

The probability measure
\nu_{c}
is determined by moments and its density
\nu_{c}(x)
is given in (
2
).

Next, we define the limiting probability measure-valued process
(\mu_{t})
as

\mu_{t}(dx)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\nu_{c}(x/\sqrt{t})dx,\quad(t>0),\quad\mu_{0}=\delta_{0}.

Then

\langle\mu_{t},x^{n}\rangle=u_{n}t^{n/2}=m_{n}(t).

Now we rewrite the convergence of moment processes in the following form: for
n=0,1,\dots,
as
N\to\infty
,

\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},x^{n}\rangle\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}\langle\mu_{t},x^{n}\rangle,

\partial_{t}\langle\mu_{t},x^{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mu_{t},n(n-1)x^{n-2}\rangle+\frac{c}{2}\iint\frac{nx^{n-1}-ny^{n-1}}{x-y}d\mu_{t}(x)d\mu_{t}(y).

Lemma
2.4
implies that the convergence holds for any polynomial
p(x)
and any polynomial
f(t,x)
. The equation (
7
) follows immediately from the equation (
5
). Theorem
2.1
is proved.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

As the limiting measure of the empirical distribution of the Gaussian beta ensemble (
1
) in the regime
\beta N\to 2c\in(0,\infty)
,
the measure
\nu_{c}
in the proof of Theorem
2.1
was calculated explicitly in
[
1
,
19
]
. It is nothing but the probability measure of associated Hermite polynomials (see
[
4
]
or
[
24
, §5.6]
).
Theorem
2.1
not only shows the convergence at the process level but also provides another way to identify the limiting measure
\nu_{c}
. Recall that fluctuations around the limit were established in
[
32
,
43
]
by using the tridiagonal random matrix model. It is known that for a continuously differentiable function
f
whose derivative
f^{\prime}
is of polynomial growth, the following central limit theorem holds. As
N\to\infty
,

\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle L_{N},f\rangle-{\mathbb{E}}[\langle L_{N},f\rangle]\Big)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{f,c}^{2}),

where
\sigma_{f,c}^{2}\geq 0
is the limiting variance. In the next subsection, we are going to derive CLTs at the process level. As a consequence, we recover the above CLT when
f
is a polynomial with more information on the limiting variance.

2.2
Central limit theorem

We begin this section by introducing several concepts on the convergence in distribution of continuous processes.

For
d\geq 1
, let
C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
denote the space of continuous functions
f\colon[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
endowed with the supremum norm. (When
d=1
, we have used
C([0,T]])
instead of
C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.) Since
C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is a complete, separable metric space, the weak convergence, or the convergence in distribution of
C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
-valued random elements are defined as usual as follows. A sequence
X^{(N)}
converges in distribution/weakly to
X
if for any bounded continuous function
F\colon C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

{\mathbb{E}}[F(X^{(N)})]\to{\mathbb{E}}[F(X)]\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty.

We use the same notation ‘
\overset{d}{\to}
’ to denote that convergence. The joint convergence of
C([0,T])
-valued random elements will be understood as the convergence of
C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
-valued random elements. Namely, we say that
X^{(N;1)},X^{(N;2)},\dots,X^{(N;d)}
jointly converge in distribution to
X_{1},\dots,X_{d}
(as random elements on
C([0,T])
) if
X^{(N)}=(X^{(N;1)},X^{(N;2)},\dots,X^{(N;d)})
converges in distribution to
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
as random elements on
C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

The continuous mapping theorem implies the convergence of finite dimensional distributions, that is,
for any
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in[0,T]
,

(X^{(N)}(t_{1}),X^{(N)}(t_{2}),\dots,X^{(N)}(t_{k}))\overset{d}{\to}(X(t_{1}),X(t_{2}),\dots,X(t_{k}))\text{ as }N\to\infty,

as the convergence in distribution of
{\mathbb{R}}^{kd}
-valued random elements. The converse is not true in general. It turns out that the sequence
\{X^{(N)}\}_{N}
converges in distribution to
X
, if and only if (i) the sequence
\{X^{(N)}\}_{N}
is tight, and (ii)
\{X^{(N)}\}_{N}
converges in finite dimensional distributions to
X
.

We now deal with the convergence in distribution of the martingale part in (
5
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
f_{k}
be a polynomial in
t
and
x
, for
k=1,\dots,n
.
Define

\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}f_{k}(u,\lambda_{i}(u))db_{i}(u).

Then
\{\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}\}_{k=1}^{n}
jointly converge to Gaussian processes
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
of mean zero and covariance

{\mathbb{E}}[\eta_{k}(s)\eta_{l}(t)]=\int_{0}^{s\wedge t}\langle\mu_{u},f_{k}(u,x)f_{l}(u,x)\rangle du.

Proof.

Note that
\{\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}\}_{k=1}^{n}
are martingales with the following cross-variation

\displaystyle[\Phi^{(f_{k};N)},\Phi^{(f_{l};N)}](t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{k}(u,\lambda_{i}(u))f_{l}(u,\lambda_{i}(u))}{N}du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mu_{u}^{(N)},f_{k}(u,x)f_{l}(u,x)\rangle du.

Since the product
f_{k}f_{l}
is again a polynomial, the LLN in Theorem
2.1
implies that

[\Phi^{(f_{k};N)},\Phi^{(f_{l};N)}](t)\to\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mu_{u},f_{k}(u,x)f_{l}(u,x)\rangle du\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty.

For each
t
, the convergence holds in probability and in
L^{q}
, for any
q\in[1,\infty)
. Then by a general theorem in
[
35
]
, there are Gaussian processes
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
of mean zero and covariance

{\mathbb{E}}[\eta_{k}(s)\eta_{l}(t)]=\int_{0}^{s\wedge t}\langle\mu_{u},f_{k}(u,x)f_{l}(u,x)\rangle du,

to which
\{\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}\}_{k=1}^{n}
jointly converge. The lemma is proved.
∎

Now let us study the moment processes. Let

\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}(t)=\sqrt{N}\Big(S_{n}^{(N)}(t)-m_{n}(t)\Big).

It follows from the formula (
6
) and the relation (
8
) that

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{i}(u)^{n-1}db_{i}(u)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{cn}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{N}\Big(S_{j}^{(N)}(u)S_{n-2-j}^{(N)}(u)-m_{j}(u)m_{n-2-j}(u)\Big)du

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{S}_{n-2}^{(N)}(u)du-\frac{c}{2\sqrt{N}}n(n-1)\int_{0}^{t}S_{n-2}^{(N)}(u)du.

(11)

Note that the martingale part in the above formula is identical with
\Phi^{(nx^{n-1};N)}
. Thus, Lemma
2.6
implies that
\{\Phi^{(mx^{m-1};N)}\}_{m=1}^{n}
jointly converge in distribution to Gaussian processes
\{\eta_{m}\}_{m=1}^{n}
. The following result is analogous to Theorem 4.3.20 in
[
3
]
in case
\beta
is fixed.

Theorem 2.7
.

For each
n
,

\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}(t)=\sqrt{N}\left(S_{n}^{(N)}(t)-m_{n}(t)\right)\overset{d}{\to}\xi_{n},

where
\xi_{n}
is a Gaussian process defined inductively as

\xi_{n}(t)=\eta_{n}(t)+cn\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\int_{0}^{t}m_{j}(u)\xi_{n-2-j}(u)du+\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{n-2}(u)du.

(12)

The joint convergence also holds.

We need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

Assume that
\tilde{S}_{n_{1}}^{(N)}
and
\tilde{S}_{n_{2}}^{(N)}
jointly converge in distribution to
\xi_{n_{1}}
and
\xi_{n_{2}}
. Then

\sqrt{N}\Big(S_{n_{1}}^{(N)}(t)S_{n_{2}}^{(N)}(t)-m_{n_{1}}(t)m_{n_{2}}(t)\Big)-\Big(m_{n_{1}}(t)\tilde{S}_{n_{2}}(t)+m_{n_{2}}(t)\tilde{S}_{n_{1}}(t)\Big)\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}0.

(13)

Consequently,

\sqrt{N}\Big(S_{n_{1}}^{(N)}(t)S_{n_{2}}^{(N)}(t)-m_{n_{1}}(t)m_{n_{2}}(t)\Big)\overset{d}{\to}m_{n_{1}}(t)\xi_{n_{2}}(t)+m_{n_{2}}(t)\xi_{n_{1}}(t).

(14)

Proof.

It is clear that

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\Big(S_{n_{1}}^{(N)}(t)S_{n_{2}}^{(N)}(t)-m_{n_{1}}(t)m_{n_{2}}(t)\Big)-\Big(m_{n_{1}}(t)\tilde{S}_{n_{2}}(t)+m_{n_{2}}(t)\tilde{S}_{n_{1}}(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=(S_{n_{1}}(t)-m_{n_{1}}(t))\tilde{S}_{n_{2}}(t)\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}0.

Now as a consequence of the continuous mapping theorem, the joint convergence of
\tilde{S}_{n_{1}}
and
\tilde{S}_{n_{2}}
implies that

m_{n_{1}}(t)\tilde{S}_{n_{2}}(t)+m_{n_{2}}(t)\tilde{S}_{n_{1}}(t)\overset{d}{\to}m_{n_{1}}(t)\xi_{n_{2}}(t)+m_{n_{2}}(t)\xi_{n_{1}}(t).

(15)

The convergence (
14
) follows from the two equations (
13
) and (
15
) by a general result (Theorem 3.1 in
[
7
]
).
The lemma is proved.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

Let
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
be fixed. We aim to show that

\{\tilde{S}_{m}^{(N)}\}_{m=1}^{M}\text{ jointly converge to }\{\xi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M},

with
\xi_{n}(t)
defined in (
12
). Let us start from the joint convergence

\{\Phi^{(mx^{m-1};N)}\}_{m=1}^{M}\overset{d}{\to}\{\eta_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}.

Since
\tilde{S}_{1}^{(N)}=\Phi^{(1x^{0};N)}
, we replace the last entry in the above vector of
\Phi
’s by
\tilde{S}_{1}^{(N)}
without changing the vector of
\eta
’s (
\xi_{1}=\eta_{1}
). Assume for induction that for
n<M
, the following joint convergence holds

{\mathbb{X}}^{(N)}:=\left\{\{\Phi^{(mx^{m-1};N)}\}_{m=n}^{M},\{\tilde{S}_{m}^{(N)}\}_{m=1}^{n-1}\right\}\overset{d}{\to}\left\{\{\eta_{m}\}_{m=n}^{M},\{\xi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{n-1}\right\}.

(16)

Our task is to show the above convergence with
n
replaced by
n+1
.
Define the function
F\colon C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{M})\ni\{x^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}\to C([0,T])
by

\displaystyle F(\{x^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M})(t)

\displaystyle=x^{(n)}(t)+cn\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\int_{0}^{t}m_{j}(u)x^{(n-2-j)}(u)du

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)\int_{0}^{t}x^{(n-2)}(u)du.

It is clear that
F
is a continuous function. Thus, by the continuous mapping theorem,

\left\{\{\Phi^{(mx^{m-1};N)}\}_{m=n+1}^{N},F({\mathbb{X}}^{(N)}),\{\tilde{S}_{m}^{(N)}\}_{m=1}^{n-1}\right\}\overset{d}{\to}\left\{\{\eta_{m}\}_{m=n+1}^{M},\{\xi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{n}\right\}.

Now the formula (
11
) for
\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}
together with Lemma
2.8
implies that

F({\mathbb{X}}^{(N)})-\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}0.

Again by a general theorem (Theorem 3.1 in
[
7
]
), it follows that
F({\mathbb{X}}^{(N)})
can be replaced by
\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}
. The proof is complete.
∎

The joint convergence in Theorem
2.7
implies that for any polynomial
f=f(t,x)
in
t
and
x
,

\sqrt{N}\left(\langle\mu^{(N)}_{t},f\rangle-\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle\right)\overset{d}{\to}\langle\xi,f\rangle,

where the limiting process, denoted by
\langle\xi,f\rangle
, is defined to be a finite linear combination of
\{\xi_{n}\}
. In particular, the following linearity holds

\left\langle\xi,\sum_{finite}a_{f}f\right\rangle=\sum_{finite}a_{f}\langle\xi,f\rangle,\quad a_{f}\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Using such notations, we can rewrite the statement in Lemma
2.8
as

\displaystyle\iint(x^{n_{1}}y^{n_{2}}+x^{n_{2}}y^{n_{1}})\Big[\sqrt{N}d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(x)d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(y)-\sqrt{N}d\mu_{t}(x)d\mu_{t}(y)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\overset{d}{\to}\left\langle\xi,2\int(x^{n_{1}}y^{n_{2}}+x^{n_{2}}y^{n_{1}})d\mu_{t}(y)\right\rangle.

Here and in what follows,
\xi
is assumed to act upon functions of
t
and
x
variables.

Now let
f=f(t,x)
be a polynomial in
t
and
x
. Since

\frac{f^{\prime}(t,x)-f^{\prime}(t,y)}{x-y}

is a linear combination of
t^{k}(x^{n_{1}}y^{n_{2}}+x^{n_{2}}y^{n_{1}})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\iint\frac{f^{\prime}(t,x)-f^{\prime}(t,y)}{x-y}\Big[\sqrt{N}d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(x)d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(y)-\sqrt{N}d\mu_{t}(x)d\mu_{t}(y)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\overset{d}{\to}\left\langle\xi,2\int\frac{f^{\prime}(t,x)-f^{\prime}(t,y)}{x-y}\mu_{t}(dy)\right\rangle.

Consequently, we get the following result.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
f=f(t,x)
be a polynomial in
t
and
x
. Denote by
\langle\eta,f\rangle
the limit of the martingale part in the expression of
\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle
, or the limit of
\Phi^{(f^{\prime};N)}
which is coupling with
\{\eta_{m}\}_{m}
such that the joint convergence holds. Then

\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle\mu^{(N)}_{t},f\rangle-\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle\Big)\overset{d}{\to}\langle\xi,f\rangle,

and it holds that

\langle\xi,f\rangle=\langle\eta,f\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\xi,c\int\frac{f^{\prime}(s,x)-f^{\prime}(s,y)}{x-y}d\mu_{s}(y)+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}+\partial_{s}f\right\rangle ds.

(17)

Proof.

The formula (
5
), together with the above formulation, immediately yields the desired result.
∎

Next, we borrow an idea from
[
10
]
to identify limiting processes more explicitly.
The idea is to choose orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\nu_{c}
. Let us extract here a useful result on orthogonal polynomials (see
[
12
, Chapter 2]
or
[
40
, §3.8]
, for example). From two sequences
a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
b_{n}>0
, a Jacobi matrix
J
is formed by

J=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{2}&a_{3}&b_{3}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

Then there is a probability measure
\mu
such that

\langle\mu,x^{n}\rangle=J^{n}(1,1),\quad n=0,1,\dots.

In case there is a unique probability measure satisfying the above moment condition, or the measure
\mu
is determined by moments, we call
\mu
the spectral measure of
J
. The existence follows from Theorem 3.8.4 in
[
40
]
while Corollary 3.8.4 therein provides a useful sufficient condition for the uniqueness, that is, the uniqueness holds, if
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}^{-1}=\infty
.

The spectral measure
\mu
is related to orthogonal polynomials as follows. Define a sequence of polynomials
\{p_{n}\}
as

\displaystyle p_{0}=1,\quad p_{1}=x-a_{1},

\displaystyle p_{n+1}=xp_{n}-a_{n+1}p_{n}-b_{n}^{2}p_{n-1},\quad(n\geq 1).

Then
\{p_{n}\}
are orthogonal polynomials with respect to the spectral measure
\mu
,

\int p_{m}(x)p_{n}(x)d\mu(x)=\delta_{mn}\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}^{2},

(18)

where
\delta_{mn}=1
, if
m=n
and
\delta_{mn}=0
, if
m\neq n
.

Back to our problem, we need the fact that the probability measure
\nu_{c}
is the spectral measure of the following Jacobi matrix
[
19
]

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{c+1}\\
\sqrt{c+1}&0&\sqrt{c+2}\\
&\sqrt{c+2}&0&\sqrt{c+3}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

Then polynomials
\{p_{n}\}
recursively defined as

\displaystyle p_{0}=1,\quad p_{1}=xp_{0}=x,

\displaystyle p_{n+1}=xp_{n}-(n+c)p_{n-1},\quad(n\geq 1),

are orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\nu_{c}
,

\langle p_{n},p_{m}\rangle_{\nu_{c}}:=\int p_{n}(x)p_{m}(x)d\nu_{c}(x)=\delta_{mn}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(c+i).

(19)

When
c=0
,
\{p_{n}\}
are nothing but probabilist’s Hermite polynomials. For general
c
, polynomials
\{p_{n}\}
are defined by shifting the coefficients in the recurrence relation by
c
, and thus, they are called associated Hermite polynomials.

Note that the polynomial
p_{n}
is odd for odd
n
and even for even
n
. Then we can choose a primitive
P_{n}
of
p_{n}
, a polynomial of order
(n+1)
, to be either an odd or even polynomial. Define

\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)=t^{(n+1)/2}P_{n}(x/\sqrt{t}).

(20)

Then
\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)
is a polynomial in
t
and
x
. Now Corollary
2.9
implies that

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle\mu^{(N)}_{t},\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)\rangle-\langle\mu_{t},\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\to}\langle\xi,\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle=\langle\eta,\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\xi,c\int\frac{\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime}(s,x)-\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime}(s,y)}{x-y}d\mu_{s}(y)+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime\prime}+\partial_{s}\tilde{P}_{n}\right\rangle ds.

(21)

As proved latter in Theorem
2.11
, by working with
\tilde{P}_{n}
, the limiting processes
\{\langle\eta,\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle\}_{n\geq 0}
are independent.

Lemma 2.10
.

For each
n
, it holds that

c\int\frac{\tilde{P}^{\prime}_{n}(s,x)-\tilde{P}^{\prime}_{n}(s,y)}{x-y}d\mu_{s}(y)+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{P}^{\prime\prime}_{n}+\partial_{s}\tilde{P}_{n}=const\times s^{(n-1)/2}.

Consequently, the last term in the equation (
21
) vanishes.

Proof.

It is clear that

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime}(s,x)=s^{n/2}P_{n}^{\prime}(x/\sqrt{s})=s^{n/2}p_{n}(x/\sqrt{s}),

(22)

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(s,x)=s^{(n-1)/2}p^{\prime}_{n}(x/\sqrt{s}),

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\tilde{P}_{n}(s,x)=\frac{n+1}{2}s^{(n-1)/2}P_{n}(x/\sqrt{s})-\frac{1}{2}s^{(n-2)/2}xp_{n}(x/\sqrt{s}).

Next, by using the property that
\int f(x/\sqrt{s})d\mu_{s}(x)=\int f(x)d\nu_{c}(x)
, we obtain that

c\int\frac{\tilde{P}^{\prime}_{n}(s,x)-\tilde{P}^{\prime}_{n}(s,y)}{x-y}d\mu_{s}(y)+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{P}^{\prime\prime}_{n}+\partial_{s}\tilde{P}_{n}=s^{(n-1)/2}F(x/\sqrt{s}),

where

F(x)=c\int\frac{p_{n}(x)-p_{n}(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{c}(y)+\frac{1}{2}p_{n}^{\prime}(x)+\frac{n+1}{2}P_{n}(x)-\frac{1}{2}xp_{n}(x).

(23)

Thus, it suffices to show that
F(x)=const
.

Define a sequence
\{q_{n}\}
as

q_{n}(x)=\int\frac{p_{n}(x)-p_{n}(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{c}(y).

Then it is straightforward to check that
\{q_{n}\}
are polynomials satisfying the same recurrence relation as
\{p_{n}\}
but with different initial conditions

q_{n+1}=xq_{n}-(n+c)q_{n-1},\quad(n\geq 1),\quad q_{0}=0,q_{1}=1.

Now the first term of
F(x)
in the equation (
23
) is equal to
cq_{n}(x)
. To prove that
F(x)=const
, we show that its derivative is zero, that is,

cq_{n}^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}p_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{n}{2}p_{n}(x)-\frac{1}{2}xp_{n}^{\prime}(x)=0.

(24)

This relation can be proved by induction using the recurrence relation of
\{p_{n}\}
and
\{q_{n}\}
. We omit detailed arguments but noting that in the proof, we need another relation

p_{n}^{\prime}+cq_{n}-(n+c)p_{n-1}=0,

which can be easily proved by induction. The lemma is proved.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

For any
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\{\sqrt{N}(\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)\rangle-\langle\mu_{t},\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)\rangle)\}_{n=0}^{M}
jointly converge in distribution to
\{\tilde{\eta}_{n}(t)\}_{n=0}^{M}
,
where
\{\tilde{\eta}_{n}(t)\}
are independent Gaussian processes with mean zero and covariance

{\mathbb{E}}[\tilde{\eta}_{m}(s)\tilde{\eta}_{n}(t)]=\delta_{mn}\frac{\langle p_{n},p_{n}\rangle_{\nu_{c}}}{n+1}(s\wedge t)^{n+1}.

(25)

In particular, for the Gaussian beta ensemble (
1
) (the distribution of the eigenvalue process
\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}
at time
t=1
), as
N\to\infty
with
\beta N=2c
,

\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle L_{N},P_{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},P_{n}\rangle\Big)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{P_{n}}^{2}),

with

\sigma^{2}_{P_{n}}=\frac{1}{n+1}\langle p_{n},p_{n}\rangle_{\nu_{c}}=\frac{1}{n+1}(c+1)\cdots(c+n).

For the joint convergence, they converge to independent Gaussian random variables.

Proof.

Corollary
2.9
and Lemma
2.10
imply the joint convergence and the limiting processes
\langle\xi,\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle
coincide with the limit of
\Phi^{(\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime};N)}
, which we denote by
\tilde{\eta}_{n}
.
Then Lemma
2.6
states that the limiting processes
\{\tilde{\eta}_{n}\}
have mean zero and covariance

{\mathbb{E}}[\tilde{\eta}_{m}(s)\tilde{\eta}_{n}(t)]=\int_{0}^{s\wedge t}\langle\mu_{u},\tilde{P}_{m}^{\prime}(u,x)\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime}(u,x)\rangle du.

It now follows from the formula (
22
) for the derivatives of
\tilde{P}_{m}
and
\tilde{P}_{n}
that

\langle\mu_{u},\tilde{P}_{m}^{\prime}(u,x)\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime}(u,x)\rangle=u^{(n+m)/2}\langle\mu_{u},p_{m}(x/\sqrt{u})p_{n}(x/\sqrt{u})\rangle=u^{(n+m)/2}\langle p_{m},p_{n}\rangle_{\nu_{c}},

which is zero when
n\neq m
.
Thus, the covariance of
\tilde{\eta}_{m}(s)
and
\tilde{\eta}_{n}(t)
is given by the equation (
25
). That the covariance is zero when
m\neq n
implies that Gaussian processes
\{\tilde{\eta}_{m}\}
are independent, which completes the proof of the first part of this theorem. The second one is just a particular case of the first. The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

Let
\tilde{p}_{n}=p_{n}/\sqrt{\langle p_{n},p_{n}\rangle_{\nu_{c}}}
be a sequence of orthonormal polynomials. For a polynomial
F
, we express
f=F^{\prime}
in terms of
\{\tilde{p}_{n}\}
,

f=\sum_{n}\langle f,\tilde{p}_{n}\rangle_{\nu_{c}}\tilde{p}_{n}=\sum_{n}\alpha_{n}\tilde{p}_{n}.

We wish to simplify the limiting variance

\sigma_{F}^{2}=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n+1}\alpha_{n}^{2}=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n+1}\langle f,\tilde{p}_{n}\rangle_{\nu_{c}}^{2}.

Let us now express the limiting variance as

\displaystyle\sigma_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n+1}\langle f,\tilde{p}_{n}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n+1}\int f(x)\tilde{p}_{n}(x)\nu_{c}(x)dx\int f(y)\tilde{p}_{n}(y)\nu_{c}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\iint f(x)f(y)\left(\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n+1}\tilde{p}_{n}(x)\tilde{p}_{n}(y)\right)\nu_{c}(x)\nu_{c}(y)dxdy.

Note that
\{\tilde{p}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis in the space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\nu_{c})
. It follows that
\{\tilde{p}_{n}(x)\tilde{p}_{n}(y)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal system in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2},\nu_{c}\otimes\nu_{c})
, and thus,

K_{c}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\tilde{p}_{n}(x)\tilde{p}_{n}(y)

is well-defined with equality in
L^{2}
. Now for any polynomial
F
,

\sigma_{F}^{2}=\iint F^{\prime}(x)F^{\prime}(y)K_{c}(x,y)\nu_{c}(x)\nu_{c}(y)dxdy.

We are unable to simplify
K_{c}(x,y)
yet. However, in case of Hermite polynomials (the trivial case where
c=0
), Mehler’s formula gives us that for
\rho\in[0,1)
,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{p_{n}(x)p_{n}(y)}{n!}{\rho^{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\rho^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})-2\rho xy}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\right).

Thus,

K_{0}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\rho^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})-2\rho xy}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\right)d\rho.

3
Laguerre case

This section deals with the Laguerre case. We consider the following (scaled) beta Laguerre ensembles with parameters
\alpha,\beta>0
whose joint density is proportional to

\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}|^{\beta}\prod_{l=1}^{N}\lambda_{l}^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda_{l}},\quad(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in\Omega_{L},

(26)

where

\Omega_{L}=\{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}:0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}\}.

They are generalizations of the eigenvalue distribution of Wishart and Laguerre matrices in terms of the joint density. Refer to Chapter 1 in
[
21
]
for some basic properties of these ensembles.

The so-called beta Laguerre processes satisfy the following system of SDEs

\begin{cases}d\lambda_{i}(t)=\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}}db_{i}(t)+\alpha dt+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\dfrac{2\lambda_{i}(t)}{\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}dt,\\
\lambda_{i}(0)=0,\end{cases}i=1,\dots,N,

(27)

and
(\lambda_{1}(t),\dots,\lambda_{N}(t))\in\Omega_{L}
, for all
t>0
,
where
\{b_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent standard Brownian motions. For
\beta=1
and
\beta=2
, they are the eigenvalue process of the Wishart process and Laguerre process, respectively
[
8
,
9
,
27
,
28
]
. For
\beta\geq 1
, the above system of SDEs has a unique strong solution
[
23
]
. However, when
\beta\in(0,1)
, we should define beta Laguerre processes as the squared of type B radial Dunkl processes
[
13
]
. For this, the condition
\alpha>1/2
is required. Similar to the Gaussian case, the set of
t
such that
\lambda_{i}(t)=\lambda_{j}(t)
, for some
i\neq j
, has Lebesgue measure zero, almost surely. In what follows, we consider a regime where
\alpha>1/2
is fixed and
\beta=2c/N
for a given constant
c\in(0,\infty)
. Under the above trivial initial condition (
\lambda_{i}(0)=0
), for
t>0
, the joint distribution of
\{\lambda_{i}(t)/t\}
coincides with the beta Laguerre ensemble (
26
) (cf.
[
37
]
).

Let

\mu^{(N)}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(t)}

be the empirical measure process.
For
f(t,x)\in C^{2}([0,\infty)\times[0,\infty))
, by Itô’s formula, we obtain

\displaystyle d\langle\mu^{(N)}_{t},f\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}(t)}f^{\prime}(t,\lambda_{i}(t))db_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\mu^{(N)}_{t},\alpha f^{\prime}(t,x)+xf^{\prime\prime}(t,x)+\partial_{t}f(t,x)\rangle dt

\displaystyle\quad+c\iint\frac{xf^{\prime}(t,x)-yf^{\prime}(t,y)}{x-y}d\mu^{(N)}_{t}(x)d\mu^{(N)}_{t}(y)dt

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{c}{N}\langle\mu^{(N)}_{t},xf^{\prime\prime}(t,x)+f^{\prime}(t,x)\rangle dt.

(28)

Again, note that the above formula holds when
\lambda_{1}(t),\dots,\lambda_{N}(t)
are distinct.

We follow the same routine as in the Gaussian case. The equation (
28
) with
f=x^{n}
yields a recurrence relation for the
n
th moment process
S_{n}^{(N)}
,

\displaystyle dS_{n}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{n}{N}\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}(t)}\lambda_{i}(t)^{n-1}db_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha nS_{n-1}^{(N)}(t)dt+cn\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}S_{i}^{(N)}(t)S_{n-i-1}^{(N)}(t)dt

\displaystyle\quad+n(n-1)S_{n-1}^{(N)}(t)dt-\frac{cn^{2}}{N}S_{n-1}^{(N)}(t)dt.

(29)

Let

M_{n}^{(N)}(t)=\frac{n}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}(s)}\lambda_{i}(s)^{n-1}db_{i}(s)=\frac{\sqrt{2}n}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{i}(s)^{n-1/2}db_{i}(s)

be the martingale part with the quadratic variation

[M_{n}^{(N)}](t)=\frac{2n^{2}}{N}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(s)^{2n-1}}{N}ds.

(30)

Writing down
S_{n}^{(N)}(t)
in the integral form
and using similar induction arguments as in the Gaussian case, we deduce that the
n
th moment process
S_{n}^{(N)}(t)
converges to a deterministic limit
m_{n}(t)
satisfying

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}m_{n}(t)=n\left((\alpha+n-1)m_{n-1}(t)+c\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}m_{i}(t)m_{n-i-1}(t)\right),\\
m_{n}(0)=0,\end{cases}\quad(n\geq 1).

(31)

Again, these equations lead to a simple solution

m_{n}(t)=u_{n}t^{n},

where
\{u_{n}\}
are defined recursively as

u_{n}=(\alpha+n-1)u_{n-1}+c\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}u_{i}u_{n-i-1},\quad n=1,2,\dots,(u_{0}=1).

The measure
\nu_{\alpha,c}
having moments
\{u_{n}\}
is unique and its density can be directly calculated from the above relation by a method in
[
31
]
. However, we will use an existing result that
\nu_{\alpha,c}
is the probability measure of associated Laguerre polynomials of Model II
[
41
,
42
]
. Its explicit density can be found in
[
25
]
, or in
[
2
]
.

The limiting probability measure-valued process
(\mu_{t})
is now defined as

\mu_{t}(dx)=\frac{1}{t}\nu_{\alpha,c}(x/t)dx,\quad(t>0),\mu_{0}=\delta_{0}.

We obtain the following law of large numbers.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any polynomial
f(t,x)
in
t
and
x
, as
N\to\infty
,

\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle.

Recall that this is the convergence in probability in the space
C([0,T])
.
Moreover, it holds that

\partial_{t}\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle=c\iint\frac{xf^{\prime}(t,x)-yf^{\prime}(t,y)}{x-y}d\mu_{t}(x)d\mu_{t}(y)+\langle\mu_{t},\alpha f^{\prime}+xf^{\prime\prime}+\partial_{t}f\rangle.

(32)

Next, we study the central limit theorem.
Let

\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}(t)=\sqrt{N}\left(S_{n}^{(N)}(t)-m_{n}(t)\right).

Then by using the equations (
29
) and (
31
), we deduce that

\displaystyle d\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle=d\sqrt{N}M_{n}^{(N)}(t)+n(\alpha+n-1)\tilde{S}_{n-1}^{(N)}(t)dt

\displaystyle\quad+cn\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\sqrt{N}S_{i}^{(N)}(t)S_{n-i-1}^{(N)}(t)-\sqrt{N}m_{i}(t)m_{n-i-1}(t)\right)dt

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{cn^{2}}{\sqrt{N}}S_{n-1}^{(N)}(t)dt.

We state without proof the following results analogous to the Gaussian case.

Theorem 3.2
.

The following hold.

(i)

For each
n\geq 1
, the martingale part
\sqrt{N}M_{n}^{(N)}
converges in distribution to a Gaussian process
\eta_{n}
of mean zero. The joint convergence also holds.

(ii)

\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}
converges in distribution to a Gaussian process
\xi_{n}
defined inductively as

\xi_{n}(t)=\eta_{n}(t)+n(\alpha+n-1)\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{n-1}(s)ds+2cn\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}m_{i}(s)\xi_{n-i-1}(s)ds\right),

for
n\geq 2,

with
\xi_{0}(t)\equiv 0,\xi_{1}(t)=\eta_{1}(t)
. The joint convergence also holds.

(iii)

More generally, for any polynomial
f=f(t,x)
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\left(\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle-\langle\mu_{t},f\rangle\right)\overset{d}{\to}\langle\xi,f\rangle,

where the limiting Gaussian processes
\langle\xi,f\rangle
satisfies the following relation

\displaystyle\langle\xi,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\eta,f\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\xi,2c\int\frac{xf^{\prime}(s,x)-yf^{\prime}(s,y)}{x-y}d\mu_{s}(y)+\partial_{s}f+\alpha f^{\prime}+xf^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle ds,

(33)

with
\langle\eta,f\rangle
the limit of the martingale part

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}}f^{\prime}(s,\lambda_{i})db_{i}(s)\overset{d}{\to}\langle\eta,f\rangle.

Gaussian processes
\langle\eta,f\rangle
have mean zero and are coupling with covariance

{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\eta,f\rangle(s)\langle\eta,g\rangle(t)]=2\int_{0}^{s\wedge t}\langle\mu_{u},xf^{\prime}(u,x)g^{\prime}(u,x)\rangle du.

(34)

Based on these results, especially based on the covariance formula (
34
), in order to study more about the limiting processes
\langle\xi,f\rangle
, the idea now is to take orthogonal polynomials with respect to
x\nu_{\alpha,c}(dx)
. For that purpose, let us extract some results on the probability measure
\nu_{\alpha,c}
.

(i)

The probability measure
\nu_{\alpha,c}
is the spectral measure of the following Jacobi matrix

J_{\alpha,c}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
d_{1}&c_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}&d_{1}\\
&c_{2}&d_{2}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{2}&c_{1}d_{1}\\
c_{1}d_{1}&c_{2}^{2}+d_{1}^{2}&c_{2}d_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

where

c_{n}=\sqrt{\alpha+c+n-1},\quad d_{n}=\sqrt{c+n}.

(ii)

The explicit formula for the density
\nu_{\alpha,c}(x)
is given by

\nu_{\alpha,c}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(c+1)\Gamma(c+\alpha)}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}}{|\Psi(c,1-\alpha;xe^{-i\pi})|^{2}},\quad x\geq 0.

Here
\Psi(a,b;z)
is Tricomi’s confluent hypergeometric function.
This is Model II of associated Laguerre polynomials
[
25
]
.

(iii)

The probability measure
\tilde{\nu}_{\alpha,c}(dx)=\frac{1}{\alpha+c}x\nu_{\alpha,c}(x)dx
is the probability measure of Model I of associated Laguerre polynomials, that is,
\tilde{\nu}_{\alpha,c}(dx)
is the spectral measure of the following Jacobi matrix

\tilde{J}_{\alpha,c}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{2}^{2}+d_{0}^{2}&c_{2}d_{1}\\
c_{2}d_{1}&c_{2}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}&c_{3}d_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

(35)

This observation is based on the explicit formulae for the two models of associated Laguerre polynomials in
[
25
]
. We give another explanation in Remark
3.6
below.

From the Jacobi matrix
\tilde{J}_{\alpha,c}
, we define orthogonal polynomials with respect to
x\nu_{\alpha}
or
\tilde{\nu}_{\alpha,c}
as follows

\displaystyle p_{0}=1,\quad p_{1}(x)=x-(c_{2}^{2}+d_{0}^{2})=x-(\alpha+2c+1),

\displaystyle p_{n+1}=xp_{n}-(c_{n+1}^{2}+d_{n+1}^{2})p_{n}-c_{n+1}^{2}d_{n}^{2}p_{n-1}

\displaystyle\qquad=xp_{n}-(\alpha+2c+2n+1)p_{n}-(\alpha+c+n)(c+n)p_{n-1},\quad(n\geq 1).

(36)

The orthogonal relations are given by

\int p_{m}(x)p_{n}(x)d\tilde{\nu}_{\alpha,c}(x)=\delta_{mn}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i+1}^{2}d_{i}^{2})=\delta_{mn}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(c+i)(\alpha+c+i).

Let
P_{n}
be a primitive of
p_{n}
. Define

\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x):=t^{n+1}P_{n}\left(\frac{x}{t}\right).

Again, the idea of working with
\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)
is to make the limiting processes
\{\langle\eta,\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle\}
independent.

Lemma 3.3
.

The last term in the equation (
(iii)
) vanishes for
f=\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)
.

Proof.

A direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime}(t,x)=t^{n}p_{n}(\frac{x}{t}),

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t,x)=t^{n-1}p_{n}^{\prime}(\frac{x}{t}),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)=(n+1)t^{n}P_{n}(\frac{x}{t})-xt^{n-1}p_{n}(\frac{x}{t}).

Thus, if suffices to show that

2c\int\frac{xp_{n}(x)-yp_{n}(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{\alpha,c}(y)+\alpha p_{n}(x)+xp^{\prime}_{n}(x)+(n+1)P_{n}(x)-xp_{n}(x)=const.

(37)

We only sketch main steps in the proof of the above equation.

Step 1. Define polynomials
\{q_{n}\}
as

q_{n}:=\int\frac{p_{n}(x)-p_{n}(y)}{x-y}\tilde{\nu}_{\alpha,c}(y)dy.

Then
\{q_{n}\}
satisfy the same recurrence relation as
\{p_{n}\}
but with different initial conditions
q_{0}=0,q_{1}=1
. We now express the integral in the first term of the equation (
37
) in terms of
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
,

\displaystyle\int\frac{xp_{n}(x)-yp_{n}(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{\alpha,c}(y)

\displaystyle\quad=\int\frac{xp_{n}(x)-yp_{n}(x)+yp_{n}(x)-yp_{n}(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{\alpha,c}(y)

\displaystyle\quad=p_{n}(x)+\int\frac{p_{n}(x)-p_{n}(y)}{x-y}(\alpha+c)d\tilde{\nu}_{\alpha,c}(y)

\displaystyle\quad=p_{n}(x)+(\alpha+c)q_{n}(x).

This enables us to show the equation (
37
) by induction.

Step 2. The equation (
37
) is equivalent to the following obtained by taking its derivative

(\alpha+2c+1)p_{n}^{\prime}+2c(\alpha+c)q_{n}^{\prime}+xp_{n}^{\prime\prime}+np_{n}-xp_{n}^{\prime}=0.

When showing that relation by induction, we need a further relation that

xp_{n}^{\prime}-np_{n}+c(c+\alpha)q_{n}-(\alpha+c+n)(c+n)p_{n-1}=0

which can be showed easily by induction. The proof is complete.
∎

We arrive at main results for the Laguerre case. Their detailed proofs are omitted because arguments are quite similar to the Gaussian case.

Theorem 3.4
.

For any
M
,

\Big\{\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)\rangle-\langle\mu_{t},\tilde{P}_{n}(t,x)\rangle\Big)\Big\}_{n=0}^{M}

jointly converge to independent Gaussian processes
\tilde{\eta}_{n}(t)
of mean zero and covariance

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\tilde{\eta}_{n}(s)\tilde{\eta}_{n}(t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{(s\wedge t)^{2n+2}}{n+1}\int p_{n}(x)^{2}xd\mu_{\alpha,c}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(s\wedge t)^{2n+2}(\alpha+c)}{n+1}\int p_{n}(x)^{2}d\tilde{\mu}_{\alpha,c}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(s\wedge t)^{2n+2}(\alpha+c)}{n+1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(c+i)(\alpha+c+i).

Corollary 3.5
.

Consider the beta Laguerre ensemble (
26
) in a regime where
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
is fixed and
\beta N=2c
. Then the following hold.

(i)

The empirical distribution
L_{N}
converges weakly to
\nu_{\alpha,c}
as
N\to\infty
(in probability).

(ii)

As
N\to\infty
,

\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle L_{N},P_{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{\alpha,c},P_{n}\rangle\Big)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{P_{n}}^{2}),

where

\sigma_{P_{n}}^{2}=\frac{\alpha+c}{n+1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(c+i)(\alpha+c+i).

The limits of the joint convergence are independent Gaussian random variables.

Note that by using a tridiagonal matrix model for beta Laguerre ensembles, the strong LLN (with the almost sure convergence) for
L_{N}
and CLTs for continuously differentiable functions were established in
[
41
]
.
We conclude this paper with the following remark.

Remark 3.6
.

We claim that if
\mu
is the spectral measure of

J=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
d_{1}&c_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}&d_{1}\\
&c_{2}&d_{2}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{2}&c_{1}d_{1}\\
c_{1}d_{1}&c_{2}^{2}+d_{1}^{2}&c_{2}d_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

then
\nu=c_{1}^{-2}\times x\mu
is the spectral measure of

H=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{2}+d_{1}^{2}&c_{2}d_{1}\\
c_{2}d_{1}&c_{2}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}&c_{3}d_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

provided that the spectral measure of
J
(and of
H
) is unique.
Equivalently, given two Jacobi matrices
J
and
H
of the above form, the claim means that for any
n\geq 0
,

c_{1}^{2}H^{n}(1,1)=J^{n+1}(1,1).

(38)

We roughly present an idea of proof. Since both sides of the relation (
38
) are polynomials of
\{c_{i}\}
and
\{d_{i}\}
, we assume without loss of generality that the two sequences are bounded.
Consider an operator
{}\hat{}\colon J\mapsto\hat{J}

J=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{2}&c_{1}d_{1}\\
c_{1}d_{1}&c_{2}^{2}+d_{1}^{2}&c_{2}d_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\hat{J}=\begin{pmatrix}0&c_{1}\\
c_{1}&0&d_{1}\\
&d_{1}&0&c_{2}\\
&&c_{2}&0&d_{2}\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

Then one may check the relation that
(\hat{J})^{2n}(1,1)=J^{n}(1,1)
. In other words, if
\mu
and
\hat{\mu}
are the spectral measure of
J
and
\hat{J}
, respectively, then

\langle\hat{\mu},x^{2n}\rangle=\langle\mu,x^{n}\rangle,\quad\langle\hat{\mu},x^{2n+1}\rangle=0,\quad n=0,1,\dots.

It follows that
S_{\hat{\mu}}(z)=zS_{\mu}(z^{2})
, where
S_{\nu}(z)
denotes the Stieltjes transform of a measure
\nu
,

S_{\nu}(z)=\int\frac{\nu(dx)}{x-z},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}.

Formally,

S_{\nu}(z)=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\langle\nu,x^{n}\rangle}{z^{n+1}}

is a generating function of the sequence of moments
\langle\nu,x^{n}\rangle
.

Consider Jacobi matrices

K=\begin{pmatrix}d_{0}^{2}&d_{0}c_{1}\\
d_{0}c_{1}&c_{1}^{2}+d_{1}^{2}&d_{1}c_{2}\\
&d_{1}c_{2}&c_{2}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}&d_{2}c_{3}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},\hat{K}=\begin{pmatrix}0&d_{0}\\
d_{0}&0&c_{1}\\
&c_{1}&0&d_{1}\\
&&d_{1}&0&c_{2}\\
&&&c_{2}&0&d_{2}\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\hat{J}
is obtained from
\hat{K}
by removing the top row and the left most column. Similarly,
H
is obtained from
K
in the same way. Let
\xi
(resp.
\hat{\xi}
,
\mu
and
\nu
) be the spectral measure of
K
(resp.
\hat{K}
,
J
and
H
). We need to show that
c_{1}^{2}\nu=x\mu
. The following relations hold:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{S_{\xi}(z)}=z-d_{0}^{2}+d_{0}^{2}c_{1}^{2}S_{\nu}(z),

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{S_{\hat{\xi}}(z)}=z-0+d_{0}^{2}S_{\hat{\mu}}(z),

\displaystyle S_{\hat{\mu}}(z)=zS_{\mu}(z^{2}),

\displaystyle S_{\hat{\xi}}(z)=zS_{\xi}(z^{2}).

Here the first two relations are relations of the Stieltjes transform of the spectral measure of the original Jacobi matrix and that of the Jacobi matrix after removing the top row and the left most column
[
40
, Theorem 3.2.4]
.
From which, we deduce that

1+z^{2}S_{\mu}(z^{2})=c_{1}^{2}S_{\nu}(z^{2}),

implying the desired relation (
38
).
