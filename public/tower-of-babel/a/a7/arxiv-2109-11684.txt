From the AKNS system to the matrix Schrödinger equation with vanishing potentials: Direct and inverse problems LaTeX compilation date and time: August 7, 2026 at \currenttime
Francesco Demontis, Cornelis van der Mee

Abstract
We relate the scattering theory of the focusing AKNS system with vanishing boundary conditions to that of the matrix Schrödinger equation. The corresponding Miura transformation which allows this connection, converts the focusing matrix nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation into a new nonlocal integrable equation. We apply the matrix triplet method to derive the multisoliton solutions of the nonlocal integrable equation, thus proposing a new method to solve the matrix NLS equation.

2
Lax Pair for the new integrable model

As to be indicated shortly, the matrix NLS system is governed by a Lax pair

\{L,A\}
of linear operators
[
49
,
5
,
26
]
, where
Lv=kv
is the AKNS
eigenvalue problem and
v_{t}=Av
describes the time evolution. Then the matrix
NLS system is equivalent to the zero curvature condition

L_{t}+LA-AL=\boldsymbol{0},

where
\boldsymbol{0}
denotes the zero operator on a suitable function space. We now
observe that
\{L^{2},A\}
is also a Lax pair of linear operators, where

L^{2}v=k^{2}v
is the matrix Schrödinger eigenvalue problem and
v_{t}=Av

describes the time evolution. Then the accompanying nonlinear evolution equation
is equivalent to the zero curvature condition

(L^{2})_{t}+L^{2}A-AL^{2}=\boldsymbol{0}.

Putting

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\partial_{x}I_{n}-\mathcal{Q}),

(2.1a)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\left(2\partial_{x}^{2}I_{n}-2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}-\mathcal{B}_{0}\right),

(2.1b)

where
n=m_{1}+m_{2}
and
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is to be determined, we compute

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}=-(L_{t}+LA-AL)

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\partial_{x}I_{n}-\mathcal{Q})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\left(2\partial_{x}^{2}I_{n}-2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}-\mathcal{B}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\left(2\partial_{x}^{2}I_{n}-2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}-\mathcal{B}_{0}\right)\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\partial_{x}I_{n}-\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}_{t}+(\partial_{x}I_{n}+\mathcal{Q})\left(2\partial_{x}^{2}I_{n}-2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}-\mathcal{B}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(2\partial_{x}^{2}I_{n}+2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{B}_{0}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\right)(\partial_{x}I_{n}-\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}_{t}+2\partial_{x}^{3}I_{n}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}\partial_{x}-2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}^{2}-(\mathcal{B}_{0})_{x}-\mathcal{B}_{0}\partial_{x}+2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}^{2}-2\mathcal{Q}^{2}\partial_{x}-\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{B}_{0}-2\partial_{x}^{3}I_{n}

\displaystyle+2\mathcal{Q}_{xx}+4\mathcal{Q}_{x}\partial_{x}+2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}^{2}-2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}^{2}+2\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{x}+2\mathcal{Q}^{2}\partial_{x}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{B}_{0}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\partial_{x}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{B}_{0}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}_{t}-2(-\mathcal{Q}_{x}+\tfrac{1}{2}[\mathcal{B}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{B}_{0}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}])\partial_{x}

\displaystyle-(\mathcal{B}_{0})_{x}-\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{B}_{0}+2\mathcal{Q}_{xx}+2\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{x}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{B}_{0}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}.

To cancel the
\partial_{x}
terms,
\mathcal{B}_{0}
should be the sum of
\mathcal{Q}_{x}
and a matrix
commuting with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}
. Thus taking
\mathcal{B}_{0}=\mathcal{Q}^{2}+\mathcal{Q}_{x}=\boldsymbol{Q}
, we get the matrix
NLS equation

i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}_{t}+\mathcal{Q}_{xx}-2\mathcal{Q}^{3}=0_{n\times n}.

(2.2)

Putting
\mathcal{L}=L^{2}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+\boldsymbol{Q}
, we compute

\displaystyle i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}_{t}+\mathcal{L}A-A\mathcal{L}]=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}

\displaystyle-(-\partial_{x}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3})\left[2\partial_{x}^{2}-2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}-\boldsymbol{Q}\right]+\left[2\partial_{x}^{2}-2\mathcal{Q}\partial_{x}-\boldsymbol{Q}\right](-\partial_{x}^{2}+\boldsymbol{Q})

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}+4(-\mathcal{Q}_{x}+\tfrac{1}{2}[\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}])\partial_{x}^{2}

\displaystyle+2(-\mathcal{Q}_{xx}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q})\partial_{x}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}-2\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-\boldsymbol{Q}^{2}.

Then (

(2.3)

where

\mathcal{Q}(x;t)=-\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\right).

(2.4)

A proof of the integrability of (

1.4

(
1.6

A

)

the block diagonal component yields the commutator of (

Let us define the

Then, for each

\displaystyle F_{r}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx}A_{r}(k)+e^{ikx}B_{r}(k)+o(1),

\displaystyle A_{r,l}(k)

\displaystyle=I_{n}-\frac{1}{2ik}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\boldsymbol{Q}(y)m_{r,l}(y,k),

\displaystyle B_{r,l}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2ik}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,e^{\mp 2iky}\boldsymbol{Q}(y)m_{r,l}(y,k).

Thus

is continuous in

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{l}(x,k)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}

∎

A_{r,l}(k)^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}B_{r,l}(-k)=B_{r,l}(k)^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}A_{r,l}(-k),

(3.9)

where
0\neq k\in\mathbb{R}
. Using the second part for
\boldsymbol{V}=\boldsymbol{F}_{l}
and
\boldsymbol{W}=\boldsymbol{F}_{r}

we obtain

A_{r}(k)^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}A_{l}(-k)\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3},\qquad B_{r}(k)^{\dagger}=-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}B_{l}(k)\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3},

(3.10)

where
0\neq k\in\mathbb{R}
. Finally, using the first part for
\boldsymbol{V}=\boldsymbol{W}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
we get

A_{r,l}(-k^{*})^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}A_{l,r}(k)\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3},

(3.11)

where
0\neq k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
.

Introducing the
reflection coefficients

R_{r,l}(k)=B_{r,l}(k)A_{r,l}(k)^{-1}=-A_{l,r}(k)^{-1}B_{l,r}(-k)

(3.12)

and the
transmission coefficients

A_{r,l}(k)^{-1}
, we obtain the

Riemann-Hilbert problem

\begin{pmatrix}F_{l}(x,-k)&F_{r}(x,-k)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{r}(x,k)&F_{l}(x,k)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{r}(k)^{-1}&-R_{l}(k)\\
-R_{r}(k)&A_{l}(k)^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

(3.13)

where the matrix
\boldsymbol{S}(k)
containing the
A
and
R
quantities is called the

scattering matrix
and the discussion of the nonsingularity of
A_{r,l}(k)

will be presented shortly. Then it is easily verified that

R_{r,l}(k)^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}R_{r,l}(-k)\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3},

(3.14)

and

\boldsymbol{S}(k)^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3})\boldsymbol{S}(k)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3},

(3.15)

provided
0\neq k\in\mathbb{R}
and
\det A_{r,l}(k)\neq 0
.

Above we have defined
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r,l}
as follows:

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r,l}=\lim_{k\to 0}\,2ikA_{r,l}(k)=-\lim_{k\to 0^{\pm}}\,2ikB_{r,l}(k),

where the first limit may be taken from the upper half-plane. Then the matrices

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r,l}
have the same determinant. If
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r,l}
is nonsingular, we say
that we are in the
generic case
; if instead
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r,l}
is singular,
we are in the
exceptional case
. We are said to be in the

superexceptional case
if
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r,l}=0_{n\times n}
and
A_{r,l}(k)
tends
to a nonsingular matrix,
A_{r,l}(0)
say, as
k\to 0
from within
\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
.

Throughout this article, we assume the absence of
spectral singularities
,
i.e., the absence of nonzero real
k
for which
\det A_{r,l}(k)=0
. Under this
condition the reflection coefficients
R_{r,l}(k)
are continuous in

0\neq k\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.2
Triangular representations and Wiener algebras

The Jost solutions allow the triangular representations

\displaystyle F_{l}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{ikx}I_{n}+\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{iky}K(x,y),

(3.16a)

\displaystyle F_{r}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx}I_{n}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{-iky}J(x,y),

(3.16b)

where for every
x\in\mathbb{R}

\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\|K(x,y)\|+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,\|J(x,y)\|<+\infty.

(3.17)

To derive (
3.16
), we introduce the auxiliary matrix functions
K(x,y)
and

J(x,y)
as the unique solutions of the Volterra integral equations

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\frac{1}{2}[x+y]}^{\infty}dz\,\boldsymbol{Q}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{x}^{\infty}dz\,\boldsymbol{Q}(z)\int_{\max(z,x+y-z)}^{z+y-x}d\hat{y}\,K(z,\hat{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\frac{1}{2}[x+y]}^{\infty}dz\,\boldsymbol{Q}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{x}^{y}dz\int_{x+\frac{1}{2}(y-z)}^{\infty}dw\,\boldsymbol{Q}(w)K(w,w+z-x),

(3.18a)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}[x+y]}dz\,\boldsymbol{Q}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{x}dz\,\boldsymbol{Q}(z)\int_{z+y-x}^{\min(z,x+y-z)}d\hat{y}\,J(z,\hat{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}[x+y]}dz\,\boldsymbol{Q}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{y}^{x}dz\int_{-\infty}^{x-\frac{1}{2}[z-y]}dw\,\boldsymbol{Q}(w)J(w,w+z-x).

(3.18b)

Solving (
3.18
) by iteration we easily derive (
3.16
) and
(
3.17
). Moreover,

K(x,x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\boldsymbol{Q}(y),\qquad J(x,x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,\boldsymbol{Q}(y).

(3.19)

Let us now introduce some necessary terminology and well-known results on
Fourier transforms of
L^{1}
-functions. By the (continuous) Wiener algebra
\mathcal{W}

we mean the complex vector space of constants plus Fourier transforms of

L^{1}
-functions

\mathcal{W}=\{c+\hat{h}:c\in\mathbb{C},\ h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\}

endowed with the norm
|c|+\|h\|_{1}
. Here we define the Fourier transform as
follows:
(\mathcal{F}h)(k)=\hat{h}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,e^{iky}h(y)
.
The invertible elements of the commutative Banach algebra
\mathcal{W}
with unit
element are exactly those
c+\hat{h}\in\mathcal{W}
for which
c\neq 0
and

c+\hat{h}(k)\neq 0
for each
k\in\mathbb{R}

[
30
]
.

The algebra
\mathcal{W}
has the two closed subalgebras
\mathcal{W}^{+}
and
\mathcal{W}^{-}
consisting
of those
c+\hat{h}\in\mathcal{W}
for which
h
is supported on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\mathbb{R}^{-}
,
respectively. The invertible elements of
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}
are exactly those

c+\hat{h}\in\mathcal{W}^{\pm}
for which
c\neq 0
and
c+\hat{h}(k)\neq 0
for each

k\in\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\cup\mathbb{R}

[
30
]
. Letting
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{0}
and
\mathcal{W}_{0}
stand for the
(nonunital) closed subalgebras of
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}
and
\mathcal{W}
consisting of those

c+\hat{h}
for which
c=0
, we obtain the direct sum decompositions

\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{+}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{-}_{0},\qquad\mathcal{W}_{0}=\mathcal{W}^{+}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{-}_{0}.

By
\Pi_{\pm}
we now denote the (bounded) projections of
\mathcal{W}
onto
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{0}

along
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{\mp}_{0}
. Then
\Pi_{+}
and
\Pi_{-}
are complementary
projections. In fact,

(\Pi_{\pm}f)(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\zeta\,\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-(k\pm i0^{+})},

(3.20)

where
f\in\mathcal{W}_{0}\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for some
p\in(1,+\infty)
. These direct sum
decompositions can be coupled by the Fourier transform to the natural direct
sum decomposition of
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}L^{1}(\mathbb{R})&=&L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{-})&\oplus&L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\\
\Big\downarrow\vbox{\hbox to0.0pt{$\mathcal{F}$\hss}}&&\Big\downarrow\vbox{\hbox to0.0pt{$\mathcal{F}$\hss}}&&\Big\downarrow\vbox{\hbox to0.0pt{$\mathcal{F}$\hss}}\\
\mathcal{W}_{0}&=&\mathcal{W}^{-}_{0}&\oplus&\mathcal{W}^{+}_{0}\end{array}

Throughout this article we denote the vector spaces of
n\times m
matrices with
entries in
\mathcal{W}
,
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}
, and
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{0}
by
\mathcal{W}^{n\times m}
,

{\mathcal{W}^{\pm}}^{n\times m}
, and
{\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{0}}^{n\times m}
, respectively. We write

L^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{n\times m}
and
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{\pm})^{n\times m}
for the vector spaces of

n\times m
matrices with entries in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{\pm})
, respectively.
Using a submultiplicative matrix norm, we can turn all of these vector spaces
into Banach spaces. It is then clear that
\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
and

{\mathcal{W}^{\pm}}^{n\times n}
are noncommutative Banach algebras with unit element and

{\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{0}}^{n\times n}
are (nonunital) noncommutative Banach algebras. The
projections
\Pi^{\pm}
can be extended in a natural way to act on matrices of
Wiener algebra elements. Hence, according to (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), for
each
x\in\mathbb{R}
the Faddeev functions
m_{r,l}(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{W}_{+}^{n\times n}
.

For
n=1
the following result is most easily proved using the Gelfand theory
of commutative Banach algebras
[
30
]
. The extension to arbitrary
n
is
elementary, because the determinant and the cofactor matrix of an element of

\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
belong to
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
, respectively.

Theorem 3.2

If for some complex
n\times n
matrix
H_{\infty}
and some

H\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{n\times n}
the Fourier transform

H_{\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,e^{ikz}H(z)
is a nonsingular matrix for
every
k\in\mathbb{R}
and if
\det H_{\infty}\neq 0
, then there exists

K\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{n\times n}
such that

\left[H_{\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,e^{ikz}H(z)\right]^{-1}=(H_{\infty})^{-1}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,e^{ikz}K(z)

for every
k\in\mathbb{R}
.

Using that
m_{r,l}(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{W}_{+}^{n\times n}
, we easily prove with the help
of (
3.4
) that
2ik[I_{n}-A_{r,l}(k)]
belongs to
\mathcal{W}_{+}^{n\times n}
and

2ikB_{r,l}(k)
belongs to
\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
. Assuming the absence of spectral
singularities and to be in the generic case, we easily prove that the reflection
coefficients
R_{r,l}(k)
belong to
\mathcal{W}_{0}^{n\times n}
and the transmission
coefficients
A_{r,l}(k)^{-1}
to
\mathcal{W}_{+}^{n\times n}
. Indeed,

\frac{k}{k+1}A_{r,l}(k)=\frac{k}{k+i}I_{n}-\frac{2ik[I_{n}-A_{r,l}(k)]}{2i(k+i)}

belongs to
\mathcal{W}_{+}^{n\times n}
and therefore, by Theorem
3.2
and

k/(k+i)\in\mathcal{W}
,

A_{r,l}(k)^{-1}=\frac{k}{k+i}\left[\frac{k}{k+i}A_{r,l}(k)\right]^{-1}

belongs to
\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
. Analogously, since

\frac{k}{k+i}B_{r,l}(k)=\frac{2ikB_{r,l}(k)}{2i(k+i)}

belongs to
\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
, we easily see that

R_{r,l}(k)=\left[\frac{k}{k+i}B_{r,l}(k)\right]\left[\frac{k}{k+i}A_{r,l}(k)\right]^{-1}

belongs to
\mathcal{W}_{0}^{n\times n}
.

The exceptional case requires more attention. Assuming that the potential

\boldsymbol{Q}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};(1+|x|)^{2}dx)
, we can differentiate (
3.2
) with respect to

k
and solve the resulting integral equations by iteration. Next, with the help
of (
3.4
) we obtain

A_{r,l}(k)+B_{r,l}(k)=I_{n}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\frac{e^{\mp 2iky}-1}{2ik}\boldsymbol{Q}(y)m_{r,l}(y,k).

This expression satisfies

\lim_{k\to 0^{\pm}}[A_{r,l}(k)+B_{r,l}(k)]=I_{n}\mp\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,y\boldsymbol{Q}(y)m_{r,l}(y,0)=I_{n}\mp E_{r,l},

provided
\boldsymbol{Q}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};(1+|x|)^{2}dx)
. Taking the limit as
k\to 0^{\pm}
we get

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\to 0\\
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\frac{2ikA_{r,l}(k)-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r,l}}{2ik}

\displaystyle=I_{n}-\frac{1}{2i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\boldsymbol{Q}(y)\dot{m}_{r,l}(y,0)

\displaystyle=I_{n}-\frac{1}{2i}G_{r,l},

(3.21a)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to 0^{\pm}}\frac{2ikB_{r,l}(k)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r,l}}{2ik}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\boldsymbol{Q}(y)[\mp ym_{r,l}(y,0)+\tfrac{1}{2i}\dot{m}_{r,l}(y,0)]

\displaystyle=\mp E_{r,l}+\frac{1}{2i}G_{r,l},

(3.21b)

where the overdot indicates differentiation with respect to
k
.

In the superexceptional case, where
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{r,l}=0_{n\times n}
, it is clear that

A_{r,l}\in\mathcal{W}_{+}^{n\times n}
, provided
\boldsymbol{Q}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};(1+|x|)^{2}dx)
. Assuming
the absence of spectral singularities, using the nonsingularity of
A_{r,l}(0)
,
and applying Theorem
3.2
, we get the transmission coefficients

A_{r,l}(k)^{-1}
to belong to
\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
. Since
A_{r,l}(k)+B_{r,l}(k)

belongs to
\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
, we conclude that

R_{r,l}(k)=[A_{r,l}(k)+B_{r,l}(k)]A_{r,l}(k)^{-1}-I_{n}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}^{n\times n}.

At present it is not known if, under the absence of spectral singularities, the
reflection and transmission coefficients belong to
\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
in any
other exceptional case and for general
\boldsymbol{Q}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};(1+|x|)dx)
. Under the
condition
\boldsymbol{Q}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R};(1+|x|)dx)
, the continuity of the reflection and
transmission coefficients at
k=0
is known for
n=1

[
41
]
and for
selfadjoint potentials
[
10
]
.

4
Inverse scattering problem

In this section we introduce the Marchenko integral equations for the matrix
Schrödinger equation (
1.5
) with Faddeev class potential
\boldsymbol{Q}

satisfying (
1.2
). We make use of the hypothesis that the reflection
coefficients
R_{r,l}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}^{n\times n}
, something that we have not been able
to prove in the most general exceptional case. For the sake of simplicity we
assume that the poles of
A_{r,l}(k)^{-1}
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
are simple. The extension
to multiple pole situations is rather technical but straightforward
[
24
]
.
Inverse scattering theory is well documented in the scalar case

[
28
,
22
,
19
,
20
,
2
]
, as it is in the matrix half-line case

[
7
,
12
,
13
]
. Without any details, the matrix full-line case is given in

[
56
]
.

Let us first write the
transmission coefficients
in the form

A_{r}(k)^{-1}=A_{r0}(k)+\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,\frac{\tau_{r;s}}{k-k_{s}},\quad A_{l}(k)^{-1}=A_{l0}(k)+\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,\frac{\tau_{l;s}}{k-k_{s}},

(4.1)

where
k_{1},\ldots,k_{N}
are the distinct simple poles of
A_{r,l}(k)^{-1}

in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
\tau_{r;s}
and
\tau_{l;s}
are the residues of
A_{r}(k)^{-1}
and

A_{l}(k)^{-1}
at
k=k_{s}
(
s=1,\ldots,N
), and
A_{r0}(k)
and
A_{l0}(k)
are
continuous in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
, are analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and tend to
I_{n}
as

k\to\infty
from within
\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
.

Letting
\tau_{r;s}
and
\tau_{r;{\overline{s}}}
(or:
\tau_{l;s}
and
\tau_{l;{\overline{s}}}
)
stand for the residues of
A_{r}(k)^{-1}
(or:
A_{l}(k)^{-1}
) at
k_{s}
and

-k_{s}^{*}
, we get with the help of (
3.11
)

\displaystyle\tau_{r;{\overline{s}}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to-k_{s}^{*}}\,(k+k_{s}^{*})A_{r}(k)^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\lim_{k\to-k_{s}^{*}}\,(k+k_{s}^{*}){A_{l}(-k^{*})^{\dagger}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\left(\lim_{\zeta\to k_{s}}\,(\zeta-k_{s})A_{l}(\zeta)^{-1}\right)^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}=-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\tau_{l;s}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}.

(4.2)

Similarly, we get
\tau_{l;{\overline{s}}}=-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\tau_{r;s}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}
.

Next, let us consider the Riemann-Hilbert problem

\begin{pmatrix}F_{l}(x,-k)&F_{r}(x,-k)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{r}(x,k)&F_{l}(x,k)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{r}(k)^{-1}&-R_{l}(k)\\
-R_{r}(k)&A_{l}(k)^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

(4.3)

where
\det A_{r,l}(k)\neq 0
for
0\neq k\in\mathbb{R}
. In the generic and
superexceptional cases while assuming the absence of spectral singularities,
we write

R_{r}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\alpha\,e^{-ik\alpha}\hat{R}_{r}(\alpha),\qquad R_{l}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\alpha\,e^{ik\alpha}\hat{R}_{l}(\alpha),

(4.4)

where the entries of the
n\times n
matrix functions
\hat{R}_{r,l}(\alpha)

belong to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. In fact, we only need to assume the absence of spectral
singularities as well as
R_{r,l}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}^{n\times n}
for the derivations below
to be valid. Rewriting the left half of (
4.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle e^{ikx}

\displaystyle F_{l}(x,-k)=e^{ikx}F_{r}(x,k)A_{r}(k)^{-1}-e^{-ikx}F_{l}(x,k)e^{2ikx}R_{r}(k)

\displaystyle\!=\!e^{ikx}F_{r}(x,k)A_{r0}(k)+\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,\frac{e^{ikx}F_{r}(x,k)-e^{ik_{s}x}F_{r}(x,k_{s})}{k-k_{s}}\tau_{r;s}

\displaystyle+i\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,e^{2ik_{s}x}\frac{e^{-ik_{s}x}F_{l}(x,k_{s})}{k-k_{s}}N_{r;s}-e^{-ikx}F_{l}(x,k)e^{2ikx}R_{r}(k),

(4.5)

where the
norming constants

N_{r;s}
(
s=1,\ldots,N
) are defined by

F_{r}(x,k_{s})\tau_{r;s}=i\,F_{l}(x,k_{s})N_{r;s},\qquad s=1,\ldots,N.

(4.6)

By the same token, rewriting the right half of (
4.3
) we get

\displaystyle e^{-ikx}

\displaystyle F_{r}(x,-k)=e^{-ikx}F_{l}(x,k)A_{l}(k)^{-1}-e^{ikx}F_{r}(x,k)e^{-2ikx}R_{l}(k)

\displaystyle\!=\!e^{-ikx}F_{l}(x,k)A_{l0}(k)+\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,\frac{e^{-ikx}F_{l}(x,k)-e^{-ik_{s}x}F_{l}(x,k_{s})}{k-k_{s}}\tau_{l;s}

\displaystyle+i\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,e^{-2ik_{s}}\frac{e^{ik_{s}x}F_{r}(x,k_{s})}{k-k_{s}}N_{l;s}-e^{ikx}F_{r}(x,k)e^{-2ikx}R_{l}(k),

(4.7)

where the norming constants
N_{l;s}
(
s=1,\ldots,N
) are defined by

F_{l}(x,k_{s})\tau_{l;s}=i\,F_{r}(x,k_{s})N_{l;s},\qquad s=1,\ldots,N.

(4.8)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
the identities (
4.5
) and (
4.7
) are equations in

\mathcal{W}^{n\times n}
. Using
\Pi_{-}
to project these two equations onto

\mathcal{W}_{-}^{n\times n}
along
\mathcal{W}_{+,0}^{n\times n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle e^{ikx}F_{l}(x,-k)

\displaystyle=I_{n}+i\sum_{s=1}^{N}e^{2ik_{s}x}\frac{e^{-ik_{s}x}F_{l}(x,k_{s})}{k-k_{s}}N_{r;s}

\displaystyle-\Pi_{-}\left[e^{-ikx}F_{l}(x,k)e^{2ikx}R_{r}(k)\right],

\displaystyle e^{-ikx}F_{r}(x,-k)

\displaystyle=I_{n}+i\sum_{s=1}^{N}e^{-2ik_{s}x}\frac{e^{ik_{s}x}F_{r}(x,k_{s})}{k-k_{s}}N_{l;s}

\displaystyle-\Pi_{-}\left[e^{ikx}F_{r}(x,k)e^{-2ikx}R_{l}(k)\right]\!,

respectively. Using (
3.16
), (
4.4
), and the identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}dw\,e^{-ikw}e^{ik_{s}w}=\frac{-i}{k-k_{s}},\qquad s=1,2,\ldots,N,

(4.9)

we strip off the Fourier transforms and arrive at the Marchenko integral
equations

\displaystyle K(x,y)+\Omega_{r}(x+y)

\displaystyle+\int_{x}^{\infty}dz\,K(x,z)\Omega_{r}(z+y)=0_{n\times n},

(4.10a)

\displaystyle J(x,y)+\Omega_{l}(x+y)

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dz\,J(x,z)\Omega_{l}(z+y)=0_{n\times n},

(4.10b)

where the
Marchenko integral kernels
are given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{r}(w)

\displaystyle=\hat{R}_{r}(w)+\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,e^{ik_{s}w}N_{r;s},

(4.11a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{l}(w)

\displaystyle=\hat{R}_{l}(w)+\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,e^{-ik_{s}w}N_{l;s}.

(4.11b)

Indeed, to prove (
4.10a
) and (
4.11a
) we compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\alpha\,e^{-ik\alpha}K(x,x+\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\alpha\,e^{-ik\alpha}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\left[e^{ik_{s}(\alpha+2x)}N_{r;s}+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,e^{ik_{s}(\alpha+\beta+2x)}K(x,x+\beta)N_{r;s}\right]

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\alpha\,e^{-ik\alpha}\left[\hat{R}_{r}(\alpha+2x)+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,K(x,x+\beta)\hat{R}_{r}(\alpha+\beta+2x)\right].

Stripping off the Fourier transform and putting
y=x+\alpha
and
z=x+\beta
, we get
(
4.10a
) and (
4.11a
). Analogously, to prove (
4.10b
) and
(
4.11b
) we compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\alpha\,e^{-ik\alpha}J(x,x-\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\alpha\,e^{-ik\alpha}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\left[e^{-ik_{s}(2x-\alpha)}N_{l;s}\!+\!\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,e^{-ik_{s}(2x-\alpha-\beta)}J(x,x-\beta)N_{l;s}\right]

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\alpha\,e^{-ik\alpha}\left[\hat{R}_{l}(2x-\alpha)+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,J(x,x-\beta)\hat{R}_{l}(2x-\alpha-\beta)\right].

Stripping off the Fourier transform and putting
y=x-\alpha
and
z=x-\beta
, we get
(
4.10b
).

As in
[

]
, we can prove the adjoint symmetry relations

\Omega_{r,l}(w)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\Omega_{r,l}(w)^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3},

(4.12)

thus implying the following symmetry relations for the norming constants:

N_{r;s}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}N_{r;s}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3},\qquad N_{l;s}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}N_{l;s}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}.

(4.13)

For the rather tedious details we refer to Appendix

5
AKNS vs. matrix Schrödinger data

Let us define the Jost solutions of the AKNS system (
1.3
) as those
solutions of (
1.3
) that for
k\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy the asymptotic
relations

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}[I_{n}+o(1)],

\displaystyle x\to+\infty,

(5.1a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}[I_{n}+o(1)],

\displaystyle x\to-\infty.

(5.1b)

Since we are dealing with a first order system, there exist nonsingular matrices

\mathbb{A}_{l}(k)
and
\mathbb{A}_{r}(k)
such that

\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k)=\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k)\mathbb{A}_{r}(k),\qquad\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k)=\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k)\mathbb{A}_{l}(k),

(5.2)

and hence
\mathbb{A}_{l}(k)
and
\mathbb{A}_{r}(k)
are each other’s inverses. Equations
(
5.1
) and (
5.2
) imply the asymptotic relations

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbb{A}_{l}(k)[I_{n}+o(1)],

\displaystyle x\to-\infty,

(5.3a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbb{A}_{r}(k)[I_{n}+o(1)],

\displaystyle x\to+\infty.

(5.3b)

Since the matrix
-ik\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}+\mathcal{Q}
in (
1.3
) has trace
-ik(m_{1}-m_{2})
, the
determinants of
\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k)
and
\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k)
equal
e^{-ik(m_{1}-m_{2})x}
. As
a result,
\det\mathbb{A}_{r,l}(k)=1
.

Let us relate the AKNS and matrix Schrödinger Jost solutions. Putting

\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=I_{m_{1}}\oplus 0_{m_{2}\times m_{2}},\qquad\boldsymbol{e}_{2}=0_{m_{1}\times m_{1}}\oplus I_{m_{2}},

we obtain from the principal asymptotic relations

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k)

\displaystyle=F_{l}(x,-k)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+F_{l}(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{2},

(5.4a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k)

\displaystyle=F_{r}(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+F_{r}(x,-k)\boldsymbol{e}_{2},

(5.4b)

as well as

\displaystyle F_{l}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,-k)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{2},

(5.5a)

\displaystyle F_{r}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,-k)\boldsymbol{e}_{2}.

(5.5b)

Using
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{2}=\boldsymbol{e}_{2}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0_{n\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{2}+\boldsymbol{e}_{2}^{2}=I_{n}
it is easy
to derive (
5.5
) from (
5.4
) and vice versa. Using
(
5.4
) we obtain the triangular representations for the AKNS system

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}+\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,K(x,y)e^{-iky\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}},

(5.6a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,J(x,y)e^{-iky\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}},

(5.6b)

where the kernel functions
K
and
J
coincide with those for the matrix
Schrödinger system.

Let us now relate the AKNS and matrix Schrödinger scattering coefficients.
Let us introduce the standard partitioning

H=\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}&H_{2}\\
H_{3}&H_{4}\end{pmatrix}

(5.7)

of an
n\times n
matrix
H
into the
m_{1}\times m_{1}
matrix
H_{1}
, the

m_{1}\times m_{2}
matrix
H_{2}
, the
m_{2}\times m_{1}
matrix
H_{3}
, and the

m_{2}\times m_{2}
matrix
H_{4}
. Using the asymptotic relations (
3.3
) and
(
5.3
) we obtain for
k\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle e^{ikx}A_{l}(k)+e^{-ikx}B_{l}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{ikx}\mathbb{A}_{l1}(-k)&e^{-ikx}\mathbb{A}_{l2}(k)\\
e^{-ikx}\mathbb{A}_{l3}(-k)&e^{ikx}\mathbb{A}_{l4}(k)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle e^{-ikx}A_{r}(k)+e^{ikx}B_{r}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-ikx}\mathbb{A}_{r1}(k)&e^{ikx}\mathbb{A}_{r2}(-k)\\
e^{ikx}\mathbb{A}_{r3}(k)&e^{-ikx}\mathbb{A}_{r4}(-k)\end{pmatrix}.

Consequently, for
0\neq k\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle A_{l}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{A}_{l1}(-k)&0_{m_{1}\times m_{2}}\\
0_{m_{2}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{A}_{l4}(k)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\quad A_{r}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{A}_{r1}(k)&0_{m_{1}\times m_{2}}\\
0_{m_{2}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{A}_{r4}(-k)\end{pmatrix},

(5.8a)

\displaystyle B_{l}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m_{1}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{A}_{l2}(k)\\
\mathbb{A}_{l3}(-k)&0_{m_{2}\times m_{2}}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\quad B_{r}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m_{1}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{A}_{r2}(-k)\\
\mathbb{A}_{r3}(k)&0_{m_{2}\times m_{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.8b)

Conversely, for
0\neq k\in\mathbb{R}
we get in terms of blocks of
A_{r,l}(k)
and

B_{r,l}(k)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{l}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A_{l1}(k)&B_{l2}(k)\\
B_{l3}(-k)&A_{l4}(k)\end{pmatrix},

(5.9a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{r}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A_{r1}(k)&B_{r2}(-k)\\
B_{r3}(k)&A_{r4}(-k)\end{pmatrix}.

(5.9b)

Since
\mathbb{A}_{l}(k)
and
\mathbb{A}_{r}(k)
are continuous in
k\in\mathbb{R}
and tend to
I_{n}
as

k\to\pm\infty
, we see that
A_{l}(k)
,
B_{l}(k)
,
A_{r}(k)
, and
B_{r}(k)
are
continuous in
k\in\mathbb{R}
and have finite limits as
k\to\pm\infty
. Of course,
the block entries of
\mathbb{A}_{l,r}(k)
have all the usual continuity and analyticity
properties, also at
k=0
. Thus, we are always in the exceptional case. We are
in the superexceptional case iff
k=0
is not a spectral singularity of the AKNS
system (
1.3
). Further, the matrix Schrödinger reflection
coefficients are given by

\displaystyle R_{l}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m_{1}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{R}_{l1}(k)\\
\mathbb{R}_{l4}(k)&0_{m_{2}\times m_{2}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m_{1}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{A}_{l2}(k)\mathbb{A}_{l4}(k)^{-1}\\
\mathbb{A}_{l3}(-k)\mathbb{A}_{l1}(-k)^{-1}&0_{m_{2}\times m_{2}}\end{pmatrix},

(5.10a)

\displaystyle R_{r}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m_{1}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{R}_{r4}(k)\\
\mathbb{R}_{r1}(k)&0_{m_{2}\times m_{2}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m_{1}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{A}_{r2}(-k)\mathbb{A}_{r4}(-k)^{-1}\\
\mathbb{A}_{r3}(k)\mathbb{A}_{r1}(k)^{-1}&0_{m_{2}\times m_{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.10b)

Finally, the AKNS system is reflectionless iff the matrix Schrödinger
system is reflectionless.

Let
\tau_{r;s}
and
\tau_{l;s}
be the residues of
A_{r}(k)^{-1}
and

A_{l}(k)^{-1}
at the simple pole
k_{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
. Now let
\tau_{l4;s}
and

\tau_{r1;s}
be the residues of
\mathbb{A}_{l4}(k)^{-1}
and
\mathbb{A}_{r1}(k)^{-1}
at

k=k_{s}
and
\tau_{l1;s}
and
\tau_{r4;s}
the residues of
\mathbb{A}_{l1}(k)^{-1}

and
\mathbb{A}_{r4}(k)^{-1}
at the simple pole
k=-k_{s}
. Then (
5.8a
) implies
that

\tau_{r;s}=\tau_{r1;s}\oplus(-\tau_{r4;s}),\qquad\tau_{l;s}=(-\tau_{l1;s})\oplus\tau_{l4;s}.

Let us define the AKNS norming constants as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k_{s})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\tau_{r1;s}

\displaystyle=i\,\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k_{s})\boldsymbol{e}_{2}\mathbb{N}_{r1;s},

(5.11a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,-k_{s})\boldsymbol{e}_{2}\tau_{r4;s}

\displaystyle=-i\,\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,-k_{s})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\mathbb{N}_{r4;s},

(5.11b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,k_{s})\boldsymbol{e}_{2}\tau_{l4;s}

\displaystyle=i\,\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,k_{s})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\mathbb{N}_{l4;s},

(5.11c)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{l}(x,-k_{s})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\tau_{l1;s}

\displaystyle=-i\,\mathbb{F}_{r}(x,-k_{s})\boldsymbol{e}_{2}\mathbb{N}_{l1;s}.

(5.11d)

We then obtain

\displaystyle N_{r;s}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m_{1}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{N}_{r4;s}\\
\mathbb{N}_{r1;s}&0_{m_{2}\times m_{2}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad N_{l;s}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m_{1}\times m_{1}}&\mathbb{N}_{l1;s}\\
\mathbb{N}_{l4;s}&0_{m_{2}\times m_{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.12)

6
Time evolution

In this section we establish the time evolution of the scattering data of the
matrix Schrödinger equation and the accompanying AKNS system. We then go
on to derive, in either case, the Marchenko integral kernels as a function of
time. These results allow us, in the next section, to derive the reflectionless
solutions of the nonlocal integrable equation (
2.3
) and hence of the
focusing matrix NLS equation.

6.1
Matrix Schrödinger time evolution

The Lax pair
\{\mathcal{L},A\}
for the matrix Schrödinger equation (
2.3
)
is given by (
2.1
), where
\mathcal{B}_{0}=\boldsymbol{Q}
. Equation (
2.3
) is compatible
with the linear system

\mathcal{L}v=k^{2}v,\qquad v_{t}=Av,

where
\mathcal{L}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+\boldsymbol{Q}
. We may therefore write

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=Av=2i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}v_{xx}-2i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}v_{x}-i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}v

\displaystyle=2i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\boldsymbol{Q}-k^{2}\boldsymbol{1})v-2i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}v_{x}-i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}v

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\left\{(\boldsymbol{Q}-2k^{2}\boldsymbol{1})v-2\mathcal{Q}v_{x}\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{1}
stands for the identity operator on a suitable function space.
Next, we compute

\displaystyle(v_{x})_{t}

\displaystyle=(Av)_{x}=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\left((\boldsymbol{Q}-2k^{2}\boldsymbol{1})v_{x}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}v-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}v_{x}-2\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{Q}-k^{2}\boldsymbol{1})v\right)

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\left(\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-2\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}+2k^{2}\mathcal{Q}\right)v+i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\boldsymbol{Q}-2k^{2}\boldsymbol{1}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x})v_{x}.

Hence, putting
\boldsymbol{V}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}v\\
v_{x}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

we get the linear system

\boldsymbol{V}_{x}=\boldsymbol{X}(x,k;t)\boldsymbol{V},\qquad\boldsymbol{V}_{t}=\boldsymbol{T}(x,k;t)\boldsymbol{V},

(6.1)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}(x,k;t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0_{n\times n}&I_{n}\\
\boldsymbol{Q}(x;t)-k^{2}I_{n}&0_{n\times n}\end{pmatrix},

(6.2a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}(x,k;t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\boldsymbol{Q}-2k^{2}I_{n})&-2i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}\\
i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-2\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}+2k^{2}\mathcal{Q})&i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\boldsymbol{Q}-2k^{2}I_{n}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x})\end{pmatrix}.

(6.2b)

Then we easily compute

i(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3})\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{T}_{x}+\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{X}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\\
\boldsymbol{E}_{21}&0_{n\times n}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}_{21}

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}-2(\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q})_{x}+2k^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{x}

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\boldsymbol{Q}-k^{2}I_{n})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}(\boldsymbol{Q}-2k^{2}I_{n})+(\boldsymbol{Q}-2k^{2}I_{n}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x})(\boldsymbol{Q}-k^{2}I_{n})

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}+\boldsymbol{Q}^{2}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}-2\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-4\mathcal{Q}_{x}\boldsymbol{Q}

\displaystyle+k^{2}\left(2\mathcal{Q}_{x}+2\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}+\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{Q}-2\boldsymbol{Q}+2\mathcal{Q}_{x}\right)

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}+\boldsymbol{Q}^{2}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}-2\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-2\left(\boldsymbol{Q}^{2}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\right)

\displaystyle=i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}-\boldsymbol{Q}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}-2\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}_{x},

as claimed. Thus the zero curvature condition for the AKNS pair
\{\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{T}\}

is equivalent to the nonlinear evolution equation (
2.3
).

There exist nonsingular matrices
C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)
and
C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;t)
not
depending on
k\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\boldsymbol{F}_{l}(x,k;t)=\boldsymbol{V}(x,k;t)C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)^{-1},\qquad\boldsymbol{F}_{r}(x,k;t)=\boldsymbol{V}(x,k;t)C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;t)^{-1}.

Then a simple differentiation yields

\displaystyle\left[C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)\right]_{t}C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F}_{l}^{-1}\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{l}-\boldsymbol{F}_{l}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{F}_{l}]_{t},

\displaystyle\left[C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;t)\right]_{t}C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;t)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F}_{r}^{-1}\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{r}-\boldsymbol{F}_{r}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{F}_{r}]_{t},

where the two left-hand sides do not depend on
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We may therefore
compute the
x\to\pm\infty
limits of the two right-hand sides and obtain

\left[C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)\right]_{t}C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)^{-1}=\left[C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;t)\right]_{t}C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;t)^{-1}=-2ik^{2}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}).

Consequently,

\displaystyle C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)

\displaystyle=\left[e^{-2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\oplus e^{-2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\right]C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;0),

(6.3a)

\displaystyle C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;t)

\displaystyle=\left[e^{-2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\oplus e^{-2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\right]C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;0).

(6.3b)

Relating
\boldsymbol{F}_{r,l}(x,k;t)
by means of the equalities

\boldsymbol{F}_{r}(x,k;t)=\boldsymbol{F}_{l}(x,k;t)\boldsymbol{A}_{r}(k;t),\qquad\boldsymbol{F}_{l}(x,k;t)=\boldsymbol{F}_{r}(x,k;t)\boldsymbol{A}_{l}(k;t),

where the factors
\boldsymbol{A}_{r,l}(k;t)
are given by the matrices [cf. (
3.5
)]

\boldsymbol{A}_{r}(k;t)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{r}(k;t)&B_{r}(-k;t)\\
B_{r}(k;t)&A_{r}(-k;t)\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{A}_{l}(k;t)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{l}(-k;t)&B_{l}(k;t)\\
B_{l}(-k;t)&A_{l}(k;t)\end{pmatrix},

we compute

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{A}_{r}]_{t}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{F}_{l}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{F}_{l}]_{t}\boldsymbol{F}_{l}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}_{r}+\boldsymbol{F}_{l}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{F}_{r}]_{t}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{F}_{l}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{l}-\boldsymbol{F}_{l}\left[C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)\right]_{t}C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{A}_{r}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{F}_{l}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{r}-\boldsymbol{F}_{r}\left[C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;t)\right]_{t}C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{r}}(k;t)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)\right]_{t}C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{r}-\boldsymbol{A}_{r}\left[C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)\right]_{t}C_{\boldsymbol{F}_{l}}(k;t)^{-1}

\displaystyle=2ik^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3})-(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3})\boldsymbol{A}_{r}\right).

Using that
\boldsymbol{A}_{r,l}(k;t)
are each other’s inverses, we get

\boldsymbol{A}_{r,l}(k;t)=\left[e^{-2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\oplus e^{-2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\right]\boldsymbol{A}_{r,l}(k;0)\left[e^{2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\oplus e^{2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\right].

Therefore,

\displaystyle A_{r,l}(k;t)

\displaystyle=e^{-2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}A_{r,l}(k;0)e^{2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}},

(6.4a)

\displaystyle B_{r,l}(k;t)

\displaystyle=e^{-2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}B_{r,l}(k;0)e^{2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}.

(6.4b)

For the reflection coefficients we get

R_{r,l}(k;t)=e^{-2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}R_{r,l}(k;0)e^{2ik^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}.

(6.5)

Moreover, if
k_{s}
is a simple pole of
A_{r,l}(k;t)
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, (
6.4a
)
implies that

\tau_{r;s}(t)=e^{-2ik_{s}^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\tau_{r;s}(0)e^{2ik_{s}^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}},\qquad\tau_{l;s}(t)=e^{-2ik_{s}^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}\tau_{l;s}(0)e^{2ik_{s}^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}.

As a result,

[\tau_{r;s}]_{t}=2ik_{s}^{2}\left(\tau_{r;s}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\tau_{r;s}\right),\qquad[\tau_{l;s}]_{t}=2ik_{s}^{2}\left(\tau_{l;s}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\tau_{l;s}\right).

(6.6)

Let us now study the time evolution of the norming constants corresponding to
a simple pole
k_{s}
of
A_{r}(k;t)^{-1}
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. Equations (
6.3
) imply

[\boldsymbol{F}_{r,l}]_{t}=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{r,l}+2ik^{2}\boldsymbol{F}_{r,l}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}).

(6.7)

Now recall (
4.6
) and (
4.8
) in the form

\displaystyle F_{r}(x,k_{s};t)\tau_{r;s}

\displaystyle=iF_{l}(x,k_{s};t)N_{r;s}(t),

(6.8a)

\displaystyle F_{l}(x,k_{s};t)\tau_{l;s}

\displaystyle=iF_{r}(x,k_{s};t)N_{l;s}(t).

(6.8b)

Using the right/left upper block of (
6.7
) for
k=k_{s}
to differentiate
(
6.8
) with respect to
t
for
k=k_{s}
and then substituting (
6.6
)
and (
6.8
), we get

\displaystyle F_{l}(x,k_{s};t)\left\{[N_{r;s}]_{t}-2ik_{s}^{2}\left(N_{r;s}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}N_{r;s}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=0_{n\times n},

\displaystyle F_{r}(x,k_{s};t)\left\{[N_{l;s}]_{t}-2ik_{s}^{2}\left(N_{l;s}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}N_{l;s}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=0_{n\times n}.

Using (
3.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle[N_{r;s}]_{t}

\displaystyle=2ik_{s}^{2}\left(N_{r;s}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}N_{r;s}(t)\right),

\displaystyle[N_{l;s}]_{t}

\displaystyle=2ik_{s}^{2}\left(N_{l;s}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}N_{l;s}(t)\right).

Consequently,

\displaystyle N_{r;s}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-2ik_{s}^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}N_{r;s}(0)e^{2ik_{s}^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}},

(6.9a)

\displaystyle N_{l;s}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-2ik_{s}^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}N_{l;s}(0)e^{2ik_{s}^{2}t\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}}.

(6.9b)

Appendix A
Deriving the nonlocal integrable equation

In this appendix we present an alternative proof of (
2.3
). Indeed, let us
start from the matrix NLS equation

i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}_{t}+\mathcal{Q}_{xx}-2\mathcal{Q}^{3}=0_{n\times n},

where we seek solutions
\mathcal{Q}
that anticommute with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}
. Putting

\boldsymbol{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{2}+\mathcal{Q}_{x},

so that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}+\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{x}+\mathcal{Q}_{xx},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{xx}\mathcal{Q}+2\mathcal{Q}_{x}^{2}+\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{xx}+\mathcal{Q}_{xxx},

we obtain

\displaystyle-i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}

\displaystyle=-(i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}_{t})\mathcal{Q}+\mathcal{Q}(i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}_{t})-(i\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{Q}_{t})_{x}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{Q}_{xx}-2\mathcal{Q}^{3})\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{Q}_{xx}-2\mathcal{Q}^{3})+\mathcal{Q}_{xxx}-2(\mathcal{Q}^{3})_{x}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{xx}\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{xx}+\mathcal{Q}_{xxx}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}^{2}-2\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}-2\mathcal{Q}^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{x}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{x})\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{x})

\displaystyle+(\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}-\mathcal{Q}_{xx}\mathcal{Q}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}^{2}-\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{xx})-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}^{2}-2\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}-2\mathcal{Q}^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{x}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}

\displaystyle-3\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}^{2}-2\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{x}-\mathcal{Q}_{xx}\mathcal{Q}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}^{2}-\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}_{xx}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}-\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}^{2}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}^{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}-2\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}\mathcal{Q}^{2}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}^{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Q}_{xx}-2\mathcal{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}_{x}-2\mathcal{Q}_{x}\boldsymbol{Q},

where
\mathcal{Q}_{x}=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3})
and hence

\mathcal{Q}(x;t)=-\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{Q}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{3}\right).

Here the integrand depends on
(y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(
2.3
) by a more direct method.

Theorem B.3

(\boldsymbol{A}_{l},\boldsymbol{B}_{l},\boldsymbol{C}_{l})
be a matrix triplet satisfying (
7.3
) for which
the Sylvester equation (
7.5
) has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{P}_{l}
. Then the
matrix function
\boldsymbol{Q}(x;t)
defined by

\boldsymbol{Q}(x;t)=-4\boldsymbol{C}_{l}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{l}(x;t)^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{l}e^{-2x\boldsymbol{A}_{l}}e^{-4it\tilde{\sigma}_{3}\boldsymbol{A}_{r}^{2}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{l}(x;t)^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{l}

satisfies the nonlinear evolution equation (
2.3
) in those
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

for which the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{l}(x;t)
is nonsingular.
