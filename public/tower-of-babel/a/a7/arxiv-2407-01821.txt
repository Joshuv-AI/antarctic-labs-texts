Fluctuation-Response Relation in Non-Equilibrium Systems and Active Matter
T.R. Kirkpatrick 1 and D. Belitz 2,3

Abstract
We use dynamic equations to derive a relation between correlation functions and response or relaxation functions in many-body systems. The relation is very general and holds both in equilibrium, when the usual fluctuation-dissipation theorem is valid, and to linear order in an expansion about arbitrary non-equilibrium states, when it is not. We illustrate our results by discussing fluids, both in equilibrium and non-equilibrium states, as well as several active-matter systems.

I
Introduction

Important concepts in many-body systems include the correlations of spontaneous fluctuations of
observables about a given state, and the response of observables to external perturbations. The
former are described by time correlation functions; the latter, to linear order in the external fields,
by (linear) response functions. The response functions also describe the energy dissipated by the
system
[
1
]
. For systems in thermodynamic equilibrium both functions are
translationally invariant with respect to both time and space, and there is a simple relation between
them known as the fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT)
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
,
see Sec.
II.3
below. That is, the spontaneous fluctuations in equilibrium
determine the linear response of the system to external fields, as well as its relaxation towards
equilibrium once the external fields have been turned off. The correlation functions are directly
measurable by light (or neutron) scattering. This can be difficult if low temperatures or/and
small scattering angles are required. The response or relaxation functions, by contrast, are measurable via
macroscopic perturbations that one has experimental control of irrespective of the temperature.
Apart from being of fundamental interest, the FDT thus provides an alternative way of probing
correlations in equilibrium many-body systems.

For systems that are not in thermal equilibrium, or for non-thermal systems that are not coupled
to a heat bath, the standard FDT does not hold. Examples include fluids in the presence of an externally
imposed temperature gradient, fluids with turbulent flow, and active matter. Many of these display
interesting correlations that can be very hard to probe directly. There is a large body of work on
non-equilibrium fluctuations (see, e.g., Refs.
5
,
6
,
7
and
references therein), on non-equilibrium linear response
[
8
,
9
]
,
and on relations between the two, with various applications
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
7
,
16
]
.
Generally, such relations are desirable since
the response of a system to external perturbations is usually relatively easy to control and measure.
In this paper we point out a particularly simple relation between correlation functions and bilinear
products of linear response functions in non-equilibrium systems. This fluctuation-response relation
takes the form of a structural isomorphism rather than an identity. In addition to its simplicity, its
significance is that it
always
holds to linear order in an expansion about an arbitrary state,
irrespective of how badly the standard FDT is violated. It thus provides an alternative way of
probing correlations that is valid very generally.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we define the relevant correlation and
response functions, and discuss the FDT. In Sec.
III
we derive an isomorphism between
correlation functions and bilinear response functions that is generally valid and the main
result of the paper. In Sec.
IV
we discuss various examples, and in Sec.
V

we conclude with a summary and a general discussion.

II
Correlation functions and response functions

Consider observables
A_{i}(x)

(i=1,2,\ldots)
, where
x=({\bm{x}},t)
comprises spatial position
{\bm{x}}

and time
t
. Let the average values be
\langle A_{i}\rangle
, and let
\delta A_{i}=A_{i}-\langle A_{i}\rangle
.
In a classical system,
\langle\ldots\rangle
is a statistical mechanics average taken with an appropriate
distribution function; in a quantum mechanical system, it denotes a quantum mechanical expectation value
plus a statistical mechanics average. In an equilibrium state the averages are constants independent of
space and time; in general they are space and time dependent. We define temporal and spatial Fourier transforms

\displaystyle\delta A_{i}({\bm{x}},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}\,\delta A_{i}(x)\ ,

(1a)

\displaystyle\delta A_{i}({\bm{k}},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d{\bm{x}}\,e^{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\,\delta A_{i}(x)\ .

(1b)

with
{\bm{k}}
the wave vector and
\omega
the frequency. We use
\int dt
to
indicate an integral over all times, with analogous notation in temporal Fourier space, and

\int d{\bm{x}}
a spatial integral over the system volume
V
.

In what follows we recall the definitions of correlation functions, which describe the correlations of the

\delta A_{i}
, and of response functions, which describe the response of
\langle A_{i}\rangle
to external
fields. Alternatively, the response functions describe the relaxation of the
\langle A_{i}\rangle
from initial
values induced by the fields.

II.1
Correlation functions

In classical systems, the two-point correlation function is the van Hove function

S_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\delta A_{i}(x)\,\delta A_{j}(x^{\prime})\rangle\ ,

(2)

with analogous expressions in Fourier space. In quantum systems, one needs to distinguish between the
symmetrized, or anticommutator, correlation function

C_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta A_{i}(x)\,\delta A_{j}(x^{\prime})+\delta A_{j}(x^{\prime})\,\delta A_{i}(x)\rangle\ ,

(3)

and the antisymmetrized, or commutator one
[
17
]

\chi_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\hbar}\langle\delta A_{i}(x)\,\delta A_{j}(x^{\prime})-\delta A_{j}(x^{\prime})\,\delta A_{i}(x)\rangle\ .

(4)

In the presence of space and time-translational invariance one has

\varphi_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})=\varphi_{ij}(x-x^{\prime})\ ,

(5a)

where
\varphi
can stand for
S
,
C
, or
\chi
. In Fourier space this becomes

\varphi_{ij}({\bm{k}},{\bm{k}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})=2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\,V\delta_{{\bm{k}},-{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}\,\varphi_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)\ ,

(5b)

with obvious modifications if only space or time-translational invariance is present.

The relation between
S
, or
C
, and
\chi
is complicated and known only for certain
special cases. In thermal equilibrium, when all of the correlation functions have structures
given by Eq. (
5b
), the relation is
[
1
,
18
]

C_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\hbar\,\coth(\hbar\omega/2T)\,\chi^{\prime\prime}_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)\ ,

(6a)

with
T
the temperature.
\chi^{\prime\prime}
is the spectral density of the commutator correlation
function as defined in Eq. (
82d
). In the classical limit this simplifies to

S_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)=(2T/\omega)\,\chi^{\prime\prime}_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)\ .

(6b)

By integrating over all frequencies we obtain

S_{ij}^{0}({\bm{k}})=T\chi_{ij}^{0}({\bm{k}})\ .

(7a)

Here
S^{0}
is the equal-time van Hove function and

\chi_{ij}^{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\chi^{+}_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega=0)=\chi_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};z=i\epsilon)

(7b)

is the static commutator correlation function. Here and throughout the paper we denote by

g^{\pm}(t)=g(t)\,\Theta(\pm t)\ ,

with
\Theta
the step function, the positive and negative-time parts of time-dependent functions

g(t)
, see Appendix
A
. The final expression in Eq. (
7b
) is the Laplace transform
as defined in Eq. (
82b
), with
\epsilon>0
infinitesimal.
We emphasize that Eqs. (
6
,
7a
) by themselves represent relations between
two different types of correlation functions. Their usual interpretation as the FDT requires
the identification of
\chi
with a response function. For non-equilibrium systems one needs
to carefully distinguish between commutator correlation functions and response functions,
and we will discuss this distinction in the following subsections.

II.2
Response functions

II.2.1
The initial-value problem, and relaxation functions

External fields or perturbations that couple to the observables will produce
a change
\delta\langle A_{i}\rangle
of the average values:

a_{i}(x)\equiv\delta\langle A_{i}(x)\rangle=\langle A_{i}(x)\rangle-\langle A_{i}(x)\rangle_{0}
,
with
\langle\ldots\rangle_{0}
the average in the absence of the perturbations.
Suppose the perturbations are switched off at
t=0
,
at which time they have created initial values
a_{i}^{0}({\bm{x}})\equiv a_{i}({\bm{x}},t=0)
. Then for

t>0
the observables will relax toward their average values in the absence of the perturbations
according to

a_{i}^{+}(x)=\sum_{j}\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\,\phi_{ij}^{+}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};t)\,a_{j}^{0}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ .

(8)

The
\phi_{ij}^{+}
are relaxation functions
[
19
]
.

II.2.2
Response to an external field

Let
h_{i}(x)
be external fields that are conjugate to the variables in the sense that they couple
linearly to the variables in a Hamiltonian or free-energy functional
[
20
]
. Then the
change
a_{i}
of
\langle A_{i}\rangle
in response to the fields
h_{j}
, to linear order in the fields, is given by
response functions
X_{ij}
via
[
21
,
18
,
22
]

a_{i}(x)=\int dx^{\prime}\,\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\,\sum_{j}\,X_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})\,h_{j}(x^{\prime})\ .

(9)

Here
\int dx^{\prime}=\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\int dt^{\prime}
, and the step function ensures causality.

For many applications,
X_{ij}
is time translationally invariant, at least in an approximate
sense
[
23
]
. For the discussion of response functions, we will assume
that this is the case; generalizations will be the subject of a separate investigation.
Equation (
9
) then becomes

a_{i}({\bm{x}},t)=\int dx^{\prime}\sum_{j}\,X_{ij}^{+}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};t-t^{\prime})\,h_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(10a)

or, after a temporal Fourier transform,

a_{i}({\bm{x}},\omega)=\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\sum_{j}X_{ij}^{+}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)\,h_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},\omega)\ .

(10b)

II.2.3
Relation between response and relaxation

A relation between response and relaxation can be established as follows
[
1
,
18
]
.
Consider fields that are switched on infinitely slowly in the distant past and switched off at time
t=0
:

h_{i}({\bm{x}},t)=h_{i}({\bm{x}})\,e^{\epsilon t}\,\Theta(-t)\ ,

(11a)

or

h_{i}({\bm{x}},\omega)=h_{i}({\bm{x}})\,\frac{1}{i\omega+\epsilon}\ ,

(11b)

with
\epsilon>0
infinitesimal. For negative times,
Eq. (
10a
) yields

\displaystyle a_{i}^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\int dx^{\prime}\,e^{-\epsilon t^{\prime}}\,X^{+}_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};t^{\prime})\,h_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\,e^{\epsilon t}

(12a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\,X_{ij}^{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\,h_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},t)\ ,

or

a_{i}^{-}({\bm{x}},\omega)=\frac{1}{i\omega+\epsilon}\sum_{j}\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\,X_{ij}^{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\,h_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ ,

(12b)

with

X_{ij}^{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=X^{+}_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega=0)=X_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};z=i\epsilon)

(12c)

the static response function. At time
t=0
the fields have produced values of the observables given by

a_{i}^{0}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{j}\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\,X_{ij}^{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\,h_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ .

(13)

For positive times, the
a_{i}
will relax from these initial values, see Eq. (
8
). From Eqs. (
10b
),
(
12b
), and (
13
) we obtain

\displaystyle a_{i}^{+}({\bm{x}},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i\omega}\sum_{j}\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\left[X^{+}(\omega)(X^{0})^{-1}-1\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}^{\prime}})\,

(14a)

\displaystyle\hskip 90.0pt\times a_{j}^{0}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ .

where the term in square brackets is to be interpreted in a matrix sense. The relaxation functions
from Eq. (
8
) are thus related to the response functions via

\phi_{ij}^{+}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)=\frac{1}{i\omega}\,\left[X^{+}(\omega)(X^{0})^{-1}-1\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}^{\prime}})\ .

(14b)

Note that Eq. (
14a
) contains the same information as Eq. (
10b
), but it requires
knowledge of the initial values of the observables only; not of the nature of the external fields that
have produced the initial values.

II.3
The fluctuation-dissipation theorem and its validity

In general, there is no obvious simple relation between the response functions
X
and
the various correlation functions. However, in thermal equilibrium, when
C_{ij}
and
\chi_{ij}

have structures given by Eqs. (
5
), the commutator correlation functions determine
the response functions via

X^{+}_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\chi^{+}_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)\ .

(15a)

Given this identity, it is natural to define response functions at negative times
[
24
]
via

X^{-}_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega):=\chi^{-}_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)\ .

(15b)

The Laplace transforms in the negative half plane, and the spectral densities
\chi^{\prime\prime}
and
X^{\prime\prime}
,
for both
\chi
and
X
are then defined via Eqs. (
82
) and we have

X_{ij}^{\prime\prime}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\chi_{ij}^{\prime\prime}({\bm{k}},\omega)=-\chi_{ji}^{\prime\prime}({\bm{k}},-\omega)\ .

(15c)

and in particular

X_{ij}^{0}({\bm{k}})=\chi_{ij}^{0}({\bm{k}})\ .

(15d)

The relations (
6
,
7a
) then turn into the FDT. That is, they relate fluctuations,
as described by the correlation functions
S
or
C
, to the response to external perturbations, and
the related dissipation, as described by
X=\chi
. For the purposes of this paper we just state the
FDT for classical systems:

\displaystyle S_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)=(2T/\omega)\,X^{\prime\prime}_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)\ .

(16a)

\displaystyle S_{ij}^{0}({\bm{k}})=TX_{ij}^{0}({\bm{k}})\ .

(16b)

The above statements in general hold only for systems in thermal equilibrium. For non-equilibrium
systems Eq. (
15a
) does in general not hold, which invalidates Eq. (
16
). Other aspects
of the FDT also break down; for instance, the
\coth
factor in Eq. (
6a
) relies both on a constant
temperature throughout the system and on the distribution function being the equilibrium one, but for the
examples we will consider the breakdown of Eq. (
15a
) is the most crucial aspect of non-equilibrium.

In Secs.
III
and
IV
we will show that nonetheless there still is a simple relation between
correlation functions and response functions in non-equilibrium systems, and also in non-thermal systems
where no temperature concept exists.

III
Dynamic equations

We now turn to the dynamic equations that govern the time evolution of observables. Consider an arbitrary,
in general time dependent, state. Linearizing about this state
[
25
]
leads to an equation
of motion of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\,\delta A_{i}(x)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\int dx^{\prime}\,\lambda^{\text{r}}_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})\,\delta A_{j}(x^{\prime})

(17a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\int dx^{\prime}\,b_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})\,h_{j}(x^{\prime})+f_{i}(x)\ .\qquad\ \

The matrix
\lambda_{ij}
contains couplings between the variables, as well as relaxation rates,
or memory functions, and

\lambda^{\text{r}}_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})=\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\,\lambda_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})

(17b)

is the retarded part of these functions. The
f_{i}
in Eq. (
17a
) are stochastic, or Langevin, forces whose
average is zero. For a system in thermal equilibrium they describe thermal fluctuations. More generally,
they describe noise that is inherent to the dynamics of the system, such as the random stirring in a stirred
fluid, or imprecisions in the propagation of particles in active matter. We will assume them to be Gaussian
distributed with second moments

\langle f_{i}(x)\,f_{j}(x^{\prime})\rangle=\Delta_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})\ ,

(18a)

or, in temporal Fourier space,

\langle f_{i}({\bm{x}},\omega)\,f_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle=\Delta_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})\ .

(18b)

The
b_{ij}
describe the coupling of the dynamic variables to the external fields. They will be discussed
in Sec.
III.2.2
.

Note that the system of equations (
17a
) is linear by construction; the average values

\langle A_{i}\rangle
that one expands about are in general described by nonlinear equations.

III.1
Correlations

In the absence of external fields we formally solve Eq. (
17a
) for
\delta A
and express
the correlation functions in terms of the correlations of the Langevin forces. For simplicity, we will
consider classical systems unless otherwise noted; a generalization to anticommutator correlations
instead of van Hove correlations is straightforward. We define a temporal Fourier transform of

\lambda^{\text{r}}
by

\lambda_{ij}^{\text{r}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}\int dt^{\prime}\,e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\,\lambda_{ij}^{\text{r}}(x,x^{\prime})\ .

(19)

In the absence of fields, Eq. (
17a
) then becomes

\sum_{j}\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\int d\omega^{\prime}\,G_{ij}^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})\,\delta A_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})=f_{i}({\bm{x}},\omega)\ .

(20a)

Here

\displaystyle G_{ij}^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\omega\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\,\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})

(20b)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{ij}^{\text{r}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})\ ,

is the inverse of a propagator
G
that is defined by

\displaystyle\sum_{l}\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\hskip-20.0pt\int d\omega^{\prime\prime}\,G_{il}^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime\prime})\,G_{lj}({\bm{x}}^{\prime\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega^{\prime\prime},\omega^{\prime})

(20c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}\,\delta({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\,\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\ .

The formal solution of Eq. (
20a
) thus is

\delta A_{i}({\bm{x}},\omega)=\sum_{j}\int d{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\int d\omega^{\prime}\,G_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})\,f_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\ .

(21)

For the matrix of correlation functions this yields

S_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\left[G\,\Delta\,G^{\text{T}}\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})\ ,

(22)

with the expression in brackets to be interpreted as a matrix product, and
G^{\text{T}}
the transposed
propagator
[
26
]
. In quantum systems, one obtains the same structure for the anticommutator
correlation function
C
.

Equation (
22
) holds in complete generality. If we assume time translational invariance we have

\langle f_{i}({\bm{x}},t)\,f_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=\Delta_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};t-t^{\prime})

(23a)

or

\langle f_{i}({\bm{x}},\omega)\,f_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle=2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\Delta_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)\rangle\ ,

(23b)

and

G_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\,G_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)

(24a)

with
G
the inverse of

G_{ij}^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)=-i\omega\,\delta_{ij}\delta({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime})+\lambda^{+}_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)\ .

(24b)

Note that
\lambda^{+}(\omega)=\lambda(z=\omega+i\epsilon)
, see Eq. (
82c
).
This yields for the correlation functions

S_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)=\left[G(\omega)\,\Delta(\omega)\,G^{\dagger}(\omega)\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ ,

(25)

with the adjoint
G^{\dagger}
the complex conjugate of
G^{\text{T}}
.

If the system is translationally invariant in space as well, we have

S_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\left[G({\bm{k}},\omega)\,\Delta({\bm{k}},\omega)\,G^{\dagger}({\bm{k}},\omega)\right]_{ij}\ .

(26)

We note that in equilibrium there is no freedom for choosing the Langevin-force correlations
\Delta_{ij}
; they
are dictated by the FDT in the sense that Eq. (
26
) must yields the correct equilibrium equal-time
correlation functions
S_{ij}^{0}
. More generally, the
\Delta_{ij}
are part of what characterizes the
nonequilibrium/nonthermal nature of the system.

III.2
Linear Response

To discuss the response, we average Eq. (

As in Sec.

The initial-value problem

Suppose initial values

\displaystyle-i\omega\,a_{i}^{+}({\bm{x}},\omega)\hskip-4.0pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{i}^{0}({\bm{x}})\ ,

or

Response to an external field

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\,|a(t))

\displaystyle\qquad=\int dt^{\prime}\,\lambda^{+}(t-t^{\prime})\,|a^{-}(t^{\prime}))\ .\qquad

\displaystyle G^{-1}(\omega)\,|a(\omega))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}(\omega)\,|a^{-}(\omega))

\displaystyle\left(G^{-1}(\omega)+i\omega\right)\frac{1}{i\omega+\epsilon}\,|a^{0})\ .

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i\omega}\,\left(1+i\omega\,G(\omega)\right)\,|a^{0})\ ,

\displaystyle|a^{+}(\omega))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\omega)\,|a^{0})\ .

Alternatively, we can use Eq. (

) to write

|a(\omega))=G(\omega)\,b(\omega)\,|h(\omega))

(37a)

with the elements of the matrix

\displaystyle b_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\lambda^{+}(\omega)\,X^{0}\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})

(37b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\left(G^{-1}(\omega)+i\omega\right)X^{0}\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ .\qquad\quad

By comparing with Eq. (
9
) we find for the matrix of response functions

\displaystyle X^{+}_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle X_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};z=\omega+i\epsilon)

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[G(\omega)\,b(\omega)\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\left(1+i\omega\,G(\omega)\right)X^{0}\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ .\qquad

Solving Eq. (
38
) for
G
we see that Eq. (
36b
) is identical to
Eq. (
14a
), which was obtained by different but equivalent reasoning.

If the system is translationally invariant in space we have

\displaystyle X^{+}_{ij}({\bm{k}},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[G({\bm{k}},\omega)\,b({\bm{k}},\omega)\right]_{ij}

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\left(1+i\omega\,G({\bm{k}},\omega)\right)X^{0}({\bm{k}})\right]_{ij}\ .

We stress again that the response problem and the initial-value problem are equivalent: Eqs. (
37
)
describe the response of the observables to the external field, while Eqs. (
36
) describe the
relaxation of the observables from the initial condition created by the field. Note, however,
that the matrices
b
and
X^{0}
are in general not diagonal, and hence Eq. (
37a
) may
contain couplings that are absent in Eq. (
36a
). We will see an example of this in
Sec.
IV.3
.

III.3
A general relation between response formulas and fluctuation formulas

Now define a matrix of bilinear products of observables,

{\cal A}_{ij}^{++}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})=a_{i}^{+}({\bm{x}},\omega)\,a_{j}^{+}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\ .

(40)

Equation (
29
) yields

{\cal A}_{ij}^{++}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\left[G\,{\cal A}^{00}\,G^{\text{T}}\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})

(41a)

where

{\cal A}^{00}_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=a_{i}^{0}({\bm{x}})\,a_{j}^{0}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ .

(41b)

In the special case of time translational invariance we define

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{ij}^{++}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{ij}^{++}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,-\omega)

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[G(\omega)\,{\cal A}^{00}\,G^{\dagger}(\omega)\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ .\qquad

By using Eq. (
31c
) we can write, equivalently,

{\cal A}_{ij}^{++}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega)=\left[G(\omega)X^{0}\,{\cal H}^{00}X^{0\,\text{T}}G^{\dagger}(\omega)\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})

(43a)

where

{\cal H}^{00}_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=h_{i}({\bm{x}})\,h_{j}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ .

(43b)

If in addition we have spatial translational invariance we define

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{ij}^{++}({\bm{k}},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{ij}^{++}({\bm{k}},-{\bm{k}};\omega)

(44a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[G({\bm{k}},\omega)\,{\cal A}^{00}({\bm{k}})\,G^{\dagger}({\bm{k}},\omega)\right]_{ij}\ .\qquad

with

{\cal A}_{ij}^{00}({\bm{k}})={\cal A}_{ij}^{00}({\bm{k}},-{\bm{k}})\ .

(44b)

Now compare the relaxation formulas, Eqs. (
41
), or the response formula,
Eqs. (
43
), with the corresponding fluctuation formula, Eq. (
22
),
which we write down again for clarity:

S_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\left[G\,\Delta\,G^{\text{T}}\right]_{ij}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime};\omega,\omega^{\prime})\ ,

(3.6)

We see that the response, or relaxation, formulas have the same structure as the fluctuation formula;
the only difference is the matrix sandwiched between the propagators.
This isomorphism between the fluctuation formula and the relaxation/response formula is our main
result. It is very general and holds for linear deviations from arbitrary states. In particular,
it holds for deviations from an equilibrium state, when the usual FDT holds, as we
will see below. However, regardless of whether or not the system is in equilibrium, any
interesting correlations are encoded in the propagator
G
, and
S_{ij}
and
{\cal A}_{ij}^{++}

just represent different linear combinations of the propagator elements. The correlations can
thus be probed by response, or relaxation, experiments even in absence of the usual FDT.
Physically, this reflects the fact that, to linear order, the spontaneous fluctuations, the relaxation
from an initial condition, and the response to external fields contain the same information. This is a
manifestation of Onsager’s regression hypothesis
[
28
]
.
In the following section we will discuss various examples.

IV
Examples

In this section we discuss various examples that illustrate the fluctuation-response relation
derived in Sec.
III
. We first demonstrate that the isomorphism does indeed hold
in equilibrium, as it must, and is equivalent to the FDT, by considering shear diffusion
in a fluid in equilibrium. In more complicated systems, including fluids in non-equilibrium steady
states (NESS)
[
29
,
30
]
,
stirred fluids
[
31
]
, or active matter
[
32
,
33
]
,
the general fluctuation-response isomorphism still holds, but the FDT breaks down due
to one or both of the following mechanisms:

Mechanism I:
The stochastic-force correlations are different from those required by the FDT.
This obviously invalidates the FDT, while the isomorphism between the response or relaxation
formulas and the fluctuation formula demonstrated in Sec.
IV.3
remains intact. It
is interesting to distinguish between to subcases:

(a)

The wave-number dependence of the random-force correlation
\Delta
is the same
as in equilibrium. In this case the FDT is violated only by a constant factor, and the correlations
have the same structure as in equilibrium. Examples are active Brownian motion, see Sec.
IV.2.1
,
and some models of randomly stirred fluids discussed in Sec.
IV.2.2
below.

(b)

The wave-number dependence of
\Delta
is different from the equilibrium case. This
can lead to long-ranged correlations that are not present in equilibrium. Examples are certain
models for randomly stirred fluids, Sec.
IV.2.2
, and models of cell motion in biological tissues,
see Sec.
IV.2.3
.

Mechanism II:
The inverse propagator
G^{-1}
in Eqs. (
18
) contains terms that are not present
in ordinary linearized hydrodynamics. This will in general lead to a breakdown of the FDT as well as
to long-ranged correlations that are not related to the properties of the random forces. An example is
a fluid in a constant temperature gradient, see the discussion in Sec.
IV.3
.

IV.1
Fluid in equilibrium

Consider shear diffusion in a classical fluid (or a quantum fluid in the hydrodynamic regime)

[
34
,
21
,
18
]
.
In this case there is only one relevant observable, viz., one component of the transverse fluid velocity, which
decouples from the other hydrodynamic variables and which we denote by
u_{\perp}
. The system is
translationally invariant with respect to both time and space. The inverse propagator is given by

G^{-1}({\bm{k}},\omega)=-i\omega+\nu k^{2}\ ,

(45a)

with
\nu
the kinematic viscosity. From Eq. (
24b
) we see that
\lambda^{+}
is
frequency independent and given by

\lambda^{+}({\bm{k}})=\nu k^{2}\ .

(45b)

For a fluid in equilibrium the random-force correlation is
[
35
,
36
]

\Delta({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{2T}{\rho}\,\nu{\bm{k}}^{2}\ ,

(46)

with
\rho
the mass density. For the van Hove function, Eq. (
26
) yields

S({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{2T}{\rho}\,\frac{\nu k^{2}}{\omega^{2}+(\nu k^{2})^{2}}\ .

(47)

The static susceptibility is
[
18
,
37
]

\chi^{0}=\int(d\omega/\pi)\,\chi^{\prime\prime}({\bm{k}},\omega)=1/\rho\ .

(48a)

Since this is an equilibrium system, Eq. (
15a
) holds. We have

X^{0}=\chi^{0}=1/\rho\ ,

(48b)

and from Eqs. (
38
), (
45a
) and (
48b
) we find

X^{+}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\chi^{+}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{1}{\rho}\,\frac{\nu k^{2}}{-i\omega+\nu k^{2}}\ .

(49a)

As a commutator correlation function,
\chi
is an odd function of the frequency, so

\chi^{-}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{1}{\rho}\,\frac{\nu k^{2}}{i\omega+\nu k^{2}}\ ,

(49b)

and the spectral densities are (see Eq. (
82d
), and also Eqs. (
15b
,
15c
))

X^{\prime\prime}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\chi^{\prime\prime}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{1}{\rho}\,\frac{\omega\,\nu k^{2}}{\omega^{2}+(\nu k^{2})^{2}}\ .

(49c)

Comparing Eqs. (
47
) and (
49c
) we see that the FDT, Eqs. (
16
), holds.

Now consider the bilinear response formula. From, Eq. (
41b
) we have

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{00}({\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|u_{\perp}^{0}({\bm{k}})|^{2}

(50a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\,|h_{\perp}({\bm{k}})|^{2}

(50b)

with
h_{\perp}
the field conjugate to
u_{\perp}
. Equations (
44
) yield

{\cal A}^{++}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+(\nu k^{2})^{2}}\,{\cal A}^{00}({\bm{k}})\ ,

(50c)

which expresses the bilinear response in terms of the initial condition, or, alternatively, the
external field. Comparing
{\cal A}^{++}
with the van Hove function, Eq. (
47
),
we explicitly see the isomorphism discussed in Sec.
III.3
.

In order to make contact with Ref.
38
we also note that if
we define a closely related observable,

\displaystyle{\cal B}^{+}({\bm{k}},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a({\bm{k}},\omega)\,a^{+}(-{\bm{k}},-\omega)

\displaystyle\hskip-40.0pt=\left[(1+i\omega G({\bm{k}},\omega))X^{0}\,{\cal H}({\bm{k}},\omega)(-i\omega)X^{0}G({\bm{k}},-\omega)\right]\ ,

with

{\cal H}({\bm{k}},\omega)=|h({\bm{k}},\omega)|^{2}\ ,

(51b)

we find

{\cal B}^{+}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{-i}{\rho}\,\chi^{\prime\prime}({\bm{k}},\omega)\,{\cal H}({\bm{k}},\omega)\ .

(52)

In this form, the relation between the fluctuations, as represented by
\chi^{\prime\prime}=(\omega/2T)S
, and the
bilinear response, as represented by
{\cal B}^{+}
, was formulated in
Ref.
38
(the quantity
u_{\perp}^{+}
was denoted by

{\tilde{u}}_{\perp}
in that reference).

The above relations between the bilinear response functions
{\cal A}^{++}
, or
{\cal B}^{+}
, and
the correlation functions
S
, or
\chi^{\prime\prime}
, are equivalent to the usual FDT. As derived from the Langevin
equation (
17a
), they are actually more plausible, and easier to understand, than the FDT,
given that the fluctuating force and the external field are both linear inhomogeneities in Eq. (
17a
),
and the correlation function is quadratic in the fluctuating force.

IV.2
Examples of Mechanism I

We now discuss various examples where the FDT breaks down due to the properties of the
fluctuating-force correlations, while the isomorphism explained in Sec.
IV.3
(see
Eqs. (
41
) and (
22
)) remains valid.

IV.2.1
Active Brownian motion

Ordinary Brownian motion is the random walk experienced by a particle of mass
m
suspended
in a fluid in equilibrium at temperature
T

[
18
,
39
]
. The effect of the fluid on
the particle is described by two forces: a frictional force that is proportional to the particle’s velocity,
and a stochastic or Langevin force. We also include an external force
{\bm{h}}
that is conjugate
to the velocity. The equation of motion for the velocity then is

\frac{d}{dt}\,{\bm{v}}(t)+\gamma\,{\bm{v}}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\,\gamma\,{\bm{h}}(t)+{\bm{f}}(t)\ .

(53a)

Here
\gamma>0
is the friction coefficient and
{\bm{f}}
is the Langevin force with correlations

\langle f_{i}(t)\,f_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,\Delta\ .

(53b)

Equations (
53
) are a simplified (linearized, and considered in zero spatial dimensions) version
of Model A in Ref.
40
. The form of the external force term is consistent
with Eqs. (
37
); the static response function is

X_{ij}^{0}=\delta_{ij}/m\ ,

(54)

see also Eq. (
48b
). The
conjugate force
{\bm{h}}
is related to the physical force
{\bm{F}}
on the particle by

{\bm{h}}={\bm{F}}/\gamma

[
20
]
.
The average kinetic energy of the particle,
E=m\langle{\bm{v}}(t)\cdot{\bm{v}}(t)\rangle/2
, is proportional
to the equal-time velocity autocorrelation function
S^{0}
. The equipartition theorem yields

S_{ij}^{0}=\delta_{ij}\,T/m

(55)

and the FDT, Eq. (
16b
), holds.
The inverse propagator is
G_{ij}^{-1}(\omega)=\delta_{ij}\,G^{-1}(\omega)
with

G^{-1}(\omega)=-i\omega+\gamma\ ,

(56)

and the dynamic van Hove function
S_{ij}(\omega)=\delta_{ij}\,S(\omega)
is

S(\omega)=\frac{\Delta}{\omega^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\ .

(57a)

In order for Eqs. (
55
) and (
57a
) to be consistent, the random-force
correlation in equilibrium must be
[
36
]

\Delta=\gamma\,T/2m\ .

(57b)

The particle’s mean-square displacement can be derived from the equation of motion for the position,

\frac{d}{dt}\,{\bm{r}}(t)={\bm{v}}(t)\ .

(58)

Differentiating again with respect to
t
, inserting Eq. (
53a
), multiplying by
{\bm{r}}
,
averaging, and integrating yields Langevin’s result
[
41
]

\langle{\bm{r}}^{2}(t)\rangle=\frac{6T}{m\gamma}\left[t-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(1-e^{-\gamma t}\right)\right]\ .

(59)

To obtain the dynamic response function we average Eq. (
53a
) and find

X^{+}_{ij}(\omega)=\delta_{ij}\,X^{+}(\omega)
with

\displaystyle X^{+}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi^{+}(\omega)=\frac{1}{m}\,\frac{\gamma}{-i\omega+\gamma}\ ,

(60a)

\displaystyle X^{\prime\prime}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=\frac{1}{m}\,\frac{\gamma\omega}{\omega^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\ ,

(60b)

where the reasoning is the same as in Sec.
IV.1
.
Comparing Eqs. (
57
) and (
60b
) we see that the FDT in the dynamic form
(
16a
) also holds, as it must.

‘Active Brownian motion’ refers to a Brownian particle equipped with an energy source that is independent
of the heat bath provided by the fluid; for a review, see Ref.
42
.
One way to model such a system is to make the friction coefficient velocity dependent such that it is
negative for small
v
and changes sign at some
v_{0}>0
:

\gamma\to-\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}v^{2}\ ,

(61)

with
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
. The fluctuating-force correlations are still given by
Eq. (
53b
), but
\Delta
is not constrained by the equipartition theorem.
This makes the system an example for Mechanism I (a).
Equation (
61
) leads to a nonzero value of the velocity in the absence
of an external field, making the particle self-propelled. Let this spontaneous velocity point in the

z
-direction,

\langle{\bm{v}}\rangle_{h=0}=v_{0}\,\hat{\bm{z}}\ ,

(62)

where
v_{0}=\sqrt{\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{2}}
. Linearizing about this NESS we obtain an equation
of motion for
u_{z}=v_{z}-v_{0}

[
43
]

\frac{d}{dt}\,u_{z}(t)+2\gamma_{1}\,u_{z}=\frac{1}{m}\,2\gamma_{1}\,h_{z}(t)+f_{z}(t)\ .

(63)

The problem now maps onto the equilibrium case with
\gamma\to 2\gamma_{1}
. However,
the FDT does not hold due to Mechanism I (a), since
\Delta
is unconstrained and the
equal-time correlation function is not given by Eq. (
55
), whereas the response
function, which does not depend on
\Delta
, is unchanged. The bilinear
relaxation function is

{\cal A}^{++}(\omega)=\frac{(u_{z}^{0})^{2}}{\omega^{2}+4\gamma_{1}^{2}}

(64)

and the isomorphism between
S
and
{\cal A}^{++}
holds.

IV.2.2
Randomly stirred fluid

Various models of a randomly stirred fluid were discussed in Ref.
31

with a focus on turbulence and low dimensions. In their simplest form, a linearization of these models
yields a forced diffusion equation for the fluid velocity as in Sec.
IV.1
, but with a
random-force correlation that does not obey the FDT. Accordingly, the inverse propagator is given by
Eq. (
45a
). The random-force correlation is a frequency-independent quantity
\Delta({\bm{k}})

that is
not
given by Eq. (
46
), but either proportional to
k^{2}
with an independent coefficient,
or a constant that is nonzero in a certain wave-number range. The former case is an example of
Mechanism I (a); the latter, of Mechanism I (b). The van Hove function is

S({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{\Delta({\bm{k}})}{\omega^{2}+(\nu k^{2})^{2}}

(65)

and the bilinear relaxation function
{\cal A}^{++}
is given by Eq. (
50c
). We see that the
isomorphism between
S
and
{\cal A}^{++}
still holds, but the FDT does not.

For the static or equal-time correlation function, Eq. (
65
) yields

S^{0}({\bm{k}})=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,S({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{\Delta({\bm{k}})}{2\nu k^{2}}\ .

(66)

In models where
\Delta(k=0)\neq 0
, Mechanism I (b) thus leads to long-range static correlations,
whereas the static response function is the same as in equilibrium,
X^{0}=1/\rho
.
This is in contrast to the example discussed in Sec.
IV.3
below, where the fluctuating-force
correlations are the same as in equilibrium, the long-range correlations
are encoded in the propagator via a coupling between two diffusive fluctuations,
and they manifest themselves both in the equal-time correlation functions, Eqs. (
76
),
and the static response functions, Eq. (
77
).

IV.2.3
Cell motion in biological tissues

Another example of an active-matter system
[
33
]
is the model for cell motion
in biological tissues driven by cell division and apoptosis studied in Ref.
44
.
The two relevant hydrodynamic variables are the density
\rho
of the active matter and one component
of the transverse fluid velocity,
u_{\perp}
. The linearized model dynamic equations are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\,\rho-D{\bm{\nabla}}^{2}\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{\rho}\ ,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\,u_{\perp}-\nu{\bm{\nabla}}^{2}u_{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{\perp}\ ,

(67)

with
D
a diffusion coefficient and
\nu
the kinematic viscosity of the fluid.
Accordingly, the inverse propagator is

G^{-1}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}-i\omega+Dk^{2}&0\\
0&-i\omega+\nu k^{2}\end{pmatrix}\ .

(68)

The fluctuating-force correlations in this model are constants
\Delta_{\rho}
and
\Delta_{\perp}
,
respectively, which makes this model an example of Mechanism I (b) and maps the linearized
problem onto the randomly stirred fluid, Sec.
IV.2.2
, with a
{\bm{k}}
-independent

\Delta
. The FDT breaks down, while
S
and
{\cal A}^{++}
are still
isomorphic.

We also note that taking into account the nonlinearities in the equations leads to a very strong
long-time tail:
D
and
\nu
become frequency dependent and diverge for
\omega\to 0
as

1/\omega^{1/5}
in spatial dimensions
d=3
, and
1/\omega^{1/2}
in
d=2
. In time space,
this corresponds to an autocorrelation function that decays for long times as
1/t^{4/5}
in
d=3
and

1/t^{1/2}
in
d=2
, respectively
[
44
]
. This temporal long-ranged correlation, just
as the spatial one in Eq. (
66
), is a result of Mechanism I (b), albeit one that manifests
itself only at the level of the nonlinear theory. In
d=2
these strong long-time tails have been
observed experimentally, see Ref.
44
and references therein.

IV.2.4
Flocking

A rather complex example of active matter is the Toner-Tu flocking theory
[
45
,
46
]
,
which is an effective continuum theory for the model proposed by Vicsek et al.
[
47
]
; for
reviews, see Refs.
48
,
32
. The hydrodynamic variables
are the coarse-grained density
\rho({\bm{x}},t)
of the flock, with average value
\rho_{0}
, and a velocity field

{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}},t)
that is the coarse-grained velocity of the members of the flock. In the isotropic, or disordered,
phase, where the mean velocity is zero, the linearized dynamic equations
are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\rho_{0}{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\ ,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{i}+\gamma v_{i}+\sigma_{1}\partial_{i}\rho-D_{\text{B}}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}v_{j}-D_{\text{T}}{\bm{\nabla}}^{2}v_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{i}\ .

Here
D_{\text{B}}
and
D_{\text{T}}
are diffusion coefficients that are analogous to linear combinations
of the shear and bulk viscosities in an equilibrium fluid, and the coefficient
\sigma_{1}
arises from
an expansion of the pressure in powers of the density fluctuations.
\gamma>0
is a friction coefficient,
and the
f_{i}
are random forces with correlations given by Eq. (
53b
).

Consider for simplicity the transverse components of the velocity. The inverse propagator is

G^{-1}({\bm{k}},\omega)=-i\omega+\gamma+D_{\text{T}}k^{2}\ .

(70)

and the corresponding van Hove function is

S({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{\Delta}{\omega^{2}+(\gamma+D_{\text{T}}k^{2})^{2}}\ .

(71)

Note that the static correlation function is finite at
{\bm{k}}=0
, in contrast to the case
of a stirred fluid. This is a consequence of the constant damping term
[
49
]
.
The flock is not a thermal system (there is no heat bath, and no temperature concept), so
the question of the validity of the FDT is not well posed. However, the discussion in
Sec.
III.3
still applies and the relaxation formula is

{\cal A}^{++}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+(\gamma+D_{\text{T}}k^{2})^{2}}\,{\cal A}^{00}({\bm{k}})

(72)

with
{\cal A}^{00}
given by Eq. (
50a
). This demonstrates the isomorphism between

S
and
{\cal A}^{++}
in the isotropic phase.

The self-propulsion capability of the members of the flock is modeled as in the active Brownian
motion model, via Eq. (
61
). The resulting phase with a nonzero average velocity is quite
complex, and we will not discuss it here. The propagators and correlation functions for the linearized
theory have been given in Refs.
46
,
48
,
and
32
. Given the propagators,
{\cal A}^{++}
is again given by
Eqs. (
44
).

The spontaneously broken rotational symmetry leads to an equal-time velocity autocorrelation function
that diverges in the long-wavelength limit
[
45
]
, in contrast to the isotropic phase.
Nonetheless, the model yields true long-range order in spatial dimension
d=2
. This is a result
of the nonlinear terms in the theory, and consistent with numerical simulations
[
47
]
.

IV.3
An Example of Mechanism II: Fluid in a NESS

A simple example of a non-equilibrium system where Mechanism II leads to a breakdown
of the FDT and to long-ranged correlations is a fluid
subject to a constant temperature gradient
[
30
,
50
,
51
]
. Such a system is in a NESS,
so time translational invariance holds exactly. Furthermore, the leading effect of the temperature
gradient turns out to be the coupling between the temperature and the shear velocity in the
Navier-Stokes equations; all other occurrences of the spatially varying temperature can
be replaced by the average temperature. In this leading approximation the system shows
spatial translational invariance as well.

In the current context, this problem has been discussed in Ref.
38
.
It is an example of Mechanism II: The externally imposed temperature gradient promotes a term
that is present, but nonlinear, in equilibrium to a linear term in the equations linearized about the
NESS. The relevant observables are (one component of) the shear velocity
u_{\perp}\equiv\delta A_{1}
and
the temperature fluctuations
\delta T\equiv\delta A_{2}
. The inverse propagator is

G^{-1}({\bm{k}},\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}-i\omega+\nu k^{2}&0\\
\alpha&-i\omega+D_{\text{T}}k^{2}\end{pmatrix}

(73)

with
\nu
the kinematic viscosity and
D_{\text{T}}
the thermal diffusion coefficient.
\alpha
is a
coupling constant that is proportional to the externally fixed temperature gradient. It is real, even under
parity, and dimensionally a frequency. The fluctuating-force correlations are the same as in
equilibrium
[
52
]
, viz.

\Delta({\bm{k}},\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}2T\nu{\bm{k}}^{2}/\rho&0\\
0&2T^{2}D_{\text{T}}{\bm{k}}^{2}/c_{p}\end{pmatrix}

(74)

with
c_{p}
the specific heat at constant pressure.

For the van Hove functions Eq. (
26
) yields

\displaystyle S_{11}({\bm{k}},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2T}{\rho}\,\frac{\nu k^{2}}{\omega^{2}+(\nu k^{2})^{2}}\ ,\qquad

(75a)

\displaystyle S_{12}({\bm{k}},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha\,\frac{2T}{\rho}\,\frac{\nu k^{2}}{\omega^{2}+(\nu k^{2})^{2}}\,\frac{1}{i\omega+D_{\text{T}}k^{2}}\ ,\qquad

V
Summary, and Discussion

In summary, we have presented a relation between correlation functions and response or relaxation
functions in many-body systems that remains valid in non-equilibrium states where the usual
fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT) breaks down. It takes the form of an isomorphism between the
two-point correlation functions and bilinear products of linear response formulas. The technical
underpinning is the fact that fluctuating forces, which determine the correlations, and external
fields, which determine the response, both enter as linear inhomogeneities in a generalized
Langevin equation. The practical significance lies in the fact that fluctuation formulas and
response formulas contain the same information about correlations, but the latter are amenable
to experimental probes even in situations where the direct measurement of correlation functions
is very difficult.

In what follows we present various discussion points, some of which elaborate on remarks
that were already made in the main text.

1.

We have used a generalized Langevin equation, Eqs. (
17
), that is
obtained by linearizing about an arbitrary state that can be an equilibrium or non-equilibrium
state, and may or may not be time dependent. Within this formalism the crucial isomorphism,
expressed in Sec.
III.3
, manifests itself in a very simple way. It is an obvious
result of the fluctuating forces and the conjugate fields both being linear inhomogeneities in
the Langevin equation, and it is arguably more plausible than the usual fluctuation-dissipation
theorem. This simplicity is somewhat deceptive, as the result is much less obvious if one uses
different techniques. In this context it is useful to remember that in non-equilibrium states the
dynamics play a much more crucial role than in equilibrium ones. For instance, in non-equilibrium
states the equal-time correlation functions in general depend on the dynamics (see, for instance,
Eqs. (
76
)), whereas in
equilibrium they are thermodynamic quantities that are entirely determined by the partition
function. The use of dynamic equations is therefore very natural in this context.

A related point is that the isomorphism reflects Onsager’s regression hypothesis
[
28
]
,
as was mentioned at the end of Sec.
III
. Actually, the very fact that one can
use the dynamic equation (
17a
) to discuss both fluctuations and response or relaxation
relies on Onsager’s hypothesis. In the language of the current paper, it states that the relaxation
of the dynamic variables from an initial value is governed by the averaged version of the same
dynamic equation that governs the dynamics of the spontaneous fluctuations.
As a practical consequence, the correlation functions even in non-equilibrium systems can
effectively be determined by measuring response or relaxation functions, and the size of the
latter is given by macroscopic driving forces that are experimentally controllable even if the
fluctuations are small. This point was made in the context of the example of a fluid in a
NESS, Sec.
IV.3
, in Refs.
55
,
38
.

2.

What constitutes a violation of the FDT is to some extent a matter of choice. The
most common notion, which we have adopted, is that Eqs. (
16
)
must hold as
equalities
, as opposed to just proportionality relations, in order for
the FDT to hold. We have discussed two mechanisms for violating the FDT, see
Sec.
IV
. Mechanism I is very simple: it consists of stipulating a fluctuating-force
correlation that does not lead to the equilibrium equal-time correlation functions. While
technically trivial, this reflects the fact that the equal-time correlation functions are in
general not known in non-equilibrium systems and depend on the dynamics. Since the
response functions do not depend on the fluctuating-force correlations this obviously
leads to a violation of the FDT. This simple but fundamental point implies that an
equilibrium equal-time correlation function that is different from the equilibrium one implies a
violation of the FDT. Mechanism I does, however, leave intact the structure of the
equilibrium theory. For Mechanism I (a) in particular, where the fluctuating-force correlation
has the same wave-number dependence as in equilibrium, the FDT is violated only in the
sense that Eq. (
16b
) turns into a proportionality relation with a constant
proportionality factor. One might call this a weak violation of the FDT. Examples we have
discussed are active Brownian motion, Sec.
IV.2.1
, and the randomly
stirred fluid, Sec.
IV.2.2
, with a
\Delta({\bm{k}})\propto k^{2}

that is not given by Eq. (
46
). In these cases the fluctuation-response relation
from Sec.
III.3
is no stronger a statement than the violated FDT, since it takes the form of an
isomorphism rather than an identity. However, we emphasize again that the isomorphism

always
holds, irrespective of whether, or how badly, the FDT is
violated.

For Mechanism I (b) the above structural remarks are still true, but the wave-number
dependence of the fluctuating-force correlations can lead to long-range correlations that are
not present in equilibrium. These long-range correlations can be spatial in nature, as in the
example of a stirred fluid with
\Delta({\bm{k}})=\text{const.}
, Eq. (
66
), or temporal,
as in the example of cell motion in Sec.
IV.2.3
. In these examples the
equal-time correlation function scales differently with the wave number than the static
response function. This is a strong violation of the FDT.

Mechanism II consists of couplings between the dynamic variables that are
different from the equilibrium theory, which leads to structural changes. Of our
examples, the fluid in a NESS, Sec.
IV.3
, falls into this category. In this
case the equal-time correlation functions are proportional to the static response
functions, see Eqs. (
76
,
77
), although the non-equilibrium
contributions to both are singular functions of the wave number and the proportionality
factors depend on kinetic coefficients. Also, one of the response functions vanishes
whereas the corresponding equal-time correlation functions is nonzero. This is in
between the weak and strong violations of the FDT as defined above.

It is possible to have both mechanisms present, although this is not the
case in any of the examples we have considered. This is an interesting problem to consider.

3.

In the context of Mechanism II, which relies on terms that are absent in equilibrium
hydrodynamics, it is worthwhile mentioning that terms that
are
present in ordinary
hydrodynamics, but are anomalously large due to a non-equilibrium situation, can lead
to qualitative effects. An example is the model of active particles suspended in a
momentum-conserving fluid studied in Ref.
56
,
where an anomalously large Burnett coefficient leads to a hydrodynamic instability.

4.

In equilibrium statistical mechanics, the static response functions
X^{0}
are naturally
considered as input into the linear-response problem. In this case, they are purely
thermodynamic quantities and are given by commutator correlation functions
\chi^{0}
. In
non-equilibrium systems none of the preceding statements are true. In the context of the
dynamic equations (
17
), it is the
b_{ij}
that describe the coupling of the
variables to the external fields, and it is natural to consider them fundamental
ingredients of the theory on the same level as the couplings and relaxation rates

\lambda_{ij}
. The static response functions
X^{0}
then are derived quantities
that are related to the fundamental couplings
b_{ij}
via Eq. (
37b
).
Even though the
X^{0}
are not frequency dependent, in a non-equilibrium system
they in general depend on the dynamics via kinetic coefficients, as we have seen
explicitly in Sec.
IV.3
, see Eq. (
77
). We also note that the
frequency dependence of
b
is a consequence of the frequency dependence of

\lambda
, i.e., of memory effects. If one neglects memory effects, both
\lambda

and
b
are delta functions in time space and hence frequency independent.

5.

In all of our examples the Langevin-force correlations were short ranged. This raises
the question as to whether long-ranged correlations ever arise from long-ranged stochastic
forces. This is to some degree a matter of how the theory is formulated. Consider the
example of a fluid in a NESS, Sec.
IV.3
. As was shown in
Ref.
54
, it is possible to formulate a theory for
the temperature fluctuations only, while the velocity fluctuations are hidden by integrating
them out in a Martin-Siggia-Rose formalism. The resulting effective Langevin equation
for the temperature fluctuations has a long-ranged stochastic force. This is analogous to
the well known phenomenon in field theory, where integrating out soft, or massless, modes
leads to long-ranged effective interactions. Conversely, a long-ranged Langevin force can
be made short-ranged by introducing suitable auxiliary variables that make explicit the
‘hidden sector’ of the theory.

6.

For our discussion of response functions we have assumed time translational
invariance. This is an exact property of steady states, but for general non-equilibrium
states it is true at best in some approximate sense. An exploration of the conditions
under which such an approximation is valid, as well as a possible generalization of the
fluctuation-response relation to situations where time-translational invariance does not
hold, are interesting problems.

With this proviso in mind we stress again the generality of the fluctuation-response
relation derived in Sec.
III.3
. For instance, the large fluctuations present
in the NESS discussed in Sec.
IV.3
can give rise to instabilities such
as Rayleigh-Bénard convection
[
51
]
. This leads to a new NESS that may,
as a function of suitable parameters, become unstable itself and ultimately lead to a time-dependent
state. The fluctuation-response relation will remain valid in such a scenario if one linearizes
about the appropriate state. Similar remarks hold for instabilities in active matter systems,
see Ref.
56
, and also Ref.
32

and references therein.
