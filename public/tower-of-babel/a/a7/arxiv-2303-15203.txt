Transduction of Automatic Sequences and Applications
Jeffrey Shallit

Abstract
We consider the implementation of the transduction of automatic sequences, and their generalizations, in the Walnut software for solving decision problems in combinatorics on words. We provide a number of applications, including (a) representations of n ! n! as a sum of three squares (b) overlap-free Dyck words and (c) sums of Fibonacci representations. We also prove results about iterated running sums of the Thue-Morse sequence.



1
Introduction

The
k
-automatic sequences form an interesting class that has been studied for more than fifty years now
[
7
]
. One nice property is that the first-order theory of these sequences, with addition, is algorithmically decidable

[
4
]
.
Walnut
, a free software system created and implemented by Hamoon Mousavi
[
15
]
, makes it possible to state and evaluate the truth of nontrivial first-order statements about automatic sequences, often in a matter of seconds
[
18
]
. With it, we can easily reprove old theorems in a simple and uniform way, explore unresolved conjectures, and prove new theorems.

This class is defined as follows: a sequence

(a(n))_{n\geq 0}
over a finite alphabet is
k
-automatic if there exists a deterministic finite automaton with output (DFAO) that, on completely processing an input of
n
expressed in base
k
, reaches a state
s
with output
a(n)
.

According to a famous theorem of Cobham
[
7
]
, the class of
k
-automatic sequences is closed under deterministic
t
-uniform transductions. The particular model of transducer here is the following: outputs are associated with transitions, so that every input letter results in the output of exactly
t
letters.
Cobham’s theorem was later extended to the more general class of morphic sequences (defined below) by Dekking
[
9
]
.

Transducers make it possible to manipulate automatic sequences in useful and interesting ways. For example, it follows that the running sum and running product (taken
modulo a natural number
M\geq 2
)
of a
k
-automatic sequence taking
values in
\Sigma_{t}=\{0,1,\ldots,t-1\}

is
k
-automatic.

In this paper we report on a recent implementation of Dekking’s result in
Walnut
, carried out by the second author.
This new capability of transducers has been implemented in the latest version of
Walnut
, which is available for free download at

https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~shallit/walnut.html
.

With it we are able to obtain a number of results, including reproving the results of Burns
[
6
]
on the representation of
n!
as a sum of three squares, in a simpler and more general way.
We also prove a new result on overlap-free Dyck words, and several results about iterated running sums of the Thue-Morse sequence. Lastly, we discuss the transduction of Fibonacci automata and automata in other numeration systems.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Basic definitions

Given a finite alphabet
\Sigma
, a
word
over
\Sigma
is a concatenation of elements of
\Sigma
. If
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{k}
is a finite word over
\Sigma
, then the length of
w
is denoted by
|w|=k
. The set of all finite words over
\Sigma
is denoted by
\Sigma^{*}
, which also includes the empty word of length
0
, denoted by
\epsilon
. The
reversal
of
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{k}
is defined as
w^{R}=w_{k}\cdots w_{1}
. An infinite word
{\bf a}=a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
over
\Sigma
is a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of elements of
\Sigma
.

Let
Q
be a finite set. A function
h\colon Q^{*}\to Q^{*}
is called a
morphism
if
h(vw)=h(v)h(w)
for all
v,w\in Q^{*}
. Note that a morphism must, by definition, satisfy
h(\epsilon)=\epsilon
. Furthermore, we say that a morphism
h
is
k
-uniform
if
|h(q)|=k
for every
q\in Q
. A morphism can be applied to infinite words and sequences in the obvious way: if
{\bf q}=q_{0}q_{1}q_{2}\cdots
is an infinite word over
Q
, then
h({\bf q})=h(q_{0})h(q_{1})h(q_{2})\cdots
.

If
h
is a morphism, by
h^{n}
we mean the
n
-fold
composition of
h
with itself.
If
h
is prolongable, that is, if

h(a)=ax
and
h^{n}(x)\not=\epsilon
for all
n\geq 0
, then we can define the infinite word
h^{\omega}(a):=axh(x)h^{2}(x)\cdots
; it is a
fixed point of
h
.

Let
\Delta
be a finite alphabet. A
coding

\lambda\colon Q^{*}\to\Delta^{*}
is a
1
-uniform morphism (satisfying
|\lambda(q)|=1
for all
q\in Q
). An infinite sequence
\bf x
over
\Delta
is a
morphic sequence
if
{\bf x}=\lambda(h^{\omega}(a))
where
\lambda
is a coding,
h
is a prolongable morphism, and
a
is a letter.

2.2
Numeration systems

We give a brief overview of the numeration systems covered in this paper. See
[
2
, Chapter 3]
for an in-depth treatment of numeration systems.

For
k=2,3,4,\ldots
, the
most-significant-digit-first (msd) base-
k
representation
of
n\in\mathbb{N}
is
(n)_{k}=d_{t}d_{t-1}\cdots d_{1}d_{0}
where
n=\sum_{i=0}^{t}d_{i}k^{i}
and
d_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
for all
i=0,\ldots,t
. We abbreviate this as the
msd
-
k
representation of
n
. The least-significant-digit-first (lsd) base-
k
representation of
n
is
d_{0}d_{1}\cdots d_{t-1}d_{t}
. For example, the msd-
2
representation of
13
is
1101
, and the lsd-
2
representation of
13
is
1011
.

Define
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, and
F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}
for
n\geq 2
. The most-significant-digit-first
Fibonacci representation
of an integer
n\geq 0
is
(n)_{F}=d_{t}d_{t-1}\cdots d_{1}d_{0}
, where
n=\sum_{i=0}^{t}d_{i}F_{i+2}
and
d_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, with no consecutive
1
s, i.e., there does not exist
0\leq i\leq t-1
such that
d_{i}=d_{i+1}=1
. Every integer
n\geq 0
can be uniquely written in this way
[
13
,
20
]
. For example, the most-significant-digit-first Fibonacci representation of
14
is
(14)_{F}=100001
. If
n=\sum_{i=0}^{t}d_{i}F_{i+2}
, then the least-significant-digit-first Fibonacci representation of
n
is
d_{0}d_{1}\cdots d_{t-1}d_{t}
.

2.3
DFAOs

A
deterministic finite automaton with output
(DFAO) is a
6
-tuple
M=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\lambda\rangle
, where

•

Q
is a finite set of
states
;

•

\Sigma
is a finite set representing the
input alphabet
;

•

\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\to Q
is the
transition function
;

•

q_{0}\in Q
is the
initial state
;

•

\Delta
is a finite set representing the
output alphabet
; and

•

\lambda\colon Q^{*}\to\Delta^{*}
is the
coding
, defined by a single value
\lambda(q)
for each state
q\in Q
.

We can extend the transition function to words over
Q
in the obvious way.

If
\Sigma=\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}

computed
by
M
, defined as

x_{n}=\lambda(\delta(q_{0},(n)_{k})),

where
(n)_{k}
denotes the msd-
k
representation of
n
. Intuitively,
x_{n}
is the output when
M
receives the msd-
k
representation of
n
as input.

The sequence
{\bf x}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is called
k
-automatic
if there exists a DFAO
M
with input alphabet
\Sigma=\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
that computes it. Theorem 3 of Cobham
[
7
]
proves that a
k
-automatic sequence
\bf x
is a morphic sequence with respect to some
k
-uniform morphism. We provide the construction given in Cobham below, as it will be useful to us in Section
4
. Given a DFAO
M
with alphabet
\Sigma=\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
, define a morphism
h\colon Q^{*}\to Q^{*}
by

h(a)=\delta(a,0)\;\delta(a,1)\;\cdots\;\delta(a,k-1).

Then the sequences
\bf x
and
\lambda(h^{\omega}(q_{0}))
are equal. Conversely, given a
k
-automatic sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}=\lambda(h^{\omega}(q_{0}))
where
q_{0}\in Q
, a
k
-uniform morphism
h\colon Q^{*}\to Q^{*}
and a coding
\lambda\colon Q^{*}\to\Delta^{*}
, we define the DFAO
N=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\lambda\rangle
where
\Sigma=\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
and
\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\to Q
is defined by
\delta(q,i)=h(q)[i]
, where
h(q)=h(q)[0]\,\cdots\,h(q)[k-1]
. Then
N
computes the sequence
\bf x
.

2.4
Transducers

A
1-uniform deterministic finite-state transducer
(or
transducer
for short) is a
6
-tuple

T=\langle V,\Delta,\varphi,v_{0},\Gamma,\sigma\rangle,

where
V
is a finite set of
states
,
\Delta
is a finite set representing the
input alphabet
,
\varphi\colon V\times\Delta\to V
is the
transition function
,
v_{0}\in V
is the
initial state
,
\Gamma
is a finite set representing the
output alphabet
, and
\sigma\colon V\times\Delta\to\Gamma
is the
output function
. A transducer can be viewed as a DFA with an output function
\sigma
layered on top, where a single element of
\Gamma
is output for each transition.

We are interested in taking a sequence
{\bf x}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
transducing
it, meaning we pass the sequence through the transducer symbol by symbol, obtaining a single symbol output with each transition. Formally, the transduction of a sequence
{\bf x}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
over the alphabet
\Delta
by a transducer
T
is defined to be the infinite sequence

T({\bf x})=\sigma(v_{0},x_{0})\;\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}),x_{1})\;\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}x_{1}),x_{2})\;\cdots\;\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1}),x_{n})\;\cdots.

In this paper we only consider
1
-uniform transducers; the more general case of
t
-uniform transducers, as mentioned previously, can easily be handled by applying the appropriate
t
-uniform morphism to the output of a
1
-uniform transducer.

Example 1
.

Allouche and Bousquet-Melou
[
1
]

studied the “generalized Rudin Shapiro” sequences,
which are running sums, taken modulo 2, of the paperfolding sequences.

Let us show that if you start the sequence of
unfolding instructions

1,1,-1,1,-1,1,-1,1,-1,...,

in other words,
1
, followed by
(1,-1)
repeating,
and then apply a morphism
1\rightarrow 0
,
-1\rightarrow 1

to the resulting
resulting paperfolding word

1,1,-1,-1,1,-1,-1,\cdots
, prepend a
0
, and
transduce this with a running sum transducer, mod
2
,
you get the classical Rudin-Shapiro sequence.

We need to define a transducer
RUNSUM2
,
which transduces a sequence
a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
over
\{0,1\}
into the sequence
b_{0}b_{1}b_{2}\cdots
where the
k
’th term is the running sum mod
2
of the subsequence
a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}
, that is,
b_{k}=(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i})\bmod 2
. The transducer is illustrated in Figure
1
. See the Appendix for the
Walnut
definition of the transducer.

Figure 1:
Transducer
RUNSUM2
for running sum mod 2.

Then we use the following
Walnut
code.

reg apfcode {-1,0,1} "1(1[-1])*0*":
# make unfolding instructions 1, 1, -1, 1, -1, 1, -1, ...

def apf "?lsd_2 Ex $apfcode(x) & FOLD[x][n]=@-1":
# return i in {0,1} if paperfolding sequence equals (-1)^i at position n

def apfm "?msd_2 ‘$apf(n)":
# turn an lsd-first automaton into an msd-first

combine PF1 apfm:
# turn it into a DFAO

transduce RS3 RUNSUM2 PF1:
# transduce it with running sum mod 2 transducer

eval check "An RS3[n]=RS[n]":
# check if it’s the same as Rudin-Shapiro. It is!

Example 2
.

Consider the famous Thue-Morse sequence
\mathbf{t}=h^{\omega}(0)
defined by the
2
-uniform morphism
h\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\Sigma^{*}
given by
h(0)=01
,
h(1)=10
, with
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
. The first few terms of the sequence are:

\mathbf{t}=0110100110010110\cdots.

Suppose we wish to compute the running sum (mod
2
) of the Thue-Morse sequence. We can do this using transduction: define the transducer
T=\langle V=\{v_{0},v_{1}\},\Sigma,\varphi,v_{0},\Sigma,\sigma\rangle
where the transition function
\varphi:V\times\Sigma\to V
is defined by

\varphi(v_{0},0)=v_{0},\quad\varphi(v_{0},1)=v_{1},\quad\varphi(v_{1},0)=v_{1},\quad\varphi(v_{1},1)=v_{0},

and the output function
\sigma:V\times\Sigma\to\Sigma
is defined by

\sigma(v_{0},0)=0,\quad\sigma(v_{0},1)=1,\quad\sigma(v_{1},0)=1,\quad\sigma(v_{1},1)=0.

Then

T(\mathbf{t})=0100111011100101\cdots

is the running sum of
\mathbf{t}
.
The transducer
T
is illustrated in Figure

.
The resulting sequence
is sequence
A255817
in the

On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
(OEIS)
[
19
]
.

If we iteratively apply
T
to
\mathbf{t}
arbitrarily often to generate running sums
T(\mathbf{t}),T^{2}(\mathbf{t}),T^{3}(\mathbf{t}),\ldots
(where
T^{2}(\mathbf{t})\coloneqq T(T(\mathbf{t}),T^{3}(\mathbf{t})\coloneqq T(T(T(\mathbf{t})))
, etc.), we can plot each running sum on a separate row to get a fractal consisting of Sierpiński triangles, as illustrated in Figure
2
. This structure was also found by Prunescu
[

]

3
Dekking’s result

It turns out that the transduction of an
k
-automatic sequence always produces another
k
-automatic sequence, formalized in the next theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a
k
-automatic sequence over
\Delta
, and let
T=\langle V,\Delta,\varphi,v_{0},\Gamma,\sigma\rangle
be a transducer. Then the sequence

T(\mathbf{x})=\sigma(v_{0},x_{0})\;\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}),x_{1})\;\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}x_{1}),x_{2})\;\cdots\;\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1}),x_{n})\;\cdots

is
k
-automatic.

Dekking
[
9
]
provided a constructive argument that we use to prove this theorem, which we recapitulate for the convenience of the reader.

We first give a few definitions. Suppose that
{\bf x}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a
k
-automatic sequence, so
\mathbf{x}=\lambda({\bf q})
where
h\colon Q^{*}\to Q^{*}
is a prolongable
k
-uniform morphism, the coding
\lambda
maps
Q^{*}\to\Delta^{*}
, and
{\bf q}=h^{\omega}(q_{0})=q_{0}q_{1}q_{2}\cdots
.
For all
y\in\Delta^{*}
, define the function
f_{y}\colon V\to V
by
f_{y}(v)=\varphi^{*}(v,y)
. Note that
f_{y_{1}y_{2}}=f_{y_{2}}\circ f_{y_{1}}
for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\Delta^{*}
.
We need the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

For all
w\in Q^{*}
, the sequences
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}(w)=(f_{\lambda(w)},f_{\lambda(h(w))},f_{\lambda(h^{2}(w))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h^{n}(w))},\ldots)
are ultimately periodic with the same period and preperiod, i.e., there exist integers
p\geq 1
,
r\geq 0
such that
f_{\lambda(h^{i}(w))}=f_{\lambda(h^{p+i}(w))}
for all
w\in Q^{*}
and
i\geq r
.

Proof.

Suppose that
Q=\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}\}
. Define the map
\Phi\colon\left(V^{V}\right)^{m}\to\left(V^{V}\right)^{m}
such that for every
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\in Q^{*}
, we have

\Phi(f_{\lambda(w_{1})},\ldots,f_{\lambda(w_{m})})=(f_{\lambda(h(w_{1}))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h(w_{m}))}).

Then, for all
n\geq 1
, it is obvious that

\Phi^{n}(f_{\lambda(w_{1})},\ldots,f_{\lambda(w_{m})}))=(f_{\lambda(h^{n}(w_{1}))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h^{n}(w_{m}))}).

Recall that the
orbit
of
(f_{\lambda(q_{1})},\ldots,f_{\lambda(q_{m})})
under
\Phi
is the sequence

\displaystyle\left((f_{\lambda(q_{1})},\ldots,f_{\lambda(q_{m})}),\Phi(f_{\lambda(q_{1})},\ldots,f_{\lambda(q_{m})}),\ldots,\Phi^{n}(f_{\lambda(q_{1})},\ldots,f_{\lambda(q_{m})}),\ldots\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\left((f_{\lambda(q_{1})},\ldots,f_{\lambda(q_{m})}),(f_{\lambda(h(q_{1}))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h(q_{m}))}),\ldots,(f_{\lambda(h^{n}(q_{1}))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h^{n}(q_{m}))}),\ldots\right).

As
\left(V^{V}\right)^{m}
is a finite set, the orbit of
(f_{\lambda(q_{1})},\ldots,f_{\lambda(q_{m})})
under
\Phi
is ultimately periodic with some period
p\geq 1
and preperiod
r\geq 0
, meaning that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
with
j\geq r
, we have

(f_{\lambda(h^{j}(q_{1}))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h^{j}(q_{m}))})=(f_{\lambda(h^{p+j}(q_{1}))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h^{p+j}(q_{m}))}),

so
f_{\lambda(h^{j}(q_{i}))}=f_{\lambda(h^{p+j}(q_{i}))}
for each
i=1,\ldots,m
, and by comparing each coordinate, we see that the sequences
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}(q)=(f_{\lambda(q)},f_{\lambda(h(q))},f_{\lambda(h^{2}(q))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h^{n}(q))},\ldots)
are ultimately periodic with the same period
p
and preperiod
r
for all
q\in Q
. Now, for
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}\in Q^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\infty}(w)

\displaystyle=(f_{\lambda(w)},f_{\lambda(h(w))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h^{n}(w))},\ldots)

\displaystyle=(f_{\lambda(w_{1})\cdots\lambda(w_{\ell})},f_{\lambda(h(w_{1}))\cdots\lambda(h(w_{\ell}))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h^{n}(w_{1}))\cdots\lambda(h^{n}(w_{\ell}))},\ldots)

\displaystyle=(f_{\lambda(w_{\ell})}\circ\cdots\circ f_{\lambda(w_{1})},f_{\lambda(h(w_{\ell}))}\circ\cdots\circ f_{\lambda(h(w_{1}))},f_{\lambda(h^{n}(w_{\ell}))}\circ\cdots\circ f_{\lambda(h^{n}(w_{1}))},\ldots).

As each of the
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}(w_{i})
is ultimately periodic with period
p
and preperiod
r
, it follows that
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}(w)
is also ultimately periodic with period
p
and preperiod
r
, as desired.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
4
.

Proof of
Theorem
4
.

By Lemma
5
, the sequence
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}(w)
is ultimately periodic with the same period
p
and preperiod
r
for every
w\in Q^{*}
. For each
w\in Q^{*}
, we define

\mathcal{I}(w)=(f_{\lambda(w)},f_{\lambda(h(w))},\ldots,f_{\lambda(h^{p+r-1}(w))}).

We also define

\widetilde{Q}=\{(a,\mathcal{I}(w))\,:\,a\in Q,w\in Q^{*}\}.

(1)

We define our
k
-uniform morphism
\widetilde{h}\colon\widetilde{Q}^{*}\to\widetilde{Q}^{*}
by

\widetilde{h}(a,\mathcal{I}(w))=(h(a)_{1},\mathcal{I}(h(w)))\>(h(a)_{2},\mathcal{I}(h(w)h(a)_{1}))\>\cdots\>(h(a)_{k},\mathcal{I}(h(w)h(a)_{1}\cdots h(a)_{k-1})),

(2)

where
h(a)_{i}
denotes the
i
’th symbol of
h(a)
. Note that
(q_{0},\mathcal{I}(\epsilon))=\widetilde{q}_{0}
is the first symbol of
\widetilde{h}(q_{0},\mathcal{I}(\epsilon))
, as
h(q_{0})
starts with
q_{0}
and
h(\epsilon)=\epsilon
. We can then consider the fixed point

\widetilde{\bf q}=\left(\widetilde{q}_{n}\right)_{n\geq 0}=\widetilde{h}^{\omega}(q_{0},\mathcal{I}(\epsilon)).

Define the coding
\widetilde{\lambda}\colon\widetilde{Q}^{*}\to\Gamma^{*}
by

\widetilde{\lambda}(a,\mathcal{I}(w))=\sigma(f_{\lambda(w)}(v_{0}),\lambda(a))=\sigma(\varphi^{*}(v_{0},\lambda(w)),\lambda(a)).

(3)

We claim that
\widetilde{\lambda}(\widetilde{\bf q})=T(\mathbf{x})
, from which the theorem statement follows. To show this, it is sufficient to show that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\widetilde{q}_{n}=(q_{n},\mathcal{I}(q_{0}\cdots q_{n-1})).

(4)

We proceed inductively. For
0\leq n\leq k-1
, this follows from (
2
) and the fact that
x_{0}=\lambda(q_{0})
.
Now, for arbitrary
m\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose that (
4
) holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
0\leq n<|h^{m}(q_{0})|
. We show that (
4
) holds for
0\leq n<|h^{m+1}(q_{0})|
. Since
h
is
k
-uniform with
k>1
, we have
|h^{m+1}(q_{0})|>|h^{m}(q_{0})|
, so proving this inductive step shows that (
4
) holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. As
h^{m+1}(q_{0})=h(h^{m}(q_{0}))
, for each
n
such that
|h^{m}(q_{0})|\leq n<|h^{m+1}(q_{0})|
, there are integers
i,j
with
1\leq i\leq|h^{m}(q_{0})|
and
1\leq j\leq|h(q_{i})|=k
such that

q_{0}q_{1}\cdots q_{n}=h(q_{0}q_{1}\cdots q_{i-1})\,h(q_{i})_{1}\cdots h(q_{i})_{j},

(5)

Using
\widetilde{h}(\widetilde{\bf q})=\widetilde{\bf q}
and
|h(a)|=|\widetilde{h}(a,\mathcal{I}(w))|=k
for
a\in Q
and
w\in Q^{*}
, we get

\widetilde{q}_{0}\widetilde{q}_{1}\cdots\widetilde{q}_{n}=\widetilde{h}(\widetilde{q}_{0}\widetilde{q}_{1}\cdots\widetilde{q}_{i-1})\,\widetilde{h}(\widetilde{q}_{i})_{1}\cdots\widetilde{h}(\widetilde{q}_{i})_{j}.

(6)

It then follows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{q}_{n}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{h}(\widetilde{q}_{i})_{j}

(looking at the last symbol of both sides in (
6
))

\displaystyle=\widetilde{h}(q_{i},\mathcal{I}(q_{0}\cdots q_{i-1}))_{j}

(by the induction hypothesis and (
4
))

\displaystyle=(h(q_{i})_{j},\mathcal{I}(h(q_{0}q_{1}\cdots q_{i-1})\>h(q_{i})_{1}\cdots h(q_{i})_{j-1}))

(by the definition of
\widetilde{h}
)

\displaystyle=(q_{n},\mathcal{I}(q_{0}q_{1}\cdots q_{n-1})),

(by (
5
))

which completes the proof.
∎

4.1
Complexity analysis

The proof of Lemma
5
finds
p
and
r
by iterating through the finite set
(V^{V})^{|Q|}
until a repetition is found, which requires to iterate through at most
|V|^{|Q|\cdot|V|}
maps. Consequently we get that
p,r\leq|V|^{|Q|\cdot|V|}
. The total number of states
|\Omega|
is, in the worst case, at most
|\widetilde{Q}|\leq|Q|\cdot|V|^{(p+r)|V|}\leq|Q|\cdot|V|^{2\cdot|V|^{|Q|\cdot|V|+1}}
. To find the reachable states
\Omega
, we run a breadth-first search starting from the initial state, which, in the worst-case, takes time
\mathcal{O}(|Q|\cdot|V|^{(p+r)|V|}+k\cdot|Q|\cdot|V|^{(p+r)|V|})=\mathcal{O}((k+1)\cdot|Q|\cdot|V|^{2\cdot|V|^{|Q|\cdot|V|+1}})
.

In practice, however, we do not usually see this kind of astonishingly large worst-case complexity.

5.1
Factorial as a sum of three squares

Let
S_{3}=\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,8,\ldots\}

be the set of natural numbers that can be
expressed as the sum of three squares of natural
numbers.
A well-known theorem of Legendre (see, for example,
[

) states that

if and only if it cannot be expressed in the
form

, where
i,j\geq 0
are natural numbers. It follows that the characteristic
sequence of the set
S_{3}
is
2
-automatic,
a fact first observed by Cobham
[
7
, Example 8, p. 172]
. Indeed,

if and only if
(N)_{2}
is accepted by the automaton depicted in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Automaton accepting
(S_{3})_{2}
.

Recently a number of authors
[
10

5

6

have been interested in studying the properties of the set

. In particular, the set

is
2

denote the exponent of the highest power of

Clearly

5.2
Overlap-free Dyck words

In this section we cover another interesting application of transducers, to overlap-free Dyck words.
We need a few definitions.

A word of the form
axaxa
, where
a
is a single letter, and
x
is a (possibly empty) string, is called an
overlap
.
An example is the French word
entente
.
A string is said to be
overlap-free

if it contains no block that is an overlap.
A binary word is a
Dyck word
if it represents a string of balanced parentheses, if
1
is treated as a left paren and
0
as a right paren.
The
nesting level

N(x)
of a finite Dyck word
x
is defined as follows:

N(\epsilon)=0
,
N(1x0)=N(x)+1
if

x
is balanced, and
N(xy)=\max(N(x),N(y))
if

x,y
are both balanced.

In the paper
[
14
]
, the following result is proved:
there are arbitrarily long overlap-free binary words of nesting level
3
. Here we can prove the same result using a different, explicit construction involving the Thue-Morse morphism
\mu
, where
\mu(0)=01
and

\mu(1)=10

Theorem 6
.

Define
x_{0}=10
and
x_{n+1}=\mu(101\mu(x_{n})101)
for
n\geq 0
.

n\geq 0
. Then

is overlap-free for
n\geq 0
and

y_{n}
has nesting level
3
for
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

We start by defining the infinite binary

.
We will prove the results about the
y_{n}
, indirectly, by proving results
about
\bf d
instead.
We claim that

(a)

for
n\geq 0

(b)

for

differs from

precisely
at the indices in

is the unique infinite binary

We now prove each of the claims.

(a)

Iterated running sums of Thue-Morse

Throughout this section, we write
\Sigma_{2}=\{0,1\}

T=\langle V=\{v_{0},v_{1}\},\Sigma_{2},\varphi,v_{0},\Sigma_{2},\sigma\rangle

We first begin with a few lemmas that help us characterize the running sums.

Lemma 8
.

For any
k\geq 0
and
m\geq 1
,

\mathbf{t}_{m}[k]\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{m-1+(k-j)}{k-j}\mathbf{t}[j]\pmod{2}.

(8)

Proof.

Our proof will be by induction on
m
and

\mathbf{t}_{m}[k]\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbf{t}_{m-1}[j]\pmod{2},

(9)

for all
m\geq 1
and
k\geq 0
, it is easy to check that

\mathbf{t}_{m}[k]\equiv\mathbf{t}_{m-1}[k]+\mathbf{t}_{m}[k-1]\pmod{2}

(10)

for any

and
k\geq 1

\mathbf{t}_{m}[0]=\mathbf{t}_{m-1}[0]

(11)

for any
m\geq 1

Showing that (

) holds for all
m\geq 1
with
k=0
. is clear from (

) holds for
\mathbf{t}_{0}[0]
and by (

) we have
\mathbf{t}_{m}[0]=\mathbf{t}[0]
for all
m\geq 1
. Showing that (
8
) holds for

and all

is trivial from (

):

\mathbf{t}_{1}[k]\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbf{t}[j]\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{1-1+(k-j)}{k-j}\mathbf{t}[j]\pmod{2}.

For the inductive step, suppose that (
8
) holds for
\mathbf{t}_{m-1}[k]
and
\mathbf{t}_{m}[k-1]
for
m\geq 2
. From (

), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}_{m}[k]

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbf{t}_{m-1}[k]+\mathbf{t}_{m}[k-1]\pmod{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{m-2+(k-j)}{k-j}\mathbf{t}[j]+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{m-1+(k-1-j)}{k-1-j}\mathbf{t}[j]\pmod{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left[\binom{m-2+(k-j)}{k-j}+\binom{m-2+(k-j)}{k-1-j}\right]\mathbf{t}[j]+\binom{m-2}{0}\mathbf{t}[k]\pmod{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{m-1+(k-j)}{k-j}\mathbf{t}[j]+\binom{m-1+0}{0}\mathbf{t}[k]\pmod{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{m-1+(k-j)}{k-j}\mathbf{t}[j]\pmod{2},

completing the proof of (

Lemma 9
.

For any
k\geq 0
and
n\geq 0

\mathbf{t}_{2^{n}}[k]\equiv\mathbf{t}_{1}\left[\,\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n}}\,\right\rfloor\right]+\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right\rfloor+1\right)\mathbf{t}[k\bmod 2^{n}]\pmod{2}

(12)

Proof.

Setting
m=2^{n}
in (

\mathbf{t}_{2^{n}}[k]\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{2^{n}-1+(k-j)}{k-j}\mathbf{t}[j]\pmod{2}

(13)

By Lucas’s theorem,
\binom{2^{n}-1+(k-j)}{k-j}\equiv 1\pmod{2}
if and only if the binary representation of

does not contain any

for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
, which we may rewrite as
j=k-i2^{n}
, which then allows us to write (
13
) as

\mathbf{t}_{2^{n}}[k]\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n}}\rfloor}\mathbf{t}[k-i2^{n}]\pmod{2}

As
\mathbf{t}[k-i2^{n}]
is the sum of the bits in the binary representation of
k-i2^{n}
, we split it as

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}_{2^{n}}[k]

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n}}\rfloor}\left(\mathbf{t}\left[\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right\rfloor-i\right]+t[k\bmod 2^{n}]\right)\pmod{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n}}\rfloor}\mathbf{t}\left[i\right]+\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right\rfloor+1\right)t[k\bmod 2^{n}]\pmod{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbf{t}_{1}\left[\,\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n}}\,\right\rfloor\right]+\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n}}\right\rfloor+1\right)\mathbf{t}[k\bmod 2^{n}]\pmod{2}.

∎

From (

), we get explicit expressions for
\mathbf{t}_{2^{n}}
:

Corollary 10
.

For any
n\geq 0
and
k\geq 0
, if we let
q=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{n+2}}\right\rfloor
and

, then

\mathbf{t}_{2^{n}}[2^{n+2}q+r]\equiv\begin{cases}\mathbf{t}[q]+\mathbf{t}[r]\pmod{2},&\text{if $0\leq r\leq 2^{n}-1$};\\
1,&\text{if $2^{n}\leq r\leq 2\cdot 2^{n}-1$};\\
\mathbf{t}[q]+\mathbf{t}[r-2\cdot 2^{n}]\pmod{2},&\text{if $2\cdot 2^{n}\leq r\leq 3\cdot 2^{n}-1$};\\
0,&\text{if $3\cdot 2^{n}\leq r\leq 2^{n+2}-1$}.\end{cases}

(14)

We can also write this in a more elegant way:

Corollary 11
.

For every
n\geq 0
, we have

\mathbf{t}_{2^{n}}=(g_{n}\circ h^{\omega})(0),

where
h\colon\Sigma_{2}^{*}\to\Sigma_{2}^{*}
is the Thue-Morse morphism defined by

, and

is the

-uniform morphism defined by

.

We now present the main result of this section.

Proof of
Theorem
7
.

The idea of this proof is to use the Myhill-Nerode theorem
[
12
, Thm. 3.4]
by showing that there are exactly
2^{n+3}
equivalence classes of the Myhill-Nerode congruence. Define the language

L=\{0^{*}(k)_{2}:k\in\mathbb{N},\mathbf{t}_{2^{n}}[k]=1\}

to be the set of all binary representations of
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbf{t}_{2^{n}}[k]=1
, allowing for arbitrarily many leading zeroes. Define the
Myhill-Nerode congruence

\sim
as follows:
x\sim y
if and only if we have that
xz\in L\iff yz\in L
for all
z\in\Sigma_{2}^{*}
. This is an equivalence relation, and the Myhill-Nerode theorem (Theorem 4.1.8 of
[
2
]
) gives us that
L
is a regular language if and only if there are finitely many equivalence classes for
\sim
, and furthermore the number of equivalence classes of
\sim
is exactly the number of states of a minimal DFA that recognizes
L
. This minimal DFA can then be turned into a minimal DFAO computing
L
by defining a coding
\lambda\colon Q^{*}\to\Sigma_{2}^{*}
by
\lambda(q)=1
if

and
\lambda(q)=0
otherwise for all
q\in Q
.

First note that clearly
(k)_{2}\sim 0^{m}(k)_{2}
for any
k,m\geq 0
.
Let

, and let

,

We prove that
(k)_{2}\sim(k^{\prime})_{2}
if and only if
\mathbf{t}[q]=\mathbf{t}[q^{\prime}]
and

.

The “only if” direction follows immediately from (
14
).

We prove the “if” direction contrapositively. Recall that
\mathbf{t}[m]
is the parity of the

s in
(m)_{2}
for any

, and note that
|(r)_{2}|=|(r^{\prime})_{2}|=n+2
. The whole argument will consist of appending strings to both

by the definition of the congruence

.
Here is an example of the appending process for

\displaystyle(k)_{2}=\underbrace{110111}_{q}\underbrace{0101}_{r}\xrightarrow{\text{Append $00$}}\underbrace{11011101}_{q_{\rm new}}\underbrace{01\mathbf{00}}_{r_{\rm new}}=(k_{\rm new})_{2}

We now proceed with the cases.

Case 1:
\mathbf{t}[q]\neq\mathbf{t}[q^{\prime}]

.

For the remainder of the proof, we will imply the “new” subscript whenever we append anything to

Case 2:

6
Going beyond
k
-automatic sequences

6.1
Generalizations to non-uniform morphisms

So far, we have only worked with transducers for
k
-automatic sequences and automata that take input over base-
k
representations of numbers.
To generalize this, suppose we have a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
that is automatic in an arbitrary numeration system
\mathcal{N}
where the digits of all representations are in
\Sigma=\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, written most significant digit first (least-significant-digit-first numeration systems are considered in
6.3
).

The sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is computed by a DFAO
M=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\lambda\rangle
, where for each
q\in Q
and
a\in\Sigma
, we have
|\delta(q,a)|\in\{0,1\}
(that is, there is
at most
one edge going out of
q
on input
a
).
When
M
receives the
\mathcal{N}
-representation (i.e., the representation in the numeration system
\mathcal{N}
) of
n
as input, the automaton computes
x_{n}
.

Example 12

(Fibonacci-Thue-Morse)
.

Consider the
Fibonacci-Thue-Morse
sequence
\mathbf{ftm}=(\mathbf{ftm}[n])_{n\geq 0}
, where
\mathbf{ftm}[n]
is defined to be the sum (mod
2
) of the Fibonacci representation of
n
(as in §
2.2
). The first few terms of the sequence are as follows:

\mathbf{ftm}=01110100100011000101\cdots

The sequence
\mathbf{ftm}
is
Fibonacci-automatic
: there is a finite automaton with output
M
that computes
\mathbf{ftm}[n]
on input the Fibonacci representation of
n
. Such an automaton
M
is pictured in Figure
17
.

Figure 17:
Fibonacci automaton
M
computing
\mathbf{ftm}
.

Note that the above automaton is defined on Fibonacci representations only, i.e.,it is only defined on binary strings that do not have consecutive
1
s.

As in the example above, the automaton
M
is only defined on inputs of valid
\mathcal{N}
-representations. To allow us to apply our existing transduction algorithm to
M
, we wish to extend
M
to be defined on all base-
k
representations of numbers. We accomplish this by adding a dead state for all strings that are not
\mathcal{N}
-representations. The new automaton behaves identically to the original on all inputs that are valid
\mathcal{N}
-representations, but ends in the dead state on an input that does not correspond to a valid
\mathcal{N}
-representation.

In the example of the
FTM
automaton, this extension is depicted in Figure
18
.

Figure 18:
Extension
M^{\prime}
of Fibonacci automaton
M
to all base-
2
inputs.

Note that all inputs of
1
from states that were previously undefined on an input of
1
now go to a dead state with a special output. All valid Fibonacci representations will behave normally, while all binary strings that are not valid Fibonacci representations (i.e., strings with consecutive 1s) will end on the new dead state.

Formally, the extension of
M=\langle Q,\Sigma=\{0,\ldots,k-1\},\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\lambda\rangle
is a new DFAO
M^{\prime}=\langle Q^{\prime},\Sigma,\delta^{\prime},q_{0},\Delta^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}\rangle
, where
Q^{\prime}=Q\cup\{q_{\#}\}
,
\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta\cup\{\#\}
, and
\lambda^{\prime}:Q^{\prime}\to\Delta^{\prime}
is defined as follows:

\lambda^{\prime}(q^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\#,&\text{if $q^{\prime}=q_{\#}$};\\
\lambda(q^{\prime}),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The transition function
\delta^{\prime}:Q^{\prime}\times\Sigma\to Q
is defined as follows:

\delta^{\prime}(q^{\prime},a)=\begin{cases}q_{\#},&\text{if $q^{\prime}=q_{\#}$ or $\delta(q^{\prime},a)=\emptyset$};\\
q^{\prime\prime}\in\delta(q^{\prime},a),&\text{otherwise, where $\delta(q^{\prime},a)=\{q^{\prime\prime}\}$}.\end{cases}

Intuitively,
M^{\prime}
sends all inputs that are not valid representations to a new dead state
q_{\#}
with output
\#
. As
M^{\prime}
is now defined over all inputs in
\Sigma^{*}
, it computes a
k
-automatic sequence
y=(y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. We now show that
M^{\prime}
behaves identically as
M
on
\mathcal{N}
-representations, i.e., both automata compute the same output when given an
\mathcal{N}
-representation. The idea is for valid representations of numbers in
\mathcal{N}
to be instead read in base
k
, resulting in possibly another number. For example, the string
10100
represents
11
in Fibonacci representation, but also represents
20
in base
2
. If the base-
k
representation of
m\in\mathbb{N}
is the same as the
\mathcal{N}
-representation of some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then we want the equality
y_{m}=x_{n}
to hold. This is formalized by Theorem
13
.

Theorem 13
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

y_{m}=\begin{cases}x_{n},&\text{if there exists $n\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $[n]_{\mathcal{N}}=[m]_{k}$};\\
\#,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose that there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
[n]_{\mathcal{N}}=[m]_{k}
. Then

\displaystyle x_{n}

\displaystyle=\lambda(\delta(q_{0},[n]_{\mathcal{N}}))

\displaystyle=\lambda(\delta^{\prime}(q_{0},[n]_{\mathcal{N}}))

(as
\delta(q_{0},[n]_{\mathcal{N}})\neq\emptyset
)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}(\delta^{\prime}(q_{0},[n]_{\mathcal{N}}))

(as
\delta^{\prime}(q_{0},[n]_{\mathcal{N}})\neq q_{\#}
)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}(\delta^{\prime}(q_{0},[m]_{k}))

\displaystyle=y_{m}.

Conversely, if
y_{m}\neq\#
, then by definition of
M^{\prime}
we must have that
[m]_{k}
is a valid representation in
\mathcal{N}
of some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,
[n]_{\mathcal{N}}=[m]_{k}
. Therefore, if such an integer
n
does not exist, we must have
y_{m}=\#
, completing the proof.
∎

We can generalize this with Lemma
14
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
[n]_{\mathcal{N}}=[m]_{k}
. Then

y_{0}\cdots y_{m}=s_{0}\,x_{0}\,s_{1}\,\cdots\,s_{n-1}\,x_{n-1}\,s_{n}\,x_{n},

where
s_{i}\in\{\#\}^{*}
for all
i=0,\ldots,n
.

Proof.

Let
0=\alpha_{0}<\ldots<\alpha_{n}=m
where
\alpha_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
is the value when the representation of
i=0,\ldots,n
in
\mathcal{N}
is instead read in base-
k
representation, i.e.,
[\alpha_{i}]_{k}=[i]_{\mathcal{N}}
. By Theorem
13
, we have that
x_{i}=y_{\alpha_{i}}
for all
i=0,\ldots,n
. For
\gamma\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
such that
\gamma\neq\alpha_{i}
for all
i=0,\ldots,n
, we know that
[\gamma]_{k}
is not a valid representation in
\mathcal{N}
, hence
y_{\gamma}=\#
by Theorem
13
. The claim follows.
∎

Suppose we wish to transduce
M
through the transducer
T=\langle V,\Delta,\varphi,v_{0},\Gamma,\sigma\rangle
. Instead of transducing
M
directly, we transduce the extension
M^{\prime}
through an extension
T^{\prime}
of
T
. The extension is defined by
T^{\prime}:=\langle V,\Delta^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime},v_{0},\Gamma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle
, where the input and output alphabets are extended to include
\#
; i.e.,
\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta\cup\{\#\}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\cup\{\#\}
, and
\varphi^{\prime}:V\times\Delta^{\prime}\to V
is defined as follows:

\varphi^{\prime}(v,a)=\begin{cases}v,&\text{if $a=\#$};\\
\varphi(v,a),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The output function
\sigma^{\prime}:V\times\Delta^{\prime}\to\Gamma^{\prime}
is defined as follows:

\sigma^{\prime}(v,a)=\begin{cases}\#,&\text{if $a=\#$};\\
\sigma(v,a),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

As
M^{\prime}
now generates a
k
-automatic sequence
y=(y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, we may apply Dekking’s method to transduce
M^{\prime}
through
T^{\prime}
, giving a new
k
-automatic sequence

.

Theorem 15
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
[n]_{\mathcal{N}}=[m]_{k}
. Then
T^{\prime}(y)_{m}=T(x)_{n}
.

Proof.

We see that

\displaystyle T^{\prime}(y)_{m}=T(x)_{n}

\displaystyle\iff\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1}),x_{n})=\sigma^{\prime}(\varphi^{\prime}(v_{0},y_{0}\cdots y_{m-1}),y_{m})

\displaystyle\iff\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1}),x_{n})=\sigma^{\prime}(\varphi^{\prime}(v_{0},y_{0}\cdots y_{m-1}),x_{n})

(by Theorem
13
)

\displaystyle\iff\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1}),x_{n})=\sigma(\varphi^{\prime}(v_{0},y_{0}\cdots y_{m-1}),x_{n})

(as
x_{n}\neq\#
)

\displaystyle\iff\sigma(\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1}),x_{n})=\sigma(\varphi^{\prime}(v_{0},s_{0}x_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{n-1}x_{n-1}s_{n}),x_{n})

by Lemma
14
, where

for all
i=0,\ldots,n
.
Therefore, it suffices to show that
\varphi^{\prime}(v_{0},s_{0}\,x_{0}\,s_{1}\,\cdots\,s_{n-1}\,x_{n-1}\,s_{n})=\varphi(v_{0},x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1})
, but this follows from the definition of
\varphi^{\prime}
, completing the proof.
∎

Thus we get

.

If

is a generalized automatic sequence
over a numeration system
\cal N
, and

is
a deterministic

-uniform finite state transducer, then

is also a generalized automatic sequence over
\cal N
.

6.3
Transducing lsd-first automatic sequences

In this section, we consider the transduction of automata that read in least-significant-digit-first representations to compute elements of an automatic sequence. We say that a DFAO
M
computing an automatic sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
lsd_k
if it computes computes
x_{n}
when it reads the lsd-
k
representation of
n
.

Clearly, a DFAO
M
reads in a most-significant-digit-first representation
d_{t}d_{t-1}\cdots d_{1}d_{0}
of
n
and computes
x_{n}
, then the reversal
M^{R}
of
M
will compute
x_{n}
when it reads the least-significant-digit-first representation
d_{0}d_{1}\cdots d_{t-1}d_{t}
of
n
, and vice versa. This is formalized by a theorem of Allouche and Shallit
[
2
]
:

Theorem 19

(4.3.3-4.3.4 of Allouche and Shallit
[
2
]
)
.

If a DFAO
M=\langle Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},\Delta,\lambda\rangle
with
n
states computes a function
f\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\Delta
, then there is a DFAO
M^{R}
with at most
|\Delta|^{n}
states that computes
f^{R}\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\Delta
defined by
f^{R}(w)=f(w^{R})
.

Suppose that we wish to transduce an lsd-
k
DFAO
M
that computes an automatic sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with a transducer
T
. To leverage our existing transduction algorithms for msd-
k
automata, we can instead transduce the msd-
k
reversal
M^{R}
, giving us a new msd-
k
automaton
N^{R}
that computes
T(\mathbf{x})
. Reversing
N^{R}
then gives us an lsd-
k
automaton
N
that also computes
T(\mathbf{x})
.
