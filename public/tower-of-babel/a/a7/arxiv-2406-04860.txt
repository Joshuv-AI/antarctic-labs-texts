Multi-View Stochastic Block Models
Vincent Cohen-Addad, Tommaso d’Orsi, Silvio Lattanzi, Rajai Nasser

Abstract
Graph clustering is a central topic in unsupervised learning with a multitude of practical applications. In recent years, multi-view graph clustering has gained a lot of attention for its applicability to real-world instances where one has access to multiple data sources. In this paper we formalize a new family of models, called multi-view stochastic block models that captures this setting. For this model, we first study efficient algorithms that naively work on the union of multiple graphs. Then, we introduce a new efficient algorithm that provably outperforms previous approaches by analyzing the structure of each graph separately. Furthermore, we complement our results with an information-theoretic lower bound studying the limits of what can be done in this model. Finally, we corroborate our results with experimental evaluations.

Introduction

Clustering graphs is a fundamental topic in unsupervised learning. It is used in a variety of fields, including data mining, social sciences, statistics, and more. The goal of graph clustering is to partition the vertices of the graph into disjoint sets so that similar vertices are grouped together and dissimilar vertices lie in different clusters. In this context, several notions of similarity between vertices have been studied throughout the years resulting in different clustering objectives and clustering algorithms
(
Von Luxburg, 2007
;
Ng et al., 2001
;
Bansal et al., 2004
;
Goldberg, 1984
;
Dasgupta, 2016
)
.

Despite the rich literature, most of the algorithmic results in graph clustering only focus on the setting where a single graph is presented in input. This is in contrast with the increasing practical importance of multimodality and with the growing attention in applied fields to multi-view or multi-layer clustering
(
Paul & Chen, 2016
;
Corneli et al., 2016
;
Han et al., 2015
;
De Bacco et al., 2017
;
Khan & Maji, 2019
;
Zhong & Pun, 2021
;
Hu et al., 2019
;
Abavisani & Patel, 2018
;
Kim et al., 2016
;
Gujral et al., 2020
;
Ni et al., 2016
;
De Santiago et al., 2023
;
Papalexakis et al., 2013
;
Gujral & Papalexakis, 2018
;
Gorovits et al., 2018
)
.
In practice it is in fact observed that while a single data source only offers a specific characterization of the underlying objects, leading to a coarse partition of the data, a careful combination of multiple views often allows a semantically richer network structure to emerge
(
Fu et al., 2020
;
Fang et al., 2023
)
.
For a practical example, consider the task of clustering users of a social network platform like Facebook, Instagram or X. To solve such task one could simply cluster the friendship graph, or one could cluster such graph by looking together at the friendship graph, the co-like graph (a graph where two users are connected if they like the same picture/video), the co-comment graph (a graph where two users are connected if they comment on the same post), the co-repost graph (a graph where two users are connected if they repost the same post) and so on and so forth. In practice, one would expect the second approach to work better in many settings because it provides a more fine-grained description of the behaviors of the users.

Despite the large number of basic applications, very little is known on the theoretical aspect
of the problem. Several works
(
Paul & Chen, 2016
;
Corneli et al., 2016
;
Han et al., 2015
;
De Bacco et al., 2017
)
consider the multi-layer stochastic block models where the goal is to identify

k
communities given several instances (i.e., layer or view) of the stochastic block model,
each with
k
communities. In this paper, we would
like to work in a more general and more realistic setup, where there are
k
communities but each instance only provides
partial information
about these
k
communities.
Very recently and concurrently to us,
(
De Santiago et al., 2023
)
introduced the
multi-view stochastic block model
. In this model, one is given in input multiple graphs, each coming from a stochastic block model, and the goal is to leverage the information contained in the graphs to recover the underlying clustering structure.
More precisely, given a vector of labels

Armed with this new model we study different approaches to cluster the input graphs

Before formally introducing our model, we recall the classic definition of the stochastic block model.

The

In the context of multimodality, we define the following multi-view model.

Model 1.1

(Multi-View stochastic block model)
.

Let
k\geqslant 1
and let
\mathcal{T}
be a sequence of
t
tuples
(d_{\ell},\varepsilon_{\ell})
where
d_{\ell}\geqslant 0
and
\varepsilon_{\ell}\in(0,1)\,.

We refer to the following joint distribution
(\mathbf{z},(\mathbf{f_{1},\mathbf{G}_{1}}),\ldots(\mathbf{f}_{t},\mathbf{G}_{t}))\sim\textnormal{($\mathcal{T}$,k,t)-}\mathsf{MV\textnormal{-}SBM}_{n}
as the
(\mathcal{T},k,t)
-multi-view stochastic block model:

1.

for each
\ell\in[t]
, independently draw a mapping
\mathbf{f}_{\ell}:[k]\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
uniformly at random;

2.

independently draw a vector
\mathbf{z}
from
[k]^{n}
uniformly at random;

3.

for each
\ell\in[t]
, independently draw a graph
\mathbf{G}_{\ell}\sim\mathsf{SBM}_{n,2,d_{\ell},\varepsilon_{\ell}}(\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}))
, where
\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})
is the
n
-dimensional vector with entries
\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\,.

Given
\mathbf{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{G}_{t}
, the goal is to approximately recover the unknown vector
\mathbf{z}
of labels.

When
\mathcal{T}=\{(d_{\ell},\varepsilon_{\ell})\}_{\ell\in[t]}
is such that
(d_{\ell},\varepsilon_{\ell})=(d,\varepsilon)
for some
d,\varepsilon,
we denote the model simply by
\textnormal{(d,$\varepsilon$,k,t)-}\mathsf{MV\textnormal{-}SBM}_{n}
.

Although
Model

1.1
captures the algorithmic phenomena of multi-view models used in practice, more general versions of
Model

1.1
could be defined, we discuss them in
Section

6
.
Similarly to the vanilla stochastic block model, weak recovery can also be defined for
Model

1.1
. We say that an algorithm achieves weak recovery for
\textnormal{($\mathcal{T}$,k,t)-}\mathsf{MV\textnormal{-}SBM}_{n}
with
t
observations, if it outputs a vector
\hat{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{G}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{G}_{t})
satisfying:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(R(\hat{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{G}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{G}_{t}),\mathbf{z})\geqslant\frac{1}{k}+\Omega(1)\right)\geqslant 1-o_{t}(1)\,.

(3)

Differently from the vanilla stochastic block model, the complexity of
Model

1.1
is governed both by the
\mathsf{SBM}
parameters in
\mathcal{T}

and
by the number of observations
t
. A good algorithm should then achieve weak recovery with the best possible multiway tradeoff between the edge-densities of the graphs, the biases and the number of observations at hand.
That is, extract as much information as possible so to require as few observations as possible. This novel interplay of parameters immediately raises two natural questions, which are the main focus of this work.

How many observations are needed?

The problem gets easier the larger the number of observations one has access to (see
Appendix

A
for a formal proof).
It is also easy to see that for
t=o(\log k)
, it is information theoretically
impossible
to approximately recover the communities (since
\log_{2}k
bits are needed to encode
k
labels). Furthermore, as we will see, stronger lower bounds can also be obtained.

How many observations suffice?

To understand how many observations suffice to recover the communities, it is instructive to consider the union graph
\mathbf{G}^{*}=\bigcup_{\ell\in[t]}\mathbf{G}_{\ell}
of an instance from
\textnormal{(d,$\varepsilon$,k,t)-}\mathsf{MV\textnormal{-}SBM}_{n}
, which turns out to follow a
k
-communities stochastic block model with parameters
d^{*}=\Theta(dt)\,,\varepsilon^{*}=\Theta(\varepsilon)
(see
Appendix

A
).
Building on the aforementioned results, this implies that at least
t\geqslant\Omega(k^{2}/d\varepsilon^{2})
observations are needed for efficient weak recovery of the communities from
\mathbf{G}^{*}
! However, as we show later, exponentially better algorithms can bridge this gap.

Techniques

We outline here the main ideas behind

Behavior of the union graph

The algorithm behind
Theorem

1.2
is remarkably simple and leverages known algorithms for weak recovery of stochastic block models (particularly related to the robust algorithms of
(
Ding et al., 2022
;
Ding et al., 2023
)
).
As a first step, to gain intuition, it is instructive to understand why the union graph instead requires
t\geqslant\Omega(k^{2})
observations (see
Theorem

A.1
).
An instance
\bm{\mathcal{I}}
of
\textnormal{(d,$\varepsilon$,k,t)-}\mathsf{MV\textnormal{-}SBM}_{n}
is given by a vector
\mathbf{z}\in[k]^{n}
and a collection of
t
independent pairs
(\mathbf{f}_{1},\mathbf{G}_{1})\,,\ldots\,,(\mathbf{f}_{t},\mathbf{G}_{t})
, where each
\mathbf{G}_{\ell}
is sampled from
\mathsf{SBM}_{2,d,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}))\,.
Since, by definition, each edge
\{i,j\}
appears in
\mathbf{G}_{\ell}
with probability
\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\right)\cdot\tfrac{d}{n}\,,
in the union graph
\bigcup_{\ell\in[t]}\mathbf{G}_{\ell}
the same edge appears roughly with probability

\displaystyle\frac{d}{n}\cdot\sum_{\ell\in[t]}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\right)\,.

The intuition here is that if
\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}
the second term in the sum would be close to
0
, while for
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{j}
it would be
\frac{\varepsilon t}{2}\,.

Hence, we will see this edge in the union graph roughly with probability

\displaystyle\frac{dt}{n}\cdot(1+O(\varepsilon)\cdot\llbracket\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{j}\rrbracket)\,.

That is, the union graph behaves similarly to a vanilla stochastic block model with
k
communities, bias
O(\varepsilon)
and expected degree
dt\,.

As stated in the introduction, existing efficient algorithms can achieve weak recovery for that distribution whenever
(dt)\varepsilon^{2}/k^{2}>\Omega(1)\,,
implying
t\geqslant\Omega(k^{2})
in the regime
4<d\varepsilon^{2}\leqslant O(1)
where weak recovery for each observation is possible.

Amplifying the signal-to-noise ratio via black-box estimators

The above approach of taking the union graph and then running community detection on it yields sub-optimal guarantees because the graph does not keep all information regarding the instance
\bm{\mathcal{I}}\,.

Our strategy to overcome this issue is to proceed in the reverse order:
first
extract as much information as possible from each graph, and
then
combine the data.
Concretely, in the context of
\textnormal{(d,$\varepsilon$,k,t)-}\mathsf{MV\textnormal{-}SBM}_{n}
, our plan is to accurately estimate the matrix
\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}){\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
for each graph
\mathbf{G}_{\ell}\,.

Indeed, the polynomial
\sum_{\ell\in[t]}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}
strongly correlates with
\llbracket\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{j}\rrbracket
in the sense that

\displaystyle t=

\displaystyle\E\left[\sum_{\ell\in[t]}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{j}\right]>\E\left[\sum_{\ell\in[t]}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}\right]=0\,.

In other words, we can accurately estimate whether
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{j}
or not by accurately estimating the products
\sum_{\ell\in[t]}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\,.

Information theoretic lower bounds for black-box algorithms

The proof of

consists of two main steps. In the first step, we bound how much information an estimate

and

|D_{x,x^{\prime}}|\leqslant 4\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}n\,,

where

D_{x,x^{\prime}}=|i\in[n]:x_{i}\neq x_{i}^{\prime}|\,.

Now construct a random graph
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
as follows:

•

If
i,j\in[n]\setminus D_{x,x^{\prime}}\,,
i.e., if
x_{i}=x_{i}^{\prime}
and
x_{j}=x_{j}^{\prime}\,,
then we let
\{i,j\}\in\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
if and only if
\{i,j\}\in\mathbf{G}
.

•

If either
i\in D_{x,x^{\prime}}
or
j\in D_{x,x^{\prime}}\,
then we put the edge
\{i,j\}
in
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
with probability
\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}x_{i}^{\prime}\cdot x_{j}^{\prime}\right)\,.

•

The events
\left(\{i,j\}\in\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\right)_{i,j\in[n]}
are mutually independent.

It is not hard to see that:

•

\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{SBM}_{n,2,d,\varepsilon}(x^{\prime})\,,
and

•

\mathbf{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 4\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}}}{{\approx}}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\,,
i.e.,
\mathbf{G}
can be obtained from
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
by adding or removing edges incident to at most
4\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}n
vertices.

Since
4\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}\leqslant\frac{4}{100}\mu_{\delta}\leqslant\mu_{\delta}
, it follows from
Theorem

C.2
that

\E\left[\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),x^{\prime}x^{\prime}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}=x\right]\geqslant C_{\delta}\cdot n^{2}\,.

(25)

Now notice that

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),xx{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle=\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),x^{\prime}x^{\prime}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle+\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),xx{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-x^{\prime}x^{\prime}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle\,,

and

\displaystyle\left\lvert\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),xx{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-x^{\prime}x^{\prime}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G})\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\left\lVert xx{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-x^{\prime}x^{\prime}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{1}\leqslant 1\cdot\left\lVert xx{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-x^{\prime}x^{\prime}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left\lVert x-x^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}\cdot n=4|D_{x,x^{\prime}}|\cdot n\leqslant 16\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}\cdot n^{2}\,.

Combining this with (
25
), we get

\displaystyle\E\left[\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),xx{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}=x\right]\geqslant\left(C_{\delta}-16\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}\right)\cdot n^{2}\geqslant\frac{84}{100}C_{\delta}\cdot n^{2}\,.

Now since this is true for all
x\in\mathcal{E}\,,
we conclude that

\displaystyle\E\left[\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}\right]\geqslant\left(C_{\delta}-16\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}\right)\cdot n^{2}\geqslant\frac{84}{100}C_{\delta}\cdot n^{2}\,,

where the last inequality is true because
\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{100}\min\{\mu_{\delta},C_{\delta}\}
.
Combining the above with (
24
), we can deduce that for
n
large enough, we have

\displaystyle\E\left[\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle\right]\geqslant\frac{3}{4}C_{\delta}\cdot n^{2}\,.

∎

The following lemma takes the algorithm of
Lemma

C.3
and applies a symmetrization argument in order to get “a positive correlation at the edge level”.

Lemma C.4

(Pair-wise weak recovery for sufficiently balanced 2 communities stochastic block mode)
.

Let
n,d,\varepsilon,p,\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
and
\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{SBM}_{n,2,d,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
be as in
Theorem

3.1
. Let
\delta=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d}{4}-1>0
and let
\gamma=\left\lvert\frac{1}{2}-p\right\rvert
be the unbalancedness of
\mathbf{x}
. There exist constants
\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}>0
and
C_{\delta}^{\prime}>0
and a randomized polynomial-time algorithm
17
17

17

The subscript
\mathrm{sb}
in
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}
stands for “sufficiently balanced”.

\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}
taking
\mathbf{G}
as input and producing a matrix
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})\in[-1,+1]^{n\times n}
such that if

\gamma\leqslant\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}\,,

then for every
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle C_{\delta}^{\prime}\leqslant\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]-\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\,.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{\delta},C_{\delta}
and
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}
be as in
Theorem

C.2
, and let
\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}=C_{\delta}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{100}\min\{\mu_{\delta},C_{\delta}\}
.
Lemma

C.3
shows that

\displaystyle\E\left[\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle\right]\geqslant\frac{3}{4}C_{\delta}\cdot n^{2}\,.

The algorithm
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})
can be obtained by “symmetrizing” the algorithm
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}
as follows: Let
{\bm{\sigma}}
be a (uniformly) random permutation
[n]\to[n]
and let
\mathbf{G}_{{\bm{\sigma}}}
be the graph obtained from
\mathbf{G}
by
{\bm{\sigma}}
-permuting its vertices, i.e., we let the edge
18
18

18

For simplicity, We denote
{\bm{\sigma}}(i)
as
{\bm{\sigma}}_{i}
.

\{{\bm{\sigma}}_{i},{\bm{\sigma}}_{j}\}
belong to
\mathbf{G}_{\bm{\sigma}}
if and only if
\{i,j\}\in\mathbf{G}
. We define the matrix
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})\in[-1,+1]^{n}
as follows:

\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}=\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{r}(\mathbf{G}_{{\bm{\sigma}}})_{{\bm{\sigma}}_{i}{\bm{\sigma}}_{j}}\,.

In other words, we apply the random permutation
{\bm{\sigma}}
to graph
\mathbf{G}\,,
we apply the algorithm
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}\,,
and then we apply the inverse of the permutation on the resulting matrix.

Due to the symmetry of the SBM distribution, it is not hard to see that for every
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[n]:\\
i^{\prime}\neq j^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\bm{\sigma}}_{i}=i^{\prime},{\bm{\sigma}}_{j}=j^{\prime}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left({\bm{\sigma}}_{i}=i^{\prime},{\bm{\sigma}}_{j}=j^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[n]:\\
i^{\prime}\neq j^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{r}(\mathbf{G}_{{\bm{\sigma}}})_{{\bm{\sigma}}_{i}{\bm{\sigma}}_{j}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\bm{\sigma}}_{i}=i^{\prime},{\bm{\sigma}}_{j}=j^{\prime}\right]\cdot\frac{1}{n(n-1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ast)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[n]:\\
i^{\prime}\neq j^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{r}(\mathbf{G})_{{\bm{\sigma}}_{i}{\bm{\sigma}}_{j}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{{\bm{\sigma}}_{i}}\mathbf{x}_{{\bm{\sigma}}_{j}}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\bm{\sigma}}_{i}=i^{\prime},{\bm{\sigma}}_{j}=j^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[n]:\\
i^{\prime}\neq j^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G})_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i^{\prime}}\mathbf{x}_{j^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\E\left[\langle\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G}),\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rangle\right]-\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\in[n]}\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\mathbf{G})_{ii}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\cdot\frac{3}{4}C_{\delta}\cdot n^{2}-\frac{1}{n-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{3}{4}C_{\delta}-o(1)\,,

where
(\ast)
follows from the symmetry of the SBM distribution under the simultaneous permutation of vertices and labels.

Now notice that

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right)-\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\cdot\left(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2}\right)-\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\cdot\left(2p(1-p)\right)

\displaystyle=\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}+2\gamma^{2}\right)-\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\gamma^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]+2\gamma^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]+2\gamma^{2}\,,

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
\left\lvert\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\right\rvert\leqslant 1
. We can deduce that for
n
large enough, we have

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle-\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 2\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]-8\gamma^{2}\geqslant\frac{3}{2}C_{\delta}-o(1)-8\gamma^{2}\geqslant C_{\delta}\,,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that

8\gamma^{2}\leqslant 8\mu_{\delta}^{\prime 2}\leqslant 8\left(\frac{1}{100}C_{\delta}\right)^{2}\leqslant\frac{C_{\delta}}{100}\,.

By picking
C_{\delta}^{\prime}=C_{\delta}\,,
the lemma follows.
∎

Now we turn to show that if
\mathbf{x}
is sufficiently unbalanced, then there exists an efficient algorithm that achieves pair-wise weak recovery.

Lemma C.5

(Pair-wise weak recovery for sufficiently unbalanced 2 communities stochastic block mode)
.

Let
n,d,\varepsilon,p,\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
and
\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{SBM}_{n,2,d,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
be as in
Theorem

3.1
. Further assume
that
p\in[0,1]
. Let
\delta=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d}{4}-1>0
and let
\gamma=\left\lvert\frac{1}{2}-p\right\rvert
be the unbalancedness of
\mathbf{x}
, and let
\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}
be as in
Lemma

C.4
. There exists a constant
C_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}>0
and a randomized polynomial-time algorithm

19
19

19

The subscript
\mathrm{su}
in
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}
stands for “sufficiently unbalanced”.

\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}
taking
\mathbf{G}
as input and producing a matrix
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G})\in[-1,+1]^{n\times n}
such that if

\gamma\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}\,,

then for every
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle C_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}\leqslant\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]-\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G})_{{ij}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\,.

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity we may assume without loss of generality that
p>\frac{1}{2}
, i.e.,
p=\frac{1}{2}+\gamma
and hence “
+1
” is the larger community in expectation.

For every
i,j\in[n]
, let

\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)=\left\lvert\left\{v\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}:\{i,v\}\in\mathbf{G}\right\}\right\rvert

be the number of vertices in
[n]\setminus\{i,j\}
which are adjacent to
i
in
\mathbf{G}
.

Let
C>0
be a large enough constant (to be chosen later) and let

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)\cdot\left\llbracket\left\lvert\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right\rvert\leqslant C\right\rrbracket\in[-1,1]\,,

and define the matrix
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G})\in[-1,+1]^{n\times n}
as:

\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}=\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})\cdot\hat{x}_{j}^{(i)}(\mathbf{G})\,.

It is not hard to see that given
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\,,
the random variables
\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)
and
\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq i}(j)
are conditionally independent. Therefore,
\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})
and
\hat{x}_{j}^{(i)}(\mathbf{G})
are conditionally independent given
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\,,
hence

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\cdot\E\left[\hat{x}_{j}^{(i)}(\mathbf{G})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]\cdot\E\left[\hat{x}_{j}^{(i)}(\mathbf{G})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\,.

Now, for every
v\in[v]\setminus\{i,j\}\,,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\{i,v\}\in\mathbf{G}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\E\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\{i,v\}\in\mathbf{G}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{v}\right)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]=\E\left[\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{v}\right)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}\E[\mathbf{x}_{v}]\right)=\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\cdot\left(p-(1-p)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\,.

Therefore, the conditional distribution of
\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)
given
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is
\mathrm{Binomial}\left(n-2,\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)
, hence

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=(n-2)\cdot\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\,,

and so

\E\left[\frac{1}{C}\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]=\frac{d(1-2/n)\cdot{\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}}}{C}\,.

(26)

On the other hand, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\E

\displaystyle\left[\left\lvert\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})-\frac{1}{C}\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)\right\rvert\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\left[\frac{1}{C}\left\lvert\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right\rvert\cdot\left\llbracket\left\lvert\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right\rvert>C\right\rrbracket\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{C}\E\left[\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]^{1/2}\cdot\E\left[\left\llbracket\left\lvert\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right\rvert>C\right\rrbracket^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{C}\E\left[\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right\rvert>C\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)^{1/2}\,,

and by Chebychev’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right\rvert>C\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\E\left[\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]}{C^{2}}\,,

hence

\E\left[\left\lvert\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})-\frac{1}{C}\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)\right\rvert\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]\leqslant\frac{\E\left[\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]}{C^{2}}\,.

(27)

Now notice that

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\left(\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)+\left(\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)d(1-2/n)\right)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ast)}}{{\leqslant}}\E\left[2\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)^{2}+2\left(\left(\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)d(1-2/n)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\E\left[\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]+2d^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{2}\,,

where
(\ast)
is true because
(a+b)^{2}\leqslant 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now since the conditional distribution of
\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)
given
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is
\mathrm{Binomial}\left(n-2,\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)
, its conditional expectation is equal to
d(1-2/n)\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)
and its conditional variance is equal to

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=(n-2)\left(\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\cdot\left(1-\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant d(1+\varepsilon\gamma)\leqslant 2d\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)^{2}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 4d+2d^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{2}\,,

and hence from (
27
) we get

\displaystyle\E

\displaystyle\left[\left\lvert\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})-\frac{1}{C}\left(\mathrm{\textbf{deg}}_{\neq j}(i)-d(1-2/n)\right)\right\rvert\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]\leqslant\frac{4d+2d^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{2}}{C^{2}}\,.

Combining this with (
26
) we get

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d(1-2/n)\cdot\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}}{C}\pm O\left(\frac{d+d^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{2}}{C^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d(1-2/n)}{C}\left(\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\pm O\left(\frac{1+d\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{2}}{C}\right)\right)\,.

Let

C=\tilde{C}(1-2/n)\left(d\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\varepsilon\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}}\right)\,,

for some large enough constant
\tilde{C}\geqslant 1
to be chosen later. We have

O\left(\frac{1}{C}\right)\leqslant O\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{C}(1-2/n)/(\varepsilon\mu^{\prime}_{\delta})}\right)=O\left(\frac{\varepsilon\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}}{\tilde{C}}\right)\leqslant O\left(\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{\tilde{C}}\right)\,,

where the last inequality follows from
\gamma\geqslant\frac{\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}}{2}
. Furthermore,

O\left(\frac{d\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{2}}{C}\right)\leqslant O\left(\frac{d\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\tilde{C}(1-2/n)\cdot d\varepsilon}\right)\leqslant O\left(\frac{\varepsilon\gamma^{2}}{\tilde{C}}\right)\leqslant O\left(\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{\tilde{C}}\right)\,.

We conclude that

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d(1-2/n)}{C}\left(\varepsilon\gamma\mathbf{x}_{i}\pm O\left(\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{\tilde{C}}\right)\right)=\frac{d(1-2/n)\varepsilon\gamma}{C}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{C}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d\varepsilon\gamma}{\tilde{C}\left(d\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\varepsilon\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}}\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{C}}\right)\right)=C^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}\gamma\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{C}}\right)\right)\,,

where

\displaystyle C^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{d\varepsilon^{2}}{\tilde{C}\left(d\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}}\right)}=\frac{4(1+\delta)}{\tilde{C}\left(4(1+\delta)+\frac{1}{\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}}\right)}=\frac{4(1+\delta)\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}}{\tilde{C}\left(4(1+\delta)\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}+1\right)}\,.

Notice how
C^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}
depends only on
\delta\,.

Finally,

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}(\mathbf{G})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right]\cdot\E\left[\hat{x}_{j}^{(i)}(\mathbf{G})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=C^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}\gamma\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{C}}\right)\right)\cdot C^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}\gamma\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{C}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(C^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}\gamma)^{2}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{C}}\right)\right)\,,

and so

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]-\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=(C^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}\gamma)^{2}\left(2\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{C}}\right)\right)\,.

By choosing
\tilde{C}
to be an absolute constant which is large enough, we get

\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]-\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\geqslant(C^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}\gamma)^{2}\geqslant\frac{(C^{\prime\prime}_{\delta}\mu_{\delta}^{\prime})^{2}}{4}\,,

where the last inequality is true because we assume that
\gamma\geqslant\frac{\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}}{2}
.
∎

Now we can leverage

Lemma

C.4
and
Lemma

C.5
in order to prove
Theorem

3.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Let
\gamma,\mu_{\delta}^{\prime},C_{\delta}^{\prime},C_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}
be as in
Lemma

C.4
and
Lemma

C.5
.

We will first distinguish between the sufficiently balanced and sufficiently unbalanced cases by counting the number of edges which are incident to a random sublinear (but sufficiently high) set of vertices. Let
m=\lceil n^{3/4}\rceil
and let
\mathbf{I}
be a random subset of
[n]
of size
m
.

Let
\mathrm{deg}(\mathbf{I})
be the number of edges in
\mathbf{G}
from
\mathbf{I}
to
[n]\setminus\mathbf{I}
. We have

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathrm{deg}(\mathbf{I})\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{I}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I},j\in[n]\setminus\mathbf{I}}\E\left[\left\llbracket\{i,j\}\in\mathbf{G}\right\rrbracket\right]=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I},j\in[n]\setminus\mathbf{I}}\E\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\{i,j\}\in\mathbf{G}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I},j\in[n]\setminus\mathbf{I}}\E\left[\frac{d}{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right)\right]=\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I},j\in[n]\setminus\mathbf{I}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\E\left[\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I},j\in[n]\setminus\mathbf{I}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I},j\in[n]\setminus\mathbf{I}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\left(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2}-2p(1-p)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{dm(n-m)}{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\left(2p-1\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{dm(n-m)}{n}\left(1+2\varepsilon\gamma^{2}\right)=(1\pm o(1))dn^{3/4}\left(1+2\varepsilon\gamma^{2}\right)\,.

So the algorithm
\hat{\mathbf{X}}
is defined as follows:

•

If
\mathrm{deg}(\mathbf{I})\geqslant dn^{3/4}\left(1+2\varepsilon\left(\frac{3\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}}{4}\right)^{2}\right)\,
we apply the algorithm
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}
on the subgraph
\mathbf{G}([n]\setminus\mathbf{I})
of
\mathbf{G}
induced on
n\setminus\mathbf{I}
and define

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}=\begin{cases}\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{su}}(\mathbf{G}([n]\setminus\mathbf{I}))_{ij}\quad&\text{if }i,j\in[n]\setminus\mathbf{I}\,,\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

•

If
\mathrm{deg}(\mathbf{I})<dn^{3/4}\left(1+2\varepsilon\left(\frac{3\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}}{4}\right)^{2}\right)\,
we apply the algorithm
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}
on the subgraph
\mathbf{G}([n]\setminus\mathbf{I})
of
\mathbf{G}
induced on
n\setminus\mathbf{I}
and define

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{G})_{ij}=\begin{cases}\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}(\mathbf{G}([n]\setminus\mathbf{I}))_{ij}\quad&\text{if }i,j\in[n]\setminus\mathbf{I}\,,\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

By standard concentration inequalities, we can show that:

•

If
\gamma<\frac{\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}}{2}\,,
then with probability
1-o(1)
we have
\mathrm{deg}(\mathbf{I})<dn^{3/4}\left(1+2\varepsilon\left(\frac{3\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}}{4}\right)^{2}\right)\,
and so we apply the algorithm
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{sb}}
which will succeed in achieving pair-wise weak recovery according to
Lemma

C.4
, assuming
i,j\in[I]
.

•

If
\gamma\geqslant\frac{\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}}{2}\,,
then with probability
1-o(1)
we have
\mathrm{deg}(\mathbf{I})\geqslant dn^{3/4}\left(1+2\varepsilon\left(\frac{3\mu_{\delta}^{\prime}}{4}\right)^{2}\right)\,
and so we apply the algorithm
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{ub}}
which will succeed in achieving pair-wise weak recovery according to
Lemma

C.5
, assuming
i,j\in[I]
.

•

If
\frac{\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}}{2}<\gamma<\mu^{\prime}_{\delta}\,,
then it follows from
Lemma

C.4
and
Lemma

C.5
that it does not matter which algorithm we apply because both of them achieve pair-wise weak recovery, assuming
i,j\in[I]
.

Now for any
i,j\in[n]\,,
the probability of picking any of them in
\mathbf{I}
is vanishingly small. We conclude that the algorithm
\hat{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies the guarantees sought in
Theorem

3.1
.

∎

,

Next we prove
Fact

4.5
.

Proof.

For each
\ell\in[t]\,,
define

\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{G}_{\ell})_{ij}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}=\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\right]=:C^{*}_{\ell}

and let

C^{*}=\sum_{\ell\in[t]}C_{\ell}\,.

By independence of the observations, the inequality of
Fact

4.5
for the case case
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{j}
follows with an application of Hoeffding’s inequality.

The case
\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}
needs a bit more work. By
Theorem

3.1
, for each
\ell\in[t]
, we have

\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{G}_{\ell})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}=\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\right]=C^{*}_{\ell}\,,

and

\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{G}_{\ell})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}\neq\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\right]\leqslant C^{*}_{\ell}-C_{\ell}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{G}_{\ell})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}\right]=

\displaystyle\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{G}_{\ell})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}=\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}=\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle+\E\left[\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{G}_{\ell})_{ij}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}\neq\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{i}\neq\mathbf{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})_{j}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C^{*}_{\ell}-C_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\,,

and so

\E\left[\sum_{\ell\in[t]}\hat{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{G}_{\ell})_{ij}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}\right]\leqslant\sum_{\ell\in[t]}\left(C^{*}_{\ell}-C_{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\right)=C^{*}-\frac{\overline{C}\cdot t}{2}\,.

The inequality of
Fact

4.5
for case
\mathbf{z}_{i}\neq\mathbf{z}_{j}
follows with another application of Hoeffding’s inequality.
∎

Now we prove
Lemma

4.8
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.8
.

Let
A_{i}
be a
(p,q)
-representative and
A_{j}
be a
(p^{\prime},q)
-representative, for
p\,,p^{\prime}\in[k]\,.

It suffices to show that if
p=p^{\prime}
then
\left\lVert A_{i}-A_{j}\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant n/k
and otherwise
\left\lVert A_{i}-A_{j}\right\rVert^{2}>n/k\,.
Then no
q
-representative index remains unassigned at the end of step
1
.
By the reverse triangle inequality

\displaystyle\left\lvert\left\lVert A_{i}-A_{j}\right\rVert-\left\lVert c_{p}(z)-c_{p^{\prime}}(z)\right\rVert\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert A_{i}-A_{j}-c_{p}(z)+c_{p^{\prime}}(z)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert A_{i}-c_{p}(z)\right\rVert+\left\lVert A_{j}-c_{p^{\prime}}(z)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant 2n\cdot e^{-q\cdot\overline{C}^{2}\cdot t}\,.

For
p=p^{\prime}
we have
\left\lVert c_{p}(z)-c_{p^{\prime}}(z)\right\rVert=0
and by choice of
t\,,
the first inequality follows.
For
p\neq p^{\prime}
we have
\left\lVert c_{p}(z)-c_{p^{\prime}}(z)\right\rVert\geqslant\frac{n}{k}(2-o(1))
since
z
is balanced and the second inequality follows again by choice of
t
.
∎
