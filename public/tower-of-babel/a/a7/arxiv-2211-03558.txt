Disk potential functions for polygon spaces
Yoosik Kim, Siu-Cheong Lau, Xiao Zheng

Abstract
. We derive a Floer theoretical SYZ mirror for an equilateral and generic polygon space. The disk potential function of the monotone torus fiber of the caterpillar bending system is calculated by computing non-trivial open Gromov–Witten invariants from the structural result of the monotone Fukaya category, the topology of fibers of completely integrable systems, and toric degenerations. Then, combining the result with the work of Nohara–Ueda [ NU20 ] and Marsh–Rietsch [ MR20 ] , we obtain the disk potential functions of bending systems and produce a mirror cluster variety of type A A without frozen variables via Lagrangian Floer theory.



1.
Introduction

For a tuple
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{n+3})
of positive numbers, a polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
is defined to be the moduli space of vectors
(v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n+3})
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with side length
\mathbf{r}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n+3}v_{j}=0
modulo orientation preserving isometries, that is,

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}=\left\{\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n+3})\in\prod_{j=1}^{n+3}\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{j})\colon\sum_{j=1}^{n+3}v_{j}=0\right\}/\penalty\mathrm{SO}(3).

The polygon space can be viewed and constructed in many different ways. It has been broadly investigated from various aspects of geometry and topology. Also, there have been its applications to physics and chemistry as the polygon space provides a model for the moduli spaces of constraint brownian motions and polymers.

The main purpose of this paper is to explore polygon spaces from the aspect of Lagrangian Floer theory and SYZ mirror symmetry. Each polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
carries a completely integrable system, called a
bending system
, constructed by Kapovich–Millson
[
KM96
]
. Each component of the system generates a Hamiltonian bending
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-action along the diagonal corresponding to the component. A triangulation of a polygon, a representative of an element of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
, obtained by adding non-crossing diagonals gives rise to a bending system, a completely integrable system consisting of moment maps of the bending actions. This paper concerns Lagrangian Floer theory of a Lagrangian torus orbit of this system.

If the polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
is a toric manifold and a bending system is a toric integrable system, the study of the bending system boils down to the toric case, which has been extensively studied. Lagrangian Floer theory for toric fibers of toric manifolds was developed in Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono
[
FOOO10
,
FOOO11
]
, see also Woodward
[
Woo11
]
. In particular, when the toric manifold is Fano, Cho–Oh in
[
CO06
]
computed the disk potential function, which agrees with Givental–Hori–Vafa potential. The holomorphic disks of Maslov index two bounded by a toric fiber are in one-to-one correspondence with the facets of its moment polytope. Such a disk emanating from the inverse image of a facet is called
basic
. The second named author with Chan, Leung, and Tseng in
[
CLLT17
]
computed the disk potential function of semi-Fano toric manifold.

In this paper, we shall study
non-toric
bending systems. Especially, we are concerned with bending systems on the space of equilateral polygons with a fixed length. The given tuple
\mathbf{r}
is called
equilateral
if the lengths of all edges are equal, that is,
r_{1}=\cdots=r_{n+3}
. In the case where
\dim\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}\geq 4
, every bending system on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
is
not
toric because it always admits a fiber having an
\mathbb{S}^{2}
-factor. The main part of this paper focuses on the disk potential function of the system, which can be employed to compute deformed Floer cohomology and to provide (a cluster chart of) Landau–Ginzburg mirror of the polygon space.

A Floer theoretical SYZ mirror for the space of equilateral polygons is a Landau–Ginzburg (LG) model, which is obtained by gluing the charts arising from the moduli space of deformations of a bending torus fiber. To describe the mirror LG model systematically, consider the complex Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+3})

of two planes in the complex vector space
\mathbb{C}^{n+3}
, regarding it as an embedded subvariety of
\mathbb{C}P(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n+3})
via the Plücker embedding. We denote the Plücker coordinates for
\mathbb{C}P(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n+3})
by
\{p_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n+3\}
. Regard the variable
p_{i,j}
in the affine chart
p_{1,2}\neq 0
as affine coordinate
p_{i,j}/p_{1,2}
by setting
p_{i,j}\coloneqq p_{i,j}/p_{1,2}
.

The given tuple
\mathbf{r}
is called
generic
if
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
is a smooth algebraic variety. The main theorem of this paper is then stated below.

Theorem A
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{n+3})
is equilateral and generic (and hence
n
is even). Let

\check{X}\coloneqq(\mathrm{Gr}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+3})\backslash\{p_{1,2}p_{2,3}\cdots p_{n+2,n+3}p_{1,n+3}=0\})\cap\{p_{1,2}=\cdots=p_{n+2,n+3}=p_{1,n+3}\}

in
\mathbb{C}P(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n+3})
. Passing
\check{X}
to a subvariety of the affine chart given by
p_{1,2}\neq 0
, regard the variable
p_{i,j}
as affine coordinate
p_{i,j}/p_{1,2}
by setting
p_{i,j}\coloneqq p_{i,j}/p_{1,2}
. Then a Floer theoretical SYZ mirror of the polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
arising from the bending systems is a Landau–Ginzburg model consisting of the mirror space
\check{X}
together with the superpotential

(1.1)

W_{\mathrm{Bend}}(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{n+3}p_{i,i+2}\colon\check{X}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{C}.

Moreover, this mirror has a cluster structure of
A
-type without frozen variables. The seeds of the cluster variety are parametrized by the set of triangulations of the regular
(n+3)
-gon by adding non-crossing diagonals.

The LG model for the polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
in (
1.1
) with an equilateral and generic tuple
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{n+3})
can be combinatorially written from the regular
(n+3)
-gon as follows. Label the vertices of the
(n+3)
-gon by
\{1,2,\cdots,n+3\}
with counter-clockwise orientation. Note that each line segment joining two vertices
i
and
j
corresponds to the variables
p_{i,j}
of the affine chart of
\mathbb{C}P(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n+3})
. Then the superpotential (
1.1
) is equal to the summation of all variables corresponding to a red line in the first figure of Figure
1
.

The fiber at the center of each bending system is a monotone Lagrangian torus, see Section
3.1
for the precise definition of monotone Lagrangian submanifolds. The disk potential function of the monotone bending torus fiber of each bending system can be written from (
1.1
) as follows. Take a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of the regular
(n+3)
-gon. Then the disk potential function

W_{\mathrm{Bend}}(\mathbf{p})|_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{T}}}\colon\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{T}}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{C}

is derived by restricting
W_{\mathrm{Bend}}
to the cluster chart

\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{T}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Spec}\left(\mathbb{C}[\{p^{\pm}_{i,j}\colon(i,j)\in\mathcal{T}\}]\right)\subset\check{X}.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{0}
be the triangulation obtained by adding all diagonals containing the vertex labeled by
1
as in the second figure of Figure
1
. The
caterpillar bending system
is a bending system associated to
\mathcal{T}_{0}
and its disk potential function can be written as follows.

Theorem B
.

The disk potential function of the caterpillar bending system of an equilateral and generic polygon space is

(1.2)

\displaystyle W_{\mathcal{T}_{0}}=p_{1,3}+\frac{2}{p_{1,3}}+p_{1,n+2}+\frac{2}{p_{1,n+2}}+\sum_{j=3}^{n+1}\left(\frac{p_{1,j+1}}{p_{1,j}}+\frac{p_{1,j}}{p_{1,j+1}}+\frac{1}{p_{1,j}p_{1,j+1}}\right),

which is defined on
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
with the coordinates
p_{13},p_{14},\cdots,p_{1,n+2}
associated to
\mathcal{T}_{0}
.

Moreover, the monotone caterpillar bending torus fiber cannot be displaced by any Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms.

The space of equilateral pentagons is isomorphic to a del Pezzo surface
dP_{5}
of degree five,
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
blown up at generic four points as an algebraic variety. Pascaleff–Tonkonog
[
PT20
]
computed the disk potential function of a Lagrangian torus constructed via Lagrangian surgery for
dP_{5}
. Their superpotential is equal to the disk potential function of the caterpillar bending system in (
1.2
). Recently, the scattering diagram and the disk potential of a special Lagrangian fibration in a non-monotone del Pezzo surface were computed by Bardwell-Evans–Cheung–Hong–Lin in
[
BECHL21
]
, which agrees with the scattering diagram of Gross–Pandharipande–Siebert and Gross–Hacking–Keel
[
GPS10
,
GHK15
]
. Their chamber structure agrees with the chambers structure given by various bending systems. Gräfnitz–Ruddat–Zaslow and You showed that the Carl–Pumperla–Siebert’s LG superpotential in
[
CPS22
]
counting tropical disks agrees with the open mirror map in
[
GRZ22
,
You22
]
in the toric case. The disk potential function should be equal to the LG superpotential obtained via tropical coutings.

At the level of completely integrable systems, Nohara–Ueda constructed a toric degeneration of bending systems in
[
NU14
]
. By running their minimal model program, Charest and Woodward provide a method, which can detect torus orbits having non-trivial Floer cohomology in
[
CW15
]
. Also, Woodward and Xu computed Floer cohomology of some non-torus fiber of polygon spaces in
[
WX18
]
.

Yet, the full disk potential function of non-toric bending systems of higher dimensional polygon spaces remains unknown due to the following difficulties. The major difficulty is to analyze contributions from
non-basic
disks to the disk potential function. Although the bending system has a toric degeneration, we would like to emphasize that it does
not
fit into the situation of Nishinou–Nohara–Ueda
[
NNU10
]
. Indeed, there exist holomorphic disks emanating from the inverse image of dimensional stratum of codimension
\geq 4
. Heuristically, as the bending system admits vanishing cycles, the inverse image can have an extra dimension so that there is a chance for the disk class to have Maslov index two even if it intersects lower dimensional stratum.

Along the line of the classification of disks, another difficulty lies on a combinatorial description for the image of a bending system. The image is a polytope given by the intersection of half spaces from the triangle inequalities of a chosen triangulation. Indeed, some of half spaces are
not
essential. It means that the superpotential merely written from the triangle inequalities may contain a Laurent monomial which does appear in the disk potential. In the equilateral case, we manage to describe the image via the relation of Grassmannians and polygon spaces obtained by the Gelfand–MacPherson correspondence in
[
GM82
,
HK97
]
.

The strategy to compute the disk potential function is in order. Exploiting the non-redundant combinatorial description for the image and the corresponding toric degeneration, we classify the possible homotopy classes which can be realized as a holomorphic disk contributing the disk potential and compute the counting invariants for basic disks. In other words, we classify lattice points of the Newton polytope of the disk potential which can be a non-zero term of the disk potential. The plan is to compute unknown counting invariants via relation between closed and open mirror symmetry.

As the dimension of a polygon space increases, the number of undetermined counting invariants that we should compute increases. It makes the computing process hard and sometimes impossible. To get around this difficulty, we implement the following strategy. According to Bouloc
[
Bou18
]
, a fiber of the bending systems can be expressed as a product of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
,
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Using them non-toric Lagrangian submanifold as an anchor, we obtain a local model for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
. The above classification result ensures that each disk belongs to one of the local models. We then compute the counting invariant by embedding each local model into a simpler global model.

The method we present in the main body for computing the disk potential for equilateral case should not only restricted to the equilateral and generic case. It should work as long as
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
is smooth and monotone and the combinatorial structure is analyzed. To demonstrate it, we work out non-equilateral examples when
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}=2
.

The authors hope that the result in this paper is paved way for testing many interesting theory by providing concrete examples. The first one is about the relation on the equivariant Floer theory and Floer theory. The polygon space is the symplectic reduction of a Grassmannian. The disk potential function on the quotient should be related to the equivariant disk potential function on the pre-quotient. This process is a subtlety on a choice of a stability condition or a level set. We illustrate this point by examples. Also, we discuss a role of the polygon space for a construction problem for the mirror cluster variety. Namely, the polygon space with bending systems is a symplectic model for the cluster variety of type A without frozen variables.

2.
Polygon spaces and bending systems

In this section, we review construction of polygon spaces and bending systems. Polygon spaces and bending systems were formulated by Kapovich and Millson
[
KM96
]
, which serve as a class of very interesting integrable systems in symplectic geometry. The relation between polygon spaces and Grassmannians of two planes are also reviewed.

2.1.
Polygon spaces and Grassmannians

Let
n
be a positive integer.
For an
(n+3)
-tuple
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{n+3})
of positive real numbers, consider the set of all vectors
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n+3}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfying

(1)

\|v_{j}\|=r_{j}
for each
j=1,2,\cdots,n+3
and

(2)

\sum_{j=1}^{n+3}v_{j}=0
.

For each tuple
(v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{n+3})
of vectors in this set, we locate the initial point of the first vector
v_{1}
at the origin and the initial point of the vector
v_{j+1}
at the terminal point of the vector
v_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,n+2
. Arranging the vectors in this manner, the terminal point of
v_{n+3}
is the origin by the second condition
(2)
. Thus, each tuple of vectors can be regarded as an
(n+3)
-gon in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Let the real special orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}(3)
act on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
linearly. For each
j
, the linear
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-action induces an action on each sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{j})
with the radius
r_{j}
centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We then have the diagonal
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-action on the product
\prod_{j=1}^{n+3}\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{j})
of spheres. The
polygon space

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
is defined by the quotient of the space of
(n+3)
-gons in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by the diagonal
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-action, that is,

(2.1)

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}=\left\{\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n+3})\in\prod_{j=1}^{n+3}\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{j})\colon v_{1}+v_{2}+\cdots+v_{n+3}=0\right\}/\penalty\mathrm{SO}(3).

If
\mathbf{r}
, the tuple of lengths of vectors, does not meet all necessary
polygonal
inequalities, the polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
becomes empty. To avoid this triviality, we require that

(2.2)

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n+3}r_{j}\right)-2r_{i}>0\quad\mbox{for all $i=1,2,\cdots,n+3$.}

The polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
in (
2.1
) can be seen as the GIT quotient
(\mathbb{C}P^{1})^{n+3}\sslash\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{C})
and hence
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
is a projective algebraic variety of complex dimension
n
.

Definition 2.1
.

A tuple
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{n+3})
obeying (
2.2
) is called
generic
if there is no
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\cdots,\epsilon_{n+3})\in\{\pm 1\}^{n+3}
such that

(2.3)

\sum_{j=1}^{n+3}\epsilon_{j}r_{j}=0.

The polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
is a smooth variety if and only if
\mathbf{r}
is generic. Depending on our choice of
\mathbf{r}
, polygon spaces can produce different algebraic varieties. For instance, when
n=2
, the projective space
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
,
\mathbb{C}P^{1}\times\mathbb{C}P^{1}
, blow-ups of
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
at a point, two points, three points, and four points can be realized as polygon spaces of pentagons with different choices of stability condition
\mathbf{r}
, cf. Appendix
A
.

Fix a generic tuple
\mathbf{r}
. To adorn the polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
with a Kähler structure, we regard
\mathbb{C}P^{1}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{2}
as the coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}_{(r_{j},-r_{j})}
. It is identified with the conjugation
\mathrm{U}(2)
-orbit of the
(2\times 2)
-diagonal matrix with entries
(r_{j},-r_{j})
via the Killing form, that is,

\mathcal{O}_{(r_{j},-r_{j})}=\left\{Q\ \mathrm{diag}(r_{j},-r_{j})\,Q^{-1}\mid Q\in\mathrm{U}(2)\right\}.

Each coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}_{(r_{j},-r_{j})}
naturally comes with an invariant symplectic form determined by
r_{j}
, which is called a Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau (KKS) symplectic form. The linear
\mathrm{SO}(3)
-action on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(and
\mathbb{S}^{2}(r_{j})
) is induced from the adjoint action of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
on
\sqrt{-1}\mathfrak{su}(2)
(and
\mathcal{O}_{(r_{j},-r_{j})}
) via the double cover
\mathrm{SU}(2)\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{SO}(3)
and the identification between
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\sqrt{-1}\mathfrak{su}(2)\colon

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}x_{3}&x_{1}-\sqrt{-1}x_{2}\\
x_{1}+\sqrt{-1}x_{2}&-x_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

The polygon space is the symplectic reduction of the product of
\mathcal{O}_{(r_{j},-r_{j})}\simeq\mathbb{C}P^{1}
by the diagonal
\mathrm{SU}(2)
-action, that is,
\prod_{j=1}^{n+3}{\mathcal{O}}_{(r_{j},-r_{j})}\sslash\mathrm{SU}(2)
. It is equipped with the reduced symplectic form
\omega_{\mathbf{r}}
from the product KKS forms. Then the polygon space is Kähler with respect to the reduced symplectic form and the complex structure from the GIT quotient
(\mathbb{C}P^{1})^{n+3}\sslash\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.

The polygon spaces are related with the Grassmannians of two planes. First, let us recall the description of Grassmannians as a coadjoint orbit. For each
i=1,2,\cdots,n+3
, we set

(2.4)

|\mathbf{r}|_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{i}r_{j}\,\mbox{ and }\,|\mathbf{r}|\coloneqq|\mathbf{r}|_{n+3}

and take an
(n+3)
-tuple

(2.5)

\lambda=(|\mathbf{r}|,|\mathbf{r}|,0,\dots,0).

The choice yields the diagonal matrix
\mathrm{diag}(\lambda)=\mathrm{diag}(|\mathbf{r}|,|\mathbf{r}|,0,\dots,0)
. We denote by
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
the conjugation
\mathrm{U}(n+3)
-orbit of the diagonal matrix whose entries are
\lambda
, that is,

(2.6)

\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\coloneqq\left\{Q\,\mathrm{diag}(|\mathbf{r}|,|\mathbf{r}|,0,\dots,0)\,Q^{-1}\mid Q\in\mathrm{U}(n+3)\right\}.

The adjoint action
\mathrm{U}(n+3)
on
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
is transitive, and
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}=\mathrm{U}(n+3)/(\mathrm{U}(2)\times\mathrm{U}(n+1))
, the complex Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+3})
of two-planes in the complex vector space
\mathbb{C}^{n+3}
.
When
|\mathbf{r}|
is integral in particular, one can associate
\mathrm{Gr}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+3})
to the pull-backed Kähler form
|\mathbf{r}|\cdot\omega_{\mathbb{C}P^{N}}
on
\mathbb{C}P^{N}
where
N={n+3\choose 2}-1
via the Plücker embedding. This pull-backed form agrees with the KKS form
\omega_{\lambda}
.

Grassmannians can also be constructed via a symplectic reduction. Let us consider the space
\hom_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{n+3})\simeq\mathbb{C}^{(n+3)\times 2}
of complex linear transformations from
\mathbb{C}^{2}
to
\mathbb{C}^{n+3}
. The group
\mathrm{U}(n+3)
(resp.
\mathrm{U}(2)
) acts on
\hom_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{n+3})
on the left (resp. right) matrix multiplication in a Hamiltonian fashion. A moment map of the
\mathrm{U}(2)
-action can be taken as

(2.7)

\Phi_{\mathrm{U}(2)}\colon\mathbb{C}^{(n+3)\times 2}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathfrak{u}(2)^{*},\quad\Phi_{\mathrm{U}(2)}(A)\coloneqq\frac{A^{H}A}{2}

where
A^{H}
is the conjugate transpose (or Hermitian transpose) of
A
. Then the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+3})\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
is isomorphic to the reduction of
\mathbb{C}^{(n+3)\times 2}
by
\mathrm{U}(2)\colon

(2.8)

\mathrm{Gr}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+3})\simeq\mathbb{C}^{(n+3)\times 2}\sslash_{|\mathbf{r}|\cdot I_{2}}\mathrm{U}(2).

We discuss a relation between
\mathrm{Gr}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+3})
and the polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
. Let
T_{\mathrm{U}(n+3)}
be the maximal torus consisting of the diagonal matrices of
\mathrm{U}(n+3)
. The torus also acts on
\mathbb{C}^{(n+3)\times 2}
linearly with a moment map

(2.9)

\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}&z_{2}&\dots&z_{n+3}\\
w_{1}&w_{2}&\dots&w_{n+3}\\
\end{pmatrix}^{T}\mapsto\left(\frac{|z_{1}|^{2}+|w_{1}|^{2}}{2},\frac{|z_{2}|^{2}+|w_{2}|^{2}}{2},\dots,\frac{|z_{n+3}|^{2}+|w_{n+3}|^{2}}{2}\right).

The Hamiltonian torus action descends to the quotient (
2.8
). Then the polygon space is isomorphic to the symplectic reduction of
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
by the residual
T
-action, where
T
is the quotient of
T_{\mathrm{U}(n+3)}
by the diagonal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-action.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
HK97
]
)
.

For an
(n+3)
-tuple
\mathbf{r}
of positive real numbers, suppose that
\lambda
is given by (
2.5
). Then the polygon space
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}},\omega_{\mathbf{r}})
is symplectomorphic to the symplectic reduction
{T}\bbslash_{2\mathbf{r}}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
of the coadjoint orbit
(\mathcal{O}_{\lambda},\omega_{\lambda})
.

This paper mainly deals with the case where

(2.10)

r_{1}=r_{2}=\dots=r_{n+3}.

In this case, the tuple
\mathbf{r}
is called
equilateral
. The corresponding polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
is monotone, namely, the first Chern class
c_{1}(T\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}})=\frac{1}{2\pi r_{1}}[\omega_{\mathbf{r}}]\in H^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}})
. By normalizing the Kähler form, we may assume that

c_{1}(T\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}})=\frac{1}{r_{1}}[\omega_{\mathbf{r}}].

The coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
with the KKS form
\omega_{\lambda}
is denoted by
(\mathcal{O}_{\lambda},\omega_{\lambda})
.

Remark 2.3
.

The polygon space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}
can be a monotone symplectic manifold even though
\mathbf{r}
is not equilateral. Such spaces would be also of interest, see some examples in Appendix
A
.

2.2.
Bending systems and Gelfand–Zeitlin systems

The coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\simeq\mathrm{Gr}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+3})
in (
2.6
) carries a peculiar completely integrable system, which is called a Gelfand–Zeitlin system
[
GS83
]
. We shall briefly recall this system below.

Let
A^{(i)}
be the leading principal
(i\times i)
submatrix of
A\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
. As every element in
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
is a Hermitian matrix, every eigenvalue of each submatrix
A^{(i)}
is real. Our specific choice of the tuple
\lambda
of the form (
2.5
) implies that each submatrix
A^{(i)}
has at most two non-zero eigenvalues. We denote by
{\Phi}_{i,1}(A)
(resp.
{\Phi}_{i-1,2}(A)
) the first (resp. the second) largest eigenvalue of
A^{(i)}
. They define real-valued functions,
{\Phi}_{i,1}\colon\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}
and
{\Phi}_{i-1,2}\colon\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.4
.

The
Gelfand–Zeitlin system
(
GZ system

3.
Potential functions of polygon spaces

The aim of this section is to state the main results and discuss the results from the perspectives of equivariant Floer theory and the theory of cluster algebras and varieties. After reviewing the disk potential functions, we derive the disk potential function of a monotone Lagrangian torus fiber of the caterpillar bending system from equivariant Floer theory. By using the relation of generalized Gelfand–Zeitlin fibers, we construct a glued SYZ mirror of the equilateral and generic polygon space, which will be a cluster variety of type
A
.

3.1.
Disk potential functions

We begin by recalling the definition of a monotone Lagrangian submanifold. A Lagrangian submanifold
L
of a symplectic manifold
(X,\omega)
is called
monotone
if the Maslov index and the symplectic area are positively proportional, that is, there exists a positive number
c>0
such that
\omega(\beta)=c\cdot\mu(\beta)
for all
\beta\in\pi_{2}(X,L)
.

In order to prove that the Lagrangian torus fiber at the center of a caterpillar bending polytope is monotone, we recall the Maslov index formula for a gradient holomorphic disk in
[
CK21
]
, which is useful to verify the monotonicity of a Lagrangian submanifold when an ambient symplectic manifold admits a Hamiltonian (local) torus action. Using this formula, we shall prove the Lagrangian torus located at the center of the caterpillar bending polytope is monotone.

Consider a symplectic manifold
X
equipped with a Hamiltonian
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-action with a moment map
H
. Suppose that we have an
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-invariant Lagrangian submanifold
L
contained in a level set of
H

\colon

(1)

an
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-invariant compatible almost complex structure
J
,

(2)

a free
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-orbit
\vartheta
in
L
.

Let
X_{H}
be the Hamiltonian vector field generated by
H
. Then the moment map
H
generates the gradient vector field
\nabla H=JX_{H}
using the Riemannian metric
g_{J}
from
\omega
and
J
, which is defined by
g_{J}(\nabla H,\cdot)=dH(\cdot)
. Assume that the integral curve
\gamma
of the vector field
\nabla H
with an initial condition

converges to a fixed point. The collection of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-orbits of
\gamma
forms a
J
-holomorphic disk whose boundary is
\vartheta
. We call such a holomorphic disk a

of
\vartheta

Suppose that a connected Lie group
G
acts on the manifold

if the action is free on the complement of the set of fixed points. In the following circumstance, the Maslov index of a gradient holomorphic disk of

Lemma 5.9
.

Suppose that a homology class
\alpha\in H_{2}({X}^{1},L^{1};\mathbb{Q})

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a holomorphic disk of Maslov index two in

Suppose that

Lemma 5.10
.

For each

, the output of
\partial\beta

is one of

Proof.

For every double index

The condition

Lemma 5.11
.

For two indices

To derive a contradiction, suppose that both

\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}a_{i,0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i,1}+\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i,2}\right)-a_{r,s}<1

Since each

can contribute only

\delta_{\ell}(\partial\beta_{r,s})=0
. Because of (

, which leads to a contradiction.

Either one of the following holds.

(1)

(2)

,
\delta_{\ell+1}(\partial\beta)=\pm 1
, and
\delta_{k}(\partial\beta)=0

.

Proposition 5.13
.

The set of homotopy classes which can be represented by a holomorphic disk bounded by
L^{1}
of Maslov index two in
{X}^{1}
are contained in the set consisting of the following classes
\colon

•

\alpha_{i,0}
for
i=2,3,\cdots,n
,

•

\alpha_{i,1}
for
i=2,3,\cdots,n+1
,

•

\alpha_{i,2}
for
i=0,1,\cdots,n-1
,

•

(\alpha_{i,0}+\alpha_{i,1})/2
for
i=2,\cdots,n-1
,

•

(\alpha_{2,0}+\alpha_{1,2})/2+c_{1}\alpha_{1}
,
(\alpha_{n,0}+\alpha_{n,1})/2+c_{n}\alpha_{n}
for
c_{1}
and
c_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

The proof is simply a case-by-case analysis. Suppose that
\alpha
is represented by a holomorphic disk bounded by
L^{1}
of Maslov index two. Set
\beta=\psi_{1,0,*}(\alpha)
. By Corollary
5.12
, it suffices to deal with the the following two cases.

Case 1
. Assume that (1) in Corollary
5.12
occurs. Then
\delta_{\ell}(\partial\beta)
is either
1
or
-1
.

•

Case 1-1
. Suppose that
\delta_{\ell}(\partial\beta)=1
.

–

Case 1-1-1
. Assume that
\ell=1
. The only four classes
\alpha_{0,2},\alpha_{1,2},\alpha_{2,0}
, and
\alpha_{2,1}
can contribute to
\delta_{1}
. Thus,

(5.20)

\delta_{1}(\partial\beta)=-a_{2,0}+a_{2,1}+a_{0,2}-a_{1,2}.

It implies that

\begin{cases}a_{2,1}+a_{0,2}=1\mbox{ and }\\
a_{i,j}=0\mbox{ for all $(i,j)\neq(2,1),(0,2)$}.\end{cases}

Furthermore,
a_{0,2}=1
and
a_{2,1}=0
since
\delta_{2}(\partial\beta)=-a_{2,1}
. In this case, we have
\beta_{0,2}
.

–

Case 1-1-2
. Assume that
\ell=n
. In this case, by applying a similar argument as in Case 1-1-1, we have
\beta_{n+1,1}
.

–

Case 1-1-3
. Assume that
1<\ell<n
. The only six classes
\alpha_{\ell,0},\alpha_{\ell+1,0},\alpha_{\ell,1}
,
\alpha_{\ell+1,1}
,
\alpha_{\ell-1,2}
, and
\alpha_{\ell,2}
can contribute to
\delta_{\ell}
. Thus,

(5.21)

\delta_{\ell}(\partial\beta)=-a_{\ell,0}-a_{\ell+1,0}-a_{\ell,1}+a_{\ell+1,1}+a_{\ell-1,2}-a_{\ell,2}.

It implies that

\begin{cases}a_{\ell+1,1}+a_{\ell-1,2}=1\mbox{ and }\\
a_{i,j}=0\mbox{ for all $(i,j)\neq(\ell+1,1),(\ell-1,2)$}.\end{cases}

Since
\delta_{\ell+1}(\partial\beta)=-a_{\ell+1,1}
and
\delta_{\ell-1}(\partial\beta)=-a_{\ell-1,2}
, either
\delta_{\ell+1}(\partial\beta)\neq 0
or
\delta_{\ell-1}(\partial\beta)\neq 0
holds. There are no corresponding classes in this case.

•

Case 1-2
. Suppose that
\delta_{\ell}(\partial\beta)=-1
.

–

Case 1-2-1
. Assume that
\ell=1
. By (
5.20
), we have

\begin{cases}a_{2,0}+a_{1,2}=1\mbox{ and }\\
a_{i,j}=0\mbox{ for all $(i,j)\neq(2,0),(1,2)$.}\end{cases}

Since
\delta_{2}(\partial\beta)=-a_{2,0}+a_{1,2}
, we have
a_{2,0}=a_{1,2}=1/2
. Thus, we have
(\beta_{2,0}+\beta_{1,2})/2
.

–

Case 1-2-2
. Assume that
\ell=n
. In this case, by applying a similar argument as in Case 1-2-1, we have
(\beta_{n,0}+\beta_{n,1})/2
.

–

Case 1-2-3
. Assume that
1<\ell<n
. By (
5.21
),

\begin{cases}a_{\ell,0}+a_{\ell+1,0}+a_{\ell,1}+a_{\ell,2}=1\mbox{ and }\\
a_{i,j}=0\mbox{ for all $(i,j)\neq(\ell,0),(\ell+1,0),(\ell,1),(\ell,2)$}.\end{cases}

Since
\delta_{\ell-1}(\partial\beta)=0
and
\delta_{\ell+1}(\partial\beta)=0
, we have
a_{\ell,0}=a_{\ell,1}
and
a_{\ell+1,0}=a_{\ell,2}
. As
\beta_{\ell,0}+\beta_{\ell,1}-\beta_{\ell+1,0}-\beta_{\ell,2}=0
, we may assume that
2(a_{\ell+1,0}+a_{\ell,2})=1
. Therefore, in this case, the corresponding class is

(\beta_{\ell+1,0}+\beta_{\ell,2})/2=(\beta_{\ell,0}+\beta_{\ell,1})/2
.

Case 2.
Assume that (2) in Corollary
5.12
occurs.

•

Case 2-1
. Suppose that
\delta_{\ell}(\partial\beta)=1
and
\delta_{\ell+1}(\partial\beta)=1

5.21

\begin{cases}a_{\ell+1,1}+a_{\ell-1,2}=1\mbox{ and }\\
a_{\ell+2,1}+a_{\ell,2}=1.\end{cases}

We cannot make both
a_{\ell+1,1}+a_{\ell-1,2}
and
a_{\ell+2,1}+a_{\ell,2}
one.

•

Case 2-2
. Suppose that
\delta_{\ell}(\partial\beta)=-1
and
\delta_{\ell+1}(\partial\beta)=-1
. By (
5.21
),

\begin{cases}a_{\ell,0}+a_{\ell+1,0}+a_{\ell,1}+a_{\ell,2}=1\mbox{ and }\\
a_{\ell+1,0}+a_{\ell+2,0}+a_{\ell+1,1}+a_{\ell+1,2}=1.\end{cases}

Thus,
a_{\ell+1,0}=1
and
a_{i,j}=0
for all
(i,j)\neq(\ell+1,0)
. The corresponding class is
\beta_{\ell+1,0}
.

•

Case 2-3
. Suppose that
\delta_{\ell}(\partial\beta)=1
and
\delta_{\ell+1}(\partial\beta)=-1

5.21
),

\begin{cases}a_{\ell+1,1}+a_{\ell-1,2}=1\mbox{ and }\\
a_{\ell+1,0}+a_{\ell+2,0}+a_{\ell+1,1}+a_{\ell+1,2}=1.\end{cases}

Thus,
a_{\ell+1,1}=1
and
a_{i,j}=0
for all
(i,j)\neq(\ell+1,1)
. The corresponding class is
\beta_{\ell+1,1}
.

•

Case 2-4
. Suppose that
\delta_{\ell}(\partial\beta)=-1
and
\delta_{\ell+1}(\partial\beta)=1

),

\begin{cases}a_{\ell,0}+a_{\ell+1,0}+a_{\ell,1}+a_{\ell,2}=1\mbox{ and }\\

Thus,
a_{\ell,2}=1

a_{i,j}=0
for all

such that
\iota^{t}(0,T^{n})=L^{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0})
and
\iota^{t}(1,T^{n})=L^{t}(\mathbf{w}_{0})
. To show that the counting invariants are preserved through the isotopy, we construct a cobordism between the moduli space of pseudo-holomorphic disks bounded by
L(\textbf{u}_{0})
and that by
L(\textbf{w}_{0})
. As most of them are non-monotone in
{X}^{t}
, a Lagrangian torus over a certain point in the segment may bound a pseudo-holomorphic disks of Maslov index less than or equal to zero. Indeed, the existence of such a disk is sensitive to a choice of compatible almost complex structure. Nevertheless, we may choose a position
\textbf{w}_{0}
“close” to the center
\mathbf{u}_{0}
so that the holomorphic disk of Maslov index
\leq 0
does not occur along the isotopy.

Lemma 6.11
.

For a generic and compatible almost complex structure
J
, there exists a position
\mathbf{w}_{0}
in
[\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{k})
such that the fiber of each point
\mathbf{u}\in[\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{0}]
does not bound any
J
-holomorphic disk of Maslov index less than or equal to zero.

Proof.

First, the generic choice of compatible almost complex structure
J
ensures that any simple
J
-holomorphic disks of Maslov index
-\ell
for sufficiently large
\ell
do not occur. Second, appearance of simple
J
-holomorphic disks of Maslov index
k
for any given fixed integer
k\leq 0
is a closed condition. Lastly,
L(\mathbf{u}_{0})
does not bound any non-constant
J
-holomorphic disks of Maslov index
\leq 0
since
L(\mathbf{u}_{0})
is monotone. Therefore, combining the above three facts, we see that there exists a position
\mathbf{w}_{0}
(depending on
J
) sufficiently close to
\mathbf{u}_{0}
such that the torus located at each point
\mathbf{u}\in[\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{0}]
does not bound any
J
-holomorphic disk of Maslov index
\leq 0
.
∎

When
k=1
, we are now aiming at computing the (sum of) counting invariants for the classes

(6.12)

\alpha{(c)}\coloneqq c\cdot\alpha_{1}+(\alpha_{1,0}+\alpha_{1,1})/2=c\cdot\alpha_{1}+(\alpha_{2,0}+\alpha_{1,2})/2\quad(c\in\mathbb{Z})

in Proposition

The sum of counting invariants of those classes is equal to

Proposition 6.12
.

The counting invariant for
\alpha{(c)}
in
\pi_{2}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}},L(\mathbf{u}_{0}))
is equal to that for
\alpha{(c)}
in
\pi_{2}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}},L(\mathbf{u}))
for
\mathbf{u}\in[\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{k})
such that
\mathbf{u}
is sufficiently close to
\mathbf{u}_{k}
.

Proof.

We proceed the proof of Proposition
6.12
in five steps.

(1)

Proposition
3.3
asserts that the Lagrangian torus
L^{1}(\mathbf{u}_{0})
at the center is monotone. For each nonzero

, the symplectomorphism

gives rise to an oriented cobordism so that

n(\alpha{(c)};\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{r}}\simeq{X}^{1},L^{1}(\mathbf{u}_{0}))=n(\psi_{1,t,*}(\alpha{(c)});{X}^{t},L^{t}(\mathbf{u}_{0}))

(2)

Note that each

sufficiently close to

, there exists a position
\mathbf{w}_{0}

\mathbf{u}_{0}

(3)

We claim that each holomorphic disk
\varphi
