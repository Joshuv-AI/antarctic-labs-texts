Abstract
The full quasistatic thermomechanical system of PDEs, describing water diffusion with the possibility of freezing and melting in a visco-elasto-plastic porous solid, is studied in detail under the hypothesis that the pressure-saturation hysteresis relation is given in terms of the Preisach hysteresis operator. The resulting system of balance equations for mass, momentum, and energy coupled with the phase dynamics equation is shown to admit a global solution under general assumptions on the data. Keywords: porous media, phase transitions, hysteresis 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 74F10, 76S05, 47J40, 35A01



Introduction

A model for fluid flow in partially saturated porous media with thermomechanical interaction
was proposed and analyzed in
[
2
,
4
]
.
The model was subsequently extended in
[
15
]
by including the effects of freezing and melting of the water in the pores. Typical examples in which such situations arise are related to groundwater flows and to the freezing-melting cycles of water sucked into the pores of concrete. Due to the specific volume difference between water and ice, this process produces important pressure changes and represents one of the main reasons for the degradation of construction materials in buildings, bridges, and roads. The model of
[
15
]
still neglects the influence of changes of microstructure, as for example the breaking of pores, but the main thermomechanical interactions between the state variables are taken into account.

The model is based on the assumption that slow diffusion of the fluid through the porous solid is a dominant effect, so that the Lagrangian description is considered to be appropriate. It is assumed that volume changes of the solid matrix material are negligible with respect to the pore volume evolution during the process. The pores are filled with a mixture of
\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
and gas, and
\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
itself is a mixture of the liquid (water) and the solid phase (ice). That is, in addition to the standard state variables like capillary pressure, displacement, and absolute temperature, we need to consider the evolution of a phase parameter
\chi
representing the relative proportion of water in the
\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
part and its influence on pressure changes due to the different mass densities of water and ice.

The resulting system consists of mechanical balance equation for the deformations of the solid body, mass balance equation based on the Darcy law for the fluid diffusion with interaction terms similar to the Biot system studied, e. g., in
[
20
]
, a differential inclusion for the phase fraction
\chi
of relaxed Stefan type as in
[
22
]
and governing the water-ice phase transition, and the energy balance equation derived from the first and the second principles of thermodynamics with heat sources due to viscosity, plasticity, diffusion, and phase transition.

The present paper develops the ideas of
[
15
]
in the sense that the effects of capillary hysteresis, which is assumed to be of Preisach type in agreement with the results of
[
6
]
, shear stresses, and elastoplasticity are considered in full generality. This represents an enormous increase of mathematical complexity. While the momentum balance in the shear-stress-free case in
[
15
]
can be reduced to an ODE, here, we need to exploit deeper results from the theory of PDEs to control the interactions between individual components of the system, as well as a generalized Moser iteration scheme from
[
8
]
. Additional difficulties are due to the effects of the three heat sources produced by mechanical hysteresis dissipation (plasticity, capillarity, phase transitions).

The paper is divided into five sections. In the next Section
1
, we briefly recall the principles of the model introduced in
[
15
]
, taking into account capillary hysteresis and elastoplastic hysteresis effects as in
[
4
]
. Section
2
is devoted to a short survey of the Preisach hysteresis model. In Section
3
, we state the mathematical problem, the main assumptions on the data, and the main Theorem
3.3
, the proof of which is split into Sections
4
and
5
. The steps of the proof are as follows. In Section
4
, we first cut off some of the pressure and temperature dependent terms in the system by means of a cut-off parameter
R
, regularize the mass balance equation with a fourth order term depending on an additional small regularizing parameter
\eta
, solve the related problem employing a Galerkin approximation scheme, and pass to the singular limit
\eta\to 0
in the regularizing term. Then, in Section
5
, we derive a series of
R
-independent estimates like the energy estimate, the so-called Dafermos estimate (with negative small powers of the temperature), Moser-type and then higher-order estimates for the capillary pressure and for the temperature which allow us in Section
6
to pass to the limit in the cut-off system as
R\to\infty
, which will conclude the proof of the existence result.

1  The model

Consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
of class
C^{1,1}
filled with an elastoplastic solid matrix material with pores containing a mixture of
\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
and gas, where we assume that
\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
may appear in one of the two phases: water or ice. We state the balance laws in referential (Lagrangian) coordinates. We have in mind construction materials where large deformations are not expected to occur. This hypothesis enables us to reduce the complexity of the problem and assume that the deformations are small in order to avoid higher degree nonlinearities. We denote for
x\in\Omega
and time
t\in[0,T]

p(x,t)
… capillary pressure;

u(x,t)
… displacement vector in the solid;

\varepsilon(x,t)=\nabla_{s}u(x,t)
… linear strain tensor,
(\nabla_{s}u)_{ij}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)
;

\theta(x,t)
… absolute temperature;

\chi(x,t)\in[0,1]
… relative amount of water in the
\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
part.

The model derived in
[
15
]
aims at coupling the effects of capillarity, interaction between a deformable solid matrix material and
\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
in the pores which may undergo water-ice phase transitions, and energy exchange between the individual components of the system. Hysteresis is included following the modeling section of
[
4
]
. The full system consists of equations describing mass balance (
1.1
), mechanical equilibrium (
1.2
), energy balance (
1.3
) and phase evolution (
1.4
) in the form

\displaystyle\big((\chi{+}\rho^{*}(1{-}\chi))(G[p]+\mathrm{\,div\,}u)\big)_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\,div\,}(\mu(p)\nabla p),

(1.1)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{\,div\,}(\mathbf{B}\nabla_{s}u_{t}+P[\nabla_{s}u])+\nabla(p(\chi{+}\rho^{*}(1{-}\chi))+\beta(\theta-\theta_{c}))

\displaystyle=g,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta)_{t}-\mathrm{\,div\,}(\kappa(\theta)\nabla\theta)=\mathbf{B}\nabla_{s}u_{t}:\nabla_{s}u_{t}+\mu(p)|\nabla p|^{2}

\displaystyle+\|D_{P}[\nabla_{s}u]_{t}\|_{*}

\displaystyle+(\chi{+}\rho^{*}(1{-}\chi))|D_{G}[p]_{t}|+\gamma(\theta,\mathrm{\,div\,}u)\chi_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{L}{\theta_{c}}\theta\chi_{t}-\beta\theta\mathrm{\,div\,}u_{t},

(1.3)

\displaystyle-\gamma(\theta,\mathrm{\,div\,}u)\chi_{t}+(1-\rho^{*})\left(p\,G[p]-U_{G}[p]+p\mathrm{\,div\,}u\right)+L\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_{c}}-1\right)

\displaystyle\in\partial I_{[0,1]}(\chi).

(1.4)

We refer to
[
15
]
for the details of the physical arguments. Let us just mention that
the mass balance (
1.1
) is derived from Darcy’s law, and
\mu(p)\nabla p
is the specific liquid mass flux. The constant
\rho^{*}\in(0,1)
is the ratio between ice and water mass densities, whereas the symbol
G
describes the pressure-saturation curve and, following
[
2
,
4
]
, is of the form

G[p]=f(p)+G_{0}[p].

Here
f
is a bounded monotone function satisfying Hypothesis
3.1
(vi) below, whereas
G_{0}
is the Preisach hysteresis operator from Section
2
. The momentum balance (
1.2
) is derived by assuming that the process is quasi-static, so that the inertia term is negligible. Here
\mathbf{B}
is a positive definite viscosity matrix,
P
the constitutive operator of elastoplasticity defined below in (
3.6
),
\beta
is the thermal expansion coefficient,
\theta_{c}
is the melting temperature at standard pressure and
g
is a given volume force (gravity, e. g.). The term
p(\chi{+}\rho^{*}(1{-}\chi))
represents the pressure component due to the phase transition. Finally, the energy balance (
1.3
) and the inclusion (
1.4
) for the evolution of the phase parameter are derived from the principles of thermodynamics with the aid of the energy balance for both the plasticity and the pressure-saturation operator

P[\varepsilon]:\varepsilon_{t}=U_{P}[\varepsilon]_{t}+\|D_{P}[\varepsilon]_{t}\|_{*},\qquad G[p]_{t}p=U_{G}[p]_{t}+|D_{G}[p]_{t}|,

(1.5)

where
U_{P},U_{G}
and
D_{P},D_{G}
are the potential and dissipation operators, and
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is a seminorm in the space
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}
of symmetric
3\times 3
tensors. In (
1.3
),
\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta)
is the caloric component of the internal energy,
\kappa(\theta)
is the heat conductivity coefficient,
L
is the latent heat,
I_{[0,1]}
is the indicator function of the interval
[0,1]
and
\partial I_{[0,1]}
is its subdifferential,
\gamma(\theta,\mathrm{\,div\,}u)
is the phase relaxation time (which we assume to explicitly depend on both
\theta
and
\mathrm{\,div\,}u
for technical reasons).

Note that the values of
G
have to be naturally confined between
0
and
1
, so that the system is degenerate in the sense that we do not control a priori the time derivatives of
p
in (
1.1
). Another difficulty is related to the lack of spatial regularity of
\chi
. The temperature field is problematic as well: equation (
1.3
) contains high order heat source terms, which are difficult to handle and prevent the temperature from being regular.

We complement the system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) with initial conditions

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}p(x,0)&=&p^{0}(x)\\
u(x,0)&=&u^{0}(x)\\
\theta(x,0)&=&\theta^{0}(x)\\
\chi(x,0)&=&\chi^{0}(x)\end{array}\right\}\mbox{in}\ \Omega\,,

(1.6)

and boundary conditions

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}u&=&0\\
\mu(p)\nabla p\cdot n&=&\alpha(x)(p^{*}-p)\\
\kappa(\theta)\nabla\theta\cdot n&=&\omega(x)(\theta^{*}-\theta)\end{array}\right\}\mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega\,,

(1.7)

where
p^{*}
is a given outer pressure,
\theta^{*}
is a given outer temperature,
\alpha:\partial\Omega\to[0,\infty)
is the permeability of the boundary and
\omega:\partial\Omega\to[0,\infty)
is the heat conductivity of the boundary.

The solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) was only constructed in
[
15
]
under the assumption that shear stresses in the momentum balance equation (
1.2
) as well as all hysteresis effects are neglected. Then (
1.2
) turns into an ODE for the relative volume change
\mathrm{\,div\,}u
, which considerably simplifies the analysis. Here we prove existence of a global solution for the full problem under suitable hypotheses.

2  Hysteresis in capillarity phenomena

The operator
G
is considered as a sum

G[p]=f(p)+G_{0}[p],

(2.1)

where
f
is a monotone function satisfying Hypothesis
3.1
(vi) in Section
3
below, and
G_{0}
is a Preisach operator that we briefly describe here.

For a given input function
p\in W^{1,1}(0,T)
and a memory parameter
r>0
we define the scalar function
\xi_{r}(t)
as the solution of the variational inequality

\begin{cases}|p(t)-\xi_{r}(t)|\leq r&\forall t\in[0,T],\\
(\xi_{r})_{t}(p(t)-\xi_{r}(t)-z)\geq 0\ &\text{a.\,e. }\forall z\in[-r,r],\end{cases}

(2.2)

with prescribed initial condition

\xi_{r}(0)=\max\{p(0)-r,\min\{0,p(0)+r\}\}.

(2.3)

We have indeed for all
r>0
the initial bound

\xi_{r}(0)\leq|p(0)|.

(2.4)

The mapping
\mathfrak{f}_{r}:W^{1,1}(0,T)\rightarrow W^{1,1}(0,T)
which with each
p\in W^{1,1}(0,T)
associates the solution
\xi_{r}=\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p]\in W^{1,1}(0,T)
of (
2.2
)–(
2.3
) is called the
play
. This concept goes back to
[
10
]
, and the proof of the following statements can be found e.g. in
[
12
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For each
r>0
, the mapping
\mathfrak{f}_{r}:W^{1,1}(0,T)\rightarrow W^{1,1}(0,T)
is Lipschitz continuous and admits a Lipschitz continuous extension to
\mathfrak{f}_{r}:C[0,T]\rightarrow C[0,T]
in the sense that for every
p_{1},p_{2}\in C[0,T]
and every
t\in[0,T]
we have

|\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p_{1}](t)-\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p_{2}](t)|\leq\max_{\tau\in[0,t]}|p_{1}(\tau)-p_{2}(\tau)|.

(2.5)

Moreover, for each
p\in W^{1,1}(0,T)
, the energy balance equation

\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p]_{t}p-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathfrak{f}_{r}^{2}[p]\right)_{t}=|r\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p]_{t}|

(2.6)

and the identity

\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p]_{t}p_{t}=\left(\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p]_{t}\right)^{2}

(2.7)

hold a. e. in
(0,T)
.

Given a nonnegative function
\psi\in L^{1}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})
, called the
Preisach density
, we define the Preisach operator
G_{0}
as a mapping that with each
p\in C[0,T]
associates the integral

G_{0}[p](t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p](t)}\psi(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r.

(2.8)

Hence,

G_{0}[p](t)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r=:C_{\psi}^{+},\quad-G_{0}[p](t)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\psi(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r=:C_{\psi}^{-}

(2.9)

for all
p\in C[0,T]
and all
t\in[0,T]
, and we assume

0<C_{\psi}^{\pm}<\frac{1}{2}.

(2.10)

From (
2.6
)–(
2.8
) we immediately deduce the Preisach energy identity

G_{0}[p]_{t}p-U_{0}[p]_{t}=|D_{0}[p]_{t}|\text{ a.\,e.},

(2.11)

provided we define the Preisach potential
U_{0}
and the dissipation operator
D_{0}
by the integrals

U_{0}[p](t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p](t)}v\psi(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r,\qquad D_{0}[p](t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p](t)}r\psi(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r.

(2.12)

The energy identity in (
1.5
) then holds with the choice

U_{G}[p]=pf(p)-\int_{0}^{p}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z+U_{0}[p],\qquad D_{G}[p]=D_{0}[p].

(2.13)

With the notation

\Phi(p)=\int_{0}^{p}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,\quad V(p)=pf(p)-\Phi(p)=\int_{0}^{p}f^{\prime}(z)z\,\mathrm{d}z

(2.14)

we can also write

U_{G}[p]=V(p)+U_{0}[p],

(2.15)

thus separating the hysteretic from the non-hysteretic part.

For our purposes, we adopt the following hypothesis on the Preisach density.

Hypothesis 2.2
.

There exists a function
\psi^{*}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
such that for a. e.
(r,v)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
we have
0\leq\psi(r,v)\leq\psi^{*}(r)
and

C_{\psi}^{*}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+r^{2})\psi^{*}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r<\infty.

A straightforward computation shows that
G_{0}
(and, consequently,
G
) are Lipschitz continuous in
C[0,T]
. Indeed, by (
2.5
) and Hypothesis
2.2
we obtain for
p_{1},p_{2}\in C[0,T]
and
t\in[0,T]
that

|G_{0}[p_{2}](t)-G_{0}[p_{1}](t)|=\bigg|\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p_{1}](t)}^{\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p_{2}](t)}\psi(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg|\leq C_{\psi}^{*}\max_{\tau\in[0,t]}|p_{2}(\tau)-p_{1}(\tau)|.

(2.16)

Moreover, the Preisach potential is continuous from
L^{2}(\Omega;C[0,T])
to
L^{1}(\Omega;C[0,T])
, as it holds

|U_{0}[p_{2}](t)-U_{0}[p_{1}](t)|\leq\frac{C_{\psi}^{*}}{2}\max_{\tau\in[0,t]}|p_{2}-p_{1}|(\tau)\left(|p_{2}(t)|+|p_{1}(t)|+2\right).

(2.17)

From Hypothesis
2.2
and identity (
2.7
) for the play we also obtain

0<U_{0}[p]\leq C_{\psi}^{*}(1+|p|)^{2},\qquad|D_{0}[p]_{t}|\leq C|p_{t}|.

(2.18)

The Preisach operator admits also a family of “nonlinear” energies. As a consequence of (
2.7
), we have for a. e.
t
the inequality

\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p]_{t}(p-\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p])\geq 0,

hence

\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p]_{t}(h(p)-h(\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p]))\geq 0

for every nondecreasing function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Hence, for every absolutely continuous input
p
, a counterpart of (
2.11
) in the form

G_{0}[p]_{t}h(p)-U_{h}[p]_{t}\geq 0\text{ a.\,e.}

(2.19)

holds with a modified potential

U_{h}[p](t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\mathfrak{f}_{r}[p](t)}h(v)\psi(r,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}r.

(2.20)

This is related to the fact that for every absolutely continuous nondecreasing function
\hat{h}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the mapping
G_{\hat{h}}:=G_{0}\circ\hat{h}
is also a Preisach operator, see
[
13
]
.

3  Statement of the problem

We introduce the spaces

X=W^{1,2}(\Omega)\,,\quad X_{0}=\{\psi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}):\psi\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0\}\,,\quad X_{q^{*}}=W^{1,q^{*}}(\Omega)

(3.1)

for some
q^{*}>2
that will be specified below in Theorem
3.3
. Taking into account the boundary conditions (
1.7
), we consider (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) in variational form

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\big((\chi+\rho^{*}(1-\chi))(f(p)+G_{0}[p]+\mathrm{\,div\,}u)\big)_{t}\,\phi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\mu(p)\nabla p\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\partial\Omega}\alpha(x)(p^{*}-p)\,\phi\,\mathrm{d}s(x),\end{aligned}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}(P[\nabla_{s}u]+\mathbf{B}\nabla_{s}u_{t}):\nabla_{s}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\big(p(\chi+\rho^{*}(1-\chi))+\beta(\theta-\theta_{c})\big)\,\mathrm{\,div\,}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\Omega}g\cdot\psi\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{aligned}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\Bigg(\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta)_{t}-\mathbf{B}\nabla_{s}u_{t}:\nabla_{s}u_{t}-\|D_{P}[\nabla_{s}u]_{t}\|_{*}-\mu(p)|\nabla p|^{2}-(\chi{+}\rho^{*}(1{-}\chi))|D_{0}[p]_{t}|\\
&\quad-\gamma(\theta,\mathrm{\,div\,}u)\chi_{t}^{2}+\left(\frac{L}{\theta_{c}}\chi_{t}+\beta\mathrm{\,div\,}u_{t}\right)\theta\Bigg)\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\theta)\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega(x)(\theta^{*}-\theta)\,\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{aligned}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\gamma(\theta,\mathrm{\,div\,}u)\chi_{t}+\partial I_{[0,1]}(\chi)\ni(1-\rho^{*})\!\left(\Phi(p)+pG_{0}[p]-U_{0}[p]+p\mathrm{\,div\,}u\right)+L\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_{c}}-1\right)\ \text{a.\,e.}

(3.5)

for a. e.
t\in(0,T)
and all test functions
\phi\in X
,
\psi\in X_{0}
and
\zeta\in X_{q^{*}}
. Note that we split the capillary hysteresis terms in hysteretic and non-hysteretic part according to (
2.1
), (
2.13
)–(
2.15
). This is done in view of the regularization performed in Section
4
, where only the non-hysteretic part will be affected by the cut-off.

We assume the following hypotheses to hold.

Hypothesis 3.1
.

There exist constants
A^{\flat}>0
,
B^{\flat}>0
,
\bar{\theta}>0
such that

(i)

\mathbf{A}_{e}
,
\mathbf{A}_{h}
,
\mathbf{B}
are constant symmetric positive definite fourth order tensors such that
\mathbf{A}_{e}\,\xi:\xi\geq A^{\flat}|\xi|^{2}
,
\mathbf{A}_{h}\,\xi:\xi\geq A^{\flat}|\xi|^{2}
,
\mathbf{B}\,\xi:\xi\geq B^{\flat}|\xi|^{2}
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
;

(ii)

g\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}))\cap W^{1,2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}))
is a given function and there exists a function
\widehat{G}\in L^{4}(\Omega\times(0,T))
such that
g=-\nabla\widehat{G}
;

(iii)

\alpha\in W^{1,\infty}(\partial\Omega)
,
\alpha(x)\geq 0
a. e. and
\int_{\partial\Omega}\alpha(x)\,\mathrm{d}s(x)>0
;
\omega\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
,
\omega(x)\geq 0
a. e. and
\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}s(x)>0
;

(iv)

p^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega\times(0,T))
and
p^{*}_{t}\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega\times(0,T))
,

,

,
\theta^{*}(x,t)\geq\bar{\theta}
a. e.;

(v)

p^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap W^{2,2}(\Omega)
,
u^{0}\in X_{0}\cap W^{1,4}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
,
\theta^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,2}(\Omega)
,
\theta^{0}(x)\geq\bar{\theta}
a. e.,
\chi^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
\chi^{0}(x)\in[0,1]
a. e.

We also assume that there exist constants
f^{\sharp}>f^{\flat}>0
,
\nu\in(0,1/2]
,
\mu^{\flat}>0
,
c^{\sharp}>c^{\flat}>0
,
1/2\leq b<\hat{b}<1
,
\kappa^{\sharp}>\kappa^{\flat}>0
,
0<a<1-b
,
a<\hat{a}<\frac{(8+3a+2b)(1+b)}{7-2b}
,
\gamma^{\sharp}>\gamma^{\flat}>0
such that the nonlinearities satisfy the following conditions:

(vi)

G[p]=f(p)+G_{0}[p]
where
f:\mathbb{R}\to(C_{\psi}^{-},1-C_{\psi}^{+})
with
C_{\psi}^{\pm}
from (
2.10
) is a continuously differentiable function,
f^{\flat}(1+|p|)^{-1-\nu}\leq f^{\prime}(p)\leq f^{\sharp}
for all
p\in\mathbb{R}
, and
G_{0}
is the Preisach operator from Section
2
with density function satisfying Hypothesis
2.2
and with potential
U_{0}
and dissipation
D_{0}
as in (
2.12
);

(vii)

\mu:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function,
\mu(p)\geq\mu^{\flat}
for all
p\in\mathbb{R}
;

(viii)

\mathcal{C}_{V}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a continuously differentiable function,
\mathcal{C}_{V}^{\prime}(\theta)=:c_{V}(\theta)
is such that
c^{\flat}(1+\theta^{b})\leq c_{V}(\theta)\leq c^{\sharp}(1+\theta^{\hat{b}})
for all
\theta\geq 0
;

(ix)

\kappa:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a continuous function,
\kappa^{\flat}(1+\theta^{1+a})\leq\kappa(\theta)\leq\kappa^{\sharp}(1+\theta^{1+\hat{a}})
for all
\theta\geq 0
;

(x)

\gamma:[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a continuous function,
\gamma^{\flat}(1+\theta+|\mathrm{\,div\,}u|^{2})\leq\gamma(\theta,\mathrm{\,div\,}u)\leq\gamma^{\sharp}(1+\theta+|\mathrm{\,div\,}u|^{2})
for all
\theta\geq 0,\,u\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

We model the elastoplasticity following
[
17
]
. We assume that a convex subset

with nonempty interior representing the admissible plastic stress domain is given in the space
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}
of symmetric tensors, and that the constitutive relation between the strain tensor
\varepsilon
and the stress tensor

involves two fourth order tensors
\mathbf{A}_{h}
(the kinematic hardening tensor) and
\mathbf{A}_{e}
(the elasticity tensor). We define the constitutive operator

by the formula

P[\varepsilon]=\mathbf{A}_{h}\varepsilon+\sigma^{p},

(3.6)

where

is the solution of the variational inequality

\sigma^{p}\in Z,\ \big(\varepsilon_{t}-\mathbf{A}_{e}^{-1}\sigma^{p}_{t}\big):\big(\sigma^{p}-z\big)\geq 0\quad\mbox{a.\,e. }\ \forall z\in Z\,,\quad\sigma^{p}(0)=Q_{Z}(\varepsilon(0))

(3.7)

for a given
\varepsilon\in W^{1,1}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}})
, where

is the orthogonal projection onto
Z
. The variational inequality (
3.7
) has a unique solution
\sigma^{p}\in W^{1,1}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}})
and the solution mapping

is strongly continuous, see
[

]
. It holds

\left|P[\varepsilon]_{t}\right|\leq|\varepsilon_{t}|.

(3.8)

The energy potential
U_{P}
and the dissipation operator

associated with

are defined by the formula

U_{P}[\varepsilon]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{A}_{h}\varepsilon:\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{A}_{e}^{-1}\sigma^{p}:\sigma^{p},\qquad D_{P}[\varepsilon]=\varepsilon-\mathbf{A}_{e}^{-1}\sigma^{p}.

(3.9)

Let

denote the Minkowski functional of the polar set
Z^{*}
to

. The energy identity

P[\varepsilon]:\varepsilon_{t}-U_{P}[\varepsilon]_{t}=\left\|D_{P}[\varepsilon]_{t}\right\|_{*}\ \mbox{a.\,e.},

(3.10)

where

is a seminorm in

, and the inequalities

(3.11)

hold for all inputs
\varepsilon\in W^{1,1}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}})
.

The operator

can be extended to a continuous operator in the space

in the sense that if

(3.12)

For two inputs

we denote

,

\displaystyle(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}):\left((\varepsilon_{1})_{t}-(\varepsilon_{2})_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\mathbf{A}_{h}(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}):(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})+\mathbf{A}_{e}^{-1}(\sigma^{p}_{1}-\sigma^{p}_{2}):(\sigma^{p}_{1}-\sigma^{p}_{2})\Big)\ \mbox{a.\,e.},

\displaystyle|\sigma_{1}(t)-\sigma_{2}(t)|

For inputs

\nabla_{s}u_{t}(\cdot,t)\in L^{r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}})
, and it holds

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{s}u_{t}|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}|w|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for a.\,e. }t\in(0,T).

Integrating over
t\in(0,T)
we obtain that
\nabla_{s}u_{t}\in L^{r}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}})
.

Step 2.
Let us define the convex and closed subset
\mathcal{U}_{r}:=\{w\in L^{r}(0,T;X_{0}):\nabla_{s}w_{t}\in L^{r}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}),\ w(x,0)=u^{0}(x)\text{ a.\,e.}\}\subset L^{r}(0,T;X_{0}\cap W^{1,r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}))
. Note that the trace operator is well defined on this space, so that the initial condition makes sense. Let
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{r}
, and let
u
be the solution of the equation

\int_{\Omega}P[\nabla_{s}\hat{u}](x,t):\nabla_{s}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{B}\nabla_{s}u_{t}(x,t):\nabla_{s}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{\,div\,}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

the existence of which follows from Step 1. We prove that the mapping
\hat{u}_{t}\mapsto u_{t}
is a contraction with respect to a suitable norm.

Indeed, let
\hat{u}_{1},\hat{u}_{2}
be given, and let
u_{1},u_{2}
be the corresponding solutions. The difference
\bar{u}=u_{1}-u_{2}
is the solution of the equation

\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{B}\nabla_{s}\bar{u}_{t}(x,t):\nabla_{s}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}(P[\nabla_{s}\hat{u}_{1}]-P[\nabla_{s}\hat{u}_{2}])(x,t):\nabla_{s}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

According to Step 1, we have

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{s}\bar{u}_{t}|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}|P[\nabla_{s}\hat{u}_{1}]-P[\nabla_{s}\hat{u}_{2}]|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{a.\,e.}

(4.23)

By inequality (
3.14
) we have for a. e.
(x,t)

|P[\nabla_{s}\hat{u}_{1}]-P[\nabla_{s}\hat{u}_{2}]|(x,t)\leq C\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla_{s}(\hat{u}_{1}-\hat{u}_{2})_{t}|(x,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

with a constant
C>0
. Hence, by Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|P[\nabla_{s}\hat{u}_{1}]-P[\nabla_{s}\hat{u}_{2}]|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla_{s}(\hat{u}_{1}-\hat{u}_{2})_{t}|(x,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{r}\,\mathrm{d}x

(4.24)

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{r-1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{s}(\hat{u}_{1}-\hat{u}_{2})_{t}|^{r}(x,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(4.25)

Now, set

W(t)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{s}(u_{1}-u_{2})_{t}|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\hat{W}(t)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{s}(\hat{u}_{1}-\hat{u}_{2})_{t}|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x.

It follows from (
4.23
) and (
4.25
) that

W(t)\leq Ct^{r-1}\int_{0}^{t}\hat{W}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

We now multiply both sides of the above inequality by
\mathrm{e}^{-Ct^{r}}
, and after an integration over
t\in[0,T]
we obtain from the Fubini Theorem

\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{-Ct^{r}}W(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{p}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-C\tau^{r}}-\mathrm{e}^{-CT^{r}}\right)\hat{W}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\frac{1}{p}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{-Ct^{r}}\hat{W}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

This means that the mapping
\hat{u}_{t}\mapsto u_{t}
is a contraction in
L^{r}(0,T;X_{0}\cap W^{1,r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}))
with respect to the weighted norm

|||u_{t}|||=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{-Ct^{r}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{s}u_{t}|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/r},

hence it has a unique fixed point which is a solution of (
4.22
).

Step 3.
The mapping which with a right-hand side
w\in L^{r}(\Omega\times(0,T))
associates the solution
u_{t}\in L^{r}(0,T;X_{0}\cap W^{1,r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}))
of (
4.22
) is Lipschitz continuous. Indeed, consider
w_{1},w_{2}
and the corresponding solutions
u_{1},u_{2}
, and set as before
\bar{w}=w_{1}-w_{2}
,
\bar{u}=u_{1}-u_{2}
. As a counterpart of (
4.23
) we get

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{s}\bar{u}_{t}|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}\left(|P[\nabla_{s}u_{1}]-P[\nabla_{s}u_{2}]|^{r}+|\bar{w}|^{r}\right)(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{a.\,e.},

and the computations as in (
4.25
) yield

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{s}\bar{u}_{t}|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq Ct^{r-1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{s}\bar{u}_{t}|^{r}(x,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau+C\int_{\Omega}|\bar{w}|^{r}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{a.\,e.}

We obtain the Lipschitz continuity result when we test by
\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{C}{p}t^{r}}
and integrate over
t\in[0,T]
, similarly as in Step 2.

Now, coming back to our equation (
4.18
), we see that it is of the form (
4.22
) with
w(x,t)=w^{(n)}(x,t):=p^{(n)}(\chi^{(n)}+\rho^{*}(1-\chi^{(n)}))+\beta(Q_{R}((\theta^{(n)})^{+})-\theta_{c})(x,t)+\widehat{G}(x,t)
, where
\widehat{G}
is from Hypothesis
3.1
(ii). Therefore, denoting by
\mathcal{S}:w^{(n)}\mapsto u^{(n)}_{t}
its associated solution operator, we conclude that (
4.17
)–(
4.20
) give rise to a system of ODEs with a locally Lipschitz continuous right-hand side containing the operator
\mathcal{S}
.

Thus system (
4.17
)–(
4.20
) has a unique strong solution in a maximal interval of existence
[0,T_{n}]\subset[0,T]
. This interval coincides with the whole
[0,T]
, provided we prove that the solution remains bounded in
[0,T_{n})
.

We now derive a series of estimates. Note that we decompose the auxiliary variables
v
and
z
instead of
p
and
\theta
into a Fourier series with respect to the basis
\mathcal{W}
because we are going to test equations (
4.17
) and (
4.19
) by nonlinear expressions of
p
and
\theta
, namely, by their Kirchhoff transforms (
4.12
). Indeed, the Galerkin method allows only to test by linear functions and their derivatives.

Moreover, we do not discretize the momentum balance equation because considering the full PDE is the only way to deduce compactness of the sequence
\{\nabla_{s}u^{(n)}_{t}\}
, which is needed in order to pass to the limit in some nonlinear terms. Indeed, we will not be able to control higher derivatives of
u^{(n)}
, and this will prevent us from applying the usual embedding theorems.

Estimate 1.
We test (

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}(\chi^{(n)}+\rho^{*}(1-\chi^{(n)}))G_{0}[p^{(n)}]_{t}M_{R}(p^{(n)})\,\mathrm{d}x

U_{M,R}[p]\leq C_{R}\left(1+|p|\right)^{2}

2.18

and Hypothesis

Moreover, thanks again to Hypothesis

(vii), the boundary term is such that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\alpha(x)(p^{(n)}-p^{*})M_{R}(p^{(n)})\,\mathrm{d}s(x)

where for

we set

M_{R}^{\prime}(0)&\text{ for }p=0.\end{cases}

Moreover, by Hölder’s inequality and Hypothesis

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(g\cdot u^{(n)}_{t})(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x

(4.31)

where in the last line we used first Korn’s inequality (
3.16

4.6
), (
4.27
) and (

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\Big((\chi^{(n)}+\rho^{*}(1-\chi^{(n)}))(V_{M,R}(p^{(n)})+U_{M,R}[p^{(n)}])+U_{P}[\nabla_{s}u^{(n)}]\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

where we used also Hypothesis
3.1
(i), Young’s inequality and the pointwise inequality

|\mathrm{\,div\,}u|^{2}(x,t)\leq 3\,|\nabla_{s}u|^{2}(x,t)\quad\text{for all }(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T)

to absorb

We need to control

\chi^{(n)}_{t}+\partial I_{[0,1]}(\chi^{(n)})\ni F^{(n)}

F^{(n)}=\frac{(1-\rho^{*})\left(\Phi_{R}(p^{(n)})+p^{(n)}G_{0}[p^{(n)}]-U_{0}[p^{(n)}]+p^{(n)}\mathrm{\,div\,}u^{(n)}\right)+L\left(Q_{R}((\theta^{(n)})^{+})/\theta_{c}-1\right)}{\gamma_{R}(p^{(n)},\theta^{(n)},\mathrm{\,div\,}u^{(n)})}\,,

\chi^{(n)}\in[0,1],\quad(F^{(n)}-\chi^{(n)}_{t})(\chi^{(n)}-\tilde{\chi})\geq 0\quad\text{a.\,e.}\ \ \forall\tilde{\chi}\in[0,1].

for a. e.

Estimate 3.
We test (

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Bigg(\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta^{(n)})_{t}-\mathbf{B}\nabla_{s}u^{(n)}_{t}:\nabla_{s}u^{(n)}_{t}-\|D_{P}[\nabla_{s}u^{(n)}]_{t}\|_{*}-\mu_{R}(p^{(n)})Q_{R}(|\nabla p^{(n)}|^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad+(\chi^{(n)}+\rho^{*}(1-\chi^{(n)}))|D_{0}[p^{(n)}]_{t}|+\gamma_{R}(p^{(n)},\theta^{(n)},\mathrm{\,div\,}u^{(n)})|\chi^{(n)}_{t}|^{2}

5  Estimates independent of

We now come back to our cut-off system (

5.1 Positivity of the temperature

For every nonnegative test function
\zeta\in X
we have, by virtue of (

with a constant

depending on
L,\,\theta_{c},\,\beta,\,\mathbf{B},\,\gamma^{\flat}
. Let now

from Hypothesis

is nondecreasing and positive. Taking into account the fact that

we have in particular

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\big(\mathcal{C}_{V}(\varphi)-\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta)\big)_{t}\,\zeta+\kappa(Q_{R}(\theta^{+}))\nabla(\varphi-\theta)\cdot\nabla\zeta\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega(x)(\theta-\theta^{*})\zeta\,\mathrm{d}s(x)

Consider now the following regularization of the Heaviside function

H_{\varepsilon}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 0&\quad\text{for }z\leq 0,\\
\frac{z}{\varepsilon}&\quad\text{for }0<z\leq{\varepsilon},\\
1&\quad\text{for }z>{\varepsilon},\end{aligned}\right.

By the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem we can pass to the limit in the above inequality for

, getting

\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathcal{C}_{V}(\varphi)-\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta)\big)_{t}\,H(\varphi-\theta)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 0,

that is, by the monotonicity of

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{C}_{V}(\varphi)-\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta)\right)^{+}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 0,\qquad(\mathcal{C}_{V}(\varphi)-\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta))^{+}(x,0)=0

which implies
(\mathcal{C}_{V}(\varphi)-\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta))^{+}\equiv 0
. Owing again to the monotonicity of
\mathcal{C}_{V}
and
\varphi
, we conclude that, independently of
R
,

\theta(x,t)\geq\varphi(t)\geq\varphi(T)=:\theta_{T}>0\quad\text{for all }x\text{ and }t.

(5.1)

We now pass to a series of estimates independent of
R

5.5 Estimate for the capillary pressure

\bar{r}:=\frac{(1+b)\bar{p}}{(4+b)}>\hat{a},

(5.61)

with
\hat{a}
from Hypothesis
3.1
(ix),
and using also (
5.59
) we obtain

\|\hat{\theta}\|_{\bar{p}}+\mathop{\mathrm{sup\,ess}}_{t\in(0,T)}|\hat{\theta}(t)|_{\bar{r}+1+b}\leq C

(5.62)

with
\bar{p}
arbitrarily close to
\frac{(8+3a+2b)(4+b)}{7-2b}
. We now come back to (
5.9
), which we test by
\zeta=\theta
(note that this is an admissible choice by Proposition
4.1
). It holds

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{C}_{V}(\theta)_{t}\theta(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}c_{V}(\theta)\theta\theta_{t}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{\theta(x,t)}c_{V}(s)s\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

hence from Hypothesis
3.1
(ix) and (
5.55
) we obtain, after a time integration,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\theta^{2+b}(x,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\hat{\theta})|\nabla\theta|^{2}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

(5.63)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega(x)\theta^{2}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}s(x)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\left(1+\|\theta\|_{(4+b)/(1+b)}\right).

Using the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (
3.18
) again with
q=\frac{4+b}{1+b}
,
s=1+b
(note that
1+b<\frac{4+b}{1+b}<6
under our hypotheses),
r=2
and
N=3
we have that, for each fixed
t\in(0,T)
,

|\theta(t)|_{(4+b)/(1+b)}\leq C\left(1+|\nabla\theta(t)|_{2}^{\delta}\right)

with
\delta=\frac{6(3-b^{2}-b)}{(5-b)(4+b)}
and where we used estimate (
5.9
). Raising to the power
(4+b)/(1+b)
and integrating
\int_{0}^{T}\,\mathrm{d}t
we get

\|\theta\|_{(4+b)/(1+b)}\leq C\left(1+\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\delta/2}\right)\leq C\left(1+\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\hat{\theta})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\delta/2}\right).

Plugging this back into (
5.63
) and using Young’s inequality we deduce

\int_{\Omega}\theta^{2+b}(x,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\hat{\theta})|\nabla\theta|^{2}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega}\omega(x)\theta^{2}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}s(x)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C.

(5.64)

This enables us to derive an upper bound for the integral
\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\hat{\theta})\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x
, which we need for getting an estimate for
\theta_{t}
from equation (
5.9
). By Hölder’s inequality and Hypothesis
3.1
(ix) we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\kappa(\hat{\theta})\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\zeta|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|\kappa^{1/2}(\hat{\theta})\nabla\theta\cdot\kappa^{1/2}(\hat{\theta})\nabla\zeta|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\hat{\theta})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega}\max\{1,\hat{\theta}^{1+\hat{a}}\}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}.

(5.65)

Let us now choose
\hat{q}>1
such that
(1+\hat{a})\hat{q}=1+\bar{r}+b
, where
\bar{r}
is defined in (
5.61
). Note that such a
\hat{q}
exists since
1+\bar{r}+b>1+\hat{a}+b>1+\hat{a}
. Defining

q^{*}:=\frac{2\hat{q}}{\hat{q}-1}=2+\frac{2}{\hat{q}-1}>2\,,

(5.66)

we get from Hölder’s inequality with conjugate exponents
\left(\hat{q}\,,\,\frac{q^{*}}{2}\right)
that

\int_{\Omega}\hat{\theta}^{1+\hat{a}}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}\hat{\theta}^{1+\bar{r}+b}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/\hat{q}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\zeta|^{q^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2/q^{*}}\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\zeta|^{q^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2/q^{*}}

by virtue of (
5.62
). Inequality (
5.65
) then yields the bound

\int_{\Omega}|\kappa(\hat{\theta})\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\zeta|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\hat{\theta})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\zeta|^{q^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/q^{*}}.

Hence, by (
5.64
),

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\kappa(\hat{\theta})\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\zeta|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\|\zeta\|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,q^{*}}(\Omega))}.

From (
5.55
) it follows that testing with
\zeta\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,q^{*}}(\Omega))
is admissible, in the sense that the term
\Gamma\zeta
is integrable. This is obvious if
q^{*}\geq 3
. For
q^{*}<3
the space
W^{1,q^{*}}(\Omega)
is embedded in
L^{q^{*}_{S}}(\Omega)
with

\frac{1}{q^{*}_{S}}=\frac{1}{q^{*}}-\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{2\hat{q}},

so that
\frac{3}{4+b}+\frac{1}{q^{*}_{S}}\leq 1
. We thus obtain from (
5.9
) that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\theta_{t}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\|\zeta\|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,q^{*}}(\Omega))}.

(5.67)

6  Passage to the limit as
R\to\infty

In this section we conclude the proof of Theorem
3.3
by passing to the limit in (
5.39
)–(
5.42
) as
R\to\infty
. Most of the convergences can be handled as at the end of Subsection
4.3
, hence we focus here on the main differences.

Let
R_{i}\nearrow\infty
be a sequence such that
R_{i}>R_{\sigma}
, with
R_{\sigma}
as in (
5.38
), and let
(p,u,\chi,\theta)=(p^{(i)},u^{(i)},\chi^{(i)},\theta^{(i)})
be solutions of (
5.39
)–(
5.42
) corresponding to
R=R_{i}
, with
\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}^{(i)}=Q_{R_{i}}(\theta^{(i)})
and test functions
\phi,\zeta\in X
,
\psi\in X_{0}
. Our aim is to check that at least a subsequence converges as
i\to\infty
to a solution of (
3.2
)–(
3.5
) with test functions
\phi\in X
,
\psi\in X_{0}
and
\zeta\in X_{q^{*}}
.

First, for the capillary pressure
p=p^{(i)}
we have the estimates (
5.38
), (
5.46
), (
5.47
), (
5.48
) and (
5.49
), which imply that, passing to a subsequence if necessary,

\begin{array}[]{rcll}p^{(i)}_{t}&\to&p_{t}&\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T)),\\
p^{(i)}&\to&p&\quad\text{strongly in }L^{q}(\Omega;C[0,T])\ \text{for all }q\in[1,\infty),\\
\nabla p^{(i)}&\to&\nabla p&\quad\text{strongly in }L^{q}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3})\ \text{for all }q\in\left[1,\frac{10}{3}\right),\end{array}

by compact embedding. We easily show that

Q_{R_{i}}(|\nabla p^{(i)}|^{2})\to|\nabla p|^{2}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{q}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3})\ \text{for all }q\in\left[1\,,\,\frac{5}{3}\right).

(6.1)

Indeed, let
\Omega_{T}^{(i)}\subset\Omega\times(0,T)
be the set of all
(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T)
such that
|\nabla p^{(i)}(x,t)|^{2}>R_{i}
. By (
5.49
) we have

C\geq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla p^{(i)}(x,t)|^{10/3}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\iint_{\Omega_{T}^{(i)}}|\nabla p^{(i)}(x,t)|^{10/3}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\geq|\Omega_{T}^{(i)}|R_{i}^{5/3},

hence
|\Omega_{T}^{(i)}|\leq CR_{i}^{-5/3}
. For
q<\frac{5}{3}
we use Hölder’s inequality to get the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\left|Q_{R_{i}}(|\nabla p^{(i)}|^{2})-|\nabla p^{(i)}|^{2}\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\iint_{\Omega_{T}^{(i)}}\left|R_{i}-|\nabla p^{(i)}|^{2}\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\iint_{\Omega_{T}^{(i)}}|\nabla p^{(i)}|^{2q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\left(\iint_{\Omega_{T}^{(i)}}|\nabla p^{(i)}|^{10/3}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{3q/5}|\Omega_{T}^{(i)}|^{1-3q/5}\leq CR_{i}^{-(5-3q)/3},

and (
6.1
) follows.

For the temperature
\theta=\theta^{(i)}
we proceed in a similar way. By estimates (
5.64
) and (
5.67
) we obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\nabla\theta^{(i)}&\to&\nabla\theta&\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3}),\\
\theta^{(i)}_{t}&\to&\theta_{t}&\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;W^{-1,q^{*}}(\Omega)),\\
\theta^{(i)}&\to&\theta&\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T)),\end{array}

where for the last convergence we exploited
[
18
, Theorem 5.1]
and the embedding
W^{-1,q^{*}}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{-1,2}(\Omega)
(recall that
q^{*}>2
). Furthermore, estimate (
5.62
) entails that
\hat{\theta}^{(i)}
are uniformly bounded in
L^{q}(\Omega\times(0,T))
for every
q<\frac{(8+3a+2b)(4+b)}{7-2b}
. Hence a similar argument as above yields that

\hat{\theta}^{(i)}=Q_{R_{i}}(\theta^{(i)})\to\theta\quad\text{strongly in }L^{q}(\Omega\times(0,T))\ \text{for all }q\in\left[1\,,\,\frac{(8+3a+2b)(4+b)}{7-2b}\right).

Estimate (
5.48
) and the Sobolev embeddings yield an inequality similar to (
4.56
), but with a constant independent of both
\eta
and
R
. This is enough to obtain that
\nabla_{s}u^{(i)}\to\nabla_{s}u
,
\nabla_{s}u^{(i)}_{t}\to\nabla_{s}u_{t}
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega;C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}))
and in
L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}})
, respectively. The strong convergences
\chi^{(i)}\to\chi
,
\chi^{(i)}_{t}\to\chi_{t}
then follow as at the end of Subsection
4.2
, as well as the convergence of the hysteresis terms.

Therefore the limit as
i\to\infty
yields a solution to (
3.2
)–(
3.5
), and the proof of Theorem
3.3
is completed.
