Perturbative expansion of irreversible works in symmetric and asymmetric processes
T. Koide

Abstract
The systematic expansion method of the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation is developed by generalizing the formulation proposed in [J. Phys. A50, 325001 (2017)]. Using this method, we obtain a new formula to calculate the mean work perturbatively which is applicable to systems with degeneracy in the eigenvalues of the Fokker-Planck operator. This method enables us to study how the geometrical symmetry affects thermodynamic description of Brownian motion. To illustrate the application of the derived theory, we consider the Fokker-Planck equation with a two-dimensional harmonic potential. To investigate the effect of symmetry of the potential, we study thermodynamic properties in symmetric and asymmetric deformation processes of the potential: the rotational symmetry of the harmonic potential is held in the former, but it is broken in the latter. Optimized deformations in these processes are defined by minimizing mean works. Comparing these optimized processes, we find that the difference between the symmetric and asymmetric processes is maximized when the deformation time of the potential is given by a critical time which is characterized by the relaxation time of the Fokker-Planck equation. This critical time in the mean work is smaller than that of the change of the mean energy because of the hysteresis effect in the irreversible processes.

II
Thermodynamic interpretation in Fokker-Planck equation

Before discussing the perturbation theory, we briefly summarize the thermodynamic description
of the systems described by the Fokker-Planck equation.
The discussion in this section is based on stochastic energetics
sekimoto-book
.

We consider a
D
-dimensional Brownian particle which is confined in an external potential
V
and interacts with a thermal bath with temperature
T
.
When the effect of friction is sufficiently large and hence that of inertia is negligibly small,
the distribution of a Brownian particle in the configuration space,
\rho({\bf x},t)
, is described by
the Fokker-Planck equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho({\bf x},t)=\nabla\cdot\left\{\frac{1}{\nu\beta}\nabla+\frac{1}{\nu}(\nabla V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t}))\right\}\rho({\bf x},t)\equiv{\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})\rho({\bf x},t)\,.

(1)

where
\nu
is the coefficient of friction and
\beta=1/(k_{B}T)
with
k_{B}
being the Boltzmann constant.
In this work, the temperature is constant but the form of the potential
V
is deformed
by changing the control parameters
{\bf a}_{t}=(a^{(1)}_{t},a^{(2)}_{t},\cdots)
.
Because of the confinement potential
V
, the particle distribution
\rho({\bf x},t)
vanishes quickly
at an infinite distance,
{\displaystyle\lim_{|{\bf x}|\rightarrow\infty}}\rho({\bf x},t)=0
.

As is well-known, the Fokker-Planck equation (
1
) is reproduced from Brownian motion which is described by the stochastic differential equation,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{\bf x}_{t}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\nu}\nabla V({\bf x}_{t},{\bf a}_{t})+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta\nu}}\mathrm{d}{\bf W}_{t}\,.

(2)

where

\mathrm{d}{\bf W}_{t}={\bf W}(t+dt)-{\bf W}(t)
is the inclination of the standard Wiener process
{\bf W}(t)
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{d}{W}^{i}_{t}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{d}{W}^{i}_{t}\mathrm{d}{W}^{j}_{t^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\,\delta_{t,t^{\prime}}\,\delta_{i,j}\,.

(4)

The ensemble average of the Wiener process is denoted by
\mathrm{E}[\,\,\,\,]
.
See, for example, Ref.
gardiner
for detail.
Using this stochastic trajectory of the Brownian particle,
the particle distribution is defined by

\displaystyle\rho({\bf x},t)=\int\mathrm{d}{\bf R}\,\rho_{0}({\bf R})\mathrm{E}\left[\delta^{(D)}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{t})\right]\,,

(5)

where
D
is the spatial dimension and
\rho_{0}({\bf R})
denotes the initial distribution of the Brownian particle at the initial position
{\bf R}
.
It is straightforward to show that
\rho({\bf x},t)
satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation (
1
)
gardiner
;
sekimoto-book
.

In this model, the Brownian particle interacts with the thermal bath through
the dissipative force
(-\nu\mathrm{d}{\bf x}_{t}/\mathrm{d}t)
and the random force
(\sqrt{{2}/{\beta\nu}}\,{\mathrm{d}{\bf W}_{t}}/{\mathrm{d}t})
.
In stochastic energetics, the heat
\mathrm{d}q_{t}
associated with a single stochastic trajectory
is interpreted as the work done by these interactions and thus given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\left(-\nu\frac{\mathrm{d}{x}^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta\nu}}\frac{\mathrm{d}{W}^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)\circ\mathrm{d}{x}^{i}_{t}\,,

(6)

where
\circ
is the Stratonovich definition of the product,

\displaystyle{A}_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}{W}_{t}=\frac{{A}_{t}+{A}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}{2}\mathrm{d}{W}_{t}\,.

(7)

Using Eq. (
2
) to the definition (
6
), we can show that the above heat is reexpressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{q}_{t}=\mathrm{d}V({\bf x}_{t},{\bf a}_{t})-\mathrm{d}{w}_{t}\,.

(8)

The first term on the right-hand side gives the change of the internal energy
and the second term represents the work done by an external system, which is induced by the deformation of the external potential and defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{w}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial V({\bf x}_{t},{\bf a}_{t})}{\partial a^{i}_{t}}\mathrm{d}a^{i}_{t}\,.

(9)

Note that the relation (
8
) is satisfied for each stochastic trajectory

The expectation value of Eq. (
8
) leads to a law analogous to the first law of thermodynamics,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Q_{t}=\{E({t+\mathrm{d}t})-E(t)\}-\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,,

(10)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Q_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf R}\,\rho_{0}({\bf R})\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{d}q_{t}]\,,

(11)

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf R}\,\rho_{0}({\bf R})\mathrm{E}[V({\bf x}_{t},{\bf a}_{t})]=\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,\rho({\bf x},t)V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}W_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf R}\,\rho_{0}({\bf R})\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{d}w_{t}]=\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,\rho({\bf x},t)\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}{\partial a^{i}_{t}}\frac{\mathrm{d}a^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,.

(13)

Using the Shannon entropy (multiplying the Boltzmann constant) defined by

\displaystyle S(t)=-k_{B}\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,\rho({\bf x},t)\ln\rho({\bf x},t)\,,

(14)

we further find that there exists the following inequality
sekimoto-book
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}-\frac{1}{T}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\geq 0\,,

(15)

where the equality is satisfied for the stationary state of the Fokker-Planck equation.
This inequality can be regarded as the second law of thermodynamics.
Note, however, that the Shannon entropy is definable even in non-equilibrium states and thus
the above inequality is not exactly the same as the the second law of thermodynamics.

In this paper, we consider the irreversible processes where the system interacts with the thermal bath with a fixed temperature
T
,
and thus the mean work should satisfy the following inequality:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}W_{t}=\{E({t+\mathrm{d}t})-E(t)\}-\mathrm{d}Q_{t}\geq F_{helm}({\bf a}_{t+\mathrm{d}t})-F_{helm}({\bf a}_{t})\,.

(16)

This is obtained by using the inequality (
15
) and
the Helmholtz free energy defined by

\displaystyle F_{helm}({\bf a}_{t})=E(t)-TS(t)\,.

(17)

Here, however,
S(t)
is not the thermodynamic entropy but the Shannon entropy introduced above.
Therefore this Helmholtz free energy is not the same as the corresponding quantity in thermodynamics.

III
Eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of time-dependent Fokker-Planck operator

In this section, we define the expansion basis following Ref.
koide17
.
A similar expansion basis is discussed, for example, in Ref.
gardiner
.
We generalize this method to the case of the time-dependent Fokker–Planck operator referring to the discussion in Ref.
namiki
.
Note that the eigenvalue theory of the time-periodic Fokker-Planck operator (the Kolmogorov operator)
is discussed in Ref.
caceres
and the properties found below are consistent with the result.
See also Ref.
risken
.

The eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the time-dependent Fokker-Planck operator
{\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})

are defined by

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})\,\underline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=-\underline{\lambda}_{n}(t)\,\underline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\,.

(18)

We call these eigenfunctions the
foot
states.
Here the index
n
characterizes the eigenvalue
\underline{\lambda}_{n}(t)
which is a function of time.
The degeneracy associated with the index
n

is characterized by a set of non-negative integers
\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}=(\alpha_{1,n},\alpha_{2,n},\cdots)
, satisfying

0\leq\alpha_{i,n}\leq d^{(i,n)}({\bf a}_{t})
.
Thus the upper limit of the sums of degeneracy is characterized by
a set of integers
{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})=(\mathrm{d}^{(1,n)}({\bf a}_{t}),\mathrm{d}^{(2,n)}({\bf a}_{t}),\cdots)
.
If there is no degeneracy,
{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})=(0,0,\cdots)={\bf 0}
,

\alpha_{i,n}
can take only zeros,
\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}=(0,0,\cdots)={\bf 0}
.

Note that
{\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})
is not self-adjoint and the above eigenfunctions do not form a complete set.
To find the complete set, we define the adjoint operator of
{\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})
by

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,g({\bf x}){\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})f({\bf x})=\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,\left({\cal L}^{\dagger}_{t}({\bf x})g({\bf x})\right)f({\bf x})\,,

(19)

where
f({\bf x})
and
g({\bf x})
are smooth arbitrary functions and

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\dagger}_{t}({\bf x})=\left[\frac{1}{\nu\beta}\nabla^{2}-\frac{1}{\nu}(\nabla V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t}))\cdot\nabla\right]\,.

(20)

The eigenfunctions of
{\cal L}^{\dagger}_{t}({\bf x})
are called the
head
states and defined by

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\dagger}_{t}({\bf x})\overline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=-\overline{\lambda}_{n}(t)\overline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\,.

(21)

As shown soon later, the eigenvalue of the head state
\overline{\lambda}_{n}(t)

is the same as that of the foot state
\underline{\lambda}_{n}(t)
.

Note that both of
{\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})
and
{\cal L}^{\dagger}_{t}({\bf x})
are characterized
by a self-adjoint operator
{\cal H}_{t}({\bf x})
defined by

\displaystyle e^{G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})/2}{\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})e^{-G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})/2}=e^{-G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})/2}{\cal L}^{\dagger}_{t}({\bf x})e^{G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})/2}=-{\cal H}_{t}({\bf x})\,,

(22)

where

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{t}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{\nu\beta}\nabla^{2}-\frac{1}{2\nu}(\nabla^{2}V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t}))+\frac{\beta}{4\nu}(\nabla V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t}))^{2}\right],

(23)

\displaystyle G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\,.

(24)

To give the representations of the foot and head states, we introduce the eigenfunctions of
{\cal H}_{t}({\bf x})
,

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{t}({\bf x})u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=\lambda_{n}(t)u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\,.

(25)

These eigenfunctions form a complete orthogonal set,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})u_{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=\delta_{n,m}\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}},\\
\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf a}_{t})=\delta^{(D)}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime})\,.\end{split}

(26)

where
\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}=\delta_{\alpha_{1n},\beta_{1,n}}\,\delta_{\alpha_{2,n},\beta_{2,n}}\,\cdots
.
Note that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}(t)
are non-negative real numbers as shown later
and thus
u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})
can be chosen to be a real function.

Using these results, we construct the foot and head states.
The set of the foot and head states form a bi-orthogonal system and satisfies
the following orthogonal and completeness conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\ \overline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\underline{\rho}_{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=\delta_{n,m}\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m}}\,,\\
\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}\underline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\overline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf a}_{t})=\delta^{(D)}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime})\,.\end{split}

(27)

Because of these properties, the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation can be expanded using the foot and head states.
Using the eigenfunctions of
{\cal H}_{t}
, the foot and head states are represented by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})&=&\sqrt{{\cal N}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf a}_{t})}e^{-G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})/2}u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\,,\\
\overline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal N}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf a}_{t})}}e^{G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})/2}u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\,.\end{split}

(28)

Here we introduced
the real factor
{\cal N}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf a}_{t})
.
It is because, differently from quantum mechanics, the normalization condition is applied to a pair of foot and head states
and thus, for example, a foot state itself is not necessarily normalized by one.
This factor is not considered in the formulation of Ref.
koide17
.

From Eqs. (
28
), we find that all eigenvalues of

\underline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})
,
\overline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})

and
u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})
are equal,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(t)=\overline{\lambda}_{n}(t)=\underline{\lambda}_{n}(t)\,.

(29)

Moreover, the foot and head states satisfy the relation:

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=\frac{1}{{\cal N}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf a}_{t})}e^{G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}\underline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\,.

(30)

Therefore it is easy to understand that the degeneracy of
\underline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})

is the same as that of
\overline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})
.

For an arbitrary external potential,
we can show the following properties:

1.

The smallest eigenvalue is given by zero. Thus, without loss of generality, we can set the order of the eigenvalues as

0=\lambda_{0}(t)<\lambda_{1}(t)<\lambda_{2}(t)<\cdots\,.

(31)

2.

The “ground” foot and head states
\underline{\rho}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})
and
\overline{\rho}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})
have the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}(t)=0
. These states are not degenerate and given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underline{\rho}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})&=\sqrt{{\cal N}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf a}_{t})}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t})}}e^{-\beta V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}\,,\\
\overline{\rho}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal N}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf a}_{t})}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t})}}\,,\end{split}

(32)

respectively.
Here we introduced

{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t})=\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,e^{-\beta V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}\,.

(33)

The derivations of these properties are the same as those in one dimension
koide17
and
are summarized in Appendix
A
.

When we choose

\displaystyle{\cal N}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf a}_{t})=\frac{1}{{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t})}\,,

(34)

the ground head state becomes trivial

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=1\,.

(35)

This choice is considered in Ref.
koide18
, where
it is shown that the time-evolution of the Fokker-Planck, the Kramers and relativistic Kramers systems can be regarded
as a special case of Schrödinger’s reciprocal process
and then the Jarzynski relation is reproduced from the symmetry of the Fokker-Planck operator.

III.1
Bra-ket representation

For the sake of simplicity, we introduce the quantum-mechanical bra-ket notation.
The foot and heat states are expressed in terms of the inner products of bra-ket vectors,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underline{\rho}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})&=\langle{\bf x}|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,,\\
\overline{\rho}_{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})&=\langle{\bf x}|\overline{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,.\end{split}

(36)

Here we introduce the position basis
|{\bf x}\rangle
which satisfies
(|{\bf x}\rangle)^{\dagger}=\langle{\bf x}|
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,|{\bf x}\rangle\langle{\bf x}|&=1\,,\\
\langle{\bf x}|{\bf x}^{\prime}\rangle&=\delta^{(D)}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime})\,.\end{split}

(37)

Then the conditions for the complete orthogonal set, Eq. (
27
),
are represented by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\overline{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m},{\bf a}_{t}}|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle&=\delta_{m,n}\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}\,,\\
\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\langle\overline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}|&=1\,,\end{split}

(38)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}(|\overline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle)^{\dagger}&=&\langle\overline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}|\,,\\
(|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle)^{\dagger}&=&\langle\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}|\,.\end{split}

(39)

From Eq. (
30
), we find

\displaystyle|\overline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle=\frac{1}{{\cal N}_{n,\alpha_{n}}({\bf a}_{t})}e^{\widehat{G}({\bf a}_{t})}|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,,

(40)

where the operator
\widehat{G}({\bf a}_{t})
is defined by

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}|\widehat{G}({\bf a}_{t})|{\bf x}^{\prime}\rangle=G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\delta^{(D)}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime})\,.

(41)

In this notation, the Fokker-Planck equation is represented by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}|\rho(t)\rangle=\widehat{\cal L}_{t}|\rho(t)\rangle\,,

(42)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle{\bf x}|\widehat{V}_{t}|{\bf x}^{\prime}\rangle&=V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})\delta^{(D)}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime})\,,\\
\langle{\bf x}|\widehat{\cal L}_{t}|{\bf x}^{\prime}\rangle&={\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})\delta^{(D)}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime})\,.\end{split}

(43)

The eigenvalue equations are then expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\cal L}_{t}|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle&=-\lambda_{n}(t)|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,,\\
\widehat{\cal L}^{\dagger}_{t}|\overline{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle&=-\lambda_{m}(t)|\overline{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,.\end{split}

(44)

Here we have used Eq. (
29
).

One may notice that these representations in the bi-orthogonal basis look very similar to
the non-Hermitian generalization of quantum mechanics
(which is, sometimes, called
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics)
bender
;
brody
;
most
.
Indeed, the operator
e^{G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}/{\cal N}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf a}_{t})
in Eq. (
30
)
seems to be a kind of the metric operator. Thus a head state is obtained from a foot state by multiplying it and vice versa.
See also the discussion in Ref.
koide18
.

IV
Perturbative solution of time-dependent Fokker-Planck equation

There are various expansions to express the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation. See Refs.
talkner
;
dreger
;
hesam
;
welles2014
;
wadia
and references therein.
In our method, we expand the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation
in terms of the foot states introduced in the previous section,

\displaystyle|\rho(t)\rangle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}A_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t)e^{-\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,\lambda_{n}(s)}|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,,

(45)

where
t_{i}
is an initial time.
Note that the sums in the above expansion can be time-dependent
because of
{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})
.
In the following calculation, however, we consider the case where
the sums associated with
n
and
\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}
are independent of time,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t^{\prime}})}\,\,\,\,\,\,(t\neq t^{\prime})\,.

(46)

For the sake of simplicity,
we further decompose the coefficient
A_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t)
as

\displaystyle A_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t)=\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}C_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)\,.

(47)

Here the matrix
[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}
is obtained by the solution of the following differential equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}=-\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}\langle\overline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}|\partial_{t}|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}\,,

(48)

with the initial condition

\displaystyle[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t_{i})]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}=\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}\,.

(49)

Substituting this expansion into the Fokker-Planck equation,
we find that the expansion coefficients are given by the solutions of the following differential equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}C_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t)+\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}\sum_{m\neq n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{m},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\delta$}}_{m}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(m)}({\bf a}_{t})}e^{\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,(\lambda_{n}(s)-\lambda_{m}(s))}

\displaystyle\times\frac{[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]^{-1}_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(n,m)}(t)\right]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{m}}[{\bf M}^{(m)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{m},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\delta$}}_{m}}}{\lambda_{n}(t)-\lambda_{m}(t)}C_{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\delta$}}_{m}}(t)=0\,,

(50)

where

\displaystyle\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(n,m)}(t)\right]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m}}=\langle\overline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}|(\partial_{t}\widehat{\cal L}_{t})|\underline{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,,

(51)

and the inverse matrix is defined by

\displaystyle\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]^{-1}_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{n}}[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}=\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}\,.

(52)

It should be noted that the coefficient
C_{0,{\bf 0}}(t)
is time-independent,

\displaystyle C_{0,{\bf 0}}(t)=C_{0,{\bf 0}}(t_{i})\,,

(53)

because

\displaystyle\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(0,m)}(t)\right]_{{\bf 0},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m}}\propto\int\mathrm{d}^{D}x\,\nabla\cdot\left\{\underline{\rho}_{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{m}}({\bf x},t)\nabla(\partial_{t}V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t}))\right\}=0\,.

(54)

IV.1
Evolution from equilibrium state

In the following, we focus on the time evolution from a thermal equilibrium state with the control parameters
{\bf a}_{t_{i}}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{eq}({\bf x},t_{i})=\frac{1}{{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t_{i}})}e^{-\beta V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t_{i}})}\,,

(55)

where
{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t_{i}})
is defined by Eq. (
33
).
The expansion of this state in terms of the foot state leads to

\displaystyle\rho_{eq}({\bf x},t_{i})=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}C_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t_{i})\langle{\bf x}|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha_{n}$}},{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}\rangle\,,

(56)

where

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C_{0,{\bf 0}}(t_{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal N}_{0,{\bf 0}}(t_{i})}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t_{i}})}}=\langle\overline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}|{\bf x}\rangle&\\
C_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t_{i})=0&(n>0)\end{array}\right.\,.

For this initial condition, Eq. (
50
) is further simplified.
Let us reexpress the expansion coefficient
C_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t)
as

\displaystyle C_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t)=\langle\overline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}|{\bf x}\rangle D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha_{n}$}}}(t)\,.

(60)

Then, by solving Eq. (
50
) formally, the expansion coefficient
D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha_{n}$}}}(t)

is determined by solving the following equation iteratively:

\displaystyle D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t_{i})-\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{s})}\sum_{m\neq n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{m},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\delta$}}_{m}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(m)}({\bf a}_{s})}e^{\int^{s}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,(\lambda_{n}(s^{\prime})-\lambda_{m}(s^{\prime}))}

(61)

\displaystyle\times\frac{[{\bf M}^{(n)}(s)]^{-1}_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(n,m)}(s)\right]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{m}}[{\bf M}^{(m)}(s)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{m},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\delta$}}_{m}}}{\lambda_{n}(s)-\lambda_{m}(s)}D_{m,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\delta$}}_{m}}(s)\,,

with the initial conditions

\displaystyle D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}(t_{i})=\delta_{n,0}\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\,{\bf 0}}\,.

(62)

Again, note that
D_{0,{\bf 0}}(t)
is independent of time and thus

\displaystyle D_{0,{\bf 0}}(t)=D_{0,{\bf 0}}(t_{i})\,,

(63)

because of Eq. (
53
).
In the following calculations, we use exclusively this expansion coefficient
D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha_{n}$}}}(t)
.

IV.2
Pseudo density matrix

Let us introduce the pseudo density matrix defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}(t)}e^{-\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,\lambda_{n}(s)}|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)\langle\overline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}|\,.

(64)

This satisfies the following properties:

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\widehat{\rho}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\,,

(65)

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}^{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}(t)\,.

(66)

To show the second equation, we have used

\displaystyle\langle\overline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}|=[{\bf M}^{(0)}(t)]^{-1}_{{\bf 0},{\bf 0}}\langle\overline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t}}|\,.

(67)

The expectation values are represented
by the trace with the pseudo density matrix.
For example, the mean work induced by the deformation of the external potential is given by

\displaystyle W=\int\mathrm{d}t\,{\rm Tr}[(\partial_{t}\widehat{V}_{t})\widehat{\rho}(t)]\,.

(68)

V
Formula for mean work

Let us consider the time evolution from a thermal equilibrium state which is induced
by changing the control parameters
{\bf a}_{t}
in a finite time interval
t_{i}\leq t\leq t_{f}
where
t_{f}
is a finial time.
The mean work induced by the deformation of the potential is given by
the time integral of Eq. (
13
). Using Eq. (
64
), we find

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}W

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}t\,\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}={\bf 0}}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}e^{-\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}\,\lambda_{n}(s)}[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)\langle\overline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}|(\partial_{t}\widehat{V}_{t})|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,.

This formula is the generalization of Eq. (38) of Ref.
koide17
to the systems in higher spatial dimensions.
Indeed, this is reduced to Eq. (38) when we set

[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}=e^{-\theta_{n}(t)}\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf 0}}\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n},{\bf 0}}
.
The role of
\theta_{n}(t)
is discussed in Sec.
V.3
.

To expand the right-hand side in terms of the time derivative of the control parameters,
\dot{\bf a}_{t}
,
we introduce the expansions of the mean work
W
and the coefficients

D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)
,

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W^{(1)}+W^{(2)}+\cdots\,,

(70)

\displaystyle D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{(0)}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)+D^{(1)}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)+\cdots\,,

(71)

where
W^{(m)}
and
D^{(m)}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)
indicate the
m
-th order terms of
\dot{\bf a}_{t}
.
From the initial condition (
62
), we find

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}D^{(0)}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)=\delta_{n,0}\delta_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n},{\bf 0}}&\\
D^{(m)}_{0,{\bf 0}}(t)=0&(m\geq 1)\end{array}\,.\right.

V.1
Lowest order contribution
W^{(1)}

The lowest order contribution in the mean work is given by
W^{(1)}
,

\displaystyle W^{(1)}=\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}t\,[{\bf M}^{(0)}(t)]_{{\bf 0},{\bf 0}}\langle\overline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}|(\partial_{t}\widehat{V}_{t})|\underline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,.

(75)

This quantity is calculated exactly without referring to the detailed behavior of the control parameters
{\bf a}_{t}
, and
given by the difference of the free energies,

\displaystyle W^{(1)}=F({\bf a}_{t_{f}})-F({\bf a}_{t_{i}})\,,

(76)

where

\displaystyle F({\bf a}_{t})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left(\int\mathrm{d}^{D}x\,e^{-\beta V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}\right)\,.

(77)

It should be emphasized that this free energy is not the Helmholtz free energy defined by Eq. (
17
).
We see that
W^{(1)}
is determined only by the initial and final values of
{\bf a}_{t}

and independent of the intermediate deformation processes of
V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})
.

By definition, the initial state is given by a thermal equilibrium state and thus this free energy agrees with the
Helmholtz free energy (
17
) at the initial time
t_{i}
,

F({\bf a}_{t_{i}})=F_{helm}({\bf a}_{t_{i}})
.
In general,
F({\bf a}_{t})
for
t>t_{i}
does not necessarily agree with
F_{helm}({\bf a}_{t})
.
However, in the quasi-static process, the final state is approximately given by a thermal equilibrium state and then
the right-hand side of Eq. (
76
) is identified with the change of the Helmholtz free energy.
This is the well-known behavior in the reversible process and thus
W^{(1)}
is identified with the reversible work in this limit.

V.2
Higher order contributions

All higher order terms in the mean work
W^{(n)}
(
n\geq 2
) give irreversible contributions.
The term of
n=2
,
W^{(2)}
, is obtained
by using
D^{(1)}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)
,

\displaystyle W^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}t\,\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}=0}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{t})}e^{-\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,\lambda_{n}(s)}[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}D^{(1)}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)\langle\overline{0,0,{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}|(\partial_{t}\widehat{V}_{t})|\underline{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,,

(78)

where

\displaystyle D^{(1)}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}(t)=-(1-\delta_{n,0})\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,\sum_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{n}=0}^{{\bf d}^{(n)}({\bf a}_{s})}e^{\int^{s}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\lambda_{n}(s^{\prime})}\frac{[{\bf M}^{(n)}(s)]^{-1}_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{n}}\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(n,0)}(s)\right]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{n},{\bf 0}}[{\bf M}^{(0)}(s)]}{\lambda_{n}(s)}\,.

(79)

The factor
(1-\delta_{n,0})
is due to the property (
62
).
When
\widehat{V}_{t}
is given by the harmonic potential,
higher order terms
W^{(n)}

(n\geq 3)
vanish and the exact mean work is given by

\displaystyle W=W^{(1)}+W^{(2)}\,.

(80)

This is discussed in detail soon later.

V.3
Relation to Berry’s phase

In quantum mechanics, it is known that the wave function acquires a phase induced by a cyclic adiabatic motion of a system.
This additional phase is affected by the geometrical properties of the parameter of the Hamiltonian
and called Berry’s geometric phase.

In the quasi-static limit, the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation is obtained by substituting
D_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha_{n}$}}}(t)=D^{(1)}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha_{n}$}}}(t)
,

\displaystyle|\rho(t)\rangle=\langle\overline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}|{\bf x}\rangle e^{-\theta_{0}(t)}|\underline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,,

(81)

where

\displaystyle\theta_{0}(t)=-\ln[{\bf M}^{(0)}(t)]_{{\bf 0},{\bf 0}}=\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\langle\overline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{s}}|\partial_{s}|\underline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{s}}\rangle\,.

(82)

We observe that a non-trivial time-dependent factor
e^{-\theta_{0}(t)}
appears besides the time evolution of
|\underline{0,{\bf 0},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle

and this factor corresponds to Berry’s geometric phase.
See also the discussion in Ref.
koide17
.

Wilczek and Zee found that Berry’s phase becomes a matrix in the system with degeneracy
wilczek-zee
.
Indeed,
[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{n}}
is generally given by a matrix for
n\neq 0
.
In the present calculation, the ground foot state is always not degenerate.
Therefore, to observe the Wilczek-Zee-type phase, we should consider the time evolutions in the “excited” state
of the Fokker-Planck operator.

VI
Application (I) : symmetric process

To see the applications of the derived formula,
we consider the two-dimensional harmonic potential,

\displaystyle V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=\frac{1}{2}a^{(1)}_{t}x^{2}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}a^{(2)}_{t}x^{2}_{2}\,.

(83)

The Fokker-Planck operator with this potential is expressed as

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{t}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e^{-G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}\left\{{\cal H}^{(1)}_{t}(x_{1})+{\cal H}^{(2)}_{t}(x_{2})\right\}e^{G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}\,,

(84)

where

\displaystyle{\cal H}^{(i)}_{t}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\nu\beta}\partial^{2}_{i}-\frac{a^{(i)}_{t}}{2\nu}+\frac{\beta}{4\nu}(a^{(i)}_{t}x_{i})^{2}\,,

(85)

\displaystyle G({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{\beta}{2}a^{(i)}_{t}x^{2}_{i}\,.

(86)

To construct the foot and head states, we introduce the eigenfunctions of
{\cal H}^{(i)}_{t}(x_{i})
which are given by

\displaystyle u_{n}(x_{i},a^{(i)}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z(a^{(i)}_{t})}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}}e^{-\beta a^{(i)}_{t}x^{2}_{i}/4}H_{n}(\sqrt{\beta a^{(i)}_{t}/2}x_{i})\,,

(87)

where
H_{n}(x)
denote the Hermite polynomials and

\displaystyle Z(a)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\beta a}}\,.

(88)

In the two-dimensional system, the degeneracy related to the rotational symmetry of the potential is characterized by a single integer.
Let us consider the deformation of the harmonic potential with
a^{(1)}_{t}=a^{(2)}_{t}=a_{t}
.
Then the potential has the rotational symmetry around
(x_{1},x_{2})=(0,0)
in the two-dimensional plane during the deformation process.
Then
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}(a_{t})
are given by time-independent constants,
\mathrm{d}^{(n)}
, and thus
the condition (
46
) is satisfied.
The foot and head states are defined by

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}|\underline{n,\alpha_{n},{\bf a}_{t}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{\rho}_{\alpha_{n}}(x_{1},a_{t})\underline{\rho}_{n-\alpha_{n}}(x_{2},a_{t})\,,

(89)

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}|\overline{n,\alpha_{n},{\bf a}_{t}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{\alpha_{n}}(x_{1},a_{t})\overline{\rho}_{n-\alpha_{n}}(x_{2},a_{t})\,,

(90)

where
\alpha_{n}
take non-negative integers satisfying
0\leq\alpha_{n}\leq\mathrm{d}^{(n)}=n
, and

\displaystyle\underline{\rho}_{n}(x,a_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{N_{n}(a_{t})}e^{-\beta a_{t}x^{2}/4}u_{n}(x,a_{t})\,,

(91)

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{n}(x,a_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{n}(a_{t})}}e^{\beta a_{t}x^{2}/2}u_{n}(x,a_{t})\,.

(92)

Here
N_{n}(a_{t})
is an arbitrary real function associated with the ambiguity of the normalization of the foot states discussed below Eq. (
28
).
The eigenvalues of
|\underline{n,\alpha_{n},{\bf a}_{t}\rangle}
(and equivalently of
|\overline{n,\alpha_{n},{\bf a}_{t}\rangle}
) are given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(t)=\frac{a_{t}}{\nu}n\,\,\,\,\,\,(n\geq 0)\,.

(93)

The lowest order contribution in the mean work
W^{(1)}
is already calculated.
The next order contribution is given by Eq. (
78
), which is simplified as

\displaystyle W^{(2)}_{sym}=\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}t\,\sum_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}=0}^{\mathrm{d}^{(2)}}e^{-\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}\,\lambda_{2}(s)}[{\bf M}^{(2)}(t)]_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}}D^{(1)}_{2,\beta_{2}}(t)\langle\overline{0,0,{\bf a}_{t_{i}}}|(\partial_{t}\widehat{V}_{t})|\underline{2,\alpha_{2},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle\,,

(94)

because, in the harmonic potential, we find

\displaystyle\langle\overline{0,0,{\bf a}_{\tau}}|(\partial_{t}\widehat{V})|\underline{n,\alpha_{n},{\bf a}_{t}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sqrt{\frac{N^{2}_{0}(a_{t})}{N^{2}_{0}(a_{\tau})}}\left(\frac{a_{\tau}}{a_{t}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{\beta a_{t}}\left[\delta_{n,0}e^{-\mathsf{i}(\delta_{n+2}(a_{t})-\delta_{n}(a_{t}))}+\sqrt{\frac{N_{2}(a_{t})}{N_{0}(a_{t})}}e^{-\mathsf{i}(\delta_{n-2}(a_{t})-\delta_{n}(a_{t}))}\sqrt{2}\delta_{n,2}\right]\left[\delta_{0,\alpha_{n}}+\delta_{n,\alpha_{n}}\right]\,.

The expansion coefficient
D^{(1)}_{2,\beta_{2}}(t)
is given by Eq. (
79
).
Substituting it into Eq. (
94
), we obtain

\displaystyle W^{(2)}_{sym}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}t\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left[\frac{1}{2\beta a^{2}_{s}}e^{-\int^{t}_{s}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\frac{2a_{s^{\prime}}}{\nu}}\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,,

(96)

where we have used Eq. (
67
) and
the following results:

\displaystyle[{\bf M}^{(n)}(t)]_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{N_{\alpha_{n}}(a_{t_{i}})N_{n-\alpha_{n}}(a_{t_{i}})}{N_{\alpha_{n}}(a_{t})N_{n-\alpha_{n}}(a_{t})}}\left(\frac{a_{t}}{a_{t_{i}}}\right)^{(n+1)/2}\,,

\displaystyle\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(n,m)}(\tau)\right]_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\nu}\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{\tau}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left[m\delta_{n,m}\delta_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{N_{\beta_{m}}(a_{\tau})}{N_{\beta_{m}+2}(a_{\tau})}}\sqrt{(\beta_{m}+2)(\beta_{m}+1)}\delta_{n,m+2}\delta_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{m}+2}\right.

(98)

\displaystyle\left.+\sqrt{\frac{N_{m-\beta_{m}}(a_{\tau})}{N_{m-\beta_{m}+2}(a_{\tau})}}\sqrt{(m-\beta_{m}+2)(m-\beta_{m}+1)}\delta_{n,m+2}\delta_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{m}}\right]\,.

In the harmonic potential,
the exact mean work is given by

\displaystyle W_{sym}=W^{(1)}+W^{(2)}_{sym}\,.

(99)

To understand this, note that, as seen from Eqs. (
61
) and (
V
),
the expansion coefficient
D^{(n)}_{2,\beta_{2}}(t)
has the following product:

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}\neq 2}\sum_{m_{2}\neq m_{1}}\cdots\sum_{m_{n-1}\neq 0}[{\bf M}^{(2)}(\tau_{1})]^{-1}\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(2,m_{1})}(\tau_{1})\right][{\bf M}^{(m_{1})}(\tau_{1})]

\displaystyle\times[{\bf M}^{(m_{1})}(\tau_{2})]^{-1}\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(m_{1},m_{2})}(\tau_{2})\right][{\bf M}^{(m_{2})}(\tau_{2})]\cdots

\displaystyle\times[{\bf M}^{(m_{n-1})}(\tau_{n})]^{-1}\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(m_{n-1},0)}(\tau_{n})\right][{\bf M}^{(0)}(\tau_{n})]\,.

(100)

From Eq. (
98
), it is easy to see that

\left[\dot{\bf L}^{(n,m)}(\tau)\right]
has a finite contribution only when

n=m+2
and
n=m
, but the latter is excluded in the sums.
Therefore all higher order terms
D^{(n)}_{2,\beta_{2}}(t)
(
n\geq 2
) disappear.

The mean work in the one-dimensional harmonic potential is obtained in Ref.
koide17
.
Comparing the one-dimensional and two-dimensional results,
we find that
W^{(2)}_{sym}
in the two-dimensional calculation is twice as large as the one-dimensional result given by Eq. (50) in Ref.
koide17
.
The same property is found in the three-dimensional symmetric deformation of the harmonic potential.
Therefore the D-dimensional result is comprehensively summarized by

\displaystyle W^{(2)}_{sym}=D\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}t\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left[\frac{1}{2\beta a^{2}_{s}}e^{-\int^{t}_{s}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\frac{2a_{s^{\prime}}}{\nu}}\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,,

(101)

where
D
is the number of the spatial dimension and can take
D=1,2
or
3
.
This simple
D
dependence is understood by the fact that
the harmonic potential is given by the independent sum of the each spatial component
and thus the Fokker-Planck operator is represented by the independent sum of the spatial components,

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\left[\frac{1}{\nu\beta}\partial^{2}_{i}-\partial_{i}\frac{a_{t}x_{i}}{\nu}\right]\,.

(102)

Therefore
the optimization with respect to the
i
-th spatial component does not affect that to the
j
-th spatial components when
i\neq j
,
and the mean work is given by a linear function of
D
in the present symmetric process.

VI.1
Energy and Thermodynamic Relation

Following the thermodynamic interpretation introduced in Sec.
II
,
the mean energy of this system is defined by Eq. (
12
),

\displaystyle E(t)={\rm Tr}[\widehat{V}_{t}\widehat{\rho}(t)]\,.

(103)

Applying the same procedure used in the calculation of the mean work,
the exact mean energy is calculated by using
D^{(1)}_{2,\beta_{2}}
in the pseudo density matrix.
The result is given by

\displaystyle E_{sym}(t)=\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{a_{t}}{\beta}\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{-\int^{t}_{s}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\frac{2a_{s^{\prime}}}{\nu}}\frac{1}{a^{2}_{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\,.

(104)

Because
E_{sym}(t_{i})=1/\beta
,
the change of the mean energy induced in the symmetric process is given by the second term of the above equation,

\displaystyle\Delta E_{sym}=E_{sym}(t_{f})-E_{sym}(t_{i})=\frac{a_{t_{f}}}{\beta}\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{-\int^{t_{f}}_{s}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\frac{2a_{s^{\prime}}}{\nu}}\frac{1}{a^{2}_{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\,.

(105)

VI.2
Optimization in symmetric process

We are in particular interested in the optimized process which minimizes the mean work.
Because
W^{(1)}
is determined only by
{\bf a}_{t_{i}}
and
{\bf a}_{t_{f}}
independently of processes,
it is sufficient to consider the variation of
W^{(2)}_{sym}
by fixing the operation time
\tau_{op}=t_{f}-t_{i}
.
The variation of the control parameter is defined by

\displaystyle a_{t}\longrightarrow a_{t}+\delta a_{t}\,.

(106)

We fix the initial and final forms of the harmonic potential and hence this variation satisfies the boundary conditions,

\displaystyle\delta a_{t_{i}}=\delta a_{t_{f}}=0\,.

(107)

The variation of
W^{(2)}_{sym}
leads to

\displaystyle\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{1}{a^{2}_{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\partial_{t}e^{-\frac{2}{\nu}\int^{t}_{s}\mathrm{d}\tau\,a_{\tau}}+\frac{1}{a^{2}_{t}}\int^{t_{f}}_{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\partial_{t}e^{-\frac{2}{\nu}\int^{s}_{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,a_{\tau}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\nu}\int^{t_{f}}_{t}\mathrm{d}s_{2}\int^{t}_{t_{i}}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\,e^{-\frac{2}{\nu}\int^{s_{2}}_{s_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau\,a_{\tau}}\frac{1}{a^{2}_{s_{1}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{s_{1}}}{\mathrm{d}s_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{s_{2}}}{\mathrm{d}s_{2}}=0\,.

(108)

Because of the reason discussed below Eq. (
101
),
this integro-differential equation is the same as Eq. (59) of Ref.
koide17
which determines the optimized process in the one-dimensional harmonic potential.
See Appendix B in Ref.
koide17
for the detailed derivation.

The optimized parameter is determined by solving this equation, but, so far,
the exact solution is not known.
Therefore we consider an approximated process in the large
\tau_{op}
limit as is discussed in Ref.
koide17
.
For this purpose, we reexpress
W^{(2)}_{sym}
in the adimensional form,

\displaystyle W^{(2)}_{sym}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int^{\tau_{1}}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{a}_{\tau_{2}}}{\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}}\left[e^{-\frac{2\tau_{op}a_{t_{i}}}{\nu}\int^{\tau_{1}}_{\tau_{2}}d\tau_{3}\,\bar{a}_{\tau_{3}}}\frac{1}{2\beta(\bar{a}_{\tau_{2}})^{2}}\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{a}_{\tau_{1}}}{\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}}\,,

(109)

where adimensional quantities are introduced by

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(t-t_{i})/\tau_{op}\,,

(110)

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{t_{i}+\tau\tau_{op}}}{a_{t_{i}}}\,.

(111)

In the large
\tau_{op}
limit, we consider the following approximation:

\displaystyle\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int^{\tau_{1}}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\,G(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})e^{-\frac{2\tau_{op}a_{t_{i}}}{\nu}\int^{\tau_{1}}_{\tau_{2}}d\tau_{3}\,\bar{a}_{\tau_{3}}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int^{\tau_{1}}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\,G(\tau_{1},\tau_{1})e^{-\frac{2\tau_{op}a_{t_{i}}}{\nu}\bar{a}_{\tau_{1}}(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})}

(112)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\,G(\tau_{1},\tau_{1})\frac{\nu}{2\tau_{op}a_{i}\bar{a}_{\tau_{1}}}\,,

where
G(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
is a smooth function.
Using this approximation,
W^{(2)}_{sym}
is calculated as

\displaystyle W^{(2)}_{sym}=\frac{\nu}{2\beta a_{t_{i}}\tau_{op}}\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{1}{(\bar{a}_{\tau})^{3}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{a}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right)^{2}\,.

(113)

We minimize this approximated mean work.
Applying the variation defined above to this, we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{a}^{3}_{\tau}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\bar{a}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}}-\frac{3}{2}\frac{1}{\bar{a}^{4}_{\tau}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{a}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right)^{2}=0\,.

(114)

This equation is analytically solvable and the optimized external parameter in the large
\tau_{op}
limit is given by

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{\tau}=\frac{\bar{a}_{1}}{\left[\sqrt{\bar{a}_{1}}-(\sqrt{\bar{a}_{1}}-1)\tau\right]^{2}}\longrightarrow a_{t}=\frac{\tau_{op}^{2}\,a_{t_{i}}a_{t_{f}}}{\left[(t-t_{i})\sqrt{a_{t_{i}}}+(t_{f}-t)\sqrt{a_{t_{f}}}\right]^{2}}\,.

(115)

This is the same as those found in one-dimensional harmonic oscillator
sekimoto-sasa
;
schmiedl-seifert2007
;
koide17
.

Substituting this into Eq. (
113
),
the optimized mean work in the large
\tau_{op}
limit is expressed as

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau_{op}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{W_{sym}}{W^{(1)}}=\lim_{\tau_{op}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{W^{(1)}+W^{(2)}_{sym}}{W^{(1)}}=1+\frac{2}{\bar{a}_{1}\ln\bar{a}_{1}}\left(\sqrt{\bar{a}_{1}}-1\right)^{2}\frac{\nu}{a_{t_{i}}\tau_{op}}\,,

(116)

where, from Eq. (
76
),

\displaystyle W^{(1)}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\frac{{\cal Z}(a_{t_{f}})}{{\cal Z}(a_{t_{i}})}=\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\bar{a}_{1}\,.

(117)

The time evolution in the large
\tau_{op}
limit is very slow
and thus
\rho({\bf x},t_{f})
is approximately given by the thermal equilibrium state with the parameter
a_{t_{f}}
,
\rho_{eq}({\bf x},t_{f})
.
Then
W^{(1)}
in Eq. (
76
) corresponds to the one in the reversible process
and is represented by the change of the Helmholtz free energy.
In this limit, one can see that the irreversible contribution of the mean work asymptotically disappears
as a function of
\tau^{-1}_{op}
in Eq. (
116
).
It is worth emphasizing that this
\tau_{op}
dependence is experimentally verified
blickle2
.

In a similar fashion, the change of the mean energy (
105
) is calculated with this approximated optimized process (
115
).
In the large
\tau_{op}
limit, this quantity is simplified as

\displaystyle\Delta E_{sym}\approx\frac{\nu}{2\tau_{op}a_{t_{i}}\beta}\frac{1}{\bar{a}^{2}_{1}}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{a}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right|_{\tau=1}\,.

(118)

Substituting Eq. (
115
) into this, the change of the mean energy is calculated as

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau_{op}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Delta E_{sym}}{E(t_{i})}=\frac{1}{\bar{a_{1}}}\left(\sqrt{\bar{a}_{1}}-1\right)\frac{\nu}{a_{t_{i}}\tau_{op}}\,.

(119)

This equation implies that there is no change of the mean energy in the quasi-static process.
This is reminiscent of the quasi-static isothermal process in the ideal gas.

Later, we will discuss the asymmetric deformation of the harmonic potential
and compare the results with those in the symmetric process.
In the asymmetric process,
we choose the initial and final parameters of the harmonic potential as
(a^{(1)}_{t_{i}},a^{(2)}_{t_{i}})=(a_{0},a_{0})
and
(a^{(1)}_{t_{f}},a^{(2)}_{t_{f}})=(2a_{0},2a_{0})
, respectively.
The corresponding symmetric process is realized by choosing

a_{t_{i}}=a_{0}
and
a_{t_{f}}=2a_{0}
.
Then, from Eqs. (
116
) and (
119
),
the mean work and the change of the mean energy in the large
\tau_{op}
limit are given by

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau_{op}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{W_{sym}}{W^{(1)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\left(\sqrt{2}-1\right)^{2}}{\ln 2}\frac{\tau^{*}}{\tau_{op}}\,,

(120)

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau_{op}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Delta E_{sym}}{E(t_{i})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\sqrt{2}-1\right)}{2}\frac{\tau^{*}}{\tau_{op}}\,,

(121)

respectively. Here we introduced
\tau^{*}=\nu/a_{0}
.

IX
concluding remarks

In this paper, we developed the systematic expansion of the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation with degeneracy by generalizing the formulation developed in Ref.
koide17
.
The Fokker-Planck equation describes the thermal relaxation of a Brownian particle confined in an external potential,
which is deformed by changing control parameters.
The time derivative of these parameters are utilized as the expansion parameter of the perturbation theory.
Differently from the theory in Ref.
koide17
, the present theory is applicable to systems in arbitrary spatial dimensions and
we obtained a new formula to calculate the mean work perturbatively which is applicable to the systems with degeneracy in the eigenvalues of the Fokker-Planck operator.
This formula enables us to study how the geometrical symmetry of the external potential affects thermodynamic description of Brownian motion.

The application of the derived formula depends on the degeneracy of systems.
To illustrate this, we considered the thermodynamic description of a Brownian particle confined in the two-dimensional harmonic potential.
By changing the control parameters, the harmonic potential is monotonically compressed.
Fixing the initial and final forms of the potential, we considered the symmetric and asymmetric processes.
The rotational symmetry of the harmonic potential in the two-dimensional plan is held in the former, but it is broken in the latter.
Perturbative calculations are affected by these deformation processes because
the degeneracy of the expansion basis (foot and head states) depends on the symmetry of the potential.

The optimized deformation processes of the harmonic potential are obtained by minimizing the mean works.
The exact solutions have, however, not yet been known (see the discussion in Ref.
koide17
) and thus
we considered the optimization in the limit of the large operation time
\tau_{op}=t_{f}-t_{i}
.
Using these approximated optimized processes,
we found that the mean works and the changes of the mean energy in the asymmetric process are always larger than those in the symmetric process for any
\tau_{op}
, because
the time scales of the deformation in the asymmetric process is shorter than that in the symmetric process.

When the operation time
\tau_{op}
is very small, there is no enough time for the initial state to evolve.
Moreover the processes in the large
\tau_{op}
limit converge to the quasi-static process.
Therefore the symmetric and asymmetric processes show the same behaviors in these limits.
To see the difference between the symmetric and asymmetric processes, we have to choose
\tau_{op}
appropriately.
We calculated the difference of the mean works in the optimized symmetric and asymmetric processes as a function of
\tau_{op}

and found that the difference is maximized when
\tau_{op}\sim\tau^{*}=\nu/a_{0}

where
\nu
is the coefficient of friction in the Fokker-Planck equation
and
a_{0}
denotes the initial value of the spring constant of the harmonic potential.

The similar difference can be calculated for the changes of the mean energy.
We then found that the difference between the symmetric and asymmetric processes
is maximized when
\tau_{op}
is close to
\tau^{*}
but is smaller than the corresponding value in the mean works.
This deviation may be related to a hysteresis effect:
the change of the mean energy is obtained directly from the initial and final states
while the mean work depends on the whole time evolution of the pseudo density matrix.
It is thus reasonable to consider that the maximum in the mean work appears in retard compared to that in the change of the mean energy.

In this theory, the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation is expanded with the foot and head states which are the eigenfunctions
of the time-dependent Fokker-Planck operator.
These states form the bi-orthogonal system and thus the foot or head state itself is not necessarily normalized by one.
As a consequence, there is an ambiguity to multiply a real factor to the foot and head states.
See the factor
{\cal N}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf a}_{t})
in Eq. (
28
).
Our perturbation theory should be invariant for this ambiguity.
Indeed, the mean works and the changes of the mean energy in the harmonic potential do not depend on this additional real factor
{\cal N}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf a}_{t})
.
This invariance is however not yet shown for general external potential.

To apply the present formulation to non-linear potentials,
we have to develop another perturbation theory to express the foot and head states
in terms of those of an analytically solvable potential like the harmonic potential.
This perturbation has not yet been studied.
The Floquet theory is known to be useful to solve periodic linear differential equations and
provides a convenient method in quantum mechanics

shirley
.
This approach is applied to the Fokker-Planck equation
caceres
but
the corresponding optimization problems has not yet been investigated.
In the present paper, we assumed that the changes of the control parameters are given by smooth deterministic functions, but these changes
can be stochastic in a microscopic time scale. For the optimization of stochastic control parameters,
the minimization of the mean work will be replaced with the stochastic calculus of variation
svm_review1
;
svm_review2
.
The applications to relativistic
kk-rel
;
koide18
;
pal
;
parag
and quantum
qse-koide
systems have not yet been investigated sufficiently.
These generalizations are left as future tasks.

Then the eigenvalues are shown to be non-negative,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(t)=\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,u^{*}_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},t){\cal H}_{t}u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},t)=\frac{1}{\nu\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{D}|B_{i}u_{n,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}}({\bf x},t)|^{2}\geq 0\,.

(175)

Thus, without loss of generality, the eigenvalues are ordered as

\displaystyle 0=\lambda_{0}(t)<\lambda_{1}(t)<\lambda_{2}(t)\cdots\,.

(176)

The foot state with
\lambda_{0}=0
is given by solving
B_{i}u_{0,\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{0}}=0

and we find

\displaystyle\underline{\rho}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=\sqrt{{\cal N}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf a}_{t})}e\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t})}}e^{-\beta V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}\,,

(177)

where

\displaystyle{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t})=\int\mathrm{d}^{D}{\bf x}\,e^{-\beta V({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})}\,.

(178)

One can see that this is the stationary solution of the Fokker-Planck equation when
V
is not time-dependent explicitly.
There is no degeneracy in the stationary solution and thus we set
\mbox{\small{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{0}={\bf 0}
.
The corresponding head state is easily found by using Eq. (
30
),

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf x},{\bf a}_{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal N}_{0,{\bf 0}}({\bf a}_{t})}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal Z}({\bf a}_{t})}}\,.

(179)

It should be noted that
B_{i}
and
B^{\dagger}_{i}
are not the lowering and raising operators in general because
the right-hand side of Eq. (
172
) is not necessarily constant.
