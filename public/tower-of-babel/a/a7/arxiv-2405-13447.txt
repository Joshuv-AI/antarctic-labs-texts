Relaxations for binary polynomial optimization via signed certificates
Liding Xu, Leo Liberti

Abstract
We consider the problem of minimizing a polynomial f f over the binary hypercube. We show that, for a specific set of polynomials, their binary non-negativity can be checked in a polynomial time via minimum cut algorithms, and we construct a linear programming representation for this set through the min-cut-max-flow duality. We categorize binary polynomials based on their signed support patterns and develop parameterized linear programming representations of binary non-negative polynomials. This allows for constructing binary non-negative signed certificates with adjustable signed support patterns and representation complexities, and we propose a method for minimizing f f by decomposing it into signed certificates. This method yields new hierarchies of linear programming relaxations for binary polynomial optimization. Moreover, since our decomposition only depends on the support of f f , the new hierarchies are sparsity-preserving.



1
Introduction.

Given an
n
-variate polynomial
f
, the non-negativity problem over a set
S
asks whether
f
is non-negative over
S
. We let
S
be the binary hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
. We call the polynomials that are non-negative over
S

binary non-negative polynomials
, and the associated decision problem the
binary non-negativity problem
(BNP).

The
binary polynomial optimization
(BPO) problem
\min\limits_{{\bm{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}f({\bm{x}})
has deep connections with combinatorial optimization (see
Chlamtac and Tulsiani 2012
;
De Loera et al. 2013
;
Lasserre 2002b
). One can understand the BNP and the BPO through a dual perspective. First, the BPO is equivalent to its dual problem, which seeks the maximum lower bound
\lambda
such that the polynomial
f-\lambda
is binary non-negative. Since the binary non-negative polynomials form a convex cone (binary non-negative cone), the dual problem is a conic optimization problem:
\max\limits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\{\lambda:f-\lambda\textup{ is binary non-negative}\}
. Therefore, the BNP is the feasibility problem of the dual formulation of the BPO. We remark that linear optimization over the binary non-negative cone is generally intractable, because it would be equivalent to solving the BPO that contains many
\mathbf{NP}
-hard subclasses.

Given the aforementioned perspective, a common approach is to address the BPO approximately. If there exists a conic inner approximation of the binary non-negative cone and an efficient algorithm to optimize over it, then the optimal value derived from the linear optimization problem over the inner approximation is a lower bound to the optimal value of the BPO. The members of this inner approximation are usually called binary non-negativity certificates, since the membership of
f
(or
f-\lambda
) in these certificates leads to a YES answer to the BNP.

The Lasserre and
Sherali-Adams hierarchies are well-known successive convex relaxations of BPO based on binary non-negativity certificates (see
Lasserre 2002a
;
Sherali and Adams 1990
). The Lasserre hierarchy uses the sum of squares (SoS) polynomials as binary non-negativity certificates, and the relaxations can be solved via semidefinite programming (SDP) (see
Lasserre 2002a
;
Lasserre 2002c
;
Parrilo 2003
;
Putinar 1993
). The Sherali-Adams hierarchy uses the sum of factor product (SoFP) polynomials as binary non-negativity certificates, and the relaxations can be solved via linear programming (LP) (see
Krivine 1964
;
Lasserre 2002c
;
Sherali and Tuncbilek 1992
;
Sherali and Tuncbilek 1997
).

The Lasserre hierarchy yields a better lower bound for BPO than the Sherali-Adams hierarchy at each level of relaxations (see
Laurent 2003
). However, the size of the first level Lasserre relaxation increases combinatorially with the dimension
n
and the degree
d
: the size of the matrices in the SDP is
\mathcal{O}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{2}{n}{d}
. The current state of SDP solvers greatly limits the scalability of the Lasserre hierarchy. Various methods, which can exploit sparsity (
Waki et al. 2006
;
Wang et al. 2021a
;
Wang et al. 2021b
) and symmetry (
Gatermann and Parrilo 2004
) of SoS polynomials, have been proposed to reduce the SDP size. However, these methods still depend on the degree.

To overcome the issue with the Lasserre hierarchy, some approaches develop sparse non-negativity certificates for polynomial optimization. One example is the sum of the non-negative circuit (SoNC) polynomials, see
Dressler et al. 2017
. A circuit polynomial is of the form
\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in A}g_{{\bm{\alpha}}}{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}-g_{{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}
, where
A
contains integral vertices of a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
{\bm{x}}^{\bm{\alpha}}:=\prod_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}x_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}
with
g_{\bm{\alpha}}>0
for all
{\bm{\alpha}}\in A
, and
{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}
lies in the relative interior of this simplex. Circuit polynomials have a simple signed support pattern: at most one monomial has a negative coefficient, while the other monomials have positive coefficients. SoNC polynomials have several convex programming representations (
Dressler et al. 2019
;
Ghasemi and Marshall 2012
;
Magron and Wang 2023a
).

In this paper, we propose a new type of sparse binary non-negativity certificate for the BPO. We explore the discrete properties of binary polynomials and their signed support patterns, and construct relaxations for the BPO for any binary polynomial. Our study is inspired by several observations for polynomial optimization.

First, we observe that, in the broader setting of semi-algebraic optimization (
Lasserre 2015
;
Parrilo and Thomas 2020
), the non-negativity of SoS, SoNC, and SoFP polynomials could hold over larger domains than binary hypercubes, such as hypercubes, spheres, and semi-algebraic sets. When restricting to the BPO, the corresponding domain shrinks to the binary hypercube. Using the binary property that
x_{j}^{2}=x_{j}
, one can convert these polynomials to multilinear polynomials and use them as binary non-negativity certificates. Therefore, we can simply focus on multilinear polynomials that are non-negative over the binary hypercube.

Secondly, for the binary polynomial
f
, its signed support vector in
\{-1,0,1\}^{2^{n}}
encodes the signs of coefficients of its monomial
{\bm{x}}^{\bm{\alpha}}
indexed by
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. A subclass of binary polynomials with a specific signed support pattern are
nonlinearly negatively signed (NNS) polynomials
, and it is easy to identify them, as their nonlinear monomials all have negative coefficients (their linear parts can have arbitrary signs). Moreover, NNS polynomials are submodular functions with nice algorithmic properties (see
Crama and Hammer 2011
).

1.1
Our contribution.

We show that the binary non-negativity of an NNS polynomial with
m
monomials of degree
d
can be checked in
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}d)
time via a
min cut
algorithm. This leads to an
\mathcal{O}(md)
-size LP formulation of the cone of binary non-negative NNS polynomials.
For an arbitrary
n
-variate polynomial, we can decompose it as the sum of an NNS polynomial and a positively signed (PS) polynomial (that only contains nonlinear monomials with positive coefficients). We introduce two methods to construct piece-wise linear concave extensions of the PS component. These methods lead to an LP characterization of the binary non-negativity of the arbitrary polynomial and an LP reformulation of the BPO for such a polynomial. Because the size of the LP reformulation maybe huge, we further propose a refined signed support decomposition to decompose this polynomial as binary non-negativity certificates with simpler signed support patterns and lower representation complexities. Finally, we construct hierarchies of LP relaxations for the BPO that converge in at most
\lceil\log(n)\rceil
steps, and each level of the relaxations can be solved in a time doubly exponential in its level number
i\leq\lceil\log(n)\rceil
(hence exponential in
n
).

1.3
Related work.

The book by
Magron and Wang 2023b
summarizes several proposals for sparse versions of the Lasserre hierarchy, including correlative sparsity (
Grimm et al. 2007
;
Lasserre 2006
;
Waki et al. 2006
) and term sparsity (
Wang et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2021b
).

In the literature, we find some methods that look for a new parametrization of sparse non-negative polynomials, e.g., SoNC polynomials (
Dressler et al. 2017
;
Dressler et al. 2019
). The non-negativity of a circuit polynomial can be reduced to the non-negativity of a signomial through the exponential transformation (
Murray et al. 2021
), and the latter problem can be solved by a convex relative entropy program proposed by
Chandrasekaran and Shah 2016
.

Given an
n
-variate
d
-degree binary polynomial
f
and a finite family
\{f^{i}\}
of binary non-negativity certificates, one can usually use a convex program to search for a decomposition of
f
into the sum of
f^{i}
. In the Sherali-Adams hierarchy, since the constraints
0\leq x_{j}\leq 1
are valid for binary variables, the certificate
f^{i}
is expressed as a bound-factor product form
\prod_{j\in S_{-}}(1-x_{j})\prod_{j\in S_{+}}x_{j}
(
Handelman 1988
), where
S_{-},S_{+}
are disjoint subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The upper bounds on the degree of
f_{i}
and the size of the convex program are respectively
n
and
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
(
Conforti et al. 2014
;
Laurent 2003
). In the SoNC hierarchy, the certificate
f^{i}
is a non-negative circuit polynomial. The upper bounds on the degree of
f_{i}
and the size of the convex program are respectively
n
and
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
(
Dressler et al. 2022
). In the Lasserre hierarchy, the certificate polynomial
f^{i}
is the square of a binary polynomial
g^{i}
.
Lasserre 2002a
showed that the maximum degree of
g^{i}
is bounded by
n
, and
Sakaue et al. 2017
gave a stronger bound by
\lceil(n+d-1)/2\rceil
. Therefore, the upper bound on the size of the convex program is either
\mathcal{O}(2^{(n+d-1)})
or
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
. We refer to
Laurent 2003
;
Lasserre 2002c
for a comparison of the Sherali-Adams and Lasserre hierarchies.

Many exact algorithms (
Helmberg and Rendl 1998
;
Krislock et al. 2014
;
Malick and Roupin 2013
;
Rendl et al. 2010
) for
max cut
problems and binary quadratic programs use convex relaxations and strong valid inequalities.
Del Pia and Khajavirad 2017
introduced the notion of sparse support patterns for binary polynomials using hypergraphs, enabling a graph-theoretical approach to BPO. Furthermore,
Del Pia and Di Gregorio 2023
;
Del Pia and Khajavirad 2023
gave a polynomial-time approach to optimize beta-acyclic binary polynomials. These findings also suggest that the binary non-negativity of such polynomials can be verified in polynomial time.

There are many studies on the strength of BPO relaxation hierarchies.
Gouveia et al. 2010
studied SDP-based relaxations for BPO through an SoS perspective. For instance, the degree-2 SoS gives the relaxation of
Goemans and Williamson 1995
for
max cut
.
Lee et al. 2015
investigated lower bounds on the size of SDP relaxations for
max cut
,
max stable set
, and

TSP
.
Slot and Laurent 2023
analysed the error of the Lasserre hierarchy. Other relaxation hierarchies in integer programming include the Lovász-Schrijver hierarchy (
Lovász and Schrijver 1991
), Balas-Ceria-Cornuéjols hierarchy (
Balas et al. 1993
), and Bienstock-Zuckerberg hierarchy (
Bienstock and Zuckerberg 2004
).

The submodularity of a pseudo-Boolean function is characterized by its second order derivatives (
Billionnet and Minoux 1985
;
Nemhauser et al. 1978
).
Recognition of submodular/supermodular binary polynomials is
\mathbf{co}
-
\mathbf{NP}
-complete (
Crama 1989
;
Gallo and Simeone 1989
). However, for submodular/supermodular binary polynomials of degree at most 3, their explicit form is shown by
Billionnet and Minoux 1985
;
Nemhauser et al. 1978
. A subfamily of submodular binary polynomials that can be recognized is the NNS ones. We refer to
Boros and Hammer 2002
;
Crama and Hammer 2011
for topics on pseudo-Boolean functions,
Fujishige 2005
for submodular function analysis, and
Murota 2003
for discrete convex analysis.

2
Preliminaries.

In this paper, we assume that the vector space
\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
is spanned by binary monomials (i.e., monomials over a binary domain). As
\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{B}}
, we do not distinguish between the binary polynomials in
\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
and the vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{B}}
. We first present a classification of binary polynomials based on their signed support vectors.

Definition 1
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
be a binary polynomial. Then,

•

f
is an affine polynomial, if
\ssv(f)_{\mathbb{B}_{2:n}}={\bm{0}}
;

•

f
is an NS (resp. PS) polynomial, if
\ssv(f)\leq{\bm{0}}
(resp.
\ssv(f)\geq{\bm{0}}
);

•

f
is an NNS (resp. NPS) polynomial, if
\ssv(f)_{\mathbb{B}_{2:n}}\leq{\bm{0}}
(resp.
\ssv(f)_{\mathbb{B}_{2:n}}\geq{\bm{0}}
).

Note that, in the above definition, NS (resp. PS) is an abbreviation for “negatively signed” (resp. “positively signed”); NNS (resp. NPS) is an abbreviation for “nonlinearly negatively signed” (resp. “nonlinearly positively signed”). Obviously, the above definition could be adapted for the polynomials over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For example, a circuit polynomial, defined over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is the sum of a PS polynomial and an NS monomial.

We next consider special classes of functions defined over
\mathbb{B}
. We say that a function
g:\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{R}
is
submodular
(resp.
supermodular
) function, if
for every
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{B}
,
g({\bm{x}})+g({\bm{y}})\geq g\left({\bm{x}}\lor{\bm{y}}\right)+g\left({\bm{x}}\land{\bm{y}}\right)
(resp.
g({\bm{x}})+g({\bm{y}})\leq g\left({\bm{x}}\lor{\bm{y}}\right)+g\left({\bm{x}}\land{\bm{y}}\right)
), where
\lor,\land
are element-wise minimum and maximum. A
modular function
is both submodular and supermodular. The classification in
Definition

1
allows for characterizing submodularity/supermodularity of binary polynomials as follows.

Lemma 1
.

(
Crama and Hammer 2011
, Thm. 13.21)

Every affine polynomial is a modular function. Every NNS (resp. NPS) binary polynomial is a submodular (resp. supermodular) function.

Moreover,
Nemhauser et al. 1978
showed that all submodular binary polynomials are NNS polynomials, when the degree is 2. However,
Billionnet and Minoux 1985
showed that, when the degree is 3, some submodular binary polynomials are not NNS polynomials.

We then describe the
signed support decomposition
of binary polynomials. Through this decomposition, every binary polynomial can be written as the sum of an NNS polynomial and a PS polynomial (
Xu and Liberti to appear
). To describe the decomposition, we define two transforms in
\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
:

\displaystyle\nn:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})\to\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}}),f({\bm{x}})\mapsto\nn(f)({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{0:1}}f_{\bm{\alpha}}{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}+\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{2:n}}\min(f_{\bm{\alpha}},0){\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\ppn:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})\to\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}}),f({\bm{x}})\mapsto\ppn(f)({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{2:n}}\max(f_{\bm{\alpha}},0){\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}},

where
\nn(f)
is the NNS component of
f
and
\ppn(f)
is the PS component of
f
. It is obvious that
f=\nn(f)+\ppn(f)
, which gives the decomposition for
f
as well as the decomposition for the signed support vector of
f
.

Because we are going to exploit the signed support patterns of binary polynomials, we will define a partial order in
\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
to specify the patterns.

Definition 2
.

Given
{\bm{s}}^{\prime},{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
, the relation
{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\preceq{\bm{s}}
holds, if, for every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{0:1}
,
|s^{\prime}_{\bm{\alpha}}|\leq|s_{\bm{\alpha}}|
, and for every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{2:n}
, either
0\leq s^{\prime}_{\bm{\alpha}}\leq s_{\bm{\alpha}}
or
0\geq s^{\prime}_{\bm{\alpha}}\geq s_{\bm{\alpha}}
.

For brevity, given
f\in\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
and
{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
, we denote
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
(and say that
f
is within
{\bm{s}}
) if
\ssv(f)\preceq{\bm{s}}
. This relation implies that the support of
f
is a subset of the support of
{\bm{s}}
, and signs of nonlinear monomials in
f
cannot be opposite to those of
{\bm{s}}
. More precisely, the condition can be expressed as the constraints on the entries
f_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
:

f\preceq{\bm{s}}\iff\begin{cases}f_{\bm{\alpha}}=0\textup{ (zero)}&\textup{for }{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{0:1}\;\mbox{ s.t. }s_{\bm{\alpha}}=0\\
f_{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}\textup{ (free)}&\textup{for }{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{0:1}\;\mbox{ s.t. }|s_{\bm{\alpha}}|=1\\
f_{\bm{\alpha}}=0\textup{ (zero)}&\textup{for }{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{2:n}\;\mbox{ s.t. }s_{\bm{\alpha}}=0\\
f_{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\textup{ (non-negative)}&\textup{for }{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{2:n}\;\mbox{ s.t. }s_{\bm{\alpha}}=1\\
f_{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}_{-}\textup{ (non-positive)}&\textup{for }{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}_{2:n}\;\mbox{ s.t. }s_{\bm{\alpha}}=-1.\end{cases}

(1)

We call
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
a
signed support constraint
(w.r.t.
{\bm{s}}
), and it defines a convex cone
\ssc({\bm{s}})\coloneqq\{f\in\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}}):f\preceq{\bm{s}}\}
.
Supposing
{\bm{s}}
is fixed, there are only
|{\bm{s}}|
non-zero constraints in (
1
), so this sparsity condition implies that it suffices to consider these constraints and corresponding entries of
f
.

3
Binary non-negativity of NNS polynomials.

In this section, we consider binary non-negative NNS polynomials and study their representations. We introduce a method to check the binary non-negativity of any NNS polynomial in a time polynomial in the encoding size of this polynomial. We show that the binary non-negative NNS polynomials of the same signed support pattern have a polynomial-size extended formulation.

Throughout this section, we fix a signed support vector
{\bm{s}}
and consider NNS polynomials within
{\bm{s}}
. This allows for characterizing binary non-negative NNS polynomials through their signed support patterns. Next, we give the mathematical description of those NNS polynomials.

Definition 3
.

Given
{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
such that
s_{\mathbb{B}_{2:n}}\leq 0
, the set of binary non-negative NNS polynomials within
{\bm{s}}
is

\nnn({\bm{s}})\coloneqq\{f\in\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}}):\ssv(f)\preceq{\bm{s}}\land\ \forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{B}\;f({\bm{x}})\geq 0\}.

(2)

We can compare the signed support patterns of binary non-negative NNS polynomials with non-negative circuit polynomials (
Dressler et al. 2017
). A non-negative circuit polynomial can have at most one NS monomial, and it is non-negative over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(including
\mathbb{B}
). Thus, such a circuit polynomial closely resembles an NPS polynomial. A binary non-negative NNS polynomial has nonlinear NS terms, and the terms in its affine component have arbitrary signs. In conclusion, the signed support patterns of these two types of polynomials are complementary.

We next study the properties of
\nnn({\bm{s}})
.
The set
\nnn({\bm{s}})
is defined by two types of constraints. The first signed support constraint,
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
, specifies the signed support pattern of
f
, as in (
1
). The second type of constraint,
\forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{B}\;f({\bm{x}})\geq 0
, stipulates the binary non-negativity of
f
. This constraint is called the
non-negativity constraint
. Note that the binary vector
{\bm{x}}
in the non-negativity constraint serves both as an index and data for its
linear sub-constraints
:
f({\bm{x}})\geq 0
, where
f({\bm{x}})=\sum\limits_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}}{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f_{{\bm{\alpha}}}
is interpreted as a linear function over the vector
f
.

Let
m\coloneqq|{\bm{s}}|
be the number of non-zeros in
{\bm{s}}
, and let
d\coloneqq\max\limits_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}})}|{\bm{\alpha}}|
. Polynomials in
\nnn({\bm{s}})
have at most
m
monomials and are at most of degree
d
. The set
\nnn({\bm{s}})
is defined by a system of linear constraints over
monomial variables

\{f_{{\bm{\alpha}}}\}_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}})}
, and it satisfies the following properties.

Theorem 1
.

For any
s\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
,
\nnn({\bm{s}})
is a convex polyhedral cone with an LP formulation of
m
monomial variables subject to a signed support constraint and a non-negativity constraint. Moreover, every NNS polynomial
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
is binary non-negative if and only if
f\in\nnn({\bm{s}})
.

Proof.

Proof.
The constraint
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
implies that the number of non-zero entries of
f
is
m
, while the other entries are fixed. Therefore,
\nnn({\bm{s}})
has an LP formulation of
m
monomial variables, so it is a convex polyhedral set. We know that
\nnn({\bm{s}})
is the set of all binary non-negative NNS polynomials within
{\bm{s}}
. For every
f\in\nnn({\bm{s}})
,
\lambda f
is binary non-negative for positive scalar
\lambda
.
∎

3.1
Separation algorithms for the non-negativity constraint.

The next question is algorithmic: how can we optimize over the cone
\nnn({\bm{s}})
? As the number of nontrivial linear sub-constraints equals
|\mathbb{B}|=2^{n}
, we can consider the
separation problem
for
\nnn({\bm{s}})
, which asks whether all linear sub-constraints are satisfied for any given
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
. If not, it outputs a most violated constraint. Thus, the BNP for
f
is equivalent to the separation problem between
\nnn({\bm{s}})
and
f
.

The separation problem can be reduced to finding
\argmin\limits_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{B}}f({\bm{x}})
. Throughout this paper, we assume that the coefficients of
f
are fixed-size rationals.
As indicated by
Lemma

1
, the NNS polynomial
f
is submodular, so the optimization problem is a submodular minimization problem. Therefore, the separation problem can be addressed by general-purpose submodular minimization algorithms. For example, employing the algorithm of
Orlin 2009
, one can minimize
f
in
\mathcal{O}(n^{5}md+n^{6})
time, where
\mathcal{O}(md)
is the time complexity of evaluating
f
.

Another tailored method further reduces the minimization problem into a
min cut
/
max flow
problem and uses the dedicated algorithms (
Billionnet and Minoux 1985
;
Hansen 1974
;
Picard and Queyranne 1982
), which have better time complexities. We begin with the reformulation procedure proposed by
Picard and Queyranne 1982
and then analyze the size of the resulting
min cut
problem. Let us write

f({\bm{x}})=f_{{\bm{0}}}+\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in A}f_{{\bm{\alpha}}}{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}f_{{\bm{e}}_{j}}x_{j},

(3)

where for all
{\bm{\alpha}}\in A
,
|{\bm{\alpha}}|\geq 2

\displaystyle f^{b}({\bm{x}})

4
Concave extensions of PS polynomials.

We have shown how to certify the non-negativity of NNS polynomials. As PS polynomials are complementary to NNS polynomials, we can adapt the previous result. In this section, we introduce two
piece-wise linear concave extensions
of PS polynomials. As we let the basis of
\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
be the set of binary monomials, any binary polynomial
f({\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
is isomorphic to a vector
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{B}}
, whose entries are coefficients of
f({\bm{x}})
. Thus, we can view binary polynomials as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{B}}
.

Therefore, we can express every linear map in
\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
by a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{B}}
that transforms any binary polynomial
f({\bm{x}})=\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}}f_{\bm{\alpha}}x^{\bm{\alpha}}
to another
(Mf)({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{B}}M({\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime},{\bm{\alpha}}){\bm{x}}^{\bm{\alpha}}
, where
M({\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime},{\bm{\alpha}})
is the
({\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime},{\bm{\alpha}})
-th entry of the matrix
M
.
We define the extensions as linear maps (i.e., matrices) that transform PS polynomials into linear polynomials.

Definition 4
.

Given
{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
such that
s_{\mathbb{B}_{0:1}}={\bm{0}},s_{\mathbb{B}_{2:n}}\geq{\bm{0}}
,
M\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{B}}
is called an overestimation matrix w.r.t.
{\bm{s}}
, if, for every PS polynomial
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
,
Mf
is a linear polynomial, and for every
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{B}
,
(Mf)({\bm{x}})\geq f({\bm{x}})
.

For every PS polynomial of a given signed support vector, applying the overestimation matrix yields a linear polynomial that overestimates the PS polynomial on
\mathbb{B}
. Note that overestimation matrices are sparse in the sense that their non-zero entries are determined by the signed support patterns of PS polynomials. We introduce the set of overestimation matrices that recover the evaluation of such a PS polynomial on
\mathbb{B}
exactly.

Definition 5
.

A set
\mathcal{M}
of overestimation matrices w.r.t.
{\bm{s}}
is exact, if, for every PS polynomial
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
and every
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{B}
, the equation
\min\limits_{M\in\mathcal{M}}(Mf)({\bm{x}})=f({\bm{x}})
holds. The set is called minimal if no proper subset of
\mathcal{M}
is exact w.r.t.
{\bm{s}}
.

We find that
\min\limits_{M\in\mathcal{M}}(Mf)({\bm{x}})
is a
piece-wise linear concave extension
of
f({\bm{x}})
. Concave extensions are not unique. We will propose two types of extensions through a unified matrix-based parametrization. Let
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}\coloneqq\{j\in\mathcal{N}:\exists{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}})\ \alpha_{j}=1\}
, and let
n^{\prime}\coloneqq|\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}|
. This means that the PS polynomials within
{\bm{s}}
depend only on
n^{\prime}
variables indexed by
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}
.

4.1
Standard extension.

The first concave extension, called
standard extension
(or paved extension) (
Crama 1993
), is based on the standard linearization method for binary polynomials (
Glover and Woolsey 1973
). We say that a map
\sigma:\supp({\bm{s}})\to\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}
is a
variable selector
on
{\bm{s}}
, if, for every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}})
, there exists a
j\in\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}
such that
\alpha_{j}=1
and
\sigma({\bm{\alpha}})=j
. Let
\Sigma({\bm{s}})
be the set of all variable selectors on
{\bm{s}}
.

For each
\sigma\in\Sigma({\bm{s}})
, we construct a matrix
M_{\sigma}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{B}}
as follows. For every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}})
, we set the entries
M_{\sigma}({\bm{b}}_{\sigma({\bm{\alpha}})},{\bm{\alpha}})
to 1; and we set the other entries to 0. Note that
{\bm{b}}_{\sigma({\bm{\alpha}})}\in\mathbb{B}
indexes the monomial
{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{b}}_{\sigma({\bm{\alpha}})}}=x_{\sigma({\bm{\alpha}})}
. We find that only rows of
M_{\sigma}
indexed by
{\bm{b}}_{j}
(for
j\in\mathcal{N}_{{\bm{s}}}
) are non-zeros. For every
\sigma\in\Sigma({\bm{s}}),j\in\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}

\sigma^{-1}(j)=\{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}}):\sigma({\bm{\alpha}})=j\}
be the set of monomials selected as
j
.
Consequently, we have:

(M_{\sigma}f)({\bm{x}})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}}(M_{\sigma}f)_{{\bm{b}}_{j}}x_{j}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}}\big(\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\sigma^{-1}(j)}f_{\bm{\alpha}}\big)x_{j}=\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}})}f_{\bm{\alpha}}x_{\sigma({\bm{\alpha}})}.

(16)

The standard extension of

The second concave extension, called

For each
\pi\in\Pi({\bm{s}})

\pi^{j}\coloneqq\{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}}):\pi(j)\in\supp({\bm{\alpha}})\subseteq\pi([j])\}

(

For every

and

{\bm{\alpha}}\in\pi^{j}

(for

\begin{split}(M_{\pi}f)({\bm{x}})&=\sum_{j\in[n^{\prime}]}(M_{\pi}f)_{{\bm{b}}_{\pi(j)}}x_{\pi(j)}=\sum_{j\in[n^{\prime}]}\big(\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\pi^{j}}f_{\bm{\alpha}}\big)x_{\pi(j)}\\

(17)

\pi([j])
through the ordering

4.3
Comparison of concave extensions.

We compare the proposed concave extensions. It follows from
Definition

4
that an overestimation matrix has its non-zero rows indexed by
\{{\bm{b}}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{s}}
(corresponding to linear monomials) and its non-zero columns indexed by
\supp({\bm{s}})
(corresponding to monomials of
f
). Then, the encoding size of
M
only depends on these non-zero rows and columns.

Let
m\coloneqq|{\bm{s}}|
be the number of monomials indexed by
\supp({\bm{s}})
. Let
d\coloneqq\max_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}})}|{\bm{\alpha}}|
be the max degree of PS polynomials within
{\bm{s}}
. Recall that
n^{\prime}=|\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}|
. Thus, the number of non-zero entries of
M
is
\mathcal{O}(n^{\prime}m)
, which is polynomial in the sizes of the PS polynomials.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an exact set of overestimation matrices w.r.t
{\bm{s}}
in
Definition

5
. Recall that
the cardinality of
\mathcal{M}
is the number of matrices required to extend any PS polynomial within
{\bm{s}}
. Therefore, we are primarily interested in the cardinality number of the two extension methods. Let
\Gamma({\bm{s}})
denote an upper bound on the minimum cardinality of the exact set w.r.t.
{\bm{s}}
, which we call the
concave-extension complexity
w.r.t.
{\bm{s}}
. For the standard extension approach, its cardinality number
|\mathcal{M}|
is
|\Sigma({\bm{s}})|=\prod_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}})}|{\bm{\alpha}}|
with an upper bound
d^{m}
. For the Lovász extension approach, its cardinality number
|\mathcal{M}|
is
|\Pi({\bm{s}})|=n^{\prime}!
with an upper bound
n^{\prime n^{\prime}}
.

Lovász extension (
Lovász 1983
;
Xu and Liberti to appear
) yields the lowest concave overestimator (concave envelope) for any supermodular function
g:\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{R}
. However, it encodes overestimation matrices redundant for extension, as we only need exact concave extensions that consist of the fewest overestimation matrices possible. As shown later, we can relax the Lovász extension to reduce the number of overestimation matrices.

We remark that the Lovász extension relates every linear overestimator
M_{\pi}f
to its
exact binary vectors

{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{B}
that
(M_{\pi}f)({\bm{x}})=f({\bm{x}})
. This relation shows that
M_{\pi}f
is a facet of the concave envelope of
f
and gives the binary points where
M_{\pi}f
equals
f
. We propose a
filtering method
that outputs a relaxed Lovász extension for
f
with a better bound on
|\mathcal{M}|
. Starting with
\mathcal{M}=\varnothing
, the method involves scanning every point
{\bm{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}}
. If there is no overestimator
M_{\pi}f
for
M_{\pi}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
(M_{\pi}f)({\bm{x}})=f({\bm{x}})
, then, using the exact relation between facets and binary points, it adds a new overestimator
M_{\pi}f
exact at
{\bm{x}}
. As
|\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{N}_{\bm{s}}}|\leq 2^{n^{\prime}}
, by the Pigeon hole principle, it outputs an exact set of overestimation matrices, whose cardinality is at most
2^{n^{\prime}}
.

Finally, the output set is guaranteed to be
minimal
, i.e., there is no proper subset of the output set that results in a concave extension. Otherwise, the filtering method could have removed redundant overestimators. We obtain the following result on the concave-extension complexity.

Corollary 2
.

Given
{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
,

\Gamma({\bm{s}})\leq\min(2^{n^{\prime}},d^{m})
, where
2^{n^{\prime}}
(resp.
d^{m}
) is achievable by the relaxed Lovász (resp. standard) extension.

The standard extension is oriented towards the monomials, as it can extend any PS polynomial with a specified set of monomials. It is particularly useful when the polynomial is sparse. In principle, one can apply a similar filtering method to remove redundant overestimation matrices for the standard extension. On the other hand, the relaxed Lovász extension is oriented towards the variables, as it can extend any PS polynomial over at most
n^{\prime}
variables regardless of its monomials. This approach is particularly useful when the polynomial is dense. Thereby, these two approaches complement each other.

As shown,
concave extensions for PS polynomials are not unique. Furthermore, minimal concave extensions may not be unique. We use two major classes of concave extensions, but conceptually, one may discover and use other classes of concave extensions.

5
BNP and BPO for arbitrary polynomials.

In this section, we characterize the set of binary non-negative polynomials within a given signed support vector, and propose a reformulation of BPO for these polynomials. We have shown that a given polynomial has a unique signed support decomposition and its PS component has piece-wise linear concave extensions. As NNS polynomials can incorporate linear terms, we leverage concave extensions to reformulate BNP for the polynomial into BNPs for multiple NNS polynomials. This finally leads to the reformulation of BPO for that polynomial. We first present a necessary and sufficient condition for the binary non-negativity.

Lemma 6
.

For every binary polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})
, let
\mathcal{M}
be an exact set of overestimation matrices w.r.t.
\ssv(\ppn(f))
. Then
f
is binary non-negative if only if for every
M\in\mathcal{M}
, the NNS polynomial
\nn(f)+M\ppn(f)
is binary non-negative.

Proof.

Proof.
Let
M\in\mathcal{M}
.
Since
\ppn(f)
is NPS and
M
is an overestimation matrices w.r.t.
\ssv(\ppn(f))
,
M\ppn(f)
is a linear polynomial. Since
\nn(f)
is NNS,
\nn(f)+M\ppn(f)
is NNS as well. The exactness of
\mathcal{M}
means that
\ppn(f)({\bm{x}})=\min\limits_{M\in\mathcal{M}}(M\ppn(f))({\bm{x}})
for every
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{B}
. It follows that
f
equals
\min\limits_{M\in\mathcal{M}}(\nn(f)+M\ppn(f))
on
\mathbb{B}
. Thus, the binary non-negativity condition
\min\limits_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{B}}f({\bm{x}})\geq 0
holds, if and only if, for every
M\in\mathcal{M}
, the NNS polynomial
\nn(f)+M\ppn(f)
is binary non-negative.
∎

We note that the NNS polynomial
\nn(f)+M\ppn(f)
is an overestimator for the binary polynomial
f
on
\mathbb{B}
. Moreover, the BNP for
f
is equivalent to BNPs for all its NNS overestimating polynomials induced by
\mathcal{M}
. We also have a signed support decomposition of signed support vectors, as a counterpart of that decomposition of binary polynomials.

Definition 6
.

Given
{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
with
\mathbb{B}_{0:1}\subseteq\supp({\bm{s}})
, its signed support decomposition
{\bm{s}}={\bm{s}}^{1}+{\bm{s}}^{2}
satisfy that
{\bm{s}}^{1},{\bm{s}}^{2}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}},s^{1}_{\mathbb{B}_{2:n}}\leq{\bm{0}},s^{2}_{\mathbb{B}_{2:n}}\geq{\bm{0}},s^{2}_{\mathbb{B}_{0:1}}={\bm{0}}
, and
\supp({\bm{s}}^{1})\cap\supp({\bm{s}}^{2})=\varnothing
.
The derived parameters of
{\bm{s}}^{i}
(
i\in\{1,2\}
) are
m_{i}\coloneqq|s^{i}|,d_{i}\coloneqq\max_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\supp({\bm{s}}^{i})}|{\bm{\alpha}}|
,
n_{i}\coloneqq|\mathcal{N}_{s^{i}}|
. The derived parameters of
{\bm{s}}
include those of
{\bm{s}}^{1},{\bm{s}}^{2}
and
m\coloneqq m_{1}+m_{2}
.

In the above definition, if the binary polynomial
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
, then the signed support vectors
{\bm{s}}^{1},{\bm{s}}^{2}
correspond to
\nn(f),\ppn(f)
, respectively, i.e.,
\nn(f)\preceq{\bm{s}}^{1}
and
\ppn(f)\preceq{\bm{s}}^{2}
. More precisely, this implies that
\nn(f)
is an
n_{1}
-variate (
n_{1}=n
)
d_{1}
-degree binary polynomial with
m_{1}
monomials, and
\ppn(f)
is an
n_{2}
-variate
d_{2}
-degree binary polynomial with
m_{2}
monomials. We will use these parameters to quantify the complexities of BNP and BPO for
f
. We next consider the representation of binary polynomials within a given signed support vector.

Definition 7
.

Given
{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
with
\mathbb{B}_{0:1}\subseteq\supp({\bm{s}})
, let
\mathcal{M}
be an exact set of overestimation matrices w.r.t.
{\bm{s}}^{2}
. The set
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
of binary non-negative polynomials w.r.t.
{\bm{s}},\mathcal{M}
is

\{f\in\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}}):\nn(f)\preceq{\bm{s}}^{1}\land\;\ppn(f)\preceq{\bm{s}}^{2}\land\forall M\in\mathcal{M}\;\nn(f)+M\ppn(f)\in\nnn({\bm{s}}^{1})\}.

(18)

The above definition explains the necessity of the condition
\mathbb{B}_{0:1}\subseteq\supp({\bm{s}})
in
Definition

6
. This condition ensures that, after applying an overestimation matrix
M
, the inclusions
\supp(M\ppn(f))\subseteq\mathbb{B}_{0:1}\subseteq\supp({\bm{s}}^{1})
are satisfied. Thus,
\nn(f)+M\ppn(f)\preceq{\bm{s}}^{2}
, and (
18
) is well-defined.
We observe that (
18
)
has an alternative extended representation:

\exists f^{1}\;f^{2}\in\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}})\;\ssv(f_{1})\preceq{\bm{s}}^{1}\land\ \ssv(f_{2})\preceq{\bm{s}}^{2}\land\ f=f^{1}+f^{2}\land\forall M\in\mathcal{M}\;f^{1}+Mf^{2}\in\nnn({\bm{s}}^{1}),

(19)

where the signed support constraints can be translated into simple constraints on the coefficients of
f
.
The above system has the following properties.

Theorem 3
.

\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
is a convex polyhedral cone, and it has an extended LP formulation of
m
monomial variables subject to a signed support constraint and
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2}))
non-negativity constraints. Moreover, every polynomial
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
is binary non-negative if and only if
f\in\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
.

Proof.

Proof.
The signed support constraints on
f^{1}
and
f^{2}
can be translated into a signed support constraint on
f
itself, and we need
m=m_{1}+m_{2}
monomial variables to represent
f
. As
|\mathcal{M}|\leq\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2})
, there are at most
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2}))
cones
\nnn({\bm{s}}^{1})
, each of which has a non-negativity constraint. Thus,
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
is a convex polyhedron with an LP representation of the stated size.
Suppose
f
is a binary polynomial within
{\bm{s}}
. Since
\mathbb{B}_{0:1}\subseteq\supp({\bm{s}}^{1})
, it follows that
f^{1}+Mf^{2}\preceq{\bm{s}}^{1}
for every
M\in\mathcal{M}
. It follows from
Lemma

6
and
Theorem

1
that
f
is binary non-negative if and only if
f^{1}+Mf^{2}\in\nnn({\bm{s}}^{1})
is binary non-negative for every
M\in\mathcal{M}
. This condition is equivalent to
f\in\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
. Note that for any
f\in\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
,
\lambda f
is non-negative for any positive scalar
\lambda
, so
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
is a cone.
∎

We next characterize the complexity of BNP for any polynomial
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
. The BNP is equivalent to a separation problem for
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
, which asks to separate
f
from the cone. The separation complexity is implicitly parametrized by
{\bm{s}}
as follows.

Proposition 3
.

There is an algorithm solving the separation problem for the convex polyhedral cone
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
in
\mathcal{O}(d_{1}m_{1}^{2}\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2}))
time.

Proof.

Proof.
There are at most
\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2})
sub-cones
\nnn({\bm{s}}^{1})
in
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
. It follows from
Definition

6
that
n_{2}\leq n\leq m_{1}
, so
m_{1}+n_{2}=\mathcal{O}(m_{1})
. It suffices to search for a sub-cone
\nnn({\bm{s}}^{1})
that does not contain the target polynomial
\nn(f)+M\ppn(f)
and separate it from that cone. Then, the result follows from
Corollary

1
.
∎

Then, we want to solve BPO for
f
:

\lambda^{\star}\coloneqq\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{B}}f({\bm{x}}).

(20)

The BPO is equivalent to the dual problem
\lambda^{\star}=\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\{\lambda:f-\lambda\textup{ is binary non-negative}\}
.
Since
\lambda
is a scalar, by
Definition

6
,
f-\lambda\preceq{\bm{s}}
. As every binary non-negative
f-\lambda
must be in
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
, the dual problem is equivalent to a linear optimization on the convex cone
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
, which we call
signed reformulation
of BPO:

\lambda^{\star}=\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\{\lambda:f-\lambda\in\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})\}.

(21)

The signed reformulation can be solved via the ellipsoid algorithm (
Grötschel et al. 1981
), the complexity of which is polynomial in the separation complexity bound
\mathcal{O}(d_{1}m_{1}^{2}\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2}))
given in
Proposition

3
. Moreover, it follows from
Theorem

2
that the signed reformulation admits an extended formulation of size
\mathcal{O}(d_{1}m_{1}\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2})+md)
.

The dominant factor in the complexity of solving the signed reformulation is the concave-extension complexity
\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2})
, which determines the number of sub-cones in
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
.
By
Corollary

2
, this factor
\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2})
grows combinatorially (exponentially) to the derived parameters of
{\bm{s}}^{2}
. To address this issue, we look at the Lasserre hierarchy for BPO, as it illustrates how inner approximations based on binary non-negativity polynomials lead to a hierarchy of relaxations for BPO.

The
i
-th level Lasserre relaxation takes the form
\lambda^{i}_{sos}\coloneqq\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\{\lambda:f-\lambda\in\mathsf{SoS}^{i}(n)\},
where
\mathsf{SoS}^{i}(n)
is the set of
n
-variate SoS non-negative polynomials of degree at most
i
. The relaxation value
\lambda^{i}_{sos}
is a lower bound to
\lambda^{\star}
. In the worst case, it requires
i=n
to make the Lasserre relaxation exact (i.e.,
\lambda^{n}_{sos}=\lambda^{\star}
) (
Lasserre 2002a
). Since
\mathsf{SoS}^{1}(n)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathsf{SoS}^{n}(n)
, their lower bounds are non-decreasing, i.e.,
\lambda^{1}_{sos}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda^{n}_{sos}
. Generally, one expects to obtain an exact or a good lower bound at a level lower than
n
.

We compare the signed reformulation with the last level Lasserre relaxation.
The set
\mathsf{SoS}^{n}(n)
has an SDP representation of size
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
, which does not depend on the sparsity. The size
\mathcal{O}(n\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2}))
of the LP representing
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
has an upper bound
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
(see
Corollary

2
), but the size can be made small depending on the sparsity and signed support patterns. The worst-case size of the signed reformulation is comparable to that of the Lasserre hierarchy. The signed reformulation will be the last level relaxation in our hierarchies of relaxations (see later).

6
Refined signed support decomposition.

For a binary polynomial, we refine the signed support decomposition to further exploit its signed support pattern. The refined decomposition uses certain binary non-negative polynomials, which we call
signed certificates
. The refined decomposition allows us to construct nested families of conic inner approximations of
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
, which lead to hierarchies of relaxations for BPO (
20
). We first define the refined signed support decomposition for signed support vectors.

Definition 8
.

Given
{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
, a collection
\Theta\coloneqq\{{\bm{\theta}}^{k}\}_{[\ell]}
of signed support vectors is called a refined signed support decomposition of
{\bm{s}}
, if
\mathbb{B}_{0:1}\subseteq\supp({\bm{\theta}}^{k})
and
\supp({\bm{s}})\subseteq\cup_{k\in[\ell]}\supp({\bm{\theta}}^{k})
.

We then introduce the refined signed support decomposition that decomposes any binary polynomial
f\preceq{\bm{s}}
into a sum of signed certificates and a PS polynomial.
We call the signed support vector of each summand a
decomposed signed support vector
. The key idea is to make decomposed signed support vectors as a refined signed support decomposition of
\ssv(f)
.

Definition 9
.

Given
{\bm{s}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{B}}
, let
\Theta\coloneqq\{{\bm{\theta}}^{k}\}_{k\in[\ell]}
be a refined signed support decomposition of
{\bm{s}}
. For each
k\in[\ell]
, let
{\bm{\theta}}^{k,1},{\bm{\theta}}^{k,2}
be the signed support decomposition of
{\bm{\theta}}^{k}
, let
\mathcal{M}^{k}
be an exact set of overestimation matrices w.r.t.
{\bm{\theta}}^{k,2}
. Let
\mathfrak{M}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{M}^{k}\}_{k\in[\ell]}
. Then the set
\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
of refined signed decomposable polynomials w.r.t.
{\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M}
is

\{f\in\mathbb{R}({\bm{x}}):f=g+\sum_{k\in[\ell]}f^{k}\land\ \forall k\in[\ell]\;f^{k}\in\nm({\bm{\theta}}^{k},\mathcal{M}^{k})\land\ g\geq{\bm{0}}\land\ \supp(g)\subseteq\supp({\bm{s}})\cup\supp(\mathbb{B}_{0:1})\},

(22)

We look at the set
\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
and its representation. Any polynomial
f\in\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
could be decomposed as the sum of a PS polynomial
g
and binary non-negative polynomials
f^{k}
(used as signed certificates). This way of parametrization is flexible, as one can adjust the signed support vector
{\bm{\theta}}^{k}
of
f^{k}
to decompose that of
f
. The sum of the concave-extension complexities
\Gamma({\bm{\theta}}^{k,2})
of signed certificates
f^{k}
may be smaller than
\Gamma({\bm{s}}^{2})
of
f
. This opens up possibilities for constructing relaxations for BPO with controllable complexity, and we have the following observation.

Lemma 7
.

The set
\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
in (
22
) is a convex polyhedral cone, and
any
f\in\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
is binary non-negative.

Proof.

Proof.

\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
is a direct sum of the convex polyhedral cones
\nm({\bm{\theta}}^{k},\mathcal{M}^{k})
and
\{g:g\geq{\bm{0}}\land\ \supp(g)\subseteq\supp({\bm{s}})\cup\supp(\mathbb{B}_{0:1})\}
, so it is a convex polyhedral cone as well.
As
f^{k},g
are binary non-negative,
f
is binary non-negative as well.
∎

Taking a closer look at
\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
reveals that it encompasses two opposing categories of binary polynomials:
g
and
f^{k}
. They enable us to represent two extreme scenarios where
f
can be either a PS or an NS polynomial, while maintaining a low representation complexity.

In the following, we restrict the set
\Theta
to satisfy the following additional conditions: (i) (NNS equivalence) for every
k\in[\ell]
,
{\bm{\theta}}^{k,1}={\bm{s}}^{1}
, (ii) (disjoint PS partition) for every
i,j\in[\ell]
,
\supp({\bm{\theta}}^{i2})\cap\supp({\bm{\theta}}^{j2})=\varnothing
, and
{\bm{s}}^{2}=\sum_{k\in[\ell]}{\bm{\theta}}^{k,2}
. We remark that these conditions are adjustable in practice, since there are other choices, which may depend on the sparsity/signed support pattern of
f
. For example, if
\supp({\bm{s}}^{1})=\varnothing
, we can set: for every
k\in[\ell]
,
{\bm{\theta}}^{k,1}={\bm{s}}
; for every
i,j\in[\ell]
,
\supp({\bm{\theta}}^{i2})\cap\supp({\bm{\theta}}^{j2})=\varnothing
, and
{\bm{s}}=\sum_{k\in[\ell]}{\bm{\theta}}^{k,2}
.

Since enlarging the support
{\bm{\theta}}^{k,1}
of the NNS component of
{\bm{\theta}}^{k}
only incurs an additional computational cost polynomial in the derived parameters of
{\bm{\theta}}^{k}
(see
Proposition

3
and
Corollary

1
), this yields a computationally cheap way to enlarge the search space. Thus, the NNS equivalence constraint stipulates that the support of
{\bm{\theta}}^{k,1}
equals
{\bm{s}}^{1}
. Enlarging the support
{\bm{\theta}}^{k,2}
of the PS component of
{\bm{\theta}}^{k}
incurs a computational cost exponential in the derived parameters of
{\bm{\theta}}^{k}
(see
Proposition

3
and
Corollary

2
). Consequently, enlarging
\Theta
in the latter manner is computationally expensive. This explains why we make the supports of
{\bm{\theta}}^{k,2}
form a disjoint partition of
{\bm{s}}^{2}
rather than a whole cover of it.

Based on the refined signed support decomposition, one can construct a convex relaxation for BPO, by replacing
\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
in the signed reformulation (
21
) with its conic inner approximation
\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
:

\lambda^{\star}({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})\coloneqq\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\{\lambda:f-\lambda\in\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})\}.

(23)

Since
\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})\subseteq\nm({\bm{s}},\mathcal{M})
,
\lambda^{\star}({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
is a lower bound on the optimal value
\lambda^{\star}
of BPO.

One crucial question is the
support inference problem
, which asks to determine the appropriate decomposed signed support vectors in
\Theta
for a refined signed support decomposition so that the lower bound
\lambda^{\star}({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
can be as large as possible. This question can be viewed as an inverse problem, where we aim to infer the decomposed signed support vectors from observing the signed support vector of
f
. The problem arises naturally when designing sparse non-negativity certificates to approximate a dense polynomial.

7
Hierarchies of relaxations for BPO.

In this section, we tackle the challenging support inference problem. We adopt a hierarchical approach that takes advantage of the refined signed support decomposition and construct the family
\Theta
. Our approach involves gradually expanding the sets in
\Theta
by incorporating them into more signed support vectors. This leads to nested cones
\snm({\bm{s}},\Theta,\mathfrak{M})
of binary non-negative polynomials. As (
23
), we construct two types of hierarchies of relaxations for BPO.

We first introduce the template tree-based partition, and then describe two applications of this partition to generate the family
\Theta
. Given a finite set
C
, the output of the tree-based partition is a binary tree
T(C)
. The
height
of a node in the tree is the number of nodes in the path to the root node. Let
h(C)
denote the maximum height among all nodes, and let
r(C)
denote the root node. The
depth
of a node at height
h
is defined as
h(C)+1-h
. The set of nodes at depth
i
is denoted by
V^{i}(C)
, and its
width

\ell^{i}(C)
is defined as its cardinality
|V^{i}(C)|
of nodes at this depth. We write
V^{i}(C)=\{V^{i}_{1}(C),\ldots,V^{i}_{\ell^{i}(C)}(C)\}
.

The tree construction starts from a trivial partition
V^{1}(C)\coloneqq\{\{c\}|c\in C\}
and builds
V^{i+1}(C)
from
V^{i}(C)
recursively as follows. If
\ell^{i}(C)
is even, then
V^{i}(C)
is partitioned into pairs, and the union of two nodes in each pair is taken as their parent node in
V^{i+1}(C)
. If
\ell^{i}(C)
is odd, then a special node of
\varnothing
is added to
V^{i}(C)
, and the above procedure is applied as the even case. When
\ell^{i}(C)=1
, the construction terminates, and we let
r(C)=V^{i}(C)
. The tree
T(C)
is called a
partition tree
of
C
, and it has the following properties.

Lemma 8
.

Given a finite set
C
,
r(C)=\{C\}
, and
h(C)\leq\lceil\log|C|\rceil
. For every
i\in[h(C)]
,

V^{i}(C)
is a partition of
C
,
|V^{i}(C)|\leq 2^{h(C)+1-i}
, and, for every
k\in[\ell^{i}(C)],|V^{i}_{k}(C)|\leq 2^{i-1}
.

Proof.

Proof.
We know that at the leaves,
V^{1}(C)
is a partition of
C
. Using a basic induction argument, we know that at each depth
1\leq i\leq h(C)-1
, as
V^{i}(C)
is a partition of
C
and the nodes of
V^{i+1}(C)
are the union of two nodes of
V^{i}(C)
,
V^{i+1}(C)
is also a partition of
C
. Since
|r(C)|=1
and

Computational results.

In this section, we conduct computational experiments to test the proposed relaxations. The source code, data, and detailed results can be found in our online repository:
github.com/lidingxu/BPOrelaxations
. The experiments are conducted on a server with Intel Xeon W-2245 CPU @ 3.90GHz and 126GB main memory. We use JuMP as the modeling system and Mosek as the solver for both LP and SDP solving.

The Sherali-Adams, Lasserre, and signed relaxations are tested over
max cut
problems.
Consider an undirected graph
G=(V,E,w)
, where
V
is the set of nodes,
E
is the set of edges, and

is a weight function over subsets of
E

S
and the other end node in
V\setminus S
. The
max cut

S\subseteq V
with the maximum cut capacity. Let
V=[n]
, and we use a binary variable vector

indicating whether vertices belong to
S
. The problem can be formulated as a quadratic BPO problem:
\max_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}((1-x_{i})x_{j}+x_{i}(1-x_{j})).

The Biq Mac library by
Wiegele 2007

of medium size.

Our initial observation reveals that the SheraliAdams 1 relaxation exhibits the weakest performance in terms of closing the duality gap; however, its computation is the fastest, almost negligible. Among the small instances (pm1s_ni, w01_100, t3gn_seed), the standard signed relaxations require more computation time compared to the Lasserre 1 relaxation, yet their computation time is generally comparable. 9
Conclusion.

We characterize binary non-negativity for binary polynomials based on their signed support vectors. We identify a conic set of binary polynomials whose binary non-negativity can be certificated in strongly polynomial time. We construct an LP representation of such binary polynomials for a given signed support pattern. This yields new binary non-negativity certificates, called signed certificates. We present an LP to search for the refined signed support decomposition of any binary polynomial as signed certificates. Using tree-based partitions, we present two general approaches to construct nested families of cones of binary non-negative polynomials. These families yield new hierarchies of relaxations for BPO. Future work could be design of efficient numerical algorithms for solving the relaxations.
