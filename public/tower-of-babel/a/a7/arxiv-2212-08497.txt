Modeling Schrödinger equation diffraction with generalized function potentials and initial values
Günther Hörmann, Ljubica Oparnica, Christian Spreitzer

Abstract
. We discuss spectral properties of a regularization approach to a Schrödinger equation set-up for the diffraction of a quantum particle at almost planar patterns. Physically meaningful initial values and potentials are modeled in terms of regularizing families and the solutions can be interpreted as generalized functions. We establish spectral and scattering theoretical properties of the regularizing solution families and provide some comparison with the more direct approximations and simplifications used in physics.

1.
Introduction

In the introductory parts of quantum physics text books, one often finds discussions of double-slit experiments. They perfectly serve as an illustration of the fundamental so-called quantum logic and are still a prominent subject of research, not only as a
Gedankenexperiment
but also in contemporary experimental physics (see, e.g.,
[
19
]
and
[
7
]
). The methodological parts of standard text book models are then based on Schrödinger equations with various potentials.
However, as a somewhat surprising observation, it seems difficult to find discussions of the foundational double-slit experiment among the quantum mechanical text book models with such Schrödinger equation set-up. The desire to have such a theory in early quantum physics is nicely illustrated by the following quote from Weinberg’s text book (
[
41
, pages 14-15]
):“There is a story that in his oral thesis examination, de Broglie was asked what other evidence might be found for a wave theory of the electron, and he suggested that perhaps diffraction phenomena might be observed in the scattering of electrons by crystals. …. What was needed was some way of extending the wave idea from free particles, described by waves …, to particles moving in a potential, …”.

The standard physics argument justifying the occurrence of the interference pattern observed in a double-slit experiment is, in fact, a pure classical approximation based on point sources for waves at the narrow slits. The two discussions of the double-slit in
[
31
]
and
[
4
]
are based on the Feynman path technique, which means that mathematically speaking they make use of the typical distribution theoretic fundamental solution to the free Schrödinger equation and produce a somewhat refined justification of the usual approximation via classical wave theory and diffraction. A recent numerical and qualitative analysis based on the Bohmian point of view with particle trajectories is presented in
[
13
]
, which is very similar to our approach in Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
in its modeling of the initial value and the potential. In fact, as noted already in
[
29
, Remark 2.4]
, the regularization methods from theories of generalized functions would apply also in studies of the Bohmian flow related to diffraction problems based on Schrödinger equations.

The double slit problem is often treated as a “boundary value” problem with a free wave function “approaching the double-slit”. However, the Schrödinger equation does not have the property of finite propagation speed that a wave equation—or, more generally speaking, any strictly hyperbolic equation has. Thus on a fundamental level it is not justified a priori why an approximation with classical wave propagation starting from the slits works so well in explaining the measured diffraction pattern on a screen at some distance from the slits. Moreover, at least in principle and a priori, one cannot rule out partial reflections from the blocking objects and that a superposition of what came in from one side of the slits and what was reflected could generate a stationary state (as described in
[
24
, Subsection 3.7.3]
) corresponding to a solution of the time-independent Schrödinger equation (i.e., an eigenfunction of the Hamiltonian).

A further difficulty is that modeling the potential for a realistic double-slit configuration in a Schrödinger operator is mathematically delicate and even distributional potentials, e.g. producing Dirac-
\delta
-type “sources for passing waves” at the slits, seem not truly appropriate due to their idealizations from the outset. Instead one might rather strive for an implementation via regularizations that can more accurately capture the nature of an essentially insurmountable high barrier away from the slits that may at the same time be of infinitesimal width, thus almost located in a plane perpendicular to the “main propagation direction” as seen from the source. Thus we will attempt to accurately model such potentials by corresponding generalized functions which can be conveniently represented via families of regularizations. In addition, a realistic initial value configuration will not be modeled by some
L^{2}
function, but rather by a family of wave packet type regularizations defining a generalized function. We emphasize that neither for the initial value nor for the potential are these regularizations expected to be convergent as distributions. Instead they only obey certain asymptotic estimates. For this reason, our approach cannot make direct use of the elaborated and successful Hilbert space theory of Schrödinger operators with delta-type singularities in the potentials as described in
[
8
,
6
,
18
,
5
,
11
,
10
,
12
]
. However, the method of modeling impenetrable barriers as regularizations of infinite potentials and employing concepts from the theory of Colombeau generalized functions to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions and analyze their properties has a much wider range of applicability. For instance, as an alternative to boundary conditions, generalized infinite potentials may also be used to model geometric constraints, e.g. particles confined to a half-space or to a box.

In the context of the regularization based approach of Colombeau-generalized solutions,
well-posedness of Cauchy problems for Schrödinger equations allowing for discontinuous or distributional coefficients, initial data, and right-hand sides was shown in
[
30

. Previously, several Colombeau-generalized solutions to special types of linear and nonlinear Schrödinger equations have been constructed in
[

39

Recent results in

Employing concepts based on so-called very weak solutions which are obtained as families of regularizations, the main results are well-posedness in the corresponding sense even with very singular initial data and consistency with Sobolev space solutions in case the coefficient has better regularity. In a similar set-up,

Outline of the paper and brief summary of the main results

Section 2 discusses the details of the mathematical regularization modeling for the potential and the initial values, while Section 3 establishes the key spectral properties of these regularizations and ensures the applicability of basic methods from scattering theory. Section 4 makes an effort to relate the regularization and generalized function set-up with various approximations or calculational simplifications employed successfully in physics.

The various results of our analysis can be summarized under the following aspects:

Modeling with regularizations and unique generalized solutions:
Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
give precise specifications of the potential
V
for scattering at a planar pattern, e.g., in a double-slit setting, and of the initial value

in the form of a wave packet as regularizing families
(V_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
and
(g_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}

It is guaranteed then by Theorem

that with a such a set-up we have uniqueness of Coulombeau-type generalized function solutions to the Schrödinger equation Cauchy problem

\partial_{t}u=i\,\Delta u-iVu,\quad u|_{t=0}=g.

Spectral properties:
Subsection
3.1
gives a careful analysis of the spectrum of the Hamilton operator
H_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta+V_{\varepsilon}
arising in these Coulombeau-generalized Schrödinger operators. In particular, the absence of bound states is shown by Theorem
3.2
and that the essential spectrum is preserved from the free part
-\Delta
. Interpreting and extending then in Subsection
3.2
the notion of eigenvalues to the context of Colombeau-type generalized functions, we determine in Proposition
3.5
the generalized point spectrum and obtain that it contains the essential spectrum of

as a subset.

Scattering theoretical representations:
The modeling options for the potential regularizations are so flexible that they allow also to have compact support of

at each instance of
\varepsilon>0
and letting the size of the supports grow as
\varepsilon\to 0

and obtain a concrete distributional representation for the so-called distorted Fourier transform of the generalized solution in Equation (

Comparison with standard approximations in physics:
A common simplification of the model is to drop the potential from the operator and instead move information about some anticipated behavior in the scattering process or an intuitive qualitative property of the solution into the initial value or a new source term for the Cauchy problem. We study such strategies on a very basic level, but arrive already at the end of Subsection
4.1
at an intuitively appealing agreement with physics in terms of a wave intensity distribution on a parallel screen based on the Fourier transform of the characteristic function of the slit configuration. In Subsections
4.2
and
4.3
we investigate the plausibility of approaches in physics that consider first the propagation of a free solution with initial values approximating a Dirac delta towards the scattering plane and then use essentially the interaction
V_{\varepsilon}\cdot u_{\varepsilon}
of the potential function with this free solution at the scattering plane as a source term
F_{\varepsilon}
for a new Cauchy problem. The analysis is inherently vague at a few points, but we do obtain a certain coherence for the crucial Equation (

2.2.
Regularizations representing the initial configuration

Intuitively, during the time while the “source quantum particle approaches the scattering obstacle”, the idealization of “least possible localization in

g_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)e^{ip_{0}x},

where
(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}

are

-moderate families of nonnegative functions on

such that
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\subseteq(-\infty,-c-1]

{\left\|\rho_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{L^{2}}=1={\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{L^{2}}

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)\rho^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy=(\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)^{2}/2)|^{\infty}_{-\infty}=0

and we obtain,
by construction, that

\langle g_{\varepsilon}|-i\partial_{y}g_{\varepsilon}\rangle=0\quad\text{and}\quad\langle g_{\varepsilon}|-i\partial_{x}g_{\varepsilon}\rangle=p_{0}.

Moreover,
{\left\|g_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{L^{2}}=1

3.2.
Generalized eigenvalues

On the level of generalized functions, the family of operators
(H_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
defines a linear map, assigning to the class represented by
(\psi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
the class of
(H_{\varepsilon}\psi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
. In striving for a domain as large as possible and noting that we have

\widetilde{H^{2}}:=\mathcal{G}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=:\widetilde{L^{2}}

as modules over the ring of generalized complex numbers
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
, we thus obtain the
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
-linear map

H\colon\widetilde{H^{2}}\to\widetilde{L^{2}},\quad\text{class of }(\psi_{\varepsilon})\mapsto\text{ class of }(H_{\varepsilon}\psi_{\varepsilon}).

We may then study the question of eigenvalues
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
of
H
in the sense whether
H\psi=\lambda\psi
can hold with some
0\neq\psi\in\mathcal{G}_{H^{2}}
.
From the investigation of eigenvalue problems in
[
28
, Section 2 and Subsection 3.2]
, we learn that we should
require of a prospective eigenfunction
\psi
to be a
free vector
and not just nonzero, in order to avoid some artefacts due to the existence of zero divisors in
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
. Recall that a vector
\psi\in\widetilde{L^{2}}
is free, if
\psi
is linearly independent over the ring
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.,
\lambda\psi=0
in
\widetilde{L^{2}}
with
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
always implies
\lambda=0
. A free vector certainly is nonzero, but there are nonzero vectors in
\widetilde{L^{2}}
that are not free.

We thus define
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
to be a
generalized eigenvalue
of
H
, if there exists a free vector
\psi\in\widetilde{H^{2}}\subseteq\widetilde{L^{2}}
such that

H\psi=\lambda\psi

holds. In this case we call
\psi
a
generalized eigenfunction
or
eigenvector
of
H
. The
generalized point spectrum

\sigma_{gp}(H)
shall be be the subset of
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
consisting of the generalized eigenvalues of
H
.

Fortunately, in case of the
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
-module
\widetilde{L^{2}}
it is not difficult to characterize the free vectors very much along the lines of
[
28
, Lemma 2.5]
(originating from
[
32
, Theorem 5.8]
).

Lemma 3.4
.

A vector
\psi\in\widetilde{L^{2}}
is free, if and only if the generalized number
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}
, represented by
(\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
, is strictly positive, i.e., there is some
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}>\varepsilon^{m}
holds for small
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

If
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}
is strictly positive and
\lambda\psi=0
for some
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
, then the family
(|\lambda_{\varepsilon}|\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}})
is negligible, which can only be true if
(\lambda_{\varepsilon})
is negligible, thus
\lambda=0
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
.

Suppose now that
\psi
is free. Then
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
implies
\lambda\psi\neq 0
, which in turn gives
|\lambda|\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}\neq 0
. Therefore
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}
cannot be a zero divisor in
\widetilde{\mathbb{R}}
and hence is invertible by
[
28
, Lemma 2.2]
, which in turn implies the lower bound required in strict positivity.
∎

We will determine
\sigma_{gp}(H)
in Proposition
3.5
below and find that, in particular,

[0,\infty)\subseteq\sigma_{gp}(H),

so that all spectral values found in Theorem
3.2
for each
H_{\varepsilon}
are indeed generalized eigenvalues. On the other hand, it will turn out that all generalized eigenvalues of
H
are still infinitesimally close to the subset
[0,\infty)
, which we will make precise based on the following generalized distance concept: For an arbitrary subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
with representative
(\lambda_{\varepsilon})
we define
d(\lambda,D)\in\widetilde{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
to be the class of
(d(\lambda_{\varepsilon},D))_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
, where as usual
d(\mu,D):=\inf\{|\mu-z|\mid z\in D\}
for any
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
.

In contrast to the classical complex number set
\mathbb{C}
, it may happen for a closed subset
D
that a generalized number
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}\setminus D
satisfies
d(\lambda,D)=0
. To give a concrete example with
D:=[0,\infty)
, consider the class
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
of the representative
(\lambda_{\varepsilon})
with
\lambda_{\varepsilon}:=0
if
1/\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda_{\varepsilon}:=1
otherwise. Then
\lambda\neq r
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
for every
r\in[0,\infty)
, while clearly
d(\lambda,[0,\infty))=0
.

Proposition 3.5
.

We have
\sigma_{gp}(H)=\{\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}\mid d(\lambda,[0,\infty))=0\}
.

Proof.

Let us introduce the temporary notation
E:=\{\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}\mid d(\lambda,[0,\infty))=0\}
.

We show that
E\subseteq\sigma_{gp}(H)
: Let
\lambda\in E
with representative
(\lambda_{\varepsilon})
and let
q\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary.

Since
(d(\lambda_{\varepsilon},[0,\infty))_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
is negligible, we can find
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
c>0
such that for all
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
we have
d(\lambda_{\varepsilon},[0,\infty))\leq c\,\varepsilon^{q+2}
. Thus, for every such
\varepsilon
we can find
r_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
with
|\lambda_{\varepsilon}-r_{\varepsilon}|\leq c\,\varepsilon^{q+1}
.

Due to Theorem
3.2
the number
r_{\varepsilon}
belongs to the spectrum of the self-adjoint operator
H_{\varepsilon}
, hence is an approximate eigenvector (
[
24
, Proposition 9.18]
) and we can thus find
w_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}=1
and
\|(H_{\varepsilon}-r_{\varepsilon})w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon^{q+1}
. We may therefore conclude that

(17)

\|(H_{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{\varepsilon})w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|(H_{\varepsilon}-r_{\varepsilon})w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}+|r_{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{\varepsilon}|\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon^{q+1}+c\,\varepsilon^{q+1}=O(\varepsilon^{q})\quad(\varepsilon\to 0).

Note that we further obtain
H^{2}
-moderateness of the family
(w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
, since

\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{2}}\leq\|(I-\Delta)w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\underbrace{\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}}_{1}+\|-\Delta w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}=1+\|(-\Delta+V_{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{\varepsilon})w_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon}w_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\\
\leq 1+\|(H_{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{\varepsilon})w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}+\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}+|\lambda_{\varepsilon}|\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}=1+O(\varepsilon^{q})+\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}+|\lambda_{\varepsilon}|.

Therefore, with
w
denoting the class of
(w_{\varepsilon})
in
\widetilde{H^{2}}
we have arrived at
Hw=\lambda w
with
\|w\|_{L^{2}}=1
, thus
w
is free and an eigenvector for
H
with generalized eigenvalue
\lambda
.

We show that also
\sigma_{gp}(H)\subseteq E
holds by proving
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}\setminus E\subseteq\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma_{gp}(H)
: By contradiction, suppose
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}\setminus E
and
\lambda\in\sigma_{gp}(H)
. Let
(\lambda_{\varepsilon})
be a representative of
\lambda
. Since
\lambda
is a generalized eigenvalue, there is an element
\psi\in\widetilde{H^{2}}
with
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}
invertible in
\widetilde{\mathbb{R}}
and such that
H\psi=\lambda\psi
. Therefore, for any representative
(\psi_{\varepsilon})
of
\psi
, the following holds: There is some
m\in\mathbb{N}
and for every
q\in\mathbb{N}
some
c>0
such that

\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\geq\varepsilon^{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\|(H_{\varepsilon}-\lambda_{\varepsilon})\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq c\,\varepsilon^{q}\quad(\varepsilon\to 0).

Since
\lambda\not\in E
we know that
(d(\lambda_{\varepsilon},[0,\infty))_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
is not negligible. Hence there is some
p\in\mathbb{N}
and a strictly decreasing sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
(
k\to\infty
) such that

d(\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}},[0,\infty))>\varepsilon_{k}^{p}\quad(k\in\mathbb{N}).

In particular,
\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)=\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H_{\varepsilon_{k}})
and we have the standard
L^{2}
-operator norm estimate

\|(H_{\varepsilon_{k}}-\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}})^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{d(\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}},\sigma(H_{\varepsilon_{k}}))}=\frac{1}{d(\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}},[0,\infty))}

for the resolvent of the self-adjoint and positive operator
H_{\varepsilon_{k}}
(
[
24
, Exercises 9.11.17-18]
). The latter implies

\|(H_{\varepsilon_{k}}-\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}})u\|_{L^{2}}\geq d(\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}},[0,\infty))\|u\|_{L^{2}}\quad\text{ for all }u\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

and hence in particular, for every
q\in\mathbb{N}
and for large
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

c\,\varepsilon_{k}^{q}\geq\|(H_{\varepsilon_{k}}-\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}})\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\geq d(\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}},[0,\infty))\|\psi_{\varepsilon_{k}}\|_{L^{2}}\geq\varepsilon_{k}^{p}\,\varepsilon_{k}^{m}=\varepsilon_{k}^{p+m},

which gives a contradiction for
k\to\infty
if
q>p+m
.
∎

\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subseteq\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as described in
[

or
[

\mathchar 632
4.5]
. Namely, there exists a generalized eigenvector

of

in the sense that

Now one might attempt to relate the family

.

3.3.
Aspects of scattering theory

As already considered briefly in Remark

, one may model the regularizing potentials

with compactly supported functions for each

Lemma 3.7
.

If

has compact support, then the corresponding multiplication operator on

is a

of

in the sense of
[

Proof.

In fact, the two conditions in

.
i) For any

is bounded in

thanks to Poincaré’s inequality, thus precompact in

by Rellich’s embedding theorem, and the continuous inclusion

gives precompactness in

such that

, if

with

we have

for every

with

and may put

(notation of
[

and recall

∎

We may combine the property of

Remark 3.9
.

(On the “boundary value”
R_{0}(\lambda+i0)
of the resolvent of
-\Delta
.)
The resolvent
R_{0}(z)=(-\Delta-z)^{-1}
is defined and holomorphic for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
, but the above constructions made implicit use of the expression
R_{0}(\lambda+i0)
for
0<\lambda=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}(\lambda+i\alpha)
in describing the action of the distorted Fourier transform on the function space
B
with
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subseteq B\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
in (
20
). Let us try to understand now
R_{0}(\lambda+i0)f
or its Fourier transform
{\mathcal{F}}(R_{0}(\lambda+i0)f)
, in terms of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
when
f
is a test function from
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. For
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
we certainly have

{\mathcal{F}}(R_{0}(z)f)(\theta)=\frac{\widehat{f}(\theta)}{|\theta|^{2}-z}\qquad(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{2})

and note that
\theta\mapsto 1/(|\theta|^{2}-z)
is a smooth bounded function, hence belongs to
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Let
r_{0}(z)\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be its inverse Fourier transform, then we may write (with convolution
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}*\mathscr{S}
)

R_{0}(z)f=r_{0}(z)*f\quad\text{or}\quad{\mathcal{F}}(R_{0}(z)f)={\mathcal{F}}r_{0}(z)\cdot\widehat{f}.

Inserting
z=\lambda+i\mu
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mu\geq 0
and letting
\mu\to 0+
we would hope that the above formulae allow for a “boundary value” involving a distributional kernel

r_{0}(\lambda+i0)=\mathscr{S}^{\prime}\text{-}\lim_{\mu\to 0+}r_{0}(\lambda+i\mu).

We may equivalently try to define
{\mathcal{F}}r_{0}(\lambda+i0)
via
\lim_{\mu\to 0+}v_{\mu}
in
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, where

v_{\mu}(\theta):=\frac{1}{|\theta|^{2}-\lambda-i\mu}\qquad(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\mu>0).

Let
g\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, use polar coordinates
\theta=r\omega
with
r\geq 0
,
\omega\in S^{1}
, and introduce
Mg(r):=\int_{S^{1}}g(r\omega)\,d\omega
to deduce in a first step (with the change of coordinates

in the last equality)

\langle v_{\mu},g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{g(\theta)}{|\theta|^{2}-\lambda-i\mu}\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r}{r^{2}-\lambda-i\mu}\;\int_{S^{1}}g(r\omega)\,d\omega\,dr=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{Mg(\sqrt{s})}{s-\lambda-i\mu}\,ds.

The easy case is
\lambda<0
, in which
R_{0}(\lambda)
is already well-defined and

is a bounded function on
[0,\infty)
, thus we directly obtain also

\langle{\mathcal{F}}r_{0}(\lambda+i0),g\rangle=\lim_{\mu\to 0+}\langle v_{\mu},g\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S^{1}}\frac{g(\sqrt{s}\omega)}{2(s-\lambda)}\,d\omega\,ds.

The relevant case
\lambda\geq 0
is tricky, since

has a nonintegrable singularity at
s=\lambda
. Upon a shift by
\lambda
, we would like to interpret the above integral in terms of the one-dimensional distribution
(s-i0)^{-1}
, defined as distributional boundary value of the holomorphic function
z\mapsto 1/z
in the lower complex half plane (
[
27

). The action of
(s-\lambda-i0)^{-1}
on a test function
\phi
on
\mathbb{R}
can be given by (cf.
[
27

)

\langle(s-\lambda-i0)^{-1},\phi\rangle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\log(|s-\lambda|)\phi^{\prime}(s)\,ds+i\pi\phi(\lambda).

We see that its suffices to have
\phi
as
C^{1}
function here. The remaining difficulty now is that
\phi(s):=Mg(\sqrt{s})
is so far only defined for
s\geq 0
and will in general not be
C^{1}
up to
s=0
. However, at least on the subspace of test functions
g\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

and

, we always obtain a

extension of

upon setting

for

and may then write

\langle{\mathcal{F}}r_{0}(\lambda+i0),g\rangle=\lim_{\mu\to 0+}\langle v_{\mu},g\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle(s-\lambda-i0)^{-1},Mg(\sqrt{s})\rangle.

4.
Alternative approximations and regularizations: Cauchy problems with source term or initial value replacing the potential

We now leave the detailed specifications of the previous sections, vary certain aspects of the modeling and allow for simplifications and approximations. Interpreting the term involving the potential,
V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}
, as a source term (right-hand side) for the equation, we investigate connections to approximate solution methods used in physics.

Assuming that we would have convergence
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
in
C(\mathbb{R},\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, then necessarily

g_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}(0)\to u(0),

and, in the sense of distributions,

(23)

iV_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}=i\Delta u_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}\to i\Delta u-\partial_{t}u.

We would then obtain that both
u_{\varepsilon}
and
V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}
converge as distributions, suggesting that
u_{\varepsilon}\to 0
near
\{0\}\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus S)
since
V_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
on that same set, where classical particles should be blocked. Thus the approximative idea to consider the set
S
as sources of waves propagating into the region
x>0
, as it is often described in the physics literature, can be given some mathematical support.

Suppose that instead of employing truly a (regularized) potential function in the Schrödinger operator we look at a simplified Cauchy problem, where the influence of the “interaction product”
Vu
of the potential with the wave function is somehow replaced by a source term
F
and an initial value
f
so that we have the following inhomogeneous Cauchy problem without potential

(24)

\partial_{t}w=i\,\Delta w-iF,\quad w|_{t=0}=f.

The solution can be written in terms of the fundamental solution
E\in C(\mathbb{R},\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
,
E(t;x,y)=\exp(i(x^{2}+y^{2})/4t)/(4\pi it)
, satisfying

\partial_{t}E-i\Delta E=0,\quad E(0)=\delta,

with Duhamel’s principle in the form

w(t)=E(t)*f-i\int_{0}^{t}E(t-\tau)*F(\tau)\,d\tau.

Equivalently, upon applying a spatial Fourier transform to (
24
) and using a notation like
\widehat{w}(t,\xi,\eta)
etc., we obtain an ordinary differential equation with initial condition

\partial_{t}\widehat{w}(t,\xi,\eta)=-i(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})\widehat{w}(t,\xi,\eta)-i\widehat{F}(t,\xi,\eta),\quad\widehat{w}(0,\xi,\eta)=\widehat{f}(\xi,\eta).

Employing the abbreviation
\theta=(\xi,\eta)
, the spatially Fourier transformed solution is given by

(25)

\widehat{w}(t,\theta)=e^{-it|\theta|^{2}}\widehat{f}(\theta)-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-\tau)|\theta|^{2}}\,\widehat{F}(\tau,\theta)\,d\tau.

(Checking with
[
27
, Theorem 7.6.1]
also confirms that
\widehat{E}(t;\xi,\eta)=\exp(-it(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}))
.)

4.1.
The approximate solutions from theoretical physics

An inspection of the discussions in
[
4
]
and
[
31
]
shows that the basic solution formulae obtained in theoretical physics can be put into the context of (
24
) as follows: In a first step, let
w_{0}
denote the solution to (
24
) with initial value
f=\delta_{(-x_{0},0)}
and
F=0
, where
x_{0}>0
; thus,
w_{0}
corresponds to the wave function of a free particle emitted at time
t=0
at the location
(-x_{0},0)
and is given by

w_{0}(t,x,y)=(E(t)*\delta_{(-x_{0},0)})(x,y)=E(t,x+x_{0},y)
.
Suppose that
y\mapsto b_{0}(y)
describes the pattern and shapes of slits in the plane
x=0
, but contrary to the potential function above now with value
1
for passing through and
0
for blocking, e.g.,
[
4
]
uses the characteristic function of one or two bounded intervals, and that the particle passes through
x=0
at time
t_{0}>0
.

In a second step, we consider now the solution
w_{1}
to a Cauchy problem for the free Schrödinger equation with initial value corresponding to the particle represented by
w_{0}
passing through the slits at time
t_{0}
, namely

(26)

\partial_{t}w_{1}=i\,\Delta w_{1},\quad w_{1}(t_{0},x,y)=\delta_{0}(x)w_{0}(t_{0},0,y)b_{0}(y)=\delta_{0}(x)E(t_{0},x_{0},y)b_{0}(y)=:f_{0}(x,y).

The qualitative properties of the intensity distribution
y\mapsto|w_{1}(t_{0}+T,x_{1},y)|^{2}
found on a screen located at distance
x_{1}>0
from the slits and at time
t_{0}+T
,
T>0
, are then studied in detail in
[
4
,
31
]
for appropriate asymptotic relations between the relevant parameters from physics (de Broglie wavelength of the particle,
t_{0}
, and
T
) and geometry (
x_{0}
,
x_{1}
and shape of the slits) and seem to give reasonable approximations to the interference features seen in actual experiments.

We can easily obtain an explicit expression from (
26
) upon applying the above solution formulae to
w_{1}(t+t_{0})
, i.e.,
w_{1}(t)=E(t-t_{0})*f_{0}
, noting that the
x
-convolution is trivial due to the factor
\delta_{0}(x)
in
f_{0}
, i.e.,
w_{1}(t,x,y)=\Big(E(t-t_{0},x,.)*\big(E(t_{0},x_{0},.)b_{0}(.)\big)\Big)(y)
, and writing out the remaining
y
-convolution as an integral:

w_{1}(t,x,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}E(t-t_{0},x,y-s)E(t_{0},x_{0},s)b_{0}(s)\,ds\\
=\frac{1}{(4\pi i)^{2}(t-t_{0})t_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i(x^{2}+(y-s)^{2})/4(t-t_{0})}e^{i(x_{0}^{2}+s^{2})/4t_{0}}b_{0}(s)\,ds\\
=\frac{-e^{\frac{i}{4}(\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{t-t_{0}}+\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}})}}{16\pi^{2}(t-t_{0})t_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i(s^{2}-2ys)/4(t-t_{0})}e^{is^{2}/4t_{0}}b_{0}(s)\,ds\\
=\frac{-e^{\frac{i}{4}(\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{t-t_{0}}+\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}})}}{16\pi^{2}(t-t_{0})t_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\frac{-isy}{2(t-t_{0})}}\underbrace{e^{\frac{its^{2}}{4t_{0}(t-t_{0})}}}_{\phi(t,t_{0},s)}b_{0}(s)\,ds=\frac{-e^{\frac{i}{4}(\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{t-t_{0}}+\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{t_{0}})}}{16\pi^{2}(t-t_{0})t_{0}}\,{\mathcal{F}}\Big(\phi(t,t_{0},.)\,b_{0}(.)\Big)\big(y/2(t-t_{0})\big).

Therefore, the corresponding intensity distribution as a function of
y
is proportional to

16^{2}\pi^{4}T^{2}t_{0}^{2}\cdot|w_{1}(t_{0}+T,x_{1},y)|^{2}=\Big|{\mathcal{F}}\Big(\phi_{0}b_{0}\Big)\big(y/2T\big)\Big|^{2},

where
\phi_{0}(s):=\phi(t_{0}+T,t_{0},s)=\exp(i(t_{0}+T)s^{2}/t_{0}T)
and we recall that
b_{0}
is the characteristic function of the slits.

4.2.
A plausibility check

Let
u_{0,\varepsilon}
denote the solution to the free Schrödinger equation with initial value
g_{\varepsilon}
, i.e.,

\partial_{t}u_{0,\varepsilon}=i\Delta u_{0,\varepsilon},\quad u_{0,\varepsilon}|_{t=0}=g_{\varepsilon}.

Comparing with
w_{0}
in Subsection
4.1
we have:
If
g_{\varepsilon}\to\delta_{(-x_{0},0)}
as

, then
u_{0,\varepsilon}\to w_{0}
.

Consider the difference between the scattered and the free solution

,
which is characterized by

\partial_{t}w_{\varepsilon}=i\Delta w_{\varepsilon}-iV_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon},\quad w_{\varepsilon}|_{t=0}=0.

Interpreting this via Equation (

) we have the “source term”

and the corresponding solution formula implies

w_{\varepsilon}(t)=-i\int_{0}^{t}E(t-\tau)*(V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}(\tau))\,d\tau.

Now from the set-up of
V_{\varepsilon}
, in case we think of
b_{\varepsilon}
approximationg
\delta_{0}
, we could argue for an immediate plausible approximation in the integrand by using

(27)

F_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y):=V_{\varepsilon}(x,y)u_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)\approx\delta_{0}(x)h_{\varepsilon}(y)u_{\varepsilon}(t,0,y).

In view of the observations following (
23
), arguing that the “free solution from the region

enters through the slits” (represented by the set

) at an instance of time

according to a “typical travel time from the source to the plane

we might go a further bold step and consider the following additional “approximate replacement”

\beta_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y):=\delta_{0}(x)h_{\varepsilon}(y)u_{\varepsilon}(t,0,y)\approx\delta_{0}(x)1_{S}(y)u_{0,\varepsilon}(t,0,y)\delta_{t_{0}}(t).

Combining (
27
) and (

F_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)\approx\beta_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)\approx\delta_{t_{0}}(t)\delta_{0}(x)1_{S}(y)u_{0,\varepsilon}(t_{0},0,y),

which yields the following simplified approximate solution formula

w_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)\approx-i\Big(E(t-t_{0},x,.)*\big(u_{0,\varepsilon}(t_{0},0,.)1_{S}(.)\big)\Big)(y).

On the other hand, if we consider

\partial_{t}\widetilde{w_{1}}=i\Delta\widetilde{w_{1}}-i\delta_{t_{0}}(t)\delta_{0}(x)b_{0}(y)w_{0}(t_{0},0,y),\quad\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\widetilde{w_{1}}\subseteq[t_{0},\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Thus,

satisfies the Cauchy problem (

which nicely matches (

which happens to agree with the distributional limit, as

, of the right-hand side in (

4.3.
Improving on the plausibility of (

The coherence result in Corollary

In such circumstance we have support for the approximation (
27

V_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=b_{\varepsilon}(x)h_{\varepsilon}(y)
:

(32)

b_{\varepsilon}\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}b_{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx=1,\quad\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(b_{\varepsilon})\subseteq[-c_{\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon}],\quad{\left\|h_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\sqrt{c_{\varepsilon}}\to 0\;(\varepsilon\to 0).

In fact, the following proposition justifies the approximations chosen in (
27
) and (
28
) in the context of distributions. Note that in this context neither
V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}
nor
\beta_{\varepsilon}
is distributionally convergent.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose (
31
) and (
32
) hold and let
\beta_{\varepsilon}(t)
denote the distribution encountered in (
27
) and (
28
), i.e.,

\langle\beta_{\varepsilon}(t),\varphi\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{\varepsilon}(y)u_{\varepsilon}(t,0,y)\varphi(0,y)\,dy\qquad(\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})),

then we have
\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\big(V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}(t)-\beta_{\varepsilon}(t)\big)=0
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for every
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

For any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have (since
\int b_{\varepsilon}=1
and
h_{\varepsilon},b_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
)

|\langle V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}(t)-\beta_{\varepsilon}(t),\varphi\rangle|=|\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{\varepsilon}(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}}b_{\varepsilon}(x)\big(u_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)\varphi(x,y)-u_{\varepsilon}(t,0,y)\varphi(0,y)\big)\,dx\,dy|\\
\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{\varepsilon}(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}}b_{\varepsilon}(x)\,|\underbrace{u_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)\varphi(x,y)-u_{\varepsilon}(t,0,y)\varphi(0,y)}_{=:\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)}|\,dx\,dy,

where we may write

\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{d}{ds}(u_{\varepsilon}(t,s,y)\varphi(s,y))\,ds=\int_{0}^{x}\big(\partial_{x}u_{\varepsilon}(t,s,y)\varphi(s,y)+u_{\varepsilon}(t,s,y)\partial_{x}\varphi(s,y)\big)\,ds.

Inserting this above and using the fact that
|x|\leq c_{\varepsilon}
in
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(b_{\varepsilon})
we obtain

|\langle V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}(t)-\beta_{\varepsilon}(t),\varphi\rangle|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{\varepsilon}(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}}b_{\varepsilon}(x)\int_{-c_{\varepsilon}}^{c_{\varepsilon}}|\partial_{x}u_{\varepsilon}(t,s,y)\varphi(s,y)+u_{\varepsilon}(t,s,y)\partial_{x}\varphi(s,y)|\,ds\,dx\,dy\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{\varepsilon}(y)\int_{-c_{\varepsilon}}^{c_{\varepsilon}}|\partial_{x}u_{\varepsilon}(t,s,y)\varphi(s,y)+u_{\varepsilon}(t,s,y)\partial_{x}\varphi(s,y)|\,ds\,dy\\
\leq{\left\|h_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{[-c_{\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon}]\times\mathbb{R}}\big(|\partial_{x}u_{\varepsilon}(t,s,y)\varphi(s,y)|+|u_{\varepsilon}(t,s,y)\partial_{x}\varphi(s,y)|\big)\,d(s,y)\\
\leq{\left\|h_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\Big({\left\|\partial_{x}u_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|}_{L^{2}(M_{\varepsilon})}{\left\|\varphi\right\|}_{L^{2}(M_{\varepsilon})}+{\left\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|}_{L^{2}(M_{\varepsilon})}{\left\|\partial_{x}\varphi\right\|}_{L^{2}(M_{\varepsilon})}\Big)\\
\leq C_{0}{\left\|h_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}{\left\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|}_{H^{1}(M_{\varepsilon})}{\left\|\varphi\right\|}_{H^{1}(M_{\varepsilon})},

where we have put
M_{\varepsilon}:=[-c_{\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon}]\times\mathbb{R}
and
C_{0}
is some positive constant. If
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(\varphi)\subseteq[-l_{\varphi},l_{\varphi}]^{2}
, then we clearly have

{\left\|\varphi\right\|}_{H^{1}(M_{\varepsilon})}^{2}\leq 3\cdot 2l_{\varphi}\cdot 2c_{\varepsilon}\cdot{\left\|\varphi\right\|}_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}

and this implies (with some constant
C_{1}
depending on
\varphi
)

(33)

|\langle V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}(t)-\beta_{\varepsilon}(t),\varphi\rangle|\leq C_{1}\,\sqrt{c_{\varepsilon}}\,{\left\|h_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}{\left\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|}_{H^{1}(M_{\varepsilon})}.

Due to (
31
) we have
{\left\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\to{\left\|u(t)\right\|}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, hence there is some
1>\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
{\left\|u(t)\right\|}_{H^{1}(M_{\varepsilon})}\leq{\left\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq 2{\left\|u(t)\right\|}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, inserted into (
33
) we finally obtain from (
32
) that

|\langle V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}(t)-\beta_{\varepsilon}(t),\varphi\rangle|\leq 2C_{1}{\left\|u(t)\right\|}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\,\sqrt{c_{\varepsilon}}\,{\left\|h_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\to 0\qquad(\varepsilon_{0}>\varepsilon\to 0).

Appendix A
Generalized function solutions and coherence properties

In this appendix, we review the main results of
[
30
]
. Before going into details, we recall a few basics from the theory of Colombeau generalized functions. This can be seen as an extension of the so-called
sequential approach to distributions
, where each distribution is represented by approximating regularizing weakly convergent sequences modulo null sequences.

The fundamental idea of Colombeau-type regularization methods is to model nonsmooth objects by approximating nets of smooth functions, convergent or not, but with
moderate
asymptotics and to identify regularizing nets whose differences compared to the moderateness scale are
negligible
. For a modern introduction to Colombeau algebras we refer to
[
23
]
. Here we will also make use of constructions and notations from
[
21
]
, where generalized functions based on a locally convex topological vector space
E
are defined:
Let
E
be a locally convex topological vector space whose topology is given by the family of seminorms
\{p_{j}\}_{j\in J}
. The elements of

\mathcal{M}_{E}:=\{(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in E^{(0,1]}:\,\forall j\in J\,\,\exists N\in\mathbb{N}\quad p_{j}(u_{\varepsilon})=O(\varepsilon^{-N})\,\text{as}\,\varepsilon\to 0\}

and

\mathcal{N}_{E}:=\{(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in E^{(0,1]}:\,\forall j\in J\,\,\forall q\in\mathbb{N}\quad p_{j}(u_{\varepsilon})=O(\varepsilon^{q})\,\text{as}\,\varepsilon\to 0\},

are called
E
-moderate
and
E
-negligible
, respectively. With operations defined componentwise, e.g.,
(u_{\varepsilon})+(v_{\varepsilon}):=(u_{\varepsilon}+v_{\varepsilon})
etc.,
\mathcal{N}_{E}
becomes a vector subspace of
\mathcal{M}_{E}
. We define the
generalized functions based on
E
as the factor space
\mathcal{G}_{E}:=\mathcal{M}_{E}/\mathcal{N}_{E}
. If
E
is a differential algebra, i.e., an associative commutative algebra possessing commuting linear maps
\partial_{j}\colon E\to E
(
j=1,\ldots,n
) that satisfy the Leibniz rule
\partial_{j}(f\cdot g)=(\partial_{j}f)\cdot g+f\cdot\partial_{j}g
, then
\mathcal{N}_{E}
is an ideal in
\mathcal{M}_{E}
and
\mathcal{G}_{E}
is a differential algebra as well.

Particular choices of
E
reproduce the standard Colombeau algebras of generalized functions. For example,
E=\mathbb{C}
with the absolute value as norm yields the generalized complex numbers
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
; for
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
open,
E=\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with the topology of compact uniform convergence of all derivatives provides the so-called special Colombeau algebra
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\mathcal{G}(\Omega)
. Recall that
\Omega\mapsto\mathcal{G}(\Omega)
is a fine sheaf, thus, in particular, the restriction
u|_{B}
of
u\in\mathcal{G}(\Omega)
to an arbitrary open subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is well-defined and yields
u|_{B}\in\mathcal{G}(B)
. Moreover, we may embed
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
into
\mathcal{G}(\Omega)
by appropriate localization and convolution regularization.

If
E\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, then certain generalized functions can be projected into the space of distributions by taking weak limits: We say that
u\in\mathcal{G}_{E}
is
associated
with
w\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, if
u_{\varepsilon}\to w
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
holds for any (hence every) representative
(u_{\varepsilon})
of
u
. This fact is also denoted by
u\approx w
.

Consider open strips of the form
\Omega_{T}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\,]0,T[\,\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
(with
T>0
arbitrary) and the spaces
E=H^{\infty}({\Omega_{T}})=\{h\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega_{T}):\partial^{\alpha}h\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})\;\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}\}
with the family of (semi-)norms

{\left\|h\right\|}_{H^{k}}=\Big(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}{\left\|\partial^{\alpha}h\right\|}_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\quad(k\in\mathbb{N}),

as well as

E=W^{\infty,\infty}({\Omega_{T}})=\{h\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega_{T}):\partial^{\alpha}h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})\;\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}\}
with the family of (semi-)norms

{\left\|h\right\|}_{W^{k,\infty}}=\max_{|\alpha|\leq k}{\left\|\partial^{\alpha}h\right\|}_{L^{\infty}}\quad(k\in\mathbb{N}).

Clearly,
\Omega_{T}
satisfies the strong local Lipschitz property
[
1
, Chapter IV, 4.6, p. 66]
, hence every element of
H^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
and
W^{\infty,\infty}(\Omega_{T})
belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega_{T}})
by the Sobolev embedding theorem
[
1
, Chapter V, Theorem 5.4, Part II, p. 98]
.

We will employ the notation

\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]):=\mathcal{G}_{H^{\infty}({\Omega_{T}})}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]):=\mathcal{G}_{W^{\infty,\infty}({\Omega_{T}})}.

Thus, we will represent a generalized function
u\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T])
by a net
(u_{\varepsilon})
with the moderateness property

\forall k\,\exists m:\quad{\left\|u_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{H^{k}}=O(\varepsilon^{-m})\quad(\varepsilon\to 0).

If
(\widetilde{u_{\varepsilon}})
is another representative of
u
, then

\forall k\,\forall p:\quad{\left\|u_{\varepsilon}-\widetilde{u_{\varepsilon}}\right\|}_{H^{k}}=O(\varepsilon^{p})\quad(\varepsilon\to 0).

Similar constructions and notations are used in case of
E=H^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
E=W^{\infty,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Note that by Young’s inequality (
[
20

and
L^{p}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
(

).
