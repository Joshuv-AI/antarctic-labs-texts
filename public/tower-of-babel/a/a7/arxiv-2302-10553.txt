An inverse problem for data-driven prediction in quantum mechanics
Pedro Caro, Alberto Ruiz

Abstract
. Data-driven prediction in quantum mechanics consists in providing an approximative description of the motion of any particles at any given time, from data that have been previously collected for a certain number of particles under the influence of the same Hamiltonian. The difficulty of this problem comes from the ignorance of the exact Hamiltonian ruling the dynamic. In order to address this problem, we formulate an inverse problem consisting in determining the Hamiltonian of a quantum system from the knowledge of the state at some fixed finite time for each initial state. We focus on the simplest case where the Hamiltonian is given by − Δ + V -\Delta+V , where the potential V = V ⁡ ( t , x ) V=V(\mathrm{t},\mathrm{x}) is non-compactly supported. Our main result is a uniqueness theorem, which establishes that the Hamiltonian ruling the dynamic of all quantum particles is determined by the prescription of the initial and final states of each particle. As a consequence, one expects to be able to know the state of any particle at any given time, without an a priori knowledge of the Hamiltonian just from the data consisting of the initial and final state of each particle.



1.
Introduction

In quantum mechanics, the state of some moving particles
during an interval of time

[0,T]
is described by the family of wavefunctions

\{u(t,\centerdot):t\in[0,T]\}
, here

u(t,\centerdot)
denotes the function that takes the value
u(t,x)\in\mathbb{C}
at
the point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If the motion takes place
under the influence of an electric potential
V=V(\mathrm{t},\mathrm{x})
and the initial
state
u(0,\centerdot)
is prescribed by
f
, then the family of
wavesfunctions satisfies
the initial-value problem for the Schrödinger equation

(1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}u=-\Delta u+Vu&&\textnormal{in}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
&u(0,\centerdot)=f&&\textnormal{in}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{aligned}\right.

Thus, given an initial state
f
we predict the motion of these
quantum particles in the presence of
V
by solving (
1
).
This classic approach
requires the exact knowledge of the Hamiltonian
-\Delta+V
governing
the dynamic to predict the evolution of the system.
For this reason, it is usually referred to as

phenonmenology-driven prediction
.

In the past two decades, we have witnessed how the use of data has become a
game-changer for innovation and the development of new technology. Thus, it
is natural to investigate how data can be used to predict the evolution of
physical systems. For this reason, data-driven
approximation of solutions of partial differential equations is emerging as a
powerful paradigm
[
1
,
10
]
.

In this paper, we formulate a
data-driven prediction problem in quantum mechanics
and propose an inverse problem to provide a theoretical framework that explains
why one can solve the initial value problem (
1
)
ignoring the exact Hamiltonian if enough data,
regarding initial and final states, is available.

In order to formulate the data-driven prediction problem in quantum mechanics,
we introduce the family of operators

\{\mathcal{U}_{t}:t\in[0,T]\}

that, for every initial state
f
, is given by

u(t,\centerdot)=\mathcal{U}_{t}f

for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
u
solves (
1
);
and for
N\in\mathbb{N}
we define the

initial-to-final-state set with
N
elements

\mathcal{D}=\{(f_{1},\mathcal{U}_{T}f_{1}),\dots,(f_{N},\mathcal{U}_{T}f_{N})\}.

Then, the
data-driven approximate prediction problem

consists in analysing when it is possible
to compute, from the initial-to-final-state set
\mathcal{D}
,
a suitable approximation —as
N
increases— of the wavefunctions

u(t,\centerdot)=\mathcal{U}_{t}f
, for all
t\in[0,T]
and all initial
state
f
.
Note that this formulation assumes
that the Hamiltonian has a particular structure but ignores a full description of
the electric potential
V
. The formulation of this problem is motivated by
applications, where data will be given by the set
\mathcal{D}
with a very large

N
. There are many non-trivial challenges around this question,
however, in this article we focus on an exact version of this problem which will
establish a robust theoretical framework to address the approximate problem.

Recall that whenever
V\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
we have
that, for every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, there exists a unique

u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
solving (
1
) —see for example

[
11
,
8
]
.
Moreover,
the evolution map

\mathcal{U}:f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mapsto u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

is linear and bounded, and consequently we have that

\mathcal{U}_{t}:f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mapsto u(t,\centerdot)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

is also bounded for all
t\in[0,T]
.
The solutions of the form
u=\mathcal{U}f
with
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are called

physical
, while for the final time
T

the operator
\mathcal{U}_{T}
is referred to as
the
initial-to-final-state map
.
Note that the initial-to-final-state set
\mathcal{D}
consists of
N
points
in the graph of
\mathcal{U}_{T}
:

\graph(\mathcal{U}_{T})=\{(f,\mathcal{U}_{T}f):f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

Then, the
data-driven prediction problem

in quantum mechanics consists in computing the evolution map

\mathcal{U}
from the initial-to-final-state map

\mathcal{U}_{T}
.

A fundamental step to solve the data-driven prediction problem is to
show that the initial-to-final-state map uniquely determines the evolution map.
The main result of this article is to show that this is the case whenever

n\geq 2
and the electric potential
satisfies an a priori assumptions about its
decay at infinity.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the open subset

(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We say that

V\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
has
super-exponential decay
if

e^{\rho|\mathrm{x}|}V\in L^{2}(\Sigma)\cap L^{\infty}(\Sigma)
for all
\rho>0
.

The super-exponential decay is sufficient to ensure that the initial-to-final-state map uniquely determines the evolution map.

Theorem 1
.

Consider
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
in
L^{1}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with
n\geq 2
.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{T}^{j}
and
\mathcal{U}^{j}
with
j\in\{1,2\}
denote
the initial-to-final-state and evolution maps associated to the Hamiltonian

-\Delta+V_{j}
. If

V_{1}
and
V_{2}
have super-exponential decay, then

\mathcal{U}_{T}^{1}=\mathcal{U}_{T}^{2}\,\Rightarrow\,\mathcal{U}^{1}=\mathcal{U}^{2}.

Our approach to solve the data-driven problem consists in analysing an
inverse problem whose goal is to determine uniquely
the potential
V
from its corresponding initial-to-final-state map

\mathcal{U}_{T}
, and reconstructing
V
from
\mathcal{U}_{T}
whenever
this unique determination holds. It is straightforward to see that solving the

initial-to-final-state inverse problem
is sufficient to solve
the data-driven problem.

In this paper we prove a uniqueness theorem,
for dimension
n\geq 2
, that consists in
determining the electric potential
V
with super-exponential decay, from the
knowledge of the final state for each initial
state.

Theorem 2
.

Consider
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
in
L^{1}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with
n\geq 2
.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{T}^{j}
with
j\in\{1,2\}
denote
the initial-to-final-state map associated to the Hamiltonian

-\Delta+V_{j}
. If

V_{1}
and
V_{2}
have super-exponential decay, then

\mathcal{U}_{T}^{1}=\mathcal{U}_{T}^{2}\,\Rightarrow\,V_{1}=V_{2}.

The
1
is a straight
forward consequence of the
2
.
Furthermore, note that a reconstruction algorithm based on the proof of our

2
would yield a way of computing the evolution map

\mathcal{U}
from the initial-to-final-state map
\mathcal{U}_{T}
, which would
solve the data-driven prediction problem in its exact form.
Furthermore, such a reconstruction algorithm would
open the possibility to construct quantum Hamiltonians that would make many
different initial states end at once in some specific final states
—whenever these initial and final states belong to
\graph(\mathcal{U}_{T})

for some
V
.

The scheme of the proof of the
2
is as follows.
From the equality
\mathcal{U}_{T}^{1}=\mathcal{U}_{T}^{2}

we derive an orthogonality relation, that reads

\int_{\Sigma}(V_{1}-V_{2})u_{1}\overline{v_{2}}\,=0

for all physical solutions
u_{1}
and
v_{2}
in
C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

of the equations

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta-V_{1})u_{1}=0\kern 5.0pt\textnormal{and}\kern 5.0pt(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta-\overline{V_{2}})v_{2}=0\kern 5.0pt\textnormal{in}\kern 5.0pt\Sigma.

With this identity at hand, and inspired by the method of complex geometrical
optics (CGO for short) solutions for the Calderón problem, we aim to construct
solutions of the form

u_{1}=e^{\varphi_{1}}(u_{1}^{\sharp}+u_{1}^{\flat}),\qquad v_{2}=e^{\varphi_{2}}(v_{2}^{\sharp}+v_{2}^{\flat}),

with
\varphi_{1}(t,x)+\overline{\varphi_{2}(t,x)}=0

for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the equations

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta-V_{1}^{\rm ext})u_{1}=0\kern 5.0pt\textnormal{and}\kern 5.0pt(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta-\overline{V_{2}^{\rm ext}})v_{2}=0\kern 5.0pt\textnormal{in}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

where

(2)

V_{j}^{\rm ext}(t,x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &V_{j}(t,x)&&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
&0&&(t,x)\notin\Sigma.\end{aligned}\right.

The solutions
u_{1}
and
v_{2}
will be parametrized by

\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\nu|\geq\rho_{0}
so that
the real parts of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
satisfy

\Re\varphi_{1}=-\Re\varphi_{2}=-\nu\cdot\mathrm{x}
,
and the inequalities

\|u_{1}^{\sharp}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\nu}_{<R})}+\|v_{2}^{\sharp}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\nu}_{<R})}\lesssim(TR)^{1/2}

and

(3)

\|u_{1}^{\flat}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\nu}_{<R})}+\|v_{2}^{\flat}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\nu}_{<R})}\lesssim\frac{(TR)^{1/2-\theta}}{|\nu|^{1/2}}

hold for all
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\nu|\geq\rho_{0}>0
, where

\Sigma^{\nu}_{<R}=(0,T)\times\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x\cdot\nu|<|\nu|R\}

and
\theta\in(0,1/2)
. The functions
u_{1}^{\sharp}
and
v_{2}^{\sharp}
will
depend on
\hat{\nu}=\nu/|\nu|\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
Thus, if plugging our CGO solutions
into the orthogonality relation were allowed, we
would conclude, by making
|\nu|
go to infinity, that

\int_{\Sigma}(V_{1}-V_{2})u_{1}^{\sharp}\overline{v_{2}^{\sharp}}\,=0\qquad\forall\hat{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

Then, performing a density argument for all possible products

u_{1}^{\sharp}\overline{v_{2}^{\sharp}}
we would prove that

V_{1}(t,x)=V_{2}(t,x)
for almost every
(t,x)\in\Sigma
.

This approach presents two non-trivial challenges: first to construct the CGO
solutions for the Schrödinger equation satisfying the above properties
—including that

\{u_{1}^{\sharp}\overline{v_{2}^{\sharp}}:\hat{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\}

is somehow dense—,
and then showing that the orthogonality relation is satisfied
for CGO solutions —note that their restrictions to
\Sigma
grow exponentially
in certain regions of the space and, in consequence, they are not physical.

We will see in the
section

2
that in order to construct the CGO
solutions we only need

(4)

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}2^{j(1/2+\theta)}\|V_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\nu}_{j})}<\infty

for all
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
\theta\in(0,1/2)
as in the
inequality (
3
).
Here and throughout the article
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
. Additionally,
for
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, the sequence
of strips
\{\Sigma^{\nu}_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\subset\Sigma

in the direction of
\nu
are given by

\Sigma^{\nu}_{j}=(0,T)\times\Omega^{\nu}_{j}

with

\Omega^{\nu}_{0}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x\cdot\nu|\leq|\nu|\},\quad\Omega^{\nu}_{j}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:2^{j-1}|\nu|<|x\cdot\nu|\leq 2^{j}|\nu|\}\kern 5.0pt\forall j\in\mathbb{N}.

The condition (
4
)
is substantially weaker than having a super-exponential decay. The
super-exponential decay is only used to extend the orthogonal
identity from physical solutions to CGO solutions.
We have not analysed the necessity of this condition to prove the uniqueness
property of the initial-to-final-state inverse problem. However, we think that
it is interesting to study if the super-exponential decay could be
replaced by a weaker exponential decay
(see
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
).

As we have mentioned earlier, in order to solve the data-prediction problem in
quantum mechanics seems crucial to derive a reconstruction algorithm to compute

V
from its corresponding initial-to-final-state map
\mathcal{U}_{T}
.
This question does not seem trivial in the light of our analysis:
on the one hand, CGO solutions conform an essential tool, while on the other hand,
they cannot be used as pieces of data because they are not physical.
Fortunately, part of our analysis to extend the orthogonality identity to CGO
solutions can be understood as a sort of density property, or approximation of
CGO by physical solutions.

It is known that solutions for the Schrödinger equation has
a unique continuation property whenever the initial and final states satisfy a
condition known as the Hardy uncertainty principle (see

[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
2
]
.)
We believe of capital importance to decide if the condition

\mathcal{U}_{T}^{1}=\mathcal{U}_{T}^{2}
can be replaced
by something on the spirit of the
Hardy uncertainty principle for the difference of
\mathcal{U}_{T}^{1}
and

\mathcal{U}_{T}^{2}
. Because of the property of weak unique continuation from the
boundary, a weaker condition connected to the Hardy uncertainty principle
can be thought of as a counterpart of
the partial Dirichlet and Neumann data measured
on the same open subset of the boundary for the Calderón problem
(see, for example,
the Calderón problem with local data in
[
9
]
).

2.
Complex geometrical optics solutions

In order to motivate our choice of CGO solutions for the Schrödinger equation
with a Hamiltonian
-\Delta+V
,
we start by discussing the set of exponential solutions solving the
free Schrödinger equation

(5)

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)e^{\varphi}=0\kern 5.0pt\textnormal{in}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

A simple computation shows that (
5
) holds if and only if

(6)

i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}\varphi+\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\varphi+\Delta\varphi=0\kern 5.0pt\textnormal{in}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

To simplify the search of solutions of the previous equation, we restrict to
linear phases:

\varphi(t,x)=\gamma\,t+\zeta\cdot x\qquad\forall(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

with
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. The equation
(
6
) is equivalent to

i\gamma+\zeta\cdot\zeta=0

for
\varphi
linear, or equivalently,

\Im\gamma=|\Re\zeta|^{2}-|\Im\zeta|^{2},\qquad\Re\gamma=-2\Re\zeta\cdot\Im\zeta.

Among the exponential solutions of the free Schrödinger equation with linear
phases, we are going to select two types of solutions: the first type is

(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto e^{-i|\kappa|^{2}t+i\kappa\cdot x}

with
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, while the second type is

(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto e^{i|\nu|^{2}t+\nu\cdot x}

with
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Note that the first type of solutions
is unimodular and belongs to the
tempered distributions
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
while the second type has certain exponential
growth and only belongs to
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Let us explain the implications that
these different behaviours have for our purposes.

The product of solutions of the first type are
dense
:
If
F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies that

(7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(t,x)\,e^{-i|\kappa_{1}|^{2}t+i\kappa_{1}\cdot x}\,\overline{e^{-i|\kappa_{2}|^{2}t+i\kappa_{2}\cdot x}}\,\mathrm{d}(t,x)=0\qquad\forall\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

then
F(t,x)=0
for almost every
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Indeed, start by noticing that (
7
) is equivalent to
state that the Fourier transform of
F
satisfies

(8)

\widehat{F}(|\kappa_{1}|^{2}-|\kappa_{2}|^{2},\kappa_{2}-\kappa_{1})=0\qquad\forall\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Given
(\tau,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
\xi\neq 0
, we choose

(9)

\kappa_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\frac{\tau}{|\xi|^{2}}\Big)\xi,\qquad\kappa_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1-\frac{\tau}{|\xi|^{2}}\Big)\xi,

to obtain that
\widehat{F}(\tau,\xi)=0
. Additionally,
if
(\tau,\xi)=(0,0)
we choose
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=0
, and we obtain that

\widehat{F}(0,0)=0
. Consequently,
\widehat{F}
vanishes in

\{(\tau,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:\xi\neq 0\}\cup\{(0,0)\}
.
Since the
\widehat{F}
is
continuous in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
we can conclude that
\widehat{F}(\tau,\xi)=0

for all
(\tau,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since the Fourier transform is
injective, we have that
F(t,x)=0
for a.e.
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

This density property also holds for the product of
physical solutions of the free Schrödinger equation

\{u\in C(\mathbb{R};L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})):u=e^{i\mathrm{t}\Delta}f,\kern 5.0ptf\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

since these are superpositions of solutions of the first type.
More precisely, if
F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

satisfies that

(10)

\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}F\,e^{i\mathrm{t}\Delta}f_{1}\,\overline{e^{i\mathrm{t}\Delta}f_{2}}\,=0\qquad\forall f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

then
F(t,x)=0
for a.e.
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Indeed, we can write

\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}F\,e^{i\mathrm{t}\Delta}f_{1}\,\overline{e^{i\mathrm{t}\Delta}f_{2}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\widehat{f_{1}}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{f_{2}}(\eta)}\widehat{F}(|\xi|^{2}-|\eta|^{2},\xi-\eta)\,\mathrm{d}(\xi,\eta).

Then, by the fact that

\{g_{1}\otimes g_{2}:g_{j}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

is dense in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
the identity (
10
) is equivalent to

\widehat{F}(|\xi|^{2}-|\eta|^{2},\eta-\xi)=0\qquad\forall\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This is the exact same condition as (
8
). Hence,
F(t,x)=0

for a.e.
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In the argument above we have used the
notation
g_{1}\otimes g_{2}(x,y)=g_{1}(x)g_{2}(y)
for all

(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

After these observations, it seems natural to consider the CGO solutions
in the form

u=e^{i\mathrm{t}\Delta}f+v,

where
v
is a correction to make
u
solution for the Schrödinger equation
with Hamiltonian
-\Delta+V
. However, in order to apply the density of
product of physical solutions, we need to be able to ensure that the
contribution of the correction term
v

with respect to that of
e^{i\mathrm{t}\Delta}f
is negligible.
To do so, we introduce the exponential scale of the second type of solutions
since they will make the correction term to be negligible. The intuition behind
this claim is the following: Let us write

(11)

\varphi(t,x)=i|\nu|^{2}t+\nu\cdot x\qquad\forall(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

with
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and note that

e^{-\varphi}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)[e^{\varphi}w]=(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta+2\nu\cdot\nabla)w

for every
w\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Furthermore, by Plancherel’s
theorem we have that

\|e^{-\varphi}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)[e^{\varphi}w]\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}=\|(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+4\|\nu\cdot\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}.

Recall that Poincaré’s inequality tells that there exists an absolute constant

C>0
so that

|\nu|\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq CR\|\nu\cdot\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}

whenever

(12)

\supp w\subset\mathbb{R}\times\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\nu\cdot x|<|\nu|R\}.

Thus, the exponential scale introduced by
e^{\varphi}
ensures that
there exists an absolute constant

C>0
so that

|\nu|\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq CR\|(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta+2\nu\cdot\nabla)w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}

whenever the condition (
12
) holds. This yields a gain of

|\nu|
for
\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.
The reason behind this gain comes from the fact that the exponential conjugation

e^{-\varphi}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)\circ e^{\varphi}=i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta+2\nu\cdot\nabla

gives as a result a perturbation of
i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta
whose symbol has an
imaginary part that grows as
|\nu|
.
As we will see later, a gain in the same spirit
allows us to construct a correction term
that becomes negligible when
|\nu|
is large.

Now that we know that the second type of exponential solutions will be also
useful, we need to make them go along with the physical solutions: Notice that

e^{-\varphi}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)[e^{\varphi}e^{i\mathrm{t}\Delta}f]=0\Leftrightarrow\nu\cdot\nabla(e^{i\mathrm{t}\Delta}f)=0.

Thus, the solutions of the free Schrödinger equation that we are going to
consider are
e^{\varphi}u^{\sharp}
with
\varphi
as in (
11
)
and

(13)

u^{\sharp}(t,x)=[e^{it\Delta^{\prime}}\psi](Qe_{1}\cdot x,\dots,Qe_{n-1}\cdot x)\qquad\forall(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
-\Delta^{\prime}
is the free Hamiltonian in

\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
and
Q\in\mathrm{O}(n)
—the orthogonal
group— such that
\nu=|\nu|Qe_{n}
.
It is convenient to state that
u^{\sharp}
is not a physical solution:
Note that it is constant in the direction of
\nu
, it satisfies

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta+2\nu\cdot\nabla)u^{\sharp}=0\kern 5.0pt\textnormal{in}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

and there exists an absolute constant
C>0
so that

(14)

\|u^{\sharp}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{j}\times\Omega^{\nu}_{k})}\leq C2^{(j+k)/2}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})},

for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\|Sg\|_{L^{2}(I_{\alpha_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times J_{\alpha_{2}})}\lesssim|\nu|^{1+1/2}2^{|\alpha|\theta}\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}}2^{|\beta|(1/2-\theta)}\|g\|_{L^{2}(I_{\beta_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times J_{\beta_{2}})}.

Then, the identities in (

Proof of the

.

Since the partitions

(

Sf_{\alpha}(t,x^{\prime},x_{n})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{ix^{\prime}\cdot\xi^{\prime}}e^{it|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}}T[g_{\alpha}(\centerdot;\xi^{\prime})](t,x_{n})\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime}

In the

(34)

Lemma 3.3

Proof.

Consider
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

its

4.
The intial-to-final-state map

4.1.
An integral identity for physical solutions

The main goal here is to prove an integral formula that relates the
initial-to-final-state maps with their corresponding potentials. The
proof is based on a suitable integration-by-parts identity. We end this section
by generalizing that integration-by-parts identity.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be

L^{1}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with
T>0
.
For every
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have that

(39)

i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\mathcal{U}_{T}^{1}-\mathcal{U}_{T}^{2})f\,\overline{g}\;=\int_{\Sigma}(V_{1}-V_{2})u_{1}\overline{v_{2}}\,,

where
u_{1}
is the solution of (
1
) with potential
V_{1}
and initial
data
f
, while
v_{2}
is the physical solution of the following
final-value problem

(40)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}v_{2}=-\Delta v_{2}+\overline{V_{2}}v_{2}&&\textnormal{in}\,\Sigma,\\
&v_{2}(T,\centerdot)=g&&\textnormal{in}\,\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{aligned}\right.

In order to prove this proposition, we need the following integration-by-parts
formula.

Proposition 4.2
.

For every

u,v\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

such that
(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u
and
(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v

belong to
L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, we have

\int_{\Sigma}\big[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u\overline{v}-u\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}\big]\,=i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big[u(T,\centerdot)\overline{v(T,\centerdot)}-u(0,\centerdot)\overline{v(0,\centerdot)}\big]\,.

In order to focus first on how to relate the
initial-to-final-state maps with the corresponding potentials, we postpone the
justification of the integration-by-parts formula after proving
the
proposition

4.1
.

Proof of the
proposition

4.1
.

Since
u_{1}
and
v_{2}
solve the initial-value problem (
1
)
and final-value problem (
40
),
one has that the right-hand side of
(
39
) is equal to

\int_{\Sigma}\big[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u_{1}\overline{v_{2}}-u_{1}\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v_{2}}\big]\,.

Since
u_{1},v_{2}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, and
the potentials
V_{1}
and
\overline{V_{2}}
belong to the space

L^{1}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
,
the
proposition

4.2
yields

(41)

\int_{\Sigma}(V_{1}-V_{2})u_{1}\overline{v_{2}}\,=i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big[u_{1}(T,\centerdot)\overline{v_{2}(T,\centerdot)}-u_{1}(0,\centerdot)\overline{v_{2}(0,\centerdot)}\big]\,.

Now we introduce a third solution
u_{2}
of the initial-value problem (
1
) with potential
V_{2}
and initial data
f
.
If, in the computations to obtain (
41
), we replace
V_{1}
and
u_{1}
by
V_{2}
and
u_{2}
respectively, we can conclude that

(42)

i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big[u_{2}(T,\centerdot)\overline{v_{2}(T,\centerdot)}-u_{2}(0,\centerdot)\overline{v_{2}(0,\centerdot)}\big]=0.

Subtracting the left-hand side of (
42
) to the right-hand side of (
41
), we get

\int_{\Sigma}(V_{1}-V_{2})u_{1}\overline{v_{2}}\,=i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big[u_{1}(T,\centerdot)-u_{2}(T,\centerdot)\big]\overline{v_{2}(T,\centerdot)}

since
u_{1}(0,\centerdot)=u_{2}(0,\centerdot)
. Rewriting this identity in terms of the
\mathcal{U}_{T}^{1}f
,
\mathcal{U}_{T}^{2}f
and
g
, we prove (
39
).
∎

Proof of the
proposition

4.2
.

Start by noticing that under the assumptions of the

proposition

4.2
, we can apply the dominate convergence theorem
to ensure that

(43)

\int_{\Sigma}\big[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u\overline{v}-u\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}\big]\,=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\Sigma_{\delta}}\big[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u\overline{v}-u\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}\big]

where
\Sigma_{\delta}=(\delta,T-\delta)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The idea now is to approximate
u
and
v
by
u_{\varepsilon}
and

v_{\varepsilon}
respectively, which will be smooth in
\Sigma_{\delta}
and
compactly supported in space.

Consider a smooth cut-off
\chi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

such that
0\leq\chi(x)\leq 1
for
all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\supp\chi\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x|\leq 2\}
and

\chi(x)=1
whenever
|x|\leq 1
. Consider
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with compact supports and such that

\supp\psi\subset\{t\in\mathbb{R}:|t|\leq 1\}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi=1
and
\phi(x),\psi(t)\in[0,\infty)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Whenever
\varepsilon>0
, define

\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\chi(\varepsilon x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-n}\phi(x/\varepsilon)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and

\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)=\varepsilon^{-1}\psi(t/\varepsilon)

for
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

For
w\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
\varepsilon<\delta
,
we consider

w_{\varepsilon}:(t,x)\in\Sigma_{\delta}\mapsto\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)\int_{(0,T)}\psi_{\varepsilon}(t-s)\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi_{\varepsilon}(x-y)w(s,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s.

One can check that

(44)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|w(t,\centerdot)-w_{\varepsilon}(t,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=0\qquad\forall t\in[\delta,T-\delta].

Furthermore, if

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

(45)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w-(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}((\delta,T-\delta);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=0.

Let us give an explanation about (
45
).
For notational convenience, we write

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)

for
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
\tilde{w}
denote the
trivial extension of

\tilde{w}(t,\centerdot)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &w(t,\centerdot)&&t\in[0,T],\\
&0&&t\notin[0,T].\end{aligned}\right.

Thus, we have that

w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{w})(t,x)

for all
(t,x)\in\Sigma_{\delta}
.
In order to show that (
45
) holds,
let us compute

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w_{\varepsilon}=\chi_{\varepsilon}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{w})+2\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{w})+\Delta\chi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{w})\kern 5.0pt\textnormal{in}\kern 5.0pt\Sigma_{\delta}.

Thus, (
45

(46)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w-\chi_{\varepsilon}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{w})\|_{L^{1}((\delta,T-\delta);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=0

and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Big(\|\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{w})\|_{L^{1}((\delta,T-\delta);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|\Delta\chi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{w})\|_{L^{1}((\delta,T-\delta);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\Big)=0.

We just make a comment on how to prove (
46

By the triangle inequality

(47)

\displaystyle\|(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w-\chi_{\varepsilon}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}

\displaystyle+\Delta)(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{w})\|_{L^{1}((\delta,T-\delta);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w-\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)\tilde{w}]\|_{L^{1}((\delta,T-\delta);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}

\displaystyle+\|(1-\chi_{\varepsilon})(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w\|_{L^{1}((\delta,T-\delta);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}.

To write the first term on the right-hand side as above, we have used

\|\chi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1
, and

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{w})=\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)\tilde{w}]
.
It is obvious that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|(1-\chi_{\varepsilon})(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w(t,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=0\kern 5.0pt\textnormal{for almost every}\,t\in(\delta,T-\delta).

Hence, by the dominate convergence theorem,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|(1-\chi_{\varepsilon})(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w\|_{L^{1}((\delta,T-\delta);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=0.

To conclude that (
46
) holds, we still have to analyse the
first term on the right-hand side of the inequality (
47
).
Note that since
\varepsilon<\delta
, we have that

\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)\tilde{w}](t,x)=\int_{\Sigma}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t-s,x-y)[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w](s,y)\,\mathrm{d}(s,y)\quad\textnormal{a.e.}\,(t,x)\in\Sigma_{\delta}.

Hence, one can check by using standard arguments that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)w-\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)\tilde{w}]\|_{L^{1}((\delta,T-\delta);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=0.

Therefore, (
45
) holds.

We now apply these results for the corresponding

u_{\varepsilon}
and
v_{\varepsilon}
.
By the standard integration-by-parts rules we have that

\int_{\Sigma_{\delta}}\big[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u_{\varepsilon}\overline{v_{\varepsilon}}-u_{\varepsilon}\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v_{\varepsilon}}\big]\,=i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big[u_{\varepsilon}(T-\delta,\centerdot)\overline{v_{\varepsilon}(T-\delta,\centerdot)}-u_{\varepsilon}(\delta,\centerdot)\overline{v_{\varepsilon}(\delta,\centerdot)}\big]\,.

After (
44
) and (
45
) we can conclude,
by the dominate convergence theorem, that

\int_{\Sigma_{\delta}}\big[(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u\overline{v}-u\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}\big]\,=i\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big[u(T-\delta,\centerdot)\overline{v(T-\delta,\centerdot)}-u(\delta,\centerdot)\overline{v(\delta,\centerdot)}\big]\,.

The limits (

) and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big[u(T,\centerdot)\overline{v(T,\centerdot)}-u(0,\centerdot)\overline{v(0,\centerdot)}\big]=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big[u(T-\delta,\centerdot)\overline{v(T-\delta,\centerdot)}-u(\delta,\centerdot)\overline{v(\delta,\centerdot)}\big]

yield the identity in the statement.
∎

We end this section by proving a generalization of the integration-by-parts
formula that will be useful at a later stage.

Proposition 4.3
.

For every
u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
such that

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
and

u(0,\centerdot)=u(T,\centerdot)=0
,
and every

such that

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma})
with

\tilde{\Sigma}=(-\delta,T+\delta)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\delta>0
, we have

\int_{\Sigma}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u\overline{v}=\int_{\Sigma}u\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}\,.

Proof.

Consider
\tilde{u}\in C([-\delta,T+\delta];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

the trivial extension of
u
given by

\tilde{u}(t,x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &u(t,x)&&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
&0&&(t,x)\in\tilde{\Sigma}\setminus\Sigma.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that

\langle(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)\tilde{u},\phi\rangle=\int_{\Sigma}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u\,\phi

for all
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\Sigma})
, which implies that

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)\tilde{u}\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma})
.

For
\varepsilon\in(0,\delta)
, define

u_{\varepsilon}:(t,x)\in\tilde{\Sigma}\mapsto\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)[\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{u}](t,x)

with
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)

for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\chi_{\varepsilon}
,
\phi_{\varepsilon}

and
\psi_{\varepsilon}
as in the proof of the
proposition

.
Then,

\int_{\Sigma}u\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}\,=\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}}(\tilde{u}-u_{\varepsilon})\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}\,+\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}}u_{\varepsilon}\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}

with

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\tilde{u}-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma})}=0.

This implies that

(48)

\int_{\Sigma}u\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}\,=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}}u_{\varepsilon}\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}.

Since

, we have by the
distributional definition of

that

(49)

\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}}u_{\varepsilon}\overline{(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)v}\,=\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u_{\varepsilon}\overline{v}\,.

Additionally,

\displaystyle(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u_{\varepsilon}=\chi_{\varepsilon}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)[\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{u}]+2\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{u})+\Delta\chi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{u}).

Since

(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)[\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{u}]=\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)\tilde{u}

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)\tilde{u}-\chi_{\varepsilon}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)[\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{u}]\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma})}=0.

One can check that

(50)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Big(\|2\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{u})\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma})}+\|\Delta\chi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ast\tilde{u})\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma})}\Big)=0.

Since
v\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma})
, we have that

(51)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u_{\varepsilon}\overline{v}\,=\int_{\Sigma}(i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}+\Delta)u\overline{v}\,.

Taking limits as
\varepsilon
goes to zero at each side of the identity
(
49

48

(

∎

It is convenient to recall the

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} &i\partial_{\mathrm{t}}v=-\Delta v+\overline{V}v&&\textnormal{in}\,\Sigma,\\
&v(T,\centerdot)=g&&\textnormal{in}\,\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

Consider

, and let

of the corresponding final-value problem. Then, by the

and

Hence,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(T,\centerdot)\overline{g}\,=0

Since

Lemma 4.7
