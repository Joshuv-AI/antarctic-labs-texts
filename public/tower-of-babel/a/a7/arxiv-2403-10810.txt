Global Existence for an Isotropic Landau Model
David Bowman and Sehyun Ji

Abstract
. Following the recent ideas of Guillen and Silvestre in [ 9 ] , we prove that the Fisher information is non-increasing along the flow of the isotropic Landau equation. We then use this fact to deduce global existence for the equation ∂ t f = ( − Δ ) − 1 ​ f ⋅ Δ ​ f + f 2 \partial_{t}f=(-\Delta)^{-1}f\cdot\Delta f+f^{2} under a relatively lax set of conditions on the initial data. In particular, we remove the restrictive radially decreasing assumption of previous works.



1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the global existence of smooth solutions to the isotropic Landau equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f=\partial_{v_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v-w|^{2+\gamma}(\partial_{v_{i}}-\partial_{w_{i}})[f(v)f(w)]\;\mathrm{d}w

for some parameter
\gamma\in[-3,-2]
and a non-negative function
f=f(t,v):[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
. We shall also be interested in the non-divergence form

(1.2)

\partial_{t}f=a[f]\Delta f-(2+\gamma)h[f]f

where

a[f]\coloneqq f\ast|\cdot|^{2+\gamma},\ \ \ h[f]\coloneqq\begin{cases}(3+\gamma)f\ast|\cdot|^{\gamma}&\gamma\in(-3,-2]\\
4\pi f&\gamma=-3.\end{cases}.

After a simple time rescaling, (
1.2
) in the most singular case
\gamma=-3
takes the form

(1.3)

\partial_{t}f=(-\Delta)^{-1}f\cdot\Delta f+f^{2}.

This was the equation initially proposed in
[
13
]
by Krieger and Strain, after whom we term (
1.1
) the
Krieger-Strain equation
, as a modification of the
Landau-Coulomb equation

(1.4)

\partial_{t}f=\partial_{ij}(-\Delta)^{-2}f\cdot\partial_{ij}f+f^{2}.

For this reason we refer to the case
\gamma=-3
of the Krieger-Strain equation as the
Coulomb
case. The Landau-Coulomb equation (
1.4
) is one of the classical equations of physics that has drawn a great deal of attention from the mathematical community over the last half of a century. A satisfying understanding of (
1.4
) seemed far out of reach until recently, when Guillen and Silvestre made an important breakthrough and resolved the question of global existence using an innovative and novel viewpoint for the equation, see
[
9
]
.

Our approach for analyzing (
1.1
) follows the new ideas from
[
9
]
. Taking their method as inspiration, our main tool for proving global existence of solutions to (
1.1
) is the Fisher information. For a positive function
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to(0,\infty)
, the Fisher information of
f
is given by

(1.5)

i(f)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{f}\;\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla\log f|^{2}f\;\mathrm{d}v=4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla\sqrt{f}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}v.

It can be extended for
f
with vacuum regions by defining
|\nabla f|^{2}/f=0
on those points. From the third form of the Fisher information in (
1.5
) and the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\subset L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, it is clear that control of
i(f)
implies the control of
\|f\|_{L^{3}}
.

One of the purposes of this paper is to extend the class of evolution equations whose solutions have monotone decreasing Fisher information, which is an extremely powerful tool for obtaining regularity estimates. At least in the folklore of analysis, it has been known for more than half a century that the Fisher information decreases along the flow of the heat equation, for instance see
[
22
]
.
Next, in the 1960s, McKean obtained results in the Maxwell Molecules case (corresponding to
\gamma=0
in (
1.1
)) for a one dimensional toy model of the Boltzmann equation known as the Kac equation, see
[
14
]
.
The next progress wasn’t until the 1990, when Toscani proved the monotonicity of the Fisher information for the
2D
Boltzmann equation with Maxwell Molecules
[
19
]
.
After a few years later, Villani proved the monotonicity in the Maxwell Molecules case of the Landau and Boltzmann equations
[
20
,
21
]
. Finally, very recently, Guillen and Silvestre extended the result to the Landau-Coulomb equation (
1.4
) and the broader range of the Landau equation (
1.7
). See
[
9
]
and the references therein for further discussion of the history of the Fisher information. Overall, very few equations are known to decrease the Fisher information. We prove the following theorem, which expands this class of equations to include (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
be a classical solution with rapid decay to the equation

(1.6)

\partial_{t}f=\partial_{v_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\alpha(|v-w|)|v-w|^{2}(\partial_{v_{i}}-\partial_{w_{i}})[f(v)f(w)]\;\mathrm{d}w

where
\alpha:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is an arbitrary interaction potential. Assume that
\alpha(\cdot)
satisfies

\frac{r\alpha^{\prime}(r)}{\alpha(r)}\in[2-3\sqrt{3},-2+2\sqrt{2}]\ \ \text{ for all }r>0.

Then the Fisher information
i(f)
is monotone decreasing as a function of time. In particular, taking
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}
, we recover the monotonicity of the Fisher information for solutions to (
1.1
) for
\gamma\in[2-3\sqrt{3},-2+2\sqrt{2}].

Note that
2-3\sqrt{3}\simeq-3.196
, and
-2+2\sqrt{2}\simeq 0.828.
It is only when studying the Fisher information that we consider the general interaction potential
\alpha(\cdot)
, which has a more compact notation. It is interesting to note that in the case
\gamma=-2
, the Krieger-Strain equation (
1.1
) is simply the heat equation, thanks to the conservation of
L^{1}
norm. Therefore one can view the above theorem as starting at the known result for the heat equation and extending to the more complicated Coulomb case (
1.3
).

We then seek to exploit the newfound control of
\|f(t)\|_{L^{3}}
to obtain a global existence result for (
1.1
). Let us focus our discussion on the Coulomb case. Whether (
1.3
) should have smooth solutions has not always been so clear. The equation bears resemblance to the semilinear heat equation with quadratic nonlinearity

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u+u^{2},

which is known to exhibit finite-time blowup. However, the diffusion operator in (
1.3
) seems to have a stronger regularization effect than the heat operator, which might prevent the emergence of a blow-up of the solution to (
1.3
). Indeed, around points where the mass of
f
may be accumulating, the diffusion coefficient
(-\Delta)^{-1}f
grows larger, competing with the reaction term
f^{2}
to drive the solution downward.
The primary contribution of our paper is to prove that in fact smooth solutions do exist globally.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f_{\text{in}}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
be an initial data that belongs to
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{1}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for sufficiently large
m>0
. Assume also that the initial data has finite Fisher information, i.e.,

i(f_{\text{in}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla\log f_{\text{in}}|^{2}f_{\text{in}}\;\mathrm{d}v<\infty.

Then there exists a classical global solution
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
to the Krieger-Strain equation (
1.1
) which is
C^{\infty}
and strictly positive for positive time
t>0
.

As we shall see, the uniform estimates on
\|f(t)\|_{L^{3}}
provided by Theorem
1.1
will allow us to apply the methods and results of Silvestre in
[
15
]
to obtain stronger ellipticity bounds on
(-\Delta)^{-1}f
, and thus
L^{\infty}
estimates on
f
. This is the crucial step towards preventing blow-up and proving the global existence of smooth solutions.

Up until now, the only global existence results for (
1.1
) have been either for radially symmetric and decreasing data. In fact, there is no pre-existing local existence theory or continuation criterion for the Krieger-Strain equation which does not make the strong structural assumptions of radial symmetry and monotonicity. In order to have a satisfying global existence theory, we also prove the following local existence theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that the initial data
f_{\text{in}}
is non-negative and that
f_{\text{in}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{1}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for some sufficiently large
m>0
.
Then there exists a classical solution
f:[0,T_{*})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
of the Krieger-Strain equation (
1.2
), attaining the initial data in the weak-
\ast

L^{\infty}
sense. Additionally, if we assume further that the Fisher information of the initial data is finite, i.e., that
i(f_{\text{in}})<\infty
, then
i(f(t))
exists for all
t\in[0,T_{*})
and is monotone decreasing as a function of time. Lastly, the maximal time of existence
T_{*}
is such that if
T_{*}<+\infty
, then

\lim_{t\nearrow T_{*}}\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}=+\infty.

The method is standard, following the approach of Golding and Loher
[
5
]
. First we establish a growth estimate on
\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
that depends only on
\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}}
. We then construct solutions for smooth and decaying data
f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}
(see Appendix
B
) which approximate the initial data
f_{\text{in}}.
Via standard regularity estimates, we are able to prove the solutions corresponding to the
\{f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\}
converge in a strong sense to a smooth solution
f
with the right initial data.

1.1.
History and Review of the Krieger-Strain equation

As previously mentioned, the Coulomb case of Krieger-Strain (
1.3
) was introduced as a modification of the celebrated Landau-Coulomb equation (
1.4
). In the same way, one may consider (
1.1
), or more generally (
1.6
), as a modification of the Landau equation

(1.7)

\partial_{t}f=\partial_{v_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\alpha(|v-w|)a_{ij}(v-w)(\partial_{v_{j}}-\partial_{w_{j}})[f(v)f(w)]\;\mathrm{d}w,

where
a_{ij}(z)=|z|^{2}\delta_{ij}-z_{i}z_{j}
is a weighted orthogonal projection onto
z^{\perp}
, and
\alpha(\cdot)
is a radial interaction potential, typically taken to be
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}
. There are several key differences that complicate the transference of results from the Landau equation to Krieger-Strain equation. The Landau equation features a conservation of the energy, defined below, while this is not the case with (
1.1
). A lack of any a priori higher moment estimates for the Krieger-Strain equation complicates the matter of obtaining unconditional
L^{\infty}
estimates following the method of Silvestre in
[
15
]
. To see this, let us define respectively the mass, energy, and entropy of a solution
f
at time
t
by

(1.8)

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,v)\;\mathrm{d}v,

(1.9)

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,v)|v|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}v,

(1.10)

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,v)\log f(t,v)\;\mathrm{d}v.

One may see in exactly the same manner that solutions to both the Landau and Krieger-Strain equations preserve mass (
\frac{d}{dt}M(t)=0
) and dissipate entropy (
\frac{d}{dt}H(t)\leq 0
), but energy is only conserved in the former case. In fact, the flow of (
1.1
) increases energy, which can be seen with the following simple computation:

(1.11)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}\partial_{t}f\;\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}\partial_{v_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v-w|^{2+\gamma}\partial_{v_{i}-w_{i}}(f(v)f(w))\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

(1.12)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2v_{i}|v-w|^{2+\gamma}\partial_{v_{i}-w_{i}}(f(v)f(w))\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

(1.13)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(v_{i}-w_{i})|v-w|^{2+\gamma}\partial_{v_{i}-w_{i}}(f(v)f(w))\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

(1.14)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(5+\gamma)|v-w|^{2+\gamma}f(v)f(w)\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

With the notation of (
1.2
), we find that the energy increases with rate

(1.16)

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=(5+\gamma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}a[f(t,v)]f(t,v)\;\mathrm{d}v,

a fact that was first observed in
[
7
]
. That there is an increase in energy is a departure from the Landau setting and poses an additional roadblock toward obtaining estimates on the solution. Heuristically, entropy dissipation implies that the
L^{1}
mass of the solution cannot concentrate on too small of a set, while the energy bound implies that the
L^{1}
mass may not escape to infinity. Both are necessary in order to prove ellipticity estimates on the diffusion coefficient
a[f]
. In Section
5
, the question of controlling the energy will be explored further, and in fact there shall be obtained higher moment estimates of all orders. We emphasize that these estimates will only made possible due to the monotonicity of the Fisher information from Theorem
1.1
and the
L^{3}
bound that it implies.

One of the major areas of investigation for the Landau equation (
1.7
) is the convergence to an equilibrium as
t\to+\infty
. The Landau equation features a rich family of stationary solutions of the form
\mathcal{M}(v)=a\exp(-b|v-u|^{2})
for some
a,b\geq 0,u\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Such steady states are called
Maxwellians
, and there is a rich history of papers exploring the convergence to Maxwellians of solutions to (
1.7
), see for instance
[
1
,
2
,
10
,
17
,
18
]
. The Krieger-Strain equation (
1.2
) has only one stationary solution:
f\equiv 0
. Let us briefly explore whether a solution
f(t,v)
to (
1.2
) may converge to the steady state of zero as
t\to+\infty
, and see how this question is connected to the increase of energy (
1.16
). If the energy
E(t)
stays bounded as
t\to\infty
(which is the case for solutions to the Landau equation), then it is impossible for the convergence

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t,v)=0

to take place point-wise almost everywhere. Indeed, the energy being bounded would provide uniform integrability of the family
\{f(t)\}_{t\geq 0}\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, which combined with pointwise convergence implies strong
L^{1}
convergence. This would contradict the conservation of mass
M(t)\equiv M(0)\neq 0
. In this way we may see that the long-time behavior for the Krieger-Strain equation can differ drastically from that of the Landau equation.

Lastly, we point out that when
\gamma\in(-2,1]
, the Krieger-Strain equation departs from the Landau equation. When written in non-divergence form (see
[
15
]
, the Landau equation features a reaction term of the form
c_{d,\gamma}[f\ast|\cdot|^{\gamma}]f
, where
c_{d,\gamma}>0
. The non-divergence form of the Krieger-Strain equation, on the other hand, features the reaction term
-(2+\gamma)(3+\gamma)[f\ast|\cdot|^{\gamma}]f
. For
\gamma>-2
, the reaction term becomes negative, and thus no longer drives the solution upwards. This is a dramatic qualitative difference that greatly simplifies the analysis. Still, we remark that all of the theorems developed in this paper may be extended to a larger range of
\gamma
, with very similar proofs. We elect to omit the analysis of this range of
\gamma
in order to have the cleanest presentation possible.

Now we review the history of the Krieger-Strain equation. In 2011, Krieger and Strain
[
13
]
introduced the equation

\partial_{t}f=(-\Delta)^{-1}f\cdot\Delta f+\delta f^{2},\ \ \ \delta\in[0,1]

in order to better understand the Landau-Coulomb equation, as previously mentioned. The point of the
\delta
was to discount the strength of the quadratic nonlinearity, which is known to cause blow-up for the semilinear heat equation. Here they proved global existence for radially symmetric, bounded, and decreasing data, for
\delta\in[0,\frac{2}{3}]
. Shortly after, Gressman, Krieger, and Strain improved the range to
\delta\in[0,\frac{74}{75}]
in
[
6
]
. Half a decade later, Gualdani and Guillen reach the goal of
\delta=1
, still in the radially decreasing setting. Finally, in 2021, Gualdani and Guillen were able to prove classical solutions to (
1.1
) remain smooth for as long as they exist, for
\gamma\in[-\frac{5}{2},-2]
, using a novel approach based on Hardy’s inequality and the propagation of certain
L^{p}
norms. While this contribution was notable in that it was the first time the radial symmetry condition was dropped, the Hardy’s inequality approach fails as
\gamma\to-3^{+}
. Finally, we call attention to the work of Gualdani and Zamponi in
[
8
]
, who managed to prove a global existence result for initial data which are merely bounded and even. With the exception of
[
7
]
, the existence results in the literature make weak assumptions on the regularity of the data, but strong assumptions on the structure. Noticeably absent from the literature has been a local existence theory for more generic initial data, or a continuation criterion for extending solutions.

We also point out the interesting work of Snelson on the isotropic Boltzmann equation
[
16
]
. In this work a global existence result was obtained for a modification of the Boltzmann equation which is analogous to the Krieger-Strain equation. Because the range of
\gamma
considered for the Boltzmann equation does not reach the particularly singular range
\gamma\simeq-3
, it was possible for Snelson to argue based on the Hardy’s inequality approach of Gualdani and Guillen in
[
7
]
. His approach has the same limitations, and so new ideas are needed.

1.2.
Outline of Paper and Main Ideas

The first half of the paper is devoted to showing the Fisher information decreases along the flow of the Krieger-Strain equation (
1.1
). Our method is inspired by the recent novel approach of Guillen and Silvestre
[
9
]
. Let us briefly explain their approach, as it is the starting point for ours.
The starting point is the observation that the Fisher information interacts nicely with tensor products
f\otimes f(v,w)\coloneqq f(v)f(w)
. Indeed, defining
\displaystyle I(F)\coloneqq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{|\nabla F|^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v
to be the
lifted
Fisher information for
F(v,w):\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}
, it holds for any probability density function
f
that

i(f)=\frac{1}{2}I(f\otimes f).

The authors of
[
9
]
then observe that it suffices to consider the Fisher information along the flow of the equation in doubled variables

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}F=Q_{L}(F),\\
F(0,v,w)=f\otimes f(v,w),\end{cases}

where
f
solves (
1.6
) and the lifted Landau collision operator
Q_{L}
is a linear, degenerate elliptic operator with
local
coefficients that can be explicitly computed. The simplified form of
Q_{L}
makes it considerably more advantageous to study. More details will be given in Section
2
. Continuing, they then decompose
Q_{L}(F)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}L_{k}\circ L_{k}(F)
, where the
L_{k}
are transport operators in directions parallel to the level sets of
|v-w|
. In other words, the flows of the operators
L_{k}
are rotations when restricted to level sets of
|v-w|
, but not necessarily when moving across the layers in the normal direction. A more precise description will be given in Section
2
. This new formulation allows for a sort of Lagrangian perspective on the Landau equation and the Fisher information, based on tracking the flows of particles transported by the
\{L_{k}\}
. With this viewpoint in mind they decompose the Fisher information with
I=I_{\text{tan}}+I_{n}
, where
I_{\text{tan}}
takes into account derivatives of
F
in directions tangent to the level sets of
|v-w|
, and
I_{n}
derivatives of
F
in the direction
n
normal to the level sets. At this point, they make the basic observation that the Fisher information is preserved by rotation. With this fact in mind, Guillen and Silvestre argue that from the perspective of
I_{\text{tan}}
, the flow of each
L_{k}
is rotational and therefore
I_{\text{tan}}
is constant along the flow of each
L_{k}
. By a simple convexity argument it is proven that
I_{\text{tan}}
decreases along the flow of the lifted Landau equation. A concrete estimate is obtained for its dissipation.

The majority of the analysis in
[
9
]
is dedicated to analyzing the Fisher information in the normal direction,
I_{n}
. There is no reason a priori to expect any monotonicity of
I_{n}
, as the flows of
L_{k}
are no longer isometric as one moves across layers in the normal direction. Performing an even more delicate decomposition, Guillen and Silvestre reduce the problem to proving a Poincare-like inequality on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, ultimately proving that the Fisher information is monotone decreasing for all radial interaction potentials
\alpha(\cdot)
with
\frac{|r\alpha^{\prime}(r)|}{\alpha(r)}\leq\sqrt{19}
.

Our approach for proving Theorem
1.1
is to make use of the analysis done in
[
9
]
. Performing the same lifting procedure for (
1.1
), we obtain that the “lifted” solutions satisfy an equation of the form

\partial_{t}F=Q_{L}(F)+R(F)

for some remainder operator
R
. The remainder operator
R
involves first and second order derivatives in the direction normal to the level sets of
|v-w|
. In the Landau setting, one must only account for one derivative in the normal direction, which is that coming from the definition of the Fisher information. The Landau operator itself does not involve derivatives normal to the level sets of
|v-w|
. In our case, the remainder operator
R
is particularly undesirable in that it involves first and second order derivatives in the normal direction. The entirety of our proof is spent showing the contribution of this term is not too disadvantageous. Through careful computation, we are able to show that the estimate from
[
9
]
is just barely strong enough to control the error term
R
in the Coulomb case of Krieger-Strain (
1.3
). In Section
2
, we set forth the groundwork for the lifting procedure, and in Section
3
we perform the computation in the simplest
Maxwell-Molecules
case (corresponding to
\gamma=0
in (
1.1
) or
\alpha\equiv 1
in (
1.6
)), which captures the spirit of the computation without the technicality of the general case. Finally, in Section
4
, we prove the Fisher information decreases for a wide range of interaction potentials, including (
1.3
).

Since the literature for the equation is relatively sparse, we must build from the ground up. The monotonicity of the Fisher information forms the foundation for everything that follows in the proof of global existence of solutions to (
1.1
). Sections
2-4
are dedicated to the analysis of the Fisher information. It is only in these sections that we use the framework of the lifting procedure outlined above. In Section
5
, we prove new growth estimates on the moments
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,v)|v|^{s}\;\mathrm{d}v
, which enables us to apply the methods of Silvestre
[
15
]
to obtain an a priori estimate on
\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
. In Section
6
we obtain delicate estimates on the propagation of Sobolev norms
\|f(t)\|_{H^{k}}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which will be crucial towards proving the solution does not form implosion singularities. Finally in Section
7
, we establish an
L^{\infty}\cap L^{1}_{m}
local existence theory, without making any further assumptions on the structure of the solution. In order to do this, we first construct solutions to (
1.2
) for initial data lying in
H^{3}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. This is merely an intermediate step whose proof is standard, so it is relegated to Appendix B. With all of these pieces put together, the global existence result follows straightforwardly in Section
8
. Finally, some key convolution and interpolation estimates may be found in Appendix A.

2.
Preliminary Set-up

We follow the approach of
[
9
]
by lifting (
1.6
) onto
\mathbb{R}^{6}
and decomposing it into the sum of squares of flows along certain vector fields, plus a first-order correction term. Let us quickly summarize the framework of
[
9
]
and recall the main lemmas. Given
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
, one may define the tensor product
F:\mathbb{R}^{3+3}\to\mathbb{R}
by

F(v,w)\coloneqq(f\otimes f)(v,w)=f(v)f(w)

which turns a scalar function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
into a scalar function on
\mathbb{R}^{3+3}
. One may go the other direction by projecting onto the marginal with

\pi F(v)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(v,w)\;\mathrm{d}w.

Let us recall from (
1.6
) that the Krieger-Strain equation with a general radial potential
\alpha(\cdot)
is written as

(2.1)

\partial_{t}f=\partial_{v_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\alpha(|v-w|)|v-w|^{2}(\partial_{v_{i}}-\partial_{w_{i}})[f(v)f(w)]\;\mathrm{d}w\coloneqq\mathcal{Q_{KS}}(f,f).

Ultimately we will concern ourselves with
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}
. The advantage of this formulation is that it allows to more easily compare with the form of the Landau equation (
1.7
) considered in
[
9
]
.

Now we perform the lifting procedure of Guillen and Silvestre for (
2.1
). First, we define the lifted Krieger-Strain collision operator

(2.2)

\displaystyle Q_{KS}(F)(v,w)\coloneqq(\partial_{v_{i}}-\partial_{w_{i}})[(\alpha(|v-w|)|v-w|^{2}(\partial_{v_{i}}-\partial_{w_{i}})]F(v,w)].

For a function
f(v)
, we consider the initial value problem

(2.3)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}F=Q_{KS}(F),\\
F(0,v,w)=f(v)f(w).\end{cases}

As in
[
9
]
, we have doubled the variables to obtain a linear equation with local and explicit coefficients.

The following sequence of lemmas is literally the exact same as in
[
9
]
. The point of the lemmas is to show that it suffices to consider the “lifted” Fisher information

I(F)\coloneqq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{|\nabla F|^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|\nabla\log F|^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v=4\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|\nabla\sqrt{F}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

along the flow of (

) in order to prove the Fisher information
i(f(\cdot))
decreases along the flow of (
1.1
). Only Lemmas
2.1
and
2.4
have anything to do with an equation, and the proofs are the exact same as in the Landau case.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any twice-differentiable function
f
which is nonnegative and has
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f=1
, the Krieger-Strain operator
\mathcal{Q}_{KS}
, defined as in (

), coincides with

\mathcal{Q}_{KS}(f,f)=\pi(Q_{KS}(F))=\partial_{t}[\pi F]_{\bigm|_{t=0}}

where

is the solution to (

).

The proof is the exact same as in
[

]
. Note that we can rescale the equation (

) to make
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f=1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any non-negative
C^{1}
function
f
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f=1
and
i(f)<\infty
, we have

i(f)=\frac{1}{2}I(f\otimes f).

Lemma 2.3
.

For any non-negative
C^{1}
function
F:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to[0,\infty)
with
F(v,w)=F(w,v)
, we have

i(\pi F)\leq\frac{1}{2}I(F).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f
be a solution of (
2.1
) and let
F
be a solution of (

), then

\partial_{t}i(f)\leq\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}I[F]_{\bigm|_{t=0}}

The preceding sequence of lemmas from
[
9
]
shows that it suffices to prove that the Fisher information
I[\cdot]
on
\mathbb{R}^{6}
decreases along the flow of the lifted equation (
2.3
). Our next order of business is to compare
Q_{KS}
with the operator
Q_{L}
obtained in
[
9
]
, since we shall use the analysis of
Q_{L}
extensively. First they obtain the expression

Q_{L}(F)\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{3}(\partial_{v_{i}}-\partial_{w_{i}})\alpha(|v-w|)(b_{k})_{i}(b_{k})_{j}\cdot(\partial_{v_{j}}-\partial_{w_{j}})F,

where the
b_{k}=b_{k}(v-w)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are given by

b_{1}(v-w)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
w_{3}-v_{3}\\
v_{2}-w_{2}\end{pmatrix},\ \ b_{2}(v-w)=\begin{pmatrix}v_{3}-w_{3}\\
0\\
w_{1}-v_{1}\end{pmatrix},\ \ \text{and }b_{3}(v-w)=\begin{pmatrix}w_{2}-v_{2}\\
v_{1}-w_{1}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

The vectors
\{b_{k}(v-w)\}
span
(v-w)^{\perp}
and satisfy the identity

\sum_{k=1}^{3}b_{k}\otimes b_{k}=a_{ij}(v-w),

where
a_{ij}(v-w)=|v-w|^{2}\delta_{ij}-(v_{i}-w_{i})(v_{j}-w_{j})
as in (
1.7
). In particular, the
\{b_{k}\}
span the tangent space to the level sets of
|v-w|
. They then proceed to set

\tilde{b}_{k}(v,w)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}b_{k}(v-w)\\
-b_{k}(v-w)\end{pmatrix}

to arrive at

(2.4)

Q_{L}(F)\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F).

Here
\nabla
refers to the gradient in the full variables
(v,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
. In words, they express the lifted Landau collision operator
Q_{L}
as the sum of squares of first order transport operators
\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla
, where
\tilde{b}_{k}
always lies tangent to the level sets of
\alpha(\cdot)
. Here and throughout the paper, we abuse notation with
\alpha
, thinking of it also as a function of
(v,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
. Now we return to the Krieger-Strain setting by defining

b_{0}(v-w)\coloneqq v-w\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

for which it holds that

(2.5)

|v-w|^{2}\text{Id}_{3}=a_{ij}(v-w)+b_{0}(v-w)\otimes b_{0}(v-w),

where
a_{ij}
is as in (
1.7
). We remark that
b_{0}(v-w)
points in the direction normal to the level sets of
|v-w|
, which is a departure from the Landau setting. It is immediate that (
2.2
) becomes

(2.6)

\displaystyle Q_{KS}(F)=Q_{L}(F)+(\partial_{v_{i}}-\partial_{w_{i}})[\alpha(|v-w|)(b_{0})_{i}(b_{0})_{j}(\partial_{v_{j}}-\partial_{w_{j}})F(v,w)].

Now we complete the lifting by setting

(2.7)

\tilde{b}_{0}(v,w)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}v-w\\
w-v\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}

at which point we see (
2.6
) may be written as

(2.8)

\displaystyle Q_{KS}(F)

\displaystyle=Q_{L}(F)+\nabla\cdot(\alpha(v-w)\tilde{b}_{0}\otimes\tilde{b}_{0}\nabla F)

(2.9)

\displaystyle=Q_{L}(F)+\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F)).

where now the divergence and gradient are in the full variables
(v,w)
. We also define the normalized
\tilde{b}_{0}
as
n
, which is

(2.10)

n\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}|v-w|}\begin{pmatrix}v-w\\
w-v\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}.

We record for posterity that

\nabla\sqrt{\alpha}=\frac{\nabla\alpha}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{v-w}{|v-w|}\\
\frac{w-v}{|v-w|}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}\frac{\tilde{b}_{0}}{|v-w|}=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2\alpha}}n.

This further complicates the Krieger-Strain situation. In the Landau setting (
2.4
), one may move any powers of
\alpha
through the differential operators
\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla
for
1\leq k\leq 3
, since
\nabla\alpha\perp\tilde{b}_{k}
. Because of this, as we manipulate (
2.8
) and pass the derivative inside, we pick up an error term:

(2.11)

\displaystyle Q_{KS}(F)

\displaystyle=Q_{L}(F)+\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F)+\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F)

(2.12)

\displaystyle=Q_{L}(F)+\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F)+(\sqrt{\alpha}\dv(\tilde{b}_{0})+\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2\alpha}}n\cdot\tilde{b}_{0})(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F)

(2.13)

\displaystyle=Q_{L}(F)+\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F)+\left(6\sqrt{\alpha}+\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}|v-w|\right)(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F).

Here we used that
\dv(\tilde{b}_{0})=6
in the three dimensional case. This is the form of
Q_{KS}
that we shall make use of. We will need to consider the derivative of the Fisher information along the flow of each of the three operators in (
2.13
). In doing so, we will make direct use of the computation for
\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{L}(F)\rangle
from
[
9
]
.

Before proceeding, it is worthwhile to understand a little flows of the the transport operators
\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla
for
0\leq k\leq 3
. To fix ideas, let us set
\alpha\equiv 1
(the
Maxwell-Molecules
case). The flows are given by

(2.14)

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{v}(t)\\
\dot{w}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\tilde{b}_{j}(v,w),\ \ \ (v(0),w(0))=(v_{0},w_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}.

As discussed in
[
9
]
, for the Landau equation
1\leq j\leq 3
, the following properties hold:

•

v(t)+w(t)
is constant (preservation of midpoint),

•

|v(t)|^{2}+|w(t)|^{2}
is constant (preservation of norm),

•

|v(t)-w(t)|
is constant (preservation of distance).

The facts imply the flows of the vector fields
\tilde{b}_{k}
for
1\leq j\leq 3
lie on the two-dimensional Boltzmann sphere

\text{sphere}(v,w)\coloneqq\{(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}:v^{\prime}+w^{\prime}=v+w,|v^{\prime}|^{2}+|w^{\prime}|^{2}=|v|^{2}+|w|^{2}\}.

The geometry is different in the Krieger-Strain equation, for we must consider the flow along the vector field
\tilde{b}_{0}
, which points normal to the level sets of
|v-w|
in
\mathbb{R}^{6}
. Solving (
2.14
) for
\tilde{b}_{0}
, it is easy to see that midpoints are preserved, but distances and norms are not. In particular, considering points
(v,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
as pairs of points
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the flow of
\tilde{b}_{0}
pulls points apart with exponential speed

|v(t)-w(t)|^{2}=|v_{0}-w_{0}|^{2}e^{4t}.

This fact complicates the geometry of
Q_{KS}
as compared to
Q_{L}
, as our operator is no longer generated by rotations. The vector field
\tilde{b}_{0}
is less well-suited to computation than the Landau vector fields
\tilde{b}_{k},1\leq k\leq 3
. For instance, whereas the derivatives
D\tilde{b}_{k}
are anti-symmetric for
1\leq k\leq 3
, and in particular the
\tilde{b}_{k}
are divergence-free, the derivative
D\tilde{b}_{0}
takes the form

(2.15)

D\tilde{b}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\text{Id}_{3}&-\text{Id}_{3}\\
-\text{Id}_{3}&\text{Id}_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times 6.}

The lack of the divergence-free structure for
\tilde{b}_{0}
leads to more complicated expressions in the analysis of the Fisher information.

We conclude the preliminary section with some straightforward lemmas about various derivatives of the Fisher information which we shall refer back to throughout the next two sections. One piece of notation we make use of is the commutator
[a,b]
. For given smooth vector fields
a,b:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}^{6}
, we define

[a,b]\cdot\nabla G\coloneqq a\cdot\nabla(b\cdot\nabla G)-b\cdot\nabla(a\cdot\nabla G).

That is,
[a,b]
measures the error one incurs when switching the order of differentiation along the vector fields
a
and
b
. There is a more in depth discussion in
[
9
]
, but we would like to highlight the following fact

(2.16)

[a,b]=0\text{ if and only if }(Da)b=(Db)a.

This is obtained easily by differentiating. We shall use (
2.16
) in Section
4
. Continuing on, we will often need to consider a partial, weighted type of Fisher information

I_{e}^{\beta}(F)\coloneqq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\beta|e\cdot\nabla\log F|^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\beta\frac{|e\cdot\nabla F|^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

for a given smooth vector field
e:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}^{6}
and smooth scalar function
\beta:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}
. Additionally, we adopt from
[

]
the notation

(2.17)

L_{b}(F)\coloneqq b\cdot\nabla F

for a smooth vector field

.
Taking
\beta=1
, it is observed in
[

]
that the following identity holds:

Lemma 2.5

(Essentially from Lemma
4.1
of
[

]

.

Let
e

be vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{6}
, then

(2.18)

\langle I^{\prime}_{e}(F),L_{b}(F)\rangle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2(e\cdot\nabla\log F)([e,b]\cdot\nabla\log F)F-\dv(b)(e\cdot\nabla\log F)^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

The proof of this fact is contained in the proof of Lemma

from
[

]

many times, often for a constant vector

Let

(2.19)

\langle(I_{e}^{\beta})^{\prime}(F),L_{b}(F)\rangle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2\beta(e\cdot\nabla\log F)([e,b]\cdot\nabla\log F)F-\dv(\beta b)(e\cdot\nabla\log F)^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

Proof.

Again, this comes directly from the proof of Lemma
4.1

(2.20)

\displaystyle\langle(I_{e}^{\beta})^{\prime}(F),L_{b}(F)\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2\beta\frac{(e\cdot\nabla F)(e\cdot\nabla(b\cdot\nabla F))}{F}-\frac{(e\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F^{2}}(\beta b\cdot\nabla F)\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2\beta\frac{(e\cdot\nabla F)(b\cdot\nabla(e\cdot\nabla F))}{F}-\frac{(e\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F^{2}}(\beta b\cdot\nabla F)+2\beta\frac{(e\cdot\nabla F)([e,b]\cdot\nabla F)}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

\displaystyle 2\beta\frac{(e\cdot\nabla F)(b\cdot\nabla(e\cdot\nabla F))}{F}-\frac{(e\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F^{2}}(\beta b\cdot\nabla F)

3.
The Maxwell Molecules Case

In this section we focus on the Maxwell Molecules case
\alpha(\cdot)\equiv 1
in (
2.13
). As in
[
9
]
, proving that the Fisher information is decreasing along the Krieger-Strain flow is considerably simpler in the case of Maxwell Molecules.

Specializing to the case
\alpha(\cdot)\equiv 1
in (
2.13
),
Q_{KS}
takes the form

(3.1)

Q_{KS}=Q_{L}+L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}\circ L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}+6L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}.

where
\tilde{b}_{0}
is as in (
2.7
) and
L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}
is as in (
2.17
). Here we have used that
\dv(\tilde{b}_{0})=6
in dimension
d=3
. We will have to analyze the derivative of the Fisher information in the direction of each of the three operators on the righthand side. In particular, our proof uses that
\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{L}(F)\rangle\leq 0
, from
[
9
]
.

In our computations, it will be useful to define the following three vectors:

\nu_{1}\coloneqq e_{1}+e_{4},\ \ \nu_{2}\coloneqq e_{2}+e_{5},\ \ \nu_{3}\coloneqq e_{3}+e_{6},

with
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
being the
i
’th standard basis vector.

We begin by studying the first order term of (
3.1
) with the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any smooth positive function
F:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to(0,\infty)
with rapid decay at infinity we have

(3.2)

\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F)\rangle=-2I(F)-2\sum_{i=1}^{3}I_{\nu_{i}}(F).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.7
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F)\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{\nabla F\cdot(2D\tilde{b}_{0}-\dv(\tilde{b}_{0})\text{Id}_{6})\nabla F}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

Computing directly,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\dv(\tilde{b}_{0})\text{Id}_{6}-D\tilde{b}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}2\text{Id}_{3}&\text{Id}_{3}\\
\text{Id}_{3}&2\text{Id}_{3}\end{pmatrix}\geq 0.

Lastly we decompose the above matrix into
\text{Id}_{6}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\nu_{i}\otimes\nu_{i}
, at which point (
3.3
) reduces to (
3.2
) as claimed.
∎

The decomposition according to the
\nu_{i}
is particularly useful thanks to the observation that differentiation along
\nu_{i}
commutes with differentiation along
\tilde{b}_{0}
, i.e.,

[\nu_{i},\tilde{b}_{0}]=0,\ \ \text{ for each }1\leq i\leq 3.

With this fact, we may apply Lemma
2.5
to immediately obtain

Lemma 3.2
.

For any smooth positive function
F:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to(0,\infty)
with sufficient decay at infinity, there holds

(3.5)

\displaystyle\langle I_{\nu_{i}}^{\prime}(F),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F)\rangle=-6I_{\nu_{i}}(F).

With these two lemmas in hand, we follow the approach of
[
9
]
and study the first order transport equation associated with
L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}
. For an arbitrary function
F_{0}=F_{0}(v,w)
, solve

(3.6)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}F(t,v,w)=L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F),\\
F(0,v,w)=F_{0}(v,w).\end{cases}

The following lemma is immediate.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any smooth, positive
F_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to(0,\infty)
with rapid decay at infinity, if
F
solves (
3.6
), then

(3.7)

\frac{d}{dt}I(F)=-2I(F)-2\sum_{i=1}^{3}I_{\nu_{i}}(F)

and

\partial_{tt}F(0,v,w)=L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}\circ L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F_{0})(v,w).

Finally, we are able to prove the following special case of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 3.4
.

The Fisher information is monotone decreasing along the flow of the Krieger-Strain equation (
1.6
) when
\alpha\equiv 1.

Proof.

For an arbitrary smooth and positive
F_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to(0,\infty)
with sufficient decay, it suffices to prove

\langle I^{\prime}(F_{0}),Q_{KS}(F_{0})\rangle\leq 0.

With the decomposition of
Q_{KS}
in (
3.1
), and the powerful result

\langle I^{\prime}(F_{0}),Q_{L}(F_{0})\rangle\leq 0

from
[
9
]
, it suffices to prove that

\langle I^{\prime}(F_{0}),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}\circ L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F_{0})\rangle+6\langle I^{\prime}(F_{0}),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F_{0})\rangle\leq 0.

The former term must be computed; the latter term is computed in Proposition
3.1
. To estimate the derivative of
I[\cdot]
along
L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}\circ L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}
, we follow the approach of Guillen and Silvestre. Letting
F(t,v,w)
solve (
3.6
), we have on the one hand that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}I(F)\biggm|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\langle I^{\prime}(F_{0}),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}\circ L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F_{0})\rangle+\langle I^{\prime\prime}(F_{0})L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F_{0}),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F_{0})\rangle\geq\langle I^{\prime}(F_{0}),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}\circ L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F_{0})\rangle

thanks to Lemma
3.3
and the convexity of
I[\cdot]
. On the other hand, we compute

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}I(F)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\langle I^{\prime}(F),\partial_{t}F\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F)\rangle

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left(-2I(F)-2\sum_{i=1}^{3}I_{\nu_{i}}(F)\right)

(3.11)

\displaystyle=4I(F)+16\sum_{i=1}^{3}I_{\nu_{i}}(F),

by applying the identities (
3.5
) and (
3.2
). In particular,
\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}I(F)\bigm|_{t=0}=4I(F_{0})+16\sum_{i=1}^{3}I_{\nu_{i}}(F_{0}).
Applying (
3.2
) again, it follows that

(3.12)

\displaystyle\langle I^{\prime}(F_{0}),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}\circ L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F_{0})\rangle+6\langle I^{\prime}(F_{0}),L_{\tilde{b}_{0}}(F_{0})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 4I(F_{0})+16\sum_{i=1}^{3}I_{\nu_{i}}(F_{0})+6\left(-2I(F_{0})-2\sum_{i=1}^{3}I_{\nu_{i}}(F_{0})\right)

(3.13)

\displaystyle=-8I(F_{0})+4\sum_{i=1}^{3}I_{\nu_{i}}(F_{0})\leq 0.

The last inequality follows simply from the fact that
\left\{\frac{\nu_{i}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\}
is an orthonormal set in
\mathbb{R}^{6}
, which allows us to sum over the squares:

\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\nu_{i}\cdot\nabla F_{0}|^{2}\leq 2|\nabla F_{0}|^{2},

which is enough to conclude the theorem.
∎

We remark that in the Maxwell-Molecules case, we did not need to use any precise estimates from
[
9
]
for
\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{L}(F)\rangle
. And despite the proof being more complicated than the proof of the corresponding Proposition
5.4
of
[
9
]
, we obtain an even stronger decrease:

\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{KS}(F)\rangle\leq\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{L}(F)\rangle\leq 0.

This will not be the case for general
\alpha(\cdot)
, wherein worse estimates are obtained in the Krieger-Strain setting. The reason for this is that in the general case, one must contend with derivatives in the direction
n
normal to the level sets of
\alpha(\cdot)
.

4.
The Fisher Information along the Krieger-Strain flow

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
. For a general radial interaction potential
\alpha(\cdot)
, it is convenient to introduce the notation

(4.1)

\Gamma=\Gamma(r;\alpha)\coloneqq\frac{r\alpha^{\prime}(r)}{\alpha(r)}\text{ for }r>0.

If we take
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}
, then
\Gamma(r;\alpha)=\gamma
identically. In the lifted setting, Theorem
1.1
may be reformulated as

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to(0,\infty)
be a smooth function with rapid decay at infinity so that
F(v,w)=F(w,v)
. Then there holds

\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{KS}(F)\rangle\leq 0

for any interaction potential
\alpha(\cdot)
satisfying

\Gamma=\Gamma(r;\alpha)\in[2-3\sqrt{3},-2+2\sqrt{2}]\text{ for all }r>0.

We now turn to showing that
\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{KS}(F)\rangle\leq 0
. We define
L_{0}\coloneqq\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla
to simplify the notation. With this new notation,
Q_{KS}
takes the form (
2.13
)

(4.2)

Q_{KS}=Q_{L}+L_{0}\circ L_{0}+\left(6\sqrt{\alpha}+\frac{|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)L_{0}.

We will have to analyze the derivative of the Fisher information along each of the three terms. As mentioned in the previous section, we will make use of more precise estimates for
\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{L}(F)\rangle
. Throughout this section, we consider the lifted Krieger-Strain collision operator
Q_{KS}
obtained in (
4.2
) and the lifted Landau collision operator
Q_{L}
obtained in (
2.4
) with the same interaction potential
\alpha(\cdot)
. The following proposition is one of the main inequalities from
[
9
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to(0,\infty)
be a smooth function with rapid decay at infinity so that
F(v,w)=F(w,v)
. Then with the
\tilde{b}_{k}
as in Section
2
, the following inequality holds:

(4.3)

\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{L}(F)\rangle\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(\Gamma(r)^{2}-19)\frac{\alpha}{|v-w|^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v,

where here
r=|v-w|
.

This is a precise estimate for the decrease of the Fisher information along the flow of the Landau equation, for interaction potentials
\alpha(\cdot)
with
|\Gamma(r,\alpha)|\leq\sqrt{19}
for all
r>0
. Unlike in the Maxwell Molecules case, it does not suffice for us to simply use
\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{L}(F)\rangle\leq 0
. This is not strong enough to compensate for the unfavorable terms
\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{0}\circ L_{0}F\rangle
and
\langle I^{\prime}(F),6\sqrt{\alpha}+\frac{|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}L_{0}(F)\rangle.

Let us begin by analyzing the first derivative of the Fisher information along the flow of
L_{0}(F)
. In order to compactify notation, will be convenient to define the following two quantities:

(4.4)

\beta_{1}\coloneqq 6\sqrt{\alpha}+\frac{|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}=\dv(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}),

and

(4.5)

\beta_{2}\coloneqq 2\sqrt{\alpha}+\frac{|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}=\beta_{1}-4\sqrt{\alpha}.

These two scalar quantities arise naturally throughout the proof. We remind the reader that the main departure of the Krieger-Strain setting from the Landau setting is that one must contend with higher order derivatives in the direction
n
normal to level sets of
\alpha(|v-w|)
, where

n=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}|v-w|}\tilde{b}_{0}.

The following lemma is an example of this.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any smooth positive function
F:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to(0,\infty)
with rapid decay, one has

(4.6)

\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{0}(F)\rangle=2\sum_{k=1}^{3}I_{\tilde{b}_{k}}^{\sqrt{\alpha}/|v-w|^{2}}(F)+2I^{\beta_{2}}_{n}(F)-I^{\beta_{1}}(F).

The quantities in (
4.6
) are defined in Section
2
.

Proof.

First, from Lemma
2.7
we have that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{0}(F)\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2\langle D(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})\nabla\log F,\nabla\log F\rangle F-\dv(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})|\nabla\log F|^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

Noting that
\dv(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})=\beta_{1}
, we proceed to deal with the quadratic form
D(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})\nabla\log F\cdot\nabla\log F
. The derivative of
D\tilde{b}_{0}
may be written as

(4.8)

D\tilde{b}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\text{Id}_{3}&-\text{Id}_{3}\\
-\text{Id}_{3}&\text{Id}_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{3}\frac{1}{|v-w|^{2}}\tilde{b}_{k}\otimes\tilde{b}_{k}\right).

Further computation gives

(4.9)

\displaystyle D(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\alpha}D\tilde{b}_{0}+\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2\alpha}}n\otimes\tilde{b}_{0}

(4.10)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\alpha}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{3}\frac{1}{|v-w|^{2}}\tilde{b}_{k}\otimes\tilde{b}_{k}\right)+\frac{|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}n\otimes n

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{|v-w|^{2}}\tilde{b}_{k}\otimes\tilde{b}_{k}\right)+\left(2\sqrt{\alpha}+\frac{|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)n\otimes n

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{|v-w|^{2}}\tilde{b}_{k}\otimes\tilde{b}_{k}\right)+\beta_{2}n\otimes n.

The claimed identity (
4.6
) follows.
∎

We now begin to deal with each term on the right-hand side individually. In computing
\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{0}\circ L_{0}(F)\rangle
, we will need to compute the derivatives along
L_{0}
of the three functionals on the righthand side of (
4.6
). For this reason, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any smooth positive function
F:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}
with rapid decay, there holds

(4.13)

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(I_{\tilde{b}_{k}}^{\sqrt{\alpha}/|v-w|^{2}}\right)^{\prime}(F),L_{0}(F)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}-2\left(\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}|v-w|}{|v-w|^{2}}\right)\frac{(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v,

(4.14)

\displaystyle\left\langle(I_{n}^{\beta_{2}})^{\prime}(F),L_{0}(F)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(2\beta_{2}^{2}-\dv(\beta_{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}))\frac{(n\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v,

(4.15)

\displaystyle\left\langle(I^{\beta_{1}})^{\prime}(F),L_{0}(F)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2\beta_{1}\frac{\langle D(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})\nabla F,\nabla F\rangle}{F}-\dv(\beta_{1}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})\frac{|\nabla F|^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

Proof.

We begin with (
4.13
). From Lemma
2.6
, we have that for each
k=1,2,3
, the lefthand side of (
4.13
) is equal to

(4.16)

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{|v-w|^{2}}(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\log F)([\tilde{b}_{k},\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}]\cdot\nabla\log F)F-\dv\left(\frac{\alpha}{|v-w|^{2}}\tilde{b}_{0}\right)(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\log F)^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

(4.17)

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\dv\left(\frac{\alpha}{|v-w|^{2}}\tilde{b}_{0}\right)(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\log F)^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

(4.18)

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left(6\frac{\alpha}{|v-w|^{2}}+2\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{|v-w|}-4\frac{\alpha}{|v-w|^{2}}\right)(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\log F)^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

(4.19)

\displaystyle=-2\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left(\frac{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}|v-w|}{|v-w|^{2}}\right)(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\log F)^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v,

as desired. Note that we used

[\tilde{b}_{k},\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}]=\sqrt{\alpha}[\tilde{b}_{k},\tilde{b}_{0}]=0\ \ \ \text{ for }k=1,2,3,

where the first equality which is a simple consequence of the fact that
\tilde{b}_{k}\perp\nabla\alpha
, and the second is verified with (
2.16
) and direct computation. We record the following two commutator identities for
[n,\tilde{b}_{0}]
and
[n,\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}]
, with the latter following from the former:

(4.20)

[n,\tilde{b}_{0}]=[n,\sqrt{2}|v-w|n]=\sqrt{2}(n\cdot\nabla|v-w|)(n\cdot\nabla)=2(n\cdot\nabla),

(4.21)

[n,\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}]=(n\cdot\nabla\sqrt{\alpha})(\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla)+\sqrt{\alpha}[n,\tilde{b}_{0}]=\left(\frac{|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}+2\sqrt{\alpha}\right)(n\cdot\nabla)=\beta_{2}(n\cdot\nabla).

With the calculation
[n,\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}]=\beta_{2}(n\cdot\nabla)
, we apply Lemma
2.6
to obtain the desired identity (
4.14
) as follows:

(4.22)

\displaystyle\langle(I^{\beta_{2}}_{n})^{\prime}(F),L_{0}(F)\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2\beta_{2}(n\cdot\nabla\log F)([n,\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}]\cdot\nabla\log F)F-\dv(\beta_{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})(n\cdot\nabla\log F)^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

(4.23)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(2\beta_{2}^{2}-\dv(\beta_{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}))(n\cdot\nabla\log F)^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

Moving onto (
4.15
), we use Lemma
2.8
to immediately obtain the final identity

(4.24)

\displaystyle\langle(I^{\beta_{1}})^{\prime}(F),L_{0}(F)\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2\beta_{1}\langle D(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})\nabla\log F,\nabla\log F\rangle F-\dv(\beta_{1}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})|\nabla\log F|^{2}F\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

∎

Now we continue with the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

For any smooth positive function
F_{0}
with rapid decay,
F_{0}=F_{0}(v,w)
, let us consider the initial value problem

(4.25)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}F(t,v,w)=L_{0}(F),\\
F(0,v,w)=F_{0}(v,w).\end{cases}

As in the Maxwell Molecules case, we have

(4.26)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}I(F)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{0}(F)\rangle

(4.27)

\displaystyle=\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{0}\circ L_{0}(F)\rangle+\langle I^{\prime\prime}(F)L_{0}(F),L_{0}(F)\rangle

(4.28)

\displaystyle\geq\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{0}\circ L_{0}(F)\rangle

owing to the convexity of the Fisher information.
On the other hand, thanks to Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
, we may compute

(4.29)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}I(F)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{0}(F)\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\left(2\sum_{k=1}^{3}I_{\tilde{b}_{k}}^{\sqrt{\alpha}/|v-w|^{2}}(F)+2I^{\beta_{2}}_{n}(F)-I^{\beta_{1}}(F)\right)

(4.30)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left(\frac{-4\alpha-4\alpha^{\prime}|v-w|}{|v-w|^{2}}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}+(4\beta_{2}^{2}-2\dv(\beta_{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}))\frac{(n\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}

(4.31)

\displaystyle-2\beta_{1}\frac{\langle D(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})\nabla F,\nabla F\rangle}{F}+\dv(\beta_{1}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})\frac{|\nabla F|^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

At this point it is useful to rewrite
Q_{KS}
in terms of
\beta_{1}
as follows:

(4.32)

Q_{KS}(F)=Q_{L}(F)+L_{0}\circ L_{0}(F)+(6\alpha+|v-w|\alpha^{\prime})(\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F)=Q_{L}+L_{0}\circ L_{0}(F)+\sqrt{\alpha}\beta_{1}(\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F).

To estimate
I^{\prime}(F)
along the flow of the first order operator
\sqrt{\alpha}\beta_{1}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla
, we apply Lemma
2.7
to find

(4.33)

\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{\sqrt{\alpha}\beta_{1}\tilde{b}_{0}}(F)\rangle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2\frac{\langle D(\sqrt{\alpha}\beta_{1}\tilde{b}_{0})\nabla F,\nabla F\rangle}{F}-\dv(\sqrt{\alpha}\beta_{1}\tilde{b}_{0})\frac{|\nabla F|^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

Using (
4.33
) and the estimate on
\langle I^{\prime}(F),L_{0}\circ L_{0}(F)\rangle
from (
4.31
), we obtain after some cancellation the estimate

(4.34)

\displaystyle\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{KS}(F)-Q_{L}(F)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left(\frac{-4\alpha-4\alpha^{\prime}|v-w|}{|v-w|^{2}}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}+(4\beta_{2}^{2}-2\dv(\beta_{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}))\frac{(n\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}

(4.35)

\displaystyle+2\frac{(\nabla\beta_{1}\cdot\nabla F)(\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}\cdot\nabla F)}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

(4.36)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left(\frac{-4\alpha-4\alpha^{\prime}|v-w|}{|v-w|^{2}}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}+(4\beta_{2}^{2}-2\dv(\beta_{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0}))\frac{(n\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}

(4.37)

\displaystyle+4|v-w|\sqrt{\alpha}\beta_{1}^{\prime}\frac{(n\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

(4.38)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left(\frac{-4\alpha-4\alpha^{\prime}|v-w|}{|v-w|^{2}}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}

(4.39)

\displaystyle+\left(2\frac{(|v-w|\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}{\alpha}-8\alpha+8|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}\right)\frac{(n\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

Let us briefly explain where the coefficient of
\displaystyle\frac{(n\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}
comes from. The strategy is to write
\beta_{2}=\beta_{1}-4\sqrt{\alpha}
and to write everything in terms of
\beta_{1}
. Then one writes all gradients in terms of
n
, for given a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}
which depends only on
|v-w|
, one has, with obvious abuse of notation, the identity
\nabla\varphi=\sqrt{2}\varphi^{\prime}n
. With this in mind, it is easy to compute the derivatives, which we record plainly as follows:

(4.41)

\displaystyle\dv(\beta_{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})

\displaystyle=\dv(\beta_{1}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})-4\dv(\alpha\tilde{b}_{0})

(4.42)

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}^{2}+2|v-w|\sqrt{\alpha}\beta_{1}^{\prime}-24\alpha-8|v-w|\alpha^{\prime},

and therefore

(4.43)

\displaystyle 4\beta_{2}^{2}-2\dv(\beta_{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{b}_{0})+4|v-w|\sqrt{\alpha}\beta_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2\beta_{1}^{2}-32\sqrt{\alpha}\beta_{1}+112\alpha+16|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}

(4.44)

\displaystyle=2\frac{(|v-w|\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}{\alpha}-8\alpha+8|v-w|\alpha^{\prime},

after using the definition of
\beta_{1}
and simplifying. We remark that the
\beta_{1}^{\prime}
terms cancel out, which spares us from having to contend with second derivatives of
\alpha
. This is the only point where
\alpha^{\prime\prime}
terms arise. We continue by writing (
4.39
) in terms of
\Gamma=\Gamma(|v-w|;\alpha)=\displaystyle\frac{|v-w|\alpha^{\prime}(|v-w|)}{\alpha(|v-w|)}
with

(4.45)

\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{KS}(F)-Q_{L}(F)\rangle\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left(\frac{-4\alpha(1+\Gamma)}{|v-w|^{2}}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}+(2\Gamma^{2}+8\Gamma-8)\alpha\frac{(n\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

If
2\Gamma^{2}+8\Gamma-8\leq 0
for all
r>0
, or equivalently if

(4.46)

\Gamma=\Gamma(r)\in[-2-2\sqrt{2},-2+2\sqrt{2}]\text{ for all }r>0,

then it holds that

(4.47)

\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{KS}(F)-Q_{L}(F)\rangle\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left(\frac{-4\alpha(1+\Gamma)}{|v-w|^{2}}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

The condition (
4.46
) is the first requirement we impose upon
\Gamma
. Finally, we apply the result of Guillen and Silvestre from Proposition
4.2
to obtain

(4.48)

\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{KS}(F)\rangle\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(\Gamma^{2}-4\Gamma-4-19)\frac{\alpha}{|v-w|^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla F)^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

Therefore, if in addition to (
4.46
), we impose that
\Gamma^{2}-4\Gamma-23\leq 0
for all
r>0
, equivalently

(4.49)

\Gamma=\Gamma(r)\in[2-3\sqrt{3},2+3\sqrt{3}]\text{ for all }r>0,

we have the desired monotonicity

(4.50)

\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q_{KS}(F)\rangle\leq 0.

Taking the intersection of the conditions (
4.46
) and (
4.49
) we obtain the condition on
\Gamma
as stated in Proposition
4.1
. With the theory set forth in Section
2
, this also concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

As an immediate corollary of the preceding and the Sobolev embedding, we obtain the following bound on the

.

Let
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
be a classical solution to (

) such that

. Then

\|f(t)\|_{L^{3}}\leq Ci(f_{\text{in}})

for an absolute constant
C
.

We remark that from this point forward, we return to the original equation (
1.2
) on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. With the monotonicity of the Fisher information
i(f)
in hand, we make no more use of the lifted equation (

).

5.
Propagation of Moments and an
L^{\infty}
Estimate

In this section, we establish growth estimates on all
s
-th moments of
f
, defined as

(5.1)

E_{s}(f)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(v)|v|^{s}\;\mathrm{d}s.

for
s\geq 0
, and as a consequence an unconditional a priori estimate on
\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
. The estimates rely on the quantity
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}
, which is controlled thanks to the monotonicity of the Fisher information and Corollary
4.5
.
In this section and in what follows, we assume
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in[-3,-2)
. We will not state the range of
\gamma
in every statement.

We also define the weighted Lebesgue norms
L^{p}_{m}
and Sobolev norms
H^{k}_{m}
of
f
as follows:

(5.2)

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}_{m}}:=\left\|\langle v\rangle^{m}f\right\|_{L^{p}},\quad\left\|f\right\|_{\dot{H}^{k}_{m}}\coloneqq\left\|D^{k}f\right\|_{L^{2}_{m}},\quad\left\|f\right\|_{H^{k}_{m}}\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left\|D^{j}f\right\|_{L^{2}_{m}}.

Note that
\left\|g\right\|_{L^{1}_{s}}
is not the same as
E_{s}(g)
, but is comparable to
E_{s}(g)+E_{0}(g)
. The mass and energy discussed in Section 2 correspond to
E_{0}(f(t))
and
E_{2}(f(t))
, respectively. Recall that the equation (
1.2
) conserves the mass, but not the energy. Even though the energy is not conserved, a linear growth estimate for the energy was obtained in
[
7
]
, which is the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1

(Essentially from
[
7
]
)
.

Let
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a classical solution to (
1.1
), with initial data
f_{\text{in}}
having finite mass and energy. Then the energy
E_{2}(f(t))
satisfies

E_{2}(f(t))\leq E_{2}(f_{\text{in}})+tC_{\gamma}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,t];L^{3})}^{1-\theta_{0}}\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}^{1+\theta_{0}}\leq E_{2}(f_{\text{in}})+TC_{\gamma}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{3})}^{1-\theta_{0}}\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}^{1+\theta_{0}}

where
\theta_{0}\coloneqq\frac{|2+\gamma|}{2}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

The first inequality was obtained in
[
7
]
, using the computation (
1.16
). The second inequality is trivial.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

In
[
7
]
, the linear growth of the energy was obtained for the range of
\gamma\in[-\frac{5}{2},-2]
, leaving open the range
[-3,-\frac{5}{2})
. Their novel method was to apply Hardy’s inequality to obtain the propagation of
L^{p}
norm. In particular, they prove a propagation of
L^{p}
norm for
1\leq p\leq\frac{3+\gamma}{-2-\gamma}
, which necessitates
\gamma\geq-\frac{5}{2}.
And then to control the energy, they need an estimate on
\|f(t)\|_{L^{p}}
for
p>\frac{3}{5+\gamma}
. Our propagation of
L^{3}
norm from Corollary
4.5
, which does not depend on
\gamma
, is strong enough to obtain the propagation of energy for all
\gamma\in[-3,-2)
.

Before proving a growth estimate for all
s-
th moments, we need estimates of the upper and lower bounds for
a[f]
.
Interpolated with the conservation of
L^{1}
norm, the control of
L^{3}
norm gives us a control on
L^{p}
norm for every
1\leq p\leq 3
. A control on
L^{p}
norm can be used to obtain an upper bound on the diffusion coefficient
a[f]=f\ast|\cdot|^{2+\gamma}
by the following convolution inequality. The proof is contained in Remark
4.2
of
[
7
]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for
p>\displaystyle\frac{3}{5+\gamma}
, it holds that

\left\|a[f]\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{p,\gamma}\|f\|_{L^{p}}^{\theta}||f\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta},\text{ where }\theta\coloneqq\frac{|2+\gamma|p^{\prime}}{3},

where
p^{\prime}
is the Hölder conjugate of
p
.
In particular, for
p=3
,

(5.3)

\left\|f\ast|\cdot|^{2+\gamma}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{\gamma}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{3}}^{\theta_{0}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}^{1-\theta_{0}}

where
\theta_{0}:=\frac{|2+\gamma|}{2}
.

The lower bound for
a[f]
is obtained in Corollary 4.3 of
[
7
]
as a consequence of the inequality in our Lemma
5.1
.

Lemma 5.4

(Corollary 4.3 of
[
7
]
)
.

Let
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
be a classical solution to (

). The diffusion coefficient

satisfies the bounds

\ell\langle v\rangle^{2+\gamma}\leq a[f](t,v)

Here

\ell\coloneqq c_{\gamma}\frac{\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}^{-1-\gamma}}{\left(c_{1}+c_{2}T\|f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{3})}^{1-\theta_{0}}\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}^{1+\theta_{0}}\right)^{-2-\gamma}}

where
\theta_{0}=\frac{|2+\gamma|}{2}.

Proof.

We can plug in
p=3
to Corollary 4.3 of
[
7
]
since
3>\frac{3}{5+\gamma}
.
∎

The lower bound for the diffusion coefficient for the Landau equation was obtained in Lemma 3.2 of
[
15
]
by Silvestre, which uses the following idea. Heuristically, bounded energy prevents the
L^{1}
mass from escaping off to infinity, and the bounded entropy prevents the mass from concentrating on a set of small measure. Since the energy is conserved for the Landau equation, it allows one to prove the following: there is a ball
B_{R}

Proposition 5.5

Let

Proof.

The proof is by induction on even

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{k}(f(t))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{k}\partial_{v_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v-w|^{2+\gamma}\partial_{v_{i}-w_{i}}(f(v)f(w))\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v

Here we have employed summation notation. Evaluating the derivative and summing over

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{k}(f(t))

\displaystyle=k(k+1)\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{k-2}|v-w|^{2+\gamma}f(v)f(w)\;\mathrm{d}v\;\mathrm{d}w+2k(2+\gamma)\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}v\cdot(v-w)|v|^{k-2}|v-w|^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\coloneqq J_{1}(t)+J_{2}(t).

and conclude with the lower bound on

from Lemma

from Lemma

Suppose that (

Case Two: Estimating the

for

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta_{1}}a[f]\Delta\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\partial^{\beta_{1}}f,\partial^{\beta_{2}}f)\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta_{1}}a[f]\Delta\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\;\mathrm{d}v

6.3

, we may bound

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta_{1}}a[f]\Delta\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\;\mathrm{d}v\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}}\|f\|_{H^{k-1}}(\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k+1}_{(2+\gamma)/2}}+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\theta}})\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{H^{k}}^{2}\left(1+\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{s}}\right)+\varepsilon\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k+1}_{(2+\gamma)/2}}^{2}

.

Now we move onto the

Case Five: Estimating the
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
terms when

In these two cases, for

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\partial^{\beta}f,f)\;\mathrm{d}v=-(2+\gamma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta}h[f]f\;\mathrm{d}v\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{H^{k}}^{2}\left(1+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\theta}}^{2}\right)+\varepsilon\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k+1}_{(2+\gamma)/2}}^{2}

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\mathcal{Q}_{2}(f,\partial^{\beta}f)\;\mathrm{d}v=-(2+\gamma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}fh[f]\partial^{\beta}f\;\mathrm{d}v\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-\gamma/3}\|f\|_{H^{k}}^{2}.

The second estimate (

) is immediate from Lemma

. In the case

6.29

In the case

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta}h[f]f\;\mathrm{d}v

Case Six: Estimating the
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
terms when

In this case, for all

(6.34)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\partial^{\beta_{1}}f,\partial^{\beta_{2}}f)\;\mathrm{d}v=-(2+\gamma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta_{1}}h[f]\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\;\mathrm{d}v\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{H^{k}}^{2}\left(1+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\theta}}^{2}\right)+\varepsilon\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k+1}_{(2+\gamma)/2}}^{2}

In the Coulomb case

(6.35)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta_{1}}h[f]\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=4\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta_{1}}f\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\;\mathrm{d}v\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}}\|\partial^{\beta_{1}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\|_{L^{2}}

(6.36)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}}\|f\|_{H^{2+|\beta_{1}|}}\|f\|_{H^{k-1}}\lesssim C_{k-1}\|f\|_{H^{k}}^{2},

using the Sobolev embedding

, as well as that

and

. In particular, we use the induction hypothesis to control the

(6.37)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta_{1}}h[f]\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta_{1}}h[f]\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}}\|f\|_{H^{k-1}}\|\partial^{\beta_{1}}\Delta a[f]\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}}\|f\|_{H^{k-1}}\|f\|_{H^{2+|\beta_{1}|}_{q}}

(6.39)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{H^{k}}^{2}\left(1+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\theta}}^{2}\right)+\varepsilon\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k+1}_{(2+\gamma)/2}}^{2}

and Lemma
A.4

Case Seven: Estimating the

For
k\geq 3
, there exists an
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following inequality holds:

(6.41)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\partial^{\beta_{1}}f,\partial^{\beta_{2}}f)\;\mathrm{d}v\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}}^{2}\left(\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k}_{(2+\gamma)/2}}+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\theta}}\right)

, we estimate as follows:

(6.42)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}f\partial^{\beta_{1}}h[f]\partial^{\beta_{2}}f\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}}\|f\|_{H^{3}}\|\partial^{\beta_{1}}f\|_{L^{2}_{q}}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{k}}\|f\|_{H^{3}}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k-1}_{q}}

and (

. Note we used

In the case

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}fQ_{2}(\partial^{\beta_{1}}f,\partial^{\beta_{2}}f)\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=(2+\gamma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}h[f]\left((\partial_{ij}f)^{2}+\partial_{jii}f\partial_{j}f\right)\;\mathrm{d}v

(6.47)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-\gamma/3}\|f\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2+\gamma}|D^{3}f|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}v+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}h[f]^{2}\langle v\rangle^{-2-\gamma}|\nabla f|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-\gamma/3}\|f\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{3}_{(2+\gamma)/2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-2\gamma/3}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}_{-(2+\gamma)/2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-\gamma/3}\|f\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{3}_{(2+\gamma)/2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-2\gamma/3}\left(\|f\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\theta}}^{2}\right)

)-(

By Corollary

, there exists a sufficiently large

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}_{\theta}}+\left\|f\right\|_{L^{4}_{q}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}_{m}}\lesssim K+\left\|f_{\text{in}}\right\|_{L^{1}_{m}}.

For some
\tilde{C}_{k-1}>0

\|D^{k}f(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left(\left\|D^{k}f_{\text{in}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{k-1}}{c_{0}}C_{k-1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}(1+F_{k-1}(s)^{2})\;\mathrm{d}s\right)\exp\left(\frac{\tilde{C}_{k-1}}{c_{0}}\int_{0}^{T}(1+F_{k-1}(s)^{2})\;\mathrm{d}s\right)\leq C_{k}.

7.
L^{\infty}
Local Existence Theory

There does not seem to be a satisfying local existence theory for the Krieger-Strain equation (
1.2
). The existing theorems in the literature concern initial data which is radially symmetric and monotone decreasing, see the citations in the introduction. In this section, we follow the ideas of Golding and Loher in
[
5
]
to establish an
L^{\infty}
short-time existence theory. To do so, we first need the intermediate step of constructing solutions for data with sufficient regularity. We do so with the following Proposition
7.1
, which establishes the short-time existence of solutions for initial data lying in weighted Sobolev spaces.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\gamma\in[-3,-2)
and
q>\frac{7}{2}+\gamma
. For any non-negative initial condition
f_{\text{in}}\in H^{3}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, there exists
T>0
depending only on
\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{H^{3}_{q}}
and a unique non-negative solution

f\in L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{3}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\cap W^{1,\infty}([0,T];H_{q}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
to the Krieger-Strain equation

\partial_{t}f=\mathcal{Q}_{KS}(f,f).

If in addition
f_{\text{in}}\in H^{k}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for any
k\geq 3
or
q^{\prime}>q
, then for any
t\in[0,T]
, there holds

\|f(t)\|_{H^{k}_{q^{\prime}}}\leq\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{H^{k}_{q^{\prime}}}\exp(Ct(1+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,t];H^{3}_{q}}))),\ \ t\in[0,T].

In particular, if
\langle v\rangle^{q}f_{\text{in}}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for all
q>0
, then
\langle v\rangle^{q}f\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
for all
q>0
.

The statement of this theorem is completely analogous to the corresponding Theorem
1.3
of Snelson’s work on the isotropic Boltzmann equation,
[
16
]
. The proof is quite standard. Following Snelson, we establish energy estimates for the linearized equation and then apply the method of continuity followed by an iteration procedure. Because it is not our main local existence result, the proof is postponed until Appendix
B
.
As a corollary, we obtain the following sort of
L^{\infty}
continuation criterion.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
f_{\text{in}}
be an initial data which is nonnegative, smooth, and belonging to
H^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap H^{3}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{1}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for some
q>\frac{7}{2}+\gamma
and
m
as in Proposition
6.1
. Then there exists a classical solution
f:[0,T_{*})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
to the equation (
1.2
), where
[0,T_{*})
is the maximal interval of existence. If
T_{*}=+\infty
,
f
is a global solution, and if
T_{*}<+\infty
, it is characterized by the blow-up criterion

\lim_{t\nearrow T_{*}}\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}=+\infty.

Proof.

Proposition
7.1
yields a classical solution
f:[0,T_{*})\to[0,\infty)
. If
T_{*}<\infty
, then it must be that
\|f(t)\|_{H^{3}_{q}}\to\infty
as
t\to T_{*}
. Because we have assumed
f_{\text{in}}\in L^{1}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for
m
large enough, the growth estimates of Proposition
6.1
imply that
\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\to\infty
as
t\to T_{*}
. Indeed, if for all
t\in[0,T_{*})
it were the case that
\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq K<\infty
for some
K>0
, then
\|f(t)\|_{H^{4}}
would stay bounded on
[0,T_{*})
. Interpolating the
H^{4}
estimates with the
L^{1}_{m}\cap L^{\infty}\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{m/2}
estimates, we obtain a uniform estimate on
\|f(t)\|_{H^{3}_{q}}
which does not blow up, reaching a contradiction. And for each time
t<T_{*}
, the Sobolev embedding implies
\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
, and therefore
T_{*}
is the actual blow-up time for
\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
∎

Our first step towards a satisfying
L^{\infty}
existence theorem is to prove a small-time growth estimate for
\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
that relies only on
\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
be a nonnegative solution of (
1.2
)
with initial data
f_{\text{in}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).
Suppose further that the maximum of
f(t,\cdot)
is attained for each
t>0
.
Then for almost every
t\in[0,T]
,

\partial_{t}\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-\gamma/3}

with constant
C
depending only on
\gamma
and
\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}
. As a consequence, for all
0\leq t\leq\displaystyle\frac{3(1-2^{-\gamma/3})\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\gamma/3}}{2^{-\gamma/3}\gamma C}
, it holds that

\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Proof.

See
[
3
]
for a rigorous argument, which applies directly to our context with no modification necessary. The spirit of the argument is that at a maximum
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
of
f(t,\cdot)
, it holds that
\Delta f(t,v)\leq 0
and therefore

\partial_{t}\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}=\partial_{t}f(t,v)=a[f]\Delta f-(2+\gamma)h[f]f\leq-(2+\gamma)h[f]f\lesssim\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}^{1+\gamma/3}\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-\gamma/3}

by Lemma
5.7
.
∎

We will use this estimate for smooth solutions with
H^{3}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{2}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
regularity and decay. By the Sobolev embedding type result of Lemma
A.5
, such solutions actually belong to
L^{\infty}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for each time
t
. This guarantees decay at infinity, which is enough to say that the maximum is attained in the above proof.

Now we arrive at the following
L^{\infty}
type existence theorem. It is completely analogous to the existence theorem of
[
5
]
. For this reason, we only sketch the proof, following their outline. We remark that we make no assumption on the entropy dissipation, since in our paper the entropy dissipation is effectively replaced by the monotonicity of the Fisher information.

Now we establish the local existence result of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Given
f_{\text{in}}
satisfying

(7.1)

\left\|f_{\text{in}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\text{in}}\langle v\rangle^{m}\;\mathrm{d}v\leq M

for some
M\geq 0
, let us approximate
f_{\text{in}}
with a mollification sequence
f^{\varepsilon}_{\text{in}}=f_{\text{in}}\ast\rho_{\varepsilon}
, with
\rho_{\varepsilon}
being a standard mollifier. As
f_{\text{in}}\geq 0
, it holds that
f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\text{in}}\;\mathrm{d}v
. Additionally,
f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup^{*}f_{\text{in}}
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, so we obtain by Jensen’s inequality and the weak
-\ast
lower semicontinuity of the
L^{\infty}
norm the convergence of the norms
\|f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}\to\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

Further,
f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\to f_{\text{in}}
in
L^{1}_{m}\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for every
1\leq p<\infty
. In light of these convergences, it holds for all
\varepsilon>0
small enough that

(7.2)

\left\|f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\langle v\rangle^{m}\;\mathrm{d}v\leq 2M.

Passing to a further subsequence if necessary, we have pointwise almost everywhere convergence
f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\to f_{\text{in}}
as well. Finally,
f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap H^{k}_{m/2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, since
L^{1}_{m}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{m/2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).
Since we are taking
m
large enough, we may apply Proposition
7.1
to obtain the existence of smooth classical solutions
f_{\varepsilon}:[0,T_{\varepsilon})\to[0,\infty)
to (
1.2
) with initial data
f^{\varepsilon}_{\text{in}}
. Here
T_{\varepsilon}>0
is the maximal time of existence for
f_{\varepsilon}
, which by Corollary
7.2
, is such that either
T_{\varepsilon}=+\infty
or
T_{\varepsilon}<+\infty
and is characterized by

\lim_{t\nearrow T_{\varepsilon}}\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}=\infty.

In order to construct from the family
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}
a solution to (
1.2
) with initial data
f_{\text{in}}
, we need first to obtain a lower bound on the maximal times of existence
T_{\varepsilon}
. The estimate of Lemma
7.3
ensures that

\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2\|f^{\varepsilon}_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 4M

for all
0\leq t\leq\frac{3(1-2^{-\gamma/3})(2M)^{\gamma/3}}{2^{-\gamma/3}\gamma C}\coloneqq T_{0}>0
. Therefore, we obtain
T_{\varepsilon}\geq T_{0}
for all
\varepsilon>0
. We now proceed to construct a solution on
[0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
in a sequence of steps.

Step One: Uniform Estimates.
We claim that the family
f_{\varepsilon}:[0,T_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
constructed above satisfies

\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{k}(t_{0},T_{0})\times B_{R}}\leq C(M,R,t,k)

uniformly in
\varepsilon
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
t_{0}>0
, and
R>0
. We prove this using the parabolic regularity theory and bootstrapping. The first observation is that the family
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}([0,T_{0}];L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
.
This is obtained by interpolating the conservation of mass
\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{1}}=\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}
for any
\varepsilon>0,t\geq 0
, with the uniform
L^{\infty}
estimates on
[0,T_{0}]
thanks to Lemma
7.3
and the choice of
T_{0}
. In doing so we obtain that
a[f_{\varepsilon}]
satisfies the ellipticity estimates of Lemma
5.4
, uniform in
\varepsilon
. In particular,
a[f_{\varepsilon}](t,v)\geq c(M,R)
on
[0,T_{0}]\times B_{R}
. By Lemma
5.7
,
h[f_{\varepsilon}]f_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}([0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
with the uniform bound

\|h[f_{\varepsilon}]f_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}^{1+\gamma/3}\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-\gamma/3}.

Therefore we find that on
[0,T_{0}]\times B_{R}
, the solutions
f_{\varepsilon}
satisfy a uniformly parabolic non-divergence form equation with bounded right-hand side:

\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}-a[f_{\varepsilon}]\Delta f_{\varepsilon}=-(2+\gamma)h[f_{\varepsilon}]f_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}([0,T_{0}]\times B_{R}).

Applying the Krylov-Safonov theory, we obtain
f_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\alpha}((t_{0},T_{0})\times B_{R})
, with an estimate independent of
\varepsilon
. As in
[
5
]
, we then iteratively apply the Schauder theory in a standard way; each time showing that the diffusion coefficients
a[f_{\varepsilon}]
and the reaction terms
h[f_{\varepsilon}]f_{\varepsilon}
are more regular (see Lemma
5.1
of
[
5
]
to see how pass regularity estimates from a function
g
to
a[g]
and
h[g]
).

Step Two: Compactness.
Applying standard compactness results, we may pass to a limit
f_{\varepsilon}\to f:[0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
, with the convergence happening in each of the following senses:

•

Strongly in
C^{k}((t_{0},T_{0})\times B_{R})
for each
k\in\mathbb{N},R>0,
and
0<t_{0}<T_{0}.

•

Pointwise a.e., in
[0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}^{3},

•

Weak-
\ast
in
L^{\infty}([0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}^{3})

•

Strongly in
L^{1}([0,T_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}).

The justifications are standard, and the same as in
[
5
]
. The first bullet point implies that the limit
f
is nonnegative,
f\geq 0
, and
C^{\infty}
regular on
(0,T_{0})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The latter three points are used to justify that the limit
f
is a solution. Notice that the strong
L^{1}_{t,v}
convergence is a corollary of the pointwise convergence and the uniform integrability guaranteed by the
L^{1}_{m}
bound. We remark that the entropy bound of
[
5
]
is not necessary for our purposes, which is why we do not assume it.

Step Three: Passing to a Limit.
We use the viscosity formulation. Because the limit
f
is smooth for positive time, we just need to show that it is a viscosity solution to (
1.2
). Because of the
C^{k}_{loc}((0,T_{0})\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
convergence, it suffices to prove that the nonlocal terms
a[f_{\varepsilon}]\to a[f]
and
h[f_{\varepsilon}]\to h[f]
converge locally uniformly. Let us focus on the convergence of the more singular terms,
h[f_{\varepsilon}]\to h[f]
. We aim to show that
h[f_{\varepsilon}]\to h[f]
uniformly on
(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})\times B_{R}
for arbitrary
0<\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<T_{0}
and
R>0
. This is a simple corollary of the fact that
f_{\varepsilon}\to f
locally uniformly. Indeed, fix
t\in(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
and
|v|<R
. For
\gamma=-3
, there is nothing to do. For
\gamma\in(-3,-2)
, we find

(7.3)

\displaystyle|h[f_{\varepsilon}](t,v)-h[f](t,v)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{R}(v)}|v-w|^{\gamma}|f_{\varepsilon}(t,v)-f(t,v)|\;\mathrm{d}v+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B_{R}(v)}|v-w|^{\gamma}|f_{\varepsilon}(t,v)-f(t,v)|\;\mathrm{d}v

(7.4)

\displaystyle\leq C(\gamma)\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)-f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}(v))}R^{3+\gamma}+R^{\gamma}\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)-f(t)\|_{L^{1}}

(7.5)

\displaystyle\leq C(\gamma)R^{3+\gamma}\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)-f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R})}+2\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}R^{\gamma}.

First pick
R>0
large so that the latter term is small. Then for this
R
, send
\varepsilon\to 0
and use the local uniform convergence of
f
to make the former term small. We have shown that
h[f_{\varepsilon}]\to h[f]
uniformly on
(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})\times B_{R}
for
R
large, which obviously implies the local uniform convergence for all
R
. The local uniform convergence of the coefficients ensures that the limit
f
is a viscosity solution to the Krieger-Strain equation (
1.2
). Then because
f
instantaneously becomes smooth, it is a classical solution on
(0,T_{0})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Step Four: Behavior at the Initial Time.
We argue that
f(t)\rightharpoonup^{*}f_{\text{in}}
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as
t\to 0
by compactness. First we claim that
\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
in the space
L^{\infty}([0,T_{0}];W^{-2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
. Using the equation, it suffices to check that
a[f_{\varepsilon}(t)]\Delta f_{\varepsilon}(t)
and
h[f_{\varepsilon}(t)]f_{\varepsilon}(t)
are bounded in
W^{-2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, uniformly in
t
and
\varepsilon
. The latter term
h[f_{\varepsilon}(t)]f_{\varepsilon}[t]
is in fact bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\subset W^{-2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, uniformly in
t\in[0,T_{0}]
and
\varepsilon>0
. Indeed,

\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)h[f_{\varepsilon}(t)]\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|f(t)\|_{L^{1}}^{1+\gamma/3}\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-\gamma/3}\lesssim\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}^{1+\gamma/3}\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-\gamma/3}

thanks to the conservation of
L^{1}
norm, Lemma
5.7
, and the choice of
T_{0}
. To handle
a[f_{\varepsilon}]\Delta f_{\varepsilon}
, fix
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and observe

(7.6)

\displaystyle\langle a[f_{\varepsilon}]\Delta f_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle_{W^{-2,\infty},W^{2,1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\varepsilon}\Delta(a[f_{\varepsilon}]\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}v

(7.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\varepsilon}\left((2+\gamma)h[f_{\varepsilon}]\varphi+2\nabla a[f_{\varepsilon}]\cdot\nabla\varphi+a[f_{\varepsilon}]\Delta\varphi\right)

(7.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim C(\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}})\|\varphi\|_{W^{2,1}}

as
\|f\ast|\cdot|^{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|f\ast|\cdot|^{1+\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|f\ast|\cdot|^{2+\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}}
may be controlled by
\|f\|_{L^{1}}
and
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}
, as per Lemmas
5.4
and
5.7
.
This verifies our claim that
\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}
is bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon>0
in the space
L^{\infty}([0,T_{0}];W^{-2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
.
As
f_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
in
L^{\infty}([0,T_{0}];L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
by our choice of
T_{0}
, we may apply the Aubin-Lions Lemma to deduce
f_{\varepsilon}
is compact in
C([0,T_{0}];W^{-1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
. Necessarily, the full sequence must converge to
f
. This combined with
f^{\varepsilon}_{\text{in}}\rightharpoonup^{*}f_{\text{in}}
allows us to deduce that
f
is weak
-\ast
continuous in time taking values in
W^{-1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, with limit
f_{\text{in}}
as
t\to 0
. By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, the same limit must be attained in the weak
-\ast

L^{\infty}
sense.

Step Five: Monotonicity of the Fisher Information.

Suppose further that
f_{\text{in}}
has finite Fisher information, i.e.,
i(f_{\text{in}})<\infty
. We aim to show that for the solution
f
constructed above, the Fisher information
i(f(\cdot))
decreases as a function of time. To do so, we use the same approximation
f_{\varepsilon}
. First of all, because the Fisher information is convex, it holds by Jensen’s inequality that

i(f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon})\leq i(f_{\text{in}})<\infty.

Furthermore,
\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{\text{in}}}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with
\|f^{\varepsilon}_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\text{in}}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Weak lower semicontinuity of the
H^{1}
norm implies

i(f_{\text{in}})=4\|\sqrt{f_{\text{in}}}\|_{\dot{H^{1}}}^{2}\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}4\|\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{\text{in}}}\|_{\dot{H^{1}}}^{2}=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}i(f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}),

so in fact we have
i(f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon})\to i(f_{\text{in}})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

In Theorem
1.3
of
[
4
]
, they extend the monotonicity of the Fisher information for the Landau-Coulomb equation to initial data belonging to
C^{\infty}\cap L^{1}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Since we have the exact same tools, an identical proof applies in our case. In particular, for the solutions
f_{\varepsilon}
corresponding to the initial data
f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon}=f_{\text{in}}\ast\rho_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}\cap L^{1}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, it holds that
i(f_{\varepsilon}(t))
decreases as a function of time. In particular, for each
t>0
,

\|\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}(t)}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}+4i(f_{\varepsilon}(t))\leq\|f_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{1}}+4i(f_{\text{in}}).

By the same reflexivity and weak lower semicontinuity argument, we obtain

i(f(t))\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}i(f_{\varepsilon}(t))\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}i(f_{\text{in}}^{\varepsilon})\leq i(f_{\text{in}})<\infty.

Now, fix
s\in(0,T_{0})
, and let
g_{\varepsilon}
solve the equation (
1.2
) with initial data
f_{\varepsilon}(s)\in H^{3}_{q}\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).
By the uniqueness guaranteed by Proposition
7.1
, the solutions
g_{\varepsilon}(t)=f_{\varepsilon}(t+s)
, and in particular
g_{\varepsilon}(t)\to f(t+s)
in
C^{k}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Applying the method of Golding, Gualdani, and Loher from Theorem
1.3
of
[
4
]
, we have that
i(g_{\varepsilon}(\cdot))
decreases as a function of time. Applying the reasoning of the previous paragraphs, we find for any
s>0
and
0<t<T_{0}-s
, there holds

i(f(t+s))\leq i(f(s)).

This is the desired monotonicity of the Fisher information. Let us quickly remark that the Jensen’s inequality based reasoning of the beginning was enough to show that
i(f(\cdot))
is bounded in time, which would suffice in proving global existence. To actually get monotonicity, we no longer had that
f_{\varepsilon}(t)
was a mollification of
f(t)
. This is where it became convenient to source from
[
4
]
.
∎

8.
Concluding the Global Existence Theorem

In this section we briefly put together the proof of the global existence theorem. It is just a matter of patching together the results of each section.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We begin with
0\leq f_{\text{in}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{1}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with sufficiently large
m
, and assume further that
f_{\text{in}}
has finite Fisher information, i.e.,
i(f_{\text{in}})<+\infty
. The short-time existence result of Theorem
1.3
furnishes a classical solution
f:[0,T_{*})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
of the Krieger-Strain equation (
1.1
), for some maximal interval
[0,T_{*})
. It must be shown that the assumption
i(f_{\text{in}})<+\infty
implies
T_{*}=+\infty
. Recall from the local theory,

T_{*}<\infty\text{ if and only if }\lim_{t\nearrow T_{*}}\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}=\infty.

We begin by noting that by the arguments of
[
11
,
12
]
, and the local theory,
f
is smooth and strictly positive on
(0,T_{*})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Next, Theorem
1.3
ensures that
i(f(\cdot))
is monotone decreasing in time, from which we apply Corollary
4.5
to obtain that
f\in L^{\infty}([0,T_{*});L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
. This control combined the moment estimates of Proposition
5.5
allow us to apply Theorem
5.8
to deduce that
\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}
stays bounded as
t
increases, and as a consequence the finite time blow-up criterion is never met.
∎

Appendix A
Technical Inequalities

We make extensive use of the following elementary but important lemmas. The first two lemmas are taken verbatim from
[
16
]
. For the body of the paper, we always assume
d=3
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Let
p^{\prime}
be the Hölder conjugate of
p
. If
-\frac{d}{p^{\prime}}<\mu<0
and
m>\frac{d}{p^{\prime}}+\mu
, then

(A.1)

\left\|f\ast|\cdot|^{\mu}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

for some
C=C(d,\mu,p,m).

In particular,

•

If
f\in L^{\infty}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
m>d+\mu
, then

\|f\ast|\cdot|^{\mu}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

for some
C=C(d,\mu,m)>0
.

•

If
f\in L^{2}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
m>\frac{d}{2}+\mu>0
, then

\|f\ast|\cdot|^{\mu}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

for some
C=C(d,\mu,m)>0
.

Lemma A.2
.

For
-d<\mu\leq-d/2,\theta>\mu+d
, and
g\in L^{2}_{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has

\|g\ast|\cdot|^{\mu}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{2}_{\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Suppose that
-\frac{d}{p^{\prime}}<\mu<0
and
m>\frac{d}{p^{\prime}}
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(\mu,d,p)
such that

\left\|f\ast|\cdot|^{\mu}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{-p^{\prime}\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Proof.

It is equivalent to

(A.2)

\left\|f\ast|\cdot|^{\mu}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\langle v\rangle^{p^{\prime}\mu}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

From Lemma
A.1
, we have that

(A.3)

\left\|f\ast|\cdot|^{\mu}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

On the other hand, write

(f\ast|\cdot|^{\mu})(v)\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|w|^{p^{\prime}\mu}\langle v-w\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}\;\mathrm{d}w\right)^{1/p^{\prime}}.

To estimate the latter term, split the integral into

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|w|^{p^{\prime}\mu}\langle v-w\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}\;\mathrm{d}w=\int_{|w|<v/2}|w|^{p^{\prime}\mu}\langle v-w\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}\;\mathrm{d}w+\int_{|w|\geq|v|/2}|w|^{p^{\prime}\mu}\langle v-w\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}\;\mathrm{d}w\coloneqq I_{1}+I_{2}.

Where
|w|<|v|/2
, we have
|v-w|>|v|/2
and so
\langle v-w\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}\lesssim\langle v\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}
, so

I_{1}\lesssim\langle v\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}|v|^{p^{\prime}\mu+d}\lesssim\langle v\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m+p^{\prime}\mu+d}\leq\langle v\rangle^{p^{\prime}\mu}.

thanks to
\frac{d}{p^{\prime}}+\mu>0
.
For
I_{2}
,

(A.4)

I_{2}\leq\left(\frac{|v|}{2}\right)^{p^{\prime}\mu}\int_{|w|\geq|v|/2}\langle v-w\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}\;\mathrm{d}w\lesssim|v|^{p^{\prime}\mu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle w\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}\;\mathrm{d}w

Since
p^{\prime}m>d
, we get

(A.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle w\rangle^{-p^{\prime}m}\;\mathrm{d}w<+\infty.

Hence, from (
A.3
) and (
A.5
),

(A.6)

(f\ast|\cdot|^{\mu})(v)\leq C\min(1,|v|^{p^{\prime}\mu})\|f\|_{L^{p}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\langle v\rangle^{p^{\prime}\mu}\|f\|_{L^{p}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

∎

The following lemma ensures that we may interpolate with negative weights, which is a need that naturally arises in proving the propagation of
H^{k}
norms.

Lemma A.4
.

For
q,\theta\geq 0
, and
\delta>0

\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{q}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{2q+\theta}}+\delta\|D^{2}f\|_{L^{2}_{-\theta}}.

As a corollary, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a constant
C=C(k,d)
and an exponent
m
depending on
k,q
and
\theta
such that

\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k}_{q}}\leq C(\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m}}+\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k+1}_{-\theta}}).

Proof.

Integrating by parts,

(A.7)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{q}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\langle v\rangle^{2q}|\nabla f|^{2}=-2q\int f\langle v\rangle^{2q-2}v\cdot\nabla f-\int\langle v\rangle^{2q+\theta}f\langle v\rangle^{-\theta}\Delta f

(A.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}\varepsilon\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{q}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{q-1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{2q+\theta}}^{2}+\delta\|D^{2}f\|_{L^{2}_{-\theta}}^{2}.

Choosing
\varepsilon>0
small enough we find

\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{q}}\lesssim_{q}\frac{1}{\delta}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{2q+\theta}}+\delta\|D^{2}f\|_{L^{2}_{-\theta}},

for
\delta>0
a parameter at our disposal.

The general case follows from the following argument

\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k}_{q}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k-1}_{2q+\theta}}+\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k+1}_{-\theta}}\lesssim\delta\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k}_{q}}+\frac{1}{\delta}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k-2}_{m}}+\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k+1}_{-\theta}}.

Choose
\delta>0
small to absorb the
\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k}_{q}}
norm into the lefthand side and continue iterating down to
k=0
.
∎

Since at various points we have needed to control weighted
L^{\infty}_{m}
norms with Sobolev norms, we also need the following lemma.

Lemma A.5
.

For any
m\in\mathbb{R}
the embedding
H^{2}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\subset L^{\infty}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
holds with the following inequality:

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{m}}\lesssim_{m}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m}}+\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{2}_{m}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{2}_{m}}.

The only point of this proof is to show that we may control from above
\|\langle v\rangle^{m}f\|_{H^{2}}
, which is the definition of the
H^{2}_{m}
norm taken in
[
16
]
, by our choice of the
H^{2}_{m}
norm, which is
\sum_{j=0}^{2}\|D^{j}f\|_{L^{2}_{m}}
. Of course, this will hold for any
H^{k}_{m}
and any dimension, but we prove it for
k=2,\ \text{dim}=3
out of convenience.

Proof.

The standard Sobolev embedding
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
yields

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{m}}\lesssim\|\langle v\rangle^{m}f\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m}}+\|D^{2}(\langle v\rangle^{m}f)\|_{L^{2}}

after interpolating out the first order term.

Seeing as

(A.9)

\displaystyle D^{2}(\langle v\rangle^{m}f)

\displaystyle=\langle v\rangle^{m}D^{2}f+2\nabla(\langle v\rangle^{m})\otimes\nabla f+fD^{2}(\langle v\rangle^{m})

(A.10)

\displaystyle=\langle v\rangle^{m}D^{2}f+2m\langle v\rangle^{m-2}v\otimes\nabla f+f(m\langle v\rangle^{m-2}\text{Id}+m(m-2)\langle v\rangle^{m-4}v\otimes v),

we may control

\|D^{2}(\langle v\rangle^{m}f)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{m}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{2}_{m}}+\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{m-1}}+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m-2}}.

To control
\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{m-1}}
, we use the usual integration by parts and Cauchy’s inequality argument:

(A.11)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{m-1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla f|^{2}\langle v\rangle^{2(m-1)}\;\mathrm{d}v=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}2(m-1)\langle v\rangle^{2(m-2)}fv\cdot\nabla f\;\mathrm{d}v-\int f\Delta f\langle v\rangle^{2(m-1)}\;\mathrm{d}v

(A.12)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{m-1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m-2}}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m-1}}^{2}+\|D^{2}f\|_{L^{2}_{m-1}}^{2}.

Taking
\varepsilon>0
small enough and cheaply increasing weights, we find
\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}_{m-1}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m}}+\|D^{2}f\|_{L^{2}_{m}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{2}_{m}}.

Cheaply increasing weights again, we obtain

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{m}}\lesssim_{m}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m}}+\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{2}_{m}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{2}_{m}}.

as desired.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Proposition
7.1
:
H^{3}_{q}
Local Existence Theory

We establish the local existence theory for
H^{3}_{q}
initial data.
To do so, we follow the work of Snelson on the isotropic Boltzmann equation in
[
16
]
. The entire argument therein transfers almost directly to the isotropic Landau case. In fact, it is even simpler. Because of this, we merely provide a sketch of the proof, pointing out the differences when they arise. The method is standard: we linearly interpolate between (
1.2
) and the heat equation and prove the propagation of weighted Sobolev norms for solutions to such equations, and then apply the method of continuity and an iteration argument.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
q>0
and
m>\frac{7}{2}+\gamma
and
T>0
be fixed, and let
f\in L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{k}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
and
R\in L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
be fixed. Assume further that
f\geq 0
. For
\sigma\in[0,1]
, let
g_{\sigma}
be the solution of

(B.1)

\partial_{t}g_{\sigma}=\sigma\mathcal{Q}_{KS}(f,g_{\sigma})+(1-\sigma)\Delta g_{\sigma}+R,

on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then

(B.2)

\|g_{\sigma}(t)\|_{H^{k}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq\left(\|g_{\sigma}(0)\|_{H^{k}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+C\int_{0}^{t}\|R(s)\|_{L^{2}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}\ ds\right)\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}1+\|f(s)\|_{H^{k}_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\ ds\right)

for
C=C(\gamma,k,q,m)
absolute and independent of
\sigma
.

The proof is very similar to both the proof of global existence and the proof of Lemma
6.2
in
[
16
]
.

Proof.

For aesthetic purposes, we denote
g_{\sigma}
simply by
g
. Differentiate the equation by
\partial^{\beta}
for a fixed multi-index
\beta
of order
|\beta|\leq k
. Then multiply by
\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial^{\beta}g
. We get

(B.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}(\partial^{\beta}g)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\sigma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial^{\beta}\mathcal{Q}_{KS}(f,g)\;\mathrm{d}v+(1-\sigma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial^{\beta}g\Delta\partial^{\beta}g\;\mathrm{d}v

(B.4)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}R\partial^{\beta}g\;\mathrm{d}v.

The latter two terms are straightforward. As in
[
16
]
,

(B.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}R\partial^{\beta}g\;\mathrm{d}v\leq\|R\|_{L^{2}_{q}}\|g\|_{H^{k}_{q}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|R\|_{L^{2}_{q}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|g\|_{H^{k}_{q}}^{2}

and

(B.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial^{\beta}g\Delta\partial^{\beta}g\;\mathrm{d}v\leq C_{q}\|g\|_{H^{k}_{q}}^{2}

where the proof is the exact same. The real work is in handling the
\partial^{\beta}\mathcal{Q}_{KS}(f,g)
term. Recall the definition of
\mathcal{Q}_{1},\mathcal{Q}_{2}
from (
6.4
). By the product rule,

\partial^{\beta}\mathcal{Q}_{1}(f,g)=\sum_{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=\beta}\partial^{\beta_{1}}a[f]\partial^{\beta_{2}}\Delta g

and

\partial^{\beta}\mathcal{Q}_{2}(f,g)=-(2+\gamma)\sum_{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=\beta}\partial^{\beta_{1}}h[f]\partial^{\beta_{2}}g.

We handle the
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
terms separately, using the above identities to estimate the first term of (
B.3
). As in Section
6
, we have to take care estimating depending on where the derivatives fall.

Beginning with the
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
terms, we will prove in all cases there holds

(B.7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial^{\beta}g\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\partial^{\beta_{1}}f,\partial^{\beta_{2}}g)\;\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial^{\beta}g\partial^{\beta_{1}}a[f]\Delta\partial^{\beta_{2}}g\;\mathrm{d}v\lesssim\|g\|_{H^{k}_{q}}^{2}\|f\|_{H^{k}_{m}}.

Case One: Estimating the
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
terms when
|\beta_{2}|\leq k-2
.
In this case, we find immediately that for
m>\frac{7}{2}+\gamma
, there holds

(B.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial^{\beta}g\partial^{\beta_{1}}a[f]\Delta\partial^{\beta_{2}}g\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{H^{|\beta|}_{q}}\|g\|_{H^{2+|\beta_{2}|}_{q}}\|\partial^{\beta_{1}}a[f]\|_{L^{\infty}}

(B.9)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{H^{k}_{q}}^{2}\|f\|_{H^{|\beta_{1}|}_{m}}\leq\|g\|_{H^{k}_{q}}^{2}\|f\|_{H^{k}_{m}},

applying Lemma
6.3
to pass from the first line to the second.

Case Two: Estimating the
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
terms when
|\beta_{2}|=k
.
Integrating by parts, we find

(B.10)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial^{\beta}ga[f]\Delta\partial^{\beta}g\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla\partial^{\beta}g|^{2}\langle v\rangle^{2q}a[f]\;\mathrm{d}v-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}g\nabla\partial^{\beta}g\cdot\nabla\left(\langle v\rangle^{2q}a[f]\right)\;\mathrm{d}v

(B.11)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int\nabla\left(\partial^{\beta}g\right)^{2}\cdot\nabla\left(\langle v\rangle^{2q}a[f]\right)\;\mathrm{d}v=\frac{1}{2}\int(\partial^{\beta}g)^{2}\Delta(a[f]\langle v\rangle^{2q})\;\mathrm{d}v.

We record

\Delta(a[f]\langle v\rangle^{2q})=(2+\gamma)h[f]\langle v\rangle^{2q}+4q\langle v\rangle^{2q-2}\nabla a[f]\cdot v+a[f](4q(q-1)|v|^{2}\langle v\rangle^{2q-4}+6q\langle v\rangle^{2q-2})

in dimension

From Lemma
A.1
we find
\|a[f]\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}_{m}}
for

6.3

for

6.3

\|h[f]\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\begin{cases}\|f\|_{H^{2}}&\gamma=-3,\\
\|f\|_{H^{2}_{m}}&-3<\gamma<-2,\quad m>\frac{7}{2}+\gamma.\end{cases}

The Coulomb case is a simple application of the Sobolev embedding
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

, we arrive at the estimate

(B.12)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial^{\beta}ga[f]\Delta\partial^{\beta}g\;\mathrm{d}v

\mathcal{Q}_{1}
terms when
|\beta_{2}|=k-1
.

In this case, it is convenient to remember that we are not simply estimating
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial^{\beta}g\mathcal{Q}(\partial^{\beta_{1}}f,\partial^{\beta_{2}}g)\;\mathrm{d}v

As we will see, keeping the sum allows us to introduce a useful Laplacian term. Let us define

(B.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial_{i}G\partial_{i}a[f]\Delta G\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial_{i}G\partial_{i}a[f]\partial_{jj}G\;\mathrm{d}v

(B.14)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{j}\left(\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial_{i}a[f]\right)\partial_{i}G\partial_{j}G\;\mathrm{d}v-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}\partial_{i}a[f]\partial_{i}(\partial_{j}G)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\coloneqq J_{1}+J_{2}.

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2+\gamma}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q}|\nabla G|^{2}h[f]\;\mathrm{d}v+q\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2q-2}v_{i}\partial_{i}a[f](\partial_{j}G)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|_{H^{k}_{q}}^{2}\|f\|_{H^{2}_{m}}
