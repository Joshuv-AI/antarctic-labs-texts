Obstructions to distinguished Riemannian metrics via Lorentzian geometry
Amir Babak Aazami

Abstract
. We approach the problem of finding obstructions to curvature distinguished Riemannian metrics by considering Lorentzian metrics to which they are dual in a suitable sense. Obstructions to the latter then yield obstructions to the former. This framework applies both locally and globally, including to compact manifolds, and is sensitive to various aspects of curvature. Here we apply it in two different ways. First, by embedding a Riemannian manifold into a Lorentzian one and utilizing Penrose’s “plane wave limit,” we find necessary local conditions, in terms of the Hessian of just one function, for large classes of Riemannian metrics to contain within them those that have parallel Ricci tensor, or are Ricci-flat, or are locally symmetric. Second, by considering Riemannian metrics dual to constant curvature Lorentzian metrics via a type of Wick rotation, we are able to rule out the existence of a family of compact Riemannian manifolds (in all dimensions) that deviate from constant curvature in a precise sense.



1.
Introduction & Overview of Results

What role may the Lorentzian geometry of general relativity play in the service of Riemannian geometry? In this article we promote the viewpoint that techniques and results specific to Lorentzian geometry can be used to obtain
nonexistence
results in the Riemannian setting. In what follows we shall demonstrate two ways in which this can happen, one local, one global. While these two ways are quite different in flavor from each other, they both yield obstructions to the existence of distinguished Riemannian metrics, and they both rest firmly on the belief that it is worthwhile to ask whether a given Riemannian metric is somehow or other related to a distinguished Lorentzian metric.

1.1.
The local case.

We show in Section
2
that it is possible to “simplify” a Riemannian metric
g
— in such a way that some features of its curvature are preserved, such as the condition of being Ricci-flat or of being locally symmetric — by realizing
g
as the induced metric on an appropriately chosen submanifold of a Lorentzian manifold. There are precedents to this approach (e.g.,
[
FG85
]
), but our motivation derives from the following fact, well known to practitioners of general relativity (see, e.g.,
[
Bla11
,
And04
]
): Every Lorentzian manifold admits, locally, a gravitational
plane wave
metric via an appropriate limit, known as Penrose’s “plane wave limit”
[
Pen76
]
. As remarked by Penrose himself, his construction is analogous to that of the tangent space to a manifold at a given point — except that here the point is replaced by the integral curve of a “null” vector field; i.e., one that is nonzero but orthogonal to itself. (In Section
2.1
and the Appendix, we provide a self-contained description of Penrose’s plane wave limit and of plane wave metrics.) Putting aside the connection to gravitational physics, of interest for us here are the following two features of this construction:

i.

A plane wave metric is typically a
far simpler
metric than the original Lorentzian metric to which it was a limit. Indeed, when written down in so called
Brinkmann coordinates
(see (
220
) of the Appendix), only one of its metric components is nonconstant. This has the desirable consequence that most of the components of its curvature 4-tensor vanish.

ii.

By a more general argument due to Geroch
[
Ger69
]
, certain curvature properties of the Lorentzian metric are
preserved
in this limit — and this despite the fact that Penrose’s limit is highly coordinate-dependent. Geroch coined these the “hereditary” properties of the limit; in Proposition
1
of Section
2.1
, we provide for convenience a proof of this (well known) fact when the metric is either Ricci-flat, or locally symmetric, or locally conformally flat, or has parallel Ricci tensor. (In fact, if any covariant tensor constructed from the Riemann curvature tensor and its derivatives happens to vanish for the Lorentzian metric, then it will also vanish for its plane wave limit.)

How may this state of affairs be of use to a Riemannian geometer? As follows: Suppose that one has a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, viewed locally in some choice of coordinates, and one would like to know, e.g., if it has parallel (i.e., covariantly constant) Ricci tensor, or if it is Ricci-flat, or if it is locally symmetric, by which is meant, respectively, that

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\text{Ric}=0\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\text{Ric}=0\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\nabla\text{Rm}=0,

where
Ric
and
Rm
are the Ricci tensor and curvature 4-tensor of
g
, and
\nabla
is its Levi-Civita connection. If any of these was the case, then the components
g_{ij}
would satisfy a certain system of PDEs in the given coordinates. Now, instead of examining these PDEs directly, suppose that one realizes
(M,g)
as a time-symmetric slice of the Lorentzian manifold

(\mathbb{R}\times M,-(dt)^{2}+g),

and then takes the plane wave limit of this — the motivation being, of course, precisely points i. and ii. above. The virtue of a time-symmetric embedding is that curvature properties of
g
either carry over directly to
-(dt)^{2}+g
or else are only slightly altered (see Lemma
1
in Section
2.2
). (While a time-symmetric embedding is, for this reason, the most desirable choice for our purposes, in Propositions
5
and
6
of the Appendix we show that there is a rigid relationship between Riemannian metrics and the class of Lorentzian
Brinkmann spacetimes
of which gravitational plane waves are an example.) Our main result is that, by applying Penrose’s plane wave limit to
g
in this way, one can directly identify — with very little “initial data” — quite large classes of Riemannian metrics that
don’t
satisfy (
1
); thanks to Penrose’s limit, all that is needed is the Hessian of just one function:

Theorem 1
.

Let
(\bar{g}_{ij}(r))_{i,j=2,\dots,n}
be a symmetric, positive-definite matrix of smooth functions
\bar{g}_{ij}(r)
, and let
\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bar{g}$}}\!
denote the set of all Riemannian metrics
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{R}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{(r,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})\}
of the form

(2)

\displaystyle g_{\scalebox{0.4}{R}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=(dr)^{2}+g_{ij}(r,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})dx^{i}dx^{j}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptg_{ij}(r,0,\dots,0)=\bar{g}_{ij}(r).

On
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}=\{(r,t,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})\}
, define the Lorentzian plane wave metric

\displaystyle h_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{PW}}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\bar{g}_{22}(r)&\cdots&\bar{g}_{2n}(r)\\
0&0&\bar{g}_{32}(r)&\cdots&\bar{g}_{3n}(r)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\bar{g}_{n2}(r)&\cdots&\bar{g}_{nn}(r)\end{pmatrix}\cdot

Express
h_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{PW}}}
in “Brinkmann coordinates” as in (
220
), with corresponding function
H(u,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})
given by (
236
). Let
\Delta H
denote the
(
Euclidean
)
Laplacian of
H
with respect to
x^{2},\dots,x^{n}
. Then the following is true
:

1.

If
\Delta H
is nonconstant, then no
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{R}}\in\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bar{g}$}}\!
can have parallel Ricci tensor.

2.

If
\Delta H\neq 0
, then no
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{R}}\in\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bar{g}$}}\!
can be Ricci-flat.

3.

If any
H_{iju}\neq 0
, then no
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{R}}\in\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bar{g}$}}\!
can be locally symmetric.

Let us make a few remarks about this result.

i.

First, observe that there is no obstruction to a Riemannian metric taking the form
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{R}}}
in (
2
), because every Riemannian metric can be put locally in such a form; see (
40
) in Section
2.2
.

ii.

Second, as shown in (
236
), the function
H(u,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})
is always a quadratic polynomial in
x^{2},\dots,x^{n}
(though not necessarily in
u
), so that
\Delta H
is always a function of
u
alone.

iii.

Finally, the following analogy is helpful in interpreting Theorem
1
. Suppose on the closed unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one specifies a set of functions
\{\bar{g}_{ij}\}
on the boundary sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and then asks whether a Riemannian metric
g
exists in the unit ball that is both Ricci-flat (say)
and
such that each component
g_{ij}
approaches the corresponding function
\bar{g}_{ij}
at
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Theorem
1
is akin to this — except that an
(n-1)
-dimensional boundary
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is replaced by a
one
-dimensional one (namely, the
r
-axis). Viewed in this light, Theorem
1
can be interpreted as providing (local) obstructions to curvature distinguished Riemannian metrics based on very little “initial” or “boundary” data. This data is encoded in the function
H
, which is
uniquely
associated to the given set
\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bar{g}$}}
.

1.2.
The global case.

We show in Section
3
that a certain one-parameter family of Riemannian metrics
g_{\scalebox{0.5}{\emph{$\lambda$}}}
cannot exist on any compact manifold
M
— not by appealing directly to a topological argument, or by constructing vector bundles over
M
, or even by examining the conformal class of
g_{\scalebox{0.5}{\emph{$\lambda$}}}
— but rather by showing that if such a
g_{\scalebox{0.5}{\emph{$\lambda$}}}
existed, then so would a certain Lorentzian metric
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
on
M
, the existence of which is known to be impossible. The
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
we have in mind is a
spherical space form
; i.e., a Lorentzian metric with constant positive sectional curvature. In the noncompact setting, de Sitter spacetime is the classic example, on
M=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n}
; however, when
M
is compact, it is well known, by a landmark result due collectively to
[
CM62
]
and
[
Kli96
]
, that there are no such space forms in any dimension. In order to “apply” the nonexistence of such a metric to Riemannian geometry, we must first of all decide how to form Riemannian metrics from Lorentzian ones. There are several ways of doing this in the literature. For example, one may do this in the manner of the Gibbons-Hawking ansatz for producing gravitational instantons in dimension 4
[
GH78
,
GH79
]
. Or one can embed a Riemannian manifold as a submanifold of a Lorentzian one — Proposition
6
in Section
Appendix: the geometry of plane waves and Penrose’s limit
provides a particularly interesting example of this. Or one can pursue a general form of Wick rotation, one that is independent of coordinates, by picking a unit timelike vector field
T
on a Lorentzian manifold
(M,g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}})
and forming the metric

(3)

\displaystyle g\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}+2T^{\flat_{\scalebox{0.3}{\emph{L}}}}\otimes T^{\flat_{\scalebox{0.3}{\emph{L}}}},

where
T^{\flat_{\scalebox{0.3}{\emph{L}}}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(T,\cdot)
(“unit timelike” here means that
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(T,T)=-1
). Note that
g(T,T)=1
and
T^{\flat}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g(T,\cdot)=-g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(T,\cdot)
, so that we may just as well have expressed (
3
) as

g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}=g-2T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat}.

All three of these constructions yield a Riemannian metric that is somehow “dual” to a Lorentzian one. In this paper we center our attention on (
3
), and observe that it immediately yields nonexistence results: For if “
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
” in (
3
) does not exist (e.g., if it’s a compact spherical space form), then for any choice of
T
neither can the corresponding Riemannian metric
g
. The problem, however, is that for an arbitrary choice of
T
the curvature of
g
can vary wildly from that of
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
(see, e.g.,
[
Ole14
]
); therefore, we must take care to choose both a “nonexistent”
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}

and
a suitable choice of
T
, preferably one for which the corresponding Riemannian metric
g
has distinguished curvature. Here we take our cue from de Sitter spacetime itself, which is the warped product
(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n},-dt^{2}+f(t)\mathring{g})
, where
\mathring{g}
is the standard Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and where

f(t)=r^{2}\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{t}{r}\right),

with
r
the radius of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. With respect to this metric, the vector field
T\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\partial_{t}
is not only unit timelike, it is also
closed
:
dT^{\flat}=0
. The condition of being closed plays a very important role here. As we show in Proposition
4
of Section
3.2
, for any such
T
the 2-tensor
\nabla T^{\flat}
will be symmetric, one consequence of which is that the curvature 4-tensors
Rm
and
\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}
of
g
and
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
in (
3
) will be related to each in a very direct way:

\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}=\text{Rm}+\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat}.

(Here
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection of
g
and

{\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}

is the Kulkarni-Nomizu product.) In such a case, our main result is the following:

Theorem 2
.

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian

-manifold
(n\geq 3)
and
T
a unit length closed vector field on
M
. For

takes the form

\displaystyle\emph{\text{Rm}}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda g\raisebox{0.40881pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g-2\lambda g\raisebox{0.40881pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})-\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.40881pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat}

if and only if the Lorentzian metric

has constant curvature

is flat, and its universal cover splits isometrically as a product

Thus the nonexistence of compact Lorentzian spherical space forms rules out the existence of a certain family of Riemannian metrics that are themselves closely related to (Riemannian) space forms (as evidenced by the constant curvature term
\frac{1}{2}\lambda g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g
in (
2
)). For such choices of

and
g
, we would argue, the relationship (
3
) is valuable.

With that said, we now begin in the local setting, with the construction of a Riemannian version of Penrose’s “plane wave limit.”

2.
Local obstructions to Distinguished Riemannian metrics

2.1.
Penrose’s plane wave limit

Penrose’s “plane wave limit”
[
Pen76
]
is in fact a special case of a more general notion of “spacetime limit” due to Geroch
[
Ger69
]
. Here we give a self-contained description of Penrose’s limit, though we point out that the material in this Section can be found (in different notation) in
[
Ger69
,
Pen76
,
Phi06
]
. Consider a Lorentzian metric
g
(with Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
) given locally in so called “null coordinates”
(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2},\dots,x^{n})

(29)

\displaystyle(g_{ij})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0&\cdots&0\\

(In fact every Lorentzian metric

\partial_{0}=\text{grad}\,x^{1}

\nabla_{\!\partial_{0}}{\partial_{0}}=0

\displaystyle(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2},\dots,x^{n})\overset{\varphi_{\scriptscriptstyle\varepsilon}}{\mapsto}(\tilde{x}^{0},\tilde{x}^{1},\tilde{x}^{2},\dots,\tilde{x}^{n})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\Big(x^{0},\frac{x^{1}}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{x^{2}}{\varepsilon},\dots,\frac{x^{n}}{\varepsilon}\Big),

where each component

\displaystyle(h_{\scriptscriptstyle\varepsilon})_{11}(\tilde{x}^{0},\tilde{x}^{1},\tilde{x}^{2},\dots,\tilde{x}^{n})\!\!

As it turns out, (

2.2.
The plane wave limit of a Riemannian manifold

Now that we have a firm understanding of Penrose’s plane wave limit, we can make use of Proposition
1
to arrive at results purely in Riemannian geometry. To set the stage, let
(M,\bar{g})
be a Riemannian
n
-manifold, and recall the existence of
semigeodesic coordinates
, namely, at each point of
M
, local coordinates
(r,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})
exist in which the integral curves
s\mapsto(s,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})
of
\partial_{r}
are geodesics normal to the level sets of
r
:

(40)

\displaystyle\bar{g}=(dr)^{2}+\bar{g}_{ij}(r,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})dx^{i}dx^{j}.

It follows that
\partial_{r}=\text{grad}_{\scalebox{.5}{$\bar{g}$}}r
and that
r
is a local distance function (see, e.g.,
[
Lee18
, Proposition 6.41, p. 182]
). Choose such coordinates in a neighborhood
\mathscr{U}\subseteq M
, and henceforth restrict
\bar{g}
to
\mathscr{U}
. In order to take the “plane wave limit of
(\mathscr{U},\bar{g}|_{\mathscr{U}})

(\mathscr{U},\bar{g}|_{\mathscr{U}})
as a “time-symmetric” hypersurface in the
(n+1)
-dimensional Lorentzian manifold

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\times\mathscr{U}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptg\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=-(dt)^{2}+\bar{g}.

In the coordinate basis
\{\partial_{t},\partial_{r},\partial_{2},\dots,\partial_{n}\}
, this Lorentzian metric is

\displaystyle(g_{\alpha\beta})=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\bar{g}_{22}&\bar{g}_{23}&\cdots&\bar{g}_{2n}\\
0&0&\bar{g}_{32}&\bar{g}_{33}&\cdots&\bar{g}_{3n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\bar{g}_{n2}&\bar{g}_{n3}&\cdots&\bar{g}_{nn}\end{pmatrix}\cdot

In what follows, we reserve the Latin indices

for the coordinates

and Greek indices
\alpha,\beta
for

. The reason for this choice of embedding is the direct relationship between the curvatures of
\bar{g}
and

:

Lemma 1
.

If
\bar{g}
in (
40
) is locally symmetric, then so is
g
in (
41
). If
\bar{g}
has parallel Ricci tensor, then so does
g
. Finally, if
\bar{g}
is Einstein,
\emph{\text{Ric}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{g}}=\lambda\bar{g}
, then
\emph{\text{Ric}}_{\scriptscriptstyle g}=\lambda(g+(dt)^{2})

Proof.

Let
X,Y,Z
be the lifts to
\mathbb{R}\times\mathscr{U}
of vector fields on
\mathscr{U}
, and let
\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle g},R_{\scriptscriptstyle g}
and
\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{g}},R_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{g}}
denote the Levi-Civita connections and curvature endomorphisms of
g
and
\bar{g}
, respectively. Then

\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle g}_{\!X}{Y}=\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{g}}_{\!X}{Y}
, while
\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle g}_{\!X}{\partial_{t}}=\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle g}_{\!\partial_{t}}X=\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle g}_{\!\partial_{t}}\partial_{t}=0
; see, e.g.,
[

It follows that if
\bar{g}
is locally symmetric,
\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{g}}\text{Rm}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{g}}=0
, then so is
g
, and if
\bar{g}
has parallel Ricci tensor,
\nabla^{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{g}}\text{Ric}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{g}}=0
, then so does
g
. Similarly,

\text{Ric}_{\scriptscriptstyle g}(X,Y)=\text{Ric}_{\scriptscriptstyle\bar{g}}(X,Y)=\lambda\bar{g}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\text{Ric}_{\scriptscriptstyle g}(\partial_{t},\cdot)=0,

so that the equality
\text{Ric}_{\scriptscriptstyle g}=\lambda(g+(dt)^{2})
holds.
∎

Proof.

For any Riemannian extension
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{R}}}\in\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{\emph{$\bar{g}$}}}
, simply form the Lorentzian metric
g\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=-(dt)^{2}+g_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{R}}}

as in (
41
), and use Proposition
2
.
∎

This result is much more appealing when the plane wave metric (
46
) is expressed in “Brinkmann coordinates” (see (
220
) in the Appendix), for in such coordinates its curvature 4-tensor is effectively determined by the Hessian of just one function:

Theorem 1
.

Let
(\bar{g}_{ij}(r)),\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{\emph{$\bar{g}$}}}
, and
h_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{PW}}}
be as in Proposition
3
, and express
h_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{PW}}}
in “Brinkmann coordinates” as in (
220
), with corresponding function
H(u,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})
given by (
236
). Let
\Delta H
denote the
(
Euclidean
)
Laplacian of
H
with respect to
x^{2},\dots,x^{n}
. Then the following is true
:

1.

If
\partial_{u}(\Delta H)\neq 0
, then no
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{R}}\in\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{\emph{$\bar{g}$}}}
has parallel Ricci tensor.

2.

If
\Delta H\neq 0
, then no
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{R}}\in\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{\emph{$\bar{g}$}}}
is Ricci-flat.

3.

If any

(

)
, then no
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{R}}\in\mathscr{F}_{\scalebox{0.5}{\emph{$\bar{g}$}}}
is locally symmetric.

Proof.

As shown in Proposition
6
of the Appendix, the plane wave (
46
) is in so called “Rosen coordinates.” An isometry will then put
h_{\scalebox{.4}{PW}}
in the “Brinkmann coordinates”
(v,u,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})
shown in (
220
),

\displaystyle h_{\scalebox{.4}{PW}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
1&H&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix},

with

a function quadratic in
x^{2},\dots,x^{n}
(see (
236
)). In this form, it is straightforward to verify, using the Christoffel symbols (
231
), that the only nonvanishing components of
Rm
and
Ric
are

\text{Rm}(\partial_{i},\partial_{u},\partial_{u},\partial_{j})=-\frac{1}{2}H_{ij}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\text{Ric}(\partial_{u},\partial_{u})=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta H.

In particular, if

, then

will not be Ricci-flat. Similarly, the only nonvanishing components of

and

(when

(\nabla_{\!\partial_{u}}\text{Rm})(\partial_{i},\partial_{u},\partial_{u},\partial_{j})=-\frac{1}{2}H_{iju}\hskip 7.22743pt,\hskip 7.22743pt(\nabla_{\!\partial_{u}}\text{Ric})(\partial_{u},\partial_{u})=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}(\Delta H).

In particular, if any

, then

will not be locally symmetric; if

, then

will not have parallel Ricci tensor. Applying Proposition

∎

3.
Global Obstructions to Distinguished Riemannian metrics

3.1.
Introduction

We now switch our focus from the local to the global, and search for obstructions to distinguished Riemannian metrics in the compact setting. The obstructions we seek are to metrics that are deformations of constant curvature metrics. Recall that a Riemannian metric
g
has
constant
(
sectional
)
curvature
if its Riemann curvature 4-tensor
Rm
satisfies

\displaystyle\text{Rm}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. When
\lambda>0
, the Riemannian metric
g
is called a
spherical space form
; such metrics have been classified in
[
Wol72
]
, and include among them the standard round spheres
(\mathbb{S}^{n},\mathring{g})
, as well as the class of
Lens spaces

L(p,q)
in dimension 3. However, there are no such examples in the Lorentzian world. Indeed, by
[
CM62
]
and
[
Kli96
]
, there are no Lorentzian spherical space forms when
M
is compact, a foundational result in Lorentzian geometry (see
[
Lun15
]
for a comprehensive account). They only exist in the noncompact setting, the canonical one being
de Sitter spacetime

(\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n},g_{\scalebox{0.4}{dS}})
(
n\geq 3
), which is the warped product
(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1},-(dt)^{2}+f(t)\mathring{g})
, where
\mathring{g}
is the standard Riemannian metric above and

f(t)=r^{2}\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{t}{r}\right),

with
r
the radius of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
; consult, e.g.,
[
Har17
]
and
[
BEE96
, p. 183ff.]
. This is a complete Lorentzian manifold with constant positive curvature (
\lambda=1/r^{2}
). The starting point of our analysis is the observation that the vector field

T\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!}{t}=-\partial_{t}

is a special case of a unit length
closed
vector field. As a consequence, the Riemannian metric (
3
) formed from de Sitter spacetime by “removing the minus sign,”

g\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=(dt)^{2}+f(t)\mathring{g},

is also complete (see
[
O’N83
, Lemma 40, p. 209]
) but more importantly, it has Riemann curvature 4-tensor
Rm
related to that of
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
in a simple and elegant way:

\displaystyle\text{Rm}=\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}-\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat}.

We will prove, and generalize, (
3.1
) in Proposition
4
below. For now we point out that, since de Sitter spacetime has constant curvature,
\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}=\frac{1}{2}g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
(with
\lambda=1
), and since
g=g_{\scalebox{0.4}{dS}}+2(dt)^{2}
,
Rm
can in fact be written directly in terms of
g
, as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Rm}=\frac{1}{2}g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g-2g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})-\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat}.

This makes clear precisely how
g
deviates from the constant curvature case (
3.1
), as we describe in more detail in Section
3.4
below. In Theorem
2
, we will generalize this result by first generalizing (
3.1
), in Proposition
4
. First, let us recall some basic properties of closed vector fields.

3.2.
Closed vector fields

Recall that a vector field
T
on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
is
closed
if its metric 1-form
T^{\flat}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g(T,\cdot)
is closed:
dT^{\flat}=0
. If
M
is simply connected (and noncompact), then such a
T
is necessarily a gradient and
\nabla T^{\flat}
is its Hessian (with
\nabla
the Levi-Civita connection of
g
); generally speaking,
T
is always at least locally a gradient. But the crucial property of such vector fields, for our purposes, is the following:

Lemma 2
.

Let
T
be a unit length vector field on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
. Then
T
is closed if and only if the endomorphism

\mathscr{D}\colon TM\longrightarrow TM\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptV\mapsto\nabla_{\!V}{T}

is self-adjoint with respect to
g
. Equivalently,
T
is closed if and only if the 2-tensor
\nabla T^{\flat}
is symmetric.

Proof.

Suppose that
T
is closed. Then it must have geodesic flow,
\nabla_{\!T}{T}=0
, because

(90)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\,dT^{\flat}(T,V)\,}_{0}=g(\nabla_{\!T}{T},V)\hskip 14.45377pt\text{for all $V\in\mathfrak{X}(M)$}.

Furthermore, its orthogonal complement
T^{\perp}\subseteq TM
will be integrable, because

(91)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\,dT^{\flat}(X_{i},X_{j})\,}_{0}=-g(T,[X_{i},X_{j}])\hskip 14.45377pt\text{for all $X_{i},X_{j}\in\Gamma(T^{\perp})$}.

Then, with respect to any
g
-orthonormal basis of the form
\{T,X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}\}
, observe that

\displaystyle g(\nabla_{\!T}{T},X_{i})\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eqn:prop0}}{=}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!0\,=\,g(\nabla_{\!X_{i}}{T},T),

\displaystyle g(\nabla_{\!X_{i}}{T},X_{j})\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!-g(T,\nabla_{\!X_{i}}{X_{j}})\!\overset{\eqref{eqn:prop1}}{=}\!-g(T,\nabla_{\!X_{j}}{X_{i}})=g(\nabla_{\!X_{j}}{T},X_{i}).

It follows that
g(\nabla_{\!V}{T},W)=g(\nabla_{\!W}{T},V)
for all
V,W\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, so that
\mathscr{D}
is indeed self-adjoint. The converse is also clear from these two equations.
Finally, suppose that
T
is closed and consider the 2-tensor
\nabla T^{\flat}
; then

\nabla T^{\flat}(X_{i},X_{j})=-g(T,\nabla_{\!X_{j}}{X_{i}})\overset{\eqref{eqn:prop1}}{=}-g(T,\nabla_{\!X_{i}}{X_{j}})=\nabla T^{\flat}(X_{j},X_{i}).

Similarly, by (
90
) it will be the case that
\nabla T^{\flat}(T,X_{i})=\nabla T^{\flat}(X_{i},T)=0
. Thus
\nabla T^{\flat}
is symmetric; the converse is also clear from these equations.
∎

Henceforth we will assume that
T
is a unit length closed vector field. As the endomorphism
\mathscr{D}
will be self-adjoint, let
\{T,X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\}
be a local
g
-orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
\mathscr{D}
,

(92)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(T)=\nabla_{\!T}{T}=0\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\mathscr{D}(X_{i})=\nabla_{\!X_{i}}{T}=\lambda_{i}X_{i},

for some
\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. Now consider the Lorentzian metric
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
defined by

g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g-2T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat}.

Observe that
\{T,X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\}
is simultaneously a
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
-orthonormal basis, except that
T
is now unit “timelike”:
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(T,T)=-1
. The Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!}{}
of
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
is related to that of
g
as follows:

Lemma 3
.

Let
\{T,X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\}
be a local
g
-orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
\mathscr{D}
with eigenvalues (
92
). Then the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{L}}
of the Lorentzian metric
g_{\scalebox{.4}{L}}=g-2T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{L}}_{\!T}{T}&=&\hskip-36.135pt-\nabla_{\!T}{T}=0,\\
\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{L}}_{\!X_{i}}{T}&=&\hskip-21.68121pt\nabla_{\!X_{i}}{T}=\lambda_{i}X_{i},\\
\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{L}}_{\!X_{i}}{X_{j}}&=&\!\!2\lambda_{i}\delta_{ij}T+\nabla_{\!X_{i}}{X_{j}}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

These all follow by the Koszul formula, together with (
92
).
∎

Lemmas
2
and
3
combine to yield an elegant relationship between the curvature 4-tensors of
g
and
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
; before stating it, recall the
Kulkarni-Nomizu product
: for any pair of symmetric 2-tensors
P,Q
, the 4-tensor
P\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}Q
defined by

\displaystyle(P\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}Q)(v,w,x,y)\!\!

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=

\displaystyle\!\!P(v,y)Q(w,x)+P(w,x)Q(v,y)

\displaystyle\hskip 50.58878pt-P(v,x)Q(w,y)-P(w,y)Q(v,x)

is an algebraic curvature tensor (i.e., possessing the same symmetries as the Riemann curvature 4-tensor; note, e.g., that
P\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}Q=Q\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}P
is one of these symmetries).

Proposition 4

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian
n

where
\nabla
and

For

1.

(106)

Next, the components

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{1}{2}\Big[X_{k}(g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(Z,X_{l}))+Z(g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{l},X_{k}))-X_{l}(g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{k},Z))

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt-g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(Z,[X_{k},X_{l}])-g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{l},[Z,X_{k}])+g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{k},[X_{l},Z])\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt+\,2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{k}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}.

Every “
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
” term here contains at least one
X\in T^{\perp}
, so that it equals
g(\cdot,\cdot)
. We thus have that

(109)

\displaystyle g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!X_{k}}{\!\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!X_{i}}{X_{j}}},X_{l})\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{1}{2}\Big[X_{k}(g(Z,X_{l}))+Z(g(X_{l},X_{k}))-X_{l}(g(X_{k},Z))

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt-g(Z,[X_{k},X_{l}])-g(X_{l},[Z,X_{k}])+g(X_{k},[X_{l},Z])\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt+\,2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{k}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!g(\nabla_{\!X_{k}}{Z},X_{l})+2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{k}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!g(\nabla_{\!X_{k}}{\!\nabla_{\!X_{i}}{X_{j}}},X_{l})+2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{k}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}.

Similarly with
(b)
,

(110)

\displaystyle g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!X_{i}}{\!\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!X_{k}}{X_{j}}},X_{l})\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!X_{i}}{\underbrace{\nabla_{\!X_{k}}{X_{j}}}_{``Z\,"}},X_{l})+2\lambda_{k}\delta_{kj}\,g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(\underbrace{\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!X_{i}}{T}}_{\lambda_{i}X_{i}},X_{l})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{1}{2}\Big[X_{i}(g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(Z,X_{l}))+Z(g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{l},X_{i}))-X_{l}(g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{i},Z))

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt-g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(Z,[X_{i},X_{l}])-g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{l},[Z,X_{i}])+g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{i},[X_{l},Z])\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt+\,2\lambda_{k}\lambda_{i}\delta_{kj}\delta_{il}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!g(\nabla_{\!X_{i}}{\!\nabla_{\!X_{k}}{X_{j}}},X_{l})+2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{k}\delta_{kj}\delta_{il}.

For
(c)
, observe that because
[X_{k},X_{i}]\in T^{\perp}
, it is of the form
[X_{k},X_{i}]=\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}a_{r}X_{r}
with the

\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\![X_{k},X_{i}]}{X_{j}}=\nabla_{\![X_{k},X_{i}]}{X_{j}}+2a_{j}\lambda_{j}T
. Hence

(111)

\displaystyle g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\![X_{k},X_{i}]}{X_{j}},X_{l})\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!g(\nabla_{\![X_{k},X_{i}]}{X_{j}},X_{l}).

Taken together, (
109
), (
110
), and (
111
) yield

\displaystyle\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{k},X_{i},X_{j},X_{l})\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\text{Rm}(X_{k},X_{i},X_{j},X_{l})+2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{k}(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}-\delta_{kj}\delta_{il})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\text{Rm}(X_{k},X_{i},X_{j},X_{l})+(\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat})(X_{k},X_{i},X_{j},X_{l}).

Given (
107
), (
108
), and (

), and the fact that

∎

Note that the components (
107
) and (
108
) can also be found in
[
Ole14
]
; however, the most important curvature component in our Proposition
4
, namely (
3.
), has not (to the best of our knowledge) appeared explicitly in the literature. Be that as it may, here are two examples illustrating Proposition
4
, both in the noncompact setting, beginning with the canonical one:

Example 1.
For de Sitter spacetime
(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1},-dt^{2}+f(t)\mathring{g})
,

T\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!}{t}=-\partial_{t}

is a unit length closed vector field. It will also be so for the corresponding Riemannian metric

g\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}+2T^{\flat_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}}\otimes T^{\flat_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}}=dt^{2}+f(t)\mathring{g}.

Example 2.
On
\mathbb{R}^{3}=\{(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})\}
, consider a Lorentzian metric
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
whose components
({g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}}_{ij})
with respect to the coordinate basis
\{\partial_{1},\partial_{2},\partial_{3}\}
take the form

({g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}}_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&H(x^{1})&0\\
0&0&H(x^{1})/2\end{pmatrix},

where
H(x^{1})
is a smooth function, to be determined below, such that
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
will have constant positive curvature on an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In dimension 3, this is equivalent to being an Einstein metric with positive Einstein constant, which we can take to be
1
:

\text{Ric}_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}=g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}.

Though we forego the computations here, it is straightforward to show that this will hold if and only if

H(x^{1})=(x^{1}+a)^{2}/2,a\in\mathbb{R}.

For example, if we take
a=2
, then
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
will be a Lorentzian metric in the open subset
\{(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3}):x^{1}>-2\}
. Finally, let
f(x^{1})
be a smooth function and consider its
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}
-gradient:

\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!}{f}=-\frac{f^{\prime}}{2}(x^{1}+2)^{2}\,\partial_{1}+f^{\prime}\,\partial_{2}.

Another computation shows that
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!}{f},\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!}{f})=-1
if and only if

(f^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{2}{(x^{1}+2)^{2}}\cdot

Taking the smooth solution
f(x^{1})=\sqrt{2}\,\text{ln}\big(\frac{x^{1}+2}{2}\big)
, we thus have that the pair
(g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}},\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!}{f})
, when restricted to
\{(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3}):x^{1}>-2\}
, yields a Lorentzian manifold with constant positive curvature and a closed, unit timelike vector field. The corresponding Riemannian metric
g
is then
g\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}+2df\otimes df.

With respect to
g
,
\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}_{\!}{f}
will also be a unit length closed vector field.

3.3.
Obstructions to Riemannian metrics in the compact setting

We are now ready to prove our main Theorem of this section:

Theorem 2
.

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian
n
-manifold
(n\geq 3)
and
T
a unit length closed vector field on
M
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the curvature 4-tensor of
g
takes the form

\displaystyle\emph{\text{Rm}}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda g\raisebox{0.40881pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g-2\lambda g\raisebox{0.40881pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})-\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.40881pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat}

if and only if the Lorentzian metric
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{L}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g-2T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat}
has constant curvature
\lambda
. If
M
is compact, then
\lambda=0
and
T
is parallel
;
hence
g
is flat, and its universal cover splits isometrically as a product
\mathbb{R}\times N
.

Proof.

Suppose a pair
(g,T)
satisfying (
2
) exists on a compact manifold
M
of dimension
\geq 3
, and such that
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g-2T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat}
has constant curvature
\lambda
. We first consider the case
\lambda\leq 0
; it turns out that the obstruction in this case occurs at the level of the Ricci tensor, as follows. Set

g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g-2T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat}.

Then

(147)

\displaystyle\text{Ric}(T,T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\text{Rm}(X_{i},T,T,X_{i})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eqn:Ric1}}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}(X_{i},T,T,X_{i})=\text{Ric}_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}(T,T),

so that if
(M,g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}})
has constant curvature
\lambda\leq 0
— more generally, if
(M,g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}})
is Einstein with nonpositive Einstein constant — then

(148)

\displaystyle\text{Ric}(T,T)=\text{Ric}_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}(T,T)=(n-1)\lambda\underbrace{\,g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}(T,T)\,}_{-1}\geq 0.

Next, setting
i=j
in (
106
) and summing over
i=1,\dots,n-1
yields the following Bochner-type equation,

(149)

\displaystyle T(\text{div}\,T)=-\text{Ric}(T,T)-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}^{2},

where we’ve used the fact that

\text{div}\,T=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}=\text{tr}_{g}\nabla T^{\flat}.

Now, via the Schwarz inequality

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{n-1}(\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n-1})^{2},

(
149
) reduces to

(150)

\displaystyle T(\text{div}\,T)\ \leq\ -\text{Ric}(T,T)-\frac{(\text{div}\,T)^{2}}{n-1},

which permits, in turn, the following well known Riccati analysis: since
T
is complete (
M
being compact),

\text{Ric}(T,T)\geq 0\overset{\eqref{eqn:Bochner3}}{\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt}\text{div}\,T=0\overset{\eqref{eqn:Bochner1}}{\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt}\text{Ric}(T,T)=\lambda_{i}=0.

It follows that that the case
\text{Ric}(T,T)>0
cannot occur, and if
\text{Ric}(T,T)=0
, then
T
must be parallel. The former implies that
\lambda<0
cannot occur; the latter, that if
\lambda=0
, then
\nabla T^{\flat}
and (
2
) both vanish, in which case
(M,g)
is flat. Furthermore, its universal covering splits isometrically as a product
\mathbb{R}\times N
, by the de Rham Decomposition Theorem (see
[
Pet16
, p. 384]
). This settles the case
\lambda\leq 0
. For the case
\lambda>0
, we will employ a different strategy; indeed, since
\text{Ric}(T,T)<0
when
\lambda>0
(via (
148
)), the Riccati analysis we applied to (
150
) is unavailable here. Instead, we substitute
g=g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}+2T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat}
into Proposition
4
(note that
T^{\flat_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}}\otimes T^{\flat_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}}=T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat}
), to obtain

\displaystyle\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\text{Rm}+\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eqn:1}}{=}

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{1}{2}\lambda g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g-2\lambda g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{1}{2}\lambda g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}+2\lambda g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})-2\lambda g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{1}{2}\lambda g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{L}}},

where we’ve used the fact that
(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})=0
. But as mentioned in the Introduction, such a (compact) Lorentzian manifold is impossible when
\lambda>0
, by
[
CM62
]
and
[
Kli96
]
.
∎

3.4.
Concluding remarks

We close with four remarks on Theorem
2
.

i.

In the proof of Theorem
2
, it was only the case
\lambda>0
that was truly nontrivial, relying in an essential way on the nonexistence of compact Lorentzian spherical space forms
[
CM62
,
Kli96
]
. The case
\lambda\leq 0
, on the other hand, relied instead on the well known Bochner technique (
149
).

ii.

Observe that compact Lorentzian manifolds with constant
negative
curvature certainly do exist; examples can be found, e.g., in
[
KR85
]
and
[
Gol85
]
. So do flat ones: any
(\mathbb{S}^{1}\!\times\!N,-dt^{2}\oplus h)
, with
(N,h)
a compact flat Riemannian manifold, yields an example, with
\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{L}}}t
serving the role of
T
above.

iii.

The sectional curvature of (
2
) has the following behavior: The term
g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})
vanishes on the frame
\{T,X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}\}
except on the components

g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})(X_{i},T,T,X_{i})=1,

so that the sectional curvature of any 2-plane containing
T
is

\lambda-2\lambda=-\lambda,

as opposed to
\lambda
, which would have been the case with constant curvature
\text{Rm}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g
. On the other hand, the term
\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat}
vanishes except on the components

(\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat})(X_{i},X_{j},X_{j},X_{i})=2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pti\neq j,

so that 2-planes spanned by
\{X_{i},X_{j}\}
now have sectional curvature

\lambda-2\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}.

It is in this sense that
T
“breaks” the constant curvature of the term
\text{Rm}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g
in (
2
).

iv.

Finally, observe that Theorem
2
is uninteresting in dimension 2. Indeed, although any Riemannian 2-manifold satisfies
\text{Rm}=\frac{1}{2}Kg\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g
with
K
the Gaussian curvature (see, e.g.,
[
Lee18
, p. 250]
), and although
K
is neither constant nor signed in general, nevertheless
K=-\lambda
if (
2
) were to be satisfied, because in dimension 2

\nabla T^{\flat}\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}\nabla T^{\flat}=0\hskip 14.45377pt\text{and}\hskip 14.45377ptg\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}(T^{\flat}\otimes T^{\flat})=\frac{1}{2}g\raisebox{0.39993pt}{\,${\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\bigcirc$\cr$\displaystyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\textstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}{\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\wedge$\cr}}}{}}}$\,}g.

But by the Gauss-Bonnet Theorem, if the Euler characteristic is zero (i.e., the manifold is a 2-torus or a Klein bottle) — recall that this must be the case if the compact 2-manifold supports a nowhere vanishing vector field like
T
— then the only compact Riemannian 2-manifold with constant curvature is the flat one.

Appendix: the geometry of plane waves and Penrose’s limit

For the convenience of the reader, in this Appendix we provide a self-contained introduction to the plane wave metrics
h_{\scalebox{.4}{PW}}
we encountered in (
36
); this material already exists in the literature (e.g.,
[
CBS13
,
LS16
]
), though our presentation differs markedly from these. To begin with, recall that a vector field
N
on a Lorentzian manifold
(M,g)
is
null
(or
lightlike
) if
N\neq 0
and
g(N,N)=0
. Here now is the coordinate-independent definition of a plane wave metric in Lorentzian geometry:

Definition 2

(
[
LS16
]
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a Lorentzian manifold endowed with a parallel null vector field
N
. Let
R
denote its curvature endomorphism and
\nabla
its Levi-Civita connection. Then
(M,g)
is a
pp -wave
if

(211)

\displaystyle R(X,Y)\,\cdot=0\hskip 7.22743pt\text{for all}\hskip 7.22743ptX,Y\in\Gamma(N^{\perp}).

If, in addition to this,
R
also satisfies

(212)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!X}R=0\hskip 7.22743pt\text{for all}\hskip 7.22743ptX\in\Gamma(N^{\perp}),

then
(M,g)
is a
plane wave
.

What is not clear at the moment is why such metrics will necessarily take the form (
36
). Showing this involves a number of steps, which we now detail. First, suppose
(M,g)
is
(n+1)
-dimensional and choose local coordinates
(x^{0},\dots,x^{n})
with respect to which

N=\partial_{0}.

Because
N
is also parallel, we can, by a further change to a “null coordinate chart” (which we’ll continue to denote by
(x^{0},\dots,x^{n})
), arrange it so that
N=\partial_{0}=\text{grad}\,x^{1},

so that
g
takes the form that we saw in (
29
):

(213)

\displaystyle(g_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
1&g_{11}&g_{12}&g_{13}&\cdots&g_{1n}\\
0&g_{21}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}g_{22}}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}g_{23}}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cdots}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}g_{2n}}\\
0&g_{31}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}g_{32}}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}g_{33}}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cdots}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}g_{3n}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vdots}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vdots}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\ddots}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\vdots}\\
0&g_{n1}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}g_{n2}}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}g_{n3}}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cdots}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}g_{nn}}\end{pmatrix}\cdot

Consider now the
red
submatrix
(g_{ij})_{i,j=2,\dots,n}
; it is positive-definite because the coordinate vectors
\partial_{2},\dots,\partial_{n}
, being orthogonal to the null vector field
\partial_{0}
and linearly independent to it, must span a positive-definite subspace (
[
O’N83
, Lemma 28, p. 142]
). In other words, each codimension 2 embedded submanifold

(214)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\left\{\big(b,c,x^{2},\dots,x^{n}\big)\right\},

with induced metric

(215)

\displaystyle g_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{b,c}}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}g_{ij}(b,c,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})dx^{i}\otimes dx^{j},

is a Riemannian manifold. Furthermore, notice the following fact, a basic one from linear algebra:

\text{$\text{det}\,g=-\text{det}\,(\text{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}red block}})$};

i.e., the fact that
g
in (
213
) is a Lorentzian metric in no way depends on the components
g_{11},g_{12},\dots,g_{1n}
, in the following sense: Changing any of these will change the metric, but it cannot change the nondegeneracy or the Lorentzian signature of the metric. It is precisely this fact that Penrose exploited when he took his plane wave limit (recall (
32
) and (
36
)), for these are precisely the terms that vanished in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
— the novelty of Penrose’s limit, therefore, is that, via the homothety (
32
), he managed to do this while
preserving
the geometry. But in fact there is much more geometry here: Because
N=\partial_{0}
is also parallel, the Riemann curvature tensor
\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}
of
g_{\scalebox{0.4}{\emph{b,c}}}
is intimately related to that of the ambient Lorentzian metric
g
:

Proposition 5
.

The null vector field
\partial_{0}
in (
213
) is parallel if and only if all components of the metric (
213
) are independent of
x^{0}
. In such a case, the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{b,c}}
of
(\Lambda_{\scalebox{.5}{b,c}},g_{\scalebox{0.4}{b,c}})
in (
214
) is related to the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
of
g
as follows
:
for any
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\Lambda_{\scalebox{.5}{b,c}})
,

(216)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!X}{Y}=\nabla^{\scalebox{0.4}{b,c}}_{\!X}\,Y+\alpha\partial_{0},

for some smooth function
\alpha
. As a result, their curvature tensors satisfy

(217)

\displaystyle\emph{\text{Rm}}_{\scalebox{.5}{b,c}}=\emph{\text{Rm}}\hskip 14.45377pton\hskip 14.45377pt\mathfrak{X}(\Lambda_{\scalebox{.5}{b,c}}).

Proof.

If the null vector field
\partial_{0}=\text{grad}\,x^{1}
is parallel, then

\nabla_{\!\partial_{0}}{\partial_{i}}=\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{0}}=0\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pti=0,1,2,\dots,n,

which immediately implies that each
\partial_{0}(g_{ij})=0
. Conversely, if each
\partial_{0}(g_{ij})=0
, then each of the Christoffel symbols
\Gamma_{i0}^{k}
vanishes, hence each
\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{0}}=0
. Next, consider each embedded 2-submanifold
\Lambda_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}
given by (
214
), with its corresponding induced Riemannian metric

g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{b,c}}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{i,j=2}^{n}g_{ij}(c,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})dx^{i}\otimes dx^{j}.

(Since each
g_{ij}
is independent of
x^{0}
, the components
g_{ij}
are
x^{0}
-independent.) By the Gauss Equation (
[
Lee18
, Theorem 8.5, p. 230]
), we know that the components
\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{i},\partial_{j},\partial_{k},\partial_{l})
of the curvature tensor of
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{b,c}}}
are related to those of
g
by

(218)

\displaystyle\text{Rm}(\partial_{i},\partial_{j},\partial_{k},\partial_{l})\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\text{Rm}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{i},\partial_{j},\partial_{k},\partial_{l})

\displaystyle\hskip-36.135pt-\,g(\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{i},\partial_{l}),\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{j},\partial_{k}))+g(\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{i},\partial_{k}),\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{j},\partial_{l})),

where
\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}
is the second fundamental form of
g_{\scalebox{.4}{\emph{b,c}}}
. But when
\partial_{0}
is parallel, this equation simplifies considerably, because in such a case any vector field
\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{j}}
, for
i,j=2,\dots,n
, has no
\partial_{1}
-component; i.e.,

(219)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{j}}=\alpha_{ij}\partial_{0}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\beta_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k},

for some smooth functions
\alpha_{ij},\beta_{ij}^{2},\dots,\beta_{ij}^{n}
. Thus, since
\partial_{0}
is orthogonal to
\Lambda_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}
, the normal component of (
219
) is

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{i},\partial_{j})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=(\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{j}})^{\perp}=\alpha_{ij}\partial_{0}.

This verifies (
216

S\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\text{span}\{\partial_{2},\dots,\partial_{n}\}
is positive-definite, its orthogonal complement
S^{\perp}
is necessarily timelike (i.e., of Lorentzian index), and we have the direct sum
T_{p}M=S_{p}\oplus S_{p}^{\perp}
(see, e.g.,
[
O’N83

). Therefore,
\alpha_{ij}\partial_{0}
in (

\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{j}}
.) But as
\partial_{0}
is
null
— and here is where Lorentzian geometry plays the crucial role — the last two terms in (

\underbrace{\,g(\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{i},\partial_{l}),\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{j},\partial_{k}))\,}_{\alpha_{il}\alpha_{jk}g_{00}}=\underbrace{\,g(\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{i},\partial_{k}),\mathrm{II}_{\scalebox{.5}{\emph{b,c}}}(\partial_{j},\partial_{l}))\,}_{\alpha_{ik}\alpha_{jl}g_{00}}=0.

This confirms (
217
) and completes the proof.
∎

When endowed with a parallel null vector field, a Lorentzian manifold

is called a

Proposition 6
.

An

(v,u,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})
about any point of

(220)

\displaystyle(g_{ij})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0&\cdots&0\\

. There is a local isometry converting plane waves to the “Rosen coordinates” of (

Proof.

(

\displaystyle\partial_{0}=\text{grad}\,x^{1}.

1.

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!\partial_{1}}{X_{i}}\Big|_{(0,x^{1},0,\dots,0)}=0\hskip 14.45377pt\text{for each $i=2,\dots,n$}.

\displaystyle\partial_{1}(g({\partial_{0}},{X_{i}}))=g({\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{1}}{\partial_{0}}}_{0}},{X_{i}})+g({\partial_{0}},{\hskip-3.61371pt\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{1}}{X_{i}}}_{\text{$0$ by \eqref{app:pp0}}}\hskip-1.4457pt})=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{2}(g({\partial_{0}},{X_{i}}))=g({\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{2}}{\partial_{0}}}_{0}},{X_{i}})+g({\partial_{0}},{\hskip-3.61371pt\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{2}}{X_{i}}}_{\text{$0$ by \eqref{app:pp1*}}}\hskip-1.4457pt})=0.

We would now like to modify these coordinates to make

(228)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\,g({\partial_{u}},{\partial_{v}})\,}_{\text{nowhere vanishing}}\hskip-12.28577pt=g\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\partial x^{k}}{\partial u}\partial_{k},\partial_{0}\Big)=\frac{\partial x^{1}}{\partial u}\underbrace{\,g({\partial_{1}},{\partial_{0}})\,}_{1},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{j}}=0\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt\Gamma_{ij}^{v}=0\hskip 14.45377pt\Rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt\partial_{i}g_{uj}=-\partial_{j}g_{ui}.

),

\displaystyle\underbrace{\,\partial_{k}(g({\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{u}}},{\partial_{j}}))\,}_{\text{$\frac{1}{2}(\cancel{\partial_{k}\partial_{i}g_{uj}}-\partial_{k}\partial_{j}g_{ui})$}}\!\!

\displaystyle\!\!g({\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\!\nabla_{\!\partial_{k}}{\partial_{u}}}},{\partial_{j}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!g({\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\!\nabla_{\!\partial_{k}}{\partial_{u}}}},{\partial_{j}})+g({\nabla_{\!\partial_{k}}{\partial_{u}}},{\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{j}}}_{0}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\partial_{i}(g({\nabla_{\!\partial_{k}}{\partial_{u}}},{\partial_{j}}))

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{1}{2}(\cancel{\partial_{i}\partial_{k}g_{uj}}-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}g_{uk}),

\partial_{k}\partial_{j}g_{ui}=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}g_{uk}.

On the other hand, by (

so that each

8.

We now go one step further and show that in fact each

is a function of

alone. Indeed, recalling (

(0,t,0,\dots,0)
on the

(230)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\,\nabla_{\!\partial_{u}}{\partial_{i}}\Big|_{(0,t,0,\dots,0)}\,}_{\text{$\partial_{u},\partial_{i}$ are with respect to $(v,u,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})$}}\hskip-43.36243pt=\hskip-28.90755pt\overbrace{\,\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\partial x^{k}}{\partial u}\nabla_{\!\partial_{k}}{X_{i}}\bigg|_{(0,t,0,\dots,0)}\,}^{\text{$\partial_{k}$ here is with respect to $(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2},\dots,x^{n})$}}\hskip-21.68121pt\overset{\eqref{app:pp0},\eqref{app:frame2}}{=}0.

Now, because

satisfies
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x^{1}}\neq 0

x^{1}
-axis will be a smooth curve in the coordinates
(v,u,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})

t\mapsto(v(t),u(t),x^{2}(t),\dots,x^{n}(t))
. Monotonicity in

u=\text{const}.
hypersurface exactly once, and so any point

u(t_{*})
for some unique parameter value

. Since each
\partial_{i}g_{uj}
is a function of

alone, its value at
p
depends only on the

hypersurface containing
p
; as a result, if
p
has
u
-coordinate

, then

\hskip 21.68121pt\partial_{i}g_{uj}\Big|_{\text{$p\,=\,(v(p),u(p),x^{2}(p),\dots,x^{n}(p))$}}=\partial_{i}g_{uj}\Big|_{\text{$p_{*}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.72334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.72334pt}{$\cdot$}}=(v(t_{*}),u(t_{*}),x^{2}(t_{*}),\dots,x^{n}(t_{*}))$}}.

As a consequence,

\partial_{i}g_{uj}\Big|_{p}=\partial_{i}g_{uj}\Big|_{\text{$p_{*}$}}=g({\!\!\!\!\!\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{u}}}_{\text{$\nabla_{\!\partial_{u}}{\partial_{i}}\big|_{\text{$p_{*}$}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\overset{\eqref{app:final}}{=}\!0$}}\!\!\!\!},{\partial_{j}})+g({\partial_{u}\,},{\!\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{j}}}_{0}})\bigg|_{\text{$p_{*}$}}=0.

Thus in fact each
h_{i}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g_{ui}
is a function of

alone, and the metric components take the simpler form

(g_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
1&g_{uu}&h_{2}(u)&h_{3}(u)&\cdots&h_{n}(u)\\

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

To bring this metric into the final form of (

denote an antiderivative of

&\vdots&\\

(\Leftarrow)
Conversely, suppose that at every point on an
(n+1)

(v,u,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})

220
); observe that
\partial_{v}
is a parallel null vector field. For such a metric, the nonvanishing Christoffel symbols are

(231)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}{\partial_{u}}=\nabla_{\!\partial_{u}}{\partial_{i}}=\frac{H_{i}}{2}\partial_{v}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377pt\nabla_{\!\partial_{u}}{\partial_{u}}=\frac{H_{u}}{2}\partial_{v}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=2}^{n}H_{i}\partial_{i},

for

. It follows that

for all

, as does

for all

and

. (This is usually taken to be the definition of pp-wave in the literature (e.g.,

), although, as we’ve just shown, the weaker condition (

Next, suppose that a pp-wave also satisfies

for all

. Then, in Brinkmann coordinates (

\displaystyle\underbrace{\,(\nabla_{\!\partial_{k}}\text{Rm})(\partial_{u},\partial_{i},\partial_{j},\partial_{u})\,}_{0}\!\!

\displaystyle\!\!\partial_{k}(\text{Rm}(\partial_{u},\partial_{i},\partial_{j},\partial_{u}))-\text{Rm}(\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{k}}{\partial_{u}}}_{\text{$\frac{H_{k}}{2}\partial_{v}$}},\partial_{i},\partial_{j},\partial_{u})

\displaystyle\hskip-3.61371pt-\,\text{Rm}(\partial_{u},\!\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{k}}{\partial_{i}}}_{0},\partial_{j},\partial_{u})-\text{Rm}(\partial_{u},\partial_{i},\!\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{k}}{\partial_{j}}}_{0},\partial_{u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\partial_{k}\big(g(\nabla_{\!\partial_{u}}\!\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}\partial_{j}}_{0}-\nabla_{\!\partial_{i}}\!\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\partial_{u}}\partial_{j}}_{\text{$\frac{H_{j}}{2}\partial_{v}$}},\partial_{u})\big)

\displaystyle-\frac{H_{kij}}{2}g(\partial_{v},\partial_{u})=-\frac{H_{kij}}{2},

Finally, the isometry converting a plane wave in “Rosen coordinates” (

and parallel transported along

\displaystyle g_{ij}f_{k}^{i}f_{l}^{j}=\delta_{kl}\hskip 14.45377pt,\hskip 14.45377ptg_{ij}\dot{f}_{k}^{i}f_{l}^{j}=g_{ij}f_{k}^{i}\dot{f}_{l}^{j},\hskip 14.45377ptk,l=2,\dots,n,

Finally, we mention that there is an even more geometric way to define pp-waves than Definition
2
, via the following vector bundle that sits above any Brinkmann spacetime (see
[
CBS13
]
and
[
LS16
]
):

Proposition 7
.

Let
(M,g)
be a Lorentzian manifold and
N
a parallel null vector field, with orthogonal complement
N^{\perp}\subseteq TM
. Then the vector bundle
N^{\perp}/N
admits a positive-definite inner product
\bar{g}
,

\bar{g}([X],[Y])\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=g(X,Y)\hskip 14.45377pt\text{for all}\hskip 14.45377pt[X],[Y]\in\Gamma(N^{\perp}/N),

and a corresponding linear connection
\overline{\nabla}\colon\mathfrak{X}(M)\times\Gamma(N^{\perp}/N)\longrightarrow\Gamma(N^{\perp}/N)
,

\overline{\nabla}_{\!V}{[Y]}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=[\nabla_{\!W}{Y}]\hskip 14.45377pt\text{for all}\hskip 14.45377ptV\in\mathfrak{X}(M)\hskip 14.45377pt\text{and}\hskip 14.45377pt[Y]\in\Gamma(N^{\perp}/N).

This connection is flat if and only if
(M,g)
is a pp-wave.

Proof.

The metric
\bar{g}
will be well defined, and positive definite, whenever
V
is null. To check the latter, note that every
X\in\Gamma(N^{\perp})
not proportional to
V
must satisfy
g(X,X)>0
, so that
\bar{g}
is nondegenerate, in fact positive-definite. To verify the former, note that if
[X]=[X^{\prime}]
and
[Y]=[Y^{\prime}]
, so that
X^{\prime}=X+fN
and
Y^{\prime}=Y+kN
for some smooth functions
f,k
, then

\bar{g}([X^{\prime}],[Y^{\prime}])=g(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=g(X,Y)=\bar{g}([X],[Y]).

On the other hand, the connection
\overline{\nabla}
is not well defined for an arbitrary null vector field
N
, but it will be so if
N
is parallel, the reason being that
\nabla_{\!V}{Y}\in\Gamma(N^{\perp})
if
N
is parallel, in which case

\overline{\nabla}_{\!V}{[Y^{\prime}]}=[\nabla_{\!V}{Y^{\prime}}]=[\nabla_{\!V}{Y}]+\cancelto{0}{[V(k)N]}+\cancelto{0}{[k\nabla_{\!V}{N}]}\,=\overline{\nabla}_{\!V}{[Y]}.

That
\overline{\nabla}
is indeed a linear connection follows easily. Now, if this connection is flat, then by definition its corresponding curvature endomorphism

\displaystyle\bar{R}\colon\mathfrak{X}(M)\times\mathfrak{X}(M)\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\Gamma(N^{\perp}/N)\longrightarrow\Gamma(N^{\perp}/N),~\text{given by}

\displaystyle\bar{R}(V,W)[X]\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\overline{\nabla}_{\!V}[\overline{\nabla}_{\!W}[X]]-\overline{\nabla}_{\!W}[\overline{\nabla}_{\!V}[X]]-\overline{\nabla}_{\![V,W]}{[X]},

vanishes, for any section
[X]\in\Gamma(N^{\perp}/N)
and vector fields
V,W\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. Using
\bar{g}
, this flatness condition is equivalent to

\bar{g}(\bar{R}(V,W)[X],[Y])=0\hskip 7.22743pt\text{for all}\hskip 7.22743ptV,W\in\mathfrak{X}(M)\hskip 7.22743pt,\hskip 7.22743pt[X],[Y]\in\Gamma(N^{\perp}/N).

But if we unpack the definitions of
\overline{\nabla}
and
\bar{g}
, we see that

\bar{g}(\bar{R}(V,W)[X],[Y])=\text{Rm}(V,W,X,Y)=\text{Rm}(X,Y,V,W).

It follows that
\bar{R}=0
if and only if
R(X,Y)V=0
for all
X,Y\in\Gamma(N^{\perp})
and
V\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, which, by Definition
2
— and our discussion below (
231
) — is precisely the condition to be a pp-wave.
∎
