Bounds on piercing and line-piercing numbers in families of convex sets in the plane
Shira Zerbib

Abstract
A family of sets has the ( p , q ) (p,q) property if among any p p members of it some q q intersect. It is shown that if a finite family of compact convex sets in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} has the ( p + 1 , 2 ) (p+1,2) property then it is pierced by ⌊ p 2 ⌋ + 1 \lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor+1 lines. A colorful version of this result is proved as well. As a corollary, the following is proved: Let ℱ \mathcal{F} be a finite family of compact convex sets in the plane with no isolated sets, and let ℱ ′ \mathcal{F}^{\prime} be the family of its pairwise intersections. If ℱ \mathcal{F} has the ( p + 1 , 2 ) (p+1,2) property and ℱ ′ \mathcal{F}^{\prime} has the ( r + 1 , 2 ) (r+1,2) property, then ℱ \mathcal{F} is pierced by ( ⌊ r 2 ⌋ 2 + ⌊ r 2 ⌋ ) ​ p (\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor^{2}+\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor)p points when r ⩾ 2 r\geqslant 2 , and by p p points otherwise. The proofs use the topological KKM theorem.



1
Introduction

For integers
p\geqslant q>0
, a family of sets
\mathcal{F}
has the
(p,q)
property
if
among any
p
members of
\mathcal{F}
some
q
have a nonempty intersection.
The
matching number
of
\mathcal{F}
is the largest size a subfamily of pairwise disjoint sets in
\mathcal{F}
. Note that
\mathcal{F}
has the
(p+1,2)
property if and only if its matching number is at most
p
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
pierced by
k
points
if there exists a set
C\subseteq\bigcup\mathcal{F}
, such that
|C|=k
and every
F\in\mathcal{F}
contains a point from
C
.
The
piercing number
of
\mathcal{F}
, denoted by
\tau(\mathcal{F})
, is the smallest
k
such that
\mathcal{F}
is pierced by
k
points.

The classical theorem of Helly
[
12
]
states that any family of compact
convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the
(d+1,d+1)
-property is pierced by one point.
Hadwiger and Debrunner
[
11
]
considered the more general problem of studying
the piercing numbers of families of compact, convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that
satisfy the
(p,q)
-property. They proved that whenever
p\geqslant q\geqslant d+1
and
p(d-1)<d(q-1)
, any family of compact, convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is pierced by
p-q+1
points. They also conjectured that for every
p\geqslant q\geqslant d+1
there exists a constant
c=c(p,q;d)
such that any family of compact, convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the
(p,q)
property is pierced by
c
points. This conjecture was proved by Alon and Kleitman in 1992
[
1
]
.

It is known that such a constant
c
does not exist when
q\leqslant d
. For example, when
d=q=2
, a family of pairwise intersecting line segments in general position
in the plane has piercing number at least
|\mathcal{F}|/2
, which is not bounded by a constant. However, such a constant may exist if the family is further restricted. For example, Danzer
[
4
]
showed that a family of disks in the plane with the
(2,2)
property has piercing number at most 4;
another example is
a well-known conjecture of Wegner from 1965
[
22
]
, asserting that a family of axis-parallel rectangles in the plane with the
(p+1,2)
property is pierced by
2p
points (where the best current known upper bound, proven by Correa, Feuilloley, Pérez-Lantero, and Soto
[
3
]
is
\tau\leqslant O(p)(\log\log p)^{2}
).

The question of finding or improving bounds on the piercing numbers in general families of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the
(p,q)
property for
q\geqslant d+1
, or in restricted families when
q
may be smaller than
d+1
, has received great attention over the years, see e.g.,
[
7
,
15
,
18
]
for general families, and
[
2
,
10
,
14
,
17
]
for restricted families.

In this note we give a new bound for the piercing numbers in general families
\mathcal{F}
of compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the
(p+1,2)
property (namely, when
q=d=2
), where the bound is given in terms of the matching number of the family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\{S\cap S^{\prime}\mid S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\}
of pairwise intersections in
\mathcal{F}
.

An
isolated set
is a set in
\mathcal{F}
that does not intersect any other set in
\mathcal{F}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p,r
be positive integers. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite family of compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with no isolated sets, and let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
be the family of its pairwise intersections. If
\mathcal{F}
has the
(p+1,2)
property and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has the
(r+1,2)
property then
\tau(\mathcal{F})\leqslant(\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor^{2}+\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor)p
when
r\geqslant 2
, and
\tau(\mathcal{F})\leqslant p
otherwise.

Note that the condition that
\mathcal{F}
has no isolated sets does not pose a significant restriction on the families
\mathcal{F}
we may consider in the theorem, as any isolated set would add exactly 1 to both the matching number and the piercing number of
\mathcal{F}
.

When
p=r=1
, Theorem
1.1
implies Helly’s theorem in the plane, as the condition that both
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
have the
(2,2)
property is equivalent to the condition that
\mathcal{F}
has the
(3,3)
property, by Radon’s theorem
[
20
]
.

Moreover, if
p=1
and
r\geqslant 2
, then the conditions
\mathcal{F}
has the
(p+1,2)
property and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has the
(r+1,2)
property imply that
\mathcal{F}
has the
(t,3)
property for some
t
satisfying
r+1\leqslant{t\choose 2}
. Thus, by the Alon-Kleitman theorem, there exists some constant
c=c(t,3;2)
such that
\tau(\mathcal{F})\leqslant c
. The current best known bound on this constant, proved in
[
15
]
, is
c(t,3;2)\leqslant O(t^{4})
.
Theorem
1.1
gives a specific bound rather than an order estimation, under the stronger condition that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has the
(r+1,2)
property.

Similarly, for any
p,r\geqslant 2
, the conditions
\mathcal{F}
has the
(p+1,2)
property and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has the
(r+1,2)
property imply that
\mathcal{F}
has the
(t,4)
property, for some
t\leqslant 2r+p+1
. Indeed, let
\mathcal{T}
be a subfamily of
\mathcal{F}
of size
2r+p+1
. At each step pick two sets that intersect and remove them, until less than
p+1
sets remain. We get
r+1
intersecting pairs, and by the
(r+1,2)
property of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
some two of them intersect.
The bound proven in
[
15
]
then implies
\tau(\mathcal{F})\leqslant O((2r+p)^{3})
. Our bound, under the stronger conditions of Theorem
1.1
, provides a specific bound rather than an order estimation, and an improvement in order when
p>>r
or
r>>p
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
follows from a bound on the line-piercing numbers in families of convex sets in the plane. We say that a family
\mathcal{F}
of sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
pierced by
k
lines
, if there exist
k
lines in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose union intersects every set in
\mathcal{F}
. The
line-piercing number
of
\mathcal{F}
is the smallest
k
such that
\mathcal{F}
is pierced by
k
lines. We say that
\mathcal{F}
has
property
T(r)
if every
r
or fewer sets in
\mathcal{F}
are pierced by a line.

The problem of bounding the line-piercing numbers of families of convex sets in the plane with the
T(r)
property has been studied since the 1940’s. In 1969 Eckhoff
[
5
]
proved that if a family of compact convex sets has the
T(k)
property, for
k\geqslant 4
, then it is pierced by two lines.
In 1993
Eckhoff
[
6
]
proved that a family of compact convex sets with the
T(3)
property is pierced by 4 lines, and conjectured that this bound can be improved to 3. This conjecture was proved in
[
19
]
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a finite family of compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the
(p+1,2)
property, then
\mathcal{F}
is pierced by
p
lines; this follows from projecting
\mathcal{F}
onto a line and using a theorem of Gallai (Theorem
1.6
below), asserting that the matching and piercing numbers in families of intervals in
\mathbb{R}
are equal.
Here we obtain a tight bound on the line-piercing numbers of such families:

Theorem 1.2
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a finite family of compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the
(p+1,2)
property then it is pierced by
\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor+1
lines.

The bound in Theorem
1.2
is tight by the following example. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of edges of a regular
(2p+1)
-gon. Then
\mathcal{F}
has the
(p+1)
-property, and since every line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
intersects at most 4 elements of
\mathcal{F}
, at least
\lceil\frac{2p+1}{4}\rceil=\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor+1
lines are needed to pierce
\mathcal{F}
.

We shall also prove the following colorful version of Theorem
1.2
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{F}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{F}_{2k}
are finite families of compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, such that for any choice of sets
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i},~i\in[2k]
, some two sets among
F_{1},\dots,F_{2k}
intersect. Then there exists
i\in[2k]
such that
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is pierced by
k
lines.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
uses the topological KKM theorem, due to Knaster, Kuratowski and Mazurkiewicz
[
16
]
. Let

\Delta_{k}=\Big\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})\mid\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}x_{i}=0,x_{i}\geqslant 0~\text{ for all
}i\in[k+1]\Big\}

be the standard
k
-dimensional simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
. For a face
\sigma
of
\Delta_{k}
, we write
i\in\sigma
if
\sigma
contains the
i
-th vertex of
\Delta_{k}
, namely the point
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})
with
x_{i}=1
and
x_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
. Note that if
x_{i}>0
for some point
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})\in\sigma
then
i\in\sigma
.

Theorem 1.4

(The KKM theorem)
.

If open subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k+1}
of the
k
-dimensional simplex
\Delta_{k}

satisfy
\sigma\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\sigma}A_{i}
for
every face
\sigma
of
\Delta_{k}
, then
\bigcap_{i=1}^{k+1}A_{i}\neq\emptyset
.

A family
(A_{1},\dots,A_{k+1})
of subsets of
\Delta_{k}
satisfying the conditions of Theorem
1.4
is called a
KKM cover
of
\Delta_{k}
. Let
S_{n}
be the group of permutations of the elements in
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
The proof of Theorem
1.3
will use a colorful generalization of the KKM theorem, due to Gale
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.5

(The colorful KKM theorem)
.

If
(A^{i}_{1},\dots,A^{i}_{k+1}),i\in[k+1]
, are
k+1
KKM covers of
\Delta_{k}
,
then there exists a permutation
\pi\in S_{k+1}
such that
\bigcap_{i=1}^{k+1}A^{\pi(i)}_{i}\neq\emptyset
.

The application of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
in the proof of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
is similar to their application in
[
18
,
19
]
: in all cases one uses a simplex of appropriate dimension to model the configuration space of some special sets of lines in the plane, and then defines a KKM cover corresponding to certain regions, obtained from the partition of the plane by the lines. Those ideas appeared first in
[
18
,
19
]
. The new ingredient in the proof of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
is the new way the regions are defined: here we define the regions inductively, and they are pairwise disjoint, which allows us to consider any number of distributed lines, and therefore to prove the theorem for all
p
. This shows the robustness of this KKM-based method, which was first introduced in
[
18
,
19
]
: changing the way the regions are defined allows for more applications of the method.

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
we will also use the following theorem of Gallai
[
9
]
, which is a special case of the theorem of Hadwiger and Debrunner mentioned above.

Theorem 1.6
.

If
\mathcal{F}
a finite family of compact intervals in
\mathbb{R}
with the
(p+1,2)
property, then
\tau(\mathcal{F})\leqslant p
.

Finally, we will use a theorem bounding the piercing numbers in families of
d
-intervals.
A
(separated)
d
-interval
is a union of at most
d
compact (possibly empty) intervals, one on each of
d
copies of
\mathbb{R}
. The following was proved by Tardos
[
21
]
for
d=2
and by Kaiser
[
13
]
for all
d\geqslant 2
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
. If
\mathcal{F}
a finite family of compact
d
-intervals with the
(p+1,2)
property, then
\tau(\mathcal{F})\leqslant(d^{2}-d)p
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

We apply Theorem
1.2
with the family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
of pairwise intersections. Let
k=\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor
. By Theorem
1.2
, there exist
k+1
lines
\ell_{0},\dots,\ell_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
piercing
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. Write
L=\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}\ell_{i}
.

For
S\in\mathcal{F}
, let
\bar{S}=S\cap L
. Then
\bar{\mathcal{F}}=\{\bar{S}\mid S\in\mathcal{F}\}
is a family of
(k+1)
-intervals. We claim that
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
has the
(p+1,2)
property. Indeed, suppose for contradiction that there exist pairwise disjoint sets
\bar{S}_{1},\dots,\bar{S}_{p+1}\in\bar{\mathcal{F}}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}
has the
(p+1,2)
property, there exist integers
1\leqslant q<q^{\prime}\leqslant p+1
so that
S_{q}\cap S_{q^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
. But then
S_{q}\cap S_{q^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, which means that there exists some line
\ell_{i}
, so that
S_{j}\cap S_{k}\cap\ell_{i}\neq\emptyset
. However, this contradicts
\bar{S_{q}}\cap\bar{S_{q^{\prime}}}=\emptyset
.

If
k=0
then
r=1
and
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
is a family of intervals. Therefore, by Theorem
1.6
, we have
\tau(\bar{\mathcal{F}})\leqslant p
. If
k\geqslant 1
, then by Theorem
1.7
we have
\tau(\bar{\mathcal{F}})\leqslant((k+1)^{2}-(k+1))p=(\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor^{2}+\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor)p
.

Finally, we claim that
\tau(\mathcal{F})\leqslant\tau(\bar{\mathcal{F}})
, which will conclude the proof of the theorem. Indeed, let
S\in\mathcal{F}
. Since
S
is not isolated, there exists some
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
with
S\cap S^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, and moreover, there exists some
i\in\{0,\dots,k\}
with
S\cap S^{\prime}\cap\ell_{i}\neq\emptyset
. This implies that
\bar{S}\neq\emptyset
for all
S\in\mathcal{F}
, entailing the claim. ∎
