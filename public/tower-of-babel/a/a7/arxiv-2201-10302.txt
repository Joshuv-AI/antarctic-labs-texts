An inverse Fraïssé limit for finite posets and duality for posets and lattices
Szymon Głąb, Michał Pawlikowski

Abstract
We consider a category of all finite partial orderings with quotient maps as arrows and construct a Fraïssé sequence in this category. Then we use commonly known relations between partial orders and lattices to construct a sequence of lattices associated with it. Each of these two sequences has a limit object – an inverse limit, which is an object of our interest as well. In the first chapter there are some preliminaries considering partial orders, lattices, topology, inverse limits, category theory and Fraïssé theory, which are used later. In the second chapter there are our results considering a Fraïssé sequence in category of finite posets with quotient maps and properties of inverse limit of this sequence. In the third chapter we investigate connections between posets and order ideals corresponding to them, getting an inductive sequence made of these ideals; then we study properties of the inverse limit of this sequence.



Key words and phrases.
partially ordered set, Fraïssé sequence, inverse Fraïssé limit, inverse limit, order ideals, distributive lattice

Introduction

Fraïssé
theory has its beginning in the Georg Cantor’s work, where Cantor has shown that any countable, dense linear ordering without endpoints is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Q},\leq)
. In the 1950s Roland
Fraïssé
expanded on the idea of Cantor and formulated it in the model theory framework
[
5
]
, which marked the beginning of a theory named after him. Some examples of
Fraïssé
limits are well-known objects, such as

(\mathbb{Q},\leq)
– a limit of all linear orderings; the random graph – a limit of all finite graphs; and some are completely new, like for example a limit of all finite partially ordered sets, called the random poset, as well as many more countable, ultrahomogeneous structures. In late 1970s James Schmerl in
[
10
]
characterized all possible countable ultrahomogeneous posets. Up to isomorphism, there are only countably many ultrahomogeneous countable posets, each of one of four types. Since ultrahomogeneous countable structures are
Fraïssé
limits of a finite structures family of the same type (this family is called age of the structure), Schmerl characterized all possible
Fraïssé
limits for finite posets.

Due to Birkhoff Theorem (Theorem
2.10
) there is duality between finite posets and finite distributive lattices. Therefore one could expect the similar characterization to that of Schmerl for lattices. However Abogatma and Truss in
[
1
]
proved that there are continuum many pairwise non-isomorphic ultrahomogeneous lattices. On the other hand if one demand families of finite lattices to be varieties, then by result
[
4
]
of Day and Ježek there are only three possible non-isomorphic
Fraïssé
limits.

In XXI century new variants of
Fraïssé
theory were introduced by Sławomir Solecki, Trevor Irwin
[
7
]
and Wiesław Kubiś
[
9
,
8
]
, and then further developed by other mathematicians. Limit objects in these theories can be uncountable and have found remarkable applications in topology and functional analysis.

In this article we work in generalised
Fraïssé
theory formulated by Wiesław Kubiś
[
9
]
which uses category-theoretic approach. In this approach classical
Fraïssé
limit for posets is a direct limit of finite posets with embeddings. Here we are studying finite posets with
quotients
. Then the limit object is an uncountable poset with a natural topological structure. It turns out that this is the Cantor space with the extremely simple order structure, namely there are two kind of points: isolated or comparable with only one other point. The motivation for our investigation comes from
[
6
]
, where the authors studied inverse
Fraïssé
limit for graphs.

As we have mentioned finite lattices are dual to finite posets. Therefore we are also interested in finding dual limit to that of posets.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we present basic definitions and facts on posets, lattices and inverse limits, and gives an introduction to the categorical
Fraïssé
theory. In Section
3
we build the model of the inverse
Fraïssé
limit for finite posets
\mathbb{P}
, and its properties. In Section
4
using duality between posets and lattices, having
\langle\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{p^{n}_{k}\}_{k<n}\rangle
an inductive sequence consisting of finite posets with quotient maps and its limit
P=\varprojlim\langle\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{p^{n}_{k}\}_{k<n}\rangle
, we investigate an inverse limit
\mathbb{O}(P)
of order ideals
\mathcal{O}(P_{n})
. We show that
\mathbb{O}(P)
is a
\sigma
-complete atomic lattice, and in Theorem
4.16
we show the connection between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{P})
. In Section
5
we define category of finite lattice in which
\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{P})
is an inverse
Fraïssé
limit, and present a different way of looking on order ideals
\mathcal{O}(P_{n})
and induced quotients
\hat{p}^{n}_{k}
.

Definitions and facts concerning posets and lattices are taken from Davey and Priestley monograph
[
3
]
. Awodey’s book
[
2
]
and Kubiś’s notes
[
8
]
were used to gather the information on category theory and
Fraïssé
theory. Some topological facts are taken from Srivastava’s monograph
[
11
]
.

x\wedge(y\vee z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)

Lemma 2.4
.

2.2
Topological definitions and facts concerning the inverse limits

In this section we present the definition of the inverse limit and the notion of profinite poset.
Let
X
be a topological space. We say that
X
is
0
-dimensional
, if it has a base of clopens.

Let
P,Q
be posets with topology
\tau,\sigma
respectively. We say that
f:P\rightarrow Q
is a
topological isomorphism
if it is a homeomorphism and an order-isomorphism.

Let
P_{n}
be non-empty, finite sets with the discrete topology. Let
E_{n}
be relations on
P_{n}
. We equip
P_{1}\times P_{2}\times\dots
with the product topology.
Sets of the form

\{x_{1}\}\times\{x_{2}\}\times\dots\times\{x_{n}\}\times P_{n+1}\times P_{n+2}\times\dots

form a base for topology on
P_{1}\times P_{2}\times\dots
. Moreover,
P_{1}\times P_{2}\times\dots
is
0
-dimensional, compact and metrizable.

Now assume we have a family of onto homomorphisms
\{p_{k}^{m}:m,k\in\mathbb{N},k<m\}
where
p_{k}^{m}:P_{m}\rightarrow P_{k},m\in\mathbb{N},k<m
, such that
p_{\ell}^{k}\circ p_{k}^{n}=p_{\ell}^{n}
for
\ell<k<n
. An inverse limit of
P_{n}
with
p_{k}^{m}
is a set

\varprojlim\left<\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{p^{m}_{k}\}_{k<m}\right>:=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\in\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P_{i}:p_{n}^{n+1}(x_{n+1})=x_{n}\text{ for each }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

We define relation
E
on inverse limit as follows:

(x_{n})E(y_{n})\iff x_{n}E_{n}y_{n}\text{ for each }n\in\mathbb{N}

If relations
E_{n}
are partial orders, then
E
is also a partial ordering. Then the inverse limit with this ordering (or structure topologically isomorphic to it) is called a
profinite poset
.

Lemma 2.11
.

P:=\varprojlim\left<\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{p^{m}_{k}\}_{k<m}\right>
is closed.

Proof.

We will show that
(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P_{i})\setminus P
is open. Let
(x_{n})\in(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P_{i})\setminus P
and
n
be the smallest index such that
p_{n}^{n+1}(x_{n+1})\neq x_{n}
. Then see that
(x_{n})\in\{x_{1}\}\times\{x_{2}\}\times\dots\times\{x_{n+1}\}\times P_{n+2}\times\dots\subset(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P_{i})\setminus P
, so
(\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P_{i})\setminus P
is open, thus
P
is closed.
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

Relation
E
is a closed set in
P\times P
.

Proof.

We will show that
P^{2}\setminus E
is an open set. Take
((x_{n}),(y_{n}))\in P^{2}\setminus E
. Let
n
be the smallest index such that
\neg(x_{n}E_{n}y_{n}
).

Then
((x_{n}),(y_{n}))\in(P\cap\{x_{1}\}\times\{x_{2}\}\times\dots\times\{x_{n}\}\times P_{n+1}\times\dots)\times(P\cap\{y_{1}\}\times\{y_{2}\}\times\dots\times\{y_{n}\}\times P_{n+1}\times\dots)\subset P^{2}\setminus E
and
(P\cap\{x_{1}\}\times\{x_{2}\}\times\dots\times\{x_{n}\}\times P_{n+1}\times\dots)\times(P\cap\{y_{1}\}\times\{y_{2}\}\times\dots\times\{y_{n}\}\times P_{n+1}\times\dots)
is open in

, thus

is open, which makes

closed.
∎

Lemma
2.11
proves that our inverse limit is closed and it is a sub-space of compact space, thus it is compact. See that sets
P\cap(\{x_{1}\}\times\dots\times\{x_{n}\}\times P_{n+1}\times\dots)
form a base for the inverse limit, thus it is
0
-dimensional and it is also metrizable.

A

is a structure of the form
\mathfrak{K}=\langle V,A,\dom,\cod,\circ\rangle
where
V
is a class of objects,
A
is a class of arrows,
\dom:A\rightarrow V
and
\cod:A\rightarrow V
are the domain and codomain functions, and
\circ
is a partial binary operation on arrows, such that the following conditions are satisfied:

1.

f\circ g
is defined whenever
\cod(g)=\dom(f)
and
\circ
is associative, that is,
(f\circ g)\circ h=f\circ(g\circ h)
whenever
\cod(h)=\dom(g)
and
\cod(g)=\dom(f)

2.

For each object
a\in V
there is an arrow
\id_{a}
with
\dom(\id_{a})=\cod(\id_{a})=a
and such that
\id_{a}\circ g=g,f\circ\id_{a}=f
holds for every
f,g\in A
with
\cod(g)=\dom(f)=a
.

3.

For every objects
a,b\in V
the class

\mathfrak{K}(a,b):=\{f\in A:\dom(f)=a\wedge\cod(f)=b\}

is a set.

Let
\mathfrak{K}
be a category. We say that
a
is an
initial object
, if for every object

in
\mathfrak{K}
there is a unique arrow
f:a\rightarrow b
.
a
is a
terminal object
if for every object
b
in
\mathfrak{K}
there is a unique arrow

.

Let
\mathfrak{K}
be a category. We say that
\mathfrak{K}
is

3.3

Now we shall proof that in fact
\mathbb{P}
is universal, in other words every profinite poset is an image of some continuous quotient mapping from
\mathbb{P}
. To achieve this we need to proof the following lemma.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
(G_{i})_{i\in\omega}
be a sequence of non-empty, finite sets and
q_{k}^{i}:G_{i}\rightarrow G_{k}
be functions such that
q^{k}_{\ell}\circ q^{i}_{k}=q^{i}_{\ell}
for every
\ell<k<i
. We define

T_{i}:=\{(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{i})\in G_{0}\times G_{1}\times\ldots\times G_{i}:q_{k}^{i}(a_{i})=a_{k}\text{ for every }k<i\}

for
i\in\omega
. If
T_{i}
is non-empty for every
i\in\omega
then there exists a sequence
(x_{n})\in\prod_{i\in\omega}G_{i}
such that
q_{n}^{n+1}(x_{n+1})=x_{n}
for all
n\in\omega
.

Proof.

Let us define
p_{k}^{i}(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{i})=(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
for
k\leq i
. Note that the condition
q^{i}_{k}(a_{i})=a_{k}
for every
k<i
implies
q^{k}_{\ell}(a_{k})=a_{\ell}
for every
\ell<k<i
.
Therefore
p_{k}^{i}\left[T_{i}\right]\subseteq T_{k}
for all
i\in\omega
and
k<i
.
Thus
p_{0}^{i+1}\left[T_{i+1}\right]=p_{0}^{i}\left[p_{i}^{i+1}\left[T_{i+1}\right]\right]\subseteq p_{0}^{i}\left[T_{i}\right]
for every
i
. Furthermore as
T_{i}
are finite non-empty sets then
p_{0}^{i}\left[T_{i}\right]
are finite and not-empty. Because of that
\left(p_{0}^{i}\left[T_{i}\right]\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a decreasing sequence of non-empty finite sets. Eventually this sequence must stabilize so
\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}p_{0}^{i}\left[T_{i}\right]=p_{0}^{\ell}\left[T_{\ell}\right]\neq\emptyset
for some
\ell\in\omega
. Similarly
\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}^{i+k}\left[T_{i+k}\right]\neq\emptyset
for all
k\in\omega
. Let
V_{k}:=\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}^{i+k}\left[T_{i+k}\right]
for
k\in\omega
. Note that
p_{k}^{i}\left[V_{i}\right]=V_{k}
for every
k\leq i
. Now we are ready to define the desired sequence.

Let
x_{0}\in G_{0}
such that
x_{0}\in V_{0}
. As
p_{0}^{1}\left[V_{1}\right]=V_{0}
we find
x_{1}\in G_{1}
such that
(x_{0},x_{1})\in V_{1}
, thus
q_{0}^{1}(x_{1})=x_{0}
. In general we can assume that we have
(x_{0},x_{1}\ldots,x_{n})
such that
q_{k}^{k+1}(x_{k+1})=x_{k}
for
k<n
. We can find
x_{n+1}\in G_{n+1}
such that
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})\in V_{n+1}
because
p_{n}^{n+1}\left[V_{n+1}\right]=V_{n}
. Therefore
q_{n}^{n+1}(x_{n+1})=x_{n}
. So we can find a sequence
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in\prod_{i\in\omega}G_{i}
such that
q_{n}^{n+1}(x_{n+1})=x_{n}
for every
n\in\omega
.

\widehat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x_{k})=\downarrow\!\!z_{k}
. Then

\hat{p}\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!x_{k}\right)=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x_{k})=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!z_{k}=Z.

Now we will show that
\hat{p}
is a strict poset homomorphism. Let
Y,Z\in\mathcal{O}(P)
and
Y\subset Z
.
Then
Y=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!y_{k}\subset Z=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!z_{k}
.
Then for each
y_{k}
we find
z_{l_{k}}
such that
\downarrow\!\!y_{k}\subset\downarrow\!\!z_{l_{k}}
which means that
y_{k}\leq z_{l_{k}}
. As

is strict, there are

with
y_{k}^{\prime}\leq z_{l_{k}}^{\prime}

and

.

Put
Y^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!y_{k}^{{}^{\prime}}
. Then
\hat{p}\left(Y^{\prime}\right)=Y
. Further for each

such that

for all

z_{p}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\in Q

. Then

and the latter union is over all
p
not in

is a quotient map for posets.
∎

It turns out that the value of mapping

does not depend on how one represents

as a union of principal down-sets.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
P,Q
be finite posets,
p:Q\to P
be a quotient map and
\hat{p}
be a quotient map induced by
p
. Let
A\subset Q
be a down-set with canonical decomposition
\{\downarrow\!\!x_{i}:i<n\}
and let
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!y_{i}=A
. Then

\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!p(y_{i})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!y_{i})=\hat{p}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!y_{i}\right)=\hat{p}(A)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!p(x_{i}).

It follows that if
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!y_{i}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{l}\downarrow\!\!z_{i}\subset Q
, then
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!p(y_{i})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{l}\downarrow\!\!p(z_{i})
.

To properly define the inductive sequence of
\mathcal{O}(P_{n})
’s such that diagram (

) commutes we need to show that
\hat{p}^{n}_{k}=\hat{p}^{k+1}_{k}\circ\dots\circ\hat{p}^{n}_{n-1}
for all

with

, which follows from the following.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
P,Q,H
be finite posets and
p:Q\to P
,
q:P\to H
be quotient mappings. Then
\widehat{q\circ p}=\widehat{q}\circ\widehat{p}
.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{O}(Q)
. Consider the following cases:

•

if
A=\emptyset
, then
\widehat{q\circ p}(A)=\emptyset=\hat{q}(\emptyset)=\hat{q}(\hat{p}(\emptyset))
.

•

if
A
is a principal down-set
\downarrow\!\!x
, then
\widehat{q\circ p}(\downarrow\!\!x)=\downarrow\!\!q(p(x))=\hat{q}(\downarrow\!\!p(x))=\hat{q}(\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x))=(\hat{q}\circ\hat{p})(\downarrow\!\!x)
.

•

if
A
is non-empty, non-principal down-set, then let
\{\downarrow\!\!x_{i}:i<n\}
be its canonical decomposition. Then
\widehat{q\circ p}(A)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{q\circ p}(\downarrow\!\!x_{i})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\hat{q}(\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x_{i}))=\hat{q}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x_{i}))=\hat{q}(\hat{p}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!x_{i}))=(\hat{q}\circ\hat{p})(A)
.

Thus
\widehat{q\circ p}(A)=(\hat{q}\circ\hat{p})(A)
for every

, which gives the assertion.
∎

Now, we provide the equivalent conditions for the induced mapping to be lattice homomorphism.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
P,Q
be finite posets and
p:Q\to P
be a quotient map. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

\hat{p}
is a lattice-homomorphism.

(2)

\bigcup_{z\in\downarrow\!\!\>x\cap\downarrow\!\!\>y}\limits\downarrow\!\!p(z)\supset\downarrow\!\!p(x)\cap\downarrow\!\!p(y)
for all
x,y\in Q
.

(3)

If
t\leq p(x)
and
t\leq p(y)
, then there is
z\in Q
, such that
z\leq x,z\leq y
and
t\leq p(z)
, for all
x,y\in Q
and
t\in P
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (2) and (3) is pretty clear, so we will only show that (1) and (2) are equivalent. Assume that (2) is true.
Take any
x,y\in Q
. Observe that

\bigcup_{z\in\downarrow\!\!\>x\cap\downarrow\!\!\>y}\downarrow\!\!p(z)=\bigcup_{\downarrow\!\!\>z\subset\downarrow\!\!\>x\cap\downarrow\!\!\>y}\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!z)\subset\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y)

It follows that
\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x)\cap\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!y)=\downarrow\!\!p(x)\cap\downarrow\!\!p(y)\subset\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y)
.
Since
\hat{p}
is an order-homomorphism, then
\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y)\subset\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x)\cap\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!y)
.
Thus
\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x)\cap\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!y)=\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y)

Now take any two non-empty, down-sets
A,B
. Then
A=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!a_{i},B=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!b_{i}
. See that

\hat{p}(A\cap B)=\hat{p}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!a_{i}\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!b_{i})=\hat{p}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!a_{i}\cap\downarrow\!\!b_{j})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!a_{i}\cap\downarrow\!\!b_{j})=

=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!a_{i})\cap\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!b_{j})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!a_{i})\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!b_{i})=\hat{p}(A)\cap\hat{p}(B).

Note that
\hat{p}(A\cap\emptyset)=\emptyset=\hat{p}(A)\cap\hat{p}(\emptyset)
for any down-set

, thus
\hat{p}
is a lattice-homomorphism.

Now, assume (1). Take any
x,y\in Q
.
We need to consider two cases

•

\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y=\emptyset

•

\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y\neq\emptyset

If
\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y=\emptyset
, then
\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y)=\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x)\cap\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!y)=\emptyset
. Thus
\downarrow\!\!p(x)\cap\downarrow\!\!p(y)\subset\bigcup_{z\in\downarrow\!\!\>x\cap\downarrow\!\!\>y}\downarrow\!\!p(z)
.

If
\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y\neq\emptyset
and
\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!z_{i}=
where
z_{i}
’s form its canonical decomposition, then
\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y)\neq\emptyset
. Let
\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!r_{i}
be its canonical decomposition. Since
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\downarrow\!\!p(z_{i})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!r_{i}
, then
\{r_{1},\dots,r_{n}\}\subset\{p(z_{1}),\dots,p(z_{m})\}
. Without loss of generality we may assume that

for

. Finally

\downarrow\!\!p(x)\cap\downarrow\!\!p(y)=\hat{p}(\downarrow\!\!x\cap\downarrow\!\!y)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\downarrow\!\!p(z_{i})=\bigcup_{z\in\downarrow\!\!\>x\cap\downarrow\!\!\>y}\limits\downarrow\!\!p(z),

which means that (2) holds.
∎

4.2

\mathbb{O}(P)

Let
\langle\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{p^{n}_{k}\}_{k<n}\rangle

Definition 4.7
.

We say that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{O}(P)
is a

if
\mathbf{x}=(\downarrow\!\!a_{1},\downarrow\!\!a_{2},\dots)
for some

We already know that
\mathbb{O}(P)

Theorem 4.8
.

\mathbb{O}(P)
is a lattice.

Proof.

We will show that for every
x,y\in\mathbb{O}(P)
their supremum exists.
Let
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
,
(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)\in\mathbb{O}(P)
. It is easy to see that
(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots):=(x_{1}\cup y_{1},x_{2}\cup y_{2},\ldots)
is an upper bound of
\{x,y\}
because it is an element of
\mathbb{O}(P)
as
\hat{p}_{n}^{n+1}
preserves set unions and
x_{n}\subset z_{n},y_{n}\subset z_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Now we will show it is actually the smallest upper bound. Let
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)
be any upper bound of
\{x,y\}
. Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
x_{n}\subset s_{n},y_{n}\subset s_{n}
thus
x_{n}\cup y_{n}\subset s_{n}
so
(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots)\leq(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)
. Therefore
(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots)
is the supremum of
\{x,y\}
.

Now we will show that
\{x,y\}
has an infimum.
It is easy to see that
(\emptyset,\emptyset,\ldots)
is a lower bound of
\{x,y\}
. Consider the set

A_{1}:=\{z_{1}\in\mathcal{O}(P_{1}):\text{ there is a lower bound of }\{x,y\}\text{ such that }z_{1}\text{ is its first coordinate}\}.

Clearly it is a non-empty set. Because supremum of two lower bonds of
\{x,y\}
is still a lower bound of
\{x,y\}
we get that
A_{1}
has the greatest element, say
r_{1}
. Now consider the set

A_{2}:=\{z_{2}\in\mathcal{O}(P_{2}):\text{ there is a lower bound of }\{x,y\}\text{ such that }r_{1}\text{ is its first coordinate and }

z_{2}\text{ is the second}\}.

Similarly this set is non-empty and it has the greatest element
r_{2}
. There is a lower bound of
\{x,y\}
of the form
(r_{1},r_{2},z_{3},z_{4},\dots)\in\mathbb{O}(P)
, which in particular means that
\hat{p}^{2}_{1}(r_{2})=r_{1}
.
Proceeding inductively we define
A_{n}
’s and a sequence
(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots)
. By the construction
\hat{p}^{n+1}_{n}(r_{n+1})=r_{n}
for all

, which means
(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots)\in\mathbb{O}(P)
. Furthermore it is a lower bound of
\{x,y\}
.

Assume that
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)
is a lower bound of
\{x,y\}
. Then by the first part of the proof
(s_{1}\cup r_{1},s_{2}\cup r_{2},\ldots)
is a lower bound of
\{x,y\}
as well and

. Thus by the definition of

’s and simple induction we get

. Thus

, and consequently
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)\leq(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots)
.
∎

Let us present some properties of a limit lattice
\mathbb{O}(P)
. Firstly note that
(\emptyset,\emptyset,\ldots)
is zero (or the least) element of
\mathbb{O}(P)
. Moreover,
(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots)
is one (or the greatest) element of

. As we have already proved
\sup\{(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots),(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)\}=(x_{1}\cup y_{1},x_{2}\cup y_{2},\ldots)

Theorem 4.9
.

\mathbb{O}(P)
is atomic.

Proof.

Take any
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\in\mathbb{O}(P)\setminus\{(\emptyset,\emptyset,\dots)\}
. We will find an atom

(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)\leq(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)

(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)

.

See that then set

A_{1}:=\{z_{1}\in\mathcal{O}(P_{1})\setminus\{\emptyset\}:\text{there is a non-zero element of }\mathbb{O}(P)\text{ that is strictly less than }

is non-empty. Similarly, as a non-empty subset of a finite poset, it has a minimal element, say

’s and sequence
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)
