Analysis of Deep Image Prior and Exploiting Self-Guidance for Image Reconstruction
Shijun Liang, Student Member, IEEE, Evan Bell, Qing Qu, Member, IEEE, Rongrong Wang, Saiprasad Ravishankar, Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract
The ability of deep image prior (DIP) to recover high-quality images from incomplete or corrupted measurements has made it popular in inverse problems in image restoration and medical imaging including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, conventional DIP suffers from severe overfitting and spectral bias effects. In this work, we first provide an analysis of how DIP recovers information from undersampled imaging measurements by analyzing the training dynamics of the underlying networks in the kernel regime for different architectures. This study sheds light on important underlying properties for DIP-based recovery. Current research suggests that incorporating a reference image as network input can enhance DIP’s performance in image reconstruction compared to using random inputs. However, obtaining suitable reference images requires supervision, and raises practical difficulties. In an attempt to overcome this obstacle, we further introduce a self-driven reconstruction process that concurrently optimizes both the network weights and the input while eliminating the need for training data. Our method incorporates a novel denoiser regularization term which enables robust and stable joint estimation of both the network input and reconstructed image. We demonstrate that our self-guided method surpasses both the original DIP and modern supervised methods in terms of MR image reconstruction performance and outperforms previous DIP-based schemes for image inpainting. Our code is available at https://github.com/sjames40/Self-Guided-DIP

However, a significant limitation of these learned approaches is their reliance on extensive training datasets, making adaptability to varying experimental settings or distribution shifts, etc., challenging. Numerous recent works, starting with

Many previous works have studied this phenomenon
[

[
32
]
. Recent research has demonstrated that neural networks with excess parameters, trained using stochastic gradient descent, converge towards a Gaussian process as the number of weights approaches infinity. As a result of this

[
31
]
. The NTK has been used to study the performance of DIP in image denoising successfully. One interesting finding is that the disparity between theory and practice stems from the fact that DIP is typically trained using the Adam optimizer instead of gradient descent (GD)

These prior works hypothesized that the interpretation of the DIP as an image filter
could explain its bias toward clean images for image denoising and explain

36

37

One challenge for DIP based reconstruction is that the network necessarily fits an image to noisy, undersampled, or corrupted measurements. Thus, if trained to convergence, the reconstruction will exhibit artifacts caused by the measurement corruptions.

In this work, we primarily address the application of DIP to image reconstruction from very limited measurements. A recent study
[

it remains uncertain

[

Inspired by the ability of reference-based guidance to improve the performance

I-A

Contributions

We summarize the paper’s main contributions as follows.

\bullet
To gain a deeper understanding of image
reconstruction using DIP, we conduct an analysis of
gradient descent-trained CNNs
in the over-parameterized regime. We
employ a realistic imaging forward operator instead of a Gaussian measurement matrix for our analysis of the case of compressed sensing. Our primary finding is that as the number of gradient descent steps used to optimize the standard DIP objective function approaches infinity, the difference between the network estimate and the ground truth will reside in a subspace related to the null space of the forward operator and the network’s neural tangent kernel.

\bullet
The choice of network architecture significantly affects the ability of DIP to recover
the image
in compressed sensing tasks. We demonstrate both theoretically and empirically that certain generator architectures will have much greater difficulty recovering missing information or frequencies than others.

\bullet
We propose a self-guided DIP method, which eliminates the
need for separate reference images (for network input) and
gives much better image reconstruction
quality than the prior reference-guided method
as well as several other related and competing schemes. The proposed method relies on a crucial denoising-based regularization.

II
Deep Image Prior Based Image Reconstruction

II-A

Image reconstruction problem

To ensure accurate image reconstruction, an ill-posed inverse problem can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}=\underset{\bm{x}}{\arg\min}\,\|\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}_{2}+\mathcal{R}(\bm{x}),

(1)

where
\bm{A}
is a linear measurement operator,
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are the measurements, and
\hat{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
is the reconstructed image.
The first term in the minimization is referred to as a data-fidelity function and can also take on alternative forms depending on imaging setup.
In classical image inpainting,
\bm{A}
is a binary masking operator.
For the task of reconstructing a multi-coil MRI image, represented by
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{q}
the optimization problem is

\hat{\bm{x}}=\underset{\bm{x}}{\arg\min}~\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\|\bm{A}_{c}\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{c}\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda\mathcal{R}(\bm{x}),

(2)

where the
k
-space measurements taken from
N_{c}
coils are represented by
\bm{y}_{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{p},\ c=1,\ldots,N_{c}
. The coil-wise forward operator is denoted as
\bm{A}_{c}=\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\mathbf{S}_{c}
, where
\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{p\times q}
is a masking operator that captures the data sampling pattern in
k
-space,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times q}
is the Fourier transform operator, and
\mathbf{S}_{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times q}
represents the
c
th coil-sensitivity map (a diagonal matrix).

An explicit regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
is employed to limit the solutions to the domain of desirable images.
Various regularizers have been used in image reconstruction. For example, it can be the
\ell_{1}
penalty on wavelet coefficients, a total variation penalty, patch-based sparsity in learned dictionaries, or as in our technique, a denoising type regularization involving e.g., a convolutional neural network (CNN).

II-B

Deep Image Prior for Image Reconstruction

Image reconstruction using DIP is typically formulated as:

\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\underset{\bm{\theta}}{\arg\min}~\|\bm{A}\boldsymbol{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2},\;\;\;\;\hat{\bm{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\vskip-7.22743pt

(3)

Here,
\boldsymbol{f}
is a neural network with parameters
\bm{\theta}
, and
\boldsymbol{z}
is a typically fixed network input that is randomly chosen (e.g., a random Gaussian vector or tensor). We will refer to this formulation as “vanilla DIP” in this work.

II-C

Neural Tangent Kernel Analysis for Image Reconstruction

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
[
31
]
is a mathematical tool used to analyze the training dynamics of neural networks, particularly in the infinite-width setting. It provides an approximation of the function space explored by a neural network during gradient-based training, such as gradient descent or stochastic gradient descent.
When trained with gradient descent, a network’s weights are updated according to the equation:

{\bm{w}}_{t+1}={\bm{w}}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{w}}_{t}),

(4)

where
{\bm{w}}
are the trainable network parameters at a certain training iteration
t
,
\eta
is a step size parameter, and
\mathcal{L}
represents the loss function to be minimized. Rearranging equation (
4
) then gives

\frac{{\bm{w}}_{t+1}-{\bm{w}}_{t}}{\eta}=-\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{w}}_{t}).

(5)

If
\eta
is small, this approximates the differential equation

\frac{d{\bm{w}}}{dt}=-\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{w}}).

(6)

Because the network input is fixed in the vanilla DIP setting, we can view the network output
\boldsymbol{z}
as a function of
{\bm{w}}
. Applying the chain rule yields

\frac{d\boldsymbol{z}({\bm{w}})}{dt}=\nabla\boldsymbol{z}({\bm{w}})^{T}\frac{d{\bm{w}}}{dt}.

(7)

Substituting the loss from equation (
3
) into equations (
6
) and (
7
) gives

\frac{d\boldsymbol{z}({\bm{w}})}{dt}=-\nabla\boldsymbol{z}({\bm{w}})^{T}\nabla\boldsymbol{z}({\bm{w}})\bm{A}^{T}(\bm{A}\boldsymbol{z}({\bm{w}})-\bm{y}).

(8)

The critical assumption of NTK theory is that the matrix
\bm{W}:=\nabla\boldsymbol{z}({\bm{w}})^{T}\nabla\boldsymbol{z}({\bm{w}})
– called the neural tangent kernel – remains fixed throughout training. In this regime, equation (
8
) can be rediscretized to show that the training dynamics of DIP for MRI reconstruction will reduce to

\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{z}_{t}+\eta\bm{W}(\bm{A}^{T}\bm{y}-\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}).

(9)

We start gradient descent from a random initialization
\bm{\theta}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\omega\bm{I})
. With these preliminaries, we now state our first theorem on the training dynamics of DIP for image reconstruction.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
be a full row rank forward operator. Let
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}=\bm{0}
and let
\boldsymbol{z}^{\infty}
be the reconstruction as the number of training iterations approaches infinity. Let
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
be the ground truth image and let
\bm{W}
be the NTK of the reconstructor network. Further suppose that the acquired measurements are free of noise so that
\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}
. If the learning rate satisfies
\eta<\frac{2}{\|\bm{B}\|}
, where
\bm{B}:=\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A}\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, then

•

If the NTK kernel
\bm{W}
is non-singular, then the difference between
\boldsymbol{z}^{\infty}
and
\bm{x}
lies in the null space
N(\bm{A})
of
\bm{A}
, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{z}^{\infty}-\bm{x}\in N(\bm{A}).

(10)

Moreover, as long as
P_{N(\bm{A})}\bm{x}\neq 0
, the reconstruction error
\boldsymbol{z}^{\infty}-\bm{x}\neq\bm{0}
. Here,
P_{N(\bm{A})}
is the projector onto the subspace
N(\bm{A})
.

•

If the NTK
\bm{W}
is singular, and
P_{N(\bm{A})\cap R(\bm{W})}\bm{x}=\mathbf{0}
with
R(\bm{W})
denoting the column or range space of
\bm{W}
, then the difference
\boldsymbol{z}^{\infty}-\bm{x}
will be linear in
P_{N(\bm{W})}\bm{x}
, which is the component of
\bm{x}
lying in the null-space of
\bm{W}
,

\boldsymbol{z}^{\infty}-\bm{x}=-P_{N(\bm{W})}\bm{x}+\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{A}\bm{W}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\dagger}\bm{A}P_{N(\bm{W})}\bm{x}.

(11)

•

If the NTK
\bm{W}
is singular,
P_{N(\bm{A})\cap R(\bm{W})}\bm{x}=\mathbf{0}
and
x\in R(\bm{W})
, then the reconstruction is exact or

\boldsymbol{z}^{\infty}=\bm{x}.

(12)

In practice, the NTK matrix isn’t precisely singular (or low-rank). Nevertheless, the above theorem can be extended to the nearly singular case with some error correction terms. The principal message, however, remains consistent, as outlined below.

When the NTK kernel
\bm{W}
is non-singular, it can result in inaccurate reconstruction (
10
) at convergence. On the other hand, if the NTK kernel
\bm{W}
is singular or say low-rank, then surprisingly, there is a possibility of exact reconstruction. Specifically, (
12
) indicates that exact recovery is possible if the NTK operator effectively represents the underlying image, meaning
\bm{x}\in R(W)
and if the measurement matrix
\bm{A}
exhibits sufficient incoherence with the NTK, in the sense that
N(\bm{A})\cap R(\bm{W})=\emptyset
or
P_{N(\bm{A})\cap R(\bm{W})}\bm{x}=\mathbf{0}
. An example of a situation that meets these criteria is that the true image
\bm{x}
consists of a few non-bandlimited wavelet elements, the NTK kernel
\bm{W}
is sufficient to represent this
\bm{x}
, and
\bm{A}
includes a range of low-frequency Fourier modes. Provided that the wavelet elements constituting
\bm{x}
cannot be linearly combined to form a band-constrained signal, they would not be included in the kernel of
\bm{A}
, which consists of such signals. Consequently, the condition
P_{N(\bm{A})\cap\textrm{range}(\bm{W})}\bm{x}=\mathbf{0}
would be met.

Now consider the more practical scenario when the NTK kernel
\bm{W}
is almost singular (but not exactly). Taking Theorem
1
into account, we can anticipate certain interesting outcomes. First of all, despite
\bm{W}
being nearly singular, it retains full rank. Therefore, as per the result for the non-singular NTK outlined in equation (
10
), the reconstruction will incur a non-zero (likely non-negligible) error in
N(\bm{A})
(e.g., MRI images invariably contain frequency content outside the sampled frequencies). However, this substantial reconstruction error will only emerge if the algorithm is allowed to converge fully over a sufficiently long duration.

In the early stages of the iterations, however, the near singular nature of
\bm{W}
and the use of the gradient descent algorithm imply that the larger elements of

\bm{W}
will predominantly influence the gradient directions. As a result, the initial reconstructions will closely resemble those in scenarios with a singular or low-rank
\bm{W}
. According to the third statement of Theorem
1
, under certain conditions, this leads to minimal reconstruction errors. Therefore, it is plausible to observe a pattern where reconstruction errors initially decrease significantly in the early iterations, before increasing (towards the level indicated by (
10
)) after a prolonged period. A full proof of the theorem is provided in Appendix
A
.

II-D

Extending Our Analysis to the Noisy Setting

The proof of Theorem
1
requires the assumption that the measurements
\bm{y}
are free of noise, or that we have
\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}
exactly. In this section, we extend our analysis to the more realistic setting where the acquired imaging measurements are corrupted by noise, i.e., the acquired measurements are of the form
\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}+\mathbf{n}
with
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}\bm{I})
.

In this case, we estimate the mean squared error (MSE) of the reconstruction through the decomposition
\text{MSE}=||\textbf{Bias}||_{2}^{2}+\text{Variance}

[
33
]
.

We first investigate the
Bias
term. In Appendix B, we use the recursion in equation (
9
) to show that

||\textbf{Bias}_{t}||_{2}^{2}=||\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{n}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{t}]-\bm{x}||_{2}^{2}=||(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{W}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})^{t}\bm{x}||_{2}^{2}.

(13)

We also compute the covariance of
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}
,
\textbf{Cov}_{t}
and find that:

\displaystyle\textbf{Cov}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{n}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}^{T}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{n}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{t}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{n}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{t}]^{T}

(14)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}(\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{W}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})^{t})\bm{A}^{\dagger}(\bm{A}^{\dagger})^{T}(\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A}\bm{W})^{t}).

(15)

If we define
\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}:=(\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{W}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})^{t})\bm{A}^{\dagger}
, then we can write:

\text{Var}_{t}=\text{tr}(\textbf{Cov}_{t})=\sigma^{2}\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}^{T})=\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nu_{t,i}^{2},

(16)

where
\nu_{t,i}
are the singular values of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{t}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
be a full row rank measurement operator. Suppose that
the acquired measurements
are
\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}+\mathbf{n}
, where
\bm{x}
is the ground truth image and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}\bm{I})
. Then the MSE for DIP based reconstruction at iteration
t
is given by

\text{MSE}_{t}=||(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{W}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})^{t}\bm{x}||_{2}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nu_{t,i}^{2},

(17)

where
\nu_{t,i}
are the singular values of the matrix
(\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{W}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{A})^{t})\bm{A}^{\dagger}
.

A full proof of the theorem is provided in Appendix
B
.
As a corollary to Theorem
2
, we consider a special case in the setting of MRI reconstruction. Since MR images are complex-valued, in practice it is common to use a network in DIP with real-valued input, real-valued weights, and a two-channel output, representing the real and imaginary components of the reconstructed image.
The following corollary considers this setting with single-coil MRI. Note that the typical MRI measurement operator mapping a complex-valued image to complex-valued measurements could readily be rewritten as a mapping from/to the stacked real and imaginary parts of the vectors.

Corollary 1
.

We consider the single-coil MRI forward operator
\bm{A}=\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}
. Suppose the network outputs vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2q}
, representing the real and imaginary parts of the reconstruction concatenated together. Further suppose that the NTK, which we write as
\tilde{}\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{2q\times 2q}
has an eigendecomposition of the form:

\tilde{}\bm{W}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}}^{T}\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}};\quad\quad\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Lambda}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{\Lambda}\end{bmatrix},

(18)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{R}&-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{I}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{I}&\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{R}\end{bmatrix}
,
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{R}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{I}
are the real and imaginary parts of the Fourier transform operator. Then the MSE at iteration
t
is given by:

MSE_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{q}[(1-\eta\lambda_{i}m_{i})^{2t}|(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\bm{x})_{i}|^{2}+\sigma^{2}(1-(1-\eta\lambda_{i}m_{i})^{t})^{2}],

(19)

where
\lambda_{i}
s are the diagonal entries of
\bm{\Lambda}
,
m_{i}
denotes the
i
th diagonal entry of
\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{M}
, and
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\bm{x})_{i}
is the
i
th entry of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\bm{x}
.

The above structure for
\tilde{}\bm{W}
has a natural interpretation: applying
\tilde{}\bm{W}
to a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2q}
can be seen to be equivalent to applying the matrix
\bm{W}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}^{H}\bm{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}
to the corresponding complex vector in
\mathbb{C}^{q}
.
Thus, the setting corresponds to an equivalent circulant
\bm{W}
, whose eigenvectors are fully coherent with the Fourier forward operator.

Furthermore, we can interpret equation
19
in the limit as
t\rightarrow\infty
. In this limit, the first term in the sum will tend to
0
for all sampled frequencies, provided
\eta
is sufficiently small, and it is a constant
|(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\bm{x})_{i}|^{2}
at nonsampled frequencies (a result of coherence between
\bm{W}
and measurement operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}
similar to what is described in section
II-E
).
On the other hand, the second term is
0
for all of the
unsampled
frequencies, and will tend to
\sigma^{2}
for the sampled frequencies.
This behavior indicates that we expect a bias-variance tradeoff, where the bias decreases as
t\rightarrow\infty
, the variance increases as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and the optimal performance is achieved for some intermediate
t
.
A full proof of the corollary is provided in Appendix
C
.

II-E

Example of the Relationship Between the NTK and the Forward Operator

Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
show that the training dynamics of DIP for inverse problems such as MRI are largely governed by the relationship between the forward operator
\bm{A}
and the NTK
\bm{W}
. In this section, we analyze a simple network architecture to theoretically demonstrate how this relationship affects the network’s ability to recover missing frequency
content.

In
[
35
]
, the authors analyze simple generator networks
G
of the form

G_{\bm{C}}(\,\cdot\,)=\text{ReLU}(\bm{U}\bm{C}(\,\cdot\,))\boldsymbol{v},

(20)

where
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is a weight matrix,
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a convolution operator, and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a vector with
\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\begin{bmatrix}1,\ldots,1,-1,\ldots,-1\end{bmatrix}^{T}
with half of its entries 1 and half -1, which represent fixed last layer weights of the generator. It is then proven that in expectation

\mathbb{E}[\bm{W}]=\sum_{l=1}^{k}v_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}(\bm{U}\boldsymbol{c}^{(l)})\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}(\bm{U}\boldsymbol{c}^{(l)})^{T}\right]\odot\bm{U}\bm{U}^{T},

(21)

where
\sigma^{{}^{\prime}}
is the derivative of the ReLU activation,
\odot
denotes the entry-wise product,
v_{l}
is the
l
th entry of
\boldsymbol{v}
, and
\boldsymbol{c}^{(l)}
is the
l
th column of
\bm{C}
. It is then shown that

[\mathbb{E}[\bm{W}]]_{i,j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{j}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{j}\|_{2}}\right)/\pi\right),

(22)

where
\mathbf{u}_{r}
denotes the
r
th row of
\bm{U}
.
In this case, with circulant
\bm{U}
, it is possible to show that
\bm{W}
is also circulant, and hence is diagonalized by Fourier operators. Thus, networks of this form are related to the case in Corollary
1
(in expectation).

II-G

Overfitting and Spectral Bias in Deep Image Prior

Because DIP typically uses corrupted and/or limited data for network training, any related distortions will inevitably manifest in the network’s output if it is trained until the loss function reaches equilibrium. This issue impacts not only DIP’s performance in well-researched areas like image denoising, but also in inverse problems such as MRI reconstruction, where forward operators may have high dimensional null spaces. Fig.
2
quantitatively demonstrates the overfitting phenomenon in MRI reconstruction. One can see that the reconstruction reaches peak performance quickly and then slowly diminishes as the training persists. This highlights the necessity for implementing an early stopping criterion when using vanilla DIP to solve inverse problems.

II-H

Understanding Spectral Bias and Overfitting for DIP MRI

To gain insights into the spectral bias inherent in vanilla DIP MRI image reconstruction, we utilize a frequency band metric to explore the disparity between the reconstructed frequencies and the actual ones. We compare the multi-coil
k
-space of the output image
\boldsymbol{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})
at every stage of network training to the fully-sampled the
k
-space
\bm{y}_{c},c=1,...,N_{c}
of the ground truth image
\bm{x}
to study how various frequency components converge (refer to Fig.
2
). We execute this by calculating a normalized error metric for low, medium, and high-frequency bands:

\text{NMSE}:=\frac{~\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\begin{Vmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{\textbf{freq}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\mathbf{S}_{c}\boldsymbol{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})-\mathbf{M}_{\textbf{freq}}\bm{y}_{c}\end{Vmatrix}_{2}^{2}}{~\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\begin{Vmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{\textbf{freq}}\bm{y}_{c}\end{Vmatrix}_{2}^{2}}

(23)

where
\mathbf{M}_{\textbf{freq}}
is the frequency band mask.
Intuitively, the above metric measures the consistency between the reconstructed image
\boldsymbol{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})
and the true
k
-space
\bm{y}_{c}
in the frequency domain. Fig.
2
plots this metric computed across three frequency bands for vanilla DIP MRI reconstruction. The result shows that the low frequencies are learned more quickly and with lower error, confirming that spectral bias is present in MRI reconstruction using DIP.

Bottom row: reconstruction PSNR plot on the left illustrates the overfitting issue that occurs during MRI reconstruction. Spectral bias also affects the performance of DIP for MRI reconstruction (right plot), as different frequency bands are reconstructed at different rates.

III
Methodology

To more effectively address the overfitting issue inherent in the vanilla deep image prior (DIP), certain methods have been introduced including using matched references.
In contrast to the approach of using a reference image, we propose the introduction of a self-regulation method as an enhancement.

III-A

Reference-Guided DIP

The reference-guided DIP formulation was proposed in
[
41
]
as

\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\underset{\bm{\theta}}{\arg\min}~\|\bm{A}\boldsymbol{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2},\;\;\;\;\;\hat{\bm{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{z}).

(24)

This formulation is identical to the problem in (
3
), except that the input to the network is no longer fixed random noise, but is instead a reference image that is very similar to the one being reconstructed.
The input to the network introduces some additional structural information, and we can consider the network as essentially performing image refinement or style transfer rather than image generation from scratch.
This method is quite reasonable in cases where a dataset of structurally similar images is available, and there is a systematic way to choose the network input image from the dataset based on only undersampled
k
-space observations at testing time.

In
[
41
]
, the input image seems to be chosen by hand. As a more realistic modification of this method, we
propose
an approach similar to the recent LONDN-MRI
[
42
]
method to search for the reference image (using a distance metric such as Euclidean distance or other metric) that is most similar to an estimated test reconstruction from undersampled data. In our experiments, we used
\bm{A}^{H}\bm{y}
as estimated test image, and used corresponding versions of reference images to find the closest neighbor.

III-B

Self-Guided DIP

To circumvent the need for a prior chosen reference to guide DIP, we introduce the following method, which adaptively estimates such a reference that we call
self-guided DIP
:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{\theta},\boldsymbol{z}}{\arg\min}~\underbrace{\|\bm{A}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta}}[\boldsymbol{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}+\bm{\eta})]-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\text{data consistency}}

\displaystyle+\alpha\underbrace{\|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta}}[\boldsymbol{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}+\bm{\eta})]-\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\text{denoiser regularization}}

(25)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{\eta}}\left[\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}+\bm{\eta})\right]

(26)

In this optimization,
\boldsymbol{z}
is no longer a reference image, but is instead initialized appropriately and updated. For example, in multi-coil MRI, the initialization can be a zero-filled (for missing
k
-space) reconstruction
\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\bm{A}_{c}^{H}\bm{y}
. Furthermore,
\bm{\eta}
is random noise drawn from some distribution
P_{\bm{\eta}}
(either uniform or Gaussian in our experiments). The first term in the optimization enforces data consistency, while the second term is a regularization penalty.
The input
\boldsymbol{z}
is optimized here, in contrast to both vanilla and reference-guided DIP. Hence, we call this method “self-guided” because at each iteration (of an algorithm) the network’s “reference” is updated, with the regularization also guiding the process. Another intriguing feature of this method that we have observed is that the optimal performance is obtained when the magnitude of
\bm{\eta}
is quite large.

The proposed regularization smooths the network output over input perturbations. This strategy has been exploited in approaches such as randomized smoothing and makes the network mapping more stable. The regularizer attempts to match the smoothed output to the unperturbed input, mimicking a denoiser.
A somewhat simpler form of the objective would place the expectation outside the norms rather than inside. In this case, the regularization term would push the network to act as a usual denoiser, i.e., ensure
\boldsymbol{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}+\bm{\eta})\approx\boldsymbol{z}
.
We place the expectation inside of the norms (with the reconstruction being
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta}}[\boldsymbol{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}+\bm{\eta})]
). This offers the learned network some more flexibility and yielded slightly better image reconstructions in our studies. For example, with the expectation inside, the regularization loss would be
0
for zero-mean
\bm{\eta}
if the network were a denoising autoencoder or even just an autoencoder.

The proposed loss is optimized using the Adam optimizer.
In Fig.
3
, the important role of the regularization component in the optimization process of a U-Net network (for multi-coil MRI with 4x and 8x undersampling mask) is underscored. In the absence of this element,
\boldsymbol{z}
fails to be updated correctly, resulting in unstable training and inferior performance. This illustrates the efficiency of leveraging smoothing and denoising based regularization.

Fig.
4
shows how the network’s input
\boldsymbol{z}+\bm{\eta}
evolves throughout the self-guided DIP optimization. It is observable that the input
\boldsymbol{z}
progressively acquires more feature information, which facilitates the network’s learning process, but the input continues to change because of the added noise
\bm{\eta}
. In each iteration, the network and input are updated as in Fig.
5
.

III-C

Post-processing Data Correction

In some applications, it may be desirable to ensure that the reconstructed image is completely consistent with the acquired measurements. This could be the case in compressed sensing problems, when signal-to-noise ratios are good. For example, consider
\bm{y}=\mathbf{M}\bm{\Psi}\bm{x}
, where
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
is a subsampling matrix and
\bm{\Psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times q}
is a full measurement matrix. Then the matrix
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
, subsamples the same measurements, but has zero rows for measurements that are not sampled.
Define
\mathbf{\overline{M}}=\bm{I}-\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
. Then,
for any reconstruction
\hat{\bm{x}}
, we can construct new, “fully sampled” measurements
\bm{y}_{\text{new}}\in\mathbb{C}^{q}
as
\bm{y}_{\text{new}}=\mathbf{M}^{T}\bm{y}+\mathbf{\overline{M}}\bm{\Psi}\hat{\bm{x}}
.
Then with these measurements, we can obtain a corrected (data consistent) reconstruction by solving
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\text{corrected}}=\underset{\bm{x}}{\arg\min}\,||\bm{\Psi}\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{\text{new}}||_{2}^{2}
.

For multi-coil MRI, assuming appropriately normalized coil sensitivity maps (
\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\mathbf{S}_{c}^{H}\mathbf{S}_{c}=\bm{I}
) yields

\bm{y}_{c_{\text{new}}}=\mathbf{M}^{T}\bm{y}_{c}+\mathbf{\overline{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\mathbf{S}_{c}\hat{\bm{x}},\quad\hat{\bm{x}}_{\text{corrected}}=\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\mathbf{S}_{c}^{H}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}^{H}\bm{y}_{c_{\text{new}}}.\\

V
Discussion of Results

We have introduced a novel
self-guided image reconstruction method requiring no training data that iteratively optimizes the reconstructor network and its input. This approach is completely unsupervised and instance-adaptive, and demonstrated strong reconstruction performance on the multi-coil fastMRI knee, brain datasets and the Stanford FSE dataset.
The approach does not require pre-training and can easily accommodate variations in most MRI reconstruction settings. Additionally, it was found to outperform supervised methods like image-domain U-Net and hybrid-domain MoDL, especially on smaller, more diverse datasets. We also showed that the networks learned in self-guided DIP demonstrate better stability and generalizability compared to those learned in vanilla DIP. Finally, we demonstrated the effectiveness of self-guided DIP for image inpainting on the CBSD68 dataset.

While the self-guided DIP algorithm does require more optimization steps than vanilla DIP because the network’s input must also be optimized, this additional cost is not detrimental.
For example, self-guided DIP with a randomly initialized U-Net took
2
minutes to run on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX A5000 GPU (with a batch size of
2
and
1500
training iterations). By comparison, vanilla DIP takes about
1
minute to reach peak performance (measured when ground truth is known) over iterations with the same GPU. The reference-guided DIP and TV-regularized DIP also take approximately 1 minute each. Even though the self-guided method does incur some additional computational cost, it provides a significant performance gain over the other DIP methods and mitigates over-fitting.

VI
Conclusions

In this study, we first presented theoretical results that help explain the training dynamics of unsupervised neural networks for general image reconstruction. We empirically validated our findings using some simple example problems.

We then proposed a novel self-guided deep image prior based MRI reconstruction technique that iteratively optimizes the network input while also training the model to be robust to large random perturbations of its input. This was achieved by introducing a new regularization term that encourages the reconstructor to act as a denoiser.

We empirically demonstrated that this method yields promising results for MRI reconstruction and image inpainting on different datasets. Notably, our approach does not involve any pre-training, and can thus readily handle changes in the measured data. Moreover, this self-guided method showed better performance than the same model trained in a supervised manner on a large dataset (with lengthy training times). This shows that highly adaptive learning approaches may have the potential to outperform traditional data-driven learning approaches in image reconstruction.
In the future, we hope to carry out more theoretical analyses to better understand the performance of self-guided DIP for image reconstruction and analyze how the optimization of the network’s input improves reconstruction performance. We also plan to study whether similar self-guided schemes could improve the performance of DIP for other imaging modalities and restoration tasks such as deblurring and super-resolution.
