Heavy Fermions as an Efficient Representation of Atomistic Strain and Relaxation in Twisted Bilayer Graphene
Jonah Herzog-Arbeitman, Jiabin Yu, Dumitru Călugăru, Haoyu Hu, Nicolas Regnault, Oskar Vafek, Jian Kang, B. Andrei Bernevig

Abstract
Although the strongly interacting flat bands in twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) have been approached using the minimal Bistritzer-MacDonald (BM) Hamiltonian, there is mounting evidence that strain and lattice relaxation are essential in correctly determining the order of the correlated insulator groundstates. These effects can be incorporated in an enhanced continuum model by introducing additional terms computed from the relaxation profile. To develop an analytical and physical understanding of these effects, we include strain and relaxation in the topological heavy fermion (HF) model of TBG. We find that strain and relaxation are very well captured in first order perturbation theory by projection onto the fully symmetric HF Hilbert space, and remarkably do not alter the interacting terms in the periodic Anderson model. Their effects are fully incorporated in the single-particle HF Hamiltonian, and can be reproduced in a minimal model with only 4 symmetry-breaking terms. Our results demonstrate that the heavy fermion framework of TBG is an efficient and robust representation of the perturbations encountered in experiment.

Since it is impossible to obtain fully symmetric, exponentially Wannier states (and hence a physical tight-binding model) for the two flat bands, we are motivated to include additional states in the Hilbert space. Ref.

II.3
Outlook

Symmetries play an important role in identifying and obtaining the heavy fermion representation. However, adding heterostrain and relaxation will break all symmetries except
C_{2z}\mathcal{T}
. Despite this reduction,
C_{2z}\mathcal{T}
alone is enough to protect the fragile topology of the flat bands and obstruct a tight-binding model with local symmetries. This means that the necessity of a heavy fermion framework for describing the flat bands with a local model remains.

More important than these single-particle considerations is the interacting problem. For instance, strong coupling
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
, momentum space Monte Carlo
[
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
,
76
]
, and projected Hartree-Fock calculations
[
26
,
16
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
encounter no fundamental difficulties in the flat band Hilbert space, despite its topology and lack of local description. Instead, strain and relaxation hinder the application of these methods because the active bands become less flat and less well isolated. As the gap becomes smaller, interactions increasingly mix the flat and dispersive bands, causing flat band approximations to fail and requiring projected calculations to include a larger set of active bands and increasing computational expense. On the other hand, the larger Hilbert space accessible by interactions is well suited to the HF framework, which already includes nearby states in the dispersive bands. The locality of the model allows powerful numerical tools like dynamical mean field theory (DMFT) to be applied
[
37
]
.

IV
Strain and Relaxation Corrections to the Heavy Fermion Model

Order of magnitude estimates indicate that the relevant perturbations to the BM model can be captured by perturbation theory within the unperturbed HF Hilbert space. This assertion implies that the perturbed HF model takes the form

\displaystyle h^{K}_{HF,\epsilon,\Lambda}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=h^{K}_{HF}(\mathbf{k})+\delta h^{K}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{k})+\delta h^{K}_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{k})

(20)

acting on the
unperturbed

f
- and
c
-mode wavefunctions. Here the correction terms
\delta h_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{k}),\delta h_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{k})
correspond to strain and non-local tunneling. They are simply the first-order perturbation theory expressions

\displaystyle\hbox{}[\delta h]_{nn^{\prime}}=\braket{\mathbf{k},n|\delta H_{BM}|\mathbf{k},n^{\prime}}

(21)

where
\delta H_{BM}
includes all symmetry-breaking terms, including strain and non-local tunneling,
\ket{\mathbf{k},n}=c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G}=0,n}\ket{0},f^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},n}\ket{0}
are the unperturbed basis states of the HF model. In practice, we will keep only constant terms in
\delta h^{K}_{\epsilon}
and only constant and linear terms in
\delta h_{\Lambda}^{K}(\mathbf{k})
, just like in the unperturbed HF model. Hence all overlaps can be computed from overlaps at
\mathbf{k}=0
(see App.
B.1
). To derive the form of
\delta h_{\epsilon}
, we use the fact that strain preserves
C_{2z}\mathcal{T},P
, and transforms as a vector under
C_{3z},C_{2x}
.
These constraints on the constant terms (see App.
B.1
) yield the form

\displaystyle\delta h_{\epsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}c\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}&c^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}&i\gamma^{\prime}\epsilon_{+}\sigma_{3}\\
&M^{\prime}\epsilon_{+}\sigma_{2}&c^{\prime\prime}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot(\sigma_{0},-i\sigma_{3})\\
h.c.&&M_{f}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(22)

in terms of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{xy},\epsilon_{-})
and the 6 strain couplings which we compute in Table
1
.

Similarly, the non-local tunneling terms obey all crystalline symmetries but commute with
P
, giving the form

\displaystyle\delta h_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}\sigma_{0}+v_{2}\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}&v_{1}\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}&0\\
&\mu_{2}\sigma_{0}&v_{3}\mathbf{k}\cdot(\sigma_{0},-i\sigma_{3})\\
h.c.&&\mu_{f}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(23)

with the
P
-breaking orbital potentials
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
(we always set
\mu_{f}=0
) and velocities
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
(see Table
1
).
We find that
v_{3}
is an order of magnitude smaller and can be neglected. In fact, we will show in Sec.
VI.1
that the velocities
v_{1},v_{2}
can also be neglected without losing accuracy in the flat bands.

We compare the band structures between the BM model and generalized HF model in Fig.
2
, finding excellent agreement. This validates our assumption that the underlying
f
- and
c
-mode basis does not need to be altered. Rather, all symmetry breaking comes from the corrections to the Hamiltonian, and no re-Wannierization is necessary. Fig.
3
demonstrates that not only the spectrum, but also the symmetry-breaking patterns of the flat band eigenstates are captured by the HF model. This is quantified by the deviation of the symmetry sewing matrix from unitarity, i.e.

\displaystyle\text{error}_{g}(\mathbf{k})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}(\Sigma_{n}[B_{g}(\mathbf{k})]-1)^{2}

(24)

where
\Sigma_{n}[A]
is the
n
th singular value of
A
and
B_{g}(\mathbf{k})=U^{\dagger}(g\mathbf{k})D[g]U(\mathbf{k})
is the sewing matrix of
g
(here
D[g]
is the representation of
g
and
U(\mathbf{k})
is a column matrix of the flat band eigenvectors, the two bands closest to charge neutrality). If
g
is a symmetry of the Hamiltonian, then
B_{g}(\mathbf{k})
is unitary and
\Sigma_{n}[B_{g}(\mathbf{k})]=1
. We see from Fig.
3
that
\text{error}(g)
is very small away from the
\Gamma
point. This is easily understood from the perturbation theory. The inclusion of symmetry breaking terms
\delta h
can alter the flat bands in two ways: it can recombine the flat band eigenstates and/or mix them with dispersive band eigenstates. To begin, we consider mixing outside the flat band manifold. Recall the first order perturbation theory expression

\displaystyle\delta U_{0}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{U_{n}(\mathbf{k})U^{\dagger}_{n}(\mathbf{k})}{\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{k})}\,\delta h(\mathbf{k})\,U_{0}(\mathbf{k}),

(25)

where
U_{n}(\mathbf{k})
are the dispersive eigenstates with gap
\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{k})
, and
U_{0}
are the unperturbed flat bands eigenvectors. We see that away from
\Gamma
, there is a large gap
>50
meV to the closest dispersive bands compared to the
\sim 10
meV symmetry breaking terms. Thus corrections outside the flat band manifold are suppressed. Within the flat band manifold, the flat band states will be strongly mixed, but since both states are included in the sewing matrix
B_{g}(\mathbf{k})
, only mixing outside the flat band manifold causes deviations from unitarity. Hence
\text{error}_{g}(\mathbf{k})
is expected to be peaked at
\Gamma
where the gap to the dispersive bands is smallest.

Lastly, we check numerically that including
\delta h_{\Lambda}
to break particle-hole symmetry has a very weak effect on the eigenstates: the maximum deviation of the sewing matrix from unitarity is
<2.5\%
despite visible particle-hole breaking in the spectrum
[
65
]
. We will see in the following section that a full treatment of relaxation in the KV model beyond Eq. (
18
) results in significant violations of particle-hole symmetry in the eigenstates, which nevertheless are captured within a fully symmetry HF Hilbert space.

Fully Relaxed KV and HF Model

We now consider the full effect of lattice relaxation in TBG using the Kang-Vafek model introduced in Refs.
[
62
,
81
]
. First, it is necessary to introduce the relaxation field which arises from the classical continuum mechanics of the inter-layer interactions.

V.1
Atomistic Hamiltonian and Relaxation

We index the graphene atoms in the two layers by their location
\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{R},\ell,\alpha}
where
\mathbf{R}
is a graphene unit cell,
\ell=\pm
are the top and bottom layers, and
\alpha=
A/B are the two sublattices at positions
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\alpha}
. We decompose the positions as

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{R},\ell,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{R},\ell,\alpha}

(26)

where we have separated out the in-plane reference coordinates
\mathbf{R}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\alpha}
from the deformation
\mathbf{u}
. We follow Refs.
[
62
]
and
[
81
]
and consider only in-plane relaxation.

In principle, the determination of the displacement field
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{R},\ell,\alpha}
is a fully quantum mechanical problem solved with density functional theory (DFT) by self-consistently minimizing the electronic and lattice energies, see e.g. Refs.
[
82
,
83
]
. However, it can also be approached classically using a continuum approximation with
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{R}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})\equiv\mathbf{u}_{\ell,\alpha}(\mathbf{R}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\alpha})=\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{R},\ell,\alpha}

(27)

which assumes that
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})
does not rapidly vary on the atomic scale of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\alpha}
. The profile of
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})
is variationally determined by minimizing the elasticity Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H[\mathbf{u}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}T[\mathbf{u}_{\ell}]+V[\mathbf{u}_{+}-\mathbf{u}_{-}]

(28)

where
T[\mathbf{u}]
is the kinetic energy functional depending on
\partial_{i}u_{j}
, and
V[\mathbf{u}_{+}-\mathbf{u}_{-}]
is the adhesion energy functional (see App.
C
). Using the Euler-Lagrange equations for
H[\mathbf{u}]
,
\mathbf{u}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})
can be solved numerically using an iterative procedure starting from the initial rigidly twisted configuration

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{0}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})=R(l\frac{\theta}{2})\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}

(29)

until self-consistency is achieved. Explicit expressions can be found in App.
C
and Ref.
[
62
]
. The boundary conditions are periodic on the moiré lattice, so that
\mathbf{u}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{a}_{1})=\mathbf{u}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{a}_{2})=\mathbf{u}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})
. Note that
\mathbf{u}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})
will preserve all the crystalline symmetries of the initial state, and therefore we distinguish
\mathbf{u}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})
from the symmetry-breaking heterostrain.

Having determined the relaxation profile
\mathbf{u}_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})
, Ref.
[
62
]
next obtains an effective continuum model starting from the microscopic tight-binding Hamiltonian where the hopping between two atoms only depends on their relative displacement and orientation. The microscopic Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{R}\ell\alpha,\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}\beta}t(\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{R},\ell,\alpha}-\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\ell^{\prime},\beta})c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\ell,\alpha}c_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\ell^{\prime},\beta}

(30)

and the electron operators obey discrete anti-commutation relations
\{c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\ell,\alpha},c_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\ell^{\prime},\beta}\}=\delta_{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}
. The hopping function
t(\mathbf{d})
can be chosen as the Slater-Koster function for
p_{z}
orbitals or fit to DFT data. By expanding
t(\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{R},\ell,\alpha}-\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\ell^{\prime},\beta})
in powers of
\nabla\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
, Ref.
[
62
]
obtains a generalized continuum model taking the form

\displaystyle h^{K}_{KV}(\mathbf{r})=\begin{pmatrix}v_{F}(-i\boldsymbol{\nabla}-\mathcal{A})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-\theta/2}&T(\mathbf{r})+\frac{1}{2}\{-i\boldsymbol{\nabla},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\}\\
h.c.&v_{F}(-i\boldsymbol{\nabla}+\mathcal{A})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\theta/2}\end{pmatrix}

(31)

where
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{r})
is a relaxation gauge field computed to five shells for convergence, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{G}|\leq 5|\mathbf{b}_{1}|}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\mathcal{A}_{i,\mathbf{G}},

(32)

T(\mathbf{r}),\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbf{r})
are inter-layer couplings computed to three shells for convergence, e.g.

\displaystyle T(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Big(e^{i\mathbf{q}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{r}}T_{i}+e^{i(-2\mathbf{q}_{i})\cdot\mathbf{r}}T^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}e^{i(2\mathbf{q}_{i}-\mathbf{q}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{r}}T^{\prime\prime}_{i,j}\Big),

(33)

and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\theta/2}=e^{i\sigma_{3}\theta/2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}e^{-i\sigma_{3}\theta/2}
. The gradient coupling
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbf{r})
in Eq. (
18
) is also computed to three shells. Eq. (
31
) neglects higher order gradient terms that have negligible effect. Explicit formulas for the expansion coefficients of
\mathcal{A},T,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
in terms of the relaxation field
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})
are involved and can be found in the appendix of Ref.
[
62
]
. We note that
v_{F}
in Eq. (
31
) is computed from the full Slater-Koster parameterization to be
v_{F}=5360
meV Å, which is about
10\%
smaller than the Dirac velocity used in the BM model (Sec.
II.1
).

The band structure at
\theta=1.05^{\circ}
is shown in Fig.
4
, and features a larger bandwidth
\sim 15
meV, a smaller gap to the dispersive bands
\sim 5
meV, and strong particle-hole asymmetry. Despite the complexity of the Hamiltonian, we now show it is well captured by a heavy fermion representation.

VI
Analytical Expressions for the Flat Bands

Having constructed the HF model and its perturbations, we are now in a position to study the flat bands and understand their behavior under strain and relaxation. We make use of a perturbation theory starting from the limit
M=0
, where the bands are perfectly flat due to an enhanced
U(4)
symmetry
[
21
]
. In this limit,
h_{HF}^{K}
has the following flat band eigenvectors

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\pm}(\mathbf{k})=D_{\theta}(0,0,\pm v^{\prime}k+\gamma,v^{\prime}k\pm\gamma,-vk,\mp vk)^{T}

(38)

up to a normalization, and
D_{\theta}=\text{diag}(e^{i\theta},e^{i2\theta},1,e^{i3\theta},e^{i\theta},e^{i2\theta})
,
k=|\mathbf{k}|
. The
SO(2)
symmetric form of the wavefunctions arises from keeping only the
\mathbf{G}=0
shell of
c
-modes, and it is thanks to this emergent symmetry of the flat band limit of the HF model that we may find simple analytical expressions for the eigenstates. Eq. (
38
) shows clearly how
\gamma
is responsible for mixing the
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}

c
-modes (carrying the angular momenta
0,3=0\mod 3
respectively, determined from
D_{\theta}
) into the flat band wavefunctions near
\Gamma
, whereas at large
k
they are dominated by the
f
-modes (with angular momenta
1,2=\pm 1\mod 3
) since
|v|>|v^{\prime}|
. It is also straightforward to compute the (abelian) Fubini-Study metric
[
84
]
of the two flat bands

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\ (\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathcal{P})^{2}=\frac{4\gamma^{2}v^{2}}{\left(k^{2}v^{2}+\gamma^{2}\right)^{2}}

(39)

showing the characteristic
\Gamma
point peak. In Eq. (
39
), we used
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
and set
v^{\prime}=0
for simplicity. It is interesting to note that
g(\mathbf{k})\to 4\pi\delta(\mathbf{k})
as
\gamma\to 0
, showing that hybridization of the
f
- and
c
-modes smooths out the intrinsic quantum geometry of the Dirac node at
\Gamma
.

It is now straightforward to compute the corrections away from the perfectly flat band limit doing degenerate perturbation via expectation values in
\mathcal{U}_{\pm}(\mathbf{k})
, resulting in a
2\times 2
effective Hamiltonian for the flat bands. However, to obtain the simplest possible expressions, we will also take
v^{\prime}=0
. This approximation will still capture the key characteristics of the flat bands because
(i)

v^{\prime}k
vanishes near the BZ center and
(ii)

|v^{\prime}k|<|vk|
is subdominant for large
k
near the BZ edge.

We study each term in the Hamiltonian

VII
Conclusion

This work has constructed a single-particle heavy fermion model for TBG in the presence of realistic strain and lattice relaxation in two models: the perturbed BM model with particle-hole breaking and minimally-coupled heterostrain, and the KV model incorporating fully microscopic relaxation. Despite the significant differences between these Hamiltonians, they are described by the same (generalized) heavy fermion model, albeit with different parameterizations. This model accurately reproduces the lowest 6 bands within a
\pm 50
meV window, as well as reproducing the patterns of symmetry breaking in the eigenstates. All parameters of the model are computed directly from the
f
-mode wavefunctions obtained with Wannier90 and the continuum Hamiltonians — no fitting or optimization is performed. Although we have obtained the heavy fermion parameters numerically, good analytical estimates are possible
[
85
]
and left for other work.

We made use of the simple form of the HF model to analytically obtain flat band wavefunctions and their dispersions in the presence of relaxation and strain. We found that the key features of the active bands are determined by a small number of parameters, the three symmetry-breaking terms
\mu_{2},M^{\prime},
and
M_{f}
in addition to the
v,\gamma,M
terms of the original HF model, which could be useful for the study of reduced models.

Ultimately, the purpose of the HF model is not merely to simplify the single-particle physics, but to approach the strongly interacting problem. It is worth repeating that a minimal tight-binding model within a reduced Hilbert space — the typical tool in this pursuit — is unavailable due to the stable topological obstruction protected by the
PC_{2z}\mathcal{T}
symmetry emergent in the BM model. Even
P
-breaking perturbations do not provide an easy escape since these perturbations reduce the gap to the dispersive bands, making a projection onto the flat bands unreliable due to the large Hubbard
U_{1}
.

The HF model was originally proposed to circumvent these difficulties
[
21
]
. It provides a faithful model whose simple form is amenable to analytical calculation, and whose locality and low dimensionality (after truncating the
c
-modes) make it suitable for intensive numerics. The inclusion of realistic strain and relaxation poses no difficulty for the formalism, underscoring the advantage of heavy fermions. In Ref.
[
56
]
, we leverage this result to develop an analytical understanding of the IKS as a heavy fermion state.
