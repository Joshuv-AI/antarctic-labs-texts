On self-similar converging shock waves
Juhi Jang, Jiaqi Liu, Matthew Schrecker

Abstract
In this paper, we rigorously prove the existence of self-similar converging shock wave solutions for the non-isentropic Euler equations for γ ∈ ( 1 , 3 ] \gamma\in(1,3] . These solutions are analytic away from the shock interface before collapse, and the shock wave reaches the origin at the time of collapse. The region behind the shock undergoes a sonic degeneracy, which causes numerous difficulties for smoothness of the flow and the analytic construction of the solution. The proof is based on continuity arguments, nonlinear invariances, and barrier functions.

Introduction

The converging
shock wave problem is a classical hydrodynamical problem in gas dynamics, where a spherical shock originates from infinity or a large radius (for example, by a spherical piston) in a spherically symmetric medium and propagates towards the center of symmetry, becoming stronger as it approaches the origin. In finite time, the spherical shock collapses at the center. The problem was first discussed by Guderley in his seminal work
[
20
]
(see also Landau
[

and Stanyukovich
[

[

It has been long known since Guderley that for an inviscid perfect gas, only a particular choice of similarity exponent would lead to a converging self-similar radially symmetric shock wave. Despite many works
[

In this paper, we give a rigorous construction of self-similar converging shock wave solutions described by the non-isentropic compressible Euler equations for an ideal perfect gas.

The study of the compressible Euler equations has a long history and a correspondingly vast literature, much of it focused on the one-dimensional problem. As is well known, a fundamental difficulty
in the analysis of the compressible Euler equations stems from the expected formation of singularities in the solutions, a phenomenon known since the time of Riemann and Stokes. For a survey of the literature on the 1D Euler equations, including existence of weak solutions and formation of singularities, we refer to
[

Although there is no general theory for the existence of weak solutions for the multi-dimensional problem, in recent years, the existence of weak entropy solutions for the isentropic system under the assumption of spherical symmetry has been established in

The formation of singularities in the multi-dimensional compressible Euler equations was first rigorously established in

[
41
]

To better understand the structure of the singularities, there has been much interest in the study of shock formation in solutions of the multi-dimensional compressible Euler equations. The first rigorous results are those in spherical symmetry of
[
46

[

[

16

In recent years, there have been further exciting

to allow for non-trivial vorticity and entropy and to remove symmetry assumptions

Moreover, in

As well as these shock solutions, other kinds of strong singularity

Main result and methodology

Many authors have claimed that, for each

to the origin

The self-similar solutions that we construct are built by concatenating two trajectories in the phase-plane in such a way that we obtain an analytic solution of the ODE (

•

The first trajectory connects

to either

or

The second trajectory connects either

Remark 2.10

To see that these solutions from Theorem

(and hence

simply by integrating, away from critical points. As

is an analytic function in

and

and

are both analytic in

with simple zeros at the triple points

and

remains smooth (indeed, analytic), as it passes through a unique sonic point

where
(V(x_{\textup{sonic}}),C(x_{\textup{sonic}}))
is either

or
P_{8}
. As

, it is clear that if, for some

Our strategy for proving the existence of the solutions constructed in Theorem

proceeds in three key stages, inspired by recent mathematical constructions of self-similar gravitational collapse

and we construct a local solution around

for all

. In order to show the local existence of such solutions, we first choose a local branch

) and

Once we have found the recurrence relation for the higher order coefficients, a series of combinatorial estimates and an inductive argument allow us to bound coefficients to all orders and establish the convergence of the series in Theorem

The second main step of the proof is to show the existence, for each

The third main step in the construction is to prove that the solution connecting

Definition 2.1

(Lower barrier function and upper barrier function)

We say that a differentiable function

on

if

on
(V_{a},V_{b})

on
(V_{a},V_{b})
.

In practice,

is a specific differentiable function where we design

\frac{d}{dV}(C-B)\big|_{\overline{V}}\leq 0,\ \text{ respectively }\ \frac{d}{dV}(C-B)\big|_{\overline{V}}\geq 0.

To derive a contradiction, we therefore prove that, whenever

, then we must have

\frac{d}{dV}(C-B)\big|_{\overline{V}}>0,\ \text{ respectively }\ \frac{d}{dV}(C-B)\big|_{\overline{V}}<0.

(2.20)

\displaystyle k(\gamma,z_{M})V+\frac{4zk(\gamma,z_{M})}{1+V}=\frac{k(\gamma,z_{M})}{1+V}(V^{2}+V+4z)>\frac{k(\gamma,z_{M})}{1+V}(V_{6}^{2}+V_{6}+4z)

for any

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\gamma-2)^{2}z^{2}+2(2-\gamma)zw+2(6-\gamma)z}{4}>0.

Therefore, by (

Proposition 4.7
.

For any

.

Proof.

When

, we have

, thus obviating the need for further discussion.
If

, by Theorem
4.4
, it is equivalent to demonstrating that the solution trajectory can not connect to
P_{8}
. We will discuss

and

, the solution trajectory cannot connect to
P_{8}
, since the solution of (
2.1
) with

is decreasing by

3.10
. We further note
that

leads to

. By Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.6
,

C_{8}(z)\geq C_{1}|_{\gamma=2}>C_{1}(\gamma)
for any
\gamma\in(1,\gamma_{\star}]
and
z\leq\frac{2}{3(\gamma+4)}
.
On the other hand, it is easy to check

\displaystyle z_{m}-\frac{2}{3(\gamma+4)}=\frac{\gamma-1}{(2\gamma-1)(\gamma+1)}-\frac{2}{3(\gamma+4)}=\frac{(2-\gamma)(\gamma-5)}{(\gamma-\frac{1}{2})(\gamma+1)(\gamma+4)}<0,

and hence, the conclusion follows for

When

by Lemma
4.6
.

\displaystyle\sqrt{-k(\gamma,z_{M})V_{6}(z)}<C_{8}(z),\ \text{ equvalently }\ -k(\gamma,z_{M})V_{6}(z)-C_{8}^{2}(z)<0.

Since
-k(\gamma,z_{M})V_{6}(z_{M})-C_{8}^{2}(z_{M})=C_{6}(z_{M})^{2}-C_{8}(z_{M})^{2}=0

by (
2.11
) and (
4.15
), the proof will be complete upon

-k(\gamma,z_{M})V_{6}(z)-C_{8}^{2}(z)

z

z
(for any fixed
\gamma\in(1,\gamma_{\star})
),
and recalling
\frac{dC_{8}}{dz}<0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}\left(-k(\gamma,z_{M})V_{6}(z)-C_{8}^{2}(z)\right)=-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}C_{8}(z_{M})\frac{dV_{6}}{dz}-2C_{8}(z)\frac{dC_{8}}{dz}>C_{8}(z_{M})\left(-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\frac{dV_{6}}{dz}-2\frac{dC_{8}}{dz}\right).

The inner bracket is

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\frac{dV_{6}}{dz}-2\frac{dC_{8}}{dz}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}(\frac{\gamma-2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\gamma+2)-(\gamma-2)^{2}z}{w})-(\gamma-2-\frac{(\gamma+2)-(\gamma-2)^{2}z}{w})

\displaystyle=(2-\gamma)(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}+1)+\frac{(\gamma+2)-(\gamma-2)^{2}z}{w}(1-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}})>0

where we have used

and
\gamma_{\star}<2
to conclude the positivity. ∎

4.3
No connection to

for

In this subsection, we shall employ another

We define

From

\displaystyle\frac{C_{8}(z_{M})}{V_{8}(z_{M})}=-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}.

(4.20)

First we will show that the solution trajectory of (

Lemma 4.8
.

For any
\gamma\in[2,3]
and
z\in(0,z_{M}]
, the curve

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\cfrac{dC}{dV}=\cfrac{F(V,C;\gamma,z)}{G(V,C;\gamma,z)},\quad V\in[V_{1},-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}}C_{8}(z)),\\
&C(V_{1})=C_{1}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Next, we employ a barrier argument (
cf.
(
2.20

4.21

,

, and

\displaystyle\cfrac{F(V,-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}V;\gamma,z)}{G(V,-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}V;\gamma,z)}+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}>0.

By Lemma
3.10

for any

,

and

By (
1.18

1.17

\displaystyle F(V,-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}V;\gamma,z)+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}G(V,-\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}V;\gamma,z)=-m\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}V^{2}\Big[(-\gamma+\frac{1}{2})V+\frac{z\gamma V}{2(1+V)}+\frac{(\gamma z-1)(\gamma-1)}{2}-z\gamma\Big].

-m\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}V^{2}<0

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}(V,z):=(-\gamma+\frac{1}{2})V+\frac{z\gamma V}{2(1+V)}+\frac{(\gamma z-1)(\gamma-1)}{2}-z\gamma>0

for any

,

and

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}}C_{8}(z)<-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}}C_{8}(z_{M})=V_{8}(z_{M}).

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}(V_{8}(z_{M}),\gamma,z_{M})=\cfrac{F(V_{8}(z_{M}),C_{8}(z_{M}),\gamma,z_{M})+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}G(V_{8}(z_{M}),C_{8}(z_{M}),\gamma,z_{M})}{-m\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}V_{8}(z_{M})^{2}}=0.

In what follows, recalling the definitions (

(5.5)

(5.6)

We rewrite

\displaystyle\cfrac{d\log{C}}{dV}=\frac{1}{C}\frac{dC}{dV}=\cfrac{C^{2}f_{1}(V)-f_{2}(V)}{C^{2}g_{1}(V)-g_{2}(V)}.

For

at

(defined in an equivalent manner). A detailed discussion of

and

is given in
[

. However, for our analysis, we require an understanding of

only in the range

admits an explicit representation as

\displaystyle z_{g}=\begin{cases}\cfrac{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+(\gamma-1)^{2}}-\gamma}{\gamma(\gamma-1)}\quad&\text{ when }m=1,\\

(

(

To simplify our approach and avoid the complications associated with this implicit integral, we will derive

\gamma\in(1,2]

Lemma 5.1

\gamma\in(1,2]

\displaystyle\cfrac{d\log{C}}{dV}-\frac{f_{1}(V)}{g_{1}(V)}=\cfrac{C^{2}f_{1}(V)-f_{2}(V)}{C^{2}g_{1}(V)-g_{2}(V)}-\frac{f_{1}(V)}{g_{1}(V)}=-\frac{g_{1}(V)f_{2}(V)-f_{1}(V)g_{2}(V)}{[C^{2}g_{1}(V)-g_{2}(V)]g_{1}(V)}.

Our next step is to show that for any

Lemma 5.2

For any
\gamma\in(1,2]

(5.14)

We first evaluate

7
Solving to right

In the previous sections, for each
\gamma\in(1,3]
, we established
the existence of
z_{std}(\gamma;P_{*})
and a
range of
z
which
z_{std}(\gamma;P_{*})
must belong to.
This
z_{std}
allows the solution

of (

) to pass smoothly from

through
the
triple point
P_{*}
, where
P_{*}
is either
P_{6}
or
P_{8}
.
The remaining goal
is to extend this smooth solution from the triple point
P_{*}
to the origin
P_{0}
while ensuring that it remains within the second quadrant in the phase plane (that is, that we retain both
V<0
and
C>0
up to time the flow meets the origin). To prove this property for the solution associated to
z_{std}
, we will in fact prove the stronger property that the local solution to the right of the sonic
P_{*}
always extends to
P_{0}
within the second quadrant for all
z
within the range containing
z_{std}(\gamma;P_{*})
.

For notational convenience, we define unified notation for the various possible ranges of
z
containing
z_{std}
from the results of Sections

–
6
.

\displaystyle\mathring{\mathcal{Z}}(\gamma;P_{*})=\begin{cases}&(z_{g}(\gamma),z_{M}(\gamma)]\quad\text{for }\gamma\in(1,2]\text{ at }P_{6},\\
&(z_{1}(\gamma),z_{M}(\gamma)]\quad\text{for }\gamma\in(\gamma_{\star},\gamma_{1}]\text{ at }P_{8},\\
&(z_{2}(\gamma),z_{M}(\gamma)]\quad\text{for }\gamma\in(\gamma_{1},3]\text{ at }P_{8}.\end{cases}

(7.1)

In this section, we will show that for any
\gamma\in(1,3]
and

, the local analytic solutions around
P_{*}
constructed in Theorem

continue to the origin

in the second quadrant.

From the phase portrait analysis, three possibilities arise for the extension of the local analytic solution.

1.

The trajectory intersects the negative
V
-axis before reaching
V=0
.

2.

The trajectory intersects the positive
C
-axis when
V=0
.

3.

The trajectory converges to
P_{0}
within the second quadrant.

To rule out the first two possibilities, we will use suitable barrier functions
to establish an invariant region for the solutions ensuring convergence to
P_{0}
within the second quadrant. We begin with
the extension for the local analytic solution to the right of the triple point.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\gamma\in(1,3]
and
z\in\mathring{\mathcal{Z}}
be given and
let
P_{*}=(V_{*},C_{*})
be either
P_{6}
or
P_{8}
. Consider the local, analytic solution
C:[V_{*},V_{*}+\epsilon]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, guaranteed by Theorem
3.8
. This solution extends smoothly
to the right within the second quadrant onto the domain

C:[V_{*},V_{0})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
V_{0}=\min\{C^{-1}(0),0\}
. Furthermore, except at the triple point, the solution enjoys

\frac{dC}{dV}<0
,
F<0
,
G>0
,
D<0
.

Proof.

The result follows
from our choice of
c_{1}
(
cf.
(
3.18
)),
F_{C}(V_{*},C_{*})>0
(
cf.
(
3.7
)),
G_{C}(V_{*},C_{*})<0
(
cf.
(
3.5
)).
∎

Next, we will eliminate the first possibility.

Lemma 7.2
.

For any
\gamma\in(1,3]
and
z\in\mathring{\mathcal{Z}}
, the solution constructed in
Lemma
7.1

does not intersect the negative
V
-axis (i.e., in the notation of that Lemma,
V_{0}=0
).

Proof.

We argue by contradiction.
Suppose the solution intersects the negative
V
-axis before reaching
V=0
, and let
(\overline{V},0)
denote the point of intersection of the solution trajectory with the
V
-axis.
Consider the initial value problem:

\begin{cases}&\cfrac{dC(V)}{dV}=\cfrac{F(V,C;\gamma,z)}{G(V,C;\gamma,z)},\\
&C(\overline{V})=0.\end{cases}

In a small rectangular region around
(\overline{V},0)
, it is evident that
\frac{F(V,C;\gamma,z)}{G(V,C;\gamma,z)}
is continuously differentiable. By
the standard theorem for existence and uniqueness of solutions to ODEs with locally Lipschitz right hand side (e.g.
[
44
]
), this initial value problem possesses a unique solution on the interval
(\overline{V}-\epsilon,\overline{V}+\epsilon)
for sufficiently small

we see trivially that

solves this problem, and so must be the unique solution, leading to a contradiction.
∎

We remark that Lemma
7.2

yields
V_{0}=0
in Lemma

.
The rest of this section is devoted to ruling out the second possibility by the barrier argument.

7.1
Connecting

to the origin

In this subsection, we prove that the local analytic solutions around

Lemma 7.3
.

For any
\gamma\in(1,2]
and

7.1

Proof.

According to (

for any

C_{6}<B_{1}(V_{6})

7.1
,
\frac{dC}{dV}<0
holds for

. Observing that
-C_{6}^{2}>V_{6}

C(V)<B_{1}(V)

Thus, employing the barrier argument (
2.20
), we verify the validity of the following inequality for any
\gamma\in(1,2]
,

\displaystyle\frac{F(V,\sqrt{-V};\gamma,z)}{G(V,\sqrt{-V};\gamma,z)}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{-V}}<0.

\mathfrak{B}_{1}(V,z,m)=\frac{2F(V,\sqrt{-V};\gamma,z)}{\sqrt{-V}}+\frac{G(V,\sqrt{-V};\gamma,z)}{-V}<0,

for
m=1,2
,
\gamma\in(1,2]
,

,
V\in(-C_{6}^{2},0)
,
where we recall from (

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{1}(V,z,m)

\displaystyle=(m-1-m\gamma)V^{2}+(-3+2m-m\gamma+m(\gamma-2)\gamma z)V-m\gamma z-1+\frac{2mz}{1+V}.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathfrak{B}_{1}(V,z,m)}{\partial V}

\displaystyle=(m-1-m\gamma)(1+2V)+m-2+m(\gamma-2)\gamma z-\frac{2mz}{(1+V)^{2}}

for

For any fixed
\gamma\in(1,2]
, we write

By

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathfrak{B}_{1}(-C^{2}_{6}(z),z,m)}{dz}=\frac{\partial\mathfrak{B}_{1}}{\partial V}(-C^{2}_{6}(z),z,m)(-2C_{6}(z)C_{6}^{\prime}(z))+\frac{\partial\mathfrak{B}_{1}}{\partial z}(-C^{2}_{6}(z),z,m)>0,

where we also used
\frac{\partial\mathfrak{B}_{1}}{\partial z}(-C^{2}_{6}(z),z,m)=-m(\gamma-2)\gamma C^{2}_{6}(z)+m(\frac{2}{1-2C^{2}_{6}(z)}-\gamma)>0

By (

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{1}(-C^{2}_{6}(\gamma,z_{M}),z_{M},m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{1}(-C^{2}_{6}(\gamma,z_{M}),z_{M},1)

\displaystyle 4\gamma^{2}z^{2}_{M}-2\gamma z_{M}+2\gamma^{2}z_{M}-\gamma^{3}z^{3}_{M}-2\gamma^{3}z^{2}_{M}-1+4z_{M}

\displaystyle\frac{F(V,\sqrt{-V};\gamma,z)}{G(V,\sqrt{-V};\gamma,z)}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{-V}}<0.
