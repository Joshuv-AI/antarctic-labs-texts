Expansive Multisets: Asymptotic Enumeration
Konstantinos Panagiotou, Leon Ramzews

Abstract
Consider a non-negative sequence c n = h ⁡ ( n ) ⋅ n α − 1 ⋅ ρ − n c_{n}=h(n)\cdot n^{\alpha-1}\cdot\rho^{-n} , where h h is slowly varying, α > 0 \alpha>0 , 0 < ρ < 1 0<\rho<1 and n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} . We investigate the coefficients of G ⁡ ( x , y ) = ∏ k ≥ 1 ( 1 − x k ​ y ) − c k G(x,y)=\prod_{k\geq 1}(1-x^{k}y)^{-c_{k}} , which is the bivariate generating series of the multiset construction of combinatorial objects. By a powerful blend of probabilistic methods based on the Boltzmann model and analytic techniques exploiting the well-known saddle-point method we determine the number of multisets of total size n n with N N components, that is, the coefficient of x n ​ y N x^{n}y^{N} in G ⁡ ( x , y ) G(x,y) , asymptotically as n → ∞ n\to\infty and for all ranges of N N . Our results reveal a phase transition in the structure of the counting formula that depends on the ratio n / N n/N and that demonstrates a prototypical passage from a bivariate local limit to an univariate one.

Introduction and Main Results

Multisets

The focus of this work is on a simple, yet powerful and ubiquitous construction. Given a set
{\mathcal{C}}
,
we define the class
{\mathcal{G}}=\text{{Mset}}({\mathcal{C}})
of
{\mathcal{C}}
-multisets
or
partitions weighted by
{\mathcal{C}}
to be the collection of all elements of the form

\big\{(C_{1},d_{1}),\dots,(C_{k},d_{k})\big\},\quad C_{i}\in{\mathcal{C}}\text{ pairwise distinct, }\penalty\ d_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\penalty\ \penalty\ 1\leq i\leq k\in\mathbb{N}.

The tuple
(C,d)
(informally) means that the object
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
occurs
d
times in the multiset. The asymptotic study – enumeration, properties, limit laws – of multisets has a long and rich history. One of the very first and monumental results in this context was achieved by
Hardy and Ramanujan 1918
, who studied number partitions, which are nothing else than multisets of natural numbers, i.e.,
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{N}
in our notation. They discovered the beautiful asymptotic formula

|{\cal P}_{n}|\sim\frac{1}{4\sqrt{3}n}\exp\left\{\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}n}\right\},\quad\textrm{as }n\to\infty,

where
{\cal P}_{n}
contains all number partitions of
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In their paper they made the simple, though substantial, observation that a very general class of enumeration problems can be solved by applying Cauchy’s formula
and performing an appropriate analysis of the resulting complex integral.
This powerful idea, which is nowadays known as the
saddle-point method
or
method of steepest descent
and which is (much) older than
[
23
]
, has seen tremendous extensions, has been rediscovered numerous times, and has seen widespread applications in various fields inside and outside of mathematics, particularly in physics.
The field is far too broad so that we could review it here; we refer to the classical textbook
[
11
]
, the modern exposition
[
34
]
, and to the excellent treatments in
[
15
,
41
]
, which also put a particular emphasis on the combinatorial perspective.

Another predominant idea in this area is to reformulate the counting problem into a probabilistic question, so as to profit from probability theory’s huge toolbox. A central result was established by
Arratia and Tavaré 1994
, who showed that many random combinatorial objects possess a component structure whose joint distribution is equal to that of random variables that are conditioned to have a specific (weighted) sum and are otherwise
independent
. This, of course, rings the bell of every probabilist and calls for local limit theorems.
Moreover, it lies at the core of
Khinchin’s probabilistic method
that originated in
[
28
]
, see
[
16
]
for some historical perspective, and that makes the relation of counting and probability a general and guiding principle.

In this paper we are concerned with the asymptotic enumeration of multisets, and the two fundamentally different methods/principles will play an important role in what is to come.
To be precise, assume for the moment that
{\mathcal{C}}
is a combinatorial class, that is, it is equipped with a size function
\lvert\cdot\rvert:{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}:=\{C\in{\mathcal{C}}:\lvert C\rvert=n\}
contains only finitely many objects for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we associate to elements in
{\mathcal{G}}=\text{{Mset}}({\mathcal{C}})
in a natural way a (total) size and a number of components, that is,

\lvert G\rvert:=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}d_{i}\lvert C_{i}\rvert,\quad\kappa(G):=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}d_{i},\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad G=\big\{(C_{1},d_{1}),\dots,(C_{k},d_{k})\big\}\in{\mathcal{G}}.

We further set

{\mathcal{G}}_{n}:=\{G\in{\mathcal{G}}:\lvert G\rvert=n\}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N}:=\{G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{n}:\kappa(G)=N\},\qquad n,N\in\mathbb{N}.

We write
C(x):=\sum_{k\geq 1}\lvert{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\rvert x^{k}
for the generating series of
{\mathcal{C}}
. Then it is well-known, see
[
15
,
32
]
, that the bivariate generating series for the class
{\mathcal{G}}
fulfills the fundamental relations

G(x,y):=\sum_{k,\ell\geq 1}\lvert{\mathcal{G}}_{k,\ell}\rvert x^{k}y^{\ell}=\prod_{k\geq 1}(1-x^{k}y)^{-c_{k}}=\mathrm{exp}\bigg\{\sum_{j\geq 1}C(x^{j})y^{j}/j\bigg\}.

For a generating series
F(x)
we will write
[x^{n}]F(x)
for the coefficient of
x^{n}
in
F(x)
, and for a bivariate series
F(x,y)
we will denote by
[x^{n}y^{N}]F(x,y)
the coefficient of
x^{n}y^{N}
in
F(x,y)
.
As already mentioned, if
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{N}
, then we obtain the class of number partitions
{\mathcal{P}}=\text{{Mset}}(\mathbb{N})
and
\lvert{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\rvert=[x^{n}]G(x,1)
, which is the starting point for the analytical methods employed in
[
23
]
.
Naturally, the determination of
g_{n}:=[x^{n}]G(x,1)
for general
C(x)
, respectively
(|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}|)_{n\geq 1}
, has become a very active research topic. From today’s viewpoint, the asymptotic value of
g_{n}
is very well-understood and was determined under various general conditions and for several specific classes. A particularly prominent and broad setting, and the one considered here, is when

\displaystyle c_{n}:=|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}|=h(n)\cdot n^{\alpha-1}\cdot\rho^{-n},\quad\penalty\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R},\penalty\ 0<\rho\leq 1,

(1.1)

where
h:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is an eventually positive, continuous and slowly varying, which means that

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{h(\lambda x)}{h(x)}=1\penalty\ \text{ for all }\penalty\ \lambda>0.

As it turns out, depending on the value of
\alpha
the models have fairly different features. The state-of-the-art results for multiset enumeration, i.e., the (asymptotic) determination of
g_{n}=[x^{n}]G(x,1)
in the so-called
expansive
case
\alpha>0
are by
Granovsky and Stark 2006
, whereas the
logarithmic
case
\alpha=0
is treated in the excellent book by
Arratia et al. 2003
and the
convergent
case
\alpha<0
is studied in
Stufler 2020
. There are many other treatises that also highlight several other aspects, like the typical structure of multisets, and that provide explicit formulas
[
21
,
36
,
35
,
22
,
16
,
3
,
5
,
33
]
, the methods ranging from purely analytic to probabilistic as previously described.

Our Contribution

While the enumeration problem is fairly well-studied, the fundamental question about the distribution of the number of components in a (uniformly random) multiset, and in particular the asymptotic determination of
g_{n,N}:=|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N}|
, is far less understood and actually much more complex.
Indeed, both the saddle-point method and the probabilistic method hit a barrier.
In the saddle point method, we are faced with an integral over
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and obtaining control over the integration contour is – as the orders of magnitude of
n
and
N
may be vastly incompatible – extremely challenging from a technical viewpoint; in the probabilistic method, on the other hand, we have to deal with bivariate local limit theorems for random variables with a specific dependency structure.
Although there are many general and notable results that address the multivariate setting in both the analytic and probabilistic settings, see for example the extensive treatment in
[
41
]
, they are not sufficient for the desired level of generality considered here.

In this paper we study the expansive case
\alpha>0
in (
1.1
) and we demonstrate that a
combination
of both methods is very effective for determining
g_{n,N}
(and not only …) in all relevant cases, that is, when
n\to\infty
and
N
is not too close to one or to the maximum possible number of components. Indeed, we first set up an appropriate probabilistic framework, which differs substantially from the “classical” aforementioned ones used in enumeration of multisets; to wit, the previous approaches, that are directly or indirectly based on
[
28
]
, use a description with negative binomials, while our approach, which is inspired by modern Boltzmann sampling techniques in combinatorics
[
7
]
, makes use of Poisson variables. A thorough discussion and comparison is made in Section
1.2
. Having achieved this, we use probabilistic methods to reduce the determination of
g_{n,N}
to an one dimensional problem, which then, in turn, is tackled with the saddle-point method. At this point it seems unavoidable to resort to probabilistic methods only, as the problem involves a genuine triangle array of random variables for which we need a local limit theorem.

Before we build up the theory and present the results in the following sections we review some previous work that addresses the determination of
g_{n,N}
in specific settings.
In the context of number partitions
{\cal P}=\textsc{Mset}(\mathbb{N})
the picture is quite clear.
In
[
29
]
the asymptotic order of
|{\cal P}_{n,N}|
for
n,N,n-N\to\infty
is determined and a phase transition, depending on whether
N
is
\mathcal{O}(n^{1/2})
or
\omega(n^{1/2})
, in the structure of the counting sequence is observed.
For
N\geq d\sqrt{n}\ln n
and
d>d_{0}
for some
d_{0}>0
it is even true that
g_{n,N}\sim|{\cal P}|_{n-N}
, as shown in
[
24
]
. Another setting in which the picture is fully described is the subexponential case in (
1.1
), that is, when
\alpha<0
. For example, if
c_{1}=1
, the authors showed that there is an
A>0
such that
g_{n,N}\sim Ac_{n-N}
for all
n,N,n-N\to\infty
, that is, the number of
N
-component
{\mathcal{C}}
-multisets is proportional to
c_{n-N}
for all ranges of
n-N
. The result was shown with the help of a probabilistic method, but as the result itself suggests, this bivariate problem is actually and in essence a univariate one.
Finally, in the expansive case
{\mathcal{G}}=\textsc{Mset}({\mathcal{C}})
, under the stronger assumptions
c_{n}\sim Cn^{\alpha-1}
for
C>0
,
\alpha>0
and additional analytical assumptions,
g_{n,N}
was determined asymptotically for
N=\omega(\ln^{3}n)
and
N=o(n^{\alpha/(\alpha+1)})
in
Stark 2021
. Actually, in that paper the author accomplishes the herculean task of performing a bivariate saddle-point integration, though only for a limited range of the parameters. In any case, the results of
[
42
]
give reason to conjecture that
g_{n,N}
, as in the case of number partitions, undergoes a phase transition depending on the ratio of
N
and
n^{\alpha/(\alpha+1)}
in this general setting as well. We will confirm in this paper that such transitions are prototypical for the considered counting problems.

1.1
Main Results

Let us fix the setting that we consider. Let
h:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be an eventually positive, continuous and slowly varying function.
Consider the real-valued sequence
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
given by

\displaystyle c_{n}=h(n)\cdot n^{\alpha-1}\cdot\rho^{-n}\quad\text{where }\alpha>0,0<\rho<1\text{ and }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(1.2)

Note that there are two differences to (
1.1
). First, the sequence here does not have to be a counting sequence, since the
c_{n}
’s may not be integral. Second, we assume
\rho<1
and exclude the single case
\rho=1
, as our methods necessitate this.
Following
[
20
]
, we call sequences
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
fulfilling (
1.2
)
expansive
. We also say that the corresponding generating series
C(x):=\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}x^{k}
is expansive.

Further, define
m=m(C)\in\mathbb{N}
to be the smallest integer such that
c_{m}\neq 0
, that is,
c_{1}=\cdots=c_{m-1}=0
and
c_{m}>0
. Recall/consider the power series

\displaystyle G(x,y):=\exp\left\{\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{C(x^{j})}{j}y^{j}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad G^{\geq 2}(x,y):=\exp\left\{\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{C(x^{j})}{j}y^{j}\right\}.

(1.3)

Note that since we defined
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to be real-valued the series
G(x,y)
has a priori no combinatorial meaning. However, if
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the counting sequence of a combinatorial class
{\mathcal{C}}
, then, as we saw previously,
G(x,y)
is the generating series of
{\mathcal{G}}=\text{{Mset}}({\mathcal{C}})
, where
x
tags the size and
y
the number of components.
Nonetheless, we study
G(x,y)
as a series having possibly non-integral coefficients for the rest of this work unless stated otherwise.
The series
G^{\geq 2}(x,y)
is an auxiliary object that we will need later.

As already mentioned, the quantity
g_{n}=[x^{n}]G(x,1)
is a well-researched object. The authors of
[
20
]
investigated, among other cases,
g_{n}
in the expansive case.
We present the following theorem that is a straightforward consequence from the results and their proofs in
[
20
, Thm. 1 and Cor. 1]
. However, as we use a different notation and the connection to
[
20
]
is not immediately obvious, we will give a short self-contained two-page proof that also demonstrates our methodology quite well in Section
4.10
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
C(x)
is expansive as in (
1.2
). Let
z_{n}
be the unique solution to
z_{n}C^{\prime}(z_{n})=n
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

z_{n}\sim\rho\quad\textrm{and}\quad[x^{n}]G(x,1)\sim G^{\geq 2}(\rho,1)\cdot\frac{\exp\left\{C(z_{n})\right\}}{\sqrt{2\pi z_{n}^{2}C^{\prime\prime}(z_{n})}}\cdot z_{n}^{-n}.

(LLT)

The form of the enumeration result (
LLT
) is prototypical: there is a saddle-point (
z_{n}
), an exponential term (
z_{n}^{-n}
), a term in which the power series is evaluated (
G^{\geq 2}(\rho,1)\exp{C(z_{n})}\sim G(z_{n},1)
), and a polynomial term
(2\pi z_{n}^{2}C^{\prime\prime}(z_{n}))^{-1/2}
. The latter is the result of an appropriate integration around the saddle point, or, in a terminology that we prefer here, the result of a local limit theorem, i.e., the probability that a sum of specific independent and identically distributed random variables equals its mean. We will see much more of that later. Let us remark, however, that in the generality considered here, we cannot expect to be able to say much more than (
LLT
): in general, it is not possible to derive a more explicit asymptotic expression for
\exp\{C(z_{n})\}
(though it is possible to do so for
C(z_{n})
and for its derivatives), and the actual order of magnitude depends very much on the micro-structure of
h
.

We now move on the main mission of this work, namely the study of
g_{n,N}=[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)
. We need some preparations. For
(n,N)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
consider the system of equations in the variables
x,y

\displaystyle xyC^{\prime}(x)+mc_{m}\frac{x^{m}y}{1-x^{m}y}=n,\kern 5.0ptyC(x)+c_{m}\frac{x^{m}y}{1-x^{m}y}=N,\kern 5.0ptx,y>0\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptx^{m}y<1.

(1.4)

We will explain later in detail where these equations come from and only tease for now that the left-hand sides are (more or less) the expected value of the size and the number of components, which we “tune” to
n
and
N
, respectively, in a specifically designed random multiset. Equations (
1.4
) are – in some sense – the bivariate equivalent of the saddle-point equation in Theorem
1.1
.
Further, for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
consider the equation in the single variable
u

\displaystyle u\cdot h(u)^{1/(\alpha+1)}=v^{1/(\alpha+1)}\quad\text{and}\quad 1\leq u\leq v.

(1.5)

Our first auxiliary result is that the previous systems of equations have unique solutions.

Lemma 1.2
.

The following statements are true.

(i)

Suppose that
C(x)
is expansive. For
n,N
and
n-mN
sufficiently large there is a unique solution
(x_{n,N},y_{n,N})
to (
1.4
).

(ii)

For
v
sufficiently large there is a unique solution
u_{v}
to (
1.5
) given by
u_{v}=v^{1/(\alpha+1)}/g(v)
, where
g:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is slowly varying.

With the slowly varying function
g
from Lemma
1.2
(ii)
at hand, define the “magic” value

\displaystyle N_{n}^{*}:=C_{0}\cdot g(n)\cdot n^{\alpha/(\alpha+1)},\quad\text{where}\quad C_{0}:=\alpha^{-1}{(\rho^{-m}\Gamma(\alpha+1))^{{1}/(\alpha+1)}}.

(1.6)

As we will see shortly, the quantity
N^{*}_{n}
marks a phase transition in the structure of the counting sequence
[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)
(and much more …) dependent on whether
N/N^{*}_{n}<1
or
N/N^{*}_{n}>1
.
More precisely, assume that we are given a real-valued positive sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle N_{n}=\lambda_{n}N^{*}_{n}\in\mathbb{N},\quad N_{n}\to\infty\quad\text{and}\quad n-mN_{n}\to\infty.

(1.7)

Note that by these definitions
N_{n}
becomes a function of
n
and the solutions
({x}_{n},y_{n})\equiv(x_{n,N_{n}},y_{n,N_{n}})
to (
1.4
) and
N^{*}_{n}
from (
1.6
) are all well defined for sufficiently large
n
due to Lemma
1.2
. We will from now on distinguish the two cases

(I):\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}<1\qquad\text{and}\qquad(II):\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}>1.

Our first main result determines the asymptotic growth (in several equivalent forms that have their own merits) of
[x^{n}y^{N_{n}}]G(x,y)
as
n\to\infty
in case
(I)
.

Theorem 1(I)
.

Suppose that
C(x)
is expansive. In case
(I)
, that is, if
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}<1
,

\displaystyle[x^{n}y^{N_{n}}]G(x,y)

\displaystyle\sim G^{\geq 2}(\rho,y_{n})\cdot\frac{\exp\left\{y_{n}C({x}_{n})\right\}}{2\pi\sqrt{N_{n}y_{n}{x}_{n}^{2}C^{\prime\prime}({x}_{n})/(\alpha+1)}}\cdot{x}_{n}^{-n}y_{n}^{-N_{n}}

(LLT-I)

\displaystyle\sim G^{\geq 2}(\rho,y_{n})\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{2\pi}\cdot\exp\bigg\{-\frac{c_{m}\rho^{m}y_{n}}{1-\rho^{m}y_{n}}\bigg\}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot{x}_{n}^{-n}(y_{n}/\mathrm{e})^{-N_{n}}

(Explicit-I)

\displaystyle\sim G^{\geq 2}(\rho,y_{n})\cdot\frac{1}{N_{n}!}[x^{n}]C(x)^{N_{n}}.

(Comb-I)

Some remarks are in place. First, (
LLT-I
) is the prototypical form of the result that very much resembles (
LLT
) in a bivariate setting.
The second form (
Explicit-I
) is handy and most convenient to work with, as it includes no evaluation of derivatives and – crucially – powers of
C
at the saddle point. We feel lucky that we were able to derive such a form of the sequence, and this is mostly owed to the structure of the Equations (
1.4
) that have a very special property in case
(I)
(just to look ahead a bit, in that case
x^{m}y
stays bounded away from one, so that
1/(1-x^{m}y)
remains bounded).
The last identity (
Comb-I
) hints at an interesting fact when
G(x,y)
is viewed in the combinatorial setting, i.e., as the generating series of multisets of a class
\cal C
.
Indeed,
C(x)^{N}
is the generating series for
sequences

(C_{1},\dots,C_{N})
that are composed of
N
objects from
\cal C
.
Then
C(x)^{N}/N!
enumerates
sets

\{C_{1},\dots,C_{N}\}
,
provided that all elements are distinct
; otherwise there is no reasonable interpretation.
So, (
Comb-I
) may let us speculate that a typical
\cal C
-multiset in
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N_{n}}
has only distinct components, and moreover, that we can accurately describe a typical/random element in
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N_{n}}
by a sequence of
N_{n}
objects from
\cal C
that are conditioned to have total size
n
and are otherwise independent.
All this and actually much more is somehow true as we show in the companion paper
[
38
]
, where we study limit laws of random multisets in
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N_{n}}
.

Let us finish the discussion about Theorem
1(I)
with the following remark.
If
\lambda:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}\in[0,1)
exists, then, as we shall see in Lemma
4.1
below,
\lim_{n\to\infty}y_{n}=\rho^{-m}\lambda^{\alpha+1}
. Consequently, by writing
d(\lambda)=G^{\geq 2}(\rho,\rho^{-m}\lambda^{\alpha+1})
, the counting sequence has the simple(-r) form

[x^{n}y^{N_{n}}]G(x,y)\sim d(\lambda)\cdot\frac{1}{N_{n}!}[x^{n}]C(x)^{N_{n}}\sim d(\lambda)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{2\pi}\cdot\exp\bigg\{-\frac{c_{m}\lambda^{\alpha+1}}{1-\lambda^{\alpha+1}}\bigg\}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot{x}_{n}^{-n}(y_{n}/\mathrm{e})^{-N_{n}}.

In order to treat
g_{n,N}
in the second case
(II)
we define

G_{>m}^{\geq 2}(x,y):=\exp\left\{\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{C(x^{j})-c_{m}x^{jm}}{jx^{jm}}y^{j}\right\}.

Let us give a quick explanation for the choice of notation. Consider the class
{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}
of all elements in
{\mathcal{C}}
of size greater than
m
together with the modified size function
\lvert C\rvert_{>m}:=\lvert C\rvert-m>0
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}
. We obtain that the generating series of
{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}
is given by
C_{>m}(x)=(C(x)-c_{m}x^{m})/x^{m}
. Then the generating series of
{\mathcal{G}}_{>m}:=\text{{Mset}}({\mathcal{C}}_{>m})
is given by
G_{>m}(x)=\textrm{exp}\{\sum_{j\geq 1}C_{>m}(x^{j})/j\}
. The superscript in
G_{>m}^{\geq 2}
accounts for the fact that we only sum up starting at
j=2
.

Theorem 1(II)
.

Suppose that
C(x)
is expansive. In case
(II)
, that is, if
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}>1
, there is a non-negative sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
given by

a_{n}:=\lambda_{n}^{-1}\cdot\frac{g(n-mN_{n})}{g(n)}\cdot\left(\frac{n-mN_{n}}{n}\right)^{\alpha/(\alpha+1)}

such that

\displaystyle[x^{n}y^{N_{n}}]G(x,y)

\displaystyle\sim G_{>m}^{\geq 2}(\rho)\cdot\frac{\exp\left\{C_{>m}({x}_{n})\right\}}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho^{-m}{x}_{n}^{2}C^{\prime\prime}({x}_{n})}}\cdot{x}_{n}^{-(n-mN_{n})}

(LLT-II)

\displaystyle\sim G_{>m}^{\geq 2}(\rho)\cdot\frac{\big((1-a_{n})N_{n}\big)^{c_{m}-1}}{\Gamma(c_{m})}\cdot[x^{n-mN_{n}}]\mathrm{e}^{C_{>m}(x)},\quad n\to\infty.

(Comb-II)

We have again remarks.
First, (
LLT-II
) is the classical form that looks like (
LLT-I
) and (
LLT
).
Note that, however, (
LLT-II
) looks much more like (
LLT
) in the sense that it resembles a
uni
variate local limit theorem –
y_{n}
does not appear in the formulation at all!
This simplification is quite surprising, as we would expect a bivariate law like in Theorem
1(I)
.
Here the alternative form (
Comb-II
) comes to help and gives – as before – a hint about what may be going on.
When
G(x,y)
is the generating series of
{\mathcal{C}}
-multisets, then
[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)
is the number of such multisets with
N
components and size
n
. But the factor

((1-a_{n})N)^{c_{m}-1}/\Gamma(c_{m})\sim\binom{(1-a_{n})N+c_{m}-1}{c_{m}-1}

in (
Comb-II
) counts the number of ways to create a multiset with
\sim(1-a_{n})N
objects from
{\mathcal{C}}_{m}
. Where are the remaining
a_{n}N
components? For the other term note that
\exp\left\{C_{>m}(x)\right\}=\sum_{k\geq 0}C_{>m}(x)^{k}/k!
.
Then, as before,
C_{>m}(x)^{k}
is the generating series of sequences of
{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}
-objects of length
k
and, provided all elements in the sequence are distinct,
C_{>m}(x)^{k}/k!
counts sets of
k
objects.
Then
[x^{n-mN}]\exp\left\{C_{>m}(x)\right\}
enumerates
sets
of distinct
{\mathcal{C}}_{>m}
-objects with a varying number of components, which, however, concentrates around
a_{n}N
, and thus with (actual) size
\sim n-mN_{n}(1-a_{n})
.
Concluding, we may speculate that a typical
{\mathcal{C}}
-multiset in
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,N}
contains a random set of
N^{\prime}
(that typically is
\sim a_{n}N
) components with size
>m
, and the remaining
N-N^{\prime}
components are of size
m
. So, since there is no restriction for the component count for objects of size
>m
, we arrive at a univariate limit law as in (
LLT-II
). Finally, let us note that in case
(II)
an explicit form as in (
Explicit-I
) is in general out of reach due to the reasons outlined after Theorem
1.1
– although it is possible to establish the first order of
C_{>m}(x_{n})
(and its derivatives), achieving a similar statement for
\exp\left\{C_{>m}(x_{n})\right\}
seems intractable in the general setting considered here.

Let us finish the discussion about Theorem
1(II)
with the following remark. As we will see in Lemma
4.2
below, if the limit
\lambda:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}
exists, then
a_{n}\sim\lambda^{-1}
so that defining
d(\lambda):=G_{>m}^{\geq 2}(\rho)(1-\lambda^{-1})^{c_{m}-1}
, Theorem
1(II)
yields the slightly simpler form

[x^{n}y^{N_{n}}]G(x,y)\sim d(\lambda)\cdot\frac{N^{c_{m}-1}}{\Gamma(c_{m})}\cdot[x^{n-mN_{n}}]\mathrm{e}^{C_{>m}(x)}.

Let us close this section with a final remark. We, unfortunately, cannot offer a Theorem
(I\textrm{\textonehalf})
that describes what happens when
\lambda_{n}\to 1
. The point is that the answer actually depends on how this limit is approached and at what speed. In this context we leave it as an open problem to describe the scaling window and the exact behavior in- and outside of it.

1.2
Notes on the Proof & Perspective

Let us consider the combinatorial setting in which
C(x)
is the generating series of a combinatorial class
{\mathcal{C}}
and
G(x,y)
is the (bivariate) generating series of
{\mathcal{G}}
, the class of all
{\mathcal{C}}
-multisets.
The main idea of the proof is to translate the task of determining
g_{n,N}=[x^{n}y^{N}]G(x,y)
into a probabilistic problem by considering a multiset
\mathsf{G}_{n}
drawn uniformly at random from
{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
.
Let us first discuss the quantity
g_{n}:=[x^{n}]G(x,1)
– the
total
number of multisets of size
n
with no restrictions on the number of components – for which this kind of analysis has already been carried out successfully in several cases. Assume that we have at our disposal a randomized algorithm/stochastic process
\mathsf{G}
that outputs elements from
{\mathcal{G}}
with
a priori
no control on the size or the number of components, but with the guarantee that all objects of the same size are equiprobable. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{g_{n}}=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathsf{G}_{n}=G\right]=\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathsf{G}=G\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert=n\right]}\quad\text{for any }G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{n}.

(1.8)

The goal is to find an algorithm such that we can determine/estimate the terms in the latter expression, namely
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathsf{G}=G\right]
(that is usually easy, by design of the algorithm) and
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert=n\right]
(the hard one). As it turns out, such algorithms exist and two different approaches stand out in the literature.

The first and classical approach, based on Khinchin’s probabilistic method and also referred to as
conditioning relation
, generates
\mathsf{G}
by (first) sampling for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
independently a random number
X_{k}
of components of size
k
; thus
|\mathsf{G}|=\sum_{k\geq 1}kX_{k}
. The “right” choice for
X_{k}
is a negative binomial distribution with parameters
(c_{k},{x}_{n}^{k})
for a control value
{x}_{n}
.
This is related to the alternative (and equivalent) representation of the generating function (
1.3
) for
\cal C
-multisets given by

G(x,y)=\prod_{k\geq 1}(1-yx^{k})^{-c_{k}}

(1.9)

for
y=1
, see
[
15
,
32
]
. Then, by “tuning”
{x}_{n}
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert\right]=n
a local limit theorem for
\mathrm{Pr}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert=n\right]
can be shown to be true in certain cases.
For example, if
c_{n}=\Theta(n^{\alpha-1}\rho^{-n})
with
\alpha>0,0<\rho\leq 1
the authors of
[
20
]
proceed as described to determine
g_{n}
asymptotically.
This tuning procedure is in general a feasible method for expansive multisets, as
\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert\right]
is proportional to
C^{\prime}({x}_{n})/C({x}_{n})
and
\lim_{{x}_{n}\to\rho}C^{\prime}({x}_{n})=\infty
for
\alpha>0
; in other cases, in particular when
\alpha<0
, tuning is not possible.
The conditioning relation is also used by other authors for solving various related problems, see for example the aforementioned works
[
21
,
35
,
22
,
3
,
2
,
17
]
. Some historical remarks about Khinchin’s probabilistic method
[
28
]
are made in
[
16
]
.

On the other hand, and this is the method that we choose and develop further in this work, the
Pólya-Boltzmann model

[
8
]
is used to find a decomposition of
\mathsf{G}
into random
{\mathcal{C}}
-objects attached to cycles of a random permutation, which is helpful to get rid of cumbersome appearances of symmetries and that gives rise to Poisson distributions instead of negative binomials; this difference is reflected by the two different representations (
1.3
) and (
1.9
).
Again we obtain independent random variables tuned by a control parameter
{x}_{n}
that can be used to describe
\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert
in a better-to-handle manner.
For a precise description of the involved random variables have a peek at Section
3
, where we present (a
bi
variate extension of) the framework in full detail.
The crucial property of the corresponding variables is that all objects attached to cycles of length
j\geq 2
in the permutation are drawn according to a probability distribution with mean proportional to
C({x}_{n}^{j})
. Since
0<{x}_{n}\leq\rho
we infer that
C({x}_{n}^{j})
is decreasing exponentially fast in
j
for
0<\rho<1
.
Hence, objects associated to fixpoints, that is, for
j=1
, are dominant in the structure of
\mathsf{G}
; this essentially reduces the analysis to the study of iid random variables, rendering this method particularly useful whenever
0<\rho<1
. For example,
[
43
]
determines (among other more general things)
g_{n}
with this technique in the prominent setting where
c_{n}
is subexponential, which corresponds to our setting with
\alpha<0
.
For other applications of the Pólya-Boltzmann model regarding random multisets, see for instance
[
40
,
39
]
.

Let us return to our actual problem, where we want to find an asymptotic expression for
g_{n,N}
as
n\to\infty
and for essentially all
N\to\infty
. As in (
1.8
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{g_{n,N}}{g_{n}}=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\kappa(\mathsf{G}_{n})=N\right]=\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert=n,\kappa(\mathsf{G})=N\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert=n\right]}.

(1.10)

Here we see a major difficulty that suddenly may appear: the one-parametric models have the property
\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert\right]=n
that we obtained by tuning
{x}_{n}
, but at the same time
\mathbb{\kappa(\mathsf{G})}
can possibly be far away from
N
.
This, however, is not always a problem, and the one-parameteric models can also be useful, as demonstrated in
[
37
]
in the aforementioned subexponential case.
On the other hand, in the expansive case a two-parametric description of
\mathsf{G}
must be found in order to tune the expectation of
\kappa(\mathsf{G})
to be (close to)
N
. Then the problem of determining
g_{n,N}
boils down to finding a two-dimensional local limit law for
K_{n,N}:=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert=n,\kappa(\mathsf{G})=N\right]
. Achieving this is a difficult problem, since there is a significant interplay between
\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert
and
\kappa(\mathsf{G})
. The author of
[
42
]
solved this problem under the assumption that
c_{n}\sim Cn^{\alpha-1}
for
C,\alpha>0
and the restrictions
N=o(n^{\alpha/(\alpha+1)})
and
N=\omega(\ln^{3}n)
by using the conditioning relation and a bivariate saddle-point integration.
Other parameter ranges have not been studied as far as we are aware of.

In this work we consider the general setting (
1.2
). To get a grip on
K_{n,N}
we conduct a novel application of a bivariate Boltzmann model with the parameters
{x}_{n,N}
and
y_{n,N}
solving (
1.4
). This solution asserts (more or less) that
\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert\right]=n
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\mathsf{G})\right]=N
. As the two parameters interact in a complex way such that depending on the ratio of
n
and
N
the main contribution to
\lvert\mathsf{G}\rvert
or
\kappa(\mathsf{G})
is not necessarily given only by the fixpoints anymore, we come to the conclusion that there is a phase transition in
N
at which the behaviour of
g_{n,N}
changes significantly. This can be recognized in the different natures of Theorems
1(I)
and
1(II)
.

Plan of the Paper

The preparations for the proofs of the main theorems are contained in Section

2.3
Estimates for Power Series

In the proofs of our main results we will often find ourselves in the situation where we have to retrieve coefficients of the product of two power series. In this basic setting the coefficient of the product is proportional to the coefficient of the series with the smaller radius of convergence as shown in the next classical lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
9
, Thm. 3.42]

Let
A(x),R(x)
be power series with radii of convergence
\rho_{A},\rho_{R}>0
. Suppose that
[x^{n-1}]A(x)/[x^{n}]A(x)\sim\rho_{A}^{-1}
. Moreover, assume that
\rho_{R}>\rho_{A}
and
R(\rho_{A})\neq 0
. Then

[x^{n}]A(x)R(x)\sim R(\rho_{A})\cdot[x^{n}]A(x),\quad n\to\infty.

In our forthcoming arguments the involved series
A,R
will also depend on
n
; there we will use the following (rather technical) statement, which we tried to simplify as much as possible; it seems that the conditions cannot be weakened to obtain the desired conclusion that mimics Lemma
2.3
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\big(A_{n}(x)\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of power series and
(\rho_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a real-valued positive sequence with
\overline{\rho}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{[x^{n-k}]A_{n}(x)}{[x^{n}]A_{n}(x)}

\displaystyle\sim\rho_{n}^{k}\quad\text{for }k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\text{ and as }n\to\infty.

(2.11)

Moreover, assume that there exist
\varepsilon>0
and
n_{0},k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{[x^{n-k}]A_{n}(x)}{[x^{n}]A_{n}(x)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\rho_{n}^{k}\quad\text{for }k_{0}\leq k\leq n\text{ and }n\geq n_{0}.

(2.12)

Let
(R_{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of power series with radii of convergence at least
a:=(1+\varepsilon)\overline{\rho}
. Moreover, suppose that there is a sequence
(d_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lvert[x^{k}]R_{n}(x)\rvert\leq d_{k}
for all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sum_{k\geq 1}d_{k}a^{k}<\infty
. In addition, let
Q:[0,a]\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
R_{n}
converges uniformly to
Q
on
[0,a]
. If
\inf_{n\geq n_{0}}Q(\rho_{n})>0
, then

[x^{n}]A_{n}(x)R_{n}(x)\sim Q(\rho_{n})\cdot[x^{n}]A_{n}(x)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

We abbreviate

a_{k,n}:=[x^{k}]A_{n}(x)

and

r_{k,n}:=[x^{k}]R_{n}(x)

for
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Write for
K\in\mathbb{N}

\frac{1}{a_{n,n}}[x^{n}]A_{n}(x)R_{n}(x)=I(0,K)+I(K,n),\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{where}\penalty\ \penalty\ I(i,j):=\sum_{i\leq k<j}\frac{a_{n-k,n}}{a_{n,n}}r_{k,n}.

Let
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
.
Let
K_{0}
be such that for all
K\geq K_{0}

\Big|\sum_{0\leq k<K}r_{k,n}\rho_{n}^{k}-Q(\rho_{n})\Big|\leq|R_{n}(\rho_{n})-Q(\rho_{n})|+\Big|\sum_{k>K}r_{k,n}\rho_{n}^{k}\Big|<\varepsilon^{\prime};

such a
K_{0}
exists since
R_{n}\to Q
uniformly on
[0,a]
,
\rho_{n}\leq a
for all
n
, and since

|\sum_{k>K}r_{k,n}\rho_{n}^{k}|\leq\sum_{k>K}d_{k}a^{k}

gets arbitrarily small by the assumption that
\sum_{k\geq 1}d_{k}a^{k}
converges.

Note further that for any
K\geq K_{0}
the property (
2.11
) entails for sufficiently large
n

\Big|I(0,K)-\sum_{0\leq k<K}r_{k,n}\rho_{n}^{k}\Big|<\varepsilon^{\prime}.

The triangle inequality readily implies that
\lvert I(0,K)-Q(\rho_{n})\rvert<2\varepsilon^{\prime}
for all
K\geq K_{0}
and
n
sufficiently large.
Further, (
2.12
) guarantees that there is
\varepsilon>0
such that
0<a_{n-k,n}/a_{n,n}\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\rho_{n}^{k}
for all
k,n
sufficiently large. From that we conclude that there is a
K_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
K\geq K_{1}

\lvert I(K,n)\rvert\leq\sum_{k\geq K}\left|r_{k,n}\right|(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\rho_{n}^{k}\leq\sum_{k\geq K}d_{k}(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\overline{\rho}^{k}<\varepsilon^{\prime}.

All together, fixing
K\geq\max\{K_{0},K_{1}\}
, we proved that there is an error term
|E|<3\varepsilon^{\prime}
such that

a_{n,n}^{-1}\cdot[x^{n}]A_{n}(x)R_{n}(x)=Q(\rho_{n})+E

for
n
sufficiently large. Since
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
was arbitrary and
Q(\rho_{n})
is bounded away from zero, the claim follows.
∎

The next statement applies Lemma

Lemma 2.5

(2.13)

(ii)

(2.14)

(iii)

Proof.

\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle[x^{n}]\frac{1}{(1-x)^{\gamma}}\mathrm{e}^{h_{n}x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq k\leq n}\binom{n-k+\gamma-1}{\gamma-1}\frac{h_{n}^{k}}{k!}

The following simple and well-known saddle-point estimate for the coefficients of a power series will be useful several times.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let

and

\displaystyle f_{n}=[x^{n}]F(x)\leq F(z){z^{-n}}.

(2.16)

Finally, we present a simple estimate which holds for any power series with non-negative coefficients and positive radius of convergence.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
F(x)=\sum_{k\geq m}f_{k}x^{k}
be a power series with non-negative coefficients such that
f_{m}>0
and radius of convergence

. Let

. Then there exists

such that for all

Proof.

The first inequality follows directly from the definition of

and

. Abbreviate

and note that since

. Then

\frac{F(z)}{f_{m}z^{m}}\leq 1+z\frac{1}{f_{m}}\sum_{k>m}f_{k}a^{k-m-1}=1+z\frac{a^{-m-1}}{f_{m}}\sum_{k>m}f_{k}a^{k}.

Thus, as

2.4
Euler-Maclaurin Summation

For a function
g
we will need several times to compute the sum
\sum_{k=a}^{b}g(k)
for some
a<b
. The Euler-Maclaurin summation formula, discussed for example in
[
19
, Ch. 9.5]
, relates the sum to an integral, which is sometimes easier to compute.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable and
P_{1}(x):=x-\lfloor x\rfloor-1/2
. Then for any
a<b

\sum_{k=a}^{b}g(k)=\int_{a}^{b}g(x)dx+\frac{g(a)+g(b)}{2}+\int_{a}^{b}g^{\prime}(x)P_{1}(x)dx.

We will apply this to functions of the form
g(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-dx^{2}}
for some
d>0
. Note that
\lvert P_{1}(x)\rvert\leq 1
and
\lvert g^{\prime}(x)\rvert
equals
g^{\prime}(x)
for
x<0
and
-g^{\prime}(x)
for
x>0
. Accordingly,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=a}^{b}g(k)=\int_{a}^{b}g(x)dx+Q,\quad\text{where}\quad\lvert Q\rvert\leq\frac{g(a)+g(b)}{2}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|g^{\prime}(x)|dx\leq 3.

(2.18)

This will simplify the computation of
\sum g(k)
considerably, as it will turn out that the remainder
Q
is negligible compared to
\int g(x)dx
which, in turn, is well-studied.

3
General Proof Strategy

In this section we describe the general proof strategy. On the way, several important intermediate statements are stated and we explain how to assemble them in order to prove Theorems
1(I)
and
1(II)
. Then all is left to show is that these intermediate statements are valid, the proof of which is deferred to Section

3.1
Proof Setup: the Boltzmann Model

We begin with the definition of the Boltzmann model that will be central in the forthcoming considerations. Given a non-negative real-valued sequence
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, the associated power series
C(x)
, and a positive real
x_{0}
such that
C(x_{0})<\infty
, we define the random variable
\Gamma C(x_{0})
by

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\Gamma C(x_{0})=n\right]=c_{n}\frac{x_{0}^{n}}{C(x_{0})},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

We will refer to this distribution as the
Boltzmann distribution
or the
Boltzmann model
for
C(x)
or for
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
at
x_{0}
.
The Boltzmann model has a natural interpretation, if
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the counting sequence of some combinatorial class
\cal C
; this is actually where this terminology originates, see the seminal paper
[
14
]
.
Indeed, imagine in that case that we put a “weight” of
x_{0}^{n}
to any object of size
n
in
\cal C
, so that the total weight
C(x_{0})
is finite.
If we then draw an object from
\cal C
with a probability that is proportional to its weight, then we get precisely the Boltzmann distribution. Since we are going to need that later several times without explicitly referencing it, note that

\mathbb{E}[\Gamma C(x_{0})]=\frac{x_{0}C^{\prime}(x_{0})}{C(x_{0})}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma C(x_{0})^{2}\big]=\frac{x_{0}^{2}C^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}{C(x_{0})}+\frac{x_{0}C^{\prime}(x_{0})}{C(x_{0})}.

In a completely analogous way we can define a bivariate variant of the Boltzmann model. Suppose that we are given a sequence of non-negative sequences
((g_{n,N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with associated power series
G(x,y)
and positive reals
x_{0},y_{0}
such that
G(x_{0},y_{0})<\infty
. Then we consider the
\mathbb{N}^{2}
-valued random variable
\Gamma G(x_{0},y_{0})
with distribution

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\Gamma G(x_{0},y_{0})=(n,N)\right]=g_{n,N}\frac{x_{0}^{n}y_{0}^{N}}{G(x_{0},y_{0})},\qquad n,N\in\mathbb{N},

(3.1)

that we also call a Boltzmann distribution/model for
G
at
(x_{0},y_{0})
.

The crucial point behind the previous definitions is that we can exploit them to actually determine
c_{n}
and
g_{n,N}
. Indeed, if we could determine the probability on the left-hand side of (
3.1
), then would also know
g_{n,N}
. This consideration is of course only useful if we had an appropriate hands-on description of
\Gamma G(x_{0},y_{0})
that we could study appropriately. Here the Boltzmann models come into play: a particularly useful property of them – and one that made them so successful in combinatorics – is that they
compose well
, see also
[
14
,
8
]
. For example, suppose that
C(x)=A(x)B(x)
and
x_{0}
be such that
A(x_{0}),B(x_{0})<\infty
. Then we can relate the Boltzmann models
\Gamma C(x_{0})
and
\Gamma A(x_{0}),\Gamma B(x_{0})
as follows. Consider the following simple random process, that first draws independently from
\Gamma A(x_{0}),\Gamma B(x_{0})
and then creates the sum:

(P)

Let independently

and

.

Then it is quite easy to see that
C
is distributed like
\Gamma C(x_{0})
; we do not show that here, since we do not need it, but we keep the guiding principle in mind: the
product of power series corresponds to independent components in the Boltzmann model
. Let us study a second example. Suppose that we are given
C(x)
and
D(x)=\exp\{C(x)\}

1

1

and as

Set

.
Due to (

) we have for any

\displaystyle\frac{[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)}{[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{n}^{-1}}{1-\alpha_{n,k}^{-1}}\right)^{c_{m}}\frac{N_{n}!}{(N_{n}-k)!}N_{n}^{-k}\left(\frac{N_{n}}{h_{n}}\right)^{k}\sim\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{n}^{-1}}{1-\alpha_{n,k}^{-1}}\right)^{c_{m}}\frac{N_{n}!}{(N_{n}-k)!}N_{n}^{-k}\rho_{n}^{k}.

Since
\alpha_{n,k}\geq\alpha_{n}
and

we obtain for any
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\frac{[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)}{[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)}\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\rho_{n}^{k},\quad N_{n}-k\text{ sufficiently large.}

(4.11)

Lemma

(i)
and (

) imply that for any

such that

\displaystyle\frac{[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)}{[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)}

\displaystyle\sim(1-\alpha_{n}^{-1})^{c_{m}}\frac{N_{n}!}{(N_{n}-k)!}h_{n}^{-k}\sim(1-\alpha_{n}^{-1})^{c_{m}}\frac{N_{n}!}{(N_{n}-k)!}N_{n}^{-k}\rho_{n}^{k}\leq\rho_{n}^{k}.

Again this entails that for any
\varepsilon>0
and
N_{n}
sufficiently large

\displaystyle\frac{[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)}{[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)}\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\rho_{n}^{k}\quad\text{for }N_{n}-k=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right).

(4.12)

Together with (
4.11
) this shows that condition (
2.12
) is fulfilled for any
\varepsilon>0
as long as
n
is sufficiently large.
Let us proceed with checking the remaining conditions of Lemma
2.4
. Choose

in (

) and (

) such that

. Then the series

.
Moreover, by the same lemma,

. In particular, as

Moreover, Lemma

guarantees the existence of a sequence

\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\sim Q(\rho_{n})\cdot\frac{({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{N_{n}}}{G({x}_{n},y_{n})}\left(\frac{1}{1-\rho_{n}}\right)^{c_{m}}\cdot\frac{h_{n}^{N_{n}}}{N_{n}!}.

In what follows, we simplify this term. First note that

Further, recalling that

Together with Lemma

as claimed. To show the second statement (

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\geq 2}jP_{j}=K\right]\leq[x^{K}]\exp\left\{\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j})y_{n}^{j}}{j}x^{j}\right\}.

Applying (
2.16

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\geq 2}jP_{j}=K\right]\leq\exp\left\{\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j})}{j}\right\}y_{n}^{K}.

(4.13)

Further

. At the same time we observe with Lemma

that there is some

Since according to Lemma

finishing the proof together with (

∎

Proof of Lemma
3.5(II)
.

Abbreviate
h_{n}:=C({x}_{n})/{x}_{n}^{m}
and set

A(x):=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{c_{m}}}\cdot\exp\left\{h_{n}x\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad R(x):=\exp\left\{-c_{m}x+\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j})-c_{m}{x}_{n}^{jm}}{j{x}_{n}^{jm}}x^{j}\right\}.

Then, from (
4.7
) we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{N_{n}}}{G({x}_{n},y_{n})}\cdot[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)\cdot R(x).

We will apply Lemma
2.4
to
A(x)R(x)
. Let us first verify the conditions (
2.11
), (
2.12
). Set
\alpha_{n}:=h_{n}/N_{n}
and
\alpha_{n,k}:=h_{n}/(N_{n}-k)
. Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence from (
4.4
). Then, as stated in Lemma
4.2
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}=\frac{y_{n}C({x}_{n})}{{x}_{n}^{m}y_{n}}\frac{1}{N_{n}}\sim a_{n}

and

\displaystyle[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)=[x^{N_{n}-k}]\frac{1}{(1-x)^{c_{m}}}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{n,k}(N_{n}-k)x},\quad p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(4.14)

For (fixed)
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and using (
4.4
) we obtain that
\limsup\alpha_{n,k}=\limsup\alpha_{n}\leq\limsup\lambda_{n}^{-1}<1
. Thus Lemma
2.5
(iii)
yields

[x^{N_{n}-p}]A(x)\sim\frac{\big((1-\alpha_{n})N_{n}\big)^{c_{m}-1}}{\Gamma(c_{m})}\mathrm{e}^{h_{n}},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},n\to\infty.

(4.15)

Hence condition (
2.11
) is fulfilled with
\rho_{n}\sim 1
and
\overline{\rho}=\limsup\rho_{n}=1
. We verify condition (
2.12
) as well. Let
\delta>0
be such that
(1+\delta)\limsup h_{n}/N_{n}<1
. Such a
\delta
exists, since
\limsup h_{n}/N_{n}=\limsup\alpha_{n}<1
, as we already saw before. With this at hand we split up
\{0,\dots,N_{n}\}
into

B_{1}:=\{0,\dots,N_{n}-(1+\delta)h_{n}-1\}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{2}:=\{N_{n}-(1+\delta)h_{n},\dots,N_{n}\}.

Consider
k\in B_{1}
. Then

\limsup\alpha_{n,k}\leq\limsup\alpha_{n}N_{n}/((1+\delta)h_{n})=(1+\delta)^{-1}<1
.
Further
N_{n}-k\to\infty
in this case, so that we obtain from (
4.14
) and Lemma
2.5
(iii)

[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)\sim\frac{\big((1-\alpha_{n,k})(N_{n}-k)\big)^{c_{m}-1}}{\Gamma(c_{m})}\mathrm{e}^{h_{n}}.

This leads to

\frac{[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)}{[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)}\sim\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{n,N_{n}-k}}{1-\alpha_{n}}\right)^{c_{m}-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{N_{n}}\right)^{c_{m}-1}=\left(1-\frac{k}{N_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})}\right)^{c_{m}-1},\quad k\in B_{1}.

If
c_{m}-1\geq 0
, then this is at most 1. If
c_{m}-1\in(-1,0)
, then it is at most
(1-{k}/{N_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})})^{-1}=1+k/(N_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})-k)\leq e^{k/(N_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})-k)}
. By assumption
N_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})-k\to\infty
, and so
for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)}{[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)}\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{k},\quad k\in B_{1}.

(4.16)

Next consider
k\in B_{2}
. Set
z:=1-h_{n}^{-1/2}
. Then the bound (
2.16
) together with (
4.15
) yield

\displaystyle\frac{[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)}{[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)}\leq\frac{(1-z)^{-c_{m}}\exp\left\{h_{n}z\right\}z^{-(N_{n}-k)}}{[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)}=\mathcal{O}\left(N_{n}\left(\frac{\sqrt{h_{n}}}{N_{n}}\right)^{c_{m}}\exp\{-\sqrt{h_{n}}\}z^{k-N_{n}}\right).

(4.17)

We further get that for any
k\in B_{2}
that there exists
-h_{n}\leq t\leq\delta h_{n}
with
N_{n}-k=h_{n}+t
so that

z^{k-N_{n}}\leq\exp\left\{-h_{n}^{-1/2}(k-N_{n})\right\}=\exp\left\{\sqrt{h_{n}}+{t}/{\sqrt{h_{n}}}\right\}.

Since
\sqrt{h_{n}}=o(N_{n})
we get from (
4.17
)

\frac{[x^{N_{n}-k}]A(x)}{[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)}=o\left(N_{n}\exp\left\{{t}/{\sqrt{h_{n}}}\right\}\right)=o\left(N_{n}\exp\left\{\delta\sqrt{N_{n}}\right\}\right)\quad\text{for all }k\in B_{2}.

However, since any
k\in B_{2}
satisfies
k=\Omega(N_{n})
, this bound is also
\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}
for any
\varepsilon>0
and
n
sufficiently large.
Together with (
4.16
) this finally verifies condition (
2.12
) of Lemma
2.4
(recall that
\rho_{n}\sim 1
).

We show that the remaining conditions of Lemma
2.4
are satisfied as well. By applying Lemma
4.3
we obtain that
R(x)
converges uniformly to

Q(x):=\exp\Bigg\{-c_{m}x+\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{C(\rho^{j})-c_{m}\rho^{jm}}{j\rho^{jm}}\cdot x^{j}\Bigg\}

on any interval
[0,a]
with
a<\rho^{-1}
. Since
\rho^{-1}>1
we may even choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
a=(1+\varepsilon)\overline{\rho}
and
1=\overline{\rho}<a<\rho^{-1}
.
Then Lemma
4.3
gives us the existence of the sequence
(d_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
\lvert[x^{k}]R(x)\rvert\leq d_{k}
and
\sum_{k\geq 1}d_{k}a^{k}<\infty
. Finally
Q(\rho_{n})\sim Q(1)>0
for all

so that all conditions of Lemma
2.4
are met and we deduce from (

)

\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\sim\frac{({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{N_{n}}}{G({x}_{n},y_{n})}\cdot Q(1)\cdot[x^{N_{n}}]A(x)\sim\frac{({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{N_{n}}}{G({x}_{n},y_{n})}\cdot Q(1)\cdot\frac{\left(\left(1-a_{n}\right)N_{n}\right)^{c_{m}-1}}{\Gamma(c_{m})}\mathrm{e}^{h_{n}}.

Lemma
3.4(II)
gives us (the asymptotics of)

and we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\sim\frac{c_{m}^{c_{m}}}{(1-a_{n})\Gamma(c_{m})}\cdot\frac{({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{N_{n}}}{N_{n}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{h_{n}-y_{n}C({x}_{n})}.

To conclude we observe by appyling Lemma

\displaystyle h_{n}-y_{n}C({x}_{n})=\frac{C(x_{n})}{x_{n}^{m}}-y_{n}C(x_{n})=y_{n}C({x}_{n})\cdot\frac{1-{x}_{n}^{m}y_{n}}{{x}_{n}^{m}y_{n}}=\frac{y_{n}C({x}_{n})}{S_{n}}\sim c_{m}\frac{a_{n}}{1-a_{n}}.

(4.18)

This shows the first statement of the lemma. Next we prove the second statement (
3.8
). Recall that
K\equiv K_{n}
is such that
K_{n}\to\infty
as

.
From (

) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j>\ell}jP_{j}=K\right]=\frac{({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{K}}{G^{(\ell)}({x}_{n},y_{n})}\cdot[x^{K}]\frac{1}{(1-x)^{c_{m}}}\cdot R^{(\ell)}(x).

Similar to the case

2.4
to

, but this time it is much easier as

does not depend on

\displaystyle[x^{K}]\frac{1}{(1-x)^{c_{m}}}=\binom{K+c_{m}-1}{K}\sim\frac{K^{c_{m}-1}}{\Gamma(c_{m})},\quad\text{as }K\to\infty.

(4.19)

Hence (
2.11
) is fulfilled with
\rho_{n}=1
and
\overline{\rho}:=\limsup\rho_{n}=1
, and the explicit form readily allows us to establish
condition (

) as well.
Moreover, Lemma
4.3
asserts that
R^{(\ell)}(x)
converges to

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j>\ell}jP_{j}=K\right]\sim\frac{({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{K}}{G^{(\ell)}({x}_{n},y_{n})}\cdot Q^{(\ell)}(1)\cdot\frac{K^{c_{m}-1}}{\Gamma(c_{m})}.

(4.20)

By a similar reformulation as in the proof of Lemma

we derive with Lemma

and plugging this into (

4.5
Proof of Lemma
3.6

In order to prove that
\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]
gets exponentially small in
r
as claimed in Lemma
3.6
we show as a preparation the following estimate (that is nothing else than a Chernoff-type bound).

Lemma 4.4
.

Set
\Omega_{n}:=\{(p_{1},\dots,p_{N_{n}})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N_{n}}:p_{1}+2p_{2}\cdots+N_{n}p_{N_{n}}=N_{n}\}
and let
\lambda>0
be such that
0<\lambda<-\ln\rho/2
. Then for
(\tau_{j})_{j\geq 2}:=(C({x}_{n}^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j})y_{n}^{j}/\mathrm{e}^{\lambda jm})_{j\geq 2}

\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}{C({x}_{n}^{j})y_{n}^{j}}/{j}}\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p_{1}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]}\cdot\prod_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}\frac{(\tau_{j}/j)^{p_{j}}}{p_{j}!},\qquad r\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

The choice of
\lambda
guarantees that
\mathrm{e}^{j\lambda}{x}_{n}^{j}<\rho
for all
j\geq 2
. Hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j(C_{j,i}-m)}\right]=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda jm}\frac{\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}({x}_{n}^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j})^{k}}{C({x}_{n}^{j})}=\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j})}{C({x}_{n}^{j})\mathrm{e}^{\lambda jm}},\qquad i\geq 1,j\geq 2.

Then by Bayes and the independence of the
P_{j}
’s

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]&=\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{n}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq r\biggm|\bigcap_{1\leq j\leq N}\{P_{j}=p_{j}\}\right]\mathrm{Pr}\left[\bigcap_{1\leq j\leq N}\{P_{j}=p_{j}\}\biggm|{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\\
&=\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]^{-1}\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{n}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\geq 2}j\sum_{1\leq i\leq p_{j}}(C_{j,i}-m)\geq r\right]\prod_{1\leq j\leq N_{n}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{j}=p_{j}\right].\end{split}

(4.21)

With Markov’s inequality and the independence of the
(C_{j,i})_{j,i\geq 1}
and
(P_{j})_{j\geq 1}
we obtain

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\geq 2}j\sum_{1\leq i\leq p_{j}}(C_{j,i}-m)\geq r\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\prod_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j(C_{j,i}-m)}\right]^{p_{j}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\prod_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}\left(\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j})}{C({x}_{n}^{j})\mathrm{e}^{\lambda jm}}\right)^{p_{j}}.

By plugging this into (
4.21
) and using that
P_{j}\sim\textrm{Po}(C(x_{n}^{j})y_{n}^{j}/j)
we obtain the claimed statement.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.6
in case
(I)
.

By applying Lemma
4.4
and using that the Poisson distribution is maximised at its mean, so that
\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p_{1}\right]/\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\leq 1
, we obtain for some
\lambda>0

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{n}}\prod_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}\frac{(\tau_{j}/j)^{p_{j}}}{p_{j}!}.

Lemma
2.7
yields that for some
A>0

\tau_{j}\leq c_{m}({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{j}(1+A{x}_{n}^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j}),\quad j\geq 2.

Lemma
4.1
states that there is a
0<\varepsilon<1
such that
{x}_{n}^{m}y_{n}<1-\varepsilon
for all sufficiently large
n
. We deduce that
\exp\{\sum_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}\tau_{j}/j\}=\exp\{{\cal O}(\sum_{j\geq 2}(1-\varepsilon)^{j}/j)\}
is bounded. Consequently, setting
H=\sum_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}jH_{j}
with

independent, we obtain for some (other)
A>0

\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\leq A\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\cdot\sum_{0\leq p\leq N_{n}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[H=N_{n}-p\right]\leq A\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
3.6
in case
(II)
.

Our starting point is Lemma
4.4
so that

\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\cdot\exp\left\{-\sum_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j})y_{n}^{j}}{j}\right\}\sum_{\mathrm{p}\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p_{1}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]}\cdot\sum_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}\frac{(\tau_{j}/j)^{p_{j}}}{p_{j}!}

for
0<\lambda<-\ln\rho/2
.
Using Lemmas
4.2
and
2.7
we obtain the estimate

\exp\left\{\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j})y_{n}^{j}}{j}\right\}=\frac{1}{(1-{x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{c_{m}}}\exp\left\{-c_{m}{x}_{n}^{m}y_{n}+\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j})y_{n}^{j}-c_{m}{x}_{n}^{jm}y_{n}^{j}}{j}\right\}={\cal O}(N_{n}^{c_{m}}).

Next let
H_{j}\sim\mathrm{Po}\left(\tau_{j}/j\right)
be independent random variables for
j\geq 2
and set
H:=\sum_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}jH_{j}
. Abbreviate
\Upsilon:=\exp\{\sum_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}\tau_{j}/j\}
. We obtain for some
A_{1}>0

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[R=r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]\leq A_{1}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\cdot N^{-c_{m}}\cdot\Upsilon\cdot\sum_{0\leq p\leq N_{n}}\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]}\mathrm{Pr}\left[H=N_{n}-p\right].

(4.22)

Since
(H_{j})_{j\geq 2}
are independent

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[H=N_{n}-p\right]=\Upsilon^{-1}[x^{N_{n}-p}]\exp\Big\{\sum_{j\geq 2}{\tau_{j}}x^{j}/j\Big\}.

(4.23)

In the last expression we actually have to restrict to
2\leq j\leq N_{n}
; however, for all
0\leq M\leq N_{n}
,
[x^{M}]\exp\{\sum_{2\leq j\leq N_{n}}\tau_{j}x^{j}/j\}=[x^{M}]\exp\{\sum_{j\geq 2}\tau_{j}x^{j}/j\}
. Then

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{\sum_{j\geq 2}\tau_{j}x^{j}/j\Big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-{x}_{n}^{m}y_{n}x)^{c_{m}}}\cdot\exp\left\{-c_{m}{x}_{n}^{m}y_{n}x+\sum_{j\geq 2}(\tau_{j}-c_{m}({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{j})\frac{x^{j}}{j}\right\}

and so, for any
0\leq M\leq N_{n}

\displaystyle[x^{M}]\exp\Big\{\sum_{j\geq 2}\tau_{j}x^{j}/j\Big\}

\displaystyle=({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{M}\cdot[x^{M}]\frac{1}{(1-x)^{c_{m}}}\cdot\exp\left\{-c_{m}x+\sum_{j\geq 2}\left(\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j})}{{x}_{n}^{jm}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda jm}}-c_{m}\right)\frac{x^{j}}{j}\right\}.

With Lemma
2.7
we get a bound which holds uniformly for some
A>0
and all
j\geq 2

0<a_{n,j}:=\frac{C({x}_{n}^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j})}{{x}_{n}^{jm}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda jm}}-c_{m}\leq\frac{C(\rho^{j}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda j})}{\rho^{jm}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda jm}}-c_{m}\leq A(\rho\mathrm{e}^{\lambda})^{j}=:a_{j}.

Using the fact that for a power series
f
with non-negative coefficients the coefficients of
\mathrm{e}^{f(x)}
can get only larger if we make the coefficients of
f
larger we get

\displaystyle({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{-M}[x^{M}]\exp\Big\{\sum_{j\geq 2}\tau_{j}x^{j}/j\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}=M}[x^{m_{1}}]\frac{1}{(1-x)^{c_{m}}}\cdot\lvert[x^{m_{2}}]\mathrm{e}^{-c_{m}x}\rvert\cdot\bigg\lvert[x^{m_{3}}]\exp\Big\{\sum_{j\geq 2}a_{n,j}x^{j}/j\Big\}\bigg\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}=M}[x^{m_{1}}]\frac{1}{(1-x)^{c_{m}}}\cdot[x^{m_{2}}]\mathrm{e}^{c_{m}x}\cdot[x^{m_{3}}]\exp\Big\{\sum_{j\geq 2}a_{j}x^{j}/j\Big\}

\displaystyle=[x^{M}]\frac{1}{(1-x)^{c_{m}}}\exp\Big\{c_{m}x+\sum_{j\geq 2}a_{j}x^{j}/j\Big\}=:[x^{M}]A(x)\tilde{R}(x).

Inserting this into (
4.23
) we obtain
\mathrm{Pr}\left[H=N_{n}-p\right]\leq\Upsilon^{-1}(x_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{N_{n}-p}[x^{N_{n}-p}]A(x)\tilde{R}(x)
.
The advantage of this estimate is that
\tilde{R}
does not depend on
n
anymore. We have that, compare to (
4.19
),
[x^{n}]A(x)\sim n^{c_{m}-1}/\Gamma(c_{m})
implying that
[x^{n-1}]A(x)/[x^{n}]A(x)\sim 1
. In addition,
\tilde{R}(1)<\infty
, as the radius of convergence of
\tilde{R}
is at least
(\rho\mathrm{e}^{\lambda})^{-1}>1
. With Lemma
2.3
we consequently obtain
[x^{n}]A(x)\tilde{R}(x)\sim\tilde{R}(1)n^{c_{m}-1}/\Gamma(c_{m})
.
This, on the other hand, implies that we can find a
A_{2}>0
such that uniformly in
n
and
0\leq p<N_{n}

[x^{N_{n}-p}]A(x)\tilde{R}(x)\leq A_{2}(N_{n}-p)^{c_{m}-1}.

All in all, noting that
({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{N_{n}-p}\leq 1
, we get uniformly in
n
and
0\leq p<N_{n}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[H=N_{n}-p\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\Upsilon^{-1}(N_{n}-p)^{c_{m}-1}\right).

For the case
p=N_{n}
note that the probability that
H
equals
0
is
\Upsilon^{-1}
.
Putting these pieces together into (
4.22
) we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[R\geq r\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda r}\cdot N^{-c_{m}}\cdot\left(\sum_{0\leq p<N_{n}}\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]}\left(N_{n}-p\right)^{c_{m}-1}+\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=N_{n}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]}\right)\right).

Since according to Lemma
3.5(II)
we have that
\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]=\Theta\big(({x}_{n}^{m}y_{n})^{N_{n}}/N_{n}\big)
and
{x}_{n}^{m}y_{n}=1-\Theta(1/N_{n})
due to Lemma
4.2
, we have
\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]=\Theta(1/N_{n})
. Further Lemma
4.2
yields that
\limsup y_{n}C({x}_{n})/N_{n}\leq\limsup\lambda_{n}^{-1}<1
so that we can estimate
\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=N_{n}\right]=\exp\left\{-\Omega(N_{n})\right\}
by (
2.9
). Hence

N^{-c_{m}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=N_{n}\right]/\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(N_{n}^{1-c_{m}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(N_{n})}\right)=o(1).

With the estimates for
\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]
we further obtain

N_{n}^{-c_{m}}\cdot\sum_{0\leq p<N_{n}}\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\left[{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{n}}\right]}\left(N_{n}-p\right)^{c_{m}-1}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{0\leq p<N_{n}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p\right]\left(1-\frac{p}{N_{n}}\right)^{c_{m}-1}\right).

To finish the proof we show that the latter expression is
\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
. This is clear for
c_{m}\geq 1
. If
0<c_{m}<1
we note that there is some
\delta>0
such that
(1+\delta)\limsup\mathbb{E}\left[P_{1}\right]/N_{n}=(1+\delta)\limsup y_{n}C({x}_{n})/N_{n}\leq(1+\delta)\lambda_{n}^{-1}<1
due to Lemma
4.2
. Then
\sum_{0\leq p\leq N_{n}/(1+\delta)}\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p\right](1-p/N_{n})^{c_{m}-1}\leq(1+\delta^{-1})^{1-c_{m}}
. For
N/(1+\delta)<p<N_{n}
, on the other hand, we get that
\sum_{N_{n}/(1+\delta)<p<N_{n}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}=p\right](1-p/N_{n})^{c_{m}-1}\leq N_{n}^{1-c_{m}}\mathrm{Pr}\left[P_{1}>N_{n}/(1+\delta)\right]
.
This is
\mathrm{e}^{-\Omega(N_{n})}
by (
2.9
) and the proof is completed.
∎

4.6
Proof of Lemma
3.7

This entire section is devoted to the proof of Lemma
3.7
.
First of all note that the probability generating function of
C_{1,1}
is given by
H(x)=C({x}_{n}x)/C({x}_{n})
, that is,
\mathrm{Pr}\left[C_{1,1}=k\right]=[x^{k}]H(x)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Define
K_{p}:=\sum_{1\leq i\leq p}C_{1,i}
and
\nu:={x}_{n}C^{\prime}({x}_{n})/C({x}_{n})
. Then

\mathrm{Pr}\left[L_{p}=\mu_{p}+t\sigma_{p}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[K_{p}=p\nu+t\sigma_{p}\right]=[x^{p\nu+t\sigma_{p}}]H(x)^{p}.

The tool of our choice for tackling this problem is the saddle-point method. Therefore we need appropriate bounds for
H
in
\mathbb{C}
on a circle centred at the origin with radius close to
\rho
; the next lemma shows a rather diverse picture. For future reference and since we will need this often, we apply Theorem
A.4
to obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}h(k)k^{\beta-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta k}\sim\Gamma(\beta)h(\eta^{-1})\eta^{-\beta}\quad\beta>0,\text{ as }\eta\to 0.

(4.24)

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\alpha>1,0<\rho<1
.
Then there exist
\eta_{0}>0,c<1,A>0
such that the following is true.
Let
0<\eta<\eta_{0}
and set
G(x):=C(\omega x)/C(\omega)
, where
\omega=\rho\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}
. Then

\displaystyle\lvert G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})\rvert\leq 1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\left(\frac{\theta}{\eta}\right)^{2}\quad\text{for any}\quad\lvert\theta\rvert\leq{\eta}/(24\alpha^{2}).

(4.25)

Moreover,

\lvert G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})\rvert\leq c\quad\text{for any}\quad{\eta}/(24\alpha^{2})\leq\lvert\theta\rvert\leq\pi

(4.26)

and

\lvert G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})\rvert\leq A\cdot\max\left\{{\eta}/{\lvert\theta\rvert},\eta\right\}\quad\text{for any}\quad\eta\leq\lvert\theta\rvert\leq\pi.

(4.27)

Proof.

We start with showing the first bound. Recall the basic inequalities

\cos(x)\leq 1-{x^{2}}/{2}+{x^{4}}/{24}\quad\text{and}\quad\sin(x)\leq|x|,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

that we will use more than once in the proof. Let
\delta_{1}\in(0,1)
. Let
R
denote the real part of
G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})
. Then, using (
4.24
), for
\eta>0
sufficiently small,

R=\frac{1}{C(\omega)}\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\rho^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta k}\cos(\theta k)\leq 1-(1-\delta_{1})\frac{(\alpha+1)\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{\eta}\right)^{2}+\frac{(\alpha+3)(\alpha+2)(\alpha+1)\alpha}{12}\left(\frac{\theta}{\eta}\right)^{4}.

Further, if
\lvert\theta\rvert\leq((\alpha+3)(\alpha+2))^{-1/2}\sqrt{\delta_{1}}\eta
,

R\leq 1-(1-2\delta_{1})\frac{(\alpha+1)\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{\eta}\right)^{2}.

Moreover, consider the imaginary part
I
of
G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})
. Then we obtain with (
4.24
) that for
\eta>0
sufficiently small

I^{2}=\frac{1}{C(\omega)^{2}}\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\rho^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta k}\sin(\theta k)\right)^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{1})\alpha^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{\theta}{\eta}\right)^{2}.

Combining these bounds yields for
\delta_{1}>0
and
|\theta|\leq((\alpha+3)(\alpha+2))^{-1/2}\sqrt{\delta_{1}}\eta
that

|G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})|^{2}=R^{2}+I^{2}\leq 1-\Big((1-2\delta_{1})\alpha-3\delta_{1}\alpha^{2}\Big)\left(\frac{\theta}{\eta}\right)^{2}+(\alpha+1)^{2}\alpha^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{\eta}\right)^{4}.

Then, as

|\theta|\leq((\alpha+3)(\alpha+2))^{-1/2}\sqrt{\delta_{1}}\eta\leq\sqrt{\delta_{1}}\eta/(\alpha+1)
, we obtain

|G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})|^{2}\leq 1-\Big((1-2\delta_{1})\alpha-4\delta_{1}\alpha^{2}\Big)\left(\frac{\theta}{\eta}\right)^{2}.

Choosing
\delta_{1}=1/(4+8\alpha)
yields
|G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})|\leq 1-\alpha(\theta/\eta)^{2}/2
, as claimed and being very generous in the bound for
\theta
. Note that for
\alpha>1
we have
1/(24\alpha^{2})\leq\sqrt{\delta_{1}}/\sqrt{(\alpha+2)(\alpha+3)}\leq\sqrt{\delta_{1}}/(\alpha+1)
so that (
4.25
) follows.

We continue with the proof of (
4.26
). Here
we will use a basic trick that was used in similar forms already long ago, see
[
18
]
, where
|G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})|
is related to the sum of the differences of consecutive terms. Here we use the following construction. Note that

(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})=\frac{1}{C(\omega)}\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta k-\eta k}(\rho^{k}c_{k}-\rho^{k-1}c_{k-1}).

Note that
\rho^{-1}c_{k-1}/c_{k}=h(k-1)/h(k)\cdot(1-k^{-1})^{\alpha-1}\leq(1-k^{-1})^{\alpha-1+o(1)}
due to (
A.2
). Accordingly, since
\alpha>1
, we have for sufficiently large
k
that
\rho^{-1}c_{k-1}\leq c_{k}
. Let
\delta>0
. Then, using (
A.2
), whenever
k
is sufficiently large,

\displaystyle|\rho^{k}c_{k}-\rho^{k-1}c_{k-1}|

\displaystyle=\rho^{k}c_{k}\left(1-\frac{\rho^{-1}c_{k-1}}{c_{k}}\right)=\rho^{k}c_{k}\left(1-\frac{h(k-1)}{h(k)}\Big(1-\frac{1}{k}\Big)^{\alpha-1}\right)\leq\rho^{k}c_{k}\left(1-\Big(1-\frac{1}{k}\Big)^{\alpha-1+\delta}\right).

Note that
(1-x)^{a}\geq 1-(1+\delta)ax
for any
a,\delta>0
and
x
sufficiently small. We thus obtain for sufficiently large
k
that

|\rho^{k}c_{k}-\rho^{k-1}c_{k-1}|\leq\frac{\alpha-1+\delta(\alpha+\delta)}{k}\rho^{k}c_{k}.

Using this and the triangle inequality we obtain that for any
\delta>0
there is some
K\in\mathbb{N}
and
d>0
such that

|G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})|\leq\frac{1}{C(\omega)|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}|}\left((\alpha-1+\delta(\alpha+\delta))\sum_{k\geq K}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta k}\frac{\rho^{k}c_{k}}{k}+d\right).

(4.28)

A simple calculation reveals that

|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}|^{2}=1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\eta}-2\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\cos(\theta).

Since
|\theta|\in[\eta/24\alpha^{2},\pi]
the
\cos
is maximized for
|\theta|=\eta/24\alpha^{2}
; using
\mathrm{e}^{-x}=1-x+{x^{2}}/2+\mathcal{O}\left(x^{3}\right)
and
\cos(x)=1-x^{2}/2+\mathcal{O}\left(x^{4}\right)
for
x\to 0
and abbreviating
a=2(24\alpha^{2})^{2}
we get for sufficiently small
\eta

\displaystyle|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\eta}-2\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\cos(\eta/24\alpha^{2})

\displaystyle=1+(1-2\eta+2\eta^{2})-2(1-\eta+\eta^{2}/2)(1-\eta^{2}/a)+\mathcal{O}\left(\eta^{3}\right)

\displaystyle=(1+2/a)\eta^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\eta^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1+1/a)\eta^{2}.

(4.29)

Moreover, since
\alpha>1
and using (
4.24
) as
\eta\to 0

\sum_{k\geq K}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta k}\frac{\rho^{k}c_{k}}{k}\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta k}\frac{\rho^{k}c_{k}}{k}\sim\Gamma(\alpha-1)h(\eta^{-1})\eta^{-\alpha+1}.

All in all, if
\alpha>1
and using the latter inequality as well as
C(\omega)\sim\Gamma(\alpha)h(\eta^{-1})\eta^{-\alpha}
we obtain by plugging in (
4.29
) into (
4.28
) that for any
\delta>0
and
\eta
sufficiently small

\displaystyle|G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})|\leq(1+\delta)\frac{\alpha-1+\delta(\alpha+\delta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)h(\eta^{-1})\eta^{-\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-1)h(\eta^{-1})\eta^{-\alpha+1}}{(1+1/a)^{1/2}\eta}\leq\frac{1+\delta(1+(\alpha+\delta)/(\alpha-1))}{(1+1/a)^{1/2}}.

(4.30)

Since
\delta>0
was arbitrary and
a>0
the claim in (
4.26
) is established by choosing
\delta
sufficiently small.

We complete the proof by showing (
4.27
). Note that there is a constant
b>0
such that for all
0\leq x\leq\pi

|\mathrm{e}^{-x}-(1-x)|\leq bx^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad|\cos(x)-(1-x^{2}/2)|\leq bx^{4}.

Applying this to any occurrence of
\mathrm{e}^{x}
and
\cos(x)
below we obtain that there is a
b>0
such that for all
|\theta\rvert\leq\pi
and
\eta
sufficiently small

\big||1-\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}|^{2}-\theta^{2}\big|=\big|\big(1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\eta}-2\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\cos(\theta)\big)-\theta^{2}\big|\leq b(\eta^{2}+\eta\theta^{2}+\theta^{4}).

In particular, if
|\theta|\geq\eta
we obtain that

\big||1-\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}|^{2}-\theta^{2}\big|\leq 3b\theta^{4}.

Especially, if
|\theta|\leq(6b)^{-1/2}
, then
|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}|^{2}\geq\theta^{2}/2
. Moreover, since
|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}|^{2}
is monotone increasing for
\theta\in[0,\pi]
we obtain
|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\eta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}|^{2}\geq(12b)^{-1}
for all
|\theta|\geq(6b)^{-1/2}
. Using these two last statements instead of (
4.29
) in (
4.28
) we arrive similarly to (
4.30
) at the desired estimate (
4.27
).
∎

The previous statement applies only when
\alpha>1
. To handle the case
0<\alpha\leq 1
we show the following property, which establishes that summing up iid random variables with probability generating function
G(x)
sufficiently often we obtain a random variable based on the modified sequence
\tilde{c}_{n}=\tilde{h}(n)n^{\tilde{\alpha}-1}\rho^{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
where
\tilde{h}
is slowly varying and
\tilde{\alpha}>1
. With this trick we will be able to apply Lemma
4.5
even in the case
0<\alpha\leq 1
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
0<\rho<1
. Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
Y_{s}:=\sum_{1\leq i\leq s}X_{i}
where
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
are iid with probability generating function
G(x)=C(\omega x)/C(\omega)
, where
\omega=\rho e^{-\eta}
. Then there exists a eventually positive, continuous and slowly varying function
\tilde{h}
such that

\Pr[Y_{s}=n]=\frac{\tilde{h}(n)n^{s\alpha-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta n}}{C(\omega)^{k}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

Let
\alpha^{\prime}=\ell\alpha
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\beta=\alpha-1
as well as
\beta^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}-1
. Let
f
be an eventually positive slowly varying function. We will show that

S:=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}f(k)k^{\beta^{\prime}}\cdot h(n-k)(n-k)^{\beta}=\tilde{h}(n)n^{\beta^{\prime}+\beta+1}

(4.31)

for some slowly varying
\tilde{h}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\tilde{h}(n)\sim I(\beta^{\prime})\cdot f(n)h(n)
for
I(\beta^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\beta^{\prime}}(1-x)^{\beta}dx
. Then the claimed statement follows readily by induction.
In order to see (
4.31
) let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary and consider the (
middle
) sum

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=\varepsilon n}^{(1-\varepsilon)n}f(k)k^{\beta^{\prime}}\cdot h(n-k)(n-k)^{\beta}=f(n)h(n)n^{\beta^{\prime}+\beta}\sum_{k=\varepsilon n}^{(1-\varepsilon)n}\frac{f(k)}{f(n)}\frac{h(n-k)}{h(n)}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{\beta^{\prime}}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{\beta}.

By applying the Uniform Convergence Theorem
A.1
we obtain that
f(k)/f(n)
and
h(n-k)/h(n)
both tend to
1
for any
k\in\{\varepsilon n,\dots,(1-\varepsilon)n\}
as
n\to\infty
. Further note that

\frac{1}{(1-2\varepsilon)n+2}\sum_{k=\varepsilon n}^{(1-\varepsilon)n}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{\beta^{\prime}}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{\beta}

is a Riemann sum of
I_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon}x^{\beta^{\prime}}(1-x)^{\beta}dx\in\mathbb{R}
. As
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary and
I_{0}<\infty

M\sim I_{\varepsilon}\cdot f(n)h(n)n^{\beta^{\prime}+\beta+1},\quad n\to\infty.

(4.32)

Next consider the (
tail
) sum. With (
A.3
), Corollary
A.3
and
h(\varepsilon n)\sim h(n)
as well as
f(\varepsilon n)\sim f(n)
we obtain

\displaystyle T:=\sum_{k=1}^{\varepsilon n}f(k)k^{\beta^{\prime}}\cdot h(n-k)(n-k)^{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(1-\varepsilon)n\leq k\leq n}h(k)k^{\beta}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\varepsilon n}f(k)k^{\beta^{\prime}}=\varepsilon^{\beta^{\prime}+1}\cdot\mathcal{O}\left(f(n)h(n)n^{\beta^{\prime}+\beta+1}\right).

Analogously, consider the remaining terms

T^{\prime}:=\sum_{t=1}^{\varepsilon n}f(n-k)(n-k)^{\beta^{\prime}}\cdot h(k)k^{\beta}=\varepsilon^{\beta+1}\cdot\mathcal{O}\left(f(n)h(n)n^{\beta^{\prime}+\beta+1}\right).

Comparing the estimates for
T
and
T^{\prime}
with (
4.32
) and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
we see that
S\sim I_{0}f(n)h(n)n^{\beta^{\prime}+\beta+1}
. Moreover, we readily see that
\tilde{h}(n)=I_{0}\cdot f(n)h(n)+o(f(n)h(n))
, which is obviously slowly varying as
f,h
are.

∎

Combining all these statements we are finally able to prove Lemma
3.7
.

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

For the ease of reading we repeat some definitions. For
p\in\mathbb{N}
let
K_{p}:=\sum_{1\leq i\leq p}C_{1,i}
and define
\nu:=\mathbb{E}[C_{1_{i}}]={x}_{n}C^{\prime}({x}_{n})/C({x}_{n})
. Further we need

\sigma_{p}^{2}:=\textrm{Var}[K_{p}]=p(x_{n}^{2}C^{\prime\prime}(x_{n})/C(x_{n})-\nu+\nu^{2})
.
Then (
3.12
),(
3.13
) assert that

\nu\sim\alpha\chi_{n}^{-1}
and
\sigma_{p}^{2}\sim\alpha p\chi_{n}^{-2}
.
We will use these properties several times without explicitly referencing them. Set
M:=p\nu+t\sigma_{p}
. Then

\mathrm{Pr}\left[L_{p}=\mu_{p}+t\sigma_{p}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[K_{p}=M\right]=[z^{M}]H(z)^{p},\qquad H(z)=\frac{C({x}_{n}z)}{C({x}_{n})}.

With Cauchy’s integral formula, where we integrate over an arbitrary closed curve encircling the origin,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[L_{p}=\mu_{p}+t\sigma_{p}\right]=\frac{C({x}_{n})^{-p}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}\oint\mathrm{e}^{f(z)}\frac{dz}{z},\qquad f(z)=p\ln C({x}_{n}z)-M\ln z.

(4.33)

Since we will need that several times, let us note that

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(z)=p\frac{x_{n}C^{\prime}(x_{n}z)}{C(x_{n}z)}-\frac{M}{z},\quad f^{\prime\prime}(z)=p\left(\frac{{x}_{n}^{2}C^{\prime\prime}({x}_{n}z)}{C({x}_{n}z)}-\left(\frac{{x}_{n}C^{\prime}({x}_{n}z)}{C({x}_{n}z)}\right)^{2}\right)+\frac{M}{z^{2}}\quad\text{and}\\
f^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)=p\left(\frac{{x}_{n}^{3}C^{\prime\prime\prime}({x}_{n}z)}{C({x}_{n}z)}-3\frac{{x}_{n}^{3}C^{\prime\prime}({x}_{n}z)C^{\prime}({x}_{n}z)}{C({x}_{n}z)^{2}}+\left(\frac{{x}_{n}C^{\prime}({x}_{n}z)}{C({x}_{n}z)}\right)^{3}\right)-2\frac{M}{z^{3}}.\end{split}

(4.34)

The subsequent proof follows a very clear route that is strewn with several technical statements (as it is typical in this area). These statements, which have self-contained proofs are clearly marked, and the proofs are presented at the end of the section.
We have in mind to apply the saddle-point method, that is, we will split the integral in (
4.33
) up into one dominating part, where a quadratic expansion of
f(z)
is valid, and one negligible part. To this end, consider the saddle-point equation

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(z)=0\penalty\ \Leftrightarrow\penalty\ p\frac{{x}_{n}zC^{\prime}({x}_{n}z)}{C({x}_{n}z)}=M.

(4.35)

This equation has obviously a unique solution that we call
w\equiv w(n,p,t)
, the saddle-point. Note that
w=1
if
t=0
. We claim that in general
w
satisfies

\displaystyle w=\mathrm{e}^{\xi},\quad\text{where}\quad\xi=t\sigma_{p}^{-1}+\xi_{1},\quad\xi_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(t^{2}p^{-1}\chi_{n}\right)

(SaddleAsym)

uniformly for all
t=o(p^{1/6})
; the self-contained proof is at the end of the section. We proceed by specifying in (
4.33
) the curve over which integrate. We choose the simplest curve that passes the saddle point, i.e., the circle with radius
w
. By switching to polar coordinates

\displaystyle\frac{C({x}_{n})^{-p}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}\oint\mathrm{e}^{f(z)}\frac{dz}{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{C({x}_{n})^{-p}}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{e}^{f(w\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})}d\theta.

(4.36)

In the next step we study
f(w\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})
by considering the Taylor series around
\theta=0
. Then
f^{\prime}(w)=0
guarantees that
f(w\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})=f(w)-w^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(w)\theta^{2}/2+R
for some remainder
R
that should be negligible in an appropriate interval around
0
.
Let us substantiate this. Set for the remainder of the proof

\eta:=\chi_{n}-\xi\sim\chi_{n},\qquad\theta_{0}:=p^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\eta\textrm{ for some }0<\varepsilon<1/6.

By applying (
4.24
) to (
4.34
) we obtain

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(w)\sim\alpha p\eta^{-2}=\Theta(p\eta^{-2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad f^{\prime\prime\prime}(w)=\mathcal{O}\left(p\eta^{-3}\right),

(4.37)

so that the saddle-point heuristic
f^{\prime\prime}(w)\theta_{0}^{2}=\omega(1)
and
f^{\prime\prime\prime}(w)\theta_{0}^{3}=o(1)
is fulfilled. We claim that a Gaussian expansion holds for
f(w\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})
as
\eta\to 0
uniformly in
\lvert\theta\rvert<\theta_{0}
, that is, for
t=o(p^{1/6})
,

\displaystyle f(w\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})=f(w)-w^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(w){\theta^{2}}/{2}+o(1),

(GaussExp)

the proof of which is at the end of this section.
With this at hand, we split up (
4.36
) into the integral over the – as we will establish, dominant – arc
\{w\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta}:-\theta_{0}\leq\theta\leq\theta_{0}\}
and the remainder, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[L_{p}=\mu_{p}+t\sigma_{p}\right]=\frac{C({x}_{n})^{-p}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}\oint\mathrm{e}^{f(z)}\frac{dz}{z}=\frac{C({x}_{n})^{-p}}{2\pi}\left(\int_{-\theta_{0}}^{\theta_{0}}+\int_{\theta_{0}\leq\lvert\theta\rvert\leq\pi}\right)\mathrm{e}^{f(w\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})}d\theta=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

(4.38)

We start with
I_{1}
. Since
\theta_{0}
is such that
\theta_{0}^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(w)\to\infty
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{-y^{2}/2}dy=\sqrt{2\pi}
we obtain due to (

)

\displaystyle I_{1}\sim\frac{C({x}_{n})^{-p}}{2\pi}\int_{-\theta_{0}}^{\theta_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{f(w)-w^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(w)\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}}d\theta\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{C({x}_{n}w)}{C({x}_{n})}\right)^{p}w^{-M}\frac{1}{\sqrt{w^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(w)}}.

(4.39)

We claim that for
t=o(p^{1/6})
the first order of
I_{1}
– the alleged dominating integral – satisfies

\displaystyle I_{1}\sim e^{-{t^{2}}/{2}}(2\pi w^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(w))^{-1/2}.

(DomInt)

Due to (
4.37
) and using that
w\sim 1
as well as
\eta\sim\chi_{n}
we obtain that
w^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(w)\sim\alpha p\chi_{n}^{-2}\sim\sigma_{p}^{2}
. Plugging this into (

) we conclude that

I_{1}\sim\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{p}}\sim\mathrm{e}^{-{t^{2}}/{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\chi_{n}}{\sqrt{p\alpha}}.

This establishes the claimed first order asymptotic of
\mathrm{Pr}\left[K_{p}=M\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[L_{p}=\mu_{p}+t\sigma_{p}\right]
. Hence, to finish the proof, we need to show that
I_{2}
in (
4.38
) is negligible compared to
I_{1}
. We first reformulate

\displaystyle I_{2}=\left(\frac{C({x}_{n}w)}{C({x}_{n})}\right)^{p}w^{-M}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\theta_{0}\leq\lvert\theta\rvert\leq\pi}G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta M}d\theta,\qquad G(y)=\frac{C({x}_{n}wy)}{C({x}_{n}w)}.

(4.40)

In light of (
4.39
) and (
4.37
), in order to show that
I_{2}=o(I_{1})
we just need to show that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\pi}G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta M}d\theta\right\rvert=o\left(\eta p^{-1/2}\right).

(4.41)

We first consider the case
\alpha>1
.
With Lemma
4.5
we obtain that there are

such that

\displaystyle\lvert G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})\rvert\leq\begin{cases}1-\alpha\left({\theta}/{\eta}\right)^{2}/2,&\theta\leq b\eta\\
c,&\theta\geq b\eta\\
A\max\{\eta/|\theta|,\eta\},&\theta=\omega(\eta)\end{cases}.

(4.42)

Applying the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\pi}G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta M}d\theta\right\rvert\leq\left(\int_{\theta_{0}}^{b\eta}+\int_{b\eta}^{\min\{\pi,\eta\ln p\}}+\int_{\min\{\pi,\eta\ln p\}}^{\pi}\right)\lvert G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})\rvert^{p}d\theta=:R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}.

Due to (
4.41
) all that is left to show in order to obtain

is

\displaystyle R_{i}=o\left(\eta p^{-1/2}\right),\qquad i=1,2,3.

(4.43)

Applying the first inequality of (
4.42
) and

for sufficiently small

entails that

\displaystyle R_{1}\leq\int_{\theta_{0}}^{b\eta}\exp\left\{-\frac{p\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{\eta}\right)^{2}\right\}d\theta=\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{p\alpha}}\int_{\sqrt{\alpha}p^{\varepsilon}}^{b\sqrt{\alpha}p^{1/2}}\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/2}dx=o\left(\eta p^{-1/2}\right)

showing (
4.43
) for
i=1
.
We proceed to
R_{2}
, where we apply the second inequality in (
4.42
) to obtain

R_{2}\leq\eta\ln p\cdot c^{p}=({\eta}p^{-1/2})\cdot(p^{1/2}\ln p\cdot c^{p}).

Since

this expression is
o(\eta p^{-1/2})
. For the remaining case we apply the third inequality in (
4.42
) to obtain that

\displaystyle R_{3}\leq\left(\int_{\min\{1,\eta\ln p\}}^{1}+\int_{1}^{\pi}\right)\lvert G(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta})\rvert^{p}d\theta\leq(A\eta)^{p}\int_{\min\{1,\eta\ln p\}}^{1}\theta^{-p}d\theta+\pi\cdot(C\eta)^{p}.

(4.44)

Then, as

,

\displaystyle 0\leq(A\eta)^{p}\int_{\eta\ln p}^{1}\theta^{-p}d\theta=(A\eta)^{p}\left[-\frac{\theta^{-p+1}}{p}\right]_{\eta\ln p}^{1}\leq\frac{A^{p}\eta(\ln p)^{-p+1}}{p}\leq\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{p}}\cdot\left(\frac{A}{\ln p}\right)^{p}=o\left({\eta}p^{-1/2}\right).

Hence all the terms on the right hand side of (
4.44
) are in
o(\eta p^{-1/2})
validating (
4.43
) for
i=3
. We have just demonstrated the validity of (
4.43
) for
i=1,2,3
and thus the assertion of Lemma
3.7
is fully proven for
\alpha>1
.

In the case

. Instead of (

Now

As

).

Let
A>0
be a (large) constant. For

we obtain that

. Thus

Appendix: Slowly Varying Functions

Let
h:[1,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a slowly varying function, that is,
h
is measurable and for any
\lambda>0

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{h(\lambda x)}{h(x)}=1.

“Slowly varying” means essentially smaller than any polynomial, see also below in (
A.2
) for formal variants of this statement. All the results in this section for slowly varying strictly positive

do straightforwardly hold for
h[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that
h
is eventually positive and (
A.1
) is valid.

Results on Slowly Varying Functions

The theory presented in this chapter goes back to Jovan Karamata, who proved all the basic results in his works
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
. A thorough overview can be found in the comprehensive textbooks
[
6
]
or
[
30
, Chapter IV]
. Let us begin with the
Uniform Convergence Theorem
that will be useful later.

Theorem A.1

(
[
6
, Thm. 1.2.1]
)
.

The convergence in (
A.1
) is uniform for
\lambda
in any compact subset of
(0,\infty)
.

Let us continue with the famous
Representation Theorem
, first obtained by
[
27
]
in the continuous setting and again for arbitrary measurable functions by
[
31
]
, c.f.
[
6
, Theorem 1.3.1]
. It states that there exist bounded measurable functions
c(x)
and
\varepsilon(x)
such that

h(x)=c(x)\exp\left(\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\varepsilon(t)}{t}dt\right),

where, for some
c>0

c(x)\to c\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varepsilon(x)\to 0\quad\text{ as }x\to\infty.

From this we immediately obtain for any
\delta>0
that there is an
{x}_{n}
such that

x^{-\delta}\leq h(x)\leq x^{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad\Big(\frac{x}{x^{\prime}}\Big)^{\delta}\leq\frac{h(x^{\prime})}{h(x)}\leq\Big(\frac{x^{\prime}}{x}\Big)^{\delta}\quad\text{ for all }x^{\prime}\geq x\geq{x}_{n}.

(A.2)

Moreover, see
[
6
, Theorem 1.5.3]
, we obtain that for any
\mu>0

\sup_{1\leq y\leq x}h(y)y^{\mu}\sim h(x)x^{\mu}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{y\geq x}h(y)y^{-\mu}\sim h(x)x^{-\mu}\qquad\text{ as }x\to\infty.

(A.3)

Let
\alpha>0
. From here we consider the function

c(s)=h(s)s^{\alpha-1},\quad s\geq 1,

where
h
is continuous. Note that this is no restriction in our setting as
c_{n}
given in (
1.2
) is only defined for natural numbers, such that we can simply interpolate linearly to obtain continuity.
We proceed with the following important result, known as
Karamata’s Theorem
.

Theorem A.2

(
[
6
, Prop. 1.5.8]
)
.

Let
h
be slowly varying
and
\alpha>0
. Then for any
a\geq 1

\int_{a}^{x}c(t)\textrm{d}t\sim\alpha^{-1}\,h(x)\,x^{\alpha},\quad\text{as }x\to\infty.

We will be interested in sums rather than integrals. A simple trick and the “sub-polynomiality” of slowly varying functions will help us here. We doubt that the following statement was not known before, but we know of no reference.

Corollary A.3
.

The previous theorem holds with
\int_{a}^{x}
replaced by
\sum_{a}^{x-1}
for
a\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

The bounds in (
A.2
) guarantee that
c(s)^{-1}\sup_{0\leq x\leq 1}c(s+x)\leq\sup_{0\leq x\leq 1}(1-x/s)^{\alpha}=1
and also
c(x)^{-1}\inf_{0\leq x\leq 1}c(s+x)\geq\inf_{0\leq x\leq 1}(1+x/s)^{-1}\sim 1-x/s
for
s\to\infty
. Hence for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\bigg\lvert\sup_{0\leq x\leq 1}c(s+x)-\inf_{0\leq x\leq 1}c(s+x)\bigg\rvert\leq c(s)\varepsilon\penalty\ \text{ for all }\penalty\ s\in\mathbb{N},s\geq s_{0}.

(A.4)

This helps us in proving the claimed statement as follows. Note that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{x-1}c(s)-\int_{s_{0}}^{x}c(t)dt\right|\leq\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{x-1}\left|c(s)-\int_{s}^{s+1}c(t)dt\right|\leq\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{x-1}\bigg|\sup_{0\leq x\leq 1}c(s+x)-\inf_{0\leq x\leq 1}c(s+x)\bigg|.

By using (
A.4
) we infer that this sum is at most
\varepsilon\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{x-1}c(s)
. Hence

\left\lvert 1-\frac{\sum_{a}^{x-1}c(s)}{\int_{a}^{x}c(t)dt}\right\rvert\sim\left\lvert 1-\frac{\sum_{s_{0}}^{x-1}c(s)}{\int_{s_{0}}^{x}c(t)dt}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

As
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the proof is completed.
∎

Another ingredient in our proofs is the following property of sums, where the terms depend on some slowly varying function. Let

If

for all

, then

\hat{U}(\chi_{n})=\sum_{s\geq 0}U(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\chi_{n}s}.

(A.5)

The next result is referenced to as

and was derived in
[

]
.

Theorem A.4

(
[
6
, Thm. 1.7.1]
)

Let
