Linear Monte Carlo quadrature with optimal confidence intervals
Robert J. Kunsch

Abstract
We study the numerical integration of functions from isotropic Sobolev spaces W p s ​ ( [ 0 , 1 ] d ) W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d}) using finitely many function evaluations within randomized algorithms, aiming for the smallest possible probabilistic error guarantee ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 at confidence level 1 − δ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) 1-\delta\in(0,1) . For spaces consisting of continuous functions, non-linear Monte Carlo methods with optimal confidence properties have already been known, in few cases even linear methods that succeed in that respect. In this paper we promote a new method called stratified control variates (SCV) and by it show that already linear methods achieve optimal probabilistic error rates in the high smoothness regime without the need to adjust algorithmic parameters to the uncertainty δ \delta . We also analyse a version of SCV in the low smoothness regime where W p s ​ ( [ 0 , 1 ] d ) W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d}) may contain functions with singularities. Here, we observe a polynomial dependence of the error on δ − 1 \delta^{-1} which cannot be avoided for linear methods. This is worse than what is known to be possible using non-linear algorithms where only a logarithmic dependence on δ − 1 \delta^{-1} occurs if we tune in for a specific value of δ \delta .



Keywords.
Monte Carlo integration;
Sobolev functions;
information-based complexity;
linear methods;
asymptotic error;
confidence intervals.

1
Introduction

We want to compute the integral

\Int f=\int_{G}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathup{d}\boldsymbol{x}

(1)

of
f\colon G\to{\mathbb{R}}
from
a normed linear space
\mathcal{W}
of integrable functions
defined on a domain
G\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
in this paper we mainly restrict to the unit cube
G=[0,1]^{d}
.
The integral shall be approximated
using randomized
linear
quadrature rules,
that is, measurable mappings

Q_{n}\colon\Omega\times\mathcal{W}\to{\mathbb{R}}
of the shape

Q_{n}^{\omega}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{\omega}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\omega})\,,

(2)

where
\omega\in\Omega
represents the randomness
provided by a probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,{\mathbb{P}})
,
the nodes
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\omega}
shall be random variables on
the domain
G
,
and
w_{i}^{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}
are suitable weights.
We study the
probabilistic error
of such a method
for a given
confidence level

\delta\in(0,1)
(or
uncertainty

\delta
)
and an input
f\in\mathcal{W}
,
namely

e(Q_{n},\delta,f):=\inf\bigl\{\varepsilon>0\colon{\mathbb{P}}(|Q_{n}(f)-\Int f|>\varepsilon)\leq\delta\bigr\}\,.

(3)

The accuracy of
Q_{n}
on a given set
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{W}
is defined via the worst case,

e(Q_{n},\delta,\mathcal{F}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}e(Q_{n},\delta,f)\,,

(4)

where the input set
\mathcal{F}
is typically the unit ball of the function space
\mathcal{W}
,
that is,
\mathcal{F}=\{f\in\mathcal{W}\colon\|f\|_{\mathcal{W}}\leq 1\}
,
in which case we will simply write
\mathcal{W}
instead of
\mathcal{F}

on the left-hand side of (
4
).
Alternatively,
\mathcal{F}
could also consist of functions bounded by
1
with respect to a semi-norm in
\mathcal{W}
,
and indeed, the error bounds we prove in this paper equally hold for such larger input sets.
The cost of an algorithm (
2
) is dominated by the amount
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}

of function evaluations required by the method, the so-called
cardinality
.
Aiming for the best possible approximation, we thus take the infimum over all linear methods
Q_{n}
with cardinality
n
,

e^{\textup{linMC}}_{\textup{prob}}(n,\delta,\mathcal{F}):=\inf_{Q_{n}}e_{\textup{prob}}(Q_{n},\delta,\mathcal{F})\,.

(5)

If we allow general non-linear methods, we write
e^{\textup{MC}}_{\textup{prob}}(n,\delta,\mathcal{F})
;
restricting to deterministic methods without
\omega
-dependence
leads to a quantity
e^{\textup{det}}(n,\mathcal{F})
.

The error criterion (
3
) is common in statistics,
usually expressed in terms of confidence intervals:
If
\varepsilon\geq e(Q_{n},\delta,\mathcal{F})\in(0,\infty)
,
then
[Q_{n}(f)-\varepsilon,Q_{n}(f)+\varepsilon]
is a confidence interval
at confidence level
1-\delta

for the quantity
\Int(f)
, provided
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
In
information-based complexity
(IBC), though,
the standard notion of
Monte Carlo error

is the root mean squared error

\sqrt{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|Q_{n}(f)-\Int f|^{2}}
,
or simply the expected error
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|Q_{n}(f)-\Int f|
,
see for instance
[
17
,
20
]
.
The paper
[
14
]
, however, discussed an integration problem with non-convex input set
where positive results were only possible for the probabilistic error criterion,
it thus represents the more general yet challenging error criterion.
The subsequent publication
[
15
]
seems to be the first thorough study
on classical integration problems in terms of the probabilistic error.
There, mainly isotropic Sobolev spaces
W_{p}^{s}(G)

on domains
G\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
were considered. These are defined by

W_{p}^{s}(G):=\biggl\{f\in L_{p}(G)\,\bigg|\,\|f\|_{W_{p}^{s}(G)}:=\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}\\
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|_{1}\leq s\end{subarray}}\|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}f\|_{L_{p}(G)}^{p}\biggr)^{1/p}<\infty\biggr\}\,,

with integrability parameter
1\leq p\leq\infty

(with the usual modification for
p=\infty
)
and integer smoothness
s\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
imposing Lebesgue integrability for weak partial derivatives

D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}f=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{d}}^{\alpha_{d}}f

for multi-indices
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}

with total degree
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|_{1}=\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{d}
at most
s
.
Provided we have
sufficient smoothness,
s>d/p
,
hence guaranteeing continuity of functions,
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\hookrightarrow C([0,1]^{d})
,
the precise probabilistic error rate was determined in
[
15
, Thm 4]
, namely

e^{\textup{MC}}_{\textup{prob}}\left(n,\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)\asymp\begin{cases}n^{-s/d}\cdot\min\left\{1,\,\left(\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{n}\right)^{1-1/p}\right\}&\text{if }1\leq p<2\,,\\
n^{-s/d}\cdot\min\left\{1,\,\sqrt{\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{n}}\right\}&\text{if }p\geq 2\,.\end{cases}

(6)

These rates reveal the benefit from Monte Carlo compared to deterministic quadrature
in the regime
n\succ\log\delta^{-1}
.
Upper bounds were achieved by a variant of
control variates
(CV):
Half of the information budget is spent on an
L_{p^{\prime}}
-approximation
g

of the function
f
, where
p^{\prime}:=\min\{2,p\}
and the integral
\Int g
is known;
the other half of the budget is used on estimating the integral
of the
L_{q}
-residual
f-g
via a
non-linear
Monte Carlo method,
namely, the
median-of-means
(MoM),
which is a probability amplification scheme
applied to the standard Monte Carlo method, see (
29
) for details.
For smoothness
s=1
however,

stratified sampling
was shown to achieve optimal rates
without the need for non-linear algorithmic features.
Instead of employing a probability amplification scheme like the median in some stage of the algorithm,
for the analysis of stratified sampling Hoeffding’s inequality was used,
one of the best known concentration inequalities in probability theory.
This raises the general question in which cases linear Monte Carlo methods
have the potential of optimal confidence properties.

In the present article we give a positive result stating
that linear Monte Carlo methods achieve optimal probabilistic error rates
for Sobolev spaces
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
of continuous functions,
see Theorem
2.1
.
This is achieved by a new method we call
stratified control variates
(SCV)
which combines the two aforementioned classical variance reduction techniques,
see Section
2
for a detailed description.
The new approach exploits that a control variate
g
for
f
may locally have
much better approximation properties than globally,
say, the residual
f-g
may be bounded on sub-domains of
[0,1]^{d}

but on the whole domain we can only give an
L_{2}
-bound
with considerable approximation rates.
Besides linearity and unbiasedness, SCV has yet another main advantage
over the combination of control variates with the median-of-means (CV+MoM),
namely, SCV exhibits optimal confidence properties universally for all uncertainty levels
\delta
,
whereas the non-linear approach CV+MoM contains a parameter that needs to be adjusted to
\delta
:
In the MoM stage we take the median of
k\approx 2\log_{2}(2\delta)^{-1}
repetitions
of the standard Monte Carlo method applied to the residual
f-g
,
see
[
15
, Sec 3]
for details.

We also analyse SCV for the low smoothness regime
where
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
is not embedded in the space of continuous functions.
Here, the probabilistic error guarantees are worse than what is known to be possible with non-linear methods,
namely, for linear algorithms we find a polynomial dependence on
\delta^{-1}
.
In Section
3
we show that this worse tail behaviour cannot be avoided for linear methods,
yet, the precise asymptotics of the optimal joint
(n,\delta)
-dependence remains vague.

We close the paper with some numerical experiments comparing different integration methods
that use the same control variate,
see Section
4
.
The results underscore the superiority of our new method.

Asymptotic notation:

For functions
e,f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\times(0,1)\to{\mathbb{R}}

we use the notation

e(n,\delta)\preceq f(n,\delta)
,
meaning that there is some
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

e(n,\delta)\leq cf(n,\delta)

for all
n\geq n_{0}

and
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})

with some constant
c>0
that may depend on other parameters.
Asymptotic equivalence
e(n,\delta)\asymp f(n,\delta)

is a shorthand for
e(n,\delta)\preceq f(n,\delta)\preceq e(n,\delta)
.

2
Stratified control variates

This section is devoted to upper bounds for the numerical integration
of functions from isotropic Sobolev spaces
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
,
where
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
d,s\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
This is done by analysing a new algorithm we call
stratified control variates

which combines the well known ideas of control variates
and stratified sampling.

For the analysis we consider the usual Sobolev semi-norms

|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G)}:=\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}\\
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|_{1}=s\end{subarray}}\|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}f\|_{L_{p}(G)}^{p}\biggr)^{1/p}\,.

(7)

This semi-norm
|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G)}
is finite if and only if
f\in W_{p}^{s}(G)
,
its zero set is the space
\mathcal{P}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

of
d
-variate polynomials of total degree smaller than
s
,
that is,
|g|_{W_{p}^{s}(G)}=0
precisely for
g\in\mathcal{P}^{s}(G)
.
This polynomial space has the dimension

n_{0}=n_{0}(s,d):=\dim\mathcal{P}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=\binom{s+d-1}{d}\,.

(8)

Piecewise interpolation with such polynomials will
provide the control variate. There exists a so-called
s
-regular set

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}^{s}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\subset[0,1]^{d}

such that for any given values
\left(y_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}

there is exactly one polynomial
g\in\mathcal{P}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
satisfying
g(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})=y_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,n_{0}
,
see for instance
[
22
, Appendix A]
.
When dealing with continuous functions, i.e.
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\hookrightarrow C([0,1]^{d})
,
which holds for
s>d/p
, see
[
5
, Thm 4.12 (1)]
,
a key observation is that

\|f\|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}^{\prime}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}|f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})|+|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}

(9)

is an equivalent norm on
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
, see, for instance
[
8
, eq (3.1.11)]
.
We write
P_{\mathcal{X}}f
for the polynomial
g\in\mathcal{P}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

that interpolates the function values of
f
on
\mathcal{X}
,
i.e. for all
j=1,\ldots,n_{0}
we match
g(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
.
For the difference
f-P_{\mathcal{X}}f
we have

\|f-P_{\mathcal{X}}f\|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}^{\prime}=|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\,.

(10)

Since we assumed
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
to be continuously embedded into the space of continuous functions,
there exists a constant
c_{p,\infty}^{s,d}>0
such that

\|f-P_{\mathcal{X}}f\|_{L_{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leq c_{p,\infty}^{s,d}\,|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\,.

(11)

If, however, the function spaces contains discontinuous functions and we only have
the embedding

W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\hookrightarrow L_{q}([0,1]^{d})
for some
q\in(p,\infty)
,
namely for
\frac{1}{q}\geq\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}
, see
[
5
, Thm 4.12, eq (5)]
,
we need a randomly shifted point set

\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}:=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\right\}_{j=0}^{n_{0}}\subset[0,1]^{d}\qquad\text{with }\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\Uniform([0,1]^{d})\,.

(12)

From Heinrich
[
11
, eq (21)]
we know that for such a randomized interpolation we have the bound

\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\|f-P_{\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}f\|_{L_{q}([0,1]^{d})}^{q}\right)^{1/q}\leq c_{p,q}^{s,d}\,|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\,,

(13)

with a suitable constant
c_{p,q}^{s,d}>0
.
In what follows we describe the algorithm with randomly shifted point set
\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
,
keeping in mind that for spaces of continuous functions the deterministic point set
\mathcal{X}
can do the job as well.

The semi-norm
|\cdot|_{W_{p}^{s}(G)}
satisfies two important properties that are important
when decomposing the domain
[0,1]^{d}
into
m^{d}
essentially disjoint sub-cubes

G_{\boldsymbol{i}}=G_{m,\boldsymbol{i}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\frac{i_{j}}{m},\frac{i_{j}+1}{m}\right]

(14)

for
\boldsymbol{i}\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}^{d}=:[0:m)^{d}
, namely, the decomposition property

|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}=\left\|\left(|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\right)_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}\right\|_{\ell_{p}}\,,

(15)

and the scaling property

\left|f\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}}\right|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}=m^{d/p-s}|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\quad\text{where}\quad\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{i}}{m}\,.

(16)

We define (randomized) interpolates of
f
on the sub-cubes
G_{\boldsymbol{i}}
via

g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}:=\bigl(P_{\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(f\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}})\bigr)\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}}^{-1}\,.

(17)

The integrals of the local interpolation polynomials,

a_{\boldsymbol{i}}:=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}P_{\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(f\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}})\,\mathup{d}\boldsymbol{x}=m^{d}\int_{G_{\boldsymbol{i}}}g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathup{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,,

(18)

can be computed exactly and provide initial approximations
for the mean value of
f
on the sub-cubes
G_{\boldsymbol{i}}
.
Finally, we estimate the residual via stratified sampling
by taking independent samples
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(j)}\sim\Uniform(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})
that are also independent from
\boldsymbol{\xi}
,

stratified control variates
(SCV) is then defined as the linear and unbiased method

A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}(f):=\frac{1}{m^{d}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}\left(a_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}+\frac{1}{n_{0}(s,d)}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}(s,d)}[f-g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}](\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(j)})\right)\,.

(19)

This method requires
n=2n_{0}m^{d}
function evaluations of
f
.
The decision to take
n_{0}
random samples on each sub-cube
G_{\boldsymbol{i}}

follows the usual heuristic for control variates to evenly distribute the information budget
on the two stages of approximating
f
and of estimating the residual.
Stratified control variates can be described as the strategy of averaging
the means of
f
on subcubes
G_{\boldsymbol{i}}
, the so-called
strata
,
where the means on the individual strata
G_{\boldsymbol{i}}
are approximated via
a basic control variates method with fixed cardinality.
Note that we use the same shift
\boldsymbol{\xi}
on all subcubes.

The first theorem gives upper bounds for spaces of continuous functions.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
s,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
with
s>d/p
.
Then there exists a family
(Q_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

of linear randomized quadrature rules that achieve optimal probabilistic error rates,
namely, for
n\geq 2n_{0}(s,d)
and all
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
we have

\displaystyle e\left(Q_{n},\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)

\displaystyle\asymp e_{\textup{prob}}^{\textup{linMC}}\left(n,\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)\asymp e_{\textup{prob}}^{\textup{MC}}\left(n,\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)

\displaystyle\asymp\begin{cases}n^{-s/d}\cdot\min\left\{1,\,\left(\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{n}\right)^{1-1/p}\right\}&\text{if }1\leq p<2\,,\\
n^{-s/d}\cdot\min\left\{1,\,\sqrt{\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{n}}\right\}&\text{if }p\geq 2\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

Lower bounds for arbitrary methods and
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
can be found in
[
15
, Thm 1]
.
For upper bounds we employ the SCV algorithm (
19
)
with
m:=\bigl\lfloor(\frac{n}{2n_{0}})^{1/d}\bigr\rfloor
.

The assumption
s/d>1/p
implies that
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\hookrightarrow C([0,1]^{d})
,
so we may confine ourselves to interpolation with a fixed point set
\mathcal{X}

and use a scaled version of (
11
)
with deterministic interpolants
g_{\boldsymbol{i}}
in place of (
17
).
In detail, using the scaling property (
16
), we get

\displaystyle b_{\boldsymbol{i}}

\displaystyle:=\|f-g_{\boldsymbol{i}}\|_{L_{\infty}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}

\displaystyle=\|f\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}}-P_{\mathcal{X}}(f\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}})\|_{L_{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{p,\infty}^{s,d}\,|f\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}}|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}

\displaystyle=c_{p,\infty}^{s,d}\,m^{-(s-d/p)}\,|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\,.

(20)

First, these bounds give a worst case bound on the error of SCV, namely, in combination with (
15
) we estimate

\displaystyle\left|A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}(f)-\Int f\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2m^{-d}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}b_{i}

\displaystyle\leq 2m^{-d/p}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}b_{i}^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{p,\infty}^{s,d}\,m^{-s}\,|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\,.

(21)

The bounds (
20
) on the range of the residual estimators also play a role in Hoeffding’s inequality
by which we find the probabilistic bound

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\left|A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}(f)-\Int f\right|>\varepsilon\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n_{0}^{2}m^{2d}\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}b_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\delta\,.

Resolving for
\varepsilon
, we obtain the error bound

\varepsilon=\frac{1}{n_{0}m^{d}}\cdot\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)}b_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2}}\,.

This is useful for
p\geq 2
, where with (
15
) and (
20
) we estimate

\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}b_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq m^{d(1/2-1/p)}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}b_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq c_{p,\infty}^{s,d}\,m^{-(s-d/2)}\,|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\,,

For
1\leq p<2
we use an alternative Hoeffding type inequality, see Lemma
A.1
, providing the error bound

\varepsilon=\frac{3}{n_{0}m^{d}}\cdot\left(2\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{1-1/p}\cdot\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)}b_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\,,

where (
15
) and (
20
), again,
imply an appropriate relation to the Sobolev norm,

\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}b_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq c_{p,\infty}^{s,d}\,m^{-(s-d/p)}\,|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\,.

Writing
p^{\prime}:=\min\{2,p\}
to accommodate both regimes,
for
f
from the unit ball of
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
we thus get
the following bound holding with probability
1-\delta\in(0,1)
:

\left|A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}(f)-\Int f\right|\leq\frac{c_{p,\infty}^{s,d}}{n_{0}}\cdot m^{-s-d(1-1/p^{\prime})}\cdot\left(2\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{1-1/p^{\prime}}\,.

With
n\asymp m^{d}
we achieve the randomized rates as claimed,
where (
21
) is the bound we fall back to for very small
\delta
.
∎

Remark 2.2

(Stratified sampling)
.

For spaces
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
of smoothness
s=1
and integrability
d>p
,
stratified sampling without control variates
already yields optimal probabilistic error rates.
This was shown in
[
15
, Thm 6]

for integrability
p\geq 2
,
in the case of low integrability
1<p<2
, though,
namely spaces
W_{p}^{1}([0,1])
of univariate functions,
a gap in the power of
\log\delta^{-1}
remained open.
With the help of the new
p
-norm version of Hoeffding’s inequality, see Lemma
A.1
,
this gap can be closed with the lines following the proof of Theorem
2.1
,
implying that stratified sampling is optimal in that case as well.

The second theorem gives probabilistic integration rates for spaces of functions that are not necessarily continuous.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
s,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
1\leq p<\infty
with
s<d/p
.
Then there exists a family
(Q_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

of linear randomized quadrature rules that for
n\geq 2n_{0}(s,d)
and all
\delta\in(0,1)

achieve the following probabilistic error rates:

\displaystyle e_{\textup{prob}}^{\textup{linMC}}\left(n,\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)

\displaystyle\leq e\left(Q_{n},\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)

\displaystyle\preceq\begin{cases}n^{-\left(\frac{s}{d}+1-\frac{1}{p}\right)}\cdot\delta^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}\right)}&\text{if }1\leq p\leq 2\,,\\
n^{-\left(\frac{s}{d}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\cdot\delta^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}\right)}&\text{if }2\leq p<\infty\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

We employ the SCV algorithm (
19
) with
m:=\bigl\lfloor(\frac{n}{2n_{0}})^{1/d}\bigr\rfloor

and randomized interpolation.
In what follows, we write
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
for the expectation that averages over the random shift
\boldsymbol{\xi}
,
while
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\,\cdot\,|\boldsymbol{\xi}]
is the conditional expectation for fixed
\boldsymbol{\xi}
,
hence, producing a random variable derived from
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

For
q>p
with
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}>0

the embedding
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\hookrightarrow L_{q}([0,1]^{d})
holds
and we may apply (
13
).
Using randomized interpolants
g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}
on sub-domains
G_{\boldsymbol{i}}
, see (
17
),
the scaling property (
16
) leads to

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|f-g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{L_{q}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=m^{-d/q}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|f\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}}-P_{\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(f\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}})\|_{L_{q}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq c_{p,q}^{s,d}\,m^{-d/q}\,|f\circ\Phi_{m,\boldsymbol{i}}|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}

\displaystyle=c_{p,q}^{s,d}\,m^{d\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)-s}\,|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\,.

(22)

In order to estimate the
L_{q}
-error of the SCV algorithm,
we need to understand the
q
-th moment of the random variables

Z_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(j)}:=[f-g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}](\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(j)})

with

\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bigl|Z_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(j)}\bigr|^{q}\middle|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right]\right)^{1/q}=m^{d/q}\,\|f-g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{L_{q}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\,.

For any fixed random shift
\boldsymbol{\xi}
these random variables are independent
and the algorithm is unbiased, hence, we can
apply Lemma
A.3
.
Writing
q^{\prime}:=\min\{2,q\}
we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl[\bigl|A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}(f)-\Int f\bigr|^{q}\Big|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right]\Bigr)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{q}}{n_{0}m^{d}}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bigl|Z_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(j)}\bigr|^{q}\middle|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right]\right)^{q^{\prime}/q}\right)^{1/{q^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=c_{q}\cdot n_{0}^{-(1-1/q^{\prime})}\cdot m^{-d(1-1/q)}\,\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}\|f-g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{L_{q}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}^{q^{\prime}}\right)^{1/q^{\prime}}\,.

(23)

We also need the
q
-th moment of (
23
)
with respect to the expectation over the random shift
\boldsymbol{\xi}

in order to establish relations to the local semi-norms via (
22
).
For
q^{\prime}=q\leq 2
this causes no problems,
but for
q^{\prime}=2<q
it requires the use of Lemma
A.2

with random variables
Y_{\boldsymbol{i}}:=\|f-g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{L_{q}(G_{i})}
,
thus

\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}\|f-g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{L_{q}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}^{q^{\prime}}\right)^{q/q^{\prime}}\right)^{1/q}\leq\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}^{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|f-g_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{L_{q}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}^{q}\right)^{q^{\prime}/q}\right)^{1/q^{\prime}}\\
\leq c_{p,q}^{s,d}\,m^{d\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)-s}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}^{q^{\prime}}\right)^{1/q^{\prime}}\,.

(24)

Consider
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
.
If
p<2
, we have
p<q^{\prime}
, hence,
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\ell_{q^{\prime}}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\ell_{p}}
.
If
p\geq 2
, we have
q^{\prime}=2
, hence,

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\ell_{q^{\prime}}}=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq M^{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}\right)}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\ell_{p}}
.
Writing
p^{\prime}:=\min\{2,p\}
, with

\boldsymbol{x}=\bigl(|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\bigr)_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}

and
M=m^{d}
, and with the help of the decomposition property (
15
),
we estimate

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}^{q^{\prime}}\right)^{1/q^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq m^{d\left(\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{p}\right)}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[0:m)^{d}}|f|_{W_{p}^{s}(G_{\boldsymbol{i}})}^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=m^{d\left(\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{p}\right)}\,|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\,.

(25)

For inputs from the unit ball, that is,
|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\leq\|f\|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\leq 1
,
combining (
23
), (
24
), and (
25
),
we then have

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\bigl|A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}(f)-\Int f\bigr|^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{c_{q}\,c_{p,q}^{s,d}\cdot n_{0}^{-(1-1/q^{\prime})}}_{=:C_{p,q}^{s,d}}\cdot\,m^{-s-d(1-1/p^{\prime})}\,.

Via Markov’s inequality, for an error threshold
\varepsilon>0

we get the following bound on the failure probability:

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\bigl|A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}(f)-\Int f\bigr|>\varepsilon\right\}\leq\left(C_{p,q}^{s,d}\cdot m^{-s-d(1-1/p^{\prime})}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}\right)^{q}\,.

This is guaranteed to be no bigger than a given
\delta\in(0,1)
for

\varepsilon=C_{p,q}^{s,d}\cdot m^{-s-d(1-1/p^{\prime})}\cdot\delta^{-1/q}\,.

With
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}
and
n\asymp m^{d}
, we obtain the desired bounds.
∎

Remark 2.4

(Comparison with non-linear methods)
.

The above theorem gives error bounds of the shape

e_{\textup{prob}}^{\textup{linMC}}(n,\delta)\preceq n^{-\varrho}\cdot\delta^{-r}\,,

where the main rate is a number
\varrho\in(0,1)
,
and the tail of the error distribution becomes thinner the larger the regularity,
namely,
r\searrow 0
for, say,
p\nearrow d/s
with fixed
d
and
s
.
A small value of
r
close to
0
is desirable
as this means that the size of the confidence intervals grows at a slower rate
when we decrease the acceptable uncertainty
\delta
.

Recall that applying, for instance, the median trick
to a given family
(Q_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of linear methods can further reduce the dependence on
\delta^{-1}
,
in our situation we would find

e_{\textup{prob}}^{\textup{MC}}(n,\delta)\preceq\left(\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{n}\right)^{\varrho}\,,

see
[
15
, Thm 2]
.
For this we need
k\asymp\log\delta^{-1}
independent repetitions of the linear method
Q_{n_{1}}
of which we take the median,
the resulting method is non-linear and uses
n=kn_{1}
samples.
The optimal
\delta
-dependence for non-linear methods in the low smoothness regime, though, remains an open problem.

Remark 2.5
.

The SCV algorithm is exact on the space
\mathcal{P}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
of polynomials of total degree less than
s
.
Furthermore, the bounds in Theorems
2.1
and
2.3
still hold
if, instead of the classical unit ball in
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
, we consider the larger input set

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:=\left\{f\in W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\colon|f|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\leq 1\right\}\,.

Restricting to functions from the unit ball, however,
we would have

|\Int f|\leq\|f\|_{L_{p}([0,1]^{d})}\leq\|f\|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\leq 1\,,

hence, the zero algorithm
A_{0}(f)=0
would have an error of at most
1
,
the so-called
initial error
.
An error bound of the shape
n^{-\varrho}\cdot\delta^{-r}
as it is given in Theorem
2.3

would exceed the initial error for very small uncertainty levels
\delta\prec n^{-\varrho/r}
.
Hence, instead of performing SCV one could simply return
0
.
We might therefore give an upper bound

e_{\textup{prob}}^{\textup{linMC}}(n,\delta)\preceq\min\left\{1,\,n^{-\varrho}\cdot\delta^{-r}\right\}\,.

The zero algorithm is linear but it introduces a bias, further, we need to decide,
depending on
\delta
,
whether we use SCV or just return
0
.
The rates given by the Theorem, however, hold for SCV without the need to adjust anything to
\delta
.
Besides, the trick of returning
0
in order to reduce the error
whenever a small uncertainty
\delta
is given,
does not work if we consider the larger input set
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
with the semi-norm bound.
Furthermore, SCV is a method with good confidence properties for any
\delta

without the need to adjust the algorithm
(the only decision is to be made on the smoothness
s
we aim to exploit,
which is determined by the degree of interpolation).
As Theorem
3.1
will show,
fixing a method for given cardinality
n
,
the
\delta
-dependence we obtained for SCV is of optimal order
among all linear methods.
Finally, at least for low integrability
1\leq p\leq 2
,
we find matching lower bounds for the
joint
(n,\delta)
-dependence of the
error of SCV, see Theorem
3.3
.

For the sake of completeness we provide a result for boundary smoothness.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
s,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
1<p<\infty
with
s=d/p
.
Then there exists a family
(Q_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

of linear randomized quadrature rules
such that for
n\geq 2n_{0}(s,d)

we achieve probabilistic error rates
with sub-polynomial dependence on
\delta^{-1}
,
namely, for all
r>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
we have

\displaystyle e_{\textup{prob}}^{\textup{linMC}}\left(n,\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)

\displaystyle\leq e\left(Q_{n},\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)

\displaystyle\preceq\begin{cases}n^{-1}\cdot\delta^{-r}&\text{if }1<p\leq 2\,,\\
n^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\cdot\delta^{-r}&\text{if }2\leq p<\infty\,,\end{cases}

where the implicit constant depends on
r
, as well as on the space parameters
p
,
s
, and
d
.

Proof.

If
s=d/p
, then
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\hookrightarrow L_{q}([0,1]^{d})
for all
q<\infty
.
We follow the lines in the proof of Theorem
2.3
with
r=\frac{1}{q}
for
q\in[2,\infty)

and achieve the claimed bounds for
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.
The bounds for larger
r
are trivial.
∎

Remark 2.7

(Integrability
p=1
)
.

The case of integrability
p=1
and
s=d
was not included in Corollary
2.6

because there deterministic quadrature rules already achieve a rate
that cannot be improved by randomization:

e_{\textup{prob}}^{\textup{linMC}}\bigl(n,\delta,W_{1}^{s}([0,1]^{s})\bigr)\asymp e^{\textup{det}}\bigl(n,W_{1}^{s}([0,1]^{s})\bigr)\asymp n^{-1}\,.

For this statement we can make use of the embedding
W_{1}^{s}([0,1]^{s})\hookrightarrow C([0,1]^{s})
,
which is a special case mentioned in
[
5
, Thm 4.12, eq (1)]
,
and the proof of Theorem
2.1
contains all the subsequent arguments for the worst case guarantee.

The case of
p=1
and
s<d
, however, is contained in Theorem
2.3

because for spaces of discontinuous functions
deterministic quadrature cannot provide any worst case guarantee whatsoever
while probabilistic guarantees are still possible in the randomized setting.

Remark 2.8

(Randomized interpolation for high smoothness)
.

We discussed two versions of stratified control variates:
One with deterministic interpolation for spaces of continuous functions,
one with randomly shifted interpolation nodes in the low smoothness regime.
In applications one might not know the precise class an integrand belongs to,
so it is desirable to have a method that equally works in all settings.
In fact, the latter version of SCV with random interpolation
could also be applied in the high smoothness regime without any loss in the order of convergence.
Indeed, for spaces
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\hookrightarrow C([0,1]^{d})
,
the minimal constant in (
11
) depends continuously
on the shift
\boldsymbol{\xi}
when taking a point set
\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
instead of
\mathcal{X}
.
This is because for high smoothness
s>d/p

we can even find a Hölder exponent
\beta\in(0,1)
such that
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\hookrightarrow C^{\beta}([0,1]^{d})
,
namely
\beta\leq s-d/p
, see
[
5
, Thm 4.12, eq (7)]
.
Since the random shift
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is from a compact domain,
there exists a universal constant such that (
11
) holds
for all point sets
\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
with this constant.

Remark 2.9

(Other function spaces)
.

The approach of using polynomial interpolation to reduce the function space norm to a semi norm
with nice scaling and decomposition properties also works for more general function spaces,
see for instance
[
22
, Appendix A]
concerning Bezov spaces.
The semi-norm representation of Bezov spaces in
[
22
]
suggests
that even if the local polynomial interpolation we use is of degree
s
or higher,
we still obtain the same order for
W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
, potentially with worse constants though.
Spaces of dominating mixed smoothness pose yet another challenge
and it would be interesting to see what can be achieved with a method in the spirit of SCV,
see
[
15
, Sec 4]
for an introductory discussion of such spaces
in the context of the probabilistic error criterion.

Remark 2.10

(Other domains)
.

The idea of stratified control variates (SCV) is not restricted to rectangular domains.
If we have a triangulation of a domain
G
,
provided a bound on the maximal side-length of each simplex
G_{i}\subset G
,
we may find a scaling property similar to (
16
).
The final method will also need to take into account small variations
in the volume of different simplices.

3
Lower bounds for low smoothness

So far we only discussed algorithms
Q_{n}
with fixed cardinality
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
One might also consider algorithms with varying cardinality
\widetilde{n}(\omega)

where
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\widetilde{n}(\omega)\leq n
is the constraint we impose,
and this additional freedom is indeed present in many randomized integration methods,
see for instance
[
12
,
21
,
13
,
16
,
18
]
.
As discussed in
[
15
, Sec 2.1]
, however,
restricting to fixed cardinality is no major constraint.
Fixed cardinality facilitates the discussion of lower bounds.

Lower bounds for integration methods in Sobolev spaces are usually found
with the help of so-called bump functions,
for Sobolev spaces
W_{p}^{s}
of smoothness
s\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
one may define
a basic bump
\psi_{0}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as

\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\begin{cases}\left(1-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\right)^{s}&\text{for }\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\ell_{2}}:=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{d}^{2}}\leq 1,\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

For this function we have

1\geq\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{s}=:b_{0}>0\qquad\text{for $\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}$\,,}

and a positive integral (computable in polar coordinates using the Beta function),

\gamma_{0}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathup{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\frac{\Gamma(s+1)\cdot\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+s+1)}>0\,.

Further, partial derivatives
D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\psi_{0}
are continuous (in particular at the boundary)
up to the order
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|_{1}\leq s-1
, and still bounded and existing in a weak sense for
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|_{1}=s
,
hence,
\psi_{0}\in W_{p}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with a finite norm

c_{0}=c_{0}(p,s,d):=\|\psi_{0}\|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}\in(0,\infty)\,.

Taking a scaling parameter
\sigma>0
and a shift
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

we define

\psi_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{c_{0}}\,\sigma^{-(d/p-s)}\,\psi_{0}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}}{\sigma}\right)\,,

(26)

and thanks to proper normalisation we have
\|\psi_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{W_{p}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq 1
,
compare the scaling property for semi norms (
16
).
The fact that in the low smoothness regime
s<d/p
we have

\psi_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{1}{c_{0}}\cdot\sigma^{-(d/p-s)}\xrightarrow[\sigma\to 0]{}\infty\,,

reflects the potential unboundedness of functions from
W_{p}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
This stands in contrast to the shrinking integral of such a bump,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathup{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\frac{\gamma_{0}}{c_{0}}\cdot\sigma^{s+d\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)}\xrightarrow[\sigma\to 0]{}0\,.

(27)

We start with a negative result on the
\delta
-dependence of the probabilistic error
for any fixed linear method
Q_{n}

which inevitably will be polynomial in
\delta^{-1}
in the case of low smoothness.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
s,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
1\leq p<\infty
with
s<d/p
.
Then, for any linear randomized quadrature rule
Q_{n}\neq 0
that uses
n
function values,
there exist constants
c_{n}>0
and
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that
for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
we have

e\left(Q_{n},\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)\geq c_{n}\cdot\delta^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}\right)}\,.

Proof.

Let the method
Q_{n}
be given as in (
2
).
For
t>0
define the random index set

I^{\omega}_{t}:=\left\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\colon|w_{i}^{\omega}|\geq t\right\}\,.

Since
Q_{n}\neq 0
there exists a threshold
t_{0}>0
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\#I_{t_{0}}]>0
.
Fix this parameter
t_{0}
and for
r>0
define the random set

J^{\omega}_{r}:=\Bigl\{i\in I^{\omega}_{t_{0}}\colon\sup_{j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\setminus\{i\}}\widetilde{\dist}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\omega})\geq r\Bigr\}\,,

where
\widetilde{\dist}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{k}\|_{\ell_{2}}

is the so-called “wrap-around distance” on the
d
-dimensional torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,
that is the periodization of
[0,1]^{d}
with opposing faces glued together, identifying the coordinates
0
and
1
.
We may assume that for
i\neq j
the two integration nodes
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\omega}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{\omega}

are almost surely distinct.
(If not,
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{\omega}
could be moved elsewhere and assigned
a new weight
\widetilde{w}_{j}^{\omega}=0

while the new weight for
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\omega}
is
\widetilde{w}_{i}^{\omega}:=w_{i}^{\omega}+w_{j}^{\omega}
.)
Hence, there exists an
r_{0}>0
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\#J_{r_{0}}]>0
.

For
0<\varrho\leq\min\{\frac{r_{0}}{3},\frac{1}{4}\}

let
\Xi_{\varrho}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})_{j=1}^{M(\varrho)}
be a
\varrho
-cover of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,
that is, the union of the
\varrho
-balls

B_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}):=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\colon\widetilde{\dist}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})<\varrho\right\}

covers the entirety of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}=[0,1]^{d}
.
It is well known that there exists a
\varrho
-cover such that the
\varrho/2
-balls
B_{\varrho/2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})

do not overlap.
Volume estimates show that such a
\varrho
-covering has the size

M(\varrho)\leq V_{d}\cdot\left(\frac{2}{\varrho}\right)^{d}\,,

where
V_{d}
is the volume of the
d
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball.
(On the
d
-torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
the volume of balls up to radius
\frac{1}{2}
is the same as in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.)
By definition of the (random) index set
J_{r_{0}}
, and since
r_{0}>2\varrho
,
for each
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}
we know that the random variable

Y_{j}^{\omega}(\varrho):=\#\left(B_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})\cap\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\omega}\mid i\in J_{r_{0}}^{\omega}\}\right)

takes only values
0
or
1
.
Adding things up, we find

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\#J_{r_{0}}]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M(\varrho)}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}Y_{j}(\varrho)\,,

hence there exists
j_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,M(\varrho)\}
such that

q(\varrho):={\mathbb{P}}\bigl\{Y_{j_{0}}(\varrho)=1\bigr\}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}Y_{j_{0}}(\varrho)\geq\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\#J_{r_{0}}]}{M(\varrho)}>q_{0}\cdot\varrho^{d}

with a suitable constant
q_{0}>0
.
If
Y_{j_{0}}^{\omega}(\varrho)=1
, let
i_{0}^{\omega}
denote the unique index
such that
\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{0}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{0}^{\omega}}^{\omega}\in B_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}})
,
in which case for all
j\neq i_{0}
we have
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{\omega}\notin B_{2\varrho}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}})

since

\widetilde{\dist}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}})\geq\widetilde{\dist}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{0}})-\widetilde{\dist}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{0}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}})\geq r_{0}-\varrho\geq 2\varrho\,.

Consider the periodized bump centred around
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}
with scaling
\sigma=2\varrho
,

\widetilde{\psi}_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\widetilde{\psi}_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}+\boldsymbol{k}}\,,

where for its restriction to
[0,1]^{d}
, of course, only the summation over
\boldsymbol{k}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
is relevant.
With
\sigma\leq\frac{1}{2}
, no overlap of shifted copies occurs,
so we preserve the norm estimate

\bigl\|\widetilde{\psi}_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}}\bigr\|_{W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})}=\bigl\|\psi_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}}\bigr\|_{W_{p}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq 1
,
as well as the integral value (
27
) which decays with
\sigma\to 0
.
In contrast, for the algorithm
Q_{n}
we can state
that with probability
q(\varrho)=q(\sigma/2)

the algorithm hits one of the large values of the bump multiplying it with a large weight,
and with other sample points it detects only zero values outside the support of the bump function,
in detail:

Y_{j_{0}}(\varrho)=Y_{j_{0}}(\sigma/2)=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bigl|Q_{n}(\widetilde{\psi}_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}})\bigr|\geq t_{0}\cdot\frac{b_{0}}{c_{0}}\cdot\sigma^{-(d/p-s)}\xrightarrow[\sigma\to 0]{}\infty\,.

Hence, there exists
0<\varrho_{0}\leq\min\{\frac{r_{0}}{3},\frac{1}{4}\}
such that

\bigl|Q_{n}(\widetilde{\psi}_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}})\bigr|\geq 2\Int\widetilde{\psi}_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}}

for
\varrho=\sigma/2\in(0,\varrho_{0}]
.
Define
\delta_{0}:=q_{0}\cdot\varrho_{0}^{d}
,
then for
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
,
putting
\varrho=\sqrt[d]{\delta/q_{0}}
,
we find that

\left|Q_{n}(\widetilde{\psi}_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}})-\Int\widetilde{\psi}_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j_{0}}}\right|\geq\frac{t_{0}b_{0}}{2c_{0}}\cdot\left(\frac{q_{0}}{2^{d}\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}}\,.

with probability greater than
\delta
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The theorem above shows that the
\delta
-dependence of the upper bound in Theorem
2.3

cannot be improved without choosing different methods for different
\delta
.
The lower bounds in
[
15
, Thm 1]
imply that
the
n
dependence of the error bound in Theorem
2.3
is also optimal,
that is,

e^{\textup{linMC}}_{\textup{prob}}\left(n,\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)\asymp n^{-\left(\frac{s}{d}+1-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\right)}\cdot c_{\delta}

with
p^{\prime}:=\min\{2,p\}
and
c_{\delta}>0
for
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
.
Of course, it would be desirable to have lower bounds that reflect the joint dependence
of the probabilistic error on
n
and
\delta
, at least for not too small values of
\delta
.
The following theorem provides such a lower bound
but only specifically for the SCV quadrature rule.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
s,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
1\leq p<\infty
with
s<d/p
.
Let
n_{0}=n_{0}(s,d)
as in (
8
) and consider
the respective SCV algorithm
A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}
for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
see (
19
).
Then there exists a constant
c=c(p,s,d,n_{0})>0
and
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)

such that for
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
and with
n=2n_{0}\,m^{d}
we have

e\left(A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}},\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)\succeq n^{-\left(\frac{s}{d}+1-\frac{1}{p}\right)}\cdot\delta^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}\right)}\,.

Proof.

We reuse some of the notation from the proof of Theorem
3.1

and skip details that are parallel.
Let
\boldsymbol{0}:=(0,\ldots,0)\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
denote the zero multi index.
Consider the bump function
\psi_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}

at
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=\left(\frac{1}{8m},\ldots,\frac{1}{8m}\right)

with scaling
0<\sigma\leq\frac{1}{8m}
.
With probability
p_{1}:=1-2^{-d}\geq\frac{1}{2}

the shift parameter satisfies
\boldsymbol{\xi}\notin[0,\frac{1}{2}]^{d}
,
hence the control variate is zero, in particular
g_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}}=0
on the lower corner sub-cube
G_{\boldsymbol{0}}
.
An individual sample
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{0}}^{(j)}\sim\Uniform(G_{\boldsymbol{0}})

hits the ball
B_{\sigma/2}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
with probability

p_{2}:=\frac{1}{V_{d}}\cdot\left(\frac{m\sigma}{2}\right)^{d}\asymp n\cdot\sigma^{d}\,,

the probability of any of the sample points
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{0}}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{0}}^{(n_{0})}

to hit this ball is

p_{3}:=1-(1-p_{2})^{n_{0}}\geq\frac{n_{0}p_{2}}{2}\qquad\text{for sufficiently small }p_{2}.

If any sample point hits the ball, the output of the algorithm is at least

A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}(\psi_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{x}_{0}})\geq\frac{1}{n_{0}\,m^{d}}\cdot\frac{b_{0}}{c_{0}}\cdot\sigma^{-(d/p-s)}\asymp n^{-1}\cdot\sigma^{-(d/p-s)}\,.

Choosing
\sigma=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{m}\cdot\delta^{1/d}\asymp(\delta/n)^{1/d}

with a suitable constant
0<\sigma_{0}\leq\frac{1}{8}
,
the ball
B_{\sigma/2}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
is hit with probability
p_{1}p_{3}>\delta

for small
\delta
(and, hence, small
p_{2}
).
Provided that
\delta
and thus
\sigma
is small enough,
the integral of
\psi_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
is neglectable compared to the output of the algorithm,
namely

e\left(A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}},\delta,W_{p}^{s}([0,1]^{d})\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}A_{m,s}^{\textup{SCV}}(\psi_{\sigma,\boldsymbol{x}_{0}})\asymp n^{-\left(\frac{s}{d}+1-\frac{1}{p}\right)}\cdot\delta^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}\right)}\,.

This is the lower bound as claimed.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Comparing the upper bounds of Theorem
2.3
with the lower bounds of the theorem above,
for higher integrability
p>2
we see that
a gap of order
n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}
in the
n
-dependence occurs.
Considering, say, functions that consist of several bumps on different sub-domains
does not seem to lead to a larger lower bound.
This comes as a surprise
given that general lower bounds for arbitrary (potentially non-linear) algorithms
are found with functions that fill the domain with bumps
in the case of high integrability
p\geq 2
, see
[
15
, Lem 1]
.
The main difference in the proofs is that for general lower bounds
the source of error is a lack of knowledge on the input function
while lower bounds for linear methods are caused by the corrupting effect of outliers.
It remains an open problem whether the upper bound analysis of the SCV algorithm can be improved,
or whether some trick in the lower bound has the potential of closing the gap.

Yet another much more challenging problem, of course, is
to prove lower bounds on the joint
(n,\delta)
-dependence for general linear algorithms
in the low smoothness regime,
especially if the algorithm can be tuned in to
\delta
.
One could also try to prove lower bounds for classes of algorithms
with certain desirable properties.
For instance, using randomized quasi Monte Carlo methods with equal weights,
the constant
t_{0}
in the proof of Theorem
3.1
can be made explicit.
If we avoid negative weights, we can lift the restriction
that only one function evaluation occurs on the support of the bump.
Algorithms with a high probability that the sampling set has low discrepancy
(or some other suitable property of “well distribution”) might allow for more precise estimates
on the probability of hitting large function values of a bump function.

Appendix A
Stochastic inequalities

We start with a Hoeffding type inequality for bounded random variables
where we know a bound on the
\ell_{p}
-norm of the vector
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{i})

of individual absolute bounds.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
1<p<2
and consider a family
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
of
independent random variables with absolute bounds

|Z_{i}|\leq b_{i}
, writing
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Further, denote the means
a_{i}:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}Z_{i}

and their average
a:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
.
Then for
\varepsilon>0
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-a\right|>\varepsilon\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n\,\varepsilon}{3\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\ell_{p}}}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)\,.

Equivalently, for
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-a\right|\leq 3\,n^{-1}\cdot\left(2\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{1-1/p}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\ell_{p}}\,,

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality,
b_{1}\geq\ldots\geq b_{n}\geq 0
.
Split up the vector into its head
\boldsymbol{b}_{[k]}:=(b_{i})_{i=1}^{k}

and tail
\boldsymbol{b}_{\setminus k}:=(b_{i})_{i=k+1}^{n}
.
Using the triangle inequality, we have

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-a\right|\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(Z_{i}-a_{i})\right|+\left|\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}(Z_{i}-a_{i})\right|\right)\,.

(31)

For the head, again via the triangle inequality, we obtain the absolute bound

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(Z_{i}-a_{i})\right|\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}=2\,\|\boldsymbol{b}_{[k]}\|_{\ell_{1}}\leq 2\,k^{1-1/p}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{b}_{[k]}\|_{\ell_{p}}\,.

(32)

From Hoeffding’s inequality, for the tail and a threshold
t>0
we know

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\left|\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}(Z_{i}-a_{i})\right|>t\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}b_{i}^{2}}\right)=:\delta\,.

Resolving for
t
, we find the probabilistic bound

t=\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{b}_{\setminus k}\|_{\ell_{2}}\,.

(33)

We use a well-known fact about best
k
-term approximation,

\|\boldsymbol{b}_{\setminus k}\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq(k+1)^{-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\ell_{p}}\,,

(34)

see
[
19
, Lem 2.1]
for the first occurance of this inequality,
also check out
[
9
, eq (2.4)]
for its application in compressed sensing,
and
[
10
, Sec 7.4]
for a survey of extensions and historical context.
Choosing
k:=\left\lfloor 2\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right\rfloor
,
as long as
2\log\frac{2}{\delta}<n
, from (
32
), and (
33
) combined with (
34
),
all plugged into (
31
), we find that

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-a\right|\leq\frac{3}{n}\cdot\left(2\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{1-1/p}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\ell_{p}}=:\varepsilon

(35)

holds with probability
1-\delta
, as claimed.
If
2\log\frac{2}{\delta}\geq n
, then we only use the head estimate (
32
)
with
k=n
, which is even better than (
35
).
Resolving (
35
) for
\delta
, we obtain
the alternative representation of the lemma.
∎

For
L_{p}
-integrable random variables
Z
, with
1\leq p<\infty
, we write

\|Z\|_{L_{p}}:=\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|Z|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\,.

This norm interpretation helps to obtain the following inequality.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
2\leq q<\infty
and let

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
be
L_{q}
-integrable random variables.
Then

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{2}\right)^{q/2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|Y_{i}|^{q}\right)^{2/q}\right)^{q/2}\,.

Proof.

Writing
p:=q/2\geq 1
, we employ the triangle inequality for the
L_{p}
-norm and estimate

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{2}\right)^{q/2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{2}\right\|_{L_{p}}^{p}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Y_{i}^{2}\|_{L_{p}}\right)^{p}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Y_{i}\|_{L_{q}}^{2}\right)^{q/2}\,,

using
\|Y_{i}^{2}\|_{L_{p}}=\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|Y_{i}|^{2p}\right)^{1/p}=\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|Y_{i}|^{q}\right)^{2/q}=\|Y_{i}\|_{L_{q}}^{2}
. ∎

We finally give a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund type inequality for the
q
-th central absolute moment
of the mean of independent random variables.

Lemma A.3
.

For
1\leq q<\infty
there exists a constant
c_{q}>0

such that for any collection
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
of independent,
L_{q}
-integrable random variables with mean

a_{i}:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}Z_{i}
, and
a:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
,
the following inequality holds:

\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-a\right\|_{L_{q}}\leq\frac{c_{q}}{n}\cdot\begin{cases}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|Z_{i}\|_{L_{q}}^{q}\right)^{1/q}&\text{for }1\leq q<2\\
\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|Z_{i}\|_{L_{q}}^{2}}&\text{for }2\leq q<\infty\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

For
1\leq q\leq 2
we use
[
6
, Thm 3]
, rescaled by
\frac{1}{n}
, and obtain

\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-a\right\|_{L_{q}}\leq\frac{2^{1/q}}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Z_{i}-a_{i}\|_{L_{q}}^{q}\right)^{1/q}\,.

For
q\geq 2

we employ the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality
which states that

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-a_{i})\right|^{q}\right)\leq B_{q}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-a_{i})^{2}\right)^{q/2}\right]\,,

(36)

with
B_{q}=(q-1)^{q}
, see
[
7
]
.
Lemma
A.2
applied to
Y_{i}:=Z_{i}-a_{i}
gives

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-a_{i})^{2}\right)^{q/2}\right]\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Z_{i}-a_{i}\|_{L_{q}}^{2}\right)^{q/2}.

Combined with (
36
), taking the
q
-th root, and rescaling with
\frac{1}{n}
,
we get

\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-a\right\|_{L_{q}}\leq\frac{B_{q}^{1/q}}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Z_{i}-a_{i}\|_{L_{q}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,.

Finally, with
\|Z_{i}-a_{i}\|_{L_{q}}\leq\|Z_{i}\|_{L_{q}}+|a_{i}|\leq 2\|Z_{i}\|_{L_{q}}
,
we arrive at the assertion for both cases with the following upper bounds on the constant:

c_{q}\leq\begin{cases}2^{1+1/q}&\text{for }1\leq q\leq 2\,,\\
2(q-1)&\text{for }2\leq q\leq\infty\,.\end{cases}

These estimates on
c_{q}
are not optimal. In fact, for
q=1
one can directly show that even
c_{1}\leq 2
is valid.
For
q=2
, Bienaymé’s identity and
\|Z_{k}-a_{i}\|_{L_{2}}\leq\|Z_{k}\|_{L_{2}}

imply the optimal constant
c_{2}=1
.
∎
