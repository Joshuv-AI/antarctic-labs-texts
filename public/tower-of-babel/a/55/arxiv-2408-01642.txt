Neural Term Structure of Additive Process for Option Pricing
Jimin Lin, Guixin Liu

Abstract
The additive process generalizes the Lévy process by relaxing its assumption of time-homogeneous increments and hence covers a larger family of stochastic processes. Recent research in option pricing shows that modeling the underlying log price with an additive process has advantages in easier construction of the risk-neural measure, an explicit option pricing formula and characteristic function, and more flexibility to fit the implied volatility surface. Still, the challenge of calibrating an additive model arises from its time-dependent parameterization, for which one has to prescribe parametric functions for the term structure. For this, we propose the neural term structure model to utilize feedforward neural networks to represent the term structure, which alleviates the difficulty of designing parametric functions and thus attenuates the misspecification risk. Numerical studies with S&P 500 option data are conducted to evaluate the performance of the neural term structure.



1
Introduction

Providing an arbitrage-free valuation formula and specifying risk-neutral dynamics are essentially two sides of the same coin in option pricing. Yet, the modeling methodology has been leaning towards the latter for decades. That is, the invention of an option pricing model typically starts with proposing a stochastic process that is a martingale for the underlying asset, so that the corresponding risk-neural measure is constructed, and henceforth the arbitrage-free option valuation can be determined either analytically or numerically. Such a methodology was established through the pioneering work of
Bachelier [4]
and
Black and Scholes [9]
, and since then, almost all of the prevailing models have been invented along this paradigm. The list includes but is not limited to local volatility models by
Dupire [18]
,
Cox [15]
, stochastic volatility models by
Heston [21]
,
Hagan et al. [19]
,
Bates [8]
, jump-diffusion models by
Merton [29]
,
Kou [25]
, and other models built upon Lévy processes by
Madan et al. [27]
,
Barndorff-Nielsen [7]
.

Nonetheless, the reverse approach, which first provides an arbitrage-free valuation formula, as in
Carr and Madan [11]
,
Davis and Hobson [16]
, and then finds the underlying martingale supporting the formula, is still possible, as noted in
[
22
,
28
]
. In recent work,
Carr and Torricelli [12]
starts with one particular pricing formula that yields logistically distributed marginals. Although there is no underlying Lévy process that produces such marginals, by allowing the increment to be nonstationary, an additive logistic process can be constructed to support that pricing formula. Treatment of using additive processes to price and calibrate options can be traced back to
Cont and Tankov [13]
.
Azzone and Torricelli [3]
shows that the additive logistic model has desirable qualities for option pricing - a simple closed formula for fast computation, the flexibility of shape control for calibration, and the ease to simulate with fast Fourier transform-based Monte Carlo
[
2
]
.

An appealing alternative approach to generate option pricing models is hence revealed by
Carr and Torricelli [12]
,
Azzone and Torricelli [3]
. One can choose a candidate density from an infinitely divisible family and generalize it with some skew transformation to access more control over its moments, such that its marginal density can fit the volatility smile first at one single expiry. Then, if technical conditions hold as in
[
23
, Theorem 9.8]
, an additive process supporting such marginal can be constructed in a straightforward manner by making the distribution parameters time-dependent, or in other words, prescribing a
term structure
to the distribution parameter. Finally, it is easy to calculate the compensator to make this additive process a martingale.

It is interesting to compare the
[
12
,
3
]
procedure with the way in which
[
17
,
14
,
10
]
build arbitrage-free volatility surfaces, analogically. Both start by specifying one or a few marginals and then find the martingale measure supporting the marginals, while complexity occurs in different stages of each process. The latter can freely use non-parametric methods to fit marginals, but acquiring a martingale measure involves complicated approaches, such as Markov functional construction with the Sinkhorn optimization algorithm. On the other hand, for the former, it is not easy to find a proper infinitely divisible marginal to produce an additive process, but once found, the martingale is readily obtained.

Suppose one successfully constructs an additive process with a proper distribution, it remains non-trivial to specify its term structure, i.e., a set of time-dependent, deterministic, and continuous functions such that the measure agrees with the market behaviors. For example,
[
3
]
provides parametric candidates for the term structure based on a detailed analysis that covers short- and long-maturity asymptotics, large strike asymptotics, and behaviors of the model-induced implied volatility.

In this research, we alleviate the difficulty of term structure selection by utilizing the universal approximation property of a feedforward neural network. Constraints on the term structure can be enforced by regulating the neural network behavior with penalty terms. We call the term structure represented by a neural network the
neural term structure
. Throughout this research, we exemplify the usage of neural term structure specifically on the additive logistic process, but this framework can be easily implemented with any other additive process.

Numerical studies are conducted to assess the performance of the neural term structure with additive logistic processes. We first see that the neural term structure can fit the synthetic surface generated by the parametric term structure in
[
3
]
. Then with an S&P 500 implied volatility surface, we show that the neural term structure better calibrates to the real market data than the chosen parametric term structure. Meanwhile, we notice there may exist multiple dissimilar term structures that can achieve close calibration performance on a single surface. This raises concerns about the robustness of the calibration. For this, we extend the neural term structure to jointly train on a sequence of historical surfaces. It produces a smooth flow of term structures that fits both generic surfaces and specific local dynamics, which is visualized as the
term structure surfaces
.

The remaining content of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
scrutinizes the new option pricing model generating procedure based on the additive process, exemplified with the additive logistic process that is extensively studied in
[
12
,
3
]
. Section
3
discusses methods for calibrating the additive process based model and introduces the neural term structure for fitting a single implied volatility surface and a sequence of historical surfaces. Section
4
demonstrates the advantage of the neural term structure with numerical experiments. Section
5
concludes this paper.

2
Additive Option Pricing Model

In this section, we first provide a review of the most relevant properties of the generalized logistic (GL) distribution. More details on GL can be found in
[
5
,
6
]
. Then, following the work of
Carr and Torricelli [12]
,
Azzone and Torricelli [3]
, we explain the construction of the additive logistic process to model the underlying log-price process and the corresponding closed-form option pricing formula.

2.1
Generalized Logistic Distribution

Denote
\text{L}(\mu,\sigma)
the location-scale logistic distribution,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, let
f^{L}
and
F^{L}
be respectively its PDF and CDF given by

\displaystyle f^{L}(x;\mu,\sigma)=\frac{e^{-\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}}}{\sigma\left(1+e^{-\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}}\right)^{2}},

\displaystyle F^{L}(x;\mu,\sigma)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}}}.

(2.1)

The generalized logistic distribution of type IV, also known as logistic-beta distribution,
\text{GL}(\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha,\ \beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, is generated by applying beta-skew-transformation to
\text{L}(\mu,\sigma)
, whose PDF
f^{GL}
and CDF
F^{GL}
are given by

\displaystyle f^{GL}(x;\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)}f^{L}(x;\mu,\sigma)\left(F^{L}(x;\mu,\sigma)\right)^{\alpha-1}\left(1-F^{L}(x;\mu,\sigma)\right)^{\beta-1},

(2.2)

\displaystyle F^{GL}(x;\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\int_{0}^{F^{L}(x;\mu,\sigma)}u^{\alpha-1}\left(1-u\right)^{\beta-1}\differential u.

(2.3)

where
B(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}
is the beta function and
\Gamma
is the gamma function. Note that
F^{GL}
is obtained simply by a change of variable
\differential u=F^{L}(\differential x;\mu,\sigma)
to the integral of
f^{GL}
and can be recognized as the regularized incomplete beta function integrated up to
F^{L}(x;\mu,\sigma)
. In addition, using the property of the location-scale family, we can pivot the
\text{L}(\mu,\sigma)
and hence define the standard GL distribution with skew parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
by

\Phi(x;\alpha,\beta):=F^{GL}(x;0,1,\alpha,\beta),

which is useful to express the option pricing formula more compactly in the later content.

\text{GL}(\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta)
has the characteristic function for
z\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\widehat{f}^{GL}(z;\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta)=\left(\frac{B(\alpha+i\sigma z,\beta-i\sigma z)}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\right)e^{i\mu z}.

(2.4)

The finite divisibility and self-decomposability of
\text{GL}(\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta)
are important to construct an additive logistic process, which can be identified through its characteristic function.

Lemma 2.1
.

\text{GL}(\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta)
is infinitely divisible with the Lévy triplet
(a,0,v(x)\differential x)
given by

\displaystyle a=\sigma\int_{0}^{\sigma^{-1}}\frac{e^{-\beta x}-e^{-\alpha x}}{1-e^{-x}}\differential x+\mu,

\displaystyle v(x)=\frac{e^{-\left(\beta\mathbbm{1}_{x>0}+\alpha\mathbbm{1}_{x<0}\right)\frac{\absolutevalue{x}}{\sigma}}}{\absolutevalue{x}\left(1-e^{\frac{-\absolutevalue{x}}{\sigma}}\right)}.

(2.5)

Moreover, it is also self-decomposable.

Proof.

Express the beta function in (
2.4
) with the integral representation of the log-gamma function

\displaystyle\log\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-zx}-e^{-x}}{x(1-e^{-x})}-(z-1)\frac{e^{-x}}{x}\right)\differential x,

\displaystyle\real(z)>0.

(2.6)

The cumulant function, i.e. natural logarithm of the characteristic function (
2.4
) can be organized into the representation

\displaystyle\log\widehat{f}^{GL}(z;\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta)=iza-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}b+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(e^{izx}-1-izx\mathbbm{1}_{\absolutevalue{x}<1}\right)v(x)\differential x,

(2.7)

where the triplet
(a,b,v(x)\differential x)
reads
b=0
,
a
and
v(x)
are as in the Lemma
2.1
; hence, by the Lévy-Khintchine Theorem we have the infinitely divisibility. Notice the criteria
\differential(\abs{x} v(x))/\differential x
is positive for
x<0
and negative for
x>0
, the self-decomposability follows by
[
26
, Theorem 5.11.2]
.
∎

2.2
Additive Logistic Process

Based on Lemma
2.1
, we can construct an additive process whose marginal distribution is
GL
by simply replacing the constant parameters
\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta
in Section
2.1
by appropriate continuous functions of time. These time-dependent parameters, named the
term structure
, jointly determine the cumulants, especially the mean, volatility, skewness, and kurtosis of interest, of the entire process. Consequently, we also have the Lévy triplet in Equation (
2.5
) become time-dependent here,
(a(t),0,v(t,x)\differential x)
. Assumptions required on the term structure are as follows.

Assumption 2.2
.

\mu:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma,\alpha,\beta:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
are continuous functions and we have

i.

\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}
and
\frac{\beta}{\sigma}
non-increasing,

ii.

\sigma
non-decreasing and
\sigma(0)=0
,

iii.

0<\alpha,\ \beta<\infty
,

iv.

\sigma<\beta
.

The first three conditions in Assumption
2.2
are sufficient to construct the additive logistic process.

Proposition 2.3
.

For
\mu
,
\sigma
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
satisfying conditions
i
-
iii
in Assumption
2.2
, there exists in law a unique additive process
(X_{t})_{t>0}
starting at zero such that

\displaystyle X_{t}\sim\text{GL}\left(\mu(t),\sigma(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t)\right).

(2.8)

Proof.

It is easy to verify that both
a(t)
and
v(t,\cdot)
are continuous for
t\geq 0
,
a(0)=0
,
v(0,\cdot)=0
, and
v(s,\cdot)\leq v(t,\cdot)
for
0\leq s\leq t
, see
[
12
]
for details. The result follows by applying the second part of
[
23
, Theorem 9.8]
.
∎

Notice from the
GL
characteristic function (
2.4
) that we will need condition
iv
in Assumption
2.2
for
X_{t}
to have a finite expectation. With one more extra condition on
\mu
, we can hitherto obtain an additive logistic martingale.

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume
r>0
is the constant risk-free rate,
\sigma
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
satisfy Assumption
2.2
, and

\displaystyle\mu(t)=\log\left(\frac{B(\alpha(t)+\sigma(t),\beta(t)-\sigma(t))}{B(\alpha(t),\beta(t))}\right),

(2.9)

then there exists in law a unique additive logistic process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

\displaystyle X_{t}\sim\text{GL}\left(rt-\mu(t),\sigma(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t)\right),

\displaystyle X_{0}=0,

(2.10)

whose discounted exponential
e^{-rt}S_{t}=e^{-rt+X_{t}}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-martingale.

Proof.

Existence follows from Proposition
2.3
. For martingality, inserting Equation (
2.9
) to Equation (
2.4
) we have
e^{-rt}\mathbb{E}[S_{t}]=\varphi_{t}(-i)=1
, where
\varphi_{t}(\cdot)=\hat{f}^{GL}(\cdot\ ;rt-\mu(t),\sigma(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t))
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Note that the stationary increment assumption, which we are used to in Lévy processes, is dropped for additive processes, i.e. for all
0\leq s<t
,
X_{t}-X_{s}\simeq X_{t-s}
does not necessarily hold. With additive processes, one should be careful of any methods based on time-homogeneity property. For instance,
X_{t}
cannot be simulated with conventional Monte Carlo i.i.d. sampling. Nonetheless, we still have the analytic characteristic function for increment
\mathbb{E}[e^{iz(X_{t}-X_{s})}]=\varphi_{t}/\varphi_{s}
that can be used to simulate
X_{t}
by fast Fourier transform
[
2
]
.

2.3
Option Pricing Formula

Assume the price process of the underlying asset follows
S_{t}=S_{0}e^{X_{t}}
where
S_{0}
is the spot price and
X_{t}
is an additive logistic martingale as in Proposition
2.4
. The explicit marginal density of
X_{t}
leads to the explicit pricing formulas for the initial value of options.

Proposition 2.6
.

At time
t=0
, the initial value of an European call
C(K,T)
and put
P(K,T)
with strike
K
and time-to-maturity
T
is priced by

\displaystyle C(K,T)

\displaystyle=S_{0}\Phi\left(d;\beta(T)-\sigma(T),\alpha(T)+\sigma(T)\right)-e^{-rT}K\Phi\left(d;\beta(T),\alpha(T)\right),

(2.11)

\displaystyle P(K,T)

\displaystyle=e^{-rT}K\Phi\left(-d;\alpha(T),\beta(T)\right)-S_{0}\Phi\left(-d;\alpha(T)+\sigma(T),\beta(T)-\sigma(T)\right),

where

d=\frac{\log\frac{S_{0}}{K}+rT-\mu(T)}{\sigma(T)}.

Proof.

Pivoting the log-return
X_{T}\simeq rT-\mu(T)+\sigma(T)Z
with a standard GL random variable
Z\sim\text{GL}(0,1,\alpha(T),\beta(T))
we have

\displaystyle P(K,T)

\displaystyle=e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(K-S_{T}\right)^{+}\right]=e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(K-S_{0}e^{X_{T}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{Z\leq-d}\right]

(2.12)

\displaystyle=e^{-rT}K\mathbb{P}\left(Z\leq-d\right)-S_{0}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{Z}\mathbbm{1}_{Z\leq-d}\right].

(2.13)

Notice that
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{Z}\mathbbm{1}_{Z\leq-d}\right]=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Z}\leq-d)
with
\tilde{Z}\sim\text{GL}(0,1,\alpha(T)+\sigma(T),\beta(T)-\sigma(T))
, and reexpress
\mathbb{P}
by
\Phi
, we obtain the result.
C(K,T)
follows by put-call parity and beta reflection symmetry.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The pricing formulas (
2.11
) are analogous to, and yield the same simplicity and computability as the Black-Scholes formula with standard GL CDFs with prescribed term structure
(\sigma,\alpha,\beta)
substituting the standard normal CDF. However, it is not directly applicable to price the option price process, say
C_{t}(K,T)
conditioning in
S_{t}
for
t>0
, due to the time-inhomogeneity, as noted in Remark
2.5
.

3
Calibrating additive model with neural term structure

With the additive logistic martingale (
2.10
) constructed, it remains to specify functional forms for term structure
(\sigma,\alpha,\beta)\in\Xi
to calibrate the model to the implied volatility surface. Selecting the term structure is non-trivial and subject to the modeler’s empirical experience to translate between the surface characteristic and the proper functional forms, and it is impractical to manually configure the functions by trial and error. A uniform framework to find the optimal term structure is desired.

For this, we design the machine learning-based approach by utilizing a feedforward neural network that is regulated by Assumption
2.2
to represent the term structure, named the
neural term structure
. The neural term structure is not only capable of calibrating to a single volatility surface, but is also scalable to fit a long historical sequence of implied volatility surfaces to capture the surface dynamic. In this section, we begin with a single surface calibration case, and then we will see that it is effortless to extend the method for joint calibration on surface dynamics.

3.1
Calibration with a single surface

One implied volatility represents the information of an option chain, i.e., a set of market options listed by a grid of combinations of strikes and maturities, that are quoted at one time. Let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{1},\dots,K_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\}
respectively collect
n\in\mathbb{N}
strikes and
m\in\mathbb{N}
maturity dates, let each
(K,T)\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{T}
,
C^{M}(K,T)
be the market price of a call option with strike
K
and maturity
T
, and let
C(K,T;\sigma(T),\alpha(T),\beta(T))
be the call price given by formula (
2.11
) prescribed by the term structure
(\sigma,\alpha,\beta)
. Let
\Xi
be the space of all functional forms of term structure that satisfy the Assumption
2.2
. The calibration task is to minimize the pricing error

\displaystyle\min_{(\sigma,\alpha,\beta)\in\Xi}L_{P}(\sigma,\alpha,\beta).

(3.1)

Based on specific calibration purposes, different metrics and weighting schemes can be applied to
L_{P}
, such as bid-ask spread weighted mean square error and Black-Scholes vega weighted mean square error in
[
20
]
. Here we simply use the plain mean squared error

\displaystyle L_{P}(\sigma,\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\cK}\absolutevalue{\cT}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\in\mathcal{K}\\
T\in\mathcal{T}\end{subarray}}\left(C^{M}(K,T)-C(K,T;\sigma(T),\alpha(T),\beta(T))\right)^{2}.

(3.2)

There are three approaches to minimizing
L_{P}
.

Calibration on slices of marginals
: naively, one can fit marginals slice by slice with scalar parameters, and then interpolate the calibrated parameters between slices to construct the term structure. Ideally, such a term structure is feasible, otherwise ad-hoc adjustment is needed. That is, for every fixed maturity
T_{i}\in\mathcal{T}

(1\leq i\leq m)
, one finds
(\sigma_{T_{i}},\alpha_{T_{i}},\beta_{T_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}
such that

\displaystyle(\hat{\sigma}_{T_{i}},\hat{\alpha}_{T_{i}},\hat{\beta}_{T_{i}})=\argmin_{\sigma,\alpha,\beta}\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\cK}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\left(C^{M}(K,T_{i})-C(K,T_{i};\sigma,\alpha,\beta)\right)^{2}.

(3.3)

Next, the term structure
(\hat{\sigma},\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
is constructed by interpolating the time slices
(\hat{\sigma}(T_{i}),\hat{\alpha}(T_{i}),\hat{\beta}(T_{i}))=(\hat{\sigma}_{T_{i}},\hat{\alpha}_{T_{i}},\hat{\beta}_{T_{i}})
for
1\leq i\leq m
, with either lines, polynomials or splines. The interpolated term structure is feasible if
(\hat{\sigma},\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})\in\Xi
.

However, this approach does not guarantee feasibility. A limited number of samples in each slice could induce noise in parameter estimation, which poses difficulty in obtaining a proper interpolation. Should the infeasibility occur, one still needs to jointly regulate the term structure across the slices, which in turn undermines the straightforwardness of this approach.

Calibration with prescribed functions
: one can design parametric functions to fully control the term structure according to its knowledge of the implied volatility surface behavior. This reduces the optimization problem from the functional space
\Xi
to a smaller space of scalar parameters.
Carr and Torricelli [12]
tests the simplest symmetric standard GL with

\displaystyle\sigma(\tau)=\left(1-e^{-\tau\sigma_{0}^{1/H}}\right)^{H},

\displaystyle\alpha(\tau)=\beta(\tau)\equiv 1,

(3.4)

where the term structure is parameterized by only two parameters, the volatility level
\sigma_{0}
and volatility term exponent
H
.

Azzone and Torricelli [3]
provides for each term structure function a simple form and a sophisticated form

\displaystyle\sigma^{a}(\tau)=\sigma_{0}\tau^{H_{0}},

\displaystyle\alpha^{a}(\tau)=\alpha_{0},

\displaystyle\beta^{a}(\tau)=\beta_{0}+\sigma^{a}(\tau)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sigma^{b}(\tau)=\frac{\arctan(\sigma_0 \frac{\pi}{2}\tau^{H_0})}{\arctan(\sigma_1^{-1} \frac{\pi}{2}\tau^{-H_1})},

\displaystyle\alpha^{b}(\tau)=\alpha_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{1}}{1+\sigma(\tau)},

\displaystyle\beta^{b}(\tau)=\beta_{1}+\frac{\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}}{1+\sigma^{b}(\tau)}+\sigma^{b}(\tau),

where
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\beta_{0},\beta_{1},H_{0},H_{1}
are parameters. One can choose any combination of them to configure the term structure. The basic term functions can serve as baselines and the sophisticated ones with more parameters have better flexibility to control the shape of the implied volatility surface.

The obvious shortcomings of this approach are that it is not easy to design proper functions and that, even if so, the designed function can hardly be optimal in
\Xi
.

Calibration with the neural term structure
: denote an
N\in\mathbb{N}
layer neural network
\eta

\displaystyle\eta:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+},

\displaystyle\tau\mapsto\eta(\tau)=\left(\sigma^{\eta}(\tau),\alpha^{\eta}(\tau),\beta^{\eta}(\tau)\right),

(3.6)

with

\displaystyle\eta=\phi_{N}\circ g_{N}\dots\circ\phi_{1}\circ g_{1},

(3.7)

where
\circ
denotes the function composition. For
1\leq i\leq N
,
d_{0}=1
and
d_{N}=3
,
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
are composable affine maps given by

\displaystyle g_{i}(\tau)=w_{i}\tau+b_{i},

\displaystyle w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i-1}\times d_{i}},

\displaystyle b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}},

(3.8)

and
\phi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
are some activation function. Let
w=\{w_{i},b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
gather the neural network weights, the neural network can be represented by
\eta(\cdot;w)
. The calibration task with mean squared error (
3.1
) is thus translated to

\displaystyle\min_{\{w:\ \eta(\cdot;w)\in\Xi\}}L_{P}(\sigma^{\eta},\alpha^{\eta},\beta^{\eta};w),

(3.9)

that is to train a neural network
\eta
which represents a term function triplet
(\sigma^{\eta},\alpha^{\eta},\beta^{\eta})
satisfying Assumption
2.2
, and minimizes the calibration error. Moreover, these assumptions can be translated into either soft constraints or hard constraints as follows.

Monotonic conditions
i
and
ii
can be facilitated by soft constraints with loss functions

\displaystyle L_{C}(\sigma^{\eta},\alpha^{\eta},\beta^{\eta};w)=\left(\partial_{\tau}\frac{\alpha^{\eta}(\tau)}{\sigma^{\eta}(\tau)}\right)^{+}+\left(\partial_{\tau}\frac{\beta^{\eta}(\tau)}{\sigma^{\eta}(\tau)}\right)^{+}+\left(-\partial_{\tau}\sigma^{\eta}(\tau)\right)^{+},

(3.10)

where the derivatives on the neural network can be easily evaluated with automatic differentiation, which is available in existing machine learning frameworks such as
[
1
]
and
[
30
]
. The positivity condition
iii
is guaranteed by choosing a proper activation function at the final layer. For instance, we can let the last activation function
\phi^{N}
in Equation (
3.7
) be the softplus activation function. The inequality
iv
can be satisfied by reformulating
\eta=(\sigma,\alpha,\beta-\sigma)
, i.e., learning the difference between
\beta
and
\sigma
and ensuring its positivity by a certain activation function, instead of learning
\beta
directly.

Therefore, combining the pricing loss
L_{P}
and regularization loss
L_{C}
, the neural term structure can be trained with the total loss function

\displaystyle L(w)=L_{P}(\sigma^{\eta},\alpha^{\eta},\beta^{\eta};w)+\lambda L_{C}(\sigma^{\eta},\alpha^{\eta},\beta^{\eta};w)

(3.11)

for some regularization parameter
\lambda>0
that is properly chosen to penalize any violation of Assumption
2.2
. The neural network with the total loss function (
3.11
) can be trained with choices of gradient-based optimizers such as the conventional Adam
[
24
]
.

3.2
Calibration with Sequence of Surfaces

Due to perpetually changing market conditions, the implied volatility surface changes dynamically over time as well. Consequently, a previously well-calibrated model might become outdated and cannot produce rightful option prices in the next time step. Recalibration is a common practice in real scenarios to update the model to the newest market information.

A conventional choice is to periodically redo the calibration task (
3.1
) in Section
3.1
. To notate the recalibration procedure clearly in the dynamic scenario, we reserve the lowercase
t
to the calendar date, let uppercase
T
notate for the expiry date of options, and denote the tenor of options by
\tau
, e.g., at a time
t
, an option expire at time
T
has the time-to-maturity
\tau=T-t
. It is practical to quote the market price of a call option by moneyness
\kappa
and tenor
\tau
instead of strike
K
and maturity date
T
, and it is easy to adapt the pricing formula (
2.11
) to such reparametrization. With slight abuse of notations, we override
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{T}
to be the set of
m
moneyness and
n
tenors, i.e.
\mathcal{K}=\{\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{m}\}
and
\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}\}
.

The term structure calibrated to the market at
t
is then subscribed by the time index as well:
\tau\mapsto(\sigma_{t}(\tau),\alpha_{t}(\tau),\beta_{t}(\tau))
. Assume the calibration happens daily and is scheduled in a certain set of
l\in\mathbb{N}
time
\mathcal{D}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{l}\}
. Based on the discussed recalibration practice, every day we will do optimization (
3.1
) for the optimal term structure, so we will have
l
sets of term structure
(\sigma_{t_{i}}(\tau),\alpha_{t_{i}}(\tau),\beta_{t_{i}}(\tau))_{i=1}^{l}
. However, such day-by-day recalibration is not robust.

Even when the market condition mildly changes from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
, due to numerical instability, the term structure calibrated at
t_{2}
might significantly differ from the one calibrated at
t_{1}
. Consequently, a decision such as hedging based on
t_{1}
calibration may deviate substantially from the optima at
t_{2}
. Therefore, a desirable calibration should not only fit the market at both
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, but also be robust against self-variation. Such robustness can be enhanced by training the neural term structure jointly with a sequence of historical implied volatility surfaces.

We hence extend the neural term structure in Equation (
3.6
) to

\displaystyle\eta:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3},

\displaystyle(t,\tau)\mapsto\eta(t,\tau)=\left(\sigma^{\eta}(t,\tau),\alpha^{\eta}(t,\tau),\beta^{\eta}(t,\tau)\right).

(3.12)

Correspondingly, the loss function in (
3.1
) is modified to cover the market data across multiple dates in
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle L_{P}^{\prime}(\sigma,\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\cD}\absolutevalue{\cK}\absolutevalue{\cT}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\mathcal{D}\\
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}\\
\tau\in\mathcal{T}\end{subarray}}\left(C^{M}_{t}(\kappa,\tau)-C(\kappa,\tau;\sigma(t,\tau),\alpha(t,\tau),\beta(t,\tau))\right)^{2}.

(3.13)

Substituting
L_{P}
by
L_{P}^{\prime}
in the total loss function (
3.11
), we readily adapt the neural term structure framework to deal with the joint calibration task.

By extending the neural term structure to jointly calibrate to the implied volatility surface sequence, in addition to the enhanced robustness of the term structure, we obtain a side product in the form of the
term structure surfaces
represented by
(\sigma(t,\tau),\alpha(t,\tau),\allowbreak\beta(t,\tau))
, which gives a dynamic view of the implied volatility evolution.

Recall that a conventional implied volatility surface
\Sigma_{t}(\kappa,\tau)
only informs a static view of the market at time
t
. Along the direction of moneyness
\kappa
, the volatility smile essentially implies that the risk-neutral measure has a heavier tail than the Gaussian density, as used in the Black-Scholes model. The volatility smirk implies the asymmetry of the risk-neutral measure. Both the smile and smirk are quantified by the skew of the implied volatility
\partial_{\kappa}\Sigma
. Through the direction of tenor
\tau
, the implied volatility term structure
\partial_{\tau}\Sigma
informs dispersion of the risk measure from the viewpoint of time-average instantaneous Gaussian volatility.

Though one can quickly infer the current market status from the implied volatility surface, it is hard to keep track of market variation by contrasting multiple implied surface
(\Sigma_{t_{i}})_{i=1}^{l}
snapshots across different days. Because the term structure
(\sigma(t,\tau),\alpha(t,\tau),\beta(t,\tau))
innately encompasses the skews without the explicit parameterization for the moneyness, we can make use of the spare one dimension for the calendar date to reflect the term structure dynamics. Three 3D surfaces with value, time, and tenor axes that can equivalently encode the market information, the term structure surfaces, can be hitherto plotted.

For a fixed date
t
, along the tenor dimension,
\sigma(t,\cdot)
represents the market view on the dispersion of the risk-neural density through different maturities, while
\alpha(t,\cdot)
and
\beta(t,\cdot)
indicates the left-and right-skewness of the density. For a fixed tenor
\tau
, one can make use of
(\sigma(\cdot,\tau),\alpha(\cdot,\tau),\beta(\cdot,\tau))
to track the market morph trough time. Additionally, for an option released on date
t_{0}
with tenor
\tau_{0}
, then surfaces along the line
(t_{0}+\delta t,\tau_{0}-\delta t)
for
0\leq\delta t\leq\tau_{0}
cover the historical information through the life span of this option.

5
Conclusion

The neural term structure proposed in this research is a simple, yet powerful approach for calibrating an additive model to implied volatility surfaces. Without the need to prescribe any parametric function, it attains even better calibration accuracy. Moreover, by slightly extending the neural network to feed on time-varying volatility surfaces, it can robustly encapsulate the change of market conditions with smooth flows of term structures that are numerically stable. We demonstrate the advantage of the neural term structure with perhaps the simplest additive process that is supported by the GL distribution. Even as parsimonious as with only three parameters, the additive logistic option pricing model empowered by the neural term structure achieves persuasive calibration performance. It is promising for future research to develop better additive option pricing models by devising more sophisticated additive processes and combining them with the neural term structure.
