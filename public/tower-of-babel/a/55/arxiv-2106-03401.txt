Finite-frequency response of Rasba electron gas with two-particle scattering
K. E. Nagaev

Abstract
Two-dimensional systems with Rashba spin-orbit coupling are not Galilean invariant and therefore electron–electron collisions in them may affect the current. However when taken alone, they cannot ensure a nonzero dc resistivity, so their effects are masked by impurity scattering. Here we calculate the related finite-frequency response and show that the electron–electron scattering in clean Rashba conductors decreases the Drude weight while resulting in a finite dissipative component of the response outside of the Drude peak.



I
Introduction

Electron systems with spin-orbit coupling are not Galilean-invariant and therefore the electron–electron scattering may
affect the electrical current in them. A considerable amount of recent theoretical work was related with their optical conductivity
and finite-frequency absorption. Many papers dealt with single-particle absorption due to the transitions between spin-split
subbands
Magarill et al. 2001
;
Xu 2003
;
Wang 2005
;
Gumbs 2005
;
Kushwaha and Ulloa 2006
;
Pletyukhov and Gritsev 2006
;
Maiti et al. 2015
, which leads to a box-shaped contribution to
the real part of optical conductivity
at zero temperature. A negative correction to the Drude weight due to electron–electron interaction was obtained using the
time-dependent Hartree-Fock approximation
Agarwal et al. 2011
. In Ref.
Farid and Mishchenko 2006
, the finite-temperature absorption that
results from excitation of electron–hole pairs was calculated by treating the electron–electron interaction as perturbation to the second order.

In this paper, we calculate the finite-frequency and finite-temperature electric response of two-dimensional (2D) Rashba
electron gas
with electron–electron scattering beyond the perturbation theory. To this end, we use the Boltzmann equation similar to that
derived recently
Nagaev and Manoshin 2020
for calculating the dc conductivity of these systems. Despite the lack of Galilean invariance,
the Rashba electron gas is translationally invariant and therefore there is a perturbation of electron distribution of a
definite form that
is stable with respect to electron–electron collisions. For this reason, a finite dc conductivity may be ensured only in the
presence of an additional mechanism of scattering like impurities that suppress this perturbation. But in the case of an ac response, the time derivative in the Boltzmann equation plays the same role as the additional scattering and eliminates the
divergence of the current. We
restrict ourselves to the frequencies much lower than those related with intersubband transitions.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
presents general equations. In Section
III
, the existence
of the perturbation of electron distribution immune to the electron–electron scattering is proved for an arbitrary dispersion
law. Section
IV
presents the calculations and results, and finally, Section
V
contains their
discussion. The Appendix presents some lengthy expressions.

II
The model

As a model, we consider a 2D electron gas with Rashba spin-orbit coupling (SOC), which resides in the
xy
plane and is described
by the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}_{x}^{2}+\hat{p}_{y}^{2}}{2m}+\alpha\,(\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{p}_{y}-\hat{\sigma}_{y}\hat{p}_{x}),

(1)

where
\alpha
is the Rashba coupling constant and
\hat{\sigma}_{x,y}
are the Pauli matrices. The diagonalization of this
Hamiltonian results in two subbands with dispersion laws

\varepsilon_{\nu}({\bm{p}})=\frac{p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2}}{2m}+\nu\alpha\sqrt{p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2}},\quad\nu=\pm 1,

(2)

which correspond to the two opposite helicities of electrons (see Fig.
1
). These subbands are tangent
at
p_{x}=p_{y}=0
, and the Fermi surface is doubly connected below and above
E_{F}=0
. The minimum of the lower subband is
given by
-E_{SO}
, where
E_{SO}=m\alpha^{2}/2
. The eigenstates of the Hamiltonian Eq. (
1
) are two-component spinors
with components in the
\hat{s}_{z}
basis

\psi_{{\bm{p}}\nu}({\bm{r}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,e^{i{\bm{p}}{\bm{r}}/\hbar}\binom{e^{i\chi_{{\bm{p}}}/2}}{\nu e^{-i\chi_{{\bm{p}}}/2}},

(3)

where
\chi_{{\bm{p}}}=\arctan(p_{x}/p_{y})
, so the spin is directed perpendicularly to
{\bm{p}}
either clockwise or counterclockwise.
We assume that the electron–electron interaction is weak and does not affect the electron spectrum.

III
Boltzmann equation and collision integral

The most simple and convenient way to calculate the response of weakly interacting electron system to a slowly varying electric
field as compared with the subband separation is the Boltzmann equation

\frac{\partial f_{\nu}}{\partial t}+e{\bm{E}}(t)\,\frac{\partial f_{\nu}}{\partial{\bm{p}}}=I_{\nu}^{ee},

(4)

where
f_{\nu}({\bm{p}},t)
is the electron distribution in the basis of exact eigenstates of the Hamiltonian (
1
).
The electron–electron collision integral may be written in the form

I_{\nu}^{ee}({\bm{p}})=\sum_{\nu_{1}}\sum_{\nu_{2}}\sum_{\nu_{3}}\int\frac{d^{2}p_{1}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{2}}\int\frac{d^{2}p_{2}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{2}}\int d^{2}p_{3}\,\\
\times\delta({\bm{p}}+{\bm{p}}_{1}-{\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{3})\,\delta(\varepsilon_{\nu}+\varepsilon_{\nu_{1}}-\varepsilon_{\nu_{2}}-\varepsilon_{\nu_{3}})\\
\times W(\nu{\bm{p}},\nu_{1}{\bm{p}}_{1};\nu_{2}{\bm{p}}_{2},\nu_{3}{\bm{p}}_{3})\\
\times\bigl[(1-f)(1-f_{1})\,f_{2}\,f_{3}-f\,f_{1}\,(1-f_{2})(1-f_{3})\bigr].

(5)

provided that the scattering is microscopically reversible, i. e.

W(\nu{\bm{p}},\nu_{1}{\bm{p}}_{1};\nu_{2}{\bm{p}}_{2},\nu_{3}{\bm{p}}_{3})=W(\nu_{2}{\bm{p}}_{2},\nu_{3}{\bm{p}}_{3};\nu{\bm{p}},\nu_{1}{\bm{p}}_{1}).

(6)

This equality may not hold if the system lacks either time-reversal or inversion symmetry, but its violations
show up only beyond the Born approximation
Belinicher and Sturman 1980
;
Gorban 2014
.

Regardless of the number of subbands and the explicit form of
\varepsilon_{\nu}({\bm{p}})
, the collision integral Eq.
(
5
) is always turned into zero by a distribution of the form
Maslov et al. 2011

f_{\nu}({\bm{p}})=\bar{f}(\varepsilon_{\nu}+{\bm{u}}{\bm{p}}),

(7)

where
\bm{u}
is an arbitrary constant vector and
\bar{f}(\varepsilon_{\nu})
is the Fermi distribution function.
With this substitution, one easily obtains that

(1-f)(1-f_{1})\,f_{2}\,f_{3}=ff_{1}f_{2}f_{3}\\
\times\exp\left[\frac{\varepsilon_{\nu}({\bm{p}})+\varepsilon_{\nu_{1}}({\bm{p}}_{1})+{\bm{u}}\,({\bm{p}}+{\bm{p}}_{1})}{T}\right]

(8)

and a similar equality for
ff_{1}\,(1-f_{2})(1-f_{3})
.
Therefore the difference in the square brackets in Eq. (
5
) turns into zero because of the momentum
and energy conservation. As a consequence, any perturbation of the form

\delta f_{\nu}({\bm{p}})=\bm{u}{\bm{p}}\,\bar{f}(\varepsilon_{\nu})\,[1-\bar{f}(\varepsilon_{\nu})]

(9)

turns the collision integral into zero to the first approximation. This is the reason why the electron–electron scattering alone cannot ensure a finite
dc conductivity even for multiband electron systems
Nagaev and Manoshin 2020
.

IV
Electrical response

We assume that the electron–electron interaction in the conductor is screened by a nearby metallic gate such
that the distance to the gate
d_{0}
is smaller than the Fermi wavelength,
so that the interaction potential may be presented in the form
V({\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}^{\prime})=4\pi e^{2}d_{0}\,\kappa^{-1}\,\delta({\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}^{\prime})
,
where
e
is the electron charge and
\kappa
is the dielectric constant.
Therefore the scattering probability
W
in Eq. (
5
) may be calculated in the Born approximation
and equals

W(\nu{\bm{p}},\nu_{1}{\bm{p}}_{1};\nu_{2}{\bm{p}}_{2},\nu_{3}{\bm{p}}_{3})=8\pi^{3}\,\frac{e^{4}d_{0}^{2}}{\hbar\kappa^{2}}\,[1-\nu\,\nu_{1}\cos(\widehat{{\bm{p}},{\bm{p}}_{1}})]\\
\times[1-\nu_{2}\,\nu_{3}\cos(\widehat{{\bm{p}}_{2},{\bm{p}}_{3}})].

(10)

As the system is rotationally symmetric in the
xy
plane,
the linear response to the electric field
\bm{E}(t)
may be conveniently sought in the form

f_{\mu}(\varepsilon,\varphi)=\bar{f}(\varepsilon)+C_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\,\bar{f}(\varepsilon)\,[1-\bar{f}(\varepsilon)]\cos\varphi,

(11)

where
\mu=\pm 1
labels the Fermi contours with larger momentum
p_{1}
and smaller momentum
p_{-1}
for a given
E_{F}
,
the energy
\varepsilon
is measured from
E_{F}
,
and
\varphi
is the angle between
\bm{E}
and
{\bm{p}}
.
Note that the corresponding velocities
v_{\mu}=(\partial p_{\mu}/\partial\varepsilon)^{-1}
for the same
\varepsilon
are equal
at
E_{F}>0

but have opposite signs at
E_{F}<0
, so that
v_{-1}=v_{1}\sgn E_{F}
. Assuming that
p_{\mu}
and

v_{\mu}
are independent of energy near the Fermi level and that the coefficients
C_{\mu}
are even
functions of
\varepsilon
, one may bring the linearized collision integral Eq. (
5
) to the form
Nagaev and Manoshin 2020

I_{\mu}^{ee}(\varepsilon,\varphi)=\frac{2\,\Gamma_{ee}\cos\varphi}{T^{2}}\int\!d\varepsilon^{\prime}K(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})\biggl\{\ln\frac{E_{F}}{T}\,\Phi_{\mu}\bigl[C_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\\
-C_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\bigr]+\Psi_{\mu}\,\frac{p_{\mu}\,C_{-\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})-p_{-\mu}\,C_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})}{p_{1}+p_{-1}}\biggr\},

(12)

where
\Gamma_{ee}(T)=16\pi^{2}e^{4}\,d_{0}^{2}\,T^{2}\,(p_{1}+p_{-1})/32\pi^{3}\hbar^{5}\kappa^{2}v_{1}^{3}
is the effective
rate of electron–electron collisions,

\displaystyle K(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})=\bigl[1-\bar{f}(\varepsilon)\bigr]\,\frac{\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}}{e^{(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})/T}-1}\,\bar{f}(\varepsilon^{\prime}),

(13)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu}=4\,\frac{p_{\mu}+3\,p_{-\mu}}{p_{1}+p_{-1}},

(14)

and the explicit expressions for the dimensionless functions
\Psi_{\mu}(E_{F}/E_{SO})
are given in the Appendix. It is
clearly seen that
I^{ee}_{\mu}(\varepsilon,\varphi)
is turned into zero by the distribution of the form Eq. (
9
), i. e. with

p_{1}C_{-1}=p_{-1}C_{1}={\rm const}(\varepsilon)
. The logarithmic singularity in the first term in curly brackets in Eq.
(
12
) is a characteristic feature of 2D scattering that results from head-on and small-angle collisions and manifests
itself in the inverse quasiparticle lifetime
Hodges et al. 1971
;
Giuliani and Quinn 1982
and thermal conductivity
Lyakhov and Mishchenko 2003
for 2D
conductors with singly connected Fermi surface.

Assume that the electric field has a sinusoidal time dependence
{\bm{E}}(t)=e^{-i(\omega+i\delta)t}\,{\bm{E}}_{\omega}
,
where
\delta
is infinitely small and positive.
With the substitution Eq. (
11
), the Boltzmann equation Eq. (
4
) results in a system of two integral
equations in
C_{\mu}(\varepsilon)
. This system may be solved by the method first proposed by Brooker and Sykes
Brooker and Sykes 1968
. To
this end, we introduce a new variable

\rho_{\mu}(\varepsilon)=[\bar{f}\,(1-\bar{f})]^{1/2}\,C_{\mu}(\varepsilon),

(15)

which makes the kernel of the integral in Eq. (
12
) a function of the difference
\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon
. Therefore the system
of integral equations may be brought to the differential form by a Fourier transform

\tilde{\rho}_{\mu}(u)=\int d\varepsilon\,e^{-i\varepsilon u}\,\rho_{\mu}(\varepsilon).

(16)

A subsequent introduction of the new independent variable
\xi=\tanh(\pi Tu)
brings Eqs. (
4
) to the form

\Gamma_{ee}\left[\ln\frac{E_{F}}{T}\,\Phi_{\mu}\left(\hat{L}+2\right)\,\tilde{\rho}_{\mu}-2\,\Psi_{\mu}\,\frac{p_{-\mu}\,\tilde{\rho}_{\mu}-p_{\mu}\,\tilde{\rho}_{-\mu}}{p_{1}+p_{-1}}\right]\\
+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\,\frac{i\omega}{1-\xi^{2}}\,\tilde{\rho}_{\mu}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\,\frac{eEv_{\mu}}{\sqrt{1-\xi^{2}}},

(17)

where
\hat{L}
is the differential operator

\hat{L}\,\phi=\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\biggl[(1-\xi^{2})\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\xi}\biggr]-\frac{\phi}{1-\xi^{2}}.

(18)

The solutions of Eqs. (
17
) may be presented in the form of a series

\tilde{\rho}_{\mu}(\xi)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{\mu m}\,\phi_{2m}(\xi),

(19)

where
\phi_{m}(\xi)
are the eigenfunctions of operator
\hat{L}
with corresponding eigenvalues
-(m+1)(m+2)

Landau and Lifshitz 2013
. A substitution of the expansions Eqs. (
19
) into Eqs. (
17
) results
in an infinite system of equations for the coefficients
\gamma_{\mu m}
of the form

2\,\Gamma_{ee}\biggl[m\,(2m+3)\,\ln\frac{E_{F}}{T}\,\Phi_{\mu}\,\gamma_{\mu m}\\
+\Psi_{\mu}\,\frac{p_{-\mu}\,\gamma_{\mu m}-p_{\mu}\,\gamma_{-\mu m}}{p_{1}+p_{-1}}\biggr]-i\,\frac{\omega}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Y_{mn}\,\gamma_{\mu n}\\
=eEv_{\mu}\,X_{m}/\pi.

(20)

where
Y_{mn}
are the matrix elements of
(1-\xi^{2})^{-1}
between
\phi_{2m}
and
\phi_{2n}
, and
X_{m}
are the projections
of
(1-\xi^{2})^{-1/2}
on
\phi_{2m}
. The explicit expressions for these quantities are given in the Appendix.
The current density is given by the sum
Nagaev and Manoshin 2020

j=\frac{e}{8\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\mu}p_{\mu}\sgn v_{\mu}\sum_{m}X_{m}\,\gamma_{\mu m}.

(21)

In the high-frequency limit, one easily obtains directly from Eq. (
4
) that
C_{\mu}=eE_{\omega}v_{\mu}/\omega T

and therefore the imaginary part of the response is

\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{0}=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\hbar^{2}\omega}\,v_{1}(p_{1}+p_{-1}),

(22)

while the dissipative part
\sigma^{\prime}
is zero. Equation (
22
) coincides with the results of
Maiti et al. 2015
.
In the opposite limit
\Gamma_{ee}\gg\omega
, one cannot simply set
\omega=0

because of the existence of the perturbation (
9
) with zero relaxation rate, which leads to the divergence of

\gamma_{\mu 0}
. This divergence can be eliminated by keeping
\omega
small but finite and isolating the most singular
in
\omega
contribution to
\sigma
. This contribution may be obtained by setting
\gamma_{\mu m}=0
for all
m\neq 0

and solving the resulting systems of two equations for
\gamma_{\mu 0}
. The resulting conductivity is

\sigma_{ee}=i\,\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\hbar^{2}}\,\frac{v_{1}}{\omega+i\delta}\\
\times\frac{(p_{1}^{2}+\sgn E_{F}\,p_{-1}^{2})(\Psi_{-1}+\sgn E_{F}\,\Psi_{1})}{\Psi_{-1}\,p_{1}+\Psi_{1}\,p_{-1}}.

(23)

At
\omega\neq 0
, it
is purely imaginary and inversely proportional to
\omega
, like
\sigma_{0}^{\prime\prime}
. Though it is temperature-independent,
it still depends on the properties of electron–electron scattering through the quantities
\Psi_{\pm 1}
.

In the leading approximation, the real part of conductivity is proportional to
\delta(\omega)
.
The dissipative part of conductivity at
\omega\neq 0
is given by the subleading term, which is
independent of
\omega
. To calculate it,
one should, in principle, take into account the components of Eq. (
19
) with higher
m
. The most singular
parts of
\gamma_{\mu 0}
should be substituted into Eqs. (
20
) with
m\neq 0
, and the solutions should
be substituted back into Eqs. (
20
) with
m=0
. However because of the condition
\ln(E_{F}/T)\gg 1
,
the contribution from
\gamma_{\mu m}
with
m>0
is small, and it is sufficient to find the subleading term in the
equations with
m=0
. Therefore at
\omega\neq 0
,

\sigma_{ee}^{\prime}=\frac{3}{16\pi^{3}}\,\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\,\frac{v_{1}\,(p_{1}+p_{-1})}{\Gamma_{ee}}\\
\times\frac{(p_{1}-\sgn E_{F}\,p_{-1})(\Psi_{-1}p_{-1}-\sgn E_{F}\,\Psi_{1}p_{1})}{(\Psi_{-1}p_{1}+\Psi_{1}p_{-1})^{2}}.

(24)

It is noteworthy that neither
\sigma^{\prime}_{ee}
nor
\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{ee}
contains the
\ln^{-1}(E_{F}/T)
factor,
much like the dc conductivity in a presence of weak impurity scattering
Nagaev and Manoshin 2020
.

At
E_{SO}\ll E_{F}
, the scattering corrections to the conductivity are proportional to
E_{SO}^{3/2}
, so that

\sigma^{\prime}_{ee}\approx\frac{3\,e^{2}E_{SO}^{3/2}}{64\pi^{3}\hbar^{2}\Gamma_{ee}E_{F}^{1/2}},\quad\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{ee}-\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{0}\approx-\frac{e^{2}E_{SO}^{3/2}}{\pi\hbar^{2}\omega E_{F}^{1/2}}.

(25)

V
Discussion

The dependence of the imaginary and real parts of conductivity on the Fermi energy at
\omega\ll E_{SO}
is shown in Figs.

2
and
3
. It is clearly seen that
\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{ee}
is suppressed relatively to

\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{0}
both at
E_{F}>0
and
E_{F}<0
and the electron–electron scattering reduces the Drude weight.
This suppression is especially pronounced near the bottom of the lower subband,
where
\sigma_{0}^{\prime\prime}\propto(E_{F}+E_{SO})^{1/2}
and
\sigma_{ee}^{\prime\prime}\propto(E_{F}+E_{SO})^{3/2}
. At
E_{F}=0
, the
electron–electron scattering does not affect the conductivity. It is noteworthy that
unlike
\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{0}
,
\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{ee}
exhibits no kink and
d\sigma_{ee}^{\prime\prime}/dE_{F}
is continuous at this point.

In contrast to the imaginary part, the dissipative part
\sigma_{ee}^{\prime}
scales as
T^{-2}
even at
\Gamma_{ee}\gg\omega
.
Another clear distinction from
\sigma_{ee}^{\prime\prime}
is the nonmonotone
\sigma_{ee}^{\prime}(E_{F})
dependence with
\sigma_{ee}^{\prime}(0)=0
.
The disappearance of dissipation at
E_{F}=0
is quite natural because the inner Fermi contour shrinks into a point at this
Fermi energy and the Fermi surface becomes effectively singly connected. Our results for
\sigma^{\prime}
sharply differ from the

T^{2}/\omega^{2}
dependence obtained in
Farid and Mishchenko 2006
in the low-frequency limit using second-order perturbation theory in

V_{0}
. The suppression of
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
is a consequence of the emergence of a finite dissipation, in agreement with the
Kramers–Kronig relations. Unlike the suppression predicted by Agarwal et al.
Agarwal et al. 2011
, it is temperature-dependent
and takes place even at weak electron–electron interaction.

Suppose now that the material is not perfectly clean.
An important question is how the dissipative conductivity
\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{ee}\propto\Gamma_{ee}^{-1}
given by
Eq. (
24
) is related with the dc conductivity found to be inversely proportional to the
impurity scattering rate
\Gamma_{imp}
in Ref.
Nagaev and Manoshin 2020
. The answer is given by Fig.
4
,
which shows the frequency dependence
of
\sigma^{\prime}
in a presence of the electron–electron and much weaker impurity scattering. This dependence
exhibits two clearly seen plateaus at
\omega\leq\Gamma_{imp}
and at
\omega\gg\Gamma_{imp}
. The former
plateau presents the results of
Nagaev and Manoshin 2020
, while the latter corresponds to Eq. (
24
). In other words, the
results of
Nagaev and Manoshin 2020
correspond to the limit
\omega\ll\Gamma_{imp}
, while Eq. (
24
) corresponds to

\omega\gg\Gamma_{imp}
. Naturally, the presence of impurities eliminates the
\delta(\omega)
peak.

The finite-frequency dissipation may be more convenient for experimental investigations of electron--electron scattering
than the dc conductivity because it does not depend on the type of impurities in the conductor

.
Though this dissipation is nonzero both above and below the band-crossing point
E_{F}=0
, it may be more
conveniently observed below this point. One of the conditions is
\omega\ll\Gamma_{ee}
.
For example, for InAs, which exhibits
Rashba parameter
\hbar\alpha=1.2\,{\rm eV\AA}

Heedt et al. 2017
, the electron concentration
10^{10}
cm
-2
,
the 2D gas–gate distance of 100 nm, and
T=4
K, the frequency has to be smaller than
\Gamma_{ee}\approx 0.5
THz.
On the other hand, these experiments would require high-quality samples because the electron–impurity scattering length has
to be much larger than
l_{ee}\sim 40
nm.

The above calculations were performed for point-like electron–electron interactions because this model allows an
analytical solution. However the existence of zero-relaxation modes, which is their cornerstone, stems from very general
properties of the system like translational invariance and Fermi statistics, so the suppression of Drude weight and
finite dissipation are not the consequences of point-like interaction and should be also observed for a long-range potential.

Appendix A
Explicit expressions for some quantities

The quantities
\Phi_{\mu}
and
\Psi_{\mu}
in the collision integral Eq. (
12
) are defined by the expressions

\ln\frac{E_{F}}{T}\,\Phi_{\mu}=\sum_{\mu_{1}}\sum_{\mu_{2}}\sum_{\mu_{3}}\frac{p_{\mu_{1}}}{p_{\mu}+p_{-\mu}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\chi\,(1-\mu\,\mu_{1}\cos\chi)\\
\times\Theta({\cal D}_{\mu..\mu_{3}})\,{\cal D}_{\mu..\mu_{3}}^{\mu_{2}\mu_{3}/2},

(26)

and

\Psi_{\mu}=\sum_{\mu_{1}}\sum_{\mu_{2}}\sum_{\mu_{3}}\frac{p_{\mu_{1}}}{p_{-\mu}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\chi\,(1-\mu\,\mu_{1}\cos\chi)\,\Theta({\cal D}_{\mu..\mu_{3}})\\
\times{\cal D}_{\mu..\mu_{3}}^{\mu_{2}\mu_{3}/2}\,\left(1-\delta_{\mu\mu_{1}}\cos\chi-2\,\delta_{\mu\mu_{2}}\lambda_{\mu..\mu_{3}}\right),

(27)

where

\mu..\mu_{3}
stands for
\mu\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}
,

{\cal D}_{\mu..\mu_{3}}=\frac{(p_{2}+p_{3})^{2}-p^{2}-p_{1}^{2}-2\,p\,p_{1}\cos\chi}{p^{2}+p_{1}^{2}+2\,p\,p_{1}\cos\chi-(p_{2}-p_{3})^{2}},

(28)

and

\lambda_{\mu..\mu_{3}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{p_{2}^{2}-p_{3}^{2}+p^{2}+p_{1}^{2}+2\,p\,p_{1}\cos\chi}{p^{2}+p_{1}^{2}+2\,p\,p_{1}\cos\chi}\\
\times(p+p_{1}\cos\chi)/{p_{2}}.

(29)

The normalized eigenfunctions of differential operator
\hat{L}
Eq. (
18
) are given by
the expressions

\phi_{m}(\xi)=\sqrt{\frac{(2m+3)(m+2)}{8\,(m+1)}}\sqrt{1-\xi^{2}}\,P_{m}^{(1,1)}(\xi),

(30)

where
P_{m}^{(1,1)}(\xi)
are Jacobi polynomials. The coefficients of expansion of
(1-\xi^{2})^{-1/2}

in these functions are given by

X_{m}=\int_{-1}^{1}d\xi\,\frac{\phi_{2m}(\xi)}{\sqrt{1-\xi^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{4m+3}{(2m+1)(m+1)}}.

(31)

The matrix elements of
1/(1-\xi^{2})
between the eigenfunctions of
\hat{L}
are given by the equation

Y_{mn}=\int_{-1}^{1}d\xi\,\frac{\phi_{2m}(\xi)\,\phi_{2n}(\xi)}{1-\xi^{2}}=\frac{\min(m,n)+1/2}{\max(m,n)+1}\\
\times\sqrt{\frac{(4m+3)(m+1)(4n+3)(n+1)}{(2m+1)(2n+1)}}.

(32)
