An average intersection estimate for families of diffeomorphisms
Axel Kodat, Michael Shub

Abstract
. We show that for any sufficiently rich compact family ℋ \mathcal{H} of C 1 C^{1} diffeomorphisms of a closed Riemannanian manifold M M , the average geometric intersection number over h ∈ ℋ h\in\mathcal{H} between h ⁡ ( V ) h(V) and W W , for V , W V,W any complementary dimensional submanifolds of M M , is approximately (i.e. up to a uniform multiplicative error depending only on ℋ \mathcal{H} ) the product of their volumes. We also give a construction showing that such families always exist.

Let

In this note we study conditions on compact

Suppose

\operatorname{(A1)}

ev:\mathcal{H}\times M\to M\times M

1.1.
Background and motivation

Formulas of the type considered here have a long history in integral geometry and a well-developed theory in the setting of homogeneous spaces of compact Lie groups. From this perspective the basic antecedent to our result is

Theorem 1.3

(Poincaré’s formula for homogeneous spaces)
.

Let
M^{n}
be a closed Riemannian manifold with
G
a compact Lie group acting smoothly and transitively on
M
by isometries. Suppose the action is also transitive on
\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(TM)
, for every
1\leq k\leq n-1
. Then for every
k,\ell\geq 0
satisfying
k+\ell\geq n

C_{k,\ell}>0
such that for any submanifolds
V,W\subset M
with
\dim(V)=k
and
\dim(W)=\ell
, we have

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{g\in G}\operatorname{vol}(gV\cap W)\ dG=C_{k,\ell}\operatorname{vol}(V)\operatorname{vol}(W),

where

denotes the Haar measure on
G

In the form given here this result is due to Howard
[
How93
]
. The basic idea behind the proof is to view the left-hand side as a double integral over what we will later call the
intersection manifold
associated to
V
and

and
W

over all
(x,y)\in V\times W
. (In other words, one replaces the average volume of intersections with the average volume of the spaces of maps realizing each possible intersection.) Since all such spaces are isometric for any bi-invariant metric on
G
inducing the Haar measure, the result follows. For details, see
[
How93
, §3]
or
[
BC13
, §A.4]
. Our adaptation of this approach below is particularly indebted to
[
BCSS98
, §13]
and
[
SS94
, §4]
. For a classic presentation of the foundational material, see
[
San04
]
.

Observe that any action

can be viewed as a compact

family of diffeomorphisms of

)
up to a uniform multiplicative error

Our primary motivation for formulating such an extension comes from smooth dynamics.

1.2.
Assumptions

Theorem
1.1
is a “backwards” theorem in the sense that we started with the estimate (
1
) and looked for reasonable (and in particular abundantly realized) assumptions that would make it true. Here we collect some comments on our eventual selections (A1) and (A2).

First, we emphasize that the statement of Theorem
1.1
is not quite optimal, in two main ways:

(i)

The upper bound in (
1
) actually follows from (A1) alone; the surjectivity assumption (A2) is needed only for the lower bound. Note also that (A1), in contrast to (A2), is a purely local property of
(\mathcal{H},\psi)
. Since the property (A1) is also easily seen to be invariant under (either left- or right-) translation of the family by a diffeomorphism
g
, this implies that an upper bound as in (
1
) can be obtained for finite-dimensional slices of
\operatorname{Diff}^{1}(M)
concentrated arbitrarily close to any given diffeomorphism
f
.

(ii)

In the other direction, it follows from the proof that (A2) is somewhat stronger than necessary for our result. What we really need is the following:

\operatorname{(A2^{@itemi})}

For any
\sigma_{p}\in\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(TM)
and
\sigma_{q}\in\operatorname{Gr}_{n-k}(TM)
, there is an
h\in\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{H}}
such

.
that
h(p)=q
and
dh(\sigma_{p})\pitchfork\sigma_{q}
.

For further discussion of this point see the remark after Claim
2.9
. We have opted to use
\operatorname{(A2)}
in the statement of Theorem
1.1
nonetheless, primarily to emphasize the analogy with the transitivity assumption in Theorem
1.3
, and also because it is no more difficult in practice to construct a family satisfying
\operatorname{(A2)}
than one satisfying the weaker condition
\operatorname{(A2^{*})}
.

These qualifications in mind, the purpose of our assumptions is heuristically the following. The assumption that
ev
is a submersion implies that no particular intersection
h(p)=q
between
h(V)\cap W
can be locally persistent (since one can move
h(p)
off itself by a small change in
h
). In particular, any non-transversal intersection between
h(V)
and
W
(near which the cardinality of
h(V)\cap W
could explode) can be destroyed by a small perturbation of
h
. This ensures that almost all intersections are isolated, and moreover that the total number of such points (summed over
\mathcal{H}
) is never too large compared to
\operatorname{vol}(V)\cdot\operatorname{vol}(W)
. This yields the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
using (A1) alone.

To get a lower bound, one needs to show that
\mathcal{H}
can always
produce
intersections between pairs of points in
V
and
W
. Moreover, since the quantity we are trying to bound is a sort of average of the number of intersections with respect to the volume measure on
\mathcal{H}
, these intersections should persist over open (thus positive measure) subsets of
\mathcal{H}
. In other words,
\mathcal{H}
should be able to produce
transverse
intersections between any pair of points in
V
and
W

\mathcal{H}
can move any tangential

-plane to any other, as guaranteed by (A2).

The following example cleanly illustrates the main ideas in the isometric case:

Example 1.4

(

1.3.
A digression on
\mathcal{H}

One technical hurdle in proving Theorem
1.1
stems from our allowance of the possibility that the parameter space
\mathcal{H}
has boundary. If one assumes instead that
\partial\mathcal{H}=\varnothing
, the availability of Ehresmann’s fibration theorem (applied to the submersion
ev
) substantially streamlines the proof; see the remark concluding §
2.4
. However, here Ehresmann’s theorem cuts both ways, imposing strong topological constraints on any boundaryless
\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\operatorname{(A1)}
. These constraints imply in particular that for general
M
, the condition
\partial\mathcal{H}=\varnothing
is too much to ask:

Proposition 1.5
.

Suppose
M
is a closed manifold and
(\mathcal{H},\psi)
a compact, connected family of
C^{1}
diffeomorphims of
M
with
\partial\mathcal{H}=\varnothing
, such that
ev_{p}
is a submersion for some

. Then
\pi_{1}(M)
is a finite extension of a quotient of a subgroup of
\pi_{1}(\operatorname{Diff}_{0}(M))
.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{H},\psi)
be such a family. After translating and perturbing we may assume that
\psi(\mathcal{H})\subset\operatorname{Diff}_{0}(M)
the identity component of
\operatorname{Diff}^{\infty}(M)
. Choose
p\in M
such that
ev_{p}
a submersion.

By Ehresmann’s theorem,
ev_{p}:\mathcal{H}\to M
is a locally trivial fibration, say with fiber
\mathcal{F}
. The end of the homotopy long exact sequence for this fibration reads

\pi_{1}(\mathcal{H})\xrightarrow{\ (ev_{p})_{*}\ }\pi_{1}(M)\xrightarrow[\ \ \ \ ]{}\pi_{0}(\mathcal{F})\xrightarrow[\ \ \ \ ]{}1.

Since
\mathcal{H}
and (therefore)
\mathcal{F}
are compact we must have
\#\pi_{0}(\mathcal{F})<\infty
, so this sequence implies that
(ev_{p})_{*}
is virtually onto. On the other hand,
ev_{p}=EV_{p}\circ\psi
where
EV_{p}:\operatorname{Diff}_{0}(M)\to M
,
f\mapsto f(p)
. Thus
(ev_{p})_{*}
factors through
\pi_{1}(\operatorname{Diff}_{0}(M))
, and the result follows.
∎

Corollary 1.6
.

Suppose
M
is a closed hyperbolic manifold with
\dim(M)\leq 3
. Then there is no boundaryless compact family of diffeomorphisms of
M
satisfying
\operatorname{(A1)}
.

Proof.

By work of Earle-Eells (in dimension 2) and Gabai (in dimension 3),
\operatorname{Diff}_{0}(M)
is contractible (
[
EE69
]
,
[
Gab01
]
). In particular,
\pi_{1}(\operatorname{Diff}_{0}(M))=0
. Since (by hyperbolicity of

\pi_{1}(M)
is infinite, by Proposition

∎

The upshot here is that Theorem
1.2
fails if one requires that
\partial\mathcal{H}=\varnothing
. More strongly, these results (combined with the restrictions on higher homotopy groups obtained by continuing the above exact sequence) suggest that manifolds which admit a submersive compact family of diffeomorphisms without boundary may be quite rare. Clearly homogeneous spaces of compact Lie groups form the main class of examples. Are there others?

Question 1.7
.

Which closed manifolds
M
admit a compact smooth family of diffeomorphisms
\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Diff}(M)

Proposition 3.1

Note that the function

is well-defined only at regular values
q
of

, for which

is an embedded
k
-dimensional submanifold of
M
with induced Riemannian density

. But then by Sard’s theorem it is defined a.e. on

, so (provided it is measurable) its integral over
N
makes sense. This application of Sard’s is the main reason for the required relation between the regularity of

and

and explains why this regularity assumption is unnecessary when

is a submersion, since in that case
every

is a regular value for

. Indeed, in the submersive case the co-area formula is an easy consequence of Fubini’s theorem.

More general versions of the co-area formula avoid these issues entirely by replacing

with the
k
-dimensional Hausdorff measure associated to the metric space structure on
M
; see in particular Proposition
4.3
. These measures agree when
F_{q}
is a
k
-dimensional submanifold of
M
.

We now apply the co-area formula to prove the following, the main result of this section:

Proposition 3.2
.

For
\mathcal{H},V,
and
W
as in Theorem
1.1
we have

\displaystyle\int_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\#(h(V)\cap W)\ d\mathcal{H}=\displaystyle\int_{(p,q)\in V\times W}\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}_{p,q}}\dfrac{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}\ d\mathcal{V}_{p,q}\right)dVdW,

with both sides finite if one is.

Proof.

Recall the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{V}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{H}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V\times W}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}\ \ \ }
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}

of Riemannian manifolds and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}}\ \operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})\ d\mathcal{V}=\displaystyle\int_{h\in\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{H}}}\left(\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}d\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\right)d\mathcal{H}=\displaystyle\int_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\#(h(V)\cap W)\ d\mathcal{H}.

for a.e.

4.
Generalization

We now describe the generalization of Theorem
1.1
to the case when
\dim(V)+\dim(W)\geq\dim(M)
, where
\#(h(V)\cap W)
is replaced by the appropriate-dimensional Hausdorff measure associated to the metric space structure on
M
.

For
r\geq 0
and any Riemannian manifold
X
, let
H_{X}^{r}
denote the
r
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
X
associated to its induced metric space structure. When
r=\dim(X)
,
H_{X}^{r}
agrees with the volume measure
\nu_{X}
induced by the metric on
X
. More generally, if
Y\subset X
is a
k
-dimensional submanifold with Riemannian metric inherited from
X
and induced volume measure
\nu_{Y}
, we have

H_{X}^{k}\big|_{Y}=H_{Y}^{k}=\nu_{Y}.

In particular, the volume of a
k
-dimensional submanifold of
X
is equal to its
k
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
X
. In this sense, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
one can think of
H_{X}^{k}
as a generalization of
k
-dimensional volume to non-smooth subsets of
X
.

We now consider a fixed closed Riemannian manifold
M
of dimension
n
. For simplicity we write
H^{r}:=H^{r}_{M}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
(\mathcal{H},\psi)
is compact
C^{1}
family of diffeomorphisms of
M
such that

\operatorname{(A1)}

ev:\mathcal{H}\times M\to M\times M
defined by
ev(h,p)=(p,h(p))
is a submersion;

\operatorname{(A2)}

\accentset{\circ}{ev}_{k}:\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{H}}\times\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(TM)\to\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(TM)\times\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(TM)
defined by
ev_{k}(h,\sigma)=(\sigma,dh(\sigma))
is surjective for all
0\leq k\leq n-1
.

Then there is a constant
C=C(\mathcal{H},\psi)\geq 1
such that for any submanifolds
V^{k},W^{\ell}\subset M
with
k+\ell\geq n
, we have

(4)

\dfrac{1}{C}\operatorname{vol}(V)\operatorname{vol}(W)\ \ \leq\ \ \displaystyle\int_{h\in\mathcal{H}}H^{k+\ell-n}(h(V)\cap W)\ d\mathcal{H}\ \ \leq\ \ C\operatorname{vol}(V)\operatorname{vol}(W).\\

The need to work with Hausdorff measures rather than volumes here derives from our insistence on assuming only
C^{1}
regularity. In this context, when
\dim(V)+\dim(W)>\dim(M)
we can no longer assume that
h(V)\pitchfork W
for a.e.
h\in\mathcal{H}
; thus
\operatorname{vol}(h(V)\cap W)
is not necessarily a.e. well-defined. However, if we let
h(V)\cap_{T}W
denote the set of transversal intersection points between
h(V)
and
W
(which when nonempty is always a
(k+\ell-n)
-dimensional submanifold of
M
), we will see below that indeed

H^{k+\ell-m}(h(V)\cap W)=H^{k+\ell-m}(h(V)\cap_{T}W)=\operatorname{vol}(h(V)\cap_{T}W)

for a.e.
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

Note that, since
H^{0}
is the counting measure on
M
, Theorem
4.1
does indeed include Theorem
1.1
as a special case. Moreover, the proof method is essentially the same. Thus in this section we will content ourselves with some remarks on the modifications needed to adapt our proof of Theorem
1.1
to the general setting.

Let
V^{k},W^{\ell}\subset M
be submanifolds with
k+\ell\geq n
. The solution manifold
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}\subset\mathcal{H}\times M\times M
remains defined as before, and again (A1) implies that

\mathcal{V}:=\left({\tilde{\pi}_{2}}|_{\text{int}(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}})}\right)^{-1}(V\times W)

is a
C^{1}
submanifold of
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
. Now, however, we have

\dim(\mathcal{V})=(d+n)-\operatorname{codim}(V\times W)=(d+n)-(2n-k-\ell)=d+k+\ell-n.

The basic observation motivating these definitions and underlying our entire proof strategy for Theorem
1.1
was that

\#(h(V)\cap W)=\#(\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)),

or equivalently,

H^{0}_{M}(h(V)\cap W)=H^{0}_{\mathcal{V}}(\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)).

The reason was that there is an obvious bijection between these sets (given explicitly by
\pi_{3}:\mathcal{V}\to M
, defined below), and bijections between metric spaces preserve
0
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. Obviously this fails for higher dimensional Hausdorff measures, so for the general case we must attend to how the maps
\pi_{3}^{h}:=\pi_{3}|_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}:\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\to h(V)\cap W
transform
(k+\ell-n)
-dimensional Hausdorff measures, where
\pi_{3}:\mathcal{V}\to W
is defined as one would expect, by
\pi_{3}(h,p,q)=q
.
8
8

8

In fact this issue is largely an artifact of our original choice of set-up: it can be avoided by replacing
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
with
\mathcal{H}\times M
and
\widetilde{\pi}_{2}:\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}\to M\times M
by the map
\mathcal{H}\times M\to M\times M
,
(h,q)\mapsto(h^{-1}(q),q)
, since for these choices the resulting identification of
\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)
with
h(V)\cap W
is isometric. See
[
BC13
]
for an example of this approach.

First suppose that
h
is a regular value of
\pi_{1}
; this is true iff
h(V)\pitchfork W
. In this case,
\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)
and
h(V)\cap W
are
(k+\ell-n)
-dimensional submanifolds of
\mathcal{V}
and
M
, respectively, and
\pi_{3}^{h}
is a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism. Their
(k+\ell-n)
-Hausdorff measures then agree with the volume measures induced by their Riemannian structures, and moreover we have

\displaystyle H^{k+\ell-n}_{M}(h(V)\cap W)=\operatorname{vol}(h(V)\cap W)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{vol}(\pi_{3}(\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)))

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}\left\lVert\textstyle\bigwedge^{k+\ell-n}d\pi_{3}^{h}\right\rVert d\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}\left\lVert\textstyle\bigwedge^{k+\ell-n}d\pi_{3}^{h}\right\rVert dH^{k+\ell-n}_{\mathcal{V}}.

Note also that

d\pi^{h}_{3}(h,p,q)=d\pi_{3}|_{T_{(h,p,q)}{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}}=d\pi_{3}|_{\ker(d\pi_{1}(h,p,q))},

and this last expression is well-defined even when
(h,p,q)
is not a regular point for
\pi_{1}
. Thus we can define

\rho:\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

by

\rho(h,p,q)=\left\lVert\textstyle\bigwedge^{k+\ell-n}d\pi_{3}\big|_{\ker(d\pi_{1}(h,p,q))}\right\rVert,

and the above equation becomes

(5)

H^{k+\ell-n}_{M}(h(V)\cap W)=\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}\rho\ dH^{k+\ell-n}_{\mathcal{V}}.

So far, (
5
) holds only when
h
is a regular value of
\pi_{1}
. In the complementary dimensional case this was enough, since when
\dim(\mathcal{H})=\dim(\mathcal{V})
Sard’s theorem applies and thus (
5
) holds for a.e.
h\in\mathcal{H}
. When
\dim(V)+\dim(W)>\dim(M)
, however, we have
\dim(\mathcal{V})>\dim(\mathcal{H})
, so Sard’s no longer applies (since
\pi_{1}
can only be assumed
C^{1}
). We appeal instead to the following generalization:

Proposition 4.2

(
[
Sim14
]
, Theorem 6.4)
.

Let
f:M^{m}\to N^{n}
be a
C^{1}
map between Riemannian manifolds, and let
C\subset M
denote the set of critical points of
f
. Set
r=\max\{0,m-n\}
. Then

(6)

H^{r}_{M}(f^{-1}(y)\cap C)=0

for Lebesgue a.e.
y\in N
.

Remark
.

The interesting case (and the one relevant here) is when
m>n
. As Simon notes in
[
Sim14
]
, this proposition is itself a straightfoward consequence of Federer’s general co-area formula; see
[
Fed69
, §3.2]
for details.

To apply this to the present setting, first write
C\subset\mathcal{V}
for the set of critical points of
\pi_{1}
, and for
h\in\mathcal{H}
, let
h(V)\cap_{T}W
denote the set of transversal intersection points between
h(V)
and
W
, and
h(V)\cap_{NT}W=(h(V)\cap W)\setminus(h(V)\cap_{T}W)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\pi_{3}(\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\setminus C)

\displaystyle=h(V)\cap_{T}W,

\displaystyle\pi_{3}(\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\cap C)

\displaystyle=h(V)\cap_{NT}W,

and

\displaystyle H^{k+\ell-n}_{M}(h(V)\cap W)

\displaystyle=H^{k+\ell-n}_{M}(h(V)\cap_{T}W)+H^{k+\ell-n}_{M}(h(V)\cap_{NT}W)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\setminus C}\rho\ dH^{k+\ell-n}_{\mathcal{V}}+H^{k+\ell-n}_{M}(\pi_{3}(\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\cap C)).

By Proposition
4.2
,

\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\setminus C}\rho\ dH^{k+\ell-n}_{\mathcal{V}}=\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}\rho\ dH^{k+\ell-n}_{\mathcal{V}}

for a.e.
h\in\mathcal{H}
. For the second term, observe that
\pi_{3}=\widetilde{\pi}_{3}|_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}
, where
\widetilde{\pi}_{3}:\widetilde{\pi}_{1}^{-1}(h)\to M
is a distance non-increasing diffeomorphism (indeed
\widetilde{\pi}_{1}^{-1}(h)
is canonically identified with
\Gamma(h)\subset M\times M
, with
\widetilde{\pi}_{3}
corresponding to projection onto the second coordinate), so that

H^{k+\ell-n}_{M}(\pi_{3}(\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\cap C))\leq H^{k+\ell-n}_{\mathcal{V}}(\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\cap C).

Since the right-hand side is zero for a.e.
h\in\mathcal{H}
by Proposition
4.2
, so is the left. We conclude that

\displaystyle H^{k+\ell-n}_{M}(h(V)\cap W)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\setminus C}\rho\ dH^{k+\ell-n}_{\mathcal{V}}+H^{k+\ell-n}_{M}(\pi_{3}(\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)\cap C))

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}\rho\ dH^{k+\ell-n}_{\mathcal{V}}

for Lebesgue a.e.
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

To apply this formula, we need the following slightly more general form of the co-area formula:

Proposition 4.3

(The co-area formula,
C^{1}
case)
.

Let
f:M^{m}\to N^{n}
be a
C^{1}
map between Riemannian manifolds,
m\geq n
. For

, write
F_{q}=f^{-1}(q)
. Then for any Borel measurable function
h

M

\displaystyle\int_{M}h\cdot\operatorname{NJ}(f)\ dM=\displaystyle\int_{q\in N}\left(\displaystyle\int_{F_{q}}h\ dH^{m-n}_{N}\right)dN,

with both sides finite if one is.

We can now proceed as before. First, apply Proposition
4.3
twice to deduce

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{h\in\mathcal{H}}H^{k+\ell-n}(h(V)\cap W)\ d\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left(\displaystyle\int_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(h)}\rho\ dH^{k+\ell-n}_{\mathcal{V}}\right)\ d\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}}\rho\cdot\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})\ d\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{(p,q)\in V\times W}\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}_{p,q}}\dfrac{\rho\cdot\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}\ d\mathcal{V}_{p,q}\right)dVdW,

where
\mathcal{V}_{p,q}=\pi_{2}^{-1}(p,q)
as before. The proof now reduces to showing that the integrand
\frac{\rho\cdot\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}
can be viewed as a continuous function on the appropriate Grassmannian bundle
\mathcal{G}_{k,\ell}
over
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
, which is positive somewhere on each fiber of the submersion
\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{G}}_{k,\ell}\to\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(TM)\times\operatorname{Gr}_{\ell}(TM).

The space
\mathcal{G}_{k,\ell}\subset\mathcal{H}\times\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(TM)\times\operatorname{Gr}_{\ell}(TM)
, the bundle

, and the functions
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
, and

(so e.g. in our original notation,
\mathcal{G}_{k}=\mathcal{G}_{k,n-k}
). Again,
\eta:\mathcal{G}_{k,\ell}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
is continuous, and positive at
(h,\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q})
iff
dh(\sigma_{p})+\sigma_{q}=T_{q}M
. To extend
\rho
to a function on
\mathcal{G}_{k,\ell}
, simply define

\displaystyle\Pi_{3}(h,\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q}):E_{k,\ell}(h,\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow T_{q}M

\displaystyle(\dot{h},v,w)

\displaystyle\longmapsto w,

and
\widehat{\rho}:\mathcal{G}_{k,\ell}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
by

\widehat{\rho}(h,\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q})=\left\lVert\textstyle\bigwedge^{k+\ell-n}\Pi_{3}(h,\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q})\big|_{\ker(\Pi_{1}(h,\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q}))}\right\rVert.

Clearly we have

\widehat{\rho}(h,T_{p}V,T_{q}W)=\rho(h,p,q).

Moreover,
\widehat{\rho}
is continuous at all points where

5.
Construction of
(\mathcal{H},\psi)

In this section we show that Theorems
1.1
and
4.1
are non-vacuous, by constructing, for an arbitrary closed manifold
M
, a compact
C^{1}
family of diffeomorphisms of
M
satisfying hypotheses (A1) and (A2). This proves Theorem
1.2
.

Actually, since it is no more difficult (and arguably more natural) to work in the smooth category here, the family we construct will be
C^{\infty}
. In addition, we will be unconcerned with compactness until the end. Specifically, we first obtain a
C^{\infty}
family
({\mathbb{R}}^{N},\psi)
of the form

\psi:({\mathbb{R}}^{N},0)\xrightarrow[\ \ \ ]{}(\operatorname{Diff}^{\infty}(M),\operatorname{id})

which satisfies hypothesis (A1) and (A2), such that the surjectivity requirements of (A2) are satisfied by
({\mathbb{B}}^{N}_{R},\psi|_{{\mathbb{B}}^{N}_{R}})
for all
R
sufficiently large.
9
9

9

Here and below we write
{\mathbb{B}}^{N}_{R},\overline{{\mathbb{B}}}^{N}_{R}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
for the open and closed balls, respectively, centered at
0
of radius
R
, with the subscript occasionally omitted in the special case
R=1
, i.e.
{\mathbb{B}}^{N}:={\mathbb{B}}^{N}_{1}
.
The desired compact family is then obtained by restricting
\psi
to
\overline{{\mathbb{B}}}^{N}_{R}
for some large
R
.

We begin with some general lemmas. Recall that for
1\leq r\leq\infty
, a
C^{r}
family of diffeomorphisms of
M
is a pair
(\mathcal{H},\psi)
, with
\mathcal{H}
a smooth manifold, possibly with boundary, and
\psi:\mathcal{H}\to\operatorname{Diff}^{r}(M)
such that the associated evaluation map
ev_{\psi}:\mathcal{H}\to M
is
C^{r}
; this implies in particular that
\psi
is continuous. Any
C^{r}
family of diffeomorphisms of
M
is, of course, a
C^{s}
family of diffeomorphisms for all
s<r
.

One motivation for interpreting smoothness in this way—instead of via the strictly stronger requirement that
\psi\in C^{r}(\mathcal{H},\operatorname{Diff}^{r}(M))
for the standard Banach/Fréchet manifold structure on
\operatorname{Diff}^{r}(M)
—is the following elementary fact, which shows that the class of
C^{r}
families of diffeomorphisms in our weaker sense is in a natural sense closed under composition.
10
10

10

That this fails for the stronger notion is a consequence of the well-known fact that the composition map
\left[\operatorname{Diff}^{r}(M)\right]^{2}\to\operatorname{Diff}^{r}(M)
is only
C^{0}
when
r<\infty
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{H}_{1},\psi_{1})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{2},\psi_{2})
be
C^{r}
families of diffeomorphisms of
M
, with
\partial\mathcal{H}_{1}=\partial\mathcal{H}_{2}=\varnothing
. Define

\psi:\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}\xrightarrow[\ \ \ ]{}\operatorname{Diff}^{r}(M)

by

6.
Appendix A: Submersions from Manifolds with Boundary

Our inclusion of the case
\partial\mathcal{H}\neq\varnothing
necessitates a discussion of some basic properties of submersions from compact Riemannian manifolds with boundary. These maps are messier than submersions from compact manifolds without boundary, essentially because their fibers can intersect the boundary badly. Most dramatically, the fibers need not be manifolds (even with boundary) in general. For similar reasons, submersions from manifolds with boundary need not be open maps at boundary points, and (most relevant for our purposes) the locally trivial fibration property enjoyed by proper submersions between manifolds without boundary (known as Ehresmann’s theorem) fails completely in this setting.
13
13

13

All these pathologies are easily illustrated by maps of the form
\pi:D\to{\mathbb{R}}
where
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a compact planar domain with smooth boundary curves and
\pi
denotes (the restriction of) projection onto some one-dimensional subspace. One can of course also obtain examples with closed codomain by post-composing
\pi
with a smooth covering map
{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Here we circumvent these issues rather naively, by restricting our maps to the interiors of the manifolds in question. An obvious drawback of this approach is the loss of compactness. Nevertheless, this trick turns out to be sufficient for our purposes because the geometry of such maps is still tamer than that of submersions from general open manifolds. The purpose of this section is to state and prove the needed constraints.

Let

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq k}C_{U}^{j}<+\infty.

Lower bound:
It remains to show that
\inf\nu>0
if
\accentset{\circ}{f}:=f|_{\accentset{\circ}{M}}
is surjective. This assumption implies that for all
q\in N
, there is a nice box chart
E_{q}\subset\accentset{\circ}{M}
adapted to
\accentset{\circ}{f}
such that
E_{q}\cap\accentset{\circ}{f}^{-1}(q)\neq\varnothing
. For each such
E_{q}
,
\accentset{\circ}{f}(E_{q})=f(E_{q})\subset N
is an open neighborhood of
q
, and

N=\displaystyle\bigcup_{q\in N}f(E_{q}).

Since
N
is compact we may extract a finite subcover
\{f(E_{1}),\dots,f(E_{\ell})\}
; in particular, every fiber
\mathcal{F}_{q}=\accentset{\circ}{f}^{-1}(q)
intersects and thus contains a fiber plaque in some
E_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq\ell
. On the other hand, the lower bound in Lemma
6.2
implies that every plaque in any
E_{j}
has volume at least
C_{L}:=\min\{C_{L}^{E_{1}},\dots,C_{L}^{E_{\ell}}\}>0
, so

\nu(q)=\operatorname{vol}(\accentset{\circ}{f}^{-1}(q))\geq C_{L}>0

for all
q\in N
, as desired. ∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
f,M,N
be as in Lemma
6.3
. Suppose
\phi\in C^{0}(M,{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0})
is such that for all
q\in N
, we have
\phi(p)>0
for some
p\in\mathcal{F}_{q}=f^{-1}(q)\cap\accentset{\circ}{M}
. Then there exists
C_{\phi}>1
such that

\dfrac{1}{C_{\phi}}\leq\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{F}_{q}}\phi\ d\mathcal{F}_{q}\leq C_{\phi}

for all
q\in N
.

Proof.

Let
C_{1}=\displaystyle\sup_{p\in M}\phi<+\infty
. Then for all
q\in N
we have

\mathcal{I}_{\phi}(q):=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{F}_{q}}\phi\ d\mathcal{F}_{q}\leq C_{1}\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{F}_{q})\leq CC_{1},

where
C
is the upper bound on fiber volumes guaranteed by Lemma
6.3
.

Thus it remains only to show that
\mathcal{I}_{\phi}(q)
is uniformly bounded away from
0
. This follows from an easy adaptation of the proof of the fiber volume lower bound. Consider the set
\mathcal{B}
of all nice box charts
B_{i}
for
f|_{\accentset{\circ}{M}}
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{p\in B_{i}}\phi(p)>0.

Since
\phi
is continuous and positive somewhere on each fiber of
f|_{\accentset{\circ}{M}}
, the image of
\mathcal{B}
under
f
forms an open cover of
N
. Extract a finite subcover
\{f(B_{1}),\dots,f(B_{k})\}\subset f(\mathcal{B})
. Then for some
\delta>0
we have
\phi(p)>\delta
for all
p\in B_{1}\cup\dots\cup B_{k}
, and by Lemma
6.2
there exists
C_{2}>0
such that the volumes of the plaques of each
B_{i}
are uniformly bounded below by
C_{2}
. Since
\{f(B_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
covers
N
, each
\mathcal{F}_{q}
contains at least one full plaque in some
B_{i}
; thus

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{F}_{q}}\phi\ d\mathcal{F}_{q}\geq\delta C_{2}

for all
q\in N
. Setting

C_{\phi}=\max\left\{CC_{1},\dfrac{1}{\delta C_{2}}\right\}

we are done. ∎

7.
Appendix B: Normal Jacobians formula

Recall that our proof of Theorem 1.1 hinges on the existence of uniform bounds for the fiber integrals

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}_{p,q}}\dfrac{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}\ d\mathcal{V}_{p,q}.

The key point is that these integrals can be identified with fiber integrals of a
universally defined
function
\eta
on a slightly larger compact manifold with boundary which submerses onto
\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(TM)\times\operatorname{Gr}_{n-k}(TM)
. By the results of Appendix A, it then suffices to show that
\eta
is (i) continuous, and (ii) positive somewhere on the interior of each fiber.

There are two main benefits to this approach. First, by using only what is required to apply Lemma
6.4
, it avoids obscuring the logic of the argument. Second, and largely as a consequence, it generalizes easily to the case when
\dim(V)+\dim(W)>M
.

The downside is that the constant
C
remains somewhat opaque. In particular, this method gives no further analysis of the function
\eta
, on which
C
depends, or equivalently of the function(s)

\dfrac{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}:\mathcal{V}\xrightarrow[\ \ \ ]{}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}.

This is unsatisfying, especially given the fairly straightforward geometric intuition motivating the bounds. In addition, the mere existence of some constant is clearly insufficient for applications where more precise control over the constant is needed.

In the complementary dimensional case, a more transparent expression for
\frac{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}
is available, which in particular allows us to decompose
C
into more tractable factors:

Proposition 7.1
.

For
(\mathcal{H},\psi)
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
and any complementary dimensional submanifolds
V,W\subset M
, we have

\dfrac{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}(h,p,q)=\dfrac{|\det(J_{(h,T_{p}V,T_{q}W)})|}{\operatorname{NJ}(d(ev_{p})_{h})}

for all
(h,p,q)\in\mathcal{V}
. Here
J
is defined as in Claim
2.1
, and
ev_{p}
as usual denotes the map

ev_{p}:=ev_{\psi}(\cdot,p):\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow M

given by
ev_{p}(h)=h(p)
.

The purpose of this section is to prove this statement. We begin with

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
V
,
W
be finite dimensional inner product spaces and suppose
S\subset V\times W
is a linear subspace such that
\pi_{1}|_{S}
is an isomorphism. Then if

G:=\pi_{2}\circ(\pi_{1}|_{S})^{-1}:V\to W,

we have
S=\Gamma(G)
. If in addition
\pi_{2}|_{S}
is surjective and
S
is endowed with the inner product inherited from the product structure on
V\times W
, then

(7)

\det(GG^{*})^{-1/2}=\dfrac{|\det(\pi_{1}|_{S})|}{\det((\pi_{2}|_{S})\cdot(\pi_{2}|_{S})^{*})^{1/2}}.

Proof.

The first statement is obvious. The second follows from some elementary linear algebra computations; see
[
BCSS98
, pp. 241-243]
for details.
∎

Remark
.

In terms of normal Jacobians, this result specifies a context in which the innocent-looking formula

(8)

\dfrac{1}{\operatorname{NJ}(AB^{-1})}=\dfrac{\operatorname{NJ}(B)}{\operatorname{NJ}(A)}

holds. Note however that this formula fails in general: normal Jacobians of general (not necessarily invertible) linear maps between inner product spaces are not typically multiplicative under composition. In particular, the fact that (
8
) holds in the setting of Lemma
7.2
depends on the assumption that the metric on
V\times W
(and thus the metric on
S
) is the product metric induced by the metrics on
V
and
W
.

Proof of Proposition
7.1
.
As in the discussion following Claim
2.1
, observe that

\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})(h,p,q)=|\det((d\pi_{1})_{(h,p,q)})|=0\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \det(J_{(h,T_{p}V,T_{q}W)})=0.

Thus it suffices to consider the case when both
d\pi_{1}
and
J
are invertible. Let
(h,p,q)
be a regular point for
\pi_{1}
. Then we can define

G\ :=\ (d\pi_{2})_{(h,p,q)}\circ(d\pi_{1})_{(h,p,q)}^{-1}:T_{h}\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow T_{p}V\times T_{q}W.

Since
\Gamma(G)=T_{(h,p,q)}\mathcal{V},
with metric induced by the product metric on
T_{h}\mathcal{H}\times T_{p}V\times T_{q}W,
Lemma
7.2
applies to give

(9)

\det(GG^{*})^{-1/2}=\dfrac{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}(h,p,q).

Moreover, the description of
T_{(h,p,q)}\mathcal{V}
given in Claim
2.1
immediately implies that the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{(h,p,q)}\mathcal{V}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{h}\mathcal{H}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{p}V\times T_{q}W}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{q}M}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d\pi_{1}\ \ \ }
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d\pi_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d(ev_{p})_{h}\ \ \ \ \ }
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ J_{(h,T_{p}V,T_{q}W)}}

In particular,

G=J_{(h,T_{p}V,T_{q}W)}^{-1}\circ d(ev_{p})_{h},

so by (
9
) we compute

\displaystyle\dfrac{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}(h,p,q)

\displaystyle=\det(GG^{*})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\det((J^{-1}\circ d(ev_{p})_{h})\cdot(J^{-1}\circ d(ev_{p})_{h})^{*})^{-1/2}

(10)

\displaystyle=\det(J^{-1}\cdot d(ev_{p})_{h}\cdot d(ev_{p})_{h}^{*}\cdot(J^{-1})^{*})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\left[\det(J^{-1})\cdot\det(d(ev_{p})_{h}\cdot d(ev_{p})_{h}^{*})\cdot\det((J^{-1})^{*})\right]^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\left[\det(J^{-1})^{2}\cdot\det(d(ev_{p})_{h})\cdot d(ev_{p})_{h}^{*})\right]^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{|\det(J)|}{\det(d(ev_{p})_{h})\cdot d(ev_{p})_{h}^{*})^{1/2}}.\qed

Remark
.

Up to a uniformly bounded factor, we have

|\det\left(J_{(h,\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q})}\right)|\approx\sin\measuredangle(dh(\sigma_{p}),\sigma_{q}).

The multiplicative error involved is at most
\eta^{k}
, where
\eta
is the supremum of
\max\left\{\left\lVert dh_{q}\right\rVert,\left\lVert dh_{q}^{-1}\right\rVert\right\}
over all
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
q\in M
.

Similarly, the fact that
ev
is a
C^{1}
submersion implies that
\det(d(ev_{p})_{h})\cdot d(ev_{p})_{h}^{*})^{1/2}
is uniformly bounded above and away from
0

Combining both errors we conclude that

\dfrac{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{1})}{\operatorname{NJ}(\pi_{2})}(h,p,q)\approx\sin\measuredangle(dh(\sigma_{p}),\sigma_{q})

up to a bounded multiplicative constant (which is itself a function of the constants bounding
\operatorname{NJ}(ev_{p})_{h},\left\lVert dh_{p}\right\rVert
, and
\left\lVert dh_{p}^{-1}\right\rVert
for all
(h,p)\in\mathcal{H}\times M
).

This means that one can view Theorem
1.1
as reducing to the following claim:

Proposition 7.3
.

For all
0\leq k\leq n
there is a constant
C_{k}>1
such that for all
(\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q})\in\operatorname{Gr}_{k}(TM)\times\operatorname{Gr}_{n-k}(TM)

Here
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}=\{h\in\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{H}}:h(p)=q\}
; this is a submanifold of
\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{H}}

\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{V}_{p,q}
(indeed
\mathcal{V}_{p,q}=\mathcal{H}_{p,q}\times\{p\}\times\{q\}

Using the results above, our original constant

can be estimated as a function of the constants

and the bounds on
\operatorname{NJ}(ev_{p})_{h},\left\lVert dh_{p}\right\rVert
, and
\left\lVert dh_{p}^{-1}\right\rVert
.
