Visibility phenomena in hypercubes
Jayadev S. Athreya, Cristian Cobeli, Alexandru Zaharescu

Abstract
. We study the set of visible lattice points in multidimensional hypercubes. The problems we investigate mix together geometric, probabilistic and number theoretic tones. For example, we prove that almost all self-visible triangles with vertices in the lattice of points with integer coordinates in 𝒲 = [ 0 , N ] d \mathcal{W}=[0,N]^{d} are almost equilateral having all sides almost equal to d ​ N / 6 \sqrt{d}N/\sqrt{6} , and the sine of the typical angle between rays from the visual spectra from the origin of 𝒲 \mathcal{W} is, in the limit, equal to 7 / 4 \sqrt{7}/4 , as d d and N / d N/d tend to infinity. We also show that there exists an interesting number theoretic constant Λ d , K \Lambda_{d,K} , which is the limit probability of the chance that a K K -polytope with vertices in the lattice 𝒲 \mathcal{W} has all vertices visible from each other.

In the present paper we study a few aspects related to visible lattice points in high dimensional hypercubes. Let
\mathcal{C}:=[0,N]^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the
d
dimensional real cube of side length
N
,
for some integers
d,N\geq 1
.
Denote by
\mathcal{W}:=\mathcal{C}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
the set of
(N+1)^{d}
points with integer coordinates in
\mathcal{C}
.
We denote by
\distance(\bm{v},\bm{w})
the Euclidean distance between any two points
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})
and
\bm{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})
.

The smallest distance between two points in
\mathcal{W}
is equal to
1
, which is always met between
two neighbor points, while the largest is attained by the opposite end points of
the longest diagonals. Such points are
\bm{v}=(0,\dots,0)
and
\bm{w}=(N,\dots,N)

and the distance between them is
\distance(\bm{v},\bm{w})=\sqrt{d\cdot N^{2}}=Nd^{1/2}
.
Then, it is natural to normalize
\distance(\bm{v},\bm{w})
to obtain

the normalized Euclidean distance

\distance_{d}(\bm{v},\bm{w})
, for which all normalized
distances between points in
\mathcal{W}
will belong to the interval
[0,1]
.
Thus,

\distance_{d}(\bm{v},\bm{w}):=\frac{1}{Nd^{1/2}}\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{d}(w_{n}-v_{n})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\,

and

\distance(\bm{v},\bm{w})=Nd^{1/2}\distance_{d}(\bm{v},\bm{w})=\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{d}(w_{n}-v_{n})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Denote by
\Omega\subset\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{W}
the set of pairs of points that
are
visible from each other
, that is, there are
no other lattice points in
\mathcal{W}
between them on the straight line
segment that joins them. Then, by definition,

\Omega
is the set of all pairs
(\bm{v},\bm{w})\in\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{W}
such that

\gcd(v_{1}-w_{1},\dots,v_{d}-w_{d})=1.

(1.1)

We show that the normalized distance between almost any two points in
\mathcal{W}

that are visible from each other is as close to

1/\sqrt{6}\approx 0.40825
as one wishes, if the dimension
d
is sufficiently large and

N
is large enough with respect to
d
.

Theorem 1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an integer
C(\varepsilon)\geq 3

such that for any integers
d\geq C(\varepsilon)
and

N\geq C(\varepsilon)d
we have:

\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\cdot\#\left\{(\bm{v},\bm{w})\in\Omega\;:\;\distance_{d}(\bm{v},\bm{w})\in\left[\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}$}-\varepsilon,\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}$}+\varepsilon\right]\right\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

(1.2)

As a consequence of Theorem
1
we see that
almost all triangles with vertices visible from each other are almost equilateral,
almost all tetrahedrons with vertices visible from each other are
almost regular and so on.

In general, for any
K\geq 2
let us denote by
\Omega_{K}

the set of
K
-polytopes with the property that any two of its vertices
are visible from each other.
Said differently, if we call
self-visible

a
K
-polytope with the property that from any of
its vertices one can see all the others without any obstruction
from any of the lattice points in
\mathcal{W}
, then

\Omega_{K}:=\{P=(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d})\in\mathcal{W}^{K}\;:\;P\text{ is self-visible}\}.

Then, essentially, Theorem
1
can be restated in the following form, which is,
at the same time, a consequence and a more general form of it.

Corollary 1
.

Let
K\geq 2
be a fixed integer.
Then, for any

\varepsilon>0
, there exists an integer

C(K,\varepsilon)\geq 3
, such that for any integers
d\geq C(K,\varepsilon)
and

N\geq C(K,\varepsilon)d
, the proportion of polytopes
P\in\Omega_{K}
for which

\distance_{d}(\bm{w}^{\prime},\bm{w}^{\prime\prime})\in\left[1/\sqrt{6}-\varepsilon,1/\sqrt{6}+\varepsilon\right]

for all distinct
\bm{w}^{\prime},\bm{w}^{\prime\prime}\in P
is greater than
1-\varepsilon
.

The next theorem answers the question of whether there is a limit
probability that a
K
-polytope in
\mathcal{W}^{K}
is self-visible.

Theorem 2
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
N\geq 2
and
2\leq K\leq 2^{d}
be integers.
Then, the probability that a
K
-polytope is self-visible is

\frac{\#\Omega_{K}}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{d}}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{K-1}{p^{d}}\right)+O\left(\frac{dK}{N^{1/2}}\right)+O\left(\frac{2^{d}K^{2}}{\log^{d-1}N}\right),

(1.3)

and the implied constants in the big
O
terms are absolute.

The infinite product over all primes in (
1.3
)
is convergent and defines an endless square of constants

\Lambda_{d,K}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\end{subarray}}\ \prod_{k=1}^{K-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{p^{d}}\right),

which increase with
d
and decrease with
K
.
They comprise some remarkable numbers for which Theorem
2
gives a probabilistic geometric interpretation.
For instance, if
K=2
, then

\Lambda_{2,2}=1/\zeta(2)=6/\pi^{2}\approx 0.6079271
and

\Lambda_{d,2}=\zeta(d)^{-1}
for
d\geq 2
.
If
d=2
and
K=3
, then

\Lambda_{2,3}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}(2C_{FT}-1)\approx 0.196138,

where

C_{FT}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}\prod_{p}\left(1-{\frac{1}{p^{2}-1}}\right)\right)\approx 0.661317

is the Feller-Tornier constant
(see Feller and Tornier
[
10
]
and sequence A065493 from
OEIS
[
20
]
), which is related to the prime zeta function.

Let us note that if
N=0
then
\mathcal{W}^{K}
and
\Omega_{K}
are
reduced to a single point.
If
N=1
, all the points in
\mathcal{W}
are vertices. Then all
points in
\mathcal{W}^{K}
are self-visible, because there are no
intermediary points in the hypercube’s lattice which blind
the view from one point to another. Then, if
N=1
,

\frac{\#\Omega_{K}}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}=1
for
2\leq K\leq 2^{d}
.

We also remark that for any
N>1
, if
K>2^{d}

then in (
1.3
) both non-error terms, the one from the left
side and the main term on the right side, are equal to zero.
In this case (
1.3
) holds true with no error terms.

Let
\vec{\bm{v}}
denote the ray that starts at the origin
\mathbf{0}=(0,\dots,0)\in\mathcal{W}

and passes through

\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}
. Denote by
\Psi
the set of pairs of rays
(\vec{\bm{v}},\vec{\bm{w}})
with
(\bm{v},\bm{w})\in\Omega
.
The next result shows that if
d
and
N/d
are sufficiently large
then almost all angles between rays from the origin
towards points that are visible to each other have the sine almost equal to
\sqrt{7}/4
.

Theorem 3
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an integer
C(\varepsilon)\geq 3
such that for
any integers
d\geq C(\varepsilon)
and
N\geq C(\varepsilon)d
we have:

\frac{1}{\#\Psi}\cdot\#\left\{(\vec{\bm{v}},\vec{\bm{w}})\in\Psi\;:\;\sin\big(\widehat{\vec{\bm{v}},\vec{\bm{w}}}\big)\in\left[\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{7}}{4}$}-\varepsilon,\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{7}}{4}$}+\varepsilon\right]\right\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

(1.4)

Note that the statement in Theorem
3
does not depend on normalization,
because the angles are preserved regardless of any scaling.

For any two subsets
\mathscr{M}^{\prime},\mathscr{M}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
, let
\spec(\mathscr{M}^{\prime},\mathscr{M}^{\prime\prime})

be the visual
spectrum
,
which we define to be the set of sines of all angles between distinct rays
that start from the origin towards the points in
\mathscr{M}^{\prime}
and

\mathscr{M}^{\prime\prime}
, that is,
denoting identically a point
\mathfrak{m}
and the ray from the origin towards
\mathfrak{m}
,

\spec(\mathscr{M}^{\prime},\mathscr{M}^{\prime\prime}):=\left\{\sin(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime\prime})\;:\;\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{M}^{\prime},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathscr{M}^{\prime\prime},\;\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\neq\mathfrak{m}^{\prime\prime}\right\}.

(1.5)

If
\mathscr{M}^{\prime}=\mathscr{M}^{\prime\prime}=\mathscr{M}
, we write shortly

\spec(\mathscr{M})
instead of
\spec(\mathscr{M},\mathscr{M})
.
The angles between rays from the origin to points in
\mathcal{W}
cover a larger
and larger set of possibilities as the dimension increases and
in the limit, as
d\to\infty
, there is a limit set of the spectrum

\spec(\mathscr{W})
, which is the interval
[0,1]
.
On top of that, choosing elements of
\Spec(\mathcal{W})
is a random variable,
which, by Theorem
3
, has a limit probability density function
f(t)

that is discrete and concentrated in a single point, and

f(t)=\delta\big(t-\frac{\sqrt{7}}{4}\big)
, for
0\leq t\leq 1
, where

\delta
is the Dirac distribution.

Since most points in
\mathcal{W}
are visible from each other, the
results in Theorems
1
,
3
and Corollary
1
may prove useful to check particularities related to randomness of large set of data.
A related result about points on hyperspheres appearred
in a theoretical context in the theory of neural network

[

).

In different contexts in nature, it happens and it is not uncommon
for a property that is proved to be valid for almost overall objects in a certain universe to be difficult or even impossible to build or to indicate just a single instance that satisfy it.
However, in the context of the hypercube lattice
\mathcal{W}
,
we can extract some distinguished polytopes that offer a cross-section
view of its inner structure.

Let
\mathscr{C}=\{\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{c}_{d}\}
to be the set of points whose
coordinates are the rows of the circular symmetric matrix

M_{\mathscr{C}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&\cdots&d-1&d\\
1&2&\cdots&d&0\\
2&3&\cdots&0&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
d-1&d&\cdots&d-3&d-2\\
d&0&\cdots&d-2&d-1\end{bmatrix}

(1.6)

and let
\mathscr{G}:=\{\mathfrak{g}_{0},\dots,\mathfrak{g}_{p-1}\}
be the set of points whose
components are the rows of the matrix

M_{\mathscr{G}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1^{-1}&\cdots&(p-2)^{-1}&(p-1)^{-1}\\
1^{-1}&2^{-1}&\cdots&(p-1)^{-1}&0\\
2^{-1}&3^{-1}&\cdots&0&1^{-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
(p-2)^{-1}&(p-1)^{-1}&\cdots&(p-4)^{-1}&(p-3)^{-1}\\
(p-1)^{-1}&0&\cdots&(p-3)^{-1}&(p-2)^{-1}\end{bmatrix}

(1.7)

Here
p
is prime, the classes of the representatives of the inverses in
M_{\mathscr{G}}

are taken from
\{1,\dots,p-1\}
and for symmetry, by convention,
we may set
0
to be ’the inverse’ of
0
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
p
be prime.
Then we have:

1. Any point in
\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}
is visible from the origin.
Any two points in
\mathscr{G}
are visible from each other. If

d=p-1
and
p
is large enough any two points in
\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}
are visible from each other.

2. The limit set of the normalized distances between points in
\mathscr{C}

is the interval
[0,1/2]
, as
d
tends to infinity.

3. The limit set of the normalized distances between points in
\mathscr{G}

consists of the single point
\{1/\sqrt{6}\}
, as
p
tends to infinity.

4. If
d=p-1
, the limit set of the normalized distances between points in
\mathscr{C}
and points in
\mathscr{G}

is also
\{1/\sqrt{6}\}
, as
p
tends to infinity.

5. The limit of the spectrum
\spec(\mathscr{C})
is the interval
[0,\sqrt{39}/8]
,
as
d
tends to infinity.

6. The limit of the spectrum
\spec(\mathscr{G})
consists of the single point
\{\sqrt{7}/4\}
,
as
p
tends to infinity.

7. If
d=p-1
, the limit of the spectrum

\spec({\mathscr{C},\mathscr{G}})
consists of the single point
\{\sqrt{7}/4\}
, also,
as
p
tends to infinity.

Note the decimal approximation of the size of the spectra
in Theorem
4
:

\sqrt{7}/4\approx 0.661438
,

\arcsin(\sqrt{7}/4)\approx 0.72273
radians or

\approx 41.40962^{\circ}
; and

\sqrt{39}/8\approx 0.78063
,

\arcsin(\sqrt{39}/8)\approx 0.89566
radians or

\approx 51.31781^{\circ}
.

As one can see from Theorem
4
, about a quarter of all distances

\distance_{d}(\bm{v},\bm{w})

with
\bm{v},\bm{w}\in\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}
are singular, being different from the other three quarters
which in the limit are all equal to
1/\sqrt{6}
.
However, in Subsection
2.4
we prove that if
d=p-1
then
A_{p}(\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G})
,
the average of all normalized distances between points in
\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}
, is still the same

1/\sqrt{6}
, in the limit as
p\to\infty
.

In Section
2.5
we complement
the phenomena observed in Theorem
4

with yet another polytope
\mathscr{B}
whose vertices are visible from each other, even though they
lie all almost aligned on a straight line.
This set wraps around the diameter of
\mathcal{W}
being composed by close neighbors of
the equally spaced points on the longest diagonal of the cube.
The limit set of the normalized distances
between the points in this example equals the full interval
[0,1]
,
if their number tends to infinity.
The same goes for the limit of the spectrum
\spec(\mathscr{B})
, which is
the interval
[0,1]
, also.
Taken together, merged into a geometric spindle shape,
the polytopes
\mathscr{C},\mathscr{G}
and
\mathscr{B}
combine their
arithmetic and probabilistic properties keeping in balance
the spinning top intrinsic qualities of the still lattice
hypercube
\mathcal{W}
.

2.
Three distinguished polytopes. Their edges and diagonals.

Here we discuss three examples of polytopes with a number of vertices
of order almost equal in size with the dimension of the hypercube.
The polytopes
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{G}
whose vertices are the rows of
the matrices
M_{\mathscr{C}}
and

M_{\mathscr{G}}
introduced by (
1.6
) and (
1.7
)
are as similar in construction as they are very different in shape.
The first has the distances between its vertices well spread over a long interval,
while the second has all the distances between the vertices approximately equal,
being as ‘equilateral’ as it could be, as
d\to\infty
.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
4
– visibility

Notice first that by the definition of
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{G}
and that
of visibility (
1.1
), all points in
\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}
are visible
from the origin.

2.1.1.
Any two points belonging to either
\mathscr{C}
or
\mathscr{G}

are visible from each other

Let us suppose
d=p-1
and let
\bm{v},\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
.
Also, suppose that the coordinates of
\bm{v}
are a
permutation of
\{0,1,\dots,d\}
and the coordinates of
\bm{w}

are a circular rotation of the coordinates of
\bm{v}
.
Then, if
\bm{v}
and
\bm{w}
were not visible from each other, then it would exist
an integer
b\geq 2
such that the following congruences would hold:

v_{0}-w_{0}\equiv 0\left(\mathrm{mod\;}b\right);\ v_{1}-w_{1}\equiv 0\left(\mathrm{mod\;}b\right);\,\dots;\ v_{d}-w_{d}\equiv 0\left(\mathrm{mod\;}b\right).

(2.1)

But since
p
is prime and
v_{0},\dots,v_{d}
are all distinct,
as
w_{0},\dots,w_{d}
also are,
and since they belong to the same set of numbers,
\{0,1,\dots,d\}
,
which appear each in exactly two of the congruences in (
2.1
),
we find that

v_{0}\equiv v_{1}\equiv\cdots\equiv v_{d}\equiv r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}b\right),

for some
r\in\{0,1,\dots,b-1\}
.
But this is impossible, unless
b=1
.
Therefore, two points that belong to one and the same set,
be it either
\mathscr{C}
or
\mathscr{G}
are visible from each other.

2.1.2.
Any two points
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathscr{C}
and
\mathfrak{g}\in\mathscr{G}

are visible from each other

Suppose
d=p-1
and let
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathscr{C}
and
\mathfrak{g}\in\mathscr{G}
.
Denote by
g=\gcd(v_{1}-w_{1},\dots,v_{d}-w_{d})
the expression
that needs to be checked in the condition (
1.1
).
Since
g
is invariant under the same circular rotation applied to both

\mathfrak{c}\in\mathscr{C}
and
\mathfrak{g}\in\mathscr{G}
, we may assume that

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccl}\mathfrak{g}=(0,&1^{-1},&\dots,&(p-1-h)^{-1},&(p-h)^{-1},&(p+1-h)^{-1},&\dots,&(p-1)^{-1}&);\\
\mathfrak{c}=(h,&h+1,&\dots,&p-1,&0,&1,&\dots,&h-1)\,,&\end{array}

(2.2)

for some
h\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
.
As usual, the coordinates are taken as their representatives modulo
p
in the interval
[0,p-1]
.
Since
1^{-1}\equiv 1\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)
and
(p-1)^{-1}\equiv p-1\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)
,
the difference between the second components of
\mathfrak{c}
and
\mathfrak{g}
is

(h+1)-1^{-1}=h
and
the difference between the last components is
(h-1)-(p-1)^{-1}=h-p
.
Because
p
is prime, these differences are relatively prime, so that
\mathfrak{c}
and
\mathfrak{g}

are visible from each other unless
h=0
.

Suppose now that
h=0
in (
2.2
).
Then, if
\mathfrak{c}
is not visible from
\mathfrak{g}
, there exists a prime number
2\leq q\leq p

such that
q\mid(n-n^{-1})
for
1\leq n\leq p-1
. Let us notice that for any
0\leq r\leq p-1

\#\left\{1\leq n\leq p-1\;:\;\left|n-n^{-1}\right|=r\right\}\leq 4.

This is because
n-n^{-1}\equiv\pm r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)
is equivalent to
n^{2}\mp rn-1\equiv 0\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)
,
congruence that has at most two solutions for each sign.
Even more precise, if
r\neq 0
, if the congruence
n-n^{-1}\equiv r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)

has solution
a
then it has solution
-a^{-1}
, also. These solutions are always distinct unless

a\equiv-a^{-1}\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)
, which happens only if
p\equiv 1\left(\mathrm{mod\;}4\right)
, in which case

a=\big(\frac{p-1}{2}\big)!
.
If
r=0
, there are exactly three values of
n\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
for which

n-n^{-1}\equiv r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)
, namely
0,1,p-1
.
In conclusion, putting together these observations, while counting separately in the cases

p=2
,
p\equiv 1\left(\mathrm{mod\;}4\right)
and
p\equiv 3\left(\mathrm{mod\;}4\right)
, we obtain in all cases the same
number of distinct absolute values of differences

\#\left\{\left|n-n^{-1}\right|\;:\;1\leq n\leq p-1\right\}=\left\lfloor\frac{p}{4}\right\rfloor+1.

(2.3)

Then, since

\left\{\left|n-n^{-1}\right|\;:\;1\leq n\leq p-1\right\}\subset\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}

and by our assumption a prime
q
divides all differences
n-n^{-1}
,
it follows that
q
has to be either
2
or
3
.

If
q=2
, the equality (
2.3
) says that the number of pairs
(n,n^{-1})
,
1\leq n\leq p-1

of the same parity is
\lfloor p/4\rfloor+1
. But this is not in agreement with
Lehmer’s conjecture
[
12
, Problem F12]
, which is proved also for shorter general
arithmetic progressions
[
8
, Theorem 1]
).
A particular case of that result shows that if
I,J\subset\{1,\dots,p-1\}
are arithmetic progressions of ratios

d_{1},d_{2}\geq 1
, then

\#\left\{(a,b)\in I\times J\;:\;ab\equiv 1\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p\right)\right\}=\frac{\#I\cdot\#J}{p}+O\left(p^{1/2}\log^{2}p\right).

(2.4)

Then, if
d_{1}=d_{2}=2
, counting the pairs
(n,n^{-1})
with either both even or both odd components,
we see that their total number is
p/2+O\left(p^{1/2}\log^{2}p\right)
,
which contradicts (
2.3
).
Likewise, in the remaining case
q=3
, with
d_{1}=d_{2}=3
, counting the pairs
(n,n^{-1})

whose components both give the same remainder
0
,
1
or
2
when dividing by
3
,
we find that their total number is
p/3+O\left(p^{1/2}\log^{2}p\right)
, which is
also different from (
2.3
).
In conclusion,
\mathfrak{c}
and
\mathfrak{g}
are visible from each other,
which concludes the proof of the first part of Theorem
4
.

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
4
– the distances

2.2.1.
Distances between points of
\mathscr{C}

Remark that the same rotation applied to the coordinates of two points,
does not change the distance between them, which, in particular, shows that we have

\distance(\mathfrak{c}_{k},\mathfrak{c}_{l})=\distance(\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{l-k})\,,\text{ for any $0\leq k\leq l\leq d$.}

(2.5)

This means that the range of values of all distances between any two points in
\mathscr{C}
is
covered by the distances between
\mathfrak{c}_{0}
and each of
\mathfrak{c}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{c}_{d}
.
A straightforward calculation shows that in closed form these are:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\distance(\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1})&=\left((d-0)\cdot 1^{2}+1\cdot(d-0)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
\distance(\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{2})&=\left((d-1)\cdot 2^{2}+2\cdot(d-1)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
\distance(\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{3})&=\left((d-2)\cdot 3^{2}+3\cdot(d-2)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
\ldots&\ldots\\
\distance(\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{d})&=\left((d-(d-1))\cdot d^{2}+d\cdot(d-(d-1))^{2}\right)^{1/2}.\end{array}

(2.6)

Not all of these numbers are distinct, because of the symmetry of the parabola:

x\mapsto f(x)
, where
f(x)=(d-(x-1))\cdot x^{2}+x\cdot(d-(x-1))^{2}=(d+1)x(d+1-x)
, for
x\in[0,d+1]
.
The maximum of
f(x)
is attained for
x=(d+1)/2
and it is equal to
(d+1)^{3}/4
.
Also,
f(x)=f(d+1-x)
and the values of
f(x)
on the integers
between
0
and
d+1
cover quite uniformly the interval
[0,(d+1)^{3}/4]
as
d
becomes sufficiently large.
Precisely, for any
y\in[0,1/2]
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there are
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathscr{C}
such that

y-\varepsilon<\distance_{d}(\mathfrak{c}^{\prime},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime\prime})<y+\varepsilon
.
We summarize in the following proposition these remarks on the polytope
\mathscr{C}
.

Proposition 2.2.1
.

Let
\mathscr{C}=\{\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{c}_{d}\}
be the set of points
whose coordinates are the rows of matrix (
1.6
).
Then, the set of normalized distances between any two points in
\mathscr{C}
is equal to

\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{C}):=\left\{\frac{\sqrt{(d+1)x(d+1-x)}}{d^{3/2}}\;:\;0\leq x\leq\left\lfloor\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{d+1}{2}$}\right\rfloor\right\}

(2.7)

and the set
\mathcal{D}(\mathscr{C})
is dense in the interval
[0,1/2]
as
d
tends to infinity.

2.2.2.
Distances between points of
\mathscr{G}

Let
p
be a prime number and
N=d=p-1
.
The polytope
\mathscr{G}=\{\mathfrak{g}_{0},\dots,\mathfrak{g}_{p-1}\}
is formally close to
\mathscr{C}
.
The components of the points are the same, except that the numbers are inverted modulo
p
.
The classes of the representatives of the inverses are taken from
\{1,\dots,p-1\}
and
by convention the inverse of an integer divisible by
p
, which does not exist,
is always replaced by
0
.
Let us remark that the influence of just a single component in the first point, while
the others are obtained by circular rotations as in
\mathscr{G}
, has small and even negligible
influence as
p\to\infty
, on the mutual distances between the points in
\mathscr{G}
.
This is why we could keep, for balance, in
\mathscr{G}
the components zero, even if zero has no
inverse modulo
p
.

The main motivation for choosing
\mathscr{G}
is the random spread of the inverses.
Various ways to measure the randomness of inverses, triggered by
[
12
, Problem F12]
,
have been studied in
[
6
,
8
,
7
]
. In
[
23
]
the focus is on the values of polynomials and rational
functions mod
p
and the results there might be also used to build
other polytopes with similar characteristics.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
p
is prime and
1\leq h\leq p-1
, we have

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus\{0,p-h\}}\left|(x+h)^{-1}-x^{-1}\right|^{2}=\frac{p^{3}}{6}+O\left(p^{5/2}\log^{2}p\right).

(2.8)

Here the inverses are calculated in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
and the absolute value calculates
the distance between two natural numbers in
\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
, the corresponding representatives of the residue classes
mod
p
of the inverses.

For
h=1
, the estimate (
2.8
) is a particular case of
[
23
, Corollary 1.3]
, which is proved using Weil’s bounds
[
22
]
for exponential sums with rational functions.
For
h\geq 2
the estimate (
2.8
) can be proved in a similar manner.

Taking the square root and dividing by
p^{3/2}
, we find by Lemma
2.1
, that
the normalized distance between any two points
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime},\mathfrak{g}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathscr{G}
is

\distance_{d}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime},\mathfrak{g}^{\prime\prime})=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}$}\left(1+O\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\log^{2}p}{\sqrt{p}}$}\right)\right),

(2.9)

which proves part 3 of Theorem
4
.

2.2.3.
Distances between
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathscr{C}
and
\mathfrak{g}\in\mathscr{G}

It suffices to find the distance between
\mathfrak{c}
and
\mathfrak{g}
in (
2.2
).
For this we have to estimate the sums

\distance^{2}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{c})=\sum_{n=0}^{p-h-1}\left|(n+h)-n^{-1}\right|^{2}+\sum_{n=p-h}^{p-1}\left|(n+h-p)-n^{-1}\right|^{2}=\Sigma_{h}^{\prime}+\Sigma_{h}^{\prime\prime},

(2.10)

where
\Sigma_{h}^{\prime}
and
\Sigma_{h}^{\prime\prime}
are the first and the second sum in (
2.10
), respectively.
To calculate
\Sigma_{h}^{\prime}
, let
L>1
be fixed, denote
u=(p-h)/L
,
v=p/L

and split the rectangle
[0,p-h]\times[0,p]
into
L^{2}
rectangles

T_{j,k}:=\big[ju,(j+1)u\big)\times\big[kv,(k+1v)\big)
.
Then

\begin{split}\Sigma_{h}^{\prime}&=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\!\!\!\sum_{\ \ (n,n^{-1})\in T_{j,k}}\left(n+h-n^{-1}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

Here the size of the summand can be kept under control, so that we can replace it
by its value on the lower left corner of
T_{j,k}
. Thus, on using (
2.4
) with

I\times J=T_{j,k}
, we have

\begin{split}\Sigma_{h}^{\prime}&=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\left(ju+h-kv+O(p/L)\right)^{2}\left(\frac{uv}{p}+O\left(p^{1/2}\log^{2}p\right)\right).\end{split}

Now we factor the terms that do not depend on
j,k
and expand the square

\begin{split}\Sigma_{h}^{\prime}&=\left(\frac{uv}{p}+O\left(p^{1/2}\log^{2}p\right)\right)\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}g(j,k,u,v,p,L)\end{split}

(2.11)

where

\begin{split}g(j,k,u,v,p,L)=&j^{2}u^{2}+h^{2}+k^{2}v^{2}+2juh-2jkuv-2hkv\\
&+O(p^{2}/L^{2})+O\Big(p(ju+h+kv)/L\Big).\end{split}

Adding together the terms separately over
k
and
j
,
the sum of powers being denoted by
S_{r}(M)=1^{r}+\cdots+M^{r}
, and then collecting together
the error terms, the double sum from (
2.11
) becomes

\begin{split}\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}g(j,k,u,v,p,L)=&Lu^{2}S_{2}(L-1)+L^{2}h^{2}+Lv^{2}S_{2}(L-1)\\
&+2LuhS_{1}(L-1)-2uvS_{1}^{2}(L-1)-2LhvS_{1}(L-1)\\
&+O(p^{2})+O\Big(p\big(LuS_{1}(L)+hL^{2}+LvS_{1}(L)\big)/L\Big)\\
=&\frac{L^{4}u^{2}}{3}+L^{2}h^{2}+\frac{L^{4}v^{2}}{3}+L^{3}uh-\frac{L^{4}uv}{2}-L^{3}vh+O\left(p^{2}L\right).\end{split}

Next we insert this into (
2.11
), replace
u,v
by their definition and reduce the terms:

\begin{split}\Sigma_{h}^{\prime}&=\Big(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{uv}{p}$}+O\big(\sqrt{p}\log^{2}p\big)\Big)\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{L^{4}(u^{2}+v^{2})}{3}$}+L^{2}h^{2}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{L^{4}uv}{2}$}-L^{3}h(v-u)+O\left(p^{2}L\right)\right)\\
&=\Big(p-h+O\big(L^{2}\sqrt{p}\log^{2}p\big)\Big)\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{p^{2}+(p-h)^{2}}{3}$}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{p(p-h)}{2}$}+O\left(p^{2}/L\right)\right)\\
&=\frac{(p-h)(p^{2}+2h^{2}-hp)}{6}+O(p^{3}/L)+O\left(L^{2}p^{5/2}\log^{2}p\right).\end{split}

(2.12)

To estimate
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}_{h}
, we make the change of variables
m=p-n
. Note that the representative
in the interval
[0,p-1]
of the inverse of
m
is
m^{-1}=p-n^{-1}
.
Then

\begin{split}\Sigma_{h}^{\prime\prime}&=\sum_{n=p-h}^{p-1}\left|(n+h-p)-n^{-1}\right)|^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{h}\left|(m+p-h)-m^{-1}\right|^{2}.\end{split}

Apart from the end limits of summation, this is exactly
\Sigma^{\prime}_{p-h}
. Therefore we have

\Sigma_{h}^{\prime\prime}=\Sigma_{p-h}^{\prime}+O(p^{2}).

(2.13)

On combining the estimate (
2.12
) with (
2.13
) and inserting
the results into (
2.10
), we obtain

\begin{split}\distance^{2}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{c})&=\frac{(p-h)(p^{2}+2h^{2}-hp)+h(2p^{2}+2h^{2}-3hp)}{6}+O\left(p^{3}/L+L^{2}p^{5/2}\log^{2}p\right)\\
&=\frac{p^{3}}{6}+O\left(p^{3}/L+L^{2}p^{5/2}\log^{2}p\right).\end{split}

(2.14)

Balancing the error terms, we find the optimal
L
that we have fixed at the beginning, namely

L=\left\lfloor p^{1/6}\log^{-2/3}p\right\rfloor
.
Thus we have proved the following result.

Proposition 2.2.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
and let
p
be prime such that
d=p-1
.
Let
\mathscr{C}=\{\mathfrak{c}_{0},\mathfrak{c}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{c}_{d}\}

be the set of points
whose coordinates are the rows of matrix
M_{\mathscr{C}}
from (
1.6
) and let

\mathscr{G}=\{\mathfrak{g}_{0},\mathfrak{g}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{g}_{d}\}
be the set of points
whose coordinates are the rows of matrix
M_{\mathscr{G}}
from (
1.7
).
Then, as
p
tends to infinity, the limit set of the normalized distances between
any two points
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathscr{C}
and
\mathfrak{g}\in\mathscr{G}
is the single point
\{1/\sqrt{6}\}

and

\distance_{d}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{c})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+O\left(p^{-1/6}\log^{2/3}p\right).

(2.15)

Proposition
2.2.2
proves part 4 of Theorem
4
.

2.3.
Proof of Theorem
4
– the spectra

From the origin, which we denote by
\bm{0}
,
the normalized distances toward points in
\mathscr{C}
or
\mathscr{G}
are equal

\distance_{d}(\bm{0},\mathfrak{d}_{h})=\distance_{d}(\bm{0},\mathfrak{c}_{h})=\left(\frac{d(d+1)(2d+1)}{6}\right)^{1/2}d^{-3/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}+O(1/d),

(2.16)

for
0\leq h\leq d
, if
p=d+1
. The distances between points in

\mathscr{C}
are given by Proposition
2.2.1
.
Since the set of distances between points in
\mathscr{C}
are in the limit,
as
d\to\infty
, dense in the interval
[0,1/2]
, the limit of the
spectrum
\spec(\mathscr{C})
is also a continuous interval.
The end points of the limit
\spec(\mathscr{C})
come from the limit angles of the
isosceles triangles with vertices in
\bm{0}
having two edges equal to

1/\sqrt{3}+O(1/d)
, according to (
2.16
),
while the third edge is the shortest and the longest distance between
points in
\spec(\mathscr{C})
, respectively.
By (
2.7
), the acutest triangle has its third edge equal to

O(1/d)
, while the triangle with the largest angle at
\bm{0}
has its third
edge equal to
1/2+O(1/d)
.
Then a straightforward calculation gives the end points of the limit spectrum

0
and
\sqrt{39}/8
.

If
d=p-1
, the mutual distances between points either from

\mathscr{G}
or from
\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}
are all equal to
1/\sqrt{6}+O\left(p^{-1/6}\log^{2/3}p\right)
, by
relation (
2.9
) and
Proposition
2.2.2
, respectively.
Then, the limit spectra
\spec(\mathscr{G})
and
\spec(\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}))

are equal and discrete, containing exactly one point.
According to (
2.16
), the limit point is the sine of the acutest angle of the isosceles triangle with two edges equal to
1/\sqrt{3}
and the third equal to

1/\sqrt{6}
. Since this is equal to
\sqrt{7}/4
,
these concludes the proof of the remaining parts of Theorem
4
.

2.4.
The average distance between points
in
\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}

Suppose
d=p-1
.
The cardinality of
\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}
is

4p^{2}
and the average of the squares
of distances between its points is
A(\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G})=T/(4p^{2})
, where
T
is the following sum

\begin{split}T=\sum_{(\mathfrak{c}^{\prime},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime\prime})\in\mathscr{C}\times\mathscr{C}}\distance^{2}(\mathfrak{c}^{\prime},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime\prime})+\sum_{(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime},\mathfrak{g}^{\prime\prime})\in\mathscr{G}\times\mathscr{G}}\distance^{2}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime},\mathfrak{g}^{\prime\prime})+2\sum_{(\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{g})\in\mathscr{C}\times\mathscr{G}}\distance^{2}(\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{g}).\end{split}

(2.17)

We denote by
T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}
the sums on the right side of (
2.17
).
By (
2.6
), the first sum is

\begin{split}T_{1}=2\sum_{h=1}^{p-1}(p-h)\cdot\big(ph(p-h)\big)=2p\big(pS_{2}(p-1)+S_{3}(p-1)\big)=\frac{p^{5}}{6}+O\left(p^{4}\right).\end{split}

(2.18)

By (
2.9
) and (
2.15
)
the last two sums in (
2.17
) together are

\begin{split}T_{2}+T_{3}=6\sum_{h=1}^{p-1}h\left(\frac{p^{3}}{6}+O\left(p^{17/6}\log^{2/3}p\right)\right)=\frac{p^{5}}{2}+O\left(p^{29/6}\log^{2/3}p\right).\end{split}

(2.19)

Now we can find the normalized distances between points in
\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}
which is defined by

\begin{split}A_{p}(\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G}):=\sqrt{A(\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G})}/p^{3/2}.\end{split}

(2.20)

Thus, on inserting (
2.19
) and (
2.18
)
into (
2.17
), we find that (
2.20
) becomes

\begin{split}A_{p}(\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{G})&=\left(\frac{1}{4p^{2}}\bigg(\frac{p^{5}}{6}+\frac{p^{5}}{2}+O\left(p^{29/6}\log^{2/3}p\right)\bigg)\right)^{1/2}p^{-3/2}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}+O\left(p^{-1/6}\log^{2/3}p\right).\end{split}

(2.21)

2.5.
A polytope stretched out along the longest diagonal of
\mathcal{W}

On the same theme, we construct a polytope
\mathscr{B}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{0},\dots,\mathfrak{b}_{d}\}

that is a cousin of
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{G}
.
The polytope
\mathscr{B}
streches along the diagonal
\big[(0,\dots,0);\;(d,\dots,d)\big]
, it has all vertices
visible from each other and the normalized distances between them
are dense in
[0,1]
, as the dimension
d
tends to infinity.
All components of
\mathfrak{b}_{h}
are set to be equal to
h
, except the
(h-1)
-th and

(h+1)
-th, which are equal to
h-1
and
h+1
, respectively.
Thus

\begin{array}[]{rlccccccccc}&\ \text{\scriptsize$0$-th}&&&&&\text{\scriptsize$h$-th}&&&&\text{\scriptsize$d$-th}\\
\mathfrak{b}_{h}=&(\ h,&h,&\dots,&h,&h-1,&h,&h+1,&h,&\dots,&h\ ),\end{array}

(2.22)

for
h=0,1,\dots,d
, with the convention that
-1=d
and
d+1=0
.

If
d=3
or
d\geq 5
the points of
\mathscr{B}
are visible from one another.
For points that are not too close this follows because most components of any point are equal,
while the neighbors of just one component are the neighbor integers of the rank of the point.
For points that are near each other it can also be checked one by one that they are visible from each other.

The distances between the points of
\mathscr{B}
are:

\distance_{d}(\mathfrak{b}_{j},\mathfrak{b}_{k})=d^{-3/2}\sqrt{d|k-j|^{2}+O(1)}=\frac{|k-j|}{d}+O(1/d),

(2.23)

for
1\leq j,k,\leq d
.
As a consequence, (
2.23
) implies that the closure of the set of distances
between points in
\mathscr{B}
equals the full interval
[0,1]
, as
d
tends to infinity.

Notice that all points in
\mathscr{B}
are visible from the origin,
because (
2.22
) assures that condition (
1.1
) is verified.

The distances from the origin to points in
\mathscr{B}
are

\distance_{d}(\bm{0},\mathfrak{b}_{h})=\frac{h}{d}+O(1/d),\text{ for $1\leq h\leq d$.}

(2.24)

Since the limit set of the mutual distances between points in
\mathscr{B}

is the full interval
[0,1]
, on combining (
2.23
)
and (
2.24
), we see that there is a limit
of the spectrum
\spec(\mathscr{B})
, which is also a closed interval.
Its smallest end point comes from the
triangle with vertices
\bm{0}
,
\mathfrak{b}_{d-2}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{d-1}
,
and its largest from the

\bm{0}
,
\mathfrak{b}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{b}_{d-1}
.
Then the angle at
\bm{0}
of the first triangle tends to zero, and that
of the second triangle tends to
\pi/2
, as
d\to\infty
.
As a consequence, the limit of the spectrum
\spec(\mathscr{B})
is the interval

[0,1]
, as
d
tends to infinity.

3.
The number of pairs in
\Omega

Let
(\bm{v},\bm{w})\in\Omega
and suppose

\bm{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})
and
\bm{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})
. Then

\bm{v}
and
\bm{w}
are visible from each other. This means that

\gcd(v_{1}-w_{1},\dots,v_{d}-w_{d})=1
.
We can rewrite this condition by bringing Möbius summation into play. Thus, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq b\leq N\\
b|v_{1}-w_{1}\\
\cdots\\
b|v_{d}-w_{d}\end{subarray}}\mu(b)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $(\bm{v},\bm{w})\in\Omega$},\\[8.0pt]
0&\text{if $(\bm{v},\bm{w})\not\in\Omega$}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

We start by finding an estimate for the cardinality of
\Omega
, which is the object of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
C_{0}>0
, such that for all
d\geq 2
and all
N\geq 3d
, we have

\left|\#\Omega-\frac{N^{2d}}{\zeta(d)}\right|\leq\begin{cases}C_{0}N^{3}\log N&\text{ if $d=2$},\\[8.0pt]
C_{0}dN^{2d-1}&\text{ if $d\geq 3$}.\end{cases}

(3.2)

Proof.

By the definition and the counting formula (
3.1
), by changing the order of summation, we have

\begin{split}\#\Omega&=\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
(\bm{v},\bm{w})\in\Omega\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{1},w_{1}\leq N\\
b|v_{1}-w_{1}\end{subarray}}\cdots\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{d},w_{d}\leq N\\
b|v_{d}-w_{d}\end{subarray}}1.\end{split}

Since the variables run independently, the summation over
\bm{v}
and
\bm{w}

can be grouped as a product as follows

\begin{split}\#\Omega&=\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{1},w_{1}\leq N\\
b|v_{1}-w_{1}\end{subarray}}1\bigg)\cdots\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{d},w_{d}\leq N\\
b|v_{d}-w_{d}\end{subarray}}1\bigg)\,.\end{split}

(3.3)

Next, we estimate the inner sums that are equal to each other for all
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.
Dropping the subscripts, we see that each of them is equal to

\begin{split}H:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v,w\leq N\\
b|v-w\end{subarray}}1&=\sum_{0\leq r\leq b-1}\#\{(v,w):0\leq v,w\leq N,v\equiv r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}b\right),w\equiv r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}b\right)\}\\
&=\sum_{0\leq r\leq b-1}\big(\#\{0\leq a\leq N\;:\;a\equiv r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}b\right)\}\big)^{2}.\end{split}

The cardinality of the inner set is equal to

\left\lfloor\frac{N-r}{b}\right\rfloor+1=\frac{N}{b}+\theta_{1}(b,r,N)
, with
|\theta(b,r,N)|\leq 1
.
Then

H=\sum_{0\leq r\leq b-1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{N-r}{b}\right\rfloor+1\right)^{2}=\sum_{0\leq r\leq b-1}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{b^{2}}+\theta_{2}(b,r,N)\cdot\frac{N}{b}\right)=\frac{N^{2}}{b}+\theta(b,N)N,

for some real numbers for which
|\theta_{2}(b,r,N)|\leq 3
and
|\theta(b,N)|\leq 3.

On inserting this estimate in (
3.3
), it yields

\begin{split}\#\Omega&=\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)H^{d}=\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\left(\frac{N^{2d}}{b^{d}}+O\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\binom{d}{k}\bigg(\frac{N^{2}}{b}\bigg)^{d-k}(3N)^{k}\right)\right).\end{split}

(3.4)

Here, the main term is

\begin{split}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\frac{N^{2d}}{b^{d}}&=N^{2d}\sum_{b=1}^{\infty}\mu(b)b^{-d}+O\left(N^{2d}\int_{N}^{\infty}x^{-d}\dd x\right)=\frac{N^{2d}}{\zeta(d)}+O\left(\frac{N^{d+1}}{d}\right).\end{split}

(3.5)

Denoting the error term in (
3.4
) by
E_{1}
and changing the order of summation we find that

\begin{split}|E_{1}|&=O\left(\sum_{b=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\binom{d}{k}\frac{N^{2d-2k}}{b^{d-k}}3^{k}N^{k}\right)=O\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}3^{k}N^{2d-k}\binom{d}{k}T_{d-k}(N)\right),\end{split}

(3.6)

where
T_{r}(N):=1^{-r}+\cdots+N^{-r}
.
Then
T_{d-k}(N)=N
, if
k=d
,
T_{d-k}(N)=\log N
, if
k=d-1
and
T_{d-k}(N)=O(1)
if
1\leq k\leq d-2
.
Then

\begin{split}|E_{1}|&=O\left(3^{d}N^{d+1}\right)+O\left(3^{d}N^{d+1}d\log N\right)+O\left(N^{2d}\sum_{k=1}^{d-2}3^{k}N^{-k}\binom{d}{k}\right),\end{split}

(3.7)

The lemma follows by inserting the estimates (
3.5
) and (
3.7
) into (
3.4
).

∎

4.
The average distance between points visible from each other

The average of the square of distances between points visible from each other is

\begin{split}A_{vis}(d,N)&:=\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{(\bm{v},\bm{w})\in\Omega}\distance^{2}(\bm{v},\bm{w}).\end{split}

(4.1)

By (
3.1
), changing the order summation this is

\begin{split}A_{vis}(d,N)&=\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}\distance^{2}(\bm{v},\bm{w}).\end{split}

(4.2)

We rewrite (
4.2
) as

\begin{split}A_{vis}(d,N)&=\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}\left((w_{1}-v_{1})^{2}+\cdots+(w_{d}-v_{d})^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}H_{j},\end{split}

(4.3)

where

\begin{split}H_{j}&:=\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}.\end{split}

(4.4)

By changing the order of summation to isolate the part that does not depend on the
j
variables,

H_{j}
can be rewritten as

\begin{split}H_{j}&=\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{j},w_{j}\leq N\\
b|(v_{j}-w_{j})\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}\bigg)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\not=j\end{subarray}}^{d}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{k},w_{k}\leq N\\
b|(v_{k}-w_{k})\end{subarray}}1\bigg)\\
&=\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\left(\frac{N^{2}}{b}+O(N)\right)^{d-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{j},w_{j}\leq N\\
b|(v_{j}-w_{j})\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}.\end{split}

(4.5)

In the interior sum from (
4.5
) we group the terms with
v_{j}
and
w_{j}

in the same residue classes mod
b
as follows:

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{j},w_{j}\leq N\\
b|(v_{j}-w_{j})\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}&=\sum_{r=0}^{b-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{j},w_{j}\leq N\\
v_{j}\equiv w_{j}\equiv r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}b\right)\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}\\
&=\sum_{r=0}^{b-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N-r}{b}\right\rfloor}\sum_{n=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N-r}{b}\right\rfloor}\big((r+mb)-(r+nb)\big)^{2}\\
&=b^{2}\sum_{r=0}^{b-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N-r}{b}\right\rfloor}\sum_{n=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N-r}{b}\right\rfloor}(m-n)^{2}.\end{split}

(4.6)

With
M=\left\lfloor\frac{N-r}{b}\right\rfloor
,
the sums over
m
and
n
are equal to

\begin{split}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{M}(m-n)^{2}&=2\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{M}m^{2}-2\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{M}mn\\
&=2\left(\frac{1}{3}M^{4}+O(M^{3})\right)-2\left(\frac{1}{4}M^{4}+O(M^{3})\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{1}{6}M^{4}+O(M^{3})\right).\end{split}

(4.7)

Combining (
4.7
) into (
4.6
) we find that

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{j},w_{j}\leq N\\
b|(v_{j}-w_{j})\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}&=\frac{N^{4}}{6b}+O(N^{3}).\end{split}

(4.8)

On inserting this estimate in (
4.5
), we obtain

\begin{split}H_{j}&=\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\left(\frac{N^{2}}{b}+O(N)\right)^{d-1}\left(\frac{N^{4}}{6b}+O(N^{3})\right)\\
&=\frac{N^{2d+2}}{6\cdot\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\frac{\mu(b)}{b^{d}}\left(1+O\bigg(\frac{b}{N}\bigg)\right)^{d}\\
&=\frac{N^{2d+2}}{6\cdot\#\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\zeta(d)}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{dN^{d-1}}\bigg)+\sum_{b=1}^{N}\frac{1}{b^{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\binom{d}{k}\bigg(\frac{b}{N}\bigg)^{k}\right).\end{split}

(4.9)

Here the interior sums are

\begin{split}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\frac{1}{b^{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\binom{d}{k}\bigg(\frac{b}{N}\bigg)^{k}=\begin{cases}O\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)&\text{ if $d=2$},\\[8.0pt]
O\left(\frac{d}{N}\right)&\text{ if $d\geq 3$}.\end{cases}\end{split}

Introducing this estimate in (
4.9
) and the result in (
4.3
) we summarize
in the next lemma the estimate obtained for
A_{vis}(d,N)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have

A_{vis}(d,N)=\begin{cases}\frac{dN^{2d+2}}{6\#\Omega\zeta(d)}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)\right)&\text{ if $d=2$},\\[8.0pt]
\frac{dN^{2d+2}}{6\#\Omega\zeta(d)}\left(1+O\left(\frac{d}{N}\right)\right)&\text{ if $d\geq 3$}.\end{cases}

(4.10)

Taking into account the size of the cardinality of

\Omega
evaluated in Lemma
3.1

into (
4.10
), it yields
the following simple estimate for
A_{vis}(d,N)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists an absolute constant
C_{1}>0
, such that for all
d\geq 2
and all
N\geq 3d
, we have

\left|A_{vis}(d,N)-\frac{dN^{2}}{6}\right|\leq\begin{cases}C_{1}N\log N&\text{ if $d=2$},\\[8.0pt]
C_{1}d^{2}N&\text{ if $d\geq 3$}.\end{cases}

(4.11)

5.
The second moment about the mean

The second moment about the mean
A_{vis}(d,N)
is the average of the squares of the differences
between the expected and the true distance between the pairs of points from
\mathcal{W}

that are visible from each other, that is,

\begin{split}\mathfrak{M}_{2,vis}(d,N):=\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
(\bm{v},\bm{w})\in\Omega\end{subarray}}\left|\distance^{2}(\bm{v},\bm{w})-A_{vis}(d,N)\right|^{2}.\end{split}

(5.1)

Replacing the coprimality condition by means of the characteristic function (
3.1
)
and changing the order of summation, we have

\begin{split}\mathfrak{M}_{2,vis}(d,N)=&\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}\left|\distance^{2}(\bm{v},\bm{w})-A_{vis}(d,N)\right|^{2}.\end{split}

Next, by expanding the square it yields

\begin{split}\mathfrak{M}_{2,vis}(d,N)=&\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}\distance^{4}(\bm{v},\bm{w})\\
&-\frac{2A_{vis}(d,N)}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}\distance^{2}(\bm{v},\bm{w})\\
&+\frac{A^{2}_{vis}(d,N)}{\#\Omega}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}1\\
=&\frac{1}{\#\Omega}\cdot\Sigma_{vis}-A^{2}_{vis}(d,N).\end{split}

(5.2)

Here we have denoted by
\Sigma_{vis}
the multiple sum over
\bm{v}
and
\bm{w}
from the first row of
relation (
5.2
) and have taken into account the fact that the term from the second row
is equal to
-2A^{2}_{vis}(d,N)
, while the term from the third row is equal to
A^{2}_{vis}(d,N)
.
Next, changing the order of summation, we split
\Sigma_{vis}
into
d^{2}
similar sums
H_{j,k}

\begin{split}\Sigma_{vis}&=\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}(w_{k}-v_{k})^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}H_{j,k},\end{split}

(5.3)

where

\begin{split}H_{j,k}&=\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\\
b|(v_{1}-w_{1})\\
\cdots\\
b|(v_{d}-w_{d})\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}(w_{k}-v_{k})^{2}.\end{split}

(5.4)

Now fix
j\neq k
.
In each
H_{j,k}
the summand depends only on four of the
2d
variables
v_{1},\dots,v_{d},w_{1},\dots,w_{d}
,
so that

\begin{split}H_{j,k}&=\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{j},w_{j}\leq N\\
b|(v_{j}-w_{j})\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{k},w_{k}\leq N\\
b|(v_{k}-w_{k})\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}(w_{k}-v_{k})^{2}\bigg)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}s=1\\
s\not=j\\
s\not=k\end{subarray}}^{d}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{s},w_{s}\leq N\\
b|(v_{s}-w_{s})\end{subarray}}1\bigg).\end{split}

(5.5)

Since the products count the number of terms in some arithmetic progressions and are equal,
we derive that

\begin{split}H_{j,k}&=\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\left(\frac{N^{2}}{b}+O(N)\right)^{d-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{j},w_{j}\leq N\\
b|(v_{j}-w_{j})\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{k},w_{k}\leq N\\
b|(v_{k}-w_{k})\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}(w_{k}-v_{k})^{2}\end{split}

(5.6)

By relation (
4.8
), we find that the interior sums are

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{j},w_{j}\leq N\\
b|(v_{j}-w_{j})\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq v_{k},w_{k}\leq N\\
b|(v_{k}-w_{k})\end{subarray}}(w_{j}-v_{j})^{2}(w_{k}-v_{k})^{2}=\frac{N^{8}}{36b^{2}}+O\left(N^{7}/b\right).\end{split}

(5.7)

On combining (
5.7
) and (
5.6
), it follows that

\begin{split}H_{j,k}&=\sum_{b=1}^{N}\mu(b)\left(\frac{N^{2}}{b}+O(N)\right)^{d-2}\left(\frac{N^{8}}{36b^{2}}+O\left(N^{7}/b\right)\right)\\
&=\frac{N^{2d+4}}{36}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\frac{\mu(b)}{b^{d}}\left(1+O\bigg(\frac{b}{N}\bigg)\right)^{d}.\end{split}

(5.8)

Following the reasoning from (
4.9
) and the relation that follows, we obtain the following estimate

\begin{split}H_{j,k}=\begin{cases}\frac{N^{2d+4}}{36\zeta(d)}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)\right)&\text{ if $d=2$},\\[8.0pt]
\frac{N^{2d+4}}{36\zeta(d)}\left(1+O\left(\frac{d}{N}\right)\right)&\text{ if $d\geq 3$}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(5.9)

If
j=k
, adapting the same steps after relation (
5.4
) we obtain the upper bound

\begin{split}H_{j,j}=O(N^{2d+4}),\text{ for $1\leq j\leq d$.}\end{split}

(5.10)

Then on inserting (
5.10
) and (
5.9
) into (
5.3
), yields

\begin{split}\Sigma_{vis}=\frac{d^{2}N^{2d+4}}{36\zeta(d)}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{d}+\frac{d}{N}\right)\right).\end{split}

(5.11)

On combining (
5.11
), (
5.2
), Lemma
3.1

and Lemma
4.2
, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
C_{2}>0
, such that for all
d\geq 2
and all
N\geq 3d
, we have

\mathfrak{M}_{2,vis}(d,N)\leq C_{2}(dN^{4}+d^{3}N^{3}).

(5.12)

Effective results and the proofs of Theorems

7.
The probability that a
K
-polytope is Self-Visible

Let
K\geq 2
be a fixed integer. The set of self-visible
K
-polytopes with
vertices in the lattice
\mathcal{W}
is

\Omega_{K}=\left\{P\in\mathcal{W}^{K}\;:\;\bm{v}^{\prime},\bm{v}^{\prime\prime}\text{ visible from each other, for all }\bm{v}^{\prime},\bm{v}^{\prime\prime}\in P\right\}.

(7.1)

Our object here is to see if there is a tendency of
the probabilities that a
K
-polytope
is self-visible as
N
gets large. We show that if
d
and
K
are kept fixed,
the limit of the ratios

Prob(d,N,K)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\Omega_{K}}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}

does exist.

If
K=2
, then
\Omega_{K}
coincides with
\Omega
, but the Möbius
summation method used in the proof of Lemma
3.1

to estimate
\#\Omega_{2}
is not suitable for larger
K
, because of
the size of the multitude of new terms introduced.
We need to have a better control on the large divisors,
so we will proceed accordingly.

Denote a generic polytope by
P=\{\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K}\}

and the coordinates of its vertices by

\bm{v}_{j}=(v_{j,1},\dots,v_{j,d})
for
1\leq j\leq K
.
Note that, for each positive integer
m
, we have the following inequality

\#\left\{(\bm{v}_{j},\bm{v}_{k})\in\mathcal{W}^{2}\;:\;\bm{v}_{j}\neq\bm{v}_{k},\ m\mid\gcd(v_{j,1}-v_{k,1},\dots,v_{j,d}-v_{k,d})\right\}\leq\frac{\#\mathcal{W}^{2}}{m^{d}},

(7.2)

because, say,
v_{j,1},\dots,v_{j,d}

are free and then each of
v_{k,1},\dots,v_{k,d}

belongs to the corresponding shifted arithmetic progression of ratio
m
.
Also, if
m\geq N
, the left side of (
7.2
)
equals zero, since there are no pairs to count.

Fix
M>0
, a parameter to be chosen later, and sum the inequalities (
7.2
)
for all
m>M
. Then the size of the resulted sum is

\leq\sum_{m>M}\frac{\#\mathcal{W}^{2}}{m^{d}}=O\left(\frac{\#\mathcal{W}^{2}}{M^{d-1}}\right).

As a consequence, any such subsum is also
\ll\#\mathcal{W}^{2}/M^{d-1}
.
In particular, the sum over all positive integers
m
that have
at least one prime factor larger than
M
.
This holds for each pair
(\bm{v}_{j},\bm{v}_{k})
, and there are
K(K-1)/2

such pairs with
1\leq j<k\leq K
.
As a consequence, it follows that

\#\left\{(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K})\in\mathcal{W}^{K}\;:\;\left.\begin{aligned} &q\mid\gcd(v_{j,1}-v_{k,1},\dots,v_{j,d}-v_{k,d})\\
&\text{ for some prime $q>M$,}\\
&\text{ for some $1\leq j<k\leq K$ }\end{aligned}\right.\right\}\ll\frac{K^{2}\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}{M^{d-1}}.

(7.3)

In other words, with the exception of at most
O\left(\frac{K^{2}\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}{M^{d-1}}\right)

K
-tuples
(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K})
,
for all the other polytopes
P=(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K})\in\mathcal{W}^{K}
, the condition

P\in\Omega_{K}
is equivalent to the condition that
P\in\Omega_{K}(M)
, where

\Omega_{K}(M):=\left\{(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K})\in\mathcal{W}^{K}\;:\;\left.\begin{aligned} &\gcd(B,v_{j,1}-v_{k,1},\dots,v_{j,d}-v_{k,d})=1\\
&\text{ for all $1\leq j<k\leq K$ }\end{aligned}\right.\right\},

(7.4)

where
B
is the primorial number

B:=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p\leq M\end{subarray}}p.

Therefore, the probability that a polytope
P\in\mathcal{W}^{K}

has all vertices visible from each other is

\frac{\#\Omega_{K}}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}=\frac{\#\Omega_{K}(M)}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}+O\left(\frac{K^{2}\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}{M^{d-1}}\right).

(7.5)

By the Prime Number Theorem, we know that
B=e^{(1+o(1))M}
, so that we will eventually choose
M
of
size
\log N
to assure that
B<N
.

Next, we split the interval
[0,N]
in subintervals of size
B
.
Accordingly, the cube
[0,N]^{d}
is split in boxes of side length
B
.
The number of these boxes is

\text{The number of boxes}=\left(\frac{N}{B}+O(1)\right)^{d}=\frac{N^{d}}{B^{d}}+O\left(\frac{dN^{d-1}}{B^{d-1}}\right).

(7.6)

Observe, by the definition, that
\Omega_{K}(M)
has the same number of elements in each such box.
Denote this number by
H(B)
, that is,

H(B):=\#\left\{(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K})\in\Omega_{K}(M)\;:\;0\leq v_{j,l}<B\text{ for all $1\leq j\leq K$, $1\leq l\leq d$}\right\}.

(7.7)

Then, by (
7.6
) and (
7.7
), as each
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K}
runs over each box,
it follows that

\#\Omega_{K}(M)=H(B)\left(\frac{N^{d}}{B^{d}}+O\left(\frac{dN^{d-1}}{B^{d-1}}\right)\right)^{K}=\frac{N^{dK}H(B)}{B^{dK}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{dKB}{N}\right)\right).

(7.8)

Since
H(B)\leq B^{dK}
and since
\#\mathcal{W}^{K}=(N+1)^{dK}=N^{dK}(1+O(dK/N))
,
it follows that

\frac{\#\Omega_{K}(M)}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}=\frac{H(B)}{B^{dK}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{dKB}{N}\right)\right)=\frac{H(B)}{B^{dK}}+O\left(\frac{dKB}{N}\right).

(7.9)

On combining (
7.8
) and (
7.5
), it yields

\frac{\#\Omega_{K}}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}=\frac{H(B)}{B^{dK}}+O\left(\frac{dKB}{N}\right)+O\left(\frac{K^{2}}{M^{d-1}}\right).

(7.10)

Now, for each prime
p\mid B
, consider the analogue of
the set
\Omega_{K}(M)
defined
by (
7.4
). Its cardinality is analogous to
H(B)
and is given by

H(p):=\#\left\{(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K})\in\mathcal{W}^{K}:\left.\begin{aligned} &\gcd(p,v_{j,1}-v_{k,1},\dots,v_{j,d}-v_{k,d})=1\\
&\text{ for all $1\leq j<k\leq K$, }\\
&0\leq v_{j,l}\leq p-1\text{ for all $1\leq j\leq K$, $1\leq l\leq d$}\end{aligned}\right.\right\}.

(7.11)

Note that each
(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K})\in\mathcal{W}^{K}
that contributes to
H(B)
produces,
via reduction modulo
p
, a
K
-tuple that contributes to
H(p)
,
and this holds for each prime divisor
p
of
B
.
Conversely, by the Chinese Remainder Theorem, each collection of

K
-tuples, with one
K
-tuple for
each prime divisor of
B
, produces a unique
K
-tuple that is counted in
H(B)
.
In conclusion,

H(B)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p\mid B\end{subarray}}H(p)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p\leq M\end{subarray}}H(p),

which combined with (
7.10
) implies

\frac{\#\Omega_{K}}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p\leq M\end{subarray}}\frac{H(p)}{p^{dK}}+O\left(\frac{dKB}{N}\right)+O\left(\frac{K^{2}}{M^{d-1}}\right).

(7.12)

Next, let us observe that since each of the coordinates

v_{j,1},\dots,v_{j,d}
and
v_{k,1},\dots,v_{k,d}

belongs to
\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
, the difference

v_{j,1}-v_{k,1}
cannot be divisible by
p
unless
v_{j,1}=v_{k,1}
,
and similarly for all differences
v_{j,2}-v_{k,2},\dots,v_{j,d}-v_{k,d}
.
As a consequence, the condition

\gcd(p,v_{j,1}-v_{k,1},\dots,v_{j,d}-v_{k,d})=1
from the definition of
H(p)
given
by (
7.11
) is equivalent to the condition that the
d
-tuples

(v_{j,1},\dots,v_{j,d})
and
(v_{k,1},\dots,v_{k,d})
are distinct.
In other words

H(p)=\#\left\{(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{K})\in\mathcal{W}^{K}\;:\;\left.\begin{aligned} &\mbox{}\bm{v}_{j}\neq\bm{v}_{k}\text{ for $1\leq j\neq k\leq K$,}\\
&0\leq v_{j,l}\leq p-1\text{ for all $1\leq j\leq K$ and $1\leq l\leq d$ }\end{aligned}\right.\right\}.

Here, there are exactly
p^{d}
choices for
\bm{v}_{1}
.
Then, for each fixed
\bm{v}_{1}
, the only restriction on
\bm{v}_{2}

is to not coincide with
\bm{v}_{1}
, so that there are
p^{d}-1
choices for
\bm{v}_{2}
.
With
\bm{v}_{1}
and
\bm{v}_{2}
fixed, the only restrictions
on
\bm{v}_{3}
are
\bm{v}_{3}\neq\bm{v}_{1}
and

\bm{v}_{3}\neq\bm{v}_{2}
, so that there are
p^{d}-2
choices for
\bm{v}_{3}
.
And so on, up to
\bm{v}_{K}
, for which there are
p^{d}-(K-1)
choices.
In conclusion

H(p)=p^{d}\left(p^{d}-1\right)\cdots\left(p^{d}-(K-1)\right).

On combining this with (
7.12
) we see that

\frac{\#\Omega_{K}}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p\leq M\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{d}}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{K-1}{p^{d}}\right)+O\left(\frac{dKB}{N}\right)+O\left(\frac{K^{2}}{M^{d-1}}\right).

(7.13)

The finite product over primes in (
7.13
)
can be replaced with the completed product over all primes,
with a change in the error term that is swallowed inside
the last error term. Indeed, if we denote

\Lambda_{d,K}(M):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p>M\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{d}}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{K-1}{p^{d}}\right),

an infinite product that converges if
d\geq 2
, then

\log\Lambda_{d,K}(M)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p>M\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leq k\leq K-1}\log\left(1-\frac{k}{p^{d}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p>M\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leq k\leq K-1}O\left(\frac{k}{p^{d}}\right).

This implies

\begin{split}|\log\Lambda_{d,K}(M)|&=O\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p>M\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leq k\leq K-1}\frac{k}{p^{d}}\bigg)=O\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p>M\end{subarray}}\frac{K^{2}}{p^{d}}\bigg)=O\bigg(\sum_{m>M}\frac{K^{2}}{m^{d}}\bigg)=O\left(\frac{K^{2}}{M^{d-1}}\right).\end{split}

It follows that

\Lambda_{d,K}(M)=\exp\left(O\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{K^{2}}{M^{d-1}}$}\right)\right)=1+O\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{K^{2}}{M^{d-1}}$}\right).

Therefore, if we denote by
\Lambda_{d,K}
the complete infinite product,

\Lambda_{d,K}:=\prod_{\text{$p$ prime}}\left(1-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{d}}$}\right)\cdots\left(1-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{K-1}{p^{d}}$}\right),

(7.14)

which is constant for any fixed
d
and
K
, we have

\begin{split}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\\
p\leq M\end{subarray}}\left(1-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{d}}$}\right)\cdots\left(1-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{K-1}{p^{d}}$}\right)&=\frac{\Lambda_{d,K}}{\Lambda_{d,K}(M)}=\Lambda_{d,K}+O\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{K^{2}}{M^{d-1}}$}\right),\end{split}

(7.15)

where the implied constant in the big
O
estimate is absolute,
because
\Lambda_{d,2}=\zeta(d)^{-1}
for
d\geq 2
and,
for any fixed
d
, the sequence
\{\Lambda_{d,K}\}_{K\geq 2}
is
decreasing.

Then, inserting (
7.15
) and (
7.14
) in (
7.13
), we arrive at the following result

\frac{\#\Omega_{K}}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{d}}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{K-1}{p^{d}}\right)+O\left(\frac{dKB}{N}\right)+O\left(\frac{K^{2}}{M^{d-1}}\right).

We now take
B
to be the largest primorial that is
\leq\sqrt{N}
,
which means that
M\sim(\log N)/2
.
Then,

\frac{\#\Omega_{K}}{\#\mathcal{W}^{K}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$p$ prime}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{d}}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{K-1}{p^{d}}\right)+O\left(\frac{dK}{\sqrt{N}}\right)+O\left(\frac{2^{d}K^{2}}{\log^{d-1}N}\right),

(7.16)

and the constants implied in the big
O
terms are absolute.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
2
.

8.
Probabilistic Intuition

In this section, we show how to interpret the constant
1/\sqrt{6}
in
Theorem
1
via probabilistic intuition. Similar arguments can
give intuition for some of the other particular constants we obtain in this
paper. We recall

\distance_{d}(\bm{v},\bm{w})=\frac{1}{Nd^{1/2}}\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{d}(w_{n}-v_{n})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}=\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=1}^{d}\left(\frac{w_{n}}{N}-\frac{v_{n}}{N}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2},

As
N\rightarrow\infty
, if we select
\bm{v}
uniformly at random from

\mathcal{W}
(or, indeed, the subset of integer vectors visible from the
origin in
\mathcal{W}
), the normalized vector
\bm{v}/N
becomes equidistributed
in the hypercube
[0,1]^{d}
. That is,

\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{W}}\delta_{\bm{v}/N}\rightarrow m,

where
m
is the standard Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d}
,
and the convergence is
in the weak*-sense as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
The same result is true with
\mathcal{W}
replaced by the subset of primitive
vectors in
\mathcal{W}
.
Thus, to try and get intuition about the
d\rightarrow\infty

behavior of our normalized distance
\distance_{d}(\bm{v},\bm{w})
,
we can consider the following probabilistic analogue.
Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots X_{d}),\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})

be independent random vectors chosen according to
Lebesgue measure on the hypercube
[0,1]^{d}
.
Thus
X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}
and
Y_{1},\ldots Y_{d}
are independent,
identically distributed (i.i.d.) uniform
[0,1]
random variables,
and also indpendent from each other.
We define

\distance_{d}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=1}^{d}\left(X_{n}-Y_{n}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Thus

\distance_{d}^{2}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=1}^{d}\left(X_{n}-Y_{n}\right)^{2}

is the sample mean of
d
independent random variables of the form

(U-V)^{2}
, where
U
and
V
are independent uniform
[0,1]

random variables.
By the strong law of large numbers, as
d\rightarrow\infty
,
this converges with probability
1
to the mean

E((U-V)^{2})=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(u-v)^{2}dudv=1/6.

That is, as
d\rightarrow\infty,
with probability
1
,

\distance_{d}^{2}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\rightarrow 1/6,

so

\distance_{d}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\rightarrow 1/\sqrt{6}.

To be clear, this does not give a direct proof of
Theorem
1
, since there are tricky issues with
the interchange of limits.
Similar arguments can yield intuition for the other constants
in our results.
