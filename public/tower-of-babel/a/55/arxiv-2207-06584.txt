Stochastic mirror descent method for linear ill-posed problems in Banach spaces
Qinian Jin, Xiliang Lu, Liuying Zhang

Abstract
. Consider linear ill-posed problems governed by the system A i ​ x = y i A_{i}x=y_{i} for i = 1 , ⋯ , p i=1,\cdots,p , where each A i A_{i} is a bounded linear operator from a Banach space X X to a Hilbert space Y i Y_{i} . In case p p is huge, solving the problem by an iterative regularization method using the whole information at each iteration step can be very expensive, due to the huge amount of memory and excessive computational load per iteration. To solve such large-scale ill-posed systems efficiently, we develop in this paper a stochastic mirror descent method which uses only a small portion of equations randomly selected at each iteration steps and incorporates convex regularization terms into the algorithm design. Therefore, our method scales very well with the problem size and has the capability of capturing features of sought solutions. The convergence property of the method depends crucially on the choice of step-sizes. We consider various rules for choosing step-sizes and obtain convergence results under a priori early stopping rules. In particular, by incorporating the spirit of the discrepancy principle we propose a choice rule of step-sizes which can efficiently suppress the oscillations in iterates and reduce the effect of semi-convergence. Furthermore, we establish an order optimal convergence rate result when the sought solution satisfies a benchmark source condition. Various numerical simulations are reported to test the performance of the method.



1.
Introduction

Due to rapid growth of data sizes in practical applications, in recent years stochastic optimization methods have received tremendous attention and have been proved to be efficient in various applications of science and technology including in particular the machine learning researches (
[
5
,
13
]
). In this paper we will develop a stochastic mirror descent method for solving linear ill-posed inverse problems in Banach spaces.

We will consider linear ill-posed inverse problems governed by the system

\displaystyle A_{i}x=y_{i},\qquad i=1,\cdots,p

(1.1)

consisting of
p
linear equations, where, for each
i=1,\cdots,p
,
A_{i}:X\to Y_{i}
is a bounded linear operator from a Banach space
X
to a Hilbert space
Y_{i}
. Such systems arise naturally in many practical applications. For instance, many linear ill-posed inverse problems can be described by an integral equation of the first kind (
[
10
,
14
]
)

(Ax)(s):=\int_{\mathscr{D}}k(s,t)x(t)dt=y(s),\quad s\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime},

where
\mathscr{D}
and
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}
are bounded domains in Euclidean spaces and the kernel
k
is a bounded continuous function on
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}\times\mathscr{D}
. Clearly
A
is a bounded linear operator from
L^{r}(\mathscr{D})
to
C(\mathscr{D}^{\prime})
for any
1\leq r\leq\infty
.
By taking
p
sample points
s_{1},\cdots,s_{p}
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}
, then the problem of determining a solution using only the knowledge of
y_{i}:=y(s_{i})
for
i=1,\cdots,p
reduces to solving a linear system of the form (
1.1
), where
A_{i}:L^{r}(\mathscr{D})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is given by

A_{i}x:=\int_{\mathscr{D}}k(s_{i},t)x(t)dt

for each
i
. Further examples of (
1.1
) can be found in various tomographic techniques using multiple measurements
[
27
]
.

Throughout the paper we always assume (
1.1
) has a solution. The system (
1.1
) may have many solutions. By taking into account of
a priori
information about the sought solution, we may use a proper, lower semi-continuous, convex function
{\mathcal{R}}:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
to select a solution
x^{\dagger}
of (
1.1
) such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})=\min\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x):A_{i}x=y_{i}\mbox{ for }i=1,\cdots,p\right\}

(1.2)

which, if exists, is called a
{\mathcal{R}}
-minimizing solution of (
1.1
). In practical applications, the exact data
y:=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{p})
is in general not available, instead we only have noisy data
y^{\delta}:=(y_{1}^{\delta},\cdots,y_{p}^{\delta})
satisfying

\displaystyle\|y_{i}^{\delta}-y_{i}\|\leq\delta_{i},\quad i=1,\cdots,p,

(1.3)

where
\delta_{i}>0
denotes the noise level corresponding to data in the space
Y_{i}
. How to use the noisy data
y^{\delta}
to construct an approximate solution of the
{\mathcal{R}}
-minimizing solution of (
1.1
) is an important topic.

Let
Y:=Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{p}
and define
A:X\to Y
by

Ax=(A_{1}x,\cdots,A_{p}x),\quad x\in X.

Then (
1.2
) can be equivalently stated as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})=\min\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x):Ax=y\right\}.

(1.4)

which has been considered by various variational and iterative regularization methods, see
[
4
,
7
,
21
,
23
,
35
]
for instance. In particular, the Landweber iteration in Hilbert spaces has been extended for solving (
1.4
), leading to the iterative method of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x_{n}^{\delta}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},x\rangle\right\},\\
&\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-t_{n}^{\delta}A^{*}(Ax_{n}^{\delta}-y^{\delta}),\end{split}

(1.5)

where
A^{*}:Y\to X^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
A
and
t_{n}^{\delta}
is the step-size.
This method can be derived as a special case of the mirror descent method; see Section
2
for a brief account. The method (
1.5
) has been investigated in a number of references, see
[
4
,
12
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. In particular, the convergence and convergence rates have been derived in
[
21
]
when the method is terminated by either an
a priori
stopping rule or the discrepancy principle

\|Ax_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}-y^{\delta}\|\leq\tau\delta<\|Ax_{n}^{\delta}-y^{\delta}\|,\quad 0\leq n<n_{\delta},

where

\delta:=\sqrt{\delta_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\delta_{p}^{2}}

denotes the total noise level of the noisy data. We remark that the minimization problem in (
1.5
) for defining
x_{n}^{\delta}

can be solved easily in general as it does not depend on
A
; in fact
x_{n}^{\delta}
can be given by an explicit formula in many interesting cases; even if
x_{n}^{\delta}
does not have an explicit formula, there
exist fast algorithms for determining
x_{n}^{\delta}
efficiently. However, note that

A^{*}(Ax_{n}^{\delta}-y^{\delta})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}^{*}(A_{i}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{i}^{\delta}).

Therefore, updating
\xi_{n}^{\delta}
to
\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}
requires calculating
A_{i}^{*}(A_{i}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{i}^{\delta})
for all
i=1,\cdots,p
.
In case
p
is huge, using the method (
1.5
) to solve (
1.4
) can be inefficient because it requires a huge amount of memory and excessive computational work per iteration.

In order to relieve the drawback of the method (
1.5
), by extending the Kaczmarz-type method
[
15
]
in Hilbert spaces, a Landweber-Kaczmarz method in Banach spaces has been proposed in
[
20
,
23
]
to solve (
1.2
) which cyclically considers each equation in (
1.2
) in a Gauss-Seidel manner and the iteration scheme takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x_{n}^{\delta}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},x\rangle\right\},\\
&\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-t_{n}^{\delta}A_{i_{n}}^{*}(A_{i_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{i_{n}}^{\delta}),\end{split}

(1.6)

where
i_{n}=(n\mod p)+1
. The convergence of this method has been shown in
[
23
]
in which the numerical results demonstrate its nice performance. However, it should be pointed out that the efficiency of the method (
1.6
) depends crucially on the order of the equations and its convergence speed is difficult to be quantified. In order to resolve these issues, in this paper we will consider a stochastic version of (
1.6
), namely, instead of taking
i_{n}
cyclically, we will choose
i_{n}
from
\{1,\cdots,p\}
randomly at each iteration step. The corresponding method will be called the stochastic mirror descent method and more details will be presented in Section
2
where we also propose the mini-batch version of the stochastic mirror descent method.

The stochastic mirror descent method, that we will consider in this paper, includes the stochastic gradient descent as a special case. Indeed, when
X
is a Hilbert space and
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\|x\|^{2}/2
, the method (
1.6
) becomes

\displaystyle x_{n+1}^{\delta}=x_{n}^{\delta}-t_{n}^{\delta}A_{i_{n}}^{*}(A_{i_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{i_{n}}^{\delta})

(1.7)

which is exactly the stochastic gradient descent method studied in
[
18
,
19
,
25
,
33
]
for solving linear ill-posed problems in Hilbert spaces.
In many applications, however, the sought solution may sit in a Banach space instead of a Hilbert space, and the sought solution may have
a priori
known special features, such as nonnegativity, sparsity and piecewise constancy. Unfortunately, the stochastic gradient descent method does not have the capability to incorporate these information into the algorithm.
However, this can be handled by the stochastic mirror descent method with careful choices of a suitable Banach space
X
and a strongly convex penalty functional
{\mathcal{R}}
.

In this paper we will use tools from convex analysis in Banach spaces to analyze the stochastic mirror descent method. The choice of the step-size plays a crucial role on the convergence of the method. We consider several rules for choosing the step-sizes and provide criteria for terminating the iterations in order to guarantee a convergence when the noise level tends to zero. The iterates produced by the stochastic mirror descent method exhibit salient oscillations and, due to the ill-posedness of the underlying problems, the method using noisy data demonstrates the semi-convergence property, i.e. the iterate tends to the sought solution at the beginning and, after a critical number of iterations, the iterates diverges. The oscillations and semi-convergence make it difficult to determine an output with good approximation property, in particular when the noise level is relatively large. When the information on noise level is available, by incorporating the spirit of the discrepancy principle we propose a rule for choosing step-size. This rule enables us to efficiently suppress the oscillations of iterates and remove the semi-convergence of the method as indicated by the extensive numerical simulations. Furthermore, we obtain an order optimal convergence rate result for the stochastic mirror descent method with constant step-size when the sought solution satisfies a benchmark source condition. We achieve this by interpreting the stochastic mirror descent method equivalently as a randomized block gradient method applied to the dual problem of (
1.1
).
Even for the stochastic gradient descent method, our convergence rate result supplements the existing results since only sub-optimal convergence rates have been derived under diminishing step-sizes, see
[
18
,
25
]
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we first collect some basic facts on convex analysis in Banach spaces and then give an account on the stochastic mirror descent method.
In Section
3
we prove some convergence results on the stochastic mirror descent method under various choices of the step-sizes. When the sought solution satisfies a benchmark source condition, in Section
4
we establish an order optimal convergence rate result. Finally, in Section
5
we present extensive numerical simulations to test the performance of the stochastic mirror descent method.

2.
The method

2.1.
Preliminaries

In this section, we will collect some basic facts on convex analysis in Banach
spaces which will be used in the analysis of the stochastic mirror descent method; for
more details one may refer to
[
34
,
38
]
for instance.

Let
X
be a Banach space whose norm is denoted by
\|\cdot\|
, we use
X^{*}
to denote its dual space. Given
x\in X
and
\xi\in X^{*}
we write
\langle\xi,x\rangle=\xi(x)

for the duality pairing; in case
X
is a Hilbert space, we also use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to denote the inner product. For a convex function
f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
, its effective domain is denoted by

\mbox{dom}(f):=\{x\in X:f(x)<\infty\}.

If
\mbox{dom}(f)\neq\emptyset
,
f
is called proper.
Given
x\in\mbox{dom}(f)
, an element
\xi\in X^{*}
is called a subgradient of
f
at
x
if

f(\bar{x})\geq f(x)+\langle\xi,\bar{x}-x\rangle,\quad\forall\bar{x}\in X.

The collection of all subgradients of
f
at
x
is denoted as
\partial f(x)
and is called the
subdifferential of
f
at
x
. If
\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset
, then
f
is called subdifferentiable at
x
. Thus

x\to\partial f(x)
defines a set-valued mapping
\partial f
whose domain of definition is defined as

\mbox{dom}(\partial f):=\{x\in\mbox{dom}(f):\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset\}.

Given
x\in\mbox{dom}(\partial f)
and
\xi\in\partial f(x)
, the Bregman distance induced by
f
at
x
in
the direction
\xi
is defined by

D_{f}^{\xi}(\bar{x},x):=f(\bar{x})-f(x)-\langle\xi,\bar{x}-x\rangle,\quad\forall\bar{x}\in X

which is always nonnegative.

A proper function
f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is called strongly convex if there exists a
constant
\sigma>0
such that

\displaystyle f(t\bar{x}+(1-t)x)+\sigma t(1-t)\|\bar{x}-x\|^{2}\leq tf(\bar{x})+(1-t)f(x)

(2.1)

for all
\bar{x},x\in\mbox{dom}(f)
and
t\in[0,1]
. The largest number
\sigma>0
such that (
2.1
)
holds true is called the modulus of convexity of
f
. It is easy to see that for a proper,
strongly convex function
f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
with modulus of convexity
\sigma>0
there holds

\displaystyle D_{f}^{\xi}(\bar{x},x)\geq\sigma\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}

(2.2)

for all
\bar{x}\in\mbox{dom}(f)
,
x\in\mbox{dom}(\partial f)
and
\xi\in\partial f(x)
.

For a proper function
f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
, its Legendre-Fenchel conjugate is
defined by

f^{*}(\xi):=\sup_{x\in X}\{\langle\xi,x\rangle-f(x)\},\quad\xi\in X^{*}

which is a convex function taking values in
(-\infty,\infty]
. By definition we have the Fenchel-Young inequality

\displaystyle f^{*}(\xi)+f(x)\geq\langle\xi,x\rangle

(2.3)

for all
x\in X
and
\xi\in X^{*}
. If
f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is proper, lower semi-continuous
and convex,
f^{*}
is also proper and

\displaystyle\xi\in\partial f(x)\Longleftrightarrow x\in\partial f^{*}(\xi)\Longleftrightarrow f(x)+f^{*}(\xi)=\langle\xi,x\rangle.

(2.4)

The following important result gives further properties of
f^{*}
which in particular shows that the strong convexity of
f
implies the continuous differentiability of
f^{*}
with gradient
\nabla f^{*}
mapping
X^{*}
to
X
; see
[
38
, Corollary 3.5.11]

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and let
f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper,
lower semi-continuous, strongly convex function with modulus of convexity
\sigma>0
.
Then
\mbox{dom}(f^{*})=X^{*}
,
f^{*}
is Fréchet differentiable and its gradient
\nabla f^{*}
maps
X^{*}
into
X
satisfying

\|\nabla f^{*}(\xi)-\nabla f^{*}(\eta)\|\leq\frac{\|\xi-\eta\|}{2\sigma}

for all
\xi,\eta\in X^{*}
.

Given a proper strongly convex function
f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
, we may consider for each
\xi\in X^{*}
the convex minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}\left\{f(x)-\langle\xi,x\rangle\right\}

(2.5)

which is involved in the formulation of the stochastic mirror descent method below. According to
[
38
, Theorem 3.5.8]
, (
2.4
) and Proposition
2.1
we have

Proposition 2.2
.

If
f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a proper, lower semi-continuous, strongly convex function, then for any
\xi\in X^{*}
the minimization problem (
2.5
) has a unique minimizer given by
\nabla f^{*}(\xi)
.

2.2.
Description of the method

In order to motivate our method, we first briefly review how to extend the gradient method in Hilbert spaces to solve the minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}f(x)

(2.6)

in Banach spaces, where
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a Fréchet differentiable function defined
on a Banach space
X
. When
X
is a Hilbert space, the gradient method for solving (
2.6
) takes the form
x_{n+1}=x_{n}-t_{n}\nabla f(x_{n})
which can be equivalently
stated as

\displaystyle x_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|x-(x_{n}-t_{n}\nabla f(x_{n}))\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|x-x_{n}\|^{2}+t_{n}\langle\nabla f(x_{n}),x\rangle\right\},

(2.7)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product on
X
and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the induced norm. When
X
is a general Banach space, inner product is no longer available and
\nabla f(x_{n})
is not necessarily an element in
X
; instead
\nabla f(x_{n})
is an element in
X^{*}
. Therefore, in order to guarantee
\langle\nabla f(x_{n}),x\rangle
to be meaningful, this expression should be understood as the dual pairing between
X^{*}
and
X
.
On the other hand, since no Hilbert space norm is available,
\frac{1}{2}\|x-x_{n}\|^{2}
in (
2.2
) should be replaced by other suitable distance-like functionals.
In order to capture the feature of the sought solution, a suitable convex penalty function
{\mathcal{R}}:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is usually chosen to enhance the feature.
In such a situation, one may use the Bregman distance induced by
{\mathcal{R}}
to fulfill the purpose. To be more precise, if
\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n})\neq\emptyset
, we may take
\xi_{n}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n})
suitably and then use the Bregman distance

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(x,x_{n})={\mathcal{R}}(x)-{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n})-\langle\xi_{n},x-x_{n}\rangle

to replace
\frac{1}{2}\|x-x_{n}\|^{2}
in (
2.2
). This leads to the new updating formula

x_{n+1}\in\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(x,x_{n})+t_{n}\langle\nabla f(x_{n}),x\rangle\right\}

for
x_{n+1}
. By the expression of
D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(x,x_{n})
, we have

x_{n+1}\in\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n}-t_{n}\nabla f(x_{n}),x\rangle\right\}.

Let
\xi_{n+1}=\xi_{n}-t_{n}\nabla f(x_{n})
, then

x_{n+1}\in\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n+1},x\rangle\right\}.

If
x_{n+1}
is well-defined, then by the optimality condition on
x_{n+1}
we have
\xi_{n+1}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n+1})
. Therefore, we can repeat the above procedure, leading to the algorithm

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x_{n}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n},x\rangle\right\},\\
&\xi_{n+1}=\xi_{n}-t_{n}\nabla f(x_{n})\end{split}

(2.8)

for solving (
2.6
) in Banach spaces, which is called the mirror descent method in optimization community; see
[
1
,
6
,
30
,
31
]
.

In order to apply the mirror descent method to solve (
1.2
) when only noisy data
y_{i}^{\delta}
are available, we may consider a problem of the form (
2.6
) with

f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|A_{i}x-y_{i}^{\delta}\|^{2}.

Note that
\nabla f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}^{*}(A_{i}x-y_{i}^{\delta})
. Therefore, an application of (
2.8
) gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x_{n}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n},x\rangle\right\},\\
&\xi_{n+1}=\xi_{n}-t_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}^{*}(A_{i}x_{n}-y_{i}^{\delta}).\end{split}

(2.9)

This is exactly the method (
1.5
) which has been considered in
[
4
,
21
,
23
]
.
Clearly, the implementation of (
2.9
) requires to calculate
A_{i}^{*}(A_{i}x_{n}-y_{i}^{\delta})
for all
i=1,\cdots,p
at each iteration. In case
p
is huge, each iteration step in (
2.9
) requires a huge amount of computational work. In order to reduce the computational load per iteration, one may randomly choose a subset
I_{n}
from
\{1,\cdots,p\}
with small size to form the partial term

f_{I_{n}}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in I_{n}}\|A_{i}x-y_{i}^{\delta}\|^{2}

of
f
and use its gradient
\nabla f_{I_{n}}(x_{n})=\sum_{i\in I_{n}}A_{i}^{*}(A_{i}x_{n}-y_{i}^{\delta})
at
x_{n}
as a replacement of
\nabla f(x_{n})
in (
2.9
). This leads to the following mini-batch stochastic mirror descent method for solving (
1.2
) with noisy data.

Algorithm 1
.

Fix a batch size
b
, pick the initial guess
\xi_{0}=0
in
X^{*}
and set
\xi_{0}^{\delta}:=\xi_{0}
. For
n\geq 0
do the following:

(i)

Calculate
x_{n}^{\delta}\in X
by solving

\displaystyle x_{n}^{\delta}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},x\rangle\right\};

(2.10)

(ii)

Randomly select a subset
I_{n}\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
with
|I_{n}|=b
via the uniform distribution;

(iii)

Choose a step-size
t_{n}^{\delta}\geq 0
;

(iv)

Define
\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}\in X^{*}
by

\displaystyle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-t_{n}^{\delta}\sum_{i\in I_{n}}A_{i}^{*}\left(A_{i}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{i}^{\delta}\right).

(2.11)

It should be pointed out that the choice of the step-size
t_{n}^{\delta}
plays a crucial role on the convergence of Algorithm
1
. We will consider in Section
3
several rules for choosing the step-size. Note also that, at each iteration step of Algorithm
1
,
x_{n}^{\delta}
is defined by a minimization problem (
2.10
).
When
{\mathcal{R}}
is proper, lower semi-continuous and strongly convex, it follows from Proposition
2.2
that
x_{n}^{\delta}
is well-defined. Moreover, for many important choices of
{\mathcal{R}}
,
x_{n}^{\delta}
can be given by an explicit formula, see Section
5
for instance, and thus the calculation of
x_{n}^{\delta}
does not take much time.

There exist extensive studies on the mirror descent method and its stochastic variants in optimization, see
[
6
,
8
,
28
,
29
,
39
]
for instance. The existing works either depend crucially on the finite-dimensionality of the underlying spaces or establish only error estimates in terms of objective function values, and therefore they are not applicable to our Algorithm
1
for ill-posed problems. We need to develop new analysis. Our analysis of Algorithm
1
is based on the following assumption which is assumed throughout the paper.

Assumption 1
.

(i)

X
is a Banach space,
Y_{i}
is a Hilbert space and
A_{i}:X\to Y_{i}
is a bounded linear operator for each
i=1,\cdots,p
;

(ii)

{\mathcal{R}}:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a proper, lower semi-continuous, strongly convex function with modulus of convexity
\sigma>0
;

(iii)

The system
A_{i}x=y_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,p
, has a solution in
\emph{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
.

According to Assumption
1
(ii) and Proposition
2.1
, the Legendre-Fenchel conjugate
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
of
{\mathcal{R}}
is continuous differentiable with Lipschitz continuous gradient, i.e.

\displaystyle\|\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi)-\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\eta)\|\leq\frac{\|\xi-\eta\|}{2\sigma},\quad\forall\xi,\eta\in X^{*}.

(2.12)

Moreover, by virtue of Assumption
1
and Proposition
2.2
, the linear system (
1.1
) has a unique
{\mathcal{R}}
-minimizing solution

x^{\dagger}
and Algorithm
1
is well-defined. By the optimality condition on
x_{n}^{\delta}
and Proposition
2.2
we have

\displaystyle\xi_{n}^{\delta}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad x_{n}^{\delta}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})

(2.13)

which is the starting point of our convergence analysis.

3.
Convergence

In order to establish a convergence result on Algorithm
1
, we need to specify a probability space on which
the analysis will be carried out. Let
\Lambda_{p}:=\{1,\cdots,p\}
and let
{\mathcal{S}}_{p}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra consisting of all subsets of
\Lambda_{p}
. Recall that, at each iteration step, a subset of indices is randomly chosen from
\Lambda_{p}
via the uniform distribution. Therefore, for each
n\geq 1
, it is natural to consider
x_{n}^{\delta}
and
\xi_{n}^{\delta}
on the sample space

\Lambda_{p}^{n}:=\underbrace{\Lambda_{p}\times\cdots\times\Lambda_{p}}_{n\mbox{ copies}}

equipped with the
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{S}}_{p}^{\otimes n}
and the uniform distributed probability, denoted as
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
.
According to the Kolmogorov extension theorem (
[
2
]
), there exists a unique probability
{\mathbb{P}}
defined on the
measurable space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}}):=(\Lambda_{p}^{\infty},{\mathcal{S}}_{p}^{\otimes\infty})
such that each
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is
consistent with
{\mathbb{P}}
. Let
{\mathbb{E}}
denote the expectation on the probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
.
Given a Banach space
X
, we use
L^{2}(\Omega,X)
to denote the space consisting of all random variables
x
with values in
X

such that
\mathbb{E}[\|x\|^{2}]
is finite; this is a Banach space under the norm

\left(\mathbb{E}[\|x\|^{2}]\right)^{1/2}.

Concerning Algorithm
1
we will use
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\}
to denote the natural filtration,
where
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}:=\sigma(I_{0},\cdots,I_{n-1})
for each
n\geq 1
. We will frequently use the identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\phi]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\phi|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}]]

(3.1)

for any random variable
\phi
on
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
. where
\mathbb{E}[\phi|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}]
denotes the expectation of
\phi
conditioned on
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
.

In this section we will prove some convergence results on Algorithm
1
under suitable choices of the step-sizes. The convergence of Algorithm
1
will be established by investigating the convergence property of its counterpart for exact data together with its stability property. For simplicity of exposition, for each index set
I=\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{b}\}\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
of size
b
we set

Y_{I}:=Y_{i_{1}}\times\cdots\times Y_{i_{b}},\quad y_{I}^{\delta}:=(y_{i_{1}}^{\delta},\cdots,y_{i_{b}}^{\delta}),\quad\delta_{I}^{2}=\delta_{i_{1}}^{2}+\cdots+\delta_{i_{b}}^{2}

and define
A_{I}:X\to Y_{I}
by

A_{I}x:=(A_{i_{1}}x,\cdots,A_{i_{b}}x),\quad\forall x\in X.

Let
A_{I}^{*}
denote the adjoint of
A_{I}
. Then the updating formula (
2.11
) of
\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}
from
\xi_{n}^{\delta}
can be rephrased as

\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-t_{n}^{\delta}A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}).

We start with the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Consider Algorithm
1
and assume that

0\leq t_{n}^{\delta}\leq\min\left\{\frac{\mu_{0}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\|^{2}}{\|A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta})\|^{2}},\mu_{1}\right\}

for all
n\geq 0
, where
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
are two positive constants with
c_{0}:=1-\mu_{0}/(4\sigma)>0
. Let
\hat{x}
be any solution of (
1.1
) in
\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
and let

\Delta_{n}^{\delta}:=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}^{\delta}}(\hat{x},x_{n}^{\delta}),\quad n=0,1,\cdots.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n}^{\delta}\right]\leq\frac{\mu_{1}b}{4c_{0}p}\delta^{2}.

Proof.

Note that

\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n}^{\delta}=\left(\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta},x_{n+1}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle-{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n+1}^{\delta})\right)+\left({\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})+\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},\hat{x}-x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle\right).

By using (
2.13
) and (
2.4
) we have

{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})=\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle

for all
n\geq 0
. Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\left({\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n+1}^{\delta})-\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta},\hat{x}\rangle\right)-\left({\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})-\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},\hat{x}\rangle\right).

Using
x_{n}^{\delta}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})
in (
2.13
) and the inequality (
2.12
) on
\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\left({\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n+1}^{\delta})-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})-\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}^{\delta},\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi_{n}^{\delta})\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\quad\,+\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}\|\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}+\langle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}-\hat{x}\rangle.

According to the definition of
\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}
and
A_{I_{n}}\hat{x}=y_{I_{n}}
, we can further obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}\left(t_{n}^{\delta}\right)^{2}\|A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta})\|^{2}-t_{n}^{\delta}\langle A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta},A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}\rangle.

By the given condition on
t_{n}^{\delta}
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we then obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}-\Delta_{n}^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\sigma}t_{n}^{\delta}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\|^{2}-t_{n}^{\delta}\langle A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta},A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-c_{0}t_{n}^{\delta}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\|^{2}+t_{n}^{\delta}\delta_{I_{n}}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4c_{0}}t_{n}^{\delta}\delta_{I_{n}}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu_{1}}{4c_{0}}\delta_{I_{n}}^{2}.

By taking the expectation and using
\sum_{I:|I|=b}
to denote a sum over all subsets
I\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}
with
|I|=b
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}^{\delta}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu_{1}}{4c_{0}}\mathbb{E}[\delta_{I_{n}}^{2}]=\frac{\mu_{1}}{4c_{0}}\frac{1}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{I:|I|=b}\delta_{I}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{1}}{4c_{0}}\frac{1}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{I:|I|=b}\sum_{i\in I}\delta_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{1}}{4c_{0}}\frac{1}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{I:|I|=b\,\&\,i\in I}\delta_{i}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{1}}{4c_{0}}\frac{{p-1\choose b-1}}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{1}b}{4c_{0}p}\delta^{2}

which shows the desired inequality.
∎

Next we consider Algorithm
1
with exact data and drop the superscript
\delta
for every quantity defined by the algorithm, Thus
x_{n},\xi_{n}
denote the corresponding iterative sequences and
t_{n}
denotes the step-size.
We now show a convergence result for Algorithm
1
with exact data by demonstrating that

\{x_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(\Omega,X)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Consider Algorithm
1
with exact data and assume that

\displaystyle\mu_{2}\leq t_{n}\leq\min\left\{\frac{\mu_{0}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}}{\|A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}})\|^{2}},\mu_{1}\right\}\quad\mbox{ when }A_{I_{n}}x_{n}\neq y_{I_{n}},

(3.2)

where
\mu_{0}
,
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are positive numbers with
c_{0}:=1-\mu_{0}/(4\sigma)>0
. Then

\mathbb{E}[\|x_{n}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}]\to 0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(x^{\dagger},x_{n})\right]\to 0

as
n\to\infty
, where
x^{\dagger}
denotes the unique
{\mathcal{R}}
-minimizing solution of (
1.1
).

Proof.

Let
\hat{x}
be any solution of (
1.1
) in
\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
and define

\Delta_{n}:=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(\hat{x},x_{n}).

By the similar argument in the proof of Lemma
3.1
we can obtain

\Delta_{n+1}-\Delta_{n}\leq-c_{0}t_{n}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}\leq-c_{0}\mu_{2}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}.

Consequently

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n+1}]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}]

\displaystyle\leq-c_{0}\mu_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}\right]=-c_{0}\mu_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}|{\mathcal{F}_{n}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=-c_{0}\mu_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{I:|I|=b}\|A_{I}x_{n}-y_{I}\|^{2}\right].

(3.3)

This shows that
\{\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}]\}
is monotonically decreasing and therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}]\mbox{ exists}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi_{n}=0

(3.4)

where

\Phi_{n}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{I:|I|=b}\|A_{I}x_{n}-y_{I}\|^{2}\right].

If
\Phi_{n}=0
for some
n
, we must have

\sum_{I:|I|=b}\|A_{I}x_{n}-y_{I}\|^{2}=0

along any sample path
(I_{0},\cdots,I_{n-1})
since there exist only finite many such sample paths each with a positive probability; consequently
\xi_{m}=\xi_{n}
and
x_{m}=x_{n}
and hence
\Phi_{m}=0
for all
m\geq n
. Based on these properties of
\Phi_{n}
, it is possible to choose a
strictly increasing sequence
\{n_{l}\}
of integers by setting
n_{0}:=0
and, for each
l\geq 1
, by letting

n_{l}
be the first integer satisfying

n_{l}\geq n_{l-1}+1\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\Phi_{n_{l}}\leq\Phi_{n_{l-1}}.

It is easy to see that for this sequence
\{n_{l}\}
there holds

\displaystyle\Phi_{n_{l}}\leq\Phi_{n},\quad\forall 0\leq n\leq n_{l}.

(3.5)

For the above chosen sequence
\{n_{l}\}
of integers, we are now going to show that

\displaystyle\sup_{l\geq k}\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n_{k}}}(x_{n_{l}},x_{n_{k}})\right]\to 0\quad\mbox{as }k\to\infty.

(3.6)

By the definition of the Bregman distance we have for any
l>k
that

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n_{k}}}(x_{n_{l}},x_{n_{k}})=\Delta_{n_{k}}-\Delta_{n_{l}}+\langle\xi_{n_{l}}-\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle.

Taking the expectation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n_{k}}}(x_{n_{l}},x_{n_{k}})\right]=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n_{k}}]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n_{l}}]+\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}}-\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right].

(3.7)

By using the definition of
\{\xi_{n}\}
we have

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{n_{l}}-\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{n_{l}-1}\langle\xi_{n+1}-\xi_{n},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{n_{l}-1}t_{n}\langle A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}),x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{n_{l}-1}t_{n}\langle A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}},A_{I_{n}}x_{n_{l}}-y_{I_{n}}\rangle.

Therefore, by taking the expectation and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}}-\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right]\right|\leq\mu_{1}\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{n_{l}-1}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}},A_{I_{n}}x_{n_{l}}-y_{I_{n}}\rangle\right]\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\mu_{1}\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{n_{l}-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n_{l}}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

Since
x_{n}
is
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
-measurable, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right]\right]=\Phi_{n}.

We can not treat the term
\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n_{l}}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}\right]
in the same way because

x_{n_{l}}
is not necessarily
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
-measurable. However, by noting that

\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n_{l}}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{I:|I|=b}\|A_{I}x_{n_{l}}-y_{I}\|^{2},

we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n_{l}}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{I:|I|=b}\|A_{I}x_{n_{l}}-y_{I}\|^{2}\right]\leq{p\choose b}\Phi_{n_{l}}.

Therefore, with
c_{p,b}:={p\choose b}^{1/2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}}-\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right]\right|\leq\mu_{1}c_{p,b}\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{n_{l}-1}\Phi_{n}^{1/2}\Phi_{n_{l}}^{1/2}.

(3.8)

By virtue of (
3.5
) and (
3
) we then have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}}-\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right]\right|\leq\mu_{1}c_{p,b}\sum_{n=n_{k}}^{n_{l}-1}\Phi_{n}\leq\frac{\mu_{1}c_{p,b}}{c_{0}\mu_{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n_{k}}]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n_{l}}]\right).

(3.9)

Combining this with (
3.7
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n_{k}}}(x_{n_{l}},x_{n_{k}})\right]\leq\left(1+\frac{\mu_{1}c_{p,b}}{c_{0}\mu_{2}}\right)\left(\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n_{k}}]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n_{l}}]\right)

which together with the first equation in (
3.4
) shows (
3.6
) immediately.

By using the strong convexity of
{\mathcal{R}}
, we can obtain from (
3.6
) that

\sup_{l\geq k}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{n_{l}}-x_{n_{k}}\|^{2}]\to 0\quad\mbox{as }k\to\infty

which means that
\{x_{n_{l}}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(\Omega,X)
. Thus there exists a random vector

x^{*}\in L^{2}(\Omega,X)
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|x_{n_{l}}-x^{*}\|^{2}]\to 0\quad\mbox{ as }l\to\infty.

(3.10)

By taking a subsequence of
\{n_{l}\}
if necessary, we can obtain from (
3.10
) and the second equation in
(
3.4
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to\infty}\|x_{n_{l}}-x^{*}\|=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{l\to\infty}\sum_{I:|I|=b}\|A_{I}x_{n_{l}}-y_{I}\|^{2}=0

(3.11)

almost surely. Consequently

\sum_{I:|I|=b}\|A_{I}x^{*}-y_{I}\|^{2}=0\ \ \mbox{almost surely},

i.e.
x^{*}
is a solution of (
1.1
) almost surely.

We next show that
x^{*}\in\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
almost surely. It suffices to show
\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})]<\infty
.
Recall that
\xi_{n_{l}}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x)+\langle\xi_{n_{l}},x_{n_{l}}-x\rangle,\quad\forall x\in X.

(3.12)

By using (
3.12
) with
x=x^{\dagger}
, taking the expectation, and noting
\xi_{n_{0}}=\xi_{0}=0
,
we can obtain from (
3.9
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}}-\xi_{n_{0}},x_{n_{l}}-x^{\dagger}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\frac{\mu_{1}c_{p,b}}{c_{0}\mu_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[D_{{\mathcal{R}}}^{\xi_{0}}(x^{\dagger},x_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=:C<\infty.

Therefore, it follows from the first equation in (
3.11
), the lower semi-continuity of
{\mathcal{R}}

and Fatou’s Lemma that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\liminf_{l\to\infty}{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})\right]\leq\liminf_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})\right]\leq C<\infty.

(3.13)

We have thus shown that
x^{*}\in L^{2}(\Omega,X)
is a solution of (
1.1
) in
\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
almost surely.

In order to proceed further, we will show that, for any
\hat{x}\in L^{2}(\Omega,X)
that is a solution of (
1.1
) in
\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
almost surely, there holds

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right]=0.

(3.14)

To see this, for any
k<l
we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}}-\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right].

Since
\hat{x}\in L^{2}(\Omega,X)
is a solution of (
1.1
) in
\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
almost surely, by the definition
of
\{\xi_{n}\}
and
\xi_{0}=0
we can use the similar argument for deriving (
3.8
) to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right]\right|\leq\mu_{1}c_{p,b}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{n_{k}-1}\Phi_{n}^{1/2}\right)\Phi_{n_{l}}^{1/2}.

This and the second equation in (
3.4
) imply for any fixed
k
that

\mathbb{E}[\langle\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle]\to 0
as
l\to\infty
. Hence

\displaystyle\limsup_{l\to\infty}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{l\geq k}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}}-\xi_{n_{k}},x_{n_{l}}-\hat{x}\rangle\right]\right|

for all
k
. By virtue of (
3.9
) and letting
k\to\infty
we thus obtain (
3.14
).

Based on (
3.12
) with
x=x^{*}
and (
3.14
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})\right]+\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n_{l}},x_{n_{l}}-x^{*}\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})]

which together with (
3.13
) then implies

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})]=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})]

(3.15)

By using (
3.15
), (
3.12
) with
x=x^{\dagger}
,
and (
3.14
) with
\hat{x}=x^{\dagger}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})]=\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})]\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\langle\xi_{n_{l}},x_{n_{l}}-x^{\dagger}\rangle]={\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger}).

Since
x^{*}
is a solution of (
1.1
) in
\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
almost surely, we have
{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})\geq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})
almost surely which implies
\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})]\geq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})
. Consequently
\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x^{*})]={\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})

and thus it follows from (
3.15
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})]={\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger}).

(3.16)

Finally, from (
3.16
) and (
3.14
) with
\hat{x}=x^{\dagger}
it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n_{l}}}(x^{\dagger},x_{n_{l}})\right]=\lim_{l\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})-{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n_{l}})-\langle\xi_{n_{l}},x^{\dagger}-x_{n_{l}}\rangle]\right)=0.

By the monotonicity of
\{\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(x^{\dagger},x_{n})]\}
we can conclude

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(x^{\dagger},x_{n})\right]=0

and hence
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{n}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}]=0
by the strong convexity of
{\mathcal{R}}
.
The proof is therefore complete.
∎

In order to use Theorem
3.2
to establish the convergence of Algorithm
1
with noisy data, we need to investigate the stability of the algorithm, i.e. the behavior of
x_{n}^{\delta}
and
\xi_{n}^{\delta}
as
\delta\to 0
for each fixed
n
. In the following we will provide stability results of Algorithm
1
under the following three choices of step-sizes:

(s1)

t_{n}^{\delta}
depends only on
I_{n}
, i.e.

t_{n}^{\delta}=t_{I_{n}}
and
0<t_{I}<4\sigma/\|A_{I}\|^{2}

for all
I\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
with
|I|=b
.

(s2)

t_{n}^{\delta}
is chosen according to the formula

t_{n}^{\delta}=\min\left\{\frac{\mu_{0}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\|^{2}}{\|A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta})\|^{2}},\tilde{\mu}_{1}\right\},

where
\mu_{0}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{1}
are two positive constants with
0<\mu_{0}<4\sigma
.

(s3)

In case the information of
\delta_{1},\cdots,\delta_{p}
is available, choose the step-size
t_{n}^{\delta}
according to the rule

\displaystyle t_{n}^{\delta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\min\left\{\frac{\mu_{0}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\|^{2}}{\|A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta})\|^{2}},\tilde{\mu}_{1}\right\}&\mbox{ if }\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\|>\tau\delta_{I_{n}},\\[5.16663pt]
0&\mbox{ otherwise},\end{array}\right.

where
\tau\geq 1
,
\mu_{0}>0
and
\tilde{\mu}_{1}>0
are preassigned numbers with
0<\mu_{0}<4\sigma
.

The step-size chosen in (s1) is motivated by the Landweber iteration which uses constant step-size. Along the iterations in Algorithm
1
, when the same subset
I
is repeatedly chosen, (s1) suggests to use the same step-size in computation. The step-sizes chosen by (s1) could be small and thus slows down the computation. The rules given in (s2) and (s3) use the adaptive strategy which may produce large step-sizes. The step-size chosen by (s2) is motivated by the deterministic minimal error method, see
[
24
]

Let

Lemma 3.3
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Consider Algorithm
1
with the step-size chosen by (s1), (s2) or (s3). Then for each fixed integer

\delta\to 0

We show the result by induction on

(I_{0},\cdots,I_{n-1})
each with positive probability, we can conclude from the induction hypothesis that

\displaystyle\|x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n}\|\to 0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}\|\to 0\quad\mbox{as }\delta\to 0

(3.17)

along every sample path
(I_{0},\cdots,I_{n-1})

(I_{0},\cdots,I_{n-1},I_{n})

\displaystyle\|x_{n+1}^{\delta}-x_{n+1}\|\to 0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n+1}\|\to 0\quad\mbox{as }\delta\to 0.

To see this, we first show
\|\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n+1}\|\to 0

\delta\to 0

We first consider the case

\displaystyle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n+1}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}-t_{n}^{\delta}A_{I_{n}}^{*}\left(A_{I_{n}}(x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n})-(y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}})\right).

Note that for all the choices of the step-sizes given in (s1), (s2) and (s3) we have
0\leq t_{n}^{\delta}\leq\hat{\mu}_{1}

\hat{\mu}_{1}

\delta

\displaystyle\|\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n+1}\|\leq\|\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}\|+\hat{\mu}_{1}\|A_{I_{n}}\|\left(\|A_{I_{n}}\|\|x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n}\|+\delta\right).

Consequently, by using (
3.17

\|\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n+1}\|\to 0

\delta\to 0

Next we consider the case
A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\neq 0

\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\|>\tau\delta_{I_{n}}

\displaystyle\langle A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}),x_{n}-x^{\dagger}\rangle=\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}}\|^{2}>0

which implies that

t_{n}^{\delta}=\min\left\{\frac{\mu_{0}\|A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\|^{2}}{\|A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta})\|^{2}},\tilde{\mu}_{1}\right\}.

By using again (
3.17

\displaystyle t_{n}^{\delta}\to t_{n}\quad\mbox{ as }\delta\to 0.

For the step-size chosen by (s1) the equation (

. Therefore, by noting that

\displaystyle\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}-\xi_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}-t_{n}^{\delta}A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta})+t_{n}A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}})

\displaystyle=\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}+(t_{n}-t_{n}^{\delta})A_{I_{n}}^{*}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}-y_{I_{n}})

Now by virtue of the formula

Finally, since there are only finitely many sample paths of the form

Theorem 3.4
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Consider Algorithm
1
with the step-size chosen by (s1), (s2) or (s3). If the integer

\delta\to 0

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{n}^{\delta}:=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}^{\delta}}(x^{\dagger},x_{n}^{\delta})
for all
n\geq 0

{\mathcal{R}}

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}]\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
. According to Lemma
3.1

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n+1}^{\delta}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n}^{\delta}\right]\leq\frac{\mu_{1}b}{4c_{0}p}\delta^{2}

for all
n\geq 0
. Let
n
be any fixed integer. Since
n_{\delta}\to\infty
as
\delta\to 0

n_{\delta}>n
for small
\delta>0
.
Thus, we may repeatedly use the above inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n}^{\delta}\right]+\frac{\mu_{1}b}{4c_{0}p}(n_{\delta}-n)\delta^{2}.

Since
\delta^{2}n_{\delta}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0

\displaystyle\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n}^{\delta}\right]=\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\left({\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},x^{\dagger}-x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle\right]\right)

for any
n\geq 0
. With the help of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},x^{\dagger}-x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n},x^{\dagger}-x_{n}\rangle\right]\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n},x^{\dagger}-x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle\right]\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n},x_{n}-x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle\right]\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|\xi_{n}^{\delta}-\xi_{n}\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{n}^{\delta}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,+\left(\mathbb{E}[\|\xi_{n}\|^{2}]\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{n}-x_{n}^{\delta}\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

Thus, we may use Lemma
3.3
to conclude

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},x^{\dagger}-x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n},x^{\dagger}-x_{n}\rangle\right].

According to the proof of Lemma
3.3
we have
\|x_{n}^{\delta}-x_{n}\|\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
along each sample path
(I_{0},\cdots,I_{n-1})
. Thus, from the lower

{\mathcal{R}}
and Fatou’s lemma it follows

\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n})]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\liminf_{\delta\to 0}{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})\right]\leq\liminf_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})\right].

Consequently

\displaystyle\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})-\liminf_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})\right]-\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},x^{\dagger}-x_{n}^{\delta}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})-\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n})]-\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\xi_{n},x^{\dagger}-x_{n}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi_{n}}(x^{\dagger},x_{n})]

for all
n\geq 0
. Letting

3.2

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}]\to 0

as
\delta\to 0
.
∎

Lemma 3.5

4.
Rate of convergence

In Section
3
we have proved the convergence of Algorithm
1
under various choices of the step-sizes. In this section we will focus on deriving convergence rates. For ill-posed problems, this requires the sought solution to satisfy suitable source conditions. We will consider the benchmark source condition of the form (
[
7
]
)

\displaystyle A^{*}\lambda^{\dagger}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})

(4.1)

for some
\lambda^{\dagger}:=(\lambda_{,1}^{\dagger},\cdots,\lambda_{,p}^{\dagger})\in Y:=Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{p}
, where, here and below, for any
\lambda\in Y
we use
\lambda_{,i}
to denote its
i
-th component in
Y_{i}
.
Deriving convergence rates under general choices of step-sizes can be very challenging. Therefore, in this section we will restrict ourselves to the step-sizes chosen by (s1) that includes the constant step-sizes. The corresponding algorithm can be reformulated as follows.

Algorithm 2
.

Fix a batch size
b
, pick the initial guess
\xi_{0}=0
in
X^{*}
and step-sizes
t_{I}
for all
I\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
with
|I|=b
. Set
\xi_{0}^{\delta}:=\xi_{0}
. For
n\geq 0
do the following:

(i)

Calculate
x_{n}^{\delta}\in X
by solving (
2.10
);

(ii)

Randomly select a subset
I_{n}\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
with
|I_{n}|=b
via the uniform distribution;

(iii)

Define
\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}\in X^{*}
by (
2.11
).

For Algorithm
2
we have the following convergence rate result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold and consider Algorithm
2
. Assume that
0<t_{I}<4\sigma/\|A_{I}\|^{2}
for all subsets
I\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
with
|I|=b
; furthermore
t_{I}=t
with a constant
t
for all such
I
in case

. If the sought solution

satisfies the source condition (
4.1
) and the integer

is chosen such that

is of the magnitude of

, then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x_{n_{\delta}}^{\delta}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\right]\leq C\delta,

where

is a positive constant independent of
\delta
,
p
and

.

When
X
is a Hilbert space and
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\|x\|^{2}/2
, the stochastic mirror descent method becomes the stochastic gradient method. In the existing literature on stochastic gradient method for solving ill-posed problems, sub-optimal convergence rates have been derived for diminishing step-sizes under general source conditions on the sought solution, see
[
18
,
25
]
. Our result given in Theorem
4.1
supplements these results by demonstrating that the stochastic gradient method can achieve the order optimal convergence rate
O(\delta)
under constant step-sizes if the sought solution satisfies the source condition (
4.1
).

The result in Theorem
4.1
also demonstrates the role played by the batch size
b

is used. However, this does not mean the corresponding algorithm with larger batch size

The proof of Theorem
4.1
is based on considering an equivalent formulation of Algorithm
2
which we will derive in the following. The derivation is based on applying the randomized
block gradient method (
[
26
,
32
,
36
]
) to the dual problem of (
1.4
) with

replaced by
y^{\delta}
.
The associated Lagrangian function is

L(x;\lambda):={\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\lambda,Ax-y^{\delta}\rangle,

where
x\in X
and
\lambda\in Y
. Thus the dual function is

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in X}L(x;\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\sup_{x\in X}\left\{\left\langle A^{*}\lambda,x\right\rangle-{\mathcal{R}}(x)\right\}+\langle\lambda,y^{\delta}\rangle=-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\left(A^{*}\lambda\right)+\langle\lambda,y^{\delta}\rangle

which means the dual problem is

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\in Y}\left\{d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda):={\mathcal{R}}^{*}\left(A^{*}\lambda\right)-\langle\lambda,y^{\delta}\rangle\right\}.

(4.2)

Note that
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
is continuous differentiable and

d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda)={\mathcal{R}}^{*}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}^{*}\lambda_{,i}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\langle\lambda_{,i},y_{i}^{\delta}\rangle.

Therefore we may solve (
4.2
) by the randomized block gradient method which iteratively selects partial components of
\lambda
at random to be updated by a partial gradient of
d_{y^{\delta}}
and leave other components unchanged. To be more precisely, for any
\lambda\in Y
and any subset
I\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
, let
\lambda_{I}
denote the group of components
\lambda_{,i}
of
\lambda
with
i\in I
. Assume
\lambda_{n}^{\delta}:=(\lambda_{n,1}^{\delta},\cdots,\lambda_{n,p}^{\delta})\in Y
is a current iterate. We then choose a subset of indices
I_{n}
from
\{1,\cdots,p\}
with
|I_{n}|=b
randomly with uniform distribution and
define
\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta}:=(\lambda_{n+1,1}^{\delta},\cdots,\lambda_{n+1,p}^{\delta})
by setting
\lambda_{n+1,i}^{\delta}=\lambda_{n,i}^{\delta}
if

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{n+1,I_{n}}^{\delta}=\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}-t_{I_{n}}\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),

(4.3)

with step-sizes
t_{I_{n}}>0
depending only on
I_{n}
, where, for each
I\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
,
\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}
denotes the partial gradient of
d_{y^{\delta}}
with respect to
\lambda_{I}
. Note that

\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda)=A_{I}\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\left(A^{*}\lambda\right)-y_{I}^{\delta}.

Therefore, by setting
x_{n}^{\delta}:=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\left(A^{*}\lambda_{n}^{\delta}\right)
, we can rewrite (
4.3

\lambda_{n+1,I_{n}}^{\delta}=\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}-t_{I_{n}}\left(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\right).

By using the definition of
x_{n}^{\delta}
and (

A^{*}\lambda_{n}^{\delta}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})
which implies

0\in\partial\left({\mathcal{R}}-\langle\lambda_{n}^{\delta},A\cdot\rangle\right)(x_{n}^{\delta})

and thus

\displaystyle x_{n}^{\delta}=\arg\min_{x\in X}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\lambda_{n}^{\delta},Ax\rangle\right\}.

(4.4)

Combining the above analysis we thus obtain the following mini-batch randomized dual block gradient method for solving (
1.2
) with noisy data.

Algorithm 3
.

Fix a batch size
b

\displaystyle\lambda_{n+1,I_{n}}^{\delta}=\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}-t_{I_{n}}\left(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\right),

\displaystyle=A_{I_{n}^{c}}^{*}\lambda_{n,I_{n}^{c}}^{\delta}+A_{I_{n}}^{*}\left(\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}-t_{I_{n}}(A_{I_{n}}x_{n}^{\delta}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta})\right)

Lemma 4.2
.

Algorithm
2
and Algorithm
3
are equivalent.

Based on Algorithm
3
, we will derive the estimate on
{\mathbb{E}}[\|x_{n}^{\delta}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}]
under the benchmark source condition (
4.1
). Let

with

replaced by

According to (

By using (

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\left(A^{*}\lambda^{\dagger}\right)=\left\langle A^{*}\lambda^{\dagger},x^{\dagger}\right\rangle,\quad{\mathcal{R}}(x_{n}^{\delta})+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\left(A^{*}\lambda_{n}^{\delta}\right)=\left\langle A^{*}\lambda_{n}^{\delta},x_{n}^{\delta}\right\rangle.

(4.6)

Lemma 4.3
.

Let Assumption
1
hold and consider Algorithm
3
. Assume that
0<t_{I}<4\sigma/\|A_{I}\|^{2}
for all
I\subset\{1,\dots,p\}

|I|=b

\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\right]+\frac{t_{\max}b}{2c_{1}p}\delta^{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2{p\choose b}}\sum_{I:|I|=b}t_{I}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}\right]

for all
n\geq 0
, where

t_{\max}:=\max_{I:|I|=b}t_{I}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad c_{1}:=\min_{I:|I|=b}\left\{1-\frac{t_{I}\|A_{I}\|^{2}}{4\sigma}\right\}>0.

Proof.

For any
\lambda\in Y
and any
I\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
with
|I|=b
we may write
\lambda=(\lambda_{I},\lambda_{I^{c}})
up to a permutation. Since the partial gradient of
d_{y^{\delta}}
with respect to
\lambda_{I}
is given by
\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda)=A_{I}\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda)-y_{I}^{\delta}
, it follows from (

\|\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{I},\lambda_{I^{c}})-\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{I}+h,\lambda_{I^{c}})\|\leq L_{I}\|h\|,\quad\forall h\in Y_{I},

where
L_{I}:=\|A_{I}\|^{2}/(2\sigma)
. Consequently, by recalling the relation between
\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta}
and
\lambda_{n}^{\delta}
, we can obtain

\displaystyle d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})

\displaystyle\leq d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})+\langle\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),\lambda_{n+1,I_{n}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}\rangle+\frac{L_{I_{n}}}{2}\|\lambda_{n+1,I_{n}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})-\left(1-\frac{L_{I_{n}}t_{I_{n}}}{2}\right)t_{I_{n}}\|\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}.

By using (
4.7
) and the definition of
\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta}
, we can see that

\displaystyle d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})

\displaystyle=d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})+\langle\lambda_{n+1,I_{n}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta},y_{I_{n}}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\rangle

\displaystyle=d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})-t_{I_{n}}\langle\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),y_{I_{n}}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\rangle.

Combining the above two equations and using the definition of

, we thus have

\displaystyle d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})

\displaystyle\leq t_{I_{n}}\langle\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),y_{I_{n}}-y_{I_{n}}^{\delta}\rangle-\left(1-\frac{L_{I_{n}}t_{I_{n}}}{2}\right)t_{I_{n}}\|\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq t_{I_{n}}\delta_{I_{n}}\|\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|-c_{1}t_{I_{n}}\|\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2c_{1}}t_{I_{n}}\delta_{I_{n}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}c_{1}t_{I_{n}}\|\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}.

By taking the expectation, using (
3.1
), and noting that
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{I_{n}}^{2}]=\frac{b}{p}\delta^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t_{\max}}{2c_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{I_{n}}^{2}\right]-\frac{1}{2}c_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[t_{I_{n}}\|\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{\max}b}{2c_{1}p}\delta^{2}-\frac{1}{2}c_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{I:|I|=b}t_{I}\|\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}\right]

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider Algorithm
3
in which
0<t_{I}<4\sigma/\|A_{I}\|^{2}
for all
I\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}

|I|=b

t
for all such
I
in case
b>1
. Assume (
4.1
) holds and let

r_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{t_{i}}\|\lambda_{n,i}^{\delta}-\lambda_{,i}^{\dagger}\|^{2}}&\mbox{ if }b=1,\\[12.05553pt]
\displaystyle{\frac{1}{t}\|\lambda_{n}^{\delta}-\lambda^{\dagger}\|^{2}}&\mbox{ if }b>1\end{array}\right.

for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[r_{n+1}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[r_{n}]+\frac{2b}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})-d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\right]+\frac{2t_{\max}^{1/2}b}{p}\delta\left(\mathbb{E}[r_{n}]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\,+\frac{1}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{I:|I|=b}t_{I}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}\right]

for all integers
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

We will only prove the result for
b>1
; the result for
b=1
can be proved similarly. By using the definition of
\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta}
and
t_{I_{n}}=t
we have

\displaystyle r_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\|\lambda_{n+1,I_{n}^{c}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I_{n}^{c}}^{\dagger}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{t}\|\lambda_{n+1,I_{n}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I_{n}}^{\dagger}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\|\lambda_{n,I_{n}^{c}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I_{n}^{c}}^{\dagger}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{t}\|\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I_{n}}^{\dagger}-t\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\|\lambda_{n,I_{n}^{c}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I_{n}^{c}}^{\dagger}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{t}\|\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I_{n}}^{\dagger}\|^{2}-2\langle\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I_{n}}^{\dagger}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\,+t\|\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=r_{n}-2\langle\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I_{n}}^{\dagger}\rangle+t\|\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}.

Taking the expectation and using (
3.1
), it gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[r_{n+1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{n}]-2\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),\lambda_{n,I_{n}}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I_{n}}^{\dagger}\rangle|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\quad\,+t\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla_{I_{n}}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{n}]-2\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{I:|I|=b}\langle\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),\lambda_{n,I}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I}^{\dagger}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\sum_{I:|I|=b}\langle\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),\lambda_{n,I}^{\delta}-\lambda_{I}^{\dagger}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{I:|I|=b}\sum_{i\in I}\langle\nabla_{i}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),\lambda_{n,i}^{\delta}-\lambda_{,i}^{\dagger}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[r_{n+1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{n}]+\frac{2b}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),\lambda^{\dagger}-\lambda_{n}^{\delta}\rangle\right]+\frac{t}{{p\choose b}}\sum_{I:|I|=b}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla_{I}d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\|^{2}\right].

, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the definition of

, we can obtain

\displaystyle\langle\nabla d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta}),\lambda^{\dagger}-\lambda_{n}^{\delta}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda^{\dagger})-d_{y^{\delta}}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})

\displaystyle\leq d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})-d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})+\delta\|\lambda_{n}^{\delta}-\lambda^{\dagger}\|

\displaystyle=d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})-d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})+\sqrt{t}\delta r_{n}^{1/2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[r_{n}]+\frac{2b}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})-d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})\right]+\frac{2\sqrt{t}b}{p}\delta\mathbb{E}[r_{n}^{1/2}]

Since

, we thus obtain the desired inequality.

Lemma 4.5
.

with the step-sizes chosen as in Lemma

, there holds

for all

, where
M_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}c_{1}r_{0}+\left(d_{y}(\lambda_{0})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})\right)
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})+\frac{1}{2}c_{1}r_{n+1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})+\frac{1}{2}c_{1}r_{n}\right]-\frac{bc_{1}}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})\right]

Recursively using this inequality then completes the proof.

In order to proceed further, we need an estimate on
{\mathbb{E}}[r_{n}]
. We will use the following elementary result.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
be two sequences of nonnegative numbers such that

a_{n}^{2}\leq b_{n}^{2}+c\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_{j},\quad n=0,1,\cdots,

where
c\geq 0
is a constant. If
\{b_{n}\}
is non-decreasing, then

a_{n}\leq b_{n}+cn,\quad n=0,1,\cdots.

Proof.

We show the result by induction. The result is trivial for
n=0
. Assume that the result is valid for all

0\leq n\leq k
for some
k\geq 0
. We show it is also true for
n=k+1
. If
a_{k+1}\leq\max\{a_{0},\cdots,a_{k}\}
, then

a_{k+1}\leq a_{l}
for some
0\leq l\leq k
. Thus, by the induction hypothesis and the monotonicity of
\{b_{n}\}
we have

a_{k+1}\leq a_{l}\leq b_{l}+cl\leq b_{k+1}+c(k+1).

If
a_{k+1}>\max\{a_{0},\cdots,a_{k}\}
, then

\displaystyle a_{k+1}^{2}\leq b_{k+1}^{2}+c\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}\leq b_{k+1}^{2}+c(k+1)a_{k+1}

which implies that

\displaystyle\left(a_{k+1}-\frac{1}{2}c(k+1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{k+1}^{2}-c(k+1)a_{k+1}+\frac{1}{4}c^{2}(k+1)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq b_{k+1}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}c^{2}(k+1)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(b_{k+1}+\frac{1}{2}c(k+1)\right)^{2}.

Taking square roots shows
a_{k+1}\leq b_{k+1}+c(k+1)
again.
∎

Now we are ready to show the following result which together with Lemma
4.2
implies Theorem
4.1
immediately.

.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})\right]+\frac{bc_{1}}{p}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{k}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})\right]

\displaystyle\leq M_{0}+\frac{t_{\max}b}{2c_{1}p}(n+1)\delta^{2}+\frac{b\sqrt{2t_{\max}c_{1}M_{0}}}{p}(n+1)\delta

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{k}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})\right]

\displaystyle\geq(n+1)\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})\right]-\frac{t_{\max}b}{4c_{1}p}(n+1)(n+2)\delta^{2}.

Combining this with (

\frac{2b^{1/2}(n+1)^{1/2}}{3p^{1/2}}\leq\frac{2}{c_{1}}+\frac{bc_{1}(n+1)}{18p},

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{bc_{1}(n+1)}{p}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2M_{0}+\frac{t_{\max}b}{p}\left(\frac{1}{2c_{1}}+\frac{2b^{1/2}(n+1)^{1/2}}{3p^{1/2}}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3c_{1}}{2}\right)\frac{b(n+1)}{p}\right)(n+1)\delta^{2}

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{y}(\lambda_{n+1}^{\delta})-d_{y}(\lambda^{\dagger})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2M_{0}}{1+\frac{b}{p}c_{1}(n+1)}+\frac{5t_{\max}b}{2c_{1}p}(n+1)\delta^{2}

which completes the proof.
∎

Example 5.5
.

In this final example we consider using the stochastic mirror descent method to reconstruct piecewise constant solutions. We consider again the linear system (
5.1
) with
[a,b]=[0,1]
and
k(s,t)=4e^{-(s-t)^{2}/0.0064}
and assume that the sought solution
x^{\dagger}
is piecewise constant. By dividing
[0,1]
into
p-1
subintervals of equal length and approximating integrals by the trapezoidal rule, we have a discrete ill-posed system
{\bf A}_{i}{\bf x}=y_{i}
, where
{\bf A}_{i}
is a row vector for each
i
. To reconstruct a piecewise constant solution, we use the model

\displaystyle\min\left\{{\mathcal{R}}({\bf x}):=\beta\|{\bf D}{\bf x}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf D}{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}:{\bf A}_{i}{\bf x}=y_{i},i=1,\cdots,p\right\},

(5.11)

where
{\bf D}
denotes the discrete gradient operator and
\beta
is a large positive number. If we apply the stochastic mirror descent method to solve (
5.11
) directly, we need to solve a minimization problem related to
{\mathcal{R}}
to obtain
{\bf x}_{n}^{\delta}
at each iteration step. This can make the algorithm time-consuming since those minimization problems can not be solved explicitly. To circumvent this difficulty, we introduce
{\bf z}:={\bf D}{\bf x}
and rephrase (
5.11
) as

\displaystyle\min\left\{\beta\|{\bf z}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf z}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}:{\bf B}_{i}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf x}\\
{\bf z}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{i}\\
{\bf 0}\end{pmatrix},i=1,\cdots,p\right\},

(5.12)

where
{\bf B}_{i}:=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf A}_{i}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf D}&-{\bf I}\end{pmatrix}
. Assuming, instead of
y_{i}
, we have the noisy data
y_{i}^{\delta}
. By applying
the stochastic mirror descent method with batch size
b=1
to (
5.12
) we can obtain

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{\bf x}_{n}^{\delta}\\[4.30554pt]
{\bf z}_{n}^{\delta}\end{pmatrix}=\arg\min_{{\bf x},{\bf z}}\left\{\beta\|{\bf z}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf z}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}-\langle\xi_{n}^{\delta},{\bf x}\rangle-\langle\eta_{n}^{\delta},{\bf z}\rangle\right\},

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{n+1}^{\delta}\\[4.30554pt]
\eta_{n+1}^{\delta}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{n}^{\delta}\\[4.30554pt]
\eta_{n}^{\delta}\end{pmatrix}-t_{n}^{\delta}{\bf B}_{i_{n}}^{T}\left({\bf B}_{i_{n}}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf x}_{n}^{\delta}\\[4.30554pt]
{\bf z}_{n}^{\delta}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}y_{i_{n}}^{\delta}\\[4.30554pt]
{\bf 0}\end{pmatrix}\right).

By calculating
{\bf x}_{n}^{\delta},{\bf z}_{n}^{\delta}
and noting
{\bf x}_{n}^{\delta}=\xi_{n}^{\delta}
, we therefore obtain the following iteration scheme

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf z}_{n}^{\delta}&=\mbox{sign}(\eta_{n}^{\delta})\max\{|\eta_{n}^{\delta}|-\beta,0\},\\
{\bf x}_{n+1}^{\delta}&={\bf x}_{n}^{\delta}-t_{n}^{\delta}\left({\bf A}_{i_{n}}^{T}({\bf A}_{i_{n}}{\bf x}_{n}^{\delta}-y_{i_{n}}^{\delta})+{\bf D}^{T}({\bf D}{\bf x}_{n}^{\delta}-{\bf z}_{n}^{\delta})\right),\\
\eta_{n+1}^{\delta}&=\eta_{n}^{\delta}-t_{n}^{\delta}({\bf z}_{n}^{\delta}-{\bf D}{\bf x}_{n}^{\delta})\end{split}

(5.13)

with the initial guess
\eta_{0}^{\delta}={\bf 0}
and
{\bf x}_{0}^{\delta}={\bf 0}
, where
i_{n}\in\{1,\cdots,p\}
is a randomly selected index via the uniform distribution and
t_{n}^{\delta}\geq 0
denotes the step-size.
