Abstract
. In this paper, we study Brownian-type operators, which are upper triangular 2 × 2 2\times 2 block matrix operators with entries satisfying some algebraic constraints. We establish a lifting theorem stating that any Brownian-type operator with subnormal ( 2 , 2 ) (2,2) entry lifts to a Brownian-type operator with normal ( 2 , 2 ) (2,2) entry, where lifting is understood in the sense of extending entries of the block matrices representing the operators in question. The spectral inclusion and the filling in holes theorems are obtained for such operators.

Introduction

Upper triangular
2\times 2
block matrices appear in various
parts of operator theory and functional analysis on the
occasion of investigating a variety of topics including the
Halmos similarity problem for polynomially bounded operators

[
17
,
28
]
, the hyperinvariant subspace problem

[
14
,
24
,
22
,
23
]
, the task of finding models
for the time shift operator for stochastic processes

[
2
]
, the question of characterizing invertibility of
upper triangular
2\times 2
block matrices
[
19
]
,
the task of searching for a model theory for
2
-hyponormal
operators
[
13
]
, the problem of determining a
complete set of unitary invariants for the class of
Cowen-Douglas operators realized as upper triangular block

2\times 2
matrices
[
21
]
, and many others. This
paper continues the study initiated in
[
10
]
of
Brownian-type operators, which are upper triangular
2\times 2
block matrix operators with entries satisfying some
algebraic constraints (see Definition
1.1
).

The goal of this paper is to prove a lifting theorem for
Brownian-type operators with subnormal
(2,2)
entry.
Typically, such a theorem involves finding an extension in a
better-understood class of operators. In our case, such a
natural class consists of Brownian-type operators with normal

(2,2)
entry. The main result of the present paper shows
that any Brownian-type operator with subnormal
(2,2)
entry
lifts to a Brownian-type operator with normal
(2,2)
entry
(see Theorem
1.4
), where lifting is understood in
the sense of extending entries of the block matrices
representing the operators in question. Such extensions are
called entrywise extensions (see Definition
1.3
). In
fact, Theorem
1.4
(iii) guarantees the existence of
an entrywise extension having additional features related to
the modulus of the
(1,2)
entry and the minimality of a
normal extension of the
(2,2)
entry. For such extensions,
we establish the spectral inclusion and the filling in holes
theorems, which usually are attributed to subnormal operators
(see Theorem
2.4
). This is rather surprising because
Brownian-type operators with subnormal (or even unitary)

(2,2)
entry are generally not subnormal (see
Lemma
2.1
and Example
5.1
). Let us mention
that the notion of entrywise extension is essentially
stronger than that of extension. Namely, as shown in
Example
5.2
, there is a
2
-isometry which is a
Brownian-type operator with subnormal
(2,2)
entry and which
has no entrywise extension to a Brownian unitary (see
Section
5
for the definition). However, by

[
2
, Theorem 5.80]
it has an extension to a Brownian
unitary.

Before stating the main theorem and related results,
we provide the necessary concepts. We say that a
bounded linear operator
T
on a (complex) Hilbert
space
\mathcal{H}
is a
2
-isometry
if
T^{*2}T^{2}-2T^{*}T+I=0
. By
[
29
, Lemma 1]
,
\triangle_{T}:=T^{*}T-I\geqslant 0
for any
2
-isometry
T
. A

2
-isometry
T
is called a
Brownian isometry

if
\triangle_{T}\triangle_{T^{*}}\triangle_{T}=0
, and a

quasi-Brownian isometry
if
\triangle_{T}T=\triangle_{T}^{1/2}T\triangle_{T}^{1/2}
. The notions of
a
2
-isometry and a Brownian isometry were introduced
in
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
, respectively. An
interest in studying
2
-isometries comes from the
investigation of the invariant subspaces of the
Dirichlet shift
[
29
]
and the time shift operator
of the modified Brownian motion process
[
2
]
.
The class of quasi-Brownian isometries was studied in

[
25
,
5
,
3
,
4
]
. Let us recall that
non-isometric Brownian and quasi-Brownian isometries
have an upper triangular
2\times 2
block matrix
representation with entries satisfying some algebraic
constraints (see
[
3
, Theorem 4.1]
and the
references therein). This provided the motivation in

[
10
]
to study a broader class of operators
called Brownian-type operators, which includes
quasi-Brownian isometries (in particular, Brownian
unitaries and Brownian isometries).

Given two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K},
we denote by

\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
the Banach space of all bounded linear
operators from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{K}
. The kernel, the range and the
modulus of an operator
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
are denoted by

\mathscr{N}(T)
,
{\mathscr{R}}(T)
and
|T|
, respectively. We regard

\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}):=\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
as a
C^{*}
-algebra. The identity
operator on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
I_{\mathcal{H}},
or simply by
I
if
no ambiguity arises. For a subset
\mathscr{F}
of

\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we denote by
\mathscr{F}^{\prime}
the
commutant

of
\mathscr{F}
, which is the set of operators
A\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
AB=BA
for all
B\in\mathscr{F}
. It
is a well-known fact that
\mathscr{F}^{\prime}
is a von Neumann
algebra and
\mathscr{F}^{\prime}=W^{*}(\mathscr{F})^{\prime}
, where

W^{*}(\mathscr{F})
denotes the von Neumann algebra generated
by
\mathscr{F}\cup\{I\}
(see
[
26
]
for more
details). Note that if
E\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
, then it follows
from
[
18
, Problem 56]
and the injectivity of

E|_{\overline{{\mathscr{R}}(|E|)}}\colon\overline{{\mathscr{R}}(|E|)}\to\mathcal{K}

that

\displaystyle\dim\overline{{\mathscr{R}}(|E|)}\leqslant\dim\mathcal{K}.

(1.1)

We say that
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
is
left-invertible
if
there exists an operator
L\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H})
, called a
left-inverse
of
T
, such that
LT=I_{\mathcal{H}}
. If
T
is
left-invertible, then
\mathscr{N}(T)=\{0\}
and
{\mathscr{R}}(T)
is closed.
It is easy to see that

T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
is injective
(
resp.,
left-invertible
)
if and only if
|T|
is injective

(
resp., invertible
)
.

(1.2)

Given two operators
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
S\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
, we
write
T\subseteq S
and say that
S

extends

T
, or
that
S
is an
extension
of
T
, if
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{K}

(an isometric embedding) and
Th=Sh
for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

We say that
S\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is
subnormal
if there
exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
and a normal operator
N\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
such that
S\subseteq N
; if this is the case,
the operator
N
is called a
normal extension
of
S
. A
normal extension
N\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
of
S
is said to be
minimal
if
\mathcal{K}
is the only closed subspace of
\mathcal{K}

containing
\mathcal{H}
and reducing
N
; if this is the case, we
write
N=\mathrm{mne}\,S
. Note that if
N
is a normal
extension of
S
, then
P\in\{N\}^{\prime}
if and only if
S
is
normal, where
P\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
is the orthogonal projection
of
\mathcal{K}
onto
\mathcal{H}
. Recall that (see

[
12
, Proposition II.2.4]
)

a normal extension
N\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
of a subnormal
operator
S\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is minimal if and only if
\mathcal{K}=\bigvee_{n=0}^{\infty}N^{*n}\mathcal{H}
.

(1.3)

Any subnormal operator has a minimal normal extension. If

N=\mathrm{mne}\,S
and
A\in\{S,S^{*}\}^{\prime}
, then
there exists a unique
B\in\{N\}^{\prime}\cap\{P\}^{\prime}
such that
A\subseteq B
, where
P
is as
above (see
[
7
, Corollary, p. 88]
). Such
B
is
called the
lift of

A

relative to

\{N\}^{\prime}
; we also say that
A

lifts
to

\{N\}^{\prime}
. The uniqueness of
B
follows from the
minimality of
N
and the fact that

BN^{*n}h=N^{*n}Ah
for all integers
n\geqslant 0

and all vectors
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

(1.4)

The following fact is a consequence of the Fuglede
theorem.

If
B
is the lift of
A
relative to

\{N\}^{\prime}
, then
B^{*}
is the lift of
A^{*}

relative to
\{N\}^{\prime}
.

(1.5)

We call an operator
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})

hyponormal

if
\|T^{*}f\|\leqslant\|Tf\|
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
. We say
that
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is
quasinormal
if

TT^{*}T=T^{*}TT
, or equivalently if and only if

T|T|=|T|T
(see
[
8
]
). Quasinormal operators
are subnormal and subnormal operators are hyponormal
(see
[
12
, Propositions II.1.7 and II.4.2]
).
Summarizing, we have the following inclusions:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\mathscr{U}\subseteq\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{Q},\\
\mathscr{U}\subseteq\mathscr{N}\subseteq\mathscr{Q}\subseteq\mathscr{S}\subseteq\mathscr{H},\end{gathered}

where
\mathscr{U}
,
\mathscr{I}
,
\mathscr{N}
,

\mathscr{Q}
,
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{H}
stand for the
classes of unitary, isometric, normal, quasinormal, subnormal
and hyponormal operators, respectively. We refer the reader
to
[
12
]
for more information on the above concepts.

Now we can recall the definition of the Brownian-type
operator.

Definition 1.1

(cf.
[
10
, Definition 1.1]
)
.

We say that
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a Brownian-type
operator if it has the block matrix form

\displaystyle T=\begin{bmatrix}V&E\\
0&X\end{bmatrix}

with respect to an orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
, where the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}

are nonzero and the operators
V\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1}),

E\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
X\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})

satisfy the following conditions:

V
is an isometry, i.e.,
V^{*}V=I,

(1.8)

\displaystyle V^{*}E=0,

(1.9)

\displaystyle XE^{*}E=E^{*}EX.

(1.10)

If
X
is unitary
(
resp., isometric, normal,
quasinormal, subnormal, hyponormal, etc.
)
, then
T

is called a Brownian-type operator of class

\mathscr{U}

(
resp.,
\mathscr{I}
,
\mathscr{N},

\mathscr{Q},

\mathscr{S},

\mathscr{H},
etc.
)
.
If
\mathscr{X}
is one of the above classes, we say
simply that
T
is an operator of class
\mathscr{X}

and write
T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&X\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{X}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
.

In connection with Definition
1.1
, it is worth
making a few comments.

Remark 1.2
.

First, note that by the square root theorem

[
33
, Theorem 2.4.4]
, the condition
(
1.10
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle X|E|=|E|X.

(1.11)

This yields the following.

If
T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&X\end{smallmatrix}\big]
is a Brownian-type
operator, then
\overline{{\mathscr{R}}(|E|)}
reduces
X
.

(1.12)

Secondly, note that by (

), we have the
following chains of inclusions:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\mathscr{U}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq\mathscr{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq\mathscr{Q}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}},\\
\mathscr{U}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq\mathscr{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq\mathscr{Q}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq\mathscr{S}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}.\end{gathered}

Thirdly, one can deduce from
[
2
, Proposition 5.37 and
Theorem 5.48]
(resp.,
[
25
, Proposition 5.1]
)
that a non-isometric operator
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a Brownian
isometry (resp., a quasi-Brownian isometry) if and only if

T
is of class
\mathscr{U}
(resp.,
\mathscr{I}
); to
avoid the injectivity of

, see

[
3
, Theorem 4.1]
. This means that Brownian

\diamondsuit

Next, we define the notion of entrywise extension, which
plays a key role in this paper.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\big[\begin{smallmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2})
and

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}\tilde{A}_{11}&\tilde{A}_{12}\\
\tilde{A}_{21}&\tilde{A}_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{2})
. We say that

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}\tilde{A}_{11}&\tilde{A}_{12}\\
\tilde{A}_{21}&\tilde{A}_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\big]
is an
entrywise extension of
\big[\begin{smallmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\big]

if

\mathcal{H}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{j}
and

A_{ij}h_{j}=\tilde{A}_{ij}h_{j}
for
h_{j}\in\mathcal{H}_{j}
and
i,j=1,2
.

(1.13)

If this is the case, then we write

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\preceq\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{A}_{11}&\tilde{A}_{12}\\
\tilde{A}_{21}&\tilde{A}_{22}\end{bmatrix}.

(1.14)

Obviously, (
1.14
) implies that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\subseteq\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{A}_{11}&\tilde{A}_{12}\\
\tilde{A}_{21}&\tilde{A}_{22}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{(extension of block matrix
operators).}

The converse implication holds provided
\mathcal{H}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{j}
for
j=1,2
. Note that if the orthogonal
decompositions
\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
\mathcal{K}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{2}

are not related to each other as in (
1.13
), then
there may exist extensions that are not entrywise (see
Example
5.2
).

(ii)

(iii)

5.
Examples

In this section, we give four examples illustrating
the subject of the paper. The first shows that
operators of class
\mathscr{S}
may not be subnormal,
the second deals with extensions that are not
entrywise, the third discusses the relationship
between the defect spaces of an operator of class

\mathscr{S}
and its taut entrywise extension, and
finally the fourth shows that powers of operators of
class
\mathscr{S}
may not be of class
\mathscr{S}
.

We begin by providing examples of operators of class

\mathscr{S}
(or even of class
\mathscr{U}
), which
do not have extensions to normal operators, but,
according to Theorem
1.4
, have taut entrywise
extensions to operators of class
\mathscr{N}
(cf. Lemma
2.1
).

Example 5.1
.

Suppose that
V\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
,
U\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
and

Q\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
are isometries such that
{\mathscr{R}}(V)\perp{\mathscr{R}}(U)
. Let
\alpha
be a nonzero complex number.
Then
T_{\alpha}=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&\alpha U\\
0&Q\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
. Since
\|Q\|^{2}+|\alpha|^{2}>1
, it follows from

[
10
, Corollary 5.3]
that
T_{\alpha}
is not
subnormal. In particular, if
Q
is unitary, then

T_{\alpha}
is a Brownian isometry, and if
Q
is unitary,

|\alpha|=1
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}={\mathscr{R}}(V)\oplus{\mathscr{R}}(U)
, then

T_{\alpha}
is a Brownian unitary (see the next paragraph
for definition).

\diamondsuit

It is well-known that any
2
-isometry has an extension to a
Brownian unitary (see
[
2
, Theorem 5.80]
). However,
as shown in Example
5.2
below, there are

2
-isometric Brownian-type operators of class
\mathscr{Q}

which have no entrywise extensions to Brownian unitaries.
Hence, in general, extensions may not be entrywise. Before
providing such an example, we recall that the quantity

\sqrt{\|A^{*}A-I\|}
is called the
covariance
of an
operator
A\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Given
\sigma\in(0,\infty)
, we
say that an operator
T
is a
Brownian unitary of
covariance

\sigma
(see
[
2
, Proposition 5.12]
) if

T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}X&\sigma Y\\
0&Z\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2})
, where

X\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
Y\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
are
isometries such that
{\mathscr{R}}(X)=\mathscr{N}(Y^{*})
and
Z\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
is unitary.

(5.1)

By a
Brownian unitary of covariance

0
, we mean any
unitary operator. In both cases
\sigma^{2}=\|T^{*}T-I\|
.
Clearly, each Brownian unitary of covariance
\sigma>0
is
of class
\mathscr{U}
.

Example 5.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
are separable
infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces. Let
U_{1},U_{2}\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{M})
be isometries such that
U_{1}
is not unitary,

D\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{L})
be a nonzero operator and
A\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{N})
be a selfadjoint operator such that
0\leqslant A\leqslant I
. Then
I-A^{2}
is a positive contraction. Define the
Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, and the operators
E\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
Q\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\oplus(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}),\quad i=1,2,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=D\oplus(U_{1}\otimes\sqrt{I-A^{2}}),

(5.2)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=I_{\mathcal{L}}\oplus(U_{2}\otimes A).

(5.3)

Note that if
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{K}
are Hilbert spaces, and

\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{1}
are their closed subspaces,
respectively, then

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{K})\ominus(\mathcal{F}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{1})=[(\mathcal{F}\ominus\mathcal{F}_{1})\otimes\mathcal{K}]\oplus[\mathcal{F}_{1}\otimes(\mathcal{K}\ominus\mathcal{K}_{1})].

(5.4)

Since
I+A
is injective and consequently

\mathscr{N}(I-A^{2})=\mathscr{N}(I-A)
, it follows from (
5.4
)
applied to
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{N}
,
\mathcal{F}_{1}={\mathscr{R}}(U_{1})

and
\mathcal{K}_{1}=\overline{{\mathscr{R}}(\sqrt{I-A^{2}})}
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}\ominus\overline{{\mathscr{R}}(E)}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{L}\ominus\overline{{\mathscr{R}}(D)}]\oplus[\mathscr{N}(U_{1}^{*})\otimes\mathcal{N}]\oplus[{\mathscr{R}}(U_{1})\otimes\mathscr{N}(\sqrt{I-A^{2}})]

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{L}\ominus\overline{{\mathscr{R}}(D)}]\oplus[\mathscr{N}(U_{1}^{*})\otimes\mathcal{N}]\oplus[{\mathscr{R}}(U_{1})\otimes\mathscr{N}(I-A)].

Since
U_{1}
is not unitary,
\dim\mathscr{N}(U_{1}^{*})\geqslant 1
, so
\dim(\mathscr{N}(U_{1}^{*})\otimes\mathcal{N})=\aleph_{0}

\dim\mathcal{H}_{1}=\aleph_{0}

V\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
such that
{\mathscr{R}}(V)\perp{\mathscr{R}}(E)
.
It is a matter of routine to verify that
T:=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&Q\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{Q}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
and

\displaystyle(|Q|^{2}-I)(|Q|^{2}+|E|^{2}-I)=0.

Using
[
10

, we conclude that

T
is a
2
-isometry of covariance
\|D\|>0
(in
particular,
T
is not an isometry). It follows from

[
2
, Theorem 5.80]
that
T
has an extension
to a Brownian unitary of covariance
\|D\|
. We show
that
T
has no entrywise extension to a Brownian
unitary of arbitrary covariance
\sigma
. Since
T
is
not an isometry, it is sufficient to consider the case
when
\sigma>0
. Suppose, on the contrary, that
T

has an entrywise extension to a Brownian unitary of
covariance
\sigma>0
, that is,
T\preceq\big[\begin{smallmatrix}X&\sigma Y\\

with

,

\diamondsuit

Example 5.3
.

For the sake of simplicity, we will assume that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and

\mathcal{H}_{2}
are separable infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{H}_{1}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{2}=\aleph_{0}.

(5.5)

Under this assumption, for a given
\mathfrak{p}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
, it is possible
to construct all Brownian-type operators

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&S\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
with isometric

E
such that

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{H}_{1}\ominus\big({\mathscr{R}}(V)\oplus{\mathscr{R}}(E)\big)=\mathfrak{p}.

(5.6)

Namely, take a subnormal operator
S\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})

and two isometries
V\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
E\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
such that
{\mathscr{R}}(V)\perp{\mathscr{R}}(E)
and
(
5.6
) holds (this is possible due to
(
5.5
)). Then clearly

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&S\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
. By (
1.8
) ad
(
1.9

\mathscr{S}
with
isometric
E
can be obtained in this way.

Let
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
. In view of the above discussion
and (
5.5
),
there is
T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&S\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
with isometric

,

S
is not normal and

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{H}_{1}\ominus\big({\mathscr{R}}(V)\oplus{\mathscr{R}}(E)\big)=\mathfrak{m}.

(5.7)

We will show that there exists an entrywise extension

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}\tilde{V}&\tilde{E}\\
0&N\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{N}_{{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}}
of

T
with isometric
\tilde{E}
such that

N=\mathrm{mne}\,S
for which

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{K}_{1}\ominus({\mathscr{R}}(\tilde{V})\oplus{\mathscr{R}}(\tilde{E}))=\mathfrak{n}.

(5.8)

For, let
N\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
be a minimal normal
extension of
S
. Consider two cases.

Case 1.

\mathfrak{n}\leqslant\mathfrak{m}
.

By Theorem
1.4
applied to the space
\mathcal{D}
of

\mathfrak{n}
,

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}\tilde{V}&\tilde{E}\\

is an isometry (in particular,

), the equality (

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{K}_{1}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{1}\overset{\eqref{sumpi}}{=}\aleph_{0}.

(5.9)

Case 2.

By a
unilateral weighted shift with weights

\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
, we mean a uniquely
determined operator
W\in\boldsymbol{B}(\ell^{2})
such that

\displaystyle We_{i}=\lambda_{i}e_{i+1},\quad i\geqslant 0,

where
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of complex
numbers and
\{e_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
denotes the standard
orthonormal basis of
\ell^{2}
. Before stating the fourth
example, we need the following characterization of the
condition (ii) of Proposition
3.8
in the case of
unilateral weighted shifts.

Proposition 5.4
.

If
W\in\boldsymbol{B}(\ell^{2})
is an injective hyponormal
unilateral weighted shift with weights

\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
and
n\geqslant 2
, then the
following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the operators
W^{n}
and

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}W^{*j}W^{j}
commute,

(ii)

the sequence

\{|\lambda_{i}|\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
is constant.

Proof.

Since
W^{*j}W^{j}e_{k}=\Big(\prod_{l=k}^{k+j-1}|\lambda_{l}|^{2}\Big)e_{k}
for all
k\geqslant 0
and
j\geqslant 1
,
and
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
for all
i\geqslant 0
, we see that

W^{n}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}W^{*j}W^{j}
commute if and
only if

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\Big(\prod_{l=k}^{k+j-1}|\lambda_{l}|^{2}\Big)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\Big(\prod_{l=k+n}^{k+n+j-1}|\lambda_{l}|^{2}\Big),\quad k\geqslant 0.

(5.10)

Suppose that (
5.10
) holds. Since
W
is
hyponormal, we see that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{l}|\leqslant|\lambda_{l+1}|,\quad l\geqslant 0.

(5.11)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}\leqslant\gamma_{k+1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\gamma_{k+n}\overset{\eqref{hrypa}}{=}\gamma_{k},\quad k\geqslant 0.

Thus

\displaystyle 0=\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\bigg(\prod_{l=k+1}^{k+j}|\lambda_{l}|^{2}-\prod_{l=k}^{k+j-1}|\lambda_{l}|^{2}\bigg),\quad k\geqslant 0.

By (
5.11
), the terms of the above sum are
nonnegative, so

\displaystyle\prod_{l=k}^{k+j-1}|\lambda_{l}|^{2}=\prod_{l=k+1}^{k+j}|\lambda_{l}|^{2},\quad k\geqslant 0,\,j=1,\ldots,n-1.

Substituting
j=1
, we see that

|\lambda_{k}|=|\lambda_{k+1}|
for all
k\geqslant 0
, so the
implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is proved. The
converse implication is straightforward.
∎

Now we are in a position to give an example of an operator of
class
\mathscr{S}
whose
n
th power is not of class

\mathscr{S}
for every integer
n\geqslant 2
.

Example 5.5
.

Let
W\in\boldsymbol{B}(\ell^{2})
be a subnormal unilateral weighted
shift with nonzero weights
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
.
Denote by
\mu
the Berger measure of
W
, that is,
\mu
is
a unique Borel probability measure on the closed half-line

[0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\|W^{k}e_{0}\|^{2}=\int_{[0,\infty)}x^{k}d\mu(x),\quad k\geqslant 0.

It is easy to see that

the sequence
\{|\lambda_{n}|\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is
constant if and only if the Berger measure
\mu
of

W
is supported in a singleton
\{\theta\}
with

\theta\in(0,\infty)
.

(5.12)

It is also well known that the sequence

\{|\lambda_{n}|\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is constant if and
only if
W
is unitarily equivalent to the positive
multiple of the (isometric) unilateral shift on

\ell^{2}
(this is true for arbitrary injective
unilateral weighted shifts, see
[
32
]
). Set

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=\ell^{2}
and take two isometries
V,E\in\boldsymbol{B}(\ell^{2})
such that
{\mathscr{R}}(V)\perp{\mathscr{R}}(E)
. Then
clearly
T:=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&W\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
. Since subnormal
operators are hyponormal, we deduce from (
5.12
) and
Propositions
3.8
and
5.4
that for every
integer
n\geqslant 3
, the following conditions are equivalent:

\bullet

T^{n}\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
,

\bullet

T^{2}\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
,

\bullet

the Berger measure of
W
is
supported in a singleton
\{\theta\}
with
\theta>0
.

In particular, if the Berger measure of
W
is not supported
in a singleton subset of
(0,\infty)
, then by
Remark
3.13
,
|E_{n}|\not\subseteq|\tilde{E}_{n}|
for
all integers
n\geqslant 2
.

\diamondsuit
