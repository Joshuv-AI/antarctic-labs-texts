Design of zero-determinant strategies and its application to networked repeated games
Daizhan CHENG, Changxi LI

Abstract
Using semi-tensor product (STP) of matrices, the profile evolutionary equation (PEE) for repeated finite games is obtained. By virtue of PEE, the zero-determinant (ZD) strategies are developed for general finite games. A formula is then obtained to design ZD strategies for general finite games with multi-player and asymmetric strategies. A necessary and sufficient condition is obtained to ensure the availability of the designed ZD strategies. It follows that player i i is able to unilaterally design k i − 1 k_{i}-1 (one less than the number of her strategies) dominating linear relations about the expected payoffs of all players. Finally, the fictitious opponent player is proposed for networked repeated games (NRGs). A technique is proposed to simplify the model by reducing the number of frontier strategies.

Introduction

In 2012, the zero-determinant (ZD) strategy was firstly proposed by Press and Dyson
[
25
]
, which shows that in an iterated prisoner’s dilemma there exist strategies that dominate any evolutionary opponent. Since then it has attracted considerable attention from game theoretic community as well as computer, information, systems and control communities.
[
15
]
calls it “an underway revolution in game theory”, because it reveals that in a repeated game, a player can unilaterally control her opponent’s payoff.

A significant development in the following researches is the so-called “Akin’s Lemma”
[
2
]
, which is a generalization of Press-Dyson’s pioneering work without using determinant form.
Akin’s original work is about two player two strategy game (prisoner’s dilemma). Then various extensions have been done. In
[
20
]
the ZD strategies of two player two strategy discounted games were discussed.
[
23
]
considers two player continuous strategy discounted games. A surprising fact is: as a player adopts ZD strategy, her actions restricting to two discrete levels of cooperation are enough to enforce a linear relationship between payoffs of two players even the opponent has infinitely many donation levels to choose. Multiplayer ZD strategies in games with two actions
have been discussed both for undiscounted payoffs
[
19
,
24
]
and for
discounted payoffs
[
13
]
. The most general case with multi-player and arbitrary number of strategies is also investigated by
[
32
,
31
]
.

Meanwhile, the characteristics of ZD strategies have also be investigated widely. Particularly, the stability of ZD strategy was analyzed in
[
28
]
; the robustness of ZD strategies has been investigated in
[
4
]
; the ZD strategies of noisy repeated games were investigated by
[
16
]
; the influence of misperception on ZD strategies was discussed in
[
12
]
, and the evolutionary stability of ZD strategies has also been investigated widely
[
27
,
1
,
18
]
.
ZD strategy technique has also been used for some particular kinds of games, such as application to public goods games
[
24
]
, mining pole games
[
3
]
, snowdrift game
[
33
]
, etc.

The early works concern more about the ZD strategy design
[
25
,
19
,
17
]
. Most later works focus on general properties of linear relation for average payoffs of players. For instance,
[
32
]
proved the existence of the solution of a set of linear relations of average payoffs enforced by ZD strategies and the independence of the relations.
[
31
]
is also concentrated on the existence theorem.

When the applications of ZD strategies to real game problems are concerned, the design formulas or numerical algorithms are necessary.
[
29
]
provides a method to design ZD strategies for
2\times 2
asymmetric games. When asymmetric games are considered, the shortage of similar works in previous studies has been claimed in
[
29
]
.

Another promising development of ZD strategy is its application to networked evolutionary games. For instance,
[
28
]
considers the extortion strategy under myopic best response arrangement over networks. Several kinds of evolutions of ZD strategy on networked evolutionary games are revealed by
[
26
,
34
]
.
[
30
]
considers the cooperative mining ZD strategy in block-chain networks, etc.

Recently, a new matrix product, called the semi-tensor product (STP) of matrices, was proposed
[
5
,
6
]
, and it has been applied to solve some problems in game theory, including the modeling and analysis of networked repeated games (NRGs)
[
8
]
, providing a formula to verify whether a finite game is potential
[
7
]
, investigating the vector space structure of finite games and its orthogonal decompositions
[
10
,
14
]
, application to traffic congestion games
[
35
]
and diffusion games
[
22
]
, just to mention a few. Readers who are interested in the STP approach to finite games are referred to a survey paper
[
11
]
.

Using STP, this paper presents a profile evolutionary equation (PEE) for general finite repeated games, which is essentially the same as the Markov matrix for the memory-one game in
[
25
]
. Then a detailed design technique and rigorous proofs are presented for this general case, which are generalizations of those proposed firstly by
[
25
]
. A necessary and sufficient condition is obtained for the availability of ZD strategies. As a by product, we also prove that if a player has
k_{i}
strategies she can provide unilaterally
k_{i}-1
linear payoff relations using ZD strategies.

Finally, the ZD strategies for networked repeated games (NRGs) are investigated. By proposing and using fictitious opponent player (FOP) a networked repeated game can be transferred to a two player game, where a player, say, player
i
, plays with the FOP, who represents the whole network except player
i
. The ZD strategies for player
i
are designed for
i
vs FOP.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: A brief survey on STP is given in Section 2. Then it is used to develop profile evolutionary equation (PEE) of finite repeated games. Finally, some properties of transition matrix of PPE are investigated, which are important for designing ZD strategies. Section 3 deduces a general formula for designing ZD strategies. A necessary and sufficient condition for the designed ZD strategies to be available is presented. Thereafter, some numerical examples are discussed to illustrate the design procedure. The FOP is proposed in Section 4 for NRGs. Using FOP, the technique of ZD strategies becomes applicable to NRGs.
Section 5 is a brief conclusion.

2
Modeling of Finite Repeated Games

2.1
A Brief Survey on STP

Definition 2.1
.

[
5
,
6
]

Let
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
,
N\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p\times q}
, and

t:=\lcm(n,p)
be the least common multiple of
n
and

.
Then the STP of
M
and
N
is defined as

\displaystyle M\ltimes N:=\left(M\otimes I_{t/n}\right)\left(N\otimes I_{t/p}\right)\in{\mathcal{M}}_{(mt/n)\times(qt/p)},

(1)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product.

Remark 2.2
.

(i)

STP is a generalization of conventional matrix product. That is, if
n=p
, then
M\ltimes N=MN
. It is not necessary (and almost impossible) to distinct STP from conventional matrix product, because in a computing process, the product might shift from one to the other because of the changes of dimensions. Hence in most cases the symbol
\ltimes
is omitted.

(ii)

As a generalization, STP keeps all major properties of conventional matrix product available, including associativity, distributivity, etc. All the properties of matrix product used in this paper are the same for both conventional matrix product and STP.

(iii)

Since conventional matrix product can be considered as a special case of STP, all the matrix products used in this paper without product symbol are assumed to be STP.

Next, we consider how to express a finite-valued mapping (or logical mapping) into a matrix form using STP.

Let
f:{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\rightarrow{\mathcal{D}}_{n}

. In this way,

can be regarded as a mapping

. In the sequel

Let

\displaystyle f(x)=M_{f}x.

(2)

As a corollary, Proposition

2.2
PEE of Finite Games

Definition 2.6
.

Consider a finite game
G=(N,S,C)
, where

(i)

N=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
is the set of players.

(ii)

S=\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}
is the profile, where
S_{i}=\{1,2,\cdots,k_{i}\},~i=1,2,\cdots,n,

is the strategies (or actions) of player
i
.

(iii)

C=(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n})
, where
c_{i}:S\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

is the payoff (or utility, cost) function of player
i
,
i=1,2,\cdots,n
.

The set of such finite games is denoted by
{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{n}]}
.
A matrix formulation of the repeated game
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{n}]}
is described as follows
[
8
]
:

(i)

Identifying
j\in S_{i}
with
\delta_{k_{i}}^{j}\in\Delta_{k_{i}}
, then
S_{i}\sim\Delta_{k_{i}}
.

(ii)

Setting
\kappa=\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
, then
S\sim\Delta_{\kappa}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{k_{i}}
.

(iii)

Let
x_{i}\in\Delta_{k_{i}}
be the vector form of a strategy for player
i
. Then
x=\ltimes_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\in\Delta_{\kappa}
is a profile.

(iv)

For each player’s payoff function
c_{i}
, there exists a unique row vector
V^{c}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa}
such that

\displaystyle c_{i}(x)=V^{c}_{i}x,\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n.

(7)

Now consider a repeated game
G^{r}
of
G
, which stands for (infinitely) repeated
G
. Then each player can determine her action at
t+1
using historic knowledge. It was proved in
[
25
]
(see also
[
15
]
) that:“ the shortest memory player sets the rule of the game, which means the long-memory strategies have no advantages over the memory-one strategies”. Based on this observation, the strategy updating rule is assumed Markov-like. That is, the strategy of player
i
at time
t+1
depends on the profile at
t
only. Then we have
[
8
]

\displaystyle x_{i}(t+1)=L_{i}x(t),\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n.

(8)

Two types of strategies are commonly used:

•

Pure strategy:

L_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k_{i}\times\kappa},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n.

•

Mixed Strategy:

L_{i}\in\Upsilon_{k_{i}\times\kappa},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n.

Multiplying (by STP) all equations in (
8
) together yields

\displaystyle x(t+1)=Lx(t),

(9)

where

L=L_{1}*L_{2}*\cdots*L_{n}.

In pure strategy case
L\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa\times\kappa}
and in mixed strategy case
L\in\Upsilon_{\kappa\times\kappa}
.
In mixed strategy case

can be considered as a distribution of profiles at time
t
. If we take into consideration that
\delta_{\kappa}^{i}
is used to express the
i
-th profile, then
x(t)
can also be considered as the expected value of profile at time
t
.

In this paper we concern only mixed strategy case. Now what a player can manipulate is his own strategy updating rule. That is, player
i
can only choose his
L_{i}
.

We arrange profiles in alphabetic order as

\begin{array}[]{ccl}S&=&\{(s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{n})\;|\;s_{i}\in S_{i},~i=1,2,\cdots,n\}\\
&=&\{(1,1,\cdots,1),(1,1,\cdots,2),\cdots,(k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{n})\}\\
&:=&\{s^{1},s^{2},\cdots,s^{\kappa}\}.\end{array}

Denote the probability of player
i
choosing strategy
j
at time
t+1
under the situation that the profile at time
t
is
s^{r}
as

\displaystyle p^{r}_{i,j}=Prob(x_{i}(t+1)=j\;|\;x(t)=s^{r}).

(10)

Then we have the strategy evolutionary equation (SEE) of player
i
as

\displaystyle x_{i}(t+1)=L_{i}x(t),

(11)

where

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}L_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}p^{1}_{i,1}&p^{2}_{i,1}&\cdots&p^{\kappa}_{i,1}\\
p^{1}_{i,2}&p^{2}_{i,2}&\cdots&p^{\kappa}_{i,2}\\
\vdots&~&~&~\\
p^{1}_{i,k_{i}}&p^{2}_{i,k_{i}}&\cdots&p^{\kappa}_{i,k_{i}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\Upsilon_{k_{i}\times\kappa},~i=1,2,\cdots,n.\end{array}

According to Corollary
2.5
, we have PEE as

\displaystyle x(t+1)=Lx(t),

(13)

where the transition matrix

\displaystyle L=L_{1}*L_{2}*\cdots*L_{n}.

(14)

We give a simple example to calculate

.

Example 2.7
.

Consider the repeated prisoners’ dilemma. Let

be the probability of player
i

under the condition

. Then a straightforward computation shows that

\begin{array}[]{l}\begin{cases}x_{1}(t+1)=L_{1}x(t),\\

, define an indicative vector
\xi_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa}
as follows

\displaystyle\xi_{i,j}=\ltimes_{\tau=1}^{n}\gamma_{\tau},

(25)

where

\gamma_{\tau}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{1}_{k_{\tau}},\tau\neq i,\\
\delta_{k_{i}}^{j},~\tau=i.\\
\end{cases}

\xi_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa}
is called strategy extraction vector, which has the following property.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider the FRG
G^{r}
. Strategy extraction vector
\xi_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa}
has the following property

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}\xi_{i,j}^{\top}L&=&\sum_{a\in\Phi_{i,j}}\Row_{a}(L)\\
&=&[p^{1}_{i,j},p^{2}_{i,j},\cdots,p^{\kappa}_{i,j}],~\forall i\in N,\forall j\in A_{i}.\end{array}

where
\Phi_{i,j}=\{a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in A~|~a_{i}=j\}\subseteq A
.

Proof:
According to the definition of
\xi_{i,j}
and
L
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}\xi_{i,j}^{\top}L&=&\xi_{i,j}^{\top}[\Col_{1}(L),\Col_{2}(L),\cdots,\Col_{k}(L)]\\
&=&[\xi_{i,j}^{\top}\Col_{1}(L),\xi_{i,j}^{\top}\Col_{2}(L),\cdots,\xi_{i,j}^{\top}\Col_{k}(L)],\end{array}

where for each column

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}\xi_{i,j}^{\top}\Col_{r}(L)&=&(\ltimes_{\tau=1}^{n}\gamma_{\tau}^{\top})(\ltimes_{s=1}^{n}\Col_{r}(L_{s}))\\
&=&(\otimes_{\tau=1}^{n}\gamma_{\tau}^{\top})(\otimes_{s=1}^{n}\Col_{r}(L_{s}))\\
&=&\otimes_{s=1}^{n}(\gamma_{s}^{\top}\Col_{r}(L_{s}))\\
&=&p^{r}_{i,j}.\end{array}

The second equality comes from STP’s property. The third equality comes from the property of Kronecker product.

\Box

Remark 3.2
.

(i) The strategy extraction vector
\xi_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa}
is called “Repeat” strategy in the existing works
[

]
,
[
32
]
.
(ii) The purpose of equation (

) is to pick out the set of rows from matrix
L
, which involve
p^{r}_{i,j}
. The row labels of such set are denoted by
\Phi_{i,j}
. Then
\xi_{i,j}^{\top}L
is the summation of the rows in
L
, which are labeled by
\Phi_{i,j}
. For each pair
(i,j)
,
\xi_{i,j}^{\top}L
realizes an elementary (equivalent) transformation for
L
, which results in a row of
L
which contains
p_{i,j}
only, i.e., this new row does not involve
p^{d}_{s,r}
,
(s,r)\neq(i,j)
.

If
L
is primitive, then it has a stationary distribution
\mu\in\Upsilon^{\kappa}
satisfying

\displaystyle L\mu=\mu\Leftrightarrow(L-I)\mu=0.

(28)

Multiplying
\xi_{i,j}
to both sides of (

) yields that

\displaystyle[\xi_{i,j}^{\top}L-\xi_{i,j}^{\top}]\mu=0.

(29)

Let
T_{i}=[\xi_{i}^{\top}L-\xi_{i}^{\top}],
where
\xi_{i}=[\xi_{i,1},\xi_{i,2},\cdots,\xi_{i,k_{i}}].

According to
[

]
, the ZD strategy of player

belong to the interaction of two subspaces.

Definition 3.3
.

[
32
]

The ZD strategy
L_{i}
of player

exists if and only if

\displaystyle\Span(V)\cap\Span(T_{i}^{\top})\neq\{{\bf 0}_{\kappa}\},

(30)

where
V=[{\bf 1}_{\kappa},(V_{1}^{c})^{\top},(V_{2}^{c})^{\top},\cdots,(V_{n}^{c})^{\top}].

Remark 3.4
.

Definition
3.3
can be used to detect whether a given strategy
L_{i}
is a ZD strategy or not. However, it is difficult to design a zero-determinant strategy for a given game.

In the following, we only consider how to derive player
i
’s zero-determinant strategy
p_{i,j}
associated with action
j
using
\xi_{i,j},
where
p_{i,j}=[p^{1}_{i,j},p^{2}_{i,j},\cdots,p^{\kappa}_{i,j}].

A general design formula is presented in the following:

Proposition 3.5
.

Consider a repeated game
G^{r}
, where
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{n}]}
. Assume player
i
is aimed at a set of linear relations on the expected payoffs as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\ell_{i,j}(Ec_{1},Ec_{2},\cdots,Ec_{n},1)=0,\quad 1\leq i\leq n;j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i}-1,\end{array}

where
\ell_{i,j}
is a linear function and
Ec_{i}
is the expected payoff of player
i
. Then her ZD strategies can be designed as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}p_{i,j}&=&(p^{1}_{i,j},p^{2}_{i,j},\cdots,p^{\kappa}_{i,j})\\
&=&\mu_{i,j}\ell_{i,j}\left(V^{c}_{1},V^{c}_{2},\cdots,V^{c}_{n},{\bf 1}_{\kappa}^{T}\right)+\xi_{i,j},\quad j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i}-1,\end{array}

where
\mu_{i,j}\neq 0
are adjustable parameters.

Proof
:
If
p_{i,j}
satisfies (
3.5
), then we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}p_{i,j}-\xi_{i,j}^{\top}&=&\mu_{i,j}\ell_{i,j}\left(V^{c}_{1},V^{c}_{2},\cdots,V^{c}_{n},{\bf 1}_{\kappa}^{T}\right)\\
&=&\mu_{i,j}\ell_{i,j}\left(Ec_{1},Ec_{2},\cdots,Ec_{n}\right)+c\\
&=&0,\end{array}

where
c
is a constant. Equation (
3.1
) implies that

\ell_{i,j}\left(Ec_{1},Ec_{2},\cdots,Ec_{n}\right)=0,~1\leq i\leq n;j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i}-1.

\Box

Remark 3.6
.

Equation (

) is a fundamental formula, which provides a convenient way to design ZD strategies for our preassigned purposes. One may concern the time complexity of the proposed formula.
We point that the complexity is related with the number of players n, the number of strategies for each
player
k_{i}
, which will be used for the construction of vector
\xi_{i,j}

ZD strategies, fictitious opponent player method is proposed in Section 4.

Definition 3.7
.

A set of ZD strategies is permissible, if the following two conditions are satisfied.

(i)

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{i,j}\leq 1,\quad j=1,2,\cdots,k_{i}-1.

(ii)

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{k_{i}-1}p_{i,j}\leq 1.

.

(i)

It is obvious that permissibility is a fundamental requirement. Non-permissible strategies are meaningless.

(ii)

It is clear that player

can unilaterally design at most

linear relations. Because when

,

are all determined,

(iii)

Indeed, player

can design

linear relations as she wish. This is an advantage of the formula (

Of course, player

(v)

The ZD design formula (

players.

This section considers how to design ZD strategies for a player,

4.1
Fictitious Opponent Player

Definition 4.1
.

[
8
]
An NRG is a triple
((N,E),G,\Pi)
, where
(N,E)
is a network graph, where
N
is the set of players;
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[2;k,k]}
is a symmetric game with two players, called the fundamental network game;
\Pi
is the strategy updating rule, which describes how each player to update his strategies using his neighborhood information.

Remark 4.2
.

(i)

G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[2;k,k]}
is symmetric, if
S_{1}=S_{2}:=S_{0}
and for any
x,y\in S_{0}

c_{1}(x,y)=c_{2}(y,x).

(ii)

If
(i,j)\in E
, then players
i
and
j
will play game
G
repeatedly. In this paper only the fixed graph is considered. Since
G
is symmetric, then the order of two players does not affect the result.

(iii)

Such an NRG is denoted by
G^{nr}=((N,E),G,\Pi)
.

Let player
i\in N
, and
\deg(i)=d
. Then she may consider
N\backslash\{i\}
as one player, called the FOP of
i
, denoted by
p_{-i}
. Assume
|S_{0}|=k
, the neighbors’ strategies can be considered as the strategies of
p_{-i}
. That is,
p_{-i}
has totally
k^{d}
strategies.

In fact, we need not to distinct different neighbors, hence if
S_{0}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{k}\}
, then the set of strategies of
p_{-i}
, denoted by
S_{-i}
, is

\displaystyle S_{-i}=\{\underbrace{s_{1}s_{1}\cdots s_{1}}_{d},\underbrace{s_{1}s_{1}\cdots s_{2}}_{d},\cdots,\underbrace{s_{k}s_{k}\cdots s_{k}}_{d}\}.

(73)

Each
s_{*}\in S_{-i}
can be expressed as

s_{*}=(\underbrace{s_{1}s_{1}\cdots s_{1}}_{d_{1}},\underbrace{s_{2}s_{2}\cdots s_{2}}_{d_{2}},\cdots,\underbrace{s_{k}s_{k}\cdots s_{k}}_{d_{k}}),

where
d_{i}\geq 0
and
d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{k}=d
.
Hence, we can also express
s_{*}
by
(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{k})
, which means
s_{j}
has been used by
d_{j}
neighbors,
1\leq j\leq k
. Using this notation, we have

\displaystyle S_{-i}=\{(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{k})\;|\;d_{j}\geq 0,\;\forall j;\;\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}=d\}.

(74)

It is easy to verify that define the strategies of
p_{-i}
in this way, by ignoring the order of neighbors, the total number of strategies is reduced from
k^{d}
to

|S_{-i}|=\frac{(k+d-1)!}{(k-1)!d!}.

Hence this treatment reduces the computational complexity.

From the point of view of player
i
, the NRG is equivalent to a game between she and
p_{-i}
, who has the set of strategies
S_{-i}
defined by (
74
).
Let
s_{*}=(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{k})\in S_{-i}
. Then the payoff functions for
c_{i}
and player
p_{-i}
, denoted by
c_{-i}
, are

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}c_{i}(x_{i},s_{*})=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}c_{i}(x_{i},s_{j}),\\
c_{-i}(x_{i},s_{*})=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}c_{j}(x_{i},s_{j}).\\
\end{array}

Note that the FOP formulation is particularly suitable for using ZD strategies, because it is not effected by the structure and size of network graph, even though the size might be
\infty
. As long as the stationary distribution of the overall network exists, ZD strategies are still applicable. Moreover, it is easily designable.

Remark 4.5
.

(i)

In the above discussion we only provide the formula for designing ZD strategies. A problem is: is the solution
\{p^{i}_{j,k},k\in[1,k_{i}]\}
obtained from the formula permissible. From designer’s point of view, it can be seen immediately from the numerical result. As for theoretical discussion, it is a challenging problem and is out of the scop of this paper. We refer to
[

]
for the existence of the proper solution.

(ii)

When an individual player
i
in an NRD using ZD strategies, she can “manipulate” her immediate neighbors’ payoffs
from her. That is the payoffs of the rest of the network got from her. Though it far no means she can manipulate the whole network’s payoffs, from her individual perspective, it might be enough.

(iii)

Under our FOP formulation, the ZD strategies in the NRG is exactly the same as the one for non-networked repeated game.

(iv)

The existence of the zero-determinant strategies is not trivial. We refer to
[

]
for some related

interesting.

5
Conclusion

This paper considers the design of ZD strategies proposed by Press and Dyson for general finite games. Using STP, a fundamental formula is presented to numerically realize ZD strategies for finite games with multi-player and asymmetric strategies. In addition to the generality, it simplifies the design procedure. Then, a necessary and sufficient condition for the availability of the designed ZD strategies is also obtained, which put the ZD technique on a solid foundation. Some numerical examples are presented to demonstrate the efficiency of the method proposed in this paper.

Finally, as an application of the general formula the NRGs are considered. A new concept, called FOP, is proposed as the opponent player for a preassigned player
i
. Using it, the ZD strategies for player
i
is designed for the game between herself and her FOP. It is interesting that one single player may be able to “control” the payoff of the rest part of the network from her by using ZD strategies, no matter how large the network is.
