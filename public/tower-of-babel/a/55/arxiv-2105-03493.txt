Causal identification of infectious disease intervention effects in a clustered population
Xiaoxuan Cai, Eben Kenah, Forrest W. Crawford[1em] 1. Department of Biostatistics, Yale School of Public Health2. Division of Biostatistics, College of Public Health, The Ohio State University3. Department of Statistics & Data Science, Yale University4. Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology, Yale University5. Yale School of Management

Abstract
Causal identification of treatment effects for infectious disease outcomes in interconnected populations is challenging because infection outcomes may be transmissible to others, and treatment given to one individual may affect others’ outcomes. Contagion, or transmissibility of outcomes, complicates standard conceptions of treatment interference in which an intervention delivered to one individual can affect outcomes of others. For example, a vaccine given to an individual may affect their risk of infection given exposure to an infectious source as well as their infectiousness if they become infected. Several statistical frameworks have been proposed to measure causal treatment effects in this setting, including structural transmission models, mediation-based partnership models, and randomized trial designs. However, existing estimands for infectious disease intervention effects are of limited conceptual usefulness: Some are parameters in a structural model whose causal interpretation is unclear, others are causal effects defined only in a restricted two-person setting, and still others are nonparametric estimands that arise naturally in the context of a randomized trial but may not measure any biologically meaningful effect. In this paper, we describe a unifying formalism for defining nonparametric structural causal estimands and an identification strategy for learning about infectious disease intervention effects in clusters of interacting individuals when infection times are observed. The estimands generalize existing quantities and provide a framework for causal identification in randomized and observational studies, including situations where only binary infection outcomes are observed. A semiparametric class of pairwise Cox-type transmission hazard models is used to facilitate statistical inference in finite samples. A comprehensive simulation study compares existing and proposed estimands under a variety of randomized and observational vaccine trial designs. Keywords: causal inference, contagion, direct effect, indirect effect, interference, vaccine

In this paper, we describe a nonparametric causal framework for defining and identifying intervention effects for transmissible infectious disease outcomes in clusters.
These methods incorporate individual treatments and covariates that may affect both susceptibility and infectiousness.
We propose new causal estimands—contagion, susceptibility, and infectiousness effects—using contrasts of potential infection outcomes under different treatment conditions.
Two key insights distinguish this approach from others:
First, we define potential infection outcomes holding constant infection times of other individuals, thereby blocking feedback from the infection status of a given individual to that of others who might infect them.

Setting and notation

Consider a population of independent clusters of individuals susceptible to infection by an infectious disease.
Following the conventional partial interference assumption
[
8
,
13
]
, we assume there is no transmission between clusters.
We develop notation and definitions for a generic cluster of
n
individuals. Let
T_{i}
be the random time at which
i
becomes infected, and let
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
be the vector of infection times of the cluster.
Let
Y_{i}(t)=\mathds{1}\left\{T_{i}\leq t\right\}
be the binary indicator of prior infection of
i
at time
t
, and let
\mathbf{Y}(t)=\big(Y_{1}(t),\ldots,Y_{n}(t)\big)
be the vector of infection indicators at time
t
of the cluster.
Let
X_{i}
be the binary treatment (e.g. vaccination) status of
i
, and let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be the treatment vector for the cluster.
Similarly, let
\mathbf{L}_{i}
be the vector of measured covariates of
i
, and let
\mathbf{L}=(\mathbf{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{L}_{n})
be the covariates for the cluster.
Denote the vector
\mathbf{T}
excluding
T_{i}
as
\mathbf{T}_{(i)}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{i},T_{i+1},\ldots,T_{n})
, and define the vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{(i)}(t)
,
\mathbf{X}_{(i)}
, and
\mathbf{L}_{(i)}
similarly.

It is useful to represent the history of infections in the cluster up to a particular time
t
.
For a time
t>0
, let
H_{i}(t)=\{Y_{i}(s):0\leq s<t\}
be the infection history of individual
i
up to time
t
, with a realization denoted by
h_{i}(t)
, and let
\mathbfcal{H}(t)=\{Y_{i}(s):0\leq s<t,i=1,\ldots,n\}
be the binary infection history of the cluster up to time
t
, with a realization denoted by
\mathbf{h}(t)
. Specifically,
\mathbfcal{H}(t)
represents the pattern of infections that have occurred in the cluster, up to time
t
.
Let
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(t)=\{Y_{j}(s):0\leq s<t,j\neq i\}
be the infection history of all individuals except
i
up to time
t
, with its realization
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t)
.
To specify a complete infection history for all times
0\leq t<\infty
, we will write
H_{i}=H_{i}(\infty)
,
\mathbfcal{H}=\mathbfcal{H}(\infty)
, and
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}=\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(\infty)
.
We can partition the joint infection history in the cluster into
\mathbfcal{H}(t)=\big(H_{i}(t),\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(t)\big)
.

Informally, every individual’s potential infection time depends on their own treatment as well as the treatments and infection times of others in the same cluster.
To express these simultaneous relationships, we define the potential infection outcome
Y_{i}(t)
(or
T_{i}
) under the joint treatment
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
and others’ infection histories
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}=\mathbf{h}_{(i)}
.
While
Y_{i}(t)
and
T_{i}
may depend on
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}
, the history
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}
also depends on
Y_{i}(t)
(or equivalently,
T_{i}
or
H_{i}
) because infection of
i
prior to
t
might have caused infection of other individuals, affecting
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(t)
.
For this reason, we will define the potential infection outcomes
T_{i}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}\right)
and
Y_{i}(t;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})=\mathds{1}\left\{T_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})\leq t\right\}

and a
deterministic

By fixing the infection histories

Following the strategy developed for two-person partnerships by
Cai et al. 2021
, we decompose the potential infection time

of a given individual
i
into a series of latent infection waiting times following the infections of individuals other than
i
.
To illustrate, let
(t_{(i)}^{1},t_{(i)}^{2},\ldots,t_{(i)}^{n-1})
be the ordered history of others’ infection times specified by
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}

is the

Denote the individual whose infection time is

, indexing the

infected cluster member for
i
.
Similarly, we rearrange the treatments for individuals except
i
by the order of their infection times as

x_{(i)}^{k}=x_{\varphi^{k}_{i}}
.
For ease of notation, we denote

t_{(i)}^{n}=\infty

Because individuals are assumed to be uninfected at time

I_{i}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})

If

is infected before

.
If

, let
I_{i}^{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})
be the additional time to infection of

.
If

is subsequently infected before the next infection among its fellow cluster members, which occurs in individual

Following the same reasoning, let

If our interest is in evaluating potential infection outcomes as a function of treatments alone, we may view the history
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}
as a nuisance variable, to be averaged out.
If we treat
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}
as a realization of the random infection times
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}
of others, what distribution should it have?
In non-interventional settings, the distribution of
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}
depends on
H_{i}
(equivalently,
T_{i}
), because
i
might infect others following their own infection.
Therefore the marginal distribution of
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}
is not of interest as a marginalizing distribution for the potential infection outcome of
i
, because it contains information about the outcome of
i
.
Instead, we block this feedback by constructing an alternative marginalizing variable, denoted
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}
, whose distribution is invariant to the infection time of
i
.

Let
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(t)
be the random history of infection times in individuals other than
i
up to time
t
under the condition that
i
cannot transmit infection to any other cluster member.
Let
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(t;\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
be the corresponding potential infection history under this circumstance.
The alternative history
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(t;\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
has a special connection to the observational infection history
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(\mathbf{x})

is infected,
i
cannot transmit infection to others, so
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(\mathbf{x})
is equivalent to
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
in distribution.
Once
i
is infected and can transmit infection to others, this alters the distribution of
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(\mathbf{x})
but not
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
, so they are no longer equal in distribution.
Since we are evaluating the potential infection outcome of
i
, the difference in the evolution of the cluster-level infection after the infection of

is inconsequential.
Therefore,
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
, the infection history in an otherwise identical group where
i
cannot transmit infection, is equal in distribution to the observed
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(\mathbf{x})
before the infection of
i

i
due to others’ infections.
We formalize this intuition in identification results presented in the next Section.

3
Identification of average potential infection outcomes

3.1
Assumptions

By the end of the observation at time
t
, we observe
\big(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{L},\mathbfcal{H}(t)\big)
.
We state the following assumptions for a generic individual
i
.

Assumption 1

(Exclusion restriction for future infection times)
.

For
k=1,\ldots,n-1
, we have

I_{i}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})=I_{i}^{0}(x_{i})
and

I_{i}^{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})=I_{i}^{k}\big(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})\big)
.

Assumption
1
states that the latent time to infection of
i
following the
k
th infection time
t_{(i)}^{k}
depends only on the treatments
\mathbf{x}
assigned at baseline and others’ infection history up to
t_{(i)}^{k}
,
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})
.
It does not depend on the infection history after
t_{(i)}^{k}
.

Assumption 2

(Independence of latent times to infection)
.

For a given history
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})
up to the
k
th infection and each
j=\varphi_{i}^{k+1},\ldots,\varphi_{i}^{n-1}
infected after time
t_{(i)}^{k}
,

I_{i}^{k}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k}))\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\models$}}I_{j}^{k}(x_{j},\mathbf{x}_{(j)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k}))\mid\mathbf{L}.

Assumption
2
states that under a given infection history up to the
k^{\text{th}}
infection, the latent time to infection for individual
i
is conditionally independent of the latent time to infection for any other uninfected individual
j
given
\mathbf{L}
. In other words, the competing (simultaneously elapsing) infection waiting times
I_{i}^{k}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k}))
and
I_{j}^{k}(x_{j},\mathbf{x}_{(j)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k}))
are conditionally independent.

Assumption 3

(Previous exposure exchangeability)
.

For all
t>0
and each
k=0,\ldots,n-1
,

I_{i}^{k}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k}))\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\models$}}\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(t_{(i)}^{k};\mathbf{x}_{(i)})\mid\mathbf{L}.

Assumption
3
states that the latent time to infection
I_{i}^{k}\big(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})\big)
under the deterministic infection history
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})
up to
t_{(i)}^{k}
is conditionally independent of the random infection history
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(t_{(i)}^{k};\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
given
\mathbf{L}
.
If we interpret
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(t;\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
as the exposure of
i
to infection up to time
t

In this way, Assumption
3
is a conventional treatment exchangeability assumption, where the “treatment” is prior exposure to infection.

Assumption 4

(Treatment exchangeability)
.

For
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
,

I_{i}^{k}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})\big)\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\models$}}\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{L}.

Assumption
4
states that the potential latent time to infection
I_{i}^{k}\big(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})\big)
is conditionally independent of the joint treatment assignment
\mathbf{X}
given
\mathbf{L}
.
This corresponds to no unmeasured confounding of the effect of treatment on the latent time to infection.

Assumption 5

(Consistency)
.

When
X_{i}=x_{i}
,
\mathbf{X}_{(i)}=\mathbf{x}_{(i)}
,
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})=\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})
and
T_{i}>t_{(i)}^{k}
,

I_{i}^{k}=I_{i}^{k}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})).

Assumption
5
states that
I_{i}^{k}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k}))
is the realized latent time to infection when the observed treatments and infection history up to
t_{(i)}^{k}
match those specified and
i
remains susceptible at
t_{(i)}^{k}
.

Assumption 6

(Positivity)
.

\Pr\big(I_{i}^{k}\leq t\,\big|\,\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k})=\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t_{(i)}^{k}),\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{l}\big)

Assumption

Assumption 7

For

3.2
Identification of average potential infection outcomes

We first seek to identify the distribution of the infection time
I_{i}^{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})
of

\begin{split}F_{I_{i}^{k}}(s\,|\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})&=\Pr\big(I_{i}^{k}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})<s\,\big|\,\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{l}\big)\\

Lemma 1

(Identification of latent time to infection under competing risks)

Corollary 2

(Equivalence of observed and potential infection outcomes)

By Assumptions

4
Causal effects based on risk differences

With these identification results in hand, we present biologically meaningful causal estimands summarizing the transmissibility of disease, as well as the effects of treatment on infection and transmision.
We define causal effects in terms of infection risk differences, but similar effects may be defined in terms of risk ratios or odds ratios.
We focus on the potential infection outcomes of a particular individual
i
.

First, we define what it means for the infection outcome to be contagious, or transmissible.

Definition 2

(Contagion effect)
.

The exposure-controlled contagion effect is

CE_{i}(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(t;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})-Y_{i}(t;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}^{\prime})].

The exposure-marginalized contagion effect is

CE_{i}(t,x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{(i)})=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}\big(t;x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})\big)-Y_{i}\big(t;x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)}^{\prime})\big)\big].

Informally, the infection outcome is “contagious” if it depends on the infection history of others, holding treatments constant. To make this idea more concrete, for
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}=(t_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,t_{i}^{(n-1)})
and
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}^{\prime}=(t_{i}^{{}^{\prime}(1)},\ldots,t_{i}^{{}^{\prime}(n-1)})
, we denote
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}\prec\mathbf{h}_{(i)}^{\prime}
if
t_{i}^{(k)}\leq t_{i}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}
element-wise for
k=1,\ldots,n-1
. We say the infection outcome is
positively contagious
absent treatment if earlier infection of cluster members increases the average potential infection risk, so
CE(t,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}(t),\mathbf{h}_{(i)}^{\prime}(t))>0
whenever
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}\prec\mathbf{h}_{(i)}^{\prime}
, when no cluster members receive treatment
[
22
]
.

Susceptibility effects compare potential infection outcomes of
i
under treatment versus no treatment of
i
, while holding infection history and treatment to others constant.

Definition 3

(Susceptibility effect)
.

The exposure-controlled susceptibility effect is

SE_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(t;1,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})-Y_{i}(t;0,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})],

the exposure-marginalized susceptibility effect is

SE_{i}(t,\mathbf{x}_{(i)})=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}\big(t;1,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})\big)-Y_{i}\big(t;0,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})\big)\big],

and the exposure-and-treatment-marginalized susceptibility effect is

SE_{i}^{p}(t)=\overline{Y}_{i}^{p}(t;1)-\overline{Y}_{i}^{p}(t;0).

This susceptibility effect summarizes the effect of changing only the treatment of
i
, holding all else constant; if it is negative, then the treatment reduces the chance of infection.

Infectiousness effects compare the potential infection outcome of
i
under different treatments to others while holding constant treatment and exposure to infection of
i
.

Definition 4

(Infectiousness effect)
.

The exposure-controlled infectiousness effect is

IE_{i}(t,x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{x}_{(i)}^{\prime},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(t;x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})-Y_{i}(t;x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)}^{\prime},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})],

and the exposure-marginalized infectiousness effect is

IE_{i}(t,x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{x}_{(i)}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}\big(t;x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})\big)-Y_{i}\big(t;x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)}^{\prime},\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})\big)\big]

where we have held the marginalizing distribution
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
constant under treatment
\mathbf{x}_{(i)}
.
The treatment-and-exposure-marginalized infectiousness effect is:

IE^{p}(t)=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}}IE_{i}(t,x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)})p(\mathbf{x}).

Informally, the infectiousness effect summarizes the effect of treatment on an infected individual’s ability to infect others. Note that the distribution of
\mathbfcal{H}_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{(i)})
is the same in both parts of the contrast, and we are only changing the integrand, not the marginalizing distribution.

5
Statistical identification using pairwise hazard models

Non-parametric identification of exposure-controlled potential infection outcomes via Lemma
1
and Theorem
1
can be difficult in finite samples because realized infection histories may be different from the history of interest, or we may not have enough realizations to adequately capture the natural distribution over infection histories.
Fortunately, flexible statistical modeling assumptions can help parameterize distribution functions
F_{I_{i}^{k}}(s|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})
so that these can be efficiently estimated, facilitating computation of expected potential infection outcomes.

Kenah 2013
,
Kenah 2015
developed a semiparametric class of infection transmission models that provides the tools needed to dramatically simplify inference of identified estimands while accommodating individual-level treatment and covariates.
The approach is based on extensive prior work modeling time-to-infection data
[
24
,
30
,
37
,
34
,
36
,
21
]
.

5.1
Hazards for time-to-transmission

We seek to parameterize the distribution of the potential waiting time
I_{i}^{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})
from the
k
th infection to the infection of
i
.
The key idea is that previously infected individuals, along with a possibly exogenous source of infection, impose competing risks of disease transmission to remaining uninfected individuals.
We view
I_{i}^{k}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{(i)}^{\mathbf{k}},\mathbf{h}_{(i)}^{\mathbf{k}})
as the minimum of these
k+1
competing transmission times, starting at the time of the
k
th infection
t_{(i)}^{k}
.

To formalize this intuition, let
\tau_{ji}
be the time for
j
to infect
i
, following infection of
j
, and suppose all
\tau_{ji}
are conditionally independent, given relevant treatments and covariates. Let
\tau_{ji}(x_{j},x_{i})
be the potential transmission time as a function of treatments to
j
and
i
, and let
\lambda_{ji}(\cdot|x_{j},x_{i},\mathbf{l}_{j},\mathbf{l}_{i})
be the hazard function of
\tau_{ji}(x_{j},x_{i})
, where we interpret
\tau_{0i}(x_{i})
as the potential transmission time to
i
from an outside (exogenous) source of transmission, and let
\lambda_{0i}(t|x_{i},\mathbf{l}_{i})
be the corresponding transmission hazard.

Assumption 9

(Independence of competing transmission times)
.

For all
i
and all
j\neq i
,
k\neq l

\begin{split}\tau_{ji}(x_{j},x_{i})&\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\models$}}\tau_{kl}(x_{k},x_{l})\mid\mathbf{L},\\
\lambda_{0i}(t\,|\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})&=\lambda_{0i}(t\,|\,x_{i},\mathbf{l}_{i}),\\
\lambda_{ji}(t\,|\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})&=\lambda_{ji}(t-t_{j}\,|\,x_{i},x_{j},\mathbf{l}_{i},\mathbf{l}_{j}).\end{split}

(5)

Assumption
5
states that the latent waiting times to infection from different sources to
i
are conditionally independent given treatments and covariates and that their hazard functions are determined by treatments and covariates in the target individual and, if needed, the infected source individual.

This assumption yields a convenient additive hazard model for the infection time of

, the infection waiting time

has hazard

\lambda_{0i}(t\,|\,x_{i},\mathbf{l}_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{\varphi_{i}^{j},i}(t-t_{(i)}^{k}\,|\,x_{\varphi_{i}^{j}},x_{i},\mathbf{l}_{j},\mathbf{l}_{i})

(6)

which is the sum of the transmission hazards from existing sources of infection. Therefore the infection hazard of
i
at an arbitrary time
t
, derived from (
6

\lambda_{i}(t\,|\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})=\lambda_{0i}(t\,|\,x_{i},\mathbf{l}_{i})+\sum_{j\neq i}y_{j}(t)\lambda_{ji}(t-t_{j}\,|\,x_{i},x_{j},\mathbf{l}_{i},\mathbf{l}_{j}).

(7)

Assumption
5
dramatically simplifies representation and estimation of causal estimands in terms of time integrals of these hazard functions. Figure

shows how
\lambda_{i}(t|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})

\mathbf{h}_{(i)}=(t_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,t_{i}^{(n-1)})
.

The hazard representation (

Theorem 3

(Identification of exposure-controlled potential outcomes under the pairwise hazard model)
.

Under Assumption

\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)})\,|\,\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{l}]=1-\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\lambda_{0i}(s|x_{i},\mathbf{l}_{i})+\sum_{j\neq i}y_{j}(s)\lambda_{ji}(s-t_{j}|x_{i},x_{j},\mathbf{l}_{i},\mathbf{l}_{j})\right)\mathrm{d}s\right]

where

This result is a consequence of the hazard representation (

t
under the specified treatments and infection history of others.

Finally, exposure-marginalized potential infection outcomes can be computed using the identified distribution of the potential exposure distribution.

Theorem 4

(Identification of exposure-marginalized potential outcomes under the pairwise hazard model)
.

Under Assumption

, Theorem

\text{d}G_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{h}_{(i)}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{l}_{(i)})=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\bigg[\lambda_{\varphi_{i}^{j}}(t_{(i)}^{j}\,|\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}^{i}_{(\varphi_{i}^{j})},\mathbf{l})\prod_{k=j}^{n-1}\exp\bigg(-\int_{t_{(i)}^{j-1}}^{t_{(i)}^{j}}\lambda_{\varphi_{i}^{k}}(s\,|\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}^{i}_{(\varphi_{i}^{k})},\mathbf{l})\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\bigg]\\

(8)

For the terms inside the square bracket, we have used the fact that the probability density for the infection time of

equals the product of its hazard and survival functions. For the product term inside the square brackets, each individual

is included for

Thus, (

\text{d}G_{(i)}^{*}(\mathbf{h}_{(i)}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{l}_{(i)})=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{\varphi_{i}^{j}}(t_{(i)}^{j}\,|\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}^{i}_{(\varphi_{i}^{j})},\mathbf{l})\times\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\exp\bigg(-\int_{0}^{t_{(i)}^{k}}\lambda_{\varphi_{i}^{k}}(s\,|\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}^{i}_{(\varphi_{i}^{k})},\mathbf{l})\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

5.2
Statistical infection hazard model

Following
Kenah 2015
, we consider a Cox-type hazard model for the pairwise infection times:

\begin{split}\lambda_{0i}(t\,|\,x_{i},\mathbf{l}_{i})&=\alpha(t)\exp[\beta_{1}x_{i}+\theta_{1}\mathbf{l}_{i}]\\
\lambda_{ji}(t\,|\,x_{i},x_{j},\mathbf{l}_{i})&=\gamma(t-t_{j})\exp[\beta_{1}x_{i}+\beta_{2}x_{j}+\theta_{1}\mathbf{l}_{i}+\theta_{2}\mathbf{l}_{j}]\end{split}

(10)

which has non-parametric baseline hazards
\alpha(t)
for the (time-varying) risk of infection from outside the cluster, and
\gamma(t-t_{j})
for the (time-varying) infection risks from already-infected cluster members
j
, with the effects of treatments
\mathbf{X}=x
and covariates
\mathbf{L}
accommodated in a log-linear model with coefficients
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
,
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
. This infection hazard model has been used by many authors to represent transmission dynamics in groups
[
30
,
42
,
36
,
35
,
26
,
21
,
43
]
.

The coefficients of the infection hazard model (

\beta_{1}<0
, vaccination decreases the hazard of infection for treated individuals against all sources of infection by a factor of
e^{\beta_{1}}
. If
\beta_{2}<0
, vaccination of other individuals decreases their hazard of transmission to
i
by a factor of
e^{\beta 2}
once they become infectious. Plugging in (

\lambda_{i}(t\,|\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})=\exp[\beta_{1}x_{i}+\theta_{1}\mathbf{l}_{i}]\Big(\alpha(t)+\sum_{j\neq i}y_{j}(t)\gamma(t-t_{j})\exp[\beta_{2}x_{j}+\theta_{2}\mathbf{l}_{j}]\Big).

(11)

5.3
Hazard ratio estimands

The parameters of the hazard model (
7
) are closely related to causal effects defined above. For example, the contagion effect is positive whenever
\gamma(t)>0
, and the sign of the susceptibility effects and infectiousness effects depend only on
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
, respectively (details are given in the Appendix). These facts suggest that the parameters of this model may have a causal interpretation in terms of their relationship to the causal contagion, susceptibility, and infectiousness effects defined above. We construct hazard ratio estimands by contrasting potential infection hazard under different exposure history and treatment.

Definition 5

(Controlled susceptibility hazard ratio)
.

The controlled susceptibility hazard ratio is defined as:

HSE^{C}(t,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})=\frac{\lambda_{i}(t\,|\,x_{i}=1,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})}{\lambda_{i}(t\,|\,x_{i}=0,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})}

The controlled hazard susceptibility ratio is the ratio of counterfactual hazards of
i
when
i
is treated versus untreated, while holding the treatments, infection histories, and covariates of others constant. Plugging (
10
) into the controlled hazard susceptibility effect, we find that

HSE^{C}(t,\mathbf{x}_{(i)},\mathbf{h}_{(i)},\mathbf{l})=e^{\beta_{1}}

(12)

regardless of the values of
t
,
\mathbf{x}_{(j)}
,
\mathbf{h}_{(i)}
, and cluster size.
If
\beta_{1}<0
, vaccination is beneficial because it decreases the hazard of infection from all sources of infection by a factor of
e^{\beta_{1}}<1
.

Definition 6

(Controlled infectiousness hazard ratio)
.

The controlled infectiousness hazard ratio is defined as:

HIE^{C}(t,h_{j},h^{\prime}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{(j)},\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})=\frac{\lambda_{i}(t|x_{j}=1,\mathbf{x}_{(j)},h^{\prime}_{j},\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})-\lambda_{i}(t|x_{j}=1,\mathbf{x}_{(j)},h_{j},\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})}{\lambda_{i}(t|x_{j}=0,\mathbf{x}_{(j)},h^{\prime}_{j},\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})-\lambda_{i}(t|x_{j}=0,\mathbf{x}_{(j)},h_{j},\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})}

where
y_{j}(t)=1
in
h^{\prime}_{j}
and
y_{j}(t)=0
in
h_{j}
.

The controlled infectiousness hazard ratio is the ratio of the hazard of
i
being infected by a given infectious neighbor
j
, when
j
is vaccinated versus unvaccinated, holding
\mathbf{x}_{(j)}
, exposure to infection from sources other than
j
, and all covariates constant.
Applying the hazard model (
10
) to the controlled infectiousness effect, we have

HIE^{C}(t,h_{j},h^{\prime}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{(j)},\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})=e^{\beta_{2}}

(13)

regardless of the values of
t
,
\mathbf{x}_{(j)}
,
\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)}
, and cluster size.
If
\beta_{2}<0
, the infectiousness effect is beneficial because it decreases the instantaneous infectiousness of
j
by a factor of
e^{\beta_{2}}<1
.

To determine whether the disease is contagious, we focus on one transmission contact between
i
and an infectious individual
j
, and evaluate if coming into contact with an infectious individual increases the infection risk.
Since treatments are irrelevant to the intrinsic transmissibility of a disease, we define the contagion effect under null treatments
\mathbf{X}=(0,\ldots,0)=\mathbf{0}
.

Definition 7

(Controlled contagion cumulative hazard ratio)
.

The controlled contagion cumulative hazard ratio is

HCE^{C}(t,h^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{j},h^{\prime}_{j},\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})=\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\big[\lambda_{i}(u;\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0},h^{\prime}_{j}(t),\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})-\lambda_{i}(u;\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0},h_{j}(t),\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})\big]du}{\int_{0}^{t}\big[\lambda_{i}(u;\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0},h^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{j}(t),\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})-\lambda_{i}(u;\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0},h_{j}(t),\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)},\mathbf{l})\big]du}

where the corresponding infection times
t^{\prime}_{j}
and
t^{\prime\prime}_{j}
contained in
h^{\prime}_{j}
and
h^{\prime\prime}_{j}
has the relationship of
t^{\prime}_{j}<t^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{j}<t
, and
y_{j}(t)=0
in
h_{j}(t)
.

Applying the hazard model (
10
) to the controlled contagion effect, we have:

HCE^{C}(t,h^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{j},h^{\prime}_{j},\mathbf{h}_{(i,j)})=\frac{\int_{t_{j}^{\prime}}^{t}\gamma(u)du}{\int_{t_{j}^{\prime\prime}}^{t}\gamma(u)du}

(14)

Therefore,
\gamma(t)>0
for any
t>0
implies positive contagion, since earlier exposure to infection increases the infection risk.

6.2
Comparison to other estimands

Statisticians and epidemiologists have proposed a wide variety of estimands summarizing the effect of interventions for infectious disease outcomes.
In this section, we evaluate the performance of two commonly used estimands – direct effect (
DE

[
27
,
50
,
13
,
3
,
51
,
52
]
– and compare them to our estimands under the same simulation scenarios. We also employ GEE regression models to adjust for covariates in the estimation of
DE
and
IDE
in the observational study setting.

7
Discussion

Causal dependence between infection outcomes is a major obstacle to identification of meaningful treatment effects in infectious disease epidemiology
[
5
,
10
,
6
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
9
,
7
]
. Inferential approaches that neglect this dependence can result in misleading estimates of vaccine effects, even in randomized trials
[
26
,
21
,
22
]
. In this paper, we have presented a framework for estimating meaningful treatment effects under contagion by clarifying the temporal relationship between infection outcomes in clusters. We describe a new strategy for identifying average potential infection outcomes. The approach proceeds by first identifying the average infection outcome under a deterministic infection exposure history, then integrating this history with respect to a distribution over others’ infections, in the situation where the focal individual is not present. This work is a direct extension of prior work by
Cai et al. 2021
and
Ogburn and VanderWeele 2017
to the general cluster setting, with no restrictions on the direction of transmission or treatment assignment.

We define effects that contrast potential infection outcomes when the infection history is either held fixed at a deterministic value (controlled) or integrated (marginalized). One advantage of this approach is that it permits definition of causal estimands in which the infection history of others (or its distribution) is held constant in a contrast of individual treatments. In this way, the susceptibility effect of Definition
3
avoids the problem of differential exposure to infection between treated and untreated individuals which arises in the definition of the “direct effect” proposed by
Hudgens and Halloran 2008
. Simulation results show that while crude contrasts like
DE(t)
may sometimes have the same sign as the true effect when the susceptibility effect is different from zero, they may be biased under certain randomization designs or when the infectiousness effect is strong.

In this work, we have drawn a connection between nonparametric causal estimands
[
20
, e.g.]
for infectious disease intervention effects and statistical models for learning about the dynamics of tranmission
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
. Hazard models provide a natural and well studied class of structural models for time-to-event data. The decomposition (
1
) and corresponding independence assumptions provide a natural way of turning a complex sequence of infection times into conditionally independent waiting times. These assumptions can endow certain hazard model parameters, corresponding to covariate effects, with causal interpretations. The hazard model respects the logic of infection disease transmission: no transmission can occur before infection, and multiple waiting times to transmission can elapse concurrently. Beyond the causal interpretation of hazard ratios described in Section
5.3
, the fitted hazard models provide a convenient statistical framework in which to learn about both exposure-controlled and exposure-marginalized estimands via Theorems
1
and
2
.

Despite the generality of the proposed framework, the approach has several limitations. First, it relies on exact observation of infection times. In reality, binary infection status outcomes may be measured either at regular intervals or at follow-up. Statistical tools for interval censoring and/or tied responses could be employed to fit hazard models of the kind proposed here, but identifiability of model parameters could become more challenging. However, some statistical estimands do not require observation of exact infection times. In particular, Corollary
2
shows that under some circumstances, meaningful causal contrasts can be computed by conditioning on the full vector of treatments within the cluster, even when infection times are unobserved. This suggest that randomized trials that identify average outcomes under fully specified treatment allocations within clusters may reveal causal effects of the kind studied here. Another limitation of this work is that practical statistical estimation requires specification of a hazard model with parametric or semiparametric structure. Lack of knowledge about the true structure of the distribution of pairwise transmission waiting times could result in bias. A potential mitigating factor is that statisticians have access to a wide variety of models for time-to-event data. Almost any of these could be usefully employed in (
10
) if the requisite conditional independence assumptions are met.
