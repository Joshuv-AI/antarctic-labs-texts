Essential dimension of isogenies
János Kollár, Ziquan Zhuang

Abstract
. We give a lower bound for the essential dimension of isogenies of complex abelian varieties. The bound is sharp in many cases. In particular, the multiplication-by- m m map is incompressible for every m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 , confirming a conjecture of Brosnan.



1.
Introduction

The
essential dimension
of a generically finite map
f\colon Y\to X
is the smallest integer
m
such that
f
is birational to the pull-back of a map of varieties of dimension
m
.
It is denoted by
\ed(Y/X)
or
\ed(f)
.
A map

f
is called
incompressible
if
its essential dimension is
\dim X
.

This notion was introduced in
[
3
]
and has been studied in a variety of contexts; see e.g.
[
22
,
18
]
for surveys and
[
9
]
for its connection to an algebraic form of Hilbert’s 13th problem through a variant call the resolvent degree.

The purpose of this note is to describe a birational geometric approach to this invariant, leading to a proof of a conjecture of Brosnan, recorded in
[
7
]
*Conjecture 6.1.
Throughout we work over an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
A
be an abelian variety. Then the multiplication-by-
m
map
m_{A}:A\to A
is incompressible for every
m\geq 2
.

The first lower bounds on the essential dimension of
m_{A}
are in

[
7
]
*Theorem 6.4.
Incompressibility is proved in
[
8
]
, provided

m
has a sufficiently large prime factor (depending on
A
).
The proof uses a
prismatic cohomology argument and also proves a stronger result, called

p
-incompressibility.

Our main technical result is a lower bound for the essential dimension
of arbitrary isogenies. For an abelian group
G
we let
\rank(G)
denote the minimum number of generators, and set
\rank_{p}(G)=\rank(G/pG)
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\alpha\colon A\to A^{\prime}
be an isogeny of abelian varieties. Then there exists an abelian subvariety
B
of
A
such that, for every prime
p
,

[formula]

\ed(\alpha)\geq\dim A-\dim B+\tfrac{p-1}{p}\rank_{p}(\ker(\alpha)\cap B).

If
\alpha=m_{A}
is multiplication by
m
and
p\mid m
, then

\rank_{p}(\ker(\alpha)\cap B)=2\dim B
, so the right hand side of
(
2
.1) is
\geq\dim A
, proving Theorem
1
.

We also get a formula for the essential dimension of isogenies whose degree has no small prime factors.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\alpha\colon A\to A^{\prime}
be an isogeny of abelian varieties whose degree is coprime to
(\dim A)!
. Then the essential dimension of
\alpha
equals the minimum of

\dim A-\dim B+\rank(\ker(\alpha)\cap B),

as
B
runs through all abelian subvarieties of
A
.

The starting point of our proof is the following observation: if the isogeny
\alpha
is birationally obtained from
Y\to Y^{\prime}
via base change, then the Galois group
G\cong\ker(\alpha)
acts birationally and faithfully on
Y
, which can be chosen as the image of the abelian variety
A
by a rational map with connected fibers.

In Section
2
we show that such
rational images of abelian varieties are quite special.
The albanese map
Y\dashrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(Y)
is surjective and its general fibers are
rationally connected.

The induced map
A\dashrightarrow Y\dashrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(Y)
is a morphism of abelian varieties; its kernel gives the abelian subvariety
B
in Theorem
2
.
The remaining task is to understand the
(G\cap B)
-action
on the fibers of
Y\dashrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(Y)
.

In Section
3
we show that an
abelian
p
-group of rank
>\frac{p}{p-1}n
can not act faithfully on a
rationally connected variety of dimension
n
. This generalizes some earlier work of Prokhorov
[
20
]
.

Putting these together, we obtain the proofs of
Theorem
2
and Corollary
3
in Section
4
. We also raise some questions about the essential dimension of endomorphisms of other varieties.

Other applications of our results are discussed in Section
5
.

We remark that the study of essential dimension is often related to questions around the Cremona groups. Dolgachev asked
[
4
]
whether the essential dimension
\ed(G)
of a finite group
G
is always bounded from below by the smallest integer
n
such that
G
embeds into the Cremona group
\mathrm{Cr}(n)
. This is open for non-abelian groups.

2.
Rational images of abelian varieties

Recall that a variety
X
is called
uniruled
(resp.
rationally connected
) if through any point (resp. any pair of points) on
X
there is a rational curve. See
[
16
]
for the general theory of such varieties.

As we indicated, the first step to the proof of Theorem
2
is the following description of the rational images of abelian varieties.

Proposition 4
.

Let
f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y
be a dominant, rational map with connected general fibers. Assume that
X
is an abelian variety and
Y
is not uniruled. Then
Y
is birational to an abelian variety.

We give several different proofs of this proposition.

First proof of Proposition
4
.

Let
\Gamma\subset X\times Y
be the closure of the graph
of
f

and
\pi_{X},\pi_{Y}
the coordinate projections. Let
E\subset\Gamma
be
the exceptional divisor of
\pi_{X}|_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\to X
.
By
[
16
]
*Theorem VI.1.9 every irreducible
component of

\pi_{Y}(E)
is uniruled, hence
\pi_{Y}(E)\neq Y
.

Hence the restriction of
f
to
X\setminus\pi_{X}(E)
is a morphism with
proper general fibers
B_{y}
. Since
B_{y}
is a general fiber, its normal
bundle is trivial, and so is its canonical class by the adjunction
formula.
Thus
B_{y}
is the translate of an abelian subvariety
B\subset X
by

[
27
]
*Corollary 10.5, which is independent of
y
since
abelian subvarieties have no flat deformations. It follows that
Y
is birational to
X/B
which is an abelian variety.
∎

Second proof of Proposition
4
.

By
[
15
]
*Theorem 14,
there is a finite, étale cover
h:X^{\prime}\to X
and a product decomposition

X^{\prime}\cong F^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
such that the composite

f\circ h\colon X^{\prime}\to X\dashrightarrow Y
factors as

X^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau}}{{\dashrightarrow}}Y,

where
\pi
is the coordinate projection, and
\tau
is generically finite.
Note that
X^{\prime}
, being a finite étale cover of an abelian variety, is also an abelian variety. The factor
F^{\prime}
is a subvariety with trivial canonical class, hence is also an abelian varieties by
[
27
]
*Corollary 10.5, and so

h\bigl(F^{\prime}\times\{y^{\prime}\}\bigr)\subset X
is a translate of an
abelian subvariety
B_{y^{\prime}}\subset X
for general
y^{\prime}\in Y^{\prime}
.
Here
B_{y^{\prime}}
depends continuously on
y^{\prime}
for general
y^{\prime}\in Y^{\prime}
, hence
in fact
B:=B_{y^{\prime}}
is independent of
y^{\prime}
.
Thus
f
factors as

f\colon X\to X/B\dashrightarrow Y,

where
X/B
is an abelian variety, and
X/B\dashrightarrow Y
is
generically finite. We assumed that
f
has connected general fibers,
so
X/B\dashrightarrow Y
is birational.
∎

Remark 5

(Communicated with Zsolt Patakfalvi)
.

Proposition
4
also holds in positive characteristics. As in the first proof above, the map
f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y
has proper general fibers over an open set
U\subseteq Y
. Let
\mathrm{Frob}^{e}\colon U^{\prime}\to U
be an iterated Frobenius morphism for some
e\gg 0
and let
X^{\prime}
to be the normalization of the reduced subscheme of
X\times_{Y}U^{\prime}
. Then the generic fiber
F
of
X^{\prime}\to U^{\prime}
is geometrically reduced. Let
g\colon F\to X
be the induced
morphism. By
[
26
, Theorem 1.1]
one has
K_{F}=g^{*}K_{X}-E
for some effective divisor
E
, hence
-K_{F}
is pseudo-effective (equivalently,
\omega^{*}_{F}
is weakly-positive as in
[
5
, Definition 2.2]
). Note that
F
has maximal albanese dimension since it admits a generically finite map to the abelian variety
X
. By
[
5
, Proposition 3.2]
, this implies that the general fiber of
X^{\prime}\to U^{\prime}
are abelian varieties and thus
K_{F}=E=0
. By
[
26
, Theorem 1.1]
, we deduce that the generic fiber of
f
is also geometrically reduced and hence the general fibers of
f
are abelian varieties as well. We then conclude as in the above proofs of Proposition
4
.

Remark 6
.

Having in mind possible generalizations, it may be worthwhile to recall how
Proposition
4
relates to
various conjectures of Iitaka; see
[
27
]
for an introduction.

Let us denote the general fiber of
X\dashrightarrow Y
by
F
. Iitaka’s so called
C_{n,m}
conjecture says that
\kappa(X)\geq\kappa(F)+\kappa(Y)
, where
\kappa(X)
is the Kodaira dimension of
X
which takes values in
\{-\infty\}\cup\mathbb{N}
. On the other hand, the non-vanishing conjecture predicts that if
Y
is not uniruled then
\kappa(Y)\geq 0
. Combining these two, and since
\kappa(X)=0
, we see that
\kappa(F)=\kappa(Y)=0
. This implies that
F
is the translate of an abelian subvariety and
Y
is birational to the quotient abelian variety as before.

Corollary 7
.

Let
X
be an abelian variety and

f\colon X\dashrightarrow Y
a dominant, rational map with connected general fibers. Then
the albanese map
Y\to\operatorname{Alb}(Y)
is surjective and its general fibers are
rationally connected.

Proof.

By
[
16
]
*Theorems 5.4 and 5.5 and
[
11
]
*Corollary 1.4, for any variety
Y

there is a dominant rational map
\sigma\colon Y\dashrightarrow Z
such that
Z
is not uniruled, and whose general fibers are rationally connected.
This map is called the
maximal rationally connected
or
MRC
fibration, and it is birationally unique.

If
Y
is the rational image of an abelian variety, then so is
Z
, hence it is birational to an
abelian variety by Proposition
4
.
Thus the the albanese map
Y\to\operatorname{Alb}(Y)
is the MRC fibration of
Y
.
∎

3.
Actions of abelian
p
-groups

Another ingredient in the proof of Theorem
2
is an optimal bound on the ranks of abelian
p
-subgroups of the birational automorphism groups of rationally connected varieties. For
p>\dim+1
,
[
28
]
obtains optimal bounds using the fixed point theorems of
[
12
]
.
We observe that, when there are no fixed points, the method of
[
12
]
still gives small orbits.
This leads to optimal bounds for
p\leq\dim+1
.

We first prove some general results about abelian
p
-groups acting on varieties. For a prime
p
and an integer
m
, set
\nu_{p}m=\sup\{a\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,p^{a}|m\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
K
be an algebraically closed field, and
G
an abelian
p
-group acting on a smooth, projective
K
-scheme
X
of pure dimension
n
.
Assume that
\operatorname{char}K\neq p
. Then there is a subgroup
H\leq G
such that

H
has a fixed point on
X
, and

\log_{p}[G:H]\leq\nu_{p}(\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}))+\tfrac{n}{p-1}.

Proof.

Let
a=\nu_{p}(\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}))
which we may assume is finite.
Let
p^{c}
be the smallest size of a
G
-orbit.
Then
p^{c}
divides the size of every
G
-orbit.

As we discuss in Paragraph
13
, the Chern numbers of
X
are represented by
G
-invariant
0
-cycles, hence they are divisible by
p^{c}
.

By
[
14
, Lemma 1.7.3]
, the exponent of the largest
p
-power dividing the denominator of the
n
th Todd class is

\lfloor\tfrac{n}{p-1}\rfloor
, thus the exponent of the largest
p
-power dividing
\operatorname{td}_{n}(X)

is at least
c-\tfrac{n}{p-1}
.
By the Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch theorem,
\chi(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})=\operatorname{td}_{n}(X)
, so this exponent equals
a
by assumption.
Thus
c\leq a+\tfrac{n}{p-1}
. Now take
H
to be the stabilizer of a point in a smallest orbit.
∎

Corollary 9
.

Let
K
be a field of characteristic
\neq p
, and
G
an abelian
p
-group acting faithfully on a smooth, projective

K
-scheme
X
of pure dimension
n
. Then

\rank(G)\leq\nu_{p}(\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}))+\tfrac{p}{p-1}n.

Proof.

Let
H\leq G
be the subgroup obtained from Proposition
8
and let
x\in X
be an
H
-fixed point. If
\operatorname{char}K\neq p
then

H
acts faithfully on the tangent space
T_{x}X
, so
H
is isomorphic to a
subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(K)
. Since
H
is abelian, it is diagonalizable, so it is the product of at most
n
cyclic groups. The statement now follows as
\rank(G)\leq\rank(H)+\log_{p}[G:H]
.
∎

Remark 10
.

In fact, we get a stronger result, that

G\cong G_{1}\times G_{2}
, where
\rank(G_{1})\leq n+\nu_{p}(\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}))
, and

|G_{2}|\leq p^{n/(p-1)}
.

If
X
is smooth, projective and rationally connected over a field of characteristic
0
, then
\chi(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})=1
. So we get the following special case.

Corollary 11
.

Let
G
be an abelian
p
-group acting faithfully on a smooth, projective, rationally connected variety
X
of dimension
n
over a field of characteristic
0
. Then

\rank(G)\leq\tfrac{p}{p-1}n\leq 2n.\hfill\qed

Example 12
.

Let
\epsilon
be a primitive
m
th root of unity.
The dihedral group
D_{2m}

acts on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
by
x\mapsto\epsilon x,x\mapsto x^{-1}
.
Thus
D_{2m}^{n}
acts on
({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{n}
coordinate wise.

For
m=2
we get a
({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{2n}
action on
({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{n}
.
So the rank bound is sharp in Corollary
11
for
p=2
.

Esser pointed out that

({\mathbb{Z}}/p)^{p}
acts diagonally on
X_{p}:=\bigl(\textstyle{\sum}_{i}x_{i}^{p}=0\bigr)\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{p}.

Powers of
X_{p}
show that
the bound is sharp for every
p
.

13
Equivariant intersection theory.

Let
G
be a reductive, abelian group acting on a smooth, projective variety
X
. In other words,
G
is abelian, its identity component
G_{0}
is a torus
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
(for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
) and
|G/G_{0}|
is not divisible by the characteristic of the field. Then one can do intersection theory with
G
-invariant cycles.
That is, the intersection of
G
-invariant cycles can be
represented by
G
-invariant cycles. This is obvious if the intersection has the expected dimension, but not otherwise. Example
14
shows that this in fact fails for non-abelian 2-groups.

For
G=T
a torus,
T
-equivariant intersection theory is treated in

[
19
]
. A more sophisticated version is in
[
1
]
.
Note, however, that torus actions have additional features, so not all claims in these papers generalize.
The treatment in
[
12
]
*Section 4 covers both
reductive, abelian groups and non-abelian
p
-groups in characteristic p.

Here we discuss the assertion that we actually use.

Claim
13
.1.
Let
G
be a reductive, abelian group acting on a smooth projective variety
X
. Then the Chern numbers of
X
are represented by
G
-invariant
0
-cycles.

Proof.
Using the formulas in
[
10
, Sec.3.2]
, this is equivalent to representing the Segre numbers.
Let
\pi:P\to X
be the projectivization of
T_{X}
with tautological line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}_{P}(1)
. Set
D:=c_{1}\bigl({\mathcal{O}}_{P}(1)\bigr)
.

By
[
10
, Sec.3.1]
, the Segre classes of
X
are
s_{i}(X):=\pi_{*}\bigl(D^{n-1+i}\bigr)
, and the Segre numbers are the intersection products
s_{i_{1}}(X)\cdots s_{i_{r}}(X)
for

i_{1}+\cdots+i_{r}=n
. Repeatedly using the projection formula, the Claim is implied by the following.

Lemma
13
.2.
Let
Z\subset P
be a
G
-invariant,
G
-irreducible cycle. Then there is a
G
-invariant divisor
D_{Z}\sim D
such that
Z\not\subset\Supp D_{Z}
.

Proof.
The
G
-action on
X
lifts to a
G
-action on
P
. Pick
z\in Z
and choose some
G
-linearized very ample line bundles
L,M
on
P
such that
D\sim L-M
. Since
G
is reductive and abelian, both
H^{0}(L)
and
H^{0}(M)
are spanned by
G
-equivariant sections. Thus there exist
G
-equivariant sections
s_{L}\in H^{0}(L)
and
s_{M}\in H^{0}(M)
such that
z
is not in their zero locus. Set
D_{Z}:=(s_{L}=0)-(s_{M}=0)
. ∎

.

Let
G
be the group of order 32 acting on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, generated by the
({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{4}
-action as in Example
12
, plus the interchanging the 2 copies of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

(0,0),(1,1),(i,i)

G
-orbit of size 4, all other

S\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

4.
Essential dimension of isogenies

We now turn to the proof of our main results.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By the definition of essential dimension, there exists a Cartesian diagram

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A^{\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y^{\prime}}

where the horizontal maps are finite and
\dim Y=\dim Y^{\prime}=\ed(\alpha)
. Replacing
Y^{\prime}
by its normalization in
A^{\prime}
and
Y
by the corresponding fiber product, we may assume that the rational maps
A^{\prime}\dashrightarrow Y^{\prime}
and
A\dashrightarrow Y
have connected fibers. In particular, the function field
\mathbb{C}(Y)
of
Y
is algebraically closed in
\mathbb{C}(A)
. From this we see that the Galois action of
G:=\ker(\alpha)
on
\mathbb{C}(A)
descends to
\mathbb{C}(Y)
: in fact, any element in the Galois conjugate of
\mathbb{C}(Y)
is finite over
\mathbb{C}(Y)
, hence is contained in
\mathbb{C}(Y)
since
\mathbb{C}(Y)
is algebraically closed in
\mathbb{C}(A)
. It follows that
G
acts birationally and faithfully on
Y
.

Let
Y\to\operatorname{Alb}(Y)
be the albanese map and
F\subset X
a general fiber.
The composite
A\dashrightarrow Y\to\operatorname{Alb}(Y)
is a morphism of abelian varieties;
let
B\subseteq A
be its kernel. It is connected by construction.

Note that
Y\to\operatorname{Alb}(Y)
is
G
-equivariant, and

L:=G\cap B
acts trivially on
\operatorname{Alb}(Y)
. Hence
L
must act faithfully on
F
.

The fiber
F
is rationally connected by Corollary
7
.
Thus Corollary
11
implies that
\dim F\geq\frac{p-1}{p}\rank_{p}(L)
. Therefore,

\ed(\alpha)=\dim Y=\dim A-\dim B+\dim F\geq\dim A-\dim B+\tfrac{p-1}{p}\rank_{p}(L),

as desired.
∎

In order to prove Corollary
3
we need some properties of essential dimension.

Lemma 15
.

Let
f_{i}\colon X_{i}\to Y

(i=1,2)
be finite morphisms and let
f\colon X=X_{1}\times_{Y}X_{2}\to Y
be the fiber product. Then
\ed(f)\leq\ed(f_{1})+\ed(f_{2})
.

Proof.

Clear from the definition.
∎

Lemma 16
.

[
3
]
*Theorem 6.1
Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a finite abelian cover with Galois group
G
. Then
\ed(f)\leq\rank(G)
.

Proof.

We may write
f
as the fiber product of
\rank(G)
cyclic covers. Since any cyclic cover has essential dimension one, the statement follows from Lemma
15
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
3
.

We first prove that

\ed(\alpha)\leq\dim A-\dim B+\rank(\ker(\alpha)\cap B)

for any abelian subvariety
B
of
A
. Let
L=\ker(\alpha)\cap B
and let
K=\ker(\alpha)/L
. Then the isogeny
A\to A^{\prime}
factors through
A_{1}=A/L
. Let
r=\rank(L)
. We can find another isogeny
A_{2}\to A^{\prime}
with kernel rank
r
such that the fiber product
A_{1}\times_{A^{\prime}}A_{2}
factors through
A
(it suffices to lift
\pi_{1}(A_{1})\to L
to some
\pi_{1}(A^{\prime})\to L^{\prime}
with
\rank(L^{\prime})=r
). By Lemma
16
, we have
\ed(A_{2}/A^{\prime})\leq r
, hence by By Lemma
15
we only need to show that

\ed(A_{1}/A^{\prime})\leq\dim A-\dim B.

To see this, let
A_{0}:=A/B
and let
A^{\prime}_{0}:=A_{0}/K\cong A^{\prime}/\alpha(B)
.

\textstyle{A_{1}=A/L\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A^{\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A_{0}=A/B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A^{\prime}_{0}}

Then the natural map
A_{1}\to A^{\prime}\times_{A^{\prime}_{0}}A_{0}
is an isomorphism: in fact, its kernel is the same as the kernel of
K\cong\ker(A_{1}\to A^{\prime})\to A_{0}
, but the latter map is injective by construction. It follows that
\ed(A_{1}/A^{\prime})\leq\dim A_{0}=\dim A-\dim B
and this gives the desired inequality.

For the reverse direction, we may assume that
\ed(\alpha)<\dim A
(otherwise there is nothing to prove). By Theorem
2
, there exists some abelian subvariety
B
of
A
such that

\ed(\alpha)\geq\dim A-\dim B+\tfrac{p-1}{p}\rank_{p}(L)

for every prime
p
, where
L=\ker(\alpha)\cap B
as before. In particular,
\frac{p-1}{p}\rank_{p}(L)\leq\dim B-1
(since
\ed(\alpha)<\dim A
). Since
\deg(\alpha)=|L|
is coprime to
(\dim A)!
, there exists some prime
p>\dim A\geq\dim B
such that
\rank_{p}(L)=\rank(L)
. For this
p
we get

\rank(L)=\rank_{p}(L)\leq\tfrac{p}{p-1}(\dim B-1)<\dim B<p,

hence
\lceil\frac{p-1}{p}\rank_{p}(L)\rceil=\rank(L)
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 17
.

Let
\alpha\colon A\to A^{\prime}
be an isogeny of simple abelian varieties whose degree is relatively prime to
(\dim A)!
. Then

\ed(\alpha)=\min\{\dim A,\rank(\ker(\alpha))\}.

Proof.

Since
A
is simple, the only abelian subvarieties are the trivial ones, hence the statement is clear from Theorem
2
.
∎

[
8
]
*Prop.2.3.10 proves a more general result about the essential dimensions of mod
p
homology covers. It would be interesting to find a geometric version of their result. A natural geometric replacement of the mod
p
homology cover is the étale cover pulled back from the
p
-multiplication map on the albanese variety, and one may guess (as a geometric analog of
loc. cit.
) that the essential dimension of this cover is at least the albanese dimension of the variety. However, the following example shows that this is not always true.

Example 18
.

Let
A
be an abelian variety of dimension
n
and let
C
be a smooth projective curve with a faithful
G:=(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{2n}
-action such that
C/G\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
. By identifying
G
with
A[p]
(which acts on
A
via translation), we get a diagonal
G
-action on
A\times C
without fixed point. Let
X:=(A\times C)/G
and consider the (étale) cover
f\colon A\times C\to X
. We have a Cartesian diagram

\textstyle{A\times C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A/G}

where the vertical arrows are induced by the natural projections and the bottom row can be identified with the
p
-multiplication map on
A\cong A/G
. We claim that the map
X\to A^{\prime}:=A/G
is the albanese morphism of
X
. For this it suffices to show that the induced map
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})_{\tf}\to H_{1}(A^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
between the torsion free part of the integral homology is an isomorphism.

Observe that the projection
X\to A^{\prime}
is a smooth fibration with (connected) fiber
C
, hence the induced map
\pi_{1}(X)\to\pi_{1}(A^{\prime})
is surjective by the homotopy exact sequence. This implies that the map
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(A^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
is surjective as well. It remains to show that
b_{1}(X)=b_{1}(A^{\prime})=2n
. By Hodge theory, this is also equivalent to
h^{1,0}(X)=n
, or
\dim H^{0}(\Omega^{1}_{A\times C})^{G}=n
. This can now be checked with a direct calculation, noting that by Künneth formula
H^{0}(\Omega^{1}_{A\times C})\cong H^{0}(\Omega^{1}_{A})\oplus H^{0}(\Omega^{1}_{C})
, the
G
-action is trivial on the first factor, while
H^{0}(\Omega^{1}_{C})^{G}=H^{0}(\Omega_{C/G}^{1})=H^{0}(\Omega_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}^{1})=0
. Thus we see that
X\to A^{\prime}
is the albanese morphism and the albanese dimension of
X
is equal to
n=\dim A^{\prime}
. The cover
f\colon A\times C\to X
is therefore the pullback of the
p
-multiplication map on the albanese variety by the previous diagram. We also have another Cartesian diagram

\textstyle{A\times C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{C/G}

similar to the one we have before. It gives
\ed(f)\leq\dim C=1
and in particular the essential dimension
\ed(f)
can be smaller than the albanese dimension.

Recall that a dominant morphism
f\colon X\to X
of normal, projective varieties is called a
polarized
(resp.
int-amplified
) endomorphism if there exists some ample line bundle
L
on
X
such that
f^{*}L=qL
some integer
q>1
(resp.
f^{*}L-L
is ample). Clearly, every polarized endomorphism is int-amplified. The simplest example is the multiplication-by-
p
map on abelian varieties. Similarly, on a normal toric variety, the
p
th power map extends from the open torus to a finite morphism of the toric variety. There are many other examples such as finite self-maps of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
(or of any variety of Picard number
1
). In positive characteristics there are also the Frobenius maps. We proved that the multiplication maps on abelian varieties are incompressible. The
p
th power maps on toric varieties are even
p
-incompressible for all
p
, see
[
21
]
*Theorem 1.2 or
[
2
]
*Proposition 10. Motivated by these results, it seems natural to ask:

Question 19
.

Is every polarized endomorphism incompressible?

Fakhruddin pointed out that int-amplified endormorphisms in characteristic zero and polarized endomorphisms in positive characteristic can have small essential dimension. To get an int-amplified example, choose pairwise coprime integers
a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}>1
, let
X=(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
and consider the endomorphism
f\colon X\to X

given coordinate-wise by the map
x\mapsto x^{a_{i}}
on the
i
-th factor. Then
f
is int-amplified but its essential dimension is one since its Galois group is cyclic. To get a polarized example in characteristic
p
, consider the endomorphism
g\colon X\to X
given by the Artin–Schreier cover
x\mapsto x^{p}-x
on each product factor. The Galois group is
(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{n}
which has essential dimension one by
[
17
]
. By
[
3
]
*Theorem 3.1(c), this implies that
\ed(g)=1
. For the same reason, any isogeny of abelian varieties with kernel
(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{n}
in characteristic
p
has essential dimension one; therefore, the statement of Corollary
3
does not hold in positive characteristic in general.

One limitation of our current approach is that it does not give much information about essential dimensions of morphisms between varieties of general type. It would be interesting to extend our geometric arguments to this more general setting. Such an extension may also be useful in addressing the
p
-incompressibility part of Brosnan’s conjecture.

The essential dimension

[

*Théorème 3.6 determines

More generally, one can ask which groups can act on

For

uses

.

.

Using Corollary

Assume that

(resp.

For local rings we get the following variant of the results in Section

Corollary 21
.

Let
R
be the local ring
(
or the completion of such
)
of a closed point on a variety of dimension
n
with rational singularities over a field of characteristic 0, and
G
an abelian
p
-group acting on
R
.
Then there is a subgroup
H\leq G
such that

H
has a fixed point on every
G
-equivariant resolution of
\operatorname{Spec}R
, and

\log_{p}[G:H]\leq\tfrac{n-1}{p-1}.

Therefore

\rank(G)\leq n+\tfrac{n-1}{p-1}\leq 2n-1.

Proof.

Let
Y\to\operatorname{Spec}R
be a
G
-equivariant resolution such that
the reduced preimage of the closed point is a simple normal crossing divisor
E\subset Y
. Note that
\chi(E,{\mathcal{O}}_{E})=1
by
[
25
]
since

R
has rational singularities.

Let
\{E_{i}:i\in I\}
be the irreducible components of
E
. For
1\leq m\leq n
set

E^{m}:=\textstyle{\amalg}_{J\subset I,|J|=m}\cap_{i\in J}E_{i},

with projection
\pi^{m}:E^{m}\to E
.
There is an exact sequence

0\to{\mathcal{O}}_{E}\to\pi^{1}_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{E^{1}}\to\pi^{2}_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{E^{2}}\to\cdots\to\pi^{n}_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{E^{n}}\to 0,

therefore

1=\chi(E,{\mathcal{O}}_{E})=\textstyle{\sum}_{m=1}^{n}(-1)^{m-1}\chi(E^{m},{\mathcal{O}}_{E^{m}}).

Thus at least one of the
\chi(E^{m},{\mathcal{O}}_{E^{m}})
is not divisible by
p
.
We can apply Proposition
8
to that

to get
H\leq G
with an
H
-fixed point on
E^{m}
. Its image by
\pi^{m}
is an
H
-fixed point on
Y
.
∎

Remark 22
.

Let
G
be an abelian
p
-group acting faithfully on a smooth, projective variety
X
of dimension
n
over a field of characteristic
0
.
Assume that
\chi(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})=\pm 1
or
\pm 2
. Then Corollary
9
implies that

[formula]

\rank G\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tfrac{p}{p-1}n\quad\mbox{if $p$ is odd, and}\\[4.30554pt]
2n+1\quad\mbox{if $p=2$.}\end{array}\right.

This covers many even dimensional Calabi-Yau varieties.
However, if
X
is a 3-dimensional Calabi-Yau variety, then

\chi(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})=0
, and our methods give no bound.

Nonetheless, Moraga conjectures that the bounds (

Note that the bounds (
22

2
-group of rank
5
acts on the K3 surface given by the intersection of three diagonal quadrics in

.

In another direction,
[

]
shows that an

-group action can not have a unique fixed point.
For these purposes,
[
13
]

develops a generalization of the Rost invariant

]
. Here we give an elementary proof.

Proposition 23
.

Let
G
be a reductive, abelian group acting on a proper variety
X
over an algebraically closed field
K
.
Assume that the component group
G/G_{0}
is a
p
-group,
\mathrm{char}(K)\neq p
,
G
has a smooth, isolated fixed point and
\dim X\geq 1
. Then
G
has at least 1 other fixed point.

Proof. Assume first that
\dim X=1
.
After normalization, we may assume that
X
is smooth.
Let
x\in X
be the fixed point.
Let
L
be a very ample,
G
-linearized line bundle on
X
.
Then

H^{0}(X,L^{p})\twoheadrightarrow H^{0}(X,L^{p}\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{X}/m_{x}^{2})\quad\mbox{is surjective.}\quad

Since
G
is reductive, any element in
m_{x}/m_{x}^{2}

lifts to a
G
-equivariant section

s\in H^{0}(X,L^{p})
.
Then
D:=(s=0)
is a 0-dimensional
G
-scheme whose degree is divisible by
p
,
and
\{x\}\subset D
is an irreducible component of degree 1.
Thus
D
has another irreducible component that is fixed by
G
and
whose degree is not divisible by
p
.
This gives another fixed point.

For
\dim X>1
we use induction on the dimension.
We may assume that
X
is projective.
Let
L
be a very ample,
G
-linearized line bundle on
X
.
Then

H^{0}(X,L)\twoheadrightarrow H^{0}(X,L\otimes{\mathcal{O}}_{X}/m_{x}^{2})\quad\mbox{is surjective.}\quad

Since
G
is reductive and abelian, it has an eigenvector on
m_{x}/m_{x}^{2}
,
which lifts to a
G
-equivariant section

s\in H^{0}(X,L)
. Thus
D:=(s=0)
is
G
-invariant and

x\in D
is a smooth, isolated fixed point.
There is a unique irreducible component
x\in D^{\prime}\subset D
.
By induction,
G
has at least 1 other fixed point on
D^{\prime}
, hence also on
X
. ∎
