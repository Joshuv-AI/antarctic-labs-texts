Complex Generalized Integral Means Spectrum of Drifted Whole-Plane SLE & LLE
Bertrand Duplantier, Yong Han, Chi Nguyen, Michel Zinsmeister

Abstract
. We present new exact results for the complex generalized integral means spectrum (in the sense of [ DHLZ18 ] ) for two kinds of whole-plane Loewner evolutions driven by a Lévy process: (1) The case of a Lévy process with continuous trajectories, which corresponds to Schramm-Loewner evolution SLE κ with a drift term in the Brownian driving function. There is no known result for its standard integral means spectrum, and we show that a natural path to access it goes through the introduction of the complex generalized integral means spectrum, which is obtained via the so-called Liouville quantum gravity. (2) The case of symmetric Lévy processes for which we generalize results by Loutsenko and Yermolayeva ( [ Lou12 , LY13 , LY14 , LY19 ] ).

Introduction

“Il apparut que, entre deux vérités du domaine réel, le chemin le plus facile et le plus court passe bien souvent par le domaine complexe.”[It came to appear that, between two truths of the real domain, the easiest and shortest path quite often passes through the complex domain] (Paul Painlevé, 1900)
[
Pai00
]
.

More than two decades ago, Oded Schramm
[
Sch00
]
introduced his celebrated theory of random growth processes SLE
κ
. As an example, in the so-called chordal case in the half-plane
\mathbb{H}
, it consists of the one-parameter family of Loewner processes driven on the real line
\partial\mathbb{H}
by
\sqrt{\kappa}B_{t}
, where
\kappa
is a nonnegative number and
B_{t}
is standard one-dimensional Brownian motion. This is the unique family of random processes satisfying a certain Markov property with continuous driving function, that is symmetric with respect to the imaginary axis. This theory may be generalized along two directions:

(1)

One can drop symmetry with respect to the imaginary axis: one then considers SLE
κ
with a
drift
term, e.g., the chordal Loewner process driven by a random function of the form

\lambda(t)=\sqrt{\kappa}B_{t}+at,

where
B_{t}
is as before standard one-dimensional Brownian motion and
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(2)

One can drop the continuity assumption while keeping symmetry: the process so obtained is Loewner evolution driven by a Lévy process, called LLE (for Lévy-Loewner evolution).

Notice that the first class of continuous drifted processes coincides with the whole class of LLE processes with continuous trajectories. For
\kappa=0
, the Loewner process generated by
\lambda(t)=at
becomes deterministic. Several deterministic chordal Loewner processes, driven by Lip-
1/2
functions, were investigated in
[
KNK04
,
MR05
,
Lin05
,
LMR10
]
.

In this paper, we shall consider both extended classes in the
whole-plane
case. In order to understand the multifractal spectra of these processes, such as their integral means spectra (

), and in the spirit of references
[
DNNZ15
,
DHLZ18
,
Ho16
,
Lou12
,
LY13
,
LY14
,
LY19
]
, we shall first investigate the cases for which the expected complex moments,

\mathbb{E}\left[|f^{\prime}(z)^{p}|\left|\left(\frac{z}{f(z)}\right)^{q}\right|\right],\,\,p,q\in\mathbb{C},

may be computed explicitly, to become part of
integrable probability
. Here
f
stands for the time
0
whole-plane map from
\mathbb{D}
to the slit plane in the corresponding Loewner process. Note that
complex
values of
(p,q)
are considered here in the case of whole-plane SLE with drift. In agreement with the citation by P. Painlevé above, the suggested passage by the complex plane will help us discover the precise form of the associated integral means spectrum in the case of SLE
κ
with drift, via its complex and generalized versions
[
DHLZ18
]
.

In Section 2, we shall make use of the so-called

1.1.
Interior whole-plane SLE

SLE is a particular case of a growth process called the Loewner process, of which several variants exist, known as
chordal
,
radial
,
dipolar
, or
whole-plane

[
Law05
,
Bel19
]
. In this work we will consider the
interior whole-plane
case, which is determined by a
driving function

\lambda:[0,+\infty)\to\partial\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
obtained as follows.
Let us start by defining
\gamma:\,[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
to be a continuous
function such that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}|\gamma(t)|=+\infty
and
\gamma(t)\neq 0,\forall t\geq 0
.
Then, for each
t>0
, the slit domain
\Omega_{t}=\mathbb{C}\backslash\gamma([t,\infty))
is a simply connected domain containing
0
. By the Riemann Mapping Theorem, there exists a unique conformal map
f_{t}:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega_{t}
such that
f_{t}(0)=0
and
f_{t}^{\prime}(0)>0
. By the Caratheodory convergence theorem,
f_{t}
converges to
f_{0}
, the Riemann mapping of
\Omega_{0}
, as
t\to 0
. We may assume without loss of generality that
f_{0}^{\prime}(0)=1
and, by re-parametrizing the curve if necessary, choose the normalization
f_{t}^{\prime}(0)=e^{t}
. Loewner’s theorem asserts that there exists a continuous function
\lambda
taking values in the unit circle such that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f_{t}(z)=z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}f_{t}(z)\frac{\lambda(t)+z}{\lambda(t)-z},\,\,\,\,\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow+\infty}f_{t}(e^{-t}z)=z,\forall z\in\mathbb{D}.

(1.1)

The Loewner method can be reversed: given a continuous function
\lambda:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow\partial\mathbb{D}
, the partial differential equation (
1.1
) has a unique solution
f_{t}(z)
, which is a conformal map from
\mathbb{D}
onto a domain
\Omega_{t}
, and the corresponding family
(\Omega_{t})_{t}
is increasing in
t
. Nevertheless the domains
\Omega_{t}
need not be slit domains as in the example above.

Whole-plane SLE
κ
is the process driven by

\lambda(t)=e^{i\sqrt{\kappa}B_{t}},

where
\kappa\in[0,+\infty)
and
B_{t}
is standard one-dimensional Brownian motion. Note that when
\kappa=0
,
f_{t}(z)=\frac{e^{t}z}{(1-z)^{2}}
is the solution to (
1.1
), so that
f_{0}
is the Koebe function. Thus, as
\kappa\to 0^{+}
, whole-plane SLE
κ
may be seen as a stochastic perturbation of the Koebe map.

In this work, we generalize SLE by adding a drift term to Brownian motion, with a driving function defined as

\lambda(t):=e^{i(\sqrt{\kappa}B_{t}+at)},a\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.2)

The process driven by
\lambda(t)
then appears for small
\kappa
as a stochastic perturbation of the
(\kappa=0,\,\,a\neq 0)
case of the
logarithmic spiral
.

1.2.
Complex generalized integral means spectrum

Let
f
be a conformal map from
\mathbb{D}
to
\mathbb{C}
with
f(0)=0,f^{\prime}(0)=1
. The generalized integral means spectrum of
f
was originally defined in
[
DHLZ18
]
as follows: for any pair of real numbers
(p,q)
, define the integral moments, for
r\in[0,1)
,

M_{f}(p,q):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}r^{q}\frac{|f^{\prime}(re^{i\theta})|^{p}}{|f(re^{i\theta})|^{q}}d\theta,\,\,r\in[0,1).

(1.3)

The
generalized integral means spectrum is then defined as

\beta_{f}(p,q):=\limsup_{r\to 1^{-}}\left[\log M_{f}(p,q)/\log\left((1-r)^{-1}\right)\right].

If the limit exists, then

M_{f}(p,q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\sim}}\left(1-r\right)^{-\beta_{f}(p,q)},\,\,\,r\to 1^{-},

(1.4)

where the notation ‘
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\sim}}
’ between two quantities stands for the equivalence of the logarithms of these quantities (i.e., their ratio tends to 1)
[
DHLZ18
]
.

One recovers for
q=0
the
standard integral means spectrum
,
\beta_{f}(p):=\beta_{f}(p,q=0)
, which is related by various Legendre transformations to the so-called
multifractal spectra

[
Man74
,
HP83
,
FP85
,
HJK+86a
,
HJK+86b
]
, like those governing the moments of the harmonic measure or the continuum of its local singularities
[
Mak98
,
GM08
]
.

For a random simply connected domain as arising from a whole-plane Loewner process with a random driving function like SLE, the question whether the equivalence (
1.4
)
holds
almost surely
is notoriously difficult. Earlier works dealt with the ‘expected spectrum’ for Brownian motion
[
LW99
,
Dup99b
]
, self-avoiding walk
[
Dup99b
]
, percolation
[
Dup99a
,
ASZ08
]
, and SLE
[
Dup00
,
Dup03
,
Has02
,
Dup04
,
Dup06
,
BRGW05
,
RBGW07
,
BS09
,
DNNZ15
,
BDZ17
]
as well as with the expected generalized spectrum of whole-plane SLE
[
DHLZ18
]
. The almost sure case was solved only recently for the standard spectrum of chordal SLE by Gwynne, Miller and Sun
[
GMS18
]
by using the so-called
imaginary geometry
of Miller and Sheffield. (See also the earlier works
[
JVL12
]
for the SLE a.s. tip spectrum,
[
ABV16
]
for the SLE a.s. boundary spectrum, and the recent work
[
Sch20
]
for the SLE
{}_{\kappa}(\rho)
a.s. boundary spectrum, where imaginary geometry was also used.)

The case of complex moments corresponds to the mixed multifractal spectrum of the harmonic measure and logarithmic rotations of the conformal map
[
Bin97
]
. It was studied in expectation in Refs.
[
DB02
,
DB08
,
BGIR08
]
for the chordal and radial SLE cases. We shall consider here the whole-plane spectrum defined in expectation for complex moments,

\int_{r\partial\mathbb{D}}\mathbb{E}\left[|f^{\prime}(z)^{p}|\left|\left(\frac{z}{f(z)}\right)^{q}\right|\right]|dz|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\sim}}\left(\frac{1}{1-r}\right)^{\beta(p,q)},\,\,\,p,q\in\mathbb{C}.

(1.5)

It is then natural to introduce the one-point function

G(z):=\mathbb{E}\left[|f^{\prime}(z)^{p}|\left|\left(\frac{z}{f(z)}\right)^{q}\right|\right],\,\,\,p,q\in\mathbb{C}.

(1.6)

The setting chosen in (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) allows for complex values
p,q\in\mathbb{C}
, which we shall need to study the drift case. In the more general case of Lévy processes, we shall see that their defining properties are exactly those needed to obtain a PDE satisfied by
G
(
1.6
), as initiated in Refs.
[
Has02
,
BS09
]
and further developed in Refs.
[
DNNZ15
,
BDZ17
,
DHLZ18
]
and
[
Lou12
,
LY13
,
LY14
,
LY19
]
.

1.3.
Interior-exterior duality

As mentioned in
[
DHLZ18
]
, it is interesting to remark that the map
\widehat{f}
,

\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}\mapsto\widehat{f}(\zeta):=1/f(1/\zeta),

is just the
exterior
whole-plane map from
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
to the slit plane considered in Ref.
[
BS09
]
by Beliaev and Smirnov and in Ref.
[
BDZ17
]
. We identically have for
0<r<1
and
p\in\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{r^{-1}\partial\mathbb{D}}\mathbb{E}\left(|\widehat{f}^{\prime}(\zeta)|^{p}\right)|d\zeta|=r^{2p-2}\int_{r\partial\mathbb{D}}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{|f^{\prime}(z)|^{p}}{|f(z)|^{2p}}\right)|dz|.

(1.7)

We thus see that the standard integral mean of order
(p,q=0)
for the exterior whole-plane map studied in
[
BS09
,
BDZ17
]

coincides
(up to an irrelevant power of
r
) with the
(p,q^{\prime})
integral mean for
q^{\prime}=2p
, for the interior whole-plane map.

Remark 1.1
.

Interior-Exterior Duality
. By conformal inversion, we have for any
p,q\in\mathbb{C}
,

\int_{r^{-1}\partial\mathbb{D}}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{\widehat{f}^{\prime}(\zeta)^{p}}{\widehat{f}(\zeta)^{q}}\right|\right)|d\zeta|=r^{2\Re p-2}\int_{r\partial\mathbb{D}}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{f^{\prime}(z)^{p}}{f(z)^{2p-q}}\right|\right)|dz|,

(1.8)

so that the
(p,q)
exterior integral means spectrum coincides with the
(p,q^{\prime})
interior integral means spectrum for
q^{\prime}-p=p-q
. In particular, the
(p\in\mathbb{R},q^{\prime}=0)
interior derivative moments studied in Ref.
[
DNNZ15
]
correspond to the
(p,q=2p)
mixed moments of the exterior map.

1.4.
Generalized spectrum for the logarithmic spiral

In this section we give an example of a generalized integral means spectrum, which is deterministic and corresponds to the
\kappa=0
case of the drifted SLE
κ
. It is nothing but the logarithmic spiral with parameter
a\in\mathbb{R}
(Fig.
1
), i.e., the curve parametrized by

\gamma(t)=\exp\left[(1+ia)t\right],\,\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.9)

Figure 1.
Logarithmic spiral
\gamma(t)
(
1.9
) for
a=+5
, restricted to
t\geq 0
.

1.4.1.
Loewner process for the logarithmic spiral

Let us define, as before,
\Omega_{t}:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\gamma[t,+\infty)
and let
f_{t}:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega_{t}
be the associated Riemann map, i.e., the conformal map such that

f_{t}(0)=0,f_{t}^{\prime}(0)>0.

(1.10)

By the Koebe distorsion theorem,
\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}f_{t}^{\prime}(0)=0
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}f_{t}^{\prime}(0)=+\infty
. Then, there exists
t_{0}
such that
f_{t_{0}}^{\prime}(0)=1
. One also has that
f_{t_{0}}(e^{i\theta_{0}})=\gamma(t_{0})=e^{(1+ia)t_{0}}
for some
\theta_{0}\in[0,2\pi).
Consider now the function
\tilde{f}_{t}
defined by

\tilde{f}_{t}(z):=e^{(1+ia)t}f_{t_{0}}(e^{-iat}z).

We have

\tilde{f}_{t}(0)=0,\tilde{f}_{t}^{\prime}(0)=e^{t},
and

\tilde{f}_{t}(e^{i(\theta_{0}+at)})=e^{(1+ia)(t+t_{0})}=\gamma(t+t_{0}).

(1.11)

Hence from (
1.11
),
\tilde{f}_{t}:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega_{t+t_{0}}
is the Loewner process corresponding to the curve

\tilde{\gamma}(t):=\gamma(t+t_{0}),\,\,t\in\mathbb{R},

with the associated driving function
\tilde{\lambda}(t):=e^{i(\theta_{0}+at)}
.

Define then the curve,

\eta(t):=e^{-i\theta_{0}}\tilde{\gamma}(t),\,\,\,t\in\mathbb{R},

and the conformal map,

h_{t}(z):=e^{-i\theta_{0}}\tilde{f}_{t}(e^{i\theta_{0}}z).

One still has
h_{t}(0)=0,h_{t}^{\prime}(0)=e^{t},
and
h_{t}(e^{iat})=\eta(t)
, so that
h_{t}
is the Loewner map corresponding to
\eta(t)
and the associated process is driven by
\lambda(t):=e^{iat}
.

Notice that the curve
\eta
is obtained by a time-translation and a rotation of the logarithmic spiral
\gamma
. Thus the integral means spectrum will be the same for the time zero Loewner maps
h_{0}
and
\tilde{f}_{0}
.

1.4.2.
Complex generalized spectrum for the complete logarithmic spiral

We first focus on the
complete
spiral, for which we first establish the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

The complex generalized integral means spectrum of the
complete
logarithmic spiral
\gamma(t)=e^{(1+ia)t},\,t\in\mathbb{R}
, is given, for
p,q\in\mathbb{C}
, by

\displaystyle\beta(p,q;a)=\sup\left\{0,\beta_{1}(p,q;0,a),\beta_{2}(p,q;0,a)\right\},

(1.12)

where

\begin{split}&\beta_{1}(p,q;0,a):=2\Re{\left(\frac{p-q}{1-ia}\right)}+\Re{p}-1,\\
&\beta_{2}(p,q;0,a):=-2\Re{\left(\frac{p-q}{1-ia}\right)}+\Re{p}-1.\end{split}

(1.13)

Proof.

Let us define on the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
, the Moebius map
\xi:z\mapsto\xi(z):=i\frac{1-z}{1+z}
, and consider the function
\Phi
defined on
\mathbb{D}
as,

\Phi(z):=\exp\left[\frac{2}{1-ia}\log\xi(z)\right]=\left(i\frac{1-z}{1+z}\right)^{\frac{2(1+ia)}{1+a^{2}}},\,\,\,z\in\mathbb{D}.

Define also the strip domain
{\mathbb{S}}_{\pi}:=\{x+iy:x\in\mathbb{R},\,\,0<y<\pi\}
. We know that
z\mapsto\xi(z)
conformally maps
\mathbb{D}
onto upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
,
while
z\mapsto\log(z)
conformally maps
\mathbb{H}
onto the strip
{\mathbb{S}}_{\pi}
. Lastly,
z\mapsto\exp\left(\frac{2}{1-ia}z\right)
conformally maps the strip domain
{\mathbb{S}}_{\pi}
onto
\mathbb{C}\setminus\gamma
, with a cut along the whole logarithmic spiral
\gamma:=\{\gamma(t)=e^{(1+ia)t},t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
.
Consequently,
\Phi
is a conformal map from the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
to the complement of the
whole
logarithmic spiral
\gamma
, with
\Phi(1)=0,\Phi(-1)=\infty
.

It enjoys the useful property,

\Phi^{\prime}(z)=\frac{2}{1-ia}\big(\log\xi(z)\big)^{\prime}\Phi(z)=-\frac{4}{1-ia}\frac{\Phi(z)}{1-z^{2}}.

(1.14)

Owing to (
1.14
),the complex mixed moments of
\Phi
read

\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(z)^{p}}{\Phi(z)^{q}}=\left(-\frac{4}{1-ia}\frac{1}{1-z^{2}}\right)^{p}\,\frac{1}{\Phi(z)^{q-p}},

(1.15)

so that

\left|\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(z)^{p}}{\Phi(z)^{q}}\right|=\left|\left(-\frac{4}{1-ia}\right)^{p}\right|\times\frac{\left|\Phi(z)^{p-q}\right|}{\left|(1-z^{2})^{p}\right|}.

(1.16)

We have explicitly

\left|(1-z^{2})^{p}\right|=\left|1-z\right|^{\Re p}\left|1+z\right|^{\Re p}e^{-\Im p\,\arg(1-z^{2})},

(1.17)

and

\left|\Phi(z)^{p-q}\right|=\exp\Re\left[\frac{2(p-q)}{1-ia}\log\xi(z)\right].

(1.18)

Setting
b=b(p,q):=\frac{2(p-q)}{1-ia}
, we have
\Re\left[b\log\xi(z)\right]=\Re b\,\log|\xi(z)|-\Im b\,\Im\log\xi(z)
, and since
\log\xi(z)\in{\mathbb{S}}_{\pi}
, its imaginary part stays
bounded
.
We thus have the following (logarithmic) equivalence near the two possible singular points
z=\pm 1
,

\left|\Phi(z)^{p-q}\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\sim}}|\xi(z)|^{\Re b(p,q)}=\left|\frac{1-z}{1+z}\right|^{\Re\frac{2(p-q)}{1-ia}}.

(1.19)

Using (
1.16
), (
1.17
), and (
1.19
), we finally arrive at

\left|\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(z)^{p}}{\Phi(z)^{q}}\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\sim}}\left|\frac{1-z}{1+z}\right|^{\Re\frac{2(p-q)}{1-ia}}\left|1-z\right|^{-\Re p}\left|1+z\right|^{-\Re p}.

(1.20)

Behaviour near infinity and near the origin.
For
z=re^{i\theta}
near
z=-1
(point at
\infty
on the spiral),
|1+z|^{2}=r^{2}+2r\cos\theta+1
behaves like
(1-r)^{2}+(\pi-\theta)^{2}
. Similarly, near
z=+1
(point
0
on the spiral),

|1-z|^{2}
behaves like
(1-r)^{2}+\theta^{2}
. The integral of (
1.20
) along the circle
|z|=r
for
r\to 1^{-}

is thus dominated near
z=-1
by the contribution of the angular neighbourhood of
\theta=\pi
, while near
z=+1
it is symmetrically dominated by that of the angular neighbourhood of
\theta=0
. From the explicit form of the integrand (
1.20
), we readily obtain the overall asymptotic behaviour as
r\to 1^{-}
of the integral means,

\int_{r\partial\mathbb{D}}\left|\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(z)^{p}}{\Phi(z)^{q}}\right||dz|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{\sim}}\left(1-r\right)^{-\beta_{\Phi}(p,q;a)},\,\,\,r\to 1^{-},

(1.21)

where the integral means spectrum
\beta_{\Phi}
is given by the largest exponent,

\displaystyle\beta_{\Phi}(p,q;a):=\beta_{1}(p,q;0,a)\vee\beta_{2}(p,q;0,a)\vee 0,

(1.22)

with the two dual spectra defined as,

\displaystyle\beta_{1}(p,q;0,a):=2\Re\frac{p-q}{1-ia}+\Re p-1,

(1.23)

\displaystyle\beta_{2}(p,q;0,a):=2\Re\frac{q-p}{1-ia}+\Re p-1.

(1.24)

∎

Remark 1.2
.

Singularity localization.

Exponent
\beta_{1}
is associated with the singularity near
z=-1
on
\mathbb{D}
in (
1.20
), i.e., at
infinity
on the spiral, while
\beta_{2}
corresponds to that near
z=+1
, i.e.,
near the tip at origin
0
, around which the spiral
indefinitely winds
.

Remark 1.3
.

Conformal invariance by inversion and duality.

The full logarithmic spiral is conformally invariant under the complex inversion,
z\mapsto 1/z
, since
1/\gamma(t)=\gamma(-t)
, and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. This inversion exchanges the roles of origin and infinity, and maps
the interior of
\mathbb{D}
to its exterior. The complex generalized integral means spectrum then obeys the
duality property
(
1.1
). Spectra (
1.23
) and (
1.24
) are indeed dual of each other under the corresponding exchange
q-p\mapsto p-q
, resulting in the expected invariance under duality of the integral means spectrum
\beta_{\Phi}
(
1.22
) for the complete logarithmic spiral.

1.4.3.
Complex generalized spectrum of the half spiral

Consider now
h_{0}(z)
, the conformal map corresponding to the whole-plane Loewner process driven by

e^{iat}
, stopped at time

, the image of which,
\gamma(t)=e^{(1+ia)t},\,t\geq 0
, we may call the
half spiral
(Fig.
1
). The complex generalized integral means spectrum of the half logarithmic spiral is given by the following theorem. (See Fig.

Theorem 1.2
.

The
complex
generalized integral means spectrum of
h_{0}
, where
h_{t}
is the whole-plane Loewner process driven by
\lambda(t)=e^{iat}
, and whose trace is
the
half
logarithmic spiral

\displaystyle\beta(p,q;\kappa=0,a)=\sup\left\{-\Re{p}-1,0,\beta_{1}(p,q;0,a)=2\Re{\left(\frac{p-q}{1-ia}\right)}+\Re{p}-1\right\}.

(1.25)

From this, one immediately deduces the following corollary, which yields the
real
generalized integral means spectrum of the half spiral.

Corollary 1.1
.

The
real
generalized integral means spectrum of
h_{0}
, where
h_{t}
is the whole-plane Loewner process driven by
\lambda(t)=e^{iat}

2.4.

2.4.1.
Discourse on the Method

We are going to use here a
Liouville quantum gravity
(LQG) approach, which historically gave the first derivation of the standard SLE multifractal spectrum
[
Dup00
]
, which was later confirmed by a standard mathematical approach
[
BS09
,
BDZ17
,
GMS18
]
. It is based on the celebrated Knizhnik-Polyakov-Zamolodchikov (KPZ) relation
[
KPZ88
,
Dav88
,
DK89
]
between scaling exponents in the Euclidean plane, and their counterparts under a random LQG measure that gives the scaling limit of the area measure on a random planar map. The KPZ relation is now mathematically proved
[
DS11a
,
RV11
,
DRSV14
]
. Although the LQG method, which originates in theoretical physics, is
heuristic
and not fully rigorous, it often offers the quickest and most natural path to the derivation of scaling exponents and multifractal spectra. It is also intimately related to the recently developed and rigorous
wedge-welding
theory in Liouville quantum gravity
[
She16
,
DMS21
]
(See in particular Appendix B in
[
DMS21
]
for a mathematically precise description of the KPZ interpretation.)

2.4.2.
Derivation of Claim
2.1

Let us first recall that in the original work on whole-plane SLE
[
DNNZ15
]
, the novel integral means spectrum,
\beta_{1}(p;\kappa):=\beta_{1}(p;q=0;\kappa,a=0)
, derived there for
p\in\mathbb{R}
, was related to some Liouville quantum gravity results obtained in
[
Dup04
]
. (See
[
DNNZ15
, Section 1.3]
.) It was found that the related
packing spectrum
, defined as,

\displaystyle s_{1}(p;\kappa):=\beta_{1}(p;\kappa)-p+1,

(2.20)

is given by

\displaystyle s_{1}(p)=s_{1}(p;\kappa):=2p+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+2\kappa p}.

(2.21)

When seen as a function of
p
, it has for inverse in terms of
s=s_{1}
,

p=\frac{s}{2}+\frac{\kappa}{8}{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}_{\kappa}(s),

where we defined

\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}_{\kappa}(x):=\frac{1}{2\kappa}\left(\kappa-4+\sqrt{(4-\kappa)^{2}+16\kappa x}\right),

\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}_{\kappa}(x):=\frac{1}{4}x\left(\kappa x+4-\kappa\right),

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}(x):={\mathcal{U}}_{\kappa}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(x+1-\frac{\kappa}{4}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{16\kappa}\left[\kappa^{2}x^{2}-(4-\kappa)^{2}\right].

Here
{\mathcal{U}}_{\kappa}
is the KPZ function of Liouville quantum gravity adapted to SLE
κ
, while
{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}
is an associated function that relates boundary scaling dimensions to bulk ones
[
Dup04
,
Dup06
]
. Here we generalize methods introduced in
[
Dup00
,
DB02
]
and expounded in
[
Dup04
,
Dup06
]
, and use notations similar to those of
[
Dup04
]
, Section 8. For simplicity, we first implicitly assume SLE paths to be simple, i.e., with
\kappa\leq 4
, since the quantum gravity composition rules differ for the simple and non-simple phases of SLE
[
Dup04
,
Dup06
]
. Nevertheless, the results obtained also hold for
\kappa>4
. One has the set of identities,

\displaystyle p=x_{1}(s)-x_{1},

(2.22)

\displaystyle x_{1}(s):=2{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}\left[{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}_{\kappa}(s)+{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}_{\kappa}(\tilde{x}_{1})\right],

(2.23)

\displaystyle x_{1}:=x_{1}(0)=\frac{1}{8\kappa}(6-\kappa)(2-\kappa),

(2.24)

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{1}:=\frac{6-\kappa}{2\kappa},\,\,\,{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}_{\kappa}(\tilde{x}_{1})=\frac{2}{\kappa}.

(2.25)

The scaling exponent
x_{1}(s)
geometrically corresponds to a configuration where the SLE
tip
is locally avoiding a bunch of
s

independent Brownian paths
. The tip here should be understood as the so-called SLE ‘second tip’ at the origin
[
BDZ17
]
, after inversion of unbounded (interior) whole-plane SLE
[
DNNZ15
]
, as in Beliaev and Smirnov’s bounded (exterior) version of whole-plane SLE
[
BS09
,
BDZ17
]
.

In the LQG approach,
s
independent Brownian paths
avoiding
an SLE path near its tip are conformally equivalent to a certain number
k(s)
of
mutually-avoiding
SLEs in a star configuration, given by

k(s)=1+\frac{{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}_{\kappa}(s)}{{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}_{\kappa}(\tilde{x}_{1})},

(2.26)

such that
x_{1}(s)=2{\mathcal{V}}_{\kappa}(2k(s)/\kappa)
. When
p\in\mathbb{C}
, its imaginary part
\tilde{t}:=\Im p
corresponds to exponentially weighting by
\exp(\tilde{t}\arg\mathcal{C})
the mutually-avoiding SLE-Brownian path configurations
\mathcal{C}
, with local
winding angle

\arg\mathcal{C}
around the tip. One can then show by Coulomb gas arguments
[
DB02
,
Dup04
,
DB08
]
that the new scaling exponent associated with the tip is

\hat{x}_{1}(s,\tilde{t}):=x_{1}(s)-\frac{\kappa}{2}\frac{\tilde{t}^{\,2}}{k^{2}(s)}.

(2.27)

The average logarithmic spiral rotation rate
a
near the tip is then obtained by Legendre transformation as
[
DB02
,
Dup04
]
,

a=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{t}}\,\hat{x}_{1}(s,\tilde{t}).

(2.28)

On the other hand, the real part of
p
,
t:=\Re p
, is now given by the generalization of (
2.22
),

\Re p=t=\hat{x}_{1}(s,\tilde{t})-x_{1},

(2.29)

whereas the packing spectrum for complex
p
,
s=s_{1}(p;\kappa)=\beta_{1}(p;\kappa)-\Re p+1
, is still given by (
2.21
), but now in terms of the reduced variable
\tau
,

\displaystyle\tau:=x_{1}(s)-x_{1},

(2.30)

\displaystyle s=s_{1}(\tau)=2\tau+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+2\kappa\tau}.

(2.31)

From Eqs. (
2.23
), (
2.26
), we find the simple identity
[
DB02
,

3.
Integrable probability for drifted whole-plane SLE

In order to anticipate the next section, let us put the computations in a more general setting.

3.1.
Some background on Lévy processes

Definition 3.1
.

A Lévy process is a stochastic process
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that

(1)

L_{0}=0
(a.s);

(2)

For any discrete ordered set
\left\{t_{i},\,i\in I_{n}:=\{0,\cdots,n\}\right\}
, such that
t_{0}=0
and
0\leq t_{i}<t_{i+1},\,\forall i\in I_{n-1}
, the successive increments ,
L_{t_{i+1}}-L_{t_{i}},\,\,i\in I_{n-1}
,
are all mutually independent;

(3)

For any
0\leq s\leq t
,
L_{t}-L_{s}
has the same law as
L_{t-s}
.

(4)

L_{t}
is continuous in probability,
\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbb{P}(|L_{t}-L_{0}|>\varepsilon)=0,\,\,\,\forall\varepsilon>0
, which rules out fixed discontinuities of the path
t\mapsto L_{t}
.

Notice that Brownian motion is a special Lévy process, and a general difference with Brownian motion is that
random jumps
are allowed. The characteristic function of a Lévy process
L_{t}
has the form

\mathbb{E}[e^{i\xi L_{t}}]=e^{-t\eta(\xi)},

(3.1)

where
\eta
, called the
Lévy symbol
, is a continuous complex function of
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
, satisfying
\eta(0)=0
and
\eta(-\xi)=\overline{\eta(\xi)}
. If
\eta(-\xi)=\eta(\xi)
,
L_{t}
is a
symmetric Lévy process
. For Brownian motion, the Lévy symbol is
\eta(\xi)=\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}
. More generally, the function

\eta(\xi)=\frac{|\xi|^{\alpha}}{2},\alpha\in(0,2],

is the Lévy symbol of the so-called
\alpha-
stable process.

3.2.
Derivation of the PDE

The inner whole-plane Loewner process is defined as the solution of the ODE in
\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}g_{t}(z)=g_{t}(z)\frac{g_{t}(z)+\lambda(t)}{g_{t}(z)-\lambda(t)},\,\,\,t\geq 0,\\
\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow+\infty}e^{t}g_{t}(z)=z,\,\,\,\,\forall z\in\mathbb{C},\end{cases}

(3.2)

with driving function
\lambda(t)=e^{iL_{t}}
where
L_{t}
is real-valued;
g_{t}
is a conformal mapping from a simply connected domain
\Omega_{t}\subset\mathbb{C}
onto
\mathbb{D}
, where
\Omega_{t}
is defined as
\Omega_{t}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\tau_{z}>t\right\}
, where

\tau_{z}:=\sup\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\inf_{s\leq t}|g_{s}(z)-\lambda(s)|>0\right\}.

Its inverse function
f_{t}:=g_{t}^{-1}
obeys the PDE (
1.1
),

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f_{t}(z)=zf_{t}^{\prime}(z)\frac{\lambda(t)+z}{\lambda(t)-z}\\
\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow+\infty}f_{t}(e^{-t}z)=z,\,\,\,\,\forall z\in\mathbb{D},\end{cases}

(3.3)

where
f_{t}
is now a mapping from
\mathbb{D}
to the domain
\Omega_{t}=\mathbb{C}\setminus K_{t}
, where the connected set
K_{t}
is the hull of the Loewner process. In this section, we will assume
L_{t},t\geq 0
, to be a
Lévy process
. The (complex) average integral means spectrum of the conformal map
f=f_{0}
, where
f_{t}
is defined by (
3.3
), describes the singular behavior of the expectation,

\mathbb{E}[|f^{\prime}(z)^{p}|]=\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}(z)^{\frac{p}{2}}\overline{f^{\prime}(z)}^{\frac{\bar{p}}{2}}\right],\quad p\in\mathbb{C}.

(3.4)

Similarly to the method used in
[

]
, we shall consider the Lévy-Loewner evolution (LLE)

for

The moment (

Let us define the auxiliary, time-dependent, radial variant of the LLE two-point function

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(z_{1},\bar{z}_{2},t)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[z_{1}^{\frac{q}{2}}\frac{\tilde{f}^{\prime}_{t}(z_{1})^{\frac{p}{2}}}{\tilde{f}_{t}(z_{1})^{\frac{q}{2}}}\overline{z}_{2}^{\frac{\bar{q}}{2}}\frac{\overline{\tilde{f}^{\prime}_{t}(z_{2})}^{\frac{\bar{p}}{2}}}{\overline{\tilde{f}_{t}(z_{2})}^{\frac{\bar{q}}{2}}}\right]

(3.12)

p=\bar{p}=2
,
which we shall hereafter assume. Under this condition, the above equation becomes

\Lambda[(1-z)(1-\bar{z})h]+(z+1)(\bar{z}-1)z\partial_{z}h+(\bar{z}+1)(z-1)\bar{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}h\\
+\left[(z-1)(3-\bar{q})\bar{z}+(\bar{z}-1)(3-q)z\right]h=0.

(4.1)

Besides the restriction to
p=2
, we shall also assume that
q\in{\mathbb{R}}
and that the Lévy process
L_{t}
is symmetric. We then get

\Lambda[(1-z)(1-\bar{z})h]+(z+1)(\bar{z}-1)z\partial_{z}h+(\bar{z}+1)(z-1)\bar{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}h\\
+(3-q)(2z\bar{z}-z-\bar{z})h=0.

(4.2)

In order to analyze this equation, we use the Fourier expansion of
t\mapsto h(re^{it},re^{-it})
:

h(z,\bar{z})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\theta_{n}(\xi)z^{n},\;\xi:=z\bar{z},\,z=re^{it}.

(4.3)

When replacing
h
by this expansion in the equation, we get a recursion formula between the
\theta_{n}
’s for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
More precisely, by writing that the
n
’th Fourier coefficient of the left side of (
4.2
) vanishes, we obtain for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle 2\xi(\xi-1)\theta_{n}^{\prime}(\xi)-\Big(\eta_{n}+n+(\eta_{n}+2q-n-6)\xi\Big)\theta_{n}

\displaystyle+\xi\Big(\eta_{n}+n+q-2\Big)\theta_{n+1}(\xi)+(\eta_{n}-n+q-2)\theta_{n-1}(\xi)=0.

(4.4)

Note that the assumption that
L_{t}
is symmetric implies that
h(z,\bar{z})
is symmetric w.r.t.
z
and
\bar{z}
, which translates into,

\theta_{-n}(\xi)=\xi^{n}\theta_{n}(\xi),

(4.5)

from which we may simply recast expansion (
4.3
) above as

h(z,\bar{z})=\theta_{0}(\xi)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\theta_{n}(\xi)(z^{n}+\bar{z}^{n})=\theta_{0}(\xi)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2\theta_{n}(\xi)r^{n}\cos{nt}.

(4.6)

Before continuing, let us recall that we are looking for the integral means, i.e., the angular integrals,

I(r)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}G(re^{it},re^{-it})dt,

that can be easily expressed in terms of
\theta_{j}
’s as

\frac{I(r)}{2\pi}=(1+r^{2})\theta_{0}(r^{2})-2r^{2}\theta_{1}(r^{2}),

(4.7)

so that we only need to compute
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
. For later purposes, let us also mention that
\theta_{0}(0)=1
.
We thus focus on the equations for
n=0
and
n=1
(recall that
\eta_{0}=0
and that
\theta_{-1}(\xi)=\xi\theta_{1}(\xi)
),

\displaystyle(\xi-1)\theta_{0}^{\prime}(\xi)-(q-3)\theta_{0}(\xi)+(q-2)\theta_{1}(\xi)=0,

(4.8)

\displaystyle 2\xi(\xi-1)\theta_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)-\left[\eta_{1}+1+(\eta_{1}+2q-7)\xi\right]\theta_{1}(\xi)+(\eta_{1}+q-1)\xi\theta_{2}(\xi)

\displaystyle+(\eta_{1}+q-3)\theta_{0}(\xi)=0.

(4.9)

There are two simple cases where we can explicitly compute
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
.

(1)

The first case is when the coefficient of the
\theta_{0}
-term in the second equation vanishes, i.e., when

\eta_{1}=3-q,

(4.10)

which requires
q<3
.
In this case we may take
\theta_{1}=0
(and actually
\theta_{n}=0
for
n\geq 1
) and
\theta_{0}
to be the solution to

(\xi-1)\theta_{0}^{\prime}(\xi)-(q-3)\theta_{0}(\xi)=0,\;\theta_{0}(0)=1.

This gives

h(z,\bar{z})=\theta_{0}(\xi)=\frac{1}{(1-\xi)^{3-q}},

(4.11)

and

G(z,\bar{z})=\frac{(1-z)(1-\bar{z})}{(1-z\bar{z})^{3-q}},

so that
\beta(2,q)=3-q>0
.

(2)

The second case is by letting the coefficient of the
\theta_{2}
-term vanish in the second equation, i.e., by taking

\eta_{1}=1-q,

(4.12)

which requires
q\leq 1
.
We then get a system of coupled ODEs for
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
, which we must solve with initial data
\theta_{0}(0)=1
,
\theta_{1}(0)
finite. We find

\begin{cases}\theta_{0}(\xi)=(1+\xi)(1-\xi)^{-(4-q)}\\[11.38109pt]
\theta_{1}(\xi)=-\frac{2}{2-q}(1-\xi)^{-(4-q)},\end{cases}

(4.13)

from which we deduce that
\beta(2,q)=4-q(>3)
.

Eqs. (
4.11
) and (
4.13
) generalize results of Ref.
[
LY14
]
to
q\neq 0
.

As noticed in
[
LY19
]
, the preceding method generalizes: more precisely for any
n\geq 1
, if we let the coefficient of the
\theta_{n-1}
-term vanish in the
n
th equation, i.e., by taking
\eta_{n}=2-q+n
, then the solution of the system is
\theta_{p}=0
for
p\geq n
, while
\theta_{0},..\theta_{n-1}
are the solutions of the
n
first equations, with the initial data
\theta_{0}(0)=1
.

Another possible generalization is by letting vanish, again in the
n
th equation, the coefficient of the
\theta_{n+1}
-term, i.e., by taking
\eta_{n}=2-q-n
; then the
n
first equations allow us to compute
\theta_{0},..\theta_{n-1}
, which is more than needed since we only need to know
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
. Having dealt in the last section with the
n=1
case, let us now investigate the
n=2
case.

4.1.
The
\eta_{2}=4-q
case

We take
\theta_{n\geq 2}=0
, and have to solve the following system of differential equations:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(x-1)\theta_{0}^{\prime}(x)+(3-q)\theta_{0}(x)+(q-2)\theta_{1}(x)=0\\
2x(x-1)\theta_{1}^{\prime}(x)-\left[1+\eta_{1}+(\eta_{1}+2q-7)x\right]\theta_{1}(x)+(\eta_{1}-3+q)\theta_{0}(x)=0.\end{cases}

We can assume the existence of a real number
\delta
(to be determined later) and of two functions
f_{0},f_{1}:\,[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
have the following form,

\theta_{0}(x)=(1-x)^{-\delta}f_{0}(x),\quad\theta_{1}(x)=(1-x)^{-\delta}f_{1}(x).

The ODE system satisfied by
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
is then

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(x-1)f_{0}^{\prime}+(3-q-\delta)f_{0}+(q-2)f_{1}=0\\
2x(x-1)f_{1}^{\prime}-\left[1+\eta_{1}+(\eta_{1}+2q-7+2\delta)x\right]f_{1}+(\eta_{1}-3+q)f_{0}=0.\end{cases}

(4.14)

The form of the equation coefficients suggests we define
\delta^{\prime}
as,

\delta^{\prime}:=\delta+q-3,

(4.15)

so that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(x-1)f_{0}^{\prime}-\delta^{\prime}f_{0}+(q-2)f_{1}=0\\
2x(x-1)f_{1}^{\prime}-\big(1+\eta_{1}+(\eta_{1}-1+2\delta^{\prime})x\big)f_{1}+(\eta_{1}-3+q)f_{0}=0.\end{cases}

(4.16)

From the first equation we extract
f_{1}
as a function of
f_{0}
and
f_{0}^{\prime}
for
q\neq 2
,

f_{1}(x)=\frac{x-1}{2-q}f_{0}^{\prime}(x)-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2-q}f_{0}(x),\quad q\neq 2.

(4.17)

Substituting into (
4.16
), we obtain the following degree two differential equation satisfied by
f_{0}
,

\displaystyle\frac{2x(x-1)^{2}}{2-q}f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\left(2x(x-1)\frac{1-\delta^{\prime}}{2-q}-\frac{x-1}{2-q}\left(1+\eta_{1}+(\eta_{1}-1+2\delta^{\prime})x\right)\right)f_{0}^{\prime}(x)+

\displaystyle\left(\eta_{1}+q-3+\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2-q}\left(1+\eta_{1}+(\eta_{1}-1+2\delta^{\prime})x\right)\right)f_{0}(x)=0.

(4.18)

We want to find
\delta^{\prime}
such that this equation reads

\displaystyle x(x-1)f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)+((a+b+1)x-c)f_{0}^{\prime}(x)+abf_{0}(x)=0,

(4.19)

so that
f_{0}(x)=\,_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c;x)
, the hypergeometric function.
This identification shows that
\delta^{\prime}
must obey the following relation,

\displaystyle E(\delta^{\prime},\eta_{1},q):=2\delta^{\prime}(\delta^{\prime}+\eta_{1})+(2-q)(\eta_{1}+q-3)=0,

(4.20)

so that (
4.1
) simplifies into

\displaystyle x(x-1)f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\left(\frac{3-\eta_{1}-4\delta^{\prime}}{2}x-\frac{1+\eta_{1}}{2}\right)f_{0}^{\prime}(x)

(4.21)

\displaystyle-\delta^{\prime}\,\frac{1-\eta_{1}-2\delta^{\prime}}{2}f_{0}(x)=0.

(4.22)

This yields

\displaystyle a=-\delta^{\prime},\quad b=\frac{1-\eta_{1}}{2}-\delta^{\prime},\quad c=\frac{1+\eta_{1}}{2},

(4.23)

and we can choose
f_{0}(x)=\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a,b;c;x)
and
\theta_{0}(x)=(1-x)^{-\delta}\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a,b;c;x)
.
The other independent solution to the hypergeometric equation is

x^{1-c}\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(1+a-c,1+b-c;2-c;x),

which is
non-analytic
at the origin
x=0
, hence is discarded as a candidate for
f_{0}
.

Before continuing, let us consider general symmetric Lévy processes: what are the possible couples
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
? We know that we must have
\eta_{2}\geq 0
and the Lévy-Khinchine formula implies that
\eta_{1}\geq\eta_{2}/4
. It happens that every couple
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
such that
\eta_{1}\geq\eta_{2}/4\geq 0
actually corresponds to some (symmetric) Lévy process
[
App09
]

. Moreover the case
\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}/4
exactly corresponds to an SLE process, while in the case
\eta_{2}=0
,

is a pure jump process with jumps equal to
k\pi
,
k
odd (notice that the other case is similar with
k
even, yielding a continuous process on the circle).

For
\eta_{2}=4-q
, the preceeding constraints on
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
become
q\leq 4
and
\eta_{1}\geq\eta_{2}/4=1-q/4
, with equality for SLE
κ
, with

. Let us then define

D_{4-q}:=\{(q,\eta_{1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2};\,q\leq 4,\,\eta_{1}\geq 1-q/4\}.

(4.24)

Let us return to Eq. (
4.20
). It can be shown (see below) that
\eta_{1}^{2}-2(2-q)(\eta_{1}+q-3)\geq 0
in
D_{4-q}
; we may thus extract
\delta^{\prime}
from (
4.20

\displaystyle\delta_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\prime}_{\pm}+3-q

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\prime}_{\pm}(q,\eta_{1}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\eta_{1}\pm\sqrt{\eta_{1}^{2}-2(2-q)(\eta_{1}+q-3)}\right).

(4.25)

Replacing in (
4.23

\delta^{\prime}
by its value in terms of
\eta_{1},q
, we get:

\displaystyle a_{\pm}=a_{\pm}(\eta_{1},q):=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{1}\mp\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\eta_{1}^{2}-2(2-q)(\eta_{1}+q-3)}

(4.26)

\displaystyle b_{\pm}=b_{\pm}(\eta_{1},q):=\frac{1}{2}\mp\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\eta_{1}^{2}-2(2-q)(\eta_{1}+q-3)}

(4.27)

\displaystyle c=c(\eta_{1},q):=\frac{1+\eta_{1}}{2}.

(4.28)

We can now compute
f_{1}
as,

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x-1}{2-q}f_{0}^{\prime}(x)+\frac{-\delta^{\prime}}{2-q}f_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{2-q}\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a,b;c;x)+\frac{ab(x-1)}{c(2-q)}\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a+1,b+1;c+1;x).

(4.29)

Using expression (
4.7
) for the integral means, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}I(r)=(1-r^{2})^{-\delta}\left[(1+r^{2})f_{0}(r^{2})-2r^{2}f_{1}(r^{2})\right].

(4.30)

4.1.1.
The
(+)
-branch

Let us first consider the case where

a=a_{+},\,b=b_{+}.

We then have
c-a_{+}-b_{+}=\sqrt{{\eta_{1}^{2}-2(2-q)(\eta_{1}+q-3)}}>0
, so that

0<\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a,b;c;1)=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)}<\infty.

(4.31)

If furthermore,
a+b+1<c
, then
\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a+1,b+1;c+1;1)<\infty
, and

\Delta:=\lim_{r\rightarrow 1}\left((1+r^{2})f_{0}(r^{2})-2r^{2}f_{1}(r^{2})\right)=2\left(1-\frac{a}{2-q}\right)\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a,b;c;1)<\infty.

, the spectrum is

\beta(2,q)=3-q+\delta_{+}^{\prime}(\eta_{1},q)=3-q+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\eta_{1}^{2}-2(2-q)(\eta_{1}+q-3)}-\eta_{1}\right).

A:=1-a_{+}/(2-q)>0

. So we get that the spectrum is equal to
\delta_{+}
on the subset of
D_{4-q}
of points for which
a+b+1<c
.
This condition is just
Z>1

(4.32)

\displaystyle Z

so that the condition

In the

\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a,b;c;x)=(1-x)^{c-a-b}\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(c-a,c-b;c;x),

\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a+1,b+1;c+1;x)=(1-x)^{c-a-b-1}\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(c-a,c-b;c+1;x).

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)=\frac{a}{2-q}\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a,b;c;x)-\frac{ab}{c}\frac{1}{(2-q)}(1-x)^{c-a-b}\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(c-a,c-b;c+1;x).

We now have

\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a,b;c;1)<\infty

Remark 4.1
.

The
q=2
case.

Up to now, we have assumed that
q\neq 2
. The solution for
q=2
, thus
\eta_{2}=2,\eta_{1}\geq 1/2
, can be obtained by continuity as the
q\to 2
limit of

and

(

4.26
) gives

\displaystyle a_{+}=\frac{1}{2\eta_{1}}(\eta_{1}-1)(q-2)+\mathcal{O}\left((q-2)^{2}\right),

\displaystyle b_{+}=\frac{1}{2}(1-\eta_{1})+\mathcal{O}(q-2),\quad c=\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta_{1}),

so that

together with

For a Lévy process, with symbols

(4.41)

In particular, if
q=2,\eta_{2}=2
, the standard integral means spectrum at
p=2
of the logarithm of the LLE is independent of
\eta_{1}\geq 1/2
, and equal to
\beta(2,2)=1
.

In the
q=0

4.41
) recovers a result of
[

]

4.1.3.
Algebraic solutions

As a transition to the next section, let us look for purely algebraic solutions of the form,

\theta_{j}(x)=(1-x)^{-\alpha}f_{j}(x),\quad f_{j}(x)=A_{j}^{0}+A_{j}^{1}(1-x),\,j=0,1.

where
A_{j}^{k},k=0,1
are fixed coefficients and with the understanding that
\theta_{j}=0
for

.
Recall that the hypergeometric function
\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}
is given by the well-known series expansion,

\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(a,b;c;x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=1+\frac{ab}{c}\frac{x}{1!}+\frac{a(a+1)b(b+1)}{c(c+1)}\frac{x^{2}}{2!}+\cdots,

(4.42)

with

\displaystyle(a)_{n}=\begin{cases}1\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,n=0\\

\displaystyle j=1:\,\,\,A_{1}^{0}=\frac{\eta_{1}-3}{\eta_{1}+1},\,\,\,A_{1}^{1}=\frac{\eta_{1}+q-1}{2-q}\frac{\eta_{1}+q-3}{\eta_{1}+1},

\displaystyle j=2:\,\,\,A_{2}^{0}=\frac{4}{q-2}\frac{\eta_{1}+q-3}{\eta_{1}+1},\,\,\,A_{2}^{1}=0.

When

k=n+1=3

4.2.4.
General solution to the Fuchsian system

From the perspective of Fuchsian systems
[
IY08
,
LY19
]
, the initial equations (
4.53
) for the vector function,

\vartheta(\xi):=(\theta_{0}(\xi),\theta_{1}(\xi),\theta_{2}(\xi))^{t}
can be written under the matrix form,

\vartheta^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{\mathbb{A}}{\xi}\vartheta(\xi)+\frac{\mathbb{B}}{1-\xi}\vartheta(\xi),

where matrices
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
do not depend on
\xi
. They are simply given here by

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
\eta_{1}+q-3&-(\eta_{1}+1)&0\\
0&-4&q-2\end{pmatrix},

(4.77)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}2(3-q)&2(q-2)&0\\
\eta_{1}+q-3&-2(\eta_{1}+q-3)&\eta_{1}+q-1\\
0&-4&6\end{pmatrix}.

(4.78)

The discriminant of
\mathbb{B}
is

\displaystyle\det(\mathbb{B}-\alpha\mathds{1})

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left[\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{1}+q-3)(2\alpha+q-6)+(\alpha-2)(\alpha+q-3)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha)E_{0}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{4}\det D_{0}(\alpha),

so that
\mathbb{B}
has for eigenvalues the zeroes of
\det D_{0}
,
\alpha_{0}^{\pm}
(
4.62
) and
1
.

For
\xi\to 1^{-}
, the vector functions
\vartheta(\xi)=(\theta_{0}(\xi),\theta_{1}(\xi),\theta_{2}(\xi))^{t}
, have for asymptotic behavior,

\vartheta(\xi)=(1-\xi)^{-\alpha_{0}^{+}}{\bf f}^{+}(\xi)+(1-\xi)^{-\alpha_{0}^{-}}{\bf f}^{-}(\xi)+(1-\xi)^{-1}{\bf f}^{1}(\xi),

(4.79)

where
{\bf f}^{\pm},{\bf f}^{1}
are vector functions with Taylor series expansions in
\xi
. In the generic
non-resonant
case, where the eigenvalues do not differ by integer numbers, these functions converge at
\xi=1
towards the eigenvectors of
\mathbb{B}
corresponding to their respective eigenvalue powers

\alpha_{0}^{\pm},1
. In the
resonan
t case, they can be polynomials in
\xi
, or can involve polynomials in
-\log(1-\xi)
, which then dominate the limit when
\xi\to 1^{-}
.

In the resonant case of the
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
ellipses of Section
4.2.3
, we have
\alpha_{0}^{-}=\alpha_{0}^{+}-(2n-1)
with
n\geq 1
.
These vector functions are then simple polynomials, with

{\bf f}^{+}(\xi)=(f_{0}(\xi),f_{1}(\xi),f_{2}(\xi))^{t},

and
{\bf f}^{-}(\xi)={\bf f}^{1}(\xi)={\bf 0}
.
At
\xi=1
,
{\bf f}^{+}(\xi)
becomes the eigenvector
{\bf f}^{+}(\xi=1)={\bf A}_{0}(\alpha_{0}^{+})
, as given in (
4.66
).

Let us conclude with the following Theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

The generalized integral means spectrum
\beta(2,q)
of a whole-plane Lévy-Loewner process with Lévy symbols
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}=-q
is given in the whole
D_{-q}
domain (
4.52
) by
\alpha_{0}^{+}
in Eq. (
4.62
).

Proof.

Because of (
4.79
), the generalized integral means spectrum must be equal to one of the three eigenvalues
\alpha_{0}^{+},\alpha_{0}^{-},1
.
Eigenvalues
\alpha_{0}^{+}
and
\alpha_{0}^{-}
are equal only when
\hat{Z}=0
, i.e., on the green ellipse which lies outside
D_{-q}
,
except for the point
P_{0}=(q=0,\eta_{1}=2)
. Therefore in
D^{*}_{-q}:=D_{-q}\setminus P_{0}
, we have
\alpha_{0}^{+}>\alpha_{0}^{-}
. One can also check that on
D_{-q}
,

\alpha_{0}^{+}>1
, whereas the equality
\alpha_{0}^{-}=1
is realized in
D_{-q}
on the branch of hyperbola of equation
(\eta_{1}+q-8)(q-4)=4
with
q\leq 0
. We know that

\beta(2,q)=3-q=\alpha_{0}^{+}
on the half-line
\eta_{1}=3-q,q\leq 0
, as well as
\beta(2,q)=4-q=\alpha_{0}^{+}
on the half-line
\eta_{1}=1-q,q\leq 0
. Because of the Hölder inequality, the
generalized integral means spectrum is
convex
in
(p,q)

[
DHLZ18
]
, hence
continuous
. By continuity,

\beta(2,q)=\alpha_{0}^{+}
on these integrability lines cannot jump to
\alpha_{0}^{-}<\alpha_{0}^{+}
or
1<\alpha_{0}^{+}
in
D^{*}_{-q}
, hence
\beta(2,q)=\alpha_{0}^{+}

in the whole domain. At the singular point
P_{0}
, the spectrum is still
\alpha_{0}^{+}
, with a change of its analytic form.
