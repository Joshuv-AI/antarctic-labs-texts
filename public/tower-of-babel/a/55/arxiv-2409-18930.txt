Abstract

For scalar conservation laws, we prove that spectrally stable stationary Lax discrete shock profiles are nonlinearly stable in some polynomially-weighted
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{\infty}
spaces. In comparison with several previous nonlinear stability results on discrete shock profiles, we avoid the introduction of any weakness assumption on the amplitude of the shock and apply our analysis to a large family of schemes that introduce some artificial possibly high-order viscosity. The proof relies on a precise description of the Green’s function of the linearization of the numerical scheme about spectrally stable discrete shock profiles obtained in
[
Coe25
]
. The present article also pinpoints the ideas for a possible extension of this nonlinear orbital stability result for discrete shock profiles in the case of systems of conservation laws.

35L65, 65M06

Keywords:
conservation laws, finite difference scheme, discrete shock profiles, nonlinear stability

1.1
Position of the problem

Definition of the scalar conservation law and the stationary Lax shock

We consider a one-dimensional scalar conservation law

\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}f(u)=0,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},x\in\mathbb{R},\\
u:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{U},\end{array}

(1.1)

where the space of states
\mathcal{U}
is an open set of
\mathbb{R}
and the flux
f:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}
function. Solutions of conservation laws tend to have discontinuities, even for smooth initial data. When considering approximations of solutions of conservation laws by finite difference methods, one of the central questions concerns the ability of approaching discontinuities. Indeed, finite difference methods are usually deduced using Taylor expansions and are thus essentially more adapted to approximate smooth solutions. Thus, a first step is to answer whether shocks are well approximated by finite difference schemes. The notion of discrete shock profiles defined below is a central tool for this purpose.

In the present paper, we focus our attention on the approximation of stationary Lax shocks by finite difference methods. We consider two states
u^{-},u^{+}\in\mathcal{U}
such that:

f(u^{-})=f(u^{+}).

(1.2)

This corresponds to the well-known Rankine-Hugoniot condition which allows us to conclude that the standing shock
u
defined by:

\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\quad u(t,x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}u^{-}&\text{ if }x<0,\\
u^{+}&\text{ else},\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

is a weak solution of (
1.1
). We also introduce the so-called Lax shock condition (see
[
Lax57
]
):

f^{\prime}(u^{+})<0<f^{\prime}(u^{-}).

(1.4)

Let us point out that starting by studying the discrete approximation of Lax shocks is fairly logical. Indeed, for convex fluxes
f
, Lax shocks correspond exactly to the entropic shocks. Furthermore, for more general fluxes, weak genuinely nonlinear Lax shocks are also entropic solutions of (
1.1
).

We consider a constant
\boldsymbol{\nu}>0
and introduce a space step
\Delta x>0
and a time step
\Delta t:=\boldsymbol{\nu}\Delta x>0
. We impose the following CFL condition on the constant
\boldsymbol{\nu}
:

(1.5)

We introduce the

defined for

\forall j\in\mathbb{Z},\quad(\mathcal{N}(u))_{j}:=u_{j}-\boldsymbol{\nu}\left(F\left(\boldsymbol{\nu};u_{j-p+1},\ldots,u_{j+q}\right)-F\left(\boldsymbol{\nu};u_{j-p},\ldots,u_{j+q-1}\right)\right),

(1.6)

where

and the numerical flux

is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function. We are interested in solutions of the conservative one-step explicit finite difference scheme defined by:

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad u^{n+1}=\mathcal{N}(u^{n})

(1.7)

with the initial condition
u^{0}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

We assume that the numerical scheme satisfies the following usual consistency condition with regards to the conservation law (
1.1
):

\forall u\in\mathcal{U},\quad F(\boldsymbol{\nu};u,\ldots,u)=f(u).

(1.8)

Let us point out that we will also introduce an additional hypothesis later on (Hypothesis
4
) on the numerical schemes considered in this article which essentially states that the scheme introduces some artificial viscosity.

Let us now introduce the so-called
discrete shock profiles
which will be the main mathematical object of the article. The presentation done here will be fairly brief and we invite the interested reader to take a closer look at
[
Ser07
]

In a general setting (i.e. for moving shocks), a discrete shock profile is a solution of the numerical scheme (
1.7
) which is a traveling wave linking the two states of the shock. We pinpoint the fact that the consistency condition (
1.8
) satisfied by the numerical scheme implies that the existing discrete shock profiles must travel at the same speed as the shock they are associated with while recalling that this speed is deduced using the well-known Rankine-Hugoniot condition (see
[
Ser07
]
for a detailed proof). In the case of stationary shocks which is studied in the present paper, stationary discrete shock profiles (SDSPs) are thus stationary solution of the numerical scheme (
1.7
) linking the two states
u^{-}
and
u^{+}
of the shock.

Hypothesis 1

(Existence of a family of stationary discrete shock profiles (SDSPs))
.

There exists an open interval
I
containing
0
and a continuous family of sequences
(\overline{u}^{\delta})_{\delta\in I}\in\left(\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{Z}}\right)^{I}
that satisfy for each
\delta\in I
:

\mathcal{N}(\overline{u}^{\delta})=\overline{u}^{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j}\underset{j\rightarrow\pm\infty}{\rightarrow}u^{\pm}.

The sequences
\overline{u}^{\delta}
are stationary discrete shock profiles (SDSPs) associated with the shock (
1.3
) we introduced earlier. We refer to Figure
1
for a visual representation of such SDSPs. The existence theory of discrete shock profiles, and thus the verification of Hypothesis
1
, has been a longstanding question
[
Jen74
,
MR79
,
Mic84
,
Smy90
,
Ser04
]
. We refer the interested reader to
[
Ser07
]
for a general overview on the existence theory. To quickly summarize, most existence results tend to necessitate fairly strict additional hypotheses. For instance, in
[
Jen74
]
, one can find an existence result for discrete shock profiles associated with Lax shocks for
monotone
schemes approximating
scalar
conservation laws. On the other hand, existence results of discrete shock profiles for
systems
of conservation laws such as
[
MR79
,
Mic84
]
tend to introduce a
weakness
assumption on the amplitude of the approximated shocks.

Let us point out that, in the present article, one of the discrete shock profile will be fixed as a reference discrete shock profile compared to the others and will have to satisfy additional hypotheses, in particular a
spectral stability
assumption (see Hypotheses
5
and
6
below). We thus assumed in Hypothesis
1
that
0
belongs to the interval
I
and we will use the notation:

\overline{u}:=\overline{u}^{0}

(1.9)

to identify this discrete shock profile more clearly from now on. The additional assumptions on the discrete shock profile
\overline{u}
that we will introduce aim at allowing the use of the result
[
Coe25
, Theorem 1]
in Section

.

We now introduce two additional assumptions (Hypotheses
2
and
3
) on the family
(\overline{u}^{\delta})_{\delta\in I}
of SDSPs.

Hypothesis 2

(Exponential localization)
.

We assume that there exist two positive constants
C,c
such that:

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathbb{N},\quad

\displaystyle\left|\overline{u}_{j}-u^{+}\right|\leq Ce^{-cj},

(1.10a)

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathbb{N},\quad

\displaystyle\left|\overline{u}_{-j}-u^{-}\right|\leq Ce^{-cj}.

(1.10b)

Up to considering a smaller interval
I
for the family of SDSPs
(\overline{u}^{\delta})_{\delta\in I}
, we will also assume that there exist two positive constants
C,c
such that:

\forall\delta\in I,\forall j\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\left|\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j}-\overline{u}_{j}\right|\leq C\left|\delta\right|e^{-c|j|}

(1.11)

and that the set

\left\{\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j},\quad(\delta,j)\in I\times\mathbb{Z}\right\}

(1.12)

is relatively compact in the space of states
\mathcal{U}
.

The inequality (
1.10
) corresponds to an exponential localization of the transition zone of the sequence
\overline{u}
between the endstates
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
while the inequality (
1.11
) is essentially linked to some Lipschitz continuity property of the family of SDSPs
(\overline{u}^{\delta})_{\delta\in I}
. We claim that those two inequalities might be obtained fairly similarly and should be a consequence of the shock being non-characteristic. Indeed, one can find proofs of similar properties on viscous approximations of Lax shocks (see
[
ZH98
, Corollary 1.2]
) or on semi-discrete approximations of moving Lax shocks (see
[
BHR03
, Proposition 2.4]
and
[
Bec+10
, Lemma 1.1]
). Let us point out that the exponential localization (
1.10
) is central in
[
Coe25
]
to study the spectrum of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}:=L^{0}
defined below by (
1.20
) which corresponds to the linearization of the numerical scheme about the discrete shock profile
\overline{u}
.

With regards to the relative compactness of the set (
1.12
), we claim that it is actually a consequence of (
1.10
) and (
1.11
). Indeed, since the space of states
\mathcal{U}
is open and using (
1.10
) and (
1.11
), there exist a radius
R>0
and an integer
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that:

\left\{\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j},\quad(\delta,j)\in I\times\mathbb{Z}\text{ with }|j|>j_{0}\right\}\subset B(u^{+},R)\cup B(u^{-},R)\subset\mathcal{U}.

One can then easily obtain the relative compactness of (
1.12
) up to introducing a smaller open interval
I
such that
0
belongs to it and the sets
\left\{\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j},\delta\in I\right\}
are relatively compact in
\mathcal{U}
for
j\in\left\{-j_{0},\ldots,j_{0}\right\}
.

We observe that the inequality (
1.11
) allows us to define the real valued continuous "mass function"
M
defined by:

\forall\delta\in I,\quad M(\delta):=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j}-\overline{u}_{j}.

(1.13)

The
mass
function
M
corresponds to summing the differences between the elements of a discrete shock profile
\overline{u}^{\delta}
and the reference discrete shock profile
\overline{u}:=\overline{u}^{0}
. Let us introduce a hypothesis related to the function
M
.

Hypothesis 3

(Identification by mass)
.

Up to considering a smaller open interval
I
containing
0
for the family of SDSPs
(\overline{u}^{\delta})_{\delta\in I}
, we will assume that the mass function
M
is injective and that there exists a constant
C_{\delta}>0
such that:

\forall\delta\in I,\quad|\delta|\leq C_{\delta}|M(\delta)|.

(1.14)

The injectivity condition we impose on the mass function
M
implies that each discrete shock profile
\overline{u}^{\delta}
for
\delta\in I
is identified by its mass difference
M(\delta)
with the reference profile
\overline{u}
. A similar result was already obtained in
[
Smy90
, Theorem 2.1]
for specific discrete shock profiles of the Lax-Wendroff scheme. Let us add that the mass function
M
shares several links with the so-called
Y
function introduced in
[
Ser07
]
and that the analysis of the
Y
function of Serre tends to supports the claim of a linear behavior of the mass function
M
.

The mass function

will be central in the nonlinear orbital stability result Theorem
1
of the present article, as we will explain in the following paragraph. Let us point out that if we were able to prove that the mass function
M
was of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on
I
and that we have:

M^{\prime}(0)\neq 0,

(1.15)

then we immediately obtain Hypothesis
3
. Up to having sufficient regularity on the family of discrete shock profiles
\left(\overline{u}^{\delta}\right)_{\delta\in I}
and on the function

, we will show at the end of Section

that we can actually obtain (

) and thus the verification of Hypothesis
3
using the result
[
Coe25
, Theorem 1]
.

Stability theory for discrete shock profiles and goal of the present article

In the following paragraph, we will present the stability theory for discrete shock profiles and where Theorem
1
of the present article stands with respect to the state of the art. We point out that, even though this article focuses on discrete shock profiles for
scalar
conservation laws, a crucial point is that most of the discussion on the stability of discrete shock profiles that follows also holds for and tackles the case of
systems
of conservation laws.

nonlinear orbital stability
of stationary discrete shock profiles. It amounts to finding two suitable Banach spaces
X
and
Y
(for instance weighted
\ell^{r}

gives a representation of this result. Essentially, the nonlinear orbital stability states that, for small perturbations
u^{0}:=\overline{u}+\textbf{h}
of a discrete shock profile
\overline{u}
, the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the numerical scheme (
1.7
) converges towards the family
\left(\overline{u}^{\delta}\right)_{\delta\in I}
of discrete shock profiles associated with the initial discrete shock profile
\overline{u}
. Let us make some important observations.

First, in the statement of nonlinear orbital stability results, one can actually hope that the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges towards a specific element of the family
\left(\overline{u}^{\delta}\right)_{\delta\in I}
, i.e. one might prove the following assertion rather than (
1.16
):

\exists\delta\in I,\quad\left\|u^{n}-\overline{u}^{\delta}\right\|_{Y}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\rightarrow}0.

(1.17)

Furthermore, due to the conservative nature of the numerical scheme (
1.7
), we claim that the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the numerical scheme (
1.7
) for an initial condition
u^{0}:=\overline{u}+\textbf{h}
satisfies:

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}u^{n}_{j}-\overline{u}_{j}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}u^{0}_{j}-\overline{u}_{j}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\textbf{h}_{j}.

(1.18)

As a consequence, if the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the numerical scheme converges for instance in the
\ell^{1}
-norm towards a specific discrete shock profile
\overline{u}^{\delta}
with
\delta\in I
, then (
1.18
) implies that:

M(\delta)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\textbf{h}_{j}

(1.19)

where the mass function
M
is defined by (
1.13
). Since Hypothesis
3
implies that the function
M
is injective, there exists a unique choice of
\delta\in I
such that the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the numerical scheme (
1.7
) with the initial condition
u^{0}:=\overline{u}+\textbf{h}
converges towards
\overline{u}^{\delta}
and the choice of
\delta
is determined by (
1.19
). We observe that Hypothesis
3
is therefore crucial. Indeed, if the function
M
was not injective, then one could not identify a unique discrete shock profile towards which the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
should converge and proving a nonlinear orbital stability result would be difficult, if not impossible. We recall that we will discuss on the verification of Hypothesis
3
at the end of Section
1.3
.

Let us make a second observation. Within the definition of the nonlinear stability results, it is not clear and easy to prove that the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the numerical scheme (
1.7
) associated with an initial condition
u^{0}:=\overline{u}+\textbf{h}
is defined for all time

. Indeed, it is possible that the solution
u^{n}
leaves the domain of definition
\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{Z}}
of the nonlinear evolution operator
\mathcal{N}
defined by (

), i.e. there can be a time
n\in\mathbb{N}
and an integer

such that
u^{n}_{j}
leaves the space of states
\mathcal{U}
. This is also a important point to prove.

Several articles have proved nonlinear orbital stability results for discrete shock profiles associated with stationary (and moving) Lax shocks (see
[
Jen74
,
Smy90
,
LX93a
,
LX93
,

,

,

]
). However, those results have recurring limitations that one would want to avoid:

•

Some stability results are strictly restricted for discrete shock profiles of
scalar
conservation laws. It is for instance the case of
[
Jen74
]
which uses intensively a
monotonicity
assumption on the choice of numerical schemes studied, which can quite obviously not be generalized to the case of
systems
of conservation laws. However, those results tend to be less subject to the following limitations.

•

In the case of
systems
of conservation laws, known stability results for discrete shock profiles impose a weakness assumption on the amplitude of the shock associated with the discrete shock profiles (see for instance
[
LX93a
,
LX93
,
Yin97
,
LY99
,
Mic02
]
).

•

Several result are applied to fairly restrictive families of numerical schemes, for instance,
monotone
conservative finite difference schemes
[
Jen74
]
, the Lax-Wendroff scheme for
[
Smy90
]
and the Lax-Friedrichs scheme in
[
LX93a
,
LX93
,
Yin97
]
.

•

Some results impose strong restrictions on the initial perturbations
h
considered (i.e. on the choice of the vector space
X
in the statement of the nonlinear stability result above). For instance, most result consider the initial perturbation
h
to be small in some weighted
\ell^{r}
-spaces. However, there can be significant differences in the choices of the weights. For instance, the initial perturbation in
[
Smy90
, Theorem 4.1]
belong to an exponentially-weighted
\ell^{2}
-space whereas the main results of
[
LX93a
,
LX93
]
only consider small initial perturbations in a polynomially-weighted
\ell^{2}
-space. Another example of restriction that can be imposed is a zero-mass assumption on the initial perturbation

. We observe in this context that the identification equation (
1.19
) implies that the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
should then converge towards the initial discrete shock profile
\overline{u}:=\overline{u}^{0}
since
M(0)=0
. This is for instance the case in
[
LX93a
,
LX93
]
, though those results were generalized for nonzero-mass perturbations in
[
Yin97
]
.

•

The results presented previously do not have any decay rates for the convergence (
1.17
), which could be a desirable feature.

One would want to prove a nonlinear stability result that discards or replaces several limitations of the previously presented results. Most importantly, we wish to avoid the introduction of a weakness assumption. An option in this direction, which is presented in
[
Ser07
, Open Question 5.3]
, would be to generalize in the case of discrete shock profiles the stability analysis initiated for viscous and relaxation approximations of shocks in
[
ZH98
,
Zum00
,
MZ02
,
MZ03
]
. The main idea would be essentially to prove that the
spectral stability
of a discrete shock profile implies its nonlinear orbital stability. This
spectral stability
assumption corresponds to a hypothesis on the point spectrum of the linearization of the numerical scheme
\mathcal{N}
about a discrete shock profile. It will be defined more precisely below in Hypotheses
5
and
6
. Considering this spectral stability assumption avoids the introduction of a weakness assumption on the strength of the approximated shocks. Furthermore, the analysis can be carried for a large family of numerical schemes contrarily to many known results. The stability analysis described in
[
ZH98
,
Zum00
,
MZ02
,
MZ03
]
was already generalized for semi-discrete approximations of shocks in
[
BHR03
,
Bec+10
]
. Up to the author’s knowledge, the combination of
[
Coe25
]
and the present article correspond to the first complete generalization of these techniques for discrete shock profiles, i.e. in a fully discrete setting, to conclude a nonlinear orbital stability result (Theorem
1
), at least for scalar conservation laws, and thus offer a partial answer to the
[
Ser07
, Open Question 5.3]
.

1.2
Linearizations of the numerical scheme about discrete shock profiles and spectral stability

One of the central element that we will use in the present article is an in-depth comprehension of the linearization of the numerical scheme about the discrete shock profile
\overline{u}
. This was the main subject of a previous paper
[
Coe25
]
by the author. We will start by introducing the linearization
{L^{\delta}}
about the discrete shock profile
\overline{u}^{\delta}
, discuss about the spectral properties of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}:=L^{0}
and finally introduce the so-called
spectral stability
assumption that is imposed on the point spectrum of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}
(see Hypotheses
5
and
6
below).

First, for
\delta\in I
, we linearize the discrete evolution operator
\mathcal{N}
about the discrete shock profile
\overline{u}^{\delta}
and thus introduce the bounded
linear
operator
{L^{\delta}}
acting on
\ell^{r}(\mathbb{Z})
with
r\in[1,+\infty]
defined by:

\forall h\in\ell^{r}(\mathbb{Z}),\forall j\in\mathbb{Z},\quad({L^{\delta}}h)_{j}:=\sum_{k=-p}^{q}a^{\delta}_{j,k}h_{j+k},

(1.20)

where for

Novelties and limitations of Theorem
1

Let us make some observations on Theorem
1
in order to compare it with the state of the art surrounding the stability theory of discrete shock profiles presented above and discuss on possible future improvements.

•

The first main observation is that, due to the adaptation of the stability analysis performed in
[
ZH98
]
, we did not have to introduce any weakness assumption of the shock (
1.3
) but rather a spectral stability assumption on the discrete shock profile
\overline{u}
(Hypotheses
5
and
6
).

•

With regards to the restriction on the numerical schemes considered, we impose on the numerical schemes that they must introduce artificial possibly high-order viscosity (Hypothesis
4
). For instance, the main result of the present article does not apply for the Lax-Wendroff scheme since it displays a dispersive behavior. This "diffusive" limitation is necessary due to the result
[
Coe25
, Theorem 1]
which only applies for numerical schemes displaying a parabolic behavior. However, this still allows us to consider all first order schemes and also higher odd ordered schemes. Let us however point out that, in a recent preprint
[
CF24
]
, Coulombel and Faye proved a nonlinear orbital stability result for stationary discrete shock profiles and for the Lax-Wendroff scheme. Similarly as in the present article, the proof relies crucially on the analysis of the Green’s function of a reference discrete shock profile to obtain a linear stability result. The proof of the nonlinear stability is then deduced using the same strategy and the lemmas that will be proved below.

•

The initial perturbations
h
have to be small in some polynomially-weighted space
\ell^{1}_{\Gamma}
. This is an improvement compared to results like
[
Smy90
]
which impose for the initial perturbations to be small in some exponentially-weighted space. Furthermore, compared to previous nonlinear orbital stability results, Theorem
1
proves decay rates (
1.44
) on the sequences
h^{n}
. As will be clearer in Section
2
when we will give concrete choices of parameters
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{\infty},\textbf{p}_{1},\textbf{p}_{\infty}\in[0,+\infty[
to choose in Theorem
1
, the definition (
1.42
) of the constant
\Gamma
implies that one could choose the decay rates
\textbf{p}_{1}
and
\textbf{p}_{\infty}
to be as high as one wishes un to considering a larger constant
\Gamma
that parametrizes the weights on the initial perturbations
h
.

•

Theorem
1
is restricted to the case of
scalar
conservation laws. However, we claim that one could hope for a generalization of such a nonlinear orbital stability result for discrete shock profiles of
systems
of conservation laws. Let us present some observations towards this direction:

\star

One would need to investigate the identification by mass introduced in Hypothesis
3
since the function
M
defined by (
1.13
) would no longer be scalar valued. Such a discussion is held in
[
Ser07
]
.

\star

The result
[
Coe25
, Theorem 1]
which provides the long-time behavior of the Green’s function associated with the linearization of the numerical scheme about spectrally stable discrete shock profiles also holds in the system case.

\star

In the case of systems of conservation laws, the behavior of the Green’s function associated with the spectrally stable discrete shock profile is far more complicated. Indeed, contrarily to the
scalar
case presented above where the long time asymptotic behavior of the Green’s function is to concentrate its mass at the shock location by activating the profile
V
, the Green’s function in the
system
case displays generalized Gaussian waves going towards
\pm\infty
for instance via reflected and transmitted waves (see
[
Coe25
]
for more details). This relates back to the identification by mass issue we mentioned above and also implies the necessity of a careful analysis for the choices of the norms to obtain a generalization of the linear decay estimates of Proposition
1
.

The difficult case of adapting Theorem
1
for systems of conservation laws could be handled in a future article.

3.1

The main goal of this section is the proof of Theorem

Definition of a neighborhood of the states of the SDSP
\overline{u}^{\delta}

An important part of the proof of Theorem
1
will rely on proving that, for small enough initial perturbations
h
, if we define the initial condition
u^{0}:=\overline{u}+\textbf{h}
, the sequences
u^{n}
constructed using the numerical scheme

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad u^{n+1}:=\mathcal{N}(u^{n}).

(3.1)

are actually defined for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This is nontrivial as there could be a time
n\in\mathbb{N}
and an integer
j\in\mathbb{Z}
such that the scalar
u^{n}_{j}
would not belong to the space of states
\mathcal{U}
of the conservation law (
1.1
) and thus the solution
u^{n}
of the numerical scheme would have left the domain of definition
\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{Z}}
of the operator
\mathcal{N}
defined by (
1.6
). We recall that we do not make any monotonicity assumption on the numerical scheme we consider here, which could allow to give a rapid and easy answer to this type of question. Since the set (
1.12
) is relatively compact, there exists a radius
R>0
such that:

\overline{\bigcup_{(\delta,j)\in I\times\mathbb{Z}}B(\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j},R)}\subset\mathcal{U}.

(3.2)

The set defined in (
3.2
) thus contains a neighborhood of the states of the SDSPs
\overline{u}^{\delta}
and is also included in the space of states
\mathcal{U}
of the scalar conservation law (
1.1
). The definition (
3.2
) of the radius
R
implies that for
h\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\left\|h\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}}<R
, we have that:

\forall\delta\in I,\forall j\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j}+h_{j}\in\mathcal{U},

i.e. the perturbation
h
is small enough so that the elements of the sequence
\overline{u}^{\delta}+h
remain in the domain of definition
\mathcal{U}
of the numerical scheme. Therefore, coming back to our initial issue of constructing the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the numerical scheme (
3.1
), if for some integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
we can define the sequence
u^{n}
and that there exists
\delta\in I
such that

\left\|u^{n}-\overline{u}^{\delta}\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}}<R,

then the definition (
3.2
) of the radius
R
implies that
u^{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and that we can construct the sequence
u^{n+1}:=\mathcal{N}(u^{n})
. This will give us later on a way to prove recursively that the solution
(u^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the numerical scheme (
3.1
) is well-defined up to any time
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Decomposition near the SDSP
\overline{u}^{\delta}
of the operator
\mathcal{N}
in linear and nonlinear parts

Let us consider a choice of
\delta\in I
and a sequence
h\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\left\|h\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}}<R
where the radius
R
is defined by (
3.2
). We recall that (
3.2
) implies that the elements of the sequence
\overline{u}^{\delta}+h
belong to the space of states

\mathcal{N}(\overline{u}^{\delta}+h)_{j}=\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j}+h_{j}\\
-\boldsymbol{\nu}\left(F\left(\boldsymbol{\nu};\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j-p+1}+h_{j-p+1},\ldots,\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j+q}+h_{j+q}\right)-F\left(\boldsymbol{\nu};\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j-p}+h_{j-p},\ldots,\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j+q-1}+h_{j+q-1}\right)\right).

(3.3)

Let us now introduce the sequence
Q^{\delta}(h)
defined for
\delta\in I
and
h\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\left\|h\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}}<R
by:

\forall j\in\mathbb{Z},\quad Q^{\delta}(h)_{j}:=\boldsymbol{\nu}F(\boldsymbol{\nu};\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j-p}+h_{j-p},\ldots,\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j+q-1}+h_{j+q-1})-\boldsymbol{\nu}F(\boldsymbol{\nu};\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j-p},\ldots,\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j+q-1})-\sum_{k=-p}^{q-1}b^{\delta}_{j,k}h_{j+k}

(3.4)

where the scalars

defined by (

) are equal to

b^{\delta}_{j,k}:=\boldsymbol{\nu}\partial_{u_{k}}F(\boldsymbol{\nu};\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j-p},\ldots,\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j+q-1}).

We observe that, since the sequence
\overline{u}^{\delta}
is a fixed point of the nonlinear evolution operator

defined by (

\left(F(\boldsymbol{\nu};\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j-p},\ldots,\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j+q-1})\right)_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}

\forall j\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\mathcal{N}(\overline{u}^{\delta}+h)_{j}=\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j}+({L^{\delta}}h)_{j}-Q^{\delta}(h)_{j+1}+Q^{\delta}(h)_{j},

about the SDSP

\forall\delta\in I,\forall h\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}),\quad\left\|h\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}}<R\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{N}(\overline{u}^{\delta}+h)=\overline{u}^{\delta}+{L^{\delta}}h+(Id-\mathcal{T})Q^{\delta}(h).

\gamma_{\infty}
that will appear throughout the proof. We recall that the set on the left side of (
3.2
) is closed and contained in
\mathcal{U}
. Therefore, using the regularity of the numerical flux
F
and Taylor’s Theorem, there exists a constant
C>0
such that:

\forall\delta\in I,\forall j\in\mathbb{Z},\forall h_{-p},\ldots,h_{q-1}\in B(0,R),\\
\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}F\left(\boldsymbol{\nu};\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j-p}+h_{-p},\ldots,\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j+q-1}+h_{q-1}\right)-\boldsymbol{\nu}F\left(\boldsymbol{\nu};\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j-p},\ldots,\overline{u}^{\delta}_{j+q-1}\right)-\sum_{k=-p}^{q-1}b^{\delta}_{j,k}h_{k}\right|\leq C\left(\sum_{k=-p}^{q-1}|h_{k}|\right)^{2}

(5.2)

where the scalars
b^{\delta}_{j,k}
are defined by (
1.21
). We also observe that there exists a positive constant
\widetilde{C}
:

\forall j\in\mathbb{Z},\forall k\in\{-p,\ldots,q-1\},\quad\begin{array}[]{c}(1+|j|)^{\gamma_{1}}\leq\widetilde{C}(1+|j+k|)^{\gamma_{1}},\\
(1+|j|)^{\gamma_{\infty}}\leq\widetilde{C}(1+|j+k|)^{\gamma_{\infty}}.\end{array}

(5.3)

Let us now start by proving (
5.1a
). We consider
\delta\in I
and
h\in\ell^{1}_{\gamma_{1}}\cap\ell^{\infty}_{\gamma_{\infty}}
such that:

\left\|h\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}}<R.

For all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, using (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), we have that:

\displaystyle\left(1+|j|\right)^{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{\infty}}\left|Q^{\delta}(h)_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\widetilde{C}^{2}\left(\sum_{k=-p}^{q-1}\left(1+|j+k|\right)^{\gamma_{1}}|h_{j+k}|\right)\left(\sum_{k=-p}^{q-1}\left(1+|j+k|\right)^{\gamma_{\infty}}|h_{j+k}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\widetilde{C}^{2}(p+q)\left\|h\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}_{\gamma_{\infty}}}\left(\sum_{k=-p}^{q-1}\left(1+|j+k|\right)^{\gamma_{1}}|h_{j+k}|\right).

Thus, by summing this inequality, we obtain (
5.1a
).

The proof of (
5.1b
) is fairly similar. We consider
\delta\in I
and a sequence
h\in\ell^{\infty}_{\gamma_{\infty}}
such that:

\left\|h\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}}<R.

For all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, using (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), we have that:

\displaystyle\left(1+|j|\right)^{2\gamma_{\infty}}\left|Q^{\delta}(h)_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\widetilde{C}^{2}\left(\sum_{k=-p}^{q-1}\left(1+|j+k|\right)^{\gamma_{\infty}}|h_{j+k}|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\widetilde{C}^{2}(p+q)^{2}{\left\|h\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}_{\gamma_{\infty}}}}^{2}.

Thus, we obtain (
5.1b
).

\Box

Proof of Lemma
3.2

Let us recall the statement of Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma
.

We consider a triplet of positive constants
(a,b,c)\in[0,+\infty[^{3}
that satisfies the condition (
H
). There exists a constant
C_{I}(a,b,c)>0
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that:

\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}\frac{1}{(m+1)^{a}(n+1-m)^{b}}\leq\frac{C_{I}(a,b,c)}{(n+2)^{c}}.

Proof

The proof is fairly immediate. First, we observe that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}\frac{1}{(m+1)^{a}(n+1-m)^{b}}=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{a+b-1}}\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{m+1}{n+1}\right)^{a}\left(1-\left(\frac{m}{n+1}\right)\right)^{b}}\right).

Since for
m\in\{0,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor\}
, we have that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\frac{1}{\left(1-\left(\frac{m}{n+1}\right)\right)^{b}}\leq 2^{b},

we thus have

\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}\frac{1}{(m+1)^{a}(n+1-m)^{b}}\leq\frac{2^{b}}{(n+1)^{a+b-1}}\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{m+1}{n+1}\right)^{a}}\right).

We observe that:

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\forall m\in\left\{1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor\right\},\quad\frac{1}{n+1}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{m+1}{n+1}\right)^{a}}\leq\int_{\frac{m}{n+1}}^{\frac{m+1}{n+1}}\frac{dt}{t^{a}}.

Thus, we have :

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{m+1}{n+1}\right)^{a}}\leq(n+1)^{a-1}+\int_{\frac{1}{n+1}}^{1}\frac{dt}{t^{a}}.

We can then easily conclude the proof by separating the cases
a\in[0,1[
,
a=1
and
a>1
.

\Box
