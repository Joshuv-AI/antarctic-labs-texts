Identification of non-isomorphic 2 2 -groups with dihedral central quotient and isomorphic modular group algebras
Leo Margolis, Taro Sakurai

Abstract
. The question whether non-isomorphic finite p p -groups can have isomorphic modular group algebras was recently answered in the negative by García-Lucas, Margolis and del Río [J. Reine Angew. Math. 783 (2022), pp. 269–274]. We embed these negative solutions in the class of two-generated finite 2 2 -groups with dihedral central quotient, and solve the original question for all groups within this class. As a result, we discover new negative solutions and simple algebra isomorphisms. At the same time, the positive solutions for most of the groups in this class give some insights what makes the negative solutions special.

Introduction

The
modular isomorphism problem
asks whether non-isomorphic finite
p
-groups can have isomorphic group algebras over a field of positive characteristic
p
.
It seems to have first been raised in the 1950s and appears in an influential survey by Brauer
[
3
, Problem 2 and Supplement §9]
.
Over the following decades, positive results for many classes of finite
p
-groups were published, cf.
[
17
]
for an overview.
However, a few years ago, García-Lucas, Margolis and del Río
[
10
]
discovered
the first non-isomorphic finite
2
-groups that have isomorphic group algebras over an arbitrary field of characteristic
2
.

Nevertheless, the problem remains poorly understood in general and open for odd primes, for instance.
Our goal in this article is to understand better how the negative solutions presented in
[
10
]
come to be.
These groups were discovered during an effort to solve the modular isomorphism problem for two-generated finite
p
-groups with cyclic derived subgroup,
after these had been classified by Broche, García-Lucas and del Río
[
4
]
.
Although considerable effort has been devoted to solving the problem in this wide class,
it has not been achieved completely so far
[
9
,
8
]
.
We consider the negative solutions from another viewpoint
and embed them in a natural class of groups for which we can completely solve the modular isomorphism problem.

We call a non-abelian group that is generated by two elements of order
2

dihedral
.
For a group
G
, we call the quotient by its center the
central quotient
of
G
.
In this article, we study the modular isomorphism problem for two-generated finite
2
-groups with dihedral central quotient.
This class of groups includes the negative solutions presented in
[
10
]
,
and we are able to identify all negative solutions to the problem—including new counterexamples—within this class of groups.

Theorem A
.

Let
G
and
H
be finite
2
-groups and
\mathbb{F}
a field of characteristic
2
.
Suppose that
G
is two-generated and the central quotient of
G
is dihedral.
Then
\mathbb{F}G\cong\mathbb{F}H
but
G\not\cong H
if and only if
G
and
H
are isomorphic to the groups

\displaystyle\langle\,x,y,z\mid x^{2^{n}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle y^{2^{m}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle z^{2^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle[y,x]

\displaystyle=z,

\displaystyle[z,x]

\displaystyle=z^{-2},

\displaystyle[z,y]

\displaystyle=z^{-2}\,\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\,a,b,c\mid a^{2^{n}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle b^{2^{m}}

\displaystyle=a^{2^{m}},

\displaystyle c^{2^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle[b,a]

\displaystyle=c,

\displaystyle[c,a]

\displaystyle=c^{-2},

\displaystyle[c,b]

\displaystyle=c^{-2}\,\rangle

for some integers
n>m>\ell\geq 2
.

The known counterexamples to the modular isomorphism problem presented in
[
10
]
correspond to the case
\ell=2
.
It should be emphasized, however, that our algebra isomorphisms are simpler than the original ones and it makes the proof cleaner.
This is largely due to the change of presentations of the groups, which is natural in our class.

For the proof of
Theorem

A
, we first give presentations of the groups in our target class.
We then establish an isomorphism between the algebras of the above groups in
Theorem

3.1
.
The proof of this isomorphism can be read independently of the rest of this article,
as it only needs the presentations of the groups.
We remark that the proof can not be carried over to odd primes,
or at least not in the most naive way (cf.
Remark

3.5
).
Finally, we prove that no more negative solutions exist within the class of two-generated finite
2
-groups with dihedral central quotient.
This is the most challenging part of the article, and what can be considered standard methods to attack the problem turn out to be insufficient to prove this.
We hence introduce a procedure that could be called a “group base approximation”.
It allows us, after considerable effort, to solve the modular isomorphism problem completely within our class.
This procedure could also be used for other classes of groups with some adjustments.

As a byproduct of identifying negative solutions, we obtain the classification of two-generated finite
2
-groups with dihedral central quotient.
Since such a classification of groups might be also of independent interest, we provide a direct proof in
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
be a group with dihedral central quotient of order
2^{\ell+1}

Then the derived subgroup

is cyclic of order

.

Proof.

First note that

G/\bm{\mathrm{Z}}(G)
is non-abelian.
Since the central quotient
G/\bm{\mathrm{Z}}(G)
is isomorphic to the dihedral group of order

the quotient
G^{\prime}\bm{\mathrm{Z}}(G)/\bm{\mathrm{Z}}(G)

2^{\ell-1}

There are two elements

and

are trivial modulo the center

Moreover,

We write

3.
Isomorphic modular group algebras

This section is devoted to proving that groups
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}
with
n>m>\ell\geq 2

have isomorphic group algebras over an arbitrary field of characteristic
2
, but are non-isomorphic groups.
In the next section, we will see that these are the only counterexamples to the modular isomorphism problem
within the class of two-generated finite
2
-groups with dihedral central quotient.

Arguably the most interesting part of this article is the following, which generalizes the negative solutions to the modular isomorphism problem presented in
[
10
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
n\geq m>\ell\geq 2
and
\mathbb{F}
a field of characteristic
2
.
Then
\mathbb{F}G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}\cong\mathbb{F}G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
G=G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}
and
H=G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}=\langle a,b,c\rangle
.
We construct an algebra homomorphism from
\mathbb{F}G
to
\mathbb{F}H
and then show that it is bijective.
Given a group homomorphism from
G
to the unit group of
\mathbb{F}H
,
we can extend it and obtain an algebra homomorphism from
\mathbb{F}G
to
\mathbb{F}H
by
[
22
, Lemma 1.1.7]
.
To obtain such a group homomorphism, it suffices to show that the units

x=a,\ y=b+a+1,\ z=[y,x]\in\mathbb{F}H\smallsetminus\Delta(H)

satisfy the defining relations for
G
by
[
15
, Proposition 4.3]
.

We first verify the commutator relations.
Evidently
[y,x]=z
.
As
x^{2}
is central in
\mathbb{F}H
, the basic commutator identity
[y,x^{2}]=[y,x][y,x]^{x}
yields
[z,x]=z^{-2}
.
Now use
ba=abc
to see

y^{2}=b^{2}+a^{2}+ab+abc+1.

Observe that
(ab)^{a}=abc=(ab)^{b}
and
(abc)^{a}=ab=(abc)^{b}
.
Hence
\{ab,abc\}
is a conjugacy class of
H
and the class sum
ab+abc
is central in
\mathbb{F}H
.
Thus so is
y^{2}
which yields
[z,y]=z^{-2}
as before.

We proceed to the power relations.
Clearly
x^{2^{n}}=1
.
As
ab
commutes with
c
, it is also easy to raise
y
to a power:

\displaystyle y^{2^{m}}

\displaystyle=(b^{2}+a^{2}+ab+abc+1)^{2^{m-1}}

\displaystyle=b^{2^{m}}+a^{2^{m}}+(ab)^{2^{m-1}}(1+c^{2^{m-1}})+1.

The first two terms vanish as
b^{2^{m}}=a^{2^{m}}
, and the third term vanishes as
c^{2^{\ell}}=1
and
m>\ell
.
Thus
y^{2^{m}}=1
.
Finally, use

and
ba=abc
to see

xy(1+z)=xy+yx=ab+ba=ab(1+c).

We raise both sides to the power of
2^{\ell}
.
Since
ab
commutes with
c
, the right hand side becomes

(ab)^{2^{\ell}}(1+c^{2^{\ell}})=0
.
On the other hand,
xy
commutes with

as

[z,xy]=[z,y][z,x]^{y}=z^{-2}z^{2}=1.

Hence the left hand side becomes

(xy)^{2^{\ell}}(1+z^{2^{\ell}})
.
As

is a unit, we obtain
z^{2^{\ell}}=1
.
Therefore all relations are satisfied and we obtain a suitable algebra homomorphism.

Since
a=x
and
b=y+x+1
belong to the image of our algebra homomorphism, it is surjective.
As
G
and
H
have the same order, it is bijective.
∎

Lemma 3.2

Let

Proof.

Let

The centralizers of the derived subgroups can distinguish these groups.
First we calculate the exponent of

Recall that the Frattini subgroup

,

From

Since

Remark 3.5
.

Given the simple proof of the negative solutions to the modular isomorphism problem for
p=2
,
it is natural to ask whether this strategy might be imitated for groups of odd order.
While we currently have no clue,
at least the isomorphism we exhibit and the proof we use can not be imitated directly, as we will quickly show.
Analyzing what facilitates the proof so much,
one important thing is that no actual commutator between any non-trivial units in the group algebra must be computed:
the action of the generators of the group on the derived subgroup follows from the fact that their squares are central,
and the correct structure of the whole derived subgroup essentially from its cyclicity.
To imitate the proof, it would be necessary to find a finite non-abelian
p
-group
G
generated by two elements,
say
x
and
y
, such that
x^{p}
and
y^{p}
are central and
G^{\prime}
is cyclic.
We show that in this case
G/\bm{\mathrm{Z}}(G)
is elementary abelian of rank two.
For such groups,
a positive answer to the modular isomorphism problem over an arbitrary field is obtained by Drensky
[
7
]
.

So assume
p
is odd,
G=\langle x,y\rangle
is a finite non-abelian
p
-group such that
x^{p},y^{p}
are central in
G
and
G^{\prime}=\langle z\rangle
is cyclic.
We assume
z=[y,x]
and let
p^{\ell}
be the order of
z
.
Then
G
is regular by
[
12
, III, Satz 10.2 (c)]
.
Hence
1=[z,x^{p}]=[z,x]^{p}
by
[
12
, III, Satz 10.6 (b)]
.
As
\Omega(G^{\prime})=\langle z^{p^{\ell-1}}\rangle
, there exists an
0\leq r\leq p-1
such that
[z,x]=z^{rp^{\ell-1}}
.
This implies
z^{x^{i}}=z^{1+irp^{\ell-1}}
.
We conclude by the standard commutator identity that
[y,x^{p}]=[y,x][y,x]^{x}\dotsb[y,x]^{x^{p-1}}=z^{k}
, where

k=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}(1+irp^{\ell-1})=p+\tfrac{1}{2}r(p-1)p^{\ell}\equiv p\mod p^{\ell}.

As
[y,x^{p}]=1
, we conclude that
\ell=1
.
Hence
x
centralizes
z
, and so does
y
.
This implies that
z
is central.
As
x^{p}
and
y^{p}
are also central, we get
\Frat(G)=\bm{\mathrm{Z}}(G)
, which shows that
G/\bm{\mathrm{Z}}(G)
is indeed elementary abelian of rank two.

We add one bibliography remark for readers interested in generalizing the groups from
Theorem

3.1
to odd primes:
in several generalizations we have tried and which were proposed to us the invariants from
[
2
]
turned out to solve the problem in the positive.

For the choice of our isomorphisms in
Theorem

3.1
, see also
Remark

4.21
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
n>m=1
. Then
\mathbb{F}G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}\not\cong\mathbb{F}G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}
.

Proof.

We will calculate the number of conjugacy classes that consist of squares for each group.
This is an invariant of the group algebra due to the work of Külshammer
[
16
, Section 1]
,
cf.
[
11
, Section 2.2]
for a short proof.
To see that these numbers are different for
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}
, consider a generic element

x^{r}y^{s}z^{t}
with
0\leq r\leq 2^{n}-1
,
0\leq s\leq 1
and
0\leq t\leq 2^{\ell}-1
.
Then, independently of the group,

(x^{r}y^{s}z^{t})^{2}=\begin{cases}x^{2r}z^{2t}&(r\equiv 0\mod 2,\ s=0)\\
x^{2r}&(r\equiv 1\mod 2,\ s=0)\\
x^{2r}y^{2}z^{2t-1}&(r\equiv 1\mod 2,\ s=1)\\
x^{2r}y^{2}&(r\equiv 0\mod 2,\ s=1).\end{cases}

Note that if a square is not central,
then its centralizer is given by the maximal subgroup
\bm{\mathrm{{C}}}_{G}(G^{\prime})=\langle x^{2},y^{2},z,xy\rangle
,
the centralizer of
G^{\prime}
.
So each conjugacy class containing squares has either one or two elements;
this can be seen from the generic form of an element.
The case
r\equiv 0\mod 2
and
s=0
covers all elements of shape

x^{u}z^{v}
with
u\equiv 0\mod 4
and
v\equiv 0\mod 2
.
These elements are formally reading the same in
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}
and they also give the same number of classes,
as the centrality of an element of this shape does not depend on which group we choose.
In the case
r\equiv 1\mod 2
and
s=0
, we get elements of shape
x^{u}
with
u\equiv 2\mod 4
.
Again this is the same for
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}
.
Next we consider the case
r\equiv 1\mod 2
and
s=1
.
In
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}
, we have
x^{2r}y^{2}z^{2t-1}=x^{2r+2}z^{2t-1}
,
so these are the elements of shape
x^{u}z^{v}
with
u\equiv 0\mod 4
and
v\equiv 1\mod 2
.
In
G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}
, we have
x^{2r}y^{2}z^{2t-1}=x^{2r+2}z^{2t-1+2^{\ell-1}}
, which gives the same kind of elements.

Finally, we consider the case where there is a difference:
r\equiv 0\mod 2
and
s=1
.
In
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}
, we have
x^{2r}y^{2}=x^{2r+2}
,
which are elements of shape
x^{u}
with
u\equiv 2\mod 4
.
These elements are not new as they already appeared in the second case (i.e.
r\equiv 1\mod 2
and
s=0
).
While in
G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}
, we have
x^{2r}y^{2}=x^{2r+2}z^{2^{\ell-1}}
,
which are elements of shape
x^{u}z^{2^{\ell-1}}
for
u\equiv 2\mod 4
.
These elements are new, so in particular there are
2^{n-2}
more conjugacy classes of squares in
G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}
than in
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}
.
∎

4.2.
Kernel sizes

We will write
\Delta
for the augmentation ideal
\Delta(G)
of
\mathbb{F}G
for brevity.
The next argument we will apply is well known and consists in computing the sizes of the kernels of certain maps.
It can be traced back to an idea of Brauer
[
3
, Supplement §9]

and was applied in practice for the first time by Passman
[
21
]
.
We define the standard
2^{m}
-power map

(4.1)

\varphi\colon\Delta/\Delta^{2}\to\Delta^{2^{m}}/\Delta^{1+2^{m}}.

The kernel of a power map
\varphi
is defined to be

(4.2)

K(G)=\{\,R+\Delta^{2}\in\Delta/\Delta^{2}\mid\varphi(R+\Delta^{2})=0+\Delta^{1+2^{m}}\,\}.

Note that this kernel is not an ideal of
\Delta
in general, but still a well-defined set stable under automorphisms of the algebra.

A basis for
\Delta
makes concrete calculations of the kernels feasible,
and we use what is called a Jennings basis, which behaves well with a filtration
\Delta\supseteq\Delta^{2}\supseteq\dotsb
.
We will work with a basis that looks formally the same in each case.
We assume some familiarity with Jennings’ theory for the proof of the next lemma.
If the reader is willing to accept it, then one can safely skip its proof because we will not use Jennings’ theory explicitly elsewhere.

We write capital letters for elements of the augmentation ideal corresponding to group elements, e.g.
X=x+1\in\Delta
for
x\in G
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
G=\langle x,y,z\rangle
be one of the groups
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}
, …,
G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}
and

a non-negative integer.
Set

and

2^{\max\{n,\ell\}}&(\text{$G=G_{\scriptsizedice{5}}$ or $G=G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}$}).\end{cases}

Then

(4.3)

\mathfrak{D}_{k}=\left\{\,X^{r}Y^{s}Z^{t}W^{u}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\begin{gathered}0\leq r\leq 2^{n}-1,\ 0\leq s\leq 2^{m}-1,\\
0\leq t\leq 2^{\ell-1}-1,\ 0\leq u\leq 1,\\
r+s+2t+qu\geq k\end{gathered}\,\right\}

is a basis of
\Delta^{k}
.
In particular, the image of
\mathfrak{D}_{k}\smallsetminus\mathfrak{D}_{k+1}
under the natural projection
\Delta^{k}\to\Delta^{k}/\Delta^{k+1}
is a basis of
\Delta^{k}/\Delta^{k+1}
.

Proof.

Let
M_{k}=G\cap(1+\Delta^{k})
, the
k

G
, for
k\geq 1
.
Observe first that
G^{\prime}=\langle z\rangle
and
\Frat(G)=\langle x^{2},y^{2},z\rangle
are abelian.
It follows from
[
6
, Theorems 11.2 and 12.9]
that
M_{1}=\langle x,y\rangle
and

(4.4)

2^{e-1}<k\leq 2^{e}\implies M_{k}=\mho_{e}(G)\mho_{e-1}(G^{\prime})=\langle x^{2^{e}},y^{2^{e}},z^{2^{e-1}}\rangle

for
e\geq 1
.
In particular, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq e\leq n-1

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\quad x^{2^{e}}

\displaystyle\in M_{2^{e}}\smallsetminus M_{1+2^{e}},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq e\leq m-1

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\quad y^{2^{e}}

\displaystyle\in M_{2^{e}}\smallsetminus M_{1+2^{e}},

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq e\leq\ell-1

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\quad z^{2^{e-1}}

\displaystyle\in M_{2^{e}}\smallsetminus M_{1+2^{e}}

and by the relations of
G
,

w\in M_{q}\smallsetminus M_{1+q}.

Since
x^{2}
and
y^{2}
are central, an element of the Jennings basis can be written in the form

\displaystyle(x+1)^{r_{0}}(y+1)^{s_{0}}(x^{2}+1)^{r_{1}}(y^{2}+1)^{s_{1}}(z+1)^{t_{0}}\dotsm(w+1)^{u}\dotsm

\displaystyle\qquad=(x+1)^{r_{0}}(x^{2}+1)^{r_{1}}\dotsm(y+1)^{s_{0}}(y^{2}+1)^{s_{1}}\dotsm(z+1)^{t_{0}}\dotsm(w+1)^{u}

\displaystyle\qquad=(x+1)^{r_{0}}(x+1)^{r_{1}2}\dotsm(y+1)^{s_{0}}(y+1)^{s_{1}2}\dotsm(z+1)^{t_{0}}\dotsm(w+1)^{u}

\displaystyle\qquad=(x+1)^{r_{0}+\dotsm+r_{n-1}2^{n-1}}(y+1)^{s_{0}+\dotsm+s_{m-1}2^{m-1}}(z+1)^{t_{0}+\dotsm+t_{\ell-2}2^{\ell-2}}(w+1)^{u}

\displaystyle\qquad=(x+1)^{r}(y+1)^{s}(z+1)^{t}(w+1)^{u}

\displaystyle\qquad=X^{r}Y^{s}Z^{t}W^{u}

for

0\leq r_{0},r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n-1},s_{0},s_{1},\dotsc,s_{m-1},t_{0},\dotsc,t_{\ell-2},u\leq 1

where

,

and

.
Hence the assertion follows from Jennings’ theorem
[

, Theorem 3.2]
.
∎

are zero.

Lemma 4.15
.

For
T\in\Delta^{2}
and
\lambda,\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\psi\left(\lambda Z+\mu XZ+\nu YZ+TZ+\Gamma^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\lambda^{2^{\ell-1}}W+\mu^{2^{\ell-1}}X^{2^{\ell-1}}W+\nu^{2^{\ell-1}}Y^{2^{\ell-1}}W+\left(\Gamma^{1+2^{\ell-1}}+\Delta^{d}\right).

Proof.

To prove the lemma, we factor
\psi
as a composition of maps.
Define the square-map

\psi_{i}\colon\Gamma^{2^{i}}/\Gamma^{1+2^{i}}\to\Gamma^{2^{i+1}}/\Gamma^{1+2^{i+1}}

for
i\geq 0
and the natural projection

\pi\colon\Gamma^{2^{\ell-1}}/\Gamma^{1+2^{\ell-1}}\to\Gamma^{2^{\ell-1}}/\left(\Gamma^{1+2^{\ell-1}}+\Delta^{d}\right).

Then
\psi=\pi\circ\psi_{\ell-2}\circ\dotsb\circ\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{0}
.
Note that this expression is well defined as
\ell\geq 2
.

Claim 1:
For
g\in G
and
i\geq 0
, one has
[g,Z^{2^{i}}]\in\Gamma^{2^{i+1}}
.

It suffices to show that
1+g^{-1}Z^{2^{i}}g\equiv 1+Z^{2^{i}}\mod\Gamma^{2^{i+1}}
.
From the general formula
(1+R)^{-1}=1+R+R^{2}+\dotsb
for
R\in\Delta
, we get

1+Z^{-2^{i}}=(1+Z^{2^{i}})^{-1}=1+Z^{2^{i}}+(Z^{2^{i}})^{2}+\dotsb\equiv 1+Z^{2^{i}}\mod\Gamma^{2^{i+1}}.

Note that
\{z^{2^{i}},z^{-2^{i}}\}
is a conjugacy class of
G
.
Thus
1+g^{-1}Z^{2^{i}}g
is equal to
1+Z^{2^{i}}
or
1+Z^{-2^{i}}
and the claim holds.

Claim 2:
For
R,S\in\mathbb{F}G
, one has

\psi_{i}\left(RZ^{2^{i}}+SZ^{2^{i}}+\Gamma^{1+2^{i}}\right)=R^{2}Z^{2^{i+1}}+S^{2}Z^{2^{i+1}}+\Gamma^{1+2^{i+1}}.

From the previous claim we know
[R,Z^{2^{i}}],[S,Z^{2^{i}}]\in\Gamma^{2^{i+1}}
, so

\displaystyle\psi_{i}

\displaystyle\left(RZ^{2^{i}}+SZ^{2^{i}}+\Gamma^{1+2^{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=(RZ^{2^{i}})^{2}+(SZ^{2^{i}})^{2}+[RZ^{2^{i}},SZ^{2^{i}}]+\Gamma^{1+2^{i+1}}

\displaystyle=R^{2}Z^{2^{i+1}}+S^{2}Z^{2^{i+1}}+[R,S]Z^{2^{i+1}}+\Gamma^{1+2^{i+1}}.

As
[R,S]\in\Gamma
, the claim follows.

Now applying the last claim stepwise to
\psi_{0}
,
\psi_{1}
, …,
\psi_{\ell-2}
gives

\displaystyle(\psi_{\ell-2}\circ\dotsb\circ\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{0})(\lambda Z+\mu XZ+\nu YZ+TZ+\Gamma^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad=\lambda^{2^{\ell-1}}W+\mu^{2^{\ell-1}}X^{2^{\ell-1}}W+\nu^{2^{\ell-1}}Y^{2^{\ell-1}}W+T^{2^{\ell-1}}W+\Gamma^{1+2^{\ell-1}}.

As
T^{2^{\ell-1}}\in\Delta^{2^{\ell}}
, this means
T^{2^{\ell-1}}W\in\Delta^{d}
.
Thus applying
\pi
eliminates the last term, and the lemma follows.
∎

Remark 4.21
.

The arguments involved in the group base approximation partly explain the choice we make for the isomorphism in
Theorem

3.1
.
There are of course many more choices for this isomorphism, but

A.2.
Inductive argument

We will now proceed to prove
Theorems

A.1
and
A.2
.
This will be achieved by a double induction.
The outer induction runs on the first parameter, i.e.
n
,
and the inner on the second parameter, i.e.
m
.
We will need the following two cases for the base of the inductions.

Recall the standard presentations of finite
2
-groups of maximal class:
the dihedral, semidihedral and generalized quaternion groups.

\displaystyle D_{2^{n+1}}=\langle\,a,b\mid a^{2}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\quad b^{2^{n}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\quad b^{a}

\displaystyle=b^{-1}\,\rangle;

\displaystyle S_{2^{n+1}}=\langle\,a,b\mid a^{2}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\quad b^{2^{n}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\quad b^{a}

\displaystyle=b^{2^{n-1}-1}\,\rangle;

\displaystyle Q_{2^{n+1}}=\langle\,a,b\mid a^{2}

\displaystyle=b^{2^{n-1}},

\displaystyle\quad b^{2^{n}}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\quad b^{a}

\displaystyle=b^{-1}\,\rangle.

Proposition A.5
.

Assume
\ell\geq 2
.
Then

\displaystyle D_{2^{\ell+2}}\cong G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(1,1,\ell);\quad S_{2^{\ell+2}}\cong G_{\scriptsizedice{5}}(1,1,\ell);\quad Q_{2^{\ell+2}}\cong G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(1,1,\ell).

In particular,
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(1,1,\ell)
,
G_{\scriptsizedice{5}}(1,1,\ell)
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(1,1,\ell)
are pairwise non-isomorphic.

Proof.

Since the three cases have the same kind of isomorphism
a\mapsto y
and
b\mapsto xy
,
we only prove that
Q_{2^{\ell+2}}\cong G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(1,1,\ell)
here.

Let
G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(1,1,\ell)=\langle x,y,z\rangle
and define
a=y
and
b=xy
.
Since

\displaystyle b^{2}

\displaystyle=xyxy=x^{2}y[y,x]y=x^{2}yzy

\displaystyle=z^{2^{\ell-1}}yzy=z^{2^{\ell-1}}y^{2}z[z,y]=z^{2^{\ell}}z[z,y]=z^{-1},

we have, using that
y^{2}
is central in the last equation,

\displaystyle a^{2}

\displaystyle=y^{2}=z^{2^{\ell-1}}=z^{-2^{\ell-1}}=b^{2^{\ell}};

\displaystyle b^{2^{\ell+1}}

\displaystyle=z^{-2^{\ell}}=1;

\displaystyle b^{a}

\displaystyle=(xy)^{y}=y^{-1}xy^{2}=yx=xy[y,x]=xyz=bb^{-2}=b^{-1}.

Hence there is an epimorphism from
Q_{2^{\ell+2}}
to
G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(1,1,\ell)
.
Since both groups have the same order, they must be isomorphic.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

Assume
n\geq 2
.
Then
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(n,1,1)\not\cong G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,1,1)
.

Proof.

Observe that
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(n,1,1)
has a maximal subgroup that is isomorphic to
C_{2^{n-1}}\times C_{2}\times C_{2}
,
namely
\langle x^{2},y,z\rangle
, while
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,1,1)
does not.
Indeed, we have
\Frat(G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,1,1))=\langle x^{2},z\rangle
,
so that the maximal subgroups of
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,1,1)
are
\langle x,z\rangle
,
\langle y,z\rangle
and
\langle xy,x^{2}\rangle
.
Note here that
(xy)^{2}=x^{2}y^{2}z=x^{4}z
, so that
z\in\langle xy,x^{2}\rangle
.
Hence all the maximal subgroups of
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,1,1)
are two-generated.
∎

We are ready for the inductions.

Proof of
Theorems

A.1
and
A.2
.

We first note that when
n=m
, then the isomorphisms

G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(n,m,\ell)\cong G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,m,\ell)
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{3}}(n,m,\ell)\cong G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}(n,m,\ell)\cong G_{\scriptsizedice{5}}(n,m,\ell)

are clear from the presentations.

We proceed by induction on
n
.
If
n=1
, then also
m=1
and the groups
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(n,m,\ell)
,
G_{\scriptsizedice{5}}(n,m,\ell)
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(n,m,\ell)
are pairwise non-isomorphic by
Proposition

A.5
.

So assume that
n\geq 2
and that
Theorems

A.1
and
A.2
hold for smaller values of
n
.
We proceed by induction on
m
.
When
m=1
, then by
Lemma

4.1
the centers of
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(n,m,\ell)
,
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,m,\ell)
,
G_{\scriptsizedice{3}}(n,m,\ell)
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}(n,m,\ell)
are not isomorphic to
the centers of
G_{\scriptsizedice{5}}(n,m,\ell)
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(n,m,\ell)
.
By induction and
Tables

7
and
7
, only
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(n,m,\ell)
or
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,m,\ell)
can map onto
one of the groups
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(n-1,m,\ell)
or
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n-1,m,\ell)
,
so these two groups are not isomorphic to either of
G_{\scriptsizedice{3}}(n,m,\ell)
or
G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}(n,m,\ell)
.
To see that
G_{\scriptsizedice{1}}(n,m,\ell)
is not isomorphic to
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,m,\ell)
note that,
again by induction and
Tables

7
and
7
,

G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,m,\ell)
maps onto
G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(n-1,m,\ell)
, while

does not.
To see that
G_{\scriptsizedice{3}}(n,m,\ell)

G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}(n,m,\ell)
note that, by
Tables

7
and
7
,
exactly one of them maps onto
G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(n-1,m,\ell)
by induction.
To distinguish between
G_{\scriptsizedice{5}}(n,m,\ell)
and
G_{\scriptsizedice{6}}(n,m,\ell)
we observe that by

7
and
7

G_{\scriptsizedice{5}}(n,m,\ell)

is
G_{\scriptsizedice{2}}(n,m,\ell-1)
.
If
\ell=2

G_{\scriptsizedice{5}}(n,m,\ell)
and

If

Consider next

A.1
and

or

Looking on the centers of the groups in

,

G_{\scriptsizedice{3}}(n,m,\ell)\not\cong G_{\scriptsizedice{4}}(n,m,\ell)

To see that

7
and
7
,
exactly one of them maps onto

Finally, to observe that
