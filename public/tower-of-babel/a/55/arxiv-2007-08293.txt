Polymer Dynamics via Cliques: New Conditions for Approximations
Tobias Friedrich, Andreas Göbel, Martin S. Krejca, Marcus Pappik

Abstract
Abstract polymer models are systems of weighted objects, called polymers, equipped with an incompatibility relation. An important quantity associated with such models is the partition function, which is the weighted sum over all sets of compatible polymers. Various approximation problems reduce to approximating the partition function of a polymer model. Central to the existence of such approximation algorithms are weight conditions of the respective polymer model. Such conditions are derived either via complex analysis or via probabilistic arguments. We follow the latter path and establish a new condition—the clique dynamics condition—, which is less restrictive than the ones in the literature. We introduce a new Markov chain where the clique dynamics condition implies rapid mixing by utilizing cliques of incompatible polymers that naturally arise from the translation of algorithmic problems into polymer models. This leads to improved parameter ranges for several approximation algorithms, such as a factor of at least 2 1 / α 2^{1/\alpha} for the hard-core model on bipartite α \alpha -expanders.

Introduction

Statistical physics models systems of interacting particles as probability distributions. This approach explains a variety of real-world phenomena, including ferromagnetism
[
18
]
, segregation
[
29
]
, and real-world network generation
[
6
]
. A characteristic of such systems is that they undergo phase transitions depending on some external parameter. Such phase transitions have been recently linked with
the tractability of computational tasks and have lead to a two-way exchange: tools from statistical physics are used to explain computational phenomena, and tools from computer science are used to explain physical phenomena. An established technique for investigating phase transitions in statistical physics that involves translating the states of a spin system as perturbations from a ground state
[
13
, Chapter 7]
has been recently introduced to computer science as an algorithmic tool for computational tasks of spin systems
[
17
]
.

To motivate the definition of the central mathematical object of this article, we give a high-level description of how to model a spin system in terms of perturbations from a ground state.
Assume we study a
q
-state spin system on a graph
G
. The states of the spin system are usually mappings
\sigma\colon V(G)\rightarrow Q
from the vertices of
G
to some finite set
Q
. Each such configuration
\sigma
has a weight
w(\sigma)\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and the sum of the weights of all the configurations
Z=\sum_{\sigma}(w(\sigma))
is called the
partition function.
For each configuration
\sigma
, the probability distribution that characterizes our system yields
\mu(\sigma)=w(\sigma)/Z
. Let
\sigma_{0}
be the ground state we use in this translation. Given a configuration
\sigma
, we identify the set of vertices
D\subseteq V(G)
where, for each
v\in D
, we have
\sigma_{0}(v)\neq\sigma(v)
. Observe that we can uniquely identify this configuration by a set
\Gamma
whose elements
\gamma
consist of a connected component of
G[D]
together with the restriction of
\sigma
on this component.
Furthermore, we assign a weight
w_{\gamma}
to each
\gamma\in\Gamma
, such that
\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma}w_{\gamma}=w(\sigma)/w(\sigma_{0})
. Thus, provided that all such sets of pairs
\Gamma
contain no two pairs
\gamma
,
\gamma^{\prime}
that are incompatible, i.e.
\Gamma
cannot be uniquely decoded to an assignment because for example
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
map the same vertex to a different element in
Q
, there is a bijection between the configurations
\sigma
and the sets
\Gamma
. Furthermore, the distribution
\mu
is expressed as a distribution over the sets
\Gamma
, since it retains the property that the probability of
\Gamma
is proportional to its weight. Such a construction suggests the following definition.

A
polymer model

\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{C},w,\ncomp)
is a tuple consisting of a non-empty, countable set
\mathcal{C}
, a set
w=\{w_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}}
of positive real weights and a reflexive and symmetric relation
\ncomp\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{2}
. The elements
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}
are called
polymers
. The relation
\ncomp
is called the
incompatibility
relation and, for
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
, we say that
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
are
incompatible
if
\gamma\ncomp\gamma^{\prime}
, and that they are
compatible
otherwise.
We call a
finite
subset
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{C}
a
polymer family
if and only if all polymers of
\Gamma
are pairwise compatible. Given a polymer model
\mathcal{P}
, let
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{P})}
denote the set of
all
polymer families of
\mathcal{P}
. Note that
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{P})}
is countable.
The
partition function
of
\mathcal{P}
is defined as

Z(\mathcal{P})=\sum\nolimits_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{P})}}\prod\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma}w_{\gamma},

(1)

which we require to be finite.
Further, the
Gibbs distribution
of
\mathcal{P}
is the probability distribution
\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}
over
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{P})}
such that, for all
\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{P})}
,

\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma}}\right)=\frac{\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma}w_{\gamma}}{Z(\mathcal{P})}.

(2)

A helpful interpretation for understanding the definition of a polymer model is the following.
Ignoring the reflexivity of
\ncomp
, we view the pair
(\mathcal{C},\ncomp)
as a graph, which we call the
polymer graph
. We observe that the families of
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{P})}
correspond to the independent sets of
(\mathcal{C},\ncomp)
. Thus, for the special case where
w_{\gamma}=\lambda\in\mathds{R}
, for each
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}
, the distribution
\mu
is the hard-core model
[
28
]
on the polymer graph and
Z(\mathcal{P})
is the independence polynomial
[
27
]
.

We aim to maximize the parameter range for which the following two computational tasks can be done in polynomial time.

[(1)]

1.

Approximately sampling from the Gibbs distribution of a polymer model, i.e., return a random family
\Gamma
from a distribution with total-variation distance of at most
\varepsilon
from
\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}
.

2.

Returning an estimate
\widetilde{Z}
, such that
(1-\varepsilon)Z(\mathcal{P})\leq\widetilde{Z}\leq(1+\varepsilon)Z(\mathcal{P})
.

1.1
Known Algorithmic Results

There is an expanding list of results that utilize abstract polymer models to obtain efficient approximation and sampling algorithms for new parameter regimes for various spin systems on graphs. This line of research was initiated by Helmuth et al.
[
17
]
, who used polymers to obtain polynomial-time approximation and sampling algorithms at a regime where the weight of the interactions of particles with an external field is low. For problems that are hard to approximate on general inputs, polynomial-time approximation algorithms are derived by utilizing restrictions upon the input graph of a spin system. Examples include spin systems on expander graphs
[
14
,
19
,
24
]
, the hard-core model on unbalanced bipartite graphs
[
3
]
, and the ferromagnetic Potts model on
d
-dimensional lattices
[
2
]
. Polymer models have also been used to approximate and sample edge spin systems (holant problems) at low temperatures
[
4
]
.

Translating a spin system on a graph
G
with
n
vertices into an abstract polymer model commonly results in an exponential number of polymers in terms of
n
—as can be observed in our initial example.
However, we are interested in approximation and sampling algorithms with a runtime polynomial in
n
.
There are two main approaches for such algorithms.

(i) Cluster Expansion

This approach considers complex weights for the polymers and is based on an infinite series expansion of
\ln Z
(the
cluster expansion
).
The essential element for polynomial-time computation is a theorem of Kotecký and Preiss
[
22
, Theorem 1]
, a condition for establishing absolute convergence of the cluster expansion. By satisfying this condition, the cluster expansion is truncated to its most significant terms, obtaining an
\varepsilon
-additive approximation for
\ln Z
. Computing the significant terms is achieved by enumerating connected induced subgraphs of the polymer graph of size up to
\log|\mathcal{C}|
. By an algorithm of Patel and Regts, the enumeration takes polynomial time in terms of the input graph of the spin system
[
26
]
. The
\varepsilon
-additive approximation of
\ln Z
immediately gives a multiplicative
\varepsilon
-approximation for
Z
. The runtime of this approach is commonly
n^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\log\Delta}}}\right)}
, where
\Delta
is the maximum degree of the input graph
G
for the spin system and
n=|V(G)|
.
Approximating
Z
together with the self-reducibility of the polymer model yields a sampling algorithm for
\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}

[
17
]
.

(ii) Markov Chain Monte Carlo

Initiated by Chen et al.
[
5
]
, this approach defines a Markov chain with state space
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{P})}
and stationary distribution
\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}
. The Markov chain requires the polymer model to have originated from a spin system on a graph
G
with
n
vertices. Iteratively, the chain samples a polymer
\gamma
with probability proportional to its weight
w_{\gamma}
and then adds or removes
\gamma
from its state if possible. When the
mixing condition

[
5
, Definition 1]
is satisfied, the Markov chain converges to
\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}
after
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\log n}}}\right)
iterations. The mixing condition matches a convergence condition arising from an analysis by Fernández et al.
[
11
]
of another stochastic process of polymers on lattices. An
\varepsilon
-approximate sampler for
\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}
is obtained by simulating the Markov chain. The computational challenge for this approach is to sample the polymer
\gamma
in order to perform a transition of the Markov chain. As Chen et al.
[
5
]
show, this can be done in expected constant time provided the
sampling condition

[
5
, Definition 4]
is satisfied. This results in an
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\log n}}}\right)
algorithm for sampling from the Gibbs distribution of a spin system. Using Simulated Annealing, Chen et al. convert this sampler into a randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS) for
Z
that runs in expected
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{2}\log n}}}\right)
time.

Comparison of Established Conditions for the Methods Above

A number of conditions for the convergence of the cluster expansion have appeared in the literature
[
7
,
12
,
22
]
. The condition of Fernández and Procacci
[
12
]
is the least restrictive among them, i.e., the other conditions imply it. Thus, using the Fernández–Procacci condition, one could potentially obtain approximation algorithms for broader parameter ranges than the ones obtained by using, e.g., the Kotecký–Preiss condition
[
22
]
.
However, the condition by Kotecký and Preiss is convenient to apply in polymer models of vertex spin systems and comes with implications on the rate of convergence of the cluster expansion used in algorithmic settings. When compared to cluster expansion conditions (restricted to non-negative real weights), the mixing condition of Chen et al.
[
5
]
is less restrictive than the Kotecký–Preiss condition, however, it is incomparable with the Fernández–Procacci condition. Note that the fast run-times of Chen et al.
[
5
]
are dependent on the sampling condition, which imposes the largest restriction on the parameter range of the applications it is used for.

1.2
Our Results

We study a new Markov chain
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
for abstract polymer models with stationary distribution
\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}
.
The dynamics of our Markov chain are based on a
clique cover,
i.e., a set
\Lambda=\{\Lambda_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
with
\bigcup\Lambda=\mathcal{C}
such that the polymers in each clique
\Lambda_{i}
are pairwise incompatible. Note that families of compatible polymers contain at most one polymer per clique. At each step, our Markov chain chooses
i\in[m]
uniformly at random and samples a family in
\Lambda_{i}
according to the distribution
\mu_{|\Lambda_{i}}
defined as follows. For
\gamma\in\Lambda_{i}
, we have
\mu_{|\Lambda_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\{\gamma\}}}\right)=w_{\gamma}/Z_{|\Lambda_{i}}
and, for the empty set,
\mu_{|\Lambda_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\emptyset}}\right)=1/Z_{|\Lambda_{i}}
, where
Z_{|\Lambda_{i}}=1+\sum_{\gamma\in\Lambda_{i}}w_{\gamma}
. If the empty family is chosen and
X_{t}
contains a polymer from
\Lambda_{i}
, then the chain removes this polymer. If the family chosen contains a polymer, then, if possible, the chain adds this polymer to its state. For a detailed description of our chain, please refer to
\Cref
def:markov_chain.

Our chain can be viewed as a natural generalization of the (spin) Glauber dynamics (cf.
[
10
, insert/delete chain]
). Any abstract polymer model has a trivial clique cover, where each clique contains exactly one polymer. From the choice of sampling from
\mu_{|\Lambda_{i}}
for each clique chosen at each iteration, the clique dynamics with the trivial clique cover coincides with the Glauber dynamics.
Clique covers with a much smaller number of cliques than the number of vertices arise naturally from the translation of spin systems into polymer models. For example, the translation we discussed earlier in the introduction yields a clique cover with
n
cliques, one for each vertex in the original graph
G
. Since the number of polymers is commonly exponential in the size of
G
, our chain utilizes that a family of compatible polymers may contain at most one polymer from each polymer clique. The chain of Chen et al.
[
5
]
also utilizes this fact, however, in a more restricted setting and with a different sampling distribution for each vertex clique.

Central to our mixing time analysis for this chain is the following condition.

Condition 1

(Clique Dynamics)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{C},w,\ncomp)
be a polymer model, and let
f\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathds{R}_{>0}
. We say that
\mathcal{P}

satisfies the clique dynamics condition with
f
if and only if, for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}
,

\sum\nolimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\colon\gamma^{\prime}\ncomp\gamma,\\
\gamma^{\prime}\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}\Big(f(\gamma^{\prime})\frac{w_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{1+w_{\gamma^{\prime}}}\Big)\leq f(\gamma)\ .

We show that when the clique dynamics condition is satisfied, the mixing time of our Markov chain is polynomial in the number of cliques in the clique cover and logarithmic in
f
(
\Cref
thm:markov_chain).
When restricted to the setting of Chen et al.
[
5
]
, the clique dynamics condition is implied by the mixing condition and thus less restrictive. The function
f
in our condition makes it easily comparable with the conditions for cluster expansion. Unlike the Kotecký–Preiss condition
[
22
]
, the clique dynamics condition allows for a more flexible choice of
f
in order to obtain a greater parameter range for the algorithmic applications (cf.
[
4
, Remark 2.4]
).

We further show that the clique dynamics condition is more general than the Fernández–Procacci
[
12
]
condition for the cluster expansion—and consequently more general than the Kotecký–Preiss condition
[
22
]
(see
\Cref
sec:comparison). An implication of our analysis is that cluster expansion conditions imply our condition for the mixing time of clique dynamics. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first connection between cluster expansion and mixing times of Markov chains for polymer models and might be of independent interest to the statistical-physics community. This is in line with the special case of the hard-core model, where the parameter range on the reals has a bigger radius than on the complex plane
[
27
]
.

2
Preliminaries

We denote the set of all natural numbers, including
0
, by
\mathds{N}
and the set of all real numbers by
\mathds{R}
. For an
n\in\mathds{N}
, let
[n]=[1,n]\cap\mathds{N}
. If the polymer model
\mathcal{P}
is clear from context, we may drop the index and write
\mathcal{F}
,
Z
, and
\mu
instead of
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{P})}
,
Z(\mathcal{P})
, and
\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}
, respectively.

We use the following formal notion of approximate sampling.
Let
\nu
be a probability distribution on a countable state space
\Omega
.
For
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, we say that a distribution
\xi
on
\Omega
is an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
\nu
if and only if
d_{\text{TV}}\big(\nu,\xi\big)\leq\varepsilon
, where
d_{\text{TV}}\big(\cdot,\cdot\big)
denotes the total-variation distance.
Further, we say that we can
\varepsilon
-approximately sample from
\nu
if and only if we can sample from any distribution
\xi
such that
\xi
is an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
\nu
.

We are also interested in approximating the partition function of polymer models, which we define as follows.
For
x\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, we call a random variable
X
a
randomized
\varepsilon
-approximation for
x
if and only if

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{(1-\varepsilon)x\leq X\leq(1+\varepsilon)x}}}\right]\geq\frac{3}{4}.

Note that if
x
is the output to an algorithmic problem on some instance and independent samples of
X
can be obtained in polynomial time in the instance size and
1/\varepsilon
, then this translates to the definition of an FPRAS.

2.1
Restricted Polymer Models

We base the transitions of our Markov chain for a polymer model
(\mathcal{C},w,\ncomp)
on restricted sets
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
. We define the set of all polymer families
restricted to
\mathcal{B}

to be
\mathcal{F}_{|\mathcal{B}}=\mathcal{F}\cap 2^{\mathcal{B}}
. Further, we define the restricted partition function
Z_{|\mathcal{B}}
to be
\cref
eq:partitionFunction but with
\mathcal{F}^{(\mathcal{P})}
replaced by
\mathcal{F}_{|\mathcal{B}}
. Similarly, we define the restricted Gibbs distribution
\mu_{|\mathcal{B}}
to be a probability distribution over
\mathcal{F}_{|\mathcal{B}}
, i.e.,
\cref
eq:gibbsDistribution but with
Z(\mathcal{P})
replaced by
Z_{|\mathcal{B}}
. Our restrictions are special sets of polymers, which we define next.

By definition, for a polymer model, a polymer family
\Gamma
cannot contain incompatible polymers. Thus, when considering a subset
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
where all polymers are pairwise incompatible, at most one polymer of
\mathcal{B}
is in
\Gamma
. We call such a subset
\mathcal{B}
a
polymer clique.

Last, for an
m\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
, we call a set
\Lambda=\{\Lambda_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
of polymer cliques a
polymer clique cover
if and only if
\bigcup\Lambda=\mathcal{C}
, and we call
m
the
size
of
\Lambda
. Note that the elements of
\Lambda
need not be pairwise disjoint. Further note that, for each
i\in[m]
, the partition function restricted to
\Lambda_{i}
boils down to

\displaystyle Z_{|\Lambda_{i}}=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{|\Lambda_{i}}}\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma}w_{\gamma}=1+\sum_{\gamma\in\Lambda_{i}}w_{\gamma},

as the polymers of
\Lambda_{i}
are pairwise incompatible and thus each family of

In order to set our clique dynamics condition in the context of existing conditions for absolute convergence of the cluster expansion, we compare it to the condition of Fernández and Procacci

As Fernández and Procacci show, their condition is an improvement over other known conditions, including the Dobrushin condition
[

and the Kotecký–Preiss condition

Definition 10

4.2

By now, we mainly discussed conditions for approximately sampling from the Gibbs distribution.
We now discuss how to turn this into a randomized approximation for the partition function.

However, note that the obvious way for applying self-reducibility, namely based on single polymers, might take

To circumvent this problem, we propose a self-reducibility argument based on polymer cliques.
By doing so, the number of reductions is bounded by the size of the clique cover that is used, thus adding no major overhead to the runtime of our proposed approximate sampling scheme.
Besides this idea of applying self-reducibility based on cliques, most of our arguments are analogous to known applications, like in

Appendix: Coupling Lemma

We discuss
\Cref
lemma:exp_potential in detail. First, we explain why the assumptions of the original theorem by Greenberg et al.
[
15
, Theorem
3.3
]
are insufficient. With
\Cref
ex:counterexampleToCouplingTheorem, we provide a counterexample. Last, we prove our version of the theorem.

Besides some minor generalizations, the most important difference between
\Cref
lemma:exp_potential and Theorem
3.3
by Greenberg et al.
[
15
]
is that we assume the coupling to be defined for
all
pairs of states.
We also require the expected change of
\delta
as well as the probability bound to hold for
all
pairs of states.
In contrast, Greenberg et al.
[
15
]
claim that it is sufficient if these properties hold for neighboring states with respect to some adjacency structure.
In what follows, we argue that this does not always suffice.

It is well known that couplings on adjacent states can be extended to all pairs of states such that the expected decrease of
\delta
for adjacent states implies an expected decrease for all pairs of states
[
9
]
.
However, a similar argument does not necessarily hold for bounds on the probability that
\delta
changes by at least a certain amount.
More precisely, it is possible to construct a Markov chain and a coupling such that

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{|\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\delta(x,y)|\geq\eta\delta(x,y)\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{|\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\delta(x,y)|\geq\eta\delta(x,y)}X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y}}\right.}}}\right]\geq\kappa

holds for all pairs of adjacent states
x,y\in\Omega
but not for all pairs of non-adjacent states.

Thus, Theorem
3.3
by Greenberg et al.
[
15
]
can be used to deduce upper bounds for mixing times that contradict known lower bounds.
We demonstrate this by giving a simple counterexample (
\Cref
ex:counterexampleToCouplingTheorem).
Using Theorem
3.3
by Greenberg et al.
[
15
]
, we bound the mixing time of a symmetric random walk on a cycle of size
n
by
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(n)^{2}\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1/\varepsilon}}\right)}}}\right)
.
This contradicts the lower bound of
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1/\varepsilon}}\right)}}}\right)
that results from the diameter of the state space
[
23
, Chapter
7.1.2
]
.

Example 20
.

We consider a symmetric random walk on a cycle of length

(i.e.,

In order to have the desired self-loop probability, we define the transitions

Let

(9)

Before we prove the theorem, we discuss the changes we made in the phrasing of the theorem.
Different to the original theorem by Greenberg et al.
[
16
, Lemma 3.5]
, we include a filtration and we use indicator functions. Our reasons are as follows. The proof of
\Cref

(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
such that, for all

is finite and

\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{t+1}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{Z_{t+1}}\mathcal{F}_{t}}}\right.}}}\right]
is itself a random variable and that the inequality
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{t+1}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{Z_{t+1}}\mathcal{F}_{t}}}\right.}}}\right]\geq Z_{t}
is stronger than
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{t+1}}}}\right]\geq\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{t}}}}\right]
(which follows by the law of total expectation). Hence, we require a filtration.

Second, the indicator functions make sure that
\cref

S
) only have to hold as long as

did not stop. Afterward, they are trivially satisfied. This is important, as

is bounded from below by

such that

, then

does not hold. However, this implies that
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{(S_{t+1}-S_{t})^{2}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{(S_{t+1}-S_{t})^{2}}\mathcal{F}_{t}}}\right.}}}\right]=0
(since the process is now almost surely deterministic), which violates
\cref
eq:varianceBound if not for the indicator functions.

Note that our additional assumptions in
\Cref

[
16

\displaystyle\forall x\in\{y\in\mathds{R}\mid\exists\omega\in\Omega\colon X_{t}(\omega)=y\land t<T(\omega)\}\colon\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{X_{t}-X_{t+1}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{X_{t}-X_{t+1}}X_{t}=x}}\right.}}}\right]\leq\delta

(12)

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{(X_{t}-X_{t+1})\cdot\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t<T}}\right\}}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{(X_{t}-X_{t+1})\cdot\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t<T}}\right\}}}\mathcal{F}_{t}}}\right.}}}\right]\leq\delta\cdot\mathds{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t<T}}\right\}}.

On the other hand,

Since for all

More formally, first note that the indicator functions in

\mathcal{F}_{t}

\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{X_{t}-X_{t+1}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{X_{t}-X_{t+1}}X_{t}=x}}\right.}}}\right]=\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{X_{t}-X_{t+1}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{X_{t}-X_{t+1}}\mathcal{F}_{t}}}\right.}}}\right](\omega)
.

Proof of
\Cref
lemma:exp_potential

We now start with the stating the proof of the coupling theorem, which we restate here for convenience.

See
5

Proof.

We aim to bound the expected time until
\delta
hits
0
for the coupled copies
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
of
\mathcal{M}
and for all pairs of starting states
x,y\in\Omega
.
This results in a bound on the expected coupling time, and, because
\mathcal{M}
is ergodic, also bounds
\tau_{\mathcal{M}}
(see, for example, Chapter
11
by Mitzenmacher and Upfal
[
25
]
for a detailed discussion).

We start by defining a scaled potential
\delta^{\prime}
such that, for all
x,y\in\Omega

\delta^{\prime}(x,y)=\delta(x,y)/d
.
Note that
\delta^{\prime}
takes values in
\{0\}\cup[1,D/d]

t\in\mathds{N}

X_{t}=Y_{t}\leftrightarrow\delta(X_{t},Y_{t})=0\leftrightarrow\delta^{\prime}(X_{t},Y_{t})=0.

Further, for all
x,y\in\Omega

\cref
eq:driftCondition, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\delta^{\prime}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\delta^{\prime}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})}X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})}X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\delta(x,y)=\delta^{\prime}(x,y)

and, by
\cref

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{|\delta^{\prime}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\delta^{\prime}(x,y)|\geq\eta\delta^{\prime}(x,y)\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{|\delta^{\prime}(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\delta^{\prime}(x,y)|\geq\eta\delta^{\prime}(x,y)}X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{|\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\delta(x,y)|\geq\eta\delta(x,y)\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{|\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\delta(x,y)|\geq\eta\delta(x,y)}X_{t}=x,Y_{t}=y}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\geq\kappa.

We define the stochastic processes whose expected hitting time we bound by
\varphi=(\varphi_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
, where
\varphi_{t}=\delta^{\prime}(X_{t},Y_{t})
.
Further, for all

\displaystyle\overline{\ln}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\begin{cases}2\ln(2)x-2\ln(2)&\text{ if }x\in[0,1),\\
\ln(x)&\text{ if }x\in[1,D/d],\end{cases}

\psi=(\psi_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}

for every pair

To this end, we aim to apply
\Cref

\psi
(with the natural filtration of

\Cref

(
\cref

Let
t\in\mathds{N}

eq:exp_potential:4,eq:exp_potential:5 and observing that for

Proof of
\Cref
lemma:convergence

See
7

Proof.

First, note that
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)
is irreducible, as there is a positive probability to go from any polymer family
\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}
to the empty polymer family
\emptyset
in a finite number of steps by consecutively removing each polymer
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
Similarly, there is a positive probability to go from
\emptyset
to any polymer family
\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
in a finite number of steps by consecutively adding all polymers
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
.

We proceed by proving that
\mu^{(\mathcal{P})}
, which we abbreviate as
\mu
, is a stationary distribution of
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)
.
To this end, we show that
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)
satisfies the detailed-balance condition with respect to
\mu
.
That is, for all
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, it holds that

\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma}}\right)\cdot P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}}}\right)=\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma^{\prime}}}\right)\cdot P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma}}\right).

(20)

Note that it is sufficient to check
\cref
eq:detailedBalance for all pairs of states with a symmetric difference of exactly one polymer.

Let
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
and assume without loss of generality that
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\cup\{\gamma\}
for some polymer
\gamma\notin\Gamma
.
Note that, by
\cref
eq:transitions,
P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}}}\right)=w_{\gamma}\cdot P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma}}\right)
.
Further, by definition of the Gibbs distribution, we have
\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma^{\prime}}}\right)=w_{\gamma}\cdot\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma}}\right)
.
Thus, we get

\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma}}\right)\cdot P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}}}\right)=\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma}}\right)\cdot w_{\gamma}\cdot P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma}}\right)=\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma^{\prime}}}\right)\cdot P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma}}\right),

which shows that
\mu
is a stationary distribution of
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)
.

Finally, we argue that
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)
is ergodic. Note that an irreducible Markov chain has a stationary distribution if and only if it is positive recurrent.
In addition, every state of
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)
has a positive self-loop probability, which implies that the chain is aperiodic.
This shows that
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)
is ergodic and concludes the proof.
∎

Proof.

We aim to apply

\delta
.
We do so by utilizing the function

\Gamma

\Gamma^{\prime}

, which only is the case when
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime}
.

We continue by constructing a coupling between two copies of
\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)
, namely between
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
.
We couple these chains such that, for each transition,

•

both choose the same index
i\in[m]
and

•

both draw the same polymer family
\Gamma_{\Delta}\in\mathcal{F}_{|\Lambda_{i}}
from
\mu_{|\Lambda_{i}}
.

This constitutes a valid coupling, as each chain transitions according to its desired marginal transition probabilities.

We now show for all
t\in\mathds{N}
and
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})}X_{t}=\Gamma,Y_{t}=\Gamma^{\prime}}}\right.}}}\right]\leq\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}).

Note that this trivially holds if
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime}
, as the chains
X
and
Y
behave identically from then on. Thus, we are left with the case that
\Gamma\neq\Gamma^{\prime}
, which implies that
|\Gamma\oplus\Gamma^{\prime}|\geq 1
.

We introduce the following notation.
For all
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}
, let
N(\gamma)=\{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\mid\gamma^{\prime}\ncomp\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\neq\gamma\}
denote the neighborhood of
\gamma
.
We extend this definition to arbitrary subsets of polymers
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
by
N(\mathcal{B})=\bigcup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{B}}N(\gamma)
.

Let
\Delta=\Gamma\oplus\Gamma^{\prime}
, and let
\gamma\in\Delta
.
Assume without loss of generality that
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
By
\cref
eq:transitions, with probability
z_{\gamma}/m
, the chain
X
removes
\gamma
, causing
\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})
to decrease by at least
\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)
.
Further, if
\gamma\in\Delta\setminus N(\Delta)
, with probability
w_{\gamma}z_{\gamma}/m
, the chain
Y
adds
\gamma
and the chain
X
remains in its state.
Again,
\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})
decreases by
\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)
.
Note that the case
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}
is exactly symmetric with
X
and
Y
swapped.

Let
\delta^{-}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})
denote the expected (conditional) decrease of
\delta
.
By the observations above, we see that

\delta^{-}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})=\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}+\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta\setminus N(\Delta)}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)w_{\gamma}\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}(1+w_{\gamma})-\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta\cap N(\Delta)}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)w_{\gamma}\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}.

Moreover,
\delta
increases whenever a polymer
\gamma
is added to only one of both chains.
This only occurs if
\gamma\in N(\Delta)\setminus\Delta
and has probability
w_{\gamma}z_{\gamma}/m
for each such polymer.
Similarly to the expected decrease, we denote the expected increase by
\delta^{+}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})
. We bound

\displaystyle\delta^{+}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})\leq\sum_{\gamma\in N(\Delta)\setminus\Delta}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)w_{\gamma}\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}=\sum_{\gamma\in N(\Delta)}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)w_{\gamma}\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}-\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta\cap N(\Delta)}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)w_{\gamma}\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in N(\gamma)}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})w_{\gamma^{\prime}}\frac{z_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{m}-\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta\cap N(\Delta)}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)w_{\gamma}\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}.

Together, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})}X_{t}=\Gamma,Y_{t}=\Gamma^{\prime}}}\right.}}}\right]=\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})+\delta^{+}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})-\delta^{-}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\leq\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})+\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in N(\gamma)}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})w_{\gamma^{\prime}}\frac{z_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{m}-\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}(1+w_{\gamma})

\displaystyle\quad=\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})+\sum_{\gamma\in\Delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in N(\gamma)}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})w_{\gamma^{\prime}}\frac{z_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{m}-\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}(1+w_{\gamma})}}\right).

We proceed by showing that, for each
\gamma\in\Delta
, the respective summand in the sum above is at most zero.
By the definition of
\delta^{\prime}
, we get

\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in N(\gamma)}\delta^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})w_{\gamma^{\prime}}\frac{z_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{m}-\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)\frac{z_{\gamma}}{m}(1+w_{\gamma})=\frac{1}{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in N(\gamma)}f(\gamma^{\prime})\frac{w_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{1+w_{\gamma^{\prime}}}-f(\gamma)}}\right).

By the definition of
N(\gamma)
and since
\mathcal{P}
satisfies the clique dynamics condition, we bound

\frac{1}{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in N(\gamma)}f(\gamma^{\prime})\frac{w_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{1+w_{\gamma^{\prime}}}-f(\gamma)}}\right)=\frac{1}{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\colon\gamma^{\prime}\ncomp\gamma\\
\gamma^{\prime}\neq\gamma\end{subarray}}f(\gamma^{\prime})\frac{w_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{1+w_{\gamma^{\prime}}}-f(\gamma)}}\right)\leq 0.

Consequently, we get that

\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})}X_{t}=\Gamma,Y_{t}=\Gamma^{\prime}}}\right.}}}\right]\leq\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}).

We now show that there are values
\eta,\kappa\in(0,1)
such that, for all
t\in\mathds{N}
and all
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
with
\Gamma\neq\Gamma^{\prime}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\big[{|\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})|\geq\eta\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})\ \big|\ \vphantom{|\delta(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})-\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})|\geq\eta\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})}X_{t}=\Gamma,Y_{t}=\Gamma^{\prime}}\big]\geq\kappa.

(21)

Note that every polymer family in
\mathcal{F}
has at most
m
polymers because it can have at most one polymer from each polymer clique.
Thus, for
\Delta=\Gamma\oplus\Gamma^{\prime}
, we bound
|\Delta|\leq 2m
.
Consequently, there is at least one polymer
\gamma\in\Delta
such that
\delta^{\prime}(\gamma)\geq\delta(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})/(2m)

Assume without loss of generality that
\gamma\in\Gamma

With probability

deletes
\gamma

Thus,
\cref
eq:probabilityBound is true for

and

.

It remains is to determine

such that, for all
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
with
\Gamma\neq\Gamma^{\prime}

We choose

thm:samplingTrunc:ctc, we get that
d_{\text{TV}}\big(\mu,\mu_{\leq k}\big)\leq\varepsilon/2
.
Further, note that truncation preserves the clique dynamics condition for the same function
f
and does not increase any quantity that is used for bounding the mixing time.
Thus,
\tau_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon/2}}\right)\leq\tau_{\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon/2}}\right)
, and we obtain
d_{\text{TV}}\big(\mu_{\leq k},P_{k}^{t^{*}}(\emptyset,\cdot)\big)\leq\varepsilon/2
for our choice of
t^{*}
.

It remains to show that the runtime is bounded by
\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({m/\varepsilon}}}\right)
.
Analogously to the proof of
\Cref
thm:sampling, due to
\cref
thm:samplingTrunc:prt,thm:samplingTrunc:pmc, we know that
\tau_{\mathcal{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{P}}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon/2}}\right)\in\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({m/\varepsilon}}}\right)
, which implies
\tau_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon/2}}\right)\in\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({m/\varepsilon}}}\right)
.
Also analogously, it holds that each step can be done in
\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({m}}}\right)
, except for sampling, for all
i\in[m]
, from
\mu_{|\Lambda_{i}}
.
However, note that, for all
i\in[m]
, we only need to sample from
\mu_{|\Lambda_{i}^{\leq k}}
.
We do so by enumerating
\Lambda_{i}^{\leq k}
in time
t(k)
.
By our choice of
k
and by
\cref
thm:samplingTrunc:ctc, this takes time at most

t(k)=t\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2Bm}{\varepsilon}}}\right)}}\right)\in\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\frac{m}{\varepsilon}}}}\right),

which proves that we can
\varepsilon
-approximately sample from
\mu
in the desired runtime.

Showing that we can
\varepsilon
-approximate
Z
in time
\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({m/\varepsilon}}}\right)
is done analogously.
By
\Cref
lemma:model_truncation and
\cref
thm:samplingTrunc:ctc, we know that for
k=g^{-1}(2Bm/\varepsilon)
it holds that
\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon/2}\leq Z_{\leq k}/Z\leq 1
, which implies
\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon/2}Z\leq Z_{\leq k}\leq Z
.
As argued above, the truncation of the polymer model
\mathcal{P}
to this size
k
satisfies the conditions of
\Cref
thm:sampling, where the sampling from each clique is done by ignoring polymers larger than
k
.
Thus, by
\Cref
thm:appx_partition_function, we obtain an
\varepsilon/2
-approximation for
Z_{\leq k}
in time
\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({2m/\varepsilon}}}\right)=\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({m/\varepsilon}}}\right)
.
Noting that, for
\varepsilon\leq 1
, it holds that

1-\varepsilon\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon/2}\text{ and }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right)\leq 1+\varepsilon,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix E
Appendix: Hard-Core Model on Bipartite Expanders

In order to demonstrate how
\Cref
thm:sampling_trunc improves known bounds for the algorithmic use of polymer models, we investigate the hard-core model for high fugacity
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
on bipartite
\alpha
-expanders with bounded maximum degree
\Delta
.
For a graph
(V,E)
and an
S\subseteq V
, let
N_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)
denote the set of all vertices that are adjacent to a vertex in
S
.

Definition 23

(bipartite
\alpha
-expander)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with partition
V=V_{\text{L}}\cup V_{\text{R}}
.
For all
i\in\{\text{L},\text{R}\}
, we call
S\subseteq V_{i}

small
if and only if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S}}\right|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{i}}}\right|/2
.
For all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, graph
G
is a
bipartite
\alpha
-expander
if and only if, for all small sets of vertices
S
, it holds that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|N_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)}}\right|\geq(1+\alpha)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S}}\right|
.

For any graph
G
, the hard-core partition function is a graph polynomial of some parameter
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, called fugacity.
Let
\mathcal{I}_{G}
be the set of all independent sets in
G
.
The hard-core partition function for fugacity
\lambda
is now formally defined as

Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G,\lambda}}\right)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{G}}\lambda^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|I}}\right|}.

We approximate
Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G,\lambda}}\right)
in terms of the partition function of two polymer models, constructed as proposed by Jenssen et al.
[
19
]
.
For a bipartite
\alpha
-expander
G
with bounded degree
\Delta
, we consider the graph
G^{2}
, which is the graph with vertices
V
and an edge between
v,u\in V
if
v,u
have at most distance
2
in
G
.
For all
i\in\{\text{L},\text{R}\}
, we define a polymer model
\mathcal{P}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}=(\mathcal{C}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)},w^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)},\ncomp)
as follows:

•

each polymer
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}
is defined by a non-empty set of vertices
\overline{\gamma}\subseteq V_{i}
such that
\overline{\gamma}
is small and induces a connected subgraph in
G^{2}
,

•

for
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}
, let
w_{\gamma}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}=\lambda^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|}/\big((1+\lambda)^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|N_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\overline{\gamma}}}\right)}}\right|}\big)
, and

•

two polymers
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}
are incompatible if and only if there are vertices
v\in\overline{\gamma},w\in\overline{\gamma^{\prime}}
with graph distance at most
1
in
G^{2}
.

To ease notation, for all
i\in\{\text{L},\text{R}\}
, we write
\mu^{(i)}
and
Z^{(i)}
instead of
\mu^{(\mathcal{P}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)})}
and
Z(\mathcal{P}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)})
, respectively.

We use
\mathcal{P}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{L}}}\right)}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{R}}}\right)}
for approximating the hard-core partition function of bipartite
\alpha
-expanders in the following sense.

Lemma 24

(
[
19
, Lemma
19
]
)
.

Given a bipartite
\alpha
-expander
G=(V_{\text{L}}\cup V_{\text{R}},E)
with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{\text{L}}\cup V_{\text{R}}}}\right|=n
, let
Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G,\lambda}}\right)
denote its hard-core partition function with fugacity
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, and let the polymer models
\mathcal{P}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{L}}}\right)},\mathcal{P}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{R}}}\right)}
be defined as above.
For all
\lambda\geq\mathrm{e}^{11/\alpha}
, it holds that

(1-\mathrm{e}^{-n})Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G,\lambda}}\right)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\lambda}}\right)^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{\text{R}}}}\right|}Z^{(\text{L})}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\lambda}}\right)^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{\text{L}}}}\right|}Z^{(\text{R})}\leq(1+\mathrm{e}^{-n})Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G,\lambda}}\right).

To apply
\Cref
thm:sampling_trunc, we have to fix a polymer clique cover
\Lambda
for each polymer model
\mathcal{P}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}
with
i\in\{\text{L},\text{R}\}
.
Based on the incompatibility relation, a natural choice is to define, for each
v\in V_{i}
, a clique
\Lambda_{v}
such that
\gamma\in\Lambda_{v}
if and only if
v\in\overline{\gamma}
.
As we need to verify the clique dynamics condition, it is useful to have a bound on the number of incompatible polymers, which the following lemma provides.

Lemma 25

(
[
1
, Lemma
2.1
]
)
.

For an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with maximum degree
\Delta
and for all
v\in V
, the number of vertex-induced connected subgraphs that contain
v
and have at most
k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
vertices is bounded from above by
\mathrm{e}^{k}\Delta^{k-1}/\big(k^{3/2}\sqrt{2\uppi}\big)
.

Commonly, the bound
(\mathrm{e}\Delta)^{k-1}/2
is applied, as it is more convenient to work with.
However, this bound actually only holds for
k\geq 2
.
Further, note that the original paper used a weaker bound, namely
(\mathrm{e}\Delta)^{k}
.
Although this bound holds for all
k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
, it yields a much worse dependency on
\Delta
.
For a fair comparison, we added the result of refined calculations for the approach by Jenssen et al.
[
19
]
to
\Cref
table:bounds.

In order to apply truncation, we further need a notion of size for polymers.
An obvious choice is to set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\gamma}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|
.
The following lemma then bounds the time for enumerating polymers in a clique up to some size
k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
.

Lemma 26

(
[
26
, Lemma
3.7
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph with maximum degree
\Delta
, and let
v\in V
.
There is an algorithm that enumerates all connected, vertex-induced sugraphs of
G
that contain
v
and have at most
k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
vertices in time
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({k\log(\Delta)}}}\right)}
.

We now prove our bound on
\lambda
for an efficient approximation of the hard-core partition function on bipartite
\alpha
-expanders.
Most of the calculations are similar to those of Jenssen et al.
[
19
]
, except that we use our newly obtained conditions.

Proposition 27
.

Let
G(V_{\text{L}}\cup V_{\text{R}},E)
be a bipartite
\alpha
-expander with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{\text{L}}\cup V_{\text{R}}}}\right|=n
and with maximum degree
\Delta\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
.
For
\lambda\geq\max\{(\mathrm{e}\Delta^{2}/0.8)^{1/\alpha},\mathrm{e}^{11/\alpha}\}
and for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, there is an FPRAS for
Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G,\lambda}}\right)
with runtime
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n/\varepsilon}}\right)^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(\Delta)}}}\right)}
.

Proof.

If
\varepsilon\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathrm{e}^{-n}}}}\right)
, we compute
Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G,\lambda}}\right)
by enumerating all independent sets.
Since there are at most
2^{n}
independent sets, which is polynomial in
1/\mathrm{e}^{-n}
, the statement then follows.
It remains to analyze the case
\varepsilon\in\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathrm{e}^{-n}}}}\right)
.
To this end, assume that
\varepsilon\geq 4\mathrm{e}^{-n}
.

By
\Cref
lemma:hc_alpha,
Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G,\lambda}}\right)
can be
\mathrm{e}^{-n}
-approximated using
Z^{(\text{L})}
and
Z^{(\text{R})}
.
We aim for an
\varepsilon/4
-approximation of
Z^{(\text{L})}
and
Z^{(\text{R})}
, each with failure probability at most
1-\sqrt{3}/2
.
Note that

1-\varepsilon\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-n}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}}}\right)\text{~~and~~}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathrm{e}^{-n}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}}}\right)\leq 1+\varepsilon.

Thus, with probability at least
(\sqrt{3}/2)^{2}=3/4
the result is an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G,\lambda}}\right)
.
We can obtain the desired error probability of at most
1-\sqrt{3}/2
for the approximations of
Z^{(\text{L})}
and
Z^{(\text{R})}
by taking the median of
\mathrm{O}\big({\ln(2/(2-\sqrt{3}))}\big)=\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({1}}}\right)
independent approximations with failure probability at most
1/4
.

Let
i\in\{\text{L},\text{R}\}
.
In order to approximate
Z^{(i)}
, we aim to apply
\Cref
thm:sampling_trunc.
To this end, for all
v\in V_{i}
, we define a polymer clique
\Lambda_{v}
containing all polymers
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}
with
v\in\overline{\gamma}
.
This results in a polymer clique cover of size
n
.

We proceed by proving that the polymer model satisfies the clique dynamics condition for
f(\gamma)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|
.
We use
\Cref
remark:spmc to simplify this step.
This also implies that
\cref
thm:samplingTrunc:prt of
\Cref
thm:sampling_trunc is satisfied.
For any
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}
we start by bounding the set of polymers
\gamma^{\prime}\ncomp\gamma
by

\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}\!\colon\gamma^{\prime}\ncomp\gamma}f(\gamma^{\prime})w_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}\leq\sum_{v\in N_{G^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\overline{\gamma}}}\right)}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{v}}f(\gamma^{\prime})w_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}=\sum_{v\in N_{G^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\overline{\gamma}}}\right)}\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{v}\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma^{\prime}}}}\right|=k\end{subarray}}f(\gamma^{\prime})w_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}.

Because
G
is a bipartite
\alpha
-expander, for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}
, we have
w_{\gamma}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}\leq 1/\lambda^{\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|}
.
Further, note that the degree of
G^{2}
is bounded by
\Delta^{2}
.
By
\Cref
lemma:count_vertex and our definition of
f
, we obtain

\sum_{v\in N_{G^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\overline{\gamma}}}\right)}\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{v}\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma^{\prime}}}}\right|=k\end{subarray}}f(\gamma^{\prime})w_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}\leq\Delta^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta^{2}}}\right)^{k-1}}{k^{3/2}\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot k\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha k}}=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}\Delta^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}}}}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}.

For
\lambda\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}\Delta^{2}/0.8}}\right)^{1/\alpha}
, we get

\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}\Delta^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}}}}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0.8}}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{2\pi}=f(\gamma).

It remains to show, for all
v\in V_{i}
, that
\Lambda_{v}
satisfies the clique truncation condition for a
g\colon\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}_{>0}
and a
B\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
To this end, for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}
, let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\gamma}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|
,, let
g(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\gamma}}\right|)=\mathrm{e}^{0.2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\gamma}}\right|}
, and let
B=1
.
Analogously to our verification of the clique dynamics condition, we see, for all
v\in V_{i}
, that

\sum_{\gamma\in\Lambda_{v}}g(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\gamma}}\right|)w_{\gamma}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}\leq\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}\Delta^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}}}}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}\mathrm{e}^{0.2k}=\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1.2}\Delta^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}}}}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}.

For
\lambda\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}\Delta^{2}/0.8}}\right)^{1/\alpha}
, we get

\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1.2}\Delta^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}}}}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{>0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0.8\mathrm{e}^{0.2}}}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}<\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}2.2\leq B.

Last, we bound the runtime of the FPRAS.
By
\Cref
lemma:enum_vertex, we can enumerate each polymer clique up to size
k
in time
t(k)\in\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({k\log(\Delta)}}}\right)}
.
As
g^{-1}\colon x\mapsto 5\ln(x)
, we have
t\circ g^{-1}\colon x\mapsto x^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(\Delta)}}}\right)}
, which is polynomial for
\Delta\in\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({1}}}\right)
.
For the runtime bound, note that we truncate to size
k=g^{-1}(n/\varepsilon)
.
Thus, the time for computing each step of the polymer Markov chain is bounded by
t(k)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n/\varepsilon}}\right)^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(\Delta)}}}\right)}
, which dominates the runtime.
∎

Choice of
f
and Calculation of
\Cref
table:bounds

Note that the choice of the function
f
used in the clique dynamics condition is very sensitive to the bound on the number of subgraphs.
For the bound stated in
\Cref
lemma:count_vertex, it turns out that using
f(\gamma)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\overline{\gamma}}}\right|
yields the best bounds on
\lambda
(see the proof of
\Cref
thm:hc_appx for details).
With this choice of
f
, the condition that we identified in
\Cref
remark:spmc is similar to the mixing condition of
[
5
, Definition 1]
, except that we do not require a strict inequality.
Further, note that such a choice of
f
is not possible for the Kotecký–Preiss condition
[
22
]
.
If purely exponential bounds on the number of subgraphs are used, the best results are usually obtained by setting
f
to take an exponential form.
A detailed understanding of how to choose
f
might be of interest for applications to specific graph classes and other combinatorial structures.

The results for the remaining applications in
\Cref
table:bounds are derived via similar calculations.
For the Potts model on expander graphs and the hard-core model on unbalanced bipartite graphs, we use
\Cref
lemma:count_vertex,lemma:enum_vertex together with the same function
f
for the clique dynamics condition as in the proof of
\Cref
thm:hc_appx.
For the perfect matching polynomial, we use the bounds for the number of polymers and for polymer enumeration that are stated by Casel et al.
[
4
]
, and we choose
f(\gamma)=\mathrm{e}^{a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\gamma}}\right|}
for
a\approx 0.2
.
