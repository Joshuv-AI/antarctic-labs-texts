The Holonomy of Optimal Mass Transport: The Gaussian-Linear Case
Mahmoud Abdelgalil, Tryphon T. Georgiou

Abstract
The theory of Monge-Kantorovich Optimal Mass Transport (OMT) has in recent years spurred a fast developing phase of research in stochastic control, control of ensemble systems, thermodynamics, data science, and several other fields in engineering and science. We herein introduce a new type of transportation problems. The salient feature of these problems is that particles/agents in the ensemble are labeled and their relative position along their journey is of interest. Of particular importance in our program are control laws that steer ensembles along cycles ensuring that individual particles return to their original position. This feature is in contrast with the classical theory of optimal transport where the primary object of study is the path of probability densities, without any concern about particle labels. In the theory that we present, we focus on the case Gaussian distributions and linear dynamics, and explore a hitherto unstudied sub-Riemannian structure of Monge-Kantorovich transport where the relative position of particles along their journey is modeled by the holonomy of the transportation schedule. From this vantage point, we discuss several other problems of independent interest.



Keywords
Monge-Kantorovich transport, sub-Riemannian geometry, mixing

The starting point of our inquiry is the observation that when the distribution of an ensemble of particles traverses a closed orbit in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
before it returns to its starting value, the particles do not necessarily return to their original positions. Indeed, traversing a closed path by an ensemble of particles, as a distribution, generically incurs a rearrangement of particle positions. For instance, when considering a closed path of three McCann geodesics in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that connect pairs of
\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{3}
in succession, unless the Monge maps
\varphi_{i,j}^{\star}
taking
\bm{\mu}_{j}
to
\bm{\mu}_{i}
commute, the composition

\varphi:=\varphi_{3\mapsto 1}^{\star}\circ\varphi_{2\mapsto 3}^{\star}\circ\varphi_{1\mapsto 2}^{\star}

will not be the identity. Thus, generically,
\varphi(x)\neq x
, in spite of the fact that
\varphi\sharp\bm{\mu}_{1}=\bm{\mu}_{1}
,
and similarly for a corresponding ordering in the case of
\bm{\mu}_{2}
and
\bm{\mu}_{3}
.

The point is further exemplified in Fig.
1
by the rudimentary case of two “tracer” particles (equivalently, Dirac distributions) being transferred in succession between three specified configurations where they are in close proximity to one another, in a way that minimizes the overall Wasserstein distance being traversed.
It is observed that after one cycle their positions are exchanged. Clearly, in such cases where distributions consist of finitely many particles (singular distributions), closed orbits induce a permutation of the particles and it is the group of permutations that effects rearrangement of particle positions.
For more general probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or on a manifold, and under sufficient regularity conditions, the relevant groups are measure-preserving diffeomorphisms
[
21
]
.

Following fluid verbiage, we refer to the phenomenon outlined above, i.e., the rearrangement of particle positions without altering their distribution, as
“mixing”
. The adopted nomenclature is not to be confused with the classical notion of mixing in ergodic theory where one studies the limiting behavior of repeated compositions of general measure preserving maps.
As will be shown in the sequel, the notion of mixing adopted herein turns out to have a natural interpretation as the
holonomy

[
24
]
of loops in the Wasserstein manifold
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
The present paper is devoted to the study of said holonomy in the case of Gaussian probability distributions. That is, we study optimal transport in the fibre bundle of linear transportation plans, where a holonomy group acts naturally, and focus on developing a suitable differential geometric framework to study such rearrangement of particle positions.

Specifically, in Section
2
we provide a brief expository on optimal transport of Gaussian distributions highlighting the holonomy that arises when traversing a closed path in the Wasserstein space. In Section
3
we develop the differential geometric setting that is suitable for studying parallel transport and holonomy along Wasserstein curves. In Section
4
we detail a hitherto unstudied sub-Riemannian structure induced by optimal transport wherein the problem of realizing a specified parallel transport map along optimal curves in the Wasserstein space can be naturally formulated and studied. Section
5
overviews examples and applications that highlight the relevance of the theory we presented. We close with an epilogue, in Section
6
, where we envision enabling new aspects of the framework and concepts discussed in the paper.

2
Transport in the space of Gaussian distributions

In the present work we focus on the transport of distributions by way of potential forces. That is, the basic model that we consider at the level of transport of individual particles is a linear system

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\phi_{t}(X_{t})=:u_{t}

(5)

\displaystyle=A_{t}X_{t}+r_{t},

with
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
distributed according to the Gaussian distribution
\mu_{t}
(denoted by
X_{t}\sim\mu_{t}
) having covariance
\Sigma_{t}
and mean
m_{t}
. The dynamics generated by a quadratic time-varying control potential
\phi_{t}(x)=-x^{\intercal}A_{t}x+x^{\intercal}r_{t}
.
It is noted that in (
5

Because the mean is directly regulated by the reference
r_{t}
,
as

\dot{m}_{t}=A_{t}m_{t}+r_{t},

we only focus on the flow of covariances. That is, we assume throughout that
r_{t},m_{t}
vanish and that

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(x)=-x^{\intercal}A_{t}x.

(6)

Thus, Gaussian distributions have zero mean and covariances
\Sigma_{t}=\mathbb{E}\{X_{t}X_{t}^{\intercal}\}
that obey the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{t}

\displaystyle=A_{t}\Sigma_{t}+\Sigma_{t}A_{t}^{\intercal}.

(7)

Moreover,
A_{t}
will be taken to be symmetric, i.e.,
A_{t}=A_{t}^{\intercal}
. Equation (

since it does not encode the actual particle location. The flow of particles is effected by a state transition matrix
\Phi_{t}
, where

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}_{t}=A_{t}\Phi_{t}

(8)

and
\Phi_{0}=I
, though occasionaly we use a double index for

\dot{\Phi}_{t,s}=A_{t}\Phi_{t,s}
for
\Phi_{s,s}=I
. Evidently,
\Sigma_{t}=\Phi_{t,s}\Sigma_{s}\Phi_{t,s}^{\intercal}

Thus, henceforth, we will consider non-degenerate Gaussian distributions
centered
at the origin. We will denote by

the space of all such distributions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., of the form

\displaystyle\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}x\right)}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\text{det}(\Sigma)}},

(9)

\displaystyle\text{Sym}^{+}(n):=\{\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}~|~\Sigma=\Sigma^{\intercal},\,\Sigma\succ 0\}.

2.2
The Holonomy of Gaussian triangles

We recapitulate the starting point of our inquiry, from the introduction, by exploring triangles in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with edges constructed via McCann interpolation between vertices.

Identifying distributions in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with their corresponding covariances, we begin by taking vertices
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{3}\subset\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
and construct edges between pairs of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
’s using McCann geodesics. For brevity, we refer to a closed cycle
\Sigma_{t}
traversing the edges over
t\in[0,1]
as a
Gaussian triangle
.
The time-indexing in traversing edges is immaterial, so we choose equal “time steps” for traversing each edge and set

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}(I+3t(\Phi_{1\mapsto 2}^{\star}-I)){\sharp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1},&t\in\left[0,\frac{1}{3}\right),\\
(I+3(t-\frac{1}{3})(\Phi_{2\mapsto 3}^{\star}-I)){\sharp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2},&t\in\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}\right),\\
(I+3(t-\frac{2}{3})(\Phi_{3\mapsto 1}^{\star}-I)){\sharp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{3},&t\in\left[\frac{2}{3},1\right],\\
\end{cases}

(14)

with
\Phi_{1\mapsto 2}^{\star},\Phi_{2\mapsto 3}^{\star},\Phi_{3\mapsto 1}^{\star}
the Monge maps between vertices.

After traversing the cycle,
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{0}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}
. The composition

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=\Phi_{3\mapsto 1}^{\star}\Phi_{2\mapsto 3}^{\star}\Phi_{1\mapsto 2}^{\star},

is a state transition matrix that satifies

\displaystyle\Theta\sharp\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1},

(15)

as can be readily verified using (
11a
). But
\Theta\neq I
, in general. Thus,
X_{1}\neq\Theta X_{0}
for a tracer particle starting at
X_{0}=x
.
The two-dimensional sketch of Figure
2
highlights the point.

Obviously, mixing is not exclusive to triangles. A succession of state transition maps that allows traversing a polygon, or any closed curve

\Sigma_{t}\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)

for that matter, i.e.,

\Sigma_{1}=\Phi_{01}\Sigma_{0}\Phi_{01}^{\intercal}=\Sigma_{0},

leaves
\Sigma_{0}
invariant under congruence. Such state transition matrices are special and will typically be denoted using the symbol
\Theta
. Hence, here, we write
\Theta=\Phi_{01}
and observe that

\Sigma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Theta\Sigma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}

is an orthogonal matrix. In fact,
\Theta
has positive determinant, and hence equal to
1
, since it arises by integrating (
8
) from the identity.
It corresponds to a

measure-preserving
map
\varphi(x)=\Theta x
that preserves the Gaussian distribution with covariance
\Sigma_{0}
under the pushforward operation.
The set of all such matrices constitutes a (closed and connected) Lie subgroup of the general linear group
\text{GL}(n)
, this is the group

\displaystyle\text{SO}(n,\Sigma):=\{\Theta\in\text{GL}(n)~|~\Theta{\sharp}\Sigma=\Sigma,\;\det(\Theta)=1\}.

(16)

A natural question that arises in the present context is whether it is possible to realize any
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma)
in this way, solving (
8
) via a choice of a control protocol

, for

, possibly piecewise constant.
This question is equivalent to the classical control-theoretic question of controllability for a certain driftless control-affine system. Finding an “optimal” closed curve that achieves a given
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma)
reduces to finding a
sub-Riemannian geodesic

[
1
]
or, equivalently, the solution to an
isoholonomic problem
in a certain principal bundle
[
31
,
32
]
. Therefore, the natural framework wherein one can formulate and study mixing in OMT is the
sub-Riemannian geometry

[
1
,
32
]
of
principal bundles

[
39
,
24
]
. The next section gives an overview of the requisite mathematical machinery, specialized to the setting of covariance matrices and linear transformations. To enhance readability, we opt to present our results in coordinates rather than in a coordinate-free fashion as is customary in differential geometry. Our choice is justified by the fact that the manifolds we work with have global charts.

3
Differential Geometry of Linear Transport

In
[
35
]
, Otto showed that the Wasserstein metric
W_{2}
is (formally) induced by a Riemannian metric, subsequently referred to as the Wasserstein-Otto metric
[
21
]
or the Wasserstein metric
[
41
]
, which can be obtained via a
Riemannian submersion
of a flat Riemannian metric on
\text{Diff}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the diffeomorphism group of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. His insight led to a vibrant body of literature on the differential geometry of OMT, Riemannian geometry of Wasserstein spaces, and geometric hydrodynamics. Otto’s geometric framework is a natural avenue for our purposes. For that reason, we now give an overview of the pertinent components of Otto’s framework, specialized to Gaussian distributions and linear diffeomorphisms.

As noted earlier, we may identify
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
, since

\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is globally parameterized by
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
from (
9
).
But
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
is an open subset of the vector space of symmetric matrices

\displaystyle\text{Sym}(n):=\{S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}~|~S=S^{\intercal}\}.

(17)

Therefore,
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
is a smooth manifold and, at any
\Sigma\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
, its tangent space

T_{\Sigma}\text{Sym}^{+}(n)

is canonically isomorphic to
\text{Sym}(n)

[
26
]
.

The group of linear diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the finite dimensional subgroup of
\text{Diff}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, namely,

\displaystyle\text{LDiff}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{\varphi\in\text{Diff}(\mathbb{R}^{n})~|~\varphi(x)=\Phi x,\,\Phi\in\text{GL}(n)\}.

It has two connected components, with the identity component being

\displaystyle\text{LDiff}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{\varphi\in\text{Diff}(\mathbb{R}^{n})~|~\varphi(x)=\Phi x,\,\Phi\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)\},

where as usual,

\text{GL}^{+}(n):=\{\Phi\in\text{GL}(n)~|~\det(\Phi)>0\},

is the identity component of
\text{GL}(n)
(i.e., matrices with positive determinants).
An element
\varphi\in\text{LDiff}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
acts on
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
from the left via the pushforward on
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, namely,

\displaystyle L_{\varphi}:\mu\mapsto\varphi{\sharp}\mu,

(18)

It is clear that
\text{LDiff}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is globally parameterized by
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
, which is a Lie group.

Moreover, direct computation of the pushforward
\varphi{\sharp}\mu
yields

\displaystyle\varphi{\sharp}\mu(x)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}(\Phi\sharp\Sigma)^{-1}x\right)}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\text{det}(\Phi\sharp\Sigma)}}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(19a)

when
\varphi\in\text{LDiff}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mu\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are

\displaystyle\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\Phi x,

\displaystyle\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}x\right)}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\text{det}(\Sigma)}}.

(19b)

In conclusion, from (
19
), we observe that the left action of
\varphi\in\text{LDiff}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
on
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
via the pushforward is equivalent to the left action of
\Phi\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)
on
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
via congruence:

\displaystyle L_{\Phi}:\Sigma\mapsto\Phi\sharp\Sigma.

(20)

With that equivalence in mind, we henceforth work with the parameterizations
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
and
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
instead of
\text{LDiff}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, respectively.

3.1
The OMT principal bundle

We begin by fixing an arbitrary reference covariance
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
. We then define the map

\displaystyle\pi\;:\;\text{GL}^{+}(n)\to\text{Sym}^{+}(n)\;:\;\Phi\mapsto L_{\Phi}(\Sigma_{\text{ref}}).

One may take
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
to be the identity, and then
\pi\;:\;\Phi\mapsto\Phi\Phi^{\intercal}
. The map
\pi
will serve to define the principle bundle structure with base space the set of covariances,
\Sigma=\Phi\Phi^{\intercal}
, or
\Sigma=\Phi\Sigma_{\text{ref}}\Phi^{\intercal}
with respect to any reference
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
.

The map
\pi
is in fact a restriction of the map introduced by Otto in his seminal paper
[
35
]
to our finite dimensional setting. In particular,
\pi
is a smooth submersion, and so
\pi:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
is a smooth fiber bundle. For any
\Sigma\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
, the fiber at
\Sigma
is the submanifold:

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\Sigma)=\{\Phi\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)~|~\pi(\Phi)=L_{\Phi}(\Sigma_{\text{ref}})=\Sigma\}.

(22)

In particular, the fiber
\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
at the reference is the
isotropy group
of
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
and, evidently, coincides with

\displaystyle\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})=\{\Theta\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)~|~L_{\Theta}(\Sigma_{\text{ref}})=\Sigma_{\text{ref}}\},

(23)

repeated here for clarity,
under the left action of
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
.
As a (closed) subgroup of
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
,
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
acts naturally from the right on
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
via matrix multiplication:

\displaystyle R_{\Theta}\;:\;\text{GL}^{+}(n)\to\text{GL}^{+}(n)\;:\;\Phi\mapsto\Phi\Theta.

(24)

This action is
free
since
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
is a subgroup of
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
. In addition, if
\Sigma\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
,
\Phi\in\pi^{-1}(\Sigma)
, and
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
, then

\displaystyle\pi\circ R_{\Theta}(\Phi)=\Phi\Theta\Sigma_{\text{ref}}\Theta^{\intercal}\Phi^{\intercal}=\Phi\Sigma_{\text{ref}}\Phi^{\intercal}=\Sigma,

(25)

which implies that
R_{\Theta}(\Phi)\in\pi^{-1}(\Sigma)
. That is, the right action of
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
on
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
preserves the fibers of the bundle
\pi:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
.

Finally, it can be shown that the action of
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
on each fiber is
transitive

[
30
]
, i.e. if
\Phi
and
\tilde{\Phi}
belong to the same fiber
\pi^{-1}(\Sigma)
, then there exists
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
such that
\tilde{\Phi}=\Phi\Theta
. Since the action of
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
on the fibers is both free and transitive, the following proposition is immediate
[
32
,
24
]
.

Proposition 1
.

The fiber bundle
\pi:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
is a principal
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
-bundle over
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
.

Henceforth, with a slight abuse of notation, we use
\pi
to refer both the map
\pi
and to the fiber bundle
\pi:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
. The meaning will be clear from the context.

Remark 2
.

The statement of the proposition, for the special case where
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}=I
, amounts to the statement that matrices
\Phi
with positive determinant constitute a principal bundle of factors over the base space of covariances
\Sigma=\Phi\Phi^{\intercal}
, and that the holonomy group of the fibers is a subgroup of the special orthogonal group of matrices
\Theta
, since
\Phi\to\Phi\Theta
leaves
\pi(\Phi\Theta)
the same and equal to
\Sigma
.
\Box

3.2
The OMT Ehresmann connection

We now consider the differential
\text{d}\pi
of
\pi
that maps the tangent space of

\text{GL}^{+}(n)
to that of
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
. Our interest is in identifying permissible directions for elements
\Phi
on the fibers
\pi^{-1}(\Sigma)
above covariances
\Sigma
on the base space. Thus, it is essential to relate elements on nearby fibers and how one is allowed to transition between those, and this is done with the concept of an Ehresmann connection.

The kernel of
\text{d}\pi
defines the so-called
vertical sub-bundle

\displaystyle\text{Ver}_{\Phi}:=\text{Ker}(\text{d}\pi_{\Phi})=\{\dot{\Phi}\in T_{\Phi}\text{GL}^{+}(n)~|~\text{d}\pi_{\Phi}(\dot{\Phi})=0\}.

A
principal Ehresmann connection

[
24
,
39
]
, or
principal connection
for short, allows transporting between different fibers and is based on a choice of a
horizontal sub-bundle
.
Specifically, this is a sub-bundle
Hor
that associates to every
\Phi\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)
a subspace
\text{Hor}_{\Phi}\subset T_{\Phi}\text{GL}^{+}(n)
so that, for all
\Phi\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)
and all
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
, the following hold:

1.

T_{\Phi}\text{GL}^{+}(n)=\text{Ver}_{\Phi}\oplus\text{Hor}_{\Phi}
,

2.

\text{Hor}_{\Phi\Theta}=(R_{\Theta})_{*}\text{Hor}_{\Phi}
.

Intuitively, the last condition can be interpreted as a property of the horizontal spaces at different points on the fiber being parallel, in a suitable sense.

In words, a principal Ehresmann connection is a
canonical
choice of a sub-bundle complementary to the vertical bundle
\text{Ver}:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\ni\Phi\mapsto\text{Ver}_{\Phi}\subset T_{\Phi}\text{GL}^{+}(n)
and invariant under the action of the isotropy group
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
. There is considerable freedom in the construction of the sub-bundle defining the Ehresmann connection, i.e., the
horizontal sub-bundle
. However, here, we are primarily concerned with the connection defined by

\displaystyle\text{Hor}_{\Phi}=\{\dot{\Phi}\in T_{\Phi}\text{GL}^{+}(n)~|~\dot{\Phi}\Phi^{-1}\in\text{Sym}(n)\}.

(26)

Our interest in (
26
) stems from the fact it is intimately connected with the geometry of optimal mass transport. Indeed, the condition that
\dot{\Phi}\Phi^{-1}\in\text{Sym}(n)
is equivalent to the requirement that
A_{t}
in (
5
) is symmetric. Consequently, the sub-bundle (
26
) encodes the infinitesmal constraint that the transition from
\Phi_{t}
to
\Phi_{t+\text{d}t}
is a Monge map. The following proposition establishes that (
26
) is, indeed, an Ehresmann connection.

Proposition 2
.

The sub-bundle (
26
) defines an Ehresmann connection on the principal bundle
\pi
.

Henceforth, we refer to the connection defined by (
26
) as the OMT connection and we denote it by
\Gamma
.

3.3
Parallel transport along Wasserstein curves

We are now in a position to consider admissible curves on the space of factors via the connection that allows to transport between fibers. This entails the notion of parallel transport where curves on the base space of covariance matrices are lifted to the space of factors. We henceforth use the compact notation
\Sigma_{.},\Phi_{.}
, subscribing a dot, to indicate functions of time taking values
\Sigma_{t},\Phi_{t}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Horizontal lifts

A
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve
\Phi_{\cdot}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{GL}^{+}(n)
is said to be a
horizontal curve
with respect to
\Gamma
if
\dot{\Phi}_{t}\in\text{Hor}_{\Phi_{t}}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Accordingly, given a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve

\Sigma_{.}\;:\;[0,1]\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)\;:\;t\mapsto\Sigma_{t},

a
horizontal lift
of
\Sigma_{.}
with respect to
\Gamma
starting at the initial point
\Phi_{\text{in}}\in\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{0})
is a horizontal curve

\Phi_{.}\;:\;[0,1]\rightarrow\text{GL}^{+}(n)\;:\;t\mapsto\Phi_{t}

satisfying

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}=\Phi_{\text{in}},\;\;\Sigma_{t}=\pi(\Phi_{t}),

(27)

for
t\in[0,1]
. Existence and uniqueness of horizontal lifts is a standard result in the theory of principal bundles equipped with Ehresmann connections
[
24
]
. For the principal bundle
\pi
, we have the following explicit construction. The proof is straightforward and hence omitted.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\Sigma_{.}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve,
A_{t}
be the unique solution to the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle A_{t}\Sigma_{t}+\Sigma_{t}A_{t}=\dot{\Sigma}_{t},

(28)

for
t\in[0,1]
,
and let
\Phi:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the solution to

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}_{t}=A_{t}\Phi_{t},\mbox{ with }\Phi_{0}=I.

(29)

Then, the (unique) horizontal lift of the curve
\Sigma_{.}
with respect to
\Gamma
starting at the initial point
\Phi_{\text{in}}\in\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{0})
exists and is of the form

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{.}\;:\;t

\displaystyle\mapsto\hat{\Phi}_{t}=\Phi_{t}\Phi_{\text{in}},\mbox{ for }t\in[0,1].

(30)

Remark 3
.

The essence of the above proposition is to relate how the particles are transported by potential forces which are designed to steer their Gaussian distribution along the curve specified by
\Sigma_{t}
in the base space. In particular, the proposition implies that the connection
\Gamma
is
complete
, i.e. that every
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve in
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
has a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
horizontal lift with respect to
\Gamma
in
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
. It is also of interest that (
29
) can be expressed directly in terms of
\Sigma_{t},\dot{\Sigma}_{t}
, albeit not in closed form, since

\displaystyle A_{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau\Sigma_{t}}\dot{\Sigma}_{t}e^{-\tau\Sigma_{t}}d\tau=:\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma_{t}}(\dot{\Sigma}_{t})

(31)

using standard Lyapunov theory.
\Box

Parallel transport

The horizontal lift of a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve
\Sigma_{.}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
takes an initial point
\Phi_{\text{in}}
on the fiber
\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{0})
to the final point
\Phi_{1}\Phi_{\text{in}}
on
\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{1})
. By fixing the curve
\Sigma_{.}
and varying the initial point
\Phi_{\text{in}}\in\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{0})
for the corresponding horizontal lift, we obtain a morphism

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{0})\rightarrow\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{1}).

between fibers. This morphism, denoted by
\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]
, is the
parallel transport map
along the curve
\Sigma_{.}
and, by construction, commutes with the right action of
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})

[
24
,
32
]
. That is, if
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
, then

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}](\Phi_{\text{in}}\Theta)=\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}](\Phi_{\text{in}})\Theta.

(32)

From Proposition
3
, it is clear that the parallel transport for the connection
\Gamma
is

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}](\Phi_{\text{in}})=\Phi_{1}\Phi_{\text{in}}.

(33)

With a slight notational abuse, we identify
\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]
with the
\Phi_{1}
, i.e. the endpoint of the solution to (
29
),
since
\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]
amounts precisely to the corresponding linear transformation of the factors.

The holonomy group

When
\Sigma_{.}
is a
loop based at
\Sigma_{0}
, i.e. a closed curve in that
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{0}
, then
\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]
is a linear isomorphism of
\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{0})
onto itself. Since the fibers of the bundle
\pi
are parameterized by
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
, it follows that, by fixing an arbitrary element
\Phi_{\text{in}}\in\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{0})
, every loop
\Sigma_{.}
based at
\Sigma_{0}
is associated with a unique
\hat{\Theta}\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
such that

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]\Phi_{\text{in}}=\Phi_{\text{in}}\,\hat{\Theta}.

(34)

If
\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{in}}
is any other element of the fiber
\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{0})
, then
\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{in}}=\Phi_{\text{in}}\Theta
for some unique
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
, and so

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{in}}=\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]\Phi_{\text{in}}\Theta=\tilde{\Phi}_{\text{in}}\Theta^{-1}\,\hat{\Theta}\Theta.

(35)

We observe that the matrix
\Theta^{-1}\,\hat{\Theta}\Theta
also belongs to
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
and, by definition, is conjugate to
\hat{\Theta}
. Thus,
the set of all parallel transport maps along loops based at
\Sigma_{0}
forms a group that can be realized as a subgroup of
\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
. This is the
holonomy group
of the connection
\Gamma
with reference point
\Sigma_{0}
and will be denoted by
\text{Hol}(\Sigma_{0})
.

The question raised at the end of the previous section, as to whether any
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
can be realized as the holonomy of some loop, is equivalent to the question of whether
\text{Hol}(\Sigma_{0})=\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
. The classical approach to answering this question, in the context of principal bundles, is the Ambrose-Singer theorem which gives a complete characterization of the Lie algebra of
\text{Hol}(\Sigma_{0})
in terms of the
curvature form
of the principal connection
[
24
]
. However, the technical machinery required to state and invoke the theorem lies beyond the scope of this manuscript. Instead, we opt to follow a control-theoretic approach to characterizing
\text{Hol}(\Sigma_{0})
. This is done in the next section.

4
Sub-Riemannian Geometry of Linear Transport

We recall our goals from the introduction section. Our first goal is to provide an answer to whether
\text{Hol}(\Sigma_{0})=\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
, which is equivalent to the question of whether any
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
can be realized as the parallel transport map along a loop in
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
. Our second goal is
optimal synthesis
, i.e. finding the shortest curve in
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
with a specified parallel transport map. Both questions are answered in this section following a purely control-theoretic approach.

4.1
The holonomy of linear OMT

To achieve our first goal, we rephrase our question, and ask instead as to whether any two elements
\Phi_{\text{in}}
and
\Phi_{\text{fn}}
in
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
can be joined by a horizontal curve
\Phi_{.}
, i.e. the horizontal lift of a curve
\Sigma_{.}
in the base space. Clearly, a positive answer implies that
\text{Hol}(\Sigma)=\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
, for every
\Sigma\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
. The following proposition provides such an answer.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\Phi_{\text{in}},\Phi_{\text{fn}}\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)
. Then, there exists a horizontal curve
\Phi_{\cdot}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{GL}^{+}(n)
such that
\Phi_{0}=\Phi_{\text{in}}
and
\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{\text{fn}}
.

Proof.

The proposition follows directly from the Chow-Rashevskii theorem, provided that the horizontal sub-bundle is
bracket-generating
, i.e., satisfies the Hörmander condition
[
1
]
. The latter assertion follows from the observation that if
A,B\in\text{Sym}(n)
, then the commutator of the two vector fields
\dot{\Phi}^{A}_{t}:=A\Phi_{t}
and
\dot{\Phi}^{B}_{t}=B\Phi_{t}
is

\displaystyle[\dot{\Phi}^{A}_{t},\dot{\Phi}^{B}_{t}]=\bm{[}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{[}B,A\bm{]}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{]}\Phi_{t}=(BA-AB)\Phi_{t},

(36)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the Lie-bracket between vector fields
and
\bm{[}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{[}\cdot,\cdot\bm{]}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{]}
denotes the matrix commutator; note the apparent reversal in order, as compared to the Lie bracket.

Any matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
can be written as the sum of its symmetric part, say,
S
and its skew symmetric matrix
A
. In turn, any skew symmetric part can be written as the commutator of two symmetric matrices, i.e.,

\displaystyle A=\bm{[}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{[}B,C\bm{]}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{]},

(37)

for some
B,C\in\text{Sym}(n)
.

Before we continue with the proof of the proposition, we give a short proof of the last statement. The validity of the statement can easily be seen in the case of

2\times 2
skew-symmetric matrix, since any such matrix is of the form

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda\\
-\lambda&0\end{pmatrix},

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, while observing that
A_{\lambda}=\bm{[}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{[}\lambda E_{1},E_{2}\bm{]}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{]}
for

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

In general, any skew-symmetric matrix
A
can be brought by an orthogonal transformation
\Theta
into a block diagonal form

\Theta A\Theta^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{\lambda_{1}}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&A_{\lambda_{2}}&&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&A_{\lambda_{r}}\end{bmatrix},

for some
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
when
n
is even, or with an additional
0
on the diagonal when
n
is odd. (This last statement can be easily shown using the fact that any skew-symmetric matrix is normal.) Thus, in general,
A
can be written as the commutator of the symmetric matrices

\displaystyle B=\Theta^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}E_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}E_{1}&&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\lambda_{r}E_{1}\end{bmatrix}\Theta,

and

\displaystyle C=\Theta^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}E_{2}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&E_{2}&&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&E_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\Theta,

when
n
is even, and similarly constructed matrices with an additional
0
on the diagonal if
n
is odd.

Returning to the proof,
we conclude that

\displaystyle\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{A,\bm{[}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{[}B,C\bm{]}\hskip-2.0pt\bm{]}\}_{A,B,C\in\text{Sym}(n)}=\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

(38)

from whence it follows that, for any
\Phi\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)
,

\displaystyle\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\dot{\Phi}^{A},[\dot{\Phi}^{B},\dot{\Phi}^{C}]\}_{A,B,C\in\text{Sym}(n)}=T_{\Phi}\text{GL}^{+}(n),

(39)

and, thus, the horizontal sub-bundle is bracket-generating.
∎

As an immediate consequence of Proposition
4
, we have the following Corollary.

Corollary 1
.

\text{Hol}(\Sigma)=\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
for all
\Sigma\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
.

Proof.

Since Proposition
4
establishes that any two points in
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
can be connected with a horizontal curve, the same applies to any two points that belong to the same fiber. These are then necessarily related by an element of the structure group, i.e.
\mathrm{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\mathrm{ref}})
. Thus, every
\Theta\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\mathrm{ref}})
can be realized as the parallel transport map along a loop in
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
. The conclusion of the corollary follows.
∎

Proposition
4
goes beyond the statement of the Corollary since it establishes
one of the essential ingredients for defining a
sub-Riemannian
metric on the principal bundle
\pi:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
; namely that the horizontal sub-bundle (
26
) is
bracket-generating
. The remaining ingredient is a choice of a Riemannian metric on the base space
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
of the bundle. Naturally, the most pertinent choice in the context of OMT is the Wasserstein-Otto metric
[
35
,
21
]
.

4.2
The sub-Riemannian structure of linear OMT

Otto’s metric (
4
), also known as the Wasserstein-Otto metric
[
21
]
, specialized to Gaussian distributions, takes the form

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\mu}_{t},\dot{\mu}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle={\operatorname{trace}}\left(A_{t}\Sigma_{t}A_{t}\right),

where
A_{t}
can be expressed directly as a function of
\Sigma_{t},\dot{\Sigma}_{t}
, as we already noted in (
31
). Thereby, expressing the metric directly on the base space of the bundle,
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
, the Wasserstein-Otto metric
[
35
,
21
]
takes the form

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\Sigma}(\dot{\Sigma}_{1},\dot{\Sigma}_{2})={\operatorname{trace}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}(\dot{\Sigma}_{1})\Sigma\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}(\dot{\Sigma}_{2})).

(40)

Here, again,
\Sigma\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
,
\dot{\Sigma}_{1},\dot{\Sigma}_{2}\in T_{\Sigma}\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}:\text{Sym}(n)\rightarrow\text{Sym}(n)
is the operator defined in (
31
).

Since, by construction, the restriction of
\text{d}\pi
to the horizontal sub-bundle is an isomorphism, the Wasserstein-Otto metric (
40
) defines a family of inner products on the horizontal sub-bundle:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\Phi}(\dot{\Phi}_{1},\dot{\Phi}_{2}):=\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{\pi(\Phi)}(\text{d}\pi_{\Phi}\dot{\Phi}_{1},\text{d}\pi_{\Phi}\dot{\Phi}_{2}),

(41)

where
\Phi\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)
,
\dot{\Phi}_{1},\dot{\Phi}_{2}\in\text{Hor}_{\Phi}
. Explicit computation shows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\Phi}(\dot{\Phi}_{1},\dot{\Phi}_{2})={\operatorname{trace}}(\dot{\Phi}_{1}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}\dot{\Phi}_{2}^{\intercal}).

(42)

Then, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 5
.

The family of inner products (
42
), in conjunction with the horizontal bundle
(
26
)
, define a sub-Riemannian metric on
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
.

Henceforth, we refer to the sub-Riemannian metric defined by Proposition
5
as the OMT sub-Riemannian metric and denote it by
\mathcal{G}
.

Geodesics and the sub-Riemannian distance

It is a standard result that a sub-Riemannian metric induces a distance function
[
1
, Definition 3.30]
. So let
d_{SR}:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\times\text{GL}^{+}(n)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be this sub-Riemannian distance induced by
\mathcal{G}
. A
sub-Riemannian geodesic
is a horizontal curve

\Phi_{.}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{GL}^{+}(n)

such that

\displaystyle d_{SR}(\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1})^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}{\mathcal{G}_{\Phi_{t}}(\dot{\Phi}_{t},\dot{\Phi}_{t})}\,\text{d}t.

(43)

The regularity of geodesics in sub-Riemannian geometry can be quite subtle. Geodesics are typically characterized as
normal
and
abnormal

[
1
, Definition 3.60]
. Normal geodesics are differentiable and satisfy the first-order (Pontryagin) necessary conditions for optimality.
On the hand, abnormal geodesics may or may not satisfy the first-order necessary conditions of optimality. Accordingly, there are abnormal geodesics that are also normal, and others that are not; the latter are referred to as
strictly abnormal
.
As shown by Montgomery
[
32
, Chapter 3, Theorem 3.4]
, the presence of strictly abnormal geodesics can be a generic phenomenon.
If present, strictly abnormal geodesics significantly complicate the local geometry of a sub-Riemannian manifold
[
1
, Chapter 11]
.
Fortunately, the OMT sub-Riemannian structure is special and does not admit strictly abnormal geodesics. The following proposition establishes this fact. The proof is straightforward and is sketched after the proposition.

Proposition 6
.

Every nontrivial geodesic with respect to the OMT sub-Riemannian structure is normal.

Proof.

From (
39
), we see that only first-order Lie brackets are required to generate the tangent bundle from the horizontal sub-bundle of the OMT connection. This property appears under many names in the literature. In
[
1
]
, the horizontal sub-bundle is said to be a “step-2” bundle when this property holds. Elsewhere, the property has been referred to as the
strong bracket generating condition

[
40
]
. Strichartz established in
[
40
, Theorem 6.1, Corollary 6.2]
that, under the strong bracket generating condition, all sub-Riemannian geodesics are normal, see also
[
1
, Corollary 12.14]
. Thus, the same is true for the OMT sub-Riemannian structure.
∎

The next proposition formulates the first-order necessary conditions for a horizontal curve to be a normal geodesic with respect to the OMT sub-Riemannian structure.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\Phi_{.}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{GL}^{+}(n)
be a normal sub-Riemannian geodesic connecting the end points
\Phi_{0}
and
\Phi_{1}
. Then,
\Phi_{.}
is smooth and there exists a unique smooth curve
\Lambda_{.}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{GL}^{+}(n)
such that

\displaystyle\dot{{\Phi}}_{t}

\displaystyle=A_{t}{\Phi}_{t},

\displaystyle\dot{{\Lambda}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-A_{t}({\Lambda}_{t}+2A_{t}{\Phi}_{t}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}),

(44)

with
A_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\pi(\Phi_{t})}({\Phi}_{t}{\Lambda}_{t}^{\intercal}+{\Lambda}_{t}{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal})
.

Proof.

The Hamiltonian associated with the geodesics of the OMT sub-Riemannian structure is given in coordinates by

\displaystyle H(\Phi,\Lambda,A)

\displaystyle={\operatorname{trace}}(A\Phi\Sigma_{\text{ref}}\Phi^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}+\Lambda^{\intercal}A\Phi)

\displaystyle={\operatorname{trace}}(A\Phi\Sigma_{\text{ref}}\Phi^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}+\Phi\Lambda^{\intercal}A),

wherein
A
is the control input. Since
A\in\text{Sym}(n)
,

\displaystyle H({\Phi},{\Lambda},A)={\operatorname{trace}}(A{\Phi}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}{\Phi}^{\intercal}A+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}({\Phi}{\Lambda}^{\intercal}+{\Lambda}{\Phi}^{\intercal})A).

The first variation of
H
with respect to its arguments is

\displaystyle\delta H

\displaystyle={\operatorname{trace}}(\delta{\Lambda}^{\intercal}A{\Phi})+{\operatorname{trace}}(\delta{\Phi}^{\intercal}A({\Lambda}+2A{\Phi}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}))

\displaystyle+{\operatorname{trace}}(\delta A({\Phi}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}{\Phi}^{\intercal}A+A{\Phi}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}{\Phi}^{\intercal}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}({\Phi}{\Lambda}^{\intercal}+{\Lambda}{\Phi}^{\intercal}))).

From the calculus of variations, the necessary conditions of optimality are

\displaystyle\dot{{\Phi}}_{t}

\displaystyle=A_{t}{\Phi}_{t},

\displaystyle\dot{{\Lambda}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-A_{t}({\Lambda}_{t}+2A_{t}{\Phi}_{t}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}),

(45)

whereas Pontryagin’s minimum principle necessitates that, for all
t\in[0,1]
,
A_{t}
is the unique solution to the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle{\Phi}_{t}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}A_{t}+A_{t}{\Phi}_{t}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}({\Phi}_{t}{\Lambda}_{t}^{\intercal}+{\Lambda}_{t}{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}).

(46)

The proof is concluded by noting that
{\Phi}_{t}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}=\pi(\Phi_{t})
, and that the solution to (
46
) is expressed via the linear operator defined in (
31
).
∎

Invariance with respect to
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}

Another important feature of the OMT sub-Riemmanian structure is that it is largely independent of the choice of
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
. The following proposition formalizes this fact.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\pi:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
and
\tilde{\pi}:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
be the two principal bundles defined by
\pi(\Phi)=L_{\Phi}(\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
and
\tilde{\pi}(\Phi)=L_{\Phi}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\text{ref}})
for any
\Sigma_{\text{ref}},\tilde{\Sigma}_{\text{ref}}\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
. Let
\mathcal{G}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
be the family of inner products defining the sub-Riemannian structure on
\pi
and
\tilde{\pi}
, respectively. Then, the bundles
\pi
and
\tilde{\pi}
are isometrically isomorphic.

Proof.

A principal bundle isomorphism
[
24
, p. 53]
between
\pi
and
\tilde{\pi}
is a diffeomorphism
\Psi:\text{GL}^{+}(n)\rightarrow\text{GL}^{+}(n)
and a group isomorphism
\psi:\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})\rightarrow\text{SO}(n,\tilde{\Sigma}_{\text{ref}})
such that

\displaystyle\Psi(\Phi\tilde{\Theta})=\Psi(\Phi)\psi(\Theta).

(47)

Let
\hat{\Phi}\in\text{GL}^{+}(n)
be any element in
\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
, i.e.
\hat{\Phi}\sharp\tilde{\Sigma}_{\text{ref}}=\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
, and define the maps

\displaystyle\Psi(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\Phi\hat{\Phi},

\displaystyle\psi(\Theta)

\displaystyle=\hat{\Phi}^{-1}\Theta\hat{\Phi}.

(48)

Then, if
\Theta\in\text{SO}(n,\Sigma_{\text{ref}})
, we have that

\displaystyle\Psi(\Phi\Theta)=\Phi\Theta\hat{\Phi}=\Phi\hat{\Phi}\hat{\Phi}^{-1}\Theta\hat{\Phi}=\Psi(\Phi)\psi(\Theta).

(49)

It is clear that
\Psi
is a diffeomorphism and that
\psi
is a group isomorphism, which implies that the pair
\Psi
and
\psi
define a principal bundle isomorphism. It remains to show that
\Psi
is also an isometry. Explicit computation gives that

\displaystyle\text{d}\Psi_{\Phi}\dot{\Phi}=\dot{\Phi}\hat{\Phi}.

(50)

Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\Psi(\Phi)}(\text{d}\Psi_{\Phi}\dot{\Phi}_{1},\text{d}\Psi_{\Phi}\dot{\Phi}_{2})=\text{trace}(\dot{\Phi}_{1}\hat{\Phi}\tilde{\Sigma}_{\text{ref}}\hat{\Phi}^{\intercal}\dot{\Phi}_{2}^{\intercal}).

(51)

However, by definition,
\hat{\Phi}\tilde{\Sigma}_{\text{ref}}\hat{\Phi}^{\intercal}=\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
, and so

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\Phi}(\dot{\Phi}_{1},\dot{\Phi}_{1})=\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\Psi(\Phi)}(\text{d}\Psi_{\Phi}\dot{\Phi}_{1},\text{d}\Psi_{\Phi}\dot{\Phi}_{2}).

(52)

It follows that
\Psi
is also an isometry
[
40
, Theorem 8.2]
.
∎

The essence of Proposition
8
is that the choice of the reference covariance
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}\in\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
in defining the principal bundle
\pi
and the OMT sub-Riemannian metric is completely arbitrary. It also establishes that the holonomy groups associated to different choices of
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
are all conjugate to one another, i.e. that they are different representations of the same algebraic object. So, the choice
\Sigma_{\rm ref}=I
with
\text{SO}(\Sigma_{\rm ref},n)
being simply
\text{SO}(n)
may be a convenient option.

The Riemannian extension

Inspection of (
42
) reveals that this is precisely the restriction to the horizontal sub-bundle of a
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
-weighted Frobenius inner product on each tangent space
T_{\Phi}\text{GL}^{+}(n)
. Extending (
42
) to the entire tangent bundle defines a (flat) Riemannian metric, henceforth referred to as the
L_{2}
-metric, on
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
. In fact, the Wasserstein-Otto metric (
40
) is typically constructed by showing that the map
\pi
is a
Riemannian submersion
and that the
L_{2}
-metric
descends
to the Wasserstein-Otto metric (
40
) under
\pi

[
35
,
30
]
. This observation is crucial in establishing many important regularity properties of the sub-Riemannian distance
d_{SR}
. For instance, the distance
d_{SR}
induces the same topology as its Riemannian extension. Another consequence is that, under the strong bracket generating condition, the distance
d_{SR}
is bounded above on compact sets by a multiple of the square root of its Riemannian extension
[
40
, Theorem 11.1]
. This important fact is recapitulated in the following proposition.

Proposition 9
.

Let
d_{R}
be the distance associated with the
L_{2}
-metric and let
K
be a compact set in the topology induced by
d_{SR}
. Then, there exists a positive constant
c
such that:

d_{R}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\leq d_{SR}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\leq cd_{R}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}},

for all
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in K
.

The significance of Proposition
9
stems from the
flatness
of the
L_{2}
-metric
[
35
,
30
,
21
]
. That is, geodesics with respect to the
L_{2}
-metric satisfy the trivial geodesic equation

\displaystyle\ddot{\Phi}_{t}

\displaystyle=0,

(53)

which is explicitly integrable; the unique geodesic
\Phi_{.}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{GL}^{+}(n)
between any
\Phi_{\text{in}}
and
\Phi_{\text{fn}}
in
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
, if it exists, is given explicitly by

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\text{in}}+t\,(\Phi_{\text{fn}}-\Phi_{\text{in}}).

(54)

Consequently, it can be shown that the distance
d_{R}
has the explicit expression

\displaystyle d_{R}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})^{2}=\text{trace}((\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2})\Sigma_{\text{ref}}(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2})^{\intercal}).

(55)

. On the other hand, it is important to point out that geodesics with respect to the
L_{2}
-metric may fail to exist. More precisely, the curves (

) may fail to remain in
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
. For example, if the dimension

is even, then the curve (

.

4.3
The Isoparallel Mass Transport Problem

Having defined the OMT sub-Riemannian structure, we are in a position to pursue our second goal, the design of a control protocol that realizes a specified parallel transport map. To this end, we formulate the following problem.

Problem 1

(Isoparallel Mass Transport (IMT))
.

Given
\Sigma_{\text{in}}
,
\Sigma_{\text{fn}}
in
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
, and
\Phi_{\text{des}}
in
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
with
\Phi_{\text{des}}\sharp\Sigma_{\text{in}}=\Sigma_{\text{fn}}
, find a curve of minimal length
\Sigma^{\star}_{\cdot}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
that satisfies
\Sigma^{\star}_{0}=\Sigma_{\text{in}},

\Sigma^{\star}_{1}=\Sigma_{\text{fn}},
and
\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}^{\star}]=\Phi_{\text{des}}
.

Evidently, the essence of Problem
1
is to effect transport that links two distributions on the base space
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, corresponding to
\Sigma_{\rm in}
and
\Sigma_{\rm fin}
, respectively, ensuring a specified terminal positioning of the particles while minimizing the quadratic cost that is associated with the length that is being traversed under the control of potential forces.
The constraint of the terminal positioning of the particles marks a departure from the classical optimal mass transport problem where only the two terminal distributions are specified.

The IMT problem is an instance of Montgomery’s
isoparallel problem

[
31
]
expressed in the context of OMT. It is equivalent to the problem of finding a geodesic with respect to the OMT sub-Riemannian structure.

First-order necessary conditions

The following proposition is a statement of the necessary conditions satisfied by a solution of the IMT problem.

Proposition 10
.

Assume that
\Sigma_{\cdot}^{\star}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
is a solution to the IMT problem. Then, there exists
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, skew symmetric, such that
\Sigma_{\cdot}^{\star}
satisfies

\displaystyle\ddot{\Sigma}^{\star}_{t}-(\Sigma^{\star}_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma_{t}^{\star}}(\dot{\Sigma}^{\star}_{t})^{2}+\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma_{t}^{\star}}(\dot{\Sigma}^{\star}_{t})^{2}\Sigma^{\star}_{t})

\displaystyle=\xi(\Sigma_{t}^{\star},\dot{\Sigma}_{t}^{\star}),

(56)

for all
t\in[0,1]
, with
\xi(\Sigma,\dot{\Sigma})=\Omega\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}(\dot{\Sigma})-\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}(\dot{\Sigma})\Omega
.

Proof.

Fixing
\Phi_{\text{in}}\in\pi^{-1}(\Sigma_{\text{in}})
, the IMT problem is equivalent to the problem of finding the normal sub-Riemannian geodesic between
\Phi_{\text{in}}
and
\Phi_{\text{des}}\Phi_{\text{in}}
. From Proposition
7
, there must exist a unique smooth curve
\Lambda_{.}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{GL}^{+}(n)
such that the sought-after geodesic satisfies

\displaystyle\dot{{\Phi}}_{t}

\displaystyle=A_{t}{\Phi}_{t},

\displaystyle\dot{{\Lambda}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-A_{t}({\Lambda}_{t}+2A_{t}{\Phi}_{t}\Sigma_{\text{ref}}),

with
A_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\pi(\Phi_{t})}({\Phi}_{t}{\Lambda}_{t}^{\intercal}+{\Lambda}_{t}{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal})
. Projecting
\dot{\Phi}_{t}
onto the tangent space of
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\star}_{t}=\text{d}\pi_{\Phi_{t}}\dot{\Phi}_{t}=A_{t}\Sigma^{\star}_{t}+\Sigma^{\star}_{t}A_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}({\Phi}_{t}{\Lambda}_{t}^{\intercal}+{\Lambda}_{t}{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}).

Let
\Pi_{t}
and
\Omega_{t}
be given by

\displaystyle\Pi_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\Phi_{t}\Lambda_{t}^{\intercal}+\Lambda_{t}\Phi_{t}^{\intercal}),

\displaystyle\Omega_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi_{t}\Lambda_{t}^{\intercal}-\Lambda_{t}\Phi_{t}^{\intercal}).

Then, an explicit calculation shows that

\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}_{t}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma^{\star}_{t}A_{t}+\Omega_{t})A_{t}+A_{t}(A_{t}\Sigma^{\star}_{t}+\Omega_{t}^{\intercal}),

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}_{t}

\displaystyle=0.

In particular, we have that

\displaystyle\Omega_{t}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{0}=\Omega=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\intercal}-\Lambda_{0}\Phi_{0}^{\intercal}).

The proof is concluded by observing that
\Pi_{t}=\dot{\Sigma}_{t}^{\star}
.
∎

The usefulness of Proposition
10
, compared to Proposition
7
, is that
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
does not appear in (
56
).

Existence of solutions to the IMT problem

Sub-Riemannian geodesics between points that are sufficiently close, with respect to the sub-Riemannian distance
d_{SR}
, are guaranteed to exist
[
32
, Theorem 1.18]
. The standard sufficient condition, see
[
31
, Proposition 1]
, to guarantee global existence of sub-Riemannian geodesics, and consequently, global existence of solutions to the isoparallel problem, include the condition that the Riemannian structure on the base space of the principal bundle is complete, i.e. that
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
equipped with the Wasserstein-Otto metric (
40
), abbreviated as
(\text{Sym}^{+}(n),\bar{\mathcal{G}})
for short, is a complete Riemannian manifold. Unfortunately, it is well-known that
(\text{Sym}^{+}(n),\bar{\mathcal{G}})
is not complete. As shown in Remark
1
, nontrivial geodesics in
(\text{Sym}^{+}(n),\bar{\mathcal{G}})
may be extended in one but never in both directions. This alone is sufficient to prove that the sub-Riemannian structure of OMT is not geodesically complete. Indeed, we have the following proposition which follows from the fact that the OMT sub-Riemannian structure is of the
bundle-type

[
32
, Chapter 11]
.

Proposition 11
.

Any horizontal lift of a McCann geodesic is a sub-Riemannian geodesic.

A geodesic in
(\text{Sym}^{+}(n),\bar{\mathcal{G}})
that cannot be extended must approach the boundary of
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
which consists of degenerate symmetric positive semi-definite matrices. The horizontal lift of such a curve will necessarily approach a degenerate
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\backslash\text{GL}^{+}(n)
, and so cannot be extended indefinitely in
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
. A potential remedy might be to
complete

(\text{Sym}^{+}(n),\bar{\mathcal{G}})
, i.e. include all its limit points in the topology induced by the metric
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
. Unfortunately, this cannot be done smoothly. Namely, by completing
(\text{Sym}^{+}(n),\bar{\mathcal{G}})
, it ceases to be a smooth manifold altogether. Instead, it becomes a
stratified space
, though it remains a
length space

[
41
]
. Alternatively, we may complete the OMT sub-Riemannian structure itself as a metric space. This approach is faced with a major obstacle: the horizontal sub-bundle, as defined, is not bracket-generating at the boundary of
\text{GL}^{+}(n)
. A third alternative is to “regularize” the OMT sub-Riemannian metric in the spirit of the Schrödinger bridge approach to regularized OMT
[
27
,
8
]
. The aforementioned potential remedies and their implications for the OMT sub-Riemannian structure will be explored in future work.

5
Application: Transportation Cycles

Modern day control applications of multiagent and particle systems have brought to the fore a slew of new type of performance objectives, such as maintaining formation, avoiding congestion, and so on, that require sophisticated path planning strategies. A model example for the theory that we put forth is the rudimentary paradigm of particles being transported along cycles, where it is of essence that they retain their initial configuration upon returning to their starting configuration.
It is envisioned that elements of this theory will soon be tailored to more general dynamics, but for now we consider that the collection of particles are actuated by potential forces.

Our problem requires that a collection of particles
cycle through a sequence
\{\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{2},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{N}\}
of specified distributions. The control cost is quadratic, and is quantified by the length of the curve in the Wasserstein space traversed by the ensemble in their flight between target distributions.

In contrast to the formalism of uncertainty control where the focus is on regulating distributions, herein the control protocol needs to guide individual particles along closed orbits. To this end, it is of essence that their position is “registered” relative to one another and relative to their common distribution. In other words, before designing a control law, one needs to know in advance how the particles are to be distributed when they arrive at the
k
th point of the cycle.

The need to register (positional) data is not unique to ensemble control.
It is a problem that is encountered in many fields. Broadly speaking, registration refers to ways that one can correspond data between different collections of such. In other words, registration can be seen as the process to generate labels for the elements of a collection, so as to establish correspondence between elements of several different collections. Herein, labels signify positional correspondence.
In another instance, imagine pictures of the same object taken from slightly different perspectives, where we wish to establish automatically a correspondence between pixels in each. Over the past twenty years, OMT has been extensively used for these as well as other related problems of matching distributions
[
45
,
17
,
36
]
.

A natural approach for registration is to identify a reference distribution. Then, for instance, one may use optimal transportation to establish pairwise matching at the level of particles. More concretely, given the collection
\{\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{2},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{N}\}
, and a reference distribution
\mu_{\text{ref}}
, “registration” between
\bm{\mu}_{i}
and
\bm{\mu}_{j}
can be effected by

\displaystyle\varphi_{i\mapsto j}

\displaystyle:=\varphi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\mapsto j}\circ\varphi^{\star}_{i\mapsto\text{ref}},

(60)

where
\varphi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\mapsto i}
is the Monge map taking
\mu_{\text{ref}}
to
\bm{\mu}_{i}
and
\varphi^{\star}_{i\mapsto\text{ref}}
is its inverse. Evidently, the holonomy of the loop

\bm{\mu}_{i}\xrightarrow{\varphi^{\star}_{i\mapsto\text{ref}}}\mu_{\rm ref}\xrightarrow{\varphi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\mapsto j}}\bm{\mu}_{j}\xrightarrow{\varphi^{\star}_{j\mapsto i}}\bm{\mu}_{i},

may not be trivial, and therefore, the choice
\varphi^{\star}_{j\mapsto i}
is generally not an option.
Instead, the pairwise matching in
(
60
) ensures that the holonomy of triangle

\bm{\mu}_{i}\xrightarrow{\varphi^{\star}_{i\mapsto\text{ref}}}\mu_{\rm ref}\xrightarrow{\varphi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\mapsto j}}\bm{\mu}_{j}\xrightarrow{\varphi_{j\mapsto i}}\bm{\mu}_{i}

is trivial.
Hence, the
interpolating curve between

\bm{\mu}_{i}
,
\bm{\mu}_{j}
traversed by particles via an optimal control law, would constitute the solution to the IMT problem, i.e., it will be the curve of minimal sub-Riemannian length that realizes (
60
). Both the specific curve and the resulting distance depend on the choice of
\mu_{\rm ref}
.

Wasserstein Polygons

We consider
N
Gaussian distributions with corresponding covariances
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{N}\}\subset\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
. In a consistent manner,
\Sigma_{\text{ref}}
signifies the covariance of a reference distribution.
We consider separately the “triangles” formed with “vertices”
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}
and
\Sigma_{\rm ref}
, for
(i,j)\in\{(1,2),(2,3),\dots,(N,1)\}
.

Denoting by
\Phi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\rightarrow i}
the Monge map linking
{\Sigma}_{\text{ref}}
to
\bm{\Sigma}_{i}
and by
\Phi^{\star}_{i\rightarrow\text{ref}}
its inverse, we associate to every pair (
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})
a family of Wasserstein triangles of the form

\displaystyle\triangle^{i,j}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}(I+3t(\Phi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\rightarrow i}-I)){\sharp}{\Sigma}_{\text{ref}},&t\in\left[0,\frac{1}{3}\right),\\
\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{\tau},\text{ with $\tau=3t-1$,}&t\in\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}\right),\\
(I+(3t-2)(\Phi^{\star}_{j\rightarrow\text{ref}}-I)){\sharp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j},&t\in\left[\frac{2}{3},1\right].\\
\end{cases}

Note that two of the “edges” are formed by McCann geodesics, whereas the third “edge” of the triangle is
\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{.}:[0,1]\rightarrow\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
, and this is considered to be any
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve satisfying

\displaystyle\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i},\;\;\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{1}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}.

(61)

In particular,
\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{.}
is the curve traversed by the distribution of the particles in their flight from
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}
to
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}
. From (
61
), it is clear that
\triangle^{i,j}_{.}
is a closed curve in
\text{Sym}^{+}(n)
. As can be verified, the holonomy of
\triangle^{i,j}_{.}
is

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\triangle^{i,j}_{.}]=\Phi^{\star}_{j\rightarrow\text{ref}}\text{Par}[\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{.}]\Phi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\rightarrow i},

where
\text{Par}[\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{.}]
is the parallel transport map along
\Sigma^{ij}_{.}
. If no mixing is to take place, i.e.
\text{Par}[\triangle^{i,j}_{.}]=I
, then we need that

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{.}]=\Phi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\rightarrow j}\Phi^{\star}_{i\rightarrow\text{ref}}.

(62)

For any collection of curves
\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{.}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\Sigma^{1\mapsto 2}_{\tau},\text{ with $\tau=Nt$,}&t\in\left[0,\frac{1}{N}\right],\\

which sequentially visits the vertices of the Wasserstein polygon in the order

We then have the following proposition.

Proposition 12
.

If
\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{.}
satisfies (
62
) for every
(i,j)\in\{(1,2),(2,3),\dots,(N,1)\}
, then
\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]=I
.

Proof.

By construction of the curve
\Sigma_{.}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]

\displaystyle=\text{Par}[\Sigma^{N\mapsto 1}_{.}]\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\text{Par}[\Sigma^{i\mapsto i+1}_{.}].

Additionally, we recall that

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\triangle^{i,j}_{.}]=\Phi^{\star}_{j\rightarrow\text{ref}}\text{Par}[\Sigma^{i\mapsto j}_{.}]\Phi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\rightarrow i},

Hence, we obtain that

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]

\displaystyle=\text{Par}[\Sigma^{N\mapsto 1}_{.}]\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\Phi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\rightarrow i+1}\text{Par}[\triangle^{i,i+1}_{.}]\Phi^{\star}_{i\rightarrow\text{ref}}.

However, by assumption,
\text{Par}[\triangle^{i,j}_{.}]=I
for every pair
(i,j)\in\{(1,2),(2,3),\dots,(N,1)\}
, and so

\displaystyle\text{Par}[\Sigma_{.}]

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\rightarrow 1}\Phi^{\star}_{N\rightarrow\text{ref}}\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\Phi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\rightarrow i+1}\Phi^{\star}_{i\rightarrow\text{ref}}.

The proposition follows by expanding the product and sequentially applying the identity
\Phi^{\star}_{\text{ref}\rightarrow i}\Phi^{\star}_{i\rightarrow\text{ref}}=I.

Remark 4
.

In principle, it is possible to seek a horizontal curve of minimal length that visits the given distributions, in the specified order, which leads to a sequence of optimal control problems that are nonlinearly coupled through their boundaries. The Agueh-Carlier barycenter

A popular choice for “averaging” a collection of distributions
\{\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{2},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{N}\}

[

\bm{\mu}_{\text{AC}}=\arg\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\bm{\mu}_{i})^{2}.

For a collection of Gaussian measures with corresponding covariances

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{AC}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{\vphantom{\frac{1}{2}}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{AC}}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{AC}}.

Remark 5
.

The path along a cycle may be optimized over the parallel transport maps along each segment, subject to the condition of a trivial holonomy. 6
Epilogue

The impact of Monge’s problem, to establish an optimal correspondence between distributions, has been foundational across mathematics, engineering, and the sciences
[
44
,
36
]
. It constitutes a basic paradigm in stochastic control
[
9
,
10
]
and has provided a setting for addressing a host of problems in statistics, optimization, probability theory, thermodynamics, economics, machine learning, to name a few.

Out of a rapidly growing literature we select two references that highlight the relevance of potential forces, i.e., generated as gradients of a potential, and hence to symmetric
A_{t}
’s. In the first one, Movilla Miangolarra etal.
[
33
]
study thermodynamic ensembles driven by potential forces to traverse a closed thermodynamic cycle, to obtain fundamental bounds on the maximal power that can be drawn from thermal anisotropy. In the next, Lavenant etal.
[
25
]
develop a framework of trajectory inference for cell differentiation and development, where the underlying potential forcing is due to the so-called Waddington’s landscape. In both situations, the constraint on vector fields governing the evolution of thermodynamic and cell states being potential arises from physical considerations.

The motivation of this work has been to explore and bring to life a hitherto unstudied angle of a Lagrangian viewpoint
in optimal transport
, where the actual trajectories and mixing of
individual (tracer)
particles are of interest.
We develop our framework in the simplest setting of linear dynamics and Gaussian distributions.
Thus, our central theme has been to control Gaussian distributions using potential forces, and to characterize the holonomy of particle trajectories. The OMT principal bundle structure duly encodes particle trajectories by recording state transition matrices
\Phi_{t}
.
Equivalently, the trajectories of
n
representative tracer particles is encoded in the columns of
\Phi_{t}
, and these provide information on the holonomy of the flow.

Our main contribution has been to introduce and study the holonomy of the Monge-Kantorovich optimal mass transport. The key insight has been to encode the Eulerian constraint on the velocity field to be potential into the OMT principal Ehresmann connection. We then explored the sub-Riemannian structure induced by this connection along with the Wasserstein-Otto metric in the context of Gaussian distributions. A more comprehensive study of this structure in finite and infinite dimensions,
as in e.g.,
[
22
]
,
is of interest, and
is currently being pursued by the authors. We also note an apparent link between the holonomy of optimal transport and quantum holonomy, as defined by Berry and Simon on the space of density matrices, where a horizontal space of Hermitian vector fields induces a natural Ehresmann connection; see, e.g.,
[
42
, Eq. (3)]
, which has given rise to growing literature on alternative geometries of positive and invertible elements of unital
C^{*}
-algebras
[
13
]
.

Finally, a similar framework to the one developed herein should prove useful in ensemble control
[
28
,
12
,
7
]
, where trajectories of multi-species populations indexed by
s
, for instance, are encoded in collections of transition matrices,
\Phi^{s}_{t}
’s, on a suitable bundle
\pi
projecting onto the configuration in a base space, e.g., of covariance data of the form
\Sigma_{t}=\sum_{s}\Phi^{s}_{t}(\Phi^{s}_{t})^{\intercal}
.
Other variants of practical interest include modeling the flow of particles based on
aggregate information and the location of tracer particles used to estimate the holonomy of the collection, and, likewise, estimation of trajectories of dynamical systems driven by stochastic excitation based on sample trajectories. These are the subject of ongoing work.
