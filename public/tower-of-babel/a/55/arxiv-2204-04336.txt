A new family of smooth copulas with arbitrarily irregular densities
Michaël Lalancette ∗, Robert Zimmerman ∗

Abstract
Copulas are known to satisfy a number of regularity properties, and one might therefore believe that their densities, when they exist, admit a certain degree of regularity themselves. We show that this is not true in general by constructing a broad family of copulas which admit densities that can hardly be considered regular. The copula densities are constructed from arbitrary univariate densities supported on the unit interval, and we show by example that the copula densities can inherit pathological behaviour from the underlying univariate densities. In particular, we construct a nontrivial univariate density which is unbounded in every open set of the unit interval, and show that it induces a copula density which is finite everywhere but unbounded in every neighborhood of the unit hypercube. Nevertheless, all of our copulas are shown to enjoy attractive smoothness properties.

Introduction

Let
d\geq 2
. A
d
-dimensional
copula
is a function
C:[0,1]^{d}\to[0,1]
satisfying the following three conditions:

\displaystyle C(1,\dots,1,u,1,\dots,1)

\displaystyle=u,\quad 0\leq u\leq 1

(1)

\displaystyle C(u_{1},\ldots,u_{i-1},0,u_{i+1},\ldots,u_{d})

\displaystyle=0,\quad 0\leq u_{j}\leq 1

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{2}\cdots\sum_{i_{d}=1}^{2}(-1)^{i_{1}+\cdots i_{d}}C\left(u_{1}^{(i_{1})},\dots,u_{d}^{(i_{d})}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad 0\leq u_{j}^{(1)}\leq u_{j}^{(2)}\leq 1.

(3)

According to a famous result in probability theory known as
Sklar’s theorem

(
Sklar 1959
)
, for any random vector
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with continuous marginal distribution functions
F_{1},\dots,F_{d}
, there is a unique copula
C
that satisfies

C(\bm{u}):=\mathbb{P}(F_{1}(X_{1})\leq u_{1},\dots,F_{d}(X_{d})\leq u_{d}),\quad\bm{u}:=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{d}).

The function
C
is said to be the
copula of

\bm{X}
; it is the joint distribution function of the random vector
(F_{1}(X_{1}),\dots,F_{d}(X_{d}))
. Conversely, every distribution function satisfying
Equation

1
is a copula. Informally, the copula fully characterizes the dependence between the random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{d}
in a way that does not depend on their distributions
F_{1},\dots,F_{d}
. An extensive literature
(
Schweizer 1991
;
Joe 1997
;
Nelsen 2007
;
Durante and
Sempi 2016
)
reveals a vast array of dependence structures that can be explicitly described using copulas.

If the distribution of
\bm{X}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, then
C
admits a
copula density

c
given by

c(\bm{u}):=\frac{\partial^{d}}{\partial u_{1}\dots\partial u_{d}}C(\bm{u}).

The fact that
Equations

1
,
2
and
3
are stronger than the standard properties of multivariate distribution functions imposes a number of regularity properties. For example,
C
satisfies sharp lower and upper bounds known as the
Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds
:

\max\left\{\sum_{h=1}^{d}u_{h}-(d-1),0\right\}\leq C(\bm{u})\leq\min\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}\}.

It is also not hard to see that
C
is 1-Lipschitz with respect to the supremum norm. One might therefore suspect that when
C
is absolutely continuous, its copula density
c
cannot be too poorly-behaved. In practice, however, one often encounters poor behavior on the boundary of the support of
c
or on sets of measure zero. For example, the Clayton copula density
(
Hofert
et al. 2012
)
is unbounded along the curve
\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}^{-\theta}=d-1
when
\theta<-1/d
, while the Gaussian copula density is unbounded at the corners
\mathbf{0}:=(0,\dots,0)
and
\mathbf{1}:=(1,\dots,1)
; other examples include the Gumbel and
t
families
(
Bouezmarni
et al. 2013
)
. Such behaviour is inconvenient but usually not overly restrictive. However, as we shall demonstrate, there exist highly regular copulas with densities that are poorly behaved on their entire domain.

This paper offers two main contributions. First, in
Section

2.1
, we construct a class
\mathscr{C}
of absolutely continuous copulas on
[0,1]^{d}
, for which the corresponding class of copula densities includes highly pathological functions. Each copula
C_{f}\in\mathscr{C}
is “generated” by an underlying univariate density
f
on
[0,1]
whose smoothness behaviour determines the smoothness of the corresponding copula density
c_{f}
. In particular, the set of discontinuity points of
f
determines that of
c_{f}
. This, among other properties of the family
\mathscr{C}
, is shown in
Section

2.2
. In
Section

3.1
, we exhibit several examples of copulas in
\mathscr{C}
. As our second main contribution, in
Section

3.2
, we identify a nontrivial copula
C_{f^{*}}\in\mathscr{C}
whose copula density
c_{f^{*}}
is unbounded in every neighborhood of the unit hypercube — that is,
\sup_{\bm{u}\in U}c_{f^{*}}(\bm{u})=\infty
for every nonempty open set
U\subset[0,1]^{d}
. Nevertheless, we show that any
C_{f}\in\mathscr{C}
, including
C_{f^{*}}
, must have partial derivatives of higher order that are continuous everywhere as well as bounded, which opens up interesting connections to statistical inference via empirical copulas. We conclude with a brief discussion and suggestions for future work in
Section

4
.

Our results, particularly our construction of the aforementioned copula
C_{f^{*}}
, showcase a fundamental decoupling between copulas and their densities. A certain degree of smoothness is required of a copula
C
for elegant probabilistic properties to hold, especially with regards to large sample statistical theory, when observations are drawn from a distribution with copula
C
. In contrast, our results show that considering only copulas with “traditional” densities (which are, for example, bounded and continuous almost everywhere) is indeed restrictive.

1.1
Notation and conventions

We write
\bm{u}
for a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{d}
for its elements,
\bm{u}_{-j}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{j-1},u_{j+1},\ldots,u_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
for
\bm{u}
with its
j
th element removed, and
\bm{u}_{J}
for the subvector indexed by the elements of an arbitrary set
J\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
. We use
\bm{e}_{i}
for the
i
th canonical basis vector.
[\bm{0},\bm{u}]
is to be understood as the rectangle
\bigtimes_{h=1}^{d}[0,u_{h}]
. By
x\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1
, we mean the fractional part of
x
given by
x-\lfloor x\rfloor
, where
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the largest integer upper bounded by
x
. All densities are taken with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, where the dimension
p
should be clear from context. We define
\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
as the set of densities on
[0,1]
, i.e., any
f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
is measurable, non-negative and integrates to 1 on
[0,1]
. To avoid ambiguity, we continue to distinguish “copula densities” from “univariate densities” throughout. All our results hold for a fixed, but arbitrary, dimension
d\geq 2
, unless specified otherwise.

2
Construction of
\mathscr{C}
and basic properties

2.1
The construction

Our construction is very simple and works in any dimension. Rather than explicitly defining a copula
C_{f}\in\mathscr{C}
, we begin with an appropriate univariate density and use it to specify the copula density
c_{f}
; this in turn determines
C_{f}
. Specifically, given any
f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
, we define

c_{f}(\bm{u}):=f\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\right),\quad\bm{u}\in[0,1]^{d}.

(4)

Our first result shows that
c_{f}
is a true copula density. We thus define our class
\mathscr{C}
as the set of
d
-dimensional copulas which have a density in
\{c_{f}:f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}\}
.

Proposition 1
.

For every univariate density
f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
, there exists a copula
C_{f}
whose copula density is given by
c_{f}
.

Proof.

The function
c_{f}
is clearly non-negative and measurable, as it is a composition of finitely many measurable functions. In view of
Equation

1
, it is therefore a valid copula density if for each
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, it satisfies the marginal constraint

\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}c_{f}(\bm{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{u}_{-j}=1

(5)

regardless of the value of
u_{j}
. In fact, the function
c_{f}
satisfies a stronger property: let
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
be an arbitrary index and fix all the coordinates of
\bm{u}
but the
i
th one. Define

v:=\sum_{j\neq i}u_{j}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1.

Then

\int_{0}^{1}c_{f}(\bm{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{i}=\int_{0}^{1-v}f(u_{i}+v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{i}+\int_{1-v}^{1}f(u_{i}+v-1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{i}=\int_{v}^{1}f(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{v}f(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=1,

(6)

where the second equality follows by an affine change of variable. It follows that
Equation

5
is satisfied; hence
c_{f}
is the density of a copula
C_{f}
.
∎

2.2
Some properties of the copulas
C_{f}

It is interesting to note that while the copulas
C_{f}
can have extremely irregular densities (see
Section

3.2
), the copulas themselves enjoy several attractive properties which we list here.

Proposition 2
.

If
\bm{U}\sim C_{f}
for some
f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
, the following hold.

1.

\bm{U}
is exchangeable.

2.

For any
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
,
\bm{U}
has the following stochastic representation, where
\Pi
denotes the independence copula:

\bm{U}_{-i}\sim\Pi,\quad\bigg(U_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}U_{j}\bigg)\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\ \bigg|\ \bm{U}_{-i}\sim f.

Proof.

Exchangeability follows immediately from
Equation

4
; the density
c_{f}(\bm{u})
depends only on
\bm{u}
through the sum of its arguments, which is a symmetric function.

For the stochastic representation, the independence of the random vector
\bm{U}_{-i}
follows from the fact that its density is given by

\int_{0}^{1}c_{f}(\bm{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{i}=1,\quad\bm{u}_{-i}\in[0,1]^{d-1}

by
Equation

6
. It then follows that given
\sum_{j\neq i}U_{j}
, the conditional density of

\bigg(U_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}U_{j}\bigg)\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1

is given by
f/1=f
.
∎

An immediate consequence of the above representation is that if a random vector
\bm{X}
has copula
C_{f}
, then the marginal vector
\bm{X}_{-i}
has the independence copula. That is, any subset of at most
d-1
of the variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{d}
are mutually independent. As a corollary, we find that the intersection of
\mathscr{C}
and the class of Archimedean copulas is trivial, so no confusion arises from our use of the word “generator” and its use for Archimedean generators.

Corollary 1
.

The only Archimedean copula in
\mathscr{C}
is
\Pi
.

Proof.

Suppose
\bm{U}
has an Archimedean copula, so that
C_{f}(\bm{u})=\psi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi^{-1}(u_{i})\right)
for some Archimedean generator
\psi
. Such a function has a continuous, decreasing inverse on
[0,1]
which satisfies
\psi^{-1}(1)=0
. For any index
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the distribution of the marginal vector
\bm{U}_{-i}
is given by

C_{f}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{i-1},1,u_{i+1},\ldots,u_{d})=\psi\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\psi^{-1}(u_{j})\right)=\prod_{j\neq i}u_{j},

where the second equality follows from
2
. Thus
\sum_{j\neq i}\psi^{-1}(u_{j})=\psi^{-1}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}u_{j}\right)
, so that
\psi^{-1}
satisfies Cauchy’s logarithmic functional equation, the continuous and decreasing solutions of which are of the form
\psi^{-1}(t)=-c\log(t)
for
c>0

(
Aczél 1966
)
. Thus
\psi(t)=\exp(-t/c)
, and it follows that
C_{f}(\bm{u})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}=\Pi(\bm{u})
.
∎

Our next result concerns the smoothness of the partial derivatives of
C_{f}
.

Proposition 3
.

For every
f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
and each
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, the
j
th partial derivative
\dot{C}_{f,j}
of
C_{f}
exists and is continuous on the set
\pazocal{V}_{j}:=\{\bm{u}\in[0,1]^{d}:u_{j}\in(0,1)\}
. If
d\geq 3
, then for each pair
(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{2}
, the second order partial derivative
\ddot{C}_{f,ij}
exists and is continuous on
\pazocal{V}_{i}\cap\pazocal{V}_{j}
, and
|\ddot{C}_{f,ij}(\bm{u})|\leq 1
.

Remark
.

It is a standard fact that wherever they exist, the first order partial derivatives of any copula are bounded by 1. That is, for any copula
C
, we have
0\leq\dot{C}_{j}(\bm{u})\leq 1
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, which follows directly from the monotonicity and 1-Lipschitz properties. However, the boundedness of
second order
partial derivatives, even assuming their existence, does not hold in general. Consider, for example, a trivariate extension of entry 4.2.9 in the well-known list of one-parameter Archimedean copulas found in Table 4.1 of
Nelsen 2007
, given by
C_{\theta}(\bm{u})=u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}\exp\left(-\theta\log(u_{1})\log(u_{2})\log(u_{3})\right)
where
\theta\in(0,1]
. This is a valid copula when
\theta\leq(3-\sqrt{5})/2
, for then its Archimedean generator satisfies the
3
-monotonicity property
(
McNeil and
Nešlehová 2009
, Theorem 2.2)
. An easy calculation shows that for fixed, positive
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
,

\ddot{C}_{\theta,11}(\bm{u})\propto u_{1}^{-1-\theta\log(u_{2})\log(u_{3})}

which is unbounded around
u_{1}=0
.

Before proving
3
, we briefly comment on the applicability of the result to statistical inference. Given independent and identically distributed observations with continuous marginals and copula
C
, the
empirical copula
is the canonical nonparametric estimator of
C
. It is given by

\widehat{C}_{n}(\bm{u}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\left\{R_{k}^{1}\leq nu_{1},\dots,R_{k}^{d}\leq nu_{d}\right\},

where
R_{k}^{j}
denotes the rank of the
k
th observation of the
j
th variable among all observations of that variable. It is well known that at a point
\bm{u}\in(0,1)^{d}
,
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{C}_{n}(\bm{u})-C(\bm{u}))
is asymptotically normal if and only if the partial derivatives of
C
exist and are continuous at
\bm{u}
. In fact,
Segers 2012
proved that the
empirical copula process

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{C}_{n}-C)
converges in distribution to a Gaussian process in
\ell^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})
if and only if each partial derivative
\dot{C}_{j}
exists and is continuous on
\pazocal{V}_{j}
(see Proposition 3.1 therein).
Radulović et al. 2017
consider the empirical copula process indexed by a class of right-continuous functions of uniformly bounded variation. There again, existence and continuity of the partial derivatives on the sets
\pazocal{V}_{j}
ensures uniform weak convergence of the empirical copula process to a tight Gaussian process (see their Theorem 5). In
Berghaus
et al. 2017
, in addition to the existence and continuity of the first order partial derivatives, the second order partial derivatives are assumed to exist, to be continuous and to satisfy a certain bound. The empirical copula process is then shown to converge in distribution to a tight Gaussian process in weighted metrics, strengthening the result of
Segers 2012
(see their Theorem 2.2).

Segers
et al. 2017
introduce smoothed versions of the empirical copula. Asymptotic normality of the so-called
empirical beta copula
and
empirical Bernstein copula
processes is shown to hold if the first order partial derivatives exist, are continuous everywhere, and satisfy a local Lipschitz property (see Theorem 3.6 therein). The latter holds, for instance, if the second order partial derivatives exist and are bounded everywhere.

3
guarantees that all of the aforementioned results hold when the data is distributed according to any copula in
\mathscr{C}
, regardless of the generator — in particular, they hold for the copula
C_{f^{*}}
with nowhere continuous and nowhere bounded density, as constructed in
Section

3.2
. Interestingly, multiple copulas appear smoother than
C_{f^{*}}
at first sight, but do not have continuous partial derivatives. For example, the so-called
checkerboard copula
on
[0,1]^{2}
has density
c:=2\mathds{1}_{[0,1/2]^{2}\cup[1/2,1]^{2}}
, which is bounded and almost everywhere continuous. Yet its partial derivatives are not continuous everywhere, so asymptotic normality of the associated empirical copula process fails.

The key to the proof of
3
is the following.

Lemma 1
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
and
J\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}
be nonempty. Then for any values of
\bm{u}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}
and
\bm{v}_{-J}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-|J|}
,

\int_{[0,\bm{u}_{J}]}f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{J}=\int_{[0,\bm{u}_{J}]}f\left(\sum_{i\in J}v_{i}+\sum_{i\notin J}v_{i}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{J}

is continuous, both in
\bm{u}_{J}
and in
\bm{v}_{-J}
.

Proof.

First note that in the proof of
1
, we show that

f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\right)

is integrable on
[0,1]^{d}
. By using the same strategy, one can show that it is also integrable on arbitrary compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The same can be said if we are integrating only with respect to the subset of variables
\bm{v}_{J}
. Continuity in
\bm{u}_{J}
thus follows from continuity from above of Lebesgue measure.

To establish continuity in the direction of
\bm{v}_{-J}
, we show that an infinitesimal change in
\bm{v}_{-J}
is equivalent to an infinitesimal change in some component of
\bm{u}_{J}
; we then simply apply the first part of the present result. Indeed, for a small vector
\bm{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, adding
\bm{\delta}_{-J}
to
\bm{v}_{-J}
shifts
\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}
to

\sum_{i\notin J}(v_{i}+\delta_{i})+\sum_{i\in J}v_{i}=\sum_{i\neq i^{*}}v_{i}+(v_{i^{*}}+\delta),

for some arbitrary
i^{*}\in J
and for
\delta:=\sum_{i\notin J}\delta_{i}
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{[0,\bm{u}_{J}]}f\left(\sum_{i\notin J}(v_{i}+\delta_{i})+\sum_{i\in J}v_{i}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{J}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\bm{u}_{J}]}f\left(\sum_{i\neq i^{*}}v_{i}+(v_{i^{*}}+\delta)\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{J}

\displaystyle=\int_{[\delta\bm{e}_{i^{*}},\bm{u}_{J}+\delta\bm{e}_{i^{*}}]}f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{J}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\int_{[0,\bm{u}_{J}]}f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{J}

as
\delta\to 0
, by the first part of the result.
∎

Proof of
3
.

We consider the first and second order partial derivatives separately.

First order partial derivatives.
By exchangeability, we may without loss of generality take
j=1
. Let
\bm{u}\in\pazocal{V}_{1}
. By Tonelli’s theorem and the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\dot{C}_{f,1}(\bm{u}):=\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{1}}\int_{0}^{u_{1}}\int_{[0,\bm{u}_{-1}]}c_{f}(v_{1},\bm{v}_{-1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{1}=\int_{[0,\bm{u}_{-1}]}c_{f}(u_{1},\bm{v}_{-1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{-1},

where we write
c_{f}(u_{1},\bm{v}_{-1})
for
c_{f}
evaluated at the vector
(u_{1},\bm{v}_{-1})
. Note that the application of the fundamental theorem of calculus in the second equality requires continuity of the right-hand side in
u_{1}
. This is a consequence of
1
, which at the same time asserts that
\dot{C}_{f,1}
is continuous at
\bm{u}
.

Second order partial derivatives.
Now suppose
d\geq 3
. Again by exchangeability, it suffices to treat
\ddot{C}_{f,12}
and
\ddot{C}_{f,11}
. Using the expression for
\dot{C}_{f,1}
above,

\ddot{C}_{f,12}(\bm{u}):=\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{2}}\int_{0}^{u_{2}}\int_{\left[0,\bm{u}_{-\{1,2\}}\right]}c_{f}(u_{1},v_{2},\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{2}=\int_{\left[0,\bm{u}_{-\{1,2\}}\right]}c_{f}(u_{1},v_{2},\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}},

which is continuous in
\bm{u}
. As before, we have used Tonelli’s theorem, the fundamental theorem of calculus and
1
.

We now evaluate
\ddot{C}_{f,11}
. Let
u\in\pazocal{V}_{1}
,
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{f,1}(\bm{u}+\delta\bm{e}_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u_{2}}\int_{\left[0,\bm{u}_{-\{1,2\}}\right]}c_{f}(u_{1}+\delta,v_{2},\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u_{2}}\int_{\left[0,\bm{u}_{-\{1,2\}}\right]}c_{f}(u_{1},v_{2}+\delta,\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{u_{2}+\delta}\int_{\left[0,\bm{u}_{-\{1,2\}}\right]}c_{f}(u_{1},v_{2},\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{2}.

In case
\delta<0
or
u_{2}+\delta>1
, the definition of
c_{f}
in
Equation

4
can be trivially extended outside
[0,1]^{d}
. Deduce that
\ddot{C}_{f,11}(\bm{u})
exists and is given by

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\dot{C}_{f,1}(\bm{u}+\delta\bm{e}_{1})-\dot{C}_{f,1}(\bm{u})}{\delta}=\int_{\left[0,\bm{u}_{-\{1,2\}}\right]}c_{f}(u_{1},u_{2},\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}}-\int_{\left[0,\bm{u}_{-\{1,2\}}\right]}c_{f}(u_{1},0,\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{-\{1,2\}},

which is continuous in
\bm{u}
by
1
. As discussed in the proof thereof,
c_{f}(u_{1},u_{2},\cdot)
is a density on
[0,1]^{d-2}
. It follows that
\ddot{C}_{f,12}(\bm{u})\in[0,1]
and
\ddot{C}_{f,11}(\bm{u})\in[-1,1]
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

In proving
3
, we find that no matter how “rough”
c_{f}
is, as long as it is integrated with respect to at least one variable, the resulting antiderivative is continuous. Since differentiating the copula essentially removes one integral, the resulting derivative exists and is continuous as long as at least one integral remains. By iterating the process, one can show that all the partial derivatives of
C_{f}
of order up to
d-1
exist and that they are continuous and bounded on suitable domains.

We now examine a traditional measure of concordance for bivariate copulas, Spearman’s
\rho
. It is certain that no copula in
\mathscr{C}
can have Spearman’s
\rho
too close to 1, since no such copula approaches the comonotonicity copula. The countermonotonicity copula, however, can be approached by choosing a generator
f
whose support approaches
\{0,1\}
. In fact, the range of values of Spearman’s
\rho
for the copulas in
\mathscr{C}
is found to be exactly
(-1,1/2)
.

Proposition 4
.

If
d=2
, then for every
f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
, the copula
C_{f}
has

\rho_{C_{f}}=6\mathbb{E}_{X\sim f}\left[X(1-X)\right]-1\in\left(-1,\frac{1}{2}\right),

where both limiting values
-1
and
1/2
can be approached.

Proof.

According to Theorem 5.1.6 in
Nelsen 2007
,

\rho_{C_{f}}=12\int_{[0,1]}u_{1}u_{2}c_{f}(\bm{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{u}-3.

(7)

The integral can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}u_{1}u_{2}f(u_{1}+u_{2}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{1}u_{1}\left\{\int_{0}^{1-u_{1}}u_{2}f(u_{1}+u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}+\int_{1-u_{1}}^{1}u_{2}f(u_{1}+u_{2}-1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{1}u_{1}\left\{\int_{u_{1}}^{1}(u_{2}-u_{1})f(u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}+\int_{0}^{u_{1}}(u_{2}-u_{1}+1)f(u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{1}u_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}\int_{0}^{1}u_{2}f(u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}-\int_{0}^{1}u_{1}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}\int_{0}^{1}f(u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}+\int_{0}^{1}F(u_{1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}uf(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u^{2}f(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u+\frac{1}{6},

where we have used the fact that for a positive integer
p
,

p\int_{0}^{1}u^{p-1}(1-F(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{1}u^{p}f(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

which is the
p
th moment of the distribution
f
. Inserting this into
Equation

7
yields

\rho_{C_{f}}=6\int_{0}^{1}u(1-u)f(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u-1,

which is the desired expression.

For the bounds, we note that
0\leq x(1-x)\leq 1/4
, and that these bounds are attained at
x\in\{0,1\}
and at
x=1/2
, respectively. Letting the density
f
put all of its mass arbitrarily close to
0
or
1
will yield
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim f}\left[X(1-X)\right]
arbitrarily small, whereas if
f
has all its mass arbitrarily close to
1/2
, that quantity will approach its maximum of
1/4
.
∎

For a random vector with copula
C_{f}
, no subvector has positive tail dependence. There is, however, no limit to the amount of negative tail dependence that it can have; for instance, if
f
assigns no mass to a neighborhood of
0
, then
C_{f}
has perfect negative lower tail dependence. The following result is stated in terms of lower tail dependence; an analogous statement holds for the upper tail.

Proposition 5
.

For every
f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
, we have

C_{f}(t,\dots,t)=o\big(t^{d-1}\big),\quad t\downarrow 0.

Proof.

For simplicity, assume that
t\leq 1/d
. First, note that the hypercube
[0,t]^{d}
is included in the region
[0,td]\cdot\Delta^{d-1}=\{r\Delta^{d-1}:0\leq r\leq td\}
, where
\Delta^{d-1}:=\{\bm{u}\in[0,1]^{d}:\sum_{j}u_{j}=1\}
is the unit simplex. Thus

C_{f}(t,\dots,t)\leq\int_{[0,td]\cdot\Delta^{d-1}}c_{f}(\bm{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{u}=\int_{[0,td]\cdot\Delta^{d-1}}f\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{u}=\int_{0}^{td}f(r)\int_{r\Delta^{d-2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,

where the last equality comes from the change of variable
r=\sum_{j}u_{j}
,
\bm{v}=\bm{u}_{-1}
. The inner integral is
r^{d-1}
times the volume under the
(d-2)
-dimensional simplex in
[0,1]^{d-1}
, which is equal to
1/(d-1)!
. Recalling that
f
is a density and hence integrable around
0
, we conclude that

C_{f}(t,\dots,t)\leq\frac{1}{(d-1)!}\int_{0}^{td}r^{d-1}f(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\leq\frac{d^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}t^{d-1}\int_{0}^{td}f(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=o\big(t^{d-1}\big).

∎

3
Examples of generators

3.1
Simple examples

To begin with, it is obvious from
Equation

4
that by choosing the generator
f_{1}:=1
, we obtain the density
c_{f_{1}}=1
of
\Pi
, as shown in the left panel of
Figure

1
. More generally, we can define a sequence of univariate densities
\{f_{n}\}
by

f_{n}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{2j-1}{n}\mathds{1}_{\left[(j-1)/n,j/n\right]}(x).

Each
f_{n}
is piecewise constant, placing uniform mass within each interval
[(j-1)/n,j/n]
. The center panel of
Figure

1
shows
c_{f_{10}}
for the bivariate case. Generally, when
n\geq 2
the piecewise constancy of
f_{n}
manifests itself in
c_{f_{n}}
as
n
pairs of isosceles trapezoids, each perpendicular to the
z
-axis and separated from one another by a distance of
1/n
.

Figure 1:
Bivariate copula densities corresponding to the generators
f_{1}
,
f_{10}
, and
f_{\infty}.

Note that
c_{f_{n}}\to c_{f_{\infty}}
pointwise, where
f_{\infty}(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)=2x
, a special case of the triangular distribution density. By Scheffé’s lemma, the sequence of copulas
C_{f_{n}}
converges weakly to
C_{f_{\infty}}
. The copula density
c_{f_{\infty}}
is shown in the right panel of
Figure

1
.

For examples of more “typical” generators, we consider the
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
distribution for several pairs of parameters. When
\alpha=\beta=3/2
, the density is

f_{\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2}}(x)=\frac{8\sqrt{x(1-x)}}{\pi}.

This density has zeros at 0 and 1, and so the corresponding copula density is zero on hyperplanes of the form
\{\bm{u}\in[0,1]^{d}:\sum_{j}u_{j}=n\}
, where
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
. In the bivariate case, this corresponds to zeros along the line segment
\Delta^{1}
as well as at the two isolated points
\bm{0}
and
\bm{1}
, as shown in the left panel of
Figure

2
. On the other hand, with
\alpha=\beta=1/2
, the univariate density is

f_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}}.

The corresponding copula density is essentially the reciprocal of the previous situation, with singularities along the same line segment and isolated points, as shown in the center panel of
Figure

2
. Finally, we can introduce asymmetry into the copula density by choosing any
\alpha\neq\beta
. For example,
\alpha=1/2
,
\beta=3/2
, yields the univariate density

f_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}}(x)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1-x}{x}}.

and the copula density is shown in the right panel of
Figure

2
.

Figure 2:
Bivariate copula densities corresponding to the generators
f_{\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2}}
,
f_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}
, and
f_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}}
.

Inspection of the plots in
Figures

1
and
2
reveals additional “graphical” properties of our bivariate copula densities, which readily extend to arbitrary dimensions
d\geq 3
. It is apparent from
Equation

4
that the copula density is constant on any hyperplane of the form
\{\bm{u}\in[0,1]^{d}:\sum_{j}u_{j}=r\}
where
r\in(0,d)
, on which the copula density is equal to
f(r\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1)
. Moreover, one can show that the projection of the graph
\{\left(\bm{u},c_{f}(\bm{u})\right):\bm{u}\in(0,1)^{d}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
onto the 2-dimensional subspace
\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:x_{1}=\cdots=x_{d}\}
returns
d
copies of the graph of
f
arranged side-by-side, each compressed horizontally by a factor of
\sqrt{d}
.

3.2
A poorly behaved univariate density

We now construct a density
f^{*}\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
which, in contrast with the examples of
Section

3.1
, is quite pathological. To this end, let
\mathbb{Q}_{1}:=\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)
and
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots
be an arbitrary enumeration of
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
. For each
q\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
, we define densities
f_{q}^{+}
and
f_{q}^{-}
that diverge at
1-q
by

f_{q}^{\pm}(u):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{(\pm(u+q))\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1}},&u\neq 1-q\\
1/2,&u=1-q\\
\end{cases}

(8)

and
f_{q}:=(f_{q}^{+}+f_{q}^{-})/2
. By a simple change of variable, we see that

\int_{0}^{1}f_{q}^{+}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{1-q}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{u+q}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u+\int_{1-q}^{1}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{u+q-1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\int_{q}^{1}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{q}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=1,

(9)

and similarly
\int_{0}^{1}f_{q}^{-}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=1
, so
f_{q}
is a proper density. Now, let weights
w_{q}>0
be given such that
\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}}w_{q}=1
. Valid choices include, for example,
w_{q_{n}}^{(1)}:=6/(\pi^{2}n^{2})
and
w_{q_{n}}^{(2)}:=2^{-n}
. Define

h^{*}:=\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}}w_{q}f_{q}.

The function
h^{*}
is non-negative and measurable, and it has Lebesgue integral equal to 1 over
[0,1]
by Tonelli’s theorem; it is thus a proper density (although it is not Riemann integrable, as we shall see shortly). To provide intuition, several normalized partial sums of
h^{*}
are shown in
Figure

3
. Each
h_{n}^{*}(u)
is constructed by choosing for
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
the
n
rationals evenly spaced within the interval
[1/(n+1),n/(n+1)]
, endpoints included. The weights are chosen as
w_{q_{j}}\propto 1.1^{-\pi(j)}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
for a random permutation
\pi
of
(1,\ldots,n)
.

Figure 3:
From left to right, the normalized partial sums
h_{1}^{*}
,
h_{10}^{*}
and
h_{100}^{*}
.

Witness to the irregularity of the associated copula density is the set of singular points of
h^{*}
, which we investigate here. Consider the set
A=\{x\in[0,1]:h^{*}(x)=\infty\}
. While
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
is clearly contained in
A
, the latter set is, in fact, much larger.

Proposition 6
.

A
is uncountable.

Proof.

Let

B_{n,k}:=\left(1-q_{n},\min\left\{1-q_{n}+\frac{w_{q_{n}}^{2k}}{4},1\right\}\right),\quad B:=\bigcap_{k\geq 1}\bigcup_{n\geq 1}B_{n,k}.

First, suppose for a contradiction that
B
is countable. Let
\epsilon>0
and fix
y\in(0,1)
. For any
n^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1-q_{n^{*}}\in(y-\epsilon,y)
, we have
|y-y^{\prime}|<\epsilon
for every
y^{\prime}\in(1-q_{n^{*}},\min\{1-q_{n^{*}}+\min\{\epsilon,w_{q_{n^{*}}}^{2k}/4\},1\})\subseteq B_{{n^{*}},k}
. It follows that
\cup_{n\geq 1}B_{n,k}
is an open, dense subset of
[0,1]
. Equivalently, its complement
\cap_{n\geq 1}B_{n,k}^{c}
is closed and nowhere dense, and so
B^{c}=\cup_{k\geq 1}\cap_{n\geq 1}B_{n,k}^{c}
is meagre. Now,
B
is also meagre because it can be written as a countable union of singletons. Therefore the union
B\cup B^{c}=[0,1]
is itself meagre, but this contradicts the well known Baire category theorem
(see Theorem 3.6 of
Levy 2012
, for instance)
. Hence
B
is uncountable.

We now show that
B\subseteq A
, from which the result will follow. If
x\in B
, then for all
k\geq 1
there exists some
n(k)\geq 1
such that
x\in B_{n(k),k}
. That is, the sets
D_{k}^{x}:=\{n:x\in B_{n,k}\}
,
k\geq 1
, are all nonempty. Then

2h^{*}(x)>\sum_{n\in\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}D_{k}^{x}}w_{q_{n}}f_{q_{n}}^{+}(x)\geq\sum_{n\in\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}D_{k}^{x}}\frac{w_{q_{n}}}{2\sqrt{(x+q_{n})\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1}}>\sum_{n\in\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}D_{k}^{x}}\frac{w_{q_{n}}}{2\sqrt{w_{q_{n}}^{2}/4}}=\left|\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}D_{k}^{x}\right|=\infty,

and so
x\in A
. To obtain the last equality, suppose for contradiction that the cardinality of the union of the sets
D_{k}^{x}
is finite. Then, since the
D_{k}^{x}
are all nonempty, there must exist an integer
n_{0}\in\limsup_{k}D_{k}^{x}
. This means that for infinitely many integers
k
,

1<x+q_{n_{0}}<1+\frac{w_{q_{n_{0}}}^{2k}}{4},

a contradiction.
∎

This is, of course, not the only way to construct a univariate density which is unbounded at uncountably many points. Given any uncountable set
D\subset[0,1]
of measure zero — of which the Cantor set is the classic example, although unlike
A
, it is nowhere dense in
(0,1)
— one can somewhat artificially define a function on
[0,1]
, such as
h:=1/\mathds{1}_{D^{c}}
, which is infinite on
D
but remains constant at 1 on
D^{c}
. While any such function is a density in
\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
, it produces only the standard uniform distribution upon integration, and hence its use as a generator simply returns
C_{h}=\Pi
. More generally, one can produce a density in
\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
which is well-behaved on
D^{c}
by choosing, say, some continuous
f\in\mathscr{F}_{[0,1]}
and then defining
h:=f/\mathds{1}_{D^{c}}
. The copulas generated by such
h
will not differ from the kinds discussed in
Section

3.1
; their copula densities
c_{h}
will not display divergent behavior anywhere. In contrast, the density of
C_{h^{*}}
is fundamentally irregular, as it
diverges
in any neighborhood: one can find a sequence converging to any point in
[0,1]^{d}
along which
c_{h^{*}}
is finite but
c_{h^{*}}\to\infty
.

To avoid functions taking on the value
\infty
, we define

f^{*}:=h^{*}\mathds{1}_{A^{c}},

which is finite on
[0,1]
but nevertheless unbounded in every neighborhood. These properties carry on directly to the copula density
c_{f^{*}}
. Indeed, since
\bm{u}\mapsto\sum_{j}u_{j}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1
is an open map, for any nonempty open
U\subset[0,1]^{d}
,
\sup_{\bm{u}\in U}c_{f^{*}}(\bm{u})=\sup_{x\in\widetilde{U}}f^{*}(x)=\infty
for some nonempty open
\widetilde{U}\subset[0,1]
. Still, the finiteness of
f^{*}
ensures that
c_{f^{*}}
itself is finite at every point.

4
Discussion

Although properties of the family
\mathscr{C}
such as the periodicity of the densities along line segments make it an unlikely choice for modelling purposes, the existence of this family should serve to illustrate the flexibility of copulas. It is easy to construct copulas (such as the checkerboard copula) whose densities are continuous almost everywhere but whose partial derivatives fail to exist on certain sets. The copula
C_{f^{*}}
, introduced in
Section

3.2
, illustrates the opposite situation: the copula is
(d-1)
-times continuously differentiable everywhere, while its density
c_{f^{*}}
violates virtually every regularity or boundedness principle one might reasonably expect.

The construction of the family
\mathscr{C}
is, of course, not unique. For example, one could replace
Equation

4
with

c_{f,\bm{i}}(\bm{u}):=f\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}(-1)^{i_{j}}u_{j}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1\right)

(10)

for any
\bm{i}:=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, the existing model corresponding to
\bm{i}=\bm{0}
. The resulting copula may no longer be exchangeable and the points at which
c_{f,\bm{i}}
behaves erratically become harder to describe; however, most other smoothness properties (
3
in particular) would still be expected to hold. Moreover, more realistic dependence models might be achieved in the general form of
Equation

10
. For instance, in the bivariate case with
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
, some of the induced copulas will have a Spearman’s
\rho
arbitrarily close to 1, in contrast to the findings of
4
. One can show that in the bivariate case, the class
\mathscr{C}
in its current form is not closed under the
*
-product operation. We conjecture, however, that it would be closed were it extended to all copulas of the form of
Equation

10
.

We have not addressed Kendall’s
\tau
in the bivariate case.
4
provides upper and lower bounds for
\rho_{C_{f}}
which are asymptotically sharp as
f
approaches certain point masses. The lower bound of
\rho_{C}\geq-1
is attained if and only if
\tau_{C}=-1
, because in this case
C
is the countermonotonicity copula
(
Embrechts
et al. 2001
)
. However, from a well known inequality of
Daniels 1950
, the upper bound
\rho_{C_{f}}\leq 1/2
implies only that
\tau_{C_{f}}\leq 2/3
, and it would be interesting to know if this bound is sharp (i.e., if there exists a sequence of univariate densities
\{f_{n}\}
such that
\kappa_{C_{f_{n}}}\to 2/3
). More generally,
4
shows that
\rho_{C_{f}}
is a linear function of the second factorial moment of
X\sim f
, and a similar representation of
\tau_{C_{f}}
in terms of
\mathbb{E}\left[g(X)\right]
for some function
g
would yield interesting insights.
