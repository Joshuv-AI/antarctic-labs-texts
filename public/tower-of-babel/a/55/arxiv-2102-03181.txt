On automorphisms of categories
G. Zhitomirski Department of Mathematics and Statistics Bar-Ilan University, 52900 Ramat Gan, Israel

Abstract
. Let 𝒱 \mathcal{V} be a variety of algebras of some type Ω \Omega . An interest to describing automorphisms of the category Θ 0 ​ ( 𝒱 ) \Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V}) of finitely generated free 𝒱 \mathcal{V} -algebras was inspired in connection with development of universal algebraic geometry founded by B. Plotkin. There are a lot of results on this subject. A common method of getting such results was suggested and applied by B. Plotkin and the author ( [ 7 , 8 ] ). The method is to find all terms in the language of a given variety which determine such Ω \Omega -algebras that are isomorphic to a given Θ 0 ​ ( 𝒱 ) \Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V}) -algebra and have the same underlying set with it. But this method can be applied only to automorphisms which take all objects to isomorphic ones. The aim of the present paper is to suggest another method that is free from the mentioned restriction. This method is based on two main theorems. Let 𝒞 \mathcal{C} be a category supplied with a faithful representative functor to the category of sets. Theorem 1 gives a general description of automorphisms of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} , using a new notion of a quasi-inner automorphism. Theorem 5 shows how to obtain the full description of automorphisms of the category Θ 0 ​ ( 𝒱 ) \Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V}) . The investigation ends with two examples. The first of them shows the preference of our method in a known situation (the variety of all semigroups) and the second one demonstrates obtaining new results (the variety of all modules over arbitrary ring with unit).



1.
INTRODUCTION

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a variety of algebras of some type
\Omega
and
\Theta(\mathcal{V})
be the category of all
\mathcal{V}
-algebras and
their homomorphisms. For an infinite set
X_{0}
, let
\Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V})
denote the full subcategory of
\Theta(\mathcal{V})
which is determined by all free
\mathcal{V}
-algebras over finite subsets of the set
X_{0}
. The problem is to describe all automorphisms of the category
\Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V})
. Motivations for this research can be found in the papers
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
. The most important case is when automorphisms of this category are inner or close to inner in a sense.

An automorphism
\Phi
of a category
\mathcal{C}
is called inner if it is isomorphic to the identity functor
Id^{\mathcal{C}}
in the category of all endofunctors of
\mathcal{C}
. It means that for every object
A
of the given category
there exists an isomorphism
\sigma_{A}:A\to\Phi(A)
such that for every morphism
\mu:A\to B
we have
\Phi(\mu)=\sigma_{B}\circ\mu\circ\sigma_{A}^{-1}
. Thus an automorphism
\Phi
may be inner only in the case when there is an isomorphism between
A
and
\Phi(A)
for every
\mathcal{C}
-object
A
.

As regards the inverse proposition, it was shown in the papers
[
7
,
8
]
that if the objects
\Phi(A)
and
A
are isomorphic for every
\mathcal{C}
-object
A
, then
\Phi
is inner or so called potentially inner. The last one means that
\Phi
is inner in a category obtained by adding to
\mathcal{C}
some new morphisms. More accurately, there exists a family
(s_{A}:A\to\Phi(A))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
of morphisms of an extended category such that for every morphism
\mu:A\to B
of the category
\mathcal{C}
we have
\Phi(\mu)=s_{B}\circ\mu\circ s_{A}^{-1}
. That way, the problem is to describe these new morphisms
s_{A}
. A general method how to do it was suggested in the mentioned papers for categories
\mathcal{C}=\Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V})
where
\mathcal{V}
is a suitable variety. Below is a summary of the essence of this method.

Consider a
\mathcal{C}
-algebra
A
free generated by the set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
of variables, where
n
is greater than arities of all
\Omega
-operators. We can assume that algebras
\Phi(A)
and
A
have the common base set
|A|
and the same free generators. Then for every
k
-ary operation symbol
\omega\in\Omega
we have two
k
-ary operations on
|A|
:
\omega_{A}
and
\omega_{\Phi(A)}
.

It is obvious that every element of the algebra
A
can be considered as a term in the language corresponding to the algebra
\Phi(A)
and vice versa. In other words, every operation
\omega_{A}
is a derived operation in
\Phi(A)
, and vice versa,
every operation
\omega_{\Phi(A)}
is a derived operation in
A
. Thus the mappings
s_{A}:|A|\to|A|
such that
s_{A}(x_{i})=x_{i}
and
s_{A}(\omega_{A}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}))=\omega_{\Phi(A)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}))
for every
\omega\in\Omega
determine the automorphism
\Phi
. The problem is reduced to fined terms
\tilde{\omega}
in the algebra
\Phi(A)
such that the derived algebra
(|A|,(\tilde{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega})
belongs to the variety
\mathcal{V}
.

Some new results were obtained on this way. Particularly, A. Tsurkov successfully applied this method to many-sorted algebras (for example in
[
9
]
). It is known that if a variety
\mathcal{V}
satisfies IBN-property then every automorphism of the category
\Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V})
takes every object to an isomorphic one. But there are varieties without IBN-property. If it is unknown whether
A
and
\Phi(A)
are isomorphic or not, the method described above does not work.

The aim of the current investigation is to fill up this gap. Since an automorphism may be not inner in the general case, we use a new type of automorphisms called the
quasi-inner automorphisms
(Definition
1
), and it turns out that this notion is enough to characterize arbitrary automorphisms. Further reasoning as a matter of fact follows the ideas of the papers
[
7
,
8
]
when
A
and
\Phi(A)
are isomorphic, but instead of the method outlined above, a new method is proposed that to the author’s opinion is more successful.

This method reduces the problem to the case when the underlying set
|A|
of an algebra
A
is a subset of the underlying set of the algebra
\Phi(A)
, and every endomorphism
\mu
of the algebra
A
is the restriction of the endomorphism
\Phi(\mu)
of the algebra
\Phi(A)
to the set
|A|
and every restriction to
|A|
of an endomorphism
\nu
of
\Phi(A)
is an endomorphism
\mu
of
A
such that
\Phi(\mu)=\nu
. That circumstance gives us an opportunity to describe the action of the automorphism
\Phi
.

The main results are formulated in two theorems. Theorem
1
states that every automorphism of
a category
\mathcal{C}
supplied with a forgetful functor
\mathbf{Q}:\mathcal{C}\to\Set
which satisfies two acceptable conditions is potentially quasi-inner. Theorem
5
states that every automorphism
\Phi
of the category
\Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V})
for an arbitrary variety
\mathcal{V}
is the product of two functors
\Phi=\Gamma\circ\Psi
. The first of them is an inner isomorphism
\Psi:\Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V})\to\mathcal{D}
,where
D
is a full subcategory of
\Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V})
which is described. The second functor
\Gamma:\mathcal{D}\to\Theta^{0}(\mathcal{V})
is a so called extension functor, that is,
|A|\subseteq|\Gamma(A)|
for every
\mathcal{C}
-algebra
A
and
\mu\subseteq\Gamma(\mu)
for every
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\mu
.

The last part of the paper contains two examples. The first of them shows the preference of our method in a known situation (the variety of all semigroups) and the second one presents a new result for the variety of all modules over arbitrary ring with unit. As a consequence of the last result we obtain that in the case when the ring does not contain zero divisors all automorphisms are semi-inner.

2.
Quasi-inner automorphisms

We consider only such categories
\mathcal{C}
which are supplied with a faithful functor
\mathbf{Q}:\mathcal{C}\to Set
, where
Set
is the category of all sets. We call
\mathbf{Q}
the forgetful functor as it is accepted.

Definition 1
.

An automorphism
\Phi
of a category
\mathcal{C}
is called inner if there is an isomorphism between
\Phi
and the identity functor
Id^{\mathcal{C}}
in the category of all endofunctors of
\mathcal{C}
. An automorphism
\Phi
is said to be quasi-inner if there is a bimorphism
Id^{\mathcal{C}}\to\Phi
.

This definition means that
\Phi
is inner if for every object
A
of the given category there exists an isomorphism
\sigma_{A}:A\to\Phi(A)
such that for every morphism
\mu:A\to B
the following diagram commutes:

\begin{CD}A@>{\sigma_{A}}>{}>\Phi(A)\\
@V{\mu}V{}V@V{}V{\Phi(\mu)}V\\
B@>{\sigma_{B}}>{}>\Phi(B)\end{CD}

Hence we get that
\Phi(\mu)=\sigma_{B}\circ\mu\circ\sigma_{A}^{-1}
.

As a generalization of this notion,
\Phi
is quasi-inner if for every object
A
of the given category there exists a
bimorphism

\sigma_{A}:A\to\Phi(A)
such that for every morphism
\mu:A\to B
the diagram above is commutative. Hence we have only the following condition
\sigma_{B}\circ\mu=\Phi(\mu)\circ\sigma_{A}
but not the expression for
\Phi(\mu)
like in the case of inner automorphism if the morphism
\sigma_{A}
is not invertible. Nevertheless, we have
\mathbf{Q}(\mu)=(\mathbf{Q}(\sigma_{B}))^{-1}\circ\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\mu))\circ\mathbf{Q}(\sigma_{A})
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Phi
be a quasi-inner automorphism of
\mathcal{C}
and
(\sigma_{A})_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}:Id^{\mathcal{C}}\to\Phi
be a bimorphism. If
\sigma_{B}\circ\mu=\nu\circ\sigma_{A}
for
\mu:A\to B,\;\nu:\Phi(A)\to\Phi(B)
, then
\nu=\Phi(\mu)
.
Hence
\Phi(\mu)
is uniquely determined by the following inclusion:
\mathbf{Q}(\sigma_{B})\circ\mathbf{Q}(\mu)\circ(Q(\sigma_{A}))^{-1}\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\mu))
.

Proof.

It is obvious because
\sigma_{A}
is an epimorphism.
∎

3.
potentially quasi-inner automorphisms

In this section, we assume that every category
\mathcal{C}
under consideration is supplied with a forgetful functor
\mathbf{Q}
such that there exist a
\mathcal{C}
-object
A_{0}
and an element
x_{0}\in\mathbf{Q}(A_{0})
which satisfy the following conditions:

1Q)

\mathbf{Q}
is represented by the pair
(A_{0},x_{0})
, i.e., for every object
A
of
\mathcal{C}
and for every element
a\in\mathbf{Q}(A)
there is exactly one morphism
\alpha:A_{0}\to A
such that
\mathbf{Q}(\alpha)(x_{0})=a
.

2Q)

for every
\mathcal{C}
-object
A
there exists a morphism
\alpha:A\to A_{0}
such that for every element
x\in\mathbf{Q}(A_{0})
we have
x=\mathbf{Q}(\alpha)(a)
for some element
a\in\mathbf{Q}(A)
, in other words,
\mathbf{Q}(\alpha):\mathbf{Q}(A)\to\mathbf{Q}(A_{0})
is surjective.

Some of the simple properties of such categories need to be noted:

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mu:A\to B
be a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism. If
\mathbf{Q}(\mu):\mathbf{Q}(A)\to\mathbf{Q}(B)
is surjective then
\mu
is an epimorphism.

A morphism
\mu:A\to B
is a monomorphism if and only if the map
\mathbf{Q}(\mu):\mathbf{Q}(A)\to\mathbf{Q}(B)
is injective.

Let
\Phi
be an automorphism of the category
\mathcal{C}
. There exists an epimorphism
\eta:A_{0}\to\Phi(A_{0})
. If
\eta
is an isomorphism then
\Phi
is potentially inner.

Proof.

It is obvious.

It is also obvious that if
\mathbf{Q}(\mu)
is injective then
\mu
is a monomorphism. Let
\mu:A\to B
is a monomorphism. Suppose that
\mathbf{Q}(\mu)
is not injective, i. e., there exist elements
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbf{Q}(A)
such that
a_{1}\not=a_{2}
but
\mathbf{Q}(\mu)(a_{1})=\mathbf{Q}(\mu)(a_{2})
. According to property 1Q, there are morphisms
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}:A_{0}\to A
such that
\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{1})(x_{0})=a_{1}
and
\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{2})(x_{0})=a_{2}
. We have
\mathbf{Q}(\mu)(\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{1})(x_{0}))=\mathbf{Q}(\mu)(\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{2})(x_{0}))
and hence
\mu\circ\alpha_{1}=\mu\circ\alpha_{2}
. Since
\mu
is a monomorphism we obtain that
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
which contradicts the supposition
a_{1}\not=a_{2}
.

Consider the object
\Phi^{-1}(A_{0})
. According to the property Q2 there is a morphism
\eta_{0}:\Phi^{-1}(A_{0})\to A_{0}
such that the mapping
\mathbf{Q}(\eta_{0}):\mathbf{Q}(\Phi^{-1}(A_{0}))\to\mathbf{Q}(A_{0})
is surjective. Thus

\eta_{0}:\Phi^{-1}(A_{0})\to A_{0}
is an epimorphism. Hence
\eta=\Phi(\eta_{0}):A_{0}\to\Phi(A_{0})
is an epimorphism too. If
\eta
is an isomorphism, then
\Phi
is potentially inner according to Theorem 1 in
[
7
]
.
∎

Definition 2
.

The surjective morphism
\eta_{0}:\Phi^{-1}(A_{0})\to A_{0}
and the epimorphism
\eta:A_{0}\to\Phi(A_{0})
introduced by the proof of Lemma
2
are fixed as main epimorphisms connected with
\Phi
and are denoted by
\eta_{0}^{\Phi}
and
\eta^{\Phi}
correspondingly. If
A_{0}=\Phi(A_{0})
we assume that
\eta^{\Phi}=\eta_{0}^{\Phi}=1_{A_{0}}
.

Definition 3
.

Let
A,B\in Ob\mathcal{C}
. A mapping
s:\mathbf{Q}(A)\to Q(B)
is called
\mathcal{C}
-bijection if
s
is injective and for any two
\mathcal{C}
-morphisms
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}:B\to C
the equality
\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{1})\circ s=\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{2})\circ s
implies
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
. Roughly speaking the mapping
s
has the epimorphism property only in relation to
\mathcal{C}
-morphisms.

It is obvious that on the one hand the notion of
\mathcal{C}
-bijection is a generalization of the notion of bijection and, on the other
hand, if for a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\sigma:A\to B
the mapping
\mathbf{Q}(\sigma):\mathbf{Q}(A)\to Q(B)
is a
\mathcal{C}
-bijection then
\sigma:A\to B
is a bimorphism.

Definition 4
.

An automorphism
\Phi
of a category
\mathcal{C}
is called potentially quasi-inner if there exists a family of
\mathcal{C}
-bijections
(s_{A}:\mathbf{Q}(A)\to\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(A)))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
such that for every
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\mu:A\to B
the following diagram commutes:

\begin{CD}\mathbf{Q}(A)@>{s_{A}}>{}>\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(A))\\
@V{\mathbf{Q}(\mu)}V{}V@V{}V{\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\mu))}V\\
\mathbf{Q}(B)@>{s_{B}}>{}>\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(B))\end{CD}

It is easy to see that for potentially quasi-inner automorphisms the fact analogous toLemma
1
is true too, i. e., if
s_{B}\circ\mathbf{Q}(\mu)=\mathbf{Q}(\nu)\circ s_{A}
for some morphism
\nu:\Phi(A)\to\Phi(B)
then
\nu=\Phi(\mu)
. Thus the morphism
\Phi(\mu)
is uniquely determined by the commutativity of the diagram above.

Definition 5
.

Let
A
be a
\mathcal{C}
-object and
a\in\mathbf{Q}(A)
. We denote by
\alpha_{a}^{A}
the unique morphism
A_{0}\to A
such that

\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{a}^{A})(x_{0})=a
.

Now we are ready to prove the first of two main theorems.

Theorem 1
.

Every automorphism of a category which satisfies conditions 1Q and 2Q is potentially quasi-inner.

Proof.

Let
\Phi
be an automorphism of a category
\mathcal{C}
and
A
be a
\mathcal{C}
-object. Define the mapping
s_{A}:\mathbf{Q}(A)\to\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(A))
by the formula:

s_{A}(a)=\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\alpha_{a}^{A})\circ\eta)(x_{0})

(1)

for every
a\in A
. Here
\eta=\eta^{\Phi}
according to Definition
2
.

Start to check that
s_{A}
is a
\mathcal{C}
-bijection.

1). Let
s_{A}(u)=s_{A}(v)
for
u,v\in\mathbf{Q}(A)
. Then
\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\alpha_{u}^{A})\circ\eta)(x_{0})=\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\alpha_{v}^{A})\circ\eta)(x_{0})
. Hence
\Phi(\alpha_{u}^{A})\circ\eta=\Phi(\alpha_{v}^{A})\circ\eta
. Since
\eta
is a
\mathcal{C}
-epimorphism we obtain that
\Phi(\alpha_{u}^{A})=\Phi(\alpha_{v}^{A})
which implies
\alpha_{u}^{A}=\alpha_{v}^{A}
, i. e.,
u=v
. Hence
s_{A}
is injective.

2).
Let
\mathbf{Q}(\gamma)\circ s_{A}=\mathbf{Q}(\delta)\circ s_{A}
for some
\mathcal{C}
-morphisms
\gamma,\delta:\Phi(A)\to B
. Using
1
we have
for all
a\in\mathbf{Q}(A)

\mathbf{Q}(\gamma)\circ\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\alpha_{a}^{A})\circ\eta)(x_{0})=\mathbf{Q}(\delta)\circ\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\alpha_{a}^{A})\circ\eta)(x_{0})

and hence

\gamma\circ\Phi(\alpha_{a}^{A})\circ\eta=\delta\circ\Phi(\alpha_{a}^{A})\circ\eta

for all
a\in\mathbf{Q}(A)
.

Since
\eta
is an epimorphism we obtain
\gamma\circ\Phi(\alpha_{a}^{A})=\delta\circ\Phi(\alpha_{a}^{A})
. Applying
\Phi^{-1}
to both sides of this equation we get that
\Phi^{-1}(\gamma)\circ\alpha_{a}^{A}=\Phi^{-1}(\delta)\circ\alpha_{a}^{A}
for all
a\in\mathbf{Q}(A)
. Hence
\Phi^{-1}(\gamma)=\Phi^{-1}(\delta)
which gives
\gamma=\delta
. Thus
s_{A}
is
\mathcal{C}
-bijective.

It remains to check that the corresponding square in Definition
4
is commutative. According to
1
we have for every
a\in\mathbf{Q}(A)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\mu))\circ s_{A}(a)=\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\mu))\circ\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\alpha_{a}^{A})\circ\eta)(x_{0})=

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\mu)\circ\Phi(\alpha_{a}^{A})\circ\eta)(x_{0})=\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\mu\circ\alpha_{a}^{A})\circ\eta)(x_{0})=

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\alpha_{\mathbf{Q}(\mu)(a)}^{B})\circ\eta)(x_{0})=s_{B}(\mathbf{Q}(\mu)(a))=s_{B}\circ\mathbf{Q}(\mu)(a).

Thus
\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\mu))\circ s_{A}=s_{B}\circ\mathbf{Q}(\mu)
.
∎

Definition 6
.

The family of
\mathcal{C}
-bijections
(s_{A}:\mathbf{Q}(A)\to\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(A)))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
defined by
1
we call the
main function
corresponding to
\Phi
.

It is easy to see that this notion is a generalization of the notion of the main function defined in
[
7
]
. In fact, if the main epimorphism
\eta:A_{0}\to\Phi(A_{0})
is an isomorphism we get the same formula for
s_{A}
(see
[
7
]
formula 2.2).

Of course the main function is not the unique function
(\mathbf{Q}(A)\to\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(A)))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
which makes the squares in Definition
4
commutative. For example, the main function for the identity functor is equal (according to
1
and Definition
2
) to
s_{A}(a)=\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{a}^{A}\circ 1_{A_{0}})(x_{0})=a
, that is,
s_{A}=\mathbf{Q}(1_{A})
. Hence in that case we have
s_{B}\circ\mathbf{Q}(\mu)=\mathbf{Q}(\mu)\circ s_{A}
for every morphism
\mu:A\to B
.
Having in mind this property we use the following notion (see
[
7
,
8
]
).

Definition 7
.

The family
(c_{A})_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
of
\mathcal{C}
-bijective mappings
c_{A}:\mathbf{Q}(A)\to\mathbf{Q}(A)
having the following property:
c_{B}\circ\mathbf{Q}(\mu)=\mathbf{Q}(\mu)\circ c_{A}
for every morphism
\mu:A\to B
is called a central function. In other words, a function
A\to c_{A}
is a central function if it determines the identity automorphism of the category
\mathcal{C}
.

Let
\Phi
be an automorphism of
\mathcal{C}
. Theorem
1
states that both
\Phi
and
\Phi^{-1}
are potentially quasi-inner.
Therefore we have two main functions
(s^{\Phi}_{A}:A\to\Phi(A))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
and
(s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{A}:A\to\Phi^{-1}(A))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
of
\mathcal{C}
-bijections and two main epimorphisms
\eta^{\Phi}:A_{0}\to\Phi(A_{0})
and
\eta^{\Phi^{-1}}:A_{0}\to\Phi^{-1}(A_{0})
.

Although in general case, the main functions do not satisfy many conditions which are valid in the case
\eta
is an isomorphism, they have some similar properties. In particular, we get the following commutative diagram for every morphism
\mu:A\to B
:

\begin{CD}\mathbf{Q}(A)@>{s^{\Phi}_{A}}>{}>\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(A))@>{s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{\Phi(A)}}>{}>\mathbf{Q}(A)\\
@V{\mathbf{Q}(\mu)}V{}V@V{}V{\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(\mu))}V@V{}V{\mathbf{Q}(\mu)}V\\
\mathbf{Q}(B)@>{s^{\Phi}_{B}}>{}>\mathbf{Q}(\Phi(B))@>{s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{\Phi(B)}}>{}>\mathbf{Q}(B)\end{CD}

We see that the family of mappings
c_{A}^{\Phi}=s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{\Phi(A)}\circ s^{\Phi}_{A}:\mathbf{Q}(A)\to\mathbf{Q}(A)
is a central function. This central function will be called the
main central function
for the automorphism
\Phi
.

It is obvious that for every function
A\mapsto s_{A}
that determines an automorphism
\Phi
and any two central functions
A\mapsto e_{A}
and
A\mapsto d_{A}
the function
A\mapsto(e_{\Phi(A)}\circ s_{A}\circ d_{A})
determines the same automorphism. The converse proposition is also true.

Proposition 1
.

Let a function
A\mapsto t_{A}
, where
A\in Ob\mathcal{C}
, determines an automorphism
\Phi
of a category
\mathcal{C}
. Then there exists a central function
A\mapsto d_{A}
such that
t_{A}=(c_{\Phi(A)}^{\Phi^{-1}})^{-1}\circ s^{\Phi}_{A}\circ d_{A}
for all
\mathcal{C}
-objects
A
.

Proof.

Let
d_{A}=s_{\Phi(A)}^{\Phi^{-1}}\circ t_{A}
. It is obvious that the function
A\mapsto d_{A}
is central.
We get
s_{\Phi(A)}^{\Phi}\circ s_{\Phi(A)}^{\Phi^{-1}}\circ t_{A}=s_{\Phi(A)}^{\Phi}\circ d_{A}
.
Since
s_{\Phi(A)}^{\Phi}\circ s_{\Phi(A)}^{\Phi^{-1}}=c_{\Phi(A)}^{\Phi^{-1}}
is injective, we get

t_{A}=(c_{\Phi(A)}^{\Phi^{-1}})^{-1}\circ s^{\Phi}_{A}\circ d_{A}

∎

4.
The categories of free universal algebras

From now until the end of the article we deal with an arbitrary variety
\mathcal{V}
of universal algebras of some signature
\Omega
and with the corresponding category
\Theta(\mathcal{V})^{0}
of all finitely generated
\mathcal{V}
-algebras and their homomorphisms. This category and all its subcategories are supplied with usual forgetful functor
\mathbf{Q}
that assigns to every algebra
A
its underlying set
|A|
and to every homomorphism
A\to B
the corresponding mapping
|A|\to|B|
. If there are no misunderstanding we denote an algebra by the same letter as its underlying set and do the same for homomorphisms.

Let
A_{0}
be a monogenic algebra free generated by an element
x_{0}
. We consider such full subcategories of
\Theta(\mathcal{V})^{0}
that contain the object
A_{0}
. It is obvious that every such category satisfies the conditions 1Q and 2Q formulated in Section
3
because the pair
(A_{0},x_{0})
represents the forgetful functor
\mathbf{Q}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a full subcategory of
\Theta(\mathcal{V})^{0}
and
A_{0}
be an object of
\mathcal{C}
. Let
\Phi
be an automorphism of
\mathcal{C}
. Theorem
1
states that both
\Phi
and
\Phi^{-1}
are potentially quasi-inner.
Therefore we have two main functions
(s^{\Phi}_{A}:A\to\Phi(A))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
and
(s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{A}:A\to\Phi^{-1}(A))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
of
\mathcal{C}

Full description of automorphisms of categories of free algebras.

The object of this section is to give a description of automorphisms of arbitrary categories which are subcategories of the category
\Theta^{0}({\mathcal{V}})
and which contain the object

. To this end, we select two kinds of functors.
Because of the fact that we may have two different algebraic structures on the same set it makes sense to use different notations for an algebra and for its underlying set, namely,
A
and
|A|
correspondingly.

Definition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be full subcategories of the category
\Theta^{0}({\mathcal{V}})
.

1. An isomorphism
\Psi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is called inner if there is a family of isomorphisms
(\sigma_{A}:A\to\Psi(A))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}
such that
\Psi(\mu)=\sigma_{B}\circ\mu\circ\sigma_{A}^{-1}
for every morphism
\mu:A\to B
of the category
\mathcal{C}
. That is,
\Psi
is isomorphic to the embedding functor
Id_{\mathcal{C}}^{\Theta^{0}({\mathcal{V}})}:\mathcal{C}\to\Theta^{0}({\mathcal{V}})
.

2. An injective functor
\Gamma:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is called an extension functor if

(i)
|A|\subseteq|\Gamma(A)|
for every
\mathcal{C}
-object
A
,

(ii)
\mu\subseteq\Gamma(\mu)
for every
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\mu
,

(iii) if
\nu:\Gamma(A)\to\Gamma(B)
,
\mu:A\to B
and
\mu\subseteq\nu
then
\nu=\Gamma(\mu)
for any
\mathcal{C}
-objects
A,B
and morphisms
\mu,\nu
.

Below we show that any automorphism of a category
\mathcal{C}
under consideration is a product of two functors the first of which is an inner isomorphism and the second one is an extension functor.

Let
\Phi
be an automorphism of a given category
\mathcal{C}
. We follow an idea used in
[
7
,
8
]
. Since

and

are
\mathcal{C}
-bijections they determine algebraic structures on the sets
\Im s^{\Phi}_{A}=s^{\Phi}_{A}|A|)\subseteq|\Phi(A)|
and
\Im s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{A}=s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{A}(|A|)\subseteq|\Phi^{-1}(A)|
correspondingly. Let
\omega
be a symbol of a
k
-ary operation and
\omega_{A}
be the corresponding operation of
A
. Define new
\omega
-operations on
\Im s^{\Phi}_{A}
and
\Im s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{A}
by setting for all

\omega_{A}^{*}(s^{\Phi}_{A}(a_{1}),\dots,s^{\Phi}_{A}(a_{k}))=s^{\Phi}_{A}(\omega_{A}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})),

\omega_{A}^{\#}(s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{A}(a_{1}),\dots,s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{A}(a_{k}))=s^{\Phi^{-1}}_{A}(\omega_{A}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})).

The set

supplied with the operations

for all

is an

Theorem 3

Let
\mu:\Phi(A)\to\Phi(B)
be a homomorphism. The restriction of

on

is a homomorphism

. Backwards, any homomorphism

Proof.

Let

. We have

. Then

\mu(s^{\Phi}_{A}(A))\subseteq s^{\Phi}_{B}(B)

\mu

Backwards, let

and
\nu\subseteq\mu
. The uniqueness of such homomorphism follows from Proposition

Corollary 1
.

The last theorem shows that the process of describing of an automorphism
\Phi
of a category
\mathcal{C}
under consideration is reduced to the following steps:

(1)

We view the elements of
A_{0}
and find the general form of the term
{\bf t}(x_{0})\in|A_{0}|
. Then
we describe the subset
s_{A}(|A|)
of an algebra
\Phi(A)
according to the formula
s_{A}(|A|)=\{{\bf t}(u):u\in|\Phi(A)|\}
. In the case
{\bf t}(x_{0})=x_{0}
this set is equal to the underlying set of
\Phi(A)
.

(2)

In order to describe operations of
A^{*}
, we use the fact that the restriction of any endomorphism of
\Phi(A)
to
s_{A}(|A|)
must be an endomorphism of the algebra
A^{*}
, and vice versa, any endomorphism of
A^{*}
could be extended up to an endomorphism of
\Phi(A)
which is uniquely determined.

(3)

We use the fact that the correspondence described above is an isomorphism between semigroups
\End(A^{*})
and
\End(\Phi(A))
.

If the requirements of these steps are fulfilled, it remains to describe the kind of embedding of
A^{*}
in
\Phi(A)
, which may be an isomorphism or some new kind of a correspondence, for example, a mirror homomorphism or a screw-homomorphism.

and

. Thus

. Let

denote the length of the word

.

Let

be an element of a basis of

, then there exists an endomorphism

of

such that

. Let the endomorphism

of

are isomorphic. We know (Section

.
Therefore
(x_{1},x_{2})
is the common basis of semigroups

which are isomorphic.

Since

\varphi(x_{1})=x_{1},\varphi(x_{2})=x_{2}

\Phi

\gamma(rx_{1}+^{*}rx_{2})=\gamma(rx_{1})+^{*}\gamma(rx_{2})=r\gamma(x_{1})+^{*}r\gamma(x_{2})=rx_{1}+^{*}0=rx_{1}.

On the other hand,
\gamma(ix_{1}+jx_{2})=ix_{1}

and hence

As a result we obtain

On the other other hand we get

Since

M

r\alpha(k_{1}k_{2})=r(\alpha(k_{1})\alpha(k_{2})).

\mathcal{C}

For every automorphism
\Phi

the main function
(s_{A}:A\to\Phi(A))_{A\in Ob\mathcal{C}}

(2) every

(3) there exist a function

(4) the function

Consider the case when the ring
R
