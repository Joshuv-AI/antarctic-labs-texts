Upper bounds for the rank of powers of quadrics
Cosimo Flavi

Abstract
. We establish an upper bound for the rank of every power of an arbitrary quadratic form. Specifically, for any s ∈ ℕ s\in\mathbb{N} , we prove that the s s -th power of a quadratic form of rank n n grows as n s n^{s} . Furthermore, we demonstrate that its rank is subgeneric for all n > ( 2 ​ s − 1 ) 2 n>(2s-1)^{2} .

Introduction

Quadratic forms are common objects in many branches of mathematics.
The general theory of quadratic forms over the rationals and rational integers dates back to the late 19
th
century and it was first introduced by H. Minkowski (see
[
22
]
).
A simple and well-known fact is that any quadratic form is equivalent to the form

q_{n}=x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
(see, e.g.
[
26
]
*chapter 4). Then, for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
q_{n}^{s}
is a
2s
-degree form which is invariant under the action of the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

We recall that, for any homogeneous polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
, a
Waring decomposition
, or simply
decomposition
, of
f
of
size

r
is
an expression of
f
as a sum of
d
-th powers of
r
different linear forms
l_{1},\dots,l_{r}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, which can be viewed as points in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, that is,

f=\sum_{j=1}^{r}l_{j}^{d}.

The
Waring rank
(or simply
rank
) of
f
, denoted by
\rk f
, is the smallest natural number
r
such that there exists a decomposition of
f
of size
r
. Currently, obtaining the rank of a given polynomial still remains a challenging problem and there is no general efficient method to solve it, except for the special case of two variables, first analyzed in
[
29
]
and completely solved in
[
8
]
.
Nevertheless, many partial results and methods have been developed over the years. For a more detailed overview of Waring decompositions, see for example
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
19
,
18
]
, where many applications are also presented, such as telecommunications in electrical engineering (see e.g.
[
7
]
and
[
9
]
) or cumulant tensors in statistics (see e.g.
[
20
]
).

The problem of determining the rank of a generic form was solved by J. Alexander and A. Hirschowitz in
[
1
]
, who proved that, except for a few cases of degree
2
,
3
, and
4
,
there exists a Zariski open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}
such that, for every
f\in\Omega
,

\rk f=\biggl\lceil\frac{1}{n}\binom{d+n-1}{d}\biggr\rceil\sim n^{d-1}.

More recently, M. C. Brambilla and G. Ottaviani provided a shorter version of the proof in
[
4
]
, which we refer to for the details.

In the present paper, we focus on the decompositions and Waring rank of powers of quadratic forms.
In general, determining the rank of
q_{n}^{s}
is a hard problem. While in the case of binary forms the problem is quite simple and completely solved (
[
25
]
*Theorem 9.5), we cannot say the same for the general case in more variables, about which there is not much information in the literature.
However, we focus on the asymptotic behavior of the rank of
q_{n}^{s}
, which grows as
n^{s}
, with
n\to+\infty
.

A particularly remarkable fact about quadratic forms is their invariance under the action of orthogonal groups. In particular, this implies that any decomposition of the form
q_{n}^{s}
has an
n
-dimensional orbit under the action of the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.
The most complete study of this subject so far has been given by B. Reznick, who in
[
25
]
provides a precise survey of both classical and more original results over the field
\mathbb{R}
. In particular, B. Reznick concentrates in his notes on real Waring decompositions, which he calls
representations
.
The author also lists several applications of decompositions of powers of quadratic forms in
[
25
]
*Section 8, including their use in number theory for studying the Waring problem and in functional analysis.
The quadratic form
q_{n}
, in terms of differential operators, is exactly the well-known Laplace operator

\mathop{}\!\Delta=\frac{\mathnormal{\partial}^{\mskip 1.0mu2}}{\mathnormal{\partial}x_{1}^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\mathnormal{\partial}^{\mskip 1.0mu2}}{\mathnormal{\partial}x_{n}^{2}},

which also plays an important role also in mathematical analysis.
From an algebraic point of view, the equality

S^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor}q_{n}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{n,d-2j},

where,
\mathcal{H}_{n,d-2j}
denotes the space of homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
d-2j
in
n
variables, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
, provides a decomposition of great relevance. This elegant formula, analyzed in detail by R. Goodman and N. R. Wallach in
[
14
]
*Corollary 5.2.5, gains much importance by considering its invariance under the action of the complex orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

In
section 2
, we present some of the most important decompositions analyzed by B. Reznick, focusing on closed formulas that provide a family of decompositions depending on the number of variables. In general, these formulas do not correspond to minimal decompositions, but can provide insights into how the rank grows as the number of variables tends to infinity. We also examine the cases in which we can establish that the rank of
q_{n}^{s}
is subgeneric. A first example is given by the formula

(1.1)

6q_{n}^{2}=\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}(x_{i_{1}}\pm x_{i_{2}})^{4}+2(4-n)\sum_{i_{1}}x_{i_{1}}^{4},

which is a decomposition of size
n^{2}
, for
n\neq 4
, presented by B. Reznick in
[
25
]
*formula (8.33). As we will see in
section 2
, this formula does not provide minimal decompositions for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. However, it exhibits an elegant pattern and a structure that is invariant under the action of the permutation group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Furthermore, it yields subgeneric decompositions for
n>17
.
This formula has already been taken into consideration by J. Buczyński, K. Han, M. Mella, and Z. Teitler in
[
5
]
*section 4.5, where they provide a similar decomposition for the exponent
3
. The latter follows the same pattern of points (cf.
section 2
). It is given by the formula

(1.2)

60q_{n}^{3}=\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}}(x_{i_{1}}\pm x_{i_{2}}\pm x_{i_{3}})^{6}+2(5-n)\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}(x_{i_{1}}\pm x_{i_{2}})^{6}+2(n^{2}-9n+38)\sum_{i_{1}}x_{i_{1}}^{6}.

We generalize this pattern in
section 3
, providing other formulas for higher powers. Unfortunately, describing the pattern is not as easy as one might expect, since linear forms involving only
1
and
-1
as coefficients are not sufficient to cover the coefficients of all monomials of degree
2s
.
To obtain a general formula for any
s
-th power, it is necessary to consider other types of linear forms. In order to do this, we need to recall the classical notion of
k
-partition function for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. This function, denoted by
\p_{k}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, associates every natural number
s
with the number of partitions of
s
into exactly
k
parts. Based on this, we provide an upper bound for the rank of
q_{n}^{s}
, which is summarized in the following theorem, which is proved in
section 4
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
n,s\in\mathbb{N}

(1.3)

\rk(q_{n}^{s})\leq 2^{s-1}\binom{n}{s}+2^{s-2}\binom{n}{s-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{s-2}2^{k-1}k!\p_{k}(s)\binom{n}{k}.

In particular, for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the rank of
q_{n}^{s}
grows at most as
n^{s}
.

Given any
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the generic rank of forms of degree
2s
grows as
n^{2s-1}
when
n\to+\infty
. Hence, by the theorem of J. Alexander and A. Hirschowitz (
[
1
]
) mentioned above, the last theorem guarantees that for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the rank can be generic or supergeneric only for a finite number of possible values of
n
.

For any form
f
, the
border rank
of
f
, denoted by
\brk f
, is the smallest natural number
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f
is in the Zariski closure of a set of homogeneous polynomials having rank equal to or less than
r
.

Thanks to a result due to B. Reznick in
[
25
]
(see
section 2
), we know that

(1.4)

\rk(q_{n}^{s})\geq\brk(q_{n}^{s})\geq\binom{s+n-1}{s}.

Putting together
section 1
and inequality (
1.4
), we have the following result, which is also proved in
section 4
.

Corollary 1.4
.

For every
s\in\mathbb{N}
,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\log_{n}\bigl(\rk(q_{n}^{s})\bigr)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\log_{n}\bigl(\brk(q_{n}^{s})\bigr)=s.

This fact agrees perfectly with the value of the border rank determined in
[
10
]
*Theorem 4.5 for ternary non-degenerate quadratic forms, which turns out to be equal to the rank of the middle catalecticant matrix, that is,

\brk(q_{3}^{s})=\binom{s+2}{2}.

In
section 5
, we provide an estimate for the maximum value
n_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
for which the rank of
q_{n}^{s}
is subgeneric for every
n>n_{s}
.
We state this in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every
s\in\mathbb{N}
, whenever

n>(2s-1)^{2}
,

\rk(q_{n}^{s})<\frac{1}{n}\binom{2s+n-1}{2s}.

In particular, the rank of
q_{n}^{s}
is subgeneric, whenever

n>(2s-1)^{2}
.

This result partially solves the problem posed by J. Buczyński, K. Han, M. Mella, and Z. Teitler in
[
5
]
*section 4.5, who asked for the values of
n
and
s
for which the rank of
q_{n}^{s}
is subgeneric. Moreover, the authors provide an analysis
on the maximal rank locus
W_{\mathrm{max},d}
, that is, the set of homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
having maximum rank. In particular, they prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
5
]
*Theorem 4.1)
.

Let
V
be a finite dimensional vector space with
\dim V=n
such that
n\geq 3
. If
W
is an irreducible component of the rank locus
W_{\mathrm{max},d}
, then

\dim(W)\geq\binom{n+1}{2}-1.

Moreover, if

\dim(W)=\binom{n+1}{2}-1,

then
d
is even, and
W
is the set of all the
\bigl(d/2\bigr)
-th powers of quadrics.

With the result of
section 1
, we improve this last statement.
By
section 1
, we can state, in particular, that the second condition of
section 1
does not hold for
n>(2s-1)^{2}
.

Corollary 1.4
.

For every
s\in\mathbb{N}
and for every
n\geq 3
, if
n>(2s-1)^{2}
and
W
is an irreducible component of
W_{\mathrm{max},2s}
, then

\dim(W)\geq\binom{n+1}{2}.

Powers of quadrics and classical decompositions

Thorough the paper, we will use the notation

\sum(a_{1}x_{1}\pm\cdots\pm a_{n}x_{n})^{m}

to denote a summation of all the
2^{n-1}
possible choices of the coefficients
+1
and
-1
, multiplying
a_{2},\dots,a_{n}
. In other words,

\sum(a_{1}x_{1}\pm\cdots\pm a_{n}x_{n})^{m}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{i}\in\{0,1\}\\
i=2,\dots,n\end{subarray}}\bigl(a_{1}x_{1}+(-1)^{j_{2}}a_{2}x_{2}+\cdots+(-1)^{j_{n}}a_{n}x_{n}\bigr)^{m},

where
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We will also use the notation

\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}(a_{1}x_{i_{1}}\pm\cdots\pm a_{k}x_{i_{k}})^{m}

to denote a summation over any choice of
k
variables
x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{k}}
such that

1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n
,
where
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. In the case where
k>n
, we assume the terms
x_{i_{n+1}},\dots,x_{i_{k}}
to be
0
, so that in this case, we have

\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}(a_{1}x_{i_{1}}\pm\cdots\pm a_{k}x_{i_{k}})^{m}=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}}(a_{1}x_{i_{1}}\pm\cdots\pm a_{k}x_{i_{n}})^{m}.

Thanks to the catalecticant map, it is possible to establish a general inequality that provides a well-known lower bound for the Waring rank, related to catalecticant matrices and apolarity (see

We denote by

In particular, B. Reznick shows that all the catalecticant matrices of

[

[

and denoted by

.
Therefore, as a direct consequence, we have that the
k
-th component of
(q_{n}^{s})^{\perp}
is equal to
0
for every

, and hence, in particular,
\Cat_{q_{n}^{s}}^{s}
has maximum rank. Thus, we immediately obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1

(B. Reznick)
.

For every

,

\rk(q_{n}^{s})\geq\brk(q_{n}^{s})\geq\rk\bigl(\Cat_{q_{n}^{s}}^{s}\bigr)=\binom{s+n-1}{s}.

Definition 2.4

For every

\mathcal{P}(s)=\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}\mathcal{P}_{j}(s).

Given a natural number

The partition function
\p(s)
has always been of great importance in several branches of mathematics. Although no closed formulas are known to describe it, there are many asymptotic approximations and also many recurrence formulas providing the exact value of
\p(s)

. The first estimate of

was provided in 1918 by G. H. Hardy and S. Ramanujan in
[

\p(s)\sim\frac{1}{4s\sqrt{3}}\mathrm{e}^{\uppi\sqrt{\frac{2s}{3}}},

which means that it grows as an exponential function of the square root of

. To get more details on the partition function
\p(s)

[

]

[

]
. For what concerns the

In
[

]

, from which we obtain the inequality

{\p}_{k}(s)\leq\frac{1}{2}\binom{s-1}{k-1}+\frac{1}{2}\leq\binom{s-1}{k-1}.

Another tighter upper bound is given by A. T. Oruç in
[

]
*Corollary 1, corresponding to the formula

\p_{k}(s)\leq\frac{5.44}{s-k}\mathrm{e}^{\uppi\sqrt{\frac{2(s-k)}{3}}}.

The variables
x_{t_{s_{1}}},\cdots,x_{t_{s_{\lambda}}}
appear among the variables
x_{t_{1}},\dots,x_{t_{k}}
a number of times equal to the binomial coefficient

\binom{n-\lambda}{k-\lambda}.

Thus, we can remove the summation depending on
s_{1},\dots,s_{\lambda}
and write

\displaystyle f_{k,\mathbf{a}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\lambda}\\
\lvert\mathbf{m}\rvert=s\end{subarray}}\sum_{t_{1}<\cdots<t_{\lambda}}2^{k-1}\binom{n-\lambda}{k-\lambda}\binom{2s}{2m_{1},\dots,2m_{\lambda}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{k})_{\mathbf{a}}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\sigma(i)}^{2m_{i}}\biggr)\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}x_{t_{i}}^{2m_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\lambda}\\
\lvert\mathbf{m}\rvert=s\end{subarray}}2^{k-1}\binom{n-\lambda}{k-\lambda}\binom{2s}{2m_{1},\dots,2m_{\lambda}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{k})_{\mathbf{a}}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\sigma(i)}^{2m_{i}}\biggr)\sum_{t_{1}<\cdots<t_{\lambda}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}x_{t_{i}}^{2m_{i}}.

Finally, we can gather the multi-indices representing the same partition of order
\lambda
of the number
k
. To do this, it is sufficient to consider an element
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{\lambda})
, such that

m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{\lambda}>0
,
and its orbit under the action of the permutation group
\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}
.
We can decompose the polynomial
f_{k,\mathbf{a}}
into more summands by distinguishing the order of each partition of
s
. Hence, we get

f_{k,\mathbf{a}}=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(s)}\sum_{\upgamma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}}\frac{2^{k-1}}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda})_{\mathbf{m}}\rvert}\binom{n-\lambda}{k-\lambda}\binom{2s}{2m_{1},\dots,2m_{\lambda}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{k})_{\mathbf{a}}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\sigma(i)}^{2m_{\gamma(i)}}\biggr)\sum_{t_{1}<\cdots<t_{\lambda}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}x_{t_{i}}^{2m_{\gamma(i)}}.

Considering the summation

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\sigma(i)}^{2m_{\gamma(i)}},

since the permutation
\gamma
just permutes the elements
1,\dots,\lambda
, it
varies among all the possible choices of the elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
and, in particular, it is invariant under the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}
.
Therefore, we have

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\sigma(i)}^{2m_{\gamma(i)}}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\sigma\gamma^{-1}(i)}^{2m_{i}}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\sigma(i)}^{2m_{i}}.

and, by formula (
2.7
), we obtain

\displaystyle f_{k,\mathbf{a}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(s)}\frac{2^{k-1}}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda})_{\mathbf{m}}\rvert}\binom{n-\lambda}{k-\lambda}\binom{2s}{2m_{1},\dots,2m_{\lambda}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{k})_{\mathbf{a}}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\sigma(i)}^{2m_{i}}\biggr)\sum_{\gamma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}}\sum_{t_{1}<\cdots<t_{\lambda}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}x_{t_{i}}^{2m_{\gamma(i)}}

(4.1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(s)}2^{k-1}\binom{n-\lambda}{k-\lambda}\binom{2s}{2m_{1},\dots,2m_{\lambda}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{k})_{\mathbf{a}}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\sigma(i)}^{2m_{i}}\biggr)M_{2\mathbf{m}}.

Thus, we have written the polynomial
f_{k,\mathbf{a}}
as a linear combination of those given by the sum of monomials belonging to the same permutation class. Since these are the same ones appearing in the development of the form
q_{n}^{s}
in formula (
2
), we need to satisfy exactly
\p(s)
conditions.

We establish these conditions by defining a polynomial
f_{k,\mathbf{a}_{j}}
for every
j=1,\dots,\p_{k}(s)
and repeating the same process for every
k=1,\dots,s
. This gives us a number of equations equal to

\p(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\p_{k}(s).

In other words, we have to guarantee the existence, for every
k=1,\dots,s
, of a set of points

\mathcal{A}_{k}=\{\mathbf{a}_{k,1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{k,\p_{k}(s)}\},

such that

(4.2)

q_{n}^{s}=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{\p_{k}(s)}c_{k,j}f_{k,\mathbf{a}_{k,j}},

for some coefficients
c_{k,j}\in\mathbb{C}
.
To find these points, we denote each coordinate of the points by considering
\mathbf{a}_{k,j}=(a_{k,j,1},\dots,a_{k,j,k})

for every
j=1,\dots,\p_{k}(s)
.
Formula (
2
) tells us that

q_{n}^{s}=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(s)}\binom{s}{m_{1},\dots,m_{k}}M_{2\mathbf{m}}.

Hence, for every
k=1,\dots,s
, we combine formulas (
2
), (
4
) and (
4.2
), to obtain a linear equation for each partition
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(s)
. This equation is obtained by equalizing the coefficients of the polynomial

, leading to

(4.3)

\sum_{\lambda=k}^{s}\sum_{j_{\lambda}=1}^{\p_{\lambda}(s)}c_{\lambda,j_{\lambda}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{k})_{\mathbf{a}_{\lambda,j_{\lambda}}}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}}\prod_{i=1}^{\lambda}a_{\lambda,j_{\lambda},\sigma(i)}^{2m_{i}}\biggr)=2^{1-k}\binom{n-\lambda}{k-\lambda}^{-1}\binom{2s}{2m_{1},\dots,2m_{\lambda}}^{-1}\binom{s}{m_{1},\dots,m_{\lambda}}.

Formula (
4.2
) provides a linear system of exactly
\p(s)
equations of the type of formula (
4.3
). The system has
\p(s)
unknowns, corresponding to the coefficients
c_{k,j_{k}}
for
k=1,\dots,s
and
j_{k}=1,\dots,\p_{k}(s)
. Denote each of the values which multiply coefficients
c_{k,j_{k}}
as

(4.4)

h_{k,j_{k},\lambda}=h_{k,j_{k}}(\mathbf{m}_{\lambda})=\frac{1}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{k})_{\mathbf{a}_{k,j}}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{k,j,\sigma(i)}^{2m_{\lambda,i}},

where
\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots\mathbf{m}_{\p_{k}(s)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
are the
k
-partitions in
\mathcal{P}_{k}(s)
. Now, the aim is to establish in which cases the linear system admits a solution. Thus we want to determine whenever the associated matrix is invertible.
This is given by

†
†
nicematrix-placeholder:
pNiceMatrix (nicematrix)

with several blocks on the diagonal of order
\p_{k}(s)
for each
k=1,\dots,s
. In particular, the matrix is invertible if and only if each block has a non-zero determinant. For simplicity, we can assume that the coordinates of the points involved are pairwise distinct. This is possible because the set of points that do not annihilate each determinant is open.
Nevertheless, the number of points used to obtain such a decomposition can be much higher than the minimal ones.
For each
k=1,\dots,s
, the elements
h_{k,j_{k},\lambda}
can be treated as polynomials with variables
a_{k,j_{k},1},\dots,a_{k,j_{k},k}
. Let us now look at the polynomials

H_{k,\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\frac{1}{\lvert(\mathfrak{S}_{k})_{\mathbf{a}_{k,j}}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{\sigma(i)}^{2m_{\lambda,i}},

\mathcal{P}_{k}(s)
. These polynomials are obtained by replacing the variables

by
a_{k,j_{k},1},\dots,a_{k,j_{k},k}
, that is,

for every point
\mathbf{a}_{k,j_{k}}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
, with
j_{k}=1,\dots,k
.
We want to prove that the polynomials
H_{k,\lambda}
are linearly independent. So, let
c_{1},\dots,c_{\mathrm{p}_{k}(s)}\in\mathbb{C}
be coefficients such that

c_{1}H_{k,1}+\cdots+c_{\mathrm{p}_{k}(s)}H_{k,\mathrm{p}_{k}(s)}=0.

Every monomial appearing in this linear combination involves exactly
k

k

c_{1}=\cdots=c_{\mathrm{p}_{k}(s)}=0,

which means that the polynomials
H_{k,\lambda}
are linearly independent.
Hence, we obtain the statement simply by applying
section 4

Subgeneric rank

We have observed from
section 3
that the size of decomposition (
3.4
) is subgeneric when
n>8
. Although it is not easy to determine the exact minimum value for which the higher numbers only give subgeneric rank, we can estimate that this value is quite low. Indeed, according to
section 1
and formula (
2.6
), we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\rk(q_{n}^{s})

\displaystyle\leq 2^{s-1}\binom{n}{s}+2^{s-2}\binom{n}{s-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{s-2}2^{k-1}k!\p_{k}(s)\binom{n}{k}

(5.1)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{s-1}\binom{n}{s}+2^{s-2}\binom{n}{s-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{s-2}\frac{2^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\frac{(s-1)!n!}{(s-k)!(n-k)!}.

Meanwhile, the generic rank is equal to

\frac{1}{n}\binom{2s+n-1}{2s}=\frac{1}{n}\biggl\lceil\frac{(2s+n-1)!}{(2s)!(n-1)!}\biggr\rceil.

Hence, in the case of
n\to+\infty
, the value of the generic rank grows as
n^{2s-1}
. However, the upper bound in formula (
5
) tells us that the rank of
q_{n}^{s}
grows more slowly than
n^{s}
as
n\to+\infty
.

Following the proof of
section 4
, the goal is to determine some points
\mathbf{a}_{k,1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{k,\p_{k}(s)}
for every block matrix

Let us consider the upper bound of

All the elements involved in the summation are quantities that have been extensively studied in the literature and for which many estimates and approximations have been provided.
A more precise estimate of the values involved in formula (

According to

Furthermore, by relation (

\frac{1}{n}\binom{2s+n-1}{2s}>\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\uppi s}}\biggl(\frac{2s+n-1}{n-1}\biggr)^{2s+n-\frac{1}{2}}\biggl(\frac{n-1}{2s}\biggr)^{2s}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{12n^{2}+72ns-23n+48s^{2}-70s+11}{24s(n-1)(12n+24s-11)}}
