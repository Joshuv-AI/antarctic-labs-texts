On triple product L-functions and a conjecture of Harris–Venkatesh
Emmanuel Lecouturier

Abstract
. Harris and Venkatesh made a conjecture relating the derived Hecke operators and the adjoint motivic cohomology in the setting of weight one modular forms. This conjecture was proved under some conditions in the dihedral case by Darmon–Harris–Rotger–Venkatesh. We use a new approach to prove more general cases of the conjecture (up to sign). Our approach relies on Waldspurger’s formula for the central value of Rankin L-series and Ichino’s formula for the triple product L-function.



1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to prove, up to sign, some cases of a conjecture of Harris–Venkatesh on dihedral weight one modular forms. This conjecture was made in
[
6
]
, and proven under some assumptions in
[
3
]
. We refer to the introduction of
[
3
]
for some background on the conjecture. While our results are similar to those of
[
3
]
, they are more general and the methods are quite different. See Remark
1.1
below for some discussion comparing our two results.

Let
N
,
p

\geq 5
be primes such that
p\mid\mid N-1
. Fix embeddings
\overline{\Q}\hookrightarrow\overline{\Q}_{p}
and
\overline{\Q}\hookrightarrow\overline{\C}_{p}
. Let
\T
(resp.
\T^{0}
) be the Hecke algebra (over
\Z
) acting on
M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
(resp.
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
). Let
I\subset\T
be the Eisenstein ideal and
\mathfrak{P}=I+(p)
be the
p
-Eisenstein maximal ideal of
\T
. We denote by
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}
and
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
the corresponding
\mathfrak{P}
-adic completions. We let
g_{p}=\text{rank}_{\Z_{p}}\T^{0}_{\mathfrak{P}}\geq 1
. Fix a surjective group homomorphism
\log:(\Z/N\Z)^{\times}\rightarrow\Z/p\Z
. This choice of
\log
gives rise to a canonical element
\mathfrak{S}\in\Hom(S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\Z_{p}),\Z/p\Z)
, called the
Shimura class
, which is annihilated by
\mathfrak{P}
(
cf.
[
6
, §3.1]
).

Let us review briefly the context and notation of the conjecture of Harris–Venkatesh
[
6
]
. Let
g\in S_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(d))
be a weight one cuspidal newform of conductor
d
. Assume that
\gcd(Np,d)=1
. We denote by
g^{*}
the dual form,
i.e.
the cusp form whose Fourier coefficients at
\infty
are the complex conjugates of those of
g
. We denote by
G
the trace of
g(z)g^{*}(Nz)
from
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(Nd))
to
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
. Attached to
g
is an odd Artin representation
\rho_{g}:\Gal(\overline{\Q}/\Q)\rightarrow\GL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{g})
, where
\mathcal{O}_{g}
is the ring of integers of a certain number field
L_{g}
containing the Fourier coefficients of
g
and contained in a cyclotomic field. We let
R=\mathcal{O}_{g}[\frac{1}{6}]
. As in
[
3
, §1.2]
, one can define a
R
-module
U_{g}
, which is a certain unit group cut out by the adjoint of
\rho_{g}
. Note that in
[
3
]
the authors invert
N
in
R
, but that this is not actually needed for the definition of
U_{g}
, and indeed we want
U_{g}
to be independent of
N
. Furthermore,
U_{g}
is equipped with a “reduction modulo
N
” map

\red_{N}:U_{g}\rightarrow(\Z/N\Z)^{\times}\otimes_{\Z}R\text{ .}

The Harris–Venkatesh conjecture is as follows:

Conjecture 1.1

(Conjecture 1.1 of
[
3
]
)
.

There exists an integer
m_{g}\geq 1
and
u_{g}\in U_{g}
such that, for all primes
N
and
p
as above, we have in
R/pR
:

m_{g}\cdot\langle G,\mathfrak{S}\rangle=\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))\text{ .}

In their recent paper
[
3
]
, Darmon–Harris–Rotger–Venkatesh prove this conjecture in certain cases when
g
is dihedral. Let
K
be a quadratic field of discriminant
D
, and let
g=\theta_{\psi_{1}}
be a weight one theta series associated to a Hecke character
\psi_{1}
of
K
. Let
\psi=\frac{\psi_{1}}{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}
, where
\psi_{1}^{\prime}
is the
\Gal(K/\Q)
-conjugate of
\psi_{1}
. We assume that
g
is cuspidal, which is equivalent to
\psi_{1}\neq\psi_{1}^{\prime}
. The main result of
[
3
]
is the following:

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 1.2 of
[
3
]
)
.

If
K
is imaginary, assume that
D
is an odd prime and that
\psi_{1}
is unramified. If
K
is real, assume that
D
is odd and that the conductor of
\psi_{1}
divides
(\sqrt{D})
. Then Conjecture
1.1
holds for
g=\theta_{\psi_{1}}
.

We prove Conjecture
1.1
up to sign, under less restrictives assumptions. The following is our main result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that
D
is odd and that
\psi:=\frac{\psi_{1}}{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}
is unramified. Then there exists
u_{g}\in U_{g}
and
m_{g}\in\Z
(depending only on
g
) such that for all primes
N
and
p
as above, we have, in
R/pR
:

(1)

m_{g}\cdot\langle G,\mathfrak{S}\rangle=\pm\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))\text{ .}

Here, the sign
\pm
may depend on
N
and
p
.

Remark 1.1
.

Let us discuss the differences between our result and the one of
[
3
]
. On the one hand, we only prove the desired identity up to sign, while the result of
[
3
]
does not have this ambiguity. On the other hand, our ramification assumption on
\psi_{1}
is weaker than the one of Theorem
1.2
. Furthermore, we remove the assumption that
D
is prime in the imaginary case. Let us also point out that
[
3
]
does not assume that
p^{2}
does not divide
N-1
. We have imposed this condition in order to simplify the commutative algebra of §
2
. We do not know whether this may be removed.

Our proof is relatively short compared to that of
[
3
]
. The main reason is that we rely on existing results of on special values of L-functions (due to Waldspurger, Gross and Popa), while
[
3
]
is more self-contained and uses the theory theta lifts. It seems to us that these two kinds of results use similar techniques (see also the comment at the beginning of
[
3
, §2]
). However, the results on special values of L-functions allow us to remove some limitations which arise in the theta lifting method. We expect to be able to remove the assumption that
\psi
is unramified, since we still have a similar, but more complicated, special L-value formula in this case.

Remark 1.2
.

In an earlier version of this paper, we limited ourselves to the case where
g
is self-dual,
i.e.
g=g^{*}
. The reason is that the special L-value formulae involve the square of the absolute value,
eg.
the left-hand side of (
18
) is
\left\lvert\langle G,f\rangle\right\rvert^{2}
. In order to get an identity up to sign, we need to remove this absolute value,
i.e.
replace
\left\lvert\langle G,f\rangle\right\rvert^{2}
by
\langle G,f\rangle^{2}
. It turns out that the phase of the complex number
\langle G,f\rangle
is easy to compute (it is basically
\chi_{g}(N)
, where
\chi_{g}
is the nebentype of
g
). The trick is to apply the Atkin–Lehner involution
w_{N}
. We thank Venkatesh for explaining this trick to us.

Our idea is to go back to the explicit formulation of the conjecture as found in
[
6
]
. Assume for simplicity that
g_{p}=1
and that
g
has coefficients in
\Z
, the general case being more technical but not fundamentally different. The space
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\Z/p\Z)_{\mathfrak{P}}
is generated by a single element, namely the Eisenstein series

(2)

f_{0}=\frac{N-1}{24}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{d\mid n,\gcd(d,N)=1}d\right)\cdot q^{n}\text{ (modulo }p\text{) .}

Therefore, the projection
G^{\Eis}
of
G
on the Eisenstein component
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\Z_{p})_{\mathfrak{P}}
is of the form

G^{\Eis}\equiv a_{0}\cdot f_{0}\text{ (modulo }p\text{)}

for some
a_{0}\in\Z_{p}
. We thus get

\langle G,\mathfrak{S}\rangle\equiv a_{0}\cdot\langle f_{0},\mathfrak{S}\rangle=a_{0}\cdot\mathcal{M}_{0}\text{ (modulo }p\text{)}

where
\mathcal{M}_{0}=-8\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{N-1}{2}}k\cdot\log(k)\neq 0
is
Merel’s unit
(its non-vanishing is equivalent to our assumption
g_{p}=1
). It turns out that we will
not need
the explicit formula for
\mathcal{M}_{0}
due to Merel. We need a way to understand the coefficient
a_{0}
.

This is where we use L-functions. As recalled in
[
3
, §1.3]
(with a missing absolue value), we have the following identity of Harris–Kudla and Ichino:

(3)

\left\lvert\langle G,f\rangle\right\rvert^{2}=C\cdot L(f/K,1)\cdot L(f/K,\psi,1)

where
f
is any newform in
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
,
\langle G,f\rangle
denote the Petersson inner product, and
C
is a certain non-zero constant, which is essentially a product of local constant terms depending on
f
and
g
. We will explain in §
3.1
that, as a consequence of Ichino’s formula, we have
C=C_{g}\cdot C_{N}
, where
C_{g}\in\overline{\Q}
depends only on
g
and
C_{N}\in\Q
depends only on
N
but is actually independent of
N
modulo
p
.

In this paper, we normalize the Petersson product as follows: for any weight
2
cusp forms
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, we define

(4)

\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=8\pi^{2}\cdot\iint_{\Gamma_{0}(N)\backslash\mathfrak{h}}f_{1}(z)\overline{f_{2}(z)}dxdy\text{ .}

We have a decomposition

G=\sum_{f}\lambda_{f}(G)\cdot f

where
f
runs through all the newforms in
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
. We can view
\lambda_{f}(G)
alternatively in
\C
or in
\overline{\Q}_{p}
thanks to our embeddings
\overline{\Q}\hookrightarrow\overline{\Q}_{p}
and
\overline{\Q}\hookrightarrow\overline{\C}_{p}
. We have
\lambda_{f}(G)=\frac{\langle G,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}
.

Since
p\mid\mid N-1
, Mazur showed that there is a
unique
newform
F
(up to conjugation) which is congruent modulo a prime above
p
to the Eisenstein series
f_{0}
. We fix such a newform (among its conjugates) in the rest of the paper. Since we are assuming
g_{p}=1
, we can canonically view
F
as having coefficients in
\Z_{p}
. We can thus view
\lambda_{F}(G)
as an element of
\Z_{p}
and we have

(5)

\lambda_{F}(G)\equiv a_{0}\text{ (modulo }p\text{) .}

By (
3
), we have:

(6)

\lambda_{F}(G)^{2}=\left\lvert\lambda_{F}(G)\right\rvert^{2}=C\cdot\frac{L(F/K,1)\cdot L(F/K,\psi,1)}{\langle F,F\rangle^{2}}\text{ .}

A general formula of Waldspurger, made more concrete by Gross (resp. Popa) when
K
is imaginary (resp. real), gives an identity which is roughly of the form:

(7)

\frac{L(F/K,1)\cdot L(F/K,\psi,1)}{\langle F,F\rangle^{2}}=\frac{\left\lvert\langle e_{F},e_{K}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\langle e_{F}^{\prime},e_{\psi}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}}{\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle^{2}}\text{ ,}

where
e_{F}
(resp.
e_{F}^{\prime}
) is an eigenvector corresponding to
F
in a certain Hecke module
M
(resp. the dual
M^{\vee}
of
M
). More precisely, if
K
is imaginary then
M
is related to the supersingular module, while if
K
is real then
M
is a space of modular symbols. Furtermore,
e_{K}^{\prime}
and
e_{\psi}
explicit elements of
M
depending only on
K
and
\psi
respectively. These elements are related to some reduction of CM curves modulo
N
if
K
is imaginary and to closed Heegner geodesics of discriminant
D
on
X_{0}(N)
if
K
is real. Let us note that if
K
is real, then an additional argument if needed for proving (
7
), namely an expression of the Petersson norm in terms of modular symbols due to Merel
[
11
]
.

The right-hand side is an element of
\Z_{p}
. To understand the numerator modulo
p
, one needs some knowledge of the
higher Eisenstein elements
in
M
. What has been done in
[
8
]
for the supersingular module and restated in a more suitable form in
[
3
]
is enough. One gets that
\langle e_{F},e_{K}\rangle
is proportional to
\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))
. On the other hand, an abstract argument shows that
\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle
is proportional to
\mathcal{M}_{0}
modulo
p
. This argument does not require the actual explicit knowledge of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
. Altogether, this proves Theorem
1.3
.

The plan of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
we carry out an abstract computation relating
\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle
to (the higher analogue of) Merel’s unit. The main result is Theorem
2.4
. In Section
3.1
, we gather some results about special values of L-functions, including Ichino’s triple product formula in a classical language (following
[
1
]
). In Section
3.2
(resp. Section
3.3
) we deal with the case where
K
is imaginary (resp. real).

2.
Higher Eisenstein elements and pairings

2.1.
Pairings

In this section, we will use the results and notation of
[
8
, Section 2]
. Recall that there is an isomorphism
I/I^{2}\otimes\Z_{p}\xrightarrow{\sim}\Z/p\Z
sending
T_{\ell}-\ell-1
to
(\ell-1)\log(\ell)
for any prime
\ell\neq N
. We fix in what follows a local generator
\eta\in I
sent to
1
via this isomorphism.

Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be
\T
-modules such that
M_{\mathfrak{P}}:=M\otimes_{\T}\T_{\mathfrak{P}}
and
M_{\mathfrak{P}}^{\prime}
are free of rank one over
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}
. Let
e_{0}
, …,
e_{g_{p}}
(resp.
e_{0}^{\prime}
, …,
e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}
) be a system of higher Eisenstein elements of
\overline{M}:=M/pM
(resp.
\overline{M^{\prime}}
). We can and do assume that for any
i\in\{0,1,...,g_{p}\}
, we have
\eta\cdot e_{i}=e_{i-1}
(this is an actual equality in
M/pM
), and similarly for the
e_{i}^{\prime}
s.

Assume that we have a
\T
-equivariant bilinear pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:M\times M^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbf{Z}
which induces a
perfect
pairing
M_{\mathfrak{P}}\times M_{\mathfrak{P}}^{\prime}\rightarrow\Z_{p}
. The quantity
\langle e_{i},e_{j}^{\prime}\rangle\in\Z/p\Z
is well-defined if
i+j\leq g_{p}
, depends only on
i+j
and is non-zero if and only if
i+j=g_{p}
(
cf.
[
8
, Corollary 2.5]
).

By
[
4
, Proposition 1.8]
, we have an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\Z\cdot T_{0}\rightarrow\T\rightarrow\T^{0}\rightarrow 0

where
T_{0}\in\T
is such that
T_{0}-\frac{N-1}{\gcd(N-1,12)}\in I
. Let
M^{0}=M/T_{0}\cdot M
and
M_{0}=M[T_{0}]
(the elements annihilated by
T_{0}
) (and similarly for
M^{\prime}
); these are both
\T^{0}
modules locally free of rank one at
\mathfrak{P}
. Note that for all
i\in\{0,1,...,g_{p}-1\}
, we have
e_{i}\in\overline{M}_{0}
and that
T_{0}\cdot\overline{M}=(\Z/p\Z)\cdot e_{0}
.

The
\Theta
-correspondence is the canonical
\T
-equivariant homomorphism

\Theta:M\otimes_{\T}M^{\prime}\rightarrow M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\Z[\frac{1}{6}])

sending
x\otimes y
to the modular form whose
q
-expansion at
\infty
is
\frac{\gcd(N-1,12)}{24}\cdot\langle x,T_{0}y\rangle+\sum_{n\geq 1}\langle x,T_{n}y\rangle\cdot q^{n}
. This induces a
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}
-equivariant isomorphism

\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}:M_{\mathfrak{P}}\otimes_{\T_{\mathfrak{P}}}M_{\mathfrak{P}}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\sim}M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\Z_{p})_{\mathfrak{P}}

and a
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
-equivariant isomorphism

\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}:(M_{\mathfrak{P}})^{0}\otimes_{\T_{\mathfrak{P}}}(M_{\mathfrak{P}}^{\prime})_{0}\xrightarrow{\sim}S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\Z_{p})_{\mathfrak{P}}\text{ ,}

which depend on
M
,
M^{\prime}
and the pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Let
f_{0}
, …,
f_{g_{p}}
be a system of higher Eisenstein elements of
M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\Z/p\Z)
. We normalise
f_{0}
as in (
2
).

Proposition 2.1
.

For all
i,j\in\{0,1,...,g_{p}\}
, we have

\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}(e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{\prime})\equiv\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot f_{i+j-g_{p}}\text{ (modulo }(\Z/p\Z)\cdot f_{0}\oplus...\oplus(\Z/p\Z)\cdot f_{i+j-g_{p}-1}\text{), }

with the convention that
f_{k}=0
if
k<0
.

Proof.

If
i+j<g_{p}
, then
e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{\prime}=0
, so the result is true. If
i+j=g_{p}
, then the element
e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{\prime}
is non-zero and annihilated by the Eisenstein ideal. Thus,
\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}(e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{\prime})=\lambda\cdot f_{0}
for some
\lambda\in\Z/p\Z
. We have
a_{1}(\lambda\cdot f_{0})=\langle e_{i},e_{j}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle
, so
\lambda=\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle
. The result for
i+j>g_{p}
is obtained by induction on
i+j
using the Hecke property satisfied by the higher Eisenstein elements (
cf.
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
).
∎

2.2.
Eigenvectors

Since we have assumed
p\mid\mid N-1
, the
\Z_{p}
-algebra
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
is isomorphic to the valuation ring
\mathcal{O}
of a totally ramified finite extension
L
of
\Q_{p}
. Fix such an isomorphism
\varphi:\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{O}
, and let
\pi=\varphi(\eta)
be a uniformizer of
\mathcal{O}
(
\eta
being our fixed local generator of
I
). We denote by
v_{\pi}
the
\pi
-adic valuation. Since
\mathcal{O}
is local, the minimal polynomial
P(X)\in\Z_{p}[X]
of
\pi
is distinguished,
i.e.
P(X)=X^{g_{p}}+\sum_{i=0}^{g_{p}-1}a_{i}X^{i}
and
p
divides
a_{i}
for all
i\in\{0,1,...,g_{p}-1\}
.

We use the notation
\mathcal{O}
for
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
when we view
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
as a “ring of coefficients”. More precisely, let
M_{\mathcal{O}}:=M_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}\otimes_{\Z_{p}}\mathcal{O}
and
M_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}:=(M_{0}^{\prime})_{\mathfrak{P}}\otimes_{\Z_{p}}\mathcal{O}
. We view
M_{\mathcal{O}}
as a (left)
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
-module via the action of
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
on
M_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
and as a (left)
\mathcal{O}
-module via the action of
\mathcal{O}
on itself. Similarly for
M_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
. We get a perfect
\mathcal{O}
-linear and
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
equivariant pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:M_{\mathcal{O}}\times M_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}
.

As in the introduction, we fix in the rest of this paper a newform
F\in S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\mathcal{O})
such that
F\equiv f_{0}\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

(i)

There is an element
e_{F}\in M_{\mathcal{O}}
, unique up to multiplication by
\mathcal{O}
, such that for any
T\in\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
, we have:
T\cdot e_{F}=\varphi(T)\cdot e_{F}
. Similarly for
e_{F}^{\prime}\in M_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
.

(ii)

We have
v_{\pi}(\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle)=v_{\pi}(P^{\prime}(\pi))
.

(iii)

One can choose
e_{F}
(resp.
e_{F}^{\prime}
) such that
e_{F}\equiv e_{1}\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
(resp.
e_{F}^{\prime}\equiv e_{0}^{\prime}\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
). With these choices, we have:

\frac{\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}\equiv\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{).}

Proof.

Proof of (
i
). Suppose such an element
e_{F}
exists. One can write uniquely
e_{F}=\sum_{i=0}^{g_{p}-1}m_{i}\otimes\pi^{i}
for some
m_{i}\in M_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
. The condition
\eta\cdot e_{F}=\pi\cdot e_{F}
together with the equation
\pi^{g_{p}}+\sum_{i=0}^{g_{p}-1}a_{i}\cdot\pi^{i}=0
implies that for all
i\in\{0,1,...,g_{p}-1\}
we have
m_{i}=\left(\sum_{k=i+1}^{g_{p}}a_{k}\cdot\eta^{k-i-1}\right)\cdot m_{g_{p}-1}
(where by convention
a_{g_{p}}=1
). Thus,
e_{F}
is unique up to a scalar in
\mathcal{O}
. Conversely, the formula

(8)

e_{F}=\sum_{i=0}^{g_{p}-1}\left(\sum_{k=i+1}^{g_{p}}a_{k}\cdot\eta^{k-i-1}\right)\cdot m\otimes\pi^{i}

defines the desired element
e_{F}
(for any choice of
m
in
M_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
).

Proof of (
ii
). For simplicity, let us assume
L/\Q_{p}
is Galois and let
G=\Gal(L/\Q_{p})
. The general case is identical, up to replacing Galois automorphisms by embeddings
\sigma:L\hookrightarrow\overline{\Q}_{p}
.

We shall need the following result:

Lemma 2.3
.

We have:

\Tr_{L/\Q_{p}}\left(\frac{\pi^{i}}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if }0\leq i<g_{p}-1\\
1&\mbox{ if }i=g_{p}-1\end{array}\right.

Proof.

For
0\leq i\leq g_{p}-1
, we have the following identity:

\frac{X^{i}}{P(X)}=\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sigma\left(\frac{\pi^{i}}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{X-\sigma(\pi)}\text{ .}

The lemma then follows from comparing the series expansion in
1/X
.
∎

Let
m\in M_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
(resp.
m^{\prime}\in(M_{0}^{\prime})_{\mathfrak{P}}
) be a generator over
\T_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}
. Choose
e_{F}
and
e_{F}^{\prime}
as in (
8
).
By Lemma
2.3
, we get
\Tr_{L/\Q_{p}}\left(\frac{e_{F}}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}\right)=m
,
i.e.

(9)

m=\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sigma\left(\frac{1}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}\right)\cdot\sigma(e_{F})\text{ .}

The family
(\sigma(e_{F}))_{\sigma\in G}
is a basis of
M_{L}:=M_{\mathcal{O}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}L
(where
L=\text{Frac}(\mathcal{O})
), and similarly for

. Since

-basis of

, we see that

-basis of

. The matrix of

of

is

. We have an equality of matrices

Since the pairing

which proves that
v_{\pi}(\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle)=v_{\pi}(P^{\prime}(\pi))
. Let us now compute,
\frac{\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}
modulo
\pi

Proof of (
iii

Regarding the first assertion, since for all

and

such that

and

comes from the fact that

is the maximal cuspidal

, while

is the maximal cuspidal

. Thus, in some sense,

(and not a higher Eisenstein element).

We have:

\displaystyle\Tr_{L/\Q_{p}}\left(\frac{\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sigma\left(\langle\frac{e_{F}}{P^{\prime}(\pi)},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in G}\langle\sigma(\frac{e_{F}}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}),\sigma(e_{F}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in G}\langle\sigma(\frac{e_{F}}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}),\tau(e_{F}^{\prime})\rangle

where the third equality comes from the fact that

\Tr_{L/\Q_{p}}(e_{F}^{\prime})\equiv\left(\sum_{k=1}^{g_{p}}a_{k}\cdot\eta^{k-1}\right)\cdot m^{\prime}\equiv e_{0}^{\prime}\text{ (modulo }p\text{).}

∎

We can finally prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
f\in S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\Z_{p})_{\mathfrak{P}}
and write
f\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{g_{p}-1}\lambda_{i}(f)\cdot f_{i}\text{ (modulo }p\text{)}
for some
\lambda_{i}(f)\in\Z/p\Z
. Let
\lambda_{F}(f)\in L
be such that
f=\Tr_{L/\Q_{p}}(\lambda_{F}(f)\cdot F)
, i.e.
\lambda_{F}(f)
is the coefficient of
f
in
F
. We have
\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(f)\in\mathcal{O}
and

\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(f)\equiv\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{g_{p}-1}(f)\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{).}

Proof.

It suffices to check to check this statement for
f=\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}(m\otimes m^{\prime})
where
m\in M_{\mathcal{O}}
and
m^{\prime}\in M_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
. Write
m\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{g_{p}}\lambda_{i}(m)\cdot e_{i}\text{ (modulo }p\text{)}
and
m^{\prime}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{g_{p}-1}\lambda_{i}(m^{\prime})\cdot e_{i}\text{ (modulo }p\text{)}
. By Proposition
2.1
, we have:

\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}(m\otimes m^{\prime})=\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\sum_{1\leq i\leq g_{p}\atop 0\leq j\leq g_{p}-1}\lambda_{i}(m)\cdot\lambda_{j}(m^{\prime})\cdot f_{i+j-g_{p}}\text{ .}

We thus get

(10)

\lambda_{g_{p}-1}(f)\equiv\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{g_{p}}(m)\cdot\lambda_{g_{p}-1}(m^{\prime})\text{ (modulo }p\text{).}

On the other hand, write
m=\Tr_{L/\Q_{p}}(\lambda_{F}(m)\cdot e_{F})
and
m^{\prime}=\Tr_{L/\Q_{p}}(\lambda_{F}(m^{\prime})\cdot e_{F}^{\prime})
. By orthogonality of the various conjugates of
e_{F}
and
e_{F}^{\prime}
, we get:

\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}(m\otimes m^{\prime})=\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}\left(\Tr_{L/\Q_{p}}(\lambda_{F}(m)\cdot\lambda_{F}(m^{\prime})\cdot e_{F}\otimes e_{F}^{\prime})\right)\text{ .}

We claim that
\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}(e_{F}\otimes e_{F}^{\prime})=\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot F
. Both sides are proportional since they have the same eigenvalues for the Hecke operators, and the constant of proportionality is given by the first Fourier coefficient of
\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}(e_{F}\otimes e_{F}^{\prime})
, which is
\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle
by definition. We thus get:

\Theta_{\mathfrak{P}}^{0}(m\otimes m^{\prime})=\Tr_{L/\Q_{p}}\left(\lambda_{F}(m)\cdot\lambda_{F}(m^{\prime})\cdot\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot F\right)\text{ .}

This proves that
\lambda_{F}(f)=\lambda_{F}(m)\cdot\lambda_{F}(m^{\prime})\cdot\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle
, and so

(11)

\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(f)=(\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(m))\cdot(\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(m^{\prime}))\text{ .}

By (
10
) and (
11
), in order to prove Theorem
2.4
, it is enough to show that
\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(m)\in\mathcal{O}
and
\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(m)\equiv\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{g_{p}}(m)\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
, and similarly
\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(m^{\prime})\in\mathcal{O}
and
\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(m^{\prime})\equiv\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{g_{p}-1}(m^{\prime})\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
. The statement is true for
m\equiv e_{g_{p}}\text{ (modulo }p\text{)}
: this follows from (
9
) (from which one gets
\lambda_{F}(m)=\frac{1}{P^{\prime}(\pi)}
) and Proposition
2.2
(
iii
). The statement is then seen to be true for
\eta^{i}\cdot m
for any
i\in\{1,...,g_{p}-1\}
(both sides of the equality
\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(m)\equiv\langle e_{0},e_{g_{p}}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{g_{p}}(m)\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
are zero), and thus for any linear combination of such terms.
∎

2.3.1.
The supersingular module

The supersingular module is

where

is the set of isomorphism classes of supersingular elliptic curves over

There is a natural

6

,

(

, Equation (91)]
),

Recall the notation

,

,

,
\psi_{1}^{\prime}
,

,

and

from the introduction. We assume in this section that

is imaginary quadratic. Let

be the Hilbert class field of

As in

Theorem 2.6
.

For all
i,j\in\{0,1,...,g_{p}\}
with
i+j=g_{p}
, we have:

\langle\Sigma_{i},\Sigma_{j}\rangle=2\cdot\langle f_{i},\mathfrak{S}_{j}\rangle=-\frac{1}{12}\langle\kappa_{i}^{+},\kappa_{j}^{-}\rangle\text{ .}

Proof.

This follows from
[
8
, Corollary 6.3]
. Let us explain the factor
-\frac{1}{12}
. The minus sign comes from the fact that the present normalisation of
\kappa_{1}^{+}
is the opposite of that of
[
8
, Theorem §4.2]
(we chose this normalisation here to be consistent with the one of
[
3
]
). The factor
\frac{1}{12}
comes from our different normalisation of
\kappa_{0}^{-}
(
cf.
the sentence after
[
8
, Theorem 1.9]
).
∎

3.
Proof of the main theorem

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.3
. We keep the notation of the previous sections. As in §
2.2
, we fix a newform
F\in S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\mathcal{O})
congruent to
f_{0}
modulo
\pi
. We are going to evaluate explicitly both sides of (
6
) (appropriately renormalised) modulo
\pi
.

3.1.
Special values of L-functions

We first make precise the constant
C
of the triple product formula (
3
). Ichino
[
7
]
expressed
C
as a product of local terms, which were computed explicitely in some cases by Woodbury. The passage from the automorphic formulation of Ichino’s formula to an entirely classical one has been done
[
1
]
. We now recall Collin’s computation. We warn the reader that Collins’ normalization of the Petersson product is
\frac{3}{[\SL_{2}(\Z):\Gamma_{0}(N)]\cdot 8\pi^{3}}
times our normalization (
4
). Let
f
be a newform in
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
and let
g
be a newform in
S_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(d))
as in the introduction (in particular, we assume that
\gcd(N,d)=1
).
We have, by
[
1
, Theorem 2.1.2]
:

\left\lvert\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f(z)\rangle\right\rvert^{2}=\frac{[\SL_{2}(\Z):\Gamma_{0}(N)]^{2}}{N}\cdot L(f,g,g^{*},1)\cdot\prod_{q\mid Nd\text{, }q\text{ prime}}\mathcal{E}_{q}I_{q}^{*}\text{ ,}

where for each prime
q
dividing
Nd
,
\mathcal{E}_{q}I_{q}^{*}
is a non-zero local factor defined by Ichino and recalled in
[
1
]
. By
[
1
, Corollary 2.2.2]
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{N}I_{N}^{*}=\frac{N}{(N+1)^{2}}
(note that
\frac{[\SL_{2}(\Z):\Gamma_{0}(N)]^{2}}{N}=\frac{(N+1)^{2}}{N}
cancels with
\mathcal{E}_{N}I_{N}^{*}
). We let
C_{g}=\prod_{q\mid d\text{, }q\text{ prime}}\mathcal{E}_{q}I_{q}^{*}
. We shall not need the exact value of
C_{g}
(which, to our knowledge, has not been computed in the litterature). Instead, the following result will be enough for our purposes.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
g=\theta_{\psi_{1}}
is as in Theorem
1.3
, then
C_{g}\cdot\frac{1}{\left\lvert D\right\rvert}
is a square in
L_{g}^{\times}
.

The proof of this proposition will require some preliminaries, which will also be used later in the paper.

First, we relate
\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f(z)\rangle^{2}
to
\left\lvert\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f(z)\rangle\right\rvert^{2}
. We denote by a bar the complex conjugation acting on
\C
. The following result was indicated to us by Venkatesh.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have

\overline{\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f(z)\rangle}=\epsilon_{N}(f)\cdot\chi_{g}(N)^{-1}\cdot\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f(z)\rangle

where
\chi_{g}:(\Z/d\Z)^{\times}\rightarrow\C^{\times}
is the Nebentype character of
g
and
\epsilon_{N}(f)\in\{1,-1\}
is the Atkin–Lehner eigenvalue of
f
(at the prime
N
).

Proof.

If
\Gamma
is a congruence subgroup, let
X(\Gamma)
be the corresponding (compact) modular curve. We denote by
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}:X(\Gamma_{0}(N)\cap\Gamma_{1}(d))\rightarrow X(\Gamma_{1}(d))
the two degeneracy maps given by
\pi_{1}(z)=z
and
\pi_{2}(z)=Nz
on the upper-half plane. Let
W_{N}
be the index
N
Atkin–Lehner involution and
\langle N\rangle
be the
N
th diamond operator. These are automorphisms of both
X(\Gamma_{0}(N)\cap\Gamma_{1}(d))
and
X(\Gamma_{1}(d))
(which we denote by the same letter, as no possible confusion will arise).
Using the moduli interpretation, we easily check that
W_{N}^{2}=\langle N\rangle
and
\pi_{1}\circ W_{N}=\langle N\rangle\circ\pi_{2}
(note that
W_{N}
is not an involution in general). We thus get

\pi_{2}\circ W_{N}=\langle N\rangle^{-1}\circ\pi_{1}\circ W_{N}^{2}=\langle N\rangle^{-1}\circ\pi_{1}\circ\langle N\rangle=\pi_{1}\text{ .}

We denote by an upper star the maps induced on the spaces of modular forms (of any weight). We have

\displaystyle W_{N}^{*}(g(z)g^{*}(Nz))

\displaystyle=W_{N}^{*}(\pi_{1}^{*}(g)\cdot\pi_{2}^{*}(g^{*}))

\displaystyle=(\pi_{1}\circ W_{N})^{*}(g)\cdot(\pi_{2}\circ W_{N})^{*}(g^{*})

\displaystyle=((\pi_{2}^{*}\circ\langle N\rangle^{*})(g))\cdot\pi_{1}^{*}(g^{*})

\displaystyle=\chi_{g}(N)\cdot g(Nz)g^{*}(z)\text{ .}

Since
f
has level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, its Fourier coefficients are totally real. We thus have

\overline{\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f(z)\rangle}=\langle g^{*}(z)g(Nz),f(z)\rangle\text{ .}

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\overline{\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f(z)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\langle g^{*}(z)g(Nz),f(z)\rangle

\displaystyle=\chi_{g}(N)^{-1}\cdot\langle W_{N}^{*}(g(z)g^{*}(Nz)),f(z)\rangle

\displaystyle=\chi_{g}(N)^{-1}\cdot\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),W_{N}^{*}(f)\rangle

\displaystyle=\chi_{g}(N)^{-1}\epsilon_{N}(f)\cdot\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f(z)\rangle

∎

In the remainder of this section, we assume that
g=\theta_{\psi_{1}}
and we fix a newform
f\in S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
. Recall that
G(z)=g(z)g^{*}(Nz)
. We have
\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f\rangle=\lambda_{f}(G)\cdot\langle f,f\rangle
, where
\lambda_{f}(G)
belongs to the compositum
K_{f}\cdot L_{g}
of
L_{g}
and the Hecke field
K_{f}
of
f
. Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}(G)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f\rangle^{2}}{\langle f,f\rangle^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi_{g}(N)\cdot\epsilon_{N}(f)\cdot\left\lvert\langle g(z)g^{*}(Nz),f\rangle\right\rvert^{2}}{\langle f,f\rangle^{2}}

By the Artin formalism, we have
L(f,g,g^{*},1)=L(f/K,1)\cdot L(f/K,\psi,1)
where we recall that
\psi=\frac{\psi_{1}}{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}
(
\psi_{1}^{\prime}
being the conjugate character of
\psi_{1}
). By Ichino’s formula, we get

(17)

\lambda_{f}(G)^{2}=C_{g}\cdot\chi_{g}(N)\cdot\epsilon_{N}(f)\cdot\frac{L(f/K,1)\cdot L(f/K,\psi,1)}{\langle f,f\rangle^{2}}

We now sketch the proof of Proposition
3.1
. Recall the notation
\mathcal{O}_{g}
,
L_{g}
,
\psi_{1}
,
\psi_{1}^{\prime}
and
\psi
from the introduction.

Proof.

Let us first assume that
K
is imaginary,
i.e.
D<0
. Let
u(K)=\#(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}/\pm 1)\in\{1,2,3\}
. Gross gave an explicit formula for the special value
L(f,\chi,1)
if
\chi
is a character of the class group of
K
(
cf.
[
5
, Proposition 11.2]
). The formula is as follows:

(18)

\frac{L(f/K,\chi,1)}{\langle f,f\rangle}=\frac{1}{u(K)^{2}\cdot\sqrt{-D}}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert\langle[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}}{\langle e_{f}^{\prime},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle}\text{ .}

where
e_{f}^{\prime}
is any non-zero element of
K_{f}[S]^{0}
(the augmentation subgroup of the supersingular module with coefficients in
K_{f}
) such that
T_{n}(e_{f}^{\prime})=a_{n}(f)\cdot e_{f}^{\prime}
for all
n\geq 1
. Here,
T_{n}
is the
n
th Hecke operator and
a_{n}(f)
is the
n
th Fourier coefficient of
f
at
\infty
.

Recall that
[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}}\in\Z[\chi][S]
was defined in §
2.3.1
, and depends on a choice of a prime ideal
\mathfrak{N}
above
N
in
F
. Recall that by (
15
) we have:

\overline{[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}}}=-\chi(h)\cdot W_{N}[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}}\text{ ,}

where
h\in\Gal(F/\Q)
is such that
\sigma_{\mathfrak{N}}=\sigma\cdot h
(using the notation of the proof of Theorem
2.5
). As in (
16
), we have
\chi_{g}(N)=-\psi_{1}(h)\cdot\psi_{1}^{\prime}(h)
.

Since our form
f
is self-dual, we get

\displaystyle\overline{\langle[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle}

\displaystyle={\langle\overline{[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle}

\displaystyle=-\chi(h)\cdot\langle W_{N}[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\chi(h)\cdot\langle[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}},W_{N}e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\epsilon_{N}(f)\cdot\chi(h)\cdot\langle[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle\text{ .}

Thus, Gross’ formula can be rewritten as

\frac{L(f/K,\chi,1)}{\langle f,f\rangle}=-\frac{\epsilon_{N}(f)\cdot\chi(h)}{u(K)^{2}\cdot\sqrt{-D}}\cdot\frac{\langle[\chi]_{\mathfrak{N}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle^{2}}{\langle e_{f}^{\prime},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle}

Applying this formula for
\chi=\mathbb{1}
and
\chi=\psi
, we get:

\frac{L(f/K,1)\cdot L(f/K,\psi,1)}{\langle f,f\rangle^{2}}=-\frac{\psi(h)}{u(K)^{4}\cdot D}\cdot\left(\frac{\langle[\mathbb{1}]_{\mathfrak{N}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\langle[\psi]_{\mathfrak{N}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle e_{f}^{\prime},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle}\right)^{2}

Using (
17
) and the fact that
\psi(h)\cdot\chi_{g}(N)=-\psi_{1}(h)^{2}
, we get:

(19)

\lambda_{f}(G)^{2}=C_{g}\cdot\frac{1}{D}\cdot\frac{\epsilon_{N}(f)\cdot\psi_{1}(h)^{2}}{u(K)^{4}}\cdot\left(\frac{\langle[\mathbb{1}]_{\mathfrak{N}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\langle[\psi]_{\mathfrak{N}},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle e_{f}^{\prime},e_{f}^{\prime}\rangle}\right)^{2}\text{ .}

We apply (
19
) in the case where
f
is the newform of
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(11))
(for which
K_{f}=\Q
and
\epsilon_{11}(f)=-1
). We get that
-C_{g}\cdot\frac{1}{D}
is a square in
L_{g}^{\times}
, which concludes the proof Proposition
3.1
when
K
is imaginary.

The proof when
K
is real is very similar, but uses Popa’s formula for the special value
L(f/K,\chi,1)
instead of Gross’ formula. See §
3.3
, and especially Remark
3.1
, for more details.
∎

3.2.
Proof of the main theorem: the imaginary quadratic case

Assume in this paragraph that
K
is imaginary. Recall that
D
is the discriminant of
K
and that
u(K)=\#(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}/\pm 1)\in\{1,2,3\}
. Let
h(K)
be the class number of
K
. Let us recall our basic assumptions:
D
is odd and
g=\theta_{\psi_{1}}
for some Hecke character
\psi_{1}
of
K
such that
\psi:=\frac{\psi_{1}}{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}
is unramified.

We apply the result of §
2.2
for
M=M^{\prime}=\Z[S]
(the supersingular module). There is a subtlety here: even though
M=M^{\prime}
, but
M_{\mathcal{O}}\neq M_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
and
e_{F}\neq e_{F}^{\prime}
. This is due to the fact that
M_{\mathcal{O}}
corresponds to the cuspidal
quotient
, while
M_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
corresponds to the cuspidal
subspace
. The canonical map
M_{0}\rightarrow M^{0}
has image
I\cdot M^{0}
. This induces a canonical map
\varphi:M_{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}\rightarrow M_{\mathcal{O}}
, and we have
\varphi(e_{F}^{\prime})=\eta\cdot e_{F}=\pi\cdot e_{F}
. This can be seen for instance using (
8
) and noting that
m^{\prime}
can be chosen such that
\varphi(m^{\prime})=\eta\cdot m
.

By Theorem
2.4
, we have:

(20)

\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{F}(G)\equiv\langle\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{g_{p}}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{g_{p}-1}(G)\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{).}

Let us now analyse the right-hand side of (
17
). Gross’ formula (
18
) gives

\frac{L(F/K,\chi,1)}{\langle F,F\rangle}=\frac{1}{u(K)^{2}\cdot\sqrt{-D}}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert\langle[\chi],e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}}{\langle e_{F}^{\prime},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle}\text{ .}

Note that the denominator of the right hand side is
not

\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle
. Since
\varphi(e_{F}^{\prime})=\pi\cdot e_{F}
, we get
\langle e_{F}^{\prime},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle=\pi\cdot\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle
.

Let
\mathbb{1}
be the trivial character. Since the degree of
[\mathbb{1}]
is
h(K)
and
e_{F}^{\prime}\equiv\Sigma_{0}\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
, we have
\langle e_{F}^{\prime},[\mathbb{1}]\rangle\equiv h(K)\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
. Since
e_{F}\equiv\Sigma_{1}\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
, Theorem
2.5
, shows that there exists a constant
D_{g}\in\Z
and
u_{g}\in U_{g}
such that
\left\lvert D_{g}\cdot\langle[\psi],e_{F}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\equiv\left\lvert\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))\right\rvert^{2}\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{)}
. Since
\varphi(e_{F}^{\prime})=\pi\cdot e_{F}
, we get

\left\lvert D_{g}\cdot\langle[\psi],e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\equiv\pi^{2}\cdot\left\lvert\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))\right\rvert^{2}\text{ (modulo }\pi^{3}\text{)}\text{ .}

Combining all of the above, we get:

D_{g}^{2}\cdot\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle^{2}\cdot\frac{L(F/K,1)\cdot L(F/K,\psi,1)}{\langle F,F\rangle^{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{u(K)^{4}\cdot\left\lvert D\right\rvert}\cdot(h(K))^{2}\cdot\left\lvert\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))\right\rvert^{2}\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{).}

By Theorem
2.5
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))\cdot\overline{\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))}

\displaystyle=-\chi_{g}(N)^{-1}\cdot\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))^{2}

so we get

(21)

D_{g}^{2}\cdot\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle^{2}\cdot\frac{L(F/K,1)\cdot L(F/K,\psi,1)}{\langle F,F\rangle^{2}}\equiv-\frac{\chi_{g}(N)^{-1}}{u(K)^{4}\cdot\left\lvert D\right\rvert}\cdot(h(K))^{2}\cdot\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))^{2}\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{).}

Since
F
is Eisenstein modulo
p
, we have
W_{N}^{*}(F)=-U_{N}(F)=-F
(note that
U_{N}=-W_{N}
on
X_{0}(N)
as
N
is prime). By (
17
), we get:

(22)

\lambda_{F}(G)^{2}=-C_{g}\cdot\chi_{g}(N)\cdot\frac{L(F/K,1)\cdot L(F/K,\psi,1)}{\langle F,F\rangle^{2}}\text{ .}

By Proposition
3.1
, one can write
C_{g}=\lvert D\rvert\cdot\frac{A_{g}^{2}}{B_{g}^{2}}
for some
B_{g}\in\Z
and
A_{g}\in\mathcal{O}_{g}
depending only on
g
. Combining (
20
), (
21
) and (
22
), we conclude that:

B_{g}^{2}\cdot D_{g}^{2}\cdot\left(\langle\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{g_{p}}\rangle\cdot\lambda_{g_{p}-1}(G)\right)^{2}\equiv\frac{A_{g}^{2}}{u(K)^{4}}\cdot(h(K))^{2}\cdot\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))^{2}\text{ (modulo }\pi\text{).}

By Theorem
2.6
, we have
\langle\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{g_{p}}\rangle=2\cdot\langle f_{g_{p}-1},\mathfrak{S}_{1}\rangle
. Finally, notice that
\langle G,\mathfrak{S}\rangle=\lambda_{g_{p}-1}(G)\cdot\langle f_{g_{p}-1},\mathfrak{S}_{1}\rangle
.
In conclusion, we have proven that there exists
M_{g}\in R
and
u_{g}\in U_{g}
depending only on
g
such that
M_{g}^{2}\cdot\langle G,\mathfrak{S}\rangle^{2}=\log(\red_{N}(u_{g}))^{2}
in
R/pR
for all
N
and
p
as before. This proves Theorem
1.3
when
K
is imaginary.

3.3.
Proof of the main theorem: the real quadratic case

Assume in this paragraph that
K
is real. Let
D
be the discriminant of
K
,
h(K)
the narrow class number and
u(K)
be a fundamental unit of
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
. We fix a prime
\mathfrak{N}
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
dividing
N
. Let
\mathcal{C}(K)
be the narrow class group of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Let us recall our basic assumptions:
D
is odd and
g=\theta_{\psi_{1}}
for some Hecke character
\psi_{1}
of
K
such that
\psi:=\frac{\psi_{1}}{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}
is unramified. We denote by
\chi_{g}:(\Z/d\Z)^{\times}\rightarrow\C^{\times}
the Nebentype character of
g
. The existence of our unramified character
\psi
of signature at infinity
(-1,-1)
implies that the narrow class group is strictly bigger than the (usual) class group,
i.e.
the norm of
u(K)
is
1
.

We apply the result of §
2.2
for
M=M^{+}=H^{1}(X_{0}(N),\cusps,\Z_{p})^{+}
and
M^{\prime}=M^{-}=H^{1}(Y_{0}(N),\Z_{p})^{-}
. One can consider our eigenform
F
as having coefficients in
\C
via our embeddings
\overline{\Q}\hookrightarrow\overline{\Q}_{p}
and
\overline{\Q}\hookrightarrow\C
. We get a “period homomorphism”
\xi_{F}:H_{1}(X_{0}(N),\Z)\rightarrow\C
given by
\xi_{F}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}2i\pi F(z)dz
for cycles
\gamma\in H_{1}(X_{0}(N),\Z)
. One can write
\xi_{F}=\xi_{F}^{+}+i\cdot\xi_{F}^{-}
, where
\xi_{F}^{\pm}:H_{1}(X_{0}(N),\Z)^{\pm}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
. Actually, one can extend
\xi_{F}^{+}
to a homomorphism
H_{1}(X_{0}(N),\cusps,\Z)^{+}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
and factor
\xi_{F}^{-}
as a homomorphism
H_{1}(Y_{0}(N),\Z)^{-}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
. One can then view
\xi_{F}^{+}
and
\xi_{F}^{-}
as elements of
H^{1}(X_{0}(N),\cusps,\mathbf{R})^{+}
and
H^{1}(Y_{0}(N),\mathbf{R})^{-}
respectively.

There exist periods
\Omega_{F}^{+},\Omega_{F}^{-}\in\C^{\times}
, unique up to an element of
\overline{\Q}^{\times}
, such that
\frac{\xi_{F}^{\pm}}{\Omega_{F}^{\pm}}
takes values in
\overline{\Q}
. One can choose the periods so that
e_{F}=\frac{\xi_{F}^{+}}{\Omega_{F}^{+}}
and
e_{F}^{\prime}=\frac{\xi_{F}^{-}}{\Omega_{F}^{-}}
. In that way,
\Omega_{F}^{+}
and
\Omega_{F}^{-}
are well-defined up to an element of
1+\pi\cdot\mathcal{O}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

One has
\frac{\langle F,F\rangle}{\Omega_{F}^{+}\cdot\Omega_{F}^{-}}=2\cdot\langle e_{F},e_{F}^{\prime}\rangle
.

Proof.

This is essentially due to Merel, who related
\langle F,F\rangle
to
\xi_{F}
(
cf.
[
11
, Corollaire 4]
). One can deduce that
-i\cdot\langle F,F\rangle=\langle\xi_{F},\overline{\xi_{F}}\rangle
(for any cusp form
F
). A detailed proof can be found in
[
9
, Theorem 2.16 and Proposition 2.17]
(where we warn the reader that the Petersson product is differently normalised than the one used in the present paper). Finally, note that
\langle\xi_{F},\overline{\xi_{F}}\rangle=-2i\cdot\langle\xi_{F}^{+},\xi_{F}^{-}\rangle
.

∎

We refer to
[
3
, §3.1]
for the formalism of Heegner cycles. The choice of our prime ideal
\mathfrak{N}
above
N
in
K
corresponds to a choice of
\delta_{N}\in\Z
(uniquely determined modulo
N
) such that
\delta_{N}^{2}\equiv D\text{ (modulo }N\text{)}
. For any
[I]\in\mathcal{C}(K)
, one can associate a closed geodesic
\gamma_{I}
in the modular curve
X_{0}(N)
. The definition of
\gamma_{I}
actually depends on the choice of
\mathfrak{N}
, but we don’t make it explicit for the ease of notation. We easily see that
\gamma_{\alpha\cdot I}
is the image
c(\gamma_{I})
of
\gamma_{I}
by the complex conjugation (on the modular curve), where
\alpha\in K^{\times}
is any element of negative norm. If
\chi:\mathcal{C}(K)\rightarrow\C^{\times}
, one defines

[\chi]:=\sum_{[I]\in\mathcal{C}(K)}\chi^{-1}(I)\cdot\gamma_{I}\in H_{1}(X_{0}(N),\Z)\otimes_{\Z}\C\text{ .}

Popa proved in
[
12
]
that we have the following formula, for any character
\chi
as above:

(23)

L(F/K,\chi,1)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\cdot\left\lvert\sum_{I\in\mathcal{C}(K)}\chi^{-1}(I)\cdot\xi_{F}(\gamma_{I})\right\rvert^{2}\text{ .}

Let us consider the analogue of (
15
) (which was needed in the proof of Proposition
3.1
).

Lemma 3.4
.

For all character
\chi
of
\mathcal{C}(K)
, we have:

\overline{[\chi]}=-\chi(\mathfrak{N})\cdot W_{N}c([\chi])\text{ .}

Here, by
\overline{[\chi]}
we mean that we apply the complex conjugation to the coefficients of the homology (not on the modular curve itself), while
c
denotes the complex conjugation acting on
X_{0}(N)
.

Proof.

We follow
[
2
, §1.4]
. Recall that we have fixed a choice of
\delta_{N}\in\Z
such that
\delta_{N}^{2}\equiv D\text{ (modulo }N\text{)}
. The corresponding ideal
\mathfrak{N}
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is given by
(N,\frac{-\delta_{N}+\sqrt{D}}{2})
.
Any ideal class in
\mathcal{C}(K)
has a representative
I
of the form
(A,\frac{-B+\sqrt{D}}{2})
where
N\mid\mid A
and
B\equiv\delta_{N}\text{ (modulo }N\text{)}
. We let
W_{N}(I)
be the ideal given by
(NC,\frac{-B+\sqrt{D}}{2})
. With such a
I
, by definition
\gamma_{I}
corresponds to the homology class of the path from
z
to
M_{I}z
in
X_{0}(N)
, where
M_{I}:=\begin{pmatrix}u-Bv&-2Cv\\
