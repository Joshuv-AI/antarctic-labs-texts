Abstract
. We construct an ancient solution to planar curve shortening. The solution is at all times compact and embedded. For t ≪ 0 t\ll 0 it is approximated by the rotating Yin-Yang soliton, truncated at a finite angle α ⁡ ( t ) = − t \alpha(t)=-t , and closed off by a small copy of the Grim Reaper translating soliton.



1.
Introduction

In
[
5
]
Daskalopoulos, Hamilton, and Sesum showed that any compact, convex, and embedded ancient solution to Curve Shortening in the plane is either a shrinking circle or the ancient paperclip solution. Qian You et.al.
[
10
,
3
]
showed that there exist many other ancient solutions that are either embedded and not compact, or otherwise compact, convex, but not embedded. Here we construct an ancient solution to Plane Curve Shortening that is embedded, compact at all times, but not convex. We note that an ancient solution satisfying these properties was also constructed independently in
[
4
]
. We present a brief comparison of the two different approaches at the end of this introduction.

Our construction begins with the Yin–Yang soliton, i.e. the rotating soliton that is invariant with respect to reflection in the origin—see Figure
1
(left). This spiral shaped curve divides the plane into two congruent parts, and under Curve Shortening evolves by rotating with unit speed in the counterclockwise direction.

Each of the two branches of the Yin–Yang spiral is a graph in polar coordinates, given by

r={\mathcal{R}}(\theta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}),\quad r={\mathcal{R}}(\theta-\tfrac{\pi}{2})

respectively. The Yin-Yang spiral is asymptotic to a Fermat spiral
[
9
]
which in polar coordinates is given by
r=a\sqrt{\theta}
. The Yin-Yang curve satisfies

(1.1)

{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)=\sqrt{2\theta}+{\mathcal{O}}(\theta^{-3/2}),\qquad{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\theta}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\theta^{-5/2})\quad(\theta\to\infty).

We review the properties and derive expansions for
{\mathcal{R}}
in appendix
A
.

The Yin–Yang soliton is itself an example of an embedded ancient (and in fact eternal) solution. It is however not compact. Our goal in this paper is to construct a compact embedded ancient solution which, for
t\to-\infty
converges to the rotating Yin–Yang solution.

. The bulk of the curve consists of an initial segment of the Yin–Yang curve rotated counterclockwise by an angle
t
, which has two ends. These ends are connected by a small cap in the shape of a translating soliton (the so-called “Grim Reaper”) located at polar angle
\theta=-t
.

In section
2
of this paper we construct an approximate solution to CSF, by truncating the Yin–Yang solution at polar angle
\alpha(t)
, and connecting the two ends with a small cap whose shape is approximately that of a rotated and rescaled copy of the Grim Reaper soliton. See Figure
1
(right).

To determine how to choose the angle
\alpha(t)
we estimate the speed
V
of the cap at time
t
. Since the cap is approximately a Grim Reaper, its speed is related to its width
w
by
V=\pi/w
. The width
w
is approximated by

w\approx{\mathcal{R}}(\alpha(t)-t+\tfrac{\pi}{2})-{\mathcal{R}}(\alpha(t)-t-\tfrac{\pi}{2})\approx{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(\alpha(t)-t)\,\pi,

so that the cap moves with speed

V\approx\frac{\pi}{w}\approx\frac{1}{{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(\alpha(t)-t).}

On the other hand, far away from the center, the arms of the Yin-Yang spiral are close to circular. The cap, which moves with angular velocity
-\alpha^{\prime}(t)
along a near circle with radius
{\mathcal{R}}(\alpha(t)-t)
, therefore has velocity
V\approx-\alpha^{\prime}(t){\mathcal{R}}(\alpha(t)-t)
. It follows that
\alpha^{\prime}(t)\approx-1/({\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})
. Since the asymptotics (
1.1
) imply
{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\approx 1
, we end up with

(1.2)

\alpha^{\prime}(t)=-1+o(1),\qquad\alpha(t)=-t+o(t).

For any
t<0
we let
\Omega(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the region given in Polar Coordinates by

(1.3)

\Omega(t)=\Bigl\{(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\;\big|\;t\leqslant\theta\leqslant-t,\;{\mathcal{R}}(\theta-t-\tfrac{\pi}{2})\leqslant r\leqslant{\mathcal{R}}(\theta-t+\tfrac{\pi}{2})\Bigr\}

We will call the boundary curve
\partial\Omega(t)
the
truncated Yin-Yang curve
. It consists of a segment of the rotating Yin-Yang curve and a straight line segment that connects the two ends at
\theta=-t
.

There exists a compact ancient solution
\{C(t)\mid t<0\}
to Curve Shortening which for
t\to-\infty
is uniformly close to the truncated Yin-Yang curve in the sense that if
\Lambda(t)
is the bounded region enclosed by
C(t)

A

The construction follows a pattern similar to the construction of ancient solutions in
[
3
,
10
]
, namely, construct a sequence of “really old solutions”
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(t)\mid-n\leqslant t\leqslant t_{0}\}
of Curve Shortening and extract a convergent subsequence whose limit is the desired ancient solution. In section
2
we first construct an ancient approximate solution
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(t)\mid-\infty<t<0\}
of Curve Shortening, i.e. a family of curves for which

(1.4)

{\mathcal{E}}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{0}}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(t)}\left|V-\kappa\right|\,ds\,dt<\infty.

Then we consider a sequence of solutions
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(t)\mid-n\leqslant t<t_{0}\}
of Curve Shortening whose initial curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(-n)
are chosen increasingly close to the approximate solution
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(-n)
at time
-n
, in the sense that the area between
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(-n)
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(-n)
tends to zero as
n\to\infty
. Arguing as in
[
3
,
10
]
we observe that the area between the solutions
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(t)
and the approximate solution
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(t)
is bounded in terms of the “error”
{\mathcal{E}}
and the area between the initial curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(-n)
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(-n)
. Since the curves we deal with in this paper are not graphical the area estimate is a bit more complicated than in
[
3
,
10
]
. In section
3
we present a more general estimate that generalizes the Altschuler-Grayson
[
2
]
area bounds for Space Curve Shortening. Finally, in section
4
we show how this bound allows us to extract a convergent subsequence of the very old solutions
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}(t)
, and provides enough control to conclude that the resulting limit satisfies the description in the Main Theorem.

In appendix
A
we recall the derivation of the Yin-Yang soliton, and obtain its asymptotic expansion at infinity.

Comparison with the construction in
[
4
]
.

The idea to construct ancient solutions as a limit of a sequence of very old solutions is natural. In
[
4
]
the same strategy is also followed, but the core of the proof where one controls the sequence of old solutions is quite different. While the approach in our paper follows the method of area comparison established in
[
3
]
, the arguments in
[
4
]
proceed by carefully controlling a sequence of old solutions via an exponential barrier for the bulk of the solution. Thereafter stability of the tip is established in
[
4
]
using a blow-up argument, combined with the uniqueness of the Grim Reaper as possible limit.

2.
Construction of the cap

2.1.
Parametrized curves and the Curve Shortening deficit

An evolving family of curves is a map
X:(t_{0},t_{1})\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
for which
X_{p}(t,p)\neq 0
for all
(t,p)
. For such a family we define

ds=\|X_{p}(t,p)\|\,dp,\qquad\frac{d}{ds}=\frac{1}{\|X_{p}(t,p)\|}\frac{d}{dp}\;.

The normal velocity and curvature of the family
X
are

V=\left\langle X_{t},JX_{s}\right\rangle,\qquad\kappa=\left\langle X_{ss},JX_{s}\right\rangle=\left\langle\frac{X_{pp}}{\|X_{p}\|^{2}},\frac{JX_{p}}{\|X_{p}\|}\right\rangle

where
J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
represents counterclockwise rotation by
\frac{\pi}{2}
. By definition, the parameterized family of curves
X
satisfies Curve Shortening if
V=\kappa
. If it does not, then we measure the “discrepancy with curve shortening” in terms of the form

(2.1)

|V-\kappa|\,ds=\left|\Bigl\langle X_{t}-\frac{X_{pp}}{\|X_{p}\|^{2}},JX_{p}\Bigr\rangle\right|\,dp

The error
{\mathcal{E}}
is obtained by integrating this form over the curve and in time.

We write
{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}=(1,0)
,
{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}=(0,1)
for the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In this basis counterclockwise rotation by
\theta
is given by

e^{\theta J}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

We will use the rotated frame
\{{{\boldsymbol{E}}_{1}}(\theta),{{\boldsymbol{E}}_{2}}(\theta)\}=\{e^{\theta J}{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}},e^{\theta J}{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}\}
, which satisfy

{{\boldsymbol{E}}_{1}}^{\prime}(\theta)={{\boldsymbol{E}}_{2}}(\theta),\qquad{{\boldsymbol{E}}_{2}}^{\prime}(\theta)=-{{\boldsymbol{E}}_{1}}(\theta).

2.2.
Z
or
(u,v)
-coordinates

We expect the tip of the ancient solution to be located near the point
R(t){{\boldsymbol{E}}_{1}}(-t)
, and to have width
\sim R^{-1}
, where

R=R(t){\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}}{\mathcal{R}}(-2t).

Thus we introduce new, time dependent, coordinates
Z=u{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}+v{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}
related to the cartesian coordinates
X=(x_{1},x_{2})
via

(2.2)

X=e^{-tJ}\{R{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}+R^{-1}Z\},\text{ i.e. }Z=R\bigl\{e^{tJ}X-R{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}\bigr\}.

2.3.
The inner and outer Yin-Yang arms

The region on one side of the Yin-Yang curve is foliated by rotated copies of the curve. At time
t\in\mathbb{R}
the leaves of this foliation are parametrized by

(2.3)

Y(\theta,t,y)={\mathcal{R}}(\theta-t+y){{\boldsymbol{E}}_{1}}(\theta)

where
y\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
determines the leaf, and
\theta\in(t-y,\infty)
is the polar angle on the leaf. The inner and outer arms of the region that contains our ancient solution correspond to
y=\pm\pi/2
. See Figure
2
.

2.4.
Lemma

For any
M>0
there is a
t_{M}<0
such that if
t\leqslant t_{M}
, then the segments of the Yin-Yang leaves
Y(\theta,t,y)
with
|\theta+t|\leqslant M\tau^{-1}\ln\tau
are graphs in
(u,v)
coordinates of the form
u=U_{y}(t,v)
, at least if
t\leqslant t_{M}
. Moreover, the functions
U_{y}(t,v)
satisfy

(2.4)

U_{y}(t,v)=y-\frac{y^{2}+v^{2}-2v}{2\tau}+{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-2+\delta})

for all
\delta>0
, where
\tau=-4t
.

Proof.

We begin with the defining equations

(2.5)

{\mathcal{R}}(\theta-t+y){{\boldsymbol{E}}_{1}}(\theta)=e^{-tJ}\bigl\{R{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}+R^{-1}Z\bigr\}=e^{-tJ}\bigl\{(R+u/R){{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}+v/R\,{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}\bigr\}.

Multiply with
e^{tJ}
on both sides:

{\mathcal{R}}(\theta-t+y){{\boldsymbol{E}}_{1}}(\theta+t)=R{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}+R^{-1}Z=(R+u/R){{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}+v/R\,{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}.

Here the left hand side is the polar form of the right hand side. Under our assumptions
R^{2}\sim\tau
and
|u|\ll\tau
, so
1+u/R^{2}>0
, and hence we have

(2.6)

{\mathcal{R}}(\theta-t+y)^{2}=R^{2}+2u+\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{R^{2}},\qquad\theta+t=\arctan\frac{v/R^{2}}{1+u/R^{2}}.

Since
\alpha\mapsto{\mathcal{R}}(\alpha)^{2}
is a monotone function, the first equation in (
2.6
) can be solved for
y
. Using the asymptotic expansion in appendix
A.3
we get

(2.7)

2(\theta-t+y)={\mathcal{R}}^{2}+2{\mathcal{R}}^{-2}+{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{R}}^{-4}),

where
{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{R}}(\theta-t+y)
. Replace
{\mathcal{R}}^{2}
by the expression in (
2.6
), to get

2(\theta-t+y)=R^{2}+2u+\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}+2}{R^{2}}+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(v^{2}R^{-4}\bigr).

To eliminate
\theta
we expand the second equation in (
2.6
),

\theta+t=\frac{v}{R^{2}}+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(v^{2}R^{-4}\bigr).

Hence

(2.8)

\displaystyle 2y

\displaystyle=2(\theta-t+y)-2(\theta+t)+4t

\displaystyle(-4t=\tau)

\displaystyle=R^{2}-\tau+2u+\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}-2v+2}{R^{2}}+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(v^{2}R^{-4}\bigr).

Expand
R=R(t)={\mathcal{R}}(-2t)
in powers of
\tau=-4t
using (
A.7
):

R^{2}={\mathcal{R}}(-2t)^{2}=\tau\left(1-2\tau^{-2}+{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-3})\right)

and substitute this in (
2.8
) to get

2y=2u+\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}-2v}{\tau}+{\mathcal{O}}(v^{2}R^{-4}).

By assumption we have
|v|\lesssim\ln\tau
and
R\sim\sqrt{\tau}
, so
v^{2}R^{-4}={\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-2+\delta})
for any
\delta>0
.

If
\tau
is sufficiently large then the above equation has a unique solution
u=U_{y}(t,v)
with

u=y-\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}-2v}{2\tau}+{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-2+\delta})=y-\frac{y^{2}+v^{2}-2v}{2\tau}+{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-2+\delta}).\qed

2.5.
General
ansatz
for the cap

We now construct the cap by assuming that it is given by

(2.9)

X(t,p)=e^{-tJ}\bigl\{R{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}+\varepsilon Z(t,p)\bigr\},\text{ with }R(t){\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}}{\mathcal{R}}(-2t),\;\varepsilon(t)=\frac{1}{R(t)},

and by computing its deviation from Curve Shortening (
2.1
)

(V-\kappa)ds=\langle X_{t}-X_{ss},JX_{s}\rangle ds=\Bigl\langle X_{t}-\frac{X_{pp}}{\|X_{p}\|^{2}},JX_{p}\Bigr\rangle\;dp{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}}W\,dp

In the following computations it will be convenient to abbreviate

R_{\theta}(t)={\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(-2t),

so that
R^{\prime}(t)=-2R_{\theta}(t)
.

The space derivatives of
X
are

X_{p}=e^{-tJ}\varepsilon Z_{p}\qquad X_{pp}=e^{-tJ}\varepsilon Z_{pp}.

The time derivative has a few more terms:

\displaystyle X_{t}=e^{-tJ}\left\{-2R_{\theta}{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}-R{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}Z-\varepsilon JZ+\varepsilon Z_{t}\right\}

Express
W=\langle X_{t}-X_{pp}/\|X_{p}\|^{2},JX_{p}\rangle
in terms of
Z
, keeping in mind that
\varepsilon=R^{-1}
,

(2.10)

W=\left\langle\varepsilon^{2}Z_{t}-\frac{Z_{pp}}{\|Z_{p}\|^{2}},JZ_{p}\right\rangle-\langle{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}},Z_{p}\rangle\\
+2\varepsilon R_{\theta}\langle{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}},Z_{p}\rangle+\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}\langle Z,JZ_{p}\rangle-\varepsilon^{2}\langle Z,Z_{p}\rangle.

We look for a cap in the form of a normal perturbation of the Grim Reaper curve, i.e. we assume

(2.11)

Z(t,p)=G(p)+f(t,p)JG_{p}(p)

where

G(p)=-\arcsin(\tanh p){{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}-\ln(\cosh p){{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}

is the arclength parametrization of the Grim Reaper.

Since
G
is an arclength parametrization
G_{p},JG_{p}
are unit tangent and normal to the Grim Reaper. Specifically,

G_{p}=-\frac{1}{\cosh p}{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}-\tanh p{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}.

The parametrization
G(p)
traces the Grim Reaper out from right to left. Furthermore, the curvature vector of the Grim Reaper is

G_{pp}(p)=\kappa(p)JG_{p},\text{ where }\kappa(p)=\frac{1}{\cosh p}.

2.6.
Detailed computation of
W
on the cap

We have

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{t}JG_{p}

\displaystyle Z_{p}

\displaystyle=G_{p}+f_{p}JG_{p}+fJG_{pp}=(1-\kappa f)G_{p}+f_{p}JG_{p}

\displaystyle JZ_{p}

\displaystyle=-f_{p}G_{p}+(1-\kappa f)JG_{p}

\displaystyle\|Z_{p}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\kappa f)^{2}+f_{p}^{2}=1-2\kappa f+\kappa^{2}f^{2}+f_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle Z_{pp}

\displaystyle=-(\kappa_{p}f+2\kappa f_{p})G_{p}+\bigl(f_{pp}+\kappa-\kappa^{2}f\bigr)JG_{p}

Substituting in (
2.10
), and using
\kappa=1/\cosh p
, we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\varepsilon^{2}Z_{t},JZ_{p}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}(1-\kappa f)f_{t}

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\frac{Z_{pp}}{\|Z_{p}\|^{2}},JZ_{p}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\kappa f)(f_{pp}+\kappa(1-\kappa f))+\kappa_{p}ff_{p}+2\kappa f_{p}^{2}}{1-2\kappa f+\kappa^{2}f^{2}+f_{p}^{2}}

\displaystyle\langle{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}},Z_{p}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\kappa(1-\kappa f)+f_{p}\tanh p.

Thus after substituting and expanding we find

(2.12)

\displaystyle W=\varepsilon^{2}(1-\kappa f)f_{t}-\frac{\kappa+f_{pp}-2\kappa^{2}f-\kappa ff_{pp}+\kappa_{p}ff_{p}+\kappa^{3}f^{2}+2\kappa f_{p}^{2}}{1-2\kappa f+\kappa^{2}f^{2}+f_{p}^{2}}+\kappa(1-\kappa f)

\displaystyle-f_{p}\tanh p-\varepsilon^{2}\langle Z,Z_{p}\rangle+2\varepsilon R_{\theta}\langle{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}},Z_{p}\rangle+\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}\langle Z,JZ_{p}\rangle.

We will choose
f(t,p)
so as to make
W
integrable in space and time. To find
f
we linearize the expression for
W
and solve the resulting first order equation for
f
. It turns out that one solution is of the form
f(t,p)=\tau^{-1}F(p)
for a function
F
that is of polynomial growth for
|p|\to\infty
. We restrict our attention to the region

(2.13)

|p|\leqslant 2K\ln\tau,\qquad\tau\gg 1,

where
K=100
is a fixed, largish, constant. We will assume that
f
and its derivatives are bounded by

(2.14)

|f|+|f_{p}|+|f_{pp}|+\tau|f_{t}|\lesssim\tau^{-1+\delta}\text{ for }|p|\leqslant 2K\ln\tau.

Here, and in what follows, when we write estimates for remainder terms of the form
{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-m+\delta})
, the estimate is implicitly meant to hold “for all
\delta>0
.”

The bound (
2.14
) will certainly hold if
f(t,p)=\tau^{-1}F(p)
for some function
F(p)
for which
F(p)
,
F^{\prime}(p)
, and
F^{\prime\prime}(p)
grow polynomially as
p\to\pm\infty
.

We now consider the many terms in (
2.12
) that add up to
W
. To begin, we have for
|p|\leqslant 2K\ln\tau
,

\varepsilon^{2}(1-\kappa f)f_{t}={\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-2+\delta}),

and also

\frac{-\kappa ff_{pp}+\kappa_{p}ff_{p}+\kappa^{3}f^{2}+2\kappa f_{p}^{2}}{1-2\kappa f+\kappa^{2}f^{2}+f_{p}^{2}}={\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-2+\delta})\text{ for any }\delta>0.

It follows from
Z=G+fJG_{p}
that

\displaystyle Z=G+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{-1+\delta}\bigr),\qquad Z_{p}=G_{p}+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{-1+\delta}\bigr).

Hence, for
|p|\leqslant 2K\ln\tau
,

\displaystyle\|G(p)\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\ln\tau

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\langle Z,Z_{p}\rangle

\displaystyle=\tau^{-1}\langle G,G_{p}\rangle+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{-2+\delta}\bigr)

\displaystyle\varepsilon R_{\theta}\langle{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}},Z_{p}\rangle

\displaystyle=\tau^{-1}\langle{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}},G_{p}\rangle+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{-2+\delta}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}\langle Z,JZ_{p}\rangle

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{-2+\delta}\bigr).

In this computation we have used the expansions
\varepsilon=R(t)^{-1}={\mathcal{R}}(-2t)^{-1}=\tau^{-1/2}+{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-3/2})
and
R_{\theta}={\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(-2t)=\tau^{-1/2}+{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-3/2})
that follow from the expansions of
{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)
and
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(\theta)
in appendix
A
.

So far we have

\displaystyle W={\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{-2+\delta}\bigr)-\frac{\kappa+f_{pp}-2\kappa^{2}f}{1-2\kappa f+\kappa^{2}f^{2}+f_{p}^{2}}+\kappa(1-\kappa f)-f_{p}\tanh p

\displaystyle+\tau^{-1}\langle 2{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}-G,G_{p}\rangle

We can simplify the fraction (for
|p|\leqslant 2K\ln\tau
) by using

\frac{1}{1-2\kappa f+\kappa^{2}f^{2}+f_{p}^{2}}=1+2\kappa f-\kappa^{2}f^{2}-f_{p}^{2}+\frac{(-2\kappa f+\kappa^{2}f^{2}+f_{p}^{2})^{2}}{1-2\kappa f+\kappa^{2}f^{2}+f_{p}^{2}}=1+2\kappa f+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{-2+\delta}\bigr)

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa+f_{pp}-2\kappa^{2}f}{1-2\kappa f+\kappa^{2}f^{2}+f_{p}^{2}}=\kappa+2\kappa^{2}f+f_{pp}-2\kappa^{2}f+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{-2+\delta}\bigr)=\kappa+f_{pp}+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{-2+\delta}\bigr),

and hence

W=-f_{pp}-\tanh(p)f_{p}-\kappa^{2}f+\frac{1}{\tau}\langle 2{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}-G,G_{p}\rangle+{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-2+\delta}).

2.7.
Computation of the correction term

We look to perturb the Grim Reaper with a term of the form

f(t,p)=\frac{F(p)}{\tau},

where
F
is a solution of

{\mathcal{L}}F{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}}F_{pp}+\tanh(p)F_{p}+\kappa^{2}F=\langle 2{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}-G,G_{p}\rangle.

The linear operator can be factored

{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{d^{2}}{dp^{2}}+\tanh(p)\frac{d}{dp}+\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(p)}=\frac{d}{dp}\circ\frac{1}{\cosh p}\circ\frac{d}{dp}\circ\cosh(p)

while we also have

\langle 2{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}-G,G_{p}\rangle=\frac{d}{dp}\bigl\langle 2{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}-\tfrac{1}{2}G,G\bigr\rangle,

all of which allows us to solve the equation for
F
:

(2.15)

F(p)=\frac{A}{\cosh p}+B\tanh p+\int_{0}^{p}\frac{\cosh r}{\cosh p}\bigl\langle 2{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}-\tfrac{1}{2}G(r),G(r)\bigr\rangle\,dr.

It appears that the first term is of no use, so we set
A=0
. The resulting function
F(p)
is an odd function of
p
. We use the asymptotic behavior of
\langle 2{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}-\tfrac{1}{2}G,G\rangle
for large
p
to find an expansion for the integral as
p\to+\infty
.

Consider

I{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}}\int_{0}^{p}\frac{\cosh r}{\cosh p}\bigl\langle 2{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}}-\tfrac{1}{2}G(r),G(r)\bigr\rangle\,dr.

The explicit expression for
G
implies

\displaystyle\langle{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}},G(r)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\ln\cosh r

\displaystyle\|G(r)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\ln\cosh r\bigr)^{2}+\bigl(\arcsin\tanh r\bigr)^{2}=\bigl(\ln\cosh r\bigr)^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}+{\mathcal{O}}(e^{-r})\qquad(r\to\infty).

To compute
I
we substitute
\lambda=\ln\cosh p
,
\mu=\ln\cosh r
, which leads to

3.
Area decreasing property of Space Curve Shortening

In 1991 Altschuler and Grayson
[
2
]
observed that for two solutions of space curve shortening the area of the minimal surface spanning them is non increasing. Here we elaborate on this and prove a similar result without using the existence of the minimal surface.

3.1.
Moving space curves

For an immersed curve
X:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
one defines the arc length one-form
ds
and the arc length derivative
\partial_{s}
of any quantity
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

ds=\|X_{p}\|\,dp,\text{ and }\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}=\frac{1}{\|X_{p}\|}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}.

The unit tangent and curvature of the curve are
X_{s}=\frac{X_{p}}{\|X_{p}\|}
and
X_{ss}
.

A moving family of space curves is a map
X:(t_{0},t_{1})\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The family evolves by Curve Shortening if it satisfies
X_{t}^{\perp}=X_{ss}
, i.e., if for some smooth function
\lambda(t,p)
one has

(3.1)

X_{t}=X_{ss}+\lambda X_{s}=\frac{1}{\|X_{p}\|}\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\Bigl(\frac{X_{p}}{\|X_{p}\|}\Bigr)+\lambda\frac{X_{p}}{\|X_{p}\|}

Since
X_{s}\perp X_{ss}
, one can always find
\lambda
from
\lambda=\langle X_{t},X_{s}\rangle
.

3.2.
Evolution of arc length and the commutator
[\partial_{t},\partial_{s}]

The following are commonly used relations. We record them here for completeness, and also because we allow the velocity
X_{t}
of the parametrizations to have a nonvanishing tangential component. Assuming that
X_{t}=X_{ss}+\lambda X_{s}
one has

(3.2)

\partial_{t}\|X_{p}\|=(\lambda_{s}-\kappa^{2})\|X_{p}\|,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}ds=(\lambda_{s}-\kappa^{2})ds=d\lambda-\kappa^{2}ds,

and

(3.3)

[\partial_{t},\partial_{s}]=(-\lambda_{s}+\kappa^{2})\partial_{s}.

Proof.

We have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\|X_{p}\|=\Bigl\langle\frac{X_{p}}{\|X_{p}\|},X_{tp}\Bigr\rangle=\langle X_{s},X_{tp}\rangle=\langle X_{s},X_{ts}\rangle\|X_{p}\|,

and hence

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}ds=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl(\|X_{p}\|dp\bigr)=\langle X_{s},X_{tp}\rangle dp=\bigl\langle X_{s},X_{ts}\bigr\rangle ds.

The evolution equation
X_{t}=X_{ss}+\lambda X_{s}
then implies

\bigl\langle X_{s},X_{ts}\bigr\rangle=\langle X_{s},X_{t}\rangle_{s}-\bigl\langle X_{ss},X_{t}\bigr\rangle=\lambda_{s}-\bigl\langle X_{ss},X_{ss}+\lambda X_{s}\bigr\rangle=\lambda_{s}-\|X_{ss}\|^{2}=\lambda_{s}-\kappa^{2},

which directly implies (
3.2
). Using (
3.2
) we get

[\partial_{t},\partial_{s}]=\left[\partial_{t},\|X_{p}\|^{-1}\partial_{p}\right]=-\frac{\partial_{t}\|X_{p}\|}{\|X_{p}\|^{2}}\partial_{p}=-(\lambda_{s}-\kappa^{2})\partial_{s}.\qed

3.3.
Dependence on a parameter

Let
[\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1}]\subset\mathbb{R}
be some parameter interval, and let
X:[\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1}]\times(t_{0},t_{1})\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a family of moving curves that depends on a parameter
\epsilon\in[\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1}]
. We compute the evolution of the first variation

X_{\epsilon}=\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,t,p).

Throughout the computation we will assume that the parametrization
X
is such that

(3.4)

X_{\epsilon}\perp X_{p}(\epsilon,t,p)\text{ for all $(\epsilon,t,p)$}

For any given parametrization
\widetilde{X}
one can find a reparametrization
\varphi(\epsilon,t,p)
so that
X(\epsilon,t,p)=\widetilde{X}(\epsilon,t,\varphi(\epsilon,t,p))
satisfies (
3.4
).

If
X:[\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1}]\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is injective with
X_{\varepsilon}\perp X_{p}
, then the double integral

\int_{\epsilon_{0}}^{\epsilon_{1}}\int_{p=0}^{1}\|X_{\epsilon}\|\,ds\,d\epsilon\qquad(\text{where }ds=\|X_{p}\|dp)

is the area (2-dim Hausdorff measure) of the image
X([\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1}]\times[0,1])
. If
X
merely satisfies
X_{\epsilon}\perp X_{p}
, without necessarily being injective, then the area formula implies that the double integral is bounded from below, by

(3.5)

\ell(X){\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}}\int_{\epsilon_{0}}^{\epsilon_{1}}\int_{p=0}^{1}\|X_{\epsilon}\|\,ds\,d\epsilon\geqslant\mathcal{H}^{2}(X([\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1}]\times[0,1]))

We will call the integral
\ell(X)
the
length of the homotopy
X
, and we will show that Curve Shortening decreases the length of homotopies.

The following improvement of the inequality
\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{s}\leqslant\|X_{\epsilon s}\|
(which follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality) will be useful.

3.4.
Lemma

Assuming (
3.4
) we have

\bigl(\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{s}\bigr)^{2}\leqslant\|X_{\epsilon s}\|^{2}-\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle^{2}.

Proof.

Split
X_{\epsilon s}
into tangential and orthogonal components:

X_{\epsilon s}=P+\langle X_{\epsilon s},X_{s}\rangle X_{s}

Since
X_{\epsilon}\perp X_{s}
we have
\langle X_{\epsilon s},X_{s}\rangle=\partial_{s}\langle X_{\epsilon},X_{s}\rangle-\langle X_{\epsilon},X_{ss}\rangle=-\langle X_{\epsilon},X_{ss}\rangle
.
Therefore

X_{\epsilon s}=P-\langle X_{\epsilon},X_{ss}\rangle X_{s},

and thus

\|X_{\epsilon s}\|^{2}=\|P\|^{2}+\langle X_{\epsilon},X_{ss}\rangle^{2}.

On the other hand

\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{s}=\Bigl\langle\frac{X_{\epsilon}}{\|X_{\epsilon}\|},X_{\epsilon s}\Bigr\rangle=\Bigl\langle\frac{X_{\epsilon}}{\|X_{\epsilon}\|},P\Bigr\rangle\leqslant\|P\|,

where we have again used that
X_{s}\perp X_{\epsilon}
. Combining these observations we arrive at

\bigl(\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{s}\bigr)^{2}-\|X_{\epsilon s}\|^{2}\leqslant\|P\|^{2}-\|X_{\epsilon s}\|^{2}=-\langle X_{\epsilon},X_{ss}\rangle^{2},

as claimed.
∎

3.5.
The commutator
[\partial_{\epsilon},\partial_{s}]

Assuming (
3.4
) one has

[\partial_{\epsilon},\partial_{s}]=\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle\partial_{s}\text{ and }[\partial_{\epsilon},\partial_{s}^{2}]=2\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle\partial_{s}^{2}+\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle_{s}\partial_{s}

Proof.

The computation follows the same pattern as the derivation of (
3.3
). Here we have no equation for
X_{\epsilon}
, but we do know that
X_{\epsilon}\perp X_{s}
. Thus

\partial_{\epsilon}\|X_{p}\|=\left\langle\frac{X_{p}}{\|X_{p}\|},X_{p\epsilon}\right\rangle=\langle X_{s},X_{\epsilon p}\rangle=\langle X_{s},X_{\epsilon s}\rangle\|X_{p}\|=-\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle\|X_{p}\|.

Apply this to
\partial_{s}=\|X_{p}\|^{-1}\partial_{p}
to get the commutator
[\partial_{\epsilon},\partial_{s}]
. The other commutator follows from expanding
[\partial_{\epsilon},\partial_{s}^{2}]=[\partial_{\epsilon},\partial_{s}]\partial_{s}+\partial_{s}[\partial_{\epsilon},\partial_{s}]
.
∎

3.6.
Lemma

The length of the first variation
X_{\epsilon}
satisfies the differential inequality

\partial_{t}\|X_{\epsilon}\|-\lambda\partial_{s}\|X_{\epsilon}\|\leqslant\partial_{s}^{2}\|X_{\epsilon}\|+\kappa^{2}\|X_{\epsilon}\|.

Proof.

Differentiating the evolution equation (

) for

\displaystyle\partial_{t}X_{\epsilon}=\partial_{\epsilon}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\epsilon}\left(X_{ss}+\lambda X_{s}\right)

\displaystyle=X_{\epsilon ss}+2\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle X_{ss}+\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle_{s}X_{s}+\lambda_{\epsilon}X_{s}+\lambda\partial_{\epsilon}X_{s}.

Hence

\displaystyle\partial_{t}X_{\epsilon}-\lambda\partial_{s}X_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=X_{\epsilon ss}+2\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle X_{ss}+\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle_{s}X_{s}+\lambda_{\epsilon}X_{s}+\lambda[\partial_{\epsilon},\partial_{s}]X

\displaystyle=X_{\epsilon ss}+2\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle X_{ss}+\left\{\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle_{s}+\lambda_{\epsilon}+\lambda\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle\right\}X_{s}

We next compute the evolution of

, keeping in mind that

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-\lambda\partial_{s})\|X_{\epsilon}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\langle X_{\epsilon},\partial_{t}X_{\epsilon}-\lambda\partial_{s}X_{\epsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle X_{\epsilon},X_{\epsilon ss}\rangle+4\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\|X_{\epsilon}\|^{2}\bigr)_{ss}-2\|X_{\epsilon s}\|^{2}+4\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=2\|X_{\epsilon}\|\,\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{ss}+2\bigl(\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{s}\bigr)^{2}-2\|X_{\epsilon s}\|^{2}+4\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle^{2}.

At this point we use Lemma

(\partial_{t}-\lambda\partial_{s})\|X_{\epsilon}\|^{2}\leqslant 2\|X_{\epsilon}\|\,\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{ss}+2\langle X_{ss},X_{\epsilon}\rangle^{2}.

\partial_{t}\|X_{\epsilon}\|-\lambda\partial_{s}\|X_{\epsilon}\|\leqslant\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{ss}+\Bigl\langle X_{ss},\frac{X_{\epsilon}}{\|X_{\epsilon}\|}\Bigr\rangle^{2}\|X_{\epsilon}\|\leqslant\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{ss}+\kappa^{2}\|X_{\epsilon}\|.\qed

3.7.
Contractive property of Curve Shortening

If
X^{0},X^{1}:[t_{0},t_{1}]\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
are two solutions of Curve Shortening (
3.1
), then a homotopy
\{X^{\epsilon}:0\leqslant\epsilon\leqslant 1\}
of solutions to Curve Shortening connecting them is, by definition, a map
X:[0,1]\times[t_{0},t_{1}]\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
(t,p)\mapsto X(\varepsilon,t,p)
is a solution of Curve Shortening with
X^{\epsilon}(t,p)=X(\epsilon,t,p)
for
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
.

Given any homotopy
X^{\epsilon}
between solutions
X^{0},X^{1}
of Curve Shortening, one can always find a reparametrization
\widetilde{X}^{\epsilon}(t,p)=X^{\epsilon}(t,\varphi(\epsilon,t,p))
for which
\partial_{\epsilon}\widetilde{X}^{\epsilon}\perp\partial_{s}\widetilde{X}^{\epsilon}
holds pointwise. We will call such a homotopy a
normal homotopy.

Our main observation in this section is the following:
if
X^{\epsilon}
is a normal homotopy between solutions
X^{0},X^{1}
of Curve Shortening, then one has for each
\epsilon\in[0,1]
and

(3.6)

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{p=0}^{1}\|\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,t,p)\|\,ds\leqslant 0

and

(3.7)

\int_{\epsilon=0}^{1}\int_{p=0}^{1}\|\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,t_{1},p)\|\,ds\,d\epsilon\leqslant\int_{\epsilon=0}^{1}\int_{p=0}^{1}\|\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,t_{0},p)\|\,ds\,d\epsilon

Proof.

We use (
3.2
) and Lemma
3.6
to differentiate under the integral:

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{p=0}^{1}\|X_{\epsilon}\|\,ds\leqslant\int\left\{\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{ss}+\kappa^{2}\|X_{\epsilon}\|+\lambda\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{s}+(\lambda_{s}-\kappa^{2})\|X_{\epsilon}\|\right\}\,ds\\
=\int\bigl\{\|X_{\epsilon}\|_{s}+\lambda\|X_{\epsilon}\|\bigr\}_{s}\,ds=0.

This implies (
3.6
). Integration in
\epsilon
and in time then leads to (
3.7
).
∎

3.8.
Deviation from an approximate solution

We
now consider the case of two moving curves
X^{0},X^{1}:[t_{0},t_{1}]\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the same initial value, i.e. with
X^{0}(t_{0},p)=X^{1}(t_{0},p)
for all
p\in[0,1]
. We assume that
X^{1}
is a solution of Curve Shortening but allow
X^{0}
to be a general moving curve. We measure its deviation from Curve Shortening in terms of

(3.8)

\Delta{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{p=0}^{1}\bigl\|(\partial_{t}X^{0})^{\perp}-\partial_{s}^{2}X^{0}\bigr\|\,ds\,dt.

In the case of plane curves
X:[t_{0},t_{1}]\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
\partial_{t}(X^{0})^{\perp}=VJX^{0}_{s}
, where
V
is the normal velocity of the curve
X^{0}
. Therefore the integrand in (
3.8
) is

\bigl\|(\partial_{t}X^{0})^{\perp}-\partial_{s}^{2}X^{0}\bigr\|\,ds=|V-\kappa|\,ds.

The quantity
\Delta
therefore coincides with the “error”
{\mathcal{E}}
defined in (
1.4
).

Assume that for each
\epsilon\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
there is a smooth solution
(t,p)\mapsto X(\epsilon,t,p)
of Curve Shortening that is defined for
t\in[\epsilon,t_{1}]
, and that has initial value
X(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)=X^{0}(\epsilon,p)
. After reparametrizing we may assume that
X_{\epsilon}\perp X_{p}
holds point-wise. Then the final values of these solutions, i.e. the curves
p\mapsto X(\epsilon,t_{1},p)
form a normal homotopy from
X^{0}(t_{1},\cdot)
to
X^{1}(t_{1},\cdot)
. We will now show that

(3.9)

\int_{\epsilon=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{p=0}^{1}\|\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,t_{1},p)\|\,ds\,d\epsilon\leqslant\Delta.

Proof.

Our argument is a nonlinear version of the Variation of Constants Formula, or of Duhamel’s principle.

For
\epsilon\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
we consider

E(\epsilon){\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}}\int_{\bar{\epsilon}=t_{0}}^{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{1}\|\partial_{\epsilon}X(\bar{\epsilon},t_{1},p)\|\,ds\,d\bar{\epsilon}.

Then

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(\epsilon)=\int_{0}^{1}\|\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,t_{1},p)\|\,ds

The contraction property (
3.6
) implies

\int_{0}^{1}\|\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,t_{1},p)\|\,ds\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\|\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)\|\,ds.

We compute
X_{\epsilon}(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)
by differentiating the relation
X(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)=X^{0}(\epsilon,p)
with respect to
\epsilon
:

\bigl(\partial_{t}X^{0}\bigr)(\epsilon,p)=\frac{\partial X^{0}(\epsilon,p)}{\partial\epsilon}=\frac{\partial X(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)}{\partial\epsilon}=X_{\epsilon}(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)+X_{t}(\epsilon,\epsilon,p).

Since
(t,p)\mapsto X(\epsilon,t,p)
evolves by Curve Shortening, we have
X_{t}(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)=X_{ss}(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)+\lambda X_{s}
. By definition of
X
we have
X(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)=X^{0}(\epsilon,p)
, so
X_{ss}(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)=X^{0}_{ss}(\epsilon,p)
.

We have parameterized the homotopy
X(\epsilon,t,p)
so that
X_{\epsilon}\perp X_{p}
, and therefore

\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)=\bigl(\partial_{t}X^{0}(\epsilon,p)\bigr)^{\perp}-X^{0}_{ss}(\epsilon,p).

Hence

E^{\prime}(\epsilon)\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\|\partial_{\epsilon}X(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)\|\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\bigl(\partial_{t}X^{0}(\epsilon,p)\bigr)^{\perp}-X^{0}_{ss}(\epsilon,p)\right\|\,ds

Integrate over
\epsilon\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
to recover (
3.9
).
∎

3.9.
Application to plane Curve Shortening

Let
X^{0},X^{1}:[t_{0},t_{1}]\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be two moving curves that are embedded at all time. Assume
X^{1}
evolves by Curve Shortening, and assume that initially
X^{1}
lies in the closed region enclosed by
X^{0}
, i.e. for all
p\in[0,1]
the point
X^{1}(t_{0},p)
lies in the region enclosed by the simple curve
p\mapsto X^{0}(t_{0},p)
.

Assume furthermore that at each time
t_{*}\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
the area enclosed by
p\mapsto X^{0}(t_{*},p)
is at least
2\pi(t_{1}-t_{*})
. By the Gage-Hamilton-Grayson theorem this guarantees that the solution to Curve Shortening starting at
X^{0}(t_{*},\cdot)
exists until time
t_{1}
.

We now consider two homotopies
A
and
B
of evolving curves. The first is the homotopy defined in the proof in the previous section
3.8
, i.e. for each
\epsilon\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
we consider the solution
(t,p)\mapsto X(\epsilon,t,p)
to Curve Shortening defined for
t\in[\epsilon,t_{1}]
and starting from
X(\epsilon,\epsilon,p)=X^{0}(\epsilon,p)
. Our first homotopy is then the family of final curves
A(\epsilon,p)=X(\epsilon,t_{1},p)
of these solutions. In section
3.8
we showed that the length of the homotopy
A
is bounded from above by

\ell(A)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{1}\|A_{\epsilon}(\epsilon,p)\|\,ds\,d\epsilon\leqslant\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|(\partial_{t}X^{0})^{\perp}-X^{0}_{ss}\right\|\,ds\,dt.

The second homotopy is constructed by evolving a homotopy between the two initial curves
p\mapsto X^{j}(t_{0},p)
(
j=0,1
). Since
X^{1}
initially lies inside
X^{0}
we can choose the homotopy
(\epsilon,p)\mapsto\bar{X}(\epsilon,t_{0},p)
so that its length is exactly the area of the region between the two initial curves, and so that the curve
p\mapsto\bar{X}(\epsilon,t_{0},p)
lies inside the curve
p\mapsto\bar{X}(\epsilon^{\prime},t_{0},p)
if
0\leqslant\epsilon\leqslant\epsilon^{\prime}\leqslant 1
. Given this initial homotopy let
(t,p)\mapsto\bar{X}(\epsilon,t,p)
be the solution to Curve Shortening starting at
\bar{X}(\epsilon,t_{0},p)
. Since all initial curves enclose
X^{1}(t_{0},p)
the corresponding solutions exist for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, and possibly longer. Our second homotopy is now
B(\epsilon,p)=\bar{X}(\epsilon,t_{1},p)
. As explained in section
3.7
, the length of the homotopy
B
is bounded by the length of the initial homotopy
(\epsilon,p)\mapsto\bar{X}(\epsilon,t_{0},p)
. We had chosen this initial homotopy so that its length is exactly the area between the two curves
p\mapsto X^{0}(t_{0},p)
and
p\mapsto X^{1}(t_{0},p)
.

By concatenating the two homotopies
A
and
B
we obtain a combined homotopy
A\#B
between
p\mapsto X^{0}(t_{1},p)
and
p\mapsto X^{1}(t_{1},p)
. The length of this homotopy is bounded by

\ell(A\#B)=\ell(A)+\ell(B)\leqslant\text{Area between $X^{0}(t_{0},\cdot)$ and $X^{1}(t_{0},\cdot)$}\\
+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|(\partial_{t}X^{0})^{\perp}-X^{0}_{ss}\right\|\,ds\,dt

If
\Omega^{0}(t)
and
\Omega^{1}(t)
are the regions enclosed by
X^{0}(t,\cdot)
and
X^{1}(t,\cdot)
, then the homotopy
A\#B
between the two curves at time
t_{1}
must pass through each point in the interior of the symmetric difference
\Omega^{0}(t_{1})\triangle\Omega^{1}(t_{1})
, as one sees by considering the winding numbers of the curves in the homotopy around any point in
\Omega^{0}(t_{1})\triangle\Omega^{1}(t_{1})
. It follows that the area of
\Omega^{0}(t_{1})\triangle\Omega^{1}(t_{1})
is a lower bound for the length of the homotopy
A\#B
, and thus we conclude that

(3.10)

\text{Area of }\Omega^{0}(t_{1})\triangle\Omega^{1}(t_{1})\leqslant\\
\text{Area between $X^{0}(t_{0},\cdot)$ and $X^{1}(t_{0},\cdot)$}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|(\partial_{t}X^{0})^{\perp}-X^{0}_{ss}\right\|\,ds\,dt.

4.
Convergence

In this section, we obtain uniform curvature bounds on a sequence of “really old solutions”
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)\}
and extract a subsequence of solutions that converges locally smoothly to an ancient solution of curve shortening flow (CSF).

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists a
T
such that for any
T^{*}>T
, the curvatures
|\kappa_{j}|
of the “really old solutions”
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)\}_{j}
are bounded independently of
j
on the interval
[-T^{*}-1/4,-T^{*}]
.

The strategy to obtain these bounds is as follows: a) decompose an element
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
of this sequence into the union of several graph representations, b) use the
L^{1}
bound on the error to obtain
L^{\infty}
estimates for these graphs, and c) apply the standard estimates for divergence-form quasilinear parabolic equations to establish a uniform curvature bound.

Let
T^{*}>0
be a large positive number, which may be increased as necessary throughout this section. The obvious candidates for the sequence of “really old solutions” are the CSF solutions defined on
[-j,-T^{*}]
starting at
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(-j)
at time
-j
— call these
\Gamma_{j}(t)
. At any time
t\in[-j,-T^{*}]
, the unsigned area enclosed by the curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(t)
and
\Gamma_{j}(t)
is bounded by the quantity

\int_{-j}^{t}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(\tau)}|V-\kappa|ds\;d\tau.

By Lemma
2.1
, this quantity is in
L^{1}
and given
\epsilon>0
, we can find
T^{*}>0
such that

{\mathcal{E}}(T^{*})=\int_{-\infty}^{-T^{*}}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(\tau)}|V-\kappa|ds\;d\tau<\epsilon.

By (
3.10
), this estimate gives a uniform bound on the unsigned area between
\Gamma_{j}(t)
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(t)
for any sufficiently large
j
and
t<-T^{*}
, whenever defined.

For simplicity of calculation, we consider an alternative sequence of “really old solutions”
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)\}
, called “square-profile approximations”, which do
not
satisfy the initial condition
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(-j)={\mathcal{C}}_{*}(-j)
. They are constructed as follows: Let
[\vartheta_{*}^{-}(t),\vartheta_{*}^{+}(t)]
be the interval of polar angles that contain the approximate solution at a given time
t
. Let
r={\mathcal{R}}(\theta)
be the central branch of the Yin-Yang foliation, so the Yin-Yang solution is given by the two branches
r={\mathcal{R}}(\theta+\pi/2-t)
and
r={\mathcal{R}}(\theta-\pi/2-t)
. We define
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
to be the solution of curve shortening which at time
t=-j
is given by

•

Two arms of the Yin-Yang soliton
r={\mathcal{R}}(\theta-\pi/2-t)={\mathcal{R}}(\theta-\pi/2+j)
, and

r={\mathcal{R}}(\theta+\pi/2-t)={\mathcal{R}}(\theta+\pi/2+j)
truncated at
\theta=\vartheta_{*}^{+}(-j)
;

•

a straight line segment connecting the two arms of the Yin-Yang soliton. This segment is part of the ray
\theta=\vartheta_{*}^{+}(-j)
.

Notice that at each time
t
along the flow, the square-profile approximations
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
enclose the curves
\Gamma_{j}(t)
, the CSF solutions starting from
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(-j)
, and
that the area bounded by these two solutions stays constant along the flow, for all
t\in[-j,-T^{*}]
. The area
A_{j}
between
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(-j)
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(-j)
is small and goes to zero as
j\to\infty
. Thus, the area between the old solution
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
with the “square initial data” and the approximate solution
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(t)
is bounded by

\mathop{\textrm{Area}}({\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t),{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(t))\leqslant A_{j}+\int_{-j}^{t}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(\tau)}|V-\kappa|\,ds\,d\tau.

In order to improve these area bounds to
L^{\infty}
bounds, we will use the geometry of the
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
and several properties of CSF. In particular, we often appeal to the maximum principle and the following Sturmian property for intersections of curve shortening flows.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider two CSF solutions

\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}:[T_{1},T_{2})\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for which

\partial\gamma_{0}(t)\cap\gamma_{1}(t)=\partial\gamma_{1}(t)\cap\gamma_{0}(t)=\varnothing

holds for any
t\in[T_{1},T_{2})
. Then the number of intersections of
\gamma_{0}(t)

and
\gamma_{1}(t)
is a finite and non-increasing function of
t\in(T_{1},T_{2})
. It
decreases whenever
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
have a tangency.

There is a useful related theorem for inflections points.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\gamma:[T_{1},T_{2})\times S^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a solution of CSF.
Then, for any
t\in(T_{1},T_{2})
,
\gamma(t)
has at most a finite number of
inflection points, and this number does not increase with time. In fact, it
drops whenever the curvature
\kappa
has a multiple zero.

While the curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
are not convex, we do have a one sided curvature bound.

Theorem 4.4
.

If
\kappa
is the curvature of a counterclockwise oriented parametrization
X
of the curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
, then

\kappa-\langle X,X_{s}\rangle>0.

Proof.

Assuming that the parametrization
X
is normal (
X_{t}\perp X_{s}
), the curvature evolves by

\kappa_{t}=\kappa_{ss}+\kappa^{3}.

A short computation using
X_{t}=X_{ss}
and
\|X_{s}\|=1
shows that

\bigl(\partial_{t}-\partial_{s}^{2}\bigr)\|X\|^{2}=2\langle X_{t},X\rangle-2\langle X_{ss},X\rangle-2\|X_{s}\|^{2}=-2.

Differentiating with respect to arclength, using the commutator
[\partial_{t},\partial_{s}]=\kappa^{2}\partial_{s}
, and also
\partial_{s}\|X\|^{2}=2\langle X,X_{s}\rangle
we get

\partial_{t}\langle X,X_{s}\rangle=\partial_{s}^{2}\langle X,X_{s}\rangle+\kappa^{2}\langle X,X_{s}\rangle.

Hence
\kappa
and
\langle X,X_{s}\rangle
satisfy the same linear equation. Therefore
\sigma=\kappa-\langle X,X_{s}\rangle
also satisfies

\sigma_{t}=\sigma_{ss}+\kappa^{2}\sigma.

The quantity
\sigma
vanishes on the rotating soliton (see the appendix).

The square-profile initial curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(-j)
consist of two arcs. One is the Yin-Yang soliton, so on this arc we have
\sigma=0
. The other arc is the radial line segment on the ray
\theta=\vartheta_{*}^{+}(-j)
. On this segment we clearly have
\kappa=0
. Since we orient
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}
counterclockwise,
X
and
X_{s}
are parallel with opposite directions; i.e.
-\langle X,X_{s}\rangle>0
. Hence
\sigma>0
on the line segment. Finally, the initial curve
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(-j)
is not smooth, having two corners where the line segment and Yin-Yang arms meet. If one rounds these corners off by replacing them with small circle arcs with radius
\rho\ll 1
, then the curvature of these arcs will be
\kappa=\rho^{-1}\gg 1
, so that
\sigma>0
on the circular arcs, provided
\rho
is sufficiently small. The resulting curve has
\sigma=0
on the Yin-Yang arms, and
\sigma>0
on the line segment, as well as the small circular arcs. The solution to CS starting from the modified initial curve therefore has
\sigma>0
. Letting
\rho\searrow 0
we conclude that
\sigma>0
also holds on
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
.
∎

With Theorem
4.2
, we can decompose the solutions
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
into exactly two graphs over the polar angle parameter.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
t\in(-j,-T^{*}]
, there is an interval
[\vartheta^{-}_{j}(t),\vartheta^{+}_{j}(t)]
such that the curve
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
can be written as the union of two graphs of polar functions,
R_{j}^{-}(\theta,t)
and
R_{j}^{+}(\theta,t)
defined for
\theta\in[\vartheta^{-}_{j}(t),\vartheta^{+}_{j}(t)]
.
The functions
t\mapsto\vartheta^{-}_{j}(t)
and
t\mapsto\vartheta^{+}_{j}(t)
are strictly increasing and decreasing, respectively.

Proof.

By the maximum principle, the “really old solutions”
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
will be contained inside of
the Yin-Yang curve. The Sturmian property, Theorem
4.2
, tells us that the
number of intersections of
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
and the rays
\theta=\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is
non-increasing, and only decreases when there is a tangency. This implies that the desired graph decomposition exists. These two graphs are bounded above and below by the branches of the Yin-Yang soliton on their polar interval of definition,
[\vartheta^{-}_{j}(t),\vartheta^{+}_{j}(t)]
.
∎

as a union of two graphs taking

\theta

Lemma 4.8

(Curvature bounds)
.

There exist
T<0
such that for any
T^{\prime}<T
the lengths
L_{j}(t)
and curvatures of
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
are uniformly bounded for all
j
and all
t\in[T^{\prime},T-2]
.

Proof.

The length bounds follow from the fact that in
(y,\theta)
coordinates each
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
is contained in a uniformly bounded rectangle
|y|\leqslant\pi/2
,
-t\leqslant\theta\leqslant\vartheta_{*}^{+}(t)+2\theta_{0}+2
, and the fact that
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
decomposes into four segments on each of which both
y
and
\theta
are monotone.

Consider a given value
T^{\prime}<T
. Assume that our Lemma fails, and that along some subsequence
j
the maximal curvature of
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
with
t\in[T^{\prime},T-2]
becomes unbounded.

For
t\in[T^{\prime}-2,T]
the lengths
L_{j}(t)
of
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
are uniformly bounded by some
L>0
. It follows that

\int_{T^{\prime}-2}^{T^{\prime}-1}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)}\kappa^{2}\,ds\,dt=\bigl[-L_{j}(t)\bigr]_{T^{\prime}-2}^{T^{\prime}-1}<L.

Therefore, there is a sequence
t_{j}\in[T^{\prime}-2,T^{\prime}-1]
such that

\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t_{j})}\kappa^{2}\,ds<L.

By a Sobolev embedding theorem this implies that the curves
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t_{j})
are uniformly
C^{1,1/2}
, i.e. they are continuously differentiable, and their tangent angles
\phi_{j}
are uniformly Hölder continuous — in fact, for any two points at arclength coordinates
s_{1},s_{2}
in
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t_{j})
one has

|\phi(s_{2})-\phi(s_{1})|=\left|\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\kappa\,ds\right|\leqslant\sqrt{s_{2}-s_{1}}\sqrt{\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\kappa^{2}ds}\leqslant\sqrt{L}\sqrt{s_{2}-s_{1}}.

It follows that all
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t_{j})
are uniformly locally Lipschitz curves. Now consider the solutions to curve shortening with
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t_{j})
as initial data, i.e. consider
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{j}(t)={\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t_{j}+t)
. These solutions all exist for
0\leqslant t\leqslant T-t_{j}\geqslant T-T^{\prime}
. Supposing that along some subsequence of
t_{j}
the curvatures of the
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{j}
are not bounded for
1\leqslant t\leqslant T-T^{\prime}
, we pass to a further subsequence for which the initial curves
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{j}(0)
converge in
C^{1}
to some limit curve
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{*}
. The enclosed areas of the
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{j}(0)
then also converge, and hence, by Grayson’s theorem
[
6
]
the evolution by Curve Shortening
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(t)
starting from
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}
exists for
0\leqslant t\leqslant T-T^{\prime}
. By continuous dependence on initial data it follows that the solutions
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{j}(t)
converge in
C^{\infty}
to
{\mathcal{C}}_{*}(t)
on any time interval
[\delta,T-T^{\prime}]
with
\delta>0
. This implies that the curvatures of the
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{j}(t)
are uniformly bounded for
t\in[1,T-T^{\prime}]
, which then implies that the curvatures of
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}(t)
are uniformly bounded after all for
t\in[t_{j}+1,t_{j}+T-T^{\prime}]\subset[T^{\prime},T-2]
.
∎

Appendix A
The Yin-Yang Soliton

Hungerbühler and Smoczyk
[
8
]
proved uniqueness and existence of a rotating soliton for curve shortening that contains the origin. See also Halldorson
[
7
]
and Altschuler et.al.
[
1
]
. Here we derive its more detailed asymptotic behavior, which we use in our construction of the approximate solution.

A.1.
The Yin–Yang soliton in polar coordinates

For an evolving family of curves written in polar coordinates
X(t,\theta)=r(t,\theta){{\boldsymbol{E}}_{1}}(\theta)
the Curve Shortening Deficit is

(V-\kappa)\,ds=\left\langle X_{t}-\tfrac{X_{\theta\theta}}{\|X_{\theta}\|^{2}},JX_{\theta}\right\rangle d\theta=\Biggl\{-rr_{t}+\frac{r(r_{\theta\theta}-r)-2r_{\theta}^{2}}{r^{2}+r_{\theta}^{2}}\Biggr\}d\theta.

It follows that
X
is a solution of CSF if and only if
r(\theta,t)
satisfies

(A.1)

r\frac{\partial r}{\partial t}=\frac{rr_{\theta\theta}-r_{\theta}^{2}}{r^{2}+r_{\theta}^{2}}-1=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Bigl(\arctan\frac{r_{\theta}}{r}\Bigr)-1.

If we look for solutions of the form
r(\theta,t)={\mathcal{R}}(\theta-t)
we get an ODE for
{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)

(A.2)

-{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}=\frac{{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime\prime}-{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime 2}}{{\mathcal{R}}^{2}+{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime 2}}-1.

Hungerbühler and Smoczyk
[
8
]
observed that this equation can be integrated once. By suitably rotating the curve around the origin we can ensure that the resulting integration constant vanishes, and we therefore have

(A.3)

\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)^{2}-\theta+\arctan\frac{{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(\theta)}{{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)}=0.

We consider the soliton that passes through the origin. When this happens
{\mathcal{R}}\to 0
and
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\to\infty
, so that (
A.3
) implies
\theta\to\pi/2
. The function
{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)
is therefore defined for all
\theta>\pi/2
, and, as proved by Hungerbühler and Smoczyk,
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(\theta)>0
for all
\theta>\pi/2
.

A.2.

{\mathcal{R}}

We now show that

has an asymptotic expansion of the form

{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)=(2\theta)^{1/2}\left\{1+\frac{c_{1}}{2\theta}+\frac{c_{2}}{(2\theta)^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{c_{N}}{(2\theta)^{N}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\theta^{-N-1})\right\}\qquad(\theta\to\infty)

for any

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\theta}-\frac{1}{(2\theta)^{3/2}}+\frac{11}{3}\frac{1}{(2\theta)^{5/2}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\theta^{-7/2})

\displaystyle(\theta\to\infty)

(A.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\theta}}+\frac{3}{(2\theta)^{5/2}}-\frac{55}{3}\frac{1}{(2\theta)^{7/2}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\theta^{-9/2})

R_{\theta}={\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}(-2t)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}\bigl\{1+3\tau^{-2}-\tfrac{55}{3}\tau^{-3}+{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{-4})\bigr\}.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof of (
