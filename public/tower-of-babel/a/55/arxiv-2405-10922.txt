Kernel Expansions for High-Dimensional Mean-Field Control with Non-local Interactions
Alexander Vidal, Samy Wu Fung, Stanley Osher, Luis Tenorio, Levon Nurbekyan

Abstract
Mean-field control (MFC) problems aim to find the optimal policy to control massive populations of interacting agents. These problems are crucial in areas such as economics, physics, and biology. We consider the non-local setting, where the interactions between agents are governed by a suitable kernel. For N N agents, the interaction cost has 𝒪 ⁡ ( N 2 ) \mathcal{O}(N^{2}) complexity, which can be prohibitively slow to evaluate and differentiate when N N is large. To this end, we propose an efficient primal-dual algorithm that utilizes basis expansions of the kernels. The basis expansions reduce the cost of computing the interactions, while the primal-dual methodology decouples the agents at the expense of solving for a moderate number of dual variables. We also demonstrate that our approach can further be structured in a multi-resolution manner, where we estimate optimal dual variables using a moderate N N and solve decoupled trajectory optimization problems for large N N . We illustrate the effectiveness of our method on an optimal control of 5000 interacting quadrotors.



I
Introduction

Mean-Field Control (MFC)
[
6
]
is a class of optimal control problems that primarily focus on controlling systems comprising a large number of identical interacting agents. These problems are designed to optimize
collective
rather than individual behaviour of the agents; in other words, the goal is to control the statistical properties of the population, e.g., the distribution of the agents in the state space or the physical space. Therefore, MFC problems can be found in numerous applications, including epidemic modeling
[
2
,
41
,
13
]
, finance
[
11
,
19
,
9
]
, and water distribution
[
3
,
4
]
.

There are two primary computational challenges when solving MFC problems, the curse-of-dimensionality, and the coupling among agents arising from
non-local interactions
.
While many methods have addressed the curse-of-dimensionality when solving MFCs
[
64
,
33
,
77
,
46
]
,
a key computational challenge remains when solving high-dimensional MFC problems with
non-local
interactions;
namely, when modeling
N
agents with non-local interactions
[
56
]
, evaluation of the interaction cost has complexity
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})

at any given time
, rendering existing MFC approaches computationally expensive (see Fig.

).

I-A

Our Contribution

We propose a primal-dual framework based on kernel expansions to solve high-dimensional MFC problems with
non-local interactions
. In particular, by introducing coefficients that capture the mean-field interaction
[
55
]
, our proposed primal-dual approach decouples all the agents at the expense of the updates of a moderate number of dual variables, rendering the optimization of primal variables parallelizable. In our numerical results, we are able to approximate optimal control of 5000 quadrotor agents (see Figure (
1
)). Accompanying animated graphics and code for our experiments can be found in
https://github.com/mines-opt-ml/kernel-expansions-for-mfc
.

II
Background

We are interested in MFC problems of the form

\begin{split}\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\rho}\,\,\,&\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\rho)\\
\text{s.t. }&\partial_{t}\rho_{t}({\bf x})+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}({\bf x})f(t,{\bf x},\boldsymbol{\theta}))=0,\quad t\in(0,T),\end{split}

(1)

where
\{\rho_{t}\}
,
t\in[0,T]
, are probability densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with known
initial density
\rho_{0}
, representing the distribution of the agents in the state-space at each time
t
. The objective function

\mathcal{J}
is defined as

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\rho)=&\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}$}}\left[\,\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{s}}L(s,{\bf x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(s))+\mathcal{F}(\rho_{s})\,\right]ds\\
+&\,\,\mathcal{G}(\rho_{T}),\end{split}

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}:{\rm I\!R}^{d}\times[0,T]\to\mathcal{U}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{q}
is the control and
\mathcal{U}
is a sufficiently regular domain (see
[
28
]
see Sec. I.3, I.8-9);
the function
f:[0,T]\times{\rm I\!R}^{d}\times{\rm I\!R}^{q}\to{\rm I\!R}^{d}
models the dynamics of the agents, and
L\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is the Lagrangian that controls a particular performance criterion, e.g., kinetic energy. Note that the indistinguishability of the agents yields controls that depend (besides the time variable) only on the position of one agent at a time.

The functionals
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
model the interactions among agents within the time-horizon
(0,T)
and at the final time
T
, respectively. For the terminal interaction cost we use

\mathcal{G}(\rho_{T})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{T}}G({\bf x})

for a suitably chosen
G
.

For the non-local interactions within the time horizon
(0,T)
we use

\mathcal{F}(\rho_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{({\bf x},{\bf y})\sim\rho_{t}\otimes\rho_{t}}K({\bf x},{\bf y}),

(3)

where
K\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a positive definite kernel
[
53
, Chapter 6]
.
In our examples, the interaction term penalizes agents from being too close to one another.
Thus, we want
K({\bf x},{\bf y})
to be large when
{\bf x}
and
{\bf y}
are close and small otherwise. Common choices of kernels include a Gaussian kernel
[
56
,
1
]
or the inverse distance kernel
[
46
,
26
]
.

Fig. 2:
Non-local mean-field interaction cost per evaluation:
Average evaluation timings for mean-field interaction term (

). Averages are obtained by evaluating the mean-field interaction term three times and taking the sample average of the evaluation times.

The double expectation (or integral) in (
3
) results in a significant computational burden when solving the MFC problem (
2
) because evaluation of
\mathcal{F}
for
N
agents results in
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
complexity, see Figure
2
.

III
Primal-Dual Framework for Agent Decoupling

III-A

Saddle Point Formulation

To address the computational challenges associated with evaluation of the interaction cost (
3
), we extend the kernel expansion framework introduced in
[
49
,
50
,
55
,
56
,
60
]
to MFC problems.
We begin by approximating the kernel in (
3
) with with a quadratic (in the feature space) function

K({\bf x},{\bf y})\approx K_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})=\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf x})^{\top}{\bf K}_{r}\,\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf y}),

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\ldots,\zeta_{r})^{\top}
,

\zeta_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}({\rm I\!R}^{d})
are some basis functions or features, and
{\bf K}_{r}
is an

r\times r
symmetric positive-definite matrix. Note that when
{\bf K}_{r}

is diagonal we obtain a truncated Mercer series approximation
[
52
]
.
While there are many ways to choose
\boldsymbol{\zeta}

[
60
,
1
]
, in this work we use a neural network approximation. More details are provided in Sec.
V
. The objective function (
2
) can now be approximated using

\begin{split}&\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\rho)=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}$}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{s}}L(s,{\bf x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(s))\,ds\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}$}}\mathbb{E}_{({\bf x},{\bf y})\sim\rho_{s}\otimes\rho_{s}}K_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})\,ds\\
&+\mathcal{G}(\rho_{T}).\end{split}

(5)

Next, we rewrite the approximate interaction term in (
5
) using (
4
) to obtain

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{({\bf x},{\bf y})\sim\rho_{t}\otimes\rho_{t}}K_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{t}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf x})^{\top}\,\mathbf{K}_{r}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf y}\sim\rho_{t}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf y})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}{\bf c}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{r}{\bf c}_{t},\end{split}

(6)

where

{\bf c}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{t}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf x}),\quad t\in(0,T).

Using convex duality, we have that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}{\bf c}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{r}{\bf c}_{t}=&\sup_{{\bf a}_{t}}\left[{\bf a}_{t}^{\top}{\bf c}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{\bf a}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{r}^{-1}{\bf a}_{t}\right]\\
=&\sup_{{\bf a}_{t}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{t}}{\bf a}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf x})-\frac{1}{2}{\bf a}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{r}^{-1}{\bf a}_{t}\right].\end{split}

(7)

Hence, optimizing (
5
) is equivalent to the saddle point problem

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\rho}\sup_{\bf a}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}$}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{s}}}L(s,{\bf x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(s))\,ds+\mathcal{G}(\rho_{T})

\displaystyle\,-\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}$}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{s}}{\bf a}_{s}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf x})\,ds-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}$}}{\bf a}_{s}^{\top}\,{\bf K}_{r}^{-1}\,{\bf a}_{s}\,ds.

Interchanging the
\inf
and
\sup
and changing the sign of the objective function, we arrive at the variational formulation in
[
56
]
:

\displaystyle\inf_{\bf a}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}$}}{\bf a}_{s}^{\top}\,{\bf K}_{r}^{-1}\,{\bf a}_{s}\,ds

\displaystyle-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}$}}\mathbb{E}_{{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{s}}}L(s,{\bf x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(s))\,ds-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}$}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{s}}{\bf a}_{s}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf x})\,ds

\displaystyle\,-\,\mathcal{G}(\rho_{T})

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\partial_{s}\rho_{s}({\bf x})+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{s}({\bf x})f(s,{\bf x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(s)))=0,~s\in(0,T).

Since Lagrangian coordinates are more suitable for high-dimensional problems
[
64
,
46
,
34
,
33
]
, we reformulate (
) in Lagrangian coordinates. More specifically, let

\partial_{s}{\bf z}_{\bf x}(s)=f(s,{\bf z}_{\bf x}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(s)),\quad{\bf z}_{\bf x}(0)={\bf x},

(9)

for
s\in(0,T)
, where we suppress the dependence of
{\bf z}
on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
until it is necessary.

Then
{\bf x}\sim\rho_{0}
yields
{\bf z}_{\bf x}(s)\sim\rho_{s}
, where
\rho_{s}
is the solution of the constraints equation in (
). Consequently, (
) can be written as

\begin{split}&\inf_{\bf a}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{0}}\;\Big[\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}$}}{\bf a}_{s}^{\top}\,{\bf K}_{r}^{-1}\,{\bf a}_{s}\,ds\,\,\,\,\,\\
&-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}$}}L(s,{\bf z}_{\bf x}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(s))\,ds-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}$}}{\bf a}_{s}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf z}_{\bf x}(s))\,ds\\
&-G({\bf z}_{\bf x}(T))\Big],\\
\text{s.t.~}&\eqref{eq : dynamics}~\text{holds.}\end{split}

(10)

The function
{\bf a}_{t}
encodes information about the interactions among the agents. Indeed, the optimization over
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in (
10
)
decouples
into regular optimal control problems

\begin{split}\phi_{{\bf a}}(t,{\bf x})=&\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}$}}L(s,{\bf z}_{x}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(s))ds\\
&+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}$}}{\bf a}_{s}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf z}_{\bf x}(s))\,ds+G({\bf z}_{\bf x}(T)),\\
\text{s.t.~}&\eqref{eq : dynamics}~\text{holds.}\end{split}

(11)

Thus, once an
optimal

{\bf a}_{t}
is known, agents only need to solve decoupled optimal control problems, and the interaction information is reflected in the dependence of the cost function in (
11
) on
{\bf a}_{t}
. Hence, throughout this paper, we refer to
{\bf a}_{t}
as the
global interaction coefficients.

The function
\phi_{\bf a}
is called the value function
[
28
]
; it is the viscosity solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

\begin{cases}-\partial_{t}\phi(t,{\bf x})+H(t,x,\nabla_{\bf x}\phi(t,{\bf x}))={\bf a}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf x})\\
\phi(T,{\bf x})=G({\bf x}).\end{cases}

(12)

Eliminating the optimization over
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in (
10
) we arrive at

\inf_{\bf a}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}$}}{\bf a}_{s}^{\top}\,{\bf K}_{r}^{-1}{\bf a}_{s}\,ds\,-\,\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\rho_{0}}\phi_{\bf a}(0,{\bf x}),

(13)

which is then the dual formulation of the optimization of (
5
) and reveals again the variational nature of the global interaction coefficients.

Some remarks are in order. First, we have transformed the
coupled
optimization problem (
2
) into a saddle point problem where the agents are
completely decoupled
at the expense of introducing a moderate number of dual variables (global interaction coefficients).
That is, there is no longer a double expectation arising from (
3
) to compute the interaction between every pair of agents. Importantly, for a fixed vector function
{\bf a}
, we can solve for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in a completely parallel manner.
In this case our original control
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is the primal variable and the interaction coefficients
{\bf a}_{t}
are the dual variables.
Second, the global interaction coefficients
{\bf a}_{t}
can be
reused
. More specifically, the primal-dual problem only needs to be solved
once
. One may then solve another instance of (
). In our experiments we show how (
) can be solved for one instance using 100, 400, 800, and 1000 agents and can then reuse the corresponding
{\bf a}^{\star}_{100},{\bf a}^{\star}_{400},{\bf a}^{\star}_{800},
and
{\bf a}^{\star}_{1000}
for an experiment with a new instance of the MFC problem with 1000 agents (see Sec.
V-E
).

III-B

Discretization and Primal-Dual Algorithm

To approximate the solution to (
10
), we use the direct transcription approach
[
25
]
for simplicity. However, we note that any optimal control/trajectory generation algorithm (e.g.,
[
77
,
64
,
59
]
) can be used with our primal-dual framework when solving for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in (
10
).
Fix a uniform time discretization

\displaystyle 0=t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n}=T,

(14)

with time-step
h=t_{k}-t_{k-1}
.
We define a discrete formulation where the time integrals in (
10
) are approximated using sums over the grid (
14
) and the expectations are approximated using sample averages over
N
agents
{\bf x}_{1},\ldots,{\bf x}_{N}

drawn independently from
\rho_{\circ}
:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}({\bf a},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{h}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}$}}\,{\bf a}_{t_{k}}^{\top}\,{\bf K}_{r}^{-1}\,{\bf a}_{t_{k}}\\
&-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{h}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}$}}\,L(t_{k},{\bf z}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(t_{k}),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(t_{k}))\\
&\quad-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{h}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}$}}\,{\bf a}_{t_{k}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf z}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(t_{k}))-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}$}}\,G({\bf z}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(T)),\end{split}

(15)

where
{\bf z}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(t_{k})
is the discretized state for the
\ell^{th}
agent at time
t_{k}
obtained using, e.g., Euler’s method to solve the ODE (
9
). From now on we will use
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
{\bf a}
to denote, respectively, the matrices with entries
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(t_{k})
and
{\bf a}_{t_{k}}
, and

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{\bf x}}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(t_{1}),\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bf x}(t_{n}))
.

Importantly, we note again that
\mathcal{L}
is
separable
with respect to the agents; that is, we may write,

\mathcal{L}({\bf a},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}$}}\,\,\mathcal{L}_{\ell}({\bf a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}),

(16)

where

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{\ell}({\bf a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\ell})=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{h}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}$}}\,{\bf a}_{t_{k}}^{\top}\,{\bf K}_{r}^{-1}\,{\bf a}_{t_{k}}\\
&-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle{h}\sum_{k=1}^{n}$}}\,L(t_{k},{\bf z}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(t_{k}),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(t_{k}))\\
&\quad-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle{h}\sum_{k=1}^{n}$}}\,{\bf a}_{t_{k}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf z}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(t_{k}))-G({\bf z}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}(T)).\end{split}

(17)

Following
[
55
,
49
]
, we approximate the saddle point of (
17
) with the following primal-dual iterations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}^{k+1}=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\ell}({\bf a}^{k},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{\bf x}_{\ell}}),\quad\ell=1,\ldots,N

(18)

\displaystyle{\bf a}^{k+1}=\argmin_{\bf a}\,\mathcal{L}({\bf a},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\ell}^{k+1})+\frac{\|{\bf a}-{\bf a}^{k}\|^{2}}{2\gamma},

(19)

where
\gamma>0
is a chosen parameter and the optimization variables
({\bf a},\boldsymbol{\theta})
are initialized randomly.
We note that the primal update (
18
) can be done
in parallel
for each agent and can be solved efficiently using, e.g., L-BFGS
[
47
]
, while the dual update consists of minimizing a quadratic term with the analytic solution

{\bf a}^{k+1}_{t_{i}}=\left({\bf I}+h_{a}\mathbf{K}_{r}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\left({\bf a}^{k}_{t_{i}}+h_{a}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}$}}\,\boldsymbol{\zeta}\left({\bf z}_{{\bf x}_{l}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{k+1}}(t_{i})\right)\right)

(20)

for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, where
h_{a}=h\gamma
.
In our experiments,
\mathbf{K}_{r}
is the identity matrix because we use a kernel expansion of the form

K_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})=\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf y}),

(21)

where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
is a neural network (see Sec.
V-A
)

Mean-Field Control

V
Numerical Experiments

In order to demonstrate the effectiveness of our primal-dual framework, we conduct experiments with swarms consisting of 1000 and 5000 agents. The primal-dual formulation proves to be valuable in addressing challenges associated with large-scale swarm control where the dimensionality of the problem arising from the number of agents poses significant computational hurdles when computing the non-local interaction between agents.
The generated trajectories showcase coordinated motion of interacting agents using two types of dynamics, double integrator and quadcopter
[
12
]
.
These experiments emphasize the algorithm’s ability to numerically solve optimal control of swarms.
For all experiments, we choose the following stopping criteria:

\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k})\|\leq\epsilon_{tol}
(optimality criterion for primal variable),
\big\|{\bf a}^{k}_{t_{i}}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}$}}\,\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf z}_{{\bf x}_{l}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{k+1}}(t_{i}))\big\|<\epsilon_{tol}
for all
t_{i}
(optimality criterion for dual variable), and
\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{r}(\theta^{k})\|<\epsilon_{tol}
in order to ensure the MFC problem (
5
) is solved. Here, we choose
\epsilon_{tol}=0.5
.

V-A

Kernel Approximation

In our experiments, we use exponential interaction kernels

K({\bf x},{\bf y})=\alpha_{1}e^{-{\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|^{2}}/{2}},\quad{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(22)

We build the approximation in (
4
) in two steps. First, we find
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=(\tilde{\zeta}_{1},\tilde{\zeta}_{2},\cdots,\tilde{\zeta}_{r})
such that

\tilde{K}({\bf x},{\bf y})=e^{-{\|x-y\|^{2}}/{2}}\approx\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}({\bf x})^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}({\bf y}).

(23)

Next, we set

\boldsymbol{\zeta}({\bf x})=\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}({\bf x}).

Hence, we achieve (
4
) with
{\bf K}_{r}
being the identity matrix.

For (
23
), we train a single-layer feed-forward neural network
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
with one 100-dimensional hidden layer. The network outputs
r=50
basis functions
\tilde{\zeta}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
and has 5450 total network parameters. The network is trained on
1e5
samples drawn uniformly from the cube
[-3,3]^{3}

for
5e4
iterations using the Adam optimizer
[
38
]
with a learning rate of
1e-3
and a scheduler that sales the learning rate by
1e-1
every
1e4
iterations.
Training occurs by minimizing the sample mean squared-error between the true kernel values and our approximate kernel values generated by the network.
In order to obtain higher accuracy, we also penalize the norm of the difference of the true gradient and the approximate gradient of the kernel.
In this experiment, we obtain a validation loss of 7.70e-03, which we found to be accurate enough for our MFC experiments.

An important benefit of our primal-dual approach is that the global interaction coefficients

can be computed, saved, and then reused on future instances of the MFC problem (

{\bf a}
is obtained, only the primal problem requires optimization
regardless of the number of agents

Consequently,
{\bf a}
can be determined using
fewer

This is particularly beneficial for situations where, for

VI
Conclusions

We propose a primal-dual framework based on kernel expansions for solving mean-field control (MFC) problems. Our approach decouples agent interactions by introducing global interaction coefficients based on kernel expansions. These coefficients allow us to reformulate the MFC problem as a saddle point problem where the primal problem can be solved in parallel.
Moreover, new instances of the MFC problem can be solved at reduced costs by reutilizing the global interaction coefficients.
Our experiments show that the primal-dual framework is effective at solving MFC problems, including an optimal swarm control problem of 5000 quadrotors.
As there are evident connections between optimal control and optimal transport
[
64
,
46
]
, future works include using similar techniques in the context of generative modeling

57

and inverse MFCs
