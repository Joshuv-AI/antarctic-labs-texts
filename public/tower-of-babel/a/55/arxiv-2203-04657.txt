Uniform Estimates for Smooth Polynomials over Finite Fields
Ofir Gorodetsky

Abstract
We establish new estimates for the number of m m -smooth polynomials of degree n n over a finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} , where the main term involves the number of m m -smooth permutations on n n elements. Our estimates imply that the probability that a random polynomial of degree n n is m m -smooth is asymptotic to the probability that a random permutation on n n elements is m m -smooth, uniformly for m ≥ ( 2 + ε ) ​ log q ​ n m\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n as q n → ∞ q^{n}\to\infty . This should be viewed as an unconditional analogue of works of Hildebrand and of Saias in the integer setting, which assume the Riemann Hypothesis. Moreover, we show that the range m ≥ ( 2 + ε ) ​ log q ​ n m\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n is sharp; this should be viewed as a resolution of a (polynomial analogue of a) conjecture of Hildebrand. As an application of our estimates, we determine the rate of decay in the asymptotic formula for the expected degree of the largest prime factor of a random polynomial.

Introduction

Given a positive integer
n
, we let
\pi_{n}
be a permutation chosen uniformly at random from
S_{n}
. Given a prime power
q
, we let
f_{n}=f_{n,q}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
be a polynomial chosen uniformly at random from
\mathcal{M}_{n,q}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
, the set of monic polynomials of degree
n
over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

We say that a permutation is
m
-smooth if all its cycles are of length at most
m
. Similarly, we say that a polynomial is
m
-smooth if all its prime (i.e. irreducible) factors are of degree at most
m
. We define

\psi_{\pi}(n,m):=\#\{\pi\in S_{n}:\pi\text{ is }m\text{-smooth}\},\qquad\psi_{q}(n,m):=\#\{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n,q}:f\text{ is }m\text{-smooth}\},

(1.1)

so that
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\psi_{\pi}(n,m)/n!
and
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\psi_{q}(n,m)/q^{n}
.

Throughout the paper,
n\geq 2
,
1\leq m\leq n
and

u:=\frac{n}{m}.

(1.2)

Smoothness probabilities in
S_{n}
were studied extensively in the literature, dating back to Goncharov
[
Gon44
]
, who studied
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
in the bounded
u
regime; see
[
SL66
,
ABT03
]
for generalizations. The bounded
m
regime received attention as well
[
CHM51
,
MW55
,
WZ85
]
. Asymptotic results covering the entire range of
m
were established by Manstavičius and Petuchovas
[
MP16
]
. See also the recent works of Ford
[
For22
]
and the author
[
Gor22
]
.

Smoothness probabilities in
\mathcal{M}_{n,q}
were studied extensively as well, starting with the work of Odlyzko
[
Odl85
, App. 1]
, which was motivated by cryptography (see Pomerance’s survey for the role of smooth numbers in cryptography
[
Pom95
]
). Subsequent works on
\psi_{q}
include
[
War91
,
Lov92
,
Man92a
,
Man92b
,
PGF98
,
LP98
,
Sou
,
ABT03
,
JL06
]
and are expanded upon later.

Here we estimate smoothness probabilities in
\mathcal{M}_{n,q}
by comparing them to the corresponding probabilities in
S_{n}
, uniformly in the parameters
n
and
q
. Unless stated otherwise, constants, both implicit and explicit, are absolute. For recent results where new function field estimates are obtained by comparing to a permutation quantity, see
[
Gor17
,
EG22
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
m\geq 6\log n
, then

\frac{\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}{\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}=1+O\left(\frac{u\log(u+1)}{mq^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\right).

(1.3)

The previous asymptotic results for
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
in this range of
m
achieved only the weaker error terms
u\log(u+1)/m
(see (
1.27
) and (
1.31
)) or
1/u
(see §
1.3.2
), but with perhaps simpler main terms. For smaller
m
, we prove

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. If
8\log n\geq m\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
, then

\frac{\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}{\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}=1+O_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{un^{\frac{1+a}{m}}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\right)

(1.4)

where
a=\mathbf{1}_{2\mid m}
.

Here and throughout,
\log_{q}
is the base-
q
logarithm.
Once
m\geq 4\log_{q}n
, the error term in (
1.4
) decays faster than
O(1/u)
, the existing error term in the asymptotic result for
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
in this range of
m
(see §
1.3.2
). In Theorem
1.3
below, the error term in both theorems is improved, at least if
m
is not too close to
n
, by modifying the main term
1
in the right-hand side of (
1.4
).

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
cover the entire range
n\geq m\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
, and in particular show that

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\sim\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})

(1.5)

holds as
q^{n}\to\infty
, uniformly in that range (a short computation shows that the relative error term in (
1.5
) is
\ll_{\varepsilon}1/(nq)^{c_{\varepsilon}}
).
When
n\to\infty
and
m\leq(1-\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
, the probabilities are no longer of the same order of magnitude. Concretely, for
n
tending to
\infty
and fixed
q
, we have

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=q^{-n+o_{q,\varepsilon}(n)}

(1.6)

for
m\sim(1-\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
by
[
Sou
, Thm. 1.4]
, while, in the same limit,

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=q^{-\frac{n}{1-\varepsilon}+o_{q,\varepsilon}(n)}

(1.7)

by Proposition
1.8
. For
m=o(\log n)
the situation is even worse:
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
decays superexponentially in
n
by Proposition
1.8
, while
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\geq q^{-n}
can never decay superexponentially.

We prove a comparative result where
m
can be as small as
(1+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
. It requires the introduction of some notation. Define
x=x_{n,m}>0
by

\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}=n.

(1.8)

Observe that
1\leq x\leq n^{1/m}
. In fact, from Lemma
5.2
it follows that
x=\Theta(n^{1/m})
. Let

G_{q}(z):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}\\
\deg(P)\leq m\end{subarray}}\left(1-\left(\frac{z}{q}\right)^{\deg(P)}\right)^{-1}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z^{i}}{i}\right),

(1.9)

where
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{q}
is the set of monic irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. By (
5.3
),
G_{q}(x)\geq 1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. If
(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n\geq m\geq(1+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
, then

\frac{\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}{\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}=G_{q}(x)\left(1+O_{\varepsilon}\left(m\left(\frac{1}{q^{m/2}}+\frac{n^{3}}{q^{2m}}\right)\right)\right)

(1.10)

where
x
is defined in (
1.8
).
If
n/(\log n\log^{3}\log(n+1))\geq m\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
, then
1\leq G_{q}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}
and

\frac{\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}{\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}=G_{q}(x)+O_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{n^{\frac{1+a}{m}}\min\{m,\log u\}}{mq^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\right)

(1.11)

where
a=\mathbf{1}_{2\mid m}
.

Estimate (
1.10
) gives an asymptotic result as
n\to\infty
if
m\geq(3/2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n\to\infty
, and in any case gives a non-trivial upper bound. Estimate (
1.11
) gives an asymptotic result as
q^{n}\to\infty
. Previous asymptotic results for the range of (
1.11
) achieved only the weaker error terms
u\log(u+1)/m
(see (
1.27
) and (
1.31
)) or
1/u
(see §
1.3.2
).

In
[
Gor22
]
we show the condition
n/(\log n\log^{3}\log(n+1))\geq m
may be omitted from (
1.11
), and that a different asymptotic result holds for
m\leq(3/2-\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
.

It is natural to ask when is the function
G_{q}(x)
asymptotic to
1
. This is answered in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

If
(m-2\log_{q}n)\log q\to\infty
then
G_{q}(x)
tends to
1
, and so in particular (
1.5
) holds in this limit. If
m=2\log_{q}n+O(1)
then
G_{q}(x)-1\gg_{q}1
. If we let
m/\log_{q}n
tend to
2-\varepsilon
(
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
) then
\log G_{q}(x)=\Theta_{q}(n^{\varepsilon+o(1)})
.

We conclude with the following uniform result, holding in the entire range, but most useful when
u
is bounded.

Proposition 1.5
.

For
n\geq m\geq 1
,

0\leq\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\leq\frac{C}{mq^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}.

(1.12)

We stress that our proofs of all of our estimates are direct, in the sense that we do not make use of existing asymptotics of
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
. Rather, we bound the differences
|\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})|
and
|\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-G_{q}(x)\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})|
themselves. In the very last step of each proof we plug existing lower bounds on
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
to convert our bounds to relative bounds. We explain the proof strategy in more detail in §
2.1
.

1.1
Optimality

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
combined state

\frac{\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}{\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}=1+O_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{un^{\frac{1+a}{m}}\min\{m,\log(u+1)\}}{mq^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\right)

(1.13)

for
m\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
. This is essentially optimal in both the error term and the range. Indeed, from (
1.11
) and the lower bound for
G_{q}(x)
given in (
5.3
), we have a matching lower bound as long as
(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n\leq m\leq n/(\log n\log^{3}\log(n+1))
and
n\gg 1
.

We observe that for
m=n-1
, (
1.13
) recovers the Prime Polynomial Theorem with squareroot error term. Indeed,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }(n-1)\text{-smooth})&=1-\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is irreducible}),\\
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }(n-1)\text{-smooth})&=1-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is an }n\text{-cycle})=1-\frac{1}{n},\end{split}

(1.14)

and so from (
1.13
)

\#\{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n,q}:f\text{ is irreducible}\}=\frac{q^{n}}{n}+O\left(\frac{q^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}}{n}\right).

(1.15)

In view of Theorem
1.4
, the range of Theorem
1.2
cannot be extended to
m\geq 2\log_{q}n
. (We can extend it to
m-2\log_{q}n\to\infty
but with a worse error term.)

1.2
Expected largest prime factor

Let
L(\pi)
be the size of the longest cycle in the cycle decomposition of
\pi
. Similarly, let
L_{q}(f)
be the largest degree of a prime polynomial dividing
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.

Golomb
[
GG97
]
proved that
\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})/n
tends to a limit, known as the Golomb–Dickman constant, and approximated it as
0.624329\ldots
. Knopfmacher and Manstavičius
[
KM97
]
proved that

\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})-\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{q\log n}}\right)

(1.16)

holds uniformly in
n
and
q
. We prove the following estimate, which uncovers a transition around
\log q\asymp n\log n
.

Theorem 1.6
.

We have

c\exp(-C\max\{\sqrt{n\log n\log q},\log q\})\leq\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})\leq C\exp(-c\max\{\sqrt{n\log n\log q},\log q\}).

(1.17)

To relate
L
and
L_{q}
to smooth permutations and polynomials, observe that
\mathbb{P}(L(\pi_{n})\leq m)
is equal to
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
and
\mathbb{P}(L_{q}(f_{n})\leq m)
is equal to
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
. The identity
\mathbb{E}X=1+\sum_{i\geq 1}(1-\mathbb{P}(X\leq i))
for
\mathbb{N}
-valued random variables shows that

\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\right).

(1.18)

By definition, the total variation distance between
L_{q}(f_{n})
and
L(\pi_{n})
is

d_{TV}(L_{q}(f_{n}),L(\pi_{n}))=\sum_{m=1}^{n}|\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\\
-(\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }(m-1)\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }(m-1)\text{-smooth}))|.

(1.19)

The fact that
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\geq\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
(see Proposition
1.5
) implies that

\frac{1}{2}d_{TV}(L_{q}(f_{n}),L(\pi_{n}))\leq\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n}),

(1.20)

so Theorem
1.6
yields an upper bound for
d_{TV}(L_{q}(f_{n}),L(\pi_{n}))
:

Corollary 1.7
.

We have

d_{TV}(L_{q}(f_{n}),L(\pi_{n}))\leq C\exp(-c\max\{\sqrt{n\log n\log q},\log q\}).

1.3
Previous works

1.3.1
Dickman function and smoothness

To discuss previous results, we introduce the Dickman function
\rho\colon[0,\infty)\to(0,1]
. It is defined as
\rho(t)=1
for
t\in[0,1]
, and for the rest of its range it is defined through the delay differential equation

t\rho^{\prime}(t)+\rho(t-1)=0.

(1.21)

It is a weakly-decreasing function, that decays superexponentially:
\rho(t)=t^{-t+o(t)}

[
HT93
, Eq. (1.7)]
. It was introduced by Dickman
[
Dic30
]
in his study of smooth integers. We say that a positive integer is
y
-smooth if its prime factors are no larger than
y
. Dickman proved that, for any fixed
a>0
,

\#\{1\leq n\leq x:n\text{ is }x^{1/a}\text{-smooth}\}\sim x\rho(a)

(1.22)

as
x\to\infty
. Goncharov
[
Gon44
]
proved that

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\rho(u)+o(1)

(1.23)

as
u=n/m
tends to a positive constant; this is a permutation analogue of (
1.22
). From Proposition
1.5
and Goncharov’s result, we immediately obtain a polynomial analogue of his result:

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\rho(u)+o(1)+O\left(\frac{1}{mq^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\right)=\rho(u)+o(1)

(1.24)

as
n/m
tends to a positive constant. This polynomial analogue was first established by Car
[
Car87
]
, without making use of Goncharov’s work. Warlimont later proved the quantitative estimate
[
War91
]

\mathbb{P}\left(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth}\right)=\rho(u)+O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right).

(1.25)

A slightly weaker version of (
1.25
) was proved independently by Panario, Gourdon and Flajolet
[
PGF98
]
.

We explain how (
1.25
) easily follows from our work. We first state a quantitative version of (
1.23
), proved in
[
MP16
]
:

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\rho(u)\left(1+O\left(\frac{u\log(u+1)}{m}\right)\right)

(1.26)

for
m\geq\sqrt{n\log n}
. For
m\geq\sqrt{n\log n}
, (
1.25
) follows at once from Proposition
1.5
and (
1.26
), with an improved error term.
For
m<\sqrt{n\log n}
,
1/m
is greater than
c\rho(u)
, so (
1.25
) amounts to
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=O(1/m)
, which follows from Proposition
1.5
and (
1.26
).

For
m\gg\sqrt{n\log n}
, Manstavičius proved
[
Man92a
, Thm. 2]

\mathbb{P}\left(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth}\right)=\rho(u)\left(1+O\left(\frac{u\log(u+1)}{m}\right)\right).

(1.27)

This follows at once from (
1.13
) and (
1.26
).

Under the Riemann Hypothesis, Hildebrand
[
Hil84
]
(cf.
[
Gra08
, Thm.]
) proved that

\#\{n\leq x:n\text{ is }y\text{-smooth}\}=x\rho\left(\frac{\log x}{\log y}\right)\exp\left(O_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{\log x}{\log y}+1\right)}{\log y}\right)\right)

(1.28)

uniformly for
\log y\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log\log x
. In fact, this result also implies the Riemann Hypothesis. In the same range, Saias improved it to

\#\{n\leq x:n\text{ is }y\text{-smooth}\}=\Lambda(x,y)\left(1+O_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\log x}{y^{1/2+\varepsilon}}\right)\right)

where
\Lambda(x,y)
is a main term introduced by de Bruijn
[
dB51
]
. We find (
1.13
) akin to Saias’s result, where in the polynomial setting,
\Lambda(x,y)/x
is replaced with
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
. This analogy led us in
[
Gor22a
]
to prove a version of Theorem
1.3
in the integers, using a very similar proof method.

Hildebrand
[
Hil86
]
conjectured that the range of validity of (
1.28
) cannot be extended beyond
y\geq(\log x)^{2+\varepsilon}
. Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
show (
1.5
) does not hold in the wider range
m\geq(2-\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
, which answers a version of Hildebrand’s conjecture in the polynomial setting (as
q^{n}
and
q^{m}
correspond to
x
and
y
in integers). This observation inspired us in
[
Gor22b
]
to resolve Hildebrand’s conjecture in integers in an unconditional manner.

It is natural to ask whether the approximation
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\sim\rho(u)
holds beyond the range
m/\sqrt{n\log n}\to\infty
implied by (
1.26
). In the appendix we show that
\rho
is not an asymptotic approximation once
m/\sqrt{n\log n}
is bounded from above. A related result was proved in
[
MP16
, Cor. 3]
by a different method.

1.3.2
Saddle point analysis

Odlyzko
[
Odl85
]
used saddle point analysis to estimate
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
for
q=2
and
n^{1/100}\leq m\leq n^{99/100}
. Lovorn
[
Lov92
]
extended this to general
q
in her thesis. The range
n^{1/100}\leq m\leq n^{99/100}
is captured in full by Theorem
1.1
.

Manstavičius
[
Man92a
,
Man92b
]
extended the range of these results to
n\geq m\log m(\log\log m)^{3}
with
m\to\infty
, and proved a relative error term of order
u^{-1}+mq^{-m}
. This result is an analogue of the work of Hildebrand and Tenenbaum
[
HT86
]
, who estimated
\#\{1\leq n\leq x:n\text{ is }y\text{-smooth}\}
uniformly in the range
2\leq y\leq x
, with a relative error term of
\log y/\log x+\log y/y
. We do not give the full statements of these asymptotics as they are somewhat complicated and are not needed here. For permutations, Manstavičius and Petuchovas obtained
[
MP16
, Thm. 2, Cor. 5]

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{D(x)}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\left(1+O\left(u^{-1}\right)\right)

(1.29)

uniformly in the range
1\leq m\leq n
, where

D(x)=\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}}{j}\right)x^{-n}

(1.30)

and
\lambda=\lambda(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}jx^{j}
. Here
x
is as defined in (
1.8
). This result provides an asymptotic as long as
u\to\infty
.

1.3.3
Inequalities

Warlimont
[
War91
]
proved the upper bound
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\leq C\exp(-cu)
in
1\leq m\leq n
. Several lower bounds have been proved. Lovorn Bender and Pomerance
[
LP98
, Thm. 2.1]
proved that
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\geq n^{-u}
for
m\leq\sqrt{n}
. Joux and Lercier proved
\log\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\geq-(1+o_{m}(1))u\log u
for fixed
m
and growing
q
and
n

[
JL06
, App. A]
. Granville, Harper and Soundararajan
[
GHS15
, Ex. 6]
show that
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\geq\rho(n/m)
and state, without proof,
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\geq\rho(n/m)\exp(cn/m^{2})
. This implies the Dickman function is not a good approximation once
m/\sqrt{n}
is bounded from above. In the appendix we make this optimal and show that the Dickman function is not an asymptotic approximation if
m/\sqrt{n\log n}
is bounded.

1.3.5
Some inaccuracies

Let

where the sum is over primes up to

holds in the range

. We are not aware of a polynomial analogue of this identity. In the survey
[

\psi_{q}(n,m)-\psi_{q}(n,m-1)=\pi_{q}(m)\psi_{q}(n-m,m)

p\leq y\end{subarray}}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{p^{m}},y\right)\log p,

(1.36)

where the sum is over

Hildebrand’s identity does have a simple polynomial analogue, namely

\psi_{q}(n,m)n=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\deg(P^{k})\leq n\\

It is proved in complete analogy with Hildebrand’s original identity.

Strategy and preliminaries

Throughout the paper, the letters
C
and
c
will stand for positive absolute constants that may change from one occurrence to the next.

2.1
Strategy

We introduce the generating functions

\begin{split}F(z)&:=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})z^{n}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\psi_{\pi}(n,m)}{n!}z^{n},\\
F_{q}(z)&:=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})z^{n}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\psi_{q}(n,m)}{q^{n}}z^{n}\end{split}

(2.1)

whose analytic properties are explored in §
2.3
below. Throughout we use the notation

G_{q}(z):=F_{q}(z)/F(z).

We apply Cauchy’s formula to
F
and
F_{q}
:

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=r}\frac{F(z)}{z^{n+1}}dz,\qquad\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=r}\frac{F_{q}(z)}{z^{n+1}}dz.

One approach to studying these integrals is showing that the integrands are ‘close’ to
\hat{\rho}(s)e^{us}
after a change of variables, where
\hat{\rho}
is the Laplace transform of
\rho
. This works when
u
is ‘small’ and implies
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
and
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
are asymptotic to
\rho(u)
in some range
[
Man92a
,
Man92b
,
MP16
]
. We modify this strategy: instead of studying the polynomial and permutations probabilities individually, we study the difference

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=r}\frac{F_{q}(z)-F(z)}{z^{n+1}}dz.

We still think of the integrand as being related to
\hat{\rho}
(which influences our choice of
r
), but now our aim is to upper bound the integral which is easier than studying the probabilities individually. Ultimately, this works because the ratio
G_{q}
is close to
1
in an appropriate sense when
u
is ‘small’.

A second approach to the study of the integrals involves approximating the integrands
F(z)/z^{n+1}
and
F_{q}(z)/z^{n+1}
as gaussians by choosing the radii
r
to be
x=x_{n,m}
and
x_{q}=x_{q,n,m}
, respectively, where
x
is the saddle point with respect to
F(z)/z^{n+1}
(defined as the real positive solution to
-z(\log F(z))^{\prime}=n
) and similarly
x_{q}
is defined as the real solution to
-z(\log F_{q}(z))^{\prime}=n

[
Man92a
,
Man92b
,
MP16
]
. The saddle point
x
coincides with
x
defined in (
1.8
).
Again, we modify this approach: we study both
F_{q}(z)/z^{n+1}
and
F(z)/z^{n+1}
near the saddle point
x
associated with
F(z)/z^{n+1}
. We consider the difference

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-G_{q}(x)\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=x}\frac{F(z)(G_{q}(z)-G_{q}(x))}{z^{n+1}}dz.

We want to bound this integral, which leads to the study of
G_{q}
and its derivatives.

Our strategy shares similarities with the work of Saias
[
Sai89
]
, who pioneered the
indirect
saddle method. He studied
\Psi(x,y)
by comparing it to de Bruijn’s approximation
\Lambda(x,y)
.

2.3
Generating functions

Since
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
and
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
are between
0
and
1
, the generating series
F
and
F_{q}
defined in (
2.1
) converge absolutely in
|z|<1
and define analytic function in the open disc. We shall show that they can be analytically continued to a larger region. The logarithm function will always be used with its principal branch.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

F(z)=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z^{i}}{i}\right),

(2.4)

while for every prime power
q
we have

G_{q}(z)=\frac{F_{q}(z)}{F(z)}=\exp\left(\sum_{i>m}\frac{a_{i}}{i}z^{i}\right),\qquad a_{i}=a_{i,m,q}:=q^{-i}\sum_{d\mid i,\,d\leq m}d\pi_{q}(d).

(2.5)

The coefficients
a_{i}
satisfy, for all
i>m
,

\frac{1}{2}q^{\max\{d\leq m:\,d\textrm{ divides }i\}-i}\leq a_{i}\leq 2q^{\max\{d\leq m:\,d\textrm{ divides }i\}-i}\leq 2q^{\min\{m,\lfloor i/2\rfloor\}-i}.

(2.6)

In particular, the functions
F_{q}
and
G_{q}
are analytic in
|z|<q
, and
a_{i}=\Theta(q^{-i/2})
for even
i\in[m+1,2m]
.

Proof.

The exponential formula for permutations
[
Sta99
, Cor. 5.1.9]
states the following. Given a function
g\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
, we construct a corresponding function on permutations (on an arbitrary number of elements) as follows:

G(\pi)=\prod_{C\in\pi}g(|C|),

(2.7)

where the product is over the disjoint cycles of
\pi
. We then have the following identity of formal power series:

1+\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\in S_{i}}G(\pi)z^{i}=\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{g(j)}{j}z^{j}\right).

(2.8)

Applying the identity with
g(j)=\mathbf{1}_{j\leq m}
, we obtain (
2.4
). For
F_{q}
we have, by unique factorization in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
,

F_{q}(z)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}\\
\deg(P)\leq m\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{i\geq 0}\left(\frac{z}{q}\right)^{\deg(P^{i})}\right)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}\\
\deg(P)\leq m\end{subarray}}\left(1-\left(\frac{z}{q}\right)^{\deg(P)}\right)^{-1}=G_{q}(z)F(z)

(2.9)

and

\log G_{q}(u)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\sum_{\deg(P)\leq m}\frac{1}{j}\left(\frac{z}{q}\right)^{\deg(P)j}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z^{i}}{i}\\
=\sum_{i\leq m}\frac{z^{i}}{i}\bigg(q^{-i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\deg(P)\leq m\\
\deg(P)\mid i\end{subarray}}\deg(P)-1\bigg)+\sum_{i>m}\frac{z^{i}}{i}q^{-i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\deg(P)\leq m\\
\deg(P)\mid i\end{subarray}}\deg(P).

(2.10)

For
i\leq m
we have
\sum_{\deg(P)\leq m,\,\deg(P)\mid i}\deg(P)/q^{i}=\sum_{d\mid i}d\pi_{q}(d)/q^{i}=1
by (
2.2
), proving (
2.5
). The bound (
2.6
) now follows from (
2.3
).
∎

,
-m\pi\leq\tau\leq m\pi
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{1}{2\pi im}\int_{\Delta}e^{us}F(e^{-s/m})\,ds,\qquad\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{1}{2\pi im}\int_{\Delta}e^{us}F_{q}(e^{-s/m})\,ds

(4.17)

where
\Delta:=\{-\xi+i\tau:-m\pi\leq\tau\leq m\pi\}
. By Lemma
2.1
, as in the proof of
[
MP16
, Cor. 3]
,

\log F(z)-H_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z^{i}-1}{i}=\int_{1}^{z}\sum_{i=1}^{m}t^{i-1}\,dt=\int_{1}^{z}\frac{t^{m}-1}{t-1}\,dt\\
=\int_{0}^{m\log z}\frac{e^{v}-1}{v}\frac{v}{m}\frac{dv}{1-e^{-v/m}}=I(m\log z)+T(m\log z)

(4.18)

where
I
,
T
are defined in §
4.1
and
z\geq 1
. Hence

F(e^{-s/m})=\exp\big(H_{m}+I(-s)+T(-s)\big),

(4.19)

which holds for all
s\in\Delta
(not only
s\leq 0
) by the uniqueness principle. By (
4.5
) and (
4.19
),

F(e^{-s/m})=\exp\big(H_{m}-\gamma+T(-s)\big)\hat{\rho}(s).

(4.20)

Hence,

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{\exp\big(H_{m}-\gamma\big)}{2\pi im}\int_{\Delta}e^{us}\hat{\rho}(s)\exp(T(-s))(G_{q}(e^{-s/m})-1)\,ds.

(4.21)

We have
|\exp(H_{m}-\gamma)/m|=O(1)
, and we turn to bound the integral in the right-hand side of (
4.21
). We partition
\Delta
as
\Delta_{0}\cup\Delta_{1}\cup\Delta_{2}
, where
\Delta_{0}=\{-\xi+i\tau:-\pi\leq\tau\leq\pi\}
,
\Delta_{1}=\{-\xi+i\tau:\tau\in[-(1+u\xi),-\pi]\cup[\pi,1+u\xi]\}
and
\Delta_{2}=\{-\xi+i\tau:\tau\in[-m\pi,-(1+u\xi)]\cup[1+u\xi,m\pi]\}
. We may assume that
m\pi>1+u\xi>\pi
since we can take
n\gg 1
and
u\gg 1
. By Lemma
4.5
,
T(-s)
is bounded in
\Delta
, and so

\begin{split}\big|\int_{\Delta_{i}}e^{us}\hat{\rho}(s)\exp(T(-s))(G_{q}(e^{-s/m})-1)\,ds\big|&\leq\int_{\Delta_{i}}\big|e^{us}\hat{\rho}(s)\exp(T(-s))(G_{q}(e^{-s/m})-1)\,ds\big|\\
&\ll\exp(-u\xi)(G_{q}(e^{\xi/m})-1)\int_{\Delta_{i}}|\hat{\rho}(s)|\,|ds|\end{split}

(4.22)

for
i=0,1
. For
i=0
we have by the first part of Lemma
4.4

\int_{\Delta_{0}}|\hat{\rho}(s)|\,|ds|\ll\exp\big(I(\xi)\big)\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp(-\tau^{2}u/2\pi^{2})\,d\tau\ll\frac{\exp\big(I(\xi)\big)}{\sqrt{u}},

(4.23)

and so, by Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
,

\big|\int_{\Delta_{0}}e^{us}\hat{\rho}(s)\exp(T(-s))(G_{q}(e^{-s/m})-1)\,ds\big|\ll\rho(u)(G_{q}(e^{\xi/m})-1).

(4.24)

Similarly, for
i=1
we have by the second part of Lemma
4.4

\int_{\Delta_{1}}|\hat{\rho}(s)|\,|ds|\ll\exp\big(I(\xi)\big)\exp(-u/(\pi^{2}+\xi^{2}))(1+u\xi),

(4.25)

and so, by Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
,

\begin{split}\big|\int_{\Delta_{1}}e^{us}\hat{\rho}(s)\exp(T(-s))(G_{q}(e^{-s/m})-1)\,ds\big|&\ll\rho(u)\sqrt{u}\exp(-u/(\pi^{2}+\xi^{2}))(1+u\xi)(G_{q}(e^{\xi/m})-1)\\
&\ll\rho(u)(G_{q}(e^{\xi/m})-1).\end{split}

(4.26)

As
G_{q}(e^{\xi/m})-1=O(u\log(u+1)/(mq^{\lceil(m+1)/2\rceil}))
by Lemma
4.6
, the integrals over
\Delta_{0}
and
\Delta_{1}
contribute at most

\ll\frac{\rho(u)u\log(u+1)}{mq^{\lceil(m+1)/2\rceil}}.

(4.27)

We wish to bound the integral over
\Delta_{2}
by the same quantity. However, using the triangle inequality as before we will incur an extra factor of
\log m
coming from the integral of
|\hat{\rho}(s)|=O(1/|s|)
on
\Delta_{2}
. To remove this factor, we first replace
\exp(T(-s))
by
1
– the error obtained is acceptable, since by Lemma
4.5

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta_{2}}e^{us}

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(s)(e^{T(-s)}-1)(G_{q}(e^{-s/m})-1)ds

(4.28)

\displaystyle\ll\int_{\Delta_{2}}|e^{us}\hat{\rho}(s)T(-s)||(G_{q}(e^{-s/m})-1)||ds|

(4.29)

\displaystyle\ll e^{-u\xi}(G_{q}(e^{\xi/m})-1)\int_{\Delta_{2}}|s|^{-1}\left(\left|\frac{s}{m}\right|+\left|\frac{s}{m}\right|^{2}+\frac{u\log(u+1)}{m}\right)|ds|

(4.30)

and this is
\ll\rho(u)(G_{q}(e^{\xi/m})-1)
which we saw is at most (
4.27
). It remains to bound

\int_{\Delta_{2}}e^{us}\hat{\rho}(s)(G_{q}(e^{-s/m})-1)\,ds.

(4.31)

Lemma
2.1
shows we may write

G_{q}(e^{-s/m})-1=\sum_{i=m+1}^{4m}\frac{a_{i}\left(e^{-s/m}\right)^{i}}{i}+O\left(\frac{e^{2\xi}}{q^{m}}\right)

(4.32)

with
a_{i}=O(q^{-\lceil i/2\rceil})
. The integral
\int_{\Delta_{2}}|e^{us}\hat{\rho}(s)e^{2\xi}/q^{m}|ds
is sufficiently small (smaller than (
4.27
)). The term corresponding to
i
is bounded as follows by Lemma
4.7
:

\int_{\Delta_{2}}e^{us}\hat{\rho}(s)\frac{a_{i}\left(e^{-s/m}\right)^{i}}{i}\,ds\ll\exp\left(\xi i/m\right)\frac{\rho(u)}{mq^{\lceil\frac{i}{2}\rceil}}.

(4.33)

Summing over
m+1\leq i\leq 4m
, we see that (
4.31
) is smaller than (
4.27
). All in all,

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=O\left(\rho(u)\frac{u\log(u+1)}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}m}\right).

(4.34)

By (
1.26
) (or Theorem
A.1
),
\rho(u)\ll\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
, which gives the desired result. ∎

5
Saddle point analysis

We shall deduce Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
from the following

Theorem 5.1
.

If
\min\{n/(\log n\log^{3}\log(n+1)),\,n/3\}\geq m>\log_{q}n
then

\frac{\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}{\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}=G_{q}(x)\left(1+O\left(\frac{G_{q}^{\prime\prime}(x)x^{2}+G_{q}^{\prime}(x)xm}{nmG_{q}(x)}\right)\right).

(5.1)

5.1
Asymptotics of parameters

Recall that
x
is the positive constant defined by
\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}=n
, and that
\lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{m}jx^{j}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

[
MP16
, Lem. 9]

For
u>1
we have
|\lambda-mn|\leq mn/\log u
. For
u\geq 3
we have

x^{m}=\Theta\left(n\min\left\{1,\frac{\log u}{m}\right\}\right).

(5.2)

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
n/3\geq m\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Let
a=\mathbf{1}_{2\mid m}
. Then

1+\frac{cux^{1+a}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\min\left\{1,\frac{\log u}{m}\right\}\leq G_{q}(x)\leq 1+\frac{C_{\varepsilon}ux^{1+a}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\min\left\{1,\frac{\log u}{m}\right\},

(5.3)

and in particular,

G_{q}(x)=1+\Theta_{n,\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\right).

(5.4)

Moreover,

|G^{\prime}_{q}(x)|\leq C_{\varepsilon}\frac{nx^{a}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\min\left\{1,\frac{\log u}{m}\right\},\qquad|G^{\prime\prime}_{q}(x)|\leq C_{\varepsilon}\frac{nmx^{a-1}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\min\left\{1,\frac{\log u}{m}\right\}.

(5.5)

Proof.

By (
2.6
),

\sum_{i>m}\frac{a_{i}}{i}x^{i}\leq\sum_{i>m}\frac{2x^{i}}{mq^{\lceil\frac{i}{2}\rceil}}.

(5.6)

Since
a_{i}\gg q^{-i/2}
if
i\in[m+1,2m]
is even, we also have

G_{q}(x)-1\geq\sum_{i>m}\frac{a_{i}}{i}x^{i}\geq\frac{cx^{m}}{m}(a_{m+1}x+a_{m+2}x^{2})\geq\frac{cx^{m}x^{1+a}}{mq^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}.

(5.7)

As
1\leq x\leq n^{1/m}\leq q^{1/(2+\varepsilon)}
, the right-hand side of (
5.6
) is at most
C_{\varepsilon}(x^{m}/m)x^{1+a}q^{-\lceil(m+1)/2\rceil}
(consider even and odd
i
separately). As
x\leq q^{1/2}
, this is
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
, which also proves the upper bound for
G_{q}(x)
in (
5.3
). For the lower bound, (
5.2
) guarantees that the right-hand side of (
5.7
) is at least as stated.

As
C_{\varepsilon}ux^{1+a}=O_{n,\varepsilon}(1)
, we obtain (
5.4
). Similarly, as
x^{m}\leq n
,

\sum_{i>m}a_{i}x^{i-1}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{x^{m}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}+\frac{x^{m+1}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+2}{2}\rceil}}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\frac{nx^{a}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\min\left\{1,\frac{\log u}{m}\right\}\leq\frac{C_{\varepsilon}}{x},

(5.8)

yielding the bound on
G^{\prime}_{q}(x)
since we have the identity
G^{\prime}_{q}(x)=G_{q}(x)\sum_{i>m}a_{i}x^{i-1}
and the above shows
G_{q}(x)=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
. Considering separately
m<i\leq 2m+1
and
i\geq 2m+2
,

\sum_{i>m}(i-1)a_{i}x^{i-2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}m\left(\frac{x^{m-1}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}+\frac{x^{m}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+2}{2}\rceil}}+\frac{x^{2m}}{q^{m+2}}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\frac{nmx^{a-1}}{q^{\lceil\frac{m+1}{2}\rceil}}\min\left\{1,\frac{\log u}{m}\right\},

(5.9)

which yields the desired bound on
G^{\prime\prime}_{q}(x)
as
G^{\prime\prime}_{q}(x)=G_{q}(x)((\sum_{i>m}a_{i}x^{i-1})^{2}+\sum_{i>m}(i-1)a_{i}x^{i-2})
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Suppose
(3-\varepsilon)\log_{q}n\geq m\geq(1+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
. Letting

S=\frac{n}{q^{m/2}}\left(\frac{n^{1/m}}{\sqrt{q}}\right)^{1+a}\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil\frac{m-2}{2}\rceil}\left(\frac{n^{2/m}}{q}\right)^{j}

(5.10)

where
a=\mathbf{1}_{2\mid m}
, we have

\frac{G_{q}^{\prime}(x)x}{G_{q}(x)}=\Theta_{\varepsilon}\left(S+\frac{n^{2}}{q^{m}}\right),\qquad\frac{G_{q}^{\prime\prime}(x)x^{2}}{G_{q}(x)}=\Theta_{\varepsilon}\left(\left(S+\frac{n^{2}}{q^{m}}\right)\left(S+\frac{n^{2}}{q^{m}}+m\right)\right),

(5.11)

\log G_{q}(x)=\Theta_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{S}{m}+\frac{n^{2}}{mq^{m}}\right).

Moreover,

S\asymp\begin{cases}m&\text{if }|m-2\log_{q}n|\leq 1/\log q,\\
\left(1-\frac{n^{2/m}}{q}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{n}{q^{m/2}}\right)^{1+\frac{1+a}{m}}&\text{if }m-2\log_{q}n\geq 1/\log q,\\
\left(1-\frac{q}{n^{2/m}}\right)^{-1}\frac{n^{2}}{q^{m}}&\text{if }m-2\log_{q}n\leq-1/\log q.\end{cases}

(5.12)

Proof.

Letting

S^{\prime}=\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m}a_{i}x^{i}

where
a_{i}
are defined in (
2.5
), we have

S^{\prime}\asymp S

by (
2.6
) and (
5.2
). To prove the estimate for
G^{\prime}_{q}(x)x/G_{q}(x)
we use (
2.6
) and argue as follows:

\frac{G_{q}^{\prime}(x)x}{G_{q}(x)}=\sum_{i>m}a_{i}x^{i}\asymp S^{\prime}+\sum_{i\geq 2m}a_{i}x^{i}=\Theta_{\varepsilon}\left(S^{\prime}+\frac{n^{2}}{q^{m}}\right).

(5.13)

The estimate for
G_{q}^{\prime\prime}(x)x^{2}/G_{q}(x)
follows similarly from

\frac{G_{q}^{\prime\prime}(x)x^{2}}{G_{q}(x)}=\left(\sum_{i>m}a_{i}x^{i}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i>m}a_{i}x^{i}(i-1)=\Theta_{\varepsilon}\left(\left(\frac{G^{\prime}_{q}(x)x}{G_{q}(x)}\right)^{2}+mS^{\prime}+\frac{mn^{2}}{q^{m}}\right).

(5.14)

In the same way, the estimate for
\log G_{q}(x)
follows from

\log G_{q}(x)=\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m-1}\frac{a_{i}x^{i}}{i}+\sum_{i\geq 2m}\frac{a_{i}x^{i}}{i}=\Theta_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{S^{\prime}}{m}+\frac{n^{2}}{mq^{m}}\right).

(5.15)

We estimate
S
by the following general estimate for geometric sums: for
a>0
we have that
\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}a^{i}
is
\Theta(d)
if
|a-1|\leq c/d
, is
\Theta(a^{d}/(a-1))
if
a-1\geq c/d
and is
\Theta(1/(1-a))
if
a-1\leq-c/d
.
∎

5.2
Proof of Theorem
5.1

Since
F
is entire, we have, by Cauchy’s integral formula,

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=x}\frac{F(z)}{z^{n+1}}\,dz.

(5.16)

The parametrization
z=xe^{it}
,
t\in[-\pi,\pi]
shows, by Lemma
2.1
, that

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}e^{itj}}{j}-n(it+\log x)\right)\,dt\\
=\frac{D(x)}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}(e^{itj}-1)}{j}-int\right)\,dt,

(5.17)

where
D(x)
is defined in (
1.30
).
As
F_{q}
is analytic in
|z|<q
, and
x<q
, we similarly have, by Lemma
2.1
, that

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=x}\frac{F_{q}(z)}{z^{n+1}}\,dz=\frac{D(x)}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}(e^{itj}-1)}{j}-int\right)G_{q}(xe^{it})\,dt.

(5.18)

Hence,

\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})G_{q}(x)\\
=\frac{D(x)}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}(e^{itj}-1)}{j}-int\right)(G_{q}(xe^{it})-G_{q}(x))\,dt.

(5.19)

By assumption,
u\geq 3
. Let
t_{0}=n^{-1/3}m^{-2/3}
and
t_{1}=1/m
. We shall bound separately the contribution of
|t|\leq t_{0}
,
t_{0}\leq|t|\leq t_{1}
and
t_{1}\leq|t|\leq\pi
to the right-hand side of (
5.19
). For
k=1,2,3
, let

I_{k}=\int_{A_{k}}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}(e^{itj}-1)}{j}-int\right)\left(G_{q}(xe^{it})-G_{q}(x)\right)\,dt,

(5.20)

where
A_{1}=[-t_{0},t_{0}]
,
A_{2}=[-t_{1},t_{1}]\setminus A_{1}
and
A_{3}=[-\pi,\pi]\setminus A_{2}
. A second-order Taylor approximation shows that for
|t|\leq\pi
,

\begin{split}G_{q}(xe^{it})-G_{q}(x)&=G^{\prime}_{q}(x)(xe^{it}-x)+O\left(G^{\prime\prime}_{q}(x)\left|xe^{it}-x\right|^{2}\right)\\
&=iG^{\prime}_{q}(x)xt+O\left(\left(G^{\prime\prime}_{q}(x)x^{2}+G^{\prime}_{q}(x)x\right)t^{2}\right).\end{split}

(5.21)

For
|t|=O(1/m)
and
1\leq j\leq m
we have
e^{itj}-1=itj-(tj)^{2}/2+O(|t|^{3}j^{3})
, so that

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}(e^{itj}-1)}{j}-int=it\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}-n\right)-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}j+O\left(|t|^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}j^{2}\right)=-\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2}+O\left(|t|^{3}\lambda_{2}\right)

(5.22)

where

\lambda_{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}j^{2}\leq m\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}j=m\lambda.

(5.23)

In
A_{1}
we have
|t|^{3}\lambda_{2}=O(n^{-1}m^{-2}m\lambda)=O(1)
by Lemma
5.2
. By (
5.21
) and (
5.22
),

I_{1}=\int_{-t_{0}}^{t_{0}}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2}\right)\left(iG^{\prime}_{q}(x)xt+O\left(|t|^{2}a+|t|^{4}b\lambda_{2}+|t|^{5}a\lambda_{2}\right)\right)\,dt

(5.24)

for
a=G_{q}^{\prime\prime}(x)x^{2}+G_{q}^{\prime}(x)x
,
b=G^{\prime}_{q}(x)x
. We have
\int_{-t_{0}}^{t_{0}}\exp(-\lambda t^{2}/2)t\,dt=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\lambda t^{2}/2)|t|^{k}\,dt\ll\lambda^{-(k+1)/2}
for
k=2,4,5
. Hence

|I_{1}|\ll\lambda^{-3/2}a+\lambda^{-5/2}b\lambda_{2}+\lambda^{-3}a\lambda_{2}\ll\frac{1}{\lambda^{3/2}}\left(a+bm+\frac{am}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right).

(5.25)

As
n\geq 3m
, we have
\lambda\geq cnm
by Lemma
5.2
. Thus

|I_{1}|\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\frac{a+bm}{nm}.

(5.26)

We turn our attention to
I_{2}
. We have
1-\cos s\geq cs^{2}
in
|s|\leq\pi
, and so

\left|\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}(e^{itj}-1)}{j}-int\right)\right|\leq\exp\left(-c\lambda t^{2}\right)

(5.27)

for
t\in A_{2}
. Moreover,
|G(x)-G(xe^{it})|\ll b|t|
, yielding

|I_{2}|\ll b\int_{A_{2}}|t|\exp\left(-c\lambda t^{2}\right)\,dt\ll b\exp\left(-c\lambda t_{0}^{2}\right)\int_{A_{2}}|t|\,dt\ll\frac{b}{m^{2}}\exp\left(-c\lambda t_{0}^{2}\right)\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\frac{b}{n}.

(5.28)

We turn our attention to
I_{3}
. We first treat the case
m\geq 4
. We have
|G_{q}(xe^{it})-G_{q}(x)|\ll b
for
|t|\leq\pi
. By
[
MP16
, Lem. 12]
,

\max_{1/m\leq|t|\leq\pi}\Re\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}(e^{itj}-1)}{j}\leq-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{u^{1-\frac{4}{m+1}}}{\log^{2}u}+\frac{2}{m}+\frac{2}{\log u}

(5.29)

for
n/m\geq 3
. Hence

\begin{split}|I_{3}|&\ll b\max_{1/m\leq|t|\leq\pi}\left|\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x^{j}(e^{itj}-1)}{j}-int\right)\right|\\
&\ll b\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{u^{1-\frac{4}{m+1}}}{\log^{2}u}+\frac{2}{m}+\frac{2}{\log u}\right)\\
&=\frac{b}{n\sqrt{\lambda}}\exp\left(\frac{\log\lambda}{2}+\log n-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{u^{1-\frac{4}{m+1}}}{\log^{2}u}+\frac{2}{m}+\frac{2}{\log u}\right).\end{split}

(5.30)

We want to show that the expression in the exponent is bounded by a constant from above, and then the upper bound for
|I_{3}|
will match that of
|I_{1}|
. The terms
2/m
and
2/\log u
are already bounded. The term
\log\lambda/2
is bounded by
C+(\log mn)/2
by Lemma
5.2
, so it suffices to bound

S(m,u):=2\log m+\frac{3\log u}{2}-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{u^{1-\frac{4}{m+1}}}{\log^{2}u}.

(5.31)

We may also assume
n\gg 1
, since otherwise
S(m,u)
is trivially bounded. As
m\geq 4
, if
4\log u>m+1
we have

S(m,u)\leq 2\log m+\frac{3\log u}{2}-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{u^{1-\frac{4}{5}}}{\log^{2}u}\leq 2\log(4\log u-1)+\frac{3\log u}{2}-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{u^{\frac{1}{5}}}{\log^{2}u},

(5.32)

which is bounded for
u\geq 3
. If
4\log u\leq m+1
and
n
is sufficiently large,

\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{u^{1-\frac{4}{m+1}}}{\log^{2}u}\geq\frac{cu}{\log^{2}u}\geq\frac{c\log n\log^{3}\log(n+1)}{\log^{2}\log(n+1)}\geq\frac{7\log n}{2}\geq 2\log m+\frac{3\log u}{2}

(5.33)

using the condition
m\leq n/(\log n\log^{3}\log(n+1))
, and so
S(m,u)\leq 0
. All in all,
S(m,u)
is bounded. If
1\leq m\leq 3
, we run the same argument but with the naive bound
\Re\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}(e^{itj}-1)/j\leq-cn^{1/m}/m
for
1/m\leq|t|\leq\pi
coming from considering only the first term in the sum. In summary,

|I_{3}|\ll\frac{b}{n\sqrt{\lambda}}.

(5.34)

By (
5.19
) and our bounds on
I_{i}
, we find that

\left|\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})G_{q}(x)\right|\ll\frac{D(x)}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\frac{a+bm}{nm}.

(5.35)

By (
1.29
),

Proof of Theorem
1.6

We shall utilize identity (
1.18
). We shall also use the fact that
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\geq\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
, see Proposition
1.5
. For
m=1
we have strict inequality.

We first assume
n\log n\leq\log q
. In this range we must prove
c/q^{C}\leq\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})\leq C/q^{c}
. Since
L(\pi_{n})
and
L_{q}(f_{n})
are bounded from above by
n
, we can bound the difference
\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})
by
n
times the total variation distance of the two random variables, which is known to be
O(1/q)

[
ABT93
, Thm. 6.1]
(cf.
[
BSG18
]
), so we have

\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})=O\left(\frac{n}{q}\right).

(6.1)

This shows
\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})\leq C\log q/q
. To produce a lower bound, we consider the contribution of
1
-smooth polynomials and permutations to (
1.18
):

\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})\geq\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }1\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }1\text{-smooth})=\frac{\binom{q+n-1}{n}}{q^{n}}-\frac{1}{n!}\\
=\frac{1}{n!}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1+\frac{i-1}{q}\right)-1\right)\geq cn^{-n}\frac{n^{2}}{q}\geq\frac{c}{q^{2}}.

(6.2)

We turn to the case
n\log n\geq\log q
; we may assume
n\gg 1
. To prove an upper bound, we use Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, and the monotonicity of
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
in
m
to obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})-\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\right)&\leq M\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }M\text{-smooth})+\sum_{m>M}\frac{n\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})}{mq^{m/2}}\\
&\ll M\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }M\text{-smooth})+\frac{n}{q^{M/2}}\end{split}

(6.3)

for any
M\geq 3\log_{q}n
. We take
M=\lceil A\sqrt{n\log n/\log q}\rceil
for large
A
(admissible if
n\gg 1
). The term
n/q^{M/2}
is
\leq C\exp(-c\sqrt{n\log n\log q})
. By Proposition
1.8
, so is
M\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }M\text{-smooth})
.

We now prove a lower bound for
\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})
. Considering only the term
m=M
in (
1.18
), and using Theorem
1.3
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}L(\pi_{n})-\mathbb{E}L_{q}(f_{n})\geq\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }M\text{-smooth})\left(G_{q}(x)-1+O\left(\frac{x^{1+a}\min\{1,\frac{\log u}{M}\}}{q^{\lceil\frac{M+1}{2}\rceil}}\right)\right).

(6.4)

The term
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }M\text{-smooth})
is
\geq c\exp(-C\sqrt{n\log n\log q})
by Proposition
1.8
. By (
5.3
),

G_{q}(x)-1\geq c\frac{ux^{1+a}\min\{1,\frac{\log u}{M}\}}{q^{\lceil\frac{M+1}{2}\rceil}}.

(6.5)

As the right-hand side of (
6.5
) dominates the error term in (
6.4
) (if
n\gg 1
) and
1/q^{\lceil(M+1)/2\rceil}
is bounded from below by
c\exp(-C\sqrt{n\log n\log q})
, we conclude the proof. ∎

Deriving Soundararajan’s result from Ford’s result

7.1
Proof of Proposition
1.8

We will use two results of Hildebrand on ratios of
\rho
values. By
[
Hil84
, Lem. 1]
we have

\frac{\rho(u-t)}{\rho(u)}=e^{t\xi}(1+O(u^{-1}))

(7.1)

uniformly for
0\leq t\leq 1
and
u\geq 1
, where
\xi\geq 0
is as in (
4.2
) and it satisfies
\xi\sim\log u
by Lemma
4.2
. The second result, given in
[
Hil86
, Lem. 1(vi)]
, states that

\frac{\rho(u-t)}{\rho(u)}\ll(u\log^{2}(u+1))^{t}

(7.2)

uniformly for
u\geq 1
and
0\leq t\leq u
.
By (
1.32
), it suffices to show that

\rho\left(\frac{n+1}{m+1}\right)-\rho\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)\ll\rho\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)\left(\exp\left(C\frac{u\log(u+1)}{m}\right)-1\right).

We express the left-hand side as

\int_{\frac{n}{m}}^{\frac{n+1}{m+1}}\rho^{\prime}(t)dt.

As
\rho^{\prime}(u)=-\rho(u-1)/u
, this is

\ll\frac{\rho\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)}{\frac{n}{m}}\int_{\frac{n+1}{m+1}}^{\frac{n}{m}}\frac{\rho(t-1)}{\rho\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)}dt.

If
u
is bounded we are done, because the integral is
\ll_{u}n/m-(n+1)/(m+1)\ll_{u}1/n
. We assume
u\geq 2
. Applying (
7.2
) with
(u,t)=(u-1,u-t)
, and also invoking (
7.1
), we see that the last expression is

\ll\frac{\rho\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)}{\frac{n}{m}}\int_{\frac{n+1}{m+1}}^{\frac{n}{m}}(u\log^{2}u)^{u-t}\frac{\rho(u-1)}{\rho(u)}dt\ll\rho(u)\log u\int_{0}^{\frac{n-m}{m(m+1)}}(u\log^{2}u)^{s}ds\ll\rho(u)((u\log^{2}u)^{\frac{u-1}{m+1}}-1),

which is
\ll\rho(u)(\exp(Cu\log u/m)-1)
as needed. ∎

7.2
Proof of Theorem
1.10

By Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, the required estimate already holds in the range
n\geq m\geq(2+\varepsilon)\log_{q}n
(with a better error term). Moreover, for bounded
n
this is trivial, so we may assume
n\gg 1
.
It remains to prove a result in the range
3\log_{q}n\geq m\geq\log_{q}(n\log n)
,
n\gg 1
.
From this point on we shall assume
m
lies in the (slightly) wider range
\log_{q}n<m\leq 3\log_{q}n
,
n\gg 1
, and we shall work out what lower bound on
m
is needed for our result to hold. Since
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\geq\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})
by Proposition
1.5
, it suffices to show
\mathbb{P}(f_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})/\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})\leq\exp(Cu\log u/m)
.

By Theorem
5.1
, it suffices to prove the following 3 bounds:

\displaystyle\log G_{q}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i>m}\frac{a_{i}x^{i}}{i}\ll\frac{u\log u}{m},

(7.3)

\displaystyle\frac{G_{q}^{\prime}(x)x}{nG_{q}(x)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i>m}\frac{a_{i}x^{i}}{n}\ll\exp\left(\frac{Cu\log u}{m}\right),

(7.4)

\displaystyle\frac{G_{q}^{\prime\prime}(x)x^{2}}{nmG_{q}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nm}\left(\sum_{i>m}a_{i}x^{i}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i>m}(i-1)a_{i}x^{i}\ll\exp\left(\frac{Cu\log u}{m}\right).

(7.5)

In the range
\log_{q}n<m\leq 3\log_{q}n
we have
u\log u/m\asymp(n/\log n)\log^{2}q
. In particular,
\exp(Cu\log u/m)
can absorb any power of
n
,
m
and
q
. This allows us to reduce the last 3 inequalities to the following:

\begin{split}\sum_{2m\geq i>m}a_{i}x^{i}&\ll n\log q,\\
\sum_{i>2m}\frac{a_{i}x^{i}}{i}&\ll\frac{n\log^{2}q}{\log n},\\
\sum_{i>2m}ia_{i}x^{i}&\ll\exp\left(\frac{Cn\log^{2}q}{\log n}\right).\end{split}

(7.6)

We will now use freely the bounds
a_{i}\ll q^{-i/2},q^{m-i}
, given in (
2.6
), as well as the estimate
x\leq n^{1/m}<q
. To prove the first inequality in (
7.6
) it suffices to show

\sum_{2m\geq i>m}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{q}}\right)^{i}\ll n\log q.

If
x\leq\sqrt{q}
, this is trivial because the left-hand side is
\leq m
. If
x/\sqrt{q}
is greater then
1.1
then the left-hand side is
\ll x^{2m}/q^{m}\ll n^{2}/q^{m}<n
. In the remaining case
1\leq x/\sqrt{q}\leq 1.1
the left-hand side is
\ll m(1.1)^{2m}\ll\log n(1.1)^{6\log_{q}n}\ll n
. Here we used
6\log_{q}1.1<0.9<1
.

We turn our attention to the second inequality in (
7.6
). It suffices to show

\sum_{i>2m}\frac{(x/q)^{i}}{i}\ll q^{-m}\frac{n\log^{2}q}{\log n}.

The left-hand side is
\ll(1-x/q)^{-1}(x/q)^{2m+1}/m\ll(1-x/q)^{-1}n^{2}/(q^{2m}\log_{q}n)
, so it suffices to show

\left(1-\frac{x}{q}\right)^{-1}\ll\frac{q^{m}\log q}{n}.

(7.7)

We write
m
as
m=\log(nf)/\log q
for
f\geq 1
. As
x\leq n^{1/m}
, it suffices to show

1-\exp\left(-\frac{\log q\log f}{\log(nf)}\right)\gg\frac{1}{f\log q}

which evidently holds if
f\geq\log n
.
We turn our attention to the last inequality in (
7.6
), which will follow from

\sum_{i>2m}\left(\frac{x}{q}\right)^{i}i\ll\exp\left(\frac{Cn\log^{2}q}{\log n}\right).

Writing
i=j+2m+1
, the left-hand side is

\ll\left(\frac{x}{q}\right)^{2m+1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{x}{q}\right)^{j}(j+2m+1)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{q^{m}}\left(2m\left(1-\frac{x}{q}\right)^{-1}+\left(1-\frac{x}{q}\right)^{-2}\right)\ll n^{2}\left(1-\frac{x}{q}\right)^{-2}.

The required inequality now follows from (
7.7
), which holds if
m\geq\log(n\log n)/\log q
.
∎

Appendix A
Limitation to approximation via the Dickman function

We define a quantity
\Delta(n,m)
by

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\rho(u)(1+\Delta(n,m)).

By (
1.26
),
\Delta(n,m)=O(u\log(u+1)/m)
if
n\log n\leq m^{2}
. We prove a matching lower bound.

Theorem A.1
.

For
n>m
,
\Delta(n,m)>0
. If furthermore
m\leq n/2
, then
\Delta(n,m)\gg u\log(u)/m
.

The inequality
\Delta>0
is not new, see
[
GHS15
, Ex. 6]
. Note that if
m
is very close to
n
, we do not expect the lower bound
\Delta(n,m)\gg u\log(u)/m
, e.g.
\Delta(n,n-1)=O(1/n^{2})=o(u\log(u+1)/m)
.

An asymptotic result for
\Delta
, in a restricted range, was proved by Manstavičius and Petuchovas in
[
MP16
, Cor. 3]
. They essentially show that
1+\Delta(n,m)\sim\exp(u\xi/2m)
for
n^{1/3+\varepsilon}\leq m\leq n^{1-\varepsilon}
, where
\xi
is as in (
4.2
). Their proof method is different than ours and is based on expressing the main term
D(x)/\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}
appearing in (
1.29
) in terms of
\rho
. Our proof is inspired by
[
Hil84
]
.

See
[
Gor22b
]
for an exploration of the inequality
\Psi(x,y)\geq x\rho(\log x/\log y)
motivated by a question of Pomerance.

A.1
Preparatory lemmas

Let

S_{1}(n,m):=\frac{1}{u\rho(u)}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho\left(u-\frac{i}{m}\right)-\int_{0}^{1}\rho(u-t)dt\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\rho(u-\frac{i}{m})}{\rho(u)}-1,

where the second equality follows from the identity
\int_{0}^{1}\rho(u-t)dt=u\rho(u)
. Let

S_{2}(n,m):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\rho(u-\frac{i}{m})\Delta(n-i,m)}{\rho(u)}.

Lemma A.2
.

For
n\geq m\geq 1
we have the relation
\Delta(n,m)=S_{1}(n,m)+S_{2}(n,m)
.

Proof.

By differentiating (
2.4
) and equating coefficients we have, for
n\geq m
,

n\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n-i}\text{ is }m\text{-smooth}).

Rewriting this in terms of
\Delta(n,m)
yields the result.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

If
n\geq m\geq 1
we have
S_{1}(n,m)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
n=m
.

Proof.

Since
\rho
is strictly decreasing for
u\geq 1
, for each
1\leq i\leq m
we have

\frac{1}{m}\rho\left(u-\frac{i}{m}\right)\geq\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}\rho(u-t)\,dt

with equality if and only if
u\leq(i-1)/m+1
. The result follows by summing over
i=1,\ldots,m
.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

For positive
x
let
F(x)=(1-e^{-x})^{-1}-x^{-1}\geq 1/2
. For
m\leq n/2
we have

S_{1}(n,m)=(1+o_{u\to\infty}(1))\frac{\log u}{m}F\left(\frac{\log(u\log u)}{m}\right).

Moreover, the
1+o_{u\to\infty}(1)
term is bounded away from
0
(that is, there is a uniform lower bound).

Proof.

We write

S_{1}(n,m)=\frac{1}{u\rho(u)}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}\left(\rho\left(u-\frac{i}{m}\right)-\rho(u-t)\right)dt=\frac{-1}{u\rho(u)}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}\int_{t}^{i/m}\rho^{\prime}(u-x)dx\,dt.

As
\rho^{\prime}(u)=-\rho(u-1)/u
and
\rho(u-1)>0
, this becomes

S_{1}(n,m)=\frac{1+O(u^{-1})}{u^{2}\rho(u)}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}\int_{t}^{i/m}\rho\left(u-x-1\right)dx\,dt.

By applying (
7.1
) to
\rho(u-x-1)/\rho(u-1)
and to
\rho(u-1)/\rho(u)
, this is

\displaystyle S_{1}(n,m)

\displaystyle=(1+o_{u\to\infty}(1))\frac{\log u}{u}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}\int_{t}^{i/m}(u\log u)^{x}dx\,dt

(A.1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+o_{u\to\infty}(1)}{\log(u\log u)}\frac{\log u}{u}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}((u\log u)^{i/m}-(u\log u)^{t})dt

(A.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+o_{u\to\infty}(1)}{u}\left(\frac{u\log u-1}{m(1-(u\log u)^{-1/m})}-\frac{u\log u-1}{\log(u\log u)}\right)

(A.3)

\displaystyle=(1+o_{u\to\infty}(1))\frac{\log u}{m}F\left(\frac{\log(u\log u)}{m}\right)

(A.4)

and the estimate follows. Running the proof when
u\geq 2
is bounded shows that the term
1+o_{u\to\infty}(1)
is
\geq c
.
∎

A.2
Proof of Theorem
A.1

Positivity follows by direct induction from Lemmas
A.2
and
A.3
. Suppose
n/2\geq m
. We introduce

a(n):=\frac{\Delta(n,m)}{\log(n/m)/m}

which by the recurrence in Lemma
A.2
and the estimates (
7.1
) and Lemma
A.4
satisfies the relation

a(n)\geq c+\frac{(1+o_{u\to\infty}(1))}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a(n-i)(u\log u)^{i/m}

for
n/2\geq m
. Moreover,
a(n)\gg 1
by Lemma
A.4
and the non-negativity of
S_{2}
.

Observe that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u\log u)^{i/m}\geq n(1+o_{u\to\infty}(1))
. Hence, if
a(n),\ldots,a(n+m-1)\geq A
then

a(n+m),\ldots,a(n+2m-1)\geq c+A(1+o_{u\to\infty}(1))\geq(c/2)+A
for
u\gg 1
. Iterating this implication yields
a(n)\gg n/m
for
u\gg 1
, implying
\Delta(n,m)\gg u(\log u)/m
, as needed. ∎
