Matrix product states, geometry, and invariant theory
Tim Seynnaeve

Abstract
. Matrix product states play an important role in quantum information theory to represent states of many-body systems. They can be seen as low-dimensional subvarieties of a high-dimensional tensor space. In these notes, we consider two variants: homogeneous matrix product states and uniform matrix product states. Studying the linear spans of these varieties leads to a natural connection with invariant theory of matrices. For homogeneous matrix product states, a classical result on polynomial identities of matrices leads to a formula for the dimension of the linear span, in the case of 2x2 matrices.

1.
Invariant theory of matrices

In this section we give an introduction to the invariant theory of matrices. The exposition is based on the first chapters of
[
DCP17
]
. We work over the field of complex numbers.

1.1.
The invariant ring of a group action

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional representation of a group
G
. Then the ring

\mathbb{C}[V]=S^{\bullet}(V^{*})

of polynomial functions on
V
comes with an action of
G
, given by

(g\cdot f)(v)=f(g^{-1}\cdot v)

for
f\in\mathbb{C}[V]
,
g\in G
, and
v\in V
. The
invariant ring
is defined as

\mathbb{C}[V]^{G}=\{f\in\mathbb{C}[V]\mid g\cdot f=f\quad\forall g\in G\}.

A central question in invariant theory is to, for a given
G
and
V
, describe this ring, i.e.

(1)

Find generators for
\mathbb{C}[V]^{G}
,

(2)

Find the (polynomial) relations between these generators.

Example 1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
be the symmetric group of order
d!
, acting on
V=\mathbb{C}^{d}
by permuting the coordinates. The invariant ring is the ring

\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}}

of symmetric polynomials. It is known that this ring is generated by the elementary symmetric polynomials
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
, where

e_{k}:=\sum_{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}}{x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}}.

Furthermore, the
e_{i}
are algebraically independent. In other words, the map

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}]

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{d}}

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto e_{i}

is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{C}
-algebras.

1.2.
The invariant ring of matrices

For the rest of this section, we will be concerned with one particular group action. We fix natural numbers
m
and
n
, and take
G=GL_{m}(\mathbb{C})
and
V=\mathrm{Mat}(m\times m,\mathbb{C})^{n}
the space of
n
-tuples of
m\times m
matrices. The action of
G
on
V
is given by simultaneous conjugation:

g\cdot(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})=(gA_{1}g^{-1},\ldots,gA_{n}g^{-1}).

We will write

\mathbb{C}[V]=\mathbb{C}[x^{k}_{ij}\mid 1\leq i,j\leq m;1\leq k\leq n]=\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}],

where
X_{k}=(x^{k}_{ij})_{ij}
is an
m\times m
matrix with generic entries. I.e. when we write
f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
, this means a polynomial in
nm^{2}
variables. The invariant ring is given by

\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{GL_{m}}=\{f\mid f(gX_{1}g^{-1},\ldots,gX_{n}g^{-1})=f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\quad\forall g\in GL_{m}\}.

Here the notation
f(gX_{1}g^{-1},\ldots,gX_{n}g^{-1})
means that we substitute the variable
x^{k}_{ij}
with the
ij
-th entry of the matrix
gX_{k}g^{-1}
.

Note that
\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
admits an
\mathbb{N}^{n}
grading, by putting
\deg(x^{k}_{ij})
equal to the
k
-th basis vector
e_{k}=(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. It is not hard to see that a polynomial is invariant if and only if its graded parts are invariant; hence the invariant ring
\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{GL_{m}}
inherits the aforementioned grading.
The trick to describing this invariant ring is to start with its multilinear part

\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{GL_{m}}_{(1,\ldots,1)}.

1.3.
The multilinear part

The key ingredient is Schur-Weyl duality, a fundamental result in representation theory. We briefly state it here, and refer the reader to e.g.
[
FH91
, Chapter 6]
for a more in-depth discussion.

Theorem 1.2
.

Consider the vector space
(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{\otimes n}
, equipped with the following actions of the general linear group
GL_{m}
and the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
:

(1)

\displaystyle g\cdot(v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{n})

\displaystyle=(g\cdot v_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes(g\cdot v_{n}),

(2)

\displaystyle\sigma\cdot(v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{n})

\displaystyle=v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}.

These actions commute, meaning that we get a ring homomorphism

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]\to\End_{GL_{m}}((\mathbb{C}^{m})^{\otimes n}).

This homomorphism is surjective. If
n\leq m
, it is an isomorphism. If
n>m
, its kernel is given by the two-sided ideal generated by the antisymmetrizer

c_{m+1}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m+1}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sgn(\sigma)\cdot\sigma.

We will denote this ideal by
I_{m,n}
.

The theorem below describes the multilinear part of
\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{GL_{m}}
as a quotient of the symmetric group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
. We will write elements in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
by cycle notation.

Theorem 1.3
.

There is a surjective linear map

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]

\displaystyle\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{GL_{m}}_{(1,\ldots,1)}

\displaystyle(i_{1}\ldots i_{k})\cdots(j_{1}\ldots j_{\ell})

\displaystyle\mapsto\Tr(X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}})\cdots\Tr(X_{j_{1}}\cdots X_{j_{\ell}}).

If
n\leq m
, it is an isomorphism. If
n>m
, its kernel is given by the two-sided ideal
I_{m,n}
described above.

Proof sketch.

Note that

\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]_{(1,\ldots,1)}=S^{\bullet}\left(\End(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{\oplus n}\right)^{*}_{(1,\ldots,1)}\cong\left(\End(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{\otimes n}\right)^{*}\cong\End\left((\mathbb{C}^{m})^{\otimes n}\right),

compatible with the
GL_{m}
-actions on both sides. But the ring

\End\left((\mathbb{C}^{m})^{\otimes n}\right)^{GL_{m}}=\End_{GL_{m}}\left((\mathbb{C}^{m})^{\otimes n}\right)

is described by Schur-Weyl-duality.
∎

Remark 1.4
.

Recall the decomposition of
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
into isotypic components:

\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash n}{I_{\lambda}},

compatible with both the left and right action of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then
I_{m,n}
is the sum of all
I_{\lambda}
, where
\lambda
is a Young diagram with at least
m+1
rows.

1.4.
The other graded parts

Fix
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
and write
d=\sum{d_{i}}
. Our goal is to describe the degree
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
part of our invariant ring. The main idea is that we can use
polarization
to reduce this to the multilinear part.

More precisely: consider the ring
\mathbb{C}[X_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{1}^{(d_{1})},\ldots,X_{n}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{n}^{(d_{n})}]
, which is just the ring
\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}]
where we relabeled the
d=\sum{d_{i}}
matrices in a suggestive way. We equip it with the action of the group
\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}
, where
\mathfrak{S}_{d_{i}}
acts by permuting the (entries of the) matrices
X_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{i}^{(d_{i})}
.

Lemma 1.5
.

We have an isomorphism of rings

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[X_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{1}^{(d_{1})},\ldots,X_{n}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{n}^{(d_{n})}]_{(1,\ldots,1)}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}}

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]_{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})}

\displaystyle f(X_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{1}^{(d_{1})},\ldots,X_{n}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{n}^{(d_{n})})

\displaystyle\mapsto f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\ldots,X_{n}).

This isomorphism is compatible with the
GL_{m}
-action.

Proof.

The inverse map is given by polarization: given
g(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]_{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})}
, first substitute each
X_{i}
for the sum
\sum_{j=1}^{d_{i}}{X_{i}^{(j)}}
, then take the multilinear part of the obtained polynomial. We leave it to the reader to check that, up to a nonzero scalar, this does define an inverse to the map above.
∎

Before we can state the main result of this section, we need some additional notation.
Let
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
be the group of permutations of the set

\{(1,1),\ldots,(1,d_{1}),\ldots,(n,1),\ldots(n,d_{n})\}.

Let us now consider the subalgebra

\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]

where the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{d}
on
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
is given by conjugation.
We will write elements of
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}}
as (linear combinations of) “pseudo-permutations”
\left(i_{1}\ldots i_{k}\right)\ldots\left(j_{1}\ldots j_{\ell}\right)
, where the number
i
appears
d_{i}
times. By definition,
\left(i_{1}\ldots i_{k}\right)\ldots\left(j_{1}\ldots j_{\ell}\right)
is the image of any element of the form

\left((i_{1},\cdot)\ldots(i_{k},\cdot)\right)\ldots\left((j_{1},\cdot)\ldots(j_{\ell},\cdot)\right)

under the symmetrization map.

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]

\displaystyle\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}}

Theorem 1.6
.

There is a surjective linear map

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}}&\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{GL_{m}}_{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})}\\
\left(i_{1}\ldots i_{k}\right)\cdots\left(j_{1}\ldots j_{\ell}\right)&\mapsto\Tr(X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}})\cdots\Tr(X_{j_{1}}\cdots X_{j_{\ell}}),\end{split}

whose kernel is given by

I_{m,d}\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}}.

Here the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{d}
on
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
is given by conjugation.

Proof sketch.

Combine
Lemma

1.5
and
Theorem

1.3
, noting that in the map

\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{C}[X_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{1}^{(d_{1})},\ldots,X_{n}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{n}^{(d_{n})}]_{(1,\ldots,1)},

is compatible with the described actions of
\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{d}
(conjugation in
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
on the left hand side; permuting matrices on the right hand side).
∎

From
Theorem

1.6
one can deduce that our invariant ring
\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{GL_{m}}
is (as a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra) generated by polynomials of the form
\Tr(X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}})
. For this reason, it is also called the
trace algebra
. In the future, we will use the notation
R_{m,n}:=\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]^{GL_{m}}
.

1.5.
Trace relations

Elements in the kernel of (
4
) are known as
trace relations
. They describe the polynomial relations that hold between our generators
\Tr(X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}})
.
Let us illustrate this by an example: take
m=2
and
n=3
, and consider the degree
(1,1,1)
part of the invariant ring
R_{2,3}=\mathbb{C}[X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}]^{GL_{2}}
. By
Theorem

1.3
this space is spanned by the 6 polynomials

\displaystyle\Tr(X_{1})\Tr(X_{2})\Tr(X_{3}),

\displaystyle\Tr(X_{1}X_{2})\Tr(X_{3}),

\displaystyle\Tr(X_{1}X_{3})\Tr(X_{2}),

\displaystyle\Tr(X_{2}X_{3})\Tr(X_{1}),

\displaystyle\Tr(X_{1}X_{2}X_{3}),

\displaystyle\Tr(X_{1}X_{3}X_{2}).

The ideal
I_{2,3}
turns out to be just the linear span of
c_{3}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{3}}{\sgn(\sigma)\cdot\sigma}
, which corresponds to the trace relation

\displaystyle\Tr(X_{1})\Tr(X_{2})\Tr(X_{3})-\Tr(X_{1}X_{2})\Tr(X_{3})-\Tr(X_{1}X_{3})\Tr(X_{2})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Tr(X_{2}X_{3})\Tr(X_{1})+\Tr(X_{1}X_{2}X_{3})+\Tr(X_{1}X_{3}X_{2})=0.

The reader is invited to verify that the above expression really yields zero, for any
2\times 2
-matrices
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
.

2.
Polynomial identities of matrices

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
be the noncommutative polynomial ring in
n
variables. An element
f\in\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
is a
polynomial identity for
m\times m
matrices
if it vanishes when we substitute
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
by generic
m\times m
matrices
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
. The quotient of
\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
by the ideal of polynomial identities is called the
ring of generic
m\times m
matrices
. We will denote this ring by
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
.

The ring of generic matrices is an important example of a
Polynomial Identity (PI) ring
. Both PI-rings in general, and rings of generic matrices in particular, have been intensively studied since the 80’s. We here present just a few concepts and results that will be relevant later. For more, the reader can consult
[
DF04
]
.

2.1.
The Hilbert series

To quantify the number of polynomial identities, one can use the
Hilbert series
of
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
. Recall that the Hilbert series of a multigraded vector space
R
is given by

H(R;t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=\sum_{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}{\dim R_{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})}t_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{d_{n}}}.

The Hilbert series of
\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
is given by

H(\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle;t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=\frac{1}{1-t_{1}-\cdots-t_{n}}.

If we also know the Hilbert series of
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
, we can compute the dimension of the space of polynomial identities of multidegree
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
as the coefficient of
t_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{d_{n}}
in the difference

\frac{1}{1-t_{1}-\cdots-t_{n}}-H(\mathcal{M}_{m,n};t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}).

Similar to the trace relations in the previous sections, one can use polarization to show it is sufficient to restrict to the multilinear part. Note that
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
is a representation of
GL_{n}
, as it is the quotient of the representation

\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle\cong\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb{N}}{(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}}

by a subrepresentation. Let us denote the degree
(1,\ldots,1)
part of
{\mathcal{M}_{m,d}}
by
P_{m,d}
. This is a quotient of

\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle_{(1,\ldots,1)},

which is equipped with a left
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-action via

\sigma\cdot(x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{d}})=x_{\sigma(i_{1})}\cdots x_{\sigma(i_{d})}.

Then we have the following theorem, which was independently proved by Berele
[
Ber82
, Theorem 2.7]
and Drensky (
[
Dre81
, Remark 1.5]
and
[
Dre84
, Lemma 2.3]
). The present formulation is based on
[
DF04
, Theorem 2.3.4]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose the decomposition of
P_{m,d}
into irreducible
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-representations is given by

P_{m,d}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash d}V_{\lambda}^{\oplus a_{\lambda}}.

Then the decomposition of
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
into irreducible
GL_{n}
-representations is given by

\mathcal{M}_{m,n}=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash d\\
\operatorname{len}(\lambda)\leq n\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\oplus a_{\lambda}}}.

Here for
\lambda\vdash d
a partition,
V_{\lambda}
denotes the associated Specht module, and
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}
the associated Schur functor.

Corollary 2.3
.

The Hilbert series of
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
is given by

H(\mathcal{M}_{m,n};t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=\sum_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash d\\
\operatorname{len}(\lambda)\leq n\end{subarray}}{a_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})},

where
a_{\lambda}
are the coefficients from
Theorem

2.2
, and
s_{\lambda}
denotes the Schur polynomial.

In general, it is a hard task to compute the multiplicities
a_{\lambda}
. However, in the case of
2\times 2
matrices (
m=2
) a formula is known
[
Dre84
,
For84
]
:

Theorem 2.4
.

For
m=2
, the coefficients
a_{\lambda}
above are
0
whenever
\operatorname{len}(\lambda)>4
. If
\operatorname{len}(\lambda)\leq 4
, then
a_{\lambda}
is given by the table below.

\lambda

a_{\lambda}

(\lambda_{1},0,0,0)

1

(\lambda_{1},1,0,0)

\lambda_{1}

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},0,0)
with
\lambda_{2}>1

(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}+1)\lambda_{2}

(\lambda_{1},1,1,0)

2\lambda_{1}-1

(\lambda_{1},1,1,1)

\lambda_{1}-1

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
not in the cases above

(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}+1)(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}+1)(\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4}+1)

Combining
Theorem

2.4
with
Corollary

2.3
yields the Hilbert series of
\mathcal{M}_{2,n}
, for any
n
. For fixed small
n
, the obtained expression can be simplified significantly. We present it here in the case
n=2
.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
For84
]
)
.

The Hilbert series of the ring two generic
2\times 2
-matrices is given by

H(\mathcal{M}_{2,2};t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{1-t_{1}-t_{2}+t_{1}t_{2}+t_{1}^{2}t_{2}+t_{1}t_{2}^{2}-t_{1}^{2}t_{2}^{2}}{(1-t_{1})^{2}(1-t_{2})^{2}(1-t_{2}t_{2})}.

2.2.
Relation to the trace algebra

Definition 2.6
.

By
R_{m,n}^{\cyc}\subseteq R_{m,n}
we mean the linear subspace spanned by elements of the form
\Tr(g(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}))
, where
g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
.
Furthermore, let
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]_{\cyc}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]
be the subspace spanned by permutations consisting of a single cycle of length
d
.

Note that (
4
) restricts to a surjective map

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]_{\cyc}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}}&\twoheadrightarrow R^{\cyc}_{m,n;(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})}\\
\left(i_{1}\ldots i_{d}\right)&\mapsto\Tr(X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{d}})\end{split}

whose kernel is given by

(6)

I_{m,d}\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]_{\cyc}^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}}.

Example 2.7
.

R_{2,3;(1,1,1)}^{\cyc}
is the linear span of
\Tr(X_{1}X_{2}X_{3})
and
\Tr(X_{1}X_{3}X_{2})
, and
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]_{\cyc}
is the linear span of
(123)
and
(132)
. In this case, the map (
5
) is an isomorphism.

Consider the linear map

\displaystyle T:\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},X_{n+1}]

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto\Tr(f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\cdot X_{n+1})

Note that this actually lands in the invariant ring
\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},X_{n+1}]^{GL_{m}}=R_{m,n+1}
. Moreover, one verifies that

•

the kernel of
T
is the ideal of polynomial identities.

•

the image of
T
is the subspace of
R_{m,n+1}^{\cyc}
consisting of all trace polynomials that have degree
1
the variable
X_{n+1}
.

In other words,
T
induces isomorphisms

(7)

\mathcal{M}_{m,n;(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})}\cong R_{m,n+1;(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n},1)}^{\cyc}.

Remark 2.8
.

Combining (
7
) and (
5
), we get a surjective linear map

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d+1}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}\times\mathfrak{S}_{1}}_{\text{cyc}}

\displaystyle\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{m,d;(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})}

\displaystyle(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n},d+1)

\displaystyle\mapsto X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{n}}

whose kernel is given by

(8)

I_{m,d+1}\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d+1}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{d_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{d_{n}}\times\mathfrak{S}_{1}}_{\text{cyc}}.

In particular, the space of multilinear polynomial identities in
d
matrices can be identified with the intersection

I_{m,d+1}\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d+1}]_{\cyc}.

In other words, we have

(9)

P_{m,d}\cong\frac{\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d+1}]_{\cyc}}{I_{m,d+1}\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d+1}]_{\cyc}}.

Recall that
P_{m,d}
came equipped with an
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-action. Under the isomorphism (
9
), this action corresponds to the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{d}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{d+1}
on
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d+1}]
by conjugation.

3.
Matrix product states

Matrix product states arise in the context of quantum many-body systems, where they are used to model ground states of certain systems. We will be concerned with two variants which model translation-invariant systems. For the purpose of these notes, we think about them as low-dimensional subvarieties of a high-dimensional tensor space. For more on matrix product states and their geometry, we refer to
[
PGVWC07
,
HMOV14
,
CM14
,
CMS23
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

The variety
\uMPS(m,n,d)
of
uniform matrix product states
is the closed image of the map

(10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})^{n}&\to(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}\\
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})&\mapsto\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\leq n}\Tr(A_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{d}})\ e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{d}}.\end{split}

The variety
\hMPS(m,n,d)
of

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})^{n+1}&\to(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}\\
(X,A_{1},\dots,A_{n})&\mapsto\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\leq n}\Tr(XA_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{d}})\ e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{d}}.\end{split}

(

3.1.
Homogeneous matrix product states

If we write
e_{I}:=e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{d}}
for a word
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})
, then
the space
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
has a basis
\{e_{I}\}_{I\in[n]^{d}}
. We will denote the dual basis by
\{x_{I}\}_{I\in[n]^{d}}
. The following observation links homogeneous matrix product states to polynomial identities of matrices:

Observation 3.2
.

An equation
\sum_{I}{\lambda_{I}x_{I}}
vanishes on
\hMPS(m,n,d)
if and only if the identity
\sum_{I}{\lambda_{I}\Tr(XA_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{d}})}=0
holds for all matrices
X,A_{i}
, if and only if
\sum_{I}{\lambda_{I}A_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{d}}}=0
is a polynomial identity of matrices.

Corollary 3.3
.

The linear span of
\hMPS(m,n,d)
has dimension equal to
\dim\mathcal{M}_{m,n;d}
, the degree
d
part of the ring of
n
generic
m\times m
-matrices. In other words, we have

\sum_{d\in\mathbb{N}}{\dim\langle\hMPS(m,n,d)\rangle t^{d}}=H(\mathcal{M}_{m,n};t,\ldots,t).

Example 3.4
.

For
m=n=2
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{d\in\mathbb{N}}{\dim\langle\hMPS(2,2,d)\rangle t^{d}}=

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{M}_{2,2};t,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-2t+t^{2}+2t^{3}-t^{4}}{(1-t)^{4}(1-t^{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+2t+4t^{2}+8t^{3}+16t^{4}+30t^{5}+53t^{6}+88t^{7}+139t^{8}+210t^{9}

\displaystyle+306t^{10}+432t^{11}+594t^{12}+798t^{13}+1051t^{14}+1360t^{15}+\cdots

Equivalently, we have the following explicit formula:

\dim\langle\hMPS(2,2,d)\rangle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{48}(d^{4}+4d^{3}+2d^{2}+44d+48)&\text{for }d\text{ even,}\\
\frac{1}{48}(d^{4}+4d^{3}+2d^{2}+44d+45)&\text{for }d\text{ odd.}\end{cases}

Compare with the top row of Table 1 in

NV18
]
. For
m=2
and larger
n
, it is possible to write down a similar formula using
Theorem

2.4
. However, larger
m
are much more difficult: even for
m=3
and
n=2
no exact formula is known to the best of my awareness.

3.2.
Uniform matrix product states

Since traces of matrices are invariant under cyclic permutations, the variety
\uMPS(m,n,d)
is contained in the linear subspace
\Cyc^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\subseteq(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
of tensors that are invariant under cyclic permutations.
Consider the equivalence relation on
[n]^{d}
given by identifying words with their cyclic permutations. The equivalence classes are known as
necklaces
of length
d
on the alphabet
[n]
; we will denote the set of such necklaces by
N_{d}([n])
.
For a necklace
N\in N_{d}([n])
, we define
e_{N}:=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in N}{e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{d}}}
. Then
\{e_{N}\}_{N\in N_{d}([n])}
is a basis of
\Cyc^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.
We will denote the dual basis by
\{y_{N}\}_{N\in N_{d}([n])}
.

As before, we see that linear equations
\sum_{N}{\lambda_{N}y_{N}}
vanishing on
\uMPS(m,n,d)
correspond to trace relations
\sum_{N}{\lambda_{N}\Tr(A_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{d}})}=0
, where
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})
is a representative of the necklace
N
. Hence we find:

Observation 3.5
.

The linear span of
\uMPS(m,n,d)
has dimension equal to
\dim R^{\cyc}_{m,n;d}
. In other words:

\sum_{d\in\mathbb{N}}{\dim\langle\uMPS(m,n,d)\rangle t^{d}}=H(R^{\cyc}_{m,n};t,\ldots,t).

In order to determine the Hilbert series of
R^{\cyc}_{m,n}
, we can try the same approach as for
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}
. In particular, I claim that the analogue of
Theorem

2.2
holds. Let us write

\widetilde{P}_{m,d}:=R^{\cyc}_{m,d;(1,\ldots,1)}.

Claim 3.6
.

Suppose the decomposition of
\widetilde{P}_{m,d}
into irreducible
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
-representations is given by

\widetilde{P}_{m,d}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash d}V_{\lambda}^{\oplus b_{\lambda}}.

Then the decomposition of
R^{\cyc}_{m,n}
into irreducible
GL_{n}
-representations is given by

R^{\cyc}_{m,n}=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash d\\
\operatorname{len}(\lambda)\leq n\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\oplus b_{\lambda}}}.

Hence, the Hilbert series of
R^{\cyc}_{m,n}
is given by

H(R^{\cyc}_{m,n};t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=\sum_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash d\\
\operatorname{len}(\lambda)\leq n\end{subarray}}{b_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})}.

Proof.

∎

We have

\widetilde{P}_{m,d}\cong\frac{\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]_{\cyc}}{I_{m,d}\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{d}]_{\cyc}}\cong P_{m,d-1}.

In particular, for
m=2

as a representation of the subgroup

Problem 3.7
