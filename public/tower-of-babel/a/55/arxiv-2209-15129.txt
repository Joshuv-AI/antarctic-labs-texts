Abstract
. Motivated by various applications, this article develops the notion of boundary control for Maxwell’s equations in the frequency domain. Surface curl is shown to be the appropriate regularization in order for the optimal control problem to be well-posed. Since, all underlying variables are assumed to be complex valued, the standard results on differentiability do not directly apply. Instead, we extend the notion of Wirtinger derivatives to complexified Hilbert spaces. Optimality conditions are rigorously derived and higher order boundary regularity of the adjoint variable is established. The state and adjoint variables are discretized using higher order Nédélec finite elements. The finite element space for controls is identified, as a space, which preserves the structure of the control regularization. Convergence of the fully discrete scheme is established. The theory is validated by numerical experiments, in some cases, motivated by realistic applications.

1.2.
Problem Setup

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\,^{3}
be a polyhedron with a Lipschitz continuous boundary denoted by
\Gamma
. Moreover, let the current density
\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{3})
, a desired field vector
\bm{u}_{d}
in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{3})
, and symmetric and positive definite functions
{\bm{\kappa}}
and
{\bm{\mu}}
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\,^{3\times 3})
,
positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
, and
\omega\neq 0
, be given. If
(\bm{u},\bm{z})
represents the state-control pair, then
the goal of this work is to study the following boundary optimal control problem:

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{(\bm{u},\bm{z})\in U\times Z}\left\{\mathcal{J}(\bm{u},{\bm{z}}):=f(\bm{u})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha\|\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{z}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\beta\|\bm{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)\right\},

subject to the time-harmonic Maxwell’s equations as constraints

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\bf{curl}}\,\left({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{u}\right)+(\mathrm{i}\omega){\bm{\kappa}}\bm{u}&=\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
\bm{u}\times\bm{n}&=\bm{z}\times\bm{n}\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma.\end{aligned}

In (
1.1a
),
\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}
denotes the scalar surface curl. For a precise definition of
\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}
, the surface divergence
\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma}
, tangential gradient
\nabla_{\Gamma}
, and the tangential vector curl
\bm{\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}}
, when
\Omega
is a Lipschitz polyhedron, see
[
12
,
13
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work on boundary control of Maxwell’s equations with complete analysis and numerical implementation in the time harmonic setting.
Another key aspect that sets us apart from the existing literature on optimal control of Maxwell type
equations is the fact we are working in the complex setting, without assuming splitting between real and
imaginary parts.

However, this introduces additional challenges. For instance, even the standard
quadratic cost functional

(1.2)

\displaystyle f(\bm{u}):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{d}|^{2}\,d\bm{x},

is not differentiable in the (complex) Gâteaux sense, thus making most of the existing gradient
based optimization algorithms not directly applicable. Provided that we can define an appropriate
notion of derivatives, the complex structure leads to elegant analysis. Additionally,
most of the modern programming languages can handle complex arithmetic, therefore it is
meaningful to work under the complex field directly.
In the particular case of quadratic functionals of the form (
1.2
), the differentiability problem can be addressed via

directional derivatives
as in
[
41
, Sec. 3.2]
, where they consider an optimal current problem with a complex control related to impressed currents (internal control), see also
[
16
, Sec. 4.1]
. Our approach allows us to consider a much wider family of functionals, and leads to the same derivative for the quadratic case.

Our notion of derivatives is motivated by the
Wirtinger derivative
(1927)
[
44
]
, which
is a well-known concept in finite dimensions. Its origin can be traced back at least to 1899 in a work
of
J.H. Poincaré
on potentials
[
36
, Théorème 8]
. This notion of derivatives is
motivated by splitting of a function into its real and imaginary components. However, it allows one to
work in the complex regime, without using any such splitting.
Wirtinger derivatives have been used in the finite dimensional optimization problems at least since
the 60’s in the works of Levinson, Mond, Hanson and Kaul, cf.
[
30
,
32
,
22
]
,
and in the engineering community since the 80’s, see
[
10
]
, with applications from

signal theory
to
Machine learning
, see
[
9
]
. We refer to
[
23
,
25
]

for more details on Wirtinger derivatives.

In this work, we extend the notion of derivatives from finite dimensions to infinite dimensions with
complex fields and rigorously derive the optimality conditions at the continuous level and identify
continuous gradients. We discretize our state and adjoint variables using higher order Nédélec finite
elements and for the control we use the lowest order Nédélec elements on each boundary face,
which by construction have continuous tangential components.
Next, we establish convergence of our numerical scheme. Numerical experiments confirms
our theoretical findings. In particular, in our first experiment, motivated by

[
5
,
6
]
, we consider a realistic application with non-homogeneous
boundary conditions where we first derive an explicit solution and next we validate our
Nédélec finite element implementation against this explicit solution, the expected rate of
convergence is observed. In the second experiment, we study the convergence of optimal
control problem, see section
5
for more details.

Tangential traces and Green’s identities for
{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)

We begin this section by defining the

of functions in

38

Definition 2.1
.

The tangential traces of

\gamma_{t}\bm{v}:=\gamma\bm{v}\times\bm{n},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{T}\bm{v}:=\bm{n}\times\left(\gamma\bm{v}\times\bm{n}\right).

Lemma 2.2
.

The following maps are linear, continuous and surjective

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}:{\bf H}^{1}(\Omega)\mapsto H_{\perp}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma),~\mbox{ and }\gamma_{T}:{\bf H}^{1}(\Omega)\mapsto H_{||}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma).

Proof.

See

∎

Definition 2.3

The spaces

\displaystyle(H_{\perp}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma),\|\cdot\|_{\perp,-\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}),~\mbox{ and }~(H_{||}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma),\|\cdot\|_{||,-\frac{1}{2},\Gamma})

\displaystyle H_{\perp}^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma):=

\displaystyle~\left\{\bm{\lambda}\in H_{\perp}^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}(\Gamma):\,\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{\lambda}\in H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\right\},

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\bm{\lambda}\|_{H_{||}^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)}:=&~\|\bm{\lambda}\|_{||,-\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}+\|\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma}\bm{\lambda}\|_{-\frac{1}{2},\Gamma},\\

Moreover, we have

The maps,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \gamma_{t}:{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)\mapsto H_{||}^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma),\mbox{ and }\gamma_{T}:{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)\mapsto H_{\perp}^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)\end{aligned}

See

2.2.
Well-posedness of the state equation

First, let us introduce the
sesquilinear form

{a:{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)\times{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)\to\mathbb{C}}
given by

(2.7)

a(\bm{u},\bm{v}):=\int_{\Omega}{\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{u}\cdot{\bf{curl}}\,{\overline{\bm{v}}}\,d\bm{x}+(\mathrm{i}\omega)\int_{\Omega}{\bm{\kappa}}\bm{u}\cdot\overline{\bm{v}}\,d\bm{x}.

The following lemmas show the well-posedness of the state equation (
1.1b
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Given
{\bm{f}}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)^{*}
, the problem, find
\bm{u}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
such that

(2.8)

\displaystyle a(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\langle{\bm{f}},\bm{v}\rangle\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega),

is a well-posed problem in the sense of Hadamard, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the duality paring between
{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
and its dual.

Proof.

The hypothesis on
{\bm{\mu}}
and
{\bm{\kappa}}
guarantees that
a(\cdot,\cdot)
defines a sesquilinear, bounded and coercive form. The Lax-Milgram lemma implies that (
2.8
) has a unique solution; moreover, we have

(2.9)

\displaystyle c({\bm{\kappa}},{\bm{\mu}};\omega)\|\bm{u}\|^{2}_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}\leq|a(\bm{u},\bm{u})|\lesssim|\langle{\bm{f}},\bm{u}\rangle|,

where
c
is a positive constant that depends on
\omega
and the eigenvalues of
{\bm{\mu}}
and
{\bm{\kappa}}
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Given
\bm{z}\in{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
and
\bm{j}_{c}\in{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)
, the problem, find
\bm{u}\in H_{\bm{z}\times\bm{n}}({\bf{curl}}\,;\Omega)
such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle a(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\int_{\Omega}\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}\cdot\overline{\bm{v}}\,d\bm{x}\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega),

is well-posed.

Proof.

Let
\bm{z}\in{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
be given. Then
\bm{z}\times\bm{n}\in\gamma_{t}{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
, which follows from the much more general results on weak rotations, see
[
38
, Prop. 16.16 and Eq. (16.42)]
. Therefore fom Lemma
2.4
, there exists
\bm{u}_{\bm{z}}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
such that
\gamma_{t}\bm{u}_{\bm{z}}=\bm{z}\times\bm{n}
and

(2.11)

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}_{\bm{z}}\|_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}\lesssim\|\bm{z}\times\bm{n}\|_{{H^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}_{||}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)}}\lesssim\|\bm{z}\|_{\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}},

where the last inequality follows because of the isometry

\|\bm{z}\times\bm{n}\|_{{H^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}_{||}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)}}=\|\bm{z}\|_{H_{\perp}^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)}
,
see
[
38
, p. 448]
,
and

\|\bm{z}\|_{H_{\perp}^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)}\leq C\|\bm{z}\|_{\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}},

which follows from the compact embedding of
L^{2}(\Gamma)
into
H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
.

Now, we look for
\bm{u}_{0}\in{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega)
such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle a(\bm{u}_{0},\bm{v})=\int_{\Omega}\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}\cdot\overline{\bm{v}}\,d\bm{x}-a(\bm{u}_{\bm{z}},\bm{v})\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega).

From Lemma (
2.6
) and (
2.11
), it follows that this problem is well-posed, and

\|\bm{u}_{0}\|\lesssim\|\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\bm{z}\|_{\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}}.

Finally,
\bm{u}:=\bm{u}_{0}+\bm{u}_{\bm{z}}
is the unique solution of (
2.10
) and

\|\bm{u}\|_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}\lesssim\|\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\bm{z}\|_{\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}},

which finishes the proof.
∎

From the previous analysis,
\bm{u}
depends on both
\bm{z}
and
\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}
. The goal of the next section is to reduce the cost functional
\mathcal{J}(\bm{u},\bm{z})=\mathcal{J}(\bm{u}(\bm{z};\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}),\bm{z})
to be only a function of
\bm{z}
, and then derive the optimality conditions.

3.
Reduced cost functional and its derivative

For the remainder of the paper, we will assume that
\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}
is given. By introducing the control-to-state map, we can obtain the so-called reduced optimization problem. The solution, or control-to-state, map is affine and it is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:Z

\displaystyle\to{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)\hookrightarrow{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)

(3.1)

\displaystyle\bm{z}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathcal{S}\bm{z}:=\bm{u},

where
\bm{u}
is the unique solution to (
2.10
) with right-hand-side
\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}
and the boundary condition
\bm{z}\times\bm{n}
. The notation
\hookrightarrow
indicate the continuous embedding, as a result, we can consider
\mathcal{S}:Z\rightarrow{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)
.
The solution operator
\mathcal{S}
is an affine map, and it is common to split
\mathcal{S}
into the part that depends on
\bm{z}
and the part that depends on
\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}
. We write

\mathcal{S}\bm{z}=\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma}\bm{z}+\bm{u}_{\Omega},

where
\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma}
is the solution operator for the state equation with
\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}\equiv{\bf 0}
, and
\bm{u}_{\Omega}
is the solution for the state equation when
\bm{z}\equiv\bm{0}
. By Lemma
2.7
,
\mathcal{S}
is continuous and there exists a
C=C({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\kappa}},\Omega)>0
such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}\bm{z}\|_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}=\|\bm{u}\|_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}\leq C\left(\|\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\bm{z}\|_{\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}}\right).

Therefore, the
reduced cost functional

j(\bm{z}):=\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{S}\bm{z},\bm{z})
is also continuous, and we use the splitting above to write

(3.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{\bm{z}\in Z}j(\bm{z})=\displaystyle\min_{\bm{z}\in Z}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma}\bm{z}-\widehat{\bm{u}}_{d}|^{2}\,d\bm{x}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Gamma}|\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{z}|^{2}\,dS+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\Gamma}|\bm{z}|^{2}\,dS,

where
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{d}=\bm{u}_{d}-\bm{u}_{\Omega}.
Therefore, without loss of generality, in what follows we will consider

\bm{j}_{\bm{c}}\equiv\bm{0}
, then
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma}
. Notice that in this case,
\bm{u}_{\Omega}=0
.

Our goal now is to discuss the existence and uniqueness of solution to the reduced optimization problem

(3.4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{\bm{z}\in Z}j(\bm{z})=\min_{\bm{z}\in Z}\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{S}\bm{z},\bm{z}).

The proof follows immediately from the direct method of calculus of variations, we sketch it for completeness.

Theorem 3.1

(existence and uniqueness)
.

The problem (
3.4
) has a unique solution
\overline{\bm{z}}\in Z
.

Proof.

Notice that
j(\cdot)
is bounded below, therefore there exists an infimizing sequence
\{\bm{z}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{z}\in Z}j(\bm{z})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}j(\bm{z}_{n})
. The previous limit and the definition of
j(\cdot)
implies that

\{\bm{z}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a bounded sequence in
Z
. Notice that
Z
is closed subspace of a Hilbert space and
is therefore a Hilbert space itself. Thus the boundedness of sequence
\{\bm{z}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
implies that there exists
a subsequence (not relabeled) that converges to
\widehat{\bm{z}}
in
Z
. It then remains to show that
\widehat{\bm{z}}
is the
minimizer of (
3.4
). This immediately follows from weak lower semicontinuity of
j(\cdot)
.
The uniqueness is a direct consequence of the strict convexity of
j(\cdot)
.
∎

As usual, in order to find the optimal control
\overline{\bm{z}}
we want to use a gradient-based method to identify the critical points of the cost functional
j(\cdot)
in (
3.3
). Nevertheless, there is a big difference between differentiability over typical real and complex fields. However, Fréchet and Gâteaux differentiability on complex fields are quite similar, cf.
[
49
,
50
,
51
]
. Notice that,
|\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma}\bm{z}-\widehat{\bm{u}}_{d}|^{2}
in (
3.3
) is smooth, but as we will see below, it is not complex Gâteaux differentiable. Thus, to study (
3.3
), we need some weaker notion for the derivative of
j
.
Next, we will develop a notion of derivative for
j
that is more general than Gâteaux or Fréchet derivatives for complex spaces but strong enough to have some notion of Taylor series expansion that will allow us to characterize critical points of
j
.

Before we begin our discussion of derivatives, we introduce what it means for a complex-valued function to be
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, but not necessarily analytic, see for instance
[
29
, (2.1)]
.

Definition 3.2

(
\mathcal{C}^{1}
functions)
.

For a complex Banach space
\mathcal{U}
, suppose
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{U}
is open and
u:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{C}
is a function. If the directional derivatives

(3.5)

\displaystyle du(\bm{z};\bm{\xi})=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{u(\bm{z}+t\bm{\xi})-u(\bm{z})}{t},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}

exist for all
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{D}
,
\bm{\xi}\in\mathcal{U}
, and
du:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{C}
is continuous, we write
u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{D})
.

Consider the function

(3.6)

\bm{z}\mapsto\bm{z}\overline{\bm{z}}=|\bm{z}|^{2},

which is
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
but not complex Fréchet differentiable, nor complex Gâteaux differentiable. This shows that complex differentiability is too restrictive, especially in the context of optimization. A weaker notion of “complex derivative” which has most of the required properties in optimization is the so-called Wirtinger derivative, cf.
[
44
]
.

In the next section, we extend the notion of Wirtinger derivatives to spaces which are the complexification of a real Hilbert space, and our analysis follows as in the case
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C},
cf.
[
43
]
.

3.1.
Wirtinger derivatives on complexified Hilbert spaces

Let
(\mathcal{U},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{U}})
be the complexification of a real Hilbert space
(H,(\cdot,\cdot)_{H})
, and let
f:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{C}
be a continuous function but not complex differentiable. We can extend
f
to a function on
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}
. Let
g:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{C}
be a continuous extension of
f
such that

g(\bm{z},\overline{\bm{z}})=f(\bm{z})\quad\forall\bm{z}\in\mathcal{U}.

Now, let us assume
g
is complex Fréchet differentiable, and define

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}\Bigr\lvert_{\bm{z}=\bm{z}_{0}}:=\nabla g\left(\bm{z}_{0},\overline{\bm{z}}_{0}\right)({\bm{e}}_{1}),\qquad\qquad\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}\Bigr\lvert_{\bm{z}=\bm{z}_{0}}:=\nabla g\left(\bm{z}_{0},\overline{\bm{z}}_{0}\right)({\bm{e}}_{2}),

where the unit vectors
{\bm{e}}_{1}
and
{\bm{e}}_{2}
will give us the first and second components of
\nabla g
.
Even though, the existence of an extension
g
, which is complex Fréchet differentiable, of
f
may look too restrictive, however such an extension exists when
f
is continuous and
f
is analytic for
\bm{z}
and
\overline{\bm{z}}
; separately, cf.
[
23
, p. 2]
, these conditions hold for the function in (
3.6
) for instance. This follows from
Hartogs’ theorem
or one of its generalizations to infinite-dimensional spaces; see for instance,
[
31
, Thm. 3.2]
. Now, for any
\bm{z}_{0}
and
\delta\bm{z}
in
\mathcal{U}
, the following limit exists and the resulting expression is linear in
\delta\bm{z}
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\bm{z}_{0};\delta\bm{z}):=&\lim_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{t\rightarrow 0}{\mathrm{Im}(t)=0}}\frac{f(\bm{z}_{0}+t\delta\bm{z})-f(\bm{z}_{0})}{t}\\
=&\lim_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{t\rightarrow 0}{\mathrm{Im}(t)=0}}\frac{g\left(\begin{pmatrix}\bm{z}_{0},\overline{\bm{z}}_{0}\,\,\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}\delta\bm{z},\overline{\delta\bm{z}}\,\,\end{pmatrix}\right)-g\begin{pmatrix}\bm{z}_{0},\overline{\bm{z}}_{0}\,\,\end{pmatrix}}{t}\\
=&\left(\begin{pmatrix}\delta\bm{z}\,\,\\
\overline{\delta\bm{z}}\,\,\end{pmatrix},\overline{\nabla g}(\bm{z}_{0},\overline{\bm{z}}_{0})\right)_{\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}}\\
=&\left(\delta\bm{z},\overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{0})\right)_{\mathcal{U}}+\left(\overline{\delta\bm{z}},\overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}}(\bm{z}_{0})\right)_{\mathcal{U}}.\end{aligned}

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be the complexification of a real Hilbert space
H
, and consider a continuous function

f:\mathcal{U}\mapsto\mathbb{R},\bm{z}\mapsto f(\bm{z})
, such that
f
is analytic in
\bm{z}
and in
\overline{\bm{z}}
, separately, then
\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\bm{z}_{0};\delta\bm{z})
defined in (
3.7
) exists. Moreover,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\bm{z}_{0};\delta\bm{z})=

\displaystyle~2\mathrm{Re}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{0}),\delta\bm{z}\right)_{\mathcal{U}}\qquad\forall\bm{z}_{0},\delta\bm{z}\in\mathcal{U}.

Proof.

The existence of
\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\bm{z}_{0};\delta\bm{z})
follows from the previous analysis.
On the other hand, if
f
is real-valued then by definition
\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\bm{z}_{0};\delta\bm{z})
is also real-valued, yielding

(3.9)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}(\bm{z}_{0})=\overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}}(\bm{z}_{0}).

In order to prove this identity, consider the inner product on
\mathcal{U}
given by

\displaystyle\left(\bm{u}_{1}+i\bm{v}_{1},\bm{u}_{2}+i\bm{v}_{2}\right)_{\mathcal{U}}:=\left(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2}\right)_{H}+\left(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\right)_{H}+\mathrm{i}\left(\left(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{u}_{2}\right)_{H}-\left(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\right)_{H}\right).

Now, we consider the splitting between real and imaginary parts

\displaystyle\delta\bm{z}=\delta^{Re}\bm{z}+\mathrm{i}\delta^{Im}\bm{z},\quad\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}(\bm{z}_{0})=f^{Re}_{\bm{z}}+\mathrm{i}f^{Im}_{\bm{z}},\mbox{ and }\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{0})=f^{Re}_{\overline{\bm{z}}}+\mathrm{i}f^{Im}_{\overline{\bm{z}}}.

In turn, from (
3.7
)

\displaystyle d^{\mathbb{R}\,}f(\bm{z}_{0};\delta\bm{z})=

\displaystyle\left(\delta\bm{z},\overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{0})\right)_{\mathcal{U}}+\left(\overline{\delta\bm{z}},\overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}}(\bm{z}_{0})\right)_{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\delta^{Re}\bm{z}+\mathrm{i}\delta^{Im}\bm{z},f^{Re}_{\bm{z}}-\mathrm{i}f^{Im}_{\bm{z}}\right)_{\mathcal{U}}+\left(\delta^{Re}\bm{z}-\mathrm{i}\delta^{Im}\bm{z},f^{Re}_{\overline{\bm{z}}}-\mathrm{i}f^{Im}_{\overline{\bm{z}}}\right)_{\mathcal{U}},

and because
\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\{d^{\mathbb{R}\,}f(\bm{z}_{0},\delta\bm{z})\}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\left(f^{Re}_{\bm{z}},\delta^{Im}\bm{z}\right)_{H}+\left(\delta^{Re}\bm{z},f^{Im}_{\bm{z}}\right)_{H}-\left(f^{Re}_{\overline{\bm{z}}},\delta^{Im}\bm{z}\right)_{H}+\left(\delta^{Re}\bm{z},f^{Im}_{\overline{\bm{z}}}\right)_{H}=0.

In particular, if
\delta^{Re}\bm{z}=\bm{0}
we get

\displaystyle\left(f^{Re}_{\bm{z}},\delta^{Im}\bm{z}\right)_{H}-\left(f^{Re}_{\overline{\bm{z}}},\delta^{Im}\bm{z}\right)_{H}=0\Leftrightarrow f^{Re}_{\bm{z}}=f^{Re}_{\overline{\bm{z}}}.

In turn, if
\delta^{Im}\bm{z}=\bm{0}
we get

\displaystyle\left(\delta^{Re}\bm{z},f^{Im}_{\bm{z}}\right)_{H}+\left(\delta^{Re}\bm{z},f^{Im}_{\overline{\bm{z}}}\right)_{H}=0\Leftrightarrow f^{Im}_{\bm{z}}=-f^{Im}_{\overline{\bm{z}}}.

Thus,

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}(\bm{z}_{0})=\overline{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{0})\right)},\mbox{ and therefore}\overline{\left(\delta\bm{z},\overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{0})\right)}_{\mathcal{U}}=\left(\overline{\delta\bm{z}},\overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}}(\bm{z}_{0})\right)_{\mathcal{U}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Our next goal is to relate
\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f
given in (
3.8
) with a gradient so that we can derive the first-order optimality conditions for problem (
1.1a
).
In order to do that, we identify
f
with a real functional
u
, namely
u:H\times H\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
f(\bm{z})=f(\bm{x}+i\bm{y})=u(\bm{x},\bm{y})
for all
\bm{z}=\bm{x}+i\bm{y}
in
\mathcal{U}.

From the regularity of
g
(as defined above) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\bm{z};\delta\bm{z})=

\displaystyle\lim_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{t\rightarrow 0}{t\in\R}}\frac{u(\bm{x}+t\delta{\bm{x}},\bm{y}+t\delta{\bm{y}})-u(\bm{x},\bm{y})}{t}

(3.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\nabla u,\begin{pmatrix}\delta{\bm{x}}\\
\delta{\bm{y}}\end{pmatrix}\right)_{H\times H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bm{x}},\delta{\bm{x}}\right)_{H}+\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bm{y}},\delta{\bm{y}}\right)_{H},

where
\delta\bm{z}=\delta{\bm{x}}+i\delta{\bm{y}}
. Consequently,
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{U})

(see Definition
3.2
) and we have the following identities

(3.11)

\displaystyle u\left(\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x},\bm{y}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\delta\bm{x},\delta\bm{y}\end{pmatrix}\right)=

\displaystyle~u\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x},\bm{y}\end{pmatrix}+\left(\nabla u(\widehat{\bm{x}},\widehat{\bm{y}}),\begin{pmatrix}\delta{\bm{x}},\delta{\bm{y}}\end{pmatrix}\right),

(3.12)

\displaystyle f(\bm{z}+\delta\bm{z})=

\displaystyle~f(\bm{z})+\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\widehat{\bm{z}};\delta\bm{z}),

for some
\widehat{\bm{z}}=\widehat{\bm{x}}+i\widehat{\bm{y}}
in the segment
[\bm{z},\bm{z}+\delta\bm{z}].
From now on, the expression
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\bm{z};\delta\bm{z})
will be called the
\mathbb{R}\,\!-
linear derivative
of
f
at
\bm{z}
in the direction
\delta\bm{z}.
Many properties for
f
can be obtained from properties of extension
g
or using the relation with
u
; for instance,
f
is (real) convex if and only if
u
is convex. Also, one can determine the directions of steepest descent and stationary points. In fact, we have the following result.

Theorem 3.4

(Steepest descent)
.

Let
f
,
g
and
u
be as above, then the direction of steepest descent for
f
at
\bm{z}_{0}=\bm{x}_{0}+\mathrm{i}\bm{y}_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle\delta\bm{z}=

\displaystyle-\overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{0})=-\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{0}),

or equivalently

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\delta\bm{x}\\
\delta\bm{y}\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle-\nabla u(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{y}_{0}).

Proof.

The result follows from the two characterizations of
\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f
given in (
3.8
) and (
3.1
),
respectively. In the first case, we have also used (
3.9
). The proof is complete.
∎

From the previous theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}(\bm{z})=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bm{x}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bm{y}}(\bm{x},\bm{y}),~\mbox{and }~\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z})=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bm{x}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})-\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bm{y}}(\bm{x},\bm{y}).

It is common to write these identities, called the
Wirtinger derivatives
(in finite dimensions) for a real-valued function
f
, as

(3.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{x}}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{y}},

(3.14)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{x}}-\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{y}}.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f
be as above. Then,
\bm{z}_{0}=\bm{x}_{0}+i\bm{y}_{0}
is a stationary point of
f
if and only if

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bm{z}}(\bm{z}_{0})=\bm{0}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{0})=\bm{0}.

Proof.

It is enough to identify
f
with
u
, then
\displaystyle\nabla u(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{y}_{0})=\bm{0}
, and using (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) the proof is complete.
∎

Finally, we give the following useful theorem holds. By
\mathrm{Dom}(f)
, we indicate the domain of function
f
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
f
be as above, and assume
f:\mathrm{Dom}(f)\to\mathbb{C}
with
\mathrm{Dom}(f)
convex. If
\bm{z}_{0}
is an optimal point then

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\bm{z}_{0};\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})\geq 0,\quad\forall\bm{z}\in\mathrm{Dom}(f).

In addition, if
f
is (real) convex then (
3.15
) is a sufficient condition.

Proof.

This result follows directly by identifying
f
with
u
and applying the well-known result for convex real-valued functions.
∎

3.2.
The
\mathbb{R}-
linear derivative of the reduced cost functional and optimality conditions

Since the map
\bm{z}\mapsto\overline{\bm{z}}\cdot\bm{z}
is not (complex) Gâteaux differentiable, the same can be concluded for the
reduced functional
j
defined in (
3.3
). The following lemma shows, however, that

\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}j
is well-defined.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})
be a complex Banach space,
(\mathcal{U},(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{U}})
be a complex Hilbert space,
\widetilde{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathcal{L}(X,\mathcal{U})
. Then, the convex function
f(\bm{z}):=\|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{z}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}

has an
\mathbb{R}\,
-linear derivative given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}f(\bm{z};\bm{v})=

\displaystyle~2\,\mathrm{Re}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{z}-\widetilde{\bm{u}},\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{v})_{\mathcal{U}},

where
\|\bm{u}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{U}}:=(\bm{u},\bm{u})_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathrm{Re}(a)
denotes the real part of
a\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

We examine the difference quotient from the definition of
\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}},
cf.
3.7
, namely:

\displaystyle\frac{f(\bm{z}+t\bm{v})-f(\bm{z})}{t}=

\displaystyle~\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{z}+t\bm{v})-\widetilde{\bm{u}}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}-\|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{z}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{\bigl(\|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{z}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}+2\,\mathrm{Re}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{z}-\widetilde{\bm{u}},t\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{v})_{\mathcal{U}}+\|t\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{v}\|^{2}_{X}\bigr)-\|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{z}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}}{t}

(3.16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\bar{t}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{z}-\widetilde{\bm{u}},\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{v})_{\mathcal{U}}+|t|^{2}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\bm{v}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{U}}}{t}.

Considering
t\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathrm{Im}(t)=0
, and then taking the limit as
t\rightarrow 0
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

From (
3.2
), we can also conclude that
f
is not complex Gâteaux differentiable.

In what follows we recall that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma}
and
\bm{u}_{d}=\widehat{\bm{u}}_{d}
.

Corollary 3.9
.

For
\bm{z}
and
\bm{\xi}
in
{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
, the
\mathbb{R}\,
-linear derivative of
j
at
\bm{z}
(given in (
3.3
)), in the direction
\bm{\xi}
is given by

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}j(\bm{z};\bm{\xi})=\mathrm{Re}\Big\{(\mathcal{S}\bm{z}-\bm{u}_{d},\mathcal{S}\bm{\xi})_{0,\Omega}+\alpha(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma},\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{\xi})_{0,\Gamma}+\beta(\bm{z},\bm{\xi})_{0,\Gamma}\Big\},

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{0,\Gamma}
denotes the inner product in both
L^{2}(\Gamma)
and
\bm{L}^{2}(\Gamma)
.

As usual we will avoid computing the term
\mathcal{S}\bm{\xi}
by introducing the adjoint of
\mathcal{S}
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Notice that since
\mathcal{S}
is bounded linear, therefore
\mathcal{S}^{*}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)^{*}
is well-defined.
Then from (
3.17
), given
\bm{\xi}\in{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
we have that
\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}j(\bm{z};\bm{\xi})
is
given by

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}j(\bm{z};\bm{\xi})=\mathrm{Re}\Big\{\left\langle\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathcal{S}\bm{z}-\bm{u}_{d}),\bm{\xi}\right\rangle_{{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)^{*},{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)}+\alpha(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{z},\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{\xi})_{0,\Gamma}+\beta(\bm{z},\bm{\xi})_{0,\Gamma}\Big\}.

We will introduce further assumptions on
{\bm{\mu}}
and
{\bm{\kappa}}
below so that this duality pairing in (
3.18
) becomes the inner product on
L_{t}^{2}(\Gamma)
. As usual, for optimal control of PDEs,
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is the solution operator for a problem similar to the state equation called the
adjoint state equation
: for
{\bm{f}}\in{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)

find
\bm{w}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
such that

(3.19)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\bf{curl}}\,\left({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\right)-(\mathrm{i}\omega){\bm{\kappa}}\bm{w}&={{\bm{f}}}\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\\
\bm{w}\times\bm{n}&=\bm{0}\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma.\end{aligned}

The weak formulation of this problem is: find
\bm{w}\in{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega)
such that

(3.20)

\displaystyle a^{*}(\bm{w},\bm{v})=({\bm{f}},\bm{v})\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega),

where

(3.21)

\displaystyle a^{*}(\bm{w},\bm{v}):=\overline{a(\bm{v},\bm{w})}\qquad\forall\bm{w},\bm{v}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega).

By the Lax-Milgram lemma (see Lemma
2.7
), the above problem is well-posed. Now, given
\bm{\xi}
in
{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
we set
\bm{u}_{\bm{\xi}}=\mathcal{S}\bm{\xi}
, the unique solution to state equation, cf. (
3.1
), namely

(3.22)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} a(\bm{u}_{\bm{\xi}},\bm{v})&=0\qquad&&\forall\bm{v}\in{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega),\\
\gamma_{t}\bm{u}_{\bm{\xi}}&=\bm{\xi}\times\bm{n}\quad&&\mbox{ on }\Gamma.\end{aligned}

In turn, given
{\bm{f}}\in{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)
let
\bm{w}
be the solution for (
3.20
), and testing the first equation in (
3.19
) with
\overline{\mathcal{S}\bm{\xi}}
, and integrating by parts
using (
2.6
) and the fact that
{\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
we arrive at

\displaystyle({\bm{f}},\mathcal{S}\bm{\xi})_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\gamma_{t}({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w}),\gamma_{T}\bm{u}_{\bm{\xi}}\right\rangle_{\Gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\gamma_{t}({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w}),\bm{\xi}\right\rangle_{\Gamma^{*}}.

Recall that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Gamma^{*}}
represents the duality pairing
H_{||}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)\!
and
\!H_{\perp}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
. Nevertheless, under some additional regularity on
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\kappa}
we will show that
\bm{w}
is smooth enough so this duality becomes an integral. Now, by setting
{\bm{f}}=\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{d}
, (
3.19
) becomes the
adjoint problem
for the state equation, and we have the following result.

Theorem 3.10
.

The adjoint operator for
\mathcal{S}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{*}:{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to H_{||}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)

(3.23)

\displaystyle{\bm{f}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathcal{S}^{*}{\bm{f}}=-\gamma_{t}({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w}),

where
\bm{w}\in{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega)
solves

(3.24)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\bf{curl}}\,\left({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\right)-(\mathrm{i}\omega){\bm{\kappa}}\bm{w}&={\bm{f}}\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\\
\bm{w}\times\bm{n}&=0\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma.\end{aligned}

Since,
{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)\hookrightarrow H_{\perp}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
, we can rewrite (
3.17
) as

(3.25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}j(\bm{z};\bm{\xi})=\mathrm{Re}\Big\{\left\langle\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathcal{S}\bm{z}-\bm{u}_{d}),\bm{\xi}\right\rangle_{\Gamma^{*}}+\alpha(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{z},\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{\xi})_{0,\Gamma}+\beta(\bm{z},\bm{\xi})_{0,\Gamma}\Big\},

for all
\bm{\xi}\in{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the above analysis
and the fact that
{\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
, and therefore
\gamma_{t}({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w})\in H_{||}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
, cf. Lemma
2.4
. Finally, since
{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)\hookrightarrow H_{\perp}^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
, therefore,

H_{||}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)=\left(H_{\perp}^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)\right)^{*}\hookrightarrow{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)^{*}
. Thus, we can replace the
{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)^{*}
–
{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
duality pairing in (
3.18
)
by the
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Gamma^{*}}
pairing and the proof is complete.
∎

3.3.
Additional regularity of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
for Lipschitz polyhedra

The goal of this section is to show that we can obtain additional regularity for
\mathcal{S}^{*}
; for instance,
\mathcal{S}^{*}:{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)\to L_{t}^{2}(\Gamma),
under suitable assumptions on
{\bm{\mu}}
and
{\bm{\kappa}}
.
These additional assumptions along with the regularity of the adjoint problem (
3.24
) will imply
\gamma({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w})\in{\bf H}^{\sigma}(\Gamma)
, for some
\sigma>0
. As a result, we can replace the duality pairing in (
3.25
) by the inner product in
\bm{L}^{2}(\Gamma)
.
From now on, we will assume that
{\bm{\kappa}}
and
{\bm{\mu}}
are in
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
. In what follows, we give some technical results that are slight variations of the ones found in
[
45
, Thm. 4.1]
. We omit most of the proof details as they mostly follow from straightforward calculations.

Lemma 3.11
.

If
{\bm{\kappa}}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
such that
{\bm{\kappa}}(x)\geq{\bm{\kappa}}_{0}>0
almost everywhere, and
\phi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then
{\bm{\kappa}}^{-1}\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and

\nabla\phi=\left({\bm{\kappa}}^{-1}\nabla{\bm{\kappa}}\right)\phi+{\bm{\kappa}}\nabla\left({\bm{\kappa}}^{-1}\phi\right).

Proof.

The proof follows immediately after using the product rule in
\nabla(\bm{\kappa}^{-1}\phi)
and rearranging the resulting
expression.
∎

Using this result we obtain the following two lemmas involving the product rule for divergence and curl.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
{\bm{\kappa}}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
such that
{\bm{\kappa}}(x)\geq{\bm{\kappa}}_{0}>0
almost everywhere, and
\bm{u}\in{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that
{\mathrm{div}\,{\bm{\kappa}}\bm{u}=v\in L^{2}(\Omega)}
, then
\bm{u}\in{H}(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
and

(3.26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{u})={{\bm{\kappa}}^{-1}}v-\left({{\bm{\kappa}}^{-1}}\nabla{\bm{\kappa}}\right)\cdot\bm{u}.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\zeta\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\zeta(x)\geq\zeta_{0}>0
almost everywhere, and
\bm{u}\in{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that
{{\bf{curl}}\,\zeta\bm{u}=\bm{v}\in{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)}
, then
\bm{u}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
and

(3.27)

\displaystyle{\bf{curl}}\,(\bm{u})=\zeta^{-1}\bm{v}+\bm{u}\times\left({\zeta^{-1}}\nabla\zeta\right).

We will show that
{\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w}
is smooth enough to have a well-defined and integrable trace, where
\bm{w}
is the solution for the adjoint problem (
3.24
).
To do that, We will now show some results for the regularity of
\bm{w}
,

Lemma 3.14
.

If
\bm{w}\in{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega)
, then
{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\in{H_{0}}(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\mathrm{div}\,({\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w})=0,
then
{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\in{H}(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
and therefore
\gamma_{\bm{n}}({\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w})
belongs to
H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma).
Similarly; for each
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
,
\nabla v\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
, and

\displaystyle\langle\gamma_{\bm{n}}({\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}),\gamma v\rangle_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v\,\mathrm{div}\,({\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w})d\bm{x}+\int_{\Omega}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\cdot\nabla vd\bm{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~0+\int_{\Omega}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf{curl}}\,(\nabla v)d\bm{x}-\langle\gamma_{t}\bm{w},\gamma_{T}(\nabla v)\rangle_{\Gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~0.

Thus, from the surjectivity of the trace map
\gamma
from
H^{1}(\Omega)
onto
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
the proof concludes. ∎

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
\bm{w}
be the solution for the adjoint problem (
3.24
) with
{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\kappa}}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
, then

{\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)\cap{H_{0}}(\mathrm{div};\Omega).

Proof.

We will first invoke Lemma
3.12
with
\bm{u}=\bm{G}\,:={\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}
and
{\bm{\kappa}}={\bm{\mu}}
.
Notice that
\bm{G}\,\in{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathrm{div}\,\bm{\mu}\bm{G}\,=\mathrm{div}\,{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}=0\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Therefore,
\bm{G}\,\in{H}(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
and

(3.28)

\mathrm{div}\,\bm{G}\,=-({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla{\bm{\mu}})\cdot({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w})\in L^{2}(\Omega).

In addition, we notice that
{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{G}\,=(\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{d})+\mathrm{i}\omega{\bm{\kappa}}\bm{w}\in{\bf L}^{2}(\Omega)
. Thus,
\bm{G}\,\in{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)\cap{H}(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
.

Finally, we show that the normal trace of
\bm{G}\,
vanishes to complete the proof. From Lemma
3.11
,
\bm{\mu}^{-1}v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
for all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, this along Lemma
3.14
, and the Green’s identity in
{H}(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
, cf.
[
18
, Lemma 1.4]
, yields

\displaystyle 0=\left\langle\gamma_{\bm{n}}\left({\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\right),\gamma(\bm{\mu}^{-1}v)\right\rangle_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}=

\displaystyle~\int_{\Omega}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\cdot\nabla(\bm{\mu}^{-1}v)dx+\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\mu}^{-1}v)\mathrm{div}\,({\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w})dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mu}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\cdot\bm{\mu}\nabla(\bm{\mu}^{-1}v)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mu}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\cdot\left(\nabla v-\bm{\mu}^{-1}\nabla\bm{\mu}v\right)dx.

Then using (
3.28
), we obtain that

\displaystyle 0=\left\langle\gamma_{\bm{n}}\left({\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\right),\gamma(\bm{\mu}^{-1}v)\right\rangle_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}=

\displaystyle~\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mu}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\cdot\nabla vdx+\int_{\Omega}v\mathrm{div}\,(\bm{\mu}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w})dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\left\langle\gamma_{\bm{n}}\left(\bm{\mu}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{w}\right),\gamma(v)\right\rangle_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\Gamma},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
\bm{w}
be the solution for the adjoint equation (
3.24
), then

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w})\in L_{t}^{2}(\Gamma).

Proof.

From
[
2
, Thm. 4.4]

{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)\cap{H_{0}}(\mathrm{div};\Omega)\hookrightarrow\bm{{\bf H}}^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon_{*}}(\Omega)
, for some
\epsilon_{*}>0
. Thus, according to Theorem
3.15
we have
{\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w}\in\bm{{\bf H}}^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon_{*}}(\Omega)
, then by trace theorem,
\gamma({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w})\in{\bf H}^{\epsilon_{*}}(\Gamma)
. In particular,
\gamma_{t}({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w})\in L_{t}^{2}(\Gamma)
.
∎

As a result of Corollary
3.16
, the duality paring in (
3.25
) becomes an integral, namely

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma_{t}({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w}),\bm{\xi}\right\rangle_{\Gamma^{*}}=

\displaystyle~\left\langle\gamma({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w})\times\bm{n},\bm{\xi}\right\rangle_{\Gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\int_{\Gamma}(\gamma({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}\nabla\times\bm{w})\times\bm{n})\cdot\overline{\bm{\xi}}\,\mathrm{d}S.

Corollary 3.17
.

The function
\widehat{\bm{z}}\in Z
is an optimal control for (
3.3
), if and only if

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{u}}=\,\mathcal{S}\widehat{\bm{z}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\zeta}}=\,\mathcal{S}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{u}}-\bm{u}_{d}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left\{\left(\widehat{\bm{\zeta}},\bm{z}-\widehat{\bm{z}}\right)_{0,\Gamma}\!+\alpha\left(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\widehat{\bm{z}},\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}(\bm{z}-\widehat{\bm{z}})\right)_{0,\Gamma}+\beta\left(\widehat{\bm{z}},\bm{z}-\widehat{\bm{z}}\right)_{0,\Gamma}\right\}=0,\quad\forall\bm{z}\in Z.

Proof.

The result is a consequence of Theorems
3.6
and
3.10
, and Corollary
3.16
.
∎

4.
Discrete Problem

Let
\Omega
be a connected polyhedral Lipschitz domain, and
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h}
be a family of shape regular simplicial triangulation for
\Omega
. For a given mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
we denote the set of faces on the boundary by
\Gamma_{h}
, and the edges belonging to
\Gamma_{h}
by
\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}^{h}
. We consider a conforming finite element space for
{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
denoted by
{\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k}}
, we utilize the basis given in
[
21
]
, here
k=0
denotes the lowest order. Thus, given
\bm{z}\in{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, we consider the semi-discrete state equation: find
\bm{u}_{h}\in{\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k}}
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left({\bm{\mu}}^{-1}{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{u}_{h},{\bf{curl}}\,\bm{v}_{h}\right)_{0,\Omega}+(\mathrm{i}\omega)\left({\bm{\kappa}}\bm{u}_{h},\bm{v}_{h}\right)_{0,\Omega}&=0\quad\forall\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k0},\\
\bm{u}_{h}\times\bm{n}&=\bm{z}\times\bm{n}\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma,\end{aligned}

where
\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k0}:={\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k}}\cap{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega)
. Because
\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k0}
is a closed subspace of
{H}_{0}(\mathrm{\bf curl};\Omega)
well-posedness of (
4.1
) follows from the continuous case, except for how the boundary condition
\bm{u}_{h}\times\bm{n}=\bm{z}\times\bm{n}
is imposed. This can be done in several ways; for instance, with a
L^{2}(\Gamma_{h})
projection plus taking an average for the edge dofs.
Nevertheless, for that approach approximation and commutativity properties seem rather difficult to prove. We impose the Dirichlet condition in (
4.1
) via moments, which allows us to use the well-known approximation theory of Nédélec and Raviart-Thomas elements in 2D along the approximation theory developed in
[
1
]
.

4.1.
Imposition of discrete boundary conditions

As we already mentioned, we impose the condition
\bm{u}_{h}\times\bm{n}=\bm{z}\times\bm{n}
through a lifting defined by moments. Now, the natural strategy would be to use the 2D local moments for
H(\mathrm{div}\,\!;F)
, for each face
F
on
\Gamma_{h}
, which is a collection of linear integral equations; see for instance
[
18
, Sec. 3]
. Nevertheless, because we consider
\bm{z}\times\bm{n}
instead of just
\bm{z}
, imposing those moments turns out to be equivalent to imposing
\gamma_{T}\bm{u}_{h}=\bm{z}
through the local moments for
H({\bf{curl}}\,\!,F)
, cf.
[
33
, Sec. 5.5]
. We now define a global lifting operator

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{L}_{h}}:\mathcal{D}_{{\mathcal{L}_{h}}}\subseteq{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)&\mapsto{\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k}}\\
\bm{z}&\mapsto{\mathcal{L}_{h}}\bm{z}:=\bm{u}_{z}^{h},\end{aligned}

where
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathcal{L}_{h}}}
denotes the domain of
{\mathcal{L}_{h}}
which is a finite subspace of
{H}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
and its elements have well-defined and continuous moments on
\Gamma_{h}
,
\bm{u}^{h}_{z}
is the unique element in
{\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k}}
which shares the local 2D Nédélec moments with
\bm{z}
for all the faces/edges in
\Gamma_{h}
, and it has zero interior moments. Because of the regularity needed for this lifting, we will choose the
discrete control space
Z^{h}
so that the following holds

(4.3)

\displaystyle Z^{h}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{{\mathcal{L}_{h}}}\subseteq\gamma_{T}\mathcal{N}^{h}_{j},~\mbox{ for some }j\in\{0,...,k\}.

4.2.
Discrete solution operator and cost functional

Let
j\in\{0,...,k\}
and define
{Z^{h}\!:=\bm{R}_{h}\!\cap\!Z}
, where

(4.4)

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\gamma_{T}\bm{u}_{h}:\bm{u}_{h}\in\mathcal{N}^{h}_{j}\right\}.

Now, let us introduce the discrete optimization problem,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{\bm{u}_{h},\bm{z}_{h}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{u}_{h},\bm{z}_{h}):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\bm{u}_{h}-\bm{u}_{d}|^{2}\,d\bm{x}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Gamma}|\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{z}_{h}|^{2}\,dS+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\Gamma}|\bm{z}_{h}|^{2}\,dS,

where
\bm{u}_{h}
is the unique solution to (
4.1
), for
\bm{z}=\bm{z}_{h}
.
As in the continuous case, we reduce this problem with the help of a (discrete) solution operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{h}:Z^{h}\subseteq\bm{R}_{h}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k}}

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{h}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{h}\bm{z}_{h}=:\bm{u}_{h}.

To prove that
\mathcal{S}_{h}
is bounded, with constant independent of
h
, let us consider the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\bm{u}
and
\bm{u}_{h}
be the solutions for the continuous and discrete state equations, (
1.1b
) and (
4.1
), respectively, with respective boundary data given by
\bm{z}\times\bm{n}
and
\bm{z}_{h}\times\bm{n}
. Then, the following holds

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{h}\|_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}\leq C\left(\min_{\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}^{h}}\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}_{h}\|_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}+\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{h}\|_{H^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}_{\perp}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)}\right),

where
C
only depends on the shape regularity of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
\Omega
and the eigenvalues of
{\bm{\mu}}
and
{\bm{\kappa}}
.

Proof.

From
[
1
, Corollary 4.4]
, we have

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{h}\|_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}\leq C\left(\min_{\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}^{h}}\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}_{h}\|_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}+\|\bm{z}\times\bm{n}-\bm{z}_{h}\times\bm{n}\|_{H^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}_{||}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)}\right)

Finally, from the well-known isometry for weak rotations between
H^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}_{\perp}(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
and
H^{-{\frac{1}{2}}}_{||}(\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma};\Gamma)
, see
[
38
, p. 448]
, the proof concludes.
∎

A useful result, related to (
4.6
),
is the existence of a uniformly bounded operator

\displaystyle L_{h}:\bm{R}_{h}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\mathcal{N}^{h}_{k}}

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{h}

\displaystyle\mapsto\bm{u}_{\bm{z}_{h}},

such that

\gamma_{T}(\bm{u}_{\bm{z}_{h}})=\bm{z}_{h},
and
\|\bm{u}_{\bm{z}_{h}}\|_{{\bf{curl}}\,,\Omega}\leq C\|\bm{z}_{h}\|_{\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}},

where
C
is independent of
h
, see
[
1
]
and references within. Thus,
\mathcal{S}_{h}
is linear and bounded independently of
h
.
We now introduce the reduced discrete cost functional

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} j_{h}:Z^{h}&\mapsto\mathbb{R}\,,\\
\bm{z}_{h}&\mapsto j_{h}(\bm{z}_{h}):=\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{S}_{h}(\bm{z}_{h}),\bm{z}_{h}).\end{aligned}

It is clear that,
j_{h}
is continuous and bounded independently of
h
. Now, under the same arguments as for the continuous case we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\mathbb{R}}j_{h}(\bm{z}_{h},\bm{\xi}_{h})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\{\left(\mathcal{S}_{h}\bm{z}_{h}-\bm{u}_{d},\mathcal{S}_{h}\bm{\xi}_{h}\right)_{0,\Omega}+\alpha(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{z}_{h},\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{\xi}_{h})_{0,\Gamma}+\beta(\bm{z}_{h},\bm{\xi}_{h})_{0,\Gamma}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\{\left\langle\mathcal{S}_{h}^{*}(\mathcal{S}_{h}\bm{z}_{h}-\bm{u}_{d}),\bm{\xi}_{h}\right\rangle_{\Gamma^{*}}+\alpha(\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{z}_{h},\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{\xi}_{h})_{0,\Gamma}+\beta(\bm{z}_{h},\bm{\xi}_{h})_{0,\Gamma}\}

for all
\bm{z}_{h},
and
\bm{\xi}_{h}
in
Z^{h}
. Where the action of the discrete adjoint operator
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{*}
will de defined later, cf. (
4.19
).

4.3.
Particular choice of discrete control space

For simplicity and because most of the theory for Nédélec elements focuses on this case, we consider the lowest order space for our discrete control
\bm{z}_{h}
, along with a higher order approximation for
\bm{u}_{h}
. As usual, we just construct the real-valued spaces. It turns out that, it is easier to inherit properties for
\bm{z}_{h}
from the space of
\bm{z}_{h}\times\bm{n}
, which is related to the Raviart-Thomas space.
In fact, given
F\in\Gamma_{h}
we recall that the local Raviart-Thomas space
\mathcal{R}T_{k}(F)
is just a
\pi/{2}
-rotation of Nédélec’s space
\mathcal{N}_{k}(F)
, see
[
17
]
.
This idea can be easily generalized to the piecewise linear manifold
\Gamma_{h}
. In order to do that, we first introduce the Raviart-Thomas space for
\Gamma_{h}

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}T_{k}(\Gamma_{h}):=\left\{\bm{q}\in L_{t}^{2}(\Gamma)\colon\,\forall F\in\Gamma_{h},~\bm{q}|_{F}\in\mathcal{R}T_{k}(F)~\mbox{ and }~\forall{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}^{h},~[\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\nu}]_{{\bm{e}}}=0\right\},

where
[\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\nu}]_{{\bm{e}}}=0
denotes the continuity of
\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\nu}
across the edge
{\bm{e}}
, and

\bm{\nu}
is the 2D “normal vector” on
{\bm{e}}
, this will be clarified later, see (
4.13
). In the same manner, the space for the discrete control
\bm{z}_{h}
will be a subspace of the Nédélec space for
\Gamma_{h}
,
which is given by

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k}(\Gamma_{h}):=\left\{\bm{q}\in L_{t}^{2}(\Gamma)\colon\,\forall F\in\Gamma_{h},~\bm{q}|_{F}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(F)~\mbox{ and }~\forall{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}^{h},~[\bm{q}]_{{\bm{e}}}\cdot\bm{t}_{{\bm{e}}}=0\right\},

where
\bm{t}_{{\bm{e}}}
is a unitary vector along the edge
{\bm{e}}
. Then, it follows that
\mathcal{R}T_{k}(\Gamma_{h})
is a
\pi/{2}
-rotation of
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\Gamma_{h})
but just facewise, which can be compactly represented as
\mathcal{R}T_{k}(\Gamma_{h})=\mathcal{N}_{k}(\Gamma_{h})\times\bm{n}
. Now, we show that
for the lowest order case,

k=0
, the identity
\mathcal{R}T_{k}(\Gamma_{h})=\mathcal{N}_{k}(\Gamma_{h})\times\bm{n}
can be reduced to an identity between elements of a particular choice of bases. To show that, notice that
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}T_{0}(\Gamma_{h})
is given by

\displaystyle\left\{\bm{q}\in L_{t}^{2}(\Gamma)\colon\,\forall F\in\Gamma_{h},\exists\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\exists b\in\mathbb{R}\,,~\bm{q}|_{F}=\bm{a}+b\bm{x}~\mbox{ and }~\forall{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}^{h},~[\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\nu}]_{{\bm{e}}}=0\right\}.

For a global basis for this space we consider the Rao-Wilton-Glisson basis
[
37
]
.
From now on, we assume that all the faces on
\Gamma_{h}
are oriented counterclockwise in terms of its vertices, and the normal vector of a face points outward. Now, let us consider an edge
{\bm{e}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}^{h}
and the two faces,
F\!\!_{+}
and
F\!\!_{-}
on
\Gamma_{h}
, which share
{\bm{e}}
. Therefore, without loss of generality, we assume that
F\!\!_{-}
has
{\bm{e}}
with a negative orientation. We now want to find

\bm{\psi}_{{\bm{e}}}
in
\mathcal{R}T_{0}(\Gamma_{h})\cap H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{-}(\Gamma)
such that
\bm{\psi}_{{\bm{e}}}\cdot\bm{\nu}|_{F\!\!_{+}}+\bm{\psi}_{{\bm{e}}}\cdot\bm{\nu}|_{F\!\!_{-}}
vanishes point-wise on
{\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}^{h}}
.
Here the main difference between this case and the 2D-case is that
\bm{\nu}|_{F\!\!_{+}}\neq-\bm{\nu}|_{F\!\!_{-}}
in general. Nevertheless, “the definition for Raviart-Thomas basis” is the same for this case. Consider,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bm{\psi}_{{\bm{e}}}:\Gamma_{h}&\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{3}\!\!,\\

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{\Gamma}\bm{\phi}_{{\bm{e}}}|_{F\!_{\pm}}=\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\bm{\psi}_{{\bm{e}}}|_{F\!_{\pm}}=\pm\frac{|{\bm{e}}|}{|F\!_{\pm}|}\mathrm{curl}_{\Gamma}\left((\bm{r}-\bm{r}(\bm{v}^{\pm}_{{\bm{e}}}))\right)=\pm\frac{|{\bm{e}}|}{|F\!_{\pm}|}({\bf{curl}}\,(\bm{r}-\bm{r}(\bm{v}^{\pm}_{{\bm{e}}})))\cdot\bm{n}_{F}=0,

which concludes the proof.
∎
