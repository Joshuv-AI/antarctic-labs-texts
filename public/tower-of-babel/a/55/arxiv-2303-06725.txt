Computing Gröbner bases and free resolutions of OI-modules
Michael Morrow, Uwe Nagel

Abstract
. Given a sequence of related modules M n M_{n} defined over a sequence of related polynomial rings, one may ask how to simultaneously compute a finite Gröbner basis for each M n M_{n} . Furthermore, one may ask how to simultaneously compute the module of syzygies of each M n M_{n} . In this paper we address both questions. Working in the setting of OI-modules over a Noetherian polynomial OI-algebra, we provide OI-analogues of Buchberger’s Criterion, Buchberger’s Algorithm for computing Gröbner bases, and Schreyer’s Theorem for computing syzygies. We also establish a stabilization result for Gröbner bases.



1.
Introduction

Computing Gröbner bases and syzygies is a classical problem in commutative and computational algebra. Recall that if
R
is a Noetherian polynomial ring over a field and
F
is a free module over
R
of finite rank, then a finite Gröbner basis of any finitely generated submodule
M
of
F
can be computed using Buchberger’s Algorithm. Furthermore, a theorem of Schreyer shows how to determine a finite Gröbner basis for the module of syzygies of
M
. These results can be used to give a constructive proof of Hilbert’s Syzygy Theorem which asserts that
M
has a finite free resolution. We refer the reader to
[
4
, Chapter 15]
for more on this topic. In this paper we extend these results to a setting where we consider a sequence
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
of related modules
M_{n}
over related polynomial rings whose number of variables increases with
n
. We show that there is a finite algorithm that simultaneously computes Gröbner bases of all modules in the sequence.

Gröbner bases in contexts beyond Noethertian polynomial rings have been considered previously, mainly for ideals (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
8
]
). Typically they focus on ideals in polynomial rings with infinitely many variables. However, in order to treat also modules a more abstract approach is beneficial.
In
[
10
]
, Nagel and Römer introduced OI-modules over OI-algebras, which may be viewed as a generalization of OI-modules over a fixed ring studied in
[
12
]
. In particular, Noetherian polynomial OI-algebras are classified in
[
10
]
. Furthermore, it is shown that any submodule
\mathbf{M}
of a finitely generated free OI-module
\mathbf{F}
over a Noetherian polynomial OI-algebra
\mathbf{P}
has a finite Gröbner basis. It follows that
\mathbf{F}
is Noetherian. The Gröbner basis result has also been used, for example in
[
11
]
, where it is shown that the equivariant Hilbert series of any finitely generated graded OI-module over
\mathbf{P}
is rational.

The argument for the existence of a finite Gröbner basis in
[
10
]
is not constructive. We address the question of computing such a Gröbner basis in
Section

3
.
In particular, we give a criterion for determining when a subset of
\mathbf{M}
forms a Gröbner basis of

\mathbf{M}
(see Theorem
3.16
), and use it to describe a finite algorithm for computing a finite Gröbner basis (see Algorithm
3.17
). A key ingredient for our finiteness results is a combinatorial statement about simultaneously factoring OI-morphisms (Lemma
3.10
). Any OI-module
\mathbf{M}
may be thought of as a sequence
(\mathbf{M}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
of
\mathbf{P}_{n}
-modules
\mathbf{M}_{n}
. It follows that our algorithm for
\mathbf{M}
produces a Gröbner basis for every
\mathbf{M}_{n}
. This is possible because stabilization occurs in the sense that there is an integer
n_{0}
such that the Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}_{n}
can be determined from the Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}_{n_{0}}
if
n\geq n_{0}
. Deciding a priori when stabilization occurs, i.e. estimating
n_{0}
, is a very interesting and open problem. We establish a condition that guarantees stabilization has occurred (see
3.21
).

We also consider the problem of computing syzygies. Given a finite Gröbner basis
G
of a submodule
\mathbf{M}
of a finitely generated free
\mathbf{P}
-module
\mathbf{F}
, it is shown in Section
4
how to determine a finite Gröbner basis for the module of syzygies of
\mathbf{M}
in terms of
G
(see
4.6
). This may be seen as an extension of Schreyer’s result that solves this problem in the classical setting.

The finiteness results in
[
10
]
were used to establish that a finitely generated
\mathbf{P}
-module
\mathbf{M}
has a free resolution with finitely generated free
\mathbf{P}
-modules. However, only a few explicit resolutions are known, see
[
5
,
6
]
for families of examples. Furthermore, if
\mathbf{M}
is a graded OI-module it is shown in
[
5
]
that
\mathbf{M}
has a graded minimal free resolution
\mathbf{F}^{\bullet}
. Here minimality means that in every homological degree
d
the rank of
\mathbf{F}^{d}
is at most the rank of
\mathbf{G}^{d}
in any other graded free resolution
\mathbf{G}^{\bullet}
of
\mathbf{M}
. By
[
5
]
, such a minimal free resolution is unique up to isomorphism of complexes.
In Section
5
we apply our results about Gröbner bases and syzygies to give a procedure for computing free resolutions. In particular, if
\mathbf{M}
is graded, we show how to compute a graded minimal free resolution of
\mathbf{M}
up to any chosen homological degree.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we recall the necessary background on OI-modules and fix notation.
Let
K
be a Noetherian commutative ring with identity.

Definition 2.1
.

Let OI be the category of totally ordered finite sets and order-preserving injective maps. An OI-
algebra
over
K
is a (covariant) functor from OI to the category of commutative, associative, unital
K
-algebras.

To define a functor out of OI, it is enough to define it on the corresponding skeleton. Therefore, we regard OI as the category whose objects are intervals of the form
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
(we put
[0]=\emptyset
) and whose morphisms are order-preserving injective maps

[m]\to[n]
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is any functor out of OI, we typically write
\mathbf{A}_{n}
in place of
\mathbf{A}([n])
. We refer to
\mathbf{A}_{n}
as the
width
n
component
of
\mathbf{A}
. If
\varepsilon
is an OI-morphism, we often write
\varepsilon_{*}
instead of
\mathbf{A}(\varepsilon)
. We abuse notation and write
\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
for the set of OI-morphisms
\textnormal{Hom}_{\textnormal{OI}}([m],[n])
from
[m]
to
[n]
.

Polynomial OI-algebras were introduced in
[
10
, Definition 2.17]
. This paper also identifies the Noetherian algebras among these. Following the notation of
[
10
]
, their building block is the following algebra.

Definition 2.2
.

Define an OI-algebra
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1}
by letting

\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1}_{n}=\begin{cases}K&\text{ if }n=0\\
K[x_{\pi}\;:\;\pi\in\textnormal{Hom}(1,n)]&\text{ if }n>0\end{cases}

and, for
\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
, defining
\varepsilon_{*}:\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1}_{m}\to\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1}_{n}
via
x_{\pi}\mapsto x_{\varepsilon\circ\pi}
.

By
[
10
, Proposition 4.8 and Theorem 6.15]
), apart from trivial direct summands, any Noetherian polynomial OI-algebra is isomorphic to
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1})^{\otimes c}
for some integer
c>0
. Identifying each
\pi
with its image in
[n]
, one may intuitively think of
\mathbf{P}=(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1})^{\otimes c}
as a sequence
(\mathbf{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
of polynomial rings, where

\mathbf{P}_{n}=K\begin{bmatrix}x_{1,1}&\cdots&x_{1,n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{c,1}&\cdots&x_{c,n}\end{bmatrix}

has
cn
variables, together with
K
-algebra homomorphisms
\varepsilon_{*}\colon\mathbf{P}_{m}\to\mathbf{P}_{n}
induced by any

\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
and defined by
x_{i,j}\mapsto x_{i,\varepsilon(j)}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Replacing the category OI by the category FI that has the same skeleton as OI, but whose morphisms are injective maps
[m]\to[n]
, one defines FI-algebras and polynomial FI-algebras

(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{FI},1})^{\otimes c}
analogously (see
[
10
, Definition 2.4]
for details). Every FI algebra is also an OI-algebra. In
[
3
]
, FI-algebras that are finitely generated in width one are studied. These are precisely the quotients of polynomial FI algebras

(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{FI},1})^{\otimes c}
.

We say an OI-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is
graded
if each
\mathbf{A}_{n}
is a graded
K
-algebra and each
\varepsilon_{*}\colon\mathbf{A}_{m}\to\mathbf{A}_{n}
is a graded homomorphism of degree 0. Any algebra

\mathbf{P}=(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1})^{\otimes c}
is an example of a graded OI-algebra by assigning each variable
degree 1. Throughout this note, we always use this grading of
\mathbf{P}
.

We now turn to OI-modules over OI-algebras.

Definition 2.4

(
[
10
]
)
.

An OI-
module

\mathbf{M}
over an OI-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is a (covariant) functor from OI to the category of
K
-modules such that

(i)

each
\mathbf{M}_{n}
is an
\mathbf{A}_{n}
-module, and

(ii)

for each
a\in\mathbf{A}_{m}
and
\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
we have a commuting diagram

(2.1)

\textstyle{\mathbf{M}_{m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{a\cdot}
\scriptstyle{\mathbf{M}(\varepsilon)}
\textstyle{\mathbf{M}_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathbf{A}(\varepsilon)(a)\cdot}
\textstyle{\mathbf{M}_{m}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathbf{M}(\varepsilon)}
\textstyle{\mathbf{M}_{n}}

where the vertical maps are multiplication by the indicated elements.

We sometimes refer to
\mathbf{M}
as an
\mathbf{A}
-
module
.

Intuitively, one may think of an
\mathbf{A}
-module
\mathbf{M}
as a sequence
(\mathbf{M}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
of
\mathbf{A}_{n}
-modules
\mathbf{M}_{n}
together with maps
\varepsilon_{*}\colon\mathbf{M}_{m}\to\mathbf{M}_{n}
induced by any
\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
.

A
homomorphism
of
\mathbf{A}
-modules is a natural transformation
\varphi:\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{N}
such that each
\varphi_{n}\colon\mathbf{M}_{n}\to\mathbf{N}_{n}
is an
\mathbf{A}_{n}
-module homomorphism. We sometimes call
\varphi
an
\mathbf{A}
-
linear map
.
\mathbf{A}
-modules together with
\mathbf{A}
-linear maps form an abelian category denoted
\textnormal{OI}\textnormal{-Mod}(\mathbf{A})
. Thus, all concepts such as subobject, quotient object, kernel, cokernel, injection, and surjection are defined “width-wise” from the corresponding concepts in the category of
K
-modules (see
[
13
, A.3.3]
). For example, the
direct sum
of OI-modules
\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N}
is the OI-module
\mathbf{M}\oplus\mathbf{N}
defined width-wise by

(\mathbf{M}\oplus\mathbf{N})_{n}=\mathbf{M}_{n}\oplus\mathbf{N}_{n}

for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Similarly, if
\varphi\colon\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{N}
is an
\mathbf{A}
-linear map then the kernel of
\varphi
is a submodule of
\mathbf{M}
defined by
(\textnormal{ker}(\varphi))_{n}=\textnormal{ker}(\varphi_{n})
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. The image of
\varphi
is a submodule of
\mathbf{N}
defined analogously.

Note that any OI-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is trivially an OI-module over itself. An
ideal
of
\mathbf{A}
is a submodule of
\mathbf{A}
.

If
f\in\mathbf{M}_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
then we call
f
an
element
of
\mathbf{M}
and write
f\in\mathbf{M}
. In this case we also say that
f

has width
n
, denoted
w(f)=n
.
A
subset

S
of
\mathbf{M}
, denoted
S\subseteq\mathbf{M}
, is a subset of the disjoint union
\coprod_{n\geq 0}\mathbf{M}_{n}
. The submodule of
\mathbf{M}

generated by
a subset
S\subseteq\mathbf{M}
is defined to be the smallest submodule of
\mathbf{M}
containing
S
and is denoted
\langle S\rangle_{\mathbf{M}}
.
For any integer
m\geq 0
we define the
m
-
orbit
of
S
as the set

\textnormal{Orb}(S,m)=\{\mathbf{M}(\varepsilon)(s)\;:\;s\in S\cap\mathbf{M}_{\ell},\;\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(\ell,m)\}\subseteq\mathbf{M}_{m}.

Thus, for every
m\geq 0
we have

(\langle S\rangle_{\mathbf{M}})_{m}=\langle\textnormal{Orb}(S,m)\rangle,

where the right-hand side is generated as an
\mathbf{A}_{m}
-submodule of
\mathbf{M}_{m}
.

Example 2.5
.

Consider
\mathbf{P}=(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1})^{\otimes 2}
and
f=x_{1,1}x_{2,2}-x_{1,2}x_{2,1}\in\mathbf{P}_{2}
. Let
\mathbf{I}
be the ideal of
\mathbf{P}
generated by
f
. Then
\mathbf{I}_{n}
is the ideal of
\mathbf{P}_{n}
that is generated by the 2-minors of a generic
2\times n

Let now
\mathbf{J}
be the ideal of
\mathbf{P}
that is generated by
g=x_{1,1}x_{2,2}-x_{1,2}x_{2,1}\in\mathbf{P}_{3}
. Then

\mathbf{I}_{n-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}

\mathbf{I}_{n-1}

\mathbf{P}_{n}

\mathbf{J}_{2}=0

\mathbf{I}_{2}=\langle f\rangle\neq 0

Finally, let
\mathbf{L}
be the ideal of
\mathbf{P}
that is generated by
x_{1,1}x_{2,3}-x_{1,3}x_{2,1}\in\mathbf{P}_{3}
. Then
\mathbf{L}_{n}
is the ideal of
\mathbf{P}_{n}
that is generated by the 2-minors of a generic
2\times n
matrix using columns
i,j
with
j\geq i+2
.

We now define the most important class of OI-modules for our purposes.

Definition 2.6

(
[
10
]
)
.

For any integer
d\geq 0
, define an OI-module
\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d}
over an OI-algebra
\mathbf{A}
as follows: For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d}_{n}=\bigoplus_{\pi\in\textnormal{Hom}(d,n)}\mathbf{A}_{n}e_{\pi}\cong(\mathbf{A}_{n})^{\binom{n}{d}}.

For
\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)

\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d}(\varepsilon)\colon\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d}_{m}\to\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d}_{n}

e_{\pi}\mapsto e_{\varepsilon\circ\pi}
.

A
free
OI-module over
\mathbf{A}
is an OI-module
\mathbf{F}
isomorphic to a direct sum

\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d_{\lambda}}
for integers
d_{\lambda}\geq 0
. If
|\Lambda|=n<\infty
, then
\mathbf{F}
is said to have
rank
n
.

To stress the dependence on
\mathbf{A}
we also write
\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d}_{\mathbf{A}}
for
\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d}
.

The fact that rank is well-defined is a special case of
[
5
, Lemma 3.6]
. Note that
\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},0}_{\mathbf{A}}
is simply the OI-algebra
\mathbf{A}
considered as an OI-module over itself.

Remark 2.7
.

Let
\mathbf{F}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d_{i}}
be a finitely generated, free OI-module.

(i)

For all
n\geq 0
we have

\mathbf{F}_{n}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\textnormal{Hom}(d_{i},n)\\
1\leq i\leq s\end{subarray}}\mathbf{A}_{n}e_{\pi,i}

where the second index on
e_{\pi,i}
is used to keep track of which summand it lives in.

(ii)

\mathbf{F}
is generated as an OI-module by all
e_{\textnormal{id}_{[d_{i}]},i}
. We call these the
basis elements
of
\mathbf{F}
, and any
e_{\pi,i}
is said to be a
local basis element
of
\mathbf{F}
.

(iii)

Let
f=\sum a_{i}e_{\pi_{i},k_{i}}\in\mathbf{F}_{m}
and let
\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
. By Condition (
2.1
), we have

\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(f)=\sum\varepsilon_{*}(a_{i})e_{\varepsilon\circ\pi_{i},k_{i}}.

(iv)

Any
\mathbf{A}
-linear map out of
\mathbf{F}
is determined by the images of the elements
e_{\textnormal{id}_{[d_{i}]},i}
.

Example 2.8
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1}
, so that
\mathbf{P}_{n}=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
for

. Consider
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},1}_{\mathbf{P}}\oplus\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},2}_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{1}

\displaystyle=K[x_{1}],

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{2}

\displaystyle=K[x_{1},x_{2}]^{2}\oplus K[x_{1},x_{2}]\cong K[x_{1},x_{2}]^{3},

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{3}

\displaystyle=K[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]^{3}\oplus K[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]^{3}\cong K[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]^{6},

\mathbf{M}

3.
Gröbner Bases and the OI-Buchberger’s Algorithm

For the rest of the paper, we assume that
K
is a field and consider OI-modules over a polynomial OI-algebra
\mathbf{P}=(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1})^{\otimes c}
with some
c\geq 1
. It has been shown in
[
10
, Theorem 6.14]
(see
[
11
, Theorem 5.6]
) that every submodule of a finitely generated free
\mathbf{P}
-module has a finite Gröbner basis.
The goal of this section is to produce an algorithm that computes such a Gröbner basis in finitely many steps.

We begin by recalling needed concepts.
Let
\mathbf{F}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d_{i}}_{\mathbf{P}}
be a free
\mathbf{P}
-module with basis
\{e_{\textnormal{id}_{[d_{i}]},i}\;:\;i\in[s]\}
.
A
monomial
in
\mathbf{F}
is an element of the form
ae_{\pi,i}
where
a
is a monomial in
\mathbf{P}
. We say the monomial
ae_{\pi,i}

involves the basis element
e_{\textnormal{id}_{[d_{i}]},i}
. The set of all monomials in
\mathbf{F}
is denoted
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{F})
. A
monomial submodule of
\mathbf{F}
is a submodule of
\mathbf{F}
generated by monomials.

We need to order the monomials in
\mathbf{F}
.

Definition 3.1

(cf.
[
10
,
11
]
)
.

A
monomial order on
\mathbf{F}
is a total order
<
on
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{F})
such that if
\mu<\nu
for any monomials
\mu,\nu\in\mathbf{F}_{m}
, one has

(i)

\mu<a\mu<a\nu
for any monomial
a\neq 1
in
\mathbf{P}_{m}
, and

(ii)

\mu<\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(\mu)<\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(\nu)
for every
\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
with
m<n
.

For example, if
c=1
, i.e.
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1}
and
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},0}\cong\mathbf{P}
consider
x_{1}e_{\pi}\in\mathbf{F}_{5}
and
x_{1}e_{\rho}\in\mathbf{F}_{3}
. Note that
\pi
maps the empty set to
[5]
, whereas

\rho
maps the empty set to
[3]
. Choose any map
\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(3,5)
with
\varepsilon(1)=1
. Then Condition (ii) in
3.1
gives
x_{1}e_{\rho}<\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(x_{1}e_{\rho})=x_{\varepsilon(1)}e_{\varepsilon\circ\rho}=x_{1}e_{\pi}
.

Monomial orders exist.

Example 3.2

(Lexicographic Order)
.

Order the monomials in each
\mathbf{P}_{m}
lexicographically with
x_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}<x_{i,j}
if either
i^{\prime}<i
or
i^{\prime}=i
and
j^{\prime}<j
. Define
e_{\rho,j}<e_{\pi,i}
if
i<j
. For a fixed
i
, identify a monomial
e_{\pi,i}\in\mathbf{F}_{m}
with a vector
(m,\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(d_{i}))\in\mathbb{N}^{d_{i}+1}
and order such monomials using the lexicographic order on
\mathbb{N}^{d_{i}+1}
. Finally, for monomials
ae_{\pi,i}
and
be_{\rho,j}
in
\mathbf{F}
, define
be_{\rho,j}<ae_{\pi,i}
if
e_{\rho,j}<e_{\pi,i}
or
e_{\pi,i}=e_{\rho,j}
and
b<a
in
\mathbf{P}
. One checks that this gives indeed a monomial order on
\mathbf{F}
.

There is a suitable generalization of Dickson’s lemma in our context. Following
[
10
, Definition 6.1]
, a monomial
be_{\sigma,j}
is said to be OI-
divisible
by a monomial
ae_{\pi,i}
if
i=j
and there is an OI-morphism
\varepsilon
such that
\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(ae_{\pi,i})=\varepsilon_{*}(a)e_{\varepsilon\circ\pi,i}
divides
be_{\sigma,j}
. It was shown in
[
10
]
(see also
[
11
, Proposition 5.3]
) that OI-divisibility gives a well-partial-order on
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{F})
. Since any monomial order on
\mathbf{F}
refines the OI-divisibility partial order on
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{F})
it follows that any monomial order
<
on
\mathbf{F}
is a
well-order
on
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{F})
, i.e. any nonempty subset of
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{F})
has a unique minimal element with respect to
<
.

The following concepts are defined as in the classical situation.

Definition 3.3
.

Fix a monomial order
<
on
\mathbf{F}
. Let
0\not=f\in\mathbf{F}
and write
f=\sum c_{i}\mu_{i}
for nonzero
c_{i}\in K
and distinct
\mu_{i}\in\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{F})
. The
leading monomial
of
f
is
\textnormal{lm}(f)=\max_{<}\{\mu_{i}\}
. If
\textnormal{lm}(f)=\mu_{i}
then the
leading coefficient
of
f
is
\textnormal{lc}(f)=c_{i}
. The
leading term
of
f
is
\textnormal{lt}(f)=\textnormal{lc}(f)\cdot\textnormal{lm}(f)
. Given any subset
E\subseteq\mathbf{F}
we set
\textnormal{lm}(E)=\{\textnormal{lm}(f)\;:\;f\in E\text{ is nonzero}\}
.

Example 3.4
.

Consider elements
a=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{t}\in\mathbf{P}
and
f=f_{1}+\cdots+f_{s}\in\mathbf{F}
with monomials
a_{i}\in\mathbf{P}
and
f_{j}\in\mathbf{F}
. Possibly after reindexing, assume
f_{1}>f_{2}>\cdots>f_{s}
and
a_{1}f_{1}>a_{2}f_{1}>\cdots>a_{t}f_{1}
. It follows that
a_{1}f_{1}>a_{i}f_{j}
if
(i,j)\neq(1,1)
, and so
\textnormal{lm}(af)=a_{1}f_{1}=a_{1}\textnormal{lm}(f)
.

The properties of a monomial order on
\mathbf{F}
imply the following useful facts.

Remark 3.5
.

For any nonzero
f,g\in\mathbf{F}_{m}
and
\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
, one has:

(i)

\textnormal{lm}(f+g)\leq\max(\textnormal{lm}(f),\textnormal{lm}(g))
,

(ii)

\textnormal{lm}(af)=a\textnormal{lm}(f)
for any monomial
a\in\mathbf{P}_{m}
, and

(iii)

\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(\textnormal{lm}(f))=\textnormal{lm}(\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(f))
.

We now define our primary object of study.

Definition 3.6

(
[
10
,
11
]
)
.

Fix a monomial order
<
on
\mathbf{F}
and let
\mathbf{M}
be a submodule of
\mathbf{F}
. A subset

is called a
Gröbner basis
(with respect to
<
) of
\mathbf{M}
if

\langle\textnormal{lm}(\mathbf{M})\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}=\langle\textnormal{lm}(G)\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}.

Every submodule of
\mathbf{M}
of
\mathbf{F}
has a Gröbner basis by taking
G=\mathbf{M}
. We are interested in determining a finite Gröbner basis, which is guaranteed to exist.

For the remainder of this section, we fix a monomial order
<
on
\mathbf{F}
. By restricting

to
\mathbf{F}_{n}
we obtain a monomial order on the free
\mathbf{P}_{n}
-module
\mathbf{F}_{n}
in the classical sense (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
), which we denote by
<_{n}
. The following result relates Gröbner bases of OI-modules with Gröbner bases of their width
n
components.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a submodule of
\mathbf{F}
. For any subset
G\subseteq\mathbf{M}
, set
G_{n}=\textnormal{Orb}(G,n)
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then
G
is a Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}
if and only if each
G_{n}
is a Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}_{n}
with respect to
<_{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
is a Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}
and let
h\in\mathbf{M}_{n}
. Then
\textnormal{lm}_{<_{n}}(h)=\textnormal{lm}(h)\in\langle\textnormal{lm}(G)\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
so that
\textnormal{lm}_{<_{n}}(h)
is divisible by some
\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(\textnormal{lm}(g))=\textnormal{lm}_{<_{n}}(\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(g))
with
g\in G
and
\varepsilon\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(g),n)
.
As
\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon)(g)
is in
G_{n}
, this
shows that
\textnormal{lm}_{<_{n}}(\mathbf{M}_{n})\subseteq\langle\textnormal{lm}_{<_{n}}(G_{n})\rangle
. Thus,
G_{n}
is a Gröbner basis for
\mathbf{M}_{n}
.

The argument for the reverse implication is similar and left to the reader.
∎

Combined with
[
10
, Lemma 2.3]
, the above observation says that, if we regard
\mathbf{M}
as a sequence of modules
(\mathbf{M}_{n})_{n}
, then computing a finite Gröbner basis

of
\mathbf{M}
is the same as “simultaneously” computing a Gröbner basis for each module
\mathbf{M}_{n}
with the additional property that the Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}_{n}
is obtained “combinatorially” by the OI-action from the Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}_{w}
for each

, where

is the maximum width of an element in
G
.

The basis for our computational results is a division algorithm.

Definition 3.8
.

Let

and

. An element

is called a
remainder of

modulo

for some

and

(R1)

either

\textnormal{lm}(r)
is not OI-divisible by any element of
\textnormal{lm}(B)
,

(R2)

\textnormal{lm}(r)<\textnormal{lm}(f)
provided

and

, and

(R3)

\textnormal{lm}(a_{i}q_{i})\leq\textnormal{lm}(f)
whenever

.

Every element

has a remainder modulo

. If

has width

can be computed by applying the classical division algorithm in

to

and the set
\textnormal{Orb}(B,n)
with respect to the monomial order

on

(see, e.g,
[

, Ch. 15]
).

Remark 3.9
.

(i)
Note that Condition (R3) implies

for any monomial

in

, see

.

(ii)
An important property of remainders is that if

has a remainder of zero modulo

, then

also has a remainder of zero modulo

(iii) and Definition

(ii).

We now introduce an important combinatorial result about OI-morphisms that will serve as a key ingredient for our finiteness arguments.

Lemma 3.10

(OI-Factorization Lemma)
.

Consider any maps
\sigma\in\textnormal{Hom}(k_{1},m)
and
\tau\in\textnormal{Hom}(k_{2},m)
with
k_{1},k_{2},m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
k_{1},k_{2}\leq m
. Set
\ell=|\textnormal{im}(\sigma)\cup\textnormal{im}(\tau)|
. Then there are maps
\bar{\sigma}\in\textnormal{Hom}(k_{1},\ell)
,
\bar{\tau}\in\textnormal{Hom}(k_{2},\ell)
and
\rho\in\textnormal{Hom}(\ell,m)
such that

\sigma=\rho\circ\bar{\sigma}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau=\rho\circ\bar{\tau}

as well as
\ell=|\textnormal{im}(\bar{\sigma})\cup\textnormal{im}(\bar{\tau})|
.

Proof.

Let
L=\textnormal{im}(\sigma)\cup\textnormal{im}(\tau)
so that
\ell=|L|
. Define a map
\gamma\colon L\to[\ell]
via
i\mapsto|L\cap[i]|
. Clearly
\gamma
is strictly increasing. Since
L
and
[\ell]
are finite sets of the same cardinality, it follows that
\gamma
is a bijection. Now define maps

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}\colon[k_{1}]\to[\ell]\quad\text{by}\quad i\mapsto\gamma(\sigma(i)),

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}\colon[k_{2}]\to[\ell]\quad\text{by}\quad i\mapsto\gamma(\tau(i)),

and

\displaystyle\rho\colon[\ell]\to[m]\quad\text{by}\quad i\mapsto\gamma^{-1}(i).

Since
\gamma
,
\sigma
and
\tau
are strictly increasing, so are
\bar{\sigma}
,
\bar{\tau}
and
\rho
. We have
\sigma=\rho\circ\bar{\sigma}
and
\tau=\rho\circ\bar{\tau}
by construction. The last claim follows from the fact that
\rho
is injective.
∎

The classical Buchberger’s Algorithm relies on a result known as Buchberger’s Criterion. It determines when a set forms a Gröbner basis by analyzing so-called
S-polynomials
. We give the corresponding construction in the OI-setting below.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
ae_{\pi,i}
and
be_{\rho,j}
be monomials in
\mathbf{F}
. Define

\textnormal{lcm}(ae_{\pi,i},be_{\rho,j})=\begin{cases}\textnormal{lcm}(a,b)e_{\pi,i}&\text{if }e_{\pi,i}=e_{\rho,j}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here,
\textnormal{lcm}(a,b)
is the least common multiple of the monomials
a
and
b
in
\mathbf{P}
.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
f,g\in\mathbf{F}_{m}
be nonzero. The
S-polynomial
of
f
and
g
is the combination

S(f,g)=\frac{\textnormal{lcm}(\textnormal{lm}(f),\textnormal{lm}(g))}{\textnormal{lt}(f)}f-\frac{\textnormal{lcm}(\textnormal{lm}(f),\textnormal{lm}(g))}{\textnormal{lt}(g)}g.

Remark 3.13
.

By design, S-polynomials produce cancellation of leading terms. Specifically, one has
\textnormal{lm}(S(f,g))<\textnormal{lcm}(\textnormal{lm}(f),\textnormal{lm}(g))
for any nonzero
f,g\in\mathbf{F}_{m}
.

We need the following observation whose straightforward proof is left to the reader.

Lemma 3.14
.

For any monomials
\mu,\nu\in\mathbf{F}_{m}
, any nonzero
f,g\in\mathbf{F}_{m}
and any
\rho\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
, one has

\mathbf{P}(\rho)\big(\frac{\textnormal{lcm}(\mu,\nu)}{\mu}\big)=\frac{\textnormal{lcm}(\rho_{*}(\mu),\rho_{*}(\nu))}{\rho_{*}(\mu)}.

and

\rho_{*}(S(f,g))=S(\rho_{*}(f),\rho_{*}(g)),

where
\rho_{*}=\mathbf{F}(\rho)
.

Central to our theory is the notion of a
critical pair
, which we define as follows.

Definition 3.15
.

Let
B\subseteq\mathbf{F}
. A tuple

(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))\in\textnormal{Orb}(B,m)\times\textnormal{Orb}(B,m)

is called a
critical pair of
B
if
b_{i},b_{j}\neq 0
and
\textnormal{lm}(b_{i})
and
\textnormal{lm}(b_{j})
involve the same basis element of
\mathbf{F}
and
m=|\textnormal{im}(\sigma)\cup\textnormal{im}(\tau)|
. The set of all critical pairs of
B
is denoted
\mathcal{C}(B)
.

An important property of critical pairs is the fact that if
B
is finite, then
\mathcal{C}(B)
is finite. Indeed, using the above notation, finiteness of
B
implies that there are only finitely many choices for
b_{i},b_{j}\in B
, and for each such pair there are only finitely many possible
m
because
m=|\textnormal{im}(\sigma)\cup\textnormal{im}(\tau)|\leq w(b_{i})+w(b_{j})
. The sets
\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{i}),m)
and
\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{j}),m)
are finite.

We are now ready to state and prove an OI-version of Buchberger’s Criterion.

Theorem 3.16

(OI-Buchberger’s Criterion)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}=\langle B\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
be the
OI
-module generated by a subset
B\subseteq\mathbf{F}
.
Then
B
is a Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}
if and only if
S(f,g)
has a remainder of zero modulo
B
for every critical pair
(f,g)\in\mathcal{C}(B)
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
B
is a Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}
. Assume there is some
(f,g)\in\mathcal{C}(B)
such that
S(f,g)
has a nonzero remainder
r
modulo
B
. Then
S(f,g)=q+r
for some
q\in\mathbf{M}
. Since
f,g\in\mathbf{M}
we have
S(f,g)\in\mathbf{M}
. It follows that
r\in\mathbf{M}
, and so
\textnormal{lm}(r)\in\langle\textnormal{lm}(\mathbf{M})\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}=\langle\textnormal{lm}(B)\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
. Thus,
\textnormal{lm}(r)
is OI-divisible by an element of
\textnormal{lm}(B)
, a contradiction to the fact that
r
is a remainder modulo
B
.

Conversely, suppose each
S(f,g)
with
(f,g)\in\mathcal{C}(B)
has a remainder of zero modulo
B
. By Lemma
3.7
it suffices to show for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
that
B_{n}=\textnormal{Orb}(B,n)
is a Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}_{n}
with respect to
<_{n}
.
Pick
f,g\in B_{n}
and write
f=\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{1})
and
g=\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{2})
for elements
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
and maps
\sigma\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{1}),n)
and
\tau\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{2}),n)
. Using the classical Buchberger’s Criterion (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Theorem 15.8]
), we need to show that
S(f,g)
has a remainder of zero modulo
B_{n}
.

Indeed, if
\textnormal{lm}(b_{1})
and
\textnormal{lm}(b_{2})
involve different basis elements of
\mathbf{F}
then
S(f,g)
is zero, and we are done. Assume
\textnormal{lm}(b_{1})
and
\textnormal{lm}(b_{2})
involve the same basis element of
\mathbf{F}
.
By Lemma
3.10
, there are maps
\bar{\sigma}\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{1}),\ell)
,
\bar{\tau}\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{2}),\ell)
and
\rho\in\textnormal{Hom}(\ell,n)
with
\ell=|\textnormal{im}(\sigma)\cup\textnormal{im}(\tau)|=|\textnormal{im}(\bar{\sigma})\cup\textnormal{im}(\bar{\tau})|
such that
\sigma=\rho\circ\bar{\sigma}
and
\tau=\rho\circ\bar{\tau}
.
Put

\bar{f}=\mathbf{F}(\bar{\sigma})(b_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{g}=\mathbf{F}(\bar{\tau})(b_{2})

and note that
(\bar{f},\bar{g})\in\mathcal{C}(B)
. Functoriality of
\mathbf{F}
gives
\mathbf{F}(\rho)(\bar{f})=f
and
\mathbf{F}(\rho)(\bar{g})=g
.
By
3.14
, we get

\mathbf{F}(\rho)(S(\bar{f},\bar{g}))=S(\mathbf{F}(\rho)(\bar{f}),\mathbf{F}(\rho)(\bar{g}))=S(f,g).

Since
S(\bar{f},\bar{g})
has a remainder of zero modulo
B
by assumption, it follows that
S(f,g)
also has a remainder of zero modulo
B
. So we can write
S(f,g)=\sum a_{i}q_{i}
for some
a_{i}\in\mathbf{P}_{n}
and some
q_{i}\in\textnormal{Orb}(B,n)
such that
\textnormal{lm}(a_{i}q_{i})\leq\textnormal{lm}(S(f,g))
whenever
a_{i}q_{i}\not=0
. As
<
and
<_{n}
agree on
\mathbf{F}_{n}
, this says that
S(f,g)\in\mathbf{M}_{n}
has a remainder of zero modulo
B_{n}
with respect to the order
<_{n}
, completing the argument.
∎

The OI-Buchberger’s Criterion leads to an algorithm for computing Gröbner bases in finite time. The idea is analogous to the classical case: check if any S-polynomial has nonzero remainder, and if so, append the remainder and repeat

Algorithm 3.17

(OI-Buchberger’s Algorithm)
.

1:

A finite subset
B\subset\mathbf{F}

2:

A finite Gröbner basis
G
of
\langle B\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}

3:

set
G=B

4:

set
P=\mathcal{C}(G)

5:

while

P\not=\emptyset

do

6:

choose
(f,g)\in P

7:

set
P=P\;\backslash\;\{(f,g)\}

8:

let
r
be a remainder of
S(f,g)
modulo
G

9:

if

r\not=0

then

10:

set
G=G\cup\{r\}

11:

set
P=\mathcal{C}(G)

12:

end

if

13:

end

while

Correctness and Termination of
3.17
.

Let
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots
denote the values of
G
each time it gets updated (so in particular,
G_{1}=B
). Suppose the algorithm terminates and returns
G_{t}
. Then
S(f,g)
has a remainder of zero modulo
G_{t}
for every
(f,g)\in\mathcal{C}(G_{t})
, so
G_{t}
forms a finite Gröbner basis for
\langle G_{t}\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
by the OI-Buchberger’s Criterion. For any
t
, if
(f,g)\in\mathcal{C}(G_{t})
and
S(f,g)
has a remainder of
r\not=0
modulo
G_{t}
, then
r\in\langle G_{t}\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
, and so
\langle G_{t}\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}=\langle G_{t+1}\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
. It follows that
\langle G_{t}\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}=\langle B\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
and the algorithm is correct.

Suppose now that the algorithm does not terminate. Since each
\mathcal{C}(G_{n})
is finite we obtain an infinite sequence
G_{1}\subseteq G_{2}\subseteq\cdots
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
r\not=0
be the remainder modulo
G_{n}
used to form
G_{n+1}
. By the definition of remainder, we have
\textnormal{lm}(r)\not\in\langle\textnormal{lm}(G_{n})\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
, and hence
\langle\textnormal{lm}(G_{n})\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}\subsetneq\langle\textnormal{lm}(G_{n+1})\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
. This yields a strictly increasing chain of submodules of
\mathbf{F}
, contradicting the fact that
\mathbf{F}
is Noetherian (see
[
10
, Proposition 4.2 and Theorem 6.15]
). Hence the algorithm terminates in finite time.
∎

The main result of this section follows easily now.

Theorem 3.19
.

Every submodule of a finitely generated free
OI
-module
\mathbf{F}
over

\mathbf{P}\cong(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1})^{\otimes c}
has a finite Gröbner basis with respect to any monomial order on
\mathbf{F}
that can be computed in finitely many steps.

Proof.

By
[
10
, Theorem 6.15]
, any submodule of
\mathbf{F}
is finitely generated. Thus, we conclude by applying
3.17
.
∎

]
).

of an OI-module

is said to be

is no longer a Gröbner basis

.

Example 3.20
.

Let
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},1}\oplus\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},1}\oplus\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},2}

\{e_{\textnormal{id}_{[1]},1},e_{\textnormal{id}_{[1]},2},e_{\textnormal{id}_{[2]},3}\}
. Suppose

so
\mathbf{P}
has two rows of variables, and let

B=\{x_{1,1}e_{\textnormal{id}_{[1]},1}+x_{2,1}e_{\textnormal{id}_{[1]},2},\;x_{1,2}x_{1,1}e_{\pi,2}+x_{2,2}x_{2,1}e_{\textnormal{id}_{[2]},3}\}

where
\pi:[1]\to[2]
is given by

. So, the elements of
B
have width one and two, respectively.
Using the Macaulay2 code of
[
9
]
, one obtains a minimal Gröbner basis for
\langle B\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
with respect to the lex order from Example
3.2
:

\displaystyle x_{1,1}e_{\textnormal{id}_{[1]},1}+x_{2,1}e_{\textnormal{id}_{[1]},2}

\displaystyle\in\mathbf{F}_{1}

\displaystyle x_{1,2}x_{1,1}e_{\pi,2}+x_{2,2}x_{2,1}e_{\textnormal{id}_{[2]},3}

\displaystyle\in\mathbf{F}_{2}

\displaystyle-x_{2,3}x_{2,2}x_{1,1}e_{\sigma_{1},3}+x_{2,3}x_{2,1}x_{1,2}e_{\sigma_{2},3}

\displaystyle\in\mathbf{F}_{3}

where
\sigma_{1}:[2]\to[3]
is given by

and

and
\sigma_{2}:[2]\to[3]
is given by

and

. In particular, the OI-Buchberger’s Algorithm needed to append exactly one more element of width

before terminating.

Given a submodule
\mathbf{M}
of
\mathbf{F}
, the fact that
\mathbf{M}
has a finite Gröbner basis
G
combined with
3.7
shows in particular that a Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}
can be obtained as the union of Gröbner bases of
\mathbf{M}_{n}
with respect to the order
<_{n}
with
n\leq w
, where
w
is the maximum width of an element in
G
. This suggests an alternative way for computing a Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}
by determining and comparing Gröbner bases of
\mathbf{M}_{n}
with respect to the order
<_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
. The problem is that a priori one does not know when to stop, i.e., what is large enough. The following result gives a sufficient condition for the desired stabilization.

Proposition 3.21
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a submodule of a free
\mathbf{P}
-module
\mathbf{F}
generated by a finite set
B\subset\mathbf{F}
. Let
<
be any monomial order on
\mathbf{F}
. For any integer
m\geq 0
, denote by
<_{m}
the restriction of
<
to
\mathbf{F}_{m}
.
Let
W\geq 1
denote the maximum width of an element in
B
. Then
B
is a Gröbner basis of
\mathbf{M}
with respect to
<
if and only if
\textnormal{Orb}(B,m)
is a Gröbner basis for
\mathbf{M}_{m}
with respect to
<_{m}
for each integer
m\leq 2W
.

Proof.

The forward direction is immediate from Lemma 3.6. For the reverse, by the OI-Buchberger’s Criterion, it suffices to show for any critical pair

(f,g)\in\mathcal{C}(B)
that
S(f,g)
has a remainder of zero modulo
B
. Considering such a critical pair, we can write
(f,g)=(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{1}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{2}))
for maps
\sigma
and
\tau
and elements
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
such that
\sigma,\tau
land in width
m=|\textnormal{im}(\sigma)\cup\textnormal{im}(\tau)|\leq w(b_{1})+w(b_{2})\leq 2W
.
Hence our hypothesis gives that
\textnormal{Orb}(B,m)
is a Gröbner basis for
\mathbf{M}_{m}
, and so
S(f,g)
has a remainder of zero modulo
\textnormal{Orb}(B,m)
with respect to
<_{m}
. But since
<
and
<_{m}
agree on
\mathbf{M}_{m}
, this says that
S(f,g)
has a remainder of zero modulo
B
with respect to
<
.
∎

4.
Syzygies and the OI-Schreyer’s Theorem

Recall that in the classical setting, if
M
is a finitely generated submodule of a free module of finite rank over a Noetherian polynomial ring, Schreyer’s Theorem
[
4
, Theorem 15.10]
computes a finite Gröbner basis for the module of syzygies of
M
. Using the theory developed in the previous section, we extend Schreyer’s result to the setting of OI-modules.

We continue to use the above notation. In particular,
\mathbf{F}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},d_{i}}_{\mathbf{P}}
always denotes a finitely generated, free OI-module over
\mathbf{P}=(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{OI},1})^{\otimes c}
with some
c\geq 1
. We fix a monomial order
<
on
\mathbf{F}
.

Our computation of syzygies of a submodule
\mathbf{M}
of
\mathbf{F}
depends on a choice of a generating set of
\mathbf{M}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\}\subset\mathbf{F}
and let
\mathbf{G}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},w(b_{i})}_{\mathbf{P}}
be a free OI-module with basis
\{\epsilon_{\textnormal{id}_{[w(b_{i})]},i}\ :\ i\in[t]\}
. We define the
module of syzygies of
B
as
\textnormal{Syz}(B)=\textnormal{ker}(\varphi)
, where
\varphi
denotes the surjective morphism

\varphi\colon\mathbf{G}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\langle\epsilon_{\textnormal{id}_{[w(b_{i})]},i}\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}\to\langle B\rangle_{\mathbf{F}},\text{ defined by $\epsilon_{\textnormal{id}_{[w(b_{i})]},i}\mapsto b_{i}$}.

Our goal in this section is to compute a finite Gröbner basis for
\textnormal{Syz}(B)
. To begin, we will need a suitable monomial order on
\mathbf{G}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\}\subset\mathbf{F}
.

(i)

For any
i\in[t]
, order the set
\{\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{i}),n)\,:\,n\geq w(b_{i})\}
: Given
\pi\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{i}),m)
and
\rho\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{i}),n)
, define
\pi<\rho
if

(m,\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(w(b_{i})))<(n,\rho(1),\ldots,\rho(w(b_{i})))
in the lexicographic order on
\mathbb{N}^{w(b_{i})+1}
, where
\mathbb{N}
is ordered by
1<2<\cdots
.

(ii)

Let
\prec_{B}
be the total order on the union of the above sets

\{(\pi,i)\;:\;i\in[t],\;\pi\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{i}),m),\;m\geq w(b_{i})\}

defined by
(\pi,i)\prec_{B}(\rho,j)
if either
i<j
or
i=j
and
\pi<\rho
.

(iii)

Define a total order
<_{B}
on
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{G})
by setting
a\epsilon_{\pi,i}<_{B}b\epsilon_{\rho,j}
if either
\textnormal{lm}(\varphi(a\epsilon_{\pi,i}))<\textnormal{lm}(\varphi(b\epsilon_{\rho,j}))
or
\textnormal{lm}(\varphi(a\epsilon_{\pi,i}))=\textnormal{lm}(\varphi(b\epsilon_{\rho,j}))
and
(\rho,j)\prec_{B}(\pi,i)
.

Remark 4.3
.

Note that for any monomial
a\epsilon_{\pi,i}\in\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{G})
, one has

\textnormal{lm}(\varphi(a\epsilon_{\pi,i}))=\textnormal{lm}(a\mathbf{F}(\pi)(b_{i}))=a\mathbf{F}(\pi)(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i}))=a\pi_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i})).

Thus, Part (iii) can be more explicitly re-stated as
a\epsilon_{\pi,i}<_{B}b\epsilon_{\rho,j}
if either
a\pi_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i}))<b\rho_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{j}))
or
a\pi_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i}))=b\rho_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{j}))
and
(\rho,j)\prec_{B}(\pi,i)
.

Together with the fact that
<
is a monomial order on
\mathbf{F}
, the following observation implies that
<_{B}
is a monomial order on
\mathbf{G}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider any maps
\pi\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{i}),m)
and
\rho\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{j}),m)
with
i,j\in[t]
and

\sigma\in\textnormal{Hom}(m,n)
. Then
(\sigma\circ\pi,i)\prec_{B}(\sigma\circ\rho,j)
if and only if
(\pi,i)\prec_{B}(\rho,j)
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\sigma
is a strictly increasing map.
∎

For the remainder of this section we assume that
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\}
is a Gröbner basis of
\langle B\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
and that each
b_{i}
is monic, possibly after multiplying by a suitable element of
K
. We need some further notation.

For any
i,j\in[t]
,
\sigma\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{i}),m)
and
\tau\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{j}),m)
with
m\geq\max(w(b_{i}),w(b_{j}))
, the remainder of
S(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))
modulo
B
is zero since
B
is a Gröbner basis. Hence the division algorithm gives an expression

S(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))=\sum_{\ell}a_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau})(b_{k_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}})

with
a_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}\in\mathbf{P}_{m}
and
\mathbf{F}(\pi_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau})(b_{k_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}})\in\textnormal{Orb}(B,m)
, where

(4.1)

\textnormal{lm}(u\mathbf{F}(\pi_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau})(b_{k_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}}))\leq\textnormal{lm}(S(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j})))

for any monomial
u
in
a_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}
(see Remark
3.9
). Define

(4.2)

s_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}=m_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}\epsilon_{\sigma,i}-m_{j,i}^{\tau,\sigma}\epsilon_{\tau,j}-\sum_{\ell}a_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}\epsilon_{\pi_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau},k_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}}\in\mathbf{G}_{m}

where

m_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}=\frac{\textnormal{lcm}(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i})),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(\textnormal{lm}(b_{j})))}{\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i}))}\in\mathbf{P}_{m}.

We need one more preparatory observation.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
(\sigma,i)\prec_{B}(\tau,j)
then
\textnormal{lm}_{<_{B}}(s_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau})=m_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}\epsilon_{\sigma,i}
.

Proof.

Note that
\varphi(m_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}\epsilon_{\sigma,i})=\frac{\textnormal{lcm}(\sigma_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i})),\tau_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{j})))}{\sigma_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i}))}\sigma_{*}(b_{i})
, and so its leading
monomial is
\textnormal{lcm}(\sigma_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i})),\tau_{*}(\textnormal{lm}(b_{j})))
. Analogously, we see that
\varphi(m_{j,i}^{\tau,\sigma}\epsilon_{\tau,j})
has the same leading monomial. Hence
(\sigma,i)\prec_{B}(\tau,j)
implies
m_{j,i}^{\tau,\sigma}\epsilon_{\tau,j}<_{B}m_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}\epsilon_{\sigma,i}
by the definition of
<_{B}
. Now fix some
\ell
and let
u
be the monomial in
a_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}
such that

\textnormal{lm}_{<_{B}}(a_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}\epsilon_{\pi_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau},k_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}})=u\epsilon_{\pi_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau},k_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}}.

Then we obtain

(by (
4.1
))

\displaystyle\textnormal{lm}(u\mathbf{F}(\pi_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau})(b_{k_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}}))

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{lm}(S(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j})))

(by Remark
3.13
)

\displaystyle<\textnormal{lcm}(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i})),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(\textnormal{lm}(b_{j})))

\displaystyle=m_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i})).

By definition of
<_{B}
, this gives
u\epsilon_{\pi_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau},k_{i,j,\ell}^{\sigma,\tau}}<_{B}m_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}\epsilon_{\sigma,i}
, and the claim follows.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.6

(OI-Schreyer’s Theorem)
.

The
s_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}
with
(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))\in\mathcal{C}(B)
and
(\sigma,i)\prec_{B}(\tau,j)
form a finite Gröbner basis for
\textnormal{Syz}(B)
with respect to
<_{B}
.

Proof.

First note that each
s_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}
is indeed a syzygy, for

\varphi(s_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau})=S(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))-S(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))=0.

Now let
h\in\textnormal{Syz}(B)
be any nonzero syzygy. We need to show that
\textnormal{lm}(h)
is divisible by the leading monomial of one of the
s_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}
in the claimed Gröbner basis of
\textnormal{Syz}(B)
.

To see this write
h=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell}x^{p_{\ell}}\epsilon_{\pi_{\ell},i_{\ell}}
for some nonzero
c_{i}\in K
and distinct
x^{p_{\ell}}\epsilon_{\pi_{\ell},i_{\ell}}\in\textnormal{Mon}(\mathbf{G})
. Note that
n\geq 2
since
h
is a nonzero syzygy. Assume the monomials of
h
are ordered so that
x^{p_{\ell}}\epsilon_{\pi_{\ell},i_{\ell}}>_{B}x^{p_{\ell^{\prime}}}\epsilon_{\pi_{\ell^{\prime}},i_{\ell^{\prime}}}
if
\ell<\ell^{\prime}
. Then
\textnormal{lt}_{<_{B}}(h)=c_{1}x^{p_{1}}\epsilon_{\pi_{1},i_{1}}
and, by definition of
<_{B}
, we get

(4.3)

x^{p_{1}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}}))\geq\cdots\geq x^{p_{n}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{n})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{n}})).

We claim that

(4.4)

x^{p_{1}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}}))=x^{p_{2}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{2})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{2}})).

Indeed, otherwise (
4.3
) becomes

(4.5)

x^{p_{1}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}}))>x^{p_{2}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{2})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{2}}))\geq\cdots\geq x^{p_{n}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{n})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{n}})).

Since
h
is a syzygy we have
\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell}x^{p_{\ell}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{\ell})(b_{i_{\ell}})=0
which, using (
4.5
), implies that
x^{p_{1}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}}))
can be written as a
K
-linear combination of strictly smaller monomials. This is impossible, and hence we have established (
4.4
).

Since
x^{p_{1}}\epsilon_{\pi_{1},i_{1}}>_{B}x^{p_{2}}\epsilon_{\pi_{2},i_{2}}

and these two elements are mapped by
\varphi
onto the same element of
\mathbf{F}
by (
4.4
), the definition of
<_{B}
implies

(4.6)

(\pi_{1},i_{1})\prec_{B}(\pi_{2},i_{2}).

Now set
\alpha=x^{p_{1}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}}))
.
By Equation (
4.4
),
\alpha
is divisible by
\mathbf{F}(\pi_{2})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{2}}))
, and so
\alpha
is divisible by

\beta=\textnormal{lcm}(\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}})),\mathbf{F}(\pi_{2})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{2}})))
. By the OI-Factorization Lemma, there are maps
\overline{\pi_{1}}\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{i_{1}}),\ell)
,
\overline{\pi_{2}}\in\textnormal{Hom}(w(b_{i_{2}}),\ell)
and
\rho\in\textnormal{Hom}(\ell,m)
with
\ell=|\textnormal{im}(\overline{\pi_{1}})\cup\textnormal{im}(\overline{\pi_{2}})|
such that

\rho\circ\overline{\pi_{1}}=\pi_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho\circ\overline{\pi_{2}}=\pi_{2}.

By construction,
(\mathbf{F}(\overline{\pi_{1}})(b_{i_{1}}),\mathbf{F}(\overline{\pi_{2}})(b_{i_{2}}))
is in
\mathcal{C}(B)
. Moreover, Relation (
4.6
) and Lemma
4.4
imply

(\overline{\pi_{1}},i_{1})\prec_{B}(\overline{\pi_{2}},i_{2})
. Hence, we get

(by Lemma
4.5
)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\mathbf{G}(\rho)(\textnormal{lm}_{<_{B}}(s_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{\overline{\pi_{1}},\overline{\pi_{2}}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\mathbf{G}(\rho)(m_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{\overline{\pi_{1}},\overline{\pi_{2}}}\epsilon_{\overline{\pi_{1}},i_{1}})

(by
3.14
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\frac{\textnormal{lcm}(\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}})),\mathbf{F}(\pi_{2})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{2}})))}{\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}}))}\epsilon_{\pi_{1},i_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{p_{1}}\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}}))}{\mathbf{F}(\pi_{1})(\textnormal{lm}(b_{i_{1}}))}\epsilon_{\pi_{1},i_{1}}

\displaystyle=x^{p_{1}}\epsilon_{\pi_{1},i_{1}}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{lm}_{<_{B}}(h).

This shows that
\textnormal{lm}_{<_{B}}(h)
is OI-divisible by
\textnormal{lm}_{<_{B}}(s_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{\overline{\pi_{1}},\overline{\pi_{2}}})
, which completes the proof.
∎

In the graded case, the method can be modified to give homogenous syzygies.

Remark 4.7
.

If
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{F}
are graded and the elements of
B
are homogenous, we use the morphism

\varphi\colon\mathbf{G}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\mathbf{F}^{\textnormal{OI},w(b_{i})}(-\deg(b_{i}))\to\langle B\rangle_{\mathbf{F}},\text{ defined by $\epsilon_{\textnormal{id}_{[w(b_{i})]},i}\mapsto b_{i}$}.

It is graded (of degree zero). One checks that as a consequence the syzygies defined in (
4.2
) are homogeneous.

If the generating set
B
consists of monomials, then one gets a more explicit Gröbner basis of
\textnormal{Syz}(B)
. The result is an OI-version of the description of the syzygy module of a monomial module in the classical situation (see, e.g,
[
4
, Lemma 15.1]
).

Corollary 4.8
.

For any finite set of monomials
B\subset\mathbf{F}_{n}
, the elements
s_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}\in\mathbf{G}
with
(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))\in\mathcal{C}(B)
and
(\sigma,i)\prec_{B}(\tau,j)
form a finite Gröbner basis for
\textnormal{Syz}(B)
with respect to
<_{B}
, where

s_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}=\frac{\textnormal{lcm}(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))}{\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i})}\epsilon_{\sigma,i}-\frac{\textnormal{lcm}(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))}{\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j})}\epsilon_{\tau,j}.

Proof.

Since
B
consists of monomials, it is a Gröbner basis of
\langle B\rangle_{\mathbf{F}}
with respect to any monomial order on
\mathbf{F}
. Thus,
4.6
is applicable. Moreover, each S-polynomial
S(\mathbf{F}(\sigma)(b_{i}),\mathbf{F}(\tau)(b_{j}))
is zero, which implies the stated form of each
s_{i,j}^{\sigma,\tau}
(see Equation (
4.2
)). We conclude by
4.6
.
∎

5.
Computing Free Resolutions

We now apply our results to the problem of computing free resolutions. We continue to use the above notation.

Recall that if
\mathbf{M}
is an
\mathbf{A}
-module, then a
free resolution
of
\mathbf{M}
is an exact sequence

\mathbf{F}^{\bullet}:\quad\cdots\to\mathbf{F}^{2}\to\mathbf{F}^{1}\to\mathbf{F}^{0}\to\mathbf{M}\to 0,

where each
\mathbf{F}^{i}
is a free
\mathbf{A}
-module. If
\mathbf{M}
is a finitely generated
\mathbf{P}
-module then
\mathbf{M}
admits a free resolution in which every free module
\mathbf{F}^{i}
is finitely generated (see
[
10
, Theorem 7.1]
). The argument in
[
10
]
is based on the finiteness results established in that paper and not constructive. Using the results of the previous section we obtain the first algorithm to compute, for
any integer
p\geq 0
, the first
p
steps of a free resolution of a submodule
\mathbf{M}
of
\mathbf{M}
. Indeed, given a finite Gröbner basis
B_{0}
of
\mathbf{M}
, define
\varphi_{0}\colon\mathbf{F}^{0}\to\mathbf{M}
by mapping the basis elements of
\mathbf{F}^{0}
onto the corresponding generators in
B_{0}
. Now let
B_{1}
be a generating set of
\textnormal{ker}(\varphi_{0})
determined by
4.6
and repeat this process.

Procedure 5.1

(Method for Computing Free Resolutions)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a submodule of a free
\mathbf{P}
-module. Suppose
\mathbf{M}
is finitely generated by a subset
B
. Compute a free resolution for
\mathbf{M}
as follows:

(i)

Determine a finite Gröbner basis
B_{0}
for
\mathbf{M}
using the OI-Buchberger’s Algorithm applied to the set
B
.

(ii)

Apply the OI-Schreyer’s Theorem to
B_{0}
to obtain a finite Gröbner basis
B_{1}
of
\textnormal{Syz}(B_{0})
.

(iii)

Repeat (ii) as many times as desired to compute a finite Gröbner basis
B_{n}
of
\textnormal{Syz}(B_{n-1})
.

(iv)

Form (the beginning of) the free resolution
\mathbf{F}^{\bullet}
by defining
\mathbf{F}^{i}
such that
\varphi_{i}
maps the basis elements of
\mathbf{F}^{i}
onto the corresponding generators in
B_{i}
as in
4.1
.

Using
4.7
, the above procedure can be modified in the case that
\mathbf{M}
is a graded OI-module to produce a graded free resolution of
\mathbf{M}
.

If
\mathbf{M}
is a graded submodule of
\mathbf{F}
, then it is shown in
[
5
, Theorem 3.10]
that
\mathbf{M}
has a graded free resolution
\mathbf{F}^{\bullet}
such that for any other graded free resolution
\mathbf{G}^{\bullet}
and for any homological degree
d
, the rank of
\mathbf{F}^{d}
is at most the rank of
\mathbf{G}^{d}
. By
[
5
, Theorem 3.10]
, the resolution

\mathbf{F}^{\bullet}
is unique up to isomorphisms of graded free resolutions and called a

minimal
graded free resolution of
\mathbf{M}
in
[
5
]
. Our methods allow us to show that there is an algorithm that produces such a minimal free resolution.

As in the classical situation, a key is to characterize the maps occurring in a graded minimal free resolution.

Definition 5.2

(
[
5
, Definition 3.1]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{G}
be free
\mathbf{P}
-modules with bases
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\}
and
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{t}\}
where each
f_{i}
lives in width
u_{i}
and each
g_{j}
lives in width
v_{j}
. A morphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{F}\to\mathbf{G}
is determined by the images of the
f_{i}
, i.e. by
s
expressions of the form

\varphi(f_{i})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq t\\
\varepsilon_{i,j}\in\textnormal{Hom}(v_{j},u_{i})\end{subarray}}a_{\varepsilon_{i,j}}\mathbf{G}(\varepsilon_{i,j})(g_{j})

where each
a_{\varepsilon_{i,j}}\in\mathbf{P}_{u_{i}}
. We say
\varphi
is
minimal
if whenever any
\varepsilon_{i,j}
is an identity map, the coefficient
a_{\varepsilon_{i,j}}
is not a unit.

This concept allows one to check whether a graded free resolution is minimal. Indeed,
[
5
, Theorem 3.10]
gives the following characterization:

Theorem 5.3
.

A graded free resolution
\mathbf{F}^{\bullet}
of
\mathbf{M}
is minimal if and only if each map between free modules in
\mathbf{F}^{\bullet}
is minimal.

We are ready to establish that
the first steps of a graded minimal free resolution can be computed algorithmically.

Theorem 5.4
.

If
\mathbf{M}
is a graded submodule of
\mathbf{F}
with a finite generating set
B
consisting of homogenous elements, then, for any integer
p\geq 0
, there is a finite algorithm that determines the first
p
steps

\mathbf{F}^{p}\to\cdots\to\mathbf{F}^{2}\to\mathbf{F}^{1}\to\mathbf{F}^{0}\to\mathbf{M}\to 0

in a graded minimal free resolution of
\mathbf{M}
.

Proof.

Using the graded version of Procedure
5.1
, we compute a graded exact sequence

\mathbf{F}^{p}\to\cdots\to\mathbf{F}^{2}\to\mathbf{F}^{1}\to\mathbf{F}^{0}\to\mathbf{M}\to 0,

where each
\mathbf{F}^{i}
is a finitely generated graded free
\mathbf{P}
-module. If one of the maps in this sequence is not minimal we prune the sequence to obtain an exact sequence such that two consecutive free modules are replaced by free modules of strictly smaller rank. Repeating this process as often as needed beginning with the right-most map one eventually obtains (the beginning) of a graded minimal free resolution of
\mathbf{M}
.

The pruning is described in detail in the proof of
[
5
, Lemma 3.5]
.
∎

and the algorithm in
5.4
have been implemented in

]
