Landau’s converse to Hölder’s inequality
Melvyn B. Nathanson

Abstract
. By Hölder’s inequality, if 𝐱 ∈ ℓ p \mathbf{x}\in\ell^{p} , then 𝐱𝐲 ∈ ℓ 1 \mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{1} for all 𝐲 ∈ ℓ q \mathbf{y}\in\ell^{q} . Landau proved the converse result: If 𝐱𝐲 ∈ ℓ 1 \mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{1} for all 𝐲 ∈ ℓ q \mathbf{y}\in\ell^{q} , then 𝐱 ∈ ℓ p \mathbf{x}\in\ell^{p} . This paper proves Landau’s theorem and considers a related problem for Hilbert’s inequality.

There are many proofs of Hölder’s inequality.
Here is a simple proof of the finite form of the inequality.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p>1
and
q>1
be real numbers such that

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

For all positive real numbers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{q}\right)^{1/q}

Proof.

The function
f(x)
is
convex
on the interval
(u,v)
if

f(ta+(1-t)b)\leq tf(a)+(1-t)f(b)

for all
a,b
such that
u<a<b<v
.
The function
f(x)
is
concave
on the interval
(u,v)

if
-f(x)
is convex on
(u,v)
or, equivalently, if

f(ta+(1-t)b)\geq tf(a)+(1-t)f(b)

for all
a,b
such that
u<a<b<v
.
The function
f(x)=\log x
is concave on
(0,\infty)
.
For all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, choosing
a=x_{i}^{p}
,
b=y_{i}^{q}
,

t=1/p
, and
1-t=1/q
,
we obtain

\log\left(\frac{1}{p}x_{i}^{p}+\frac{1}{q}y_{i}^{q}\right)\geq\frac{1}{p}\log x_{i}^{p}+\frac{1}{q}\log y_{i}^{q}=\log x_{i}y_{i}.

Exponentiating this inequality gives a simple form of
Young’s inequality
:

x_{i}y_{i}\leq\frac{1}{p}x_{i}^{p}+\frac{1}{q}y_{i}^{q}.

Summing over
i
we obtain

(1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}\leq\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{q}y_{i}^{q}.

Let

X=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\qqand Y=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

Replacing
x_{i}
with
x_{i}/X
and
y_{i}
with
y_{i}/Y
in (
1
), we obtain

\frac{1}{XY}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}\leq\frac{1}{p}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}}{X^{p}}+\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{q}}{Y^{q}}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

This completes the proof.
∎

For sequences
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}

of real or complex numbers,
we define the product sequence
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}=(x_{i}y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
.
For all
p\geq 1
, the
p
-norm of the sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

The Lebesgue space
\ell^{p}
is the set of all sequences
\mathbf{x}
such that
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}<\infty
.

Theorem
1
immediately implies the infinite form of Hölder’s inequality.

Let
\mathbf{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
be the set of positive integers.

Theorem 2
.

Let
p>1
and
q>1
be real numbers such that

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
be sequences
of real or complex numbers.
If
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
,
then

\|\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{q}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
x_{i}\neq 0
and
y_{i}\neq 0
for all
i\in\mathbf{N}
.
By Theorem
1
, for all
n\in\mathbf{N}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}y_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_{i}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|y_{i}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{q}

and so

\|\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}y_{i}|\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{q}.

This completes the proof.
∎

For all
p\geq 1
, the Lebesgue space
\ell^{p}
contains every finite sequence and so Theorem
2
, applied to the sequences

\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,
gives Theorem
1
.
Thus, Theorems
1
and
2
are equivalent.

2.
Landau’s theorem

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality states that if
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{2}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{2}
, then

\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{1}
and
\|\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
.
This is the case
p=q=2
of Hölder’s inequality.
In 1906, motivated by Hilbert’s papers on integral equations,
Hellinger and Töplitz
[
5
]
stated (but did not prove)
the following converse of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Theorem 3

(Hellinger-Töplitz)
.

If
\mathbf{x}
is a sequence such that

\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{1}
for all sequences
\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{2}
,
then
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{2}
.

In 1907, Landau
[
7
]
generalized this result
and proved a converse to the infinite Hölder’s inequality.
He wrote:

Indem ich mir einen Beweis für diesen Satz zurechtlegte, fand ich folgende Verallgemeinerung, welche vielleicht für die Zwecke der Herren Hellinger und Töplitz nützlich sein kann, und in welcher der oblige Hilfssatz als Specialfall
p=2
enthalten ist.

Riesz included Landau’s theorem in his book
[
10
, pp.46–48]

on infinite systems of linear equations in infinitely many variables.
For a history of Hölder’s inequality, see Maligranda
[
8
]

and for recent related results, see Albuquerque, et al.
[
1
]
.

be the
n

of the series. The series

(2)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{s_{n}^{\alpha}}

diverges if
\alpha\leq 1
and converges if
\alpha>1
.

Theorem 5

(Landau)
.

Let
p>1
and
q>1
be real numbers such that

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

If
\mathbf{x}
is a sequence such that

\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{1}
for all sequences
\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{q}
,
then
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{p}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that

\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}

is a sequence of positive real numbers.
We shall prove that if
\mathbf{x}\notin\ell^{p}
, then there exists
\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{q}
such that

\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\notin\ell^{1}
.

If
\mathbf{x}\notin\ell^{p}
, then the infinite series

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}^{p}

diverges.
For all
n\in\mathbf{N}
, the
n
th partial sum of the series is

s_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}.

Let
s_{0}=0
.
Then

x_{i}^{p}=s_{i}-s_{i-1}

for all
i\in\mathbf{N}
and

0=s_{0}<s_{1}<\cdots<s_{n}<s_{n+1}<\cdots.

The divergence of the series
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}^{p}
implies

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}s_{n}=\infty.

By the Abel-Dini theorem, the series

(3)

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}^{p}}{s_{i}}

diverges and the series

(4)

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}^{p}}{s_{i}^{q}}

converges. Define
\lambda>0
by

\frac{1}{\lambda^{q}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}^{p}}{s_{i}^{q}}.

For all
i\in\mathbf{N}
, let
y_{i}
be the positive real number

y_{i}=\lambda\frac{\sqrt[q]{x_{i}^{p}}}{s_{i}}.

and let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
.
We have

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i}^{q}=\lambda^{q}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}^{p}}{s_{i}^{q}}=1

and so
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
.
Because

1+\frac{p}{q}=p

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}y_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}\frac{\sqrt[q]{x_{i}^{p}}}{s_{i}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}^{(1+p/q)}}{s_{i}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}^{p}}{s_{i}}

This series diverges by Abel-Dini (
3
).
Thus, if
\mathbf{x}\notin\ell^{p}
, then there exists
\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{q}

such that
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\notin\ell^{1}
.
Equivalently, if
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{1}
for all
\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{q}
, then
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{p}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Converse to the finite Hölder inequality

Here is a converse to Theorem
1
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
p>1
and
q>1
be real numbers such that

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

If
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},
and
C
are positive real numbers
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}\leq C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{q}\right)^{1/q}

for all positive real numbers
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
, then

C\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

Proof.

For all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, let
y_{i}
be the positive real number
such that
x_{i}y_{i}=y_{i}^{q}
or, equivalently,

y_{i}=x_{i}^{1/(q-1)}.

Because

p=\frac{q}{q-1}

we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{1/(q-1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{q/(q-1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}

and

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{q}\right)^{1/q}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{q/(q-1)}\right)^{1/q}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}\right)^{1/q}.

Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}\leq C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{q}\right)^{1/q}=C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}\right)^{1/q}

and

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}\right)^{1/q}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}\right)^{1-1/p}\leq C.

This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Dini’s convergence theorem

Landau’s converse to the infinite Hölder inequality uses a convergence-divergence
result called the Abel-Dini theorem.
It was proved by Dini in 1867 and is called the Abel-Dini theorem
because of a related result proved by Abel in 1828.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}
be a divergent series of positive terms
and let
s_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
be the
n
th partial sum
of the series. The series

(5)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{s_{n}^{\alpha}}

diverges if
\alpha\leq 1
and converges if
\alpha>1
.

Proof.

The proof is from Knopp
[
6
]
.
Because the infinite series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}
diverges,
the sequence of partial sums
(s_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is strictly increasing and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}s_{n}=\infty
.
It follows that for every positive integer
k
there exists a positive integer
\ell
such that

s_{k+\ell}>2s_{k}
or, equivalently,

\frac{s_{k}}{s_{k+\ell}}<\frac{1}{2}.

For all
s_{k}\geq 1
and
\alpha\leq 1
we have

\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+\ell}\frac{a_{i}}{s_{i}^{\alpha}}\geq\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+\ell}\frac{a_{i}}{s_{i}}\geq\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+\ell}\frac{a_{i}}{s_{k+\ell}}=\frac{s_{k+\ell}-s_{k}}{s_{k+\ell}}=1-\frac{s_{k}}{s_{k+\ell}}>\frac{1}{2}.

and so the series (
5
) diverges.

Next we consider the case
\delta>0
and
\alpha=1+\delta
.
For all
r\in\mathbf{N}
and
x\in(0,1)
, we have

1-x^{r}=(1-x)\left(1+x+x^{2}+\cdots+x^{r-1}\right)<r(1-x).

Choose
r\in\mathbf{N}
such that
0<1/r<\delta
. For all
n\geq 2
we have

x=\left(\frac{s_{n-1}}{s_{n}}\right)^{1/r}\in(0,1)

and

\frac{a_{n}}{s_{n}}=\frac{s_{n}-s_{n-1}}{s_{n}}=1-\frac{s_{n-1}}{s_{n}}<r\left(1-\left(\frac{s_{n-1}}{s_{n}}\right)^{1/r}\right).

We obtain the telescoping sum

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{s_{n}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{s_{n}s_{n}^{\delta}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{a_{1}^{1/r}}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n-1}^{1/r}}\frac{a_{n}}{s_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a_{1}^{1/r}}+r\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n-1}^{1/r}}\left(1-\left(\frac{s_{n-1}}{s_{n}}\right)^{1/r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a_{1}^{1/r}}+r\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{s_{n-1}^{1/r}}-\frac{1}{s_{n}^{1/r}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{r+1}{a_{1}^{1/r}}

and so the series (
2
) converges.
This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Hilbert’s inequality

Theorem 8

(Hilbert)
.

For all
\mathbf{a}=(a_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{2}
and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{2}
,

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}b_{n}}{m+n}<\pi\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The constant
\pi
is best possible.

In Hilbert’s original proof, the constant on the right side of the inequality was
2\pi
.
Schur obtained the constant
\pi
and proved that this is best possible.

There is a vast literature on Hilbert’s inequality and its generalizations.
The following
pq
result is sometimes called the Hardy-Hilbert inequality.
Here is a short proof that uses only Hölder’s inequality.

Theorem 9
.

Let
p>1
and
q>1
satisfy

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

For all
\mathbf{a}=(a_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}
and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
,

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}b_{n}}{m+n}<\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi/p)}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

The constant
\pi/\sin(\pi/p)
is best possible.

Proof.

We begin with two identities. First,

\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{p}\right)=\sin\pi\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right).

Second, for
0<\lambda<1/p
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+t)t^{p\lambda}}dt=\frac{\pi}{\sin\left(\pi p\lambda\right)}.

Borwein
[
2
]
gives two proofs of this formula.

Maple
and
Mathemtica
also give this result.

For
\lambda>0
, let

x_{m}=\frac{a_{m}}{(m+n)^{1/p}}\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{\lambda}\qqand y_{n}=\frac{b_{n}}{(n+m)^{1/q}}\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{\lambda}.

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}b_{n}}{m+n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}}{(m+n)^{1/p}}\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{\lambda}\frac{b_{n}}{(m+n)^{1/q}}\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{m}y_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{m}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}y_{n}^{q}\right)^{1/q}\hskip 8.5359pt\text{(by H\"{o}lder's inequality)}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}^{p}}{m+n}\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{p\lambda}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{n}^{q}}{n+m}\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{q\lambda}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}^{p}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m^{p\lambda}}{(m+n)n^{p\lambda}}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}^{q}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{q\lambda}}{(n+m)m^{q\lambda}}\right)^{1/q}.

We estimate the inner sums as follows.
For all
m,n\in\mathbf{N}
, the functions

f(x)=\frac{m^{p\lambda}}{(m+x)x^{p\lambda}}\qqand g(x)=\frac{n^{q\lambda}}{(n+x)x^{q\lambda}}.

are positive and decreasing for
x>0
. The integrals

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)dx\qqand\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)dx

converge for
0<\lambda<1/p
and for
0<\lambda<1/q
, respectively.
With the substitution
x=mt
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m^{p\lambda}}{(m+n)n^{p\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{m^{p\lambda}}{(m+x)x^{p\lambda}}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+t)t^{p\lambda}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\sin\left(\pi p\lambda\right)}.

With the substitution
x=nt
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{q\lambda}}{(n+m)m^{q\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}g(m)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{n^{q\lambda}}{(n+x)x^{q\lambda}}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+t)t^{q\lambda}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\sin\left(\pi q\lambda\right)}.

Choosing

\lambda=\frac{1}{pq}

we obtain

\sin\left(\pi q\lambda\right)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{p}\right)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right)=\sin\left(\pi p\lambda\right)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}b_{n}}{m+n}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}^{p}\frac{\pi}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right)}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}^{q}\frac{\pi}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{p}\right)}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{p}\right)}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_{m}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

If
p=q=2
, then

\frac{\pi}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)}=\pi

gives Schur’s best possible upper bound for Hilbert’s inequality.
This completes the proof.
∎

Is there a converse to Hilbert’s inequality that is similar to Landau’s theorem?
For example, let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of real numbers such that,
for all
\mathbf{b}=(b_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
, the infinite series

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}b_{n}}{m+n}

converges. Does this imply
\mathbf{a}\in\ell^{p}
?
