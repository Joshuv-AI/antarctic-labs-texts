Reducing Causality to Functions with Structural Models
Tianyi Miao

Abstract
The precise definition of causality is currently an open problem in philosophy and statistics. We believe causality should be defined as functions (in mathematics) that map causes to effects. We propose a reductive definition of causality based on Structural Functional Model (SFM). Using delta compression and contrastive forward inference, SFM can produce causal utterances like “X causes Y” and “X is the cause of Y” that match our intuitions. We compile a dataset of causal scenarios and use SFM in all of them. SFM is compatible with but not reducible to probability theory. We also compare SFM with other theories of causation and apply SFM to downstream problems like free will, causal explanation, and mental causation. Keywords : Causal Modeling, Causation, Actual Causality



1
Introduction

What is causation? What does it mean to say one thing causes another? Is it possible to define causation in non-causal terms?

We can easily find examples where “correlation doesn’t imply causation.” Ice cream sales are positively correlated with deaths by drowning, but ice cream doesn’t cause drowning. However, this doesn’t tell us what causation really is. While probabilistic independence and correlation coefficients have clear mathematical definitions, the precise definition of causality remains a subject of ongoing debate.

Embracing a functional theory of causation, we argue that causality essentially
is
functions that map causes to effects.
While functions are distinct from probability theory and sufficiently general for scientific purposes, we can place additional constraints and formalize
Structural Functional Model
(SFM), which better fit intuitions in
causal utterances
:

1.

Forward inference from causes to effects:

•

What if X? Y.

•

Had it been X, it would have been Y.

2.

Actual causality
(separating “actual causes” from background conditions):

•

X causes/doesn’t cause Y.

•

X is/isn’t the cause of Y.

•

What is the cause of Y? X.

Throughout this paper, the word “function” exclusively denotes a mathematical function (Appendix
A
). We’ll never use it to mean “intended purpose or task” as in “the functions of cellphones include texting.” The word “functional” is only used as the adjective form of “function.”

For SFM, we’ll explicitly separate its representation, inference, and learning
[
Koller and Friedman 2009
]
:

•

Representation
is the declarative model of “what the world is like.”

•

Inference
assumes the representation is correct and answers queries regarding particular instances, such as computing values of unknown variables given known variables.

•

Learning
inductively constructs a representation from empirical data.

Such decoupling allows us to design general-purpose inference and learning algorithms that work for different task-specific representations.

2
Representation: A Roadmap

In this section, we build the representation of SFM by incrementally adding functions, directed graphs, composition, contrast, and delta compression into a unified model. Each additional component will help SFM better fit intuitions about causal utterances, sometimes at the cost of generality.

Motivated by theoretical and pragmatic benefits like simplicity, expressiveness, and computational efficiency, the definition of SFM is unambiguous, mathematical, and reductive. It contains no circular definition because it doesn’t rely on causal concepts like intervention and agency.

2.1
Causal Relata

When we say “
X
causes
Y
”, what kinds of things are
X
and
Y
? How do we represent a world? Classifying by causal relata, there are 4 kinds of causal relationships
[
Gallow 2022
]
:

•

Token causation
: I frequently water my flower in my garden, causing it to grow tall.

•

Type causation
: Watering a plant frequently causes it to grow tall.

•

Token influence
: How much I water my flower in my garden influences how tall it grows.

•

Type influence
: How much a plant is watered influences how tall it grows.

Influence relates variables (a variable can have one of many values); causation relates values of variables.

Tokens are specific; types are general. Since this type-token distinction applies to non-causal models too, it’s not central to causality. SFM doesn’t endorse any particular theory of physics or metaphysics, so it’s up to the user to specify how variables correspond to real-world things.

Formally, let
\mathcal{V}
be a set of
nodes
(we use “nodes” instead of “variables” to avoid confusion with random variables) and
\mathcal{D}
be a function that maps nodes to their domains. For node
u\in\mathcal{V}
, its
domain

\mathcal{D}[u]
is the set of values it can take on. An
assignment
is a function that maps each node to a value in its domain.

•

A
complete assignment

\mathbf{w}:\mathcal{V}\to\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{D}[u]
assigns values to all nodes, satisfying
\forall u\in\mathcal{V}:\mathbf{w}(u)\in\mathcal{D}[u]
.

•

A
partial assignment

\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}:\mathcal{X}\to\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{D}[u]
assigns values to a subset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
of nodes, satisfying
\forall u\in\mathcal{X}:\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}(u)\in\mathcal{D}[u]
.

•

\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbf{w}
iff
\forall u\in\mathcal{X}:\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}(u)=\mathbf{w}(u)
.

We use dictionary notations
{node1:value1, node2:value2, …}
for assignments (and discrete finite functions in general). Nodes, values, and assignments are different things.
Influence relates nodes (
Water
influences
Growth
), while causation relates assignments (
{Water:High}
causes
{Growth:Tall}
).

1.

The set of all complete assignments forms the Cartesian product
\prod_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{D}[u]
.

2.

A
team

R
is a set of complete assignments
[
Väänänen 2007
]
, so
R\subseteq\prod_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{D}[u]
.
R
is a relation.

3.

For a modal/counterfactual/possible-world interpretation, each complete assignment
\mathbf{w}
is a world.
Each node is a feature/property/aspect/variable of the world.

R
is the set of possible worlds;
(\prod_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{D}[u])\setminus R
is the set of impossible worlds.

4.

For a database interpretation, each
\mathbf{w}
is an individual/person/record/item.
R
is a population containing many individuals. Each node is a property/attribute/feature of that individual.

5.

A complete assignment
\mathbf{w}

satisfies
team
R
iff
\mathbf{w}\in R
.

6.

A team
R
is
satisfiable
iff
R
is nonempty.
R
is unsatisfiable iff
R=\emptyset
.

7.

If any domain
\mathcal{D}[u]
is empty, the Cartesian product
\prod_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{D}[u]
is empty and there’s no satisfiable
R
, so we’ll only consider nonempty domains.

8.

An assignment
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
is
permitted
by
R
iff
\exists\mathbf{w}\in R:\mathbf{w}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
.
We call this
\mathbf{w}
an
induced complete assignment
of
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
.

9.

Partial assignments
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}_{1}},\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}_{2}},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}_{k}}
are
compatible
with each other iff
\exists\mathbf{w}\in R:\forall i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}:\mathbf{w}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}_{i}}
.

We will say
“
\mathcal{X}
influences
\mathcal{Y}
”
and
“
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
causes
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
,
”
where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are sets of nodes;
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
are partial assignments.

2.2
A Functional Theory of Causation

Many causal scenarios are not reducible to probability theory. For example, flipping the light switch turns on the light, but doesn’t affect the TV. This system of electric circuits is deterministic and fully-specified. We can consistently predict the “independence” between light switch and TV and what would happen given the switches’ status, using functions alone without probabilities.

According to the
functional theory of causality
, causality essentially
is
mathematical functions (left-total, right-unique relations) that map causes to effects.

Russell 1912
briefly mentions that the cause (functionally) determines the effect.

Simon and Rescher 1966
explicitly defend that causation is “a function of one variable (the cause) on to another (the effect).”
Structural Causal Model (SCM)
[
Pearl 2009a
]
uses multi-input single-output functions in structural equations to represent “laws” or “mechanisms” of the world.

“Causality as functions” becomes immediately obvious once it’s pointed out. For example,

1.

In
y=f(x)
, we call
x
the independent variable and
y
the dependent variable, like how effects depend on causes.

2.

Describing “rain influences wheat growth” with
\texttt{WheatGrowth}=f(\texttt{Rain})
, the input-output mappings are:

•

With no rain, wheat doesn’t grow.

•

With moderate rain, wheat grows moderately.

•

With heavy rain, wheat grows very well.

3.

The light-switch-and-TV example can be described by
\texttt{Light}=f_{1}(\texttt{LightSwitch})
and
\texttt{TV}=f_{2}(\texttt{TVSwitch})
.

Two key properties distinguish functions from other kinds of relations:

1.

Right-uniqueness
: 1 input value cannot simultaneously associate with 2 or more different output values. Functions can only be many-to-one or one-to-one, never one-to-many.
This explains why causes “necessitate” or “are sufficient for” their effects (given the underlying function).

2.

(Possible) non-injectiveness
: Some functions can map different input values to the same output value, like
y=x^{2}
over real numbers. Non-injective functions cannot be inverted. This explains the asymmetry of causation: different causes can lead to the same effect.

Functional dependencies
are properties of a team
R\subseteq\prod_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{D}[u]
: For
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
,

1.

Value-level dependency
: We say “
\mathcal{Y}
functionally depends on
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
” (
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}\xrightarrow{.}\mathcal{Y}
) or “
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
functionally depends on
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
” (
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}\xrightarrow{.}\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
) when given
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
, there exists exactly one
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
that’s compatible with
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
.

2.

Node-level dependency
: We say “
\mathcal{Y}
functionally depends on
\mathcal{X}
” (
\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{.}\mathcal{Y}
) when
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}\xrightarrow{.}\mathcal{Y}
for every permitted
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
.

3.

Value-level and node-level dependencies can be different. In
\mathbf{w}(Y)=\mathbf{w}(X_{1})\lor\mathbf{w}(X_{2})\lor\mathbf{w}(X_{3})
, value-level
\{X_{1}:1\}\xrightarrow{.}\{Y:1\}
is true; node-level
\{X_{1}\}\xrightarrow{.}\{Y\}
is false; node-level
\{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\}\xrightarrow{.}\{Y\}
is true.

Node-level functional dependency
\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{.}\mathcal{Y}
satisfies right-uniqueness:
\forall\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}\in R:(\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{X}}=\mathbf{w}_{2|\mathcal{X}})\Rightarrow(\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{Y}}=\mathbf{w}_{2|\mathcal{Y}})
.
So there’s a function
f:\{\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}\ |\exists\mathbf{w}\in R:\mathbf{w}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}\}\to\{\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}\ |\exists\mathbf{w}\in R:\mathbf{w}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}\}
such that
\forall\mathbf{w}\in R:\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}=f(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}})
.
We thus define
functional determination
:

1.

Node-level determination
: We say “
\mathcal{X}
functionally determines
\mathcal{Y}
via
f
” (
\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{Y}
) when
\forall\mathbf{w}\in R:\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}=f(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}})
.

2.

Value-level determination
: We say “
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
functionally determines
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
via
f
” (
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}\xrightarrow{f}\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
) when
\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}=f(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}})
.

In compliance with conventions from dependence logic
[
Galliani 2021
]
and relational databases
[
Silberschatz et al. 2020
]
, functional dependency
\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{.}\mathcal{Y}
doesn’t contain
f
, while our functional determination
\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{Y}
does.

Influence is node-level functional determination; causation is value-level functional determination. In
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}=f(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}})
,
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
is the cause,
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
is the effect, and
f
is an underlying mechanism/law-of-nature (since
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}=f(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}})
is true in every possible world
\mathbf{w}\in R
).

Generally, causality is the study of functional dependency (e.g. Armstrong’s Axioms), functional determination, and relational independence
[
Grädel and Väänänen 2013
]
. It’s nontrivial because these concepts cannot be reduced to probability theory.

We say
“
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
causes
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
”
when
\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}=f(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}})
. We say
“
\mathcal{X}
influences
\mathcal{Y}
”
when
\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{.}\mathcal{Y}
.

2.3
Directed Graphs

Previously, we first have a team
R
and then find functional determinations as properties of
R
. Now we take the opposite direction. We start with a set of functional determinations
\textbf{FDet}=\{\mathcal{X}_{1}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}\mathcal{Y}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2}\xrightarrow{f_{2}}\mathcal{Y}_{2},\dots\mathcal{X}_{n}\xrightarrow{f_{n}}\mathcal{Y}_{n}\}
, which then select
R_{\textbf{FDet}}\subseteq\prod_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{D}[u]
as all
\mathbf{w}
that satisfies
FDet
. Here “all” is necessary for defining a unique
R_{\textbf{FDet}}
, because functional dependencies and determinations are downward-closed (if
R_{1}
satisfies
FDet
, then any subset
R_{2}\subseteq R_{1}
also satisfies
FDet

[
Galliani 2021
]
).

When we draw diagrams to illustrate causal relationships, we want arrows to point from causes to effects.
Structural Causal Model (SCM)
[
Pearl 2009a
,
Pearl 2009b
,
Halpern and Pearl 2005
]
generalizes this intuition, subsumes the graphical and potential-outcome frameworks, and is the most popular causal model in statistics, econometrics, and epidemiology. Our SFM inherits the following ideas from SCM:

1.

A causal system is represented as a (usually finite and acyclic) directed graph.

2.

One mechanism’s effect can be another mechanism’s cause. One function’s output can be another function’s input.

3.

A node’s value is functionally determined by the values of its parents.

4.

Unlike SCM, our SFM doesn’t use “intervention” in its definition at all (Section
8.4
).

Besides nodes
\mathcal{V}
and domains
\mathcal{D}
, an SFM
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{F})
also has:

1.

\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
is a set of directed edges.

2.

In a directed graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, a node
u
is
exogenous
(exo-node
u\in\mathcal{V}_{exo}
) iff it’s a root node; otherwise, it’s
endogenous
(endo-node
u\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}
).

3.

We write exo-assignment
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{V}_{exo}}
as
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
and endo-assignment
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{V}_{endo}}
as
\mathbf{w}_{endo}
.

4.

\mathcal{F}
maps every endo-node
u\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}
to exactly one
structural function

\mathcal{F}[u]:(\prod_{p\in\mathrm{Pa}(u)}\mathcal{D}[p])\to\mathcal{D}[u]
.

5.

\mathcal{F}[u]:\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)}\mapsto\mathbf{w}(u)
maps an assignment over
u
’s parents to a value of
u
.

6.

R_{\mathcal{M}}=\{\mathbf{w}\in\prod_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{D}[u]\ |\ \forall u\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}:\mathbf{w}(u)=\mathcal{F}[u](\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)})\}
is the set of all complete assignments satisfying
\mathcal{M}
.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{M}
specifies functional determinations
\textbf{FDet}_{\mathcal{M}}=\{\mathrm{Pa}(u)\xrightarrow{f_{u}}\{u\}\}_{u\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}}
, where
f_{u}(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)})=\{u:\mathcal{F}[u](\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)})\}
.

.

Consider SFM
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{F})

\mathcal{V}=\{A,B,C,D,E\}

\mathcal{E}=\{(A,B),(B,D),(C,D),(C,E)\}

Some design choices of SFM inevitably restrict the kinds of functional dependencies that we can talk about:

1.

For simplicity, we only consider finite nodes because no important application requires an infinite SFM.

2.

Not every set of functional determinations be covered (entailed) by an SFM, even if we allow cycles.

Consider
\mathcal{V}=\{X,Y,Z\}
with real-valued domains, the team
R_{1}=\{\mathbf{w}|\ \mathbf{w}(X)^{2}=\mathbf{w}(Y)=\mathbf{w}(Z)^{2}\}
has functional determinations
\{X\}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{w}(Y)=\mathbf{w}(X)^{2}}\{Y\}
and
\{Z\}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{w}(Y)=\mathbf{w}(Z)^{2}}\{Y\}
. There’s no SFM
\mathcal{M}
with
R_{\mathcal{M}}=R_{1}
.

Generally, SFM cannot represent one node being functionally determined by multiple “separate” functions/mechanisms, each individually sufficient for its value. This differs from symmetric overdetermination (Section
5.7
), which is just multi-input Boolean OR.

3.

The intersection of SFMs, however, can cover any set of functional determinations.

We say
\mathbf{w}
satisfies the
SFM-intersection
over
(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n})
if
\mathbf{w}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}R_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}
(
\mathbf{w}
satisfies every individual
\mathcal{M}_{i}
).

Theorem 2.1
.

For any set of functional determinations
FDet
over finite
\mathcal{V}
, there exists a finite SFM-intersection that covers it.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{V}
is finite,
FDet
is finite.
For every
\mathcal{X}_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{i}}\mathcal{Y}_{i}
in
FDet
, we construct
\mathcal{M}_{i}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{i},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{F}_{i})
with edges
\mathcal{E}_{i}=\mathcal{X}_{i}\times\mathcal{Y}_{i}
and structural functions
\mathcal{F}_{i}[y]:\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}_{i}}\mapsto f_{i}(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}_{i}})(y)
for
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{i}
. The SFM-intersection over all
\mathcal{M}_{i}
entails
FDet
.
∎

An
SFM-intersection-proper
is an SFM-intersection that cannot be entailed by an SFM.

Besides
\mathbf{w}(X)^{2}=\mathbf{w}(Y)=\mathbf{w}(Z)^{2}
, SFM-intersection-proper can express autonomous differential equations like
\frac{d}{dt}x(t)=f(x(t))
while SFM cannot. The differential operator
\frac{d}{dt}
is also a function, so we derive 2 functional determinations:
A\xrightarrow{\frac{d}{dt}}B
and
A\xrightarrow{f}B
. Here
\{A:x(t),B:x^{\prime}(t)\}
is permitted iff
x^{\prime}(t)=f(x(t))
.

4.

Why do people dislike SFM-intersection?

It’s nearly impossible to find an uncontrived, everyday causal system that’s only describable by SFM-intersection-proper.
Kim 2005
even explicitly formulates the
Principle of Causal Exclusion
against “more than one sufficient cause” in this spirit.
This intuitive dislike is unjustified, but when taken as a primitive desideratum, it entails people’s preference of some SFMs over others for modeling reality.

We suggest 2 possible reasons for disliking SFM-intersection-proper:

(a)

Intersection of multiple SFMs creates too much mental computational burden and people prefer simpler models.

In many cases (Section
4.2
,
9.7
), people dislike the very form of SFM-intersection, even though the underlying
R=R_{\mathcal{M}}
can be modeled by some SFM
\mathcal{M}
.

(b)

SFM-intersection-proper suffers from the
possibly-unsatisfiable-laws objection (PULO)
, which applies to any set of functional dependencies
\textbf{FDep}=\{\mathcal{X}_{i}\xrightarrow{.}\mathcal{Y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that some
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
makes
\textbf{FDet}=\{\mathcal{X}_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{i}}\mathcal{Y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
unsatisfiable.

No world satisfies
FDet
, but our actual world exists, so we must reject
FDet
. PULO takes one unjustified step further, suggesting that
FDep
should also be rejected, even if some other
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
makes
\{\mathcal{X}_{i}\xrightarrow{g_{i}}\mathcal{Y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfiable, because
FDep
“opens the gate” to unsatisfiable laws. From another perspective, PULO expresses a desire for guaranteed satisfiability under any function set.

For example,
\textbf{FDep}=\{\{X\}\xrightarrow{.}\{Y\},\{Z\}\xrightarrow{.}\{Y\}\}
suffers from PULO because
\textbf{FDet}=\{\{X\}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{w}(Y)=\mathbf{w}(X)^{2}}\{Y\},\{Z\}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{w}(Y)=-\mathbf{w}(Z)^{2}-1}\{Y\}\}
is unsatisfiable over real-valued domains.

5.

PULO strikes again: When there are self-loops or cycles in the graph, there exist function sets that make the SFM unsatisfiable, such as
A=A+1
and
\{A=B+1;B=A+1\}
:

A
\mathcal{F}[A](\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}(A)+1
A
B
\mathcal{F}[B](\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}(A)+1
\mathcal{F}[A](\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}(B)+1

6.

Besides simplicity and intuitive appeals, finite acyclic SFM
\mathcal{M}
has other nice properties (Section

):

•

\mathcal{M}
is satisfiable for any
\mathcal{F}
.

•

\mathcal{V}_{exo}
functionally determines
\mathcal{V}_{endo}
via
\mathbf{w}_{endo}\subseteq\mathbf{w}=\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{exo})
.

Are they worth the price of rejecting many (possibly satisfiable) sets of functional dependencies? We’re unsure.

7.

Different SFMs
\mathcal{M}_{1}\neq\mathcal{M}_{2}
over the same
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{D})
can be “semantically equivalent”
R_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}=R_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}
, which entails “
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}=f(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}})
in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
iff
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}=f(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}})
in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
”, including
\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{exo})=\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M}_{2},\mathbf{w}_{exo})
for all
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
.

We’ll only consider functional determinations that can be modeled by finite acyclic SFMs, where an endo-node is functionally determined by its parents.

2.4
Composition and Decomposition

Since
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
functionally determines
\mathbf{w}_{endo}
via
\mathbf{w}_{endo}\subseteq\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{exo})
(Section
3.2
), we produce all causal utterances as “
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
causes
\mathbf{w}_{endo}
.”

This syntax is simple, but an ostensible flaw is that only exo-assignments can be causes. In
A\to B\to C
, we cannot say “
\{B:b\}
causes
\{C:c\}
” because
B
is an endo-node. This problem is solved by considering the sub-SFM
B\to C
, where
B
becomes an exo-node. Sub-SFM generalizes
Pearl 2009a
’s surgical intervention, which cuts off all incoming edges to the nodes under intervention.

\mathcal{M}_{sub}=(\mathcal{V}_{sub},\mathcal{E}_{sub},\mathcal{D}_{sub},\mathcal{F}_{sub})
is a
sub-SFM
of
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{F})
when:

1.

(\mathcal{V}_{sub},\mathcal{E}_{sub})
is a subgraph of
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, i.e.
\mathcal{V}_{sub}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{E}_{sub}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, and
(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}_{sub}\Rightarrow(u\in\mathcal{V}_{sub})\land(v\in\mathcal{V}_{sub})
.

2.

\forall u\in\mathcal{V}_{sub|endo}:\mathrm{Pa}_{sub}(u)=\mathrm{Pa}(u)
.

3.

\forall u\in\mathcal{V}_{sub}:\mathcal{D}_{sub}[u]=\mathcal{D}[u]

4.

\forall u\in\mathcal{V}_{sub|endo}:\mathcal{F}_{sub}[u]=\mathcal{F}[u]

An exo-node in
\mathcal{M}
can be nonexistent or exogenous in
\mathcal{M}_{sub}
; an endo-node in
\mathcal{M}
can be nonexistent, exogenous, or endogenous (with the same parents and structural function) in
\mathcal{M}_{sub}
, so the mechanisms-of-nature are preserved.

We can
compose
a set of smaller SFMs
\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{m}\}
into a bigger SFM
\mathcal{M}
without altering any structural function, if the following prerequisites are met for any pair of
(\mathcal{M}_{i},\mathcal{M}_{j})
:

1.

\forall u\in\mathcal{V}_{i}\cap\mathcal{V}_{j}:\mathcal{D}_{i}[u]=\mathcal{D}_{j}[u]

2.

\forall u\in\mathcal{V}_{i|endo}\cap\mathcal{V}_{j|endo}:\mathrm{Pa}_{i}(u)=\mathrm{Pa}_{j}(u)

3.

\forall u\in\mathcal{V}_{i|endo}\cap\mathcal{V}_{j|endo}:\mathcal{F}_{i}(u)=\mathcal{F}_{j}(u)

These prerequisites ensure that the composition
\mathcal{M}=(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{V}_{i},\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{E}_{i},\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{D}_{i},\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{F}_{i})
is well-defined. For
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{D}_{i}
and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{F}_{i}
,

1.

\mathcal{D}_{i}
maps nodes to domains.

2.

\mathcal{F}_{i}
maps nodes to structural functions.

3.

Functions (including
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{F}
) are binary relations.

4.

The union of sets/relations/functions is well defined.

5.

The prerequisites ensure that each node
u
has exactly one unique
\mathcal{D}[u]
and at most one unique
\mathcal{F}[u]
across all
i
, so
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{D}_{i}
and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{F}_{i}
are right-unique and thus functions.

The
decomposition
of SFM
\mathcal{M}
is a set of sub-SFMs
\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{m}\}
that can compose into
\mathcal{M}
. While composition of sub-SFMs (when allowed) is unique, there can be multiple different decompositions of an SFM, the most trivial being “keeping the original SFM itself” and the most fragmented being “one sub-SFM for each endo-node and its parents.”

Composition shows how small, local, and simple sub-mechanisms can be pieced together into one big, global, and complex system, while decomposition breaks down a large system into small sub-mechanisms. Therefore, we can deductively reason about a big, unrepeatable event using its components and their interconnections.

With composition-decomposition, we can say
“
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
causes
\mathbf{w}_{endo}
”
relative to some sub-SFM.

2.5
Contrastive Causation

Currently, SFM can already perfectly express a causal system by correctly answering all “what’s
\mathbf{w}_{endo}
if
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
” questions. But in causal utterances, people only say “the actual causes” and omit background conditions (Section
5.11
).
The selection of actual causes takes 2 steps: contrast and omission. We’ll discuss contrast in this section.

Schaffer 2005
believes causation is contrastive. Besides the 2-argument surface form (cause, effect), the 4-argument underlying form includes contrast on both sides:

•

Surface form: Pam’s throwing the rock caused the window to shatter.

•

Contrastive form 1: Throwing the rock (rather than the pebble) caused the window to shatter (rather than crack).

•

Contrastive form 2: Throwing the rock (rather than not throwing it) caused the window to shatter (rather than remain intact).

We specify 2 assignments
\mathbf{w}_{a},\mathbf{w}_{c}
for contrastive causal utterance “
\mathbf{w}_{a|exo}
(rather than
\mathbf{w}_{c|exo}
) causes
\mathbf{w}_{a|endo}
(rather than
\mathbf{w}_{c|endo}
)”:

1.

Actual assignment

\mathbf{w}_{a}
corresponds to the actual world (i.e. what actually happens).

2.

Contrastive assignment

\mathbf{w}_{c}
is selected using one of two heuristics:

(a)

\mathbf{w}_{c}
is a default/expected/normal/typical world;
\mathbf{w}_{a}
is an anomalous/unexpected deviation from the default.

Normality inevitably comes with value judgments, but contrast reduces “finding the actual causes” to “finding a default world,” which is a nontrivial simplification.

(b)

With
\mathbf{w}_{a}
available first, we tweak
\mathbf{w}_{a|exo}
into
\mathbf{w}_{c|exo}
by changing the values of a few exo-nodes of interest. We then obtain
\mathbf{w}_{c}=\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{c|exo})=\texttt{CFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{a},\mathbf{w}_{c|exo})
through forward inference (Section
3.2
).

This is common when too many nodes in
\mathbf{w}_{a}
have non-default values, or when there’s no appropriate default world.

Our contrastive causation is slightly simpler than
Schaffer 2005
’s and
Halpern and Hitchcock 2015
’s, because we only need to specify one contrastive world
\mathbf{w}_{c}
(rather than many).

Contrast is common in our causal intuition:

1.

People often characterize causality as “changing the cause will also change the effect” or “making a difference.” Ignoring the manipulation aspect of an agent changing an object, change is inherently contrastive - there’s an old state that changes to a new state.

2.

Some philosophers try to define “event
X
causes event
Y
” as “
X
raises the probability of
Y
(
\Pr[Y|X]>\Pr[Y|\lnot X]
).” This definition fails to address causal asymmetry and spurious correlations
[
Hitchcock 2021
]
, so it’s never popular among statisticians. However, the very idea of “raising” contains a contrast between a world with
X
and a world with
\lnot X
.

3.

The contrast of treatment effects is formalized in statistical causal inference. Using the potential outcome notations in
Hernan and Robins 2020
,

•

causal risk difference:
\Pr[Y^{a=1}=1]-\Pr[Y^{a=0}=1]

•

causal risk ratio:
\frac{\Pr[Y^{a=1}=1]}{\Pr[Y^{a=0}=1]}

•

causal odds ratio:
\frac{\Pr[Y^{a=1}=1]/\Pr[Y^{a=1}=0]}{\Pr[Y^{a=0}=1]/\Pr[Y^{a=0}=0]}

These measurements all involve a contrast between random variables
Y^{a=0}
(effect under treatment 0) and
Y^{a=1}
(effect under treatment 1).

4.

To understand a function
y=f(x)
, we often record an initial input value
x_{0}
and its corresponding output value
y_{0}=f(x_{0})
; we then change
x_{0}
to
x_{1}
and see how the output value
y
changes in response. For example, derivatives in calculus help quantify how “sensitive” the output is with respect to the input.

With actual assignment
\mathbf{w}_{a}
and contrastive assignment
\mathbf{w}_{c}
, we say
“
\mathbf{w}_{a|exo}
(rather than
\mathbf{w}_{c|exo}
) causes
\mathbf{w}_{a|endo}
(rather than
\mathbf{w}_{c|endo}
).
”

2.6
Delta Compression

To characterize omission in causal utterances, we consider
\mathcal{C}=\{u\in\mathcal{V}|\mathbf{w}_{a}(u)\neq\mathbf{w}_{c}(u)\}
: the nodes that have different values in
\mathbf{w}_{a}
and
\mathbf{w}_{c}
.
|\mathcal{C}|
is the
Hamming distance
between
\mathbf{w}_{a}
and
\mathbf{w}_{c}
. With
\mathcal{C}_{exo}=\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{V}_{exo}
and
\mathcal{C}_{endo}=\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{V}_{endo}
, the final causal utterance is “
\mathbf{w}_{a|\mathcal{C}_{exo}}
causes
\mathbf{w}_{a|\mathcal{C}_{endo}}
.”

If something doesn’t change, we don’t mention it. We only mention the new values of changed nodes. This is an example of
delta compression

[
Suel 2019
]
:

Encoder
wants to transmit a
target file
to
Decoder
. Encoder and Decoder can both access a
reference file
. The target file is only slightly different from the reference file, so their
delta
(change/difference) is much smaller than the target file itself. To reduce the amount of transferred data, Encoder computes the delta (using target and reference files) and sends it to Decoder; Decoder reconstructs the target file by adding the delta to the reference file.

Delta compression is widely used in version control, where we want to store many successive versions of the same file, but any 2 consecutive versions differ only slightly.

Example 2.2
.

Consider nodes
\{A,B,C,D\}
with integer domains and assignments
\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{1}
:

1.

\mathbf{w}_{0}=\{A:1,B:2,C:3,D:4\}

2.

\mathbf{w}_{1}=\{A:1,B:7,C:3,D:5\}

3.

\mathcal{C}=\{B,D\}

4.

\mathbf{w}_{0|\mathcal{C}}=\{B:2,D:4\}

5.

\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{C}}=\{B:7,D:5\}

People may prefer delta compression because it shortens causal utterances without losing information or introducing ambiguities. This saving of “mental bandwidth” is especially prominent when:

1.

We want to represent many
\mathbf{w}_{1}
relative to one
\mathbf{w}_{0}
.

2.

Each
\mathbf{w}_{1}
differs only slightly from
\mathbf{w}_{0}
, i.e.
|\mathcal{C}|
is small relative to
|\mathcal{V}|
.

In default-actual contrasts, the default
\mathbf{w}_{c}
is kept constant for reference; in actual-tweaked contrasts,
\mathbf{w}_{a}
is held for reference.

We say
\mathbf{w}_{a|\mathcal{C}_{exo}}
causes
\mathbf{w}_{a|\mathcal{C}_{endo}}
, where
\mathcal{C}=\{u\in\mathcal{V}|\mathbf{w}_{a}(u)\neq\mathbf{w}_{c}(u)\}
is the set of changed nodes.

3
Inference

3.1
Constraint Satisfaction

During
inference
, we assume the SFM
\mathcal{M}
is true. An inference algorithm takes in assignment
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
over known nodes
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and a set of target nodes
\mathcal{Y}
, whose values we’re interested in inferring. It then checks whether
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
is permitted and if so, returns one or more
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
that’s compatible with
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
.

If all domains are finite, we can formulate SFM inference as a constraint satisfaction problem (CSP)
[
Russell 2010
]
and use off-the-shelf CSP solvers for inference:

1.

The domain of node
u\in\mathcal{V}
is
\mathcal{D}[u]
.

2.

For each
u\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}
, its structural equation gives a
(|\mathrm{Pa}(u)|+1)
-ary constraint
\mathbf{w}(u)=\mathcal{F}[u](\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)})
over scope
\mathrm{Pa}(u)\cup\{u\}
.

3.

Each known value
w_{u}=\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}(u)
for
u\in\mathcal{X}
is a unary constraint
\mathbf{w}(u)=w_{u}
over scope
\{u\}
.

CSP does have a few drawbacks:

1.

It’s NP-complete in general.

2.

It’s unnecessary for most thought experiments, where the SFMs are small and solvable by hand.

3.

It offers no guarantee for the existence or uniqueness of
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{Y}}
. For example, if
y=f(x)
is non-injective, different
x
can be compatible with the same
y
.

Thanks to right-uniqueness, inferring effects from causes is much easier.

3.2
Forward Inference

Forward inference
infers effects from causes.
Given SFM
\mathcal{M}
,
vanilla forward inference
(VFI) computes
\mathbf{w}=\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{exo})
, where
\mathbf{w}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{exo}
and of
\mathbf{w}\in R_{\mathcal{M}}
.

When
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
is finite and acyclic (and
\forall u\in\mathcal{V}:\mathcal{D}[u]\neq\emptyset
),

•

\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{F})
is satisfiable for any
\mathcal{F}
;

•

\mathbf{w}_{exo}
functionally determines
\mathbf{w}_{endo}
;
VFI
itself is a function.

Intuitively,
VFI
deterministically infers all effects given the root causes and mechanisms-of-nature.

Theorem 3.1
.

(Forward Inference)
In a finite acyclic SFM
\mathcal{M}
(with nonempty domains), for any exo-assignment
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
, there exists a unique complete assignment
\mathbf{w}
satisfying
\mathbf{w}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{exo}
and
\mathbf{w}\in R_{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proof.

•

Existence
: Because
\mathcal{G}
is finite,
\mathcal{V}
is finite. Because
\mathcal{G}
is acyclic, there exists a
topological order

L
of nodes: an ordered list of all nodes such that
[(L[i],L[j])\in\mathcal{E}]\Rightarrow[i<j]
. Using
topological sort
algorithms like depth-first-search and Kahn’s algorithm, we can compute
L
in
\Theta(|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}|)
time; cycle detection is done simultaneously
[
Cormen et al. 2022
]
.

Given
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
, we compute
\mathbf{w}_{1}
sequentially from
i=1
to
i=|\mathcal{V}|
inclusive:

1.

If
L[i]\in\mathcal{V}_{exo}
, we assign
\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[i])\leftarrow\mathbf{w}_{exo}(L[i])
.

2.

If
L[i]\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}
, we assign
\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[i])\leftarrow\mathcal{F}[L[i]](\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathrm{Pa}(L[i])})
.

3.

Any parent
L[j]\in\mathrm{Pa}(L[i])
must appear earlier (
j<i
) than its child
L[i]
because
L
is a topological order.
\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[j])
must have already been assigned, so
\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathrm{Pa}(L[i])}
is well-defined.

Because
\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[i])
isn’t modified after iteration
i
:

1.

If
L[i]\in\mathcal{V}_{exo}
,
\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[i])=\mathbf{w}_{exo}(L[i])
is always satisfied.

2.

If
L[i]\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}
,
\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[i])=\mathcal{F}[L[i]](\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathrm{Pa}(L[i])})
is always satisfied.

Therefore,
\mathbf{w}_{1}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{exo}
and
\mathbf{w}_{1}
satisfies
\mathcal{M}
.

This constructive proof also specifies the algorithm
\mathbf{w}=\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{exo})
, assuming every structural function
\mathcal{F}[u]
is computable.

•

Uniqueness
: Proving by contradiction, suppose instead that there’s another
\mathbf{w}_{2}\neq\mathbf{w}_{1}
satisfying
\mathbf{w}_{2}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{exo}
and
\mathbf{w}_{2}\in R_{\mathcal{M}}
. With topological order
L
, there exists a smallest integer
i
such that
\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[i])\neq\mathbf{w}_{2}(L[i])
.

Because
L
is a topological order, every parent
L[j]\in\mathrm{Pa}(L[i])
appears earlier (
j<i
). Since
L[i]
is the earliest node with different values,
\forall L[j]\in\mathrm{Pa}(L[i]):\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[j])=\mathbf{w}_{2}(L[j])
and
\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathrm{Pa}(L[i])}=\mathbf{w}_{2|\mathrm{Pa}(L[j])}
.

Because functions are right-unique,
\mathcal{F}[L[i]](\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathrm{Pa}(L[i])})=\mathcal{F}[L[i]](\mathbf{w}_{2|\mathrm{Pa}(L[i])})
. Because
\mathbf{w}(L[i])
is only modified at iteration
i
,
\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[i])=\mathbf{w}_{2}(L[i])
, which contradicts
\mathbf{w}_{1}(L[i])\neq\mathbf{w}_{2}(L[i])
. Therefore,
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w}_{2}
; the induced complete assignment from an exo-assignment is unique.

∎

Existence entails left-totality; uniqueness entails right-uniqueness, so
\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{exo})
itself is a function of
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
.
Since
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
functionally determines
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}_{endo}\subseteq\mathbf{w}
, Armstrong’s Axioms entail “
\mathbf{w}_{exo}
functionally determines
\mathbf{w}_{endo}
.”

During forward inference,
\mathcal{G}
is also a computational graph, where edges indicate the order of computation. We start with exo-nodes and the computation “flows down” to endo-nodes, computing their values based on the previously computed values of their parents. Topological sort and graph traversal both take
\Theta(|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}|)
time under adjacency-list representation of graphs. For each
u\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}
,
\mathcal{F}[u]
is computed exactly once.

Corollary 3.1.1
.

In a finite acyclic SFM
\mathcal{M}
with nonempty domains, any partial assignment
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
over any subset of exo-nodes
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{exo}
is permitted.

Proof.

For every
u\in\mathcal{V}_{exo}\setminus\mathcal{X}
, we assign an arbitrary
\mathbf{w}_{exo}(u)\in\mathcal{D}[u]
since
\mathcal{D}[u]\neq\emptyset
; for every
u\in\mathcal{X}
, we assign
\mathbf{w}_{exo}(u)\leftarrow\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}(u)
, so
\mathbf{w}_{exo}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
. By Theorem
3.1
,
\mathbf{w}=\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{exo})
satisfies
\mathbf{w}_{exo}\subseteq\mathbf{w}\in R_{\mathcal{M}}
, so
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbf{w}_{exo}\subseteq\mathbf{w}\in R_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
is permitted.
∎

Corollary 3.1.2
.

A finite acyclic SFM
\mathcal{M}
with nonempty domains is always satisfiable, regardless of its structural functions
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Because
\mathcal{G}
is finite (no infinite regress) and acyclic, there exists at least one root node
u
(Appendix
B
). Because
\mathcal{D}[u]\neq\emptyset
, we select an arbitrary value
\mathbf{w}_{|\{u\}}(u)\in\mathcal{D}[u]
. Corollary
3.1.1
says
\mathbf{w}_{|\{u\}}
is permitted, so
\exists\mathbf{w}\in R_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathbf{w}\supseteq\mathbf{w}_{|\{u\}}
and
\mathcal{M}
is satisfiable.
∎

3.3
Functional Invariance

We use
functional invariance
to describe how a multi-input function’s output doesn’t change when some inputs have changed: TVs aren’t affected by light switches; the output of
f(x,y)=2x
is invariant to
y
given
x
. Notice that
ceteris paribus
(holding other input values constant) is well-defined only if there’s a clear input-output distinction given by an underlying function.

In SFM, changing an exo-node’s value cannot influence its non-descendants. This is deduced from
\mathcal{G}
alone. With non-injective functions, new parent values may map to the old child value, resulting in even fewer changed nodes. Equivalently, for
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{exo}
,
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{V}_{exo}\setminus\mathcal{X}}
) functionally determines
\mathcal{X}
’s non-descendants.

Theorem 3.2
.

(Invariance in SFM)
In a finite acyclic SFM
\mathcal{M}
with
\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}\in R_{\mathcal{M}}
and changed nodes
\mathcal{C}=\{u\in\mathcal{V}|\mathbf{w}_{0}(u)\neq\mathbf{w}_{1}(u)\}
:

If
\mathbf{w}_{0}(u)\neq\mathbf{w}_{1}(u)
, then
u\in\bigcup_{v\in\mathcal{C}_{exo}}\mathrm{De}(v)
. (
u
’s value differs in
\mathbf{w}_{0}
and
\mathbf{w}_{1}
only if it’s the descendant of some node in
\mathcal{C}_{exo}
.)

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{C}
be any node such that
\mathbf{w}_{0}(u)\neq\mathbf{w}_{1}(u)
. If
u\in\mathcal{V}_{exo}
, then
u\in\mathcal{C}_{exo}
and we’re done. If
u\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}
, then because functions are right-unique, at least one parent
p\in\mathrm{Pa}(u)
must have a different value (
\mathbf{w}_{0}(p)\neq\mathbf{w}_{1}(p)
). We consider
p
as the new
u
and repeat this process recursively. Because the SFM graph is finite (no infinite regress) and acyclic, this path
u\leftarrow p_{1}\leftarrow p_{2}\leftarrow\dots
must terminate at some exo-node
s\in\mathcal{V}_{exo}
(Appendix
B
) such that
\mathbf{w}_{0}(s)\neq\mathbf{w}_{1}(s)
, which means
s\in\mathcal{C}_{exo}
. The path shows
u
is a descendant of
s
.
∎

3.4
Contrastive Forward Inference

Suppose we already have
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathbf{w}_{0}\in R_{\mathcal{M}}
. To compute
\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{1|exo})
, we still need to compute every
\mathcal{F}[u]
. Given all the unchanged nodes from functional invariance, can the graph structure help us reduce
\mathcal{F}[u]
evaluations?

Yes. With
\mathcal{C}_{exo}=\{u\in\mathcal{V}_{exo}|\mathbf{w}_{0|exo}(u)\neq\mathbf{w}_{1|exo}(u)\}
, the
contrastive forward inference
(CFI) algorithm
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\texttt{CFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{C}_{exo}})
evaluates
\mathcal{F}[u]
only when at least one parent of
u
has a changed value, so we don’t recompute non-descendants of
\mathcal{C}_{exo}
.
CFI
evaluates usually fewer (and always no more) structural functions than
VFI
, especially when
\mathcal{C}
is small relative to
\mathcal{V}
, when there are many
\mathbf{w}_{i|exo}
queries relative to one reference
\mathbf{w}_{0}
, or when many structural functions are non-injective.

Input:

•

\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{F})
, the SFM.

•

L=[u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{n}]
, list of all nodes in topological order (cached).

•

\mathbf{w}_{0}
, reference assignment.

•

\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{X}}
, exo-assignment over
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{exo}
for the new forward inference query.

Output:

•

\mathbf{w}_{1}
, the induced complete assignment from
\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{C}_{exo}}\cup\mathbf{w}_{0|\mathcal{V}_{exo}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{exo}}
.

\mathbf{w}_{1}\leftarrow
empty dictionary;

\mathbf{c}\leftarrow
empty dictionary;
//
whether a node’s value changed

foreach

u\in\mathcal{V}

do

if

u\in\mathcal{X}
and

Algorithm 1

Contrastive forward inference algorithm

If we draw an SFM with all arrows pointing downwards, we visually cache a topological order of nodes. We can easily identify the descendants of changed nodes and only evaluate their structural functions, without recomputing the complete assignment.

Unlike functions, contrast isn’t a fundamental and irreducible part of causality. It’s just a popular heuristic with pragmatic benefits:

1.

Delta compression reduces the length of causal utterances.

2.

CFI
recomputes (usually) fewer structural functions than
VFI
during forward inference.

3.5
Partial Forward Inference

By modifying depth-first search, we can also design
partial forward inference
algorithms, where we’re only interested in a subset of endo-nodes
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{endo}
, so we don’t have to compute values for all endo-nodes. Combined with
CFI
, it further reduces the number of function evaluations, especially when
\mathcal{Y}
is much smaller than
\mathcal{V}_{endo}
.

3.6
Inference in Practice

1.

VFI in Boolean circuits
: A combinational logic circuit
[
Patt and Patel 2020
]
is a finite acyclic SFM with
\{0,1\}
domains and Boolean functions.
Each wire’s value is 0 (no electrical current) or 1 (has current). A logic gate receives input wires and returns an output wire, like a structural function.
The output wire of one gate can be the input wire of another gate.
To infer the values of all wires given all input wires, we use VFI and produce causal utterances like “setting this input wire to 1 causes the output wire to be 0.”

2.

CFI in GNU Make
: GNU
make
is a popular open-source software that automatically determines which pieces of a large program need to be recompiled
[
gnu 2023
]
. Especially in C and C++, the source code needs to be compiled or linked into a target file, before the target file can be executed by the computer. In a
Makefile
, there are many rules. Each rule has a target file, a list of source files, and a recipe for compilation. The target file functionally depends on the source files. The target file of one rule can be a source file in another rule. This forms a finite SFM where files are nodes and rules specify edges and structural functions.

VFI compiles all files, but software development is a dynamic process:
We don’t compile the files just once. We modify some files, see the results, and repeat.
Because compilation is time-consuming, it’s costly to recompile all files after a modification.
Instead, we only need to recompile the descendants of modified files. Just like CFI,
make
only recompiles the target file if any of its source files (parents) has been modified since the previous compilation, saving lots of time. We can produce causal utterances like “modifying this file causes the final compiled program to crash.”

4.2
Light, Object, and Shadow

In a symmetric equation involving
{Light, Object, Shadow}
, any 2 nodes functionally determine the 1 remaining node. Why do we think the shadow is the effect? This asymmetric preference is entailed by people’s general dislike of SFM-intersection:

•

With multiple objects,
\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Light, Shadow\lx@text@rbrace}\xrightarrow{.}\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Object\lx@text@rbrace}
isn’t true in general. When we add another object whose shadow rests entirely in another object’s shadow, the system’s light and shadow remain the same, thus violating right-uniqueness.

•

\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Light, Shadow\lx@text@rbrace}\xrightarrow{.}\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Object\lx@text@rbrace}
cannot SFM-compose with
\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Factory\lx@text@rbrace}\xrightarrow{.}\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Object\lx@text@rbrace}
(objects determined by their production processes). Explicitly encoding both functional dependencies requires SFM-intersection.

•

With one light source and multiple objects,
\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Object(i), Shadow(i)\lx@text@rbrace}\xrightarrow{.}\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Light\lx@text@rbrace}
holds for every
Object(i)
, resulting in SFM-intersection.

•

\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Object, Shadow\lx@text@rbrace}\xrightarrow{.}\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Light\lx@text@rbrace}
cannot SFM-compose with
\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Hand\lx@text@rbrace}\xrightarrow{.}\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Light\lx@text@rbrace}
(flashlight direction determined by hand movement) or
\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace TimeOfDay\lx@text@rbrace}\xrightarrow{.}\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Light\lx@text@rbrace}
(the Sun’s position determined by time of the day), unless we use SFM-intersection.

•

\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Object, Light\lx@text@rbrace}\xrightarrow{.}\texttt{\lx@text@lbrace Shadow\lx@text@rbrace}
can seamlessly compose with upstream and downstream SFMs without SFM-intersection.

6
Discussion

6.1
Is SFM Insufficient?

Some may argue that since SFM and functions can have non-causal interpretations, they are insufficient for defining causality. We respond with 3 counterarguments:

1.

Some examples of insufficiency are results of misinterpretation. For example, student ID functionally determines all attributes (name, age, course registration, etc.) of a student in a database, but changing a student’s ID won’t cause changes in those attributes. This example doesn’t hold because if we allow arbitrary changes to ID, there could be repeated IDs in different rows and ID no longer functionally determines other attributes.

2.

Incorrect causal models (e.g. “cancer causes smoking”) are still causal, unlike non-causal models (e.g. correlations, symmetric equations), which don’t use functions at all. Since SFM comes from the conceptual analysis of what “causation” should mean, its definition cannot include all empirical facts about our world.

3.

People often use causal interpretations to understand purely mathematical functions. When we say “changing the independent variable
x
causes the dependent variable
y
to change,” we’re using CFI.

6.2
A Case Against Actual Causality

Delta compression and CFI are slightly useful heuristics that also fit our intuitions. However, the assumption that there exists a fixed set of “actual causes” is questionable in complex systems.

Example 6.1
.

A circuit has
n
binary switches
\mathcal{V}_{exo}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\}
and 1 light bulb
\mathcal{V}_{endo}=\{Y\}
, where the
n
switches functionally determine the light via a Boolean function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
.

Given the state of all switches and the light, which switches are the “actual causes” of the light being on/off?

There are
2^{2^{n}}
different
n
-input
1
-output Boolean functions
f
. For each
f
, there are
2^{n}
different possible worlds. Proponents of actual causality must accept one of the following:

1.

Provide an algorithm that can identify actual causes in
2^{2^{n}}\times 2^{n}=2^{(2^{n}+n)}
situations. Case-by-case analyses won’t scale.

2.

Admit that contrast, default, and actual causality belong to an imperfect mental heuristic that would fail in complex systems.

Graphical models don’t help because there’s only one Boolean function and we shouldn’t insert hypothetical intermediate nodes. For
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{exo}
, SFM can answer “is
\mathbf{w}_{0|\mathcal{X}}
the cause of
\mathbf{w}_{0|endo}
?” by tweaking
\mathbf{w}_{0|\mathcal{X}}
into
\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{X}}
, inferring
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\texttt{CFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{X}})
, and contrasting
\mathbf{w}_{1}(Y)
against
\mathbf{w}_{0}(Y)
. But “a fixed set of actual causes” given
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathbf{w}_{0}
remains ill-defined.
People’s intuitions may not give consistent answers and even if they do, such answers provide less information about
f
than the input-output mappings of
f
itself.

This example generalizes all “difficult causal scenarios” with binary variables and following features:

1.

Causal
: We can manipulate the switches to control the light.

2.

Deterministic
: It has no probabilistic component.

3.

Fully-specified
: Epistemological skepticism like “how do we know these laws-of-nature are true” doesn’t apply.

4.

Clear input-output distinction
: There’s no ambiguity in the direction of causal arrows.

Therefore, proposing and solving a few cases wouldn’t dissolve our objection.

Intuitions are often unreliable for modeling reality. Outside simple, everyday causal utterances, there’s no real downside in abandoning actual causality.
\mathcal{M}
itself perfectly describes the causal system and answers all “what if” inference queries like
\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{exo})
. Instead of listing “actual causes,” a scientist should try modeling the functional determinations in a system.

Actual causality is almost only used in normative theories (e.g. responsibility, blame, proximate causes, ethics, law
[
Moore 2019
]
), which handle disagreements when everyone agrees on
\mathcal{M}
(laws-of-nature) and
\mathbf{w}_{a}
(what actually happens). Working with full SFMs instead of actual causality allows us to consider strictly more normative theories.

7
Probabilistic SFM

To incorporate probability theory, we don’t need to modify the definition of SFM. We just extend domains
\mathcal{D}[u]
to include random variables and modify structural functions
\mathcal{F}[u]
accordingly. Probability isn’t required for most thought experiments on causality, but we’ll provide a rigorous mathematical foundation for probabilistic SFM. Notably,
nodes and random variables are not the same
. We avoid calling nodes “variables” precisely for this reason.

7.1
Probabilistic Extension

Think of a node
u
as a name or index. Its value
\mathbf{w}(u)
can be a random variable:
\mathbf{w}(u)=X
. A
random variable

X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
maps an outcome
\omega
(in sample space
\Omega
) to a real number
X(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}
. “
X=x
” is a shorthand for event
\{\omega\in\Omega|X(\omega)=x\}
, so we can compute its probability
\Pr[X=x]
.
X=x
isn’t an actual equation because
X
is a function and
x
is a real number. Again, “node
u
has value
X
;
X
is a random variable” and “random variable
X
takes on value
x
;
x
is a real number” are different things.

Most basically,
functions of random variables
are actually function compositions
[
Blitzstein and Hwang 2015
]
. Consider real-valued function
f(x)=2x
and random variable
X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. We want a new random variable
Y
that always “takes twice the value” of
X
:

\displaystyle Y(\omega)

\displaystyle=2X(\omega)

\displaystyle=f(X(\omega))

\displaystyle=(f\circ X)(\omega)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=f\circ X

The expression “
Y=f(X)
” is wrong by a rigorous standard, because random variable
X
isn’t in
f
’s domain of real numbers.

Formally, the
probabilistic extension
of SFM
\mathcal{M}_{old}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}_{old},\mathcal{F}_{old})
returns a new SFM
\mathcal{M}_{new}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}_{new},\mathcal{F}_{new})
:

1.

Specify the sample space
\Omega
.

2.

Specify a set of nodes
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
for probabilistic extension. Other nodes
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}
still don’t have random variables in their domains.

The set
\mathcal{S}
must be downward closed: if
u\in\mathcal{S}
, then every descendant of
u
must also be in
\mathcal{S}
, as if randomness is “contagious” and flows down the computational graph.

For random variables to be well-defined, we also require
\mathcal{D}_{old}[u]
(e.g. real numbers, vectors, graphs, functions) to be measurable for all
u\in\mathcal{S}
.

3.

Let
RV[u]
denote the set of random variables
\Omega\to\mathcal{D}_{old}[u]
. If
u\in\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{D}_{new}[u]=\mathcal{D}_{old}[u]\cup RV[u]
; otherwise,
\mathcal{D}_{new}[u]=\mathcal{D}_{old}[u]
.

4.

Recall that a random variable
X
has realization
X(\omega)
given outcome
\omega\in\Omega
.

For nodes
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, we define the
realization
of assignment
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
given outcome
\omega
as:

\texttt{Realize}(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}},\omega)=\{u:\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}(u)\textbf{ if }\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}(u)\in\mathcal{D}_{old}[u]\textbf{ else }(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}(u))(\omega)\}_{u\in\mathcal{X}}

By realizing every random variable with
\omega
and keeping other values as is,
\texttt{Realize}(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}},\omega)
is an assignment of both
\mathcal{M}_{old}
and
\mathcal{M}_{new}
because
\forall u\in\mathcal{V}:\texttt{Realize}(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}},\omega)(u)\in\mathcal{D}_{old}[u]
.

5.

For
\mathcal{F}_{new}
and endo-node
u\in\mathcal{V}_{endo}
:

•

If
\forall p\in\mathrm{Pa}(u):\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)}(p)\in\mathcal{D}_{old}[p]
(no parent value is a random variable), then
\mathcal{F}_{new}[u](\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)})=\mathcal{F}_{old}[u](\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)})\in\mathcal{D}_{old}[u]
.

•

Otherwise (at least one parent value is a random variable),
\mathcal{F}_{new}[u](\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)})
is a random variable
\Omega\to\mathcal{D}_{old}[u]
in
RV[u]
. For outcome
\omega\in\Omega
, we compute
\mathcal{F}_{new}[u](\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)})(\omega)=\mathcal{F}_{old}[u](\texttt{Realize}(\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(u)},\omega))
.

Some corollaries about probabilistic extension:

1.

For every
u\in\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{F}_{new}[u]\supseteq\mathcal{F}_{old}[u]
because both the domain and the codomain are strictly extended, hence the name “probabilistic extension.”

2.

If
\mathbf{w}
satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{old}
, then it also satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{new}
. For
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, if
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
is permitted by
\mathcal{M}_{old}
, then it’s also permitted by
\mathcal{M}_{new}
.

3.

If
\mathbf{w}
satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{new}
, then its realization
\texttt{Realize}(\mathbf{w},\omega)
also satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{old}
for every
\omega\in\Omega
.

Intuitively, random variables express uncertainty about which realization is actual. Each realization is a possible world in
\mathcal{M}_{old}
. Probability merely adds “weights” to these possible worlds, so causal mechanisms are deterministic and true in every realization. This is unlike Bayesian networks, where the mechanisms are inherently random.

We can now formalize “correlation doesn’t imply causation” using SFM: The same “observational distribution”
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathcal{X}}
(where some nodes have random variables as values;
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
) might be permitted by different SFMs
\mathcal{M}_{1}\neq\mathcal{M}_{2}
with
R_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}\neq R_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}
, which cannot be treated as equal.

7.2
Bayesian Networks

With probabilistic extension, SFM generalizes Bayesian networks, which also use directed acyclic graphs. In a Bayesian network
[
Koller and Friedman 2009
]
, each node corresponds to a random variable, each exo-node stores a marginal distribution, and each endo-node stores a conditional distribution given the node’s parents.

Bayesian networks require the exogenous random variables to be probabilistically independent, while we don’t enforce that requirement (you may enforce it explicitly).

It’s difficult for Bayesian networks to represent SFM. To encode functional determination (right-uniqueness), the conditional distributions must be degenerate. When input distributions cannot be assumed (e.g. light switch doesn’t affect TV) and we only have specific input values, the marginal distributions are degenerate too. This sacrifices nearly all expressiveness of a Bayesian network.

Any Bayesian network can be expressed by an SFM. We’ll use
probability integral transform
(PIT) to represent conditional probability distributions with deterministic functions:

1.

For simplicity, consider real-valued (
\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
) random variables
Y,X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
and conditional distribution
\Pr[Y|X_{1}=x_{1},X_{2}=x_{2},\dots,X_{n}=x_{n}]
.

2.

Create continuous uniform random variable
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
in range
[0,1]
, independent from all
X_{i}
.

3.

Let
F_{Y|X_{i}=x_{i}}(y):\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be the conditional CDF (cumulative distribution function) of
Y
, such that it has an inverse
F_{Y|X_{i}=x_{i}}^{-1}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
.

4.

By PIT
[
Blitzstein and Hwang 2015
]
, random variable
F_{Y|X_{i}=x_{i}}^{-1}\circ U
has exactly the same CDF as
F_{Y|X_{i}=x_{i}}
.

5.

We’ve created a deterministic function
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n},U)
that returns a random variable
F_{Y|X_{i}=x_{i}}^{-1}\circ U
, given real-valued
x_{i}
and random variable
U
.

Essentially, we can enforce “all mechanisms are deterministic” without sacrificing expressiveness. The inherent randomness of a mechanism is “injected” by an unobservable “noise” parent whose value is a random variable. This practice is fairly common:

1.

To sample values from
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, the
reparameterization trick
uses deterministic function
f(\mu,\sigma,\epsilon)=\mu+\sigma\times\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
is sampled from an auxiliary “noise” distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)

[
Kingma and Welling 2013
]
.

2.

Additive noise model

Y=f_{Y}(X)+N_{Y}
has random variables
X,Y,N_{Y}
, deterministic function
f_{Y}
, and additive noise
N_{Y}\perp X

[
Peters et al. 2017
]
.

3.

Randomness in computer programs often comes from built-in random number generators, while the main program is deterministic.

8
Comparison

8.1
Symmetric Laws and Causal Eliminativism

In a
symmetric equation
of
n
variables, the values of any
n-1
variables functionally determine the value of the 1 remaining variable. Newton’s second law of motion
F=ma
, the ideal gas law
pV=nRT
, and Ohm’s law
V=IR
are symmetric laws. This differs from the non-injective asymmetry of functions.
We usually view symmetric equations as non-causal, because 1 equation is simpler than
n
functional determinations.

As a causal eliminativist,
Russell 1912
argues that causality doesn’t appear in physics and should be removed from philosophy altogether. However, we’ve shown that functions and SFM are useful.
We only consider Russell’s attack on the functional theory of causation, since we don’t agree with other definitions either.

1.

Plurality of causes: Multiple alternative causes like gunshot, arsenic, etc. can map to the same effect - the person’s death. (Some functions are non-injective.)

2.

Plurality of effects: The effect can be defined as the whole state of the world, which contains many variables. (The “cause” node has multiple descendants.)

Russell incorrectly dismisses functional asymmetry (non-injectiveness) as “illusory,” as if the plurality of effects makes both sides symmetric. But these “pluralities” aren’t the same. Non-injectiveness cannot be eliminated without changing the function itself.

SFM also addresses other eliminativist challenges on causality
[
Frisch 2022
]
. SFM-causality isn’t vague; actual causality, while not appearing in physics, is a slightly useful heuristic that can be abandoned when necessary; probabilistic extension handles inherently random mechanisms; functions are compatible with different theories of space (e.g. action at a distance) and time (Section
9.8
).

8.2
Hume, Regularity, and Problem of Induction

Hume 1896
challenges causality as follows. We say “striking a match causes it to ignite.” But empirically, we only observe constant conjunctions of events like “match struck” followed by “match igniting.” We don’t directly observe the link/connection between cause and effect. So any causal “law” is an inductive generalization from particular events, with no necessary guarantee to remain true in the future
[
Morris and Brown 2022
]
.

Hume conflates 2 distinct problems:

1.

Conceptual
: What’s the definition of causality?

Claiming “causality is just a special kind of regularity” is true but non-reductive: What is that “special kind”? All inductive models (e.g. correlations, symmetric equations) model “regular connections,” but only functional determination captures our causal intuition.

Besides relying on unspecified physical/metaphysical models (e.g. time, space, contiguity), regularity conditions like “all events of type
X
are followed by an event of type
Y
”
[
Andreas and Guenther 2021
]
cannot produce causal utterances in background condition cases (Section
5.11
), which are deterministic and fully-specified.

2.

Epistemological
: How to ensure the correctness of a causal model?

Non-probabilistic SFM (due to right-uniqueness) and symmetric laws make exceptionless claims about reality, while correlation doesn’t. Perhaps that’s why Hume attacks causality first. However, all inductive generalizations from empirical data are equally susceptible to the Problem of Induction (PoI)
[
Henderson 2022
]
. Causality isn’t somehow “more unreliable” than symmetric laws or correlations.

We formulate PoI as follows. Consider a normal world
W_{1}(t)
and a piecewise world
W_{2}(t)
.
W_{2}(t)
is exactly the same as
W_{1}(t)
for all time
t
before
t_{0}
, but is drastically different after
t_{0}
. Given a world
W
and all its information before
t_{0}
, there’s no way of distinguishing whether
W
is
W_{1}
or
W_{2}
.

By enumerating different ways of
W_{2}
being “drastically different”, such as “the world exploding after
t_{0}
” or “the gravitational constant doubling after
t_{0}
”, we can construct worlds where symmetric laws and correlations break down under PoI. Therefore, PoI isn’t an attack against causality alone. Similarly, a conceptual definition of causation won’t solve PoI.

8.3
Logic and Counterfactuals

In retrospect, “if-then” material conditionals cannot replace causality because it violates right-uniqueness: both
\{p:0,q:0\}
and
\{p:0,q:1\}
satisfy
p\Rightarrow q
. It allows vacuously true propositions like “if I don’t eat anything today, then I am a billionaire,” which feels wrong causally/counterfactually. By adding the laws-of-nature (
\mathcal{M}
) to the antecedents, we can perform rigorous deduction
\mathbf{w}=\texttt{VFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{exo})
without sacrificing causal intuitions. The underlying causal formula is
q=f(p,\dots)
instead of
p\Rightarrow q
, though functions and background conditions are often omitted in causal utterances.

Like the
but-for
test, many counterfactual definitions of causation are variations of “if
x
, then
y
; if not-
x
, then not-
y
”
[
Menzies and Beebee 2020
]
. They’re usually imperfect because they don’t have the full expressiveness of functions. For example,
Mackie 1965
’s INUS condition is equivalent to disjunctive normal form
[
Kim 1971
]
, which any Boolean function can be converted to, so it’s just a circuitous way of stating “causality is functions.”

Lewis 1973
defines counterfactual conditional “if not-
x
, then not-
y
” as “in the closest possible world with not-
x
, there’s not-
y
.” However, without defining a distance metric and an algorithm to find the closest possible world, this definition cannot even describe a deterministic, fully-specified causal system. Using actual-tweaked contrast, SFM unambiguously computes the contrastive world as
\mathbf{w}_{c}=\texttt{CFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{a},\mathbf{w}_{c|\mathcal{C}_{exo}})
.

8.4
Intervention and SCM

The definition of SCM
[
Pearl 2009a
]
relies on intervention, a causal concept, so it’s often criticized for being circular and non-reductive. We develop SFM as an equally-expressive reformulation of SCM that only relies on functions, thus eliminating circularity and providing a philosophical foundation for SCM. The generality of functions also avoids anthropocentric objections that manipulation requires human agency
[
Woodward 2016
]
.

Although SCM’s surgical intervention
do(Y=y)
is generalized by sub-SFM, we can also define it as a parent of
Y
, making intervention just a type of functional determination.

Given
\mathrm{Pa}(Y)=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n},DoY\}
,
DoY
is a
surgical intervention
on
Y
when:

1.

\mathcal{D}[DoY]=\mathcal{D}[Y]\cup\{\texttt{None}\}
.
\texttt{None}\notin\mathcal{D}[Y]
means “no intervention,” like in option types and nullable types.

2.

There exists an “ordinary mechanism” function
g:\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}[X_{i}]\to\mathcal{D}[Y]
, such that

\mathcal{F}[Y](\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(Y)})=\begin{cases}g(\mathbf{w}_{|\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\}})\textbf{ if }\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(Y)}(DoY)=\texttt{None},\\
\mathbf{w}_{|\mathrm{Pa}(Y)}(DoY)\textbf{ otherwise}\end{cases}

“Conditionally overriding an ordinary mechanism” is the key intuition behind interventions. For example, barometer reading is ordinarily determined by atmospheric pressure, but it can also be manipulated by human intervention. SFM can also express more complicated interventions, like when intervention has a failure probability or when only some intervention options are possible for humans.

9
Philosophical Applications

Many philosophical discussions take “causation” as given without mathematically defining what it is, so our functional definition of causality may help clarify some downstream concepts.

9.1
Desires for SFM Learning

Several alleged “metaphysical doctrines” about causality can now be seen as epistemological desires for learning new SFMs:

1.

The Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR): “Everything has a cause” or “anything is an effect caused by earlier events”
[
Melamed and Lin 2021
]
.

PSR desires to add parents to exo-nodes that “have no causes” in old models.

2.

The Eleatic Principle (EP): For something to “exist” in an ontology, it must be able to cause changes in other things
[
Colyvan 1998
]
.

EP desires to add descendants to sink nodes that “affect nothing” in old models.

3.

Causal Nexus (CN): “Any causal relation requires a nexus, some interface by means of which cause and effect are connected”
[
Robb et al. 2023
]
.

CN desires to insert intermediate nodes between old parent-child edges.

Strictly speaking, these desires are not satisfiable if we only allow finite acyclic SFM (Appendix
B
), but they do encourage us to learn bigger SFMs to model the world.

9.2
The Uncaused

Since exo-nodes can never appear in the “effect” part of causal utterances, we define node
u
is
uncaused
relative to
\mathcal{M}
iff
u\in\mathcal{V}_{exo}
. Being uncaused/exogenous is not a metaphysical fact, but a modeling choice we make: We don’t
want
to model
u
as being determined by a mechanism and other nodes in
\mathcal{M}
.

If
\mathcal{M}_{uni}
is the SFM of the full world, we often only use some sub-SFM
\mathcal{M}_{sub}
for specific tasks.
Because a node can be uncaused (exo-node) in one sub-SFM and caused (endo-node) in another, regarding “uncaused” as a node’s metaphysical property without specifying
\mathcal{M}_{sub}
is ill-defined. This is the source of many confusions.

For something with no causal parent anywhere, we say
u
is
strongly-uncaused
iff
u
isn’t an endo-node in any sub-SFM
\mathcal{M}_{sub}
(i.e. it’s an exo-node in
\mathcal{M}_{uni}
).

9.3
Free Will

Free will loosely describes an agent’s ability to “freely” choose between different possible actions
[
O’Connor and Franklin 2022
]
. We often face seemingly conflicting intuitions:

1.

People have free will.

2.

The world’s past and laws-of-nature functionally determine the world’s future, making people’s decisions unfree.

If we accept that something is
free
if it’s “uncaused or not deterministically caused”
[
Woodward 2016
]
, then SFM offers a mathematical definition of freedom that resolves this conflict:

1.

Node
u
is
free
relative to
\mathcal{M}
iff
u
is exogenous in
\mathcal{M}
.

2.

Node
u
is
unfree
relative to
\mathcal{M}
iff
u
is endogenous in
\mathcal{M}
.

3.

Node
u
is
strongly-free
iff
u
is exogenous in every
\mathcal{M}
of interest that contains
u
.

4.

Node
u
is
strongly-unfree
iff
u
is endogenous in every
\mathcal{M}
of interest that contains
u
.

Whether an action is free depends on the model of interest.
When actions have consequences, we want to model the utility function
Q(s,a)
for taking action
a\in A
at state
s\in S
. This makes action free relative to
Q(s,a)
, so any action with consequences is not strongly-unfree. Meanwhile, the best action
a^{*}=\pi(s,Q)=\arg_{a\in A}\max Q(s,a)
is determined/unfree relative to
\pi(s,Q)
. But for discrete
S,A
without additional assumptions, finding the best action requires computing
Q(s,a)
for all
a\in A
, so modeling
a
as a “free” input is inevitable and useful: The agent evaluates the utility of each action before taking the best action.

Besides reinforcement learning
[
Sutton and Barto 2018
]
, this best-action-selection framework also applies to minimax search
[
Russell 2010
]
and decision-making in general. Although we don’t define causality using agency like
Menzies and Price 1993
, we suggest that modeling “actions functionally determine consequences” could be an origin of human causal intuitions.

Generally, the freedom/arbitrariness/uncertainty of function inputs is closer to the universal quantifier “for all/any.” It’s not determined because we don’t model it as another function’s output; it’s not random because we cannot reasonably specify its marginal distribution and even if we do, the distribution isn’t helpful for the downstream task.

1.

The light switch is free to vary, while the light is determined.

2.

To maximize
f(x)
, we freely vary
x
and record the maximum
f(x)
.

3.

We freely change causes
\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{C}_{exo}}
and infer effects
\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{C}_{endo}}\subseteq\texttt{CFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{1|\mathcal{C}_{exo}})
.

4.

A sorting algorithm works for an arbitrary input list.

9.4
Causal Explanation

We use
explanans
to explain
explanandum
. A
causal explanation
uses causes (and underlying mechanisms/laws-of-nature) to explain effects. There are also non-causal explanations that appeal to symmetric equations, correlation, or backtracking (using effects to explain causes).

With SFM, causal explanations become a subset of Deductive-Nomological (DN) explanations, where (1) explanans contains general laws and particular conditions; (2) explanandum is entailed by explanans
[
Woodward and Ross 2021
]
.

Using
\mathbf{w}_{a|\mathcal{C}_{exo}}
to explain
\mathbf{w}_{a|\mathcal{C}_{endo}}
, we use general laws
\mathcal{M}
and particular conditions
\mathbf{w}_{c},\mathbf{w}_{a|exo}
; entailment comes from
\mathbf{w}_{a|\mathcal{C}_{endo}}\subseteq\mathbf{w}_{a}=\texttt{CFI}(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{w}_{c},\mathbf{w}_{a|\mathcal{C}_{exo}})
.
In practice,
\mathcal{M}
can be learned from empirical data (inductive); some nodes’ values can be random variables (probabilistic).

SFM solves many alleged counterexamples where DN model appears insufficient for defining explanation:

1.

In the symmetric equation involving shadow length, the Sun’s position, and flagpole height, why is shadow the explanandum? Because we prefer causal explanations over non-causal explanations and shadow should be modeled as a child node (Section
4.2
).

2.

Why do people omit irrelevant background conditions in explanations? Because we use delta compression in causal utterances (Section
5.14
,
5.12
).

The asymmetry of causal explanation comes from the asymmetry of causality, which comes from functions being right-unique and often non-injective.

9.5
Disposition

Glass is fragile because it has a
disposition
to shatter. Dispositions like fragility resemble properties of objects, but they describe possible (not necessarily actual) behaviors under certain conditions: Glass may not actually shatter
[
Choi and Fara 2021
]
. We analyze dispositions with functions.

For a deterministic and fully-specified example, minerals higher on Mohs hardness scale (e.g. diamond) will scratch softer minerals (e.g. talc). Let function
f(m_{1},m_{2})
take in 2 minerals and return the mineral that gets scratched, so
\texttt{Talc}=f(\texttt{Diamond},\texttt{Talc})
. Talc has the disposition to be scratched because
\forall m:\texttt{Talc}=f(m,\texttt{Talc})
; diamond has the “power” to scratch because
\forall m:m=f(m,\texttt{Diamond})
.

Therefore, dispositions are properties of a downstream function
f
, but people colloquially associate them with input nodes (scratch-hardness of minerals) or input values (scratch-hardness of diamond).

9.6
Supervenience

“
Y

supervenes
on
X
” is equivalent to “
Y
functionally depends on
X
,” because the formal definition of supervenience (“there cannot be an
Y
-difference without a
X
-difference”
[
McLaughlin and Bennett 2021
]
) is the same as right-uniqueness: Consider team
R
,

\displaystyle\ \quad\neg([(x,y_{1})\in R]\land[(x,y_{2})\in R]\land[y_{1}\neq y_{2}])

\displaystyle=\neg([(x,y_{1})\in R]\land[(x,y_{2})\in R])\lor\neg[y_{1}\neq y_{2}]

\displaystyle=\neg([(x,y_{1})\in R]\land[(x,y_{2})\in R])\lor[y_{1}=y_{2}]

\displaystyle=([(x,y_{1})\in R]\land[(x,y_{2})\in R])\Rightarrow[y_{1}=y_{2}]

9.7
Mental Causation

Can mental kinds (property, state, event) cause physical kinds? Mental causation faces 2 conflicting intuitions:

1.

It’s common in everyday experiences: I want to raise my hand (mental state), so I raise my hand (body state).

2.

The
Exclusion Problem
: Physical effects like body movements are already determined by physical causes like brain activities, so there’s no room for a mental cause, which is also sufficient for the physical effect
[
Robb et al. 2023
]
.

The Exclusion Problem arises whenever a functional determination entailed by
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{F})
cannot be deduced from the graph structure
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
(and Armstrong’s Axioms) alone. People feel uneasy because they cannot find such dependency as a path in
\mathcal{G}
.

1.

(Assumption) On the lowest physical level, brain state functionally determines body state:
\texttt{BrainState}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}\texttt{BodyState}
.

2.

(Assumption) Mental state supervenes on brain state:
\texttt{BrainState}\xrightarrow{f_{2}}\texttt{MentalState}
. Mental state is an abstract/aggregate description of physical brain state.

Multiple realizability
(a single mental kind can be realized by many distinct physical kinds)
[
Bickle 2020
]
is true when
f_{2}
is non-injective.

It’s a coincidence that
functionalism
(name unrelated to mathematical functions) uses causality to define mental states
[
Levin 2023
]
and we reduce causality to functions.

3.

So we have an SFM
\mathcal{M}
with graph
\texttt{BodyState}\leftarrow\texttt{BrainState}\to\texttt{MentalState}
and functions
\mathcal{F}=\{\texttt{BodyState}:f_{1},\texttt{MentalState}:f_{2}\}
.

4.

(Fact) There exists a function
f_{3}
such that
\texttt{MentalState}\xrightarrow{f_{3}}\texttt{BodyState}
is true in every
\mathbf{w}\in R_{\mathcal{M}}
. Mental state does functionally determine body state.

\texttt{MentalState}\xrightarrow{f_{3}}\texttt{BodyState}
cannot be deduced from
\mathcal{G}
alone. It’s entailed by the specific functional mappings
f_{1},f_{2}
(and
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})
). Although it cannot appear as a path in
\mathcal{G}
, we see no reason to dismiss it as “excluded.” We may use SFM-intersection to explicitly/graphically encode this functional dependency, although
R_{\mathcal{M}}
is entailed by a single SFM
\mathcal{M}
(SFM-intersection-proper isn’t required).

The Exclusion Problem appears in any system with hierarchical levels of abstraction, since supervenience is just functional dependency. In fully-specified and deterministic computers, what causes a video to play on screen, the low-level chip activities or the high-level video-player program? The same reasoning applies. The only empirical question is whether higher-level functional determinations like
f_{3}
are true. If not, we simply say the abstraction is broken.

9.8
Time

SFM doesn’t endorse any particular theory of time, but we can define
T:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
that maps each node
u
to a real-valued timestamp
T(u)
. If
\forall(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}:T(u)\leq T(v)
, then causes always temporally precede their effects. But without additional assumptions,
T
might as well violate this condition.

Backward causation
occurs when an effect temporally precedes its cause
[
Faye 2021
]
. If most SFM edges
(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}
still point from past to future (
T(u)\leq T(v)
), a backward edge can create cycles, resulting in PULO or actually unsatisfiable laws. That’s why people intuitively dislike backward causation. But if the specific SFM is satisfiable or satisfied by empirical data, we cannot dismiss it
a priori
.

Are there fundamental properties of our physical world that make causal and temporal orders agree? Could it be the asymmetry of thermodynamics, radiation
[
Frisch 2022
]
, or our mental habit of “actions determining consequences” (Section
9.3
)? Further research is required.

In a causal feedback loop
A\to A
, node
A
influences its own next state. With discrete time, we can unroll it to an acyclic time-indexed causal chain
A(0)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{F}[A(1)]}A(1)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{F}[A(2)]}A(2)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{F}[A(3)]}\dots
, which may be countably infinite. When every
\mathcal{F}[A(t)]
is invertible, the system has time-symmetry and another equivalent SFM
A(0)\xleftarrow{\mathcal{F}[A(1)]^{-1}}A(1)\xleftarrow{\mathcal{F}[A(2)]^{-1}}A(2)\xleftarrow{\mathcal{F}[A(3)]^{-1}}\dots
.

Decreasing the interval between 2 consecutive timestamps towards the infinitesimal, we eventually get an uncountable number of nodes and cannot properly define an edge, because there are no 2 “consecutive” real numbers. In this case, it would make more sense for
A(t)
to determine its instantaneous rate of change
\frac{d}{dt}A(t)
, like the exponential/logistic growth rate of bacteria population size and the predator-prey dynamics in Lotka-Volterra equations. SFM-intersection-proper can represent autonomous differential equations, which include causal loop diagrams
[
Haraldsson 2004
]
. However, differential equation in general are better tools for modeling continuous-time causality.

10
Conclusion

After our conceptual analysis that reduces causality to functions, there should be nothing mysterious about the definition of causality.
Using forward inference, contrast, and delta compression, Structural Functional Model (SFM) correctly produces intuitive causal utterances.
We’ve also supported intuitive practices from an algorithmic perspective: contrast saves space and time; finite acyclic SFM is required for guaranteed satisfiability (at the cost of expressiveness).
Distinct from but compatible with probability theory, “causality as functions” allows for interesting downstream applications.

Appendix B
Generalized Münchhaussen Trilemma

We formalize a theorem that generalizes Münchhaussen Trilemma
[
Weisberg 2021
]
in epistemology:

Theorem B.1

(
Generalized Münchhaussen Trilemma (GMT)
)
.

Any directed graph
G=(V,E)
contains at least one of the following:

•

A root.

•

A cycle.

•

An infinite regress: An infinite path of distinct nodes
(\dots,u_{2},u_{1},u_{0})
ending at
u_{0}
, such that for any integer
i\geq 1
, there exists
u_{i}\in V
satisfying
(u_{i},u_{i-1})\in E
and
u_{i}\notin\{u_{j}\}_{j=0}^{i-1}

Proof.

Proving by contradiction, suppose instead that a graph has no root, no cycle, and no infinite regress. Since there’s no infinite regress, there exists a nonnegative integer
n
that is the maximum length of a path of distinct nodes ending at some
u\in V
. Let
(u_{n},u_{n-1},\dots,u_{1},u_{0})
be that maximum-length path.

Because
G
doesn’t have a root,
\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u)\geq 1
for all
u\in V
and thus
\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u_{n})\geq 1
, so there exists a node
v\in V
such that
(v,u_{n})\in E
is an edge.

If
v\in\{u_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
, then
v=u_{i}
for some integer
0\leq i\leq n
. We can construct a new path
(u_{n},u_{n-1},\dots,u_{i},u_{n})
. It’s a path because
(u_{i},u_{n})=(v,u_{n})\in E
and
(u_{i},u_{i-1})\in E
for all
1\leq i\leq n
; it’s a cycle because it starts and ends at
u_{n}
. This violates the acyclic assumption, so
v\notin\{u_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
is distinct from all nodes in the path.

We can thus construct a new path
(v,u_{n},u_{n-1},\dots,u_{1},u_{0})
of length
n+1
, where all nodes have been shown to be distinct. However, this contradicts the condition that the maximum length of distinct-node paths is
n
. Therefore, it’s impossible for a directed graph to have no root, no cycle, and no infinite regress at the same time.
∎

GMT is a general theorem about directed graphs, proven mathematically. It applies to all problems characterized by objects and directed binary relations between them (i.e. describable by a directed graph), such as “
X
causes
Y
” and “
X
justifies
Y
