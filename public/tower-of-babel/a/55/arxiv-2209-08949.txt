Polynomials represented by norm forms via the beta sieve


Abstract
. A central question in Arithmetic geometry is to determine for which polynomials f ∈ ℤ ⁡ [ t ] f\in\mathbb{Z}[t] and which number fields K K the Hasse principle holds for the affine equation f ⁡ ( t ) = 𝐍 K / ℚ ​ ( 𝐱 ) ≠ 0 f(t)=\mathbf{N}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{x})\neq 0 . Whilst extensively studied in the literature, current results are largely limited to polynomials and number fields of low degree. In this paper, we establish the Hasse principle for a wide family of polynomials and number fields, including polynomials that are products of arbitrarily many linear, quadratic or cubic factors. The proof generalises an argument of Irving [ 27 ] , which makes use of the beta sieve of Rosser and Iwaniec. As a further application of our sieve results, we prove new cases of a conjecture of Harpaz and Wittenberg on locally split values of polynomials over number fields, and discuss consequences for rational points in fibrations.

1.
Introduction

Let
K
be a number field of degree
n
, and let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
be a polynomial. A central problem in Arithmetic geometry is to determine under what conditions
f
can take values equal to a norm of an element of
K
. In order to address this question, we take an integral basis
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}
for
K
, viewed as a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
, and define the
norm form
as
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{x})=N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\omega_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\omega_{n}x_{n})
, where
N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\cdot)
is the field norm. We then seek to understand when the equation

(1.1)

f(t)=\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{x})\neq 0

has a solution with
(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
. A necessary condition for solubility over
\mathbb{Q}
is that
(\ref{object of study})
must have solutions in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{n+1}
for every prime
p
. We say that the
Hasse principle
holds if this condition alone is sufficient to guarantee existence of a solution to (
1.1
) over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Local to global questions for (
1.1
) have received much attention over the years. The first case to consider is when
f
is a non-zero constant polynomial. Here, the Hasse principle for (
1.1
) is known as the
Hasse norm principle
. More precisely, we say that the
Hasse norm principle
holds for the extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
if
\mathbb{Q}^{\times}\cap N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(I_{K})=N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(K^{\times})
, where
I_{K}=\mathbb{A}_{K}^{\times}
is the group of ideles of
K
. The Hasse norm principle has been extensively studied, beginning with the work of Hasse himself, who established that it holds for cyclic extensions
K/\mathbb{Q}
(a result known as the
Hasse norm theorem
), but does not hold for certain biquadratic extensions, such as
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{13},\sqrt{-3})
. The Hasse norm principle is also known to hold if the degree of
K
is prime (Bartels
[
1
]
), or if the normal closure of
K
has Galois group
S_{n}
(Kunyavskiĭ and Voskresenskiĭ
[
32
]
) or
A_{n}
for
n\neq 4
(Macedo
[
35
]
).

When
[K:\mathbb{Q}]=2
and
f
is irreducible of degree
3
or
4
, (
1.1
) defines a Châtelet surface. There are now many known counterexamples to the Hasse principle for Châtelet surfaces, including one by Iskovskikh
[
28
]
, which we discuss in more detail in Example
5.4
. However, Colliot-Thélène, Sansuc and Swinnerton-Dyer
[
14
]
prove that the Brauer–Manin obstruction accounts for all failures of the Hasse principle. A similar result holds when
f
is an irreducible polynomial of degree at most
3
and
[K:\mathbb{Q}]=3
, as proved by Colliot-Thélène and Salberger
[
8
]
. Both of these results make use of fibration and descent methods.

In the case when
f
is an irreducible quadratic and
K
is a quartic extension containing a root of
f
, the Hasse principle and weak approximation are known to hold for (
1.1
) thanks to the work of Browning and Heath-Brown
[
4
]
. This result was generalised by Derenthal, Smeets and Wei
[
16
, Theorem 2]
, to prove that the Brauer–Manin obstruction is the only obstruction to the Hasse principle and weak approximation for irreducible quadratics
f
and arbitrary number fields
K
. Moreover, in
[
16
, Theorem 4]
they give an explicit description of the Brauer groups that can be obtained in this family.

Results when
f
is not irreducible have so far been limited to products of linear polynomials. Suppose that
f
takes the form

(1.2)

f(t)=c\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t-e_{i})^{m_{i}},

for some
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{*},e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
.
When
r=1
, the Brauer–Manin obstruction is the only obstruction to the Hasse principle and weak approximation for any smooth projective model of (
1.1
). This is a special case of the work of Colliot-Thélène and Sansuc
[
9
]
on principal homogeneous spaces under algebraic tori. Heath-Brown and Skorobogatov
[
26
]
treat the case
r=2
by combining descent methods with the Hardy–Littlewood circle method, under the assumption that
\gcd(m_{1},m_{2},\deg K)=1
. This assumption was later removed by Colliot-Thélène, Harari and Skorobogatov
[
13
]
. Thanks to the work of Browning and Matthiesen
[
5
]
, it is now settled that for any number field
K
and and polynomial
f
of the form (
1.2
) (for arbitrary
r\geqslant 1
), the Brauer–Manin obstruction is the only obstruction to the Hasse principle and weak approximation for any smooth projective model of (
1.1
). Their result is inspired by additive combinatorics results of Green, Tao and Ziegler
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
, combined with “vertical" torsors introduced by Schindler and Skorobogatov
[
40
]
.

In general, it has been conjectured by Colliot-Thélène
[
7
]
that all failures of the Hasse principle for any smooth projective model of (
1.1
) are explained by the Brauer–Manin obstruction. Assuming Schinzel’s hypothesis, this holds true for
f
an arbitrary polynomial and
K/\mathbb{Q}
a cyclic extension, as demonstrated by work of Colliot-Thélène and Swinnerton-Dyer on pencils of Severi–Brauer varieties
[
12
]
. Recently, Skorobogatov and Sofos also establish unconditionally that when
K/\mathbb{Q}
is cyclic, (
1.1
) satisfies the Hasse principle for a positive proportion of polynomials
f
of degree
d
, when their coefficients are ordered by height
[
42
, Theorem 1.3]
.

In
[
27
]
, Irving introduces an entirely new approach to studying the Hasse principle for (
1.1
), which rests on sieve methods. Irving’s main result
[
27
, Theorem 1.1]
states that if
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
is an irreducible cubic, then the Hasse principle holds for (
1.1
) under the following assumptions:

(1)

K
satisfies the Hasse norm principle.

(2)

There exists a prime
q\geqslant 7
, and a finite set of primes
S
, such that for all
p\notin S
, either
p\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)
or the inertia degrees of
p
in
K/\mathbb{Q}
are coprime.

(3)

The number field generated by
f
is not contained in the cyclotomic field
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{q})
.

An example provided by Irving in
[
27
]
is the number field
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
, where
\alpha
is a root of
x^{q}-2
and
q\geqslant 7
is prime. We shall comment on this further in Example
5.9
.

In this paper, we generalize Irving’s arguments to establish the Hasse principle for a wide new family of polynomials and number fields. Our results cover for the first time polynomials of arbitrarily large degree which are not a product of linear factors. In fact, under suitable assumptions on
K
, we can deal with polynomials that are products of arbitrarily many linear, quadratic and cubic factors.

Throughout this paper, we let
\widehat{K}
denote the Galois closure of
K
, and we let
G={\rm Gal}(\widehat{K}/\mathbb{Q})
, viewed as a permutation group on
n
letters. We define

(1.3)

T(G)=\frac{1}{\#G}\#\{\sigma\in G:\textrm{ the cycle lengths of }\sigma\textrm{ are not coprime}\}.

We now state our main results.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K
be a number field satisfying the Hasse norm principle. Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
be a polynomial, all of whose irreducible factors have degree at most 2. Let
k
denote the number of distinct irreducible factors of
f
, and let
j
denote the number of distinct irreducible quadratic factors of
f
which generate a quadratic field contained in
\widehat{K}
. Suppose that
T(G)\leqslant\frac{0.39000}{k+j+1}
. Then the Hasse principle holds for (
1.1
).

In practice, the constant
0.39000
can be improved slightly, particularly when the majority of the factors of
f
are linear, although it will always be less than
1/2
. The precise optimal constant is obtained by finding the maximal value of
\kappa
such that (
4.55
) holds.

When
G=S_{n}
, we shall see in Lemma
5.10
that
T(S_{n})\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, so in the setting of Theorem
1.1
we can establish the Hasse principle provided
n
is sufficiently large in terms of the degree of
f
. We illustrate this by treating the case when
f
is a product of two irreducible quadratics.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
be a product of two quadratic polynomials such that the number field
L
generated by
f
is a biquadratic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
K
be a number field of degree
n
with
G=S_{n}
. Suppose that
L\cap\widehat{K}=\mathbb{Q}
. Then the Hasse principle holds for (
1.1
), provided that

n\not\in\{2,3,\ldots,10,12,14,15,16,18,20,22,24,26,28,30,36,42,48\}.

We remark that without the assumption
L\cap\widehat{K}=\mathbb{Q}
, a similar result to Corollary
1.2
still holds, although a larger list of degrees
n
would need to be excluded. For example, if
L\cap\widehat{K}
is quadratic, then the Hasse principle holds for (
1.1
) for all primes
n\geqslant 11
and all integers
n>90
, whilst if
L\cap\widehat{K}=L
, then the Hasse prinicple holds for all primes
n\geqslant 13
and all integers
n>150
.

We cannot hope to deal with all small values of
n
in Corollary
1.2
. For example, the work of Iskovskikh
[
28
]
shows that the Hasse principle can fail when
n=2
(see Example
5.4
). However, as we shall discuss in Appendix
A
, in the case
n\geqslant 3
, there is no Brauer–Manin obstruction to the Hasse principle, and so according to the conjecture of Colliot-Thélène mentioned above we should expect the Hasse principle to hold.

Our second main result allows
f
to contain irreducible cubic factors, but requires more restrictive assumptions on the number field
K
, more similar to Irving’s setup in
[
27
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
be a polynomial, all of whose irreducible factors have degree at most
3
. Then the Hasse principle holds for (
1.1
) under the following assumptions for
K
.

(1)

K
satisfies the Hasse norm principle.

(2)

The set
\mathscr{P}
of primes
p
for which the inertia degrees of
p
in
K/\mathbb{Q}
are not coprime satisfies Assumption
2.2
.

As an example, Assumption
2.2
is satisfied if there exists a prime
q
such that
\frac{\deg f+1}{q-1}\leqslant 0.32380
, and such that for all but finitely many primes
p\not\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)
, the inertia degrees of
p
in
K/\mathbb{Q}
are coprime. The constant
0.32380
appearing in Assumption
2.2
could likely be improved with more work, and in specific examples, the required bounds could be computed more precisely using (
4.59
). We remark that we have also dropped the assumption made in
[
27
]
that the number field generated by
f
is not contained in
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{q})
. This assumption is not essential to Irving’s argument, but allows for the treatment of smaller values of
q
. Reinserting this assumption and optimising (
4.59
), we could recover Irving’s result from our work.

We prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
by applying the beta sieve of Rosser and Iwaniec
[
18
, Theorem 11.13]
. The main sieve results we obtain are stated in Theorems
2.1
and
2.3
. These results in fact prove the existence of a solution to (
1.1
) with
t
arbitrarily close to a given adelic solution. Consequently, the above results could be extended to prove weak approximation for (
1.1
), provided that weak approximation holds for the norm one torus
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{x})=1
. For example, the work of Kunyavskiĭ and Voskresenskiĭ
[
32
]
and Macedo
[
35
]
demonstrates that weak approximation for the norm one torus holds when
G=S_{n}
or
G=A_{n}
and
n\neq 4
, and so weak approximation holds in the setting of Corollary
1.2
.

In Section
6
, we find a second application of Theorem
2.1
to a conjecture of Harpaz and Wittenberg
[
23
, Conjecture 9.1]
, which we restate in Conjecture
6.1
and henceforth refer to as the
Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture
. The conjecture concerns a collection of number field extensions
L_{i}/k_{i}/k
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, where
k_{i}\cong k[t]/(P_{i}(t))
for monic irreducible polynomials
P_{i}\in k[t]
. Roughly speaking, the conjecture predicts, under certain hypotheses, the existence of an element
t_{0}\in k
such that
P_{1}(t_{0}),\ldots,P_{n}(t_{0})
are locally split, i.e., each place in
k_{i}
dividing
P_{i}(t_{0})
, has a degree
1
place of
L_{i}
above it.

A major motivation for the conjecture is the development of the theory of rational points in fibrations. Given a fibration
\pi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}
, a natural question is to what extent we can deduce arithmetic information about
X
from arithmetic information about the fibres of
\pi
. A famous conjecture of Colliot-Thélène
[
7
, p.174]
predicts that for any smooth, proper, geometrically irreducible, rationally connected variety
X
over a number field
k
, the rational points
X(k)
are dense in the Brauer–Manin set
X(\mathbb{A}_{k})^{\operatorname{Br}}
. (In other words, the Brauer–Manin obstruction is the only obstruction to weak approximation.) Applied to this conjecture, the above question becomes whether density of
X(k)
in
X(\mathbb{A}_{k})^{\operatorname{Br}}
follows from density of
X_{c}(k)
in
X_{c}(\mathbb{A}_{k})^{\operatorname{Br}}
for a general fibre
X_{c}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\pi^{-1}(c)
of
\pi
(see
[
24
, Question 1.2]
). Applications of the Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture to this question are studied in
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
.

Harpaz and Wittenberg
[
23
, Section 9.2]
demonstrate that their conjecture follows from the homogeneous version of Schinzel’s hypothesis (commonly reffered to as
(\textrm{HH}_{1})
) in the case of abelian extensions
L_{i}/k_{i}
, or more generally, almost abelian extensions (see
[
23
, Definition 9.4]
). Examples of almost abelian extensions include cubic extensions, and extensions of the form
k(c^{1/p})/k
for
c\in k
and
p
prime. The work of Heath-Brown and Moroz
[
25
]
establishes
(\textrm{HH}_{1})
for primes represented by binary cubic forms, from which the Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture can be deduced in the case
k=\mathbb{Q},n=1
and
\deg P_{1}=3
. Using a geometric reformulation of
[
23
, Conjecture 9.1]
, the authors establish their conjecture in low degree cases, namely when
\sum_{i=1}^{n}[k_{i}:k]\leqslant 2
or
\sum_{i=1}^{n}[k_{i}:k]=3
and
[L_{i}:k_{i}]=2
for all
i
.

The Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture is related to the study of polynomials represented by norm forms. As a consequence of the work of Matthiesen
[
36
]
on norms as products of linear polynomials, the Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture holds in the case
k_{1}=\cdots=k_{n}=k=\mathbb{Q}

[
23
, Theorem 9.14]
. Similarly, we can deduce from
[
27
, Theorem 1.1]
that the Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture holds in the case
n=2,k=\mathbb{Q},k_{1}=K,k_{2}=\mathbb{Q},L_{1}=K(2^{1/q})
and
L_{2}=\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/q})
, where
q\geqslant 7
is a prime such that
K\not\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{q})

[
23
, Theorem 9.15]
.

Besides the work of Matthiesen
[
36
]
for
k_{1}=\cdots=k_{n}=k=\mathbb{Q}
, the aforementioned results apply only to the case
n\leqslant 2
. In Section
6
, we prove the following theorem, which establishes the Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture in a new family of extensions
k_{1}/\mathbb{Q},\ldots,k_{n}/\mathbb{Q}
, where
n
may be arbitrarily large, and each extension
k_{i}/\mathbb{Q}
may have degree up to
3
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
. Let
k=\mathbb{Q}
, and for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
k_{i},M_{i}
be linearly disjoint number fields over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
L_{i}=M_{i}k_{i}
be the compositum of
k_{i}
and
M_{i}
. Define

(1.4)

T_{i}=\frac{1}{\#{\rm Gal}(\widehat{M_{i}}/\mathbb{Q})}\#\{\sigma\in{\rm Gal}(\widehat{M}_{i}/\mathbb{Q}):\sigma\textrm{ has no fixed point}\}.

Let
d=\sum_{i=1}^{n}[k_{i}:\mathbb{Q}]
. Then the Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture holds in the following cases.

(1)

[k_{i}:\mathbb{Q}]\leqslant 2
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}\leqslant 0.39000/d
.

(2)

[k_{i}:\mathbb{Q}]\leqslant 3
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and there exist primes
q_{i}
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}1/(q_{i}-1)\leqslant 0.32380/d
, and integers
t_{i}
coprime to
q_{i}
, such that for all but finitely many primes
p\not\equiv t_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q_{i})
, there is a place of degree
1
in
M_{i}
above
p
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
be distinct primes, and let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
g_{i}(x)=x^{q_{i}}-r_{i}
is irreducible for all
i
. Let
M_{i}=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(g_{i})
and let
k_{i},L_{i}
and
d
be as in Theorem
1.4
. Suppose that one of the following holds:

(1)

[k_{i}:\mathbb{Q}]\leqslant 2
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}1/q_{i}\leqslant 0.39000/d
,

(2)

[k_{i}:\mathbb{Q}]\leqslant 3
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}1/(q_{i}-1)\leqslant 0.32380/d
.

Then the Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture holds for
k=\mathbb{Q}
and for such choices of
k_{i}
and
L_{i}
.

We remark that when applied to the setting of
[
23
, Theorem 9.15]
, the above result requires a stronger bound on
q
. However, with a more careful optimisation of (
4.58
), it should be possible to recover
[
23
, Theorem 9.15]
from our approach.

By combining Theorem
1.4
with
[
23
, Theorem 9.17]
(with the choice
B=0,M^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
and
M^{\prime}=\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}\backslash U
), we obtain the following result about rational points in fibrations.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
X
be a smooth, proper, geometrically irreducible variety over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\pi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a dominant morphism whose general fibre is rationally connected. Let
k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}
denote the residue fields of the closed points of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}
above which
\pi
has nonsplit fibres, and let
L_{i}/k_{i}
be finite extensions which split these nonsplit fibres. Assume that

(1)

The smooth fibres of
\pi
satisfy the Hasse principle and weak approximation.

(2)

The hypotheses of Theorem
1.4
hold.

Then
X(\mathbb{Q})
is dense in
X(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{\Br(X)}
.

It would be interesting to investigate whether Condition
(1)
in Theorem
1.6
could be relaxed to the assumption that the smooth fibres
X_{c}(\mathbb{Q})
are dense in
X_{c}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})^{\Br(X_{c})}
, as in the setting of
[
24
, Question 1.2]
discussed above. This would require an extension of Theorem
1.4
to cover a stronger version of the Harpaz–Wittenberg conjecture, involving strong approximation of an auxiliary variety
W
off a finite set of places
[
24
, Proposition 6.1]
. Strong approximation of
W
was studied by Browning and Schindler
[
6
]
for example, who established
[
24
, Question 1.2]
in the case when the rank of
\pi
is at most
3
, and at least one of its nonsplit fibres lies above a rational of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

2.
Main sieve results for binary forms

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
be a non-constant binary form with nonzero discriminant. We write
f(x,y)
as a product of distinct irreducible factors

(2.1)

f(x,y)=\prod_{i=0}^{m}f_{i}(x,y)\prod_{i=m+1}^{k}f_{i}(x,y),

where
f_{i}(x,y)
are linear forms for
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
, and forms of degree
k_{i}\geqslant 2
for
m+1\leqslant i\leqslant k
. If
y\mid f(x,y)
, then we define
f_{0}(x,y)=y
, and otherwise we let
f_{0}(x,y)=1
. Hence we have
y\nmid f_{i}(x,y)
for all
i\geqslant 1
.

For
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, we define

(2.2)

\displaystyle\nu_{i}(p)

\displaystyle=\#\{[x:y]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p}):f_{i}(x,y)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)\},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\nu(p)

\displaystyle=\#\{[x:y]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p}):f(x,y)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)\}.

Let
\mathscr{P}
be a set of primes, and let
\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}=\{p\in\mathscr{P}:p\leqslant x\}
. We denote by
\pi(x)
the number of primes less than
x
. For all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, we need to assume
\mathscr{P}
has the following properties, for some
\alpha,\theta_{i}>0
and any
A\geqslant 1
:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}1

\displaystyle=\alpha\pi(x)\left(1+O_{A}\left((\log x)^{-A}\right)\right),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}\nu_{i}(p)

\displaystyle=\alpha\theta_{i}\pi(x)\left(1+O_{A}\left((\log x)^{-A}\right)\right).

The reason we require explicit error terms in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) is so that the
sieve dimensions
, introduced in Section
4.2
, exist. We note that for
i=0
, we have
\theta_{0}=1
if
f_{0}(x,y)=y
, and
\theta_{0}=0
if
f_{0}(x,y)=1
. Additionally, from (
2.5
), it follows that

(2.6)

\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}\nu(p)=\alpha\theta\pi(x)\left(1+O_{A}\left((\log x)^{-A}\right)\right),

where
\theta=\theta_{0}+\cdots+\theta_{k}
.

Let
\mathscr{B}\subseteq[-1,1]^{2}
denote the compact region enclosed by a piecewise continuous simple closed curve of finite length. The perimeter of
\mathscr{B}
will always be assumed to be bounded by some absolute constant
C
. In the applications in Sections
5
and
6
, we shall make the choice

(2.7)

\mathscr{B}=\left\{(x,y)\in(0,1]^{2}:\left|\frac{x}{y}-r\right|<\xi\right\},

for a fixed real number
r>0
and a small parameter
\xi>0
, and so we may choose
C=4
, for example. We also define
\mathscr{B}N=\{(Nx,Ny):(x,y)\in\mathscr{B}\}.

Let
\Delta
be an integer and let
a_{0},b_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}/\Delta\mathbb{Z}
. We now state the main sieve results which will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.3
. They concern the sifting function

(2.8)

S(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{B},N)=\#\left\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{B}N\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\begin{aligned} &a\equiv a_{0},b\equiv b_{0}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ \Delta)\\
&p\mid f(a,b)\implies p\notin\mathscr{P}\end{aligned}\right\}.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f(x,y)
be a binary form consisting of distinct irreducible factors, all of degree at most
2
. Then there exists a finite set of primes
S_{0}
, depending on
f
, such that the following holds:

Let
S
be a finite set of primes containing
S_{0}
. Let
\Delta
be an integer with only prime factors in
S
, and let
a_{0},b_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}/\Delta\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\mathscr{P}
be a set of primes disjoint from
S
and satisfying (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) for some
\alpha,\theta_{i}>0
. Assume that
\alpha\theta\leqslant 0.39000
. Then
S(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{B},N)>0
for
N
sufficiently large.

We also have a similar sieve result when
f
may contain irreducible factors of degree up to
3
, but with a less general sifting set
\mathscr{P}
, satisfying the following assumption.

Assumption 2.2
.

There exists a positive integer
n
, a finite set of primes
S
, primes
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
, and integers
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
with
q_{j}\nmid t_{j}
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, such that

(2.9)

\mathscr{P}\backslash S\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\{p\equiv t_{j}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q_{j})\}

and

\deg f\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{q_{j}-1}\leqslant 0.32380.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f(x,y)
be a binary form consisting of distinct irreducible factors, all of degree at most
3
. Then there exists a finite set of primes
S_{0}
, depending on
f
, such that the following holds:

Let
S
be a finite set of primes containing
S_{0}
. Let
\Delta
be an integer with only prime factors in
S
, and let
a_{0},b_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}/\Delta\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\mathscr{P}
be a set of primes satisfying Assumption

. Then
S(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{B},N)>0
for

sufficiently large.

For brevity, in the remainder of the paper we shall denote the condition
a\equiv a_{0}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ \Delta),b\equiv b_{0}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ \Delta)
by
C(a,b)
.

3.
Levels of distribution

Crucial to the success of the beta sieve in proving Theorems
2.1
and
2.3
is a good level of distribution result, which provides an approximation of the quantities

\#\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{B}N\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:p\mid f_{i}(a,b),d\mid f(a,b)\}

by multiplicative functions, at least on average over
p
and
d
. (Here, and throughout this section, we keep the notation from Section
2
.) In this section, we provide such an estimate, following similar arguments developed by Daniel
[
15
, Lemma 3.3]
. We slightly generalise the setup as follows:

Let
g_{1},g_{2}
be binary forms with nonzero discriminants. Throughout this section, we fix
S,\Delta
, and
C(a,b)
, and assume that
S
contains all primes dividing the discriminants of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. We allow all implied constants to depend only the degrees of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
and a small positive constant
\epsilon
, which for convenience we allow to take different values at different points in the argument.

Let
\mathscr{R}
be the compact region of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
enclosed by a piecewise continuous simple closed curve of finite length. We denote by
\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{R})
and
P(\mathscr{R})
the volume and perimeter of
\mathscr{R}
respectively. Let

(3.1)

\displaystyle A(d_{1},d_{2})

\displaystyle=\#\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:C(a,b),d_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b),d_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b)\},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\varrho(d_{1},d_{2})

\displaystyle=\#\{(a,b)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d_{1}d_{2}):d_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b),d_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b)\}.

We define

(3.3)

r(d_{1},d_{2})=A(d_{1},d_{2})-\frac{\varrho(d_{1},d_{2})\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{R})}{d^{2}\Delta^{2}}.

In what follows, we let
d=d_{1}d_{2}
, and we assume that
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\gcd(d,\Delta)=1
. The main aim of this section is to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
g_{1}
does not contain any linear factors. Then for any
D_{1},D_{2}>0
and any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\gcd(d,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\sup_{P(\mathscr{R})\leqslant N}\left|r(d_{1},d_{2})\right|\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{\epsilon}(D_{1}D_{2}+N(D_{1}D_{2})^{1/2}+ND_{2}).

As a corollary, we obtain the following level of distribution result.

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose that
g_{1}
does not contain any linear factors. Let
\mathscr{B}\subseteq[-1,1]^{2}
be as in Section
2
, and let
\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{B}N
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for any
D_{1},D_{2}>0
with
D_{2}\ll N^{1-\epsilon}
and
D_{1}D_{2}\ll N^{2-\epsilon}
, we have

(3.4)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\gcd(d,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\left|A(d_{1},d_{2})-\frac{N^{2}\varrho(d_{1},d_{2})\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{B})}{d^{2}\Delta^{2}}\right|\\
\ll N^{2-\delta}.

Proposition
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
are generalisations of Irving’s results from
[
27
, Section 3]
, which can be recovered by taking
g_{1}(x,y)=f(x,y)
to be the cubic form Irving considered, and
g_{2}(x,y)=yf(x,y)
. The method of proof is inspired by the pioneering work of Daniel on the divisor-sum problem for binary forms, which requires a similar level of distribution result (see
[
15
, Lemma 3.3]
). Daniel’s argument is more delicate, keeping track of powers of
\log N
in place of factors of
N^{\epsilon}
, and Corollary
3.2
could be similarly refined, but this yields no advantage for our applications.

Before proceeding with the proof of Proposition
3.1
, we recall the following standard lattice point counting result.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a full-rank lattice, and let
\mathscr{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be as defined before Proposition
3.1
. Then

\#(\mathscr{R}\cap\Lambda)=\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{R})}{\det\Lambda}+O\left(\frac{P(\mathscr{R})}{\lambda_{1}}+1\right),

where
\lambda_{1}
is the length of a shortest nonzero vector in
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a fundamental domain of
\Lambda
. The translates
v+\mathscr{F}
for
v\in\Lambda
tile
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Define sets

S^{-}=\{v\in\Lambda:(v+\mathscr{F})\subseteq\mathscr{R}\},\qquad S^{+}=\{v\in\Lambda:(v+\mathscr{F})\cap\mathscr{R}\neq\emptyset\}.

Then

\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(S^{-}+\Lambda)}{\det(\Lambda)}=\#S^{-}\leqslant\#(\mathscr{R}\cap\Lambda)\leqslant\#S^{+}=\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(S^{+}+\Lambda)}{\det(\Lambda)}.

Moreover,
S^{-}+\Lambda\subseteq\mathscr{R}\subseteq S^{+}+\Lambda
, so
\operatorname{Vol}(S^{-}+\Lambda)\leqslant\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{R})\leqslant\operatorname{Vol}(S^{+}+\Lambda)
. Therefore,

\left|\#(\mathscr{R}\cap\Lambda)-\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{R})}{\det(\Lambda)}\right|\leqslant\#S^{+}-\#S^{-}.

However,
S^{+}-S^{-}=\{v\in\Lambda:(v+\mathscr{F})\cap\partial\mathscr{R}\neq\emptyset\},

where
\partial\mathscr{R}
denotes the boundary of
\mathscr{R}
. Each segment of
\partial\mathscr{R}
of length
\lambda_{1}
can intersect at most four translates of
\mathscr{F}
. Therefore,
S^{+}-S^{-}\ll P(\mathscr{R})/\lambda_{1}+1
, as required.
∎

We now commence with the proof of Proposition
3.1
. We introduce the quantities
R^{*}(d_{1},d_{2}),\varrho^{*}(d_{1},d_{2})
and
r^{*}(d_{1},d_{2})
which are defined similarly to
A(d_{1},d_{2}),\varrho(d_{1},d_{2})
and
r(d_{1},d_{2})
but with the added condition
\gcd(a,b,d)=1
.
We write
(a_{1},b_{1})\sim(a_{2},b_{2})
if there exists an integer
\lambda
such that
(a_{1},b_{1})\equiv\lambda(a_{2},b_{2})\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d)
. This forms an equivalence relation on points
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
\gcd(a,b,d)=1
. Moreover, the properties
g_{1}(a,b)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d_{1})
and
g_{2}(a,b)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d_{2})
are preserved under this equivalence. We may therefore define

\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})=\left.\left\{a,b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d):\begin{tabular}[]{l l}&$\gcd(a,b,d)=1$\\
&$d_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b),d_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b)$\end{tabular}\right\}\middle/\sim.\right.

For
\mathscr{C}\in\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})
, we define

\Lambda(\mathscr{C})=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:y\equiv\lambda(a,b)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d)\textrm{ for some }(a,b)\in\mathscr{C}\textrm{ and some }\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

It is easy to check that
\Lambda(\mathscr{C})
is a lattice in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and its set of primitive points is
\mathscr{C}
. For
e\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define

\Lambda(\mathscr{C},e)=\{(a,b)\in\Lambda(\mathscr{C}):e\mid\gcd(a,b)\}.

By Möbius inversion, we have

R^{*}(d_{1},d_{2})=\sum_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})}\sum_{e\mid d}\mu(e)\#\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\Lambda(\mathscr{C},e):C(a,b)\}.

Since
\gcd(d,\Delta)=1
, the set
\{(a,b)\in\Lambda(\mathscr{C},e):C(a,b)\}
is a coset of the lattice
\Lambda(\mathscr{C},e\Delta)
, which has determinant
de\Delta^{2}
. Therefore, by Lemma
3.3
,

(3.5)

R^{*}(d_{1},d_{2})=\sum_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})}\sum_{e\mid d}\mu(e)\left(\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{R})}{de\Delta^{2}}+O\left(1+\frac{P(\mathscr{R})}{\lambda_{1}(\mathscr{C})}\right)\right),

where
\lambda_{1}(\mathscr{C})
denotes the length of the shortest nonzero vector in
\Lambda(\mathscr{C})
.

Each equivalence class
\mathscr{C}\in\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})
consists of
\varphi(d)
elements, and so

\sum_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})}\sum_{e\mid d}\frac{\mu(e)}{e}=\sum_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})}\frac{\varphi(d)}{d}=\frac{\varrho^{*}(d_{1},d_{2})}{d}.

Moreover, we have
\#\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})\ll d^{\epsilon}
, as we now explain. We observe that
\#\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})=\varrho^{*}(d_{1},d_{2})/\varphi(d)
, and
\varrho^{*}
is multiplicative by the Chinese remainder theorem. For primes
p\notin S
, we may apply Hensel’s lemma to show that
\varrho^{*}(p^{e},1),\varrho^{*}(1,p^{e})=O(p^{e})
for any integer
e\geqslant 1
. Therefore, by the trivial bound for the divisor function
[
22
, Section 18.1]
, we conclude that

(3.6)

\#\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})=\frac{\varrho^{*}(d_{1},d_{2})}{\varphi(d)}\ll\frac{d^{1+\epsilon}}{\varphi(d)}\ll d^{\epsilon}.

Applying (
3.5
), and (
3.6
), we obtain

(3.7)

\begin{split}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\gcd(d,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\sup_{P(\mathscr{R})\leqslant N}\left|r^{*}(d_{1},d_{2})\right|\\
&\ll_{\epsilon}(D_{1}D_{2})^{\epsilon}\left(D_{1}D_{2}+N\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\gcd(d,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})}\lambda_{1}(\mathscr{C})^{-1}\right).\end{split}

Let
v_{1}(\mathscr{C})
denote a shortest nonzero vector of
\Lambda(\mathscr{C})
, and let
\|\cdot\|
be the usual Euclidean norm. Then
\|v_{1}(\mathscr{C})\|^{2}\ll|\det\Lambda(\mathscr{C})|=d\leqslant D_{1}D_{2}
. Therefore

(3.8)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\gcd(d,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2})}\lambda_{1}(\mathscr{C})^{-1}\ll\sum_{0<a^{2}+b^{2}\ll D_{1}D_{2}}\frac{M(a,b)}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}},

where

M(a,b)=\#\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{l l}&$d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2},\mathscr{C}\in\mathscr{U}(d_{1},d_{2}):$\\
&$\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\gcd(d,\Delta)=1,v_{1}(\mathscr{C})=(a,b)$\end{tabular}\right\}.

For any
d_{1},d_{2}
enumerated by
M(a,b)
, we have
d_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b)
and
d_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b)
, so

M(a,b)\leqslant\#\{d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}:d_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b),d_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b)\}.

Since
g_{1}
contains no linear factors, we know that
g_{1}(a,b)\neq 0
whenever
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
. Suppose in addition that
g_{2}(a,b)\neq 0
. Then by the trivial bound for the divisor function we have
M(a,b)\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{\epsilon}
. We deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a^{2}+b^{2}\ll D_{1}D_{2}\\
g_{2}(a,b)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{M(a,b)}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}

\displaystyle\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{\epsilon}\sum_{0<a^{2}+b^{2}\ll D_{1}D_{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}

\displaystyle\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{1/2+\epsilon}.

Now suppose that
g_{2}(a,b)=0
. Then as above, we have
O(D_{1}^{\epsilon})
choices for
d_{1}
, but now
D_{2}
choices for
d_{2}
, so that
M(a,b)\ll D_{1}^{\epsilon}D_{2}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a^{2}+b^{2}\ll D_{1}D_{2}\\
g_{2}(a,b)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{M(a,b)}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}

\displaystyle\ll D_{1}^{\epsilon}D_{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a^{2}+b^{2}\ll D_{1}D_{2}\\
g_{2}(a,b)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}.

For a fixed
b\neq 0
,
g_{2}(a,b)
is a nonzero polynomial in
a
, and so has
O(1)
roots. Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a^{2}+b^{2}\ll D_{1}D_{2}\\
g_{2}(a,b)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a^{2}+b^{2}\ll D_{1}D_{2}\\
b\neq 0\\
g_{2}(a,b)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a^{2}\ll D_{1}D_{2}\\
g_{2}(a,0)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{a}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{b\ll\sqrt{D_{1}D_{2}}}\frac{1}{b}+\sum_{a\ll\sqrt{D_{1}D_{2}}}\frac{1}{a}

\displaystyle\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{\epsilon}.

To summarize, we have established the following generalisation of
[
27
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
g_{1}
does not contain any linear factors. Then for any
D_{1},D_{2}>0
and any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\gcd(d,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\sup_{P(\mathscr{R})\leqslant N}\left|r^{*}(d_{1},d_{2})\right|\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{\epsilon}(D_{1}D_{2}+N(D_{1}D_{2})^{1/2}+ND_{2}).

Now we remove the restriction
\gcd(a,b,d)=1
. Below we write
A(d_{1},d_{2})=A(\mathscr{R},d_{1},d_{2};C(a,b))
in order to make the dependence on
\mathscr{R}
and
C(a,b)
clear. Let
k_{1}=\deg g_{1}
and
k_{2}=\deg g_{2}
. We work with multiplicative functions
\psi_{k}
for
k=k_{1}
and
k=k_{2}
, which map prime powers
p^{r}
to
p^{\lceil r/k\rceil}
. We follow the same argument as Irving, but with
\psi_{k_{1}},\psi_{k_{2}}
in place of
\psi_{3},\psi_{4}
. The motivation for this definition of
\psi_{k}
comes from the fact that for any integers
d,e,k\geqslant 1
with
e\mid\psi_{k}(d)
, and for any prime
p
, we have

(3.9)

p\mid\frac{\psi_{k}(d)}{e}\iff p\mid\frac{d}{\gcd(d,e^{k})}.

Since
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=1
, we have

(3.10)

A(\mathscr{R},d_{1},d_{2};C(a,b))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1}\mid\psi_{k_{1}}(d_{1})\\
e_{2}\mid\psi_{k_{2}}(d_{2})\end{subarray}}N(d_{1},d_{2},e_{1},e_{2}),

where

(3.11)

N(d_{1},d_{2},e_{1},e_{2})=\#\left\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\begin{tabular}[]{l l}&$C(a,b),d_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b),d_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b),$\\
&$\gcd(a,b,\psi_{k_{1}}(d_{1})\psi_{k_{2}}(d_{2}))=e_{1}e_{2}$\end{tabular}\right\}.

We make a change of variables
a^{\prime}=a/e_{1}e_{2},b^{\prime}=b/e_{1}e_{2}
in (
3.11
). Let
\overline{e_{1}e_{2}}
denote the multiplicative inverse of
e_{1}e_{2}
modulo
\Delta
, which exists due to the assumption
\gcd(d_{1}d_{2},\Delta)=1
. The congruence condition
C(a,b)
is equivalent to the congruence condition
a^{\prime}\equiv\overline{e_{1}e_{2}}a_{0}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ \Delta)
and
b^{\prime}\equiv\overline{e_{1}e_{2}}b_{0}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ \Delta)
, which we denote by
C^{\prime}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
. We have

\displaystyle d_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b)

\displaystyle\iff d_{1}\mid(e_{1}e_{2})^{k_{1}}g_{1}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

\displaystyle\iff d_{1}\mid e_{1}^{k_{1}}g_{1}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

\displaystyle\iff\frac{d_{1}}{\gcd(d_{1},e_{1}^{k_{1}})}\mid g_{1}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}),

and similarly for
d_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b)
. For convenience, we define

f_{1}=\frac{d_{1}}{\gcd(d_{1},e_{1}^{k_{1}})},\quad f_{2}=\frac{d_{2}}{\gcd(d_{2},e_{2}^{k_{2}})}.

changing notation from
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
back to
a,b
, we deduce that
N(d_{1},d_{2},e_{1},e_{2})
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\#\left\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}/(e_{1}e_{2})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\begin{tabular}[]{l l}&$C^{\prime}(a,b),f_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b),f_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b),$\\
&$\gcd(a,b,\psi_{k_{1}}(d_{1})\psi_{k_{2}}(d_{2})/e_{1}e_{2})=1$\end{tabular}\right\}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}/(e_{1}e_{2})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\begin{tabular}[]{l l}&$C^{\prime}(a,b),f_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b),f_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b),$\\
&$\gcd(a,b,f_{1}f_{2})=1$\end{tabular}\right\}.

(3.15)

\displaystyle=R^{*}\left(\mathscr{R}/(e_{1}e_{2}),f_{1},f_{2};C^{\prime}(a,b)\right).

The above arguments, but with the congruence conditions removed, and with the specific choice
\mathscr{R}=[0,d_{1}d_{2}]^{2}
also demonstrate that

\displaystyle\varrho(d_{1},d_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1}\mid\psi_{k_{1}}(d_{1})\\
e_{2}\mid\psi_{k_{2}}(d_{2})\end{subarray}}\#\left\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}/(e_{1}e_{2})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\begin{tabular}[]{l l}&$f_{1}\mid g_{1}(a,b),f_{2}\mid g_{2}(a,b),$\\
&$\gcd(a,b,\psi_{k_{1}}(d_{1})\psi_{k_{2}}(d_{2})/e_{1}e_{2})=1$\end{tabular}\right\}

(3.17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1}\mid\psi_{k_{1}}(d_{1})\\
e_{2}\mid\psi_{k_{2}}(d_{2})\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{e_{1}e_{2}f_{1}f_{2}}\right)^{2}\varrho^{*}(f_{1},f_{2}).

We denote the quantity

R^{*}(\mathscr{R}/(e_{1}e_{2}),f_{1},f_{2};C^{\prime}(a,b))-\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{R}/(e_{1}e_{2}))\varrho^{*}(f_{1},f_{2})}{(f_{1}f_{2}\Delta)^{2}}

by
E(e_{1},e_{2},f_{1},f_{2})
. Combining (
3.10
), (
3.15
) and (
3.17
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
(d_{1},d_{2})=(d_{1}d_{2},\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\sup_{P(\mathscr{R})\leqslant N}\left|r(d_{1},d_{2})\right|

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
(d_{1},d_{2})=(d_{1}d_{2},\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\sup_{P(\mathscr{R})\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1}\mid\psi_{k_{1}}(d_{1})\\
e_{2}\mid\psi_{k_{2}}(d_{2})\end{subarray}}|E(e_{1},e_{2},f_{1},f_{2})|

(3.19)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{e_{1}\leqslant D_{1},e_{2}\leqslant D_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1}\leqslant D_{1}/e_{1},f_{2}\leqslant D_{2}/e_{2}\\
(f_{1},f_{2})=(f_{1}f_{2},\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\delta(e_{1},f_{1})\delta(e_{2},f_{2})\sup_{P(\mathscr{R})\leqslant N}\left|E(e_{1},e_{2},f_{1},f_{2})\right|,

where for integers
e,f,k,D\geqslant 1
, we have defined

\delta(e,f)=\#\left\{d\leqslant D:e\mid\psi_{k}(d),f=\frac{d}{\gcd(d,e^{k})}\right\}.

We claim that
\delta(e,f)\ll e^{\epsilon}
. To see this, suppose that
p
is a prime and let
r=\nu_{p}(d),s=\nu_{p}(e)
and
t=\nu_{p}(f)
. There is a unique choice of
r
for a given
k,s
and
t
provided that
t>0
, namely,
r=ks+t
. If
t=0
, then we deduce from
f=d/\gcd(d,e^{k})
that
r\leqslant ks
. Taking a product over primes, we conclude that each
d
enumerated by
\delta(e,f)
is a divisor of
e^{k}
multiplied by a quantity that is uniquely determined by
e
and
f
. The claim follows, since the number of divisors of
e^{k}
is
O(e^{\epsilon})
. In our situation, where
e_{1}\leqslant D_{1}
and
e_{2}\leqslant D_{2}
, we obtain
\delta(e_{1},f_{1})\delta(e_{2},f_{2})\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{\epsilon}.

Therefore, applying Lemma
3.4
for each choice of
e_{1},e_{2}
in (
3.19
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
(d_{1},d_{2})=(d_{1}d_{2},\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\sup_{P(\mathscr{R}\leqslant N)}\left|r(d_{1},d_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{\epsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1}\leqslant D_{1}\\
e_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{D_{1}D_{2}}{e_{1}e_{2}}+N\left(\frac{D_{1}D_{2}}{e_{1}e_{2}}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{ND_{2}}{e_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{\epsilon}\left(D_{1}D_{2}+N(D_{1}D_{2})^{1/2}+ND_{2}\right),

which completes the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Remark 3.5
.

If
g_{2}(a,b)\neq 0
for all
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
, then we do not need to consider the case
g_{2}(a,b)=0
in the analysis of the sum in (
3.8
), and so in our final level of distribution result, we do not require the assumption
D_{2}\ll N^{1-\epsilon}
.

When
g_{1}(a,b)
does contain linear factors, we can still obtain a basic level of distribution result from the above argument using the trivial estimate
\lambda_{1}(\mathscr{C})^{-1}\leqslant 1
in (
3.7
). This establishes the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
g_{1},g_{2}
be arbitrary binary forms with nonzero discriminant. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for any
D_{1},D_{2}>0
with
D_{1}D_{2}\ll N^{1-\epsilon}
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\leqslant D_{1},d_{2}\leqslant D_{2}\\
\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=\gcd(d,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}\sup_{P(\mathscr{R})\leqslant N}\left|r(d_{1},d_{2})\right|\\
\ll N^{2-\delta}.

4.

In this section, we prove Theorem

with the beta sieve of Rosser and Iwaniec

We recall some of the notation from Section

\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{B}N

be a an integer with only prime factors in

\Delta

from (

and appropriate lifts of

\nu_{p}(f(a_{0},b_{0}))<\nu_{p}(\Delta)
for all

All implied constants in this section are allowed to depend on
\Delta
. Let
\mathscr{P}
be a set of primes disjoint from

satisfying (

) and (

). We also define
\mathscr{P^{\prime}}
to be the set of primes not in

. Let

denote the product of primes in
\mathscr{P}_{<x}
, and similarly for

. We also define

X=\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{R})/\Delta^{2}
.

For a sequence of non-negative real numbers
\mathscr{A}=(a_{n})
, and a parameter

, we define the sifting function

S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},z)=\sum_{\gcd(n,P(z))=1}a_{n}.

We make the choice

(4.1)

a_{n}=\#\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:C(a,b),f(a,b)=n\},

so that

(4.2)

\begin{split}S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},x)&=\#\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:C(a,b),\gcd(f(a,b),P(x))=1\}\\
&=S(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{P},N),\end{split}

where

is as defined in (
2.8
). Our aim is to prove that

for sufficiently large

(which may depend on
\Delta
).
For a prime
p\in\mathscr{P}
and for any

, we also consider the sequences
\mathscr{A}_{p},\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)}
defined similarly to (

) but with the additional conditions

respectively, so that

\displaystyle S(\mathscr{A}_{p},\mathscr{P},p)

\displaystyle=\#\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:C(a,b),p\mid f(a,b),\gcd(f(a,b),P(p))=1\},

\displaystyle S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},p)

\displaystyle=\#\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:C(a,b),p\mid f_{i}(a,b),\gcd(f(a,b),P(p))=1\}.

Using the Buchstab identity, we have

S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},x)=S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},N^{\gamma})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\gamma}\leqslant p<x\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p},\mathscr{P},p),

for a parameter

to be chosen later. We denote

by

then

for some

S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},x)\geqslant S_{1}-\sum_{i=0}^{m}S_{2}^{(i)}-\sum_{i=m+1}^{k}\left(S_{3}^{(i)}+S_{4}^{(i)}\right),

where

(4.3)

\begin{split}S_{2}^{(i)}&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\gamma}\leqslant p\ll N\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},p),\\
S_{3}^{(i)}&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\gamma}\leqslant p<N^{\beta_{i}}\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},p),\\
S_{4}^{(i)}&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\beta_{i}}\leqslant p<x\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},p),\end{split}

for parameters

to be chosen later.

4.1.
The beta sieve

Like most combinatorial sieves, the beta sieve provides a mechanism to estimate sifting functions of the form
S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},z)
given arithmetic information about the related quantities

|\mathscr{A}_{d}|:=\sum_{d\mid n}a_{n}
for squarefree integers
d
. More specifically, we require an approximation
|\mathscr{A}_{d}|=|\mathscr{A}_{1}|g(d)+r(d)
, where
g(d)
is a multiplicative function supported on squarefree integers and

R(z):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leqslant z\\
d\textrm{ squarefree}\end{subarray}}|r(d)|

is small. Define

V(z)=\sum_{d\mid P(z)}\mu(d)g(d)=\prod_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant z}}(1-g(p)).

We shall assume that for some
\kappa,L\geqslant 0
, we have

(4.4)

V(w)\leqslant\left(\frac{\log z}{\log w}\right)^{\kappa}\left(1+\frac{L}{\log w}\right)V(z)

for all
2\leqslant w\leqslant z
.

For some choice of sieve weights
\Lambda^{\pm}=(\lambda_{d}^{\pm})_{d\geqslant 1}
, define

V^{\pm}(z)=\sum_{d\mid P(z)}\lambda_{d}^{\pm}g(d).

We recall that if
\Lambda^{\pm}
are upper and lower bound sieves of level
z
, i.e., if
\lambda^{\pm}_{d}
are supported on squarefree integers
d<z
and

\sum_{d\mid m}\lambda^{-}_{d}\leqslant\sum_{d\mid m}\mu(d)\leqslant\sum_{d\mid m}\lambda^{+}_{d}

for all integers
m
, then we have

(4.5)

\begin{split}S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},z)&\leqslant|\mathscr{A}_{1}|V^{+}(z)+R(z),\\
S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},z)&\geqslant|\mathscr{A}_{1}|V^{-}(z)-R(z).\end{split}

The main theorem of the beta sieve we apply is given in
[
18
, Theorem 11.12]
. We record this theorem here for convenience, in the special case
s=1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
\kappa,L
are such that the assumption (
4.4
) holds. Then there is a choice of upper and lower bound sieve weights
\Lambda^{\pm}
(taking values in
\{-1,0,1\}
), and explicit constants
A(\kappa),B(\kappa)\geqslant 0
such that, as
z\rightarrow\infty
, we have

(4.6)

\begin{split}V^{+}(z)&\leqslant\left(A(\kappa)+o(1)\right)V(z),\\
V^{-}(z)&\geqslant\left(B(\kappa)+o(1)\right)V(z).\end{split}

Notation 4.2
.

Throughout the remainder of this chapter, for a sequence
\mathscr{A}
, a set of primes
\mathscr{P}
, a multiplicative function
g
, and a sifting level
z\geqslant 1
, we define
\Lambda^{\pm}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},g,z)
to be the corresponding upper and lower bound beta sieves with these parameters. We sometimes apply (
4.6
) directly without reference to a sequence, in which case the parameter
\mathscr{A}
is omitted from the notation.

In our applications of the beta sieve, the required bounds on
R(z)
are provided by Corollary
3.2
and Lemma
3.6
. For
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, we define multiplicative functions

\displaystyle\varrho_{i}(d_{1},d_{2})

\displaystyle=\#\{a,b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d_{1}d_{2}):d_{1}\mid f_{i}(a,b),d_{2}\mid f(a,b)\},

\displaystyle\varrho_{i}(d)

\displaystyle=\#\{a,b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d):f_{i}(a,b)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d)\},

\displaystyle\varrho(d)

\displaystyle=\#\{a,b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d):f(a,b)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d)\}.

We note that the function
\varrho_{i}(d_{1},d_{2})
is the same as the function
\varrho(d_{1},d_{2})
from (
3.2
) with
g_{1}(x,y)=f_{i}(x,y)
and

, but in this section we add a subscript to keep track of the dependence on

. When

, we have
\varrho_{i}(d_{1},d_{2})=\varrho_{i}(d_{1})\varrho(d_{2})
. Moreover, for any

and any prime

(4.7)

\displaystyle\varrho_{i}(p)

\displaystyle=\nu_{i}(p)(p-1)+1,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\varrho(p)

where
\nu_{i}(p)
and
\nu(p)

and define

(4.9)

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
x\geqslant 1
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, and

as in (

), there exist constants
c,c_{i}>0
such that

(4.10)

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{(\log x)^{\kappa}}\left(1+O((\log x)^{-1})\right),

(4.11)

\displaystyle V_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{i}}{(\log x)^{\kappa_{i}}}\left(1+O((\log x)^{-1})\right).

The asymptotic in (
4.11

when
f_{0}\not\equiv 1

Proof.

We follow a similar approach to
[
27
, Lemma 4.2]
. Below, we denote by
C
a constant which is allowed to vary from line to line. We have

(4.12)

\displaystyle\log V(x)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varrho(p)^{m}}{mp^{2m}}\right)

(4.13)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\nu(p)(p-1)+1}{p^{2}}+C+O((\log x)^{-1}).

(4.14)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\nu(p)}{p}+C+O((\log x)^{-1}),

where in (
4.14
) we have used that
\nu(p)\leqslant\deg f
for all but finitely many primes
p
.
To estimate the sum in (
4.14
), we apply partial summation, together with our assumption (
2.6
). For
t\geqslant 2
, we define

(4.15)

A_{t}=\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant t}}\nu(p).

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\nu(p)}{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{x}}{x}+\int_{2}^{x}\frac{A_{t}}{t^{2}}\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\kappa\int_{2}^{x}\frac{\pi(t)\left(1+O\left((\log t)^{-1}\right)\right)}{t^{2}}\textrm{d}t+O((\log x)^{-1})

\displaystyle=\kappa\int_{2}^{x}\frac{\textrm{d}t}{t\log t}+C+O((\log x)^{-1})

(4.16)

\displaystyle=\kappa\log\log x+C+O((\log x)^{-1}).

We deduce (
4.10
) by taking the exponential of (
4.16
).

We can prove (
4.11
) in a similar way. When
i=0
and
f_{0}\not\equiv 1
, we have
\varrho_{0}(p)=p
, and so the result is a consequence of Mertens’ theorem
[
29
, Equation (2.16)]
. For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, we have

\displaystyle\log V_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P^{\prime}}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\nu_{i}(p)}{p}+C+O((\log x)^{-1})

(4.17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leqslant x\textrm{ prime}}\frac{\nu_{i}(p)}{p}-\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\nu_{i}(p)}{p}+C+O((\log x)^{-1}).

Similarly to above, using partial summation and (
2.5

(4.18)

\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\nu_{i}(p)}{p}=\alpha\theta_{i}\log\log x+C+O((\log x)^{-1}).

To treat the first sum in (
4.17

L_{i}
to be the number field generated by
f_{i}
. For all but finitely many primes
p

in

\sum_{p\leqslant x\textrm{ prime}}\nu_{i}(p)=\pi_{L_{i}}(x)+O(x^{1/2}).

Using partial summation, as above, together with the Prime ideal theorem
[

(4.19)

\sum_{p\leqslant x\textrm{ prime}}\frac{\nu_{i}(p)}{p}=\log\log x+C+O((\log x)^{-1}).

Combining this with (

There exists constants

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\varrho_{i}(p)}{p^{2}}

(4.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\varrho_{i}(p)}{p^{2}}

(4.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\varrho_{i}(p)}{p^{2}}\log p

Proof.

The estimates (
4.20
) and (
4.21
) are immediate consequences of (
4.16
), (
4.18
) and (

), together with fact that

\frac{\varrho_{i}(p)}{p^{2}}=\frac{(p-1)\nu_{i}(p)+1}{p^{2}}=\frac{\nu_{i}(p)}{p}+O(p^{-2}).

To prove (

4.16

(4.23)

\pi(t)=\frac{t}{\log t}+\frac{t}{(\log t)^{2}}+O\left(\frac{t}{(\log t)^{3}}\right).

For

as defined in (

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\varrho_{i}(p)}{p^{2}}\log p

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\nu_{i}(p)}{p}\log p+O(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{x}\log x}{x}-\int_{2}^{x}A_{t}\left(\frac{\log t}{t}\right)^{\prime}\textrm{d}t+O(1)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{i}\int_{2}^{x}\frac{(\log t-1)\pi(t)(1+O((\log t)^{-A})}{t^{2}}\textrm{d}t+O(1)

4.3.
The sum
S_{1}

We apply the lower bound sieve
\Lambda^{-}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},g,N^{\gamma})

4.4.
The sums
S_{2}^{(i)}

We write

, for

Let

\nu_{l}(r)=\nu_{l}(f_{i}(a,b))\leqslant\nu_{l}(f(a,b))=\nu_{l}(f(a_{0},b_{0}))<\nu_{l}(\Delta).

Therefore,
\gcd(r^{\prime},\Delta)=1
, and by the assumption that every prime in
S
divides
\Delta
, this implies
r^{\prime}
has no prime factors in
S
. Moreover,
p
is the smallest prime in
\mathscr{P}
dividing
f_{i}(a,b)
. Since
r<p
, this means that
r^{\prime}
has no prime factors in
\mathscr{P}
, and hence only has prime factors in
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
.

On the other hand,
f_{i}(a,b)/r^{\prime}
has no prime factors in
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
. Therefore,

(4.27)

S_{2}^{(i)}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\ll R\\
\gcd(r^{\prime},P(R)\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}S_{2}^{(i)}(r^{\prime}),

where

(4.28)

S_{2}^{(i)}(r^{\prime})=\#\left\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\begin{tabular}[]{l l l}&$C(a,b),r^{\prime}\mid f_{i}(a,b),$\\
&$\gcd(f_{i}(a,b)/r^{\prime},P^{\prime}(z))=1$\\
&$\gcd(f(a,b),P(z))=1$\end{tabular}\right\}.

Below, for convenience, we change notation from
r^{\prime}
back to
r
.

Let
\Lambda_{1}^{+},\Lambda_{2}^{+}
be upper bound sieves of level
z
. Defining
A(dr,e),r(dr,e)
as in Section
3
with
g_{1}(x,y)=f_{i}(x,y)
and
g_{2}(x,y)=f(x,y)
, and writing

m_{1}=\gcd\left(\frac{f_{i}(a,b)}{r},P^{\prime}(z)\right),\qquad m_{2}=\gcd(f(a,b),P(z)),

we obtain

\displaystyle S_{2}^{(i)}(r)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
C(a,b)\end{subarray}}\sum_{d\mid m_{1}}\mu(d)\sum_{e\mid m_{2}}\mu(e)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
C(a,b)\end{subarray}}\sum_{d\mid m_{1}}\lambda_{1}^{+}(d)\sum_{e\mid m_{2}}\lambda_{2}^{+}(e)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid P^{\prime}(z)\\
\gcd(d,r)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{1}^{+}(d)\sum_{e\mid P(z)}A(dr,e)\lambda_{2}^{+}(e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid P^{\prime}(z)\\
\gcd(d,r)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{1}^{+}(d)\sum_{e\mid P(z)}\lambda_{2}^{+}(e)\left(\frac{X\varrho(dr,e)}{(dre)^{2}}+r(dr,e)\right)

(4.29)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\frac{X\varrho_{i}(r)}{r^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid P^{\prime}(z)\\
\gcd(d,r)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{1}^{+}(d)g_{i}(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\mid P(z)\end{subarray}}\lambda_{2}^{+}(e)g(d)\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,e\leqslant z\\
\gcd(dr,e)=1\\
\gcd(dre,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}|r(dr,e)|.

Choose
\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{2}^{+}
to be the beta sieves
\Lambda^{+}(\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\backslash\{p\mid r\},g_{i},N^{1/3}),\Lambda^{+}(\mathscr{P},g,N^{1/3})
respectively. Then the remainder term in (
4.29
) can be bounded by

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,e\leqslant N^{1/3}\\
\gcd(dr,e)=1\\
\gcd(dre,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}|r(dr,e)|\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\ll N^{1/3}R\\
e\ll N^{1/3}\\
\gcd(dr,e)=1\\
\gcd(dre,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}|r(d,e)|,

which is negligible by Lemma
3.6
because
N^{2/3}R=N^{5/3-\gamma}\ll N^{1-\epsilon}
.

Define a multiplicative function
h_{i}(r)
supported on squarefree integers
r
, which is zero unless all prime factors of
r
are in
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
and

(4.30)

h_{i}(r)=\frac{\varrho_{i}(r)}{r^{2}}\prod_{p\mid r}\left(1-\frac{\varrho_{i}(p)}{p^{2}}\right)^{-1}

otherwise. Applying Theorem
4.1
, we conclude that

S_{2}^{(i)}(r)\leqslant Xh_{i}(r)V(N^{1/3})V_{i}(N^{1/3})(AA_{i}+o(1)).

From Lemma
4.3
, we obtain

S_{2}^{(i)}\leqslant\frac{(cc_{i}AA_{i}+o(1))X}{(\log N^{1/3})^{\kappa+\kappa_{i}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\ll R\end{subarray}}h_{i}(r).

To deal with the sum over
h_{i}(r)
, we note that

(4.31)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\ll R\end{subarray}}h_{i}(r)\leqslant\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\\
p\ll R\end{subarray}}\left(1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}h_{i}(p^{m})\right)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\\
p\ll R\end{subarray}}\left(1-g_{i}(p)\right)^{-1}(1+O(p^{-2})).

Applying Lemma
4.3
to the product, we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\ll R\end{subarray}}h_{i}(r)\ll(\log R)^{\kappa_{i}}.

Since
R=N^{1-\gamma}
, we deduce that

S_{2}^{(i)}\ll\frac{cc_{i}AA_{i}X}{(\log N)^{\kappa}}\left(\frac{(1-\gamma)^{\kappa_{i}}}{(1/3)^{\kappa_{i}+\kappa}}\right).

Therefore,
S_{2}^{(i)}
can be made negligible compared to
S_{1}
by taking
\gamma
arbitrarily close to 1.

4.5.
The sums
S_{3}^{(i)}

For a fixed prime
p
and an upper bound sieve
\lambda^{+}
of level
z
, we have

S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},z)\leqslant\sum_{d\mid P(z)}\lambda^{+}(d)A(p,d),

where
A(p,d)
is as in Section
3
with
g_{1}(x,y)=f_{i}(x,y)
and
g_{2}(x,y)=f(x,y)
.

We first deal with the primes
p
in the interval
I:=(N^{\gamma},N^{2-\gamma-\epsilon}]
. For any
p\in I
, let
\lambda^{+}
be the beta sieve
\Lambda^{+}(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},g_{i}(p)g,N^{\gamma})
. We obtain

\displaystyle S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},p)

\displaystyle\leqslant S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},N^{\gamma})

(4.32)

\displaystyle\leqslant(A+o(1))XV(N^{\gamma})g_{i}(p)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leqslant N^{\gamma}\\
\gcd(d,p\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}|r(p,N^{\gamma})|.

We apply Corollary
3.2
with
D_{1}=N^{2-\gamma-\epsilon}
and
D_{2}=N^{\gamma}
. These choices of
D_{1},D_{2}
satisfy the hypotheses of Corollary
3.2
. Taking a sum over
p\in I
, the contribution from the remainder term in (
4.32
) is negligible. We obtain

(4.33)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in I\cap\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},p)\leqslant(A+o(1))XV(N^{\gamma})\sum_{p\in I\cap\mathscr{P}}g_{i}(p).

It follows from Lemma
4.4
that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\gamma}<p\leqslant N^{2-\gamma-\epsilon}\end{subarray}}g_{i}(p)

\displaystyle=\log\log N^{2-\gamma-\epsilon}-\log\log N^{\gamma}+o(1)

\displaystyle=\log(2-\gamma-\epsilon)-\log\gamma+o(1).

Therefore, the contribution to
S_{3}^{(i)}
from this range is negligible if we take
\gamma
arbitrarily close to 1.

In the remaining range
N^{2-\gamma-\epsilon}<p<N^{\beta_{i}}
, we split into dyadic intervals
(R,2R]
. Note that for
p\in(R,2R]
, the assumption
\gamma<1
implies that
N^{2-\epsilon}/R<p
, so
S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},p)\leqslant S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},N^{2-\epsilon}/R)
. For each dyadic interval, we apply the beta sieve
\Lambda^{+}(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},g_{i}(p)g,N^{2-\epsilon}/R)
and the level of distribution result from Corollary
3.2
with
D_{1}=2R
and
D_{2}=N^{2-\epsilon}/R
. At this point we need to assume that
\beta_{i}<2
for all
i
, so that
D_{2}\geqslant 1
. We obtain

(4.34)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{2-\gamma-\epsilon}<p<N^{\beta_{i}}\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},p)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\textrm{ dyadic}\\
N^{2-\gamma-\epsilon}<R<N^{\beta_{i}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in(R,2R]\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p}^{(i)},\mathscr{P},N^{2-\epsilon}/R)

\displaystyle\leqslant(A+o(1))X\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\textrm{ dyadic}\\
N^{2-\gamma-\epsilon}<R<N^{\beta_{i}}\end{subarray}}V(N^{2-\epsilon}/R)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in(R,2R]\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}g_{i}(p)

(4.35)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(cA+o(1))X}{(\log N)^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{2-\gamma-\epsilon}\leqslant p<N^{\beta_{i}}\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}\frac{g_{i}(p)}{(2-\epsilon-\frac{\log p}{\log N})^{\kappa}},

where the last line follows from Lemma
4.3
and the fact that
V(N^{2-\epsilon}/R)<V(N^{2-\epsilon-\log p/\log N})
for all
p\in(R,2R]
.

We denote the sum in (
4.35
) by
T(\beta_{i},\kappa)
. Since
\gamma<1
, we have
2-\gamma-\epsilon>1
for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, and so we may upper bound
T(\beta_{i},\kappa)
by enlarging its range of summation to
N<p<N^{\beta_{i}}
. Define

A(t)=\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{\leqslant t}}g_{i}(p),\qquad h(t)=\left(2-\epsilon-\frac{\log t}{\log N}\right)^{-\kappa}.

From Lemma
4.4
, we have
A(t)=(1-\kappa_{i})\log\log t+C+o(1)
for some constant
C
. In particular, for any
r>0
, we have
A(N^{r})-A(N)=(1-\kappa_{i})\log r+o(1)
. Applying summation by parts, followed by the substitution
t=N^{s}
, we obtain

\displaystyle T(\beta_{i},\kappa)

\displaystyle\leqslant(A(N^{\beta_{i}})-A(N))h(N^{\beta_{i}})-\int_{N}^{N^{\beta_{i}}}(A(t)-A(N))h^{\prime}(t)\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle=(A(N^{\beta_{i}})-A(N))h(N^{\beta_{i}})-\int_{1}^{\beta_{i}}(A(N^{s})-A(N))\frac{\partial h(N^{s})}{\partial s}\textrm{d}s

\displaystyle=(1-\kappa_{i})\log\beta_{i}h(N^{\beta_{i}})-(1-\kappa_{i})\int_{1}^{\beta_{i}}(\log s)\frac{\partial h(N^{s})}{\partial s}\textrm{d}s+o(1)

(4.36)

\displaystyle=(1-\kappa_{i})\int_{1}^{\beta_{i}}\frac{h(N^{s})}{s}\textrm{d}s+o(1).

Taking
\epsilon
sufficiently small and redefining it appropriately, we may replace
h(N^{s})
by
(2-s)^{-\kappa}
at the cost of adding
\epsilon
in (
4.36
). Combining with (
4.35
), we conclude that for any
\epsilon>0
,

(4.37)

S_{3}^{(i)}\leqslant\frac{(cA+\epsilon+o(1))X}{(\log N)^{\kappa}}\cdot(1-\kappa_{i})\int_{1}^{\beta_{i}}(2-s)^{-\kappa}\frac{\textrm{d}s}{s}.

Due to the factor
1-\kappa_{i}=\alpha\theta_{i}
appearing in the above estimate,
S_{3}^{(i)}
becomes negligible compared to
S_{1}
as
\alpha\rightarrow 0
. We perform a more precise quantitative comparison in Section
4.7
.

4.6.
The sums
S_{4}^{(i)}

We begin in a similar manner to the sums
S_{2}^{(i)}
, by writing
f_{i}(a,b)=pr
, for
p\in\mathscr{P}
, where now
N^{\beta_{i}}\leqslant p<x
and
R=x/N^{\beta_{i}}
. Let
D_{1}=N^{\eta_{1}}
and
D_{2}=N^{\eta_{2}}
for parameters
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}>0
(which may depend on
r
and
i
) to be chosen later. We assume
\eta_{2}<\beta_{i}
so that the condition
\gcd(f(a,b),P(p))=1
can be replaced by the weaker condition
\gcd(f(a,b),P(N^{\eta_{2}}))=1
. Proceeding as in Section
4.4
, we have

(4.38)

S_{4}^{(i)}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\ll R\\
\gcd(r,P(N^{\beta_{i}})\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}S_{4}^{(i)}(r),

where

(4.39)

S_{4}^{(i)}(r)=\#\left\{(a,b)\in\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\begin{tabular}[]{l l l}&$C(a,b),r\mid f_{i}(a,b),$\\
&$\gcd(f_{i}(a,b)/r,P^{\prime}(N^{\eta_{1}}))=1$\\
&$\gcd(f(a,b),P(N^{\eta_{2}}))=1$\end{tabular}\right\}.

Similarly to (
4.29
), for any upper bound sieves
\lambda_{1}^{+},\lambda_{2}^{+}
of levels
N^{\eta_{1}},N^{\eta_{2}}
, the quantity
S_{4}^{(i)}(r)
is bounded above by

\left(\frac{X\varrho_{i}(r)}{r^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid P^{\prime}(N^{\eta_{1}})\\
\gcd(d,r)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{1}^{+}(d)g_{i}(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\mid P(N^{\eta_{2}})\end{subarray}}\lambda_{2}^{+}(e)g(d)\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leqslant N^{\eta_{1}},e\leqslant N^{\eta_{2}}\\
\gcd(dr,e)=1\\
\gcd(dre,\Delta)=1\end{subarray}}|r(dr,e)|.

In order to ensure the error terms from applying the level of distribution result from Corollary
3.2
are negligible after summing over
r
, we need
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
to satisfy

(4.40)

\eta_{1},\eta_{2}>0,\quad\eta_{2}\leqslant 1-\delta\qquad(\textrm{for all }r\leqslant R),\\

(4.41)

\sum_{r\leqslant R}N^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}\leqslant N^{2-\delta}.

Remark 4.5
.

There are no
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
satisfying (
4.40
) and (
4.41
) unless
R\leqslant N^{2-\delta}
, i.e.,
x\leqslant N^{2-\delta+\beta_{i}}
. Since we had to assume
\beta_{i}<2
in the treatment of the sums
S_{3}^{(i)}
in Section
4.5
, this means our approach cannot handle the case
d\geqslant 4
, in which
f(x,y)
has an irreducible factor of degree
\geqslant 4
. Therefore, we proceed with the additional assumption that
d\leqslant 3
.

Choose
\lambda^{+}_{1},\lambda^{+}_{2}
to be the beta sieves
\Lambda^{+}(\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\backslash\{p\mid r\},g_{i},N^{\eta_{1}}),\Lambda^{+}(\mathscr{P},g,N^{\eta_{2}})
respectively. Recalling the definition of
h_{i}(r)
from (
4.30
), we obtain

(4.42)

S_{4}^{(i)}(r)\leqslant(AA_{i}+o(1))Xh_{i}(r)V_{i}(N^{\eta_{1}})V(N^{\eta_{2}})

Using Lemma
4.3
to estimate the products in (
4.42
), and taking a sum over
r
, we obtain

(4.43)

S_{4}^{(i)}\leqslant\frac{(cc_{i}AA_{i}+o(1))X}{(\log N)^{\kappa_{i}+\kappa}}\sum_{r\leqslant R}\frac{h_{i}(r)}{\eta_{1}^{\kappa_{i}}\eta_{2}^{\kappa}}.

We divide the sum over
r
into dyadic intervals
r\in(R_{1},2R_{1}]
, and take
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
depending only on
R_{1}
and
i
. To obtain a good estimate for (
4.43
), we maximise
\eta_{1}^{\kappa_{i}}\eta_{2}^{\kappa}
subject to the constraints

\eta_{1},\eta_{2}>0,\qquad\eta_{2}\leqslant 1-\delta,\qquad\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\leqslant 2-\delta-\frac{\log R_{1}}{\log N}.

By a similar computation to
[
27
, Section 6.5]
, the optimal solution is

\displaystyle\eta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\kappa+\kappa_{i}}\left(2-\delta-\frac{\log R_{1}}{\log N}\right),

\displaystyle\eta_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+\kappa_{i}}\left(2-\delta-\frac{\log R_{1}}{\log N}\right).

We note that for
\delta>0
sufficiently small, this solution satisfies
\eta_{2}\leqslant 1-\delta
due to the assumption
\kappa<1/2
. Substituting this choice of
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
into (
4.43
), we obtain

(4.44)

\sum_{r\leqslant R}\frac{h_{i}(r)}{\eta_{1}^{\kappa_{i}}\eta_{2}^{\kappa}}\leqslant\sum_{r\leqslant R}w(r,\delta)h_{i}(r),

where

(4.45)

w(r,\delta)=\left(\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+\kappa_{i}}\right)^{-\kappa}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\kappa+\kappa_{i}}\right)^{-\kappa_{i}}\left(2-\delta-\frac{\log r}{\log N}\right)^{-(\kappa+\kappa_{i})}.

We treat the sum in (
4.44
) using partial summation, for which we require estimates for
\sum_{r\leqslant t}h_{i}(r)
. For the case
d=2
, the estimate already found in (
4.31
) is sufficient. Below, we find a more refined estimate which we use in the case
d=3
.

We first consider the contribution to (
4.44
) from squarefree values of
r
. We would like to apply
[
18
, Theorem A.5]
, which states that under certain hypothesis on the function
h_{i}(r)
, we have

(4.46)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x\\
\mu^{2}(m)=1\end{subarray}}h_{i}(m)=c_{h_{i}}(\log x)^{\kappa_{i}}+O((\log x)^{\kappa_{i}-1}),

where

c_{h_{i}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\kappa_{i}+1)}\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\kappa_{i}}(1+h_{i}(p)).

In the following lemma, we verify that the function
h_{i}(r)
satisfies the required hypotheses for
[
18
, Theorem A.5]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
x\geqslant 1
and any
2\leqslant w<z
, the function
h_{i}(r)
satisfies the following estimates.

(4.47)

\displaystyle\prod_{w\leqslant p<z}(1+h_{i}(p))

\displaystyle\ll\left(\frac{\log z}{\log w}\right)^{\kappa_{i}},

(4.48)

\displaystyle\sum_{p}h_{i}(p)^{2}\log p

\displaystyle<\infty,

(4.49)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leqslant x}h_{i}(p)\log p

\displaystyle=\kappa_{i}\log x+O(1).

Proof.

To prove (
4.47
), we note that
1+h_{i}(p)=\left(1-\frac{\varrho_{i}(p)}{p^{2}}\right)^{-1}
for all
p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
. The result is then immediate from Lemma
4.3
. To prove (
4.48
), we recall that
\varrho_{i}(p)\ll p
, and so
h_{i}(p)\ll p^{-1}
. Therefore

\sum_{p}h_{i}(p)^{2}\log p\ll\sum_{p}\frac{\log p}{p^{2}}<\infty.

Finally, we note that

\sum_{p\leqslant x}h_{i}(p)\log p=\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{\leqslant x}}\frac{\varrho_{i}(p)}{p^{2}}\log p+O(1),

so that (
4.49
) follows by applying Lemma
4.4
.
∎

We can now evaluate the sum in (
4.44
) using partial summation. We obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leqslant R\\
\mu^{2}(r)=1\end{subarray}}w(r,\delta)h_{i}(r)

\displaystyle=w(R,\delta)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leqslant R\\
\mu^{2}(r)=1\end{subarray}}h_{i}(r)-\int_{1}^{R}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leqslant t\\
\mu^{2}(r)=1\end{subarray}}h_{i}(r)\right)w^{\prime}(t,\delta)\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle=c_{h_{i}}\left[(\log R)^{\kappa_{i}}w(R,\delta)-\int_{1}^{R}w^{\prime}(t,\delta)(\log t)^{\kappa_{i}}\textrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\quad+o(1)\left[(\log R)^{\kappa_{i}}w(R,\delta)+\int_{1}^{R}w^{\prime}(t,\delta)(\log t)^{\kappa_{i}}\textrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=c_{h_{i}}\kappa_{i}\int_{1}^{R}w(t,\delta)(\log t)^{\kappa_{i}-1}t^{-1}\textrm{d}t+o\left((\log R)^{\kappa_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{h_{i}}\kappa_{i}(\log N)^{\kappa_{i}}\int_{0}^{\log R/\log N}w(N^{s},\delta)s^{\kappa_{i}-1}\textrm{d}s+o\left((\log R)^{\kappa_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant(c_{h_{i}}\kappa_{i}+o(1))(\log N)^{\kappa_{i}}\int_{0}^{d-\beta_{i}}W(s)\textrm{d}s,

where
W(s)=w(N^{s},0)s^{\kappa_{i}-1}.

We now consider the contribution to (
4.30
) from those
r
which are not squarefree. Let
d_{i}
denote the degree of
f_{i}
. From
[
15
, Lemma 3.1]
, we have
\varrho_{i}(p^{\alpha})\ll p^{2\alpha(1-1/d_{i})}
for all primes
p\notin S
and any positive integer
\alpha
. By the multiplicativity of
h_{i}(r)
, it follows that
h_{i}(r)\ll r^{-2/d_{i}+\epsilon}
.

We recall that a positive integer
n
is
squareful
if for any prime
p\mid n
, we also have
p^{2}\mid n
. Since
d_{i}\leqslant 3
, we have

\sum_{r\textrm{ squareful}}h_{i}(r)\ll\sum_{r\textrm{ squareful}}r^{-2/d_{i}+\epsilon}\leqslant\sum_{r\textrm{ squareful}}r^{-2/3+\epsilon}<\infty,

where the last inequality follows from partial summation together with the fact that there are
O(M^{1/2})
squareful positive integers less than
M
. Since
h_{i}(r)
is supported on integers with no prime factors in
S
, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists a set of primes
S_{0}
, depending only on
\epsilon
and
f
, such that for any
S\supseteq S_{0}
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\textrm{ squareful}\\
r>1\end{subarray}}h_{i}(r)<\epsilon.

For the remainder of this section, we assume that
S\supseteq S_{0}
. Proceeding as in
[
27
, Lemma 6.2]
, we use that
w(r,\delta)\ll 1
, and decompose each non-squarefree
r
into
r=r_{1}r_{2}
, where
r_{1}
is squarefree and
r_{2}>1
is squareful. We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leqslant R\\
\mu^{2}(r)=0\end{subarray}}w(r,\delta)h_{i}(r)\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1}\leqslant R\\
\mu^{2}(r_{1})=1\end{subarray}}h_{i}(r_{1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{2}\textrm{ squareful}\\
r_{2}>1\end{subarray}}h_{i}(r_{2}).

Combining with (
4.46
), we deduce that for any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leqslant R\\
\mu^{2}(r)=0\end{subarray}}w(r,\delta)h_{i}(r)\leqslant(\epsilon+o(1))(\log N)^{\kappa_{i}}.

In conclusion, we have the upper bound

(4.50)

S_{4}^{(i)}\leqslant\frac{(cc_{i}AA_{i}c_{h_{i}}\kappa_{i}+\epsilon+o(1))X}{(\log N)^{\kappa}}\int_{0}^{d-\beta_{i}}W(s)\textrm{d}s,

where

(4.51)

W(s)=\left(\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+\kappa_{i}}\right)^{-\kappa}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\kappa+\kappa_{i}}\right)^{-\kappa_{i}}(2-s)^{-(\kappa+\kappa_{i})}s^{\kappa_{i}-1}.

Remark 4.7
.

Whilst
c,c_{i},c_{h_{i}}
all depend on
S
, the ratio
\kappa_{i}c_{i}c_{h_{i}}AA_{i}/B
of the constants in the bounds for
S_{4}^{(i)}
and

4.9.
Details of the numerical computations

We now explain how we obtained numerical values with guaranteed error bounds in the proofs of Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
2.3
. From
[
18
, Equations (11.62), (11.63)]
, we have for
0<\kappa<1/2
that

A(\kappa)=\frac{2e^{\gamma\kappa}}{\Gamma(1-\kappa)}\frac{q(\kappa)}{p(\kappa)+q(\kappa)},\qquad B(\kappa)=\frac{2e^{\gamma\kappa}}{p(\kappa)+q(\kappa)},

where

\displaystyle p(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{-\kappa}\exp(-z+\kappa\operatorname{Ei}(-z))\operatorname{d}z

\displaystyle q(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{-\kappa}\exp(-z-\kappa\operatorname{Ei}(-z))\operatorname{d}z

and where
\operatorname{Ei}(-z)=-\int_{z}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-u}}{u}\operatorname{d}u
is the standard exponential integral. For any
0<\kappa<1/2
, the integrand defining
p(\kappa)
is monotonically decreasing, decays exponentially as
z\rightarrow\infty
, and is bounded above by
2
. Similarly, the integrand defining
q(\kappa)
is monotonically decreasing and decays exponentially as
z\rightarrow\infty
, but diverges as
z\rightarrow 0
. To analyse its behaviour near
0
, we note that for
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\kappa<0.4
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta}z^{-\kappa}e^{-z}\exp(-\kappa\operatorname{Ei}(-z))\operatorname{d}z

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{\delta}z^{-0.4}\operatorname{exp}\left(0.4\int_{z}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-u}}{u}\operatorname{d}u\right)\operatorname{d}z

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{\delta}z^{-0.4}\operatorname{exp}\left(0.4\int_{z}^{1}\frac{\operatorname{d}u}{u}+0.4\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-u}\operatorname{d}u\right)\operatorname{d}z

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{\delta}z^{-0.4}\exp(-0.4\log(z)+0.4e^{-1})\operatorname{d}z

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\int_{0}^{\delta}z^{-0.8}\operatorname{d}z

\displaystyle\leqslant 10\delta^{0.2}.

Therefore, we can obtain estimates for
p(\kappa),q(\kappa)
(and hence also
A(\kappa),B(\kappa)
with provable error bounds using standard numerical integration methods such as the trapezoid rule. The functions from (
4.55
) and (
4.61
) are bounded, continuous, and monotonically decreasing in
\kappa
, and so it is straightforward to find their roots to the desired precision. This was implemented in Sage, which produced the values
0.39000,0.32380
in
12.3
seconds on a standard laptop with
2
cores.

5.1.
Algebraic reduction of the problem

Let
K
be a number field of degree

satisfying the Hasse norm principle. In
[
27
, Lemma 2.6]
, Irving turns the problem of establishing the Hasse principle for

(5.1)

into a sieve problem. Irving assumes that
f(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
is an irreducible cubic polynomial. However, in the following result, we demonstrate that Irving’s strategy can be applied to establish a similar result for an arbitrary polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
. We recall that
f(x,y)
denotes the homogenisation of
f
. Throughout this section, we make the choice

(5.2)

\mathscr{P}=\{p\notin S:\textrm{ the inertia degrees of }p\textrm{ in }K/\mathbb{Q}\textrm{ are not coprime}\}

for a finite set of primes
S
containing all ramified primes in
K/\mathbb{Q}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that (
5.1
) has solutions over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
for every
p
and over
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathscr{P}
and
S
be as in (
5.2
). Then there exists
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
, divisible only by primes in
S
, integers
a_{0},b_{0}
, and real numbers
r,\xi>0
such that the following implication holds:

Suppose that
a,b
are integers for which

(1)

a\equiv a_{0}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ \Delta)
and
b\equiv b_{0}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ \Delta)
,

(2)

|a/b-r|<\xi
,

(3)

bf(a,b)
has no prime factors in
\mathscr{P}
.

Then
(\ref{HP for general products of quadratics})
has a solution over
\mathbb{Q}
.

By multiplicativity of norms, it suffices to find integers
a,b
such that
b
and
f(a,b)
are in
N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(K^{*})
. Since
K
satisfies the Hasse norm principle, we have
\mathbb{Q}^{*}\cap N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(I_{K})=N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(K^{*})
, where

denotes the ideles of

. Consequently, to show that

is a norm from

for each place

of

(1)

For all but finitely many places
v
of
K

(this ensures that
(x_{v})\in I_{K}
).

(2)

For all places

\mathbb{Q}

(5.3)

\prod_{v\mid w}N_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{w}}(x_{v})=c.

The arguments in
[

go through without changes. We summarise them below.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose

(1)

(2)

for each place

of

.

(3)

We now give a slight generalisation of

Lemma 5.3
.

Let

be a prime for which

Proof.

We define

be such that

The set

∎

K/\mathbb{Q}
are coprime. On the other hand, when
p\equiv 3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
, the prime
p
is inert in
K/\mathbb{Q}
and has degree 2, so the inertia degrees are not coprime. Therefore, we have
\mathscr{P}=\{p:p\equiv 3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)\}
. We choose
S=\{2\}
. In this example, it can be checked that the congruence conditions
a\equiv 8\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 16)
and
b\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 16)
are sufficient. Finally, for the infinite place, we just have the condition
f(a,b)>0
. The sieve problem we obtain is to find integers
a,b
such that

(1)

a\equiv 8\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 16),b\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 16)
,

(2)

f(a,b)>0
,

(3)

f(a,b)
has no prime factors
p\equiv 3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
.

We remark that
f(a/b)=b^{-4}f(a,b)
, and since
2=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
divides
4
,
b^{-4}
is automatically a norm from
K
. This explains why in (3) above, we can consider prime factors of
f(a,b)
rather than
bf(a,b)
.

An integer is the sum of two squares if and only if it is non-negative and all prime factors
p\equiv 3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
occur with an even exponent. The above conditions are stronger than this, so the algebraic reduction performed in Proposition
5.1
is consistent with what we already knew for this example.

Since the Hasse principle fails for this example, we know that the above sieve problem cannot have a solution. This is indeed the case, since condition (1) implies that
-a^{2}+3b^{2}\equiv 3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
, and so
f(a,b)
must contain a prime factor
p\equiv 3\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
.

The fact that the above sieve problem has no solutions also does not contradict Theorem
2.1
. For a prime
p>3
, we have that
\nu_{1}(p)
is 2 if
p\equiv\pm 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)
and zero otherwise, and
\nu_{2}(p)
is 2 if
p\equiv\pm 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12)
and zero otherwise. Consequently, by Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, asymptotically one half of the primes
p\in\mathscr{P}
have
\nu_{i}(p)=2
and half have
\nu_{i}(p)=0
. We conclude that
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=1
, so that
\theta=2
. Theorem
2.1
therefore requires the density of
\mathscr{P}
to be less than
0.39000.../\theta=0.19503...
. However, the density of
\mathscr{P}
here is
1/2
, and so Theorem
2.1
does not apply to this example.

5.2.
The Chebotarev density theorem

In order to apply the sieve results from Section
2
to prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.3
, we need to compute the densities (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) for the choice of
\mathscr{P}
given in (
5.2
). For more complicated number fields
K
, such as when
K/\mathbb{Q}
is non-abelian, it is not possible to write down the set
\mathscr{P}
explicitly in terms of congruence conditions. However, we can still compute the densities (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) by appealing to the
Chebotarev density theorem
, which can be viewed as a vast generalisation of Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a number field of degree
n
. Let
p
be a prime, unramified in
K/\mathbb{Q}
. We can factorise the ideal
(p)
as
(p)=\mathfrak{p}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{r}
, where
\mathfrak{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{p}_{r}
are distinct prime ideals in
\mathscr{O}_{K}
. The
splitting type
of
p
in
K/\mathbb{Q}
is the partition
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
of
n
, where
a_{i}
is the inertia degree of
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
, i.e.,
N(\mathfrak{p}_{i})=p^{a_{i}}
. Equivalently, the splitting type is the list of degrees of the irreducible factors of the minimum polynomial of
K/\mathbb{Q}
, when factorised modulo
p
.

Suppose first that
K/\mathbb{Q}
is Galois, with Galois group
G
. Then
G
acts transitively on
\{\mathfrak{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{p}_{r}\}
. Fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
. The
Decomposition
group
D_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}
is the stabilizer of
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
under this action. Note that
D_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}
is cyclic, and there is an isomorphism

\psi_{i}:D_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}\rightarrow{\rm Gal}((\mathscr{O}_{K}/\mathfrak{p}_{i})/(\mathbb{Z}/(p))).

The group
{\rm Gal}((\mathscr{O}_{K}/\mathfrak{p}_{i})/(\mathbb{Z}/(p)))
is generated by the
Frobenius element
defined by
x\mapsto x^{p}
, which has order
a_{i}
. Let
\sigma_{i}
denote the preimage of the Frobenius element under
\psi_{i}
. We define the
Artin symbol

\left[\frac{K/\mathbb{Q}}{p}\right]=\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{r}\}.

The Artin symbol is a conjugacy class of
G
. Indeed, all the
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
’s lie in the same orbit of
G
(there is only one orbit as
G
acts transitively). Stabilisers of points in the same orbit of an action are conjugate, and so all the
D_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}
are conjugate.

We now come to the statement of the Chebotarev density theorem. For a conjugacy class
C
of
G
, we let
\pi_{C}(x)
denote the number of primes
p\leqslant x
whose Artin symbol is equal to
C
. We define the (natural)
density
of a set of primes
\mathscr{P}
to be

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\#\{p\in\mathscr{P}:p\leqslant x\}}{\pi(x)}\right),

if such a limit exists.

Theorem 5.5

(Chebotarev density theorem)
.

Let
C
be a conjugacy class of
G
. The density of primes
p
for which the Artin symbol is equal to
C
is
\#C/\#G
.

In order to obtain the explicit error terms from (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we need an effective version of the Chebotarev density theorem. The following lemma is a straightforward consequence of a more refined result due to Lagarias and Odlyzko
[
33
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 5.6

(Effective Chebotarev density theorem)
.

For any
A\geqslant 1
, we have

\pi_{C}(x)=\pi(x)\left(\frac{\#C}{\#G}+O_{A}((\log x)^{-A})\right),

where the implied constant may depend on
K,C
and
A
.

We now consider the non-Galois case. As above, let
(p)=\mathfrak{p}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{r}
be the factorisation of
(p)
in
\mathscr{O}_{K}
. Let
\widehat{K}
denote the Galois closure of
K
, and let
G={\rm Gal}(\widehat{K}/\mathbb{Q})
. This time, there is no action of
G
on
\{\mathfrak{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{p}_{r}\}
, because the
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
’s could split further in
\widehat{K}
, and elements of
G
could permute prime factors which are above different
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
’s.

To get around this, we define
H={\rm Gal}(\widehat{K}/K)
, and instead consider the action of
G
on the set
X
of left cosets of
H
in
G
. For an element
\sigma\in G
, the cyclic group
\langle\sigma\rangle
generated by
\sigma
acts by left multiplication on
X
. The sizes of the orbits of this action form a partition of
[G:H]=[K:\mathbb{Q}]=n
. Moreover, it can be checked that conjugate elements of
G
give the same orbit sizes, so we can associate a single partition of
n
with each Artin symbol
\left[\frac{\widehat{K}/\mathbb{Q}}{p}\right]
. The following fact relating this partition with the splitting type of
p
can be found in
[
30
, Ch. 3, Proposition 2.8]
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\sigma\in\left[\frac{\widehat{K}/\mathbb{Q}}{p}\right]
. Then
p
has splitting type
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
in
K/\mathbb{Q}
if and only if the action of
\langle\sigma\rangle
on
X
has orbit sizes
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
, and let
g
be the minimum polynomial of
\alpha
. We can also view
\langle\sigma\rangle
as acting on the set of
n
roots of
g
in
\widehat{K}
. By definition of
H
, we have that
\sigma\alpha=\sigma^{\prime}\alpha
if and only if
\sigma H=\sigma^{\prime}H
. It follows that the orbit sizes of
\langle\sigma\rangle
acting on
X
are the same as the orbit sizes of
\langle\sigma\rangle
acting on the roots of
g
, which in turn are the cycle lengths of
\sigma
viewed as a permutation on the
n
roots of
g
in
\widehat{K}
. The set of
\sigma\in G
with cycle lengths
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
is a union
\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}C_{i}
of conjugacy classes
C_{i}
. We may now apply Theorem
5.6
to each of these conjugacy classes separately. Putting everything together, we have the following result on densities of splitting types in non-Galois extensions.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
n
over
\mathbb{Q}
, and let
\widehat{K}
denote its Galois closure. Let
G={\rm Gal}(\widehat{K}/\mathbb{Q})
, viewed as a permutation group on the
n
roots of the minimum polynomial of
K
in
\widehat{K}
. For a partition
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
of
n
, let
\mathscr{P}(\mathbf{a})
denote the set of primes with splitting type
\mathbf{a}
in
K/\mathbb{Q}
, and let
T(\mathbf{a})
denote the proportion of elements of
G
with cycle shape
\mathbf{a}
. Then for any
A\geqslant 1
,

\#\{p\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbf{a}):p\leqslant x\}=\pi(x)\left(T(\mathbf{a})+O_{A}((\log x)^{-A})\right).

Example 5.9

We consider the example

discussed by Irving
[

[
1
]

G={\rm Gal}(\widehat{K}/\mathbb{Q})
. The minimum polynomial of
K/\mathbb{Q}
is
x^{q}-2
, which has roots
\{\beta,\beta\omega,\ldots,\beta\omega^{q-1}\}
, where
\omega
is a primitive
q
th root of unity and
\beta
is the real root of
x^{q}-2
. We identify these roots with
\{0,\ldots,q-1\}
in the obvious way. An element
\sigma\in G
is determined by the image of
0
and
1
, since
\beta,\beta\omega
multiplicatively generate all the other roots. Therefore,
\sigma
takes the form
\sigma_{a,b}:x\mapsto ax+b
for some
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times},b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Conversely, the maps
\sigma_{1,b}
correspond to the
q
different embeddings
K
into
\widehat{K}
, and the maps
\sigma_{a,0}
for

are elements of
{\rm Gal}(\widehat{K}/K)\leqslant G
. Combining these, we see that
\sigma_{a,b}\in G
for any

. We conclude that
G\cong\operatorname{AGL}(1,q)
, the group of affine linear transformations on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

When
a=1
and
b\neq 0
,
\sigma_{a,b}
is a
q

a,b

has a solution

, and so
\sigma_{a,b}
has a fixed point. Therefore,
T(G)=(q-1)/\#G=1/q
. From this, we see that Irving’s choice of
\mathscr{P}=\{p\notin S:p\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)\}
is not quite optimal, because it has density
\alpha=1/(q-1)
, whereas the set of primes we actually need to sift out has density

. In fact, we can see directly that even when

, there is sometimes a solution to

(e.g.

,

). However, it can be checked that even with this smaller sieve dimension, we are still not able to handle the cases

or

Proof of Corollary
1.2

We now consider the case when

and

, with a view to proving Corollary
1.2
. Such number fields automatically satisfy the Hasse principle by the work of Kunyavskiĭ and Voskresenskiĭ
[

. To ease notation, we shall write
T(n)
in place of

. In the following lemma, we find an estimate for

.

Lemma 5.10
.

For all
n\geqslant 1
, we have

(5.5)

\displaystyle T(n)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{k\mid n}\frac{\mu(k)\Gamma((n+1)/k)}{\Gamma(1/k)\Gamma(n/k+1)},

(5.6)

\displaystyle T(n)

\displaystyle<\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}n^{1/r-1}\omega(n),

where
r
is the smallest prime factor of
n
and
\omega(n)
is the number of prime factors of
n
.

Proof.

Define

T_{k}(n)=\frac{1}{n!}\#\{\sigma\in S_{n}:\textrm{ the cycle lengths of }\sigma\textrm{ are all divisible by $k$}\}.

By Möbius inversion, we have
T(n)=1-\sum_{k\mid n}\mu(k)T_{k}(n)
. We now find an explicit formula for
T_{k}(n)
. For
j\geqslant 1
, let
a_{jk}
denote the number of cycles of length
jk
in
\sigma
. The cycle lengths of
\sigma
are all a multiple of
k
if and only if
\sum_{j=1}^{n/k}jka_{jk}=n
. We apply the well-known formula for the number of permutations of
S_{n}
with a given cycle shape to obtain

\displaystyle T_{k}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{k},a_{2k},\ldots,a_{n}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n/k}jka_{jk}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{\prod_{j=1}^{n/k}(jk)^{a_{jk}}a_{jk}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{n/k}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n/k}jb_{j}=n/k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{n/k}(jk)^{b_{j}}b_{j}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n/k}k^{-i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{n/k}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n/k}jb_{j}=n/k\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n/k}b_{j}=i\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{n/k}j^{b_{j}}b_{j}!}

(5.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{i=1}^{m}k^{-i}c(m,i),

where
m=n/k
and
c(m,i)
is the number of
\sigma^{\prime}\in S_{m}
with exactly
i
cycles. The quantity
c(m,i)
is called the
Stirling number of the first kind
. In order to evaluate (
5.7
), we follow the argument from
[
17
, Example II.12]
. We define a bivariate generating function

(5.8)

P(w,z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}w^{i}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{m}}{m!}c(m,i).

By
[
17
, Proposition II.4]
, we have

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{m}}{m!}c(m,i)=\frac{1}{i!}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{1-z}\right)\right)^{i}.

Therefore,

P(w,z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{i}}{i!}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{1-z}\right)\right)^{i}=\exp\left(w\log\left(\frac{1}{1-z}\right)\right)=(1-z)^{-w}.

Applying the Binomial theorem, we find that the
z^{m}
coefficient of
P(w,z)
is equal to
w(w+1)\cdots(w+m-1)/m!
. On the other hand, if we substitute

, the
z^{m}
coefficient of (
5.8
) is precisely (
5.7
). We conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{i=1}^{m}k^{-i}c(m,i)

\displaystyle=(1/k)(1+1/k)\cdots(m-1+1/k)/m!

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m+1/k)}{\Gamma(1/k)\Gamma(m+1)},

which completes the proof of (
5.5
).

We now establish the upper bound in (
5.6
). A basic bound on the gamma function is that for any real
s\in(0,1)
and any positive real number
x
, we have

x^{1-s}<\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(x+s)}<(1+x)^{1-s}.

Applying this with
x=m
and
s=1/k
, we have

\frac{\Gamma(m+1/k)}{\Gamma(m+1)}<m^{1/k-1}.

Moreover, we have

\frac{1}{\Gamma(1/k)}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+1/k)}\frac{\Gamma(1+1/k)}{\Gamma(1/k)}\leqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}k},

since
\Gamma(1+1/k)
for integers
k\geqslant 2
achieves its minimum at
k=2
, where we have
\Gamma(1+1/k)=\Gamma(3/2)=\sqrt{\pi}/2
. We conclude that

T_{k}(n)<\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}k}(n/k)^{1/k-1}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}n^{1/k-1}k^{-1/k}<\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}n^{1/k-1}.

Taking a sum over
k=p
prime, we obtain

T(n)<\sum_{p\mid n}T_{p}(n)\leqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}n^{1/r-1}\omega(n),

as required.

Remark 6.3

In contrast to Section

For a permutation group

Lemma 6.4

(
[

)
.

Any Frobenius group can be realised as a Galois group over

.

Lemma 6.5

(
[

Let

(1)

We have

, with equality if and only if

and

(2)

In all other cases,
h(G)\geqslant 2/k
.

Proof of Corollary

.

As computed in Example

q_{i}
. Consequently, by the Chinese remainder theorem,
z_{0},\ldots,z_{r}\in\prod_{i=0}^{r}R_{K_{i}/K}\mathbb{A}^{1}
uniquely determine
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}\in\mathbb{A}^{1}_{K}
.

For any number field extension
E/M
, and any
y\in E
, we have

N_{E/M}(y)=\prod_{\sigma\in\operatorname{Emb}_{M}(E,\overline{M})}\sigma(y).

Therefore,

\displaystyle N_{K/k}(y)=\prod_{\sigma\in\operatorname{Emb}_{k}(K,\overline{k})}\sigma(y)=\prod_{i=0}^{r}\prod_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Emb}_{K}(K_{i},\overline{k})}\sigma^{\prime}\sigma_{i}(y)=\prod_{i=0}^{r}N_{K_{i}/K}(\sigma_{i}(y)),

and so

(A.6)

\mathbf{N}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})=\prod_{i=0}^{r}N_{K_{i}/K}(z_{i}).

We deduce that the equations (
A.5
) define an isomorphism from
Y_{K}
to the variety
V\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{1}_{K}\times\prod_{i=0}^{r}R_{K_{i}/K}\mathbb{A}^{1}
given by
z_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{r}N_{K_{i}/K}(z_{i})=f(t)\neq 0
.
For
t,z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}
satisfying the Zariski open condition
\prod_{i=1}^{r}N_{K_{i}/K}(z_{i})\neq 0
, we have
z_{0}=f(t)/\prod_{i=1}^{n}N_{K_{i}/K}(z_{i})
. Therefore, the projection of
V
onto
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{K}\times\prod_{i=1}^{r}R_{K_{i}/K}\mathbb{A}^{1}
is birational. We conclude that the map
\varphi
from (
A.3
) is birational.
∎

We now commence with the proof of Theorem
A.1
. Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero. For a smooth irreducible variety
X/k
with function field
\kappa(x)
, we recall that
\Br(X)
consists of all elements of
\Br(\kappa(X))
which are unramified everywhere on
X
. Constant classes are unramified, and so we have
\Br(k)\subseteq\Br(X)\subseteq\Br(\kappa(X))
. By the purity theorem
[
11
, Theorem 3.7.1]
, the ramification locus of
\mathscr{A}\in\Br(\kappa(X))
is pure of codimension one. Consequently, to check
\mathscr{A}\in\Br(X)
, it suffices to check it is unramified at all codimension one points of
X
.

Let
C
be a codimension one point. We recall from
[
11
, Section 1.4.3]
the
residue map

\partial_{C}:\Br(\kappa(X))\rightarrow H^{1}(\kappa(C),\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})

is such that
\mathscr{A}
is unramified at
C
if and only if
\partial_{C}(\mathscr{A})
is trivial.

In our setting, codimension one points of
X
come in two types:

(1)

Irreducible components of fibres
X_{c}=\pi^{-1}(c)
above codimension one points
c
of
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1}
,

(2)

Codimension one points on the generic fibre
X_{\eta}
of
\pi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1}
.

We recall that
\kappa(X)=\kappa(X_{\eta})
. The codimension one points of
X_{\eta}
are a subset of the codimension one points of
X
, and so we have an inclusion
\Br(X)\hookrightarrow\Br(X_{\eta})
. Since
X_{\eta}
is a smooth projective model of
\mathbf{N}_{K(t)/k(t)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(t)
over
k(t)
, it follows from Theorem
A.2
, applied to the extension
K(t)/k(t)
and with
Z=X_{\eta}
, that
\Br(k(t))\rightarrow\Br(X_{\eta})
is surjective. Putting everything together, we obtain a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(X)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(X_{\eta})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(\kappa(X_{\eta}))=\Br(\kappa(X))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(k)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(k(t))}}

Let
\alpha\in\Br(X)
. By the above diagram, we can find
\beta\in\Br(k(t))
whose image in
\Br(X_{\eta})
is equal to the image of
\alpha
in
\Br(X_{\eta})
. We want to show that
\beta
is the image of an element of
\Br(k)
, because then it follows from commutativity of the diagram that
\alpha
is the image of an element of
\Br(k)
.

For any
n\geqslant 1
and any field
k
, we have
\Br(\mathbb{P}_{k}^{n})=\Br(k)

[
11
, Theorem 6.1.3]
. In particular, we have
\Br(k)=\Br(\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1})
. Also,
k(t)=\kappa(\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1})
, so
\Br(k(t))=\Br(\kappa(\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1}))
. Therefore, as discussed above, to prove that
\beta
is in the image of
\Br(k)
, it suffices to show
\beta
is unramified at every codimension one point of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}
. This is formalised by the
Faddeev exact sequence

[
11
, Theorem 1.5.2]
, which is the exact sequence

(A.7)

0\rightarrow\Br(k)\hookrightarrow\Br(k(t))\rightarrow\bigoplus_{Q\in(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{(1)}}H^{1}(k_{Q},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})\twoheadrightarrow H^{1}(k,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow 0,

where
(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{(1)}
denotes the codimension one points of
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1}
and the third map is the direct sum of the residue maps
\partial_{Q}
. In other words, to show that
\beta\in\Br(k(t))
is actually in
\Br(k)
, it suffices to show that
\partial_{Q}(\beta)=0
for all
Q\in(\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1})^{(1)}
. We have
\partial_{Q}(\beta)=0
unless
Q
is an irreducible factor of
f(t)
by
[
11
, Proposition 11.1.5]
, so we suppose from now on that
Q
is an irreducible factor of
f(t)
.

By Lemma
A.3
, the base change
X_{K}=X\times_{k}K
is birational to
\mathbb{P}_{K}^{n}
. Since the Brauer group is a birational invariant on smooth projective varieties
[
11
, Corollary 6.2.11]
, it follows that
\Br(X_{K})=\Br(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K})=\Br(K)
. Therefore, we obtain the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(X_{K})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(X_{K,\eta})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(X)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(X_{\eta})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(K)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(K(t))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\bigoplus\limits_{Q\in(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{(1)}}H^{1}(Kk_{Q},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(k)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Br(k(t))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\bigoplus\limits_{Q\in(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{(1)}}H^{1}(k_{Q},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi_{K}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota_{K}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

The map
\varphi
is a direct sum over the restriction maps
\varphi_{Q}:H^{1}(k_{Q},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow H^{1}(Kk_{Q},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})
for each
Q\in(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{(1)}
.

Lemma A.4
.

We have
\partial_{Q}(\beta)\in\ker\varphi_{Q}
, where
\varphi_{Q}
is as defined above.

Proof.

Let
\beta_{K}
denote the image of
\beta
in
\Br(K(t))
, and
\partial_{K,Q}
the residue map at
Q
on
\Br(K(t))
. We want to show that
\partial_{K,Q}(\beta_{K})=0
. By exactness of the Faddeev exact sequence over
K
, for this it suffices to show
\beta_{K}
is in the image
\Br(K)\rightarrow\Br(K(t))
. We know that
\psi(\beta)=\iota(\alpha)
. Applying a base change to
K
, we see that
\psi_{K}(\beta_{K})=\iota_{K}(\alpha_{K})
, where
\alpha_{K},\beta_{K}
are the images of
\alpha,\beta
under base change. Since
\Br(K)\cong\Br(X_{K})
, we have that
\alpha_{K}
is in the image of
\Br(K)\rightarrow\Br(X_{K})
, and hence by commutativity of the diagram,
\beta_{K}
is in the image of
\Br(K)\rightarrow\Br(K(t))
, as required.
∎

Let
M
be a number field, and let
G_{M}={\rm Gal}(\overline{M}/M)
. For a finite Galois extension
M^{\prime}/M
, we consider
{\rm Gal}(M^{\prime}/M)
as a topological space with the discrete toplology. We can then put the profinite topology on
G_{M}
, which is defined as the inverse limit

G_{M}=\varprojlim_{M^{\prime}/M\textrm{ Galois}}{\rm Gal}(M^{\prime}/M).

We recall that
H^{1}(M,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})=\operatorname{Hom}_{\textrm{cont}}(G_{M},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})
, the continuous group homomorphisms
G_{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}

[
11
, pp.16]
. Suppose that
\theta\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\textrm{cont}}(G_{M},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})
. Then
\ker\theta
is an open subgroup of
G_{M}
. Since
G_{M}
is a profinite group, this implies that
\ker\theta
has finite index in
G_{M}
. By the fundamental theorem of Galois theory,
\operatorname{im}\theta\cong G_{M}/\ker\theta\cong{\rm Gal}(M^{\prime}/M)
, for some finite Galois extension
M^{\prime}/M
. Moreover,
\operatorname{im}\theta
is a finite subgroup of
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
. All finite subgroups of
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
are cyclic groups of the form
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}
for some positive integer
n
. Consequently,
\ker\theta={\rm Gal}(M^{\prime}/M)
for a cyclic extension
M^{\prime}/M
. To summarise, we have the identification

H^{1}(M,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})=\{M^{\prime}/M\textrm{ cyclic, with a given map }\gamma:{\rm Gal}(M^{\prime}/M)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}\}.

We now describe
\varphi_{Q}:H^{1}(k_{Q},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow H^{1}(Kk_{Q},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})
explicitly. Using the above identification, we view an element
\theta\in H^{1}(k_{Q},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})
as a pair
(M^{\prime}/k_{Q},\gamma)
. The map
\varphi_{Q}
is given by taking the compositum with
K
. More precisely, it sends the above pair to
(KM^{\prime}/Kk_{Q},\gamma)
, where now
\gamma
is viewed as a map
{\rm Gal}(KM^{\prime}/KK_{Q})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
via the natural identification of
{\rm Gal}(KM^{\prime}/Kk_{Q})
as a subgroup of
{\rm Gal}(M^{\prime}/k_{Q})
. Therefore,
(M^{\prime}/k_{Q},\gamma)\in\ker\varphi_{Q}
if and only if
M^{\prime}/k_{Q}
is a cyclic subextension of
Kk_{Q}/k_{Q}
.

Due to the assumption that
k_{Q}
and
L
are linearly disjoint over
k
, we have
{\rm Gal}(Kk_{Q}/k_{Q})\cong{\rm Gal}(K/k)\cong S_{n}
. We now complete the proof of Theorem
A.1
with the following elementary group theory fact.

Lemma A.5
.

Suppose that
K/k
is a finite extension of degree
n\geqslant 3
, and the Galois group of the Galois closure
{\rm Gal}(L/k)
is isomorphic to
S_{n}
. Then there are no nontrivial cyclic extensions
M/k
with
M\subseteq K
.

Proof.

By the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, if
M/k
is a subextension of
K/k
, then
{\rm Gal}(L/K)\leqslant{\rm Gal}(L/M)\leqslant{\rm Gal}(L/k)=S_{n}
. However,
{\rm Gal}(L/K)\cong S_{n-1}
. (More explicitly, if
K=k(\alpha_{1})
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
are the roots of the minimum polynomial of
\alpha_{1}
over
k
, then
{\rm Gal}(L/K)
consists of all permutations of
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
which fix
\alpha_{1}
.) However,
S_{n-1}
is a maximal subgroup of
S_{n}
, and so
M=k
or
M=K
. Since
n\geqslant 3
, the extension
K/k
is not cyclic. Therefore,
M=k
.
∎

In conclusion, the map
\varphi_{Q}
is injective by Lemma
A.5
, and
\partial_{Q}(\beta)\in\ker\varphi_{Q}
by Lemma
A.4
, and hence
\partial_{Q}(\beta)=0
. This means that all the residue maps of
\beta
are trivial, so
\beta
is in the image of
\Br(k)\rightarrow\Br(k(t))
. Hence
\alpha
is in the image of
\Br(k)\rightarrow\Br(X)
, and so
\Br(X)=\Br(k)
. This completes the proof of Theorem
A.1
.
