Improved Approximation Algorithms for Capacitated Vehicle Routing with Fixed Capacity
Jingyang Zhao, Mingyu Xiao

Abstract
The Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP) is one of the most extensively studied problems in combinatorial optimization. According to the property of the demand of customers, we distinguish three variants of CVRP: unit-demand, splittable and unsplittable. We consider k k -CVRP in general metrics and general graphs, where k k is the capacity of the vehicle and all the three versions are APX-hard for each fixed k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . In this paper, we give a ( 5 / 2 − Θ ⁡ ( 1 / k ) ) (5/2-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k})) -approximation algorithm for splittable and unit-demand k k -CVRP and a ( 5 / 2 + ln ⁡ 2 − Θ ⁡ ( 1 / k ) ) (5/2+\ln 2-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k})) -approximation algorithm for unsplittable k k -CVRP. Our approximation ratio is better than all previous results for k k smaller than a sufficiently large value, say k ≤ 1.7 × 10 7 k\leq 1.7\times 10^{7} . For small k k , we also design independent elegant algorithms with further improvements. For the splittable and unit-demand cases, we improve the ratio from 1.792 1.792 to 1.500 1.500 for k = 3 k=3 , and from 1.750 1.750 to 1.500 1.500 for k = 4 k=4 too. For the unsplittable case, we improve the ratio from 1.792 1.792 to 1.500 1.500 for k = 3 k=3 , from 2.051 2.051 to 1.750 1.750 for k = 4 k=4 , and from 2.249 2.249 to 2.157 2.157 for k = 5 k=5 . The approximation ratio for k = 3 k=3 also surprisingly achieve the same ratio for the splittable case. Note that for small k k such as 3 3 , 4 4 and 5 5 , some previous results have also been kept for decades. Our techniques, such as the EX-ITP method – an extension of the classic ITP method, has potential to improve algorithms for more routing problems. Keywords: Combinatorial Optimization, Capacitated Vehicle Routing, Approximation Algorithms, Graph Algorithms

Introduction

In the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP), we are given an undirected complete graph
G=(V\cup\{v_{0}\},E)
with an edge weight
w
satisfying the symmetric and triangle inequality properties. The
n
nodes in
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
represent
n
customers and each customer
v_{i}
has a demand
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. A vehicle with a capacity of
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
is initially located at the depot
v_{0}
. A tour is a walk that begins and ends at the depot and the sum of deliveries to all customers in it is at most
k
. The distance of a tour is the sum of the weights of edges in the tour. In CVRP, we wish to find a set of tours to satisfy the demand of every customer with the minimum total distance of all the tours. We use
k
-CVRP to denote the problem where the capacity
k
is a fixed integer. In the
unsplittable
version of the problem, the demand of each customer can only be delivered by a single tour. In the
splittable
version, the demand of each customer can be delivered by more than one tour. If the demand of each customer is a unit, it is called the
unit-demand
version.

Since CVRP was raised by Dantzig and Ramser
[
20
]
in 1959, it has become a very famous problem with numerous applications in combinatorial optimization. It has been widely studied in both theory and application. Readers can refer to a survey
[
52
]
for its applications and fast solvers in practice. In theory, it is a rather rich problem in approximation algorithms
[
16
,
15
,
47
,
48
,
19
,
25
]
.

When
k=1
or
k=2
,
k
-CVRP can be solved in polynomial time
[
7
]
.
For each fixed
k\geq 3
, the problem is APX-hard even for the unit-demand case
[
6
]
.
A classic algorithm based on a given Hamiltonian cycle, called
Iterated Tour Partitioning (ITP)
, was proposed about 40 years ago
[
32
]
.
ITP is not only fast in practice but also can achieve a good approximation ratio in theory.
Assume that we are given an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for metric TSP.
For splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP, ITP can achieve an approximation ratio of
\alpha+1-\alpha/k

[
32
]
.
For unsplittable
k
-CVRP, a modification of ITP, called UITP, can achieve a ratio of
\alpha+2-2\alpha/k
for even
k

[
2
]
.
For metric TSP, there is a well-known
3/2
-approximation algorithm
[
17
,
51
]
. Currently, there was a breakthrough on metric TSP by Karlin, Klein and Oveis Gharan
[
39
,
40
]
: an approximation algorithm achieves the ratio
3/2-\varepsilon
, where the improvement
\varepsilon
is about
10^{-36}
. Although this is a nice breakthrough, in our algorithms for
k
-CVRP, we will still assume the ratio
\alpha=3/2
for metric TSP for ease of comparison since the improvement is too small to make a difference.
ITP itself is simple and the 3/2-approximation metric TSP algorithm is also simple.
Due to the simplicity, the ITP algorithm is versatile and has been adapted to other related vehicle routing problems
[
46
]
.
However, for general metrics, there have been few improvements over the ITP algorithm in approximating
k
-CVRP.

One interesting improvement was done by Bompadre
et al.

[
16
]
about 20 years ago.
For any
\alpha\geq 1
, the ratio was improved by a term of
\frac{1}{3k^{3}}
for all the three versions.
For
\alpha=3/2
, the improvement could be
\frac{1}{4k^{2}}
for splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP, and
\frac{1}{3k^{2}}
for unsplittable
k
-CVRP. Before this paper, the results in
[
16
]
were still the best approximation ratios for many small constants
k
.
Very recently, one significant progress was done by Blauth
et al.

[
15
]
. They improved the approximation ratio to
\alpha+1-\varepsilon
for splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP, and to
\alpha+2-2\varepsilon
for unsplittable
k
-CVRP, where
\varepsilon
is a value related to
\alpha
and
\varepsilon>\frac{1}{3000}
when
\alpha=3/2
.
This improves the constant part of the approximation ratio (
\varepsilon
is only related to
\alpha
and not related to
k
), but the improvement is small and the compensation of other parts is large. As a result, they cannot improve the ratio in
[
16
]
for small
k
.
Also very recently, Friggstad
et al.

[
26
]
proposed two further improvements for unsplittable
k
-CVRP: the first is an
(\alpha+1.75)
-approximation algorithm based on a combinatorial method, and the second is an
(\alpha+\ln 2+\frac{1}{1-\delta})
-approximation algorithm based on an LP rounding method with a running time
n^{O(1/\delta)}
. Both of the ratios can be further improved by a small constant
\varepsilon^{\prime}
by combining the method in
[
15
]
.

For small
k
,
k
-CVRP has drawn independent interests. For unit-demand
3
-CVRP, ITP has a ratio of
2
. Based on the
25/33
-approximation algorithm for MAX TSP on general graphs
[
37
]
, Bazgan
et al.

[
8
]
proposed an
1.990
-approximation algorithm, which is the first improvement to our knowledge. Using the best-known
4/5
-approximation algorithm for MAX TSP
[
23
]
, the ratio of their algorithm can be further improved to
1.934
.
For unit-demand
4
-CVRP, ITP has a ratio of
2.125
. Anily and Bramel
[
4
]
showed an approximation algorithm for capacitated TSP with pickups and deliveries. Their algorithm can be applied to unit-demand
k
-CVRP and achieve a better ratio only for the case of
k=4
. The ratio is
1.750
, which is also the best-known result.

Apart from the above algorithms, there is another way to solve
k
-CVRP: reduce it to a more general problem, called the
minimum weight
k
-set cover problem

[
18
,
35
,
31
]
. In the reduction, each feasible tour is seen as a
k
-set. Since there are
n^{O(k)}
feasible tours, the reduction is polynomial when
k
is a constant.
Recently, Gupta
et al.

[
31
]
improved the ratio for the minimum weight
k
-set cover problem to
\min\{H_{k}-\frac{1}{8k},\ H_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\log i}{8ki}\}
, where
H_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{i}
is the
k
-th harmonic number. For
k=3
, the ratio can achieve 1.792, which is even better than all previous ratios for splittable and unit-demand 3-CVRP. Furthermore, this algorithm leads to the best ratio for unsplittable
k
-CVRP with
k\leq 9
.

In this paper, we will study approximation algorithms for
k
-CVRP with a bounded
k
. As mentioned in
[
14
,
16
]
, several problems that arise in practice have small
k
. We also mention an application raised by a logistics company: they need to use a truck that can carry at most six cars to transport the newly produced cars. In this example, the capacity
k
is 6. In the benchmark set, the capacities of instances are also usually integers in hundreds or thousands of levels
[
53
]
. All above show that it is interesting to consider bounded
k
.

Our Contributions

We present several approximation algorithms for
k
-CVRP and improve the best-known approximation ratios for any
k\leq 1.7\times 10^{7}
. This value is already large. The capacity of all practical and artificial instances as far as we know is not larger than this value.
We summarize our contributions to splittable (also unit-demand)
k
-CVRP and unsplittable
k
-CVRP separately below.

For splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP, we have the following contributions.

1.

Based on a new concept of
home-edges
, i.e., the edges incident to the depot in an optimal solution, we obtain tighter lower bounds for
k
-CVRP based on the minimum spanning tree and several different kinds of cycle packings (Section
3
). Our new lower bounds shows that if the lower bound used for the connection part of ITP in
[
2
,
32
]
is tight, the weight of the optimal solution (denoted by
OPT
) will be completely dominated by home-edges, and we can compute in polynomial time a Hamiltonian cycle with a cost of
OPT
and a cycle packing with a zero cost. Since the upper bound of the Hamiltonian cycle used in
[
2
,
32
]
is
\frac{3}{2}\mbox{OPT}
, we can obtain significant improvements for this case. Otherwise, since the lower bound for the connection part is not tight, we obtain improvements as well.
Note that our lower bounds are also suitable for the unsplittable case.

2.

The classic ITP algorithm is based on a given Hamiltonian cycle of the graph. We extend ITP to an algorithm based on any cycle packing, called EX-ITP. One advantage is that an optimal cycle packing is polynomially computable. Based on EX-ITP with the new lower bounds, we can quickly improve the ratio from 1.792 to 1.500 for 3-CVRP and from 1.750 to 1.667 for 4-CVRP; based on a good structure property, we can further surprisingly improve the ratio to
1.500
for 4-CVRP (Section

Definitions, Assumptions and Notations

A
walk
in a graph is a succession of edges in the graph, where the same edge can appear more than one time. We will use a sequence of vertices to denote a walk:

(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},\dots,v_{l})
means a walk with edges
(v_{1},v_{2})
,
(v_{2},v_{3})
, and so on.
A
path
in a graph is a walk such that no vertex appears twice in the sequence, and a
cycle
is a walk such that only the first and the last vertices are the same.
A cycle containing
l
edges is called an
l
-cycle
and the
length
of it is
l
.
Two subgraphs (or two sets of edges) are
vertex-disjoint
if they do not have a common vertex.
Given an edge-weighted graph, where the number
n
of vertices is a multiple of
k
, a
minimum
k
-cycle packing
is a set of exactly
n/k
vertex-disjoint
k
-cycles with the minimum total weight of edges in the
k
-cycles in the set.
A
minimum mod-
k
-cycle packing
(resp.,
minimum mod-
k
-tree packing
) is a set of vertex-disjoint cycles (resp., trees) such that the length of each cycle (resp., the number of vertices on each tree) is divisible by
k
, each vertex of the graph appears in exactly one cycle, and the total weight of edges in the cycles in the set is minimized.
A
minimum cycle packing
is a set of vertex-disjoint cycles such that the length of each cycle is at least three, each vertex of the graph appears in exactly one cycle, and the total weight of edges in the cycles in the set is minimized.

2.1
Problem Definitions

We use
G=(V\cup\{v_{0}\},E)
to denote a complete graph, where the vertex
v_{0}
represents the depot and vertices in
V
represent customers. There is a non-negative weight function
w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on the edges in
E
, which denotes the distance between two endpoints of the edge.
The weight function
w
is a semi-metric function, i.e., it satisfies the symmetric and triangle inequality properties.
For any weight function
w:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we will extend it to subsets of
X
, i.e., we define
w(Y)=\sum_{x\in Y}w(x)
for any
Y\subseteq X
.
An
itinerary
is a walk starting and ending at vertex
v_{0}
.
An itinerary can be split into several minimal cycles containing
v_{0}
, and each such cycle is called a
tour
.
The Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP) can be described as follows.

Definition 1
.

An instance
(G=(V\cup\{v_{0}\},E),w,d,k)
of CVRP consists of:

•

a complete graph
G
, where
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
represents the
n
customers and
v_{0}
represents the depot,

•

a semi-metric weight function on edges
w
:
(V\cup\{v_{0}\})\times(V\cup\{v_{0}\})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, which represents the distances,

•

the demand of each customer
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
, where
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
is the demand required by customer
v_{i}\in V
,

•

the capacity
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
of the vehicle that initially stays at
v_{0}
.

A feasible solution is an itinerary such that

•

each tour delivers at most
k
of the demand to customers on the tour,

•

the union of tours meets the demand of every customer.

The goal is to find such an itinerary
I
, minimizing the total distances of the succession of edges in the walk, i.e.,
w(I)\coloneqq\sum_{e\in I}w(e)
.

According to the property of the demand, we can define three different versions of the problem. If the demand of each customer should be delivered in one tour, we call it
unsplittable CVRP
. If the demand of a customer can be split into several tours, we call it
splittable CVRP
. If the demand of each customer is a unit, we call it
unit-demand CVRP
.

2.2
Some Assumptions

In our problems, we will also make some assumptions which can be guaranteed by some simple observations or polynomial-time reductions.

Assumption 2
.

Assume that the capacity
k
is a constant satisfying
k\geq 3
, and in an optimal itinerary, each tour is a simple cycle.

If there exists a tour that is not a simple cycle, we can get a simple cycle by shortcutting.

Assumption 3
.

If the vehicle just goes to place

and passes place

without delivering anything to place

So in each tour, the vehicle only visits customers where it delivers.

A tour is called

We show some properties of optimal itineraries to splittable CVRP.

Lemma 4

(
[

For splittable CVRP, there exists an optimal itinerary where no two tours visit two common customers.

To our knowledge, this property was first used in

Lemma 5

For splittable CVRP, there exists an optimal itinerary that contains at most

Proof.

Given an optimal itinerary

Next, we assume to the contrary that there are more than

For two vertices visited in the same tour

Based on the cycle

3
Lower Bounds on CVRP

In this section, we study lower bounds.
First of all, we mention that all the lower bounds in this section hold for all the three versions of CVRP.
Unit-demand CVRP can be considered as a relaxed version of unsplittable CVRP and lower bounds on unit-demand CVRP will also hold on unsplittable CVRP.
An instance of splittable CVRP can also be transformed into an equivalent unit-demand CVRP instance by replacing each customer with demand
d_{i}
as
d_{i}
customers with the unit-demand. The optimal solution will not change. So lower bounds for unit-demand CVRP will also hold for splittable CVRP. Next, we assume that the problem considered is unit-demand CVRP and prove some lower bounds.

We will use the concept of
\chi
to obtain some refined lower bounds, which were not considered in previous lower bounds. The first lower bound related to the minimum Hamiltonian cycle
H^{*}
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
was used in most previous papers.

Lemma 12

(
[
32
,
2
]
)
.

w(I^{*})\geq w(H^{*})
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that each vertex in
I^{*}
has an even degree. Since
I^{*}
is a connected graph, we can obtain an Euler tour and then get a Hamiltonian cycle on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
by shortcutting, which has at least the weight of the minimum Hamiltonian cycle. Thus, the lemma will hold.
∎

Next, we consider the relation to
\Delta
.
For unit-demand CVRP,
\Delta
is exactly the sum of the weights of all edges incident on
v_{0}
.
It is easy to get
\frac{k}{2}w(I^{*})\geq\Delta
by the triangle inequality. However, we still need to use the middle and tighter relations in the following lemma.

Lemma 13
.

\frac{k}{2}w(I^{*})\geq(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2})w(I^{*})\geq\Delta
.

Proof.

Since
0\leq\chi\leq 1
, we have that

\frac{k}{2}=(1+\frac{k-2}{2})\geq(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2})
. Thus, the first inequality holds.

Now we show the second.
By Assumption
11
,
I^{*}
consists of a set of
(k+1)
-cycles. We consider an arbitrary
(k+1)
-cycle
C=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{k},v_{0})
in
I^{*}
.
Since
k\geq 3
, then for each
i\in\{2,3,\dots,k-1\}
, it holds that
w(C)\geq 2w(v_{0},v_{i})
by shortcutting.
Thus, we have that

\sum_{i\in\{1,\dots,k\}}w(v_{0},v_{i})=w(h(C))+\sum_{i\in\{2,\dots,k-1\}}w(v_{0},v_{i})\leq w(h(C))+\frac{k-2}{2}w(C).

By summing up the above inequality for all cycles in
I^{*}
, we get that

\sum_{v_{i}\in V}w(v_{0},v_{i})\leq\sum_{C\in I^{*}}w(h(C))+\frac{k-2}{2}\sum_{C\in I^{*}}w(C)
, i.e.,

\Delta=\sum_{v_{i}\in V}w(v_{0},v_{i})\leq\chi w(I^{*})+\frac{k-2}{2}w(I^{*})=\left(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2}\right)w(I^{*}).

∎

Lemma 14
.

(1-\frac{1}{2}\chi)w(I^{*})\geq\mbox{MST}
.

Proof.

First, it holds that
w(h(I^{*}))=\chi w(I^{*})
according to the definition. For each tour in
I^{*}
, there are exactly two home-edges. We can get a spanning tree of the graph from
I^{*}
by deleting the longer home-edge in each tour in
I^{*}
. The weight of this spanning tree is at least
w(I^{*})-\frac{1}{2}\chi w(I^{*})=(1-\frac{1}{2}\chi)w(I^{*})
, which is at least the weight of the minimum spanning tree. So, the lemma holds.
∎

Recall that
H_{CS}
is the Hamiltonian cycle on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
obtained by the Christofides-Serdyukov algorithm. The following bound will be used in our algorithm.

Lemma 15

(
[

51
]
)

\mbox{MST}+\frac{1}{2}w(H^{*})\geq w(H_{CS})
.

By Lemmas
12

14

15

Lemma 16

\frac{3-\chi}{2}w(I^{*})\geq w(H_{CS})
.

Lemma 17

\min\{2(1-\chi),1\}w(I^{*})\geq w(\mathcal{C}_{k}^{*})\geq w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}^{*})\geq w(\mathcal{C}^{*})
.

Proof.

We first show that there exists a

-cycle packing of

By the triangle inequality, we have

Note that the edges

4
The ITP Algorithms

ITP (Iterated Tour Partitioning) is a frequently used technique for (unit-demand) CVRP. The main idea of ITP is to construct feasible solutions for CVRP based on given Hamiltonian cycles: first split the Hamiltonian cycle into several connected pieces of length at most
k
and then construct a tour for each piece. The algorithm will consider several different ways to split the Hamiltonian cycle and choose the best one.

There are different versions of the ITP algorithm for CVRP according to the Hamiltonian cycle containing the depot
v_{0}
or not. We briefly introduce them below and then introduce an extension. Now we assume the problem is unit-demand
k
-CVRP.

4.1
The AG-ITP Algorithm

We first review the ITP algorithm introduced by Altinkemer and Gavish
[
3
]
, where the Hamiltonian cycle needs to go through the depot
v_{0}
.
Assume that there is a Hamiltonian cycle
H=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n},v_{0})
on

as a part of the input. The AG-ITP algorithm will select the best solution from the following
k

For each
1\leq i\leq k
, the
i
-th solution contains the following
(\lceil(n-i)/k\rceil+1)

(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})

(v_{0},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{i+k},v_{0})

(v_{0},v_{i+k+1},\dots,v_{i+2k},v_{0})
,
\dots
,
(v_{0},v_{i+(\lceil(n-i)/k\rceil-1)k+1},\dots,v_{n},v_{0})

It is easy to see that the algorithm can be carried out in

Lemma 18

(
[
3
]
)

Given a Hamiltonian cycle

Using an

, by Lemmas

Corollary 19

The approximation ratio of the AG-ITP algorithm is

(v_{0},v_{i+k},\dots,v_{i+2k-1},v_{0})
,
(v_{0},v_{i+2k},\dots,v_{i+3k-1},v_{0})
,
\dots
,
(v_{0},v_{i+(\lceil n/k\rceil-1)k},\dots,v_{i+n-1},v_{0})
where we let
v_{n+i^{\prime}}=v_{i^{\prime}}
(
i^{\prime}>0
). Except for the last tour, each tour contains exactly
k
customers. The last tour contains
k
customers only when
n\bmod k=0
.
The running time is
O(n^{2})
time.

Lemma 20

(
[
32
]
)
.

Given a Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V
as a part of the input, for unit-demand
k
-CVRP with any
k\geq 3
, the HR-ITP algorithm can use
O(n^{2})
time to output a solution with a weight of at most
(2\lceil n/k\rceil/n)\Delta+(1-\lceil n/k\rceil/n)w(H)
.

Note that if
n
is divisible by
k
, the HR-ITP algorithm can generate a solution with the same bounded weight of
(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)w(H)
in Lemma
18
. Otherwise, the weight maybe worse than
(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)w(H)
.

4.3
An Extension of ITP

The above two ITP algorithms are based on given Hamiltonian cycles. In fact, the requirement of Hamiltonian cycles is not necessary. We can replace the Hamiltonian cycle with a cycle packing (a 2-factor of the graph) in the algorithms to construct feasible solutions to CVRP in a similar way.

Given a cycle packing
\mathcal{C}
of the graph
G[V]
or
G[V\cup\{v_{0}\}]
(either containing the depot
v_{0}
or not), for each cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}
, we call the HR-ITP algorithm on it if
C
does not contain the depot
v_{0}
, and call the AG-ITP algorithm on it if
C
contains the depot
v_{0}
. After adding all together, we get a feasible solution to CVRP. The quality of the solution is related to the cycle packing. We call the algorithm the EX-ITP algorithm. Although the algorithm is still simple, it will play an important role in our algorithms.

Lemma 21
.

Given a cycle packing
\mathcal{C}
of
G[V]
or
G[V\cup\{v_{0}\}]
as a part of the input, for unit-demand
k
-CVRP with any
k\geq 3
, the EX-ITP algorithm uses
O(n^{2})
time to output a feasible solution with a weight of at most
2g\Delta+(1-g)w(\mathcal{C})
, where
g=\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\lceil|C|/k\rceil/|C|
.

Proof.

Define
\Delta_{C}=\sum_{v_{i}\in C}w(v_{0},v_{i})
. For the cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
v_{0}\in C
, the AG-ITP algorithm can generate an itinerary on
C
with a weight of at most
(2/k)\Delta_{C}+(1-1/k)w(C)
.
For each cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
v_{0}\notin C
, the HR-ITP algorithm can generate an itinerary on
C
with a weight of at most
(2\lceil|C|/k\rceil/|C|)\Delta_{C}+(1-\lceil|C|/k\rceil/|C|)w(C)
.
Note that for each cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}
, we can get that
(2/k)\Delta_{C}+(1-1/k)w(C)\leq(2\lceil|C|/k\rceil/|C|)\Delta_{C}+(1-\lceil|C|/k\rceil/|C|)w(C)\leq 2g\Delta_{C}+(1-g)w(C)
, where the inequalities follows from
w(C)\leq 2\Delta_{C}
by the triangle inequality and
2/k\leq 2\lceil|C|/k\rceil/|C|\leq 2g
.
Note that
\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\Delta_{C}=\Delta
. Hence, the total weight is bounded by
\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}(2g\Delta_{C}+(1-g)w(C))=2g\Delta+(1-g)w(\mathcal{C})
.
∎

In our algorithm, we frequently use a special case that the input cycle packing is a mod-
k
-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}
of
G[V]
. We have a good approximation ratio for this special case and a good algorithm to find a mod-
k
-cycle packing.
By Lemma
21
and the previous analysis, we can get that

Corollary 22
.

Given a mod-
k
-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}
of
G[V]
as a part of the input, for unit-demand
k
-CVRP with any
k\geq 3
, the EX-ITP algorithm can use
O(n^{2})
time to output a feasible solution with a weight of at most
(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k})
.

5
Applications of the EX-ITP Algorithm

In this section, we demonstrate that the EX-ITP algorithm can be used to design improved approximation algorithms for splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP. We show as examples, the approximation ratio can be significantly improved from
1.792

[
31
]
to
3/2=1.500
for 3-CVRP, and from
1.750

[
4
]
to
5/3<1.667
for 4-CVRP. At last, we show that the approximation ratio of 4-CVRP can be further improved to
3/2=1.500
based on a unique structural property.

5.1
Unit-demand and Splittable 3-CVRP

There are little improvements for 3-CVRP in the last 20 years. Only recently an improved result for the general weighted
k
-set cover problem
[
31
]
leads to the current best result for 3-CVRP. We show that we can easily improve the ratio by using EX-ITP. Our algorithm computes a minimum cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{*}
of
G[V]
, and then calls EX-ITP on
\mathcal{C}^{*}
.

Lemma 23
.

For unit-demand 3-CVRP, there is a polynomial-time algorithm that can generate a solution with a weight of at most
\Delta+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{*})
.

Proof.

Our algorithm first computes a minimum cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{*}
of
G[V]
in polynomial time, and then
call the EX-ITP algorithm on
\mathcal{C}^{*}
.

Next, we analyze the quality of the solution. Since
|C|\geq 3
for each cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}^{*}
, it is easy to get that

g=\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}^{*}}\frac{\lceil|C|/3\rceil}{|C|}\leq\max_{|C|\geq 3}\frac{\lceil|C|/3\rceil}{|C|}=\frac{\lceil 4/3\rceil}{4}=\frac{1}{2}.

By Lemma
21
, the weight of the solution is at most
\Delta+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{*})
.
∎

By Lemmas
13
and
17
, we have

\Delta+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{*})\leq\left(\chi+\frac{1}{2}\right)w(I^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\min\{2(1-\chi),1\}w(I^{*})\leq\frac{3}{2}w(I^{*}).

Note that we have
\chi=\frac{1}{2}
in the worst case. Therefore, we can get that

Theorem 24
.

For splittable and unit-demand 3-CVRP, there is a
\frac{3}{2}
-approximation algorithm.

5.2
Unit-demand and Splittable 4-CVRP

The idea of the algorithm is similar.
However, we will first construct a good mod-2-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2}
on
V
, instead of using a minimum cycle packing on
V
, and then call the EX-ITP algorithm.

We compute the mod-2-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2}
in this way: first find a minimum perfect matching
\mathcal{M}^{*}
in graph
G[V]
, then find a minimum perfect matching
\mathcal{M}^{**}
in graph
G[V]\setminus\mathcal{M}^{*}
, and then let

\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2}=\mathcal{M}^{*}\cup\mathcal{M}^{**}
.
It is easy to see that
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2}
is a
mod-2-cycle packing without 2-cycles since
\mathcal{M}^{*}

and
\mathcal{M}^{**}
are two edge-disjoint perfect matchings of the graph. Recall that we use
\mathcal{C}_{4}^{*}
to denote a minimum 4-cycle packing of
G[V]
. We have the following lemma.

Lemma 25
.

w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2})\leq w(\mathcal{C}_{4}^{*})
.

Proof.

We first prove the following property.

Claim 26
.

Given a minimum 4-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}
and a minimum perfect matching
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, there is a way to color edges in
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}
with red and blue such that

(1)

the blues (resp., red) edges form a perfect matching
\mathcal{M}_{b}
(resp.,
\mathcal{M}_{r}
);

(2)

\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}=\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}_{r}
;

(3)

\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}
is a mod-2-cycle packing without 2-cycles.

Proof.

For each 4-cycle
C=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{1})
in
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}
, we color the four edges in it by considering three cases.

Case 1.
|C\cap\mathcal{M}^{*}|=0
:
We color the edges
(v_{1},v_{2})
and
(v_{3},v_{4})
with blue and the edges
(v_{1},v_{4})
and
(v_{2},v_{3})
with red. Now blue edges (resp., red edges) are vertex-disjoint.

Case 2.
|C\cap\mathcal{M}^{*}|=1
:
Without loss of generality, we assume
C\cap\mathcal{M}^{*}=\{(v_{1},v_{2})\}
. Then, we color the edges
(v_{1},v_{2})
and
(v_{3},v_{4})
with red and the edges
(v_{1},v_{4})
and
(v_{2},v_{3})
with blue.

Case 3.
|C\cap\mathcal{M}^{*}|=2
:
Without loss of generality, we assume
C\cap\mathcal{M}^{*}=\{(v_{1},v_{2}),(v_{3},v_{4})\}
. Then, we color the edges
(v_{1},v_{2})
and
(v_{3},v_{4})
with red and the edges
(v_{1},v_{4})
and
(v_{2},v_{3})
with blue.

It is easy to see that the set of blues edges
\mathcal{M}_{b}
and the set of red edges
\mathcal{M}_{r}
are two perfect matchings and
\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}_{r}=\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}
. Moreover, we know that
\mathcal{M}_{b}\cap\mathcal{M}^{*}=\emptyset
, and hence
\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}
is a mod-2-cycle packing without 2-cycles. Thus, the claim holds.
∎

By the claim, we get that

w(\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*})\leq w(\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}_{r})=w(\mathcal{C}_{4}^{*}).

Since the mod-2-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2}=\mathcal{M}^{*}\cup\mathcal{M}^{**}
is the minimum mod-2-cycle packing containing the minimum perfect matching
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, we have that

w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2})=w(\mathcal{M}^{*}\cup\mathcal{M}^{**})\leq w(\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}).

Thus,
w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2})\leq w(\mathcal{C}_{4}^{*})
and the lemma holds.
∎

Lemma 27
.

For unit-demand 4-CVRP, there is a polynomial-time algorithm that can generate a solution with a weight of at most
\frac{2}{3}\Delta+\frac{2}{3}w(\mathcal{C}_{4}^{*})
.

Proof.

We call the EX-ITP algorithm on
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2}
. Since
|C|
is even and
|C|\geq 4
for each cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2}
, we can get that

g=\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2}}\frac{\lceil|C|/4\rceil}{|C|}\leq\max_{|C|\in\{4,6,\dots\}}\frac{\lceil|C|/4\rceil}{|C|}=\frac{\lceil 6/4\rceil}{6}=\frac{1}{3}.

By Lemmas
21
and
25
, we know that the weight of the solution is at most
\frac{2}{3}\Delta+\frac{2}{3}w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod 2})\leq\frac{2}{3}\Delta+\frac{2}{3}w(\mathcal{C}_{4}^{*})
.
∎

By Lemmas
13
and
17
, we have

\frac{2}{3}\Delta+\frac{2}{3}w(\mathcal{C}_{4}^{*})\leq\frac{2}{3}(\chi+1)w(I^{*})+\frac{2}{3}\min\{(2-2\chi),1\}w(I^{*})\leq\frac{5}{3}w(I^{*}).

Note that we have
\chi=\frac{1}{2}
in the worst case. Therefore, we can get that

Theorem 28
.

For splittable and unit-demand 4-CVRP, there is a
\frac{5}{3}
-approximation algorithm.

For even
k>4
we did not find similar good cycle packings (based on a minimum matching).

5.3
A Further Improvement on Unit-demand and Splittable 4-CVRP

Note that we can get a solution with a weight of at most
\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\frac{3}{4}w(\mathcal{C}_{4}^{*})
for unit-demand 4-CVRP by calling the EX-ITP algorithm on a minimum 4-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}

as mentioned before. Surprisingly, in this subsection, we show how to obtain a solution with the same upper bound in polynomial time without using

3/2
-approximation algorithm, which even matches the approximation ratio for unit-demand 3-CVRP.

The claim in Lemma
25
can be strengthened as follows based on a similar odd cycle elimination technique in

Lemma 29
.

Given a minimum 4-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}
and a minimum perfect matching
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, there is a way to color edges in
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}
with red and blue such that

(1)

the blues (resp., red) edges form a perfect matching
\mathcal{M}_{b}
(resp.,
\mathcal{M}_{r}

(2)

\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}=\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}_{r}

(3)

\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}

Proof.

Previously, we have shown that (1) and (2) holds, and
\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}

Then, we modify the cycle packing
\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}
so that (1), (2), and (3) hold. By the previous coloring, we know that each cycle of
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}
contains two blue edges and two red edges. Two blue/red edges are called
matched
if they fall on the same cycle of
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}
.

Consider a cycle
C_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}
with a minimum length not divisible by 4. Since
\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}
is a cycle packing without 2-cycles and the length of every cycle is divisible by 2, the number of blue edges on

is odd. Hence, there must be a blue edge
e_{1}
such that its matched blue edge
e_{3}
falls on a different cycle
C_{j}\in\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}^{*}
. Moreover, there are two red edges
e_{2}
and
e_{3}
sharing common vertices with them. See Figure

for an illustration.

Consider a multi-graph
G^{\prime}=G[V]/\mathcal{M}^{*}

in

w(v_{0},u^{\prime})+w(e_{i})+w(u,v)+w(e_{j})+w(v^{\prime},v_{0}),w(v_{0},u^{\prime})+w(e_{i})+w(u,v^{\prime})+w(e_{j})+w(v,v_{0}),

w(v_{0},u)+w(e_{i})+w(u,v)+w(e_{j})+w(v^{\prime},v_{0}),w(v_{0},u)+w(e_{i})+w(u,v^{\prime})+w(e_{j})+w(v,v_{0}),

which measure the weights of tours:
(v_{0},u^{\prime},u,v,v^{\prime},v_{0})
,
(v_{0},u^{\prime},u,v^{\prime},v,v_{0})
,
(v_{0},u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime},v_{0})
,
(v_{0},u,u^{\prime},v^{\prime},v,v_{0})

Then, a matching
\mathcal{M}
in
G^{\prime}
corresponds to a solution of unit demand 4-CVRP with an augmented weight of
\widetilde{w}(\mathcal{M})

G^{\prime}
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}^{**}
, has the following property.

Lemma 30
.

\widetilde{w}(\mathcal{M}^{**})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\frac{3}{4}w(\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
29
,
\mathcal{M}^{*}\cup\mathcal{M}_{b}
is a mod-4-cycle packing. So, the number of blue edges on each cycle
C\in\mathcal{M}^{*}\cup\mathcal{M}_{b}
is even. All blue edges in
\mathcal{M}_{b}
can be decomposed into two matchings
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
in
G[V]/\mathcal{M}^{*}

\widetilde{w}(\mathcal{M}_{1})+\widetilde{w}(\mathcal{M}_{2})=\Delta+2w(\mathcal{M}^{*})+w(\mathcal{M}_{b})
. We have
\widetilde{w}(\mathcal{M}^{**})\leq\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{w}(\mathcal{M}_{1})+\widetilde{w}(\mathcal{M}_{2}))=\frac{1}{2}\Delta+w(\mathcal{M}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{M}_{b})

\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4}=\mathcal{M}_{b}\cup\mathcal{M}_{r}
and
w(\mathcal{M}^{*})\leq\min\{w(\mathcal{M}_{b}),w(\mathcal{M}_{r})\}\leq\frac{1}{2}(w(\mathcal{M}_{b})+w(\mathcal{M}_{r}))=\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4})

w(\mathcal{M}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{M}_{b})\leq\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{M}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{M}_{r})+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{M}_{b})\leq\frac{3}{4}w(\mathcal{C}^{*}_{4})

.

For unit-demand 4-CVRP, there is a polynomial-time algorithm that can generate a solution with a weight of at most
\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\frac{3}{4}w(\mathcal{C}_{4}^{*})
.

Note that we have
\chi=\frac{1}{2}

Theorem 32
.

For splittable and unit-demand 4-CVRP, there is a
\frac{3}{2}
-approximation algorithm.

6
An Improvement for Splittable
k
-CVRP

In this section, we show how to use the ITP algorithm and the EX-ITP algorithm with the new lower bounds in Section
3
to improve splittable
k
-CVRP with larger
k
.
Given an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for metric TSP, where
1\leq\alpha\leq 3/2
, we will prove a ratio of
\alpha+1-\alpha/k-\Theta(1/k)
for splittable
k
-CVRP, which improves the ratio of
\alpha+1-\alpha/k-\max\{\Omega(1/k^{3}),\varepsilon\}

[
15
,
16
]
for some small
k
.

To get the claimed ratio, we need to make a trade-off among three algorithms.
Recall that if the upper bound of
\Delta
is tight, i.e.,
\chi=1
, we get
w(H_{CS})\leq w(I^{*})
and
w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}^{*})=0
. So, apart from using an
\alpha
-approximate Hamiltonian cycle
H
, we may also consider the Hamiltonian cycle
H_{CS}

[
17
,
51
]
and a 2-approximate mod-
k
-cycle packing
[
28
]
. We apply the mod-
k
-cycle packing to call the EX-ITP algorithm and the two Hamiltonian cycles to call the AG-ITP algorithm, and make a trade-off among them.
Let
\rho_{1}(\chi)
,
\rho_{2}(\chi)
, and
\rho_{3}(\chi)
denote the ratio of the three algorithms, respectively. They are functions on the parameter
\chi
. Our final ratio is
\max_{0\leq\chi\leq 1}\min\{\rho_{1}(\chi),\ \rho_{2}(\chi),\ \rho_{3}(\chi)\}
.

Our first algorithm is to call the EX-ITP algorithm on a mod-
k
-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}
on
V
. Note that the 2-approximate mod-
k
-cycle packing
[
28
]
is obtained by doubling a mod-
k
-tree packing and then shortcutting. We may find a minimum matching on odd-degree vertices in the mod-
k
-tree packing and then shortcutting like the Christofides-Serdyukov algorithm
[
17
,
51
]
. We use the following three steps to compute
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}
.

Step 1.
Compute a mod-
k
-tree packing
\mathcal{T}_{\bmod k}
of
G[V]
using the primal-dual algorithm in
[
28
]
.

Step 2.
Find a minimum matching
\mathcal{M}
on odd-degree vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{\bmod k}
.

Step 3.
Construct a mod-
k
-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}
of
G[V]
from
\mathcal{T}_{\bmod k}\cup\mathcal{M}
by shortcutting.

The second algorithm is to call the AG-ITP algorithm on any
\alpha
-approximate Hamiltonian cycle on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
. The third algorithm is to call the AG-ITP algorithm on the Hamiltonian cycle
H_{CS}
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
.

We first analyze some properties of the first algorithm. The mod-
k
-tree packing computed in the first step
has the following property.

Lemma 33

(

]
)
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that can generate a mod-

-tree packing
\mathcal{T}_{\bmod k}
of

.

In Steps 2 and 3, we generate a mod-

-cycle packing
based on the mod-

-tree packing
\mathcal{T}_{\bmod k}
and a

\mathcal{M}
on odd-degree vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{\bmod k}
using the Christifides-Serdykov method for TSP. Despite it may not guarantee a constant approximation ratio for the minimum mod-

-cycle packing problem, it has a better property for CVRP.

Lemma 34
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that generates a mod-
k
-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}
of

such that
w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k})\leq w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}w(H^{*})

After finding the mod-
k
-tree packing
\mathcal{T}_{\bmod k}
of
G[V]
, we go to find a minimum matching
\mathcal{M}
on odd-degree vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{\bmod k}

Then, we can get a set of components, where in each component the number of vertices is divisible by

k
-cycle packing
\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}
such that
w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k})\leq w(\mathcal{T}_{\bmod k})+w(\mathcal{M})\leq w(\mathcal{C}_{\bmod k}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}w(H^{*})
.
∎

Now, we are ready to analyze the three algorithms.

Theorem 35

Suppose there is an
\alpha

Proof.

7
A Further Improvement for Splittable
k
-CVRP

The current approximation ratio for metric TSP is about

\alpha=3/2
. The approximation ratio of splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP is
5/2-3.5/k=5/2-\Theta(1/k)
in the previous section.
In this section, we will further improve the ratio to
5/2-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k})
.

Our motivation is that the approximation ratio
5/2-3.5/k
in the previous section is obtained with
\chi=0
in the worst case. That is, all home-edges in the optimal solution
I^{*}
have a weight of 0 in the worst case. Recall that each tour of
I^{*}
contains two home-edges and the lower bound on
MST
in Lemma
14
is obtained by deleting the more weighted home-edge in each tour of
I^{*}
. Since
\chi=0
in the worst case, we have
w(I^{*})\geq\mbox{MST}
by Lemma
14
. So, by deleting a zero-weighted home-edge, we gain no revenues. We may obtain a refined lower bound on
MST
by deleting the most weighted edge in each tour, which is clearly better than just deleting a zero-weighted home-edge. Based on this, we give refined lower bounds based on
MST
as well as
\Delta
to combine them, and prove an approximation ratio of
5/2-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k})
.

Our algorithm is simply to call the classic AG-ITP algorithm on the Hamiltonian cycle
H_{CS}
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
. Using the Hamiltonian cycle
H_{CS}
, by Lemmas
15
and
18
, the AG-ITP algorithm can generate an itinerary
I
with

w(I)\leq(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)w(H_{CS})\leq(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)\mbox{MST}+(1/2)(1-1/k)w(H^{*}).

(1)

To prove that the ratio is
\frac{5}{2}-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k})
, we show that
\frac{2}{k}\Delta+(1-\frac{1}{k})\mbox{MST}\leq(2-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k}))w(I^{*})
.

Now, we analyze the structure in more details. By Assumption
11
,
I^{*}
consists of a set of
(k+1)
-cycles.
Consider an arbitrary
(k+1)
-cycle
C=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{k},v_{0})
in
I^{*}
.
First, we use
\Delta_{C}
to denote the sum of the weights of edges between vertices
v_{0}
and
v_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
, i.e.,
\Delta_{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w(v_{0},v_{i})
. Note that
\Delta=\sum_{C\in I^{*}}\Delta_{C}
.
Then, we use
T_{C}
to denote the spanning tree of
G[C]
obtained by deleting the most weighted edge in
C
, i.e.,
w(T_{C})=w(C)-\max_{0\leq i\leq k}w(v_{i},v_{(i+1)\bmod(k+1)})
.
After we delete the most weighted edge for each cycle
C\in I^{*}
, the remaining graph is a spanning tree of
G
. So, we have
\mbox{MST}\leq\sum_{C\in I^{*}}w(T_{C})
. Moreover, since
w(I^{*})=\sum_{C\in I^{*}}w(C)
, it is sufficient to prove
\frac{2}{k}\Delta_{C}+(1-\frac{1}{k})w(T_{C})\leq(2-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k}))w(C)
. We remark that
\max\frac{\frac{2}{k}\Delta_{C}+(1-\frac{1}{k})w(T_{C})}{w(C)}
can be solved by the following LP.

maximize

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{2}{k}\Delta_{C}+(1-\frac{1}{k})w(T_{C})}{1}

subject to

\displaystyle w(C)=\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}x_{i,(i+1)\bmod(k+1)}=\ 1,

\displaystyle\Delta_{C}=\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{0,i},

\displaystyle w(T_{C})\leq\

\displaystyle w(C)-x_{i,(i+1)\bmod(k+1)},\quad

\displaystyle\forall\ i\in\{0,1,\dots,k\},

\displaystyle 0\leq\

\displaystyle x_{i,j}\leq\ x_{i,t}+x_{j,t},\quad

\displaystyle\forall\ i,j,t\in\{0,1,\dots,k\}.

In this LP, we use
x_{i,j}
to denote
w(v_{i},v_{j})
. The first constraint follows from that the weight of the
(k+1)
-cycle
C=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{k},v_{0})
is
\sum_{i=0}^{k}w(v_{i},v_{(i+1)\bmod(k+1)})
and w.l.o.g. assumed to 1, the second from
\Delta_{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w(v_{0},v_{i})
, the third from
w(T_{C})=w(C)-\max_{0\leq i\leq k}w(v_{i},v_{(i+1)\bmod(k+1)})
, and the last from the triangle inequality. Let
l=\lceil\frac{\sqrt{2k-1}-1}{2}\rceil
.
In the following, we will prove that the value of this LP is
2-\frac{2l^{2}+k-1}{2kl}
, and therefore, we obtain an approximation ratio of
5/2-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k})
. Our proof is based on generalizing the concept of home-edges.

Lemma 36
.

When
k\geq 3
, for any cycle
C\in I^{*}
, we have that

\frac{2}{k}\Delta_{C}+\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)w(T_{C})\leq\left(2-\frac{2l^{2}+k-1}{2kl}\right)w(C),

where
l=\lceil\frac{\sqrt{2k-1}-1}{2}\rceil
.

Proof.

The details can be seen in Section
14
.
∎

Next, we are ready to analyze the AG-ITP algorithm.

Theorem 37
.

For splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP, the AG-ITP algorithm admits an approximation ratio of
\frac{5}{2}-\frac{2l^{2}+k+l-1}{2kl}<\frac{5}{2}-\sqrt{2/k}
, where
l=\lceil\frac{\sqrt{2k-1}-1}{2}\rceil
.

Proof.

Using the Hamiltonian cycle
H_{CS}
, by (
1
), the AG-ITP algorithm can output a solution with a weight of at most
(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)\mbox{MST}+(1/2)(1-1/k)w(H^{*})
.
By Lemmas
12
and
36
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{2}{k}\Delta+\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\mbox{MST}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)w(H^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C\in I^{*}}\left(\frac{2}{k}\Delta_{C}+\frac{k-1}{k}w(T_{C})\right)+\frac{k-1}{2k}w(I^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C\in I^{*}}\left(2-\frac{2l^{2}+k-1}{2kl}\right)w(C)+\frac{k-1}{2k}w(I^{*})

\displaystyle=\left(2-\frac{2l^{2}+k-1}{2kl}\right)\sum_{C\in I^{*}}w(C)+\frac{k-1}{2k}w(I^{*})

\displaystyle=\left(2-\frac{2l^{2}+k-1}{2kl}+\frac{k-1}{2k}\right)w(I^{*})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{5}{2}-\frac{2l^{2}+k+l-1}{2kl}\right)w(I^{*}).

To show the ratio
\frac{5}{2}-\frac{2l^{2}+k+l-1}{2kl}<\frac{5}{2}-\sqrt{2/k}
holds for any
k\geq 3
, it is sufficient to prove

\frac{2l^{2}+k+l-1}{2kl}>\sqrt{2/k}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2l^{2}-(2\sqrt{2k}-1)l+k-1>0.

The latter holds since the discriminant of the quadratic equation satisfies
(2\sqrt{2k}-1)^{2}-8(k-1)=9-4\sqrt{2k}<0
for any
k\geq 3
.
∎

In the previous section, when
\alpha=3/2
and
k>5
, the approximation ratio is
5/2-3.5/k
. It is easy to see that the new result
5/2-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k})
in Theorem
37
is strictly better for any
k>5
, and is also better than
5/2-1.005/3000-1.5/k
for any
k\leq 1.7\times 10^{7}
. Please see Table
1
.
When
3\leq k\leq 5
, the approximation ratio equals
2-1/k
, which is tight for this algorithm since it has been shown
[
45
]
that the approximation ratio of the AG-ITP algorithm is at least
2-1/k
in general metrics even using an optimal Hamiltonian cycle.

We also remark that we only consider
\alpha=3/2
in this section. In fact, the approximation ratios for
7/6\leq\alpha<3/2
and
k>5
(which are based on the Hamiltonian cycle
H_{CS}
) in Section

may be further improved in a similar way by using refined lower bounds based on

and

provided in this section.
However, the analysis is sophisticated
and a significant improvement for metric TSP seems hard. So, we do not present the details here.

8
ITP for Unsplittable CVRP

From this section, we consider unsplittable CVRP.
The ITP-based algorithms play an important role in solving splittable
k
-CVRP. A natural idea is to extend ITP-based algorithms for unsplittable
k
-CVRP.
In order to generate a feasible solution without splitting the demand of one customer into two tours, we need to modify the initial solution obtained by ITP.
One of the most famous algorithms for unsplittable CVRP is the ITP-based algorithm introduced by Altinkemer and Gavish
[
2
]
.
The main idea of the algorithm is as follows.
Given a Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
as a part of the input, the algorithm first uses the AG-ITP algorithm to generate a solution for unit-demand
k/2
-CVRP with a weight of at most
(4/k)\Delta+(1-2/k)w(H)
. Then, in the initial solution, if the demand of one customer is delivered by two tours, then set all the demand to one tour to get a feasible solution for unsplittable
k
-CVRP without increasing the weight of the solution. By using Lemmas
12
and
13
, we have

(4/k)\Delta+(1-2/k)\alpha w(H^{*})\leq 2w(I^{*})+(1-2/k)\alpha w(I^{*})=(\alpha+2-2\alpha/k)w(I^{*}).

The approximation ratio is
\alpha+2-2\alpha/k
. Note that in this algorithm
k
should be an even number. For odd
k
, there are two possible ways to solve it.
The first one is to double the capacity and all the demands and call the above algorithm for
2k
. For this case, we may get a ratio of
(\alpha+2-\alpha/k)
.
The second idea is to solve unit-demand
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
-CVRP in the first step. For the second method, we may get an even worse ratio.

Next, we give a refined algorithm for unsplittable CVRP based on ITP.
The refined AG-UITP algorithm has two advances: it has an uniform form for both odd and even
k
, and the approximation ratio will be improved.

A customer is called
big
(resp.,
small
) if the demand of it is greater than (resp., less than or equal to)
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
. Let
V_{big}
(resp.,
V_{mall}
) denote the set of big customers (resp., small customers). Define
\Delta_{big}=\sum_{v_{i}\in V_{big}}d_{i}w(v_{0},v_{i})
, and

\Delta_{small}=\sum_{v_{i}\in V_{small}}d_{i}w(v_{0},v_{i})
.

The refined AG-UITP algorithm works as follows. First, for each big customer, we assign a single trivial tour. Second, we compute a Hamiltonian cycle
H_{small}
on small customers and
v_{0}
by shortcutting all the big customers in a Hamiltonian cycle
H
of
G
. Third, we call the AG-ITP algorithm for unit-demand
(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)
-CVRP on
H_{small}
. Last,
if the demand of one customer is delivered by two tours in the solution of the third step, then adjust all the demand to one tour: if one customer
v_{1}
is split into two tours, say
T_{1}=(v_{0},\dots,v_{1},v_{0})
, and
T_{2}=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{0})
, we let
T_{2}
deliver all the demand for
v_{1}
without exceeding the capacity and delete
v_{1}
from
T_{1}
; if
one customer
v_{1}
is split into three tours, say
T_{1}=(v_{0},\dots,v_{1},v_{0})
,
T_{2}=(v_{0},v_{1},v_{0})
, and
T_{3}=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{0})
, we take
T_{2}
as a trivial tour delivering all the demand of
v_{1}
, and remove
v_{1}
from
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.

Lemma 38
.

Given a Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
as a part of the input, for unsplittable
k
-CVRP with any
k\geq 3
, the refined AG-UITP algorithm uses
O(nk)
time to output a feasible solution of weight at most

\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)w(H).

Proof.

First, we analyze the quality of the solution before modifying the demands of customers.

For all big customers, we assign a single trivial tour. The total weight is at most

\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta_{big}.

For all small customers, we can get a Hamiltonian cycle
H_{small}
by shortcutting all the big customers in
H
. Then, we use the AG-ITP algorithm with a capacity of
\lceil k/2\rceil+1
. By Lemma
18
, the total weight is at most

\frac{2}{\lceil k/2\rceil+1}\Delta_{small}+(1-\frac{1}{\lceil k/2\rceil+1})w(H_{small}).

Note that
w(H_{small})\leq 2\Delta_{small}
and
w(H_{small})\leq w(H)
by the triangle inequality. We have

\frac{2}{\lceil k/2\rceil+1}\Delta_{small}+(1-\frac{1}{\lceil k/2\rceil+1})w(H_{small})\leq\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta_{small}+(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})w(H_{small})\leq\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta_{small}+(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})w(H).

Therefore, the total weight is bounded by

\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}(\Delta_{big}+\Delta_{small})+(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})w(H)=\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})w(H).

Next, we analyze the modification in the last step of our algorithm. If a customer is split into three tours,
the modification clearly erases an infeasible case without increasing the weight. If a customer
v_{i}
is
split into two consecutive tours
T_{1}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{2}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
in the clockwise direction of the Hamiltonian cycle,
we modify them by letting
T_{1}^{*}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{0})
and
T_{2}^{*}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
.
Then, we show the new itinerary is a feasible solution with a non-increasing weight for unsplittable
k
-CVRP.

By the triangle inequality, it holds

w(T_{1}^{*})+w(T_{2}^{*})\leq w(T_{1})+w(T_{2}).

So, the weight is non-increasing. To prove the feasibility, we only need to prove that the total demand in
T_{1}^{*}
and
T_{2}^{*}
is bounded by
k
.
For
T_{1}^{*}
, the total demand becomes less and hence is feasible. For
T_{2}^{*}
, the total demand is bigger than the total demand of
T_{2}
. Note that the tour
T_{2}
has a total demand of at most
\lceil k/2\rceil+1
. We only need to analyze the newly added demand. Note that any small customer has a demand of at most
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
. Thus, the newly added demand is at most
\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1
. Therefore, for
T_{2}^{*}
, the total demand is bounded by

(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)+(\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1)=k.

Thus, after modifying all two consecutive conflict tours, we can get a feasible itinerary with a non-increasing weight.
∎

Using an
\alpha
-approximate Hamiltonian cycle on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
, by Lemmas
12
and
38
, the refined AG-UITP algorithm satisfies that

\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})\alpha w(H^{*})\leq(\alpha+\frac{k}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}-\frac{\alpha}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})w(I^{*}).

Hence, the approximation ratio is
\alpha+\frac{k}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}-\frac{\alpha}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}
for any
k\geq 3
, which is even better than the ratio
\alpha+2-2\alpha/k-\Omega(1/k^{3})
in
[
16
]
.

In the refined AG-UITP algorithm, we call the AG-ITP algorithm with a capacity of
\lceil k/2\rceil+1
on the Hamiltonian cycle of small customers and then we can modify them by adjusting the demands. The idea can be used on a cycle packing of small customers. We can call the EX-ITP algorithm with a capacity of
\lceil k/2\rceil+1
on the cycle packing of small customers and then modify the conflicting tours in the same way. The algorithm is called the EX-UITP algorithm. By Lemma
21
, we can get

Lemma 39
.

Given a cycle packing
\mathcal{C}
of
G[V]
or
G[V\cup\{v_{0}\}]
as a part of the input, assuming all customers are small customers, for unsplittable-demand
k
-CVRP with any
k\geq 3
, the EX-UITP algorithm uses
O(n^{2})
time to output a feasible solution with a weight of at most
2g\Delta+(1-g)w(\mathcal{C})
, where
g=\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\lceil|C|/(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)\rceil/|C|
.

The lemma can be used to analyze the algorithms based on a cycle packing
\mathcal{C}
, where we may assign a trivial tour to each big customer and then call the EX-UITP algorithm on the cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
of small customers obtained by shortcutting all big customers in
\mathcal{C}
.
However, we remark that if we simply assign a trivial tour to each big customer, the structure of obtained cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
may become extremely bad, e.g., it may become a cycle that contains a unit-demand customer only. So, for unsplittable
k
-CVRP, algorithms based on a cycle packing are harder to design and analyze.

but Lemma

2g\Delta_{C}+(1-g)w(C)
, where
g=\lceil(|C|-1)/(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)\rceil/|C|
.

Proof.

Let
m=g|C|=\lceil(|C|-1)/(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)\rceil
. Note that
m\geq 2
since
|C|>k\geq 4
.
We will show that we can assign
m
tours on
|C|
with a weight of at most
2g\Delta_{C}+(1-g)w(C)
.

The first tour has a capacity of
\lceil k/2\rceil+2
, the middle
m-2
tours has a capacity of
\lceil k/2\rceil+1
, and the last tour has a capacity of
|C|-(\lceil k/2\rceil+2)-(m-2)(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)
. Note that
0\leq|C|-(\lceil k/2\rceil+2)-(m-2)(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)\leq\lceil k/2\rceil+1
since
(|C|-1)/(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)\leq m=\lceil(|C|-1)/(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)\rceil<(|C|-1)/(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)+1
. The total capacity is exactly
|C|
. Hence, we can generate
m
such tours on
C
. Similar to the HR-ITP algorithm, by considering
|C|
solutions and selecting the best one, we can get a solution on
|C|
with a weight of at most
(2m/|C|)\Delta_{C}+(1-m/|C|)w(C)=2g\Delta_{C}+(1-g)w(C)
.

Then, we show these tours can be modified to a feasible solution with a non-increasing weight.

The idea is similar to the analysis in Lemma
38
. Note that the tour has a capacity of at most
\lceil k/2\rceil+1
in Lemma
38
.
However, in the
m
tours, the first tour, denoted by
T_{1}
, has a capacity of
\lceil k/2\rceil+2
, while the other tours, denoted by
T_{2},\dots,T_{m}
in the clockwise direction of the cycle
C
, have a capacity of less than or equal to
\lceil k/2\rceil+1
. The main difference is due to the tour
T_{1}
. We will use a similar modification in Lemma
38
while making sure the tour
T_{1}
is feasible.

We consider the following three cases.

Case 1.
There is no tour having a conflict with
T_{1}
: we can simply modify every conflict tours
T_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
by letting
T^{*}_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
. The modification is in the clockwise direction, which is the same as that in Lemma
38
. In this case, the tour
T_{1}
does not have an effect on modification. For other tours, by a similar argument in Lemma
38
, it is easy to see the modification is feasible.

If there exist conflict tours with
T_{1}
, the modification in the clockwise direction in Lemma
38
may cause the tour
T_{1}
infeasible. For example, we let
T_{m}=(v_{0},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{0})
where the delivered demands of
v_{i}
in

and
T_{1}
are
\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1
and 1, respectively. Using the same modification in Lemma
38
, we have
T^{*}_{m}=(v_{0},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{0})
and
T^{*}_{1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{0})
. The original demand of
T_{1}
is
\lceil k/2\rceil+2
and the newly added demand is
\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1
. The total demand is
k+1
and hence infeasible. Actually, in this case, we can modify the tours
T_{m}
and
T_{1}
in the counterclockwise direction by letting
T^{*}_{m}=(v_{0},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T^{*}_{1}=(v_{0},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{0})
. Hence, we will consider two following cases.

Case 2.1
There is only one tour having a conflict with
T_{1}
: if the tour is
T_{2}
, we modify every conflict tours
T_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
in the clockwise direction by letting
T^{*}_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
; if the tour is
T_{m}
, we modify every conflict tours
T_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
in the counterclockwise direction by letting
T^{*}_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
. The directions of these two modifications are opposite. The modified tour
T_{1}
will always have a less demand and hence will always be feasible. For other tours, by a similar argument in Lemma
38
, we know the modification is safe.

Case 2.2
There are two tours having conflicts with
T_{1}
: we can simply modify every conflict tours
T_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
in the clockwise direction by letting
T^{*}_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
. The modification is the same with that in Lemma
38
. We only need to prove
T^{*}_{1}
is feasible. The original demand of
T_{1}
is at most
\lceil k/2\rceil+2
. Since
T_{1}
has a conflict with the tour
T_{m}
, the newly added demand of

in the modification is at most
\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1
since all small customers have a demand of at most
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
. Note that
T_{1}
also has a conflict with the tour
T_{2}
, and hence, it reduces at least 1 demand according to the modification. The demand of
T^{*}_{1}
is at most
(\lceil k/2\rceil+2)+(\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1)-1=k
. Hence, the tour
T_{1}
will always be feasible. For other tours, by a similar argument in Lemma
38
, we know the modification is safe.
∎

Lemma 42
.

For unsplittable
k
-CVRP, if there exists a cycle
C
with
|C|>k\geq 4
and there is only one big customer and the demand of it is
\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1
in
C
, we can assign
\lceil|C|/(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)\rceil
tours on
C
with a weight of at most
2g\Delta_{C}+(1-g)w(C)
, where
g=\lceil|C|/(\lceil k/2\rceil+1)\rceil/|C|
.

Proof.

First, we call the EX-ITP algorithm with a capacity of
\lceil k/2\rceil+1
on
C
. By Lemma
21
, we know the weight is at most
2g\Delta_{C}+(1-g)w(C)
.

Next, we modify the conflict tours. Compared with Lemma
39
the only difference is that there is one big customer with a demand of
\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1
. We only need to prove the tours involving the big customer can be modified into feasible tours. We will consider the following two cases.

Case 1.
The big customer is not split: we can simply modify every conflict tours
T_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
in the clockwise direction by letting
T^{*}_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
. The modification is the same with that in Lemma
38
. In this case, the big customer does not have an effect on modification. Hence, the modification is feasible.

Case 2.
The big customer is split in two tours. Assume that the big customer is
v_{1}
and is split in tours
T_{1}=(v_{0},\dots,v_{1},v_{0})
and
T_{2}=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{0})
. The delivered demand of
v_{1}
in
T_{1}
(resp.,
T_{2}
) is denoted by
x_{1}
(resp.,
x_{2}
). If
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
, we modify every conflict tours
T_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
in the clockwise direction by letting
T^{*}_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
. If
x_{1}\geq x_{2}
, we modify every conflict tours
T_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
in the counterclockwise direction by letting
T^{*}_{l}=(v_{0},v_{h},\dots,v_{i},v_{0})
and
T_{l+1}=(v_{0},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{j},v_{0})
. The directions of these two modifications are opposite. Due to symmetry, we only consider
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
. The big customer is entirely contained in the tour
T^{*}_{2}
after modification. We only need to prove

is feasible. The demand of the original tour

has a demand of at most

. The newly added demand is

and

. Therefore, the demand of

is at most

and hence feasible.
∎

10
Improvements on Unsplittable 3-CVRP and 4-CVRP

In this section, we demonstrate that the techniques on cycle packings in the above two sections can be used to improve unsplittable 3-CVRP and 4-CVRP. The cycle packings that we will use follow from the ideas in Section

10.1
Unsplittable 3-CVRP

We improve the ratio from
1.792

[
31
]
to
3/2=1.500
for unsplittable 3-CVRP. Note that this ratio is already the ratio for splittable 3-CVRP. Any further improvement on unsplittable 3-CVRP will also improve splittable 3-CVRP.

By Assumption
10
, no customer’s demand is greater than 2. Thus, the demand of each customer is either 1 or 2.
Our algorithm contains three steps.

First, we find a cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{**}
on all customers
V
, where
\left|C_{i}\right|=\sum_{v_{j}\in C_{i}}d_{j}\geq 3
for each
C_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{**}
. The cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{**}
is computed in the following way: we consider the instance as a unit-demand instance
G^{\prime}
by taking each customer
v_{i}
as
d_{i}
customers with the unit-demand as the same place. Then, we use the polynomial time algorithm in
[
34
]
to find a minimum cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{*}
in
G^{\prime}[V^{\prime}]
. Note that
\mathcal{C}^{*}
may not be a
cycle packing for the original graph
G[V]
. For a vertex
v_{i}
, there will be
2d_{i}
edges incident on it in
\mathcal{C}^{*}
. So, by shortcutting cycles in
\mathcal{C}^{*}
, we can get a cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{**}
on
V
such that
w(\mathcal{C}^{**})\leq w(\mathcal{C}^{*})
.

Second, we will deal with all 2-demand customers: if a cycle
C_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{**}
contains no 1-demand customer or at least two 1-demand customers, assign a trivial tour for each 2-demand customer in
C_{i}
and obtain a small cycle
C^{\prime}_{i}
from
C_{i}
by shortcutting all 2-demand customers (having been delivered);
if a cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}^{**}
contains exactly one 1-demand customer
u
, then there is at least one 2-demand customer
u^{\prime}
in
C
, and we assign a tour
T
visiting the two customers
u
and
u^{\prime}
only and a trivial tour for each other 2-demand customer in
C
except
u^{\prime}
. Now we get a cycle packing
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\{C^{\prime}_{i}\}
on all customers that have not been delivered yet. These customers are 1-demand customers.

Third, we apply the EX-ITP algorithm on
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

We analyze the quality of this solution. We partition the tours constructed in the algorithm into three parts.

The first part contains
all trivial tours visiting one 2-demand customer. Let
V_{1}
denote this set of 2-demand customers and
\Delta_{1}=\sum_{v_{i}\in V_{1}}d_{i}w(v_{0},v_{i})=2\sum_{v_{i}\in V_{1}}w(v_{0},v_{i})
.
Thus, the total weight of the tours in the first part is
\Delta_{1}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{**}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{**}
be the set of cycles containing exact one 1-demand customer.
For each cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}^{**}_{2}
, the algorithm will construct a tour
T=(v_{0},u,u^{\prime},v_{0})
visiting one 1-demand customer
u
and one 2-demand customer
u^{\prime}
in
C
in the second step of the algorithm. The second part consists of all these tours, and we let
V_{2}
denote the set of customers visited by these tours. Let
\Delta_{2}=\sum_{v_{i}\in V_{2}}d_{i}w(v_{0},v_{i})
.
The weight of
T_{0}
is
w(v_{0},u)+w(v_{0},u^{\prime})+w(u,u^{\prime})
, where
w(u,u^{\prime})
is at most the half
of
w(C)
. Thus, the total weight of the tours in the second part is at most
\Delta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}^{**}_{2}}w(C)
.

The third part is the tours generated by the EX-ITP algorithm in the last step. Let
V_{3}
denote the set of 1-demand customers visiting in the tours in the third part and
\Delta_{3}=\sum_{v_{i}\in V_{3}}d_{i}w(v_{0},v_{i})=\sum_{v_{i}\in V_{3}}w(v_{0},v_{i})
. Note that for each cycle
C^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
there are at least two 1-demand customers and hence we have
|C^{\prime}|\geq 2
.
By Lemma
21
, we know that the weight of tours in the third part is at most
\Delta_{3}+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
(
g\leq\frac{1}{2}
in Lemma
21
),
where
w(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\leq w(\mathcal{C}^{**}\setminus\mathcal{C}^{**}_{2})
.

Recall that
w(\mathcal{C}^{**})\leq w(\mathcal{C}^{*})
. By adding the three parts together, we know the weight of the solution is at most

\Delta_{1}+\left(\Delta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{**}_{2})\right)+\left(\Delta_{3}+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\right)\leq\Delta+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{**}_{2})+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{**}\setminus\mathcal{C}^{**}_{2})=\Delta+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{**})\leq\Delta+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{*}).

By Lemmas
13
and
17
, we have

\Delta+\frac{1}{2}w(\mathcal{C}^{*})\leq\left(\chi+\frac{1}{2}\right)w(I^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\min\{2(1-\chi),1\}w(I^{*})\leq\frac{3}{2}w(I^{*}).

Note that we have
\chi=\frac{1}{2}
in the worst case. Therefore, we can get that

Theorem 43
.

For unsplittable 3-CVRP, there is a
\frac{3}{2}
-approximation algorithm.

We can see that the algorithm is more complicated than the algorithm for splittable 3-CVRP.

An Improvement for Unsplittable
k
-CVRP

In this section, we improve unsplittable
k
-CVRP with larger

.
Given an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for metric TSP, where
1\leq\alpha\leq 3/2
, we will prove a ratio of
\alpha+1+\ln 2-2\alpha/k-\Theta(1/k)
. Our algorithm will use several techniques.
The first one is the LP-based technique used in
[
26
]
.

11.1
An LP-Based Algorithm

When
k
is a constant, there are at most
n^{k}=n^{O(1)}
different tours, denoted by
\mathcal{T}
.
The idea of the LP-based algorithm is to define a variable
x_{T}
for each tour
T\in\mathcal{T}
, and then solve the following LP in
n^{O(1)}
time.

minimize

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}w(T)\cdot x_{T}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathcal{T}:\\
v\in T\end{subarray}}x_{T}\geq\

\displaystyle 1,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\ v\in V,

\displaystyle x_{T}\geq\

\displaystyle 0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}.

Recall that
k
-CVRP can be reduced to the minimum weight
k
-set cover problem. The idea of the above LP is the same as the LP of
k
-set cover. Each vertex
v\in V
can be seen as an element. Each tour
T\in\mathcal{T}
can be seen as a set which contains all vertices
v\in T
and has a weight of
w(T)
. We need to find a minimum weight collection of sets to cover all vertices.

Next, we give a simple analysis of the LP-based algorithm. The details can be seen in
[
26
]
.

Using a constant
\gamma\geq 0
, we can make a randomized rounding for each tour with a probability of
\min\{1,\gamma\cdot x_{T}\}
.
The chosen tours will form a partial itinerary
I^{\prime}
with an expected weight of
\mathbb{E}[w(I^{\prime})]\leq\gamma\cdot w(I^{*})
. The customer in
I^{\prime}
is denoted by
V^{\prime}
. Then, for the undelivered customers in
V^{\prime\prime}=V\setminus V^{\prime}
, we use the UITP algorithm to compute an itinerary
I^{\prime\prime}
such that
\mathbb{E}[w(I^{\prime\prime})]\leq e^{-\gamma}\cdot(4/k)\Delta+(1-2/k)w(H^{\prime\prime})
where
H^{\prime\prime}
is a Hamiltonian cycle on
V^{\prime\prime}\cup\{v_{0}\}
. Note that given a Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
we can get a Hamiltonian cycle
H^{\prime\prime}
on
V^{\prime\prime}\cup\{v_{0}\}
by shortcutting such that
w(H^{\prime\prime})\leq w(H)
. We have

\mathbb{E}[I]=\mathbb{E}[I^{\prime}\cup I^{\prime\prime}]\leq\gamma\cdot w(I^{*})+e^{-\gamma}\cdot(4/k)\Delta+(1-2/k)w(H).

Combining the LP-based method with the refined AG-UITP algorithm, we obtain an LP-UITP algorithm.

Lemma 46
.

Given a Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
, for unsplittable
k
-CVRP with any
k\geq 3
and any constant
\gamma\geq 0
, the LP-UITP algorithm can use
n^{O(k)}=n^{O(1)}
time to generate a solution with a weight at most

\gamma\cdot w(I^{*})+e^{-\gamma}\cdot\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)w(H).

Note that the LP-UITP algorithm will become the refined AG-UITP algorithm if setting
\gamma=0
.

11.2
Trade-off Between Two Results

Next, we show that by using Lemma
46
and two initial Hamiltonian cycles, we can get the desired ratio by doing a trade-off.
It follows the ideas in Section
6
. We use two initial Hamiltonian cycles
H
and
H_{CS}
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
as the input to run the LP-UITP algorithm, where
H
is an arbitrary
\alpha
-approximate solution to metric TSP and
H_{CS}
is the cycle obtained by the Christofides-Serdyukov algorithm. We choose the better one.
Note that we do not consider the mod-
k
-cycle packing used in Section
6
since for unsplittable
k
-CVRP algorithms based on cycle packings will become complicated to analyze (as we see in Section
10
).

The following theorem shows the ratio of the algorithm.

Theorem 47
.

Given an
\alpha
-approximate Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
with
1\leq\alpha\leq 3/2
, for
k\geq 3
, there is an approximation algorithm for unsplittable
k
-CVRP such that

•

If
3\leq k\leq 5
, the ratio is
\frac{2\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\ln\frac{k}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}
;

•

If
k=6
and
\frac{7}{6}\leq\alpha\leq\frac{3}{2}
, the ratio is
\frac{15}{8}+\ln\frac{4}{3}<2.163
;

•

If
k=7
and
\frac{17}{12}\leq\alpha\leq\frac{3}{2}
, the ratio is
\frac{33}{16}+\ln\frac{4}{3}<2.351
;

•

Otherwise, the ratio is
\frac{(\alpha+1)\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\ln\frac{k-4(\alpha-1)}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}
.

Proof.

Given two Hamiltonian cycles
H
and
H_{CS}
, we can use the LP-UITP algorithm to generate two solutions. We will select the better one.
In the worst case, we assume
\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2})\geq 1
.
We will show that the ratio will be better if
\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2})<1
.

Using the Hamiltonian cycle
H
, by Lemmas
12
,
13
, and
46
, the LP-UITP algorithm can generate an itinerary
I_{1}
such that

\displaystyle w(I_{1})\leq\

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma\geq 0}\left\{\gamma\cdot w(I^{*})+e^{-\gamma}\cdot\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)w(H)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma\geq 0}\left\{\gamma+e^{-\gamma}\cdot\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\left(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)\alpha\right\}w(I^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\left(1+\ln\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\ln\left(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)\alpha\right)w(I^{*}).

The approximation ratio is
1+\ln\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\ln(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2})+(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})\alpha
.

Using the Hamiltonian cycle
H_{CS}
, by Lemmas
12
,
16
,
13
,
14
, and
46
, the LP-UITP algorithm can generate an itinerary
I_{2}
such that

\displaystyle w(I_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\gamma\geq 0}\left\{\gamma\cdot w(I^{*})+e^{-\gamma}\cdot\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)w(H_{CS})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\gamma\geq 0}\left\{\gamma+e^{-\gamma}\cdot\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\left(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)\frac{3-\chi}{2}\right\}w(I^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\ln\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\ln\left(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)\frac{3-\chi}{2}\right)w(I^{*}).

The approximation ratio is
1+\ln\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\ln(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2})+(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})\frac{3-\chi}{2}
.

We can make a trade-off between them. The results can be briefly summarized as follows.

Case 1.
When
3\leq k\leq 5
, we have
\chi=1
in the worst case, and the ratio is

1+\ln\frac{k}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)=\frac{2\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\ln\frac{k}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}.

Case 2.
When
k=6
and
\frac{7}{6}\leq\alpha\leq\frac{3}{2}
, we have
\chi=\frac{2}{3}
, and the ratio is
\frac{15}{8}+\ln\frac{4}{3}<2.163
.

Case 3.
When
k=7
and
\frac{17}{12}\leq\alpha\leq\frac{3}{2}
, we have
\chi=\frac{1}{6}
, and the ratio is
\frac{33}{16}+\ln\frac{4}{3}<2.351
.

Case 4.
For the rest case, i.e., when
k=6
and
1\leq\alpha\leq\frac{7}{6}
,
k=7
and
1\leq\alpha\leq\frac{17}{12}
, or
k\geq 8
, we have
\chi=3-2\alpha
, and the ratio is

1+\ln\frac{k-4(\alpha-1)}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)\alpha=\frac{(\alpha+1)\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}+\ln\frac{k-4(\alpha-1)}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}.

Now we consider the case
\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\left(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2}\right)<1
. It is sufficient to show that we can get a better ratio in this case.

On one hand, setting
\gamma=0
, by Lemmas
12
and
13

\begin{split}w(I_{1})\leq\ &\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)w(H)\\
\leq\ &\left(\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\left(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)\alpha\right)w(I^{*})\\
\leq\ &\left(1+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)\alpha\right)w(I^{*}).\end{split}

The ratio is better than the ratio in Case 4. Moreover, when
k=6
or
k=7
, the ratio is at most
1+(1-\frac{1}{4})\cdot\frac{3}{2}<2.125
which is also better than the ratios in Cases 2 and 3.

On the other hand, we have
0\leq\chi\leq\frac{\lfloor k/2\rfloor-k+3}{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}
when
3\leq k\leq 5
. Setting
\gamma=0
, when
3\leq k\leq 5
, by Lemmas
13
and
16
, we get

\begin{split}w(I_{2})\leq\ &\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)w(H_{CS})\\
\leq\ &\left(\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\left(\chi+\frac{k-2}{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)\frac{3-\chi}{2}\right)w(I^{*})\\
\leq\ &\left(\frac{5}{4}+\frac{k-9/4}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)w(I^{*}),\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from
\chi\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
3\leq k\leq 5
, and
(\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}-\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}))\chi\leq(\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}-\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}))\cdot\frac{1}{2}
.
When
k=3
,
k=4
, and
5
, the new ratios are 1.625, 1.834, and 2.167 respectively, which are better than the ratios 1.906, 1.955, and 2.178 respectively in Case 1.
∎

12
A Further Improvement for Unsplittable
k
-CVRP

In the above section, the approximation ratio of unsplittable
k
-CVRP is
\frac{5\lfloor k/2\rfloor+2}{2\lfloor k/2\rfloor+2}+\ln\frac{k-2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}=5/2+\ln 2-\Theta(1/k)
for
k\geq 8
under the ratio
\alpha=3/2
for metric TSP.
In this section, we further improve the ratio to less than
5/2+\ln 2-\sqrt{2/k}
. The main idea is to use the refined lower bounds based on
MST
and
\Delta
, provided in Section
7
. However, the analysis may be slightly different.

Our algorithm is simply to run the LP-UITP algorithm on the Hamiltonian cycle
H_{CS}
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
.
By Lemma
46
, for unsplittable
k
-CVRP with any constant
\gamma\geq 0
and
k\geq 3
, the LP-UITP algorithm can generate a feasible itinerary
I
such that

w(I)\leq\gamma\cdot w(I^{*})+e^{-\gamma}\cdot\frac{2}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+\left(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)w(H_{CS}).

We first show a simple analysis.

By setting
\gamma=\ln 2
, we can get that
w(I)\leq\ln 2\cdot w(I^{*})+\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+(1-\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})w(H_{CS})\leq\ln 2\cdot w(I^{*})+\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\Delta+(1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1})w(H_{CS})\leq\ln 2\cdot w(I^{*})+\frac{2}{k}\Delta+(1-\frac{1}{k})w(H_{CS})
, where the last inequality follows from
w(H_{CS})\leq 2\Delta
by the triangle inequality.
Note that the lower bounds of unit-demand
k
-CVRP are also the lower bounds of unsplittable
k
-CVRP.
Therefore, by (
1
) and the previous analysis in Theorem
37
, we have
\frac{2}{k}\Delta+(1-\frac{1}{k})w(H_{CS})<(5/2-\sqrt{2/k})w(I^{*})
. Hence, we have
w(I)\leq\ln 2\cdot w(I^{*})+\frac{2}{k}\Delta+(1-\frac{1}{k})w(H_{CS})<(5/2+\ln 2-\sqrt{2/k})w(I^{*})
.
Therefore, a simple analysis has already led to an approximation ratio of less than
5/2+\ln 2-\sqrt{2/k}
.

Next, we give a more refined analysis with a better result.

Theorem 48
.

Given any fixed
k\geq 7
, for unsplittable
k
-CVRP, the approximation ratio of the LP-UITP algorithm satisfies

•

For any odd
k\geq 7
, the ratio is
\max\{\rho_{o}(\lceil l_{o}\rceil),\rho_{o}(\lfloor l_{o}\rfloor)\}
, where
l_{o}=\frac{\sqrt{(k-1)^{2}+8(k-1)(k+1)^{2}}-(k-1)}{4(k+1)}
and
\rho_{o}(l_{o})=\frac{5}{2}+\ln 2+\ln\frac{k+1-l_{o}}{k+1}-\frac{k-1+6l_{o}}{2(k+1)l_{o}}
;

•

For any even

x=l_{e}=\frac{\sqrt{k^{2}+8k(k+1)(k+2)}-k}{4(k+2)}
, we can get that
g^{\prime}(x)=0
. Hence, it is easy to see that
\max_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}}g(x)=\max\{g(\lceil l_{e}\rceil),g(\lfloor l_{e}\rfloor)\}=\max\{\rho_{e}(\lceil l_{e}\rceil),\rho_{e}(\lfloor l_{e}\rfloor)\}
.

Similarly, we can determine the value
\gamma
as follows: if
\max_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}}g(x)=g(\lceil l_{e}\rceil)
, let
x^{*}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lceil l_{e}\rceil}
; otherwise, let
x^{*}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lfloor l_{e}\rfloor}
. We can get
\gamma=\ln\left(\frac{k+1-\frac{1}{x^{*}}}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}\right)
.

16
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we consider
k
-CVRP in general metrics and improve the approximation ratio for
k
smaller than a sufficiently large value, say
k\leq 1.7\times 10^{7}
.
Although most of our algorithms, such as EX-ITP, are simple or extensions of the classic methods, the analysis is technically involved. We need to carefully analyze the structure of the solutions and most of our analysis is based on pure combinatorial analysis. We think that analyzing better theoretical bounds for simple and classic algorithms is an interesting and important task in algorithms.
We also believe that our methods have potential to be applied to more routing problems, such as the Capacitated Arc Routing Problem (CARP)
[
29
]
, where the demands are defined for edges instead of vertices.

Appendix B
Calculating the Ratio for the Splittable Case

In this section, we will prove the approximation ratio of splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP in
[

is at least
5/2-1.005/3000-1.5/k
. To show our result
5/2-\Theta(\sqrt{1/k})
is better for any

5/2-1.005/3000-1.5/k
for any
k\leq 1.7\times 10^{7}
.

We remark that Blauth
et al.

[
15
]
proved an approximation ratio of
2.5-1/3000
, which works for any
k
. However, using their results, one may easily obtain better ratios for small
k
. Since we consider
k
as a constant, we need to calculate the best ratios that can be obtained by their results.

B.1
The Main Idea of the Algorithm

The idea in
[
15
]
is to distinguish two kinds of instances.

Fixing a constant
\varepsilon>0
, there are two kinds of instances.

•

If
(1-\varepsilon)w(I^{*})\geq(2/k)\Delta
, the instance is
simple
.

•

Otherwise, the instance is
difficult
.

Lemma 64

(
[
15
]
)
.

For hard instances, there is a function
f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon\rightarrow 0\end{subarray}}f(\varepsilon)=0
and a polynomial-time algorithm to get a Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
such that
w(H)\leq(1+f(\varepsilon))w(I^{*})
.

Note that the function
f
can be seen in Appendix
D
.
The above lemma shows that we can find a good Hamiltonian cycle in the difficult instances if
\varepsilon
is small.
Hence, using the AG-ITP algorithm, there will always be an improvement by making a trade-off between the two kinds of instances.

Given a Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
, the weight of the AG-ITP algorithm in
[
3
]
for splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP is at most

(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)w(H)\leq(2/k)\Delta+w(H).

B.2
A Simple Analysis

In this subsection, we use the weaker result
(2/k)\Delta+w(H)
of the AG-ITP algorithm. We will show the ratio in
[
15
]
is at least
5/2-1.005/3000
.

Case 1.
For easy instances, by the definition, we have
(1-\varepsilon)w(I^{*})\geq(2/k)\Delta
. Using an
\alpha
-approximate Hamiltonian cycle on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
, by Lemma
12
, we can get that

(2/k)\Delta+\alpha w(H^{*})\leq(1-\varepsilon)w(I^{*})+\alpha w(I^{*})=(\alpha+1-\varepsilon)w(I^{*}).

Case 2.
For hard instances, we have
w(I^{*})\geq(2/k)\Delta
by Lemma
13
.
Using the Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
in Lemma
64
, we can get that

(2/k)\Delta+w(H)\leq w(I^{*})+(1+f(\varepsilon))w(I^{*})=(2+f(\varepsilon))w(I^{*}).

Therefore, the approximation ratio of splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP is

\min_{\varepsilon>0}\max\{\alpha+1-\varepsilon,\ 2+f(\varepsilon)\}=\alpha+1-\varepsilon^{*},

where
\varepsilon^{*}
is the maximum value satisfying
f(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\leq\alpha-1
.
When
\alpha=3/2
, we have

1.005/3000-6\times 10^{-9}<\varepsilon^{*}<1.005/3000-5\times 10^{-9}.

Therefore, the ratio is at least
5/2-1.005/3000
and it holds
\varepsilon^{*}<1.005/3000
.

B.3
A Tighter Analysis

In the previous subsection, we know the ratio is at least
5/2-1.005/3000
. Since we consider
k=O(1)
, using the stronger result
(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)w(H)
of the AG-ITP algorithm, the ratio may be better under a tighter analysis. However, we will show the improvement is at most
1.5/k
, i.e., the ratio is at least
5/2-1.005/3000-1.5/k
.

Case 1.
For easy instances, by the definition, we have
(1-\varepsilon)w(I^{*})\geq(2/k)\Delta
. Using an
\alpha
-approximate Hamiltonian cycle on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
, by Lemma
12
, we can get that

(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)\alpha w(H^{*})\leq(1-\varepsilon)w(I^{*})+(1-1/k)\alpha w(I^{*})=(\alpha+1-\varepsilon-\alpha/k)w(I^{*}).

Case 2.
For hard instances, we have
w(I^{*})\geq(2/k)\Delta
by Lemma
13
.
Using the Hamiltonian cycle
H
on
V\cup\{v_{0}\}
in Lemma
64
, we can get that

(2/k)\Delta+(1-1/k)w(H)\leq w(I^{*})+(1-1/k)(1+f(\varepsilon))w(I^{*})=(1+(1-1/k)(1+f(\varepsilon))w(I^{*}).

Therefore, the approximation ratio of splittable and unit-demand
k
-CVRP is

\min_{\varepsilon>0}\max\{\alpha+1-\varepsilon-\alpha/k,\ 1+(1-1/k)(1+f(\varepsilon))\}=\alpha+1-\varepsilon^{**}-\alpha/k,

where
\varepsilon^{**}
is the maximum value satisfying
(1-1/k)f(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\leq(1-1/k)(\alpha-1)
.

Recall that
\varepsilon^{*}
is the maximum value satisfying
f(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\leq\alpha-1
. Hence, we have
f(\varepsilon^{*})+\varepsilon^{*}=\alpha-1
.
Therefore, we can get
(1-1/k)f(\varepsilon^{*})+\varepsilon^{*}=(1-1/k)(\alpha-1-\varepsilon^{*})+\varepsilon^{*}>(1-1/k)(\alpha-1)
. So, when
\alpha=3/2
, we have
\varepsilon^{**}<\varepsilon^{*}<1.005/3000
. The ratio satisfies that

\alpha+1-\varepsilon^{**}-\alpha/k>\alpha+1-\varepsilon^{*}-\alpha/k>5/2-1.005/3000-1.5/k.

Therefore, a tighter analysis shows that the ratio in
[
15
]
is at least
5/2-1.005/3000-1.5/k
.

C.1
A Simple Analysis

In this subsection, we use the weaker result

of the AG-UITP algorithm. Then, the LP-based algorithm in

\min_{\gamma\geq 0}\{\gamma\cdot w(I^{*})+e^{-\gamma}\cdot(4/k)\Delta+w(H)\}.

Case 1.
For easy instances, by the definition, we have
(1-\varepsilon)w(I^{*})\geq(2/k)\Delta

\alpha
-approximate Hamiltonian cycle on
