Abstract
. We prove a generalization of the polarization identity of linear algebra expressing the inner product of a complex inner product space in terms of the norm, where the field of scalars is extended to an associative algebra equipped with an involution, and polarization is viewed as an averaging operation over a compact multiplicative subgroup of the scalars. Using this we prove a general form of the Jordan-von Neumann theorem on characterizing inner product spaces among normed linear spaces, when the scalars are taken in an associative algebra.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

The goal of this article is to present a general version
of the
polarization identity
of elementary linear algebra. This well-known identity
takes the form

\left\langle x,y\right\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\left\|x+y\right\|^{2}+i\left\|x+iy\right\|^{2}-\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}-i\left\|x-iy\right\|^{2}\right),\quad i=\sqrt{-1},

(1.1)

for vectors
x,y
in a complex inner product space,
where the inner product is denoted by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
and the induced norm by
\left\|\cdot\right\|
.
The identity
has very important applications in classical Hilbert space theory (e.g.
[
JvN35
,
Hal51
]
).
This paper takes the point of view, not unknown in the literature (see
[
You88
, p. 12, Problem 1.10]
and
[
D’A02
, pp. 53–54]
), that this identity is an instance of a fundamental and ubiquitous process in mathematics, that of
averaging over a group (a.k.a. Haar integration). From this point of view, (
1.1
) is really saying that

\left\langle x,y\right\rangle=\int_{G}\left\|x+gy\right\|^{2}gdg,

(1.2)

where
G=\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
is the four element cyclic group of complex fourth roots of unity, and the integral is that of the complex valued function

g\mapsto\left\|x+gy\right\|^{2}g
on the compact group
G
with respect to the Haar measure of
G
, normalized to a probability measure. Our
generalization of (
1.1
) (Theorem
1.1
below) will replace the field of complex numbers by an associative algebra
\mathfrak{A}

over
{\mathbb{R}}
equipped with an involution,
the inner product with a general Hermitian form, the group
G
with a compact multiplicative group of unitary elements of
\mathfrak{A}
(where an element of
\mathfrak{A}
is unitary if
the involution maps it to its inverse).

Several authors have considered the natural problem of obtaining polarization identities for
Hermitian forms over scalars other than
{\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
, and the closely related Jordan-von Neumann theorem in this setting (see, e.g.,
[
Kur65
,
Jam70
,
Šem86
,
Vuk87
,
GR93
,
Ili05b
,
Ili05a
]
etc.).
The polarization process has also been studied by Waterhouse (
[
Wat95
]
) from a purely algebraic perspective for involutive
algebras over very general commutative rings. Our results here have interesting intersections with the generalized polarization identities obtained by these authors, but emphasize the role of
averaging over a group as a key feature of the polarization process of recovering a Hermitian form from its restriction to the diagonal. We also restrict ourselves to algebras over the real numbers

{\mathbb{R}}
, first, to take advantage of classical integration theory, and second, because of the application to a generalized
Jordan-von Neumann theorem (see Theorem
1.3
below). However, it is clear that ideas and results of this paper
make sense over other fields, and it would be interesting to look at such generalizations.

1.2.
Polarizable algebras and polarizing subgroups

Let
\mathfrak{A}=(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a
real involutive algebra,

by which we mean a finite dimensional unital associative algebra over
{\mathbb{R}}
equipped with
an anti-automorphism
\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A},\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{*}
of order two (the
involution
), i.e., the involution is
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear,
(\alpha\beta)^{*}=\beta^{*}\alpha^{*}

and
(\alpha^{*})^{*}=\alpha
for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{A}
. The multiplicative identity of
\mathfrak{A}
will be denoted by
1_{\mathfrak{A}}
(or simply 1 if confusion is unlikely).
We also call real involutive algebras
*
-algebras
for short, and these
*
-algebras will replace the field
{\mathbb{C}}
in the generalized polarization identity.

Thought of as a finite dimensional real vector space, a
*
-algebra

\mathfrak{A}
has a natural linear topology in which the algebraic operations are continuous. We will always endow
\mathfrak{A}
with this topology, and its subsets with the induced subspace topology. Then the group
\mathfrak{A}^{\times}
of units of
\mathfrak{A}
is a locally compact
topological group. If
G
is a compact subgroup of
\mathfrak{A}^{\times}
, then
G
is a compact topological group, and therefore admits a (simultaneously left and right invariant) Haar measure, which will be always normalized to a probability measure. Given a
function
f:G\to V
taking values in a finite dimensional real vector space
V
, we can define
the integral with respect to Haar measure of
f
, which we will denote by

\int_{G}fdg\quad\text{ or}\quad\int_{G}f(g)dg

(1.3)

and can be defined invariantly as the unique vector in the space
V
satisfying the condition

\phi\left(\int_{G}fdg\right)=\int_{G}\left(\phi\circ f\right)\,dg

(1.4)

for each linear functional
\phi:V\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the integral on the right being the classical integral of a real valued function on
G
with respect to Haar measure. The existence and uniqueness of the integral in (
1.3
) is easily established by choosing a basis of the vector space
V
and
working in the corresponding coordinates.

An element
u\in\mathfrak{A}
will be said to be
unitary
if we have

uu^{*}=1_{\mathfrak{A}}
, and the collection of all unitary elements

\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)=\{u\in\mathfrak{A}:uu^{*}=1_{\mathfrak{A}}\},

(1.5)

will be called the
unitary group
of the algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)
. It is therefore
a locally compact topological group, and
generalizes
the circle group
\{\left|z\right|=1\}
in
{\mathbb{C}}
.

We will say that a closed subgroup
G\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)

generates
the
*
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
if
\mathfrak{A}
is the smallest
*
-subalgebra of
\mathfrak{A}
containing the group
G
.
It is clear that
\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})=\{\left|z\right|=1\}
generates
{\mathbb{C}}
. Notice that since
G
is closed under the operations of multiplication and involution of
\mathfrak{A}
, it follows that
G
generates
\mathfrak{A}
if and only if

\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G=\mathfrak{A}

i.e.
G
generates
\mathfrak{A}
also as a vector space. We now make the following definition:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a
*
-algebra. We say that
(\mathfrak{A},*)
is
polarizable
if the unitary group
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
is compact and generates
\mathfrak{A}
.

Polarizable algebras will replace the complex numbers in the general polarization identity (
1.8
) below. A key property of polarizable algebras is that a version of Maschke’s theorem
holds for them, showing that they are semisimple and leading to a complete classification of these algebras under an appropriate notion of equivalence (see Theorem
1.2
below). We now
introduce the groups which will replace, in the general polarization formula, the group
G
of
4
-th roots of unity that appears in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
):

Definition 1.2
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a
*
-algebra. A compact subgroup
G\subseteq\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
is said to be
polarizing
if
G
generates
\mathfrak{A}
as an algebra and

\int_{G}gdg=0,

(1.6)

where the integral is that of the inclusion function
g:G\to\mathfrak{A}
of the group
G
in the vector space
\mathfrak{A}
, and the integral is taken with respect to the (bi-invariant) Haar measure.

The following facts shed some light on the notions introduced in Definitions
1.1
and
1.2
:

(a)

In a polarizable algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)
, the unitary group
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
is polarizable. The condition (
1.6
) is satisfied for
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
since
-1_{\mathfrak{A}}
, the negative of the identity of the algebra
\mathfrak{A}
, automatically belongs to
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
, so we have by Haar invariance:

\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)}gdg=\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)}(-1_{\mathfrak{A}})gdg=-\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)}gdg.

(1.7)

(b)

We will see below that (
1.6
) is satisfied for many groups
G
(Proposition
3.2
).

(c)

On the other hand, as soon as a
*
-algebra has a polarizing subgroup (or even a compact subgroup of the unitary group that generates the algebra), it is polarizable (see Proposition 2.4 below).

(d)

Finally, each polarizable algebra admits a
finite
polarizing subgroup (see Proposition
5.3
) below. It follows
that the polarization process is always possible in terms of algebraic formulas such as (
1.1
) rather than integral formulas such as (
5.3
), where the integral is over a Lie group of positive dimension.

1.3.
Generalized Polarization identity

Let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a
*
-algebra and let

\mathsf{X}
be a left
\mathfrak{A}
-module. By an (
\mathfrak{A}
-valued)
Hermitian form
on

\mathsf{X}
we mean a map
Q:\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{X}\to\mathfrak{A}
such that
for
x,y,z\in\mathsf{X}
,
\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}
we have

Q(x+y,z)=Q(x,z)+Q(y,z),\quad Q(\alpha x,y)=\alpha Q(x,y),\quad\text{ and }\quad Q(x,y)^{*}=Q(y,x).

Then we have the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a polarizable
*
-algebra.
Then there is an element
\kappa\in\mathfrak{A}
such that whenever
\mathsf{X}
is a left
\mathfrak{A}
-module and

Q:\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{X}\to\mathfrak{A}
is a Hermitian form, then we have the polarization identity

Q(x,y)=\kappa\int_{G}q(x+gy)gdg,\quad x,y\in\mathsf{X}

(1.8)

where
q(x)=Q(x,x)
,
G\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{A})
is a polarizing subgroup,
and the integral is that of an
\mathfrak{A}
-valued function on
G
with respect to the normalized Haar measure.

We emphasize that in Theorem
1.1
, the constant
\kappa
depends only on the (polarizable)
*
-algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)

and not on the particular polarizing group
G
. This element
\kappa=\kappa(\mathfrak{A},*)
will be called the
polarization constant
of
(\mathfrak{A},*)
, and in Theorem
1.2
below we show how to compute it.

1.4.
More definitions and constructions related to
*
-algebras

Before we proceed further, we collect a few definitions.

(1)

If
(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)
is a
*
-algebra, there is a natural way to define an induced involution on the matrix algebra
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
by setting

A\mapsto(A^{\sharp})^{T},

(1.9)

where
A^{\sharp}
is the matrix obtained from
A
by applying the involution
\sharp
to each entry, and
B^{T}
is the transpose of the matrix
B
. We call this involution on
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
the
involution induced by
\sharp
.

(2)

Each of the real division algebras
\mathbb{D}={\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}},\mathbb{H}
has its own standard involution traditionally called a “conjugation”, which is the identity for
{\mathbb{R}}
, the complex conjugation
for
{\mathbb{C}}
and the quaternionic conjugation

a+ib+jc+kd\mapsto a-ib-jc-kd

(1.10)

for
\mathbb{H}
. We denote the conjugation operation in each case by
z\mapsto\overline{z}
, following the standard convention.
The involution induced by the conjugation map on
\mathbb{D}
on the algebra
M_{n}(\mathbb{D})
will be called the “conjugate-transpose” involution of

M_{n}(\mathbb{D})
. We will see later that
M_{n}(\mathbb{D})
has many other involutions apart from the conjugate-transpose (see Proposition
2.2
below).

(3)

In the category of involutive algebras, a morphism is called a
*
-homomorphism
:

\phi:(\mathfrak{A},*)\to(\mathfrak{B},\sharp)

where
\phi
is a homomorphism of unital associative algebras, and preserves the involution, i.e.

\phi(\alpha^{*})=\phi(\alpha)^{\sharp}.

It is clear what is meant by a
*
-isomorphism, or a
*
-subalgebra.

(4)

Given a finite collection
\{(\mathfrak{A}_{j},*_{j}),1\leq j\leq N\}
of
*
-algebras, we define its
*
-direct sum
be the direct sum

\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}\mathfrak{A}_{j}
equipped with the “direct sum involution”

(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N})^{*}=(\alpha_{1}^{*_{1}},\dots,\alpha_{N}^{*_{N}}),\quad\alpha_{j}\in\mathfrak{A}_{j},1\leq j\leq N.

1.5.
Polarization constants

We now determine the structure of polarizable algebras and use it to compute the polarization constant of an algebra:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a
*
-algebra. Then
(\mathfrak{A},*)
is polarizable if and only if there is a
*
-isomorphism

\phi:\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}(M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{D}_{j}),*)\to(\mathfrak{A},*),\

(1.11)

where in the left is the
*
-direct sum of a a finite number of matrix algebras over real division rings,
each endowed with the “conjugate-transpose” involution. The polarization constant of
(\mathfrak{A},*)
is

\phi\left(\frac{n_{1}\delta_{1}}{(n_{1}-1)\delta_{1}+2}I_{n_{1}},\dots,\frac{n_{j}\delta_{j}}{(n_{j}-1)\delta_{j}+2}I_{n_{j}},\dots,\frac{n_{N}\delta_{N}}{(n_{N}-1)\delta_{N}+2}I_{n_{N}}\right),

(1.12)

where
\delta_{j}=\dim_{{\mathbb{R}}}(\mathbb{D}_{j})=1,2
or
4
denotes the dimension of the division algebra
\mathbb{D}_{j}
as a real vector space and
I_{n_{j}}
is the
n_{j}\times n_{j}
identity matrix of
M_{n}(\mathbb{D}_{j})
for
1\leq j\leq N
.

The fact that a polarizable algebra such as
(\mathfrak{A},*)
has the above structure (i.e. it is semisimple) allows us to understand the module
\mathsf{X}
and Hermitian form
Q
of Theorem
1.1

in terms of
\mathfrak{A}
(see
[
Lam01
]
). Since the precise structure of the module
\mathsf{X}
does not play any role in the polarization phenomenon of Theorem
1.1
, we do not discuss this further.

1.6.
Generalized Jordan-von Neumann-Jamison theorem

The Jordan-von Neumann theorem (
[
JvN35
]
)
solves the problem of
geometrically characterizing inner product spaces among normed linear spaces over
{\mathbb{C}}
or
{\mathbb{R}}
: the norm of
a normed space
(X,\left\|\cdot\right\|)
arises from an inner product (i.e., there is an inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
on
X
such that
\left\|x\right\|=\sqrt{\left\langle x,x\right\rangle}
for each
x\in X
)
if and only if the
parallelogram identity

\left\|x+y\right\|^{2}+\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}=2\left(\left\|x\right\|^{2}+\left\|y\right\|^{2}\right)

(1.13)

holds for all
x,y\in X
. In
[
Jam70
]
, the field of scalars was already extended to the
quaternions (and octonions). Here, we will prove a generalization to Hermitian forms over
polarizable algebras.

The analog of the map
x\mapsto\left\|x\right\|^{2}
occurring in formulas such as (
1.1
) and (
1.13
)
in the situation of non-commutative scalars from
\mathfrak{A}
will be called a
quadrance
, i.e., a “squaring”,
a name inspired by the unorthodox work of Norman J. Wildberger
[
Wil05
]
.

Definition 1.3
.

If
(\mathfrak{A},*)
is a
*
-algebra, and
\mathsf{X}
a left
\mathfrak{A}
-module,
a map
q\colon\mathsf{X}\to\mathfrak{A}
is called a
quadrance
on
\mathsf{X}
if

(1)

q(x)=q(x)^{*}
for all
x\in\mathsf{X}

(2)

q(\alpha x)=\alpha q(x)\alpha^{*}
for all
x\in\mathsf{X},\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}

(3)

For
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
the map

{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A}

given by

(\lambda,\alpha)\mapsto q(\lambda x+\alpha y)

(1.14)

is continuous.

Notice that the square of a norm on a real or complex vector space is obviously a quadrance. In this case, the vector space acquires a norm-topology, in which
the norm is continuous, by the “reverse triangle inequality”
\left|\left\|x\right\|-\left\|y\right\|\right|\leq\left\|x-y\right\|
, which is an immediate consequence of the “triangle inequality”
\left\|x+y\right\|\leq\left\|x\right\|+\left\|y\right\|
postulated
on norms. Notice also that we have not endowed the module
\mathsf{X}
with a topology, but we impose
the continuity condition (
1.14
) on a quadrance
q
.
We show below in Proposition
4.1
that the restriction of a Hermitian form to the diagonal is a quadrance.
Conversely we have the following generalization of the Jordan-von Neumann theorem to non-commutative scalars:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a polarizable
*
-algebra, let
\mathsf{X}
be a left
\mathfrak{A}
-module,
and let
q
be a quadrance on
\mathsf{X}
.
Then there is a Hermitian form
Q
on
\mathsf{X}
such that
q(x)=Q(x,x)

if and only if
q
satisfies the
classical parallelogram
identity

q(x+y)+q(x-y)=2(q(x)+q(y)),

(1.15)

for all
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
. If such a form
Q
exists, then it is unique.

1.7.
Examples

In the last section of the paper, we show that group algebras of finite groups over the reals, as well as
real Clifford algebras are polarizable, and determine some polarizing subgroups, as well as the polarization constants. This gives examples of concrete polarization identities in new contexts. The case of classical Clifford numbers
\mathsf{C}_{0,n}
associated
to negative definite inner
products was considered in
[
GR93
]
. We also make a few comments about polarizing subgroups in matrix algebras.

2.
Structure of polarizable algebras

In this section our goal is to prove the first half of Theorem
1.2
, i.e.,
a
*
-algebra is polarizable if and only if it is isomorphic to a
*
-direct sum of matrix algebras over real division rings, each endowed with the conjugate-transpose involution.

2.1.
Involutions on matrix algebras and compact unitary groups

The following result is needed to
prove the existence of isomorphism (
1.11
):

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{D}={\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}
or
\mathbb{H}
be a real division algebra, and let
\sharp
be an involution on
M_{n}(\mathbb{D})
. If the involutive algebra
(M_{n}(\mathbb{D}),\sharp)

is polarizable then it is
*
-isomorphic to
(M_{n}(\mathbb{D}),*)
where
*
is the conjugate-transpose involution of
M_{n}(\mathbb{D})
.

The proof will employ brute force, using a classification of all the involutions of the matrix algebras
M_{n}(\mathbb{D})
, and a characterization of their unitary groups, and
then singling out the ones which have polarizable unitary groups. Since we have not been able to locate a full statement of this classification in
the literature, we include it below in Proposition
2.2
, and sketch a proof based on the general results in
[
Sch85
]

on involutions of matrix algebras.

We begin by
recalling some facts and introducing some notation to simplify the presentation of this result.

(1)

On the quaternions
\mathbb{H}
, it is known that any involution is either the conjugation (
1.10
), or is a so-called
nonstandard involution
(see
[
Rod14
, Theorem 2.4.4]
): an example of a nonstandard involution is the mapping

x\mapsto\widetilde{x}
given for
x=x_{0}+x_{1}i+x_{2}j+x_{3}k
by

\widetilde{x}=x_{0}-x_{1}i+x_{2}j+x_{3}k,

(2.1)

and any other nonstandard involution is conjugate to it. A computation shows that the nonstandard involution (
2.1
) and
the conjugation
x\mapsto\overline{x}
are related by

\widetilde{x}=i\overline{x}i^{-1}=-i\overline{x}i.

(2.2)

Given a matrix
A
of
quaternions, we denote by
\widetilde{A}
the matrix obtained by applying the involution
\widetilde{\cdot}
to each element of
A
.

(2)

Let
J_{1}\in M_{2}({\mathbb{R}})

\displaystyle{J_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\

(3)

Let
p,q
be nonnegative integers such that

. We

the diagonal matrix where the first
p

If

\mathbb{D}={\mathbb{C}}

second kind

The following is the special case for
the base field

Proof of Proposition

In view of Lemma

Once involution is reduced to a normal form, the computation of the unitary group of the last column is straightforward.

The case
\mathbb{D}={\mathbb{R}}

Thanks to Lemma

(for

2.2.
Semisimplicity of polarizable algebras

We begin with the following simple application of the Weyl averaging trick:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be an involutive algebra and suppose there is a compact subgroup
G
of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
which generates
\mathfrak{A}
. Then

(1)

there exists an inner product on the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
\mathfrak{A}
:

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle\colon\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{A}\to{\mathbb{R}}

such that
for
\alpha,\beta,\eta\in\mathfrak{A}
we have

\left\langle\eta\alpha,\beta\right\rangle=\left\langle\alpha,\eta^{*}\beta\right\rangle.

(2.5)

(2)

the
*
-algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)
is
*
-isomorphic to a
*
-subalgebra of
(M_{n}({\mathbb{R}}),T)
, were
n=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{A}
(as a vector space) and
T
denotes the transposition involution.

(3)

the
*
-algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)
is polarizable.

Proof.

(1)

Let
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{0}\colon\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{A}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a real inner product on
\mathfrak{A}
, i.e. a positive-definite, symmetric, bilinear form. Then

\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle:=\int_{G}\left\langle g\alpha,g\beta\right\rangle_{0}dg

is also an inner product, and by Haar invariance we have for this inner product
for each
g\in G
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{A}
that

\left\langle g\alpha,g\beta\right\rangle=\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle.

(2.6)

Therefore

\left\langle h\alpha,\beta\right\rangle=\left\langle h\alpha,hh^{*}\beta\right\rangle=\left\langle\alpha,h^{*}\beta\right\rangle

for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{A}
and
h\in G
. By the
{\mathbb{R}}
-bilinearity of the inner product, and the fact that
\mathfrak{A}=\mathrm{span}_{{\mathbb{R}}}G
the equality (
2.5
)
follows.

(2)

Let

\rho:\mathfrak{A}\to\End_{{\mathbb{R}}}(\mathfrak{A})

(2.7)

be the injective
{\mathbb{R}}
-algebra homomorphism given by
\rho(\alpha)(\beta)=\alpha\beta
.
On the endomorphism algebra
\End_{{\mathbb{R}}}(\mathfrak{A})
there is an involution

\sharp
given by taking adjoints with respect to the inner product of part (1) above, i.e.
for an
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear map

, the map

is characterized by the fact that
\left\langle A\alpha,\beta\right\rangle=\left\langle\alpha,A^{\sharp}\beta\right\rangle
. Notice that (

) says that for each

, we have
\rho(\eta)^{\sharp}=\rho(\eta^{*})
, i.e.,
\rho
is a
*
-homomorphism
from
(\mathfrak{A},*)
into
(\End_{{\mathbb{R}}}(\mathfrak{A}),\sharp)
, which is known to be injective, so that
(\mathfrak{A},*)
is isomorphic to a
*
-subalgebra of
(\End_{{\mathbb{R}}}(\mathfrak{A}),\sharp)
. Now choosing a basis of the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space

, the algebra
(\End_{{\mathbb{R}}}(\mathfrak{A}),\sharp)
is

-isomorphic to
(M_{n}({\mathbb{R}}),*)
and the result follows.

(3)

Identifying

with the matrix algebra

by choosing a basis, we see that the map

of (

)

\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)=\rho^{-1}(O(n)),

where

, it follows a fortiori that

. The result follows.

, it follows that each
\sharp_{j}
is the conjugate transpose involution.

∎

2.3.
Polarizability of matrix algebras

In this subsection, we will prove that
a finite
*
-direct sum of matrix algebras over division algebras is polarizable. We note some properties of the vector-valued integral (
1.3
) defined
by the condition (
1.4
):

(1)

If
f:G\to V
is a continuous function on the group
G
with values in a finite dimensional real vector space
V
,

W
is another finite dimensional real vector space and
T:V\to W
is an
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear map, then we have:

T\left(\int_{G}fdg\right)=\int_{G}\left(T\circ f\right)\,dg

(2.9)

where now the integral on the right is the vector-valued integral of a
W
-valued function on
G
. The relation (
2.9
) is easily verified using the defining condition (
1.4
).

(2)

Again, let
f:G\to V
be a continuous function on the group
G
with values in a finite dimensional real vector space
V
. Then
we have

\int_{G}fdg\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(f(G)\right),

(2.10)

where
f(G)=\{f(g):g\in G\}\subset V
is the image of the map
f
. To see (
2.10
), let
W=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(f(G)\right)
, and let
\pi:V\to V/W
be the quotient map. Then by (
2.9
),

\pi\left(\int_{G}fdg\right)=\int_{G}\pi(f(g))dg=0,

which shows that
\int_{G}fdg\in W
. With a little more work, one can show that
\int_{G}fdg
belongs to the convex hull of
f(G)
, but we do not need
this.

We also recall a few definitions. Let
\sigma\in S_{n}
be a permutation, i.e., a bijection of
\{1,\dots,n\}
with itself. We can associate with
\sigma
an
n\times n

permutation matrix

P_{\sigma}
, where
the
j
-th column of
P_{\sigma}
is
e_{\sigma(j)}
for
1\leq j\leq n
, with
e_{i}
denoting the
n\times 1
column matrix with an
1
in the
i
-th place and 0’s everywhere else. It is clear that

P_{\sigma}e_{j}=e_{\sigma(j)}
. Given a group
H
of permutation matrices, we say that it is
transitive
if the corresponding permutations act transitively on the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)
be a
*
-algebra, and let
G\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)
be a polarizing group. Let
n\geq 2
and let

\Delta_{G}\subset M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
be the collection of
n\times n
diagonal matrices with the diagonal entries taken from
G
:

\Delta_{G}=\{\diag(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}):g_{j}\in G\},

and let
H
be a transitive group of permutation matrices in
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
. Then
the set

\Delta_{G}H=\{DP:D\in\Delta_{G},P\in H\}

is a compact subgroup of
(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}),*)
which is polarizing, where
*
is the involution induced on
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
by the involution
\sharp
of
\mathfrak{A}

as in (
1.9
).

Proof.

It is clear that
\Delta_{G}\cong G^{n}
is a compact group as a Cartesian power of the compact group
G
and
\Delta_{G}H
is compact as a union of finitely many homeomorphic
copies of
\Delta_{G}
. If
D\in\Delta_{G}
, we can write
D=\diag(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
for
g_{j}\in G,1\leq j\leq n
, so that we have

D^{*}D=\diag(g_{1}^{\sharp},\dots,g_{n}^{\sharp})\cdot\diag(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=\diag(1_{\mathfrak{A}},\dots,1_{\mathfrak{A}}),

since
G\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)
by hypothesis. Also, we have for a permutation matrix
P\in M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
:

P^{*}P=(P^{\sharp})^{T}P=P^{T}P=I.

It follows that
\Delta_{G}H
is a compact subset of the unitary group
\Gamma(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}),*)
.

A direct computation shows that for each
\sigma\in S_{n}
, and
D\in\Delta_{G}
we have

(P_{\sigma})^{-1}DP_{\sigma}=P_{\sigma^{-1}}DP_{\sigma}=D^{\prime}\in\Delta_{G}

(2.11)

where
D^{\prime}
is a diagonal matrix whose nonzero entries are a permutation of the diagonal entries of
D
. More precisely, if
D=\diag(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
where
g_{j}\in G
,
then
D^{\prime}=\diag(g_{\sigma(1)},\dots,g_{\sigma(n)})
.

The relation (
2.11
) shows not only that
\Delta_{G}H
is a group, but also that
\Delta_{G}
is normal subgroup of the group
\Delta_{G}H
. The intersection

\Delta_{G}\cap H
consists of diagonal matrices which are permutation matrices and therefore is
\{I\}
. It folows that
\Delta_{G}H
is the (internal) semidirect product of the two subgroups

\Delta_{G}
and
H
. Since on a semidirect product, the Haar measure is equal to the product measure (e.g.
[
Nac76
, pp. 96ff.]
), we have, using Fubini’s theorem:

\int_{\Delta_{G}H}gdg=\int_{H}\int_{\Delta_{G}}DPdDdP=\left(\int_{\Delta_{G}}DdD\right)\cdot\left(\int_{H}PdP\right)=0\cdot\left(\int_{H}PdP\right)=0,

using the fact that
G
is polarizable so (
1.6
) holds.

We now show that
\Delta_{G}H
generates
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
. Let
E_{i,j}
be the
n\times n
matrix with
1_{\mathfrak{A}}
in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column and zeros everywhere else. First we show that
E_{i,j}
is in the
subalgebra of
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
generated by the group
\Delta_{G}H
.
For
1\leq j\leq n
and
g\in G
, denote by
D_{j}(g)\in\Delta_{G}
the diagonal matrix
with a
1_{\mathfrak{A}}
in the
j
-th place and
g
in each other place. Then we clearly have, using (
1.6
) that

E_{j,j}=\int_{G}D_{j}(g)dg.

It follows by (
2.10
) that
E_{j,j}
is in the algebra generated by
\Delta_{G}
. Now observe that

E_{i,j}e_{k}=\begin{cases}e_{i}&\text{if }k=j\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Since
H
is transitive, by definition there exists a
P\in H
such that
Pe_{j}=e_{i}
, so that

PE_{j,j}e_{k}=\begin{cases}Pe_{j}=e_{i}&\text{if }k=j\\
P(0)=0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Hence
PE_{j,j}=E_{i,j}
, so that
\Delta_{G}H
generates all of the
E_{i,j}
.

Now we note that
\alpha I
is generated by
\Delta_{G}H
for each
\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}
. Since
G
generates
\mathfrak{A}
, there exist
g_{1},\dots g_{n}\in G
and
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
so that
\alpha=\sum\lambda_{i}g_{i}
. Hence

\alpha I=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(g_{i}I),

where each
g_{i}I\in\Delta_{G}
. Writing a matrix
A=(\alpha_{i,j})\in M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
as

A=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i,j}I)\cdot E_{i,,j}

we see that
\Delta_{G}H
is a polarizing subgroup of
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
.
∎

From this we deduce the following:

Proposition 2.7
.

A
*
-algebra which is
*
-isomorphic to a finite
*
-direct sum of matrix algebras over real division algebras, each endowed with the conjugate-transpose involution, is polarizable.

Proof.

From part (3) of Proposition
2.4
it follows that a
*
-algebra is polarizable if and only if it has a compact subgroup of the unitary group whose
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear span is the whole algebra. In particular, any
*
-algebra which admits
a polarizing subgroup is polarizable.

Using this criterion it is easy to see that
{\mathbb{R}}
,
{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{H}
are each polarizable if
endowed with the standard conjugation operation.
Indeed, each of the finite groups
\{\pm 1\},\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
and
\{\pm 1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}
consist of unitary elements in
{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{H}
respectively and span the respective division algebra.

Therefore, by Proposition
2.6
, the
*
-algebra
(M_{n}(\mathbb{D}),*)
, where the involution
*
is the standard conjugate-transpose operation, is polarizable, where

\mathbb{D}
is one of
{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}},\mathbb{H}
. Notice also that the
*
-direct sum of a finite number of polarizable
*
-algebras is easily seen to be polarizable. Therefore the
*
-direct sum
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}(M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{D}_{j})*)
is polarizable. Finally polarizability is clearly preserved by
*
-isomorphisms, so the result follows.

∎

3.
Proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

3.1.
Integrals on compact multiplicative groups

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
*
-algebra and let
G
be a compact subgroup of
\mathfrak{A}^{\times}
, the group of
units (i.e. invertible elements) of
\mathfrak{A}
. For an integer

k\geq 0
, we define the
k
-th
moment
of
G
to be the element of
\mathfrak{A}
given by

\mu_{k}(G)=\int_{G}g^{k}dg,

(3.1)

where the integral is that of the
\mathfrak{A}
-valued function
g\mapsto g^{k}

taken with respect to the normalized
Haar measure of the compact group
G
, defined as in (
1.4
). The relation (
2.9
) will be used repeatedly without further comments in the computations below. We now collect some basic information about moments.

Proposition 3.1
.

For each compact subgroup
G\subset\mathfrak{A}^{\times}
and each integer
k\geq 0
, the element
\mu_{k}(G)
belongs to the subalgebra
\mathfrak{B}
of
\mathfrak{A}
generated by
G
, and in fact
lies in the center of
\mathfrak{B}
, i.e., for each
h\in\mathfrak{B}
, we have
h\mu_{k}(G)=\mu_{k}(G)h
.

Proof.

Since
G
being a group is closed under multiplication in
\mathfrak{A}
, one sees easily that

\mathfrak{B}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G,

i.e. the algebra
\mathfrak{B}
generated by
G
coincides with the linear span of the subset of
G
of the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
\mathfrak{A}
. However the integrand of the vector-valued integral (
3.1
) takes values in the linear subspace
\mathfrak{B}
of
\mathfrak{A}
, and therefore by (
2.10
) the value of the integral lies in
\mathfrak{B}
.
Now let
h\in G
, then

h\mu_{k}(G)h^{-1}=\int_{G}hg^{k}h^{-1}dg=\int_{G}(hgh^{-1})^{k}dg=\int_{G}g^{k}dg=\mu_{k}(G),

using the invariance of the Haar measure. Therefore, we have for each
h\in G
that

h\mu_{k}(G)=\mu_{k}(G)h
. Since
\mathfrak{B}
is the collection of real linear combinations of the elements of
G
, it follows that the same relation holds for
h\in\mathfrak{B}
as well.
∎

We now compute the first and second moments of a group under appropriate hypotheses.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G\subset\mathfrak{A}^{\times}
be a compact subgroup and
suppose that one of the following hold:

(a)

-1_{\mathfrak{A}}\in G
,

(b)

G\not=\{1_{\mathfrak{A}}\}
and the subalgebra
\mathfrak{B}
of
\mathfrak{A}
generated by
G
is simple (i.e. has no nontrivial two sided ideals).

Then we have

\mu_{1}(G)=0.

Proof.

If condition
(a)
holds, then by the invariance of Haar measure

\mu_{1}(G)=\int_{G}(-1_{\mathfrak{A}})gdg=-\mu_{1}(G).

Now, assume
(b)
. If
\mathfrak{B}={\mathbb{R}}
, then by the nontriviality of
G
, we must have
G=\{\pm 1_{\mathfrak{A}}\}
, so that
\mu_{1}(G)=\frac{1}{2}(1-1)=0
. Assuming therefore that

\mathfrak{B}\not={\mathbb{R}}
, since by Proposition
3.1
, the element
\mu_{1}(G)
is in the center of

\mathfrak{B}
it follows that the set
\mathfrak{J}=\{h:h\mu_{1}(G)=0\}
is a two sided ideal in
\mathfrak{B}
. Since
\mathfrak{B}\not={\mathbb{R}}
, there are two
{\mathbb{R}}
-linearly
independent elements
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
. If we let
h=g_{1}-g_{2}
, we have
0\not=h\in\mathfrak{J}
since

h\mu_{1}(G)=\int_{G}g_{1}gdg-\int_{G}g_{2}gdg=\int_{G}gdg-\int_{G}gdg=0.

By simplicity of
\mathfrak{B}
,
\mathfrak{J}
is all of
\mathfrak{B}
. It follows that
\mu_{1}(G)=1_{\mathfrak{A}}\cdot\mu_{1}(G)=1\mu_{1}(G)=0
.
∎

The following proposition summarizes the properties of the second moment that we will need in our application:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a
*
-algebra, and
G\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a compact subgroup of unitary elements of
\mathfrak{A}
that generates
\mathfrak{A}
. Then
for each
\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}
, we have

\int_{G}g\alpha gdg=\alpha^{*}\mu_{2}(\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)).

(3.2)

Remark:
Thanks to part (3) of Proposition
2.4
, we know that the unitary group
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
is compact, and therefore
\mu_{2}(\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*))
makes sense.
Taking
\alpha=1_{\mathfrak{A}}
in (
3.2
), we have the remarkable fact that in a polarizable
*
-algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)
:

\mu_{2}(G)=\mu_{2}(\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)),

(3.3)

for each compact subgroup
G
of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
which generates the algebra
\mathfrak{A}
. For a polarizable algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)
we will denote, by abuse of notation,

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A})=\mu_{2}(\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)).

We refer to the element
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A})\in\mathfrak{A}
as the
second moment
of the polarizable algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)
.

Proof.

For each
h\in G
we have, by an application of Haar invariance, that

\int_{G}ghgdg=h^{*}h\int_{G}ghgdg=h^{*}\int_{G}(hg)^{2}dg=h^{*}\int_{G}g^{2}dg=h^{*}\mu_{2}(G),

using the fact that
hh^{*}=1_{\mathfrak{A}}
. Since
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}{G}=\mathfrak{A}
, we
can write each
\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}
as a finite sum
\alpha=\sum\lambda_{j}h_{j}

for
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}\in G
and
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence

\int_{G}g\alpha gdg=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\int_{G}gh_{j}gdg=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}h_{j}^{*}\int_{G}g^{2}dg=\alpha^{*}\mu_{2}(G).

(3.4)

To complete the proof we need to establish the relation (
3.3
). This is obtained from the following computation:

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(G)

\displaystyle=\mu_{2}(G)\cdot\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)}d\gamma=\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)}\gamma\gamma^{*}\mu_{2}(G)d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)}\gamma\cdot\left(\int_{G}g\gamma gdg\right)d\gamma

using (
3.4
)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)}\int_{G}(\gamma g)^{2}dgd\gamma=\int_{G}\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)}(\gamma g)^{2}d\gamma dg

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)}\gamma^{2}d\gamma dg

by Haar invariance on
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\mu_{2}(\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*))dg=\mu_{2}(\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)).

∎

3.2.
The key computation

We begin with the following consequence of the computations of Section
3.1
:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
*
-algebra and let
G\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a compact subgroup which generates the algebra
\mathfrak{A}
. Then for each
left
\mathfrak{A}
-module
\mathsf{X}
, and each Hermitian form
Q:\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{X}\to\mathfrak{A}
, we have

\int_{G}q(x+gy)gdg=(q(x)+q(y))\cdot\mu_{1}(G)+Q(x,y)\cdot(1_{\mathfrak{A}}+\mu_{2}(\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*))),

(3.5)

where
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
, and we set

q(x)=Q(x,x),\quad x\in\mathsf{X}.

Proof.

For
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
and
g\in G\subset\mathfrak{A}
we have

\displaystyle q(x+gy)

\displaystyle=Q(x+gy,x+gy)

\displaystyle=Q(x,x)+Q(x,gy)+Q(gy,x)+Q(gy,gy)

\displaystyle=q(x)+Q(x,y)g^{*}+gQ(y,x)+gq(y)g^{*}.

Therefore, multiplying on the right by
g
and integrating on
G
with respect to Haar measure we have

\int_{G}q(x+gy)gdg=A+B+C+D,

where

A=\int_{G}q(x)gdg=q(x)\mu_{1}(G),

B=\int_{G}Q(x,y)g^{*}gdg=\int_{G}Q(x,y)dg=Q(x,y)\int_{G}dg=Q(x,y),

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\int_{G}gQ(y,x)gdg=Q(y,x)^{*}\int_{G}g^{2}dg=Q(y,x)^{*}\mu_{2}(\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*))

by (
3.2
) and (
3.3
)

\displaystyle=Q(x,y)\mu_{2}(\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)).

and

D=\int_{G}gq(y)g^{*}gdg=\left(\int_{G}gdg\right)q(y)=\mu_{1}(G)q(y)=q(y)\mu_{1}(G),

where in the last step we use the fact that
\mu_{1}(G)
lies in the center of
\mathfrak{A}
(see Proposition
3.1
above).
Combining these equations, the result follows.
∎

3.3.
Second moments of matrix algebras

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)
be a polarizable algebra, and endow the matrix algebra
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
with the involution
*
given by
A\mapsto A^{*}=(A^{\sharp})^{T}
induced by
\sharp
as
in (
1.9
). Then by Proposition
2.6
,
the
*
-algebra
(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}),*)
is polarizable. The second moment of this algebra is
given by:

\mu_{2}(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}))=\dfrac{1}{n}\cdot\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A})I,

(3.6)

where
I
is the identity matrix of
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta:=\Delta_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}
be the collection of diagonal matrices in
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
with entries from the unitary group
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)

as in Proposition
2.6
. Let
H
be the cyclic group of permutation matrices in
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
corresponding to the
n
-cycle

\sigma=(1,2,\dots,n)\in S_{n}
,
i.e.

H=\{P_{\sigma^{k}}:0\leq k\leq n-1\},

where for
\tau\in S_{n}
,
P_{\tau}
is the permutation matrix such that
P_{\tau}e_{i}=e_{\tau(i)}
, where
e_{j}
is the column vector with 1 in the
j
-th row and zeroes
everywhere else.

A computation analogous to that in (
2.11
) shows that for
D=\diag(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in\Delta
(with
g_{j}\in\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)
) and
P_{\sigma^{k}}\in H
we have

DP_{\sigma^{k}}DP_{\sigma^{k}}=P_{\sigma^{2k}}D^{\prime\prime}D^{\prime}

where

D^{\prime}=\diag\left(g_{\sigma^{k}(1)},\dots,g_{\sigma^{k}(n)}\right),\quad D^{\prime\prime}=\diag\left(g_{\sigma^{2k}(1)},\dots,g_{\sigma^{2k}(n)}\right)\in\Delta_{G}.

Therefore we have (with
e_{1}^{T}
the row vector with 1 in the first slot and zeroes everywhere else):

\displaystyle e_{1}^{T}DP_{\sigma^{k}}DP_{\sigma^{k}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=e_{1}^{T}P_{\sigma^{2k}}D^{\prime\prime}D^{\prime}e_{1}

\displaystyle=e_{1}^{T}P_{\sigma^{2k}}D^{\prime\prime}g_{\sigma^{k}(1)}e_{1}

\displaystyle=e_{1}^{T}P_{\sigma^{2k}}g_{\sigma^{2k}(1)}g_{\sigma^{k}(1)}e_{1}

\displaystyle=e_{1}^{T}g_{\sigma^{2k}(1)}g_{\sigma^{k}(1)}e_{\sigma^{2k}(1)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}g_{1}g_{\sigma^{k}(1)}&\text{ if $\sigma^{2k}=\mathrm{id}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

By Proposition
3.1
the element
\mu_{2}(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}))
is in the center of
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
. It is not difficult to see that
the center of
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
consists of matrices of the form
zI
, where
z
belongs to
the center of
\mathfrak{A}
and
I
is the identity matrix of
M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
.
Therefore there is a
z
in the center of
\mathfrak{A}
such that
\mu_{2}(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}))=zI
. We have

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=e_{1}^{T}\mu_{2}(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}))e_{1}=\int_{H}\int_{\Delta}e_{1}^{T}DPDPe_{1}dDdP

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{\Delta}e_{1}^{T}DP_{\sigma^{k}}DP_{\sigma^{k}}e_{1}dD=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{2k}=\mathrm{id}\\
0\leq k\leq n-1\end{subarray}}\int_{\Delta}g_{1}g_{\sigma^{k}(1)}dD

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{2k}=\mathrm{id}\\
0\leq k\leq n-1\end{subarray}}\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}\dots\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}g_{1}g_{\sigma^{k}(1)}dg_{1}\dots dg_{n}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}{\left(\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}\dots\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}g_{1}^{2}dg_{1}\dots dg_{n}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{2k}=\mathrm{id}\\
1\leq k\leq n-1\end{subarray}}\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}\dots\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}g_{1}g_{\sigma^{k}(1)}dg_{1}\dots dg_{n}\right)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}{\left(\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}g_{1}^{2}dg_{1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{2k}=\mathrm{id}\\
1\leq k\leq n-1\end{subarray}}\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}g_{1}g_{\sigma^{k}(1)}dg_{1}dg_{\sigma^{k}(1)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}{\left(\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{2k}=\mathrm{id}\\
1\leq k\leq n-1\end{subarray}}{\left(\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}g_{1}dg_{1}\right)}{\left(\int_{\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)}g_{\sigma^{k}(1)}dg_{\sigma^{k}(1)}\right)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A}),

using in the last line the property (
1.6
) of polarizable subgroups such as
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)
.
∎

To use the above, we need to know the second moment of the algebra
(\mathfrak{A},\sharp)
. We now do this for the division algebras.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathbb{D}
be a real division algebra, thought of as a
*
-algebra with the standard conjugate-transpose involution. Then we have

\mu_{2}(\mathbb{D})=\frac{2}{\delta}-1,\quad\delta=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{D}=1,2,\text{or }4.

(3.7)

Consequently, for the
*
-algebra
(M_{n}(\mathbb{D}),*)
, we have

\mu_{2}(M_{n}(\mathbb{D}))=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{2}{\delta}-1\right)I_{n},

(3.8)

where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.

Proof.

We use the relation (
3.3
) to compute the second moments in (
3.7
). When
\mathbb{D}={\mathbb{R}}
, the trivial multiplicative subgroup
G=\{1\}
is compact, contained in the unitary group, and generates the algebra
\mathbb{D}
over
{\mathbb{R}}
, so we have

\mu_{2}({\mathbb{R}})=\mu_{2}(G)=1=\frac{2}{\dim_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{R}}}-1.

When
\mathbb{D}={\mathbb{C}}
, we can take
G
to be the group
\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
fourth roots of 1. Then we have

\mu_{2}({\mathbb{C}})=\frac{1}{4}\left(1^{2}+(-1)^{2}+i^{2}+(-i)^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{4}(1+1+(-1)+(-1))=0=\frac{2}{\dim_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{C}}}-1

verifying the assertion.

When
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{H}
, the 8 element “quaternion group”
G=\{\pm 1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}

is compact and spans
\mathbb{H}
over
{\mathbb{R}}
. We therefore have

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(1^{2}+(-1)^{2}+i^{2}+(-i)^{2}+j^{2}+(-j)^{2}+k^{2}+(-k)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}(1+1-1-1-1-1-1-1)=-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{2}{\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}}-1,

verifying (
3.7
) in all cases. The formula (
3.8
) now follows using Proposition
3.5
.
∎

3.4.
End of proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

We have now assembled all the ingredients to prove these two results, which we will prove
simultaneously. As in Theorem
1.1
, let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a polarizable
*
-algebra,
and let
G\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a polarizing subgroup, and let
Q,q
and
\mathsf{X}
also
have the same meaning as in the statement of that theorem. By hypothesis (
1.6
)
we have, using the computation (
3.5
), that for
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
we have:

\int_{G}q(x+gy)gdg=Q(x,y)\cdot(1_{\mathfrak{A}}+\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A})),

(3.9)

so to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
we need to show that the element

1_{\mathfrak{A}}+\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A})

(3.10)

of
\mathfrak{A}
is a unit of the ring
\mathfrak{A}
, i.e., it is invertible, and its inverse is the polarization constant
\kappa
of the algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)
, i.e.

\kappa=\kappa(\mathfrak{A},*)=(1_{\mathfrak{A}}+\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A}))^{-1}.

(3.11)

By Proposition
2.5
, the
*
-isomorphism
\phi
of (
1.11
) exists if
(\mathfrak{A},*)
is polarizable, and by Proposition
2.7

if
\phi
exists, then
(\mathfrak{A},*)
is polarizable.
To complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we need to show that the multiplicative inverse of the element (
3.10
) is given by the formula (
1.12
).

Thanks to the invariance of the problem under
*
-isomorphisms, it is clear that we only need to consider the case where

(\mathfrak{A},*)
is already a
*
-direct sum of the matrix algebras
(M_{n}(\mathbb{D}_{j}),*)
where the involution is the conjugate-transpose involution, i.e. the map
\phi
of (
1.11
)
is the identity.
In this case the element (
3.10
) is given by

\displaystyle 1_{\mathfrak{A}}+\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A})

\displaystyle=1_{\mathfrak{A}}+\left(\mu_{2}(M_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{D}_{1}))I_{1},\dots,\mu_{2}(M_{n_{N}}(\mathbb{D}_{N})I_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=\left((1+\mu_{2}(M_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{D}_{1}))I_{1},\dots,(1+\mu_{2}(M_{n_{N}}(\mathbb{D}_{N}))I_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{1}}-1\right)\right)I_{1},\dots,\left(1+\frac{1}{n_{N}}\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{N}}-1\right)\right)I_{N}\right).

Now since for each
j
we have
\delta_{j}\leq 4
and
n_{j}\geq 1
, therefore

1+\frac{1}{n_{j}}\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{j}}-1\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}.

It follows that the element (
3.10
) is invertible, thus completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
. It also follows that its inverse is

(1_{\mathfrak{A}}+\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A}))^{-1}=\left(\frac{n_{1}\delta_{1}}{(n_{1}-1)\delta_{1}+2}I_{n_{1}},\dots,\frac{n_{j}\delta_{j}}{(n_{j}-1)\delta_{j}+2}I_{n_{j}},\dots,\frac{n_{N}\delta_{N}}{(n_{N}-1)\delta_{N}+2}I_{n_{N}}\right),

completing the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

4.
Quadrances and Hermitian forms

4.1.
Quadrances arising from Hermitian forms

Proposition 4.1
.

If
Q
is a Hermitian form on a left
\mathfrak{A}
-module
\mathsf{X}
, then the diagonal restriction

q:\mathsf{X}\to\mathfrak{A},\quad q(x)=Q(x,x),\text{ where }x\in\mathsf{X}

is a quadrance, and the
generalized parallelogram identity holds
:
for a compact subgroup
H\subseteq\mathfrak{A}^{\times}
such that

(a)

q(hx)=q(x)
for all
h\in H
, and

(b)

\mu_{1}(H)=0

hold, we have for
x,y\in X
,

q(x)+q(y)=\int_{H}q(x+hy)dh.

(4.1)

Notice that any real
*
-algebra always has a subgroup
H
of
\mathfrak{A}^{\times}
that satisfies the hypotheses of the above proposition. This is
the two element subgroup
H=\{\pm 1_{\mathfrak{A}}\}
for which (
4.1
)
becomes the
classical parallelogram identity
(
1.15
)
which reduces to (
1.13
) for
inner product spaces over
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathsf{X},\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}
we have
Q(x,x)^{*}=Q(x,x)
, i.e.

q(x)^{*}=q(x),

and
Q(\alpha x,\alpha x)=\alpha Q(x,x)\alpha^{*}
, i.e.

q(\alpha x)=\alpha q(x)\alpha^{*},
thus verifying the first two conditions in the definition of a quadrance (Definition
1.3
above).
For
x,y,\in\mathsf{X}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}={\mathbb{R}}\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{A}},\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}
, we have

\begin{split}q(\lambda x+\alpha y)&=Q(\lambda x+\alpha y,\lambda x+\alpha y)\\
&=Q(\lambda x,\lambda x)=Q(\lambda x,\alpha y)+Q(\alpha y,\lambda x)+Q(\alpha y,\alpha y)\\
&=\lambda^{2}q(x)+\lambda Q(x,y)\alpha^{*}+\alpha Q(y,x)\lambda+\alpha q(y)\alpha^{*}.\end{split}

Since multiplication in
\mathfrak{A}
is continuous, it follows that the mapping (
1.14
) is continuous. This shows that
q
is a quadrance.

For
x,y\in X
a computation yields (
4.1
):

\begin{split}\int_{H}q(x+hy)dh&=\int_{H}Q(x+hy,x+hy)dh\\
&=\int_{H}q(x)dh+\int_{H}q(hy)dh+\int_{H}Q(x,hy)dh+\int_{H}Q(hy,x)dh\\
&=\int_{H}q(x)dh+\int_{H}q(y)dh+Q(x,y)\int_{H}h^{*}dh+{\left(\int_{H}hdh\right)}Q(y,x)\\
&=q(x)+q(y),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle{\int_{H}h^{*}dh={\left(\int_{H}hdh\right)}^{*}=0}

by
{\mathbb{R}}
-linearity of
*
.

∎

Remark:
It would be very interesting to know if the classical parallelogram identity
(
1.15
) can be replaced in Theorem
1.3
by a generalized parallelogram identity with respect to some other compact group
H\subset\mathfrak{A}^{\times}
.

4.2.
Polarization of quadrances and proof of Theorem
1.3

The proof of Theorem
1.3
consists of applying the operation on the right hand side of (
1.8
) to the given quadrance, and showing that the resulting function is a Hermitian form. The following is the first step in the argument:

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},*)
be a polarizable
*
-algebra and let
\mathsf{X}
be a left
\mathfrak{A}
-module. Let
q\colon\mathsf{X}\to\mathfrak{A}
be a quadrance on
\mathsf{X}
and let
G\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{A})
be a polarizing subgroup. Define

Q_{G}\colon\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{X}\to\mathfrak{A}
by

Q_{G}(x,y)=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(x+gy)gdg,

(4.2)

where
\kappa
is the polarization constant of
(\mathfrak{A},*)
.
Then
Q_{G}
satisfies the following properties, for all
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
:

(a)

Q_{G}(x,x)=q(x).

(b)

Q_{G}(x,y)^{*}=Q_{G}(y,x)
.

(c)

Q_{G}(hx,y)=hQ_{G}(x,y)
for all
h\in G
.

(d)

Q_{G}(x,0)=0
.

Proof.

(a)

We compute

\displaystyle Q_{G}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q((1+g)x)gdg=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}(1+g)q(x)(1+g)^{*}gdg

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot{\left(q(x)\int_{G}gdg+q(x)\int_{G}dg+\int_{G}gq(x)gdg+{\left(\int_{G}gdg\right)}q(x)\right)}

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot{\left(q(x)+q(x)^{*}\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A})\right)}=\kappa\cdot(1_{\mathfrak{A}}+\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{A}))q(x)=q(x),

using the facts that
q(x)^{*}=q(x)
and
\mu_{1}(G)=0
,

(b)

We see from the formula (
1.12
) that
the polarization constant
\kappa
satisfies
\kappa^{*}=\kappa
and lies in the center of
\mathfrak{A}
. Therefore we
can compute:

\begin{split}Q_{G}(x,y)^{*}&=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}(q(x+gy)g)^{*}dg=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}g^{*}q(x+gy)dg\\
&=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(g^{*}x+y)g^{*}dg=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(gx+y)gdg\\
&=Q_{G}(y,x).\end{split}

(c)

This follows by an application of Haar invariance, and the properties of
\kappa
used in the previous part:

\begin{split}Q_{G}(hx,y)&=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(hx+gy)gdg=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}hq(x+h^{*}gy)h^{*}gdg\\
&=\kappa\cdot h\int_{G}q(x+h^{*}gy)h^{*}gdg=h\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(x+gy)gdg\\
&=hQ_{G}(x,y).\end{split}

(d)

We have:
\displaystyle{Q_{G}(x,0)=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(x)gdg=0.}

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
G
be a polarizing subgroup of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
(for example, we could take

G
to be
\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
itself). Define the map
Q_{G}:\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{X}\to\mathfrak{A}
by the formula
(
4.2
). We will prove that

Q_{G}
is a Hermitian form on
\mathsf{X}
; by part (a) of Proposition
4.2
, the result would follow if we can also show uniqueness: if
Q
is a Hermitian form such that

Q(x,x)=q(x)
, then we have
Q=Q_{G}
. But by the polarization identity (
1.8
) of Theorem
1.1
we have for
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
that

Q(x,y)=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(x+gy)gdg=Q_{G}(x,y).

Thanks to the properties of
Q
already established in Proposition
4.2
,
it is enough to show that
Q_{G}
is
\mathfrak{A}
-linear in the first argument.
For any
x,y\in\mathsf{X}
we have

\displaystyle Q_{G}(2x,y)

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(x+x+gy)gdg

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}{\left(2{\left(q(x)+q(x+gy)\right)}-q(gy)\right)}gdg

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot{\left(2\int_{G}q(x)gdg+2\int_{G}q(x+gy)gdg-\int_{G}gq(y)dg\right)}

\displaystyle=2\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(x+gy)gdg

\displaystyle=2Q_{G}(x,y),

where we have used the parallelogram identity (
1.15
) to get the second equality. Using part
(b)
of Proposition
4.2
we have:

Q_{G}(x,2y)=Q_{G}(2y,x)^{*}=2Q_{G}(y,x)^{*}=2Q_{G}(x,y).

(4.3)

Now, for
x,y,z\in\mathsf{X}
we have using the classical parallelogram identity (
1.15
) that

\begin{split}Q_{G}(x,z)+Q_{G}(y,z)&=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}{\left(q(x+gz)+q(y+gz)\right)}gdg\\
&=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}\dfrac{1}{2}{\left(q(x+y+2gz)+q(x-y)\right)}gdg\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(x+y+g(2z))gdg+\dfrac{1}{2}\kappa\cdot q(x-y)\cdot\int_{G}gdg\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}Q_{G}(x+y,2z),\end{split}

and so, by (
4.3
), it follows that
Q_{G}(x,z)+Q_{G}(y,z)=Q_{G}(x+y,z)
for all
x,y,z\in X
.
From this we deduce easily that
Q_{G}(\cdot,z)
is
\mathbb{Q}
-homogeneous, i.e.
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
we have

Q(\lambda x,y)=\lambda Q(x,y).

(4.4)

To extend (
4.4
) to the situation when
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, fix

x,y\in\mathsf{X}
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and let
\{\lambda_{n}\}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{Q}
converging to
\lambda
. Let
\{F_{n}\}
be the sequence of
\mathfrak{A}
-valued functions on
G
given by

F_{n}(g)=q(\lambda_{n}x+gy)g
. Since the
\mathfrak{A}
-valued function
(\xi,g)\mapsto q(\xi x+gy)g
is uniformly continuous on the compact space
[\lambda-1,\lambda+1]\times G
, it follows that

F_{n}\to F
uniformly on
G
where
F(g)=q(\lambda x+gy)g
. Therefore

\displaystyle\lambda Q_{G}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}Q_{G}(x,y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{G}(\lambda_{n}x,y)=\kappa\cdot\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{G}q(\lambda_{n}x+gy)gdg

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot\int_{G}q(\lambda x+gy)gdg\quad\text{by uniform convergence}

\displaystyle=Q_{G}(\lambda x,y).

Finally, letting
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i}\in\mathfrak{A}
for some
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
we have

Q_{G}(\alpha x,y)=Q_{G}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}g_{j}x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i}Q_{G}(x,y)=\alpha Q_{G}(x,y)

completing the proof that
Q_{G}
is a Hermitian form. We have already proved uniqueness above.
∎

5.
Some Examples

5.1.
The three real division algebras

We illustrate Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
by classifying all the polarizing subgroups of
{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{H}
, and therefore obtaining all polarization formulas for Hermitian forms defined on vector spaces over these scalars. By (
3.7
),
the polarization constants of the algebras (made into
*
-algebras with the usual conjugation operations), are

\kappa(\mathbb{D})=\left(1+\mu_{2}(\mathbb{D})\right)^{-1}=\left(1+\left(\frac{2}{\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{D})}-1\right)\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{D}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&\text{ if $\mathbb{D}={\mathbb{R}}$}\\
1&\text{ if $\mathbb{D}={\mathbb{C}}$}\\
2&\text{ if $\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{H}$}.\end{cases}

(5.1)

5.1.1.
The real numbers

In this case the unitary group is
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}},\mathrm{id})=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}:x^{2}=1\}=\{\pm 1\}
. Therefore the only polarizing subgroup of the reals is the group
\{\pm 1\}
. The
polarization formula for a Hermitian form
Q
on a real vector space is (which in this situation is simply a bilinear form):

\displaystyle Q(x,y)

\displaystyle=\kappa\int_{\{\pm 1\}}q(x+gy)gdg=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot q(x+y)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot q(x-y)\cdot(-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(q(x+y)-q(x-y)\right).

Of course, at the bottom, after diagonalizing the bilinear form, this is nothing but the identity
xy=\frac{1}{4}\left((x+y)^{2}-(x-y)^{2}\right)
for real numbers.

5.1.2.
Locus classicus: complex numbers

For
({\mathbb{C}},\mathrm{conj})
, the unitary group is
\Gamma({\mathbb{C}},\mathrm{conj})=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\left|z\right|=1\}=U(1)
. Any compact subgroup of
this one dimensional Lie group is either a finite subgroup, or the whole of
U(1)
. It is easy to see that a finite subgroup of
U(1)
is a cyclic group
G_{N}=\{\omega^{j}:0\leq j\leq N-1\}

generated by the primitive
N
-th root of unity
\omega=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{N}\right)
. Notice that
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G_{N}={\mathbb{C}}
if and only if
N\geq 3
. It is clear also that
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}U(1)={\mathbb{C}}
,
and thanks to part (b) of Proposition
3.2
, condition (
1.6
) holds for both
G_{N}
and
U(1)
. It follows that any polarizing subgroup of
{\mathbb{C}}
is either
U(1)
or

G_{N}
for
N\geq 3
.

For the group
G_{N}
, we obtain the “
N
-th root polarization identity” (
[
You88
, p. 12, Problem 1.10]
or
[
D’A02
, pp. 53–54]
)

Q(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q(x+\omega^{k}y)\omega^{k},\quad\omega=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{N}},\text{ where }N\geq 3,

(5.2)

of which (
1.1
) is the special case for
N=4
and
Q=\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
. For the polarizing group
G=U(1)
we obtain the “integral polarization identity”

Q(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}q(x+e^{i\theta}y)e^{i\theta}d\theta,

(5.3)

which can also be obtained from (
5.2
) as the limit when
N\to\infty
, by interpreting its right hand side as a Riemann sum of the integral on the right hand side of (
5.3
). Notice that this passage to the limit is possible only because in the generalized polarization identity (
1.8
), the constant
\kappa
depends only on the
algebra of scalars
\mathfrak{A}
and not on the polarizing group
G
.

5.1.3.
The quaternions

Just as with the complex numbers, there is an abundant
supply of polarizing subgroups in the quaternions:

Proposition 5.1
.

A subgroup of
\mathbb{H}^{\times}
is polarizing if and only if it is compact and nonabelian.

Proof.

Let
G
be a compact subgroup of
\mathbb{H}^{\times}
. The map
\mathbb{H}^{\times}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
given by

x\mapsto\left\|x\right\|
is a continuous homomorphism of the group of nonzero quaternions under multiplication to the group of positive real numbers under multiplication,
and therefore the image of a compact subgroup
G
of
\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}
is a compact subgroup of
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, i.e.,
\{1\}
, so that
G\subset Sp(1)
.

Now suppose that
G
is polarizing in
\mathbb{H}
, so in particular
G
generates
\mathbb{H}
.
Since
\mathbb{H}
is not commutative, it follows that
G
must be nonabelian.

For the converse assume that
G
is nonabelian, and let
\mathfrak{B}
be the subalgebra of
\mathbb{H}
generated by the group
G
. Then
\mathfrak{B}
(with the involution induced from
\mathbb{H}
) is noncommutative and polarizable, since it is generated by a compact nonabelian subgroup
G\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{B},\mathrm{conj})
of
unitary elements (see Proposition
2.4
). Then by Theorem
1.2
the algebra
\mathfrak{B}
is isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{D}_{j})
for some real division algebras
\mathbb{D}_{j}
and positive integers
n_{j}
. Therefore,
if
\delta_{j}=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{D}_{j})\in\{1,2,4\}
we will have

{1\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{j}n_{j}^{2}\leq 3.}

It follows that
\mathfrak{B}
is isomorphic as an algebra to one of

{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{R}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}\oplus{\mathbb{C}},

each of which is commutative. Since
\mathbb{H}
is not commutative, this shows that
\mathfrak{B}=\mathbb{H}
.
Since
\mathbb{H}
, being a division ring, is simple, the condition (
1.6
) now follows from part (b) of Proposition
3.2
.

∎

Therefore the task of classifying all polarizing subgroups of
the quaternions (and therefore finding all quaternionic polarization identities) is reduced to the classification of
closed nonabelian subgroups of
Sp(1)
. Notice that
if
G
is a polarizing subgroup in a noncommutative polarizable algebra
(\mathfrak{A},*)
, then any subgroup
H
of the unitary group which is conjugate to
G
(i.e., there is a

\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathfrak{A},*)
such that
H=\gamma G\gamma^{-1}
) is clearly also polarizing. Therefore it will be sufficient to classify polarizing subgroups up to conjugacy.

Proposition 5.2
.

A subgroup of
\Gamma(\mathbb{H},\mathrm{conj})=Sp(1)
is polarizing if and only if it is one of the following:

(1)

The full group
Sp(1)
.

(2)

A subgroup conjugate to the group

\{\cos\theta+i\sin\theta:0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\}\cup\{j\cos\phi+k\sin\phi:0\leq\phi\leq 2\pi\},

(5.4)

a Lie group isomorphic to
O(2)
.

(3)

A finite subgroup of
\mathbb{H}

conjugate to one of the groups in the following table,
where

\omega=\frac{1}{2}\left(-1+i+j+k\right),

and the last row corresponds to an infinity of groups, one for each integer
n\geq 2
.

Traditional name

Order

Generators

Binary tetrahedral (2
A_{4}
)

24

\langle\omega,i\rangle

Binary octahedral (2
S_{4}
)

48

\left\langle\omega,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(j+k)\right\rangle

Binary icosahedral (2
A_{5}
)

120

\left\langle\omega,\frac{1}{2}i+\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{4}j+\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{4}k\right\rangle

Binary dihedral (2
D_{2n}
)

4n

\left\langle\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)+\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)i,j\right\rangle

(
n\geq 2
an integer)

Proof.

The simply connected Lie group
Sp(1)
is geometrically the three dimensional sphere
S^{3}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
and is isomorphic to
SU(2)
. Its Lie algebra
L
can therefore be identified with the tangent space to
S^{3}
at
(1,0,0,0)
and therefore to the space of purely vector
quaternions
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}=\{x_{1}i+x_{2}j+x_{3}k:x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
. The Lie bracket on
L
is

[X,Y]=XY-YX=(-\langle X,Y\rangle+X\times Y)-(-\langle Y,X\rangle+Y\times X)=2(X\times Y),

where
\times
denotes the cross product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product.
This Lie algebra
L
cannot have a subalgebra of dimension 2, since as soon as we have in the subalgebra two linearly independent vectors
u,v
, their Lie bracket
2(u\times v)
is another vector linearly independent of
u
and
v
. Therefore a polarizing subgroup
G
of
Sp(1)
can be of dimension 3, 1 or 0 (i.e. finite). The only Lie subalgebra of
L
of dimension 3 is
L
itself, and the corresponding subgroup of
Sp(1)
is
Sp(1)
, which is
polarizing as the unitary group of
\mathbb{H}
.

Now let
G
be a polarizing subgroup of
Sp(1)
of dimension 1, and let
G^{0}
be its identity component. Then there is a purely vector quaternion
{u}\in L
with
\left\|u\right\|=1

such that
G^{0}=\{\exp(\theta{u}):\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
. Since
u^{2}=-1
, it follows that
\exp(\theta u)=\cos\theta+(\sin\theta)u
. Since
G^{0}
is abelian,

G\not=G^{0}
. Let
z\in G\setminus G^{0}
, so that, since
G^{0}
is a normal subgroup of
G
we have
zG^{0}z^{-1}=G^{0}
.
Therefore for each
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
there is a
\phi\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
z\exp(\theta u)z^{-1}=\exp(\phi u)
. By looking at the scalar and vector parts, we see that
\cos\theta=\cos\phi
and
(\sin\theta)zuz^{-1}=(\sin\phi)u
. The first equation gives
\theta=\pm\phi\,\mathrm{mod}\,2\pi
, which when substituted into the second gives
zuz^{-1}=\pm u
.
Recall that for a unit quaternion
z\in Sp(1)
, the map
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}=\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}\ni w\mapsto zwz^{-1}
is a rotation of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
whose axis is spanned by the vector part
\operatorname{Im}z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
of
z
.
Therefore, if
zuz^{-1}=u
, then
z=\cos\alpha+(\sin\alpha)u
for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, which means that
z\in G^{0}
which contradicts
z\not\in G^{0}
. Therefore we must have

zuz^{-1}=-u
. This means that the rotation
w\mapsto zwz^{-1}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
has for axis a unit vector
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
orthogonal to
u
and has angle of rotation
\pi
, so that

z=\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)+\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)v=v
. Therefore each element of
G\setminus G^{0}
is a unit vector of
\operatorname{Im}(\mathbb{H})
orthogonal to
u
. If
v\in G\setminus G^{0}
, notice that each element of the coset
G^{0}v=\{\left(\cos\theta\right)v+(\sin\theta)uv:\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
is a unit vector of
\operatorname{Im}(\mathbb{H})
orthogonal to
u
, therefore

G=\{\cos\theta+(\sin\theta)u:0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\}\cup\{\left(\cos\phi\right)v+(\sin\phi)uv:0\leq\phi\leq 2\pi\}.

There is an element
A\in SO(3)
which maps the ordered basis
\{u,v,uv=u\times v\}
into the standard basis
\{i,j,k\}
. Thanks to the double covering
Sp(1)\to SO(3)
then there is a
\gamma\in Sp(1)
such that for each
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}=\operatorname{Im}(\mathbb{H})
we have
Aw=\gamma w\gamma^{-1}
. It follows that
\gamma G\gamma^{-1}
is the group of (
5.4
).

Finite multiplicative subgroups of
\mathbb{H}^{\times}

can be classified up to conjugacy by using the double cover
Sp(1)\to SO(3)
and then using the well-known classical classification of the finite subgroups of rotations of three dimensional space (see, e.g.,
[
CS03
, p. 33]
). Using this classification one obtains the complete list of nonabelian finite subgroups of
\mathbb{H}^{\times}
in the table above, and therefore of finite polarizing subgroups of
\mathbb{H}
.

∎

5.2.
Group algebras

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
\mathfrak{A}={\mathbb{R}}[G]
be its real group algebra. Recall that this consists of formal linear combinations

\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g
, where
a_{g}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, with the natural
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear structure and a bilinear, associative multiplication induced by the multiplication operation of the group
G
. We can define
an involution
*
on
\mathfrak{A}
by the
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear extension of the inversion operation on the group
G
:

\left(\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\right)^{*}=\left(\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g^{-1}\right),

which is an involution since
(gh)^{*}=(gh)^{-1}=h^{-1}g^{-1}=h^{*}g^{*}
. The identity
1_{\mathfrak{A}}
of the algebra
\mathfrak{A}
is clearly
1\cdot e_{G}
, where
e_{G}
is the identity element of the group
G
.

Identifying
g\in G
with the element
1\cdot g\in{\mathbb{R}}[G]
we see that
G\subset\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}[G],*)
, since for
g\in G
, we have
gg^{*}=gg^{-1}=e_{G}
. It follows that
G
is a compact subgroup of the unitary group of
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
which generates it as an algebra, and
consequently
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
is polarizable, thanks to Proposition
2.4
. The group

\{(\pm 1)g:g\in G\}\subset\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}[G],*)

isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times G
contains
-1\cdot e_{g}
, hence is a polarizing group in
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
by Proposition
3.2
. Theorem
1.2
in this case reduces to the classical Maschke’s theorem (semisimplicity of the group algebra). The polarization constant of
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
can be computed in terms of
the representation theory of the group
G
. We introduce the following notation:

•

\mathrm{Irr}(G)
is the set of irreducible complex characters of
G
,

•

\deg\chi=\chi(e_{G})
is the degree of
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, i.e. the dimension of the vector space on which the representation affording
\chi
is defined, and

•

for
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
,
s(\chi)
denotes the Frobenius-Schur indicator of
\chi
. Recall that this is respectively
1,0
or
-1
according
to whether the representation affording
\chi
is real, complex or quternionic.

Proposition 5.4
.

Then

\kappa({\mathbb{R}}[G],*)=\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}\left(\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\frac{(\deg\chi)^{2}\cdot\overline{\chi(g)}}{\deg\chi+s(\chi)}\right)\cdot g.

(5.5)

Notice that if
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
is afforded by a quaternionic representation, we must have
\deg\chi\geq 2
. Therefore in all cases we have

\deg\chi+s(\chi)\geq 1
. It is also not difficult to see that the coefficient of each
g\in G
in the sum on the right hand side of (
5.5
) is real valued.

Proof.

Let

r_{2}(g)=\left|\{x\in G:x^{2}=g\}\right|

be the number of square roots of the element
g
in the group
G
. Then with respect to the inner product
(\phi,\psi)=\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}\phi(g)\overline{\psi(g)}
on the space of complex-valued functions on
G
, we have for
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
:

(r_{2},\chi)=\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}r_{2}(g)\overline{\chi(g)}=\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\chi(g^{2})}=\overline{s(\chi)}=s(\chi),

where we use the representation
s(\chi)=\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}{\chi(g^{2})}
and the fact that
s
is real valued.
Since
r_{2}:G\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a class function, which corresponds to the fact (Proposition
3.1
) that

\mu_{2}(G)
is in the center of
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
, and since
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
is an orthonormal basis of the class functions with respect to the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
defined above, we can write

r_{2}=\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}(r_{2},\chi)\chi=\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}s(\chi)\chi.

(5.6)

Since
G
is a compact (even finite) subgroup of the algebra
\mathfrak{A}={\mathbb{R}}[G]
generating it as an algebra, the second moment of
\mathfrak{A}
can be
computed using (
3.3
):

\displaystyle\mu_{2}({\mathbb{R}}[G])

\displaystyle=\mu_{2}(G)=\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}g^{2}=\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}r_{2}(g)g

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}r_{2}(g^{-1})g\quad\text{ since $r_{2}(g)=r_{2}(g^{-1})$ }

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}\left(\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}s(\chi)\chi(g^{-1})\right)g\quad\text{using \eqref{eq-r2}.}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\frac{s(\chi)}{\deg\chi}\cdot\epsilon_{\chi},

where for
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, we set

\epsilon_{\chi}=\frac{\deg\chi}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g^{-1})g.

(5.7)

It is known (see
[
Isa76
, Chapter 2]
) that the collection of elements
\{\epsilon_{\chi}:\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[G]
form a complete set of primitive orthogonal idempotents in the commutative ring
Z({\mathbb{R}}[G])
, the center of
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
, i.e. they satisfy

\epsilon_{\chi}^{2}=\epsilon_{\chi},\quad\epsilon_{\chi}\epsilon_{\lambda}=0\quad\text{ for }\chi,\lambda\in\mathrm{Irr}(G),\chi\not=\lambda,\quad\text{and }\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\epsilon_{\chi}=1_{{\mathbb{R}}[G]}.

Therefore by (
3.11
) we have

\displaystyle\kappa({\mathbb{R}}[G],*)

\displaystyle=\left(1_{{\mathbb{R}}[G]}+\mu_{2}(G)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\epsilon_{\chi}+\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\frac{s(\chi)}{\deg\chi}\cdot\epsilon_{\chi}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\left(1+\frac{s(\chi)}{\deg\chi}\right)\epsilon_{\chi}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\frac{\deg\chi}{\deg\chi+s(\chi)}\cdot\epsilon_{\chi}.

Substituting
\epsilon_{\chi}
from (
5.7
) leads to the formula (
5.5
).
∎

5.3.
Clifford algebras

For nonnegative integers
p,q
, let
\mathsf{C}_{p,q}
be the real Clifford algebra of signature
(p,q)
(see
[
LM89
]
). Recall that this is a
*
-algebra over
{\mathbb{R}}
of dimension
2^{p+q}
generated by the
p+q
elements
\{e_{i}:1\leq i\leq p+q\}
satisfying the relations

e_{i}^{2}=1\quad\text{ for }1\leq i\leq p,\quad e_{j}^{2}=-1\quad\text{ for }p\leq j\leq p+q,\quad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i}\quad\text{ for }i\not=j.

The involution of
\mathsf{C}_{p,q}
is determined by
e_{i}^{*}=e_{i},1\leq i\leq p
and
e_{i}^{*}=-e_{i},p+1\leq i\leq p+q
.
If for
I={\left\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\right\}}\subseteq{\left\{1,\dots,p+q\right\}}
we set

e_{I}=e_{i_{1}}e_{i_{2}}\dots e_{i_{r}}

then
e_{I}^{*}=e_{i_{r}}^{*}e_{i_{r-1}}^{*}\dots e_{i_{1}}^{*}
.
It is easy to see that

G_{p,q}={\left\{\pm e_{I}\colon I={\left\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\right\}}\subseteq{\left\{1,\dots,p+q\right\}}\right\}}

is a finite subgroup of
\Gamma(\mathsf{C}_{p,q},*)
which generates
\mathsf{C}_{p,q}
. Therefore by part (3) of Proposition
2.4
the algebra
\mathsf{C}_{p,q}
is polarizable.
We now find its polarization constant.

Proposition 5.5
.

\displaystyle{\kappa(\mathsf{C}_{p,q})=\dfrac{1}{1+{2^{-\frac{p+q-1}{2}}}\cos{\left(\frac{p-q-1}{4}\pi\right)}}}

Proof.

Suppose that
I={\left\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\right\}}\subseteq{\left\{1,\dots,p+q\right\}}
, so that
\left|I\right|=k
. Assume that
\left|I\cap{\left\{1,\dots,p\right\}}\right|=\ell
. Then

\displaystyle e_{I}^{2}

\displaystyle=e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{\ell}}e_{i_{\ell+1}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}\cdot e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{\ell}}e_{i_{\ell+1}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}\cdots(-1)^{1}e_{i_{1}}^{2}\cdots e_{i_{\ell}}^{2}e_{i_{\ell+1}}^{2}\cdots e_{i_{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}(-1)^{k-\ell}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}(-1)^{\ell}.

For such an
I
there are
\begin{pmatrix}p\\
\ell\end{pmatrix}
possibilities for
I\cap{\left\{1,\cdots,p\right\}}
and
\begin{pmatrix}q\\
k-\ell\end{pmatrix}
possibilities for
I\setminus{\left\{1,\cdots,p\right\}}
. Hence there are
\begin{pmatrix}p\\
\ell\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}q\\
k-\ell\end{pmatrix}
such
I
and

\mu_{2}(\mathsf{C}_{p,q})=\mu_{2}(G_{p,q})=\dfrac{1}{2^{p+q}}\sum_{I}e_{I}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{2^{p+q}}\sum_{k=0}^{p+q}(-1)^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
\ell\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}q\\
k-\ell\end{pmatrix}(-1)^{\ell}.

Next observe that
(-1)^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}
is periodic in
k
with period
4
. The equality

(-1)^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}=\operatorname{Re}((1+i)i^{k})

is easily verified by periodicity. From this we get

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(\mathsf{C}_{p,q})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2^{p+q}}\operatorname{Re}{\left((1+i)\sum_{k=0}^{p+q}i^{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
\ell\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}q\\
k-\ell\end{pmatrix}(-1)^{\ell}\right)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2^{p+q}}\operatorname{Re}{\left((1+i){\left(\sum_{r=0}^{p}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
r\end{pmatrix}(-1)^{r}i^{r}\right)}{\left(\sum_{s=0}^{q}\begin{pmatrix}q\\
s\end{pmatrix}i^{s}\right)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2^{p+q}}\operatorname{Re}{\left((1-i)^{p}(1+i)^{q+1}\right)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2^{\frac{p+q+1}{2}}}{2^{p+q}}\operatorname{Re}{\left(e^{-p\pi i/4}e^{(q+1)\pi i/4}\right)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2^{\frac{p+q-1}{2}}}\cos{\left(\frac{p-q-1}{4}\pi\right)}.

The result follows by (
3.11
).
∎

For the algebras
\mathsf{C}_{0,q}
, the polarization identity corresponding to the group
G_{0,q}
was found in
[
GR93
]
. The case
q=2
(quaternions) was already done in
[
Jam70
]

5.3.1.
Matrix algebras

A simple special case of Theorem
1.2
is that
the matrix algebras
M_{n}(\mathbb{D})
where
D={\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}},\mathbb{H}
with the conjugate-transpose involution are polarizable,
and the polarization constant is
\kappa=\frac{n\delta}{(n-1)\delta+2}I_{n}
, where
\delta=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{D}
.
Here we note some further information about polarizing subgroups of
M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
and
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
obtained by using the classical theorem of Burnside that a subgroup of
GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
spans
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
over
{\mathbb{C}}
if and only if the natural representation of
G
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by matrix multiplication is irreducible (see e.g.
[
Lan02
]
):

Proposition 5.6
.

(1)

A compact subgroup
G
of
\Gamma(M_{n}({\mathbb{R}}))=O(n)
is polarizing in
M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
if and only if
G\not=\{I_{n}\}
and the natural representation of
G
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is irreducible.

(2)

A compact subgroup
G
of
\Gamma(M_{n}({\mathbb{C}}))=\mathrm{U}(n)
is polarizing in
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
if and only if
G\not=\{I_{n}\}
, the natural representation of
G
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is irreducible and
iI_{n}\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G
where
I_{n}
is the identity in
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
is polarizing in
M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
. Then
\mu_{1}(G)=0
implies that
G
is nontrivial. Since
G
generates
M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
as a real algebra it follows that
G
generates
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
as a complex algebra. Thus Burnside’s theorem implies that the natural action of
G
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is irreducible.

Conversely, if
G
acts irreducibly on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, then
G
generates
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
as a complex algebra by Burnside’s theorem. Since the matrices have real entries,
G
generates
M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
as a real algebra. Then by part (b) of Proposition
3.2
,
\mu_{1}(G)=0
. Therefore
G
is polarizing in
M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Now suppose that
G
is polarizing in
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. Again,
\mu_{1}(G)=0
implies that
G
is nontrivial. Since
G
generates
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
as a real algebra, i.e.
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G=M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, it follows that
iI_{n}\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G
. It follows that
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}G
, and so Burnside’s theorem says that the natural action of
G
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is irreducible.

Conversely, suppose that
G
is nontrivial, the natural representation of
G
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is irreducible, and
iI_{n}\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G
. Burnside’s theorem yields that
G
generates
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
as a complex algebra, i.e.
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}G=M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. But as was noted above, the fact that
iI_{n}\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G
implies that
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}G=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}G=M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. Then again
Proposition
3.2
shows that

\mu_{1}(G)=0
. Therefore
G
is polarizing in
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
.
∎

As a result we have the following:

Proposition 5.7
.

The group
SO(n)
(resp.
SU(n)
) is polarizing in
M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
(resp.
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
) if and only if
n\geq 3
.

Proof.

If
n=1,2
it is clear that
SO(n)
(resp.
SU(n)
) fails to span the algebra
M_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
(resp.
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
): note that
SO(2)
(resp.
SU(2)
) generates an algebra isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}
(resp.
\mathbb{H}
). Also it is not difficult to show that the natural representation of
SO(n)
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is irreducible using Schur’s lemma. The same holds for
SU(n)
as it contains
SO(n)
. To complete the proof we only need to verify that
iI_{n}\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(SU(n))
for
n\geq 3
.

If
n=4k
for some
k\geq 1
, then in fact
iI_{n}\in SU(n)
. If
n=4k+1
for some
k\geq 1
, consider the diagonal matrices
D_{\ell}\in M_{4k+1}({\mathbb{C}})
with a
1
in the
\ell
-th entry and an
i
in each other entry. Each of these is in
SU(4k+1)
and
D_{\ell}-D_{\ell}^{-1}
is the diagonal matix with a
0
in the
\ell
-th entry and
2i
in each of the other entries. Thus

iI_{4k+1}=\dfrac{1}{8k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{4k+1}(D_{\ell}-D_{\ell}^{-1})\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}SU(4k+1).

A similar argument can be used to show that
iI_{4k+3}\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}SU(4k+3)
for
k\geq 0
by considering the diagonal matrices
D_{\ell}\in SU(4k+3)
with a
-1
in the
\ell
-th entry and an
i
in each other entry. Thus

iI_{4k+3}=\dfrac{1}{2(4k+2)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{4k+3}(D_{\ell}-D_{\ell}^{-1})\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}SU(4k+3).

For
n=4k+2
,
k\geq 1
, a further modification is needed. In this case we consider the diagonal matrices
D_{k,\ell}\in SU(4k+2)
with a
1
in the
k
-th and
\ell
-th entries and an
