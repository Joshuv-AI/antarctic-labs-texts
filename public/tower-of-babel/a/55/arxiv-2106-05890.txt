Strong Gaussian Approximation for the Sum of Random Vectors
Nazar Buzun, Nikolay Shvetsov, Dmitry V. Dylov

Abstract
This paper derives a new strong Gaussian approximation bound for the sum of independent random vectors. The approach relies on the optimal transport theory and yields explicit dependence on the dimension size p p and the sample size n n . This dependence establishes a new fundamental limit for all practical applications of statistical learning theory. Particularly, based on this bound, we prove approximation in distribution for the maximum norm in a high-dimensional setting ( p > n p>n ).

Introduction

Arguably, accurate estimation of a strong approximation is much more interpretable and meaningful than accurate estimation of the proximity of distributions.
In the latter case, one requires
a joint distribution of the original sum and the Gaussian vector to be built in one probabilistic space so that their proximity to each other could be gauged.
Gaussian approximation for the sum of random vectors attracts the attention of the mathematicians because of the uncertain dependence of the outcome on the dimension size
p

[
28
,
3
,
10
]
and is one of the most important tasks in the field of limit theorems of the probability theory.
In
[
28
]
, the uncertain property of the approximation was discerned, yielding the following finite-sample bound:

I\!\!P\left(\sup_{1\leq h\leq n}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{h}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{h}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}\right\|>C_{1}(\alpha)p^{\frac{23}{4}+\alpha}\log p\log n+t\,C_{2}p^{\frac{7}{2}}\log p\right)\leq e^{-t},

(1)

under conditions that the vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are centered and independent,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}\in\mathcal{N}(0,\varSigma)
have the same variance matrix and finite exponential moments over the same domain in
I\!\!R^{p}
.
The handicap of this result is the power of
p
, which requires extremely large sample sizes
n
for the approximation to be of any practical value. Asymptotically it is required
n>p^{11}
samples.

On the other hand, the multivariate Gaussian approximation in distribution in the general case, studied in
[
3
]
, has been known to yield an alternative error bound that favors smaller sample size. Namely, for all convex events
\mathcal{A}
:

\left|I\!\!P\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\right)-I\!\!P\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\right)\right|\leq O\left(\frac{p^{7/4}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(2)

For a particular case of Euclidean norm, it was proven in
[
6
]
that the upper bound approaches the asymptotic value
p/\sqrt{n}
.
Naturally, the observed gap in inequalities (
1
) and (
2
) suggests that the dependence on
p
in the strong Gaussian approximation may be improved.

In this paper, we narrow this gap by deriving a new type of strong Gaussian approximation bound for the sum of independent random vectors. Our approach is based on the optimal transport theory and the Gaussian approximation in the Wasserstein distance.
We derive the approximation in probability in a simplified form without the maximum by
h
(refer to expression (
1
)) and our convergence rate is comparable with the approximation in distribution (Refs.
[
3
,
6
]
).
Specifically, given the sub-Gaussian assumption for vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
we prove that

I\!\!P\left(\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}\right\|>O\left(\frac{p^{3/2}\log p\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\,e^{t/\log(np)}\right)\leq e^{-t}.

2
Main result

In this work, we prove the Theorem that finds the upper bound for the strong Gaussian approximation. Herein, we consider a sum of independent zero-mean random vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
in
I\!\!R^{p}
that has a covariance matrix

\varSigma=I\!\!E\boldsymbol{\xi}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}.

A Gaussian random vector
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{N}(0,\varSigma)
has the same 1-st and the 2-nd moments.
We say that a vector
\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\,
has restricted exponential or sub-Gaussian moments if
\exists\;\mathtt{g}>0

\log I\!\!E\exp\bigl(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\bigr)\leq\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|^{2}/2,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in I\!\!R^{p},\quad\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|\leq\mathtt{g}.

(3)

Theorem 2.1
.

Under condition that
\forall i:\,\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\nu_{0}}\varSigma^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
is sub-Gaussian (expression (
3
) with
\,0.3\mathtt{g}\geq\sqrt{p}\,
) exists a Gaussian vector
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{N}(0,\varSigma)
such that

I\!\!P\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\|>C(n,p)\|\varSigma\|^{1/2}\,e^{t/\log(np)}\right)\leq e^{-t},

where

C(n,p)=C\,\nu_{0}^{2}\,\frac{p\log(np)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{n}}+5\nu_{0}^{3}\,\frac{p^{3/2}\log(np)\log(2n)}{\sqrt{n}},

for some absolute constant
C
.

Summary of the proof:
Our approach is based on the optimal transport theory
[
21
,
4
,
5
]
. It consists of the following steps.

•

A very useful tool in approximation in probability is the Wasserstein distance (expression
4
). It reveals the joint distribution and minimises the difference between two random variables by increasing their dependence with some fixed marginal distributions. The multivariate Central Limit Theorem for
W_{2}
distance was described by
[
5
]
, where the author
uses Stein’s method and the entropy estimation for the derivative of
W_{2}
. Below, basing on the same technique, we obtain Gaussian approximation bound for
W_{L}
with explicit dependence on the power
L
of the cost function and dimension parameter
p
(Theorem
3.9
), such that

W_{L}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\leq\frac{C\,L^{3/2}\nu_{0}^{2}\|\varSigma\|^{1/2}}{\log L}\left(\frac{p}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{L}{n^{1-1/L}}\right)+\frac{5L\nu_{0}^{3}p^{3/2}\|\varSigma\|^{1/2}\log(2n)}{\sqrt{n}}.

The proof of the last statement is the most difficult part of this work. It starts with derivative estimation of
W_{L}
. For that we construct a Markov random process
X_{t}
that transits
X_{0}=\boldsymbol{\xi}
to
X_{\infty}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. From Lemma
3.2
follows that for the random process
X_{t}
under smoothness condition it holds

W_{L}(X_{a},X_{b})\leq\int_{a}^{b}\left\{I\!\!E\left\|I\!\!E\left[\frac{d}{dt}X_{t}\bigg|X_{t}\right]\right\|^{L}\right\}^{1/L}dt.

Then in order to derive an upper bound for
I\!\!E\left\|I\!\!E\left[\frac{d}{dt}X_{t}|X_{t}\right]\right\|^{L}
we involve Rosenthal’s martingale inequality (Lemma
3.3
), convolution with Hermite polynomials (Lemma
3.4
), and modified variant of Stein’s method (Theorem
3.6
).

•

The sub-Gaussian property is required for moments estimation of random variables
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\|
(used in Theorem
3.6
) and in Lemma
3.8
we have proved the following upper bound for each
k\geq 2
and
1\leq i\leq n

I\!\!E\left\|\varSigma^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right\|^{k}\leq\frac{4\nu_{0}^{k}}{n^{k/2}}(\sqrt{p}+\sqrt{2k})^{k}.

•

Denote a joint distribution of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
by
\pi=\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
. Markov’s inequality provides the following statement

\min_{\pi\in\varPi[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma}]}I\!\!P\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\|>\varDelta\right)\leq\frac{W_{L}^{L}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{\varDelta^{L}},

where we set
L=\log(np)
and
\varDelta=C(n,p)\|\varSigma\|^{1/2}\,e^{t/\log(np)}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that convergence in probability in Central Limit Theorem does not exist,
i.e.
, there is no random vector
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, such that
\boldsymbol{\xi}(n)
converges to it when
n\to\infty
. It follows from the Kolmogorov’s
0
-
1
law. However, at the same time, for each
n
one should take a new Gaussian vector which depends on
n
and

\|\boldsymbol{\xi}(n)-\boldsymbol{\gamma}(n)\|\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,\quad n\to\infty,

which follows from Theorem
2.1
.

3
Approximation in probability

Having introduced the main definitions in the previous section, we want to find an upper bound for
|\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|-\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\||
in probability.
Markov’s inequality gives the following for some
L\geq 0
:

I\!\!P\left(\left|\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|-\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\|\right|>\varDelta\right)\leq I\!\!P\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\|>\varDelta\right)\leq\frac{I\!\!E\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\|^{L}}{\varDelta^{L}}.

The differences
\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\|
decrease when the dependence between the random variables increases. So we want to make them as dependent as possible.
Denote by
\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
a joint distribution of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
One then has to minimize over
\pi
with a fixed marginals (
\pi\in\varPi[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma}]
) the following Wasserstein distance

W_{L}^{L}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\min_{\pi\in\varPi[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma}]}\left\{I\!\!E\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|^{L}\right\}.

(4)

Notation
\pi\in\varPi[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma}]
means that
\int\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi},d\boldsymbol{\gamma})
equals the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\int\pi(d\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
equals the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. Accounting to the previous inequalities,

\min_{\pi\in\varPi[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma}]}I\!\!P\left(\left|\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|-\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\|\right|>\varDelta\right)\leq\frac{W_{L}^{L}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{\varDelta^{L}}.

(5)

Below, we will study one/multi dimensional cases separately because of the difference in the complexity of the proof. An accurate solution of this problem is applicable for a lot of different fields of science
[
14
,
10
,
7
,
23
]
and thus so important.

3.1
One dimensional case

Here we will obtain a simple and close to optimal (up to a logarithmic factor) estimation of the main bound (
5
) for the random variable
\xi
with additional assumption on its distribution (
6
). We will generalise and improve this estimation in the next section with much more difficult approach.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
4
]
Proposition 5.1)
.

Let
\xi,\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
have the cumulative distributions
F
and
\varPhi
(standard Gaussian cdf) respectively.
Assume that, for some real numbers
\varepsilon>0
and
d\geq 1
,

|F(x)-\varPhi(x)|\leq\varepsilon\left(1+|x|^{d}\right)e^{-x^{2}/2}.

(6)

Then

W_{L}(\xi,\gamma)\leq C(L,d)\varepsilon,

where one may take
C(L,d)=(CLd)^{3(d+1)/2}
with some absolute constant
C
.

Corollary 3.1
.

If the random variables
\xi
and
\gamma
fulfill condition (
6
), then

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\varPi[\xi,\gamma]}I\!\!P\left(\left|\xi-\gamma\right|>eC(\log n,d)\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}.

where
C(\log n,d)
is defined in the previous lemma.

Apply Lemma
3.1
for pair
(\xi,\gamma)
:

\displaystyle W_{L}^{L}(\xi,\gamma)=\min_{\pi\in\varPi[\xi,\gamma]}I\!\!E|\xi-\gamma|^{L}\leq C^{L}(L,d)\varepsilon^{L}.

In order to find a bound for the probability involve expression (
5
):

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\varPi[\xi,\gamma]}I\!\!P\left(\left|\xi-\gamma\right|>\varDelta\right)\leq\left(\frac{C(L,d)\varepsilon}{\varDelta}\right)^{L}.

Set
L=\log n
and
\varDelta=eC(L,d)\varepsilon
.

∎

Remark 3.1
.

It
(2d)^{d/2}<1/\varepsilon
condition (
6
) is stronger than sub-Gaussian property (
3
) that we assume in our main Theorem
2.1
. The justification relies on the following integration heuristic:

\varepsilon\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\lambda x}|x|^{d}e^{-x^{2}/2}dx\leq\varepsilon\sup_{x}|x|^{d}e^{-x^{2}/4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\lambda x}e^{-x^{2}/4}dx\leq\varepsilon(2d)^{d/2}e^{2\lambda^{2}}.

3.2
Multidimensional case

This part involves multivariate Gaussian approximation in the Wasserstein distance. We first resort proving the approximation of derivative of
W_{L}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
, and only then we utilize Markov’s inequality (
5
) in order to obtain the approximation in probability (
5
) discussed above.
Here we will use a specified for the Wasserstein distance version of Stein’s method
[
8
]
proposed in paper
[
5
]
. For that define a smooth transition from
\boldsymbol{\xi}
to
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
parametrized by
t
:

X_{t}=e^{-t}\boldsymbol{\xi}+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}\boldsymbol{\gamma},\quad t\in[0,\infty].

(7)

Remark 3.2
.

The choice of this particular transition is explained by the fact that Markov process
X_{t}
has generator
Af(x)=\nabla^{T}\varSigma\nabla f(x)-x^{T}\nabla f(x)
and Gaussian stationary measure
[
2
]
. It is also known as Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.

Further we will require one important property of this process that will help us to find an upper bound for the derivative of
W_{L}
by
t
. It was proposed in paper
[
21
]
that deals with particular case derivative estimation of
W_{2}
. There exists a function
\mathcal{G}_{t}(x)
that effects the transition of measure
\mu_{t}(x)
of
X_{t}
in the following way
\forall f\in\mathbb{C}^{1}
:

\frac{d}{dt}\int f(x)d\mu_{t}(x)=\int\nabla^{T}f(x)\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(x)d\mu_{t}(x),\quad t\in[0,\infty).

(8)

Its gradient has explicit representation

\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(X_{t})=I\!\!E\left[\frac{d}{dt}X_{t}\bigg|X_{t}\right],

since

\lim_{\varDelta\to 0}\frac{I\!\!Ef(X_{t+\varDelta})-I\!\!Ef(X_{t})}{\varDelta}=\lim_{\varDelta\to 0}I\!\!E\nabla^{T}f(X_{t})\frac{I\!\!E[X_{t+\varDelta}-X_{t}|X_{t}]}{\varDelta}=I\!\!E\nabla^{T}f(X_{t})I\!\!E\left[\frac{d}{dt}X_{t}\bigg|X_{t}\right].

And differentiating equation (
7
) by time we derive that

\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(X_{t})=-e^{-t}I\!\!E\left(X_{0}-\frac{e^{-t}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}}X_{\infty}\bigg|X_{t}\right).

(9)

The next result allows us to bound an
increment the of Wasserstein distance in a small interval of the transition parameter
t
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(X_{t})\in I\!\!R^{p}
,
t\in[0,\infty]
be uniformly continuous and bounded random process. For any cost function
c(x-y)
in Wasserstein distance
W(c)
and infinitely small time shift
ds
it holds

W(c)(X_{t},X_{t+ds})\leq I\!\!Ec\big(ds\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(X_{t})\big)

and moreover if
\exists f:I\!\!R\to I\!\!R
such that
\forall t,s\geq 0

f\left\{W(c)(X_{0},X_{t+s})\right\}-f\left\{W(c)(X_{0},X_{t})\right\}\leq f\{W(c)(X_{t},X_{t+s})\}

(10)

then for any
a,b\geq 0

f\{W(c)(X_{a},X_{b})\}\leq\int_{a}^{b}\frac{f\left\{I\!\!Ec(ds\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t})\right\}}{ds}dt.

Particularly, if
c(x-y)
is any norm in power
L
, then

W_{L}(X_{a},X_{b})\leq\int_{a}^{b}\left\{I\!\!E\|\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}\|^{L}\right\}^{1/L}dt.

Fix an arbitrary point
t
. Define a new mapping
T_{s}
on the space of the random variable
X_{t}
by equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}T_{s}(x)=\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t+s}(T_{s}(x)),\quad T_{0}(x)=x,\quad s\geq 0.

(11)

It turns out that function
x\to T_{s}(x)
is the push-forward transport mapping, such that
X_{t+s}=T_{s}(X_{t})
in distribution (see proof of Lemma 2 in
[
21
]
). In order to be consistent with our notation we provide below another proof of this proposition.
Note that for an arbitrary functions
h\in C^{1}
and
h_{s}=h(T^{-1}_{s})
it holds

\frac{d}{ds}h_{s}(T_{s})=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}h_{s}(T_{s})+\nabla^{T}h_{s}(T_{s})\frac{\partial}{\partial s}T_{s}=0

and with differential equation for
T_{s}
(
11
) one gets

\forall x\in\varOmega(X_{t}):\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial s}h_{s}(x)=-\nabla^{T}\mathcal{G}_{t+s}(x)\nabla h_{s}(x).

Then involving property (
8
) leads to

\frac{d}{ds}\int h_{s}(x)d\mu_{t+s}(x)=\int\frac{\partial}{\partial s}h_{s}(x)+\nabla^{T}\mathcal{G}_{t+s}(x)\nabla h_{s}(x)d\mu_{t+s}=0.

It proves that
T_{s}^{-1}(X_{t+s})=X_{t}
and subsequently
X_{t+s}=T_{s}T_{s}^{-1}(X_{t+s})=T_{s}(X_{t})
.
By the definition of Wasserstein distance and the push-forward transport mapping
T_{s}
(
11
)

W(c)(X_{t},X_{t+ds})\leq I\!\!Ec\big(T_{ds}(X_{t})-T_{0}(X_{t})\big)=I\!\!Ec\big(ds\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(X_{t})\big)

and consequently with the triangle inequality assumption (
10
)

\frac{d}{dt}f\{W(c)(X_{0},X_{t})\}\leq\frac{1}{ds}f\{W(c)(X_{t},X_{t+ds})\}\leq\frac{1}{ds}f\{I\!\!Ec(ds\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(X_{t}))\}.

After the integration we obtain the second statement of this lemma. In case
c(x-y)=\|x-y\|^{L}
we set
f=\sqrt[L]{}
and apply equality

I\!\!E\|ds\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{L}=(ds)^{L}I\!\!E\|\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{L}.

∎

Involve a sequence of useful lemmas that will help us to estimate term
I\!\!E\|\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{L}
from the previous result.

Lemma 3.3

(Rosenthal’s inequality
[
19
]
)
.

For all martingales
S_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}
and
2\leq q<\infty
:

\left[I\!\!E\max_{0\leq k<\infty}\|S_{k}\|^{q}\right]^{1/q}\leq\frac{Cq}{\log q}\left[I\!\!E\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}I\!\!E_{i-1}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{q/2}+I\!\!E\max_{0\leq i<\infty}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}\|^{q}\right]^{1/q}.

By
I\!\!E_{i-1}
we denote conditional expectation on previous values of the martingale
S_{0},\ldots,S_{i-1}
and by definition
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}
are increments of the martingale which may be dependent in general case.

Lemma 3.4
.

[
5
]
Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{p}
:
x_{\alpha}\in I\!\!R^{p}
and
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\varSigma)
be the multivariate Hermite polynomials, defined by

H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\varSigma)=(-1)^{|\alpha|}e^{\frac{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{T}\varSigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{2}}\partial^{\alpha}e^{-\frac{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{T}\varSigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{2}},\quad H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},I).

(12)

Then

\left[I\!\!E\left\|\sum_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}H_{\alpha}(\gamma)\right\|^{q}\right]^{2/q}\leq\sum_{\alpha}\max(1,q-1)^{|\alpha|}\alpha!\left\|x_{\alpha}\right\|^{2}.

Corollary 3.2
.

In general case one may use coordinates replacement and property
\nabla_{(Ax)}=A^{-1}\nabla_{x}
, such that

\left[I\!\!E\left\|\sum_{\alpha\geq 0}x_{\alpha}H_{\alpha}(\gamma,\varSigma)\right\|^{q}\right]^{2/q}\leq\sum_{\alpha\geq 0}\max(1,q-1)^{|\alpha|}\alpha!\left\|(\varSigma^{-1/2}x)_{\alpha}\right\|^{2}.

Combine the previous two lemmas.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{N}(0,\varSigma)
and for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{p}
:
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{P}(I\!\!R^{p})
and
H_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\varSigma)
be the multivariate Hermite polynomials (
12
) and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\alpha}=\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}^{\alpha}
, where
\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}^{\alpha}\}
are independent random vectors. Then for
2\leq q<\infty

\displaystyle\left[I\!\!E\left\|\sum_{i,\alpha}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}^{\alpha}H_{\alpha}(\gamma,\varSigma)\right\|^{q}\right]^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq C(q)\left[\sum_{i,\alpha}\alpha!(q-1)^{|\alpha|}I\!\!E\left\|\varSigma^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}^{\alpha}\right\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\left[\sum_{\alpha}\alpha!(q-1)^{|\alpha|}\left\|\varSigma^{-1/2}I\!\!E\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\alpha}\right\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle+C(q)\left[\sum_{i}I\!\!E\left(\sum_{\alpha}\alpha!(q-1)^{|\alpha|}\left\|\varSigma^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}^{\alpha}\right\|^{2}\right)^{q/2}\right]^{1/q}.

where one may take
C(q)=\frac{Cq}{\log q}
for some absolute constant
C
.

Without loss of generality assume
\varSigma=I
. Note that for a random variable
\xi
operator
[I\!\!E\xi^{q}]^{1/q}
is the probability norm
L_{q}
. Use notation

\delta=\left\|\left\|\sum_{i,\alpha}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}^{\alpha}H_{\alpha}(\gamma)\right\|\right\|_{L_{q}}.

In order to construct a martingale one has to substitute expectation from each
X^{\alpha}
.
For that use the triangle inequality

\delta\leq\left\|\left\|\sum_{i,\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}^{\alpha}-I\!\!E[\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}^{\alpha}])H_{\alpha}(\gamma)\right\|\right\|_{L_{q}}+\left\|\left\|\sum_{i,\alpha}I\!\!E[\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}^{\alpha}]H_{\alpha}(\gamma)\right\|\right\|_{L_{q}}.

Without loss of generality assume that

\varDelta>0

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}<t-\varDelta\right)-I\!\!P\left(|\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}-\max_{k}\xi_{k}|\geq\varDelta\right)\leq I\!\!P\left(\max_{k}\xi_{k}<\varDelta\right)

\displaystyle\leq I\!\!P\left(\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}<t+\varDelta\right)+I\!\!P\left(|\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}-\max_{k}\xi_{k}|>\varDelta\right).

Next, we use anti-concentration (Lemma
4.1
) that allows us to “move”
\varDelta
out of the probability function:

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}<t+\varDelta\right)\leq I\!\!P\left(\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}<t\right)+\varDelta C_{A},

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}<t-\varDelta\right)\geq I\!\!P\left(\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}<t\right)-\varDelta C_{A}.

Now, we have to minimize the following expression by
\varDelta
:

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\varPi[\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\gamma_{p}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\xi}]}I\!\!P\left(|\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}-\max_{k}\xi_{k}|>\varDelta\right)+\varDelta C_{A},

where
\pi
is a common distribution of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and notation
\pi\in\varPi[\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\gamma_{p}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\xi}]
means that the distribution of each gamma component and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
are fixed, but it does not include the common distribution of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
as opposed to
\varPi[\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\xi}]
.
We will find the bound for
I\!\!P(|\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}-\max_{k}\xi_{k}|>\varDelta)
combining Boole’s inequality and one dimensional variant of equation (
5
).

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\varPi[\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\gamma_{p}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\xi}]}I\!\!P\left(|\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}-\max_{k}\xi_{k}|>\varDelta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\pi\in\varPi[\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\gamma_{p}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\xi}]}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{p}I\!\!P(|\gamma^{\prime}_{k}-\xi_{k}|>\varDelta)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\min_{\pi_{k}\in\varPi[\gamma_{k}^{\prime},\xi_{k}]}I\!\!P(|\gamma^{\prime}_{k}-\xi_{k}|>\varDelta).

In the last step we set the argmin
\pi=\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi})\pi_{1}(\gamma_{1}^{\prime}|\xi_{1})\ldots\pi_{p}(\gamma_{p}^{\prime}|\xi_{p})
and this allowed us to swap the sum and min operations. From (
5
) we obtain that
\forall k

\min_{\pi_{k}\in\varPi[\gamma_{k}^{\prime},\xi_{k}]}I\!\!P(|\gamma^{\prime}_{k}-\xi_{k}|>\varDelta)\leq\frac{1}{\varDelta^{L}}\sum_{k}W_{L}^{L}(\xi_{k},\gamma^{\prime}_{k}).

Note that for a fixed component
k
:
W_{L}(\xi_{k},\gamma^{\prime}_{k})=\varSigma_{kk}W_{L}(\xi_{k}/\varSigma_{kk},\gamma^{\prime}_{k}/\varSigma_{kk})
and from Theorem
3.9
follows

W_{L}(\xi_{k}/\varSigma_{kk},\gamma^{\prime}_{k}/\varSigma_{kk})\leq\frac{CL^{3/2}\nu_{0}^{3}}{\log L\sqrt{n}}.

(19)

Therefore, the minimization task is

\varDelta C_{A}+\frac{p}{\varDelta^{L}}\left(\frac{C\overline{\sigma}L^{3/2}\nu_{0}^{3}}{\log L\sqrt{n}}\right)^{L}.

Choose
L=\log(np)
and
\varDelta
that will satisfy the condition

\displaystyle\frac{p}{\varDelta^{L}}\left(\frac{C\overline{\sigma}L^{3/2}\nu_{0}^{3}}{\log L\sqrt{n}}\right)^{L}\leq\frac{1}{n},

\displaystyle\varDelta=\frac{Ce\overline{\sigma}\log^{3/2}(np)\nu_{0}^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Setting this value in the minimization task, gives the required bound in the approximation in probability. So, we have

\left|I\!\!P\left(\max_{k}\xi_{k}<t\right)-I\!\!P\left(\max_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}<t\right)\right|\leq O\left(\nu_{0}^{3}\frac{\overline{\sigma}}{\underline{\sigma}}\frac{\log^{2}(np)}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Now compare
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}
with
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. For that one has to estimate
\|\varSigma-\varSigma^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
. In the next expression we will consider arbitrary element
\varSigma^{\prime}_{kl}
and imply property
W_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)\leq W_{L}(\cdot,\cdot)
(Jensen’s inequality) and Cauchy–-Bunyakovsky inequality for the correlation of two random variables.

\displaystyle\left|I\!\!E\xi_{k}\xi_{l}-I\!\!E\gamma^{\prime}_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{l}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|I\!\!E(\xi_{k}\xi_{l}-\xi_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{l}+\xi_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{l}-\gamma^{\prime}_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{l})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{I\!\!E\xi^{2}_{k}}\sqrt{I\!\!E(\gamma^{\prime}_{l}-\xi_{l})^{2}}+\sqrt{I\!\!E(\gamma^{\prime}_{l})^{2}}\sqrt{I\!\!E(\gamma^{\prime}_{k}-\xi_{k})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\overline{\sigma}^{2}W_{L}(\xi_{k}/\varSigma_{kk},\gamma^{\prime}_{k}/\varSigma_{kk})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\overline{\sigma}^{2}L^{3/2}\nu_{0}^{3}}{\log L\sqrt{n}}.

In the last inequality we have used the upper bound (
19
).
Denote
\delta_{\varSigma}=\max_{kl}\left|I\!\!E\xi_{k}\xi_{l}-I\!\!E\gamma^{\prime}_{k}\gamma^{\prime}_{l}\right|/\lambda_{\min}(\varSigma)
. From Lemma
4.2
follows
\forall t

\displaystyle\left|I\!\!P\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\gamma_{k}\leq t\right)-I\!\!P\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\gamma^{\prime}_{k}\leq t\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\delta_{\varSigma}\log p\,\log\left(\frac{\overline{\sigma}}{\delta_{\varSigma}\underline{\sigma}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\overline{\sigma}^{2}\log(np)^{5/2}\nu_{0}^{3}}{\lambda_{\min}(\varSigma)\sqrt{n}}\log n.

∎

We have performed empirical validation of the asymptotic of Gaussian approximation. It includes two experiments: with Wasserstein distance

Theorem
3.9
states that the convergence rate of Gaussian approximation for

is
O(L^{3/2}p/\sqrt{n}+Lp^{3/2}/\sqrt{n})

O(p^{3/2}L^{1/2}/\sqrt{n})

n
is
0.498\approx 0.5
, power of
p
is
1.506\approx 1.5
and power of
L
is
0.439\approx 0.5
. The measurements of this experiment are presented in Figure
1
.

The second experiment relates to Theorem
4.4
. It states that the convergence rate of the Gaussian approximation for maximum norm is
O(\log^{5/2}p/\sqrt{n})

O(\log p/\sqrt{n})
. More precisely, the computed power of
n
is
0.496\approx 0.5
, power of
p
is
0.997\approx 1.0
. It seems that the logarithmic factor may be improved in this theorem and it also depends on the power of
L
in the previous asymptotic. The measurements of this experiment are presented in Figure
