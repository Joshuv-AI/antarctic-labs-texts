On Gelfand and MacPherson’s combinatorial formula for Pontrjagin classes
Olakunle Abawonse, Laura Anderson

Abstract
. In [ GM92 ] Gelfand and MacPherson gave a local and combinatorial formula for the Pontrjagin classes of a differential manifold. We give an expanded version of their discussion and highlight the origins of combinatorial differential manifolds in their work.



1.
Introduction

In 1992 Gelfand and MacPherson (
[
GM92
]
) found a local and combinatorial formula for the Pontrjagin classes of a compact smooth manifold by introducing a new combinatorial approach to smooth structures. Their approach, in 5 pages, encompassed a formidable array of techniques, and proofs were omitted or sketched. Since then some elements of their approach have gotten further exposition and development (
[
Mac93
]
,
[
And99a
]
,
[
And99b
]
), but their full proof has never been presented in a broadly accessible form.

Our aim here is to provide a full and reasonably self-contained presentation of the results in
[
GM92
]
. We expect few readers to come with knowledge of all of the background mathematics involved, so we have included appendices outlininging such knowledge.
In the course of preparing this we found some minor errors in the original paper, which we have listed in Appendix
E
.

2.
Overview

Throughout,
M
denotes a compact smooth manifold.

There are two main elements of Gelfand and MacPherson’s approach. The first is an alternative formulation of Chern-Weil theory that gives a formula for the inverse rational Pontrjagin classes of a smooth bundle in terms of the Chern class of an associated circle bundle. The second is the development of a combinatorial model for differential manifolds, within which we can find a combinatorial formula for this Chern class, when the smooth bundle in question is the tangent bundle.

Chern-Weil theory gives formulas for characteristic classes of smooth bundles in terms of curvature forms. This may seem like an unlikely place to look for combinatorics, but read on. Given a smooth real vector bundle
\xi:E\to M
of even rank
a
with a Riemannian metric, we consider the associated
Grassmannian
2
-plane bundle

\pi:{\mathcal{Y}}\to M
. Here

{\mathcal{Y}}=\{(m,V):m\in M,\mbox{ $V$ a $2$-dimensional subspace of $\xi^{-1}(m)$}\}

and
\pi
is projection onto the first coordinate. This is a fiber bundle with fiber the Grassmannian
\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a})
.
There is a canonical rank 2 real vector bundle
\xi_{2}:E_{2}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
, where
E_{2}=\{(m,V,v):(m,V)\in{\mathcal{Y}},v\in V\}
.
The alternative view of Chern-Weil theory in
[
GM92
]
gives a formula for the inverse Pontrjagin classes of
\xi
in terms of a curvature form on
\xi_{2}
(see Theorem
4.7
.)

Stepping back from the smooth world, we can interpret this formula (Corollary
4.10
) in terms of the Chern class of
\xi_{2}
, or equivalently, the Chern class of the circle bundle associated to
\xi_{2}
(with coefficients twisted by the orientation presheaf).
Thus much of the task of finding the rational Pontrjagin classes of a smooth bundle reduces to finding the Chern class of a circle bundle.
Finding the Chern class of a circle bundle is a more tempting combinatorial target.

The second major element of Gelfand and MacPherson’s approach is
combinatorial differential manifolds
.
A combinatorial differential manifold is a simplicial complex
X
, which plays the role of the base space of a manifold, a cell decomposition
\hat{X}
of
X
, and an association of an
oriented matroid
to each cell in
\hat{X}
, which plays the role of a coordinate chart at that cell. From a smooth triangulation
X\to M
of a smooth manifold
M
we obtain a combinatorial differential manifold. The combinatorial formula for Pontrjagin classes then comes from combinatorially mimicking the construction of
\xi_{2}
to get a simplicial
S^{1}
qusifibration, and then showing that the Chern class of this quasifibration leads to a formula for the Pontrjagin classes of
M
.

We should note that Gelfand and MacPherson’s formula is not quite “combinatorial” in the sense of clearly depending only on the combinatorics of
X
. The smooth structure on
M
is used to construct the combinatorial differential manifold over
X
. The final formula is in terms of a
fixing cycle
derived from the combinatorial differential structure, and it’s not clear that the fixing cycle is independent of the combinatorial differential structure.

3.
History

Thom (
[
Tho58
]
) and Rohlin and Švarc (
[
Rv57
]
) independently showed, by nonconstructive arguments, that the rational Pontrjagin classes of a smooth manifold are combinatorial invariants: that is, if
M
is a smoothing of a simplicial complex
X
then
X
determines the rational Pontrjagin classes of
M
. Levitt and Rourke
[
LR78
]
showed, by a nonconstructive argument, the existence of a formula for the Pontrjagin classes that is local (i.e., given by a cocycle whose value on an open set in
X
depends only on the combinatorics of
X
in that set).

Gelfand and MacPherson’s approach grew out of work of Gabrielov, Gelfand, and Losik (
[
GGL75
]
,
[
GGL76
]
,
[
Mac78
]
) that gave a formula for the first Pontrjagin class and suggested a route to all of the Pontrjagin classes. A key advance in Gelfand and MacPherson’s approach was the use of oriented matroids as combinatorial analogs to tangent spaces.
The resulting notion of
combinatorial differential manifolds
, and its generalization
matroid bundles
, was expanded on in
[
Mac93
]
,
[
And99a
]
,
[
And99b
]
.

Questions on characteristic classes associated to combinatorial differential manifolds and matroid bundles are closely tied to questions on the topology of the
combinatorial Grassmannians
described in Sections
5.3
and
5.4
, in particular the
MacPhersonian
. Since Gelfand and MacPherson’s work, some progress has been made on elucidating the topology of the MacPhersonian, including its mod 2 cohomology (
[
AD02
]
), and its first few homotopy groups (
[
And98
]
). The conjecture that
\mathop{\rm Gr}(r,{\mathbb{R}}^{S})\simeq\|\mathop{\rm MacP}(r,S)\|
remains open. (An attempt at a proof in 2009 (
[
Bis09
]
) was withdrawn.)

4.
An alternate form of Chern-Weil theory

Chern-Weil theory is the study of characteristic classes within the context of de Rham cohomology. It computes rational characteristic classes of smooth manifolds, and more generally principal bundles, in terms of connections and curvature.

Let
\xi:E\to B
be a smooth bundle with a connection, and let
\Omega
be the associated curvature form. Classical Chern-Weil theory (cf. §5.4 in
[
Mor01
]
)) says that the total Pontrjagin class of
E
with coefficients in
{\mathbb{R}}
is

P(E)=\sum_{i}p_{i}(E)=\left[\det\left(I+\frac{1}{2\pi}\Omega\right)\right]

Since
p_{0}(E)=1
,
P(E)
has an inverse
\tilde{P}(E)=\sum_{i}\tilde{p}_{i}(E)
in
H^{*}(B)
. Gelfand and MacPherson’s alternate form of Chern-Weil theory gives a formula for
\tilde{P}
rather than
P
, and it is in terms of the curvature form of a rank 2 bundle associated to
E
rather than the original bundle.

Throughout this section
\xi:E\to M
denotes a real vector bundle over a smooth compact manifold
M
, and
\pi:{\mathcal{Y}}\to M
denotes the associated Grassmannian
2
-plane bundle. For most of Section
4
we assume that
a
is even: this saves us many issues around orientability. The modifications needed when
a
is odd are outlined in Section
4.4
.

4.1.
Orientability of
\pi

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
a
be even, and let
\xi:E\to M
be a rank
a
real vector bundle. Then the associated Grassmannian 2-plane bundle
\pi:{\mathcal{Y}}\to M
is orientable.

Notation 4.2
.

For a matrix
N
with
a

1\leq x<y\leq a
, let
N_{x,y}
be the submatrix consisting of the columns of
N
indexed by
x
and

N\backslash\{x,y\}
be the matrix obtained by deleting the columns indexed by
x
and
y
.

Definition 4.3
.

A matrix

is
\{x,y\}
-reduced
if
N_{x,y}=I
.

We begin by describing coordinate charts on
\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a})
. For each
x<y
let
W_{x,y}
be the subset of
\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a})
consisting of spaces of the form
\mathop{\rm row}(N)
, where
N
is
\{x,y\}
-reduced. A coordinate chart on
W_{x,y}
is given by the map
\Phi_{x,y}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2(a-2)}\to W_{x,y}
sending
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{a(a-2)})
to the
\{x,y\}
-reduced matrix
N
with

N\backslash\{x,y\}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}&n_{3}&\cdots&n_{2(a-2)-1}\\
n_{2}&n_{4}&\cdots&n_{2(a-2)}\end{pmatrix}

An element of
W_{w,x}\cap W_{y,z}
can be written as the row space of a
\{w,x\}
-reduced matrix
N
and as the row space of a
\{y,z\}
-reduced matrix
N_{y,z}^{-1}N
. (The intersection
W_{w,x}\cap W_{y,z}
is exactly the set of all
\mathop{\rm row}(N)
such that
N
is
\{w,x\}
-reduced and
N_{y,z}
is invertible.) This gives us the transition function
g_{(w,x),(y,z)}
between the two coordinate systems on
W_{w,x}\cap W_{y,z}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
a
is even then
g_{(w,x),(y,z)}
is orientation preserving.

In particular,
\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a})
is orientable.

Proof.

We abbreviate
g_{(w,x),(y,z)}
by
g
. It suffices to show that the Jacobian matrix
(\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial m_{j}})_{i,j}
has positive determinant.
For simplicity we assume
(w,x)=(1,2)
and
\{y,z\}\subseteq\{1,2,3,4\}
.

For a
\{1,2\}
reduced matrix
N
we have

N=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&n_{1}&n_{3}&\cdots&n_{2(a-2)-1}\\
0&1&n_{2}&n_{4}&\cdots&n_{2(a-2)}\end{pmatrix}

and so

N_{y,x}^{-1}N
has columns

N_{y,x}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},N_{y,x}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},N_{y,x}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}\\
n_{2}\end{pmatrix},\ldots,N_{y,x}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}n_{2(a-2)-1}\\
n_{2(a-2)}\end{pmatrix}

We abuse notation by writing a vector of length
2(a-2)
as a vector of
a-2
vectors of length 2. Thus

g\left(\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}\\
n_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}n_{3}\\
n_{4}\end{pmatrix},\cdots,\begin{pmatrix}n_{2(a-2)-1}\\
n_{2(a-2)}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}b_{3}\\
b_{4}\end{pmatrix},N_{y,z}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}n_{5}\\
n_{6}\end{pmatrix}\cdots,N_{y,z}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}n_{2(a-2)-1}\\
n_{2(a-2)}\end{pmatrix}\right)

where the values
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}
depend on our values of
y
and
z
. Thus the Jacobian of
g
has the form

\begin{pmatrix}J_{1}&0\\
J_{2}&J_{3}\end{pmatrix}

where
J_{1}
is
4\times 4
and
J_{3}
is a block-diagonal matrix whose
a-4
blocks are all
N_{y,z}^{-1}
. Thus the Jacobian has determinant
|J_{1}||N_{y,z}|^{a-4}
. Since
a
is even, we need only check that
|J_{1}|
is positive. This is a tedious but easy check for each of the possible values of
\{y,z\}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Of course, we may assume
a>2
. Consider a cover
\{U_{i}\}
of
M
and local coordinates
\phi_{i}:U_{i}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{a}\to\xi^{-1}(U_{i})
. The identification
\phi_{i}
gives us an identification of
\pi^{-1}(U_{i})
with
U_{i}\times\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a})
. Let
p\in U_{i}\cap U_{j}
. The transition map from
U_{i}
coordinates to
U_{j}
coordinates on
\xi^{-1}(p)
is given by right multiplication by a matrix
A
. Thus the transition map on
\pi^{-1}(p)
takes each
\mathop{\rm row}(N)
to
\mathop{\rm row}(NA)
.

We wish to show that this map is orientation preserving. The matrix
A
is a product of elementary matrices, so it’s enough to show that when
A
is an elementary matrix then this map is orientation preserving. For each
x<y
let
W_{x,y}
be the subset of
\pi^{-1}(p)
consisting of elements that, in the
U_{i}
trivialization, have the form
\mathop{\rm row}(N)
with
N

\{x,y\}
-reduced, and let
W^{\prime}_{x,y}
be the similar set with respect to the
U_{j}
-trivialization. Thanks to Lemma
4.4
, it is enough to find some nonempty
W_{w,x}\cap W^{\prime}_{y,z}
such that the transition map between these charts is orientation-preserving.

Case 1: Let
A
be a permutation matrix, and let
\sigma
be the corresponding permutation. Then
NA
is

\{\sigma(1),\sigma(2)\}
-reduced. and
W_{1,2}=W^{\prime}_{\sigma(1),\sigma(2)}
. Also,
\sigma
induces a permutation
\sigma^{\prime}
taking the columns of
N\backslash\{1,2\}
to the columns of
(NA)\backslash\{\sigma(1),\sigma(2)\}
for each
N\in W_{1,2}
. Let
\tilde{A}
be the matrix of
\sigma^{\prime}
. The transition map from
W_{1,2}
to
W^{\prime}_{\sigma(1),\sigma(2)}
is given by the matrix

Q^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{A}&0\\
0&\tilde{A}\end{pmatrix}Q

where
Q
is the permutation matrix for the permutation
1,3,5,\ldots,2,4,6,\ldots
. This matrix has positive determinant
|\tilde{A}|^{2}
, and so the transition map is orientation preserving.

Case 2: Let
A
be an invertible diagonal matrix, with diagonal entries
d_{1},\ldots,d_{a}
. Then
W_{1,2}=W^{\prime}_{1,2}
, and the transition matrix between these charts is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
\frac{1}{d_{1}},\frac{1}{d_{2}},\frac{d_{3}}{d_{1}},\frac{d_{3}}{d_{2}},\ldots,\frac{d_{a}}{d_{1}},\frac{d_{a}}{d_{2}}
. The product of these entries is a square, so is positive.

Case 3: Let
A
be a matrix with 1’s on the diagonal and a single nonzero entry off the diagonal. Then there is an
x<y
such that
W_{x.y}=W^{\prime}_{x,y}
and the transition matrix between them is a triangular matrix with 1’s on the diagonal, and so this transition map is orientation-preserving.

∎

4.2.
Rank 2 Grassmannians

There are two canonical bundles over
\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a})
.

•

Let
\mathrm{EGr}_{2}=\{(V,x):x\in V\in\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a})\}
and
\tau_{2}:\mathrm{EGr}_{2}\to\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a})
be the projection map.

•

Let
\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}=\{(V,x):V\in\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a}),x\in V^{\perp}\}
and
\tau_{(a-2)}:\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}\to\mathop{\rm Gr}({2},{\mathbb{R}}^{a})
be the projection map.

The product
\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}\oplus\mathrm{EGr}_{2}
is a trivial bundle and the total Pontrjagin class is multiplicative, and so
P(\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)})=\tilde{P}(\mathrm{EGr}_{2})
.
With respect to the orientation on the Grassmannian given in Section
4.1
we have a formula for the Poincare dual of the top Pontrjagin class of
\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
a
is even then

p_{\frac{a-2}{2}}(\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)})\frown[\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,{\mathbb{R}}^{a})]=(-1)^{\frac{a-2}{2}}.

Proof.

If
\xi:E\to B
is an oriented rank
2k
vector bundle then
its
k
th Pontrjagin class is the square of its Euler class
(cf. Corollary 15.8 in
[
MS74
]
). Also, Euler classes are multiplicative, so
e(E)^{2}=e(E\oplus E)
. Thus in our case we have
p_{\frac{a-2}{2}}(\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)})=e(\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}\oplus\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)})
.

The Poincare dual of
e(\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}\oplus\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)})
can be given as the sum of the number of zeroes of a section, counted with multiplicity (Appendix
A.4
).
Define a smooth section
s:\mathop{\rm Gr}(2,\mathbb{R}^{a})\rightarrow\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}\oplus\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}
by
s(V)=(V,\pi_{V}({\mathbf{e}}_{a-1}),\pi_{V}({\mathbf{e}}_{a})))
, where
\pi_{V}
denotes projection onto
V^{\perp}
. The section
s
coincides with the zero section only at
V_{0}:=\mathop{\rm row}(\mathbf{0}|I)\in W_{a-1,a}
, and so
e(\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}\oplus\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)})
is the multiplicity of
V_{0}
.

Let
\tilde{s}
denote the composition, using the local coordinates on
W_{a-1,a}
described in Section
4.1
,

{\mathbb{R}}^{2(a-2)}\cong W_{a-1,a}\xrightarrow{s}(\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}\oplus\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)})|{W_{a-1,a}}\xrightarrow{\cong}W_{a-1,a}\times V_{0}^{\perp}\times V_{0}^{\perp}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{a-2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{a-2}

The multiplicity of the intersection point
V_{0}
is 1 if
\tilde{s}_{*}:T_{0}{\mathbb{R}}^{2(a-2)}\to T_{(0,0)}({\mathbb{R}}^{a-2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{a-2})
is orientation preserving and
-1
if
\tilde{s}_{*}
is orientation-reversing. The map
(\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)}\oplus\mathrm{EGr}_{(a-2)})|_{W_{a-1,a}}\xrightarrow{\cong}W_{a-1,a}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{a-2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{a-2}
takes each
(x,v_{1},v_{2})
to
(x,v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime})
, where
v_{i}^{\prime}
is obtained from
v_{i}
by deleting the last two components.

The tangent space
T_{0}{\mathbb{R}}^{2(a-2)}
has as standard basis the tangents to the curves
t{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2(a-2)}
. Composing these curves with our map
\tilde{s}
, we see

\tilde{s}(t{\mathbf{e}}_{i})=\begin{cases}(\frac{-t}{1+t^{2}}{\mathbf{e}}_{\frac{i+1}{2}},0)&\mbox{if $i$ is odd}\\
(0,\frac{-t}{1+t^{2}}{\mathbf{e}}_{\frac{i}{2}})&\mbox{if $i$ is even}\end{cases}

Thus

\tilde{s}_{*}({\mathbf{e}}_{i})=\begin{cases}(-{\mathbf{e}}_{\frac{i+1}{2}},0)&\mbox{if $i$ is odd}\\
(0,-{\mathbf{e}}_{\frac{i}{2}})&\mbox{if $i$ is even}\end{cases}

Thus the standard positively oriented ordered basis of
T_{(0,\ldots,0)}{\mathbb{R}}^{2(a-2)}
is mapped by
\tilde{s}_{*}
to the an ordered basis with the same sign as
(({\mathbf{e}}_{1},0),(0,{\mathbf{e}}_{1}),({\mathbf{e}}_{2},0),(0,{\mathbf{e}}_{2}),\ldots,({\mathbf{e}}_{a-2},0),(0,{\mathbf{e}}_{a-2}))
. The sign of the permutation taking this to the standard basis

(({\mathbf{e}}_{1},0),\ldots,({\mathbf{e}}_{a-2},0),(0,{\mathbf{e}}_{1}),\ldots,(0,{\mathbf{e}}_{a-2}))
of
T({\mathbb{R}}^{a-2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{a-2})
is
(-1)^{\frac{(a-3)(a-2)}{2}}=(-1)^{\frac{(a-2)}{2}}
.

∎

The alternate formula

Recall from the Introduction the bundle
\xi_{2}:E_{2}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
. Let
E_{(a-2)}=\{(m,V,v):(m,V)\in{\mathcal{Y}},v\in V^{\perp}\}
and
\xi_{(a-2)}:E_{(a-2)}\to{\mathcal{Y}}

A connection on

\omega=\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{1}&0\\

where

\omega_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\omega_{12}\\

The corresponding curvature matrix is

\Omega_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\Omega_{12}\\
-\Omega_{12}&0\par\par\end{bmatrix}

where
\Omega_{12}=d\omega_{12}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

p_{1}(E_{2})=[(\frac{1}{2\pi}\Omega_{12})^{2}]

Proof.

This follows from the definition of the total Pontrjagin class as

5.1.

5.2.

The classifying space for rank
n
real vector bundles is the Grassmannian
\mathop{\rm Gr}(n,{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty})
. Thus, a smooth
n
-dimensional manifold
M
has a
classifying map

M\to\mathop{\rm Gr}(n,{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty})
such that the pullback of the canonical bundle over
\mathop{\rm Gr}(n,{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty})
is isomorphic to
TM
. In this section we’ll describe how a smoothing
\eta:\|X\|\to M
gives such a map, specifically from
M
to
\mathop{\rm Gr}(n,{\mathbb{R}}^{X^{0}})
.

As in Appendix
C
, it will be convenient to view a matrix as an arrangement of column vectors and to allow the columns to be indexed by an arbitrary finite set

, not necessarily
[m]
. Thus we’ll feel free to view
({\mathbf{v}}_{i}:i\in S)
as either an arrangement of vectors or as an object with, for instance, a row space (contained in
{\mathbb{R}}^{S}
).

The flattenings induced by a smooth triangulation of

give such vector arrangements: as we have seen, at each
p\in M
we get a vector arrangement
(f_{p}(v):v\in\mathop{\rm star}(\Delta_{p})^{0})
, and by setting

for each

we get a vector arrangement

. But the resulting map

taking each
p
to
\mathop{\rm row}(f_{p}(v):v\in{X^{0}})

is not continuous: typically, as we go from the interior of some
\eta(\Delta)
to its boundary, the number of nonzero vectors in our arrangement increases.

To get a continuous map

, we’ll extend our vector arrangements a bit.

For convenience we assume

Definition 5.15

There are two key facts in proving this. The first

Theorem 5.22
.

[
Bab94
]
Let

be a poset map satisfying both of the following.

(1)

If

then
\|f^{-1}(q)_{\geq p}\|
is contractible.

(2)

If
q\leq f(p)
then
\|f^{-1}(q)_{\leq p}\|
is contractible.

Then
\|f\|:\|P\|\to\|Q\|
is a quasifibration.

Proof.

We need to check two criteria for each

:

(1)

If
(\Delta^{\prime},t^{\prime},y^{\prime})\geq(\Delta,t,y)
then
\|\{(\Delta^{\prime},t^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}):(\Delta^{\prime},t^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\geq(\Delta,t,y,z)\}\|
is contractible.

(2)

If
(\Delta^{\prime},t^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq(\Delta,t,y)
then
\|\{(\Delta^{\prime},t^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}):(\Delta^{\prime},t^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\leq(\Delta,t,y,z)\}\|
is contractible.

In either criterion, the question is whether a certain set of covectors

of

is contractible.

For the first criterion, the poset

indexes the cells in an open arc in

indexes the cells in a closed arc in

. This arc is nonempty by Theorem 0.1 in
[

, hence the order complex of

∎

As shown in Appendix

Proof.

For each

Appendix A
De Rham and simplicial cohomology

In the main body of the paper we go from formulas in terms of de Rham cohomology to combinatorial formulas in terms of simplicial cohomology. He we briefly review how one goes between these cohomology theories.

Most of this discussion is quite standard: see, for instance
[
BT82
]
and
[
Hat02
]
for more detail.

De Rham cohomology is a cohomology with real coefficients associated to smooth manifolds.

Definition A.1
.

A
k
-form on a smooth manifold
M
is a smooth assignment to each
p\in M
of an alternating
k
-linear function

\omega_{p}:T_{p}M\times\cdots\times T_{p}M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

We denote the space of all
k
-forms on
M
by
\Omega_{dR}^{k}(M)
.

In local coordinates on an
n
-dimensional smooth manifold, a
k
-form can be expressed as
\omega=\sum_{I}\omega_{I}dx^{I}
, where
I
ranges over
k
-subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and each
\omega_{I}:M\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function.
There is a differential map
d:\Omega_{dR}^{k}(M)\rightarrow\Omega_{dR}^{k+1}(M)
that is expressed in terms of local coordinates as

d(\sum_{I}\omega_{I}dx^{I})=\sum_{I}d\omega_{I}\wedge dx^{I}

The cochain complex
\{\Omega_{dR}^{k}(M),d\}
of a compact smooth manifold
M
is called the
de Rham cochain complex
, and the cohomology associated to this cochain complex is the
de Rham cohomology
.

Simplicial cohomology can be defined with coefficients in an arbitrary ring
R
. If
X
is a simplicial complex and
\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}\}
is the vertex set of a simplex
\sigma
then we define two orderings of
\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}\}
to be equivalent if they differ by an even permutation, and we define an
orientation
on
\sigma
to be one of the two equivalence classes. We define
C_{k}(X,R)
to be the module over
R
generated by the set of oriented
k
-simplicies of
X
, modulo the relation that opposite orientations of a simplex are inverses, and we define
C^{k}(X,R)
to be the dual
\mathop{\rm Hom}(C_{k}(X),R)
. There are differentials

\displaystyle d:C_{k}(X,R)

\displaystyle\to C_{k-1}(X,R)

\displaystyle d[x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}[x_{0},\ldots,\hat{x}_{i},\ldots,x_{k}]

and

\displaystyle\partial:C^{k}(X,R)

\displaystyle\to C^{k+1}(X,R)

\displaystyle(\partial\sigma)([x_{0},\ldots,x_{k+1}])

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{i}\sigma[x_{0},\ldots,\hat{x}_{i},\ldots,x_{k+1}]

We define the simplicial homology
H_{*}(X,R)
and cohomology
H^{*}(X,R)
of
X
with coefficients in
R
to be the homology and cohomology associated to
\{C_{k}(X,R),d\}
and
\{C^{k}(X,R),\partial\}
. Any two simplicial complexes with the same underlying topological space
M
have canonically isomorphic homology and cohomology, so we refer to the simplical homology and cohomology of the underlying space.

To see an isomorphism between the de Rham and simplicial cohomology with real coefficients of a smoothly triangulated manifold, we assume our simplicial complex
X
to be a geometric simplicial complex in Euclidean space. If
h:X\to M
is a homeomorphism that is smooth on closed simplices, then we define
I:\Omega_{dR}^{k}(M)\to C^{k}(X,{\mathbb{R}})
by

I([\Omega])([\sum_{\sigma}a_{\sigma}\sigma])=\sum_{\sigma}a_{\sigma}\int_{[\sigma]}h^{*}\Omega

This is a map of cochain complexes by Stokes’ Theorem, and it can be shown to induce an isomorphism in cohomology.

If
\xi:E\to B
is a real vector bundle and
\omega
is a form on
E
, we say that
\omega
has
compact vertical support
if, for each compact
K\subseteq M
,
\xi^{-1}(K)\cap\mathop{\rm supp}(\omega)
is compact. The set of forms with compact vertical support forms a subcomplex of the de Rham cochain complex of
E
, and the cohomology of this complex is the
compact vertical cohomology

H^{*}_{cv}(E)
of
E
.

Let
\xi:E\to M
be a real vector bundle over a compact manifold
M
. Let
i:M\to E
denote the zero section, let
E_{0}=E-i(M)
, and let
\xi_{0}:E_{0}\to M
be the restriction. Let
S(E)
be the quotient of
E_{0}
by the action of
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
on fibers. Let
D(E)
be the fiberwise join of
S(E)
and
i(M)
. Then
\xi
induces a sphere bundle
S(\xi):S(E)\to M
and a disk bundle
D(\xi):D(E)\to M
.
If
D(\xi)
is triangulated then simplicial cohomology
H^{*}(D(\xi),S(\xi),R)
is canonically isomorphic to
H^{*}(E,E_{0},R)
for every ring
R
, and
H^{*}(E,E_{0},{\mathbb{R}})
is canonically isomorphic to
H^{*}_{cv}(E)
. In what follows we will identify
H^{*}(E,E_{0},{\mathbb{R}})
with
H^{*}_{cv}(E)
.

A.1.
Cohomology with various coefficients

The particular characteristic classes we will define in terms of de Rham cohomology are each the image of a class defined in integer chomology under the composition

H^{i}(M,\mathbb{Z})\to\mathop{\rm Hom}(H_{i}(M,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Z})\hookrightarrow\mathop{\rm Hom}(H_{i}(M,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Q})\cong H^{i}(M,\mathbb{Q})\hookrightarrow\mathop{\rm Hom}(H_{i}(M,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{R})\cong H^{i}(M,\mathbb{R})

Thus these classes exist in
H^{i}(M,\mathbb{Q})
, and we call them
rational characteristic classes
(e.g. rational Pontrjagin classes). For definitions of the corresponding integer characteristic classes see, for instance,
[
MS74
]
.

Let
\pi:N\to M
be a fiber bundle with orientation presheaf
{\mathcal{O}}
and
R
a ring. We denote by
H^{*}(M,{\mathcal{O}})
the cohomology of
M
with coefficients in
R
, twisted by the
{\mathcal{O}}
. (If
\pi
is orientable then

H^{*}(M,{\mathcal{O}})
is canonically isomorphic to
H^{*}(M,R)
.) The same comments on integer vs. rational vs. real coefficients hold in the twisted setting, and this justifies some ambiguity in specifying the ring
R
. Most notably, in the context of de Rham cohomology we will define a twisted first Chern class
c_{1}(E_{2},{\mathcal{O}})
of a vector bundle, and in this setting the ring is
{\mathbb{R}}
. But because Chern classes are defined with twisted integer coefficients, this class will make sense as a twisted rational cohomology class.

A.2.
Cap product and Poincare duality

If
[x_{0},\ldots,x_{k+l}]
is an oriented
(k+l)
-simplex and
\psi\in C^{k}(X,R)
then we define
[x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}]\frown\psi\in C_{l}(X,R)
by

([x_{0},\ldots,x_{k+l}]\frown\psi)=\psi([x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}])[x_{k},\ldots,x_{l}]
. This induces the
cap product

\frown:H_{k+l}(X,R)\times H^{k}(X,R)\to H_{l}(X,R)
.
If
X
is a simplicial complex whose underlying space is a compact orientable
n
-dimensional manifold
M
then an orientation on
M
determines an orientation on each maximal simplex of
X
. The sum of all of these oriented simplices, denoted
[M]
, generates
H_{n}(M,R)
, and Poincare Duality states that the map

\mbox{PD}:H^{i}(M,R)\to H_{n-i}(M,R)
given by
PD(c)=[M]\frown c
is an isomorphism.

An analog in de Rham chomology to the cap product is given by integration over a smooth submanifold. Let
[N]
denote the homology class determined by an oriented compact
(k+l)
-dimensional smooth submanifold
N
of
M
. If
\alpha
is a
k
-form on
M
then we get an element
[N]\frown[\alpha]\in(H^{l}_{dR}(M))^{*}
defined by

([N]\frown[\alpha])[\beta]=\int_{N}\alpha\wedge\beta

For homology classes which can be represented by formal sums of submanifolds, this cap product coincides with the simplicial cap product.
If
M
itself is oriented of dimension
n
, then Poincare duality for de Rham cohomology says that the map

\displaystyle\mbox{PD}:H^{k}_{dR}(M)

\displaystyle\rightarrow(H^{n-k}_{dR}(M))^{*}

\displaystyle\mbox{PD}([\omega])([\tau])

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\omega\wedge\tau

is an isomorphism. The Universal Coefficient Theorem gives a canonical isomorphism from
(H^{*}_{dR}(M))^{*}
to the (simplicial) homology of
M
, and so we identify
(H^{*}_{dR}(M))^{*}
with
H_{*}(M)
.

Poincare duality generalizes to non-orientable manifolds if we twist coefficients. Let
X
be a simplicial complex whose underlying space is a compact
n
-dimensional manifold
M
with orientation presheaf
{\mathcal{D}}
. The same chain-level definition as before gives a cap product
H_{k+l}(M,{\mathcal{D}})\times H^{k}(M,R)\to H_{l}(M,{\mathcal{D}})
. In
H_{n}(M,{\mathcal{D}})
we have a fundamental class
[M]
, and cap product with
[M]
gives an isomorphism
PD:H^{i}(M,R)\to H_{n-i}(M,{\mathcal{D}})
.

A.3.
Integration over the fiber

Let
\pi:N\to M
be a smooth orientable bundle with compact
k
-dimensional fiber
F
. We can integrate an
i
-form
\alpha
on
E
over each fiber to get a form on
M
, giving us the
transfer map

\pi^{!}:H^{*}(E)\to H^{*}(M)
. If
i<k
then
\pi^{!}([\alpha])=0
. Otherwise
\pi^{!}([\alpha])\in H_{dR}^{i-k}(M,{\mathcal{D}})
.

Lemma A.2
.

(Projection formula for fiber bundles) Let
\pi:N\to M
be a smooth fiber bundle with
M
and
N
compact. For every form
\alpha
on
M
and
\beta
on

,

(1)

\pi^{!}[(\pi^{*}\alpha)\wedge\beta]=[\alpha]\smile\pi^{!}[\beta]

(2)

\pi_{*}[\int_{N}(\pi^{*}(\alpha)\wedge\beta)]=[\int_{M}\alpha]\smile\pi^{!}[\beta]

Proposition A.3
.

Let
\pi:N\to M
be a smooth fiber bundle with
N
and
M
compact. For any
[\alpha]\in H^{*}_{dR}(N)

\pi_{*}([\alpha]\frown[N])=\pi^{!}[\alpha]\frown[M].

Proposition
A.3
gives us an alternate description of the transfer map in terms of
Poincare Duality:

\pi^{!}=PD^{-1}_{M}\pi_{*}PD_{N}

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be a
(k+f)
-form, so that both sides of the equation in the proposition are in

. For each
[\tau]\in H^{n-k}(M,{\mathcal{D}})
,

\displaystyle[\tau]\frown(\pi_{*}([\alpha]\frown[N])

\displaystyle=\pi_{*}(\pi^{*}[\tau]\frown([\alpha]\frown[N]))

by naturality of cap product

\displaystyle=\pi_{*}((\pi^{*}[\tau]\smile[\alpha])\frown[N])

\displaystyle=\pi_{*}(\int_{N}\pi^{*}(\tau)\wedge\alpha)

\displaystyle=[\int_{M}\tau]\smile\pi^{!}[\alpha]

by the Projection Formula

\displaystyle=(\tau\smile\pi^{!}[\alpha])\frown[M]

\displaystyle=\tau\frown(\pi^{!}[\alpha]\frown[M])

∎

The
(rational) Thom class
of
E
is the unique
\tau\in H^{*}_{cv}(E)
such that the restriction of
\tau
to a fiber is the preferred generator. The
Thom isomorphism

\Phi:H^{k}_{dR}(B)\to H^{k+r}_{cv}(E)
sends each

\xi^{*}(\alpha)\smile\tau
. In simplicial cohomology we can define the Thom class

\tau\in H^{r}(E,E_{0},R)
and the Thom isomorphism
\Phi:H^{k}(B,R)\to H^{k+r}(E,E_{0},R)
in the same way.

Proposition A.4
.

For a real vector bundle
\xi:E\to B
the transfer map is the inverse of the Thom isomorphism.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in H^{*}(B,R)
. Then

\displaystyle\xi^{!}(\Phi(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\xi^{!}(\xi^{*}(\alpha)\smile\tau)

\displaystyle=\alpha\smile\xi^{!}(\tau)

by the Projection Formula

\displaystyle=\alpha\smile 1

\displaystyle=\alpha

∎

A.4.
The Euler class

The real
Euler class
of an oriented vector bundle
\xi:E\to B
is defined to be the image of the Thom class under
i^{*}:H^{*}_{cv}(E)\to H_{dR}^{*}(M)
.

When
M
is a smooth oriented manifold, the Euler class of the tangent bundle has a beautiful geometric interpretation (cf. Proposition 12.8 in
[
BT82
]
): for any section
s
of
\xi
which intersects the zero section transversely, the Euler class is the Poincare dual to the homology class given by the zero locus of
s
(once appropriate orientation is given to this zero locus). This comes out particularly simply when the dimension of the base space is also

, since then the zero locus is a discrete set of points. For each such point

, transversality of
s
and the zero section

tells us that
T_{s(p)}(E)=s_{*}(T_{p}(M))\oplus z_{*}(T_{p}(M))
. Define
c(p)
to be 1 if the orientations of the two sides of this equation agree and
-1
if they disagree. Then the Poincare dual of

is

e(E)\frown[M]=\sum_{p}c(p)

where the sum is over all points in the zero locus of
s
.

Appendix B
The Chern class with twisted coefficients

For an oriented circle bundle
\rho:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, the Chern class is the obstruction to obtaining a closed global 1-cocycle in
H^{1}(\mathcal{Z})
which restricts to a generator of the cohomology in each fiber. A circle bundle that is not necessarily oriented has a twisted Chern class, which lives in cohomology with coefficients in the orientation presheaf. This class can be defined in terms of the Leray-Serre spectral sequence, and this definition can be extended to other maps with similarly-behaved Leray-Serre spectral sequences, not just bundles. In this section we’ll lay out a setup that admits this generalization.
Our discussion is adapted from
[
BT82
]
.

Definition B.1
.

A
quasifibration
is a surjective map
f:E\to B
such that, for each
b\in B
and
e\in f^{-1}(b)
, each induced map
f_{*}:\pi_{i}(E,f^{-1}(b),e)\to\pi_{i}(B,b)
is a bijection.

It follows that a quasifibration induces a long exact sequence

\cdots\to\pi_{i+1}(B,b)\to\pi_{i}(f^{-1}(b),e)\to\pi_{i}(E,e)\to\pi_{i}(B,b)\cdots

A fibrewise map between quasifibrations induces a morphism of long exact sequences.

Every continuous map has an associated fibration (cf.
[
Qui73
]
, Section 4.3), whose fiber we call the homotopy fiber of the map. If
f
is a quasifibration, then each fiber of
f
has the same weak homotopy type as the homotopy fiber of
f
.

We’ll be interested in a simplicial quasifibration in which the fiber over each vertex is a simplicial
S^{1}
(and therefore the fiber over every point is a homotopy
S^{1}
).

Proposition B.2
.

Let
E
and
B
be connected simplicial complexes and
f:E\rightarrow B
be a simplicial quasifibration with connected homotopy fiber
F
. Let
{\mathcal{U}}
be the cover of
B
by open stars, and let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a presheaf of modules that is locally constant with respect to
{\mathcal{U}}
.
Then for each
q
the presheaf

{\mathcal{H}}^{q}(U)=H^{q}(f^{-1}U,{\mathcal{P}}(U))

is locally constant.

Proof.

Let
\tau\subset\sigma
be simplices of
B
.
By Whitehead’s Theorem with twisted coefficients (
[
Qui67
]
, Proposition 4, Chapter 2, Section 3), it’s enough to show that the inclusion
f^{-1}U_{\sigma}\hookrightarrow f^{-1}U_{\tau}

Appendix C
Oriented matroids

An
oriented matroid
is a kind of combinatorial data that can be expressed in several equivalent (“cryptomorphic”) ways. All of these ways involve the set
\{0,+,-\}
, which is viewed as a poset with unique minimum 0 and maxima
+
and
-
. For any set
S
, the set
\{0,+,-\}^{S}
is ordered componentwise. Commutative multiplication on
\{0,+,-\}
is defined in the usual way:
0\cdot x=0
for all
x
,
+\cdot+=-\cdot-=+
,
+\cdot-=-
. If
{\mathbf{v}}=(v_{e}:e\in E)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
then
\mathop{\rm sign}({\mathbf{v}})
denotes
(\mathop{\rm sign}(v_{e}):e\in E)\in\{0,+,-\}^{E}
.

C.1.
Motivation: vector arrangements and subspaces

Let
({\mathbf{v}}_{e}:e\in E)
be an arrangement of column vectors in a rank
r
vector space
V
over
{\mathbb{R}}
which spans
V
. By choosing coordinates in
V
and an ordering of
E

we can view this arrangement as an
r\times|E|
matrix
M
with columns indexed by
E
.

Consider the following two ways to extract combinatorial data from
M
.

(1)

Let
\chi:E^{r}\to\{0,+,-\}
be the function taking each
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{r})\in E^{r}
to the sign of the determinant of the matrix
({\mathbf{v}}_{e_{1}},\ldots,{\mathbf{v}}_{e_{r}})
. The function
\chi
is called the
chirotope
associated to
M
.

We are interested in combinatorial data that is independent of the choice of coordinates. If
A\in GL_{r}
and
\chi^{\prime}
is the chirotope associated to
AM
then
\chi^{\prime}=\pm\chi
. So our interest is in the pair
\{\chi,-\chi\}
.

(2)

For each vector
{\mathbf{w}}=(w_{e}:e\in E)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
, let
\mathop{\rm sign}({\mathbf{w}})=(\mathop{\rm sign}(w_{e}):e\in E)\in\{0,+,-\}^{E}
. Let
{\mathcal{V}}^{*}=\{\mathop{\rm sign}({\mathbf{w}}):{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathop{\rm row}(M)\}
.
{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
is the
covector set
associated to
M
.

{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
is coordinate-independent: for any
A\in GL_{r}
,
M
and
AM
have the same associated covector set.

It is not hard to see that the pair
\pm\chi
determines the set
{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
and vice-versa. The
oriented matroid
associated to the vector arrangement is the combinatorial information encoded by either
\pm\chi
or
{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
.

Every rank
r
subspace
V
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is the row space of some rank
r
matrix
M\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times E}
. Viewing
M
as a vector arrangement, we see that the covector set of the oriented matroid associated to
M
is
\{\mathop{\rm sign}({\mathbf{v}}):{\mathbf{v}}\in V\}
. Thus it makes sense to also call this the oriented matroid corresponding to
V
. The set of full-rank matrices with row space
V
is
GL_{r}M
; as vector arrangements, the elements of this set are the images of the vector arrangement
M
under invertible linear transformations.

There are many geometric interpretations of the data encoded by an oriented matroid: we list a few.

•

The pair
\{\chi,-\chi\}
of chirotopes associated to a vector arrangement tells us

–

which
(r-1)
-tuples from the arrangement span hyperplanes, and

–

for each
(r-1)
-tuple spanning a hyperplane
H
, which of the remaining vectors are in
H
and which pairs among the remaining vectors are in the same open half-space bounded by
H
.

•

The covector set associated to
M=({\mathbf{v}}_{e}:e\in E)
is
\{\mathop{\rm sign}({\mathbf{y}}M):{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}\}
, and the component of
\mathop{\rm sign}({\mathbf{y}}M)
corresponding to
e\in E
says whether the vector
{\mathbf{v}}_{e}
lies on the same side of the hyperplane
{\mathbf{y}}^{\perp}
as
{\mathbf{y}}
, on the opposite side as
{\mathbf{y}}
, or on
{\mathbf{y}}^{\perp}
.

•

The chirotopes
\pm\chi
associated to a subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is the pair of signs of Plücker coordinates for that subspace.

•

The covector set associated to a subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
tells us which orthants of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
have nonempty intersection with that subspace.

C.2.
Formal definitions

Definition C.1
.

Let
E
be a finite set and
r
a positive integer. A
rank
r
chirotope on elements

is a nonzero alternating function
\chi:E^{r}\to\{0,+,-\}
satisfying the following
combinatorial Grassmann-Plücker relations
: for each
e_{2},e_{3},\ldots,e_{r},f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in E
, the set

\{(-1)^{i}\chi(f_{i},e_{2},\ldots,e_{r})\chi(f_{0},\ldots,\hat{f}_{i},\ldots,f_{r}):i\in\{0,\ldots,r\}\}

either is
\{0\}
or contains both

and

.

Notation C.2

Let

{\mathbf{y}}(e)&\mbox{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Let

.

(1)

(2)

(3)

If

A rank

of chirotopes or the corresponding covector set is called an oriented matroid.

The motivating examples of Section

are easily seen to satisfy these definitions. However, there are oriented matroids which are not

as vector arrangements.

C.4.
The Topological Representation Theorem

Let
{\mathcal{V}}^{*}
be the set of covectors arising from an arrangement

of vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{r}

\{{\mathbf{y}}\in S^{r-1}:{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathop{\rm sign}({\mathbf{y}}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{e}):e\in E)\}

(2)

or

.

An essential arrangement

.

If
M
is a smooth manifold, a
Whitney stratification
of a closed subset
T
of
M
is a partition of
T
into manifolds that behaves well with respect to the smooth structure on
M
. Specifically, a Whitney stratification of
T
is a locally finite partition
(S_{i}:i\in P)
of
T
, where each
S_{i}
is a locally closed smooth submanifold of
M
and
P
is a poset, satisfying

(1)

For each
\alpha,\beta\in P
,

S_{\alpha}\cap\overline{S_{\beta}}\neq\emptyset\Leftrightarrow S_{\alpha}\subseteq\overline{S_{\beta}}\Leftrightarrow\alpha\leq\beta.

(2)

Suppose
\alpha\leq\beta
in
P
, and suppose
(x_{i})
is a sequence of points in
S_{\beta}
converging to some
y\in S_{\alpha}
. Suppose a sequence
(y_{i})
in
S_{\alpha}
also converges to
y
, and suppose that, in local coordinates on
M
, the secant lines
l_{i}=\overline{x_{i}y_{i}}
converge to some line
l
, and the tangent planes
T_{x_{i}}S_{\beta}
converge to some plane
\tau
. Then

(a)

(Whitney’s condition A)

T_{y}S_{\alpha}\subseteq\tau
and

(b)

(Whitney’s condition B)

l\subseteq\tau
.

(It is not hard to see (cf.
[
GM88
]
,
[
Tro79
]
) that Whitney’s condition B implies Whitney’s condition A.)

We summarize here all we need to know about Whitney stratifications for our purposes.
