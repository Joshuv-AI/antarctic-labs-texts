1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study the cyclicity problem with respect to the forward shift operator
S_{b}
acting on the de Branges–Rovnyak space
\mathscr{H}(b)
, associated to a function
b
belonging to the closed unit ball of
H^{\infty}
and satisfying
\log(1-|b|)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. This problem of cyclicity has a long and outstanding history and many efforts have been dedicated to solving it in various reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. It finds its roots in the pioneering work of Beurling who showed that cyclicity of a function
f
in the Hardy space
H^{2}
is equivalent to
f
being outer. Brown and Shields studied the cyclicity problem in the Dirichlet spaces
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
for polynomials that do not have zeros inside the disc, but that may have some zeros on its boundary. Such functions are cyclic in
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
if and only if
\alpha\leqslant 1
. They also proved that the set of zeros on the unit circle (in the radial sense) of cyclic functions in the Dirichlet space
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
has zero logarithmic capacity, and this led them to ask whether any outer function with this property is cyclic
[
4
]
. This problem is still open although there has been relevant contributions to the topic by a number of authors; e.g. see
[
2
,
3
,
11
,
12
,
22
,
26
]
. We also mention the paper
[
10
]
where the authors prove the Brown–Shields conjecture in the context of some particular Dirichlet type spaces
\mathcal{D}(\mu)
, which happen to be related to our context of de Branges–Rovnyak spaces
[
6
]
.

The de Branges–Rovnyak spaces
\mathscr{H}(b)
(see the precise definition in Section
2
) have been introduced by L. de Branges and J. Rovnyak in the context of model theory (see
[
7
]
). A whole class of Hilbert space contractions is unitarily equivalent to
S^{*}|\mathscr{H}(b)
, for an appropriate function
b
belonging to the closed unit ball of
H^{\infty}
. Here
S
is the forward shift operator on
H^{2}
and
S^{*}
, its adjoint, is the backward shift operator on
H^{2}
. The space
\mathscr{H}(b)
is invariant with respect to
S^{*}
for every
b
in the closed unit ball of
H^{\infty}
, and
S^{*}
defines a bounded operator on
\mathscr{H}(b)
, endowed with its own Hilbert space topology.
On the contrary,
\mathscr{H}(b)
is invariant with respect to
S
if and only if
\log(1-|b|)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, i.e. if and only if
b
is a non-extreme point of the closed unit ball of
H^{\infty}
. See
[
18
, Corollary 25.2]
. In
[
19
]
, it is proved that if
\log(1-|b|)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, the cyclic vectors of
S^{*}|\mathscr{H}(b)
are precisely the cyclic vectors of
S^{*}
which live in
\mathscr{H}(b)
. Note that cyclic vectors of
S^{*}
have been characterised by Douglas–Shapiro–Shields
[
8
]
. The result of
[
19
]
is based on a nice description, due to Sarason, of closed invariant subspaces of
S^{*}|\mathscr{H}(b)
. See also
[
18
, Corollary 24.32]
. Unfortunately an analogous description of closed invariant subspaces of
S_{b}=S|\mathscr{H}(b)
remains an unsolved and difficult problem. In
[
24
]
, Gu–Luo–Richter give an answer in the case where
b
is a rational function which is not inner, generalising a result of Sarason
[
27
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to study the cyclic vectors of
S_{b}
when the function
b
is such that
\log(1-|b|)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. In Section
2
, we present a quick overview of some useful properties of de Branges–Rovnyak spaces. Then, in Section
3
, we give some general facts on cyclic vectors and completely characterise holomorphic functions in a neighborhood of the closed unit disc which are cyclic for
S_{b}
. In Section
4
, we give a characterisation of cyclic vectors for
S_{b}
when
b
is rational (and not inner). Of course, this characterisation can be derived from the description,
given in
[
24
]
,
of invariant subspaces of
S_{b}
when
b
is a non-inner rational function. Nevertheless, we will give a more direct and easier proof of this characterisation. Finally, Section
5
will be devoted to the situation where
b=(1+I)/2
, where
I
is a non-constant inner function such that the associated model space
K_{I}=\mathscr{H}(I)
has an orthonormal basis of reproducing kernels.

2.
Preliminaries on
\mathscr{H}(b)
spaces

2.1.
Definition of de Branges-Rovnyak spaces

Let

\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty}):=\Big\{b\in H^{\infty}:\|b\|_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|b(z)|\leqslant 1\Big\}

be the closed unit ball of
H^{\infty}
, the space of bounded analytic functions on the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}
, endowed with the sup norm.
For
b\in\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
, the
de Branges–Rovnyak space

\mathscr{H}(b)
is the reproducing kernel Hilbert space on
\mathbb{D}
associated with the positive definite kernel
k_{\lambda}^{b}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, defined as

(2.1)

k^{b}_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{1-\overline{b(\lambda)}b(z)}{1-\overline{\lambda}z},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

It is known that
\mathscr{H}(b)
is contractively contained in the well-studied Hardy space
H^{2}
of analytic functions
f
on
\mathbb{D}
for which

\|f\|_{H^{2}}:=\Big(\sup_{0<r<1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f(r\xi)|^{2}dm(\xi)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

where
m
is the normalised Lebesgue measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{C}:|\xi|=1\}

[
9
,
21
]
. For every
f\in H^{2}
, the radial limit

\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}f(r\xi)=:f(\xi)
(even the non-tangential limit
f(\xi):=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\to\xi\\
\sphericalangle\end{subarray}}f(z)
) exists for
m
-a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
, and

(2.2)

\|f\|_{H^{2}}=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f(\xi)|^{2}dm(\xi)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Though
\mathscr{H}(b)
is contractively contained in
H^{2}
, it is generally not closed in the
H^{2}
norm. It is known that
\mathscr{H}(b)
is closed in
H^{2}
if and only if
b=I
is an inner function, meaning that
|I(\zeta)|=1
for a.e.
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
. In this case,
\mathscr{H}(b)=K_{I}=(IH^{2})^{\perp}
is the so-called
model space
associated to
I
. Note that
K_{I}=H^{2}\cap I\overline{zH^{2}}
(see
[
20
, Proposition 5.4]
), and then
K_{I}=\operatorname{Ker}T_{\bar{I}}
, where
T_{\bar{I}}
is the Toeplitz operator with symbol
\bar{I}
defined on
H^{2}
as
T_{\bar{I}}f=P_{+}(\bar{I}f)
, where
P_{+}
denotes the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}
onto
H^{2}
.

We refer the reader to the book
[
28
]
by Sarason and to the monograph
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
by Fricain and Mashreghi for an in-depth study of de Branges-Rovnyak spaces and their connections to numerous other topics in operator theory and complex analysis.

In this paper, we will always assume that
b
is a non-extreme point of
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
, which is equivalent to requiring that
\log(1-|b|)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. Under this assumption, there is a unique outer function
a
, called
the pythagorean mate
for
b
, such that
a(0)>0
and
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. There are two important subspaces of
\mathscr{H}(b)
which can be defined via this function
a
. The first one is the space
\mathscr{M}(a)=aH^{2}
, equipped with the range norm

\|af\|_{\mathscr{M}(a)}=\|f\|_{2},\qquad f\in H^{2}.

The second one is
\mathscr{M}(\bar{a})=T_{\bar{a}}H^{2}
, equipped also with the range norm

\|T_{\bar{a}}f\|_{\mathscr{M}(\bar{a})}=\|f\|_{2},\qquad f\in H^{2}.

Note that since
a
is outer, the Toeplitz operator
T_{\bar{a}}
is one-to-one and so the above norm is well defined. It is known that
\mathscr{M}(a)
is contractively contained into
\mathscr{M}(\bar{a})
, which itself is contractively contained into
\mathscr{H}(b)
. See
[
18
, Theorem 23.2]
. Note that
\mathscr{M}(a)
is not necessarily closed in
\mathscr{H}(b)
. See
[
18
, Theorem 28.35]
for a characterisation of closeness of
\mathscr{M}(a)
in
\mathscr{H}(b)
-norm. There is also an important relation between
\mathscr{H}(b)
and
\mathscr{M}(\bar{a})
which gives a recipe to compute the norm in
\mathscr{H}(b)
. Indeed, if
f\in H^{2}
, then
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
if and only if there is a function
f^{+}\in H^{2}
satisfying
T_{\bar{b}}f=T_{\bar{a}}f^{+}
(and then
f^{+}
is necessarily unique). Moreover, in this case, we have

\|f\|_{b}^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}+\|f^{+}\|_{2}^{2}.

Also,

(2.3)

\langle f,g\rangle_{b}=\langle f,g\rangle_{2}+\langle f^{+},g^{+}\rangle_{2}\quad\textrm{ for every }f,g\in\mathscr{H}(b).

See
[
18
, Theorem 23.8]
. Finally, let us recall that
\mathscr{H}(b)=\mathscr{M}(\bar{a})
if and only if
(a,b)
forms a corona pair, that is

\qquad\inf_{\mathbb{D}}(|a|+|b|)>0.

See
[
18
, Theorem 28.7]
.

A crucial fact on de Branges-Rovnyak space is that the space
\mathscr{H}(b)
is invariant with respect to the shift operator
S:f\mapsto zf
if and only if the function
b
is non-extreme. Since we will consider in this paper only the case where
b
is non-extreme,
\mathscr{H}(b)
is indeed invariant by
S
, and
S
defines a bounded operator on
\mathscr{H}(b)
, endowed with its own Hilbert space topology, which we will denote by
S_{b}
. The functions
z^{n}
belong to
\mathscr{H}(b)

for every
n\geq 0
. Actually, we have

(2.4)

\mbox{Span}(z^{n}:n\geq 0)=\mathscr{H}(b),

where
\mbox{Span}(A)
denotes the closed linear span generated by vectors from a certain family
A
.
In other words, the polynomials are dense in
\mathscr{H}(b)
. See
[
18
, Theorem 23.13]
.
Note that (
2.4
) exactly means that the constant function
1
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

Another tool which will turn out to be useful when studying the cyclicity for the shift operator is the notion of
multiplier
. Recall that the set
\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))
of multipliers of
\mathscr{H}(b)
is defined as

\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))=\{\varphi\in\mbox{Hol}(\mathbb{D}):\varphi f\in\mathscr{H}(b),\forall f\in\mathscr{H}(b)\}.

Using the closed graph theorem, it is easy to see that when
\varphi\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))
, then
M_{\varphi}
, the multiplication operator by
\varphi
, is bounded on
\mathscr{H}(b)
. The algebra of multipliers is a Banach algebra when equipped with the norm
\|\varphi\|_{\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b)}=\|M_{\varphi}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathscr{H}(b))}
. Using standard arguments, we see that
\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))\subseteq H^{\infty}\cap\mathscr{H}(b)
. In general, this inclusion is strict. See
[
18
, Example 28.24]
. However, we will encounter below a situation (when
b
is a rational function which is not a finite Blaschke product) where we have the equality
\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))=H^{\infty}\cap\mathscr{H}(b)
.

2.2.
Some properties of the reproducing kernels of
H^{2}
in de Branges-Rovnyak spaces

Recall that we are supposing that
b
is non-extreme. If we denote by
k_{\lambda}(z)=(1-\overline{\lambda}z)^{-1}
the reproducing kernel of
H^{2}
at the point
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, then
k_{\lambda}
belongs to
\mathscr{H}(b)
and

(2.5)

\mbox{Span}(k_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\mathbb{D})=\mathscr{H}(b).

See
[
18
, Corollary 23.26]
or
[
23
, Lemma 7]
. We also know (see
[
18
, Theorem 23.23]
) that
bk_{\lambda}\in\mathscr{H}(b)
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, and that for every
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
we have

(2.6)

\langle f,k_{\lambda}\rangle_{b}=f(\lambda)+\frac{b(\lambda)}{a(\lambda)}f^{+}(\lambda)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\langle f,bk_{\lambda}\rangle_{b}=\frac{f^{+}(\lambda)}{a(\lambda)}\cdot

Using these two equations, we can produce an interesting complete family in
\mathscr{H}(b)
which will be of use to us.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
b
be a non-extreme point of the closed unit ball of
H^{\infty}
, and let

c
be a complex number with
|c|<1
. Then

\mbox{Span}(k_{\mu}-cbk_{\mu}:\mu\in\mathbb{D})=\mathscr{H}(b).

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathscr{H}(b)
, and assume that for every
\mu\in\mathbb{D}
,
h
is orthogonal in
\mathscr{H}(b)
to
k_{\mu}-cbk_{\mu}
. According to (
2.6
), we have

0=h(\mu)+\frac{b(\mu)}{a(\mu)}h^{+}(\mu)-\overline{c}\frac{h^{+}(\mu)}{a(\mu)}\cdot

This can be rewritten as
ah=-bh^{+}+\overline{c}h^{+}
. Multiplying this equality by
\bar{b}
and using the fact that
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, we obtain

a(\bar{b}h-\bar{a}h^{+})=-(1-\overline{c}\bar{b})h^{+}.

Note that
|1-\overline{c}\bar{b}|\geq 1-|c|>0
, and so the last identity can be written as

\frac{\bar{b}h-\bar{a}h^{+}}{1-\overline{c}\bar{b}}=-\frac{h^{+}}{a}\cdot

On the one hand, this equality says that
\frac{h^{+}}{a}
belongs to
L^{2}
and since
a
is outer, we have
\frac{h^{+}}{a}\in H^{2}
. See
[
25
, page 43]
. On the other hand, by definition of
h^{+}
, the function
\bar{b}h-\bar{a}h^{+}
belongs to
\overline{H^{2}_{0}}
and since
(1-\overline{c}\bar{b})^{-1}
is in
\overline{H^{\infty}}
, we also have
\frac{h^{+}}{a}\in\overline{H^{2}_{0}}
. Then
\frac{h^{+}}{a}
belongs to
H^{2}\cap\overline{H^{2}_{0}}=\{0\}
. Finally we get that
h^{+}=0
and thus that
h=0
.
∎

2.3.
Boundary evaluation points on
\mathscr{H}(b)

An important tool in the cyclicity problem will be the boundary evaluation points for
\mathscr{H}(b)
. It is known that the description of these points depends on the inner-outer factorisation of
b
. Recall that any
b
in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
can be decomposed as

(2.7)

b(z)=B(z)S_{\sigma}(z)O(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

where

B(z)=\gamma\,\prod_{n\geq 1}\left(\,\frac{|a_{n}|}{a_{n}}\frac{a_{n}-z}{1-\overline{a}_{n}z}\,\right)\quad\textrm{is a Blaschke product,}

with
|\gamma|=1
,
a_{n}\in\mathbb{D}
for every
n\geq 1
, and
\sum_{n\geq 1}(1-|a_{n}|)<+\infty
,

S_{\sigma}(z)=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\xi+z}{\xi-z}\,d\sigma(\xi)\right)\quad\textrm{is a singular inner function,}

with
\sigma
a positive finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
which is singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and

O(z)=\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\xi+z}{\xi-z}\log|b(\xi)|\,dm(\xi)\right)

is the outer part of
b
.
Now, let
E_{0}(b)
be the set of points
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
satisfying the following condition:

(2.8)

\sum_{n}\frac{1-|a_{n}|^{2}}{|\zeta-a_{n}|^{2}}+\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{d\sigma(\xi)}{|\zeta-\xi|^{2}}+\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\big|\log|b(\xi)|\big|}{|\zeta-\xi|^{2}}\,\,dm(\xi)<\infty.

It is proved in
[
16
]
that for every point
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, every function
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
has a non-tangential limit at
\zeta
if and only if
\zeta\in E_{0}(b)
. This is also equivalent to the property that
b
has an
angular derivative (in the sense of Carathéodory) at
\zeta
, meaning that
b
and
b^{\prime}
both have a non-tangential limit at
\zeta
and
|b(\zeta)|=1
.
Moreover, in this case, the linear map

(2.9)

f\longmapsto f(\zeta):=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\to\zeta\\
\sphericalangle\end{subarray}}f(z)

is bounded on
\mathscr{H}(b)
.
The function
k_{\zeta}^{b}
defined by

k_{\zeta}^{b}(z)=\frac{1-\overline{b(\zeta)}b(z)}{1-\overline{\zeta}z},\quad z\in\mathbb{D},

belongs to
\mathscr{H}(b)
, and

\langle f,k_{\zeta}^{b}\rangle_{b}=f(\zeta)\quad\textrm{ for every }f\in\mathscr{H}(b).

We call the function
k_{\zeta}^{b}

the reproducing kernel of
\mathscr{H}(b)
at the point
\zeta
, and (
2.9
) means that the reproducing kernels
k_{z}^{b}
tend weakly to
k_{\zeta}^{b}
as
z\in\mathbb{D}
tends non-tangentially to
\zeta
. See
[
18
, Theorem 25.1]
. There is also a nice connection between the boundary evaluation points and the point spectrum of
S_{b}^{*}
in the case where
b
is a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
: for
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, we have that

(2.10)

\bar{\zeta}\mbox{ is an eigenvalue for }S_{b}^{*}\mbox{ if and only if }b\mbox{ has an angular derivative at }\zeta.

The boundary evaluation points play a particular role in the description of certain orthogonal basis of reproducing kernels in model spaces
K_{I}
, the
so-called
Clark basis
. Given an inner function
I
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
, recall that by Herglotz theorem, there is a unique finite positive Borel measure
\sigma_{\alpha}
on
\mathbb{T}
, singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure, such that

(2.11)

\frac{1-|I(z)|^{2}}{|\alpha-I(z)|^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|\xi-z|^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\alpha}(\xi),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

The collection
(\sigma_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}}
is the family of
Clark measures
of
I
.

Let
E_{\alpha}=\{\zeta\in E_{0}(I):I(\zeta)=\alpha\}.
By
[
18
, Theorem 21.5]
, the point
\zeta
belongs to
E_{\alpha}
if and only if the measure
\sigma_{\alpha}
has an atom at
\zeta
. In this case,

(2.12)

\sigma_{\alpha}(\{\zeta\})=\dfrac{1}{|I^{\prime}(\zeta)|}=\dfrac{1}{\|k_{\zeta}^{I}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot

See
[
18
, Theorems 21.1 and 21.5]
.
When
\sigma_{\alpha}
is a discrete measure, its support is exactly the set
E_{\alpha}
, which is necessarily countable, and we write it as

(2.13)

E_{\alpha}=\{\zeta_{n}:n\geq 1\}=\{\zeta\in E_{0}(I):I(\zeta)=\alpha\}.

Then, in this case, Clark proved in
[
5
]
that the family
\{k_{\zeta_{n}}^{I}:n\geq 1\}
forms an orthogonal basis of
K_{I}
(and the family
\{{k_{\zeta_{n}}^{I}}/{\|k_{\zeta_{n}}^{I}\|_{2}}:n\geq 1\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
K_{I}
). It is called
the Clark basis of
K_{I}
associated to point
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
.

2.4.
A description of
\mathscr{H}(b)
when
b
is a rational function

Although the contents of the space
\mathscr{H}(b)
may seem mysterious for a general non-extreme
b\in\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
, it turns out that when
b
is a rational function (and not a finite Blaschke product – in which case
b
is an inner function, and thus extreme), the description of
\mathscr{H}(b)
is quite explicit. Since our
b
is a non-extreme point of
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
, it admits a pythagorean mate
a
, which is also a rational function. In fact, the function
a
can be obtained from the Fejér–Riesz theorem (see
[
13
]
). Let
\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n}
denote the
distinct
roots of
a
on
\mathbb{T}
, with corresponding multiplicities
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}
, and define the polynomial
a_{1}
by

(2.14)

a_{1}(z):=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(z-\zeta_{k})^{m_{k}}.

Results from
[
6
,
13
]
show that
\mathscr{H}(b)
has an explicit description as

(2.15)

\mathscr{H}(b)=a_{1}H^{2}\oplus\mathscr{P}_{N-1}=\mathcal{M}(a_{1})\oplus\mathscr{P}_{N-1},

where
N=m_{1}+\dots+m_{n}
, and
\mathscr{P}_{N-1}
denotes the set of polynomials of degree at most
N-1
. Since
a/a_{1}
is invertible in
H^{\infty}=\mathfrak{M}(H^{2})
, note that
\mathcal{M}(a)=\mathcal{M}(a_{1})
. The notation
\oplus
above denotes a topological direct sum in
\mathscr{H}(b)
. But this sum may not be an orthogonal one. See
[
13
]
. In particular,
\mathcal{M}(a_{1})\cap\mathscr{P}_{N-1}=\{0\}
. Moreover, if
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
is decomposed with respect to (
2.15
) as

(2.16)

f=a_{1}\widetilde{f}+p,\quad\mbox{where $\widetilde{f}\in H^{2}$ and $p\in\mathscr{P}_{N-1}$},

an equivalent norm on
\mathscr{H}(b)
(to the natural one induced by the positive definite kernel
k_{\lambda}^{b}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, above) is

(2.17)

\vvvert a_{1}\widetilde{f}+p\vvvert^{2}_{b}:=\|\widetilde{f}\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\|p\|^{2}_{H^{2}}.

Note that the functions
\widetilde{f}\in H^{2}
and
p\in\mathscr{P}_{N-1}
appearing in the decomposition (
2.16
) are unique.
It is important to note that
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert_{b}
is only equivalent to the original norm
\|\cdot\|_{b}
associated to the kernel in (
2.1
), and its scalar product as well as the reproducing kernels and the adjoints of operators defined on
\mathscr{H}(b)
will be different. However, the cyclicity problem for
S_{b}
does not depend on the equivalent norm we consider. So, in the rational case, there is no problem to work with the norm given by (
2.17
).

Note also that when the zeros
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n}
of the polynomial
a_{1}
are simple (i.e. when
m_{k}=1
,
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
), then the space
\mathscr{H}(b)
coincides with a Dirichlet type space
\mathcal{D}(\mu)
, where
\mu
is a finite sum of Dirac masses at the points
\zeta_{k}
,
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. See
[
6
]
. So our results are also connected to the works
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
on the cyclicity problem for Dirichlet spaces.

Using (
2.16
) and the standard estimate that any
g\in H^{2}
satisfies

(2.18)

|g(z)|\leqslant\frac{\|g\|_{2}}{\sqrt{1-|z|^{2}}}\quad\mbox{for all $z\in\mathbb{D}$},

we see that for fixed
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
and for each
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
we have

(2.19)

f(\zeta_{k})=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\to\zeta_{k}\\
\sphericalangle\end{subarray}}f(z)=p(\zeta_{k}),

where
f=a_{1}\widetilde{f}+p
with
\widetilde{f}\in H^{2}
and
p\in\mathscr{P}_{N-1}
. In particular,

(2.20)

E_{0}(b)=\{\zeta_{k}:1\leqslant k\leqslant n\}.

Finally, let us mention that when
b\in\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
is a rational function and not a finite Blaschke product, then
\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))=H^{\infty}\cap\mathscr{H}(b)
. See
[
15
]
.

2.5.
A description of
\mathscr{H}(b)
when
b=(1+I)/2
, with
I
an inner function

There is another situation where we have an explicit description of the space
\mathscr{H}(b)
: this is when
b=\frac{1+I}{2}
and
I
is an inner function with
I\not\equiv 1
. In this case,
b
is a non-extreme point of
\textrm{ball}(H^{\infty})
, and its Pythagorean mate (up to a unimodular constant) is
a=\frac{1-I}{2}
. Moreover,
(a,b)
satisfies (HCR), since

|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
on
\mathbb{D}
. In particular
\mathscr{H}(b)=\mathcal{M}(\bar{a})
, with equivalent norms.

Under the assumption that
I(0)\not=0
, it is proved in
[
14
]
that

(2.21)

\mathscr{H}(b)=\mathcal{M}(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}_{b}K_{I},

where the direct sum
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}_{b}
is orthogonal with respect to the
\mathscr{H}(b)
norm.
In particular, every
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
can be written in a unique way as

(2.22)

f=(1-I)g_{1}+g_{2},\qquad\mbox{with }g_{1}\in H^{2}\mbox{ and }g_{2}\in K_{I}.

It turns out that the same proof holds without any assumption on the value of
I(0)
. For completeness’s sake, we present it in Lemma
2.2
below.
We also give an equivalent norm on
\mathscr{H}(b)
analogue to (
2.17
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
I
be an inner function with
I\not\equiv 1
, and let
b=(1+I)/2
. Then the following assertions hold:

(i)

\mathscr{H}(b)=(1-I)H^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}_{b}K_{I}
, where

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}_{b}
denotes an orthogonal direct sum in
\mathscr{H}(b)
;

(ii)

if for
f=(1-I)g_{1}+g_{2}\in\mathscr{H}(b)
,
g_{1}\in H^{2}
,
g_{2}\in K_{I}
, we define

|||f|||_{b}^{2}=||g_{1}||_{2}^{2}+||g_{2}||_{2}^{2},

then
|||\,.\,|||_{b}
is a norm on
\mathscr{H}(b)
which is equivalent to
||\,.\,||_{b}
.

Proof.

(i) We have
\mathscr{H}(b)=\mathcal{M}(\bar{a})
with equivalent norms, where
a=\frac{1-I}{2}
is the Pythagorean mate of
b
. Also,

\frac{\bar{a}}{a}=\frac{1-\bar{I}}{1-I}=-\bar{I}

a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, and thus
T_{\bar{a}/a}=-T_{\bar{I}}
. Hence
\operatorname{Ker}T_{\bar{a}/a}=\operatorname{Ker}T_{\bar{I}}=K_{I}
. Moreover,
T_{a/\bar{a}}=-T_{I}
has closed range, and thus

(2.23)

\displaystyle H^{2}=\textrm{Ran}(T_{I})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}\operatorname{Ker}(T_{I}^{*})=\textrm{Ran}(T_{I})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}\operatorname{Ker}(T_{\bar{I}})

\displaystyle=T_{a/\bar{a}}H^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}K_{I}

(the sign
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}
denotes here an orthogonal direct sum in
H^{2}
).
Using now the fact that
T_{\bar{a}}
is an isometry from
H^{2}
onto
\mathcal{M}(\bar{a})=T_{\bar{a}}H^{2}
(equipped with the range norm), applying
T_{\bar{a}}
to the equation (
2.23
), and using the identity
T_{\bar{a}}\,T_{a/\bar{a}}=T_{a}
, we obtain

\mathscr{H}(b)=\mathcal{M}(\bar{a})=\mathcal{M}(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}_{\,\bar{a}}T_{\bar{a}}\,K_{I},

where the notation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}_{\,\bar{a}}
represents an orthogonal direct sum with respect to the range norm on
\mathcal{M}(\bar{a})
. Since

T_{\bar{I}}\,K_{I}=\{0\}
and
\bar{a}=(1-\bar{I})/2
, we have
T_{\bar{a}}\,K_{I}=(Id-T_{\bar{I}})\,K_{I}=K_{I}
, and so

(2.24)

\mathscr{H}(b)=\mathcal{M}(\bar{a})=\mathcal{M}(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}_{\,\bar{a}}K_{I}=(1-I)H^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}_{\,\bar{a}}K_{I}.

It now remains to prove that the direct sum in this decomposition of
\mathscr{H}(b)
is in fact orthogonal with respect to the
\mathscr{H}(b)
norm.

Let
f\in H^{2}
and
g\in K_{I}
. Our aim is to show that
\langle{(1-I)f},{g}\rangle_{b}=0
. Note that

T_{\bar{b}}\,g=T_{(1+\bar{I})/2}\,g=\dfrac{1}{2}\,g=T_{\bar{a}}\,g

from which it follows that

(2.25)

g^{+}=g.

Moreover, since
\bar{b}\,a=-\bar{a}\,b
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, we have

T_{\bar{b}}\,\bigl((1-I)f\bigr)=P_{+}\,(2\bar{b}\,af)=-P_{+}(2\bar{a}\,bf)=T_{\bar{a}}\,(-2bf),

whence we get

(2.26)

\bigl((1-I)f\bigr)^{+}=-2bf=-(1+I)f.

By (
2.3
), (
2.25
) and (
2.26
), it follows that

\displaystyle\langle{(1-I)f},{g}\rangle_{b}

\displaystyle=\langle{(1-I)f},{g}\rangle_{2}-\langle{2bf},{g}\rangle_{2}=\langle{(1-I-2b)f},{g}\rangle_{2}=-2\,\langle{If},{g}\rangle_{b}=0

because
g
belongs to
K_{I}
.

(ii) Since
\mathscr{H}(b)=(1-I)H^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\perp}}{{\oplus}}_{\,b}K_{I}
, we have

||(1-I)g_{1}+g_{2}||_{b}^{2}=||(1-I)g_{1}||_{b}^{2}+||g_{2}||_{b}^{2},\qquad g_{1}\in H^{2},\ g_{2}\in K_{I}.

But observe that by (
2.3
) and (
2.26
) we have

||(1-I)g_{1}||_{b}^{2}=||(1-I)g_{1}||_{2}^{2}+||(1+I)g_{1}||_{2}^{2}=4||g_{1}||_{2}^{2},

while we get from (
2.3
) and (
2.25
) that
||g_{2}||_{b}^{2}=2||g_{2}||_{2}^{2}
. Thus

||(1-I)g_{1}+g_{2}||_{b}^{2}=4||g_{1}||_{2}^{2}+2||g_{2}||_{2}^{2},

and from this the norm
|||\,.\,|||_{b}
is easily seen to be equivalent to
||\,.\,||_{b}
.
∎

The next result is an analogue of (
2.19
) for the case where
b=(1+I)/2
with respect to decomposition (
2.22
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
I
be an inner function,
I\not\equiv 1
, and let
b=(1+I)/2
. Let
\zeta\in E_{0}(I)
be such that
I(\zeta)=1
. Then
\zeta\in E_{0}(b)
. Moreover, if
f=(1-I)g_{1}+g_{2}
,
g_{1}\in H^{2}
and
g_{2}\in K_{I}
, then
f(\zeta)=g_{2}(\zeta)
.

Proof.

As mentioned above, since
\zeta\in E_{0}(I)
the function
g_{2}
has a non-tangential limit at the point
\zeta
. Thus it remains to prove that
(1-I)g_{1}
has a zero non-tangential limit at
\zeta
. To this purpose, write for
z\in\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle(1-I(z))g_{1}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\overline{I(\zeta)}I(z)}{1-\overline{\zeta}z}(1-\overline{\zeta}z)g_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{\zeta}^{I}(z)\,\overline{\zeta}\,(\zeta-z)g_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle k_{\zeta}^{I},k_{z}^{I}\rangle_{2}\,\overline{\zeta}\,(\zeta-z)g_{1}(z).

Now, since
\zeta\in E_{0}(I)
,
k_{z}^{I}
tends weakly to
k_{\zeta}^{I}
as
z
tends to
\zeta
non-tangentially. Hence

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\to\zeta\\
\sphericalangle\end{subarray}}\langle k_{\zeta}^{I},k_{z}^{I}\rangle_{2}=\|k_{\zeta}^{I}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty.

Moreover, using the estimate (
2.18
), we obtain that

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\to\zeta\\
\sphericalangle\end{subarray}}(\zeta-z)g_{1}(z)=0,

from which it follows that

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\to\zeta\\
\sphericalangle\end{subarray}}(1-I(z))g_{1}(z)=0.

∎

In the case where
b=(1+I)/2
and
I
is an inner function with
I\not\equiv 1
, there is no complete characterisation of multipliers for
\mathscr{H}(b)
. Nevertheless, we have at our disposal a sufficient condition which will be useful for our study of cyclicity. Before stating this result (Lemma
2.6
) on multipliers, we recall a well-known property of model spaces, of which we provide a proof for completeness’s sake.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
I
be an inner function and let
f\in K_{I}
and
g\in K_{I}\cap H^{\infty}
. Then
fg\in K_{I^{2}}
.

Proof.

Using that
K_{I}=H^{2}\cap I\overline{zH^{2}}
, write
f=I\overline{z\widetilde{f}}
and
g=I\overline{z\widetilde{g}}
, with
\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g}\in H^{2}
. Since
g\in H^{\infty}
, we indeed have
|\widetilde{g}|=|g|\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
, and thus
\widetilde{g}\in H^{\infty}
. Moreover,
fg\in H^{2}
, and

fg=I^{2}\overline{z^{2}\widetilde{f}\widetilde{g}},

whence
fg\in H^{2}\cap I^{2}\overline{zH^{2}}=K_{I^{2}}
.
∎

In the case where
b=(1+I)/2
, the de Branges-Rovnyak space
\mathcal{H}(b)
contains a sequence of model spaces.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
I
be an inner function,
I\not\equiv 1
, and let
b=(1+I)/2
. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

the function
I
is a multiplier of
\mathscr{H}(b)
;

(b)

for every
n\geq 1
,
K_{I^{n}}\subseteq\mathscr{H}(b)
.

Proof.

(a): Let
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. According to (
2.22
), we can decompose
f
as
f=(1-I)g_{1}+g_{2}
with
g_{1}\in H^{2}
and
g_{2}\in K_{I}
. Then

If=(1-I)(Ig_{1})+Ig_{2}=(1-I)(Ig_{1}-g_{2})+g_{2}

and
Ig_{1}-g_{2}\in H^{2}
and
g_{2}\in K_{I}
. Thus, using one more time (
2.22
), it follows that
If\in\mathscr{H}(b)
.

(b): We argue by induction. For
n=1
, the property follows from Lemma
2.2
. Assume that for some
n\geq 1
,
K_{I^{n}}\subseteq\mathscr{H}(b)
. It is known that
K_{I^{n+1}}=K_{I}\oplus IK_{I^{n}}
. See
[
20
, Lemma 5.10]
. The conclusion now follows from the induction assumption and (a).
∎

Here is now our sufficient condition for
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
to be a multiplier of
\mathscr{H}(b)
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
I
be an inner function,
I\not\equiv 1
, and let
b=(1+I)/2
. Assume that
f
decomposes as
f=(1-I)g_{1}+g_{2}
, with
g_{1}\in H^{\infty}
and
g_{2}\in H^{\infty}\cap K_{I}
. Then
f\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))
.

Proof.

We need to show that for every
\varphi\in\mathscr{H}(b)
, we have
\varphi f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. According to (
2.22
), write
\varphi=(1-I)\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}
, with
\varphi_{1}\in H^{2}
and
\varphi_{2}\in K_{I}
. Then

\varphi f=(1-I)\varphi_{1}f+\varphi_{2}f.

Since
f\in H^{\infty}
,
\varphi_{1}f\in H^{2}
, and so the first term
(1-I)\varphi_{1}f
belongs to
(1-I)H^{2}
which is contained in
\mathscr{H}(b)
. Thus it remains to prove that
\varphi_{2}f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. In order to deal with this term, write

\varphi_{2}f=(1-I)\varphi_{2}g_{1}+g_{2}\varphi_{2},

and as before, since
g_{1}\in H^{\infty}
, the term
(1-I)\varphi_{2}g_{1}
belongs to
(1-I)H^{2}
, and so to
\mathscr{H}(b)
. It remains to prove that
g_{2}\varphi_{2}\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. Lemma
2.4
implies that
g_{2}\varphi_{2}\in K_{I^{2}}
, and the conclusion follows now directly from Lemma
2.5
.

∎

3.
Some basic facts on cyclic vectors for the shift operator

Recall that if
T
is a bounded operator on a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
, then a vector
f\in\mathscr{H}
is said to be cyclic for
T
if the linear span of the orbit of
f
under the action of
T
is dense in
\mathscr{H}
, i.e. if

\mbox{Span}(T^{n}f:n\geq 0)=\overline{\{p(T)f:p\in\mathbb{C}[X]\}}=\mathscr{H}.

When
T=S_{b}
is the shift operator on
\mathscr{H}(b)
, we have
p(S_{b})f=pf
for every
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
and every polynomial
p\in\mathbb{C}[X]
. Thus a function
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
is cyclic for
S_{b}
if and only if

\overline{\{pf:p\in\mathbb{C}[X]\}}=\mathscr{H}(b).

In fact, it is sufficient to approximate the constant function
1
by elements of the form
pf
,
p\in\mathbb{C}[X]
, to get that
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
b
a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
and
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(a)

f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

(b)

There exists a sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})_{n}
such that

\|p_{n}f-1\|_{b}\to 0,\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

Follows immediately from the density of polynomials in
\mathscr{H}(b)
and the boundedness of
S_{b}
.
∎

The general meaning of our next result is that the set of zeros of a cyclic vector
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
for
S_{b}
cannot be too large.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
b
a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
and
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. Assume that
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
. Then we have the following properties:

(a)

f
is outer;

(b)

for every
\zeta\in E_{0}(b)
,
f(\zeta)\neq 0
.

Proof.

(a) Since
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
, there exists a sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})_{n}
such that

(3.1)

\|p_{n}f-1\|_{b}\to 0,\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

Now, using the fact that
\mathscr{H}(b)
is contractively contained into
H^{2}
, we get that

\|p_{n}f-1\|_{2}\to 0,\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

That proves that
f
is cyclic for
S
in
H^{2}
, and so
f
is outer by Beurling’s theorem.

(b) Since the functional
f\longmapsto f(\zeta)
is bounded on
\mathscr{H}(b)
for every
\zeta\in E_{0}(b)
, we deduce from (
3.1
) that

|p_{n}(\zeta)f(\zeta)-1|\to 0,\mbox{ as }n\to\infty

for every
\zeta\in E_{0}(b)
.
This property implies directly that
f(\zeta)\neq 0
.
∎

We will encounter in the sequel of the paper some situations where the converse of Lemma
3.2
is also true, i.e. where conditions (a) and (b) of Lemma
3.2
give a necessary and sufficient condition for a function
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
to be cyclic.

We now provide some elementary results concerning cyclic functions for
S_{b}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
b
a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
. Suppose that
f\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))
and that
1/f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. Then
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

Proof.

Using (
2.4
), we see that there exists a sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})_{n}
such that

\|p_{n}-f^{-1}\|_{b}\to 0,\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

Now, since
f\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))
, the multiplication operator by
f
is bounded on
\mathscr{H}(b)
, and thus we get that

\|p_{n}f-1\|_{b}\to 0,\mbox{ as }n\to\infty,

which by Lemma
3.1
implies that
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.
∎

In the following result, the set
\mbox{Hol}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
denotes the space of analytic functions in a neighborhood of the closed unit disc
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
b
a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
. Let
f\in\mbox{Hol}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
and assume that
\inf_{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}|f|>0
. Then
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

Proof.

When
b
is a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
, we have
\mbox{Hol}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subseteq\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))
. See
[
18
, Theorem 24.6]
.
Hence
f\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))
. Moreover, the conditions on
f
also imply that
1/f\in\mbox{Hol}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
. In particular,
1/f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. It remains to apply Lemma
3.3
in order to get that
f
is cyclic.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(a)

the product function
f_{1}f_{2}
is cyclic for
S_{b}
;

(b)

each of the functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

Proof.

(a)\implies(b)
: Assume that
f_{1}f_{2}
is cyclic. By symmetry, it suffices to prove that
f_{1}
is cyclic. Let
\varepsilon>0
. There exists a polynomial
q
such that
\|qf_{1}f_{2}-1\|_{b}\leqslant\epsilon
. Now since the polynomials are dense in
\mathscr{H}(b)
, we can also find a polynomial
p
such that

\|f_{2}q-p\|_{b}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{\|f_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))}}\cdot

Thus

\displaystyle\|pf_{1}-1\|_{b}\leqslant

\displaystyle\|pf_{1}-f_{1}f_{2}q\|_{b}+\|f_{1}f_{2}q-1\|_{b}\leqslant\|f_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))}\|p-f_{2}q\|_{b}+\varepsilon\leqslant 2\varepsilon,

which proves that
f_{1}
is cyclic.

(b)\implies(a)
: Assume that
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are cyclic for
\mathscr{H}(b)
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. There exists a polynomial
p
such that
\|pf_{1}-1\|_{b}\leqslant\epsilon
. On the other hand, there is also a polynomial
q
such that

\|qf_{2}-1\|_{b}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{\|pf_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))}}\cdot

Now we have

\displaystyle\|pqf_{1}f_{2}-1\|_{b}\leqslant

\displaystyle\|pqf_{1}f_{2}-pf_{1}\|_{b}+\|pf_{1}-1\|_{b}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|pf_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))}\|qf_{2}-1\|_{b}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\varepsilon.

Hence the function
f_{1}f_{2}
is cyclic.
∎

Our next result is motivated by the Brown–Shields conjecture and the work
[
10
]
for Dirichlet type spaces
\mathcal{D}(\mu)
. Indeed,
let
\mu
be a positive finite measure on
\mathbb{T}
, and let
\mathcal{D}(\mu)
be the associated Dirichlet space (i.e. the space of holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
whose derivatives are square-integrable when weighted against the Poisson integral of the measure
\mu
). It is shown in
[
6
]
,
[
23
]
that in some cases, Dirichlet spaces and de Branges-Rovnyak spaces are connected. More precisely, let
b\in\textrm{ball}(H^{\infty})
be a rational function (which is not a finite Blaschke product), and let
a
be its pythagorean mate. Let also
\mu
be a positive finite measure on
\mathbb{T}
. Then
\mathcal{D}(\mu)=\mathscr{H}(b)
with equivalent norms if and only if the zeros of
a
on
\mathbb{T}
are all simple, and the support of
\mu
is exactly the set of these zeros
[
6
]
.
In the context of Dirichlet spaces, the authors of
[
10
]

prove the Brown–Shields conjecture when the measure
\mu
has countable support, using two notions of capacity (which they denote
c_{\mu}(F)
and
c_{\mu}^{a}(F)
respectively) and showing that they are comparable:
c_{\mu}(F)\leqslant c_{\mu}^{a}(F)\leqslant 4\,c_{\mu}(F)
for every
F\subseteq\mathbb{T}
(
[
10
, Lemma 3.1]
).
In the same spirit, we introduce the following notions of capacity in
\mathscr{H}(b)
-spaces. For a set
F\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, we define
c_{1}(F)
and
c_{2}(F)
as

c_{1}(F)=\inf\{\|f\|_{b}:f\in\mathscr{H}(b),\,|f|\geq 1\mbox{ a.e. on a neighborhood of }F\},

and

c_{2}(F)=\inf\{\|f\|_{b}:f\in\mathscr{H}(b),\,|f|=1\mbox{ a.e. on a neighborhood of }F\}.

Observe that
c_{1}(F)\leqslant c_{2}(F)
. We do not know if
c_{1}(F)
and
c_{2}(F)
are comparable in general in our context of de Branges-Rovnyak spaces.

Our next result should be compared to
[
10
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
b
a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
.
Consider the following assertions:

(a)

z-\zeta
is not cyclic for
S_{b}
;

(b)

\zeta\in E_{0}(b)
;

(c)

c_{1}(\{\zeta\})>0
;

(d)

c_{2}(\{\zeta\})>0
.

Then
(a)\iff(b)
,
(b)\implies(d)
and
(c)\implies(a)
.

Proof.

(b)\implies(a)
: follows immediately from Lemma
3.2
.

(a)\implies(b)
: our assumption (a) exactly means that

[z-\zeta]:=\mbox{Span}((z-\zeta)z^{n}:n\geq 0)\subsetneq\mathscr{H}(b).

Denote by
\pi
the orthogonal projection from
\mathscr{H}(b)
onto
[z-\zeta]^{\perp}
. First note that
\pi(1)\neq 0
, otherwise we would have
1\in[z-\zeta]
and then the function
z-\zeta
would be cyclic for
S_{b}
, which is a contradiction.

Let us now prove that
[z-\zeta]^{\perp}=\mathbb{C}\pi(1)
. For every
g\in[z-\zeta]^{\perp}
and every
n\geq 0
, we have

0=\langle g,(z-\zeta)z^{n}\rangle_{b}=\langle g,z^{n+1}\rangle_{b}-\overline{\zeta}\langle g,z^{n}\rangle_{b}.

From this, we immediately get that

(3.2)

\langle g,z^{n}\rangle_{b}={\overline{\zeta}}^{n}\langle g,1\rangle_{b},\quad n\geq 0.

This implies that
\langle\pi(1),1\rangle_{b}\neq 0
(otherwise, by (
3.2
) we would have that
\pi(1)
is orthogonal to
z^{n}
for every
n\geq 0
, which implies that
\pi(1)=0
). Secondly, if we define
c:=\frac{\langle g,1\rangle_{b}}{\langle\pi(1),1\rangle_{b}}
, then we have
\langle g-c\pi(1),z^{n}\rangle_{b}=0
for every
n\geq 0
. By the density of polynomials in
\mathscr{H}(b)
, we deduce that
g=c\pi(1)
, which proves that
[z-\zeta]^{\perp}
is of dimension
1
, generated by
\pi(1)
.

Now consider the continuous linear functional
\rho:\mathbb{C}\pi(1)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by
\rho(\alpha\pi(1))=\alpha
for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. Let us check that for every
n\geq 0
,

(3.3)

(\rho\circ\pi)(z^{n})=\zeta^{n}.

For
n=0
, this is true by definition. Assume that (
3.3
) is satisfied for some integer
n\geq 0
. Then,

(\rho\circ\pi)(z^{n+1})=(\rho\circ\pi)(z^{n}(z-\zeta))+\zeta(\rho\circ\pi)(z^{n})=\zeta^{n+1}.

By induction, we deduce (
3.3
) and by linearity, for any polynomial
p
, we have
(\rho\circ\pi)(p)=p(\zeta)
. Now, using the continuity of
\rho
and
\pi
, we obtain that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

|p(\zeta)|\leqslant C\|p\|_{b},\qquad\mbox{for any polynomial }p\in\mathbb{C}[X].

Denote by
L_{\zeta}
the linear functional defined on
\mathbb{C}[X]
by
L_{\zeta}(p)=p(\zeta)
,
p\in\mathbb{C}[X]
. Then
L_{\zeta}
is continuous on

\mathbb{C}[X]
endowed with the topology of

\mathscr{H}(b)
. Hence it extends to a continuous linear map on
\mathscr{H}(b)
. By the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a unique vector
h_{\zeta}\in\mathscr{H}(b)
,
h_{\zeta}\neq 0
, such that

p(\zeta)=L_{\zeta}(p)=\langle p,h_{\zeta}\rangle_{b},\qquad\mbox{for any polynomial }p\in\mathbb{C}[X].

Now, note that for any polynomial
p
, we have

\langle p,S_{b}^{*}h_{\zeta}\rangle_{b}=\langle zp,h_{\zeta}\rangle_{b}=\zeta p(\zeta)=\langle p,\overline{\zeta}h_{\zeta}\rangle_{b},

whence, using (
2.4
),
S_{b}^{*}h_{\zeta}=\overline{\zeta}h_{\zeta}
. In particular,
\overline{\zeta}
belongs to the point spectrum of
S_{b}^{*}
. But by (
2.10
), this implies that
b
has an angular derivative at
\zeta
, which is equivalent to the property that
\zeta\in E_{0}(b)
. Note that the function
h_{\zeta}
is in fact the reproducing kernel
k_{\zeta}^{b}
of
\mathscr{H}(b)
at the point
\zeta
.

(b)\implies(d)
: assume now that
\zeta\in E_{0}(b)
. Let
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
be such that
|f|=1
a.e. on a neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
\zeta
. Let us consider the inner-outer factorisation of
f=f_{i}f_{o}
, where
f_{i}
is the inner part and
f_{o}
the outer part of
f
. Since by definition
|f_{i}|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, we have
|f_{o}|=1
a.e. on
\mathcal{O}
. Moreover,
f_{o}\in\mathscr{H}(b)
and
\|f_{o}\|_{b}\leqslant\|f\|_{b}
. Indeed,
f_{o}=T_{\bar{f}_{i}}f
, where
T_{\bar{f}_{i}}
is the Toeplitz operator with symbol
\bar{f}_{i}
and
\mathscr{H}(b)
is invariant with respect to co-analytic Toeplitz operators. Furthermore,

\|f_{o}\|_{b}=\|T_{\bar{f}_{i}}f\|_{b}\leqslant\|f_{i}\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{b}=\|f\|_{b}.

See
[
18
, Theorem 18.13]
. Since
f_{o}
is outer and
\log|f_{o}|=0
a.e. on
\mathcal{O}
, we have

f_{o}(z)=\lambda\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}\setminus\mathcal{O}}\frac{\xi+z}{\xi-z}\log|f_{o}(\xi)|\,dm(\xi)\right),

for some constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
. Hence
f_{o}
is analytic in a neighborhood of
\zeta
and in particular, we deduce that
|f_{o}(\zeta)|=1
. Using now the fact that
\zeta\in E_{0}(b)
, we know that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
|g(\zeta)|\leqslant C\|g\|_{b}
for every
g\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. Hence

1=|f_{o}(\zeta)|\leqslant C\|f_{o}\|_{b}\leqslant C\|f\|_{b}

for every function
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
such that
|f|=1
a.e. on a neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
\zeta
. We deduce that
c_{2}(\{\zeta\})\geq C^{-1}>0
.

(c)\implies(a)
: by contradiction, assume that
z-\zeta
is cyclic for
S_{b}
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we can find a polynomial
q
such that
\|q(z-\zeta)-1\|_{b}\leqslant\varepsilon
. Note that the value of the polynomial
q(z-\zeta)-1
at
\zeta
is
-1
. So by continuity, we can find a neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
\zeta
on
\mathbb{T}
such that
|q(z-\zeta)-1|\geq 1/2
on
\mathcal{O}
. Hence
|2(q(z-\zeta)-1)|\geq 1
on
\mathcal{O}
and by definition of
c_{1}(\{\zeta\})
, we obtain that

c_{1}(\{\zeta\})\leqslant 2\|q(z-\zeta)-1\|_{b}\leqslant 2\varepsilon.

Since this is true for every
\varepsilon>0
, we deduce that
c_{1}(\{\zeta\})=0
, which contradicts
(c)
.
∎

If we knew that
c_{1}(F)
and
c_{2}(F)

3.6
would be equivalent. This motivates the following question:

.

\kappa>0

for every

?

(ii) Is it true that

if and only if

?

Remark 3.7
.

It can be easily seen from Theorem

in Corollary

is not necessary for

, where

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{d\delta_{1}(\xi)}{|\zeta_{0}-\xi|^{2}}=\frac{1}{|\zeta_{0}-1|^{2}}=\infty

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
b
a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
. Let
p
be a polynomial.
The following assertions are equivalent:

(a)

p
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

(b)

p(z)\neq 0
for every
z\in\mathbb{D}\cup E_{0}(b)
.

Proof.

(a)\implies(b)
: follows immediately from Lemma
3.2
.

(b)\implies(a)
: factorise the polynomial
p
as
p(z)=c\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-\zeta_{j})
, where by definition the roots
\zeta_{j}
belong to
\mathbb{C}\setminus(\mathbb{D}\cup E_{0}(b))
. On the one hand, if
|\zeta_{j}|>1
, then, according to Corollary
3.4
, the function

is cyclic for

. On the other hand, if
|\zeta_{j}|=1
, then
\zeta_{j}\not\in E_{0}(b)
and Theorem
3.6
implies that the function
z-\zeta_{j}
is also cyclic for

. Thus, for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
, the function

is cyclic and it follows from Lemma
3.5
that

itself is cyclic for
S_{b}
. ∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Let

a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
. Let

.
The following assertions are equivalent:

(b)

for every

.

(b)\implies(a)

, the function

4.
The rational case

The main result of this section is a characterisation of cyclic functions for
S_{b}
when
b
is a rational function which is not a finite Blaschke product. As mentioned already in the Introduction, this result can be derived from the work
[
24
]
by Luo – Gu – Richter, but we provide here an elementary proof, the ideas of which will turn out to be also relevant to the case where
b=(1+I)/2
(see Section
5
below). Note that Theorem
4.1
extends a result proved in
[
19
]

in the particular case where
b(z)=(1+z)/2
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
b\in\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
and assume that
b
is rational (but not a finite Blaschke product). Let
a_{1}
be the associated polynomial given by (
2.14
), and let
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(a)

f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

(b)

f
is an outer function and for every
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
,
f(\zeta_{k})\neq 0
.

Proof.

(a)\implies(b)
: according to (
2.20
), we know that
E_{0}(b)=\{\zeta_{k}:1\leqslant k\leqslant n\}
. Hence this implication follows from Lemma
3.2
.

(b)\implies(a)
: according to (
2.16
), write
f=a_{1}\widetilde{f}+p
, where
\widetilde{f}\in H^{2}
and
p\in\mathscr{P}_{N-1}
. By (
2.19
),
p(\zeta_{k})\neq 0
,
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
.
Let now
r\in\mathscr{P}_{N-1}
be the unique polynomial satisfying the following interpolation properties: for every
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
,

r^{(j)}(\zeta_{k})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p(\zeta_{k})}&\mbox{if }j=0\\
-\frac{1}{p(\zeta_{k})}\,{\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}\binom{j}{\ell}r^{(\ell)}(\zeta_{k})p^{(j-\ell)}(\zeta_{k})}&\mbox{if }1\leqslant j\leqslant m_{k}-1.\end{cases}

This polynomial
r
can be constructed using Hermite polynomial interpolation, see for instance
[
1
, Chapter 1, E. 7]
.
By Leibniz’s rule, we easily see that for every
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
and
0\leqslant j\leqslant m_{k}-1
, we have
(rp-1)^{(j)}(\zeta_{k})=0
. Hence
a_{1}
divides the polynomial
rp-1
. In other words, there exists a polynomial
q
such that
rp-1=a_{1}q
. Using that
f
is outer, we can find a sequence of polynomials
(q_{n})_{n}
such that
\|q_{n}f+r\widetilde{f}+q\|_{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Define now a sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})
by
p_{n}=a_{1}q_{n}+r
,
n\geq 1
. Observe that

\displaystyle p_{n}f-1=

\displaystyle(a_{1}q_{n}+r)f-1=a_{1}q_{n}f+r(a_{1}\widetilde{f}+p)-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{1}(q_{n}f+r\widetilde{f})+rp-1=a_{1}(q_{n}f+r\widetilde{f}+q).

Then it follows from (
2.17
) that

\vvvert p_{n}f-1\vvvert_{b}=\vvvert a_{1}(q_{n}f+r\widetilde{f}+q)\vvvert_{b}=\|q_{n}f+r\widetilde{f}+q\|_{2}\to 0\,\textrm{ as }n\to\infty.

Therefore
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.
∎

Example 4.2
.

Let
b(z)=\frac{1}{2}(1-z^{2})
. Then it is proved in
[
13
]
that
a(z)=c(z-i)(z+i)
, for some constant
c
. Thus, according to Theorem
4.1
, a function
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
is cyclic for
S_{b}
if and only if
f
is outer,
f(i)\neq 0
and
f(-i)\neq 0
.

5.
The case where
b=(1+I)/2

Our main result in this section is the following:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
I
be an inner function,
I\not\equiv 1
, and assume that its Clark measure
\sigma_{1}
associated to point
1
(defined in (
2.11
)) is a discrete measure. Let
\{\zeta_{n}:n\geq 1\}=\{\zeta\in E_{0}(I):I(\zeta)=1\}
. Let
b=(1+I)/2
, and
f\in\mathscr{H}(b)
which we decompose according to (
2.22
) as
f=(1-I)g_{1}+g_{2}
, with
g_{1}\in H^{2}
,
g_{2}\in K_{I}
. Assume that:

(a)

g_{1},g_{2}\in H^{\infty}
;

(b)

f
is outer;

(c)

we have

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{|f(\zeta_{n})|^{2}|I^{\prime}(\zeta_{n})|}<\infty.

Then
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds along the same lines as in the rational case.
According to Lemma
2.6
,
f\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b))
and by Lemma
2.3
we have
f(\zeta_{n})=g_{2}(\zeta_{n})
,
n\geq 1
.

First step
: We claim that there exists a sequence of functions
(\psi_{n})_{n}
in
\mathscr{H}(b)
such that
\|\psi_{n}f-1\|_{b}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

In order to construct the sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n}
, let us first consider the function
r
given by

r=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(\zeta_{n})}\frac{k_{\zeta_{n}}^{I}}{\|k_{\zeta_{n}}^{I}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot

Recall that by (
2.12
),
\|k_{\zeta_{n}}^{I}\|_{2}^{2}=|I^{\prime}(\zeta_{n})|
.
Combining this with condition (c) and the fact that the family
(k_{\zeta_{n}}^{I}/\|k_{\zeta_{n}}^{I}\|_{2})_{n}
forms an orthonormal basis of
K_{I}
(since
\sigma_{1}
is discrete, Clark’s theorem holds true), we see that the series defining the function
r
is convergent in
K_{I}
. In others words,
r\in K_{I}
and
r(\zeta_{n})=1/f(\zeta_{n})=1/g_{2}(\zeta_{n})
for every
n\geq 1
.

Let us now prove that
rg_{2}-1\in(1-I)H^{2}
. Observe that Lemma
2.4
implies that
rg_{2}\in K_{I^{2}}=K_{I}\oplus IK_{I}
. Hence there exist
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in K_{I}
such that
rg_{2}-1=\varphi_{1}+I\varphi_{2}-1
. Since
r(\zeta_{n})g_{2}(\zeta_{n})-1=0
, we have
\varphi_{1}(\zeta_{n})+\varphi_{2}(\zeta_{n})-1=0
for every
n\geq 1
.
Note that
(1-\overline{I(0)})^{-1}(1-\overline{I(0)}I)=((1-\overline{I(0)})^{-1}k_{0}^{I}\in K_{I}
and

(1-\overline{I(0)})^{-1}(1-\overline{I(0)}I(\zeta_{n}))=1\qquad\textrm{for every}\ n\geq 1.

Since the family
\bigl(k_{\zeta_{n}}^{I}\bigr)_{n}
is complete in
K_{I}
, and since
\varphi_{1}(\zeta_{n})+\varphi_{2}(\zeta_{n})=1
for every
n\geq 1
, we deduce that

\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}=(1-\overline{I(0)})^{-1}(1-\overline{I(0)}I).

Hence

\displaystyle rg_{2}-1

\displaystyle=\varphi_{1}+I\varphi_{2}-1=-\varphi_{2}+(1-\overline{I(0)})^{-1}(1-\overline{I(0)}I)+I\varphi_{2}-1

\displaystyle=(1-I)(-\varphi_{2})+(1-\overline{I(0)})^{-1}(1-\overline{I(0)}I)-1.

Observe that

(1-\overline{I(0)})^{-1}(1-\overline{I(0)}I)-1=(1-\overline{I(0)})^{-1}\overline{I(0)}(1-I),

from which it follows that

rg_{2}-1=(1-I)(-\varphi_{2}+\overline{I(0)}(1-\overline{I(0)})^{-1}).

This proves that
rg_{2}-1
belongs to
(1-I)H^{2}
. Write
rg_{2}-1
as

rg_{2}-1=(1-I)g_{3}
, with
g_{3}\in H^{2}
.

Using now that
f
is outer, and that
rg_{1}\in H^{2}
(as
g_{1}\in H^{\infty}
) we can find a sequence of polynomials
(q_{n})_{n}
such that
\|q_{n}f+rg_{1}+g_{3}\|_{2}\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
. We then define for each
n\geq 1
a function
\psi_{n}
as

\psi_{n}:=(1-I)q_{n}+r,

where
q_{n}
and
r
are defined above. Note that
\psi_{n}\in(1-I)H^{2}+K_{I}=\mathscr{H}(b)
and

\displaystyle\psi_{n}f-1=

\displaystyle(1-I)q_{n}f+rf-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-I)q_{n}f+(1-I)rg_{1}+rg_{2}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-I)(q_{n}f+rg_{1}+g_{3}).

It follows from Lemma
2.2
that there exists a positive constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\|\psi_{n}f-1\|_{b}=

\displaystyle\|(1-I)(q_{n}f+rg_{1}+g_{3})\|_{b}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\,\vvvert(1-I)(q_{n}f+rg_{1}+g_{3})\vvvert_{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\,\|q_{n}f+rg_{1}+g_{3}\|_{2},

from which it follows that
\|\psi_{n}f-1\|_{b}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Second step
: Let us now prove that there exists a sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})_{n}
such that
\|p_{{}_{n}}f-1\|_{b}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

By the density of polynomials in
\mathscr{H}(b)
, we can find a sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})_{n}
such that
\|p_{n}-\psi_{n}\|_{b}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Now write

\displaystyle\|p_{n}f-1\|_{b}\leqslant

\displaystyle\|p_{n}f-\psi_{n}f\|_{b}+\|\psi_{n}f-1\|_{b}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathfrak{M}(\mathscr{H}(b)}\|p_{n}-\psi_{n}\|_{b}+\|\psi_{n}f-1\|_{b},

and by the choice of the sequence
(p_{n})_{n}
and the first step, we get the conclusion of the second step.

We finally conclude that
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

If
I

, its Clark measure

5.1
replacing

Remark 5.4
.

There exists a recipe to construct an inner function satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem
5.1
. Let
\sigma
be a positive discrete measure on
\mathbb{T}
and let
H\sigma
be its Herglotz transform,

H\sigma(z)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\xi+z}{\xi-z}\,d\sigma(\xi),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

We easily see that
H\sigma
defines an analytic function on
\mathbb{D}
and satisfies
\Re e(H\sigma(z))\geq 0
for every
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Now define a function
I
on
\mathbb{D}
as

I=\frac{H\sigma-1}{H\sigma+1}\cdot

Since
\Re eH\sigma\geq 0
, it is easy to check that
I\in H^{\infty}
and
|I|\leqslant 1
. Moreover, for every
0<r<1
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, we have

(5.1)

|I(r\zeta)|=\frac{(\Re e(H\sigma(r\zeta))-1)^{2}+(\Im m(H\sigma(r\zeta))^{2}}{(\Re e(H\sigma(r\zeta))+1)^{2}+(\Im{m}(H\sigma(r\zeta))^{2}}\cdot

Since
\sigma
is a singular measure, it is well-known that for almost all
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, we have

\Re e(H\sigma(r\zeta))=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1-r^{2}}{|\xi-r\zeta|^{2}}\,d\sigma(\xi)\to 0\quad\mbox{as }r\to 1^{-}.

See
[
17
, Corollary 3.4]
. Moreover, the radial limit of
\Im{m}(H\sigma)
also exists and is finite for almost all
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
. See
[
17
, page 113]
. Thus, it follows from (
5.1
) that
|I(\zeta)|=1
for almost all
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, meaning that
I
is an inner function. Of course, we have
I\not\equiv 1
. Now, we easily check that

\frac{1-|I(z)|^{2}}{|1-I(z)|^{2}}=\Re e(H\sigma(z))=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|\xi-z|^{2}}\,d\sigma(\xi),

which implies by unicity of the Clark measure that
\sigma_{1}=\sigma
. Therefore
I
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
I
be an inner function,
I\not\equiv 1
, and assume that
\sigma_{1}
is a discrete measure. Let
b=(1+I)/2
.
Then
k_{\lambda}^{b}
is a cyclic vector for
S_{b}
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

Let us prove that
k_{\lambda}^{b}
satisfies the assumptions
(a),(b)
and
(c)
of Theorem
5.1
. First, using that
b=(1+I)/2
, straightforward computations show that

\frac{1-\overline{b(\lambda)}b(z)}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}=(1-I(z))\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{(1-\overline{I(\lambda)})}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1-\overline{I(\lambda)}I(z)}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}\cdot

In other words,
k_{\lambda}^{b}
can be written as
k_{\lambda}^{b}=(1-I)g_{1}+g_{2}
, with
g_{1}=\frac{1}{4}(1-\overline{I(\lambda)})k_{\lambda}
and
g_{2}=\frac{1}{2}k_{\lambda}^{I}
. In particular,
g_{1},g_{2}\in H^{\infty}
and
k_{\lambda}^{b}
satisfies the assumption
(a)
.

Observe now that
\Re e(1-\overline{b(\lambda)}b(z))\geq 0
and
\Re e(1-\overline{\lambda}z)\geq 0
for every
z\in\mathbb{D}
, which implies that the functions
1-\overline{b(\lambda)}b(z)
and
1-\overline{\lambda}z
are outer. See
[
25
, page 67]
. So
k_{\lambda}^{b}
is outer as the quotient of two outer functions. It remains to check that
k_{\lambda}^{b}
satisfies assumption
(c)
. But

|k_{\lambda}^{b}(\zeta_{n})|=\left|\frac{1-b(\lambda)}{1-\overline{\lambda}\zeta_{n}}\right|\geq\frac{|1-b(\lambda)|}{1+|\lambda|},

and the property
(c)
follows from the fact that

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{|I^{\prime}(\zeta_{n})|}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sigma_{1}(\{\zeta_{n}\})\leqslant\sigma_{1}(\mathbb{T})<+\infty.

Thus
k_{\lambda}^{b}
satisfies the assumptions
(a),(b),(c)
of Theorem
5.1
, and
k_{\lambda}^{b}
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

∎

It should be noted that in Corollary
5.5
, the reproducing kernels
f=k_{\lambda}^{b}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, which are cyclic for
S_{b}
, are such that
1/f\in H^{\infty}
.
As we already observed in Lemma
3.3
,
certain invertibility conditions for
f
make cyclicity easier. Using Theorem
5.1
, we now construct a family of functions
f
which are cyclic for
S_{b}
but are such that
1/f\notin H^{2}
.

Example 5.6
.

Let
I
be a non-constant inner function, and assume that
\sigma_{1}
is a discrete measure. Let
b=(1+I)/2
and
f=(1+I)k_{\lambda}^{I}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. Then
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
and
1/f\notin L^{2}
.

Proof.

First observe that

f=(1-I)(-k_{\lambda}^{I})+2k_{\lambda}^{I},

so that
f=(I-I)g_{1}+g_{2}
, with
g_{2}=-2g_{1}=2k_{\lambda}^{I}\in H^{\infty}\cap K_{I}
. In particular,
f
satisfies condition
(a)
of Theorem
5.1
. Moreover, the function
f
is outer as the product of two outer functions (use the same arguments as in the proof of Corollary
5.5
). Finally, since
|f(\zeta_{n})|=2|k_{\lambda}^{I}(\zeta_{n})|\geq|1-I(\lambda)|
,
f
satisfies condition
(c)
. Hence by Theorem
5.1
,
f
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

Let us now check that
1/f\not\in L^{2}
. First observe that there exist two positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that

C_{1}\frac{1}{|1+I(\zeta)|}\leqslant\frac{1}{|f(\zeta)|}\leqslant C_{2}\frac{1}{|1+I(\zeta)|}\quad\mbox{ for a.e. }\zeta\in\mathbb{T},

because

\frac{1-|I(\lambda)|}{2}\leqslant|k_{\lambda}^{I}(\zeta)|\leqslant\frac{2}{1-|\lambda|}\cdot

Now assume that
1/f
belongs to
L^{2}
. Then
1/(1+I)\in L^{2}
. But since
1+I
is outer, we get that
1/(1+I)\in H^{2}
. See
[
25
, page 43]
. As

\frac{1}{1+I}=\overline{\frac{I}{1+I}}\quad\mbox{ for a.e. }\zeta\in\mathbb{T},

we deduce that
1/(1+I)
also belongs to
\overline{H^{2}}
. Thus
1/(1+I)
is constant, which is a contradiction.
∎

In the context of Corollary
5.5
, it is easy to see that
(a,b)
forms a corona pair, and we have seen that
k_{\lambda}^{b}
is cyclic for
S_{b}
. In fact, this cyclicity result holds true under the (HCR) condition only.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
b
be a non-extreme point in
\operatorname{ball}(H^{\infty})
, and let
a
be its pythagorean mate. Assume that
(a,b)
satisfies (HCR). Then the following. assertions hold:

(a)

k_{\lambda}^{b}
is cyclic for
S_{b}
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
;

(b)

If
b
is furthermore assumed to be outer, then
bk_{\lambda}
is also cyclic for
S_{b}
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. In particular,
b
is a cyclic vector for
S_{b}
.

Proof.

(a) We have
p(S_{b})k_{\lambda}^{b}=(1-\overline{b(\lambda)}b)p(S_{b})k_{\lambda}
for every polynomial
p\in\mathbb{C}[X]
. Since
(a,b)
satisfies (HCR), we have
\mathscr{H}(b)=\mathscr{M}(\bar{a})
, and
b
is a multiplier of
\mathscr{H}(b)
. See
[
18
, Theorems 28.7 and 28.3]
. In particular, the multiplication operator
T=M_{1-\overline{b(\lambda)}b}
is bounded on
\mathscr{H}(b)
and we have

p(S_{b})k_{\lambda}^{b}=Tp(S_{b})k_{\lambda}\qquad\textrm{for every polynomial }p\in\mathbb{C}[X].

Since
k_{\lambda}
is cyclic for
S_{b}
(see Example
3.10
), in order to check that
k_{\lambda}^{b}
is also cyclic for
S_{b}
it is sufficient to check that
T
has dense range. Let
h\in\mathscr{H}(b)
be such that
h\perp\mbox{Range}(T)
. Then
h\perp Tk_{\mu}=k_{\mu}-\overline{b(\lambda)}bk_{\mu}
for every
\mu\in\mathbb{D}
. Lemma
2.1
now implies that
h=0
, proving that
T
has dense range. It follows that
k_{\lambda}^{b}
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.

(b) The proof of (b) proceeds along the same lines of (a). We have

p(S_{b})bk_{\lambda}=Vp(S_{b})k_{\lambda}\qquad\textrm{for every polynomial }p\in\mathbb{C}[X],

where
V=M_{b}
is the multiplication operator by
b
. As previously, in order to show that
bk_{\lambda}
is cyclic for
S_{b}
, it is sufficient to check that
V
has a dense range. Let
h\in\mathscr{H}(b)
be such that
h\perp\mbox{Range}(V)
. Then
h\perp Vk_{\mu}=bk_{\mu}
for every
\mu\in\mathbb{D}
. By (
2.6
), it then follows that
h^{+}(\mu)=0
for every
\mu\in\mathbb{D}
. Then
h^{+}=0
and
T_{\bar{b}}h=T_{\bar{a}}h^{+}=0
. But, since
b
is outer,
T_{\bar{b}}
is one-to-one, which implies that
h=0
. It then follows that
bk_{\lambda}
is cyclic for
S_{b}
.
∎
