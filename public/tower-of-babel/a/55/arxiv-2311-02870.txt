Optimal symplectic positions for the mean width
Jonghyeon Ahn, Ely Kerman

Abstract
. In this work we study convex bodies in ℝ 2 ​ n {\mathbb{R}}^{2n} with the property that their mean width can not be decreased by the action of natural classes of symplectomorphisms. We also study the relationship between their mean widths and various symplectic invariants. The common theme of our results is that toric symmetry is a preferred feature of convex bodies that are in optimal symplectic position with respect to the mean width. Among other things, we prove that the mean width of a toric subset can not be decreased by any linear symplectic map. We also show that toric convex bodies are critical points of the mean width under all symplectic deformations.



1.
Introduction

Symplectomorphisms of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
preserve the volume of subsets. They do not preserve other classical measurements of subsets such as the surface area or the mean width. A natural problem then is to determine when, and by how much, a classical measurement of a subset can be decreased by the action of symplectomorphisms in some fixed class. In this work we consider this problem for the mean width of convex bodies. Our primary motivation for this choice is the relationship between the mean width and the symplectic capacity of convex bodies that was established by Artstein-Avidan and Ostrover in
[
2
]
.

The first task in this direction is to identify those convex bodies which are already in optimal position in the sense that their mean width can not be decreased by symplectomorphisms in the given class. As described below, we will consider three versions of this problem corresponding to three classes of symplectomorphisms. The set of convex bodies in optimal position can be viewed as a distinguishing feature of a class of symplectomorphisms and differences with the corresponding sets for volume preserving diffeomorphisms can be viewed as aspects of symplectic rigidity.

Recall that the mean width of a convex body
K
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is defined as

\displaystyle{{\mathrm{M}}}(K)=\int_{S^{d-1}}(h_{K}(u)+h_{K}(-u))\,d\sigma,

where

\displaystyle h_{K}(u)=\sup_{k\in K}\,\langle k,u\rangle

is the support function of
K
, and
\sigma
is the rotationally invariant probability measure on
S^{d-1}
. The quantity
h_{K}(u)+h_{K}(-u)
being averaged is the caliper width of
K
in the
u
direction. This definition extends to any bounded subset
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and, denoting the convex hull of
X
by
{\mathrm{Conv}}(X)
, we have
{{\mathrm{M}}}({\mathrm{Conv}}(X))={{\mathrm{M}}}(X)
.

The first class of symplectomomorphisms we consider are the linear maps represented by the group of symplectic matrices,
\mathrm{Sp}(2n)
. We consider the measurement

{\mathrm{M}}_{\mathrm{Sp}}(K)\;{:=}\;\inf_{P\in\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}{{\mathrm{M}}}(P(K))

Problem 1.1
.

Characterize the convex bodies
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
that satisfy

(1)

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PK)={{\mathrm{M}}}(K).

Let

and the problem:

Problem 1.2

Characterize the convex bodies

\displaystyle\inf_{\phi\in\operatorname{Symp}}\left\{{{\mathrm{M}}}(\phi(K)\mid\phi(K)\text{ is convex}\right\}={{\mathrm{M}}}(K).

1.1.
Motivation from symplectic topology

Minimizing the mean width of the symplectic images of a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
is similar in spirit to a symplectic embedding problem. Indeed, there are several relationships between the measurements defined above and well studied symplectic invariants. The primary motivation for this work is to better understand the details of these relationships.

Problems

(8)

\displaystyle{\overline{{\mathrm{Vol}}}}(K)\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\inf_{\phi\in\operatorname{Symp}}\left\{{{\mathrm{M}}}(\phi(K))\mid\phi(K)\text{ is convex}\right\}\right)^{2}.

In dimension two, refinements (

8

If one views the right-hand term as a means to approximate the left-hand term, it is easy to see, say for

\displaystyle\mathrm{Area}(K)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\inf_{\phi\in\operatorname{Symp}}\left\{\mathrm{Length}(\partial(\phi(K)))\mid\phi(K)\text{ is convex}\right\}\right)^{2}

since one can easily find a smooth area preserving map such that the image of

\displaystyle E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\ \rule[-9.95845pt]{0.28453pt}{25.60747pt}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{a^{2}_{i}}+\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{b^{2}_{i}}\right)\leq 1\right\}.

. A straight forward computation, in polar coordinates

, yields

(16)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\right|_{t=0}{{\mathrm{M}}}(\phi_{H}^{t}(B^{2}))=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\left(\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial\theta^{2}}(\theta,1)\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta}(\theta,1)\right)^{2}\right)\,d\theta.

Wirtinger’s inequality, applied to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta}(\theta,1)=k_{1}\sin(\theta-k_{2})

for constants

and

. Given this, one can easily find a function
G
, say,

, whose Hamiltonian vector field is not tangent to
\partial B^{2}
and whose Hamiltonian flow

, satisfies

\left.\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\right|_{t=0}{{\mathrm{M}}}(\phi_{G}^{t}(B^{2}))=0.

It does not seem possible to recover the fact that
B^{2}
has the smallest mean width (boundary length) among all its nearby symplectic positions by arguing along these lines.

Application: There is no symplectic reverse Urysohn inequality

Theorem

yields a negative answer to Question

Corollary 1.24
.

For
n>1
there is no constant
\gamma_{n}
such that

\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PK)\leq\gamma_{n}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(K)}{\mathrm{Vol}(B^{2n})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2n}}

holds for all convex bodies
K
.

Proof.

To the
n
-tuple of positive numbers
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
we associate the symplectic polydisk

\mathbf{P}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=a_{1}B^{2}\times a_{2}B^{2}\times\cdots\times a_{n}B^{2}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}={\mathbb{R}}^{2n}.

Since each such polydisk is toric, Theorem
1.18
implies that

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(P\,\mathbf{P}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))

\displaystyle={{\mathrm{M}}}(\mathbf{P}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})).

Using the product formula for the mean width from
[
14
]
, we then have

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(P\,\mathbf{P}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))=\frac{\pi{{\mathrm{Vol}}}(B^{2n-1})}{{{\mathrm{Vol}}}(B^{2n})}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{{\mathrm{Vol}}}(\mathbf{P}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))}{{{\mathrm{Vol}}}(B^{2n})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2n}}=(n!)^{\frac{1}{2n}}(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})^{\frac{1}{n}}.

The relation between the arithmetic mean and the geometric mean of the
a_{i}
yields the desired conclusion. For example, if
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1},a_{n})=(1,\dots,1,r)

{\mathrm{M}}_{\mathrm{Sp}}(\mathbf{P}(1,\dots,1,r))
is proportional to

whereas
\left(\frac{{{\mathrm{Vol}}}(\mathbf{P}(1,\dots,1,r))}{{{\mathrm{Vol}}}(B^{2n})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2n}}
is proportional to
r^{\frac{1}{n}}.

∎

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.16

We need to show that

\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PE(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}))={{\mathrm{M}}}(E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}))

if and only if
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}
. To each ellipsoid
E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
we associate the function
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
on
\mathrm{Sp}(2n)
defined by

\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}(P)=\frac{1}{2}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PE(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})).

It suffices to show that the identity matrix
{\mathbb{I}}
is a global minimum of
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
if and only if
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}
. The following two results will imply this.

Proposition 2.1
.

The identity matrix is a local minimum of
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
if and only if
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For each
\mathbf{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
, the identity matrix is a global minimum of
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})}
.

2.1.
Proof of Proposition
2.1

We begin by analyzing the formula for
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
.
For any matrix
T\in\mathrm{GL}(d),
the support function of the ellipsoid
TB^{d}
is given by

h_{TB^{d}}(u)=\|T^{t}u\|.

Since
E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})B^{2n}
, for
\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\mathrm{diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}),
the formula for
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}(P)
can be simplified to

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}(P)=\int_{S^{2n-1}}\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})P^{t}u\|d\sigma.

The domain of
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
can also be restricted to
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
, the set of symmetric positive definite symplectic matrices. To see this, recall that
every symplectic matrix
P
has a unique polar decomposition,
P=QS
,
where
Q
belongs to
\mathrm{U}(n)
and
S
belongs to
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
. The value of
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}(P)
does not depend on the unitary factor
Q
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}(P)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{2n-1}}\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})S^{t}Q^{t}(u)\|d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{Q(S^{2n-1})}\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})S^{t}(u)\||\det(Q)|d\sigma

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}(S).

Note also that
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
is a smooth manifold since it is diffeomorphic to the vector space of symmetric matrices in
\mathfrak{sp}(2n)
via the exponential map.

Our conventions for integrating over the sphere are as follows. Define the Hopf coordinates
(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{n},r_{1},\cdots,r_{n})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
by

\displaystyle x_{i}=r_{i}\cos{\theta_{i}}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,y_{i}=r_{i}\sin{\theta_{i}}\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\,i=1,\cdots,n.

We also consider the coordinate functions
(\theta,r,\rho)=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{n},r_{1},\cdots,r_{n-1},\rho)
where

\displaystyle\rho=\sqrt{r_{1}^{2}+\cdots+r_{n}^{2}}.

The unit sphere
S^{2n-1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
corresponds to
\rho=1
and

(\theta,r)=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{n},r_{1},\cdots,r_{n-1})

are coordinate functions on
S^{2n-1}
where the points
r=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n-1})
belong to the
region

\displaystyle{R=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}r_{j}^{2}\leq 1\right\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}_{\geq 0}}.

Integration over
S^{2n-1}
, in these coordinates, then becomes

\displaystyle\int_{S^{2n-1}}F(u)\,d\sigma=\frac{(n-1)!}{2\pi^{n}}\int_{R\times T^{n}}F(u(\theta,r))r_{1}\cdots r_{n-1}dr_{1}\cdots dr_{n-1}d\theta_{1}\cdots d\theta_{n}

where
T^{n}
is the standard
n
-torus,
({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{R}})^{n}
.

Proceeding with the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we may assume that
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
is defined on
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
, and it suffices to show that
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}
if and only if
{\mathbb{I}}
is a local minimum of
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{b}
, then
{\mathbb{I}}
is not a local minimum of
\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
.

Proof.

Relabelling if necessary, we may assume that
a_{1}\neq b_{1}
. For the case
a_{1}<b_{1}
, set

S(s)={\mathbb{I}}+(e^{s}-1){\mathbb{I}}_{1,1}+(e^{-s}-1){\mathbb{I}}_{n+1,n+1}

where
{\mathbb{I}}_{i,j}
is the matrix whose
(i,j)-
th entry is one and whose other entries are zero. Note that
S(s)
is a smooth path
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
with
S(0)={\mathbb{I}}
. We then have

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}(S(s))

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})S^{\prime}(0)u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})u\|}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{a_{1}^{2}x_{1}^{2}-b_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{2}}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})u\|}\,d\sigma,

which we claim is negative. In Hopf coordinates,

\frac{a_{1}^{2}x_{1}^{2}-b_{1}^{2}y_{1}^{2}}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})u\|}=\frac{r_{1}^{2}(a_{1}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{1}-b_{1}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{1})}{\left(r_{1}^{2}(a_{1}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{1}+b_{1}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{1})+\sum_{j=2}^{n}r_{j}^{2}(a_{j}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}+b_{j}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{j})\right)^{1/2}}.

Define
c>0
by
(1+c)a_{1}^{2}=b_{1}^{2}
, and set
A_{1}=r_{1}^{2}a_{1}^{2}
and
B_{1}=\sum_{j=2}^{n}r_{j}^{2}(a_{j}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}+b_{j}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{j})
. Considering the integral over
\theta_{1}
first, it suffices to show that the function

\displaystyle I(c)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\text{\large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.22911pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{A_{1}(\cos^{2}\theta_{1}-(1+c)\sin^{2}\theta_{1})}{\left(A_{1}(\cos^{2}\theta_{1}+(1+c)\sin^{2}\theta_{1})+B_{1}\right)^{1/2}}\,\,d\theta_{1}

is negative for all
c>0
. Clearly,
I(0)=0
and a simple computation yields

\displaystyle I^{\prime}(c)

\displaystyle=-\mathop{\text{\large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.22911pt0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{A_{1}\sin^{2}\theta_{1}\left[\frac{3A_{1}}{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{1}+(1+c)\frac{A_{1}}{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{1}+B_{1}\right]}{\left(A_{1}(\cos^{2}\theta_{1}+(1+c)\sin^{2}\theta_{1})+B_{1}\right)^{3/2}}\,\,d\theta_{1}.

This is clearly negative for all
c>0
, and so the proof is complete. For the case
a_{1}>b_{1}
, one can choose
S(s)={\mathbb{I}}+(e^{-s}-1){\mathbb{I}}_{1,1}+(e^{s}-1){\mathbb{I}}_{n+1,n+1}
and argue similarly.
∎

To complete the proof of Proposition
2.1
, it remains to prove the following.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any smooth curve
S\colon[-1,1]\to Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
with
S(0)={\mathbb{I}}
, we have

(21)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})}(S(s))=0.

If, in addition,
S^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, then

(22)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})}(S(s))>0.

Proof.

The derivative
S^{\prime}(0)
has the form

\displaystyle S^{\prime}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}C&D\\
D&-C\end{pmatrix}

for symmetric
n\times n
matrices
C=(c_{ij})
and
D=(d_{ij})
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})}(S(s))=\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S^{\prime}(0)u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|}\,d\sigma

where

\displaystyle\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S^{\prime}(0)u\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{ii}a_{i}^{2}(x_{i}^{2}-y_{i}^{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}c_{ij}a_{i}^{2}(x_{i}x_{j}-y_{i}y_{j})+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}d_{ij}a_{i}^{2}(x_{i}y_{j}+x_{j}y_{i}).

To prove (
21
), it suffices to show that for integers
i
and
j
between
1
and
n
we have:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{x_{i}^{2}-y_{i}^{2}}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|}\,d\sigma=0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{x_{i}x_{j}-y_{i}y_{j}}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|}\,d\sigma=0\quad\text{ for }i\neq j,

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{x_{i}y_{j}}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|}\,d\sigma=0.

Since
\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|
is independent of the (Hopf) variables
\theta_{i}
, these integrals can all be put in the form

\left(\int_{R}F(r)\,\,r_{1}\cdots r_{n-1}\,dr_{1}\cdots dr_{n-1}\right)\left(\int_{T^{n}}\Theta(\theta)\,\,d\theta_{1}\cdots d\theta_{n}\right).

In each case, it is then simple to check that the corresponding factor
\int_{T^{n}}\Theta(\theta)\,\,d\theta_{1}\cdots d\theta_{n}
vanishes.

Moving onto the verification of (
22
), we note that for any
s
in the interior of the domain of
S(s)
, the second derivative,
\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\,\,\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})}(S(s)),
is equal to

\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S^{\prime}(s)u\|^{2}+\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S^{\prime\prime}(s)u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u\|}-\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S^{\prime}(s)u\rangle^{2}}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u\|^{3}}\,d\sigma.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the second term and simplifying we get

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\,\,\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})E}(S(s))\geq\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S^{\prime\prime}(s)u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u\|}\,d\sigma.

Now,
S^{\prime}(s)=S(s)X(s)
for a family of symmetric matrices
X(s)\in\mathfrak{sp}(2n)
of the form

\displaystyle X(s)=\begin{pmatrix}C(s)&D(s)\\
D(s)&-C(s)\end{pmatrix}

such that
X(0)\neq 0
.
Hence

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}(s)=S(s)(X(s)^{2}+X^{\prime}(s)),

and

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\right|_{s=0}\,\,\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})}(S(s))

\displaystyle\geq\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})X(0)^{2}u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|}\,d\sigma+\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})X^{\prime}(0)u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|}\,d\sigma.

Arguing as above, with
X^{\prime}(0)
playing the role of
S^{\prime}(0)
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})X^{\prime}(0)u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|}\,d\sigma=0

since all the integrals which result from the expansion of
\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})X^{\prime}(0)u\rangle
vanish as before.
Note that

\displaystyle X(0)^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}L&M\\
-M&L\end{pmatrix},

with
L=C(0)^{2}+D(0)^{2}
and
M=C(0)D(0)-D(0)C(0)
. This implies that

\displaystyle\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})X(0)^{2}u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|}\,d\sigma=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}l_{ii}\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2}}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})u\|}d\sigma

where

. (The other integrals in the expansion of

C(0)=(c_{ij})
and

are both symmetric and
X(0)\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle l_{ii}=\sum_{k}(c_{ik}c_{ki}+d_{ik}d_{ki})=\sum_{k}(c_{ik}^{2}+d_{ik}^{2})>0

and hence

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\right|_{s=0}\,\,\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})}(S(s))>0.

∎

This completes the proof of Proposition

.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
D
be an

be an
m\times m
positive definite matrix. For any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\,\,\langle Du,DXu\rangle\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
D=\text{diag}\,(a_{1},\cdots,a_{m})
and
X=(x_{ij})
.
Then

\displaystyle\langle Du,DXu\rangle=\sum_{i}a_{i}u_{i}\left(\sum_{j}x_{ij}a_{j}u_{j}\right)=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}u_{i}x_{ij}a_{j}u_{j}\geq 0.

The last inequality follows from the positive definiteness of
X
.
∎

Assume that
S_{1}
in
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
is another local minimum of
\mathcal{M}_{E}
. The Euler decomposition of
S_{1}
has the form

\displaystyle S_{1}=Q\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&0\\
0&\Lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}Q^{t}

where
Q
is in
U(n)
and
\Lambda=\text{diag}(\delta_{1},\cdots,\delta_{n})
with
\delta_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
. For
s\in[0,1]
, consider the smooth path of symplectic matrices

\displaystyle S(s)=Q\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda(s)&0\\
0&\Lambda(s)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}Q^{t}.

from
{\mathbb{I}}
to
S_{1}
where
\Lambda(s)=\text{diag}(\delta_{1}^{s},\cdots,\delta_{n}^{s})
. Note that
S^{\prime\prime}(s)=S(s)Y=YS(s)
for the positive semi-definite matrix
Y=QL^{2}Q^{t}
where

\displaystyle L^{2}=\text{diag}((\ln{\delta_{1}})^{2},\cdots,(\ln{\delta_{n}})^{2},(\ln{\delta_{1}})^{2},\cdots,(\ln{\delta_{n}})^{2}).

Let
f\colon[0,1]\to\mathrm{Sp}(2n)
be the smooth function given by
f(s)=\mathcal{M}_{E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})}(S(s))
. Arguing as above, we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(s)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S^{\prime\prime}(s)u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u\|}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{2n-1}}\frac{\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})YS(s)u\rangle}{\|\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})S(s)u\|}\,d\sigma.

The quadratic form
\langle\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})\cdot,\Delta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a})Y\cdot\rangle
is positive semi-definite by Lemma
2.5
. Hence,
f^{\prime\prime}(s)\geq 0
for all
s\in(0,1)
. In fact, since
f
goes between the local minimizers
{\mathbb{I}}
and
S_{1}
, we have
f^{\prime}(0^{+})=f^{\prime}(1^{-})=0
and hence
f^{\prime\prime}(s)=0
for all
s\in(0,1)
. From this it follows that
Y
must be the zero matrix. Hence,
\delta_{i}=1
for all
i=1,\dots n
and so
S_{1}={\mathbb{I}}
, as required.
This concludes the proof of Proposition
2.2
and hence Theorem
1.16
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.18

3.1.
Building blocks: Symplectic polydisks and polyannuli

Any toric subset
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
is completely determined by its image under the moment map,
\mu\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
, given by

\mu(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=(\pi|z_{1}|^{2},\dots,\pi|z_{n}|^{2}).

Equivalently, any subset
\Omega
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
determines a unique toric subset
X_{\Omega}=\mu^{-1}(\Omega)
. To prove Theorem
1.18
, it will be useful to approximate
\mu(X)
by collections of boxes to obtain simple toric approximations to
X
.

The
symplectic polydisk
,
\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r})
, corresponding to the
n
-tuple of positive real numbers
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\cdots,r_{n})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r})=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\ \rule[-9.95845pt]{0.28453pt}{25.60747pt}\ \frac{x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}}{r_{1}^{2}}\leq 1\,,\dots,\frac{x_{n}^{2}+y_{n}^{2}}{r_{n}^{2}}\leq 1\right\}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r})=\mu^{-1}([0,\pi r_{1}^{2}]\times\cdots\times[0,\pi r_{n}^{2}]).

It follows from the definition of the support function, that for
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in S^{2n-1}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle h_{\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r})}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\|u_{i}\|.

For a general box of the form

[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]=\left[\pi a_{1}^{2},\pi b_{1}^{2}\right]\times\cdots\times\left[\pi a_{n}^{2},\pi b_{n}^{2}\right]\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0},

the corresponding toric domain
X_{[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]}=\mu^{-1}([\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}])
will be referred to as a
symplectic polyannulus
. If any of the
a_{j}
is nonzero then
X_{[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]}
is not convex. However,

{\mathrm{Conv}}(X_{[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]})=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{b})

and

\displaystyle h_{X_{[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]}}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\|u_{i}\|.

3.2.
Convex bodies built from symplectic polyannuli

For a finite collection of
\ell
boxes

\displaystyle[\mathbf{a}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k}]=\left[\pi a_{k1}^{2},\pi b_{k1}^{2}\right]\times\cdots\times\left[\pi a_{kn}^{2},\pi b_{kn}^{2}\right],

for
k=1,\dots,\ell
, set

\mathbf{X}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell}X_{[\mathbf{a}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k}]}.

In this section, we prove the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
\mathbf{X}
as above, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(P\mathbf{X})={{\mathrm{M}}}(\mathbf{X}).

Arguing as in Section
2
, it suffices to show that the identity matrix is a global minimum of the function
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}\colon Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}(S)=\int_{S^{2n-1}}h_{\mathbf{X}}(Su)\,d\sigma.

Step 1 (Criticality).
We begin by proving the following.

Proposition 3.2
.

The identity matrix is a critical point of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}
.

Given a smooth path
S(s)
in
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
with
S(0)={\mathbb{I}}
, we need

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}(S(s))=0.

If
\nabla h_{\mathbf{X}}
was defined everywhere then we would have

(23)

\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}(S(s))=\int_{S^{2n-1}}\langle\nabla h_{\mathbf{X}}(u),S^{\prime}(0)u\rangle d\sigma.

We now show that
\nabla h_{\mathbf{X}}
is defined almost everywhere, so that equation (
23
) still holds. We also show that the right hand term can be simplified significantly.

For any finite collection of subsets,
X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}
, one has

\displaystyle h_{\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i}}(u)=\max\{h_{X_{1}}(u),\cdots,h_{X_{\ell}}(u)\}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle h_{\mathbf{X}}(u)=\max\{h_{X_{[\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{1}]}}(u),\dots,h_{X_{[\mathbf{a}_{\ell},\mathbf{b}_{\ell}]}}(u)\}=\max\{h_{1}(u),\dots,h_{\ell}(u)\}

where

\displaystyle h_{j}(u)=h_{P(\mathbf{{b}_{j})}}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{ji}\|u_{i}\|.

Given a matrix
S\in Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
and an integer
j
between
1
and
\ell
, set

\displaystyle U_{j}(S)=\{u\in S^{2n-1}\,|\,h_{j}(Su)>\max\{h_{1}(Su),\cdots,h_{j-1}(Su),h_{j+1}(Su),\cdots,h_{\ell}(Su)\}\}.

In these terms, we have

(24)

\displaystyle\nabla h_{\mathbf{X}}(Su)=\nabla h_{j}(Su)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{ji}\frac{(Su)_{i}}{\|(Su)_{i}\|}\quad\text{ for all $u$ in }U_{j}(S).

Here
(Su)_{i}
is the
i
-th component of
S(u)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

The
U_{j}(S)
are pairwise disjoint and their closures cover
S^{2n-1}
. In particular,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}\left(U_{j}(S)\cup\partial U_{j}(S)\right)=S^{2n-1}

where

\displaystyle\partial U_{j}(S)=\{u\in S^{2n-1}\,|\,h_{j}(Su)=\max\{h_{1}(Su),\cdots,h_{j-1}(Su),h_{j+1}(Su),\cdots,h_{\ell}(Su)\}\}.

Lemma 3.3
.

Each
\partial U_{j}(S)
has measure zero in
S^{2n-1}
.

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle\partial U_{j}(S)\subseteq\bigcup_{\alpha\neq j}\{u\in S^{2n-1}\,|\,h_{j}(Su)=h_{\alpha}(Su)\},

it suffices to show that for each
\alpha\neq j
,

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}:=\{u\in S^{2n-1}\,|\,h_{j}(Su)=h_{\alpha}(Su)\}

has measure zero in
S^{2n-1}
. Again, letting
(Su)_{i}
denote the
i
th component of
S(u)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
we split each
E_{\alpha}
into two components,

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}=\left(E_{\alpha}\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{u\in S^{2n-1}\,|\,(Su)_{i}=0\}\right)\cup\left(E_{\alpha}\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{u\in S^{2n-1}\,|\,(Su)_{i}\neq 0\}\right).

The first component has measure zero because each

\displaystyle\{u\in S^{2n-1}\,|\,(Su)_{i}=0\}

is a submanifold of
S^{2n-1}
of codimension 2. It remains to show that the second component of
E_{\alpha}
has measure zero. Setting

\displaystyle N:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{u\in S^{2n-1}\,|\,(Su)_{i}\neq 0\}

we note that
N
is a dense open submanifold of
S^{2n-1}
. Define the function
r_{\alpha}:N\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle r_{\alpha}(u)=h_{j}(Su)-h_{\alpha}(Su).

Since

\displaystyle\nabla r_{\alpha}(u)=S^{t}(\nabla h_{j}(Su)-\nabla h_{\alpha}(Su))=S^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(b_{ji}-b_{\alpha i})(Su)_{i}}{\|(Su)_{i}\|}

is nonsingular and nonvanishing on
N
it follows from the regular value theorem that
r_{\alpha}^{-1}(0)=E_{\alpha}\cap N
is a submanifold of
N
of codimension 1. This completes the proof.
∎

Equation (
24
) and Lemma
3.3
imply the following.

Corollary 3.4
.

Given a smooth path
S(s)
in
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}(S(s))=\int_{S^{2n-1}}\langle\nabla h_{\mathbf{X}}(S(s)u),S^{\prime}(s)u\rangle\,d\sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\int_{U_{j}(S(s))}\langle\nabla h_{j}(S(s)u),S^{\prime}(s)u\rangle\,d\sigma

where

\displaystyle\nabla h_{j}(S(s)u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{ji}\frac{(S(s)u)_{i}}{\|(S(s)u)_{i}\|}.

With this, we can now complete the proof of Proposition
3.2
.
Given a smooth path
S(s)
in
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
with
S(0)={\mathbb{I}}
, Corollary
3.4
yields

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}(S(s))}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{U_{j}}b_{ji}\frac{\langle u_{i},S^{\prime}(0)u\rangle}{\|u_{i}\|}\,d\sigma

where
U_{j}=U_{j}({\mathbb{I}})
. Since

\displaystyle S^{\prime}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}C&D\\
D&-C\end{pmatrix}

for symmetric
n\times n
matrices
C=(c_{ij})
and
D=(d_{ij})
, we have

\displaystyle\langle u_{i},S^{\prime}(0)u\rangle

\displaystyle=x_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{ij}x_{j}+d_{ij}y_{j})+y_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(d_{ij}x_{j}-c_{ij}y_{j})

\displaystyle=c_{ii}(x_{i}^{2}-y_{i}^{2})+\sum_{j\neq i}c_{ij}(x_{i}x_{j}-y_{i}y_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{ij}(x_{i}y_{j}+x_{j}y_{i}).

It suffices to prove the following.

Lemma 3.5
.

For integers
i
and
j
between
1
and
n
and integers
k
between
1
and
\ell
we have

(1)

\displaystyle\int_{U_{k}}\frac{x_{i}^{2}-y_{i}^{2}}{\|u_{i}\|}d\sigma=0
,

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{U_{k}}\frac{x_{i}x_{j}-y_{i}y_{j}}{\|u_{i}\|}d\sigma=0
for
i\neq j
,

(3)

\displaystyle\int_{U_{k}}\frac{x_{i}y_{j}}{\|u_{i}\|}d\sigma=0.

Proof.

Each subset
U_{k}
is toric, and each function
\|u_{i}\|
is invariant under the standard Hamiltonian
T^{n}
action. Hence each of the integrals
can be put in the form

\left(\int_{R_{k}}F(r)\,\,r_{1}\cdots r_{n-1}\,dr_{1}\cdots dr_{n-1}\right)\left(\int_{T^{n}}\Theta(\theta)\,\,d\theta_{1}\cdots d\theta_{n}\right),

where the subset
R_{k}\subset R
defines
U_{k}
, and
\Theta
is determined by the numerator of the corresponding integrand. In all three cases it is easy to verify that
\int_{T^{n}}\Theta(\theta)\,\,d\theta_{1}\cdots d\theta_{n}
vanishes.
∎

This completes the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Step 2 (Local minimality).

Proposition 3.6
.

The identity matrix is a local minimimum of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}
.

Proof.

Let
S(s)
be a curve in
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(2n))
with
S(0)={\mathbb{I}}
and
S^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
. Starting with the formula from Corollary
3.4
, and invoking the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality as in Lemma
2.4
, we get

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}(S(s))\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{U_{j}}b_{ji}\frac{\langle(S(s)u)_{i},S^{\prime\prime}(s)u\rangle}{\|(S(s)u)_{i}\|}\,d\sigma.

As above,
S^{\prime}(s)=S(s)X(s)
for some path
X(s)\in\mathfrak{sp}(2n)

of the form

X(s)=\begin{pmatrix}C(s)&D(s)\\
D(s)&-C(s)\end{pmatrix}

where
C(s)
and
D(s)
are
n\times n
symmetric matrices. Since

S^{\prime\prime}(s)=S^{\prime}(s)X(s)+S(s)X^{\prime}(s)=S(s)(X(s)^{2}+X^{\prime}(s)),

inequality (
25
) yields

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}(S(s))\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{U_{j}}b_{ji}\frac{\langle u_{i},X(0)^{2}u+X^{\prime}(0)u\rangle}{\|u_{i}\|}\,d\sigma.

Using Lemma
3.5
one can show that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{U_{j}}b_{ji}\frac{\langle u_{i},X^{\prime}(0)u\rangle}{\|u_{i}\|}\,d\sigma=0.

Note that

\displaystyle X(0)^{2}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}C(0)^{2}+D(0)^{2}&C(0)D(0)-D(0)C(0)\\
D(0)C(0)-C(0)D(0)&C(0)^{2}+D(0)^{2}\end{pmatrix}=:\begin{pmatrix}L&M\\
-M&L\end{pmatrix}.

Since

\displaystyle\langle u_{i},X(0)^{2}u\rangle

\displaystyle=x_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(l_{ij}x_{j}+m_{ij}y_{j})+y_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-m_{ij}x_{j}+l_{ij}y_{j})

\displaystyle=l_{ii}(x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2})+\sum_{j\neq i}l_{ij}(x_{i}x_{j}+y_{i}y_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{ij}(x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i}),

it again follows from Lemma
3.5
that

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}(S(s))\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{U_{j}}b_{ji}l_{ii}\|u_{i}\|\,d\sigma.

The right hand term is positive since, by the symmetry of
C
and
D
and the fact that
S^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, we have

l_{ii}=\sum_{k}(c_{ik}c_{ki}+d_{ik}d_{ik})=\sum_{k}(c_{ik}^{2}+d_{ik}^{2})>0.

∎

Step 3 (Global minimality).
It remains to prove the following analogue of Proposition
2.2
.

Proposition 3.7
.

The identity matrix is a global minimum of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}
.

Proof.

The proof is nearly identical. Given another local minimum
S_{1}
of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}
we again express it in the form

\displaystyle S_{1}=Q\begin{pmatrix}\Delta&0\\
0&\Delta^{-1}\end{pmatrix}Q^{t}

where
\Delta=\text{diag}(\delta_{1},\cdots,\delta_{n})
for some
\delta_{i}>0
and
Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
. Setting
\Delta(s)=\text{diag}(\delta_{1}^{s},\cdots,\delta_{n}^{s})
,
we consider the path of symplectic matrices

\displaystyle S(s)=Q\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(s)&0\\
0&\Delta(s)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}Q^{t}.

The function
f(s)=\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{X}}(S(s))
is defined on
[0,1]
and has critical points (local minima) at its endpoints. It suffices to show that
f
is constant. As in the proof of Proposition
3.6
we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(s)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{U_{j}}b_{ji}\frac{\langle(S(s)u)_{i},S^{\prime\prime}(s)u\rangle}{\|(S(s)u)_{i}\|}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{U_{j}}b_{ji}\frac{\langle(S(s)u)_{i},\left((QL(s)^{2}Q^{t})S(s)u\right)_{i}\rangle}{\|(S(s)u)_{i}\|}\,d\sigma.

By Lemma
2.5
, the quadratic form
v\mapsto\langle\pi_{i}v,\,\pi_{i}(QL(s)^{2}Q^{t}v)\rangle
, where
\pi_{i}\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is projection onto the
i
th factor, is positive semidefinite. Hence
f^{\prime\prime}(s)\geq 0
and
f
must be constant.
∎

3.3.
Approximation and completion

We can now complete the proof of Theorem
1.18
. Given a toric convex body
K
, we have
K=\mu^{-1}(\Omega)
for some compact subset
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
. Choose a sequence
\{\Omega_{i}\}
of subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
such that each
\Omega_{i}
is a finite union of boxes in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
that contains
\Omega
, the sequence is nested in the sense that

\displaystyle\Omega_{i+1}\subseteq\Omega_{i}\quad\text{for all}\,\,i\in{\mathbb{N}},

and the sequence converges to
\Omega
in the sense that

\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{i}=\Omega.

Let
X_{i}=\mu^{-1}(\Omega_{i})
, we then have
K\subseteq X_{i+1}\subseteq X_{i}
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}X_{i}=K.

Lemma 3.8
.

For the set
K
and the sequence
\{X_{i}\}
above, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}{{\mathrm{M}}}(X_{i})={{\mathrm{M}}}(K).

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle|{{\mathrm{M}}}(X_{i})-{{\mathrm{M}}}(K)|\,\leq 2\int_{S^{2n-1}}|h_{X_{i}}(u)-h_{K}(u)|d\sigma,

it suffices to show that

\lim_{i\to\infty}\|h_{X_{i}}-h_{K}\|_{\infty}=0.

As

\displaystyle\|h_{X_{i}}-h_{K}\|_{\infty}=d_{H}(X_{i},K),

this is equivalent to

\lim_{i\to\infty}\left(\sup_{x\in X_{i}}d_{H}(x,K)\right)=0.

Arguing by contradiction, assume that there is an
\epsilon_{0}>0
and a sequence
\{i_{k}\}
of natural numbers such that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}i_{k}=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{x\in X_{i_{k}}}d_{H}(x,K)\geq\epsilon_{0}\,\,\text{for all}\,\,k.

For each
k
we can then choose an
x_{i_{k}}\in X_{i_{k}}
such that

\displaystyle d_{H}(x_{i_{k}},K)\geq\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}>0.

Since the sequence
\{x_{i_{k}}\}
is bounded, we may assume that
\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{i_{k}}=x_{0}
. Note that limit
x_{0}
lies in
K
, i.e.,

\displaystyle x_{0}\in\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}X_{i_{k}}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}X_{i}=K.

On the other hand, by the continuity of the distance function we have
d_{H}(x_{0},K)\geq\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}>0
which yields the desired contradiction.
∎

Arguing as in Lemma
3.8
, one can also show that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PX_{i})={{\mathrm{M}}}(PK)

for any
P\in\mathrm{Sp}(2n)
. So, for any
\epsilon>0
, we can choose
N
such that

\displaystyle-\epsilon+{{\mathrm{M}}}(PK)<{{\mathrm{M}}}(PX_{i})<{{\mathrm{M}}}(PK)+\epsilon

holds for all
i\geq N
. Taking the infimum over
P\in\mathrm{Sp}(2n)
and invoking Theorem
3.1
we get

\displaystyle-\epsilon+\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PK)\leq\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PX_{i})={{\mathrm{M}}}(X_{i})\leq\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PK)+\epsilon.

This implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}{{\mathrm{M}}}(X_{i})=\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PK)

which, by Lemma
3.8
, implies

\displaystyle{{\mathrm{M}}}(K)=\inf_{P\in\mathrm{\mathrm{Sp}(2n)}}{{\mathrm{M}}}(PK).

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.18
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.19

To prove the assertion that
{\mathrm{M}}_{\mathrm{Sp}}(\mathbf{P}_{L})={{\mathrm{M}}}(\mathbf{P}_{L})
, we argue as in the proofs of Theorems
1.16
and
1.18
and show that
{\mathbb{I}}
is a global minimum of the function
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{P}_{L}}:Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(4))\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{P}_{L}}(S)=\int_{S^{3}}h_{\mathbf{P}_{L}}(Su)d\sigma.

The differences between this proof and those of Theorems
1.16
and
1.18
are apparent in the first step, showing that
{\mathbb{I}}
is a critical point. The remaining steps of the proof will be presented in less detail.

For
u\in S^{3}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
let
u_{x}
be the projection to the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane and
u_{y}
be the projection to the
y_{1}y_{2}
-plane. We then have
h_{\mathbf{P}_{L}}(u)=\|u_{x}\|+\|u_{y}\|.
For any smooth curve
S(s)
in
Sym^{+}(\mathrm{Sp}(4))
with
S(0)={\mathbb{I}}

(26)

\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{P}_{L}}(S(s))=\int_{S^{3}}\left\langle\frac{u_{x}}{\|u_{x}\|}+\frac{u_{y}}{\|u_{y}\|},S^{\prime}(0)u\right\rangle d\sigma.

Setting
S^{\prime}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}C&D\\
D&-C\end{pmatrix}
we have

\displaystyle\langle u_{x},S^{\prime}(0)u\rangle

\displaystyle=c_{11}x_{1}^{2}+2c_{12}x_{1}x_{2}+c_{22}x_{2}^{2}+d_{11}x_{1}y_{1}+d_{12}(x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1})+d_{22}x_{2}y_{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(-\frac{1}{r_{i}}\frac{\partial\dot{f}_{0}}{\partial\theta_{i}}(\theta,r)\cos\theta_{i}+\left(-\frac{\partial\dot{f}_{0}}{\partial r_{i}}(\theta,r)-\frac{r_{i}\dot{f}_{0}(\theta,r)}{f^{2}_{0}(r)}\right)\sin\theta_{i}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}

\displaystyle+\left(-\frac{1}{r_{n}(0,r)}\frac{\partial\dot{f}_{0}}{\partial\theta_{n}}(\theta,r)\cos\theta_{n}+\frac{(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}r_{j}^{2})}{r_{n}(0,r)}\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(\theta,r)}{f^{2}_{0}(r)}\sin\theta_{n}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{n}}.

A straight forward computation yields

Lemma 6.9
.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|v_{0}(\theta,r)\|^{2}\,=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{r_{i}}{f_{0}(r)}-\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial r_{i}}(r)\right)^{2}+\frac{r_{n}(0,r)^{2}}{f_{0}(r)^{2}}.

In particular,

Another simple computation yields.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle v_{0}(\theta,r),\dot{v_{0}}(\theta,r)\rangle=\left(\frac{1}{f_{0}(r)^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}r_{i}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial r_{i}}(r)\right)\dot{f_{0}}(\theta,r)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial r_{i}}(r)-\frac{r_{i}}{f_{0}(r)}\right)\frac{\partial\dot{f_{0}}}{\partial r_{i}}(\theta,r).

Lemma 6.11
.

The function

Proof.
