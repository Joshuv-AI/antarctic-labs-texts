Abstract
. In this paper, we study dependence uncertainty and the resulting effects on tail risk measures, which play a fundamental role in modern risk management. We introduce the notion of a regular dependence measure, defined on multi-marginal couplings, as a generalization of well-known correlation statistics such as the Pearson correlation. The first main result states that even an arbitrarily small positive dependence between losses can result in perfectly correlated tails beyond a certain threshold and seemingly complete independence before this threshold. In a second step, we focus on the aggregation of individual risks with known marginal distributions by means of arbitrary nondecreasing left-continuous aggregation functions. In this context, we show that under an arbitrarily small positive dependence, the tail risk of the aggregate loss might coincide with the one of perfectly correlated losses. A similar result is derived for expectiles under mild conditions. In a last step, we discuss our results in the context of credit risk, analyzing the potential effects on the value at risk for weighted sums of Bernoulli distributed losses. Keywords: Dependence uncertainty, dependence measure, risk aggregation, multi-marginal coupling, copula, tail event, tail risk measure, value at risk, expectile JEL Classification: G22; G28; G32



1.
Introduction

The estimation of tail risk is an integral part of modern regulation as it is supposed to quantify the liquidity of financial institutions in the case of rare extreme events. For banks, credit risk is the most important type of risk. Loan portfolios or, equivalently, in an insurance context, insurance portfolios typically comprise a large number of customers, each of them representing the risk of a potential loss for the bank or the insurance company. While the loss distribution of each customer, individually, may be well-known, e.g., due to ratings or data for classification in risk categories,
the distribution of the aggregate portfolio loss is usually difficult to assess as it requires precise knowledge of the dependence structures within the portfolio, i.e., the
joint
distribution of the loss profiles of all customers. However, the modelling and estimation of dependencies within large portfolios represents a complex and daunting task from a mathematical and statistical point of view, respectively, cf.
[
19
,
29
]
and the references therein. Therefore, the analysis of the joint distribution is typically confined to a quantification of dependencies using dependence measures, such as the Pearson correlation, Kendall’s tau or Spearman’s rho. As a consequence, the joint distribution is highly susceptible to model uncertainty, and a broad strand of literature has formed around the study of risk bounds under dependence uncertainty, i.e., the study of worst-case scenarios for risk measures of the aggregate loss when the dependence structure among the random variables of interest in not completely specified.

In this context, the literature distinguishes between two cases, the first one being the case of full dependence uncertainty, i.e., when no information on the dependence structure is available and only the marginal distributions are known. Early and seminal contributions in this direction include
[
28
]
and
[
34
]
, where the authors solve the problem of finding the worst-case value at risk (VaR) for the sum of two random variables, followed by a series of related publications, most of them focusing on the study of worst-case VaR for the sum of an arbitrary number of random losses, cf.
[
6
,
14
,
18
,
38
]
. A more recent contribution that studies bounds for spectral risk measures of general aggregation functions using an optimal transport approach is
[
22
]
, and general results for tail risk measures of the aggregate sum have been derived in
[
26
]
.

Although a complete specification of the dependence structure may be difficult to attain in practice, it is realistic to assume that at least some partial knowledge can be inferred from available data. This observation has motivated the second strand of literature, which studies risk aggregation problems under partial information on the dependence structure, in addition to the knowledge of the marginal distributions.
One possibility is to work under the assumption that some point wise bounds are available for the copula or the joint distribution of interest, cf.
[
9
,
17
]
. Another possibility to describe partial knowledge is to assume that the copula is specified on a subset of its domain. This case has been studied in
[
8
]
. In the financial industry, partial dependence information is usually described using one or more measures of dependence, the most popular of them being the Pearson correlation, cf.
[
27
]
. In this context,
[
5
]
shows that, for the sum of two random variables, a partial specification of the dependence structure via bounds under a dependence measure, such as Pearson’s correlation, hardly affects the worst-case VaR. We also refer to
[
24
]
for an analysis of the worst-case tail behaviour based on a property called tail convexity.

For an arbitrary number of random variables, risk aggregation problems have been studied under quite specific assumptions on the considered dependence measure. For instance,
[
7
]
studied VaR bounds, assuming that the variance of the sum is known to be less or equal than a certain threshold. A similar situation in the context of credit risk with exchangeable losses has been considered in
[
21
]
.

In the present paper, we introduce a general notion of dependence measure, comprising essentially all dependence measures used in risk management, cf. Example
4
. In our setting, a dependence measure is a map that assigns a value between
-1
and
1
to multi-marginal couplings and zero to the product measure. Moreover, we say that a dependence measure is regular if it is lower semicontinuous at the product measure with respect to convergence in distribution. Given a regular dependence measure, our first main result, Theorem
5
, shows that, if an arbitrarily small positive dependence between losses cannot be excluded, their joint probability distribution might have perfectly correlated tails beyond a certain threshold while pretending to have independent marginals before this threshold, so that the true dependence structure in the tails is only revealed after observing an extreme event with perfectly correlated losses.

In Section
4
, we then apply Theorem
5
to tail risk measures of aggregate losses for arbitrary nondecreasing left-continuous aggregation functions. Theorem
17
shows that the worst-case tail risk could potentially be greater or equal than the tail risk under perfect correlation. This becomes even more apparent if one considers a coherent tail risk measure, such as the expected shortfall, together with a positively weighted sum as an aggregation function and identically distributed marginals. In this case, the worst-case expected shortfall of the aggregate loss coincides with the weighted sum of the individual expected shortfalls, cf. Corollary
18
and the discussion thereafter. Using the main result in
[
2
]
, the same holds true for certain expectiles despite the fact that they are not tail risk measures, cf. Theorem
19
.

In Example
21
, we illustrate our results in a credit risk context for the value at risk of a sum of Bernoulli distributed random variables for the typical threshold of
99.9\%
, corresponding to a once in a thousand years event. Already for
1000
customers having a correlation less than
0.1
and
1\%
probability of default, the worst-case value at risk exceeds the value at risk, given that we have not yet observed a once in a thousand years event, by a factor of
50
. This simple example underlines the high sensitivity of tail risk for aggregate positions with respect to imperfect knowledge of the dependence structure.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notion of a regular dependence measure and state the fist main result, Theorem
5
, where we construct the previously described joint distribution. In Section
3
, we investigate monotonicity properties of the joint distribution in lower orthant order and PQD order with respect to the dependence constraint. In Section
4
, we transfer the results from Section
2
to the value at risk, cf. Theorem
16
and, in a second step, to arbitrary tail risk measures and expectiles, cf. Theorem
17
and Theorem
19
. The Appendix
A
contains two standard results on multi-marginal couplings and the convergence of copulas, which we state and prove for the reader’s convenience.

.

Y

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
with distribution

\VaR_{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha}(X):=\inf\big\{a\in\mathbb{R}\,\big|\,\mathbb{P}(X>a)\leq 1-\alpha\big\}=F_{\nu}^{-1}(\alpha)

\alpha\in(0,1)

.

2.
Multivariate dependence measures and first main result

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In this section, we introduce the notion of a dependence measure for general Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
\pr_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be the
i
-th coordinate projection.

For
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, we denote by
\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
the set of all
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with

\pi\circ\pr_{i}^{-1}=\mu_{i}\quad\text{for all }i=1,\ldots,n.

The elements of
\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
are called
multi-marginal couplings
. We refer to
[
23
]
and
[
32
]
for an overview on multi-marginal optimal transport problems and applications. Moreover, we refer to
[
1
]
and
[
37
]
for a survey on classical optimal transport and related topics. Observe that, for all
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, the product measure
\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
is always an element of
\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
. In the sequel, we also use the notation

\cpl(\mu)=\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})\quad\text{for }\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{n}

and, for a nonempty set
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, we define

\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M}):=\bigcup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\cpl(\mu).

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
be nonempty.

a)

A map
\varrho\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
is called a
dependence measure
if

\varrho\big(\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}\big)=0\quad\text{for all }\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}.

b)

We say that a dependence measure
\varrho\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
is
regular
if, for all
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
and any sequence
(\pi^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
with
\pi^{k}\to\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
as
k\to\infty
, it follows that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\varrho(\pi^{k})\leq 0.

Note that we do not exclude the case
n=1
. However, in this case, the only dependence measure is
\varrho\equiv 0
. If
n=2
, we use the terminology
bivariate dependence measure
. In situations, where we explicitly consider the case
n>2
, we also use the expression
multivariate dependence measure
.

Remark 2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
be nonempty. Then, we say that a dependence measure
\varrho\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
is
symmetric
if, for all multi-marginal couplings
\pi\in\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})
and any permutation
\sigma\colon\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

\varrho(\pi)=\varrho\big(\pi\circ(x\mapsto x_{\sigma})^{-1}\big),

where
x_{\sigma}:=(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Example 3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
be nonempty.

a)

Let
\varrho\colon\cpl(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
be a dependence measure with

\lim_{k\to\infty}\varrho(\pi^{k})=0

for any sequence
(\pi^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
with
\pi^{k}\to\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
as
k\to\infty
and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
. Then, both,
\varrho
and
-\varrho
are regular dependence measures.

b)

Let
\varrho_{j}\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
be a regular dependence measure for
j=1,\ldots,\ell
with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
D\colon[-1,1]^{\ell}\to[-1,1]
be a nondecreasing function with

D(0)=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}D(\delta\textbf{1})=0,

where
1
denotes the
\ell
-dimensional vector consisting of only ones. Then, the map
\varrho\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
, given by

\varrho(\pi):=D\big(\varrho_{1}(\pi),\ldots,\varrho_{\ell}(\pi)\big)\quad\text{for }\pi\in\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M}),

defines a regular dependence measure. In fact, let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
. Then,

\varrho(\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n})=D\big(\varrho_{1}(\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}),\ldots,\varrho_{\ell}(\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n})\big)=D(0)=0.

Now, let
(\pi^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\cpl^{n}(\mu)
with
\pi^{k}\to\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
as
k\to\infty
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then, there exists some
\delta>0
such that
D(\delta\textbf{1})<\varepsilon
. Since
\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{\ell}
are regular, there exists some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sup_{k\geq k_{0}}\varrho_{j}(\pi^{k})\leq\delta\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,\ell.

Since
D
is nondecreasing, it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{k\geq k_{0}}\varrho(\pi^{k})

\displaystyle=\sup_{k\geq k_{0}}D\big(\varrho_{1}(\pi^{k}),\ldots,\varrho_{\ell}(\pi^{k})\big)\leq D\bigg(\sup_{k\geq k_{0}}\varrho_{1}(\pi^{k}),\ldots,\sup_{k\geq k_{0}}\varrho_{\ell}(\pi^{k})\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq D(\delta\textbf{1})<\varepsilon.

Explicit examples for the function
D
are given by the following constructions.

•

Weighted sum.
For fixed weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell}\in[0,1]
with
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}w_{j}\leq 1
, let

D(r):=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}w_{j}r_{j}\quad\text{for }r=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell})\in[-1,1]^{\ell}.

•

Weighted minimum/maximum.
For fixed weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell}\in[0,1]
, let

D(r):=\min_{j=1,\ldots,\ell}w_{j}r_{j}\quad\text{or}\quad D(r):=\max_{j=1,\ldots,\ell}w_{j}r_{j}\quad\text{for }r\in[-1,1]^{\ell}.

c)

Let
n\geq 0
and
\varrho_{ij}\colon\cpl^{2}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
be a regular bivariate dependence measure for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
i\neq j
. For
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, let

\pi_{ij}:=\pi\circ(\pr_{i},\pr_{j})^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad\text{for }i,j=1,\ldots,n\text{ with }i\neq j.

By definition,
\pi_{ij}\in\cpl^{2}(\mathcal{M})
for
\pi\in\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})
and
i,j=1,\ldots,n
with
i\neq j
, and

\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1],\;\pi\mapsto\varrho_{ij}(\pi_{ij})

is a regular dependence measure for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
with
i\neq j
. In fact, for all
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
and
i,j=1,\ldots,n
with
i<j
,

(\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n})_{ij}=\mu_{i}\otimes\mu_{j}

and
\pi_{ij}^{k}\to\mu_{i}\otimes\mu_{j}
as
k\to\infty
for any sequence
(\pi^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})
with
\pi^{k}\to\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
as
k\to\infty
.

Hence, by part a), for any nondecreasing function
D\colon[-1,1]^{n(n-1)}\to[-1,1]
with
D(0)=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}D(\delta\textbf{1})=0
, where
1
denotes the
n(n-1)
-dimensional vector consisting of only ones,
\varrho\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
, given by

(1)

\varrho(\pi):=D\Big(\big(\varrho_{ij}(\pi_{ij})\big)_{i\neq j}\Big)\quad\text{for }\pi\in\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M}),

defines a regular dependence measure.

In the following example, we present prominent bivariate regular dependence measures, which can be used as building blocks for multivariate dependence measures, as described in the previous example.

Example 4
.

a)

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\mu({\rm d}x)<\infty
, and recall that we use the convention
\frac{0}{0}=0
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, let

\var(\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}x^{2}\,\mu({\rm d}x)-\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,\mu({\rm d}x)\bigg)^{2}.

Then, for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}
and
\pi\in\cpl(\mu,\nu)
, the
Pearson correlation

\cor(\pi):=\frac{\cov(\pi)}{\sqrt{\var(\mu)\var(\nu)}}

defines a symmetric and regular dependence measure, where

\cov(\pi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}xy\,\pi({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,\mu({\rm d}x)\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\,\nu({\rm d}y).

Clearly,
\cor
is a dependence measure. In order to prove the regularity of
\cor
, let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
and
(\pi^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\cpl(\mu,\nu)
with
\pi^{k}\to\mu\otimes\nu
as
k\to\infty
. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}x^{2}+y^{2}\,\pi^{k}({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\mu({\rm d}x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}y^{2}\,\nu({\rm d}y)\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, by
[
37
, Definition 6.8]
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}xy\,\pi^{k}({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,\mu({\rm d}x)\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\,\nu({\rm d}y)\quad\text{as }k\to\infty,

so that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\cor(\pi^{k})=0
.

b)

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of all Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
with continuous distribution function. By Sklar’s theorem, for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}
and
\pi\in\cpl(\mu,\nu)
, there exists a unique copula
c\colon[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
with

\pi\big((-\infty,a]\times(-\infty,b]\big)=c\big(F_{\mu}(a),F_{\nu}(b)\big)\quad\text{for all }a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

Then, for
\pi\in\cpl(\mathcal{M})
with copula
c
,
Spearman’s rho

\spear(\pi):=12\int_{[0,1]^{2}}c(u,v)\,{\rm d}(u,v)-3

defines a symmetric and regular dependence measure. Since
\int_{[0,1]^{2}}uv\,{\rm d}(u,v)=\frac{1}{4}
, it follows that
\spear(\mu\otimes\nu)=0
for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. Now, let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
and
(\pi^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\cpl(\mu,\nu)
with
\pi^{k}\to\mu\otimes\nu
as
k\to\infty
. Since
F_{\mu}
and
F_{\nu}
are continuous, by Lemma
23
, it follows that

\sup_{u,v\in[0,1]}\big|c^{k}(u,v)-uv\big|\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty,

where
c^{k}
is the copula related to
\pi^{k}
. Hence,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}c^{k}(u,v)\,{\rm d}(u,v)=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}uv\,{\rm d}(u,v),

so that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\spear(\pi^{k})=0
.

c)

Consider the same situation as in the previous example. Then, for
\pi\in\cpl(\mathcal{M})
with copula
c
,
Kendall’s tau

\tau(\pi):=4\int_{[0,1]^{2}}c(u,v)\,{\rm d}c(u,v)-1

defines a symmetric and regular dependence measure. In fact, since

\int_{[0,1]^{2}}uv\,{\rm d}(u,v)=\frac{1}{4},

it follows that
\tau(\mu\otimes\nu)=0
for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. In order to prove the regularity, let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
and
(\pi^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\cpl(\mu,\nu)
with
\pi^{k}\to\mu\otimes\nu
as
k\to\infty
. Again, since
F_{\mu}
and
F_{\nu}
are continuous, by Lemma
23
, it follows that

\sup_{u,v\in[0,1]}\big|c^{k}(u,v)-uv\big|\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty,

where
c^{k}
is the copula related to
\pi^{k}
. Hence, by Hölder’s inequality,

\bigg|\int_{[0,1]^{2}}c^{k}(u,v)\,{\rm d}c^{k}(u,v)-\frac{1}{4}\bigg|\leq\sup_{u,v\in[0,1]}\big|c^{k}(u,v)-uv\big|+\bigg|\int_{[0,1]^{2}}uv\,{\rm d}c^{k}(u,v)-\frac{1}{4}\bigg|\to 0

as
k\to\infty
. We have therefore shown that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau(\pi^{k})=0
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be nonempty,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be an atomless probability space, and
\varrho\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[0,1]
be a dependence measure. Then, for random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
on
\Omega
, we use the notation

\varrho(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}):=\varrho\big(\mathbb{P}\circ(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})^{-1}\big).

Recall that a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is atomless if and only if there exists a uniformly distributed random variable
U\colon\Omega\to(0,1)
. The following theorem is the first main result.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space,
U\colon\Omega\to(0,1)
uniformly distributed,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
nonempty, and
\varrho\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
a regular dependence measure. Then, for all
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
, there exist
\gamma\in(0,1)
and random variables
X_{1},\ldots X_{n}
on
\Omega
such that

(i)

X_{i}\sim\mu_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,

(ii)

\varrho(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\leq\delta
,

(iii)

X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are conditionally independent on the event
\{U\leq\gamma\}
,

(iv)

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\big\{X_{i}>F_{\mu_{i}}^{-1}(\gamma)\big\}\cap\{U\leq\gamma\}\big)=0\quad\text{and}\quad X_{i}\mathds{1}_{\{U>\gamma\}}=F_{\mu_{i}}^{-1}(U)\mathds{1}_{\{U>\gamma\}}.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
and
Y_{i}:=F_{\mu_{i}}^{-1}(U)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. For
\gamma\in(0,1)
, we define
C^{\gamma}:=\{U\leq\gamma\}
. Since
U
is uniformly distributed,
(C^{\gamma},\mathcal{F}\cap C^{\gamma},\mathbb{P}^{\gamma})
is atomless, where

\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}(A):=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A)}{\gamma}\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{F}\cap C^{\gamma}

and
\mathcal{F}\cap C^{\gamma}:=\{A\cap C^{\gamma}\,|\,A\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Hence, there exist
Z_{1}^{\gamma},\ldots,Z_{n}^{\gamma}\colon C^{\gamma}\to\mathbb{R}
that are independent under
\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}
and satisfy

\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}(Z_{i}^{\gamma}\leq a)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\{Y_{i}\leq a\}\cap C^{\gamma})}{\gamma}\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }i=1,\ldots,n.

For
i=1,\ldots,n

X_{i}^{\gamma}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
by

X_{i}^{\gamma}(\omega):=\begin{cases}Z_{i}^{\gamma}(\omega),&\omega\in C^{\gamma},\\
Y_{i}(\omega),&\omega\in\Omega\setminus C^{\gamma}.\end{cases}

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{i}^{\gamma}\leq a)

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}(Z_{i}^{\gamma}\leq a)+\mathbb{P}\big(\{Y_{i}\leq a\}\cap(\Omega\setminus C^{\gamma})\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\{Y_{i}\leq a\}\cap C^{\gamma})+\mathbb{P}\big(\{Y_{i}\leq a\}\cap(\Omega\setminus C^{\gamma})\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}\leq a)=F_{\mu_{i}}(a)

for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
i=1,\ldots,n
. Therefore,
X_{i}^{\gamma}\sim\mu_{i}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\big\{X_{i}^{\gamma}>F_{\mu_{i}}^{-1}(\gamma)\big\}\cap\{U\leq\gamma\}\big)

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\big(Z_{i}^{\gamma}>F^{-1}_{\mu_{i}}(\gamma)\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\big\{Y_{i}>F^{-1}_{\mu_{i}}(\gamma)\big\}\cap C^{\gamma}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\big\{F_{\mu_{i}}^{-1}(U)>F^{-1}_{\mu_{i}}(\gamma)\big\}\cap\{U\leq\gamma\}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)=0\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n.

We define
\pi^{\gamma}:=\mathbb{P}\circ(X^{\gamma})^{-1}\in\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
for all
\gamma\in(0,1)
, and it remains to show that
\pi^{\gamma}\to\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
as
\gamma\to 1
. To that end, let
(\gamma^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,1)
with
\gamma^{k}\to 1
. Since
\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
is weakly compact, cf. Lemma
22
, there exists a subsequence
(\gamma^{k_{l}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\pi^{\gamma^{k_{l}}}\to\pi\in\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
as
l\to\infty
. On the other hand, by dominated convergence, for all
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\borel_{\rm b}(\mathbb{R})
with
f_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(f_{1}(X_{1}^{\gamma})\cdots f_{n}(X_{n}^{\gamma})\big)

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbb{E}^{\gamma}\big(f_{1}(Z_{1}^{\gamma})\cdots f_{n}(Z_{n}^{\gamma})\big)+\mathbb{E}\big(f_{1}(Y_{1})\cdots f_{n}(Y_{n})\mathds{1}_{\{U>\gamma\}}\big)

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbb{E}^{\gamma}\big(f_{1}(Z_{1}^{\gamma})\big)\cdots\mathbb{E}^{\gamma}\big(f_{n}(Z_{n}^{\gamma})\big)+\mathbb{E}\big(f_{1}(Y_{1})\cdots f_{n}(Y_{n})\mathds{1}_{\{U>\gamma\}}\big)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{1-n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big(f_{i}(Y_{i})\mathds{1}_{\{U\leq\gamma\}}\big)+\mathbb{E}\big(f_{1}(Y_{1})\cdots f_{n}(Y_{n})\mathds{1}_{\{U>\gamma\}}\big)

(2)

\displaystyle\to\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big(f_{i}(Y_{i})\big)\quad\text{as }\gamma\to 1,

where
\mathbb{E}^{\gamma}
denotes the expected value with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}
. Since
\pi^{\gamma^{k_{l}}}\to\pi
as
l\to\infty
, this implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{1}(x_{1})\cdots f_{n}(x_{n})\,\pi({\rm d}x_{1},\ldots,{\rm d}x_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{i}(x_{i})\,\mu_{i}({\rm d}x_{i})

for all
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\cnt_{\rm b}(\mathbb{R})
with
f_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Using monotone convergence together with the fact that
\mathds{1}_{U}
is bounded and lower semicontinuous for all open sets
U\subset\mathbb{R}
, it follows that

\pi(U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{n})=\mu_{1}(U_{1})\cdots\mu_{n}(U_{n})

for all open sets
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Since the system of all open subsets of
\mathbb{R}
is an intersection-stable generator of the Borel
\sigma
-algebra, it follows that
\pi=\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
by Dynkin’s lemma. Hence,
\pi^{\gamma^{k_{l}}}\to\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
as

, which implies that
\pi^{\gamma}\to\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
as
\gamma\to 1
, since we have shown that every subsequence has a further subsequence that converges to the same limit
\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
. Since
\varrho
is regular, it follows that

\limsup_{\gamma\to 1}\varrho(\pi^{\gamma})\leq 0.

The proof is complete.

Remark 6
.

Although Theorem
5
is formulated in terms of a single constraint

(3)

\varrho(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\leq\delta,

it can easily be extended to include, for example, multiple constraints of the form

(4)

\varrho_{j}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\big[\delta_{j}^{-},\delta_{j}^{+}\big]\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,\ell

with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, dependence measures
\varrho_{j}\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
with
\lim_{k\to\infty}\varrho_{j}(\pi^{k})=0
for all sequences
\pi^{k}\in\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
with
\pi^{k}\to\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
as
k\to\infty
, and
\pm\delta_{j}^{\pm}\in(0,1)
for

. In fact, let

and define

(5)

\varrho(\pi):=\delta\max_{j=1,\ldots\ell}\bigg(\frac{\varrho_{j}(\pi)}{\delta_{j}^{+}}\vee\frac{\varrho_{j}(\pi)}{\delta_{j}^{-}}\bigg)\quad\text{for all }\pi\in\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M}).

Then, by Example
3
a) & b),
\varrho\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
is a regular dependence measure. Since
\delta_{j}^{+}>0
and
\delta_{j}^{-}<0
, the
\ell
constraints formulated in (

) are equivalent to the single constraint (

) for
\varrho
given by (

).

Similarly, for regular dependence measures
\varrho_{j}\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
and
\delta_{j}\in(0,1)
for

, one can consider multiple one-sided constraints of the form

(6)

by setting

and

for

.

for all

. For a detailed discussion of the lower orthant order and related multivariate stochastic orders, we refer to
[
33

. For applications of such comparison criteria in the context of risk aggregation problems, we refer to
[

]
and the references therein. For two random vectors with

T^{1}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm d}}{{=}}T^{2}_{i}\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n,

the inequality

than among the components of
T^{2}
. In the case

and assuming identical marginals for

and

, the lower orthant order coincides with the order of

\leq_{\rm PQD}

for short,
originally introduced in
[

and sometimes called

, cf.
[
16
]
, which is one of the most popular stochastic orders adopted to formalize the intuition of stronger positive dependence between two random variables. For instance, all measures of concordance in the sense of
[
35
]
, such as Spearman’s rho and Kendall’s tau, are consistent with PQD order, cf.
[
30
]
. The same is true for the Pearson correlation, cf.
[
25

. For an overview and a more detailed discussion of these dependence concepts, we refer to

Proposition 8
.

Let
0<\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}<1
. Then,

X^{\gamma_{2}}\leq_{\rm lo}X^{\gamma_{1}}.

Proof.

For
j=1,2
, let
U^{\gamma_{j}}
be defined as the vector whose
i
-th element is given by

U^{\gamma_{j}}_{i}=\gamma_{j}V_{i}\mathds{1}_{\{U\leq\gamma_{j}\}}+U\mathds{1}_{\{U>\gamma_{j}\}},

where
(V_{1},V_{2},...,V_{n},U)
are i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
(0,1).

First, we show that
\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}
implies
U^{\gamma_{2}}\leq_{\rm lo}U^{\gamma_{1}}
. Since the marginal distributions of
U^{\gamma_{1}}
and
U^{\gamma_{2}}
are uniform on the interval
(0,1)
, we only have to show that

\mathbb{P}\big(U^{\gamma_{2}}\leq a\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(U^{\gamma_{1}}\leq a\big)\quad\text{for all }a\in(0,1)^{n}.

If
a\in\prod^{n}_{i=1}(\gamma_{1},1)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(U^{\gamma_{1}}\leq a\big)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\big\{U_{i}^{\gamma_{1}}>a_{i}\big\}\bigg)=1-\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\big\{U>a_{i}\big\}\bigg)=\mathbb{P}\big((U,\ldots,U)\leq a\big)

\displaystyle=\min_{i=1,\ldots,n}a_{i}\geq\mathbb{P}\big(U^{\gamma_{2}}\leq a\big).

Now, let
a\in(0,1)^{n}\setminus\prod^{n}_{i=1}(\gamma_{1},1)
. Then, there exists some
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
a_{k}<\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}
. Now, let
j\in\{1,2\}
and
\omega\in\Omega
with
U^{\gamma_{j}}(\omega)\leq a
. Assume, towards a contradiction, there existed some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
U^{\gamma_{j}}_{i}(\omega)>\gamma_{j}
. Then,
\gamma_{j}<U_{i}^{\gamma_{j}}(\omega)=U(\omega)=U^{\gamma_{j}}_{k}(\omega)\leq a_{k}
, which contradicts
a_{k}<\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}(U^{\gamma_{j}}\leq a)=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\big\{U_{i}^{\gamma_{j}}\leq a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{j}\big\}\bigg)\quad\text{for }j=1,2.

Moreover, by construction of
U^{\gamma_{j}}
,

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\big\{U_{i}^{\gamma_{j}}\leq a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{j}\big\}\cap\{U>\gamma_{j}\}\bigg)=0\quad\text{for }j=1,2,

which implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(U^{\gamma_{j}}\leq a\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\big\{U_{i}^{\gamma_{j}}\leq a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{j}\big\}\cap\{U\leq\gamma_{j}\}\bigg)=\gamma_{j}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\big(\gamma_{j}V_{i}\leq a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{j}\big)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}=a_{k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{i=1}\\
{i\neq k}\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}\quad\text{for }j=1,2.

Since
\frac{a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}}\geq\frac{a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{2}}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, it follows that

\mathbb{P}\big(U^{\gamma_{1}}\leq a\big)=a_{k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{i=1}\\
{i\neq k}\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}}\geq a_{k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{i=1}\\
{i\neq k}\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{i}\wedge\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{2}}=\mathbb{P}\big(U^{\gamma_{2}}\leq a\big).

We have therefore shown that
U^{\gamma_{2}}\leq_{\rm lo}U^{\gamma_{1}}
. Finally, the statement
X^{\gamma_{2}}\leq_{\rm lo}X^{\gamma_{1}}
follows from the observation that
X^{\gamma_{j}}_{i}=F^{-1}_{\mu_{i}}\big(U^{\gamma_{j}}_{i}\big)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,2
together with the fact that the lower orthant order is preserved under nondecreasing transforms, cf.
[
36

.

Risk aggregation and tail risk measures

In this section, we put the first main result, Theorem

, in the context of so-called tail risk measures as introduced in
[
26
]
. Throughout, we work in the setup of Theorem
5
. That is, we consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, a uniformly distributed random variable
U\colon\Omega\to(0,1)
, a nonempty set
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, and a regular dependence measure
\varrho\colon\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})\to[-1,1]
. Then, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta>0
and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
, let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be random variables on
\Omega
such that (i) - (iv) in Theorem
5
is satisfied. As before, we use the notation
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
with
Y_{i}:=F_{\mu_{i}}^{-1}(U)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Note that

is a comonotonic vector, i.e., it consists of perfectly correlated random variables, that has the same marginals as
X
. We point out that comonotonicity is often seen as the strongest possible form of positive dependence and describes a complete lack of diversification benefits, cf.
[
15
]
for a detailed discussion of this concept.

Moreover, let
C^{\gamma}:=\{U\leq\gamma\}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\colon\mathcal{F}\cap C^{\gamma}\to[0,1]
be given by

\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}(A):=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A)}{\gamma}\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{F}\cap C^{\gamma}.

We define
Z_{i}\colon C^{\gamma}\to\mathbb{R}
by
Z_{i}(\omega):=X_{i}(\omega)
for all
\omega\in C^{\gamma}
and
i=1,\ldots,n
. Again, we use the notation
Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})
.

Let

be a nondecreasing and left-continuous function, i.e.,

f(x)\leq f(y)\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ with }x\leq y

and

. The aim of this section is to analyze the value at risk of

. In particular, we show that
f(X)
and

exhibit the same tail risk starting from the risk threshold
\gamma
, i.e., for all
\alpha\in[\gamma,1)

\VaR_{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha}(f(X))=\VaR_{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha}(f(Y)).

In this context, we define

q(\alpha):=f\big(F^{-1}_{\mu_{1}}(\alpha),\ldots,F^{-1}_{\mu_{n}}(\alpha)\big)\in\mathbb{R}.

Observe that the map
(0,1)\to\mathbb{R},\;\alpha\mapsto q(\alpha)
is nondecreasing and left-continuous, since
f
and
F_{\mu_{i}}^{-1}
for

are nondecreasing and left-continuous. We start with the following three auxiliary results.

Lemma 13
.

Let

with

. Then,
\mathbb{P}\big(\{f(X)>a\}\cap\{U\leq\gamma\}\big)=0
.

Proof.

Let
\omega\in\Omega
with

By definition of
q(\gamma)
and since
f
is nondecreasing, there exists some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
X_{i}(\omega)>F^{-1}_{\mu_{i}}(\gamma)
. Hence, using property (iv) in Theorem
5
, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\{f(X)>a\}\cap\{U\leq\gamma\}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{X_{i}>F^{-1}_{\mu_{i}}(\gamma)\}\cap\{U\leq\gamma\}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\big(\{X_{i}>F^{-1}_{\mu_{i}}(\gamma)\}\cap\{U\leq\gamma\}\big)=0.

∎

Lemma 14
.

For all
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\VaR_{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha}\big(f(Y)\big)=q(\alpha)

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and observe that

implies that

for all
\omega\in\Omega
by monotonicity of
f
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}\big(f(Y)>q(\alpha)\big)\leq\mathbb{P}(U>\alpha)\leq 1-\alpha,

so that
\VaR_{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha}\big(f(Y)\big)\leq q(\alpha)
. Now, let

with

q
, there exists some
\beta\in(0,\alpha)
with

. Hence,

1-\alpha<1-\beta=\mathbb{P}(U\geq\beta)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(f(Y)\geq q(\beta)\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(f(Y)>a\big),

\VaR_{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha}\big(f(Y)\big)=q(\alpha)
.
∎

Lemma 15
.

For all
\alpha\in(0,1)

\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\big(f(Z)\big)=\VaR_{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha\gamma}\big(f(X)\big).

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then, by defintion of

and

, for all

\VaR_{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha\gamma}\big(f(X)\big)\leq\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\big(f(Z)\big).

Since, by property (iv) in Theorem
5
,

\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\big(Z_{i}\leq F_{\mu_{i}}^{-1}(\gamma)\big)=1\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n

\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\big(f(Z)\big)\leq q(\gamma).

Now, let

with

. Then,
a<q(\gamma)
, so that, by monotonicity of

,
f\big(X(\omega)\big)\leq a
implies that

for all

. Hence,

\mathbb{P}\big(f(X)\leq a\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\{f(X)\leq a\}\cap\{U\leq\gamma\}\big)=\gamma\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\big(f(Z)\leq a\big)<\alpha\gamma,

which implies that

and, consequently,
\VaR_{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha\gamma}\big(f(X)\big)\geq\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\big(f(Z)\big)

To that end, observe that

\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\big(f(X)\big)=\inf\big\{a\in\mathbb{R}\,\big|\,\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\big(f(Z)>a\big)=0\big\}.

Now, let

with

. Then, using the left-continuity of

, there exists some

with

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\big(f(Z)>a\big)

∎

Theorem 16
.

For all

with

\mathbb{P}\big(f(Y)\leq a\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(f(X)\leq a\big).

We briefly recall the notion of a tail risk measure, introduced in

]

\mathcal{X}

be a uniformly distributed random variable with

\mathbb{P}

is implicitly assumed to be atomless via the existence of a uniformly distributed random variable. Then, the tail risk of

also depends on the choice of the uniformly distributed random variable

. However, passing from

to another uniformly distributed random variable, say

T^{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm d}}{{=}}F_{\nu}^{-1}(\alpha+(1-\alpha)V)\quad\text{for all }\alpha\in(0,1).

is unique up to equality in distribution.

Z
is defined as the unique solution to the equation

\alpha\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(T-{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(T)\big)_{+}\Big)=(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(T-{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(T)\big)_{-}\Big).

Note that, for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
,
{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(T)=\mathbb{E}(T)
. It is well-known that
{\rm ex}^{\alpha}
is a law-invariant risk measure, so that we also use the notation
{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\nu):={\rm ex}^{\alpha}(T)
if
T\sim\nu
. We refer to
[
3
,
4
,
12
,
13
]
for a detailed and axiomatic study of expectiles as well as their interpretation in a financial context.

As a consequence of
[
2
, Theorem 3]
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 19
.

Assume that
\mu_{i}=\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\,\mu({\rm d}x)<\infty
, and let
\lambda_{1},\ldots\lambda_{n}\in[0,\infty)
. Then, for all
\alpha\in\big[\frac{1}{2},1\big)
with
{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\geq F_{\mu}^{-1}(\gamma)
,

{\rm ex}^{\alpha}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}X_{i}\bigg)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(X_{i})={\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}.

Proof.

For
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
the statement is clear since, in this case, the
\alpha
-expectile is the mean. Let
\alpha\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)
with
{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\geq F_{\mu}^{-1}(\gamma)
. By
[
2
, Theorem 3]
, we have to show that

(14)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big(\lambda_{i}X_{i}-{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\lambda_{i}X_{i})\big)\big(\lambda_{j}X_{j}-{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\lambda_{j}X_{j})\big)<0\Big)=0\quad\text{for }i,j=1,\ldots,n.

Since
{\rm ex}^{\alpha}
is law-invariant and positively homogeneous, and
X_{i}\sim\mu
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, this is equivalent to showing that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big(X_{i}-{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\big)\big(X_{j}-{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\big)<0\Big)=0\quad\text{for }i,j=1,\ldots,n.

To that end, first observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\big(X_{i}-{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\big)\big(X_{j}-{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\big)<0\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\big(\{X_{i}<{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\cap\{X_{j}>{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\big(\{X_{i}>{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\cap\{X_{j}<{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\big)

for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
. Hence, it remains to show that

\mathbb{P}\big(\{X_{i}<{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\cap\{X_{j}>{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\big)=0\quad\text{for }i,j=1,\ldots,n.

Since
{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\geq F_{\mu}^{-1}(\gamma)
, by property (iv) in Theorem
5
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\{X_{i}<{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\cap

\displaystyle\{X_{j}>{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\{X_{i}<{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\cap\{X_{j}>{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\}\cap\{U>\gamma\}\big)

\displaystyle\;=\mathbb{P}\big(\big\{F_{\mu}^{-1}(U)<{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\big\}\cap\big\{F_{\mu}^{-1}(U)>{\rm ex}^{\alpha}(\mu)\big\}\cap\{U>\gamma\}\big)=\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)=0

for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
. We have therefore proved the validity of (
14
), and the statement now follows from
[
2
, Theorem 3]
.
∎

Remark 20
.

Consider the case, where
\mu_{i}=\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\mu({\rm d}x)<\infty
and
m^{\gamma}:=\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{\gamma}F_{\mu}^{-1}(u)\,{\rm d}u>0
. Moreover, let
\alpha\in[\gamma,1)
and
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,\infty)
with

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}=0.

In this remark, we aim to study the asymptotic behaviour of the ratio

r^{\alpha}(n):=\frac{\VaR^{\alpha}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}X_{i}\big)}{\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}Z_{i}\big)}

as the number of customers
n
tends to infinity.

First observe that
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
are i.i.d. with distribution
\mu^{\gamma}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, mean
m^{\gamma}
, and variance
\var(\mu^{\gamma})<\infty
. Let
\varepsilon>0
, then, using Markov’s inequality and the independence of
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\big(Z_{i}-m^{\gamma}\big)>n\varepsilon\bigg)\leq\frac{\var(\mu^{\gamma})}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}.

Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}=0
, there exist
c>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\geq\frac{1}{c}
and

\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}Z_{i}>n\varepsilon+m^{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\bigg)=\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\big(Z_{i}-m^{\gamma}\big)>n\varepsilon\bigg)\leq 1-\alpha

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq n_{0}
. Hence, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq n_{0}
,

\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}Z_{i}\bigg)\leq n\varepsilon+m^{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\leq\big(m^{\gamma}+c\varepsilon\big)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i},

so that, by Theorem
18
,

r^{\alpha}(n)\geq\frac{F_{\mu}^{-1}(\alpha)}{m^{\gamma}+c\varepsilon}\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ with }n\geq n_{0}.

Since
F_{\mu}^{-1}
is nondecreasing, we have therefore shown that

(15)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\VaR^{\alpha}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}X_{i}\big)}{\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}Z_{i}\big)}\geq\frac{\VaR^{\alpha}(\mu)}{\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\alpha}F_{\mu}^{-1}(u)\,{\rm d}u}\geq 1.

In particular, there exists some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(16)

\frac{\VaR^{\alpha}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}X_{i}\big)}{\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}Z_{i}\big)}\geq\frac{\VaR^{\alpha}(\mu)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,\mu({\rm d}x)}\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ with }n\geq n_{0}.

Example 21

(Value at risk for credit portfolio)
.

Tail risk measures such as value at risk or expected shortfall are an essential part of risk management for most financial institutions. In this example, we discuss the implications of our theoretical results on dependence uncertainty for the value at risk in the context of portfolio credit loss for a bank caused by defaulting borrowers.

Throughout, we use the same notation as in the proof of Theorem
5
and consider a simplified setting with
n
borrowers, where the credit loss caused by a default equals the remaining exposure of the respective borrower, i.e., for
i=1,\ldots,n
, the loss of borrower
i
can be interpreted as a random variable

L_{i}=\text{Exposure}_{i}\cdot X_{i}\quad\text{with}\quad X_{i}\sim B(1,p_{i}),

where
p_{i}\in(0,1)
is the probability of default (PD) of borrower
i
over a one year time horizon. Note that, in practice, the true PD might of course differ from the estimated PD of the rating model. However, in order to isolate the
impact of
dependence uncertainty on the portfolio value at risk, we neglect the possibility of uncertain PDs, and implicitly assume
p_{i}
to be the true PD of borrower
i
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. For an axiomatic study of model uncertainty related to PDs, including applications in the context of regulatory capital requirements, we refer to
[
31
]
.

For
\pi\in\cpl^{n}(\mathcal{M})
with
\mathcal{M}
consisting of all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,\mu({\rm d}x)<\infty
, we consider

\varrho(\pi):=\max_{i\neq j}\cor\big(\pi^{ij}\big)

where
\pi^{ij}
is given as in Example
3
c).
Our aim is now to calculate the value of risk at level
\alpha=0.999
of

f\big(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\big):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Exposure}_{i}\cdot X_{i},

allowing for possible dependences up to a certain level
\delta\in(0,1)
, i.e.,
\varrho(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\leq\delta
.
Observe that, if
\lambda_{i}=\text{Exposure}_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, the function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by
f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is nondecreasing and continuous. We point out that the rather high value for
\alpha
is the confidence level commonly used in practice for the value at risk over a one year horizon, describing a once in a thousand years event. From Example
12
a) i), we know that if

(17)

\gamma:=\alpha=0.999\geq\frac{1}{1+\delta\frac{p_{\min}}{1-p_{\min}}},

the conditions of Theorem
17
are met. In this case,
\gamma>1-p_{\min}\geq 1-p_{i}
and, on the event
C^{\gamma}
, the random variables
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
are independent under
\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}
with
\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\big(Z_{i}=0\big)=\frac{1-p_{i}}{\gamma}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}\big(Z_{i}=1\big)=\frac{\gamma-1+p_{i}}{\gamma}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Hence,

Z_{i}\sim B\left(1,\frac{\gamma-1+p_{i}}{\gamma}\right)\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n.

For the sake of simplicity, we now consider a portfolio consisting of
n=1000
borrowers, each having an exposure of
1
and a PD of
1\%
, but the results can easily be transferred to more complex portfolio constellations with different exposures and different PDs as previously described. Choosing
\delta=\frac{1}{10}
, one sees that (
17
) is satisfied. In this case,

f(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\sim B\left(n,\frac{\gamma-1+p}{\gamma}\right),

so that the conditional value at risk on the event
C^{\gamma}
is given by

\VaR^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}\big(f(Z)\big)=20.

On the other hand, using Theorem
5
and Theorem
16
, we obtain

\VaR^{\alpha}\big(f(X)\big)=1000=50\cdot\VaR^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}\big(f(Z)\big),

i.e., the potential value at risk for a once in a thousand years event could be
50
times higher than the value at risk, given that we have not observed such an event before. By (
15
), we know that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\VaR^{\alpha}\big(f(X)\big)}{\VaR_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}^{\alpha}\big(f(Z)\big)}\geq\frac{0.999}{0.999-0.99}=111.

The threshold

\frac{\VaR^{\alpha}\big(B(1,p)\big)}{p}=100,

given in (
16
), is reached for
n_{0}=100\,000
customers. The asymptotic behaviour of the ratio
r^{0.999}
is depicted in Figure
3
.

In applications, one usually only has a default rate history of a few decades. Consequently, with a very high probability one only observes default rates on the event
C^{\gamma}
, where in our example the defaults behave independently. Information about tail behavior for
\alpha\geq 0.999
is usually not available and needs to be estimated. Simply extrapolating correlation effects from the available data history, in this case
C^{\gamma},
might not be sufficient to estimate the value at risk for such large values of
\alpha
, but could lead to a drastic underestimation of the true risk, in our case, possibly by a factor of
50
.

Last but not least, we point out that, by Theorem
16
, we also have

\VaR^{\alpha}\big(f(X)\big)=\VaR^{\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}\big(f(Z)\big)\geq\VaR^{\gamma}_{\mathbb{P}^{\gamma}}\big(f(Z)\big)\text{ for }\alpha<0.999.

In Figure

both values coincide on the chosen grid for the displayed values of
\alpha<0.999
.

Appendix A
Some auxiliary results

For the proof of the first main result, we need the following standard result from optimal transport.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, the set
\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
is weakly compact.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then, there exists a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
with

\mu_{i}(\mathbb{R}\setminus K)<\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\quad\text{for all }i=1,\ldots,n.

Hence, for any
\pi\in\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
,

\pi(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus K^{n})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}(\mathbb{R}\setminus K)<\varepsilon.

We have therefore shown that the set
\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
is tight. Moreover,

\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\big\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\,\big|\,\pi\circ\pr_{i}^{-1}=\mu_{i}\big\}

is weakly closed since
\pr_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.
∎

The following lemma is sort of a folklore theorem for copulas. For the reader’s convenience, we state and prove it in our setup.

Lemma 23
.

Let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
with continuous distribution functions
F_{\mu_{1}},\ldots,F_{\mu_{n}}
and
(\pi^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
be a sequence of couplings with
\pi^{k}\to\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in distribution as
k\to\infty
. Then, there exist unique copulas
c
and
(c^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

(18)

\pi\big((-\infty,a]\big)=c\big(F_{\mu_{1}}(a_{1}),\ldots,F_{\mu_{n}}(a_{n})\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\pi^{k}\big((-\infty,a]\big)=c^{k}\big(F_{\mu_{1}}(a_{1}),\ldots,F_{\mu_{n}}(a_{n})\big)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover,

\sup_{u\in[0,1]^{n}}\big|c^{k}(u)-c(u)\big|\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

In particular,

\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\pi^{k}\big((-\infty,a]\big)-\pi\big((-\infty,a]\big)\big|\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Proof.

By Lemma
22
, it follows that
\pi\in\cpl(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
. Since
F_{\mu_{1}},\ldots,F_{\mu_{n}}
are continuous, by Sklar’s theorem, there exist unique copulas
c
and
(c^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that (
18
) is satisfied. Now, let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Again, since
F_{\mu_{1}},\ldots,F_{\mu_{n}}
are continuous,

\pi\bigg(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\pr_{j}^{-1}\big(\{a_{j}\}\big)\cap(-\infty,a]\bigg)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi\circ\pr_{j}^{-1}\big(\{a_{j}\}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{j}\big(\{a_{j}\}\big)=0.

Hence, by the portmanteau theorem
[
10
, Corollary 8.2.10]
, it follows that

c^{k}\big(F_{\mu_{1}}(a_{1}),\ldots,F_{\mu_{n}}(a_{n})\big)=\pi^{k}\big((-\infty,a]\big)\to\pi\big((-\infty,a]\big)=c\big(F_{\mu_{1}}(a_{1}),\ldots,F_{\mu_{n}}(a_{n})\big)

as
k\to\infty
. Since
F_{\mu_{1}},\ldots,F_{\mu_{n}}
are continuous, this implies that

(19)

\lim_{k\to\infty}c^{k}(u)=c(u)\quad\text{for all }u\in[0,1]^{n}.

Now, let
\varepsilon>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
\frac{n}{m}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Let
u_{i}:=\frac{i}{m}
for
i=0,\ldots,m
. Then,

c(u_{i_{1}+1},\ldots,u_{i_{n}+1})-c(u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{n}})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(u_{i_{j}+1}-u_{i_{j}}\big)\leq\frac{n}{m}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

for all
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
. Moreover, by (
19
), there exists some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\big|c^{k}(u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{n}})-c(u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{n}})\big|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq k_{0}
and
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
.

Now, let
u\in[0,1]^{n}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq k_{0}
, and
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
with

u_{i_{j}}\leq u_{j}\leq u_{i_{j}+1}\quad\text{for all }j=1,\ldots,n.

Then,

c^{k}(u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{n}})-c(u)\leq c^{k}(u)-c(u)\leq c^{k}(u_{i_{1}+1},\ldots,u_{i_{n}+1})-c(u)\quad\text{for }k\in\mathbb{N}.

Since

\displaystyle c(u)-c^{k}(u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{n}})

\displaystyle<c(u)-c(u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{n}})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

\displaystyle\leq c(u_{i_{1}+1},\ldots,u_{i_{n}+1})-c(u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{n}})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<\varepsilon

and

\displaystyle c^{k}(u_{i_{1}+1},\ldots,u_{i_{n}+1})-c(u)

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+c(u_{i_{1}+1},\ldots,u_{i_{n}+1})-c(u)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+c(u_{i_{1}+1},\ldots,u_{i_{n}+1})-c(u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{n}})<\varepsilon,

it follows that
\big|c^{k}(u)-c(u)\big|<\varepsilon
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq k_{0}
.
∎
