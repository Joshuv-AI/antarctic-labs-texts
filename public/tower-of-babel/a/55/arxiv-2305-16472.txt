Analysis of Density Matrix Embedding Theory around the non-interacting limit
Eric Cancès, Fabian Faulstich, Alfred Kirsch, Eloïse Letournel, Antoine Levitt

Abstract
This article provides the first mathematical analysis of the Density Matrix Embedding Theory (DMET) method. We prove that, under certain assumptions, (i) the exact ground-state density matrix is a fixed-point of the DMET map for non-interacting systems, (ii) there exists a unique physical solution in the weakly-interacting regime, and (iii) DMET is exact at first order in the coupling parameter. We provide numerical simulations to support our results and comment on the physical meaning of the assumptions under which they hold true. We show that the violation of these assumptions may yield multiple solutions of the DMET equations. We moreover introduce and discuss a specific N N -representability problem inherent to DMET.

Introduction

Electronic structure theory is a powerful quantum mechanical framework for investigating the intricate behavior of electrons within molecules and crystals.
At the core lies the interaction between particles, specifically the electron-electron and electron-nuclei interactions.
Embracing the essential quantum physical effects, this theory is the foundation for
ab initio
quantum chemistry and materials science calculations performed by many researchers in chemistry and related fields, complementing and supplementing painstaking laboratory work.
With its diverse applications in chemistry and materials science, electronic structure theory holds vast implications for the mathematical sciences.
Integrating mathematical doctrines into this field leads to the development of precise and scalable numerical methods, enabling extensive

The synergy between mathematics and electronic structure theory offers the potential for groundbreaking advancements in addressing these global challenges.

Within the realm of electronic structure theory, the treatment of

Here, the application of high-accuracy quantum chemical methods that are able to capture the electronic correlation effects at chemical accuracy is inevitable.

2
The DMET formalism

2.1
The quantum many-body problem and its fragment decomposition

We consider a physical system with
L
quantum sites, with one orbital per site, occupied by
1\leq N<L
electrons, and assume that magnetic effects (interaction with an external magnetic field, spin-orbit coupling, etc.) can be neglected. This allows us to work with real-valued wave-functions and density matrices. We set

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:={\mathbb{R}}^{L}\quad\text{(one-particle state space)},\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{at}}:=\{e_{{\kappa}}\}_{\kappa\in[\![1,L]\!]}\quad\text{(canonical basis of ${\mathbb{R}}^{L}$)},

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{n}:=\bigwedge^{n}\mathcal{H}\quad\text{($n$-particle state space)},\quad

\displaystyle{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}):=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{L}\mathcal{H}_{n}\quad\text{(real fermionic Fock space)}.

We denote by
\widehat{a}_{\kappa}
and
\widehat{a}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}
the generators of the (real) CAR algebra associated with the canonical basis of
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.

\widehat{a}_{\kappa}:=\widehat{a}(e_{{\kappa}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{a}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}=\widehat{a}^{\dagger}(e_{{\kappa}}).

Recall that the maps

{\mathbb{R}}^{L}\ni f\mapsto\widehat{a}^{\dagger}(f)\in\mathcal{L}({\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}))\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathbb{R}}^{L}\ni f\mapsto\widehat{a}(f)\in\mathcal{L}({\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H})),

are both linear in this setting since we work in a real Hilbert space framework. Here and below,
\mathcal{L}(E)
is the space of linear operators from the finite-dimensional vector space
E
to itself. We also define the number operator
\widehat{N}
by

\displaystyle\widehat{N}:=\sum_{n=0}^{L}n\,\widehat{{\mathds{1}}}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}=\sum_{\kappa=1}^{L}\widehat{a}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\mbox{(particle number operator)}.

For each linear subspace
E
of
\mathcal{H}
, we denote the orthogonal projector on
E
by
\Pi_{E}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
.
We assume that the Hamiltonian of the system in the second-quantized formulation reads

\widehat{H}:=\sum_{\kappa,\lambda=1}^{L}h_{\kappa\lambda}\widehat{a}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\kappa,\lambda,\nu,\xi=1}^{L}V_{\kappa\lambda\nu\xi}\widehat{a}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\xi}\widehat{a}_{\nu},

(2)

where the matrix
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}
and the 4th-order tensor
V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L\times L\times L}
satisfy the following symmetry properties:

h_{\kappa\lambda}=h_{\lambda\kappa}\quad\mbox{and}\quad V_{\kappa\lambda\nu\xi}=V_{\nu\lambda\kappa\xi}=V_{\kappa\xi\nu\lambda}=V_{\nu\xi\kappa\lambda}.

We denote by
\mathcal{D}
the Grassmannian of rank-
N
orthogonal projectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
:

\mathcal{D}:={\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{R}}(N,L)=\{D\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}_{\rm sym}\;|\;D^{2}=D,\;\mathrm{Tr}(D)=N\},

(3)

and by
\mathrm{CH}({\mathcal{D})}
the convex hull of
\mathcal{D}
, i.e.

\mathrm{CH}({\mathcal{D})}=\{D\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}_{\rm sym}\;|\;0\leq D\leq 1,\;\mathrm{Tr}(D)=N\}.

(4)

Physically, the set
\mathrm{CH}({\mathcal{D})}
corresponds to the set of (real-valued, mixed-state)
N
-representable one-body density matrices with
N
electrons, and
\mathcal{D}
is the set of one-body density matrices generated by (real-valued) Slater determinants in
\mathcal{H}_{N}
.

We consider a fixed partition of the
L
sites into
N_{f}
non-overlapping fragments
\{\mathcal{I}_{x}\}_{x\in[\![1,N_{f}]\!]}
of sizes
\{L_{x}\}_{x\in[\![1,N_{f}]\!]}
such that
L_{x}<N
for all
x
. Up to reordering the sites, we can assume that the partition is the following:

[\![1,L]\!]=\bigg\{\underbrace{(1,\cdots,L_{1})}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}},\underbrace{(1+L_{1},\cdots,L_{1}+L_{2})}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}},\cdots,\underbrace{(1+L_{1}+\cdots+L_{N_{f}-1},\cdots,L)}_{\mathcal{I}_{N_{f}}}\bigg\}.

(5)

This partition corresponds to a decomposition of the space into
N_{f}
fragment subspaces fulfilling

\mathcal{H}=X_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{N_{f}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad X_{x}:=\mathrm{Span}(e_{{\kappa}},\;\kappa\in\mathcal{I}_{x}).

(6)

For
M\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}_{\rm sym}
, we set

\mathrm{Bd}(M):=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\Pi_{x}M\Pi_{x},

(7)

where
\Pi_{x}:=\Pi_{X_{x}}
is the orthogonal projector on
X_{x}
.
The operator
\mathrm{Bd}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}_{\rm sym})
is the orthogonal projector onto the set of block-diagonal matrices for the partition (
5
) (endowed with the Frobenius inner product).

As we will see, a central intermediary in DMET is the diagonal blocks of the density matrix,

P=\mathrm{Bd}(D)\in\mathrm{Bd}(\mathcal{D})
. It is clear that these blocks
must satisfy
0\leq P_{x}\leq 1
and
\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\mathrm{Tr}(P_{x})=N
.
Conversely, it is easy to see that grouping these
blocks together into a block-diagonal matrix produces a matrix in

\mathrm{CH}({\mathcal{D})}
; therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}:=\mathrm{Bd}(\mathrm{CH}({\mathcal{D})})=\bigg\{P=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}P_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&P_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&P_{N_{f}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\mbox{ s.t. }\forall 1\leq x\leq N_{f},\;P_{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L_{x}\times L_{x}}_{\rm sym},\;0\leq P_{x}\leq 1,\;\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\mathrm{Tr}(P_{x})=N\bigg\}.

(12)

From a geometrical viewpoint,
\mathcal{P}
is a non-empty, compact, convex subset of an affine vector subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}_{\rm sym}
with base vector space

{\mathcal{Y}}:=\bigg\{Y=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}Y_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&Y_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&Y_{N_{f}}\\
\end{array}\right)\mbox{ s.t. }\forall 1\leq x\leq N_{f},\;Y_{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L_{x}\times L_{x}}_{\rm sym},\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\mathrm{Tr}(Y_{x})=0\bigg\}.

(13)

The structure of the set
\mathrm{Bd}(\mathcal{D})\subset\mathcal{P}
is a more subtle issue that we will investigate in Section
6
.

2.2
The impurity high-level problem

Given one of the spaces
X_{x}
and a one-body density matrix
D\in\mathcal{D}
, we set:

W_{{x},{D}}:=X_{x}+DX_{x}=DX_{x}\oplus(1-D)X_{x}\quad\mbox{($x$-th impurity subspace)}.

(14)

We will assume in the following that

\dim(DX_{x})=\dim((1-D)X_{x})=\dim(X_{x})=L_{x}\quad\mbox{(maximal-rank assumption)},

(15)

so that
\dim(W_{{x},{D}})=2L_{x}
.
Decomposing
\mathrm{Ran}(D)
and
\mathrm{Ker}(D)
as

\mathrm{Ran}(D)=DX_{x}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{{x},{D}}^{\mathrm{core}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathrm{Ker}(D)=(1-D)X_{x}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{{x},{D}}^{\mathrm{virt}},

we obtain the following decomposition of
\mathcal{H}={\mathbb{R}}^{L}
:

\mathcal{H}=W_{{x},{D}}\oplus\underbrace{\mathcal{H}_{{x},{D}}^{\mathrm{core}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{{x},{D}}^{\mathrm{virt}}}_{=:\mathcal{H}_{{x},{D}}^{\mathrm{env}}}.

Note that the space
\mathcal{H}_{{x},{D}}^{\mathrm{core}}
has dimension
(N-L_{x})
. The matrix
D
can be seen as the one-body density matrix associated with the Slater determinant

\Psi^{0}_{N,{D}}=\Psi^{0,\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\wedge\Psi^{0,\mathrm{core}}_{x,D}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\Psi^{0,\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\in\bigwedge^{L_{x}}DX_{x}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Psi^{0,\mathrm{core}}_{x,D}\in\bigwedge^{(N-L_{x})}\mathcal{H}_{{x},{D}}^{\mathrm{core}},

where
\Psi^{0,\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}
and
\Psi^{0,\mathrm{core}}_{x,D}
are normalized.
More precisely,
\Psi^{0}_{N,{D}}
is the Slater determinant built from an orthonormal basis of
L_{x}
orbitals in
DX_{x}
and an orthonormal basis of
(N-L_{x})
orbitals in
\mathcal{H}_{{x},{D}}^{\mathrm{core}}
. The so-defined wave-function
\Psi^{0}_{N,{D}}
is unique up to an irrelevant sign.

We denote by
\widehat{N}_{X_{x}}\in\mathcal{L}({\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}))
the projection of the number operator onto the fragment Fock space
{\rm Fock}(X_{x})
.
Solving the impurity problem aims at minimizing, for a given
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
which will be specified later, the thermodynamic potential

\langle\Psi|(\widehat{H}-\mu\widehat{N}_{X_{x}})|\Psi\rangle

(16)

over the set of normalized trial states in
{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H})
of the form

\Psi=\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\wedge\Psi^{0,\mathrm{core}}_{x,D}

(17)

with
\Psi^{0,\mathrm{core}}_{x,D}
fixed, and
\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}
in

{\rm Fock}(W_{{x},{D}}):=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{L_{x}}\bigwedge^{n}W_{{x},{D}}\quad\mbox{($x$-th impurity Fock space)}.

The impurity Hamiltonian is the unique operator
\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{{x},{D}}
on
{\rm Fock}(W_{{x},{D}})
such that

\forall\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\in{\rm Fock}(W_{{x},{D}}),\quad\langle\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}|\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{{x},{D}}|\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\rangle=\langle\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\wedge\Psi^{0,\mathrm{core}}_{x,D}|\widehat{H}|\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\wedge\Psi^{0,\mathrm{core}}_{x,D}\rangle.

(18)

For an explicit expression of
\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{{x},{D}}
, see Proposition

7
.

The impurity problem defined by (
16
)-(
17
) can then be reformulated as

\min_{\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\in{\rm Fock}(W_{{x},{D}}),\lVert\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\rVert=1}\langle\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}|\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{{x},{D}}-\mu\widehat{N}_{X_{x}}|\Psi^{\mathrm{imp}}_{x,D}\rangle\quad\mbox{(impurity problem)}.

(19)

In practice, this full-CI problem in the Fock space
{\rm Fock}(W_{{x},{D}})
is solved by an approximate correlated wave-function method such as CASSCF, CCSD or DMRG for example, but we assume in this analysis that it can be solved exactly.

If (
19
) has a non-degenerate ground state for all

x
, we denote the one-body ground-state density matrices by
P_{\mu,{x}}(D)
, seen as matrices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}_{\mathrm{sym}}
, and finally set

\boxed{F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\mu,{x}}(D):=\Pi_{X_{x}}P_{\mu,{x}}(D)\Pi_{X_{x}}.}

(20)

Let us remark incidentally that if the ground state of the impurity problem is degenerate, we can either consider
F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\mu,{x}}(D)
as a multivalued function or define them from finite-temperature versions of (
19
), which are strictly convex compact problems on the set of density operators on the Fock space, and therefore always have a unique minimizer. We will not proceed further in this direction and only consider here the case of impurity problems with non-degenerate ground states.

The combination of the
N_{f}
impurity problems introduced in (
19
) (see also (
20
)) gives rise to a high-level DMET map
F^{\mathrm{HL}}

\mathcal{D}\ni D\mapsto F^{\mathrm{HL}}(D)\in\mathcal{P}

(21)

formally defined by

\boxed{F^{\mathrm{HL}}(D):=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\mu,{x}}(D)\quad\mbox{(high-level map)}}

(22)

with
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
chosen such that
\mathrm{Tr}(F^{\mathrm{HL}}(D))=N
. The domain of
F^{\mathrm{HL}}
and the regularity properties of this map will be studied in Section
5
.

2.3
The global low-level problem

The low-level map is defined by

\boxed{F^{\mathrm{LL}}(P):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{D\in\mathcal{D},\;\mathrm{Bd}(D)=P}{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{HF}}\left(D\right)\quad\mbox{(low-level map)},}

(23)

where
{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{HF}}
is the Hartree-Fock (mean-field) energy functional of the trial density-matrix
D
. The latter reads

{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{HF}}(D):=\mathrm{Tr}(hD)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(J(D)D)-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(K(D)D),

(24)

where

[J(D)]_{\kappa\lambda}:=\sum_{\nu,\xi=1}^{L}V_{\lambda\xi\kappa\nu}D_{\nu\xi}\quad\mbox{and}\quad[K(D)]_{\kappa\lambda}:=\sum_{\nu,\xi=1}^{L}V_{\kappa\xi\nu\lambda}D_{\nu\xi}.

(25)

The existence and uniqueness of a minimizer
to (
23
) will be discussed in
Section
6
.

2.4
The DMET problem

Finally, the full DMET map is formally defined as the self-consistent solution to the system

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=F^{\mathrm{LL}}(P)\in\mathcal{D},

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=F^{\mathrm{HL}}(D)\in\mathcal{P}.

In particular,
D=F^{\mathrm{LL}}(P)
implies that
P=\mathrm{Bd}(D)
. Equivalently, we can formulate the problem as

\boxed{P=F^{\mathrm{DMET}}(P):=F^{\mathrm{HL}}(F^{\mathrm{LL}}(P)).}

Assuming that the solution to this fixed-point problem exists and is unique,
P
is expected to provide a good approximation of the diagonal blocks (in the decomposition (
6
) of
\mathcal{H}
) of the ground-state one-body density matrix of the interacting system.
The mathematical properties of this self-consistent loop will be studied in the next section, first for the non-interacting case, and second, for the interacting case in a perturbative regime.

3
Main results

We now embed the Hamiltonian
H

\widehat{H}_{\alpha}:=\sum_{\kappa,\lambda=1}^{L}h_{\kappa\lambda}\widehat{a}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\lambda}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{\kappa,\lambda,\nu\xi=1}^{L}V_{\kappa\lambda\nu\xi}\widehat{a}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\xi}\widehat{a}_{\nu},\quad\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}},

(26)

acting on

For
\alpha=0

\widehat{H}_{0}:=\sum_{\kappa,\lambda=1}^{L}h_{\kappa\lambda}\widehat{a}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\lambda}

(27)

\alpha=1

F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\alpha}
,
F^{\mathrm{LL}}_{\alpha}
, and

the high-level, low-level, and DMET maps constructed from
\widehat{H}_{\alpha}
.

We first assume that the non-interacting problem is non-degenerate.
Denoting by
\varepsilon_{n}
the

(A1)

\varepsilon_{N}<0<\varepsilon_{N+1}

where without loss of generality we have chosen the Fermi level to be

By perturbation theory, the ground state of

-particle sector is non-degenerate for all
\alpha\in(-\alpha_{+},\alpha_{+})
for some

the corresponding ground-state one-body density matrix.
As a consequence of analytic perturbation theory for hermitian matrices, the map

is real-analytic.

Second, we make the maximal-rank assumption:

(A2)

For all
1\leq x\leq N_{f}
,
\dim(D_{0}X_{x})=\dim((1-D_{0})X_{x})=\dim(X_{x})=L_{x}

Assumption (A2) implies that the impurity problem (

We emphasize however that this does not prejudge that the so-obtained

impurity problems are well-posed (i.e. have a unique ground-state) for a given value of

, nor
a fortiori
that

is in the domain of the high-level map

.
We will elaborate more on the meaning of Assumptions (A2) in Section

DMET is then consistent in the non-interacting case:

.

D_{*}
.
We will make use of this important observation in the proof of Theorem
4
.

We now study the DMET equations in the perturbative regime of
\alpha
small.
In order to use perturbative techniques, we need to determine the space in which we seek
P
.
Generically, at
\alpha\neq 0
, we expect
P
to be equal to the block diagonal of the one-body density matrix, which is not a projector.
Therefore it is natural to seek
P
in
\mathcal{P}=\mathrm{Bd}(\mathrm{CH}({\mathcal{D})})
.
However, in the DMET method,
D
is constrained to be a projector, and therefore
P
will necessarily belong to
\mathrm{Bd}(\mathcal{D})
.
We will study in Section
6
the relationship between the two sets
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathrm{Bd}(\mathcal{D})
(the
N
-representability problem), and in particular show that, in the regime of interest to DMET (many relatively small fragments, so that
L\gg\max_{x}L_{x}
), the two sets are (generically) locally the same.
Therefore, it is natural to assume the local
N
-representability condition:

(A3)

The linear map
\mathrm{Bd}
is surjective from

\mathcal{T}_{D_{0}}\mathcal{D}
to
\mathcal{Y}
,

where
{\mathcal{Y}}
is the vector subspace defined in (
13
).
Indeed,
\mathcal{P}
is a (non-empty, compact, convex) subset of the affine space
P_{0}+{\mathcal{Y}}
and Assumption (A2) implies that
P_{0}\in\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{P}}^{\circ}
, where
\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{P}}^{\circ}
is the interior of
{\mathcal{P}}
in
P_{0}+{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Thus
{\mathcal{Y}}
can be identified with the tangent space at
P_{0}
to the manifold
\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{P}}^{\circ}
.
By the local submersion theorem, this implies that any
P
in the neighborhood of
P_{0}
can be expressed as the block diagonal of a density matrix in the neighborhood of
D_{0}
in
\mathcal{D}
.

Our last assumption is concerned with the response properties of the impurity problems at the non-interacting level.
Consider a self-adjoint perturbation
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}_{\rm sym}
of the one-particle Hamiltonian
h
, non-local but block-diagonal in the fragment decomposition, i.e. such that
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}I_{L}+{\mathcal{Y}}
, and denote by
\widetilde{F}^{\mathrm{HL}}_{h+Y}(D)
the non-interacting high-level map obtained by replacing
h
with
h+Y
(so that
\widetilde{F}^{\mathrm{HL}}_{h}(D)=F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{0}
).
Formally, we have

\widetilde{F}^{\mathrm{HL}}_{h+Y}(D_{0})=P_{0}+RY+o(\lVert Y\rVert),

(30)

with
R:{\mathbb{R}}I_{L}+{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
linear (the fact that
RY\in{\mathcal{Y}}
is due to particle-number conservation).
The map
R
can be interpreted as a non-interacting static 4-point density-density linear response function for frozen impurity spaces.
It follows from Assumption (A1) that constant perturbations do not modify the density matrix:
R(I_{L})=0
.
Our fourth assumption reads:

(A4)

the 4-point linear response function
R:{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
is invertible.

This condition is somewhat reminiscent of the Hohenberg-Kohn theorem from Density Functional Theory.
Together with the local inversion theorem, it implies that, locally around
h
, in the non-interacting case and for frozen impurity spaces
W_{{x},{D_{0}}}
, the high-level map defines a one-to-one correspondence between non-local fragment potentials (up to a constant shift) and fragment density matrices.

Remark 3
.

We will show in Section
7.6.4
that in the case when
N_{f}=L
(one site per fragment), it holds: under Assumptions (A1)-(A2),

(A3) is satisfied

\displaystyle\implies D_{0}\mbox{ is an irreducible matrix}\iff\mbox{(A4) is satisfied}.

On the other hand, numerical simulations indicate that in the general case, Assumption (A4) is not a consequence of Assumptions (A1)-(A3).

We are now in position to state our main results.

Theorem 4

(DMET is well-posed in the perturbative regime)
.

Under assumptions (A1)-(A4),
there exist
0<\widetilde{\alpha}_{+}\leq\alpha_{+}
, and a neighborhood
\Omega
of
D_{0}
in
\mathcal{D}
such that for all
\alpha\in(-\widetilde{\alpha}_{+},\widetilde{\alpha}_{+})
, the fixed-point DMET problem

P^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha}=F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\alpha}(D^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha}),\quad D^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha}=F^{\mathrm{LL}}_{\alpha}(P^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha})

has a unique solution
(D^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha},P^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha})
with
D^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha}\in\Omega
(otherwise stated, the DMET map for
H_{\alpha}
has a unique fixed point
P^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha}
in the neighborhood of
P_{0}
).
In addition, the maps
(-\widetilde{\alpha}_{+},\widetilde{\alpha}_{+})\ni\alpha\mapsto D^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}_{\rm sym}
and
(-\widetilde{\alpha}_{+},\widetilde{\alpha}_{+})\ni\alpha\mapsto P^{\rm DMET}_{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}_{\rm sym}
are real-analytic and such that

D_{0}^{\rm DMET}=D_{0}={\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,0]}(h),\quad P_{0}^{\rm DMET}=P_{0}=\mathrm{Bd}(D_{0}).

As is standard, the first-order perturbation of the exact density matrix is given by the Hartree-Fock method.
DMET is able to reproduce this, and is therefore exact at first order:

Theorem 5

(DMET is exact to first order)
.

Under Assumptions (A1)-(A4) and with the notation of Theorem
4
, it holds

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{\rm DMET}=D_{\alpha}^{\rm exact}+O(\alpha^{2})=D_{\alpha}^{\rm HF}+O(\alpha^{2}),

where
D_{\alpha}^{\rm HF}
is the Hartree-Fock ground-state density matrix for
\widehat{H}_{\alpha}
, which is unique for
\alpha
small enough.

The numerical simulations reported in the next section show that such exactness property is not expected to hold at second order.

In the weakly interacting regime, the solution
D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{DMET}}
to the DMET fixed-point problem is the only physical one because it is the only one laying in the vicinity of
D_{0}
, where the exact ground-state density matrix is known to be by analytic perturbation theory.

Impurity problems and high-level map

5.1
Impurity Hamiltonians

It follows from the considerations in Section
2.2
that if

\forall x\in[\![1,N_{f}]\!],\quad\dim(DX_{x})=\dim((1-D)X_{x})=L_{x}.

the impurity problem is well-defined for each fragment since the maximal rank assumption (
15
) is satisfied for each
X_{x}
.
The next lemma gives useful equivalent characterizations of these conditions.

Let us introduce the matrix

E_{x}:=\textrm{mat}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{at}}}(e_{{\kappa}},\kappa\in\mathcal{I}_{x})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0_{L_{x}^{\prime}\times L_{x}}\\
I_{L_{x}}\\
0_{L_{x}^{\prime\prime}\times L_{x}}\end{array}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L_{x}}\quad\mbox{with }\left\{\begin{matrix}L_{x}^{\prime}:=\sum_{1\leq x^{\prime}<x}L_{x^{\prime}}\\
L_{x}^{\prime\prime}:=\sum_{x<x^{\prime}\leq N_{f}}L_{x^{\prime}}\\
\end{matrix}\right.

(31)

representing the orbitals of fragment
x\in[\![1,N_{f}]\!]
, whose range is
X_{x}
.
We recall that
\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{P}}^{\circ}
denotes the interior of the set
\mathcal{P}=\mathrm{Bd}(\mathrm{CH}({\mathcal{D})})
in the affine space
P_{0}+{\mathcal{Y}}
.

Lemma 6

(Compatibility conditions)
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}
. The following assertions are equivalent:

1.

\mathrm{Bd}(D)\in\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{P}}^{\circ}
;

2.

\forall x\in[\![1,N_{f}]\!],\quad\dim(DX_{x})=\dim((1-D)X_{x})=L_{x}
;

3.

\forall x\in[\![1,N_{f}]\!],\quad 0<E_{x}^{T}DE_{x}<1\quad\mbox{(all the eigenvalues of $E_{x}^{T}DE_{x}$ are in $(0,1)$)}
;

4.

\forall x\in[\![1,N_{f}]\!],\quad E_{x}^{T}DE_{x}\in\mathrm{GL}_{\mathbb{R}}(L_{x})\quad\mbox{and}\quad E_{x}^{T}(1-D)E_{x}\in\mathrm{GL}_{\mathbb{R}}(L_{x})
.

If
D
satisfies these conditions, we say that it is compatible with the fragment decomposition.

It is easily seen that if
D
is compatible with the fragment decomposition, then the column vectors defined by the matrix

C^{x}(D):=\bigg(DE_{x}\left(E_{x}^{T}DE_{x}\right)^{-1/2}\,\bigg|(1-D)E_{x}\left(E_{x}^{T}(1-D)E_{x}\right)^{-1/2}\bigg)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times 2L_{x}}

(32)

form an orthonormal basis of the impurity one-particle state space
W_{{x},{D}}
defined in (
14
).
More precisely, the first
L_{x}
columns of
C^{x}(D)
form an orthonormal basis of
DX_{x}
and its last
L_{x}
columns form an orthonormal basis of
(1-D)X_{x}
. Likewise, the column vectors of the matrix

\widetilde{C}^{x}(D):=\bigg(E_{x}\,\bigg|(1-\Pi_{x})DE_{x}\left(E_{x}^{T}D(1-\Pi_{x})DE_{x}\right)^{-1/2}\bigg)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times 2L_{x}}

(33)

form an orthonormal basis of
X_{x}\oplus(1-\Pi_{x})DX_{x}
.

We denote by
\widehat{a}_{j}^{x}(D)
and
\widehat{a}_{j}^{x}(D)^{\dagger}
,
1\leq j\leq 2L_{x}

the annihilation and creation operators in the basis of the columns
of
C^{x}(D)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{j}^{x}(D)=\sum_{\kappa=1}^{L}(C^{x}(D))_{\kappa j}\widehat{a}_{\kappa},\quad\widehat{a}_{j}^{x}(D)^{\dagger}=\sum_{\kappa=1}^{L}(C^{x}(D))_{\kappa j}\widehat{a}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}.

These operators allow for an explicit form of the impurity Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{{x},{D}}
as follows.

Proposition 7

(Impurity Hamiltonian)
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}
be compatible with the fragment decomposition. The
x
-th impurity Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{{x},{D}}

is the operator on
{\rm Fock}(W_{{x},{D}})
given by

\displaystyle\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{{x},{D}}

\displaystyle=E^{\mathrm{env}}_{x}(D)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2L_{x}}\left[C^{x}(D)^{T}\left(h+J({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D))-K({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D))\right)C^{x}(D)\right]_{ij}\widehat{a}_{i}(D)^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}(D)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{2L_{x}}[V^{x}(D)]_{ijkl}\widehat{a}_{i}(D)^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}(D)^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\ell}(D)\widehat{a}_{k}(D),

(34)

where

•

the Coulomb and exchange matrices
J({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}
and
K({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}
for the
x
-th impurity are constructed from the density matrix

{\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D):=D-DE_{x}(E_{x}^{T}DE_{x})^{-1}E_{x}^{T}D\in{\rm Gr}(N-L_{x},L);

(35)

•

the rank-4 tensor
V^{x}(D)
is given by

[V^{x}(D)]_{ijkl}:=\sum_{\kappa,\lambda,\nu,\xi=1}^{L}V_{\kappa\lambda\nu\xi}[C^{x}(D)]_{\kappa i}[C^{x}(D)]_{\lambda j}[C^{x}(D)]_{\nu k}[C^{x}(D)]_{\xi\ell};

(36)

•

the value of the (irrelevant) constant
E^{\mathrm{env}}_{x}(D)
is given in (
43
).

Note that the matrix
{\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D)
is in fact the one-body density matrix associated with the Slater determinant
\Psi^{0,\mathrm{core}}_{x,D}
(see Section
2.2
).

Domain of the high-level map

A matrix

is in the domain of the high-level map

.

Lemma 8

(Global

1.

More precisely,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bd}(\mathcal{D})=\big\{P\in\mathcal{P}\;\big|\;\forall 0<n<1,\;{\rm dim}({\rm Ker}(\Pi_{1}P\Pi_{1}-n))={\rm dim}({\rm Ker}(\Pi_{2}P\Pi_{2}-(1-n))\big\}.

Definition 9

(Local
N

Let

Let us first show that (A3) implies that

We deduce from the second assertion of Lemma

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,x^{\prime}=1}^{N_{f}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Pi_{x}C^{x}_{0}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(Y-\widetilde{\ell}(Y)\Pi_{x}\right){C^{x}_{0}}\right){C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x}\Pi_{x^{\prime}}C^{x^{\prime}}_{0}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x^{\prime}}^{+}\left({C^{x^{\prime}}_{0}}^{T}\left(Y-\widetilde{\ell}(Y)\Pi_{x^{\prime}}\right){C^{x^{\prime}}_{0}}\right){C^{x^{\prime}}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Pi_{x}C^{x}_{0}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(Y-\widetilde{\ell}(Y)\Pi_{x}\right){C^{x}_{0}}\right){C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x}C^{x}_{0}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(Y-\widetilde{\ell}(Y)\Pi_{x}\right){C^{x}_{0}}\right){C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\mathrm{Tr}\left({\mathfrak{p}}_{x}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(Y-\widetilde{\ell}(Y)\Pi_{x}\right){C^{x}_{0}}\right){\mathfrak{p}}_{x}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(Y-\widetilde{\ell}(Y)\Pi_{x}\right){C^{x}_{0}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\|{\mathfrak{p}}^{x}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(Y-\widetilde{\ell}(Y)\Pi_{x}\right){C^{x}_{0}}\right){\mathfrak{p}}^{x}\|^{2}.

Using (
68
) and (
7.5
)-(
77
), we obtain after straightforward algebraic manipulations that

\displaystyle(RY=0)

\displaystyle\iff\left(\forall 1\leq x\leq N_{f},\;{\mathfrak{p}}^{x}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(Y-\widetilde{\ell}(Y)\Pi_{x}\right){C^{x}_{0}}\right){\mathfrak{p}}^{x}=0\right)

\displaystyle\iff\left(\forall 1\leq x\leq N_{f},\;(1-D_{0,x})^{1/2}\widetilde{N}_{x}(Y)D_{0,x}^{1/2}+D_{0,x}^{1/2}\widetilde{N}_{x}(Y)^{T}(1-D_{0,x})^{1/2}=0\right),

with

\widetilde{N}_{x}(Y):=N\left((1-D_{0,x})^{-1/2}E_{x}^{T}(1-D_{0})YD_{0}E_{x}D_{0,x}^{-1/2}-\widetilde{\ell}(Y)D_{0,x}^{1/2}(1-D_{0,x})^{1/2}\right).

In the case when
N_{f}=L
, we have
L_{x}=1
for all
x
, and thus,
D_{0,x}
and
\widetilde{N}(Y)
are scalar quantities. We then have in this special case by assumption (A2),

\displaystyle(RY=0)

\displaystyle\iff\left(\forall 1\leq x\leq N_{f},\;N_{x}(Y)=0\right)\iff\left(My=\widetilde{\ell}(Y)z\right),

where
y=(Y_{11},\cdots,Y_{LL})^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
,
z=(D_{0,1}(1-D_{0,1}),\cdots,D_{0,L}(1-D_{0,L}))^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
, and
M\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}_{\rm sym}
is the matrix with entries

M_{xx}=[D_{0}]_{xx}-[D_{0}]_{xx}^{2},\quad M_{xx^{\prime}}=-[D_{0}]_{xx^{\prime}}^{2}\;\mbox{if }x\neq x^{\prime}.

Still by Assumption (A2),
\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}z_{x}>0
, and therefore using the fact that
D_{0}
is an orthogonal projector (hence that
\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}[D_{0}]_{x,x^{\prime}}^{2}=[D_{0}^{2}]_{xx}=[D_{0}]_{xx}
), we get

(My=\widetilde{\ell}(Y)z)\implies\left(\widetilde{\ell}(Y)=\frac{\sum_{x,x^{\prime}=1}^{N_{f}}M_{x,x^{\prime}}y_{x^{\prime}}}{\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}z_{x}}=\frac{\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}[D_{0}]_{x,x}y_{x}-\sum_{x,x^{\prime}=1}^{N_{f}}[D_{0}]_{x,x^{\prime}}^{2}y_{x^{\prime}}}{\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}z_{x}}=0\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle(RY=0)

\displaystyle\iff\left(My=0\right).

The matrix
M
is hermitian, diagonal dominant with positive diagonal elements and non-positive off-diagonal elements, and such that

\forall 1\leq x\leq N_{f},\quad M_{xx}=-\sum_{x^{\prime}\neq x}M_{xx^{\prime}}.

Therefore the kernel of
M
is reduced to
{\mathbb{R}}(1,\cdots,1)^{T}
if and only if
M
is irreducible. Besides, we see from the expressions of the coefficients of
M
and Assumption (A2) that
M
is irreducible if and only if
D_{0}
is irreducible. We conclude that
R
is injective, hence bijective, if and only if
D_{0}
is irreducible.

7.7.1
Perturbation expansion in the Fock space

This calculation is classical in the physics and chemistry literature, but we report it here for the sake of completeness. Consider a family of Hamiltonians
(\widehat{H}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}
of the form

\widehat{H}_{\alpha}:=\widehat{H}_{0}+\alpha(\widehat{W}_{1}+\widehat{W}_{2})

on the real Fock space
{\rm Fock}({\mathbb{R}}^{N_{b}})
where

\widehat{H}_{0}:=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N_{b}}[h_{0}]_{mn}c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{W}_{1}:=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N_{b}}[W_{1}]_{mn}c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}

are one-body Hamiltonians and

\widehat{W}_{2}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,n,p,q,=1}^{N_{b}}[W_{2}]_{mnpq}c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{q}c_{p}

is a two-body Hamiltonian.

Let us provisionally assume that
h_{0}
is diagonal, and more precisely that

h_{0}=\mbox{diag}(\varepsilon_{1}^{0},\cdots,\varepsilon_{N_{b}}^{0})\quad\mbox{with}\quad\varepsilon_{1}^{0}\leq\cdots\leq\varepsilon_{\mathcal{N}}^{0}<0<\varepsilon_{{\mathcal{N}}+1}^{0}\leq\cdots\varepsilon_{N_{b}}^{0}.

This amounts to working in a molecular orbital basis set of the unperturbed one-body Hamiltonian
h_{0}
and assuming that the Fermi level
\epsilon_{\rm F}
for having
\mathcal{N}
particles in the ground state can be chosen equal to zero.
The ground state
\Psi_{0}
of
\widetilde{H}_{0}
in the
{\mathcal{N}}
-particle sector then is unique and so is the one of
\widetilde{H}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha
small by perturbation theory. We have

\Psi_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\mathcal{N}}!}}c_{\mathcal{N}}^{\dagger}\cdots c_{1}^{\dagger}|0\rangle,\quad E_{0}:=\langle\Psi_{0}|\widehat{H}_{0}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\varepsilon_{i}^{0}.

Denoting by
d(\alpha)
the ground-state one-body reduced density matrix of
\widehat{H}_{\alpha}
, the map
\alpha\mapsto d(\alpha)
is real-analytic in the neighborhood of
0
and

d(\alpha)=d_{0}+\alpha d_{1}+O(\alpha^{2})\quad\mbox{with}\quad d_{0}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{\mathcal{N}}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right).

In addition, we have

[d_{1}]_{mn}=\langle\Psi_{1}|c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}|\Psi_{0}\rangle+\langle\Psi_{0}|c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}|\Psi_{1}\rangle,

where
\Psi_{1}
is the first-order perturbation of the ground-state wave-function
\Psi_{0}
, solution to

(\widehat{H}_{0}-E_{0})\Psi_{1}=-\Pi_{\Psi_{0}^{\perp}}\left((\widehat{W}_{1}+\widehat{W}_{2})\Psi_{0}\right),\qquad\Psi_{1}\in\Psi_{0}^{\perp}.

For
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq{\mathcal{N}}
(occupied orbitals) and
m+1\leq a_{1}<\cdots<a_{r}\leq N_{b}
(virtual orbitals), we set

\Phi_{0}^{0}:=\Psi_{0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}=c_{a_{r}}^{\dagger}\cdots c_{a_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{i_{1}}\cdots c_{i_{r}}\Phi_{0}^{0}.

The
\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}
’s (
0\leq r\leq\min({\mathcal{N}},N_{b}-{\mathcal{N}})
,
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq{\mathcal{N}}
,
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{r}\leq N_{b}
, form an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of the restriction of
\widehat{H}_{0}
to the
\mathcal{N}
-particle sector and it holds

\widehat{H}_{0}\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}=E_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad E_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}=E_{0}+\sum_{s=1}^{r}\varepsilon_{a_{s}}-\sum_{s=1}^{r}\varepsilon_{i_{s}}.

We thus obtain the sum-over-state formula

\Psi_{1}=-\sum_{1\leq r\leq\min({\mathcal{N}},N_{b}-{\mathcal{N}})}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq{\mathcal{N}}}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}+1\leq a_{1}<\cdots<a_{r}\leq N_{b}}\frac{\langle\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}|\widehat{W}_{1}+\widehat{W}_{2}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle}{E_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}-E_{0}}\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}},

yielding

\displaystyle[d_{1}]_{mn}=-\sum_{1\leq r\leq\min({\mathcal{N}},N_{b}-{\mathcal{N}})}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq{\mathcal{N}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}+1\leq a_{1}<\cdots<a_{r}\leq N_{b}}\frac{\langle\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}|\widehat{W}_{1}+\widehat{W}_{2}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle}{E_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}-E_{0}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\langle\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}|c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle+\langle\Phi_{0}^{0}|c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}|\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}\rangle\right).

Since
\langle\Phi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{r}}|a_{m}^{\dagger}a_{n}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle=0
if
r\geq 2
, and

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{i}^{a}|c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,i}\delta_{m,a},

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{i}^{a}|c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{q}c_{p}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\delta_{m,q}\delta_{n,i}\delta_{p,a}\delta_{q\leq{\mathcal{N}}}+\delta_{m,p}\delta_{n,i}\delta_{q,a}\delta_{p\leq{\mathcal{N}}}+\delta_{m,i}\delta_{n,q}\delta_{p,a}\delta_{q\leq{\mathcal{N}}}-\delta_{m,i}\delta_{n,p}\delta_{q,a}\delta_{p\leq{\mathcal{N}}},

this expression reduces to

\displaystyle[d_{1}]_{mn}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\sum_{a={\mathcal{N}}+1}^{N_{b}}\frac{\langle\Phi_{i}^{a}|\widehat{W}_{1}+\widehat{W}_{2}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle}{\varepsilon_{a}^{0}-\varepsilon_{i}^{0}}\left(\delta_{n=i}\delta_{m=a}+\delta_{m=i}\delta_{n=a}\right).

We obtain that
d_{1}
is of the form

d_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&d_{1}^{+-}\\
{d_{1}^{+-}}^{T}&0\end{array}\right)\quad\mbox{with}\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq{\mathcal{N}}<{\mathcal{N}}+1\leq a\leq N_{b},\quad[d_{1}]_{ai}=\frac{\langle\Phi_{i}^{a}|\widehat{W}_{1}+\widehat{W}_{2}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle}{\varepsilon_{a}^{0}-\varepsilon_{i}^{0}}.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle[d_{1}]_{ai}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N_{b}}[W_{1}]_{mn}\frac{\langle\Phi_{i}^{a}|c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle}{\varepsilon_{a}^{0}-\varepsilon_{i}^{0}}+\sum_{m,n,p,q=1}^{N_{b}}[W_{2}]_{mnpq}\frac{\langle\Phi_{i}^{a}|c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}^{\dagger}c_{q}c_{p}|\Phi_{0}^{0}\rangle}{\varepsilon_{a}^{0}-\varepsilon_{i}^{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{[W_{1}+J_{W_{2}}(d_{0})-K_{W_{2}}(d_{0})]_{ai}}{\varepsilon_{a}^{0}-\varepsilon_{i}^{0}},

where the direct and exchange operators are respectively given by

[J_{W_{2}}(d)]_{mn}:=\sum_{p,q=1}^{N_{b}}[W_{2}]_{npmq}d_{pq}\quad\mbox{and}\quad[K_{W_{2}}(d)]_{mn}:=\sum_{p,q=1}^{N_{b}}[W_{2}]_{npqm}d_{pq}.

Introducing the linear response operator
\mathfrak{L}^{+}_{h_{0}}
such that

{\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,\epsilon_{\rm F}]}(h_{0}+W)=\underbrace{{\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,\epsilon_{\rm F}]}(h_{0}+W)}_{=d_{0}}-\mathfrak{L}^{+}_{h_{0}}W+O(\|W\|),

we finally obtain

d_{1}=-\mathfrak{L}^{+}_{h_{0}}\left(W_{1}+J_{W_{2}}(d_{0})-K_{W_{2}}(d_{0})\right),

(100)

this formula remaining valid in the general case when
h_{0}
is not
a priori
diagonal and
\epsilon_{\rm F}
not
a priori
equal to zero.

7.7.2
Perturbation expansion of the DMET ground-state

Under Assumption (A1), the Hartree-Fock problem

\mathop{\rm argmin}_{D\in\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{HF}}_{\alpha}(D)

has a unique minimizer
D^{\rm HF}(\alpha)
for
\alpha
small enough and the map
\alpha\mapsto D^{\rm HF}(\alpha)
is real-analytic in the neighborhood of
0
. This results from a straightforward application of nonlinear perturbation theory, which we do not detail here for the sake of brevity. We set
P^{\rm HF}(\alpha):={\rm Bd}(D^{\rm HF}(\alpha))
, and

\displaystyle D_{1}^{\rm exact}:=\frac{dD^{\rm exact}}{d\alpha}(0),\qquad D_{1}^{\rm HF}:=\frac{dD^{\rm HF}}{d\alpha}(0),\qquad D_{1}^{\rm DMET}:=\frac{dD^{\rm DMET}}{d\alpha}(0),

\displaystyle P_{1}^{\rm exact}:=\frac{dP^{\rm exact}}{d\alpha}(0),\qquad P_{1}^{\rm HF}:=\frac{dP^{\rm HF}}{d\alpha}(0),\qquad P_{1}^{\rm DMET}:=\frac{dP^{\rm DMET}}{d\alpha}(0).

We are going to prove that the above first three matrices on the one hand, and the last three ones on the other hand are equal in
T_{D_{0}}\mathcal{D}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
respectively.

First, we deduce from (
100
) applied with
N_{b}=L
,
\epsilon_{\rm F}=0
,
h_{0}=h
,
W_{1}=0
,
W_{2}=v
, that

D_{1}^{\rm exact}=-\mathfrak{L}^{+}_{h}\left(J(D_{0})-K(D_{0})\right),

where
J
and
K
are the direct and exchange operators for the two-body interaction potential
\widehat{V}
introduced in (
25
).

Next, by differentiating the self-consistent equation

D^{\rm HF}(\alpha)={\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,0]}\left(h^{\rm MF}(\alpha,D^{\rm HF}(\alpha))\right),

where

h^{\rm MF}(\alpha,D)=h+\alpha\left(J(D)-K(D)\right)

is the Fock Hamiltonian for the interaction parameter
\alpha
, we get

D^{\rm HF}_{1}=-\mathfrak{L}^{+}_{h}\left(J(D_{0})-K(D_{0})\right).

Hence

D^{\rm HF}_{1}=D_{1}^{\rm exact}\quad\mbox{and}\quad P^{\rm HF}_{1}=\mathrm{Bd}(D^{\rm HF}_{1})=\mathrm{Bd}(D_{1}^{\rm exact})=P_{1}^{\rm exact}.

Let us now show that
P^{\rm DMET}_{1}=P^{\rm HF}_{1}
. For convenience, we will use the following notation

\displaystyle F^{\mathrm{LL}}(\alpha,P):=F^{\mathrm{LL}}_{\alpha}(P),\qquad F^{\mathrm{HL}}(\alpha,D)=F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\alpha}(D),

\displaystyle F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(\alpha,D):=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\Pi_{x}C^{x}(D){\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,0]}\left(C^{x}(D)^{T}\left(h^{\rm MF}(\alpha,D)-\mu^{\rm HF}(\alpha,D)\Pi_{x}\right)C^{x}(D)\right)C^{x}(D)^{T}\Pi_{x},

where
\mu^{\rm HF}(\alpha,D)\in{\mathbb{R}}
is the Lagrange parameter of the charge conservation constraint. The map
F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(\alpha,D)
is the high-level Hartree-Fock map for the interacting parameter
\alpha
, introduced in Remark
2
for
\alpha=1
.

We know from Theorem
4
that for all
\alpha
small enough

F^{\mathrm{HL}}\left(\alpha,F^{\mathrm{LL}}\left(\alpha,P^{\rm DMET}(\alpha)\right)\right)=P^{\rm DMET}(\alpha).

Taking the derivative at
\alpha=0
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D_{0})+\partial_{D}F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D_{0})\left(\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{LL}}(0,P_{0})+\partial_{P}F^{\mathrm{LL}}(0,P_{0})P^{\rm DMET}_{1}\right)=P^{\rm DMET}_{1}.

(101)

The same arguments as in the proof of Proposition
1
allow one to show that for all
\alpha
small enough

F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}\left(\alpha,F^{\mathrm{LL}}\left(\alpha,P^{\rm HF}(\alpha)\right)\right)=P^{\rm HF}(\alpha),

yielding

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(0,D_{0})+\partial_{D}F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(0,D_{0})\left(\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{LL}}(0,P_{0})+\partial_{P}F^{\mathrm{LL}}(0,P_{0})P^{\rm HF}_{1}\right)=P^{\rm HF}_{1}.

(102)

Since
F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(0,D)=F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D)
for all
D
in the neighborhood of
D_{0}
, we have

\partial_{P}F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(0,D_{0})=\partial_{P}F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D_{0}).

Using (
89
) and the invertibility of
d_{P}\Phi(0,P_{0})
established in Section
7.6.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle P^{\rm DMET}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\left(d_{P}\Phi(0,P_{0})\right)^{-1}\left(\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D_{0})+\partial_{D}F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D_{0})\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{LL}}(0,P_{0})\right),

(103)

\displaystyle P^{\rm HF}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\left(d_{P}\Phi(0,P_{0})\right)^{-1}\left(\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(0,D_{0})+\partial_{D}F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D_{0})\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{LL}}(0,P_{0})\right).

(104)

Let us show that
\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D_{0})=\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(0,D_{0})
. On the one hand, we have

\displaystyle F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(\alpha,D_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\Pi_{x}C^{x}_{0}{\mathds{1}}_{(-\infty,0]}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(h+\alpha\left(J(D_{0})-K(D_{0})\right)-\mu_{\rm HF}(\alpha,D_{0})\Pi_{x}\right)C^{x}_{0}\right){C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x},

and therefore

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(0,D_{0})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\Pi_{x}C^{x}_{0}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(J(D_{0})-K(D_{0})\right){C^{x}_{0}}-\partial\mu_{\rm HF}(0,D_{0}){\mathfrak{p}}^{x}\right){C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x}.

(105)

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle F^{\mathrm{HL}}(\alpha,D_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\Pi_{x}C^{x}_{0}D_{x,D_{0}}^{\rm imp}(\alpha){C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x},

where
D_{x,D_{0}}^{\rm imp}(\alpha)
is the ground-state one-body reduced density matrix in the basis of
Y_{x,D_{0}}
defined by
C^{x}_{0}
of the impurity Hamiltonian (see Proposition
7
)

\displaystyle\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{{x},{D_{0}}}(\alpha)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{2L_{x}}\left[{C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(h+\alpha(J({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0}))-K({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0})))\right)C^{x}_{0}\right]_{ij}\widehat{a}_{i}(D_{0})^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}(D_{0})

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{2L_{x}}[V^{x}(D_{0})]_{ijkl}\widehat{a}_{i}(D_{0})^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}(D_{0})^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\ell}(D_{0})\widehat{a}_{k}(D_{0})

\displaystyle-\mu(\alpha)\sum_{i,j=1}^{2L_{x}}\left[{C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x}C^{x}_{0}\right]_{ij}\widehat{a}_{i}(D_{0})^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}(D_{0}),

where
\mu(\alpha)
is the Lagrange multiplier of the charge neutrality constraint and where we have discarded the irrelevant constant
E^{\rm env}_{x}(D_{0})
. Using the notation introduced in (
7.5
), this Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\widehat{H}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{{x},{D_{0}}}(\alpha)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{2L_{x}}[{\mathfrak{h}}^{x}]_{ij}\widehat{a}_{i}(D_{0})^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}(D_{0})

\displaystyle+\alpha\bigg(\sum_{i,j=1}^{2L_{x}}\left[{C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(J({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0}))-K({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0}))\right)C^{x}_{0}\right]_{ij}\widehat{a}_{i}(D_{0})^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}(D_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{2L_{x}}[V^{x}(D_{0})]_{ijkl}\widehat{a}_{i}(D_{0})^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}(D_{0})^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\ell}(D_{0})\widehat{a}_{k}(D_{0})\bigg)

\displaystyle-\mu(\alpha)\sum_{i,j=1}^{2L_{x}}\left[{C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x}C^{x}_{0}\right]_{ij}\widehat{a}_{i}(D_{0})^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{j}(D_{0}).

We have

D_{x,D_{0}}^{\rm imp}(0)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{L_{x}}&0\\

Since

and

is real-analytic, we can easily adapt the analysis done in the previous section to the case when

N_{b}=2L_{x},\quad h_{0}={\mathfrak{h}}^{x},\quad W_{1}={C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(J({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0}))-K({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0}))-\mu^{\prime}(0)\Pi_{x}\right)C^{x}_{0},\quad W_{2}=V^{x}(D_{0}),

and infer that

\displaystyle D_{x,D_{0}}^{\rm imp}(\alpha)=D_{x,D_{0}}^{\rm imp}(0)-{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\bigg(

\displaystyle{C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(J({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0}))-K({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0}))-\mu^{\prime}(0)\Pi_{x}\right)C^{x}_{0}

\displaystyle+J_{V^{x}(D_{0})}(D_{x,D_{0}}^{\rm imp}(0))-K_{V^{x}(D_{0})}(D_{x,D_{0}}^{\rm imp}(0))\bigg)+O(\alpha^{2}),

where
{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}
is the linear response operator introduced in (
72
). Observing that

{C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(J({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0}))-K({\mathfrak{D}}^{x}(D_{0}))\right)C^{x}_{0}+J_{V^{x}(D_{0})}(D_{x,D_{0}}^{\rm imp}(0))-K_{V^{x}(D_{0})}(D_{x,D_{0}}^{\rm imp}(0))={C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(J(D_{0})-K(D_{0})\right)C^{x}_{0},

we obtain that

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D_{0})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x=1}^{N_{f}}\Pi_{x}C^{x}_{0}{\mathfrak{L}}_{x}^{+}\left({C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\left(J(D_{0})-K(D_{0})\right)C^{x}_{0}-\mu^{\prime}(0){\mathfrak{p}}^{x}\right){C^{x}_{0}}^{T}\Pi_{x}.

(106)

Since the roles of the scalars
\partial_{\alpha}\mu(0,D_{0})
in (
105
) and
\mu^{\prime}(0)
in (
106
) are simply to ensure charge neutrality, these two scalars are the same. It follows that
\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}_{\rm HF}(0,D_{0})=\partial_{\alpha}F^{\mathrm{HL}}(0,D_{0})
, which allows us to deduce from (
103
)-(
104
) that
P_{1}^{\rm DMET}=P_{1}^{\rm HF}
. Finally, we obtain that
D_{1}^{\rm DMET}=D_{1}^{\rm HF}
by differentiating the relations

D^{\rm DMET}(\alpha)=F^{\mathrm{LL}}(\alpha,P^{\rm DMET}(\alpha))\quad\mbox{and}\quad D^{\rm HF}(\alpha)=F^{\mathrm{LL}}(\alpha,P^{\rm HF}(\alpha)),

and using the fact that
P_{1}^{\rm DMET}=P_{1}^{\rm HF}
.
