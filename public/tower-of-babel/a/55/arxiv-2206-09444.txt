Bayesian non-conjugate regression via variational message passing
Cristian Castiglione, Mauro Bernardi

Abstract
Variational inference is a popular method for approximating the posterior distribution of hierarchical Bayesian models. It is well-recognized in the literature that the choice of the approximation family and the regularity properties of the posterior strongly influence the efficiency and accuracy of variational methods. While model-specific conjugate approximations offer simplicity, they often converge slowly and may yield poor approximations. Non-conjugate approximations instead are more flexible but typically require the calculation of expensive multidimensional integrals. This study focuses on Bayesian regression models that use possibly non-differentiable loss functions to measure prediction misfit. The data behavior is modeled using a linear predictor, potentially transformed using a bijective link function. Examples include generalized linear models, mixed additive models, support vector machines, and quantile regression. To address the limitations of non-conjugate settings, the study proposes an efficient non-conjugate variational message passing method for approximate posterior inference, which only requires the calculation of univariate numerical integrals when analytical solutions are not available. The approach does not require differentiability, conjugacy, or model-specific data-augmentation strategies, thereby naturally extending to models with non-conjugate likelihood functions. Additionally, a stochastic implementation is provided to handle large-scale data problems. The proposed method’s performances are evaluated through extensive simulations and real data examples. Overall, the results highlight the effectiveness of the proposed variational message passing method, demonstrating its computational efficiency and approximation accuracy as an alternative to existing methods in Bayesian inference for regression models. Keywords: General Bayesian inference; Mixed regression models; Non-conjugate models; Variational message passing.



1
Introduction

The increasing prevalence of big volume and velocity data, eventually coming from different data sources, entails a great opportunity but also a major challenge of modern data analysis and, in particular, of Bayesian statistics.
The computational burden required by Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation
(
Gelman et al., 2013
)
, that is the state-of-the-art approach to Bayesian inference, is often not compatible with time constraints and memory limits.
Therefore, in the last two decades many efforts have been spent to develop alternative estimation methods not involving posterior simulation.
In this context, optimization-based algorithms play an important role because of their ability to provide a reasonably good approximation of the posterior, while keeping a high level of efficiency.
Within this family, two important examples are the Laplace approximation
(
Raftery, 1996
)
, along with its integrated nested generalization
(
Rue et al., 2009
)
, and the variational inference approach, which includes, among others, local variational approximation
(
Jaakkola and Jordan, 2000
)
, mean field variational Bayes
(
Ormerod and Wand, 2010
;
Blei et al., 2017
)
, expectation propagation
(
Minka, 2013
;
Bishop, 2006
, Chapter 10)
, stochastic variational inference
(
Hoffman et al., 2013
)
, black-box variational inference
(
Ranganath et al., 2014
;
Kucukelbir et al., 2017
;
Ong et al., 2018
)
and natural gradient variational inference
(
Khan and Lin, 2017
;
Khan and Nielsen, 2018
)
.
All of these are mainly concerned with the estimation of hierarchical Bayesian models with a regular likelihood function, often belonging to the exponential family.
For instance, both Laplace approximation and stochastic variational inference require for the log-likelihood function to be differentiable with continuity from one to three times, depending on the implementation.
On the other hand, mean field variational Bayes and expectation propagation are employed when the hierarchical model can be described through a Bayesian factor graph with locally conjugate nodes, like for the Gibbs sampling algorithm
(
Gelman et al., 2013
)
.

The joint lack of smoothness and conjugacy constitutes one of the main issues when it comes to approximating a posterior density.
In such cases, well-designed data-augmentation strategies might help in representing an
elaborate
distribution
(
Wand et al., 2011
)
as the marginal law of an enlarged joint model by introducing a set of latent working variables.
This tool, firstly introduced for motivating the expectation-maximization algorithm
(
Dempster et al., 1977
)
, usually permits to restore the regularity in the augmented parameter space, drastically simplifying the calculations.
Though, a convenient stochastic representation is not always available and sometimes can lead to important computational drawbacks, mainly concerned with a slowdown of the convergence speed and a worsening of the approximation quality in the original parameter space.
See, for instance,
Lewandowski et al., 2010
,
Neville et al., 2014
,
Duan et al., 2018
and
Johndrow et al., 2019
for an up-to-date discussion on this topic.
Furthermore, the lack of general recipes for extending model-specific augmentation techniques strongly narrows the range of applications of such an approach for solving general inferential problems.
A classical example is given by Binomial regression models, for which data-augmented representations are available only under probit and logit links, while they are not for, e.g., Cauchy and complementary log-log links.

Many of these issues are amplified when the parameters of interest do not index a known family of probability distributions but are defined according to a minimum risk criterion and are linked to the data through a loss function.
In these cases, the minimal regularity conditions for an estimation problem to be well-defined may not be satisfied, as it happens for support vector machines
(
Vapnik, 1998
)
, quantile and expectile regression
(
Koenker, 2005
)
.

The present work aims to introduce a unified, yet simple to implement, variational methodology for approximating the general posterior distribution of Bayesian mixed regression models
(
Bissiri et al., 2016
)
.
Specifically, we here consider regression and classification models predicting the response variable through a linear predictor, eventually transformed using a bijective link, and where the prediction misfit is measured by either a loss function or a pseudo-likelihood.
Such a formulation includes as special cases generalized linear models and generalized estimating equations
(
McCullagh and Nelder, 1989
, see, e.g.,)
.
We deserve particular attention to loss functions characterized by non-regular, i.e. non-differentiable and non-conjugate, behavior and for which standard Bayesian approximation techniques can not be directly employed or may suffer severe drawbacks, such as slow convergence or poor approximation quality.

Starting from a general formulation of non-conjugate variational message passing
(
Knowles and Minka, 2011
;
Wand, 2014
;
Rohde and Wand, 2016
)
, we develop both deterministic and stochastic optimization algorithms to perform variational inference for linear mixed models.
Doing this, we obtain numerical methods which mimic, in their formulation, the penalized reweighted least squares algorithm for generalized linear models
(
McCullagh and Nelder, 1989
)
, where the derivatives of the likelihood with respect to the linear predictor are replaced by their variational expectations.
This formulation has two main advantages: it only involves the calculation of univariate integrals, instead of multidimensional ones, and, in addition, it easily applies to non-differentiable losses.
Indeed, the variational expectation of the loss derivatives is well-defined and easy to compute even when the proper loss derivatives are not.
Moreover, the proposed method theoretically outperforms, in Kullback-Leibler divergence, the state-of-the-art mean field variational Bayes approximations based on conjugate data-augmented representations while maintaining the same level of computational efficiency.

In this framework, an additional benefit of the proposed approach is to combine the efficiency and modularity of mean field variational Bayes
(
Ormerod and Wand, 2010
;
Blei et al., 2017
)
with the flexibility of Gaussian variational approximations
(
Wand, 2014
)
to deal with parameters not having a conjugate distribution.
Previously similar strategies, such as semiparametric variational Bayes
(
Rohde and Wand, 2016
)
, or natural gradient approach
(
Khan and Lin, 2017
;
Khan and Nielsen, 2018
)
, has been used for the estimation of Bayesian generalized linear mixed models
(
Ormerod and Wand, 2012
;
Tan and Nott, 2013
)
, semiparametric models for overdispersed count data
(
Luts and Wand, 2015
)
, semiparametric heteroscedastic regression
(
Menictas and Wand, 2015
)
and Gaussian process regression
(
Opper and Archambeau, 2009
;
Khan and Lin, 2017
)
.

The article is organized as follows.
Section
2
reviews some fundamental concepts of mean field variational inference and non-conjugate variational message passing.
Section
3
describes the proposed message passing methods for linear mixed models, characterizes their properties and presents several examples for both regular and non-regular models, including quantile regression and support vector machines.
Section
4
provide a theoretical connection between our approach and augmented-data mean field variational Bayes.
Section
5
presents an extensive simulation study designed to evaluate the quality and the efficiency the proposed approximation with respect to gold standard methods in the literature.
Section
6
discuss four real-data examples concerned with electric power consumption in US, air quality measurements in Beijing, a bank marketing study in Portugal and a forest cover-type analysis in Colorado.
Section
7
is devoted to a closing discussion.

2
Variational learning

Let
\pi(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
be a generic statistical model for the observed data vector
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\top}
and the unknown parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
, which has prior distribution
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
We here consider either proper generative models, where
\pi(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\propto\exp\{\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{y})\}
is a genuine likelihood function, and risk based models, where
\pi(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\propto\exp\{-nR_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/\phi\}
denotes a
pseudo-likelihood
function with empirical risk
R_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(y_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})/n
and loss function
L(y,\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
The additional parameter
1/\phi>0
, called
temperature
in the generalized Bayesian inference literature
(
Bissiri et al., 2016
)
, is used to balance the weight of the risk function relative to the prior log-density.
Classical and generalized Bayesian inference
(
Germain et al., 2016
;
Bissiri et al., 2016
)
on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
may be performed by applying the Bayes formula and studying the properties of the (generalized) posterior distribution
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})=\pi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})/\pi(\mathbf{y})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\pi(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})/\pi(\mathbf{y})
, which is available up to the normalization constant
\pi(\mathbf{y})=\int_{\Theta}\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\pi(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\textrm{{d}}\boldsymbol{\theta}
, called
marginal likelihood
or
model evidence
.

Typically, the explicit calculation of
\pi(\mathbf{y})
is infeasible for any non-trivial model, and it must be approximated via numerical methods.
Variational inference
(
Bishop, 2006
;
Ormerod and Wand, 2010
;
Blei et al., 2017
)
circumvents such a problem by replacing the true posterior distribution
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
with a more tractable density function
q^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}
, which minimizes some appropriate divergence measure.
The most common choice in the literature is
variational Bayes
inference, which search for the optimal approximation
q^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}
by minimizing the
Kullback-Leibler divergence

\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})/\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}\big]=\int_{\Theta}q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\log\bigg\{\frac{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})}\bigg\}\,\textrm{{d}}\boldsymbol{\theta},

(1)

or equivalently, by maximizing the
evidence lower bound
,

\underline{\ell}\{\mathbf{y};q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log\{\pi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})/q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}\big]=\log\pi(\mathbf{y})-\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}\leq\log\pi(\mathbf{y}),

(2)

where
\mathbb{E}_{q}(\cdot)
denotes the expectation calculated with respect to the variational density
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
If
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{Q}
, the solution
q^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
is the unique minimizer of (
1
), and indeed
\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}=0
if and only if
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
almost everywhere
(
Kullback and Leibler, 1951
)
.

The key ingredient in the variational optimization problem (
1
), or equivalently (
2
), is the choice of the searching space
\mathcal{Q}
, which determines the fundamental restrictions on
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
to ensure the numerical tractability of the approximation.
Here, we consider the general set of restrictions

q(\boldsymbol{\theta})=q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})\times\cdots\times q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{H}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})=\exp\big\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{\top}s(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})-b(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h})+c(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\big\},

(3)

that are:
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
factorizes according to a prespecified partition
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{H}})
(
mean field assumption
;
Ormerod, 2011
), and each density
q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
,
h=1,\dots,H
, belongs in a compoent-specific exponential family indexed by the variational parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}
(
parametric assumption
;
Blei et al., 2017
).
We recall that any identifiable exponential family is uniquely determined by its
canonical parameter

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}
,
natural statistics

s(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
,
log-partition function

b(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h})
and
base measure

c(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
.

The maximization of the evidence lower bound (
2
) under restrictions (
3
) is the main challenge in variational inference
(
Rohde and Wand, 2016
)
, for which many deterministic and stochastic optimization methods have been developed.
Some examples are exact coordinate ascent
(
Ormerod and Wand, 2010
;
Wand et al., 2011
;
Blei et al., 2017
)
, quasi-Newton methods
(
Rohde and Wand, 2016
;
Nocedal and Wright, 2006
)
, natural conjugate gradient
(
Honkela et al., 2008
;
Honkela et al., 2010
)
, stochastic variational inference
(
Hoffman et al., 2013
)
and black-box variational inference
(
Ranganath et al., 2014
;
Kucukelbir et al., 2017
)
.
A convenient alternative, specifically tailored for exponential family approximations, is provided by non-conjugate variational message passing
(
Knowles and Minka, 2011
)
, which consists in the application of the iterative fixed-point updating

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\big[\mathbf{A}_{h}^{(t)}\big]^{-1}\mathbf{b}_{h}^{(t)},\qquad h=1,\dots,H,

(4)

where
t
denotes the iteration counter,
\mathbf{A}^{(t)}=\mathbb{E}_{q}^{(t)}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}}^{2}\log q^{(t)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
is the Fisher information matrix relative to
q^{(t)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
, and
\mathbf{b}_{h}^{(t)}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}}\,\mathbb{E}_{q}^{(t)}\{\log\pi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
is the gradient of the expected log-posterior density.
This approach is also known as
natural gradient
method
(
Khan and Lin, 2017
;
Khan and Nielsen, 2018
)
, since it updates the current estimate of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}
following the direction of the gradient
\mathbf{b}_{h}^{(t)}
adjusted for the geometry of the variational parameter space, namely its metric tensor
\mathbf{A}_{h}^{(t)}

(
Amari, 1998
)
.

As proved by
Hoffman et al., 2013
,
Tan and Nott, 2013
,
Wand, 2014
and
Khan and Lin, 2017
, in case of (conditionally) conjugate exponential prior distributions, the natural gradient iteration (
4
) is equivalent to updating the
h
th variational distribution using the exact mean field solution
q^{(t+1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\propto\exp\big[\,\mathbb{E}_{-\theta_{h}}^{(t)}\{\log\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}|\textup{rest})\}\big]
, where
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}|\textup{rest})
is the full-conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
and
\mathbb{E}_{-\theta_{h}}^{(t)}(\cdot)
denotes the expectation taken with respect to the density
q^{(t)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/q^{(t)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})=\prod_{k\neq h}q^{(t)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
.
Equation (
4
) thus provides a general scheme for conjugate and non-conjugate variational learning in complex Bayesian models
(
Khan and Rue, 2021
)
.

In case of multivariate Gaussian variational approximation
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\sim_{q}\textrm{{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h})
, the canonical parameter
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}
may be written as a bijective transformazion of the variational mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}
and variance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}
, that is:
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h1}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h2}^{\top})^{\top}
, with
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2h}=-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}^{-1}/2
.
Then, following
Wand, 2014
, the optimal variational message passing update for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}
may be written as

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h1}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\mathbf{g}_{h}^{(t)}-\mathbf{H}_{h}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}^{(t)},\qquad\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h2}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\mathbf{H}_{h}^{(t)}/2.

(5)

Or, equivalently, in the mean-variance parametrization, we have

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}^{(t)}-\big[\mathbf{H}_{h}^{(t)}\big]^{-1}\mathbf{g}_{h}^{(t)},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow-\big[\mathbf{H}_{h}^{(t)}\big]^{-1},

(6)

where
\mathbf{g}_{h}^{(t)}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}}\mathbb{E}_{q}^{(t)}\{\log\pi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
and
\mathbf{H}_{h}^{(t)}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{q}^{(t)}\{\log\pi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
.

Khan and Lin, 2017
and
Khan and Nielsen, 2018
then considered a stochastic version of the message passing (
4
) in order to improve the scalability of the algorithm in massive data problems.
The resulting optimization schedule cycles over the Robbins-Monro
(
Robbins and Monro, 1951
)
update

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow(1-\rho_{t})\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(t)}+\rho_{t}\big[\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{h}^{(t)}\big]^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{h}^{(t)},

(7)

where
\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{h}^{(t)}
and
\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{h}^{(t)}
are unbiased stochastic estimates of
\mathbf{A}^{(t)}
and
\mathbf{b}^{(t)}
, often obtained using minibatch samples, while
\rho_{t}
is a deterministic learning rate parameter satisfying the Robbins-Monro convergence conditions
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\rho_{t}=\infty
and
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\rho_{t}^{2}<\infty

(
Robbins and Monro, 1951
)
.
Under Gaussian approximations, such an optimization take the simplified form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h1}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow(1-\rho_{t})\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h1}^{(t)}+\rho_{t}\big[\,\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{h}^{(t)}-\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{h}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}^{(t)}\,\big],\qquad\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h2}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow(1-\rho_{t})\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{h2}^{(t)}+\rho_{t}\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{h}^{(t)}/2,

(8)

then,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h}^{(t+1)}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h}^{(t+1)}
may be simply recovered by reparametrization.

A major challenge in the practical implementation of variational message passing algorithms is the calculation of the expected value
\mathbb{E}_{q}^{(t)}\{\log\pi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
along with its derivatives.
For twice differentiable log-posterior, Bonnet’s and Price’s theorems
(
Bonnet, 1964
;
Price, 1958
)
allow us to differentiate under integral sign, obtaining
\mathbf{g}_{h}^{(t)}=\mathbb{E}_{q}^{(t)}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}}\log\pi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
and
\mathbf{H}_{h}^{(t)}=\mathbb{E}_{q}^{(t)}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}}^{2}\log\pi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
.
Whenever such expectations are not available in closed form, Monte Carlo integration may be a practical solution, which anyway may lead to numerical instabilities if the number of integration points is not sufficiently high.

Differentiation under integral sign is a legitimate operation only for models enjoying regular likelihood and priors, while, in general, it does not apply to models having non-smooth posterior, such as quantile regression and support vector machines.
In the rest of this article, we employ the natural gradient approach to a relevant class of mixed regression models, which includes as special case generalized linear mixed models.
Doing so, we extend the derivation under integral sign results by
Bonnet, 1964
and
Price, 1958
to non-differentiable log-posterior density functions under Gaussian variational approximations.
This result allows us to easily derive closed form variational updating schemes for quantile and expectile regression, robust Huber regression and classification, support vector regression and classification.

The natural gradient approach for exponential family variational approximations has been discussed by
Knowles and Minka, 2011
,
Hoffman et al., 2013
and
Salimans and Knowles, 2013
, among others.
Then,
Wand, 2014
derived the simplified form for Gaussian variational approximations in (
5
), which has been also discussed by
Tan and Nott, 2013
in the context of generalized linear mixed models.
Lately,
Khan and Lin, 2017
and
Khan and Nielsen, 2018
characterized the variational updates (
5
)–(
6
) as the iterative solution of a mirror descent algorithm and proposed the stochastic formulation in (
8
).

Lin et al., 2019
and
Lin et al., 2021b
;
Lin et al., 2021a
then extended the stochastic natural gradient to more structured variational approximations, including mixtures of exponential families and Gaussian approximations with low-rank variance matrices.
More recently,
Khan and Rue, 2021
and
Knoblauch et al., 2022
pointed out an interesting interpretation of variational natural gradient approach as a general prototype of learning algorithm, called
Bayesian learning rule
, which takes as special cases Newton algorithm, gradient ascent, expectation maximization, Laplace approximation, and many others.

Applications of variational message passing range from mixed logistic regression
(
Knowles and Minka, 2011
;
Tan and Nott, 2013
;
Nolan and Wand, 2017
)
, count data models
(
Wand, 2014
;
Luts and Ormerod, 2014
)
, heteroscedastic regression
(
Menictas and Wand, 2015
)
, high-dimensional semiparametric regression
(
Wand, 2017
)
, Gaussian process regression
(
Opper and Archambeau, 2009
;
Khan and Lin, 2017
)
, factor models
(
Khan and Lin, 2017
)
, deep neural networks
(
Khan et al., 2018
)
, and structured variational approximations
(
Lin et al., 2019
;
Lin et al., 2021b
;
Lin et al., 2021a
)
.

3
Variational mixed effect models

In this section, we present the main contribution of the article, which consists of a new simplified message passing method for making approximate posterior inference for a broad family of linear mixed models under non-conjugate priors and, possibly, non-regular likelihoods.

We here consider mixed regression and classification models which aim to predict a response variable
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
through a deterministic transformation of the available covariate vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathbf{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
i=1,\dots,n
.
To this end, we define the additive linear predictor

\eta_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u},\qquad\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}=\mathbf{z}_{i,1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}+\dots+\mathbf{z}_{i,{\scriptscriptstyle{H}}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle{H}},

(9)

with
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
being a vector of fixed-effect parameters,

\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle{H}}^{\top})^{\top}
being a vector of random-effect parameters, such that
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}
, and
d=d_{1}+\dots+d_{\scriptscriptstyle{H}}
.
We also denote by
\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\top}=(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top},\mathbf{z}_{i,1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{i,{\scriptscriptstyle{H}}}^{\top})
the row vector collecting all the fixed- and random-effect covariates in the model, and we introduce the completed design matrix
\mathbf{C}=\big[\,\mathbf{X},\,\mathbf{Z}_{1},\dots,\,\mathbf{Z}_{\scriptscriptstyle{H}}\big]
, which stacks all the vectors
\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\top}
by row.

Taking a generalized Bayesian point of view
(
Bissiri et al., 2016
)
, we consider pseudo log-likelihood functions of the form

\log\pi(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\frac{n}{\phi}R_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\frac{1}{\phi}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(y_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\frac{n}{\phi}\log\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\frac{1}{\phi\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(y_{i},g(\eta_{i})),

(10)

where
\psi:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a continuous, typically convex, function measuring the misfit between the
i
th data point
y_{i}
and the corresponding transformed linear predictor
g(\eta_{i})
;
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
is an appropriate bijective link function mapping the linear predictor
\eta_{i}
into the image
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
;
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}>0
is a dispersion parameter measuring the variability of the marginal prediction error calculated in the loss scale;
\phi>0
is a fixed temperature parameter calibrating the weight of the risk function relative to the log-prior.

In the same vein of generalized linear models
(
McCullagh and Nelder, 1989
)
, which are based upon the specification of an exponential family, a link function
g
and a linear predictor
\eta_{i}
, the empirical risk formulation in (
10
) describes a wide range of regression models where the exponential family log-likelihood is replaced by the generic loss function
\psi
.
Doing this, generalized linear models can be recovered by choosing an appropriate specification for
\psi
.
In such case,
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
can be interpreted as the dispersion parameter of an exponential dispersion family.
Without loss of generality, hereafter we will lighten the notation by defining
\psi_{0}(y_{i},\eta_{i})=\psi(y_{i},g(\eta_{i}))
.

We complete the model specification by introducing a set of standard prior distributions which reflects the available subjective beliefs about the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{u},\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{H}}^{2},\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
:

\boldsymbol{\beta}\sim\textrm{{N}}\big(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\,\mathbf{R}_{\beta}^{-1}\big),\quad\boldsymbol{u}_{h}|\sigma_{h}^{2}\sim\textrm{{N}}\big(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{h}^{2}\mathbf{R}_{h}^{-1}\big),\quad\sigma_{h}^{2}\sim\textrm{{IG}}(A_{h},B_{h}),\quad\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\sim\textrm{{IG}}(A_{\varepsilon},B_{\varepsilon}),

(11)

for
h=1,\dots,H
, where
\sigma_{\beta}^{2},A_{\varepsilon},B_{\varepsilon},A_{h},B_{h}>0
are fixed user-specified prior parameters;
\sigma_{h}^{2}>0
is an unknown scale parameter controlling the marginal variability of the
h
th random effect vector
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
; while
\mathbf{R}_{\beta}
and
\mathbf{R}_{h}
are non-stochastic positive semi-definite matrices determining the prior conditional correlation structure among the elements of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
, respectively.

Prior specification (
11
) represents a standard choice in Bayesian mixed modelling, which can be naturally extended to more structured prior configurations, such as mixtures of Gaussians for the regression parameters and mixtures of Inverse-Gammas for the scale parameters; see, e.g.,
Wand, 2014
.
Although the approach described in the following sections can easily handle such cases, we here consider only the default specification in (
11
) to lighten the notation and to simplify exposition.

3.1
Variational approximation

The unnormalized posterior distribution relative to pseudo-likelihood specification (
10
) and prior distributions (
11
) is given by

4
Connection with data augmentation

Data augmented variational Bayes is an approximation scheme routinely used for Bayesian inference in non-conjugate models.
It combines two fundamental ingredients: an augmented representation of the model distribution and a conjugate variational approximation of the augmented posterior.

Commonly used data augmentation schemes for regression models involve a Gaussian mixture representation of the likelihood function, as in
t
-distributed
(
Lange et al., 1989
)
, Skew-Normal
(
Azzalini and Dalla Valle, 1996
)
, Asymmetric-Laplace
(
Kozumi and Kobayashi, 2011
)
, Bernoulli probit
(
Albert and Chib, 1993
)
, Bernoulli logistic
(
Polson et al., 2013
)
and SVM
(
Polson and Scott, 2011
)
regression.
In all these cases, conjugate computations under standard mean field restrictions (
12
) yield analytic approximations of the form (
13
); see, e.g.,
Wand et al., 2011
and
McLean and Wand, 2019
.
The iterative optimization of the evidence lower bound can thus be performed via coordinate ascent, where the regression parameters are updated using a penalized least squares step similar to the one considered in Algorithm
1
.
The following example illustrates some basic concepts of variational data-augmentation for a quantile regression model.

Example 1
.

Consider a Bayesian quantile regression model with misspecified Asymmetric-Laplace likelihood
y_{i}|\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{u},\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\sim\textrm{{AL}}(\eta_{i},\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2},\tau)
,
i=1,\dots,n
, where
\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the
\tau
th conditional quantile of
y_{i}
,
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}>0
is a scale parameter and
\tau\in(0,1)
is the quantile level.
Thanks to the Gaussian mixture representation of the Laplace distribution
(
Kotz et al., 2001
)
, we may exploit the equivalent hierarchical formulation

y_{i}|\omega_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{u},\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\sim\textrm{{N}}\bigg(\eta_{i}+\frac{(1-2\tau)}{\tau(1-\tau)}\omega_{i},\frac{2\omega_{i}}{\tau(1-\tau)}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\bigg),\qquad\omega_{i}|\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\sim\textrm{{Exp}}(1/\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

Doing this, we recover the conditional conjugacy of prior (
11
) with the augmented Gaussian-Exponential likelihood, and the full-conditional distributions
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{u}|\textup{rest})
,
(\sigma_{h}^{2}|\textup{rest})
,
(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}|\textup{rest})
and
(\omega_{i}|\textup{rest})
have simple analytic solutions
(
Kozumi and Kobayashi, 2011
)
.
Hence, imposing the factorization
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})=q(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{u})\,q(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})\Big[\prod_{h=1}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{H}}}q(\sigma_{h}^{2})\Big]\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{n}q(\omega_{i})\Big]
, the optimal variational distributions for the parameters may be computed in closed-form.
In particular, it can be shown that
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{u})\sim_{q}\textrm{{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, where the optimal updates for the mean and variance are
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{W}}^{(t)}\bar{\mathbf{y}}^{(t)}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\big[\bar{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)}+\mathbf{C}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{W}}^{(t)}\mathbf{C}\big]^{-1}
for some appropriate pseudo-data vector
\bar{\mathbf{y}}^{(t)}
and weighting matrix
\bar{\mathbf{W}}^{(t)}
; see, e.g.,
Wand et al., 2011
and
McLean and Wand, 2019
.

Despite its broad application, variational data augmentation suffers from severe drawbacks.
It is a model specific tool which requires the existence of a convenient conjugate augmented representation, which is not always available (see, e.g., Poisson, Gamma and heteroscedastic regression). It is prone to slow convergence
(
Lewandowski et al., 2010
;
Duan et al., 2018
;
Johndrow et al., 2019
)
and, in some situations, it may lead to poor approximation results
(
Neville et al., 2014
;
Fasano et al., 2022
)
.
Such problems are often exacerbated and amplified by restrictive factorization assumptions on the variational augmented posterior.
Non-conjugate variational message passing methods instead directly approximate the target posterior, without introducing additional auxiliary variables.
Thereby, it is of interest to theoretically investigate the relationship, in the Kullback-Leibler metric, between augmented and non-augmented variational approximations belonging to the same approximating family.

To this end, we denote by
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(y_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
the original, or
marginal
, target posterior distribution that we need to approximate, we define
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(y_{i},\omega_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
as its equivalent
augmented
representation, for some vector of local variables
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})^{\top}
, and we assume
\pi(y_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\int_{\Omega}\pi(y_{i},\omega_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\textrm{{d}}\omega_{i}
.
We denote by
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
q(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})
the variational approximations of
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
and
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
, respectively.
Finally, we define
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})=q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
as the global minimizers of, respectively,
\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
and
\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}
under the restrictions
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
and
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}

\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}

Here, the subscripts

a
and

and

In the light of Proposition
4
, Proposition
5
states the suboptimality in Kullback-Leibler divergence of any augmented variational approximation compared to an appropriate marginal approximation, except in the case where
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})

Similar results have been already studied in the literature for particular model specifications and variational restrictions on
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
.
See, e.g., Proposition 1 by
Fasano et al., 2022

Loaiza-Maya et al., 2022
, which considered conditional variational approximations for high-dimensional latent variable models.
The advantage of our formulation relies on its generality: we do not restrict our attention on particular model specifications and variational assumptions, such as mean field factorization or parametric constraints.
Instead, Proposition
5
only impose that
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
belong in the same functional space
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
, so that to ensure a fair comparison between their divergences.
Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 by
Loaiza-Maya et al., 2022
are indeed a subcase of Proposition
5
.

The results in Propositions
4
and
5
hold true for any form of
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}

In particular, if we consider mixed regression models of the form (
10

11
), where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{u},\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{H}}^{2})

Then, if the optimal approximations

,

and

6.1
Power load consumption

The first dataset we examine has been utilized in the load forecasting track of the Global Energy Competition 2014 and is readily accessible in the supplementary material of

This dataset comprises hourly load consumption and temperature data in the United States from January 2005 to December 2012, encompassing a total of 60600 observations.
Previous studies conducted by
Gaillard et al., 2016
and
Fasiolo et al., 2021
have analyzed this dataset using additive quantile regression methods to predict the temporal evolution of power load consumption.

In this context, the data typically exhibit long-term non-stationary trends, multiple seasonal cycles of various amplitudes (e.g., daily, weekly, and monthly patterns), heteroscedasticity, and potential contamination from extreme values.
Consequently, estimating multiple quantiles can provide a more comprehensive description of the data distribution than traditional mean regression.

Following the approaches of
Gaillard et al., 2016
and
Fasiolo et al., 2021
, we model the
\tau

We employ non-linear basis expansions for all covariates, employing cubic B-splines for non-periodic effects and Fourier bases for cyclic variables.
As a result, each observation
y_{i}
is associated with a 94-dimensional completed covariate vector
\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\top}
, where
i
ranges from 1 to 60600.
The coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
for each non-linear effect is treated as a random effect vector with its own variance parameter
\sigma_{h}^{2}
, where
h
ranges from 1 to 8.
To incorporate regularization, we utilize classical second-order differential penalization and construct the corresponding regularization matrix
\mathbf{R}_{h}
for Fourier and B-spline basis coefficient vectors

Among the considered approaches, the proposed batch non-conjugate variational message passing algorithm demonstrates excellent performance, exhibiting both a good efficiency and a superior accuracy.
It converges rapidly and achieves a substantial speed gain over Markov chain Monte Carlo, conjugate mean field variational Bayes and stochastic variational message passing in the first three applications, while in the fourth one, it is the second fastest method after stochastic message passing outperforming Monte Carlo sampling and conjugate mean field approximation.
In comparison to conjugate mean field variational Bayes and stochastic variational message passing, batch variational message passing showcases the highest average accuracy and evidence lower bound across all scenarios.
Notably, it consistently surpasses
95\%
accuracy in all settings, with minor deviations from the central value.

Conversely, stochastic variational message passing requires more iterations to converge, but, thanks to minibatch subsampling, each iteration is computationally cheaper than both mean field variational Bayes and non-conjugate variational message passing.
Consequently, stochastic variational message passing exhibits favorable scalability, making it well-suited for handling massive data problems, where batch optimization would necessitate an impractical amount of memory to store the dataset and approximate the posterior distribution.
Actually, in the forest cover-type application, which has the highest number of observations among the considered examples, stochastic message passing yields a substantial speed gain over Markov chain Monte Carlo, mean field variational Bayes and batch variational message passing.

Although the proposed stochastic method does not attain the same precision as batch variational message passing in terms of accuracy, it consistently achieves superior average accuracy and evidence lower bound compared to conjugate mean field variational Bayes.
This behavior is not surprising, since the approximation obtained via stochastic optimization is, by definition, a noisy version of the deterministic approximation yielding from batch variational message passing.
The precision of the proposed stochastic optimization technique thus depends on the amount of randomness injected in the algorithm via minibatch subsampling and by the number of iterations set before to stop the execution.

Overall, we can conclude that the proposed variational message passing methods are competitive with alternative conjugate mean field variational Bayes both in terms of computational speed and posterior approximation accuracy, systematically outperforming data-augmentation based approximations in all the simulation studies and real-data examples discussed in Sections

7
Discussion and future extensions

We have developed a novel variational message passing method to estimate risk-based Bayesian regression models.
Our approach is highly versatile and can be applied to a broad range of mixed regression models, which extends far beyond the limited examples we have presented in this work.
By effectively addressing challenges associated with non-regular loss functions and non-conjugate priors, our method maintains a high level of efficiency while surpassing the accuracy of conjugate mean field variational Bayes in numerous instances.
We have observed promising results in approximating the true target posterior distribution through both simulations and real-data examples.

This approach allows for several extensions and adaptations.
For instance, specialized algorithms can be developed to handle models with multiple and nested random effects
(
Nolan et al., 2020
;
Menictas et al., 2023
)
, dynamic linear models
(
Durbin and Koopman, 2012
;
Triantafyllopoulos, 2021
)
, latent spatial fields
(
Rue et al., 2009
;
Lindgren et al., 2011
)
, and inducing shrinkage priors
(
Carvalho et al., 2010
;
Armagan et al., 2013
;
Bhattacharya et al., 2015
)
.

Frequentist mixed regression models can also be effectively addressed using a careful combination of expectation-maximization algorithms and Gaussian variational approximation, as demonstrated in studies such as
Ormerod and Wand, 2010
,
Hall et al., 2011a
,
Hall et al., 2011b
,
Ormerod and Wand, 2012
,
Hui et al., 2019
and
Westling and McCormick, 2019
among others.
This approach presents a valuable alternative to cumbersome Monte Carlo integration
(
Geraci and Bottai, 2007
)
, multivariate quadrature methods
(
Geraci and Bottai, 2014
)
and nested optimization
(
Geraci, 2019
)
for models that lack the classical regularity conditions required for implementing the Laplace approximation.
Exploring the use of variational methods for frequentist inferential purposes will be the focus of future research.

Another notable contribution of our work is the application of loss smoothing to address non-regular minimization problems.
Loss smoothing is a commonly employed technique for optimizing non-smooth risk functions, involving the replacement of a non-regular loss with a tilted one.
This substitution creates a new objective function that is uniformly differentiable throughout its domain, enabling the utilization of efficient optimization routines such as Newton and quasi-Newton algorithms.
Examples of loss smoothing in the quantile regression literature can be found in the works of
Hunter and Lange, 2000
,
Yue and Rue, 2011
,
Oh et al., 2011
, and
Fasiolo et al., 2021
.
Similar approaches have been considered in the support vector machines literature
(
Lee and Mangasarian, 2001
)
.

As demonstrated in Proposition
3
, the variational loss averaging defined in formula (
14
) presents a novel method for constructing smooth majorizing objective functions from non-differentiable loss functions.
Unlike other existing smoothing methods based on geometric considerations, our strategy is founded on statistical reasoning with a straightforward probabilistic interpretation, akin to the expectation-maximization algorithm.
Furthermore, our proposal incorporates a practical rule to determine the local degree of smoothing induced by the approximation, which is determined by the posterior variance of the
i
-th linear predictor.
This adaptive calibration of the new loss allows for distinct smoothing factors to be applied to each observation.

\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t)}-\big[\mathbf{H}^{(t)}\big]^{-1}\mathbf{g}^{(t)}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow-\big[\mathbf{H}^{(t)}\big]^{-1}
to optimize the evidence lower bound with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

\mathbf{g}=-\frac{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}{\phi}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\{\psi_{0}(y_{i},\eta_{i})\}-\bar{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mu}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{H}=-\frac{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}{\phi}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}\,\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\{\psi_{0}(y_{i},\eta_{i})\}-\bar{\mathbf{R}}.

We recall that the expected loss function may be written as

\Psi_{0}(y_{i},m_{i},\nu_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{q}\{\psi_{0}(y_{i},\eta_{i})\}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{0}(y_{i},\eta_{i})\,\phi(\eta_{i};m_{i},\nu_{i}^{2})\,\textrm{{d}}\eta_{i},

Therefore, thanks to the chain rule, we get

\frac{\partial\Psi_{0,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\frac{\partial m_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}\frac{\partial\Psi_{0,i}}{\partial m_{i}}=\mathbf{c}_{i}\Psi_{1,i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi_{0,i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}\,\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}}=\frac{\partial m_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}\frac{\partial m_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}}\frac{\partial\Psi_{1,i}}{\partial m_{i}}=\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\top}\Psi_{2,i}.

Equivalently, in matrix form, we have
\mathbf{g}=-(\gamma_{\varepsilon}/\phi)\mathbf{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{1}-\bar{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathbf{H}=-(\gamma_{\varepsilon}/\phi)\mathbf{C}^{\top}\textrm{{diag}}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{2})\,\mathbf{C}-\bar{\mathbf{R}}

This concludes the derivation of

Proof of Proposition
3

Let us assume that, for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,
\psi_{0}(y,\cdot):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, having well-defined
r
th order weak derivative
\psi_{r}(y,\cdot)
, for
r=1,\dots,R
.
Recall that the
r
th order weak derivative of
\psi_{0}(y,x)
with respect to
x
, say
\psi_{r}(y,x)
, is defined as the integrable function satisfying the equation

\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\textrm{{d}}^{r}}{\textrm{{d}}{x}^{r}}\varphi(x)\,\psi_{0}(y,x)\,\textrm{{d}}{x}=(-1)^{r}\int_{a}^{b}\varphi(x)\,\psi_{r}(y,x)\,\textrm{{d}}{x},

(22)

for any infinitely differentiable test function
\varphi:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\varphi(a)=\varphi(b)=0
.

We also recall that
\psi_{0}(y,x)
is convex in
x
if, and only if,
\psi_{2}(y,x)\geq 0
almost everywhere.
Moreover, if
\psi_{0}(y,x)
is
r
times differentiable in
x
, then
\psi_{r}(y,x)=(\partial^{r}/\partial x^{r})\psi_{0}(y,x)
.

(a) Differentiability.

The differentiability of
\Psi_{0}(y,m,\nu)
with respect to
m
and
\nu
is guaranteed by the derivation under integral sign theorem and by the fact that
\phi(\cdot;m,\nu^{2})
is an analytic function having infinitely many continuous derivatives with respect to
m
and
\nu
.
In fact, since
\Psi_{0}(y,m,\nu)
depends on
m
and
\nu
only through
\phi(\cdot;m,\nu^{2})
, we have

\frac{\partial^{\,r}}{\partial m^{r}}\frac{\partial^{\,s}}{\partial\nu^{s}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{0}(y,x)\,\phi(x;\mu,\nu^{2})\,\textrm{{d}}{x}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{0}(y,x)\frac{\partial^{\,r}}{\partial m^{r}}\frac{\partial^{\,s}}{\partial\nu^{s}}\,\phi(x;m,\nu^{2})\,\textrm{{d}}{x},

(23)

for any non-negative integer
r
and
s
.
Moreover, we observe that the right-hand side derivative may be written as
\nabla_{m}^{r}\nabla_{\nu}^{s}\,\phi(x;m,\nu)=p(x;m,\nu)\,\phi(x;m,\nu^{2})
, where
p(x;m,\nu)
is a linear combination of Hermite polynomials of finite order parametrized by
m
and
\nu
.
Therefore, thanks to the Holder inequality, the right integral in (
23
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{0}(y,x)\,p(x;m,\nu)\,\phi(x;m,\nu^{2})\,\textrm{{d}}{x}\bigg|\leq\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\psi_{0}(y,x)\,p(x;m,\nu)|\,\phi(x;m,\nu^{2})\,\textrm{{d}}{x}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg[\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\psi_{0}(y,x)|\,\phi(x;m,\nu^{2})\,\textrm{{d}}{x}\bigg]\bigg[\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|p(x;m,\nu)|\,\phi(x;m,\nu^{2})\,\textrm{{d}}{x}\bigg]<\infty

which is finite, since
\psi_{0}(y,x)
is integrable and the moments of a Gaussian distribution are all well-defined for any
m\in\mathbb{R}
and
\nu>0
.

\square

(b) Convergence.

Let
x_{t}\sim\textrm{{N}}(m,\nu_{t}^{2})
be a sequence of random variables such that
\nu_{t}^{2}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
Then, thanks to the closure of the convergence in probability with respect to continuous transformations, if
\psi_{0}(y,x)
is continuous in
x
, we get
\displaystyle\psi_{0}(y,x_{t})\;\mathop{\rightarrow}^{\textrm{{p}}}\;\psi_{0}(y,m)
since
\displaystyle x_{t}\;\mathop{\rightarrow}^{\textrm{{p}}}\;m
.
Hence,
\Psi_{0}(y,m,\nu)=\mathbb{E}\{\psi_{0}(y,x_{t})\}\rightarrow\psi_{0}(y,m)
pointwise as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
Moreover, by the continuity of
\psi_{0}
and
\Psi_{0}
, we have
\sup_{m\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\Psi_{0}(y,m,\nu)-\psi_{0}(y,m)\big|\rightarrow 0
uniformly for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

\square

(c) Joint convexity.

We recall that convex functions are closed with respect to convolution with positive measures. More formally, if
f(u,v)
is a convex function in
u
for any
v
and if
w(v)
is a non-negative weighting function of
v
, the integral transformation
g(u)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f(u,v)\,w(v)\,\textrm{{d}}{v}
is convex in
u
(provided the integral exists); see, e.g.,
Lange, 2013
, Chapter 6.
Then, writing

\Psi_{0}(y,m,\nu)=\mathbb{E}_{q}\{\psi_{0}(y,\eta)\}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{0}(y,m+\nu z)\,\phi(z)\,\textrm{{d}}{z},

the convexity of
\Psi_{0}(y,m,\nu)
immediately follows from the linearity of
m+\nu z
and the convexity of
\psi_{0}(y,\eta)
with respect to
\eta
.

\square

(d) Majorization.

The lower bound
\psi_{0}(y,m)\leq\Psi_{0}(y,m,\nu)
immediately follows from the convexity of
\psi_{0}
and the Jensen inequality:
\psi_{0}\{y,\mathbb{E}(x)\}\leq\mathbb{E}\{\psi_{0}(y,x)\}=\Psi_{0}\{y,\mathbb{E}(x),\mathbb{V}\textrm{{ar}}(x)\}
.

\square

(e) Differentiation rule.

Let consider the Gaussian random variable
x\sim\textrm{{N}}(m,\nu^{2})
, such that

\Psi_{0}(y,m,\nu)=\mathbb{E}\{\psi_{0}(y,x)\}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\,\psi_{0}(y,x)\,\phi(x;m,\nu^{2})\,\textrm{{d}}{x}.

In order to prove the identity
(\partial^{r}/\partial m^{r})\,\mathbb{E}\{\psi_{0}(y,x)\}=\mathbb{E}\{\psi_{r}(y,x)\}
, for any
r=1,\dots,R
, we use an induction argument.
Let us start from the initial step deriving under integral sign with respect to
m
:

\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial m}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\psi_{0}(y,x)}{\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial m}\phi\bigg(\frac{x-m}{\nu}\bigg)\,\textrm{{d}}{x}\\
=-\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\,\frac{\psi_{0}(y,x)}{\nu}\,\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\phi}\bigg(\frac{x-m}{\nu}\bigg)\,\textrm{{d}}{x}.

To lighten the notation, here and elsewhere, we adopt the time-derivative notation
\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\phi}(z)=\textrm{{d}}\phi/\textrm{{d}}{z}
.
Because of the location-scale representation of the Gaussian distribution, we write
\displaystyle x=m+\nu\,z
, where
z\sim\textrm{{N}}(0,1)
and
\displaystyle\textrm{{d}}{x}=\nu\,\textrm{{d}}{z}
; in this way, we have

\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial m}=-\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\,\psi_{0}(y,m+\nu z)\,\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\phi}(z)\,\textrm{{d}}{z}.

Observing that
\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\phi}(z)
vanishes in the limit as
|z|\rightarrow\infty
for any
r
, we are allowed to integrate by parts with respect to
z
, to apply the definition of weak derivative of
\psi_{0}
, and finally to reparametrize back to
x
, obtaining

\frac{\partial\Psi_{0}}{\partial m}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\,\psi_{1}(y,m+\nu z)\,\phi(z)\,\textrm{{d}}{z}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\,\psi_{1}(y,x)\,\phi(x;m,\nu)\,\textrm{{d}}{x}=\mathbb{E}\{\psi_{1}(y,x)\},

where, thanks to the chain rule,
\textrm{{d}}\psi_{0}(y,m+\nu z)=\psi_{1}(y,m+\nu z)\,\nu\,\textrm{{d}}{z}
.
Such a result satisfy the identity in
3
(e) for
r=1
, hence the initial step is concluded.

Let us move to the induction step.
We consider the
r
th order derivative of
\Psi_{0}
, namely
\Psi_{r}
, and we differentiate again under integral sign with respect to
m
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial m}\frac{\partial^{r}\Psi_{0}}{\partial m^{r}}=\frac{\partial\Psi_{r}}{\partial m}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{r}(y,x)\frac{\partial}{\partial m}\phi(x;m,\nu^{2})\,\textrm{{d}}{x}.

Following the same arguments used before, sequentially applying derivation, location-scale transformation, integration by parts and back-transformation, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Psi_{r}}{\partial m}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\psi_{r}(y,x)}{\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial m}\phi\bigg(\frac{x-m}{\nu}\bigg)\,\textrm{{d}}{x}=-\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\psi_{r}(y,x)}{\nu}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\phi}\bigg(\frac{x-m}{\nu}\bigg)\,\textrm{{d}}{x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{r}(y,m+\nu\,z)\,\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\phi}(z)\,\textrm{{d}}{z}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{r+1}(y,m+\nu\,z)\,\phi(z)\,\textrm{{d}}{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\psi_{r+1}(y,x)\,\phi(x;m,\nu^{2})=\mathbb{E}\{\psi_{r+1}(y,x)\}.

This concludes the induction step and also the proof.

\square

Proof of Proposition

q(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})=q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
be any density function over the parameter space
\Omega\times\Theta
, then we may write the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
q(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
as follows

\displaystyle\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}

\displaystyle=\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\,\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Theta\times\Omega}q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\log\bigg\{\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\,\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})}{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\bigg\}\,\textrm{{d}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,\textrm{{d}}\boldsymbol{\omega}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Theta}q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigg[\int_{\Omega}q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\log\bigg\{\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})}{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}\bigg\}\,\textrm{{d}}\boldsymbol{\omega}\bigg]\,\textrm{{d}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\int_{\Theta}q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\log\bigg\{\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})}{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\bigg\}\,\textrm{{d}}\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}\,\textrm{{d}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}\big]}_{\geq 0}\;+\;\underbrace{\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}}_{\geq 0}.

Notice that the first term nullifies if and only if
\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}=0
for almost every
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
, while the second term corresponds to the Kullback-Leibler divergence calculated over the marginal posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, which does not depend on
q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
The optimal posterior approximation of
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
is then the solution of the following variational problem

\displaystyle\min_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}},q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}\big]+\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}

\displaystyle\quad=\min_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}}\bigg[\min_{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}\big]+\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}\bigg]

which involve a first profiling with respect to
q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}
and then an external optimization with respect to
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
.
Therefore, denoting by
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}
the optimal approximation of
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})
for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
, which is the minimizer of
\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}\big]
, we obtain

q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathop{\textrm{{argmin}}}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}}\;\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}+\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}\big].

This concludes the proof of Proposition
4
.

\square

Proof of Proposition
5

The first scenario we consider is
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}
.
In this case, thanks to the fundamental properties of the Kullback-Leibler divergence
(
Kullback and Leibler, 1951
)
and to Proposition
4
, the unique solution
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
is given by

\displaystyle q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{{argmin}}}_{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{{argmin}}}_{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}}\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}=\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y}),

since the Kullback-Leibler divergence is strictly convex with respect to
q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})
is the only density function such that
\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}=0
, for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
.
As a consequence, the optimal variational approximation of
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
is the solution of

\displaystyle q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{{argmin}}}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}}\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}+\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{{argmin}}}_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}}\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}=q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

which, by definition, leads to
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
almost everywhere.
The equality of the Kullback-Leibler divergences in (
19
) directly follows from the correspondence between
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and by the global optimality of
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})
.
The resulting approximation for
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
then corresponds to
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\,q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
This concludes the first part of the proof.

The second scenario we consider is
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\neq\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}
.
In this case,
\textrm{{KL}}\{q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}>0
for any
q(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{C}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
, in fact
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\neq\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})
almost everywhere.
As a consequence, the optimal distribution
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the solution of the regularized variational problem in (
17
).
By strict convexity and by definition of global minimum,
\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}\leq\textrm{{KL}}\{\bar{q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}
for any
\bar{q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
.
In particular, the inequality holds for
\bar{q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and

\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}\leq\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}<\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}}\big[\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{y})\}\big].

This concludes the proof of Proposition
5
.

\square

Remark 2
.

The key assumption in Proposition
5
is that
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
belong in the same functional space
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
.
Whenever such a compatibility assumption is not satisfied there are no guaranties that Proposition
5
holds true.
In the following we show this fact by using a counterexample.

Example 2
.

Let us consider a generic augmented space
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}
such that
\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}
. On the opposite, we consider
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
such that
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\notin\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
. This way,
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
are not compatible by construction, since there exists at least one element of
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}
whose marginal density does not belong to
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
, namely
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})=\int_{\Omega}\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\textrm{{d}}\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
Recall that the Kullback-Leibler divergence is always non-negative, i.e.
\textrm{{KL}}(q\parallel\pi)\geq 0
, and reaches 0 if and only if
q=\pi
almost everywhere, namely
\textrm{{KL}}(\pi\parallel\pi)=0
. Then, there exists at least one element of
\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}
such that
\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}=0
, which corresponds to
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{A}}}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
, whereas
\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}>0
for any
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
, since
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\notin\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}
. This means that

\displaystyle\underbrace{\textrm{{KL}}\{q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}}_{\textstyle>0}\;\nleq\;\underbrace{\textrm{{KL}}\{\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\parallel\pi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\}}_{\textstyle=0},\qquad\forall\,q_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{{M}}},

which contradicts the inequality in Proposition
5
.
