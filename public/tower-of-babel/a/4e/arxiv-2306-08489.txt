Analysis and Approximate Inference of Large Random Kronecker Graphs
Zhenyu Liao, Yuanqian Xia, Chengmei Niu, Yong Xiao

Abstract
Random graph models are playing an increasingly important role in various fields ranging from social networks, telecommunication systems, to physiologic and biological networks. Within this landscape, the random Kronecker graph model, emerges as a prominent framework for scrutinizing intricate real-world networks. In this paper, we investigate large random Kronecker graphs, i.e., the number of graph vertices N N is large. Built upon recent advances in random matrix theory (RMT) and high-dimensional statistics, we prove that the adjacency of a large random Kronecker graph can be decomposed, in a spectral norm sense, into two parts: a small-rank (of rank O ⁡ ( log ⁡ N ) O(\log N) ) signal matrix that is linear in the graph parameters and a zero-mean random noise matrix. Based on this result, we propose a “denoise-and-solve” approach to infer the key graph parameters, with significantly reduced computational complexity. Experiments on both graph inference and classification are presented to evaluate the our proposed method. In both tasks, the proposed approach yields comparable or advantageous performance, than widely-used graph inference (e.g., KronFit) and graph neural net baselines, at a time cost that scales linearly as the graph size N N .

Introduction

We are living in an increasingly connected world, with a rapidly growing amount of data arising from large-scale interactive systems such as social
[
44
,
42
]
, traffic, biological
[
46
]
, and financial networks.
Graph model, in this respect, provides a natural way to describe and assess the behavior of these non-Euclidean data, e.g., how they interact with each other, in the form of pairwise relationships.

When facing large-scale networks, probabilistic graph model is a useful tool to analyze the complex behavior of entities or agents with only a small number of parameters, facilitating further analysis and graph-type data mining.
The most widely known random graph model is the Erdős–Rényi graph, for which the presence or absence of the edge between two graph vertices is modeled as an independent Bernoulli random variable with a probability parameter
p\in(0,1)
.
More advanced random graph models such as the stochastic block model
[
34
]
and Watts–Strogatz model
[
59
]
have then been proposed to better characterize the community structure and small-world behavior of realistic graphs.

In this paper, we investigate another popular random graph model, the random Kronecker graph model.
It is first introduced in
[
36
]
and applies the Kronecker product operation on a small initiator matrix
\mathbf{P}_{1}
(of size
m
by
m

\mathbf{P}_{K}
of much larger size (e.g.,
m^{K}
by
m^{K}
after
K
times of operation).
See
1

A random Kronecker graph can then be generated using

with, a priori, all its structural information (such as its hierarchical structure and “fractal” propriety, see
[
36
]
) summarized in the few parameters of

Random Kronecker graph model has already shown promising potential in analyzing realistic graphs and networks in its graph statistics such as degree distributions, and/or eigenspectral behavior.

The Kronecker graph model can be used in the following two ways:
(i) as a generative model to produce large-scale graphs that, while synthetic, closely mimic realistic graphs, and can be used for simulation and sampling purposes
[
36
]
; and
(ii) as a prominent (low-dimensional) features extractor (e.g., the Kronecker initiator
\mathbf{P}_{1}
) for the graph of interest, for better visualization and/or for performance in downstream tasks such as graph classification
[
55
,
20

For the second type of application, one needs to estimate the graph parameters

from a given graph.
This presents the following technical challenges:
(i) for a Kronecker graph having
N
vertices with
N

computationally efficient

a non-trivial number

(ii) the Kronecker graph model naturally describes a set of isomorphic graphs (see
1

Related Work

Here, we provide a brief review of related previous efforts.

Kronecker graph model and its applications.

The use of Kronecker products in graphs dates back to
[
60
]
, and was largely popularized since the introduction of the Kronecker graph model

The Kronecker graph model is a commonly adopted complex graph generator that has shown promising results in fitting many realistic networks and/or graphs, e.g., social, biological, and chemical networks, see, e.g.,
[

It is inspired from the fact that many realistic graphs possess the self-repeating, also called

Many (theoretical) properties of the random Kronecker graph model have been established in a sequence of works, e.g. the (asymptotic) size of its giant component

Practical algorithms such as the KronFit approach

[

[
55
]

Random graph model and random matrix theory.

Random graph models have attracted significant research interest in applied math, computer science, and machine learning, with applications ranging from unsupervised
[
38
,
15
]
, semi-supervised
[
7
,
64
]
, and more recently, to self-supervised learning
[

Since the (eigen/singular) spectra play a crucial role in the analysis of random graph models

12
,
13

,

[

]

We refer the readers to

Here, our result extends the line of works on the spectral analysis of dense random graphs

Definition 1

\mathbf{P}_{1}=\{P_{uv}\}_{u,v=1}^{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m},\quad\quad P_{uv}\in(0,1),

For a Kronecker graph

1
1

1

(4)

As such, the inference of

14
) as follows,

\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\hat{\sigma}_{i})\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\sf T},~\text{with}~f\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},

(15)

for some nonlinear function
f
.
The hard thresholding SVD in (
14
) is a special case of shrinkage estimator with
f(t)=t
for the largest
\rank(\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}})
singular values of
\bar{\mathbf{A}}
and zero otherwise.
This specific choice of hard thresholding function is, however, of limited interest since it requires additional efforts to be practically implemented when
\rank(\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}})
is unknown and needs to be determined.

While the signal-plus-noise model of the type
\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}+\mathbf{Z}/\sqrt{N}
for small rank
\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
and random matrix
\mathbf{Z}
having i.i.d. zero-mean entries has been widely studied in the literature of RMT and high-dimensional statistics, previous efforts only focus on the case of fixed rank for
\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
as
N\to\infty
and does not apply to Kronecker graph inference for which the rank of
\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}

may grow with
N
in a logarithmic fashion
, see again Item (iii) in
1
.

We further provide, in
Appendix

C
, a detailed analysis of the singular spectrum of
\bar{\mathbf{A}}
for small but growing rank of
\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
.
This further leads to, by carefully adapting the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 1]
to the Kronecker graph in
1
, the
denoising
shrinkage estimator
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K}
of
\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
.
This estimator
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K}
will then be used in
Section

3.3
below to infer the Kronecker graph parameter
\mathbf{P}_{1}
.

The shrinkage estimator in
Algorithm

2
is more interesting than, e.g., the naive hard thresholding SVD approach in that: (i) it uses a truncation threshold
2\sqrt{\hat{\bar{p}}(1-\hat{\bar{p}})}
that can be predetermined from the graph adjacency and (ii) it yields the
minimum
(asymptotic) Frobenius norm error among all shrinkage estimators of the form (
13
), see again
Appendix

C
for a detailed discussion on this.

.

3.3
Kronecker solving via permuted linear regression

Having obtained the estimate
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K}
of the permuted signal matrix
\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
using, say the shrinkage estimation in
Algorithm

2
of
Section

3.2
, we now discuss how to
solve
for the Kronecker graph parameter
\mathbf{X}
from this estimate
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K}
.

Note that we have, up to permutation by
\boldsymbol{\Pi}
, that
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K}\simeq\boldsymbol{\Pi}\mathbf{S}_{K}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{-1}
for
N
large, with

\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K}\simeq\boldsymbol{\Pi}\mathbf{S}_{K}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{-1}={\rm mat}((\boldsymbol{\Pi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Pi})\boldsymbol{\Theta}{\rm vec}(\mathbf{X})),

(16)

by
4
in
Appendix

A
, with
known
coefficients
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
(from binomial expansion, see again
1
) and
unknown
permutation
\boldsymbol{\Pi}
.
To recover both
{\rm vec(\mathbf{X})}
and
\boldsymbol{\Pi}
, we sort to the following optimization problem:

(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Pi}},\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\Pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{N},\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{2}}}\|(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Pi})\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mathbf{x}-{\rm vec}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K})\|_{2}^{2},

(17)

for
\mathcal{P}_{N}
the set of permutation matrices of size
N
.

The optimization problem of the type (
17
) is known in the literature as linear regression with “broken samples,” or
permuted linear regression
.
This problem is known to be extremely challenging (in fact proven to be NP-hard unless in some trivial cases) and has attracted significant research interest by, e.g., considering different simplifying statistical assumptions on the noise (the entries of
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K}-\mathbf{S}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
in the context of this paper) and/or the coefficient matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, see for example
[
43
,
52
,
29
,
45
,
48
,
54
]
.

Denote
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}
the permutation matrix that corresponds to the
true
matching of the
N
vertices, and
d_{H}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{*},\mathbf{I}_{N})\equiv|\{i:[\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{*}]_{ii}=0\}|
the Hamming distance between
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{*}
and the identity matrix (which characterizes the number of mismatched vertices), the permuted linear regression problem in (
17
) then writes

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\Pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{N},\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{2}}}

\displaystyle\|{\rm vec}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K})-(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Pi})\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2},

(18)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle d_{H}(\boldsymbol{\Pi},\mathbf{I}_{N})\leq s,

for
\boldsymbol{\Pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}
,
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{2}}
, and some auxiliary (sparsity) variable
s\leq N
.
The optimization problem in (
18
) is known to be NP-hard as long as
s=O(N)
, unless in the trivial case of
m=1
, see
[
45
]
.

To solve efficiently the Kronecker inference problem, here we consider the setting where the permutation is
sparse
(so that
s\ll N
in (
18
)), and first relax the constraint in (
18
) as
d_{H}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Pi},\mathbf{I}_{N^{2}})\leq 2sN-s^{2}\leq 2sN
.
Introducing
\mathbf{d}=(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Pi}-\mathbf{I}_{N^{2}})\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N^{2}}
, the problem in (
18
) can be relaxed as

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{2}},~\mathbf{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N^{2}}}

\displaystyle\|{\rm vec}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K})-\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{d}\|_{2}^{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{d}\|_{0}\leq 2sN,

which is still not convex due to the
\ell_{0}
-norm constraint.
To solve (
19
), we consider the following two approaches:

(I)

the iterative hard thresholding (IHT) approach
[
9
,
31
]
, by working directly on the non-convex
\ell_{0}
-norm constraint, and using
H_{s}(\cdot)
the hard thresholding operator to set the entries of a vector with small magnitude to zero and retain the large ones unaltered; or

(II)

further relax the non-convex problem in (
19
) by replacing the
\ell_{0}
-norm by
\ell_{1}
-norm, to get the following Lagrangian form,

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{2}},~\mathbf{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N^{2}}}\left\|{\rm vec}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K})-\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{d}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\|\mathbf{d}\|_{1},

(20)

for some hyperparameter
\gamma>0
that trade-offs the mean squared loss and the sparsity level in
\mathbf{d}
.

These two approaches allow for effectively solving the relaxed permuted linear regression in (
19
) by alternately solving for
\mathbf{d}
(via hard or soft thresholding) and
\mathbf{x}
(via least squares, with sampling and/or sketching
[
40
,
25
]
if necessary, see
3
below).
The whole Kronecker
solving
program is summarized in
Algorithm

3
.
In particular, note that when working with the convexly relaxed problem in (
20
), the convergence of this alternative minimization-type algorithm is always ensured, see for example
[
31
, Section 4.3]
.

solve
for
\mathbf{X}

\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{K}
(from
Algorithm

2

\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and hyperparameter
\gamma
for convex relaxation or step length
\eta
and sparsity level
s
for IHT.

Output:
Estimation of graph parameter
\hat{\mathbf{X}}
by solving the permuted linear regression in (

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the large-dimensional behavior of random Kronecker graphs
We show that the graph adjacency
\mathbf{A}
is close, in spectral norm, to the sum of a small-rank signal
\mathbf{S}_{K}
and a random noise matrix
\mathbf{Z}
.
Based on this observation, we propose a “denoise-and-solve”
Algorithm

1
for graph parameters inference, and discuss its practical implementation.
Numerical experiments are provided to validate the effectiveness (in terms of performance and running time) of the proposed approach against the KronFit method, on random Kronecker graphs.
We further propose to use Kronecker graph model and
Algorithm

1
as features extractors for realistic graph classification, and provide comparative analysis to a few popular GNN baselines.

A few useful lemmas that will be consistently exploited in the proof are listed in
Appendix

A
.
The proofs of the technical results in the paper are given in
Appendix

B
.
We provide in

Appendix A
Useful Lemmas

Here we list a few lemmas that will be constantly used in the proof.

Lemma 2

(Weyl’s inequality,
[
27
, Theorem 4.3.1]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
be symmetric matrices and let the respective eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}
be arranged in decreasing order, i.e.,
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{p}
. Then, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,

\lambda_{i+j-1}(\mathbf{A})+\lambda_{p+1-j}(\mathbf{B})\leq\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})\leq\lambda_{i-j}(\mathbf{A})+\lambda_{j+1}(\mathbf{B})

(21)

In particular,

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq p}|\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A})-\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{B})|\leq\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2}.

Lemma 3

(SVD of Kronecker product,
[
28
, Theorem 4.2.15]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times q},\mathbf{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
having rank
r_{\mathbf{A}},r_{\mathbf{B}}
, and let
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf T}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{B}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{B}}^{\sf T}
be the singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, respectively.
Then, the singular value decomposition of the Kronecker product
\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B}
is given by

\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf T})\otimes(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{B}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{B}}^{\sf T})=(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{B}})(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{B}})(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{B}})^{\sf T},

(22)

with
\rank(\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B})=\rank(\mathbf{B}\otimes\mathbf{A})=r_{\mathbf{A}}\cdot r_{\mathbf{B}}
and
\|\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{B}\|_{2}
.

Lemma 4
.

For matrices
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
,
\mathbf{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times p}
, and
\mathbf{C}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times q}
, we have

{\rm vec}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C})=(\mathbf{C}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{A}){\rm vec}(\mathbf{B}).

(23)

B.1.1
Proof of

Item (i)

First note, under Assumption
1
and by the Binomial theorem that,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{P}_{k}]_{ij}=\prod_{u,v=1}^{m}P_{uv}^{C_{uv;ij}}=\prod_{u,v=1}^{m}(p+X_{uv}/\sqrt{N})^{C_{uv;ij}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{u,v=1}^{m}\left(p^{C_{uv;ij}}+C_{uv;ij}\cdot p^{(C_{uv;ij}-1)}X_{uv}/\sqrt{N}+\tilde{O}(N^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle=p^{k}+p^{(k-1)}\sum_{u,v=1}^{m}C_{uv;ij}\cdot X_{uv}/\sqrt{N}+\tilde{O}(N^{-1}),

(24)

for
1\leq k\leq K
with coefficients
C_{uv;ij}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\sum_{u,v=1}^{m}C_{uv;ij}=k
.
This gives, by the definition of the Kronecker power, that in matrix form,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{k-1}\otimes\mathbf{P}_{1}

\displaystyle=(p^{k-1}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{K-1}}^{\sf T}+\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{{k-1}}+\tilde{O}_{\|\cdot\|_{2}}(1))\otimes(p\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}+\mathbf{X}/\sqrt{N})

\displaystyle=p^{k}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}^{\sf T}+p^{k-1}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{X}/\sqrt{N}+p\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{{k-1}}\otimes(\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T})+\tilde{O}_{\|\cdot\|_{2}}(1),

where we used in the second line
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{{k-1}}
to denote terms in (
24
) that are linear in (the entries of)
\mathbf{X}
with
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{1}=\mathbf{X}/\sqrt{N}
, and
\tilde{O}_{\|\cdot\|_{2}}(1)
to denote matrices of spectral norm order
\tilde{O}(1)
, as a consequence of the fact that
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}\leq N\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\max}
for
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
and
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\max}\equiv\max_{i,j}|A_{ij}|
; and in third line the fact that
\|\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{k-1}\|_{2}=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{N})
and Lemma
3
so that
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{k-1}\otimes\mathbf{X}/\sqrt{N}=\tilde{O}_{\|\cdot\|_{2}}(1)
.

Note that by definition of
\mathbf{S}_{k}
in (
6
),
\mathbf{P}_{k}
can be rewritten as the following recursion on
\mathbf{S}_{k}
,

\mathbf{P}_{k}=p^{k}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}^{\sf T}+\sqrt{N}\mathbf{S}_{k}+\tilde{O}_{\|\cdot\|_{2}}(1),\quad\mathbf{S}_{1}=\mathbf{X}/N,

(25)

by taking
\mathbf{S}_{k}=\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{k}/\sqrt{N}
for
1\leq k\leq K
, and by taking
k=K
, one has

\mathbf{P}_{K}=p^{K}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\sf T}+\sqrt{N}\mathbf{S}_{K}+\tilde{O}_{\|\cdot\|_{2}}(1)\equiv\mathbf{P}^{\rm lin}_{K}+\tilde{O}_{\|\cdot\|_{2}}(1),\quad\mathbf{S}_{1}=\mathbf{X}/N,

(26)

where we introduced the
linearized
probability matrix
\mathbf{P}^{\rm lin}_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
as in (
7
).
This concludes the proof of Item (i).

B.1.2
Proof of
1
Item (ii)

For Item (ii), note from the recursive definition in (
6
) that

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{k-1}}{N}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{X}+p\mathbf{S}_{k-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{k-1}}{N}\underbrace{(\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{X}+\cdots+\mathbf{X}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T})}_{k~\text{times}}.

Since each term in the bracket is a linear combination of
{\rm vec}(\mathbf{X})
, with coefficients of the type
p^{k-1}C_{uv;ij}/N=\tilde{O}(N^{-1})
as in (
24
).
Taking
k=K
allows one to conclude that
\mathbf{S}_{K}={\rm mat}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}{\rm vec}(\mathbf{X}))
for some coefficients
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N^{2}\times m^{2}}
with
\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}\|_{\max}=\tilde{O}(N^{-1})
.

In the following, we will show that
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mathbf{1}_{m^{2}}=\frac{p^{K-1}K}{N}\mathbf{1}_{N^{2}},\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\sf T}\mathbf{1}_{N^{2}}=\frac{p^{K-1}KN}{m^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{2}}
.
Note that the former follows straightforwardly from the binomial expansion in (
24
) that
\sum_{u,v=1}^{m}C_{uv;ij}=K
after
K
-th Kronecker product.

We now prove
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\sf T}\mathbf{1}_{N^{2}}=\frac{p^{K-1}KN}{m^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{2}}
by exploiting the structures in the columns of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
.
First, for
1\leq k\leq K
, we have

\mathbf{S}_{k}={\rm mat}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}{\rm vec}(\mathbf{X})),

(27)

for some coefficients
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{2k}\times m^{2}}
determined by the binomial expansion and
p,k
.
With a slight abuse of notations, we denote
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N^{2}\times m^{2}}
.
Denote
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i,k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{2k}}
the
i
-th column of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}
, we have, for
1\leq i,j\leq m
and
q=i+m(j-1)
, that

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k}=\frac{p^{k-1}}{N}{\rm vec}\underbrace{(\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{E}_{ij}+\cdots+\mathbf{E}_{ij}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T})}_{k~\text{times}},

(28)

where we define the canonical matrix
\mathbf{E}_{ij}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
in such a way that
[\mathbf{E}_{ij}]_{uv}=\delta_{iu}\cdot\delta_{jv}
.
Then, we have
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}^{\sf T}\mathbf{1}_{m^{2k}}=\frac{p^{k-1}}{N}km^{2k-2}\mathbf{1}_{m^{2}}
as a consequence of
5
(to be proven below), and therefore
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\sf T}\mathbf{1}_{N^{2}}=\frac{p^{K-1}KN}{m^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{2}}
by taking
k=K
.

In the proof above, we use the following two technical lemmas.

Lemma 5
.

For
1\leq q\leq m^{2},1\leq k\leq K
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{2k}}
defined as (
28
) with the understanding that the definition of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k}
here disregards the coefficient
p^{k-1}/N
for simplicity, one has

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{m^{2k}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k}=km^{2k-2}.

Proof of
5
.

For
1\leq i,j\leq m
, we have
\sum_{u,v}[\mathbf{E}_{ij}]_{uv}=1
, so that for a fixed
k
, the value of
\mathbf{1}_{m^{2k}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k}
is
independent
of
q
.

Note that the entries of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k}
is between
0
and
k
.
We use
\phi_{i,q,k}
to denote the number of occurrences of element
i
in
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k}
.
Then, for
2\leq k\leq K
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k}={\rm vec}\left(\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{E}_{q}+{\rm mat}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}\right),

(29)

where we use
\mathbf{E}_{q}
instead of
\mathbf{E}_{ij}
in (
28
).

Thus, for
2\leq k\leq K
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{0,q,k}

\displaystyle=(m^{2}-1)\phi_{0,q,k-1},

\displaystyle\phi_{i,q,k}

\displaystyle=(m^{2}-1)\phi_{i,q,k-1}+\phi_{i-1,q,k-1},

\displaystyle\phi_{k,q,k}

\displaystyle=1.

By
6
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{m^{2k}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q,k}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}i\phi_{i,q,k}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\frac{k!}{(i-1)!(k-i)!}(m^{2}-1)^{k-i}

\displaystyle=km^{2k-2},

and thus the conclusion of the proof of
5
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

For
2\leq k\leq K,0\leq i\leq k
, define
\{\phi_{i,k}\}
as

\displaystyle\phi_{0,k}

\displaystyle=(m^{2}-1)\phi_{0,k-1},

\displaystyle\phi_{i,k}

\displaystyle=(m^{2}-1)\phi_{i,k-1}+\phi_{i-1,k-1},

\displaystyle\phi_{k,k}

\displaystyle=1,

where
\phi_{0,1}=m^{2}-1,\phi_{1,1}=1
. Then a general formula of
\{\phi_{i,k}\}
is

\displaystyle\phi_{i,k}=\frac{k!}{i!(k-i)!}(m^{2}-1)^{k-i}.

(30)

Proof of
6
.

For k = 2, we have that

\displaystyle\phi_{0,2}=(m^{2}-1)^{2},\quad\phi_{1,2}=2(m^{2}-1),\quad\phi_{2,2}=1.

Then, assume that (
30
) holds for
2\leq k\leq K-1
, then we will show that (
30
) holds for
k+1
. For
1\leq i\leq k+1
, we have that

\displaystyle\phi_{0,k+1}

\displaystyle=(m^{2}-1)\phi_{0,k}=(m^{2}-1)^{k+1},

\displaystyle\phi_{i,k+1}

\displaystyle=(m^{2}-1)\phi_{i,k}+\phi_{i-1,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{k!}{i!(k-i)!}(m^{2}-1)^{k-i+1}+\frac{k!}{(i-1)!(k-i+1)!}(m^{2}-1)^{k-i+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k+1)!}{i!(k-i+1)!}(m^{2}-1)^{k-i+1}.

This thus concludes the proof of
6
.
∎

B.1.3
Proof of
1
Item (iii)

Item (i) and (ii) of
1
are already proven in the main text, it remains to prove Item (iii) of
1
by establishing, for
\mathbf{S}_{K}
as defined in (
6
), that

\rank(\mathbf{S}_{K})\leq(m-1)K+1.

(31)

We will in fact show that for all
1\leq k\leq K
, one has

\rank(\mathbf{S}_{k})\leq(m-1)k+1.

(32)

To prove the above fact,

(i)

we first explore the iterative definition of
\mathbf{S}_{K}
in (
6
) to write it as the sum of
K
matrices
\mathbf{M}_{\ell,K},\ell\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
of rank at most
m
(which already provides an upper bound of the rank
\rank(\mathbf{S}_{K})\leq mK
); and

(ii)

with a more detailed analysis on how the (left and right) singular spaces of
\mathbf{M}_{\ell,K}
“intersect” with each other when summing over
\ell
to
\mathbf{S}_{K}
, we can further tighten the upper bound to
\rank(\mathbf{S}_{K})\leq(m-1)K+1
as in the statement.

Recall the recursive definition of
\mathbf{S}_{k}
in (
6
) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{k}=\frac{p^{k-1}}{N}(\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}^{\sf T})\otimes\mathbf{X}+p\mathbf{S}_{{k-1}}\otimes(\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}),\quad\mathbf{S}_{1}=\mathbf{X}/N,

with
k=1,2,\ldots,K
and
N=m^{K}
.
It then follows from the iterative definition in (
6
) that

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{k}

\displaystyle=p^{k-1}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{S}_{1}+p\mathbf{S}_{k-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}

\displaystyle=p^{k-1}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-1}}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{S}_{1}+p(p^{k-2}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-2}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-2}}^{\sf T}\otimes\mathbf{S}_{1}+p\mathbf{S}_{k-2}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\sf T}

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle=p^{k-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}^{\sf T})\otimes\mathbf{S}_{1}\otimes(\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}}^{\sf T}).

For
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
and
0\leq\ell\leq k-1
, denote the shortcut

\mathbf{M}_{\ell,k}=(\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}^{\sf T})\otimes\mathbf{S}_{1}\otimes(\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}}^{\sf T}),

(33)

one has

\mathbf{S}_{k}=p^{k-1}\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\mathbf{M}_{\ell,k}.

(34)

Note that this already provides us with an upper bound of the rank,

\rank(\mathbf{S}_{K})\leq mK.

(35)

To further improve this (upper bound) estimate of the rank of
\mathbf{S}_{K}
, we need to perform a more detailed analysis of the singular spaces of
\mathbf{M}_{\ell,k}
, particularly when they are summed over
\ell
to get
\mathbf{S}_{k}
.

To that end, consider, without loss of generality that
\mathbf{S}_{1}=\mathbf{X}/N
is of full rank (which indeed leads to an upper bound on the rank of
\mathbf{S}_{K}
eventually) the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\mathbf{S}_{1}
as

\mathbf{S}_{1}=\mathbf{X}/N=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\sf T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m},

(36)

with orthonormal
\mathbf{U}=[\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{m}],\mathbf{V}=[\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
and diagonal
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
.

So that
\mathbf{M}_{\ell,k}
, as the Kronecker product between
\mathbf{S}_{1}
and matrices of all ones per its definition in (
33
), admits the following decomposition (which is almost an SVD but with “unnormalized” singular vectors),

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\ell,k}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}^{\sf T})\otimes\mathbf{S}_{1}\otimes(\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}}^{\sf T})

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}^{\sf T}\right)\otimes\left(\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\right)\otimes\left(\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}}\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}}^{\sf T}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}})(\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}\otimes\mathbf{v}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}})^{\sf T}

where we used the fact that
(\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B})(\mathbf{C}\otimes\mathbf{D})=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C})\otimes(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{D})
with
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D}
of appropriate dimension.

To further perform an in-depth analysis of how the left and right singular spaces of
\mathbf{M}_{\ell,K}
intersect for different
\ell\leq K-1
, we introduce the following shortcuts

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell,i,k}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}},

(37)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell,i,k}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{m^{\ell}}\otimes\mathbf{v}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m^{k-\ell-1}},

(38)

so that
\mathbf{M}_{l,k}
can be compactly rewritten as following sum of
m
rank-one matrices,

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\ell,k}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell,i,k}\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell,i,k}^{\sf T},

(39)

so that

\mathbf{S}_{k}=p^{k-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\mathbf{M}_{\ell,k}=p^{k-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell,i,k}\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell,i,k}^{\sf T}.

(40)

In the following, we focus on the subspace spanned by the vectors of
\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell,i,k}
(which in fact forms the left singular space of
\mathbf{M}_{\ell,k}
).
First note that by definition in (
37
), one has, for
1\leq\ell\leq k-1
, the following recursive relation when increasing the value of
k
or
\ell
,

\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell,i,k+1}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell,i,k}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m},\quad\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell+1,i,k+1}=\mathbf{1}_{m}\otimes\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell,i,k}.

(41)

A direct consequence of the recursion in (
41
) is the following lemma, saying that for any
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, the vector of all ones
\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}
is in the linear span of
\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1,k},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,2,k},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,m,k}
.

Lemma 7
.

For
1\leq k\leq K
and
\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell,i,k}
defined as in (
37
), one has that

\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}\in\spn\{\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1,k},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,2,k},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,m,k}\},

(42)

and for
1\leq k\leq K-1
that

\mathbf{1}_{m^{k+1}}\in\spn\{\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{1,1,k+1},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{1,2,k+1},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{1,m,k+1}\}.

(43)

Proof of
7
.

We shall prove
7
based on an induction on the index
k
.
For
k=1
, we have that

\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1,1}=\mathbf{u}_{1},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,2,1}=\mathbf{u}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,m,1}=\mathbf{u}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},

(44)

which, by definition, forms a basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, so that there exists a set of coefficients
\{\alpha_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^{m}
such that

\mathbf{1}_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i,1}\mathbf{u}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i,1}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,i,1}.

(45)

Then, assume that (
42
) holds for
1\leq k\leq K-1
, so that there exists a set of coefficients
\{\alpha_{i,k}\}_{i=1}^{m}
such that

\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i,k}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,i,k}.

(46)

Then, one has

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{m^{k+1}}=\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i,k}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,i,k}\right)\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i,k}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,i,k+1},

where we used the iterative relation in (
41
).
This allows us to conclude the proof of (
42
) in
7
.

For (
43
), it suffices to write, with (
42
) that

\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i,k}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,i,k},

(47)

for some coefficients
\{\alpha_{i,k}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, so that

\mathbf{1}_{m^{k+1}}=\mathbf{1}_{m}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{m^{k}}=\mathbf{1}_{m}\otimes\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i,k}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,i,k}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i,k}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{1,i,k+1},

(48)

This thus concludes the proof of
7
.
∎

With the recursion in (
41
) and
7
at hand, we are now already to characterize the precise “interaction” of the left singular space of
\mathbf{M}_{\ell,k}
and that of
\mathbf{M}_{\ell^{\prime},k}
with
\ell^{\prime}\leq\ell
.
This is described in the following result.

Lemma 8
.

For
2\leq k\leq K
, define the linear spans of vectors
\mathcal{A}_{k},\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{k}}
as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}

\displaystyle=\spn\{\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1,k},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,m-1,k},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{1,1,k},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{1,m-1,k},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1,1,k},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1,m-1,k},\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1,m,k}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{k}

\displaystyle=\spn\{\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0,m,k},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2,m,k}\}.

Remark 6

The moment-based approach proposed in

It employs three strategies to solve for the Kronecker graph initiator: the direct minimization approach, the grid-search approach, and the leading-term-matching approach.

The direct minimization approach is (believed to be) able to achieve similar performance as the grid-search approach, albeit with significantly reduced computational time, see

Conversely, the leading-term-matching approach can offer a noteworthy computational speed advantage, owing to its distinctive solution methodology.

[
