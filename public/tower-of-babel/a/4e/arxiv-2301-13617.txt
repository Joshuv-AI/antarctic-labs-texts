A Closer Look into Recent Video-based Learning Research: A Comprehensive Review of Video Characteristics, Tools, Technologies, and Learning Effectiveness DOI: XXXXXXX.XXXXXXX
Evelyn Navarrete, Andreas Nehring, Sascha Schanze, Ralph Ewerth, Anett Hoppe

Abstract
. People increasingly use videos on the Web as a source for learning, be it for daily tasks or in formal as well as informal educational settings. To support this way of learning, researchers and developers are continuously developing tools, proposing guidelines, analyzing data, and conducting experiments. However, it is still not clear what characteristics a video should have to be an effective learning medium. In this paper, we present a comprehensive review of 257 articles on video-based learning from computer science databases for the period from 2016 to 2021 using the PRISMA guidelines. One of the aims of the review is to identify the video characteristics that have been explored by previous work. Based on our analysis, we suggest a taxonomy which organizes the video characteristics and contextual aspects into eight categories: (1) audio features, (2) visual features, (3) textual features, (4) instructor behavior, (5) learners’ activities, (6) interactive features (quizzes, etc.), (7) production style, and (8) instructional design. Also, we identify four representative research directions: (1) proposals of tools to support video-based learning, (2) studies with controlled experiments, (3) data analysis studies, and (4) proposals of design guidelines for learning videos. We find that the most explored characteristics are textual features followed by visual features, learner’s activities, and interactive features. Text of transcripts, video frames, and images (figures and illustrations) are most frequently used by tools that support learning through videos. The learner’s activity is heavily explored through log files in data analysis studies, and interactive features have been frequently scrutinized in controlled experiments. We complement our review by contrasting research findings that investigate the impact of video characteristics on the learning effectiveness, report on tasks and technologies used to develop tools that support learning, and summarize trends in the development of design guidelines to produce learning videos.



1.
Introduction

Videos become increasingly a choice in the everyday life of a learner,
facilitated not only by formal institutional platforms but also on commercial general-purpose video platforms such as YouTube.
In May 2019 it was stated that
about 500 hours of new content was uploaded every minute
(
Clement 2019
)
on YouTube, where around 50% of the views
have been associated with learning purposes
(
Smith et al. 2018
)
.
Furthermore, it was predicted that 82% of all IP traffic in the world will be caused by video consumption by 2022
(
Cisco 2018
)
.
Learners who turn to video sources have to rely on the technologies offered by the platform for providing suitable learning material.
Moreover, they need to trust the expertise (if any) of the content creators and instructors
in designing and producing effective learning videos.
However, even on dedicated platforms videos are published with no guidelines or good practices in mind
(
Guo et al. 2014
)
.
The reason is that, although efforts to better understand video-based learning (VBL) have increased
(
Poquet et al. 2018
;
Giannakos 2013
;
Yousef et al. 2014
)
, essential questions are still not answered, for example, what are the characteristics of a video

Previous VBL reviews have performed descriptive analysis on the growth tendency of the field, the
research directions, the discipline addressed by the videos, the production style, the mode of use (i.e., main or supplementary learning medium), the context or setting (i.e., formal or informal learning), video characteristics, and other dimensions
(
Poquet et al. 2018
;
Giannakos 2013
;
Yousef et al. 2014
)
.
Nevertheless, they have put special emphasis on experimental research, and did not dive into
different research directions.

In this paper,
we present a comprehensive review of 257 articles on video-based learning from computer science databases for the period from 2016 to 2021 using the PRISMA guidelines
(
Page et al. 2021
)
.
Compared to other reviews, we have collected the largest number of articles and provide a comprehensive overview on VBL research.
As a consequence, we can achieve a more robust analysis of the state of the art in the field.
In contrast to previous reviews that focused on experimental research, we identify and consider four main types of research articles:

(1)

proposals of tools to support video-based learning,

(2)

studies with controlled experiments,

(3)

data analysis studies, and

(4)

proposals of design guidelines for learning videos.

We present a close investigation of

(a)

video characteristics,

(b)

research approaches,

(c)

tasks and technologies used to develop tools, frameworks, and pipelines that support learning,

(d)

guidelines to design and create learning videos, as well as

(e)

findings from controlled experiments and data analysis.

Furthermore, we derive a taxonomy that organizes video
characteristics and contextual aspects into eight categories (and further sub-categories):

(1)

audio features,

(2)

visual features,

(3)

textual features,

(4)

instructor behavior,

(5)

learners’ activities,

(6)

interactive features,

(7)

production style, and

(8)

instructional design.

The presented results are relevant for different audiences:

(a)

platform providers who wish to support their users with the best possible content,

(b)

content producers who aim to develop effective learning resources,

(c)

teachers who want to enhance their teaching by
adopting multimedia resources, and

(d)

researchers who seek to update on the current state of VBL, identify potential research gaps, and bring in ideas for further research.

This review is an extended version of our previous work
(
Navarrete et al. 2021
)
, where we have presented a review of the literature on video-based learning (VBL) for only two years (2020 and 2021).
Here, we expand the reviewed period to 2016-2021 and also include the most relevant results of the articles by contrasting, analyzing and summarizing their findings.

The rest of the paper continues with a discussion of related reviews (Section
2
), the description of the methodology (Section
3
), the analysis of the field of video-based learning, which provides an introduction to the importance of identifying the characteristics of learning videos (Section
4
), and the results of the review (Section
5
).
After that, we discuss the impact of said video characteristics on learning effectiveness (Section
6

Finally, we draw conclusions and point out limitations and future work (Section

3.
Methodology

Figure 1.
The diagram shows the process of finding VBL-related articles in the databases. From an initial set of 390 papers, we removed duplicates and papers that did not fit the inclusion criteria. The final set consists of 257 articles from 2016 to 2021.

The methodology for our systematic review was inspired by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines
(
Page et al. 2021
)
.
They provide a guide to summarize results of reviewed research articles in an appropriate manner.

The papers covered by this review were found at SpringerLink
(
spr 2022
)
, the Digital Library of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM DL)
(
acm 2022
)
, and the Digital Bibliography and Library Project (DBLP)
(
dbl 2022
)
.
These are focused in computer science (CS) domains, and therefore, fit well our task of reviewing the technological aspects of video-based learning.
In each of these databases, we used advanced search options as a search strategy to refine our results.
In ACM DL, we applied filters to retrieve publications that match the query terms only in the title and that fit the time frame.
In SpringerLink, we applied the same filters complemented by an additional filter to obtain articles only in CS disciplines.
In DBLP, however, we did not apply any filter since the search engine covers only CS and already retrieves articles that only match the query term in the title and sorts them all per year.

4.
Video-based Learning

In this section, we discuss important aspects of video-based learning, potentials, challenges, and video characteristics. This lays the groundwork and motivates our literature review in the following section.

Potentials and Challenges:
Video-based learning is a terminology that has already been adopted in several studies (e.g.,
(
Giannakos 2013
;
Poquet et al. 2018
;
Yousef et al. 2014
;
Sablić et al. 2021
)
), and can be considered as the task of learning using video sources, that is, acquiring knowledge and skills through video content. Videos have been implemented in classrooms as a teaching
medium
(
Poquet et al. 2018
)
and they form an important part of students’ habits
(
Smith et al. 2018
)
.
The trends show that videos might become the main learning medium in online education
(
Hansch et al. 2015
)
due to its
wide availability.
Learning platforms, such as MOOCs, can disseminate videos in simple and scalable ways
(
Giannakos 2013
;
Guo et al. 2014
)
.

The debate about the benefits of VBL continues.
Yousef et al.
(
Yousef et al. 2014
)
, for instance, concluded from their results that learning videos are effective because they can improve the learning outcome, motivation, engagement, satisfaction, interaction, and communication between learners.
They further argued that videos can support various learning styles and convey information otherwise hard to communicate by modalities such as plain text.
However, the question of how to further support different learners’ needs remains open, since, for example, VBL usually does not allow synchronous communication and feedback as in classic classroom settings.

Moreover, further issues have been found in the way videos are designed and created.
Guo et al.
(
Guo et al. 2014
)
found in their interviews to video producers
that “video editing was not done with any specific pedagogical “design patterns” in mind”
(
Guo et al. 2014
, p. 45)
and “MOOC video producers currently base their decisions on anecdotes, folk wisdom, and best practices distilled from studies with at most dozens of subjects and hundreds of video watching sessions”
(
Guo et al. 2014
, p. 42)
.
Additionally, methods from typical classroom scenarios have been
transferred to create learning videos, however, it is unclear whether this can be successful
(
Guo et al. 2014
)
.

Examination of video characteristics:
Learning videos can be a complex learning medium since audio, visual, textual, and a large set of derived features might collide when expressing a message.
This complexity leads us to the following research question: Which of these characteristics make a video effective for learning?
The analysis and exploration of characteristics in learning videos have been examined for different purposes, from experiments that try to understand the impact on the learning effectiveness to the construction of tools for supporting learning.
Consequently, there is a multitude of video characteristics that have been extracted, studied, and manipulated.
These can range from low-level characteristics (e.g., text color, visual luminance, audio energy) to high-level characteristics such as models of a video’s semantic content.

Video-based learning does not only involve the video and its
instructors but also the surrounding hosting system or e-learning platform.
Therefore, we analyzed characteristics related to the platform, for example, those related to the learner’s activity, such as the usage of play and pause buttons, or number of views and likes.
Also, more complex characteristics attempt to consider the human aspects in the video (e.g., instructor gestures and speech).
In out analysis, we focused exclusively on characteristics related to the video content and its hosting platform while excluding learner-related characteristics, as for instance, previous knowledge, age, gender, etc.

5.1.1.
Information Extraction Tools

The reviewed articles in this section focused mainly on the extraction of text from videos (handwritten or machine printed) and metadata, as well as
the extraction of key information such as the extraction of key video segments.

Extraction of text:

We found seven proposals that extracted handwritten text
(
Davila et al. 2021
;
Kota et al. 2020
;
Kota et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2019a
;
Lee et al. 2017b
;
Garber et al. 2017
)
and two papers on extracting machine-printed text (e.g., from slide-based videos)
(
Zhong and Ma 2018
;
Khattabi et al. 2019
)
.
Davila et al.
(
Davila et al. 2021
)
retrieved handwriting using a convolutional neural network (CNN).
Kota et al.
(
Khattabi et al. 2019
)
used multiscale histogram of gradients for feature representation and deep neural networks to extract handwriting.
In another article, Kota et al.
(
Kota et al. 2019
)
detected bounding boxes with handwriting and used
clustering algorithms to group them, which could identify also content that was occluded by the instructor when she was standing in front of the whiteboard.
The handwriting was then extracted by enclosing it in bounding boxes and binarizing it.
Lee et al.
(
Lee et al. 2017b
)
detected changes
in the the handwriting pixels and extracted them using an adaptive threshold and time-series denoising techniques.
Davila and Zanibbi
(
Davila and Zanibbi 2018
)
extracted handwritten formulas using a random forest classifier trained on handwritten symbols.
Xu et al.
(
Xu et al. 2019a
)
identified the main instructor’s actions such as writing and erasing as clues to identify the video content.
The actions were detected using pose estimation technologies.
In the same way, Garber et al.
(
Garber et al. 2017
)
identified handwriting through instructor’s actions of writing and erasing.
The instructor could be removed by masking the regions where there is a change among video frames.
Zhong and Ma
(
Zhong and Ma 2018
)
extracted machine printed text using the maximally stable extremal regions method to detect candidate regions and clustering techniques were used to ensemble lines of text.
Finally, Khattabi et al.
(
Khattabi et al. 2019
)
detected parts of the video that contained text using an AdaBoost meta-classifier.

Extraction of key information:
Ghauri et al.
(
Ghauri et al. 2020
)
classified key segments in videos using different fusion schemes and bi-long-short-term memory network modules.
Che et al.
(
Che et al. 2016
)
detected the most important video segments using the slides structure and speech characteristics.
Kravvaris and Kermanidis
(
Kravvaris and Kermanidis 2019
)
identified important topics in the transcripts using Latent Dirichlet Allocation.
They contrasted them with relevant terms from the learners’ comments which were found using the Maximum Likelihood algorithm.
Lin et al.
(
Lin et al. 2019
)
classified knowledge from non-knowledge by using a CNN that processes image and sound simultaneously.
The classifier is trained on public datasets containing audio and visual human conversations, cooking events, and also collected video lectures; however, it was not clarified what the criteria were to classify something as "knowledge".
Similarly, Liu et al.
(
Liu et al. 2018b
)
extracted medical information according to their level of knowledge (low or high) by building a Logistic Regression classifier using the text from transcripts.
Finally, for computer programming video tutorials, Ponzanelli et al.
(
Ponzanelli et al. 2019
)
classified segments according to the kind of content, for example, theoretical concepts, software source code, etc.
The classifier employs temporal, structural, semantic, and code-related characteristics.

Extraction of metadata:

Jung et al.
(
Jung et al. 2019
)
extracted keywords from transcripts using the TextRank algorithm. Tags were extracted using singular value decomposition techniques and other relevant information was extracted using entity recognition techniques.
Escobar-Avila et al.
(
Escobar-Avila et al. 2017
)
compared three algorithms (term frequency–inverse document frequency, Latent Dirichlet Allocation using genetic algorithms, and the BM25F ranking function) to find the best technique to extract tags from video titles, description, and transcripts.
Gomes et al.
(
Jr. et al. 2019
)
extracted related topics with the help of a knowledge base, where a knowledge graph was built measuring the semantic similarity between concepts.
The similarity was based on the number of resources (Uniform Resource Identifiers of instances in the knowledge base) the videos share and the categories that the resources share, and was calculated with the Sorensen-Dice coefficient.
Furini
(
Furini 2018
)
extracted tags by measuring the most frequent words.
Ghosh et al.
(
Ghosh et al. 2021
)
detected
concepts that are not familiar or do not belong to the domain of the video content
but could be relevant to better understand the video.
The concepts were linked to Wikipedia articles, which were used to build a concept similarity network.
Unfamiliar topics were extracted using community detection techniques.
Similarly, Nangi et al.
(
Nangi et al. 2019
)
extracted unfamiliar topics, which were considered as cross-discipline concepts, also using community analysis in a concept graph.

Other:
Part of the studies presented tools to extract key-phrases
(
Yadid and Yahav 2016
;
Albahr et al. 2021
)
, slides
(
Guan et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2017
)
, and
software source code (from video tutorials to learn computer programming)
(
Khandwala and Guo 2018
;
Yadid and Yahav 2016
;
Alahmadi et al. 2018
)
.

5.1.2.
Interactive Tools

The development of interactive tools (tools that implement features allowing for communication and interaction between the video and the learner) mainly targeted in-video annotations, quizzes embedded in the video, and, interestingly, feedback to the learner for preventing mind wandering.

In-video Annotations:

These tools offered a feature that allowed the learner to take notes while watching the video, and also were accompanied with other supporting characteristics.
Fong et al.
(
Fong et al. 2018
)
suggested a tool in which the learner can interact not only through annotations but also interface elements for navigation.
Liu et al.
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
allowed the learner to create annotations and highlight parts of the transcript.
Malchow et al.
(
Malchow et al. 2018
)
let the learner create annotations for each slide presented in the video.
Lai et al.
(
Lai et al. 2018
)
did not only offer text-based annotations but also sketch-based annotations where the learner could draw
into the video frame.
Similarly, Singh et al.
(
Singh et al. 2016
)
created a tool for text-based and sketch-based annotations, and supported the student in comment creation, color-coding, and
interface elements for navigation.
Nguyen and Liu
(
Nguyen and Liu 2016
)
automated the note taking process by tracking eye gaze.
The tracking system automatically extracted relevant parts as candidate notes.
Deb et al.
(
Deb et al. 2017
)
allowed the viewer to create annotations but did not introduce another supporting feature.

Quizzes:
Herault et al.
(
Herault et al. 2018
)
proposed a 360-degree interactive environment with quizzes embedded in the video and
feedback to support the learner.
Kohen-Vacs et al.
(
Kohen-Vacs et al. 2016
)
introduced a tool to embed quizzes and announcements along the playback into the video.
Walk et al.
(
Walk et al. 2020
)
included quizzes in the video with the aim of enhancing active learning.
Wachtler et al.
(
Wachtler et al. 2016b
)
presented an application that can include several types of questions such as single- and multiple-choice questions, as well as questions to the instructor.
Ma and Ma
(
Ma and Ma 2019
)
developed an automatic question generation method using the video transcript and an external
knowledge graph.
Wachtler and Ebner
(
Wachtler and Ebner 2019
)
created an application to schedule quizzes and other interactive features along the playing video.

Feedback to learner for preventing mind wandering:
Lin et al.
(
Lin and Chen 2019
)
introduced feedback in learning videos to alert the viewers in mind-wandering states.
The tool kept track of brainwave signals to detect loss of focus and recommended to review again parts of the video.
Jo and Lim
(
Jo and Lim 2017
)
instead used a game that identified high-frequency words.
To detect mind wandering, the game consisted in presenting a word to the learner and ask if that word was present previously in the video.
Robal et al.
(
Robal et al. 2018
)
made use of web page, mouse, and face tracking technologies to determine whether the viewer was paying attention.
If the user was inattentive, the system rang a bell, displayed a red frame around the video, and paused it.
Sharma et al.
(
Sharma et al. 2016a
)
employed eye gaze tracking to identify mind wandering and displayed red marks when distraction was detected.
In a similar way, Llanda
(
Llanda 2019b
)
offered a tool that provided feedback to the learner, although, not with regard to mind wandering but to the sentiment state.
The tool tracked the viewers’ emotions by analyzing face patterns and detecting negative sentiment to consequently suggest re-watching the video.

Other:
Some studies suggested tools to create videos that integrate 360-degree characteristics
(
Herault et al. 2018
;
Koumaditis and Chinello 2019
;
Berns et al. 2018
)
.
Other papers introduced clickable objects
(
Jung et al. 2018
;
To et al. 2016
)
, interactive concept maps
(
Liu et al. 2018a
)
, feedback for the learners adapted to their previously identified
proficiency level
(
Zhu et al. 2017
)
, and tools to interact directly with the video content for practicing, for example, image editing
(
Kleftodimos and Evangelidis 2016
)
and computer programming
(
Bao et al. 2019
;
Khandwala and Guo 2018
)
.

5.1.3.
Recommender and Retrieval Systems

Recommender systems have mainly considered the similarity between transcripts and metadata to suggest new videos.
There were, however, other interesting characteristics that have been exploited, for instance, video popularity (e.g., likes and views), learning style, teaching style, etc.

Based on the similarity of transcripts and metadata:
Zhao et al.
(
Zhao et al. 2018a
)
suggested recommendations based on topic similarity, whereby the video is represented by means of its latent topics using the Latent Dirichlet Allocation algorithm.
Waykar and Bharathi
(
Waykar and Bharathi 2017
)
retrieved videos based on keywords extracted from on-screen text and also the texture of keyframes using a texture histogram.
Then, the videos were arranged with the help of Probability Extended Neighbor Classification.
Bleoanca et al.
(
Bleoanca et al. 2020
)
retrieved the top-N videos based on topic similarity using Latent Semantic Indexing to extract them, clustering techniques to group videos by discipline, and Wikipedia articles to augment the query context.
Basu et al.
(
Basu et al. 2016b
)
recommended videos when the learner was navigating Wikipedia pages.
They used the Latent Dirichlet Allocation algorithm to extract relevant topics from Wikipedia content and video transcripts.
Jung et al.
(
Jung et al. 2019
)
recommended similar videos based on keywords, tags, and the readability of the video.
In a similar manner, Ji et al.
(
Ji et al. 2018
)
generated recommendations using metrics of linguistic difficulty and similarity of keywords identified by the TextRank algorithm.
Jordán et al.
(
Jordán et al. 2020
)
built a system that considers not only the characteristics of the current video but also previous videos watched by the learner.
Walk et al.
(
Walk et al. 2020
)
made use of word embeddings constructed from the video transcript and compared them against the user search query to retrieve the most similar video segment.
Tavakoli et al.
(
Tavakoli et al. 2020
)
built a system to recommend videos for learning skills required in the labor market.
The authors use the skills that can be learned from the video and the skills demanded by the market together with video popularity features (e.g., likes and views) and length.

Based on ontologies and external knowledge bases:
Schulten et al.
(
Schulten et al. 2020
)
determined related concepts through external ontologies based on Wikipedia articles and extracted keywords using the term frequency–inverse document frequency algorithm to discover new learning resources
through a web search API.
In the same way, Borges and Reis
(
Borges and dos Reis 2019
)
extracted concepts and their relationships from an existing knowledge base to recommend videos.
Coccoli and Vercelli
(
Coccoli and Vercelli 2016
)
extracted concepts and also related concepts by linking the content to Wikipedia articles.
The learners could receive recommendations of videos and also Web pages.
Borges and Silveira
(
Borges and Silveira 2019
)

retrieved videos by analyzing the subject-object-predicate construction of the user query and searching keywords in a semantic graphic database.

Playlists:
Aytekin et al.
(
Aytekin et al. 2020
)
assembled a set of videos for recommendations based on prerequisite relationship detection techniques, that is, identifying when a concept refers to another concept.
Likewise, Tang et al.
(
Tang et al. 2021
)
recommended a playlist by building a concept map that showed the most relevant concepts in videos and how they are linked to each other.
The authors also considered the sentiment analyzed from likes, dislikes, and comments.
Furini et al.
(
Furini et al. 2018
)
recommended a playlist composed of video segments that covered the keywords given by the learner.
For segmenting the video, silence periods and also visual transitions were detected using histogram-based techniques.
Techniques for optical character recognition were employed to understand the video content and match it to the user query.
Cooper et al.
(
Cooper et al. 2018
)
recommended videos based on the main topics, which were found by using the Latent Dirichlet Allocation algorithm and inter-topic relationships from the course syllabus.
For creating the playlist, the authors used sequential pattern mining techniques to discover relationships.

Other:
Further studies presented recommender systems based on video popularity
(
Kravvaris and Kermanidis 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2018b
)
, videos delivered by the same instructor
(
Guan et al. 2019
;
Imran et al. 2019
)
, viewer’s learning style
(
Ciurez et al. 2019
)
, the current software application that the learner is using
(
Fraser et al. 2019
)
, and multiple pedagogical criteria
(
Acuña-Soto et al. 2020
)
.

5.1.4.
Classifiers and Predictors

Classifiers and predictors
were mainly used to estimate learning outcome, while some other noticeable tasks were discipline classification, instructor behavior prediction, and dropout prediction.

Learning outcome prediction:
Lemay and Doleck
(
Lemay and Doleck 2020
)
used a repeated incremental pruning algorithm to predict the learners’ grades based on the frequency of video viewing.
Mbouzao et al.
(
Mbouzao et al. 2020
)
predicted whether a learner will pass or fail a MOOC course using attendance rate (number of videos the student played divided by the number of videos mandatory to watch),
utilization rate (percentage of video play time activity divided by the sum of video lengths of all videos), and watching index (attendance rate multiplied by utilization rate) metrics in a straightforward rule that compared a single student against the average.
Mubarak et al.
(
Mubarak et al. 2021
)
trained a long-short-term memory network to predict the learning outcome as pass or fail based on learner’s activity data (e.g., play, pause, speed, etc.).
Furukawa et al.
(
Furukawa et al. 2020
)
used the number of views, interval of video views (e.g., 0 to 19 days), and the first access to videos in a multiple regression predictor to evaluate whether the learner will pass or fail a MOOC course.
Similarly, Korosi et al.
(
Korösi et al. 2018
)
predicted whether a learner will pass or fail a MOOC course using random forests and bagging based on characteristics related to platform usage (e.g., scrolling actions) and not representing the video itself.
In a different way to determine learning success, Lallé and Conati
(
Lallé and Conati 2020
)
clustered learners into low and high achievers using
characteristics such as the average proportion of videos watched per week.
Aouifi et al.
(
Aouifi et al. 2021
)
, instead, predicted learning outcome in terms of passing or failing the next quiz.
The authors take as characteristics the sequence of previous quiz results to feed them into a recurrent neural network.
Brinton et al.
(
Brinton et al. 2016
)
also predicted whether the learner will pass a quiz successfully but using
characteristics such as play and pause, revisiting, etc.

Discipline classification:
Stoica et al.
(
Stoica et al. 2019
)
built a support vector machine trained on a Wikipedia dataset to classify video transcripts and keywords into the discipline to which they belong.
Dessi et al.
(
Dessì et al. 2017
)
introduced a model based on variants of support vector machine algorithms to classify videos into their corresponding disciplines using transcripts, on-screen text features, and a relevance score for each feature.
Basu et al.
(
Basu et al. 2016a
)
clustered videos according to their disciplines by analyzing the topic similarity between the transcript and Wikipedia articles.
Othman et al.
(
Othman et al. 2017
)
classified videos into categories such as Education and Science and Technology but also to rather different types including How-to and Style.
The authors used decision trees and a Naive Bayes classifier for classification based on a various video metadata features.
In a different approach, Chatbri et al.
(
Chatbri et al. 2017
)
built a CNN fed with image-like representations that characterize the transcript to then classify the video into its discipline.
The images are generated using statistical co-occurrence transformation techniques, which arranges the transcript into an array of characters and uses ASCII codes of the characters to create gray scale images.

Instructor behavior prediction:
Chen et al.
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
built a neural network to predict teachers’ enthusiasm using facial expressions, voice, volume, and pose characteristics.
Godavarthi
(
Godavarthi et al. 2020
)
also used facial landmarks characteristics but to predict instructor emotions using a CNN.
Sharma et al.
(
Sharma et al. 2016b
)
predicted video liveliness (energetic or active) using two different long-short-term memory networks fed with audio features, visual features from the instructor actions, and production setup (e.g., instructor sitting or standing, slide-based video or whiteboard-based video).
Xu et al.
(
Xu et al. 2020
)
predicted the instructor’s actions (write, pick eraser, erase, etc.) based on pose characteristics using a random forest and adaptive CNN classifiers.

Dropout prediction:
Jeon and Park
(
Jeon and Park 2020
)
used
learner’s activity data to predict weekly course dropout in a MOOC platform by embedding click data sequences and feeding them into a gate recurrent unit.
Furini et al.
(
Furini et al. 2021
)
investigated dropout prediction in an e-learning platform relying on characteristics related to video access, and using random forest and K-nearest neighbor classification.

Other:
Further articles proposed models to predict how engaging a video was
(
Bulathwela et al. 2020
)
, when a learner will jump back
(
Zhang 2017
)
, and to classify learners’ comments
(
Poché et al. 2017
)
.

5.1.5.
Navigation and Segmentation Tools

Many articles proposed navigation in the style of a table of contents, but also navigation using keywords and visual elements has been presented.
The suggested tools commonly use segmentation techniques to match the extracted information with the corresponding video segment to allow user navigation.

Table of contents navigation:
Such tools consist of a table of contents which the user can click on and jump to the corresponding part in the video.
These approaches usually allow users to find segments where a certain sub-topic or keyword is presented.
The segments to which the sub-topic or keyword belong are identified and indexed to allow the learner to click on them and to jump there directly
(
Das and Das 2020
;
Kravvaris and Kermanidis 2019
;
Mahapatra et al. 2018a
;
Mukherjee et al. 2019
;
Husain and Meena 2019
;
Gandhi et al. 2016
;
Kumar et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2017
;
Yadav et al. 2016
;
Bian and Huang 2019
)
.
Sub-topics and keywords have been extracted with technologies such as the Latent Dirichlet Allocation algorithm
(
Mukherjee et al. 2019
;
Kravvaris and Kermanidis 2019
;
Husain and Meena 2019
;
Bian and Huang 2019
)
, TextRank algorithm
(
Yadav et al. 2016
;
Mahapatra et al. 2018b
)
, Markov chain Monte Carlo method
(
Bian and Huang 2019
)
, and concept extraction algorithms
(
Mahapatra et al. 2018b
)
.

Keyword navigation:
Another frequent kind of proposal for navigation extracted keywords and indexed them according to specific segments of the video
(
Ohnishi et al. 2019
;
Koka et al. 2020
;
Kravvaris and Kermanidis 2019
;
Kanadje et al. 2016
;
Kumar et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2017
)
.

Navigation using visual elements:
This kind of approach enabled exploration by presenting visual parts of the video, for example, main slides, figures, tables, charts, etc., which the viewer clicked and then jumped to the corresponding segment in the video
(
Zhao et al. 2018b
;
Ma et al. 2017
;
Kumar et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2017
;
Yadav et al. 2016
)
.

Video segmentation:
One of the natural ways of splitting a video is to detect slide changes,
which can be used to extract key video frames.
For video segmentation, TextTiling
(
Mukherjee et al. 2019
;
Zhao et al. 2017
)
and dynamic programming
(
Gandhi et al. 2016
)
were suggested.
For the detection of slide changes, edge-based differences between video frames
(
Zhao et al. 2017
)
and overlapping patches
(
Mahapatra et al. 2018a
)
were expoited.
Also, we found studies dedicated to exclusively find better segmentation methods for learning videos (e.g.
(
Soares and Barrére 2019
;
Galanopoulos and Mezaris 2019
;
Soares and Barrére 2018
)
).
These approaches used natural language processing for phrase extraction, named entity recognition, and part-of-speech-tagging
(
Galanopoulos and Mezaris 2019
)
to segment the video.
Other studies used audio features to segment according to sound characteristics (e.g., power spectral density, fundamental frequencies, low level acoustic characteristics)
(
Soares and Barrére 2018
;
Soares and Barrére 2019
)
.

5.1.6.
Analytic Tools

Analytic tools relied heavily in
the learner’s activity data to understand video usage, and in some cases, contrasted this usage with the learners’ performance.
Another small group of analytic tools used characteristics such as metadata and eye gaze.

Based on learner’s activity:

General analytic tools exploited log data of learner activity from e-learning platforms to show how videos are used.
Walk et al.
(
Walk et al. 2020
)
developed a tool that shows the audience retention for specific video segments, the number of times the segment was re-watched, and how much time learners spent on it.
Wachtler et al.
(
Wachtler et al. 2016b
)
presented a tool that provided an analysis about the video (e.g., number of views) and also about the student (e.g., extent to which the video was watched).
Long et al.
(
Long et al. 2018
)
proposed a framework to analyze patterns regarding video usage, for instance, usage of a single video, specific video segments, all videos in a course, etc.

Other tools used log data to understand the learners’ behavior in relation to their academic performance.
Min et al.
(
Min et al. 2019
)
suggested an analytic system that contrasted the log data and the student performance on the quizzes with the watching behavior.
He et al.
(
He et al. 2018a
)
analyzed the viewing behavior using two metrics, where the first one records if the learner watched the video and the second one records the amount of video viewing.
Later, He at al.
(
He et al. 2018b
)
showed how these metrics can be adopted to understand the results in academic performance.

In addition,
learner activity data has been used in other interesting analytic tools for diverse purposes.
Mbouzao et al.
(
Mbouzao et al. 2019
)
presented a method to embed the viewer’s sequence of activities into a vector and found if they characterize a specific video, this is, if a certain video elicits particular behavior.
Bothe and Meinel
(
Bothe and Meinel 2020
)
developed a metric to track viewers’ visits to previous MOOC videos, considering that learners do not always strictly follow the path suggested by a platform.
Choi et al.
(
Choi et al. 2019
)
included smartwatch data in their system log data to improve the measurement of interest and difficulty of a video.

Based on video metadata:
Othman et al.
(
Othman et al. 2016
)

mined MOOC videos by exploiting their metadata (e.g., video length, comments, title, country).
Stepanek and Dorn
(
Stepanek and Dorn 2017
)
presented a framework to analyze viewers’ comments to identify and predict the engagement generated by a video.
Sung et al.
(
Sung et al. 2016
)
analyzed viewers’ comments considering the sentiment, its relevance, its type, and its topic for displaying this information in the video timeline.

Other:
Zhang et al.
(
Zhang et al. 2018a
)

analyzed learning videos using data from eye tracking systems.

5.1.7.
Summarization Tools

Summarization
approaches aim at generating a concise and shorter
representation of the entire video.
Text summarization was the most applied approach, however, also summarization of visual elements was suggested.

Text-based summarization:
This kind of summarization was achieved by sentence segmentation, named entity recognition,
extraction of key phrase extract, subtitles, and other metadata using transcripts as a main source
(
Vimalaksha et al. 2019
;
Balasubramanian et al. 2016
;
Kim and Kim 2016
)
and on-screen text in a few cases
(
Xu et al. 2019b
;
Cagliero et al. 2019
)
.
Methods that supported this task were term frequency–inverse document frequency
(
Vimalaksha et al. 2019
)
, Naive Bayes
(
Balasubramanian et al. 2016
)
, and Latent Semantic Analysis
(
Yoo et al. 2021
)
for identifying the relevant information.

Image-based summarization:
Rahman et al.
(
Rahman et al. 2020
)
proposed a visual summary by extracting a group of
relevant images (figures or illustrations) from slide-based videos.
The image was included or not in the summary according to its importance, which was computed based on its size,
unique aspects in the image, and the duration of its occurrence in the video.

5.1.8.
Other

Further interesting articles proposed methods to create videos automatically

(
Hartholt et al. 2020
;
Hayashi et al. 2020
;
Furini 2021
)
, alleviate the split attention problem
(
Sharma et al. 2016c
;
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2017
)
, enhance speech recognition
(
Hernandez and Yang 2021
;
Sanchez-Cortina et al. 2016
)
, and find content that is similar not in other videos but in other media such as in textbooks
(
Wang et al. 2020a
)
and books
(
Horovitz and Ohayon 2020
)
.

5.4.
Design Guidelines

General guidelines:
Wijnker et al.
(
Wijnker et al. 2019
)
developed a framework that applies a film theory perspective to learning videos.
The authors provided guidance to analyze and categorize these videos according to their design, aim and type in film theory
(
Bordwell et al. 1993
)
.
Voronkin
(
Voronkin 2019
)
proposed universal guidelines focusing on methodological, psychological, didactic, ergonomic, and technical aspects.
Lange and Costley
(
Lange and Costley 2020
)
considered media delivery problems and addressed them by proposing guidelines that aim at improving the pace, intelligibility, quality, media diversity, and congruence.
The authors grounded their guidelines in the limited capacity theory in multimedia learning
(
Mayer 2014
)
,
generative theory
(
Mayer 2014
)
, and
extraneous processing research
(
Leppink et al. 2013
)
.
Dodson et al.
(
Dodson et al. 2018b
)
introduced a framework of active viewing that categorized learner’s watching behavior.
The authors stated that VBL can be designed in a way to promote this behavior.

Guidelines for specific disciplines
: Minnes et al.
(
Minnes et al. 2019
)
proposed design guidelines for producing short learning podcast videos for computer science courses and emphasized the use of representation characteristics, especially call-out boxes, zooming, and animations.
Hodges
(
Hodges 2018
)
also collected several recommendations for creating videos in CS.
The authors emphasized the need of captions, descriptions of animations as they occur, zooming in relevant code, and for video systems that allow learners to pause, seek, and speed up or slow down the video.
Stephenson
(
Stephenson 2019
)
concentrated on the development of programming videos following a specific content structure.
Lei et al.
(
Lei et al. 2016
)
discussed an approach to produce videos for Humanities disciplines. They suggested to favor short videos, use animations, highlight key concepts and terminologies, include an introduction and conclusions, employ consistent topography and graphic styles, and to add music in specific parts.
Ge and Li
(
Ge and Li 2020
)
identified beneficial characteristics to learn English as a foreign language.
They recommended to take care of the speech rate, sentences and vocabulary complexity, use of closely related examples, use of realistic visuals when higher levels of context are demanded, include the instructor, and use keyword subtitles instead of complete subtitles.

Guidelines for videos with interactive features

Weinert et al.
(
Weinert et al. 2020
)
introduced reference guidelines to design
Interaction Theory
(
Moore 1989
)
, Cognitive Load Theory
(
Sweller 1994
)
, and own research.
The authors recommended to include elements for interacting not only with the content but also with other learners and the instructor.
They also discussed recommendations for the best positions to place specific types of interactive features.
Hung et al.
(
Hung et al. 2018
)
proposed guidelines to design interactive tasks for learning video content based on literature on embodied cognition.
The authors propose that the video should include engaging, prompting, experiencing, facilitating, demonstrating, and questioning tasks.

6.
Impact of video characteristics on the learning effectiveness

In this section, we contrast, analyze, and summarize the findings of studies from controlled experiments and data analysis research. These are organized according to the different categories and subcategories of video characteristics.

6.1.
Instructor Behavior Characteristics

Gestures:
Study results showed that gestures do not have a significant impact on the learning outcome
(
Beege et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2019a
;
Horovitz and Mayer 2021
)
.
One study even found that gestures can cause negative comments
(
Ding 2018
)
.
However, learners still care about gestures, facial expressions, and body language
(
Shoufan 2019a
)
.
Among the benefits, gestures were found to reduce mental effort
(
Beege et al. 2020
)
, increase presence
(
Beege et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2016
)
and satisfaction
(
Wang et al. 2019a
)
.
Moreover, studies have obtained a positive effect on delayed learning outcome tests and a higher impact of human gestures compared to gestures of instructors as animations (human-like artificial agents)
(
Wang et al. 2019a
;
Horovitz and Mayer 2021
)
.

Speech:
Data analysis studies revealed that characteristics related to the speech such as speaking rate, lexical diversity, etc., affected how learners interacted with the video
(
Atapattu and Falkner 2017
)
.
Speech characteristics were also found to correlate with sound characteristics
(
Shi et al. 2019
)
.
Further findings showed that speech characteristics had an impact on dwell time
(
van der Sluis et al. 2016
)
and the level of understanding
(
Shoufan 2019a
)
.
For example, whether the instructor’s language was native or not significantly affected the level of understanding in the learner, and the number of likes in the video
(
Shoufan 2019a
;
Shoufan and Mohamed 2017
)
.
Also, pronunciation and intonation was found to affect the learner’s attention.
Based on these discoveries, it has been recommended to reduce lexical diversity, long sentences, and speech rate
(
Atapattu and Falkner 2017
)
.

Other:
Other characteristics related to instructor behavior which the learners cared about and had an impact were the appearance, clothes, and enthusiasm
(
Shoufan 2019a
;
Ding 2018
)
.
Interestingly, the gender did not have a significant impact
(
Shoufan 2019a
)
, it did not affect the learning outcome, the perception of self-efficacy
(
Hoogerheide et al. 2018
)
, and learners liking or disliking a video
(
Shoufan and Mohamed 2017
)
.
Yet, it was observed that learners perceived themselves more similar to the instructor when the gender was the same
(
Hoogerheide et al. 2018
)
.
With regard to instructors as animations, these were considered interesting and attractive
(
Ding 2018
)
.

6.2.
Interactive Features

Quizzes and questions
: Most of the studies that experimented with quizzes evidenced that the learning outcome was positively affected
(
Kleftodimos and Evangelidis 2018
;
Leisner et al. 2020
;
Wachtler et al. 2016a
;
Palaigeorgiou and Papadopoulou 2019
;
da Graça Campos Pimentel et al. 2019
)
.
For Palaigeorgiou and Papadopoulou
(
Palaigeorgiou and Papadopoulou 2019
)
, the positive effect was found when quizzes were included together with other interactive features.
For Wachtler et al.
(
Wachtler et al. 2016a
)
, the positive effect was found when quizzes were placed at the right time.

Furthermore, positive impacts were found on other aspects such as satisfaction
(
Kleftodimos and Evangelidis 2018
;
Palaigeorgiou and Papadopoulou 2019
)
and engagement
(
da Graça Campos Pimentel et al. 2019
)
.
Data analysis studies have also confirmed these findings.
Kovacs
(
Kovacs 2016
)
found that most viewers engaged in quizzes, which seemed to reduce dropout.
On the contrary, Ding
(
Ding 2018
)
showed that quizzes
improved understanding of the content. But they did not draw the learner’s attention in cases where the quizzes were complex and at the end of the video.

Virtual reality and 360-degree characteristics:

The studies in this categories commonly reported an improved learning outcome, however, not to a significant degree
(
Chen 2020
;
Araiza-Alba et al. 2021
;
Daher and Sleem 2021
;
Huang et al. 2020
)
.
Huang et al.
(
Huang et al. 2020
)
found positive effects only in certain parts of the post test.
Although statistically insignificant, Araiza-Alba et al.
(
Araiza-Alba et al. 2021
)
reported short- and long-term improvements in learning outcome.
Studies also showed differing impact of virtual reality and 360-degree environments on learner motivation.
While Huang et al.
(
Huang et al. 2020
)
found no positive effect on the motivation, Muñoz-Carpio et al.
(
Muñoz-Carpio et al. 2020
)
and Chen
(
Chen 2020
)
found that viewers felt indeed more motivated.
Regarding satisfaction, several findings confirmed an increase
(
Chen 2020
;
Muñoz-Carpio et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2020
)
.
Furthermore, Huang et al.
(
Huang et al. 2020
)
also found a positive impact on self-efficacy.
Our analysis over time shows that these kind of characteristics have been investigated more frequently in the last years
(
Chen 2020
;
Muñoz-Carpio et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2020
;
Araiza-Alba et al. 2021
;
Daher and Sleem 2021
)
.
Naturally, experiments in this category evaluated the impact in other non-common dependent variables such as sense of "being there"
(
Araiza-Alba et al. 2021
;
Guervós et al. 2019
)
and cybersickness
(
Guervós et al. 2019
)
.
Interestingly, most of these experiments were conducted with non-STEM videos
(
Chen 2020
;
Araiza-Alba et al. 2021
;
Daher and Sleem 2021
;
Huang et al. 2020
)
.

Other:
Most of the studies that experimented with in-video annotations found an increase in the learning outcome
(
Leisner et al. 2020
;
Taskin et al. 2019
;
Kuhail and Aqel 2020
)
, whereas neutral effects were found for cognitive load
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
.
Regarding navigational features, experiments using table content navigation
(
Kuhail and Aqel 2020
;
Cojean and Jamet 2017
)
and timeslines with clickable points
(
da Graça Campos Pimentel et al. 2019
;
Cojean and Jamet 2017
)
found a positive effect on the learning outcome
(
Kuhail and Aqel 2020
;
da Graça Campos Pimentel et al. 2019
;
Cojean and Jamet 2017
)
.
However, this was not always significant
(
da Graça Campos Pimentel et al. 2019
)
,
or depended
on the combination with other features to be more beneficial such as table content navigation together with timelines with markers
(
Cojean and Jamet 2017
)
.
According to Granjo and Rasteiro
(
Granjo and Rasteiro 2018
)
,
videos with interactive features needed to be used with other pedagogical techniques to improve the learning outcome.

6.3.
Visual and Audio Features

Instructor-related representational characteristics:
Tian and Bourguet
(
Tian and Bourguet 2016
)
observed that
the most common
gestures that instructors used as cues were pointing and extending (extend the arms away from the body with palms up) cues.
Results in evaluating the impact of these types of cues have been shown to increase the learning outcome in most cases
(
Wang et al. 2020b
;
Pi et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019b
;
Pi et al. 2017b
)
.
However, no significant effects
(
Sharma et al. 2016c
)
or even negative effects where found when the instructor was an animation
(
Moon and Ryu 2021
)
.

Content-related representational characteristics:
Examples of these characteristics are arrows, color-coded cues, highlighted content, etc.
Experiments showed that these cues improve the learning outcome in most cases
(
Wang et al. 2020b
;
Pi et al. 2017b
;
Sharma et al. 2016c
)
, but had no significant effect on cognitive load
(
Sharma et al. 2016c
)
or increased it
(
Moon and Ryu 2021
)
.
When comparing content-related cues with instructor-related ones, the results were contradictory.
In one case, instructor-related cues had a higher positive impact on the learning outcome than content-related cues when hand-pointing gestures were compared to arrows on slide-based videos
(
Pi et al. 2017b
)
.
In another case, contrary results were found
when comparing hand-pointing gestures (a pencil following the handwriting) with gaze overlay defined by an expert
(explicit drawing
of the path that learners should follow with their eyes)
(
Sharma et al. 2016c
)
.

Other visual and audio elements:
General visual and audio features such as the image and audio quality seemed to matter to the learners and caused positive effects on the learning outcome
(
Ding 2018
;
Schreiber and Dougherty 2017
)
.
In the experiments of Uchidiuno et al.
(
Uchidiuno et al. 2018
)
, English language students often avoided videos that did not have visual support (figures, charts, equations, or on-screen text) but presented only an instructor talking.
Interestingly, Ding
(
Ding 2018
)
found that music in the background helped to keep students focused.

6.4.
Production Style

Instructor presence:
Experiments have shown that viewers preferred a video in which the instructor appears
(
Pérez-Navarro et al. 2021
;
Davila et al. 2021
)
.
Hew and Lo
(
Hew and Lo 2020
)
observed that the three most widely used styles in a sample of YouTube videos in computer science had an instructor:

(1)

slide-based
videos,

(2)

classroom-style videos, and

(3)

Khan-style videos with talking head.

In addition, viewers voiced their preference for videos that show the instructor’s hands
(
Pérez-Navarro et al. 2021
)
.
However, although the presence of an instructor was preferred, it did not bring notable advantages
(
Davila et al. 2021
;
Hew and Lo 2020
)
.
Davila et al.
(
Davila et al. 2021
)
and Hew and Lo
(
Hew and Lo 2020
)
found that a talking head did not show a significant effect on the learning outcome, the social presence
(
Hew and Lo 2020
)
and the cognitive load
(
Davila et al. 2021
)
.
Experiments have also tested the effects of the instructor’s size in the video.
A small size seemed to cause higher learning gains
(
Pi et al. 2017a
)
and satisfaction
(
Cao et al. 2018
)
when the topic was not complex.
However, the size of the instructor had no significant effect on cognitive load, mental effort
(
Pi et al. 2017a
;
Cao et al. 2018
)
and social presence
(
Pi et al. 2017a
;
Cao et al. 2018
)
.

Dialogue-style vs. Monologue-style:
The experiments did not find a significant effect of dialogue-style over the monologue-style on learning gain
(
Ding et al. 2018
;
Nugraha et al. 2020
;
Lee and Muldner 2020
)
.
In addition, no effect on the social presence was found
(
Lee and Muldner 2020
)
.
Ohashi et al.
(
Ohashi et al. 2019
)
conducted an experiment using videos in computer programming topics, one using slides only, another one with slides accompanied by two puppets in a dialogue.
They found that
the
number of viewers of the video including the dialogue decreased over time (throughout a compulsory course).
The authors argued
that the puppets’ dialogue was effective at the beginning, but not when the learners became increasingly familiar with the course content.
Nevertheless, the dialogue style seemed to offer benefits with regard to self-efficacy
(
Lee and Muldner 2020
)
and positive sentiment
(
Ding et al. 2018
)
.

Animations:

Animations in videos caused higher learning outcomes when compared to

(a)

text-only videos (i.e., slide-based videos only with text, without illustrations, figures, charts, and similar),
(
Srivastava et al. 2020
)

(b)

textbooks
(
Cookson et al. 2020
)
, and

(c)

traditional live classroom lectures
(
Al-Khateeb and Alduwairi 2020
)
.

Animations also affected other aspects such as participation
(
Cookson et al. 2020
)
, cognitive load
(
Lee and Muldner 2020
)
, mental effort
(
Srivastava et al. 2020
)
, and perceived difficulty
(
Srivastava et al. 2019
)
.

Video tutorials and video lectures:
In several studies, learners believed that video tutorials
were more interesting, attractive, kept them engaged, and helped them understand better
(
Ding 2018
;
Ou et al. 2016
)
.
It was also found that learners tended to skip theoretical discussions, which were usually found in video lectures
(
Lei et al. 2019
)
.
However, other studies showed that learners not only watched lectures significantly more often than tutorials
(
Hildebrand and Ahn 2018
)
but also watched them significantly more often to completion
(
Thornton et al. 2017
)
.
In any case, Hildebrand and Ahn
(
Hildebrand and Ahn 2018
)
reported that both styles had a similar positive correlation with the learning outcome.

Other:
Other studies found that media diversity correlated positively with learning outcome, satisfaction, engagement, and interest
(
Costley and Lange 2017
)
.
Shoufan and Mohamed
(
Shoufan and Mohamed 2017
)
reported that the most predominant styles in CS videos were paper-based explanations, whiteboard-based explanations, slide-based videos, and Khan-style videos.

6.5.
Pace of the Video

Automatic pauses vs. non-automatic pauses:
No significant positive effects of automatic pauses in videos were found compared to non-automatic pauses (where learners can pause the video, also called self-pacing)
(
Schroeder et al. 2020
)
.
Indeed, systems with automatic pauses in videos were ineffective
(
Garrett 2021
)
or even increased cognitive load
(
Garrett 2021
;
Garrett 2018
)
.
Merkt et al.
(
Merkt et al. 2018
)
did not find advantages of automatic pauses even in long lasting videos.
On the contrary, viewers in the
non-automatic pauses condition were more successful and reported a lower mental effort
(
Garrett 2018
;
Schroeder et al. 2020
)
.

Video acceleration: The acceleration rate at which the video is watched was found to be important for learners
(
Ding 2018
)
.
Furthermore, there was evidence that students tend to accelerate the videos at least once
(
Yan and Baxter 2018
)
.
However, the impact reported on the learning outcome has been diverse.
On the one hand, Lang et al.
(
Lang et al. 2020
)
showed that when the learner accelerated the video, the learning outcome improved for learners in Stanford Online
(
veena 2020
)
(a platform of open online courses) in diverse disciplines such as CS and mathematics.
On the other hand, Garrett
(
Garrett 2021
)
reported that learners who watched
accelerated CS videos did not outperform those who watched
non-accelerated videos.
However, in this experiment the learner could not decide when to play, pause or accelerate the video but this was given automatically by the system.

6.6.
Beginners, Specialists, High and Low Performers

Learners show specific behaviors depending on their performance level with respect to the learning topic.
High achievers engaged in more diverse activity patterns (e.g., play, pause seek)
(
Rodríguez et al. 2018
)
, interacted more intensely with quizzes embedded in the video
(
Kovacs 2016
)
, and
watched videos more constantly every week
(
Rodríguez et al. 2018
)
.
Galster et al.
(
Galster et al. 2018
)
reported that
students who were more active through in-video annotations, also watched more videos.
One study suggested that if low-performance learners imitate this behavior, advantages could be attained
(
Angrave et al. 2020
)
.
However, Galster et al.
(
Galster et al. 2018
)
did not find differences in learning outcomes between more engaged and less engaged learners.

Regarding the differences between beginners and specialists, the findings showed that specialists engaged more often in different video activities than beginners
(
Li 2019
)
and achieved a higher learning outcome
(
Stöhr et al. 2019
)
.
Nevertheless, the same level of engagement was found in both groups and they also consumed the same amount of videos
(
Stöhr et al. 2019
;
Li 2019
)
.

6.7.
Video Completion and Length

Video completion:
It was reported that learners usually watched 30% to 50% of the total video length (on a YouTube channel
(
Saurabh and Gautam 2019
)
and on the MOOC platform edX
(
Stöhr et al. 2019
;
edx 2022
)
) and that skipping through videos was a very common behavior
(
Hildebrand and Ahn 2018
)
.
However, studies in other learning contexts obtained different results.
If the videos were part of a university course, most of the learners watched the full video
(
Yan and Baxter 2018
;
Giannakos et al. 2016
)
.
Regarding the gain in the learning outcome, studies showed that the extent to which a video was watched impacted the student’s grades
(
Hildebrand and Ahn 2018
;
Angrave et al. 2020
)
and that viewers who watched the video to completion were more likely to watch other videos
(
Wynn et al. 2019
)
.
Regarding dropping behavior, the findings show that this occurred within the first five seconds.
On the other hand, when the learners watched the video for a longer time, dropping behavior seemed to be reduced
(
Wynn et al. 2019
)
.
Wynn et al. found that when viewers surpassed the first five minutes, they were likely to watch the video to completion
(
Wynn et al. 2019
)
.
Strategies such as including in-video quizzes seemed to reduce video dropout
(
Kovacs 2016
)
.

Video length:
Several studies have concluded that short learning videos were preferred by learners
(
Saurabh and Gautam 2019
;
Ding 2018
;
Rahim and Shamsudin 2019
;
Engeness and Nohr 2019
)
.
Videos within five to seven minutes were preferred in two studies
(
Engeness and Nohr 2019
;
Ding 2018
)
.
One of the studies experimented with videos with talking head, PowerPoint slides, drawings on a board, and animations for learning English as a foreign language by undergraduate students
(
Ding 2018
)
.
The other study experimented with videos of a MOOC on digital competence that presented pedagogical aspects and tutorials for teachers
(
Engeness and Nohr 2019
)
.
Further findings in both studies showed that although longer videos up to 10 minutes were not preferred, they were still well received
(
Engeness and Nohr 2019
;
Ding 2018
)
.
Saurabh and Gautam
(
Saurabh and Gautam 2019
)
found that the most popular learning videos lasted 10 to 15 minutes.
Nevertheless, contrary findings presented by Shoufan
(
Shoufan 2019b
)
showed that videos perceived as too short were not well received.
In this study, the videos were selected from YouTube with different liking ratios (number of likes of a video divided by the sum of likes and dislikes), different instructors, different speaking accents, and covering different topics in digital logic design and embedded systems targeted to students of an engineering program.
For lecture styles, Giannakos et al.
(
Giannakos et al. 2016
)
found that the preferred length of videos in software engineering was close to the traditional 45-minute classroom lectures for university students of a computer science degree.
In any case, the context is an important factor to consider to select an appropriate video length
(
Shoufan 2019b
)
.

6.8.
Video popularity

Saurabh and Gautam
(
Saurabh and Gautam 2019
)
found that viewers tend to like videos that helped them to learn and dislike the ones that did not.
Shoufan and Mohamed
(
Shoufan and Mohamed 2017
)
discovered that the number of likes for learning videos increase

(a)

with higher video resolution,

(b)

with higher talking rates (words per second),

(c)

when English was the native language of the instructor,

(d)

when the production style included paper-based or a whiteboard-based explanations, and

(e)

when more than one production style was used.

Later, Shoufan
(
Shoufan 2019b
)
, found in a survey that learners liked a video when they perceived that

(a)

the explanation seemed appropriate

(b)

the presentation used animations, diverse colors, understandable handwriting, and appropriate usage of supporting technology and software,

(c)

the video seemed interesting and easy to focus on,

(d)

practical examples were included

(e)

the instructor was concise, used a simple vocabulary, and

(f)

had a confident and clear voice.

On the other hand, in the same study, the authors
(
Shoufan 2019b
)
found that learners perceived that they disliked a video when the explanation was inappropriate, there were no practical examples, the problem was solved in non-common ways, unneeded details were included, the voice of the instructor seemed dull or annoying, or the content was boring and difficult to focus on.
Additionally, in a different study, Shoufan
(
Shoufan 2019a
)
analyzed a survey and found that learners perceived that they disliked a video when they felt like they did not understand the video content, when the technical quality of audio or video was insufficient, when the instructor seemed incompetent, not friendly, or not enthusiastic, or the length of the video was not appropriate.
Curiously, it was found that lengthy videos appeared to generate slightly less dislikes
(
Shoufan and Mohamed 2017
)
.

Lee and Osop
(
Lee et al. 2017a
)
detected that popular videos have a high amount of comments with strong negative sentiment.
Moreover, Lei et al.
(
Lei et al. 2019
)
found that popular videos had a high number of views but lower retention rates.
The authors found that popular videos usually provided a general background, while less popular videos dived deeper into the topic, which might be the reason why they had higher retention rates.
Indeed, it was warned that higher amount of views and comments might not necessarily entail a good video quality
(
Lee et al. 2017a
)
.

Conclusions

Figure 9.
the diagram shows all video characteristics identified in this review

In this paper, we have reviewed 257 articles in the field of video-based learning (2016-2021) following the PRISMA guidelines
(
Page et al. 2021
)
.
Throughout the review, we

(a)

identified major research directions

(b)

surveyed researched video characteristics
and organized them into a taxonomy,

(c)

reported on target tasks and technologies,

(d)

revealed derived trends in instructional design guidelines to produce videos, and

(e)

contrasted research findings and summarized
the impact of the features on the learning effectiveness.

Research directions:
We have identified four
research directions in the field of VBL:

(1)

proposals of tools,

(2)

reports on controlled experiments,

(3)

data analysis studies, and

(4)

design guidelines proposals.

The majority of papers proposed tools for the support of VBL, while a moderate proportion of papers presented studies on experiments in controlled settings. A small part of articles were dedicated to data analysis studies and design guidelines.

Schulten et al. 2020
;
Borges and dos Reis 2019
;
Coccoli and Vercelli 2016
;
Borges and Silveira 2019
)
.
Some interesting approaches considered other characteristics such as video readability
(
Jung et al. 2019
)
, the skills demanded by the labor market
(
Tavakoli et al. 2020
)
, video popularity
(
Kravvaris and Kermanidis 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2018b
)
, videos from the same instructor
(
Guan et al. 2019
;
Imran et al. 2019
)
, learning style
(
Ciurez et al. 2019
)
, and pedagogical characteristics
(
Acuña-Soto et al. 2020
)
.

For classifiers and predictors the main task was to predict the learning outcome using
learner’s activity data
(
Lemay and Doleck 2020
;
Mbouzao et al. 2020
;
Mubarak et al. 2021
;
Furukawa et al. 2020
;
Korösi et al. 2018
;
Lallé and Conati 2020
;
Brinton et al. 2016
)
.
Other, but less frequent tasks were discipline classification
(
Stoica et al. 2019
;
Dessì et al. 2017
;
Basu et al. 2016a
;
Othman et al. 2017
;
Chatbri et al. 2017
)
, instructor behavior prediction
(
Chen et al. 2020
;
Godavarthi et al. 2020
;
Sharma et al. 2016b
;
Xu et al. 2020
)
, and dropout prediction
(
Jeon and Park 2020
;
Furini et al. 2021
)
.

Navigation tools popularly proposed table content navigation styles
(
Das and Das 2020
;
Kravvaris and Kermanidis 2019
;
Mahapatra et al. 2018a
;
Mukherjee et al. 2019
;
Husain and Meena 2019
;
Gandhi et al. 2016
;
Kumar et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2017
;
Yadav et al. 2016
;
Bian and Huang 2019
)
but some interesting approaches have emerged such as navigation according to keywords
(
Ohnishi et al. 2019
;
Koka et al. 2020
;
Kravvaris and Kermanidis 2019
;
Kanadje et al. 2016
;
Kumar et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2017
)
or visual elements
(
Zhao et al. 2018b
;
Ma et al. 2017
;
Kumar et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2017
;
Yadav et al. 2016
)
.
These tools have relied heavily in technologies to segment the video and to extract subtopics, concepts, and keywords.

Analytic tools relied usually
on characteristics of the learner’s activity
to understand video usage
(
Walk et al. 2020
;
Wachtler et al. 2016b
;
Long et al. 2018
)
and contrasted it with the learners performance
(
Min et al. 2019
;
He et al. 2018a
;
He et al. 2018b
)
.
Some interesting tools analyzed the comments of videos to understand the learners’ sentiment
(
Sung et al. 2016
)
and their engagement
(
Stepanek and Dorn 2017
)
.

Summarization methods used commonly text transcripts
(
Vimalaksha et al. 2019
;
Balasubramanian et al. 2016
;
Kim and Kim 2016
)
, however, there were also approaches using (visual) frame-based video content
(
Rahman et al. 2020
)
.
Relevant methods were related to the extraction of keywords, relevant subtitles, entities, and metadata.

Design guidelines:
Some of these studies proposed general guidelines
(
Wijnker et al. 2019
;
Voronkin 2019
;
Lange and Costley 2020
;
Dodson et al. 2018b
)
, other suggested guidelines for specific disciplines
(
Minnes et al. 2019
;
Hodges 2018
;

;

;
Ge and Li 2020
)
, and two cases suggested guidelines for creating interactive videos

Weinert et al. 2020

.
They have leveraged on theories and frameworks, for example, film theory
(
Bordwell et al. 1993
)
, limited capacity theory in multimedia learning
(
Mayer 2014
)
, interaction theory
(
Moore 1989
)
, active viewing framework
(
Dodson et al. 2018b
)
, etc.
These guidelines aimed at the improvement of a wide variety of aspects such as pace, intelligibility, quality, ergonomics, interaction, visuals, etc.

Experiments, data analysis, and their results on the effectiveness of video characteristics:
One of the major characteristics explored in controlled experiments were interactive features.
Other characteristics that were experimented are related to production style, instructor behavior, and cues.
Learner’s activity characteristics have been specially inspected in data analysis studies.

Interactive features focused mainly on quizzes, virtual reality and 360-degree characteristics, and in-video annotations.
Quizzes showed to be effective
(
Kleftodimos and Evangelidis 2018
;
Leisner et al. 2020
;
Wachtler et al. 2016a
;
Palaigeorgiou and Papadopoulou 2019
;
Ding 2018
;
da Graça Campos Pimentel et al. 2019
;
Kovacs 2016
)
,
but not in all cases – sometimes they were only effective when they were used together with other interactive features and placed at the right moment
(
Wachtler et al. 2016a
;
Palaigeorgiou and Papadopoulou 2019
;
Ding 2018
)
.
Virtual reality and 360-degree characteristics seemed to be effective; however, the impact was not significant
(
Chen 2020
;
Araiza-Alba et al. 2021
;
Daher and Sleem 2021
;
Huang et al. 2020
)
.
In-video annotations seemed to improve the learning outcome
(
Leisner et al. 2020
;
Taskin et al. 2019
;
Kuhail and Aqel 2020
)
and also navigational elements, specifically, table content navigation and timelines with clickable points, impacted positively the learning outcome
(
Kuhail and Aqel 2020
;
da Graça Campos Pimentel et al. 2019
;
Cojean and Jamet 2017
)
; however, results were not always statistically significant
(
da Graça Campos Pimentel et al. 2019
)
or they were more beneficial when they were together with other features
(
Cojean and Jamet 2017
)
.
Indeed, further claims state that videos with interactive features need to consider other pedagogical techniques to improve the learning outcome
(
Granjo and Rasteiro 2018
)
.

Experiments on production styles investigated mainly the videos with instructor’s presence, videos with animations, video tutorials vs. video lectures, and dialogue-style vs. monologue-style.
The learners preferred the presence of an instructor, although the benefits were not always significant
(
Pérez-Navarro et al. 2021
;
Davila et al. 2021
;
Hew and Lo 2020
;
Pérez-Navarro et al. 2021
;
Davila et al. 2021
;
Hew and Lo 2020
)
.
Animations caused higher learning outcomes when compared to text media and traditional class
(
Srivastava et al. 2020
;
Cookson et al. 2020
;
Al-Khateeb and Alduwairi 2020
)
.
Tutorials and lecture styles correlated positively with the learning outcome
(
Hildebrand and Ahn 2018
)
, however, preferences for one style over the other were different between studies
(
Ding 2018
;
Ou et al. 2016
;
Lei et al. 2019
;
Hildebrand and Ahn 2018
;
Thornton et al. 2017
)
.
Dialogue-style instead of the typical monologue-style showed no significant effects on learning outcome
(
Ding et al. 2018
;
Nugraha et al. 2020
;
Lee and Muldner 2020
)
.
In any case, the use of divers styles appeared to be effective
(
Costley and Lange 2017
)
.

Studies on instructor behavior examined particularly human gestures and speech-related characteristics, but very little other visual elements such as pose and gender.
Speech characteristics seemed to impact certain aspects of the learning effectiveness such as understanding, attention, etc
(
Atapattu and Falkner 2017
;
Shoufan 2019a
;
van der Sluis et al. 2016
)
.
Gestures proved to benefit only certain aspects such as satisfaction and presence, but very little the learning outcome
(
Beege et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2019a
;
Horovitz and Mayer 2021
)
.
Instructor-related cues caused a positive effect on the learning outcome
(
Wang et al. 2020b
;
Pi et al. 2019
)
.
However, this did not apply to
animated instructors (human-like artificial agents), in those it even caused negative effects
(
Sharma et al. 2016c
;
Moon and Ryu 2021
)
.
Content-related cues have also positively affected the learning outcome
(
Wang et al. 2020b
;
Pi et al. 2017b
;
Sharma et al. 2016c
)
; however, they can also cause cognitive overload
(
Moon and Ryu 2021
)
.

Regarding the choice of video length, several studies found that short videos were preferred by the learners
(
Saurabh and Gautam 2019
;
Ding 2018
;
Rahim and Shamsudin 2019
;
Engeness and Nohr 2019
)
.
However, this was not true for every type of learning videos and context, in some cases longer videos were still preferred
(
Giannakos et al. 2016
;
Shoufan 2019b
)
.
Regarding video completion, experiments showed that the learning outcome improved when learners watched the video more than a certain proportion
(
Hildebrand and Ahn 2018
;
Angrave et al. 2020
)
.
However, video completion was affected by common behavior such as skipping and watching only parts of the video
(
Hildebrand and Ahn 2018
;
Yan and Baxter 2018
;
Giannakos et al. 2016
)
.
Studies on the impact of video pace showed no significant positive effects of automatic pauses, and even negative effects on the learning outcome were found
(
Schroeder et al. 2020
;
Garrett 2018
;
Garrett 2021
;
Merkt et al. 2018
)
.
Interestingly, watching videos at faster speed was beneficial in some cases and counterproductive in others
(
Lang et al. 2020
;
Garrett 2021
)
.

Studies on experts and high performance learners revealed that these type of viewers engaged more actively with the video
(
Rodríguez et al. 2018
;
Kovacs 2016
;
Rodríguez et al. 2018
;
Li 2019
)
and achieved a higher learning outcome
(
Stöhr et al. 2019
)
.
However, in some cases they achieved the same learning outcome and engagement as their counterparts
(
Galster et al. 2018
;
Stöhr et al. 2019
;
Li 2019
)
.
Also, behavior might change according to the discipline, topics, and content of the video
(
Saurabh and Gautam 2019
;
Uchidiuno et al. 2018
;
Hildebrand and Ahn 2018
)
.
Also, YouTube videos might be preferred over institutional videos
(
Giannakos et al. 2016
)
.

Regarding video popularity, studies identified that learners tended to like videos perceived as helpful for learning
(
Saurabh and Gautam 2019
)
, however, a high popularity might not necessarily entail a good video quality
(
Lee et al. 2017a
)
.

Further trends and directions:
Our analyses have confirmed several findings from previous reviews.
We observed an increase in the number of VBL research articles
(
Giannakos 2013
;
Poquet et al. 2018
)
,
and a significant emphasis on the use of videos in STEM disciplines
(
Giannakos 2013
;
Poquet et al. 2018
)
.
We also identified a tendency to use primarily the learning outcome as an indicator to measure learning effectiveness, traditionally in terms of recall tests and, more recently, through transfer tests
(
Poquet et al. 2018
;
Yousef et al. 2014
)
.
Moreover, similarly to Poquet et al.
(
Poquet et al. 2018
)
, we found that the next most popular indicators were cognitive load, satisfaction, mental effort, social presence, motivation, and self-efficacy.
We could also confirm that eye-tracking
(
Nugraha et al. 2020
;
Lee and Muldner 2020
;
Stull et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2019b
;
Kikusawa et al. 2016
;
Pi et al. 2019
;
Zhang 2021
;
Sharma et al. 2016c
;
Moon and Ryu 2021
;
Pi et al. 2017b
;
Srivastava et al. 2019
)
and log data
(
da Graça Campos Pimentel et al. 2019
;
Lang et al. 2020
)
characteristics were used; however, we also found studies that used thermal
(
Srivastava et al. 2019
;
Srivastava et al. 2020
)
, neurophysiological
(
Winkler et al. 2020
)
, and facial expressions-tracking
(
Srivastava et al. 2019
)
characteristics.
Regarding production styles, we could recognize slide-based productions as one of the most used production styles in controlled experiments.
In addition, we found that the experiments frequently used videos with a visible instructor.
However, contrary to Poquet et al.
(
Poquet et al. 2018
)
, we did not find that animations were popularly used.
Among the styles that were less used, there were handwriting, screencast, and virtual reality styles.
We conclude that VBL research in controlled experiments have not tested the diverse range of learning video styles, disciplines, and video sources.
Thus, the field requires more research efforts in the areas not targeted yet.

Another tendency in the reviewed articles was that audio, visual, textual and further features are usually treated in a separated manner.
However, learning through videos requires the fusion and interpretation of synchronous audio and visual sensory input.
Thus, we believe that VBL could benefit from
multimodal representations and analysis
in order to better capture the relationships between the different modalities.

Research on video, text, and image analysis has been dominated by deep learning approaches in recent years.
This is also the case for VBL,
especially for the development of tools that support the learning process.
We noticed that deep learning was not only used for basic tasks such as video segmentation, optical character recognition, neural word representations (embeddings), speech-to-text tasks, etc. but also in entire pipelines of recommendation systems, learning outcome predictors, navigational tools, etc.

Contradictory results were found about the impact of video characteristics on the learning effectiveness.
We could observe that the discrepancies originate not only from the small or big differences in the test conditions but also from the production style of the video.
In some cases it was not possible to know in detail these differences, since not all the studies reported accurately the characteristics of the investigated videos.

Future work:

Experimental research should consider more the diversity in video styles and also the diverse disciplines apart from STEM subjects.
An important gap we see is that research needs to move towards multimodal representations were video characteristics can be unified to approach to the way human beings learn.
There are very few articles that propose design guidelines.
We believe that more research in this matter, especially guidelines customized to specific disciplines, can be highly useful for practical scenarios such as content creation.
Although articles that propose tools for supporting learning encompass most of VBL research in our sample, we think that more efforts are needed to bring new technologies to the field for understanding whether they can improve the way people learn through videos.
Finally, finding what characteristics a video should have to be effective for learning remains ongoing work.
Obviously, more research is needed to identify what makes us learn in a video and in what learning scenarios.

Limitations:
In this review, we searched and retrieved articles exclusively from academic computer science data bases.
Therefore, we did not consider other disciplines that could contribute to VBL advances, e.g., instructional insights from science education.
We have researched in three dedicated databases, where one of them (DBLP) can be considered to track the vast majority of relevant CS venues, including journals and conference proceedings
(
dbl 2022
)
.
We retrieved articles with the query terms only in the title to make sure that the main focus was on VBL – however, there might be articles treating VBL-related topics without it being reflected in the title.
Moreover, we restricted our review to articles that used and experimented with video characteristics, thus, advances in other use cases such as teacher reflection were not reported.
Finally, it is important to recognize that an accurate comparison of the effects of video characteristics on the learning effectiveness is difficult due to the heterogeneity in the experimental settings and test conditions.
This is the reason why we could not provide a meta-analysis about these effects.
