Planar Prop of Differential Operators
Slava Pimenov

Introduction

There are many different approaches to generalize the notion of differential calculus to the noncommutative setting, extending various
aspects of the commutative theory (such as
[
Co
]
,
[
CQ2
]
,
[
LR
]
,
[
Sa
]
,
[
TT
]
to name a few). They
produce distinct albeit interconnected and equally meaningful theories. In this paper we propose another approach to extend the
notion of differential operators to the setting of associative algebras.

We believe that a “correct” notion of the differential operators should satisfy the following principles and we
will be using them as guidelines for our definition:

a)

the construction of differential operators should respect quasi-isomorphisms between dg-algebras,

b)

there should be a natural symbol map from differential operators to polyvector fields,

c)

the role of the associative algebra structure should be made explicit in the construction.

Since the notion of differential operators is not a functor, the first principle is better applied to the corresponding notion
of
D
-modules. Basically it says that the definition should be readily extendible to dg-algebras and it should be a Quillen
functor, so that its derived functor can be computed using cofibrant resolutions of associative dg-algebras. In a sense,
this can be thought of as a dg-version of Kashiwara equivalence theorem.

Principle (b) essentially means that whatever we define has a right to be called differential operators. For our construction
in this paper we convert it to a precise statement (proposition
2.4.6
).

The reason for the third principle is the fact that differential operators are closely related to the deformation theory.
Instead of associative algebras one could consider the category of
\mathcal{P}
-algebras with the algebraic structure given for instance
by some operad
\mathcal{P}
and ask what is the corresponding notion of
\mathcal{P}
-differential operators and its connection to the
deformation theory of
\mathcal{P}
-algebras. Of course one expects that
when applied to the operad
\mathcal{C}om
of commutative algebras one recovers the classical notion of differential operators.
In this paper we will not be using this principle explicitly in the definition, however in section
2.3

we construct another planar prop
\mathcal{E}(A)
from automoprhisms of the trivial formal associative deformation of
A

with a natural map to the planar prop of multi-differential operators
\mathcal{D}(A)
.

0.0.1
.

As the basis for our definition we use the following identities satisfied by the differential operators of a commutative
algebra
A
. For an operator
P
of order
1

we have

P(ab)=P(a)b+aP(b)-aP(1)b,

for an order
2
operator we have

P(abc)=P(ab)c+aP(bc)+bP(ac)-P(a)bc-aP(b)c-abP(c)+abcP(1),

and so forth. While the first relation can be applied without change to an associative algebra, the relations for higher order
operators can not. In order to address this issue our operator
P\colon A\to A
comes with “companions”
P_{k}\colon A^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits k}\to A^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits k}
,
which are also poly-differential maps. Each companion measures the defect of its predecessor to be a poly-derivation.

The spaces
D(A)
(proposition
2.2.1
) and
\mathcal{D}(A)
(theorem
2.2.2
) that we define are analogs of the Rees algebra

\mathrm{Rees}_{F}(D)
associated to the standard filtration on differential operators in commutative setting.

Outline of the paper..

In the first section we define an algebraic structure that we call the planar prop. It is part of the menagerie of similar
structures, such as operads, properads, half-props, props, etc. In section
1.1
we give the precise definition
using language of graph-based Feynman categories to set up the notation and clarify the details. Then in section
1.2

we convert this definition of a planar prop into a more elementary one, as a collection of spaces equipped with two operations
satisfying certain explicit relations. This alternative definition will be used to show that the space of multi-differential
operators has a structure of a planar prop.

We begin section
2.1
by introducing some notations and constructions concerning the category
\Delta
of totally
ordered finite sets that will be carrying the combinatorics of the definition of differential operators. Then we proceed with
giving the main definition of operators at
f
for a map of associative algebras
f\colon A\to B
, which can be thought as
operators on
A
with values in
B
. Finally, we extend this to operators with multiple inputs and outputs and call the resulting
space the multi-differential operators
\mathcal{D}(A)
.

Section
2.2
is dedicated to showing that the constructed space
\mathcal{D}(A)
equipped with bigrading by the number of
inputs and outputs forms a planar prop. We also observe that if we assign a genus to an operator, which is basically the
difference between its order and the number of outputs, we in fact obtain the modular planar prop structure.

In section
2.3
we consider trivial associative families of algebras with fiber
A
over completions
of free associative algebras. We use automorphisms of such families as a source of differential operators. In fact we
construct another planar prop
\mathcal{E}(A)
with a map to
\mathcal{D}(A)
. The reason for this construction is to follow through with
the third guiding principle. This is also the reason to consider operators with multiple inputs and outputs.

Finally, the section
2.4
is dedicated to the case of formally smooth associative algebras. We show that in this
case there is a surjective symbol map from
\mathcal{D}(A)
to poly-derivations and we identify its kernel as the image of simplicial degeneracy
maps. We also show that for formally smooth algebras the image of the natural map
\mathcal{E}(A)\to\mathcal{D}(A)
is formed by operators
of totally positive genus.

1
Planar Prop

We define the notion of a planar prop analogously to that of a prop (see for example
[
Va
]
) except we do not keep
track of the action of symmetric groups on the inputs and outputs of operations, and we only allow compositions along
planar graphs. First, we give the definition using formalism of Feynman categories (
[
KW
]
).

1.1
Graph-based definition

1.1.1
.

A
directed
graph
G
consists of finite sets of vertices
V
and half-edges
E
, equipped with

•

a map
\alpha\colon E\to\{\mathrm{In},\mathrm{Out}\}
, decorating half-edges as either inputs or outputs,

•

the incidence map
i\colon E\to V
describing how the half-edges are attached to vertices,

•

the involution
\varepsilon\colon E\to E
describing how the half-edges are glued together to form edges. This involution
is compatible with the decoration
\alpha
, in the sense that outputs are glued with inputs.

For each vertex
v\in V
we denote
\mathrm{In}(v)
and
\mathrm{Out}(v)
the sets of incident half-edges marked as inputs and outputs
respectively. A half-edge fixed by involution
\varepsilon
and marked as input (output) is called an input (respectively output) of
G
.
The rest of the
\mathbb{Z}/2
-orbits with the free action of the involution are called internal edges. In other words an internal edge
is an ordered pair
(e_{1},e_{2})
such that
e_{1}\in\mathrm{Out}(v_{1})
and
e_{2}\in\mathrm{In}(v_{2})
for some vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.
We say that this edge goes from
v_{1}
to
v_{2}
.

A directed cycle in
G
is a sequence of internal edges
(e_{k},f_{k})
,
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, such that
i(f_{k})=i(e_{k+1})

for
1\leqslant k\leqslant(n-1)
and
i(f_{n})=i(e_{1})
.

G
is called
acyclic
if it has no directed cycles.

We say that
G
is a
ribbon
graph if the sets
i^{-1}(v)
are cyclically ordered for every
v\in V
.
Furthermore, we say that
G
is
planar
if

•

the cyclic order on
i^{-1}(v)
is such that all inputs (and therefore outputs) are gathered together.
In other words, if
v
has both inputs and outputs there are
i\in\mathrm{In}(v)
and
o\in\mathrm{Out}(v)
, such that

(1.1)

\forall x\in\mathrm{In}(v):i\preceq x\prec o,\quad\text{and}\quad\forall y\in\mathrm{Out}(v):o\preceq y\prec i.

•

the geometric realization of
G
can be embedded into a unit disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
respecting the cyclic order at each vertex,
with the free ends of inputs and outputs of
G
lying on the boundary of the disk, in a way that the induced cyclic order
satisfies
1.1

We say that graph
G
is
essential
if every vertex has at least one input and at least one output.

All graphs are assumed to be directed acyclic planar graphs unless stated otherwise. We denote the set of such graphs by
\mathcal{G}
.

Remark 1.1.2
.

Our notion of a planar graph differs from the usual notion by imposing condition
1.1
. If
G
is essential, it is equivalent to
providing linear orders on the (non-empty) sets
\mathrm{In}(v)
and
\mathrm{Out}(v)
. Alternatively, one could think of such a graph as having a
global direction, in the sense that there exists an embedding as above, with additional property that all edges go in
a prescribed direction, say downward (see lemma
1.2.4
).

1.1.3
Genus markings..

Let
G
be a graph as above, and consider a function
\gamma\colon V\to\mathbb{Z}
that we will refer to as genus marking of
vertices. We allow negative integers here, since our graphs may not be connected. We define the genus of
G
as

\gamma(G)=\mathrm{dim}\ H_{1}(G)-\mathrm{dim}\ H_{0}(G)+1+\sum_{v\in V}\gamma(v).

One can check that this definition is compatible with the composition of graphs. Fix vertex
v\in V
and take a genus marked graph
H
, such
that number of inputs and outputs of
H
are the same as inputs and outputs of
v
, and
\gamma(H)=\gamma(v)
.
The composition
H\circ_{v}G
is the graph obtained from
G
by replacing vertex
v
with
H
and connecting
inputs and outputs of
v
to inputs and outputs of
H
preserving the cyclic ordering. Then
\gamma(H\circ_{v}G)=\gamma(G)
.

We will write
\mathcal{G}_{m}
for the set of genus marked graphs.

1.1.4
Feynman category..

A Feynman category is a triple
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{F},i\colon\mathcal{V}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{F})
, where
\mathcal{V}
is a groupoid,

\mathcal{F}
is a symmetric monoidal category and
i
is an inclusion functor, that satisfy the following conditions.

a)

Isomorphism condition: the induced functor
i^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}\colon\mathcal{V}^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}\to\mathrm{Iso}(\mathcal{F})
is an equivalence.
Here
\mathcal{V}^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}
denotes the symmetric monoidal category generated by
\mathcal{V}
.

b)

Hereditary condition: the induced functor
(\mathrm{Iso}(\mathcal{F}\downarrow\mathcal{V}))^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits}\to\mathrm{Iso}(\mathcal{F}\downarrow\mathcal{F})

is an equivalence.

c)

Size condition: for any
v\in\mathcal{V}
,
(\mathcal{F}\downarrow v)
is a small category.

As is customary with graph-based Feynman categories we put
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{C}or
, the groupoid of corollas,
i.e. graphs with a single vertex. Isomorphism class of an object
v\in\mathcal{C}or
is determined by a pairs of non-negative
integers
(m,n)
, where
m=|\mathrm{Out}(v)|
and
n=|\mathrm{In}(v)|
. We set
\mathrm{Aut}(v_{m,n})=1
if
mn\neq 0
and

\mathrm{Aut}(v_{n,0})=\mathrm{Aut}(v_{0,n})=\mathbb{Z}/n
.

Objects of
\mathcal{F}
are collections
of corollas (i.e graphs without internal edges), the monoidal structure is given by disjoint union. In virtue of the
hereditary condition above, a map in
\mathcal{F}
to a collection of corollas is completely determined by a map to each
of the corolla in the collection. We set
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{F}}(X,v)
to be the set of graphs
G
, such that number of inputs
and outputs of
G
are the same as for corolla
v
, and vertices of
G
together with their inputs and outputs are in
bijection with the corollas in
X
. The composition of morphisms is given by the composition of graphs.

Similarly, we form the genus marked Feynman category
\mathcal{F}_{m}
by taking
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{C}or\times\mathbb{Z}
the
groupoid of corollas marked by an integer, and
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{F}_{m}}(X,v)
to be the set of suitable genus marked graphs.

Definition 1.1.5
.

A planar prop is a symmetric monoidal functor
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathcal{F}\to\mathrm{Vect}
. A modular planar prop is a symmetric monoidal
functor
\mathcal{Q}\colon\mathcal{F}_{m}\to\mathrm{Vect}
.

The term
modular
is used to indicate that this notion is basically a planar analog of the modular operad of
Getzler-Kapranov (
[
GK
]
).

Since a corolla
v
is completely determined by a pair of non-negative integers
(m,n)
, where
m=|\mathrm{Out}(v)|
,
n=|\mathrm{In}(v)|
, the
restriction of a planar prop
\mathcal{P}
to the groupoid of corollas gives a collection of vector spaces
\mathcal{P}(m,n)
. Informally, the
planar prop structure prescribes how to compose elements of
\mathcal{P}(m,n)
along planar graphs.

1.1.6
.

We denote the category of planar props by
\mathrm{PProp}
and the category of modular planar props by
\mathrm{MPP}
. Consider the monoidal
structure
\mathop{\otimes}\limits
on planar props given by the coproduct in
\mathrm{PProp}
. The unit for this monoidal structure is given by the planar
prop
\mathcal{I}
, with

\mathcal{I}(m,n)=\begin{cases}k,\quad\text{if $m=n$},\\
0,\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We say that a planar prop
\mathcal{P}
is
unital
if it admits a unit map
u\colon\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{P}
, i.e. the composition

\textstyle{\mathcal{P}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\simeq}
\textstyle{\mathcal{I}\mathop{\otimes}\limits\mathcal{P}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{u\mathop{\otimes}\limits\mathrm{id}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{P}\mathop{\otimes}\limits\mathcal{P}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathcal{P}}

is identity.

1.1.7
.

From the definition of the unital planar prop
\mathcal{P}
we immediately see that

a)

\mathcal{P}(0,0)
is a commutative algebra,

b)

\mathcal{P}(m,n)
are
\mathcal{P}(0,0)
-modules for all
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
,

c)

\mathcal{P}(1,1)
is an associative
\mathcal{P}(0,0)
-algebra, and
\mathcal{P}(0,0)
is contained in its center,

d)

\mathcal{P}(n,0)
and
\mathcal{P}(0,n)
are equipped with the action of the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}/n
, and an invariant pairing

\mathcal{P}(0,n)\mathop{\otimes}\limits\mathcal{P}(n,0)\to\mathcal{P}(0,0)
.

A unital planar prop
\mathcal{P}
is called
reduced
if
\mathcal{P}(n,0)=\mathcal{P}(0,n)=0
for
n\geqslant 1
and
\mathcal{P}(0,0)=k
.
In the rest of the paper we will be mostly concerned with reduced planar props.

1.2
Elementary definition

Let us give an alternative definition of a planar prop as a collection of vector spaces and operations.

Definition 1.2.1
.

An elementary prop
\mathcal{P}
is a collection of vector spaces
\mathcal{P}(m,n)
,
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1}
, and
\mathcal{P}(0,0)=k
,
with a vertical unit element
u\in\mathcal{P}(1,1)
, horizontal unit
u^{0}\in\mathcal{P}(0,0)
, and two operations: horizontal composition

\circ_{h}\colon\mathcal{P}(m_{1},n_{1})\mathop{\otimes}\limits\mathcal{P}(m_{2},n_{2})\to\mathcal{P}(m_{1}+m_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}),

and vertical composition

\circ_{v}\colon\mathcal{P}(m,n)\mathop{\otimes}\limits\mathcal{P}(n,k)\to\mathcal{P}(m,k).

Operations
\circ_{h}
and
\circ_{v}
are associative and satisfy the following relations. Let
a\in\mathcal{P}(m,n)

and
b\in\mathcal{P}(p,q)
, and denote by
u^{k}\in\mathcal{P}(k,k)
the horizontal composition of
k
copies of
u
, for
k\geqslant 1
.

a)

Horizontal unit:

u^{0}\circ_{h}a=a=a\circ_{h}u^{0}.

b)

Compatibility:

(a\circ_{h}u^{p})\circ_{v}(u^{n}\circ_{h}b)=a\circ_{h}b=(u^{m}\circ_{h}b)\circ_{v}(a\circ_{h}u^{q}).

A morphism between two elementary props is a map between corresponding collections preserving units and the two
compositions.

Definition 1.2.2
.

The expression of the type

(u^{i_{1}}\circ_{h}a_{1}\circ_{h}u^{j_{1}})\circ_{v}\cdots\circ_{v}(u^{i_{r}}\circ_{h}a_{r}\circ_{h}u^{j_{r}})

with
a_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(m_{i},n_{i})

is said to be in the normal form if for any
1\leqslant k\leqslant r
and
1\leqslant l\leqslant(k-1)

i_{k}<\min(i_{l},\ldots i_{k-1})\quad\text{implies}\quad i_{k}+m_{k}>\min(i_{l},\ldots i_{k-1}).

Proposition 1.2.3
.

The category of elementary props is equivalent to the category of reduced unital planar props.

Before proving the proposition let us show that our planar graphs can be embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in a certain way.

Lemma 1.2.4
.

Every essential graph
G
, admits a level embedding into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e. an embedding such that

•

all vertices of
G
lie in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

•

every line
\mathbb{R}\times\{n\}
contains at most one vertex,

•

every edge goes downwards.

•

there is
N\in\mathbb{Z}
, such that all vertices lie in
\mathbb{R}\times(-N,N)
, free ends of inputs of
G
lie on

\mathbb{R}\times\{N\}
and free ends of outputs lie on
\mathbb{R}\times\{-N\}
.

Moreover, there is a canonical linear ordering on the set of vertices of
G
, and this embedding can be constructed in
such a way that the ordering of vertices by their levels coincides with this canonical order.

Proof:
We prove the statement by induction on the maximal number of vertices in a connected component of
G
. Clearly, if each
connected component of
G
admits the required embedding then so does the entire
G
.
If
G
has only one vertex there is nothing to show.
Otherwise, since
G
has no directed cycles, there exists a vertex
v
such that all outputs of
v
are outputs of
G
.
Take the first such vertex with respect to the linear order on
\mathrm{Out}(G)
, and construct a new graph
G^{\prime}
first by removing
vertex
v
from
G
along with all half-edges in
\mathrm{Out}(v)
as well as half-edges in
\mathrm{In}(v)
which are part of an internal edge
of
G
. Then for all half-edges
e\in\mathrm{In}(v)\cap\mathrm{In}(G)
we add a new vertex
v_{e}
and a half-edge
e^{\prime}
, so that

\mathrm{In}(v_{e})=\{e\}
and
\mathrm{Out}(v_{e})=\{e^{\prime}\}
.

By construction,
G^{\prime}
is again an essential graph and since each new vertex
v_{e}
belongs to its own connected component the maximal
number of vertices of a connected component of
G^{\prime}
is strictly less than that of
G
. Therefore, it admits a level embedding into
\mathbb{R}\times[-N^{\prime},N^{\prime}]
. Let us extend
this embedding to
G
: we place
v
on
\mathbb{R}\times\{-N^{\prime}\}
, connect its inputs to appropriate outputs of
G^{\prime}
by line segments
going down from
\mathbb{R}\times\{-N^{\prime}+1\}
to
v\in\mathbb{R}\times\{-N^{\prime}\}
(this can be done because of the cyclic order condition

1.1
), then embed outputs of
v
as line segments going
from
v
to
\mathbb{R}\times\{-N^{\prime}-1\}
, and finally extend the remaining outputs of
G^{\prime}
down to
\mathbb{R}\times\{-N^{\prime}-1\}
.
This gives us a level embedding of
G
into
\mathbb{R}\times[N^{\prime}+1,-N^{\prime}-1]
.

\square

by the graph

\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{a}
\textstyle{b}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}

and the vertical composition
a\circ_{v}b

\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}

\textstyle{a}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}

The relations for

Now, let
\mathcal{P}
be an elementary prop, we want to show that it has a structure of a reduced unital planar prop.
Since the prop is reduced it is enough to define operations corresponding to essential graphs. According to lemma

the composition along such a graph can be written in the normal form uniquely determined by the graph. Let
G
be an essential

(v_{1},\ldots v_{r})
be the set of its vertices ordered as in the lemma. Denote
m_{i}=|\mathrm{Out}(v_{i})|
and

\mathcal{P}(G)(a_{1},\ldots a_{r})=(u^{i_{1}}\circ_{h}a_{1}\circ_{h}u^{j_{1}})\circ_{v}\cdots\circ_{v}(u^{i_{r}}\circ_{h}a_{r}\circ_{h}u^{j_{r}})

is in the normal form.

It remains to show that this assignment is well defined, in other words that any expression involving
\circ_{h}
and
\circ_{v}

can be reduced to the normal form using relations in

Finally, observe that the horizontal and vertical units determine a map

, and combining relations (a)

Example 1.2.6
(Braid prop.).

As an illustration of the notion we give the following example. Denote
\mathrm{Br}_{n}
the braid group on
n

\mathcal{B}r(m,n)=\begin{cases}k[\mathrm{Br}_{n}],\quad\text{if $m=n$}\\

This is a unital planar prop generated by a single element
s\in\mathcal{B}r(2,2)

(s\circ_{h}u)\circ_{v}(u\circ_{h}s)\circ_{v}(s\circ_{h}u)=(u\circ_{h}s)\circ_{v}(s\circ_{h}u)\circ_{v}(u\circ_{h}s).

Differential operators

2.1
Definition and basic properties

Definition 2.1.1
.

An ordered partition of
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
of size
d
is a sequence
\lambda_{\bullet}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots\lambda_{d})
,
such that each
\lambda_{i}\geqslant 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}=n
.

A partition
\lambda_{\bullet}
is said to be degenerate if either
n>0
and at least one of
\lambda_{i}=0
, or
n=0
and
d>1
.

One can think of such a partition as a decomposition of the integral interval
I=[1,n]\cap\mathbb{Z}
into a disjoint union of
intervals (possibly empty)
I=\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}I_{i}
, where
|I_{i}|=\lambda_{i}
. We will write
|\lambda_{\bullet}|

for the size of the partition.
Denote by
\Lambda_{d}(n)
the set of all ordered partitions of
n
of size
d
, and
\Lambda_{d}^{\circ}(n)
the subset of non-degenerate partitions.

Let
\lambda_{\bullet}
be a partition of
n
of size
k
and
\mu_{\bullet}
a partition of some
\lambda_{i}
of size
l
.
Then we define the composition

(\mu\circ_{i}\lambda)_{\bullet}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots\lambda_{i-1},\mu_{1},\ldots\mu_{l},\lambda_{i+1},\ldots\lambda_{k}).

We say that a partition
\lambda^{\prime}
is a
refinement
of
\lambda
if
\lambda^{\prime}
can be obtained from
\lambda
by a
sequence of compositions

\lambda^{\prime}=\mu^{(1)}\circ_{i_{1}}(\mu^{(2)}\circ_{i_{2}}\cdots(\mu^{(p)}\circ_{i_{p}}\lambda)\cdots).

In other words, every interval in partition
\lambda^{\prime}
is contained in some interval in
\lambda
.
A refinement is said to be

2.4
Filtration and the symbol map

Denote by
\Delta^{>0}_{\mathrm{Epi}}
the subcategory of
\Delta
formed by objects
\{[n]\}
with
n>0
and surjective
maps between them. For any category
\mathcal{C}
an
epi-simplicial
object in
\mathcal{C}
is a functor
(\Delta^{>0}_{\mathrm{Epi}})^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathcal{C}
,
in other words it is the part of a simplicial object formed by all degeneracy maps.

Consider spaces
D_{n}(f)
for some morphism of associative algebras
f\colon A\to B
. To any surjective map
\sigma\colon[m]\twoheadrightarrow[n]
,
we associate a degeneracy map
s=s(\sigma)\colon D_{n}(f)\to D_{m}(f)
, by putting for any
P\in D_{n}(f)

s(P)_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}P_{\lambda},&\text{if $\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda\sigma$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is straightforward to check that this collection is a well defined element
s(P)\in D_{m}(f)
. Applying this to
B=T^{\bullet}_{A}(A^{e})

we obtain

Proposition 2.4.1
.

The collection
\{\mathcal{D}_{n-1}(A)\mid n\geqslant 1\}
and degeneracy maps
s
form an epi-simplicial vector space.

\square

Using degeneracy maps we construct the order filtration on
\mathcal{D}_{m}(A)
by putting

F_{n}(\mathcal{D}_{m}(A))=\sum_{\sigma\colon[m]\twoheadrightarrow[n]}\mathrm{Im}\ \left(s(\sigma)\colon\mathcal{D}_{n}(A)\to\mathcal{D}_{m}(A)\right).

2.4.2
.

Recall that an associative algebra
A
is called
formally smooth
if the space of non-commutative forms
\Omega^{1}_{A}
,
defined as the kernel

\textstyle{\Omega^{1}_{A}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A\mathop{\otimes}\limits A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{m}

is a finitely generated projective

-module. Recall also that for a formally smooth algebra

the Hochschild cohomology

Lemma 2.4.3

Consider the following diagram

\textstyle{\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Hom}(A^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits 2},B)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Hom}(A^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits 3},B)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d}
\textstyle{\cdots}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Hom}(A^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits 2},B^{\mathop{\otimes}\limits 2})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

-bimodule structure on

Lemma 2.4.4
.

Let
A
be any associative algebra and
P\in D_{n}(f)
, then the sum

Lemma 2.4.5
.

Let
A
be a formally smooth algebra and

.

Proof:
Take
\mu\in\Lambda^{\circ}(m+n)

\mu

is well defined

Now, if
\mu

\mu_{j}
be the interval spanning the gap between
(n)
and
(m)

Theorem 2.4.7
