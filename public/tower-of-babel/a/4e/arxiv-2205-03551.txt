Abstract
. We study the contact process on random graphs with low infection rate λ \lambda . For random d d -regular graphs, it is known that the survival time is O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) below the critical λ c \lambda_{c} . By contrast, on the Erdős-Rényi random graphs 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , d / n ) \mathcal{G}(n,d/n) , rare high-degree vertices result in much longer survival times. We show that the survival time is governed by high-density local configurations. In particular, we show that there is a long string of high-degree vertices on which the infection lasts for time n λ 2 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{\lambda^{2+o(1)}} . To establish a matching upper bound, we introduce a modified version of the contact process which ignores infections that do not lead to further infections and allows for a sharper recursive analysis on branching process trees, the local-weak limit of the graph. Our methods, moreover, generalize to random graphs with given degree distributions that have exponential moments.



1.
Introduction

Stochastic processes on random networks can be affected by both global properties of the graph like expansion but also local properties like the local-weak limit and the presence of rare local neighbourhoods. Our goal in this paper is to investigate what properties of sparse Erdős-Rényi random graphs determine the survival time for infection processes.

The contact process is a model for the spread of infections on a graph
G=(V,E)
. For a given
\lambda>0
, the contact process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with infection rate
\lambda
and recovery rate
1
is a continuous-time Markov chain, with state space
\{0,1\}^{V}
, tracking whether a vertex
v
is either infected (
X_{t}(v)=1
) or healthy (
X_{t}(v)=0
). The process evolves according to the following rules:

•

Each infected vertex infects each of its neighbors independently at Poisson rate
\lambda
and is healed at Poisson rate
1
;

•

Infection and recovery events in the process happen independently.

On finite graphs, the contact process has a single absorbing state which is no infections and we define the time to reach it as the
survival time
. The primary question is how quickly this occurs and how it depends on
\lambda
. We can ask if the behaviour on Erdős-Rényi random graphs is the same as for random
d
-regular graphs. In terms of the threshold of
\lambda
between fast (polynomial) and slow (exponential) survival times, the answer is yes, both are at approximately
\frac{1}{d}
(see
[
28
,
30
]
) which is determined by the global branching rate of the process.
When
\lambda
is small, the contact process on random regular graphs survives for time
O(\log n)
(see
[
15
,
27
]
). One can ask whether the survival time is still logarithmic for other sparse random graphs, in particular the Erdős-Rényi random graph
\mathcal{G}(n,d/n)
?
The answer turns out to be negative for a surprisingly simple reason. Since the largest degree in an Erdős-Rényi random graph is asymptotically
\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
, this directly gives a lower bound of
\exp(\frac{c\log n}{\log\log n})
which far exceeds
\log n
.

It is then natural to ask whether
\exp(\frac{c\log n}{\log\log n})
gives the correct order of the survival time. The convergence to equilibrium of the high-temperature Glauber dynamics of the Ising model gives an interesting comparison. Its mixing time is determined by coupling the all-plus and all-minus initial conditions and the set of disagreements can be thought of as a kind of infection process where updates can remove disagreements or spread them to neighbours. In
[
23
]
, it was shown that the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics for the high-temperature continuous-time ferromagnetic Ising model on
\mathcal{G}(n,d/n)
is
\exp(\frac{\Theta(1)\log n}{\log\log n})
. It is essentially determined by the time to mix around the maximal-degree vertices in the graph. We show, by contrast, that the contact process survives for time polynomial in
n
, much longer than would be predicted by just the high-degree vertices. In the following theorem we, moreover, determine the exponent of the polynomial up to the log factors.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d>1
and
G_{n}\sim\mathcal{G}(n,d/n)
. There exist positive constants
\lambda_{*}(d),C(d)
such that the contact process on
G_{n}
starting at all vertices infected and infection rate
\lambda<\lambda_{*}
has survival time
T_{\lambda,n}
that satisfies

n^{c\lambda^{2}/\log^{2}(\lambda^{-1})}\leq T_{\lambda,n}\leq n^{C\lambda^{2}\log(\lambda^{-1})}\qquad

with probability
1-o(1)
as
n\to\infty
where
c=10^{-9}
.

This result is a special case of our more general results on random graphs with given degree distributions described later in the section. As we will describe, the survival time comes from large connected components of vertices of large constant degree. The discrepancy between the contact process and the Glauber dynamics can be explained by the existence of a phase for the contact process in dimension one but not for the Glauber dynamics. As a result, it is different rare local structures which determine the survival times.

Background

The contact process was first introduced by Harris

11

1.3.
Main ideas

In the first part of the paper, we prove the lower bound in Theorem
1.2
.
It is well-known that the contact process survives for a long time on dense sub-graphs. The simplest such example is a large star graph, a vertex with
k
neighbors. It is easy to verify that the expected survival length behaves like
e^{ck\lambda^{2}}
(see Lemma
3.4
). Since the largest degree in an Erdős-Rényi random graph is asymptotically
\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
, this directly gives a lower bound of
\exp(\frac{c\log n}{\log\log n})
which far exceeds the
\log n
survival time in the random regular graph. However, these highest-degree vertices are not the optimal local structure. Instead we find that for paths of vertices of degree
10^{6}\lambda^{-2}\log\tfrac{1}{\lambda}
, the contact process behaves effectively like a supercritical one-dimensional contact process. This has an infection time that is exponential in the length of the path and we show that with high probability there are paths of logarithmic length which gives the bound in Theorem
1.2
.

The second, and more challenging, part is to prove the matching upper bound of Theorem
1.3
. As in
[
2
,
28
]
, we first control the contact process on Galton-Watson Branching process trees which are the local weak limits of the random graphs. In
[
2
]
, we developed a recursive scheme to show that the expected survival time of an infection started from the root is constant, independent on the depth of the tree. This was further refined in
[
28
]
, giving tail bounds on the conditional expected survival time given the tree as well as for the expected number of infections that reach the leaves. These tail bounds, however, would only translate into an upper bound on the expected survival time on the random graph of
n^{\lambda^{1+o(1)}}
. To explain where this analysis was deficient, it effectively coupled the process with a modifed process where the root of the tree must remain infected until all its subtrees became uninfected. The advantage of this coupling is that the processes in the subtrees become independent. However, if the degree of the root is
D
then in the modified process, it will take time
\exp(c\lambda D)
rather than
\exp\left(c\lambda^{2}D\right)
until all the children of the root become uninfected. What this analysis misses is that each infected child has only probability
\lambda
of sending an infection back to its parent.

To give a sharp analysis, we introduce a modified dynamics that stochastically dominates the original where particles are strongly infected if they will send an infection to a neighbour before healing and weakly infected otherwise. Weak infections can then be effectively ignored. A vertex
u
sends a strong infection to a neighbour
v
with rate
\frac{\lambda^{2}d_{v}}{1+\lambda d_{v}}
which is roughly
C\lambda^{2}
when the degree of
v
is constant. With this formulation, we get sharper bounds on the tail of the expected survival time and number of particles to hit the leaves.

To complete the proof, we use this analysis to show by a union bound that for any vertex
v
, starting with
v
infected, the expected number of infections to exit a ball of radius
C\lambda^{2}\log(\lambda^{-1})\log n
is
o(1)
. When infections leave the ball, we analyse them as new contact processes started from the exit point and stochastically dominate this process by a subcritical branching process which with high probability terminates in
n^{C\lambda^{2}\log(\lambda^{-1})}
steps. Furthermore, we again use our recursive bounds to show that the maximum expected survival time on balls of radius
C\lambda^{2}\log(\lambda^{-1})\log n
in the graph is at most
n^{C\lambda^{2}\log(\lambda^{-1})}
with high probability. Combining these results establishes Theorem
1.3
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider the contact process
(X_{t})
with infection rate

on the star graph

is at least

.

Proof.

Let
s=\frac{16C\log\ell}{\ell}
.
The probability that the root is still infected until time

is
\mathbb{P}(\text{Pois}(s)=0)=e^{-s}\geq 1-s=1-\frac{16C\log\ell}{\ell}
.

The probability that there are at least

to its leaves in time
[0,s]
is

\mathbb{P}(\text{Pois}(\ell s)\geq 8C\log\ell)=\mathbb{P}(\text{Pois}(16C\log\ell)\geq 8C\log\ell)\geq 1-2\exp(-8C\log\ell/6)\geq 1-2\ell^{-C},

where we use the following standard tail bound for Poisson distribution (see, for instance,
[
3
, Chapter 2]
):

(6)

\mathbb{P}(|\text{Pois}(\Lambda)-\Lambda|\geq x)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2(\Lambda+x)}\right),\quad\forall x>0.

Under this event, letting
M
be the integer

, the probability that these rings correspond to at least
M
distinct leaves is at least

1-\frac{{k\choose M}M^{2M}}{k^{2M}}\geq 1-\left(\frac{ekM^{2}}{Mk^{2}}\right)^{M}=1-\left(\frac{4eC\log\ell}{k}\right)^{4C\log\ell}\geq 1-\exp(-4C\log\ell)=1-\ell^{-4C},

where we used
C\leq k/(32\log\ell)
.

For each leaf infected before time
s
, the probability that it remains infected until time

is at least
\mathbb{P}(\text{Pois}(1)=0)=e^{-1}
. Thus, given that there are
\geq M
leaves infected before time

, at least
M/4
of them remain infected until time

with probability at least

where
we used the Chernoff inequality

\mathbb{P}(\text{Bin}(M,q)\leq Mq\delta)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}Mq}{2}\right),\quad\forall M\in\mathbb{N},\text{ }q,\delta\in(0,1).

Combine all of these events and the union bound, we obtain the desired statement.

The next two lemmas show that once many leaves are infected, they keep the root well-infected which then keeps the number of infected leaves from decreasing.

Lemma 3.2

Consider the contact process

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq\lambda M/8\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lambda M/8}\eta_{i}\geq 1/4\right)

5.
Survival time on trees

To prove Theorem
1.3
, we first derive an upper bound on the survival time on trees. Following the methods in
[
2
]
and
[
28
]
, we can only get an upper bound of

rather than

. The key new idea here is that as

is small compared to the degree of a typical vertex, a significant portion of infections are

in the sense the vertex infected recovers before spreading the infection. Therefore, we introduce a modified process that focuses on

infections and this allows us to get the power

in the upper bound.

5.1.
The coupling with the starred contact process

In this section, we introduce the

contact process which takes into account whether a new infection at a vertex

is
strong
. If we ignore the weak infections, the contact process basically remains the same except that at any time, there could be vertices that are infected and then recover at a later time without passing the infection to any other vertices.

To be specific, let us sample the Poisson clocks for recoveries and infections on

with

is read from the graphical representation (See Section

At time

•

,

•

recovers before sending any infection out.

Otherwise, we say that

•

If the clock is a recovery at a vertex

•

If the clock is an infection from a healthy vertex

(that is

) to another vertex

•

If the clock is an infection from an infected vertex
v
(that is

equals 1 or
1^{*}
) to another infected vertex

X^{*}_{t}(v)=1
.

•

If the clock is an infection from an infected vertex
v
(that is

equals 1 or
1^{*}
) to a healthy vertex

at

remains healthy at time

. In either cases, set

In any cases, the state at other vertices remain the same.
We observe that for any time

and any vertex

and

then in the process
(X_{s})
, after this time,

will recover before infecting any other vertices. Denote by

and

the survival time of the processes

and

Since

Thus, it suffices to bound

. For the rest of this paper, we shall only work on the starred contact process and so, for simplicity, we write

for the starred contact process from now on.

M_{i}^{\otimes}(T)
, correspondingly. We define

M^{\otimes}(T)=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}M_{i}^{\otimes}(T).

By a standard coupling between
(X^{\sharp}_{t})
and
(X_{t})
based on their graphical representations, we have
M_{l}(T)\leq M^{\sharp}(T)
.

During one excursion of
(X^{\sharp}_{t})
,
\rho
first sends an infection to either
\rho^{+}
or one of its children
\rho_{i}
. The probability that this is a strong infection to one of the
\rho_{i}
is
\frac{D}{D+1}\theta
where
\theta
is defined in (
18
). Between this first infection and the end of the excursion of
(X^{\sharp}_{t})
, the number of excursions of
(X^{\otimes}_{i,t})
is a geometric random variable with “success” probability
\frac{1}{1+\lambda D\theta}
. This is because
\rho
recovers at rate
1
and sends a strong infection to the
\rho_{i}
with rate
\lambda D\theta
; and the excursion of
(X^{\sharp}_{t})
ends precisely when
\rho
recovers. So, the expected number of excursions of
(X^{\otimes}_{i,t})
in one excursion of
(X^{\sharp}_{t})
is
\left(\frac{D}{D+1}+\lambda D\right)\theta
. Thus,

(26)

\displaystyle M^{\sharp}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{D\theta}{D+1}M^{\otimes}(T)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\lambda D\theta}{1+\lambda D\theta}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{1+\lambda D\theta}kM^{\otimes}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{D+1}+\lambda\right)D\theta M^{\otimes}(T).

Now we control
M^{\otimes}(T)
in terms of
\{M_{l-1}(T_{i})\}
. Let
X^{\otimes}_{t}
be the contact process on
T
with
\rho
always infected and all other vertices initially healthy. We observe that

(27)

\begin{split}&\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}_{\textsc{cp}}\left[\,\left|\left\{s\in[0,t]:\;X_{s}^{\otimes}(v)=1^{*}\textnormal{ and }X_{s-}^{\otimes}(v)=0\right\}\right|\,\right]\\
&=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\text{the number of excursions of $(X^{\otimes}_{s})$ in $[0,t]$}}{t}M^{\otimes}(T)\\
&=\frac{M^{\otimes}(T)}{(\lambda D\theta)^{-1}+S^{\otimes}(T)}.\end{split}

On the other hand, let
\mathcal{L}_{i}=\{v\in T_{i}:\textnormal{dist}(v,\rho_{i})=l-1\}
for each
i\in[D]
and
(X^{(i)}_{t})
be the restriction of
(X^{\otimes}_{t})
on
T_{i}
. We observe that

\begin{split}&\sum_{v\in\mathcal{L}}\left|\left\{s\in[0,t]:\;X_{s}^{\otimes}(v)=1^{*}\textnormal{ and }X_{s-}^{\otimes}(v)=0\right\}\right|\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\left|\left\{s\in[0,t]:\;X_{s}^{(i)}(v)=1^{*}\textnormal{ and }X_{s-}^{(i)}(v)=0\right\}\right|.\end{split}

Thus, the identity in (
27
) is also equal to

\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{M_{l-1}(T_{i})}{(\lambda w(T_{i}))^{-1}+S(T_{i})},

where
w(T_{i})
, as defined in (
14
), is the probability that an infection from
\rho
to
\rho_{i}
is strong.
Therefore,

\displaystyle M^{\otimes}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda D\theta}\left(1+\lambda D\theta S^{\otimes}(T)\right)\sum_{i=1}^{D}\lambda w(T_{i})M_{l-1}(T_{i})(1+\lambda w(T_{i})S(T_{i}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{D}w(T_{i})M_{l-1}(T_{i})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\,\leq\,j\,\leq\,D\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}(1+\lambda w(T_{j})S(T_{j})),

where in the second equality we used equation (
22
). Finally, combining this with (
26
) and
M^{\sharp}(T)\geq M_{l}(T)
, we deduce the conclusion.
∎

5.4.
The tail bound lemma

In the previous sections, we have derived recursive bounds for the survival time
S(T)
and the number of infections at the leaves
M_{l}(T)
. These quantities are random variables defined on the probability space of the Galton-Watson tree. In this section, we provide a key lemma that gives tail estimates for
S(T)
and
M_{l}(T)
using the recursions. Let
D\sim\mu
where
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}e^{3D}<\infty
. In particular, the measure
\mu
in Theorem
1.3
satisfies this condition.
For the rest of the paper, let
A,B,c
be any constants that satisfy
A\ll\frac{1}{c}\ll B
. For definiteness, we set

(28)

A=\log\mathbb{E}e^{3D}+2e,\quad c=\frac{1}{A},\quad B=24A.

We assume that the infection rate
\lambda
is sufficiently small compared to
B^{-1}
. Let

(29)

q=\frac{c}{16\lambda^{2}\log(\lambda^{-1})}

and

(30)

p=(B\lambda)^{-q}\exp(-c\lambda^{-2}/B).

Definition 5.4
.

A nonnegative random variable
X
is said to have a good tail (with respect to
A,B,\lambda
) if

(31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)\leq f(t):=\begin{cases}\exp(-ct\lambda^{-1})\quad\text{for }A\lambda\leq t\leq\frac{1}{B\lambda}\\
pt^{-q}(\log(eBt\lambda))^{-2}\quad\text{for }\frac{1}{B\lambda}\leq t.\end{cases}

A random variable
X
is said to have a strong tail if

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)\leq f(3t)/3\quad\text{for all $t\geq A\lambda/3$}.

Since
f
is decreasing, it is clear that a random variable with a strong tail also has a good tail. Moreover, if
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
have strong tails then
X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3}
has a good tail because

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3}\geq t)\leq\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\geq t/3)+\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\geq t/3)+\mathbb{P}(X_{3}\geq t/3).

A random variable with a good tail starts with an exponential tail and then a power law tail. The extra term
(\log(eBt\lambda))^{-2}
in the latter tail is merely a technical device (used in (
44
)) for us to prove the following recursive lemma which is essential for our subsequent analysis.

Proposition 5.5

(Recursive tail bound)
.

Let
D\sim\mu
where
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}e^{3D}<\infty
. There exists
\lambda_{0}(\mu)>0
such that the following holds. Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{D}
be independent, not necessarily identically distributed, nonnegative random variables with good tails. Then for
0<\lambda<\lambda_{0}(\mu)
,

(33)

X=\prod_{i=1}^{D}(1+\lambda X_{i})-1,

has a strong tail.

Remark 5.6
.

We remark that this lemma works for any
q
satisfying

(34)

20\log\frac{1}{\lambda}<q<\frac{c}{8\lambda^{2}\log(\lambda^{-1})}.

Here, we set
q
as in (
29
) for clarity.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
5.5
.

For notational convenience, we let
\Lambda=\frac{1}{\lambda}
. We split the proof into steps depending on the range of
t
in
\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)
.

5.4.1.
Small
t
:
\frac{A\lambda}{3}\leq t\leq\frac{\Lambda}{3B}

We need to show that

(35)

\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)\leq\exp(-3ct\Lambda)/3=f(3t)/3=:p_{t}.

Let
M=t\Lambda\geq A/3
by (
28
).
Since
\mathbb{E}e^{3D}<\infty
by (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D\geq M)\leq\mathbb{E}e^{3D}\exp\left(-3M\right)\leq\exp\left(-3ct\Lambda\right)/24=p_{t}/8.

So, it suffices to assume that
D\leq M
.
The next lemma allows to use truncation on
X_{i}
.

Lemma 5.7
.

We have

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\leq M:X_{i}\geq\frac{2\Lambda}{B}\right)\leq\exp(-3ct\Lambda)/24=p_{t}/8.

Proof.

Since the
X_{i}
have good tails, by the union bound, we have

(36)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\leq M:X_{i}\geq\frac{2\Lambda}{B}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M2^{-q}\exp(-c\Lambda^{2}/B)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2}2^{-q}\exp(-c\Lambda^{2}/B)\leq\frac{1}{24}\exp(-c\Lambda^{2}/B)\leq\exp(-3ct\Lambda)/24,

where we used (
28
), (
29
) and
t\leq\frac{\Lambda}{3B}
.
∎

Lemma
5.7
allows us to get the tail bound for the sum of
X_{i}
as follows.

Lemma 5.8
.

We have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}\geq(\log 2)\Lambda\right)\leq\exp(-3ct\Lambda)/12=p_{t}/4.

Proof.
Let

X^{\prime}_{i}=X_{i}\textbf{1}_{X_{i}\leq\frac{2\Lambda}{B}}
.
By Lemma
5.7
, it suffices to show that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}X^{\prime}_{i}\geq(\log 2)\Lambda\right)\leq\exp(-ct\Lambda)/24.

Let
\xi_{i}=\Lambda X^{\prime}_{i}\leq\frac{2\Lambda^{2}}{B}
then

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{c\xi_{i}/4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{c}{4}\int_{0}^{\frac{2\Lambda^{2}}{B}}e^{cx/4}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}\geq x)dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+e+\frac{c}{4}\int_{A}^{\frac{2\Lambda^{2}}{B}}e^{cx/4}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}\geq x)dx\quad\text{since $cA\leq 4$ by \eqref{eq:const:1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+e+\frac{c}{4}\int_{A}^{\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{B}}e^{-cx/2}dx+\frac{c}{4}\int_{\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{B}}^{\frac{2\Lambda^{2}}{B}}e^{cx/4}e^{-c\Lambda^{2}/B}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+e+2+1\leq e^{2}.

And so

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\xi_{i}\geq(\log 2)\Lambda^{2}\right)\leq e^{-c(\log 2)\Lambda^{2}/4}e^{2M}=\exp\left(2t\Lambda-c(\log 2)\Lambda^{2}/4\right)\leq\exp(-3ct\Lambda)/24,

where we used (
28
) to see that
2t\Lambda+3ct\Lambda\leq\Lambda^{2}(2+c)/B\leq\frac{c(\log 2)\Lambda^{2}}{8}
.
∎

Combining the above lemmas, we get

\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)\leq p_{t}/2+\mathbb{P}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{M}(1+\lambda X_{i})-1\geq t,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}\leq(\log 2)\Lambda,\quad X_{i}\leq\frac{2\Lambda}{B}\text{ for all $i$}\right).

When
\sum_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}\leq(\log 2)\Lambda
, we have

(38)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{M}(1+\lambda X_{i})\leq e^{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}}\leq 1+2\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{M}X_{i},

where in the second inequality, we used that
e^{s}\leq 1+2s
for all
0\leq s\leq\log 2
.

Thus,

\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)\leq p_{t}/2+\mathbb{P}\left(2\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}\textbf{1}_{X_{i}\leq\frac{2\Lambda}{B}}\geq t\right).

Let
\xi_{i}=\Lambda X_{i}\textbf{1}_{X_{i}\leq\frac{2\Lambda}{B}}
then as in (
37
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{c\xi_{i}/4}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{2}.

and so for
\Lambda
sufficiently large,

\mathbb{P}\left(2\sum_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}\textbf{1}_{X_{i}\leq\frac{2\Lambda}{B}}\geq t\Lambda\right)\leq e^{-ct\Lambda^{2}/8}e^{2M}=\exp\left(2t\Lambda-ct\Lambda^{2}/8\right)\leq\exp(-3ct\Lambda)/6=p_{t}/2.

This proves (
35
).

5.4.2.
Large
t
:
t>\frac{\Lambda}{3B}
.

We need to show that

(39)

\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)\leq f(3t)/3=p(3t)^{-q}(\log(3eBt\lambda))^{-2}/3=:p_{t}.

Firstly, we note that for every
r>0
, by Markov’s inequality and the assumption (
3
) on the exponent moment of
\mu
,

(40)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D\geq r\right)\leq\mathbb{E}e^{3D}e^{-3r}.

Let
Y_{i}=\log(1+\lambda X_{i})
. Then
\log X\leq\sum_{i=1}^{D}Y_{i}
and by the good tail of
X_{i}
,

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i}\geq s\right)\leq\begin{cases}\exp(-c(e^{s}-1)\Lambda^{2})\quad\text{if }\log(1+A\lambda^{2})\leq s\leq\beta,\\
p\lambda^{q}(e^{s}-1)^{-q}\log^{-2}(eB(e^{s}-1))\quad\text{if }s>\beta,\end{cases}

where
\beta=\log\left(1+\frac{1}{B}\right)
.

Let
d=\mathbb{E}D
, we get

(42)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\log X\geq\log t)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D}Y_{i}\geq\log t\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2d}Y_{i}\geq\log t\right)+\sum_{r=2d+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}Y_{i}\geq\log t,D=r\right).

To handle
\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}Y_{i}\geq\log t\right)
, we decompose the sum
\sum Y_{i}
into subsums in which
Y_{i}\leq\beta
, and
Y>\beta
and denote the probability that each subsum is large as follows

\displaystyle p_{1,h,s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{h}\leq r}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{h}Y_{i_{j}}\geq s,Y_{i_{j}}\leq\beta\right)

\displaystyle p_{2,h,s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{h}\leq r}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{h}Y_{i_{j}}\geq s,Y_{i_{j}}>\beta\right).

Observe that if
\sum_{i=1}^{r}Y_{i}\geq\log t
then by setting
s
to be the integer part of the first subsum, the second subsum must be at least
\log t-s-1
. Thus,

(43)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}Y_{i}\geq\log t\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r}{r\choose k}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{N},s\leq\log t}p_{1,k,s}p_{2,r-k,\log t-s-1}.

In the next two lemmas, we bound these probabilities.

Lemma 5.9
.

We have for every
k,s\in\mathbb{N}
,

p_{1,k,s}\leq 8^{k}e^{-\frac{1}{2}c\Lambda^{2}s}.

Proof.

Let
Y^{\prime}_{i}=Y_{i}\textbf{1}_{Y_{i}\leq\beta}
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}Y_{i}\geq s,Y_{i}\leq\beta\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}Y^{\prime}_{i}\geq s\right).

For a positive number
z
, we have
e^{zY^{\prime}_{i}}\leq 1+e^{zY_{i}}\textbf{1}_{Y_{i}\leq\beta}=1+(1+\lambda X_{i})^{z}\textbf{1}_{X_{i}\leq\Lambda/B}
and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{zY^{\prime}_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+(1+A\lambda^{2})^{z}+\lambda z\int_{A\lambda}^{\Lambda/B}(1+\lambda t)^{z-1}\mathbb{P}(X_{i}\geq t)dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+(1+A\lambda^{2})^{z}+\lambda z\int_{A\lambda}^{\Lambda/B}(1+\lambda t)^{z-1}e^{-ct\Lambda}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+(1+A\lambda^{2})^{z}+\lambda z\int_{A\lambda}^{\Lambda/B}e^{-t(c\Lambda-\lambda(z-1))}dt.

By choosing
z=\frac{1}{2}c\Lambda^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{zY^{\prime}_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+(1+A\lambda^{2})^{c\Lambda^{2}/2}+\frac{1}{2}c\Lambda\int_{A\lambda}^{\infty}e^{-tc\Lambda/2}dt\leq 1+2e^{cA/2}\leq 8,

where we used
cA\leq 1
.
Thus, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}Y^{\prime}_{i}\geq s\right)\leq e^{-zs}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}e^{zY^{\prime}_{i}}\leq 8^{k}e^{-\frac{1}{2}c\Lambda^{2}s}

giving the desired bound.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

For every
m\geq 1
and
s^{\prime}>2\beta m
, we have

p_{2,m,s^{\prime}}\leq\left(\frac{8p2^{q}\lambda^{q}}{\beta^{2+q}}\right)^{m}e^{-q(s^{\prime}-\beta m)}(s^{\prime}-\beta m)^{-2}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\geq s^{\prime},Y_{i}>\beta\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{u\mathbb{N}^{m}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}\geq(s^{\prime}/\beta-m)}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}\in\beta(u_{i},u_{i}+1])

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{u}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}>\beta u_{i}),

where the last sum runs over all
m
-tuples
(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
with
u_{i}\geq 1
and
\sum u_{i}=s^{\prime}/\beta-m
(note that the condition
s^{\prime}>2\beta m
implies that such a tuple exists).
By (
41
) (with
s=\beta u_{i}\geq\beta
), we have

\displaystyle p_{2,m,s^{\prime}}\leq p^{m}\lambda^{mq}\sum_{u}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(e^{\beta u_{i}}-1\right)^{-q}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\log^{-2}(eB(e^{\beta u_{i}}-1)).

We observe since
\beta\leq 1
we have that for all
x\geq\beta
, we have
e^{x}-1\geq\frac{x}{x+1}e^{x}\geq\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}e^{x}\geq\frac{\beta}{2}e^{x}.
Thus,

\displaystyle p_{2,m,s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle p^{m}\lambda^{mq}\sum_{u}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\beta}{2}e^{\beta u_{i}}\right)^{-q}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\log^{-2}(eB\beta e^{\beta u_{i}}/2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{m}\lambda^{mq}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}\right)^{qm}\sum_{u}e^{-q\beta\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta u_{i}+\tau\right)^{-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle p^{m}\lambda^{mq}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}\right)^{qm}e^{-q(s^{\prime}-\beta m)}\sum_{u}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta u_{i}+\tau\right)^{-2},

where
\tau=\log\left(eB\beta/2\right)\geq\log(2e/5)>\beta
as
B\geq 32
.

To handle the sum-product, we use the following observation that holds for all integer
w\geq 2
:

(44)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{w-1}(\tau+\beta v)^{-2}(\tau+\beta(w-v))^{-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{v=1}^{w/2}(\tau+\beta v)^{-2}(\tau+\beta w/2)^{-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8}{\beta^{2}(2\tau+\beta w)^{2}}\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}\left(v+\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)^{-2}\leq\frac{8}{\beta\tau(\tau+\beta w)^{2}}.

By induction over
m
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{u}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta u_{i}+\tau\right)^{-2}=\sum_{u_{1}+\dots+u_{m}=s^{\prime}/\beta-m}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta u_{i}+\tau\right)^{-2}\leq\frac{8^{m}}{\beta^{m}\tau^{m}}(\tau+s^{\prime}-\beta m)^{-2}.

Thus,

p_{2,m,s^{\prime}}\leq\left(\frac{8p2^{q}\lambda^{q}}{\beta^{q}\beta\tau}\right)^{m}e^{-q(s^{\prime}-\beta m)}(\tau+s^{\prime}-\beta m)^{-2}\leq\left(\frac{8p2^{q}\lambda^{q}}{\beta^{q+2}}\right)^{m}e^{-q(s^{\prime}-\beta m)}(s^{\prime}-\beta m)^{-2}.

∎

We now put everything together in (
43
). We control
\sum Y_{i}
by a case analysis writing

S_{1}=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:s>(\log t)/2\},

S_{2}=S_{2,k,r}=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:s\leq(\log t)/2,s^{\prime}\leq 2(r-k)\beta\},

and

S_{3}=S_{3,k,r}=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:s\leq(\log t)/2,s^{\prime}>2(r-k)\beta\},

where
s^{\prime}=\log t-s-1
, and setting

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}Y_{i}\geq\log t\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r}{r\choose k}\sum_{s\in S_{1}}p_{1,k,s}+\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}{r\choose k}\sum_{s\in S_{2}}p_{2,r-k,s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}{r\choose k}\sum_{s\in S_{3}}p_{1,k,s}p_{2,r-k,s^{\prime}}=:T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}.

Note that in the sums defining
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
, the index
k
is at most
r-1
because otherwise
s
would have been
[\log t]>\log t/2
.
By Lemma
5.9
and
t\geq\Lambda/(3B)
, we have

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r}{r\choose k}\sum_{s\in S_{1}}8^{k}e^{-\frac{1}{2}c\Lambda^{2}s}\leq 2^{r}8^{r}e^{-\frac{1}{4}c\Lambda^{2}\log t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 16^{r}p(3t)^{-q}(\log(3eBt\lambda))^{-2}/(9\Lambda)=16^{r}p_{t}/(3\Lambda).

The contribution from
S_{2}
is bounded by

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}{r\choose k}\sum_{s\in S_{2}}\max_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r-k}\leq r}\prod_{j=1}^{r-k}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i_{j}}>\beta\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}{r\choose k}\sum_{s\in S_{2}}\left(p\lambda^{q}B^{q}\right)^{r-k}\quad\text{by \eqref{tail:Y:1}}.

We note that for
s\in S_{2}
,

2(r-k)\beta\geq s^{\prime}=\log t-s-1\geq\log t/3.

So,

r-k\geq\frac{\log t}{6\beta}\geq\frac{\log t}{3}.

Hence,

T_{2}\leq 2^{r}|S_{2}|\left(p\lambda^{q}B^{q}\right)^{\log t/3}\leq 2^{r}(\log t)\left(p\lambda^{q}B^{q}\right)^{\log t/3}\leq 16^{r}p_{t}/(3\Lambda).

By Lemma
5.10
, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle t^{-q}e^{q}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}{r\choose k}8^{k}\left(\frac{8p2^{q}\lambda^{q}e^{q\beta}}{\beta^{q+2}}\right)^{r-k}\sum_{s\in S_{3}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}c\Lambda^{2}s}(s^{\prime}-\beta(r-k))^{-2},

where we used
q\leq\frac{1}{4}c\Lambda^{2}
to get
e^{-\frac{1}{4}c\Lambda^{2}s}e^{-qs^{\prime}}\leq e^{-q(s+s^{\prime})}=t^{-q}e^{q}
.
By the definition of
S_{3}
, we have
s\leq\log t/2
and so
s^{\prime}\geq\log t/3
. Since
s^{\prime}\geq 2(r-k)\beta
, we have
s^{\prime}-(r-k)\beta\geq s^{\prime}/2\geq\log t/6
. Thus,
(s^{\prime}-\beta(r-k))^{-2}\leq 36(\log(3eBt\lambda))^{-2}
. So

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 36t^{-q}(\log(3eBt\lambda))^{-2}e^{q}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}{r\choose k}8^{k}\left(\frac{8p2^{q}\lambda^{q}e^{q\beta}}{\beta^{q+2}}\right)^{r-k}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{4}c\Lambda^{2}s}

\displaystyle\frac{16^{2d}}{\Lambda}+\sum_{r=2d+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}e^{3D}e^{-3r}\frac{16^{r}}{\Lambda}\leq 1

for sufficiently small

5.5.1.
Tail bounds for the excursion time

Lemma 5.11
.

Let

w(\mathcal{T}_{\leq l})S(\mathcal{T}_{\leq l})
has a good tail where

is the weight defined in (

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
l

is trivial because
w(\mathcal{T}_{\leq l})S(\mathcal{T}_{\leq l})=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}
. Assume that we have the statement for
l-1

Let

and
X_{i}=w(\mathcal{T}_{i})S(\mathcal{T}_{i})
where
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is the subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{\leq l}
containing the
i
-th child of the root of
\mathcal{T}_{\leq l}
and its successors. By Lemma
5.1
, we have

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{(1+\lambda(D+1))^{2}}+\prod_{i=1}^{D}(1+\lambda X_{i})\leq X_{a}+\prod_{i=1}^{D}(1+\lambda X_{i})-1,

where
X_{a}=2\lambda(D+1)+\left[\frac{\lambda(D+1)}{1+\lambda(D+1)}\right]^{2}
contains the constant part.

By the induction hypothesis,
X_{i}
has a good tail for all
i
. Thus, by Proposition
5.5
,
\prod_{i=1}^{D}(1+\lambda X_{i})-1
has a strong tail. Since

and

. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

5.5.2.
Tail bounds for the total infections at the leaves

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
l\geq 0
be an integer. Let
\mathcal{T}\sim\textsf{\footnotesize{{GW}}}(\mu)
and

be the expected total infections at depth-

Proof of Lemma

.

Let

We shall prove by induction that

has a good tail. This will imply that
(1.5)^{l}w(\mathcal{T})M_{\leq l}(\mathcal{T}_{\leq l})

\displaystyle w(\mathcal{T}_{\leq l})S(\mathcal{T}_{\leq l})\leq X_{a}+\prod_{i=1}^{D}(1+\lambda w(\mathcal{T}_{i})S(\mathcal{T}_{i}))-1\leq X_{a}+\prod_{i=1}^{D}(1+2\lambda W^{l-1}(\mathcal{T}_{i}))-1,

\displaystyle 1.5\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{D}W^{l-1}(\mathcal{T}_{i})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\,\leq\,j\,\leq\,D\\

So, for all

Definition 6.1

be the expected total infections at leaves.

For an

Proposition 6.13
.

Let

be a probability measure satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem

. Let

The result for

6.4.
Finishing the proof of Theorem

We are now ready to finish the proof of Theorem

It suffices to show that with high probability on the randomness of

Recall the definition of

Lemma 6.15

Let

\sup_{s}\mathbb{E}\exp(tZ_{s}\tilde{d}^{-s})<\infty,

Proof of Lemma

.

We recall the notion of augmented distributions

and

before the statement of Lemma

For the contact process

on

Lemma 6.16
.

We have that

(48)

\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{v\in V(G_{n})}S_{v}\leq n^{\delta},\max_{v\in V(G_{n})}\quad M_{v}\leq\frac{1}{\log n}\right]\geq 1-o(1).

Proof.

Fix

. By Lemma

,

and

are dominated by
\textsf{\footnotesize{{EGW}}}(\mu^{\sharp},\widetilde{\mu}^{\sharp};l,s)_{\leq\delta\log n}
and
\textsf{\footnotesize{{GW}}}(\mu^{\sharp},\widetilde{\mu}^{\sharp})_{\leq\delta\log n}

where

is some constant depending on

and

∎

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the intersection of the good events in Lemmas

Lemma 6.17
.

Let

. Let
C
be the constant in Lemma
6.14
. For each vertex

, let

be the survival time of

and
\mathbf{M}_{v}
be the number of infections at the leaves of
B_{v}
of
(X^{(v)}_{B_{v}})(t)

(49)

\mathbb{P}_{\textsc{cp}}(\mathbf{S}_{v}\geq jn^{(C+1)\delta})\leq 2^{-j},\quad\text{for all }j\geq 1

and

(50)

\mathbb{P}_{\textsc{cp}}(\mathbf{M}_{v}\geq jn^{(3C+1)\delta})\leq 2^{-j+1},\quad\text{for all }1\leq j\leq C\log n.

Proof.

To see (
49

, the probability that

survives until time

is bounded as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\textsc{cp}}(\mathbf{S}_{v}>t+n^{(C+1)\delta}|\mathbf{S}_{v}\geq t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in B_{v}}\mathbb{P}_{\textsc{cp}}(\mathbf{S}_{v}>n^{(C+1)\delta}|X^{(v)}_{B_{v}}\text{ starts with $u$ initially at $1^{*}$})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|B_{v}|n^{-C\delta}\leq 1/2,

where in the second inequality we use Markov’s inequality, Lemma
6.16
and the fact that

contains

49

Next, for (
50
), let
{\bf C}_{v,t_{0}}
be the total number of infection clock rings in

before time

. We observe that

Thus,

(51)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\textsc{cp}}(\mathbf{M}_{v}\geq jn^{(3C+1)\delta})\leq\mathbb{P}_{\textsc{cp}}(\mathbf{S}_{v}\geq jn^{(C+1)\delta})+\mathbb{P}_{\textsc{cp}}({\bf C}_{v,jn^{(C+1)\delta}}\geq jn^{(3C+1)\delta}).

Lemma 6.18
.

Let
k=n^{(6C+6)\delta}
. Let
\mathbf{M}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{M}_{k}
be independent random variables satisfying (
50
) and
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{M}_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\log n}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{M}_{i}\geq k-1\right)\leq n^{-C/2}.

Proof.

By (
50
),

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{M}_{i}\geq C\log n\cdot n^{(3C+1)\delta})\leq 2n^{-C}.

And so, as
\delta
is sufficiently small,

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{M}_{i}\leq C\log n\cdot n^{(3C+1)\delta},\ i=1,\dots,k)\geq 1-2kn^{-C}\geq 1-\frac{1}{2n^{C/2}}.

Applying the Azuma’s inequality to the random variables
X_{i}:=\mathbf{M}_{i}\textbf{1}_{\mathbf{M}_{i}\leq C\log n\cdot n^{(3C+1)\delta}}
with
\mathbb{E}X_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\log n}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}\geq k-1)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}}{8\sum_{i=1}^{k}||X_{i}||^{2}_{\infty}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{k}{8C^{2}(\log n)^{2}\cdot n^{(6C+2)\delta}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2n^{C/2}},

where
||X_{i}||_{\infty}:=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:X_{i}\leq x\text{\ a.s.}\}\leq C\log n\cdot n^{(3C+1)\delta}

∎

6.4.2.
Combining the local pieces

Finally, we show how to combine the local information to bound the survival time on the whole graph. We use the following
decomposed process
, which is a decomposition of the contact process
(X_{t}^{(v)})
on
G_{n}
by stochastically dominating it by a collection of processes running on local neighborhoods
\{B_{u}\}_{u}
.

1.

Initially, run
(X_{B_{v}}^{(v)}(t))
.

2.

In
B_{v}
, when some
u\in\mathcal{L}(B_{v})
becomes strongly infected in
B_{v}
at time
t
(and has been healthy until time
t-
), initiate an independent copy of
(X_{B_{u}}^{(u)})
on
B_{u}
.

3.

Repeat Step 2 on every running copies of
(X_{B_{u}}^{(u)})
until all the processes have terminated, that is, when all vertices in every generated copy are healthy.

The survival time of this decomposed process stochastically dominates the process on
G_{n}
.
Let
{\bf R}_{v}
denote the termination time of the decomposed process and let
R_{v}
be its expectation.
Let
\textbf{U}_{v}
be the enumeration of vertices
u
of which a copy of
(X_{B_{u}}^{(u)})
has been generated during the decomposed process, counted with multiplicities.
The size of
\textbf{U}_{v}
can be controlled by
{\bf M}_{v}
.

Lemma 6.19
.

Let
C
be the constant in Lemma
6.14
. For each
v\in V(G_{n})
,

(52)

\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{U}_{v}|\geq n^{(6C+6)\delta})\leq n^{-C/2}.

Thus, with high probability, for all
v\in V
, we have
|\mathbf{U}_{v}|\leq n^{(6C+6)\delta}
.

Proof of Lemma
6.19
.

We think about
{\bf U}_{v}
as the total number of vertices in the tree with a root at
v
and for each vertex
u
of the tree, the number of its children equals the number of infections at the leaves of
B_{u}
. In particular,
{\bf U}_{v}
can be thought of as a Galton-Watson tree in which
{\bf M}_{v}
is the number of vertices in the first generation, denoted by
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
. Then
{\bf M}_{v_{1}}+\dots+{\bf M}_{v_{k}}
is the number of vertices in the second generation and so on. Assume that the total number of vertices of the tree is
|\mathbf{U}_{v}|=K
. We then have

1+{\bf M}_{1}+{\bf M}_{2}+\dots+{\bf M}_{K}=K,

where
{\bf M}_{i}
is the number of children of the
i
-th vertex of
{\bf U}_{v}
. These
{\bf M}_{i}
are independent and satisfies (
50
) by Lemma
6.17
. Note that for every
l\leq K
, we have
1+{\bf M}_{1}+\dots+{\bf M}_{l}\geq l
as the LHS is at least the number of vertices in the tree formed by vertices
1,\dots,l
. Thus, letting
k=n^{(6C+6)\delta}
, by Lemma
6.18
, we obtain

(53)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{U}_{v}|\geq n^{(6C+6)\delta})\leq\mathbb{P}(1+{\bf M}_{1}+\dots+{\bf M}_{k}\geq k)\leq n^{-C/2}

proving the lemma.
∎

Back to the proof of Theorem
1.3
, thanks to Lemma
6.19
, we conclude that with high probability, for all
v\in V(G_{n})
, at any time
t
, the number of infected local neighborhoods

is at most
n^{(6C+6)\delta}
. By Lemma
6.16
, conditioned on this event and on

, the expected survival time of
(X^{(v)}_{s})_{s\geq t}
is at most

\sum_{|X^{(v)}_{t}\cap{\bf U}_{v}|}n^{\delta}\leq n^{(6C+7)\delta}

and so
(X_{s})
survives until time

with probability at most

. Repeating this argument for

with

, we conclude that the process
(X^{(v)}_{t})
survives until time

with probability at most
n^{-2}
.

Taking the union bound over all
n
vertices
v
, with probability at most

, the contact process starting at all vertices infected survives until time

, concluding the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Appendix B: Proof of Lemma
6.15

Let
N_{i}\sim\widetilde{\mu}
be independent. We have for all
s\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(tZ_{s+1}\tilde{d}^{-(s+1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{s}}tN_{i}\tilde{d}^{-(s+1)}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{s}}tN_{i}\tilde{d}^{-(s+1)}\right)\big|Z_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}\exp\left(tN_{1}\tilde{d}^{-(s+1)}\right)\right)^{Z_{s}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\exp\left(Z_{s}\log\left(\mathbb{E}\exp\left(tN_{1}\tilde{d}^{-(s+1)}\right)\right)\right).

Since
\mathbb{E}_{N\sim\mu}e^{cN}<\infty
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{N\sim\widetilde{\mu}}e^{uN}<\infty
for all
u<c
. Let
b=c/2
.
For all
u\in(0,b]
, we have

(60)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{uN_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{i}N^{i}}{i!}\leq 1+u\widetilde{d}+\frac{u^{2}}{b^{2}}\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\frac{b^{i}N^{i}}{i!}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{u\widetilde{d}}+\frac{u^{2}}{b^{2}}\mathbb{E}e^{bN_{1}}\leq e^{u\widetilde{d}}+\exp\left(\frac{u^{2}}{b^{2}}\mathbb{E}e^{bN_{1}}\right)-1\leq\exp\left(u\widetilde{d}(1+u\alpha)\right),
