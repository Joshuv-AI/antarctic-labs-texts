An exterior calculus framework for polytopal methods


Abstract
We develop in this work the first polytopal complexes of differential forms. These complexes, inspired by the Discrete De Rham and the Virtual Element approaches, are discrete versions of the de Rham complex of differential forms built on meshes made of general polytopal elements. Both constructions benefit from the high-level approach of polytopal methods, which leads, on certain meshes, to leaner constructions than the finite element method. We establish commutation properties between the interpolators and the discrete and continuous exterior derivatives, prove key polynomial consistency results for the complexes, and show that their cohomologies are isomorphic to the cohomology of the continuous de Rham complex.

This work is a first step towards merging two extremely successful avenues of research in numerical analysis: finite element differential forms and arbitrary-order polytopal methods.

However, due to their higher-level design, which does not require the existence and computability of globally conforming piecewise polynomial basis functions, polytopal methods offer distinctive advantages over finite elements.
These include, in addition to the support of general meshes, the possibility to reduce the dimension of discrete spaces, sometimes below their finite element counterparts

The purpose of the present work is to take one step further and show how exterior calculus can be used to generalise the construction and analysis of polytopal complexes.
More specifically, we present two discrete de Rham complexes in arbitrary dimension and with arbitrary approximation degree that generalise those introduced in
[
36
]
(DDR) and
[
8
]
(VEM).
Three key features set these constructions apart from Finite Element complexes:

\bullet

No explicit spaces of globally conforming differential forms (i.e., subspaces of
H\Lambda(\Omega)
) are needed.
Instead, we work with
fully discrete spaces
made of vectors of polynomial components on the mesh cells (of various dimensions). The meaning of these components is provided by the interpolators on the fully discrete spaces.

\bullet

Due to the absence of explicit underlying conforming spaces, the differential operator of the complex cannot be the exterior derivative. Instead, a
discrete exterior derivative
is constructed combining the polynomial components to mimic the Stokes formula.

\bullet

Discrete potentials
are also designed, again mimicking the Stokes formula.
They are piecewise (discontinuous) polynomial forms on the mesh used, in particular, to define an
L^{2}
-structure on the discrete spaces (an essential tool to discretise PDEs written in weak form).

The choice of the polynomial components in the spaces and the design of discrete exterior derivatives and potentials revolve around two key properties:
polynomial consistency
, which is related to the ability to reproduce exactly polynomial differential forms up to a selected polynomial degree, and
compatibility
, linked to the existence of an isomorphism between the cohomology of the discrete and continuous de Rham complexes.
Notice that, in the finite element framework, polynomial consistency simply corresponds to the fact that suitable polynomial spaces are contained in the (local) finite element space.
While both the DDR- and VEM-inspired constructions heavily rely on discrete versions of the Stokes formula, they do so in a radically different spirit: in the DDR construction, the choice of components in the discrete spaces is inspired by the formula to reconstruct a discrete exterior derivative, which is then used to construct discrete potentials.
In the VEM construction, on the other hand, the space components (and, in particular, those associated with differentials) are chosen based on the formula used to define a discrete potential.
While the choice in the DDR construction leads to leaner spaces, the study of its properties is more elaborated.
Notice that, at this early stage, we haven’t tried to identify the virtual (conforming) spaces that underlie the VEM-inspired construction, and we have made no effort whatsoever in trying to reduce the dimension of the discrete spaces through serendipity.

Setting

We present here the main notions used in the construction of the polytopal complexes of differential forms. For the reader not used to the framework of differential forms, we recall in Appendix

2.1
Spaces of differential forms

Let
M
denote an
n
-dimensional manifold.
In what follows,
M
will typically be a cell of a polytopal mesh (see Section

\mathbb{R}^{m}

For any natural number

\ell

When relevant, regularity is made explicit by prepending the appropriate space (e.g.,

2.2
Integration by parts

We recall the following integration by parts (Stokes) formula:

\int_{M}\mathrm{d}\omega\wedge\mu=(-1)^{\ell+1}\int_{M}\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\partial M}\tr_{\partial M}\omega\wedge\tr_{\partial M}\mu\\
\forall(\omega,\mu)\in C^{1}\Lambda^{\ell}(\overline{M})\times C^{1}\Lambda^{n-\ell-1}(\overline{M}),

(2.1)

where, for any form degree
m
,
\tr_{\partial M}:C^{0}\Lambda^{m}(\overline{M})\to C^{0}\Lambda^{m}(\partial M)
is the trace operator, i.e., the pullback of the inclusion
\partial M\subset M
, and
\partial M
is oriented with respect to
M
.
Formula (
2.1
) will provide the starting point to define discrete counterparts of the exterior derivative and of the corresponding potentials on mesh cells.
It will also drive the choice of the components in the discrete spaces, geared at ensuring that the reconstructions preserve certain polynomial differential forms.

2.3
Hodge star

Assume now that
M
is an open set in a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We denote by
\star:\Lambda^{\ell}(M)\to\Lambda^{n-\ell}(M)
the Hodge star operator, and we set

\star^{-1}\coloneq(-1)^{\ell(n-\ell)}\star,

(2.2)

a notation justified observing that, for any
\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{\ell}(V)
(with
{\rm Alt}^{\ell}(V)
denoting the set of alternating
\ell
-linear forms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

\star^{-1}\star\omega=\omega
(see (
A.3
) in the appendix).
Notice that, while the Hodge star operator depends on

2.4
L^{2}
-orthogonal projectors

Integrating the inner product of
{\rm Alt}^{\ell}(V)
over

yields the inner product of
L^{2}\Lambda^{\ell}(M)
. For any closed subspace

of

-orthogonal projector

on

, defined by the following relation:
For all
\omega\in L^{2}\Lambda^{\ell}(M)

\pi_{\mathcal{X}}\omega\in\mathcal{X}
satisfies

\int_{M}\pi_{\mathcal{X}}\omega\wedge\star\mu=\int_{M}\omega\wedge{\star\mu}\qquad\forall\mu\in\mathcal{X}.

(2.3)

To improve legibility, in the next sections we also introduce specific notations for

-orthogonal projectors on polynomial subspaces

\displaystyle(\mu,\nu)\mapsto{}

Lemma 4

(Traces of trimmed polynomial spaces)
.

The trace preserves trimmed spaces: For all integers
d\in[0,n]
,
d^{\prime}\in[0,d]
and
\ell\in[0,d^{\prime}]
, all
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
, and all
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d^{\prime}}(f)
, we have

\tr_{f^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{\ell}(f)\subset\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{\ell}(f^{\prime}).

Proof.

We first notice that the case
\ell=0
is obvious since, in this case, trimmed spaces are full polynomial spaces (see (
2.12a
)), and the trace preserves full polynomial spaces. We therefore assume in the rest of the proof that
\ell\geq 1
.
As the Koszul operators on differential forms on
f
and
f^{\prime}
are not the same (due to the translation by
\boldsymbol{x}_{f}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{f^{\prime}}
, respectively), we temporarily denote them in this proof by
\kappa_{f}
and
\kappa_{f^{\prime}}
.

The trace is a pullback, so it commutes with
\mathrm{d}
, and we thus have

\tr_{f^{\prime}}(\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{\ell-1}(f))=\mathrm{d}(\tr_{f^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{\ell-1}(f))\subset\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{\ell-1}(f^{\prime}),

where the inclusion holds since the trace preserves full polynomial spaces.
Given the definition (
2.12b
) of the trimmed spaces, the lemma follows if we show that

\tr_{f^{\prime}}\mathcal{K}_{r}^{\ell}(f)\subset\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{\ell}(f^{\prime})\overset{\eqref{eq:trimmed.between}}{=}\mathcal{P}_{r-1}\Lambda^{\ell}(f^{\prime})+\mathcal{K}_{r}^{\ell}(f^{\prime}).

(2.18)

Let
\omega\in\mathcal{P}_{r-1}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(f)
. The definitions of
\tr_{f^{\prime}}
and
\kappa_{f}
give, for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in f^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}
tangent to
f^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\tr_{f^{\prime}}(\kappa_{f}\omega)_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})={}

\displaystyle\omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{f^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})+\omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{f^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\alpha_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell})+(\kappa_{f^{\prime}}\tr_{f^{\prime}}\omega)_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}),

where we have used the linearity of
\omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}
with respect to its first argument to obtain the second equality, and introduced the differential form
\alpha\coloneq\omega(\boldsymbol{x}_{f^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{f},\cdot)
in the third equality. Hence,
\tr_{f^{\prime}}(\kappa_{f}\omega)=\alpha+\kappa_{f^{\prime}}\tr_{f^{\prime}}\omega

(as
\boldsymbol{x}_{f^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{f}
is constant) and
\tr_{f^{\prime}}\omega\in\mathcal{P}_{r-1}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(f^{\prime})

Remark 5

(Choice of polynomial components)
.

The choice of using in (
3.1
) component spaces spanned by
(d-k)
-forms instead of
k

[

through vector proxies; see Example
13

Applying the Hodge-star operator to these components could, in the Euclidean setting at least, enable us to consider components in
\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{k}(f)
(the situation is however different when designing the method on manifolds
[
52
]

Notice that it is by no means clear that full polynomial spaces could be used instead of trimmed spaces while ensuring that the discrete cohomology is isomorphic to the continuous one (cf. Theorem
14
below).
As a matter of fact, as noticed in
[
39
, Section 4.2]

In dimension

[
61

that, in the lowest-order case, the dimension of the kernel of the discrete gradient depends on the number of edges of the polygon, which clearly prevents one from establishing an isomorphism with the de Rham cohomology.

Remark 6

(Virtual spaces)
.

It is possible to identify virtual spaces underlying
\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}
and its restrictions to mesh faces
f
in the spirit of
[
7
]
.
These spaces, however, play no role in the following discussion, so we do not present them here to avoid confusion.
In the present framework, the connections between polynomial components attached to a mesh cell and its boundary are not realised by a virtual function, but rather by the reconstructions presented in Section
3.1.3
below.

Remark 7

(Comparison with trimmed finite element sequences)
.

A detailed comparison between the number of degrees of freedom for the DDR and classical trimmed finite element sequences for
n=3
has been made in
[
37
, Table 3]
.
This comparison shows that the DDR complex without serendipity reduction has slightly more degrees of freedom than trimmed finite elements on tetrahedra, but fewer on hexahedra.
This table also shows that the difference on tetrahedra can be slimmed down (and the advantage on hexahedra increased) using serendipity to reduce face and element degrees of freedom.

3.1.2

Remark 12

(Space of DDR potentials)
.

The space of DDR reconstructed potentials, that is,

\{(P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{h})_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h}),\,d\in[k,n]}\,:\,\underline{\omega}_{h}\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}\}

3.1.5
Discrete
L^{2}
-products

Using the potentials built in Section
3.1.3
, we can define, for all
k\in[0,n]
, an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{k,h}:\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}\times\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
that induces an
L^{2}
-structure on
\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}
.
Specifically, we set:
For all
(\underline{\omega}_{h},\underline{\mu}_{h})\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}\times\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}
,

\begin{gathered}(\underline{\omega}_{h},\underline{\mu}_{h})_{k,h}\coloneq\sum_{f\in\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}(\underline{\omega}_{f},\underline{\mu}_{f})_{k,f}\\
\text{
with
$(\underline{\omega}_{f},\underline{\mu}_{f})_{k,f}\coloneq\int_{f}P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge\star P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\mu}_{f}+s_{k,f}(\underline{\omega}_{f},\underline{\mu}_{f})$
for all $f\in\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h})$,
}\end{gathered}

(3.15)

where
s_{k,f}:\underline{X}_{r,f}^{k}\times\underline{X}_{r,f}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
is the stabilisation bilinear form such that

s_{k,f}(\underline{\omega}_{f},\underline{\mu}_{f})\\
=\sum_{d^{\prime}=k}^{n-1}h_{f}^{n-d^{\prime}}\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d^{\prime}}(f)}\int_{f^{\prime}}(\tr_{f^{\prime}}P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}-P_{r,f^{\prime}}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f^{\prime}})\wedge\star(\tr_{f^{\prime}}P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\mu}_{f}-P_{r,f^{\prime}}^{k}\underline{\mu}_{f^{\prime}}),

denoting the diameter of
f
.
The first term in the right-hand side of

is responsible for consistency, while the second one ensures the positivity and definiteness of this bilinear form (required for
(\cdot,\cdot)_{k,h}
to define an inner product).
More specifically, by Theorem
15
and Remark
16
it holds, for all

,

(\underline{I}_{r,f}^{k}\omega,\underline{\mu}_{f})_{k,f}=\int_{f}\omega\wedge\star P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\mu}_{f}\qquad\forall\omega\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{k}(f)\,,\;\forall\underline{\mu}_{f}\in\underline{X}_{r,f}^{k}.

(3.16)

Additionally, by (
3.37
) below, the mapping
\underline{X}_{r,f}^{k}\ni\underline{\omega}_{f}\mapsto\|\underline{\omega}_{f}\|_{k,f}\coloneq(\underline{\omega}_{f},\underline{\omega}_{f})_{k,f}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\in\mathbb{R}
defines a norm on
\underline{X}_{r,f}^{k}
.
Numerical schemes for linear PDEs related to the de Rham complex are typically obtained replacing continuous spaces and
L^{2}
-products with their discrete counterparts, according to the principles illustrated in the next section; see also
[

.

3.2
Application to the Hodge Laplacian

In this section we write a DDR scheme for the Hodge Laplacian and use this model problem to showcase the relevant properties for the analysis.

3.2.1
DDR scheme

In view of Remark
21
below, to keep the exposition as simple as possible, we assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has trivial topology, so that the spaces of harmonic forms are trivial.
Given a form degree
k\geq 0
and a form
g\in\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)
smooth enough, we focus on the following mixed formulation:
Find
(\sigma,u)\in H\Lambda^{k-1}(\Omega)\times H\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle_{k-1}-\langle u,\mathrm{d}^{k-1}\tau\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\tau\in H\Lambda^{k-1}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{d}^{k-1}\sigma,v\rangle_{k}+\langle\mathrm{d}^{k}u,\mathrm{d}^{k}v\rangle_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\langle g,v\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle\forall v\in H\Lambda^{k}(\Omega),

where
\langle\omega,\mu\rangle_{\ell}\coloneq\int_{\Omega}\omega\wedge\star\mu
denotes the
L^{2}
-product of
\ell
-forms.
Let a polynomial degree
r\geq 0
be fixed.
Assuming
g
smooth enough for
\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k}g
to be well-defined, the DDR scheme is obtained with obvious substitutions, and reads:
Find
(\underline{\sigma}_{h},\underline{u}_{h})\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k-1}\times\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle(\underline{\sigma}_{h},\underline{\tau}_{h})_{k-1,h}-(\underline{u}_{h},\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\tau}_{h})_{k,h}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\underline{\tau}_{h}\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k-1},

\displaystyle(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\sigma}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})_{k,h}+(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}\underline{u}_{h},\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}\underline{v}_{h})_{k+1,h}

\displaystyle=(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k}g,\underline{v}_{h})_{k,h}

\displaystyle\forall\underline{v}_{h}\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k},

or, equivalently,

\mathcal{A}_{h}((\underline{\sigma}_{h},\underline{u}_{h}),(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{v}_{h}))=(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k}g,\underline{v}_{h})_{k,h}\qquad\forall(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k-1}\times\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k},

(3.18)

where the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}_{h}:\big[\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k-1}\times\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}\big]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that

\mathcal{A}_{h}((\underline{\sigma}_{h},\underline{u}_{h}),(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{v}_{h}))\\
\coloneq(\underline{\sigma}_{h},\underline{\tau}_{h})_{k-1,h}-(\underline{u}_{h},\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\tau}_{h})_{k,h}+(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\sigma}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})_{k,h}+(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}\underline{u}_{h},\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}\underline{v}_{h})_{k+1,h}.

(3.19)

Remark 20

(Regularity requirement on

)

s=r+1
) and the commutation property (
3.40
) below yield the following estimate:

\|\varepsilon_{\pi}\|_{L^{2}\Lambda^{k-1}(\Omega)}+\|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d},h}\|_{L^{2}\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)}+\|e_{\pi}\|_{L^{2}\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)}+\|e_{\mathrm{d},\pi}\|_{L^{2}\Lambda^{k+1}(\Omega)}\\
+|\hat{\underline{\sigma}}_{h}|_{k-1,h}+|\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\hat{\underline{\sigma}}_{h}|_{k,h}+|\hat{\underline{u}}_{h}|_{k,h}+|\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\hat{\underline{u}}_{h}|_{k+1,h}\lesssim h^{r+1},

(3.22)

where, for all
\ell\geq 0
, we introduced the stabilisation seminorm

|{\cdot}|_{\ell,h}\coloneq s_{\ell,h}(\cdot,\cdot)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Notice that the terms involving this seminorm can be interpreted as a measure of the “jumps” between the traces of the potential reconstruction in the highest-dimensional cells and the potential reconstructions on their subcells.

Bounding the discrete components
(\underline{\varepsilon}_{h},\underline{e}_{h})
of the error requires the following adjoint consistency results that, for
n=3
, follow from the analysis done in
[
36
, Section 6.2]
using vector proxies.
Defining, for any form degree
\ell\geq 0
, any
\omega\in\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\Omega)
smooth enough for the interpolators to make sense, and any
\underline{\mu}_{h}\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{\ell}
,

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r,h}^{\ell}(\omega;\underline{\mu}_{h})\coloneq(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{\ell}\delta\omega,\underline{\mu}_{h})_{\ell,h}-(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{\ell+1}\omega,\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{\ell}\underline{\mu}_{h})_{\ell+1,h},

(3.23)

(where
\delta
is the co-differential) it holds, under additional piecewise regularity assumptions on
\omega
,

|\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r,h}^{\ell}(\omega;\underline{\mu}_{h})|\lesssim h^{r+1}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\underline{\mu}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{\ell,h}\qquad\forall\underline{\mu}_{h}\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{\ell}.

(3.24)

Then, letting, for all
(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{\mu}_{h})\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k-1}\times\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}
,

\mathcal{E}_{h}(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})\coloneq(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k}g,\underline{v}_{h})-\mathcal{A}_{h}((\hat{\underline{\sigma}}_{h},\hat{\underline{u}}_{h}),(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})),

(3.25)

we write

|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|(\underline{\varepsilon}_{h},\underline{e}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{h}\overset{\eqref{eq:inf-sup}}{\lesssim}\sup_{(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k-1}\times\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{h}((\underline{\varepsilon}_{h},\underline{e}_{h}),(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{v}_{h}))}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{h}}\overset{\eqref{eq:discrete},\,\eqref{eq:Eh}}{=}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathcal{E}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{h,*}.

(3.26)

with
|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|{\cdot}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{h,*}
denoting the norm dual to
|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|{\cdot}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{h}
.
Recalling that
g=\mathrm{d}\sigma+\delta\mathrm{d}u
almost everywhere and expanding the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}_{h}
according to its definition (
3.19
), we next observe that, for all
(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{\mu}_{h})\in\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k-1}\times\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}
, and provided
\mathrm{d}\sigma
and
\delta\mathrm{d}u
are smooth enough for their interpolation to make sense,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{h}(\underline{\tau}_{h},\underline{\mu}_{h})

\displaystyle=\cancel{(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k}\mathrm{d}\sigma,\underline{v}_{h})_{k,h}}+(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k}\delta\mathrm{d}u,\underline{v}_{h})_{k,h}

(3.27)

\displaystyle-(\hat{\underline{\sigma}}_{h},\underline{\tau}_{h})_{k-1,h}+(\hat{\underline{u}}_{h},\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\tau}_{h})_{k,h}

\displaystyle-\cancel{(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\hat{\underline{\sigma}}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})_{k,h}}-(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}\hat{\underline{u}}_{h},\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}\underline{v}_{h})_{k+1,h}

\displaystyle=(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k}\delta\mathrm{d}u,\underline{v}_{h})_{k,h}-(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k+1}\mathrm{d}u,\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}\underline{v}_{h})_{k+1,h}

\displaystyle-(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k-1}\delta u,\underline{\tau}_{h})_{k-1,h}+(\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k}u,\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\tau}_{h})_{k,h}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:adjoint.error}}{}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r,h}^{k}(\mathrm{d}u;\underline{v}_{h})-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r,h}^{k-1}(u;\underline{\tau}_{h}),

where the cancellation in the first step is a consequence of the commutation property (
3.40
) below,
while, in the second step, we have used again this commutation property to write
\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}\hat{\underline{u}}_{h}=\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k+1}\mathrm{d}u
and
\hat{\underline{\sigma}}_{h}=\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k-1}\sigma=\underline{I}_{r,h}^{k-1}\delta u
.
Combining (
3.26
) and (
3.27
), and using the adjoint consistency (
3.24
), we finally get

|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|(\underline{\varepsilon}_{h},\underline{e}_{h})|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{h}\lesssim h^{r+1}.

(3.28)

Finally, using triangle inequalities, invoking (
3.22
) and (
3.28
), and using the
|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|{\cdot}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{h}
-boundedness of the
L^{2}
-norm of the potential (resulting from the defintion of this norm), we arrive at the following error estimate:

\|\sigma-P_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}\Lambda^{k-1}(\Omega)}+\|\mathrm{d}\sigma-\mathrm{d}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)}\\
+\|u-P_{r,h}^{k}\underline{u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)}+\|\mathrm{d}u-\mathrm{d}_{r,h}^{k}\underline{u}_{h}\|_{L^{2}\Lambda^{k+1}(\Omega)}\\
+|\underline{\sigma}_{h}|_{k-1,h}+|\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\sigma}_{h}|_{k,h}+|\underline{u}_{h}|_{k,h}+|\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{u}_{h}|_{k+1,h}\lesssim h^{r+1}.

Notice that, as in the finite element framework, improved error estimates for certain components of the above error can be obtained using the Aubin–Nitsche trick, which has been extended to the fully discrete in
[
33
, Section 2.3]
.
This topic will be explored in a future work.

\bullet

3.3
Complex property

We denote by
\Lambda^{k+1}(\partial f)
the space
\bigtimes_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)}\Lambda^{k+1}(f^{\prime})
, which can be intuitively understood as a space of piecewise
(k+1)
-forms on
\partial f
.
For all integers
d\in[k+2,n]
, the piecewise polynomial boundary exterior derivative
\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,\partial f}:\underline{X}_{r,\partial f}^{k}\to\Lambda^{k+1}(\partial f)
is defined such that
(\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,\partial f})_{|f^{\prime}}\coloneq\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f^{\prime}}
for all
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)
(
\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f^{\prime}}
being the discrete exterior derivative on the
(d-1)
-cell
f^{\prime}
defined by (
3.4
)).
The following lemma generalises the links between element gradients (resp., curls) and face gradients (resp., curls) proved in
[
36
, Propositions 1 and 4]
using vector proxies.

Lemma 22

(Link between discrete exterior derivatives on subcells)
.

It holds, for all
d\geq k+2
, all
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
, and all
\underline{\omega}_{f}\in\underline{X}_{r,f}^{k}
,

\int_{f}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge\mathrm{d}\alpha=(-1)^{k+1}\int_{\partial f}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,\partial f}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\alpha\qquad\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{d-k-2}(f).

(3.29)

Proof.

Take
\mu=\mathrm{d}\alpha\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k-1}(f)
in (
3.4
) and use
\mathrm{d}\circ\mathrm{d}=0
and
\tr_{\partial f}\mathrm{d}=\mathrm{d}\tr_{\partial f}
(since the trace is a pullback, it commutes with the exterior derivative) to get

\int_{f}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge\mathrm{d}\alpha=\int_{\partial f}P^{k}_{r,\partial f}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\mathrm{d}\tr_{\partial f}\alpha.

(3.30)

For each
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)
forming
\partial f
, by Lemma
4
we have
\tr_{f^{\prime}}\alpha\in\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{d-k-2}(f^{\prime})
so, by (
3.5
) applied to
f^{\prime}
instead of
f
with test function
(\mu,\nu)=(\tr_{f^{\prime}}\alpha,0)
(see Remark
10
), we have, additionally using the fact that
\tr_{\partial f^{\prime}}(\tr_{f^{\prime}}\alpha)=\tr_{\partial f^{\prime}}\alpha
,

(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f^{\prime}}P^{k}_{r,f^{\prime}}\underline{\omega}_{f^{\prime}}\wedge\mathrm{d}\tr_{f^{\prime}}\alpha=\int_{f^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f^{\prime}}\underline{\omega}_{f^{\prime}}\wedge\tr_{f^{\prime}}\alpha-\int_{\partial f^{\prime}}P^{k}_{r,\partial f^{\prime}}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f^{\prime}}\wedge\tr_{\partial f^{\prime}}\alpha.

Multiplying the first term in the right-hand side by the relative orientation
\varepsilon_{f\!f^{\prime}}
and summing over
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)
, we have

\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)}\varepsilon_{f\!f^{\prime}}\int_{f^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f^{\prime}}\underline{\omega}_{f^{\prime}}\wedge\tr_{f^{\prime}}\alpha\overset{\eqref{eq:integral.relative.orientation}}{=}\int_{\partial f}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,\partial f}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\alpha.

Proceeding similarly for the second term in the right-hand side, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)}\varepsilon_{f\!f^{\prime}}\int_{\partial f^{\prime}}P^{k}_{r,\partial f^{\prime}}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f^{\prime}}\wedge\tr_{\partial f^{\prime}}\alpha

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \overset{\eqref{eq:integral.relative.orientation}}{}&=\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)}\varepsilon_{f\!f^{\prime}}\Bigg(\sum_{f^{\prime\prime}\in\Delta_{d-2}(f^{\prime})}\varepsilon_{f^{\prime}\!f^{\prime\prime}}\int_{f^{\prime\prime}}P^{k}_{r,f^{\prime\prime}}\underline{\omega}_{f^{\prime\prime}}\wedge\tr_{f^{\prime\prime}}\alpha\Bigg)\\
&=\sum_{f^{\prime\prime}\in\Delta_{d-2}(f)}\Bigg(\underbrace{\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)\text{ s.t. }f^{\prime\prime}\in\Delta_{d-2}(f^{\prime})}\varepsilon_{f\!f^{\prime}}\varepsilon_{f^{\prime}\!f^{\prime\prime}}}_{=0}\Bigg)\int_{f^{\prime\prime}}P^{k}_{r,f^{\prime\prime}}\underline{\omega}_{f^{\prime\prime}}\wedge\tr_{f^{\prime\prime}}\alpha=0.\end{aligned}

Thus,
\int_{\partial f}P^{k}_{r,\partial f}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\mathrm{d}\tr_{\partial f}\alpha=(-1)^{k+1}\int_{\partial f}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,\partial f}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\alpha
which, plugged into (
3.30
), gives the desired result.
∎

Theorem 23

(Link between discrete potentials and exterior derivatives, complex property)
.

It holds, for all integers
k\in[1,n]
and
d\geq k
, all
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
, and all
\underline{\omega}_{f}\in\underline{X}_{r,f}^{k-1}
,

P^{k}_{r,f}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})=\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f},

(3.31)

and, if
d\geq k+1
,

\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})=0.

(3.32)

As a consequence, the sequence (
3.9
) defines a complex.

Proof.

The proof is done by induction on
\rho\coloneq d-k
.

If
\rho=0
(i.e.,
d=k
), by the definitions (
3.3
) of the discrete potential and (
3.8
) of the global discrete exterior derivative with
k-1
instead of
k
, we have
P^{k}_{r,f}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})=\star^{-1}(\star\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})=\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f}
(notice that, in the first passage, we can omit the projector in front of
\star\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f}
since this quantity sits in
\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{0}(f)=\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{0}(f)
, and is therefore left unchanged by
\pi_{r,f}^{-,0}
).
This proves (
3.31
), and the relation (
3.32
) is irrelevant here since
d=k
.

Let us now assume that (
3.31
) and (
3.32
) hold for a given
\rho\geq 0
, and let us consider
d
and
k
such that
d-k=\rho+1
.
We start by considering (
3.32
) (which we need to prove since
d\geq k+1
in the present case).
Let us take
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
. Applying (
3.4
) with
\underline{\mathrm{d}}^{k-1}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f}
instead of
\underline{\omega}_{f}
and a generic
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k-1}(f)
, we have, expanding the local discrete exterior derivative
\underline{\mathrm{d}}^{k-1}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f}
according to its definition (i.e., the restriction to
f
of (
3.8
) with
k-1
instead of
k
),

\int_{f}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}^{k-1}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f})\wedge\mu={}(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\star^{-1}(\pi_{r,f}^{-,d-k}(\star\mathrm{d}^{k-1}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f}))\wedge\mathrm{d}\mu\\
+\int_{\partial f}P^{k}_{r,\partial f}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}^{k-1}_{r,\partial f}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f})\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\mu.

(3.33)

By the induction hypothesis, (
3.31
) holds on each
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)
(since
(d-1)-k=\rho
), and thus

P^{k}_{r,\partial f}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}^{k-1}_{r,\partial f}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f})=\mathrm{d}_{r,\partial f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}.

(3.34)

Invoking then (
2.4
) with
(\mathcal{X},\omega,\mu)\leftarrow(\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{d-k}(f),\mathrm{d}^{k-1}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f},\mathrm{d}\mu)
, noticing that
\mathrm{d}\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{r-1}\Lambda^{d-k}(f)\subset\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{d-k}(f)
(by (
2.14
) with
\ell=d-k
) to handle the first term in the right-hand side of (
3.33
), we infer

\int_{f}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}^{k-1}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f})\wedge\mu=(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\mathrm{d}^{k-1}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\partial f}\mathrm{d}^{k-1}_{r,\partial f}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\mu=0,

(3.35)

where the conclusion follows from the link (
3.29
) between discrete exterior derivatives on subcells applied with
k-1
instead of
k
and
\alpha=\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k-1}(f)\subset\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{d-(k-1)-2}(f)
.
Since
\mu
is arbitrary in
\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k-1}(f)
, (
3.35
) proves (
3.32
).

We next prove (
3.31
).
For any
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{d-k-1}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r}^{d-k}(f)
, the definition (
3.5
) of the potential applied to
\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f}
gives

(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}P^{k}_{r,f}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})\wedge(\mathrm{d}\mu+\nu)=\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})\wedge\mu\\
-\int_{\partial f}P_{r,\partial f}^{k}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,\partial f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f})\wedge\tr_{\partial f}{\mu}+(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\star^{-1}\pi_{r,f}^{-,d-k}(\star\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})\wedge\nu,

where we have additionally used, in the last term, the definition of the local discrete exterior derivative
\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f}
, corresponding to the restriction to
f
of (
3.8
) with
k-1
instead of
k
.
Using the complex property (
3.32
) that we have just proved, we have
\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})=0
.
Moreover, the induction hypothesis (
3.34
) yields
P_{r,\partial f}^{k}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,\partial f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f})=\mathrm{d}_{r,\partial f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}
.
Hence, invoking (
3.29
) with
k-1
instead of
k
and
\alpha=\mu
(notice that
\mu\in\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{d-k-1}(f)\subset\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{d-(k-1)-2}(f)
by (
2.12
)) and applying (
2.4
) with
(\mathcal{X},\omega,\mu)\leftarrow(\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{d-k}(f),\mathrm{d}^{k-1}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f},\nu)
, which is valid since
\nu\in\mathcal{K}_{r}^{d-k}(f)\subset\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{d-k}(f)
by (
2.12b
) with
\ell=d-k\geq 1
, we obtain

(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}P^{k}_{r,f}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})\wedge(\mathrm{d}\mu+\nu)\\
=-(-1)^{k}\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge\mathrm{d}\mu+(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge\nu.

Simplifying by
(-1)^{k+1}
and recalling the isomorphism (
2.9
) concludes the proof of (
3.31
).
∎

3.4
Commutation

The following lemma shows that the reconstructed potential
P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}
on a
d
-cell
f
is built by adding a high-order enhancement to
\star^{-1}\omega_{f}
; this enhancement is designed to obtain a polynomial consistency unachievable by the component alone (see (
3.10
)).

Lemma 24

(Links between component and potential reconstruction)
.

For all integers
d\in[0,n]
and
k\leq d
, if
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
and
\underline{\omega}_{f}\in\underline{X}_{r,f}^{k}
, then it holds

(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge(\mathrm{d}\mu+\nu)=(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\star^{-1}\omega_{f}\wedge(\mathrm{d}(\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu)+\nu)\\
+\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge(\mu-\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu)-\int_{\partial f}P_{r,\partial f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}{(\mu-\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu)}\\
\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{d-k-1}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r}^{d-k}(f).

(3.36)

As a consequence,

\pi_{r,f}^{-,d-k}(\star P^{k}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f})=\omega_{f}.

(3.37)

Proof.

If
d=k
, the relation (
3.36
) follows from
\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{d-k-1}(f)=\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{-1}(f)=\{0\}
and
P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}=\star^{-1}\omega_{f}
(see (
3.3
)), which also establishes (
3.37
) since
\pi_{r,f}^{-,0}={\rm Id}
on
\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{0}(f)=\mathcal{P}_{r}^{-}\Lambda^{0}(f)
.

Consider now
d\geq k+1
and take
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{d-k-1}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r}^{d-k}(f)
.
Inserting
\pm\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu
into the definition (
3.5
) of
P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}
we have

\displaystyle(-1)^{k+1}{}

\displaystyle\int_{f}P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge(\mathrm{d}\mu+\nu)

(3.38)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu+\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge(\mu-\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu)-\int_{\partial f}P_{r,\partial f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}{\mu}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\star^{-1}\omega_{f}\wedge\nu.

On the other hand, the definition (
3.4
) of
\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}
applied to
\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu
yields

\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu\\
=(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\star^{-1}\omega_{f}\wedge\mathrm{d}(\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu)+\int_{\partial f}P_{r,\partial f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}(\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu).

Substituting this relation into (
3.38
) yields (
3.36
).

To prove (
3.37
) we apply (
3.36
) with
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{K}_{r}^{d-k-1}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r}^{d-k}(f)
and notice that
\mu=\pi_{r,f}^{d-k-1}\mu
since
\mathcal{K}_{r}^{d-k-1}(f)\subset\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k-1}(f)
, to get

\int_{f}P^{k}_{r,f}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge(\mathrm{d}\mu+\nu)=\int_{f}\star^{-1}\omega_{f}\wedge(\mathrm{d}\mu+\nu).

(3.39)

The isomorphism (
2.17
) with
\ell=d-k\geq 1
shows that

3.6
Cohomology

A strategy to establish the exactness of the de Rham complex (for a domain with trivial topology) is to design a Poincaré operator
p:C^{1}\Lambda^{k}(\overline{\Omega})\to C^{1}\Lambda^{k-1}(\overline{\Omega})
, that satisfies
\mathrm{d}p+p\mathrm{d}={\rm Id}
.
The Poincaré operator is built integrating a certain flow of contracted differential forms; see
[
29
,
57
]
for details and applications to the design of finite element complexes. Extending such a construction to the context of fully discrete spaces is not trivial, as it is not clear how the discrete polynomial components on cells should evolve with such a flow. We therefore select an alternative approach, more suited to hierarchical discrete spaces.

The starting point is the following idea: if
\eta\in C^{1}\Lambda^{k}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies
\mathrm{d}\eta=0
and we have
\omega\in C^{2}\Lambda^{k-1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\mathrm{d}\omega=\eta
, then (
2.1
) shows that, for any
d
-cell
f
,

(-1)^{k}\int_{f}\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{f}\eta\wedge\mu-\int_{\partial f}\tr_{\partial f}\omega\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\mu\qquad\forall\mu\in C^{1}\Lambda^{d-k}(\overline{\Omega}).

(3.44)

In the discrete setting,
\omega
is built starting from the lowest-dimensional cells, and (
3.44
) thus gives a condition on
\omega
over
f
based on the already constructed
\tr_{\partial f}\omega
. To start this process, we must fix the values of
\omega
on the lowest-dimensional cells, which is not an easy task in general. Actually, from the point of view of differential forms, the lowest-dimensional cells encode the topology of the domain, and thus the cohomology of the complex; for a generic
\eta
, the recursive construction of
\omega
can therefore only be fully complete if the complex is exact, and thus the topology trivial.

This limitation is circumvented by using the following idea: if
\eta
has zero average on
k
-cells, then
\omega
can be set to zero on
(k-1)
-cells, which completes the construction above (see Lemma
27
below). This result is then exploited, through the extension/reduction strategy developed in
[
37
,
38
]
, to compare the cohomology of the arbitrary-order
\mathrm{DDR}(r)
complex to that of the lowest-order
\mathrm{DDR}(0)
complex, which is trivially isomorphic to the CW complex based on the mesh.

We therefore start by considering the subspace
\underline{X}_{r,h,\flat}^{k}
of
\underline{X}_{r,h}^{k}
made of vectors of differential forms whose integrals over cells of dimension
d=k
vanish:

\underline{X}_{r,h,\flat}^{k}\coloneq\left\{\underline{\omega}_{h}=(\omega_{f})_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h}),\,d\in[k,n]}\,:\,\int_{f}\star^{-1}\omega_{f}=0\quad\forall f\in\Delta_{k}(\mathcal{M}_{h})\right\}.

4
A VEM-inspired complex

In this section we consider an alternative construction inspired by the Virtual Element complex of
[
8
]
.
This complex hinges on Koszul complements, unlike the one of
[
10
]
, which was based on orthogonal complements (as noticed in
[
36
]
, the latter are less natural to prove analytical properties).
Notice that we make here no effort to reduce the polynomial degree of certain components of the discrete spaces, which is known to be possible; see, e.g.,
[
9
]
and also
[
37
]
for a general framework with application to DDR methods.
Notice also that we work in a fully discrete spirit, without attempting to identify the underlying virtual spaces (which are not needed for the purposes of the present work).

Let again a polynomial degree
r\geq 0
be fixed.
The general principle to design the VEM-inspired sequence is to select polynomial components that make it possible to reconstruct, for each
d
-cell and inductively on the dimension
d
, a discrete potential capable of reproducing polynomial forms in
\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{k}(f)
.
The main difference with respect to the DDR approach illustrated in Section
3
is that, with the exception of
(k+1)
-cells, the required information on the discrete exterior derivative is directly encoded in the discrete spaces.

Adopting this approach has several, far-reaching, consequences.
The first one is that the discrete spaces contain a mix of both traces and exterior derivatives (which, in passing, requires higher regularity on the domains of the interpolators).
The components on
k
- and
(k+1)
-cells in the discrete space of
k
-forms play a slightly different role than the others (and are, as a result, treated separately in the definition of the space).
The second consequence is that the proofs of key properties (polynomial consistency, cohomology, etc.) are carried out by induction on the dimension (and not on the difference between the dimension and the form degree, as in Theorems
15
and
23
).
This leads to somewhat simpler arguments, at the cost of larger discrete spaces.
Also, the commutation property is essentially obtained by definition of the local discrete exterior derivative (with the exception of lowest-dimensional cells).

4.1
Definition

4.1.1
Discrete spaces

We define the following discrete counterpart of
H\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)
,
0\leq k\leq n
:

\underline{V}_{r,h}^{k}\coloneq\bigtimes_{f\in\Delta_{k}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{0}(f)\times\bigtimes_{f\in\Delta_{k+1}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{1}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r}^{0}(f)\right)\\
\times\bigtimes_{d=k+2}^{n}\bigtimes_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}\left(\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{d-k}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{d-k-1}(f)\right).

(4.1)

Notice that, on
(k+1)
-cells, the second component has polynomial degree reduced by one compared to

4.1.3
Global discrete exterior derivative and VEM complex

For all
f\in\Delta_{k+1}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
, we define the
discrete exterior derivative

\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}:\underline{V}_{r,f}^{k}\to\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{k+1}(f)
such that, for all
\underline{\omega}_{f}\in\underline{V}_{r,f}^{k}
,

\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge(\mu+\nu)=\int_{\partial f}\star^{-1}\omega_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\mu+\int_{f}\star^{-1}D_{\omega,f}\wedge\nu\\
\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\Lambda^{0}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r}^{0}(f),

(4.4)

where, as before,
\omega_{\partial f}
is defined by
(\omega_{\partial f})_{|f^{\prime}}=\omega_{f^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{0}(f^{\prime})
for all
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{k}(f)
.
Notice that the above equation defines
\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}
uniquely since, by (

),
\mu+\nu
spans
\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{0}(f)
as
(\mu,\nu)
spans
\mathcal{P}_{0}\Lambda^{0}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r}^{0}(f)
.
Moreover, taking
\mu=0
and letting
\nu
span
\mathcal{K}_{r}^{0}(f)
, we infer, using (
2.4
) with
(\mathcal{X},\omega,\mu)\leftarrow(\mathcal{K}_{r}^{0}(f),\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f},\nu)
,

D_{\omega,f}=\pi_{r,f}^{\mathcal{K},0}(\star\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f})\qquad\forall f\in\Delta_{k+1}(\mathcal{M}_{h}).

(4.5)

Unlike the DDR complex, the construction of a
global discrete exterior derivative
for the VEM complex does not require to first reconstruct traces on lower-dimensional cells, as all the necessary information is encoded in the polynomial components
(D_{\omega,f})_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h}),\,d\in[k+2,n]}

supplemented by
(\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f})_{f\in\Delta_{k+1}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}
.

, we let
\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}:\underline{V}_{r,h}^{k}\to\underline{V}_{r,h}^{k+1}
be such that, for all

\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{h}\coloneq\big((\star\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f})_{f\in\Delta_{k+1}(\mathcal{M}_{h})},(D_{\omega,f},0)_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h}),\,d\in[k+2,n]}\big)

(4.6)

(compare with (

) and notice the different positioning, compared to

, of the polynomial components

).

\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k}
the restriction of

to

with

such that

.

4.1.4
Local discrete potentials and discrete exterior derivatives

Given a form degree
k\in[0,n]
, for all
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
,
k\leq d\leq n
, we define the
local discrete potential

P_{r,f}^{k}:\underline{V}_{r}^{k}(f)\to\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{k}(f)
by induction on
d
as follows:
For all
\underline{\omega}_{f}\in\underline{V}_{r,f}^{k}
,

\bullet

If
d=k
, we simply set

P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\coloneq\star^{-1}\omega_{f}\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d}(f)=\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{d}(f),

(4.8)

where the last equality follows from (
2.13
) if
d=0
(after noticing that
\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d}(f)\cong\mathbb{R}\cong\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{d}(f)
) and from (
2.15
) if
d\geq 1
;

\bullet

If
k+1\leq d\leq n
, using the isomorphism (
2.17
) with
\ell=d-k\geq 1
and
r
replaced by
r+1
, we define
P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\in\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{k}(f)
as the unique solution of the following equation:

(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge(\mathrm{d}\mu+\nu)\\
=\int_{f}\star^{-1}\widetilde{D}_{\omega,f}\wedge\mu-\int_{\partial f}P_{r,\partial f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\mu+(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\star^{-1}\omega_{f}\wedge\nu\\
\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{d-k-1}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r+1}^{d-k}(f),

(4.9)

where

\widetilde{D}_{\omega,f}\coloneq\begin{cases}\star\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}&\text{if $d=k+1$,}\\
D_{\omega,f}&\text{if $d\geq k+2$,}\end{cases}

(4.10)

and we have introduced the piecewise polynomial boundary potential
P_{r,\partial f}^{k}:\underline{V}_{r,\partial f}^{k}\to\Lambda^{k}(\partial f)
such that
(P_{r,\partial f}^{k})_{|f^{\prime}}\coloneq P_{r,f^{\prime}}^{k}
for all
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)
.

Leveraging the above-defined discrete potentials, we can define the
discrete exterior derivative

\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}:\underline{V}_{r,f}^{k}\to\mathcal{P}_{r+1}^{-}\Lambda^{k+1}(f)
for all
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
,
k+2\leq d\leq n-1
, setting:

\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\coloneq P_{r,f}^{k+1}\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\qquad\forall\underline{\omega}_{f}\in\underline{V}_{r,f}^{k}.

(4.11)

These discrete exterior derivatives, which were previously only defined for
d=k+1
(see (
4.4
)), are not relevant in the definition of the VEM complex, but may be useful in practical applications.

4.3
Complex property

Lemma 32

(Complex property)
.

The sequence (
4.7
) defines a complex, i.e., for all integers
k\in[1,n-1]
and all
\underline{\omega}_{h}\in\underline{V}_{r,h}^{k-1}
,

\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{h})=\underline{0}.

Proof.

Applying the definition (
4.6
) of the global discrete exterior derivative for
k-1
, we obtain

\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{h}=\left((\star\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})_{f\in\Delta_{k}(\mathcal{M}_{h})},(D_{\omega,f},0)_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h}),\,d\in[k+1,n]}\right)\in\underline{V}_{r,h}^{k},

(4.14)

which shows that, for all
d\in[k+1,n]
and all
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
, the exterior derivative components of
\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{h}
are zero, and thus that

\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{h})=\left(\big(\star\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})\big)_{f\in\Delta_{k+1}(\mathcal{M}_{h})},(0,0)_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h}),\,d\in[k+2,n]}\right)\in\underline{V}_{r,h}^{k+1}.

The assertion is therefore proved if we show that
\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})=0
for all
f\in\Delta_{k+1}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
.
Applying the definition of the local discrete exterior derivative (see (
4.4
)) with
\underline{\omega}_{f}
replaced by
\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f}
obtained by restricting (
4.14
) to
f
, we get:
For all
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\Lambda^{0}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r}^{0}(f)
,

\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}(\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f})\wedge(\mu+\nu)=\int_{\partial f}\mathrm{d}_{r,\partial f}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\mu=0,

where the conclusion follows using the definition (
4.4
) of
\mathrm{d}_{r,f^{\prime}}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{f^{\prime}}
with
(\mu,\nu)\leftarrow(\tr_{f^{\prime}}\mu,0)
for all
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{k}(f)
and noticing, as at the end of the proof of Lemma
22
, that the sum over
f^{\prime}
of the integrals over
\partial f^{\prime}
is zero.
∎

(Commutation property for the discrete exterior derivative in dimension

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C^{0}\Lambda^{k+1}(\overline{f})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{V}_{r}^{k}(f)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{k+1}(f).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{d}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{I}_{r,f}^{k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\star^{-1}\pi_{r,f}^{0}\star}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}}

Proof.

Plugging the definition (
4.3
) of the interpolator into (
4.4
) we get, for all
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\Lambda^{0}(f)\times\mathcal{K}_{r}^{0}(f)

where

denotes the piecewise

-orthogonal projector obtained patching together the

,

) with

(since

Lemma 35

(Exactness property for

.

For all

, if

\underline{\omega}_{h}\in\underline{V}_{r,h,\flat}^{k-1}
such that
\underline{\eta}_{h}=\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k-1}\underline{\omega}_{h}
,
where, in accordance with the sequence (

\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{-1}=\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{n}\coloneq 0
.

Proof.

Recalling the definition (
4.6

5
Related works

We provide here some elements of comparison between the DDR and VEM constructions of Sections
3
and
4
, and two other families of discrete complexes.

5.1
Finite Element Exterior Calculus and Finite Element Systems

Finite Element Exterior Calculus (FEEC) is the (conforming) finite element approach for the unified analysis of discrete complexes
[
4
,
2
]
. It is based on the selection of piecewise polynomial subspaces of
H\Lambda(\Omega)
that form a subcomplex of the continuous complex (
1.2
). Finite Element Systems (FES) is a framework for designing subcomplexes that generalises FEEC to cover finite dimensional spaces spanned by differential forms that may not be piecewise polynomial on the selected mesh
[
31
,
29
]
. In FEEC complexes, only the spaces of differential forms in the continuous complex are replaced with discrete counterparts: the graded map that links these spaces is the usual exterior derivative
\mathrm{d}
.
Generalised FES is an abstract setting which also gives freedom on the definition of the graded maps in the complex
[
29
]
.

A (generalised) FES space is a space of
k
-forms on all
d
-cells with
d\geq k
, with a compatibility condition on the traces:

A^{k}(\mathcal{M}_{h})=\Bigg\{\underline{v}_{h}\in\!\!\bigtimes_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{$f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})$}}}{{\text{$d\in[k,n]$}}}}\!\!A^{k}(f)\,:\,\\
\text{$\tr_{f^{\prime}}v_{f}=v_{f^{\prime}}$ for all $(f,f^{\prime})\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})\times\Delta_{d^{\prime}}(f)$ with $k\leq d^{\prime}\leq d$}\Bigg\},

(5.1)

where each
A^{k}(f)
is a finite-dimensional space of
k
-forms and
v_{f}
denotes the component of
\underline{v}_{h}
on
f
.
In the original FES setting (in which the graded maps are
\mathrm{d}
), each element of
A^{k}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
can be identified with an element of
H\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)
.
This setting contains the usual FEEC complexes (in which case
A^{k}(f)
are certain polynomial subspaces – typically full polynomial spaces or trimmed polynomial spaces depending on the considered finite element), but has also been used to develop other discrete complexes, e.g. based on macro-elements (in which case
A^{k}(f)
is a space of piecewise polynomial forms on a subdivision of
f
) or with higher inter-element regularity (
C^{1}
spaces, for example).

The concept of (faithful)
mirror system
plays the role of degrees of freedom in the FES framework.
Mirror systems are constructed on a case-by-case basis for each FES, and are auxiliary tools in the framework: they are not required to design the FES spaces (or maps), but they identify (by duality) a basis of such spaces.
A mirror system for
A^{k}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
is a family of subspaces of linear forms:

Z^{k}(\mathcal{M}_{h})=\bigtimes_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{$f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})$}}}{{\text{$d\in[k,n]$}}}}Z^{k}(f)\qquad\text{ with $Z^{k}(f)\subset A^{k}(f)^{*}$ for all $f\in\Delta(\mathcal{M}_{h})$},

(5.2)

where
A^{k}(f)^{*}
is the dual space of
A^{k}(f)
(actually, to link mirror systems and interpolators, each
Z^{k}(f)
is chosen as a subspace of
\hat{X}^{k}(f)^{*}
with
\hat{X}^{k}(f)\supset A^{k}(f)
, but we won’t need this in the discussion here). As can be seen in (
5.2
), a mirror system is built hierarchically on the mesh, and each
Z^{k}(f)
identifies the modes of the FES forms that are “interior” to
f
; to obtain all the modes (interior and boundary) associated with
f
, one must consider
\bigtimes_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d^{\prime}}(f),\,d^{\prime}\in[k,d]}Z^{k}(f^{\prime})
.

A particular case of interest in the present context is when
Z^{k}(f)\subset L^{2}\Lambda^{k}(f)^{*}
(see Remark
9
). Using the Riesz representation theorem and applying the Hodge star transformation,
Z^{k}(f)
can then be identified with a family of subspaces of
L^{2}
-integrable
(d-k)
-forms:

Z^{k}(\mathcal{M}_{h})\cong\bigtimes_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{$f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})$}}}{{\text{$d\in[k,n]$}}}}\widetilde{Z}^{d-k}(f)\quad\text{ with $\widetilde{Z}^{d-k}(f)\subset L^{2}\Lambda^{d-k}(f)$}.

(5.3)

Here, and contrary to (
5.1
), no compatibility condition of the traces is imposed: the spaces
\widetilde{Z}^{d-k}(f)
are completely disconnected from each other.

The FEEC framework provides a setting for the
algebraic
and
analytical
study of discrete complexes based on piecewise polynomial subspaces of the continuous spaces; the constraint of having piecewise polynomial subspaces and of imposing the suitable inter-element continuities restricts the design of finite element methods to certain types of meshes – mostly tetrahedral and hexahedral. The FES framework is more general in the sense that it does not, in principle, require to identify conforming subspaces (or accepts conforming subspaces that are not piecewise polynomial on the chosen mesh – these spaces are then usually not explicitly known). Its main restriction, compared to FEEC, is that it only provides
algebraic
results on the discrete complexes, not analytical results such as Poincaré inequalities, or primal and adjoint consistencies – all critical for the numerical analysis of numerical schemes based on the complex. Moreover, as far as we could see in the literature, all complexes based on the FES framework and fully computable (that is, the spaces and operators are explicitly known) seem to rely on the design of discrete subcomplexes of the continuous complex, which imposes restrictions on the types of meshes that can be considered (as in FEEC, mostly tetrahedral and hexahedral meshes, with the graded map being the exterior derivative).
On the contrary, the DDR and VEM constructions of Sections
3
and
4
provide explicit and computable discrete complexes on generic polytopal meshes, that do not rely on finding computable conforming subspaces of the de Rham spaces.
These polytopal methods can be entirely built using spaces of polynomial functions on the mesh, without any compatibility condition on the traces. The spaces are explicit, their bases are directly given by the polynomial components, and the graded map (acting as a discrete exterior derivative) is explicitly expressed in terms of these components.

Comparing (
3.1
) and (
5.3
) for example, we see that the DDR space plays the role of a mirror system, and puts discrete polynomial components at the center of the construction. A similar approach is also true for the VEM-inspired spaces (
4.1
), with, contrary to DDR, some polynomial components representing exterior derivatives; see the definition (
4.3
) of the interpolator.

A closer link between DDR and FES can be drawn by noticing that the FES
[
28
, Section 2.1]
has the DDR spaces as mirror system (in the sense of (
5.3
)).
The spaces of this FES are based on liftings of harmonic functions on each cell, which cannot be explicitly described in general, and therefore cannot be directly used, say, in a weak formulation of a PDE to design a numerical scheme. This is in contrast with the fully discrete approach employed by the DDR technique, which not only identifies explicit discrete spaces and exterior derivatives, but also consistent
L^{2}
-inner products on these spaces, therefore providing all the tools required to build numerical schemes, see, e.g.
[
35
,
11
,
56
]
).
We also notice, in passing, that, in the context of vector proxies, different conforming (non-explicit) spaces having the DDR components as degrees of freedom were also identified in
[
11
, Section 6.2]
.

Finally, it can be shown, using the results of Section
3.3
and Lemma
27
, that the DDR complex fits into the generalised FES framework.
[
29
, Theorem 1]
then provides an alternative study of the cohomology of the DDR complex.
The approach developed in the proof of Theorem
14
provides a practical way to compute the cohomology spaces of the DDR complex based on those of the underlying CW complex (see
[
38
, Remark 13]
for details), for which efficient algorithms are available
[
48
]
.
Moreover, as mentioned above, the FES framework does not cover any analytical properties of the discrete complexes. In particular, for DDR, it only relies on the global discrete exterior derivative
\underline{\mathrm{d}}_{r,h}^{k}
defined in (
3.8
), and would not identify or make use of the local potential reconstructions
P_{r,f}^{k}
and discrete exterior derivatives
\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}
which encode the optimal consistency properties of the method (see Theorem
15
and (
3.16
)).

5.2
Distributional Differential Forms

The theory of Distributional Differential Forms (DDF) has been introduced in
[
58
]
as a generalisation of the construction in
[
20
]
for the a posteriori error analysis of Nédélec edge elements. DDF are built on triangulations of the domain and, using their relation with the underlying simplicial complexes (as well as the concept of double complexes), their cohomology was analysed in
[
58
]
for rather general boundary conditions.
Poincaré–Friedrichs inequalities were later established in
[
30
]
.

As is the case for the spaces appearing in the DDR and VEM complexes, DDF spaces are collections of differential forms on cells of various dimensions, with form degree depending on the dimension of the cell: if the domain
\Omega
has dimension
n
, the DDF space of degree
k
is made of
(k-n+d)
-forms on
d
-cells. No compatibility of the traces is enforced on these forms, which can be completely discontinuous between two
d
-simplices.
The discrete distributional exterior derivative on the DDF space is then composed of two contributions: the exterior derivative inside the simplices, and a trace term. For example, focusing on the highest dimension
d=n
, if the DDF space of
k
-forms is

\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{-2}(\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h}))=\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{-1}(\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h}))\oplus\hat{\Lambda}^{k-1}_{-1}(\Delta_{n-1}(\mathcal{M}_{h})),

(5.4)

(with
\hat{\Lambda}^{\ell}_{-1}
subspace of piecewise
C^{\infty}\Lambda^{\ell}
forms, the index
-1
expressing the absence of continuity properties at the interfaces), for a family
\omega_{n,h}=(\omega_{f})_{f\in\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{-1}(\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h}))
, we define the distributional derivative
\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{k}_{h}:\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{-1}(\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h}))\to\hat{\Lambda}^{k+1}_{-2}(\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h}))
by

\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{k}_{h}\omega_{n,h}=\left((\mathrm{d}^{k}\omega_{f})_{f\in\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h})},\left(-\sum_{f\in\Sigma_{n}(f^{\prime})}\varepsilon_{f\!f\textquoteright}\tr_{f^{\prime}}\omega_{f}\right)_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{n-1}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}\right),

(5.5)

where
\Sigma_{n}(f^{\prime})
is the set of
n
-simplices
f
that share
f^{\prime}
(that is,
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{n-1}(f)
), and
\varepsilon_{f\!f^{\prime}}
is the relative orientation of the simplex
f^{\prime}
with respect to the simplex
f
.
Note that, in (
5.5
), we have adopted a presentation of the distributional derivative that distributes its two contributions (
\mathrm{D}
and
\mathrm{T}
in
[
58
]
) on the corresponding components
(\hat{\Lambda}^{k+1-i}_{-1}(\Delta_{n-i}(\mathcal{M}_{h})))_{i=0,1}
of
\hat{\Lambda}^{k+1}_{-2}(\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h}))
(see (
5.4
) with
k+1
instead of
k
), instead of writing
\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{k}_{h}
as a sum of elements in the global space
\hat{\Lambda}^{k+1}_{-2}(\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h}))
; this is to better compare with the definition (
3.8
).
This definition of distributional derivative is a global one, obtained by testing the piecewise smooth form
\omega_{n,h}
against globally smooth forms, which classically results in a term inside each
f\in\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
corresponding to the standard exterior derivative (first component in (
5.5
)), and a jump across the
(n-1)
-sub-simplices based on the difference of traces on the two adjacent
n
-simplices (second component in (
5.5
)).

A crucial remark is that, in (
5.5
), the component
(\mathrm{d}^{k}\omega_{f})_{f\in\Delta_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}
of
\hat{d}_{h}^{k}\omega_{n,h}
on
n
-cells only depends on the values
\omega_{n,h}
of the discrete distributional differential form on
n
-cells, not on the values of these forms on lower-dimensional cells (e.g.,
\hat{\Lambda}^{k-1}_{-1}(\Delta_{n-1}(\mathcal{M}_{h}))
in (
5.4
)).
This is in contrast with the discrete exterior derivatives in DDR and VEM complexes, whose definition on higher-dimensional cells depends on polynomial components on their sub-cells; see (
3.4
) and (
4.6
). Another difference between DDR and DDF can be seen when recasting the discrete exterior derivative: integrating by parts (
3.4
) yields the following characterisation:

\int_{f}\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}\wedge\mu=-\int_{f}\mathrm{d}(\star^{-1}\omega_{f})\wedge\mu+\int_{\partial f}(P_{r,\partial f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}-\tr_{\partial f}(\star^{-1}\omega_{f}))\wedge\tr_{\partial f}{\mu}\\
\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k-1}(f).

This relation reveals that
\mathrm{d}_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}
is, as in DDF, composed of an exterior derivative term in the
d
-cell and a boundary term involving jumps. However, contrary to DDF, the jumps here are between the trace of the
d
-cell unknown and the potential
P_{r,\partial f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{\partial f}
reconstructed on
(d-1)
-cells (which depends on the unknowns on all
d^{\prime}
-subcells of
f
,
k\leq d^{\prime}\leq d
), not between traces of two
d
-cells unknowns (as in (
5.5
) with
d=n
).
In this respect, the “jump” term in DDR relates more to the kind of face differences encountered in polytopal methods (e.g., the HHO method
[
34
]
) while the jump term in DDF is more akin to those arising in discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods
[
41
]
.

This comparison can be extended to the potential reconstructions themselves. Equation (
3.36
) shows that
P_{r,f}^{k}\underline{\omega}_{f}
is obtained applying a higher-order enhancement to the cell component
\star^{-1}\omega_{f}
, designed from the discrete exterior derivative on
f
and the potentials on
\partial f
.
This enhancement ensures the high-order consistency of the method starting from lower-order polynomial unknowns. In the context of elliptic equations, it is commonly used in methods with unknowns in the elements and on the faces of the mesh, but it is not directly available in DG methods. In DDF, as in DG, the cell unknown itself must be used (e.g., in a scheme to discretise the source term), and the consistency is therefore limited by the degree of this unknown.

Appendix A
Differential forms and vector proxies

In this section, we briefly recall basic concepts on alternating (resp. differential) forms, and their representation in terms of vectors (resp. vector fields); these representations are often referred to as “vector proxies”.
We refer the reader to
[
2
, Chapter 6]
for a presentation in the framework of Finite Element Exterior Calculus, and to
[
15
]
,
[
49
, Chapter 1]
,
[
55
, Chapter 1]
for an introduction in more general scientific and engineering contexts.

A.1
Exterior algebra in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

A.1.1
Alternating forms

Let
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\}_{i\in[1,n]}
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, equipped with the standard inner product.
A basis for the space of linear forms over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., the dual space
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is given by
\{\mathrm{d}x^{i}\}_{i\in[1,n]}
,
with
\mathrm{d}x^{i}(\boldsymbol{e}_{j})\coloneq\delta^{i}_{j}
(Krönecker symbol), for all
(i,j)\in[1,n]^{2}
. The starting point of exterior calculus is to consider
alternating
multilinear forms, vanishing
whenever they are applied to a set of linearly dependent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any integer
k\geq 1
, the set of alternating
k
-linear forms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
; by convention, we set
{\rm Alt}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneq\mathbb{R}
.
We also note that
{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\prime}
and that
{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{0\}
if
k>n

(since families of
k>n
vectors are always linearly dependent).
It can be checked that
\dim{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})={n\choose k}
. In particular,
{\rm Alt}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the 1-dimensional space spanned by the determinant in the canonical basis
\mathrm{vol}
(called the volume form).

A.1.2
Exterior product

Given two alternating multilinear forms
\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{i}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mu\in{\rm Alt}^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, their
exterior product

\omega\wedge\mu\in{\rm Alt}^{i+j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined, for any vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{i+j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by

(\omega\wedge\mu)(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{i+j})\coloneq\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{i,j}}\text{sign}(\sigma)\,\omega(\boldsymbol{v}_{\sigma_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\sigma_{i}})\,\mu(\boldsymbol{v}_{\sigma_{i+1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{\sigma_{i+j}}),

where
\Sigma_{i,j}
is the set of all permutations
\sigma
of the
(i+j)
-tuple
(1,\ldots,i+j)
such that
\sigma_{1}<\cdots<\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{i+1}<\cdots<\sigma_{i+j}
. The exterior product satisfies the anticommutativity law

\omega\wedge\mu=(-1)^{ij}\mu\wedge\omega,

(A.1)

so that, in particular, we have

\mathrm{d}x^{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{i}=0
and
\mathrm{d}x^{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{j}=-\mathrm{d}x^{j}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{i}
.
With these definitions, for
k\in[1,n]
a basis of the space
{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
\{\mathrm{d}x^{\sigma_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\sigma_{k}}\}_{\sigma}
where
\sigma
spans all strictly increasing functions
[1,k]\to[1,n]
. Hence, any
\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be written

\omega=\sum_{1\leq\sigma_{1}<\cdots<\sigma_{k}\leq n}a_{\sigma}\ \mathrm{d}x^{\sigma_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\sigma_{k}},\quad a_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}.

(A.2)

A.1.3
Hodge star operator

The scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
induces a scalar product, denoted by
\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle
, on
{\rm Alt}^{n-k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
– namely, the scalar product for which the aforementioned basis
\{\mathrm{d}x^{\sigma_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\sigma_{n-k}}\}_{\sigma}
of
{\rm Alt}^{n-k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is orthonormal. The

Hodge star operator
is the unique linear mapping
\star:{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to{\rm Alt}^{n-k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that, for all
\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\langle\star\omega,\mu\rangle\mathrm{vol}=\omega\wedge\mu
for all
\mu\in{\rm Alt}^{n-k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
It can be checked that

\star(\mathrm{d}x^{\sigma_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\sigma_{k}})=\text{sign}(\sigma,\tau)(\mathrm{d}x^{\tau_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\tau_{n-k}}),

where
(\sigma,\tau)=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k},\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n-k})
is a permutation of
(1,\ldots,n)
such that
\sigma_{1}<\cdots<\sigma_{k}
and
\tau_{1}<\cdots<\tau_{n-k}
. From the above identity, one can infer that

\star(\star\omega)=(-1)^{k(n-k)}\omega\qquad\forall\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(A.3)

and, hence, that
\langle\star\omega,\star\mu\rangle=\langle\omega,\mu\rangle
, i.e.,
\star
is an isometry. Formula (
A.3
) justifies the definition (
2.2
) of
\star^{-1}
. The anticommutativity (
A.1
) of
\wedge
, the definition of
\star
, and the symmetry of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
then give

{\star^{-1}\omega}\wedge\mu=\mu\wedge\star\omega=\omega\wedge\star\mu\qquad\forall\omega,\mu\in{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(A.4)

Example 36

(Hodge star operator in two and three dimensions).

If
\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, i.e.,
\omega=a_{12}\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{2}+a_{13}\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{3}+a_{23}\ \mathrm{d}x^{2}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{3}
(see (
A.2
)), one obtains
\star\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with

\star\omega=a_{12}\ \mathrm{d}x^{3}-a_{13}\ \mathrm{d}x^{2}+a_{23}\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}.

If
\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, i.e.,
\omega=a_{1}\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}+a_{2}\ \mathrm{d}x^{2}
, then
\star\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

\star\omega=a_{1}\ \mathrm{d}x^{2}-a_{2}\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}.

A.1.4
Vector proxies for alternating forms

As already mentioned in Section
A.1.1
,
{\rm Alt}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathbb{R}
and
{\rm Alt}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\mathbb{R}
.
Using the Riesz representation theorem to identify
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can identify two more spaces of alternating forms:
{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
and, writing
\star{\rm Alt}^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})={\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
since
\star
is bijective,
{\rm Alt}^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Applied with
n=3
, and recalling the formula for Hodge star transformations of 2-forms in Remark
36
,
these identifications lead to considering a vector
\boldsymbol{v}=(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
as a
proxy
for both the alternating linear and bilinear forms

{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\ni\omega=a\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}+b\ \mathrm{d}x^{2}+c\ \mathrm{d}x^{3}\ \text{and}\ {\rm Alt}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\ni\mu=a\ \mathrm{d}x^{2}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{3}-b\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{3}+c\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{2}.

On the other hand, when
n=2
, the discussion above gives two possible ways to identify
{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})={\rm Alt}^{2-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This leads to associating
a\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}+b\ \mathrm{d}x^{2}=\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
either to the vector
\boldsymbol{v}=(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, or to its rotation by a right angle

\varrho_{-\pi/2}\boldsymbol{v}=(b,-a)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Based on the above identifications, when
n=3
, one can interpret the exterior product of two alternating multilinear forms
\omega\wedge\mu
in terms of vector proxies
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})
as follows:

\bullet

the vector product
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\ni(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})\mapsto\boldsymbol{w}\times\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

when
(\omega,\mu)\in{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\times{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
;

\bullet

the dot product
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\ni(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})\mapsto\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}
when
(\omega,\mu)\in{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\times{\rm Alt}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

On the other hand, if
n=2
and
\omega,\mu\in{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we can write
\omega\wedge\mu=(a\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}+b\ \mathrm{d}x^{2})\wedge(f\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}+g\ \mathrm{d}x^{2})=(ag-bf)\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{2}
. Considering the correspondences
\omega\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{w}=(a,b)
and
\mu\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{v}=(f,g)
, we obtain

\omega\wedge\mu=(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\varrho_{-\pi/2}\boldsymbol{v})\ \mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{2}.

(A.5)

A.1.5
Contraction and trace

For a given vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
contraction

\omega\lrcorner\boldsymbol{v}\in{\rm Alt}^{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of
\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\boldsymbol{v}
is defined, for any
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by

(\omega\lrcorner\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k-1})\coloneq\omega(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k-1}).

(A.6)

In terms of vector proxies, in the case where
n=3
, this contraction with
\boldsymbol{v}
corresponds to

\bullet

the scalar product
\mathbb{R}^{3}\ni\boldsymbol{w}\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}
when
\boldsymbol{w}\leftrightarrow\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
;

\bullet

the vector product
\mathbb{R}^{3}\ni\boldsymbol{w}\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
when
\boldsymbol{w}\leftrightarrow\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
;

\bullet

the multiplication of a real number
\mathbb{R}\ni w\mapsto w\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
when
w\leftrightarrow\omega\in{\rm Alt}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Let now
V\subset W
be finite dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\iota_{V}:V\hookrightarrow W
be the inclusion of
V
in
W
.
The
trace

{\tr_{V}}:{\rm Alt}^{k}(W)\to{\rm Alt}^{k}(V)
is the pullback under
\iota_{V}
: For any
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\in V
,

\tr_{V}\omega(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k})\coloneq\omega(\iota_{V}\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\iota_{V}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}).

(A.7)

The trace respects the exterior product, i.e.,
{\tr_{V}}(\omega\wedge\mu)={\tr_{V}}\omega\wedge{\tr_{V}}\mu
.

It is easy to see that, through the vector proxy of
{\rm Alt}^{1}
spaces,
{\tr_{V}}:{\rm Alt}^{1}(W)\to{\rm Alt}^{1}(V)
is the orthogonal projection
\pi_{V}:W\to V
of a vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in W
onto
V
.

Let us fix an integer
m\in[1,n]
and suppose that
\dim(W)=m
and
\dim(V)=m-1
, and that both spaces are oriented; let
\boldsymbol{n}_{V}
be the unit normal to
V
such that, given a positively oriented basis
(\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{m-1})
of
V
, the family
(\boldsymbol{n}_{V},\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{m-1})
forms a positively oriented basis of
W
. Then, an identification of the trace
{\tr_{V}}:{\rm Alt}^{m-1}(W)\to{\rm Alt}^{m-1}(V)
through vector proxies is the scalar product with the vector
\boldsymbol{n}_{V}
, that is,
W\ni\boldsymbol{w}\mapsto\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}
.

A.2.3
Koszul differential

Given

\kappa_{M}:\Lambda^{k}({M})\to\Lambda^{k-1}({M})

is defined pointwise over
{M}

\boldsymbol{x}\in{M}

(\kappa_{M}\omega)_{\boldsymbol{x}}\coloneq\omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}\lrcorner(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{M}).

Its interpretation in terms of vector fields proxy is then analogous to that of a contraction of an alternating multilinear form with a vector,

\mathbb{R}^{n}\ni\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

The terminology “differential” is legitimate, as
\kappa_{M}
satisfies the complex

(since any alternating form applied to the same vector twice vanishes).

\int_{M}\mathrm{d}\omega\wedge\mu=\int_{M}\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\partial{M}}\tr_{\partial{M}}\omega\wedge\tr_{\partial{M}}\mu\qquad\forall(\omega,\mu)\in\Lambda^{1}({M})\times\Lambda^{1}({M}).

(A.11)

Given the previous interpretations of the exterior derivative and product in terms of vector proxies, if
\omega\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{w}
and
\mu\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{v}
, then
\mathrm{d}\omega\wedge\mu\leftrightarrow\CURL\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}
and
\omega\wedge\mathrm{d}\mu\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\CURL\boldsymbol{v}
. This leads to the following integration by parts formula for the curl:

\int_{M}\CURL\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\int_{M}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\CURL\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\partial{M}}(\boldsymbol{n}\times(\boldsymbol{w}\times\boldsymbol{n}))\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{n}),

(A.12)

where
\boldsymbol{n}
is the outer unit normal vector field over
\partial{M}
.
For any fixed
\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial{M}
, we have
\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\times(\boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{x})\times\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x}))=\pi_{T_{\boldsymbol{x}}\partial{M}}\boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{x})
(here,
T_{\boldsymbol{x}}\partial{M}
is the tangent space of
\partial{M}
at
\boldsymbol{x}
),
whereas

\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})\times\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})=\varrho_{-\pi/2}(\pi_{T_{\boldsymbol{x}}\partial{M}}\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x}))
,
where the rotation is considered with respect to the orientation of the tangent plane given by
\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
The boundary terms of (
A.11
) and (
A.12
) therefore coincide, through the vector proxy for the exterior product of 1-forms in dimension 2 (see (
A.5
)).
