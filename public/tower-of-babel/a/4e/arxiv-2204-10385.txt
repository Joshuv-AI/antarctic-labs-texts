Lattice and Non-lattice Piercing of Axis-Parallel Rectangles: Exact Algorithms and a Separation Result
Adrian Dumitrescu Josef Tkadlec

Abstract
For a given family of shapes ℱ \mathcal{F} in the plane, we study what is the lowest possible density of a point set P P that pierces (“intersects”, “hits”) all translates of each shape in ℱ \mathcal{F} . For instance, if ℱ \mathcal{F} consists of two axis-parallel rectangles the best known piercing set, i.e., one with the lowest density, is a lattice: for certain families the known lattices are provably optimal whereas for other, those lattices are just the best piercing sets currently known. Given a finite family ℱ \mathcal{F} of axis-parallel rectangles, we present two algorithms for finding an optimal ℱ \mathcal{F} -piercing lattice. Both algorithms run in time polynomial in the number of rectangles and the maximum aspect ratio of the rectangles in the family. No prior algorithms were known for this problem. Then we prove that for every n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , there exist a family of n n axis-parallel rectangles for which the best piercing density achieved by a lattice is separated by a positive (constant) gap from the optimal piercing density for the respective family. Finally, we sharpen our separation result by running the first algorithm on a suitable instance, and show that the best lattice can be sometimes worse by 20 % 20\% than the optimal piercing set. Keywords : axis-parallel rectangle, piercing set, piercing lattice, canonical vector basis, periodic tiling, exact algorithm, approximation algorithm.

Introduction

Piercing and covering are interrelated ubiquitous topics in geometry.
Given a family of sets
\mathbb{E}
in the Euclidean space, a
piercing set
is a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

collectively intersecting every set in
\mathbb{E}
. If
\mathbb{E}
contains an infinite subfamily consisting of pairwise
disjoint members then clearly every piercing set for
\mathbb{E}
must be infinite as well.
Given a finite family of shapes
\mathcal{F}
in the plane, what is the lowest possible
density

of a point set
P
that pierces (“intersects”, “hits”) all translates of each shape in
\mathcal{F}
?
Regardless of what
\mathcal{F}
is, such a set must be infinite.
In this paper we consider this problem for families
\mathcal{F}
of axis-parallel rectangles where the goal is finding
a point set of minimum density
\pi_{T}(\mathcal{F})
that collectively pierces each translate of a rectangle in
\mathcal{F}
.
The problem was introduced in
[
4
]
, where an approximation algorithm and several other
results have been obtained for such families.

If
\mathcal{F}
consists of a single axis-parallel rectangle
R
, an optimal piercing set is a rectangular lattice
given by a tiling of the plane with copies of
R
(i.e., a covering of the plane by interior-disjoint
translates of
R
). If
\mathcal{F}
consists of two axis-parallel rectangles, the answer is unknown!
The best piercing set currently known in this setting, i.e., one with the lowest density, is a lattice:
for certain families of two rectangles, the known lattices are provably optimal whereas for others,
the answer remains elusive
[
4
]
. As such, the question posed above appears as a basic open problem.

The starting point of this paper is the observation that for certain families
\mathcal{F}
of
axis-parallel rectangles, there exist surprisingly sparse
\mathcal{F}
-piercing point sets
that are not lattices. For instance, for the family
\mathcal{F}=\{6\times 1,1\times 6,3\times 3\}

there exists a
periodic

\mathcal{F}
-piercing non-lattice set with the (optimal) density
1/6
;
see
Fig.

1
. The indicated piercing set is a subset of the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
so the verification of the piercing property can be done visually and is left to the reader.
Building up on the above observation we prove that no lattice with density
1/6
is
\mathcal{F}
-piercing, i.e.,
the sparsest lattice is sometimes strictly worse than the sparsest point set, i.e.,

\pi_{L}(\mathcal{F})>\pi(F)
. Moreover, we prove a separation result showing that
the sparsest lattice is sometimes significantly worse than the sparsest point set.
This last step requires a careful setup (see properties P1–P3 below).

Setup.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\}
be a family of axis-parallel rectangles
R_{i}=w_{i}\times h_{i}
.
Without loss of generality (after suitable scaling) we may assume that
(i) the minimum rectangle width and height are both
1
; i.e.,
\min w_{i}=1
, and
\min h_{i}=1
; and
(ii) no input rectangle is contained in another.
We further set
k=\max\{w,h\}
, where
w=\max\{w_{i}\}
and
h=\max\{h_{i}\}
,
i.e.,
k
is the maximum dimension (extent) of a rectangle in
\mathcal{F}
.

As a first step, we will show how to find a
canonical vector basis

[u,v]
for any lattice
\Lambda
;
this may require a reflection of the
x
-axis and a
90^{\circ}
rotation of the lattice and the rectangles.
Such a basis satisfies the following three properties
(we have opted for
b>0
since this would be the case for the integer lattice with
a,b,c,d=0,1,1,0
).
Consequently, all our algorithms work with or rely on the existence of such a basis.

P1:

if
u
is a shortest vector in
\Lambda
, then

u
has positive (or
\infty
) slope and
v
has negative (or
0
) slope, i.e.,
u=[a,b]
,
v=[c,-d]
with

a,b,c,d\geq 0
, and
b>0
.

P2:

1/2\leq|u|\leq|v|\leq\sqrt{\frac{7}{3}}\,\frac{A}{\lambda}
,
where
\lambda=|u|
and
A=ad+bc
is the area of a fundamental parallelogram of
\Lambda
.

P3:

the angle
\alpha
between
u
and
v
is between
45^{\circ}
and
135^{\circ}
.

Definitions and notations.

Given a point set
P
and a bounded domain
D
with area
\text{Area}(D)
,
we define the
density of
P
over
D
by
\delta(P,D)=\frac{|P\cap D|}{\text{Area}(D)}
.
In particular, the density of a point lattice is the reciprocal of the determinant
\det[u,v]
, where

[u,v]
is a vector basis of the lattice
[
1
, p. 2]
.

Outline.

In Section
3
we show the existence of a canonical vector basis (satisfying properties P1–P3).
For lattices satisfying P1–P3, in Section
4
we present a decision algorithm: given
a family
\mathcal{F}
and a canonical vector basis the decision algorithm determines if the corresponding lattice
is
\mathcal{F}
-piercing.
In Section

we present two algorithms for computing an optimal

-piercing lattice:
given a family
\mathcal{F}
, each of the two algorithms finds an optimal
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice.
The key for these algorithms is the fact that candidate lattices must be tight (in a sense that will be made precise).
Each of the two algorithms uses the decision algorithm as a subroutine.
In the second part of the paper we analyze lattice piercing versus non-lattice piercing
and demonstrate explicit instances for which an optimal piercing lattice is non-optimal
over all piercing sets.

Our results.

The first result is a decision algorithm. Recall that
k
is the maximum width or height of a rectangle in
\mathcal{F}
.

Theorem 1
.

Given a family
\mathcal{F}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\}
consisting of
n
axis-parallel rectangles,
and a canonical vector basis
[u,v]
of a lattice
\Lambda
, there is an algorithm that determines
whether
\Lambda
is an
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice in
\mathcal{O}((k+n)\log{k})
time.

The existence of a decision algorithm allows us to obtain two algorithms for computing an optimal piercing lattice.
Both algorithms employ the decision algorithm in
Theorem

1
as a subroutine.
Our first algorithm computes an optimal piercing lattice by generating and solving linear systems
with two variables.

Theorem 2
.

Given a family
\mathcal{F}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\}
consisting of
n
axis-parallel rectangles,
an optimal
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice can be found in
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{8}n^{4}(n+k)\log{k}\right)
time.

For rectangles with integer side-lengths we give a simpler algorithm that does not solve
any linear systems but uses structural properties of the underlying systems.

Theorem 3
.

Given a family
\mathcal{F}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\}
consisting of
n
axis-parallel rectangles with integer dimensions,
an optimal
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice can be found in
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{18}(k+n)\log{k}\right)
time.

Note that for rectangles with integer side-lengths we have
n\leq k^{2}
.
So when
k=\mathcal{O}(1)
, both algorithms run in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time on rectangles with integer side-lengths.

In the second part of the paper we analyze lattice piercing versus non-lattice piercing
and demonstrate explicit instances for which an optimal piercing lattice is non-optimal
over all piercing sets. While the resulting separation is small, its proof is nontrivial.

Theorem 4
.

There exists a family
\mathcal{F}_{0}
of
3
axis-parallel rectangles and a positive constant
\varepsilon>0

where the best piercing density achieved by a lattice is at least
\pi(F_{0})+\varepsilon
.

The proof of

yields a value

.
In the final part of the paper (Sections

and
9
)
we manage to amplify the separation result by roughly six orders of magnitude

Theorem 5
.

There exists a family
\mathcal{F}_{0}
of
3
axis-parallel rectangles for which the best piercing density
achieved by a lattice is at least
\frac{36}{31}\pi(F_{0})=(1.161\ldots)\,\pi(F_{0})
.

Theorem 6
.

There exists a family
\mathcal{F}_{1}
of
5
axis-parallel rectangles for which the best piercing density
achieved by a lattice is at least
\frac{6}{5}\,\pi(F_{1})
.

Theorems
4
,
5
and
6
can be extended
to any larger number of rectangles. Consequently, we obtain the following general
separation result.

Theorem 7
.

For every
n\geq 5
,
there exists a family
\mathcal{F}
of
n
axis-parallel rectangles for which the best piercing density
achieved by a lattice is at least
\frac{6}{5}\,\pi(F)
.

Related result.

To put Theorem
7
in perspective we point out that from the other direction
the following approximation result has been recently obtained. We in fact use this approximation
algorithm as a subroutine in our exact algorithm for finding an optimal piercing lattice.

Theorem 8
.

[

Given a family
\mathcal{F}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\}

n

The output piercing set is a lattice with density at most

Other related work.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite family of axis-parallel rectangles.
The problem of determining whether a given (infinite) point set
P
is
\mathcal{F}
-piercing,
which is one of the problems we consider here, is closely related to that of
determining all maximal empty rectangles amidst the points in
P
.
Indeed, a point set
P
is
\mathcal{F}
-piercing if and only if there is no
maximal
empty rectangle
amidst the points in
P
strictly containing one of the rectangles from
\mathcal{F}
in its interior.
Note that infinite point sets may allow short descriptions,
for instance
periodic
sets and
lattice
sets in particular.
See for instance the monograph by Grünbaum and Shephard
[
6
]
for a discussion
of periodic tilings.

It is known that the number of maximal empty rectangles amidst
n
points is
O(n^{2})
,
and this bound is tight
[
7
]
.
Given an axis-parallel rectangle
R
in the plane containing
n
points,
the problem of computing a maximum-area empty axis-parallel sub-rectangle contained in
R

has been studied extensively.
Recently, an essentially asymptotically optimal algorithm running in nearly
\mathcal{O}(n\log{n})
time
has been designed by Chan
[
2
]
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite family of axis-parallel rectangles.
The
piercing number
of
\mathcal{F}
, denoted
\tau(\mathcal{F})
, is the minimum cardinality of an
\mathcal{F}
-piercing set.
The
independence number
or
matching number
of
\mathcal{F}
,
namely the maximum number of pairwise disjoint sets in
\mathcal{F}
,
is denoted by
\alpha(\mathcal{F})
or
\nu(\mathcal{F})
. Clearly,
\nu(\mathcal{F})\leq\tau(\mathcal{F})
.
The main unsettled question here is whether
\tau(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{O}(\nu(\mathcal{F}))
.
The best known upper bound is due to Correa et al.
[
3
]
.

Lattice piercing

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite family of axis-parallel rectangles; denote by

\mathcal{F}

Let

\mathcal{F}

The following observation is in order:

Observation 1
.

Two parameters of interest in a lattice are (see also

•

\lambda

•

\lambda

These are lattice lines of direction

Note that

We start with a lemma of independent interest spelling out two inequalities between these parameters.

Lemma 1
.

The following inequalities hold:

(i)

\lambda\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\mu

(ii)

\lambda\leq\sqrt{\frac{2A}{\sqrt{3}}}

Proof.

Consider two consecutive parallel
\lambda
-lines

at distance
\mu
from each other.
Refer to
Fig.

2

:
Two consecutive parallel
\lambda

Let

be consecutive lattice points on
\ell_{1}
and

be a lattice point whose orthogonal projection

onto
\ell_{1}

lies between

and

.
Consider the translation

along
\ell_{2}
that brings

into the midpoint of

and

The translation

Taking into account that

A decision algorithm (proof of Theorem
1
)

Suppose in what follows that the premises of Theorem
1
are satisfied.
Since the lattice is periodic, it suffices to consider (i) rectangles whose left sides contain the origin
and (ii) rectangles whose lower sides contain the origin.
Indeed, if
R
is a maximal empty rectangle amidst the points of the lattice, there is a congruent
maximal empty rectangle
R^{\prime}
supported on the left or from below by the origin.
The latter case appears when the lattice contains horizontal lattice lines
(i.e., there exist lattice points with the same
y
-coordinate);
and can be easily dealt with by examining the distance between consecutive
horizontal lattice lines.
We call such rectangles
supported
(by the origin, on the left or from below).
Moreover, since each side of each rectangle is at most
k
, we can restrict our attention to
lattice points contained in a
board rectangle

D=[0,k]\times[-k,k]
.
Let
N=\mathcal{O}(k^{2}/A)
be the number of lattice points
i\cdot u+j\cdot v
,
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
contained in
D
. In particular, for each lattice point in
D
we have

|i|=\mathcal{O}(k/\lambda)=\mathcal{O}(k)
and
|j|=\mathcal{O}(k/A)
by Lemma
3
.

The decision algorithm proceeds in
two steps
:
First, find all maximal supported rectangles that are contained in
D
.
Second, check if any of those rectangles is coordinate-wise larger than any rectangle in
\mathcal{F}
.
If not, output that the lattice
\Lambda
is
\mathcal{F}
-piercing.

For the first step, construct the set of lattice points
P=\{p\in D\}

such that the rectangle with diagonal
op
is empty. It turns out that this set has a special structure
as described in the following.

Lemma 4
.

There are
\mathcal{O}(k)
maximal empty rectangles supported by the origin on the left side
and all can be found in
\mathcal{O}(k\log{k})
time.

Proof.

Let
P
be the set of lattice points
p\in D
such that

is empty.
Here
R(o,p)
denotes the rectangle determined by
o
and
p
.
Let
P=P^{+}\cup P^{-}
be the partition of
P
induced by the
x
-axis
(
P^{+}
and
P^{-}
are the points above and respectively below the
x
-axis, with ties broken arbitrarily.)
Order
P^{+}
and
P^{-}
by
x
-coordinate and observe that:
(i)
P^{+}
must form a sequence of points with decreasing

-coordinates; and
(ii)
P^{-}
must form a sequence of points with increasing
y
-coordinates.
We refer to

as the

is the

and

is the

.
Recall that the sides of

have length
\mathcal{O}(k)

Since

.

Generating the funnel.

Recall that
u
lies in the first quadrant and
v
in the fourth quadrant.
For a fixed

be the lattice consisting of the lattice points

Since

and no other

.
We process the other

the lines below

are processed in increasing order of

When processing the lines above
\ell
, we compute the point with smallest non-negative

form a

starting at

-axis.
Similarly, we add to
P^{+}
the points that form a branch starting at

and approaching the positive part of
the
x
-axis; these points are obtained by processing the lines below
\ell
.
The points in
P^{+}
(the union of two branches) are then sorted by their

As before, we construct two branches, this time approaching the positive part of the

Decision algorithm and its complexity.

Since
A\geq 1
and
|v|\geq|u|\geq 1/2
, we have
|v|=\Omega(1)
, thus only
\mathcal{O}(k)
lines are to be processed.
For each processed
u
-line, the required computation takes
\mathcal{O}(\log{k})
time.
Each line contributes
\mathcal{O}(1)
points to the funnel

.
Together with the sorting the first step takes
\mathcal{O}(k\log k)

has size
\mathcal{O}(k)
.

For the second step, consider each rectangle in
R\in\mathcal{F}
one by one.
The funnel structure is binary searched by the rectangle width

and the corresponding implied height is
obtained from the funnel structure. The necessary decision is immediately taken based on the rectangle height
h
.
There are
n
rectangles in
\mathcal{F}
and it takes
\mathcal{O}(\log{k})
time to process each of them.
The resulting time for the second step is
\mathcal{O}(n\log k)
.

The overall complexity of the decision algorithm is thus
\mathcal{O}(k\log k+n\log k)=\mathcal{O}((k+n)\log{k})
.

5
Computing an optimal
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice

We present two pseudo-polynomial time algorithms for finding the optimal piercing density
for a set of
n
axis-parallel rectangles: their running time are polynomial in the number of rectangles
and the maximum aspect ratio of the rectangles in the family.
(See
[
9
, Ch. 9]
for basic technical terms regarding algorithm complexity.)
The second algorithm assumes that the rectangles have integer side-lengths.

5.1
Preliminaries

By properties P1–P3, the area of a fundamental parallelogram of
\Lambda
is
A=ab+cd>0
.
As such,
A
is an increasing function in each of the four variables.

An
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice
\Lambda
is said to be
x
-
tight
with respect to
\mathcal{F}

if there exist (not necessarily distinct) rectangles
R_{s},R_{t}\in\mathcal{F}
such that for some

i_{s},j_{s},i_{t},j_{t}\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\displaystyle i_{s}\cdot a+j_{s}\cdot c

\displaystyle=w_{s},

\displaystyle i_{t}\cdot a+j_{t}\cdot c

\displaystyle=w_{t},

where both constraints are linearly independent
(the determinant of the linear system in
a,c
is nonzero).
Note that each equation has at least one positive term, i.e.,
\max(i_{s},j_{s})\geq 1
and

\max(i_{i},j_{t})\geq 1
.

An
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice
\Lambda
is said to be
y
-
tight
with respect to
\mathcal{F}

if there exist (not necessarily distinct) rectangles
R_{s},R_{t}\in\mathcal{F}
such that for some

i_{s},j_{s},i_{t},j_{t}\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\displaystyle i_{s}\cdot b+j_{s}\cdot d

\displaystyle=h_{s},

\displaystyle i_{t}\cdot b+j_{t}\cdot d

\displaystyle=h_{t},

where both constraints are linearly independent.
Similarly each equation has at least one positive term.

An
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice
\Lambda
is said to be
tight
with respect to
\mathcal{F}

(or
\mathcal{F}
-
tight
)
if
\Lambda
is
x
-
tight
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
and
y
-
tight
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
.
The key to both algorithms is the following.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\Lambda
be an
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice with minimum density. Then
\Lambda
is
\mathcal{F}
-tight.

Proof.

Consider the set of maximal rectangles of width and height at least
1

supported from the left or from below by the origin. Since every such maximal rectangle is contained in
D
,
this set is finite. For each such rectangle
R=w^{\prime}\times h^{\prime}
, and for each input rectangle
R_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
,
the piercing condition requires

w^{\prime}\leq w_{i}\text{ or }h^{\prime}\leq h_{i}.

(2)

Observe that each constraint of the form
w^{\prime}\leq w_{i}
can be expressed as a linear inequality
in
a,c
, whereas each constraint of the form
h^{\prime}\leq h_{i}
can be expressed as a linear inequality
in
b,d
. Indeed, since the points that support
R
are lattice points,
w^{\prime}
can be expressed as
an integer combination of
a
and
c
, and
h^{\prime}
can be expressed as an integer combination of
b
and
d
.
Construct a finite system
\Psi
of linear inequalities by putting in
\Psi
every inequality of this type.
Let
g_{ac}\leq 2
and
g_{bd}\leq 2

be the number of independent
x
-tight inequalities (equations), and respectively
y
-tight inequalities
that can be extracted from the system. Next we show that
g_{ac}=g_{bd}=2
in a proof by contradiction.

If
g_{ac}=0
, pick a variable (
a
or
c
) that appears with a positive coefficient in at least one
inequality in
\Psi
and increase it (i.e., continuously modify
\Lambda
) by a small
\varepsilon>0

(during which interval the system of inequalities describing the lattice constraints on the rectangles
does not change). Since the piercing condition is maintained and
A
increases, this contradicts the
optimality of
\Lambda
. Therefore
g_{ac}\geq 1
. By a symmetric argument we also have
g_{bd}\geq 1
.

If one of
g_{ac},g_{bd}
is
1
and the other one is
2
, we may assume without loss of generality that

g_{ac}=1
and
g_{bd}=2
. This means that
b,d\geq 0
are determined (i.e., constants).
Since
g_{ac}=1
, the area
A=ad+bc
is a linear function in one variable (
a
or
c
),
say,
A=\ell(a)
for some
a>0
.
Then every remaining inequality in
\Psi
is linear in
a
.
Depending on the sign of the coefficient of
a
,
increase or decrease
a
(i.e., continuously modify
\Lambda
) by a small
\varepsilon>0

such that
A
increases. Since
g_{ac}=1
and
\varepsilon>0
is small,
the inequalities defining the rectangles do not switch. Since the piercing condition is maintained
and
A
increases, this contradicts the optimality of
\Lambda
.

The last case to consider is
g_{ac}=g_{bd}=1
. Then the area expression
A=ad+bc

becomes a function of two variables (one from
\{a,c\}
and one from
\{b,d\}
),
say,
A=ad+bc=r_{1}ab+r_{2}a+r_{3}b
, where
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
.
In addition, there exist
a,b>0
such that
f(a,b):=r_{1}ab+r_{2}a+r_{3}b>0
.
It suffices to show that
f(a,b)
has no local maximum for
a,b>0
.
If
r_{1}=0
, this is obvious since
f(a,b)
is then a linear function in
a
and
b
,
and a suitable small change in one of the variables leads to an increase in
A=f(a,b)
,
contradicting the optimality of
\Lambda
. If
r_{1}\neq 0
, write

f_{a}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial a},\ \ f_{b}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial b},\ \ f_{aa}:=\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial a^{2}},\ \ f_{bb}:=\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial b^{2}},\ \ f_{ab}:=\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial b\partial a}\text{ or }\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial a\partial b},

and let
\Delta:=f_{aa}f_{bb}-(f_{ab})^{2}
.
By a well known theorem in calculus (see, e.g.,
[
11
, Thm. 22.15]
), if
\Delta<0
at
(a,b)
,
then
(a,b)
is not a local maximum (nor a local minimum).
We verify that
f_{aa}=f_{bb}=0
and
f_{ab}=r_{1}
, thus

\Delta=f_{aa}f_{bb}-(f_{ab})^{2}=-r_{1}^{2}<0
and so
f(a,b)
has no local maxima for
a,b>0
.
As such, a suitable small change in one of the variables leads to an increase in
A=f(a,b)
,
contradicting the optimality of
\Lambda
.

Consequently, the only remaining case is
g_{ac}=g_{bd}=2
, as required.
∎

The first algorithm

By Lemma

, an
\mathcal{F}
-piercing lattice of optimal density is tight. Therefore, it suffices to generate
all tight lattices and output a lattice that is
\mathcal{F}
-piercing and has minimum density (i.e., largest area
A
).
The algorithm essentially guesses the optimal lattice by exhaustive enumeration of all
lattices that are tight with respect to
\mathcal{F}
and have density in a prescribed interval.
It retains those that are
\mathcal{F}
-piercing and finally returns one with optimal density.

For guessing the four tight inequality constraints,
it suffices to choose two lattice points
p_{1},p_{2}\in D
so that the point pairs

and
o,p_{2}

correspond to the two
x
-constraints and four lattice points
p_{3},p_{4},p_{5},p_{6}\in D
so that the point pairs

p_{3},p_{4}
and
p_{5},p_{6}
correspond to the two
y
-constraints (not all six points need to be distinct).
One can draw a
constraint graph
on the lattice points by including these point pairs;
an example appears in Fig.

.

Recall that
D
is the board rectangle of diameter
\mathcal{O}(k)
.
Since
p_{3},p_{4}
are lattice points,
p_{3}p_{4}=op^{\prime}_{3}
for a suitable lattice point
p^{\prime}_{3}\in D
.
Similarly,
p_{5}p_{6}=op^{\prime}_{4}
for a suitable lattice point
p^{\prime}_{4}\in D
.
Here choosing a lattice point means specifying the integers
i,j
for the lattice point

L(i,j):=i\cdot u+j\cdot v
. It is worth noting that this choice is symbolical in the sense that
the vectors

are left unspecified.
By Lemma
2
, any pair of lattice points

can be connected by a

2
-edge polygonal path along directions
u
and
v
and whose length is
\mathcal{O}(k)
.
Moreover, since
\lambda=\Omega(1)
(by Lemma
3
),
if

, then
|i|+|j|=\mathcal{O}(k)
. In summary, guessing one constraint
is equivalent to choosing
i
and

as above and this can be done in
\mathcal{O}(N)=\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
ways.
Recall that
N=\mathcal{O}(k^{2}/A)
is the number of lattice points
i\cdot u+j\cdot v
,
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}

contained in
D
.
(This argument is applied once again in Sections
8
and
9
with extra
care for obtaining sharper numeric bounds for the specific rectangle family considered there.)

6
The second algorithm

The algorithm works on input families whose rectangles have integer side-lengths.
The general scheme is the same as in the first algorithm. However,

For a fixed

10
Conclusion

We list several directions to be explored.

1.

Given a family
\mathcal{F}
of
n
axis-parallel rectangles,
what is the computational complexity of determining the translative piercing density
\pi_{T}(\mathcal{F})

and the lattice piercing density
\pi_{L}(\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{F}
?
Is there a polynomial-time algorithm independent of
k
for any of these problems?

2.

What is the largest possible relative gap between the optimal lattice and non-lattice piercing densities
for a family of axis-parallel rectangles? We have shown a lower bound of
1.2
and an upper bound
of
1.895
(in a previous work) on this gap.

3.

Is it true that
\pi_{T}(\mathcal{F})=\pi_{L}(\mathcal{F})
for families
\mathcal{F}
of two rectangles?

4.

How does the gap between the optimal lattice and non-lattice piercing densities
for families of axis-parallel boxes grow with the dimension of the space?

5.

The two algorithms for finding an optimal piercing lattice appear to be extendable to
piercing axis-parallel boxes in higher dimensions. This is left as an open problem.
