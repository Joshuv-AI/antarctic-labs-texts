Local Statistics in Normal Matrix Models with Merging Singularity
Torben Krüger, Seung-Yeop Lee, Meng Yang

Abstract
We study the normal matrix model, also known as the two-dimensional one-component plasma at a specific temperature, with merging singularity. As the number n n of particles tends to infinity we obtain the limiting local correlation kernel at the singularity, which is related to the parametrix of the Painlevé II equation. The two main tools are Riemann-Hilbert problems and the generalized Christoffel-Darboux identity. The correlation kernel exhibits a novel anisotropic scaling behavior, where the corresponding spacing scale of particles is n − 1 / 3 n^{-1/3} in the direction of merging and n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} in the perpendicular direction. In the vicinity at different distances to the merging singularity we also observe Ginibre bulk and edge statistics, as well as the sine-kernel and the universality class corresponding to the elliptic ensemble in the weak non-Hermiticity regime for the local correlation function.

Introduction and Main Results

The normal matrix model with external potential
Q:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is an ensemble of
n
particles,
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, distributed according to the probability density

\rho_{n}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\prod_{i<j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{2}{\rm e}^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q(z_{j})},

(1.1)

where
Z_{n}
is the normalization constant and
N
is a positive real parameter. The name, normal matrix model, reflects that (
1.1
) is the joint density of complex eigenvalues of random normal matrices (see e.g.
[
21
,
22
]
). For example, the choice
Q(z)=|z|^{2}
yields the complex Ginibre ensemble, i.e., the eigenvalue distribution of an
n\times n
random matrix with independent complex standard normal-distributed entries
[
39
]
. Another interpretation of (
1.1
) is as the Gibbs measure of the two-dimensional one-component plasma (OCP) at a specific temperature. The OCP is also called Coulomb gas or
\log
-gas. It consists of
n
particles in the potential
Q
subject to a logarithmic pair-interaction and plays a role in the fractional quantum Hall effect
[
7
,
42
]
, the theory of superfluid-superconducting films
[
47
]
and two-dimensional turbulence
[
37
,
46
,
48
]
. For an extensive overview of the connection between eigenvalues of random matrices and
\log
-gases we refer to
[
36
,
17
]
.

One of the fundamental questions in the interacting particle system is to understand the correlations among the particles. This correlation is characterized by the
k
-point correlation function

R_{n,N}^{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k}):=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n-k}}\rho_{n}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}{\rm d}A(z_{j}),

where
{\rm d}A
is the Lebesgue area measure of the complex plane. This function encodes the marginal probability for
k
particles.

The
k
-point correlation function can be written as

R_{n,N}^{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=\det\big[{\bf K}_{n,N}(z_{i},z_{j})\big]_{i,j=1}^{k}

(1.2)

(see e.g.
[
28
]
), where the correlation kernel
{\bf K}_{n,N}(z,{\zeta})
is given by

{\bf K}_{n,N}(z,{\zeta}):={\rm e}^{-\frac{N}{2}Q(z)-\frac{N}{2}Q(\zeta)}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{h_{k}}p_{k,N}(z)\overline{p_{k,N}(\zeta)},

(1.3)

in terms of the monic orthogonal polynomial with degree
n
, subject to the orthogonality conditions

\int_{\mathbb{C}}p_{n,N}(z)\,\overline{p_{m,N}(z)}{\rm e}^{-NQ(z)}\,{\rm d}A(z)=h_{n}\delta_{nm},\quad n,m\geq 0,

(1.4)

where
h_{n}
is the positive norming constant and
\delta_{nm}
is the Kronecker delta.
These orthogonal polynomials satisfy

p_{n,N}(z)={\mathbb{E}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-z_{j}),

where the expectation is taken over the probability distribution in (
1.1
).

Equation (
1.2
) implies that the correlation kernel
{\bf K}_{n,N}
determines the statistical behavior of the particles.
In the scaling limit of
n\to\infty
, while
n/N
is fixed to a constant, the correlation kernel
{\bf K}_{n,N}
, with the proper scaling of coordinates, often converges to a universal function.
It is expected that this universal function only depends on the symmetry class of the model and the local behaviour of the limiting density
\lim_{n,N\to\infty}R^{1}_{n,N}
, i.e. on whether the density vanishes and if so on its vanishing order.
Below, we describe several known results about the asymptotic behaviors of the correlation kernels.

In contrast to the
2
-dimensional setting decribed above, in unitary ensembles, all the eigenvalues are confined on the real axis, and the joint distribution of eigenvalues is proportional to

\prod_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}{\rm e}^{-N\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q(x_{j})}\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\rm d}x_{k},

with
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
If
Q
is real analytic and grows sufficiently fast at infinity, the eigenvalues are confined to a union of intervals
[
29
]
. It is known that the local scaling limits of the correlation kernel are universal in the sense that they only depend on the vanishing order of the limiting density
\sigma(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}R_{n,N}^{1}(x)
. By
[
31
,
12
,
49
]
, in the bulk of the spectrum, the universal correlation kernel at the microscopic scale is the Sine kernel; at the edge of the spectrum, the limiting density of the eigenvalues typically vanishes as a square root, and the universal correlation kernel at the microscopic scale is the Airy kernel
[
50
]
. In the critical case studied in
[
13
,
26
]
, where the spectral density vanishes quadratically in the interior of the asymptotic spectrum,
the universal limiting kernel can be written in terms of the Hastings-McLeod solution of Painlevé II equation, a.k.a. the Painlevé II kernel.

In a matrix ensemble with an external source, different universal limiting kernels emerge at points of vanishing spectral density within the interior of its support. In
[
53
,
15
,
16
,
33
]
, when the spectral density vanishes like a cubic root, the universal limiting kernel can be written in terms of the Pearcey integrals, a.k.a. the Pearcey kernel
[
51
]
.

In normal matrix models where eigenvalues are complex valued, as
N
tends to infinity proportional to the number of particles
n
, the limiting density
\sigma(z):=\lim_{n,N\to\infty}R^{1}_{n,N}(z)
is supported on a compact set
{\cal S}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, which we call the droplet
[
40
]
.

In the bulk of the droplet, i.e., at
z^{*}\in{\rm Int}({\cal S})
where
\sigma(z^{*})\neq 0
, the universal scaling limit of the correlation kernel is given
[
3
]
by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z^{*})}{\bf K}_{n,n}\bigg(z^{*}+\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z^{*})}},z^{*}+\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z^{*})}}\bigg)=G(\nu,\eta):={\rm e}^{\nu\overline{\eta}-\frac{|\nu|^{2}}{2}-\frac{|\eta|^{2}}{2}}.

(1.5)

Here

is also known as the Ginibre kernel
[
39
]
. Recently in
[
41
]
the universality has also been shown at the boundary

is a smooth Jordan curve.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\Delta Q(z^{*})}{\bf K}_{n,n}\bigg(z^{*}+\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}\nu}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z^{*})}},z^{*}+\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta}\eta}{\sqrt{n\Delta Q(z^{*})}}\bigg)=G(\nu,\eta)\frac{1}{2}{\rm erfc}\Big(\frac{\nu+\overline{\eta}}{\sqrt{2}}\Big).

(1.6)

Here the angle

is that of the outer normal direction to the boundary of the droplet at
z^{*}
, and
{\rm erfc}
denotes the complementary error function. The kernel on the right hand side is also known as the Faddeeva kernel
[
34
]
. Similar edge universality results have also been established for matrices with i.i.d. non-Gaussian entries in
[
23
,
20
]
.

For the two-dimensional OCP at inverse temperatures different from the one corresponding to the normal matrix model, universality results analogous to (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) are not known. However, central limit theorems for mesoscopic linear statistics have recently been established in
[

]

In this paper we obtain the limiting correlation kernel around the point in the droplet boundary where the merging singularity occurs, see Figure

The universal behavior at such critical points have been open problems in normal matrix models while, in unitary ensembles, where the full hierarchy of critical behaviors have been investigated
[
25
,

. It was expected and conjectured by physicists that similar behavior will emerge in the normal matrix ensemble
[

\sim n^{-1/3}

A remarkable feature of the particle spacing at the criticality is its anisotropic nature. While the particles are spaced at a distance
\sim n^{-1/3}
in the direction of merging, they are concentrated on the much smaller scale
\sim n^{-1/2}
in the perpendicular direction.

We also observe a rich scaling behaviour for the particle density. This density vanishes quadratically in the direction of merging at the critical point
b_{c}
, giving rise to the Painlevé II kernel. However, the characteristic scale on which the density varies from its value at
b_{c}
to its value in the bulk is
n^{-1/4}
, i.e. larger than the scale,
n^{-1/3}
, of inter-particle distance at the critical point. This is in sharp contrast to the regular edge behaviour (
1.6
), where the particle density varies on the same scale on which the particles are spaced. The separation of characteristic lengths for the density and particle spacing causes the appearance of additional universal particle correlations in a mesoscopic distance from the merging singularity. Letting
l
be the distance from the merging singularity, Sine-kernel statistic emerges at
n^{-1/3}\ll l\ll n^{-1/4}
. For
l=n^{-1/4}
the local universality class coincides with the one observed in the weak non-Hermiticity regime of the elliptic ensemble
[
38
,
1
]
. Beyond this distance, i.e. for
l\gg n^{-1/4}
, the familiar bulk and edge behaviours from (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) are observed.

Remark 2

Rich Scaling Behaviour Near the Merging Singularity:

In contrast to the 1 dimensional Coulomb gas, the merging singularity here exhibits an additional characteristic mesoscopic scale
N^{-1/4}
, greater than the microscopic length scale
N^{-1/3}
of particle spacing at the singularity. On this mesoscopic length scale the particle density has a non-trivial profile and drops from its bulk value down to zero. Such rich scaling behaviour is absent at regular edge points of normal matrix models.
For at the distance

from the merging point

with
\frac{1}{4}<\tau<\frac{3}{10}

\sim N^{2\tau-1}

and the particle system is effectively one dimensional, due to the Gaussian decay in the

the width of the support of the equilibrium measure becomes of order

, matching the inter-particle distance of the Ginibre ensemble. For

this width is thick enough to accommodate the two-dimensional particles system.

For

\lim_{n,N\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}R_{n,N}^{1}\bigg(b_{c}+\frac{X}{\sqrt{2N}}+\frac{{\rm i}(2cb_{c}^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}Y}{N^{1/4}}\bigg)=\frac{1}{2\pi t_{c}}\big({\rm erfc}\big(X+\sqrt{c}Y^{2}\big)-{\rm erfc}\big(X+b_{c}Y^{2}\big)\big).

(1.18)

See Figure

Theorem 2.1

&\qquad-e^{-Nz\bar{\zeta}}\frac{p_{n+1}(a)}{p_{n}(a)}\frac{N\,h_{n}/h_{n-1}}{\frac{n+Nc+1}{N}h_{n}-h_{n+1}}\overline{\psi_{n-1}(\zeta)}\Big(\psi_{n+1}(z)-z\psi_{n}(z)\Big).\end{split}

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=-\hat{s}q^{2}-q^{4}+(q^{\prime})^{2},

(A.14)

\displaystyle B_{1}(\hat{s})=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{{\rm i}(q^{2}+r^{\prime})}{2}&\frac{{\rm i}(4p_{12}-qr-q^{\prime})}{2}\\

(A.15)

(A.16)

Using the fact that

(q_{12}+q_{21})q^{\prime}=q^{2}(q_{11}+q_{22})
from (

\displaystyle q_{12}=-q_{21}=\frac{1}{16}(2\hat{s}q+q^{3}+\hat{s}^{2}q^{5}+2\hat{s}q^{7}+q^{9}-2q^{2}(\hat{s}+q^{2})q^{\prime}-2q^{3}(\hat{s}+q^{2})(q^{\prime})^{2}+2(q^{\prime})^{3}+q(q^{\prime})^{4}).

(A.17)

Therefore,

\displaystyle p_{12}(\hat{s})=p_{21}(\hat{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{q(\hat{s})r(\hat{s})+q^{\prime}(\hat{s})}{4},

(A.18)

\displaystyle p_{11}(\hat{s})=p_{22}(\hat{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{2q(\hat{s})q^{\prime}(\hat{s})(\hat{s}+q(\hat{s})^{2})+q(\hat{s})^{2}-r(\hat{s})^{2}}{8}+\frac{2q^{\prime}(\hat{s})(r(\hat{s})-q^{\prime}(\hat{s})^{2})}{8q(\hat{s})}

\displaystyle=\frac{q(\hat{s})^{2}-r(\hat{s})^{2}}{8}.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
2.1

Let
p_{j}
be the monic orthogonal polynomials satisfy the following orthogonality conditions

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}p_{j}(z)\overline{p_{k}(z)}|z-a|^{2Nc}{\rm e}^{-N|z|^{2}}\,{\rm d}A(z)=h_{j}\,\delta_{jk},

(B.1)

where
h_{j}
is the norming constant.
Let us denote

\psi_{j}(z):=(z-a)^{Nc}p_{j}(z),\qquad\phi_{j}(z):=(z-a)^{Nc}\frac{p_{j}(z)}{h_{j}}.

(B.2)

We define the inner product

\langle U|V\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\overline{U(z)}\,V(z)\,{\rm e}^{-N|z|^{2}}\,{\rm d}A(z)=\overline{\langle V|U\rangle}

(B.3)

and denote
\Psi:=[\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\cdots]^{\rm T}
,
\Phi:=[\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\cdots]^{\rm T}
, where
{\rm T}
is the transpose of a matrix. Let

\Pi_{n}:=\textup{diag}(\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{n},0,\cdots)

(B.4)

be the projection operator, we write

{\cal K}_{n}(z,\zeta)={\rm e}^{-Nz\bar{\zeta}}\Phi^{*}(\zeta)\Pi_{n}\Psi(z),

(B.5)

where the superscript
*
means the complex conjugation.
Then we have

\overline{\partial}_{\zeta}{\cal K}_{n}(z,\zeta)=-N{\rm e}^{-Nz\bar{\zeta}}\Big(z-\frac{1}{N}\overline{\partial}_{\zeta}\Big)\Phi^{*}(\zeta)\Pi_{n}\Psi(z).

(B.6)

Let us denote

L_{j,j-1}=-\frac{\langle z\psi_{j}|\phi_{0}\rangle}{\langle z\psi_{j-1}|\phi_{0}\rangle}\quad\mbox{for each $j$}.

(B.7)

Notice that the denominator does not vanish due to the fact that
p_{n+1}(0)\neq 0
and
p_{n}(z)\neq zp_{n-1}(z)
for all
n
, which is obtained by the following relation

h_{n}=-\frac{\Gamma(Nc+n+1)}{2{\rm i}N^{Nc+n+1}}\frac{\widetilde{h}_{n}}{p_{n+1}(0)}

with
\widetilde{h}_{n}\neq 0
and
h_{n}\neq 0
for all
n
from
[
8
, Proposition 3.3, Proposition 7.1]
.
Note also that

z\big(\psi_{j}(z)+L_{j,j-1}\psi_{j-1}(z)\big)\,\bot\,\phi_{0}

with respect to the inner product (
B.3
).
The numbers
L_{j,j-1}
are building blocks to define the lower diagonal matrix

L:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0&\dots\\
L_{2,1}&0&0&0&\dots\\
0&L_{3,2}&0&0&\dots\\
0&0&L_{4,3}&0&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\end{bmatrix}.

We write

\widetilde{\psi}_{j}:=\psi_{j}+L_{j,j-1}\psi_{j-1}.

(B.8)

Then if

\phi(z)=(\text{polynomials of deg}\leq j-2)\cdot(z-a)\cdot(z-a)^{Nc},

we have

\langle\phi\,|\,z\widetilde{\psi}_{j}\rangle=\langle\partial\phi\,|\,\widetilde{\psi}_{j}\rangle=0.

(B.9)

It follows that

\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{j-1}\}\,\bot\,z\widetilde{\psi}_{j},

which leads to

z\widetilde{\psi}_{j}(z)=\psi_{j+1}(z)+B_{j,j}\,\psi_{j}(z)

(B.10)

for some
B_{j,j}
.
Thus we obtain

z(I+L)\Psi=B\,\Psi,\qquad B:=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1,1}&1&0&0&\dots\\
0&B_{2,2}&1&0&\dots\\
0&0&B_{3,3}&1&\dots\\
0&0&0&B_{4,4}&\ddots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}.

(B.11)

Let us also write

U_{j,j+1}=-\frac{h_{j+1}}{h_{j}}\frac{p_{j}(a)}{p_{j+1}(a)},

(B.12)

which is well-defined due to the fact that
h_{j}\neq 0
for all
j
from
[
8
, Proposition 3.3, Proposition 7.1]
, and define the upper diagonal matrix

U:=\begin{bmatrix}0&U_{1,2}&0&0&\dots\\
0&0&U_{2,3}&0&\dots\\
0&0&0&U_{3,4}&\dots\\
0&0&0&0&\ddots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}.

Then the function

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\phi}_{j}(z)&:=\phi_{j}(z)+U_{j,j+1}\phi_{j+1}(z)=(\text{polynomials of deg}\leq j)\cdot(z-a)\cdot(z-a)^{Nc}\end{split}

(B.13)

satisfies

\langle\partial\widehat{\phi}_{j}\,|\,\psi_{k}\rangle=\langle\widehat{\phi}_{j}\,|\,z\,\psi_{k}\rangle=0\quad\text{if }k\leq j-2.

(B.14)

Thus we have

\partial\widehat{\phi}_{j}=A_{j,j}\phi_{j}+A_{j,j-1}\phi_{j-1}

(B.15)

for some
A_{j,k}
, equivalently,

\partial(I+U)\Phi=A\,\Phi,\qquad A:=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1,1}&0&0&0&\dots\\
A_{2,1}&A_{2,2}&0&0&\dots\\
0&A_{3,2}&A_{3,3}&0&\dots\\
0&0&A_{4,3}&A_{4,4}&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}.

(B.16)

We now determine
A_{j,j-1}
and
A_{j,j}
. Note that integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\overline{B}(I+U)^{t}&=\overline{B}\langle\Psi\,|\,\Phi^{t}\rangle(I+U)^{t}=\langle B\Psi\,|\,\Phi^{t}(I+U)^{t}\rangle\\
&=\langle z(I+L)\Psi\,|\,\Phi^{t}(I+U)^{t}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\langle(I+L)\Psi\,|\,\partial\Phi^{t}(I+U)^{t}\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\langle(I+L)\Psi\,|\,\Phi^{t}A^{t}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}(I+\overline{L})A^{t}.\end{split}

(B.17)

Thus we obtain the relation

\tfrac{1}{N}A(I+L^{*})=(I+U)B^{*},\qquad B=\tfrac{1}{N}(I+L)A^{*}(I+U^{*})^{-1}.

(B.18)

Comparing the terms involving
A_{j,j-1}
, one can observe that

A_{j,j-1}=N.

(B.19)

To determine
A_{j,j}
, note that

\displaystyle\partial\widehat{\phi}_{j}(z)

\displaystyle=\partial\Big(\phi_{j}+U_{j,j+1}\phi_{j+1}\Big)=\frac{1}{h_{j}}\partial\Big((z-a)^{Nc}p_{j}\Big)+\frac{U_{j,j+1}}{h_{j+1}}\partial\Big((z-a)^{Nc}p_{j+1}\Big)

\displaystyle=(z-a)^{Nc-1}\frac{1}{h_{j}}\bigg(\Big(Ncp_{j}+(z-a)p_{j}^{\prime}\Big)-\frac{p_{j}(a)}{p_{j+1}(a)}\Big(Ncp_{j+1}+(z-a)p_{j+1}^{\prime}\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=(z-a)^{Nc}\frac{1}{h_{j}}\bigg(\frac{Nc}{z-a}\Big(p_{j}-\frac{p_{j}(a)}{p_{j+1}(a)}p_{j+1}\Big)+p_{j}^{\prime}-\frac{p_{j}(a)}{p_{j+1}(a)}p_{j+1}^{\prime}\bigg).

This gives

A_{j,j}p_{j}+N\frac{h_{j}}{h_{j-1}}p_{j-1}=\frac{Nc}{z-a}\Big(p_{j}-\frac{p_{j}(a)}{p_{j+1}(a)}p_{j+1}\Big)+p_{j}^{\prime}-\frac{p_{j}(a)}{p_{j+1}(a)}p_{j+1}^{\prime}.

Comparing the coefficient of
z^{j}
term of this identity, we obtain

A_{j,j}=-\frac{p_{j}(a)}{p_{j+1}(a)}(Nc+j+1).

(B.20)

Notice in particular that
A_{j,k}
’s are real.

Now let us consider the decomposition

A=N\,T_{-}+A_{0},

(B.21)

where

T_{-}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0&\dots\\
1&0&0&0&\dots\\
0&1&0&0&\dots\\
0&0&1&0&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\end{bmatrix},\qquad A_{0}:=\textup{diag}(A_{1,1},A_{2,2},\dots)

are the translation and the diagonal part respectively. Write

A^{*}=T_{+}+A_{0}^{*},\qquad T_{+}:=T_{-}^{*}.

(B.22)

Note also that we have

\displaystyle\quad(T_{+}-\zeta)\Psi=\big(T_{+}-(I+L)^{-1}B\big)\Psi=\Big(T_{+}-(I+L)^{-1}\frac{1}{N}(I+L)A^{*}(I+U^{*})^{-1}\Big)\Psi

\displaystyle=\Big(T_{+}-\frac{1}{N}A^{*}(I+U^{*})^{-1}\Big)\Psi=(T_{+}(I+U^{*})-\frac{1}{N}A^{*})(I+U^{*})^{-1}\Psi=\Big(T_{+}U^{*}-\tfrac{1}{N}A_{0}^{*}\Big)(I+U^{*})^{-1}\Psi,

where the second and the fourth identity follow from (
B.18
) and (
B.21
) respectively.

Let us claim that
(T_{+}U^{*}-\tfrac{1}{N}A_{0}^{*})
is invertible.
Suppose that this is not the case. Then there exists some
k
such that

U^{*}_{k-1,k}-\frac{1}{N}A_{k-1,k-1}=0
. Consequently, we have
\psi_{k}(z)=z\psi_{k-1}(z)
. This contradicts the assumption that
a\neq 0
.
Therefore,
(T_{+}U^{*}-\tfrac{1}{N}A_{0}^{*})
is invertible, which is also equivalent to

h_{j+1}\not=\frac{Nc+j+1}{N}h_{j}

(B.23)

by (
B.12
) and (
B.20
).

By letting

\widehat{\Psi}:=\Big(T_{+}U^{*}-\frac{1}{N}A_{0}^{*}\Big)^{-1}(T_{+}-z)\Psi,

(B.24)

we have

(I+U^{*})\widehat{\Psi}=\Psi.

(B.25)

Note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\Psi}&:=[\widehat{\psi}_{0},\widehat{\psi}_{1},\cdots]^{t}=\Big(T_{+}U^{*}-\frac{1}{N}A_{0}^{*}\Big)^{-1}(T_{+}-z)[\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\cdots]^{t}\\
&=\textup{diag}\Big(U^{*}_{1,2}-\frac{1}{N}A_{1,1},U^{*}_{2,3}-\frac{1}{N}A_{2,2},\cdots\Big)^{-1}[\psi_{1}-z\psi_{0},\psi_{2}-z\psi_{1},\cdots]^{t}.\end{split}

Thus we have

\widehat{\psi}_{j}=\frac{\psi_{j+1}-z\psi_{j}}{U_{j,j+1}^{*}-\frac{1}{N}A_{j,j}}.

(B.26)

Here the denominator again does not vanish due to (
B.12
), (
B.20
) and (
B.23

Then by (

\displaystyle(I+L)z(I+U^{*})\widehat{\Psi}

\displaystyle=(I+L)z\Psi=B\Psi=B(I+U^{*})\widehat{\Psi}=\frac{1}{N}(I+L)A^{*}\widehat{\Psi},

which leads to

(B.27)

Combining (

\displaystyle\Big(z-\frac{1}{N}\partial_{\zeta}\Big)\Phi^{*}(\zeta)\Pi_{n}\Psi(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\Phi^{*}(\zeta)[\Pi_{n},A^{*}]\widehat{\Psi}(z)-\frac{1}{N}\partial_{\zeta}\Phi^{*}(\zeta)[\Pi_{n},I+U^{*}]\widehat{\Psi}(z).

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Since
\Delta Q(z)=(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})Q(z)=4
away from the singularity, the function,
2\log|u(z)|+4N|z-z_{0}|^{2}-NQ(z)
, is a subharmonic function (and harmonic away from the singularities) for any polynomial
u(z)
. Exponentiating the subharmonic function we get that

|u(z)|^{2}{\rm e}^{4N|z-z_{0}|^{2}-NQ(z)}
is also a subharmonic function, using Jensen’s inequality. Integrating around a small disk of radius
1/\sqrt{N}
centered at
z_{0}
, that we denote by
D(z_{0};1/\sqrt{N})
below, we get

\displaystyle|u(z_{0})|^{2}{\rm e}^{-NQ(z_{0})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N}{\pi}\int_{D(z_{0};1/\sqrt{N})}|u(z)|^{2}{\rm e}^{4N|z-z_{0}|^{2}-NQ(z)}{\rm d}A(z)

(C.1)

\displaystyle<\frac{N{\rm e}^{4}}{\pi}\int_{D(z_{0};1/\sqrt{N})}|u(z)|^{2}{\rm e}^{-NQ(z)}{\rm d}A(z)

(C.2)

\displaystyle<\frac{N{\rm e}^{4}}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|u(z)|^{2}{\rm e}^{-NQ(z)}{\rm d}A(z).

(C.3)

The above inequality holds for arbitrary
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Taking the log of the above inequality, we get

2\log|u(z)|-NQ(z)\leq\log\frac{N{\rm e}^{4}}{\pi}\|u\|_{L^{2}(NQ)},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}},

where we define

\|u\|_{L^{2}(NQ)}=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|u(z)|^{2}{\rm e}^{-NQ(z)}{\rm d}A(z).

Let us define
\widehat{Q}
, adopting the notation of
[
4
]
, by

\widehat{Q}(z)=\frac{2t}{\text{Area}({\cal S})}\int_{\cal S}\log|z-w|{\rm d}A(w)-\ell_{\text{2D}},

where
\ell_{\text{2D}}
is chosen such that
Q=\widehat{Q}
on the boundary of
{\cal S}
. Since
\widehat{Q}
is harmonic in
{\cal S}^{c}
, and
\widehat{Q}
satisfies the growth condition
\widehat{Q}(z)\sim 2t\log|z|
as
|z|\to\infty
, we have

2\log|u(z)|-N\widehat{Q}(z)\leq\log\frac{N{\rm e}^{4}}{\pi}\|u\|_{L^{2}(NQ)}

on the boundary of
{\cal S}
, and the left hand side goes to
-\infty
if
u(z)
is a polynomial of degree less than
tN
. Since the left hand side is harmonic on
{\cal S}^{c}
the above inequality holds for all
z\in{\cal S}^{c}\cup\partial{\cal S}
.

Let us take
n=tN
and
u(z)={\rm e}^{\frac{N}{2}Q(z)}{\bf K}_{n}(z,w)/\sqrt{{\bf K}_{n}(w,w)}
which is a polynomial of degree
n-1
. We have
\|u\|_{L^{2}(NQ)}=1
and, by exponentiating the above inequality, we get

{\rm e}^{N(Q(z)-\widehat{Q}(z))}\frac{|{\bf K}_{n}(z,w)|^{2}}{{\bf K}_{n}(w,w)}\leq\frac{N{\rm e}^{4}}{\pi},\quad\text{for}\quad z\in{\cal S}^{c}\cup\partial{\cal S}\quad\text{and}\quad w\in{\mathbb{C}}.

Taking
w=z
we have

{\bf K}_{n}(z,z)\leq\frac{N{\rm e}^{4}}{\pi}{\rm e}^{-N(Q(z)-\widehat{Q}(z))},\quad\text{for}\quad z\in{\cal S}^{c}\cup\partial{\cal S}.

Setting
{\cal U}_{\text{2D}}(z)=Q(z)-\widehat{Q}(z)
, we finish the proof Theorem
4.1
.
