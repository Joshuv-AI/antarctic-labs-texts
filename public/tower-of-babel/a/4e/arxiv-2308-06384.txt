Coarse geometry and its applications in solid state physics
Entry for the Encyclopedia of Mathematical Physics 2nd edition, Matthias Ludewig

Abstract




Topological insulators (or topological phases), are certain materials or meta-materials which have a certain topological non-triviality in their mathematical description.
For a general introduction, we refer to the broader physics literature and the article by Bernevig in this encyclopedia.
The use of K-theory in the classification of topological phases goes back to Kitaev
Kitaev 2009
and is now fairly standard in the mathematical physics literature
Freed and Moore 2013
;
Thiang 2016
.
Here K-theory typically refers to topological K-theory of the Bloch bundle on the Brillouin zone; see §
II
.
However, as observed by Bellissard around 40 years ago
Belissard 1986
;
Bellissard et al. 1994
, non-commutative
C^{*}
-algebras of observables are needed in order to classify disordered systems, which naturally leads to the use of operator algebra K-theory.
1
1

1

The
C^{*}
-algebras suggested by Belissard are certain crossed product algebras (also used in the modern treatment
Prodan and Schulz-Baldes 2016
).

It has been realized fairly recently that
Roe algebras
, which come from the mathematical subject of
coarse geometry
, are a particularly good choice here, as they are physically well-motivated and on the other hand allow simple proofs of many features of topological insulators
Kubota 2017
;
Ewert and Meyer 2019
;
Ludewig and Thiang 2021
;
Kubota et al. 2022
;
Ludewig and Thiang 2022a
.
In this article, we give an overview over recent developments in this direction.

One physical feature of many topological insulators is that they are insulating in their interior (the
bulk
), but extremely well-conducting on the boundary.
Mathematically, this corresponds to filling of the bulk Hamiltonian’s spectral gap when introducing boundary (the new spectrum corresponding to certain boundary-localized states
(
Prodan and Schulz-Baldes 2016
, §2.4.3)
).
Moreover, in two dimensional topological phases, the mathematical models predict certain quantized edge currents
Halperin 1982
;
Kellendonk and Schulz-Baldes 2004
;
Thiang 2020
, which have also been observed in experiments
Klembt et al. 2018
;
Nash et al. 2015
;
Mitchell et al. 2018
.
These phenomenona are robust against all kinds of perturbations.
In this article, we will focus on the above effects, which can be well described using coarse geometry.

In this article, we consider the case of no internal symmetries (i.e., topological phases of type A in the Cartan classification, see
Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
), which corresponds to the use of the usual complex Roe algebras.
To encorporate time reversal, chiral and/or particle-hole symmetry, one uses real versions of Roe algebras and their KO-theory, as discussed in
Ewert and Meyer 2019
.
Many of the methods discussed below carry over to this setup without changes, but some aspects of the theory need a different treatment due to the presence of torsion in the KO-groups.
These more refined discussions are beyond the scope of this expository article.

I
General mathematical setup

In this section, we introduce the general non-commutative geometry setup for the description of topological phases used in this article.
The choice of a coarse geometric algebra of observables will be discussed later, in §
III
.

Consider a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
with a
Hamiltonian

H
, be which we a self-adjoint (possibly unbounded) operator on
\mathscr{H}
.
For simplicity, we also assume also that
H
is positive.

I.1 Definition
.

We say that
H
is an
insulator at energy level

E\in\mathbb{R}
, if
E
is not contained in the spectrum
\mathrm{spec}(H)
of
H
.
We write
p_{E}
for the orthogonal projection of
H
onto the spectral subspace of
\mathscr{H}
corresponding to the spectrum of
H
below
E
.

In physics, this value
E
is typically called the
Fermi energy
of the physical system whose dynamics are described by
H
and we will refer to
p_{E}
as the
Fermi projection
.

Let now
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathrm{B}(\mathscr{H})
be a
C^{*}
-algebra of bounded operators on
\mathscr{H}
, which we interpret as the algebra of observables of the system.
We require that
H
is compatible with
\mathcal{A}
in the sense that for each compactly supported continuous function
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, the operator
f(H)
(formed using functional calculus) is contained in
\mathcal{A}
.
Observe that if
H
is bounded, this is equivalent to requiring
H\in\mathcal{A}
.
In general, this assumption ensures that if
H
is insulating at energy level
E
, then the spectral projection
p_{E}
is contained in
\mathcal{A}
; indeed, since
H
has a spectral gap at
E
and is positive, we can write
p_{E}=f(H)
for
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.
2
2

2

While we always have
p_{E}=f(H)
for
f
the (compactly supported) indicator function of
[0,E]
, the point here is that the spectral gap allows to choose
f
to be continuous. This is important as the observable algebra
\mathcal{A}
typically does not contain the operators
f(H)
for non-continuous
f
.

I.2 Definition
.

We say that
H
is a
topological insulator
if the corresponding class
[p_{E}]\in K_{0}(\mathcal{A})
in
C^{*}
-algebra K-theory is non-trivial.

We recall here that the K-theory group
K_{0}(\mathcal{A})
consists of formal differences of homotopy classes of projections in matrix algebras over the unitalization of
\mathcal{A}
; see, e.g.,
Wegge-Olsen 1993
or
Blackadar 1998
for an introduction to the K-theory of operator algebras.

I.3 Remark
.

More precisely, Definition
I.2
is actually that of a
type A topological insulator
(i.e., a topological insulator with
no
internal symmetries) and has to be modified in the presence of internal symmetries.
In particular, in the presence of time reversal symmetry, the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
will be real and one has to use KO-theory instead of K-theory.
While these groups can be generally described by unitaries and projections with certain additional symmetries
Boersema and Loring 2016
, it may then be convenient to use other descriptions of K-theory, e.g. that of van Daele, as advocated for in
Joseph and Meyer 2023
.

I.4 Example
.

In applications, the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
is typically either the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of square-integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or – in the so-called
tight-binding approximation
– its discretization
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
In the first case, typical examples of Hamiltonians are magnetic Schrödinger operators,

(I.1)

H=(d-iA)^{*}(d-iA),

where
A\in\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a magnetic potential.
In the second case, the Hamiltonian
H
is typically bounded, given by an infinite matrix
(H_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, which typically has the
finite propagation
property that there exists
r>0
such that
H_{ij}=0
whenever
|i-j|\geq r
.
In other words, only near sites interact.
Schrödinger operators with bounded potential can also be considered; see,
(
Ewert and Meyer 2019
, Prop. 2.5)
.

While the Hilbert space and the Hamiltonian are typically prescribed by the experiment, the algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a choice one has to make.
This choice should be made according to the properties of the Hamiltonian and depends on the physical phenoma one seeks to describe.
In the coarse geometric approach to topological insulators,
\mathcal{A}
is typically taken to be the
Roe algebra
of the underlying space; see §
III
.

II
Connection to Bloch theory

In this section, we compare the approach from §
I
to the classical approach to topological insulators using Bloch theory.
Here the Hamiltonian
H
is assumed to be periodic, in the sense that it commutes with lattice translations.

Let us for simplicity use a the discrete model, where
\mathscr{H}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Then by Fourier transform, the Hamiltonian
H
corresponds to a smooth matrix-valued function
\hat{H}:T^{d}\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
on the torus
T^{d}
(the so-called
Brillouin zone
).
The spectrum of
H
is just the union of all
\mathrm{spec}(\hat{H}(\mathbf{k}))
,
\mathbf{k}\in T^{d}
, hence a spectral gap of
H
at
E\in\mathbb{R}
implies that none of the matrices
H(\mathbf{k})
has eigenvalue
E
.
Therefore the vector spaces

V^{E}_{\mathbf{k}}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\leq E}\mathrm{Eig}(H(\mathbf{k}),\lambda),\qquad\mathbf{k}\in T^{d},

glue together to a smooth vector bundle
V^{E}
over
T^{d}
(the
Bloch bundle
of
H
), and topological insulators are those having non-trivial Bloch bundle.
While this non-triviality can be measured using topological invariants such as characteristic numbers, the K-theory class
[V^{E}]\in K^{0}(T^{d})
should be considered as the more fundamental invariant.
3
3

3

Recall that two vector bundles
V
and
W
define the same class in
K_{0}(X)
if they become isomorphic after taking the direct sum with a trivial vector bundle on both sides.
This stabilization corresponds in physics to adding several trivial “bands”.

These classical considerations correspond to taking
\mathcal{A}=C^{*}_{r}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\otimes M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
in the mathematical setup of §
I
, where
C^{*}_{r}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
is the (reduced) group
C^{*}
-algebra of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\mathcal{A}
acts on
\mathscr{H}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
via the left regular representation and matrix multiplication.
Namely, it is well known that the topological K-theory group
K^{0}(T^{d})
is isomorphic to the
C^{*}
-algebra K-theory group of the algebra
C(T^{d})
of continuous functions on the torus.
This
C^{*}
-algebra in turn is (via Fourier transform), isomorphic to
C^{*}_{r}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
.
The corresponding isomorphism

K^{0}(T^{d})\cong K_{0}(C(T^{d}))\cong K_{0}(C^{*}_{r}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}))

sends the class
[V^{E}]
of the Bloch bundle to the class defined by the Fermi projection
p_{E}
.

As pointed out by Belissard
Belissard 1986
, the assumption of periodicity on
H
is only an idealization, which can never be satisfied in physical practice.
So even if the model Hamiltonian of interest is periodic, it is crucial for
\mathcal{A}
to contain sufficiently small non-periodic perturbations of
H
.
This persistence is usually taken for granted in the physics literature.
In reality, only certain ”strong” invariants from periodic models can survive non-periodic perturbations, and we shall explain how Roe algebras provide the mathematical basis to understand this.

III
Roe algebras

In this section, we introduce Roe algebras, which are the observable algebra of choice for this article.
General references for this section are
(
Higson and Roe 2000
, §6.3)
,
(
Willett and Yu 2020
, §5)
or
(
Roe 1996
, §3)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
By an
X
-module
, we mean a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
together with an action of the
C^{*}
-algebra
C_{0}(X)
of continuous functions on
X
vanishing at infinity.
We say that
\mathscr{H}
is
ample
if
f\in C_{0}(X)
acts as a compact operator only when
f=0
.

III.1 Example
.

In the case that
X
is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or more generally, a Riemannian manifold, the reader should think of the
X
-module
\mathscr{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with the usual action of
C_{0}(X)
by multiplication.
On the other hand, for
X
a discrete metric space, the space
\mathscr{H}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
is
not
ample.
However, one may tensor with some auxiliary “coefficient” Hilbert space to obtain an ample
X
-module.
In physics, this amounts to increasing internal degrees of freedom.

By the spectral theorem for commutative
C^{*}
-algebras
(
Rudin 1974
, Thm. 12.22)
, the action of
C_{0}(X)
extends to an action of the algebra of bounded Borel functions on
\mathscr{H}
(see also
(
Willett and Yu 2020
, Proposition 1.6.11)
).
In particular, for any Borel subset of
W\subset X
the indicator function
\chi_{W}
acts as a projection operator on
\mathscr{H}
.
Throughout, for
f
a bounded Borel function on
X
, we also denote by
f
the operator on
\mathscr{H}
provided by the action.

An operator
T\in\mathrm{B}(\mathscr{H})
is
locally compact
, if
\chi_{W}T
and
T\chi_{W}
are compact for all bounded subsets
W\subseteq X
.

T
is called of
finite propagation
if there exists
r\geq 0
such that
\chi_{V}T\chi_{W}=0
whenever
d(V,W)>r
.

III.2 Definition
.

Let
\mathscr{H}
be an ample
X
-module.
The
Roe algebra

C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
is the norm-closure in
\mathrm{B}(\mathscr{H})
of the subalgebra of all locally compact, finite propagation operators on
\mathscr{H}
.

We do not mention the
X
-module
\mathscr{H}
in notation as the isomorphism type of
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
is independent of the choice of the ample
X
-module
\mathcal{H}
.
Precisely, if
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are two different ample
X
-modules, then the isomorphism of Roe algebras is implemented by a unitary transformation
U:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
; see
(
Higson and Roe 2000
, Prop. 6.3.12)
or
(
Ewert and Meyer 2019
, Theorem 2.1)
.

III.3 Example
.

The Roe algebra depends only on the large scale structure of the space
X
.
In particular, for any bounded space
X
, the finite propagation property is irrelevant, hence in this case,
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)=K(\mathscr{H})
, the algebra of compact operators on
\mathscr{H}
.

A subset
Y\subset X
is called
coarsely dense
if there exists
r>0
such that
B_{r}(Y)=X
; here
B_{r}(Y)
denotes the set of
x\in X
such that
d(x,y)\leq r
for some
y\in Y
.
The Roe algebra of
X
is isomorphic to the Roe algebra of any coarsely dense subset
(
Ewert and Meyer 2019
, Thm. 2.7)
, where the isomorphism is again implemented by a unitary isomorphism of the underlying Hilbert spaces.
For example the inclusion
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a coarse equivalence, which allows easy comparison of discretized and continuum models.
In particular, we have

(III.1)

K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X))=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&\text{if $d$ is even}\\
0&\text{if $d$ is odd}\end{cases}

for
X
either
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
More generally, the Roe algebra of two
coarsely equivalent
spaces is isomorphic; see Definition 2.8 and Thm. 2.9 of
Ewert and Meyer 2019
.

III.4 Remark
.

One can define the Roe algebra for the more general class of
bornological coarse spaces
.
We refer to the entry on “Coarse geometry” in this encyclopedia for a detailed account; see also
Bunke and Engel 2020
.

We now discuss localized Roe algebras, which are subalgebras of the Roe algebras supported near a subset.
Here it is convenient to use the following notion, which was introduced in
(
Bunke and Engel 2020
, Definition 3.2)
.

III.5 Definition
.

A
big family
in
X
is a collection
\mathcal{Y}
of subsets of
X
such that for
Y_{1},Y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
r>0
, there exists
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
with
B_{r}(Y_{1})\cup B_{r}(Y_{2})\subseteq Y^{\prime}
.

In particular, for any subset
Y\subset X
, we have the big family

(III.2)

\{Y\}:=\{Z\subset X\mid\exists r>0:Z\subseteq B_{r}(Y)\},

comprising all uniform thickenings of
Y
.
Given a big family
\mathcal{Y}
in
X
, we say that
T\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
is
supported in

\mathcal{Y}
, if there exists
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
fT=Tf=0
for all
f
with support in
X\setminus Y
.

III.6 Definition
.

Given a big family
\mathcal{Y}
, the
Roe algebra localized at
\mathcal{Y}
, denoted by
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathcal{Y})
, is the subalgebra of
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
obtained by taking the closure of all operators supported in
\mathcal{Y}
.

For any big family
\mathcal{Y}
, the localized Roe algebra
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathcal{Y})
is a two-sided ideal in
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
.
For subsets
Y\subseteq X
, the K-theory of
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})
coincides with that of
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(Y)
(taken without reference to the ambient space
X
); see the proof of Thm. 9.2 in
Roe 1996
.
It is straightforward to see that the intersection

\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}:=\{Y\cap Y^{\prime}\mid Y\in\mathcal{Y},Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\}

of two big families
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
is always a big family and we have

(III.3)

C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathcal{Y})\cap C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})=C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}).

Finally, we discuss equivariant Roe algebras; see
(
Willett and Yu 2020
, §5.2)
for a general reference.
Suppose that a countable discrete group
\Gamma
acts properly on a metric space
X
by isometries and let
\mathscr{H}
be an
X
-module with a compatible unitary representation
U:\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(\mathscr{H})
, meaning that
\gamma^{*}f=U_{\gamma^{-1}}fU_{\gamma}
for all
f\in C_{0}(X)
and all
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

III.7 Definition
.

The
equivariant Roe algebra

C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)^{\Gamma}
, is the norm closure of the algebra of all
\Gamma
-equivariant locally compact operators of finite propagation on
\mathscr{H}
.

If the group action is such that the quotient
X/\Gamma
is compact, then the choice of any bounded fundamental domain
F\subset X
for the
\Gamma
-action yields an isomorphism

C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)\cong C^{*}_{r}(\Gamma)\otimes\mathrm{K}(\mathscr{H}|_{F}),

where
\mathscr{H}|_{F}=\chi_{F}\mathscr{H}
denotes the restriction of the
X
-module to
F
and
\mathrm{K}(\mathscr{H}|_{F})
denotes the corresponding algebra of compact operators
(
Willett and Yu 2020
, §5.2)
.
As tensoring with the latter does not change K-theory, the inclusion
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)^{\Gamma}\hookrightarrow C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
induces a map

(III.4)

K_{0}(C^{*}_{r}(\Gamma))\longrightarrow K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)).

In the example
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
d=2m
even, this results in a map

(III.5)

\underbrace{K^{0}(T^{d})}_{\mathbb{Z}^{2^{d-1}}}\cong K_{0}(C^{*}_{r}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}))\longrightarrow\underbrace{K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X))}_{\mathbb{Z}}

which sends the complex vector bundle with
m
-th Chern class equal to the generator of
H^{d}(T^{d},\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
to the generator of the right hand side.

As a non-trivial class
[p_{E}]
on the left of (
III.5
) may become trivial on the right, we observe that not all periodically-topological phenomena are tractable using Roe algebras.
However, when
[p_{E}]
defines a non-trivial element in the K-theory of the Roe algebra (which happens, e.g., for
strong
topological insulators, in physics terminology), the coarse geometric approach is viable.

IV
Example Hamiltonians

In this section, we discuss examples of topological insulators (see Definition
I.2
) where the observable algebra
\mathscr{A}
is the Roe algebra of the underlying space.
In view of (
III.5
) many non-trivial examples arise from periodic Hamiltonian on lattice systems.
Concretely, let
H=(H_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
H_{ij}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, be a lattice-periodic Hamiltonian on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
; in other words,
H\in C^{*}_{r}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\otimes M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

The 2-dimensional case is of particular interest, relating to the quantum Hall effect; particular model Hamiltonian have been given by Haldane
(
Haldane 1988
, Eq. (1))
; see also Kane and Mele
(
Kane and Mele 2005
, Eq. (6))
.
A model Hamiltonian for a 2-dimensional gyroscopic metamaterial has been introduced in
(
Nash et al. 2015
, Eq. (2))
.

IV.1 Example
.

A simple toy Hamiltonian that works in any even dimension was suggested in
(
Prodan and Schulz-Baldes 2016
, Eq. (2.24))
.
It acts on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d/2}
and is given by

H=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(S_{j}-S_{j}^{*})\otimes\gamma_{j}+\Biggl(m+\sum_{j=1}^{d}(S_{j}+S_{j}^{*})\Biggr)\otimes\gamma_{0},

where
S_{j}
denotes the shift in the
j
-th coordinate direction and
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d}
are Clifford algebra generators and
\gamma_{0}=i^{d/2}\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{d}
.
If
m\notin\{-n,-n+2,\dots,n-2,n\}
, this Hamiltonian has a spectral gap at zero and hence defines a class
[p_{H}]\in K_{0}(C^{*}_{r}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}))
and, by (
III.4
), in
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
.
One can show that this class is non-trivial if
|m|<n

Joseph and Meyer 2023
.

We now give examples for topological insulators in continuum models, where the Hilbert space is
\mathscr{H}=L^{2}(X)
for a 2-dimensional Riemannian manifold
X
, with observable algebra
\mathcal{A}=C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
.
An interesting class of examples is the Landau Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{Lan}}
.
This is by definition the magnetic Schrödinger operator (
I.1
) for a magnetic potential
A
such that

dA=b\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{X}
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, where
\mathrm{vol}_{X}
is the Riemannian volume form of
X
.

IV.2 Example
.

In the case of
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the flat metric, one can show that the spectrum of the Landau Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{Lan}}
is quantized, given by

(IV.1)

\mathrm{spec}(H_{\mathrm{Lan}})=\{(2n-1)|b|\mid n=1,2,3,\dots\}.

Here each of the eigenvalues (called
Landau levels
) is infinitely degenerate.
One can show that the spectral projection onto each eigenspace of
H_{\mathrm{Lan}}
is a generator of
K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))

(
Ludewig and Thiang 2021
, §2.3 & Thm. 3)
.

IV.3 Example
.

For
X=\mathbb{H}^{2}
, the hyperbolic space, the Landau Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{Lan}}
has the finite set of eigenvalues

(2n-1)|b|-n(n-1),\quad n=1,\dots,n_{\max}<|b|+\frac{1}{2},

each of infinite multiplicity, as well as continuous spectrum
[\frac{1}{4}+b^{2},\infty)
, see
Comtet and Houston 1985
.
One can show that similar to (
III.1
), we have
K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathbb{H}^{2}))\cong\mathbb{Z}
and that the spectral projection onto each eigenspace of
H_{\mathrm{Lan}}
is a generator
(
Ludewig and Thiang 2021
, Lemma 3.1 & Thm. 3)
.

IV.4 Example
.

Example
IV.2
can be generalized to the situation where
X
is a complete Riemannian surface with not necessarily constant scalar curvature.
We may consider a magnetic Hamiltonian (
I.1
) with magnetic potential
A
satisfying
dA=\theta\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{X}
for some smooth function
\theta
such that
\theta-b
vanishes at infinity, where
b\neq 0
is a constant.
Then under suitable assumptions on
X
(non-vanishing of the
coarse index of the Dirac operator
; see the article “Coarse geometry”), the essential spectrum of
H
is again the the discrete set (
IV.1
), and for each essential eigenvalue, the corresponding eigenprojection is a generator for
K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X))

(
Kubota 2021
, Prop. 3.20)
.

V
Bulk-Boundary correspondence

So far, we only discussed the “bulk” of the material, i.e., its idealized, boundaryless version.
We now turn to investigating the behavior of a topological insulator at the boundary.

Let
X
be a metric space (which we view as the “bulk” of our material), and let
\mathscr{H}
be an ample
X
-module.
Let moreover
H
be a Hamiltonian on
\mathscr{H}
satisfying the assumptions of §
I
with respect to
\mathcal{A}=C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
, i.e., we assume that
f(H)
is contained in the Roe algebra of
X
for each
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.

For a subset
Y\subset X
, denote by

(V.1)

\partial Y:=\{Y\}\cap\{X\setminus Y\}

the
coarse boundary
of
Y
, a big family on
X
.
We then have an exact 6-term sequence of K-theory groups

(V.2)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\}))\oplus K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{X\setminus Y\}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\}))\oplus K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{X\setminus Y\}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial}

called the
coarse Meyer-Vietoris sequence

Higson et al. 1993
.

V.1 Remark
.

The statement here is slightly more general then the statement obtained in §5 of
Higson et al. 1993
, which requires the notion of
coarse transversality
, see
(
Ewert and Meyer 2019
, §3.4)
.
The result in the form stated above is essentially Prop. 8.82 of
Bunke and Engel 2020
.
It follows from Lemma 1 of
Higson et al. 1993
(see also Prop. 3.6 of
Ewert and Meyer 2019
), by observing that

C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})\cap C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{X\setminus Y\})=C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y),

by (
III.3
).

V.2 Definition
.

We say that for a subset
Y\subseteq X
, the
bulk-boundary correspondence holds
if the Mayer-Vietoris boundary map

(V.3)

\partial:K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X))\longrightarrow K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y))

is an isomorphism.

Validity of the bulk-boundary correspondence implies that non-triviality of a K-theory class determined by the Fermi projection
p_{E}
of a Hamiltonian
H
that is insulating at
E
can be detected at the (coarse) boundary.
Actually, this is already true if the map (
V.3
) injective.

V.3 Remark
.

In the physical literature, a topological insulator is often
defined
as a phase with particular properties of the boundary.
From the mathematical point of view, this is not necessarily equivalent to the definition via non-triviality in the bulk: The topological non-triviality may be detected at the boundary if the bulk-boundary correspondence holds, but this may fail in general.

V.4 Theorem
.

If
Y
and
X\setminus Y
are both
flasque
, then bulk-boundary correspondence holds.

See
(
Roe 1996
, Def. 9.3)
for the general definition of the flasqueness property.
In particular, any half space in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is flasque, as well as any subspace coarsely equivalent to a half space.
Hence this result applies to a wide class of examples.

Proof of Thm.
V.4
.

The Roe algebra K-theory of flasque spaces vanishes
(
Roe 1996
, Prop. 9.4)
, so the result follows from exactness of (
V.2
).
∎

VI
Gap-filling

In this section, we discuss a coarse geometric
gap-filling result
, which says that for a topological insulator, the spectral gap of the bulk Hamiltonian closes upon introducing boundary.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian acting on an ample
X
-module
\mathscr{H}
which is compatible with the Roe algebra
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
defined using
\mathscr{H}
.

VI.1 Definition
.

Let
Y\subset X
be a subspace.
A
Hamiltonian on
Y
adapted to

H
is a self-adjoint (possibly unbounded) operator on
\mathscr{H}
such that for all
f\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
f(\tilde{H})\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})
and
f(H)-f(\tilde{H})\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{X\setminus Y\})
.

VI.2 Example
.

If
H
is bounded, so that
H\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
, then the contraction
\tilde{H}=\chi_{Y}H\chi_{Y}
of
H
to
Y
satisfies these requirements.

VI.3 Example
.

If
X
is a complete Riemannian manifold,
\mathscr{H}=L^{2}(X)
,
Y\subset X
an open subset and
H
a magnetic Schrödinger operator as in (
I.1
), we can take
\tilde{H}
to be the restriction of
H
to
Y
with Dirichlet boundary conditions (i.e., the closure of the essentially self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(Y)
with domain
C^{\infty}_{c}(Y)
, extended by zero to
L^{2}(X)
); see
(
Ludewig and Thiang 2021
, §1.4)
.
More general boundary conditions can be treated as well; see ibid., Remark 1.8.

VI.4 Theorem
.

Let
Y\subseteq X
and let
\tilde{H}
be a Hamiltonian on
Y
adapted to
H
.
Suppose that
H
is insulating at some energy level
E
and let
[p_{E}]\in K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X))
be the class of the corresponding fermi projection.
Then if

\partial([p_{E}])\neq 0\in K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)),

we have
E\in\mathrm{spec}(\tilde{H})
.

We recall that elements of the K-theory group
K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y))
are homotopy classes of unitaries in matrix algebras over the unitalization
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)^{+}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
E\notin\mathrm{spec}(\tilde{H})
, in other words
E
is in the resolvent set of both
H
and
\tilde{H}
.
Then since the resolvent set of an operator is open, we can write
p_{E}=f(H)
for some
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f(\lambda)\in\{0,1\}
whenever
\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(\tilde{H})\cup\mathrm{spec}(H)
.
Then
\tilde{p}_{E}:=f(\tilde{H})\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})
is also a projection.

By the isomorphism

C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})/C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)\cong C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)/C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{X\setminus Y\}),

we see that the quotient algebra in the short exact sequence

(VI.1)

C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)\longrightarrow C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\longrightarrow}}C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})/C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y).

receives a “restriction” homomorphism

(VI.2)

r:C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)\longrightarrow C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})/C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y).

As
\tilde{H}
is adapted to
H
, we have
\pi(\tilde{p}_{E})=r(p_{E})
in
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})/C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)
.
We consider the six-term sequence in K-theory of this short exact sequence.
Recall that generally, the boundary map

\mathrm{Exp}:K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})/C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y))\to K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y))

of such a six-term sequence is given by

(VI.3)

\mathrm{Exp}([q])=[\exp(2\pi i\tilde{q})],

where
\tilde{q}\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})
is a self-adjoint lift of the projection
q
(see
(
Blackadar 1998
, 9.3.2)
).
In particular,
\mathrm{Exp}([q])=0
if a lift
\tilde{q}
can be found that is a projection.
We therefore conclude that
\mathrm{Exp}([r(p_{E})])=0
, since
\tilde{p}_{E}
is a projection that lifts
r(p_{E})=\pi(\tilde{p}_{E})
.
On the other hand, the Meyer-Vietoris boundary map is precisely the composition

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)/C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{X\setminus Y\}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})/C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Exp}}

see the proof of
(
Ewert and Meyer 2019
, Prop. 3.6)
.
We conclude that
\partial([p_{E}])=\mathrm{Exp}([r(p_{E})])=0
.
∎

VI.5 Example
.

The K-theoretic exponential map has also been used to show gap-filling results in
Thiang 2020
;
Ludewig and Thiang 2022b
;
Kubota et al. 2022
;
Ludewig and Thiang 2021
.
To the author’s knowledge, this kind of argument was first used in
Schulz-Baldes et al. 2000
.

VI.6 Example
.

Let
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a half space (or any open subset coarsely equivalent to a half space) and consider
H=H_{\mathrm{Lan}}
, the Landau Hamiltonian; see §
IV
.
Then if
E
lies between the
n
-th and the
(n+1)
-st Landau level, then
[p_{E}]\in K_{0}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X))\cong\mathbb{Z}
is
n
times a generator, and by Thm.
V.4
, bulk boundary correspondence holds, so that
\partial([p_{E}])\neq 0
.
We obtain that the Landau Hamiltonian on
Y
(with, say Dirichlet boundary conditions) has no spectral gaps.

VI.7 Example
.

Using Thm.
VI.4
, one can also show that the Landau Hamiltonian on coarse half spaces
Y
of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
has no spectral gaps, even though such subspaces
Y
are not flasque; see
Ludewig and Thiang 2021
.

Summarizing, it is a feature of the coarse geometric setup that one may obtain the above gap-filling results for a wide variety of subspaces
Y\subseteq X
, without having to delve into peculiarities of the involved
C^{*}
-algebras (as, e.g., in
Thiang 2020
).
Calculating the spectrum of
\tilde{H}
explicitly is typically impossible for subspaces
Y
other than the perfect half space.

VII
Edge-traveling

In this section, we give a result on how to detect non-triviality of the class
\partial([p_{E}])
, as required in Thm.
VI.4
in two-dimensional topological insulators.
To this end, we want to construct a homomorphism
K_{1}(\partial Y)\to\mathbb{Z}

that allows us to detect non-triviality of an abstract K-theory class.
As it turns out, this is closely related to edge-traveling phenomena.
The presentation roughly follows
Ludewig and Thiang 2022b
.

Let
X
be a metric proper metric space and let

VII.1 Lemma
.

For any Borel subset
W\subseteq X
and any

[\chi_{W},T]\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)

\partial W

Proof.

If

\partial W

The statement for general

VII.3 Lemma
.

For any unitary
u
in
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)^{+}\otimes M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, the operator

F_{u}:=\chi_{W}u\chi_{W}+\chi_{X\setminus W}

is a Fredholm operator on
\mathscr{H}
.

Proof.

We have

(VII.2)

F_{u}F_{u^{*}}=1+\chi_{W}u[\chi_{W},u^{*}]\chi_{W}.

By Lemma
VII.1
(which clearly also holds for
T
in the unitalization
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)^{+}
, as well as matrix algebras over it), the commutator
[\chi_{W},u^{*}]
is contained in
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y\cap\partial W)
.
But since
\partial Y\cap\partial W
is bounded, we have
C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y\cap\partial W)=K(\mathscr{H})
the algebra of compact operators on
\mathscr{H}
.
Equation (
VII.2
) therefore shows that
F_{u}
is invertible up to a compact perturbation, hence Fredholm.
∎

It is now easy to check that the map sending a unitary
u\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)^{+}\otimes M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
to the index of the Fredholm operator
F_{u}
depends only on the equivalence class of
u
in
K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y))
and yields a well-defined group homomorphism

\displaystyle\theta_{W}:K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y))\to\mathbb{Z},\qquad[u]\mapsto\mathrm{index}(F_{u}).

In other words,
\theta_{W}
gives a possibility to “measure” the non-triviality of abstract
K_{1}
-classes.
One can show that if one replaces
W
by
W^{\prime}
with the property that both

\partial Y\cap\{W\}\cap\{X\setminus W^{\prime}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial Y\cap\{W^{\prime}\}\cap\{X\setminus W\}

are bounded, then
\theta_{W}=\theta_{W^{\prime}}
.
This property of
\theta_{W}
is called
cobordism invariance
in
(
Ludewig and Thiang 2022b
, §4)
.

VII.4 Example
.

For the choice of
Y
and
W
from Example
VII.2
, one can check that the map
\theta_{W}
is an isomorphism
(
Ludewig and Thiang 2022b
, §5.2)
.
In particular, this implies that

(VII.3)

K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)\cong K_{1}(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathbb{R}))\cong\mathbb{Z}

which can be abstractly checked using the Mayer-Vietoris sequence (
V.2
), by partitioning
\mathbb{R}
into two rays, whose Roe algebra K-theory vanishes by flasqueness.
Combining the isomorphism (
VII.3
) with the fact that the Mayer-Vietoris boundary map is an isomorphism in this case (Thm.
V.4
), we obtain the isomorphism

(VII.4)

K_{0}\bigl(C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\bigr)\cong\mathbb{Z}

from (
III.1
).

VII.5 Remark
.

The subsets
Y
and
W
from Example
VII.2
give a
multi-partition
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in the sense of
Schick and Zadeh 2018
.
The isomorphism from (
VII.4
) is then a simple example for the isomorphism of Thm. 1.4 ibid.

A version of the following lemma can be found in the reference
(
Ludewig and Thiang 2022b
, Lemma 6.7)
.

VII.6 Lemma
.

Let
u\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)^{+}\otimes M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a unitary.
Then we have the “Kubo formula”

(VII.5)

\theta_{W}([u])=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(u[\chi_{W},u^{*}]\bigr),

provided that the operator on the right hand side is trace-class.

Proof.

With a view on (
VII.2
), the trace-class assumption on
u[\chi_{W},u^{*}]
implies that
F_{u}F_{u}^{*}-1
is trace-class.
By Calderon’s formula (see, e.g.,
(
Roe 1988
, Lemma 4.1)
), we therefore have

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(F_{u})=\mathrm{Tr}(F_{u}F_{u^{*}}-F_{u^{*}}F_{u})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\chi_{W}u\chi_{W}u^{*}\chi_{W}-\chi_{W}u^{*}\chi_{W}u\chi_{W})

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\chi_{W}u(1-\chi_{W})u^{*}-\chi_{W}u^{*}(1-\chi_{W})u\bigr),

using cyclicity of the trace.
To continue, observe that

[\chi_{W},u^{*}]=\chi_{W}u^{*}(1-\chi_{W})-(1-\chi_{W})u^{*}\chi_{W}.

Because the composition of the two terms on the right hand side in any order is zero, they must be individually trace-class, as
u[\chi_{W},u]
(and hence also
[\chi_{W},u^{*}]
) is trace-class.
We may therefore pull apart the trace above, to obtain

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\chi_{W}u(1-\chi_{W})u^{*}\bigr)+\underbrace{\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\chi_{W}u^{*}(1-\chi_{W})u\bigr)}_{=\mathrm{Tr}((1-\chi_{W})u\chi_{W}u^{*})}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\chi_{W}u(1-\chi_{W})u^{*}-(1-\chi_{W})u\chi_{W}u^{*}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(u[\chi_{W},u^{*}]).

Here the underbraced identity follows again from cyclicity of the trace.
∎

We now give a physical interpretation of the functional
\theta_{W}
.
We treat the case that the Hamiltonian is bounded.
Hence let
H\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(X)
be an insulator at energy level
E
(Definition
I.1
) and let
p_{E}
be the corresponding Fermi projection.
Let
\tilde{H}\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\{Y\})
be any Hamiltonian on
Y
adapted to
H
(Definition
VI.1
).

VII.7 Theorem
.

Let
H
be insulating at energy level
E
with Fermi projection
p_{E}
.
Let
(a,b)\ni E
be an open interval disjoint from the spectrum of
H
and let
\varphi
be a continuous function with integral one and compact support in
(a,b)
.
Then

\theta_{W}\bigl(\partial([p_{E}])\bigr)=-2\pi\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\varphi(\tilde{H})\cdot i[\tilde{H},\chi_{W}]\bigr),

provided that the trace on the right hand side exists.
Here
\partial
is the Mayer-Vietoris boundary map (
V.3
).

Similar formula can be found in
Elbau and Graf 2002
;
Kellendonk et al. 2002
; see also
(
Prodan and Schulz-Baldes 2016
, Prop. 7.1.2)
.
§6 of
Ludewig and Thiang 2022b
treats the case where
H
is a magnetic Schrödinger operator (
I.1
), where one obtains a similar formula.
Below we give a rather simple proof of Thm.
VII.7
.

VII.8 Physical interpretation
.

When considering the boundary states of
\tilde{H}
with energies lying in
(a,b)
, the term
i[\tilde{H},\chi_{W}]
is the time-derivative of the observable
\chi_{W}
of being in
W
, by Heisenberg’s equation of motion.
We interpret
\varphi(\tilde{H})
as a statistical ensemble of generalized eigenstates of
\tilde{H}
with energies within
(a,b)
.
So
\mathrm{Tr}(\varphi(\tilde{H})\cdot i[\tilde{H},\chi_{W}])
is the expected rate of change of probability to be inside
W
, within the statistical ensemble
\varphi(\tilde{H})
of boundary localized states.
Because this expectation value is
(2\pi)^{-1}
times some Fredholm index, we deduce, a posteriori, that
the
(a,b)
-filling boundary states of
\tilde{H}
constitute a quantized current channel flowing between
W
and its complement.

Proof of Thm.
VII.7
.

Let
\lambda_{0}
be a lower bound on the spectrum of both
H
and
\tilde{H}
and let
f\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f^{\prime}(\lambda)=-\varphi(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
.
Then by choice of
\varphi
, we have
p_{E}=f(H)
.
Recall from the proof of Thm.
VI.4
that
\partial([p_{E}])=\Exp(r([p_{E}]))
, where
r
is the restriction homomorphism (
VI.2
) and
\Exp
is the boundary map for the short exact sequence (
VI.1
).
By the explicit formula (
VI.3
) for
\Exp
and the fact that
\tilde{H}
is adapted, we therefore have

\partial([p_{E}])=\partial([f(H)])=\Exp(r([f(H)]))=[u],

where

(VII.6)

u=\exp\bigl(2\pi i\,f(\tilde{H})\bigr).

With a view on Lemma
VII.6
, we are now done if we can show that
u[\chi_{W},\tilde{u}]
is trace-class if and only if
2\pi\cdot\varphi(\tilde{H})\cdot i[\chi_{W},\tilde{H}]
is, and that they have the same trace if they are trace-class.
If
[\chi_{W},\tilde{H}]
would commute with
\tilde{H}
, then this would just follow from the chain rule (as taking the commutator with
\chi_{W}
is a derivation on the algebra of bounded operators on
\mathscr{H}
).
To establish this in general, we have to be careful.

Suppose that
u[\chi_{W},u^{*}]
is trace-class, so that (
VII.5
) holds.
In a first step, with a view on (
VII.6
), we use the formula

(VII.7)

[\chi_{W},e^{a}]=\int_{0}^{1}e^{(1-t)a}[\chi_{W},a]e^{ta}dt

with
a=-2\pi i\,f(\tilde{H})
, as well as cyclicity of the trace to obtain that

\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(u[\chi_{W},u^{*}]\bigr)=-2\pi i\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\bigl([\chi_{W},f(\tilde{H})]\bigr).

Next, we use the identity

(VII.8)

f(\tilde{H})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(s)e^{is\tilde{H}}ds,

valid for any Schwartz function of
\tilde{H}
, where
\hat{f}
is the Fourier transform of
f
.
By (
VII.7
) again, this time with
a=is\tilde{H}
, we get

\displaystyle[\chi_{W},f(\tilde{H})]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{f}(s)[\chi_{W},e^{is\tilde{H}}]ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\widehat{f^{\prime}}(s)e^{is(1-t)\tilde{H}}[\chi_{W},\tilde{H}]e^{ist\tilde{H}}dtds,

where we employed the identity
is\widehat{f}(s)=\widehat{f^{\prime}}(s)
.
Taking the trace and using its cyclicity, the
t
-integral collapses and we can use (
VII.8
) backwards (for
f^{\prime}
instead of
f
) to obtain the desired identity, keeping in mind that
f^{\prime}=-\varphi
on the spectrum of
\tilde{H}
.

Conversely, tracing back the previous calculations, we see that the trace-class property of
\varphi(\tilde{H})[\tilde{H},\chi_{W}]
implies that of
u[\chi_{W},u^{*}]
.
∎

Observe that since
\varphi
is supported in the spectral gap of
H
, we have
\varphi(H)=0
.
The fact that
\tilde{H}
is adapted to
H
therefore implies that
\varphi(\tilde{H})\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial Y)
.
On the other hand, by Lemma
VII.1
,
[\tilde{H},\chi_{W}]\in C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\partial W)
.
So
\varphi(\tilde{H})\cdot i[\tilde{H},\chi_{W}]
is supported on
\partial Y\cap\partial W
which is bounded by the transversality assumption on
Y
and
W
; hence
\varphi(\tilde{H})\cdot i[\tilde{H},\chi_{W}]
is a compact operator.

We now give a sufficient criterion for this operator to be even trace-class, so that Thm.
VII.7
applies.
Suppose that
X=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with the
X
-module
\mathscr{H}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
for some coefficient Hilbert space
\mathscr{K}
.
Let
H=(H_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, be a Hamiltonian with rapidly decaying coefficients, meaning that for each
\mu\geq 0
, one has

(VII.9)

\sup_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|H_{ij}\|\cdot d(i,j)^{\mu}<\infty.

for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Let
\tilde{H}=\chi_{Y}H\chi_{Y}
be the adapted Hamiltonian on
Y
from Example
VI.2
.
We assume that
Y
and
W
are the standard half spaces (
VII.1
).

VII.9 Theorem
.

Under the above assumptions, the operator
\varphi(\tilde{H})\cdot i[\tilde{H},\chi_{W}]
from Thm.
VII.7
is trace-class.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{rd}}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\subset C^{*}_{\mathrm{Roe}}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
be the
rapid decay algebra
consisting of operators whose matrix coefficients satisfy the decay property (
VII.9
).
As observed in
(
Ewert and Meyer 2019
, §2.16)
,
\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{rd}}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
is closed under smooth functional calculus for normal elements.
In particular,
\varphi(\tilde{H})\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{rd}}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
.
Using that
\varphi
is supported in the spectral gap of
H
, one can moreoever show that
\varphi(\tilde{H})
decays rapidly away from
\partial Y
, meaning that for each
Z\in\partial Y
and each
\mu\geq 0
, one has

(VII.10)

\sup_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|\varphi(\tilde{H})_{ij}\|\cdot d(i,Z)^{\mu}\cdot d(j,Z)^{\mu}<\infty.

Similarly,
[\chi_{W},\tilde{H}]
decays rapidly away from
\partial W
.
The product of these operators decays rapidly away from
\partial Y\cap\partial W
, which implies that the product is trace-class.
∎

Clearly, the above still holds if
Y
and
W
are modified in a bounded way, as this does not change the decay conditions (
VII.9
), (
VII.10
).
