An invitation to game comonads
Samson Abramsky, Luca Reggio

Abstract
. Game comonads offer a categorical view of a number of model-comparison games central to model theory, such as pebble and Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé games. Remarkably, the categories of coalgebras for these comonads capture preservation of several fragments of resource-bounded logics, such as (infinitary) first-order logic with n n variables or bounded quantifier rank, and corresponding combinatorial parameters such as tree-width and tree-depth. In this way, game comonads provide a new bridge between categorical methods developed for semantics, and the combinatorial and algorithmic methods of resource-sensitive model theory. We give an overview of this framework and outline some of its applications, including the study of homomorphism counting results in finite model theory, and of equi-resource homomorphism preservation theorems in logic using the axiomatic setting of arboreal categories. Finally, we describe some homotopical ideas that arise naturally in the context of game comonads.

1.
Introduction

The traditional divide in theoretical computer science between formal methods and semantics on the one hand, and algorithms and computational complexity on the other, runs deep, as can be seen e.g. in the division into two volumes of the
Handbook of theoretical computer science

(
van Leeuwen, 1990a
;
van Leeuwen, 1990b
)
. The first strand is exemplified by categorical semantics, while the second strand is exemplified by finite model theory and descriptive complexity. The aim of this survey article is to introduce the reader to the framework of game comonads, which bridges these two strands by bringing the tools of category theory to the field of finite and resource-sensitive model theory. This research programme was initiated in early 2017 and has been an active area of research since then.

1.1.
Finite and resource-sensitive model theory

Finite model theory
studies theories and their classes of
finite
models, and has sparked researchers’ interest since the early days of model theory, cf. e.g.
(
Trakhtenbrot, 1950
)
. It has flourished and grown rapidly into an independent field in the 1970s–80s thanks to its connections with complexity theory and database theory. Nowadays, finite model theory is a subfield of mathematical logic which beautifully exemplifies the convergence of mathematics and computer science, see e.g.
(
Libkin, 2004
;
Grädel et al., 2007
)
.
Many results of classical model theory fail when restricted to finite models. These include Gödel’s Completeness Theorem, stating that a sentence is true in all models of a theory
T
just when it can be proved from the axioms in
T
, and the Compactness Theorem, stating that
T
has a model just when every finite subset of
T
does. Model-theoretic tools can be used to study classes of finite models. This is the subject of
pseudo-finite model theory

(
Chatzidakis, 1997
;
Hrushovski, 2002
;
Väänänen, 2003
)
, but this approach inevitably leads to considering infinite models. For example, the theory of finite fields (i.e., the collection of all sentences true in all finite fields) has infinite models, the pseudo-finite fields.
On the other hand, finite model theory has developed its own methods, largely based on combinatorial and probabilistic techniques.

The deep link between finite model theory and complexity theory has its origin in Fagin’s theorem, showing that the problems in the complexity class NP are exactly those definable in existential second-order logic
(
Fagin, 1974
)
. This result initiated the subject of descriptive complexity, which classifies computational problems according to the logical language required to express them
(
Immerman, 1999
)
.
The idea of stratifying by
logical resources
, typically represented as complexity measures of formulas such as
quantifier rank
or
number of variables
,
has had great success and is central to finite model theory, see e.g.
(
Immerman, 1999
, Theorem 7.21 and Corollary 7.22)
for a stratification by number of blocks of second-order quantifiers. It is reflected in the use of
model-comparison games
, such as Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé
(
Ehrenfeucht, 1960
;
Fraïssé, 1954
)
or pebble games
(
Barwise, 1977
;
Immerman, 1982
)
, to determine if two models are equivalent in a given logic fragment. Here, the logical resources correspond to the resources available to the two players, Spoiler and Duplicator, such as the number of rounds or the number of pebbles (cf. §
3
).

This method, wherein logical resources are placed centre stage, applies to both finite and infinite structures, leading to what we shall call
resource-sensitive model theory
. It can also be thought of as “model theory without compactness”, since it avoids the use of the Compactness Theorem. This viewpoint is at the heart of most preservation and inexpressibility results in finite model theory, and it has also led to remarkable resource-sensitive refinements of classical results such as the Equirank Homomorphism Preservation Theorem
(
Rossman, 2008
)
; cf. §
9
.

1.2.
Game comonads

In 2017, a novel approach to logical resources based on
game comonads
, providing categorical accounts of key constructions in finite model theory, was initiated in
(
Abramsky
et al., 2017
;
Abramsky and Shah, 2018
)
.
The key insight is that model-comparison games can be naturally organised into endofunctors on categories of relational structures that carry the structure of comonads (these are the dual notion to monads, which are widely used in functional programming).
Intuitively, given a type of game
G
and a resource parameter
k
, the game comonad
\mathbb{G}_{k}
assigns to a structure
A
a new structure
\mathbb{G}_{k}A
which can be thought of as a
forest cover
(or forest-ordered decomposition) of
A
, abstracting the idea of
k
-unravelling
from modal logic. The universe of
\mathbb{G}_{k}A
is the set of all possible plays in the structure
A
in the game
G
in which the appropriate notion of resources is bounded by
k
. Thus, game comonads arise by viewing games not as external artefacts, but as semantic constructions in their own right. The first key observation is that Duplicator winning strategies for the existential
G
-game from
A
to
B
are recovered as Kleisli morphisms

\mathbb{G}_{k}A\to B.

Duplicator winning strategies for the back-and-forth game, and variants thereof, can also be recovered.
In this manner, the notion of local approximation built into the game is internalised into the category of relational structures.

Remarkably, this idea applies to a number of games central to finite model theory, leading e.g. to
Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé
and
pebbling comonads
, corresponding to Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé and pebble games, respectively.
In fact, game comonads cover a range of logics, including first-order, modal, guarded and hybrid logics, and logics with generalised quantifiers
(
Abramsky and Shah, 2021
;
Ó Conghaile and Dawar, 2021
;
Abramsky and
Marsden, 2021
;
Abramsky and
Marsden, 2022
)
. See
(
Abramsky, 2022b
)
for a survey.
In each case, the categories of coalgebras for the comonads encode, in a syntax-free fashion, important logical resources and combinatorial parameters of structures; cf. Figure
1
.

2.
Deception and logical resources

In logic, models are typically studied not as they really are, i.e. up to isomorphism, but only up to definable properties; e.g., two models are elementarily equivalent if they satisfy the same first-order sentences. This idea is pervasive in mathematics and computer science: we may not want to distinguish between two topological spaces that are homotopy equivalent, or two programs that on the same input give the same output.
From a logical viewpoint, every fragment
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathrm{FO}
of first-order logic induces an equivalence relation
\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}
on structures given by

A\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}B\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \text{for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{L}\ (A\models\varphi\ \Leftrightarrow\ B\models\varphi).

The relation
\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}
allows us to compare structures up to
\mathcal{L}
-definable properties.

When
\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{FO}
, the relation
\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}
coincides with elementary equivalence. As any two elementarily equivalent finite structures are isomorphic, the main focus in finite model theory is on relations
\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}
that are strictly coarser than elementary equivalence. This can be achieved, for instance, by considering resource-bounded fragments of first-order logic. Intuitively, given a resource parameter
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we will define a corresponding stratification of
\mathrm{FO}
, i.e. an increasing chain of logic fragments

\mathcal{L}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathrm{FO}

whose union is all of
\mathrm{FO}
. Typically, the equivalence relations
\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{k}}
are strictly coarser than
\equiv_{\mathrm{FO}}
, i.e. than elementary equivalence, but we recover the latter as

\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}{\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{k}}}\ =\ \ {\equiv_{\mathrm{FO}}}.

We illustrate two such stratifications of first-order logic, in which the resource parameters are given by the number of variables and the quantifier rank, respectively. We then outline a similar stratification in the case of modal logic. Throughout, we fix a countably infinite set of (pairwise distinct) first-order variables

x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots

from which all first-order formulas are built. Moreover, for simplicity, we shall restrict ourselves to the case of finite relational signatures, i.e. signatures
\sigma
containing finitely many relation symbols and constant symbols, but no function symbols.

2.1.
Finite-variable logic

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by

\mathrm{FO}^{n}

the set of all first-order sentences that only use the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. In particular,
\mathrm{FO}^{0}
consists of the atomic sentences (such as
c_{1}=c_{2}
,
S(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
and Boolean combinations thereof, where
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
are constant symbols and
S
is a relation symbol). Despite the severe syntactic restriction, the finite-variable fragments
\mathrm{FO}^{n}
are remarkably expressive. This expressiveness comes from the fact that variables can be
reused
. For example, for each integer
\ell\geq 1
there is a three-variable sentence
\varphi_{\ell}\in\mathrm{FO}^{3}
in the language of graphs expressing the existence of a path of length
\ell
. Such a
\varphi_{\ell}
can be defined as
\exists x_{1},x_{2}.\psi_{\ell}
, where the formula
\psi_{\ell}(x_{1},x_{2})
expresses the existence of a path of length
\ell
from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
and is defined as follows:

\psi_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\coloneqq R(x_{1},x_{2})

and, for all
\ell>1
,

\psi_{\ell}(x_{1},x_{2})\coloneqq\exists x_{3}.(R(x_{1},x_{3})\wedge\exists x_{1}.(x_{1}=x_{3}\wedge\psi_{\ell-1}(x_{1},x_{2}))).

2.2.
Bounded quantifier rank

The
quantifier rank
of a formula is the maximum number of nested quantifiers in the formula. For example, the formulas

R(x_{1},x_{2}),\ \ \exists x_{2}.R(x_{1},x_{2})\wedge\exists x_{3}.R(x_{3},x_{1})

have quantifier rank
0
and
1
, respectively, whereas the following has quantifier rank
2
:

\forall x_{2}\exists x_{3}.(R(x_{1},x_{3})\wedge(R(x_{1},x_{2})\to R(x_{3},x_{2}))).

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, write

\mathrm{FO}_{k}

for the collection of all first-order sentences of quantifier rank at most
k
. The following observation about the logic fragments
\mathrm{FO}_{k}
will be important in the following; see e.g.
(
Libkin, 2004
, Lemma 3.13)
for a proof. (Recall that we are considering only
finite
relational signatures.)

Lemma 2.1.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there are, up to logical equivalence, finitely many formulas of quantifier rank at most
k
.

In particular, the quotient of
\mathrm{FO}_{k}
with respect to logical equivalence is finite.
The analogous statement for the
n
-variable fragments
\mathrm{FO}^{n}
is false in general: for example, for any two distinct integers
\ell,m\geq 1
, the sentences
\varphi_{\ell},\varphi_{m}\in\mathrm{FO}^{3}
stating the existence of a path of length
\ell
and
m
, respectively, are not equivalent.

Remark 2.2.

We note in passing that logically equivalent formulas may have different quantifier rank or use a different number of variables. For example, the sentence

\exists x_{1},\dots,x_{4}.(R(x_{1},x_{2})\wedge R(x_{2},x_{3})\wedge R(x_{3},x_{4}))

uses
4
variables and has quantifier rank
4
; it expresses the existence of a path of length
3
, so it is equivalent to the sentence
\varphi_{3}=\exists x_{1},x_{2}.\psi_{3}
, which uses
3
variables and has quantifier rank
6
.

2.3.
Modal logic and modal depth

We consider modal logic as a fragment of first-order logic (in one free variable) via its standard translation. In more detail, let
\sigma
be a
modal signature
, i.e. a finite signature consisting of relation symbols of arity either
1
or
2
. Each unary relation symbol
{P\in\sigma}
yields a propositional variable
p
, and each binary relation symbol
R_{\alpha}\in\sigma
yields modalities
\Box_{\alpha}
and
\Diamond_{\alpha}
.
Then a
\sigma
-structure
A
corresponds to a Kripke structure for this
(multi-)
modal logic: the interpretation
P^{A}\subseteq A
gives the valuation for the propositional variable
p
, and
R_{\alpha}^{A}\subseteq A^{2}
gives the accessibility relation for
\Box_{\alpha}
and
\Diamond_{\alpha}
.

Each formula
\varphi
in this modal logic admits a translation into a first-order formula
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_{x}
in one free variable
x
; this is known as the
standard translation
, see e.g.
(
Blackburn
et al., 2002
, §2.4)
. We let
\llbracket p\rrbracket_{x}\coloneqq P(x)
and let
\llbracket-\rrbracket_{x}
commute with Boolean connectives. Further, set

\llbracket\Box_{\alpha}\varphi\rrbracket_{x}\coloneqq\forall y.\,R_{\alpha}(x,y)\to\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_{y}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \llbracket\Diamond_{\alpha}\varphi\rrbracket_{x}\coloneqq\exists y.\,R_{\alpha}(x,y)\wedge\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_{y}

where
y
is a fresh variable. Modal formulas are evaluated in
pointed Kripke structures
, that is pairs
(A,a)
where
A
is a
\sigma
-structure and
a\in A
. Then
A,a\models\varphi
according to Kripke semantics if, and only if,
A\models\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_{x}[a/x]
in the standard model-theoretic sense.

In analogy with the quantifier rank of a first-order formula, we define the
modal depth
of a modal formula as the maximum nesting of modalities in the formula. For example,
\neg p
,
\Diamond_{\alpha}p
and
\Box_{\alpha}\Diamond_{\beta}p
have modal depth
0
,
1
and
2
, respectively. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by

\mathrm{ML}_{k}

the collection of all modal formulas with modal depth at most
k
. The modal depth of a modal formula coincides with the quantifier rank of its standard translation, hence Lemma
2.1
implies that each modal fragment
\mathrm{ML}_{k}
contains only finitely many formulas, up to logical equivalence.

3.
Model-comparison games

In this section we will introduce three examples of model-comparison games: the bisimulation game for modal logic, and the Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé and pebble games for first-order logic. All are two-player games, played by Spoiler and Duplicator on
\sigma
-structures
A
and
B
. While Spoiler aims to show that
A
and
B
are different, Duplicator aims to show that they are similar, i.e. that they satisfy the same definable properties.

As before, we assume that
\sigma
is a finite relational signature (or a modal signature, in the case of modal logic).

3.1.
Bisimulation games

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The
k
-round bisimulation game for modal logic is played by Spoiler and Duplicator on pointed Kripke structures
(A,a)
and
(B,b)
as follows. The initial position is
(a_{0},b_{0})\coloneqq(a,b)
. At round
i+1
(with
0\leq i<k
) with current position
(a_{i},b_{i})
, Spoiler chooses one of the two structures and a binary relation symbol in the signature, say
A
and
R_{\alpha}
, and an element
a_{i+1}\in A
such that
R^{A}_{\alpha}(a_{i},a_{i+1})
. Duplicator must respond by choosing an element in the other structure, in this case
b_{i+1}\in B
, such that
R^{B}_{\alpha}(b_{i},b_{i+1})
. If Duplicator has no such response available, they lose. Otherwise, Duplicator wins the
k
-round bisimulation game if, for all unary predicates
P
in the signature, we have

\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,k\},\ \ P^{A}(a_{i})\Leftrightarrow P^{B}(b_{i}).

The following classical result links the existence of a Duplicator winning strategy in this game with equivalence in the bounded-depth fragment of modal logic:

Theorem 3.1 (
(
Hennessy and
Milner, 1980
)
).

Duplicator has a winning strategy in the
k
-round bisimulation game played between
(A,a)
and
(B,b)
if, and only if,
(A,a)
and
(B,b)
satisfy the same formulas in
\mathrm{ML}_{k}
.

3.2.
Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé games

As before, fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The
k
-round Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game, played by Spoiler and Duplicator on structures
A
and
B
, can be described as follows. In the
i
th round (with
1\leq i\leq k
), Spoiler chooses an element in one of the two structures, say
a_{i}\in A
, and Duplicator responds by choosing an element in the other structure, say
b_{i}\in B
. Duplicator wins after
k
rounds if the relation

\{(a_{i},b_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq k\}

is a partial isomorphism, i.e. the map
a_{i}\mapsto b_{i}
yields an isomorphism between the induced substructures of
A
and
B
determined by the sets
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
and
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}
, respectively. In particular, note that Duplicator wins the
0
-round game precisely when
A
and
B
satisfy the same atomic sentences. Furthermore, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, Duplicator wins the
(j+1)
-round game if, and only if, the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

For every
a\in A
there exists
b\in B
such that Duplicator wins the
j
-round game between the pointed structures
(A,a)
and
(B,b)
;
2
2

2

In other words, we expand the signature by adding a fresh constant symbol
\mathfrak{c}
, and regard
A
and
B
as structures for this new signature by setting
\mathfrak{c}^{A}\coloneqq a
and
\mathfrak{c}^{B}\coloneqq b
. The
j
-round game is then played on the extended structures.

(ii)

For every
b\in B
there exists
a\in A
such that Duplicator wins the
j
-round game between the pointed structures
(A,a)
and
(B,b)
.

The existence of a winning strategy for Duplicator captures precisely equivalence in first-order logic with bounded quantifier rank:

Theorem 3.2 (
(
Ehrenfeucht, 1960
;
Fraïssé, 1954
)
).

Duplicator has a winning strategy in the
k
-round Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game played between
A
and
B
if, and only if,
A
and
B
satisfy the same first-order sentences in
\mathrm{FO}_{k}
.

We briefly sketch a proof of Theorem
3.2
, which proceeds by induction on
k
. The base case
k=0
is obvious because the first-order sentences with quantifier rank
0
are exactly the atomic ones. Next, we show that if Theorem
3.2
holds for
k
, it also holds for
k+1
. To this end, for all elements
a
of a structure
A
, denote by
\tp_{k}(a)
the
rank-
k
type of
a
, i.e. the set of all first-order formulas
\varphi(x)
with quantifier rank
\leq k
such that
A\models\varphi(a)
.

In one direction, suppose
A
and
B
satisfy the same first-order sentences with quantifier rank
\leq k+1
. We must show that Duplicator has a winning strategy in the
(k+1)
-round game between
A
and
B
. We will check condition
(i)
above; the proof of
(ii)
is similar. Pick an arbitrary element
a\in A
and consider its rank-
k
type
\tp_{k}(a)
. We have

A\models\bigwedge{\tp_{k}(a)}.

Although the latter conjunction is infinite, it follows from Lemma
2.1
that it is equivalent to a single formula
\varphi_{a}(x)
of quantifier rank
\leq k
. Hence

A\models\exists x.\varphi_{a}(x),

and since the sentence
\exists x.\varphi_{a}(x)
has quantifier rank
\leq k+1
we get

B\models\exists x.\varphi_{a}(x).

If
b\in B
is a witness for the latter existential quantifier, we have
\tp_{k}(a)=\tp_{k}(b)
. Thus
(A,a)
and
(B,b)
satisfy the same first-order sentences with quantifier rank
\leq k
and so, by inductive hypothesis, Duplicator wins the
k
-round game between
(A,a)
and
(B,b)
.

The other direction follows by observing that every sentence in
\mathrm{FO}_{k+1}
is a Boolean combination of sentences of the form
\exists x.\varphi(x)
where
\varphi(x)
has quantifier rank
\leq k
, and using items
(i)
–
(ii)
above. Cf. e.g.
(
Libkin, 2004
, Theorem 3.18)
.

3.3.
Pebble games

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In the
n
-pebble game between structures
A
and
B
, Spoiler and Duplicator each have
n
pebbles available. In the
i
th round, Spoiler places some pebble
p_{i}
on an element of one of the two structures, say
a_{i}\in A
, and Duplicator responds by placing their corresponding pebble
p_{i}
on an element of the other structure, say
b_{i}\in B
. If the pebble
p_{i}
was previously placed on some other element, the effect is to move it to the newly chosen element (this corresponds to the fact that variables in a formula can be reused).
After
k
rounds have been played, we have sequences

[(p_{1},a_{1}),\ldots,(p_{k},a_{k})]\ \ \text{ and }\ \ [(p_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(p_{k},b_{k})]

that record the placings of pebbles on elements during the play. The current placing of pebble
p
is the last element in the sequence with first component
p
.
3
3

3

The current placings of the pebbles can be thought of as “windows” of size bounded by
n
onto the structures. These windows can slide around over different parts of the structures as moves are played.
Duplicator wins the round if the relation determined by the current placings of the pebbles is a partial isomorphism. Duplicator wins the
n
-pebble game if they have a strategy that is winning after
k
rounds, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Note that, whereas the resource parameter in the bisimulation and Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé games is the number of rounds, pebble games are infinite and the relevant resource parameter is the number of pebbles.
The infinite nature of pebble games entails that the existence of a winning strategy for Duplicator captures equivalence in an
infinitary extension
of first-order logic:

Definition 3.3.

Let
L_{\infty,\omega}
be the set of all sentences using, in addition to the usual first-order connectives, conjunctions and disjunctions of any cardinality.
4
4

4

The two subscripts in
L_{\infty,\omega}
indicate that we allow infinitary conjunctions and disjunctions (
\infty
) but only finitary quantifications (
\omega
). Note that for all sentences
\varphi\in L_{\infty,\omega}
, each subformula of
\varphi
contains only finitely many variables.
We write
L^{n}_{\infty,\omega}
for the fragment of
L_{\infty,\omega}
consisting of those sentences in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.

Theorem 3.4 (
(
Barwise, 1977
;
Immerman, 1982
)
).

Duplicator has a winning strategy in the
n
-pebble game played between
A
and
B
if, and only if,
A
and
B
satisfy the same sentences in
L^{n}_{\infty,\omega}
.

If the structures
A
and
B
are finite, then every infinite strategy in the
n
-pebble game is determined by a
k
-round strategy for a sufficiently large
k
, namely the one in which all finitely many possible placings of the pebbles appeared. Therefore, the equivalence relations
\equiv_{L^{n}_{\infty,\omega}}
and
\equiv_{\mathrm{FO}^{n}}
coincide over finite structures; cf. e.g.
(
Kolaitis and
Vardi, 1992
, Corollary 2.19)
or
(
Libkin, 2004
, Proposition 11.8)
.

Corollary 3.5.

Let
A
,
B
be finite structures. Duplicator has a winning strategy in the
n
-pebble game played between
A
and
B
if, and only if,
A
and
B
satisfy the same sentences in
\mathrm{FO}^{n}
.

4.
A new perspective: Game comonads

Game comonads arise from the insight that model-comparison games can be viewed as semantic constructions in their own right. More precisely, given a notion of game
G
and a resource parameter
k
, for each structure
A
we construct a structure
\mathbb{G}_{k}A
(in the same signature) whose universe consists of all possible plays in
A
in the game
G
with
k
resources. What is more, the assignment
A\mapsto\mathbb{G}_{k}A
is functorial and yields a
comonad
on the appropriate category of structures.

We start by recalling the notion of comonad, which is dual to that of a monad:

Definition 4.1.

A
comonad (in Kleisli form)
on a category
\C
is given by:

•

an object map
\mathbb{G}\colon\mathrm{Ob}(\C)\to\mathrm{Ob}(\C)
,

•

a morphism
\varepsilon_{A}\colon\mathbb{G}A\to A
for every object
A
,

•

and a
coextension operation
associating with each morphism
f\colon\mathbb{G}A\to B
a morphism
f^{*}\colon\mathbb{G}A\to\mathbb{G}B
,

such that for all morphisms
f\colon\mathbb{G}A\to B
and
g\colon\mathbb{G}B\to C
, the following equations hold:

\varepsilon_{A}^{*}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{G}A},\ \ \ \ \ \varepsilon_{B}\circ f^{*}=f,\ \ \ \ \ (g\circ f^{*})^{*}=g^{*}\circ f^{*}.

We can always extend a comonad in Kleisli form
\mathbb{G}
to a functor
\C\to\C
by setting
\mathbb{G}f\coloneqq(f\circ\varepsilon_{A})^{*}
for every morphism
f\colon A\to B
. Moreover, setting
\delta_{A}\coloneqq\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{G}A}^{*}
for every
A
in
\C
, the tuple
(\mathbb{G},\varepsilon,\delta)
is a comonad in the more traditional sense, where the natural transformations
\varepsilon
and
\delta
are the
counit
and the
comultiplication
of the comonad, respectively. In fact, these two formulations are equivalent.

The structure
\mathbb{G}_{k}A
can be thought of as a forest-ordered
cover
, or
decomposition
, of
A
. A fundamental example of this idea arises from the unravelling construction in modal logic, which we now recall.
Consider the pointed Kripke model
(A,s)
in Figure
2
; the modal signature consists of a single binary relation symbol (represented by an arrow) and two unary relation symbols (represented as red and blue nodes).
The
unravelling
of
(A,a)
is the Kripke model of all possible finite paths in
A
starting from
x
; see §
4.2
for a precise definition. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
k
-unravelling
of
(A,a)
is obtained by restricting to paths of length at most
k
. Note that the universe of the
k
-unravelling of
(A,a)
can be identified with the set of all possible plays in
(A,a)
in the
k
-round bisimulation game.

After introducing the relevant categories of relational structures on which game comonads are defined, we will show that the unravelling construction yields a comonad on pointed Kripke models, the so-called
modal comonad
, and introduce two further examples of game comonads: the
Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé
and
pebbling comonads
. These comonads can be regarded as enrichments of the
finite-list comonad
on the category of sets, and variations thereof.

4.1.
Categories of relational structures

Henceforth, we shall fix a finite relational signature
\sigma
; we will assume for simplicity that
\sigma
contains no constant symbols.
5
5

5

Note, however, that this assumption prevents us from encoding the distinguished element of a pointed Kripke model by means of a constant symbol in the signature.
Hence, a
\sigma
-structure consists of a set
A
together with, for each relation symbol
R\in\sigma
of arity
n
(with
n
an integer greater than
0
), a subset
R^{A}\subseteq A^{n}
. A
homomorphism
from a
\sigma
-structure
A
to a
\sigma
-structure
B
is given by a function
h\colon A\to B
such that, for each
R\in\sigma
of arity
n
and
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in A^{n}
,

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in R^{A}\ \Longrightarrow\ (h(a_{1}),\ldots,h(a_{n}))\in R^{B}.

Our setting will be the category

\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)

of
\sigma
-structures and their homomorphisms. Note that this is
not
the usual setting for classical model theory, which is mainly concerned with morphisms that preserve and reflect the validity of first-order sentences, such as elementary embeddings. Nevertheless, homomorphisms of relational structures are pervasive in computer science, e.g. in databases, constraint satisfaction and finite model theory, and also in combinatorics (in the study of graphs and graph homomorphisms).

When dealing with modal logic, we will assume that
\sigma
is a modal signature and consider the category

\mathbf{Struct}_{\bullet}(\sigma)

of pointed Kripke models whose morphisms are the homomorphisms that preserve the distinguished elements.

4.2.
The modal comonad

For each pointed Kripke model
(A,a)\in\mathbf{Struct}_{\bullet}(\sigma)
, we define a new model
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)\in\mathbf{Struct}_{\bullet}(\sigma)
as follows. The underlying set of
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
consists of all finite paths in
A
starting from
a
:

a\xrightarrow{R_{1}}a_{1}\xrightarrow{R_{2}}a_{2}\xrightarrow{\phantom{R_{3}}}\cdots\xrightarrow{R_{\ell}}a_{\ell}

where
R_{1},\ldots,R_{\ell}
are binary relation symbols in
\sigma
. These finite paths can be thought of as plays in the bisimulation game. The distinguished element of
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
is the trivial path
[a]
of length
0
, and the interpretation in
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
of the relation symbols is defined as follows:

•

If
P\in\sigma
is a unary relation symbol, its interpretation consists of the paths whose last element is in
P^{A}
.

•

If
R\in\sigma
is a binary relation symbol, its interpretations consists of the pairs of paths
(p,p^{\prime})
such that
p^{\prime}
is obtained by extending
p
by one step along
R
.

The pointed Kripke model
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
is the
(full) unravelling of

(A,a)
.

The assignment
(A,a)\mapsto\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
gives an object map from
\mathbf{Struct}_{\bullet}(\sigma)
to itself, and moreover we have homomorphisms of pointed Kripke models

\varepsilon_{(A,a)}\colon\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)\to(A,a)

sending a path to its last element. The coextension operation associating with a morphism
f\colon\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)\to(B,b)
in
\mathbf{Struct}_{\bullet}(\sigma)
a morphism

f^{*}\colon\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)\to\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(B,b)

is defined recursively:
f^{*}
sends the trivial path
(a)
to
(b)
, and if
p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
is obtained by extending
p
by one step along
R
to
a^{\prime}\in A
, then
f^{*}(p^{\prime})
is the sequence that extends
f^{*}(p)
by one step along
R
to
f(p^{\prime})
.
These data yield a comonad on the category
\mathbf{Struct}_{\bullet}(\sigma)
of pointed Kripke models:

Proposition 4.2.

\mathbb{M}_{\omega}
is a comonad on
\mathbf{Struct}_{\bullet}(\sigma)
.

Intuitively, the comonad
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}
corresponds to the
infinite
bisimulation game. It is often useful to consider resource-bounded variants of this construction, where the length of paths is bounded; this corresponds to considering finite bisimulation games. Accordingly, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
\mathbb{M}_{k}(A,a)
be the submodel of
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
defined by the paths of length at most
k
—this is known as the
k
-unravelling
of
(A,a)
. The morphisms
\varepsilon_{(A,a)}
and the coextension operation above restrict to
\mathbb{M}_{k}(A,a)
in the obvious manner. In fact,

Proposition 4.3.

\mathbb{M}_{k}
is a comonad on
\mathbf{Struct}_{\bullet}(\sigma)
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

We refer to the comonads
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}
and
\mathbb{M}_{k}
as
modal comonads
.
Modal comonads have an important property that is not shared by all game comonads: they are
idempotent
, meaning that the components of the comultiplication
\delta\colon\mathbb{M}_{\omega}\Rightarrow\mathbb{M}_{\omega}\mathbb{M}_{\omega}
are isomorphisms. Informally, this can be understood as the fact that the unravelling construction gives a universal way of constructing a tree-like Kripke model from an arbitrary one, and applying this construction to a model that is already tree-like yields nothing new. In particular,
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
.

Remark 4.4.

Note that the unravelling
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A,a)
can be infinite even if
A
is finite; e.g., the unravelling of a model with one node and one loop is a countably infinite chain. On the other hand, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
k
-unravelling
\mathbb{M}_{k}(A,a)
is finite whenever
A
is.

In the other direction, it is possible that
A
is infinite but
\mathbb{M}_{\omega}(A)
is finite; e.g., take
A
to be the disjoint union of a node
a
(the distinguished element) and an infinite chain not reachable from
a
. In fact, the unravelling construction ignores all parts of a model that are not reachable from its distinguished element.

4.3.
The Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé comonad

For each relational structure
A\in\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)
, we define a new structure
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(A)
whose underlying set consists of all non-empty finite sequences of elements from
A
. This choice is motivated by the fact that, while in the bisimulation game the players can only move along accessibility relations, in the Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game they can move freely within the structures.

As before, we denote by

\varepsilon_{A}\colon\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(A)\to A,\ \ \varepsilon_{A}[a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}]\coloneqq a_{\ell}

the map sending a sequence to its last element. If
R\in\sigma
is a relation symbol of arity
n
, then its interpretation in
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(A)
consists of the tuples
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
of sequences verifying the following two conditions:

•

the sequences
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
are pairwise comparable in the prefix order (i.e., for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, either
s_{i}
is a prefix of
s_{j}
or vice versa), and

•

(\varepsilon_{A}(s_{1}),\ldots,\varepsilon_{A}(s_{n}))\in R^{A}
, i.e. the last elements of the sequences are
R
-related in
A
.

It then follows that the
\varepsilon_{A}
are homomorphisms. The coextension operation sends
a homomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(A)\to B
to the homomorphism

f^{*}\colon\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(A)\to\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(B),\ \ f^{*}[a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}]\coloneqq[b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell}]

where
b_{i}\coloneqq f[a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}]
for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
.

Proposition 4.5.

\mathbb{E}_{\omega}
is a comonad on
\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)
.

In a similar way to the modal case, the comonad
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}
corresponds to the infinite Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game. To capture finite games, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
we consider the submodel
\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)
of
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(A)
consisting of sequences of length at most
k
. With the obvious restrictions of the morphisms
\varepsilon_{A}
and of the coextension operation, we have that

Proposition 4.6.

\mathbb{E}_{k}
is a comonad on
\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

We refer to the comonads
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}
and
\mathbb{E}_{k}
as
Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé comonads
.

Remark 4.7.

The structure
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}(A)
is always infinite whereas, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)
is finite if, and only if
A
is finite. The “only if” part of the latter statement follows from the fact that the components of the counit

\varepsilon_{A}\colon\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)\to A

are surjective. This feature distinguishes the Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé comonads from the modal comonads, for which the components of the counit are generally not surjective because their images cannot contain unreachable parts of the model (or even parts of the model only reachable in more than
k
steps, in the case of the comonads
\mathbb{M}_{k}
).

4.4.
The pebbling comonad

To encode pebble games, we follow the same pattern we adopted for bisimulation and Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé games. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbf{n}\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For each relational structure
A\in\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)
, we define a structure
\mathbb{P}_{n}(A)\in\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)
whose underlying set consists of all non-empty finite sequences of elements from the Cartesian product of sets
\mathbf{n}\times A
. An element
(p,a)\in\mathbf{n}\times A
can be thought of as the move in which pebble
p
is placed on
a
, and a finite sequence of such moves corresponds to a play in the
n
-pebble game. We refer to
p
as the
pebble index
of the move
(p,a)
.

As before, we have a map

\varepsilon_{A}\colon\mathbb{P}_{n}(A)\to A,\ \ \varepsilon_{A}[(p_{1},a_{1}),\ldots,(p_{\ell},a_{\ell})]\coloneqq a_{\ell}

sending a play to the element selected in the last move. For each relation symbol
R\in\sigma
of arity
j
, its interpretation in
\mathbb{P}_{n}(A)
consists of the tuples of moves
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{j})
satisfying the following properties:

•

the sequences
s_{1},\ldots,s_{j}
are pairwise comparable in the prefix order,

•

for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,j\}
, whenever
s_{i}
is a prefix of
s_{i^{\prime}}
, the pebble index of the last move in
s_{i}
does not appear in the suffix of
s_{i}
in
s_{i^{\prime}}
,
6
6

6

That is, the pebble has not been moved and is therefore still “active”.
and

•

(\varepsilon_{A}(s_{1}),\ldots,\varepsilon_{A}(s_{j}))\in R^{A}
.

With this definition, the functions
\varepsilon_{A}
are homomorphisms. The coextension operation is the same, mutatis mutandis, as with
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}
. We have:

Proposition 4.8.

\mathbb{P}_{n}
is a comonad on
\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We refer to the comonads
\mathbb{P}_{n}
as
pebbling comonads
.

Remark 4.9.

Since there is no bound on the length of plays,
\mathbb{P}_{n}(A)
is always infinite, even when
A
is finite. This cannot be avoided: there is no comonad that captures pebble games and sends finite structures to finite structures (for a formal statement, see
(
Abramsky
et al., 2017
, Theorem 7)
).

On the other hand, we can stratify
\mathbb{P}_{n}(A)
by considering the submodels defined by sequences of length at most
k
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
; this corresponds to playing
k
-round
n
-pebble games. The ensuing comonads
\mathbb{P}_{n,k}
on
\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)
do restrict to finite structures.

5.
The Kleisli category and the first logical equivalences

Game comonads provide a categorical view on a number of model-comparison games, which in turn capture in a combinatorial fashion preservation of corresponding logic fragments. In this section and in §
7
below, we will see how preservation of many logic fragments can be detected directly at the categorical level. In this section we look at the
Kleisli categories
for game comonads, and show that two natural relations in these categories—the homomorphism preorder and the isomorphism relation—capture preservation of
existential positive
fragments and extensions with
counting quantifiers
, respectively. These results hold uniformly for the game comonads we have introduced (Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé, pebbling and modal comonads) and the corresponding logical resources (quantifier rank, number of variables and modal depth).

In §
7
we will see that other logic fragments, including the full fragments
\mathrm{FO}_{k}
,
\mathrm{FO}^{n}
and
\mathrm{ML}_{k}
, can be captured categorically by working in an enlargement of the Kleisli categories, namely the categories of coalgebras for the comonads.

5.1.
The Kleisli category

Definition 5.1.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a comonad on a category
\C
. The
Kleisli category of
\mathbb{G}
, denoted by
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{G})
, is defined as follows:

•

\mathrm{Ob}(\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{G}))\coloneqq\mathrm{Ob}(\C)
.

•

An arrow
A\to B
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{G})
, called a
Kleisli arrow
, is an arrow
\mathbb{G}A\to B
in
\C
.

The Kleisli identity
A\to A
is the arrow
\varepsilon_{A}\colon\mathbb{G}A\to A
in
\C
, and the composite of Kleisli arrows
A\to B\to C
is defined using the coextension operation: given
f\colon\mathbb{G}A\to B
and
g\colon\mathbb{G}B\to C
in
\C
, we get
g\circ f^{*}\colon\mathbb{G}A\to\mathbb{G}B\to C
.

The Kleisli category
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{G})
effectively considers the same objects as
\C
but with a different notion of morphism. For example, an arrow
A\to B
in the Kleisli category
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{E}_{k})
for the Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé comonad is a homomorphism of
\sigma
-structures

\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)\to B.

The structure
\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)
consists of “explorations of depth at most
k
” of
A
, and reflects the finite amount of resources available to the players (in this case, the number of rounds).
Hence, the passage from the category
\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)
to the Kleisli category for a game comonad (and, more generally, to the category of coalgebras) allows us to work in a setting that is intrinsically resource-sensitive.
For instance, we shall see in §
5.3
that Kleisli arrows encode preservation of resource-bounded existential positive fragments. Before that, we briefly explain how to deal with the equality symbol.

5.2.
The equality symbol

In their basic form, game comonads allow us to capture fragments of logics
without
equality. This is sufficient for modal logic, since the image of the standard translation is contained in the equality-free fragment of
\mathrm{FO}
. On the other hand, to model logics
with
equality, such as
\mathrm{FO}_{k}
and
\mathrm{FO}^{n}
(and variations thereof), we can proceed as follows.

Consider a (finite) relational signature
\sigma
, a fresh binary relation symbol
I
and the expanded signature

\sigma^{I}\coloneqq\sigma\cup\{I\}.

There is a fully faithful functor
J\colon\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)\to\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma^{I})
that upgrades a
\sigma
-structure to a
\sigma^{I}
-structure by interpreting
I
as the diagonal relation. This functor has a left adjoint
H\colon\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma^{I})\to\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)
that sends a
\sigma^{I}
-structure
A
to the quotient
\underline{A}\,/{\sim}
, where
\underline{A}
is the
\sigma
-reduct of
A
and
\sim
is the equivalence relation generated by
I^{A}
. Intuitively, the functor
J
introduces (the interpretation of) the equality symbol, while
H
eliminates it. Because a generic game comonad
\mathbb{G}
is defined uniformly for any relational signature, we can consider its variant
\mathbb{G}^{I}
over
\sigma^{I}
-structures:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)}
\top
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma^{I})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{G}^{I}}

The composite

\mathbb{G}^{=}\coloneqq H\circ\mathbb{G}^{I}\circ J\colon\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)\to\mathbf{Struct}(\sigma)

is also a comonad, and it is the one that will allow us to capture logics with equality.

Consider e.g. the Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé comonad
\mathbb{E}_{k}
. Note that, for any
\sigma
-structure
A
, the interpretation of the relation
I
in the
\sigma^{I}
-structure
\mathbb{E}_{k}^{I}J(A)
consists of those pairs
(s,t)
of sequences of length at most
k
of elements of
A
such that (i)
s,t
are comparable in the prefix order
\sqsubseteq
and (ii) their last elements are equal. In other words, the relation
I^{\mathbb{E}_{k}^{I}J(A)}
detects repeating sequences. Given
\sigma
-structures
A
and
B
, Kleisli morphisms

\mathbb{E}_{k}^{=}(A)\to B

are in bijection with morphisms

\mathbb{E}_{k}^{I}J(A)\to J(B)

(because
H\dashv J
), which in turn are in bijection with Kleisli morphisms
f\colon\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)\to B
such that

\text{$\forall s,t\in\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)$, \ if $s\sqsubseteq t$ and $\varepsilon_{A}(s)=\varepsilon_{A}(t)$ then $f(s)=f(t)$},

called
I
-morphisms
. In the case of the pebbling comonad,
I
-morphisms are defined in a similar fashion.

Remark 5.2.

There is a fully faithful functor

\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{G}^{=})\to\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{G}^{I})

that sends a
\sigma
-structure
A
to
J(A)
. So, given any two
\sigma
-structures
A
and
B
, there exists an (iso)morphism
A\to B
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{G}^{=})
if, and only if, there exists an (iso)morphism
J(A)\to J(B)
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{G}^{I})
.

The approach outlined above for encoding the equality symbol, whereby one “composes” a game comonad with the adjunction

, is equivalent to the one based on

-morphisms introduced in
(
Abramsky
et al., 2017
;
Abramsky and Shah, 2021
)
and is a special case of the notion of
relative adjunction

(
Ulmer, 1968
)
.

5.3.
Existential positive fragments

An
existential positive
formula is a formula that contains no negations and no universal quantifiers. In the case of modal formulas, this amounts to barring the use of the negation symbol and the modalities
\Box_{\alpha}
. Given a class of first-order formulas
\mathcal{L}
or, more generally, of formulas in infinitary first-order logic
L_{\infty,\omega}
, we denote by

\exists^{+}\mathcal{L}

the subset of
\mathcal{L}
consisting of the existential positive formulas.

The next result states that the homomorphism preorder in the Kleisli categories of game comonads captures precisely preservation of the existential positive fragments. In this case, we do not need to consider the variant
\mathbb{G}^{=}
of a game comonad
\mathbb{G}
because the existential positive fragments admit
equality elimination
; see Remark
5.5
.
Given a logic fragment
\mathcal{L}
and structures
A
and
B
, we write

A\Rrightarrow_{\mathcal{L}}B

provided that
A\models\varphi
implies
B\models\varphi
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}
. (Hence, the equivalence relation
\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}
is the symmetrization of the preorder
\Rrightarrow_{\mathcal{L}}
.)

Theorem 5.3 (
(
Abramsky
et al., 2017
;
Abramsky and Shah, 2021
)
).

The following statements hold for each
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and (pointed) structures
A,B
:

(1)

A\Rrightarrow_{\exists^{+}\mathrm{FO}_{k}}B
if, and only if, there is an arrow
A\to B
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{E}_{k})
.

(2)

A\Rrightarrow_{\exists^{+}L^{n}_{\infty,\omega}}B
if, and only if, there is an arrow
A\to B
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
.

(3)

(A,a)\Rrightarrow_{\exists^{+}\mathrm{ML}_{k}}(B,b)
if, and only if, there is an arrow
(A,a)\to(B,b)
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{M}_{k})
.

Remark 5.4.

If
A,B
are finite, the relation
A\Rrightarrow_{\exists^{+}L^{n}_{\infty,\omega}}B
in item
2
above can be replaced with
A\Rrightarrow_{\exists^{+}\mathrm{FO}^{n}}B
(cf. §
3.3
and
(
Kolaitis and
Vardi, 1995
, Corollary 4.9)
).

We briefly illustrate the idea behind Theorem
5.3
in the case of Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé games; the same reasoning applies, mutatis mutandis, to the other cases.

It is well know that the relation
A\Rrightarrow_{\exists^{+}\mathrm{FO}_{k}}B
holds precisely when Duplicator wins the
existential positive
variant of the
k
-round Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game played between
A
and
B
. In this game, Spoiler is forced to play in
A
and Duplicator responds in
B
. The winning condition, after
k
rounds, is that the relation

\{(a_{i},b_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq k\}

determined by the chosen elements is a partial homomorphism (rather than a partial isomorphism), i.e. the map
a_{i}\mapsto b_{i}
yields a homomorphism between the induced substructures of
A
and
B
determined, respectively, by the sets
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
and
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}
.

In turn, Duplicator has a winning strategy in this game if, and only if, there exists a homomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)\to B
. Just observe that specifying such an
f
is the same as specifying its coextension
f^{*}\colon\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)\to\mathbb{E}_{k}(B)
. In turn, a map
\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)\to\mathbb{E}_{k}(B)
can be regarded as a Duplicator strategy in the existential positive game (associating with a sequence of Spoiler’s moves the corresponding sequence of Duplicator’s responses), and the winning condition amounts to saying that this map is a homomorphism.

Remark 5.5.

There is a subtlety here: suppose a homomorphism
\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)\to\mathbb{E}_{k}(B)
sends a sequence
[a_{1},a_{2},a_{1}]
to
[b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}]
with
b_{1}\neq b_{3}
. This prevents the relation
\{(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),(a_{1},b_{3})\}
from being functional, and so it does
not
define a Duplicator winning strategy. Yet, we can obtain a Duplicator winning strategy by imposing that, if Spoiler plays the same element twice (or more), Duplicator repeats their answer.

This issue can be solved by considering only the
I
-morphisms instead of all Kleisli morphisms.
In fact, there is a
bijection
between
I
-morphisms
\mathbb{E}_{k}(A)\to B
(equivalently, Kleisli morphisms
\mathbb{E}_{k}^{=}(A)\to B
) and Duplicator winning strategies in the existential positive
k
-round Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game.

In logical terms, the reason why Theorem
5.3
holds in the form stated, with no need to consider the comonads
\mathbb{G}^{=}
, is that the existential positive fragments admit equality elimination. For example, in
\exists^{+}\mathrm{FO}_{k}
the only atomic (sub)formulas using the equality symbol are those of the form
x=y
with
x,y
variables, and these can be eliminated by substituting one variable for the other in the appropriate way.
A similar reasoning applies to the pebbling comonad.

Let us mention that the existential positive variant of pebble games was first studied in
(
Kolaitis and
Vardi, 1995
)
where it is called “existential pebble game”, while the existential positive variant of bisimulation games is known as the
simulation game
.

5.4.
Fragments with counting quantifiers

In §
5.3
, we saw that the homomorphism preorder in the Kleisli categories for game comonads captures preservation of existential positive fragments. We shall now look at another natural relation between objects of the Kleisli categories, namely the isomorphism relation.

If
\mathcal{L}
is a class of first-order formulas, we write

\mathcal{L}(\#)

for the extension of
\mathcal{L}
with
counting quantifiers
. That is, for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
we add a quantifier
\exists_{\geq i}
such that a sentence
\exists_{\geq i}x.\varphi(x)
is satisfied in a structure
A
if, and only if, there are at least
i
elements of
A
witnessing the fact that
\varphi(x)
is satisfiable in
A
.
In the case of modal formulas, we add
graded modalities

\Diamond_{\alpha}^{i}
such that
\Diamond_{\alpha}^{i}\varphi
holds in a pointed Kripke model
(A,a)
if, and only if, the are at least
i
elements of
A
that are accessible from
a
through
R_{\alpha}
and satisfy
\varphi
.

We will restrict ourselves to the class of finite structures. In this case, the isomorphism relation in the Kleisli category captures equivalence in the counting extensions of the logic fragments:

Theorem 5.6 (
(
Abramsky
et al., 2017
;
Abramsky and Shah, 2021
)
).

The following statements hold for each
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and finite (pointed) structures
A,B
:

(1)

A\equiv_{\mathrm{FO}_{k}(\#)}B
if, and only if, there is an isomorphism
A\cong B
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{E}_{k}^{=})
.

(2)

A\equiv_{\mathrm{FO}^{n}(\#)}B
if, and only if, there is an isomorphism
A\cong B
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{P}_{n}^{=})
.

(3)

(A,a)\equiv_{\mathrm{ML}_{k}(\#)}(B,b)
if, and only if, there is an isomorphism
(A,a)\cong(B,b)
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{M}_{k})
.

The previous result makes use of the fact that equivalence in the logics with counting quantifiers
\mathrm{FO}_{k}(\#)
,
\mathrm{FO}^{n}(\#)
and
\mathrm{ML}_{k}(\#)
are known to be captured by variants of the usual Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé, pebble and bisimulation games that consider
bijections
between structures.

For example, the
bijective Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game

(
Hella, 1996
)
played between finite structures
A
and
B
proceeds as follows. If
A
and
B
have different cardinalities, Duplicator loses the game. Otherwise, at round

, Duplicator choses a bijection
{f_{i}\colon A\to B}
and Spoiler responds by choosing an element
a_{i}\in A
. After
k
-rounds, Duplicator wins if the relation
\{(a_{i},f_{i}(a_{i}))\mid 1\leq i\leq k\}
is a partial isomorphism. We have
A\equiv_{\mathrm{FO}_{k}(\#)}B
if, and only if, Duplicator has a winning strategy in this

-round game. In turn, such a winning strategy induces arrows

and

in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{E}_{k})
that are inverse to each other and, conversely, any isomorphism between

and
B
in
\mathbf{Kl}(\mathbb{E}_{k})
induces a Duplicator winning strategy.

Similarly, the fragments
\mathrm{FO}^{n}(\#)
and
\mathrm{ML}_{k}(\#)
are captured, respectively, by
bijective
n
-pebble games

(
Hella, 1996
)
and
k
-round
graded bisimulation games
(cf. e.g.
(
Abramsky and Shah, 2021
, §5.3)
or
(
Otto, 2023
)
). Let us point out that, in the case of the pebbling comonad, we do not need to consider the infinitary extension of
\mathrm{FO}^{n}(\#)
with arbitrary conjunctions and disjunctions because, as with classical pebble games, the bijective
n
-pebble game between
finite
structures is determined by a
k
-round strategy for
k
large enough.

A well-known consequence of the
Yoneda Lemma
states that any two objects
X,Y
of an (essentially small) category
\C
are isomorphic if, and only if, the corresponding hom-functors are
naturally isomorphic
; in symbols,

X\cong Y\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \hom(-,X)\cong\hom(-,Y).

In 1967, Lovász showed that the naturality condition can be omitted when
\C
is the category of finite
\sigma
-structures, for any finite relational signature
\sigma

(
Lovász, 1967
)
. That is, finite
\sigma
-structures
A,B
are isomorphic precisely when they admit the same (finite) number of homomorphisms from any other finite
\sigma
-structure:

A\cong B\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{for all finite $\sigma$-structures $C$, }\hom(C,A)\cong\hom(C,B).

Remark 6.12.

Two pointwise isomorphic functors (as in Lovász’ theorem) need not be naturally isomorphic, as the next example from
(
Joyal, 1981
)
shows. For any finite set
X
, let
K_{1}(X)
and
K_{2}(X)
be, respectively, the set of permutations of
X
and the set of linear orders on
X
. The assignments
X\mapsto K_{1}(X)
and
X\mapsto K_{2}(X)
can be extended to endofunctors
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, respectively, on the category of finite sets and bijections between them. The functors
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are pointwise isomorphic because the number of permutations of a finite set
X
is
|X|!
, which is also the number of linear orders on
X
. However, they are not naturally isomorphic; just note that there is no natural way of assigning a linear order to the identity permutation.

Homomorphism counting results, akin to Lovász’ seminal result, have been investigated also in finite model theory. There, one is interested in equivalence relations between structures that can be captured in terms of homomorphism counts from a restricted class of structures. Prime examples are Dvořák’s and Grohe’s homomorphism counting results:

Theorem 6.13 (
(
Dvořák, 2010
)
).

Let
\C
be the category of finite undirected graphs without loops and graph homomorphisms, and let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\C
consist of those graphs having tree-width
<n
. For any
A,B\in\C
we have

A\equiv_{\mathrm{FO}^{n}(\#)}B\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{for all $C\in\mathcal{K}$, }\hom(C,A)\cong\hom(C,B).

Theorem 6.14 (
(
Grohe, 2020
)
).

Let
\C
be the category of finite undirected vertex-coloured graphs and graph homomorphisms preserving the colours, and let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\C
consist of those graphs having tree-depth
\leq k
. For any
A,B\in\C
we have

A\equiv_{\mathrm{FO}_{k}(\#)}B\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{for all $C\in\mathcal{K}$, }\hom(C,A)\cong\hom(C,B).

The three homomorphism counting results above find a natural common generalisation in the setting of game comonads. Recall from Theorem
5.6
that equivalence in the counting logics is captured by the isomorphism relation in the Kleisli categories, or equivalently in the Eilenberg–Moore categories (since the former fully embed into the latter). In turn, by Propositions
6.9
and
6.11
, the structures having tree-width
<n
or tree-depth
\leq k
are precisely those admitting, respectively, a coalgebra structure for
\mathbb{P}_{n}
or
\mathbb{E}_{k}
. This suggests the following notion:

Definition 6.15.

Let
\C
be a locally finite category (that is, there are finitely many arrows between any two objects of
\C
). We say that
\C
is
combinatorial
if, for all
A,B\in\C
,

A\cong B\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{for all $C\in\C$, }\hom(C,A)\cong\hom(C,B).

For example, Lovász’ theorem states that the category of finite
\sigma
-structures is combinatorial. In fact, any locally finite category with pushouts and a proper factorisation system is combinatorial; see
(
Dawar
et al., 2021
)
and also
(

(

(

Let

9

A coalgebra for

is

if its underlying

7.1.
Existential fragments and pathwise embeddings

Given a class of first-order formulas
\mathcal{L}
, we write

\exists\mathcal{L}

for the fragment of
\mathcal{L}
consisting of
existential formulas
, i.e. those formulas that contain no universal quantifiers and in which negations are only applied to atomic subformulas. In the case of modal logic, the existential formulas are those in which the modalities
\Box_{\alpha}
do not appear and the negation symbol is only applied to propositional variables. Since preserving the validity of negated atomic formulas is equivalent to
reflecting
the validity of atomic formulas, we should look at coalgebra morphisms that not only preserve relations but also reflect them (at least in appropriate cases). This leads us to the notion of pathwise embedding.

Recall that a homomorphism of
\sigma
-structures
h\colon A\to B
is an
embedding
if it is injective and, for each relation symbol
R\in\sigma
of arity
n
and each
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in A^{n}
,

(h(a_{1}),\ldots,h(a_{n}))\in R^{B}\ \Longrightarrow\ (a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in R^{A}.

Now, let
\A
be any of the categories
\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{E}_{k})
,
\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
or
\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{M}_{k})
.

Definition 7.2.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be an arrow in
\A
.

•

f
is an
embedding
, written
\rightarrowtail
, if its underlying homomorphism of
\sigma
-structures is.

•

If
X
is a path and
f
is an embedding, then
f
is called a
path embedding
.

•

f
is a
pathwise embedding
if, for all path embeddings
m\colon P\rightarrowtail X
, the composite
f\circ m\colon P\to Y
is a path embedding. That is, the restriction of
f
to each branch of the forest is an embedding.

Remark 7.3.

Every embedding in
\A
is a pathwise embedding; the converse need not hold because pathwise embeddings may fail to be injective.

Akin to the homomorphism preorder, we can consider the preorder between objects determined by the existence of a pathwise embedding between them. The next result states that this preorder captures exactly preservation of the existential fragments. Recall the functor
{F}^{I}
from eq. (
3

Theorem 7.4.

The following statements hold for each
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and (pointed) structures
A,B
:

(1)

A\Rrightarrow_{\exists\mathrm{FO}_{k}}B
if and only if there is a pathwise embedding
{F}^{I}(A)\to{F}^{I}(B)
in
\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{E}_{k}^{I})
.

(2)

A\Rrightarrow_{\exists L^{n}_{\infty,\omega}}B
if and only if there is a pathwise embedding
{{F}^{I}(A)\to{F}^{I}(B)}
in
\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{P}_{n}^{I})
.

(3)

(A,a)\Rrightarrow_{\exists\mathrm{ML}_{k}}(B,b)
if and only if there is a pathwise embedding
F(A,a)\to F(B,b)
in
\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{M}_{k})
.

While Theorem
5.3
relied on the correspondence between Kleisli arrows and Duplicator winning strategies in the existential positive variant of the games, Theorem
7.4
relies on a correspondence between pathwise embeddings and Duplicator winning strategies in the
existential
variant of the games. In these games, Spoiler is forced to play in
A
and Duplicator responds in
B
, and the winning condition is the same as in the back-and-forth game.

8.
An axiomatic perspective: Arboreal categories

In the previous sections we have seen that the comonads
\mathbb{E}_{k}
,
\mathbb{P}_{n}
and
\mathbb{M}_{k}
exhibit a similar behaviour. In each case, their coalgebras can be represented concretely as forest-ordered structures, and preservation of corresponding logic fragments can be captured in terms of natural relations between co-free coalgebras, such as the homomorphism preorder, the isomorphism and bisimilarity relations, and variations thereof.

It is natural to ask if we can reason about a
generic
game comonad. (Note, however, that there is as yet no mathematical definition of a game comonad—you know one when you see one!) To this end, we introduce the framework of arboreal categories, which capture in an axiomatic way the main features of the categories of coalgebras for game comonads. In §
9
, we will show how this axiomatic approach can be used to give a uniform treatment of equi-resource homomorphism preservation theorems in logic.

8.1.
Arboreal categories

Within the categories of coalgebras

\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{P}_{n})

\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{M}_{k})

8.1.2.
Arboreal categories defined

Now that we have an abstract notion of path, we can define path embeddings, pathwise embeddings, bisimulations, and so forth, by generalising in a straightforward manner the definitions given in the concrete setting. In particular, we can define a notion of bisimilarity in any category equipped with a proper factorisation system.

Remark 8.4.

The bisimilarity relation in arboreal categories is reminiscent of (and inspired by) the notion of

introduced in
(

et al., 1993

Joyal
et al., 1996

However, there are some differences: while the latter takes as parameter a full subcategory whose objects are called “paths”, in the arboreal setting the notion of path is completely determined by the factorisation system (which is thus the only “parameter”). Moreover, the arboreal notion of open morphism is a refinement of the one used in
(

The next step is to identify sufficient conditions on the category so that this notion of bisimilarity is well-behaved. In this regard, the sub-poset

Paths are connected:

Coproducts of paths exist and each path

into a coproduct of paths (indexed by a non-empty set

Given arrows

Each object is

Example 8.6.

We give some examples of arboreal categories:

(1)

The category of forests and forest morphisms is arboreal, when equipped with the factorisation system (surjective morphisms, injective morphisms). The paths are the finite chains, i.e. the forests consisting of a single branch. Coproducts are given by disjoint union; in particular, the initial object is the empty forest. Finite chains are easily seen to be connected, and the 2-out-of-3 property holds because forest morphisms preserve height. Finally, each forest is path-generated because it is the colimit of the diagram given by its branches and the embeddings between them.

The category
\T
of non-empty trees and forest morphisms is equivalent to the category of forests (see Remark
6.6
) and so it also arboreal. Paths in
\T
are the non-empty finite chains. Non-empty coproducts in
\T
are given by smash sum, with the roots identified, and the initial object of
\T
is the one-element tree.

(2)

The categories of coalgebras
\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{E}_{k})

\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
and
\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{M}_{k})
, equipped with the factorisation systems defined in Example

\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{E}_{k})

\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
are computed in the category of forests, while coproducts in
\mathbf{EM}(\mathbb{M}_{k})
are computed in

(3)

If

is a forest, seen as a posetal category, then the category

of presheaves over

is arboreal with respect to the factorisation system (epimorphisms, monomorphisms). The paths are the representable functors along with the initial object, and the path-generation property amounts to the well-known fact that representables in a presheaf category are dense. See
(
Reggio and Riba, 2023

for more details.

This generalises item
1

is the poset of natural numbers with the usual total order,

is equivalent to the category of forests and their morphisms. Moreover, taking as

8.2.
Games in arboreal categories

8.2.1.
The functor
\Path

The next result provides an analogue to the forgetful functor from the category

into the category of forests, and is a key tool in the theory of arboreal categories.

Proposition 8.7.

Let
\A
be an arboreal category. The assignment

(f\colon X\to Y)\ \longmapsto\ (\exists_{f}\colon\Path{X}\to\Path{Y})

yields a functor

Example 8.8.

In the case of modal logic,

and

The arboreal adjunctions associated with the comonads

have the bisimilar companion property; in fact, the idempotency of the modal comonads implies the stronger property

The bisimilar companion property is correlated with the tractability of the corresponding logics: the modal and guarded fragments are decidable and have the tree-model property

Grädel, 2001

, while the quantifier rank and finite-variable fragments (which are “expressive”, in the sense that they cover the whole of first-order logic) do not. These
observations provide a first step towards using structural properties of comonadic semantics to classify logic fragments and their expressive power.

From the comonadic perspective, the bisimilar companion property is central to the analysis of homomorphism preservation theorems in logic
(

10.
Towards a homotopical view on logical resources

Many questions in (finite) model theory, and more generally in logic, can be phrased as
model existence
problems: is there a (finite) model satisfying a collection of logically specified properties? In classical model theory, the Compactness Theorem is a basic tool to tackle these problems. On the other hand, in finite and resource-sensitive model theory one typically addresses a model existence problem via the
model construction
method, which consists in explicitly constructing a model satisfying the desired properties. We saw an example of this in §
9.2
, where an upgrading argument is combined with the construction of extendable covers of certain relational structures.

In general, an object obtained by means of a model construction argument is overdetermined, in the sense that it is specified up to isomorphism and not up to equivalence with respect to the prescribed logical properties.
This idea is pervasive in mathematics and computer science: we may not want to distinguish between two topological spaces that are homotopy equivalent, or two programs that on the same input give the same output.

Our aim in this section is to make the analogy between resource-sensitive model theory and homotopy more precise using the language of abstract homotopy, also called
categorical homotopy
. We posit that categorical homotopy provides a flexible setting in which to manipulate objects up to logical equivalence and perform constructions that are invariant under such equivalences; this idea is illustrated in the case of modal logic in §
10.2
.

However, it is worth observing that the perspective of game comonads and arboreal categories points to a modal-like view on many fragments of (infinitary) first-order logic and corresponding decompositions of structures. A similar idea was put forward by Otto in
(
Otto, 2011
)
through the study of modal translations induced by
observable configurations
of relational structures. We outline two connections between arboreal categories and modal logic which may be important for extending the homotopical approach to other fragments of first-order logic.

The first connection stems from a simple rephrasing of item
1
in Theorem
8.9
. Fix an arboreal category
\A
with binary products; we shall assume that
\A
contains, up to isomorphism, only a set
\Gamma
of paths. Consider the modal vocabulary whose set of unary relation symbols is
\Gamma
and whose only binary relation symbol is denoted by
\frown
. For each object
X
of
\A
, the tree
\Path{X}
can be regarded as a Kripke model for this vocabulary, where
\frown
is interpreted as the covering relation
\prec
associated with the tree order, and the interpretation of
P\in\Gamma
consists of the (equivalence classes of) path embeddings
m\in\Path{X}
whose domain is isomorphic to
P
.
We write
\mathbb{P}^{*}{X}
for the ensuing Kripke model, whose distinguished element is the root.
With this notation, item
1
in Theorem
8.9
states that:

Theorem 10.10.

X,Y\in\A
are bisimilar if, and only if,
\mathbb{P}^{*}{X}
and
\mathbb{P}^{*}{Y}
are bisimilar as Kripke models.

When combined with the
F
-bisimilarity relation induced by an arboreal adjunction (see §
9.1
), the previous result can be regarded as a categorification of Otto’s slogan “Bisimulation as the master game”
(
Otto, 2011
, §6.2.4)
. For example, in the case of the pebbling comonad, it implies that structures
A,B
satisfy the same sentences in
n
-variable infinitary logic
L^{n}_{\infty,\omega}
if, and only if, the corresponding tree-ordered Kripke models are bisimilar. If
A
and
B
are finite structures, a similar statement holds with
\mathrm{FO}^{n}
instead of
L^{n}_{\infty,\omega}
, cf. Corollary
3.5
, but the trees are always infinite.

The second connection arises from considering presheaves over categories of paths. The arboreal category
\A
embeds into the presheaf category over its full subcategory of paths (cf. Footnote
11
). The full subcategory of paths is a preorder if, and only if, the functor
\Path\colon\A\to\T
is faithful.
On the other hand, the subcategory
\A_{p}
of
\A
defined by the paths and the
embeddings
between them is always a preorder and in fact, upon fixing a small skeleton, it is a forest order; cf. Remark
8.10
.
The restricted Yoneda embedding gives a functor

\mathcal{I}\colon\A\to\widehat{\A_{p}},\ \ \ X\mapsto\left(\A(-,X)\colon\A_{p}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\Set\right)

which is not faithful in general, unless we restrict it to the wide subcategory of
\A
defined by the pathwise embeddings, cf. eq. (
7
). Yet, the category
\widehat{\A_{p}}
is arboreal by item
3
in Example
8.6
, and the functor
\mathcal{I}
preserves and reflects the bisimilarity relation.

This observation is central to the study of Morita equivalence in the presheaf categories
\widehat{\mathbf{P}_{k}}
in the modal case, and suggests the possibility of extending the homotopical analysis beyond modal logic.

Let us conclude by pointing out that objects of
\widehat{\mathbf{P}_{k}}
can be regarded as generalised Kripke models (for simplicity, we assume that the modal language contains a single binary relation symbol
\frown
):

Definition 10.11.

A
generalised Kripke model structure
on a presheaf
X\in\widehat{\mathbf{P}_{k}}
is given by:

•

an object
\dot{P}\in\mathbf{P}_{k}
and a distinguished element
\dot{x}\in X(\dot{P})
, and

•

for each unary relation symbol
S
, a set
\{S_{Q}^{X}\subseteq X(Q)\mid Q\in\mathbf{P}_{k}\}
.

For
x_{1}\in X(Q_{1})
and
x_{2}\in X(Q_{2})
,
x_{1}\frown x_{2}
if and only if

(i)

Q_{1}\prec Q_{2}
in the forest order of
\mathbf{P}_{k}
, and

(ii)

the unique embedding
i\colon Q_{1}\rightarrowtail Q_{2}
satisfies
X(i)\colon x_{2}\mapsto x_{1}
.

We denote this by
(X,\dot{P},\dot{x})
, or even just
(X,\dot{x})
, and refer to it as a
generalised Kripke model
.

Each
A\in\mathbf{S}_{k}
(identified with the corresponding representable presheaf) can be equipped with a canonical structure of generalised Kripke model. Recall that, for all
Q\in\mathbf{P}_{k}
, the elements of
A(Q)
are the embeddings
Q\rightarrowtail A
. In turn, each such embedding determines a unique element of
A
, namely the image of the top element of
Q
.
We let
\dot{P}
be the unique root of
\mathbf{P}_{k}
such that there is a (unique) embedding
m\colon\dot{P}\rightarrowtail A
, and let
\dot{x}=m\in A(\dot{P})
. For each unary relation symbol
S
and object
Q\in\mathbf{P}_{k}
, we take as
S_{Q}^{A}
the set of embeddings
Q\rightarrowtail A
whose corresponding element of
A
satisfies
S
.
Finally, under the correspondence between elements of
A
and embeddings
Q\rightarrowtail A
(with
Q\in\mathbf{P}_{k}
), the relation
\frown
in Definition
10.11
coincides with the accessibility relation of
A
.

We can then extend in a straightforward manner the satisfaction relation for modal formulas from Kripke models to generalised Kripke models. Alternatively, generalised Kripke models can be studied using the usual Kripke semantics by considering their
externalisation
: this essentially amounts to constructing a transition system from a presheaf using the Grothendieck construction.

Definition 10.12.

The
externalisation
of a generalised Kripke model
(X,\dot{x})
is the Kripke model
\mathbf{Ext}(X,\dot{x})
defined as follows: the elements of
\mathbf{Ext}(X,\dot{x})
are the tuples
(Q,y)
such that
y\in X(Q)
, and the distinguished element is
(\dot{P},\dot{x})
. The accessibility relation
\frown
is defined as in Definition
10.11
, and similarly for the unary relation symbols (i.e.,
(Q,y)
satisfies
S
if
y\in S_{Q}^{X}
).

For all generalised Kripke models
(X,\dot{x})
and all modal formulas
\varphi
, we have

X,\dot{x}\models\varphi\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \mathbf{Ext}(X,\dot{x})\models\varphi.

Moreover, the assignment
(X,\dot{x})\mapsto\mathbf{Ext}(X,\dot{x})
is functorial with respect to the obvious notion of morphism of generalised Kripke models (which are, in particular, natural transformations). The externalisation functor allows us to give a logic (or model-theoretic) meaning to the homotopical structure on
\widehat{\mathbf{P}_{k}}
. In fact, if
f
is a morphism of generalised Kripke models that reflects the unary relations, then (the underlying natural transformation of)
f
is a

•

cofibration if, and only if,
\mathbf{Ext}(f)
is an embedding of Kripke models, and a

•

trivial fibration if, and only if,
\mathbf{Ext}(f)
is a p-morphism.

If we take the presheaf category
\widehat{\A_{p}}
, where
\A
is the arboreal category corresponding to, say,
n
-variable logic or first-order logic with bounded quantifier rank, the approach outlined above yields a
translation

(\ \ )^{*}
of these logic fragments into modal logic (for the category
\widehat{\mathbf{P}_{k}}
, this translation is the identity). The properties of this translation remain to be fully understood, and may allow one to extend the techniques described in §
10.2
to first-order logic. Consider for example the following

Open problem.
Does an Equirank Łoś–Tarski theorem hold?

Working with the arboreal category corresponding to the Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé comonad, a positive answer to this question would follow if we could prove that for every first-order sentence
\varphi
that is preserved under embeddings, its modal translation
\varphi^{*}
is also preserved under embeddings.
