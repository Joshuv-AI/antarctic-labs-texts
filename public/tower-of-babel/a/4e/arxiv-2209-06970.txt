Generative Visual Prompt: Unifying Distributional Control of Pre-Trained Generative Models
Chen Henry Wu, Saman Motamed, Shaunak Srivastava, Fernando De la Torre

Abstract
Generative models (e.g., GANs, diffusion models) learn the underlying data distribution in an unsupervised manner. However, many applications of interest require sampling from a particular region of the output space or sampling evenly over a range of characteristics. For efficient sampling in these scenarios, we propose Generative Visual Prompt (PromptGen), a framework for distributional control over pre-trained generative models by incorporating knowledge of other off-the-shelf models. PromptGen defines control as energy-based models (EBMs) and samples images in a feed-forward manner by approximating the EBM with invertible neural networks, avoiding optimization at inference. Our experiments demonstrate how PromptGen can efficiently sample from several unconditional generative models (e.g., StyleGAN2, StyleNeRF, diffusion autoencoder, NVAE) in a controlled or/and de-biased manner using various off-the-shelf models: (1) with the CLIP model as control, PromptGen can sample images guided by text, (2) with image classifiers as control, PromptGen can de-bias generative models across a set of attributes or attribute combinations, and (3) with inverse graphics models as control, PromptGen can sample images of the same identity in different poses. (4) Finally, PromptGen reveals that the CLIP model shows a ‘‘reporting bias’’ when used as control, and PromptGen can further de-bias this controlled distribution in an iterative manner. 1 1 1 The code is available at https://github.com/ChenWu98/Generative-Visual-Prompt .

have achieved promising performance on image synthesis
[

,

,

,

. Though being well praised, they still face two main limitations. First, since generative models are typically trained in an unsupervised way, they lack controllability, meaning that it is unclear how to sample from a specific region of the space. Second, generative models are prone to inherit the imbalance and bias of training data
[
60
,
31
]
.
For instance, StyleGAN2 is more likely to produce images of white individuals, see Figure

,
31
]

[

. However, these methods are either model-dependent (i.e., requiring a well-structured style space) or label-intensive (i.e., requiring all training samples to be labeled for explicit conditions), limiting their generality and practical use.

distributional control of generative models
, which enables controllability by incorporating the knowledge of off-the-shelf models (e.g., CLIP
[

, classifiers, or inverse graphics models
[

).
Based on this unified view, we propose to learn
distributions in the latent space of a pre-trained generative model while keeping the pre-trained weights fixed.
Given its conceptual similarity to prompting
[
42
,
85
,
86
,
30
,
22
]
, we term our framework as Generative Visual Prompt (PromptGen).
PromptGen requires
no training data
, and the only supervision comes from off-the-shelf models that help define the control.
Specifically, PromptGen allows the user to define controls using an energy-based model (EBM) approximated by an invertible neural network (INN).
Unlike methods that require optimization at inference in EBM sampling
[
49
,
14
,
45
,
52
]
, PromptGen samples images in a
feed-forward
manner, which is highly efficient. Moreover, PromptGen
stands alone
at inference, meaning that we can discard the off-the-shelf models, which define the control, after training.

Related Work

Over the past few years, generative models have gained the ability to generate images with high visual quality. A few of the most widely used methods include generative adversarial networks (GANs)

]
, VAE
[

]
, invertible neural networks

, and diffusion models

Despite their success, controllability and de-biasing are still two fundamental challenges generative models face.
For controllability, existing methods include explicit conditioning at training

,

]
or local editing of the learned representation, e.g, “style” codes
[

,

,

,

,

.
For de-biasing, existing methods include local editing of “style” codes

,

]
and importance sampling for either training
[

]
or inference
[

]
. Existing works study these problems separately, each requiring a specific design for the task studied. On the contrary, PromptGen is a unified framework for arbitrary controls defined by off-the-shelf models. The benefits of unifying tasks have been shown by a recent trend of works in multiple research areas across vision and language
[

with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

Among them, plug-and-play generative models (PPGMs)

However, MCMC requires inference-time optimization, which is inefficient and requires the off-the-shelf models to be available at inference; this is also the criticism for diffusion models, regardless of being gradient-guided

]

In contrast, PromptGen achieves efficient, feed-forward sampling. Table

3.1
Latent-Space EBM for Distributional Control

The plug-and-play generative model
[
49
]
was first proposed to use a latent-space EBM for controllable image synthesis. If a fixed generative model is used, then theoretically, any image-space EBM can be viewed as a latent-space EBM, as shown by
[
19
]
and
[
52
]
. We define the latent-space EBM following similar formulation as
[
49
,
19
,
52
]
, with some new energy functions.
We then extend the formulation to incorporate the moment constraint
[
7
]
, which was adopted for language modeling
[
37
]
, but unlike
[
37
]
, we define the moment constraint in the latent space to accommodate generative vision models.

We define a control
\mathcal{C}
as
M
independent properties
\{\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{M}\}
, e.g.,
\bm{y}_{1}
can be a text description and
\bm{y}_{2}
can be an attribute.
The controllability can be defined as the EBM (detailed in Appendix
B.1
)

p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})=\frac{p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})}}{Z_{X}},\quad E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}E_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{i}),Z_{X}=\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}d\bm{x}^{\prime},

(1)

which reweights the image prior
p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})
with energy
E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})
, where images with smaller energy are preferred.
Using a pre-trained generative model
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{X}
that maps a latent code
\bm{z}
to an image
\bm{x}
, the image prior
p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})
is defined by sampling a latent code
\bm{z}
from
p_{\bm{z}}=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and mapping it to
\bm{x}=G(\bm{z})
. Appendix
B.2
shows that this EBM is equivalent to the latent-space EBM

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C})=\frac{p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(G(\bm{z}))}}{Z},\\
&E_{\mathcal{C}}(G(\bm{z}))=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}E_{i}(G(\bm{z}),\bm{y}_{i}),\quad Z=\int_{\bm{z}^{\prime}}p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(G(\bm{z}^{\prime}))}d\bm{z}^{\prime}.\end{split}

(2)

Latent-space EBM allows us to use any off-the-shelf model to specify the control.
The following are some examples that are discussed in this paper (explained in Appendix
B.1
):

Classifier energy:
Given a classifier
P(\cdot|\bm{x})
and the target class
a
that we want to sample images from, we define the classifier energy as
E_{\text{classifier}}(\bm{x},a)=-\log P(a|\bm{x})
.

CLIP energy:
Using the CLIP model
[
58
]
, we define the CLIP energy as the cosine distance between the embeddings of the image and the text
\bm{t}
, averaged over
L
differentiable augmentations
[
84
,
46
]
:

E_{\text{CLIP}}(\bm{x},\bm{t})=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\bigg(1-\cos\Big\langle\text{CLIP}_{\text{img}}\big(\text{DiffAug}_{l}(\bm{x})\big),\text{CLIP}_{\text{text}}(\bm{t})\Big\rangle\bigg).

(3)

Inverse graphics energy:
Given an inverse graphics model,
f_{\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}}
, which infers image parameters (e.g., pose and expression), and the target parameters
\bm{\rho}
, we define the inverse graphics energy as

E_{\text{inv-graphics}}(\bm{x},\bm{\rho})=d\big\langle f_{\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}}(\bm{x}),\bm{\rho}\big\rangle^{2},

(4)

where
d\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the geodesic distance between the inferred parameters and the target parameters.

Moment constraint:

Some controls cannot be
directly
defined by off-the-shelf models, and the moment constraint
[
7
,
37
]
is one of them. Given a mapping
\bm{\gamma}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
(e.g.,
\bm{\gamma}
can be a classifier that outputs the probability simplex), the moment constraint defines the target distribution
p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})
as

p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})=\underbrace{\mathop{\arg\min}_{p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})}\mathbb{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\Big(p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})\|p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})\Big)}_{\text{Deviation from the pre-trained distribution}},\quad\text{s.t. }\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})}\big[\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x})\big]=\bm{\mu}}_{\text{Moment constraint}},

(5)

where
\bm{\mu}
is the user-specified vector.
For example, if we want to generate images that are uniformly distributed across races, we may use a race classifier as
\bm{\gamma}
and define
\bm{\mu}=\big(|\mathcal{A}|^{-1},\ldots,|\mathcal{A}|^{-1}\big)
where
\mathcal{A}
is the set of races.
It means that we would like to find a distribution that 1) stays close to the original, pre-trained generative model
G
and 2) mitigates
G
’s bias.
In this paper, we generalize the moment constraint to the latent space, and approximate the above objective as (detailed in Appendix
B.6
):

\displaystyle p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C})=\frac{p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z})\exp\Big(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}\big(G(\bm{z})\big)\Big)}{Z},\quad Z=\int_{\bm{z}^{\prime}}p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})\exp\Big(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}\big(G(\bm{z}^{\prime})\big)\Big)d\bm{z}^{\prime},

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\bm{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{z}^{(N)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z}),\bm{x}^{(j)}=G(\bm{z}^{(j)})}\bigg\|\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\big(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x}^{(j)})\big)\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x}^{(j)})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N}\exp\big(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x}^{(j^{\prime})})\big)}-\bm{\mu}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}.

(7)

Notably, to optimize
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
in Eq. (
7
), we leverage existing gradient-based optimization tools such as stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and Adam
[
38
]
.

3.2
Approximating EBM with Invertible Neural Network

Given the functional form of the EBM
p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C})
, our next step is to approximate it with an efficient sampling network. To achieve this,
we train a distribution
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})
that minimizes the KL divergence
\mathbb{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})\|p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C}))
.
Estimating
\mathbb{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})\|p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C}))
requires easily sampling
\bm{z}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}
and tractably computing
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})
.
Inspired by
[
54
]
, we model
p_{\bm{\theta}}
with an INN
f_{\bm{\theta}}
that defines a bijection
\bm{z}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\epsilon})
, which has two merits besides the invertibility: (1) one can easily sample
\bm{z}
by sampling
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and mapping it to
\bm{z}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\epsilon})
, and (2)
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})
has a closed-form solution:

\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})=\log\mathcal{N}(\bm{\epsilon}|\bm{0},\bm{I})-\log|\det(\frac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}})|,\quad\bm{z}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\epsilon}).

(8)

Based on these properties of INN, we can rewrite our KL divergence objective
\mathbb{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})\|p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C}))
as (full derivations in Appendix
B.3
) the following form:

\begin{split}&\mathbb{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})\|p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C}))=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}),\bm{x}=G(\bm{z})}\Big[\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})}{p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})}/Z}\Big]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}),\bm{z}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\epsilon}),\bm{x}=G(\bm{z})}\Big[-\log|\det(\frac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}})|-\log p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z})+E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})\Big]-\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})}+\log Z.\end{split}

(9)

Since
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})}
and
\log Z
are independent of
\bm{\theta}
, our training objective becomes

\mathop{\arg\min}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}),\bm{z}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\epsilon}),\bm{x}=G(\bm{z})}\Big[\underbrace{-\log|\det(\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}/\partial\bm{\epsilon})|}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{jac}}}\ \underbrace{-\ \log p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z})}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{latent-prior}}}\ \underbrace{+\ E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}}}\Big].

(10)

PromptGen in a class-embedding space
(Figure
4(g)
) Previous works
[
4
,
64
]
have shown that
class conditioning
boosts generative models’ performances on ImageNet
[
62
]
. Specifically, class-conditioned generative models map a latent code
\bm{z}
and a class embedding
\bm{y}
to
\bm{x}=G(\bm{z},\bm{y})
.
To extend PromptGen to these models, we train an INN
h_{\bm{\theta}}
to map
\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2}\bm{I})
to
\bm{y}=h_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\xi})
, where
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\sigma}
are the mean and standard deviation of
G
’s class embeddings.
The motivation for defining the distribution of
\bm{\xi}
as
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2}\bm{I})
but not
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
is that we want the learned INN
h_{\bm{\theta}}
to be volume-preserving, which is easier to train.
The training objective is to minimize
\mathbb{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z},\bm{y})\|p(\bm{z},\bm{y}|\mathcal{C}))
, which is equivalent to (full derivations in Appendix
B.4
):

\begin{split}&\mathop{\arg\min}_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}),\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2}\bm{I}),\bm{z}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\epsilon}),\bm{y}=h_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\xi}),\bm{x}=G(\bm{z},\bm{y})}\Big[-\log|\det(\frac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}})|-\log p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\log|\det(\frac{\partial h_{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial\bm{\xi}})|-\log p_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y})+E_{\mathcal{C}}\big(\bm{x}\big)\Big],\end{split}

(11)

where
p_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}^{2}\bm{I})
is the estimated class-embedding distribution.

PromptGen with conditional INN

To generalize the control to continuous values, e.g., the scene parameters
\bm{\rho}
in Eq. (
4
), we condition the INN on
\bm{\rho}
.
The condition is modeled by replacing
\bm{z}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\epsilon})
with
\bm{z}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\rho})

B.5

[
1
]

1

When the condition is modeled by the equivariant operations proposed by
[
79
]
, PromptGen also satisfies the homomorphism property
[
79
]
in the latent space.

5
Conclusions and Future Work

This paper proposes PromptGen, a unified framework to learn latent distributions for distributional control of pre-trained generative models, by leveraging the knowledge of other off-the-shelf models. Unlike previous approaches, which require optimization for generating each sample at inference, PromptGen trains an invertible neural network to approximate the latent distribution. Thus, PromptGen can be viewed as an amortization of the inference-time optimization into a feed-forward neural network, allowing for efficient, feed-forward sampling at inference. Further, this amortization also makes PromptGen not require the availability of those off-the-shelf models at inference. In practice, PromptGen offers generality for algorithmic design and modularity for control composition, and it also enables iterative controls via functional composition. Experiments validate that PromptGen applies to various generative models (StyleGAN2, StyleNeRF, diffusion autoencoder, and NVAE), control types (continuous, discrete, and moment constraint), off-the-shelf models (CLIP, classifiers, and inverse graphics models), and data domains (faces, churches, animals, ImageNet, and landscapes).

Limitation and future work:
We provide an error analysis in Appendix
F
and a discussion on societal impact in Section
6
. PromptGen is restricted by the pre-trained generative model’s coverage
[
3
,
28
]
. Notably, since PromptGen focuses on mode-seeking and reweighting instead of domain adaptation, the support set of the output distribution will not change after PromptGen learning. We find it interesting to further explore combining PromptGen and domain adaptation
[
17
]
to achieve better generalization and adaptability.
PromptGen depends on the off-the-shelf models that provide knowledge about the control (Appendix
F
);
it can be beneficial to explore how to learn energy functions
[
15
,
27
]
(besides our moment constraint) for fine-grained control with less bias.

6
Societal Impact

Like any technology, PromptGen has both benefits and drawbacks for society. On the positive side, we have demonstrated that PromptGen can uncover biases learned by text-image models (like CLIP) and to de-bias text-image models and pre-trained generative models, suggesting that PromptGen may be a helpful tool for fair AI if used properly. The efficient inference of PromptGen also helps reduce the computational expense, giving a positive impact on the environment. However, improved controllability makes it easier to synthesize targeted images; for instance, the creation of deceptive media such as DeepFakes
[
77
,
74
]
and privacy leakage. To battle these cases, we expect researchers and practitioners to use technologies that can detect fake media and mitigate privacy leakage. We also encourage practitioners to consider these risks when using PromptGen to develop systems.

Appendix B
Method Details and Derivations

B.1
Controllability as EBM

In Section
3
, we defined a control
\mathcal{C}
as
M
independent properties
\{\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{M}\}
. For example,
\bm{y}_{1}
can be a text description, and
\bm{y}_{2}
can be an attribute.
In this part, we first elaborate on why controllability can be formed as EBMs in Eq. (
1
). We then provide concrete examples of the distributions derived from different energy functions defined in Section
3.1
.

We denote the image prior as
p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})
, the only distribution that can be estimated from data when labels for the control are not provided during generative model pre-training. Given the control
\mathcal{C}=\{\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{M}\}
, we resort to Bayes’ theorem to rewrite the conditional distribution
p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})
as

\displaystyle p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle\propto p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})p(\mathcal{C}|\bm{x})

(13)

\displaystyle=p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})p(\bm{y}_{1}|\bm{x})\prod_{i=2}^{M}p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x},\bm{y}_{<i})

(14)

\displaystyle=p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})\prod_{i=1}^{M}p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x})\quad\text{(independence assumption)}.

(15)

For Eq. (
15
) to be well-defined, we need to define
p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x})
for each
\bm{y}_{i}
. In order to incorporate the knowledge of arbitrary off-the-self models (besides image classifiers), we define each
p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x})
as

p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x})=\frac{\exp(-\lambda_{i}E_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{i}))}{Z_{i}},\quad Z_{i}=\int_{\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}}\exp(-\lambda_{i}E_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}))d\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}.

(16)

Eq. (
16
) says that
p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x})
is proportional to the exponential of an energy function
E_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{i})
, where
\bm{y}_{i}
with lower energy has higher density or mass. Note that Eq. (
16
) defines a distribution over all possible values of
\bm{y}_{i}
instead of all possible values of
\bm{x}
.
Combining Eq. (
15
) and Eq. (
16
), we have

p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})=\frac{p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})}}{Z_{X}},\quad E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}E_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{i}),Z_{X}=\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}d\bm{x}^{\prime},

(17)

which is the same equation as Eq. (
1
).

In the following, we use Eq. (
16
) to derive the distributions from the classifier energy, CLIP energy, and inverse graphics energy defined in Section
3.1
.

Classifier energy:
Given a classifier
P(\cdot|\bm{x})
and the target class
a
, we define the classifier energy as
E_{\text{classifier}}(\bm{x},a)=-\log P(a|\bm{x})
. Using Eq. (
16
), we arrive at:

p_{\text{classifier}}(a|\bm{x})=\frac{\exp(\lambda_{\text{classifier}}\log P(a|\bm{x}))}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}\exp(\lambda_{\text{classifier}}\log P(a^{\prime}|\bm{x}))}=\frac{P(a|\bm{x})^{\lambda_{\text{classifier}}}}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}P(a^{\prime}|\bm{x})^{\lambda_{\text{classifier}}}},

(18)

which is equivalent to a temperature-adjusted distribution of the original classifier.

CLIP energy:
Using Eq. (
16
), the CLIP energy in Eq. (
3
) is equivalent to

p_{\text{CLIP}}(\bm{t}|\bm{x})\propto\exp\Bigg(-\frac{\lambda_{\text{CLIP}}}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\bigg(1-\cos\Big\langle\text{CLIP}_{\text{img}}\big(\text{DiffAug}_{l}(\bm{x})\big),\text{CLIP}_{\text{text}}(\bm{t})\Big\rangle\bigg)\Bigg).

(19)

Inverse graphics energy:
Using Eq. (
16
), the inverse graphics energy in Eq. (
4
) is equivalent to

p_{\text{inv-graphics}}(\bm{\rho}|\bm{x})\propto\exp(-\lambda_{\text{inv-graphics}}d\big\langle f_{\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}}(\bm{x}),\bm{\rho}\big\rangle^{2}).

(20)

If the geodesic distance
d\big\langle\cdot,\cdot\big\rangle
is the Euclidean distance, then
p_{\text{inv-graphics}}(\bm{\rho}|\bm{x})
is a Gaussian distribution whose mean is
\bm{\rho}
and whose variance depends on the hyperparameter
\lambda_{\text{inv-graphics}}
; if the geodesic distance
d\big\langle\cdot,\cdot\big\rangle
is the spherical distance (e.g., the distance between pose parameters defined on a unit sphere), then
p_{\text{inv-graphics}}(\bm{\rho}|\bm{x})
is a vMF distribution.

B.2
Equivalence between Image-Space EBM and Latent-Space EBM

Proposition 1
.

Define
\bm{x}\sim p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})
as
\bm{z}\sim p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z}),\bm{x}=G(\bm{z})
and
p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})
as
\bm{z}\sim p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C}),\bm{x}=G(\bm{z})
, where
p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C})
is defined as the following EBM:

\displaystyle p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C})=\frac{p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(G(\bm{z}))}}{Z},\quad E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}E_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{i}),Z=\int_{\bm{z}^{\prime}}p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(G(\bm{z}^{\prime}))}d\bm{z}^{\prime}.

(21)

We have

p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})=\frac{p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})}}{Z_{X}},\quad Z_{X}=\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}d\bm{x}^{\prime}.

(22)

In spirit, our proof follows the proof in
[
52
]
, which follows
[
19
]
. The difference between our proof and that in
[
52
]
is that we derive
p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})
from
p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C})
while they derived
p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C})
from
p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

Based on Lemma 1 in
[
19
]
and Lemma 1 in
[
52
]
,
p(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C})
is equivalent to rejection sampling with proposal distribution
p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z})
and acceptance probability

\displaystyle r(\bm{z})=\frac{e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(G(\bm{z}))}}{M_{\mathcal{C}}\cdot Z},\quad\text{where }\forall\bm{z},M_{\mathcal{C}}>\frac{e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(G(\bm{z}))}}{Z}.

(23)

Since
p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})
is defined as
\bm{z}\sim p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z}|\mathcal{C}),\bm{x}=G(\bm{z})
,
p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})
is equivalent to rejection sampling with proposal distribution
p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z}),\bm{x}=G(\bm{z})
and acceptance probability

\displaystyle r(\bm{x})=\frac{e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})}}{M_{\mathcal{C}}\cdot Z}.

(24)

Note that the above proposal distribution
\bm{z}\sim p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z}),\bm{x}=G(\bm{z})
is the same as

by definition. Based on Lemma 1 in
[
19
]
and Lemma 1 in
[

]
, we arrive at:

\displaystyle p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})r(\bm{x})}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}[r(\bm{x}^{\prime})]}

(25)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})}/(M_{\mathcal{C}}\cdot Z)}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}[e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}/(M_{\mathcal{C}}\cdot Z)]}

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})}}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}[e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}]}

(27)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})}}{Z_{X}},\quad Z_{X}=\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})e^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}d\bm{x}^{\prime}.

Given a mapping
\bm{\gamma}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
, moment constraint
[
7
,
37
]
defines the target distribution
p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})
as

p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})=\underbrace{\mathop{\arg\min}_{p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})}\mathbb{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})\|p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}))}_{\text{Deviation from the pre-trained distribution}},\quad\text{s.t. }\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})}\big[\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x})\big]=\bm{\mu}}_{\text{Moment constraint}},

(33)

where
\bm{\mu}
is the user-specified constraint.
Examples are provided in the main text, and we omit them here for brevity.

[
7
]
showed that Eq. (
33
) can be approximated to an arbitrary precision by

p(\bm{x}|\mathcal{C})\propto p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})\exp\big(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x})\big),

(34)

where
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
needs to be computed.
[
37
]
estimates
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
by solving the following regression problem:

\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\bm{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{x}^{(N)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})}\bigg\|\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\big(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x}^{(j)})\big)\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x}^{(j)})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N}\exp\big(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x}^{(j^{\prime})})\big)}-\bm{\mu}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}.

(35)

In
[
37
]
, sampling
\bm{x}\sim p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})
is straightforward since they focus on autoregressive language models.
In the context of latent-variable vision generative models, where

\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\bm{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{z}^{(N)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p_{\bm{z}}(\bm{z}),\bm{x}^{(j)}=G(\bm{z}^{(j)})}\bigg\|\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\big(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x}^{(j)})\big)\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x}^{(j)})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N}\exp\big(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}(\bm{x}^{(j^{\prime})})\big)}-\bm{\mu}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}.

(36)

Finally, based on Proposition

Figure 14:
PromptGen with intra-sample signed-distance constraints that generate 3D face models with varying distances in the mouth, eye openness, nose-tip stretch, and lip forward.

3D face modeling has been an active area of research that has recently gained major interest due to applications in virtual humans, deep faces, and digital actors. Existing 3D deep learning generative models build 3D compact representations of shape and appearance capable of modeling non-linearity (e.g., scatter effects, specularities) that is necessary for generating photo-realistic faces.
Providing a generative model with the capability of generating 3D faces with a particular geometry (e.g., a specific distance between eyes or nose length) is particularly useful for technical artists when creating new characters. However, this problem remains unaddressed, partially due to the lack of publicly available 3D databases labeled with such constraints.
We refer to this capability as the intra-sample constraint in 3D generative models, and this section shows how our PromptGen framework is able to achieve this by defining a new energy function based on signed distances.

We conducted the experiments on the FaceScape dataset
[
82
]
, a large-scale 3D human face dataset consisting of
16,940
topologically uniformly registered 3D face meshes along with high-quality textures. The dataset contains
847
identities with
20
expressions per identity, and each mesh consists of
26,317
vertices of which
68
are 3D landmark vertices covering eyes, nose, mouth, jaw, and eyebrows. With some abuse of notation, we denote a 3D mesh as
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3V}
(
\bm{x}
is used to represent an image in other parts of this paper), where
V=26,317
is the number of vertices. Given an index
l
of a vertex (e.g., one of the
68
landmark vertices), the 3D coordinate of this vertex is defined as
\bm{x}[l]\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We pre-train a generative mesh model
G
that maps each latent code
\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
to a 3D mesh
\bm{x}
.
PromptGen allows us to obtain a geometric control on the 3D meshes.
Given two landmark vertex indices
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
, we put an intra-sample constraint that enforces the signed distance (along a given direction) between the two vertices as
s
.
To this effect, we define the signed-distance energy as

E_{\text{signed-distance}}(\bm{x})=|d(\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2})-s|,\quad\bm{v}_{1}=\bm{x}[l_{1}],\bm{v}_{2}=\bm{x}[l_{2}]

(39)

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the signed-distance (along a given direction), and
|\cdot|
is the absolute value.

Figure
14
presents several examples of 3D mesh control, in which random textures are applied to the generated meshes. It shows that PromptGen successfully controls the generative 3D mesh model in terms of the mouth stretch, eye openness, nose-tip stretch, and lip forward.
