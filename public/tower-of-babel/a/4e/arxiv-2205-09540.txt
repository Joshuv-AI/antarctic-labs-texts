On the spectrum of twisted Laplacians and the Teichmüller representation
Frédéric Naud, Polyxeni Spilioti

Abstract
. Given a compact hyperbolic surface X = Γ \ ℍ 2 X=\Gamma\backslash{\mathbb{H}^{2}} and a linear non-unitary representation ϱ : Γ → GL ⁡ ( V ) \varrho:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}(V) , we investigate the spectrum of the twisted Laplacian Δ ϱ \Delta_{\varrho} acting on sections of the associated flat vector bundle E ϱ → X E_{\varrho}\rightarrow X . We show that this non self-adjoint operator has its spectrum inside a parabola related to a critical exponent δ \delta of the representation. In the case where ϱ \varrho is of Teichmüller type we then exhibit explicit parabolic regions determined by another constant δ 0 \delta_{0} with an asymptotic spectral density which improves Weyl’s law by a power factor. Both δ \delta and δ 0 \delta_{0} are uniquely determined by the so-called “Manhattan curve” related to the representation ϱ \varrho .



1.
Introduction and main results

The Laplace-Beltrami operator is a key object in Riemannian geometry: its spectrum encodes some fine properties of the geometry of the manifold and the dynamics of the geodesic flow. This operator is self-adjoint and has discrete spectrum on compact manifolds. It has natural generalizations to vector bundles and remains self-adjoint if the vector bundle comes equipped with a natural hermitian metric. In particular, if this vector bundle is flat, the holonomy of a loop depends only on its homotopy class and there is an associated natural unitary representation of the fundamental group. Conversely, any unitary finite dimensional representation of the fundamental group of a compact manifold give rise to a flat vector bundle on which a natural
self-adjoint Bochner-Laplace operator lives. There are however natural situations where non-unitary representations of the fundamental group lead to consider an associated non-self-adjoint Laplacian, whose discrete spectrum remains rather mysterious. In this paper, we will study non-self-adjoint Bochner-Laplace operators associated to linear, non-unitary representations of surface groups. There is a very rich litterature on this subject which leads to a wide class of non-self-adjoint Laplacians for which, apart from the general results pertaining to elliptic operators, almost nothing is known.

More precisely, let
{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
denote the hyperbolic plane and let
X=\Gamma\backslash{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
be a compact hyperbolic surface obtained by quotienting
{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
by a discrete co-compact subgroup of
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Given a linear representation
\varrho:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}(V)
, where
V
is a complex
d
-dimensional vector space, we can easily build a flat vector Bundle
E_{\varrho}
over
X
by considering the quotient
of
{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\times V
by the
\Gamma
-action defined by
\gamma.(z,v)\colon=(\gamma(z),\varrho(\gamma)v)
. Smooth sections of
E_{\varrho}
are then identified with elements
F\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{H}^{2}},V)
satisfying

\forall\ \gamma\in\Gamma,\ F(\gamma z)=\varrho(\gamma)F(z).

Let
\Delta_{\varrho}
be the usual hyperbolic Laplacian acting diagonally on smooth
\varrho
-equivariant sections, then this operator can be identified with the twisted Bochner-Laplace operator

\Delta_{\varrho}=-\mathrm{tr}((\nabla^{E_{\varrho}})^{2}),

where
\nabla^{E_{\varrho}}
is a flat connection on
E_{\varrho}
. Choosing a hermitian metric on
V
, one can define
L^{2}(X,E_{\varrho})
as the space of square integrable sections of
E_{\varrho}
. The operator
\Delta_{\varrho}
is in general not self-adjoint.
However, it has a self-adjoint
principal symbol and hence it has nice spectral properties (
[
45
]
).
It is an elliptic operator and, because of the compactness of the base
X
, it has a unique closed extension with dense domain in
L^{2}(X,E_{\varrho})
, with a compact resolvent. Its spectrum is therefore discrete
and moreover is contained in a translate of a positive cone
C\subset\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{R}^{+}\subset\mathbb{C}
(
[
45
]
,
[
28
, Lemma 2.1]
).
In addition, the spectrum of the twisted Laplacian satisfies a Weyl law, as showed by Müller in a pioneering work
[
28
]
. He also proved a general trace formula for integral operators induced by the twisted Laplacian,
using Paley-Wiener test functions.
This work generalizes Selberg’s theory in a non-self-adjoint setting for all locally symmetric space of split rank one. The higher rank case
is considered by Shen, where he used the Müller’s Selberg trace formula to study the Ruelle zeta function and its relation to the complex analytic torsion (
[
44
]
). In general, the twisted Laplacians are defined as coupling operators to a flat vector bundle
(
[
12
, Section 2]
).
One can define then also the twisted Hodge Laplacian, acting on the space of vector-valued differential forms. Consequently,
there exists an extension of the usual notion of the analytic torsion (
[
38
]
) to the case of non self-adjoint Hodge Laplacians. This is the Cappell-Miller torsion (
[
12
]
) and the refined analytic torsion of Braverman and Kappeler (
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
).

Let now
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of primitive conjugacy classes in
\Gamma
and if
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}
, let
\ell(\gamma)
be the length of the associated primitive closed geodesic on
X
. We define the critical exponent
\delta
of the representation
\varrho
by

\delta(\varrho):=\inf\left\{s>0\ :\ \sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P},k\geq 1}|\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma^{k}))|e^{-sk\ell(\gamma)}<\infty\right\}.

A simple argument using the fact that the word length and the displacement function (with respect to hyperbolic distance in
{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
) are equivalent shows that there exist

M,C>0
and a set of representatives
\{\gamma\}
of conjugacy classes such that,
|\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma))|\leq Me^{C\ell(\gamma)}
, hence
\delta(\varrho)
is finite. Since
\varrho
is assumed to be non unitary, we will have in general
\delta(\varrho)>1
. For certain type of representations
\varrho
, this critical exponent is related to the celebrated Manhattan curve, see below for details.

We then define the parabolic regions
\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}\subset\mathbb{C}
for all
\sigma\in(1/2,+\infty)
by

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}:=\left\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\ :\ x\geq\sigma(1-\sigma)+\frac{y^{2}}{(1-2\sigma)^{2}}\right\}.

We will also use the notation
P_{\sigma}:=\partial\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}
to denote the parabola given by the equation
\{x+iy\ :\ x=\sigma(1-\sigma)+\frac{y^{2}}{(1-2\sigma)^{2}}\}
. In the degenerate case where
\sigma=1/2
, we set

P_{\sigma}=\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}=[1/4,+\infty)
.
Our first result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Delta_{\varrho}
be the twisted Laplacian defined as above, then one always have

\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})\subset\mathcal{C}_{\delta(\varrho)}.

We will show in
\lx@sectionsign 3
that this inclusion is in some sense sharp: there exists families of linear representations
\varrho
such that
\Delta_{\varrho}
satisfies

\inf_{\lambda\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})}{\rm Re}(\lambda)=\delta(1-\delta).

It is tempting to believe that one always has this identity, but this is not true: in
\lx@sectionsign 3
we exhibit a family of linear representations for which
\delta(1-\delta)\not\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is standard and follows from the already known properties of the twisted Selberg
zeta functions and a Heat trace argument. As proved by Muller in
[
28
]
, the twisted Laplacian satisfies a Weyl law: as
T\rightarrow+\infty
,

\#\{\lambda\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})\ :\ |\lambda|\leq T\}=\frac{\mathrm{dim}(V)\mathrm{Vol}(X)}{4\pi}T+o(T).

We prove a much more precise fact for some specific type of representations which we call “of Teichmüller type”. Given
a fixed hyperbolic, compact surface
X=\Gamma\backslash{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
, we will view the
Teichmüller space

\mathcal{T}(X)
as the space of
faithfull discrete representations

\rho:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),

quotiented by conjugations in
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
We denote the hyperbolic distance on
{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
by
d(z,w)
, we will use the upper half-plane model
{\mathbb{H}^{2}}=\{{\rm Im}(z)>0\}
in the definition below.

Definition 1.2
.

A linear representation
\varrho:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}(V)
is said to be of Teichmüller type if there exist a norm
\|.\|_{0}
on
\mathrm{GL}(V)
, a representation

\rho\in\mathcal{T}(X)
and constants
C,\beta>0
such that for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
,

(1)

\|\varrho(\gamma)\|_{0}\leq Ce^{\beta d(i,\rho(\gamma)i)},

(2)

C^{-1}e^{\beta\ell(\rho(\gamma))}\leq|\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma))|\leq Ce^{\beta\ell(\rho(\gamma))}.

Let
\varrho:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}(V_{\varrho})

be a representation of Teichmüller type, as defined above. Clearly we have

\delta(\varrho)=\inf\left\{s>0\ :\ \sum_{\gamma,k}e^{\beta k\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)-sk\ell(\gamma)}<\infty\right\},

where
\rho:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a Teichmüller deformation of
\Gamma
and
\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)
stands for
\ell(\rho(\gamma))
. Following the work of Burger
[
11
]
,
let
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
be the convex region defined by

\mathcal{M}_{\rho}:=\left\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ :\ \sum_{\gamma,k}e^{-ak\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)-bk\ell(\gamma)}<\infty\right\}.

Then, the boundary of
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
is a convex graph
\mathfrak{C}
which contains the points
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
, called ”the Manhattan curve”. This curve is a straight line if and only if
\Gamma
and
\rho(\Gamma)
are conjugated in
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, see
[
11
,
43
]
.
It was shown by Sharp in
[
43
]
that this curve is actually real-analytic. In our setting,
\delta(\varrho)
is the unique real number such that
(-\beta,\delta(\varrho))
belongs to
\mathfrak{C}
, see the figure below.

The Manhattan curve
\mathfrak{C}
and the exponent
\delta(\varrho)
.

The main result of this paper is the following improved Weyl law.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that
\varrho
is a a linear representation of Teichmüller type. There exists
\delta/2<\delta_{0}<\delta
such that for all
\delta_{0}<\sigma\leq\delta
, one can find

\epsilon(\sigma)>0
such that as
r\rightarrow+\infty
, we have

M_{\sigma}(r):=\#\{\lambda\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}\ :\ r\leq|\lambda|\leq r+\sqrt{r}\}=O(r^{1/2-\epsilon(\sigma)}).

Moreover, similarly as
\delta
, the constant
\delta_{0}
is uniquely determined by
\beta
and the Manhattan curve: if
(-2\beta,\eta)\in\mathfrak{C}
, then
\delta_{0}=\eta/2
.

We have the following corollary.

Corollary 1.4
.

For all
\sigma>\delta_{0}
,
as
T\rightarrow+\infty
we have

N_{\sigma}(T):=\#\{\lambda\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}\ :\ |\lambda|\leq T\}=O(T^{1-\epsilon(\sigma)}),

which beats the Weyl law by a polynomial factor. As a consequence, Weyl law actually holds in
\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}
for all
\sigma>\delta_{0}
: as
T\rightarrow+\infty
,

\#\{\lambda\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})\cap\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}\ :\ |\lambda|\leq T\}=\frac{\mathrm{dim}(V)\mathrm{Vol}(X)}{4\pi}T+o(T).

Proof
. Consider the sequence
(n_{p})_{p\geq 0}
defined by
n_{0}:=1
and
n_{p+1}=n_{p}+\sqrt{n_{p}}
for all
p\geq 1
. It is a standard fact that as
p\rightarrow+\infty
we have

n_{p}=\frac{p^{2}}{4}(1+o(1)).

We fix
\sigma>\delta_{0}
, let
T>>1
and choose the smallest integer
p
such that
T\leq n_{p+1}
. We have
p=O(\sqrt{T})
, while
n_{p}<T
. We then write

N_{\sigma}(T)\leq O(1)+\sum_{k=0}^{p}M_{\sigma}(n_{k})\leq O(1)+(p+1)\max_{0\leq k\leq p}M_{\sigma}(n_{k})=O\left(T^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon(\sigma)}\right),

and the proof is done.
\square

The above theorem therefore shows that in the “high frequency” regime when we have
{\rm Re}(\lambda)\rightarrow+\infty
, the bulk of the spectrum lies in a smaller parabolic region
\mathcal{C}_{\delta_{0}}
.

Spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}
with
P_{\delta}
and
P_{\sigma}
, the bulk of the spectrum being inside
\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}
.

Some explicit bounds on
\delta_{0}
involving the “geodesic strech” can be derived, see
\lx@sectionsign 6
. The computable examples of
\lx@sectionsign 3
show that
\delta_{0}
is sharp.

In the case when
\varrho
is a
one-dimensional non-unitary representation
, Anantharaman
[
2
]
proves a similar result as Theorem
1.3
. Indeed, if we have

\varrho(\gamma)=e^{\int_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\omega},

where
\omega
is a harmonic real-valued
1
-form on
X=\Gamma\backslash{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
, and
\widetilde{\gamma}\in H^{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is the homology class determined by
\gamma
. The spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}
acting on sections of the line bundle
E_{\varrho}
is the same as the spectrum of

\Delta_{\omega}f:=\Delta_{X}f+2\omega(\nabla f),

acting on functions on
X
. This spectral problem can therefore be understood in terms of a generalized stationary damped wave equation on
X
, and the semi-classical technique of Sjöstrand
[
47
]
can be refined as done in
[
2
]
to obtain the one-dimensional analog of Theorem
1.3
. Unfortunately, this one-dimensional technique cannot be applied for higher dimensional representations. Hence our method is fairly different, but our result can be viewed as a generalization of
[
2
]
to the higher dimensional representations case. It is also interesting to observe that in
[
2
]
, independently from
[
28
]
, a trace formula for
\Delta_{\rho}
is derived and utilized, by an analytic continuation argument of the “usual” Selberg trace formula for a unitary representation.

The paper is organized as follows. In
\lx@sectionsign 2
, we recall the known facts on twisted Laplacians, including the heat trace formula and the main result on the Selberg zeta function from
[
19
]
. We then prove easily Theorem
1.1
. In
\lx@sectionsign 3
, we exhibit representations of Teichmüller type and compute the spectrum for several examples: depending on the parity of the dimension of the representation space, these spectra lie on parabolas and are related to (self-adjoint) Dirac and Laplace spectra. In
\lx@sectionsign 4
, we recall the so-called Bowen-series coding for co-compact Fuchsian groups and relate
\delta
to the topological pressure by using some basic Teichmüller theory and thermodynamical formalism. The proof of Theorem
1.3
really starts in
\lx@sectionsign 6
, where we relate the counting problem to an estimate from above of certain Hilbert-Schmidt norms for a family of transfer operators. These transfer operators act on a family of (vector valued) Bergman spaces of holomorphic functions and
rather delicate off-diagonal estimates for Bergman kernels of shrinking domains are needed: they are proved in the Appendix. We finally analyse
\delta_{0}
and prove some explicit bounds via Thermodynamical formalism. At the technical level, the core of the proof of Theorem
1.3
bears some similarities with
[
22
,
30
]
, but there are some serious additional difficulties and differences which make it interesting: the non-unitarity of the representation involved, and the need to use more general complex domains (hence the non-trivial Bounds on Bergman kernels).

2.
Twisted Selberg zeta functions and the main parabola

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
, which is completely general.
We define first the twisted Bochner–Laplace operator as introduced in
[
28
, Section 4]
. This operator acts on sections of a vector bundle over
X
. It is an elliptic operator which is in general not self-adjoint. Nevertheless, it has a self-adjoint principle symbol (with respect to the choice of a metric) and hence it has qualitatively similar spectral properties.

Let
\varrho
be a finite-dimensional, complex representation

\varrho\colon\Gamma\rightarrow\rm{GL}(V_{\varrho})

of
\Gamma
.
Denote by
E_{\varrho}=V_{\varrho}\times_{\Gamma}{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\rightarrow X
the associated flat vector bundle over
X
, equipped with a flat connection
\nabla^{E_{\varrho}}
.
We recall the construction of the twisted Bochner–Laplace operator
\Delta_{\varrho}^{\sharp}
, acting on smooth sections of
E_{\varrho}
.
The second covariant derivative
(\nabla^{E_{\varrho}})^{2}
is defined by

(\nabla^{E_{\varrho}})^{2}_{V,W}:=\nabla_{V}^{E_{\varrho}}\nabla_{W}^{E_{\varrho}}-\nabla^{E_{\varrho}}_{\nabla^{LC}_{V}W},

where
V,W\in C^{\infty}(X,TX)
,
TX
is the tangent bundle of
X
,
and
\nabla^{LC}
denotes the Levi–Civita connection on
TX
.
The twisted Bochner–Laplace operator
\Delta_{\varrho}^{\sharp}
is defined to be the corresponding connection Laplacian on
E_{\varrho}
, i.e., the negative of the trace of the second covariant derivative:

\Delta_{\varrho}^{\sharp}:=-\rm{tr}\big((\nabla^{E_{\varrho}})^{2}\big).

(1)

Locally, this operator is described as follows.
We consider an open subset
U
of
X
such that
E_{\varrho}\lvert_{U}
is trivial,
i.e.,
E_{\varrho}\lvert_{U}\cong U\times\mathbb{C}^{m}
, where
m=\rm{rank}(E_{\varrho})=\dim V_{\varrho}
.
Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
be any basis of flat sections of
E_{\varrho}\lvert_{U}
. Then, each

\phi\in C^{\infty}(U,E_{\varrho}\lvert_{U})
can be written as

\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}\otimes e_{i},

where
\phi_{i}\in C^{\infty}(U)
,
i=1,\ldots,m
. Then,

\nabla_{Y}^{E_{\varrho}}\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla_{Y}\phi_{i}\otimes e_{i},\qquad(Y\in C^{\infty}(X,TX)).

(2)

The local expression above is independent of the choice of the basis of flat sections of
E_{\varrho}\lvert_{U}
, since the transition maps comparing flat sections are constant. By (
1
) and (
2
), the twisted Bochner–Laplace operator acting on
C^{\infty}(U,E_{\chi}\lvert_{U})
is given by

\Delta_{\varrho}^{\sharp}\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\Delta\phi_{i})\otimes e_{i},

(3)

where
\Delta
denotes the usual Laplace–Beltrami operator on
X
for the hyperbolic metric.
Let now
\widetilde{E}_{\varrho}
be the pullback to
\widetilde{X}={\mathbb{H}^{2}}
of
E_{\varrho}
.
Then,

\widetilde{E}_{\varrho}\cong\widetilde{X}\times V_{\varrho},

and

C^{\infty}(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{E}_{\varrho})\cong C^{\infty}(\widetilde{X})\otimes V_{\varrho}.

(4)

With respect to the isomorphism (
4
), it follows from (
3
) that the lift
\widetilde{\Delta}^{\sharp}_{\varrho}
of
\Delta_{\varrho}^{\sharp}
to
\widetilde{E}_{\varrho}
takes the form

\widetilde{\Delta}^{\sharp}_{\varrho}=\widetilde{\Delta}\otimes\rm{Id}_{V_{\varrho}},

(5)

where
\widetilde{\Delta}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
.

If we choose a Hermitian metric on
E_{\varrho}
, then
\Delta_{\varrho}^{\sharp}
acts in
L^{2}(X,E_{\varrho})
with domain
C^{\infty}(X,E_{\varrho})
. However, it is not a formally self-adjoint operator in general. By (
3
),
\Delta_{\varrho}^{\sharp}
has principal symbol

\sigma_{\Delta_{\varrho}^{\sharp}}(x,\xi)=\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}_{x}\rm{Id}_{({E_{\varrho})_{x}}}\qquad(x\in X,\xi\in T_{x}^{*}X).

Hence,
\Delta_{\varrho}^{\sharp}
is an elliptic, second order differential
operator with the following spectral properties:
its spectrum is discrete and contained in a translate of a positive cone
C\subset\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathbb{R}^{+}\subset C
.
This fact follows from classical spectral theory of elliptic operators, under the assumption of the compactness of the manifold.
We refer the reader to
[
45
]
, and also
[
28
, Lemma 2.1]
. From now on, for notational simplicity, we will denote this Laplacian by
\Delta_{\varrho}
.

The semi-group
e^{-t\Delta_{\varrho}}
is well defined for
t>0
and trace class on
L^{2}(X,E_{\varrho})
. The following “heat trace formula”
[
19
]
holds:

\mathrm{tr}(e^{-t\Delta_{\varrho}})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})}e^{-t\mu}

(6)

=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\mathrm{dim}(V_{\varrho})\mathrm{Vol}(X)\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t(\lambda^{2}+1/4)}\lambda\pi\tanh(\lambda\pi)d\lambda

+\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P},k\geq 1}\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma^{k}))\frac{\ell(\gamma)}{2\sinh(k\ell(\gamma)/2)}e^{-t/4}e^{-\frac{(k\ell(\gamma))^{2}}{4t}}.

By working with this heat trace one can then deduce a “resolvent trace formula”
[
19
]
and obtain the following fact on the twisted Selberg zeta function.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
{\rm Re}(s)
large enough, set

Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho):=\prod_{k\geq 0}\prod_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}}\det\left(I-\varrho(\gamma)e^{-(s+k)\ell(\gamma)}\right).

Then
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)
has a holomorphic extension to
\mathbb{C}
. Its zeros set is the divisor (with multiplicities)

\bigcup_{j}\{1/2\pm ir_{j}\}\cup\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}(2g-2)\mathrm{dim}(V_{\varrho})(2k+1)\{-k\},

where
\{1/4+r_{j}^{2}\}
is the spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}
and eigenvalues are repeated according to their multiplicity. In addition the following functional equation holds for all
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
,

Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)=Z_{\Gamma}(1-s,\varrho)\mathrm{exp}\left(\mathrm{dim}(V_{\varrho})\mathrm{Vol}(X)\int_{0}^{s-1/2}r\tan(\pi r)dr\right).

Remark 2.2
.

By Theorem 2.1, the twisted Selberg zeta function
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)
has a holomorphic extension to
\mathbb{C}
. Indeed, by considering the “resolvent trace formula”
[
19
, eq. (4.21)]
, we have that the logarithmic derivative
L(s,\varrho)
of
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)
has simple poles with positive residues.
This follows from Proposition 4.2.4 and Proposition 4.2.5 in
[
19
]
. Still, we can not conclude more information about the order of the zeros of
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)
, since the “spectral” and the “trivial” zeros could overlap.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

For
{\rm Re}(s)
large, we have by a direct calculation

\frac{Z^{\prime}_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)}{Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)}=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P},k\geq 1}\frac{\ell(\gamma)}{1-e^{-k\ell(\gamma)}}\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma^{k}))e^{-sk\ell(\gamma)}.

By definition of
\delta(\varrho)
, we know that the right hand side converges absolutely for
{\rm Re}(s)>\delta
, which shows that
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)
has no zeros in the half-plane
\{{\rm Re}(s)>\delta\}
.
Using the functional equation, we deduce that non-trivial zeros of
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)
are in the strip
\{1-\delta\leq{\rm Re}(s)\leq\delta\}
. Given
s_{j}(1-s_{j})\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})
with

s_{j}=1/2\pm ir_{j}
, then we know that
s_{j}
must be a zero of
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)
: therefore we get that

\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})\subset\mathbb{Z}^{-}\cup\mathcal{C}_{\delta}.

We have to discard the possible negative integer eigenvalues that are below
\delta(1-\delta)
. To this end we go back to the heat trace formula (
6
) in the regime
t\rightarrow+\infty
.
Let us denote by
N_{\varrho}(x)
the counting function

N_{\varrho}(x):=\sum_{k\ell(\gamma)\leq x}|\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma^{k}))|.

By definition of
\delta
, we have the crude bound valid for all
\epsilon>0
as
x\rightarrow\infty
:

N_{\varrho}(x)=O_{\epsilon}(e^{(\delta+\epsilon)x}).

Set
\alpha:=\inf_{\mu\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})}{\rm Re}(\mu)
. By discreteness of the spectrum, we know that there are finitely many eigenvalues
\mu
such that
{\rm Re}(\mu)=\delta
, and therefore a simple argument involving Lebesgue dominated
convergence shows that there exists
C_{0}>0
such that for all
t
large,

C_{0}e^{-t\alpha}\leq|\sum_{j}e^{-t\mu_{j}}|=|\mathrm{tr}(e^{-t\Delta_{\varrho}})|.

Using the heat trace formula we get for
t
large

C_{0}e^{-t\alpha}\leq O(e^{-t/4})+O\left(e^{-t/4}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P},k\geq 1}|\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma^{k}))|\frac{\ell(\gamma)}{2\sinh(k\ell(\gamma)/2)}e^{-\frac{(k\ell(\gamma))^{2}}{4t}}\right).

Furthermore, we have

S(t):=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P},k\geq 1}|\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma^{k}))|\frac{\ell(\gamma)}{2\sinh(k\ell(\gamma)/2)}e^{-\frac{(k\ell(\gamma))^{2}}{4t}}

=O\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}xe^{-x/2}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}dN_{\varrho}(x)\right).

A Stieltjes integration by parts combined with the crude bound on
N_{\varrho}(x)
shows that for all
\epsilon>0
we have :

S(t)=O\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\delta+\epsilon-1/2)x}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}dx\right).

It is a standard calculation (Laplace transform of a Gaussian) to show that as
a\rightarrow\infty
we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{av}e^{-v^{2}}dv=e^{a^{2}/4}(\sqrt{\pi}+o(1)).

Using this asymptotic and a change of variable show that for all
\epsilon>0
and all
t
large,

S(t)=O\left(\sqrt{t}e^{(-\delta(1-\delta)+1/4+\epsilon)t}\right),

which implies that as
t\rightarrow+\infty
,

C_{0}e^{-\alpha t}\leq O\left(\sqrt{t}e^{(-\delta(1-\delta)+\epsilon)t}\right)

and therefore
\alpha\geq\delta(1-\delta)
. The proof is done.
\square

2.2.
On the bottom of the spectrum

From Theorem
1.1
, we know that

\inf_{\mu\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})}{\rm Re}(\mu)\geq\delta(1-\delta).

In
\lx@sectionsign 3
, we will give examples for which this inequality is strict. There is however a large class of representations for which
the equality holds true. We prove the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have
\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma))\geq 0
. Then we have

\inf_{\mu\in\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})}{\rm Re}(\mu)=\delta(1-\delta).

Proof
. Assume that the inequality is strict, then we know that the meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}

s\mapsto\frac{Z_{\Gamma}^{\prime}(s,\varrho)}{Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)}

has no pole at
s=\delta
, i.e. is analytic at
s=\delta
. On the other hand we have for all
{\rm Re}(s)>\delta

\frac{Z^{\prime}_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)}{Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)}=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P},k\geq 1}\frac{\ell(\gamma)}{1-e^{-k\ell(\gamma)}}\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma^{k}))e^{-sk\ell(\gamma)},

which is a Dirichlet series of the type
\sum_{n}a_{n}e^{-s\lambda_{n}}
with
a_{n}\geq 0
, whose abcissa of absolute convergence is precisely
\delta
. A classical theorem of Landau
[
25
]
tells us that
s=\delta

must be an analytic singularity, a contradiction.
\square

We remark that if
\varrho
is a representation of Teichmüller type, then we must have
\delta(\varrho)>1
, as depicted on the Manhattan curve. Therefore
\delta(1-\delta)<0
which means that
\Delta_{\varrho}
has indeed negative eigenvalues
under the hypothesis of positivity of the character
\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma))
.

If the character is
real-valued
, then from the heat trace formula one deduces that for all
t>0
we have

\sum_{j}e^{-t\mu_{j}}=\sum_{j}e^{-t\overline{\mu_{j}}},

which implies that
\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})=\overline{\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})}
, i.e., the spectrum is symmetric with respect to the
x
-axis. Alternatively, we can recover this fact from the identity

Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)=\overline{Z_{\Gamma}(\overline{s},\varrho)},

which follows from the equality (valid for
{\rm Re}(s)
large)

Z_{\Gamma}(s,\varrho)=\exp\left(-\sum_{\gamma}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\varrho(\gamma^{n}))}{n}\frac{e^{-s\ell(\gamma^{n})}}{1-e^{-\ell(\gamma^{n})}}\right),

and uniqueness of analytic continuation.

3.
Examples of linear representations of surface groups

In this section, we give examples of representations

3.1.
The adjoint representation

Let
G=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and denote by

the Lie algebra of

Y_{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\

The adjoint representation

Notice that
\mathrm{Ad}(-I)=I

In the basis

(2,1)

X=x_{0}X_{0}+x_{1}X_{1}+x_{2}X_{2}

The Hyperboloid model of the hyperbolic plane

{\mathbb{H}^{2}}:=\{X\in V\ :\ B(X,X)=-1\ \mathrm{and}\ x_{0}>0\}.

It is a standard fact that

induces an isomorphism between
PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and

which is the group of direct isometries of the hyperboloid model. Notice that hyperbolic distance in the hyperboloid model

\cosh(d(U,V))=-B(U,V).

In particular, if

. Given an hyperbolic element

0&e^{-\ell(\gamma/2}\end{array}\right),

\mathrm{Ad(\widetilde{\gamma})}Y_{0}=e^{\ell(\gamma)}Y_{0},\ \mathrm{Ad(\widetilde{\gamma})}Y_{1}=e^{-\ell(\gamma)}Y_{1},\ \mathrm{Ad(\widetilde{\gamma})}Y_{2}=Y_{2},

(7)

\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma))=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Ad}(\widetilde{\gamma}))=1+e^{\ell(\gamma)}+e^{-\ell(\gamma)}.

(8)

In the specific case

We point out that

The divisor of

3.2.
Spin structures and
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

Let us denote by

Given a character

3.3.
The Fuchsian representation in
\mathrm{SL}_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})

Let

Before we can give a proof of Theorem

where

is a smooth kernel on

, called the Bergman kernel of
\Omega
. Given a Hilbert basis
(\phi_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
of

, we have for all

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\phi_{l}(z)\overline{\phi_{l}(w)}=B_{\Omega}(z,w),

the convergence being uniform on every compact subset of

, see for example Krantz
[
23

. In the latter, we will work on narrow domains

with

. We will need to use the following bounds on kernels

•

There exists a constant

independent of

•

If in addition we have

Given

Proposition 6.4

Using the above notations, we have

\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}I_{-}(\rho)\leq\delta_{0}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}I_{+}(\rho).

P(2\beta\psi-2\sigma\tau)=\sup_{\mu}\left(h_{\mu}(T)+2\beta\int\psi d\mu-2\sigma\int\tau d\mu\right),

where the supremum is taken over all
T
-invariant probability measures. Since this supremum is realized by a unique ergodic (and mixing) equilibrium state, we can replace this supremum
by a sup over all
ergodic

T
-invariant measures. On the other hand, we have

P(\beta\psi-\delta\tau)=\sup_{\mu}\left(h_{\mu}(T)+\beta\int\psi d\mu-\delta\int\tau d\mu\right)=0,

and we get that

P(2\beta\psi-2\sigma\tau)\leq\sup_{\mu}\left(\beta\int\psi d\mu+(\delta-2\sigma)\int\tau d\mu\right).

Taking

\sigma=\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}\sup_{\mu}\left(\frac{\int\psi d\mu}{\int\tau d\mu}\right),

we have
P(2\beta\psi-2\sigma\tau)\leq 0
and therefore
\delta_{0}\leq\sigma
. To complete the proof of the upper bound, we just need to show that

\sup_{\mu}\left(\frac{\int\psi d\mu}{\int\tau d\mu}\right)=I_{+}(\rho).

First observe that if
T^{n}x=x
is a primitive periodic point of
T
with
\tau^{(n)}(x)=\ell(\gamma)
and
\psi^{(n)}(x)=\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)

\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}D_{T^{j}x}
, where
D_{y}
stands for the Dirac measure at

, we have

\frac{\int\psi d\mu}{\int\tau d\mu}=\frac{\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)}{\ell(\gamma)}.

Since we know that for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}
, there exists an
n
-periodic point of
T
such that both
\tau^{(n)}(x)=\ell(\gamma)
and
\psi^{(n)}(x)=\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)
hold,
we deduce that

\sup_{\mu}\left(\frac{\int\psi d\mu}{\int\tau d\mu}\right)\geq I_{+}(\rho).

On the other hand, if
\mu
is a
T
ergodic measure, one can show using Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem (see
[
29
]
, Lemma 3.4) that there exists a sequence
(x_{p})_{p\in\mathbb{N}}

-periodic points such that

But we do have for all
p

\frac{\frac{1}{n_{p}}\psi^{(n_{p})}(x_{p})}{\frac{1}{n_{p}}\tau^{(n_{p})}(x_{p})}\leq I_{+}(\rho),

and hence the equality is proved. Using
[
11
]
, we know that if
I_{-}(\rho)=I_{+}(\rho)
then they are both equal to
1
and the Manhattan curve is a straight line passing through the points
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
, which implies that
\delta=1+\beta
and
\delta_{0}=1/2+\beta
. The proof is done.
\square

Remark 6.5
.

The quantities
I_{-}(\rho)
and
I_{+}(\rho)
are called “geodesic stretch” and are related to Thurston’s asymetric distance: in particular, we have
[
33
]

\log\left(\frac{I_{+}(\rho)}{I_{-}(\rho)}\right)\leq d_{\mathcal{T}}(X,\rho(\Gamma)\backslash{\mathbb{H}^{2}}),

where
d_{\mathcal{T}}(X_{1},X_{2})
denotes the usual Teichmüller distance related to quasi-conformal deformations. In particular, if the Teichmüller distance between

X
and the “deformed” surface
\rho(\Gamma)\backslash{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
is small, then
\delta_{0}
is close to
(\delta+\beta)/2
.

7.
Final remarks

They are many ways in which this paper can be generalized. It raises also a lot of open questions. We list them below.

•

In this paper we have essentially adressed the case of representations

\varrho:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\varrho
is obtained as a composition of Teichmüller and the irreducible representation. It would be interesting to consider a more general deformation of that case, which is called the “Hintchin component” in the litterature. It is likely that one has to combine techniques from Labourie
[
24
]
and the transfer operator methods used in this paper to reach this goal. One could also look at the quasi-Fuchsian deformation of the surface group
\Gamma
into
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. We believe
this should follow from our technique without major modifications.

•

By looking at examples, it seems that on the parabola
P_{\delta}
,
\delta(1-\delta)
is the only possible eigenvalue. Can one prove that fact in a general setting?
We have shown that if the character of
\varrho
is positive, then
\delta(1-\delta)
is indeed the bottom of the spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}
. Is it a simple eigenvalue? Can one express the base eigenfunction via Patterson-Sullivan theory in higher rank by analogy with the case of geometrically finite hyperbolic manifolds?

•

Based on the examples, we believe that the spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}
always has an essential spectral gap i.e., there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathrm{Sp}(\Delta_{\varrho})\cap\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\delta-\epsilon}
is a finite set. Spectral gaps in the context of negative curvature are often derived from Dolgopyat’s techniques
[
14
]
and their generalizations to non-compact situations with unitary twists
[
32
,
26
,
40
]
. Alternatively, when the ”trapped set” is a smooth symplectic submanifold and the transverse dynamics are normally hyperbolic, Nonnenmacher and Zworski’s micro-local approach
[
31
]
provide an explicit essential spectral gap.
In this context of non-unitary representations, Dolgopyat’s arguments cannot be applied directly and the micro-local approach is also likely to fail due to the lack of contact/symplectic structure.

•

The computable examples also suggest that the spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}
may have in some situations a “band structure”, with the number of bands given by
\mathrm{dim}(V)
. In
[
17
]
, for Pollicott-Ruelle resonances related to
U(1)
-extensions of Anosov maps, a band structure of the spectrum is rigourously established by Faure and Tsujii, together with some eigenvalue distribution results based on Sjöstrand’s method
[
47
]
for damped wave equations. We also mention the relevant work
of Dyatlov
[
16
]
for the existence of band structures in quantum resonances in a setting where the trapped set is normally hyperbolic. It would be interesting to investigate to what extend the above mentioned works could be adapted to our problem.

•

By analogy with the theory of resonances for hyperbolic manifolds, it would be interesting to know if micro-local techniques used by Dyatlov in
[
15
]
could be adapted
to improve our Weyl bounds on the peripheral spectrum of
\Delta_{\varrho}
. The non-smooth nature of Teichmüller deformations (Mostow’s map is only Hölder) is likely to be an obstacle.

•

There is a current work
[
18
]
on meromorphic continuation of Selberg zeta functions with non-unitary twists, for geometrically finite groups. Despite the fact that there is no available interpretation yet of zeros as spectra of a twisted Laplacian in this non-compact context, it would be interesting to know if our result can be extended to this framework.

•

Finally, one can consider hyperbolic manifolds of higher dimension, or more general,
locally symmetric spaces
X=\Gamma\backslash Y
of real rank one, where
Y
is a real, complex, or quaternionic hyperbolic space, or the hyperbolic
Cayley plane. Considering a non-unitary representation of the lattice, the Selberg trace formula, for suitable integral operators, induced by the twisted Laplacian, should be available, and hence the meromorphic continuation of the twisted Selberg zeta function could follow. Consequently, it would be interesting to see if our results can be extended to this case.

Appendix A
Universal bounds for Bergman kernels

The results below are based on standard facts on Bergman kernels for simply connected domains. However, because we couldn’t find the exact statement needed in the classical bet, we include a detailed proof here. In particular, the “off-diagonal” estimate is not surprising but seems to be new in this setting.
Let
\mathbb{D}
denote the unit disc. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded, simply connected open set. If
\phi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
is a conformal representation,
then one can endow
\Omega
with a hyperbolic distance
\rho_{\Omega}(x,y)
by pulling back the hyperbolic distance
\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(z,z^{\prime})
on
\mathbb{D}
:

\rho_{\Omega}(x,y):=\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(\phi(x),\phi(y)).

Notice that

\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(z,z^{\prime})=\inf_{\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|}{1-|\gamma(t)|^{2}}dt,

where the infimum runs over all
C^{1}
paths
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}
such that
\gamma(0)=z
and
\gamma(1)=z^{\prime}
. The quantity

\lambda_{\mathbb{D}}(z):=\frac{2}{1-|z|^{2}}

is called the density of the hyperbolic metric and we have under the conformal mapping
\phi
the identity

\lambda_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{2|\phi^{\prime}(z)|}{1-|\phi(z)|^{2}}.

For example, if
\Omega=D(a,r)
is a euclidean disc centered at
a
and of radius
r
, then we have

\lambda_{\Omega}(z)=\frac{2r^{-1}}{1-|\frac{z-a}{r}|^{2}}.

The Bergman Kernel
B_{\Omega}(z,w)
is the Schwarz kernel of the orthogonal projection
B:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow H^{2}(\Omega)
,
where
H^{2}(\Omega)
is as usual the Hilbert space of holomorphic
L^{2}
functions on
\Omega
. For the unit disc we have the well known formula
[
23
, Chapter 1]
:

B_{\mathbb{D}}(z,w)=\frac{1}{\pi(1-z\overline{w})^{2}}.

The Bergman kernel has conformal invariance i.e., under the above notations, we have

B_{\Omega}(z,w)=\frac{\phi^{\prime}(z)\overline{\phi^{\prime}(w)}}{\pi(1-\phi(z)\overline{\phi(w)})^{2}}.

Using the formula for the hyperbolic distance
\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(u,v)
in the disc

\cosh^{2}(\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(u,v)/2)=\frac{|1-u\overline{v}|^{2}}{(1-|u|^{2})(1-|v|^{2})},

we obtain the formula:

|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\lambda_{\Omega}(z)\lambda_{\Omega}(w)}{\cosh^{2}(\rho_{\Omega}(z,w)/2)},

(15)

which expresses the kernel in terms of hyperbolic distance and density on
\Omega
.
This formula will be enough to prove the following estimate.

Proposition A.1
.

Assume that
\Omega
is a bounded simply connected domain. We have the following facts.

(1)

Assume that
z,w\in\Omega
satisfy
\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)\geq c\epsilon
,
\mathrm{dist}(w,\partial\Omega)\geq c\epsilon
, for some
c>0
. Then there exists

M(c)>0
such that for all
1\geq\epsilon>0
,

|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|\leq M(c)\epsilon^{-2}.

(2)

Assume in addition that
z,w\in\Omega
satisfy:

c\epsilon<\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)\leq\epsilon,\ c\epsilon\leq\mathrm{dist}(w,\partial\Omega)\leq\epsilon,\ |z-w|\geq c\epsilon^{\alpha}

for some
c>0
and
0<\alpha<1
, then there exists
\widetilde{M}(c)>0
such that for all
1\geq\epsilon>0
,

|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|\leq\widetilde{M}(c)\epsilon^{-1-\alpha}.

The above constants
M(c),\widetilde{M}(c)
do not depend on the domain
\Omega
.

The first bound is “a diagonal estimate” and essentially optimal without additional assumptions on
z,w
. The second bound (“off-diagonal estimate”) shows that provided
z,w
are sufficiently far away from each other, we can gain over the trivial diagonal bound. These bounds are universal: we do not assume anything on the domain
\Omega
except
that it is bounded and simply connected.

Proof
. The hyperbolic density
\lambda_{\Omega}
satisfies the domain monotonicity: if
D(z,r)\subset\Omega
then we have

\lambda_{\Omega}(z)\leq\lambda_{D(z,r)}(z)=2r^{-1}.

Using Formula (
15
) we deduce that under the hypotheses of claim
1
we have for all
1\geq\epsilon>0
,

|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|\leq\frac{1}{4\pi}\lambda_{\Omega}(z)\lambda_{\Omega}(w)\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)^{-1}\mathrm{dist}(w,\partial\Omega)^{-1}

\leq\frac{c^{-2}}{\pi}\epsilon^{-2},

and the first bound is proved.

The second bound is more subtle: we need to bound from below the hyperbolic distance
\rho_{\Omega}(z,w)
. To this end, we will use an inequality
due to Beardon
[
5
]
. Given
x,y\in\Omega
, where
\Omega
is again bounded and simply connected, set

\alpha_{\Omega}(x,y):=\sup_{a,b\in\partial\Omega}\log\left(\frac{|z-a||w-b|}{|z-b||w-a|}\right).

This pseudo-metric is called the Apollonian metric by Beardon in
[
5
]
, and satisfies the remarkable bound

\alpha_{\Omega}(x,y)\leq 2\rho_{\Omega}(x,y).

Choosing points
a,b\in\partial\Omega
such that

|z-a|=\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega),\ |w-b|=\mathrm{dist}(w,\partial\Omega),

we obtain for
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}(c)
,

\alpha_{\Omega}(z,w)\geq\left|\log\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)+\log\mathrm{dist}(w,\partial\Omega)-\log|z-b|-\log|w-a|\right|

\geq 2|\log\epsilon|-|\log|z-b|+\log|w-a||.

Using the triangle inequality we have for all
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}(c)

|z-b|\geq|z-w|-|w-b|\geq c\epsilon^{\alpha}-\epsilon\geq\frac{c}{2}\epsilon^{\alpha}.

Similarly, we obtain also for all
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}(c)

|w-a|\geq\frac{c}{2}\epsilon^{\alpha},

so that we get

|\log|z-b|+\log|w-a||\leq 2\alpha|\log\epsilon|+|\log(c)|.

Hence for all
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}(c)
we have

(1-\alpha)|\log\epsilon|-\frac{1}{2}|\log c|\leq\rho_{\Omega}(z,w).

Going back to formula (
15
) and plugging the above estimate we get the desired upper bound (valid for all
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}(c)
)

|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|\leq\frac{c^{-2}\epsilon^{-2}}{\pi}e^{\frac{1}{2}|\log c|}\epsilon^{1-\alpha}.

The proof is done since for
1\geq\epsilon>\epsilon_{0}(c)
we can obviously use the first universal bound.
\square

We point out that no assumptions on the regularity of
\partial\Omega
or the convexity of
\Omega
have been made.
