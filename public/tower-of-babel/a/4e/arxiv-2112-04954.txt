Solving the hyperbolic Anderson model 1: Skorohod setting
Xia Chen, Aurélien Deya, Jian Song, Samy Tindel

Abstract
. This paper is concerned with a wave equation in dimension d ∈ { 1 , 2 , 3 } d\in\{1,2,3\} , with a multiplicative space-time Gaussian noise which is fractional in time and homogeneous in space. We provide necessary and sufficient conditions on the space-time covariance of the Gaussian noise, allowing the existence and uniqueness of a mild Skorohod solution.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
60H15, 60H07, 60F10

1.
Introduction

In the series of articles
[
4
,
5
]
, we started a line of research aiming at a comparative study between the Skorohod and Stratonovich settings for the parabolic Anderson model in very rough environments. At the core of our project in the aforementioned papers lies the following observation: while the Stratonovich solution might be seen as more physically relevant, the Skorohod solution often offers more possibilities in terms of quantitative analysis (moments, asymptotics, see for instance
[
3
,
7
,
11
]
). In
[
4
,
5
]
, we were thus able to transfer some nontrivial information about moments of the stochastic heat equation from the Skorohod to the Stratonovich equation.

The current article can be seen as a new chapter in this global picture. Indeed, the stochastic wave equation is another canonical model of random evolution which deserves a thorough quantitative study, just like for the heat equation. In addition, the toolbox allowing to handle basic issues for the wave equation is necessarily different in nature from the parabolic case. It thus seems natural to explore connections between the Stratonovich and the Skorohod worlds in a hyperbolic setting. We start this long term program here by an in-depth study of existence-uniqueness results in the Skorohod realm.

To be more specific, consider the following stochastic wave equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d
in the set
\{1,2,3\}
,

\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)+u\,\dot{W}(t,x),~~t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\vskip 5.69046pt\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}(0,x)=u_{1}(x).\end{cases}

(1.1)

In equation (
1.1
),
\Delta
stands for the usual Laplace operator in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
u_{0},u_{1}
are initial conditions satisfying some appropriate upper bounds (see Hypotheses (
2.14
)-(
2.15
) below). As far as the forcing
\dot{W}
above is concerned, we consider a centered Gaussian noise whose covariance is given by

\mathbb{E}[\dot{W}(s,x)\dot{W}(t,y)]=|s-t|^{-\alpha_{0}}\gamma(x-y).

(1.2)

The parameter
\alpha_{0}
in (
1.2
) is an arbitrary number in
[0,1)
, which means that the noise
\dot{W}
we consider here is either fractional in time (
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
) or independent of time (
\alpha_{0}=0
). The spatial covariance of
\dot{W}
is encoded by a (possibly singular) non-negative and non-negative definite function
\gamma
whose spectral measure (i.e. the Fourier transform of
\gamma
) is denoted by
\mu
. For instance if the noise
\dot{W}
is white in space, its spatial covariance function
\gamma(x)
is the Dirac delta function
\delta(x)
with
\mu(d\xi)=d\xi
. Another example of interest for this class of functions is the Riesz kernel
\gamma(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
with

\alpha\in(0,d)
, for which we have
\mu(d\xi)=C_{\alpha}|\xi|^{\alpha-d}d\xi
.

With this set of assumptions in hand, we can state our main result in a slightly informal way (see Proposition
3.3
and Theorem
3.8
for more rigorous versions).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\dot{W}
be a Gaussian noise with covariance function given by (
1.2
), where
\alpha_{0}\in[0,1)
and
\gamma
admits a spectral measure
\mu
. Then under appropriate regularity conditions on
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big({1\over 1+|\xi|^{2}}\Big)^{3-\alpha_{0}\over 2}\mu(d\xi)<\infty,

(1.3)

is a necessary and sufficient condition on
\mu
so that equation (
1.1
) admits a unique Skorohod solution when
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
, and a sufficient condition when
\alpha_{0}=0
.

Remark 1.2
.

We would like to stress that our condition (
1.3
) encompasses a wide variety of spatial covariance functions for the Gaussian noise
\dot{W}
, besides the typical examples such as the above-mentioned Dirac delta function and Riesz kernels. For instance, one may consider a periodic
spatial covariance function
\gamma(x)
for which
\mu
becomes a discrete measure supported
on the lattice space
a{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for some constant
a>0
.
In fact, one of the appeal of Theorem
1.1
is the generality of our framework.

If the Gaussian noise
\dot{W}
possesses spatial homogeneity properties, then Theorem
1.1
generates the following convenient wellposedness criterion (see Section
3.4
for a proof of this corollary).

Corollary 1.3
.

Assume that the spatial covariance
\gamma
is non-negative, non-negative definite, and that there exists
\alpha>0
for which

\gamma(cx)=c^{-\alpha}\gamma(x)\quad\text{for all}\ c>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.4)

Then the condition (
1.3
) holds true (or equivalently, the wave equation (
1.1
) has a unique Skorohod solution) if and only if
\alpha_{0}+\alpha<3
.

Let us complete the above statement with a few remarks.

Remark 1.4
.

It is readily checked that the spectral measure of a homogeneous measure of order
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
is a homogeneous measure of order
d-\nu
. Therefore, our non-negative definiteness condition on
\gamma
actually rules out the case of
\alpha
-homogeneity with
\alpha>d
, since a homogeneous measure of negative order is identically zero (this can be seen by letting
c
tends to
0
in relation (
1.4
)).
As the only homogeneous measures of order 0 on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the constant multiples of the Lebesgue meaure,
the only case with
\alpha=d
is when
\gamma(\cdot)
is a constant multiple of Dirac function
(i.e.,
\dot{W}
is a spatial white noise).
In particular, as
(\alpha_{0},\alpha)\in[0,1)\times(0,d]
, the condition
\alpha_{0}+\alpha<3
is automatically verified for
d=1,2
.

Remark 1.5
.

Our Corollary
1.3
encompasses the Riesz kernel case
\gamma(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(0,d)
, which obviously satisfies the homogeneous property (
1.4
). Another similar example comes from fractional Brownian sheets with Hurst parameters
H_{j}\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
for
j=1,\dots,d
. In this case we have
\gamma(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}|x_{j}|^{-(2-2H_{j})}
, and the coefficient
\alpha
in (
1.4
) is given by
\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2-2H_{j})
. In both the Riesz kernel and fractional sheet situation, the condition
\alpha_{0}+\alpha<3
is necessary and sufficient in order to solve (
1.1
). For the fractional Brownian sheet situation, this condition can be recast as
H_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}H_{j}>d-1/2
.

Remark 1.6
.

The range of application of Corollary
1.3
also includes the spatial white noise. In this case we have
\gamma(x)=\delta(x)
, and we get
\alpha=d
. In particular one can solve equation (
1.1
) driven by a purely spatial white noise (
\alpha_{0}=0
,
\alpha=d
) in dimensions
d=1,2
but not in dimension
d=3
.

Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.3
are the first results giving necessary and sufficient conditions in order to solve equation (
1.1
) in the Skorohod setting for a general space-time fractional Gaussian noise.
However, the stochastic wave equation driven by multiplicative Gaussian noise (also known as hyperbolic Anderson model) has been extensively studied in recent years. We now briefly recall some literature related to the problem of existence and uniqueness of the mild Skorohod solution.

(i)

In
[
16
]
, Walsh developed an Itô-type stochastic calculus for martingale measures and used it to study stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs). In particular, the stochastic wave equation in dimension one was considered therein. Then adapting some results by Peszat and Zabczyk
[
14
]
to the random field setting in
[
9
]
, Dalang extended Walsh’s definition of stochastic integral with respect to martingale measures. This allowed him to solve SPDEs whose Green function is a Schwartz distribution rather than a classical function. As an application, assuming that the Gaussian noise
\dot{W}
in (
1.1
) is white in time (i.e., replacing the temporal covariance
|s-t|^{-\alpha_{0}}
in (
1.2
) by the Dirac delta function
\delta(s-t)
), the following so-called
Dalang’s condition

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\mu(d\xi)<\infty,

(1.5)

was proved in
[
9
]
(see Remark 14 (a) therein) to be a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence and uniqueness of mild Itô (equivalent to Skorohod in this context) solution for
d\in\{1,2,3\}
. Walsh’s theory was further extended in
[
8
]
, where stochastic wave equations in any dimension were studied. Observe that condition (
1.5
) coincides with our own assumption (
1.3
) in the temporal white noise case which corresponds to
\alpha_{0}=1
.

(ii)

When
\dot{W}
has a covariance given by (
1.2
), the Gaussian noise is colored in time and thus the stochastic calculus for martingale measures used in
[
16
,
9
,
8
]
does not apply in this situation. Balan
[
3
]
employed Malliavin calculus (see e.g.
[
12
]
) to obtain the existence and uniqueness of a mild Skorohod solution to (
1.1
) for
d\in\{1,2,3\}
. She worked with a space-time colored noise with
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
and under Dalang’s condition (
1.5
). This result was extended to any dimension
d
in
[
2
]
. Our result goes beyond the assumptions of
[
3
]
, since the hypothesis (
1.3
) is weaker than Dalang’s condition (
1.5
) whenever
\alpha_{0}<1
.

(iii)

In the special case
d=1
, a study of the fractional space-time noise was carried out in
[
15
]
. More specifically
[
15
]
handled the case of a fractional noise in time with index
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
, while
\gamma
was rougher than in
[
3
,
2
]
. Namely in
[
15
]
the spatial component of the noise is assumed to be the distributional second derivative of the function
x\mapsto|x|^{2H}
with
H<1/2
. The condition obtained therein was
\alpha_{0}\in[0,1)
and
\alpha\in(1,3/2)
.
Notice that one cannot really compare our current paper with
[
15
]
, since our positivity assumptions rule out the possibility of considering a very rough noise in space.

(iv)

In the recent paper
[
1
]
, for (
1.1
) with time-independent homogeneous Gaussian noise (i.e.,
\alpha_{0}=0
in (
1.2
) and
0<\alpha\leq d
in (
1.4
)), the existence and uniqueness of the mild Skorohod solution was obtained under the conditions
0<\alpha<d\leq 3
and
0<\alpha=d\leq 2
respectively. This is indeed consistent with our assumptions in Theorem
1.1
(see also Corollary
1.3
and the subsequent remarks). In the sequel we will highlight this point by preforming several separate computations for the specific time-independent case. It should be observed that even in the time independent case, our setting is more general than
[
1
]
. Indeed, our contribution encompasses cases with no density for the measure
\mu
, as well as no convolution decomposition (
\gamma=K*K
) and no homogeneity for
\gamma
.

As one can see from this review, our main Theorem
1.1
gives a general framework allowing to solve hyperbolic Anderson models in dimension
d\leq 3
. It includes and goes beyond most of the aforementioned references. One should also mention the recent efforts
[
10
,
13
]
in order to properly define wave equations with additive noise and polynomial nonlinear terms, in the rough paths sense. Further comments on those contributions will be made in our forthcoming paper
[
6
]
on Stratonovich solutions.

We now summarize the methodology employed in order to prove Theorem
1.1
, focusing on the sufficient condition. With respect to the heat equation situation, one of the main obstacle is that one cannot appeal to Feynman-Kac type formulae in order to analyze the equation. Therefore, we shall only rely on a proper control of the chaos expansion for a candidate solution
u
to equation (
1.1
). As we will see in (
2.12
), this chaos expansion takes the form

u(t,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)),

(1.6)

for a sequence of functions
f_{n}
based on products of the wave kernel. Our main task is thus reduced to a sharp control of some weighted norms of the functions
f_{n}
. Some important tools towards this aim are the following:

•

Second moment computations in (
1.6
) in terms some
L^{2}
-norms of functions involving the noise covariance and the wave kernel.

•

Poissonization (or Laplace transform) methods in order to be reduced to
L^{1}
(as opposed to
L^{2}
) norms and products of
1
-d integrals.

Some of the ingredients described above are already contained in
[
1
]
. However, the presence of a nontrivial time covariance induces a more technical and challenging situation. In order to proceed with the main steps described above, a delicate study based on Fourier analysis is needed.

Let us finally emphasize a striking phenomenon revealed by a close examination of the subsequent strategy and arguments. Namely, under suitable initial conditions and referring to the chaos expansion (
1.6
), proving the main convergence result

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!\lVert I_{n}(f_{n}(.,t,x))\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}^{2}<\infty,\quad\text{for all}\ t>0

is in fact equivalent to proving the (much) weaker property

\lVert I_{1}(f_{1}(.,t,x))\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty,\quad\text{for some}\ t>0.

This explains in particular how condition (
1.3
) becomes necessary in the statement of Theorem
1.1
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide some preliminaries on Gaussian analysis related to the noise
\dot{W}
and equation (
1.1
). In Section
3
, we first show that (
1.3
) is actually a necessary condition for the second moment of the first chaos of the mild Skorohod solution to be finite. This yields the necessity of condition (
1.3
). Then in Sections
3.2
-
3.3
we show that under our assumption (
1.3
), the chaos expansion of the solution does converge in
L^{2}(\Omega)
by estimating the Laplace transform of the second moment of each chaos. This corresponds to the sufficient part in Theorem
1.1
.

Throughout the paper, we use the symbol
C
for a generic positive constant which may be different in different places.

2.
Preliminaries

This section is devoted to a better grasp on the Gaussian analysis related to the noise
\dot{W}
defined by (
1.2
). Then we shall state a rigorous version of the mild formulation for equation (
1.1
).

2.1.
The Gaussian noise and Malliavin calculus

In this subsection we collect some preliminaries on Malliavin calculus for the Gaussian noise
\dot{W}
with covariance given by (
1.2
). For more details, we refer to
[
12
]
.
Let us first label our running assumptions on the noise coefficients.

Hypothesis 2.1
.

Recall that the covariance function of
\dot{W}
is formally given by (
1.2
). Then we assume that
\alpha_{0}\in[0,1)
. Moreover, the function
\gamma
is supposed to be non-negative and non-negative definite with spectral measure
\mu
.

With Hypothesis
2.1
in mind, we define a set of functions encoding the covariance structure of
\dot{W}
. Namely, let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hilbert space which is the completion of the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})
under the inner product

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|r-s|^{-\alpha_{0}}\varphi(r,x)\phi(s,y)\gamma(x-y)\,dxdydrds.

(2.1)

One can also write this inner product in spatial Fourier mode as follows:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|r-s|^{-\alpha_{0}}\widehat{\varphi}(r,\xi)\overline{\widehat{\phi}(s,\xi)}\,\mu(d\xi)drds,

(2.2)

where we recall from Hypothesis
2.1
that
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
, and
\gamma
is a non-negative definite function with spectral measure
\mu
.

We can now introduce
W
as an isonormal Gaussian process. Specifically, on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, let
W=\{W(\varphi),\varphi\in\mathcal{H}\}
be a Gaussian family with covariance given by

\mathbb{E}[W(\varphi)W(\phi)]=\langle\varphi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(2.3)

Then
W(\varphi)
for
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
is called the Wiener integral of
\varphi
with respect to
W
and we also denote
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(t,x)W(dt,dx):=W(\varphi).

Let
h(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a smooth function such that its partial derivatives have at most polynomial growth. Then for smooth and cylindrical random variables of the form
F=h(W(\varphi_{1}),\dots,W(\varphi_{n}))
, one can define the Malliavin derivative
DF
as the
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variable

DF:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{k}}(W(\varphi_{1}),\dots,W(\varphi_{n}))\,\varphi_{k}.

One can verify that
D:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
is a closable operator, and then we define the Sobolev space
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
as the closure of the space of the smooth and cylindrical random variables under the norm

\|F\|_{1,2}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[F^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|DF\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}]}.

Denote by
\text{Dom }\delta
the domain of the divergence operator
\delta
, which is the set of
u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
such that
|E[\langle DF,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]|\leq c_{F}\|F\|_{2}
with some constant
c_{F}
depending on
F
, for all
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
. The divergence operator
\delta
(also known as the Skorohod integral) is the adjoint of the Malliavin derivative operator
D
defined by the duality

\mathbb{E}[F\,\delta(u)]=E[\langle DF,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}],~\text{ for all }F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}\text{ and }u\in\text{Dom }\delta.

(2.4)

Thus, for
u\in\text{Dom }\delta
, we have
\delta(u)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. It is also readily checked from (
2.4
) with
F\equiv 1
that
\mathbb{E}[\delta(u)]=0.
Note that we will use the following notation

\delta(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,x)W(dt,dx),\quad\text{for all}\quad u\in\text{Dom }\delta.

(2.5)

To end this subsection, we recall the Wiener chaos expansion of a random variable
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Let
\mathbf{H}_{0}=\mathbb{R}
, and for integers
n\geq 1
, let
\mathbf{H}_{n}
be the closed linear subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega)
containing the set of random variables
\left\{H_{n}(W(\varphi)),\varphi\in\mathcal{H},\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1\right\}
, where
H_{n}
is the
n
-th Hermite polynomial (i.e.,
H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(e^{-x^{2}})
). Then
\mathbf{H}_{n}
is called the
n
-th Wiener chaos of
W
. Assuming
\mathcal{F}
is the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{W(\varphi),\varphi\in\mathcal{H}\}
, we have the following Wiener chaos decomposition

L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{H}_{n}.

For
n\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
be the
n
-th tensor product of
\mathcal{H}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}
be the symmetrization of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
. Then the mapping
I_{n}(h^{\otimes n})=H_{n}(W(h))
for
h\in\mathcal{H}
can be extended to a linear isometry between
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}
and the
n
-th Wiener chaos
\mathbf{H}_{n}
. Thus, for any random variable
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, the following unique Wiener chaos expansion in
L^{2}(\Omega)
holds true,

F=\mathbb{E}[F]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n}),\quad\text{with }f_{n}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}.

Furthermore, noting that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|I_{n}(f_{n})\right|^{2}\right]=n!\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}},

we have

\mathbb{E}[|F|^{2}]=\left(\mathbb{E}[F]\right)^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|I_{n}(f_{n})\right|^{2}\right]=\left(\mathbb{E}[F]\right)^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n!\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}.

(2.6)

2.2.
Mild solution for the Skorohod equation

In this subsection, we define the mild Skorohod solution to (
1.1
) and derive its Wiener chaos expansion. We start by introducing some more notation about filtrations related to our problem.

Notation 2.2
.

In the sequel we write
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
for the filtration generated by the time increments of
\dot{W}
. That is we set

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\{W(\mathbf{1}_{[0,s]}\varphi);\,0\leq s\leq t,\,\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\}\vee\mathcal{N},

where
\mathcal{N}
is the collection of null sets.

We now label some notation concerning the wave kernel.

Notation 2.3
.

We denote by
G_{t}(x)
the fundamental solution of the wave equation, and recall that we consider here the dimensions
d=1,2,3
. The generalized function
G_{t}
is characterized by its spatial Fourier transform

\widehat{G}_{t}(\xi)=\frac{\sin(t|\xi|)}{|\xi|}.

(2.7)

For two functions
u_{0},u_{1}
of the variable
x
(whose regularity will be specified below), we also set

w(t,x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(G_{t}*u_{0}\right)(x)+\left(G_{t}*u_{1}\right)(x),

(2.8)

where
*
denotes the convolution in space.

The expression (
2.7
) of the fundamental solution
\widehat{G}_{t}
in Fourier modes will prove to be crucial in our computations below. However, we will also resort to properties of
G_{t}
in direct modes. To this aim, we recall that for
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, the fundamental solution
G_{t}(x)
has explicit expressions:

G_{t}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{[|x|<t]}&\text{ if }d=1,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^{2}-|x|^{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{[|x|<t]}&\text{ if }d=2,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi t}\sigma_{t}&\text{ if }d=3,\end{array}\right.

where
\sigma_{t}
is the uniform measure on the sphere
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|=t\}
.

With this notation in hand, we are ready to state a rigorous definition of the Skorohod solution to equation (
1.1
).

Definition 2.4
.

An
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
-adapted random field
u=\{u(t,x),t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
is called a mild Skorohod solution to (
1.1
) if
\mathbb{E}[u^{2}(t,x)]<\infty
for each
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and if it satisfies the following integral equation,

u(t,x)=w(t,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)u(s,y)W(ds,dy),

(2.9)

where
w(t,x)
is given in (
2.8
) and the stochastic integral on the right-hand side is a Skorohod integral like in (
2.5
). In particular, it is implicitly assumed that for each
t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}
, the process
v_{t,x}(s,y)=G_{t-s}(x-y)u(s,y)\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}(s)
lies in Dom
\delta
(see (
2.4
)).

Let us say a few words about the chaos decomposition for the mild solution (
2.9
). First for
n\in\mathbb{N}
we denote

f^{w}_{n}(s_{1},x_{1},\dots,s_{n},x_{n},t,x)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}G_{t-s_{\sigma(n)}}(x-x_{\sigma(n)})\cdots G_{s_{\sigma(2)}-s_{\sigma(1)}}(x_{\sigma(2)}-x_{\sigma(1)})\\
\times w(s_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(1)})\mathbf{1}_{[0<s_{\sigma(1)}<\dots<s_{\sigma(n)}<t]},

(2.10)

where
\Sigma_{n}
is the set of permutations on
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,
G_{t}
is the wave kernel defined by (
2.7
) and
w
is the convolution (
2.8
). Then the following result can be found, for instance, in
[
3
]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

There exists a unique mild Skorohod solution to (
1.1
) if and only if the function
w(t,x)
given by (
2.8
) is well-defined and the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,t,x))
converges in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for all
t>0
, i.e.,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n!\|f^{w}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}<\infty,~\text{ for all }t>0\text{ and }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.11)

Whenever (
2.11
) is met, we have the following Wiener chaos expansion for the solution
u
to equation (
1.1
):

u(t,x)=w(t,x)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n}^{w}(\cdot,t,x)).

(2.12)

In order to state some necessary and sufficient conditions allowing to solve (
1.1
), we have to introduce another piece of notation. Namely we will call
f_{n}
the function
f^{w}
in (
2.10
) obtained when
w\equiv 1
. More specifically we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(s_{1},x_{1},\dots,s_{n},x_{n},t,x)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}

\displaystyle G_{t-s_{\sigma(n)}}(x-x_{\sigma(n)})\cdots G_{s_{\sigma(2)}-s_{\sigma(1)}}(x_{\sigma(2)}-x_{\sigma(1)})

\displaystyle\times\mathbf{1}_{[0<s_{\sigma(1)}<\dots<s_{\sigma(n)}<t]}.

(2.13)

We can now state our bound on the function
w
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
be the initial conditions in (
1.1
), and recall that
w
is defined by (
2.8
). Let us assume that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{1}(x)|<\infty,

(2.14)

and for
u_{0}
we suppose

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{0}(x)|<\infty~\text{ if }~d=1,\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{u}_{0}(\xi)|d\xi<\infty~\text{ if }~d=2,3.

(2.15)

Then the function
w
satisfies

\sup_{t\in[0,T],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|w(t,x)|<\infty.

(2.16)

Proof.

We resort to expression (
2.8
) and will bound the two terms therein.

First, in order to bound the term
G_{t}*u_{1}
in (
2.8
), note that (regardless of the dimension
d=1,2,3
) we have
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t}(x)dx=t
for all
t>0
. Hence if
\|u_{1}\|_{\infty}=M<\infty
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we get

|G_{t}*u_{1}(x)|\leq\|u_{1}\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t}(y)dy=Mt.

(2.17)

Regarding the term
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(G_{t}*u_{0})(x)
in (
2.8
), we separate the study according to the dimension. That is for
d=1
it is readily checked that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(G_{t}*u_{0})(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{0}(x+t)+u_{0}(x-t)\right).

Therefore, if
\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}=M
, we clearly have

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(G_{t}*u_{0})(x)\right|\leq M,

(2.18)

uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For
d=2,3
, we express the convolution in Fourier modes. Namely using
\widehat{G_{t}*u_{0}}=\widehat{G}_{t}\widehat{u}_{0}
and appealing to expression (
2.7
) for the Fourier transform of
G_{t}
, one can write

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(G_{t}*u_{0}\right)(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\sin(t|\xi|)}{|\xi|}\widehat{u}_{0}(\xi)e^{i\xi\cdot x}d\xi\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\cos(t|\xi|)\widehat{u}_{0}(\xi)e^{i\xi\cdot x}d\xi.

Hence if one assumes
\|\widehat{u}_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=M
, we get

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(G_{t}*u_{0})(x)\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{u}_{0}(\xi)|d\xi=M,

(2.19)

uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We can now conclude our claim: we simply gather (
2.17
), (
2.18
) and (
2.19
) into expression (
2.8
). This yields our estimate (
2.16
).
∎

3.
Existence and uniqueness in the Skorohod setting

We now turn to the existence and uniqueness problem for the Skorohod equation (
2.9
), starting with the statement of our main theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider equation (
2.9
) driven by a centered Gaussian noise
\dot{W}
. The covariance function of
\dot{W}
is given by (
1.2
), with
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\gamma
satisfying Hypothesis
2.1
. Then the following holds true.

(i)

If (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) are satisfied, we have that equation (
2.9
) admits a unique solution as soon as
\gamma
satisfies (
1.3
), that is

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{3-\alpha_{0}}{2}}\mu(d\xi)<\infty.

(ii)

Recall that
w
is defined by (
2.8
). If in addition we have
w(x)\geq a_{0}
with a strictly positive constant
a_{0}
, then (
1.3
) is also a necessary condition in order to get a unique solution to (
2.9
).

Remark 3.2
.

A simple example of initial condition
(u_{0},u_{1})
in (
1.1
) fulfilling (
2.14
)-(
2.15
) and giving rise to a strictly positive
w
is
u_{0}\equiv 0
and
u_{1}\equiv 1
.

The remainder of the section is devoted to a proof Theorem
3.1
. We start with the necessary condition.

3.1.
On the necessary condition

Recall that the covariance function of
\dot{W}
is given by (
1.2
), with a non-negative constant
\alpha_{0}
and a non-negative and non-negative definite function
\gamma(x)
whose Fourier transform is
\mu
. In Proposition
3.3
below, we provide a necessary condition (
1.3
) for the existence and uniqueness of the Skorohod solution to (
1.1
).

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
W
be a noise whose covariance function is given by (
1.2
). We assume that Hypothesis
2.1
holds true with
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
and a function
\gamma
. Assume that the function
w
given by (
2.8
) is lower bounded by a constant
a_{0}>0
. Then a necessary condition in order to solve equation (
1.1
) is (
1.3
).

Proof.

Recall that the function
f_{1}^{w}
corresponding to the first chaos in our decomposition (
2.12
) is defined by

f_{1}^{w}(s,y;t,x)=G_{t-s}(x-y)w(s,y)\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}(s).

(3.1)

Since we have assumed
w(x)\geq a_{0}>0
uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it suffices to show that (
1.3
) is a necessary condition for
\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}
to be finite, where we recall that we have set
f_{1}=f_{1}^{w}
for the initial condition
w(s,y)\equiv 1
.
Furthermore, thanks to (
2.2
) we have the following expression for
f_{1}
:

\displaystyle\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|r-s|^{-\alpha_{0}}\frac{\sin(r|\xi|)}{|\xi|}\frac{\sin(s|\xi|)}{|\xi|}\mu(d\xi)drds.

Using the fact that the Fourier transform of
|t|^{-\alpha_{0}}
is proportional to
|\lambda|^{\alpha_{0}-1}
, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=c_{\alpha_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\lambda|^{\alpha_{0}-1}\left|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i\lambda s}\frac{\sin(s|\xi|)}{|\xi|}ds\right|^{2}d\lambda\,\mu(d\xi).

(3.2)

We now lower bound the square in the right-hand side of (
3.2
). First, separating real and imaginary parts, we easily get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i\lambda s}\frac{\sin(s|\xi|)}{|\xi|}ds\right|^{2}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\xi|^{2}}\Big|Q_{t}(\lambda,\xi)\Big|^{2},

(3.3)

where we have set

Q_{t}(\lambda,\xi)=\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\lambda s)\sin(|\xi|s)ds.

Then notice that
Q_{t}(\lambda,\xi)
is an integral which can be computed explicitly thanks to elementary methods. We get

\displaystyle Q_{t}(\lambda,\xi)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\sin\big((\lambda+|\xi|)s\big)-\sin\big((\lambda-|\xi|)s\big)\Big)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1-\cos\big((\lambda+|\xi|)t\big)}{\lambda+|\xi|}+\frac{1-\cos\big((|\xi|-\lambda)t\big)}{|\xi|-\lambda}\right).

(3.4)

Let us focus our attention on the region
0\leq\lambda\leq|\xi|
in the right-hand side above. In this case, both terms in (
3.1
) are non-negative and as a consequence,

\Big|Q_{t}(\lambda,\xi)\Big|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1-\cos\big((|\xi|-\lambda)t\big)}{|\xi|-\lambda}\right)^{2}.

Therefore, for
|\xi|>0
, we get the following lower bound:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\lambda|^{\alpha_{0}-1}\Big|Q_{t}(\lambda,\xi)\Big|^{2}d\lambda\geq\int_{0}^{|\xi|}|\lambda|^{\alpha_{0}-1}\Big|Q_{t}(\lambda,\xi)\Big|^{2}d\lambda

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{|\xi|}|\lambda|^{\alpha_{0}-1}\left(\frac{1-\cos\big((|\xi|-\lambda)t\big)}{|\xi|-\lambda}\right)^{2}d\lambda\geq\frac{1}{4}|\xi|^{\alpha_{0}-1}\int_{0}^{|\xi|}\left(\frac{1-\cos\big((|\xi|-\lambda)t\big)}{|\xi|-\lambda}\right)^{2}d\lambda.

Thanks to the elementary change of variable
|\xi|-\lambda=\tau
, we thus end up with

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\lambda|^{\alpha_{0}-1}\Big|Q_{t}(\lambda,\xi)\Big|^{2}d\lambda\geq\frac{1}{4}|\xi|^{\alpha_{0}-1}\int_{0}^{|\xi|}\left(\frac{1-\cos(\tau t)}{\tau}\right)^{2}d\tau.

(3.5)

Let us summarize our computations so far. Plugging (
3.5
) into (
3.3
) and then (
3.2
), we have obtained

\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\geq C_{\alpha_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{\alpha_{0}-3}\int_{0}^{|\xi|}\left(\frac{1-\cos(\tau t)}{\tau}\right)^{2}\,d\tau\,\mu(d\xi),

where we recall that
C
designates a constant whose exact value can change from line to line.
Hence, lower bounding the integral in
\xi
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by an integral over
\{|\xi|\geq 1\}
we get

\displaystyle\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C_{\alpha_{0}}\int_{\{|\xi|\geq 1\}}|\xi|^{\alpha_{0}-3}\int_{0}^{|\xi|}\left(\frac{1-\cos(\tau t)}{\tau}\right)^{2}d\tau\,\mu(d\xi)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C_{\alpha_{0}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1-\cos(\tau t)}{\tau}\right)^{2}d\tau\right)\int_{\{|\xi|\geq 1\}}|\xi|^{\alpha_{0}-3}\mu(d\xi).

Observe that the integral over
\tau
above is just a strictly positive universal constant. Hence, one can recast (
3.6
) as

\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\geq C\int_{\{|\xi|\geq 1\}}|\xi|^{\alpha_{0}-3}\mu(d\xi),

where
C
is a finite positive constant. Now it is readily checked that the right-hand side above is finite if and only iff (
1.3
) is fulfilled. We have thus found that (
1.3
) is a necessary condition to have
\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}<\infty,
which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

If the noise is independent of time, namely
if
\alpha_{0}=0
, it is readily checked that (
3.2
) becomes

\displaystyle\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin(s|\xi|)}{|\xi|}ds\right)^{2}\mu(d\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\left(1-\cos(t|\xi|)\right)^{2}}{|\xi|^{4}}\mu(d\xi).

Therefore a sufficient condition (also necessary if the noise is spatially homogeneous, see
[
1
]
) for
\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
to be finite is

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\right)^{2}\mu(d\xi)<\infty.

(3.7)

Note that if we plug the value
\alpha_{0}=0
into our condition (
1.3
), we get something stronger than (
3.7
). This means that (
1.3
) is not an optimal condition for
\|f_{1}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}<\infty
when
\alpha_{0}=0
. However, our condition (
1.3
) with
\alpha_{0}=0
is still a sufficient condition for (
1.1
) with time-independent noise to have a unique Skorohod solution as stated in Theorem
1.1
. Hence we are not able (at this moment) to close the gap between necessary and sufficient condition for the time independent case
\alpha_{0}=0
. At a technical level, this is mainly due to the considerations leading to relations (
3.47
)-(
3.48
) below.

3.2.
Bound for the Laplace transform of
n
-th chaos contributions

Recall that the function
f_{n}^{w}
is given by (
2.10
), and features in (
2.11
) as the kernel for the
n
-th chaos contribution in the decomposition of
u(t,x)
. As a first step toward (
2.11
), and accordingly toward existence and uniqueness of a solution for (
1.1
), let us prove the following Proposition, which provides an upper bound for the Laplace transform of the 2nd moment
\mathbb{E}[|I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,t,x))|^{2}]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that Hypothesis
2.1
holds true and that
u_{0},u_{1}
satisfy the conditions of Proposition
2.6
. Let
p\geq 1
be a fixed parameter. Then, there exists a positive constant
C_{\alpha_{0}}
depending only on
\alpha_{0}
such that

2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2pt}\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}dt\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(C_{\alpha_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\frac{1}{|\tau|^{1-\alpha_{0}}\Phi_{p}(\tau,\eta)}\,d\tau\mu(d\eta)\right)^{n},&\alpha_{0}>0,\\
\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{\Phi_{p}(0,\eta)}\,\mu(d\eta)\right)^{n},&\alpha_{0}=0,\end{cases}

(3.8)

where
\Phi_{p}
is the function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
by

\Phi_{p}(\tau,\eta):=\Big|(p-i\tau)^{2}+|\eta|^{2}\Big|^{2}=4p^{2}\tau^{2}+(p^{2}+|\eta|^{2}-\tau^{2})^{2}.

(3.9)

Proof.

This somewhat lengthy proof will be split onto several steps for the reader’s convenience. We will focus on the case
\alpha_{0}>0
, the case
\alpha_{0}=0
being proved in a similar way.

Step 1: Reduction to a constant initial condition.
Let us write
f^{w}_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}f_{n}^{w,\sigma}
, with

\displaystyle f_{n}^{w,\sigma}(s_{1},x_{1},\dots,s_{n},x_{n},t,x)

\displaystyle:=G_{t-s_{\sigma(n)}}(x-x_{\sigma(n)})\cdots G_{s_{\sigma(2)}-s_{\sigma(1)}}(x_{\sigma(2)}-x_{\sigma(1)})\times w(s_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(1)})\mathbf{1}_{[0<s_{\sigma(1)}<\dots<s_{\sigma(n)}<t]}.

It is easily seen, thanks to Jensen’s inequality applied to the uniform measure on
\Sigma_{n}
, that

\|f^{w}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}\|f_{n}^{w,\sigma}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}.

(3.10)

Now, using first the expression (
2.1
) and then the result of Proposition
2.6
(which guarantees that
w\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
), we derive that

\displaystyle\|f^{w,\sigma}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n})^{2}}d{\bf s}d{\bf s^{\prime}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}d{\bf x}d{\bf x^{\prime}}\,\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}|s_{k}-s_{k}^{\prime}|^{-\alpha_{0}}\gamma(x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime})\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad G_{t-s_{\sigma(n)}}(x-x_{\sigma(n)})\cdots G_{s_{\sigma(2)}-s_{\sigma(1)}}(x_{\sigma(2)}-x_{\sigma(1)})\times w(s_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(1)})\mathbf{1}_{[0<s_{\sigma(1)}<\dots<s_{\sigma(n)}<t]}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad G_{t-s^{\prime}_{\sigma(n)}}(x-x^{\prime}_{\sigma(n)})\cdots G_{s^{\prime}_{\sigma(2)}-s^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)}}(x^{\prime}_{\sigma(2)}-x^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)})\times w(s^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)},x^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)})\mathbf{1}_{[0<s^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)}<\dots<s^{\prime}_{\sigma(n)}<t]}

\displaystyle\leq\|w\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\int_{([0,t]_{<}^{n})^{2}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}|s_{k}-s_{k}^{\prime}|^{-\alpha_{0}}\gamma(x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime})\bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\times\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}(x_{k}-x_{k-1})G_{s_{k}^{\prime}-s_{k-1}^{\prime}}(x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k-1}^{\prime})\bigg)\,d{\bf x}d{\bf x^{\prime}}d{\bf s}d{\bf s^{\prime}},

where we use the convention
s_{0}=0
and
x_{0}=x^{\prime}_{0}=x
, and the notation
d{\bf s}=ds_{1}\cdots ds_{n}
(we use similar conventions for
d{\bf x},d{\bf x^{\prime}}
and
d{\bf s^{\prime}}
). Note also that we use
[0,t]^{n}_{<}
to denote the
n
-th order simplex
[0<s_{1}<s_{2}<\dots<s_{n}<t]
on
[0,t]
.
Injecting the latter estimate into (
3.10
), and with expression (
2.1
) in mind, we get that

\|f^{w}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}\leq\|w\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|g_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}},

where
g_{n}
is defined by

g_{n}(s_{1},x_{1},\dots,s_{n},x_{n},t,x):=G_{t-s_{n}}(x-x_{n})\cdots G_{s_{2}-s_{1}}(x_{2}-x_{1})\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]_{<}^{n}}(s_{1},\dots,s_{n}).

(3.11)

In the sequel, we shall upper bound the terms involving
g_{n}
.

Step 2: Expression for the Laplace transform.

One has

\displaystyle\|g_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{([0,t]_{<}^{n})^{2}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}|s_{k}-s_{k}^{\prime}|^{-\alpha_{0}}\gamma(x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime})\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}(x_{k}-x_{k-1})G_{s_{k}^{\prime}-s_{k-1}^{\prime}}(x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k-1}^{\prime})\bigg)d{\bf x}d{\bf x^{\prime}}d{\bf s}d{\bf s^{\prime}}.

(3.12)

In order to ease our arguments below, let us recast (
3.12
) in a slightly more complicated way by doubling the
t
variable. That is, write

\|g_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}=F_{n}(t,t),

(3.13)

where the function
F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined by

F_{n}(t,\bar{t})=\int_{[0,t]_{<}^{n}\times[0,\bar{t}]_{<}^{n}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}|s_{k}-s_{k}^{\prime}|^{-\alpha_{0}}\gamma(x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime})\bigg)\\
\times\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}(x_{k}-x_{k-1})G_{s_{k}^{\prime}-s_{k-1}^{\prime}}(x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k-1}^{\prime})\bigg)d{\bf x}d{\bf x^{\prime}}d{\bf s}d{\bf s^{\prime}}.

(3.14)

Also recall that we are interested in the Laplace transform of
\|g_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}
, for which we introduce a notation:

\Lambda_{n}(p)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2pt}\|g_{n}(t,x,\cdot)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2pt}F_{n}(t,t)\,dt.

(3.15)

Step 3: Reverse
L^{2}
bounds.
We will now use monotonicity properties of exponential random variables in order to reduce the
L^{2}
-type norm (
3.15
) to a product of
L^{1}
-type norms. The argument is inspired by page 954 in
[
7
]
and goes as follows:

(i)

The integral in (
3.15
) can be seen as an integral with respect to an exponential variable
T
with parameter
2p
. Namely

\Lambda_{n}(p)=\frac{1}{2p}\mathbb{E}[F_{n}(T,T)],\quad\text{ with }\quad T\sim\text{exp}(2p).

(ii)

It is elementary to prove the identity in law
T\overset{(d)}{=}\tau\wedge\bar{\tau}
, with
\tau,\bar{\tau}
i.i.d. with common law exp
(p)
. Hence,

\Lambda_{n}(p)=\frac{1}{2p}\mathbb{E}[F_{n}(\tau\wedge\bar{\tau},\tau\wedge\bar{\tau})].

(3.16)

(iii)

Due to the positivity of
\gamma
and
G
, it is readily checked that both
t\mapsto F_{n}(t,\bar{t})
and
\bar{t}\mapsto F_{n}(t,\bar{t})
are non-decreasing functions. Plugging this information into (
3.16
) we obtain

\Lambda_{n}(p)\leq\frac{1}{2p}\mathbb{E}[F_{n}(\tau,\bar{\tau})].

(3.17)

Let us now recall the expression (
3.14
) for
F_{n}
and write the expected value with respect to
\tau,\bar{\tau}
in (
3.17
) explicitly. Summarizing our considerations (i)-(iii) above, we have obtained the following relation:

\Lambda_{n}(p)\leq\frac{1}{2p}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}dtd\bar{t}~p\,e^{-p(t+\bar{t})}\times\int_{[0,t]_{<}^{n}\times[0,\bar{t}]_{<}^{n}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}|s_{k}-s_{k}^{\prime}|^{-\alpha_{0}}\gamma(x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime})\bigg)\\
\times\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}(x_{k}-x_{k-1})G_{s_{k}^{\prime}-s_{k-1}^{\prime}}(x_{k}^{\prime}-x_{k-1}^{\prime})\bigg)d{\bf x}d{\bf x^{\prime}}d{\bf s}d{\bf s^{\prime}}.

(3.18)

We now rearrange terms above thanks to Fubini’s theorem, in order to obtain a more readable version of (
3.18
). To this aim, let us introduce an additional notation. Namely, for
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in[0,t]
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we set

H_{p}(s_{1},x_{1}\dots,s_{n},x_{n})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-pt}\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}(x_{k}-x_{k-1})\bigg)\mathbf{1}_{[0<s_{1}<\dots<s_{n}<t]}dt.

Then (
3.18
) can be expressed as

\Lambda_{n}(p)\leq\frac{p}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})^{2n}}H_{p}(s_{1},x_{1}\dots,s_{n},x_{n})H_{p}(s_{1}^{\prime},x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,s_{n}^{\prime},x_{n}^{\prime})\\
\times\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}|s_{k}-s_{k}^{\prime}|^{-\alpha_{0}}\gamma(x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime})\bigg)d{\bf x}d{\bf x^{\prime}}d{\bf s}d{\bf s^{\prime}}.

(3.19)

Step 4: Some algebraic manipulations.

Notice that the right-hand side of (
3.19
) can be written in terms of a convolution product. That is, we have

\Lambda_{n}(p)\leq\frac{p}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})^{n}}H_{p}(s_{1},x_{1},\dots,s_{n},x_{n})\phi_{p}(s_{1},x_{1},\dots,s_{n},x_{n})dyds,

(3.20)

where

\phi_{p}(s_{1},x_{1},\dots,s_{n},x_{n})=H_{p}*\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}|s_{k}|^{-\alpha_{0}}\gamma(x_{k})\right].

Therefore, one can express (
3.20
) in Fourier modes thanks to Plancherel’s identity. This yields, for some positive constant
C_{\alpha_{0}}
,

\Lambda_{n}(p)\leq\frac{p}{2}C^{n}_{\alpha_{0}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})^{n}}\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}{1\over|\lambda_{k}|^{1-\alpha_{0}}}\bigg)\\
\times\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp\left(i\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}s_{k}\right)\widehat{H}_{p}(s_{1},\xi_{1},\dots,s_{n},\xi_{n})d{\bf s}\right|^{2}d\lambda\mu(d\xi),

(3.21)

where we abuse the notation
d\lambda=d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{n}
and
\mu(d\xi)=\mu(d\xi_{1})\cdots\mu(d\xi_{n}),
and
\widehat{H}_{p}
means the Fourier transform of
H_{p}
in its space variable.

Let us now say a few words about the computation of
\widehat{H}_{p}
in (
3.21
). Denoting without any further mention the spatial Fourier transform by
\mathcal{F}g
or
\widehat{g}
, we have

\widehat{H}_{p}(s_{1},\xi_{1},\dots,s_{n},\xi_{n})\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-pt}\mathcal{F}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}(y_{k}-y_{k-1})\right)(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]^{n}_{<}}(s_{s},\dots,s_{n})\,dt.

(3.22)

Furthermore, performing the change of variables
z_{k}=y_{k}-y_{k-1}
and rearranging the Fourier variables thanks to some easy algebraic manipulations, it is readily checked that

\mathcal{F}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}(y_{k}-y_{k-1})\right)(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{G}_{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}\left(\sum_{j=k}^{n}\xi_{j}\right).

Plugging this identity in (
3.22
) and then in (
3.21
), we end up with

\Lambda_{n}(p)\leq\frac{p}{2}C^{n}_{\alpha_{0}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})^{n}}\left|\Psi_{p}(\lambda,\xi)\right|^{2}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}{1\over|\lambda_{k}|^{1-\alpha_{0}}}\,d\lambda\mu(d\xi),

(3.23)

where we have set

\Psi_{p}(\lambda,\xi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-pt}\int_{[0,t]_{<}^{n}}\exp\bigg(i\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}s_{k}\bigg)\prod_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{G}_{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}\Big(\sum_{j=k}^{n}\xi_{j}\Big)d{\bf s}dt.

(3.24)

Step 5: Some Fourier computations.
In this step, we write a more explicit version of (
3.23
) by computing some Fourier transforms.
Namely write
s_{k}-s_{k-1}=r_{k}
for
k=1,\dots,n+1
, where we have set
s_{0}=0
and
s_{n+1}=t
. It is easily seen that the function
\Psi_{p}
in (
3.24
) becomes

\Psi_{p}(\lambda,\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n+1}}\exp\left(-p\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}r_{k}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{n}\exp\left(ir_{k}\sum_{j=k}^{n}\lambda_{j}\right)\widehat{G}_{r_{k}}\Big(\sum_{j=k}^{n}\xi_{j}\Big)~d{\bf r}.

Integrating over the variable
r_{n+1}
, we are left with a product of
n
integrals:

\Psi_{p}(\lambda,\xi)=\frac{1}{p}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\exp\left(\Big(-p+i\sum_{j=k}^{n}\lambda_{j}\Big)r\right)\widehat{G}_{r}\Big(\sum_{j=k}^{n}\xi_{j}\Big)dr.

(3.25)

We now give a more explicit expression for
\Psi_{p}
thanks to the expression (
2.7
) for
\widehat{G}
. Namely, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
with positive real part, it is well known that

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta r}\frac{\sin(r|\eta|)}{|\eta|}dr=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}+|\eta|^{2}}.

(3.26)

Reporting this expression into (
3.25
), we get

\Psi_{p}(\lambda,\xi)=\frac{1}{p}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\bigg[\Big(p-i\sum_{j=k}^{n}\lambda_{j}\Big)^{2}+\Big|\sum_{j=k}^{n}\xi_{j}\Big|^{2}\bigg]^{-1}.

Going back to (
3.23
), we have thus obtained

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}(p)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{n}_{\alpha_{0}}}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})^{n}}\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}{1\over|\lambda_{k}|^{1-\alpha_{0}}}\bigg)\prod_{k=1}^{n}\bigg|\Big(p-i\sum_{j=k}^{n}\lambda_{j}\Big)^{2}+\Big|\sum_{j=k}^{n}\xi_{j}\Big|^{2}\bigg|^{-2}d\lambda\,\mu(d\xi).

(3.27)

Moreover, recalling the expression (
3.9
) for
\Phi_{p}
, (
3.27
) can be expressed as

\Lambda_{n}(p)\leq\frac{C^{n}_{\alpha_{0}}}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})^{n}}\bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}{1\over|\lambda_{k}|^{1-\alpha_{0}}}\bigg)\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(\Phi_{p}\left(\sum_{j=k}^{n}\lambda_{j},\sum_{j=k}^{n}\xi_{j}\right)\right)^{-1}d\lambda\,\mu(d\xi).

(3.28)

Step 6: Reduction to
1
-d integrals.
Our next aim is to transform the right-hand side of (
3.28
) into a product of integrals in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
In order to achieve this we invoke the following lemma, which is an elaboration of
[
7
, Lemma 3.1]
:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f
be a function and
\nu
be a measure, both defined on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and both positive. We assume that the Fourier transforms
\widehat{f}
and
\widehat{\nu}
of
f
and
\nu
are positive functions. Then for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}f(\xi-\eta)\nu(d\xi)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}f(\xi)\nu(d\xi).

(3.29)

Proof.

A basic application of Parseval’s identity shows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}f(\xi-\eta)\nu(d\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{ix\cdot\eta}\widehat{f}(x)\widehat{\nu}(x)dx.

We now trivially bound the oscillating exponential term by
1
in the right hand side above, and apply Parseval’s identity again, in order to get our claim (
3.29
).
∎

Lemma
3.6
is applied successively to the
\lambda,\xi
variables in (
3.28
). We now provide some details about the
(\lambda_{1},\xi_{1})
-integrals. That is, in the right-hand side of (
3.28
), the
(\lambda_{1},\xi_{1})
variables appear in the integral

\Lambda_{n,1}(p)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left|\psi_{p}(\lambda_{1}+l,\xi_{1}+x)\right|^{2}\nu(d\lambda_{1},d\xi_{1}),

(3.30)

where
l=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\lambda_{j},x=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\xi_{j}
and
where the function
\psi_{p}
and the measure
\nu
are respectively defined by

\psi_{p}(\tau,\eta)=\left(\Phi_{p}(\tau,\eta)\right)^{-1/2}=\Big((p-i\tau)^{2}+|\eta|^{2}\Big)^{-1},\quad\nu(d\lambda_{1},d\xi_{1})=\frac{1}{|\lambda_{1}|^{1-\alpha_{0}}}d\lambda_{1}\mu(d\xi_{1}).

(3.31)

If we wish to apply Lemma
3.6
to the integral in (
3.30
), we thus have to prove that the space-time Fourier transforms of
|\psi_{p}|^{2}
and
\nu
are both positive functions. This is argued below:

(a)

Owing to (
3.26
) and (
2.7
), we have

\displaystyle\psi_{p}(\tau,\eta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\exp\bigg(i(\tau t+\eta\cdot x)\bigg)\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)e^{-pt}G_{t}(x)dxdt.

Otherwise stated,
\psi_{p}
is the Fourier transform of a non-negative function
g_{p}
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
by

g_{p}(t,x)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)e^{-pt}G_{t}(x)
.
Therefore
\mathcal{F}\psi_{p}
is a positive function. Since
\mathcal{F}|\psi_{p}|^{2}=(\mathcal{F}\psi_{p})*(\mathcal{F}\bar{\psi}_{p})
, we also get that
\mathcal{F}|\psi_{p}|^{2}
is a positive function.

(b)

According to (
3.31
), the space-time Fourier transform of the measure
\nu
is given by

\mathcal{F}\nu(t,x)=C_{d}|t|^{-\alpha_{0}}\gamma(x)
,
which is a positive function.

We are thus in a position to apply Lemma
3.6
to the integral in (
3.30
), which yields

\Lambda_{n,1}(p)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left|\psi_{p}(\lambda_{1},\xi_{1})\right|^{2}\nu(d\lambda_{1},d\xi_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left(\Phi_{p}(\lambda_{1},\xi_{1})\right)^{-1}\nu(d\lambda_{1},d\xi_{1}),

(3.32)

where we observe that the right-hand side above does not depend on the variables
\lambda_{j},\xi_{j}
with
j=2,\dots,n
anymore, recalling that
\Phi_{p}
is given by (
3.9
).

As mentioned above, we now just have to iterate (
3.32
) into (
3.27
). This allows to reduce
\Lambda_{n}(p)
to a product of
n
integrals in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}(p)\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigg(C_{\alpha_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}|\tau|^{\alpha_{0}-1}\left(\Phi_{p}(\tau,\eta)\right)^{-1}d\tau\mu(d\eta)\bigg)^{n}.

(3.33)

Step 7: Conclusion.

Recall that according to (
3.15
),
\Lambda_{n}(p)
is the Laplace transform of
t\mapsto\|g_{n}(t,x,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}
. Moreover, we have reduced our computations for
f_{n}
to those of
g_{n}
in Step 1. Hence (
3.33
) easily yields (
3.8
). This finishes our proof.
∎

3.3.
On the sufficiency of the condition

In this subsection, we aim to show that the necessary condition (
1.3
) obtained in Section
3.1
is also a sufficient condition for the existence and uniqueness of the mild Skorohod solution to (
1.1
).
We first give the following preparatory result.

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume that Hypothesis
2.1
holds true, and set

L_{\alpha_{0},n}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{1-\alpha_{0}}\,\Phi_{n}(\lambda,\xi)}~\mu(d\xi)d\lambda,&\alpha_{0}>0,\\
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{\Phi_{n}(0,\xi)}~\mu(d\xi),&\alpha_{0}=0,\end{cases}

(3.34)

where
\Phi_{n}
is defined in (
3.9
).
Then under the condition (
1.3
), we have

L_{\alpha_{0},n}<\infty\ \text{ for every}\ n\geq 1,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}nL_{\alpha_{0},n}=0\,.

(3.35)

Proof.

Case 1:
\alpha_{0}>0
.
Starting from the right hand side of (
3.9
), some elementary manipulations show that

4n^{2}\lambda^{2}+(n^{2}+|\xi|^{2}-\lambda^{2})^{2}=4n^{2}|\xi|^{2}+(n^{2}+\lambda^{2}-|\xi|^{2})^{2}.

Therefore one can symmetrize
\Phi_{n}
in the following way:

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(\lambda,\xi)

\displaystyle={1\over 2}\Big\{4n^{2}\lambda^{2}+(n^{2}+|\xi|^{2}-\lambda^{2})^{2}+4n^{2}|\xi|^{2}+(n^{2}+\lambda^{2}-|\xi|^{2})^{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle=n^{4}+(|\xi|+|\lambda|)^{2}(|\xi|-|\lambda|)^{2}+2n^{2}(|\xi|^{2}+\lambda^{2}).

Next some elementary algebraic manipulations yield

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(\lambda,\xi)

\displaystyle\geq n^{4}+(|\xi|+|\lambda|)^{2}(|\xi|-|\lambda|)^{2}+n^{2}(|\xi|+|\lambda|)^{2}

\displaystyle=n^{4}+(|\xi|+|\lambda|)^{2}\big(n^{2}+(|\xi|-|\lambda|)^{2}\big).

Hence to obtain the desired assertion (
3.35
), it suffices to prove that

K_{n}<\infty\ (\text{for all}\ n\geq 1)\quad\text{and}\lim_{n\to\infty}nK_{n}=0,\quad\text{with}\quad K_{n}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(\lambda,\xi)\,\mu(d\xi)d\lambda,

(3.36)

and where the function
\tilde{\Phi}_{n}
is defined by

\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(\lambda,\xi):={1\over|\lambda|^{1-\alpha_{0}}\Big(n^{4}+(|\xi|+|\lambda|)^{2}\big(n^{2}+(|\xi|-|\lambda|)^{2}\big)\Big)}\,.

(3.37)

The remainder of the proof is devoted to show (
3.36
).

In order to bound
K_{n}
with a suitable (finite) quantity, let us fix a large constant
a>0
. Then we decompose
K_{n}
as

K_{n}=K_{n}(a)+\bar{K}_{n}(a),

(3.38)

where
K_{n}(a)
and
\bar{K}_{n}(a)
are respectively defined by

K_{n}(a):=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\{|\xi|\leq a\}}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(\lambda,\xi)\,\mu(d\xi)d\lambda,\qquad\bar{K}_{n}(a):=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\{|\xi|>a\}}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(\lambda,\xi)\,\mu(d\xi)d\lambda.

(3.39)

We now bound those two terms separately.

In order to estimate
K_{n}(a)
in (
3.38
), we simply write

\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(\lambda,\xi)\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{1-\alpha_{0}}(n^{4}+n^{2}\lambda^{2})},

which yields

nK_{n}(a)\leq\mu(|\xi|\leq a)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\lambda}{|\lambda|^{1-\alpha_{0}}n(n^{2}+\lambda^{2})}\\
\leq\frac{\mu(|\xi|\leq a)}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\lambda}{|\lambda|^{1-\alpha_{0}}(1+\lambda^{2})}.

Since
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
, the latter estimate readily entails that

K_{n}(a)<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}nK_{n}(a)=0.

(3.40)

We now analyze the term
\bar{K}_{n}(a)
in (
3.39
). For this we decompose the integral and write

\bar{K}_{n}(a)=\bar{K}_{n}^{1}(a)+\bar{K}_{n}^{2}(a),

(3.41)

where
\bar{K}_{n}^{1}(a),\bar{K}_{n}^{2}(a)
are given by

\bar{K}_{n}^{1}(a):=\int_{\{|\lambda|\leq|\xi|/2,\,|\xi|>a\}}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(\lambda,\xi)\,\mu(d\xi)d\lambda,\quad\bar{K}_{n}^{2}(a):=\int_{\{|\lambda|>|\xi|/2\},\,|\xi|>a\}}\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(\lambda,\xi)\,\mu(d\xi)d\lambda.

Now we estimate
\bar{K}_{n}^{1}(a)
. If
|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{2}|\xi|
and recalling the expression (
3.37
) for
\tilde{\Phi}_{n}
, we have

\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(\lambda,\xi)\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{1-\alpha_{0}}\big(n^{4}+|\xi|^{2}(n^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|\xi|^{2})\big)}.

Hence integrating first with respect to
\lambda
, we get

\bar{K}_{n}^{1}(a)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n^{4}+|\xi|^{2}\big(n^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|\xi|^{2}\big)}\left(\int_{-{1\over 2}|\xi|}^{{1\over 2}|\xi|}{1\over|\lambda|^{1-\alpha_{0}}}d\lambda\right)\mu(d\xi).

In addition, the elementary inequality
a^{2}+b^{2}\geq 2ab
implies that
n^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|\xi|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}n|\xi|
. We thus get

\bar{K}_{n}^{1}(a)\leq\frac{C_{\alpha_{0}}}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\xi|^{\alpha_{0}}}{n^{3}+|\xi|^{3}}\mu(d\xi).

Taking into account our assumption (
1.3
), we obtain that
\bar{K}_{n}^{1}(a)<\infty
for every fixed
n\geq 1
, and a classical dominated convergence argument allows to conclude

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\bar{K}_{n}^{1}(a)=0.

(3.42)

As far as
\bar{K}_{n}^{2}(a)
in (
3.41
) is concerned, on the set
|\lambda|>\frac{1}{2}|\xi|
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\tilde{\Phi}_{n}(\lambda,\xi)\leq\frac{C}{|\xi|^{3-\alpha_{0}}\big(n^{2}+(|\xi|-|\lambda|)^{2}\big)}.

(3.43)

Furthermore, one can argue that

\int_{\{|\lambda>|\xi|/2\}}{1\over n^{2}+(|\xi|-|\lambda|)^{2}}d\lambda\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}{1\over n^{2}+(|\xi|-|\lambda|)^{2}}d\lambda\\
=2\int_{0}^{\infty}{1\over n^{2}+(\lambda-|\xi|)^{2}}d\lambda\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}}{1\over n^{2}+\lambda^{2}}d\lambda={2\pi\over n}.

(3.44)

Plugging (
3.43
) and (
3.44
) into the definition of
\bar{K}_{n}^{2}(a)
, we thus get

nK_{n}^{2}(a)\leq C_{\alpha_{0}}\int_{\{|\xi|>a\}}|\xi|^{-(3-\alpha_{0})}\mu(d\xi),

(3.45)

which, due to assumption (
1.3
), already guarantees that
K_{n}^{2}(a)
is finite for every fixed
n\geq 1
.

Summarizing our considerations so far, we report (
3.42
) and (
3.45
) into the decomposition (
3.41
) of
\bar{K}_{n}(a)
. Taking also into account (
3.40
) and the decomposition (
3.38
), we end up with

K_{n}<\infty\ \text{for every}\ n\geq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}nK_{n}\leq C_{\alpha_{0}}\int_{\{|\xi|>a\}}|\xi|^{-(3-\alpha_{0})}\mu(d\xi).

(3.46)

Eventually recall that we are working under (
1.3
). Moreover, the parameter
a
in the right-hand side of (
3.46
) is arbitrarily large. Hence the right-hand side of (
3.46
) is arbitrarily small. We thus get

\lim_{n\to\infty}nK_{n}=0,

which means that (
3.36
) is shown. As argued in the beginning of the proof, this is sufficient to ensure that (
3.35
) holds true.

Case 2:
\alpha_{0}=0
.
Noting that
\Phi_{n}(0,\xi)=(n^{2}+|\xi|^{2})^{2}
, it suffices to prove

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n^{4}+|\xi|^{4}}\mu(d\xi)<\infty\ \text{for every fixed}\ n\geq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{n}{n^{4}+|\xi|^{4}}\mu(d\xi)=0.

(3.47)

Observe first that the condition (
1.3
) with
\alpha_{0}=0
becomes

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{1\over 1+|\xi|^{3}}\mu(d\xi)<\infty,

(3.48)

and so the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n^{4}+|\xi|^{4}}\mu(d\xi)<\infty
(for
n\geq 1
) is obvious. Besides, one has clearly

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{n}{n^{4}+|\xi|^{4}}\mu(d\xi)=\int_{\{|\xi|\leq n\}}\frac{n}{n^{4}+|\xi|^{4}}\mu(d\xi)+\int_{\{|\xi|>n\}}\frac{n}{n^{4}+|\xi|^{4}}\mu(d\xi)

(3.49)

The second term of (
3.49
) satisfies

\int_{\{|\xi|>n\}}\frac{n}{n^{4}+|\xi|^{4}}\mu(d\xi)\leq\int_{\{|\xi|>n\}}\frac{n}{n^{4}+n|\xi|^{3}}\mu(d\xi)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n^{3}+|\xi|^{3}}\mu(d\xi)

and hence, by (
3.48
) this second term converges to 0 as
n\to\infty
.
Regarding the first term of (
3.49
), we have

\int_{\{|\xi|\leq n\}}\frac{n}{n^{4}+|\xi|^{4}}\mu(d\xi)\leq\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mu(\{|\xi|\leq n\}).

In addition, observe that

\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mu(\{|\xi|\leq n\})\leq 2\int_{\{|\xi|\leq n\}}\frac{1}{n^{3}+|\xi|^{3}}\mu(d\xi)\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n^{3}+|\xi|^{3}}\mu(d\xi),

and the right hand side above also goes to 0 as
n\to 0
thanks to dominated convergence arguments, similarly to what we did for (
3.42
).
This shows (
3.47
) and our proof is complete.
∎

Lemma
3.7
was our last preliminary result before proving our existence and uniqueness theorem. We now state and prove this result, which has to be regarded as the main contribution of the current paper.

Theorem 3.8
.

Recall that the function
f_{n}
is given by (
2.10
). Assume that Hypothesis
2.1
holds true and assume the same conditions as in Proposition
2.6
. Then we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}<\infty.

(3.50)

Hence owing to Proposition
2.5
, there is a unique mild Skorohod solution to (
1.1
).

Proof.

We start by upper bounding the Laplace transform of the function
t\mapsto\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}
. To this aim, apply directly (
3.8
) with
p=n
. We get

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2nt}\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}dt\leq\left(C_{\alpha_{0}}L_{\alpha_{0},n}\right)^{n},

(3.51)

where
L_{\alpha_{0},n}
is the (finite) quantity introduced in (
3.34
).

According to Lemma
3.7
, it holds that
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}nL_{\alpha_{0},n}=0
, and hence some elementary considerations yield

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\big(n^{n}(C_{\alpha_{0}}L_{\alpha_{0},n})^{n}\big)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(\log C_{\alpha_{0}}+\log(nL_{\alpha_{0},n})\big)=-\infty.

This together with (
3.51
) lead to the following relation:

\lim_{n\to\infty}{1\over n}\log n^{n}\left(2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2nt}\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}dt\right)=-\infty.

(3.52)

This identity can be related to a single value of
\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}
in the following way: for a given constant
t>0
, write

2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2ns}\|f_{n}(\cdot,s,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}ds\geq 2\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-2ns}\|f_{n}(\cdot,s,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}ds.

Moreover, going back to expression (
3.12
) and taking into account the fact that the kernels
G
and
\gamma
are positive, it is clear that
s\mapsto\|f_{n}(\cdot,s,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}
is nondecreasing. Therefore we get

2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2ns}\|f_{n}(\cdot,s,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}ds\geq\frac{1}{n}e^{-2nt}\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}.

(3.53)

Combining (
3.52
) and (
3.53
), we thus get

\lim_{n\to\infty}{1\over n}\log n^{n}\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}=-\infty.

(3.54)

We are now ready to conclude the proof of (
3.50
). Indeed, since
n!\leq n^{n}
for all
n\geq 0
(with the convention
0^{0}=1
), we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{n}\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}.

(3.55)

Moreover, relation (
3.54
) asserts the existence of
n_{0}\geq 1
such that for
n\geq n_{0}
we have
n^{n}\|f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}\leq e^{-n}
. Plugging this information into (
3.55
), we trivially get (
3.50
). This finishes our proof.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

For the special case
stated in Corollary
1.3
, we can prove Theorem
3.8
by just gathering Proposition
3.5
and the following scaling property valid for the functions
g_{n}
defined by (
3.11
):

\|g_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}=t^{(4-\alpha-\alpha_{0})n}\|g_{n}(\cdot,1,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes n}}^{2}.

(3.56)

In particular, we do not need to invoke the behavior of
L_{\alpha_{0},n}
as
n\to\infty
. More specifically, let us recall that (
3.8
) is also valid for the functions
g_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
defined by (
3.11
). Hence

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}\|g_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}dt\leq\big(C_{\alpha_{0}}L_{\alpha_{0},1}\big)^{n},

(3.57)

where
L_{\alpha_{0},1}<\infty
. Moreover, owing to (
3.56
), the left-hand side of (
3.57
) can be recast as

\|g_{n}(\cdot,1,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}t^{(4-\alpha-\alpha_{0})n}dt.

(3.58)

Let us also recall from Remark
1.3
that in the homogeneous case our condition (
1.3
) reads

\alpha_{0}+\alpha<3
. Thus gathering (
3.57
) and (
3.58
), and reporting to elementary properties of Gamma functions, we end up with

\|g_{n}(\cdot,1,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}\leq\frac{C^{n}}{(n!)^{4-\alpha-\alpha_{0}}},

for a constant
C>0.
We can now invoke (
3.56
) again in order to get

\|g_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}\leq\frac{(Ct^{4-\alpha-\alpha_{0}})^{n}}{(n!)^{4-\alpha-\alpha_{0}}}.

Since
4-\alpha-\alpha_{0}>1
, this is enough to ensure (
3.50
).

3.4.
Proof of Corollary
1.3

It is readily checked that the Fourier transform of a
\alpha
-homogeneous measure is homogeneous of order
d-\alpha
. In other words, condition (
1.4
) can be easily recast as follows: for all bounded function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support and all
c>0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(cx)\,\mu(dx)=c^{-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\,\mu(dx)\,.

Applying this formula with
\varphi(\xi):=\mathbf{1}_{\{|\xi|\leq 1\}}
and
c=r^{-1}
, we obtain that

\mu(\mathcal{B}(0,r))=r^{\alpha}\mu(\mathcal{B}(0,1))\quad\text{for all}\ r>0,

(3.59)

where
\mathcal{B}(0,r):=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ |\xi|\leq 1\}
.

With relation (
3.59
) in mind, applying (
3.61
) in Lemma
3.10
below with
\nu=\mu
and
f(r)=(1+r^{2})^{(\alpha_{0}-3)/2}

will enable us to establish the following identity:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{3-\alpha_{0}}{2}}\mu(d\xi)=\alpha\,\mu(\mathcal{B}(0,1))\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r^{1-\alpha}}\bigg(\frac{1}{1+r^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{3-\alpha_{0}}{2}}\,,

(3.60)

from which we immediately derive the conclusion of Corollary
1.3
(recall that
\alpha>0
).

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\nu
be a Randon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and denote
g(r)=\nu(\mathcal{B}(0,r))
for
r\geq 0.

Then for any continuous function
f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(|\xi|)\nu(d\xi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)dg(r).

(3.61)

Proof.

It suffices to prove the following equality for all
R>0
:

\int_{\mathcal{B}(0,R)}f(|\xi|)\nu(d\xi)=\int_{0}^{R}f(r)dg(r),

(3.62)

where the integral on the right-hand side is a well-defined Riemann-Stieltjes integral noting that
f
is continuous and
g
is increasing.

For a fixed positive number
R
, let
0=r_{0}<r_{1}<\dots<r_{n}=R
be a partition of the interval
[0,R]
. Denoting
E_{k}=\mathcal{B}(0,r_{k})\backslash\mathcal{B}(0,r_{k-1})
for
k=1,\dots,n
, clearly we have

\int_{\mathcal{B}(0,R)}f(|\xi|)\nu(d\xi)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{E_{k}}f(|\xi|)\nu(d\xi).

By the continuity of
f
, we have that for each
k
,

\int_{E_{k}}f(|\xi|)\nu(d\xi)=f(r_{k}^{*})\nu(E_{k})=f(r_{k}^{*})[g(r_{k})-g(r_{k-1})],

for some
r_{k}^{*}\in[r_{k-1},r_{k}]
. Thus, we have

\int_{\mathcal{B}(0,R)}f(|\xi|)\nu(d\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(r_{k}^{*})[g(r_{k})-g(r_{k-1})],

and letting
n\to\infty
yields the desired (
3.62
).
