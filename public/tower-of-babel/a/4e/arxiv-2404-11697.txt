Saddle solutions for Allen-Cahn type equations involving the prescribed mean curvature operator
Renan J. S. Isneri

Abstract
. The goal of this paper is to investigate the existence of saddle solutions for some classes of elliptic partial differential equations of the Allen-Cahn type, formulated as follows: − d ​ i ​ v ​ ( ∇ u 1 + | ∇ u | 2 ) + A ⁡ ( x , y ) ​ V ′ ​ ( u ) = 0 in ℝ 2 . -div\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)+A(x,y)V^{\prime}(u)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{2}. 1.
Introduction

Over the past 50 years, in view of several concrete real-world applications, considerable attention has been devoted to addressing the existence and classification of bounded solutions of the elliptic Allen-Cahn equations, whose prototype is given by

(1.1)

-\Delta u+V^{\prime}(u)=0\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{N},

where the standard model of
V:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the classical double well Ginzburg-Landau potential

(1.2)

V(u)=\frac{1}{4}(u^{2}-1)^{2}.

The concept of “
heteroclinic solutions
” has been extensively investigated in equations of the (
1.1
) type, leading to the emergence of numerous constructions documented in the literature. This type of solutions was the starting point to provide a rich amount of differently shaped families of solutions such as saddle, stationary layered and multibump solutions. Readers seeking a more comprehensive understanding of these solution types are encouraged to explore further in references such as
[
1
,
3
,
2
,
4
,
18
,
19
]
.

Equation (
1.1
) represents a reaction-diffusion type equation originating from mathematical physics. The initial model, encompassing (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), was first proposed in 1979 by S. Allen and J. Cahn
[
5
]
to delineate the phenomenon of phase separation within multi-component alloy systems. In this framework, the solution
u
serves as a phase parameter, characterizing the material’s state at each point, with the trivial solutions
u=-1
and
u=+1
corresponding to the pure phases of the system. Since then much progress has been made on (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), and moreover, its variations have been broadly treated in the PDE literature in the last years. These variations are mostly of one of the two types:

(1)

The Laplacian operator is replaced by more general operators;

(2)

(
1.1
) is changed to a class of more general semilinear elliptic equations whenever (
1.2
) is replaced by a class of potentials with different or even similar geometry.

Typically, both cases can occur simultaneously. We would like to cite some works involving generalizations and variations concerning problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
). In
[
6
]
and
[
14
]
, discussions revolve around results concerning the fractional Laplacian operator. On the other hand,
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
delve into outcomes associated with the
\Phi
-Laplacian operator, encompassing the classical
p
-Laplacian. Other interesting works involving the prescribed mean curvature operator are found in
[
7

13
]
. Lastly, for a recent comprehensive overview of a fourth-order equation, we refer the reader to
[
12
]
.

In this work, we will show that the following class of prescribed mean curvature equations

(1.3)

-div\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)+A(x,y)V^{\prime}(u)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{2},

is
1

is small. Moreover, in the particular case, when
A(x,y)
is a positive constant, we will show that there is an infinite number of geometrically distinct saddle-type solutions for (
1.3

satisfies precisely the following assumptions:

(
A_{1}
)

is continuous and
A(x,y)>0
for each
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(

(

for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
;

(
A_{4}
)

A(x,y)=A(y,x)
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

An interesting model for
A
is described by the expression:

A(x,y)=\cos(2\pi x)\cos(2\pi y)+c,

where
c
is a constant with
c>1
. From now on, let’s consider
\mathcal{V}=\{V_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha>0}
as a class of symmetric potentials

that satisfy the following conditions:

(
V_{1}

for all

for

(

V_{\alpha}(-t)=V_{\alpha}(t)
for any

.

With respect to class

(
V_{3}
)

Given

\displaystyle\max_{|t|\in[0,\alpha]}|V^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)|\leq C,\quad\forall V_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{V}\quad\text{with}\quad\alpha\in(0,\lambda).

An important example of a family
\mathcal{V}
of potentials

, for which conditions

Recently, Kurganov and Rosenau in
[

reported an interesting physical motivation for investigating transition-type solutions to Allen-Cahn type equations involving the prescribed mean curvature, as exemplified by equation (

[

[

-\left(\frac{q^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1+(q^{\prime})^{2}}}\right)^{\prime}+a(t)V^{\prime}(q)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{in}\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{R},

where

a double-well potential with minima at
t=\pm 1
and

is asymptotic at infinity to a positive periodic function with
0<\displaystyle\operatorname{ess}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}a(t)
. After, Alves and Isneri in
[
7

also explored the existence of basic heteroclinic solution for (
1.4

is an even non-negative function with

for some

. In that paper, inspired to the one introduced by Obersnel and Omari in
[

, the authors used cutting techniques on the mean curvature operator to build up a variational framework on the usual Orlicz-Sobolev space

to show that (
1.4

q
such that

\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}q(t)=\pm\alpha

whenever the distance between the roots
\pm\alpha
of the symmetric potential
V
is small. When
a(t)=1
, it was shown in the recent work by Alves, Isneri and Montecchiari
[
9

that under certain conditions in potential
V
, there exists
\alpha_{0}>0
such that for each
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{0})
the equation (
1.4
) has, up to translation, a unique twice differentiable heteroclinic solution
q
in
C^{1,\gamma}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
satisfying the following exponential decay estimates

0<\alpha-q(t)\leq\theta_{1}e^{-\theta_{2}t}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ \penalty\ 0<q^{\prime}(t)\leq\beta_{1}e^{-\beta_{2}t}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ for all }t\geq 0

and

0<\alpha+q(t)\leq\theta_{3}e^{\theta_{4}t}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ \penalty\ 0<q^{\prime}(t)\leq\beta_{3}e^{\beta_{4}t}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ for all }t\leq 0,

for some real numbers
\theta_{i},\beta_{i}>0
. When
V(t)=\left(t^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right)^{2}
the exponential estimates above are refined to

\alpha\tanh\left(\alpha\sqrt{2}t\right)\leq q(t)\leq\alpha\tanh\left(\frac{\alpha\sqrt{2}t}{\kappa}\right)\penalty\ \text{ for }\penalty\ t\geq 0,

where
\kappa
is a positive constant that depends on
\|q^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
. Heteroclinic-type solutions are also observed in planar scenarios. For instance, in
[
8
]
, Alves and Isneri show the existence of such solutions for (

). These solutions, denoted by

, exhibit periodicity in

with a particular asymptotic behavior:

u(x,y)\rightarrow\alpha\text{ as }x\rightarrow-\infty\text{ and }u(x,y)\rightarrow\beta\text{ as }x\rightarrow+\infty,\text{ uniformly in }y\in\mathbb{R},

where
\alpha
and
\beta
represent the minima of a nonsymmetric double-well potential
V
. This phenomenon occurs under the condition that
A(x,y)
displays oscillatory behavior with respect to the variable
y
and satisfies some geometric constraints on

In this study, we show that equation (
1.3
) exhibits additional transition-type solutions, including the saddle solution, which are more intricate in nature. The key theorems of this paper are outlined below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume (
A_{1}
)-(
A_{4}
). Given
L>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for each

, the prescribed mean curvature equation

-div\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)+A(x,y)V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(u)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{2},

possesses a weak solution
v_{\alpha,L}
in
C^{1,\gamma}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
, satisfying the following properties:

(a)

0<v_{\alpha,L}(x,y)<\alpha
on the fist quadrant in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(b)

v_{\alpha,L}(x,y)=-v_{\alpha,L}(-x,y)=-v_{\alpha,L}(x,-y)
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(c)

v_{\alpha,L}(x,y)=v_{\alpha,L}(y,x)
for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(d)

v_{\alpha,L}(x,y)\rightarrow\alpha

x\rightarrow\pm\infty

y\rightarrow\pm\infty
,

(e)

v_{\alpha,L}(x,y)\rightarrow-\alpha
as

and

(f)

\|\nabla v_{\alpha,L}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\sqrt{L}
.

The items
(d)
and
(e)
of the theorem above tells us that along directions parallel to the axes, the saddle solution

is uniformly asymptotic to stationary solutions
\pm\alpha
. Moreover, when

is a positive constant, we obtain an infinite number of geometrically distinct saddle-type solutions to the equation (
1.3
). This observation is detailed in the ensuing result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
A(x,y)
is a positive constant
b
. Then, given
L>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for each
V_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{V}\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
with
\alpha\in(0,\delta)
and for each
j\geq 2
, the prescribed mean curvature equation

-div\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)+bV_{\alpha}^{\prime}(u)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{2},

possesses a weak solution
v_{\alpha,L,j}
in
C^{1,\gamma}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
, such that if
\tilde{v}_{\alpha,L,j}(\rho,\theta)=v_{\alpha,L,j}(\rho\cos(\theta),\rho\sin(\theta))

(a)

0<\tilde{v}_{\alpha,L,j}(\rho,\theta)<\alpha
for any
\theta\in[\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{2j},\frac{\pi}{2})
and
\rho>0
,

(b)

\tilde{v}_{\alpha,L,j}(\rho,\frac{\pi}{2}+\theta)=-\tilde{v}_{\alpha,L,j}(\rho,\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta)

(\rho,\theta)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}

(c)

\tilde{v}_{\alpha,L,j}(\rho,\theta+\frac{\pi}{j})=-\tilde{v}_{\alpha,L,j}(\rho,\theta)
for all

(d)

\tilde{v}_{\alpha,L,j}(\rho,\theta)\rightarrow(-\alpha)^{k+1}

whenever
\theta\in\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+k\frac{\pi}{j},\frac{\pi}{2}+(k+1)\frac{\pi}{j}\right)

(e)

\|\nabla v_{\alpha,L,j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\sqrt{L}
.

In other words, the saddle-type solution
\tilde{v}_{\alpha,L,j}
is antisymmetric with respect to the half-line
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
,
\frac{2\pi}{j}
-periodic in the angle variable and has

-norm less than or equal to
\alpha
with the asymptotic behavior at infinity described in item

. Moreover, the solutions

described in the theorem above are characterized by the fact that, along different directions parallel to the end lines, they are uniformly asymptotic to
\pm\alpha
and such solutions may appropriately be termed “pizza solutions”.

For example, a possible extension to Theorems

(2.1)

-\Delta_{\Phi}u+A(x,y)V^{\prime}(u)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{2},

where the conditions
(A_{1})
-
(A_{4})

,
(V_{1})
-
(V_{2})

(
V_{4}
)

There are

\tilde{v}_{j}(\rho,\theta+\frac{\pi}{j})=-\tilde{v}_{j}(\rho,\theta)
for all
(\rho,\theta)\in[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
,

(d)

\tilde{v}_{j}(\rho,\theta)\rightarrow(-\alpha)^{k+1}
as
\rho\rightarrow+\infty
whenever
\theta\in\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+k\frac{\pi}{j},\frac{\pi}{2}+(k+1)\frac{\pi}{j}\right)

.

In fact, the conditions on

in the aforementioned theorem can be refined; namely, conditions
(V_{6})
and

can be omitted. To illustrate this, one can combine several arguments from the paper

]
and implement the concept of partitioning

into

disjoint triangular sets, where

, as described in the construction of saddle solutions presented in

. As a result, we can present the following theorem, with the details of its verification left to the reader.

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume (
\phi_{1}
)-(
\phi_{3}
), (
\tilde{\phi}_{4}
), (
V_{1}
)-(
V_{2}
), (
V_{4}
)-(
V_{5}
) and that
A(x,y)
is a positive constant. Then, For each

, there exists

for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that

is a weak solution of (

). Moreover, by setting

(a)

0<\tilde{v}_{j}(\rho,\theta)<\alpha
for any

and

,

(b)

\tilde{v}_{j}(\rho,\frac{\pi}{2}+\theta)=-\tilde{v}_{j}(\rho,\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta)
for all

,

(c)

\tilde{v}_{j}(\rho,\theta+\frac{\pi}{j})=-\tilde{v}_{j}(\rho,\theta)

3.
Saddle solution of the prescribed mean curvature equation

Our primary goal in this section is to establish the existence of a saddle solution to the prescribed mean curvature equation (
1.3
) under the condition that the global minima of the potential
V
are sufficiently close. For this reason, we will index the number
\alpha
in
V
, denoting it as
V_{\alpha}
, and throughout this section, we will assume that
V_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{V}\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
. To achieve this, we first study an auxiliary problem of the form (
2.1
), proving the existence of a saddle solution within this context. The idea here is similar to those presented in work
[
7
]
, so the exposition will be brief. For our purposes, we employ a truncation scheme for the prescribed mean curvature operator, defined for each
L>0
as follows:

\varphi_{L}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+t}},&\mbox{if}\quad t\in[0,L],\\
x_{L}(t-L-1)^{2}+y_{L},&\mbox{if}\quad t\in[L,L+1],\\
y_{L},&\mbox{if}\quad t\in[L+1,+\infty),\end{array}\right.

where the numbers
x_{L}
and
y_{L}
are expressed by

x_{L}=\frac{\sqrt{1+L}}{4(1+L)^{2}}\penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ y_{L}=(4L+3)x_{L}.

As a consequence, for each fixed
L>0
, we get the following quasilinear equation

(3.1)

-\Delta_{\Phi_{L}}u+A(x,y)V^{\prime}_{\alpha}(u)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{in}\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{2},

where
\Phi_{L}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
is an
N
-function of the form

\Phi_{L}(t)=\int_{0}^{|t|}\phi_{L}(s)sds\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{with}\penalty\ \penalty\ \phi_{L}(t)=\varphi_{L}(t^{2}).

The following two lemmas will be frequently referenced throughout this work and they can be found in
[
7
,
9
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For each
L>0
, the functions
\phi_{L}
and
\Phi_{L}
satisfy the following properties:

(a)

\phi_{L}
is
C^{1}
.

(b)

y_{L}\leq\phi_{L}(t)\leq 1
for each
t\geq 0
.

(c)

\dfrac{y_{L}t^{2}}{2}\leq\Phi_{L}(t)\leq\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}
for any
t\geq 0
.

(d)

\Phi_{L}
is a convex function.

(e)

\left(\phi_{L}\left(t\right)t\right)^{\prime}>0
for all
t>0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For each
L>0
, it turns out that
\phi_{L}
satisfies
(\phi_{1})
-
(\phi_{3})
.

A direct check shows that
\phi_{L}
exhibits a monotonically non-increasing behavior over the interval
(0,+\infty)
. Consequently,
\phi_{L}
does not satisfy
(\phi_{4})
. However, each
\phi_{L}
satisfies
(\tilde{\phi}_{4})
, as the following lemma says.

Lemma 3.3
.

For each
L>0
, the function
\phi_{L}
satisfies
(\tilde{\phi}_{4})
.

Proof.

Indeed, according to Lemma
3.1
-
(b)
we have that

y_{L}\leq\phi_{L}(t)\leq 1\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ t\geq 0.

In particular,

y_{L}\leq\phi_{L}(|\zeta^{\prime}(t)|),\phi_{L}(\zeta(t))\leq 1\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ t\in\mathbb{R},

and therefore,

\phi_{L}(|\zeta^{\prime}(t)|)\leq\frac{1}{y_{L}}\phi_{L}(\zeta(t))\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ t\in\mathbb{R}.

Finally, since there is no restriction for the constant
a>0
in (
2.3
), the lemma follows.
∎

The evidence provided in Lemma
3.3
leads to the following observation.

Remark 1
.

By the argument of the previous lemma, we can conclude that any function
\phi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
satisfying

c_{1}\leq\phi(t)\leq c_{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ t\geq 0

for some constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
satisfies condition
(\tilde{\phi}_{4})
.

To find saddle solutions for the equation (
1.3
), our approach involves examining the existence of these solutions within the framework of the auxiliary problem (
3.1
). To begin with, note that the condition
V_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{V}\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
implies that
V_{\alpha}
satisfies the conditions
(V_{4})
with respect to
\Phi_{L}
and
(V_{5})
concerning
\phi_{L}
. Indeed, by
(V_{2})
it is possible to find numbers
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}>0
such that

|V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)|\leq d_{1}|t-\alpha|\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ t\in[\alpha-\rho_{1},\alpha+\rho_{1}]

and

d_{2}|t-\alpha|^{2}\leq V_{\alpha}(t)\leq d_{3}|t-\alpha|^{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(\alpha-\rho_{2},\alpha+\rho_{2}),

from which it follows by Lemma
3.1
that

|V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)|\leq\frac{d_{1}}{y_{L}}\phi_{L}(|t-\alpha|)|t-\alpha|\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ t\in[\alpha-\rho_{1},\alpha+\rho_{1}]

and

2d_{2}\Phi_{L}(|t-\alpha|)\leq V_{\alpha}(t)\leq\frac{2d_{3}}{y_{L}}\Phi_{L}(|t-\alpha|)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(\alpha-\rho_{2},\alpha+\rho_{2}).

Hence, we may use Theorem
2.1
to obtain, for each
L>0
, a saddle solution denoted as
v_{\alpha,L}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
to equation (
3.1
). This solution,
v_{\alpha,L}
, is a weak solution to (
3.1
) belonging to
C^{1,\gamma}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
, satisfying

|v_{\alpha,L}(x,y)|\leq\alpha\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ for all }\penalty\ \penalty\ (x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Moreover, it preserves the same sign pattern as
xy
, odd in both the variables
x
and
y
, symmetric with respect to the diagonals
y=\pm x
and presents the asymptotic behavior described in items
(d)
and
(e)
of Theorem
2.1
. We are now going to use this information together with condition
(V_{3})
to establish an estimate involving the functions
v_{\alpha,L}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that for each
V_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{V}
with
\alpha\in(0,\delta)
, the function
v_{\alpha,L}
satisfies following the estimate below

(3.2)

\|v_{\alpha,L}\|_{C^{1}(B_{1}(z))}<\sqrt{L},

where
B_{1}(z)
denotes any open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with radius 1.

Proof.

To establish this lemma, we employ a proof by contradiction. Let us assume the contrary. Then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a potential
V_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}}\in\mathcal{V}\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
with
\alpha\in\left(0,\frac{1}{n}\right)
and
z_{n}=(z_{n,1},z_{n,2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that the function
v_{\alpha_{n},L}
satisfies

(3.3)

\|v_{\alpha_{n},L}\|_{C^{1}(B_{1}(z_{n}))}\geq\sqrt{L}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

For the purpose of our analysis, we will study the regularity of specific solutions to the following elliptic equation:

(3.4)

-\Delta_{\Phi_{L}}u+B_{n}(x,y)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{2},

where the scalar measurable function
B_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as:

B_{n}(x,y)=A(x+z_{n,1},y+z_{n,2})V_{\alpha_{n}}^{\prime}(v_{\alpha_{n},L}(x,y)).

Under condition
(V_{3})
,
B_{n}
satisfies the subsequent estimate:

(3.5)

|B_{n}(x,y)|\leq C\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{ and }\forall n\geq n_{0},

for some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
C>0
, where
C
is independent of
n
. Indeed, it’s worth noting that the condition
\alpha_{n}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
implies the existence of
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha_{n}\in(0,1)
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Consequently, by
(V_{3})
, it follows that there exists a constant
C=C(1)>0
independent of
\alpha\in(0,1)
satisfying

\displaystyle\max_{|t|\in[0,\alpha]}|V^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)|\leq C,\quad\forall V_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{V}\quad\text{with}\quad\alpha\in(0,1).

Now, since
\alpha_{n}\in(0,1)
for all
n\geq n_{0}
and

|v_{\alpha_{n},L}(x,y)|\leq\alpha_{n}\quad\forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

we obtain

|V^{\prime}_{\alpha_{n}}(v_{\alpha_{n},L}(x,y))|\leq C,\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall n\geq n_{0},

which is sufficient to ensure inequality (
3.5
). A particularly relevant weak solution to (
3.4
) is given by:

u_{n}(x,y)=v_{\alpha_{n},L}(x+z_{n,1},y+z_{n,2})\penalty\ \text{ for }\penalty\ (x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Now, utilizing inequality (
3.5
), we can employ elliptic regularity estimates on
u_{n}
, as developed by Lieberman in
[
16
, Theorem 1.7]
. This yields
u_{n}\in C^{1,\gamma_{0}}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
\gamma_{0}\in(0,1)
, and

\|u_{n}\|_{C^{1,\gamma_{0}}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq R\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ n\geq n_{0},

where
R
is a positive constant independent of
n
. Consequently, from the above estimate, employing Arzelà-Ascoli’s theorem, we deduce the existence of
u\in C^{1}(B_{1}(0))
and a subsequence of
(u_{n})
, still denoted by
(u_{n})
, such that

(3.6)

u_{n}\to u\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ C^{1}(B_{1}(0)).

Since
\|v_{\alpha_{n},L}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
goes to
0
as
\alpha_{n}\to 0
,

\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\to 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{as}\penalty\ \penalty\ n\to+\infty,

thus from the convergence (
3.6
), we naturally infer that
u=0
on
B_{1}(0)
. Therefore, there exists
\tilde{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that:

\|u_{n}\|_{C^{1}\left(B_{1}(0)\right)}<\sqrt{L}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ n\geq\tilde{n},

which, arising from the definition of
u_{n}
, implies:

\|v_{\alpha_{n},L}\|_{C^{1}(B_{1}(z_{n}))}<\sqrt{L}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ n\geq\tilde{n},

which contradicts (
3.3
). Hence, the proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

The estimate (
3.2
) will play a key role in the theorem below, facilitating the investigation of the existence of saddle solutions for the prescribed mean curvature equation (

) whenever the roots

of

are sufficiently close.

The proof of Theorem

.

We claim that for each fixed

there exists
\delta>0
such that for each

with

In fact, given any

we can choose a point

such that
(x,y)\in B_{1}(z)
. By virtue of Lemma
3.4
, there exists
\delta>0
such that if
V_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{V}\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha\in(0,\delta)

\alpha\in(0,\delta)
one gets

\|v_{\alpha,L}\|_{C^{1}(B_{1}(z))}<\sqrt{L}.

In particular,

\|\nabla v_{\alpha,L}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}(z))}\leq\sqrt{L}.

Therefore, the claim (

) is valid from the arbitrariness of

, and thus, the estimate (

is a saddle solution to the equation (

) satisfying items

to

, thanks to the study developed in the auxiliary problem (

\square

In the case where

is a positive constant, the existence of infinitely many saddle-type solutions for the prescribed mean curvature equation (

4.
Final remarks

In this last section, we aim to highlight that although we have made advancements over the results presented in works
[
10
,
11
]
, particularly concerning saddle solutions to quasilinear elliptic equations represented by

(4.1)

-\Delta_{\Phi}u+A(x,y)V^{\prime}(u)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{2}

by imposing condition
(\tilde{\phi}_{4})
on
\phi
instead of
(\phi_{4})
, a significant challenge remains unresolved. Specifically, the case
\phi(t)=t^{p-2}
with
p\in(1,2)
, associated with the
N
-function

\Phi(t)=\frac{|t|^{p}}{p}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ \penalty\ p\in(1,2),

presents an open problem because, in this case,
\phi
does not satisfy
(\tilde{\phi}_{4})
. This fact arises from the observation that through a direct calculation, we have

\frac{\phi(|\zeta^{\prime}(t)|)}{\phi(\kappa_{2}\zeta(t))}\to+\infty\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{as}\penalty\ \penalty\ |t|\to\frac{j-\frac{1}{4}+L}{2},

for any positive constant
\kappa_{2}
. This divergence is evident from the behavior of

\left|\frac{\sinh(t)}{\cosh(t)}\right|=\left|\frac{e^{2t}-1}{e^{2t}+1}\right|,

which tends to zero as
|t|\to 0
, resulting in

\left|\frac{\sinh(t)}{\cosh(t)}\right|^{p-2}\to+\infty\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{as}\penalty\ \penalty\ |t|\to 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ p\in(1,2).

Finally, it’s worth noting that exploring exponential decay type estimates to determine saddle solutions in cases where (
4.1
) involves the
p
-Laplacian operator with
1<p<2
is potentially difficult and interesting.
