Rotational smoothing
Pedro Caro, Cristóbal J. Meroño, Ioannis Parissis

Abstract
. Rotational smoothing is a phenomenon consisting in a gain of regularity by means of averaging over rotations. This phenomenon is present in operators that regularize only in certain directions, in contrast to operators regularizing in all directions. The gain of regularity is the result of rotating the directions where the corresponding operator performs the smoothing effect. In this paper we carry out a systematic study of the rotational smoothing for a class of operators that includes k k -vector-space Riesz potentials in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} with k < n k<n , and the convolution with fundamental solutions of elliptic constant-coefficient differential operators acting on k k -dimensional linear subspaces. Examples of the latter type of operators are the planar Cauchy transform in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , or a solution operator for the transport equation in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . The analysis of rotational smoothing is motivated by the resolution of some inverse problems under low-regularity assumptions.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we introduce the concept of rotational smoothing to refer to a phenomenon consisting in the gain of regularity by means of averaging over rotations. In order to illustrate this phenomenon, let us consider an elliptic homogeneous polynomial of order
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
variables and complex coefficients

(1)

p(\xi)=\sum_{|\alpha|=m}c_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}.

Here
c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})
,
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
is a multi-index so that
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}
and
\xi^{\alpha}=\xi_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\xi_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
. By calling this homogeneous polynomial elliptic, we mean that if
p(\xi)=0
for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
then
\xi=0
. It is well known that the differential operator

(2)

P={p(-\mathrm{i}\partial)}=\sum_{|\alpha|=m}(-\mathrm{i})^{m}c_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}

associated to (
1

E
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
so that

is a solution of the equation
Pu=f
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and

(3)

\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}

for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

1.1.
Main results

In this article, we carry out a systematic study of the phenomenon of rotational
smoothing for a general class of operators.
These operators are defined by considering, for each

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}=(0,\infty)
, a continuous function
q_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}

satisfying the following properties:

(a)

If
q_{\alpha}(\eta)=0
then
\eta=0
.

(b)

The equality
q_{\alpha}(\lambda\eta)=\lambda^{\alpha}q_{\alpha}(\eta)

holds whenever
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
.

Then, define the symbols

p_{\alpha}(\xi,Q)=q_{\alpha}(Qe_{1}\cdot\xi,\dots,Qe_{k}\cdot\xi),\qquad(\xi,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n).

Let
P_{\alpha}(Q)
be the operator
defined by

[P_{\alpha}(Q)f](x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}\,p_{\alpha}(\xi,Q)\,\widehat{f}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi,\quad(x,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n),

for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
We ask whether we can provide a solution operator
S_{\alpha}
so that if

u(x,Q)=S_{\alpha}f(x,Q)
, then
P_{\alpha}(Q)u(\centerdot,Q)=f
in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
and

(5)

\bigg(\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\|S_{\alpha}f(\centerdot,Q)\|_{\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Note again that these operators are not elliptic since their symbols vanish on linear subspaces of codimension
k
.

In order to clarify how this framework generalizes the previous discussion about
differential operators, it may be convenient to note that if we choose
q_{m}=p

with
p
the polynomial function in (
1
), then
p_{m}(\xi,Q)
is
the polynomial
\tilde{p}_{Q}(\xi)
in (
4
) and
P_{m}(Q)

is its corresponding differential operator
\tilde{P}(Q)
. The generalization
described above allows us to also introduce non-local operators in our analysis,
such as the fractional Laplacian acting on
k
-dimensional vector subspaces of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note that if
q_{\alpha}(\eta)=|\eta|^{\alpha}
for all

\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, then
P_{\alpha}(\mathrm{id})=(-\Delta_{x^{\prime}})^{\alpha/2}
, where

\Delta_{x^{\prime}}
denotes
\partial_{x_{1}}^{2}+\dots+\partial_{x_{k}}^{2}
.

Before stating the result quantifying the rotational smoothing phenomenon, we
need to complement the conditions
(
a
) and (
b
) with an additional cancellation
assumption, whenever
\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
:

(c)

If
\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
q_{\alpha}
satisfies
that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k-1}}\frac{\theta^{\beta}}{q_{\alpha}(\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)=0,\qquad\forall\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k},\quad|\beta|=\alpha-k,

where the volume form
\mathrm{d}S
denotes the usual spherical measure on
\mathbb{S}^{k-1}
. In the case
k=1
we have that
\mathbb{S}^{0}=\{-1,1\}
, and (
c
) is interpreted as

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k-1}}\frac{\theta^{\beta}}{q_{\alpha}(\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)=\frac{(-1)^{\alpha-1}}{q_{\alpha}(-1)}+\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}(1)}=0.

This discussion presents the bare minimum framework and motivation to state our
main result. From now on we set

\lceil\alpha-k\rceil=\min\{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\alpha-k<l\}.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq 2
. Let
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and
\alpha>0
be given, and consider
q_{\alpha}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{k})
satisfying (
a
), (
b
) and (

). There exists a solution operator
S_{\alpha}
and a constant
C>0
depending on
n
,
k
,
\alpha
and
\delta
such that, for all

we have
that

\|S_{\alpha}f\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

with
\delta=\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
if
\alpha-k/2<\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
, and
\delta>\alpha-k/2
if
\alpha-k/2\geq\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
.

Note that in the range
0<\alpha<k/2
, we can choose
\delta=0
so that the
identity in
1
implies the inequality
(
5
). Unfortunately, whenever
\alpha\geq k/2

we have to replace the
L^{2}
-average on the rotations by a weaker quantity
captured by the
H^{-\delta}
-norm on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
. Nevertheless, this result is
satisfactory in terms of the regularity gain because the
-\delta
derivatives
of the Sobolev space in
\mathrm{SO}(n)
act directly on the operator and not on the
functions of its domain. Thus, we still obtain a gain of
\alpha
derivatives in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

At this point, the statement of the result is vague in the sense that it is not
clear if the solution operator
S_{\alpha}
can be explicitly defined.
Additionally, the restrictions on
\delta
imposed by the
relations between
\alpha
and
k
may seem mysterious. For this reason, we
present in
Section

2
a more precise version of

1
where the definition of
S_{\alpha}
and the
constant

are explicit. The discussion in

Section

2
also clarifies the restrictions on
\delta
.
For the case
0<\alpha<k
, the definition of
S_{\alpha}

is very simple and can be found in (
11
).

In order to analyse the need of replacing the

L^{2}
-average on the rotations by the

H^{-\delta}
-norm on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, we choose
q_{\alpha}(\eta)=|\eta|^{\alpha}
for
all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
0<\alpha<k
and define the solution map, denoted in

I_{\alpha}f(x,Q)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}\,\frac{\widehat{f}(\xi)}{|(Qe_{1}\cdot\xi)^{2}+\dots+(Qe_{k}\cdot\xi)^{2}|^{\alpha/2}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad(x,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n),

for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
The solution operator
I_{\alpha}
is the
k
-vector-space Riesz potential, and its
definition is possible because the function

\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Sigma_{Q}\longmapsto|(Qe_{1}\cdot\xi)^{2}+\dots+(Qe_{k}\cdot\xi)^{2}|^{-\alpha/2},

with

\Sigma_{Q}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Qe_{1}\cdot\xi=\dots=Qe_{k}\cdot\xi=0\}

can be extended to a locally-integrable function in

. However,
if

\|I_{\alpha}f(\centerdot,Q)\|_{\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|\xi|^{2\alpha}}{|(Qe_{1}\cdot\xi)^{2}+\dots+(Qe_{k}\cdot\xi)^{2}|^{\alpha}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\infty,\qquad\forall\,Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n).

This follows from the fact that the function

\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Sigma_{Q}\mapsto|\xi|^{\alpha}/|(Qe_{1}\cdot\xi)^{2}+\dots+(Qe_{k}\cdot\xi)^{2}|^{\alpha/2}

cannot be extended to a locally-square-integrable function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We remark that the first identity above is a consequence of Plancherel’s theorem. Thus, an
L^{2}
-average version of rotational smoothing in the
range
k/2\leq\alpha<k
cannot be global. A possible way around this is to
postulate a local version of the rotational smoothing estimate where the
left-hand side of the identity in
1
would be
replaced, in the case of the
k
-vector-space Riesz potential, by

\bigg(\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}I_{\alpha}f(\centerdot,Q)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{R})}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)\bigg)^{1/2}.

Here
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
denotes the fractional Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

B_{R}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x|<R\}
. However, there is no hope even for an
inequality of this type without loss of derivatives.
In fact, we will see in the following result that, when localizing in balls and
then averaging in
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, the
k
-vector-space Riesz potential exhibits a loss
of derivatives in the range
k/2\leq\alpha<k
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq 2
. Let
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and
k/2\leq\alpha<k
be given. For every

there exists a sequence of functions
\{f_{N}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that

and

for all

. The growth function
\mathfrak{g}
is given by
\mathfrak{g}(t)=\log t
if
\alpha=k/2
and
\mathfrak{g}(t)=t^{2\alpha-k}
if
\alpha>k/2
. The implicit constants are independent of
R
and
N
.

This theorem ensures a loss of at least
s
derivatives with
s=\alpha-k/2

if
\alpha>k/2
and
s>0
if
\alpha=k/2
, compared to the maximum
expected gain of
\alpha
derivatives. The proof of

2
is given in
Section

5
.
Putting together Theorems
1
and

2

we see that the
s

are regularized by
-\delta
derivatives in
\mathrm{SO}(n)
of

—note that

in the range

.

The homogeneity assumption (

q_{\alpha}

plays a key role in the proof of
1
. This property is
immediately transferred to the distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\}

determined by the function

\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\}\mapsto 1/q_{\alpha}(\eta)
.
To construct the solution operator
S_{\alpha}
, we need to extend
this distribution to belong to

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
. However, in order for the extension to preserve the
homogeneity in the case
\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we need to impose the
cancellation condition (

).
This is analysed in
Section

3
. Furthermore, as derived from the
following result, this cancellation property is needed not only to preserve

\alpha
derivatives.

Theorem 3
.

Assume
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq 2
. Let

k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and
\alpha>0
be such that
\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Consider
q_{\alpha}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{k})
satisfying
(

), (

), but assume that

Then, there exists a

1.2.
Rotational smoothing and inverse problems

The study of rotational smoothing is motivated by certain inverse boundary value problems in a non-regular setting. In order to give the relevant examples that drive the research reported in this paper, we consider the inverse problem consisting in determining the advection term in the convection equation from non-invasive measurements. One can consider two different physical situations when formulating this problem.

In a first approach, we can assume the advection to be described by a static vector field
A
and data to be obtained from boundary measurements of solutions of the steady-state convection equation
\Delta u+A\cdot\nabla u=0
in a bounded open subset
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 3
. Usually, boundary data is encoded in the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map defined by

\Lambda_{A}:f\mapsto\nu\cdot\nabla u|_{\partial D}

where
\partial D
denotes the boundary of
D
,
\nu
is the outward unit normal vector along
\partial D
, and
u
is the solution of the convection equation in
D
such that
u|_{\partial D}=f
—for more details on the formulation of this inverse boundary value problem see
[
3
,
8
,
7
]
. The only currently available procedure for determining
A
from
\Lambda_{A}
proceeds by constructing some special solutions for the differential operator
\Delta+A\cdot\nabla
. These solutions are called
complex geometrical optics
and they are expressed as follows

u_{\zeta}(x)=e^{\zeta\cdot x}(v_{\zeta}(x)+w_{\zeta}(x)),

where
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is such that
\zeta\cdot\zeta=0
; equivalently
|\Re\zeta|=|\Im\zeta|
and
\Re\zeta\cdot\Im\zeta=0
with
\Re\zeta
and
\Im\zeta
denoting the real and imaginary parts of
\zeta
respectively. The
amplitude

v_{\zeta}
and the
correction term

w_{\zeta}
are constructed as solutions of the equations

(6)

2\zeta\cdot\nabla v_{\zeta}+\zeta\cdot Av_{\zeta}=0,

and

(7)

(\Delta+2\zeta\cdot\nabla+A\cdot\nabla+\zeta\cdot A)w_{\zeta}=-(\Delta+A\cdot\nabla)v_{\zeta}.

In order to solve (
6
) we choose
\zeta=\tau Q(e_{1}+ie_{2})
with
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
and the amplitude in the form
v_{\zeta}=e^{\psi_{\zeta}}
, with
\psi_{\zeta}
solving

(8)

Q(e_{1}+ie_{2})\cdot\nabla\psi_{\zeta}=-\frac{1}{2}Q(e_{1}+ie_{2})\cdot A.

As we see from (
7
), the regularity of
v_{\zeta}
is important in
order to ensure that the right-hand side
-(\Delta+A\cdot\nabla)v_{\zeta}

is in an appropriate space so that we can solve for
w_{\zeta}
. In the case that
the components of
A
are just in
L^{\infty}(D)
, the a priori
regularity of solutions of the equation (
8
) is not
sufficient. For this reason, one needs to look for a hidden smoothing
phenomenon, namely rotational smoothing. Note that solutions of equation
(
8
) fit in the framework of
(
11
), with
\alpha=1
,
k=2
and

q_{1}(\eta)=i\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}
for all

\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, so that

p_{1}(\xi,Q)=q_{1}(Qe_{1}\cdot\xi,Qe_{2}\cdot\xi)=\xi\cdot Q(ie_{1}-e_{2}).

Indeed the solutions of (
8
) are given by the Cauchy transform along planes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 3
: for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
(x,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n)
,

\mathcal{C}f(x,Q)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\frac{\hat{f}(\xi)}{\xi\cdot Q(ie_{1}-e_{2})}\mathrm{d}\xi.

The second physical situation where one can consider this inverse problem corresponds to allowing advection terms modelled by time-dependent vector fields. Under these conditions, one has to consider the equation
\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+A\cdot\nabla u=0
in
D
throughout the time interval
(0,T)
. For a full description of the problem, see
[
2
]
. For the resolution of this inverse problem, one constructs geometric optics

u_{\kappa}(t,x)=e^{|\kappa|^{2}t+\kappa\cdot x}(v_{\kappa}(t,x)+w_{\kappa}(t,x)),

where
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
amplitude

v_{\kappa}
and the
correction
term

w_{\kappa}
are constructed as solutions of the equations

(9)

2\kappa\cdot\nabla v_{\kappa}-\kappa\cdot Av_{\kappa}=0

and

(\partial_{t}-\Delta-2\kappa\cdot\nabla+A\cdot\nabla+\kappa\cdot A)w_{\kappa}=(-\partial_{t}+\Delta-A\cdot\nabla)v_{\kappa}.

In order to solve (
9
) we choose
\kappa=\tau Qe_{1}
with

Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
, and the amplitude in the form
v_{\kappa}=e^{\psi_{\kappa}}
with

\psi_{\kappa}
solving

(10)

Qe_{1}\cdot\nabla\psi_{\kappa}=\frac{1}{2}Qe_{1}\cdot A.

In the case that the components of
A
are just in

L^{\infty}((0,T)\times D)
, one needs again to look for a rotational smoothing
phenomenon. Note that equation (
10
) fits the framework of
Theorem
1
with
\alpha=k=1
and

q_{\alpha}(\eta)=i\eta
for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that
\alpha-k=0
and
it also satisfies the cancellation property

1/q_{\alpha}(-1)+1/q_{\alpha}(1)=0
.

We expect that the systematic analysis of rotational smoothing carried out in
this article can be applied to the resolution of the previous and possibly other
inverse problems in non-regular settings.

Let

satisfy (

)
and (

Let

denote the restriction to

(\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}\longmapsto\left\{\begin{aligned} &1/q_{\alpha}(\xi^{\prime})&&\textnormal{if }\xi^{\prime}\neq 0,\\
&0&&\textnormal{if }\xi^{\prime}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

Thus, the rotational smoothing phenomenon can be formulated as follows.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume
n\in\mathbb{N}
with

. Let
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and

\alpha>0
be given. Consider

(

) and (

) with

.

with
\delta=0
if
\alpha<k/2
and

if

. Here

stands for the measure of the unit sphere.

Theorem

2.1
be proved in

4.2

Using Plancherel’s identity, together with the identities (

\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\|S_{\alpha}f(\centerdot,Q)\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|m_{\alpha}(\xi,\centerdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

where

(14)

m_{\alpha}(\xi,Q)=\frac{|\xi|^{\alpha}}{p_{\alpha}(\xi,Q)}\qquad(\xi,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n).

Let

be such that

m_{\alpha}(\xi,Q)=\frac{|\xi|^{\alpha}}{q_{\alpha}(|\xi|Qe_{1}\cdot Me_{1},\dots,|\xi|Qe_{k}\cdot Me_{1})}=r_{\alpha}(Q^{\mathsf{T}}Me_{1}).

In the last identity we have used the homogeneity of

Since

Here we discuss the extension of the distributions

of

as explained in

Recall that
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\})
denotes the space of smooth

\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
with compact support.
As mentioned earlier, the extension of (
18

by
q_{\alpha}^{-1}
, it will

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
and it will have the same homogeneity
property as (
18
).
More precisely, we will
construct the distribution

q_{\alpha}^{-1}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
satisfying:

(i)

For every
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that

\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1},\phi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\phi(\eta)}{q_{\alpha}(\eta)}\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

(ii)

For every
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
and

We establish the extension of the distribution in (

\alpha-k\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
and then for

\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. As we will see below, separating these cases is

\alpha-k\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
there is a unique extension

\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

there are no extensions satisfying (
ii
) unless the cancellation
condition (
c
) holds. Also, in the latter case the homogeneity
does not uniquely determine the extension. In order to state the extension
lemmas we recall once again that

\lceil\alpha-k\rceil=\min\{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\alpha-k<l\},

and introduce

\lfloor\alpha-k\rfloor=\max\{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:l\leq\alpha-k\}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha>0
be given, and consider
q_{\alpha}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{k})
satisfying (
a
) and (
b
). If
\alpha-k\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then the linear functional

\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\longmapsto\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{\phi(\eta)}{q_{\alpha}(\eta)}\,\mathrm{d}\eta,&\alpha-k<0,\\
\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-k-\lfloor\alpha-k\rfloor)}{\Gamma(\alpha-k+1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{(\eta\cdot\nabla)^{\lceil\alpha-k\rceil}\phi(\eta)}{q_{\alpha}(\eta)}\,\mathrm{d}\eta,&\alpha-k>0,\end{cases}

denoted by
q_{\alpha}^{-1}
, belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
and it is the only tempered distribution
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
satisfying the properties (
i
) and (
ii
).
Here
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function.

The extension of the distribution in (
18
) in the case
\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha>0
be given and consider
q_{\alpha}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{k})
satisfying
(
a
) and (
b
). If
\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then the linear functional

\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\longmapsto\frac{-1}{(\alpha-k)!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{\log|\eta|}{q_{\alpha}(\eta)}(\eta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k+1}\phi(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta,

denoted by
q_{\alpha}^{-1}
, belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
and satisfies (
i
). Moreover,
q_{\alpha}^{-1}
satisfies the homogeneity property (
ii
) if and only if
q_{\alpha}
satisfies (
c
).

Before going into the proof of each one of the two lemmas above we make some comments that are relevant for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha>0
.

Note that for
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\})
we can use spherical coordinates in order to write

(19)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\phi(\eta)}{q_{\alpha}(\eta)}\,\mathrm{d}\eta=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k-1}}\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}(\theta)}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{k-\alpha-1}\phi(\rho\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\rho\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta),

where the volume form
\mathrm{d}S
denotes the usual spherical measure on
\mathbb{S}^{k-1}
. With this identity in hand we see that if we aim at extending (
18
) it would be convenient to first extend the distribution in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, given by

(20)

\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\longmapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{k-\alpha-1}\psi(\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho.

In order to carry out this extension, consider for
a\in\mathbb{R}
the function
{\rm x}_{+}^{a}:\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
given by the formula

(21)

\mathrm{x}_{+}^{a}(x)=\begin{cases}x^{a},&x>0,\\
0,&x<0.\end{cases}

If
-1<a
then we can extend the function
{\rm x}_{+}^{a}
to a function defined in the whole set
\mathbb{R}
by setting for example
{\rm x}_{+}^{a}(0)=0
; this extension is locally integrable and consequently defines a tempered distribution in
\mathbb{R}
, still denoted by
{\rm x}_{+}^{a}
:

(22)

\langle{\rm x}_{+}^{a},\psi\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{a}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),\,a>-1.

Our next step is to construct a distribution on
\mathbb{R}
which extends the function
{\rm x}_{+}^{a}
when
a\leq-1
, defined by (
21
) in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. That is, we want to extend the functional

(23)

\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\longmapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{a}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

for
a\leq-1
. We first deal with the case

\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
we have that

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-1}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(x)\,\psi^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
\psi^{\prime}
denotes the first-order derivative of
\psi
. Note that the right-hand side of the previous inequality is finite for every

\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus, we define the desired extension as

(24)

\langle{\rm x}_{+}^{-1},\psi\rangle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(x)\,\psi^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\forall\,\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}).

In order to perform the extension in the range

or equivalently, if

{\rm x}_{+}^{a}=\frac{1}{(a+l)\dots(a+1)}\frac{d^{l}}{dx^{l}}{\rm x}_{+}^{a+l}.

This means that, if

,

and
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{a}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(-1)^{l}}{(a+l)\dots(a+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{a+l}\,\psi^{(l)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Here
\psi^{(l)}
denotes the

-order derivative of
\psi
. Therefore, whenever

, we can define the tempered distribution

(25)

\langle{\rm x}_{+}^{a},\psi\rangle=\frac{(-1)^{l}}{(a+l)\dots(a+1)}\langle{\rm x}_{+}^{a+l},\psi^{(l)}\rangle,\qquad\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}).

The procedure described above defines for all

\mathbb{R}
that extends the distribution given in

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. We

.

Using (

for all

such that

with

in the expressions of

) and

Proof of

.

The equality

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k-1}}\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}(\theta)}\langle{\rm x}_{+}^{k-\alpha-1},\psi_{\theta}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)=\frac{-1}{(\alpha-k)!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{\log|\eta|}{q_{\alpha}(\eta)}(\eta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k+1}\phi(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta

for
\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a consequence of the identity (
24
) when

\alpha=k
, and it follows (
25
) and (
24
) when

\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}
. Again one needs to use the relation
(
27
) to verify this.

Let us now address the homogeneity of
q_{\alpha}^{-1}
. In this case

\alpha-k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and we will have that the homogeneity holds if and only if
we assume condition (
c
).

After the change of variables
\eta=\lambda\zeta
we have that

(28)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{\log|\eta|}{q_{\alpha}(\eta)}(\eta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k+1}\phi(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta\\
&\qquad\qquad=\lambda^{-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{\log|\zeta|}{q_{\alpha}(\zeta)}(\zeta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k+1}[\lambda^{k}\phi(\lambda\centerdot)](\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad+\lambda^{-\alpha}\log\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}(\zeta)}(\zeta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k+1}[\lambda^{k}\phi(\lambda\centerdot)](\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.\end{split}

Hence, we can see that
q_{\alpha}^{-1}
satisfies (
ii
) if and
only if the last term on the right-hand side of the previous identity vanishes
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
. Let us compute this term explicitly.

Using spherical coordinates we have for any
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}(\zeta)}(\zeta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k+1}\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k-1}}\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}(\theta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{\theta}^{(\alpha-k+1)}(\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)

with
\psi_{\theta}(\rho)=\phi(\rho\theta)
for
\rho>0
, and

\psi_{\theta}^{(\alpha-k+1)}
denoting the
(\alpha-k+1)^{\rm th}
-order
derivative of
\psi_{\theta}
. Since by (
27
)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{\theta}^{(\alpha-k+1)}(\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho=-(\theta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k}\phi(0),

we have that

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}(\zeta)}(\zeta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k+1}\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta&=-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k-1}}\frac{(\theta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k}\phi(0)}{q_{\alpha}(\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)\\
&=-\sum_{|\beta|=\alpha-k}c_{\beta}\partial^{\beta}\phi(0)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{k-1}}\frac{\theta^{\beta}}{q_{\alpha}(\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)\end{split}

where the coefficients
c_{\beta}
are strictly positive. Therefore, the
right-hand side vanishes for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
if and only if
(
c
) holds. Consequently, if the cancellation property
(
c
) is verified then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}(\zeta)}(\zeta\cdot\nabla)^{\alpha-k+1}\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=0

for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, and by (
28
), property
(
ii
) is satisfied as desired. The proof of the lemma is complete
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Condition (
c
) in the
statement of Lemma
3.2
is necessary: if
q_{\alpha}
does not
satisfy (
c
) then there is no distribution in

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that (
i
) and (
ii
) are
satisfied simultaneously. To see this let
t_{\alpha}^{-1}
be a tempered
distribution satisfying (
i
). As mentioned in the proof of

Lemma

3.1

t_{\alpha}^{-1}
differs from
q_{\alpha}^{-1}
by a
linear combination of derivatives of a Dirac delta supported at
\{0\}
. It is
easy to verify that a linear combination of derivatives of
\delta_{0}
does not
affect the second summand in the right hand side of (
28
). Thus, if

t_{\alpha}^{-1}
satisfies (
i
) and (
ii
) then the
second summand in the right hand side of (
28
) must vanish and the
same proof yields that
t_{\alpha}^{-1}
must satisfy (
c
).

For future reference it is important to note that
q_{\alpha}^{-1}\in H^{-\delta}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
with
\delta=\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
if
\alpha-k/2<\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
and
\delta>\alpha-k/2
if
\alpha-k/2\geq\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
. In order to prove this property we show that
\chi q_{\alpha}^{-1}\in H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
for any
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By definition we have for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
that

(29)

|\langle\chi q_{\alpha}^{-1},\phi\rangle|\lesssim\Big(\int_{K}\Big(\log\frac{1+|\eta|}{|\eta|}\Big)^{p}\frac{|\eta|^{p\lceil\alpha-k\rceil}}{|q_{\alpha}(\eta)|^{p}}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\Big)^{1/p}\|\phi\|_{W^{\lceil\alpha-k\rceil,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{k})};

here
K=\supp\chi
. Using spherical coordinates one can check that the integral in
K
is finite whenever
p<k/[k-(\lceil\alpha-k\rceil-(\alpha-k))]
. Since
\alpha-k/2<\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
if and only if
2<k/[k-(\lceil\alpha-k\rceil-(\alpha-k))]
, we can choose
p=2
in (
29
) and deduce by duality that
\chi q_{\alpha}^{-1}\in H^{-\lceil\alpha-k\rceil}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
whenever

\alpha-k/2<\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
. If
\alpha-k/2\geq\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
, by Sobolev’s embedding theorem we have that

\begin{split}\|\phi\|_{W^{\lceil\alpha-k\rceil,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}&\eqsim\|(I-\Delta)^{\lceil\alpha-k\rceil/2}\phi\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\\
&\lesssim\|(I-\Delta)^{\lceil\alpha-k\rceil/2}\phi\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}=\|\phi\|_{H^{s+\lceil\alpha-k\rceil}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\end{split}

for
s-k/2=-k/p^{\prime}
. The upper bound on
p
imposes the lower bound
s+\lceil\alpha-k\rceil>\alpha-k/2
. Thus, if
\alpha-k/2\geq\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
, again by duality we can ensure that
\chi q_{\alpha}^{-1}\in H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
for all
\delta>\alpha-k/2
.

We conclude this section by recording some useful remarks.
Let
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
be such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\psi(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta=1
and for

t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
we set
\psi_{t}(\eta)=t^{k}\psi(t\eta)
for
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
We introduce the smooth function

(30)

q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}(\eta)=(q_{\alpha}^{-1}\star\psi_{t})(\eta)=\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1},\psi_{t}(\eta-\centerdot)\rangle,\qquad\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

First, one can verify that

(31)

\lim_{t\to 0}\|q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}=0,

This follows from (
ii
) which implies that

q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}(\eta)=\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1},\psi_{t}(\eta-\centerdot)\rangle=t^{\alpha}\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1},\psi(t\eta-\centerdot)\rangle.

Secondly, we have that for every compact subset
K
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

0\notin K

(32)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{\eta\in K}\Big|\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}(\eta)}-q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}(\eta)\Big|=0.

Thirdly, for every
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
we have that

(33)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}(\eta)\phi(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta=\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1},\phi\rangle.

Lastly, for every
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have that

(34)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\chi(q_{\alpha}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,t}^{-1})\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}=0,

with
\delta=\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
if
\alpha-k/2<\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
and
\delta>\alpha-k/2
if
\alpha-k/2\geq\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
.

Rotational smoothing in the general case

This section is divided into four subsections.
In
Section

we provide some preliminary facts about the functional
spaces and an important property of Sobolev spaces in
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, and in Sections

4.2
and
4.3
we prove

Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
. Recall from our discussion in the

Section

2
and the statement of
Theorem

2.2
,
the need of introducing a distribution
r_{\alpha}
standing formally as the
restriction to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
of
q_{\alpha}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}
.
In
Section

4.4
we give the explicit definition of
r_{\alpha}
.

4.1.
Some preliminary notions

We now summarize
the key properties of Sobolev spaces and
\mathrm{SO}(n)
that one needs in order to
prove
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
.
We provide the statements without proofs in this section, leaving the more
rigorous analysis of the functional framework for
Section

6
.

Let
(M,g)
stand for a compact boundaryless
Riemannian manifold of dimension
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote the canonical Laplace-Beltrami in
M
by
\Delta_{g}
. We denote by
\lambda_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{g}
, which are always non-negative, and denote by
\Pi_{j}
the projector onto the corresponding invariant subspace of eigenfunctions associated to
\lambda_{j}
.
This eigenvalue expansion yields a natural way to define the fractional Bessel potentials of order
s\in\mathbb{R}
for every
u\in C^{\infty}(M)

(35)

(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}u.

We can now define the fractional Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(M)
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
in an intrinsic way as the Banach completion of
C^{\infty}(M)
with respect to the norm

\|u\|_{H^{s}(M)}=\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u\|_{L^{2}(M)}=\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s}\|\Pi_{j}u\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

The next proposition yields an important relation between Sobolev spaces in

\mathrm{SO}(n)
and
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
which plays an important role in the proof of

Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix any
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and let
f
be in
H^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
. Consider the function
F_{v}:\mathrm{SO}(n)\to\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
given by

F_{v}(Q)=Q^{\mathsf{T}}v
for every rotation
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
.
Then,
it holds that

\|f\circ F_{v}\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}=S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})^{-1/2}\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

This result is a generalization for
s\neq 0
of the well known identity that we
have already stated in (
15
). The proof requires some preliminary
notions about
\mathrm{SO}(n)
and is given in
Appendix

A
. It essentially follows from an identity relating the operators
\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}
and
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
that we prove in
Lemma

A.6
.

The following proposition, which we prove in
Section

6
, will allow us to work with Sobolev spaces on local charts.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact
boundaryless Riemannian manifold of dimension
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\{(U_{1},\varphi_{1}),\dots,(U_{l},\varphi_{l})\}
be a smooth atlas for
M
,
and consider
\{\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{l}\}
a smooth partition of unity
subordinated to the atlas. Then, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

\|u\|_{H^{s}(M)}\eqsim\sum_{j=1}^{l}\|(\chi_{j}u)\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}

for all
u\in C^{\infty}(M)
. The implicit constants only depend on
m
,
s
, the
atlas and the partition of unity.

We now define the spaces
H^{s}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
that appear in the statement
of
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
denote the set of

u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
such that for every
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}

and every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists
C>0
such that

|x^{\beta}(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}\partial^{\alpha}u(x,p)|\leq C\qquad\forall\,(x,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M.

A function

u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
belongs to
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
if and only if its Fourier transform, denoted by
\widehat{u}
and defined by

(36)

(\xi,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M\longmapsto\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\xi\cdot x}u(x,p)\,\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{C},

belongs to
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
.
In
Section

6
we show that the non-negative functional

(37)

u\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)\longmapsto\Big(\int_{M}\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u(\centerdot,p)\|_{\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{g}(p)\Big)^{1/2}

is well defined for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. It defines
a norm on
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
that will be denoted from now on by

\|\centerdot\|_{H^{s}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}
. Then, the space

H^{s}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
is the Banach completion of

\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
with respect to the previous norm. For the
convenience of the reader let us point out that
L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and

H^{0}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
denote the same space.
In
Lemma

6.6
we also prove that

(38)

\|u\|_{H^{s}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2t}\|\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{H^{s}(M)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big)^{1/2}

for all
u\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Finally, we show in
Proposition

6.8
that every

u\in H^{s}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
determines a
unique distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M
.

With these ingredients we are now ready to prove

Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
. We remind the interested reader that detailed
statements and proofs of the preliminary results above are contained in

Section

6
.

4.2.
Proof of

Theorems

2.1
and
2.2

This section contains the full scheme needed to prove the rotational smoothing
phenomenon.

Consider the sequence
\{q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
with
q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}

defined in (
30
), and define for
N\in\mathbb{N}
the symbol

p_{\alpha,N}^{-1}(\xi,Q)=[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](Q^{\mathsf{T}}\xi),\qquad(\xi,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n),

and the operator

(39)

S_{\alpha,N}f(x,Q)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}p_{\alpha,N}^{-1}(\xi,Q)\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad(x,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n),

with
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. One can
check that
S_{\alpha,N}f\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n))
for all

f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. By
(
38
) we have that for
every
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the following identity holds

(40)

\displaystyle\|S_{\alpha,N}f

\displaystyle\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2\alpha}\|p_{\alpha,N}^{-1}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}^{2}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

For
v={\xi}/|\xi|
, we can write

p_{\alpha,N}^{-1}(\xi,Q)=[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](|\xi|Q^{\mathsf{T}}v),\qquad(\xi,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n),

and by

Proposition

4.1

we have that

(41)

\begin{split}\|p_{\alpha,N}^{-1}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}&=\|[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](|\xi|F_{v})\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}\\
&=S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})^{-1/2}\|[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](|\xi|\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}.\end{split}

Our first step towards deriving
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2

will be to show that, for all
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the
sequence
\{S_{\alpha,N}f:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a Cauchy sequence with respect to the
norm
\|\centerdot\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}
. Start by
considering an arbitrary
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and noting that for
L,N\in\mathbb{N}
we have that

\begin{split}S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})&\|S_{\alpha,N+L}f-S_{\alpha,N}f\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}^{2}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2\alpha}\|[(q_{\alpha,N+L}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,N}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](|\xi|\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}^{2}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.\end{split}

Recall that
\delta=\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
if
\alpha-k/2<\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
, and
\delta>\alpha-k/2
if
\alpha-k/2\geq\lceil\alpha-k\rceil
. We make the following claims:

(I)

For every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}|\xi|^{\alpha}\|[(q_{\alpha,N+L}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,N}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](|\xi|\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=0

uniformly for all
L\in\mathbb{N}
.

(II)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that

|\xi|^{\alpha}\|[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](|\xi|\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}\leq C

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and all
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

(III)

There is a distribution
r_{\alpha}\in H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|\lambda^{\alpha}[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\lambda\centerdot)-r_{\alpha}\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=0,

for every
\lambda>0
, where
r_{\alpha}
is independent of
\lambda
.

We postpone the proofs of the claims (
I
),
(
II
) and (
III
) until

Section

4.3
below. Assuming (
I
) and
(
II
), the dominated convergence theorem implies that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|S_{\alpha,N+L}f-S_{\alpha,N}f\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=0

uniformly in
L\in\mathbb{N}
, which means that
\{S_{\alpha,N}f:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
is Cauchy with respect to the norm
\|\centerdot\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}
.

We proceed to proving the identity in the statement of the theorem. Let us denote by
u_{\alpha}^{f}\in H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
the equivalence
class of the Cauchy sequence
\{S_{\alpha,N}f:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
; we have by
(
40
) and (
41
) that

\begin{split}S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\|&u_{\alpha}^{f}\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}^{2}\\
&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2\alpha}\|[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](|\xi|\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}^{2}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.\end{split}

On the one hand, as mentioned in
Section

4.1
,
u_{\alpha}^{f}
determines a distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n)
. On the other hand, we have that

S_{\alpha}f(x,Q)-S_{\alpha,N}f(x,Q)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\langle(q_{\alpha}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,N}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}},\widehat{f(Q(\centerdot+x))}\rangle.

By (
33
) we can ensure that for every compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|S_{\alpha}f-S_{\alpha,N}f\|_{L^{\infty}(K\times\mathrm{SO}(n))}=0.

Hence
S_{\alpha}f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n))
can be identified with a
distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n)
and

S_{\alpha,N}f\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}S_{\alpha}f\quad\textnormal{in}\quad\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathrm{SO}(n)).

This implies that
u_{\alpha}^{f}=S_{\alpha}f
which yields that

(42)

\displaystyle S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})

\displaystyle\|S_{\alpha}f\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2\alpha}\|[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](|\xi|\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}^{2}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Now (
III
) implies that

\|r_{\alpha}\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=\lim_{N\to\infty}|\xi|^{\alpha}\|[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](|\xi|\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}.

This identity together with (
II
) turn, by the
dominated convergence theorem, the identity (
42
) into

\|S_{\alpha}f\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathrm{SO}(n);\dot{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}^{2}=S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})^{-1}\|r_{\alpha}\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Consequently, Plancherel’s identity in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
yields the identities stated in

Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
. This completes their proofs, up to showing the
claims (
I
),
(
II
) and (
III
).

4.3.
Proofs of claims (
I
),
(
II
) and (
III
)

Before addressing these claims, we make a simple observation
about the set of distributions

\{q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}:t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
with

q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}
defined in (

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
we have that

(43)

\lambda^{\alpha}[q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\lambda\theta)=[q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\theta).

As we see in the following identities this is a consequence of the homogeneity
property of
q_{\alpha}^{-1}
(see
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
):

\begin{split}\lambda^{\alpha}q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}(\lambda\eta)&=\lambda^{\alpha}\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1},\psi_{t}(\lambda\eta-\centerdot)\rangle=\lambda^{\alpha}\lambda^{-\alpha}\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1},\lambda^{k}\psi_{t}(\lambda\eta-\lambda\centerdot)\rangle\\
&=\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1},\psi_{\lambda t}(\eta-\centerdot)\rangle=q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1}(\eta),\end{split}

where
\psi_{t}(\eta)=t^{k}\psi(t\eta)
.

We start by proving (
I
). In order to deal with the norm
of
H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
we will use
Proposition

4.2
. For this reason
we introduce an atlas

\{(U_{1},\varphi_{1}),\dots,(U_{2n},\varphi_{2n})\}
for
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
Consider the open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
given by

(44)

V=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:|y|^{2}<(n-1/2)/n\},

and let
\phi
denote the function
\phi(y)=(1-|y|^{2})^{1/2}
for all

y\in V
. The set
U=\{(y,\phi(y))\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:y\in V\}
is an open patch
in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and

\omega\in U\Rightarrow|e_{n}\cdot\omega|^{2}>1/(2n).

From this patch, we obtain an atlas of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. To do so, consider the reflections
R_{jn}
and
P_{j}
with
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
that satisfy:

(i)

R_{jn}e_{j}=e_{n}
,
R_{jn}e_{n}=e_{j}
and
R_{jn}e_{l}=e_{l}
for
l\notin\{j,n\}
,

(ii)

P_{j}e_{j}=-e_{j}
and
P_{j}e_{l}=e_{l}
for
l\neq j

With this choice
R_{nn}=\mathrm{id}
.
The atlas that we consider consists of
2n
charts denoted by
(U_{1},\varphi_{1}),\dots,(U_{2n},\varphi_{2n})
. The odd charts are defined by

(45)

\begin{split}U_{2j-1}&=\{R_{jn}\omega:\omega\in U\},\\
\varphi_{2j-1}(\theta)&=(e_{1}\cdot R_{jn}^{\mathsf{T}}\theta,\dots,e_{n-1}\cdot R_{jn}^{\mathsf{T}}\theta),\quad\theta\in U_{2j-1},\end{split}

with
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
The even charts are given by

(46)

\begin{split}U_{2j}&=\{P_{j}R_{jn}\omega:\omega\in U\},\\
\varphi_{2j}(\theta)&=(e_{1}\cdot R_{jn}^{\mathsf{T}}P_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\theta,\dots,e_{n-1}\cdot R_{jn}^{\mathsf{T}}P_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\theta),\quad\theta\in U_{2j},\end{split}

with
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. This choice ensures that

\varphi_{m}(U_{m})=V
for every
m\in\{1,\dots,2n\}
.

These charts satisfy some simple properties that we will refer to in a later analysis. For the odd charts we have that if
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
then

(47)

\zeta\in V\Rightarrow\begin{cases}e_{l}\cdot\varphi_{2j-1}^{-1}(\zeta)=\zeta_{l},&\quad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}\setminus\{j\},\\
e_{n}\cdot\varphi_{2j-1}^{-1}(\zeta)=\zeta_{j}.&\end{cases}

Additionally,

(48)

\zeta\in V\Rightarrow\begin{aligned} &e_{l}\cdot\varphi_{2n-1}^{-1}(\zeta)=\zeta_{l}\qquad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}.\end{aligned}

Moreover,

(49)

\theta\in U_{2j-1}\Rightarrow|e_{j}\cdot\theta|^{2}>1/(2n),

or equivalently,

(50)

\zeta\in{V}\Rightarrow|e_{j}\cdot\varphi_{2j-1}^{-1}(\zeta)|^{2}>1/(2n).

For the even charts we similarly have that if

j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
then

(51)

\zeta\in V\Rightarrow\begin{cases}e_{l}\cdot\varphi_{2j}^{-1}(\zeta)=\zeta_{l},&\quad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}\setminus\{j\},\\
e_{n}\cdot\varphi_{2j}^{-1}(\zeta)=\zeta_{j}.&\end{cases}

In addition,

(52)

\zeta\in V\Rightarrow\begin{aligned} &e_{l}\cdot\varphi_{2n}^{-1}(\zeta)=\zeta_{l}\qquad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}.\end{aligned}

Moreover,

(53)

\theta\in U_{2j}\Rightarrow|e_{j}\cdot\theta|^{2}>1/(2n),

or equivalently,

(54)

\zeta\in{V}\Rightarrow|e_{j}\cdot\varphi_{2j}^{-1}(\zeta)|^{2}>1/(2n).

After having introduced the atlas
\{(U_{1},\varphi_{1}),\dots,(U_{2n},\varphi_{2n})\}
for
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, let us focus on the task of proving the limit in (
I
). By (
43
) and
Proposition

4.2
we have for all
\lambda,t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
s\geq 0
,
that

\begin{split}\lambda^{\alpha}\|[(q_{\alpha,t+s}^{-1}&-q_{\alpha,t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\lambda\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}\\
&=\|(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}\\
&\eqsim\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\|[\chi_{i}(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}]\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}.\end{split}

By (
49
) and (
53
), the function defined in (
12
) is bounded in
U_{1}\cup\dots\cup U_{2k}
, so we bound the first
2k
terms of the previous sum as follows

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{2k}\|[\chi_{i}(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}&-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}]\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}\\
&\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{2k}\|[\chi_{i}(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}]\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}\\
&\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{2k}\|(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{i})}.\end{split}

Now, by (
32
), (
49
) and (
53
) we have that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{2k}\|(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{i})}=0

uniformly for in
s\geq 0
. This corresponds to the first
2k
charts. We turn
now our attention to the last
2(n-k)
charts. From the identities
(
47
), (
48
), (
51
) and (
52
) we
know that if
i\in\{2k+1,\dots,2n\}
then

(q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}})(\varphi_{i}^{-1}(\zeta))=(q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}})(\zeta)\qquad\forall\,\zeta\in\varphi_{i}(U_{i}),

and consequently that

\begin{split}\sum_{i=2k+1}^{2n}\|[\chi_{i}(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}&-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}]\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}\\
=&\sum_{i=2k+1}^{2n}\|\tilde{\chi}_{i}(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}}\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})},\end{split}

where
\tilde{\chi}_{i}(\zeta)=\chi_{i}\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}(\zeta)
for
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Additionally,

\begin{split}\big\|\tilde{\chi}_{i}(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}&-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}}\big\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})}\\
&\leq\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}}\big\|\tilde{\chi}_{i}(\centerdot,\kappa)(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\big\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\bigg)^{1/2}.\end{split}

By (
34
), we know that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{i=2k+1}^{2n}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}}\big\|\tilde{\chi}_{i}(\centerdot,\kappa)(q_{\alpha,\lambda(t+s)}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,\lambda t}^{-1})\big\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\bigg)^{1/2}=0

uniformly in
s\geq 0
. This corresponds to the last
2(n-k)
charts.
Summing up, we have proved that for every
\lambda>0

(55)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda^{\alpha}\big\|[(q_{\alpha,t+s}^{-1}-q_{\alpha,t}^{-1})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\lambda\centerdot)\big\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=0

uniformly in
s\geq 0
, which proves (
I
).

As a consequence of (
55
) in the case
\lambda=1
, we can
derive that (
III
) holds. Indeed, thanks to
(
55
) we have that, for every sequence

\{a_{N}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}

,
the sequence

\{[q_{\alpha,a_{N}}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\centerdot):N\in\mathbb{N}\}

is Cauchy in

. Moreover, given two sequences

\{a_{N}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\{b_{N}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}a_{N}=\lim_{N\to\infty}b_{N}=\infty
, we have that the
sequence
\{c_{N}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
with

c_{2L-1}=a_{L}
and
c_{2L}=b_{L}
for

L\in\mathbb{N}
also satisfies that
\lim_{N\to\infty}c_{N}=\infty
. Therefore,
the sequence

\{[q_{\alpha,c_{N}}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\centerdot):N\in\mathbb{N}\}

is also Cauchy in
H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
. Hence, the Cauchy sequences

\{[q_{\alpha,a_{N}}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\centerdot):N\in\mathbb{N}\}

and

\{[q_{\alpha,b_{N}}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\centerdot):N\in\mathbb{N}\}

belong to the same equivalence class. Consequently, all these Cauchy sequences in
H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
belong to the same equivalence class.
Let
r_{\alpha}
be the equivalence class determined by these Cauchy sequences. Then, for every sequence

\{a_{N}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that
\lim_{N\to\infty}a_{N}=\infty
, we have that

(56)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\big\|[q_{\alpha,a_{N}}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\centerdot)-r_{\alpha}\big\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=0.

Eventually, by (
43
) we have for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\big\|[\lambda^{\alpha}[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\lambda\centerdot)-r_{\alpha}\big\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\big\|[q_{\alpha,\lambda N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\centerdot)-r_{\alpha}\big\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=0.

This proves (
III
).

Finally, we address (
II
). In order to show that it holds, note that the function

t\in(0,\infty)\longmapsto\|[q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}

is continuous and by (
31
) it follows that

\lim_{t\to 0}\|[q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\centerdot)\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=0.

This together with (
56
) imply that there is a constant
C
that only depends on
k
,
n
and
\delta
such that

\sup_{t>0}\big\|[q_{\alpha,t}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\centerdot)\big\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}\leq C.

Again the identity (
43
) implies that

\lambda^{\alpha}\big\|[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}](\lambda\centerdot)\big\|_{H^{-\delta}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}\leq C

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
This concludes the proof of (
II
).

4.4.
The distribution

on

On the one hand,

determines a unique distribution
on

(see

6.3
). On the other hand, distributions
on

are a family of distributions in open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}

associated to local charts (see

). Therefore, in order to
describe the distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
determined by

, we only have
to compute the distributional limit of

\lim_{N\to\infty}[q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}]\circ\varphi_{m}^{-1}

in
\varphi_{m}(U_{m})
, where
\{(U_{1},\varphi_{1}),\dots,(U_{2n},\varphi_{2n})\}

is the atlas introduced in
Section

4.3
.
For convenience, recall that
\varphi_{m}(U_{m})=V
for all

, where

was defined in (

).

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
r_{\alpha,m}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(V)
with
m\in\{1,\dots,2n\}
be defined by

\langle r_{\alpha,m},\phi\rangle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{V}\frac{\phi(\zeta)}{q_{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta,&\quad 1\leq m\leq 2k,\\
\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}},\phi\rangle,&\quad 2k<m\leq 2n,\end{cases}

for all
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(V)
, where

q_{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}(\xi)=q_{\alpha}(e_{1}\cdot\xi,\dots,e_{k}\cdot\xi)

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, the sequence

\{r_{\alpha}^{N}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}):N\in\mathbb{N}\}
given by

r_{\alpha}^{N}(\theta)=(q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}})(\theta),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},

satisfies that

(57)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{V}r_{\alpha}^{N}(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=\langle r_{\alpha,m},\phi\rangle,

for all
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(V)
and
m\in\{1,\dots,2n\}
.

Note that the integral in
V
for

1\leq m\leq 2k
is finite for all
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(V)
since
(
50
) and (
54
) imply

|q_{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))|>0
for all

\zeta\in V
.

Proof.

By definition,

\int_{V}r_{\alpha}^{N}(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=\int_{V}(q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}})(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

From (
32
),
(
49
) and
(
53
) we know that whenever
1\leq m\leq 2k

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\theta\in\overline{U_{m}}}\Big|\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}(\theta)}-(q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}})(\theta)\Big|=0,

and consequently

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{V}r_{\alpha}^{N}(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=\int_{V}\frac{\phi(\zeta)}{q_{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}}(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

This proves (
57
) whenever
1\leq m\leq 2k
.
We focus now on the case
2k<m\leq 2n
. From the identities (
47
),
(
48
), (
51
) and (
52
) we know that

(q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k}})(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))=(q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}})(\zeta)\quad\forall\,\zeta\in V,

and consequently that

\begin{split}\int_{V}r_{\alpha}^{N}(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta&=\int_{V}(q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}})(\zeta)\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}(\eta)\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}}\phi(\eta,\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\eta.\end{split}

By (
33

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}q_{\alpha,N}^{-1}(\eta)&\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}}\phi(\eta,\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\eta=\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}}\phi(\centerdot,\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\rangle\\
&=\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}},\phi\rangle.\end{split}

This means that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{V}r_{\alpha}^{N}(\varphi_{m}^{-1}(\zeta))\phi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=\langle q_{\alpha}^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}},\phi\rangle,

which proves (

) when

.
This completes the proof of

4.4
for every

5.

This section contains two subsections devoted to the proofs of Theorem

and

,
respectively.

5.1.
Loss of derivatives when averaging in
L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))

In this section we prove
2
, which asserts
a loss of at least
s
derivatives for the
k
-plane Riesz potential of order

\alpha
with
s=\alpha-k/2
if
\alpha>k/2
and
s>0
if

\alpha=k/2
. Before providing the explicit expression of the sequence

\{f_{N}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
stated in that theorem, note that by
identities (
11
) and (
14
) with
p_{\alpha}(\xi,Q)=(|\xi\cdot Qe_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|\xi\cdot Qe_{k}|^{2})^{\alpha/2}
we have

\begin{split}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}&\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}I_{\alpha}f_{N}(\centerdot,Q)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{R})}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)\\
&=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\int_{B_{R}}\Big|\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}m_{\alpha}(\xi,Q)\widehat{f_{N}}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)\\
&=\int_{B_{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot(\xi-\eta)}K_{\alpha}(\xi,\eta)\widehat{f_{N}}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{f_{N}}(\eta)}\,\mathrm{d}(\xi,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

where

K_{\alpha}(\xi,\eta)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}m_{\alpha}(\xi,Q)m_{\alpha}(\eta,Q)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q),\qquad(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Since
x
is localized in
B_{R}
, we will localize
\xi-\eta
in a ball
of radius of the order
1/R
so that
e^{ix\cdot(\xi-\eta)}

does not oscillate. Additionally, we want
\widehat{f_{N}}
to be suitably supported on the high frequencies of the order
N
. Thus we choose

\widehat{f_{N}}(\xi)=R^{n/2}\mathbf{1}_{B(Ne_{1};\pi/(8R))}(\xi),\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Here
\mathbf{1}_{B(Ne_{1};\pi/(8R))}(\xi)
denotes the characteristic function of the ball
B(Ne_{1};\pi/(8R))

centred at
Ne_{1}
and having radius
\pi/(8R)
.
The normalization factor
R^{n/2}
easily implies the estimate

\|f_{N}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\eqsim N^{s}
whenever
N>\pi/(4R)
.
With this choice for
f_{N}
we have that

\begin{split}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}&\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}I_{\alpha}f_{N}(\centerdot,Q)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{R})}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)\\
&=R^{n}\int_{B_{R}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}^{R}}\cos(x\cdot(\xi-\eta))K_{\alpha}(\xi,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}(\xi,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

where
\Sigma_{N}^{R}=B(Ne_{1};\pi/(8R))\times B(Ne_{1};\pi/(8R))
. Because
of the localization
x\in B_{R}
and
(\xi,\eta)\in\Sigma_{N}^{R}
, we have
that
\cos(x\cdot(\xi-\eta))\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
and consequently

\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}I_{\alpha}f_{N}(\centerdot,Q)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{R})}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)\gtrsim R^{2n}\int_{\Sigma_{N}^{R}}K_{\alpha}(\xi,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}(\xi,\eta).

By the translations
\zeta=\xi-Ne_{1}
and
\kappa=\eta-Ne_{1}
,
the right-hand side of the previous inequality is equal to

R^{2n}\int_{\Sigma_{0}^{R}}K_{\alpha}(Ne_{1}+\zeta,Ne_{1}+\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}(\zeta,\kappa).

The fact that
\zeta\in B(0;\pi/(8R))
implies that

m_{\alpha}(Ne_{1}+\zeta,Q)\gtrsim\frac{1}{(|e_{1}\cdot Qe_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|e_{1}\cdot Qe_{k}|^{2}+(RN)^{-2})^{\alpha/2}}

whenever
N>\pi/(2R)
. Thus,

\begin{split}R^{2n}\int_{\Sigma_{0}^{R}}&K_{\alpha}(Ne_{1}+\zeta,Ne_{1}+\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}(\zeta,\kappa)\\
&\gtrsim\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\frac{1}{(|e_{1}\cdot Qe_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|e_{1}\cdot Qe_{k}|^{2}+(RN)^{-2})^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)\\
&\eqsim\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\frac{1}{(|\theta\cdot e_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|\theta\cdot e_{k}|^{2}+(RN)^{-2})^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta).\end{split}

In the last step we have used the well known identity for the Haar measure stated in
Proposition

A.3
. Finally, in order to complete the proof of
2
we show that the integral on the sphere has a lower bound of the order
\mathfrak{g}(RN)
whenever
N>\pi/(2R)
.

In the statement of the lemma below we remember that the growth function
\mathfrak{g}
is defined as

\mathfrak{g}(t)=\log t
if
\alpha=k/2
and

\mathfrak{g}(t)=t^{2\alpha-k}
if
\alpha>k/2
.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\frac{1}{(|\theta\cdot e_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|\theta\cdot e_{k}|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)\gtrsim\mathfrak{g}(1/\varepsilon)

whenever
\varepsilon<1
.

Proof.

To prove this lemma we could use two charts consisting on half of the sphere
each one. However, for simplicity we use again the same atlas introduced in (
44
),(
45
) and (
46
).
Let
\{\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{2n}\}
be
a partition of unity subordinated to this atlas such that

\chi_{j}(\varphi_{j}^{-1}(y))\geq c>0
whenever
|y|^{2}\leq(n-1)/n
. By (
44
) this condition can be satisfied for all
y\in V
.
We start by neglecting the charts where the integrand is bounded

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}&\frac{1}{(|\theta\cdot e_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|\theta\cdot e_{k}|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)\\

Finally, changing to spherical coordinates in

Distributions on a smooth manifolds

We begin by recalling the definition of a distribution on a smooth manifold
M

For convenience, if

that belongs to
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)

be a smooth manifold where

Assume that for every chart

\langle u_{\alpha},\phi\rangle=\langle(\varphi_{\beta}\circ\varphi_{\alpha}^{-1})^{\ast}u_{\beta},\phi\rangle\qquad\forall\,\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\varphi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta})),

.
According to
[
5

corresponding to the

in

In the remaining of

For convenience, we first recall briefly the definition of the Banach completion of a normed space
(V,\|\centerdot\|_{V})
. Let
B
denote the
set of all equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences in the normed space obtained by the following relation: Given two Cauchy sequences

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|x_{N}-y_{N}\|_{V}=0.

The vector space structure of

naturally induces a vector structure on

. Furthermore, we can endow
B
with a norm defined as follows: if

denotes the equivalence class of the Cauchy sequence
\{x_{N}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset V
, we define the norm

\|x\|_{B}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\|x_{N}\|_{V}.

Then,
(B,\|\centerdot\|_{B})
is a Banach space and contains a dense subspace isometric to

. The space
(B,\|\centerdot\|_{B})
is called the Banach completion of
(V,\|\centerdot\|_{V})
.

In order to address the last endeavour of
Section

6.1
, let
(M,\mathcal{A})
be a smooth manifold of dimension

. Assume that there exists an atlas
\{(U_{1},\varphi_{1}),\dots,(U_{l},\varphi_{l})\}

\{\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{l}\}
a smooth partition of unity subordinated to this atlas. For convenience, we agree from now on that whenever

to

. Let

denote a vector subspace of

for all

. Given a norm
\|\centerdot\|
on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that convergent sequences with respect to this norm also converge in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, we endow

with the norm defined by

\|u\|_{\mathscr{V}(M)}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\big\|(\chi_{j}u)\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}\big\|,\qquad u\in\mathscr{V}(M).

Let

Lemma 6.1
.

Assume

and
\mathscr{B}(M)
to be as above. Then every

\mathscr{B}(M)
determines a unique distribution in
M
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathscr{B}(M)
be given and consider a Cauchy
sequence
\{u_{N}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset\mathscr{V}(M)
in the equivalence class
denoted by
u
. We will show that
u
determines a unique
distribution in
M
. For every
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\varphi_{j}(U_{j}))
with

j\in\{1,\dots,l\}
the following limit exists

(65)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}u_{N}\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Indeed,

\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}u_{N}\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}(\chi_{i}u_{N})\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

On the one hand if
U_{i}\cap U_{j}=\emptyset
, then

\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}(\chi_{i}u_{N})\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

On the other hand,

, then

\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}(\chi_{i}u_{N})\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{i}\cap U_{j})}(\chi_{i}u_{N})\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Changing variable according to

we have that the right-hand side equals

(66)

\int_{\varphi_{i}(U_{i}\cap U_{j})}(\chi_{i}u_{N})\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}(y)\phi\big(\varphi_{j}\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}(y)\big)\big|\det\big(\nabla\varphi_{j}\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}(y)\big)\big|\,\mathrm{d}y.

Recall that we have assumed that convergent sequences with respect to the norm

\|\centerdot\|
also converge in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Then, since

\{(\chi_{i}u_{N})\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
with
i\in\{1,\dots,l\}
so that
U_{i}\cap U_{j}\neq\emptyset
are Cauchy sequences with
respect to
\|\centerdot\|
, we know that they converge in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Therefore the limits of the integrals (
66
) with
i\in\{1,\dots,l\}
so that
U_{i}\cap U_{j}\neq\emptyset
exist as

. This implies that the limit in (
65
) also exists.

Furthermore, the limit
(
65
) is independent of the Cauchy sequence chosen in the equivalence class denoted by
u
. Namely, if

\{v_{N}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
is another Cauchy sequence in the equivalence class of
u
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}u_{N}\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}v_{N}\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Indeed,

\begin{split}&\bigg|\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}u_{N}\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}v_{N}\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|\\

As we argued earlier, if

, then

\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}(\chi_{i}u_{N}-\chi_{i}v_{N})\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

\begin{split}\int_{\varphi_{j}(U_{j})}&(\chi_{i}u_{N}-\chi_{i}v_{N})\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\\

Changing variable according to

so that

well defined. Then we introduce

for

A convenient description of Sobolev spaces

We start this section by describing an intrinsic notion of Sobolev spaces on a compact boundaryless Riemannian manifold

used to capture the rotational smoothing phenomenon.

6.2.1.
Sobolev spaces on compact boundaryless Riemannian manifolds

Let
(M,g)
as above and denote the Laplace–Beltrami by
\Delta_{g}
as in

Section

4.1
. By Rellich’s theorem, the mapping

L^{2}(M)\ni f\mapsto u\in L^{2}(M)
, satisfying
-\Delta_{g}u=f
in
M
, is
compact. Additionally,
-\Delta_{g}
is self-adjoint and non-negative. By the
spectral theorem there exists a sequence
\{\lambda_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
of non-
negative real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(M)
formed by
eigenfunctions

\{\psi_{j}^{i}:j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,i\in\mathbb{N},\,i\leq d_{j}\}
such that

\lambda_{0}=0
,
\lambda_{j}<\lambda_{j+1}
, and

-\Delta_{g}\psi_{j}^{i}=\lambda_{j}\psi_{j}^{i}
with
\psi_{j}^{i}\in C^{\infty}(M)
for

i\in\{1,\dots,d_{j}\}
and all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Set

H_{j}=\vspan\{\psi_{j}^{i}:i\in\mathbb{N},\,i\leq d_{j}\}
and define, for

u\in L^{1}(M)
, the projector
\Pi_{j}
on
H_{j}
as

\Pi_{j}u(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{j}}\Big(\int_{M}\overline{\psi_{j}^{i}}u\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{g}\Big)\psi_{j}^{i}(p),\qquad p\in M.

Here
\mu_{g}
denotes the Riemannian density. Note that for
u\in C^{\infty}(M)
we
have that
\Pi_{j}[(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})u]=(1+\lambda_{j})\Pi_{j}u
. Consequently,

(68)

\Pi_{j}[(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})\circ\overset{k}{\cdots}\circ(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})u]=(1+\lambda_{j})^{k}\Pi_{j}u\qquad\forall\,u\in C^{\infty}(M).

The fact that
u\in C^{\infty}(M)
implies
(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})\circ\overset{k}{\cdots}\circ(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})u\in L^{2}(M)
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This conclusion together with the identity (
68
) ensures that

(69)

(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})\circ\overset{k}{\cdots}\circ(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})u=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda_{j})^{k}\Pi_{j}u

and for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda_{j})^{2k}\|\Pi_{j}u\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}<\infty.

Thus, we know that

\{\sum_{j=0}^{N}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}u:N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
with

u\in C^{\infty}(M)

is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(M)
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
. The limit of this sequence
defines the Bessel potential of order
s
. This is the definition given in
(
35
) for
u\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that for

s\in\mathbb{R}
and
u\in C^{\infty}(M)
, we have that

(70)

\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u\|_{L^{2}(M)}=\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s}\|\Pi_{j}u\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Furthermore, the non-negative functional

\|\centerdot\|_{H^{s}(M)}:u\in C^{\infty}(M)\longmapsto\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u\|_{L^{2}(M)}

defines a norm. Identity (
70
) gives a simple way of computing
\|u\|_{H^{s}(M)}
whenever
u
belongs to
C^{\infty}(M)
.
Note that the norm for
s=0
coincides with the norm of
{L^{2}(M)}
. With this norm we define the Sobolev space of order
s
in an intrinsic way.

Definition 6.2
.

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
define
H^{s}(M)
as the Banach completion of the normed space

(C^{\infty}(M),\|\centerdot\|_{H^{s}(M)})
.

By continuity, the Bessel potential of order
s
can be defined for every

u\in H^{s}(M)
and

(71)

\|u\|_{H^{s}(M)}=\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u\|_{L^{2}(M)},

holds for all
u\in H^{s}(M)
.

The intrinsic definition of the Sobolev spaces given above has an equivalent
description in terms of local charts, as we have stated in

Proposition

4.2
.
This proposition is a consequence of the results in

[
9
,
11
]
. Let us mention the main idea of the
proof. Consider first the case where
s=2k
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
. As a consequence of the identities (
71
) and
(
69
) and the fact that, in every chart
(U_{j},\varphi_{j})
, the operator

(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}=(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})\circ\overset{k}{\cdots}\circ(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})

is identified with a differential operator of order
2k
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, one sees that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}

(72)

\|u\|_{H^{2k}(M)}\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|(\chi_{j}u)\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}\|_{H^{2k}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}.

The reverse inequality follows from the well-posedness and the regularity theory for the equation
u-\Delta_{g}u=f
in
M
. This is fully detailed in the book
[
10
]
. This is a sketch of the proof in the cases where
s=2k
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For general

one first needs to show that in every chart
(U_{j},\varphi_{j})
, the operator
(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}
is identified with an elliptic pseudodifferential operator of order

with a principal symbol given by
(\sum_{i,k=1}^{m}g^{ik}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{k})^{s/2}
, for
x\in\varphi_{j}(U_{j})
and
|\xi|\gtrsim 1
(see the details in

[
9
,
11
]
). Thus, the generalization of
(
72
) to

follows from the boundedness of
pseudodifferential operators of order
s
. The reverse inequality is a
consequence of the ellipticity and the possibility of constructing the
corresponding parametrix for
(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}
in each chart.
This finishes the sketch of the
proof in the general case
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

We finish the discussion of Sobolev spaces on compact boundaryless Riemannian

are distributions in

.

.

Let

be a compact

Proof.

This follows from

6.1

4.2

6.2.2.
Mixed-norm Sobolev spaces

As previously agreed,
(M,g)
stands for a compact boundaryless
Riemannian manifold of dimension
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall the definition of
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
given in

.
As mentioned then, a function

and all

Note that for

Definition 6.4
.

For

, we define

as the Banach completion
of the normed space
(\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M),\|\centerdot\|_{L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))})

Proposition 6.5
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have that every

u\in L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
determines a unique distribution in

\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M
.

Proof.

This statement is a straightforward conclusion of

Lemma

6.1
. This becomes clear when noting that
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M
is
a smooth manifold that can be covered by an atlas

\{(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times U_{1},\mathrm{id}\otimes\varphi_{1}),\dots,(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times U_{l},\mathrm{id}\otimes\varphi_{l})\}

with a finite number
l\in\mathbb{N}
of charts. Here the charts

(U_{1},\varphi_{1}),\dots,(U_{l},\varphi_{l})
form an atlas for the compact
Riemannian manifold
M
. Then, we choose
\{\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{l}\}
a smooth

and set

\mathscr{V}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)=\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)

where
|g_{j}(y)|
denotes the determinant of the matrix representing the metric
g
in the chart
(U_{j},\varphi_{j})
. The only point left to conclude this proposition from

6.1
is to observe that a sequence in
\mathscr{V}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)=\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
is Cauchy with respect to the norm

if and only if is Cauchy with respect to
\|\centerdot\|_{L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}
.
∎

From the discussion on
Section

6.2
, we know that

(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s}u(x,\centerdot)\in L^{2}(M)
for all

u\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore,
whenever

with

we have

\begin{split}\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s}u(x,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}&\leq\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}u(x,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}\\
&\leq\mu_{g}(M)^{1/2}\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}u(x,\centerdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}\end{split}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The fact that

implies
that the function

x\mapsto\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}u(x,\centerdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}

belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Hence,
(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
for
all
u\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Below, we will see that in fact

(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s}u\in L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and we
will provide a convenient way of computing its norm. Start by noticing that from the identity
(
69
) we have that

(74)

\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda_{j})^{2k}\|\Pi_{j}\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, by the inequality (
73
), we have that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda_{j})^{2k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2t}\|\Pi_{j}\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\leq C.

In particular, from (
70
) we know that the non-negative functional

is well-defined for all

and

.

Lemma 6.6
.

For all
u\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
we have that
(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s}u\in L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Moreover,

\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u\|_{L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2t}\|\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{H^{s}(M)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big)^{1/2}

for all
u\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Proof.

Observe that
\sum_{j=0}^{N}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}u
belongs to

\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
for an arbitrary

u\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
,

N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,

(75)

\Big\{\sum_{j=0}^{N}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}u:N\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}

is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
and

t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Indeed, since
\mathcal{F}(\Pi_{j}u)=\Pi_{j}\widehat{u}
, we have for every

that

(76)

\begin{split}\Big\|\sum_{j=N}^{N+L}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}u\Big\|_{L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}^{2}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2t}\Big\|\sum_{j=N}^{N+L}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\Big\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2t}\sum_{j=N}^{N+L}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s}\|\Pi_{j}\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.\end{split}

From (
74

k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

that

\sum_{j=N}^{N+L}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s}\|\Pi_{l}\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}\leq\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}.

By the fact that
\widehat{u}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
we have that the

\xi\mapsto|\xi|^{t}\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}

belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, by the dominated convergence theorem we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2t}\sum_{j=N}^{N+L}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s}\|\Pi_{j}\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=0

uniformly in
L\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, which implies that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big\|\sum_{j=N}^{N+L}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}u\Big\|_{L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=0,

uniformly in
L\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Consequently the sequence (
75

L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. Therefore, there is
v_{s}\in L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
defined by the Cauchy sequence (
75
). By a standard limiting argument applied to (
76

N=0
and

tending to infinity, we have that

\|v_{s}\|_{L^{2}(M;\dot{H}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{2t}\|\widehat{u}(\xi,\centerdot)\|_{H^{s}(M)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big)^{1/2}.

It remains to prove that
v_{s}=(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u
.

Right before this lemma we saw that

(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
since
u\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
.
Furthermore, recall that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we defined
(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u(x,\centerdot)
as the equivalence class of

\Big\{\sum_{j=0}^{N}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}u(x,\centerdot):N\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}

which is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(M)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence, whenever
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
k>s
and
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have that

\begin{split}\Big\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u(x,\centerdot)&-\sum_{j=0}^{N}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}u(x,\centerdot)\Big\|_{L^{2}(M)}\\
&\leq\Big(\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}(1+\lambda_{j})^{k}\|\Pi_{j}u(x,\centerdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\\
&\leq\mu_{g}(M)^{1/2}\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}u(x,\centerdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}\end{split}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
u\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
, the function
x\mapsto\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{k}u(x,\centerdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}

belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. From here one can derive that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u(x,\centerdot)-\sum_{j=0}^{N}(1+\lambda_{j})^{s/2}\Pi_{j}u(x,\centerdot)\Big\|_{L^{2}(M)}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

At this point we are in the position to ensure that
v_{s}=(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u
. Indeed, on the one hand we know from
Proposition

6.5
that
v_{s}
determines a unique distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M
defined from the Cauchy sequence in (
75

(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M)
. Since
(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u
is in particular a distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M
, we can conclude that
(\mathrm{Id}-\Delta_{g})^{s/2}u
is the distribution determined by
v_{s}
. This concludes the proof of this lemma.
∎

Appendix A
The special orthogonal group

In this appendix we collect some facts about the special orthogonal group

\mathrm{SO}(n)
in order to be able to prove
Proposition

4.1
. Some of the properties stated here are well-known, other not so much.
For the sake of completeness, we have included the proofs of all the results
collected.

Let
\mathrm{M}(n)
denote the vector space of real
n\times n
matrices. The
general linear group

\mathrm{GL}(n)=\{Q\in\mathrm{M}(n):\det\,Q\neq 0\}

is an open subset of
\mathrm{M}(n)
and consequently a smooth manifold of dimension

n^{2}
. Endowed with the matrix multiplication, it becomes a Lie group. The
standard coordinates on
\mathrm{GL}(n)
are denoted by

x^{\alpha}(Q)=e_{\alpha_{1}}\cdot Qe_{\alpha_{2}}
with

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and
\alpha_{j}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Its tangent space at a point
Q\in\mathrm{GL}(n)
is defined as follows

T_{Q}\mathrm{GL}(n)=\vspan\{\partial_{x^{\alpha}}|_{Q}:\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{2}\}.

Every
X=\sum_{\alpha}X^{\alpha}\partial_{x^{\alpha}}
in
T_{Q}GL(n)
can be
identified with a matrix whose components are
X^{\alpha}
with

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
,

\alpha_{j}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\alpha_{1}
enumerating the rows and
\alpha_{2}
the columns.
2
2

2

The
summatory
\sum_{\alpha}
corresponds to the summation of all
indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
with

\alpha_{j}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Thus, we identify
T_{Q}\mathrm{GL}(n)
with

\mathrm{M}(n)
, and we will use the same notation for the corresponding elements of

T_{Q}\mathrm{GL}(n)
and
\mathrm{M}(n)
. The euclidean
metric on
T_{Q}\mathrm{GL}(n)
is defined by

e(X,Y)=\tr(X^{\mathsf{T}}Y),\qquad X,Y\in T_{Q}\mathrm{GL}(n),

where
\tr(X^{\mathsf{T}}Y)
denotes the trace of the matrix
X^{\mathsf{T}}Y
. The
orthogonal group

\mathrm{O}(n)=\{Q\in\mathrm{GL}(n):Q^{\mathsf{T}}Q=\mathrm{id}\}

is the level set
\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{id})
of the function

\Phi:\mathrm{GL}(n)\rightarrow\mathrm{M}(n)
defined by
\Phi(Q)=Q^{\mathsf{T}}Q
. Thus,

\mathrm{O}(n)
is a compact Lie subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(n)
of dimension
n(n-1)/2
. The
special orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}(n)
is the connected component of
\mathrm{O}(n)
,
consisting of matrices with positive determinant. Thus, it is also a compact
manifold of dimension
n(n-1)/2
and becomes a Lie group under matrix
multiplication. The tangent space of
\mathrm{SO}(n)
at
Q
is the vector space

T_{Q}\mathrm{SO}(n)=\{X\in T_{Q}\mathrm{GL}(n):X^{T}Q+Q^{T}X=0\}.

On each tangent space
T_{Q}\mathrm{SO}(n)
we consider the metric

g(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\tr(X^{\mathsf{T}}Y),\qquad X,Y\in T_{Q}\mathrm{SO}(n).

Thus,
(\mathrm{SO}(n),g)
becomes a compact Riemannian manifold.
The metric
g
has some simple invariances that can be described by the
following three bijective mappings: For
P\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
, we define the
left and right translations, as
L_{P}(Q)=PQ
and

R_{P}(Q)=QP
for all
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
. We also define the transposition
as
\mathsf{T}(Q)=Q^{\mathsf{T}}
for all
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
.
Note that if
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
and
X\in T_{Q}\mathrm{SO}(n)
, then
the corresponding push-forwards satisfy:

\displaystyle(L_{P})_{\ast}X\in T_{PQ}\mathrm{SO}(n),

\displaystyle(L_{P})_{\ast}X=PX,

\displaystyle(R_{P})_{\ast}X\in T_{QP}\mathrm{SO}(n),

\displaystyle(R_{P})_{\ast}X=XP,

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{\ast}X\in T_{Q^{\mathsf{T}}}\mathrm{SO}(n),

\displaystyle T_{\ast}X=X^{\mathsf{T}}.

Thus, given
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
and
X,Y\in T_{Q}\mathrm{SO}(n)
we have the following

(77)

\displaystyle[(L_{P})^{\ast}g]_{Q}(X,Y)={\frac{1}{2}}\tr(X^{\mathsf{T}}P^{\mathsf{T}}PY)={\frac{1}{2}}\tr(X^{\mathsf{T}}Y)=g_{Q}(X,Y),

(78)

\displaystyle[(R_{P})^{\ast}g]_{Q}(X,Y)={\frac{1}{2}}\tr(P^{\mathsf{T}}X^{\mathsf{T}}YP)={\frac{1}{2}}\tr(X^{\mathsf{T}}Y)=g_{Q}(X,Y),

(79)

\displaystyle[\mathsf{T}^{\ast}g]_{Q}(X,Y)={\frac{1}{2}}\tr(XY^{\mathsf{T}})={\frac{1}{2}}\tr(X^{\mathsf{T}}Y)=g_{Q}(X,Y),

since

is left and right invariant, that
is bi-invariant, and transposition invariant.

In the following lines we will construct the normalized

using the language of Riemannian geometry.
Since we are considering a very particular case, it is worth presenting a

E_{jk}=\partial_{x^{jk}}-\partial_{x^{kj}}

with

for

3
3

3

Note that every

Choose an orientation for

Lemma A.1
.

We have that
the Haar volume form is left and right invariant —bi-invariant— and transposition invariant:

(81)

\displaystyle(L_{P})^{\ast}\nu=\nu\qquad\forall\,P\in\mathrm{SO}(n),

(82)

\displaystyle(R_{P})^{\ast}\nu=\nu\qquad\forall\,P\in\mathrm{SO}(n),

(83)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}^{\ast}\nu=\nu.

These identities follow from the invariances of the metric on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
.

M
and
N

g
is a Riemannian
metric on
N
, and

is a smooth immersion, then the
pull-back
F^{\ast}g
is a Riemannian metric on
M
.
Furthermore, by the definition of Riemannian volume form
we have that

\mathrm{d}V_{F^{\ast}g}=F^{\ast}\mathrm{d}V_{g}.

Since
L_{P}
,
R_{P}
and
\mathsf{T}
are smooth immersions, the volume forms generated

(L_{P})^{\ast}g

(R_{P})^{\ast}g

are
(L_{P})^{\ast}\nu
,

(R_{P})^{\ast}\nu
and

), (
78
) and
(
79
) for the metric

), (
82
) and (

) for
\nu
.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

If
f\in L^{1}(\mathrm{SO}(n))
, then

(84)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(PQ)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)&=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(Q)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)\quad\forall\,P\in\mathrm{SO}(n),\\
\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(QP)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)&=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(Q)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)\quad\forall\,P\in\mathrm{SO}(n),\\
\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(Q^{\mathsf{T}})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)&=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(Q)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q).\end{split}

Proof.

These identities are immediately seen to hold from

Lemma

A.1
and the definition of
\mu

for functions

. The general case follows by density.
∎

The next invariance shows a relation between
\mu
and the hypersurface
measure on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

Proposition A.3
.

Let
F:\mathrm{SO}(n)\to\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be given either by
F(Q)=Qe_{1}
or by
F(Q)=Q^{\mathsf{T}}e_{1}
. If
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
then

\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(F(Q))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)=\frac{1}{S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta),

where
S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

A.2
it is enough to verify the case of
F(Q)=Qe_{1}
.
Consider the smooth map
F(Q)=Qe_{1}
. Let
\mu_{F}
denote the image measure
of
\mu
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, that is, given a Borel

\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
\mu_{F}(\Sigma)=\mu(F^{-1}(\Sigma))
.
The identity

\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(Qe_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Q)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{F}(\theta)

holds trivially for simple functions, and can be extended by density. It
remains to prove that
\mu_{F}(\Sigma)=S(\Sigma)/S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
for
every Borel
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. From the identity
(
84
) in

Proposition

A.2
, one can derive that
if
\Sigma
is a Borel set of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
then

\mu_{F}(P\Sigma)=\mu_{F}(\Sigma)
for every
P\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
. The same holds
for
S(P\Sigma)=S(\Sigma)
. This common invariance means
that
\mu_{F}
and

S
are both uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Therefore, there exists a

c>0
such that
\mu_{F}(\Sigma)=cS(\Sigma)
for all Borel sets

\Sigma
(see for example
[
6
, Theorem 3.4]
).
By testing the identity in

one gets

and this completes
the proof of this proposition.
∎

The next step is to transfer these invariances to Sobolev spaces. To do so

Lemma A.4
.

Assume

(85)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}(L_{P})^{\ast}f(Q)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(PQ)\qquad\forall\,P,Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n),

(86)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}(R_{P})^{\ast}f(Q)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(QP)\qquad\forall\,P,Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n),

(87)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\mathsf{T}^{\ast}f(Q)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}f(Q^{\mathsf{T}})\qquad\forall\,Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n).

These identities follow again from the invariances of the metric on

\mathrm{SO}(n)
. As in the proof of
Lemma

A.1
, if
M
and
N
are manifolds,

g
is a Riemannian metric on
N
, and
F:M\rightarrow N
is a smooth
immersion, then the pull-back
F^{\ast}g
is a Riemannian metric on
M
, and
the canonical Laplace–Beltrami operators
\Delta_{F^{\ast}g}
on

and

\Delta_{g}
on

\Delta_{F^{\ast}g}f\circ F(p)=\Delta_{g}f(F(p))\qquad\forall\,p\in M,\,f\in C^{\infty}(N).

Since
L_{P}
,
R_{P}
and
\mathsf{T}
are smooth immersions,
the invariances (
77
), (
78
) and
(
79
) for the metric

imply the invariances
(
85
), (
86
) and (
87
)
respectively.
∎

Proposition A.5
.

Let
f
belong to
H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))
with
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then,

\begin{split}\|(L_{P})^{\ast}f\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}&=\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}\qquad\forall\,P\in\mathrm{SO}(n),\\
\|(R_{P})^{\ast}f\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}&=\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}\qquad\forall\,P\in\mathrm{SO}(n),\\
\|\mathsf{T}^{\ast}f\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}&=\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}.\end{split}

Proof.

Assume that
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathrm{SO}(n))
. The general case follows by density.
Since the three identities follow in the same way, let
F
denote any of the
mappings
L_{P}
,
R_{P}
and
\mathsf{T}
. Recall that

\|F^{\ast}f\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda_{l})^{s}\|\Pi_{l}F^{\ast}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}^{2}

with

\|\Pi_{l}F^{\ast}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{d_{l}}\Big|\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\overline{\psi_{l}^{m}}F^{\ast}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\Big|^{2}.

By
Proposition

A.2
we have

(88)

\|\Pi_{l}F^{\ast}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{d_{l}}\Big|\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\overline{(F^{-1})^{\ast}\psi_{l}^{m}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\Big|^{2}.

Then,
Lemma

A.4
implies that
(F^{-1})^{\ast}\psi_{l}^{m}
is in
an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{l}
and belongs to
the eigenspace
H_{l}
, while

Proposition

A.2
ensures that

\{(F^{-1})^{\ast}\psi_{l}^{m}:m=1,\dots,d_{l}\}
is an orthonormal basis
of
H_{l}
. Thus, there exists a unitary matrix
A\in\mathrm{U}(d_{l})
with entries

A_{jk}
such that

(F^{-1})^{\ast}\psi_{l}^{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{l}}A_{mj}\psi_{l}^{j}.

Therefore, since
A
is unitary

\begin{split}\sum_{m=1}^{d_{l}}\Big|\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\overline{(F^{-1})^{\ast}\psi_{l}^{m}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\Big|^{2}&=\sum_{m=1}^{d_{l}}\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{d_{l}}\overline{A_{mj}}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\overline{\psi_{l}^{j}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\Big|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{l}}\Big|\int_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}\overline{\psi_{l}^{j}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\Big|^{2}=\|\Pi_{l}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}^{2}\end{split}

By the last chain of equalities together with (
88
) we have
that

\|\Pi_{l}F^{\ast}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}=\|\Pi_{l}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))},

and consequently that

\|F^{\ast}f\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}=\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}.

This proves the proposition.
∎

Lemma A.6

Let

\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}[f\circ F](Q)=\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(Qe_{1})\qquad\forall\,Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n).

This lemma can be deduced from the rather abstract

[

.
However, for the sake of completeness, we provide here a full proof in the
specific case of
\mathrm{SO}(n)
and
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

Proof.

Start by observing that it is enough to prove the identity in the statement for

Q=\mathrm{id}
since then (
85
) and the invariance under rotations of

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
imply the general result.
Then, we only need to show that

\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}[f\circ F](\mathrm{id})=\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(e_{1}),

In order to prove this identity we will use a convenient way of
writing the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{g}
on a Riemannian manifold
M
of
dimension
m
. Assume
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
p\in M
; if

\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\}
is a local frame around the point
p
and

\{X_{1}|_{p},\dots,X_{m}|_{p}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
T_{p}M
, then

(89)

\Delta_{g}f(p)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big(X_{j}(X_{j}f)-(\nabla_{X_{j}}X_{j})f\big)(p),

where
\nabla
denotes the covariant derivative on
M
.

In order to write the Laplace–Beltrami on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, we
define the vector field
X_{\alpha}
given, at every point

, by

X_{\alpha}|_{Q}=QE_{\alpha}\in T_{Q}\mathrm{SO}(n)
with
E_{\alpha}
as in
(
80
) and
\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}
. Since

\{E_{\alpha}:1\leq\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}\leq n\}
is an orthonormal basis
of

, we have that

\{X_{\alpha}:1\leq\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}\leq n\}
is
an orthonormal global frame. Hence, for every

, we have that

\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}[f\circ F](Q)=\sum_{\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}}\big(X_{\alpha}(X_{\alpha}[f\circ F])-(\nabla_{X_{\alpha}}X_{\alpha})[f\circ F]\big)(Q).

Since the
X_{\alpha}
are left invariant vector fields and the metric is bi-
invariant it follows that

\sum_{\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}}\nabla_{X_{\alpha}}X_{\alpha}=0
—see for example

[
4
, Proposition 4.2]
for a proof. Then,

(90)

\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}[f\circ F](Q)=\sum_{\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}}X_{\alpha}(X_{\alpha}[f\circ F])(Q).

We will use this formula only for
Q=\mathrm{id}
. In order to compute the right-hand
side of (
90
),
we introduce curves
\gamma_{\alpha}:(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)

with
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and
1\leq\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}\leq n
such that
\gamma_{\alpha}(0)=\mathrm{id}
and

\frac{d\phantom{t}}{dt}\gamma_{\alpha}|_{t=0}=E_{\alpha}=X_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{id}}
.
We define the curves
\gamma_{\alpha}
by giving the explicit matrix components:

\{\gamma_{\alpha}(t)\}_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}}=\begin{cases}\cos(t),\;\phantom{-0i}\text{if}\quad\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\alpha_{1}\text{ or }\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\alpha_{2},\\
\sin(t),\;\phantom{-0o}\text{if}\quad\beta_{1}=\alpha_{1},\beta_{2}=\alpha_{2},\\
-\sin(t),\;\phantom{o0}\text{if}\quad\beta_{1}=\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}=\alpha_{1},\\
1,\;\phantom{-o\sin(t)}\text{if}\quad\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}\text{ and }\beta_{1}\neq\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\\
0,\;\phantom{-o\sin(t)}\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Notice that the element
\gamma_{\alpha}(t)\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
is just a rotation of
t
radians in the
2
dimensional plane determined by
e_{\alpha_{1}}
and
e_{\alpha_{2}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that fixes the rest of the basis vectors:
\gamma_{\alpha}(t)e_{k}=e_{k}
if
k\neq\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
.
One can also verify that
X_{\alpha}|_{\gamma_{\alpha}(t)}=\frac{d\phantom{t}}{dt}\gamma_{\alpha}(t)
, so that
X_{\alpha}|_{\gamma(t)}\,g=\frac{d\phantom{t}}{dt}g(\gamma_{\alpha}(t))

for any smooth
g
defined around
\gamma(t)
. Also, since
f\circ F(\gamma_{\alpha}(t))=f(\gamma_{\alpha}(t)e_{1})
we have that
\gamma_{\alpha}(t)e_{1}=e_{1}
for all
t\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
and
\alpha
with
\alpha_{1}>1
. Using this in (
90
) yields

(91)

\begin{split}\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}[f\circ F](\mathrm{id})&=\sum_{\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}}\left.\frac{d^{2}\phantom{t}}{dt^{2}}\big[f\circ F(\gamma_{\alpha}(t))\big]\right|_{t=0}\\
&=\sum_{\alpha_{2}=2}^{n}\left.\frac{d^{2}\phantom{t}}{dt^{2}}f\left(\gamma_{(1,\alpha_{2})}(t)e_{1}\right)\right|_{t=0}.\end{split}

Now, we use (
89
) for the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
We start by noting that every point in a neighbourhood of
e_{1}
can be written
as
\gamma_{(1,2)}(t_{2})\dots\gamma_{(1,n)}(t_{n})e_{1}
with

(t_{2},\dots,t_{n})\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)^{n-1}

define the local frame
\{Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n}\}
given by the vector fields

Y_{j}|_{\gamma_{(1,2)}(t_{2})\dots\gamma_{(1,n)}(t_{n})e_{1}}=\gamma_{(1,2)}(t_{2})\dots\gamma_{(1,n)}(t_{n})e_{j}.

Note that
\{Y_{2}|_{e_{1}},\dots,Y_{n}|_{e_{1}}\}

\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}:(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\to\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}(t)=\gamma_{(1,j)}(t)e_{1}=(\cos t)e_{1}{-}(\sin t)e_{j}.

Thus,

Y_{j}|_{\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}(t)}=Y_{j}|_{\gamma_{(1,j)}(t)e_{1}}=\gamma_{(1,j)}({t})e_{j}=(\sin t)e_{1}+(\cos t)e_{j},

and consequently,

(92)

Y_{j}|_{\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}(t)}={-}\frac{d\phantom{t}}{dt}\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}(t),\quad 2\leq j\leq n.

By the identity (

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(e_{1})=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\big(Y_{j}(Y_{j}f)-(\nabla_{Y_{j}}Y_{j})f\big)(e_{1}).

Then, the identity (

are maximal circles (i.e. geodesics), implies

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(e_{1}){=\sum_{j=2}^{n}Y_{j}(0)[Y_{j}(t)f(\tilde{\gamma}(t))]\Big|_{t=0}}=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{d^{2}\phantom{t}}{dt^{2}}f\left(\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}(t)\right)\Big|_{t=0}.

Then, returning to (

\begin{split}\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}[f\circ F](\mathrm{id})&=\sum_{\alpha_{2}=2}^{n}\frac{d^{2}\phantom{t}}{dt^{2}}f\left(\gamma_{(1,\alpha_{2})}(t)e_{1}\right)\Big|_{t=0}\\
&=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{d^{2}\phantom{t}}{dt^{2}}f\left(\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}(t)\right)\Big|_{t=0}=\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(e_{1}).\end{split}

This yields the desired result.

A.3
implies that

\|\Pi^{\prime}_{k}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}=S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\|F^{\ast}\Pi^{\prime}_{k}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}=S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\|\Pi_{l}f\circ F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}

with
F^{\ast}\Pi^{\prime}_{k}f(Q)=\Pi^{\prime}_{k}f(Qe_{1})
.
Furthermore, if
\lambda_{l}\notin\{\lambda^{\prime}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
then
\Pi_{l}f\circ F(Q)=0
for all
Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
.
Thus, we can conclude that

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda_{l})^{s}\|\Pi_{l}f\circ F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathrm{SO}(n))}=S(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(1+\lambda^{\prime}_{k})^{s}\|\Pi^{\prime}_{k}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}.

This completes the proof of the proposition.
