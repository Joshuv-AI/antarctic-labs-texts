Decoupling for complex curves and improved decoupling for the cubic moment curve
Robert Schippa

Abstract
. We prove sharp ℓ 2 \ell^{2} -decoupling inequalities for non-degenerate complex curves via the bilinear argument due to Guo–Li–Yung–Zorin-Kranich. Secondly, quantifying the iteration in the cubic case, we obtain a logarithmic refinement of the decoupling inequality for the cubic moment curve.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 35B45, 35Q55, Secondary: 42B37.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Decoupling inequalities for non-degenerate complex curves

In the following we prove decoupling inequalities for non-degenerate complex curves. An example is the complex moment curve:

\mathbb{C}\ni z\mapsto\gamma(z)=(z,\frac{z^{2}}{2},\frac{z^{3}}{3!},\ldots,\frac{z^{k}}{k!})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{2k}.

By non-degeneracy we mean that the “complex torsion”

|\partial_{z}\gamma\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\partial^{2}_{z}\gamma\ldots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\partial_{z}^{k}\gamma|\gtrsim 1

is uniformly bounded from below. By
\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}
we denote the complex (
\mathbb{C}
-linear) wedge product in the exterior algebra

\Lambda(\mathbb{C}^{k})=\big\langle e_{i_{1}}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\ldots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}e_{i_{m}}\,:\,1\leq m\leq k,\;1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{m}\leq k\big\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}.

\gamma(z)\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
is translated to an element of
\mathbb{R}^{2k}
by separating real and imaginary part of the complex components. We define
F:\mathbb{C}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{2k}
via

F(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=(\Re z_{1},\Im z_{1},\Re z_{2},\Im z_{2},\ldots,\Re z_{k},\Im z_{k}).

Write
z=s+it
,
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
with
i^{2}=-1
, and we find

F(z,\frac{z^{2}}{2},\frac{z^{3}}{3!},\ldots)=(s,t,\frac{s^{2}-t^{2}}{2},st,\frac{s^{3}-3st^{2}}{3!},\frac{3s^{2}t-t^{3}}{3!},\ldots).

We shall formulate the results entirely in Euclidean space, but to take advantage of the complex structure carry out several computations using the complex notations.

Recall the canonical (anisotropic) neighbourhood for the real moment curve
\gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s)=(s,\frac{s^{2}}{2},\ldots,\frac{s^{k}}{k!})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. For an interval
I_{\delta}\subseteq[0,1]
of length
2\delta
with center
c_{I_{\delta}}
we associate the anisotropic neighbourhood:

\theta_{I_{\delta},\mathbb{R}}=\{\gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(c_{I_{\delta}})+a_{1}\partial_{s}\gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(c_{I_{\delta}})+\ldots+a_{n}\partial_{s}^{n}\gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(c_{I_{\delta}})\;:|a_{i}|\leq 4\delta^{i}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n\}.

A function
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
with
\text{supp}(\hat{f})\subseteq\theta_{I_{\delta},\mathbb{R}}
is essentially constant on dual anisotropic rectangles of size
\delta^{-1}\times\ldots\times\delta^{-k}
.
Let
g:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
be supported in an interval of length
\delta
.
Fourier extension applied to
g

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma_{\mathbb{R}}}g(x)=\int_{[0,1]}e^{ix\cdot\gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s)}g(s)ds

has on balls of size
\delta^{-n}
the properties of
f
. Let
\mathcal{I}_{\delta}
denote an essentially disjoint covering of
[0,1]
with
\delta
intervals. For
I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}
with center
c_{I}
we assign the anisotropic neighbourhood
\theta_{I,\mathbb{R}}
from above. By
f_{\theta}
we denote the Fourier projection of
f
to
\theta
.

:

Theorem 1.1

(
\ell^{2}
-decoupling for curves with torsion)
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
\gamma_{\mathbb{R}}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
denote the real moment curve.
Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
with
\text{supp}(\hat{f})\subseteq\bigcup_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}\theta_{I,\mathbb{R}}
. Then the following estimate holds:

(1)

\|f\|_{L^{p_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\big(\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}\|f_{{\theta}_{I,\mathbb{R}}}\|_{L^{p_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{k})}^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In this note we prove the extension of the above theorem to non-degenerate complex curves. To formulate the result, we need to consider the anisotropic neighbourhoods of a complex non-degenerate curve. Let
Q_{1}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0\leq\Re z,\Im z\leq 1\}
.

We make the following definition:

Definition 1.2
.

A complex curve
\gamma:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is called non-degenerate (or complex curve with torsion) if it is analytic in
Q_{1}
and if there is
c_{\gamma}>0
such that

|\partial_{z}\gamma(z)\wedge\partial^{2}_{z}\gamma(z)\wedge\ldots\wedge\partial_{z}^{n}\gamma(z)|\geq c_{\gamma}>0\;\forall z\in Q_{1}.

For future reference we define the constant:

C_{\gamma}=\sup_{1\leq m\leq 2(k+1)}\|\partial^{m}_{z}\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}.

We already remark that by a Pramanik–Seeger argument we can always normalize the complex curves, making them “close” in a quantitative sense to the complex moment curve.

The canonical (anisotropic) neighbourhood of the image of
((s,t)\mapsto F(\gamma(s+it))\in\mathbb{R}^{2k})
is determined by the almost constant property of
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}f(\underline{x})
,
f\in L^{1}([0,1]^{2})
, where

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}f(\underline{x})=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}e^{i\underline{x}\cdot F(\gamma(s+it))}f(s,t)dsdt.

For a square
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
we denote the side length by
\ell(Q)
.
Let
Q_{\delta}\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
be a square in
[0,1]^{2}
of length
\delta
. We carry out a (complex) Taylor expansion of
\gamma(z)
,
z\in Q_{\delta}
, around the center of
Q_{\delta}
given by
c_{z}=s+it
:

\gamma(z)=\gamma(c_{z})+\partial_{z}\gamma(c_{z})\cdot(z-c_{z})+\partial^{2}_{z}\gamma(c_{z})\frac{(z-c_{z})^{2}}{2}+\ldots.

Separate
(z-c_{z})^{k}=z^{(k)}_{\Re}+iz^{(k)}_{\Im}
. We have for the complex moment curve

\partial_{z}\gamma(c_{z})=(1,c_{z},c_{z}^{2}/2,\ldots)\in\mathbb{C}^{k},

and writing the vector as element of
\mathbb{R}^{2k}
real and imaginary part yields

F(\partial_{z}\gamma(c_{z}))=(1,0,s,t,\frac{s^{2}-t^{2}}{2},st,\ldots)=:\Gamma^{1}_{1}(c_{z}).

Secondly,

F(i\partial_{z}\gamma(c_{z}))=(0,1,-t,s,-st,\frac{s^{2}-t^{2}}{2},\ldots)=:\Gamma^{1}_{2}(c_{z}).

In general, for
1\leq m\leq k
we let

F(\partial^{m}_{z}\gamma(c_{z}))=\Gamma^{m}_{1}(c_{z},\gamma),\quad F(i\partial^{m}_{z}\gamma(c_{z}))=\Gamma^{m}_{2}(c_{z},\gamma).

Since
|z^{(m)}_{\Re}|
,
|z^{(m)}_{\Im}|\lesssim\delta^{m}
, we find that
|\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}f|
is essentially constant on translates of dimensions
\sim\delta^{-1}\times\delta^{-1}\times\delta^{-2}\times\delta^{-2}\times\ldots
in directions
\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma^{j}_{1}(c_{z},\gamma)\times\Gamma^{j}_{2}(c_{z},\gamma)
.

We formulate now the decoupling inequality for complex curves with torsion. Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}=(Q_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{\delta}}
be an essentially disjoint partition of
[0,1]^{2}
into squares with side length
\delta
and with centers aligned on a grid. For
c_{Q_{J}}
denoting the center of
Q_{J}
let

\begin{split}\theta_{Q_{J},\gamma}&=\{\gamma(c_{Q_{J}})+a_{11}\Gamma^{1}_{1}(c_{Q_{J}},\gamma)+a_{12}\Gamma^{1}_{2}(c_{Q_{J}},\gamma)+a_{21}\Gamma^{2}_{1}(c_{Q_{J}},\gamma)\\
&\quad+a_{22}\Gamma^{2}_{2}(c_{Q_{J}},\gamma)+\ldots:\,|a_{ij}|\leq 4\delta^{i}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,k\text{ and }j=1,2\}.\end{split}

We denote the collection of
\theta_{Q_{J},\gamma}
with
\Theta_{\delta,\gamma}
.
\gamma
shall frequently be omitted to lighten the notation.

Theorem 1.3

(
\ell^{2}
-decoupling for non-degenerate complex curves)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{k}
be a non-degenerate complex curve and
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2k})
with
\text{supp}(\hat{f})\subseteq\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}}\theta_{Q}
. The following estimate holds:

(2)

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2k})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon,c_{\gamma},C_{\gamma}}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\big(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}}\|f_{\theta_{Q}}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2k})}^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

with
p_{k}=k(k+1)
.

For
k=1
(
1
) and (
2
) is immediate from Plancherel’s theorem. For
k=2
, (
1
) is a special case of the decoupling inequalities for elliptic surfaces proved by Bourgain–Demeter
[
2
]
. As a consequence of (
1
), we obtain Vinogradov’s Mean-Value Theorem on the numbers of solutions to a system of diophantine inequalities. This was proved in the cubic case already by Wooley
[
13
,
14
]
via efficient congruencing.

Combining the arguments of Bourgain–Demeter–Guth and Wooley, Guo–Li–Yung–Zorin-Kranich
[
7
]
found a concise proof of the
\ell^{2}
-decoupling inequality for the moment curve at the endpoint
p_{k}=k(k+1)
in all dimensions. This bilinear decoupling is based on induction on dimension and transversality and will be used presently in the complex case. We sketch the key points of the argument in Section
2
. Once we have established the complex analog of transversality at all lower dimensions, the decoupling iteration due to Guo–Li–Yung–Zorin-Kranich remains unchanged.

1.2.
Applications

We record a consequence of Theorem
1.3
on the number of solutions to systems of diophantine equations. The
\ell^{2}L^{p_{k}}
-decoupling inequality from
[
4
]
resolved Vinogradov’s Mean-Value Theorem on the number of solutions to the diophantine equations. Denote
2s=p_{k}
and consider
x_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
for
i=1,\ldots,2s
. The system of diophantine equations is given by:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}x_{1}+\ldots+x_{s}&=x_{s+1}+\ldots+x_{2s},\\
x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{s}^{2}&=x_{s+1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{2s}^{2},\\
&\vdots\\
x_{1}^{n}+\ldots+x_{s}^{n}&=x_{n+1}^{n}+\ldots+x_{2s}^{n}.\end{array}\right.

Note that a lower bound on the number of solutions is given by
C_{s}N^{s}
, which is obtained from choosing
\{x_{s+1},\ldots,x_{2s}\}
as a permutation of
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}\}
. Invoking decoupling yields that the number of solution is bounded by
C_{\varepsilon}N^{s+\varepsilon}
. To formulate the corresponding results in the complex case, we recall that

F(\gamma(s+it))=(s,t,\frac{s^{2}-t^{2}}{2},st,\frac{s(s^{2}-3t^{2})}{3!},\frac{t(3s^{2}-t^{2})}{3!},\ldots).

The system of diophantine equations associated with the complex moment curve reads

(3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}k_{1}+\ldots+k_{s}&=k_{s+1}+\ldots+k_{2s},\\
j_{1}+\ldots+j_{s}&=j_{s+1}+\ldots+j_{2s},\\
k_{1}^{2}-j_{1}^{2}+\ldots+k_{s}^{2}-j_{s}^{2}&=k_{s+1}^{2}-j_{s+1}^{2}+\ldots+k_{2s}^{2}-j_{2s}^{2},\\
k_{1}j_{1}+\ldots+k_{s}j_{s}&=k_{s+1}j_{s+1}+\ldots+k_{2s}j_{2s},\\
\sum_{m=1}^{s}(F(s_{m}+it_{m}))_{l}&=\sum_{m=s+1}^{2s}(F(s_{m}+it_{m}))_{l}\text{ for }l=5,\ldots,2s.\end{array}\right.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
p_{k}=k(k+1)
,
2s=p_{k}
. Then the system of equations (
3
) with integers
1\leq k_{1},\ldots,k_{2s}\leq N
and
1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{2s}\leq N
has
\lesssim_{\varepsilon}N^{2s+\varepsilon}
solutions.

We note that the components of
F(\gamma(z))
are in general not elliptic: We have e.g. the indefinite components

F(\gamma(s+it))_{3}=s^{2}-t^{2},\quad F(\gamma(s+it))_{4}=st.

Nonetheless, the
\ell^{2}L^{6}
-decoupling holds true, and we have the essentially sharp bound for diophantine solutions to the system of equations:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}&=k_{4}+k_{5}+k_{6},\\
j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}&=j_{4}+j_{5}+j_{6},\\
k_{1}^{2}-j_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}-j_{2}^{2}+k_{3}^{2}-j_{3}^{2}&=k_{4}^{2}-j_{4}^{2}+k_{5}^{2}-j_{5}^{2}+k_{6}^{2}-j_{6}^{2},\\
k_{1}j_{1}+k_{2}j_{2}+k_{3}j_{3}&=k_{4}j_{4}+k_{5}j_{5}+k_{6}j_{6}.\end{array}\right.

Recall that sharply divergent from the present results, for the hyperbolic paraboloid
f(s,t)=s^{2}-t^{2}
the
\ell^{2}L^{4}
-estimate for the elliptic paraboloid fails dramatically
[
3
]
since the surface contains a straight line (see also the recent
\ell^{2}
-decoupling result due Guth–Maldague–Oh
[
8
]
adjusted to “flat” sets). In the present situation the complex torsion reflects a transversality, which salvages the estimates from the elliptic case.

1.3.
Logarithmic refinement in the cubic case

We take the opportunity to quantify the bilinear decoupling iteration due to Guo–Li–Yung–Zorin-Kranich
[
7
]
in the cubic case, which yields a logarithmic refinement of Vinogradov’s Mean-Value Theorem. In the following we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{k,\mathbb{R}}(\delta)
the decoupling constant for the real moment curve in
k
(real) dimensions.

Theorem 1.5

(Improved decoupling for the moment curve in three dimensions)
.

There is
0<\delta_{0}<1
and
C>0
such that for
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
, we have the following bound for the decoupling constant of the real moment curve in three dimensions:

(4)

\mathcal{D}_{3,\mathbb{R}}(\delta)\leq\exp(C\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{\log(\log(\delta^{-1}))}).

Bourgain–Demeter
[
2
]
proved that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\mathcal{D}_{2,\mathbb{R}}(\delta)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}.

Li
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
(see also
[
10
]
) observed how the double exponential bound

(5)

\mathcal{D}_{2,\mathbb{R}}(\delta)\leq A^{A^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}}\delta^{-\varepsilon}

allows one to sharpen the decoupling constant to

\mathcal{D}_{2,\mathbb{R}}(\delta)\leq\exp(C\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{\log(\log\delta^{-1})}).

Li
[
11
]
proved (
5
) via a bilinear approach motivated by efficient congruencing. More recently, Guth–Maldague–Wang
[
9
]
improved (
5
) to

(6)

\mathcal{D}_{2,\mathbb{R}}(\delta)\leq\log(\delta^{-1})^{c}

for some (possibly large) constant
c
. It is conjectured that
c=\frac{1}{6}
is sharp due to a Gauss sum example given by Bourgain
[
1
]
. Subsequently, Guo–Li–Yung
[
6
]
improved the discrete restriction constant to
C_{\varepsilon}\log(\delta^{-1})^{2+\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon>0
. The approach in
[
9
]
differs from
[
11
]
as it uses a high-low decomposition. However, the high-low method becomes more involved in higher dimensions, whereas the bilinear approach as carried out in higher dimensions by Guo–Li–Yung–Zorin-Kranich
[
7
]
(see also
[
5
]
) seems more tractable.

Here we turn to the moment curve in three dimensions.
The proof of Theorem
1.5
relies on quantifying the bilinear iteration from
[
7
]
for the moment curve in three dimensions and use improved decoupling inequalities without
\delta^{-\varepsilon}
-loss in lower dimensions. We will see how losing additional logarithmic factors in the bilinear approach still allows us to show an estimate like in (
5
), which implies (
4
) after optimizing in
\varepsilon
. The optimization argument was carried out in
[
11
]
without additional logarithms in the context of the parabola.

Outline of the paper.
In Section
2
we give an overview of the proof of Theorem
1.3
. Section
3
is devoted to making the sketch rigid except from the key bilinear decoupling inequality.
This is proved in Section
4

1.3
.
Finally, in Section

Preliminaries and Outline of the proof of Theorem

When complexifying the argument from

2.1.
Real and complex notations

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
F:\mathbb{C}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{2k}
denote the
\mathbb{R}
-linear mapping

F(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=(\Re z_{1},\Im z_{1},\Re z_{2},\Im z_{2},\ldots,\Re z_{k},\Im z_{k}).

Note that

F(i(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}))=(-\Im z_{1},\Re z_{1},-\Im z_{2},\Re z_{2},\ldots,-\Im z_{k},\Re z_{k}).

By
\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}:\Lambda(\mathbb{C}^{k})\times\Lambda(\mathbb{C}^{k})\to\Lambda(\mathbb{C}^{k})
we denote the wedge product in the exterior algebra over
\mathbb{C}^{k}
. By
\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}:\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{2k})\times\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{2k})\to\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{2k})
we denote the
\mathbb{R}
-linear wedge product. For a complex non-degenerate curve
\gamma:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{k}
we denote the “realized” higher-order tangent vectors by

F(\partial_{z}^{m}\gamma(z))=\Gamma_{1}^{m}(z,\gamma),\quad F(i\partial_{z}^{m}\gamma(z))=\Gamma^{m}_{2}(z,\gamma)\text{ for }m=1,\ldots,k.

2.2.
Normalized non-degnerate curves

The induction will be carried out over the following family of curves:

Definition 2.1

(Normalized non-degenerate curve)
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{k}
be an analytic curve in
Q_{1}
.
\gamma
is referred to as non-degenerate normalized curve if

\frac{1}{4}\leq|\partial_{z}\gamma(z)\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\ldots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\partial_{z}^{k}\gamma(z)|\leq 4,

and

|\partial^{j}_{z}\gamma(z)|\leq 2k\text{ for }1\leq j\leq k\text{ and }|\partial_{z}^{k+1}\gamma(z)|\leq\frac{2^{-10k}}{(2k)^{k}k!}.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{k}
be a non-degenerate complex curve. By finitely many Taylor expansions in
Q_{1}
and affine transformations, we can reduce the decoupling to normalized non-degenerate curves. Indeed, an application of Taylor’s theorem in a cube centered at
z_{0}
with side length
\delta
yields

\gamma(z)=\gamma(z_{0})+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\partial_{z}^{m}\gamma(z_{0})\frac{(z-z_{0})^{m}}{m!}+\partial_{z}^{k+1}\gamma(\bar{z})\frac{(z-z_{0})^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}.

We let
\delta z^{\prime}=z-z_{0}
and carry out the linear change of variables
A
which is defined by:

A(\delta^{m}F(\partial_{z}^{m}\gamma(z_{0})))=e_{2m-1},\quad A(\delta^{m}F(i\partial_{z}^{m}\gamma(z_{0})))=e_{2m}\text{ for }m=1,\ldots,k.

Letting
A^{t}\underline{x}^{\prime}=\underline{x}
, we find

\underline{x}\cdot F(\gamma(z))=\underline{x}^{\prime}\cdot F((z^{\prime},(z^{\prime})^{2}/2!,\ldots,(z^{\prime})^{k}/k!)+p(z^{\prime}))+\text{Lin}(x^{\prime})

with

\sup_{z^{\prime}\in Q_{1}}|\partial_{z^{\prime}}^{j}p(z^{\prime})|\leq\delta C_{\gamma}\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,k+1.

Let
\bar{\gamma}(z^{\prime})=(z^{\prime},(z^{\prime})^{2}/2,\ldots,(z^{\prime})^{k}/k!)+p(z^{\prime})
. Choosing
\delta=\delta(C_{\gamma})
we can satisfy the estimates for the derivatives. This incurs the dependence on
C_{\gamma}
in (
2
).
Secondly, the change of variables by
A
is non-singular by non-degeneracy of
\gamma
, which incurs the dependence on
c_{\gamma}
in (
2
).

2.3.
Bilinearization

This is analogous to
[
7
]
.
By a Whitney decomposition of
[0,1]^{2}
and rescaling, it suffices to establish a bilinear decoupling inequality:

(7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}|f_{Q_{1}}f_{Q_{2}}|^{p_{k}/2}d\underline{x}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\big(\sum_{\theta_{1}\in\Theta_{Q_{1}}}\|f_{\theta_{1}}\|_{L^{p_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{2k})}^{2}\big)^{p_{k}/4}\big(\sum_{\theta_{2}\in\Theta_{Q_{2}}}\|f_{\theta_{2}}\|^{2}_{L^{p_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{2k})}\big)^{p_{k}/4}.

Here
Q_{j}
denote squares of length
\sim 1
(say
\frac{1}{4}
), which are separated on the unit scale. The Fourier transform of
f_{Q_{j}}
is contained in
\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq Q_{j},\\
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\delta}\end{subarray}}\theta_{Q}
.

2.4.
Asymmetric bilinear expressions

The analysis revolves around asymmetric bilinear expressions like

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}|f_{Q_{a}}|^{p_{\ell}}|f_{Q_{b}}|^{p_{k}-p_{\ell}}d\underline{x}

with side-lengths of the cubes given by
\ell(Q_{a})=\delta^{a}
and
\ell(Q_{b})=\delta^{b}
for some
0<a,b<1
and
\text{dist}(Q_{a},Q_{b})\sim 1
.
We use the locally constant property of
f_{Q_{a}}
and
f_{Q_{b}}
to write

(8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}|f_{Q_{a}}|^{2}|f_{Q_{b}}|^{4}d\underline{x}\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}(|f_{Q_{a}}|^{2}*\phi_{Q_{a}})(|f_{Q_{b}}|^{4}*\phi_{Q_{b}})d\underline{x}.

Here
\phi_{Q_{a}}
denotes a bump function into directions
\Gamma^{1}_{1}(c_{a})\times\Gamma^{1}_{2}(c_{a})\times\Gamma^{2}_{1}(c_{a})\times\Gamma^{2}_{2}(c_{a})
of size
\delta^{-a}\times\delta^{-a}\times\delta^{-2a}\times\delta^{-2a}
,
c_{a}
denoting the center of
Q_{a}
, and
\phi_{Q_{b}}
into directions
\Gamma^{1}_{1}(c_{b})\times\Gamma^{1}_{2}(c_{b})\times\Gamma^{2}_{1}(c_{b})\times\Gamma^{2}_{2}(c_{b})
and of size
\delta^{-b}\times\delta^{-b}\times\delta^{-2b}\times\delta^{-2b}
with
c_{b}
denoting the center of
Q_{b}
.

The strategy is to apply a lower dimensional decoupling inequality and reduce to a coarser scale. We illustrate the argument in case of the complex paraboloid: Here the asymmetric bilinear decoupling reads

(9)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}(|f_{Q_{a}}|^{2}*\phi_{Q_{a}})(|f_{Q_{b}}|^{4}*\phi_{Q_{b}})d\underline{x}\leq C\big(\sum_{\theta_{1}\in\Theta_{Q_{a},\delta}}\|f_{\theta_{1}}\|^{2}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}\big)\big(\sum_{\theta_{2}\in\Theta_{Q_{b},\delta}}\|f_{\theta_{2}}\|^{2}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}\big)^{2}.

\mathcal{M}_{2,4}(\delta^{a},\delta^{b})
denotes the smallest constant
C
such that (
9
) holds for any choice of functions.

We average now over a two-dimensional ball of size
\delta^{-2b}
in the subspace
H=\langle\Gamma^{1}_{1}(c_{b}),\Gamma^{1}_{2}(c_{b})\rangle^{\perp}
which corresponds to directions
\Gamma_{1}^{2}(c_{b})
and
\Gamma^{2}_{2}(c_{b})

Corollary 5.7
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
5.6

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{B}\big|\sum_{J\in\mathcal{I}(\delta)}f_{J}\big|^{p_{\ell}}\leq C_{\ell}^{\prime}(C,\underline{D},\delta)\big(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{I}(\delta)}\|f_{J}\|^{2}_{L^{p_{\ell}}(\phi_{B})}\big)^{p_{\ell}/2}.

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{B}

We apply Proposition
5.6

\begin{split}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B}\big|\sum_{J}f_{J}\big|^{p_{\ell}}&\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big|\sum_{J}f_{J}\frac{\psi_{B}}{|B|^{1/p_{\ell}}}\big|^{p_{\ell}}\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big|\sum_{J}f_{J}\frac{\psi_{B}}{|B|^{1/p_{\ell}}}\big|^{p_{\ell}}\\

This follows from the rapid decay of

&=32^{1/2}C_{7}C_{0}^{1/2}2^{\frac{1}{2}\cdot 3^{10N}}C_{\varepsilon}^{1-1/3^{N}}\delta^{-\varepsilon}.\end{split}

Taking the two estimates together gives

D(\delta)\leq C_{8}2^{n_{0}\cdot 3^{10N}}C_{\varepsilon}^{1-1/3^{N}}\delta^{-\varepsilon}.

This estimate holds for all
\delta\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}
with
N
given by (
48
). Now we simplify by monotonicity in
N
. By the choice of

3^{N}\leq 3^{a/\varepsilon}
for some
a
and
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}(c)
. We obtain

D(\delta)\leq C_{8}2^{n_{0}\cdot 3^{10a/\varepsilon}}C_{\varepsilon}^{1-\frac{1}{3^{a/\varepsilon}}}\delta^{-\varepsilon}.

∎

We bootstrap this bound to find the following:

Lemma 5.16
.

There is
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(C_{8},c)

and
\delta\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}

Proof.

Let
P(C,\lambda)

for all
\delta\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}
. Lemma
5.15
implies that for
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}(c))

P(C_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon)\Rightarrow P(C_{8}\cdot 2^{n_{0}\cdot 3^{10a/\varepsilon}}C_{\varepsilon}^{1-1/3^{a/\varepsilon}},\varepsilon).

After
M
iterations of the above implication, we obtain

P(C_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon)\Rightarrow P((C_{8}\cdot 2^{n_{0}\cdot 3^{10a/\varepsilon}})^{\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}(1-1/3^{a/\varepsilon})^{j}}C_{\varepsilon}^{(1-1/3^{a/\varepsilon})^{M}},\varepsilon).

We can take limits

C_{\varepsilon}^{(1-1/3^{a/\varepsilon})^{M}}\rightarrow_{M\to\infty}1,\quad\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}(1-1/3^{a/\varepsilon})^{j}\rightarrow_{M\to\infty}3^{a/\varepsilon}.

Hence, letting
M\to\infty
, we obtain

P(C_{8}^{3^{a/\varepsilon}}\cdot 2^{n_{0}\cdot 3^{11a/\varepsilon}},\varepsilon).

By choosing
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}(C_{8},n_{0}(c))
we find for all
\delta\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}

D(\delta)\leq C_{8}^{3^{a/\varepsilon}}2^{n_{0}\cdot 3^{11a/\varepsilon}}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\leq 2^{3^{100a/\varepsilon}}\delta^{-\varepsilon}.

This finishes the proof.
∎
