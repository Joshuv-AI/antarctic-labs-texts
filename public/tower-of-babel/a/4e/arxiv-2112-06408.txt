Cauchy matrix approach to the SU(2) self-dual Yang–Mills equation
Shangshuai Li, Changzheng Qu, Xiangxuan Yi, Da-jun Zhang

Abstract
The Cauchy matrix approach is developed to solve the 𝐒𝐔 ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathbf{SU}(2) self-dual Yang–Mills equation. Starting from a Sylvester matrix equation coupled with certain dispersion relation for infinite coordinates, the self-dual Yang–Mills equation under Yang’s formulation is constructed. By imposing further constraints on complex independent variables, a broad class of explicit solutions are obtained. Keywords: self-dual Yang-Mills equation, Cauchy matrix approach, solutions, integrable system PACS numbers: 02.30.Ik, 02.30.Ks, 05.45.Yv



1
Introduction

The Yang–Mills theory is the most important development in physics in the second half of last century (see
[
41
]
).
The pioneer work is that of Yang and Mills
[
45
]
,
which laid a foundation of nonabelian gauge theories that explain the electromagnetic, the strong and weak
nuclear interactions.
The theory has also geometric interpretations in its nature,
where the concept of gauge fields is found to be identical to fiber bundles,
e.g.
[
2
,
9
,
18
,
42
]
(also see
[
8
,
39
]
and the references therein).

The Yang–Mills equation of motion is
[
28
]
(please refer Section
2
for notations)

\partial_{\mu}F_{\mu\nu}+[B_{\mu},F_{\mu\nu}]=[\mathcal{D}_{\mu},F_{\mu\nu}]=0,

(1.1)

which corresponds to the case where action functional
S
and energy functional
E

take local minima in the sense of semi-classical approximation.
It is hard to solve this equation exactly. Even one could find solutions to (
1.1
),
it is still difficult to verify
S
or
E
reaches local minima.
However, it can be shown that (see
[
6
,
28
]
)
when the gauge field strength
F_{\mu\nu}
is self dual, for given integer topological charge
q
,
the gauge fields are absolute minima of the action
S
.
The corresponding solutions are called instantons and monopoles (for static gauge fields).
The paper
[
28
]
well collected early approaches to the solutions
of the self-dual Yang–Mills (SDYM) equation before 1980,
such as the approach based on the so called Corrigan–Fairlie–’t Hooft–Wilczek ansatz
[
12
,
40
]
,
the Atiyah–Hitchin–Drinfeld–Manin construction
[
3
]
on
instantons with integer topological charge
q
and depending on (for

\mathbf{SU}(\mathcal{N})
case) at least
8q-3
parameters
[
4
]
,
and the Bäcklund transformation approach
[
13
]
based on the Atiyah–Ward ansatz
[
5
]
.
Both
[
3
]
and
[
5
]
follows Ward’s observation in 1977
[
35
]

on the connection between self-dual gauge fields and twistor theory.
One can also refer
[
28
]
and the references therein for more details.

The SDYM equation is an integrable system. It has a Lax pair and has
Painlevé property for any gauge group
[
19
,
36
]
,
therefore some methods based on integrability
have been employed to solve the SDYM equation,
such as a direct transform approach by solving Lax pair
[
7
]
,
Bäcklund transformation based on Riemann-Hilbert problem
[
33
]
,
an approach inspired from Sato’s theory
[
31
]
,
bilinear method
[
26
,
29
]
,
and Darboux transformation
[
25
]
.

In this paper we aim to solve the
\mathbf{SU}(2)
SDYM equation via a direct method,
the Cauchy matrix approach.
The Cauchy matrix approach is a method to construct and study integrable equations
by means of the Sylvester-type equations.
In this approach integrable equations are presented as closed forms of some recurrence relations
involving derivatives (or shifts).
It was first systematically used in
[
24
]
to investigate integrable quadrilateral equations and later
developed in
[
43
,
46
]
to more general cases.
We will construct the
\mathbf{SU}(2)
SDYM equation together with its explicit solutions.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
2
we recall Yang’s formulation for the self-duality of the Yang-Mills equation.
Then, in Sec.
3
we make use of the Cauchy matrix approach
to construct a SDYM equation together with its solutions.
These solutions will be elaborated in Sec.
4
by imposing constraints so that they meet
self-duality of
\mathbf{SU}(2)
gauge group.
Finally, concluding remarks are given in Sec.
5
.
There are three appendixes, which are devoted to proving Lemma
2
,
presenting solutions of the Sylvester equation (
3.1
),
and presenting examples of solutions of the SDYM equation.

2
The SDYM equation: Yang’s formulation

Let
B_{\mu}
’s be matrix valued gauge potentials defined on
\mathbb{R}^{4}

and
F_{\mu\nu}
the gauge field strength defined by

\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}\doteq\partial_{\nu}B_{\mu}-\partial_{\mu}B_{\nu}-[B_{\mu},B_{\nu}],

(2.1)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the Lie bracket defined as
[G,H]=GH-HG
,
and
\partial_{\mu}
stands for the differential operator
\partial/\partial x^{\mu}
,

(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
For a given
\mathbf{SU}(\mathcal{N})
gauge field defined on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
,
the self-duality gives rise to

F_{\mu\nu}=*F_{\mu\nu}\doteq\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha\beta},

(2.2)

where
\epsilon_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}
is the Levi-Civita tensor,

*F
is the duality of field strength
F
, and
\mu,\nu,\alpha,\beta
run over
\{0,1,2,3\}
.
In this case, the gauge potentials
B_{\mu}
’s are also self-dual and belong to
\mathbf{su}(\mathcal{N})
.
Yang extended
(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
from
\mathbb{R}^{4}
to
\mathbb{C}^{4}

and introduced coordinates (transformation)
[
44
]
(cf.
[
35
]
)

\displaystyle\bm{Y}=\sqrt{2}\begin{pmatrix}y&-\bar{z}\\
z&\bar{y}\end{pmatrix}=x^{0}-\mathrm{i}\,\bm{x}\cdot\bm{\sigma},

i.e.

y=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(x^{0}-\mathrm{i}x^{3}),~\bar{y}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(x^{0}+\mathrm{i}x^{3}),~z=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(x^{2}-\mathrm{i}x^{1}),~\bar{z}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(x^{2}+\mathrm{i}x^{1}),

(2.3)

where
\mathrm{i}^{2}=-1
,
\bm{x}=(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
,
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})

and
\sigma_{i}
’s are Pauli matrices.
Note that here
\bar{y},\bar{z}
denote variables independent of the complex conjugates,
y^{*},z^{*}
,
of
y
and
z
, so that real Euclidean space is specified by
\bar{y}=y^{*}
and
\bar{z}=z^{*}
.
The self-duality condition (
2.2
) is then reduced to

\displaystyle F_{yz}=F_{\bar{y}\bar{z}}=0,

(2.4a)

\displaystyle F_{y\bar{y}}+F_{z\bar{z}}=0.

(2.4b)

Next, introducing covariant derivative
\mathcal{D}_{\chi}:=\partial_{\chi}+B_{\chi}
,

\chi\in\{y,\bar{y},z,\bar{z}\}
, where (cf.
[
27
]
)

\displaystyle B_{y}=B_{0}+\mathrm{i}B_{3},~~B_{\bar{y}}=B_{0}-\mathrm{i}B_{3},~~B_{z}=B_{2}+\mathrm{i}B_{1},~~B_{\bar{z}}=B_{2}-\mathrm{i}B_{1},

(2.5)

one obtains a representation of (
2.4a
)
with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{\chi}
,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}_{y},\mathcal{D}_{z}]=0,~~~[\mathcal{D}_{\bar{y}},\mathcal{D}_{\bar{z}}]=0.

(2.6)

This implies that there exist two
\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
generating matrices
D
and
\bar{D}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{y}(D)=\mathcal{D}_{z}(D)=0,~~~\mathcal{D}_{\bar{y}}(\bar{D})=\mathcal{D}_{\bar{z}}(\bar{D})=0,

(2.7)

which leads to

\displaystyle B_{y}=D\partial_{y}(D^{-1}),~~B_{z}=D\partial_{z}(D^{-1}),~~B_{\bar{y}}=\bar{D}\partial_{\bar{y}}(\bar{D}^{-1}),~~B_{\bar{z}}=\bar{D}\partial_{\bar{z}}(\bar{D}^{-1}).

(2.8)

Then, for the field strength
F_{\mu\nu}
defined by (
2.1
) with
the above
B_{\chi}
’s, equation (
2.4a
) is satisfied and (
2.4b
)
is cast into
[
10
]

\displaystyle(J_{\bar{y}}J^{-1})_{y}+(J_{\bar{z}}J^{-1})_{z}=0,

(2.9)

or its alternative form

\displaystyle(J^{-1}J_{y})_{\bar{y}}+(J^{-1}J_{z})_{\bar{z}}=0,

(2.10)

where

J=D\bar{D}^{-1}.

(2.11)

When the gauge group is of
\mathbf{SU}(\mathcal{N})
,
it turns out that
[
27
,
44
]

D,\bar{D}\in\mathbf{SL}(\mathcal{N})
and
\bar{D}=(D^{\dagger})^{-1}
,
where
D^{\dagger}=(D^{*})^{T}
,
and hence
J=DD^{\dagger}
.
In other words,
J
is a positive-definite Hermitian matrix with
|J|=1
.

Equation (
2.9
) (or its alternative form) is usually the equation that was solved by using integrable methods,
see
[
21
,
25
,
26
,
29
]
.
In
\mathbf{SU}(2)
case, once
J
is obtained,
D
can be recovered by Yang’s
R
-gauge
[
44
]
as the following.
Writing
J
in the form

\displaystyle J=\frac{1}{f}\begin{pmatrix}1&-g\\
e&f^{2}-eg\\
\end{pmatrix},

(2.12)

where
f
is real and
e=-g^{*}
. Then
D
takes the form

\displaystyle D=\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
e&f\\
\end{pmatrix}U

(2.13)

where
U\in\mathbf{SU}(2)
.
Hence
B_{\chi}
’s are recovered from (
2.8
)
and so are
F_{\mu\nu}
’s from (
2.1
).

In this paper we will develop the Cauchy matrix approach to construct equation (
2.9
) together with
its explicit solution
J
.

3
The Cauchy matrix approach to the SDYM equation

In order to construct the
\mathbf{SU}(2)
SDYM equation (
2.9
),
we start from the Sylvester equation

\displaystyle\bm{K}\bm{M}-\bm{M}\bm{K}=\bm{r}\bm{s}^{T},

(3.1)

dressed with dispersion relations

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{x_{n}}=\bm{AK}^{n}\bm{r},~~\bm{s}_{x_{n}}=\bm{A}(\bm{K}^{T})^{n}\bm{s},~~(n\in\mathbb{Z}),

(3.2)

where
\{x_{n}\}
are independent infinite complex variables,

\bm{K},\bm{M},\bm{A},\bm{r},\bm{s}
are block matrices in the form of

\displaystyle\bm{K}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}_{1}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{K}_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},~\bm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{M}_{1}\\
\bm{M}_{2}&\bm{0}\\
\end{pmatrix},~\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{N_{1}}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&-\bm{I}_{N_{2}}\end{pmatrix},~\bm{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{r}_{1}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{r}_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},~\bm{s}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{s}_{1}\\
\bm{s}_{2}&\bm{0}\\
\end{pmatrix},

(3.3)

with
\bm{K}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{N_{i}\times N_{i}},\bm{M}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}_{N_{1}\times N_{2}}[{\mathbf{x}}],\bm{M}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}_{N_{2}\times N_{1}}[\mathbf{x}],\bm{r}_{i},\bm{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{N_{i}\times 1}[\mathbf{x}]
,

\bm{I}_{N_{i}}
being the
N_{i}
-th order identity matrix for
i=1,2
,

\mathbf{x}=(\cdots,x_{-1},x_{0},x_{1},\cdots)
, and
N_{1}+N_{2}=2N
.
In addition, we assume
\bm{K}_{1}
and
\bm{K}_{2}
are invertible and do not share any eigenvalues
so that the Sylvester equation (
3.1
) has a unique solution
\bm{M}
for given

\bm{K},\bm{r},\bm{s}

[
30
]
.
Define an infinite matrix
\bm{S}=(\bm{S}^{(i,j)})_{\infty\times\infty}
where
each
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
is a
2\times 2
matrix defined as

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\doteq\bm{s}^{T}\bm{K}^{j}(\bm{I}+\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{K}^{i}\bm{r}=\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}^{(i,j)}&s_{2}^{(i,j)}\\
s_{3}^{(i,j)}&s_{4}^{(i,j)}\\
\end{pmatrix},~~(i,j\in\mathbb{Z}),

(3.4)

i.e.

\displaystyle s_{1}^{(i,j)}

\displaystyle=-\bm{s}_{2}^{T}\bm{K}_{2}^{j}(\bm{I}_{N_{2}}-\bm{M}_{2}\bm{M}_{1})^{-1}\bm{M}_{2}\bm{K}_{1}^{i}\bm{r}_{1},

(3.5a)

\displaystyle s_{2}^{(i,j)}

\displaystyle=\bm{s}_{2}^{T}\bm{K}_{2}^{j}(\bm{I}_{N_{2}}-\bm{M}_{2}\bm{M}_{1})^{-1}\bm{K}_{2}^{i}\bm{r}_{2},

(3.5b)

\displaystyle s_{3}^{(i,j)}

\displaystyle=\bm{s}^{T}_{1}\bm{K}_{1}^{j}(\bm{I}_{N_{1}}-\bm{M}_{1}\bm{M}_{2})^{-1}\bm{K}_{1}^{i}\bm{r}_{1},

(3.5c)

\displaystyle s_{4}^{(i,j)}

\displaystyle=-\bm{s}^{T}_{1}\bm{K}_{1}^{j}(\bm{I}_{N_{1}}-\bm{M}_{1}\bm{M}_{2})^{-1}\bm{M}_{1}\bm{K}_{2}^{i}\bm{r}_{2},

(3.5d)

where
\bm{I}
specially denotes the
2N
-th order identity matrix.
The above settings are the same as those for deriving the Ablowitz–Kaup–Newell–Segur (AKNS) system
[
47
]
,
except here in (
3.2
) we have introduced variable
x_{0}
and its dispersion relation.
Thus we may make use of the results already obtained in
[
47
]
.
However, the variable
x_{0}
does play a useful role in our procedure
of deriving the SDYM equation (
2.9
).

For
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
defined in (
3.4
) where
\bm{K},\bm{M},\bm{r},\bm{s}
are governed by the Sylvester equation
(
3.1
), there exists a recursive relation independent of dispersion relations,
see Proposition 2 in
[
47
]
and cf.
[
43
]
.

Lemma 1
.

\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
defined in (
3.4
) satisfy

\bm{S}^{(i,j+s)}=\bm{S}^{(i+s,j)}-\sum^{s-1}_{l=0}\bm{S}^{(s-1-l,j)}\bm{S}^{(i,l)},~~(s=1,2,\cdots),

(3.6)

where
\bm{K},\bm{M},\bm{r},\bm{s}
obey the Sylvester equation (
3.1
).
In particular, when
s=1
, it reads

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(0,j)}\bm{S}^{(i,0)}=\bm{S}^{(i+1,j)}-\bm{S}^{(i,j+1)}.

(3.7)

With respect to the dispersion relation (
3.2
),
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
evolves as the following.

Lemma 2
.

\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
defined by (
3.4
) obeys evolutions

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(i+n,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j+n)}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,j)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,l)},

\displaystyle(n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}),

(3.8a)

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}_{x_{0}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j)}=[\bm{S}^{(i,j)},\bm{a}],

(3.8b)

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(i+n,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j+n)}+\sum_{l=-1}^{n}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,j)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,l)},

\displaystyle(n\in\mathbb{Z}^{-}),

(3.8c)

where
\bm{K},\bm{M},\bm{r},\bm{s}
satisfy the Sylvester equation (
3.1
)
and dispersion relation (
3.2
),
and
\bm{a}=\sigma_{3}=\mathbf{diag}(1,-1)
.

Formulae (
3.8a
) and (
3.8c
) have been derived in
[
47
]

and (
3.8b
) can be obtained similarly. For the completeness of the paper,
a proof of Lemma
2
is given in Appendix
A
.

Now we come to the first main result of this paper.

Theorem 1
.

Let

\displaystyle\bm{u}\doteq\bm{S}^{(0,0)}=\bm{s}^{T}(\bm{I}+\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{r},~~\bm{v}\doteq\bm{I}_{2}-\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}=\bm{I}_{2}-\bm{s}^{T}(\bm{I}+\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{r}.

(3.9)

Then
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
satisfy the following differential recurrence relation

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{x_{n+1}}\bm{v}^{-1}=-\bm{u}_{x_{n}},~~~(n\in\mathbb{Z}).

(3.10)

Proof.

First, the recursive formula (
3.7
) with
i=-1
yields

\bm{S}^{(-1,j+1)}=\bm{S}^{(0,j)}\bm{v},

(3.11)

which looks simple but will play a crucial role in the following proof.
It gives rise to (with
j=0
)

\bm{u}\bm{v}=\bm{S}^{(-1,1)}

(3.12)

and (with
j=-1
)

\bm{v}^{-1}=\bm{I}_{2}+\bm{S}^{(0,-1)}.

(3.13)

In addition, the evolution relation (
3.8b
) indicates (considering
(i,j)=(0,0)
and
(-1,0)
)

\bm{u}_{x_{0}}=[\bm{u},\bm{a}]

(3.14)

and

\bm{v}_{x_{0}}=[\bm{v},\bm{a}].

(3.15)

Next, looking at (
3.8a
) with
n=1
and
(i,j)=(-1,0)
, and making use of
(
3.12
) and (
3.14
), we have

\displaystyle-\bm{v}_{x_{1}}=\bm{u}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,1)}-\bm{u}\bm{a}(\bm{I}_{2}-\bm{v})=\bm{u}\bm{a}\bm{v}-\bm{a}\bm{u}\bm{v}=[\bm{u},\bm{a}]\bm{v}=\bm{u}_{x_{0}}\bm{v}.

(3.16)

Similarly, with
n=-1
and
(i,j)=(0,0)
formula (
3.8c
) gives rise to

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{x_{-1}}=\bm{a}-\bm{v}\bm{a}(\bm{I}_{2}+\bm{S}^{(0,-1)})=\bm{a}-\bm{v}\bm{a}\bm{v}^{-1}=(\bm{a}\bm{v}-\bm{v}\bm{a})\bm{v}^{-1}=-\bm{v}_{x_{0}}\bm{v}^{-1},

(3.17)

where use has been made of (
3.13
) and (
3.15
).
These two equations cover the cases
n=0
and
-1
of (
3.10
).

Next, we prove (
3.10
) for positive
n
. Formula (
3.8a
)
gives rise to (with
i=j=0
)

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{x_{n}}=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,n)}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,l)}

and (with
i=-1,j=0
)

\displaystyle-\bm{v}_{x_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,n+1)}-\sum_{l=0}^{n}\bm{S}^{(n-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,l)}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,n)}\bm{v}-\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}-\sum_{l=1}^{n}\bm{S}^{(n-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,l)},

where we have made use of (
3.11
) and separated the first term from the summation.
Then, in light of definition of
\bm{v}
, replacing the index
l
with
l+1

and using (
3.11
) once again, we arrive at

\displaystyle-\bm{v}_{x_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}\bm{v}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,n)}\bm{v}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,l+1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}\bm{v}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,n)}\bm{v}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,l)}\bm{v}=\bm{u}_{x_{n}}\bm{v},

which is (
3.10
) with
n\geq 1
.

The case of
n
less than
-1
can be proved similarly from (
3.8c
).
In details, we have

\bm{u}_{x_{n}}=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,n)}+\sum_{l=-1}^{n}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,l)}

and

\displaystyle-\bm{v}_{x_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,n+1)}+\sum_{l=-1}^{n+1}\bm{S}^{(n-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,l)}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}\bm{v}+\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,n)}\bm{v}+\sum_{l=-1}^{n+1}\bm{S}^{(n-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,l)}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}\bm{v}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,n)}\bm{v}+\sum_{l=0}^{n+1}\bm{S}^{(n-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,l)}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}\bm{v}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,n)}\bm{v}+\sum_{l=-1}^{n}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(-1,l+1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(n,0)}\bm{a}\bm{v}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,n)}\bm{v}+\sum_{l=-1}^{n}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,0)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(0,l)}\bm{v}=\bm{u}_{x_{n}}\bm{v}.

Thus, we have proved (
3.10
) for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

∎

Considering the compatibility
(\bm{u}_{x_{n}})_{x_{m}}=(\bm{u}_{x_{m}})_{x_{n}}
,
we immediately arrive at the following.

Theorem 2
.

For
\bm{v}\doteq\bm{I}_{2}-\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}=\bm{I}_{2}-\bm{s}^{T}(\bm{I}+\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{r}

where
\bm{K},\bm{M},\bm{r},\bm{s}
satisfy the Sylvester equation (
3.1
) and dispersion relation (
3.2
),
the following relation holds,

\displaystyle(\bm{v}_{x_{n+1}}\bm{v}^{-1})_{x_{m}}-(\bm{v}_{x_{m+1}}\bm{v}^{-1})_{x_{n}}=0,

(3.18)

where
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

As a by-product of (
3.18
),
\bm{u}
satisfies a potential SDYM equation (cf.
[
20
]
)

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{x_{n},x_{m+1}}-\bm{u}_{x_{m},x_{n+1}}-[\bm{u}_{x_{n}},\bm{u}_{x_{m}}]=0.

(3.19)

Note that equation (
3.18
) differs from the SDYM equation (
2.9
) by a sign “
-
”.
In next section, we will recover (
2.9
) from (
3.18
) by imposing certain reductions.
Besides, apart from
\{x_{n}\}
with dispersion relation (
3.2
), we may introduce for

Concluding remarks

In this paper we have constructed explicit solution
\bm{v}
for the SDYM equation (
4.6
)
by using a direct method, namely, the Cauchy matrix approach.
We started with the Sylvester equation (
3.1

)
with respect to infinite coordinates
\{x_{n}\}

made use of the recursive relation (

and

and imposed constraints on

,

\bm{K}_{1}
and

Our Cauchy matrix approach is based on the scheme for the AKNS system, cf.

Compared with

]

and
\bm{S}^{(j,i)}

All these elaborations enabled us to finally prove the property

and

There are similar direct approaches to construct equation (

15
]
