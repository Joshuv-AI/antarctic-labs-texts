Criticality in stochastic SIR model for infectious diseases
Shigehiro Yasui, Yutaka Hatakeyama, Yoshiyasu Okuhara

Abstract
We discuss the criticality in the stochastic SIR model for infectious diseases. We adopt the path-integral formalism for the propagation of infections among susceptible, infectious, and removed individuals, and perform the perturbative and nonperturbative analyses to evaluate the critical value of the basic reproduction number ℛ {\cal R} . In the perturbation theory, we calculate the mean values and the variances of the number of infectious individuals near the initial time, and find that the critical value ℛ c = 1 / 3 {\cal R}_{\mathrm{c}}=1/3 – 2 / 3 2/3 should be adopted in order to suppress the stochastic spread of infections sufficiently. In the nonperturbative approach, we derive the effective potential by integrating out the stochastic fluctuations, and obtain the effective Euler-Lagrange equations for the time-evolution of the numbers of susceptible, infectious, and removed individuals. From the asymptotic behaviors for a long time, we find that the critical value ℛ c = 2 / 3 {\cal R}_{\mathrm{c}}=2/3 should be adopted for the sufficient convergence of infections. We also find that the endemic state can be generated dynamically by the stochastic fluctuation which is absent in the conventional SIR model. Those analyses show that the critical value of the basic reproduction number should be less than one, against the usually known critical value ℛ c = 1 {\cal R}_{\mathrm{c}}=1 , when the stochastic fluctuations are taken into account in the SIR model.



I
Introduction

In the midst of the pandemic of Covid-19 infection since 2019
Zhu et al. 2020
,
much attention is attracted to mathematical models of infectious diseases in theoretical epidemiology (see e.g. Refs.
Bailey 1975
;
Anderson and May 1992
;
Diekmann and Heesterbeek 2000
;
Daley and Gani 2001
).
Currently there is a growing momentum for a multidimensional understanding that goes beyond traditional theoretical epidemiology. Many mathematical models used in theoretical epidemiology of infectious diseases are deterministic compartment models such as SIR, SIS, and SEIR
models, which stem from the Kermack-McKendrick models.
1
1

1

Their papers were published in 1927, 1932, and 1933, and have been later republished due to their seminal importance in 1991
Kermack and McKendrick 1991a
;
Kermack and McKendrick 1991b
;
Kermack and McKendrick 1991c
.
In these models, the differential equations uniquely determine the number of susceptible, infected, and removed individuals at a given time, and the number of each at a later time. On the other hand, since the transmission of infectious diseases is a stochastic phenomenon, formulations based on stochastic models have been studied since early days by one of the seminal paper by McKendrick
McKendrick 1925
and have been developed as the chain bimodal model by Reed and Frost (see e.g. Ref.
Daley and Gani 2001
).
One of the major advantages of considering a stochastic model over a deterministic model is that it allows us to evaluate the probabilistic confidence level of the predicted number of infected persons.
The assessment of the probabilistic uncertainty of the predicted number of infected people in the future provides an important indicator for determining epidemic control measures such as behavior change and vaccination. Especially in the early stages of infection when the number of infected people is small, or in areas with a small population, the variance due to stochastic fluctuations cannot be ignored against the mean value.

The stochastic models that have been considered in the literature can be broadly classified into (i) discrete-time branching processes (e.g., Bienamyé-Galton-Watson branching process), (ii) continuous-time branching processes (e.g., master equation and Chapman-Kolmogorov equation), (iii) stochastic differential equations (e.g., Langevin equation and Fokker-Planck equation). They are often discussed as Markov processes, although these stochastic processes can be extended to non-Markov processes in principle. Discrete-time branching processes have been studied in infectious disease epidemiology since early days
Otter 1949
, and have been utilized in actual epidemiological evaluations
Lloyd-Smith et al. 2005
;
Jacob 2010
(see also e.g. Refs.
Andersson and Britton 2012
;
Diekmann and Heesterbeek 2000
;
Daley and Gani 2001
;
Haccou et al. 2005
).
It was shown that there is a connection between the Reed-Frost epidemic model and the SIR model in large population limit
Von Bahr and Martin-Löf 1980
, and the asymptotic scaling law of the number of infected persons was obtained
Martin-Löf 1998
;
Aldous 1997
(see also Ref.
Dawson 2017
).
On the other hand, the master equation as a continuous time branching process has been mainly used in theoretical studies
Bartlett 1956
;
Daley and Gani 2001
;
Trapman et al. 2004
. The exact solutions of the stochastic SIR model were reported for the epidemics on the networks composed of linear-chain graphs
Schütz et al. 2008
and tree graphs
Sharkey et al. 2015
. In general cases, however, finding exact solutions of the master equation is not easy due to the nonlinearity in the SIR model without relying on the numerical calculations
Grenfell 1992
;
Keeling and Ross 2008
;
Jenkinson and Goutsias 2012
.
The stochastic differential equation has mathematical research examples
Dolgoarshinnykh and Lalley 2006
;
Lalley 2009
;
Lalley and Zheng 2010
;
Lalley et al. 2014
, whose application toward the actual epidemiology is developing
Maki and Hirose 2013
.

In this study, as a basic research for theoretical epidemiology of infectious diseases, we analyze the stochastic SIR model to evaluate the uncertainty of predicted number of susceptible, infected, and removed individuals due to stochastic fluctuations beyond the deterministic SIR model.
We will discuss the impact of stochastic fluctuations on the deterministic trajectory of the deterministic SIR model (i.e., the time evolution of the number of susceptible, infected, and removed individuals) and evaluate the uncertainty by stochastic fluctuations on the predictions made by the deterministic trajectory. In the present study, the master equation of the stochastic SIR model is expressed in terms of path integrals (functional integrals) according to the Doi-Peliti formalism
Doi 1976
;
Peliti 1985
, utilizing field-theoretical methods stemming from quantum physics
Grassberger 1982
;
Droz and McKane 1994
;
Lee and Cardy 1995
;
Cardy and Täuber 1996
;
Cardy 1996
;
Bettelheim et al. 2001
;
Pastor-Satorras and Solé 2001
;
Elgart and Kamenev 2004
;
Elgart and Kamenev 2006
;
Andreanov et al. 2006
;
Mobilia et al. 2007
;
Butler and Reynolds 2009
;
Täuber 2011
;
Täuber 2012
;

;

;

;
Täuber et al. 2005
;
Cardy et al. 2008
;
Täuber 2009
;
Täuber 2014
;

Interestingly, such approach was used not only for the epidemiology but also for the analysis of the cell dynamics

.
The conventional deterministic SIR model corresponds to the classical trajectory in field theory, by which the deterministic SIR model can be regarded as the
classical
or
tree-level
version of the stochastic SIR model in terms of the quantum field-theory. In this framework, stochastic fluctuations correspond to quantum fluctuations. With this setup, we focus on the basic reproduction number in the stochastic SIR model, and discuss the effect of stochastic fluctuations on the critical value of basic reproduction number
{\cal R}_{\mathrm{c}}
determining the condition of convergence of infections. We show that the critical value of the basic reproduction number

The paper is constructed as the followings.

In Sec.

Final section is devoted to the conclusion and the perspectives.

II
Formalism

II.1
Hamiltonian formalism

We consider
the stochastic SIR model for the infectious diseases
in terms of the master equation
Bartlett 1956
;
Daley and Gani 2001
;
Trapman et al. 2004
.
To begin the discussion, we define the function
P_{t}(N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}})
as the probability that the numbers of susceptible, infectious, and removed (recovered or dead) individuals at time
t
, which are denoted by
N_{\mathrm{S}}
,
N_{\mathrm{I}}
, and
N_{\mathrm{R}}
, respectively.
As a stochastic process in the time-evolution of infection, we introduce the transition rates
\beta
and
\gamma
for transmission of the infectious diseases among people in a unit time.
Here
\beta
indicates the rate that one susceptible person meets one infectious person and becomes infectious,
and
\gamma
the rate that one infectious person gets removed.
In the SIR model, it is assumed that the removed person acquires immunity and never becomes susceptible again.
Considering the simple situation that the transmission of infections and the recovery from infections (or death) can occur constantly at any time, we describe the infectious dynamics by the stochastic process through the following master equation,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}P_{t}(N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}})

\displaystyle=\beta(N_{\mathrm{S}}+1)(N_{\mathrm{I}}-1)P_{t}(N_{\mathrm{S}}+1,N_{\mathrm{I}}-1,N_{\mathrm{R}})+\gamma(N_{\mathrm{I}}+1)P_{t}(N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}}+1,N_{\mathrm{R}}-1)

\displaystyle-(\beta N_{\mathrm{S}}N_{\mathrm{I}}+\gamma N_{\mathrm{I}})P_{t}(N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}}),

(1)

as a differential equation with a first-order derivative for time.
2
2

2

This is called the Kolmogorov forward equation in Ref.
Daley and Gani 2001
, see Eq. (3.3.3) in this reference.

The equation (
1
) is a multichannel-coupled equation of many degrees of freedom with
N_{\mathrm{S}}
,
N_{\mathrm{I}}
, and
N_{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathbb{N}
(
\mathbb{N}
a set of non-negative integer numbers), thus it is difficult to solve the equation in analytic form for arbitrary populations.
The numerical calculation would also be cumbersome without simplifications because the maximum number of populations could reach an order of millions and more
Grenfell 1992
;
Keeling and Ross 2008
;
Jenkinson and Goutsias 2012
.
In the present study, we transform the master equation (
1
) to the path-integral formalism by following the prescriptions in Refs.
Doi 1976
;
Peliti 1985
(see e.g. Ref.
Mattis and Glasser 1998
for a review), and
research the properties of the stochastic SIR model by perturbative and nonperturbative methods based on the field-theoretical approaches.

To start with, we introduce the vector state at time
t
in the infinitely-dimensional vector space, which is expressed by

\displaystyle|\Phi(t)\rangle\equiv\sum_{N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}}}P_{t}(N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}})\prod_{i=\mathrm{S},\mathrm{I},\mathrm{R}}(\hat{a}_{i}^{{\dagger}})^{N_{i}}|0\rangle,

(2)

as a superposition of
P_{t}(N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}})
summing over various values of
N_{\mathrm{S}}
,
N_{\mathrm{I}}
, and
N_{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Notice that the summation is taken over for all the possible combinations of
N_{\mathrm{S}}
,
N_{\mathrm{I}}
, and
N_{\mathrm{R}}
.
In this vector space, the basis is spanned by
\prod_{i=\mathrm{S},\mathrm{I},\mathrm{R}}(\hat{a}_{i}^{{\dagger}})^{N_{i}}|0\rangle
,
where
\hat{a}_{i}
and
\hat{a}_{j}^{{\dagger}}
(
i
,
j=\mathrm{S}
,
\mathrm{I}
, and
\mathrm{R}
) are bosonic annihilation and creation operators satisfying the commutation relations,
[\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}_{j}^{{\dagger}}]=\delta_{ij}
and
[\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}_{j}]=[\hat{a}_{i}^{{\dagger}},\hat{a}_{j}^{{\dagger}}]=0
.
We suppose that the operators act as
\hat{a}_{i}|m_{i}\rangle=m_{i}|m_{i}-1\rangle
and
\hat{a}_{i}^{{\dagger}}|m_{i}\rangle=|m_{i}+1\rangle
for
|m_{i}\rangle
.
Here
|m_{i}\rangle
is the state in which there exist
m_{i}
(
\in\mathbb{N}
) individuals for
i=\mathrm{S}
,
\mathrm{I}
, and
\mathrm{R}
.
Particularly
|0\rangle
is defined to be the empty state in which there exists no individual.
We can regard that those annihilation and creation operators resemble essentially the ones used in quantum physics, although the normalization constants of the states are different from the conventional ones
Doi 1976
;
Peliti 1985
.
With the above setting, we find that the master equation (
1
) is transformed to the Schrödinger-type equation

\displaystyle-\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}|\Phi(t)\rangle=\hat{H}|\Phi(t)\rangle,

(3)

with the Hamiltonian operator defined by

\displaystyle\hat{H}\equiv\beta\bigl(-\hat{a}_{\mathrm{S}}(\hat{a}_{\mathrm{I}}^{{\dagger}})^{2}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{I}}+\hat{a}_{\mathrm{S}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{S}}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{I}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{I}}\bigr)+\gamma\bigl(-\hat{a}_{\mathrm{I}}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{R}}^{{\dagger}}+\hat{a}_{\mathrm{I}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{I}}\bigr),

(4)

in the Hamiltonian formalism.
This is just another expression of Eq. (
1
).
One of the advantages of this rewriting is that
we obtain the formal solution expressed by
|\Phi(t)\rangle=e^{-\hat{H}t}|\Phi(0)\rangle
.
In this formalism, we express the mean value of an arbitrary function
A(N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}})
with variables
N_{i}
(
i=\mathrm{S}
,
\mathrm{I}
, and
\mathrm{R}
) by

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{t}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}}}P_{t}(N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}})A(N_{\mathrm{S}},N_{\mathrm{I}},N_{\mathrm{R}})

\displaystyle=\langle{\cal P}|A(\hat{a}_{\mathrm{S}},\hat{a}_{\mathrm{I}},\hat{a}_{\mathrm{R}})|\Phi(t)\rangle,

(5)

with the projected state defined by

\displaystyle\langle{\cal P}|\equiv\langle 0|\exp\Biggl(\sum_{i=\mathrm{S},\mathrm{I},\mathrm{R}}\hat{a}_{i}\Biggr).

(6)

The projected state has the following properties:

\langle{\cal P}|\hat{a}_{i}^{{\dagger}}=\langle{\cal P}|
(
i=\mathrm{S}
,
\mathrm{I}
, and
\mathrm{R}
) as a projection operator and
\langle{\cal P}|\Phi(t)\rangle=1
as a normalization.
In spite of the existence of the formal solution, however, it is still difficult to obtain the explicit forms of solution in the Hamiltonian formalism.

II.2
Path-integral formalism

We further rewrite the Schrödinger-type equation (
3
) in terms of the path-integral (functional integral) formalism by following the prescriptions in Refs.
Peliti 1985
;
Lee and Cardy 1995
;
Pastor-Satorras and Solé 2001
;
Mobilia et al. 2007
;
Bettelheim et al. 2001
;
Täuber 2012
;
Shih and Goldenfeld 2014
(see also Refs.
Dickman and Vidigal 2003
;
Täuber et al. 2005
;
Täuber 2009
;
Täuber 2014
for more information).
For this purpose, we introduce the coherent state defined by

\displaystyle|\phi_{i}(\tau)\rangle=\exp\biggl(-\frac{1}{2}|\phi_{i}(\tau)|^{2}+\phi_{i}(\tau)\hat{a}_{i}^{{\dagger}}\biggr)|0\rangle,

(7)

for a creation operator
\hat{a}_{i}^{{\dagger}}
for
i=\mathrm{S}
,
\mathrm{I}
, and
\mathrm{R}
, where
\phi_{i}(\tau)
is a parameter value in the complex number at time
\tau

For brevity, we sometimes use the notation as a vector
\phi(\tau)=\bigl\{\phi_{\mathrm{S}}(\tau),\phi_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau),\phi_{\mathrm{R}}(\tau)\bigr\}
in the followings.
Starting from Eq. (
3
) and slicing the time interval
\tau\in[0,t]
, instead of Eq. (
5
), we obtain the mean value at time
t
,

\displaystyle\langle A(\phi(t))\rangle={\cal N}\int{\cal D}\bar{\phi}{\cal D}\phi\,A(\phi(t))e^{-S[\bar{\phi},\phi]},

(8)

for an arbitrary function
A(\phi(t))
with a variable
\phi(t)
.
Here we have introduced the path-integral on the functional of
\phi(\tau)
.
We have defined the action for
\phi
and
\bar{\phi}
,

\displaystyle S[\bar{\phi},\phi]\equiv S_{0}[\bar{\phi},\phi]+S_{\mathrm{int}}[\bar{\phi},\phi],

(9)

which consists of the free part

\displaystyle S_{0}[\bar{\phi},\phi]\equiv\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\biggl(\bar{S}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}S+\bar{I}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}I+\bar{R}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}R\biggr)+\bar{S}(0)\bigl(S(0)-\bar{n}_{S0}\bigr)+\bar{I}(0)\bigl(I(0)-\bar{n}_{I0}\bigr)+\bar{R}(0)\bigl(R(0)-\bar{n}_{R0}\bigr),

(10)

and the interaction part

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{int}}[\bar{\phi},\phi]\equiv\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\Bigl(\beta\bigl(-\bar{I}^{2}+\bar{S}\bar{I}-\bar{I}+\bar{S}\bigr)SI+\gamma\bigl(-\bar{R}+\bar{I}\bigr)I\Bigr).

(11)

This interaction term describes the dynamics of the transmission of infections and the recovery from infections (or death).
We have used the notations
\phi=\bigl\{S,I,R\bigr\}
instead of
\bigl\{\phi_{\mathrm{S}},\phi_{\mathrm{I}},\phi_{\mathrm{R}}\bigr\}
for clarity of the equations.
Here
\bar{\phi}=\bigl\{\bar{S},\bar{I},\bar{R}\bigr\}
is a conjugate field of
\phi
.
We have introduced
{\cal N}
for an overall constant, which is however irrelevant in the following discussions, and hence it can be discarded.
The derivations of Eq. (
8
) is quite analogous to that used in quantum field theory.
In Eq. (
10
), we have introduced parameters
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{S}0}
,
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{I}0}
, and
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{R}0}
for the average numbers for the susceptible, infectious, and removed individuals at the initial stage of the time-evolution of infections (
\tau=0
), since the Poisson distribution with parameters
\bar{n}_{i0}
(
i=\mathrm{S}
,
\mathrm{I}
, and
\mathrm{R}
) is assumed at the initial time.
The values of
\bar{n}_{i0}
need to be given directly from the data in real-world.
One might consider that the path-integral formalism (
8
) is much different from the original master equation (
1
) or the Hamiltonian formalism (
3
), but they are equivalently the same.
The advantage for the path-integral formalism is to enable us the systematic and analytical methods both in perturbative and nonperturbative approaches.

In order to evaluate the path-integral in Eq. (
8
) systematically, we introduce the generating functional defined by

\displaystyle Z[\bar{j},j]

\displaystyle\equiv{\cal N}\int{\cal D}\bar{\phi}{\cal D}\phi\exp\biggl(-S[\bar{\phi},\phi]+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau(\bar{\phi}j+\bar{j}\phi)\biggr),

(12)

as a functional of the source functions
j=\bigl\{\sigma,\iota,\rho\bigr\}
and
\bar{j}=\bigl\{\bar{\sigma},\bar{\iota},\bar{\rho}\bigr\}
.
The source functions are coupled to
\bar{\phi}
and
\phi
through
\bar{\phi}j=\bar{S}\sigma+\bar{I}\iota+\bar{R}\rho
and
\bar{j}\phi=\bar{\sigma}S+\bar{\iota}I+\bar{\rho}R
.
To remove the reducible diagrams in
Z[\bar{j},j]
, furthermore, we define

W[\bar{j},j]=\ln Z[\bar{j},j]

which generates the irreducible diagrams only.
The practical method for calculating the generating functional is presented in Sec.
A
in detail.
Finally,
we calculate the first- and second-order derivatives of
W[\bar{j},j]
with respect to the source functions
to obtain the mean values of
S(t)
,
I(t)
, and
R(t)

\displaystyle\langle\phi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta W[\bar{j},j]}{\delta\bar{j}(t)}\biggr|_{j=\bar{j}=0},

(13)

and their variances

\displaystyle\langle(\delta\phi(t))^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}W[\bar{j},j]}{\delta\bar{j}(t)^{2}}\biggr|_{j=\bar{j}=0}+\frac{\delta W[\bar{j},j]}{\delta\bar{j}(t)}\biggr|_{j=\bar{j}=0},

(14)

defined by
\langle(\delta\phi(t))^{2}\rangle=\langle\phi(t)^{2}\rangle-\langle\phi(t)\rangle^{2}
for each pair of
(\phi,\bar{j})=(S,\bar{\sigma})
,
(I,\bar{\iota})
, and
(R,\bar{\rho})
.
The derivation of Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
) are shown in Sec.
B
.
Notice that the equations (
13
) and (
14
) are rigorous and generally applicable.
The perturbative expansion with respect to
\beta
and
\gamma
can be performed straightforwardly as shown in Sec.
III.1
.

II.3
Effective action and Euler-Lagrange equations

For the generating functional (
12
), beyond the perturbation theory, we adopt the nonperturbative approach by separating
\phi
(
\bar{\phi}
) to classical parts and fluctuation parts.
By integrating the lowest-order terms for the fluctuation parts, we can obtain the effective action including the stochastic fluctuations at infrared (long wavelength) scale.
Here we represent the classical parts by
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}=\bigl\{S_{\mathrm{c}},I_{\mathrm{c}},R_{\mathrm{c}}\bigr\}
and
\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}}=\bigl\{\bar{S}_{\mathrm{c}},\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}},\bar{R}_{\mathrm{c}}\bigr\}
for the classical paths.
We also represent the fluctuation parts by
\phi_{\varphi}=\bigl\{S_{\varphi},I_{\varphi},R_{\varphi}\bigr\}
and
\bar{\phi}_{\varphi}=\bigl\{\bar{S}_{\varphi},\bar{I}_{\varphi},\bar{R}_{\varphi}\bigr\}
for the deviations
from the classical paths,
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}}
.
We use “classical” because
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}}
obey the conventional differential equations in the deterministic SIR model, see Eqs. (
41
)-(
43
) in Sec.
III.2
.
Using the decomposition

\phi=\phi_{\mathrm{c}}+\phi_{\varphi}

and

\bar{\phi}=\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}}+\bar{\phi}_{\varphi}
,
we rewrite the generating functional (
12
) by

\displaystyle Z[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{j},j]

\displaystyle={\cal N}e^{-I_{\mathrm{c}}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}}]+\bar{j}\phi_{\mathrm{c}}+\bar{\phi}_{c}j}\int{\cal D}\bar{\phi}_{\varphi}{\cal D}\phi_{\varphi}\,e^{-I[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{\phi}_{\varphi},\phi_{\varphi}]},

(15)

with the shorthand notation

\displaystyle\bar{j}\phi_{\mathrm{c}}+\bar{\phi}_{c}j

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\bigl(\bar{j}(\tau)\phi_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau)+\bar{\phi}_{c}(\tau)j(\tau)\bigr).

(16)

Thus the action has been divided into the classical part,
I_{\mathrm{c}}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}}]
, and the fluctuation part,
I[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}}.\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{\phi}_{\varphi},\phi_{\varphi}]
.
Here
I_{\mathrm{c}}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}}]
is given by

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{c}}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}}]

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\biggl(\bar{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}S_{\mathrm{c}}+\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}I_{\mathrm{c}}+\bar{R}_{\mathrm{c}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}R_{\mathrm{c}}+\beta\bigl(-\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+\bar{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}+\bar{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\bigr)S_{\mathrm{c}}I_{\mathrm{c}}+\gamma\bigl(-\bar{R}_{\mathrm{c}}+\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}\bigr)I_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+\Bigl(\bar{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\bigl(S_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{n}_{S0}\bigr)+\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}\bigl(I_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{n}_{I0}\bigr)+\bar{R}_{\mathrm{c}}\bigl(R_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{n}_{R0}\bigr)\Bigr)\delta(\tau)\biggr),

(17)

and
I[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{\phi}_{\varphi},\phi_{\varphi}]
is by

\displaystyle I[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{\phi}_{\varphi},\phi_{\varphi}]

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\bar{\phi}_{\varphi}\biggl(\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}-{\cal M}_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau)+\delta(\tau)\biggr)\phi_{\varphi},

(18)

with the matrix defined by

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau)\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}-\beta\bigl(\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}+1\bigr)I_{\mathrm{c}}&-\beta\bigl(\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}+1\bigr)S_{\mathrm{c}}&0\\
\beta\bigl(2\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{S}_{c}+1\bigr)I_{\mathrm{c}}&\beta\bigl(2\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{S}_{\mathrm{c}}+1\bigr)S_{\mathrm{c}}-\gamma&0\\
0&\gamma&0\end{array}\right).

As the lowest-order approximation in the fluctuation part, we have left only the bilinear terms of
\phi_{\varphi}
and
\bar{\phi}_{\varphi}

Then, because the path-integral is reduced to the Gaussian integral,

As in Sec.

Performing the path-integrals for

and

, we obtain the effective action

\displaystyle W[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{j},j]=-I_{\mathrm{c}}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}}]+\bar{j}\phi_{\mathrm{c}}+\bar{\phi}_{c}j-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\ln\Delta^{-1}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}}],

(22)

where
\Delta^{-1}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}}]
is defined by

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}}](\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\equiv\delta(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})\biggl(\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}}-{\cal M}_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau_{1})+\delta(\tau_{1})\biggr),

(23)

as a propagator of the fluctuation fields between two time points
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
.
We denote the trace by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\,F=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,F(\tau,\tau)
as an integral over the time for the two-point function
F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
.
Notice that
W[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{j},j]
is called the effective action since the fluctuation parts are included in the logarithmic term in the last.
In Eq. (
22
), finally, we apply the Legendre transformation to obtain the effective potential defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma[\bar{\phi},\phi]\equiv W[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{j},j]-\bar{j}\phi-\bar{\phi}j,

(24)

with the variables defined by

\displaystyle\phi\equiv\frac{\delta W}{\delta\bar{j}}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{j},j],\quad\bar{\phi}\equiv\frac{\delta W}{\delta j}[\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}},\phi_{\mathrm{c}};\bar{j},j].

(25)

Here
\phi=\bigl\{S,R,I\bigr\}
and
\bar{\phi}=\bigl\{\bar{S},\bar{R},\bar{I}\bigr\}
should be distinguished from the integral variables used in Sec.
II.2
, although the same symbols are used for saving the letters.
Evaluating
\phi
and
\bar{\phi}
in Eq. (
25
) by using the generating functional (
15
),
we find
\phi\approx\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\bar{\phi}\approx\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{c}}
at the lowest approximation in the loop expansion.
Thus, we obtain the effective Euler-Lagrange (EL) equations for
\phi
,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{S}}\Gamma[\bar{\phi},\phi]\biggr|_{\bar{\phi}=0}=\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{I}}\Gamma[\bar{\phi},\phi]\biggr|_{\bar{\phi}=0}=\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{R}}\Gamma[\bar{\phi},\phi]\biggr|_{\bar{\phi}=0}=0,

(26)

which give the effective paths modified by the stochastic fluctuations at the lowest order around the classical paths.

III
Results

With the setup in Sec.
II
, we calculate various quantities (mean-values, variances, and so on) related to the numbers of susceptible, infectious, and removed individuals.
Here we show the results on the basic reproduction number which is critical to the watershed of spread and convergence of infectious diseases.

III.1
Mean values and variances at classical level in perturbative expansion

We show the results of the mean values and the variances for

,

Besides the mean values, the variances are important quantities to estimate how much uncertainty can exist in the predicted number of susceptible, infectious, and removed individuals.
In the calculation, we adopt the perturbation as a series of

{\cal O}(t^{2})

As results, we obtain

\displaystyle=\bar{n}_{S0}-\beta\bar{n}_{S0}\bar{n}_{I0}t+\biggl(\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\bigl(-\bar{n}_{S0}^{2}\bar{n}_{I0}+\bar{n}_{S0}\bar{n}_{I0}^{2}\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\beta\gamma\bar{n}_{S0}\bar{n}_{I0}\biggr)t^{2}+{\cal O}(t^{3}),

(27)

In fact, it is confirmed that the sum of the mean values satisfy exactly the conservation of the total population number as

Noticing the relation

\langle I(t)\rangle_{\mathrm{cl}}=\bar{n}_{I0}+\bigl({\cal R}-1\bigr)\gamma\bar{n}_{I0}t+{\cal O}(t^{2})

for the basic reproduction number
{\cal R}=\beta\bar{n}_{S0}/\gamma
.
The value of
{\cal R}
indicates the average number of infected individuals at secondary infection when the number of infected individuals in the primary infection are negligible at the early stage of the infection process.
We then read that the number of infectious individuals starts to increase for the positive coefficient (
{\cal R}>1
), while it does to decrease for the negative coefficient (
{\cal R}<1
).
Thus, the critical value for the infection spreadings seems to be provided by

However, we should notice that the mean value
\langle I(t)\rangle_{\mathrm{cl}}
is an average value of the populations which can occur possibly at most.
We may consider the possible situations that the number of infectious individuals could still increase even for
{\cal R}<1

which case is allowed to occur within the probabilistic uncertainty.
Focusing on the uncertainty shown in Eq. (

we carefully investigate the variances

\bigl\langle\bigl(\delta I(t)\bigr)^{2}\bigr\rangle_{\mathrm{cl}}=\bar{n}_{I0}+\bigl(3{\cal R}-1\bigr)\gamma\bar{n}_{I0}t+{\cal O}(t^{2})

If we want request stringently the situation that the number of the infectious individuals decrease certainly for any possible situations in
{\cal R}<1

we need to request further condition that

should not increase as well.
We thus find another condition
{\cal R}<1/3
in addition to the conventional condition,
{\cal R}<1
.
Therefore, we need the stronger condition
{\cal R}<1/3
, giving the smaller critical value
{\cal R}_{\mathrm{c}}=1/3
, in order for that the infection starts to converge certainly within the probabilistic uncertainty.

III.2
Effective Euler-Lagrange equations with nonperturbative effects

Beyond the perturbation theory, we show the results of the effective EL equations (
26

Following the procedure in Sec.
II.3
,
we obtain the EL equations

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}S+\beta SI+(S-\bar{n}_{S0})\delta(t)

\displaystyle=0,

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}I-\beta SI+\gamma I+(I-\bar{n}_{I0})\delta(t)

\displaystyle=0,

(42)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}R-\gamma I+(R-\bar{n}_{R0})\delta(t)

\displaystyle=0,

(43)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}S+\beta SI-\frac{1}{4}\bigl(-\beta S-\gamma\bigr)+\bigl(S-\bar{n}_{S0}\bigr)\delta(t)

\displaystyle=0,

(46)

at one-loop level.
Notice the terms with
\delta

In Eqs. (
44

Precisely, the terms generated by the stochastic fluctuations are dependent on the terminal time

In the present discussion, we focus on the long-time limit as a simple case, and regard that the above equations are applied to the time-interval

In Eqs. (

For investigating the nonperturbative effect concretely, we examine the behavior of

As a simple setting for small number of infectious individuals,

The appearance of the endemic state is a particular feature in the stochastic SIR model that is qualitatively different from the classical SIR model, because there is no endemic state in the conventional SIR model.

The nonperturbative result provides a constraint condition on the critical value of the reproduction number for the convergence of infections.

Then, as an answer to the question, we find that

This critical value seems to be consistent with

2/3

IV
Conclusion and perspectives

We have discussed the critical value of the basic reproduction number in the stochastic SIR model for infectious diseases. We have formulated the master equation for the stochastic process
among susceptible, infectious, and removed individuals and have rewritten it in terms of the Hamiltonian formalism, and finally have transformed it into the path-integral formalism.
This is analogous to the formulation in quantum field theory for microscopic physics.
Based on the generating functional, we have performed the perturbative and nonperturbative analyses to evaluate the critical value of the basic reproduction number,
{\cal R}
.
In the perturbation theory, we have calculated the mean values and the variances for the numbers of susceptible, infectious, and removed individuals as a series of time near the initial time.
We have found that the critical value
{\cal R}_{\mathrm{c}}=1/3
–
2/3
should be adopted in order to suppress the stochastic spread of infections certainly within the probabilistic uncertainty.
In the nonperturbative approach, we have derived the effective potential by integrating out the stochastic fluctuations at one-loop level,
and have obtained the effective Euler-Lagrange equations for the time-evolution of the numbers of susceptible, infectious, and removed individuals.
The effective Euler-Lagrange equations include the new terms generated by the stochastic fluctuations which are absent in the classical SIR model.
Assuming that the number of susceptible individuals is much larger than that of infectious individuals,
we have found that the critical value
{\cal R}_{\mathrm{c}}=2/3
should be adopted for the complete convergence of infections.
As a conclusion, the critical value of the basic reproduction number should be less than one, against the known critical value
{\cal R}_{\mathrm{c}}=1
in the classical SIR model, when the stochastic fluctuations are taken into account.

In the present study, we have introduced the bosonic commutation relations in the Hamiltonian formalism based on the assumption that there can be infinitely many individuals.
In reality, however, there should be a maximum number of individuals.
Such restriction can be described in terms of the fermion operators as well as the parastatistics operators as an extension from the boson operators.
We have assumed that the coefficients in the stochastic SIR model are constant number which should be generalized to the time-dependent function for application to real-world data.
The age structures should also be discussed together with the extension to the SEIR and SEIRS models including the treatment and hospitalization effects.
In order to follow up the activity of each individual, the network epidemiology is also an interesting topics which can be applied by extending present analysis of the stochastic SIR model.
These subjects are left for future research.

Appendix C
Calculation of effective potential

We calculate the trace term in Eq. (
22
) in the following way:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\ln\Delta^{-1}[\bar{\phi},\phi]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\ln\Delta^{-1}[\bar{\phi},\phi](\tau,\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\langle\tau|\Biggl(\ln\bigl({\mathrm{d}}_{\tau}-{\cal M}_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau)\bigr)+\ln\biggl(1+\frac{\delta(\tau)}{{\mathrm{d}}_{\tau}-{\cal M}_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau)}\biggr)\Biggr)|\tau\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\langle\tau|\ln\bigl({\mathrm{d}}_{\tau}-{\cal M}_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau)\bigr)|\tau\rangle+\mathrm{tr}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\langle\tau|\ln\biggl(1+\frac{\delta(\tau)}{{\mathrm{d}}_{\tau}-{\cal M}_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau)}\biggr)|\tau\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\sum_{\alpha=\pm,0}\int_{0}^{m_{\mathrm{c}\alpha}(\tau)}\mathrm{d}x\,\coth\biggl(\frac{t}{2}x\biggr)+\sum_{\alpha=\pm,0}\ln\Biggl(1-\frac{1}{2}\coth\biggl(\frac{t}{2}m_{\mathrm{c}\alpha}(0)\biggr)\Biggr),

(65)

where we sum over the Matsubara frequencies
\omega_{n}=2\pi nt
(
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
).
Here we have introduced the Fourier transformation whose basis is spanned by
|\omega_{n}\rangle
, and have used the product
\langle\omega_{n}|\tau\rangle=e^{i\omega_{n}\tau}
represented by the plane-wave,
the completeness relation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\sum_{n}|\omega_{n}\rangle\langle\omega_{n}|=1,

(66)

and the summation over the Matsubara frequencies

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\frac{1}{i\omega_{n}+x}=\frac{t}{2}\coth\biggl(\frac{t}{2}x\biggr),

(67)

for an arbitral real number
x
.
In the above calculation, we have used the eigenvalues in the matrix
{\cal M}_{\mathrm{c}}
in Eq. (
II.3
),

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{c}\pm}(\tau)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\Biggl(\beta\bigl(2\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{S}_{c}+1\bigr)S_{\mathrm{c}}-\beta\bigl(\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}+1\bigr)I_{\mathrm{c}}-\gamma

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\pm\sqrt{\Bigl(\beta\bigl(2\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{S}_{c}+1\bigr)S_{\mathrm{c}}+\beta\bigl(\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}+1\bigr)I_{\mathrm{c}}-\gamma\Bigr)^{2}-4\beta^{2}\bigl(\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}+1\bigr)\bigl(2\bar{I}_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{S}_{\mathrm{c}}+1\bigr)S_{\mathrm{c}}I_{\mathrm{c}}}\Biggr),

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{c}0}(\tau)

\displaystyle\equiv 0.

(69)

65

22
),
we finally obtain the approximate form of the effective potential for arbitrary
