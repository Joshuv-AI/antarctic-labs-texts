Abstract
Generalized variational inference (GVI) provides an optimization-theoretic framework for statistical estimation that encapsulates many traditional estimation procedures. The typical GVI problem is to compute a distribution of parameters that maximizes the expected payoff minus the divergence of the distribution from a specified prior. In this way, GVI enables likelihood-free estimation with the ability to control the influence of the prior by tuning the so-called learning rate. Recently, GVI was shown to outperform traditional Bayesian inference when the model and prior distribution are misspecified. In this paper, we introduce and analyze a new GVI formulation based on utility theory and risk management. Our formulation is to maximize the expected payoff while enforcing constraints on the maximizing distribution. We recover the original GVI distribution by choosing the feasible set to include a constraint on the divergence of the distribution from the prior. In doing so, we automatically determine the learning rate as the Lagrange multiplier for the constraint. In this setting, we are able to transform the infinite-dimensional estimation problem into a two-dimensional convex program. This reformulation further provides an analytic expression for the optimal density of parameters. In addition, we prove asymptotic consistency results for empirical approximations of our optimal distributions. Throughout, we draw connections between our estimation procedure and risk management. In fact, we demonstrate that our estimation procedure is equivalent to evaluating a risk measure. We test our procedure on an estimation problem with a misspecified model and prior distribution, and conclude with some extensions of our approach.

Introduction

Many statistical estimation problems can be formulated as the optimization problem

\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\left\{M_{N}(\theta)\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}m(\theta,y_{n})\right\},

(1)

where
\{y_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset\Upsilon
are realizations of the noisy data
Y
,

\Upsilon
is the data space,
\theta\in\Theta
are the parameters to be
estimated, and
m:\Theta\times\Upsilon\to\mathbb{R}
is a pay-off or log-likelihood,
see
(
van de Geer 2000
;
van der Vaart 2000
)
and the
references therein. This class of problems encapsulates M-estimation and
empirical risk minimization problems, including regression and maximum
likelihood estimation as well as other training applications in machine learning.
On the other hand, empirical approximations of traditional stochastic
optimization problems also have the form (
1
), see e.g.,

(
Shapiro et al. 2014
)
.

In this paper, we study an extension of (
1
) that permits
measure-valued solutions. This coherent generalized variational inference
(CGVI) problem is given by

\max_{P\in\mathfrak{A}}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[M_{N}\right],

(2)

where
\mathfrak{A}
is a set of admissible probability measures defined on subsets of

\Theta
and
\mathbb{E}_{P}
denotes the expectation with respect to the probability
measure
P
. Classically, the CGVI problem (
2
) models the situation
in which
\theta
are random
incidental
parameters

(
Kiefer and Wolfowitz 1956
)
. In contrast, we view (
2
) as a
distributionally robust
version of (
1
) that enables the inclusion of prior
statistical information regarding the unknown deterministic
structural

parameters
\theta
through the definition of the set
\mathfrak{A}
. The idea for
distributionally robust optimization goes back to
(
Žáčková 1966
)
, but has
seen increased interest in recent years, see, e.g.,

(
Kouri 2017
;
Kuhn et al. 2019
;
Shapiro 2017
)
.

One practical consequence of (
2
) is improved computational
tractability. Many optimization algorithms
only guarantee convergence to locally optimal solutions, while global methods
often scale poorly with problem size, leading to intractability for large-scale
nonconcave problems. Consequently, if
M_{n}
is not concave in
\theta
, then it
may be computationally infeasible to find a global maximum of
(
1
). On the other hand, (
2
) is a concave maximization
problem as long as
\mathfrak{A}
is convex. We may therefore view (
2
)
as a concave relaxation of (
1
). Owing to the concavity of
(
2
), locally optimal solutions to (
2
) are also
globally optimal and hence, local optimization methods can be used.
Unfortunately, (
2
) is an infinite-dimensional optimization problem,
which may be impossible to solve analytically or numerically without
discretization. However, as is commonly done in distributionally robust
optimization
(
Kuhn et al. 2019
;
Shapiro 2017
)
, we can choose
\mathfrak{A}
in
such a way that (
2
) can be equivalently reformulated as a low
dimensional convex minimization problem. To achieve this, we will employ
concepts from decision theory, including utility functions and risk measures,
to quantify our maximum tolerance for deviating from a prescribed prior
probability measure
\Pi
on
\Theta
.

A common method for comparing two probability measures is to use a

\phi
-divergence
(
Csiszár 1963
;
Morimoto 1963
)
. The class of

\phi
-divergences includes the popular Kullback-Liebler (KL),
\chi^{2}
, Hellinger,
and Rényi divergences. As demonstrated in

(
Ben-Tal and Teboulle 1986
;
Ben-Tal and Teboulle 1987
;
Ben-Tal and Teboulle 2007
)
,
there is a fundamental link between
\phi
-divergences, utility functions, and
risk measures, a relationship that we will build on. In particular, we will
show that for a given utility functional
\mathcal{U}
acting on a space of random
variables
\mathcal{M}
, we can define the disutility functional
\mathcal{V}(X)=-\mathcal{U}(-X)

for
X\in\mathcal{M}
and ultimately a statistical divergence as
\Phi(p)=\mathcal{V}^{*}(p)
,
where
p
is the density of a probability measure
P
with respect to the prior

\Pi
and
\mathcal{V}^{*}
denotes the Fenchel conjugate of
\mathcal{V}
. This construction is
closely related to risk measures via optimized certainty equivalents

(
Ben-Tal and Teboulle 2007
)
and the risk quadrangle

(
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2002
)
.
This link between utility functions and statistical divergences is practically
appealing as utility functions have been intensely studied for decades, e.g.,

(
Arrow 1970
;
Fishburn 1988
;
Pratt 1964
;
von Neumann and Morgenstern 2007
)
,
and are perhaps more intuitive to choose than a statistical divergence
\Phi
.

Some of the most striking results associated with our decision-theoretic
framework for estimation arise when the utility functional
\mathcal{U}
is an expected
utility, i.e.,
\mathcal{U}(X)=\mathbb{E}[u(X)]
, where
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
scalar utility function. Many well-known utility functions
u
give rise to
popular
\phi
-divergences (cf.
(
Ben-Tal and Teboulle 1987
)
). For example,
the ubiquitous exponential utility function generates the KL
divergence, while the isoelastic utility function generates the Rényi
divergence. In this setting, we define
\mathfrak{A}
as a subset of probability
measures whose
\phi
-divergence from the prior measure
\Pi
is less than a
prescribed tolerance
\varepsilon>0
. With this
\mathfrak{A}
, (
2
) is closely
related to the Gibbs posterior

(
Bissiri et al. 2016
;
Jiang and Tanner 2008
;
Zhang 2006a
;
Zhang 2006b
)
,
and more generally generalized variational inference (GVI) as introduced in

(
Knoblauch et al. 2019
)
.
Unlike traditional Bayesian inference, our approach allows for a misspecified
prior
\Pi
, is not predicated on a specific noise structure (model
misspecification), and is computationally tractable once prior samples are
available. In fact, these three features were the primary motivation for
GVI and the so-called Rule of Three
(
Knoblauch et al. 2019
)
.
There is other related work on measure-valued M-estimators, e.g.,

(
Basu and Lindsay 1994
;
Basu et al. 2011
;
Greco et al. 2008
)
.
However,
(
Greco et al. 2008
)
considers a Bayesian posterior
based on M-estimation using pseudo-likelihoods, which is less general than our
approach, and
(
Basu and Lindsay 1994
;
Basu et al. 2011
)
develop an
estimation theory in which divergences are also employed, but the
measure-valued M-estimators assume a parametric form. Similarly,

\phi
-divergences and Wasserstein distances have recently found broad
applicability in machine learning

(
Arjovsky et al. 2017
;
Gretton et al. 2012
;
Kuhn et al. 2019
;
Nowozin et al. 2016
)
;
particularly for constructing generative adversarial
networks (GANs) to estimate probability laws.

Using the utility-based statistical divergence
\Phi
described above, we
express our tolerance for deviation from
\Pi
by restricting
\mathfrak{A}
to a
smaller class of
\Pi
-absolutely continuous densities
p
that satisfy

\Phi(p)\leq\varepsilon
and denote this set by
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}
.
By replacing
\mathfrak{A}
with
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}
, we show that (
2
) can
be reformulated as the two-dimensional convex stochastic program

\min_{\lambda\geq 0,\,\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\;\left\{\mu+\varepsilon\lambda+(\lambda\Phi)^{*}(M_{N}-\mu)\right\},

(3)

where
(\lambda\Phi)^{*}
denotes the Fenchel conjugate of
\lambda\Phi
. In fact,
we prove an explicit form for the CGVI density that depends on the
optimal
\mu
and
\lambda
, and is related to convex subgradients
of
(\lambda\Phi)^{*}
.
In many practical cases, the CGVI density has a simple and computable form,
reducing the infinite-dimensional optimization problem (
2
)
to a two-dimensional convex problem, regardless of the concavity and
smoothness of
M_{N}
and the dimension of
\Theta
.

As mentioned above, the
\phi
-divergence approach to CGVI with
\phi
giving rise to the
KL divergence is closely related to the Gibbs posterior.
The Gibbs posterior was originally used to describe statistical
mechanics phenomena

(
Dobrušin 1968a
;
Dobrušin 1968b
;
Georgii 2011
;
Lanford and Ruelle 1969
)
,
but has recently been applied in the setting of information theory and
estimation
(
Zhang 2006a
;
Zhang 2006b
)

and Bayesian statistics
(
Bissiri et al. 2016
;
Jiang and Tanner 2008
)
.
The Gibbs posterior estimation problem associated with (
1
) is

\max_{p\in\mathfrak{D}}\;\sigma^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\left[pM_{N}\right]-D_{\rm KL}(p\|\pi)

(4)

where
\sigma>0
is the learning rate,
\mathfrak{D}
is
the set of probability density functions on
\Theta
with respect to the
prior measure
\Pi
, which is assumed to have density
\pi
, and

D_{\rm KL}(\cdot\|\cdot)
is the KL divergence. This
problem was originally proposed and analyzed in the pioneering works

(
Csiszár 1975
;
Donsker and Varadhan 1975
;
Zellner 1988
)
, which
considered variational representations of Bayesian inference. The optimal
solution to (
4
) is given in closed-form by

\bar{p}(\theta)=\frac{\exp\left(\sigma^{-1}M_{N}(\theta)\right)}{\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\left[\exp\left(\sigma^{-1}M_{N}\right)\right]}.

As seen in this expression, when the pay-off function
M_{N}
is a log-likelihood
function, the Gibbs posterior
\bar{p}
recovers the traditional Bayesian
posterior by setting
\sigma=1
and multiplying with the prior density
\pi
.
Note that (
2
) with the constraint
D_{\rm KL}(p\|\Pi)\leq\varepsilon

is a
constrained
optimization problem and (
4
) is an

unconstrained
problem. In fact, (
4
) can be thought
of as an approximation to (
2
) where the
D_{\rm KL}(p\|\pi)
term
penalizes for the inequality constraint.

The learning rate
\sigma
calibrates the influence of the likelihood or payoff
function on the posterior. A significant amount of effort has recently gone
into determining the “best” learning rate for a given problem. We refer the
reader to

(
Grünwald 2012
;
Grünwald and van Ommen 2018
;
Holmes and Walker 2017
;
Miller and Dunson 2019
;
Syring and Martin 2019
;
Wu and Martin 2020
)
,
the latter of which contains numerous related references and provides a
numerical study comparing the various perspectives. We will later observe
that the parameter
\lambda
in (
3
), which is the Lagrange
multiplier for the constraint
\Phi(p)\leq\varepsilon
, is an automatic
choice for
\sigma
when
\Phi
is the KL divergence.

Though the Gibbs posterior is well-studied, it was shown in

(
Knoblauch et al. 2019
;
Knoblauch 2019
)
that it may
not perform well under a misspecified prior, which is often considered the
rule and not the exception in machine learning. In fact, the papers

(
Knoblauch et al. 2019
;
Knoblauch 2019
)
show that
the Rényi divergence can outperform the KL divergence when the prior is
misspecified. This raises the question as to whether other

\phi
-divergences have similar properties or special uses. Though we do
not limit ourselves purely to
\phi
-divergences, these observations provide
further justification for our study.

The remaining sections are organized as follows. In Section
2.1
, we
introduce the basic notation and assumptions as well as some tools from convex
and functional analysis. Section
3
is dedicated to risk-averse
estimation. We begin by highlighting the connections between

\phi
-divergences and classical utility theory. Using the general theory
developed in the first subsection, we then prove the existence of CGVI densities
and the link between GVI and CGVI. We then demonstrate the existence of and
provide a general formula for CGVI densities in the case of the

\phi
-divergence. Afterwards, we discuss the connections to risk measures
and provide four explicit examples of
\phi
-divergence CGVI densities.
Following these theoretical results, we analyze the asymptotic consistency for
empirical approximations of CGVIs, including the effects of the large data limit
and sampling limit on the computable densities in Section
4
. We
conclude this section with a numerical example that demonstrates the ability of
CGVIs to outperform both traditional Bayesian posteriors and the Gibbs
posterior when the model and prior is misspecified. Finally, we conclude in
Section
5
by introducing an extension to “Empirical CGVI” for
situations in which we are unsure which utility function to choose, but for
which a large set of observations
Y
is available and perhaps a few
observations of
\theta
.

2
Background

In this section, we provide the required background information to analyze
GVI problems in the context of risk management. We first provide the
notation and problem assumptions used throughout this paper and then review
the concepts of
\phi
-divergence, regret and risk functionals.

2.1
Notation and Problem Assumptions

We denote by
\mathcal{B}\subseteq 2^{\Theta}
a
\sigma
-algebra on
\Theta
and by

\mathfrak{P}=\mathfrak{P}(\Theta,\mathcal{B})
the set of probability measures on measurable space

(\Theta,\mathcal{B})
. We assume that we have a
probability measure describing our prior knowledge of
\theta
, denoted by

\Pi\in\mathfrak{P}
and that the probability space
(\Theta,\mathcal{B},\Pi)
is complete.
For notational convenience, we denote the expectation with respect to
\Pi

simply by
\mathbb{E}
. For arbitrary
P\in\mathfrak{P}
, we denote the expectation with
respect to
P
by
\mathbb{E}_{P}
. In the subsequent discussion, we require the set of
probability density functions with respect to the prior measure
\Pi
, which
we denote by

\mathfrak{D}\triangleq\{\,p:\Theta\to[0,\infty]\,|\,p\;\text{is $\mathcal{B}$-measurable and }\mathbb{E}[p]=1\}\subset L^{1}(\Theta,\mathcal{B},\Pi).

In particular, for every
P\in\mathfrak{P}
that is absolutely continuous with respect
to
\Pi
(denoted
P\ll\Pi
), there exists
p\in\mathfrak{D}
such that

\mathrm{d}P=p\,\mathrm{d}\Pi
, i.e.,
p
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of

P
with respect to
\Pi
. After analyzing the GVI problem through the lense of
risk management, we introduce a related GVI problem, which we call coherent
GVI or CGVI for short, of the form

\max_{P\in\mathfrak{A}}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[M_{N}\right],

(5)

where
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}
is an appropriately selected subset of probability
measures.
Clearly, if
\mathfrak{A}=\{\Pi\}
, then the maximizing measure is
\Pi

(i.e., full confidence in the prior). On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{P}
, then
the maximizing measures are convex combinations of point masses centered at the
maximizers to (
1
) (i.e., no confidence in the prior)

(
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Th. 7.37)
. The key to CGVI
is in determining reasonable and application-relevant classes of probability
measures
\mathfrak{A}
, which can be done using utility theory and risk management.
One common and inuitive form for
\mathfrak{A}
is

\mathfrak{A}=\{\,P\in\mathfrak{P}\,|\,D(P\|\Pi)\leq\varepsilon\,\},\quad\varepsilon\geq 0,

(6)

where
D:\mathfrak{P}\times\mathfrak{P}\to[0,\infty]
is a statistical metric or divergence

(
Rachev 1991
)
. In words, (
5
) seeks a maximizer of the
total expected pay-off over the set of probabilities measures that are within

\varepsilon
of the prior measure
\Pi
. Note that if
\varepsilon=0
, then

\mathfrak{A}=\{\Pi\}
and if
\varepsilon=\infty
, then
\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{P}
.

In the subsequent section, we require the following techincal definitions.
We denote by
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{M}
two Banach spaces of
\mathcal{B}
-measurable
functions from
\Theta
into
\mathbb{R}
for which

\mathbb{E}[|pX|]<\infty\quad\forall\,p\in\mathcal{L},\;X\in\mathcal{M}.

We assume that
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{M}
contain all simple functions on
\Theta

and we associate with
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{L}
the bilinear form

\langle p,X\rangle\triangleq\mathbb{E}[pX]
for
p\in\mathcal{L}
and
X\in\mathcal{M}
, making

(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
paired topological vector spaces. We
denote the topological dual space of
\mathcal{L}
by
\mathcal{L}^{*}
and similarly for

\mathcal{M}
. We abuse notation and denote the canonical embedding of
\mathcal{L}
into

\mathcal{M}^{*}
simply by
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{*}
and the canonical embedding of

\mathcal{M}
into
\mathcal{L}^{*}
by
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{*}
. We denote the weak topology
on
\mathcal{M}
induced by
\mathcal{L}
by
\sigma(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L})
and the weak topology on

\mathcal{L}
induced by
\mathcal{M}
by
\sigma(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M})
. We will restrict this
abstract setting to two situations:
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
and
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{L}^{*}
, the
latter presenting additional, significant, theoretical complications. The
first case represents a majority of use cases such as

\mathcal{M}=L^{s}(\Theta,\mathcal{B},\Pi)
with
s\in[1,+\infty)
or
\mathcal{M}
is an
appropriately defined Orlicz space. The second case covers the situation when

\mathcal{M}=L^{\infty}(\Theta,\mathcal{B},\Pi)
is paired with
\mathcal{L}=L^{1}(\Theta,\mathcal{B},\Pi)

endowed with the weak
∗
topology.

Throughout, we require a handful of common concepts from convex and variational
analysis. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Banach space with topological dual space

\mathcal{X}^{*}
and duality pairing

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathcal{X}}
. The Fenchel conjugate
of the function
f:\mathcal{X}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is defined by

f^{*}(\eta)\triangleq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\{\langle\eta,x\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathcal{X}}-f(x)\}\quad\forall\,\eta\in\mathcal{X}^{*}

and we employ the convention that the Fenchel conjugate of a function

g:\mathcal{X}^{*}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is defined on
\mathcal{X}
rather than
on all of
\mathcal{X}^{**}
. The function
f
is said to be proper if

f(x)>-\infty
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and there exists
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}

such that
f(x_{0})<\infty
. We denote the effective domain of
f
by

\mathrm{dom}\,f\triangleq\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,|\,f(x)<\infty\}
.
The function
f
is said to be closed with respect to a topology on

\mathcal{X}
(e.g., weak or strong) if its epigraph,

\mathrm{epi}\,f\triangleq\{(x,t)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}\,|\,f(x)\leq t\}
,
is closed in the respective product topology on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
.
Recall that if
f
is convex, then the subdifferential of
f
at

x\in\mathcal{X}
is defined by

\partial f(x)\triangleq\{\eta\in\mathcal{X}^{*}\,|\,f(x^{\prime})-f(x)\geq\langle\eta,x^{\prime}-x\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathcal{X}}\;\;\forall\,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\}.

If
f
is proper, closed and convex, then
f=f^{**}
, where
f^{**}
denotes
the Fenchel conjugate of
f^{*}
. Moreover, the Fenchel-Young inequality states
that

f(x)+f^{*}(\eta)\geq\langle\eta,x\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathcal{X}}

(7)

and equality holds in (
7
) if and only if
\eta\in\partial f(x)
,
or equivalently if
x\in\partial f^{*}(\eta)
. Finally, for a set

C\subset\mathcal{X}
, we denote by
\delta_{C}:\mathcal{X}\to[-\infty,+\infty]

the indicator function of
C
and by

\sigma_{C}:\mathcal{X}^{*}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
the support function of
C
. That
is,

\delta_{C}(x)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if $x\in C$}\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{C}(\eta)\triangleq\sup_{x\in C}\;\langle\eta,x\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathcal{X}}.

2.2
Connections Between the
\phi
-Divergence and Risk Management

In this subsection, we review disutility (or regret) functions and their
close relation to the
\phi
-divergence
(
Csiszár 1963
;
Morimoto 1963
)
.
We say that a function
v:\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is a

risk-averse disutility function
if it is convex, increasing, and
satisfies
v(x)\geq x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
v(x)=x
implies
x=0
. Two
popular risk-averse disutility functions are the exponential disutility
function

v(x)=\frac{(\exp(rx)-1)}{r},\quad r>0,

(8)

and the isoelastic disutility function

v(x)=\left(1+\frac{x}{\beta}\right)^{\beta}-1,\quad\beta<0.

(9)

Since
v
is increasing, convex, and satisfies
v(x)\geq x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}

and
v(0)=0
, we have that its Fenchel conjugate
v^{*}
is convex,
v^{*}(1)=0

and
v^{*}(t)=+\infty
for all
t<0
.
These properties of
v^{*}
are exactly those required of
\phi
-divergence
functions. In particular, given a function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]
that is
convex and satisfies
\phi(1)=0
and
\phi(t)=+\infty
for all
t<0
, we can
define the
\phi
-divergence of a probability measure
P\in\mathfrak{P}
from the prior

\Pi
by

D_{\phi}(P\|\Pi)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{E}[\phi(p)]&\text{if $P\ll\Pi$ and $p\,\mathrm{d}\Pi=\mathrm{d}P$}\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

One common challenge when using a
\phi
-divergence for GVI is in choosing

\phi

(
Knoblauch et al. 2019
)
—a task that is often
difficult to conceptualize. Given the close ties between
\phi
and a
disutility function
v
, we suggest first choosing
v
and then defining
\phi

as
\phi=v^{*}
. Often, the concept of disutility is more intuitive and can be
tailored to the underlying application.

A popular approach in economic utility theory is to choose disutility functions
that exhibit certain curvature properties such as constant absolute risk
aversion (CARA) in the sense of Arrow and Pratt

(
Arrow 1970
;
Pratt 1964
)
. To achieve CARA, one first defines the
absolute risk-aversion coefficient

A(x)=\frac{v^{\prime\prime}(x)}{v^{\prime}(x)}

and then generates a CARA disutility function by solving the ordinary
differential equation (ODE)
A(x)=c
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. A related
concept is constant relative risk aversion (CRRA), where the relative
risk-aversion coefficient is given by

R(x)=xA(x).

Similarly, one can generate a CRRA disutility function by solving the ODE

R(x)=c
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Recall that the exponential disutility
function satisfies CARA while the isoelastic disutility function satisfies
CRRA.
See Figure
1
for common
disutility functions and their associated
\phi
-divergence functions. We note
that the exponential disutility function generates the KL divergence, the
isoelastic disutility generates the Rényi divergence, and the quadratic
disutility function generates the
\chi^{2}
divergence. The fourth disutility
function in Figure
1
is piecewise linear and is not risk
averse. However, it is of interest since it generates the total variation
divergence.

\phi
-divergences: a) the exponential disutility (exp.) and Kullback-Leibler

For the purpose of our theoretical results, and to facilitate the relationship
between GVI and risk measures, we work in a slightly more general setting than
traditional utility theory. We say that the functional

\mathcal{V}:\mathcal{M}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is a

risk-averse expected disutility function
if it satisfies:

(V1)

Convexity:

\mathcal{V}
is proper,
\sigma(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L})
-lower semicontinuous and convex;

(V2)

Risk Aversion:
For any
X\in\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{V}(X)\geq\mathbb{E}[X]
and
\mathcal{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X]
implies
X\equiv 0
;

(V3)

Monotonicity:
If
X,\,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
satisfy
X\leq X^{\prime}
a.s., then
\mathcal{V}(X)\geq\mathcal{V}(X^{\prime})
.

Commonly,
\mathcal{V}
has the form

\mathcal{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}[v(X)],

where
v:\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is a risk-averse disutility function.
According to
von Neumann and Morgenstern 2007
, any “rational” decision
maker has an expected disutility function that characterizes their decision
when faced with risky outcomes. In their theory, the decision maker’s
preference (denoted by the binary relationships
\prec
,
\succ
and
\sim
)
follows four axioms when deciding between any three random outcomes
X
,
Y
,
and
Z
:

•

Completeness:
Either
X\prec Y
,
X\succ Y
or
X\sim Y
;

•

Transitivity:
If
X\prec Y
(
X\sim Y
) and
Y\prec Z
(
Y\sim Z
), then
X\preceq Z
(
X\sim Z
);

•

Continuity:
If
X\prec Y\prec Z
, then there exists

t\in[0,1]
such that
tX+(1-t)Z\sim M
;

•

Independence:
If
X\prec Y
, then
tX+(1-t)Z\preceq tY+(1-t)Z
for all
t\in(0,1]
.

Although the von Neumann-Morgenstern theory ensures the existence of an
expected disutility function, it does not provide a constructive means to
determine the disutility function. However, there have been attempts to
systematically select a decision maker’s disutility function

(
Warren 2019
)
.

Analogously, we can generalize the
\phi
-divergence using the expected
disutility functional
\mathcal{V}
as
\Phi(p)=\mathcal{V}^{*}(p)
. Our next result lists the
properties of
\Phi
and demonstrates that
\Phi
in fact defines a statistical
divergence.

Proposition 1

Let the regret functional
\mathcal{V}:\mathcal{M}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
satisfy (V1)–(V3).
Then, the functional
\Phi:\mathcal{L}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
defined by

\Phi(p)\triangleq\mathcal{V}^{*}(p)

is proper,
\sigma(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M})
-lower semicontinuous, convex and satisfies
the following properties:

1.

\Phi(p)\geq 0
for all
p\in\mathcal{L}
;

2.

\Phi(p)=0
if
p\equiv 1
a.s.;

3.

\Phi(p)<\infty
implies
p\geq 0
a.s.;

4.

If
\partial\mathcal{V}(0)=\{1\}
, then
\Phi(p)=0
implies
p\equiv 1
a.s.;

5.

If
\partial\mathcal{V}(0)=\{1\}
, then

\mathrm{dom}\,\Phi=\{1\}

if and only if
\mathcal{V}
is positively homogeneous.

Proof

\Phi
is proper,
\sigma(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M})
-lower semicontinuous and convex by
the definition of the Fenchel conjugate
(
Rockafellar 1971
, pg. 188)
.
Property 1 follows from the Fenchel-Young inequality and the fact that

\mathcal{V}(0)=0
, i.e.,

\Phi(p)=\Phi(p)+\mathcal{V}(0)=\Phi(p)+\Phi^{*}(0)\geq\mathbb{E}[0p]=0\quad\forall\,p\in\mathcal{L}.

(10)

Property 2 follows from (U2). That is,
X\mapsto\mathbb{E}[X]-\mathcal{V}(X)
has a
unique maximizer at
X\equiv 0
with maximum value 0, and hence

\Phi(1)=\mathcal{V}^{*}(1)=0
.
Property 3 follows from (U2) and (U3). First notice that

\mathcal{V}(X)\leq\mathcal{V}(0)=0\quad\forall\,X\leq 0\;\;\text{a.s.}

and let
p\in\mathcal{L}
be such that

\Theta_{p}=\{\theta\in\Theta\,|\,p(\theta)<0\}

has positive probability with respect to the prior, i.e.,
\Pi(\Theta_{p})>0
.
By setting

X_{p}(\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-1&\text{if $\theta\in\Theta_{p}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.,

we see that
X_{p}\in\mathcal{M}
since
\mathcal{M}
includes all simple functions and

\mathbb{E}[tX_{p}p]-\mathcal{V}(tX_{p})\geq t\mathbb{E}[X_{p}p]>0\quad\forall\,t>0.

Taking the supremum in
t>0
demonstrates that
\Phi(p)=+\infty
, proving
property 3.
For property 4, the Fenchel-Young inequality (
10
) holds with
equality if and only if

0\in\partial\Phi(p)\quad\iff\quad p\in\partial\mathcal{V}(0).

and the desired result follows since
\partial\mathcal{V}(0)=\{1\}
by assumption.
Finally, to prove property 5, we first note that if

\mathrm{dom}\,\Phi=\{1\}
, then

\Phi
is the indicator function of
\{1\}
and hence

\mathcal{V}(X)=\Phi^{*}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X]
, which is positively homogeneous. On the other
hand, if
\mathcal{V}
is positively homogeneous, then
\Phi
is an indicator
function. Since
\Phi(p)>0
unless
p\equiv 1
a.s., we must have that

\Phi(p)=\infty
unless
p\equiv 1
a.s.

Proposition
1
demonstrates that any risk-averse expected
disutility function
\mathcal{V}
that satisfies
\partial\mathcal{V}(0)=\{1\}
, defines the
statistical divergence

D_{\Phi}(P\|\Pi)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Phi(p)=\mathcal{V}^{*}(p)&\text{if $P\ll\Pi$ and $p\,\mathrm{d}\Pi=\mathrm{d}P$ with $p\in\mathcal{L}$}\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

D_{\Phi}
clearly generalizes the
\phi
-divergence since we can define

\Phi(p)=\mathbb{E}[\phi(p)]
with
\phi(t)=v^{*}(t)
and
\mathcal{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}[v(X)]
.

2.3
Risk Measures and Connections to Expected Disutility

A prinicpal contribution of this work is the demonstration of connections
between GVI and risk measures. Risk measures are functionals used to quantify
the overall loss or hazard associated with a random outcome. The concept of a
risk measure is also closely linked to utility theory. In fact, given an
expected disutility function
\mathcal{V}
, we can define the risk measure

\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{M}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
projected from
\mathcal{V}
by

\mathcal{R}(X)\triangleq\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\;\{t+\mathcal{V}(X-t)\}.

(11)

The class of risk measures projected from expected disutility functions as in
(
11
) form a so-called risk quadrangle

(
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
)
and are generalizations of optimized
certainty equivalents (OCE)

(
Ben-Tal and Teboulle 1986
;
Ben-Tal and Teboulle 2007
)
. In particualar,
an OCE is a risk measure given by (
11
) with
\mathcal{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}[v(X)]
,
where
v
is a disutility function. It is common to require that a risk
measure
\mathcal{R}
satisfies some or all of the following axioms:

(R1)

Convexity:

\mathcal{R}
is proper,
\sigma(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L})
-lower semicontinuous and convex;

(R2)

Monotonicity:
If
X,\,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
satisfy
X\leq X^{\prime}
a.s., then
\mathcal{R}(X)\geq\mathcal{R}(X^{\prime})
;

(R3)

Translation Equivariance:

\mathcal{R}(X+t)=\mathcal{R}(X)+t
for all
X\in\mathcal{M}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
;

(R4)

Positive Homogeneity:

\mathcal{R}(tX)=t\mathcal{R}(X)
for all
X\in\mathcal{M}
and
t\geq 0
.

If
\mathcal{R}
satisfies (R1)–(R3), it is referred to as a
convex
risk measure

(
Föllmer and Schied 2002
)
and if it satisfies (R1)–(R4), it is

coherent

(
Artzner et al. 1999
)
. Coherent risk
measures satisfy the worst-case expectation representation

\mathcal{R}(X)=\sup_{\eta\in\mathfrak{A}}\;\langle\eta,X\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}},

(12)

where the
risk envelope

\mathfrak{A}\subset\mathcal{M}^{*}
is given by

\mathfrak{A}\triangleq\mathrm{dom}\,\mathcal{R}^{*}=\partial\mathcal{R}(0)
. Axiom (R1) ensures that
\mathfrak{A}
is
nonempty, closed and convex, while (R2) ensures that all
\eta\in\mathfrak{A}

define nonnegative measures. In particular, let
B\in\mathcal{B}
be such that

\langle\eta,\mathbbm{1}_{B}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}<0
, where
\mathbbm{1}_{B}

denotes the characteristic function of
B
, i.e.,
\mathbbm{1}_{B}(\theta)=1

if
\theta\in B
and
\mathbbm{1}_{B}(\theta)=0
if
\theta\not\in B
. Then,
for any
X\in\mathrm{dom}\,\mathcal{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}(\eta)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{t\in[0,+\infty)}\;\{\langle\eta,X-t\mathbbm{1}_{B}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-\mathcal{R}(X-t\mathbbm{1}_{B})\}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{t\in[0,+\infty)}\;\{\langle\eta,X\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-\mathcal{R}(X)-t\langle\eta,\mathbbm{1}_{B}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}\}=+\infty.

Axiom (R3) ensures that
\eta\in\mathfrak{A}
satisfies

\langle\eta,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}=1
. To see this, we have that for all

X\in\mathrm{dom}\,\mathcal{R}
and
\eta\in\mathfrak{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}(\eta)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\,\{\langle\eta,X+t\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-\mathcal{R}(X+t)\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\,\{\langle\eta,X\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-\mathcal{R}(X)+t(\langle\eta,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-1)\}.

Consequently,
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\eta)=+\infty
unless

\langle\eta,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}=1
. Hence, the risk envelope
\mathfrak{A}
is
a subset of probability measures in
\mathcal{M}^{*}
. Finally, owing to (R4),
\mathcal{R}^{*}

is the indicator function of
\mathfrak{A}
. In general, we define the

risk identifiers

p
at
X\in\mathcal{M}
as the subgradients of
\mathcal{R}
at
X
,
i.e.,
p\in\partial\mathcal{R}(X)\subset\mathfrak{A}
. As a consequence of the Fenchel-Young
inequality, the risk identifiers of a coherent risk measure
\mathcal{R}
are the
maximizers on the right-hand side of (
12
).

Returning to the generalized OCE (
11
), it is straightfoward to
show that
\mathcal{R}
is a convex risk measure if
\mathcal{V}
is an risk-averse expected
disutility function. Moreover, it is coherent if and only if
\mathcal{V}
is
positively homogeneous. To conclude this discussion, we prove fundamental
relations between risk identifiers and subgradients of the regret function

\mathcal{V}
as well as the Fenchel conjugates
\mathcal{R}^{*}
and
\mathcal{V}^{*}
.

Lemma 2

Let
\mathcal{R}
be defined by (
11
), where
\mathcal{V}
is a risk-averse
expected disutility function, and let
X\in\mathcal{M}
be arbitrary.
Denote by
\bar{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, a solution to the optimization problem defining

\mathcal{R}(X)
, i.e.,

\mathcal{R}(X)=\bar{t}+\mathcal{V}(X-\bar{t})
.
Then, we have that

\partial\mathcal{R}(X)=\partial\mathcal{V}(X-\bar{t})\cap\{p\in\mathcal{M}^{*}\,|\,\langle p,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}=1\}.

Proof

Let
p\in\partial\mathcal{R}(X)
, then
\langle p,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}=1
by (R3)
and for all
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\{t+\mathcal{V}(X^{\prime}-t)\}-\{\bar{t}+\mathcal{V}(X-\bar{t})\}\geq\mathcal{R}(X^{\prime})-\mathcal{R}(X)\geq\langle p,(X^{\prime}-X)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}.

Consequently, subtracting
(t-\bar{t})
from both sides yields

\mathcal{V}(X^{\prime}-t)-\mathcal{V}(X-\bar{t})\geq\langle p,(X^{\prime}-t)-(X-\bar{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}\quad\iff\quad p\in\partial\mathcal{V}(X-\bar{t}).

On the other hand, let
p\in\partial\mathcal{V}(X-\bar{t})
satisfy

\langle p,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}=1
. By definition, we have that

\mathcal{V}(X^{\prime}-t)-\mathcal{V}(X-\bar{t})\geq\langle p,(X^{\prime}-t)-(X-\bar{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}=\langle p,(X^{\prime}-X)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}+(\bar{t}-t)

for all
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Subtracting
(\bar{t}-t)
from both
sides yields

t+\mathcal{V}(X^{\prime}-t)-\mathcal{R}(X)\geq\langle p,(X^{\prime}-X)\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}

and passing to the infimum over
t\in\mathbb{R}
on the left-hand side proves the
desired result.

Lemma 3

Let
\mathcal{R}
be defined by (
11
), where
\mathcal{V}
is a risk-averse
expected disutility function. Then,
\mathcal{R}^{*}
is given by

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\eta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{V}^{*}(\eta)=\Phi(\eta)&\text{if $\langle\eta,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}=1$}\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

Proof

Using (
11
), we arrive at the following equality

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{X\in\mathcal{M},\,t\in\mathbb{R}}\;\{\langle\eta,X\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-\mathcal{R}(X-t)\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{Y\in\mathcal{M},\,t\in\mathbb{R}}\;\{\langle\eta,Y\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-\mathcal{V}(Y)+t(\langle\eta,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-1)\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}^{*}(\eta)+\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\;t(\langle\eta,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-1).

The desired result then follows since the supremum in the final equality is
equal to
+\infty
unless
\langle\eta,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}=1
.

3
Generalized Variational Inference as Risk Identification

In this section, we analyze the GVI problem

\sup_{p\in\mathfrak{D}}\;\{\mathbb{E}[pM_{N}]-\sigma\Phi(p)\},

(13)

where
\Phi
is a generalized
\phi
-divergence as described in
Proposition
1
. In particular, our analysis provides
necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of solutions to
(
13
), which we refer to as
variational densities
, and
transforms the task of solving the optimization problem (
13
) into
computing subgradients of a risk measure—a task that is generally simpler
than optimization over probability density functions. We then introduce a new
GVI problem, which we call coherent GVI (CGVI). CGVI is closely linked to
coherent risk measures and again can be transformed into the problem of
computing a risk identifer.

3.1
Existence and Asymptotics of Variational Densities

In this subsection, we provide necesary and sufficient conditions for the
existence of solutions to the GVI problem (
13
). In doing so, we
relate the optimization problem (
13
) to evaluating a generalized OCE
and as a result of the Fenchel-Young inequality, show that the variational
densities in (
13
) are subgradients of that OCE. The following
result provides this characterization.

Theorem 4
(Existence of Variational Densities)

Let the generalized
\phi
-divergence
\Phi
be defined using the risk-averse
expected disutility function
\mathcal{V}
as in Proposition
1
, and
let
\mathcal{R}
denote the risk measure projected from
\mathcal{V}
as in
(
11
). Then, the GVI problem (
13
) has a solution if
and only if

\partial\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}M_{N})\cap\mathcal{L}\neq\emptyset

and the variational density is a risk identifier of
\sigma^{-1}M_{N}
, i.e.,

\bar{p}\in\partial\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}M_{N})\cap\mathcal{L}.

Proof

We first notice that (
13
) is the Fenchel conjugate of

\sigma\Phi+\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}
. Consequently, the optimal value in
(
13
) is the infimal convolution of the Fenchel conjugates

(\sigma\Phi)^{*}
and
\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}^{*}
. That is, the optimal value in
(
13
) is given by

\inf_{Y\in\mathcal{M}}\;\{(\sigma\Phi)^{*}(M_{N}-Y)+\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}^{*}(Y)\}.

(14)

Using the definition of
\Phi
, we have that

(\sigma\Phi)^{*}(Y)=\sigma\mathcal{V}(\sigma^{-1}Y)\triangleq\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}(Y)
. Moreover, the
Fenchel conjugate of
\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}
is the support function of
\mathfrak{D}
, i.e.,

\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}^{*}(Y)=\sigma_{\mathfrak{D}}(Y)=\sup_{p\in\mathfrak{D}}\mathbb{E}[pY]=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}Y,

where the last equality follows since
\mathfrak{D}
consists of all probability
density functions. Additionally, since
\mathcal{V}
is monotonic and
Y\leq\operatorname*{ess\,sup}Y

for all
Y\in\mathcal{M}
, we have that

\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}(M_{N}-Y)\geq\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}(M_{N}-\operatorname*{ess\,sup}Y)\quad\forall\,Y\in\mathcal{M}

and therefore, the random variable
Y
in (
14
) can be replaced by a
degenerate random variable (i.e., a scalar). The optimal value in
(
14
) (and hence (
13
)) can thus be equivalently written as

\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(M_{N})\triangleq\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\;\{t+\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}(M_{N}-t)\}=\sigma\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}(M_{N}-t)),

(15)

where
\mathcal{R}
is the risk measure defined in (
11
). As a result
of the Fenchel-Young inequality, we have that

\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(M_{N})=\langle p,M_{N}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}-\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}^{*}(p)\quad\forall\,p\in\partial\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(M_{N}).

Since
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(\cdot)=\sigma\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}\cdot)
, we have
that
\partial\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(M_{N})=\partial\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}M_{N})
as desired.

Remark 5
(Nonexistence)

In general, the estimation problem (
13
) need not have a solution
in
\mathcal{L}
since
\partial\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}M_{N})\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{*}
. However,
Theorem
12
guarantees that solutions exist when

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
and
\partial\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}M_{N})\neq\emptyset
.

The following two corollaries to Theorem
4
provide
sufficient conditions for existence of variational densities to
(
13
) when
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
as discussed in Remark
5
.

Corollary 6

In the setting of Theorem
4
, if
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
and

\mathcal{R}
is finite valued and continuous at
\sigma^{-1}M_{N}
, then
(
13
) has a solution.

Proof

Under the stated assumptions,

\partial\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}M_{N})=\partial\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}M_{N})\cap\mathcal{L}\neq\emptyset

(
Ekeland and Temam 1999
, Prop. 5.2)
and the result of
Theorem
4
ensures the existence of solutions.

Corollary 7

In the setting of Theorem
4
, if
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
and

\mathcal{V}
is finite valued, then (
13
) has a solution.

Proof

Since
\mathcal{V}
is finite valued,
\mathcal{R}
is also finite valued since

\mathcal{R}(Y)\leq\mathcal{V}(Y)
for all
Y\in\mathcal{M}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{R}
is continuous on

\mathcal{M}

(
Ekeland and Temam 1999
, Cor. 2.5)
. The corollary then follows
from Corollary
6
.

Theorem
4
transforms the optimization problem
(
13
) into a subdifferentiation problem involving the risk measure

\mathcal{R}
projected from the risk-averse expected disutility function
\mathcal{V}
.
Giving the connection between
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{V}
, it is possible to rewrite the
condition
p\in\partial\mathcal{R}(\sigma^{-1}M_{N})
in terms of
\mathcal{V}
.

Proposition 8

Consider the setting of Theorem
4
and let
\bar{t}
be
a minimizer in the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(M_{N})
, i.e.,

\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(M_{N})=\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\;\{t+\sigma\mathcal{V}(\sigma^{-1}(M_{N}-t))\}.

(16)

Then, any solution to (
13
) satisfies

\bar{p}\in\partial\mathcal{V}(\sigma^{-1}(M_{N}-\bar{t}))\cap\{p\in\mathcal{L}\,|\,\mathbb{E}[p]=1\}.

Proof

This result follows directly from Theorem
4
and
Lemma
2
applied to
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
.

The final result in this section concerns the large data limit (i.e., as

N\to+\infty
) and presents a type of law of large numbers result for the
variational densities
p_{N}
associated with the payoff function
M_{N}
.

Theorem 9
(Asymptotic Consistency)

Consider the setting of Theorem
4
. Let

\{\sigma_{N}\}\subset(0,\infty]
with
\sigma_{N}\geq\sigma_{0}>0
for all
N

and
\{M_{N}\}
satisfy
M_{N}\in\mathcal{M}
a.s. Further, suppose that there
exists
p_{N}\in\partial\mathcal{R}(\sigma_{N}^{-1}M_{N})\cap\mathcal{L}
a.s. for all
N
and
that
\sigma_{N}^{-1}M_{N}\to M_{\star}
in
\mathcal{M}
a.s., i.e.,

\|\sigma_{N}^{-1}M_{N}-M_{\star}\|_{\mathcal{M}}\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

Then, any
\sigma(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M})
-accumulation point,
p_{\star}
, of the variational
densities
\{p_{N}\}
satisfies

p_{\star}\in\partial\mathcal{R}(M_{\star})\cap\mathcal{L}\quad\text{a.s.}

In particular,
p_{\star}
is a variational density for the asymptotic GVI
problem

\max_{p\in\mathfrak{D}}\;\{\mathbb{E}[pM_{\star}]-\Phi(p)\}.

Proof

Outside of a null set, this result follows from

(
Clarke 1998
, Prop. 2.1.5(b))
.

Remark 10
(Uniform Law of Large Numbers)

In Theorem
9
,
M_{\star}
is typically given by

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}[m(\cdot,y)]
, where
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}
denotes the expectation
with respect to the probability law of the noisy data
Y
and
M_{N}=m(\cdot,y_{N})

are generated by identically distributed realizations
y_{N}
of
Y
. In this
setting, the requirement that
\sigma_{N}^{-1}M_{N}\to M_{\star}
in
\mathcal{M}
a.s. is related to the uniform law of large numbers (LLN). In particular, if the
uniform LLN holds and
\sigma_{N}\to\sigma_{\star}
, then

\sigma_{N}^{-1}M_{N}\to M_{\star}
also holds. This is the case if, e.g.,
\mathcal{M}

is separable,
M_{N}
and
m(\cdot,Y)
are independent and identically distributed
\mathcal{M}
-valued
random variables, and
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}[\|m(\cdot,y)\|_{\psi^{\#}}]<\infty

(
Giné and Nickl 2016
, Cor. 3.7.21)
.
This is also the case if, e.g.,
\Theta
is a compact subset of a Euclidean
space,
\Upsilon
is a Euclidean space,
m
is a Carathèodory function
and
m(\theta,\cdot)
is dominated by an integrable function that is
independent of
\theta

(
Jennrich 1969
, Th. 2)
.

3.2
Coherent Generalized Variational Inference

The risk measure
\mathcal{R}
projected from
\mathcal{V}
as in (
11
)
satisifies (R1)–(R3). However, it need not be coherent unless
\mathcal{V}
is
positively homogeneous, in which case
\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{R}
.
That is, the learning rate
\sigma^{-1}
has no effect on the estimation.
This is a desirable property since choosing
\sigma
can be difficult.
In contrast to requiring
\mathcal{V}
to be positively homogeneous, we can modify

\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
in the following way. Given
\varepsilon\geq 0
, we define the
risk measure

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(X)\triangleq\inf_{\lambda>0}\;\{\lambda\varepsilon+\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(X)\}

(17)

The risk measure
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
inherits all properties from
\mathcal{R}
with the
addition that it is positively homogeneous, and hence coherent. Since

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
is coherent, it satisfies

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(X)=\sup_{p\in\mathfrak{A}}\;\mathbb{E}[pX],

(18)

where the risk envelope
\mathfrak{A}
is given by

\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}\triangleq\{p\in\mathfrak{D}\,|\,\Phi(p)\leq\varepsilon\},

(19)

where
\Phi=\mathcal{V}^{*}
is the generalized
\phi
-divergence associated with the
regret functional
\mathcal{V}
. We refer to the optimization problem on the
right-hand side of (
18
) with
X=M_{N}
as the coherent GVI
(CGVI) problem. Intuitively, the CGVI problem seeks a density function
p

that maximizes the expected payoff
M_{N}
within
\varepsilon
of the prior
distribution, when measured by the generalized
\phi
-divergence
\Phi
.
For example, if
\mathcal{V}
is the exponential disutility function, then

\mathcal{R}(X)=\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(X)]
is the entropic risk measure and
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}

is the entropic value-at-risk. Moreover,
\Phi=\mathcal{V}^{*}
generates the KL
divergence. Consequently, (
18
) seeks a probability
density function that maximizes the expected payoff and is within
\varepsilon

of
\Pi
when measured by the KL divergence. An obvious extension is to
replace
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
with some arbitrary coherent risk measure, not
necessarily of the form (
17
). This extension leads to the
interpretation of CGVI as finding a density that maximizes the expected payoff
from the set of admissible densities
\mathfrak{A}
. However, we will restrict our
attention to (
17
) given it’s close ties with GVI.

The CGVI problem presents a trade-off between selecting the learning rate

\sigma^{-1}
as in GVI and selecting the
\phi
-diveregence tolerance

\varepsilon
. Both tasks can be challenging, however, choosing
\varepsilon

may be more intuitive in certain applications. For example, if one can sample
from the Bayesian posterior
p_{\rm post}
, then we can choose

\varepsilon>\Phi(p_{\rm post})
, ensuring that the CGVI density
\bar{p}

improves upon the traditional Bayesian solution. In addition, if an intuitive
method for choosing
\varepsilon
exists, then the CGVI formulation determines
an optimal inverse learning rate by solving for
\lambda
in the definition
of
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
.

Our first result proves that the risk envelope is indeed given by
(
19
).

Proposition 11

The risk envelope of
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon\geq 0
is given
by (
19
).

Proof

First, notice that for any
p\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
, we have

(\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon})^{*}(p)=\sup_{X\in\mathcal{M},\,\lambda>0}\{\mathbb{E}[pX]-\lambda\varepsilon-\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(X)\}=\sup_{\lambda>0}\lambda(\mathcal{R}^{*}(p)-\varepsilon).

Consequently,
(\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon})^{*}(p)
is finite if and only if

\Phi(p)\leq\varepsilon
and
\langle p,1\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}=1
.
Finally,
p\in\mathfrak{A}
satisfies
p\geq 0
a.s. since
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}

is monotonic as demonstrated in Section
2.3
.

Proposition
11
provides the intuitive interpretation of
CGVI: maximize the expected payoff while being with
\varepsilon
of the
prior. Analogous to Theorem
4
, the following result
provides a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of variational
densities.

Theorem 12
(Existence of Variational Densities)

Let the generalized
\phi
-divergence
\Phi
be defined using the risk-averse
expected disutility function
\mathcal{V}
as in Proposition
1
and
let
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
be defined as in (
17
) using the risk
measure
\mathcal{R}
projected from
\mathcal{V}
as in (
11
). Then, the CGVI
problem is given by

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(M_{N})=\sup_{p\in\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}}\;\mathbb{E}[pM_{N}]=\min_{\mu\in\mathbb{R},\,\lambda\geq 0}\;\{\mu+\lambda\varepsilon+(\lambda\Phi)^{*}(M_{N}-\mu)\},

(20)

and a variational density exists if and only if

\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(M_{N})\cap\mathcal{L}\neq\emptyset.

Moreover, the variational densities are given by

\bar{p}\in\partial\mathcal{V}(\bar{\lambda}^{-1}(M_{N}-\bar{\mu}))\cap\{p\in\mathcal{L}\,|\,\mathbb{E}[p]=1\},

where
(\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})
solve the minimization problem
on the right-hand side of (
20
).

Proof

The proof of this fact follows from the Fenchel-Young inequality,
similar to the proof of Theorem
4
.

As in Remark
5
, solutions to (
20
) may not exist
if
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{L}^{*}
, even if
\partial^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}(M_{N})\neq\emptyset
. The
following two corollaries to Theorem
12
provide sufficient
conditions for existence of variational densities to (
20
) when

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
.

Corollary 13

In the setting of Theorem
12
, if
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
and

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
is finite valued and continuous at
M_{N}
, then
(
20
) has a solution.

Proof

The proof of this fact is analogous to the proof of
Corollary
6
.

Corollary 14

In the setting of Theorem
12
, if
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
and
\mathcal{V}
is
finite valued, then (
13
) has a solution.

Proof

The proof of this fact is analogous to the proof of
Corollary
7
.

The final result in this section concerns the large data limit (i.e., as

N\to+\infty
) and presents type of law of large numbers for the CGVI densities

p_{N}
associated with the payoff function
M_{N}
.

Theorem 15
(Asymptotic Consistency)

Consider the setting of Theorem
12
and suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is
finite valued on
\mathcal{M}
. Let
\{\varepsilon_{N}\}\subset[0,+\infty)

and
\{M_{N}\}
satisfy
M_{N}\in\mathcal{M}
a.s. Furthermore, suppose
that there exists
p_{N}\in\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon_{N}}(M_{N})\cap\mathcal{L}
a.s. for
all
N
, that
\varepsilon_{N}\to\varepsilon\geq 0
and that
M_{N}\to M_{\star}
in

\mathcal{M}
a.s. Then, any
\sigma(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M})
-accumulation point,
p_{\star}
, of the
variational densities
\{p_{N}\}
satisfies

p_{\star}\in\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(M_{\star})\cap\mathcal{L}.

In particular,
p_{\star}
solves the asymptotic CGVI problem

\max_{p\in\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}}\;\mathbb{E}[pM_{\star}].

Proof

To prove this result, we first demonstrate that
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon_{N}}

converges to
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
with respect to the slice topology. If
so, then
\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon_{N}}
graph converges

\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}

(
Attouch and Beer 1993
, Th. 4.2)
.
Suppose this is the case, then for all
X\in\mathcal{M}
and

\eta\in\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(X)
there exists
(X_{N},\eta_{N})
with

\eta_{N}\in\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon_{N}}(X_{N})
for all
N
such that

\eta=\lim_{N}\eta_{N}
in
\mathcal{M}^{*}
and
X=\lim_{N}X_{N}
in
\mathcal{M}
. Let

\{(M_{N},p_{N})\}
denote a subsequence (without relabeling) on which

p_{\star}=\operatorname{\sigma(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M})-lim}_{N}p_{N}
. Since

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
is convex for all
\varepsilon^{\prime}\geq 0
,

\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
is maximally monotone. Consequently, we have
that

\langle p_{N}-\eta_{N},M_{N}-X_{N}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}\geq 0\quad\text{a.s.}\quad\forall\,N.

Since
(M_{\star}-X)=\lim_{N}(M_{N}-X_{N})
a.s. in
\mathcal{M}
and

(p_{\star}-\eta)=\operatorname{\sigma(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M})-lim}_{N}(p_{N}-\eta_{N})
, we
have that

\langle p_{\star}-\eta,M_{\star}-X\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{*},\mathcal{M}}\geq 0\quad\text{a.s.}

Since this holds for all
(X,\eta)
with

\eta\in\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(X)
, we have that

p_{\star}\in\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(M_{\star})
by the maximal monotonicity
of
\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
, as desired.

To prove slice convergence, we must show that

1.

\forall\,X\in\mathcal{M}
,
\exists\,X_{N}\to X
in
\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon_{N}}(X_{N})\to\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(X)
;

2.

\forall\,p\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
,
\exists\,p_{N}\to p
in
\mathcal{M}^{*}
such that
(\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon_{N}})^{*}(p_{N})\to(\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon})^{*}(p)

(
Attouch and Beer 1993
, Th. 3.1)
.
We will prove that
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(X)
is continuous for
fixed
X\in\mathcal{M}
by first proving that it is concave. Let

\varepsilon_{1},\,\varepsilon_{2}\geq 0
and
t\in[0,1]
, then

\mathcal{R}^{t\varepsilon_{1}+(1-t)\varepsilon_{2}}(X)=\inf_{\lambda>0}\{t(\varepsilon_{1}\lambda+\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(X))+(1-t)(\varepsilon_{2}\lambda+\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(X))\}\geq t\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon_{1}}(X)+(1-t)\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon_{2}}(X).

Moreover, since

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(X)\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}+\mathcal{R}(X)<+\infty

for all
\varepsilon^{\prime}\geq 0
,
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(X)
is
finite valued, concave, and hence continuous on
[0,+\infty)
. Consequently,
we can take
X_{N}=X
for all
N
, which proves the first condition.

To prove the second, we note that for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}\geq 0
,

(\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})^{*}
is the indicator function of
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
.
If
\varepsilon=0
, then
\mathfrak{D}_{0}=\{\pi\}
and the result is trivial.
We therefore assume
\varepsilon>0
. Let

p\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}
, then

\delta_{\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon_{N}}}(p)=+\infty
for
N
sufficiently large since
either
p\not\in\mathfrak{D}
or
\Phi(p)>\varepsilon
. In this case, we can take

p_{N}=p
for all
N
to verify the second condition. On the other hand,
suppose
p\in\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}
and define

p_{N}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p&\text{if $\varepsilon_{N}\geq\varepsilon$}\\
\tfrac{\varepsilon_{N}}{\varepsilon}p+(1-\tfrac{\varepsilon_{N}}{\varepsilon})&\text{if $\varepsilon_{N}>\varepsilon$}\end{array}\right..

Owing to convexity and the fact that
\Phi(1)=0
, we have

\Phi(p_{N})\leq\varepsilon_{N}
for all
N
. Moreover, we clearly have
that
p_{N}\to p
and
\delta_{\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon_{N}}}(p_{N})=0
for
all
N
, proving the second condition.

3.3
Application to
\phi
-Divergence-Based Variational Inference

In this subsection, we analyze GVI (
13
) and CGVI (
20
)
problems when
\Phi
is a
\phi
-divergence, i.e.,
\Phi(p)=\mathbb{E}[\phi(p)]
.
Recall that
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]
is a proper, closed and convex function
satisfying
\phi(1)=0
and
\phi(t)=\infty
for
t<0
. Moreover,
\phi

generates a risk-averse regret function
v=\phi^{*}
. In this case, the GVI
problem is

\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(M_{N})=\sup_{p\in\mathfrak{D}}\;\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[pM_{N}\right]-\sigma\mathbb{E}[\phi(p)]\right\}

(21)

and the CGVI problem is

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(M_{N})=\sup_{p\in\mathfrak{D}}\;\left\{\,\mathbb{E}[pM_{N}]\,|\,\mathbb{E}[\phi(p)]\leq\varepsilon\,\right\}.

(22)

The natural function spaces in which to analyze (
21
) and
(
22
) are the Orlicz spaces
(
Edgar and Sucheston 1992
)
. To
introduce the Orlicz spaces that we will use, we first recall that the regret
function
v
associated with
\phi
is proper, closed, convex, increasing,
and satisfies
v(0)=0
. Consequently,
v
is an Orlicz function as long as
there exists
t>0
such that
v(t)<+\infty

(
Rubshtein et al. 2016
, Def. 13.1.1)
, which we assume to hold.
We denote the Orlicz conjugate (also called the monotonic conjugate) of

v
by
\psi=v^{\#}
, i.e.,

\psi(x)\triangleq\sup_{t\geq 0}\{tx-v(t)\}.

A straightforward calculation demonstrates that the Orlicz conjugate of
v

is given by

\psi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if $x<1$}\\
\phi(x)&\text{if $x\geq 1$}\\
\end{array}\right..

and
\psi^{\#}=v
. We employ the Orlicz spaces generated by
\psi
and

\psi^{\#}
to analyze (
21
) and (
22
).

Recall that the Orlicz space
L_{\psi}\triangleq L_{\psi}(\Theta,\mathcal{B},\Pi)
is the linear
space of equivalence classes of
\mathcal{B}
-measurable functions
X
, equal up to a
set of measure zero, such that
\mathbb{E}[\psi(|X/a|)]<\infty
for some
a>0
and
that
L_{\psi}
is a decomposable Banach lattice when endowed with the Luxemburg
norm

\|X\|_{\psi}\triangleq\inf\{\,a>0\,|\,\mathbb{E}[\psi(|X/a|)]\leq 1\,\}.

We similarly define the Orlicz space
L_{\psi^{\#}}\triangleq L_{\psi^{\#}}(\Theta,\mathcal{B},\Pi)
.
We pair
L_{\psi}
with
L_{\psi^{\#}}
using the bilinear form

\langle p,X\rangle\triangleq\mathbb{E}[pX]
for
p\in L_{\psi}
and
X\in L_{\psi^{\#}}
,
which is finite by Young’s inequality. In this setting,

\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}\subset L_{\psi}
is bounded and

\sigma(L_{\psi},L_{\psi^{\#}})
-closed (see Appendix
A
).
Here,
\mathcal{L}=L_{\psi}
is the space of possible CGVI densities and

\mathcal{M}=L_{\psi^{\#}}
is the space of pay-off functions
M_{N}
. Notice that
\mathcal{R}

and
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
are finite valued on
\mathcal{M}
by definition and hence are
continuous and subdifferentiable. However, this does not guarantee existence
of solutions unless, e.g.,
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
is reflexive.

Our next results are corollaries of Theorems
4
and

12
that provide a semi-analytic expression for the variational
densities of (
21
) and (
22
) and well as techincal
assumptions on
v
and
\psi
that ensure existence of these densities.

Theorem 16
(Form of
\phi
-Divergence Densities)

Let
\mathcal{V}(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}[v(\cdot)]
, where
v=\phi^{*}
. If a variational density

\bar{p}\in\mathcal{L}
exists for (
21
), then it satisfies

\bar{p}(\theta)\in\partial v(\sigma^{-1}(M_{N}(\theta)-\bar{t})),

where
\bar{t}
solves the optimization problem in (
16
).
On the other hand, if a variational density
\bar{p}\in\mathcal{L}
exists
for (
22
), then it satisfies

\bar{p}(\theta)\in\partial v(\bar{\lambda}^{-1}(M_{N}(\theta)-\bar{\mu})),

(23)

where
(\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu})
solves the optimization problem
in (
20
).

Corollary 17
(Existence of
\phi
-Divergence Densities)

Consider the setting of Theorem
16
and suppose
v

and
\psi
are both finite valued and satisfy the (
\Delta_{2}
)
condition, i.e., there exists
x_{0}>0
and
k\in\mathbb{R}
such that

v(2x)<kv(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi(2x)<k\psi(x)\qquad\forall\,x\geq x_{0}.

Then, variational densities exist to (
21
) and
(
22
).

Proof

By construction we have that
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
are
continuous and subdifferentiable. Moreover, the stated assumptions
on
v
and
\psi
ensure that
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
is reflexive. Consequently,
solutions exist by Corollaries
7
and

14
.

Corollary
17
demonstrates that the GVI problems
(
21
) and (
22
) are well-posed, under certain
assumptions on
v
and
\psi
, for a fixed set of data
\{y_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
.
Moreover, Theorem
16
provides an explicit representation of the
variational densities. It is important to note that when
v=\phi^{*}
is
differentiable and
\bar{\lambda}>0
, these representations simplify to

\bar{p}(\theta)=v^{\prime}\left(\frac{M_{N}(\theta)-\bar{t}}{\sigma}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{p}(\theta)=v^{\prime}\left(\frac{M_{N}(\theta)-\bar{\mu}}{\bar{\lambda}}\right)

for (
21
) and (
22
), respectively.

3.4
Examples

To illustrate the connections between disutility functions, risk measures
and CGVI, we present four common
\phi
-divergence examples. We note that
the choice of
\phi
should be left to the practitioner as it is application
dependent and based on their disutility function. As such, we do not provide
recommendations on how to choose
\phi
, but rather provide discussion for four
common choices of
\phi
.

3.4.1
Kullback-Leibler Divergence

The KL divergence is generated by
\phi(t)=t\log(t)-t+1
for
t\geq 0
, in which
case
v(t)=e^{t}-1
is the exponential disutility function
(
8
) with
r=1
. See the solid orange line in
Figure
1
. For fixed
\sigma>0
, we can solve the
one-dimensional minimization problem in (
16
) with
\mathcal{V}

replaced by
\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}
, which yields

\bar{t}=\sigma\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\sigma^{-1}M_{N})]
(and similarly

\bar{\mu}=\bar{\lambda}\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\bar{\lambda}^{-1}M_{N})]
).
The associated risk measure
\mathcal{R}
is the entropic risk measure and

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
is the entropic value-at-risk
(
Ahmadi-Javid 2012
)
.
Given
\sigma>0
or the optimal
\bar{\lambda}>0
from (
20
), we
can compute the GVI and CGVI densities as

\bar{p}(\theta)=\frac{\exp\left(\sigma^{-1}M_{N}(\theta)\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sigma^{-1}M_{N}\right)\right]}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{p}(\theta)=\frac{\exp\left(\bar{\lambda}^{-1}M_{N}(\theta)\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\bar{\lambda}^{-1}M_{N}\right)\right]},

respectively. See Figure
2
(a) for an example of the CGVI
density. Note that the GVI and CGVI densities are exactly the Gibbs posterior
with learning rate
\sigma^{-1}=\bar{\lambda}^{-1}
for CGVI.
Given the relation between KL-divergence-based GVI and the Gibbs posterior, it
is natural to ask if there exists an
\varepsilon
that produces the Bayesian
posterior when
M_{N}
is a log-likelihood function. The following result
addresses this question.

Proposition 18
(Optimality of Bayesian Posterior)

Suppose the pay-off function
M_{N}
is a log-likelihood function
with
M_{N}\in L_{\psi^{\#}}
and set

\varepsilon=D_{\rm KL}(\exp(M_{N})/\mathbb{E}[\exp(M_{N})]\|\pi).

Then, the CGVI variational density
\bar{p}
with
\Phi
given by
the KL divergence is the usual Bayesian posterior. In particular,

\bar{\lambda}=1
.

Proof

This result follows by first differentiating the scalar function

\lambda\mapsto\lambda\varepsilon+\lambda\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda^{-1}M_{N})].

Since
\lambda=1
is strictly interior in the set
[0,\infty)
,
the desired optimality conditions consist of setting the aforementioned
derivative to zero and setting
\lambda=1
. In doing so, we see that

\varepsilon
must be as stated and we recover the Bayesian
posterior as
\bar{p}
.

3.4.2
\chi^{2}
Divergence

For the
\chi^{2}
-divergence,
\phi(t)=(t-1)^{2}
for
t\geq 0
and

\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}
can be equivalently rewritten as

\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}=\{\,p\in\mathfrak{D}\,|\,\mathbb{E}[(p-1)^{2}]\leq\varepsilon\,\}=\{\,p\in\mathfrak{D}\,|\,\mathbb{E}[p^{2}]\leq 1+\varepsilon\,\}.

This follows from expanding the quadratic
\mathbb{E}[(p-1)^{2}]
and noting that

\mathbb{E}[p]=1
. The set
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}
defines the second-order higher moment
coherent risk measure (see, e.g., § 8.2 in
Cheridito and Li 2008
) with
confidence level
\beta=1-(1+\varepsilon)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\in[0,1]
and
therefore

\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(M_{N})=\min_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{a+(1+\varepsilon)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[\max\{0,M_{N}-a\}^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}.

(24)

Hence, given an optimal
\bar{a}
from (
24
), we can recover the
optimal density as

\bar{p}(\theta)=\frac{\max\left\{0,M_{N}(\theta)-\bar{a}\right\}}{\mathbb{E}\left[\max\left\{0,M_{N}-\bar{a}\right\}\right]}.

This follows from
(
Cheridito and Li 2008
, Prop. 3.2)
. See
Figure
2
(b) for an example of this density. An interesting
property of the
\chi^{2}
-divergence (when compared with our other examples) is
that the corresponding CGVI truncates the support of the posterior to account for

M_{N}(\theta)\geq\bar{a}
. Moreover, the associated disutility function for the

\chi^{2}
-divergence is the truncated quadratic disutility function

v(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tfrac{1}{4}x^{2}+x&\text{if $x\geq-2$}\\
-1&\text{if $x<-2$}\end{array}\right.\qquad\implies\qquad v^{\prime}(x)=\max\{0,\tfrac{1}{2}x+1\}

and so the GVI density is given by

\bar{p}(\theta)=\frac{\max\{0,M_{N}(\theta)-(\bar{t}-2\sigma)\}}{\mathbb{E}[\max\{0,M_{N}-(\bar{t}-2\sigma)\}]}.

See the dashed blue line in Figure
1
.

3.4.3
Rényi Divergence

The Rényi divergence
(
Rényi 1961
)
is given by

D_{\alpha}(p\|\pi)\triangleq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}[p^{\alpha}],\quad\alpha>0,\;\alpha\neq 1

and
D_{\alpha}(p\|\pi)
is the KL divergence when
\alpha=1
. The
Rényi divergence is a popular alternative to the KL divergence because
as demonstrated in

(
Knoblauch et al. 2019
;
Knoblauch 2019
)
, it often
outperforms the KL divergence for GVI problems with misspecified priors.
We restrict
\alpha
to satisfy
0<\alpha<1
.
After a sequence of invertible transformations, we can equivalently write
the bound
D_{\alpha}(p\|\pi)\leq\varepsilon
as

\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\mathbb{E}[p^{\alpha}-\alpha p+(\alpha-1)]\leq\frac{1-\exp((\alpha-1)\varepsilon)}{1-\alpha}=:\varepsilon_{\alpha},

producing a
\phi
-divergence on the left-hand side with

\phi(t)=(t^{\alpha}-\alpha t+(\alpha-1))/(\alpha-1)
for
t\geq 0

and
v(t)=(1+t/\beta)^{\beta}-1
where

\beta=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}<0
. This

-divergence is sometimes

3.4.4
Total-Variation Distance

For the total variation distance, we set
\phi(t)=|t-1|
for
t\geq 0
.
As shown in

, the associated disutility function is

v(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\{0,t+1\}-1&\text{if $t\leq 1$}\\
+\infty&\text{if $t>1$}\end{array}\right.

See the dot-dashed yellow line in Figure
1
. The Orlicz
spaces generated by
\phi^{*}
are
L_{\psi}=L^{1}(\Theta,\mathfrak{B},\Pi)
and

L_{\psi^{\#}}=L^{\infty}(\Theta,\mathfrak{B},\Pi)
. As we will see,
total-variation-based GVI and CGVI densities may not exist because the
associated risk identifiers are measures that are not absolutely continuous
with respect to
\Pi
in general.

We first consider the total-variation-based CGVI problem. Upon the change of
variables
s=\mu+\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
t=\mu-\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we can
equivalently rewrite (
20
) as

\displaystyle\min_{s,\,t\in\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\left\{\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}s+(1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})t+\mathbb{E}[\max\{0,M_{N}-t\}]\right\}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}\leq s

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}+\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{(1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})t+\mathbb{E}[\max\{0,M_{N}-t\}]\right\},

(25)

where the optimal
s
is
\bar{s}=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}
and the optimal

t
solves the optimization problem in (
25
).
Given
\bar{t}
, we recover
\bar{\mu}
and
\bar{\lambda}
as

\bar{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}+\bar{t})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{\lambda}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}-\bar{t}).

Notice that if
\varepsilon\geq 2
, then (
25
) is simply

\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}
and the optimal probability measures are convex combinations
of point masses
\delta_{\theta^{\star}_{N}}
centered at solutions

\theta^{\star}_{N}\in\Theta
to (
1
) (here,
\delta_{\theta}
denotes
the usual Dirac measure, i.e., for any
S\in\mathcal{B}
,
\delta_{\theta}(S)=1
if

\theta\in S
and
\delta_{\theta}(S)=0
if
\theta\not\in S
). On the other
hand, if
0<\varepsilon<2
, then (
25
) can be equivalently rewritten
as

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}+\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\mathrm{AVaR}_{\varepsilon/2}\left(M_{N}\right),

where the average value-at-risk (AVaR) is defined by

\mathrm{AVaR}_{\beta}(X)\triangleq\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\beta}^{1}q_{\alpha}(X)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha=q_{\beta}(X)+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\mathbb{E}[\max\{0,X-q_{\beta}(X)\}]

0<\beta<1
and
q_{\alpha}(X)
is the
\alpha
-quantile of the
random variable
X

(
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2002

. In this case, the
optimal

is
\bar{t}=q_{\varepsilon/2}(M_{N})
.
Moreover, the subdifferential
\partial\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(M_{N})
is
generally set-valued and contains subgradients of the form, e.g.,

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\delta_{\theta^{\star}_{N}}+\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)P_{0},

where
P_{0}\in\mathfrak{P}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\Pi
and has
density
p_{0}\in\mathfrak{D}
satisfying

p_{0}(\theta)\in\frac{1}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{0\}&\text{if $M_{N}(\theta)<\bar{t}$}\\
[0,1]&\text{if $M_{N}(\theta)=\bar{t}$}\\
\{1\}&\text{if $M_{N}(\theta)>\bar{t}$}\end{array}\right..

In particular, if
\Pi(\{\theta\in\Theta\,|\,M_{N}(\theta)=q_{\varepsilon/2}(M_{N})\})=0
, then

p_{0}(\theta)=\frac{1}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(M_{N}(\theta)-\bar{t}).

See Figure

Unfortunately, the relationship between CGVI and AVaR is not present for the
total-variation-based GVI problem (

). In this setting, we

Clearly, if
\tfrac{1}{2}(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}-\operatorname*{ess\,inf}M_{N})\leq\sigma

\mathbb{E}[M_{N}]\leq\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(M_{N})\leq\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}
. Moreover, we have that

\sigma\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(M_{N})
is monotonically decreasing, attaining its

\sigma=\tfrac{1}{2}(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}M_{N}-\operatorname*{ess\,inf}M_{N})
and
its upper bound when
\sigma=0
.

4
Computation, Sampling and Asymptotic Analysis

In this section, we investigate several theoretical properties that are
relevant for the computation of the
\phi
-divergence-based CGVI variational
densities analyzed in Subsection
3.3
and note that it may be
possible to extend these results to general
\Phi
using empirical estimates
for law-invariant functionals
(cf.
(
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Ch. 7.2.6)
). In practice,
we can only expect to solve the one-dimensional GVI (
21
) and
two-dimensional CGVI (
22
) problems approximately by either
stochastic approximation algorithms or by empirical approximation using samples
from the prior distribution
\Pi
. The latter is often referred to as sample
average approximation (SAA) in stochastic programming. This raises important
questions regarding the asymptotic behavior of the sample-based solutions as
the sample size increases to infinity.

Given
S
independent and identically distributed (iid) samples

\{\theta_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S}
drawn from
\Pi
, we evaluate the pay-off samples

\{M_{N}^{s}\triangleq M_{N}(\theta_{s})\}_{s=1}^{S}
and then solve the SAA problem

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}_{S}(M_{N})\triangleq\inf_{\mu\in\mathbb{R},\;\lambda\geq 0}\left\{\,\mu+\lambda\varepsilon+\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}(\lambda\phi)^{*}(M_{N}^{s}-\mu)\right\}.

(26)

We denote the set of minimizers to (
26
) by
\widehat{X}^{\star}_{S}

and the minimizers to (
22
) by
X^{\star}
.
The optimization problem (
26
) is a two-dimensional convex
optimization problem that can be solved using any convex
programming methods. For example, one can employ proximal or projected
(sub)gradient-type methods. If
\phi^{*}
is sufficiently differentiable, one
can employ Newton-type methods. See
(
Beck 2017
)
for a survey of other
applicable first-order methods. Additionally, under appropriate assumptions,
one can prove that the estimators
\mu_{S}
and
\lambda_{S}
computed by solving
(
26
) converge a.s. to a solution of (
22
), see e.g.,

(
Dupacova and Wets 1988
;
King and Rockafellar 1993
;
Shapiro 1989
)
and
the result below. Moreover, when
\bar{\lambda}>0
,

(
Clarke 1983
, Prop. 2.1.5(b))
ensures that any

\sigma(L_{\psi},L_{\psi^{\#}})
-accumulation point
\bar{p}
of the sequence

\{p_{S}\}
with

p_{S}(\theta)\in\lambda_{S}\partial\phi^{*}((M_{N}(\theta)-\mu_{S})/\lambda_{S}),

satisfies (
23
) a.s.

We emphasize here that there are no limitations on the dimensions of
\Theta
.
In particular, the size of
\theta
does not change the computational
complexity of solving the two-dimensional convex optimization problem
(
26
) since the pay-off samples
M_{N}(\theta_{s})
are computed offline,
prior to solving (
26
). However, it is important to note that the
number of samples
S
required to achieve a prescribed accuracy when solving
the SAA problem (
26
) is typically dimension dependent

(
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Chap. 5)
. Once
\bar{\lambda}

and
\bar{\mu}
are computed, we can generate samples from the CGVI
distribution
\bar{p}
using similar methods as those used in Bayesian
inference such as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

(
Beskos et al. 2017
)
and transport maps

(
El Moselhy and Marzouk 2012
)
. We also note that for many choices of

\phi
, the density
\bar{p}
is given in closed form once
\bar{\lambda}

and
\bar{\mu}
are computed. This allows us to compute various statistics
such as the value of
\theta
that maximizes
\bar{p}
.

In the next result, we prove asymptotic consistency of the optimal values and
optimal solution set of (
26
) in the large sample limit (i.e.,

S\to\infty
). These statements follow by verifying the assumptions of

(
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Th. 5.4)
. We prove the various
necessary conditions in Appendix
B

and then obtain the asymptotic consistency as a corollary.
We refer the reader to
(
Rockafellar and Wets 1998
, Chap. 14)
or

(
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Chap. 7)
regarding the
terminology.

Theorem 19
(Asymptotic Consistency: Sample Limit)

If the asumptions of Corollary
16
hold, then

1.

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}_{S}(M_{N})\to\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}(M_{N})
a.s.;

2.

The deviation of
\widehat{X}^{\star}_{S}
to
X^{\star}
converges
to zero a.s. That is, we have that

\mathbb{D}(\widehat{X}^{\star}_{S},X^{\star})\triangleq\sup_{(\mu,\lambda)\in\widehat{X}^{\star}_{S}}\inf_{(\mu^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})\in X^{\star}}\|(\mu,\lambda)-(\mu^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})\|\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

Proof

This is a direct application of

(
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Th. 5.4)
in light of
Theorem
23
.

Theorems
23
and
19
lead to the following
observations. As proven in Theorem
23
, the solution sets
X^{\star}

and
\widehat{X}^{\star}_{S}
are always nonempty,
compact, and convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
, regardless of the
sample size
S
. For any
(\mu_{S},\lambda_{S})\in\widehat{X}^{\star}_{S}
, there
exists
(\mu_{S}^{\prime},\lambda_{S}^{\prime})\in X^{\star}
satisfying

\|(\mu_{S},\lambda_{S})-(\mu^{\prime}_{S},\lambda^{\prime}_{S})\|=\min_{(\mu^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})\in X^{\star}}\|(\mu_{S},\lambda_{S})-(\mu^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})\|\leq\mathbb{D}(\widehat{X}^{\star}_{S},X^{\star})

since
X^{\star}
is compact. Using again the compactness of
X^{\star}
, we can
deduce the existence of some
(\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})\in X^{\star}
and a
subsequence
\{(\mu^{\prime}_{S_{k}},\lambda^{\prime}_{S_{k}})\}
that converges to

(\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})
a.s. It follows that

\|(\mu_{S_{k}},\lambda_{S_{k}})-(\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})\|\leq\mathbb{D}(\widehat{X}^{\star}_{S_{k}},X^{\star})+\|(\mu^{\prime}_{S_{k}},\lambda^{\prime}_{S_{k}})-(\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})\|

and, by Theorem
19
, we have that

\|(\mu_{S_{k}},\lambda_{S_{k}})-(\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})\|\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

Consequently, we have that

\mu_{S_{k}}+\varepsilon\lambda_{S_{k}}+\frac{1}{S_{k}}\sum_{k=1}^{S_{k}}(\lambda_{S_{k}}\phi)^{*}(M_{N}^{k}-\mu_{S_{k}})\to\bar{\mu}+\varepsilon\bar{\lambda}+\mathbb{E}[(\bar{\lambda}\phi)^{*}(M_{N}-\bar{\mu})]\quad\text{a.s.}.

In the following corollary, we use the above observations to relate the
solutions
(\mu_{S},\lambda_{S})
to sequences of CGVI variational densities.

Theorem 20

Let the assumptions of Corollary
16
hold and suppose
L_{\psi}

is reflexive. If
\{\bar{p}_{k}\}
denotes a sequence of SAA estimators to the
CGVI posterior associated with
\{S_{k}\}
samples with
S_{k}\nearrow\infty
,
i.e.,

\bar{p}_{k}(\theta)\in\partial(\lambda_{S_{k}}\phi)^{*}(M_{N}(\theta)-\mu_{S_{k}}).

Then any a.s.
\sigma(L_{\psi},L_{\psi^{\#}})
-accumulation point

\bar{p}
of
\{\bar{p}_{k}\}
is a variational density for
(
22
), i.e.,
\bar{p}
satisfies (
23
).

Proof

This proof uses various notions of variational convergence. See

(
Attouch 1984
)
for an overview of these techniques.
One can show that the sequence of functionals

\Phi_{k}(p)\triangleq\lambda_{S_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\phi(p)]
pointwise and epi-converge
to
\overline{\Phi}\triangleq\overline{\lambda}\mathbb{E}[\phi(\cdot)]
a.s.
(
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Sect. 7.2.5)
.
Moreover, since
\langle\cdot,X\rangle:L_{\psi}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
continuous linear functional for any
X\in L_{\psi^{\#}}
,
the sequence
\Phi_{k}(\cdot)-\langle\cdot,X\rangle
also pointwise
and epi-converge a.s. Consequently, we have that

\inf_{p\in L_{\psi}}\left\{\Phi_{k}(p)-\langle p,X\rangle\right\}\to\inf_{p\in L_{\psi}}\left\{\overline{\Phi}(p)-\langle p,X\rangle\right\}\quad\text{a.s.}

(
Attouch 1984
)
.
As a result,
(\Phi_{k})^{*}
converges to
(\overline{\Phi})^{*}
pointwise a.s. In
fact, if
X_{k}\to X
with respect to the

\sigma(L_{\psi^{\#}},L_{\psi})
-topology, then passing to the limit inferior
(a.s.) on both sides and taking the supremum over
p
in the inequality

-\inf_{p\in L_{\psi}}\left\{\Phi_{k}(p)-\langle p,X_{k}\rangle\right\}\geq-(\Phi_{k}(p)-\langle p,X_{k}\rangle)\quad\forall p\in L_{\psi}\quad\text{a.s.}

yields

\liminf_{k\to\infty}(\Phi_{k})^{*}(X_{k})\geq(\overline{\Phi})^{*}(X)\quad\text{a.s.},

since the pointwise and epi-limits of
\Phi_{k}
coincide. These facts ensure
that
(\Phi_{k})^{*}
Mosco converges to
(\overline{\Phi})^{*}
and therefore the
subdifferentials
\partial(\Phi_{k})^{*}
graph converge to

\partial(\overline{\Phi})^{*}
by
(
Attouch 1984
, Th. 3.66)
.
Lastly, we note that
M_{N}-\mu_{S_{k}}
strongly converges in
L_{\psi^{\#}}

a.s. Therefore, if
\overline{p}
is an a.s.

\sigma(L_{\psi},L_{\psi^{\#}})
-accumulation point of
\left\{\bar{p}_{k}\right\}

with
\overline{p}_{k}\in\partial(\Phi_{k})^{*}(M_{N}-\mu_{S_{k}})
, then

\overline{p}\in\partial(\overline{\Phi})^{*}(M_{N}-\bar{\mu})
and by

(
Rockafellar 1974
, Th. 21)
, we have that

\bar{p}(\theta)\in\partial(\bar{\lambda}\phi)^{*}(M_{N}(\theta)-\bar{\mu})\quad\text{a.s.}

Due to Theorem
19
, the discussion above surrounding the derivation
of
(\mu_{S_{k}},\lambda_{S_{k}})
, and strong duality,
\bar{p}
is optimal for
(
2
).

5
Empirical CGVI: An “Objective” Perspective

In this brief section, we present several extensions to CGVI in which we assume that no reasonable prior measure
\Pi
exists. Instead, we wish to proceed in a more objective manner in which perhaps only samples of the prior or moments are available and we are unsure which utility function to choose. We introduce two approaches to handle this situation. The first approach falls into the class of problems described by (
6
) whereas the second approach is based on moment matching. From a computational standpoint, more regularity of
M_{N}
and the integrands
\psi
in the case of moment-matching would be necessary to compute the global minimizers. A deeper look into the computation of empirical CGVIs will be the subject of future investigations.

5.1
Wasserstein Distance and Empirical Priors

In this subsection, we assume that we have many noisy observations of
Y
and
a few noisy realizations of
\theta
. For example, these realizations could be
the result of prior estimation attempts. This could also model the practical
situation in which observations of
Y
are cheap to obtain relative to
observations of
\theta
. To formulate the estimation problem (
2
),
we resort to empirical estimation to generate the prior distribution
\Pi
.

Let
\Theta
be a locally
\sigma
-compact Polish space endowed with its
Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
.
According to
(
Williamson and Janos 1987
, Th. 1)
,
\Theta
admits a metric

d:\Theta\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
that has the Heine-Borel property
(i.e., all closed and bounded sets are compact). One obvious example for such
a
\Theta
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the usual Euclidean topology. We further require
that
M_{N}
is bounded, continuous, and concave on
\Theta
.
Given
M
observations of
\theta
, denoted by

\{\widehat{\theta}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, we define the prior measure
\Pi
as the
empirical measure:

\Pi=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{\widehat{\theta}_{m}},

One attractive approach to defining a meaningful set of measures

\mathfrak{A}
in (
2
) is to use probability metrics, cf.
(
Rachev 1991
)
. To this end, we employ the Wasserstein-1 distance (i.e.,
the Kantorovich-Rubinstein metric),
W_{1}:\mathfrak{P}\times\mathfrak{P}\to[0,\infty]
, defined by

\displaystyle W_{1}(P,Q)\triangleq\inf_{\rho\in\Gamma(P,Q)}\int_{\Theta^{2}}d(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\,\rho(\mathrm{d}\theta_{1},\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}),

where
\Gamma(P,Q)\subset\mathfrak{P}(\Theta\times\Theta)
denotes the subset of all
Borel probability measures on
\Theta\times\Theta
that have marginal
P
for
their first factor and marginal
Q
for their second. We then define the set

\mathfrak{A}
by

\mathfrak{A}=\{\,P\in\mathfrak{P}\,|\,W_{1}(P,\Pi)\leq\varepsilon\,\}.

For this choice of
\mathfrak{A}
, we have the following existence result.

Theorem 21
(Existence of Wasserstein CGVI)

Under the stated assumptions, (
2
) admits a solution
P^{\star}

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof

Since
M_{N}
is bounded and continuous,
\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[M_{N}\right]
is
continuous in
P\in\mathfrak{P}(\Theta)
with respect to the weak-convergence
of probability measures. It follows from
(
Pichler and Xu 2018
, Prop. 3)
that

\mathfrak{A}
is weakly compact. In order to use the proof of

(
Pichler and Xu 2018
, Prop. 3)
, we first note that

\mathcal{P}\triangleq\{\Pi\}
is a uniformly tight set of probability measures.
Moreover, according to
(
Williamson and Janos 1987
, Th. 1)
, given a compact
set
C\subset\Theta
and some constant
\delta>0
, the set

C_{\delta}\triangleq\left\{\theta\in\Theta\,|\,d(\theta,c)\leq\delta,c\in C\right\}

is a closed and bounded set and therefore, compact. The rest of the proof in

(
Pichler and Xu 2018
, Prop. 3)
can be used without change. Finally, the
existence of
P^{\star}
for (
2
) now follows by the usual arguments
from the direct method of the calculus of variations, cf.,

(
Attouch et al. 2006
, Th. 3.2.6)
.

Remark 22
(Assumptions in Theorem
21
)

The assumptions of Theorem
21
are restrictive in the sense
that it does not appear possible to extend the current proof to a non-trivial
infinite-dimensional setting. On the other hand,
\Theta
is still allowed to
be of arbitrarily high dimension, which is clearly of interest to modern
applications in machine learning and data science.

In light of Theorem
21
, we know that there exists a

P^{\star}\in\mathfrak{A}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\star}}[M_{N}]=\max_{P\in\mathfrak{A}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{N}].

To compute the CGVI measure
P^{\star}
, we can reformulate the estimation
problem (
2
) as a finite-dimensional optimization problem
(cf.
(
Esfahani and Kuhn 2018
)
). In
particular, the optimal value in (
2
) is equal to the optimal value
of the problem

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{M}\in\Theta}\,\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}M_{N}(\theta_{m})

(27a)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{subject\;to}\quad\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}d(\theta_{m},\widehat{\theta}_{m})\leq\varepsilon.

(27b)

The arguments in the proofs of Theorems 4.2 and 4.4 of

(
Esfahani and Kuhn 2018
)
extend directly to our more general setting.
Therefore, if
\{\theta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\theta_{M}^{\star}\}
is a solution to
(
27
), then the optimal measure is given by

P^{\star}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{\theta^{\star}_{m}}.

5.2
Moment Matching

For this approach, we assume that we are given noisy observations,

\mathfrak{m}_{m}
, of auxiliary quantities with the form
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{m}]
where

\psi_{m}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
for
m=1,\ldots,M
are
\mathcal{B}
-measurable functions that
are
\Pi
-integrable, where
\Pi\in\mathfrak{P}
again is a predetermined prior measure.
In this case, we choose
\mathfrak{A}
to be the subset of probability measures that
match the computed generalized moments
\mathfrak{m}_{m}
up to the fixed
tolerances
\varepsilon_{m}\geq 0
, i.e.,

\mathfrak{A}=\{\,P\in\mathfrak{P}\,|\,|\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi_{m}]-\mathfrak{m}_{m}|\leq\varepsilon_{m},\;\;m=1,\ldots,M\,\}.

Owing to a result from Rogosinski
(
Rogosinski 1958
)
(see also

(
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Th. 7.37)
),
the maximizing measure for (
2
) is a convex
combination of at most
2M+1
Dirac measures. Consequently,
we can reformulate (
2
) as the optimization problem

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{2M+1}\in\Theta\\
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2M+1}\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\quad\sum_{m=1}^{2M+1}\alpha_{m}M_{N}(\theta_{m})

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{subject\;to}\quad\mathfrak{m}_{k}-\varepsilon_{k}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{2M+1}\alpha_{m}\psi_{k}(\theta_{m})\leq\mathfrak{m}_{k}+\varepsilon_{k},\;\;k=1,\ldots,2M+1

\displaystyle\;\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{m=1}^{2M+1}\alpha_{m}=1,\;\;\alpha_{k}\geq 0,\;\;k=1,\ldots,2M+1.

See
(
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Th. 6.66)
for details
in the context of distributionally robust stochastic optimization.
Given optimal
\alpha_{m}^{\star}
and
\theta_{m}^{\star}
(if they exist),
the CGVI measure then

P^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{2M+1}\alpha_{m}^{\star}\delta_{\theta_{m}^{\star}}.

6
Conclusions

Extended M-estimation provides a generalization of traditional statistical
estimation procedures including maximum likelihood, regression, Bayesian
inference, and the Gibbs posterior.
Much like Bayesian inference and the Gibbs posterior, CGVI
permits the use of subjective and data-driven information to produce a
distribution of likely values for the unknown parameters, which can then be
used to perform further analyses. Additionally, these distributions often have
semi-analytical representations. In particular, they require the solution of a
small convex optimization problem in the case of the
\phi
-divergence. Many natural
extensions of this work exist, including its application to estimation using
functional data (e.g., time-dependent signals), its application to training
machine learning models such as GANs, the development of efficient sampling
methods and the development of rigorous experimental design techniques that use
CGVI.
