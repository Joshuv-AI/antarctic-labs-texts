Reflection operator and hypergeometry I: S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℝ ) SL(2,\mathbb{R}) spin chain


Abstract
In this work we consider open S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℝ ) SL(2,\mathbb{R}) spin chain, mainly the simplest case of one particle. Eigenfunctions of the model can be constructed using the so-called reflection operator. We obtain several representations of this operator and show its relation to the hypergeometric function. Besides, we prove orthogonality and completeness of one-particle eigenfunctions and connect them to the index hypergeometric transform. Finally, we briefly state the formula for the eigenfunctions in many-particle case.

Introduction

Open spin chain

In this paper we consider quantum integrable model called
open
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
spin chain
. To define this model we follow Sklyanin’s approach
[
S
]
. The basic building block is the Lax matrix

\displaystyle L(u)=\begin{pmatrix}u+S&S_{-}\\
S_{+}&u-S\end{pmatrix},

(1.1)

where
S,S_{\pm}
are generators of the group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
satisfying commutation relations

[S_{+},S_{-}]=2S,\qquad[S,S_{\pm}]=\pm S_{\pm}.

(1.2)

For any representation of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
this Lax matrix solves the Yang-Baxter equation

\displaystyle R(u-v)\,\bigl(L(u)\otimes\bm{1}\bigr)\,\bigl(\bm{1}\otimes L(v)\bigr)=\bigl(\bm{1}\otimes L(v)\bigr)\,\bigl(L(u)\otimes\bm{1}\bigr)\,R(u-v)

(1.3)

with the Yang’s
R
-matrix acting in
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}

\displaystyle R(u)=\begin{pmatrix}u+1&0&0&0\\
0&u&1&0\\
0&1&u&0\\
0&0&0&u+1\end{pmatrix}.

(1.4)

In what follows we consider representation such that the generators are realized by differential operators

S=z\partial_{z}+s,\qquad S_{-}=-\partial_{z},\qquad S_{+}=z^{2}\partial_{z}+2sz

(1.5)

acting on functions
\psi(z)
analytic in the upper half-plane
\operatorname{Im}z>0
.

The generators are anti-hermitian

S^{\dagger}=-S,\qquad S_{-}^{\dagger}=-S_{-},\qquad S_{+}^{\dagger}=-S_{+}

(1.6)

with respect to the scalar product

\langle\chi|\psi\rangle=\int\limits\mathcal{D}z\;\overline{\chi(z)}\,\psi(z),

(1.7)

where the integration is performed over upper half-plane
\operatorname{Im}z>0
and the measure is defined by the formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}z=\frac{2s-1}{\pi}\,(2\operatorname{Im}z)^{2s-2}\;d\operatorname{Re}z\;d\operatorname{Im}z.

(1.8)

For the
spin
parameter
s
we assume

s>\frac{1}{2}.

(1.9)

The generators with integer or half-integer spin correspond to
discrete series
of unitary irreducible representations of the group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})

[
GGV
, Ch. VII]
.

Lax matrix (
1.1
) contains two parameters
u
and
s
.
In what follows we also use equivalent parametrization

\displaystyle L(u)\equiv L(u+s-1,u-s),

(1.10)

where parameters
u_{1}=u+s-1
,
u_{2}=u-s
appear in a natural way in factorized expression

\displaystyle L(u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
z&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}&-\partial_{z}\\
0&u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-z&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}+1+z\partial_{z}&-\partial_{z}\\[3.0pt]
z^{2}\partial_{z}+(u_{1}-u_{2}+1)z&u_{2}-z\partial_{z}\end{pmatrix}.

(1.11)

The second basic building block of the model is the
K
-matrix

\displaystyle K(u)=\begin{pmatrix}i\alpha&u-\frac{1}{2}\\[6.0pt]
-\beta^{2}\bigl(u-\frac{1}{2}\bigr)&i\alpha\end{pmatrix},

(1.12)

which contains two additional parameters
\alpha,\beta
. The matrix
K(u)
satisfies the reflection equation
[
C
,
S
,
KS
]

R(u-v)\,\bigl(K(u)\otimes\bm{1}\bigr)\,R(u+v-1)\,\bigl(\bm{1}\otimes K(v)\bigr)\\[6.0pt]
=\bigl(\bm{1}\otimes K(v)\bigr)\,R(u+v-1)\,\bigl(K(u)\otimes\bm{1}\bigr)\,R(u-v)

(1.13)

with the same Yang’s
R
-matrix (
1.4
).

The open
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
spin chain is a model of
n
interacting particles with coordinates
z_{j}
. Denote by
L_{j}(u)
Lax matrices corresponding to each particle

\displaystyle L_{j}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}u+z_{j}\partial_{z_{j}}+s&-\partial_{z_{j}}\\[6.0pt]
z_{j}^{2}\partial_{z_{j}}+2sz_{j}&u-z_{j}\partial_{z_{j}}-s\end{pmatrix}.

(1.14)

Using the introduced objects we define the
monodromy matrix

\displaystyle T(u)=L_{n}(u)\cdots L_{1}(u)K(u)L_{1}(u)\cdots L_{n}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}A(u)&B(u)\\
C(u)&D(u)\end{pmatrix}.

(1.15)

Due to the equations for its building blocks (
1.3
), (
1.13
), the monodromy matrix obeys the same reflection equation
[
S
, Proposition 2]

R(u-v)\,\bigl(T(u)\otimes\bm{1}\bigr)\,R(u+v-1)\,\bigl(\bm{1}\otimes T(v)\bigr)\\[6.0pt]
=\bigl(\bm{1}\otimes T(v)\bigr)\,R(u+v-1)\,\bigl(T(u)\otimes\bm{1}\bigr)\,R(u-v).

(1.16)

This identity is equivalent to the commutation relations between the elements of the monodromy matrix (
1.15
). In particular, equality between
(14)
-elements in reflection equation gives commutativity

\displaystyle[B(u),B(v)]=0.

(1.17)

Thus, the coefficients
H_{k}
of the polynomial
B(u)

\displaystyle B(u)=\biggl(u-\frac{1}{2}\biggr)(u^{2n}+u^{2n-2}H_{1}+\ldots+H_{n})

(1.18)

commute with each other

[H_{k},H_{m}]=0

(1.19)

and define the quantum integrable model.

Our main goal is the diagonalization of the operator
B(u)
. In this article we mostly focus on the case of one particle. In Section
1.3
we briefly state the answer for the general case and postpone its derivation to future paper.

One-particle problem

In this section we formulate our main results concerning one-particle problem. When
n=1
the operator
B(u)
has the form

B(u)=\biggl(u-\frac{1}{2}\biggr)\bigl(u^{2}-H^{s}\bigr)

(1.20)

with the only nontrivial part being one-particle Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H^{s}

\displaystyle=S^{2}+\beta^{2}S_{-}^{2}-2i\alpha S_{-}

(1.21)

\displaystyle=(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}^{2}+(2s+1)z\partial_{z}+2i\alpha\partial_{z}+s^{2}.

We look for the eigenfunctions of this operator

\displaystyle H^{s}\,\Psi_{\lambda}(z)=-\lambda^{2}\,\Psi_{\lambda}(z)

(1.22)

analytic in the upper half-plane
\operatorname{Im}z>0
. The Hilbert space consists of such analytic functions square integrable with respect to the scalar product (
1.7
).

After rescaling and shifting

w=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{iz}{2\beta},\qquad z=i\beta(1-2w)

(1.23)

the operator (
1.21
) transforms into

H^{s}=-w(1-w)\partial_{w}^{2}-\biggl[\biggl(s+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\biggr)-(2s+1)w\biggr]\partial_{w}+s^{2}.

(1.24)

Hence, the spectral problem (
1.22
) can be rewritten as the hypergeometric equation

\Bigl(w(1-w)\partial_{w}^{2}+\bigl[c-(a+b+1)w\bigr]\partial_{w}-ab\Bigr)\Psi_{\lambda}=0

(1.25)

with the parameters

a=s+i\lambda,\qquad b=s-i\lambda,\qquad c=s+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}.

(1.26)

Therefore the eigenfunctions can be expressed in terms of hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c;w)
, whose properties, of course, are very well-known
[
DLMF
,
Ch. 15
]
.

However, there is another approach to the spectral problem, which makes use of the underlying integrability of the model. The advantage of this approach is that it can be generalized to the case of many particles.

Before we describe it, let us make few remarks about the parameters of the model
\alpha,\beta
. In this paper we always assume

\beta>0,\qquad\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}>0.

(1.27)

The reasons for these assumptions are as follows.
Spin operators are anti-hermitian
S^{\dagger}=-S
,
S_{-}^{\dagger}=-S_{-}

with respect to the scalar product (
1.7
). Therefore the Hamiltonian (
1.21
) is formally self-adjoint

(H^{s})^{\dagger}=H^{s}

(1.28)

under assumptions

\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\beta^{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.29)

From this we have two options:
\beta\in i\mathbb{R}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. The possible eigenfunctions in these cases are different due to analyticity requirement. Note that
H^{s}
(
1.21
) is invariant under reflection
\beta\to-\beta
. Thus, the general solution of the equation (
1.25
) can be written as the linear combination

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathrm{gen}}_{\lambda}(z)

\displaystyle=A\;{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl(s+i\lambda,\,s-i\lambda,\,s+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta};\,\frac{1}{2}+\frac{iz}{2\beta}\biggr)

(1.30)

\displaystyle+B\;{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl(s+i\lambda,\,s-i\lambda,\,s+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta};\,\frac{1}{2}-\frac{iz}{2\beta}\biggr).

Since
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c;w)
has branch cut from
w=1
to
\infty
, in the case
\beta\in i\mathbb{R}
both linearly independent solutions in (
1.30
) are analytic in the upper half-plane
\operatorname{Im}z>0
. On the other hand, if
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, then only one of them fits this requirement. For simplicity, in this article we consider only the second case and without loss of generality assume
\beta>0
.

The second condition in (
1.27
) is also imposed to simplify matters: in this case we exclude possibility of discrete spectra, see Remark
1
in Section
4.1
. In addition, it is convenient to denote

g=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}>0.

(1.31)

In what follows we frequently use the parameter
g
instead of
\alpha
.

So, under the above assumptions (
1.27
) the eigenfunctions of
H^{s}
analytic in the upper half-plane are given by the formula

\displaystyle\Psi_{\lambda}(z)={}_{2}F_{1}\biggl(s+i\lambda,\,s-i\lambda,\,s+g;\,\frac{1}{2}+\frac{iz}{2\beta}\biggr).

(1.32)

Notice that since
s+g>1/2
this solution is well-defined. Also observe that it is symmetric with respect to reflection
\lambda\to-\lambda
, so the corresponding eigenvalue
-\lambda^{2}
(
1.22
) is non-degenerate.

Now let us describe an alternative way to construct the eigenfunctions. Imagine the operator
\mathcal{K}(s,x)
acting on functions
\psi(z)
and intertwining Hamiltonians (
1.21
) with different spins

\displaystyle H^{s}\,\mathcal{K}(s,x)=\mathcal{K}(s,x)\,H^{x}.

(1.33)

Then acting on
1
from both sides we obtain

\displaystyle H^{s}\,\mathcal{K}(s,x)\cdot 1=x^{2}\,\mathcal{K}(s,x)\cdot 1,

(1.34)

and therefore
\mathcal{K}(s,x)\cdot 1
is an eigenfunction. Such operator can be constructed by solving the equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(s,x)\,L(u+x-1,u-s)\,K(u)\,L(u+s-1,u-x)

(1.35)

\displaystyle=L(u+s-1,u-x)\,K(u)\,L(u+x-1,u-s)\,\mathcal{K}(s,x)

with matrices defined in the previous section (
1.11
), (
1.12
). This matrix relation is equivalent to three independent equations on
\mathcal{K}
-operator derived in Section
2.1
, see (
2.7
). One of them is precisely the intertwining property (
1.33
). Since the above equation is very similar to the reflection equation on
K
-matrix (
1.13
), we call
\mathcal{K}(s,x)

reflection operator
.

In this paper we derive three formulas for the reflection operator. The first one is obtained in Section
2.1
by solving the defining equation (
1.35
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(s,x)=\frac{\Gamma\bigl(N+\frac{x-s}{2}+g\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(N+\frac{s-x}{2}+g\bigr)},\qquad N=\frac{1}{2i\beta}\bigl[(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}+(s+x)z\bigr].

(1.36)

From that in Section
2.2
we derive the second formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(s,x)=(2i\beta)^{s-x}\,(z+i\beta)^{g-s}\,\frac{\Gamma\bigl((z-i\beta)\partial_{z}+x+g\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl((z-i\beta)\partial_{z}+s+g\bigr)}\,(z+i\beta)^{x-g}.

(1.37)

The explicit action of such operators on
\psi(z)
analytic in the upper half-plane can be written using the standard beta function integral
[
DLMF
,
(5.12.1)
]
. For the second one (
1.37
) we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bigl[\mathcal{K}(s,x)\,\psi\bigr](z)&=\frac{(2i\beta)^{s-x}}{\Gamma(s-x)}\,(z+i\beta)^{g-s}\,\int_{0}^{1}dt\;(1-t)^{s-x-1}\;t^{g+x-1}\\[6.0pt]
&\hskip 56.9055pt\times\bigl(t(z-i\beta)+2i\beta\bigr)^{x-g}\;\psi\bigl(t(z-i\beta)+i\beta\bigr),\end{aligned}

(1.38)

and for the first one see (
2.33
). Using the last formula in Section
3
we prove that reflection operator produces the same eigenfunctions
\Psi_{\lambda}(z)
(
1.32
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(s,i\lambda)\cdot 1=C(s,i\lambda)\;{}_{2}F_{1}\biggl(s+i\lambda,\,s-i\lambda,\,s+g;\,\frac{1}{2}+\frac{iz}{2\beta}\biggr)

(1.39)

with the normalization

C(s,i\lambda)=\frac{\Gamma(g+i\lambda)}{\Gamma(g+s)}.

(1.40)

Finally, in Section
2.3
we derive the third expression for the reflection operator with the integrals over upper half-plane

\displaystyle\bigl[\mathcal{K}(s,x)\,\psi\bigr](z)=e^{2\pi is}\,(2i\beta)^{s-x}\,\frac{\Gamma(g+x)\,\Gamma(3s-g)}{\Gamma^{2}(2s)}\,(z+i\beta)^{g-s}

(1.41)

\displaystyle\times\int\mathcal{D}w\,\mathcal{D}v\;(z-\bar{v})^{-g-x}\,(i\beta-\bar{v})^{x-s}\,(v-\bar{w})^{g-3s}\,(w+i\beta)^{x-g}\,\psi(w).

In Appendix
A
we describe how such integrals can be represented by diagrams. In particular, we obtain diagrammatic representation for the reflection operator (
1.41
) and the eigenfunctions (
1.32
), see Figures
1
and
10
. In this approach various properties of the eigenfunctions reduce to simple transformations of corresponding diagrams. This technique was crucial in the study of other
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
spin chains
[
DKM1
,
DKM2
]
.

The formula for the reflection operator equivalent to (
1.36
) has already appeared in the work
[
FGK
, eq. (3.23), (3.25)]
, which we discovered upon completing our paper. Since the derivation in
[
FGK
]
is quite different, we leave our presentation as it is, and clarify the connection with the paper
[
FGK
]
in Appendix
B
. We also remark that in
[
FGK
]
this reflection operator was used in a different way, namely, to construct local Hamiltonian with interaction at the boundaries for a spin chain with many particles.

To our knowledge the two other formulas for the reflection operator (
1.38
), (
1.41
) are new. The close relative of the second formula (
1.38
) plays the key role in the case of open
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
spin chain
[
ABDV
, Section 3]
.

In Section
4
we show that the eigenfunctions
\Psi_{\lambda}(z)
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
are orthogonal with respect to the scalar product (
1.7
)

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{\rho}|\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle=\mu^{-1}(\lambda)\;\frac{\delta(\lambda-\rho)+\delta(\lambda+\rho)}{2},

(1.42)

where the normalization coefficient

\displaystyle\mu(\lambda)=\frac{1}{4\pi\,(2\beta)^{2s}\,\Gamma(2s)}\;\biggl|\frac{\Gamma^{2}(s+i\lambda)\,\Gamma(g+i\lambda)}{\Gamma(s+g)\,\Gamma(2i\lambda)}\biggr|^{2}.

(1.43)

We give two proofs of the orthogonality: from the asymptotics of eigenfunctions and in the language of diagrams.

In Section
5
using Barnes representation of hypergeometric function we prove the completeness relation

\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\lambda\;\mu(\lambda)\;\Psi_{\lambda}(z)\,\overline{\Psi_{\lambda}(w)}=\frac{e^{i\pi s}}{(z-\bar{w})^{2s}}.

(1.44)

Here from the right we have the kernel of the identity operator on our Hilbert space

\psi(z)=\int\mathcal{D}z\,\frac{e^{i\pi s}}{(z-\bar{w})^{2s}}\,\psi(w),

(1.45)

the so-called
reproducing kernel

[
DKM1
, (A.7)]
.

Orthogonality and completeness are equivalent to the unitarity of the transform

[T\psi](\lambda)=\int\mathcal{D}z\;\overline{\Psi_{\lambda}(z)}\,\psi(z)

(1.46)

that maps the initial Hilbert space to the space of functions of
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
square integrable with respect to the measure
\mu(\lambda)\,d\lambda
and invariant under reflection
\lambda\to-\lambda
. Interestingly enough, the transform
T
has almost the same kernel as the well-known
index hypergeometric (or Jacobi) transform

[
N
]

\displaystyle[J\psi](\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\,\,y^{s+g-1}\,(1+y)^{s-g}\;\overline{\Psi_{\lambda}\bigl(i\beta(1+2y)\bigr)}\;\psi(y),

(1.47)

which is also unitary, but acts on the different space of functions. In Section
6
we discuss the relation between these transforms.

Many-particle problem

In this section we state the result for the eigenfunctions in the case of
n
particles

B(u)\,\Psi_{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\,\lambda_{n}}=\biggl(u-\frac{1}{2}\biggr)\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}(u^{2}+\lambda_{j}^{2})\;\Psi_{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\,\lambda_{n}}.

(1.48)

For this, apart from reflection operator, we need
\mathcal{R}
-operator acting on functions of two variables
z_{i}
and
z_{j}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{ij}(s,x)=\frac{\Gamma\bigl((z_{i}-z_{j})\partial_{z_{i}}+s+x\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl((z_{i}-z_{j})\partial_{z_{i}}+2s\bigr)}.

(1.49)

The explicit formula for its action can be obtained using beta function integral. We note in passing that it interchanges the arguments in the following Lax matrices

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{R}_{12}(s,x)\,L_{1}(u+s-1,u-s)\,L_{2}(u+s-1,u-x)\\[6.0pt]
&=L_{1}(u+s-1,u-x)\,L_{2}(u+s-1,u-s)\,\mathcal{R}_{12}(s,x).\end{aligned}

(1.50)

Using
\mathcal{R}
- and
\mathcal{K}
-operators we introduce an infinite-dimensional analog of the monodromy matrix (
1.15
)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}(x)=\mathcal{R}_{n\,n-1}(x)\cdots\mathcal{R}_{21}(x)\,\mathcal{K}_{1}(x)\,\mathcal{R}_{12}(x)\cdots\mathcal{R}_{n-1\,n}(x),

(1.51)

where the operator
\mathcal{K}_{1}(x)
acts on functions of
z_{1}
and in all operators we suppressed the dependence on spin
s
. Then eigenfunctions (
1.48
) are given by the formula

\displaystyle\Psi_{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\,\lambda_{n}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\Lambda_{n}(i\lambda_{n})\,\Lambda_{n-1}(i\lambda_{n-1})\cdots\Lambda_{1}(i\lambda_{1})\cdot 1.

(1.52)

The proof will be given elsewhere. Note that for the simplest
K
-matrix
K(u)=\bm{1}
such construction of eigenfunctions is given in
[
DKM2
]
.

Reflection operator

Solving reflection equation

The operator
\mathcal{K}(s,x)
acts on functions
\psi(z)
and satisfies reflection equation

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{K}(s,x)\,L(u+x-1,u-s)\,K(u)\,L(u+s-1,u-x)\\[6.0pt]
&=L(u+s-1,u-x)\,K(u)\,L(u+x-1,u-s)\,\mathcal{K}(s,x),\end{aligned}

(2.1)

where
2\times 2
matrices
L(u_{1},u_{2})
and
K(u)
are defined in (
1.11
) and (
1.12
). This matrix equation is equivalent to four equations between matrix elements. Each matrix element in turn is polynomial in
u
. Since
\mathcal{K}(s,x)
doesn’t depend on
u
, for each matrix element we obtain several equations corresponding to different degrees of
u
.

To find these equations, first, observe that Lax matrices can be written as

\displaystyle L(u+x-1,u-s)=\begin{pmatrix}u+\frac{x-s}{2}+J&J_{-}\\[8.0pt]
J_{+}&u+\frac{x-s}{2}-J\end{pmatrix},

(2.2)

\displaystyle L(u+s-1,u-x)=\begin{pmatrix}u-\frac{x-s}{2}+J&J_{-}\\[8.0pt]
J_{+}&u-\frac{x-s}{2}-J\end{pmatrix},

(2.3)

where
J,J_{\pm}
are
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
generators of spin
(s+x)/2

J=z\partial_{z}+\frac{s+x}{2},\qquad J_{-}=-\partial_{z},\qquad J_{+}=z^{2}\partial_{z}+(s+x)z.

(2.4)

They satisfy standard commutation relations

\displaystyle[J_{+},J_{-}]=2J,\qquad[J,J_{\pm}]=\pm J_{\pm}.

(2.5)

Note also that the Casimir element

C=2J^{2}+J_{+}J_{-}+J_{-}J_{+}=\frac{(s+x)(s+x-2)}{2}

(2.6)

is symmetric with respect to
s,x
.

On the other hand, products of matrices from opposite sides of equation (
2.1
) differ only by interchange of parameters
s\leftrightarrows x
. Using commutation relations (
2.5
) and formula for the Casimir element (
2.6
) one can check that all matrix elements in (
2.1
) are divisible by
(u-1/2)
up to some constants symmetric with respect to
s,x
.

Dividing each matrix element from (
2.1
) by
(u-1/2)
we compare coefficients behind remaining degrees of
u
. Their equality reduces to the following three relations

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\,N=N\,\mathcal{K},\qquad\mathcal{K}\,H^{x}=H^{s}\,\mathcal{K},\qquad\mathcal{K}\,I^{x,s}=I^{s,x}\,\mathcal{K}

(2.7)

with the operators

\displaystyle N=\frac{1}{2i\beta}(J_{+}-\beta^{2}J_{-}),

(2.8)

\displaystyle H^{s}=\Bigl(J+\frac{s-x}{2}\,\Bigr)^{2}+\beta^{2}J_{-}^{2}-2i\alpha J_{-},

(2.9)

\displaystyle I^{s,x}=JJ_{+}-\beta^{2}J_{-}J+\frac{s-x}{2}(J_{+}+\beta^{2}J_{-})+2i\alpha J.

(2.10)

Note that the first operator is symmetric with respect to
s,x

\displaystyle N=\frac{1}{2i\beta}\bigl[(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}+(s+x)z\bigr].

(2.11)

The second operator
H^{s}
doesn’t depend on
x
and coincides with one-particle Hamiltonian (
1.21
).

The reflection operator commutes with
N
(
2.7
). Hence, we can search for it in the form

\mathcal{K}=f(N).

(2.12)

Let us derive equation on the function
f
from the two remaining relations in (
2.7
). Denote

N_{\pm}=-J\pm\frac{1}{2i\beta}(J_{+}+\beta^{2}J_{-}).

(2.13)

The operators
N,N_{\pm}
are linear combinations of generators
J,J_{\pm}
and satisfy the same commutation relations (
2.5
)

\displaystyle[N_{+},N_{-}]=2N,\qquad[N,N_{\pm}]=\pm N_{\pm}.

(2.14)

From the second relation we deduce

f(N)\,N_{\pm}=N_{\pm}\,f(N\pm 1).

(2.15)

Now we express old generators in terms of new ones

\displaystyle J_{+}=\frac{i\beta}{2}(N_{+}-N_{-}+2N),

(2.16)

\displaystyle J_{-}=\frac{i}{2\beta}(N_{+}-N_{-}-2N),\qquad J=-\frac{1}{2}(N_{+}+N_{-})

(2.17)

and using these relations rewrite operators
H^{s}
(
2.9
) and
I^{s,x}
(
2.10
) in terms of new generators

\displaystyle H^{s}=\biggl(N+\frac{x-s-1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\biggr)N_{+}+\biggl(-N+\frac{x-s-1}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\biggr)N_{-}

\displaystyle\hskip 15.6491pt-2N^{2}-\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}N+\frac{s^{2}+x^{2}-s-x}{2},

(2.18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i\beta}\,I^{s,x}=-\biggl(N+\frac{x-s-1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\biggr)N_{+}+\biggl(-N+\frac{x-s-1}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\biggr)N_{-}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+N.

(2.19)

With the help of these formulas and formula (
2.15
) we reduce two relations

f(N)\,H^{x}=H^{s}\,f(N),\qquad f(N)\,I^{x,s}=I^{s,x}\,f(N)

(2.20)

to the single difference equation

\displaystyle\frac{f(N+1)}{f(N)}=\frac{N+\frac{x-s+1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}{N+\frac{s-x+1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}.

(2.21)

Its solution can be written in terms of gamma functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(s,x)=f(N)=\frac{\Gamma\bigl(N+\frac{x-s}{2}+g\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(N+\frac{s-x}{2}+g\bigr)}.

(2.22)

where
g=1/2+\alpha/\beta
.

Of course, the expression (
2.22
) multiplied by any periodic function (with unit period) also solves the above equation. In particular, we can take function
f(N)
with reflected parameter
\beta\to-\beta
since the initial equation (
2.1
) depends on
\beta^{2}
. We chose specifically the solution (
2.22
), because in the case

\beta>0

(2.23)

it gives us analytic in the upper half-plane eigenfunctions of one-particle Hamiltonian
H^{s}
(
1.21
), see Section

Beta integral representations

In this section we obtain explicit formula for the action of the operator
\mathcal{K}(s,x)
found in the previous section

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(s,x)=\frac{\Gamma\bigl(N+\frac{x-s}{2}+g\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(N+\frac{s-x}{2}+g\bigr)},\qquad N=\frac{1}{2i\beta}\bigl[(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}+(s+x)z\bigr].

(2.24)

Moreover, to relate this operator to the hypergeometric function we derive another formula for it

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(s,x)=(2i\beta)^{s-x}\,(z+i\beta)^{g-s}\frac{\Gamma\bigl((z-i\beta)\partial_{z}+x+g\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl((z-i\beta)\partial_{z}+s+g\bigr)}\,(z+i\beta)^{x-g}.

(2.25)

For both tasks we use the Euler’s beta integral
[
DLMF
,
(5.12.1)
]
.

First, notice that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} N&=\frac{1}{2i\beta}\bigl[(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}+(s+x)z\bigr]\\[6.0pt]
&=(z+i\beta)^{-s-x}\;\biggl(\frac{1}{2i\beta}(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}+\frac{s+x}{2}\biggr)\;(z+i\beta)^{s+x}.\end{aligned}

(2.26)

As a result, the expression (
2.24
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}&=(z+i\beta)^{-s-x}\;\frac{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{1}{2i\beta}(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}+x+g\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{1}{2i\beta}(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}+s+g\bigr)}\;(z+i\beta)^{s+x}.\end{aligned}

(2.27)

The second step is to use Euler’s beta integral for the ratio of two gamma functions

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s-x)}\,(z+i\beta)^{-s-x}\,\int_{0}^{1}&dt\;(1-t)^{s-x-1}\,t^{g+x-1}\\[6.0pt]
&\times t^{\frac{1}{2i\beta}(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}}\;(z+i\beta)^{s+x}.\end{aligned}

(2.28)

Under conformal map

\displaystyle\zeta=\frac{z-i\beta}{z+i\beta},\qquad z=i\beta\,\frac{1+\zeta}{1-\zeta}

(2.29)

the operator in the integrand (
2.28
) drastically simplifies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2i\beta}(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}=\zeta\partial_{\zeta}.

(2.30)

Hence, acting on arbitrary function
\psi(z)
we obtain

\displaystyle t^{\frac{1}{2i\beta}(z^{2}+\beta^{2})\partial_{z}}\,\psi(z)

\displaystyle=t^{\zeta\partial_{\zeta}}\;\psi\biggl(i\beta\,\frac{1+\zeta}{1-\zeta}\biggr)=\psi\biggl(i\beta\,\frac{z+i\beta+t(z-i\beta)}{z+i\beta-t(z-i\beta)}\biggr).

(2.31)

Note that for
\operatorname{Im}z\geq 0
,
t\in[0,1]
and
\beta>0
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\biggl(i\beta\,\frac{z+i\beta+t(z-i\beta)}{z+i\beta-t(z-i\beta)}\biggr)\geq 0,

(2.32)

and equality holds iff
\operatorname{Im}z=0
,
t=1
. Thus, for the function
\psi(z)
analytic in the upper half-plane we stay in the region of analyticity. Acting on such function with operator (
2.28
) we arrive at

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}\,\psi(z)&=\frac{(2i\beta)^{s+x}}{\Gamma(s-x)}\,\int_{0}^{1}dt\;(1-t)^{s-x-1}\,t^{g+x-1}\\[6.0pt]
&\times\bigl(z+i\beta-t(z-i\beta)\bigr)^{-s-x}\;\psi\biggl(i\beta\,\frac{z+i\beta+t(z-i\beta)}{z+i\beta-t(z-i\beta)}\biggr).\end{aligned}

(2.33)

This formula represents the explicit action of the operator (
2.24
).

To derive the second formula (
2.25
) we change integration variable
t\to u

\displaystyle u=\frac{2i\beta\,t}{z+i\beta-t(z-i\beta)}.

(2.34)

After this change we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}\,\psi(z)=\frac{(2i\beta)^{s-x}}{\Gamma(s-x)}\,&(z+i\beta)^{g-s}\,\int_{C}du\;(1-u)^{s-x-1}\;u^{g+x-1}\\[6.0pt]
&\times\bigl(u(z-i\beta)+2i\beta\bigr)^{x-g}\;\psi\bigl(u(z-i\beta)+i\beta\bigr)\end{aligned}

(2.35)

with some contour
{C}
from
u=0
to
u=1
. Notice that the second line can be equivalently written as

\quad\bigl(u(z-i\beta)+2i\beta\bigr)^{x-g}\;\psi\bigl(u(z-i\beta)+i\beta\bigr)\\[6.0pt]
=u^{(z-i\beta)\partial_{z}}\;(z+i\beta)^{x-g}\;\psi(z).\quad

(2.36)

Let us argue that the contour
{C}
can be deformed to the interval
[0,1]
without touching any singular points of the integrand. For
\operatorname{Im}z\geq 0
,
t\in[0,1]
and
\beta>0
the new variable
u
(
2.34
) has properties

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}u\in[0,1],\qquad\sign(\operatorname{Im}u)=\sign(\operatorname{Re}z).

(2.37)

Therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\bigl(u(z-i\beta)+i\beta\bigr)=\beta(1-\operatorname{Re}u)+\operatorname{Im}z\operatorname{Re}u+\operatorname{Re}z\operatorname{Im}u\geq 0,

(2.38)

and by decreasing
|\operatorname{Im}u\,|\searrow 0
we stay in the analyticity domain of the function

\displaystyle\bigl(u(z-i\beta)+2i\beta\bigr)^{x-g}\;\psi\bigl(u(z-i\beta)+i\beta\bigr).

(2.39)

So, deforming contour we finally arrive at the formula

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}\,\psi(z)=\frac{(2i\beta)^{s-x}}{\Gamma(s-x)}\,(z+i\beta)^{g-s}\,\int_{0}^{1}&du\;(1-u)^{s-x-1}\;u^{g+x-1}\\[6.0pt]
&\times u^{(z-i\beta)\partial_{z}}\;(z+i\beta)^{x-g}\;\psi(z),\end{aligned}

(2.40)

which is equivalent to (
2.25

Orthogonality

Recall the scalar product (
1.7
)

\displaystyle\langle\chi|\psi\rangle=\int\mathcal{D}z\;\overline{\chi(z)}\,\psi(z),

(4.1)

where integration is performed over upper half-plane
\operatorname{Im}z>0
and the measure is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}z=\frac{2s-1}{\pi}\,(2\operatorname{Im}z)^{2s-2}\;d\operatorname{Re}z\;d\operatorname{Im}z.

(4.2)

In this section we prove in two ways that eigenfunctions

\displaystyle\Psi_{\lambda}(z)={}_{2}F_{1}\biggl(s+i\lambda,\,s-i\lambda,\,s+g;\,\frac{1}{2}+\frac{iz}{2\beta}\biggr)

(4.3)

with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
are orthogonal with respect to this scalar product

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{\rho}|\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle=\mu^{-1}(\lambda)\;\frac{\delta(\lambda-\rho)+\delta(\lambda+\rho)}{2}.

(4.4)

The normalization coefficient

\displaystyle\mu(\lambda)=\frac{1}{4\pi\,(2\beta)^{2s}\,\Gamma(2s)}\;\biggl|\frac{\Gamma^{2}(s+i\lambda)\,\Gamma(g+i\lambda)}{\Gamma(s+g)\,\Gamma(2i\lambda)}\biggr|^{2}

(4.5)

plays the role of integration measure in completeness relation, see Section
5
. Note that eigenfunctions (
4.3
) are invariant under reflection
\lambda\to-\lambda
, so that in the relation (
4.4
) we have symmetrized delta function.

Proof from asymptotics

The first proof is standard and usually mentioned in introductory texts on quantum mechanics (for example, see
[
FY
, §36]
). The idea is to cut-off the scalar product

\langle\chi|\psi\rangle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\langle\chi|\psi\rangle_{R},

(4.6)

so that we can write

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\Psi_{\rho}|\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle&=\lim_{R\to\infty}\langle\Psi_{\rho}|\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle_{R}\\[6.0pt]
&=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\rho^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\Bigl[\langle\Psi_{\rho}|H^{s}\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle_{R}-\langle H^{s}\Psi_{\rho}|\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle_{R}\Bigr]\\[6.0pt]
&=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\rho^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\bigl[\text{ boundary terms }\bigr],\end{aligned}

(4.7)

where on the last step we integrate by parts. Then we only need to calculate asymptotics of boundary terms. For this calculation we choose polar coordinates

z=re^{i\varphi}

(4.8)

and scalar product with radius cut-off

\langle\chi|\psi\rangle_{R}=\frac{2s-1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\varphi\,(2\sin\varphi)^{2s-2}\int_{0}^{R}dr\,r^{2s-1}\,\overline{\chi(re^{i\varphi})}\;\psi(re^{i\varphi}).

(4.9)

Moreover, since Hamiltonian (
1.21
) can be expressed in terms of spin operators

H^{s}=S^{2}+\beta^{2}S_{-}^{2}-2i\alpha S_{-},\qquad S=z\partial_{z}+s,\qquad S_{-}=-\partial_{z},

(4.10)

we first calculate boundary terms that arise from each spin operator (when we integrate it by parts).

Rewriting derivatives

\displaystyle\partial_{z}=\frac{e^{-i\varphi}}{2}\partial_{r}+\frac{e^{-i\varphi}}{2ir}\partial_{\varphi},\qquad\partial_{\bar{z}}=\frac{e^{i\varphi}}{2}\partial_{r}-\frac{e^{i\varphi}}{2ir}\partial_{\varphi}

(4.11)

we express action of spin operators on analytic functions as

\displaystyle S\psi(z)=(z\partial_{z}+\bar{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}+s)\psi(z)=(r\partial_{r}+s)\psi(z),

(4.12)

\displaystyle S_{-}\psi(z)=-(\partial_{z}+\partial_{\bar{z}})\psi(z)=\biggl(\frac{\sin\varphi}{r}\,\partial_{\varphi}-\cos\varphi\,\partial_{r}\biggr)\psi(z).

(4.13)

Next define Wronskians with respect to polar coordinates

\displaystyle W_{r}[\chi,\psi]=\chi\,\partial_{r}\psi-\psi\,\partial_{r}\chi,

(4.14)

\displaystyle W_{\varphi}[\chi,\psi]=\chi\,\partial_{\varphi}\psi-\psi\,\partial_{\varphi}\chi.

(4.15)

Using (
4.12
), (
4.13
) and integrating by parts we establish the following two identities

\displaystyle\langle\chi|S\psi\rangle_{R}=\langle-S\chi|\psi\rangle_{R}+\frac{2s-1}{\pi}\,R^{2s}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\varphi\,(2\sin\varphi)^{2s-2}\;\bigl(\bar{\chi}\,\psi\bigr)\bigr|_{r=R},

(4.16)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\chi|S_{-}\psi\rangle_{R}&=\langle-S_{-}\chi|\psi\rangle_{R}\\[6.0pt]
&-\frac{2s-1}{\pi}\,R^{2s-1}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\varphi\,(2\sin\varphi)^{2s-2}\,\cos\varphi\;\bigl(\bar{\chi}\,\psi\bigr)\bigr|_{r=R}.\end{aligned}

(4.17)

Notice that for the functions
\chi,\psi
decaying fast enough boundary terms tend to zero as
R\to\infty
, so that spin operators are anti-hermitian with respect to original scalar product. Iterating the above identities two times we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\chi|S^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}&=\langle S^{2}\chi|\psi\rangle_{R}\\[6.0pt]
&+\frac{2s-1}{\pi}\,R^{2s+1}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\varphi\,(2\sin\varphi)^{2s-2}\;W_{r}[\bar{\chi},\psi]\bigr|_{r=R},\end{aligned}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\chi|S_{-}^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}&=\langle S_{-}^{2}\chi|\psi\rangle_{R}\\[6.0pt]
&+\frac{2s-1}{\pi}\,R^{2s-1}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\varphi\,(2\sin\varphi)^{2s-2}\,\cos^{2}\varphi\;\,W_{r}[\bar{\chi},\psi]\bigr|_{r=R}\\[6.0pt]
&+\frac{2s-1}{2\pi}\,R^{2s-2}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\varphi\,(2\sin\varphi)^{2s-1}\,\cos\varphi\;\,W_{\varphi}[\psi,\bar{\chi}]\bigr|_{r=R}.\end{aligned}

(4.19)

Thus, when we integrate by parts the Hamiltonian (
4.10

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{\rho}|H^{s}\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle_{R}=\langle H^{s}\Psi_{\rho}|\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle_{R}\;+\text{ boundary terms},

(4.20)

boundary terms are given by the formulas (
4.17
), (
4.18

4.19

(4.32)

We note in passing that it is related to the well-known Cauchy integral
