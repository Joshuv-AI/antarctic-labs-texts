Fast Obstacle Avoidance Based on Real-Time Sensing
Lukas Huber ⋅ \cdot Jean-Jacques Slotine ⋅ \cdot Aude Billard

Abstract
Humans are remarkable at navigating and moving through dynamic and complex spaces, such as crowded streets. For robots to do the same, it is crucial that they are endowed with highly reactive obstacle avoidance robust to partial and poor sensing. We address the issue of enabling obstacle avoidance based on sparse and asynchronous perception. The proposed control scheme combines a high-level input command provided by either a planner or a human operator with fast reactive obstacle avoidance. The sampling-based sensor data can be combined with an analytical reconstruction of the obstacles for real-time collision avoidance. We can ensure that the agent does not get stuck when a feasible path exists between obstacles. The algorithm was evaluated experimentally on static laser data from cluttered, indoor office environments. Additionally, it was used in a shared control mode in a dynamic and complex outdoor environment in the center of Lausanne. The proposed control scheme successfully avoided collisions in both scenarios. During the experiments, the controller on the onboard computer took 1 millisecond to evaluate over 30000 data points.



I
Introduction

Local avoidance algorithms have to take into account new sensory information in real-time and adapt the global path to ensure safe navigation in the environment.

One approach to elevate distance sensor data is the Vector Field Histograms (VFH) method, which has been applied on mobile robots to navigate autonomously
[
1
]
. VFH has the advantage of taking measurement uncertainties into account. Furthermore, the short evaluation time of VFH makes it applicable to shared control on wheelchairs
[
2
,
3
]
. However, VFH has the shortcoming of the control velocity being zero in narrow pathways, for example, in front of (narrow) doorways.

An alternative set of approaches are sampling-based methos. They have been used for fast self-collision avoidance of robots in joint space
[
4
,
5
]
. The collision-free environment is taught using the sampled data. As the models require training before execution, the methods cannot adapt to fast-changing environments.

Conversely, the Velocity Obstacle (VO) approach is designed for dynamic environments. VO takes into account the motion of the obstacles and their potential future positions to calculate a collision-free motion
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. VO has been successfully used in shared control on wheelchairs
[
9
]
. However, VO is prone to local minima, which predominantly becomes apparent in static, cluttered environments.

Recently, real-time optimization algorithms have become feasible for implementing onboard robots thanks to the improved computational performance of hardware, for example, model predictive control
[
10
]
or quadratic programming
[
11
]
. However, most optimization problems cannot guarantee convergence to a feasible solution.

Recent work on Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) can ensure a feasible solution by softening some constraints
[
12
]
or using slack variables
[
13
]
. Nevertheless, these CBFs require a Lyapunov candidate function to reach the goal position and cannot be applied to dynamic environments.

Conversely, artificial potential fields create a closed-form, repulsive field to avoid collision with obstacles
[
14
]
. They can be elevated with navigation functions to ensure convergence to the desired goal around concave obstacles
[
15
,
16
]
.
Recent work extends the navigation function to discovering obstacles’ positions at runtime, but the approach relies on prior knowledge about the obstacles’ shapes
[
17
]
.
However, navigation functions are difficult to tune around dynamic obstacles. Moreover, no approach exists that combines the method with high-level control inputs, as is the case for shared control.

Similarly,
[
18
]
creates a dynamical system based algorithm inspired by harmonic potential fields, which can ensure collision-free motion in dynamic environments.
This has been extended to ensure convergence around concave obstacles by taking a (linear) velocity towards a goal and guiding it around all obstacles
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
.
However, the approach requires the analytical description of the obstacles, and interpreting the sensor data onboard a robot often introduces a significant delay
[
21
]
.

II
Preliminaries

The state of the robotics system
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
refers to the position of a
d
-dimensional state throughout this work.
The evolution of the position is governed by a dynamical system

\dot{\xi}=\mathbf{f}(t,\xi)\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{f}(t,\xi):(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

(1)

We further introduce a linear (autonomous) dynamical system which is asymptotically stable at the attractor position
\xi^{a}
:

\mathbf{f}^{l}(\xi)=-(\xi-\xi^{a})\qquad\text{with}\quad\xi^{\in}\mathbb{R}^{d}

(2)

II-A

Obstacles and Gamma Function

As in
[
19
]
, a continuous distance function
\Gamma(\xi):\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{X}^{i}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}
is defined around each obstacle, and divides the space into three regions:

Exterior points:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{e}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\Gamma(\xi)>1\}

Margin exterior points:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{g}=\{\xi-(R+D^{\mathrm{gap}})\mathbf{r}(\xi)\in\mathcal{X}^{e}\}

Boundary points:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{b}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\Gamma(\xi)=1\}

(3)

Interior points:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{i}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(\mathcal{X}^{e}\cup\mathcal{X}^{b})\}

where
D^{\mathrm{gap}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the margin distance, and
R\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
the robot radius.
By construction
\Gamma(\cdot)
increases monotonically (
C^{1}
-smoothness) with increasing distance from the center
\xi^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The obstacle avoidance algorithm
[
20
]
is defined for
star-shapes
, i.e., a shape where there exists a reference point
\xi^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
within the obstacle, such that any surface point can be seen:

\exists\,\xi^{r}\in\mathcal{X}^{i}\quad:\;\;\langle{\mathbf{r}(\xi)},\,{\mathbf{n}(\xi)}\rangle>0\;\;\;\;\forall\xi\in\mathcal{X}^{e}

(4)

where
\mathbf{r}(\xi)=(\xi-\xi^{r})/\|\xi-\xi^{r}\|
with
\mathbf{r}(\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;\|\mathbf{r}(\xi)\|=1
is denoted as the normalized reference direction.
An environment made up of
star-shaped
obstacles is referred to as a
star world
.

II-B

Obstacle Avoidance through Modulation

Assuming the agents nominal velocity command of
\mathbf{v}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which restuls from a user’s input or a high-level planner evaluated at position
\xi
.
As in
[
20
]
, the collision free velocity around a single obstacle is obtained by applying a dynamic modulation matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
:

\dot{\xi}=\mathbf{M}(\xi,\mathbf{v}^{N})\mathbf{v}^{N}\;\text{,}\quad\mathbf{M}(\xi,\mathbf{v}^{N})=\mathbf{E}(\xi)\mathbf{D}(\xi,\mathbf{v}^{N})\mathbf{E}(\xi)^{-1}

(5)

The basis matrix is defined as:

\mathbf{E}(\xi)=\left[{\mathbf{r}}(\xi)\;\;\mathbf{e}_{1}(\xi)\;\;..\;\;\mathbf{e}_{d-1}(\xi)\right]

(6)

where
\mathbf{e}_{(\cdot)}
form an orthonormal basis of the tangent plane.
1
1

1

For a spherical obstacle, decomposition matrix
\mathbf{E}
is orthonormal, hence
\mathbf{E}^{-1}=\mathbf{E}^{T}
.

The diagonal eigenvalue matrix
\mathbf{D}(\xi)
defines the stretching in each direction.

III
Fast Obstacle Avoidance

When navigating in cluttered environments, such as dense crowds, the algorithm described
[
21
]
, requires applying the modulation for all obstacles at each time step. This results in a high computational cost which increases linearly with the number of obstacles.
We propose a computationally cheaper algorithm by creating a single
virtual
obstacle that encapsulates all obstacles. The modulation is then only applied to these obstacles while ensuring collision avoidance for all obstacles. The virtual obstacle has a single modulation matrix
\mathbf{M}(\xi)
with corresponding decomposition matrix
\mathbf{E}(\xi)
and diagonal stretching matrix
\mathbf{D}(\xi)

III-A

Single Modulation Obstacle Avoidance

To evaluate the decomposition matrix
\mathbf{E}(\xi)
as defined in (
6
), we use the normalized reference direction
\mathbf{r}^{o}(\xi)=\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)/\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)\|
, and the tangent directions
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{o}(\xi)
form the orthonormal basis to
\mathbf{n}^{o}(\xi)
. The vectors are further described in the following subsections.

III-A
1
Averaged Reference Direction

The averaged reference direction
\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)
is evaluated as the weighted sum:

\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)=\sum_{i}w_{i}^{o}(\xi)\mathbf{r}_{i}^{o}(\xi)\quad i=1..N^{\mathrm{obs}}

(7)

where the reference directions
\mathbf{r}^{o}_{i}(\xi)
are evaluated for each obstacle as described in (
4
).
Additionally, the influence weights
w_{i}^{o}(\xi)
are given by

w^{o}_{i}(\xi)=\begin{cases}\hat{w}^{o}_{i}(\xi)/\hat{w}^{\mathrm{sum}}\quad&\text{if}\;\;\hat{w}^{\mathrm{sum}}>1\\
{w}^{o}_{i}(\xi)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,,\;w^{\mathrm{sum}}=\sum_{i}\hat{w}_{i}^{o}(\xi)

(8)

which is a function of the distance Gamma, see (
3
), and evaluated for each obstacle
i
as follows:

\hat{w}^{o}_{i}(\xi)=\left(\frac{D^{\mathrm{scal}}}{D^{o}_{i}(\xi)}\right)^{s}\qquad\text{with}\quad D_{i}^{o}(\xi)=\Gamma_{i}(\xi)-1

(9)

where
s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the scaling-potential and
D^{\mathrm{scal}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. We choose
s=2
and
D^{\mathrm{scal}}=1
.

III-A
2
Summing of the Normal Direction

First, the normal offset is evaluated for all obstacles

{\mathbf{n}}^{\Delta}(\xi)=\sum^{N^{\mathrm{obs}}}_{i=1}w_{i}^{o}(\xi)\left(\mathbf{n}_{i}(\xi)-\mathbf{r}^{o}_{i}(\xi)\right)

(10)

where
N^{\mathrm{obs}}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
is the number of obstacles.
Finally, the weighted normal vector is obtained as:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{n}}(\xi)=\hat{\mathbf{n}}(\xi)/\|\hat{\mathbf{n}}(\xi)\|\quad\text{with}\;\;\hat{\mathbf{n}}(\xi)=c^{n}\mathbf{r}(\xi)+{\mathbf{n}}^{\Delta}(\xi)

(11)

\displaystyle c^{n}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\;\;p^{nr}<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\\
\sqrt{2}p^{nr}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\;\;\text{with}\;p^{nr}=(-1)\frac{\langle{\mathbf{r}^{o}(\xi)},\,{\mathbf{n}^{\Delta}(\xi)}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{n}^{\Delta}(\xi)\|}

The scaling factor
c^{n}\in[1,\sqrt{2}]
ensures that the decomposition matrix
\mathbf{E}(\xi)
is invertible, see Appendix
-A
for further explanation.

III-B

Eigenvalue Matrix

The diagonal eigenvalue matrix is given as:

\mathbf{D}(\xi)=\textbf{diag}\left(\lambda^{r}(\xi),\lambda^{e}(\xi),\,\ldots,\lambda^{e}(\xi)\right)

(12)

with eigenvalues in reference direction
\lambda_{r}(\xi)\in]0,1[
and in tangent
\lambda_{e}(\xi)\in]1,2[
are a function of the averaged reference direction
\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)
:

\lambda_{r}(\xi)=1-\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)\|^{\rho}\qquad\lambda_{e}(\xi)=1+\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)\|^{\rho}

(13)

where
\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the reactivity, see
[
19
]
for more information.

III-C

Tail Negligence

The modulation-based obstacle avoidance is active even when the nominal dynamics
v^{N}
are already moving away from an obstacle. This can be an undesired effect since, in this case, the unconstrained velocity already ensures collision avoidance. This
tail
effect can be reduced by modifying the wake eigenvalues while still ensuring a smooth vector field by adapting the eigenvalues as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda^{e,t}(\xi)=w^{r}w^{v}+(1-w^{r}w^{v})\lambda^{e}(\xi)

\displaystyle\lambda^{r,t}(\xi)=\lambda^{e,t}(\xi)w^{r}\text{sgn}(w^{v})+(1-w^{r}\text{sgn}(w^{v}))\lambda^{r}(\xi)

with the weights given as

w^{r}=\min\left(1,\frac{1}{\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)\|}\right)\quad w^{v}=\max\left(0,\frac{\langle{\mathbf{r}^{o}(\xi)},\,{\mathbf{v}^{N}}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{v}^{N}\|}\right)^{c^{w}}

(14)

where
c^{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the power weight, we choose
c^{w}=0.2
.

III-C
1
Decreasing Tail Weight

The importance of obstacles in the wake of the nominal velocity
v^{N}
can be further decreased by modifying the weight based on the nominal velocity
\mathbf{v}
. For this we restate the weight from Eq. (
8

\hat{w}_{i}^{o}=\hat{w}_{i}^{o}\left(\frac{\hat{w}_{i}^{o}}{\sum_{i}\hat{w}^{o}_{i}}\right)^{1/c_{i}},\;\;c_{i}=1-\frac{\langle\mathbf{v}^{N},\,\mathbf{r}_{i}^{o}(\xi)\rangle}{\|\mathbf{v}^{N}\|\,\|\mathbf{r}_{i}^{o}(\xi)\|}

for all obstacles
i=1..N^{\mathrm{obs}}
.

IV-A

Reference Summing for Sampled Data

First, we evaluate the reference direction to each point
i

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}_{i}(\xi)=\frac{\xi^{p}_{i}-\xi}{\|\xi^{p}_{i}-\xi\|}\qquad i=1..N^{p}

(15)

Additionally, the distance is evaluated as

D_{i}(\xi)=\|\xi^{p}_{i}-\xi\|-R\qquad i=1..N^{p}

(16)

This provides the reference direction similar to (
7
). Since each data point is approximated with a small circle, the decomposition matrix
\mathbf{E}(\xi)
is constructed to be orthogonal.
Furthermore, we introduce the weight sum for discrete data points:

w^{\mathrm{sum}}=\min\left(\sum_{i}\hat{w}_{i}^{p}(\xi),w^{\mathrm{norm}}\right)

(17)

where
w^{\mathrm{norm}}>0
is the maximum distance weight, for more information see Appendix
-A
.

IV-B

Eigenvalues Based on Reference Direction

The modulated velocity
\dot{\xi}
needs to be tangent to the surface or point away from it when approaching the obstacle to ensure collision avoidance. Conversely, the obstacle avoidance controller should have no effect far away from any obstacle.

Correspondingly, the magnitude of the reference direction
\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|
reaches zeros far away from an obstacle, and approaches infinity when on the surface. Hence, we can use the magnitude to define the eigenvalues as follows:

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda^{r}(\xi)=\lambda^{e}(\xi)=1

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda^{r}(\xi)/\lambda^{e}(\xi)=0

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|\rightarrow\infty\quad\Rightarrow\quad|\lambda^{e}(\xi)|/\lambda^{r}(\xi)=0

Additionally, we set the constraint that
\lambda^{e}(\xi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, since the velocity in the tangent direction should never be reflected.
We choose the eigenvalue in reference direction
\lambda^{r}(\xi,\mathbf{v})\in[1,\,-1[
as:

\displaystyle\lambda^{r}(\xi,\mathbf{v})=\begin{cases}(-1)\lambda^{r,0}(\xi)\quad&\text{if}\;\;\langle{\mathbf{r}^{p}(\xi)},\,{\mathbf{v}}\rangle<0,\;\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|>1\\
\lambda^{r,0}(\xi)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\quad\text{with}\;\;\lambda^{r,0}(\xi)=\begin{cases}\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|)&\text{if}\;\;\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|<2\\
-1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(18)

Additionally the eigenvalues in tangent direction
\lambda^{r}(\xi)\in[1,\,2]
are given by

\lambda^{e}(\xi)=\begin{cases}1+\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|)&\text{if}\;\;\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|<1\\
2\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\|}\right)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(19)

where
\pi
is the circle constant.
Note that the eigenvalues are
C^{1}
-smooth, because of
\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x)|_{x=\pi/2}=0
and
\frac{d}{dx}\cos(x)|_{x=\pi}=0
. The resulting vector field can be seen in Fig.

V
Disparate Obstacle Descriptions

Detection algorithms can fail to identify certain obstacles. Hence, we are left with a mix of analytic obstacles and sampled sensor data. Furthermore, the analytic reconstruction of the obstacles often comes with a significant delay in time.
This results in asynchronous reception of information on obstacles, with fast sampled-based information and slow analytical model acquisition.
We address this by proposing an approach to fuse analytic obstacle descriptions (see Sec.
III
) with sample-based sensor data (see Sec.
IV
).

The first step is to remove sampled obstacle data which has been identified as an analytic obstacle. Hence, the laserscan weights in (
17
) are set to zero if they lie withing the obstacle
o=1..N^{\mathrm{obs}}
:

\hat{w}^{o}_{i}(\xi)\leftarrow 0\qquad\text{if}\;\;\exists\,o:\,\Gamma_{o}(\xi_{i})\leq 1

(20)

V-A

Fusion Weights of Sampled and Analytic Obstacles

The magnitude of the reference direction of the sampled and analytic obstacles gives us information about the proximity to obstacles.
Hence, the reference magnitude can be used to calculate the importance weight of the sampled data
w^{p}\in[0,1]
and analytic obstacles
w^{o}\in[0,1]
as:

\begin{bmatrix}w^{p}\\
w^{o}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\hat{w}^{p}+\hat{w}^{o}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{w}^{p}\\
\hat{w}^{o}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{with}\;\;\begin{cases}\hat{w}^{p}=1/\left(1-\left\|\bar{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)\right\|\right)-1\\
\hat{w}^{o}=1/\left(1-\left\|\bar{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)\right\|\right)-1\end{cases}

where
i=1..N^{\mathrm{obs}}
. The weight is used to evaluate the reference direction of the mixed environment:

\mathbf{r}^{m}(\xi)=w^{l}(\xi)\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{p}(\xi)+w^{o}(\xi)\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{o}(\xi)

(21)

The analytical description has the additional advantage that it often provides information about the velocity and shape of the obstacles. This can be used to ensure collision avoidance in dynamic environments. We propose to include the velocity similar to
[
21
]
for the fused scenario as follows:

\dot{\mathbf{\xi}}={\mathbf{M}(\xi,\mathbf{v}^{N})}\left(\mathbf{v}^{N}-\dot{\tilde{\xi}}^{\mathrm{d,\mathrm{tot}}}\right)+\dot{\tilde{\xi}}^{d,\mathrm{tot}}\;\,\quad\dot{\tilde{\xi}}^{d,\mathrm{tot}}=w^{o}\dot{\tilde{\xi}}^{\mathrm{tot}}

(22)

where
\dot{\tilde{\xi}}^{\mathrm{tot}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the averaged velocity of all analytical obstacles.

V-B

Asynchronous Evaluation

The disparate data is combined by using a controller with three inputs: the velocity command from the human operator
\mathbf{v}^{N}
, the laser scan reading
\xi^{p}
, and the estimated position of the robot
\xi
(see Fig.
6
).

The two sensor loops run at different frequencies, with the sample-based evaluator running at a higher update rate than the analytic evaluator. The latter is delayed due to the processing time to generate analytic obstacle descriptions. However, the analytic descriptions enable improved behavior due to the information about the obstacle’s shape and velocity.

V-C

Uniform Importance Scaling

An obstacle described by sampled data points or an analytic function should have the same effect on the modulation. An adequate scaling is necessary since a single obstacle often corresponds to multiple data points.

Let us consider an obstacle that can be approximated by radius
R^{\mathrm{obs}}
at a distance
D
. The number of sampling data points
N^{p}
can be approximated as:
3
3

3

If the obstacle sampling is volumetric, e.g., by obtaining a collision model of the robot in higher dimensions, the power value of the right side is
d
, i.e.,
N^{p}\sim(\cdot)^{d}
.

N^{p}\sim\left(\frac{R^{\mathrm{obs}}}{\tan(\delta)D}\right)^{d-1}\sim\left(\frac{R^{\mathrm{obs}}}{\delta D}\right)^{d-1}

(23)

It follows that the scaling weight of the sampled data is set proportional to the sampling angle
\delta
:

w^{\mathrm{scal},p}\approx\delta^{d-1}

(24)

Moreover, the scaling weight of the analytic obstacle description is dependent on the size and distance.

w^{\mathrm{scal},o}\approx\left(R^{\mathrm{obs}}/D\right)^{d-1}

(25)

As a result, we set the distance scaling

D^{\mathrm{scal}}=2\pi/\delta^{(d-1)}
in mixed environments.

VII
Discussion

This work presents a modulation-based approach for real-time obstacle avoidance based on dynamical systems. The proposed method excels in its speed and can be applied to sampled data, such as Lidar or laser scans combined with analytic obstacle descriptions.
We proved the absence of local minima in free space, and an agent controlled with our algorithm only stopped when directly driving towards obstacles. Furthermore, the method is scalable to higher dimensions.
Experimental validation was performed in simulation, static sensor data, and the standing wheelchair QOLO. It was shown that the algorithm avoids collisions while navigating in static and dynamic environments.
The evaluation with sampled sensor data showed that the algorithm could pass narrow doorways. Moreover, the algorithm had a short evaluation time onboard the robot.

VII-A

Future Work

Most theoretical development in this work is for multiple-dimensional collision avoidance. However, the implementation is on a mobile agent in crowds for navigation in two-dimensions. Future implementations will focus on exploiting the scalability to higher dimensions. Sampled data is obtained through distance sensors for two and three-dimensional cases. Conversely, in higher dimensional space, e.g., joint space, sampled data about the environment can be obtained through sampling in simulations
