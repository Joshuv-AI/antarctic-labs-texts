Non-degenerate Birkhoff functions
Mauricio Garay and Duco van Straten

Abstract
. It is known after the works of Mahler, Kleinbock, Margulis, Sprindžuk that very well approximated numbers on a manifold form a zero measure set, assuming non-degeneracy conditions. These non-degeneracy conditions are, in many applications, difficult to check. We propose here a setting in which these are automatic. Rather than functions, we consider correspondences which have a better behaviour.



1.
Introduction

In 1942, Siegel proved that a biholomorphic germ

f:(\mathbb{C},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C},0),\ z\mapsto\alpha z+o(|z|)

can be linearised, assuming an arithmetic condition on the coefficient

\alpha

[
20
]
. Ever since, the field of Diophantine
approximation has been of relevance for dynamical systems and this led
Arnold, Margulis and Pyartli and others to reconsider the classical works
on Diophantine approximation and extend their scope
[
14
,
17
]
. Nowadays, the work of Kleinbock and Margulis finds application
in KAM theory in order to prove that in certain situations the quasiperiodic
solutions make up a set of positive measure in phase space
[
18
]
.
To apply these results, one needs to assume certain
non-degeneracy conditions
, introduced in the works of Mahler, Sprindžuk, Margulis and Kleinbock
[
14
,
15
,
22
,
23
]
. To our knowledge the weakest of these conditions was introduced by Kleinbock:

Definition 1.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
U
be an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
C^{k}
-map
B:U\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{e}
is called
KMS
-non-degenerate (for Kleinbock-Margulis-Sprindžuk) in an affine space

if:

(1)

B(U)\subset{\mathcal{L}}
.

(2)

For some
l\leq k
the partial derivatives at any point of
U
of order
l
span the vector space associated to
{\mathcal{L}}
.

It is not difficult to see that real analytic maps are always KMS non-degenerate (see
3.3
) and this will constitute our starting point. A simple example of a KMS-degenerate function is given by the flat function:

\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\ x\mapsto e^{-1/x^{2}}

Assuming the KMS non-degeneracy condition, one may estimate the preimage under the map
B
of points which are badly approximated by the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{e}\subset\mathbb{R}^{e}
in various ways. For instance, under the KMS non-degeneracy condition, Kleinbock-Margulis and Kleinbock proved that the preimages of points satisfying an adequate Diophantine condition form a full measure set (
[
14
, Theorem A]
,
[
13

, see also
[
7
]
).

Unfortunately the verification of non-degeneracy conditions for the frequency map of a dynamical system often rely on tedious computations and are notoriously difficult to establish. For instance, it took many years before Féjoz and Herman proposed a proof of the non-degeneracy condition for the
N
-body problem initiated by Arnol’d in the sixties (
[
2

6
]
, see also
[

). In this paper, we will show that for the type of normal forms introduced by the authors in
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
the KMS-non degeneracy conditions are essentially automatic. So let us now explain what type of generalisation of the usual setting is needed by considering an instructive example.

1.1.
The Poincaré correspondences

In
Les Méthodes Nouvelles de la Mécanique Céleste

[
16
, § 119]
.
Poincaré considered the series

s=1+\frac{x}{1+y}+\frac{x^{2}}{1+2y}+\ldots=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{1+ky}

as a prototype of the kind of series that appear in perturbation theory (see also the paper
[
19
]
of Sauzin for the resurgence properties). In fact, similar expressions play a role in many areas of mathematics, for example in the theory of
q
-analogs of classical series (logarithm, exponential, hypergeometric), see for instance
[
21
]
.

Assume now that we want to solve the equation

0=f(x,y):=-y+\frac{x}{1+y}+\frac{x^{2}}{1+2y}++\frac{x^{3}}{1+3y}+\ldots.

If we truncate the series at
x^{n}
we obtain the rational functions

f_{n}(x,y)=-y+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{x^{k}}{1+ky}=\frac{P_{n}(x,y)}{D_{n}(y)}

which determines a polynomial
P_{n}(x,y)
in the variables
x
and
y
:

P_{1}(x,y)=-(1+y)y+x,\;\;P_{2}(x,y)=-(1+y)(1+2y)y+(1+2y)x+x^{2},\ldots

D_{1}(y)=1+y,\;\;\;D_{2}(y)=(1+y)(1+2y),\;\;\;D_{3}(y)=(1+y)(1+2y)(1+3y),\ldots

These polynomials define algebraic curves

V_{n}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:P_{n}(x,y)=0\}\subset\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}

of degree
(n+1)
in
y
and degree
n
in
x
. These curves exhibit a peculiar bending behaviour caused by the poles, located on horizontal lines, defined by the denominators
D_{n}(y)
that were cleared out.

Such a polynomial relation defines
y
as a
multivalued function
of
x
, sometimes called a
correspondence
. In a neighbourhood of
x=0,y=0
, the
n
-th curve
V_{n}
is the graph of a holomorphic function
b_{n}
that can be expanded in power series:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}b_{3}(x)&=&x+2x^{5}-14x^{6}+64x^{7}+\ldots\\
b_{4}(x)&=&x+x^{4}-3x^{5}+7x^{6}-19x^{7}+\ldots\\
b_{5}(x)&=&x+x^{4}-2x^{5}+x^{6}+12x^{7}+\ldots\\
b_{6}(x)&=&x+x^{4}-2x^{5}+2x^{6}+5x^{7}+\ldots\\
\end{array}

It is not difficult to see that
b_{n+1}(x)=b_{n}(x)+o(x^{n})
, so these series converge coefficientwise to a formal power series solution
b(x)\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]
to the equation
f(x,y)=0

b(x)=x+x^{4}-2x^{5}+2x^{6}+6x^{7}-35x^{8}+86x^{9}+\ldots

We call it the
Birkhoff expansion
of the series
f
and its truncation are called the
Birkhoff polynomials
of the series.

Correspondences in the Poincaré example.

The domain of definition of the
b_{n}
shrinks as
n\longrightarrow\infty
due to the peculiar winding, which provides a geometrical reason for the divergence of the series
b(x)
. This is an illustration of a general divergence theorem for such expressions, that we intend to publish elsewhere
[
12
]
. On the other hand, the pictures strongly suggest that the part of curves in the part
y>0
nicely converge to the graph of a
C^{\infty}
-function
B
.

We may construct such a
C^{\infty}
function as follows: start with closed disks

D_{\rho}:=\{x\in\mathbb{C}:|x|\leq\rho\},\ D_{\eta}:=\{y\in\mathbb{C}:|y|\leq\eta\},\

where
\rho<1
and remove from the disk
D_{\eta}
small disks

D_{k}:=\{y\in\mathbb{C}:|y+\frac{1}{k}|<r_{k}\}
around the poles
y=-1/k
.
The distance between two nearby poles being
1/k(k+1)
,
if we choose
r_{k}=k^{-\alpha},\ \alpha>2
, the complement of the disks
D_{k}
contains the origin. We have in the complement of
D_{k}
an obvious estimate:

\left|\frac{x^{k}}{1+ky}\right|\leq k^{\alpha-1}|x^{k}|\leq k^{\alpha-1}\rho^{k}

So for
\rho<1
, we see that
the sequence obtained by restricting the rational functions
f_{n}(x,y)
to the closed set

X_{\infty}:=D_{\rho}\times\left(D_{\eta}\setminus\cup_{k}D_{k}\right)

converges in the
C^{0}
-topology to a limit function, that we denote in the same way:

f:X_{\infty}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}

1.2.
Whitney differentiability

The closed set
X_{\infty}
does contain
0
, but it is not an interior point as the poles accumulate at the origin. However, the formal power series
b
that solves the equation
f(x,b(x))=0
is the Taylor expansion of a function,
C^{\infty}
in a sense originally discovered by Borel and now called
Whitney differentiability

[
3
]
Let us explain this property.

If
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a locally closed subset, then a function

g:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

is called
Whitney differentiable
if there exists a map

L:X\longrightarrow Hom(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{R})

such that

g(y)=g(x)+L(x)(y-x)+o(\|x-y\|).

The main difference with usual derivatives is the requirement that the variables
x,y
play a more
symmetric role. One defines similarly higher derivatives and Whitney
C^{\infty}
functions. Holomorphicity in the Whitney setting is defined by requiring that the map
L
is
\mathbb{C}
-linear
(
\mathbb{C}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{2d}
) and in that case the function was called
monogenic
by Borel.

The Whitney extension theorem states that Whitney differentiable functions are restrictions of usual differentiable functions and the Whitney derivative commutes with restriction
[
24
]
. Fefferman showed that the extension operator can be chosen to be bounded linear for any finite level of differentiability
[
5
]
. In particular, the space of functions with bounded Whitney derivatives up to order
k
form a Banach space, like for ordinary differentiability.

Let us now show that our function
f
are indeed Whitney differentiable on
X_{\infty}
. The proof is elementary but is worthwhile understanding. We will keep the notation
f_{n}
for the restriction of
f_{n}
to
X_{\infty}
.

We first create a second sequence
X^{\prime}_{n}
by starting on a slightly bigger disc
D_{\rho+\varepsilon}
,
\rho+\varepsilon<1
, and by removing from
D_{\eta}
disks
D^{\prime}_{k}\subset D_{k}
of radius smaller than
r_{k}
, say for instance
r_{k}/2
, for
k\leq n
, so that

X_{n}\subset X^{\prime}_{n}
.
The Cauchy inequalities give an estimate for the derivatives of a holomorphic function on
X^{\prime}_{n}
restricted to
X_{n}
:

\sup_{(x,y)\in X_{n}}|\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{y}^{j}g(x,y)|\leq\frac{2^{j}i!j!}{r_{k}^{j}\varepsilon^{i}}\sup_{(x,y)\in X^{\prime}_{n}}|g(x,y)|

Using the estimate on the complement of
D^{\prime}_{k}
:

\left|\frac{x^{k}}{1+ky}\right|<2k^{\alpha-1}|x^{k}|<2k^{\alpha-1}(\rho+\varepsilon)^{k},

and the Cauchy estimates, one easily deduces that, for any
k\geq 0
, the sequence
(f_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence in the
C^{k}
-Whitney holomorphic sense and therefore the sum function
f
is Whitney
C^{\infty}

in the holomorphic sense.

By the Whitney extension theorem, the function
f
is the restriction to
X_{\infty}
of a standard
C^{\infty}
function

F:\mathbb{C}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}

The set
F=0
serves as
C^{\infty}
-version of the limit correspondence defined by
f=0
.
As
\partial_{y}F(0)=\partial_{y}f(0)=1\neq 0
, the usual
C^{\infty}
implicit function
theorem shows that in a neighbourhood of the origin the set
F=0
is the
graph of a
C^{\infty}
function
B
, that we call a
C^{\infty}

Birkhoff-function
attached to
f(x,y)
. Clearly, the Taylor-series of
B
is
b
.

Somehow we proved more than expected, as the function
B
is now also defined for
y<0
. This may look surprising, if we think of the way the real parts of the curves
V_{n}
have multiple components which stretch along the horizontal lines
near the
x
-axis. The resolution of this paradox is that by removing the tubes

D_{\rho}\times D_{k}
, we removed the bendings of the curve at the same time.

1.3.
Content of the paper

If one wants to apply the Kleinbock-Margulis theorem, one should be able to define and establish the KMS non-degeneracy condition for the correspondence before going to the Birkhoff series and doing the Whitney extension. The purpose of this paper is to show that this is indeed the case.

That the Birkhoff series
b
does not see any of the bendings may explain
heuristically why establishing the KMS non-degeneracy condition based on considerations of
b
alone turns out to be very difficult. Our approach consists of working as much as possible with
the
correspondences
lying on the product space, rather than with
the implicit functions they define. So most of the paper concerns the definition and analysis of non-degeneracy conditions for correspondences.

In the last part, we explain the application of the results of this paper to
the Herman conjecture. We also hope that our result, elementary as it may look, is of interest independently of the context in which it originally emerged,
and that the deep connection between number theory and dynamical systems can
be further strengthened, in the spirit of Siegel, Arnold and Margulis.

Statement of the theorem

Birkhoff functions, series and polynomials

We consider the space

We say the series

An

Note that for

, the map-germ

(\mathbb{C}^{d},x_{0})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C},\ x\mapsto f(x,y_{0})

We use the notation:

V(f):=\{(x,y)\in U\cap X_{\infty}\;|\;f(x,y)=0\}

A Birkhoff function-germ associated to

, where

Note that the Taylor expansion of a Birkhoff function

. Indeed by the Whitney-Fefferman theorem (

The series

We have

2.2.
Non-degeneracy theorem

If
f=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a_{I}(y)x^{I}
is an
X
-convergent series then we may, by Whitney’s theorem, extend
f
to a
C^{\infty}
function-germ
F
. By the implicit function theorem, the zero locus of
F
is then the graph of a Birkhoff function-germ. So Birkhoff functions always exists. The aim of this paper is to show that the Birkhoff function can be chosen to be KMS non-degenerate:

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume that the formal series:

f(x,y)=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a_{I}(y)x^{I}

is
X
-convergent and that the matrix
\partial_{y}f(0,0):=(\partial_{y_{j}}f_{i}(0,0))
is of maximal rank. Then
the series
f
admits KMS non-degenerate Birkhoff function germs, i.e., there exists Whitney
C^{\infty}
germs:

B:(\mathbb{C}^{d},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{e},0)\text{ with }(\Gamma_{B}\cap X_{\infty},0)=(V(f),0).

It is useful to work over the field of complex numbers but of course our constructions commute with complex conjugation, in particular if the
a_{I}
are real analytic and
X
invariant under complex conjugation then the function
B
can be chosen to be real, i.e.:

B(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\overline{B(x,y)}

Our construction is going to be explicit and not just an existence theorem.

3.
Maps, non-degeneracy and the Kleinbock lemma

3.1.
The span

For a subset
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{e}
, we denote by
[X]
the
span
of
X
, defined as the smallest affine subspace containing
X
.

Clearly, if
X\subset Y
, then
[X]\subset[Y]
.
For a
germ

(X,p)
of a set we define its span as

[X,p]:=\bigcap_{U\in{\mathcal{V}}(p)}[U\cap X],

where
U
runs over the set
{\mathcal{V}}(p)
of open neighbourhoods of the point
p
.
We always can find a sufficiently small representative
U
for the germ
(X,p)
,
so that
[X,p]=[U]
.

For an open set
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
and map
g:U\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e}
we also define its
span as
[g(U)]
, and we similarly define the span of a map germ

g:(\mathbb{C}^{d},\alpha)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{e},\beta)
at
\alpha
as the affine space

[g,\alpha]:=\cap_{U}[g(U),\beta],

where the intersection runs over all representatives of the germ
g
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
g:U\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e}
be an analytic map and
U(\alpha)
the
connected component of
U
containing
\alpha
. Then
g(U(\alpha))
is
contained in the span of the germ of
g
at
\alpha
:

g(U(\alpha))\subset[g,\alpha].

Consequently,

[g(U(\alpha))]=[g,\alpha].

3.2.
The jet-span of a map

Given a multi-index
I\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, we denote by

\partial^{I}g(\alpha):=\frac{\partial^{I}g}{\partial x^{I}}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{C}^{e}

the vector of
I

g
at
\alpha
. We will also use the notation

|I|=i_{1}+\dots+i_{d},\ I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{d}).

We define the

map

g:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e}

at a point

as the affine space:

[g,\alpha]_{m}:=[\{\partial^{I}g(\alpha):I\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\ |I|\leq m\}]

The
m
-jet spans form a increasing chain, so become
stable, at some minimal index
\tau=\tau(g,\alpha)
:

[g,\alpha]_{\tau}=[g,\alpha]_{\tau+1}=\ldots

We call
\tau(g,\alpha)
the

at the point
\alpha
.
The condition

corresponds to the KMS non-degeneracy condition.

As an illustration, consider the germ

g:(\mathbb{C},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{4},0):x\mapsto(1+x,x^{3},x^{100},x+x^{3}+x^{100})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{4})

We have

[g,0]_{0}\subset[g,0]_{1}=[g,0]_{2}\subset[g,0]_{3}=[g,0]_{4}=\ldots=[g,0]_{99}\subset[g,0]_{100}=[g,0]_{101}=\ldots.

The span
[g,0]
is the affine hyperplane
[g,0]_{100}=\{y_{4}=y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}+1\}
and the map-germ has torsion index
\tau(g,0)=100
at the origin. It is KMS non-degenerate, as any holomorphic map.

A flat
C^{\infty}
map-germ like

g:(\mathbb{C},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C},0),\ x\mapsto\left\{\begin{matrix}e^{-1/x^{2}}\text{ if Re }x>0\\
0\text{ otherwise}\end{matrix}\right.

has torsion index equal to zero at the origin but it is KMS degenerate. Indeed,
the span of this map germ is
[g,0]=\mathbb{C}
, but

for any

therefore:

[g,0]_{n}\neq[g,0]

3.3.
A lemma of Kleinbock

The following observation due to Kleinbock was formulated originally in

Lemma 3.2

(
[
13
]
)

If

Proof.

For simplicity assume that

so that the span is a vector space.
Let
g:U\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e}
be a connected representative of the germ
(g,\alpha)

The sequence of spans

generated by the vectors
\partial^{I}g(\alpha)
,
|I|\leq n
stabilises at level
\tau
. Clearly
[g,\alpha]_{\tau}\subset[g(U)]
. To show the other inclusion, let

∎

\bigcup_{k}[g,\alpha]_{k}\subset[g,\alpha]\text{ but }\bigcup_{k}[g,\alpha]_{k}\neq[g,\alpha].

4.
Correspondences and non-degeneracy

4.1.
Maps and correspondences

Many naturally appearing expressions like
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
define
y
only
as a multivalued function
y=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
. Especially in the complex
domain, insisting on working with a monovalent function is cumbersome and
not very enlightening. Therefore, in algebraic geometry one is used to
working with
correspondences
, which provide a convenient extension of
the usual strict concept of function. The Poincaré example shows that this point of view is suited to the type of objects we are considering. So our purpose will be to adapt the previous notions defined for maps to correspondences.

In this paper we will use the following terminology:

Definition 4.1
.

A complex analytic variety
V\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}=\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}^{e}
is said to
define a regular correspondence at
\omega=(\alpha,\beta)
, if the following
conditions
are verified:

1) the set
V
is smooth of dimension
d
at
\omega
.

2) Under the projection onto the first factor, the tangent space
T_{\omega}V

maps isomorphically onto
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Of course, by the implicit function theorem, we then can find a local
parametrisation
g:(\mathbb{C}^{d},\alpha)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{e},\beta),x\mapsto y=g(x)

so that
(x,g(x))\in V
, or
(\Gamma_{g},\omega)=(V,\omega)
, where
\Gamma_{g}

denotes the graph of
g
.

4.2.
The relative span

For a subset
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}:=\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}^{e}
, we denote by
\langle X\rangle
the
relative span
of
X
, defined as the smallest affine subspace of the form
\mathbb{C}^{d}\times F
containing
X
where
F\subset\mathbb{C}^{e}
is an affine subspace.
Clearly, one has

\langle X\rangle=q^{-1}(q([X])),

where
q:\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}^{e}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e}
is the projection on the second factor.

Of course, the notions of span of a set and of a map germ are related:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Gamma_{g}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}^{e}=\mathbb{C}^{N}
denote the graph of

g:U\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e}
and let

q:\mathbb{C}^{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e}
be the projection onto the second factor. Then

[g(U)]=q(\langle\Gamma_{g}\rangle)

Proof.

Let
\ell:\mathbb{C}^{e}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be an affine function vanishing on

[g(U)]
then

\ell^{\prime}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}^{e}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C},\ (x,y)\mapsto\ell(y)

is an affine function vanishing on
\Gamma_{g}
and conversely.
∎

We define the relative span at a point
\omega\in X

as the affine space

\langle X,\omega\rangle=\cap_{U}\langle X\cap U,\omega\rangle,

where the intersection runs over all neighbourhoods of
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
and get an analog of Lemma
3.1
:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
be an analytic set and
V(\omega)
the
connected component of
V
that contains the point
\omega\in V
. Then
V(\omega)

is contained in the span of the germ
(V,\omega)
:

V(\omega)\subset\langle V,\omega\rangle

and consequently:

\langle V(\omega)\rangle=\langle V,\omega\rangle.

Proof.

Let
\ell
be an affine function vanishing on
\langle V,\omega\rangle
. As a holomorphic
function vanishing on the germ
(V,\omega)
, it vanishes on a neighbourhood of
V

containing
\omega
. By the identity theorem of analytic functions,
\ell
vanishes
on the connected component
V(\omega)
.
∎

4.3.
The relative jet-span of an analytic set

For a germ of an analytic set
(V,\omega)
we define the
n
-jet span

\langle V,\omega\rangle_{n}

as the smallest affine subspace of the form
\mathbb{C}^{d}\times F
that contains the
n
-th infinitesimal
neighbourhood of the fibre at
\omega\in V
of the projection
\mathbb{C}^{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
. It is alternatively defined by the kernel of the affine linear functions contained in the ideal
I+M_{\omega,x}^{n+1}
which depend only on the
y
-variables. Here
y_{1},\dots,y_{e}
denote coordinates on
\mathbb{C}^{e}
,
I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{C}^{N},p}

is the ideal defining
(V,\omega)
and
M_{\omega,x}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbb{C}^{N},\omega}
the maximal
ideal of holomorphic functions vanishing at
x=\alpha
,
\omega=(\alpha,\beta)
.

Clearly, one has

\langle V,\omega\rangle_{1}\subset\langle V,\omega\rangle_{2}\subset\langle V,\omega\rangle_{3}\subset\ldots,

so this chain stabilises for a certain index
\tau:=\tau(V,p)
, that we call the
torsion index
of
V
at
p
:

\langle V,\omega\rangle_{\tau}=\langle V,\omega\rangle_{\tau+1}=\ldots

Lemma 4.4
.

For any holomorphic map-germ
g:(\mathbb{C}^{d},\alpha)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{e},\beta)
we have

q(\langle\Gamma_{g},\omega\rangle_{m})=[g,\alpha]_{m}

for
\omega=(\alpha,\beta)
and any
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Write
\langle\Gamma_{g},\alpha\rangle_{m}=\{\ell=0\}
where

\ell:\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}^{e}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{r},\ (x,y)\mapsto\ell(y)

is some affine map depending only on the
y
-variables. By the Taylor formula, we have

\ell(g(x))=\sum_{|I|\leq m}\frac{\partial^{I}g(\alpha)}{|I|!}(x-\alpha)^{I}+o(\|x\|^{m})

and therefore the conditions
\ell(g(x))=o(\|x\|^{m})
and
\partial^{I}g(\alpha)=0
for
|I|\leq m
are equivalent.
∎

4.4.
A variant of the Kleinbock lemma

The following result is the analog of the Kleinbock lemma for analytic spaces:

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(V,\omega)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{N},0)
be a germ of an analytic space with torsion index
\tau
at
\omega
then:

\langle V,\omega\rangle=\langle V,\omega\rangle_{\tau}

Proof.

Clearly,
\langle V,\omega\rangle_{\tau}\subset\langle V,\omega\rangle
. For the converse, note that we have

\cap_{n}M_{p}^{n}=(0)
, so that a function that vanishes on all infinitesimal
neighbourhoods of
\omega
vanishes on
(V,\omega)
, from which we obtain the reverse inclusion.
∎

4.5.
n
-Correspondences and the finite determinacy lemma

We now consider truncated sums as we did in the introduction for the Poincaré example:

Definition 4.6
.

A correspondence
V
is called an
n
-correspondence at
\omega
,
if it is defined by
d
equations of the form

f(x,y)=\sum_{|I|\leq n,\ I\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a_{I}(y)x^{I}\in\mathbb{C}\{y\}[x]

The following lemma is a refinement of Lemma
4.5
:

Lemma 4.7
.

If
V
is an
n
-correspondence at
\omega
, then

\tau(V,\omega)\leq n

that is:
\langle V,\omega\rangle_{n}=\langle V,\omega\rangle
.

Proof.

Consider an affine function
\ell
vanishing on

\langle V,\omega\rangle_{n}
depending only on the
y
-variables.
Let
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be an arbitrary vector and consider the function

\gamma:(\mathbb{C},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{e},0),\ t\mapsto f(\alpha+tv,y)

By definition of the
n
-span, the polynomial
\ell\circ\gamma
in the variable
t
has a zero of multiplicity
\geq n+1
at the origin:

(\ell\circ\gamma)^{(k)}(0)=0,\ k=0,1,2,\ldots,n.

As
(V,\omega)
is an
n
-correspondence, the equation
\ell\circ\gamma(t)=0
has degree
\leq n
therefore

\ell\circ\gamma
vanishes identically. As the vector
v
is arbitrary, we deduce that
\ell
vanishes on
(V,\omega)
.
This inclusion being true for any hyperplane containing
\langle V,\omega\rangle_{n}
,
we get that
\langle V,\omega\rangle\subset\langle V,\omega\rangle_{n}
.
This proves the lemma.
∎

4.6.
The spans stabilise

We return to the situation of subsection
2.2
and consider a decreasing chains of subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{N}=\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}^{e}
:

X_{0}\supset X_{1}\supset X_{2}\supset\ldots\supset X_{\infty}:=\cap_{n}X_{n}.

with

\overline{X_{n}^{\circ}}=X_{n}

and consider a formal power series

f(x,y)=\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a_{I}(y)x^{I}\in\mathbb{C}\{y\}[[x]],\ f(0)=0

and define

f_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{|I|\leq n,\ I\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a_{I}(y)x^{I}\in\mathbb{C}\{y\}[x]

We denote their
zero-sets by

V_{n}:=\{(x,y)\in X_{n}\;|\;f_{n}(x,y)=0\}\subset X_{n}

We also have to consider

V_{\infty}:=\{(x,y)\in X_{\infty}\;|\;f(x,y)=0\}\subset X_{\infty}

Lemma 4.8
.

One has

\langle V_{n},0\rangle\subset\langle V_{n+1},0\rangle.

Proof.

We have

\langle V_{n},0\rangle=\langle V_{n},0\rangle_{n}=\langle V_{n+1},0\rangle_{n}\subset\langle V_{n+1},0\rangle_{n+1}=\langle V_{n+1},0\rangle.

For the first and last equalities we used that
V_{n}
is an
n
-correspondence
(for
n
and
n+1
), the second equality is due to the fact that

f_{n+1}(x,y)=f_{n}(x,y)+O(\|x\|^{n+1})

The middle inclusion is
by the definition of jet-span.
∎

As a consequence, the sequence
\langle V_{n},0\rangle
stabilises, say at level
\rho

and we define

W_{\infty}:=\langle V_{n},0\rangle,n\geq\rho.

Lemma 4.9
.

For
n\geq\rho
, we have:

\langle V_{\infty},0\rangle\subset\langle V_{n},0\rangle=W_{\infty}

Proof.

Take
(x_{0},y_{0})\notin W_{\infty}
. By definition of
W_{\infty}
, this means that for any
n\geq\rho
,
the point
(x_{0},y_{0})
does not belong to
\langle V_{n},0\rangle
and therefore is not in
V_{n}
, so we have

f_{n}(x_{0},y_{0})\neq 0
for
n\geq\rho
. Consequently, for
n\geq\rho
, the holomorphic map-germs

\phi_{n}:(\mathbb{C}^{d},x_{0})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e},\ x\mapsto f_{n}(x,y_{0})=\sum_{|I|\leq n}a_{I}(y_{0})x^{I}

are non-zero at
x=x_{0}
.

We now use the classical
Hurwitz lemma
(see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 5, Theorem 2]
):
Consider a sequence of holomorphic functions
\phi_{n}
converging in the
C^{0}
-topology and assume that the limit
\phi_{\infty}
is not the zero-function. Then:
if the functions
\phi_{n}
are non-zero at a point, then the limit
\phi_{\infty}
is also non-zero at this point

2
2

2

This Hurwitz lemma, sometimes called the
Hurwitz theorem
, is a straightforward consequence of the Cauchy integral formula.
.

As
f_{n}(x_{0},y_{0})\neq 0
, the coefficients
a_{I}
are not all equal to zero and therefore the holomorphic map-germ

\phi_{\infty}:(\mathbb{C}^{d},x_{0})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e},\ x\mapsto\sum_{I\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a_{I}(y_{0})x^{I}

is not identically zero. From the Hurwitz lemma we conclude that
(x_{0},y_{0})\notin V_{\infty}
and therefore

(V_{\infty},z_{0})\subset W_{\infty},\ z_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})

∎

4.7.
Proof of Theorem
2.3

We keep the same notations and let

g:({\mathcal{U}}\times W_{\infty},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{e},0)

be the restriction of
f
to
W_{\infty}
. By Lemma
4.9
,
we have

(V(f),0)=(V(g),0).

We choose a Whitney extension
G
of
g
. By the implicit function theorem the zero locus of
G
is the graph of a function-germ

\beta:(\mathbb{C}^{d},0)\longrightarrow(W_{\infty},0),\ x\mapsto\beta(x)

and we may extend the image to

The Taylor expansion of

5.
Application to the Herman conjecture

We give a brief outlook of our proof of the Herman invariant tori conjecture, details will be published elsewhere, the reader may consult
[
10
]
for further details.

5.1.
The frequency map

In KAM theory, the invariant tori of a Hamiltonian system are parametrised by the frequencies of motions which are not very well approximated by the standard integral lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{e}
.
In order to measure how a frequency vector
y\in\mathbb{C}^{e}
is approximated by the lattice, one considers the collection of resonant hyperplanes
(\Sigma_{\alpha})
with

\Sigma_{\alpha}=\{y\in\mathbb{C}^{e}:(y,\alpha)=0\},\ \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{e}

where

(y,\alpha)=\sum_{j=1}^{e}y_{j}\alpha_{j}

is the complexification of the Euclidean scalar product. We then fix a real positive sequence

(\delta_{\alpha}),\ \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{e}
, which converges to zero not too fast
and consider tubular neighbourhoods of hyperplanes

T_{\alpha}=\{y\in\mathbb{C}^{e}:|(y,\alpha)|<\delta_{\alpha}\},\ \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{e}

We now define the sequence
(X_{n})
by:

X_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{e}\setminus\bigcup_{\|\alpha\|\leq n}T_{\alpha}

and this set parametrises the invariant tori, in the sense that there is a Birkhoff function

B:U\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{e}

called the
frequency map
whose fibres above points of
X_{\infty}=\bigcap_{n}X_{n}
are (complexifications) of invariant tori. In
[
8
,
10
]
, we constructed
B
as a map obtained from a
correspondence as we now explain.

5.2.
The small denominators ring

We consider the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[\omega]=\mathbb{C}[\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{d}]
and construct a ring
SD_{\alpha}
of
small denominators
at
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The ring
SD_{\alpha}
is a subring of the field
\mathbb{C}(\omega)=\mathbb{C}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{d})
of rational functions
defined by
localisation of
\mathbb{C}[\omega]=\mathbb{C}[\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{d}]
with respect with the multiplicative subset
S
generated by all linear polynomials
(\alpha+\omega,J),J\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
:

SD_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{C}[\omega]_{S}:=\mathbb{C}[\omega,\frac{1}{(\alpha+\omega,J)},J\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}]\subset\mathbb{C}(\omega).

The elements of this ring may have poles along the resonance hyperplanes

H_{J}:=\{\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:(\alpha+\omega,J)=0\}

We will construct a normal form inside the algebra

R:=SD_{\alpha}[[\tau,p,q]]\subset\mathbb{C}[[\omega,\tau,p,q]],

a subalgebra of the power series in
4d
variables

\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{d},\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d},q_{1},\ldots,q_{d},p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}.

We provide
R
with the standard Poisson bracket, so that the Poisson centre of

R
is the ring

R_{0}:=SD_{\alpha}[[\tau]]\subset\mathbb{C}[[\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d},\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d}]].

We endow this ring of quasi-homogenous filtration with weights

w(q_{i})=w(p_{i})=1,\ w(\tau_{i})=2,\ w(\omega_{i})=0

We use the notations
[f]_{i}^{j}
for the sum of terms of weighted degree
\geq i
and
<j
and omit the indices if
i=0
or
j=+\infty
.
We also use the notation
O(n)
for terms of degree
\geq n
.

5.3.
The Hamiltonian normal form iteration

Starting from a Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\alpha_{i}p_{i}q_{i}+O(3),

we first form the
\omega
-extension of
H
:

F_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(\alpha_{i}+\omega_{i})p_{i}q_{i}+O(3),

Then we solve a
homological equation
of the form:

v_{0}(A_{0})=[F_{0}]_{3}^{4}+t_{0},\;\;\;t_{0}\in R_{0}+I^{2},

where
I
is the ideal generated by the
p_{i}q_{i}-\tau_{i}
and
v_{0}
is a Poisson derivation.
The definition might seem involved but it produces a sequence of biholomorphic maps
e^{-v_{i}}
which are defined globally and not "blocked by resonances."

Exponentiating the Poisson derivation
v_{0}
, we produce a Poisson automorphism
e^{-v_{0}}
. The application of
e^{-v_{0}}
transforms
F_{0}
into
F_{1}
, where the cubic term is removed modulo an element of
R_{0}+I^{2}
; we put
A_{1}=A_{0}+t_{0}
. For the
n=0
case, it turns out that
t=0
but at the next level we have to solve

v_{1}(A_{1})=[F_{1}]_{4}^{6}+t_{1},\;\;\;t_{1}\in R_{0}+I^{2}

for the degree
4
and
5
part of
F_{1}
on
A_{1}
and, as a general rule
t_{1}\neq 0
.

Then the application of
e^{-v_{1}}
to
F_{1}
produces
F_{2}
, where now these terms of degree
4
and
5
are removed, but certain terms in
R_{0}+I^{2}
are introduced. These remaining terms we add to
A_{1}
and obtain
A_{2}
. Next we solve the homological equation for the terms of degree
6,7,8,9
of
F_{2}
, but now on
A_{2}
, etc. Thus we obtain a by iteration a sequence of triples

(F_{n},A_{n},v_{n}),\;\;\;n=0,1,2,\ldots

We have correspondences defined by the rational functions

[e^{-v_{n}}\dots e^{-v_{0}}\omega_{i}]^{2^{n}}=\sum_{\alpha,|\alpha|\leq 2^{n}}a_{\alpha}(\omega)\tau^{\alpha},\ a_{\alpha}\in SD_{\alpha}

precisely of the kind envisioned by Poincaré! This power series is
X
-convergent, the partial sums define complex variety and the limit variety
parametrises the pairs consisting of invariant tori and associated frequencies.

The theorem of this paper shows that the limiting correspondence contains the graph of a KMS
non-degenerate map. Using an adequate variant of the Kleinbock-Margulis theorem (
[
7
]
), one deduces the fact that the invariant tori form a set of positive measure. The convergence
of the iteration scheme proposed is subtle, but it turns out that it can be
streamlined using the theory of Banach functors developed in
[
11
]
. In that
set-up, the non-degeneracy argument is easy and, in fact, as this paper shows, “almost trivial”.
