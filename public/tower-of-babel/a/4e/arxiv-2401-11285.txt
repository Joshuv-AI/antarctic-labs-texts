Radiation of a short linear antenna above a topologically insulating half-space
M. Ibarra-Meneses, A. Martín-Ruiz

Abstract
The topological magnetoelectric effect (TME) is a unique macroscopic manifestation of quantum states of matter possessing topological order and it is described by axion electrodynamics. In three-dimensional topological insulators, for instance, the axion coupling is of the order of the fine structure constant, and hence a perturbative analysis of the field equations is plenty justified. In this paper we use Green’s function techniques to obtain time-dependent solutions to the axion field equations in the presence of a planar domain-wall separating two media with different topological order. We apply our results to investigate the radiation of a short linear antenna near the domain-wall.



I
Introduction

Topological materials are not distinguished by local ordered parameters, but by topological quantum numbers which could be described and computed using topological field theories in the low energy limit. The best studied of these are the topological insulators (TIs), where the corresponding bulk band gap is similar to an ordinary insulator, but the boundary of the material hosts gapless states which are protected by the topological numbers of the bulk geometry
Qi and Zhang 2011
. The experimental discovery of TIs in HgTe quantum wells
König et al. 2007
and stoichiometric crystals
Chen et al. 2009
;
Xia et al. 2009
;
Sato et al. 2010
encourages further investigations regarding its exotic properties.

Apart from their spectroscopic distinguishing features, TIs have unconventional electromagnetic response described by axion electrodynamics, which is defined by the standard Maxwell Lagrangian plus the additional
\theta
-term (in SI units)
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=(\alpha/\pi)\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/\mu_{0}}\,\theta\,{\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{B}}
, where
{\bf{E}}
and
{\bf{B}}
are the electromagnetic fields,
\alpha
is the fine structure constant, and
\theta
is an angular variable known in particle physics as the axion field or axion angle
Wilczek 1987
. The axion field was originally proposed to solve the strong-CP problem in QCD
Peccei and Quinn 1977
;
Weinberg 1978
;
Wilczek 1978
, but its weak interaction strength with standard model particles and an elegant production mechanism in the early universe make it a promising candidate to explain dark matter as well
Preskill et al. 1983
;
Dine and Fischler 1983
;
Abbott and Sikivie 1983
. In the context of TIs, time-reversal (TR) symmetry indicates that
\theta=0,\pi
(mod
2\pi
), and hence the
\theta
-term has no effect on Maxwell equations in the bulk. The nontrivial topological property, a surface half-integer quantum Hall effect, manifests only at the boundary between two manifolds with different topologies, in which case
\theta
is not globally constant. Therefore, from the macroscopic point of view, the axion angle
\theta
can be viewed as an effective parameter characterizing the media, in the same footing as its permittivity
\epsilon
and permeability
\mu
.

The
\theta
-term encodes the most salient feature of the electromagnetic response of TIs: the topological magnetoelectric effect, where an electric field can induce a magnetic polarization and a magnetic field can induce an electric polarization
Fiebig 2005
. This is why, in the condensed matter literature, the axion angle is termed the topological magnetoelectric polarization (TMEP). A number of topological magnetoelectric effects have been predicted on the basis of this theory, namely, the appearance of charge and current densities of magnetic monopoles
Qi et al. 2009
;
Karch 2009
;
Zang and Nagaosa 2010
;
Martín-Ruiz et al. 2015
;
Martín-Ruiz et al. 2019
;
Martín-Ruiz et al. 2016
;
Martín-Ruiz 2018
;
Meier et al. 2019
, the magneto-optical Faraday and Kerr rotation
Chang and Yang 2009
;
Tse and MacDonald 2011
;
Crosse 2016
;
Wu et al. 2016
;
Filipini and Cambiaso 2023
, the Casimir effect
Grushin and Cortijo 2011
;
Grushin et al. 2011
;
Rodriguez-Lopez and Grushin 2014
;
Chen and Wan 2012
;
Rodriguez-Lopez 2011
;
Nie et al. 2013
;
Martín-Ruiz et al. 2016
, as well as the influence of TIs on atomic systems nearby
Song et al. 2014
;
Fang et al. 2015
;
Martín-Ruiz and Urrutia 2018
;
Martín-Ruiz and Chan-López 2017
;
Castro-Enriquez et al. 2020
;
Castro-Enriquez and Martín-Ruiz 2020
.

Time-independent solutions to the axion field equations have been elaborated by using the electric-magnetic duality of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
TIs
Karch 2009
as well as Green’s function techniques
Martín-Ruiz et al. 2015
;
Martín-Ruiz et al. 2016
. However, general solutions to the time-dependent case are still under investigation
Franca et al. 2019
;
Franca and Urrutia 2022
;
Franca and Buhmann 2022
. In this work we aim to fill in this gap by computing, in a perturbative fashion, solutions to the time-dependent axion field equations in the presence of a planar domain wall separating two media with different topologies. The validity of a perturbative scheme for the solution of the field equations is as follows. In the high-energy context the validity is plenty justified: the axion-to-two- photon coupling
g_{\theta\gamma\gamma}
which converts photons into axions is expected to be very small
Ouellet and Bogorad 2019
. In the condensed matter framework, the smallness of the
\theta
-term is controlled by the coupling
\alpha\theta
, which is small since
\alpha\approx 1/137
and
\theta=\pi
for a TI with a single Dirac fermion on its surface. In the hypothetical situation of a TI with a large number of Dirac fermions on its surface, our perturbative approach ceases to be valid as
\alpha\theta
approaches unity. In view that exact solutions to the static axion field equations have been reported in the literature, we shall first validate our perturbative scheme by reproducing well-known results in this case. Next we will safely extend our analysis to the time-dependent case. Finally, we apply our results to study the radiation of a short linear antenna near the domain-wall.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we briefly review the basics of axion electrodynamics. In Sec.
III
we solve, in a perturbative fashion, the static field equations and verify its consistency with previously reported results for the Green’s function for two ponderable topological insulators separated by a planar interface. Section
IV
is devoted to the evaluation of the time-dependent Green’s function by assuming equal optical properties in the two media. This configuration allow us to isolate the topological effects. The details of technical calculations are relegated to the appendices. Finally, in Sec.
V
we apply our result to investigate the radiation of a short linear antenna near a
\theta
domain-wall.

II
Topological field theory

The effective field theory governing the electromagnetic response of topological insulators, independently of the microscopic details, is defined by the action (in SI units)
Qi et al. 2008
:

\displaystyle S=\int d^{4}x\,\left[\,\frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon{\bf{E}}^{2}-\frac{1}{\mu}{\bf{B}}^{2}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\theta\,{\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{B}}\,\right],

(1)

where
\alpha=e^{2}/(4\pi\epsilon_{0}\hbar c)\simeq 1/137
is the fine structure constant,
\epsilon
and
\mu
are the permittivity and permeability of the material, respectively, and
\theta
is the topological magnetoelectric polarizability (axion field). Under periodic boundary conditions, the partition function and all physical quantities are invariant under shifts of
\theta
by any multiple of
2\pi
. Since
{\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{B}}
is odd under TR, the only symmetry allowed values of
\theta
are
0
or
\pi
(modulo
2\pi
). This leads to the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
classification of three-dimensional TR invariant TIs. Clearly, the
\theta
-term in Eq. (
1
) has no effect on Maxwell equations in the bulk, since it is a total derivative term. The nontrivial topological property, a surface half-integer quantum Hall effect, manifests only when a TR-breaking perturbation is induced on the surface to gap the surface states, thereby converting the material into a full insulator. This can be achieved by introducing magnetic dopants to the surface
Zhang et al. 2013
or by the application of an external static magnetic field
Maciejko et al. 2010
. In this situation,
\theta
is quantized in odd integer values of
\pi
, where the magnitude and sign of the multiple is controlled by the strength and direction of the TR-breaking perturbation.

The electromagnetic response of a system in the presence of the axionic term is still described by the ordinary Maxwell equations, but the constituent relations which define the electric displacement
{\bf{D}}
and the magnetic field
{\bf{H}}
acquire an extra term proportional to
\theta
, i.e.
{\bf{D}}=\epsilon{\bf{E}}+\alpha(\theta/\pi)\,\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/\mu_{0}}{\bf{B}}
and
{\bf{H}}=\mu^{-1}{\bf{B}}-\alpha(\theta/\pi)\,\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/\mu_{0}}\,{\bf{E}}
. Here, the
\theta
-dependent terms, which arise from the axionic term in Eq. (
1
), describe the topological magnetoelectric effect. The frequency components of the electromagnetic fields (introduced as usual: by making a Fourier decomposition in time of the sources and fields) obey the inhomogeneous equations

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left[\epsilon({\bf{r}},\omega)\,{\bf{E}}({\bf{r}},\omega)\right]=\rho({\bf{r}},\omega)-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\nabla\theta({\bf{r}})\cdot{\bf{B}}({\bf{r}},\omega),

(2)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\left[\mu^{-1}({\bf{r}},\omega)\,{\bf{B}}({\bf{r}},\omega)\right]+i\omega\epsilon({\bf{r}},\omega){\bf{E}}({\bf{r}},\omega)={\bf{J}}({\bf{r}},\omega)+\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\nabla\theta({\bf{r}})\times{\bf{E}}({\bf{r}},\omega),

(3)

while the homogeneous equations are untouched due to gauge invariance of the action (
1
):

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bf{B}}({\bf{r}},\omega)=0,

(4)

\displaystyle\nabla\times{\bf{E}}({\bf{r}},\omega)=i\omega{\bf{B}}({\bf{r}},\omega).

(5)

One can further verify that the divergence equations (
2
) and (
4
) are subsumed within the curl equations (
3
) and (
5
), which can be verified by taking the divergence of the latters. We observe that equations (
2
)-(
3
) depend only on the space gradient of the axion field. So, the
\theta
-term does not modify the field equations in the bulk of topological insulators. However, for a TI in contact with a conventional insulator, as shown in Fig.
1
, the axion field is piecewise constant across the interface
\Sigma
and then
\nabla\theta({\bf{r}})=(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\delta(\Sigma)\hat{{\bf{n}}}
, where
\hat{{\bf{n}}}
is the outward unit normal to
\Sigma
. These are the equations we aim to solve in a perturbative fashion along this paper. Our general approach will be to first Taylor expand the electromagnetic potentials in powers of the fine structure constant and then solve recursively the resulting equations. Before embarking to the solution of the time-dependent field equations, we first tackle the time-independent case and demonstrate consistency of our results with previously reported ones.

Geometry of two semi-infinite topologically insulating media separated by the surface
\Sigma
. The media have different magnetoelectric polarizabilities, permittivities and permeabilities, as depicted.

III
Solution to the static axion field equations

Let us consider the geometry shown in Fig.
1
with the interface
\Sigma
being the plane
z=0
. The half-space
z<0
is occupied by a topological medium with dielectric constant
\epsilon_{2}
, magnetic permeability
\mu_{2}
and axion angle
\theta_{2}
, whereas the half-space
z>0
is occupied by a medium with dielectric constant
\epsilon_{1}
, magnetic permeability
\mu_{1}
and axion angle
\theta_{1}
. Then the inhomogeneity in the permittivity, permeability and the axion field are limited to a finite discontinuity across the planar surface
z=0
.

To proceed further we write the field equations (
2
) and (
3
) in terms of the electrostatic potential
\phi({\bf{r}})
and vector potential
{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}})
. In the Coulomb gauge, the static electromagnetic potentials satisfy

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left[\epsilon({\bf{r}})\,\nabla\phi({\bf{r}})\right]=\rho({\bf{r}})-\tilde{\alpha}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\delta(z)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}\cdot\nabla\times{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}}),

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\left[\mu^{-1}({\bf{r}})\,\nabla\times{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}})\right]={\bf{J}}({\bf{r}})-\tilde{\alpha}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\delta(z)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}\times\nabla\phi({\bf{r}}),

(7)

where
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})/\pi
. To obtain a perturbative solution for the electromagnetic potentials in the presence of arbitrary external sources, we introduce the expansions
\phi({\bf{r}})=\phi^{(0)}({\bf{r}})+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi^{(n)}({\bf{r}})
and
{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}})={\bf{A}}^{(0)}({\bf{r}})+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\bf{A}}^{(n)}({\bf{r}})
, where
\phi^{(n)}
and
{\bf{A}}^{(n)}({\bf{r}})
are understood to vanish as
\tilde{\alpha}^{n}
. We now substitute the power expansion into equations (
6
) and (
7
), and comparing coefficients of each power of
\tilde{\alpha}
results in an infinite series of simultaneous equations. The zeroth-order equations are simply the Maxwell equations in the absence of the
\theta
-term,

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left[\epsilon({\bf{r}})\,\nabla\phi^{(0)}({\bf{r}})\right]=\rho({\bf{r}}),\qquad\qquad\nabla\times\left[\mu^{-1}({\bf{r}})\,\nabla\times{\bf{A}}^{(0)}({\bf{r}})\right]={\bf{J}}({\bf{r}}).

(8)

The
n
-order equations, which are the main concern in this work, are

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left[\epsilon({\bf{r}})\,\nabla\phi^{(n)}({\bf{r}})\right]=\tilde{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\delta(z)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}\cdot\nabla\times{\bf{A}}^{(n-1)}({\bf{r}}),\quad\nabla\times\left[\mu^{-1}({\bf{r}})\,\nabla\times{\bf{A}}^{(n)}({\bf{r}})\right]=-\tilde{\alpha}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\delta(z)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}\times\nabla\phi^{(n-1)}({\bf{r}}).

(9)

The process of solving these equations begins with the solution to the zeroth-order equations (
8
), which involves the well-known electro- and magnetostatic Green’s functions. We now adopt the following notation. Since translational invariance is broken in the
z
direction due to the presence of the interface, it is natural decompose any vector
{\bf{C}}
into components parallel and perpendicular to the normal direction
\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}
, i.e.
{\bf{C}}_{z}=C_{z}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}
and
{\bf{C}}_{\perp}=C_{x}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}+C_{y}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{y}
, respectively. So, the general solution for the electrostatic potential is

\displaystyle\phi^{(0)}({\bf{r}})=\int G_{\epsilon}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,\rho({\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime},

(10)

where the electric Green’s function is given by

\displaystyle G_{\epsilon}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{1}}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}{}_{\!\!\perp}|^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}}}+\frac{\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2}}{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}{}_{\!\!\perp}|^{2}+(|z|+z^{\prime})^{2}}}\right]

(11)

in which
{\bf{r}}_{\perp}=x\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}+y\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{y}
and
{\bf{r}}^{\prime}{}_{\!\!\perp}=x^{\prime}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}+y^{\prime}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{y}

J. Schwinger and Tsai 1998
. The solution to the magnetostatic problem is a bit more subtle. Decomposing the current density and the vector potential into their components parallel and perpendicular to
\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}
, i.e.
{\bf{J}}={\bf{J}}_{\perp}+{\bf{J}}_{z}
and
{\bf{A}}={\bf{A}}_{\perp}+{\bf{A}}_{z}
, respectively, the general solution for the vector potential, in the half-space
z>0
, can be written as

\displaystyle{\bf{A}}^{(0)}_{\xi}({\bf{r}})=\int G_{\mu}^{(\xi)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\bf{J}}_{\xi}({\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime},\qquad\xi=\,\perp,z

(12)

while for the region
z<0
the solution is simply
{\bf{A}}^{(0)}({\bf{r}})=\int G_{\mu}^{(\perp)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\bf{J}}({\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime}
. The corresponding magnetic Green’s function is given by
J. Schwinger and Tsai 1998

\displaystyle G_{\mu}^{(\xi)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{1}}{4\pi}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}{}_{\!\!\perp}|^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}}}+\chi\,\frac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}+\mu_{1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}{}_{\!\!\perp}|^{2}+(|z|+z^{\prime})^{2}}}\right],

(13)

where
\chi=+1\,(-1)
for
\xi=\,\perp(z)
.

With all of the above we are ready to solve equations (
9
). This is a simple but not straightforward task, so we relegate the details to Appendix
A
. The final results, up to second order in the parameter
\tilde{\alpha}
, are

\displaystyle\phi({\bf{r}})=\phi^{(0)}({\bf{r}})+\int\left[G_{0i}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,J_{i}({\bf{r}}^{\prime})+G_{00}^{(2)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,\rho({\bf{r}}^{\prime})\right]d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime},

(14)

\displaystyle A_{i}({\bf{r}})=A_{i}^{(0)}({\bf{r}})+\int\left[G_{0i}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,\rho({\bf{r}}^{\prime})+G_{ij}^{(2)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,J_{j}({\bf{r}}^{\prime})\right]d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime},

(15)

where the indices
i,j
range over the transverse coordinates
x,y
, and

\displaystyle G_{0i}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\alpha}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/\mu_{0}}}{2\pi(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})(1/\mu_{1}+1/\mu_{2})}\,\frac{\epsilon_{zji}\,({\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime})_{j}}{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}}\,\left[1-\frac{|z|+z^{\prime}}{\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}+(|z|+z^{\prime})^{2}}}\right],

(16)

\displaystyle G_{00}^{(2)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\tilde{\alpha}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/\mu_{0}}\,)^{2}}{2\pi(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})^{2}(1/\mu_{1}+1/\mu_{2})}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}{}_{\!\!\perp}|^{2}+(|z|+z^{\prime})^{2}}},

(17)

\displaystyle G_{ij}^{(2)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\tilde{\alpha}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/\mu_{0}}\,)^{2}}{2\pi(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})(1/\mu_{1}+1/\mu_{2})^{2}}\;\left[\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}{}_{\!\!\perp}|^{2}+(|z|+z^{\prime})^{2}}}+\partial_{i}K_{j}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\right],

(18)

with the definition

\displaystyle K_{j}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})=\frac{({\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime})_{j}}{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}}\,\left(\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}+(|z|+z^{\prime})^{2}}-(|z|+z^{\prime})\right).

(19)

Clearly, the electric (vector) potential is even (odd) under time-reversal operation, as expected.

Image magnetic monopole effect

As a consistency check of our results, we shall verify the image magnetic monopole effect, which has been extensively discussed in the literature by using different approaches, for example, the method of images
Qi et al. 2009
, the electric-magnetic duality of TIs
Karch 2009
and Green’s function techniques
Martín-Ruiz et al. 2016
;
Martín-Ruiz 2018
. This elusive effect consists in the appearance of an image magnetic monopole when an electric charge is placed at a certain distance from the TI’s surface.

We now consider for the charge density a pointlike electric charge
q
at
z=z_{0}
. Equations (
14
) and (
15
), together with the Green’s functions (
16
)-(
18
), give the electric and vector potentials. For
z>0
, the electric potential can be interpreted as due to two pointlike electric charges, one of strength
q
at
z=z_{0}
, and the other, the image charge, of strength

\displaystyle q^{\prime}=q\left[\frac{\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2}}{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}+\frac{2\epsilon_{1}(\tilde{\alpha}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/\mu_{0}}\,)^{2}}{(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})^{2}(1/\mu_{1}+1/\mu_{2})}\right]

(20)

at
-z_{0}
. For
z<0
only one pointlike electric charge appears, of strength
q^{\prime}+q
located at
z_{0}
. Unremarkably, the above expression reduce to the standard textbook one in the nontopological limit (
\tilde{\alpha}=0
)
J. Schwinger and Tsai 1998
. Due to the magnetoelectricy of TIs, a magnetic field is induced. The corresponding vector potential is found to be

\displaystyle{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}})=\frac{g}{4\pi}\,\frac{x\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{y}-y\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}}{r_{\perp}^{2}}\,\left[1-\frac{|z|+z_{0}}{\sqrt{r_{\perp}^{2}+(|z|+z_{0})^{2}}}\right],

(21)

which we identify as the vector potential of a straight vortex line or Dirac string over the
z
axis. For
z>0
(
z<0
) the string is over the negative (positive)
z
axis ending at a magnetic monopole at
-z_{0}
(
z_{0}
). In other words, the magnetic field for
z>0
can be interpreted as that of a magnetic monopole of strength

\displaystyle g=\frac{2q\tilde{\alpha}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/\mu_{0}}}{(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})(1/\mu_{1}+1/\mu_{2})}

(22)

at the image point
-z_{0}
. Similarly, for
z<0
the magnetic field can be understood as originated by a monopole of strength
-g
located at
z_{0}
. Our results are in a agreement with the ones reported in Refs.
Qi et al. 2009
;
Karch 2009
;
Martín-Ruiz et al. 2016
;
Martín-Ruiz 2018
. This can be further verified by power expanding the image electric and magnetic charges up to order
\tilde{\alpha}^{2}
, thus yielding our results (
20
) and (
22
), respectively.

IV
Time-dependent solutions to the axion field equations

In this Section we consider the same configuration as in Sec.
III
, but we shall investigate now time-dependent solutions to the field equations (
2
)-(
5
). In order to isolate the contribution arising from the surface Hall effect, we restrict ourselves to the case in which the permittivities are the same, i.e.
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}\equiv\epsilon
, but the topological magnetoelectric polarizability is a piecewise constant function:
\theta_{1}
for
z>0
and
\theta_{2}
for
z<0
. We assume the sources located in the region
z>0
.

We now introduce the field-potential relationships in the frequency domain,
{\bf{B}}({\bf{r}},\omega)=\nabla\times{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}},\omega)
and
{\bf{E}}({\bf{r}},\omega)=-\nabla\phi({\bf{r}},\omega)+i\omega{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}},\omega)
. In the Lorentz gauge,
\nabla\cdot{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}},\omega)=i\frac{\omega}{v^{2}}\phi({\bf{r}},\omega)
with
v=1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}
, the electromagnetic potentials satisfy

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\right)\phi({\bf{r}},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho({\bf{r}},\omega)}{\epsilon}-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\delta(z)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}\cdot\nabla\times{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}},\omega),

(23)

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\right){\bf{A}}({\bf{r}},\omega)

\displaystyle=\mu\,{\bf{J}}({\bf{r}},\omega)-\tilde{\alpha}\mu\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\delta(z)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}\times\left[\nabla\phi({\bf{r}},\omega)-i\omega{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}},\omega)\right],

(24)

where
k_{0}=\omega/v
. To solve this system of equations we follow the same perturbative program than before: we introduce the expansions
\phi({\bf{r}},\omega)=\phi^{(0)}({\bf{r}},\omega)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi^{(n)}({\bf{r}},\omega)
and
{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}},\omega)={\bf{A}}^{(0)}({\bf{r}},\omega)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\bf{A}}^{(n)}({\bf{r}},\omega)
, where
\phi^{(n)}({\bf{r}},\omega)
and
{\bf{A}}^{(n)}({\bf{r}},\omega)
vanish as
\tilde{\alpha}^{n}
, and solve the system recursively. The zeroth-order equations are exactly Maxwell equations for
\tilde{\alpha}=0
, i.e.

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\right)\phi^{(0)}({\bf{r}},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho({\bf{r}},\omega)}{\epsilon},

(25)

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\right){\bf{A}}^{(0)}({\bf{r}},\omega)

\displaystyle=\mu\,{\bf{J}}({\bf{r}},\omega),

(26)

while the
n
-order potentials are determined by

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\right)\phi^{(n)}({\bf{r}},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\delta(z)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}\cdot\nabla\times{\bf{A}}^{(n-1)}({\bf{r}},\omega),

(27)

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla^{2}+k_{0}^{2}\right){\bf{A}}^{(n)}({\bf{r}},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\alpha}\mu\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\,\delta(z)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}\times\left[\nabla\phi^{(n-1)}({\bf{r}},\omega)-i\omega{\bf{A}}^{(n-1)}({\bf{r}},\omega)\right].

(28)

The first step towards solving the higher-order equations (
27
)-(
28
) is to solve the zeroth-order equations (
25
)-(
26
). This requires the well-known frequency-dependent Green’s function of electromagnetism
J. Schwinger and Tsai 1998
:

\displaystyle G_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})=\frac{e^{ik_{0}|{\bf{r}}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}|}}{4\pi|{\bf{r}}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}|},

(29)

such that

\displaystyle\phi^{(0)}({\bf{r}},\omega)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int G_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,\rho({\bf{r}}^{\prime},\omega)\,d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime},\qquad{\bf{A}}^{(0)}({\bf{r}},\omega)=\mu\int G_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,{\bf{J}}({\bf{r}}^{\prime},\omega)\,d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime}.

(30)

With these results we can evaluate now equations (
27
) and (
28
). In this case we solve the system up to first order in the parameter
\tilde{\alpha}
. As shown in the Appendix
B.1
, for the vector potential one gets:

\displaystyle A_{i}({\bf{r}},\omega)=A_{i}^{(0)}({\bf{r}},\omega)+\int G_{ij}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,J_{j}({\bf{r}}^{\prime},\omega)\,d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime},

(31)

and for the scalar potential one spot the identity
i\frac{\omega}{v^{2}}\phi({\bf{r}},\omega)=\nabla\cdot{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}},\omega)
. Here,

\displaystyle G_{ij}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=i\frac{1}{\omega}\left[(\hat{\bf{e}}_{z}\times\nabla)_{i}\partial_{j}-k_{0}^{2}\,\epsilon_{ijz}\right]\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime}),

(32)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})=-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\perp}\frac{e^{i\sqrt{k_{0}^{2}-k_{\perp}^{2}}(|z|+|z^{\prime}|)}}{k_{0}^{2}-k_{\perp}^{2}}k_{\perp}\,J_{0}(k_{\perp}|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{\perp}|).

(33)

A detailed derivation of this expression can be found in the Appendix
B.2
. It is worth to mention that the exact electromagnetic potentials (and hence the fields) must be calculated using Eq. (
33
), which unfortunately cannot be evaluated in an analytical fashion. However, for many practical calculations, one can obtain an approximated expression valid for
|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{\perp}|\gg 1
and
|z|+|z^{\prime}|\gg 1
. In the Appendix
B.3
we derive, by two different methods, the aforementioned expression. The approximation relays in the fact that the integrand in Eq. (
33
) is a rapidly oscillating function for large
|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{\perp}|
and
|z|+|z^{\prime}|
, and hence the leading contribution can be obtained by using, for example, the stationary phase approximation. The final result is

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\approx i\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\frac{e^{ik_{0}\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{\perp}|^{2}+(|z|+|z^{\prime}|)^{2}}}}{k_{0}(|z|+|z^{\prime}|)}.

(34)

Once we have found the indexed Green’s function, we are ready to tackle problems involving different time-dependent sources near the domain wall.

The problem of a short linear antenna near a dielectric half-space has attracted great attention for over the years due to its importance to many practical applications, in particular, in the context of THz applications
Jeon and Grischkowsky 2006
, near-field optics
Novotny 1997
, plasmonics
Maier and Atwater 2005
and nanophotonics
Gordon 2008

Sommerfeld 1909
. Since then numerous investigations have been carried out in this subject. The main goal of this Section is to address the problem of the radiation pattern produced by a short linear antenna placed near the boundary between two
\theta
-half spaces. To this end, we shall employ the theory developed in the previous Section.

The geometry of the problem is shown in Fig.

z<0
is occupied by a topological insulator with axion angle
\theta_{1}
, while the right half-space
z>0
is occupied by a TI with axion angle

. We consider a simple model for a transmitting antenna oriented parallel to the interface, say along the the
x

z_{0}
from the interface, with uniform current flowing along a conductor of length

. We assume that this current varies sinusoidally with time as

\displaystyle{\bf{J}}({\bf{r}},t)=I_{0}\sin(\omega_{0} t)\,\delta(y)\delta(z-z_{0})\Theta(d-|x|)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x},

(35)

where
I_{0}
and

denotes the current amplitude and angular frequency, respectively, the

is the Heaviside step function. Because the current is discontinuous at the ends, charge must be deposited there, being of opposite sign at each end, thus forming an electric dipole. The exact zeroth-order scalar and vector potentials can be calculated in a simple fashion and can be found in a variety of textbooks on electrodynamics

. The only nonzero component of the vector potential (which is in the same direction as the current) in the time-domain is

where

\displaystyle\phi^{(0)}({\bf{r}},t)=-\frac{I_{0}}{4\pi\epsilon\omega_{0}}\left[\frac{\cos\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{|{\bf{r}}-{\bf{r}}_{+}|}{c}\right)\right]}{|{\bf{r}}-{\bf{r}}_{+}|}-\frac{\cos\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{|{\bf{r}}-{\bf{r}}_{-}|}{c}\right)\right]}{|{\bf{r}}-{\bf{r}}_{-}|}\right],

Now we are interested in the fields that survive at large distances from the source, i.e. in the radiation zone, which requires approximating the potentials for

\displaystyle A_{x}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\frac{I_{0}}{\omega_{0}k_{0}(|z|+z_{0})}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\int_{-d}^{+d}dx^{\prime}\sin\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{|{\bf{r}}^{\ast}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{i}|}{v}\right)\right],

of the vector potential contributes to radiation (to be precise, the term proportional to

in Eq. (

\displaystyle{\bf{B}}^{(1)}=-\tilde{\alpha}\frac{\mu I_{0}}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}}\cos\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{r^{2}+z_{0}|z|}{v\,r}\right)\right]\sin\left(\frac{\omega_{0}d}{v}\frac{x}{r}\right)\frac{r/x}{|z|+z_{0}}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{y}\times\hat{{\bf{n}}},\qquad{\bf{E}}^{(1)}=-v\,\hat{{\bf{n}}}\times{\bf{B}}^{(1)}.

{\bf{S}}
and energy density

VI
Summary and discussion

Topological phases are an emerging class of materials which have attracted much attention in condensed matter physics. The remarkable microscopic properties encoded in the band structure of topological phases produces exceptional macroscopic electromagnetic response, which is described by a topological field theory akin to axion electrodynamics. In general, a linear magnetoelectric media can be described by adding the term
\sim\theta_{ij}E_{i}B_{j}
to the free enthalpy of the system, where
\theta_{ij}
is the magnetoelectric tensor. In this frame, axion electrodynamics is a particular case when the ME tensor is of the form
\theta_{ij}=\theta\delta_{ij}
.

In this paper we derived, in a perturbative fashion, solutions to the time-dependent axion electrodynamics field equations for a picewise constant axion angle. The validity of the perturbative program relies in the fact that the axion coupling, in the case of topological insulators, is proportional to the fine structure constant. In the particle physics context this condition is vastly fulfilled, since it is expected to be very small. The difference is that in the former case the presence of boundaries is a natural task, while in the latter the axion is assumed to be distributed over the whole space.

To validate our perturbative scheme, we first tackle the static axion field equations and derive the components of the Green’s matrix up to second order in the axion coupling. We show that our results are consistent with the previously reported exact Green’s matrix when Taylor expanded up to second order in the coupling. We discuss in passing the image magnetic monopole effect, which is a salient manifestation of the unconventional magnetoelectric response of topological insulators. In the time-dependent case, the axion field equations become more involved and no analytical results have been reported yet, although there are some first attempts towards this direction. Here we aim to fill in this gap by applying the same perturbative program to the axion field equations. For the sake of simplicity, in this paper we choose that the bulks have the same optical properties, namely,
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}\equiv\epsilon
and
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}\equiv\mu
, which is made to highlight and isolate the effects of the axion coupling. In a forthcoming paper we will relax this condition and generalize our results to the case of two topologically insulating media characterized by different nontrivial optical parameters. This generalization is of course far from trivial. To illustrate the importance of the axion angle in the time-dependent case considered here, we investigate the radiation of a short linear antenna above a topologically insulating domain-wall with different values of
\theta
.

We first computed numerically the electromagnetic fields due to the linear antenna by using the exact Green’s function and verify that they exhibit the expected behavior. We interpret the induced fields in terms of the Hall current at the interface between the two half-spaces characterized by different
\theta
-angles. Next, to investigate the effects of the
\theta
-term in the power radiated, we use the asymptotic expression for the Green’s function and evaluate the fields in the radiation zone. These field allow us to compute the time-averaged angular distribution of the radiated power, which as expected, is dominated by the usual Maxwellian term, but becomes corrected due to the
\theta
-term. Such correction is quite interesting, since exhibits a divergent-type behavior near the interface, which arises from the field produced by the surface charge and current densities there. This is a distinctive behavior with respect to the usual radiation pattern.

In summary, a general solution to the static axion field equations has been previously elaborated in terms of Green’s function techniques; however, the time-dependent case has received less attention mainly due to its complexity. In this paper we introduced a perturbative scheme which was successfully validated with the static theory and then we extended the analysis to the time-dependent case. We obtained general expressions for the corresponding matrix of Green’s function which we also approximated in the far-field regime. We expect our method and results will be of considerable relevance and that they may constitute the basis for numerous other researches which include more intricate model of antennas and the radiation of particles near the
\theta
-interface.

\displaystyle=(\delta_{ij}\delta_{lm}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jm})\,\partial_{l}^{\prime}\,\partial_{m}^{\prime}\,\int_{\Sigma}\int_{\Sigma}\,\left(\int_{\Sigma_{{\bf{k}}_{\perp}}}\frac{d^{2}{\bf{k}}_{\perp}}{2\pi}e^{i{\bf{k}}_{\perp}\cdot({\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime\prime}_{\perp})}\frac{e^{-k_{\perp}|z|}}{k_{\perp}}\right)\,\left(\int_{\Sigma_{{\bf{k}}_{\perp}}}\frac{d^{2}{\bf{k}}_{\perp}^{\prime}}{2\pi}e^{i{\bf{k}}_{\perp}^{\prime}\cdot({\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime\prime}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{\perp})}\frac{e^{-k_{\perp}^{\prime}(0)}}{k_{\perp}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle I_{ij}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\delta_{ij}\delta_{lm}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jm})\,\partial_{l}^{\prime}\,\partial_{m}^{\prime}\,\int_{\Sigma_{{\bf{k}}_{\perp}}}\int_{\Sigma_{{\bf{k}}_{\perp}}}\int_{\Sigma_{{\bf{k}}_{\perp}}}\frac{d^{2}{\bf{k}}_{\perp}\,d^{2}{\bf{k}}_{\perp}^{\prime}\,d^{2}{\bf{k}}_{\perp}^{\prime\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-k_{\perp}|z|}e^{-k_{\perp}^{\prime\prime}z^{\prime}}}{k_{\perp}k_{\perp}^{\prime}k_{\perp}^{\prime\prime}}e^{i{\bf{k}}_{\perp}\cdot{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-i{\bf{k}}_{\perp}^{\prime\prime}\cdot{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}}\,\delta({\bf{k}}_{\perp}^{\prime}-{\bf{k}}_{\perp})\,\delta({\bf{k}}_{\perp}^{\prime\prime}-{\bf{k}}_{\perp}^{\prime})

where we have used the properties of the Dirac delta. We now introduce the polar coordinates
(k_{x},k_{y})=k_{\perp}(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)
and choose the vector
{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}
along the

\displaystyle I_{ij}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\delta_{ij}\delta_{lm}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jm})\,\partial_{l}^{\prime}\,\partial_{m}^{\prime}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\perp}\,\frac{e^{-k_{\perp}(|z|+z^{\prime})}}{k_{\perp}^{2}}J_{0}(\,|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|k_{\perp}\,).

\displaystyle I_{ij}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

To perform this integral in a convergent manner, we use the following trick:
\int_{\Lambda}^{\infty}e^{-k_{\perp}\lambda}d\lambda=e^{-k_{\perp}\Lambda}/k_{\perp}
, and therefore

\displaystyle I_{ij}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\delta_{ij}\delta_{lm}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jm})\,\partial_{l}^{\prime}\,\left[\frac{({\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime})_{m}}{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|\;}\,\int_{|z|+z^{\prime}}^{\infty}d\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\perp}\,e^{-k_{\perp}\lambda}\,J_{1}(\,|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|k_{\perp}\,)\right],

(75)

and this integral is well-known; see, for example, Ref.
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
. The result is

\displaystyle I_{ij}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\delta_{ij}\delta_{lm}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jm})\,\partial_{l}^{\prime}\,\left[\frac{({\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime})_{m}}{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}}\,\int_{|z|+z^{\prime}}^{\infty}d\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}+\lambda^{2}}}\right)\right],

from which we finally obtain

\displaystyle I_{ij}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\delta_{ij}\delta_{lm}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jm})\,\partial_{l}^{\prime}\,\left[\frac{({\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime})_{m}}{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}}\,\left(\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}+(|z|+z^{\prime})^{2}}-(|z|+z^{\prime})\right)\right].

(77)

From this expression we find

\displaystyle\mbox{tr}\,[{\bf{I}}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}{}_{\!\!\perp}|^{2}+(|z|+z^{\prime})^{2}}},

(78)

and

\displaystyle I_{ij}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\,\mbox{tr}\,[{\bf{I}}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})]+\partial_{i}\,K_{j}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime}),

(79)

where

\displaystyle K_{j}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})=\frac{({\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime})_{j}}{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}}\,\left(\sqrt{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}+(|z|+z^{\prime})^{2}}-(|z|+z^{\prime})\right).

(80)

B.3

Here we shall approximate the integral (

Method 1
. We start by using the identity

(94)

Using the Sommerfeld integral (
90

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})=i\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\xi\,\frac{e^{ik_{0}R_{\xi}}}{R_{\xi}},

(95)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})=i\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\chi\,\frac{e^{i\zeta\sqrt{1+2i\chi\tilde{z}-\chi^{2}}}}{\sqrt{1+2i\chi\tilde{z}-\chi^{2}}},

(96)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\approx i\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}e^{i\zeta}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\chi\,\frac{e^{-\left(\zeta\tilde{z}\right)\chi}}{1+i\chi\zeta}=i\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\frac{1}{\tilde{z}}\left[\pi+i\,\text{Ei}(i\zeta)\right],

whose approximation for

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\approx i\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\frac{e^{ik_{0}R_{0}}}{k_{0}(|z|+|z^{\prime}|)}.

(98)

This result corresponds to Eq. (
34
) of the main text.

Method 2

J_{0}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}[H^{(1)}_{0}(x)+H^{(2)}_{0}(x)]
together with the reflection formula
H^{(1)}_{0}(e^{i\pi}x)=-H^{(2)}_{0}(x)
, where

and
H^{(2)}_{0}
are the Hankel functions. In this case, the integral (

) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{16\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk_{\perp}\,\frac{k_{\perp}}{k_{0}^{2}-k_{\perp}^{2}}e^{ik_{z}(|z|+|z^{\prime}|)}\,H^{(1)}_{0}(k_{\perp}|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{\perp}|).

(99)

We now implement the far-field approximation into the Hankel function. For
x\gg 1
we have
H^{(1)}_{0}(x)\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}e^{ix-i\frac{\pi}{4}}
, and the integral (
99
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{16\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk_{\perp}\,\frac{k_{\perp}}{k_{0}^{2}-k_{\perp}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k_{\perp}|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{\perp}|}}\exp\left[i|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{\perp}|\left(k_{\perp}+\sqrt{k_{0}^{2}-k_{\perp}^{2}}\frac{|z|+|z^{\prime}|}{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{\perp}|}\right)-i\frac{\pi}{4}\right].

(100)

We observe that, in the far-field, the exponential factor is a rapidly oscillating function of
k_{\perp}
and hence we can apply the stationary phase approximation to evaluate the dominant contribution. A simple calculation reveals that the stationary phase point locates at

\displaystyle k_{\perp}^{\ast}=\frac{k_{0}}{\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{|z|+|z^{\prime}|}{|{\bf{r}}_{\perp}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{\perp}|}\right)^{2}}}.

(101)

Finally, we use the well-known asymptotic formula

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)e^{i\lambda f(x)}dx\approx g(x_{0})e^{i\lambda f(x_{0})+i\frac{\pi}{4}\mbox{sgn}(f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0}))}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\lambda|f^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})|}},

(102)

where
\lambda\gg 1
and
f^{\prime}(x_{0})=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\approx i\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\frac{e^{ik_{0}R_{0}}}{k_{0}(|z|+|z^{\prime}|)},

(103)

which is again the result of Eq. (
98
). This validates our expression for the far-field approximation.

Appendix C
Evaluation of the first-order potentials for a short linear antenna

Our starting point here is Eq. (
31
) for the components of the vector potential. The first order contribution in the frequency-domain is read off as

\displaystyle A_{i}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},\omega)

\displaystyle=i\frac{1}{\omega}\left[(\hat{\bf{e}}_{z}\times\nabla)_{i}\partial_{j}-k_{0}^{2}\,\epsilon_{ijz}\right]\int\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,J_{j}({\bf{r}}^{\prime},\omega)\,d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime},

(104)

where
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})
is the corresponding Green’s function. The potential in the time-domain is computed by inverse Fourier transforming the above expression. We obtain:

\displaystyle A_{i}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},t)

\displaystyle=i\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[(\hat{\bf{e}}_{z}\times\nabla)_{i}\partial_{j}+\frac{1}{v^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\,\epsilon_{ijz}\right]\int d\omega\,e^{-i\omega t}\frac{1}{\omega}\int\mathcal{G}_{\omega}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime})\,J_{j}({\bf{r}}^{\prime},\omega)\,d^{3}{\bf{r}}^{\prime}.

(105)

The current source (
35
) in the frequency domain is

\displaystyle{\bf{J}}({\bf{r}},\omega)

\displaystyle=\int e^{i\omega t}{\bf{J}}({\bf{r}},t)dt=I\,\delta(y)\delta(z-z_{0})\Theta(d-|x|)\,\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}\frac{\pi}{i}\left[\delta(\omega+\omega_{0})-\delta(\omega-\omega_{0})\right].

(106)

Substituting (
106
) into Eq. (
105
) and integrating the frequency one obtains

\displaystyle A_{i}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[(\hat{\bf{e}}_{z}\times\nabla)_{i}\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{v^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\,\epsilon_{ixz}\right]\frac{I\pi}{\omega_{0}}\int_{-d}^{+d}dx^{\prime}\,\left[e^{i\omega_{0}t}\mathcal{G}_{-\omega_{0}}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime\prime})+e^{-i\omega_{0}t}\mathcal{G}_{\omega_{0}}({\bf{r}},{\bf{r}}^{\prime\prime})\right],

(107)

where
{\bf{r}}^{\prime\prime}=x^{\prime}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}+z_{0}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}
. Since we are interested in the far-field domain, we use the Green’s function (
34
). In such case the vector potential becomes

\displaystyle A_{i}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},t)=-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\frac{I}{\omega_{0}k_{0}(|z|+z_{0})}\left[(\hat{\bf{e}}_{z}\times\nabla)_{i}\partial_{x}-\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{v^{2}}\,\epsilon_{ixz}\right]\int_{-d}^{+d}dx^{\prime}\sin\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{|{\bf{r}}^{\ast}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{i}|}{v}\right)\right],

(108)

where
{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{i}(x^{\prime})=x^{\prime}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}-z_{0}\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}
is the image point of
{\bf{r}}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
and
{\bf{r}}^{\ast}=x\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}+y\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{y}+|z|\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{z}
. Equations (
42
) and (
43
) of the main text are derived from this result by taking
i=x
and
i=y
, respectively. Now, to derive the scalar potential, we appeal to the Lorentz gauge condition. Taking the divergence of the vector potential we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{i}A_{i}^{(1)}({\bf{r}},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\frac{I}{\omega_{0}k_{0}(|z|+z_{0})}\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{v^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int_{-d}^{+d}dx^{\prime}\sin\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{|{\bf{r}}^{\ast}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{i}|}{v}\right)\right]=-\frac{1}{v^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi^{(1)}({\bf{r}},t),

(109)

wherefrom we find

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}({\bf{r}},t)=-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}}{8\pi}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\frac{I}{k_{0}(|z|+z_{0})}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int_{-d}^{+d}dx^{\prime}\cos\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{|{\bf{r}}^{\ast}-{\bf{r}}^{\prime}_{0i}|}{v}\right)\right],

(110)

which, after taking the
y
-derivative, corresponds to Eq. (

) of the main text.

Appendix D
Derivation of Eq. (
47
)

The angular distribution of the radiated power is given by
\frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{v}{\mu}r^{2}|{\bf{B}}|^{2}
. Keeping linear order in the magnetic field, i.e.
|{\bf{B}}|^{2}\approx|{\bf{B}}^{(0)}|^{2}+2{\bf{B}}^{(0)}\cdot{\bf{B}}^{(1)}
, one finds
\frac{dP}{d\Omega}\approx\frac{v}{\mu}r^{2}\left[|{\bf{B}}^{(0)}|^{2}+2{\bf{B}}^{(0)}\cdot{\bf{B}}^{(1)}\right]
. Substituting the zeroth (
41
) and first (
46
) order magnetic fields we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{d\Omega}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{v}{\mu}\left(\frac{\mu I_{0}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\cos\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{r^{2}-z_{0}z}{vr}\right)\right]\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}d}{v}\frac{x}{r}\right)\left\{\cos\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{r^{2}-z_{0}z}{vr}\right)\right]\,\frac{1-(x/r)^{2}}{(x/r)^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 199.16928pt\left.+2\tilde{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}}\cos\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{r^{2}+z_{0}|z|}{v\,r}\right)\right]\,\frac{y/x}{|z|+z_{0}}r\right\},

(111)

where we used that
(\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}\times\hat{{\bf{n}}})\cdot(\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}\times\hat{{\bf{n}}})=1-(x/r)^{2}
and
(\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{x}\times\hat{{\bf{n}}})\cdot(\hat{{\bf{e}}}_{y}\times\hat{{\bf{n}}})=-xy/r^{2}
.

Finally, averaging in time over a complete cycle yields

\displaystyle\left<\frac{dP}{d\Omega}\right>

\displaystyle\approx\frac{v}{2\mu}\left(\frac{\mu I_{0}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}d}{v}\frac{x}{r}\right)\left\{\frac{1-(x/r)^{2}}{(x/r)^{2}}+2\tilde{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\frac{\mu}{\epsilon}}\cos\left[\frac{(z+|z|)z_{0}\omega}{rv}\right]\,\frac{y/x}{|z|+z_{0}}r\right\},

(112)

where we have used:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{+T}\cos^{2}\left[\omega_{0}\left(t-\frac{r^{2}-z_{0}z}{vr}\right)\right]dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cos\left[\frac{(z+|z|)z_{0}\omega}{rv}\right].

(114)

These results stablish Eq. (
