Abstract
Pioneered by Benczúr and Karger for cuts in graphs [STOC’96], sparsification is a fundamental topic with wide-ranging applications that has been studied, e. g., for graphs and hypergraphs, in a combinatorial and a spectral setting, and with additive and multiplicate error bounds. Rafiey and Yoshida recently considered sparsification of decomposable submodular functions [AAAI’22]. We extend their work by presenting an efficient algorithm for a sparsifier for monotone k k -submodular functions of low curvature.

The algorithm we present here constructs an

.

Remark 4
.

Algorithm

Remark 5

If the size of the sparsifier is of primary interest, running

)

(iii)

In the setting of

Remark 7
.

In general, the best upper bound we know on the peak contributions is

.
Thus,

6
tells us that it is correct to invoke

with

.
Since

This results in the initial decomposition, i. e.,

Remark 8
.

There are various ways to implement
Algorithm
1
, leading to different running time bounds. The representation of the functions involved plays a key role here. In the most general scenario where no further assumptions are made, it is reasonable to assume each
f_{i}
is represented by an evaluation oracle with response time

. We may further assume an additional oracle for

The main work to be done for

.
Hence, the running time of a naive implementation is in

The main contribution of our work is to show how to approximate
p_{i}

efficiently for interesting cases, most notably for monotone
k
-submodular functions
of low curvature.

3
Monotone
k
-Submodular Functions of Low Curvature

Let
F:(k+1)^{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be decomposable as
F=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}
such that
each
f_{i}:(k+1)^{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a non-negative monotone
k
-submodular function of low
curvature. It follows from the definitions that
F
is also non-negative, monotone,
k
-submodular, and of
low curvature.

We will show how to approximate the peak contributions

p_{i}=\max_{\mathbf{A}\in(k+1)^{E}}\frac{f_{i}(\mathbf{A})}{F(\mathbf{A})}
.

To this end, it suffices to approximate

\max_{\mathbf{A}\in(k+1)^{E}}\frac{f(\mathbf{A})}{g(\mathbf{A})}

for two monotone
k
-submodular functions
f,g:(k+1)^{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
of low curvature.
Given
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})\in(k+1)^{E}
, we define

S_{f}(\mathbf{A}):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{e\in A_{i}}\Delta_{e,i}\left(f\mid\varnothing,\dots,\varnothing\right)

(14)

and

S_{g}(\mathbf{A}):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{e\in A_{i}}\Delta_{e,i}\left(g\mid\varnothing,\dots,\varnothing\right).

(15)

It turns out that
\frac{S_{f}(\mathbf{A})+f(\varnothing,\dots,\varnothing)}{S_{g}(\mathbf{A})+g(\varnothing,\dots,\varnothing)}

approximates
\frac{f(\mathbf{A})}{g(\mathbf{A})}
well.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in(k+1)^{E}
be an
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate maximiser
of
\frac{S_{f}(\mathbf{A})+f(\varnothing,\dots,\varnothing)}{S_{g}(\mathbf{A})+g(\varnothing,\dots,\varnothing)}
.
Then

3.1

If
A=B=0
,
modular-ratio-max
has a very simple solution: We can just take
I=\{i\}
for an index
i
that maximises
x_{i}/y_{i}
.
However, this is not optimal in general as the following example shows. Let
A=1
,
B=100
and
x_{1}=2
,
y_{1}=3
,
x_{2}=1
,
y_{2}=1

Clearly, the ratio
x_{i}/y_{i}
is maximised for

, leading to an overall

\displaystyle\varrho(\{2\})=\frac{1+1}{100+1}=\frac{2}{101}

as opposed to

which is clearly larger. In fact, it is not hard to see that the maximiser of
x_{i}/y_{i}
does not even provide a constant-factor approximation;
it may end up arbitrarily bad compared to an optimal solution. This indicates that we need to do something else.

The solution is an FPTAS based on binary search, outlined in Algorithm
2
.
This is possible because we can easily solve the associated decision problem:
Given a target value
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, does there exist an index set
\varnothing\neq I\subseteq[n]
such that
\varrho(I)\geq\lambda
?

The decision problem is simplified by algebraic equivalence transformations:

\displaystyle\varrho(I)\geq\lambda\iff\frac{A+\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}}{B+\sum_{i\in I}y_{i}}\geq\lambda\iff A-B\lambda+\sum_{i\in I}\left(x_{i}-\lambda y_{i}\right)\geq 0

To see if the last expression is non-negative for any non-empty index set
I

we consider the indices in non-increasing order of the quantities
x_{i}-\lambda y_{i}
.
We have to take first index in this order (as
I\neq\varnothing

i
for which
x_{i}-\lambda y_{i}

is positive. This maximises the LHS over all
I\neq\varnothing

\varrho(I)\geq\lambda
by the
above equivalences.

Let

\displaystyle\varrho(I)=\frac{A+\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}}{B+\sum_{i\in I}y_{i}}\leq\frac{A+nM}{B+m},

\displaystyle\left|\varrho^{+}-\varrho^{-}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left|\frac{A+nM}{B+m}-\frac{A+m}{B+nM}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}\frac{A+nM}{B+m}.

This is indeed satisfied for any

For the error bound, let

Combining both and the fact that

)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\bar{Z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{Z}\right]\right|\geq\varepsilon\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[F^{\prime}(A)\right]\right]\leq 2e^{-\varepsilon^{2}\kappa/3}.

(24)

Since
f_{i}(A)\geq 0
for all
1\leq i\leq N
, observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{Z}\right]=\sum_{i\in\bar{I}}f_{i}(A)\leq\sum_{i\in\bar{I}}f_{i}(A)+\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(A)=F(A)=\mathbb{E}\left[F^{\prime}(A)\right],

(25)

where the last step is by
Equation (
22
)
. We now bound
\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\bar{Z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{Z}\right]\right|\geq\varepsilon\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[F^{\prime}(A)\right]\right]

by an application of the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound
[
29
]
in the version of Theorem 2.2 in
[
34
]
.
A check of the preconditions reveals that the
Z_{i}
’s are independent random variables (since the
w_{i}
’s are sampled independently).
Letting
a:=\max_{i\in\bar{I}}Z_{i}
, the
Z_{i}
’s with
i\in\bar{I}
all have range
[0,a]
.
Choosing
\mu:=\mathbb{E}\left[F^{\prime}(A)\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{Z}\right]
, we conclude

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\bar{Z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{Z}\right]\right|\geq\varepsilon\mu\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\mu}{3a}}\\
\iff&\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\bar{Z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{Z}\right]\right|\geq\varepsilon\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[F^{\prime}(A)\right]\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}F(A)}{3a}}.\end{split}

(26)

This is very close to
Equation (
24
)
. Indeed, the last we need is an upper bound on
a
.
To accomplish this, note that

\displaystyle Z_{i}=w_{i}f_{i}(A)=\frac{f_{i}(A)}{\kappa p_{i}}\leq\frac{f_{i}(A)}{\kappa\max_{S\subseteq E}\frac{f_{i}(S)}{F(S)}}\leq\frac{f_{i}(A)}{\kappa\frac{f_{i}(A)}{F(A)}}=\frac{F(A)}{\kappa}

for all
i\in\bar{I}
. Thus, maximising over all

reveals

.
Substituting this into

yields

24
)
.
The RHS

becomes
\leq\frac{\delta}{|\mathcal{D}|}
when choosing

as in
Algorithm

above, proving

21
)
.

Part
(ii)
is a simple computation. The events

occur with probability

, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[w_{i}\neq 0\right]\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\left[w_{i}\neq 0\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa p_{i}=\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i},

where

by the choice in
Algorithm

.
Hence the overall bound

.

Part
(iii)
is shown as follows.
Let
[w_{i}\neq 0]
be an indicator random variable for the event
\{w_{i}\neq 0\}
, i. e.,
[w_{i}\neq 0]=1
if
w_{i}\neq 0

and
[w_{i}\neq 0]=0
if
w_{i}=0
.
The variance of a sum of independent random variables is the sum of their variances.
Thus,

\displaystyle\text{$\mathbb{V}$ar}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]=\text{$\mathbb{V}$ar}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}[w_{i}\neq 0]\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{$\mathbb{V}$ar}\left[[w_{i}\neq 0]\right]

where

for each

.
It follows that

.

Thus, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\textsf{size}(w)-\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]\right]\leq\frac{\text{$\mathbb{V}$ar}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]}{\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]\right)^{2}}\leq\frac{4}{\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]},

which is
\leq 4\varepsilon^{2}
because
\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]\geq 1/\varepsilon^{2}
:
If
\kappa_{i}=1
for some
i
(i. e.
I\neq\varnothing
), we know that
\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa_{i}\geq 1\geq 1/\varepsilon^{2}
(recall
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
).
If
\kappa_{i}<1
for all

(i. e.
I=\varnothing
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa_{i}=\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\overset{(\star)}{\geq}\kappa\geq 1/\varepsilon^{2}
,
where
(\star)
is justified by the following bound.

Claim:
We always have
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\geq 1
.

Fix an arbitrary element
A^{*}\in\mathcal{D}
with

. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{D}\\
F(A)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{f_{i}(A)}{F(A)}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{f_{i}(A^{*})}{F(A^{*})}=\frac{1}{F(A^{*})}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(A^{*})=\frac{1}{F(A^{*})}\cdot F(A^{*})=1.

Noting
\mathbb{P}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\geq\frac{3}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[|\textsf{size}(w)-\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]|\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\textsf{size}(w)\right]\right]\leq 4\varepsilon^{2}

(as the former implies the latter) completes the proof.
∎

B.1
Preliminaries

For a submodular function
f:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}
, the
submodular polyhedron
of

f

[
15
]
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(f):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{E}\mid x(S)\leq f(S)\text{ for all }S\subseteq E\right\}

(29)

and the
base polyhedron
of

f

[
15
]
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(f):=\left\{x\in\mathcal{P}(f)\mid x(E)=f(E)\right\},

(30)

where
x
is an
|E|
-dimensional vector and
x(S)
denotes the quantity
\sum_{e\in S}x_{e}
.

We denote by
\mathsf{EX}(P)
the set of extreme points of a polyhedron
P
.
In particular,
\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f))
is the set of extreme points of the base polyhedron of a submodular function
f
.

We will need a result that characterises the extreme points of the base polyhedron of a submodular function.

Theorem 12

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
f:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}
be submodular. A point
y\in\mathcal{B}(f)

is an extreme point of
\mathcal{B}(f)
if and only if for a maximal chain (with
respect to inclusion)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:\varnothing=S_{0}\subset S_{1}\subset\dots\subset S_{n}=E

(31)

of
2^{E}
we have
y_{e_{i}}=f(S_{i})-f(S_{i-1})
for
i=1,\dots,n
, where
\{e_{i}\}=S_{i}\setminus S_{i-1}
.

Corollary 13
.

Let
f:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}
be submodular and monotone. Then

\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f))\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, i. e., any extreme point of
the base polyhedron has only non-negative coordinates.

Proof.

Suppose
y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f))
is an extreme point of the base polyhedron.
By
Theorem
12
, there is a maximal chain
\varnothing=S_{0}\subset S_{1}\subset\dots\subset S_{n}=E

such that
y_{e_{i}}=f(S_{i})-f(S_{i-1})
for
1\leq i\leq n
, where
\{e_{i}\}=S_{i}\setminus S_{i-1}
.
By monotonicity,

\displaystyle y_{e_{i}}=f(S_{i})-f(S_{i-1})=f(S_{i-1}\cup\{e_{i}\})-f(S_{i-1})\geq f(S_{i-1})-f(S_{i-1})=0

for each
1\leq i\leq n
. Since the chain is maximal, each element
e\in E
occurs as some
e_{i}
,
implying
y_{e}\geq 0
for all
e\in E
.
∎

The next lemma expresses the values
f(A)
of a submodular function in terms of a maximisation over the base polyhedron.

Lemma 14
.

Let
f:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}
be normalised submodular. Then

\displaystyle f(A)=\max_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f))}\left\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\right\rangle

for all
A\subseteq E
.

Proof.

The inequality
\max_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f))}\left\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\right\rangle\leq f(A)

is immediate since any
y\in\mathcal{B}(f)
satisfies
y(S)\leq f(S)
for all
S\subseteq E
, including
S=A
.
Hence

\displaystyle\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle=\sum_{e\in E}y_{e}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{A}(e)=\sum_{e\in A}y_{e}=y(A)\leq f(A).

To see that the value
f(A)
is actually attained, we construct a suitable extreme point using
Theorem
12
.
Suppose
A=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\}
. Order the remaining elements arbitrarily, so
E\setminus A=\{e_{k+1},\dots,e_{n}\}
.
Now define a maximal chain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:\varnothing=S_{0}\subset S_{1}\subset\dots\subset S_{n}=E

by letting
S_{i}:=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{i}\}
for
0\leq i\leq n
. Next, we set

\displaystyle y_{e_{i}}:=f(S_{i})-f(S_{i-1})

for
1\leq i\leq n
, which defines a vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
that – by
construction – happens to be an extreme point of
\mathcal{B}(f)

by
Theorem
12
. Moreover, it satisfies

\displaystyle y(A)=\sum_{e\in A}y_{e}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{e_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(f(S_{i})-f(S_{i-1})\right)=f(S_{k})-f(\varnothing)=f(S_{k})=f(A)

as
f
is normalised.
Thus,
y
witnesses that
\max_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f))}\left\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\right\rangle\geq f(A)
.
∎

B.2
Counterexample

We will now disprove the bound
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\leq Bn
claimed by
Rafiey and Yoshida (cf. Claim 3.3 in
[
34
]
, proof in the
appendix) by providing a counterexample.

The counterexample is inspired by Cohen et al.
[
11
]
, who use it to establish a lower bound on the size of a cut sparsifier.
This is exactly what we need to disprove
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\leq Bn
: The ability to isolate edges in order to have peak contributions of
1
.
Consider the directed complete bipartite graph
G=(V,E)
with bipartition
V=L\cup R
where
L=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{5}\}
,

R=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{5}\}
and
E=L\times R
as depicted in
Fig.
1
.
For each edge
e=(u,v)\in E
, we define a cut function

\displaystyle f_{e}:2^{V}\to\mathbb{R},\quad S\mapsto\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }u\in S\text{ and }v\notin S,\\
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

It is well-known (and not hard to see) that the
f_{e}
’s are submodular.

The sum
F:=\sum_{e\in E}f_{e}

is known as the (directed) cut function of
G
, since
F(S)
is the number of edges cut connecting a vertex in
S
to a vertex outside
S
.
Let
p_{e}=\max_{S\subseteq V}\frac{f_{e}(S)}{F(S)}
denote the peak contributions of the
f_{e}
’s.

u_{1}
u_{2}
u_{3}
u_{4}
u_{5}
v_{1}
v_{2}
v_{3}
v_{4}
v_{5}

Figure 1:
Graph
G
where each edge can be isolated by a cut

We will now establish how this example violates
\sum_{e\in E}p_{e}\leq Bn
.
First,

\displaystyle B=\max_{e\in E}|\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{e}))|=2

because each base polyhedron
\mathcal{B}(f_{i})
has two extreme points.
An easy way to see this goes by
Theorem
12
.
For any maximal chain
\varnothing=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\dots\subset V_{n}=V
,
we have

\displaystyle f_{e}(V_{i+1})-f_{e}(V_{i})=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }V_{i+1}\setminus V_{i}=\{u\}\text{ and }v\notin V_{i},\\
-1&\text{ if }V_{i+1}\setminus V_{i}=\{v\}\text{ and }u\in V_{i},\\
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Thus, the only extreme points are
y
and
y^{\prime}
, where
y_{e}=0
for all
e\in E
,
and
y^{\prime}_{u}=1
,
y^{\prime}_{v}=-1
,
y^{\prime}_{e}=0
for all
e\in E\setminus\{u,v\}
.
Since
G
has
10
vertices and the vertex set
V=L\cup R

[

]
asserts that
\sum_{e\in E}p_{e}\leq Bn=20
.
By showing
p_{e}=1
for each
e\in E
, we establish

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}p_{e}=|E|=|L|\cdot|R|=5\cdot 5=25>20,

contradicting the assertion.
Fix an edge

. Letting

, as desired.

B.3
Monotone Submodularity

Now we have all tools required to establish an upper bound on
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}
, making the size bound in
Theorem
3

(ii)

simpler and more expressive.

The following lemma appears as Claim 3.3 in
[
34
]
with a very similar proof.
However, the proof of Lemma
15
below only works if the coordinates of the extreme points of the base polyhedra are non-negative.
We give greater detail and explanations of the individual steps and point out where exactly the non-negativity is needed,
hence where the proof in
[
34
]
fails.
Corollary
13
ensures non-negativity when
all
f_{i}
’s are monotone.
For the following lemma, we specialise to
\mathcal{D}=2^{E}
for some ground set
E
of size
n
.

Lemma 15
.

Let normalised, monotone submodular functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}
be given
with peak contributions defined with respect to
F=f_{1}+\dots+f_{N}
.
Then
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\leq Bn
, where
B=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}|\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{i}))|

denotes the maximum number of extreme points of the base polyhedra of the
f_{i}
’s.

Proof.

The key ingredient is
Lemma
14
. It gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max_{A\subseteq E}\frac{f_{i}(A)}{F(A)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max_{A\subseteq E}\frac{\max_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{i}))}\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\max_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))}\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle}

(32)

using the definition of the
p_{i}
’s.
Since all
f_{i}
’s are monotone, we know that
y\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
for all
y\in\mathcal{B}(f_{i})
for every
1\leq i\leq N
,
so all terms
\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle
involved in the above equation are non-negative.
For non-negative numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we always have

\displaystyle\frac{x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}}{n}\overset{(1)}{\leq}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}x_{i}\overset{(2)}{\leq}x_{1}+\dots+x_{n},

(33)

i. e., the maximum lies between the average and the sum.
This is a trick used in the proof by Rafiey and Yoshida
[
34
]
that does not hold for general submodular functions.
Non-negativity plays an important role in a couple of steps in the remainder of this proof.
Applying (1) to the denominator and (2) to the numerator
in
Equation (
32
)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\max_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{i}))}\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\max_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))}\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{i}))}\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))|}\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))}\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle}

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{e\in A}\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{i}))}y_{e}}{\sum_{e\in A}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))|}\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))}y_{e}},

(35)

where the equality step is by
\langle y,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle=\sum_{e\in A}y_{e}
.
Next, note that
\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{i}))}y_{e}\geq 0

and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))|}\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))}y_{e}\geq 0
for all
e\in A

because
y_{e}\geq 0
for all
y\in\mathcal{B}(f_{i})
and
e\in E
, so we can employ the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}}{q_{1}+\dots+q_{n}}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{x_{i}}{q_{i}}

(36)

that applies to all non-negative numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, giving

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{e\in A}\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{i}))}y_{e}}{\sum_{e\in A}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))|}\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))}y_{e}}\leq\max_{e\in A}\frac{\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{i}))}y_{e}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))|}\sum_{y\in\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f_{j}))}y_{e}}.

(37)

Combining this with
Equation (
34
)
and
Equation (
35
)
, we can extend
Equation (
32
)
to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}

Appendix C
Monotone Submodular Functions of Bounded Arity

The motivation of submodular sparsification partly arises from the
sparsification of graph cuts, which have been extensively studied not only for
graphs but also for
hypergraphs
[
39
,
25
,
10
]
.
An improvement in both size and construction time of the
\varepsilon
-sparsifier
is possible if the submodular function
F
in question can be decomposed as
F=f_{1}+\dots+f_{N}
into
f_{i}
’s of
bounded arity
.
This covers a broad range of submodular functions such as cut functions.
As a simple application of our core algorithm, we will show that an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of expected size
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
can be found (for a fixed
\delta
) if all constituent functions

f_{i}
of
F
are monotone (needed in Lemma
15
) and submodular (needed in Lemma
18
and Lemma
19
).

In detail, consider a set function
f:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Instead of writing
f(S)
for
S\subseteq E
to describe the values of
f
,
we may encode
S
as a Boolean vector of
|E|
coordinates.
Fix an arbitrary order of elements in
E
, say
E=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
and set the
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate of the vector corresponding to
S
to
1

if and only if
e_{i}\in S
. This standard identification allows us to write
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to
describe the values of
f
.
From this perspective, it is intuitive to say that
f
has
arity
a
if its values only depend on
a
arguments.
It is worth noting that this representation aligns with how one would represent
f

in the context of constraint satisfaction problems, as part of a valued constraint language
[
26
]
.
Note that
f
having arity
a
means there exists a set
C\subseteq E
of size
|C|=a

such that
f(S)=f(S\cap C)
for all
S\subseteq E
.
We call
C
the
effective support
of
f
.

We start from a decomposition
F=f_{1}+\dots+f_{N}

of a submodular function
F
into submodular functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}
of arity
\leq a
for some constant
a
.
Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{N}
denote the effective supports of
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}
, respectively.
In order to construct a sparsifier, we want to apply
Algorithm
1
.
To do so efficiently, we need to be able to compute the peak contributions, i. e., maximise the quotients
\frac{f_{i}(A)}{F(A)}
over all
A\subseteq E

for each
1\leq i\leq N
. Since the numerators only depend on how
A
intersects the
C_{i}
’s – which are of constant size –
we might brute-force this intersection and minimise the denominator with respect
to an extra constraint.

Remark 17
.

To make this idea work, it is vital for us to
know
the
C_{i}
’s.
There might be scenarios where all
f_{i}
’s are known to have bounded arity while the elements they are supported on are not computationally available.
However, if
f
is monotone and non-negative, the effective support can be found by testing each element
e\in E

for
f(\{e\})>f(\varnothing)
. If the test fails, we know that
e
does not influence the values of
f
and hence does not belong to its effective support.

1:

Functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}
with effective supports
C_{1},\dots,C_{N}
of size
\leq a

2:

p_{i}=\max_{A\subseteq E}\frac{f_{i}(A)}{F(A)}
for each
1\leq i\leq N

3:

for

i=1,\dots,N

do

4:

p_{i}\leftarrow 0

5:

for all

H\subseteq C_{i}

do

6:

Compute a minimiser
A^{*}
of
\widehat{F}:2^{E\setminus C_{i}}\to\mathbb{R},A\mapsto F(A\cup H)

7:

p_{i}\leftarrow\max\left\{p_{i},\frac{f_{i}(H)}{\widehat{F}(A^{*})}\right\}

8:

end

for

9:

end

for

p_{1},\dots,p_{N}

Algorithm 3

Computing the peak contributions for
f_{i}
’s of bounded arity

Refer to
Algorithm
3
for a formal description of how the peak contributions are computed.
For each
1\leq i\leq N
, the quantity
p_{i}
is computed as follows. We first choose a set
H\subseteq C_{i}

and then restrict ourselves to sets
A\subseteq E
with
A\cap C_{i}=H
.
This implies
f_{i}(A)=f_{i}(A\cap C_{i})=f_{i}(H)
as
C_{i}
is the effective support of
f_{i}
.
Hence, we are left with the quotient
f_{i}(H)/F(A)
that has to be maximised over all
A\subseteq E
with
A\cap C_{i}=H
.
Since the numerator no longer depends on
A
, the objective becomes minimising
F(A)
subject to
A\cap C_{i}=H
.
It turns out that this is just a submodular minimisation problem of an auxiliary function
\widehat{F}
.
The following statement is key to the correctness of this approach.

Lemma 18
.

Let
f,F:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}
be submodular functions and let
C\subseteq E
denote the effective support of
f
.
Then

\displaystyle\max_{A\subseteq E}\frac{f(A)}{F(A)}=\max_{H\subseteq C}\frac{f(H)}{\min_{A\subseteq E\setminus C}\widehat{F}(A)}

where
\widehat{F}:2^{E\setminus C}\to\mathbb{R},A\mapsto F(A\cup H)
.

Proof.

“
\leq
” Let
A^{*}
be a maximiser of the LHS.
Set
H:=A^{*}\cap C
and
A:=A^{*}\setminus C
.
We now have

\displaystyle\frac{f(A^{*})}{F(A^{*})}=\frac{f\left(A^{*}\cap C\right)}{F\left(\left(A^{*}\setminus C\right)\cup\left(A^{*}\cap C\right)\right)}=\frac{f(H)}{F\left(\left(A^{*}\setminus C\right)\cup H\right)}=\frac{f(H)}{\widehat{F}(A)}

with
H\subseteq C
and
A\subseteq E\setminus C
, so this is certainly
\leq
the RHS above.

“
\geq
” Let
(H^{*},A^{*})
be a maximising pair of the RHS.
Letting
A:=A^{*}\cup H^{*}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{f(H^{*})}{\widehat{F}(A^{*})}=\frac{f(A\cap C)}{F(A^{*}\cup H)}=\frac{f(A)}{F(A)},

which is always
\leq
the LHS as the maximisation is over all
A
.
∎

One more observation allows us to give a more precise size bound on the sparsifier in the bounded arity case.
By
Theorem
3
, the
\varepsilon
-sparsifier produced by
Algorithm
1
is of expected size

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log{|\mathcal{D}|}+\log{\frac{1}{\delta}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\right)
,
where
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\leq Bn
by
Lemma
15
, if the
f_{i}
’s are all monotone.
In general, the quantity
B
might be large. However, if the functions all have bounded arity, we can show that
B=\mathcal{O}(1)
,
allowing for an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of expected size
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
in case
of monotone constituent functions – assuming
\delta
is
constant.

The following statement claimed but not shown in
[
34
]
gives a
bound on
B
that is independent of
n
or
N
but just depends on the arity.
For completeness, we give a proof.

Lemma 19
.

Suppose
f:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}
is submodular with arity
\leq a
.
Then the base polyhedron
\mathcal{B}(f)
has at most
2^{a^{2}}
extreme points, i. e.,
|\mathsf{EX}(\mathcal{B}(f))|\leq 2^{a^{2}}
.

Proof.

By
Theorem
12
,
a point
x\in\mathcal{B}(f)
is an extreme point if and only if there is a maximal chain
\varnothing=S_{0}\subset S_{1}\subset\dots\subset S_{n}=E

with
x(e_{i})=f(S_{i})-f(S_{i-1})
for
1\leq i\leq n
, where
\{e_{i}\}=S_{i}\setminus S_{i-1}
.
Letting
C
denote the effective support of
f
, these equations become
x(e_{i})=f(S_{i}\cap C)-f(S_{i-1}\cap C)
.
Now
e_{i}\notin C
implies

\displaystyle S_{i}\cap C=(S_{i-1}\cup\{e_{i}\})\cap C=S_{i-1}\cap C,

hence
x(e_{i})=f(S_{i}\cap C)-f(S_{i-1}\cap C)=f(S_{i-1}\cap C)-f(S_{i-1}\cap C)=0
.
Therefore, only the coordinates corresponding to elements
e\in C
can be non-zero.
For any such coordinate
e\in C
, we have

\displaystyle x(e)=f(S\cup\{e\})-f(S)=f\left(\left(S\cup\{e\}\right)\cap C\right)-f(S\cap C)

where
S
is the (unique) set in the maximal chain
e
is added to.
Now observe that the RHS can attain at most
2^{a}
possible values: One for each subset
S\subseteq C
.
This is because the value is completely determined by how
S
intersects
C
.

Putting it all together, we have at most
a
non-zero coordinates with at most
2^{a}
distinct values for each of them.
Counting all combinations, we retrieve an upper bound of
\left(2^{a}\right)^{a}=2^{a^{2}}
candidates for
x
.
Thus,
\mathcal{B}(f)
has at most
2^{a^{2}}
extreme points.
∎
