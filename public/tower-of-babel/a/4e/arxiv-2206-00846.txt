Faster Rates of Convergence to Stationary Points in Differentially Private Optimization

Abstract
We study the problem of approximating stationary points of Lipschitz and smooth functions under ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -differential privacy (DP) in both the finite-sum and stochastic settings. A point w ^ \widehat{w} is called an α \alpha -stationary point of a function F : ℝ d → ℝ F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} if ‖ ∇ F ​ ( w ^ ) ‖ ≤ α \|\nabla F(\widehat{w})\|\leq\alpha . We provide a new efficient algorithm that finds an O ~ ​ ( [ d n ​ ε ] 2 / 3 ) \tilde{O}\big(\big[\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n\varepsilon}\big]^{2/3}\big) -stationary point in the finite-sum setting, where n n is the number of samples. This improves on the previous best rate of O ~ ​ ( [ d n ​ ε ] 1 / 2 ) \tilde{O}\big(\big[\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n\varepsilon}\big]^{1/2}\big) . We also give a new construction that improves over the existing rates in the stochastic optimization setting, where the goal is to find approximate stationary points of the population risk. Our construction finds a O ~ ​ ( 1 n 1 / 3 + [ d n ​ ε ] 1 / 2 ) \tilde{O}\big(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}+\big[\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n\varepsilon}\big]^{1/2}\big) -stationary point of the population risk in time linear in n n . 1.2
Our Techniques

Our methods combine multiple techniques from optimization and differential privacy in novel ways. The lower bound for the empirical norm of the gradient uses fingerprinting codes to a loss similar to that used for Differentially Private-Empirical Risk Minimization (DP-ERM)
[
11
]
, crafted to work in the unconstrained case. This lower bound can be extended to the population gradient norm by a known re-sampling argument
[
8
]
. We also give a non-private lower bound of
\Omega\left({1/\sqrt{n}}\right)
on population stationarity with
n
samples which holds even in dimension 1, as opposed to previous results
[
23
]
.

Efficient algorithms for (both empirical and population) norm of the gradient are derived using noisy versions of variance-reduced stochastic first order methods, which have proved remarkably useful in DP stochastic optimization
[
4
,
10
,
9
]
.
However, in contrast to previous work which scales noise proportionally to the Lipschitz constant
[
48
,
49
]
or (in the case of constrained optimization) the diameter of the constraint set
[
9
,
10
]
, we observe that the gradient variations between iterates
w,w^{\prime}
can be privatized more effectively by scaling the noise proportional to
L_{1}\left\|{w-w^{\prime}}\right\|
.
In the case of the empirical risk, we use a noisy version of SpiderBoost
[
45
]
. We remark that our methods can achieve comparable rates when applied to similar algorithms such as Spider
[
21
]
and Storm
[
14
]
, but SpiderBoost allows for a larger learning rate which is considered better in practice. For the population risk, it is worth noting that the empirical norm of the gradient does not translate directly into population gradient guarantees, even if the algorithm in use is uniformly stable
[
7
]
, since this type of guarantee does not enjoy a
stability-implies-generalization
property. Therefore, we opt for single pass methods that combine variance-reduction with tree-aggregation; these techniques are particularly suitable for the classical Spider algorithm
[
21
]
, which is the one we base our method on. For the convex setting, we use recursive regularization
[
5
]
which was used to achieve the optimal non-private rate by
[
23
]
.

Finally, our method for (non-convex) GLMs uses the Johnson-Lindenstrauss based dimensionality reduction technique similar to

[
1

, which focused on the convex setting.
Moreover, for population stationarity of GLMs, we give a new uniform convergence result of gradients of Lipschitz functions.
This guarantee, unlike the prior work of
[
22
]
, has only poly-logarithmic dependence on the radius of the constraint set, which is crucial for our analysis.

Preliminaries

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
denote a (loss) function taking as input, the model parameter
w
and data point
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
. We assume that the function
w\mapsto f(w;x)
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
L_{1}
-smooth. That is, for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\left|{f(w_{1};x)-f(w_{2};x)}\right|\leq L_{0}\left\|{w_{1}-w_{2}}\right\|
and
\left\|{\nabla f(w_{1};x)-\nabla f(w_{2};x)}\right\|\leq L_{1}\left\|{w_{1}-w_{2}}\right\|
.
Given a dataset
S\in{\mathcal{X}}^{n}
of
n
points, we define the empirical risk as
F(w;S)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(w;x_{i})
. Assuming that the data points are sampled i.i.d. from an unknown distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
, the population risk, denoted as
F(w;{\mathcal{D}})
is defined as
F(w;{\mathcal{D}})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}f(w;x)
. Furthermore, we define
F_{0}=F(0;S)-\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{{F(w;S)}\right\}
when discussing the empirical case and similarly for the population loss when discussing stationary points of the population loss. We use
w^{*}
to denote the population risk minimizer. Finally, we use the notation
\mathbb{I}_{d}
to denote the
d\times d
identity matrix and use
[a]
to denote the set
\left\{{1,2,...,a}\right\}
for
a\geq 1
.

Stationary points:

Given a dataset
S
, our goal is to find an
\alpha
-stationary point
\bar{w}
of either empirical or population risk; formally,
\left\|{\nabla F(\bar{w};S)}\right\|\leq\alpha
or
\left\|{\nabla F(\bar{w};{\mathcal{D}})}\right\|\leq\alpha
, respectively.

Differential Privacy (DP)
[
17
]
:

An algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private if for all datasets
S
and
S^{\prime}
differing in one data point and all events
{\mathcal{E}}
in the range of the
{\mathcal{A}}
, we have,
\mathbb{P}\left({{\mathcal{A}}(S)\in{\mathcal{E}}}\right)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left({{\mathcal{A}}(S^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{E}}}\right)+\delta
.

Generalized Linear Models (GLMs):

For data domain
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
, a loss function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a GLM if
f(w;(x,y))=\phi_{y}\left({\left\langle{w},{x}\right\rangle}\right)
for some function
\phi_{y}
. Our result for GLMs uses random matrices which satisfy the Johnson-Lindenstrauss (JL) property, defined as follows.

Definition 1

(

-JL property)
.

A random matrix

satisfies

-JL property if for any

,
\mathbb{P}\left[\left|{\left\langle{\Phi u},{\Phi v}\right\rangle-\left\langle{u},{v}\right\rangle}\right|>\gamma\left\|{u}\right\|\left\|{v}\right\|\right]\leq\beta.

It is a noisy variant of SPIDER

Namely, we run

On the other hand, when a right child node is visited, it receives a fresh set of samples and uses it to update the gradient estimator coming from the parent node. \displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{E^{c}}\left\|{\nabla F(\bar{w};S)}\right\|\geq\frac{L_{1}B^{2}}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{B}=\frac{L_{0}}{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla F(\bar{w};S)\|]

Lemma 2
.

Let
G,R\geq 0,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let
{\mathcal{W}}_{R}(w_{0})
denote the Euclidean ball around
w_{0}
of radius
R
and let
\partial{\mathcal{W}}_{R}(w_{0})
denote its boundary.
Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a differentiable convex function.
Suppose
w_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is such that
for every
v\in\partial{\mathcal{W}}_{R}(w_{0})
,

\left\langle{\nabla f(v)},{v-w_{0}}\right\rangle\geq G
, then for any
w\not\in{\mathcal{W}}_{R}(w_{0})
, we have
\left\|{\nabla f(w)}\right\|\geq\frac{G}{R}
.

Proof.

For a unit vector
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, define directional directive
f^{\prime}_{u}(w)=\left\langle{\nabla f(w)},{u}\right\rangle
. We first show that for any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\left\|{u}\right\|=1
and any
w^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the function
f^{\prime}_{u}(w^{\prime}+ru)
is non-decreasing in
r\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. This simply follows from monotonicity of gradients since
f
is convex. In particular, for any
r^{\prime}>r>0
, we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{u}(w^{\prime}+r^{\prime}u)-f^{\prime}_{u}(w^{\prime}+ru)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\nabla f(w^{\prime}+r^{\prime}u)-\nabla f(w^{\prime}+ru)},{u}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{\prime}-r}\left\langle{\nabla f(w^{\prime}+r^{\prime}u)-\nabla f(w^{\prime}+ru)},{w^{\prime}+ru-(w^{\prime}+ru)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle>0

We now prove the claim in the lemma statement. Let
w\not\in\partial W_{R}
and define
u=\frac{w-w_{0}}{\left\|{w-w_{0}}\right\|}
. Then from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the above monotonicity property, we have,

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla f(w)}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle{\nabla f(w)},{u}\right\rangle=f^{\prime}_{u}(w)\geq f^{\prime}_{u}(w_{0}+Ru)=\left\langle{\nabla f(w_{0}+Ru)},{u}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\left\langle{\nabla f(v)},{v-w_{0}}\right\rangle\geq\frac{G}{R}

which finishes the proof.

Theorem 7
.

For any
L_{0},{L_{1}}
,
n,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over some set
{\mathcal{X}}
and a
L_{0}
-Lipschitz,
{L_{1}}
-smooth (convex) loss function
w\mapsto f(w;x)
such that given
n
i.i.d samples from
{\mathcal{D}}
, the output
\bar{w}
of any algorithm satisfies,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|{\nabla{F}(\bar{w};{\mathcal{D}})}\right\|=\Omega\left({\frac{L_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}}\right)

Proof.

We construct a hard instance in
d=1
dimension.
Let
p\in[0,1]

be a parameter to be set later
and let
v\in\left\{{-1,1}\right\}
be chosen by an adversary.
Let the data domain
{\mathcal{X}}=\left\{{-1,1}\right\}
and consider the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
as follows:

\displaystyle x=\begin{cases}1&\text{with probability}\ \ \frac{1+vp}{2}\\
-1&\text{with probability}\ \ \frac{1-vp}{2}\\
\end{cases}

Note that
\mathbb{E}[x]=vp
.
Consider the loss function
f(w;x)
as

\displaystyle f(w;x)=\frac{L_{0}}{2}wx+\frac{L_{1}}{2}\Delta(w)

where
\Delta
is the Huber regularization function, defined as,

\displaystyle\Delta(w)=\begin{cases}\left|{w}\right|^{2}&\text{if}\ \left|{w}\right|\leq\frac{L_{0}}{2L_{1}}\\
\frac{L_{0}\left|{w}\right|}{L_{1}}-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{4L_{1}^{2}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Note that the loss function
w\mapsto f(w;x)
is convex,
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
L_{1}
-smooth in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, for all
x
.
The population risk function is,

\displaystyle F(w;{\mathcal{D}})=\frac{L_{0}}{2}wpv+\frac{L_{1}}{2}\Delta(w)

Let
\bar{w}
be output some algorithm given
n
i.i.d. samples from
{\mathcal{D}}
. Consider two cases:

Case 1:
\left|{\bar{w}}\right|>\frac{L_{0}}{2L_{1}}
:

The gradient norm in this case is

\displaystyle\left|{\nabla F(\bar{w};{\mathcal{D}})}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|{\frac{L_{0}}{2}vp+\frac{L_{0}\bar{w}}{2\left|{\bar{w}}\right|}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{0}^{2}p^{2}}{4}+\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{4}+\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{2\left|{\bar{w}}\right|}vp{\bar{w}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{4}-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{2}p

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{4}-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{8\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{8}

where
the first inequality follows since
v\frac{\bar{w}}{\left|{\bar{w}}\right|}\geq-1
,
the third equality follows by setting
p=\frac{1}{\sqrt{16n}}
and the second inequality follows since

. We therefore have that
\mathbb{E}\left|{\nabla F(\bar{w};{\mathcal{D}})}\right|\geq\frac{L_{0}}{2\sqrt{2}}
.

Case 2:
\left|{\bar{w}}\right|\leq\frac{L_{0}}{2L_{1}}
:

In this case, the gradient norm is,

\displaystyle\left|{\nabla F(\bar{w};{\mathcal{D}})}\right|^{2}=\left|{\frac{L_{0}}{2}vp+L_{1}\bar{w}}\right|^{2}

Suppose there exists
an algorithm with output
\bar{w}
, which, with
n
samples guarantees that
\mathbb{E}\left|{\nabla{F}(\bar{w};{\mathcal{D}})}\right|<o\left({\frac{L_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}}\right)
. Then from Markov’s inequality, with probability at least
0.9
, we have that
\left|{\nabla{F}(\bar{w};{\mathcal{D}})}\right|^{2}<o\left({\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{n}}\right)
. Let

\tilde{w}=-\frac{2L_{1}\bar{w}}{L_{0}}

, then we have that
with probability at least
0.9
,

\displaystyle\left|{\nabla{F}(\bar{w};{\mathcal{D}})}\right|^{2}\leq o\left({\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{n}}\right)\iff\left|{vp-\tilde{w}}\right|^{2}<o\left({\frac{1}{n}}\right)

This contradicts the well-known bias estimation lower bounds, with
p=\frac{1}{\sqrt{16n}}
, using Le Cam’s method (
[

, Example 7.7), hence

\mathbb{E}\left|{\nabla{F}(\bar{w};{\mathcal{D}})}\right|\geq\Omega\left({\frac{L_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}}\right)
.
Combining the two cases finishes the proof.

We here give a more detailed version of the informal discussion in Section
3.2
.
We want to emphasize that the goal of the following discussion is not to provide a universal lower bound, but rather to inform future research.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a loss function.
We say the randomized mapping
{\mathcal{O}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup\bot)\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
is a
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
-accurate oracle for
{\mathcal{L}}
if

\forall w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{O}}(w,\bot)\right]=\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w),

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})\right]=\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w^{\prime})

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w,\bot)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w)}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\tau_{1}^{2},

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\tau_{2}^{2}\left\|{w-w^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}.

In short,
{\mathcal{O}}
is an unbiased and accurate gradient/gradient variation oracle for
{\mathcal{L}}
.
Define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(G,L_{1},{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\inf_{{\mathcal{A}}}\sup_{{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{L}}}\inf\left\{{\alpha:\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{\nabla{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{O}},L_{1},{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}\right\|\right]\leq\alpha}\right\},

where the supremum is taken over
L_{1}
-smooth functions
{\mathcal{L}}
satisfying
{\mathcal{L}}(0)-\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{{{\mathcal{L}}(w)}\right\}\leq{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
, and
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
-accurate oracles for
{\mathcal{L}}
. The infimum is taken over algorithms which make at most
G
calls to
{\mathcal{O}}
.

We have the following lower bound on
\mathfrak{m}
(i.e. a lower bound on the accuracy of optimization algorithms which make at most
G
queries to the oracle) following from
[
2
, Theorem 3]
and the fact that the oracle model described above is a special case of the multi-query oracles considered by
[
2
]
.

Theorem 10

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
G,{\mathcal{L}}_{0},L_{1},\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\geq 0
and define
\alpha=\left({\frac{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\tau_{2}\tau_{1}}{G}}\right)^{1/3}+\frac{\tau_{1}}{\sqrt{G}}
.
If
d=\tilde{\Omega}\left({\big[\frac{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}L_{1}}{\alpha^{2}}\big]^{2}}\right)
,
then

\mathfrak{m}(G,L_{1},{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\Omega\left({\alpha}\right)
.

Now consider
{\mathcal{L}}
such that
{\mathcal{L}}(w)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in S}\ell(w;x)
for some
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
L_{1}
-smooth loss

\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
S\in{\mathcal{X}}^{n}
.
We are interested in designing some
(\widehat{\tau}_{1},\widehat{\tau}_{2})
-accurate and differentially private oracle,
\widehat{{\mathcal{O}}}
, which can then be used by an optimization algorithm,
{\mathcal{A}}
, to obtain an approximate stationary point
\bar{w}={\mathcal{A}}(\widehat{{\mathcal{O}}},L_{1},{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\widehat{\tau}_{1},\widehat{\tau}_{2})
.
Specifically, we want
\widehat{{\mathcal{O}}}
to be capable of answering
G
queries under
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. A common method for achieving this is to ensure each query to
{\mathcal{O}}
is at least
(\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{G}},\delta)
-DP and use advanced composition (or the more refined moment accountant) analysis.
Such a setup encapsulates numerous results in the convex setting
[
8
,
30
]
, and is even more dominant in non-convex settings
[
47
,
48
,
3
]
.

Our key observation is that under such a setup, any increase in the number of oracle calls to
G
must be met with a proportional increase in the accuracy parameters
(\widehat{\tau}_{1},\widehat{\tau}_{2})
. Thus, if such an oracle,
\widehat{{\mathcal{O}}}
is applied in a black box fashion to a stochastic optimization algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
, one can obtain a lower bound on the accuracy of the overall algorithm independent of
G
.

Specifically, since estimating the gradient and gradient variation can be viewed as mean estimation problems on
n
vectors, we can use fingerprinting code arguments to lower bound
\widehat{\tau}_{1}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{2}

[
41
]
. In Lemma
4
below, we prove that any
(\widehat{\tau}_{1},\widehat{\tau}_{2})
-accurate oracle which ensures that any query is
(\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{G}},\delta)
-DP must have

\widehat{\tau}_{1}=\Omega\Big(\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{Gd\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}\Big)
and

\widehat{\tau}_{2}=\Omega\Big(\frac{L_{1}\sqrt{Gd\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}\Big)
.
Now, observe that by Theorem
10
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(G,L_{1},{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\widehat{\tau}_{1},\widehat{\tau}_{2})=\Omega\left({\left({\frac{\sqrt{F_{0}L_{1}L_{0}}\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}}\right)^{2/3}+\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}}\right),

which matches our upper bound.

We now remark on several ways the above barrier could be circumvented.
The first and most obvious possibility is to employ a different privatization method than private oracles. However, this is particularly difficult in the nonconvex setting as existing methods which avoid private gradients (see e.g.
[
20
]
for several such methods) rely crucially on stability guarantees arising from convexity. Other possible ways to beat the above rate is by designing a stochastic optimization algorithm which leverages the structure of the noise used in private implementations of the oracle or makes use of additional assumptions
to beat the
\Omega\left({\left({\frac{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\tau_{2}\tau_{1}}{G}}\right)^{1/3}+\frac{\tau_{1}}{\sqrt{G}}}\right)
non-private lower bound.

Additional Details on Fingerprinting Bound

We conclude by giving a concrete construction for the fingerprinting argument mentioned above.

Lemma 4
.

Let
L_{0},L_{1}\geq 0
,
\varepsilon=O(1)
,
2^{-\Omega(n)}\leq\delta\leq\frac{1}{n^{1+\Omega(1)}}
and
\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}/(n\varepsilon)=O(1)
. Let
\ell,{\mathcal{L}},S
satisfy the assumptions above. Then there exists
\ell,S
such that for any oracle,
{\mathcal{O}}
, which is
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
-accurate for
{\mathcal{L}}
it holds that

\displaystyle\tau_{1}=\Omega\left({\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}}\right)

and

\displaystyle\tau_{2}=\Omega\left({\frac{L_{1}\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}}\right).

Proof.

In the following, we use
u_{j}
to denote the
j
’th component of some vector
u
.
Let
B=\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}\sqrt{d}}
and define
h:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle h(z)=\begin{cases}\frac{L_{1}}{2}w^{2}&\text{if}|w|\leq B\\
\frac{L_{0}}{\sqrt{d}}|w|-\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{2dL_{1}}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Define
d^{\prime}=\frac{d}{2}
(assume
d
is even for simplicity) and for any vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
let
u^{(1)}=[u_{1},...,u_{d^{\prime}}]^{\top}
and
u^{(2)}=[u_{d^{\prime}+1},...,u_{d}]^{\top}
.
Define
\ell(w;x)=\ell_{1}(w;x)+\ell_{2}(w;x)
where

\displaystyle\ell_{1}(w;x)=\frac{L_{0}}{\sqrt{d}}\left\langle{w^{(1)}},{x^{(1)}}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\ell_{2}(w;x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=d^{\prime}+1}^{d}h(w_{j})x_{j}.

Let
{\mathcal{W}}=\left\{{w:\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}\leq B}\right\}
and note for any
w\in{\mathcal{W}}
we have

\displaystyle\nabla\ell(w;x)=[\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{d}},...,\frac{x_{d^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{d}},w_{d^{\prime}+1}x_{d^{\prime}+1},...,w_{d}x_{d}]^{\top},

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\ell_{2}(w;x)=L_{1}\cdot\mathsf{Diag}(0,...,0,x_{d^{\prime}+1},...,x_{d})

That is, the Hessian of
\ell_{2}(w;x)
is a diagonal matrix with entries from
x
. Thus one can observe that for any
x\in\left\{{\pm 1}\right\}^{d}
we have that
\ell(\cdot;x)
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
L_{1}
-smooth over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

To prove a lower bound on
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
, it suffices to show that for any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP implementation of
{\mathcal{O}}
there exists
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w;\bot)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w)}\right\|^{2}\right]\geq\tau_{1}^{2}
and there exist
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})}\right\|^{2}\right]\geq\tau_{2}^{2}\left\|{w-w^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}
. For sake of generality, we will show that these properties hold for a set of
w,w^{\prime}
.

Note that to lower bound the gradient error, it suffices to lower bound the error with respect to the first
d^{\prime}
components. We thus argue using
\ell_{1}
, and will in fact show a lower bound for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We have for any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP oracle
{\mathcal{O}}
there exists a dataset

S\subseteq\left\{{\pm 1}\right\}^{d}
, where
|S|=n
,
of fingerprinting codes such that

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w;\bot)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w)}\right\|\right]\geq\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w;\bot)^{(1)}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in S}x^{(1)}}\right\|\right]=\Omega\left({\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}}\right).

The bound follows from standard fingerprinting code arguments. See
[
11
, Lemma 5.1]
for a lower bound and
[
41
, Theorem 1.1]
for a group privacy reduction that obtains the additional
\sqrt{\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}
factor. This fingerprinting result also induces the parameter constraints in the theorem statement.
We thus have
\tau_{1}=\Omega\left({\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}}\right)
.

Similarly, we will argue a bound on the gradient variation using
\ell_{2}
. Let
w,w^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{W}}
and
u=(w-w^{\prime})^{(2)}
. In what follows, we only use the second half of the components for each vector, and thus omit the superscript
(2)
from all vectors for readability.
We have

\nabla\ell_{2}(w;x)-\nabla\ell_{2}(w^{\prime};x)=L_{1}[u_{1}x_{1},...,u_{d^{\prime}}x_{d^{\prime}}]^{\top}
.
Then for any
c\in(0,\frac{2L_{0}}{L_{1}\sqrt{d}}]
and
u\in\left\{{\pm c}\right\}^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})-(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w^{\prime}))}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=L_{1}^{2}\cdot\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\left({{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})_{j}-\frac{u_{j}}{n}\sum_{x\in S}x_{j}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=L_{1}^{2}\cdot\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\left({u_{j}\Big(\frac{{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})_{j}}{u_{j}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in S}x_{j}\Big)}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=L_{1}^{2}\cdot\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[c^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\left({\frac{{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})_{j}}{u_{j}}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in S}x_{j}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega\left({L_{1}^{2}c^{2}\frac{d^{2}\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}}\right),

where the last step again comes from fingerprinting results. Note that the extra factor of
d
as compared to the previous bound comes from the fact that we are considering fingerprinting codes with norm larger by a factor of
\sqrt{d}
. We also use the fact that the vector
{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})
transformed using
u
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP by post processing.
Now since
c=\frac{\left\|{w-w^{\prime}}\right\|}{\sqrt{d}}
we have

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})-(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w^{\prime}))}\right\|\right]

\displaystyle=\left({L_{1}\left\|{w-w^{\prime}}\right\|\frac{\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}}\right).

Finally,
noting that
\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})-(\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(w^{\prime}))}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\underset{{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{O}}(w,w^{\prime})}\right\|^{2}\right]

we obtain
\tau_{2}=\Omega\Big(\frac{L_{1}\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\delta\right)}}{n\varepsilon}\Big)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We remark that the accuracy lower bound for the gradient variation can hold for a much more general set of vectors than that given in the proof. Specifically, the same result can be obtained for any
u=w-w^{\prime}
such that
u
has
\Theta(d)
components which are
\Omega\big(\frac{\left\|{u}\right\|}{\sqrt{d}}\big)
(i.e. any sufficiently spread out vector).
This uses the fact that it suffices to bound the number of components which disagree in sign with the fingerprinting mean and that fingerprinting codes are sampled using a product distribution, and thus the tracing attack used by fingerprinting constructions holds over any sufficiently large subset of dimensions.

Lemma 10

(Variance based bound for constant step-size SGD for strongly-convex functions)

Missing Results for Generalized Linear Models

We first give the definition of oblivious subspace embedding.

Definition 2

(
(r,\tau,\beta)
-oblivious subspace embedding)

A random matrix

-oblivious subspace embedding if for any

\displaystyle\left({1-\tau}\right)\left\|{x}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|{\Phi x}\right\|^{2}\leq\left({1+\tau}\right)\left\|{x}\right\|^{2}

It is well-known that JL matrices with embedding dimension

are

(r,\tau,\beta)
-oblivious subspace embeddings and
can be constructed efficiently
[
15
]
. A simple example is a scaled Gaussian random matrix,
\Phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\mathbf{G}
where entries of

are independent and distributed as
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
.

Since
\left\|{w}\right\|\leq\left\|{{\mathcal{W}}}\right\|
and
\left\|{x_{i}}\right\|\leq\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|
, we require only
\frac{2n\left\|{{\mathcal{W}}}\right\|\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|}{\tau}
points in
{\mathcal{W}}_{0}
to satisfy the above covering condition. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}}}\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)x_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}},w_{0}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{0}}\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\left({\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)-\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)+\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)}\right)x_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}},w_{0}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{0}}\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\left({\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)-\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)}\right)x_{i}}\right\|+\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)x_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}},w_{0}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{0}}L_{1}\left|{\left\langle{w},{x_{i}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right|\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|+\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\sup_{w_{0}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{0}}\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)x_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\tau\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|+\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\sup_{w_{0}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{0}}\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)x_{i}}\right\|

(10)

where the second last inequality follows from smoothness and the last from the definition of cover
{\mathcal{W}}_{0}
. For fixed
w_{0}
, from standard manipulations, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)x_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)x_{i}}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|{\sigma_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w_{0}},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)x_{i}}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{0}\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|}{\sqrt{n}}

Using Massart’s finite class lemma to handle all
w_{0}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{0}
, and substituting the above in Eqn. (
10
), we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}}}\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)x_{i}}\right\|\leq L_{1}\tau\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|+\frac{G\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|\operatorname{log}\left(2n\left\|{{\mathcal{W}}}\right\|\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|/\tau\right)}{\sqrt{n}}

Choosing
\tau=\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}\sqrt{n}}
, we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}}\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}}}\left\|{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}^{\prime}\left({\left\langle{w},{x_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)x_{i}}\right\|\leq\frac{2L_{0}\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|\operatorname{log}\left(2n^{3/2}\left\|{{\mathcal{W}}}\right\|L_{1}\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|/L_{0}\right)}{\sqrt{n}}

Finally, substituting the above in Eqn. (
9
) gives us the following in-expectation bound.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}}}\left\|{\nabla F(w;{\mathcal{D}})-\nabla F(w;S)}\right\|\leq\frac{4L_{0}\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|\operatorname{log}\left(2n^{3/2}\left\|{{\mathcal{W}}}\right\|L_{1}\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|/L_{0}\right)}{\sqrt{n}}

For the high-probability bound, let
\psi(S)=\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}}}\left\|{\nabla F(w;{\mathcal{D}})-\nabla F(w;S)}\right\|
and let
w^{*}\in{\mathcal{W}}
achieves the supremum. We can bound the increment between neighbouring datasets
S
and
S^{\prime}
as,

\displaystyle\left|{\psi(S)-\psi(S^{\prime})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\left\|{\nabla F(w^{*};{\mathcal{D}})-\nabla F(w^{*};S)}\right\|-\left\|{\nabla F(w^{*};{\mathcal{D}})-\nabla F(w^{*};S^{\prime})}\right\|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\nabla F(w^{*};S)-\nabla F(w^{*};S^{\prime})}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L_{0}\left\|{{\mathcal{X}}}\right\|}{n}

Finally, applying McDiarmid’s inequality gives the claimed bound.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1
.

The results follow from Theorem

provided we show that the conditions on the base algorithm in the Theorem statement are satisfied. The privacy and accuracy claims follow from Theorem
4
and
5
respectively. We note that even though we are given population stationarity guarantee for the convex case, the same bound for empirical stationarity guarantee simply follows from the re-sampling argument in

8
]
. The only thing left to show is the high-probability bound on the trajectory of the algorithm.

Non-convex setting with Private Spiderboost:

From the update in Algorithm
1
, we have that for any
t

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla_{t}}\right\|\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left\|{\Delta_{i}}\right\|+\left\|{\sum_{i=1}^{t}{g_{t}}}\right\|\leq 2tL_{0}+\left\|{\sum_{i=1}^{t}{g_{t}}}\right\|

where the last inequality follows from the Lipschitzness assumption. Note that
g_{t}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}{\mathbb{I}})
where
\sigma_{t}\leq O\left({\max{(\sigma_{1},\widehat{\sigma}_{2})}}\right)=O\left({\text{poly}(n,d,L_{0},L_{1})}\right)
. Hence
\left\|{\sum_{i=1}^{t}{g_{t}}}\right\|\leq\sqrt{d\operatorname{log}\left(1/\beta^{\prime}\right)}O\left({\text{poly}(n,d,L_{0},L_{1})}\right)
with probability at least
1-\beta^{\prime}
.
Taking a union bound over all
t\in T
gives us
\left\|{w_{t}}\right\|\leq\text{poly}(n,d,L_{0},L_{1},\operatorname{log}\left(\text{poly}(n,d)/\beta\right))
with probability at least
1-\beta
. Substituting
\beta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
yields the guarantee of Theorem
6
.

Since the iterates are restricted to the constraint set, the final output, with probability one, lies in the set of radius

which completes the proof.
