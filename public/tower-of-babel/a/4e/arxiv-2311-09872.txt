A matroidal perspective on the tropical Prym variety
Felix Röhrle, Dmitry Zakharov

Abstract
. We associate a matroid M ⁡ ( Γ ~ / Γ ) M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma) to a harmonic double cover π : Γ ~ → Γ \pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma of metric graphs. The matroid M ⁡ ( Γ ~ / Γ ) M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma) is a geometric interpretation of Zaslavsky’s signed graphic matroid. We show that the principalization Prym p ⁡ ( Γ ~ / Γ ) \Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma) of the tropical Prym variety of the double cover can be reconstructed from M ⁡ ( Γ ~ / Γ ) M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma) , equipped with certain additional decorations. We describe the simplification of the matroid M ⁡ ( Γ ~ / Γ ) M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma) and show that the Prym variety does not change under simplification.

1.
Introduction

The two most-studied objects in algebraic geometry are curves and abelian varieties. The construction of the Jacobian variety
\Jac(C)
of an algebraic curve
C
, in the guise of abelian integrals, goes back to the foundations of algebraic geometry in the 19th century. In moduli, the association
C\mapsto\Jac(C)
is called the
Torelli morphism

\tau_{J}:\mathcal{M}_{g}\to\mathcal{A}_{g}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{g}
and
\mathcal{A}_{g}
are the moduli spaces of smooth genus
g
curves and dimension
g
principally polarized abelian varieties (ppavs), respectively. The Torelli morphism is injective, and an effective description of its image is the still-unsolved
Schottky problem
.

There is another natural way to associate a ppav to an algebraic curve. Given an étale double cover
\widetilde{C}\to C
of smooth curves of genera
2g-1
and
g
, respectively, the
Prym variety

\Prym(\widetilde{C}/C)
is the connected component of the identity of the kernel of the norm map
\Jac(\widetilde{C})\to\Jac(C)
. Prym varieties have been extensively studied since their reintroduction by Mumford in
[
Mum74
]
. The Prym variety carries a principal polarization that is half the polarization induced from
\Jac(\widetilde{C})
, and the assignment
(\widetilde{C}\to C)\mapsto\Prym(\widetilde{C}/C)
defines the
Prym–Torelli map

\tau_{P}:\mathcal{R}_{g}\to\mathcal{\mathcal{}}{A}_{g-1}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{g}
is the moduli space of connected étale double covers of smooth genus
g
curves. Unlike
\tau_{J}
, the morphism
\tau_{P}
is never injective, and it is likewise an open problem to describe its image.

Tropical geometry is a modern branch of mathematics that studies certain combinatorial, piecewise-linear objects, which mimic concepts from algebraic geometry. A
tropical curve
of genus
g
is a metric graph
\Gamma
with
h_{1}(\Gamma)=g
, a
harmonic morphism
of tropical curves is a piecewise-linear map satisfying a certain balancing condition, and a
tropical abelian variety
is a real torus carrying an auxiliary integral structure. By varying lengths and combinatorial types, we obtain moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\trop}
and
\mathcal{A}_{g}^{\trop}
of tropical curves and tropical ppavs, respectively.

We can naturally associate a
tropical Jacobian

\Jac(\Gamma)
to a tropical curve
\Gamma
(see
[
MZ08
]
). The corresponding tropical Torelli map
\tau_{J}^{\trop}:\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\trop}\to\mathcal{A}_{g}^{\trop}
(which is not injective, unlike
\tau_{J}
) was investigated in
[
CV10
]
and
[
BMV11
]
. The key insight in these works is that the tropical Jacobian
\Jac(\Gamma)
is in fact entirely governed by the graphic matroid
M(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
. It is therefore possible to study
\tau_{J}
, and in particular describe its fibers, from a purely matroidal perspective, and similarly to give a matroidal interpretation of the image of
\tau_{J}^{\trop}
. Indeed, the relevance of the graphic matroid in this context was well-known before the advent of tropical geometry, see e.g.
[
Ger82
]
.

The tropical analogue of an étale double cover is a harmonic morphism
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
of metric graphs of degree two. The
tropical Prym variety

\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
was defined in
[
JL18
]
, in analogy with the algebraic setting, and is a polarized tropical abelian variety of dimension
g(\widetilde{\Gamma})-g(\Gamma)
. The tropical Prym variety was further investigated in
[
LU21
]
,
[
LZ22
]
,
[
RZ22
]
, and
[
GZ23
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to develop a matroidal perspective on the tropical Prym variety, with the aim of studying the tropical Prym–Torelli map. We show how to associate to a harmonic double cover
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
a dual pair of matroids
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
and
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
(see Definition
3.3
). These matroids turn out to be
signed (co-)graphic matroids
in the sense of
[
Zas82
]
, and the precise translation of Zaslavsky’s definition into the language of double covers is given in Proposition
3.9
. In Proposition
3.10
we give interpretations of the cryptomorphic descriptions of
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
in terms of the double cover
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
. In Section
4
we prove the main result of our paper and show that the matroid
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
, suitably decorated, recovers all of the information of the Prym variety
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
except for its polarization. We canonically associate a tropical ppav to
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
, the
principalization

\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
, and signed cographic matroid recovers this object:

Theorem A

(Theorem
4.9
)
.

Let
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a harmonic double cover of metric graphs, and let
M=M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
be the signed graphic matroid equipped with index function
\ind:\mathcal{I}(M^{*})\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
(Definition
4.3
), orientation
\overrightarrow{M}
on
M
induced by an orientation of
\pi
(Section
4.4
), and edge length function
\ell:E(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
. Then we can associate a tropical ppav
\Prym(M)
to the matroidal data such that

\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong\Prym(M).

It follows that two double covers with isomorphic decorated matroidal data have isomorphic Prym varieties, see Corollary
4.11
. Example
4.12
shows that distinct double covers may have identical matroidal data and hence isomorphic Prym varieties.

For any matroid
M
, we can construct its
simplification

M_{\mathrm{sim}}
, having no circuits of size 1 or 2. In
[
CV10
]
and
[
BMV11
]
it is shown that simplifying the cographic matroid of
\Gamma
(contracting bridges and parallel edges, with an appropriate redistribution of edge lengths) does not change the Jacobian
\Jac(\Gamma)
. In Section
5
, we describe an analogous simplification procedure for double covers, and show that it does not change the Prym variety.

Theorem B

(Theorem
5.3
)
.

Let
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a double cover of metric graphs and let
\pi_{\mathrm{sim}}:\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{sim}}\to\Gamma_{\mathrm{sim}}
be a simplification of
\pi
(see Definition
5.2
). Then
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{sim}}/\Gamma_{\mathrm{sim}})
.

In contrast to the case of metric graphs, we show by example that double covers with non-isomorphic simple matroids may have isomorphic Prym varieties.

In hindsight, it is clear that the signed graphic matroid
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
and its dual ought to have a close relation to the Prym variety
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. For example, the papers
[
LZ22
]
and
[
GZ23
]
proved a formula for the volume of
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
as a sum over certain subsets of
E(\Gamma)
called
odd genus one decompositions
or
ogods
, and these turn out to be the bases of
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
(see Section
2.8
for details).

A natural question is to investigate the tropical Prym–Torelli morphism
\tau^{\trop}_{P}
that associates to a harmonic double cover
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
its Prym variety
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. There are two related issues with defining
\tau^{\trop}_{P}
. First, the polarization type of
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
may change when the double cover
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is deformed, specifically if the number of connected components of the dilation subgraph changes. Second, the behavior of
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
is not continuous under such deformations. In future work, we plan to give an alternative definition of the tropical Prym variety that solves these issues, and investigate the redefined Prym–Torelli map from a matroidal perspective. The above Theorem shows that some of the non-injectivity of
\tau^{\trop}_{P}
is purely matroidal in nature, and describing the remaining non-injectivity is the subject of ongoing research.

We end by outlining a series of tropical problems inspired by Krichever’s solution to the algebraic Schottky problems, which we now recall. Let
X
be a ppav of dimension
g
with principal polarization
\Theta
, and let
K(X)
be the
Kummer variety
of
X
, the image of
X
under the map
\phi_{2\Theta}:X\to\mathbb{P}^{2^{g}-1}
. Fay’s trisecant identity
[
Fay06
]
states that if
X=\Jac(C)
is a Jacobian, then
K(X)
admits a four-parameter family of trisecant lines, indexed by quadruples of points of
C\subset\Jac(C)
. Gunning
[
Gun82
]
showed that the existence of such a family characterizes Jacobians among all ppavs. Welters
[
Wel83
]
,
[
Wel84
]
strengthened this result by showing that Jacobians are characterized by the existence of a formal one-parameter family of flexes of
K(X)
(limit trisecants when the three points are equal), and conjectured that the existence of a single trisecant of the Kummer variety characterizes Jacobians among ppavs. After a number of progressively stronger results, Krichever proved Welters’s conjecture in
[
Kri06
]
and
[
Kri10
]
. An analogous solution to the Prym–Schottky problem was given by Grushevsky and Krichever in
[
GK10
]
. Beauville and Debarre
[
BD87
]
showed that if
X=\Prym(\widetilde{C}/C)
is a Prym variety, then
K(X)
admits a four-parameter family of quadrisecant planes, and it was shown in
[
GK10
]
that the existence of a symmetric pair of quadrisecants (but not a single quadrisecant) characterizes Prym varieties among all ppavs.

It is natural to formulate an analogous series of questions in the tropical setting. A theory of tropical theta functions has already been developed in
[
MZ08
]
and
[
FRSS18
]
. Fay’s trisecant identity is based on an elementary property of divisorial rank (see Proposition 11.9.1 in
[
BL04
]
). It turns out that the analogous statement for tropical Baker–Norine rank is false, so it is not immediately clear what happens to Fay’s identity under tropicalization. Nevertheless it is natural to ask whether the theta functions of a tropical Jacobian satisfy any identities, whether these identities have a geometric manifestation, whether they characterize tropical Jacobians among all tropical ppavs, and whether this characterization is computationally effective. Finally, all these questions can be asked about the tropical Prym variety as well.

2.
Setup and definitions

We begin by reviewing a number of basic definitions concerning graphs, metric graphs, harmonic morphisms, double covers, and tropical abelian varieties.

2.1.
Graphs, harmonic morphisms, and double covers

A
graph

G
consists of a set of
vertices

V(G)
, a set of
half-edges

H(G)
, a
root map

r:H(G)\to V(G)
, and a fixed-point-free involution
\iota:H(G)\to H(G)
. The
edges
of
G
are the orbits of
\iota
, with each edge consisting of two half-edges, and the set of edges is denoted
E(G)
. We allow graphs with loops and multiedges. The
tangent space
to a vertex
v\in V(G)
is the set
T_{v}G=r^{-1}(v)
of half-edges rooted at
v
. The
genus

g(G)
of a graph
G
is its first Betti number:

g(G)=|E(G)|-|V(G)|+1.

Unless otherwise specified, we consider only finite connected graphs. Given a set of edges
F\subset E(G)
, we denote by
G\backslash F
the (possibly disconnected) graph obtained by removing the edges in
F
, but retaining all vertices, even isolated ones. We denote
G[F]
the minimal subgraph of
G
containing all edges in
F
, in other words
G[F]
is obtained from
G\setminus\big(E(G)\backslash F\big)
by removing all isolated vertices.

A
morphism
of graphs
f:\widetilde{G}\to G
is a pair of maps
f:V(\widetilde{G})\to V(G)
and
f:H(\widetilde{G})\to H(G)
commuting with the root and involution maps, and thus inducing a map
f:E(\widetilde{G})\to E(G)
on the edges. A
harmonic morphism
of graphs is a pair
(f,d_{f})
consisting of a morphism of graphs
f:\widetilde{G}\to G
and a
local degree
function
d_{f}:V(\widetilde{G})\cup H(\widetilde{G})\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, such that
d_{f}(\widetilde{h})=d_{f}(\widetilde{h}^{\prime})
for any edge
\widetilde{e}=\{\widetilde{h},\widetilde{h}^{\prime}\}\in E(\widetilde{G})
and such that the local balancing condition

(1)

d_{f}(\widetilde{v})=\sum_{\widetilde{h}\in T_{\widetilde{v}}\widetilde{G}\cap f^{-1}(h)}d_{f}(\widetilde{h})

holds for each vertex
\widetilde{v}\in V(\widetilde{G})
and each half-edge
h\in T_{f(\widetilde{v})}G
. A harmonic morphism
f:\widetilde{G}\to G
to a connected target graph
G
is surjective and has a well-defined
global degree
given by

\deg f=\sum_{\widetilde{v}\in f^{-1}(v)}d_{f}(\widetilde{v})=\sum_{\widetilde{h}\in f^{-1}(h)}d_{f}(\widetilde{h})

for any
v\in V(G)
or any
h\in H(G)
.

A harmonic morphism
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
of global degree two is called a
double cover
, and we describe its structure in detail. A vertex
v\in V(G)
is called
dilated
if it has a unique preimage that we label
p^{-1}(v)=\{\widetilde{v}\}
, with local degree
d_{p}(\widetilde{v})=2
. Similarly, a half-edge
h\in H(G)
is
dilated
if
p^{-1}(h)=\{\widetilde{h}\}
with
d_{p}(\widetilde{h})=2
, and a dilated edge
e=\{h,h^{\prime}\}
(consisting of dilated half-edges) has a unique edge
\widetilde{e}=\{\widetilde{h},\widetilde{h}^{\prime}\}
lying over it. The root vertex of a dilated half-edge is dilated, so the dilated set forms a subgraph
G_{\mathrm{dil}}\subset G
called the
dilation subgraph
, which is isomorphic to its preimage
p^{-1}(G_{\mathrm{dil}})
. We say that
p
is a
free
double cover if
G_{\mathrm{dil}}=\emptyset
and
dilated
otherwise. We say that
p
is
edge-free
if
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
consists of only vertices. The
dilation index
of a double cover is defined to be

d(\widetilde{G}/G)=\begin{cases}\mbox{number of connected components of }G_{\mathrm{dil}},&\text{if }p\mbox{ is dilated,}\\
1,&\text{if }p\mbox{ is free.}\end{cases}

A vertex
v\in V(G)
having two preimages is called
undilated
, and we label its preimages
p^{-1}(v)=\{\widetilde{v}^{+},\widetilde{v}^{-}\}
, where the local degrees are
d_{p}(\widetilde{v}^{\pm})=1
. Similarly, a half-edge
h\in H(G)
is
undilated
if
p^{-1}(h)=\{\widetilde{h}^{+},\widetilde{h}^{-}\}
with
d_{p}(\widetilde{h}^{\pm})=1
. If the root vertex
v=r(h)
is undilated, we label the preimages of
h
so that
r(\widetilde{h}^{\pm})=\widetilde{v}^{\pm}
(if
v
is dilated then
r(\widetilde{h}^{\pm})=\widetilde{v}
regardless of labeling). An undilated edge
e\in E(G)
is called
free
if both root vertices of
e
(which may be the same) are undilated. The
free subgraph

G_{\mathrm{fr}}\subset G
consists of the undilated vertices and the free edges, and the restriction
p|_{p^{-1}(G_{\mathrm{fr}})}:p^{-1}(G_{\mathrm{fr}})\to G_{\mathrm{fr}}
of
p
to the free subgraph is a degree two covering space in the topological sense.

The structure of the graph
\widetilde{G}
is determined by the involution map on the preimages
\widetilde{h}^{\pm}
of the undilated half-edges, in other words by how the
\widetilde{h}^{\pm}
pair up into edges. If the edge
e=\big\{h,\iota(h)\big\}\in E(G)
is undilated but not free, then we can label the preimages of the half-edges in such a way that the edges of
\widetilde{G}
lying above
e
are
\widetilde{e}^{+}=\big\{\widetilde{h}^{+},\widetilde{\iota(h)}^{+}\big\}
and
\widetilde{e}^{-}=\big\{\widetilde{h}^{-},\widetilde{\iota(h)}^{-}\big\}
, in other words the involution is
\iota(\widetilde{h}^{\pm})=\widetilde{\iota(h)}^{\pm}
. If
e
is free, however, then we record the involution by

\sigma(e)=\begin{cases}+1,&\text{if }\iota(\widetilde{h}^{\pm})=\widetilde{\iota(h)}^{\pm},\\
-1,&\text{if }\iota(\widetilde{h}^{\pm})=\widetilde{\iota(h)}^{\mp}.\end{cases}

Given an orientation on
G
, we always give
\widetilde{G}
the induced orientation. We label the oriented preimages of an oriented edge
e=(h,\iota(h))
as
\widetilde{e}^{+}=(\widetilde{h}^{+},\iota(\widetilde{h}^{+}))
and
\widetilde{e}^{-}=(\widetilde{h}^{-},\iota(\widetilde{h}^{-}))
.

The double cover
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
is thus determined by
G
, the choice of a dilation subgraph
G_{\mathrm{dil}}\subset G
that also determines
G_{\mathrm{fr}}
, and a parity assignment
\sigma:E(G_{\mathrm{fr}})\to\{\pm 1\}
, in other words the structure of a
signed graph
on
G_{\mathrm{fr}}
(in fact, as we shall see in Proposition
3.9
, the set of undilated edges
E(G)\backslash E(G_{\mathrm{dil}})
carries the structure of a signed graph, in the more general sense of Zaslavsky
[
Zas82
]
). Exchanging the preimages of an undilated vertex
v\in V(G_{\mathrm{fr}})
swaps the signs of all free edges rooted at it, so the parity assignment
\sigma
is determined up to
vertex switching equivalence
. In modern terminology, the switching equivalence class of a parity assignment
\sigma
is a well-defined element of the simplicial cohomology group
H^{1}(G_{\mathrm{fr}},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
, which is identified with the set of
\Hom\big(\pi_{1}(G_{\mathrm{fr}}),\,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\big)
of free double covers by covering space theory and the universal coefficient theorem.

For another perspective on double covers note that
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
determines an involution
i:\widetilde{G}\to\widetilde{G}
that changes signs on undilated elements and fixes dilated elements. Conversely, given an involution
i:\widetilde{G}\to\widetilde{G}
, the quotient map
p:\widetilde{G}\to\widetilde{G}/i
naturally has the structure of a double cover, with the dilation subgraph of
G
corresponding to the fixed subgraph of
\widetilde{G}
.

2.2.
Edge contractions

We extensively use the
edge contraction
operation, which is a graph-theoretic deformation that allows us to move through the moduli spaces of graphs and double covers. Let
G
be a graph, let
F\subset E(G)
be a set of edges, and let
H=G[F]
be the subgraph generated by these edges. We denote by
G_{F}
the graph obtained by contracting each connected component of
H
to a separate vertex. An edge contraction induces a natural surjective contraction homomorphism
H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(G_{F},\mathbb{Z})
.

An important property of contraction is that it can be performed for a harmonic morphism. Let
f:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a harmonic morphism of graphs, let
F\subset E(G)
be a set of edges, and let
\widetilde{H}=\widetilde{G}[\widetilde{F}]
be the subgraph of
\widetilde{G}
generated by
\widetilde{F}=f^{-1}(F)
. Consider the map
f_{F}:\widetilde{G}_{\widetilde{F}}\to G_{F}
, where the vertex
\widetilde{v}_{i}\in V(\widetilde{G}_{\widetilde{F}})
corresponding to a connected component
\widetilde{H}_{i}
of
\widetilde{H}
is mapped to the vertex corresponding to the connected component
f(\widetilde{H}_{i})
of
H
. It is elementary to check that
f_{F}
is harmonic, provided that we set the local degree of
f_{F}
at the vertex
\widetilde{v}_{i}
to be the global degree of
f
on the subgraph
\widetilde{H}_{i}
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a double cover, let
F\subset E(G)
be a set of edges, and assume for simplicity that the subgraph
H=G[F]
generated by
F
is connected. If
H
has no dilated vertices, then the restricted cover
p|_{p^{-1}(H)}:p^{-1}(H)\to H
is a free double cover, and
p^{-1}(H)
has two connected components if and only if this double cover is trivial. In this case, the vertex
v\in V(G_{F})
corresponding to
H
is undilated. However, if
p|_{p^{-1}(H)}
is free but nontrivial, or if
H
has at least one dilated vertex, then the vertex
v
is dilated. In particular, the contraction of a free double cover need not be free.

Example 2.2
.

We will frequently make use of the following construction, visualized in Figure
1
. Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a dilated double cover. We construct the edge-free double cover
p_{\mathrm{ef}}:\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{ef}}\to G_{\mathrm{ef}}
by contracting all dilated edges, in other words by contracting each connected component
G_{i}
of
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
to a dilated vertex
v_{i}
. We then resolve
p_{\mathrm{ef}}
into a free double cover
p_{\mathrm{free}}:\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{free}}\to G_{\mathrm{free}}
by adding a loop
e_{i}
at each dilated vertex
v_{i}
of
G_{\mathrm{ef}}
, and replacing the preimage vertex
\widetilde{v}_{i}
with two vertices
\widetilde{v}^{\pm}_{i}
joined by two parallel edges
{\widetilde{e}}^{\pm}_{i}
mapping to the loop
e_{i}
. The cover
p_{\mathrm{free}}
is not defined canonically (unlike
p_{\mathrm{ef}}
), since we must choose how to reattach the edges of
\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{ef}}
to the new vertices
\widetilde{v}^{\pm}_{i}
. The cover
p_{\mathrm{ef}}
is obtained from
p_{\mathrm{free}}
by contracting the new loops
e_{i}
.

2.3.
Metric graphs

Let
G
be a graph and let
\ell:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be an
edge length
assignment. The pair
(G,\ell)
defines a
metric graph

\Gamma
, which is the topological space obtained by viewing each edge
e\in E(G)
as a closed interval of length
\ell(e)
and attaching the intervals according to the structure of
G
. We endow
\Gamma
with the shortest-path metric. The pair
(G,\ell)
is called a
model
for
\Gamma
, and the genus of
\Gamma
is the genus of any underlying model. We will often abuse notation and refer to the edges and vertices of a metric graph
\Gamma
, with respect to an implied choice of model.

A
harmonic morphism of metric graphs

\phi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is a continuous, piecewise-linear map whose slopes
d_{f}(\widetilde{e})
along the edges
\widetilde{e}\in E(\widetilde{G})
are positive integers satisfying the slope-balancing condition (
1
). In other words, there exist models
(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{\ell})
and
(G,\ell)
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
and
\Gamma
, respectively, and a harmonic morphism of graphs
f:\widetilde{G}\to G
modeling
\phi
, such that
d_{\phi}(\widetilde{e})=d_{f}(\widetilde{e})
for all
\widetilde{e}\in E(\widetilde{\Gamma})
. The continuity of
\phi
imposes the restriction

\ell\big(\phi(\widetilde{e})\big)=d_{\phi}(\widetilde{e})\widetilde{\ell}(\widetilde{e})

for all edges
\widetilde{e}\in E(\widetilde{G})
. If
\phi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is a double cover of metric graphs, then
\phi
is a factor two dilation along a dilated edge and an isometry along an undilated edge, explaining the terminology.

2.4.
Divisor theory on metric graphs

For the reader’s convenience, we briefly recall the divisor theory on a metric graph
\Gamma
. The divisor group
\Div(\Gamma)
is the free abelian group on the points of
\Gamma
. A rational function on
\Gamma
is a real-valued piecewise-linear function with integer slopes, and the group of rational functions is denoted
\Rat(\Gamma)
. The divisor of a rational function is

\operatorname{div}:\Rat(\Gamma)\longrightarrow\Div(\Gamma),\qquad f\longmapsto\sum_{x\in\Gamma}(\mbox{sum of incoming slopes at }x)\cdot x

The Picard group
\Pic(\Gamma)
is the cokernel of
\operatorname{div}
. The degree map

\deg:\Div(\Gamma)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z},\qquad\sum_{x\in\Gamma}a_{x}\cdot x\longmapsto\sum_{x\in\Gamma}a_{x}

descends to
\Pic(\Gamma)
, and we denote
\Pic_{d}(\Gamma)
the set of equivalence classes of degree
d
divisors.

2.5.
Tropical abelian varieties

A
real torus with integral structure

\Sigma
of dimension
g
, or an
integral torus
for short, is defined by a triple
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},[\cdot,\cdot])
, where
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
are free abelian groups of rank
g
and
[\cdot,\cdot]:\Lambda\times\Lambda^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
is a nondegenerate pairing. The torus itself is
\Sigma=\Hom(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})/\Lambda^{\prime}
, where
\Lambda^{\prime}
is embedded in
\Hom(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})
via the assignment
\lambda^{\prime}\mapsto[\cdot,\lambda^{\prime}]
. The integral lattice
\Hom(\Lambda,\mathbb{Z})
in the universal cover
\Hom(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})
of
\Sigma
may be viewed as a tropical analogue of a complex structure on a real torus.

Let
\Sigma_{1}=(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{1}^{\prime},[\cdot,\cdot]_{1})
and
\Sigma_{2}=(\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{2}^{\prime},[\cdot,\cdot]_{2})
be integral tori, and let
f^{\#}:\Lambda_{2}\to\Lambda_{1}
and
f_{\#}:\Lambda_{1}^{\prime}\to\Lambda_{2}^{\prime}
be homomorphisms satisfying the relation

\big[f^{\#}(\lambda_{2}),\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\big]_{1}=\big[\lambda_{2},f_{\#}(\lambda_{1}^{\prime})\big]_{2}

for all
\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{1}^{\prime}
. The Hom-dual
\Hom(\Lambda_{1},\mathbb{R})\to\Hom(\Lambda_{2},\mathbb{R})
of
f^{\#}
restricts to
f_{\#}
on
\Lambda_{1}^{\prime}
, hence the pair
f=(f^{\#},f_{\#})
defines a
homomorphism of integral tori

f:\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
. The maps
f^{\#}
and
f_{\#}
have the same rank, which is denoted
\rk f
and which is equal to the dimension of
f(\Sigma_{1})
.

Let
f:\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
be a homomorphism of integral tori. The group-theoretic kernel
\Ker f
is the direct sum of a finite abelian group and an integral torus
(\Ker f)_{0}
, which we call the
kernel torus
of
f
. It is defined by the triple

(\Ker f)_{0}=\big((\Coker f^{\#})^{\mathrm{tf}},\Ker f_{\#},[\cdot,\cdot]_{K}\big),

where for an abelian group
A
we denote
A^{\mathrm{tf}}
the quotient by its torsion subgroup, and
[\cdot,\cdot]_{K}
is the pairing
(\Coker f^{\#})^{\mathrm{tf}}\times\Ker f_{\#}\to\mathbb{R}
induced by
[\cdot,\cdot]_{1}
. The pair
i=(i^{\#},i_{\#})
, where
i_{\#}:\Ker f_{\#}\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{1}^{\prime}
is the natural inclusion and
i^{\#}:\Lambda_{1}\twoheadrightarrow(\Coker f^{\#})^{\mathrm{tf}}
is the quotient map, defines the natural injective homomorphism of real tori
i:(\Ker f)_{0}\to\Sigma_{1}
.

A
polarization
on an integral torus
\Sigma=(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},[\cdot,\cdot])
is a group homomorphism
\xi:\Lambda^{\prime}\to\Lambda
with the property that the induced bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)=[\xi(\cdot),\cdot]:\Lambda^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}\times\Lambda^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R}
is symmetric and positive definite. A polarization is necessarily injective, and its
type
is determined by the invariant factors of its Smith normal form. A polarization is
principal
if it is bijective, in other words if its type is
(1,\ldots,1)
. An integral torus with a principal polarization is called a
principally polarized tropical abelian variety
, or
pptav
for short. Given an integral torus
\Sigma=(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},[\cdot,\cdot])
with a polarization
\xi:\Lambda^{\prime}\to\Lambda
, we define the
principalization
to be the integral torus
\Sigma_{p}=(\Im\xi,\Lambda^{\prime},[\cdot,\cdot])
with the principal polarization
\xi:\Lambda^{\prime}\to\Im\xi\cong\Lambda^{\prime}
. The injection
j:\Im\xi\to\Lambda
and the identity map
\Id:\Lambda^{\prime}\to\Lambda^{\prime}
define a homomorphism
(j,\Id):\Sigma\to\Sigma_{p}
, which is a
dilation
in the sense of Definition 4.6 of
[
RZ22
]
, so in particular it is a bijection on the underlying tori.

1

In Lemma 4.11 of
[
RZ22
]
we introduced a different notion of principalization
\Sigma^{\mathrm{pp}}
which naturally comes with a dilation
\Sigma^{\mathrm{pp}}\to\Sigma

\Sigma^{\mathrm{pp}}\to\Sigma_{p}

.

Let
f:\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
be a homomorphism of integral tori, and let
\xi_{2}
be a polarization on
\Sigma_{2}
. If
f
is
finite
, in other words if
\rk f=\dim\Sigma_{1}
, then the homomorphism
\xi_{1}=f^{\#}\circ\xi_{2}\circ f_{\#}
determines an
induced polarization
on
\Sigma_{1}
. As in the algebraic setting, a polarization induced from a principal polarization is not necessarily principal; we shall see that the Prym variety of a double cover is an example of this.

Let
\Sigma=(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},[\cdot,\cdot])
be an integral torus of dimension
n
with polarization
\xi
. The bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)=[\xi(\cdot),\cdot]
on
\Lambda^{\prime}
extends to an inner product on the vector space
\Hom(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})\supset\Lambda^{\prime}
, which is the universal cover of
\Sigma=\Hom(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})/\Lambda^{\prime}
, and hence defines a translation-invariant Riemannian metric on
\Sigma
. The
volume of
\Sigma
is the volume of a fundamental parallelotope and is given by the Grammian determinant

(2)

\Vol^{2}(\Sigma)=\det\big((\lambda_{i}^{\prime},\lambda_{j}^{\prime})\big)_{i,j},

where
\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\lambda_{n}^{\prime}
is any basis of
\Lambda^{\prime}
.

2.6.
The tropical Jacobian and the Abel–Jacobi map

Let
\Gamma
be a metric graph, and choose an oriented model
(G,\ell)
. The simplicial chain group
C_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
is the free abelian group on
E(G)
and contains the simplicial homology group
H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
which is of rank
g(\Gamma)
. The chain groups naturally fit into a directed system with respect to refinements of models, and the images of the homology groups agree. We denote the direct limits
C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
and
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
, and refer the reader to
[
BF11
]
for details.

It is conceptually convenient to introduce the group
\Omega^{1}_{\Gamma}
of
tropical 1-forms
on
\Gamma
. This group is canonically isomorphic to
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
, and a cycle
\gamma=\sum\gamma(e)\cdot e\in H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
is written
\sum\gamma(e)de
when it is viewed as a
1
-form. There is a natural
integration pairing

(3)

[\cdot,\cdot]:\Omega^{1}_{\Gamma}\times C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\qquad\left[\sum\gamma(e)de,\sum\delta(e)\cdot e\right]=\int_{\gamma}\omega=\sum\gamma(e)\delta(e)\ell(e),

which restricts to a perfect pairing
\Omega^{1}_{\Gamma}\times H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{R}
. The
Jacobian variety
of
\Gamma
is the
g(\Gamma)
-dimensional pptav

\Jac(\Gamma)=\big(\Omega^{1}_{\Gamma},\allowbreak\,H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}),\allowbreak\,[\cdot,\cdot]\big)=\Hom(\Omega^{1}_{\Gamma},\mathbb{R})\,/\,H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}),

where the principal polarization is the trivial isomorphism
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})=\Omega^{1}_{\Gamma}
.

Remark 2.3
.

We note that the integration pairing (
3
), and hence the induced inner product on the universal cover
\Hom(\Omega^{1}_{\Gamma},\mathbb{R})
of
\Jac(\Gamma)
, has a physical peculiarity: it is linear, rather than quadratic, in the edge lengths
\ell(e)
. Hence the units that are used to measure edge lengths on
\Gamma
become units of area from the point of view of the Riemannian geometry of
\Jac(\Gamma)
.

Let
q\in\Gamma
be a base point, and for
p\in\Gamma
let
\gamma_{p}\in C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
denote any path from
q
to
p
. The
Abel–Jacobi map

\phi_{q}:\Gamma\to\Jac(\Gamma)
relative to
q
is given by

p\longmapsto\left(\omega\longmapsto\int_{\gamma_{p}}\omega\right).

The map
\phi_{q}
extends to symmetric powers of
\Gamma
and thus to divisors. The map respects linear equivalence, and hence induces a map
\Pic_{0}(\Gamma)\to\Jac(\Gamma)
that does not depend on the choice of
q
. The tropical Abel–Jacobi theorem (see Theorem 6.3 in
[
MZ08
]
) states that this map is an isomorphism. Under this identification, the Abel–Jacobi map sends
p
to the divisor class
p-q
.

2.7.
The tropical Prym and the Abel–Prym map

Let
\phi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a harmonic morphism of metric graphs. We consider the natural pushforward and pullback homomorphisms

\displaystyle\phi^{*}=\Nm^{\#}:\Omega^{1}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}},

\displaystyle\phi_{*}=\Nm_{\#}:H_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}),

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E(\Gamma)}\gamma(e)de

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{e\in E(\Gamma)}\gamma(e)\sum_{\widetilde{e}\in\phi^{-1}(e)}d_{\phi}(\widetilde{e})\,d\widetilde{e},

\displaystyle\sum_{\widetilde{e}\in E(\widetilde{\Gamma})}\gamma(\widetilde{e})\cdot\widetilde{e}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{\widetilde{e}\in E(\widetilde{\Gamma})}\gamma(\widetilde{e})\cdot\phi(\widetilde{e}).

The pair
\Nm=(\Nm^{\#},\Nm_{\#})=(\phi^{*},\phi_{*})
defines the surjective
norm homomorphism

\Nm:\Jac(\widetilde{\Gamma})\to\Jac(\Gamma)
of pptavs, which corresponds, under the identification of
\Jac
with
\Pic_{0}
, to the direct image homomorphism

\Nm:\Pic_{0}(\widetilde{\Gamma})\longrightarrow\Pic_{0}(\Gamma),\qquad\sum_{\widetilde{x}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}}a_{\widetilde{x}}\,\widetilde{x}\longmapsto\sum_{\widetilde{x}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}}a_{\widetilde{x}}\,\phi(\widetilde{x}).

We now consider a double cover
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
of metric graphs of genera
\widetilde{g}=g(\widetilde{\Gamma})
and
g=g(\Gamma)
, respectively. Let

\pi_{*}:H_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}),\qquad\pi^{*}:\Omega^{1}_{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}

denote the pushforward and pullback homomorphisms, denote

K=(\Coker\pi^{*})^{\mathrm{tf}},\qquad K^{\prime}=\Ker\pi_{*},

and denote
[\cdot,\cdot]_{K}:K\times K^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
the pairing induced by the integration pairing on
\Jac(\widetilde{\Gamma})
.

Definition 2.4
.

The
tropical Prym variety

\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
of the double cover
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is the kernel torus of the norm homomorphism:

\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)=(\Ker\Nm)_{0}=\left(K,K^{\prime},[\cdot,\cdot]_{K}\right)=\frac{\Hom(\Coker\pi^{*},\mathbb{R})}{\Ker\pi_{*}}.

The tropical Prym variety
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
has dimension
h=\widetilde{g}-g
and is the connected component of the identity of the kernel
\Ker\Nm
of the norm homomorphism
\Nm:\Jac(\widetilde{\Gamma})\to\Jac(\Gamma)
. The structure of the kernel is described by Proposition 6.1 in
[
JL18
]
. Let
i:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\widetilde{\Gamma}
denote the involution associated to
\pi
, then any element in
\Ker\Nm
has a representative (viewed as an element of
\Pic_{0}(\widetilde{\Gamma})
) of the form
E-i(E)
, where
E
is an effective divisor
E
on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. If
\pi
is a free double cover, then the parity of
\deg E
does not depend on the choice of
E
, and
\Ker\Nm
has two connected components distinguished by the parity. If
\pi
is dilated, however, then
\Ker\Nm
is connected.

The tropical Prym variety
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
has a polarization induced from the principal polarization on
\Jac(\widetilde{\Gamma})
. Its type was computed in Theorem 1.5.7 of
[
LU21
]
and Proposition 4.21 of
[
RZ22
]
and is
(1,\ldots,1,2,\ldots,2)
with
d-1
many
1
’s, where
d
is the dilation index of the double cover. For the most part of this article we will work with the principalization of the Prym variety, which we denote by
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
and refer to as
Prym variety
as well. For later reference, we spell out the defining data of
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
and introduce the following notation. Any element of
\Ker\pi_{*}
is contained in the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of elements
\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-}
for
e
ranging over the undilated edges of
\Gamma
. Hence we can view elements
\gamma\in\Ker\pi_{*}
as
chains
on
\Gamma
and write

(4)

\gamma=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\gamma(e)\cdot(\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-})=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\gamma(e)\cdot e\in C_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}).

Then
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
is the pptav defined by the triple
(\Ker\pi_{*},\Ker\pi_{*},[\cdot,\cdot])
, where the pairing is induced from
H_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\mathbb{Z})
and is given by

(5)

[\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}]=\sum_{\widetilde{e}\in E(\widetilde{\Gamma})}\gamma(\widetilde{e})\gamma^{\prime}(\widetilde{e})\ell(\widetilde{e})=\sum_{e\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(\Gamma)}2\gamma(e)\gamma^{\prime}(e)\ell(e)

for all
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Ker\pi_{*}
, where the second equality uses
\ell(\widetilde{e}^{+})=\ell(\widetilde{e}^{-})=\ell(e)
.

We also define the tropical Abel–Prym map. Let
q\in\widetilde{\Gamma}
be a base point, and for
p\in\widetilde{\Gamma}
let
\gamma_{p}\in C_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\mathbb{Z})
denote any path from
q
to
p
. Let
i_{*}\gamma_{p}
denote the image of this path under the involution. The
Abel–Prym map

\psi_{q}:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
is given by

p\longmapsto\left(\omega\longmapsto\int_{\gamma_{p}}\omega-\int_{i_{*}\gamma_{p}}\omega\right).

We note that
\psi_{q}=p-i(p)-(q-i(q))
when viewed as an element of
\Pic_{0}(\widetilde{\Gamma})
, hence lies in the connected component of the identity of
\Ker\Nm
(which is not true of
p-i(p)
if the double cover is free).

Volumes of the Jacobian and the Prym

One of the motivations for this paper are the formulas for the volume of the tropical Jacobian
\Jac(\Gamma)
found in

and the tropical Prym variety
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
found in

and

. In both cases, the square of the volume is a polynomial in the lengths of the edges of

. We shall see in the next section that the monomials are in fact indexed by the bases of an appropriate matroid on the set of edges of

We recall the results of
[

]
,
[

]
and
[

Theorem 2.5

(Theorem 5.2 of

Let

. The volume of the Jacobian of

\Vol^{2}\big(\Jac(\Gamma)\big)=\sum_{F\subset E(G)}\prod_{e\in F}\ell(e),

where the sum is taken over all

such that

is a tree.

The dimensional peculiarity noted in Remark

makes its appearance: the square of the volume of

(which is a Riemannian manifold of dimension

, not the expected

. The volume of the tropical Prym variety of a free and dilated double cover was computed in
[
LZ22
]
and

, respectively.

Theorem 2.6

(Theorem 3.4 of
[
LZ22
]
and Theorem 3.3 of

.

Let

3.
The matroid of a double cover

The papers
[
CV10
]
and
[
BMV11
]
studied the tropical Torelli map that associates to a metric graph
\Gamma
its Jacobian
\Jac(\Gamma)
, and showed that its fibers are most conveniently understood in terms of a combinatorial object, the
graphic matroid

M(\Gamma)
(we note that many of these results earlier appeared in German and without the use of the tropical language in
[
Ger82
]
). In this section, we describe an analogous object, the
signed graphic matroid

M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
of a double cover
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
, which plays a role in describing the fibers of the tropical Prym–Torelli map
(\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma)\mapsto\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
in Section
5
. This matroid was originally defined by Zaslavsky
[
Zas82
]
in the framework of signed graphs, and we translate his definition into the language of double covers. We begin with the basic definitions.

3.1.
Matroids and graph theory

A
matroid

M
consists of a finite set
E(M)
, called the
ground set
of
M
, and a family of subsets
\mathcal{I}(M)
of
E(M)
, called the
independent sets
, satisfying the following axioms:

(1)

\emptyset\in\mathcal{I}(M)
.

(2)

If
A\subset B
and
B\in\mathcal{I}(M)
, then
A\in\mathcal{I}(M)
.

(3)

If
A,B\in\mathcal{I}(M)
and
A
has more elements than
B
, then there exists
x\in A\backslash B
such that
B\cup\{x\}\in\mathcal{I}(M)
.

The maximal elements of
\mathcal{I}(M)
are the
bases
of the matroid, and the set of bases is denoted
\mathcal{B}(M)
. All bases have the same cardinality, called the
rank
of
M
. A subset of
E(M)
that is not independent is called
dependent
, and the set of dependent sets is denoted
\mathcal{D}(M)
. A minimal dependent set is a
circuit
, and the set of circuits is denoted
\mathcal{C}(M)
. Each of the sets
\mathcal{I}(M)
,
\mathcal{B}(M)
,
\mathcal{D}(M)
, and
\mathcal{C}(M)
determines the others and satisfies a set of properties that can be used to give an equivalent definition of a matroid (see Chapter 1 in
[
Oxl11
]
).

Starting with a matroid
M
, we construct several related matroids. The
dual matroid

M^{*}
has the same ground set
E(M)
, and
B^{*}\subset E(M)=E(M^{*})
is a basis of
M^{*}
if and only if
E(M)\backslash B^{*}
is a basis of
M
. Given a subset
X\subset E(M)
, the
restriction
of
M
to
X
is the matroid
M|_{X}
with ground set
X
and independent set
\mathcal{I}(M|_{X})=\{I\in\mathcal{I}(M):I\subset X\}
. The
deletion
of
X
from
M
is the restriction
M\backslash X=M|_{E(M)-X}
to the complement. Finally, the
contraction
of
M
along
X
is the matroid
M/X=(M^{*}\backslash X)^{*}
.

An
n
-circuit
of a matroid
M
is a circuit with
n
elements (a
1
-circuit is sometimes called a
loop
, but we avoid this terminology). Two elements
e,f\in E(M)
are
parallel
if
\{e,f\}
is a
2
-circuit, and a
parallel class
of
M
is a maximal subset of
F\subset E(M)
containing no
1
-circuits and such that any
2
-element subset is a
2
-circuit. A parallel class is
trivial
if it consists of a single element, and a matroid is called
simple
if it has no non-trivial parallel classes. Given a matroid
M
, the
simplification
of
M
is the simple matroid
M_{\mathrm{sim}}
constructed by deleting all
1
-circuits, and deleting all but one element from each parallel class. The simplification procedure is non-canonical, since it involves choosing a representative from each parallel class, but any two simplifications are isomorphic as matroids.

Example 3.1
.

Let
G
be a connected graph of genus
g
. The
graphic matroid

M(G)
has ground set
E(G)
, and a subset
F\subset E(G)
is independent if the subgraph
G[F]
generated by
F
is a forest (equivalently, if the homology group
H_{1}\big(G[F],\mathbb{Z}\big)
is trivial). The bases of
M(G)
are the spanning trees of
G
, the dependent sets are subgraphs having a nontrivial cycle, and the circuits are subgraphs consisting of a simple cycle. Given a set of edges
F\subset E(G)
, the deletion
M(G)\backslash F
and contraction
M(G)/F
are the graphic matroids of the graphs
G\backslash F
and
G_{F}
, respectively. A
1
-circuit of
M(G)
is a loop of
G
, and a parallel class is the set of edges between two vertices. The matroid
M(G)
is simple if and only if the graph
G
is simple, and
M(G)_{\mathrm{sim}}
is the graphic matroid of the simplification of the graph
G
, obtained by deleting all loops, and deleting all but one edge in each set of multiedges between any pair of vertices.

The dual matroid
M^{*}(G)
is called the
cographic
or
bond matroid
. The independent sets of
M^{*}(G)
are the sets of edges
F\subset E(G)
whose removal does not disconnect
G
(equivalently, such that the reduced homology group
\widetilde{H}_{0}(G\backslash F,\mathbb{Z})
is trivial). The bases of
M^{*}(G)
are the complements of the spanning trees of
G
, and the rank of
M^{*}(G)
is equal to
g
. A
1
-circuit of
M^{*}(G)
is a bridge edge. A parallel class consists of a set of
n
edges whose removal disconnects
G
into
n
connected components that are cyclically linked in
G
by the removed edges. The simplification of
M^{*}(G)
is obtained by contracting the bridge edges and all but one edge from each parallel class (since
M^{*}(G)
is the dual, matroid-theoretic deletion is graph-theoretic contraction).

3.2.
The matroid of a double cover

In the seminal paper
[
Zas82
]
, Zaslavsky introduces the
signed graphic matroid

M(G,\sigma)
on the edge set
E(G)
of a graph
G
equipped with an edge parity assignment
\sigma:E(G)\to\{\pm 1\}
. As described in Section
2.1
, the pair
(G,\sigma)
determines a free double cover
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
, and Zaslavsky shows that the matroid
M(G,\sigma)
is switching-equivalent, in other words depends only on the cover
p
(Corollary 5.4 in
[
Zas82
]
). He then considers the contraction of the matroid
M(G,\sigma)
along a subset
F\subset E(G)
. Since the contracted double cover
p_{F}:\widetilde{G}_{\widetilde{F}}\to G_{F}
may no longer be free, in order to stay within the framework of signed graphs, Zaslavsky is forced to introduce a more complicated category of graphs, having
half-arcs
and
free loops
in addition to ordinary edges (see Proposition
3.9
for details).

In this section, we give an elementary geometric description of the signed graphic matroid and its dual in terms of double covers, which works equally well in the free and dilated cases. In addition, the description via double covers allows us to define an auxiliary function on the independent sets called the
index
(see Definition
4.3
), which is crucial for reconstructing the Prym variety and which cannot be seen from Zaslavsky’s approach. To motivate our definition, we recall that a set of edges of a graph
G
is independent for the cographic matroid
M^{*}(G)
if removing it does not disconnect
G
. We give an analogous definition for double covers.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a double cover of graphs, with
G
possibly disconnected. We say that
p
is
relatively connected
if the preimage of each connected component of
G
is connected in
\widetilde{G}
.

A double cover
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
of a connected graph
G
is relatively connected if and only if it is not the trivial free double cover. If
e\in E(G)
is a dilated edge, then
G\backslash\{e\}
may or may not be connected, but each connected component has a dilated vertex, and hence the double cover obtained from
p
by deleting
e
from
G
and its preimage from
\widetilde{G}
is still relatively connected. Removing an undilated edge, however, may relatively disconnect
p
. Hence we make the following definition.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a nontrivial double cover of a connected graph
G
. We define the matroid
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
of
p
as follows:

(1)

The ground set of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
is
E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)=E(G)\backslash E(G_{\mathrm{dil}})
, the set of undilated edges of
G
.

(2)

A set of undilated edges
F\subset E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)
is independent for the matroid
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
if the double cover
p|_{\widetilde{G}\backslash p^{-1}(F)}:\widetilde{G}\backslash p^{-1}(F)\to G\backslash F
obtained by removing these edges from
G
and their preimages from
\widetilde{G}
is relatively connected. Equivalently,
F
is independent if the double cover
p|_{\widetilde{G}\backslash p^{-1}(F)}
induces an injective map on the reduced homology groups
\widetilde{H}_{0}\big(\widetilde{G}\backslash p^{-1}(F),\mathbb{Z}\big)\to\widetilde{H}_{0}(G\backslash F,\mathbb{Z})
.

We show in Proposition
3.9
that
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
is a matroid, by identifying it with the signed graphic matroid of Zaslavsky. We first investigate when it is possible to remove a single undilated edge without relatively disconnecting the double cover. To simplify notation, given a double cover
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
and an undilated edge
e\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)
, we denote
G^{e}=G\backslash\{e\}
,
\widetilde{G}^{e}=\widetilde{G}\backslash p^{-1}(e)
, and
p^{e}:\widetilde{G}^{e}\to G^{e}
the restriction of
p
. We first determine the relatively connected double covers that do not admit the removal of an edge.

Definition 3.4
.

An
elementary double cover

p:\widetilde{G}\to G
is one of the following two types (see Figure
2
):

(1)

p
is dilated,
G
and
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
are connected, and
g(G_{\mathrm{dil}})=g(G)
.

(2)

p
is free,
G
is connected of genus one, and
p
is not the trivial free double cover.

Figure 2
.
Elementary double covers of the first and second types.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be an elementary double cover. Then
p
is relatively connected, but for any undilated edge
e\in E(G)
the double cover
p^{e}:\widetilde{G}^{e}\to G^{e}
is not relatively connected.

Proof.

If
p
is of the first type, then
G
consists of the dilation subgraph
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
together with a number of undilated trees attached to it. Hence for any undilated edge
e
, the graph
G^{e}
has two connected components, one of which is a tree with no dilation. Hence
p^{e}
is not relatively connected since a tree has no nontrivial free double cover.

Similarly, if
p
is free and
g(G)=1
, then
G^{e}
is either a tree or a disjoint union of a genus one graph and a tree, and in both cases
p^{e}
is not relatively connected.
∎

The next lemma shows that no other double covers have this property.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a double cover of connected graphs of any of the following three types:

(1)

p
is dilated and
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
is not connected.

(2)

p
is dilated,
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
is connected, and
g(G)>g(G_{\mathrm{dil}})
.

(3)

p
is free and
g(G)\geq 2
.

There exists an undilated edge
e\in E(G)
such that
p^{e}:\widetilde{G}^{e}\to G^{e}
is relatively connected.

Proof.

If
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
has two distinct connected components, choose a path between them consisting of undilated edges, and let
e
be any edge on this path. The graph
G^{e}
may be disconnected (if
e
is a bridge), but each of its connected components has dilation, hence
p^{e}
is relatively connected.

If
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
is connected but
g(G)>g(G_{\mathrm{dil}})
, then the graph obtained by contracting
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
to a single vertex has genus
g(G)-g(G_{\mathrm{dil}})>0
and hence a nontrivial cycle. Let
e
be an (undilated) edge on this cycle. Removing
e
from the original graph
G
does not disconnect it, hence
p^{e}
is relatively connected since
G^{e}
has dilation.

Finally, if
p
is free, then there is a simple cycle
C
on
G
such that the restriction of
p
to
C
is not trivial. If
g(G)\geq 2
, we can also find a non-bridge edge
e\in G
that does not lie on
C
. Removing
e
does not disconnect
G
, and the cover
p^{e}
remains relatively connected.
∎

We now prove that
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
is a matroid by showing that it is the dual of the signed graphic matroid of Zaslavsky. For the convenience of the reader, we give the bases of the matroid
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
as a separate definition.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a nontrivial double cover of a connected graph
G
, let
F\subset E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)
be a set of undilated edges, let
G\backslash F=G_{1}\cup\cdots\cup G_{k}
be the decomposition into connected components, and let
p_{i}:p^{-1}(G_{i})\to G_{i}
be the restrictions of
p
. The set
F
is called an
odd genus one decomposition
, or
ogod
for short, if each
p_{i}
is an elementary double cover.

Remark 3.8
.

This terminology was introduced in
[
LZ22
]
in the context of free double covers. If
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
is free, then each
p_{i}:p^{-1}(G_{i})\to G_{i}
is a non-trivial double cover of a genus one graph by a genus one graph, corresponding to the nontrivial (i.e.
odd
) element of
H^{1}(G_{i},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. If
p
is edge-free, then each
G_{i}
may also be viewed as a genus one graph by assigning each dilated vertex an intrinsic genus. Hence the acronym
“ogod”
. The terminology breaks down for double covers with edge dilation, however, we generally do not consider such covers (see Lemma
4.1
).

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a nontrivial double cover of a connected graph
G
. Then
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
is a matroid of rank
g(\widetilde{G})-g(G)
on the set
E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)
of undilated edges of
G
, whose bases are the ogods of
p
.

Proof.

It follows immediately from Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
that a subset
F\subset E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)
is a basis, in other words a maximal independent set of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
, if and only if each
p_{i}
is an elementary double cover. To show that
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
is a matroid, we show that its bases are the complements of the bases of the matroid
M(\Sigma)
of a signed graph
\Sigma
defined by Zaslavsky in
[
Zas82
]
. We recall the definitions. Consider a tuple
\Sigma=(V,E,A,F,\sigma)
of the following kind:

(1)

V
and
E
are respectively the vertex and edge sets of a graph
G^{\prime}
, which is not necessarily connected. The set of connected components of
G^{\prime}
is denoted
\pi(\Sigma)
.

(2)

A
is a set of
half-arcs
, equipped with a root map
r:A\to V(G^{\prime})
. An element
e\in A
is an edge having one end rooted on the graph
G^{\prime}
and one loose end. When speaking of connected components of
G^{\prime}
, we include the half-arcs rooted on it.

(3)

F
is a set of
free loops
, which are edges with no root vertices.

(4)

\sigma:E\cup F\to\{\pm 1\}
is a partial parity assignment on the edges, taking values
\sigma(f)=+1
on all
f\in F
.

The set of
arcs
of
\Sigma
is
E\cup A\cup F
and it is the ground set of the matroid
M(\Sigma)
. A closed edge path
e_{1}\cdots e_{n}
in
G^{\prime}
is called
balanced
if
\sigma(e_{1})\cdots\sigma(e_{n})=1
. A set of arcs is called
balanced
if it contains no half-arcs and if every closed path in it is balanced. The set of connected components of
G^{\prime}
whose arc set is balanced is denoted
\pi_{b}(\Sigma)
.

The bases of the matroid
M(\Sigma)
are defined by Theorem 5.1 in
[
Zas82
]
(we note that part (g) of Theorem 5.1, which specifically defines bases, is inaccurately stated, and was subsequently corrected in
[
Zas83
]
). A set
S\subset E\cup A\cup F
is a basis if the following conditions hold:

(1)

For each balanced component
B\in\pi_{b}(\Sigma)
, the edges of
S
in
B
form a spanning tree for
B
.

(2)

Each unbalanced connected component of the subgraph induced by
S
is either a tree together with a unique half-arc, or a genus one graph with unbalanced cycle and no half-arcs.

(3)

S
contains no free loops.

Now let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a double cover. We define the associated tuple
\Sigma=(V,E,A,F,\sigma)
as follows:

(1)

The graph
G^{\prime}
is the free subgraph
G_{\mathrm{fr}}\subset G
, and
\sigma:E\to\{\pm 1\}
is the parity assignmend defining the free double cover
p|_{p^{-1}(G_{\mathrm{fr}})}:p^{-1}(G_{\mathrm{fr}})\to G_{\mathrm{fr}}
with respect to some choice of vertex labeling (see Section
2.1
for details).

(2)

The half-arcs
A
are the undilated edges having one free root vertex on
G_{\mathrm{fr}}
and one dilated root vertex (which we discard).

(3)

The free loops
F
are the undilated edges both of whose root vertices are dilated.

In other words, we delete the dilated vertices and edges from
G
, and leave dangling any undilated edges with missing root vertices, so that
E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)=E\cup A\cup F
according to whether an undilated edge loses none, one, or both root vertices.

Suppose further that
G
is connected. If
p
is free and nontrivial, then
G^{\prime}=G
is the unique (unbalanced) connected component, and there are no half-arcs or free loops. If
p
is dilated, then each connected component of
G^{\prime}
has at least one half-arc (an undilated edge with one dilated and one undilated root vertex) or is a free loop. We see that
\pi_{b}(\Sigma)
is empty in both cases.

Now let
F\subset E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)
be a basis of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
, let
G\backslash F=G_{1}\cup\cdots G_{k}
be the decomposition into connected components, and let
p_{i}:p^{-1}(G_{i})\to G_{i}
be the corresponding elementary double covers. If
p_{i}
is of the first type, then
G_{i}
consists of its dilation subgraph
(G_{i})_{\mathrm{dil}}
with a number of attached undilated trees. Removing
(G_{i})_{\mathrm{dil}}
, each tree acquires a unique half-arc, and no free loops are formed. If
p_{i}
is of the second type, then
G_{i}
is a genus one graph and its unique cycle is unbalanced because the free double cover
p_{i}
is nontrivial. Hence
E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)\backslash F
is a basis of the signed graphic matroid
M(\Sigma)
. Reversing this construction, we see that all bases are obtained in this way. Hence
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
is the dual matroid to
M(\Sigma)
.

To complete the proof, we show that the rank of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
is equal to
g(\widetilde{G})-g(G)
. We use the construction of Example
2.2
. Let
p_{\mathrm{ef}}:\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{ef}}\to G_{\mathrm{ef}}
be the edge-free double cover obtained from
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
by contracting each of the
n
connected components of
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
to a separate dilated vertex. The matroids
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
and
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{ef}}/G_{\mathrm{ef}})
are canonically isomorphic. Indeed,
G
and
G_{\mathrm{ef}}
have the same set of undilated edges, and for any
F\subset E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)=E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G_{\mathrm{ef}})
, the connected componets of
G_{\mathrm{ef}}\backslash F
are obtained from the connected components of
G\backslash F
by contracting the dilated edges. Hence
F
is independent for
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
if and only if it is independent for
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{ef}}/G_{\mathrm{ef}})
. Furthermore,
g(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{ef}})-g(G_{\mathrm{ef}})=g(\widetilde{G})-g(G)
, since both
\widetilde{G}
and
G
lose genus equal to the total genera of the components of
G_{\mathrm{dil}}
.

Now let
p_{\mathrm{free}}:\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{free}}\to G_{\mathrm{free}}
be the free double cover obtained by resolving the
n
dilated vertices of
G_{\mathrm{ef}}
. Then
g(G_{\mathrm{free}})=g(G_{\mathrm{ef}})+n
and
g(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{free}})=g(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{ef}})+n
, so
g(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{free}})-g(G_{\mathrm{free}})=g(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{ef}})-g(G_{\mathrm{ef}})
. Furthermore, under the natural inclusion
E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G_{\mathrm{ef}})\subset E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G_{\mathrm{free}})
, any basis of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{ef}}/G_{\mathrm{ef}})
is also a basis of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{free}}/G_{\mathrm{free}})
. Hence it is enough to prove the assertion for the free double cover
p_{\mathrm{free}}:\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{free}}\to G_{\mathrm{free}}
. Let
F\subset E(G_{\mathrm{free}})=E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G_{\mathrm{free}})
be a basis of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{free}}/G_{\mathrm{free}})
, then the connected components
G_{\mathrm{free}}\backslash F=G_{1}\cup\cdots\cup G_{k}
all have genus one. Since the quantity
g(\cdot)-1=|E(\cdot)|-|V(\cdot)|
is additive in connected components, it follows that

\rk M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)=|F|=g(G_{\mathrm{free}})-1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big(g(G_{i})-1\big)=g(G_{\mathrm{free}})-1=g(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{free}})-g(G_{\mathrm{free}})=g(\widetilde{G})-g(G).

∎

3.3.
The dual matroid
M(\widetilde{G}/G)

In this section, we describe the

, which is the dual of the signed cographic matroid

Let

(1)

F

contains all undilated vertices of

(2)

4.
Recovering the Prym variety from the matroid

In this section, we explain how to reconstruct the principalized Prym variety
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
of a double cover
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
of metric graphs from the signed graphic matroid equipped with certain additional decorations. We always implictly choose a model for the double cover, and by abuse of notation denote its signed graphic and cographic matroid by
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
and
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. To motivate the reconstruction procedure, we first recall how to reconstruct the Jacobian variety of a metric graph from the graphic matroid.

4.1.
Matroidal reconstruction of Jacobian and Prym: an overview

Let
\Gamma
be a metric graph. Choose an orientation for
\Gamma
. Given a circuit
C
of the graphic matroid
M(\Gamma)
, there exists a simple cycle
\delta_{C}=\sum\delta_{C}(e)\cdot e
in
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
supported on
C
with
\delta_{C}(e)=\pm 1
for all edges
e\in C
. The cycle
\delta_{C}
is unique up to sign. While the support of
\delta_{C}
can be determined from
M(\Gamma)
, the signs of its coefficients depend on the chosen orientation and are determined by the condition that
\delta_{C}
is a closed cycle. This information is not contained in
M(\Gamma)
.

To make the construction fully matroid-theoretic, we use the chosen orientation on
\Gamma
to define the structure of an
oriented matroid

\overrightarrow{M}(\Gamma)
on
M(\Gamma)
(see Example
4.6
or
[
BLVS+99
, Section 1.1]
). The coefficients of the cycles
\delta_{C}
are then simply the signs with which the edges of
C
occur in an oriented circuit
\overrightarrow{C}
of
\overrightarrow{M}(\Gamma)
with underlying (unoriented) circuit
C
.

It is an elementary fact that the cycles
\big\{\delta_{C}:C\in\mathcal{C}\big(M(\Gamma)\big)\big\}
are a spanning set for
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
. Moreover, we can choose a basis as follows. Let
B=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{g}\}
be a basis of the cographic matroid
M^{*}(\Gamma)
, so that
T=\Gamma\backslash B
is a spanning tree. For each
i=1,\ldots,g
let
C_{i}
be the unique circuit of
M(\Gamma)
supported on
T\cup\{e_{i}\}
, then the cycles
\delta_{C_{i}}
form a basis for
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
.
Therefore, we can associate a pptav
\Jac\big(\overrightarrow{M}(\Gamma)\big)
to the oriented matroid
\overrightarrow{M}(\Gamma)
equipped with the edge length function
\ell:E(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and
\Jac\big(\overrightarrow{M}(\Gamma)\big)
is isomorphic to the Jacobian
\Jac(\Gamma)
.

However, even more is true. The graph Jacobian
\Jac(\Gamma)
does not depend on the choice of orientation of
\Gamma
. By
[
BL78
, Corollary 6.2.8]
, graphic matroids have a unique reorientation class, hence any oriented matroid having underlying matroid

4.2.
The fundamental cycle construction

In this section, we describe an analogue of the fundamental cycle construction for double covers. We first observe that the dilated edges of
G
do not contribute to the matroid
M(\widetilde{G}/G)
, and we show that they are not seen by the Prym variety either.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a double cover of metric graphs and let
\pi_{\mathrm{ef}}:\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{ef}}\to\Gamma_{\mathrm{ef}}
be the edge-free double cover obtained by contracting all dilated edges of
\Gamma
(see Example
2.2
). Then the signed graphic matroids and the Prym varieties of the two double covers are isomorphic:

M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong M(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{ef}}/\Gamma_{\mathrm{ef}}),\qquad\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{ef}}/\Gamma_{\mathrm{ef}}),\qquad\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{ef}}/\Gamma_{\mathrm{ef}}).

Proof.

We already established that
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong M(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{ef}}/\Gamma_{\mathrm{ef}})
in the last part of the proof of Proposition
3.9
.
To prove that the Pryms are isomorphic, we consider what happens when a single dilated edge
e
is contracted. Let
\pi_{e}:\widetilde{\Gamma}_{e}\to\Gamma_{e}
be the double cover obtained by contracting
e
.
There are natural surjective contraction maps
H_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{e},\mathbb{Z})
and
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(\Gamma_{e},\mathbb{Z})
, given by setting the corresponding edges to zero. If
\gamma
is a cycle in
\Ker\big(H_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\big)
, then
e
does not occur in
\gamma
, hence
\gamma
corresponds to an element of
\Ker\big(H_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{e},\mathbb{Z})\to H_{1}(\Gamma_{e},\mathbb{Z})\big)
, and therefore the two kernels are identified by the contraction maps. We similarly identify
(\Coker\pi^{*})^{\mathrm{tf}}
and
(\Coker\pi_{e}^{*})^{\mathrm{tf}}
, and it is clear that the pairings and polarizations agree. Hence we have
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{e}/\Gamma_{e})
and therefore
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{e}/\Gamma_{e})
as well.
∎

By Lemma
4.1
it is possible to restrict attention to edge-free double covers, in other words replace each connected component of the dilation subgraph by a single dilated vertex. We now recall the notation for
\Ker p_{*}
of a double cover
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
introduced in Sections
2.2
and
2.7
. Recall that we chose a labeling
p^{-1}(v)=\{\widetilde{v}^{\pm}\}
of the undilated vertices
v\in V(G)\backslash V(G_{\mathrm{dil}})
. We then label
p^{-1}(h)=\{\widetilde{h}^{\pm}\}
the preimages of the undilated half-edges, and require
r(\widetilde{h}^{\pm})=\widetilde{r(h)}^{\pm}
if an undilated half-edge is rooted at an undilated vertex. The involution on
\widetilde{G}
is given by a parity assignment
\sigma:E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)\to\{\pm 1\}
via
\iota(\widetilde{h}^{\pm})=\widetilde{\iota(h)}^{\pm\sigma(e)}
, where
\sigma(h)=\sigma\big(\iota(h)\big)=\sigma(e)
for an edge

, and we can further relabel to assume that
\sigma(e)=+1
if
e
has at least one dilated root vertex. Choosing consistent orientations for
\widetilde{G}
and
G
determines a labeling
p^{-1}(e)=\{\widetilde{e}^{+},\widetilde{e}^{-}\}
for undilated edges
e\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)
, and we represent elements
\gamma\in\Ker p_{*}
as chains on the target graph (see Equation (
4
)):

\gamma=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\gamma(e)\cdot(\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-})=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\gamma(e)\cdot e\in C_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}).

A chain
\gamma\in C_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
represents an element of
\Ker p_{*}
if the above expression is closed as a chain on
\widetilde{G}
. This is most conveniently stated in the language of oriented matroids, which we introduce in Section
4.4
.

We observed in Proposition
3.10
that a set
F\subset E(G)
is dependent for the signed graphic matroid
M(\widetilde{G}/G)
if and only if the induced map
p[F]_{*}
on homology has nontrivial kernel. If
F
is a circuit, in other words a minimal dependent set, then the kernel is one-dimensional and has a unique generator up to sign.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be an oriented double cover, let
C
be a circuit of the signed graphic matroid
M(\widetilde{G}/G)
, and let
p[C]:p^{-1}(G[C])\to G[C]
be the restriction of
p
. The
fundamental cycle
\gamma_{C}
associated to
C

\gamma_{C}=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\gamma_{C}(e)\cdot(\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-})=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\gamma_{C}(e)\cdot e

is the unique (up to sign) generator of
\Ker p[C]_{*}
.

Figure
5
shows the six circuit types and gives formulas for the fundamental cycles. Specifically, define the cycles
\gamma^{\prime}_{C}\in\Ker p_{*}
by the formulas

(8)

\gamma^{\prime}_{C}(e)=\begin{cases}\pm 2,&\text{if }e\in C\mbox{ is a bridge of }G[C],\\
\pm 1,&\text{if }e\in C\mbox{ is not a bridge of }G[C],\\
0,&\text{if }e\notin C,\end{cases}

where the signs are chosen in such a way that
\gamma^{\prime}_{C}
is closed. Then it is easy to see that

(9)

\gamma_{C}=\frac{\gamma^{\prime}_{C}}{\gcd\big\{\big|\gamma^{\prime}_{C}(e)\big|:e\in C\big\}}=\begin{cases}\gamma^{\prime}_{C},&\text{if }C\mbox{ is of types I-V},\\
\gamma^{\prime}_{C}/2,&\text{if }C\mbox{ is of type VI}.\end{cases}

4.3.
The index function

We now describe the additional decorations of the signed cographic matroid of a double cover that are needed to determine the coefficients of the cycles
\gamma_{C}
, and hence reconstruct
\Ker p_{*}
and the Prym variety.

Equation (
8
) tells us that we need a way to detect whether an edge
e
of a circuit is a bridge or not. This information is contained in the matroid
M(G)
, but not in
M(\widetilde{G}/G)
. Instead, we observe that Equation (
7
) for the volume of the Prym (in contrast to Equation (
6
) for the Jacobian) contains an extra quantity, namely the index of an ogod, which must therefore play a role in the reconstruction. We now extend this function from the ogods, which are the bases of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
, to all independent sets of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
.

Definition 4.3
.

For a double cover
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
, we endow the matroid
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
with the
index function
defined as

\ind:\mathcal{I}\big(M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)\big)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{>0},\qquad F\longmapsto\text{number of connected components of }G\backslash F.

We emphasize that the index function is defined graph-theoretically and cannot be computed from the matroid
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
. In particular, when counting the connected components of
G\backslash F
, isolated dilated vertices are taken into account, so Zaslavsky’s definition of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
cannot be used to determine the index. We collect some basic properties of the index function in the following lemma, which we leave without proof.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a double cover of graphs, let
M^{*}=M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
be the signed cographic matroid, and let
\ind
be the index function. Then the following hold.

(1)

If
F\in\mathcal{I}(M^{*})
and
F^{\prime}\subset F
, then
\ind(F^{\prime})\leq\ind(F)
.

(2)

For every
F\in\mathcal{I}(M^{*})
we have
\ind(F)\leq\rk M^{*}+1
. If
F
is a basis then we also have
d(\widetilde{G}/G)\leq\ind(F)
.

(3)

If
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(M^{*})
differ by only one element, then
\big|\ind(B^{\prime})-\ind(B)\big|\leq 1
.

Let
C
be a circuit of the signed graphic matroid
M(\widetilde{G}/G)
. We now show how to determine the absolute values of the coefficients of the fundamental cycle
\gamma_{C}\in\Ker p_{*}
. Let
e\in C
be an edge. The set
C\backslash\{e\}
is independent in
M(\widetilde{G}/G)
and is contained in a basis
E(G)\backslash F
, where
F\subset E(G)
is an ogod. Each connected component of
G\backslash F
contains a unique undilated cycle or a dilated component, but not both. Hence if
e\in C
is a bridge, then the two connected components of
C\backslash\{e\}
lie in different connected components of
G\backslash F
. In other words,
e
is a bridge of
C
if and only if
G\backslash F
has one more connected component than
G\setminus\big(F\backslash\{e\}\big)
. Therefore, we have proved the following.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a double cover and let
C
be a circuit of the signed graphic matroid
M(\widetilde{G}/G)
. Then the absolute values of the coefficients in Equation (
8
) can be computed as

\big|\gamma^{\prime}_{C}(e)\big|=\begin{cases}2^{\ind(F)-\ind(F\backslash\{e\})},&\text{if }e\in C,\\
0,&\text{else,}\end{cases}

where
F
is any ogod such that
C\backslash\{e\}\subset E(G)\backslash F
.

4.4.
Oriented matroids

To reconstruct the signs of the coefficients
\gamma_{C}(e)
of the fundamental cycles, it is convenient to use the language of oriented matroids. We recall the definitions (a standard reference is
[
BLVS+99
]
).

Given a finite set
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, a
signed subset

\overrightarrow{S}
of
E
is a subset of
E^{+}\sqcup E^{-}
, where
E^{+}=\{e_{1}^{+},\ldots,e_{n}^{+}\}
and
E^{-}=\{e_{1}^{-},\ldots,e_{n}^{-}\}
are copies of
E
. For a signed subset
\overrightarrow{S}
of
E
define

•

the
opposite set

-\overrightarrow{S}=\big\{e^{+}:e^{-}\in\overrightarrow{S}\big\}\cup\big\{e^{-}:e^{+}\in\overrightarrow{S}\big\}
,

•

the
underlying set

S=\big\{e:e^{+}\in\overrightarrow{S}\text{ or }e^{-}\in\overrightarrow{S}\big\}
, and

•

the
positive (resp. negative) part

S^{+}=\big\{e:e^{+}\in\overrightarrow{S}\big\}
(resp.
S^{-}=\big\{e:e^{-}\in\overrightarrow{S}\big\}
).

It is understood that the underlying sets of
S^{+}
and
S^{-}
are disjoint. Therefore, for
e\in E
we write

\overrightarrow{S}(e)=\begin{cases}+1,&\text{if }e^{+}\in\overrightarrow{S},\\
-1,&\text{if }e^{-}\in\overrightarrow{S},\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For our purposes, it is convenient to define an
oriented matroid

\overrightarrow{M}
on the ground set
E
in terms of its
oriented circuits

\mathcal{C}(\overrightarrow{M})
, a family of signed subsets of
E
satisfying the following axioms:

(1)

\emptyset\not\in\mathcal{C}(\overrightarrow{M})
.

(2)

If
\overrightarrow{S}\in\mathcal{C}(\overrightarrow{M})
then
-\overrightarrow{S}\in\mathcal{C}(\overrightarrow{M})
.

(3)

For
\overrightarrow{S},\overrightarrow{T}\in\mathcal{C}(\overrightarrow{M})
with
S\subset T
we have either
\overrightarrow{S}=\overrightarrow{T}
or
\overrightarrow{S}=-\overrightarrow{T}
.

(4)

For all
\overrightarrow{S},\overrightarrow{T}\in\mathcal{C}(\overrightarrow{M})
with
\overrightarrow{S}\neq\pm\overrightarrow{T}
and
e\in S^{+}\cap T^{-}
there exists
\overrightarrow{U}\in\mathcal{C}(\overrightarrow{M})
such that
U^{+}\subset(S^{+}\cup T^{+})\setminus\{e\}
and
U^{-}\subset(S^{-}\cup T^{-})\setminus\{e\}
.

For any oriented matroid
\overrightarrow{M}
on
E
, there is an underlying matroid
M
on
E
whose circuits are the underlying sets
C
of the oriented circuits
\overrightarrow{C}\in\mathcal{C}(\overrightarrow{M})
. Furthermore, for every circuit
C
of
M
there are precisely two circuits
\overrightarrow{C}_{1},\overrightarrow{C}_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(\overrightarrow{M})
with underlying set
C
and
\overrightarrow{C}_{1}=-\overrightarrow{C}_{2}
.
A matroid
M
is called
orientable
if there is an oriented matroid
\overrightarrow{M}
having underlying matroid
M
.

In
[
Zas91
]
, Zaslavsky described how to orient the signed graphic matroid of a signed graph, and here we translate his construction into the language of double covers. To simplify exposition, we first describe how to orient the graphic matroid of an ordinary graph.

Example 4.6
.

Let
G=\big(V(G),H(G),r,\iota\big)
be a graph. An orientation on
G
is defined by a function
o:H(G)\to\{\pm 1\}
on the half-edges satisfying the property

(10)

o(h)o\big(\iota(h)\big)=-1

for any edge
e=\{h,h^{\prime}\}\in E(G)
. We view a half-edge
h\in H(G)
rooted at
v=r(h)
as pointing towards
v
if
o(h)=+1
and away from
v
if
o(h)=-1
. With respect to this choice of orientation, we can define the simplicial chain complex of
G
as

d:\mathbb{Z}^{E(G)}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{V(G)},\qquad e\longmapsto\sum_{h\in e}o(h)\cdot r(h),

and the simplicial homology group as

H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})=\Ker d=\left\{\sum_{e\in E(G)}\gamma(e)\cdot e\in\mathbb{Z}^{E(G)}:\sum_{h\in T_{v}G}o(h)\gamma(h)=0\mbox{ for all }v\in V(G)\right\},

where
\gamma(h)=\gamma(e)
for
h\in e
. Each circuit
C
of
M(G)
supports a primitive cycle
\delta_{C}\in H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
, unique up to sign, such that
\delta_{C}(e)=\pm 1
for all
e\in C
and
0
otherwise. The circuits
\overrightarrow{C}=C^{+}\sqcup C^{-}
of the
oriented graphic matroid

\overrightarrow{M}(G)
of the oriented graph
G
have the form

C^{+}=\big\{e\in C:\delta_{C}(e)=+1\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C^{-}=\big\{e\in C:\delta_{C}(e)=-1\big\},

where
C
ranges over the circuits of
M(G)
and
\delta_{C}
is one of the two primitive cycles supported on
C
. It is elementary to verify that
\overrightarrow{M}(G)
is an oriented matroid on the ground set
E(G)
, having underlying matroid
M(G)
. The cycles
\delta_{C}
can be recovered from
\overrightarrow{M}(G)
up to sign and span
H_{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
.
Hence the oriented matroid
\overrightarrow{M}(G)
determines, after choosing a length function
\ell:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the tropical Jacobian.

We now consider a double cover
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
. Choose an orientation for
G
, in other words choose a function
o:H(G)\to\{\pm 1\}
satisfying Equation (
10
). This induces an orientation on
\widetilde{G}
, and in terms of the labeling introduced in Section
4.2
, any element
\gamma\in\Ker p_{*}
has the form

\gamma=\sum_{e\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)}\gamma(e)\cdot(\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-}).

Denoting
\gamma(h)=\gamma(e)
when
h\in e
, it is then elementary to verify that

(11)

\Ker p_{*}=\left\{\sum_{e\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)}\gamma(e)\cdot(\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-}):\sum_{h\in T_{v}G}\tau(h)\gamma(h)=0\mbox{ for all }v\in V(G)\backslash V(G_{\mathrm{dil}})\right\}

where the function
\tau:H_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)\to\{\pm 1\}
is defined by

\tau(h)=\begin{cases}\sigma(e),&\text{if }o(h)=1\mbox{ and }h\in e,\\
-1,&\text{if }o(h)=-1.\end{cases}

The function
\tau
satisfies

\tau(h)\tau\big(\iota(h)\big)=-\sigma(e)

and defines an
orientation of the signed graph

(G,\sigma)
(see Equation (2.2) in
[
Zas91
]
). This in turn defines the structure of an oriented matroid
\overrightarrow{M}(\widetilde{G}/G)
on
M(\widetilde{G}/G)
, and hence an induced orientation on
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
, as follows (see Theorem 3.3 in
[
Zas91
]
). Given an orientation
\tau:H_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)\to\{\pm 1\}
, Equation (
11
) determines which
1
-chains supported on
E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)
represent elements of
\Ker p_{*}
. In particular, given a circuit
C\in\mathcal{C}\big(M(\widetilde{G}/G)\big)
, we can now determine the signs of the coefficients of the fundamental cycle
\gamma_{C}
, the absolute values having been determined in Section
4.3
:

(12)

\gamma_{C}=\sum_{e\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)}\overrightarrow{C}(e)\big|\gamma_{C}(e)\big|\cdot e=\sum_{e\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(G)}\overrightarrow{C}(e)\big|\gamma_{C}(e)\big|\cdot(\widetilde{e}^{+}-\widetilde{e}^{-}).

The functions
\overrightarrow{C}(e)
then represent the two oriented circuits of
\overrightarrow{M}(\widetilde{G}/G)
lying over
C
, for the two possible generators
\gamma_{C}
of
\Ker p[C]_{*}
.

4.5.
Reconstructing the Prym variety from the matroid

We now explain how to recover the Prym variety of a double cover
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
from the signed graphic matroid
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
equipped with the index function
\ind:\mathcal{I}\big(M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\big)\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, orientation
\overrightarrow{M}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
, and edge length function
\ell:E(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. The circuits
C\in\mathcal{C}\big(M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\big)
determine fundamental cycles
\gamma_{C}\in\Ker\pi_{*}
. Specifically, the index determines the
\big|\gamma_{C}(e)\big|
by Equation (
9
) and Proposition
4.5
, while the orientation determines the signs by Equation (
12
). This data is sufficient to reconstruct
\Ker\pi_{*}
.

Proposition 4.7
.

The lattice
\Ker\pi_{*}\subset H_{1}(\widetilde{\Gamma},\mathbb{Z})
of a double cover
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
is spanned by the fundamental cycles
\gamma_{C}
, where
C
ranges over the circuits of
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
.

Proof.

By definition,
\gamma_{C}\in\Ker\pi_{*}
for all circuits
C
of
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. Conversely, let
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a double cover. If the dilation index
d(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)=1
, then in fact
\Ker\pi_{*}
has a basis consisting of fundamental cycles
\gamma_{C}
obtained by choosing an ogod
F\subset\mathcal{B}\big(M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\big)
of minimal index; we prove this in Proposition
4.14
. Hence we assume that
d(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)>1
. Let
\gamma\in\Ker\pi_{*}
. Choose an edge
e_{1}\in E_{\mathrm{ud}}(\Gamma)
lying on a type VI circuit
C_{1}
(for example, choose any path connecting distinct connected components of
\Gamma_{\mathrm{dil}}
). Then
\gamma_{C_{1}}(e_{1})=\pm 1
, and we choose
\gamma_{C_{1}}
so that
\gamma_{C_{1}}(e_{1})=1
. We then have
\gamma=\gamma(e_{1})\gamma_{C_{1}}+\gamma_{1}
, where
\gamma_{1}\in\Ker\pi_{*}
and is supported on
\Gamma\backslash\{e_{1}\}
.

We now remove
e_{1}
from
\Gamma
and proceed by induction to obtain
\gamma=\sum\gamma(e_{i})\gamma_{C_{i}}+\gamma^{\prime}
, where each
\gamma_{C_{i}}
is a type VI circuit and
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Ker\pi_{*}
is supported on the disconnected graph
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\backslash\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
, which has no type VI circuits. It follows that each connected component of
\Gamma^{\prime}
has connected dilation subgraph and hence dilation index one, so the restriction of
\gamma^{\prime}
to each connected component lies in the span of the fundamental circuits by Proposition
4.14
. This completes the proof.
∎

For the reconstruction of
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
it is convenient to collect all the necessary matroidal data in a definition.

Definition 4.8
.

Consider the following data:

•

A signed graphic matroid
M
on a ground set
E
, together with an orientation
\overrightarrow{M}
induced by an oriented double cover representing
M
.

•

An index function
\ind:\mathcal{I}(M^{*})\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
induced from the oriented double cover representing
\overrightarrow{M}
.

•

An edge length function
\ell
on
E
.

We define a pptav
\big(\Lambda,\Lambda,[\cdot,\cdot]\big)
, which depends on all of this data but which we simply denote
\Prym(M)
. For each circuit
C\in\mathcal{C}(M)
, let
\gamma_{C}\in\mathbb{Z}^{E}
be the fundamental cycle given by Equation (
12
). We let
\Lambda
be the lattice spanned in
\mathbb{Z}^{E}
by the
\gamma_{C}
for
C
ranging over the oriented circuits of
M
, and define the pairing to be

(13)

[\gamma_{C_{1}},\gamma_{C_{2}}]=\sum_{e\in E}2\gamma_{C_{1}}(e)\gamma_{C_{2}}(e)\ell(e).

Finally, the polarization
\xi:\Lambda\to\Lambda
is the identity map.

We now state the main result of our paper.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a double cover of metric graphs. Choose an orientation for
\pi
, and let
M=M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
be the signed graphic matroid of
\pi
with induced orientation
\overrightarrow{M}
. Let
\ind:\mathcal{I}(M^{*})\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be the index function and let
\ell:E(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be the edge length function. Then
\Prym(M)
is a pptav and

\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong\Prym(M)

as pptavs. In particular,
\Prym(M)
only depends on the reorientation class of
\overrightarrow{M}
and not on
\overrightarrow{M}
itself.

Proof.

Recall that the Prym variety
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
is the polarized tropical abelian variety defined by the lattices
K=(\Coker\pi^{*})^{\mathrm{tf}}
,
K^{\prime}=\Ker\pi_{*}
, and the pairing
[\cdot,\cdot]
and polarization
\xi:K^{\prime}\to K
induced by the integration pairing and polarization on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. The principalization
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
is obtained by replacing
K
by the image
\Im\xi

Remark 4.10
.

We note that we are not currently able to reconstruct, directly from the matroid, the Prym variety
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
, except in the following two cases. Recall that the induced polarization on
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
has type
(1,\ldots,1,2,\ldots,2)
, where the number of

. If

(in other words, if

is free or has connected dilation subgraph), then the induced polarization is twice a principal polarization, and we can reconstruct

by doubling the lattice

. On the other hand, if

(equivalently, if

), then the induced polarization on
\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
is already principal and
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)=\Prym(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. We also note that the dilation index

(and hence the polarization type) can in fact be read off the indexed matroid
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
as the minimal index of an ogod

(see Lemma

The following is an immediate consequence of Theorem
4.9
.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
\pi_{i}:\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}\to\Gamma_{i}
for

be double covers. Let
(M_{i},\overrightarrow{M}_{i},\ind_{i},\ell_{i})
be the associated matroidal data packages, i.e.
M_{i}=M(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}/\Gamma_{i})
with oriented matroid structure
\overrightarrow{M}_{i}
induced by the choice of some orientation on
\pi_{i}
,
\ind_{i}

and
\ell_{i}
the edge-length function on
M_{i}
. If there is an isomorphism of the matroidal data packages, i.e. a bijection
E_{\mathrm{ud}}(\Gamma_{1})\to E_{\mathrm{ud}}(\Gamma_{2})
commuting with the

which induces a bijection
\mathcal{I}(M^{*}_{1})\to\mathcal{I}(M^{*}_{2})
commuting with the

and which induces an isomorphism between

and a reorientation of
\overrightarrow{M}_{2}

then
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}/\Gamma_{1})\cong\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}/\Gamma_{2})
as pptavs.

A natural question is to what extent a double cover can be recovered from the associated matroid (cf. Problem 5.1 in
[

]
). We give an example, and explore this question from a matroid-theoretic perspective in Section

Example 4.12
.

Consider a double cover
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
where
\Gamma
is a tree consisting of
n
undilated edges all of length 1, and such that every vertex is dilated. We claim that all such covers have the same Prym variety. Indeed, since all vertices are dilated, removing edges never relatively disconnects
\pi
. Therefore the signed cographic matroid
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)=U_{n,n}
is uniform, and the index function is
\ind(F)=|F|+1

\Gamma

is in fact a graphic matroid, it is uniquely orientable up to reorientation. This shows that up to reorientation the matroidal package of
\pi
depends only on

that any two double covers
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
of the aforementioned form have the same Prym variety.

4.6.
Effective reconstruction and a basis of fundamental cycles

In Theorem
4.9
we reconstruct the principalized tropical Prym variety from the matroidal data of the double cover, by considering the fundamental cycles corresponding to all circuits of
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. This procedure is not computationally effective. In the case of graphs, to reconstruct the Jacobian, it is sufficient to use the circuits associated to a single spanning tree, obtained by adding each of the complementary edges one by one. We now explore whether an analogous construction produces the Prym variety. In Lemma
4.4
, we noted that the index function on a basis of
M^{*}(\widetilde{G}/G)
is bounded from below by the dilation index
d(\widetilde{G}/G)
. We now show that this bound is sharp.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a double cover with
d(\widetilde{G}/G)=1
(so
p
is either free or has connected dilation subgraph). Then there exists an ogod
F\subset E(G)
of index one.

Proof.

If
p
is free, we pick an odd cycle on
G
, having connected preimage in
\widetilde{G}
. We then construct
F
by iteratively removing edges of
G
without disconnecting
G
or breaking the chosen cycle. If
p
is dilated with connected dilation subgraph, we assume for simplicity that
p
is edge-free with a unique dilated vertex. Then
F
is the set of edges in the complement of any spanning tree.
∎

We now show that a basis for
\Ker p_{*}
can be constructed by starting with an ogod of index one. This gives an exact analogue for Pryms of the fundamental cycle construction for Jacobians.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
be a double cover, and suppose that
F=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{h}\}\subset E(G)
is an ogod of index one. For each
i=1,\ldots,h
, let
C_{i}
denote the unique circuit of
M(\widetilde{G}/G)
in
G\backslash F\cup\{e_{i}\}
. Then the fundamental cycles
\gamma_{C_{i}}
are a basis for
\Ker p_{*}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
the index function is monotonous, so
\ind(F)=1
implies that
\ind(F\backslash\{e_{i}\})=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,h
. Hence Proposition
4.5
shows that
\gamma_{C_{i}}(e_{i})=\pm 1
, and we choose signs so that
\gamma_{C_{i}}(e_{i})=1
. In addition,
e_{j}\notin C_{i}
and therefore
\gamma_{C_{i}}(e_{j})=0
for
j\neq i
.

Now let
\gamma\in\Ker p_{*}
. Since
F
is an ogod, the map
p^{-1}(G\backslash F)\to G\backslash F
is injective on
H_{1}
. Hence
\gamma=0
if
\gamma(e_{i})=0
for all
i
. It follows that

\gamma=\gamma(e_{1})\gamma_{C_{1}}+\cdots+\gamma(e_{h})\gamma_{C_{h}},

and therefore the
\gamma_{C_{i}}
are a basis.
∎

The argument above shows that the fundamental cycles
\gamma_{C_{i}}
associated to an ogod
F
of index
\ind(F)\geq 2
are linearly independent and span a finite index sublattice of
\Ker p_{*}
. The index of this lattice in
\Ker p_{*}
does not seem to be directly related to
\ind(F)
. For example, the unique ogod of Example
4.12
has index
n
but gives a basis for
\Ker p_{*}
. On the other hand, Figure
6
gives an example of a double cover with dilation index two for which no ogod gives a basis for
\Ker p_{*}
. Hence the fundamental cycle construction using a single ogod cannot generally be used when the dilation subgraph is disconnected.

5.
Invariance of
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
under the simplification of
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)

The papers
[
CV10
]
and
[
BMV11
]
considered the tropical Torelli problem, in other words whether and to what extent a metric graph
\Gamma
can be reconstructed from its Jacobian
\Jac(\Gamma)
. The authors reconstruct
\Jac(\Gamma)
from the graphic matroid
M(\Gamma)
and show that the Jacobian does not change when
M(\Gamma)
is replaced by its simplification, and that conversely the simplification class of the matroid determines the Jacobian.

In this section, we construct the simplification of the signed cographic matroid
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
of a double cover of metric graphs
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
, and show that
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
does not change under simplification. We then give an example of two double covers having non-isomorphic simple matroids but isomorphic Pryms, contrary to the case of Jacobians. Hence the fibers of the tropical Prym–Torelli map are not fully described by the simplification procedure.

5.1.
Simplification of the signed cographic matroid

We recall that the simplification
M_{\mathrm{sim}}
of a matroid
M
is given by deleting all
1
-circuits, and then iteratively deleting elements in 2-circuits until there are no more 2-circuits. For the cographic matroid
M^{*}(\Gamma)
, the simplification
M_{\mathrm{sim}}^{*}(\Gamma)
is obtained by contracting all bridges in
\Gamma
, and an edge
e_{1}
in each pair
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of separating edges. It is shown in
[
CV10
]
that if we also set the new length of
e_{2}
to be
\ell(e_{1})+\ell(e_{2})
, then
\Jac(\Gamma)
does not change.

We now adapt this procedure to the signed cographic matroid
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
of a double cover
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
of metric graphs. A circuit of
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
is a minimal set of undilated edges
F
of
\Gamma
such that the restricted cover
\pi^{-1}(\Gamma\backslash F)\to\Gamma\backslash F
is not relatively connected. The types of the 1- and 2-circuits of
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
are shown on Figures
3
and
4
. We label the edges of a
2
-circuit with multiplicities as follows.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
f_{i}
be an edge lying in a
2
-circuit
F=\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
of the signed cographic matroid
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. The
multiplicity of
f_{i}
is

\mult(f_{i})=2^{\ind(\{f_{i}\})-\ind(\emptyset)}=2^{\ind(\{f_{i}\})-1}\in\{1,2\}.

We note that
\mult(f)=2
if and only if
f
is a bridge edge of
\Gamma
. We now define a non-canonical simplification process for double covers of metric graphs.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a double cover of metric graphs. The
simplification

\pi_{\mathrm{sim}}:\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{sim}}\to\Gamma_{\mathrm{sim}}
of
\pi
is defined as follows. First, we contract all edges of
\Gamma
that are
1
-circuits of
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
(see Figure
3
). Then, for every
2
-circuit
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
of
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
(see Figure
4
), we contract one of the edges, say
f_{1}
, and redefine the length of the other edge to be
\ell(f_{2})+\left(\frac{\mult(f_{1})}{\mult(f_{2})}\right)^{2}\ell(f_{1})
. The contracted edge can be chosen arbitrarily except for a
2
-circuit
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
with
\mult(f_{1})=2
and
\mult(f_{2})=1
(lower left diagram on Figure
4
), for which we always contract the edge
f_{1}
having multiplicity
2
.

By construction,
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{sim}}/\Gamma_{\mathrm{sim}})
is the simplification of
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. We note that we do not contract a loop whose preimage is a pair of parallel edges, except for the
2
-circuit on the top right of Figure
4
. This type of circuit only occurs for free double covers, and the resulting contraction is a double cover with a single dilated vertex. Therefore, simplification does not change the dilation index of a double cover. We now show that the Prym variety does not change under simplification.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\pi:\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
be a double cover of metric graphs and let
\pi_{\mathrm{sim}}:\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{sim}}\to\Gamma_{\mathrm{sim}}
be a simplification of
\pi
. Then
\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\cong\Prym_{p}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{sim}}/\Gamma_{\mathrm{sim}})
.

Proof.

Choose an orientation for the double cover
\pi
. This induces oriented matroid structures on
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
and
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. By
[
BLVS+99
, Theorem 3.4.3]
for every oriented circuit
\overrightarrow{C}\in\mathcal{C}\big(\overrightarrow{M}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\big)
and every oriented circuit
\overrightarrow{D}\in\mathcal{C}\big(\overrightarrow{M}^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)\big)
, either
C\cap D=\emptyset
or there are distinct edges
e,f\in C\cap D
such that
\overrightarrow{C}(e)\overrightarrow{D}(e)=-\overrightarrow{C}(f)\overrightarrow{D}(f)
. This implies that if
D=\{f\}
is a 1-circuit of
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
, then
f
does not lie in any circuit of
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
. But the Prym variety is generated by the fundamental cycles of the circuits of
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
, and thus contracting
f
does not change the Prym variety.

Now let
D=\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
be a 2-circuit of
M^{*}(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
and let
C_{1},C_{2}
be any circuits of
M(\widetilde{\Gamma}/\Gamma)
, possibly
C_{1}=C_{2}
. We show that the pairing (

[\gamma_{C_{1}},\gamma_{C_{2}}]=\sum_{e\in E(\Gamma)}2\gamma_{C_{1}}(e)\gamma_{C_{2}}(e)\ell(e)=\sum_{e\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}}2\big|\gamma_{C_{1}}(e)\big|\big|\gamma_{C_{2}}(e)\big|\overrightarrow{C}_{1}(e)\overrightarrow{C}_{2}(e)\ell(e)

does not change under the suggested contraction of
f_{1}
. If

then neither

nor
f_{2}
contribute to

f_{1}\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}

f_{2}\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}
as well by the aforementioned theorem. Still without loss of generality we may assume that

(if not, simply replace

f_{2}

2\big|\gamma_{C^{\prime}_{1}}(f_{2})\big|\big|\gamma_{C^{\prime}_{2}}(f_{2})\big|\ell^{\prime}(f_{2})=2\big|\gamma_{C^{\prime}_{1}}(f_{2})\big|\big|\gamma_{C^{\prime}_{2}}(f_{2})\big|\left(\frac{\mult(f_{1})}{\mult(f_{2})}\right)^{2}\ell(f_{1})+2\big|\gamma_{C^{\prime}_{1}}(f_{2})\big|\big|\gamma_{C^{\prime}_{2}}(f_{2})\big|\ell(f_{2}).

To show that the right hand side of (

containing

This can be verified on a case-by-case basis by comparing types of

is isomorphic to

.
∎
