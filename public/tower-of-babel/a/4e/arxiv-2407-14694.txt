Polarizabilities as Probes for P, T, and PT Violation
Sebastian Lahs *, Daniel Comparat

Abstract
Searches for violations of the fundamental symmetries of parity P P and time reversal T T in atomic and molecular systems provide a powerful tool for precise measurements of the physics of and beyond the standard model. In this work, we investigate how these symmetry violations affect the response of atoms and molecules to applied electric and magnetic fields. We recover well-known observables such as the P P -odd, T T -odd spin-electric field coupling that is used for searches of the electron electric dipole moment (eEDM) or the effect of P P -odd, T T -even optical rotation in atomic gases. Besides these, we obtain several other possible observables. This includes, in particular, effects that can only be seen when using oscillating or inhomogeneous fields.

At the present day, precision measurements in atomic and molecular systems are among the most powerful tools to test the standard model of particle physics (SM) at low energy scales

The interaction of atoms and molecules with these new particles would lead to small shifts in their spectra. The magnitude of almost all of these small corrections is far below the uncertainty at which absolute energy levels can presently be calculated. To avoid this problem, it makes sense to search for observables that are forbidden by the symmetries of atomic physics. 2
Derivation of the
P
and
T
odd polarizabilities

2.1
Requirements on the system

The atomic or molecular system under consideration has to fulfill a few requirements for the following treatment to be valid. Namely, the Hamiltonian
H^{0}
of the unperturbed system needs to commute with parity
P
, the square of the total angular momentum
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and its projection on an arbitrary axis
J_{z}
. Therefore, we require
H^{0}
to be invariant under
SU(2)
rotations and spatial reflections. Generally, this will be true for any free atom or molecule due to the rotational symmetry of their wavefunctions. Interestingly enough, this holds regardless of how precise our model for the system is. It even remains valid for a full relativistic QED+QCD description of all its inner processes. Only the inclusion of the weak force or hypothetical new physics interactions breaks these symmetries. Therefore, we exclude these from the definition of
H^{0}
and instead add them later as small perturbations.
From all this, we know that the eigenstates to
H^{0}
can (without approximation) be written in a basis
\left|n\right\rangle=\left|aJm\right\rangle
. Here,
J
is the quantum number of the total angular momentum,
m
is the quantum number related to its projection on a chosen quantization axis, and
a
includes all the other quantum numbers of the system.

This comes with a small caveat. To use all this, we need to be able to prepare a system in these ”true” eigenstates. This will, for example, not be possible for chiral molecules whose states of different chirality are separated by such a large energy barrier that they will realistically never be found in a Parity eigenstate (Hund’s Paradox
[
16
]
). Apart from chiral molecules
[
17
,
18
]
,
spontaneous breakings of
P
and
T
-symmetries can also arise in solid-state systems depending on their crystal symmetries
[
19
,
20
]
. These do not originate from the fundamental physics processes we want to consider here. We, therefore, require that all such spontaneous symmetry-breakings are absent. This is generally fulfilled in free atoms and (small) molecules.

Throughout this paper, we are using natural units:
c=\hbar=\varepsilon_{0}=1
. In the following, we refer to the situation where an operator is both odd under
P
and
T
as
PT
-violation. We use the notation
\boldsymbol{J}
for the total angular momentum, including possible contributions of the nuclear angular momentum, even though the total angular momentum in such a case would usually be denoted by
\boldsymbol{F}
.

2.2
Perturbative treatment of the interaction with the electromagnetic field

We want to study how the system
H^{0}
behaves under the influence of applied electromagnetic fields. To do this, we are using a perturbative treatment in which we add the semiclassical interaction with an electromagnetic field in the 2nd-order multipole approximation:

\displaystyle V^{EM}=-\boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}-Q_{ij}\partial_{i}\mathcal{E}_{j}

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and
\boldsymbol{Q}
are the electric dipole, the magnetic dipole, and the electric quadrupole (Hermitian) operators, respectively. We recall that in a simple single electron system, these moments are defined as follows
[
21
]
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}

\displaystyle=e\boldsymbol{r}-\mu_{B}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\times\boldsymbol{p})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}

\displaystyle=\mu_{B}\left(g_{s}\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{r}\times\boldsymbol{p}\right)

\displaystyle Q_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{2}\left(r_{i}r_{j}-\frac{r^{2}}{3}\delta_{ij}\right)

Here,
e
is the absolute value of the electron charge,
\boldsymbol{r}
is the position- and
\boldsymbol{p}
the momentum-vector of the electron,
\mu_{B}
is the Bohr magneton,
g_{s}
, the electron
g
-factor, and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the vector of the three Pauli matrices. In the definition of
\boldsymbol{d}
, we included the first-order relativistic correction
[
22
]
. In the remainder of this work, we do not refer to the specific form of these operators and only use their behaviors under parity and time-reversal transformations (see table
1
). That way, the treatment remains valid for multi-electron atoms and molecules in which
\boldsymbol{d}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and
Q_{ij}
are obtained by summing over all bodies in the system.

As usual, is the energy of the system
H^{0}+V^{EM}
is given by:

\displaystyle E=

\displaystyle\left\langle N\right|H^{0}+V^{EM}\left|N\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle N\right|H^{0}\left|N\right\rangle-\left\langle N\right|\boldsymbol{d}\left|N\right\rangle\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}-\left\langle N\right|\boldsymbol{\mu}\left|N\right\rangle\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}-\left\langle N\right|Q_{ij}\left|N\right\rangle\partial_{i}\mathcal{E}_{j}

with

being the eigenvectors of

In the following, we want to treat

\boldsymbol{d}
. The perturbations for
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and

as a sum of a static and a time-varying component. Following this, the general perturbative expression, valid for both static and varying fields, can be built from the solutions to the time-independent and dependent problems. Thus, we first perform a time-independent perturbation calculation for the static components of the field and, afterward, a time-dependent calculation for the time-varying components. There are multiple, slightly different, formulations for the
Dirac-type
time-dependent perturbation theory. One has to be careful in choosing the right one to avoid the appearance of secular terms and obtain a correct normalization and convergence in a static limit
[

,
24
,
25
,
26

.

A free atom (or molecule) is fully degenerate in the quantum number
m
. We assume that there are no further (accidental) degeneracies present. For these assumptions, the states
\left|n\right\rangle=\left|aJm\right\rangle
will be ”good states”
[
27
]
in respect to all the operators involved, which allows us to perform a treatment analogous to a nondegenerate one. The maximal field strengths to which such a perturbative treatment will stay valid are highly dependent on the system and states under consideration.

2.2.1
Time-independent perturbation

In a static system,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{0}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{0}
. The 1st order (Rayleigh-Schrödinger) perturbation theory for a potential

leads to

\displaystyle\left|N\right\rangle\simeq\left|n\right\rangle-\sum_{k}\frac{\left\langle k\right|V\left|n\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}}\left|k\right\rangle

(2)

with
\omega_{kn}=E_{k}-E_{n}=E_{a^{\prime}J^{\prime}}-E_{aJ}
. Thus, up to 1st order, the matrix element
\left\langle N\right|d_{i}\left|N\right\rangle
under the influence of the perturbation

can be written as:

\displaystyle\left\langle N\right|d_{i}\left|N\right\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq\left\langle n\right|d_{i}\left|n\right\rangle+2Re\sum_{k}\Bigg[\frac{\left\langle n\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{j}\left|n\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}}\mathcal{E}_{j}^{0}

(3)

\displaystyle+\frac{\left\langle n\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|\mu_{j}\left|n\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}}\mathcal{H}_{j}^{0}+\frac{\left\langle n\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|Q_{j\varkappa}\left|n\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}}\partial_{j}\mathcal{E}_{\varkappa}^{0}\Bigg]

(4)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\ell\neq 0}\sum_{k}

By expanding the fractions and identifying

2.6
Induced
\langle V^{P}\rangle
,
\langle V^{PT}\rangle
, and
\langle V^{T}\rangle

In addition to the induced moments that are defined above, also the expectation values of the symmetry-violating potentials
\langle V^{P}\rangle
,
\langle V^{PT}\rangle
, and
\langle V^{T}\rangle
themselves contribute to the full perturbative expressions of
\langle H\rangle
, and
\left|N\right\rangle

we show how the lowest order contributions to

\langle V^{PT}\rangle

\displaystyle\langle V^{P}\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq\prescript{d}{P}{\beta^{\prime}}^{\ell}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}^{\ell}

(17)

\displaystyle\langle V^{PT}\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq\prescript{d}{PT}{\beta}^{\ell}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\langle V^{T}\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq\prescript{\mu}{T}{\beta^{\prime}}^{\ell}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}^{\ell}+\prescript{Q}{T}{\beta^{\prime}}^{\ell}\,\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\partial_{i}\dot{\mathcal{E}}^{\ell}_{j}

Here, the

\displaystyle\langle V^{P}\rangle\simeq-2Im\sum_{k}

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle n\right|V^{P}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{i}\left|n\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2}}\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{\ell}\equiv\prescript{d}{P}{\beta^{\prime}}^{\ell}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}^{\ell}

Regarding the (time-dependent) mixing of states or energy shifts, the terms above constitute the lowest order (in

) corrections that violate
P
,
PT
, and
T
, respectively.

3
Resulting observables

We now connect the terms we derived above to possible experimental observables. It will become apparent that the derivation presented in this paper recovers many observables that have been considered in other works. Additionally, we obtain some

3.1
Static homogeneous fields

We start by reviewing the situation for static homogeneous fields (
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{i},\,\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{i}=0,\,\partial_{i}{\mathcal{E}}_{j}=0
). Under these considerations, most terms in Eqs. (
14

15
), and (
16
) vanish. We are left with:

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{d}\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq\prescript{d}{PT}{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}+\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{s}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}+\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}

(18)

\displaystyle+\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\alpha}_{v}\,({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}})+\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\alpha}_{s}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}+\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\alpha}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq\prescript{\mu}{}{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}+\prescript{\mu\mu}{}{\alpha}_{s}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}+\prescript{\mu\mu}{}{\alpha}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle+\prescript{\mu d}{P}{\alpha}_{v}\,({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}})+\prescript{\mu d}{PT}{\alpha}_{s}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}+\prescript{\mu d}{PT}{\alpha}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle+\left(\prescript{Q\mu}{}{\alpha}_{t_{s}}\,\mathcal{S}_{\varkappa ij}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}+\prescript{Q\mu}{}{\alpha}_{v_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{\varkappa ij}^{\boldsymbol{J}}\right){\mathcal{H}}_{\varkappa}+\prescript{Q\mu}{T}{\alpha}_{t_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{\varkappa ij}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varkappa}

In Appendix
E
, we derive that the induced energy shift of the system can be expressed through the same polarizabilities:

\displaystyle\Delta E\simeq

\displaystyle-\prescript{\mu}{}{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\mathcal{H}}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{s}\,{\mathcal{E}}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\prescript{\mu\mu}{}{\alpha}_{s}\,{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{t}\,\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}{\mathcal{E}}_{i}{\mathcal{E}}_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}\prescript{\mu\mu}{}{\alpha}_{t}\,\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}{\mathcal{H}}_{i}{\mathcal{H}}_{j}

\displaystyle-\prescript{d}{PT}{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}-\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\alpha}_{s}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}-\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\alpha}_{v}\,(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}-\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\alpha}_{t}\,\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\mathcal{E}_{i}{\mathcal{H}}_{j}

where the first line shows the terms that are even and the second line the terms that are odd under either

.

We start by discussing the former. The first two terms are well-known and usually have the largest influence on the system. They describe the linear Zeeman- and the quadratic Stark effect. The third term is the quadratic Zeeman effect
[
31
]
. It is best visible in diamagnetic systems where the linear Zeeman effect is absent. The fourth term is the Stark tensor-polarizability that can be relevant for systems with
J>1

[
32
]
. The final term is an equivalent Zeeman tensor-interaction. It has been first considered in Ref.
[
33
]
as a way to induce an electric quadrupole moment through an applied magnetic field.

Now, we are going to discuss the symmetry-violating interactions, which are the main interest of this study.

The term
\prescript{d}{PT}{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
is well known in the context of searches for the electric dipole moment of the electron (eEDM). It allows to restrict
P
- and
T

PT
-violating interactions, like Schiff moments, the scalar-pseudoscalar electron-nucleon coupling, or the electron interaction with the nuclear magnetic quadrupole moment. For a review on the topic, see Ref.
[
6
]
. For purely diamagnetic systems,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
is zero. Such systems are, therefore, insensitive to the effects of
\prescript{d}{PT}{\alpha}
.

The remaining three terms in Eq. (
19
) were first considered in Ref.
[
11
]
. The first of them,
\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\alpha}_{s}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, describes a direct coupling of the electric and magnetic fields similar to the magnetoelectric effect that can arise in some solids
[
19
]
. In Ref.
[
15
]
, its effects were investigated in more detail. Through its scalar nature,
\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\alpha}_{s}
contributes to para- and diamagnetic systems alike.

The properties of the term
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
were studied in Ref.
[
34
]
, and
[
35

. Because of its antisymmetric structure, it is only nonvanishing when the angular momentum is misaligned with the direction of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
(and also the plane spanned by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
).

The final term
\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\alpha}_{t}\,\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\mathcal{E}_{i}{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
again describes a
PT
-odd electric magnetic field coupling. Apart from the initial proposal of this expression in Ref.
[
11
]
, we do not know of any other work discussing this coupling for static fields. This term requires a system with

.

To measure these terms, one could directly measure the energy shift (
19
) depending on the strength and direction of the applied fields. Alternatively, one could use a large sample and measure the induced polarization
\langle\boldsymbol{d}\rangle
, or magnetization
\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle
, from Eq. (
18

[

[
37

3.1.1
Alignment of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
in externally applied fields

The expectation value of the angular momentum
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}=\left\langle n\right|\boldsymbol{J}\left|n\right\rangle
is a vector whose orientation depends only on the unperturbed state
\left|n\right\rangle
of the system. Even though
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
is not directly dependent on the applied
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
fields, its direction can still be influenced by them.
This is because the energy shift in (
19
) defines which
m
-substate in the
aJ
-manifold has the lowest energy. If the system possesses the ability to decay into its ground state
\left|N_{G}\right\rangle
, this will determine a particular
\left|aJm\right\rangle
state, which in turn implies a certain orientation of
\left\langle n\right|\boldsymbol{J}\left|n\right\rangle
.

For example, if we apply a strong magnetic field to a sample, the Zeeman coupling
\Delta E_{Z}=-\prescript{\mu}{}{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
dominates all the other terms. It is minimized if
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
becomes maximally oriented to the direction of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
. In this case, we can perform the replacement
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\rightarrow J\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}{|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}|}
. Here,
J
is the usual angular momentum quantum number.

Alternatively, if magnetic fields are absent, then the application of an electric field will orient
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
directly through the EDM coupling
\Delta E_{EDM}=-\prescript{d}{PT}{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
. We know from sec.
2.5
that an oriented angular momentum directly leads to an oriented magnetic moment
\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle
. It again could be picked up through magnetometry. This method of measuring the eEDM was first proposed in
[
38
]
.

3.2
Static inhomogeneous fields

If we allow the electric field to vary in space (
\partial_{i}{\mathcal{E}}_{j}\neq 0
), then the electric quadrupole terms will also contribute to the energy of the system. One possibility to apply strong field gradients to atoms or molecules is to embed them into a crystalline matrix
[
39
,
40
,
41
]

In inhomogeneous fields, the dipole moments are extended by:

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{d}\rangle

\displaystyle=\dotsb+\prescript{dQ}{P}{\alpha}_{t_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\partial_{j}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varkappa}+\left(\prescript{dQ}{PT}{\alpha}_{t_{s}}\,\mathcal{S}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}+\prescript{dQ}{PT}{\alpha}_{v_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\partial_{j}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varkappa}

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\dotsb+\prescript{\mu Q}{T}{\alpha}_{t_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\partial_{j}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varkappa}+\left(\prescript{\mu Q}{}{\alpha}_{t_{s}}\,\mathcal{S}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}+\prescript{\mu Q}{}{\alpha}_{v_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\partial_{j}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varkappa}

The dots represent the terms from in Eq. (

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle=\dotsb-\prescript{Q}{}{\alpha}\,\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\partial_{i}\mathcal{E}_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}\prescript{QQ}{}{\alpha}_{ij\varkappa l}\,\partial_{i}\mathcal{E}_{j}\partial_{\varkappa}\mathcal{E}_{l}

(20)

\displaystyle-\left(\prescript{\mu Q}{}{\alpha}_{t_{s}}\,\mathcal{S}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}+\prescript{\mu Q}{}{\alpha}_{v_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}\right)\mathcal{H}_{i}\partial_{j}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varkappa}

\displaystyle-\prescript{dQ}{P}{\alpha}_{t_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\mathcal{E}_{i}\partial_{j}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varkappa}-\prescript{\mu Q}{T}{\alpha}_{t_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\mathcal{H}_{i}\partial_{j}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varkappa}

\displaystyle-\left(\prescript{dQ}{PT}{\alpha}_{t_{s}}^{\ell}\,\mathcal{S}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}+\prescript{dQ}{PT}{\alpha}_{v_{m}}\,\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}\right)\mathcal{E}_{i}\partial_{j}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varkappa}

The expressions in the first two lines are classically allowed. The first two of these terms describe the interactions of static and induced electric quadrupole moments with the inhomogeneous electric field analogous to the linear and quadratic Stark and Zeeman effects. The next two terms describe the induction of an electric quadrupole moment through an applied magnetic field. That such an interaction, linear in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
, exists was only considered quite recently in Ref.
[
42
]
.

The third and fourth lines in Eq. (

P
,
T
, and

-odd effects. We proceed by analyzing these terms in the scenario where the angular momentum structures
\mathcal{S}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}
,
\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}

The first of the two
\mathcal{M}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij\varkappa}
-dependent terms in Eq. (

\displaystyle\Delta E\propto\prescript{dQ}{P}{\alpha}_{t_{m}}\,q\,(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}+\dotsb

That a

[

Analogously to the derivation of

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{d}\rangle\propto\prescript{dQ}{P}{\alpha}_{t_{m}}\,q\,(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})+\dotsb

In contrast to the

For the second

\displaystyle\Delta E

Alternatively, to align the different angular momentum structures through applied fields, one could, of course, also prepare the system in a different state by optical pumping.

So far, we have neglected the effects from the natural linewidth of the excited states. These are not naturally contained in a semiclassical model for the interaction with the electromagnetic field. However, it is possible to model them by adding a non-Hermitian term to the Hamiltonian

4.2
Implication for time-dependent fields

Returning to the general case
\omega_{\ell}\neq 0
, both the imaginary and real parts of the polarizabilities couple to the fields and their derivatives. We saw in previous sections that the observables for
T
-even and
T
-odd terms are different in that if one of them contains a field, the other necessarily contains its derivative. Now, with the inclusion of the natural linewidth, this is no longer true. Measuring a certain electromagnetic field response of a system no longer allows us to uniquely distinguish if it was caused by a
P
-odd
T
-even or by a
P
-odd
T
-odd effect because the same field configuration couples to both
{}_{P}{{\alpha}}^{\ell}
, and
{}_{PT}{{\alpha}}^{\ell}
. In the known standard model interactions, the strength of
P
-violating processes far exceeds that of
P
- and
T
-violating ones
[
29
]
. Therefore, it is natural to assume the hierarchy
V^{P}\gg V^{PT}
.
P
-violating interactions can, therefore, pose a large background for experimental detections of
PT
-violating ones. To avoid this, we need the
G^{\ell k}
factors to become small. This happens when
\omega^{\ell}
is far detuned from all the resonances that contribute to the polarizability. As we have seen before, this can be achieved by choosing a low frequency. This has the potential disadvantage that also the field derivatives
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}^{\ell}
, and
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}^{\ell}
become small in this limit. In the other case of very high frequencies, the polarizabilities
\prescript{}{}{{\alpha}}^{\ell k}\sim\left(\omega^{\ell}\right)^{-2}
themselves decrease rapidly.

In Refs.
[
49
,
50
]
, it was shown that oscillating electric fields in neutral atoms and molecules are not well shielded at the nucleus. It was proposed to use this effect to measure nuclear EDMs.

In
[
51
]
, a different interesting new scheme was proposed to measure
P
,
T
violations with clock transitions. It as well relies on utilizing an electric field oscillating close to the transition frequency.

The here presented effect of
P
- violation ”mimicking” as
PT
-violation through the effects of the natural linewidths could pose an important background for these kinds of measurements.

The situation is even worse for
P
-even
T
-odd observables. Through the same effect, these mix with classical
P
-even,
T
-even potentials, whose size will be many orders of magnitude above the expected size of
T
-odd physics. It is, therefore, advantageous to use static polarizabilities such as
\prescript{\mu Q}{T}{\alpha}_{t_{m}}
to study ToPe effects.

5
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, we investigated how the violation of the symmetries of parity
P
and time reversal
T
leads to atomic and molecular responses to externally applied electric and magnetic fields that are classically forbidden. This led us to general expressions for the induced electric dipole (
14
), magnetic dipole (
15
) and electric quadrupole moment (
16
). We find that atoms and molecules can obtain electric dipole moments, not only through
PT
-violation (see appendix
C.1.2
), but also through
P
without
T
-violation in time-dependent (sec.
2.6
,
4
, appendix
C.2.3
), and even purely static fields (sec.
3.2
). Additionally, we derived Eqs. (
19
), (
20
) for the energy shift in static fields and Eq. (
17
) for the effects in time-varying ones. We connected most of these terms to effects that have previously been considered or used in experimental searches for atomic
P
- and
T
- violations (sec.
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
). Finally, we argued how the effects of natural linewidths could cause problems in searches of
T
- and
PT
- odd effects (sec.
4
).

To assess the feasibility of detecting the various terms presented in this work, it is, of course, necessary to evaluate their size as well as the signal-to-noise ratio that can be reached in a given experiment. However, making general statements about them is difficult as they depend heavily on the specific system under consideration. This is the level structure of the atom or molecule, which governs the size of
\omega_{nk}
, as well as the specific symmetry-violating effects that one wants to measure. Usually, one considers potentials
V^{P}
,
V^{PT}
, and
V^{T}
that involve electron-nucleon contact interactions and, therefore, scale steeply with the number of nucleons and the overlap of the electrons with the nucleus
[
30
,
46
]
. Therefore, searches involving very heavy atoms or molecules are usually the best choice
[
52
]
. However, there also exist models introducing long-range interactions
V^{P}
,
V^{PT}
, and
V^{T}
through new light bosons that may be easier to restrict in lighter systems
[
3
,
30
,
53
,
54
,
55
]
.

To maximize the size of the polarizabilities, one also wants large
\boldsymbol{d}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and
\boldsymbol{Q}
transition elements between close-lying states. Such configurations are usually easier to achieve in molecules than in atoms.

After identifying promising systems using all these criteria, it will be necessary to perform atomic structure calculations in the future to evaluate the feasibility of measuring the various observables.

We hope that the treatment we presented in this paper can also provide a starting point for further investigations of other symmetry-violating effects that are not captured in our model due to its initial assumptions. In this paper, we only studied diagonal elements
\left\langle n\right|\boldsymbol{d}\left|n\right\rangle
. But with some modifications, one could also study the
P
or
T
forbidden dipole transitions
\left\langle n\right|\boldsymbol{d}\left|m\right\rangle
. It could also be interesting to extend the multipole expansion to include the magnetic quadrupole
[
56
]
and toroidal moments
[
13
]
. One could also include a different class of new physics interactions by introducing
P
,
T
odd potentials that are time-dependent or that couple directly to external fields. Finally, it would also be interesting to understand what happens if the field strength becomes too strong to justify the non-degenerate perturbative treatment that we used.

Appendix A
Interactions with electromagnetic fields

In the following, we define the electric and magnet fields
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
in respect to the electromagnetic vector and scalar potentials
\phi
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
in a way that allows us to separate the static and time-dependent components of the fields.

\displaystyle V^{EM}=-\boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}-Q_{ij}\partial_{i}\mathcal{E}_{j}

(24)

The electric and magnetic fields
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}=-\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\,,\;\;\;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}

We define
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
in a rather general way, as a superposition of a static component and plane waves with arbitrary frequencies and polarizations:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{0}+\sum_{\ell\neq 0}Re\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\ell}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}^{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-i\omega^{\ell}t}\right)

\boldsymbol{k}^{\ell}
is the wavevector and
\omega^{\ell}
is the angular frequency of the wave. The real part

is defined as

, with

. Throughout this paper, both upper and lower indices are used for ease of notation without the positioning carrying a specific meaning. From these choices, the electric field is given by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{0}+\sum_{\ell\neq 0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\phi+\sum_{\ell\neq 0}Re\left(i\omega^{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\ell}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}^{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-i\omega^{\ell}t}\right)

(25)

with the first term describing the static and the second the time-dependent field component. Accordingly, for the magnetic field
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{0}+\sum_{\ell\neq 0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{0}+\sum_{\ell\neq 0}Re\left(i\left(\boldsymbol{k}^{\ell}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\ell}\right)e^{i\boldsymbol{k}^{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-i\omega^{\ell}t}\right)

(26)

The Jacobian of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\mathcal{E}_{j}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\phi-\sum_{\ell\neq 0}Re\left(\omega^{\ell}k_{i}^{\ell}\mathcal{A}_{j}^{\ell}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}^{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-i\omega^{\ell}t}\right)

In the multipole approximation, only the field values at the center of mass contribute

21
]

\displaystyle V^{EM}=-\boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|_{\boldsymbol{r}=0}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}|_{\boldsymbol{r}=0}-Q_{ij}(\partial_{i}\mathcal{E}_{j})|_{\boldsymbol{r}=0}

Appendix B
The time-reversal operator

As an antiunitary operator, the time reversal operator

has some unintuitive properties with respect to the usual bra-ket notation (see, for example, Ref.

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi\right|(L\left|\psi\right\rangle)=(\left\langle\varphi\right|L)\left|\psi\right\rangle\equiv\left\langle\varphi\right|L\left|\psi\right\rangle

For an antiunitary operator, this is not true. There we have:

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi\right|(A\left|\psi\right\rangle)=\left[(\left\langle\varphi\right|A)\left|\psi\right\rangle\right]^{*}

For ease of notation, we do not indicate the parentheses explicitly in the rest of the paper. Instead, we define:

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi\right|A\left|\psi\right\rangle\equiv\left\langle\varphi\right|(A\left|\psi\right\rangle)

For the action of the inverse of the antiunitary time reversal operator

onto a

follows:

\displaystyle\left\langle aJm\right|T^{\dagger}\left|k\right\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle aJm\right|(T^{\dagger}\left|k\right\rangle)=\big[(\left\langle k\right|T)\left|aJm\right\rangle\big]^{*}=\left\langle k\right|(T\left|aJm\right\rangle)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{J-m}\langle k|aJ-m\rangle=(-1)^{J-m}\big[\langle aJ-m|k\rangle\big]^{*}

C.1

We start by discussing the vector polarizabilities
\prescript{d}{}{\alpha}_{i}

\displaystyle\prescript{d}{P}{\alpha}_{i}

\displaystyle\left\langle aJm\right|d_{i}\left|aJm\right\rangle=\left\langle aJm\right|T^{\dagger}Td_{i}T^{\dagger}T\left|aJm\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\left\langle aJ-m\right|Td_{i}T^{\dagger}\left|aJ-m\right\rangle\right)^{*}=\left(\left\langle aJ-m\right|d_{i}\left|aJ-m\right\rangle\right)^{*}

Analogously, we can show for the RHS of Eq. (
29
):

\displaystyle\left\langle aJm\right|J_{i}\left|aJm\right\rangle=-\left(\left\langle aJ-m\right|J_{i}\left|aJ-m\right\rangle\right)^{*}

where we used that
J_{i}
is
T
-odd.
We can now rename
-m\rightarrow m
and use the fact that both
d_{i}
, and
J_{i}
are Hermitian, to arrive at:

\displaystyle\left\langle aJm\right|d_{i}\left|aJm\right\rangle=-c_{J}^{a}\left\langle aJm\right|J_{i}\left|aJm\right\rangle

Then the comparison with Eq. (
29
) clearly implies
\left\langle aJm\right|d_{i}\left|aJm\right\rangle=0
.

We now show how this argument fails under the introduction of a
T
-odd potential
V^{T}
or
V^{PT}
:

\displaystyle Re\left(\prescript{d}{PT}{\alpha}_{i}\right)=

\displaystyle 2Re\sum_{k}\left\langle aJm\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|V^{PT}\left|aJm\right\rangle\omega_{nk}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle aJm\right|\sum_{k}\left[\left(d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|V^{PT}\right)+\left(V^{PT}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{i}\right)\right]\left|aJm\right\rangle\omega_{nk}^{-1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle aJm\right|D_{i}\left|aJm\right\rangle

Here we introduced the Hermitian vector operator
D_{i}
. This operator is odd under time reversal (and even under Parity):

\displaystyle TD_{i}T^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left[\left(Td_{i}T^{\dagger}T\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|T^{\dagger}TV^{PT}T^{\dagger}\right)+\left(TV^{PT}T^{\dagger}T\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|T^{\dagger}Td_{i}T^{\dagger}\right)\right]\omega_{nk}^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k}\left[\left(d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|V^{PT}\right)+\left(V^{PT}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{i}\right)\right]\omega_{nk}^{-1}

where we used that
V^{PT}
is
T
-odd while
d_{i}
and
\sum_{k}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|\omega_{nk}^{-1}
are
T
-even.
Previously, for the Lee-Yang argument, the contradiction arose because
d
and
J
, which are proportional to each other, transform differently under
T
. Now that both
D
and
J
are
T
-odd, the contradiction vanishes.

The combined requirements on parity and time reversal leave
\prescript{d}{PT}{\alpha}_{i}
as the only possible source for an EDM and tells us that such a property is directly proportional to the size of
V^{PT}
.

C.1.3
Magnetic dipole moment
\boldsymbol{\mu}

Contrary to the electric dipole moment, the 0st-order expectation value of the magnetic dipole moment is already classically allowed. We can also easily see this from the fact that
D_{i}
and
\mu_{i}
transform in the same way under
P
and
T

,
V^{T}
, and
V^{PT}
. Therefore,
\prescript{\mu}{}{\alpha}_{i}
is the only non-zero contribution to the static magnetic dipole moment.
Through the Wigner-Eckart theorem, we can express it as

\displaystyle\prescript{\mu}{}{\alpha}_{i}=\prescript{\mu}{}{\alpha}\left\langle n\right|{J}_{i}\left|n\right\rangle=\prescript{\mu}{}{\alpha}\mathcal{J}_{i}

where
\prescript{\mu}{}{\alpha}
is a real constant and equivalent to the
c_{J}^{a}
factor that we introduced above. We further define the notation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\equiv\left\langle n\right|\boldsymbol{J}\left|n\right\rangle

for the expectation value of the total angular momentum in a given state.

C.1.4
Electric quadrupole moment

The electric quadrupole operator
{Q}_{ij}
is both symmetric and traceless. The expectation value of such an object can only be proportional to the angular momentum quadrupole
[
59

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\equiv\left\langle n\right|\tfrac{1}{2}\Big[J_{i}J_{j}+J_{j}J_{i}-\tfrac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\sum_{l}J_{l}J_{l}\Big]\left|n\right\rangle

(30)

Therefore, we can write:

\displaystyle\langle Q_{ij}\rangle=\prescript{Q}{}{\alpha}\,\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}

C.2
P
and

We now continue by analyzing the rank-2 tensor polarizabilities
\prescript{}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}

C.2.1
Restrictions from parity

As previously in Eq. (
28
), we can analyze the
\prescript{}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
that are defined in Eq. (
13
) by introducing
P
into the expression. It can easily be seen that terms that contain an odd number of

-odd operators will vanish (
\prescript{d\mu}{T}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
for example contains the
P
-even operators
\mu_{j}
, and
V^{T}
and the
P
-odd operator
d_{i}
). Therefore, half of all possible polarizabilities vanish due to parity reasons.

C.2.2
Tensor decomposition of
\prescript{}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}

Before we are able to analyze
\prescript{}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
in terms of time reversal, we first need to understand the structure of these (Eucledian) rank-2 tensors. Such objects can be decomposed into a sum of three components that transform as scalar, antisymmetric vector, and traceless symmetric tensors respectively
[
59
]
:

\displaystyle 3\otimes 3

\displaystyle=\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\underset{\text{scalar}}{1}

\displaystyle\oplus

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\;\underset{\text{vector}}{3}

\displaystyle\oplus

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\;\underset{\text{tensor}}{5}

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{\varkappa}\alpha_{\varkappa\varkappa}^{\ell}\;\delta_{ij}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\alpha_{ij}^{\ell}-\alpha_{ji}^{\ell}\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\alpha_{ij}^{\ell}+\alpha_{ji}^{\ell}\right]-\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{\varkappa}(\alpha^{\ell}_{\varkappa\varkappa})\delta_{ij}\Big)

with
\delta_{ij}
being the Kronecker delta. Angular momentum algebra tells us that the expectation value of a vector operator is necessarily oriented along
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
, and the one of a traceless rank-2 tensor needs to be proportional to
\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}

[
59
]
. This allows us to express the tensor
{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
, with only three constants
{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}

,\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}
, and
\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\prescript{}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}=\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}+\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\;\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}+\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\;\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}

(31)

with
\varepsilon_{ijk}
the Levi-Civita symbol. A non-zero value for the vector term requires the system to possess
J\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
, while the tensor term will only exist for systems with
J\geq 1
.
{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}

,\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}
, and
\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}
depend on the quantum numbers
a
and
J
, but not
m
. We use the bar above them to indicate that they are complex-valued. In the following, we will construct expressions for the tensor decomposition in terms of purely real constants.

C.2.3
Restrictions from time reversal

To further eliminate terms, we can again make use of their transformation under time reversal similar to
[
60
]
and to what we did in sec.
C.1.2
.

We will perform this procedure on the example of
\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
(we ignore for now that this polarizability vanishes for Parity reasons). Again, we can decompose
\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
into its irreducible components:

\displaystyle\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}+\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\left\langle aJm\right|J_{\varkappa}\left|aJm\right\rangle\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}

(32)

\displaystyle+\left\langle aJm\right|\tfrac{1}{2}\left[J_{i}J_{j}+J_{j}J_{i}-\tfrac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\sum_{l}J_{l}J_{l}\right]\left|aJm\right\rangle\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}

We introduce
1=T^{\dagger}T
into the LHS that we previously defined trough Eq. (
7
) to get:

\displaystyle\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k}\frac{\left\langle aJm\right|T^{\dagger}Td_{i}T^{\dagger}T\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|T^{\dagger}T\mu_{j}T^{\dagger}T\left|aJm\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2}}\omega_{kn}

(33)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{k}\frac{\left(\left\langle aJ-m\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|\mu_{j}\left|aJ-m\right\rangle\right)^{*}}{\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2}}\omega_{kn}

The RHS of Eq. (
32
) transforms as:

\displaystyle\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}=

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}-\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\left\langle aJ-m\right|J_{\varkappa}\left|aJ-m\right\rangle\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}

(34)

\displaystyle+\left\langle aJ-m\right|\tfrac{1}{2}\left[J_{i}J_{j}+J_{j}J_{i}-\tfrac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\sum_{l}J_{l}J_{l}\right]\left|aJ-m\right\rangle\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}

The important observation here is that the vector part gets a minus sign in relation to the scalar and tensor parts.

We can now equate Eq. (
33
) with Eq. (
34
) and rename
-m\rightarrow m
to obtain:

\displaystyle-\Bigg[2\sum_{k}\frac{\left\langle aJm\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|\mu_{j}\left|aJm\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2}}\omega_{kn}\Bigg]^{*}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}-\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\left\langle aJm\right|J_{\varkappa}\left|aJm\right\rangle\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\;+\left\langle aJm\right|\tfrac{1}{2}\left[J_{i}J_{j}+J_{j}J_{i}-\tfrac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\sum_{l}J_{l}J_{l}\right]\left|aJm\right\rangle\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}\right)^{*}=-\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}+\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}-\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}

The comparison with Eq. (
32
) finally allows us to express
\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
in terms of real coefficients:

\displaystyle\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}=i\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{s}^{\ell}+\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{v}^{\ell}+i\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{t}^{\ell}

with
\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{v}^{\ell}\equiv Re\left(\prescript{d\mu}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}\right)
. The decomposition of
\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha^{\prime}}_{v}^{\ell}
, that only differs by a factor of
-\omega_{kn}
follows in the exact same way.

If we perform an analog treatment on
\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
, everything is basically the same except that we do not get the global minus sign in Eq. (
33
) due to the different
T
-transformation behavior of
d_{j}
and
\mu_{j}
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\left(\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}\right)^{*}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{dd}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}-\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\;\prescript{dd}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}+\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\;\prescript{dd}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{s}^{\ell}+i\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\;\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{v}^{\ell}+\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\;\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{t}^{\ell}

(35)

C.2.4
Behavior of the
d\mu
-terms under time reversal

We now demonstrate that the argument we presented above for the transformation of
\prescript{d\mu}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
and
\prescript{dd}{}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
under time reversal
T
still applies for the more complex
P
,
T
odd polarizability expressions like
\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
.

We again start by decomposing
\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
into its irreducible components:

\displaystyle\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}+\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\left\langle aJm\right|J_{\varkappa}\left|aJm\right\rangle\;\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}

(36)

\displaystyle+\left\langle aJm\right|\tfrac{1}{2}\left[J_{i}J_{j}+J_{j}J_{i}-\tfrac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\sum_{l}J_{l}J_{l}\right]\left|aJm\right\rangle\;\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}

It is clear that the RHS will transform exactly the same as in Eq. (
34
). For
\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
on the LHS, we can introduce
1=T^{\dagger}T
into Eq. (
44
). After using the transformation behaviors of the different operators given in table
1
, we get:

\displaystyle\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\alpha}_{ji}^{\ell}=-2\sum_{k,\,l}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle aJ-m\right|V^{P}\left|l\right\rangle\left\langle l\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|\mu_{j}\left|aJ-m\right\rangle}{(\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2})\omega_{ln}}\omega_{kn}

(37)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle aJ-m\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|\mu_{j}\left|l\right\rangle\left\langle l\right|V^{P}\left|aJ-m\right\rangle}{(\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2})\omega_{ln}}\omega_{kn}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle aJ-m\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|V^{P}\left|l\right\rangle\left\langle l\right|\mu_{j}\left|aJ-m\right\rangle}{(\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2})(\omega_{ln}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2})}\left(\omega_{kn}\omega_{ln}+(\omega^{\ell})^{2}\right)\Bigg]^{*}

Equating this with the transformed version of the RHS of Eq. (
36
) and renaming

, we get:

\displaystyle\left(\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}\right)^{*}=-\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}+\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\;\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}-\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}

This tells us that the symmetric part is purely imaginary while the antisymmetric vector component is real.

It is now also clear that when considering
\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
instead of
\prescript{d\mu}{P}{\alpha}_{ji}^{\ell}
, the RHS of Eq. (

\displaystyle\left(\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}\right)^{*}=\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell}-\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\;\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell}+\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\;\prescript{d\mu}{PT}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell}

(38)

We conclude that polarizabilities containing an even number of
T
-odd operators possess purely real scalar and tensor components and a purely imaginary vector component. Polarizabilities with an odd number of
T
-odd operators, on the other hand, have a real vector component but imaginary scalar and tensor parts. From the definitions in
2.4
, we can see that the polarizabilities
\alpha
that couple to the fields need to be necessarily real while the polarizabilities
\alpha^{\prime}
that couple to the field derivatives need to be imaginary.

C.2.5
Dipole-quadrupole terms: rank-3 tensor decomposition

We continue by examining the rank-3 polarizability tensors. These arise in Eqs. (
6
), and (
8
) as cross polarizabilities between electric and magnetic dipoles and the electric quadrupole moment.

As a symmetric tensor, the quadrupole operator can only have 6 independent elements, leading to an
\alpha_{ij\varkappa}^{\ell}
with
3\otimes 6=18
elements. The decomposition of a rank-3 tensor that is symmetric in its last two components under
SU(2)
was derived in Ref.
[
61
]
:

\displaystyle 18=\underset{vector}{3}\oplus\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}tensor\\
(octupole)\end{subarray}}{7}\oplus\underset{vector}{3}\oplus\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}tensor\\
(quadrupole)\end{subarray}}{5}

where the first two tensors are symmetric and the second two are of mixed symmetry. This allows us to write the rank-3 polarizabilities as:

\displaystyle\prescript{}{}{\alpha}_{ij\varkappa}^{\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}\,\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v_{s}}^{\ell}+\mathcal{S}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}\,\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t_{s}}^{\ell}+\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}\,\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v_{m}}^{\ell}+\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\,\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t_{m}}^{\ell}

where
S_{ij\varkappa}
are real symmetric, and
M_{ij\varkappa}
are real mixed-symmetry tensors. They depend on the indicated powers of
\boldsymbol{J}
. From the definitions given in
[
61
]
, we can derive the following tensors:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{ij\varkappa}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{5}\left(\mathcal{J}_{i}\delta_{j\varkappa}+\mathcal{J}_{j}\delta_{i\varkappa}+\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\delta_{ij}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{ij\varkappa}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{6}(J_{i}J_{j}J_{\varkappa}+J_{j}J_{\varkappa}J_{i}+J_{\varkappa}J_{i}J_{j}+J_{j}J_{i}J_{\varkappa}+J_{\varkappa}J_{j}J_{i}+J_{i}J_{\varkappa}J_{j})

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}_{ij\varkappa}

\displaystyle=\left\langle n\right|\Bigr[\mathcal{T}_{ij\varkappa}-\tfrac{1}{5}(\mathcal{T}_{inm}\delta_{nm}\delta_{j\varkappa}+\mathcal{T}_{njm}\delta_{nm}\delta_{i\varkappa}+\mathcal{T}_{nm\varkappa}\delta_{nm}\delta_{ij})\Bigl]\left|n\right\rangle

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{ij\varkappa}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}(2\delta_{j\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{i}-\delta_{ij}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}-\delta_{i\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{j})

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij\varkappa}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{jl}\varepsilon_{im\varkappa}+\delta_{\varkappa l}\varepsilon_{imj})\mathcal{Q}_{lm}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}

So far, we have not used the fact that the quadrupole tensor is traceless. This reduces its number of independent elements by one, leading to
3\otimes 5=15
. For the tensor
\prescript{}{}{\alpha}_{ij\varkappa}^{\ell}
, this means that the partial trace over the last two components vanishes.
\mathcal{M}^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{ij\varkappa}
,
\mathcal{M}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij\varkappa}
, and
\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}_{ij\varkappa}
fullfill this condition. For the symmetric vector component
\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{ij\varkappa}
, we have however:

\displaystyle 0=\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v_{s}}^{\ell}\,\sum_{l}\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{ill}

\displaystyle=\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v_{s}}^{\ell}\,\sum_{l}\tfrac{1}{5}\left(\mathcal{J}_{i}\delta_{ll}+\mathcal{J}_{l}\delta_{il}+\mathcal{J}_{l}\delta_{il}\right)=\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v_{s}}^{\ell}\,\mathcal{J}_{i}

This implies
\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v_{s}}^{\ell}=0
. Therefore the contribution of
\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{ij\varkappa}
vanishes and we arrive at the decomposition of the rank-3 tensor into only 3 irreducible structures:

\displaystyle\prescript{}{}{\alpha}_{ij\varkappa}^{\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{3}}\,\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t_{s}}^{\ell}+\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}\,\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v_{m}}^{\ell}+\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\,\prescript{}{}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t_{m}}^{\ell}

Again, the decomposition of
{\alpha^{\prime}}_{ij\varkappa}^{\ell}
follows completely analogous.

C.2.6
Dipole-quadrupole terms: restrictions under parity and time reversal

The restrictions from parity still apply as before: Only polarizabilities containing an even number of

To analyze the behavior under time reversal, we can proceed analogously to sec.

C.2.8
Insensitivity of

From the arguments presented in sec.

T
-odd terms. We demonstrate this on the example of
\prescript{dd}{T}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\prescript{dd}{T}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}=2\sum_{k,\,l}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle n\right|V^{T}\left|l\right\rangle\left\langle l\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{j}\left|n\right\rangle}{(\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2})\omega_{ln}}\omega_{kn}+\frac{\left\langle n\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{j}\left|l\right\rangle\left\langle l\right|V^{T}\left|n\right\rangle}{(\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2})\omega_{ln}}\omega_{kn}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle n\right|d_{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|V^{T}\left|l\right\rangle\left\langle l\right|d_{j}\left|n\right\rangle}{(\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2})(\omega_{ln}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2})}\left(\omega_{kn}\omega_{ln}+(\omega^{\ell})^{2}\right)\Bigg]

From this definition, it follows that
\big(\prescript{dd}{T}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}\big)^{*}=\prescript{dd}{T}{\alpha}_{ji}^{\ell}
. Regarding the tensor decomposition, this relation implies:

\displaystyle\left(\prescript{dd}{T}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell k}\right)^{*}=\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{dd}{T}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell k}-\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\;\prescript{dd}{T}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell k}+\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\;\prescript{dd}{T}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell k}

(40)

where we used that
\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}
is antisymmetric.
However, from its transformation under time reversal, we know that
\prescript{dd}{T}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell}
, which contains an odd number of
T
-odd operators, must fulfill the relation:

\displaystyle\left(\prescript{dd}{T}{\alpha}_{ij}^{\ell k}\right)^{*}=-\delta_{ij}\;\prescript{dd}{T}{\bar{\alpha}}_{s}^{\ell k}+\varepsilon_{ij\varkappa}\mathcal{J}_{\varkappa}\;\prescript{dd}{T}{\bar{\alpha}}_{v}^{\ell k}-\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\;\prescript{dd}{T}{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}^{\ell k}

(41)

Together, these relations can only be true if all three components are zero.

We can make analogous arguments for the polarizabilities that are quadratic in
\mu
.

In the case of
\prescript{QQ}{T}{\alpha}_{ij\varkappa l}^{\ell k}
, we are dealing with a rank-4 tensor. Because the quadrupole operator is symmetric, it obeys the relation
\left(\prescript{QQ}{}{\alpha}_{ij\varkappa l}^{\ell k}\right)^{*}=\prescript{QQ}{}{\alpha}_{\varkappa lij}^{\ell k}
. One can again see that this relation is in conflict with the relations that follow from time reversal.

In Ref.
[
15
]
, macroscopic equations were formulated describing the possible
P
,
PT
, and
T
odd responses of systems to homogeneous time-dependent electromagnetic fields. From the results of this section, we can see that (under our initial assumptions) there are no microscopic processes that could generate the proposed
T
-odd,
P
-even responses.

C.3
Derivation of
\langle V^{P}\rangle
,
\langle V^{PT}\rangle
, and
\langle V^{T}\rangle

In the following, we illustrate how the values for the induced energy shifts

,
\langle V^{PT}\rangle
, and
\langle V^{T}\rangle

Obviously, all these potentials vanish in 0st order in

\displaystyle\left\langle n\right|V^{P}\left|n\right\rangle=\left\langle n\right|V^{PT}\left|n\right\rangle=\left\langle n\right|V^{T}\left|n\right\rangle=0

If we now consider how

,

We consider the perturbation of

through
-\boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}

2.2
, cf. Eq. (
5

\displaystyle\langle V^{P}\rangle\simeq 2Re\sum_{k}

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle n\right|V^{P}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{i}\left|n\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2}}\omega_{kn}\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\ell}

(42)

\displaystyle-2Im

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle n\right|V^{P}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{i}\left|n\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2}}\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{\ell}

C.1.2
, it follows that
\left\langle n\right|V^{P}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{i}\left|n\right\rangle
must be fully imaginary.

While the real part (that is similar to the perturbation of
\boldsymbol{d}
through
V^{P}

\displaystyle\langle V^{P}\rangle\simeq-2Im\sum_{k}

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle n\right|V^{P}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|d_{i}\left|n\right\rangle}{\omega_{kn}^{2}-(\omega^{\ell})^{2}}\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{\ell}\equiv\prescript{d}{P}{\beta^{\prime}}^{\ell}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}^{\ell}

(43)

From this, we can also conclude that even if the Lee-Yang argument (see appendix
C.1.2
) forbids an atom to possess a static electric dipole moment without
T
-violation, this does not mean that there cannot be a purely
P
-odd linear response to an applied (time-dependent) electric field.

We find the following general rules: For
\beta

\beta^{\prime}
to be nonzero, they must contain an even number of
P
-odd operators. If they are rank-1 tensors, then an even number of

-odd operators makes them imaginary, while an odd number of
T
-odd operators makes them real. For rank-2 tensors, this is the other way around. These criteria lead to eq. (

\langle V^{P}\rangle
,
\langle V^{PT}\rangle
, and
\langle V^{T}\rangle
.

Deriving the 2nd order perturbations through

[

63

26

,

Appendix F
Alignment of
\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}

In sec.

, we discuss observables in static inhomogeneous fields. They rely on the angular momentum tensor structures
\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}

The tensor
\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}
depends on
\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}
(defined in (
30

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij\varkappa}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{jl}\varepsilon_{im\varkappa}+\delta_{\varkappa l}\varepsilon_{imj})\mathcal{Q}_{lm}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}

(46)

Similar to the alignment of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}

\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
, and
\partial_{i}\mathcal{E}_{j}
compete with each other to define the specific state
\left|aJm\right\rangle

\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}_{ij}\rightarrow J^{2}\frac{b_{1}\partial_{i}\mathcal{E}_{j}+b_{2}{\mathcal{E}}_{i}{\mathcal{E}}_{j}+b_{3}{\mathcal{H}}_{i}{\mathcal{H}}_{j}}{\sqrt{\sum_{\varkappa,l}\left(b_{1}\partial_{\varkappa}\mathcal{E}_{l}+b_{2}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varkappa}{\mathcal{E}}_{l}+b_{3}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varkappa}{\mathcal{H}}_{l}\right)^{2}}}-\tau\delta_{ij}

(47)

Here

b_{2}
, and
b_{3}
are real constants that depend on the sizes of the different classically allowed polarizabilities. The expression has been normalized through the Frobenius norm.

We now proceed by deriving the energy shifts and induced dipole moments that originate from terms proportional to
\mathcal{M}_{ij\varkappa}^{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\partial_{j}\mathcal{E}_{\varkappa}
.

The quadrupole component of an electrostatic field is given by the potential:
\phi^{Q}=q[(\boldsymbol{r}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})^{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}\boldsymbol{r}^{2}]
. It is oriented along some axis
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
and has an amplitude of
q
. without loss of generality, we can take
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
in
z
-direction:
