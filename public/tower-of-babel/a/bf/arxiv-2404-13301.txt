Abstract
We investigate the minimization of a quadratic function over Stiefel manifolds (the set of all orthogonal r r -frames in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} ), which has applications in high-dimensional semi-supervised classification tasks. To reduce the computational complexity, we employ sequential subspace methods (SSM) to reduce the high-dimensional problem to a sequence of low-dimensional subproblems. In this paper, our goal is to achieve an optimal solution of high quality, referred to as a “qualified critical point”. Qualified critical points are defined as those where the associated multiplier matrix meets specific upper-bound conditions. These points exhibit near-global optimality in quadratic optimization problems. In the context of a general quadratic, SSM generates a sequence of “qualified critical points” through low-dimensional “surrogate regularized models”. The convergence to a qualified critical point is guaranteed when each SSM subspace is constructed from the following vectors: (i) a set of orthogonal unit vectors associated with the current iterate, (ii) a set of vectors representing the gradient of the objective, and (iii) a set of eigenvectors associated with the smallest r r eigenvalues of the system matrix. Furthermore, incorporating Newton direction vectors into the subspaces can significantly accelerate the convergence of SSM.



Keywords:
Procrustes problem, Stiefel manifold, Sequential subspace methods, Trust region methods.

While our discussion primarily focuses on quadratic functions for clustering applications, the results presented in this paper can also offer insights into minimizing general objectives over Stiefel manifolds. Below, we outline the key contributions of this paper.

•

We analyze the optimality conditions in (

•

SSM constructs a sequence of subspaces and solves the corresponding low-dimensional subproblems.
When the subspaces are spanned by columns of

,

subproblem solutions converge to a qualified critical point of (

To achieve fast convergence, we can incorporate a Newton direction within the subspace. However, calculating Newton steps is often the most time-consuming aspect of this process. To address this issue, we impose orthogonality against the ground eigenvectors during the computation of the Newton step. This method improves the conditioning of the Hessian operator and reduces the total number of conjugate gradient (CG) iterations needed.

•

In applications, there is a need to address a “locally biased” problem, such as finding a partition or clustering that is guided by a predefined ”ground truth” partition of a seed set of vertices. These locally biased problems can be particularly challenging for popular eigenvector-based machine learning and data analysis tools.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed SSM on various artificial test cases. The quality of the local solutions obtained for these problems directly impacts classification accuracy. Numerical studies indicate that the SSM achieves high-quality clusters, both in terms of balanced graph cut metrics and the accuracy of labeling assignments across several real-world datasets. These improvements are consistent with SSM attaining lower objective values and smaller first-order residuals.

2.4
Regularized problems

In general, a qualified critical point of (

Theorem 3

(Global solutions)
.

Let

Proof.

For

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(X)={\mathcal{L}}(X,\Lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(X^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}\left\{\langle(X-X^{\prime}),A(X-X^{\prime})C\rangle-\langle(X-X^{\prime}),(X-X^{\prime})\Lambda^{\prime}\rangle\right\},

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(X^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}\langle(X-X^{\prime})^{\top}(X-X^{\prime}),d_{1}C-\Lambda^{\prime}\rangle\geq\mathbf{f}(X^{\prime}),

When

Generally, when

To proceed, we consider approximate “regularized” models, where the eigenvalues of the system matrix are lifted,

\widetilde{A}:=A+\sum_{k=1}^{r-1}(d_{r}-d_{k})v_{k}v_{k}^{\top}=A+V_{g}(d_{r}I_{r}-\hbox{\rm diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}))V_{g}^{\top},\;V_{g}:=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}.

Corollary 2.6
.

Consider the regularized problem,

\min_{X}\{\mathbf{f}(X,\widetilde{A},B,C)=\langle X,\widetilde{A}XC\rangle-2\langle X,B\rangle:X\in St(n,r)\}.

(49)

A qualified critical point is a global solution.

We provide several additional cases, where a qualified critical point is a global minimizer.
The following proposition serves as a preliminary example. Assume the reduced SVD:

with rank

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose

.
Suppose left singular vectors of

, i.e.,

.
Then

in (

(50)

In this situation,
X^{\top}B
is symmetric and positive semidefinite, and
\gamma_{j}\leq d_{j}
for

Proof.

The objective

has a lower bound: for any

.
In addition,

\Lambda

Since the diagonal of

.
∎

Motivated by the above case, we propose a non-degenerate condition on

and Theorem

The following safeguard estimate demonstrates that when the projection of

Definition 3
.

Let

be the smallest singular value of

. When

Theorem 4

(Safeguard estimate)
.

Let
V_{g}=[v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
be the ground eigenvector matrix. Let
(X,\Lambda)
be a qualified critical point in (
2
).
Let
\sigma
be the smallest singular value of
V_{g}^{\top}BC^{-1}
. Then

\gamma_{j}\leq d_{r}-\sigma\textrm{ for $j=1,\ldots,r$.}

(53)

In addition,
suppose

\sigma>d_{r}-d_{1}.

(54)

Then
\Lambda\preceq d_{1}C
, and thus

is a global minimizer.

Proof.

Start with the first-order condition
AX=X\Lambda C^{-1}+BC^{-1}
.
Let
u_{j}
be a unit eigenvector of
\Lambda C^{-1}
corresponding to eigenvalue
\gamma_{j}
. Taking the product of the first order condition with

and

from the right and left-hand sides yields

(55)

which implies

(d_{i}-\gamma_{j})v_{i}^{\top}Xu_{j}=v_{i}^{\top}BC^{-1}u_{j}.

(56)

The second-order condition of a qualified point

indicates

\gamma_{j}\leq d_{r}
for
j=1,2,\ldots,r
.

(57)

Note that

\|V_{g}^{\top}Xu_{j}\|\leq\|Xu_{j}\|\leq 1

Since

,
then with

d_{r}-\gamma_{j}\geq(d_{r}-\gamma_{j})|v_{r}^{\top}Xu_{j}|=|v_{r}^{\top}BC^{-1}u_{j}|\geq\sigma.

(58)

From (

we have

for each

∎

3
Algorithms

3.1
Gradient projection methods

Projected gradient methods or gradient methods with retractions can be used
to reach a local solution of (
2
).

•

When
Y\in T_{X}{\mathcal{M}}
and
X\in St(n,r)
, singular values of
X+Y
are greater or equal to
1
. Hence,
the metric retraction
{\mathcal{R}}_{X}(Y)
is actually the projection of
X+Y
on the convex hull of
St(n,r)
.
The Euclidean gradient of
\mathbf{f}
is
\nabla\mathbf{f}=AXC-B
. The standard gradient projection method, i.e.,
the iterations

X^{(k+1)}={\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X_{k}-\alpha(AX_{k}C-B)),\textrm{ for $k=1,2,\ldots$, }

(69)

can be used to compute a stationary point
with proper step size
\alpha>0
. The convergence analysis under the Armijo rule can be found in Prop. 2.3.3
[
13
]
.

•

The Riemannian gradient descent method is one popular method to solve (
2
).
The Riemannian gradient at
X
is given by

\displaystyle\textrm{grad }\mathbf{f}(X)=\textrm{Proj }_{X}[AXC-B]

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(AXC-B)-X\Lambda_{sym},

(71)

where
\Lambda=X^{\top}(AXC-B)
.
The Riemannian gradient descent is

X_{k+1}={\mathcal{R}}_{X_{k}}(-\alpha_{k}\textrm{grad }\,\mathbf{f}(X_{k}));

(72)

For the gradient method with the Armijo rule,
the convergence to a stationary point is verified in Theorem 4.3.1
[
4
]
or Cor. 4.13
[
16
]
.

•

When
X_{k}
is near a critical point, we can use
the Newton method to speed up the convergence,

X_{k+1}={\mathcal{R}}_{X_{k}}(\alpha_{k}Z);

(73)

where
the Newton direction
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is computed from

\textrm{Hess }\,\mathbf{f}(X)[Z]=-\textrm{grad }\,\mathbf{f}(X),

(74)

The right-hand side is

\displaystyle\textrm{grad }\,\mathbf{f}(X)=\textrm{Proj }_{X}\left(\nabla\mathbf{f}(X)\right)

(75)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle AXC-B-X\Lambda_{sym},\;\Lambda=X^{\top}(AXC-B)

(76)

and the left-hand side ( the calculation is based on (7.29), (7.39) in
[
16
]
)
is

\displaystyle\textrm{Hess }\mathbf{f}(X)[Z]=\textrm{Proj }_{X}\left\{\nabla^{2}\mathbf{f}(X)[Z]-Z(X^{\top}\nabla\mathbf{f}(X))_{sym}\right\}

(77)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textrm{Proj }_{X}\left\{AZC-Z\Lambda_{sym}\right\}.

(78)

From an optimization perspective, the Newton scheme in (
73
) can be interpreted as a local version of Sequential Quadratic Programming (see section 18.1
[
43
]
).
However, when
X_{k}

is significantly far from a critical point, the system in equation (
74
) may fail to be positive definite, which can render the computation of
Z
ineffective. In particular,
Z
may be an ascent direction. In the next section, we will propose a method for determining
\Lambda
in regularized problems to address this issue.

3.2
Sequential subspace methods(SSM)

Manifold optimization involves multiple retractions at each step-length determination, which can be time-consuming, especially when
n
is much larger than
r
. To address this, sequential subspace methods have been proposed for solving the sphere minimization problem outlined in (
6
)
[
26
]
.
Specifically, the authors consider incorporating the Newton direction vector and the ground vector into the subspace to ensure convergence to a global minimizer for the sphere minimization problem.
Further applications of subspace methods can be found in
[
51
]
. These methods concentrate on conducting retractions within a smaller dimensional space, leading to a significant reduction in computational complexity when dealing with large-scale problems. In this section, we will extend the framework in
[
26
]
to address the
St(n,r)
minimization problem in (
2
), emphasizing the quality of the solutions.

For a general smooth function
\mathbf{f}(X)
on
St(n,r)
, the idea of SSM is to introduce a proper isometric matrix
V\in St(n,l)
and
to express
X
in the form of

X=V\widetilde{X}
with
\widetilde{X}\in St(l,r)
,
r<l<n

(79)

so that
the original problem can be approximated by the low-dimensional problem

\min_{\widetilde{X}}\{\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}(\widetilde{X}):=\mathbf{f}(V\widetilde{X})\}.

(80)

More precisely, (
2
) can be handled by the alternating scheme

\min_{X\in St(n,r)}\mathbf{f}(X;A,B,C)=\min_{V}\min_{X\in St(n,r)}\{\mathbf{f}(V^{\top}X;V^{\top}AV,V^{\top}B,C):V^{\top}V=I,\;V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 4r}\}.

(81)

The matrix
V
can be regarded as one descriptor of
one subspace
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
of the
n\times l
matrices, whose columns are spanned by

•

column vectors of
V_{g}
: the ground eigenvectors
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}\}
of
A
corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
,

•

column vectors of
X_{k}
,

•

column vectors of SQP direction
Z_{k}
(the computation is deferred in section
3.4.1
),

•

column vectors of gradient
\nabla\mathbf{f}(X_{k})=AX_{k}C-B
of the cost function at
X_{k}
.

The details will be given in the next section.
In short,
an approximate solution
X_{k+1}
is chosen from a low-dimensional space

{\mathcal{S}}_{k}=\{[V_{g},X_{k},AX_{k}C-B,Z_{k}]S:S\in\mathbb{R}^{4r\times r}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}.

(82)

If no confusion, we shall write
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}=span\{V_{g},X_{k},AX_{k}C-B,Z_{k}\}
.
Use one isometric matrix
V=V_{k}
,
from the qr-factorization of
[V_{g},X_{k},AX_{k}C-B,Z_{k}]
to express
each
X\in St(n,r)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
as
X=V_{k}\widetilde{X}
for some
\widetilde{X}\in St(4r,r)
.
Introduce

A_{k}:=V_{k}^{\top}AV_{k},\;B_{k}:=V_{k}^{\top}B.

(83)

From (
81
), the minimizer
X_{k+1}
of

\min_{X\in St(n,r)\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}\mathbf{f}(X)=\min_{\widetilde{X}\in St(4r,r)}\mathbf{f}(\widetilde{X};V_{k}AV_{k}^{\top},V_{k}^{\top}B,C).

(84)

can be expressed as

X_{k+1}=V_{k}\widetilde{X}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{4r\times r}
, where

\widetilde{X}=\widetilde{X}_{k}
is the minimizer of the
St(4r,r) problem
induced by
S_{k}
,

\widetilde{X}_{k}:=arg\{\min_{\widetilde{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{4r\times r}}\mathbf{f}(\widetilde{X};A_{k},B_{k},C)\}.

(85)

The above discussion outlines the basic framework of the SSM. However, when dealing with high-dimensional data, we need to solve many subspace problems. A key requirement is
to achieve fast convergence for each subspace problem.
Achieving fast convergence relies on introducing Newton directions in the subspace
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
. However, if the Hessian matrix in (
78
) is not positive semidefinite,
the Newton step may not result in a decrease in the objective function.
To address this issue, Alg.
1
makes two adjustments to the subspace method described by
84
and (
84
)

•

Replacing the objective function
\mathbf{f}
in (
81
) with a surrogate regularized function
\mathbf{f}_{k}
, as shown in (
86
). Here, the ground eigenvectors are included in the subspace
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
to ensure the solution quality of each subproblem.

•

Introducing additional orthogonality to
V_{g}
when calculating the Newton step.

These adjustments bring two advantages. First, as stated in Corollary.
2.6
, any qualified critical point is guaranteed to be the global minimum of every low-dimensional subspace problem in (
90
). Secondly, the Hessian matrix associated with the qualified multipliers is positive semi-definite, which allows us to use the conjugate gradient method to calculate the Newton step size from (
120
), and the resulting Newton/SQP direction can be used as a descent direction.

3.3
Regularized surrogate models

We start with the introduction of a regularized model at the base point
X_{k}
.

Definition 4
.

Let
D:=\widetilde{A}-A\succeq 0
and

.
At each base point

\mathbf{f}_{k}(X)

\mathbf{f}_{k}(X):=\frac{1}{2}\langle X,\widetilde{A}XC\rangle-\langle X,B_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle X_{k},DX_{k}C\rangle,\textrm{ with }B_{k}=B+DX_{k}C.

(86)

Here,

at

Proof.

that exceeds

in the right-hand side of (
103

(104)

3.4.1

Proposition 3.2

(SQP direction)
.

Let

be the minimizer of the unconstrained objective function

(118)

for some

in
\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}

and the column space of

.

(119)

Then

Proof.

X
as

X=X_{k}+z

around

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{k}(z)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{k}(0)+\langle z,\widetilde{A}X_{k}C-B_{k}-X_{k}\Lambda_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}(\langle\widetilde{A}zC,z\rangle-\langle\Lambda_{k},z^{\top}z\rangle)

(123)

the minimizer

Remark 3.3
.

In simulations, calculating the Newton step is the most computationally intensive part of the entire algorithm. The orthogonality between

and

is introduced to ensure one positive definite system in (

This approach allows us to use conjugate gradient methods to determine a Newton direction, which facilitates the rapid convergence of the SSM.

3.4.2
Initialization in SSM

The following discusses an effective initialization of
(X,\Lambda)
in SSM.
Observe one lower bound for
\mathbf{f}(X;\widetilde{A},B,C)
: for any
X\in St(n,r)
,

\mathbf{f}(X;\widetilde{A},B,C)\geq\frac{1}{2}(tr(C)d_{r})-\|B\|_{*}.

(125)

The lower bound is attained if columns of
B
lie in
V_{g}
and
X=P_{\mathcal{M}}(B)

Proposition 3.4

(Initialization)
.

Consider
the approximate regularized problem

\min_{X}\{\mathbf{f}(X;\widetilde{A},V_{g}V_{g}^{\top}B,C):X\in St(n,r)\}.

(126)

Then

the multiplier
\Lambda=X^{\top}(\widetilde{A}XC-V_{g}V_{g}^{\top}B)
satisfies
\Lambda\preceq d_{r}C
.

Proof.

Consider
X
in the form of
X=V_{g}Q
, where

Q
is one orthogonal matrix. The condition of maximize
\langle Q,V_{g}^{\top}B\rangle
is the polar decomposition, i.e.,
Q^{\top}V_{g}^{\top}B
is symmetric and positive semidefinite (See
[
30
]
).
Since the lower bound in (
125
) is reached, we have the optimality of
X
.
Note that

(127)

is symmetric and
\Lambda\preceq d_{r}C
.
Hence,

\Lambda
can be expressed as the difference between two Hermitian matrices, and by

4.4
Complexity of SSM and outlook

Finally, we briefly analyze the computational cost of SSM, which is dominated by the SQP routine
to compute the SQP directions
Z
(Newton directions).
The SQP direction
Z
is the solution to the system characterized by the linearization of the first-order optimality conditions. Namely, within each iteration of our procedure, we compute the Lagrangian multipliers as well as the SQP update for
X
. As in Newton’s method for unconstrained problems, SQP-based methods necessitate the computation of inverse-vector products involving symmetric PSD linear systems.
Assume that by exploiting the sparsity of the graph Laplacian
L
, vector-vector and matrix-matrix multiplication can be done in linear time. The primary overhead of our method lies in the computation
Z
, which necessitates the calculation of the solution to a specific linear system in the subspace orthogonal to
\text{span}\{V_{g}\}
. We empirically show that this linear system is well-conditioned. In summary, SSM is an efficient tool for computing a qualified critical point of large-dimensional problems in (
2
).

Finding global minimizers in nonlinear optimization often presents significant challenges. In this study, we introduce a novel convex relaxation approach for quadratic minimization on Stiefel manifolds, which significantly improves the quality of solutions. It raises an interesting question: can similar results/algorithms be achieved in a broader context involving nonsmooth objectives? Specifically, we wonder if proximal algorithms
[
8
]
can be adapted for use on Stiefel manifolds. This topic will be explored in future research.

A.2

.

•
