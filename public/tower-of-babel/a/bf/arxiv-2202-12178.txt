Nonunitary Superconductivity in Complex Quantum Materials
Aline Ramires

Abstract
We present a comprehensive discussion on nonunitary superconductivity in complex quantum materials. Starting with a brief review of the notion of nonunitary superconductivity, we discuss its spectral signatures in simple models with only the spin as an internal degree of freedom. In complex materials with multiple internal degrees of freedom, there are many more possibilities for the development of nonunitary order parameters. We provide examples focusing on d-electron systems with two orbitals, applicable to a variety of materials. We discuss the consequences for the superconducting spectra, highlighting that gap openings of band crossings at finite energies can be attributed to a nonunitary order parameter if this is associated with a finite superconducting fitness matrix F ^ C ​ ( 𝕜 ) \hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}}) . We speculate that nonunitary superconductivity in complex quantum materials is in fact very common and can be associated with multiple cases of time-reversal symmetry breaking superconductors.

The presence of internal DOFs in the effective description of the electronic structure of these materials opens new possibilities for the internal structures of the superconducting order parameter. For simple superconductors, with only the spin as an internal DOF, the only possibilities for pairing are even-parity spin-singlet, or odd-parity spin-triplet states. In presence of one extra internal DOF, one can now have pairs that are symmetric or anti-symmetric with respect to this new DOF. In this paper, we propose a comprehensive discussion on nonunitary superconductivity in complex quantum materials. We start introducing the concept of superconducting fitness, which is going to be key in the later discussion of spectral signatures for both simple and complex scenarios. We briefly review the notion of nonunitary order parameters in simple superconductors, emphasizing that the development of nonunitary superconductivity is directly associated with symmetry-breaking order parameters. Without delving into the details of the pairing mechanism, we are going to discuss the spectral signatures of these order parameters. We highlight that in the context of simple superconductors, gap openings at high energy particle-hole band crossings are primarily associated with a non-zero superconducting fitness matrix, not with the nonunitary character of the order parameter. In the context of complex materials, with multiple internal DOFs, nonunitary order parameters can develop without extra symmetry breaking. We highlight some examples of nonunitary pairing in complex materials focusing on d-electron systems with two orbitals. We discuss the consequences for the superconducting spectrum in these more complex scenarios based on our refined understanding of nonunitary superconductivity in simple systems. We conclude providing a discussion that connects with multiple recent works, and we speculate on the connections between nonunitary and time-reversal symmetry breaking superconductors in complex materials.

We start introducing the effective Bogoliubov-de Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{BdG}=\sum_{\mathbb{k}}\Psi_{{\mathbb{k}}}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{BdG}({\mathbb{k}})\Psi_{{\mathbb{k}}},

(1)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{BdG}({\mathbb{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})&\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\\
\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})&-\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})\end{pmatrix},

(2)

and
\Psi_{{\mathbb{k}}}^{\dagger}=(\Phi_{\mathbb{k}}^{\dagger},\Phi_{\mathbb{k}}^{T})
is a Nambu spinor, where
\Phi_{\mathbb{k}}^{\dagger}=(c_{{\mathbb{k}}1\uparrow}^{\dagger},c_{{\mathbb{k}}1\downarrow}^{\dagger},c_{{\mathbb{k}}2\uparrow}^{\dagger},c_{{\mathbb{k}}2\downarrow}^{\dagger},...,c_{{\mathbb{k}}n\uparrow}^{\dagger},c_{{\mathbb{k}}n\downarrow}^{\dagger})
, and
c_{{\mathbb{k}}\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}
(
c_{{\mathbb{k}}\alpha\sigma}
) creates (annihilates) an electron with momentum
{\mathbb{k}}
and spin
\sigma
in the internal DOF
\alpha

\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
corresponds to the normal state Hamiltonian, and
\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})
encodes the order parameter, both are
2n\times 2n
matrixes in case the parameter
\alpha
can acquire
n

2.1
Superconducting Fitness and inter-band pairing

Superconductivity is usually discussed as an electronic instability out of a metallic state characterized by one or more bands. Even in the case of a single internal DOF, such as models with spinfull electrons on a single orbital, we can find multiple bands in presence of external symmetry breaking fields. In case of multiple internal DOFs, we naturally find multiple electronic bands, which can be doubly degenerate in presence of time-reversal and inversion symmetries. If pairing happens between electrons in the same band (intra-band pairing), superconductivity is stablished for an arbitrarily small attractive interaction through the Cooper instability with the formation of superconducting pairs of electrons with total zero momentum. If pairing happens between electrons in different bands (inter-band pairing), the superconducting state is not as stable since a finite attractive interaction is necessary. Based on these ideas, below we present an heuristic discussion and introduction of the concept of superconducting fitness
[
31
]
.

In presence of external symmetry breaking fields or multiple orbitals or sublattices, the normal state Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
is generally not diagonal in the microscopic basis (based on the orbital and sublattice DOFs). As the Hamiltonian is an Hermitian matrix, there is always a unitary transformation
\hat{U}({\mathbb{k}})
which diagonalizes it, or rotates it to the band basis:
\hat{H}^{B}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})=\hat{U}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})
(the superscript
B
stands for the band, or diagonal, basis). The gap matrix, by connecting particle-particle spaces, transforms in a slightly different manner:
\hat{\Delta}^{B}({\mathbb{k}})=\hat{U}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{T}(-{\mathbb{k}})
. In case of pure intra-band pairing
\Delta^{B}({\mathbb{k}})
is block diagonal. A non block-diagonal gap matrix is an indication of inter-band pairing.

To get some intuition on the origin of the concept of superconducting fitness, we consider the minimal multi-orbital problem consisting of two orbitals. In presence of time reversal and inversion symmetries,
\hat{H}_{0}^{B}({\mathbb{k}})
has doubly degenerate states with energy
\epsilon_{a}
, where
a
is the band label, and therefore has a structure with
2\times 2
blocks proportional to the identity
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
. Note that for an arbitrary gap matrix with intra-
\hat{\Delta}_{a}
and inter-
\hat{\Delta}_{ab}
band components, we have, omitting the momentum dependence:

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{B}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{1}\hat{\sigma}_{0}&0\\
0&\epsilon_{2}\hat{\sigma}_{0}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 14.22636pt\hat{\Delta}^{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\Delta}_{1}&\hat{\Delta}_{12}\\
\hat{\Delta}_{21}&\hat{\Delta}_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

Note that these matrices do not commute for finite inter-band pairing, unless the artificial condition
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}
is satisfied. On the other hand, in case
\hat{\Delta}_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})=0
,
\hat{\Delta}^{B}({\mathbb{k}})
is block diagonal and commutes with the bare Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{B}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
in the band basis.

We can now look at the condition for absence of inter-band pairing from the microscopic basis perspective. Using the unitary transformation introduced above and the fact that
\hat{H}^{B}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
and
\hat{\Delta}^{B}({\mathbb{k}})
commute in case of pure intra-band pairing, we can write:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}^{B}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}^{B}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}}),

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}^{B}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}^{B}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}^{B}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}^{B}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}^{B}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{T}(-{\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}^{B}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}}).

We can identify the first three factors in the last line with
\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})
. For the last three factors, we use inversion symmetry (already assumed above to guarantee the double degeneracy of the bands) and the fact that the eigenvalues of
\hat{H}_{0}^{B}({\mathbb{k}})
are real to write
\hat{H}^{*}_{0}(-{\mathbb{k}})=\hat{U}^{T}(-{\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}^{B}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{U}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})
, so we have:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})-\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}^{*}_{0}(-{\mathbb{k}})=0.

(5)

If
\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
and
\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})
satisfy this condition, the system develops only intra-band pairing and consequently has a robust superconducting instability. In case the identity above is not satisfied, we have a measure of the incompatibility between the superconducting state and the normal state, associated with the presence of inter-band pairing, which we label as the superconducting fitness matrix
\hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})
:

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})=\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})-\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}^{*}_{0}(-{\mathbb{k}}).

(6)

2.2
Superconducting Fitness and the critical temperature

A finite superconducting fitness matrix
\hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})
has been shown to be directly associated with the suppression of the superconducting critical temperature from its maximum possible value in single band superconductors
[
31
]
. For models with two orbitals or sublattices, the superconducting critical temperature can be explicitly written as
[
32
]
:

\displaystyle k_{B}T_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4e^{\gamma}}{\pi}\frac{\omega_{C}}{2}e^{-1/2|v|\alpha}e^{-\delta/\alpha},

(7)

where
\gamma
is the Euler constant,
\omega_{C}
is an energy cutoff,
|v|
is the magnitude of the effective attractive interaction on the superconducting channel of interest. In addition,

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{\omega_{C}^{2}}{32}\sum_{a}N_{a}(0)\left\langle\frac{Tr[|\hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]}{q({\mathbb{k}}_{Fa})^{2}}\right\rangle_{\!\!FS_{a}},

(8)

and

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{a}N_{a}(0)\langle Tr[|\hat{F}_{A}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}]\rangle_{FS_{a}},

(9)

where the sum over the index
a
runs over the bands with density of states at the Fermi surface
N_{a}(0)
, and
\langle...\rangle_{FS_{a}}
denotes the average over the respective Fermi surface. Here
q({\mathbb{k}})=\epsilon_{a}({\mathbb{k}})-\epsilon_{b}({\mathbb{k}})
is the energy difference between the two bands, which we assume are well separated,
q({\mathbb{k}})>>\omega_{C}
. The matrix inside the trace for the parameter
\alpha
is the anti-commutator counterpart of the first superconducting fitness matrix defined above:

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{A}({\mathbb{k}})=\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})+\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}^{*}_{0}(-{\mathbb{k}}).

(10)

From the closed form equation for the critical temperature, Eq.
7
, it is clear that the largest the parameter
\alpha
[so the larger
\hat{F}_{A}({\mathbb{k}})
], the largest the critical temperature, while a finite
\delta
[or a finite
\hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})
] suppresses the critical temperature from its potentially maximum value.

2.3
Superconducting Fitness and odd frequency correlations

Recently, the superconducting fitness measure
\hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})
was also associated with the presence of odd-frequency superconducting correlations
[
39
]
. This can be shown by manipulating the BdG Green’s function, defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{BdG}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})=\left[i\omega_{m}\hat{I}_{2n}-\hat{H}_{BdG}({\mathbb{k}})\right]^{-1},

(11)

where
\hat{I}_{2n}
is the
2n\times 2n
identity matrix, and
\omega_{m}
is a Matsubara frequency. This Green’s function can be broken down in particle and hole spaces, such that we can write:

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\begin{pmatrix}\hat{G}_{e}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})&\hat{F}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})\\
\hat{F}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})&\hat{G}_{h}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})\end{pmatrix}.\begin{pmatrix}\hat{I}_{n}i\omega_{m}-\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})&-\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\\
-\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})&\hat{I}_{n}i\omega_{m}+\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})\end{pmatrix}=\hat{I}_{2n}.

(12)

From the equation above we can extract two identities:

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{e}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})(\hat{I}_{n}i\omega_{m}-\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}}))-\hat{F}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})=\hat{I}_{n},

(13)

\displaystyle-\hat{G}_{e}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})+\hat{G}_{h}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})(\hat{I}_{n}i\omega_{m}+\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}}))=0.

(14)

Isotaling
\hat{G}_{e}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})
from the first equation, substituting it in the second and identifying
\hat{G}_{e}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})=(\hat{I}_{n}i\omega_{m}-\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}}))^{-1}
and
\hat{G}_{h}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})=(\hat{I}_{n}i\omega_{m}+\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}}))^{-1}
as the normal state particle and hole Green’s functions, we can write:

\displaystyle\hat{F}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})=\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})[\hat{I}_{n}-\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})]^{-1},

(15)

where we identified
\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})=\hat{G}_{e}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{G}_{h}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})
as the first order contribution in
\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})
to the anomalous correlation function
\hat{F}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})
.

Note that all the frequency dependence in
\hat{F}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})
appears through
\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})
, such that if the last is odd in frequency, the first should also be. This simplifies the discussion, as we can consider the simpler form of
\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})
to extract the condition for the presence of odd frequency anomalous correlations. Writing
\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})
explicity:

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})=(\hat{I}_{n}i\omega_{m}-\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}}))^{-1}\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})(\hat{I}_{n}i\omega_{m}+\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}}))^{-1},

(16)

we can make the first and last factors even in frequency by multiplying them by a convenient identity factor. After some manipulation we find:

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\hat{I}_{n}(i\omega_{m})^{2}-[\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})]^{2}\}^{-1}

(17)

\displaystyle\times[(i\omega_{m})^{2}\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})-\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})+i\omega_{m}\hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})]

\displaystyle\times\{\hat{I}_{n}(i\omega_{m})^{2}-[\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})]^{2}\}^{-1}.

From the last line, it is evident that the presence of odd frequency correlations is directly associated with a nonzero superconducting fitness matrix
\hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})
:

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\hat{F}^{0}_{odd}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},i\omega_{m})-\hat{F}^{0}({\mathbb{k}},-i\omega_{m})

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\omega_{m}\{\hat{I}_{n}(i\omega_{m})^{2}-[\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})]^{2}\}^{-1}\hat{F}_{C}({\mathbb{k}})\{\hat{I}_{n}(i\omega_{m})^{2}-[\hat{H}_{0}^{*}(-{\mathbb{k}})]^{2}\}^{-1}.

3
Superconductivity in simple metals

The normal state Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
, has the general form:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})=\epsilon({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\sigma}_{0}+{\mathbb{s}}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},

(19)

where
\epsilon({\mathbb{k}})
describes a doubly degenerate electronic dispersion in presence of time-reversal symmetry (TRS) and inversion symmetry (IS). The three-dimensional vector
{\mathbb{s}}({\mathbb{k}})
introduces time-reversal symmetry breaking (TRSB) as an external magnetic field, if
{\mathbb{s}}({\mathbb{k}})=-{\mathbb{b}}
, or inversion symmetry breaking (ISB) as odd spin-orbit coupling (SOC), if
{\mathbb{s}}({\mathbb{k}})={\mathbb{g}}({\mathbb{k}})=-{\mathbb{g}}(-{\mathbb{k}})
. Here
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
is the two-dimensional identity matrix and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=(\hat{\sigma}_{x},\hat{\sigma}_{y},\hat{\sigma}_{z})
is a three-dimensional vector of Pauli matrices.

The superconducting order parameter,
\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})
, can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})=[d_{0}({\mathbb{k}})+{\mathbb{d}}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}](i\hat{\sigma}_{2}).

(20)

Following fermionic antisymmetry, the order parameter matrix should satisfy
\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})=-\hat{\Delta}^{T}(-{\mathbb{k}})
. As a consequence,
d_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
is an even function of momenta, while
{\mathbb{d}}({\mathbb{k}})
is an odd function of momenta. Time-reversal symmetry is implemented as
\hat{\Theta}=(i\hat{\sigma}_{2})\mathcal{K}
, where
\mathcal{K}
stands for complex conjugation, and should be accompanied by the change in momenta
{\mathbb{k}}\rightarrow-{\mathbb{k}}
. This definition, when applied to the superconducting gap gives

\hat{\Delta}_{T}({\mathbb{k}})=\Theta\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\Theta^{-1}=d_{0}^{*}({\mathbb{k}})(i\sigma_{2})+{\mathbb{d}}^{*}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(i\sigma_{2})
,
such that if we choose the function
d_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
and all components of
{\mathbb{d}}({\mathbb{k}})
to have the same phase, the order parameter is time-reversal invariant [up to an overall
U(1)
gauge transformation].

A gauge-invariant composition of the order parameter with itself is

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})=\Delta^{2}_{U}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\sigma}_{0}+{\mathbb{q}}_{NU}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},

(21)

where

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}_{U}({\mathbb{k}})=\left[|d_{0}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}+|{\mathbb{d}}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}\right]

(22)

is the magnitude of the unitary part of the gap, and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{q}}_{NU}({\mathbb{k}})=\left[d_{0}({\mathbb{k}}){\mathbb{d}}^{*}({\mathbb{k}})+d_{0}^{*}({\mathbb{k}}){\mathbb{d}}({\mathbb{k}})+i{\mathbb{d}}({\mathbb{k}})\times{\mathbb{d}}^{*}({\mathbb{k}})\right],

(23)

is the vector that characterizes the nonunitary part of the gap. As a definition, the order parameter is unitary if
{\mathbb{q}}_{NU}({\mathbb{k}})=0
, and is nonunitary otherwise. From the explicit form of
{\mathbb{q}}_{NU}({\mathbb{k}})
, we can conclude that the order parameter is nonunitary if at least one of the following conditions are met: i) ISB in the normal state, allowing parity mixing in the superconducting state; ii) TRSB order parameter with
{\mathbb{d}}^{*}({\mathbb{k}})
not parallel to
{\mathbb{d}}({\mathbb{k}})
. Note that only in the later case the order parameter spontaneously breaks the mentioned symmetry. In the case of an ISB order parameter, inversion symmetry is already broken in the normal state, so it is not spontaneously broken in the superconducting state.

A nonunitary order parameter leads to a finite expectation value of
\langle\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\rangle
, which is associated with a finite spin polarization of the pair at a given
{\mathbb{k}}
. Note that the average over the Fermi surface can lead to a zero net spin polarization [as in the case of ISB in the normal state, since
d_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
is even and
{\mathbb{d}}({\mathbb{k}})
is odd in momenta]. This tells us that only a TRSB superconductor could sustain a finite average spin polarization of the pair.

In this regard, it is useful to refine the notion of nonunitary order parameters introducing the time-reversal-odd gauge-invariant product
[
6
]
:

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})-\hat{\Delta}_{T}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}_{T}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})={\mathbb{q}}_{TRO}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

(24)

where
\hat{\Delta}_{T}({\mathbb{k}})
is the time-reversed order parameter defined above, and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{q}}_{TRO}({\mathbb{k}})=2i{\mathbb{d}}({\mathbb{k}})\times{\mathbb{d}}^{*}({\mathbb{k}}).

(25)

If
{\mathbb{q}}_{TRO}({\mathbb{k}})\neq 0
the TRSB order parameter develops a spin polarization. It has recently been proposed that a finite
{\mathbb{q}}_{TRO}({\mathbb{k}})
, and not a finite
{\mathbb{q}}_{NU}({\mathbb{k}})
, should be taken as the definition of nonunitary pairing
[
6
]
.

where

Below we analyze three different senarios: unitary, nonunitary associated with ISB [
{\mathbb{q}}_{TRO}({\mathbb{k}})=0

Case I: Unitary order parameter

Let’s start with the simpler case with

\displaystyle[\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})]^{2}+\hat{\Delta}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\Delta}^{\dagger}({\mathbb{k}})

\displaystyle+2\epsilon({\mathbb{k}})\mathbf{s}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}.

The eigenvectors of the square of the BdG Hamiltonian are then

\displaystyle E^{2}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\epsilon({\mathbb{k}})\pm|\mathbf{s}({\mathbb{k}})|]^{2}+|\Delta_{U}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pm\sqrt{[\epsilon({\mathbb{k}})\pm|\mathbf{s}({\mathbb{k}})|]^{2}+|\Delta_{U}({\mathbb{k}})|^{2}}.

{\mathbb{g}}({\mathbb{k}})\cdot{\mathbb{b}}=0

5
Superconductivity in complex quantum materials

The discussion of nonunitary order parameters becomes much richer if we move to scenarios with more than one internal degree of freedom. The first non-trivial scenario appears considering models with two internal DOFs, which can be associated with two orbitals, or a sublattice structure. The BdG Hamiltonian for the two orbital scenario has the same form as the one displayed in Eq.
2
, with
n=2
.

The normal state Hamiltonian can be generally written as:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})=\sum_{ab}h_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})\hat{\tau}_{a}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{b},

(35)

where
h_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})
are real functions of momenta encoding all the information about hopping amplitudes and spin-orbit coupling (SOC).

and

Here we focus on d-electron systems, belonging to the EP scenario discussed above. The conclusions drawn here can be applied to multiple families of materials (ruthenates, pnictides, and transition metal dichalcogenides, to name a few), and can be easily generalized to scenarios of orbitals with OP or with a SL structure.

7
Signatures of nonunitary order parameters in the two orbital scenario

Now we translate some of the conclusions we found for the case of simple superconductors, carrying only the spin as an internal DOF, to the more complex scenario of superconductors with an extra orbital DOF.

In the normal state, in presence of inversion and time-reversal symmetries, the spectrum is constituted of two doubly-degenerate bands. The normal state spectrum in presence of TRS and IS can be obtained by diagonalizing
\hat{H}_{0}({\mathbb{k}})
, leading to:

\displaystyle\xi_{\pm}({\mathbb{k}})=h_{00}({\mathbb{k}})\pm|{\mathbb{h}}({\mathbb{k}})|,

(48)

where
{\mathbb{h}}({\mathbb{k}})

for
(a,b)\neq(0,0)

{\mathbb{s}}({\mathbb{k}})

\{d_{xz},d_{yz}\}
and point group
D_{2h}
. We write the parameters
h_{ab}({\mathbb{k}})
as expansions around the
\Gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{k}}|^{2}/(2m_{1})-\mu_{1},

\displaystyle\beta k_{x}k_{y},

\displaystyle h_{21}({\mathbb{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}k_{x}k_{z},

\displaystyle h_{22}({\mathbb{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}k_{y}k_{z},

\displaystyle h_{23}({\mathbb{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha,

\displaystyle h_{30}({\mathbb{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{k}}|^{2}/(2m_{2})-\mu_{2},

8
Conclusion

In this work we have revisited the notion of nonunitary order parameters. We have started with simple superconductors, emerging from electronic states with only the spin as an internal DOF. We highlight the fact that nonunitary superconducting states are necessarily associated with either ISB or TRSB of the superconducting order parameter and with the development of a two-gap structure. Furthermore, we discuss how external symmetry breaking fields in the normal state change the spectra in the superconducting state. In particular, we find that symmetry breaking terms in the normal state lead to crossings of the particle and hole bands at energies away from the Fermi energy, and that these crossings are inherited by the superconducting state in case the fitness matrix is zero,
\hat{F}_{c}({\mathbb{k}})=0
. Once the fitness matrix is nonzero,
\hat{F}_{c}({\mathbb{k}})\neq 0
, these crossings are lifted.

With this more refined understanding of the superconducting spectra in simple superconductors, we moved to the minimal scenario to treat complex superconductors, considering an extra internal DOF which can acquire two flavours. We find that there are many more possibilities to construct a nonunitary order parameter, some of which do not break any symmetry besides
U(1)
. Focusing on d-electron systems, we find that multiple point group symmetries allow for nonunitary order parameters in the trivial irreducible representation. Concerning the spectra, we find that particle-hole and particle-particle band crossings are protected for unitary order parameters with
\hat{F}_{c}({\mathbb{k}})=0
, but that the particle-hole crossings are lifted as soon as
\hat{F}_{c}({\mathbb{k}})\neq 0
. Considering nonunitary order parameters, the crossings are again protected for
\hat{F}_{c}({\mathbb{k}})=0
, but both particle-particle and particle-hole crossings are lifted as soon as
\hat{F}_{c}({\mathbb{k}})\neq 0
. In conclusion, the particle-hole crossings away from the Fermi energy are lifted as soon as
\hat{F}_{c}({\mathbb{k}})\neq 0
, and particle-particle crossings require both
\hat{F}_{c}({\mathbb{k}})\neq 0
and a nonunitary order parameter. The later type of gap opening can be used as an indicator of nonunitary superconductivity.

Nonunitary order parameters have been recently discussed in multiple contexts, motivated by materials characterization and theoretical investigations. It was proposed that one of the signatures of nonunitary order parameters is the opening of gaps away from the Fermi surface in Dirac material
[
30
]
. Previous work has associated these gap openings to a measure of odd-frequency pairing correlations in Ising
[
40
]
and multi-band superconductors
[
41
]
. The discussion in this manuscript comes as a reference to clarify how these spectral signatures proposed for the identification of nonunitary superconducting states and odd-frequency correlations are all tied to the same underlying concept of superconducting fitness.

An interesting perspective of this work is the general association of nonunitary order parameters with TRSB. In complex superconductors with multiple realizations for the order parameter within a single irreducible representation, a TRSB order parameter is possible within the trivial irreducible representation. The discussion of the stabilization this type of order parameter is left for future investigation.
