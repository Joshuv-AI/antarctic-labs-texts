Eigenvalue asymptotics for the one-particle kinetic energy density operator
Alexander V. Sobolev

Abstract
. The kinetic energy of a multi-particle system is described by the one-particle kinetic energy density matrix τ ⁡ ( x , y ) \tau(x,y) . Alongside the one-particle density matrix γ ⁡ ( x , y ) \gamma(x,y) it is one of the key objects in the quantum-mechanical approximation schemes. We prove the asymptotic formula λ k ∼ ( B ​ k ) − 2 \lambda_{k}\sim(Bk)^{-2} , B ≥ 0 B\geq 0 , as k → ∞ k\to\infty , for the eigenvalues λ k \lambda_{k} of the self-adjoint operator 𝗧 ≥ 0 \boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}\geq 0 with kernel τ ⁡ ( x , y ) \tau(x,y) .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Consider on
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
the Schrödinger operator

(1.1)

\displaystyle H=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigg(-\Delta_{k}-\frac{Z}{|x_{k}|}\bigg)+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|},

describing an atom with
N
charged particles
with coordinates
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N}),\,x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
k=1,2,\dots,N
,
and a nucleus with charge
Z>0
. The notation
\Delta_{k}
is used for
the Laplacian w.r.t. the variable
x_{k}
.
The operator
H
acts on the Hilbert space
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})

and it is self-adjoint on the domain

D(H)=\textup{{{W}}}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, since the potential in (
1.1
)
is an infinitesimal perturbation
relative to the unperturbed operator
-\Delta=-\sum_{k}\Delta_{k}
,
see e.g.
[
26
, Theorem X.16]
.
Let
\psi=\psi(\mathbf{x})

be an eigenfunction of the operator
H
with an eigenvalue
E\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
\psi\in D(H)
and

\displaystyle(H-E)\psi=0.

For each
j=1,\dots,N
, we represent

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},x_{j}),\quad\textup{where}\ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{N}),

with obvious modifications if
j=1
or
j=N
.
The
one-particle density matrix
is defined as the function

(1.2)

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\overline{\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},x)}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},y)\ d\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}.

This is a major object in quantum mechanics, see e.g.

[
12
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
.
Introduce also the function

(1.3)

\displaystyle\tau(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\overline{\nabla_{x}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},x)}\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},y)\ d\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},

that we call the
one-particle kinetic energy density matrix
.
The choice of this term does not seem to be standard, but it is partly
motivated by the fact that the trace

\displaystyle\tr\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\tau(x,x)\,dx

gives the kinetic energy of the
N
particles, see e.g.

[
13
, Section 2A]
,
[
23
, Chapter 3]
or

[
22
, Section 4]
.
Denote by
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}=\iop(\gamma)
and
\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}=\iop(\tau)
the integral operators
with kernels
\gamma(x,y)
and
\tau(x,y)
respectively.
We call
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}

the one-particle density operator
,
and
\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}
-
the one-particle kinetic energy density operator
.
If we assume that the particles are spinless fermions (resp. bosons), i.e. that the eigenfunction
is fully antisymmetric (resp. symmetric) with respect to the permutations
x_{k}\leftrightarrow x_{j}
,

j,k=1,2,\dots,N,j\not=k
,
then the formulas for
\gamma(x,y)
and
\tau(x,y)
take a more compact form:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\gamma(x,y)=N\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\overline{\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},y)\ d\hat{\mathbf{x}},\\[14.22636pt]
\tau(x,y)=N\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\overline{\nabla_{x}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)}\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},y)\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}},\end{cases}

where
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{N}
. In this paper, however,
we do not make these assumptions and
work with the general definitions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).
Since
\psi,\nabla\psi\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
,
both
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}
are trace class.
We are interested in the exact decay rate of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k},k=1,2,\dots,

for
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}
.
Both operators play a central
role in quantum chemistry computations of atomic and molecular bound states, see e.g. the papers

[
16
,
17
,
20
,
21
]
and the book
[
25
]
.
The knowledge of the eigenvalue behaviour should
serve to estimate the errors due to finite-dimensional
approximations, see e.g.

[
9
]
,

[
11
]
,
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
.

The eigenvalue asymptotics for the operator
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}
were studied in
[
29
]

under the assumption that the function
\psi
satisfies an exponential bound

(1.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}}e^{\varkappa|\mathbf{x}|_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152540}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152541}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle 1$}}}}}}}}\,|\psi(\mathbf{x})|<\infty

with some
\varkappa>0
, where
|\,\cdot\,|_{1}
denotes the
\textup{{{l}}}^{1}
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
.
This condition always holds if
E
is a discrete eigenvalue. For detailed discussion
and bibliography on the property (
1.5
) we refer to

[
27
]
.
It was shown in
[
29
]
that

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}k^{\frac{8}{3}}\,\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma})=A^{\frac{8}{3}}

with some coefficient
A\geq 0
, see also
[
9
]
and
[
10
]

for relevant quantum chemistry calculations.
In the author’s paper
[
30
]
the main ideas of the proof
were presented for the simplified variant of the density matrix, containing only one
term from the sum on the right-hand side of (
1.2
).

The subject of the current paper is to establish
for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}})
the formula

(1.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}k^{2}\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}})=B^{2},

with a coefficient
B\geq 0
. The precise statement, including the formula for
the coefficient
B
, requires further properties
of the function
\psi
and
it is given in Theorem
2.4
. Moreover, in Sect.
2
for comparison
we also provide a formula for the coefficient
A
.

1.2.
Outline of the proof

The proof follows the plan of
[
29
]
, and it
splits into three steps that are briefly summarized below.

Step 1: factorization.

First we represent the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}
as
the product
\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}=\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}^{*}\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
, where

\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}:\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\mapsto\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N-3};\mathbb{C}^{3N})
is a
suitable compact operator.
This representation implies that
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}})=s_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}})^{2}
,
k=1,2\dots,
,
where
s_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}})
are the singular values of the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
.
Therefore the asymptotic formula (
1.7
) takes the form

(1.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}k\,s_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}})=B.

If we take formula (
1.4
) as the definition of
\tau(x,y)

(which is the case for fermions or bosons), then the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
can be taken to be
the integral operator
acting from
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
into
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N-3};\mathbb{C}^{3})

with the vector-valued kernel
\sqrt{N}\,\nabla_{x}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)
, i.e.

(1.9)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}u)(\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\sqrt{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{x}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)u(x)dx,\ u\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

For the general case the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}

has a more complicated form and it is defined
in Subsect.
4.1
.
The factorization
\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}=\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}^{*}\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}

reduces the study of the asymptotics
to the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
which is convenient since the properties of
the functions
\psi
have been very well studied in the literature.
For the rest of the introduction we will assume for simplicity that
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
is given by
the formula (
1.9
).

For the sake of comparison we note that the operator
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}
also admits a factorization:
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{*}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
. Under the assumption that

\gamma(x,y)
is given by the formula (
1.4
) the
scalar operator
\boldsymbol{\Psi}:\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\mapsto\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N-3})
has the form

(1.10)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\Psi}u)(\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\sqrt{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)u(x)dx,\ u\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

This factorization was a key observation in the study of the operator
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}
in
[
29
]
.

Step 2: estimates for singular values.

The asymptotic analysis of the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
begins with effective bounds for the
singular values of
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
. By effective bounds we mean bounds for the
operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
with weights

a=a(x),x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
b=b(\hat{\mathbf{x}}),\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}
,
of the form

\displaystyle s_{k}(b\,\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}a)\leq C(a,b)k^{-1},

where the factor on the right-hand side depends explicitly on some
integral norms of
a
and
b
.
The precise statement is the subject of Theorem
5.1
.
The appropriate bounds for the operator
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
were derived in
[
28
]

using the following two ingredients:
global estimates for the derivatives of
\psi

obtained by S. Fournais and T.Ø. Sørensen in the recent paper
[
14
]
,
and
the results by
M.S. Birman and M.Z. Solomyak
[
5
]
on
estimates for singular values of
integral operators via norms of their kernels in certain

Sobolev
spaces.
Later the author realized that Sobolev spaces are not the most suitable ones
for these purposes. Indeed,
application of the bounds from
[
14
]

in the Sobolev spaces framework makes the proof in
[
28
]

quite long and unnaturally complicated.
In the current paper we observe that
the Fournais-Sørensen
bounds almost immediately imply that the kernel
\nabla_{x}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)

belongs to a certain
Besov

space. Thus a different group of estimates from
[
5
]
is applicable – the one that uses
the Besov spaces instead of the Sobolev spaces. This approach
makes the proof of the effective bounds for
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
much shorter and more direct.

The Fournais-Sørensen paper
[
14
]
is one of the most recent contributions to the
long history of regularity properties for the multi-particle Schrödinger equation.
We refer to
[
14
]
for further references to the vast bibliography on this subject.

Step 3: asymptotics.
This step follows the approach of
[
29
]
where the
asymptotic formula (
1.6
) was proved.
In order to obtain an
asymptotic formula for
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}

one needs to know the behaviour of the kernel
\nabla_{x}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)

near the coalescence points. An appropriate representation
for the function
\psi
was obtained
in
[
15
]
.
To simplify the explanation we assume that the system consists of two particles,
i.e. that
N=2
.
Under this assumption the problem retains all its crucial features,
but permits to avoid some tedious technical details. For
N=2
we have
\mathbf{x}=(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
and the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
as defined in (
1.9
)
acts from
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
into
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
According to
[
15
]
, there exists a neighbourhood

\Omega_{1,2}\subset\big(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}\big)\times\big(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}\big)
of the
diagonal set
\{(x,x):x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}\}

and two functions
\xi_{1,2},\eta_{1,2}
, real analytic in
\Omega_{1,2}
, such that
the eigenfunction
\psi=\psi(t,x)
admits the representation

(1.11)

\displaystyle\psi(t,x)=\xi_{1,2}(t,x)+|t-x|\,\eta_{1,2}(t,x),\quad\textup{for all}\quad(t,x)\in\Omega_{1,2},

and hence the kernel of the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
has the form
\sqrt{2}\,\nabla_{x}\psi(t,x)
,
where

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\psi(t,x)=

\displaystyle\ \nabla_{x}\xi_{1,2}(t,x)+|t-x|\,\nabla_{x}\eta_{1,2}(t,x)+\frac{x-t}{|x-t|}\eta_{1,2}(t,x),

(1.12)

\displaystyle\ \qquad\qquad\textup{for all}\quad(t,x)\in\Omega_{1,2}.

Each of the terms on the right-hand side
of (
1.2
)
gives a different contribution
to the asymptotics of the singular values. According to
[
5
]
, infinitely smooth kernels
lead to a decay rate which is faster than any negative power of the singular value number.
This allows us to say that the first term on the right-hand side of (
1.2
) gives a zero contribution.
The other two terms contain homogeneous factors of order one and zero respectively.
Spectral asymptotics for operators with homogeneous kernels were studied extensively by
M. Birman and M. Solomyak in
[
3
,
4
]
and
[
6
]
,
see also
[
5
]
. A summary of the results needed for our purposes here can be found
in
[
29
]
.

The homogeneity order one kernels played a central role in paper

[
29
]
in the study of the operator
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
defined in (
1.10
).
In this case the Birman-Solomyak theory led
to the asymptotics

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}k^{\frac{4}{3}}s_{k}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})=A^{\frac{4}{3}},

which, in its turn, implied (
1.6
).
In the current paper the decay of the singular values of the operator

\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
is determined by the term of homogeneity order zero in (
1.2
).
Precisely, according to the Birman-Solomyak results (see
[
29
]
for a summary),
kernels of order zero produce the asymptotics
of order
k^{-1}
, which agrees with (
1.8
). However, as in
[
29
]
,
the known formulas for integral operators with homogeneous kernels are not directly applicable,
since we have information neither on the smoothness of the functions
\xi_{1,2},\eta_{1,2}

on the closure

\overline{\Omega_{1,2}}
nor on the integrability of these functions and
their derivatives on
\Omega_{1,2}
.
To resolve this problem we
approximate
\xi_{1,2},\eta_{1,2}
by suitable

\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}
-functions supported inside
\Omega_{1,2}
.
The estimates obtained in Step 2 ensure that the induced error in the spectral asymptotics
tends to
zero as the smooth approximations converge.
In the limit this leads to the formula
(
1.8
) with the constant

\displaystyle B=\frac{4}{3\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|2^{1/2}\eta_{1,2}(x,x)|dx.

Interestingly,
the finiteness of the above integral is a by-product of the
spectral bounds.
We emphasize that the coalescence points
x=0
or
t=0
do not contribute to the asymptotics.

For
N\geq 3
the proof needs to be modified to incorporate representations of the form
(
1.11
) in the neighbourhood of all pair coalescence points

x_{j}=x_{k}
,
j,k=1,2,\dots,N
,
j\not=k
, see (
2.6
).
The kernel of the operator

\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
near the
coalescence points becomes more complex and as a result the proof requires an
extra step involving an
integral operator whose structure mimics that of the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
.
Such a model operator was studied in the author’s paper
[
29
]
. In order
to make it adaptable for the use in different situations, the model operator was allowed to have
arbitrary order of homogeneity at the pair coalescence points. For order one the model operator
was the key point in the analysis for
the operator
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
conducted in
[
29
]
. In the current paper we
use the same model operator but with homogeneity of order zero, which is relevant to the operator

\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
. The required results are collected in Subsect.
3.3
.
Using these facts together with
approximations of
\boldsymbol{{\sf{V}}}
similar to the ones in the case
N=2
,
we arrive at the asymptotics (
1.8
) with the coefficient
B
given in (
2.8
),
thereby concluding the proof.

1.4.
Notational conventions

We conclude the introduction with some general notational conventions.

Coordinates.

As mentioned earlier, we use the following standard notation for the coordinates:

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})
, where
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
j=1,2,\dots,N
.
The vector
\mathbf{x}
is often represented in the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},x_{j})\quad\textup{with}\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{3N-3},

for arbitrary
j=1,2,\dots,N
. Most frequently we use this notation with
j=N
, and
write
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{N}
, so that
\mathbf{x}=(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x_{N})
.
In order to write
formulas in a more compact and unified way, we sometimes use the notation

x_{0}=0
.

In the space
\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 1,

the notation
|x|
stands for the Euclidean norm.
Sometimes it is convenient to use the
\textup{{{l}}}^{1}
-norm of
x

which we denote by
|x|_{1}
.

For
N\geq 3
it is also useful to introduce the notation
for
\mathbf{x}
with
x_{j}
and
x_{k}
taken out:

(1.13)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k,j}=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,k}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{N}),\quad\textup{for}\ j<k.

If
j<k
, then we write
\mathbf{x}=(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,k},x_{j},x_{k})
. For any
j\leq N-1

the vector
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
can be represented as
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,N},x_{j})
.

The notation
B_{R}
is used for the Cartesian product

\bigtimes_{j=1}^{N}\{x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x_{j}|<R\}
.

Derivatives.

Let
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
If
x=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
m=(m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
, then
the derivative
\partial_{x}^{m}
is defined in the standard way:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{m}=\partial_{x^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}\partial_{x^{\prime\prime}}^{m^{\prime\prime}}\partial_{x^{\prime\prime\prime}}^{m^{\prime\prime\prime}}.

For any
l=0,1,2,\dots
, we use the notation

(1.14)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x}^{l}u|=\sum_{|m|=l}|\partial_{x}^{m}u|.

The symbols
\textup{{{W}}}^{l,p}(\Omega)
,
\textup{{{W}}}^{l,p}_{\textup{loc}}(\Omega)
,
l=0,1,\dots
,

1\leq p\leq\infty
,
stand for the standard Sobolev spaces on the open set
\Omega
.

Bounds.

For two non-negative numbers (or functions)

X
and
Y
depending on some parameters,
we write
X\lesssim Y
(or
Y\gtrsim X
) if
X\leq CY
with
some positive constant
C
independent of those parameters.
To avoid confusion we may comment on the nature of
(implicit) constants in the bounds.
We also use the notation
X\wedge Y=\min\{X,Y\}
.

Cut-off functions.

We systematically use the following smooth cut-off functions. Let

(1.15)

\displaystyle\theta,\zeta\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}([0,\infty)),\quad\zeta=1-\theta,

be functions such that
0\leq\theta\leq 1
and

(1.16)

\displaystyle\theta(t)=0,\quad\textup{if}\quad t>1;\ \quad\theta(t)=1,\quad\textup{if}\quad 0\leq t<\frac{1}{2}.\

Integral operators.

The notation
\iop(\mathcal{K})
is used for the integral operator with kernel
\mathcal{K}
,
e.g.
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}=\iop(\gamma)
.
The functional spaces, where
\iop(\mathcal{K})
acts are obvious from the context.

2.
Regularity of
\psi
, main result,
compact operators

2.1.
Regularity of the eigenfunction

Our argument relies on the following regularity properties of the eigenfunction
\psi
.
For convenience, along with the standard
notation
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}

below we use the notation
x_{0}=0
.
Thus, unless otherwise stated, the indices labeling the particles, run from
0
to
N
.
Denote the set

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\sf S}_{l,s}={\sf S}_{s,l}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}:x_{l}\not=x_{s}\},\ l\not=s.

Since the Coulomb potential is real analytic away from the origin,
by an elementary ellipticity argument (see e.g.
[
19
]
),
the eigenfunction
\psi
is real analytic away from the coalescence points, i.e. on the set

\displaystyle{\sf{U}}=\bigcap_{0\leq l<s\leq N}\,{\sf S}_{l,s}.

Apart from this qualitative fact we need explicit bounds for
\psi
and its derivatives
w.r.t.
x_{j}
,
j=1,2,\dots,N,
on the set

\displaystyle{\sf{\Pi}}_{j}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\not=j\\
0\leq l\leq N\end{subarray}}\,{\sf S}_{l,j},

which does not contain the points of coalescence of the variable
x_{j}
with the remaining ones,
but allows
x_{k}=x_{s}
for all
k\not=j,s\not\not=j
.
Such bounds were obtained
by S. Fournais and T.Ø. Sørensen in
[
14
]
. Let

\displaystyle\dc(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},x_{j})=\min\{1,|x_{j}-x_{k}|,\ 0\leq k\leq N,\,k\not=j\},\quad j=1,2,\dots,N.

The following proposition is a consequence of
[
14
, Corollary 1.2]
.
Recall that
|\,\cdot\,|_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{173727}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{173727}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152540}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152540}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle 1$}}}}}}}
denotes the
\textup{{{l}}}^{1}
-norm.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
\psi
satisfies
(
1.5
). Then for all
\mathbf{x}=(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},x_{j})\in{\sf{\Pi}}_{j}
,
j=1,2,\dots,N
,
we have the bound

(2.2)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x_{j}}^{m}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},x_{j})|\lesssim\big(1+\dc(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},x_{j})^{1-m}\big)e^{-\varkappa{|\mathbf{x}|_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152540}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152541}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle 1$}}}}}}}},\,\quad\textup{for all}\quad m=0,1,\dots,

where we have used the notation (

In order to state the main result we also need the formula for the function
\psi
describing its behaviour
in a neighbourhood of the pair coalescence points, obtained in
[

]
.
Along with the set
{\sf{U}}
, for each pair

, define the set

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\sf{U}}_{j,k}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\not=s\\
(l,s)\not=(j,k)\end{subarray}}{\sf S}_{l,s}.

It contains the coalescence point
x_{j}=x_{k}
,
whereas the other pairs of variables are separated from each other.
We are interested in the shape of function
\psi

near the diagonal

(2.4)

\displaystyle{\sf{U}}^{(\rm d)}_{j,k}=\{\mathbf{x}\in{\sf{U}}_{j,k}:x_{j}=x_{k}\}.

The sets introduced above are obviously symmetric with respect to permutations of
indices, e.g.
{\sf{U}}_{j,k}={\sf{U}}_{k,j}
,
{\sf{U}}^{(\rm d)}_{j,k}={\sf{U}}^{(\rm d)}_{k,j}
.

The following property follows from
[
15
, Theorem 1.4]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For each pair of indices
j,k=0,1,\dots,N
such that
j\not=k
, there exists
an open connected set
\Omega_{j,k}=\Omega_{k,j}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3N}
, such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle{\sf{U}}_{j,k}^{(d)}\subset\Omega_{j,k}\subset{\sf{U}}_{j,k},

and two uniquely defined functions

, both

\Omega_{j,k}
, such that for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{j,k}

the following representation holds:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x})=\xi_{j,k}(\mathbf{x})+|x_{j}-x_{k}|\eta_{j,k}(\mathbf{x}).

Because of the uniqueness of functions
\xi_{j,k},\eta_{j,k}
, they are
symmetric with respect to the permutations

, i.e.

\xi_{j,k}=\xi_{k,j}
,
\eta_{j,k}=\eta_{k,j}
for all
j\not=k
.

The asymptotic coefficient

in the formula (
1.7
) is defined via the functions

\eta_{j,k}
,

, on the sets (
2.4
).
Using the notation (

) we write the function

on

as

. Proposition

gives no information on the properties of
\xi_{j,k},\eta_{j,k}
on the closure

,
but while proving the asymptotic formula (

) we find the following integrability
property of

.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
N\geq 3
, then each function
\eta_{j,k}(\ \cdot\ ,x,x)
,

,
belongs to
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N-6})
a.e.

and
the function

(2.7)

\displaystyle H(x):=\bigg[2\sum\limits_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-6}}\big|\eta_{j,k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,k},x,x)\big|^{2}d\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,k}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}},

\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

If

, then the function

belongs to
\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Now we are in a position to state the main result of the paper.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose that the eigenfunction
\psi
satisfies the bound (
1.5
).
Then the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}),k=1,2,\dots,

of the operator
\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}
satisfy the asymptotic formula (

)
with the constant

\displaystyle B=\frac{4}{3\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}H(x)\,dx.

A few remarks are in order.

(1)

Theorem
2.4
extends to the case of a
molecule with several nuclei whose positions are fixed.
The modifications are straightforward.

(2)

The formula (
2.8
) shows that the spectral asymptotics
depend only on the behaviour of the eigenfunction
\psi
near the pair coalescence points

x_{j}=x_{k}
,
j,k=1,2,\dots,N
,
j\not=k
.
Neither the points

x_{j}=0,j=1,2,\dots,N
, nor
the coalescence points of higher orders (e.g.
x_{j}=x_{k}=x_{l}
with pair-wise distinct
j,k,l
)
contribute to the asymptotics (
1.7
).

(3)

For the sake of comparison we give here the formula for the coefficient

A
in the asymptotic formula (
1.6
) for the operator
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}
.
As shown in
[
29
, Theorem 2.2]
, the function
H
also belongs to
\textup{{{L}}}^{3/4}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

and

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{3}\bigg(\frac{2}{\pi}\bigg)^{\frac{5}{4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}H(x)^{\frac{3}{4}}\,dx.

Thus both asymptotic formulas (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) are determined only by
the functions
\eta_{j,k}
.

(4)

Both coefficients
A
and
B
may be equal to zero.
Indeed, if the particles
are spinless fermions, i.e. the eigenfunction
\psi

is fully antisymmetric,
then so are the functions
\xi_{j,k}
and
\eta_{j,k}
in
(
2.6
), and hence
\eta_{j,k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,k},x,x)=0
for
all
\mathbf{x}\in{\sf{U}}_{j,k}^{(\dc)}
, which means that
H(x)=0
, a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

If we introduce the spin, then the parity of the functions
\xi_{j,k}
and
\eta_{j,k}
depends
on the configuration formed by the pair
j,k
, see
[
18
, Subsect. 3.3]
. If
j
and
k

are in a
triplet
configuration, then

\xi_{j,k}
and
\eta_{j,k}
are antisymmetric with respect to the permutation
j\leftrightarrow k
and hence
\eta_{j,k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,k},x,x)=0
, as before.
If however,
j
and
k
form a
singlet
,
then
\xi_{j,k}
and
\eta_{j,k}
are
symmetric with respect to the permutation
j\leftrightarrow k
and

\eta_{j,k}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j,k},x,x)
is not identically zero on
{\sf{U}}_{j,k}^{(\dc)}
,
so both
A
and
B
are positive.
A somewhat more detailed discussion can be found in
[
29
, Remark 2.4]
.

For completeness one should say that a preliminary analysis shows that
in the totally antisymmetric case (i.e. when all pairs are triplets and hence

)
the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma}
and

\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}}
should satisfy the bounds

\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{\sf\Gamma})=O(k^{-10/3})
and
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\sf{T}}})=O(k^{-8/3})
. Proof of these
bounds will make part of another publication.

3.
Singular values of integral operators

In this section we collect some basic results on singular values of integral operators
that will be instrumental in the proof of the main Theorem 2.4.
The first such result is borrowed from
[
5
]
and it describes
the membership of integral operators
in classes
\mathbf{S}_{p,\infty}
with some
p>0
in terms of the smoothness of
their integral kernels.
More specifically, we need the bounds in

\mathbf{S}_{p,\infty}
under the assumption that the integral kernel
lies in a certain
Besov space
.
Thus we start with
basic facts about Besov
spaces. More details can be found
in
[
24
, Chapter 4]
,
[
8
, Sect. 5.3]
,

[
31
, Sect. V.5]
and
[
32
, Ch. 4]
.

3.1.
Besov spaces

For a function
u=u(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and arbitrary
l=0,1,2,\dots,

define the finite difference

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}^{(l)}u(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{l}(-1)^{j+l}{l\choose j}u(x+jh),

and the
\textup{{{L}}}^{q}
-modulus of smoothness of order

l
:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\omega_{q}^{(l)}(u;t)=\sup_{|h|\leq t}\|\Delta_{h}^{(l)}u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{q}},\quad t>0.

The function
u
is said to belong to the Besov space
\textup{{{B}}}^{s}_{q,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

s>0
,
q\in[1,\infty]
, if
for some
l>s
we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\textup{{{B}}}^{s}_{q,\infty}}:=\|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{q}}+\sup_{t>0}t^{-s}\omega_{q}^{(l)}(u;t)<\infty.

This quantity defines a norm on

\textup{{{B}}}^{s}_{q,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Different values of
l>s
lead to equivalent norms (
3.3
),
see
[
8
, Sect. 5.3]
.
The space
\textup{{{B}}}^{s}_{q,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is just one representative of the whole scale
of Besov spaces
\textup{{{B}}}^{s}_{q,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
0<r\leq\infty
. In this paper we need only

r=\infty
.
Following
[
24
, Chapter 4]
we use for the space
\textup{{{B}}}^{s}_{q,\infty}

the notation
\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}
and write

\|u\|_{\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}}
for the norm (
3.3
). Sometimes in the literature this space is called
the
Nikol’skii space
, see e.g.
[
5
]
.

Note that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}^{(l)}u(x)=\Delta_{h}^{(l-m)}(\Delta_{h}^{(m)}u)(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{l-m}(-1)^{j+l-m}{l-m\choose j}\Delta^{(m)}_{h}u(x+jh),

for all
m<l
.
Thus, under the assumption

u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{l,1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
iterating the identity

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}^{(1)}u(x)=u(x+h)-u(x)=\int_{0}^{1}h\cdot\nabla u(x+sh)ds,

we obtain the formula

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}^{(l)}u(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}(h\cdot\nabla)^{l}u\big(x+\sum_{j=1}^{l}s_{j}h\big)\,ds_{1}ds_{2}\dots ds_{l}.

For a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
the space
\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}(\Omega)
is defined as the restriction
of
\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\Omega
. The corresponding norm of the function

u\in\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}(\Omega)
is defined as

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}(\Omega)}=\inf\|g\|_{\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where the infimum is taken over all functions
g\in\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such
that
u=g
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
. In other words, a function
u
belongs to

\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}(\Omega)
if it has an extension
g\in\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Here we have adopted the definitions from
[
32
, Sect. 4.2.1]
.
They will be sufficient for our purposes.

There are also intrinsic (i.e. not based on restriction) definitions of the Nikol’skii and Besov
spaces on domains, see for example
[
24
, Sect. 4.3]
or
[
1
, Section 7.32]
.
For domains
with “good” extension properties the intrinsic and restriction definitions are equivalent, see

[
1
, Sect. 7.32]
.
The collection of such domains includes
bounded domains satisfying the cone condition (see
[
32
, Sect. 4.2.3]
),
and in particular, bounded Lipschitz domains.

We are interested in functions that fall in the appropriate spaces
\textup{{{H}}}^{s}_{q}
in a natural way.
Let

, where

,

and some

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x}^{j}u(x)|\lesssim A\big(1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(|x-a_{k}|\wedge 1\big)^{\alpha-j}\big),\quad\textup{for all}\quad x\notin X\quad\textup{and}\quad j=0,1,\dots,

where, as explained in the Introduction,

for any two non-negative

A good example that “almost” satisfies this condition
with

functions

such that

Under the assumption

at the points

3.2.

Proposition 3.3

As the next step towards the kernel (

\theta\big(|x-x_{k}|\varepsilon^{-1}\big)

In view of (

\displaystyle Q_{R}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})Y_{\delta}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}){\sf{v}}_{j}(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)K_{R}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\phi_{j,k}[\delta,R,\varepsilon](\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)+\rho_{j}[\delta,R,\varepsilon](\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)

\displaystyle\phi_{j,k}[\delta,R,\varepsilon](\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)=

Lemma 5.3
.

For each

and

\displaystyle{\sf{G}}_{1}\big(\iop\big(\rho_{j}[\delta,R,\varepsilon]\big)\big)=0,\ j=1,2,\dots,N.

Proof.

By the definitions (

) and (

\displaystyle{\bigcap_{0\leq l<s\leq N}{\sf S}_{l,s}(\varepsilon/2)\cap\,B_{R}}.

By Proposition

\iop(\rho_{j}[\delta,\mathbb{R},\varepsilon])\in\mathbf{S}_{p,\infty}

Lemma 5.4
.

For each
\delta>0,R>0

(5.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sf{G}}_{1}\big(\iop\big(\phi_{j,0}[\delta,R,\varepsilon]\big)\big)=0,\ j=1,2,\dots,N.

\displaystyle\phi_{j,0}[\delta,R,\varepsilon](\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)=Q_{R}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})Y_{\delta}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}){\sf{v}}_{j}(\hat{\mathbf{x}},x)\theta\big(|x|\varepsilon^{-1}\big)K_{R}(x).

Denote by

) and (

6.
Appendix

In this appendix we prove an elementary extension result for Sobolev spaces.
We consider spaces of functions that depend either
on one variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
or on two variables
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{l}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Denote
K=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{l}:|t|<1\}
,
B=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|<1\}
and

B_{0}=B\setminus\{0\}
. In Section
3
(see Remark
3.2
)
we need only the case of one variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The following lemma however holds for
the more general case of two variables
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{l}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
as well,
which may come in handy in different
circumstances.

Our objective is to establish the following elementary extension result.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let the dimension
l
be arbitrary, let

d\geq 2
,
m\geq 1
, and

p\in[d(d-1)^{-1},\infty]
. Then

\textup{{{W}}}^{m,p}(B_{0})=\textup{{{W}}}^{m,p}(B)
and
\textup{{{W}}}^{m,p}\big(K\times B_{0}\big)=\textup{{{W}}}^{m,p}\big(K\times B\big)
.

Proof.

We prove only the second equality since the first one is obtained in the same way.
It suffices to show that
\textup{{{W}}}^{1,p}\big(K\times B_{0}\big)\subset\textup{{{W}}}^{1,p}\big(K\times B\big)
.
Let
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,p}\big(K\times B_{0}\big)
.
We show that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d+l}
,
|j|=1
, and all test
functions
\phi\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}(K\times B)
the following identity holds:

(6.1)

\displaystyle\int u\,\partial^{j}\phi\,dtdx=-\int\partial^{j}u\,\phi\,dtdx,

where by
\partial^{j}u
we denote the distributional
derivatives of
u
on the set
K\times B_{0}
.
Let
\theta
be the function as defined in (
1.15
) and (
1.16
), and introduce

\theta_{\varepsilon}(x)=\xi(|x|\varepsilon^{-1})
. Then

\displaystyle\int u\,\partial^{j}\phi\,dtdx=\int u\,\partial^{j}\big(\phi\,(1-\theta_{\varepsilon})\big)\,dtdx+\int u\,\partial^{j}\big(\phi\,\theta_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dtdx.

Since
1-\theta_{\varepsilon}(x)=0
for
|x|<\varepsilon/2
, in the first integral
on the right-hand side we can integrate by parts:

\displaystyle\int u\,\partial^{j}\phi\,dtdx=

\displaystyle\ -\int\partial^{j}\,u\,\phi\,(1-\theta_{\varepsilon})\,dtdx+\int u\,\partial^{j}\big(\phi\,\theta_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dtdx

(6.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ -\int\partial^{j}\,u\,\phi\,dtdx+\int\partial^{j}\,u\,\phi\,\theta_{\varepsilon}\,dtdx+\int u\,\partial^{j}\big(\phi\,\theta_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dtdx.

Further proof is conducted for
p<\infty
only.
The modifications for the case
p=\infty
are obvious.
By Hölder’s inequality, the second term on the right-hand side of
(
6
) estimates as follows:

\displaystyle\bigg|\int\partial^{j}\,u\,\phi\,\theta_{\varepsilon}\,dtdx\bigg|\leq\bigg[\int\limits_{|x|<\varepsilon}|\partial^{j}u|^{p}\,dtdx\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\bigg[\int\limits_{|x|<\varepsilon}\,dtdx\bigg]^{\frac{1}{q}},

where
q^{-1}=1-p^{-1}
. Thus the right-hand side does not exceed

(6.3)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{d}{q}}\bigg[\int\limits_{|x|<\varepsilon}|\partial^{j}u|^{p}\,dtdx\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}\to 0,\quad\textup{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

Consider the third integral on the right-hand side of (
6
).
By Hölder’s inequality
again,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int u\,\partial^{j}\big(\phi\,\theta_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dtdx\bigg|\leq\bigg[\int\limits_{|x|<\varepsilon}|u|^{p}\,dtdx\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\max|\partial^{j}(\phi\,\theta_{\varepsilon})|\,\bigg[\int\limits_{|x|<\varepsilon}\,dtdx\bigg]^{\frac{1}{q}}.

As
\max|\partial^{j}(\phi\theta_{\varepsilon})|\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
, and
q=(1-p^{-1})^{-1}\leq d
,
the right-hand side does not exceed

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{d}{q}-1}\bigg[\int\limits_{|x|<\varepsilon}|u|^{p}\,dtdx\bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}\to 0,\quad\textup{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

Together with (
6.3
) this implies that the last two terms on the right-hand side
of (
6
) tend to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This entails
(
6.1
), as required. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎
