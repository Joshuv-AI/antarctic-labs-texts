Primal subgradient methods with predefined stepsizes
Yu. Nesterov

Abstract
In this paper, we suggest a new framework for analyzing primal subgradient methods for nonsmooth convex optimization problems. We show that the classical step-size rules, based on normalization of subgradient, or on the knowledge of optimal value of the objective function, need corrections when they are applied to optimization problems with constraints. Their proper modifications allow a significant acceleration of these schemes when the objective function has favorable properties (smoothness, strong convexity). We show how the new methods can be used for solving optmization problems with functional constraints with possibility to approximate the optimal Lagrange multipliers. One of our primal-dual methods works also for unbounded feasible set.



Keywords:
Convex optimization, nonsmooth optimization, subgradient methods, constrained problems, optimal Lagrange multipliers.

1
Introduction

Motivation.
The first method for unconstrained minimization of nonsmooth convex function was proposed in
[
8
]
. This was a primal subgradient method

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{k+1}&=&x_{k}-h_{k}d_{k},\quad d_{k}\;=\;f^{\prime}(x_{k}),\quad k\geq 0,\end{array}

(1.1)

with constant step sizes
h_{k}\equiv h>0
, where
f^{\prime}(x_{k})
is a subgradient of the objective function at point
x_{k}
. In the next years, there were developed several strategies for choosing the steps (see
[
1
]
for historical remarks and references). Among them, the most important one is the rule of the
first-order
divergent series:

\begin{array}[]{c}h_{k}>0,\quad h_{k}\to 0,\quad\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}h_{k}=\infty,\end{array}

(1.2)

with the optimal choice
h_{k}=O(k^{-1/2})
. As a variant, it is possible to use in (
2.9
) the normalized directions

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{k}&=&{f^{\prime}(x_{k})\over\|f^{\prime}(x_{k})\|}.\end{array}

(1.3)

Another alternative for the step sizes is based on the known optimal value
f^{*}

[
1
]
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}h_{k}&=&{f(x_{k})-f^{*}\over\|f^{\prime}(x_{k})\|^{2}}.\end{array}

(1.4)

In both cases, the corresponding schemes, as applied to functions with bounded subgradients, have the optimal rate of convergence
O(k^{-1/2})
, established for the best value of the objective function observed during the minimization process
[
3
]
. The presence of simple set constraints was treated just by applying to the minimization sequence an Euclidean projection onto the feasible set.

The next important advancement in this area is related to development of
mirror descent method
(MDM) (
[
3
]
, see also
[
2
]
). In this scheme, the main information is accumulated in the dual space in the form of aggregated subgradients. For defining the next test point, this object is mapped (
mirrored
) to the primal space by a special prox-function, related to a general norm.
Thus, we get an important possibility of describing topology of convex sets by the appropriate norms.

After this discovery, during several decades, the research activity in this field was concentrated on the development of dual schemes. One of the drawbacks of the classical MDM is that the new subgradients are accumulated with the vanishing weights
h_{k}
. It was corrected in the framework of
dual averaging

[
5
]
, where the aggregation coefficients can be even increasing, and the convergence in the primal space is achieved by applying some vanishing scaling coefficients.
Another drawback is related to the fact that the convergence guarantees are traditionally established only for the best values of the objective function. This inconvenience was eliminated by development of
quasi-monotone dual methods

[
6
]
, where the rate of convergence is proved for all points of the minimization sequence.

Thus, at some moment, primal methods were almost forgotten. However, in this paper we are going to show that in some situations the primal schemes are very useful. Moreover, there is still space for improvement of the classical methods. Our optimism is supported by the following observations.

Firstly, from the recent developments in Optimization Theory, it becomes clear that the size of subgradients of objective functions for the problems with simple set constraints must be defined differently. Hence, the usual norms in the rules (
1.3
) and
1.4
) can be replaced by more appropriate objects.

Secondly, for an important class of
quasi-convex functions
, linear approximations do not work properly. Hence, for corresponding optimization problems, only the primal schemes can be used.
Finally, as we will see, the proper primal schemes provide us with a very simple and natural possibility for approximating the optimal Lagrange multipliers for problems with functional constraints, eliminating somehow the heavy machinery, which is typical for the methods based on Augmented Lagrangian.

Contents.
In Section
2
, we present a new subgradient method for minimizing quasi-convex function on a simple set. Its justification is based on a new concept of
directional proximity measure
, which is a generalization of the old technique initially presented in
[
4
]
. In this method, we apply and
indirect strategy
for choosing the step size, which needs solution of a simple univariate equation.
In unconstrained case, this strategy is reduced to the normalization step (
1.3
). The main advantage of the new method is a possibility of automatic acceleration for functions with Hölder-continuous gradients.

In Section
3
, we present a method for solving composite minimization problem with max-type objective function. For choosing the step size, we use a proper generalization of the rule (
1.4
), based on an optimal value of the objective. This method admits a linear rate of convergence for smooth strongly convex functions (see Section
4
). Note that a simple example demonstrates that the classical rule does not benefit from strong convexity. Method of Section
3
automatically accelerates on functions with Hölder continuous gradient.

In Sections
5
, we consider a minimization problem with single max-type constraint containing an additive composite term. For this problem, we apply a swithching strategy, where the steps for the objective function are based on the rule of Section
2
, and for improving the feasibility we use the step size strategy of Section
3
. For controlling the step sizes, we suggest a new rule of the
second-order divergent series
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau_{k}&\geq&\tau_{k+1}\;>\;0,\quad\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\tau_{k}^{2}\;=\;\infty.\end{array}

For the bounded feasible sets, it eliminates an unpleasant logarithmic factors in the convergence rate. The method automatically accelerates for the problem with smooth functional components. It is interesting that the rates of convergence for the objective function and the constraints can be different.

The remaining sections of the paper are devoted to the methods, which can approximate optimal Lagrange multipliers for convex problems with functional inequality constraints. In Section
6
, we consider simplest swithcing strategy of this type, where for the steps with the objective function we use the rule of Section
2
, and for the steps with violated constraints we use the rule of Section
3
. In the method of Section
7
, both steps are based the rule of Section
2
.
In both cases, we obtain the rates of convergence for infeasibility of the generated points, and the upper bound for the duality gap, computed for the simple estimates of the optimal dual multipliers. Such an estimate is formed as a sum of steps at active iterations for each violated constraint divided by the sum of steps at iterations when the objective function was active.

In Section
8
, we provide the theoretical guarantees for our estimates of the optimal Lagrange multipliers in terms of value of the dual function. They depend on the depth of Slater condition of our problem. Finally, in Section
9
, we present a swithching method, which can generate the approximate dual multipliers for problems with unbounded feasible set.

Notation.

Denote by
\mathbb{E}
a finite-dimensional real vector space,
and by
\mathbb{E}^{*}
its dual space composed by linear functions
on
\mathbb{E}
. For such a function
s\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
, denote by

\langle s,x\rangle
its value at
x\in\mathbb{E}
.
For measuring distances in
\mathbb{E}
, we use an arbitrary norm
\|\cdot\|
. The corresponding dual norm is defined in a standard way:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|g\|_{*}\;=\;\max\limits_{x\in\mathbb{E}}\Big\{\;\langle g,x\rangle,\;\|x\|\leq 1\;\Big\},\quad g\in\mathbb{E}^{*}.\end{array}

Sometimes it is convenient to measure distances in
\mathbb{E}
by Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{B}
. It is defined by a self-adjoint positive-definite linear operator
B:\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{E}^{*}
in the following way:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|x\|_{B}&=&\langle Bx,x\rangle^{1/2},\;x\in\mathbb{E},\quad\|g\|^{*}_{B}\;=\;\langle g,B^{-1}g\rangle^{1/2},\;g\in\mathbb{E}^{*}.\end{array}

(1.5)

In case of
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we use notation
\|x\|^{2}_{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(x^{(i)})^{2}
.

For a differentiable function
f(\cdot)
with convex and open domain
{\rm dom\,}f\subseteq\mathbb{E}
, denote by
\nabla f(x)\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
its gradient at point
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
. If
f
is convex, it can be used for defining the
Bregman distance
between two points
x,y\in{\rm dom\,}f
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{f}(x,y)&=&f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\;\geq\;0.\end{array}

(1.6)

In this paper, we develop new
proximal-gradient methods
based on a predefined
prox-function

d(\cdot)
, which can be restricted to a convex open domain
{\rm dom\,}d\subseteq\mathbb{E}
. This domain always contains the basic feasible set of the corresponding optimization problem. We assume that
d(\cdot)
is continuously differentiable and
strongly convex
on
{\rm dom\,}d
with parameter one:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d(y)&\geq&d(x)+\langle\nabla d(x),y-x\rangle+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|y-x\|^{2},\quad x,y\in{\rm dom\,}d.\end{array}

(1.7)

Thus, combining the definition (
1.6
) with inequality (
1.7
), we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(x,y)&\geq&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x-y\|^{2},\quad x,y\in{\rm dom\,}d.\end{array}

(1.8)

2
Subgradient method for quasi-convex problems

Consider the following constrained optimization problem:

\min\limits_{x\in Q}\;f_{0}(x),

(2.1)

where function
f_{0}(\cdot)
is closed and quasi-convex on
{\rm dom\,}f_{0}
and the set
Q\subseteq{\rm dom\,}f_{0}
is closed and convex. Denote by
x^{*}
one of the optimal solutions of (
2.1
) and let
f_{0}^{*}=f_{0}(x^{*})
. We assume that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x^{*}&\in&{\rm int\,}{\rm dom\,}f_{0}.\end{array}

(2.2)

Let us assume that at any point
x\in Q
it is possible to compute a vector
f_{0}^{\prime}(x)\in\mathbb{E}^{*}\setminus\{0\}
, satisfying the following condition: for any
y\in\mathbb{E}
, we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x),y-x\rangle\geq 0&\Rightarrow&f_{0}(y)\;\geq\;f_{0}(x).\end{array}

(2.3)

(If
y\not\in{\rm dom\,}f_{0}
, then
f_{0}(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}+\infty
.) If
f_{0}(\cdot)
is differentiable at
x
, then
f^{\prime}_{0}(x)=\nabla f_{0}(x)
.

In order to justify the rate of convergence of our scheme, we need the following characteristic of problem (
2.1
):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu(r)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{E}}\Big\{f_{0}(x)-f_{0}^{*}:\;\|x-x^{*}\|\leq r\Big\},\quad r\geq 0.\end{array}

(2.4)

If
y\not\in{\rm dom\,}f_{0}
and
r=\|y-x^{*}\|
, then
\mu(r)=+\infty
. In view of assumption (
2.2
), this function is finite at least in some neighborhood of the point
r=0
.

We say that vector
d\in\mathbb{E}
defines a
direction
in
\mathbb{E}
if
\|d\|=1
. If
\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x),d\rangle>0
, we call it a
recession direction
of function
f_{0}(\cdot)
at point
x\in Q
. Using such a direction, we can define the
directional proximity measure
of point
x\in Q
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\delta_{d}(x)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&{\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x),x-x^{*}\rangle\over\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x),d\rangle}\;\geq\;0.\end{array}

(2.5)

Lemma 1

Let
d
be a recession direction at point
x\in Q
. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{0}(x)-f_{0}^{*}&\leq&\mu(\delta_{d}(x)).\end{array}

(2.6)

Proof:

Indeed, let us define
y=x^{*}+\delta_{d}(x)d
. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x),y\rangle&=&\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x),x^{*}\rangle+\delta_{d}(x)\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x),d\rangle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-PM})}}{{=}}\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x),x\rangle.\end{array}

Since
f_{0}(\cdot)
is quasi-convex, this means that
f_{0}(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Grad})}}{{\geq}}f_{0}(x)
. Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{0}(x)-f_{0}^{*}&\leq&f_{0}(y)-f_{0}^{*}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Grow})}}{{\leq}}\;\mu(\|y-x^{*}\|)\;=\;\mu(\delta_{d}(x)).\hskip 21.52771pt\Box\end{array}

In our analysis, we use the following univariate functions:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)&=&\max\limits_{x\in Q}\Big\{\lambda\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(\bar{x}),\bar{x}-x\rangle-\beta_{d}(\bar{x},x)\Big\},\quad\lambda\geq 0,\end{array}

(2.7)

where
\bar{x}\in Q
. The optimal solution of this optimization problem is denoted by
T_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)
. Note that function
\varphi_{\bar{x}}(\cdot)
is convex and continuously differentiable with the following derivative:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi_{\bar{x}}^{\prime}(\lambda)&=&\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(\bar{x}),\bar{x}-T_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)\rangle.\end{array}

(2.8)

Thus,
\varphi_{\bar{x}}(0)=0
,
T_{\bar{x}}(0)=\bar{x}
, and
\varphi^{\prime}_{\bar{x}}(0)=0
. Consequently,
\varphi_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)\geq 0
for all
\lambda\geq 0
.

The basic subgradient method for solving the problem (
2.1
) looks as follows.

\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\\
\mbox{\bf Basic Subgradient Method}\\
\\
\hline\cr\\
\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{{\bf Initialization.} Choose $x_{0}\in Q$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf$k$th iteration ($k\geq 0$).} a) Choose step-size parameter $\lambda_{k}>0$.}\\
\\
\mbox{b) Compute $x_{k+1}=T_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\arg\min\limits_{x\in Q}\Big\{\lambda_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x\rangle+\beta(x_{k},x)\Big\}$.}\\
\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(2.9)

Lemma 2

At each iteration
k\geq 0
of method (
2.9
), for any
x\in Q
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(x_{k+1},x)&\leq&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)-\lambda_{k}\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x\rangle+\varphi_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k}).\end{array}

(2.10)

Proof:

Note that the first-order optimality condition for the problem at Step b) can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\lambda_{k}f_{0}^{\prime}(x_{k})+\nabla d(x_{k+1})-\nabla d(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle&\geq&0,\quad\forall x\in Q.\end{array}

(2.11)

Therefore, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\beta_{d}(x_{k+1},x)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Breg})}}{{=}}\;d(x)-d(x_{k+1})-\langle\nabla d(x_{k+1}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle\\
\\
=&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)+d(x_{k})+\langle\nabla d(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\rangle-d(x_{k+1})-\langle\nabla d(x_{k+1}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle\\
\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-COpt})}}{{\leq}}&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)+d(x_{k})+\langle\nabla d(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\rangle-d(x_{k+1})\\
\\
&+\lambda_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle-\langle\nabla d(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle\\
\\
=&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)+\lambda_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle-\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})\\
\\
=&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)-\lambda_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x\rangle+\varphi_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k}).\hfill\Box\end{array}

In our method, we will use an
indirect control
of the
dual
step sizes
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, which is based on a predefined sequence of
primal
step-size parameters
\{h_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. As we will prove by Lemma
3
, the convergence of the process can be derived, for example, from the following standard conditions:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\{h_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}:\quad\mbox{a) }h_{k}>0,\quad\mbox{b) }\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}h_{k}=\infty.\end{array}

(2.12)

Then, inequality (
2.10
) justifies the choice of the
dual
step-size parameter
\lambda_{k}
as a solution to the following equation:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})&=&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2},\quad k\geq 0\end{array}

(2.13)

Example 1

If
Q=\mathbb{E}
and
d(x)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x\|^{2}_{B}
, then

\varphi_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Phi})}}{{=}}{\lambda^{2}\over 2}(\|f^{\prime}_{0}(\bar{x})\|^{*}_{B})^{2}
, and equation (
2.13
) gives us
\lambda_{k}=h_{k}/\|f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k})\|^{*}_{B}
. In this case, method (
2.9
) coincides with the classical variant of subgradient method
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-h_{k}{f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k})\over\|f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k})\|^{*}_{B}}
. However, for
Q\neq\mathbb{E}
, the rule (
2.13
) allows a proper scaling of the step size by the boundary of feasible set.
\Box

At each iteration of method (
2.9
), the corresponding value of
\lambda_{k}
can be found by an efficient one-dimensional search procedure, based, for example, on a Newton-type scheme. Since the latter scheme has local quadratic convergence, we make a plausible assumption that it is possible to compute an exact solution of equation (
2.13
). This solution has the following important interpretation (in view of (
2.8
)):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{k}&=&\max\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\Big\{\lambda:\;\varphi_{x_{k}}(\lambda)\leq\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}\Big\}.\end{array}

(2.14)

At the same time, we have

\langle\lambda_{k}f_{0}^{\prime}(x_{k})+\nabla d(x_{k+1})-\nabla d(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-COpt})}}{{\geq}}0
.
Thus

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Breg})}}{{\geq}}&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})+\beta_{d}(x_{k+1},x_{k})\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BGrow})}}{{\geq}}&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}.\end{array}

Hence

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Cond})}}{{=}}&\varphi_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Phi})}}{{\geq}}\;\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2},\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

(2.15)

Our complexity bounds follow from the rate of convergence of the following values:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\delta_{k}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&\delta_{d_{k}}(x_{k}),\quad d_{k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;{x_{k}-x_{k+1}\over\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|}.\end{array}

Lemma 3

Let condition (
2.13
) be satisfied. Then for any
N\geq 0
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}h_{k}\delta_{k}&\leq&\beta_{d}(x_{0},x^{*})+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}h_{k}^{2}.\end{array}

(2.16)

Proof:

Indeed, in view of inequality (
2.10
), for
r_{k}=\beta_{d}(x_{k},x^{*})
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}r_{k+1}&\leq&r_{k}-\lambda_{k}\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\varphi_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Cond})}}{{=}}\;r_{k}-\lambda_{k}\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}.\end{array}

On the other hand,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{k}\langle f_{0}^{\prime}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Cond})}}{{=}}&\beta(x_{k},x_{k+1})+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BGrow})}}{{\geq}}\;\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}\\
\\
&\geq&h_{k}\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|.\end{array}

Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}r_{k+1}&\leq&r_{k}-h_{k}\delta_{k}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}.\end{array}

(2.17)

Summing up these inequalities for
k=0,\dots,N
, we obtain inequality (
2.16
).

\Box

Let us look now at one example of the rate of convergence of method (
2.9
) for an objective function from a nonstandard problem class.
For simplicity, let us measure distances by Euclidean norm
\|x\|_{B}
(see (
1.5
)). In this case, we can take
d(x)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x\|^{2}_{B}
and get
\beta_{d}(x,y)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x-y\|^{2}_{B}
.
Let us assume that function
f_{0}(\cdot)
in problem (
2.1
) is
p
-times continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{E}
and its
p
th derivative is Lipschitz-continuous with constant
L_{p}
. Then we can bound function
\mu(\cdot)
in (
2.4
) as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu(r)&\leq&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}{r^{i}\over i!}\|D^{i}f_{0}(x^{*})\|+{L_{p}\over(p+1)!}r^{p+1},\quad r\geq 0,\end{array}

(2.18)

where all norms for derivatives are induced by
\|\cdot\|_{B}
.

Let us fix the total number of steps
N\geq 1
and assume the bound
R_{0}\geq\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|_{B}
be available. Then, defining the step sizes

\begin{array}[]{rcl}h_{k}&=&h\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;{R_{0}\over\sqrt{N+1}},\quad 0\leq k\leq N,\end{array}

(2.19)

we get
\delta^{*}_{N}=\min\limits_{0\leq k\leq N}\delta_{k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Rate})}}{{\leq}}{R_{0}\over\sqrt{N+1}}
. Hence, in view of inequality (
2.18
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{N}^{*}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&\min\limits_{0\leq k\leq N}f(x_{k})\;\leq\;f_{0}^{*}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}{1\over i!}\|D^{i}f_{0}(x^{*})\|\left({R_{0}\over\sqrt{N+1}}\right)^{i}+{L_{p}\over(p+1)!}\left({R_{0}\over\sqrt{N+1}}\right)^{p+1}.\end{array}

(2.20)

Note that the first
p
coefficients in estimate (
2.16
) depend on the local properties of the objective function at the solution, and only the last term employs the global Lipschitz constant for the
p
th derivative. Clearly, we do not need to know the bounds for all these derivatives in order to define the step-size strategy (
2.19
).

3
Step-size control for max-type convex problems

Let us consider now the following problem of
composite optimization

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F_{*}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\min\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}\psi}\Big\{\;F(x)&=&f(x)+\psi(x)\;\Big\},\end{array}

(3.1)

where
\psi(\cdot)
is a simple closed convex function, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f(x)&=&\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}\;f_{i}(x),\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}\psi,\end{array}

(3.2)

with all
f_{i}(\cdot)
,
1\leq i\leq m
, being closed and convex on
{\rm dom\,}\psi
. Denote by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\ell_{\bar{x}}(x)&=&\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}[\;f_{i}(\bar{x})+\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(\bar{x}),x-\bar{x}\rangle\,]\;\leq\;f(x),\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}\psi,\end{array}

the linearization of function
f(\cdot)
, and by
x_{*}\in{\rm dom\,}\psi
an optimal solution of this problem.

Similarly to (
2.7
), let us define the following univariate functions:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\varphi}_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)&=&\max\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}\psi}\Big\{\lambda\Big[F(\bar{x})-\ell_{\bar{x}}(x)-\psi(x)\Big]-\beta_{d}(\bar{x},x)\Big\},\quad\lambda\geq 0,\end{array}

(3.3)

where
\bar{x}\in{\rm dom\,}\psi
. The unique optimal solution of this optimization problem is denoted by
\hat{T}_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)
. Note that function
\hat{\varphi}_{\bar{x}}(\cdot)
is convex and continuously differentiable with the following derivative:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\varphi}_{\bar{x}}^{\prime}(\lambda)&=&-\Big[\ell_{\bar{x}}(\hat{T})+\psi(\hat{T})-F(\bar{x})\Big],\quad\lambda\geq 0,\end{array}

(3.4)

where
\hat{T}=\hat{T}_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)
.
Thus,
\hat{\varphi}_{\bar{x}}(0)=0
,
\hat{T}_{\bar{x}}(0)=\bar{x}
, and
\hat{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\bar{x}}(0)=0
. Hence,
\hat{\varphi}_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)\geq 0
for all
\lambda\geq 0
. Let us prove the following variant of Lemma
2
.

Lemma 4

Let
\bar{x}\in{\rm dom\,}\psi
and
\hat{T}=\hat{T}_{\bar{x}}(\lambda)
for some
\lambda\geq 0
. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(T,x_{*})&\leq&\beta_{d}(\bar{x},x_{*})+\lambda\Big[\ell_{\bar{x}}(x_{*})+\psi(x_{*})-F(\bar{x})\Big]+\hat{\varphi}_{\bar{x}}(\lambda).\end{array}

(3.5)

Proof:

In view of the first-order optimality condition for the minimization problem in (
3.3
), for all
x\in{\rm dom\,}\psi
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla d(\hat{T})-\nabla d(\bar{x}),x-\hat{T}\rangle+\lambda\Big[\ell_{\bar{x}}(x)+\psi(x)\Big]&\geq&\lambda\Big[\ell_{\bar{x}}(\hat{T})+\psi(\hat{T})\Big].\end{array}

(3.6)

Therefore, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(\hat{T},x_{*})&=&\beta_{d}(\bar{x},x_{*})+d(\bar{x})+\langle\nabla d(\bar{x}),x_{*}-\bar{x}\rangle-d(\hat{T})-\langle\nabla d(\hat{T}),x^{*}-\hat{T}\rangle\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-COpt1})}}{{\leq}}&\beta_{d}(\bar{x},x_{*})+d(\bar{x})+\langle\nabla d(\bar{x}),x_{*}-\bar{x}\rangle-d(\hat{T})\\
\\
&&-\langle\nabla d(\bar{x}),x_{*}-\hat{T}\rangle+\lambda\Big[\ell_{\bar{x}}(x_{*})+\psi(x_{*})-\ell_{\bar{x}}(\hat{T})-\psi(\hat{T})\Big]\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Phi1})}}{{=}}&\beta_{d}(\bar{x},x_{*})+\lambda\Big[\ell_{\bar{x}}(x_{*})+\psi(x_{*})-F(\bar{x})\Big]+\hat{\varphi}_{\bar{x}}(\hat{T}).\hfill\Box\end{array}

In this section, we analyze the following optimization scheme.

\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\\
\quad\mbox{\bf Basic Composite Subgradient Method}\\
\\
\hline\cr\\
\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{{\bf Initialization.} Choose $x_{0}\in{\rm dom\,}\psi$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf$k$th iteration ($k\geq 0$).}}\\
\\
\mbox{a) Choose step-size parameter $\lambda_{k}>0$.}\\
\\
\mbox{b) Compute $x_{k+1}=\hat{T}_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})$.}\\
\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(3.7)

Suppose that the optimal value
F_{*}
is known. In view of convexity of function
f(\cdot)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\ell_{\bar{x}}(x_{*})+\psi(x_{*})-F(\bar{x})&\leq&F_{*}-F(\bar{x}).\end{array}

Hence, for points
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, generated by method (
3.7
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(x_{k+1},x_{*})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Step1})}}{{\leq}}&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{*})+\Big(\hat{\varphi}_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})-\lambda_{k}[F(x_{k})-F_{*}]\Big).\end{array}

(3.8)

This observation explains the following step-size strategy:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{\framebox{$\lambda_{k}=\arg\min\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\Big\{\;\hat{\varphi}_{x_{k}}(\lambda)-\lambda[F(x_{k})-F_{*}]\;\Big\}$}}\end{array}

(3.9)

This strategy has a natural optimization interpretation.

Lemma 5

Let
\lambda_{k}
be defined by (
3.9
). Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{T}_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})&=&\arg\min\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}\psi}\Big\{\;\beta_{d}(x_{k},x):\;\ell_{x_{k}}(x)+\psi(x)\leq F_{*}\;\Big\}.\end{array}

(3.10)

Proof:

Indeed,

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\min\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\Big\{\;\hat{\varphi}_{x_{k}}(\lambda)-\lambda[F(x_{k})-F_{*}]\;\Big\}\\
\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Phi1})}}{{=}}&\min\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\max\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}\psi}\Big\{\;-\lambda\Big[\ell_{x_{k}}(x)+\psi(x)-F(x_{k})\Big]-\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)-\lambda[F(x_{k})-F_{*}]\;\Big\}\\
\\
=&\max\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}\psi}\min\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\Big\{\;-\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)+\lambda\Big[F_{*}-\ell_{x_{k}}(x)-\psi(x)]\Big]\;\Big\}\\
\\
=&-\min\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}\psi}\Big\{\;\beta_{d}(x_{k},x):\;\ell_{x_{k}}(x)+\psi(x)\leq F_{*}\;\Big\}.\hfill\Box\end{array}

Lemma
5
has two important consequences allowing us to estimate the rate of convergence of method (
3.7
) with the step-size rule (
3.9
). Namely, for any
k\geq 0
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(x_{k+1},x_{*})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Step2})}}{{\leq}}&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{*})-\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1}),\end{array}

(3.11)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\ell_{x_{k}}(x_{k+1})+\psi(x_{k+1})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-THat})}}{{\leq}}F_{*}.\end{array}

(3.12)

Note that method (
3.7
) is not monotone. However, inequality (
3.11
) gives us a global rate of convergence for the following characteristic:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\rho_{k}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&\min\limits_{0\leq i\leq k}\|x_{i}-x_{i+1}\|\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BGrow})}}{{\leq}}\;\min\limits_{0\leq i\leq k}\sqrt{2\beta_{d}(x_{i},x_{i+1})}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-DecStep})}}{{\leq}}\;{r_{0}\over\sqrt{k+1}},\quad k\geq 0,\end{array}

(3.13)

where
r_{0}=\beta_{d}(x_{0},x_{*})
.

Theorem 1

Let all functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
have Hölder-continuous gradients:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{i}(y)&\leq&f_{i}(x)+\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(x),y-x\rangle+{L_{\nu}\over 1+\nu}\|y-x\|^{1+\nu},\quad x,y\in{\rm dom\,}\psi,\;1\leq i\leq p,\end{array}

(3.14)

where
\nu\in[0,1]
and
L_{\nu}\geq 0
. Then for the step-size rule (
3.9
) in method (
3.7
) we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F_{k}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&\min\limits_{0\leq i\leq k}F(x_{i})\;\leq\;F_{*}+{L_{\nu}r_{0}^{1+\nu}\over 1+\nu}\left({1\over k}\right)^{1+\nu\over 2},\quad k\geq 1.\end{array}

(3.15)

Proof:

Indeed, let
\rho_{k}=\|x_{i_{k}}-x_{i_{k}+1}\|
for some
i_{k}
,
0\leq i_{k}\leq k
. Then for any
k\geq 0
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F_{k+1}\;\leq\;F(x_{i_{k}+1})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Hol})}}{{\leq}}&\ell_{x_{i_{k}}}(x_{i_{k}+1})+{L_{\nu}\over 1+\nu}\rho_{k}^{1+\nu}+\psi(x_{i_{k}+1})\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-NewF})}}{{\leq}}&F_{*}+{L_{\nu}\over 1+\nu}\rho_{k}^{1+\nu}.\end{array}

It remains to use inequality (
3.13
).

\Box

Note that the step-size strategy (
3.9
) does not depend on the Hölder parameter
\nu
in the condition (
3.14
). Hence, the number of iterations of method (
3.7
), (
3.9
), ensuring an
\epsilon
-accuracy in function value, is bounded from above by the following quantity:

\begin{array}[]{c}\inf\limits_{0\leq\nu\leq 1}\left[{L_{\nu}\over(1+\nu)\epsilon}\right]^{2\over 1+\nu}\,r_{0}^{2}.\end{array}

(3.16)

To conclude the section, let us show that the step-size rule (
3.9
) can behave much better than the classical one.

Example 2

Let
\mathbb{E}=R^{2}
,
\psi(x)=\mbox{\rm Ind}\,Q
with
Q=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;x^{(2)}\leq 0\Big\}
,
\|x\|^{2}=x^{T}x
,
and
f(x)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}(x^{(1)})^{2}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}(x^{(2)}-1)^{2}
. Then
\nabla f(x)=(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}-1)^{T}
and
f_{*}=0
. Consider the point
x_{k}=(x^{(1)}_{k},0)^{T}
. Then, by the classical rule, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{k+1}&=&\pi_{Q}\left[x_{k}-{f(x_{k})-f_{*}\over\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}\nabla f(x_{k})\right]\;=\;\pi_{Q}\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x^{(1)}_{k}\\
0\end{array}\right)-{\left(x^{(1)}_{k}\right)^{2}\over 2\left(1+\left(x^{(1)}_{k}\right)^{2}\right)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x^{(1)}_{k}\\
-1\end{array}\right)\right].\end{array}

Thus,
x^{(2)}_{k+1}=0
and
x^{(1)}_{k+1}=x^{(1)}_{k}-{\left(x^{(1)}_{k}\right)^{3}\over 2\left(1+\left(x^{(1)}_{k}\right)^{2}\right)}
. This means that
x_{k}^{(1)}=O(k^{-1/2})
.

On the other hand, the rule (
3.10
) as applied to the same point
x_{k}
defines
x=x_{k+1}
as an intersection of two lines

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-x\rangle=f(x_{k})-f_{*}\Big\},\quad\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;x^{(2)}=0\Big\}.\end{array}

This means that
x^{(1)}_{k}\left(x^{(1)}_{k}-x^{(1)}_{k+1}\right)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\left(x^{(1)}_{k}\right)^{2}
, and we get the linear rate of convergence to the optimal point
x_{*}=0
.

\Box

4
Step-size control for problems with smooth

strongly convex components

A linear rate of convergence, demonstrated by method (
3.7
) in the Example
2
), provides us with motivation to look at behavior of this method on smooth and strongly convex problems. Let us introduce a Euclidean metric by (
1.5
) and define
d(x)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x\|^{2}_{B}
,
x\in\mathbb{E}
. In this case,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(x,y)&=&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x-y\|^{2}_{B},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{E}.\end{array}

Suppose that all functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
,
i=1,\dots,m
in (
3.2
) are continuously differentiable and have Lipschitz-continuous gradients with the same constant
L_{f}\geq 0
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{i}(y)&\leq&f_{i}(x)+\langle\nabla f_{i}(x),y-x\rangle+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}L_{f}\|x-y\|^{2}_{B},\quad x,y\in{\rm dom\,}\psi.\end{array}

(4.1)

In the notation of Section
3
, these inequalities imply the following upper bound for the objective function of problem (
3.1
):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(y)&\leq&\ell_{x}(y)+\psi(y)+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}L_{f}\|x-y\|^{2}_{B},\quad x,y\in{\rm dom\,}\psi.\end{array}

(4.2)

Hence, for the sequence of points
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, generated by method (
3.7
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(x_{k+1})&\leq&\ell_{x_{k}}(x_{k+1})+\psi(x_{k+1})+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}L_{f}\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}_{B}\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-NewF})}}{{\leq}}&F_{*}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}L_{f}\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}_{B}.\end{array}

(4.3)

Thus, we can prove the following statement.

Theorem 2

Under condition (
4.1
), the rate of convergence of method (
3.7
) can be estimated as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{1\over T}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{T}F(x_{k})-F_{*}&\leq&{1\over 2T}L_{f}\|x_{0}-x_{*}\|^{2}_{B},\quad T\geq 1.\end{array}

(4.4)

If in addition functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
,
i=1,\dots,m
, are strongly convex:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{i}(y)&\geq&f_{i}(x)+\langle\nabla f_{i}(x),y-x\rangle+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\mu_{f}\|x-y\|^{2}_{B},\quad x,y\in{\rm dom\,}\psi,\end{array}

(4.5)

with
\mu_{f}>0
, then the rate of convergence is linear:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|x_{k}-x_{*}\|^{2}_{B}&\leq&\left({L_{f}\over\mu_{f}+L_{f}}\right)^{k}\|x_{0}-x_{*}\|^{2}_{B},\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

(4.6)

Proof:

Indeed, substituting inequality (
4.3
) into relation (
3.11
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x_{k+1}-x_{*}\|^{2}_{B}&\leq&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x_{k}-x_{*}\|^{2}_{B}-{1\over L_{f}}[F(x_{k+1})-F_{*}],\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

(4.7)

Summing up these inequalities for
k=0,\dots,T-1
, we get inequality (
4.4
).

If in adition, the conditions (
4.5
) are satisfied, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(y)&\geq&\ell_{x}(y)+\psi(y)+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\mu_{f}\|x-y\|^{2}_{B},\quad x,y\in{\rm dom\,}\psi.\end{array}

(4.8)

Note that the first-order optimality conditions for problem (
3.1
) can be written in the following form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\ell_{x_{*}}(y)+\psi(y)&\geq&F_{*},\quad\forall y\in{\rm dom\,}\psi.\end{array}

Therefore, inequality (
4.8
) implies that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(y)-F_{*}&\geq&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\mu_{f}\|y-x_{*}\|^{2}_{B},\quad\forall y\in{\rm dom\,}\psi.\end{array}

Thus,
\|x_{k+1}-x_{*}\|^{2}_{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-DecSF})}}{{\leq}}{L_{f}\over\mu_{f}+L_{f}}\|x_{k}-x_{*}\|^{2}_{B}
, and we get inequality (
4.6
).

\Box

5
Convex minimization with max-type composite constraint

Let us show that both step-size strategies described in Sections
2
and
3
can be unified in one scheme for solving constrained optimization problems. In this section, we deal with the problem in the following
semi-composite
form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\min\limits_{x\in Q}\Big\{f_{0}(x):\;F(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(x)+\psi(x)\leq 0\Big\}\end{array}

(5.1)

where
\psi(\cdot)
is a simple closed convex function,
Q\subseteq{\rm dom\,}\psi
is a closed convex set, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f(x)&=&\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}f_{i}(x),\end{array}

with all functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
,
i=0,\dots,m
, being closed and convex on
{\rm dom\,}\psi
. We assume
Q
to be bounded:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(x,y)&<&D,\quad\forall x,y\in Q.\end{array}

(5.2)

In order to solve problem (
5.1
), we propose a method, which combines two different types of iterations. One of them improves the
feasibility
of the current point, and the second one improves its
optimality
.

Iteration of the first type is based on the machinery developed in Section
3
with the particular value
F_{*}=0
. It is applied to some point
x_{k}\in Q
.

\begin{array}[]{l}\\
\quad\mbox{Compute $\lambda_{k}=\arg\min\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\Big\{\hat{\varphi}_{x_{k}}(\lambda)-\lambda F(x_{k})\Big\}$. Set $x_{k+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-THat})}}{{=}}\hat{T}_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})$.}\\
\\
\end{array}

(5.3)

Note that for
F(x_{k})\leq 0
, we have
\lambda_{k}=0
and
\hat{T}_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})=x_{k}
.

For iteration
k
of the second type, we need to choose a primal step size bound
h_{k}>0
. Then, at the test point
y_{k}\in Q
, we define the function
\varphi_{y_{k}}(\cdot)
by (
2.7
) and apply the following rule:

\begin{array}[]{l}\\
\quad\mbox{Find $\lambda_{k}$ from equation
$\varphi_{y_{k}}(\lambda_{k})=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}$. Set $x_{k+1}=T_{y_{k}}(\lambda_{k})$.}\\
\\
\end{array}

(5.4)

Since in both rules parameters
\lambda_{k}
are functions of the test points, we will use shorter notations
T(y_{k})
and
\hat{T}(x_{k})
. Consider the following optimization scheme.

\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\\
\quad\mbox{\bf Double-Step Subgradient Method for Semi-Composite Problem}\\
\\
\hline\cr\\
\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{{\bf Initialization.} Choose $x_{0}\in{\rm dom\,}\psi$ and sequence of steps ${\cal H}=\{h_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf$k$th iteration ($k\geq 0$).} {\bf 1.} Compute $y_{k}=\hat{T}(x_{k})$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf 2.} If $\beta_{d}(x_{k},y_{k})\geq\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}$, then a) $x_{k+1}=y_{k}$. Else, b) compute $x_{k+1}=T(y_{k})$.}\\
\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(5.5)

Thus, method (
5.5
) is defined by a sequence of primal step bounds
{\cal H}=\{h_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. However, since in (
5.5
) we apply a switching strategy, it is impossible to say in advance what will be the type of a particular
k
th iteration. Therefore, as compared with the classical conditions (
2.12
), we need additional regularity assumptions on
{\cal H}
.

It will be convenient to relate this sequence with another sequence of
scaling coefficients

{\cal T}=\{\tau_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, satisfying the
second-order divergence condition
(compare with (
2.12
)).

\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\\
\mbox{\bf Second-Order Divergence Condition}\\
\\
\hline\cr\\
\begin{array}[]{rl}\mbox{\bf a)}&\mbox{$\tau_{k}\geq\tau_{k+1}>0$ for any $k\geq 0$.}\quad\mbox{ {\bf b)} $\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\tau_{k}^{2}=+\infty$.}\end{array}\\
\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(5.6)

Note that condition (
5.6
) ensures
\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\tau_{k}=+\infty
. Thus, it is stronger than (
2.12
). In order to transform the sequence
{\cal T}
into
convergence rate
of some optimization process, we need to introduce the following characteristic.

Definition 1

For a sequence
{\cal T}
, the integer-valued function
a(k)
,
k\geq 0
, is called the divergence delay (of degree two) if
a(k)\geq 0
is the minimal integer value such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sum\limits_{i=k}^{k+a(k)}\tau_{i}^{2}&\geq&1,\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

(5.7)

Clearly, condition (
5.6
)
b
ensures that all values
a(k)
,
k\geq 0
, are well defined. At the same time, from (
5.6
)
a
, we have
a(k+1)\geq a(k)
for any
k\geq 0
.

Let us give two important examples of such sequences.

Example 3

a)
Let us fix an integer
N\geq 0
and define

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau_{k}&=&{1\over\sqrt{N+1}},\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

(5.8)

Then condition (
5.6
)
a
is valid and

\sum\limits_{i=k}^{k+a(k)}\tau_{i}^{2}={a(k)+1\over N+1}
. Thus,
a(k)=N
.

b)
Consider the following sequence:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau_{k}&=&\sqrt{2\over k+1},\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

(5.9)

For
k\geq 1
, denote by
S_{k}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}\tau_{i}^{2}
.
Then
S_{1}=2
,
S_{2}=3
, and the difference

\begin{array}[]{rcl}S_{2k}-S_{k}&=&\sum\limits_{i=k}^{2k-1}\tau_{i}^{2}\end{array}

is monotonically increasing in
k\geq 1
. Thus,
a(k)\leq k-1
for all
k\geq 1
.

\Box

Let us analyze performance of method (
5.5
) with an appropriately chosen sequence
{\cal H}
. Namely, let us choose

\begin{array}[]{rcl}h_{k}&=&\sqrt{2D}\;\tau_{k},\quad k\geq 0,\end{array}

(5.10)

where the sequence
{\cal T}
satisfies condition (
5.6
) and
D
is taken from (
5.2
).

We are interested only in the values of objective function
f_{0}(\cdot)
computed at the points with small values of the functional constraint
F(\cdot)
. As we will see, these points are involved in Step 2b). For the total number of steps
N\geq 1+a(0)
, denote

\begin{array}[]{rcl}k(N)&=&\max\Big\{k\geq 0:\;k+a(k)\leq N-1\Big\},\\
\\
{\cal F}_{N}&=&\Big\{k:\;k(N)\leq k\leq N-1,\;y_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2b)}}{{\longrightarrow}}x_{k+1}\Big\}.\end{array}

(5.11)

Let us define the following directional proximity measures:

\begin{array}[]{rclll}\delta_{k}&=&\delta_{d_{k}}(y_{k})&\mbox{with $\;d_{k}={y_{k}-x_{k+1}\over\|y_{k}-x_{k+1}\|}$},&k\in{\cal F}_{N}.\end{array}

We are interested in the rate of convergence to zero of the following characteristic:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\delta_{N}^{*}&=&\min\limits_{k\in{\cal F}_{N}}\delta_{k}.\end{array}

Theorem 3

For any
N\geq 1+a(0)
, the number
k(N)\geq 0
is well defined and
\delta_{N}^{*}<h_{k(N)}
.

Moreover, if all functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
,
i=1,\dots,m
, have Hölder-continuous gradients on
Q
with parameter
\nu\in[0,1]
and constant
L_{\nu}>0
, then, for any
k\in{\cal F}_{N}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(y_{k})&\leq&{L_{\nu}\over 1+\nu}h_{k(N)}^{1+\nu}.\end{array}

(5.12)

Proof:

Let us bound the distances
r_{k}=\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{*})
for
k\geq k(N)
. If
k\not\in{\cal F}_{N}
, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}r_{k+1}&=&\beta_{d}(y_{k},x_{*})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-DecStep})}}{{\leq}}\;r_{k}-\beta(x_{k},y_{k})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{met-SGM2})}}{{\leq}}\;r_{k}-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}.\end{array}

If
k\in{\cal F}_{N}
, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}r_{k+1}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-RNext})}}{{\leq}}&\beta(y_{k},x_{*})-h_{k}\delta_{k}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-DecStep})}}{{\leq}}\;r_{k}-h_{k}\delta_{k}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}\;\leq\;r_{k}-h_{k}\delta^{*}_{N}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}.\end{array}

Summing up these inequalities for
k=k(N),\dots N-1
, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}r_{N}&<&D-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal F}_{N}}h_{k}^{2}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal F}_{N}}(h_{k}^{2}-2h_{k}\delta^{*}_{N})\\
\\
&=&D-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\sum\limits_{k=k(N)}^{N-1}h_{k}^{2}+\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal F}_{N}}(h_{k}^{2}-h_{k}\delta^{*}_{N})\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-DD}),(\ref{def-HStep2})}}{{<}}&\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal F}_{N}}h_{k}(h_{k}-\delta^{*}_{N})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-TGrow})_{a}}}{{\leq}}\;(h_{k(N)}-\delta^{*}_{N})\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal F}_{N}}h_{k}.\end{array}

Thus,
\delta^{*}_{N}<h_{k(N)}
.
Finally, inclusion
k\in{\cal F}_{N}
implies

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|y_{k}-x_{k}\|^{2}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BGrow})}}{{\leq}}&\beta_{d}(x_{k},y_{k})\;\leq\;\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-TGrow})_{a}}}{{\leq}}\;\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k(N)}^{2}.\end{array}

Since
F_{*}=0
, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(y_{k})&\leq&\ell_{x_{k}}(y_{k})+{L_{\nu}\over 1+\nu}\|y_{k}-x_{k}\|^{1+\nu}+\psi(y_{k})\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-NewF})}}{{\leq}}&{L_{\nu}\over 1+\nu}\|y_{k}-x_{k}\|^{1+\nu}\;\leq\;{L_{\nu}\over 1+\nu}h_{k(N)}^{1+\nu},\end{array}

and this is inequality (
5.12
).

\Box

As a straightforward consequence of Theorem
3
, we have the following rate of convergence in function value:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\min\limits_{x\in{\cal F}_{N}}f_{0}(y_{k})&\leq&f^{*}_{0}+\mu(h_{k(N)}),\end{array}

(5.13)

where the function
\mu(\cdot)
is defined by (
2.4
).

Thus, the actual rate of convergence of method (
5.5
) depends on the rate of convergence of sequence
{\cal T}
and the magnitude of divergence delay. For example, for the choice (
5.9
), in view of inequality
a(k)\leq k-1
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}k(N)&=&\max\Big\{k:\;k+a(k)\leq N-1\Big\}\;\geq\;\max\Big\{k:\;2k-1\leq N-1\Big\}.\end{array}

Hence, for
N=2M
, the choice (
5.9
) ensures
k(N)\geq M=N/2
, and we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}h_{k(N)}&\leq&h_{M}\;=\;{2D^{1/2}\over\sqrt{M+1}}.\end{array}

(5.14)

It is interesting that the rate of convergence (
5.12
) for the constraints can be higher than the rate (
5.13
) for the objective function.

6
Approximating Lagrange Multipliers, I

Despite to the good convergence rate (
5.13
), when the number of functional components
m
in problem (
5.1
) is big, the implementation of one iteration (
5.3
) can be very expensive. In this section, we consider a simpler switching strategy for solving convex optimization problems with potentially many functional constraints. Our method is also able to approximate the corresponding optimal Lagrange multipliers.

Consider the following
constrained
optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{0}^{*}\;=\;\min\limits_{x\in Q}\Big\{f_{0}(x):\;f_{i}(x)\leq 0,\;i=1,\dots,m\Big\},\end{array}

(6.1)

where all functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
are closed and convex,
i=0,\dots,m
, and set
Q
is closed, convex, and bounded. Denote by
x^{*}
one of its optimal solutions. Sometimes we use notation
\bar{f}(x)=(f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{m}(x))^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Denote
{\cal F}=\{x\in Q:\;\bar{f}(x)\leq 0\}
.

We assume that functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
are subdifferentiable on
Q
and it is possible to compute their subgradients with uniformly bounded norms:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|f^{\prime}_{i}(x)\|_{*}&\leq&M_{i},\quad x\in Q,\quad i=0,\dots,p.\end{array}

(6.2)

Denote
\bar{M}=(M_{1},\dots,M_{m})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

For the set
Q
, we assume existence of a prox-function
d(\cdot)
defining the corresponding Bregman distance
\beta_{d}(\cdot,\cdot)
. In our methods, we need to know a constant
D
such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(x,y)&<&D,\quad\forall x,y\in Q.\end{array}

(6.3)

Let us introduce the Lagrangian

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\cal L}(x,\bar{\lambda})&=&f_{0}(x)+\langle\bar{\lambda},\bar{f}(x)\rangle,\quad\bar{\lambda}=(\lambda^{(1)},\dots,\lambda^{(m)})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+},\end{array}

and the dual function
\phi(\bar{\lambda})=\min\limits_{x\in Q}{\cal L}(x,\bar{\lambda})
. By Sion’s theorem, we know that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sup\limits_{\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}\phi(\bar{\lambda})&=&f_{0}^{*}.\end{array}

(6.4)

Our first method is based on two operations presented in Sections
2
and
3
. For defining iterations of the first type, we need functions
\varphi_{i,x}(\lambda)
with
\lambda\geq 0
, parameterized by
x\in Q
, and defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi_{i,x}(\lambda)&=&\max\limits_{T\in Q}\Big\{\lambda\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(x),x-T\rangle-\beta_{d}(x,T)\Big\},\quad i=0,\dots,m.\end{array}

(6.5)

An appropriate value of
\lambda
can be found from the equation

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi_{i,x_{k}}(\lambda)&=&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2},\end{array}

(6.6)

and used for setting
x_{k+1}=T_{i,x_{k}}(\lambda)
, the optimal solutions of problem (
6.5
). In this section, we perform this iteration only for the objective function (
i=0
). The possibility of using the steps
T_{i,x_{k}}(\lambda)
for inequality constraints is analyzed in Sections
7
and
9
.

The iteration of the second type is trying to improve feasibility of the current point
x_{k}\in Q
(compare with (
5.3
)). It needs computation of all Bregman projections

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{T}_{i}(x_{k})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&\arg\min\limits_{T\in Q}\Big\{\beta_{d}(x_{k},T):\;f_{i}(x_{k})+\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k}),T-x_{k}\rangle\leq 0\Big\},\end{array}

(6.7)

for indexes
i=1,\dots,m
. The first-order optimality condition for problem (
6.7
) is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla d(T)-\nabla d(x_{k})+\lambda f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k}),x-T\rangle&\geq&0,\quad\forall x\in Q,\end{array}

(6.8)

where
T=\hat{T}_{i}(x_{k})
and
\lambda=\lambda_{i}(x_{k})\geq 0
is the optimal Lagrange multiplier for the linear inequality constraint in (
6.7
). We assume that this multiplier can be also computed. Note that for
x=x_{k}
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{i}(x_{k})\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k}),x_{k}-T\rangle&\geq&\beta_{d}(x_{k},T)+\beta_{d}(T,x_{k})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BGrow})}}{{\geq}}\;\|x_{k}-T\|^{2}.\end{array}

Hence, if
T\neq x_{k}
, then
\lambda_{i}(x_{k})>0
.

Consider the following optimization scheme.

\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\\
\quad\mbox{\bf Subgradient Method for Problem (\ref{prob-FConst})}\\
\\
\hline\cr\\
\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{{\bf Initialization.} Choose $x_{0}\in Q$ and a sequence of step bounds ${\cal H}=\{h_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf$k$th iteration ($k\geq 0$).} {\bf 1.} Compute $r_{k}^{i}=\beta_{d}(x_{k},\hat{T}_{i}(x_{k}))$, $1\leq i\leq m$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf 2.} If $\exists\;i\in\{1\dots m\}:r_{k}^{i}\geq\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h^{2}_{k}$, then set $i_{k}=i$, $\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{i}(x_{k})$, $x_{k+1}=\hat{T}_{i}(x_{k})$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf 3.} Else, set $i_{k}=0$, compute $\lambda_{k}$ by (\ref{eq-HLambda}) and set $x_{k+1}=T_{x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})$.}\\
\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(6.9)

Let us prove that method (
6.9
) can find an approximate solution of the primal-dual problem (
6.1
), (
6.4
). Let us choose a sequence
{\cal T}
satisfying condition (
5.6
) and define

\begin{array}[]{rcl}h_{k}&=&\sqrt{2D}\;\tau_{k},\quad k\geq 0,\end{array}

(6.10)

where
D
satisfies inequality (
6.3
). Then, for the number of steps
N\geq 1+a(0)
, let us define function
k(N)
by the first equation in (
5.11
). Now we can define the following objects:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\cal A}_{i}(N)&=&\{k:\;k(N)\leq k\leq N-1,\;i_{k}=i\},\\
\\
\sigma_{i}(N)&=&\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{i}(N)}\lambda_{k},\quad i=0,\dots,m,\\
\\
\lambda^{*}_{i}(N)&=&\sigma_{i}(N)/\sigma_{0}(N),\quad i=1,\dots,m.\end{array}

(6.11)

Clearly, the vectors of dual multipliers
\bar{\lambda}_{*}(N)=(\lambda^{*}_{1}(N),\dots,\lambda^{*}_{m}(N))
are defined only if
{\cal A}_{0}(N)\neq\emptyset
. As usual, the sum over an empty set of iterations is assumed to be zero.

Theorem 4

Let the sequence of step bounds
{\cal H}
in method (
6.9
) be defined by (
6.10
).
Then for any
N\geq 1+a(0)
, we have
{\cal A}_{0}(N)\neq\emptyset
.
Moreover, if all functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
,
0=1,\dots,m
, satisfy (
6.2
), then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}{1\over M_{i}}f_{i}(x_{k})&\leq&h_{k(N)},\quad k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N),\end{array}

(6.12)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{1\over\sigma_{0}(N)}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{k}f_{0}(x_{k})&\leq&\phi(\bar{\lambda}^{*}(N))+M_{0}h_{k(N)}.\end{array}

(6.13)

Proof:

Indeed, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}B_{N}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&\max\limits_{x\in Q}\Big[\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{k}f_{0}(x_{k})-\sigma_{0}(N)f_{0}(x)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(N)f_{i}(x)\Big]\\
\\
&=&\max\limits_{x\in Q}\Big[\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{k}[f_{0}(x_{k})-f_{0}(x)]-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(N)f_{i}(x)\Big].\end{array}

Let us fix some
x\in Q
and denote
r_{k}(x)=\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)
. Then, for
k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{k}[f_{0}(x_{k})-f_{0}(x)]&\leq&\lambda_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x\rangle.\end{array}

(6.14)

At the same time, from the first-order optimality condition for problem (
6.5
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}0\;\leq&\langle\nabla d(x_{k+1})-\nabla d(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle+\lambda_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle\\
\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-F0})}}{{\leq}}&\langle\nabla d(x_{k+1})-\nabla d(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle-\lambda_{k}[f_{0}(x_{k})-f_{0}(x)]+\lambda_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle\\
\\
=&\langle\nabla d(x_{k+1})-\nabla d(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle-\lambda_{k}[f_{0}(x_{k})-f_{0}(x)]+\varphi_{0,x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})+\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1}).\end{array}

Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rl}&r_{k+1}(x)-r_{k}(x)\;=\;\langle\nabla d(x_{k})-\nabla d(x_{k+1}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle-\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})\\
\\
\leq&-\lambda_{k}[f_{0}(x_{k})-f_{0}(x)]+\varphi_{0,x_{k}}(\lambda_{k})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HLambda})}}{{=}}\;-\lambda_{k}[f_{0}(x_{k})-f_{0}(x)]+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}.\end{array}

On the other hand,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(N)f_{i}(x)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Sigma})}}{{=}}&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{i}(N)}\lambda_{k}f_{i}(x)\;=\;\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{i_{k}}(x_{k})f_{i_{k}}(x)\\
\\
&\geq&\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{i_{k}}(x_{k})[f_{i_{k}}(x_{k})+\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\rangle]\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-TLambda})}}{{=}}&\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{i_{k}}(x_{k})[\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle]\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-TLambda})}}{{\geq}}&\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\langle\nabla d(x_{k+1})-\nabla d(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x\rangle\\
\\
&=&\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\Big[r_{k+1}(x)-r_{k}(x)+\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})\Big]\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{met-SGM3})_{2}}}{{\geq}}&\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\Big[r_{k+1}(x)-r_{k}(x)+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}\Big].\end{array}

Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}B_{N}&\leq&\max\limits_{x\in Q}\Big[\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}[r_{k}(x)-r_{k+1}(x)+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}]-\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}[r_{k+1}(x)-r_{k}(x)+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}]\;\Big]\\
\\
&=&\max\limits_{x\in Q}\Big[r_{k(N)}(x)-r_{N}(x)+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}h_{k}^{2}-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}h_{k}^{2}\Big]\\
\\
&<&D-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\sum\limits_{k=k(N)}^{N-1}h_{k}^{2}+\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}h_{k}^{2}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-HStep3})}}{{\leq}}\;\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}h_{k}^{2}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-TGrow})_{a}}}{{\leq}}\;h_{k(N)}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}h_{k}.\end{array}

Let us assume now that
{\cal A}_{0}(N)=\emptyset
. Then
B_{N}<0
, and this is impossible since the point
x_{*}
is feasible. Thus, we have proved that
{\cal A}_{0}(N)\neq\emptyset
.

Further, for any
k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HLambda})}}{{=}}&\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}.\end{array}

Hence,
r_{k}^{0}=\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|>0
and we conclude that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{k}M_{0}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GBound})}}{{\geq}}\lambda_{k}\|f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k})\|_{*}\;\geq{\lambda_{k}\over r_{k}^{0}}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BGrow})}}{{\geq}}\;{1\over 2r_{k}^{0}}\Big[(r_{k}^{0})^{2}+h_{k}^{2}\Big]\;\geq\;h_{k}.\end{array}

Thus, we have proved that
B_{N}\leq M_{0}\sigma_{0}(N)h_{k(N)}
. Dividing this inequality by
\sigma_{0}(N)>0
, we get the bound (
6.13
).

Finally, note that for any
k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)
we have
\|\hat{T}_{i}(x_{k})-x_{k}\|\leq h_{k}
for all
i
,
1\leq i\leq m
. Note that either
f_{i}(x_{k})\leq 0
, or
f_{i}(x_{k})>0
and
f_{i}(x_{k})+\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k}),\hat{T}_{i}(x_{k})-x_{k})\rangle=0
. In the latter case, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{i}(x_{k})&=&\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k}),x_{k}-\hat{T}_{i}(x_{k})\rangle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GBound})}}{{\leq}}\;M_{i}h_{k}\;\leq\;M_{i}h_{k(N)}.\end{array}

Thus, inequality (
6.12
) is proved.

\Box

Note that for the choice of scaling sequence (
5.9
), method (
5.5
) is globally convergent. In this case, the computation of Lagrange multipliers by (
6.11
) for all values of
N
, requires storage of all coefficients
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. This inconvenience can be avoided if we decide to accumulate the sums for Lagrange multipliers only starting from the moments
k(N_{q})
with
N_{q}=2^{q}
,
q\geq 1
. Then the method (
5.5
) will be allowed to stop only at the moments
2N_{q}
.

7
Approximating Lagrange Multipliers, II

Method (
6.9
) has one hidden drawback. If
i_{k}\geq 1
, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}0&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-TLambda})}}{{=}}&f_{i_{k}}(x_{k})+\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\;\leq\;f_{i_{k}}(x_{k+1}).\end{array}

Thus, this scheme most probably generates infeasible approximations of the optimal point, which violate some of the functional constraints. In order to avoid this tendency, we propose a scheme which uses for both types of iterations (improving either feasibility or optimality) the same step-size rule (
6.6
).

\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\\
\quad\mbox{\bf Fixed-Step Switching Subgradient Method for Problem (\ref{prob-FConst})}\\
\\
\hline\cr\\
\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{{\bf Initialization.} Choose $x_{0}\in Q$ and a sequence of step bounds ${\cal H}=\{h_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf$k$th iteration ($k\geq 0$).} {\bf 1.} Compute $T_{i,x_{k}}(\lambda_{i,k})$ by (\ref{eq-HLambda}), $1\leq i\leq m$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf 2.} If $\exists\;i\in\{1\dots m\}:\lambda_{i,k}f_{i}(x_{k})\geq h^{2}_{k}$, then set $i_{k}=i$ and $x_{k+1}=T_{i,x_{k}}(\lambda_{i,k})$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf 3.} Else, set $i_{k}=0$, compute $T_{0,x_{k}}(\lambda_{0,k})$ by (\ref{eq-HLambda}), and set $x_{k+1}=T_{0,k}(\lambda_{0,k})$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf 4.} Set $\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{i_{k},k}$.}\\
\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(7.1)

Thus, for both types of iterations, we use the same step-size strategy (
6.6
). Note that for any
i=0,\dots,m
and
T_{i}=T_{i,x_{k}}(\lambda_{i,k})
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{i,k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k}),x_{k}-T_{i}\rangle&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-NPhi})}}{{=}}&\varphi_{i,x_{k}}(\lambda_{i,k})+\beta_{d}(x_{k},T_{i})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HLambda})}}{{=}}\;\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}+\beta_{d}(x_{k},T_{i})\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BGrow})}}{{\geq}}&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x_{k}-T_{i}\|^{2}\;\geq\;h_{k}\|x_{k}-T_{i}\|.\end{array}

Hence, since
T_{i}\neq x_{k}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{i,k}\|f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k})\|_{*}&\geq&h_{k},\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

(7.2)

In method (
7.1
), we choose
{\cal H}
in accordance to (
6.10
). Then, for
N\geq 1+a(0)
, we define function
k(N)
by the first equation in (
5.11
) and introduce by (
6.11
) the approximations of the optimal Lagrange multipliers.

Theorem 5

Let the sequence of points
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by method (
7.1
). Then for any
N\geq 1+a(0)
the set
{\cal A}_{0}(N)
is not empty. Moreover, if all functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
,
0=1,\dots,m
, satisfy (
6.2
), then for all
k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{i}(x_{k})&\leq&\|f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k})\|_{*}h_{k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GBound})}}{{\leq}}\;M_{i}h_{k(N)},\quad i=1\dots m,\end{array}

(7.3)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{1\over\sigma_{0}(N)}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{k}f_{0}(x_{k})&\leq&\phi(\bar{\lambda}^{*}(N))+M_{0}h_{k(N)}.\end{array}

(7.4)

Proof:

As in the proof of Theorem
4
, for
x\in Q
, we denote
r_{k}(x)=\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)
, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}B_{N}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&\max\limits_{x\in Q}\Big[\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{k}f_{0}(x_{k})-\sigma_{0}(N)f_{0}(x)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}(N)f_{i}(x)\Big]\\
\\
&=&\max\limits_{x\in Q}\Big[\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{k}[f_{0}(x_{k})-f_{0}(x)]-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(N)f_{i}(x)\Big].\end{array}

Then, for
k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)
, we have proved that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}r_{k+1}(x)-r_{k}(x)&\leq&-\lambda_{k}[f_{0}(x_{k})-f_{0}(x)]+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}.\end{array}

On the other hand,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(N)f_{i}(x)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Sigma})}}{{=}}&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{i}(N)}\lambda_{k}f_{i}(x)\;=\;\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{i_{k},k}f_{i_{k}}(x)\\
\\
&\geq&\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\lambda_{i_{k},k}[f_{i_{k}}(x_{k})+\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\rangle]\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{met-SGM4})_{2}}}{{\geq}}&\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}\Big[h_{k}^{2}+\lambda_{i_{k},k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\rangle\Big].\end{array}

Note that the first-order optimality condition for problem (
2.7
) is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla d(T_{i})-\nabla d(x_{k})+\lambda f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k}),x-T_{i}\rangle&\geq&0,\quad\forall x\in Q,\end{array}

where
T_{i}
is its optimal solution, Therefore, for all
x\in Q
an
k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\lambda_{i_{k},k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\rangle\;=\;\lambda_{i_{k},k}\Big[\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle+\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle\Big]\\
\\
\geq&\lambda_{i_{k},k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+\langle\nabla d(x_{k+1})-\nabla d(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x\rangle\\
\\
=&\lambda_{i_{k},k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+r_{k+1}(x)-r_{k}(x)+\beta_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})\\
\\
=&r_{k+1}(x)-r_{k}(x)-\varphi_{i_{k},x_{k}}(\lambda_{i,k})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HLambda})}}{{=}}\;r_{k+1}(x)-r_{k}(x)-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}.\end{array}

Thus, as in the proof of Theorem
4
, we conclude that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}B_{N}&\leq&\max\limits_{x\in Q}\Big[\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}[r_{k}(x)-r_{k+1}(x)+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}]-\sum\limits_{k\not\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}[r_{k+1}(x)-r_{k}(x)+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}h_{k}^{2}]\;\Big]\\
\\
&\leq&h_{k(N)}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}h_{k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-LHLow})}}{{\leq}}\;M_{0}h_{k(N)}\sigma_{0}(N).\end{array}

Since
{\cal A}_{0}(N)\neq\emptyset
(see the proof of Theorem
4
), dividing this inequality by
\sigma_{0}(N)>0
, we get inequality (
7.4
).

Finally, note that for all
k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)
and
i=1,\dots,m
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{i,k}f_{i}(x_{k})&\leq&h_{k}^{2}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-LHLow})}}{{\leq}}\;\lambda_{i,k}\|f^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k})\|_{*}h_{k}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GBound})}}{{\leq}}\;\lambda_{i,k}M_{i}h_{k}\;\leq\;\lambda_{i,k}M_{i}h_{k(N)}.\end{array}

Thus, inequality (
7.3
) is proved.

\Box

8
Accuracy guarantees for the dual problem

In Sections
6
and
7
, we developed two convergent methods (
6.9
) and (
7.1
), which are able to approach the optimal solution of the primal problem (
6.1
), generating in parallel an approximate solution of the dual problem (
6.4
). Indeed, for
N\geq 1+a(0)
, denote

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{0}^{*}(N)&=&\min\limits_{k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)}f_{0}(x_{k}),\quad x^{*}_{N}\;=\;\arg\min\limits_{x}\Big\{f_{0}(x):\;x=x_{k},\;k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)\Big\}.\end{array}

Then, in view of Theorems
4
and (
5
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{0}^{*}(N)-\phi(\bar{\lambda}^{*}(N))&\leq&M_{0}h_{k(N)},\\
\\
\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}{1\over M_{i}}f_{i}(x_{k})&\leq&h_{k(N)},\quad k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N).\end{array}

(8.1)

Since
\phi(\bar{\lambda}^{*}(N))\leq f^{*}_{0}
, this inequality justfies that the point
x^{*}_{N}
is a good approximate solution to primal problem (
6.1
).

However, note that in our reasonings, we did not assume yet the
existence
of optimal solution of the dual problem (
6.4
). It appears that under our assumptions, this may not happen. In this case, since
f_{0}^{*}(N)
can be
significantly smaller
than
f_{0}^{*}
, inequality (
8.1
) cannot justify that vector
\bar{\lambda}^{*}(N)
delivers a good value of the dual objective function.

Let us look at the following example.

Example 4

Consider the following problem

\begin{array}[]{c}\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Big\{x^{(2)}:\;1-x^{(1)}\leq 0,\;\|x\|_{2}\leq 1\Big\}.\end{array}

In this case,
{\cal L}(x,\lambda)=x^{(2)}+\lambda(1-x^{(1)})
. Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi(\lambda)&=&\min\limits_{\|x\|_{2}\leq 1}{\cal L}(x,\lambda)\;=\;\lambda-[1+\lambda^{2}]^{1/2}.\end{array}

Thus, there is no duality gap:
\phi_{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sup\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\phi(\lambda)=0
. However, the optimal dual solution

\lambda^{*}
does not exist.

Let us look now at the perturbed feasible set

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\cal F}()\epsilon)&=&\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;\|x\|_{2}\leq 1,\;1-\epsilon\leq x^{(1)}\},\end{array}

where
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small. Note that it contains a point with the second coordinate equal to
\phi_{*}-\sqrt{\epsilon(2-\epsilon)}
. This means that the condition (
8.1
) can guarantee only that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi(\lambda^{*}(N)&\geq&\phi_{*}-\epsilon-\sqrt{\epsilon(2-\epsilon)}.\end{array}

Hence, for dual problems with nonexisting optimal solutions, we can expect a significant drop in the quality of approximation in terms of the function value.

\Box

Thus, in our complexity bounds, we need to take into account the size of optimal dual solution
\bar{\lambda}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
. Let the sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by one of the methods (
6.9
) or (
7.1
). Then, for any
k\geq 0
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}f_{0}(x_{k})\geq&\min\limits_{x\in Q}\Big\{f_{0}(x):\;\bar{f}(x)\leq\bar{f}(x_{k})\Big\}=\min\limits_{x\in Q}\max\limits_{\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}\Big\{f_{0}(x)+\langle\bar{\lambda},\bar{f}(x)-\bar{f}(x_{k})\rangle\Big\}\\
\\
=&\max\limits_{\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}\min\limits_{x\in Q}\Big\{f_{0}(x)+\langle\bar{\lambda},\bar{f}(x)-\bar{f}(x_{k})\rangle\Big\}\;=\;\max\limits_{\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}\Big\{\phi(\bar{\lambda})-\langle\bar{\lambda},\bar{f}(x_{k})\rangle\Big\}\\
\\
\geq&f^{*}_{0}-\langle\bar{\lambda}^{*},\bar{f}(x_{k})\rangle\;\geq\;f^{*}_{0}-\langle\bar{\lambda}^{*},\bar{M}\rangle\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}{1\over M_{i}}f_{i}(x_{k}).\end{array}

(8.2)

Thus, we have proved the following theorem.

Theorem 6

Under conditions of Theorem
4
or
5
, for all
N\geq 1+a(0)
and all
k\in{\cal A}_{0}(N)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{0}^{*}-\phi(\bar{\lambda}^{*}(N))&\leq&(M_{0}+\langle\bar{\lambda}^{*},\bar{M}\rangle)\,h_{k(N)}.\end{array}

(8.3)

Proof:

Indeed, in view of inequalities (
6.12
) and (
7.3
), we have
\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}{1\over M_{i}}f_{i}(x_{k})\leq h_{k(N)}
. Thus, we get the bound (
8.3
) from (
8.1
) and (
8.2
).

\Box

Recall that the size of optimal dual multipliers can be bounded by the standard
Slater condition
. Namely, let us assume existence of a point
\hat{x}\in Q
such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{i}(\hat{x})&<&0,\quad i=1,\dots,m.\end{array}

(8.4)

Then, in accordance to Lemma 3.1.21 in
[
7
]
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\bar{\lambda}^{*},-\bar{f}(\hat{x})\rangle&\leq&f_{0}(\hat{x})-f^{*}_{0}.\end{array}

(8.5)

Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\bar{\lambda}^{*},\bar{M}\rangle&=&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\lambda^{*}_{i}(-f_{i}(\hat{x}))\cdot{M_{i}\over-f_{i}(\hat{x})}\;\leq\;\langle\bar{\lambda}^{*},-\bar{f}(\hat{x})\rangle\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}{M_{i}\over-f_{i}(\hat{x})}\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-MBound})}}{{\leq}}&(f_{0}(\hat{x})-f^{*}_{0})\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}{M_{i}\over-f_{i}(\hat{x})}.\end{array}

Thus, the Slater condition provides us with the following bound:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{0}^{*}-\phi(\bar{\lambda}^{*}(N))&\leq&\Big(M_{0}+(f_{0}(\hat{x})-f^{*}_{0})\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq m}{M_{i}\over-f_{i}(\hat{x})}\Big)\,h_{k(N)},\end{array}

(8.6)

which is valid for all
N\geq 1+a(0)
. Note that we are able to compute vector
\bar{\lambda}^{*}(N)
without computing values of the dual function
\phi(\cdot)
, which can be very complex. In fact, computational complexity of a single value
\phi(\lambda)
can be of the same order as the complexity of solving the initial problem (
6.1
), or even more.

9
Subgradient method for unbounded feasible set

In the previous sections, we looked at optimization methods applicable to the bounded sets (see condition (
6.3
)). If this is not true, the second-order divergence condition (
5.6
) cannot help, and we need to find another way of justifying efficiency of the subgradient schemes. This is the goal of the current section.

We are still working with the problem (
6.1
), satisfying condition (
6.2
). However, the set
Q
is not bounded anymore.
Hence, we cannot count on Sion’s theorem (
6.4
).

In our method, we have a sequence of scaling coefficients
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfying condition

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\gamma_{k+1}&>&\gamma_{k}\;\geq\;0,\quad k\geq 0,\end{array}

(9.1)

a tolerance parameter
\epsilon>0
, and a rough estimate for the distance to the optimum
D_{0}>0
.

For functions
\varphi_{i,y}(\cdot)
defined by (
6.5
) with
y\in Q
, denote by
a=a_{i,k}(y)
the unique solution of the equation

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi_{i,y}\left({a\over\gamma_{k+1}}\right)&=&\left(1-{\gamma_{k}\over\gamma_{k+1}}\right)D_{0},\quad i=0,\dots,m.\end{array}

(9.2)

Let us look at the following optimization scheme.

\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\\
\quad\mbox{\bf Switching Subgradient Method for Unbounded Sets}\\
\\
\hline\cr\\
\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{{\bf Initialization.} Choose $x_{0}\in Q$, a sequence $\Gamma$ (see (\ref{eq-CondG})), and some $D_{0}>0$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf$k$th iteration ($k\geq 0$).} {\bf 1.} Define $\tau_{k}={\gamma_{k}\over\gamma_{k+1}}$ and $y_{k}=(1-\tau_{k})x_{0}+\tau_{k}x_{k}$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf 2.} If $\exists\;i\in\{1,\dots,m\}:\;f_{i}(y_{k})\geq\epsilon$, then set $i_{k}=i$. Otherwise, set $i_{k}=0$.}\\
\\
\mbox{{\bf 3.} Compute $a_{k}=a_{i_{k},k}(y_{k})$ by (\ref{eq-NStep}), and set $x_{k+1}=T_{i_{k},y_{k}}\Big({a_{k}\over\gamma_{k+1}}\Big)$.}\\
\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(9.3)

It seems that now, the selection of violated constraint by Step 2 looks more natural than in the methods (
6.9
) and (
7.1
).

For
x\in Q
, denote
\Delta_{k}(x)=\gamma_{k}\Big(\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)-\beta_{d}(x_{0},x)\Big)
. This is a linear function of
x
. In what follows, we assume the Bregman distance is convex with respect to its first argument:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(\alpha u_{1}+(1-\alpha)u_{2},x)&\leq&\alpha\beta_{d}(u_{1},x)+(1-\alpha)\beta_{d}(u_{2},x),\\
\\
&&\forall u_{1},u_{2},x\in Q,\;\alpha\in[0,1].\end{array}

(9.4)

This property is not very common. However, it is valid for the following two important examples.

Example 5

1.
Let
d(x)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x\|^{2}_{B}
( see (
1.5
)). Then
\beta_{d}(x,y)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x-y\|^{2}_{B}
and (
9.4
) holds.

2.
Let
Q=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x^{(i)}=1\}
. Define
d(x)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x^{(i)}\ln x^{(i)}
. Then, for
x,y\in Q
, we have
\beta_{d}(x,y)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y^{(i)}\ln{y^{(i)}\over x^{(i)}}
, and (
9.4
) holds also.

\Box

Now we can prove the following statement.

Lemma 6

Let the Bergman distance
\beta_{d}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfy (
9.4
). Then, for the sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
generated by method (
9.3
), and any
x\in Q
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Delta_{k+1}(x)&\leq&\Delta_{k}(x)+a_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k}),x-y_{k}\rangle+(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k})D_{0},\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

(9.5)

Proof:

Note that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Delta_{k+1}(x)-\Delta_{k}(x)&=&\gamma_{k+1}\Big(\beta_{d}(x_{k+1},x)-\beta_{d}(x_{0},x)\Big)-\gamma_{k}\Big(\beta_{d}(x_{k},x)-\beta_{d}(x_{0},x)\Big)\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BConv})}}{{\leq}}&\gamma_{k+1}\Big(\beta_{d}(x_{k+1},x)-\beta_{d}(y_{k},x)\Big)\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-Breg})}}{{=}}&\gamma_{k+1}\Big(\langle\nabla d(y_{k})-\nabla d(x_{k+1}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle-\beta_{d}(y_{k},x_{k+1}\rangle\Big).\end{array}

Since the first-order optimality conditions for point
x_{k+1}
tells us that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle a_{k}f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k})+\gamma_{k+1}(\nabla d(x_{k+1})-\nabla d(y_{k})),x-x_{k+1}\rangle&\geq&0,\quad x\in Q,\end{array}

we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Delta_{k+1}(x)-\Delta_{k}(x)&\leq&a_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k}),x-x_{k+1}\rangle-\gamma_{k+1}\beta_{d}(y_{k},x_{k+1}\rangle\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-NPhi})}}{{=}}&a_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k}),x-y_{k}\rangle+\gamma_{k+1}\varphi_{i,y_{k}}\left({a_{k}\over\gamma_{k+1}}\right)\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-NStep})}}{{=}}&a_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k}),x-y_{k}\rangle+(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k})D_{0},\end{array}

and this is inequality (
9.5
).

\Box

Since
\Delta_{0}(x)=0
, we can sum up the inequalities (
9.5
) for
k=0,\dots,N-1
with
N\geq 1
, and get the following consequence:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\gamma_{N}\beta_{d}(x_{N},x)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N-1}a_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k}),y_{k}-x\rangle&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Change})}}{{\leq}}&\gamma_{N}(\beta_{d}(x_{0},x)+D_{0}),\end{array}

(9.6)

which is valid for all
x\in Q
.

In order to approximate the optimal Lagrange multipliers of problem (
6.4
), we need to introduce the following objects:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\cal S}_{i}(N)&=&\{k:\;0\leq k\leq N-1,\;i_{k}=i\},\quad N\geq 1,\\
\\
\hat{\sigma}_{i}(N)&=&\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal S}_{i}(N)}a_{k},\quad i=0,\dots,m,\\
\\
\hat{\lambda}_{i}(N)&=&\hat{\sigma}_{i}(N)/\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N),\quad i=1,\dots,m.\end{array}

(9.7)

Denote
\hat{\lambda}(N)=(\hat{\lambda}_{1}(N),\dots,\hat{\lambda}_{m}(N))^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
and
f_{0}^{*}(N)=\min\limits_{k\in{\cal S}_{0}(N)}f_{0}(x_{k})
. Note that for all
k\in{\cal S}_{0}(N)
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{i}(x_{k})&\leq&\epsilon,\quad k=1,\dots,m.\end{array}

(9.8)

For our convergence result, we need to assume existence of the optimal solution
x^{*}
to problem (
6.1
). Let us introduce some bound

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D&\geq&\beta_{d}(x_{0},x^{*}),\end{array}

which is not used in the method(
9.3
).
Denote
Q_{D}=\{x\in Q:\;\beta_{d}(x_{0},x)\leq D\}
. Clearly, if we replace in problem (
6.1
) the set
Q
by
Q_{D}
, then its optimal solution will not be changed. However, now we can correctly
define a
restricted
dual function
\phi_{D}(\lambda)=\min\limits_{x\in Q_{D}}{\cal L}(x,\lambda)
. Let us prove our main result.

Theorem 7

Let functional components of problem (
6.1
) satisfy the following condition:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|f^{\prime}_{i}(x)\|_{*}&\leq&M,\quad i=1,\dots,m.\end{array}

(9.9)

Then, as far as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Sigma_{N}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;{1\over\gamma_{N}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N-1}\Big[\gamma_{k+1}(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k})\Big]^{1/2}&>&{\rho_{0}+D_{0}\over\sqrt{2D_{0}}}\cdot{M\over\epsilon},\end{array}

(9.10)

where
\rho_{0}=\min\limits_{y\in{\cal F}}\beta_{d}(x_{0},y)
,
we have
{\cal S}_{0}(N)\neq\emptyset
and
\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N)>0
. Moreover, if

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Sigma_{N}&>&{D+D_{0}\over\sqrt{2D_{0}}}\cdot{M\over\epsilon},\end{array}

(9.11)

then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f^{*}_{0}(N)-f^{*}_{0}\;\leq\;f^{*}_{0}(N)-\phi_{D}(\hat{\lambda}(N))&\leq&\epsilon.\end{array}

(9.12)

Proof:

Indeed, for any
x\in Q
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N-1}a_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k}),y_{k}-x\rangle&=&\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal S}_{0}(N)}a_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{0}(y_{k}),y_{k}-x\rangle+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal S}_{i}(N)}a_{k}\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(y_{k}),y_{k}-x\rangle\\
\\
&\geq&\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal S}_{0}(N)}a_{k}[f_{0}(y_{k})-f_{0}(x)]+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{k\in{\cal S}_{i}(N)}a_{k}[f_{i}(y_{k})-f_{i}(x)]\\
\\
&\geq&\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N)[f^{*}_{0}(N)-f_{0}(x)]+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\sigma}_{i}(N)[\epsilon-f_{i}(x)].\end{array}

Hence, in view of inequality (
9.6
), we get the following bound

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N)[f^{*}_{0}(N)-f_{0}(x)]+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\sigma}_{i}(N)[\epsilon-f_{i}(x)]&\leq&\gamma_{N}(\beta_{d}(x_{0},x)+D_{0}).\end{array}

Note that
\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\sigma}_{i}(N)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N-1}a_{k}
. Therefore, this inequality can be rewritten as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\gamma_{N}(\beta_{d}(x_{0},x)+D_{0})&\geq&\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N)[f^{*}_{0}(N)-f_{0}(x)-\epsilon]+\epsilon\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N-1}a_{k}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\sigma}_{i}(N)f_{i}(x).\end{array}

(9.13)

In view of condition(
9.2
), for
T_{i}=T_{i,y_{k}}\left({a_{i,k}(y_{k})\over\gamma_{k+1}}\right)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}&a_{i,k}(y_{k})\langle f^{\prime}_{i}(y_{k}),y_{k}-T_{i}\rangle\;=\;\gamma_{k+1}\beta_{d}(y_{k},T_{i})+(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k})D_{0}\\
\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BGrow})}}{{\geq}}&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\gamma_{k+1}\|T_{i}-y_{k}\|^{2}+(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k})D_{0}\;\geq\;\Big[2\gamma_{k+1}(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k})D_{0}\Big]^{1/2}\|T_{i}-y_{k}\|.\end{array}

Thus,
Ma_{k}(y_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-UGBound})}}{{\geq}}\Big[2\gamma_{k+1}(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k})D_{0}\Big]^{1/2}
, and we conclude that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N-1}a_{k}&\geq&{1\over M}\sqrt{2D_{0}}\gamma_{N}\Sigma_{N}.\end{array}

(9.14)

Let us assume that
{\cal S}_{0}(N)=\emptyset
. Then
\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N)=0
and for
x=\arg\min\limits_{y\in{\cal F}}\beta_{d}(x_{0},y)
inequality (
9.13
) leads to the following relation:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\gamma_{N}(\rho_{0}+D_{0})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-SigmaN})}}{{\geq}}&{\epsilon\over M}\sqrt{2D_{0}}\gamma_{N}\Sigma_{N}.\end{array}

However, this cannot happen in view of condition (
9.10
). Hence, inequality (
9.10
) implies
{\cal S}_{0}(N)\neq\emptyset
and
\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N)>0
. In this case, inequality (
9.13
) can be rewritten as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta_{d}(x_{0},x)+D_{0}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-SigmaN})}}{{\geq}}&{1\over\gamma_{N}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N)[f^{*}_{0}(N)-{\cal L}(x,\hat{\lambda}(N))-\epsilon]+{\epsilon\over M}\sqrt{2D_{0}}\Sigma_{N}.\end{array}

Maximizing the right-hand side of this inequality in
x\in Q_{d}
, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D+D_{0}&\geq&{1\over\gamma_{N}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}(N)[f^{*}_{0}(N)-\phi_{D}(\hat{\lambda}(N))-\epsilon]+{\epsilon\over M}\sqrt{2D_{0}}\Sigma_{N}.\end{array}

Hence, inequality (
9.11
) implies (
9.12
).

\Box

Thus, for the fast rate of convergence of method (
9.3
), we need to ensure a fast growth of the valies
\Sigma_{N}
. Let us choose

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\gamma_{k}&=&\sqrt{k},\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

(9.15)

Then
\gamma_{k+1}(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k})={\sqrt{k+1}\over\sqrt{k+1}+\sqrt{k}}\geq\mbox{${1\over 2}$}
. Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Sigma_{N}&\geq&{1\over\sqrt{N}}\cdot{N\over\sqrt{2}}\;=\;\sqrt{N\over 2}.\end{array}

(9.16)

In this case, inequality (
9.12
) can be ensured in
O(\epsilon^{-2})
iterations.

Note that method (
9.3
) is quite different from the existing optimization schemes. Let us write down how it looks like in the case
Q=\mathbb{E}
,
d(x)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|x\|^{2}_{B}
, and the parameter choice (
9.15
). In this case, the equation (
9.2
) can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{a^{2}\over 2\gamma_{k+1}^{2}}\|f^{\prime}_{i}(y_{k})\|^{2}&=&{\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}\over\gamma_{k+1}}D_{0}.\end{array}

Thus,
a_{k}=\sqrt{2\gamma_{k+1}(\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k})D_{0}}\cdot{1\over\|f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k})\|^{*}_{B}}\approx{\sqrt{D_{0}}\over\|f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k})\|^{*}_{B}}
, and the method looks as follows:

\begin{array}[]{|rl|}\hline\cr&\\
&\hskip 34.44434pt\mbox{\bf Switching Subgradient Method With $Q=\mathbb{E}$}\\
&\\
\hline\cr&\\
\mbox{\bf 0.}&\mbox{Choose $x_{0}\in\mathbb{E}$ and $D_{0}>0$. {\bf For $k\geq 0$, iterate:}}\\
&\\
\mbox{\bf 1.}&\mbox{Define $\tau_{k}=\sqrt{k\over k+1}$ and $y_{k}=(1-\tau_{k})x_{0}+\tau_{k}x_{k}$.}\\
&\\
\mbox{\bf 2.}&\mbox{If $\exists i:1\leq i\leq m,\;f_{i}(y_{k})\geq\epsilon$, then set $i_{k}=i$. Otherwise, set $i_{k}=0$.}\\
&\\
\mbox{\bf 3.}&\mbox{Set $x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\sqrt{D_{0}\over k+1}\cdot B^{-1}f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k})/\|f^{\prime}_{i_{k}}(y_{k})\|^{*}_{B}$.}\\
&\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(9.17)
