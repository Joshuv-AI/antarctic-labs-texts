C ∗ C^{*} -algebras associated to directed graphs of groups, and models of Kirchberg algebras
Victor Wu

Abstract
. We introduce C ∗ C^{*} -algebras associated to directed graphs of groups. In particular, we associate a combinatorial C ∗ C^{*} -algebra to each row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and show that this C ∗ C^{*} -algebra is Morita equivalent to the crossed product coming from the corresponding group action on the boundary of a directed tree. Finally, we show that these C ∗ C^{*} -algebras (and their Morita equivalent crossed products) contain the class of stable UCT Kirchberg algebras.



1.
Introduction

Universal
C^{*}
-algebras for generators and relations encoding an underlying combinatorial object have long been studied because of their tractable nature, and their ability to prove rich sources of examples. The study of these
C^{*}
-algebras, commonly referred to as combinatorial
C^{*}
-algebras, began with the work of Cuntz and Krieger, who in
[
8
]
associated a
C^{*}
-algebra to a finite
\{0,1\}
-matrix. These algebras developed into directed graph
C^{*}
-algebras, which in turn have branched out into various generalisations, including
C^{*}
-algebras of higher rank graphs
[
18
]
and left cancellative small categories
[
33
]
(see also
[
27
]
for an overview).

One abundant class of combinatorial objects are
graphs of groups
, which were introduced by Serre in
[
31
]
and further studied by Bass in
[
4
]
. Roughly speaking, these are (undirected) graphs where each vertex and edge has an associated group, and each edge group embeds into its adjacent vertex groups. The key idea of what is known as Bass–Serre theory is that graphs of groups “encode” group actions on trees: given a group acting on a tree (with a mild assumption), we can construct a graph of groups from the quotient graph and the stabiliser subgroups at the vertices and edges; and from this graph of groups we can construct a group, called the
fundamental group
, and an action of this group on a tree, called the
Bass–Serre tree
, which is isomorphic to the original action. We provide a more detailed review of Bass–Serre theory in Section
2
, which also contains other background definitions and theory necessary for the rest of the paper.

Graphs of groups were first studied in a
C^{*}
-algebraic setting in
[
22
]
, which considered finite graphs of finite groups; these were then generalised to countable graphs of countable groups (with some regularity assumptions) in
[
6
]
. The authors of
[
6
]
also proved a ‘
C^{*}
-algebraic Bass–Serre theorem’, which states that their graph of groups
C^{*}
-algebra is stably isomorphic to the crossed product coming from the action of the fundamental group on the boundary of the Bass–Serre tree.

In this paper, we consider what we call
directed
graphs of groups: these are simply graphs of groups where the graph additionally carries an orientation. While these objects are not so interesting from a geometric group theory perspective (as the orientation data does not fundamentally change anything in the theory), they have found interest in other fields: for example, it was shown in
[
20
]
that directed graph of groups are in a one-to-one correspondence with skeletal cancellative Levi categories.

Our motivation for studying directed graphs of groups comes from the observation that Bass–Serre theory implies that directed graphs of groups are in a one-to-one correspondence with group actions on
directed
trees: we can simply ‘lift’ the orientation of a directed graph of groups to the Bass–Serre tree. These actions fit into the framework studied in
[
7
]
, which provides structural theorems and
K
-theoretic formulae for crossed products coming from such actions.

In Section
3
we develop the theory of directed graphs of groups that we will use in the rest of the paper. As part of this, we give an alternate approach to associating a category (which we will call the
word category
) to a directed graph of groups; this category matches the one from
[
20
]
, but the construction is more direct and is more natural for our purposes.

Inspired by the relations in
[
6
]
, we associate a
C^{*}
-algebra to each directed graph of groups in Section
4
, where we also show that this algebra is isomorphic to the
C^{*}
-algebra of the word category of the directed graph of groups. These directed graph of groups
C^{*}
-algebras generalise directed graph
C^{*}
-algebras (in that for a directed graph of groups where all groups are trivial, the
C^{*}
-algebra of the directed graph of groups is isomorphic to that of the underlying directed graph), and many properties of directed graph
C^{*}
-algebras generalise to this broader class of
C^{*}
-algebras as well.

One such property is that these
C^{*}
-algebras are Morita equivalent to certain crossed products by commutative
C^{*}
-algebras. For a directed graph
E
, it was shown in
[
17
]
that its
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(E)
is Morita equivalent to the crossed product
C_{0}(\partial T)\rtimes G
, where
T
is the universal covering tree of
E
and
G
is the fundamental group of
E
, which acts naturally on
T
. For a directed graph of groups
\mathcal{G}_{+}
, we have the following generalisation (this can also be viewed as a ‘
C^{*}
-algebraic
directed
Bass–Serre theorem’, in line with
[
6
]
).

Theorem A

(Theorem
5.1
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite (connected) directed graph of groups with no sources, and choose a base vertex
x\in\Gamma^{0}
. Write
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
for the fundamental group of
\mathcal{G}
based at
x
, and write
X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
for the directed Bass–Serre tree based at
x
. Then
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
is Morita equivalent to
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
.

Section
5
is dedicated to the proof of this theorem, which has two steps: first we find a tractable groupoid model for
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
with some help from the general results in
[
23
]
concerning groupoid models for the
C^{*}
-algebras of categories; then we show that this groupoid is equivalent to the transformation groupoid
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\ltimes\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
, whose
C^{*}
-algebra is the crossed product in the theorem statement.

Since Morita equivalence preserves
K
-theory and many structural properties of
C^{*}
-algebras such as simplicity and nuclearity, we can use the results of
[
7
]
to study these properties for directed graph of groups
C^{*}
-algebras. In Section
6
, the final section of this paper, we study these properties for the special case of directed graphs of
infinite cyclic
groups (that is, directed graphs of groups where all vertex and edge groups are infinite cyclic); these are simple enough to study while also providing a rich class of examples of
C^{*}
-algebras.

As a demonstration of this, we turn to the study of Kirchberg algebras (simple, separable, nuclear and purely infinite
C^{*}
-algebras). By the celebrated Kirchberg–Phillips theorem (
[
16
,
26
]
), stable Kirchberg algebras satisfying the Universal Coefficient Theorem (UCT) are classified completely by their
K
-theory, making this an interesting class of
C^{*}
-algebras to study. This classification result has led to the development of concrete models for Kirchberg algebras, such as Spielberg’s hybrid graph algebras
[
32
]
and Katsura’s topological graph algebras
[
14
]
, which have in turn led to a deeper understanding of Kirchberg algebras (see for example
[
15
]
).

In this paper, we find sufficient conditions on a directed graph of infinite cyclic groups for its
C^{*}
-algebra to be a UCT Kirchberg algebra, and we also provide a formula for the
K
-theory of these algebras. Using these results, we are able to prove the following theorem, which provides two additional models of Kirchberg algebras.

Theorem B

(Theorem
6.2
)
.

Let
A
be a stable UCT Kirchberg algebra. There exists a countable, row-finite directed graph of infinite cyclic groups
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
with no sources, such that
A\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})\cong C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{+},x)
for any
x\in\Gamma^{0}
.

Notably, we show how to construct any stable UCT Kirchberg algebra as a crossed product
C^{*}
-algebra. Crossed product
C^{*}
-algebras, particularly those arising from a group action on a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra, are a fundamental class of examples of
C^{*}
-algebras, and have been greatly studied from the perspective of determining conditions under which they are Kirchberg algebras (
[
2
,
11
,
13
,
19
]
). It is known that all stable UCT Kirchberg algebras are crossed products by
A\mathbb{T}
-algebras
[
30
]
, but the construction is not concrete. Theorem
B
is, to the best of our knowledge, the first concrete construction of (stable) UCT Kirchberg algebras as crossed products. We hope that this might help develop our understanding of Kirchberg algebras, as well as promote further research into the modelling of classifiable
C^{*}
-algebras by crossed products.

2.
Background

In this section we collect the background material required for the paper. We recall definitions about graphs and trees in Section
2.1
, left cancellative small categories and their
C^{*}
-algebras in Section
2.2
, and groupoids of dynamical origin in Section
2.3
. Finally in Section
2.4
we recount some of the theory of (undirected) graphs of groups.

2.1.
Graphs and trees

We start by defining the notions of graph that we will use throughout this paper.

Definition 2.1
.

A
directed graph

E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
consists of countable sets of
vertices

E^{0}
and
edges

E^{1}
, along with
range
and
source
maps
r,s\colon E^{1}\to E^{0}
. We say that
E
is
row-finite
if
r^{-1}(v)
is finite for all
v\in E^{0}
(that is, each vertex receives finitely many edges), and
E

has no sources
if
r^{-1}(v)\neq\emptyset
for all
v\in E^{0}
.

Definition 2.2
.

An
undirected graph
(or simply a
graph
)
\Gamma=(\Gamma^{0},\Gamma^{1},r,s)
is a directed graph equipped with an “edge reversal” map
e\mapsto\overline{e}
on
\Gamma^{1}
such that for all
e\in\Gamma^{1}
, we have

\overline{e}\neq e,\quad\overline{\overline{e}}=e,\quad\text{and}\quad s(e)=r(\overline{e}).

An
orientation
of
\Gamma
is a subset
\Gamma_{+}^{1}
of
\Gamma^{1}
containing exactly one element of
\{e,\overline{e}\}
for each
e\in\Gamma^{1}
.

If
\Gamma_{+}^{1}
is an orientation of the graph
\Gamma
, then we can consider the directed graph
\Gamma_{+}=(\Gamma^{0},\Gamma_{+}^{1},r,s)
, where the range and source maps are inherited from
\Gamma
. The original graph
\Gamma
can be recovered from
\Gamma_{+}
by including a “reversal”
{\overline{e}}
for each
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
; we call
\Gamma
the
underlying undirected graph
of
\Gamma_{+}
.

A
path (of length
n
)
in a directed graph
E
is a sequence of edges
e_{1}e_{2}\dots e_{n}
in
E^{1}
satisfying
s(e_{i})=r(e_{i+1})
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
(in the case that
n=0
, then a path is just a single vertex
v\in E^{0}
). We note that we are using the “Australian” convention for path notation, to be consistent with
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
. For a path
\lambda=e_{1}e_{2}\dots e_{n}
in
E
, we write
\lvert\lambda\rvert=n
for the length of
\lambda
. We write
E^{n}
for the set of paths in
E
of length
n
, and we write
vE^{n}
for the subset of
E^{n}
consisting of paths with range
v\in E^{0}
. We write
E^{*}
for the set of all paths in
E
.

For (undirected) graphs, we have the additional notion of a
reduced
path, which is a path
e_{1}e_{2}\dots e_{n}
such that
e_{i+1}\neq{\overline{e}}_{i}
for
1\leq i<n
(that is, there is no “back-tracking” in the path). An undirected graph is
connected
if there is a (reduced) path between any two vertices, and a directed graph is connected if its underlying undirected graph is connected. All directed and undirected graphs in this paper will be connected unless specified otherwise.

A
tree
is a graph such that there is a unique reduced path from any vertex to any other vertex. A
directed tree
is a directed graph whose underlying undirected graph is a tree.

We will be considering the following natural topological spaces arising from directed graphs and trees:

(1)

Let
E
be a directed graph. We write
E^{\infty}
for the space of infinite paths in
E
(that is, infinite sequences of edges
e_{1}e_{2}\dots
in
E^{1}
with
s(e_{i})=r(e_{i+1})
for all
i\geq 1
) with topology generated by finite unions, intersections and set differences of subsets of the form
\lambda E^{\infty}
,
\lambda\in E^{*}
.

(2)

Let
T
be a directed tree. We can define an equivalence relation on the set of infinite paths in
T
as follows. We say that two infinite paths
e_{1}e_{2}\dots
and
f_{1}f_{2}\dots
in
T
are
shift-tail equivalent
if there exist positive integers
N,M
such that
e_{N+i}=f_{M+i}
for all
i\geq 0
. The
boundary

\partial T
of
T
is the space of shift-tail equivalence classes of infinite paths in
T
, with topology generated by finite unions, intersections and set differences of subsets of the form
Z(v):=\{[\lambda]:\lambda\in vT^{\infty}\}
,
v\in T^{0}
.

2.2.
Left cancellative small categories and their
C^{*}
-algebras

We now recall definitions relating to left cancellative small categories and their
C^{*}
-algebras.

Definition 2.3
.

A small category
\Lambda
is
left cancellative
if
\alpha\beta=\alpha\gamma\implies\beta=\gamma
for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\Lambda
with
s(\alpha)=r(\beta)=r(\gamma)
. We will refer to left cancellative small categories as
LCSC
s for short.

For any
\alpha\in\Lambda
, we will write
\alpha\Lambda
to denote the set of morphisms in
\Lambda
of the form
\alpha\lambda
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

Definition 2.4
.

An LCSC
\Lambda
is
finitely aligned
if for every
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
there is a finite set
F\subseteq\Lambda
such that
\alpha\Lambda\cap\beta\Lambda=\bigcup_{\gamma\in F}\gamma\Lambda
. If
F
can always be chosen to have at most one element (that is, for every
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
either
\alpha\Lambda\cap\beta\Lambda=\emptyset
, or there is
\gamma\in\Lambda
such that
\alpha\Lambda\cap\beta\Lambda=\gamma\Lambda
), then
\Lambda
is
singly aligned
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC. Define a relation
\leq
on
\Lambda
by
\alpha\leq\beta
if
\beta\in\alpha\Lambda
(this relation is reflexive and transitive but not necessarily antisymmetric, so it is not a partial order in general). A
common extension
of
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
is an element
\gamma\in\Lambda
such that
\alpha,\beta\leq\gamma
, and a common extension
\gamma
of
\alpha,\beta
is
minimal
if there is no common extension
\delta
of
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
\delta\leq\gamma
and
\gamma\not\leq\delta
. Write
\alpha\vee\beta
for the set of minimal common extensions of
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC, and let
C\subseteq\Lambda
. A subset
E\subseteq C
is
exhaustive
if for every
\alpha\in C
, there exists
\beta\in E
such that
\alpha\Lambda\cap\beta\Lambda\neq\emptyset
.

In
[
33
]
, Spielberg defined the
Cuntz–Krieger algebra
for a LCSC. In the finitely aligned case, we have the following presentation by generators and relations, which we use here as an alternate definition:

Definition 2.7

(
[
33
, Theorem 10.15(i)]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC. A
Cuntz–Krieger
\Lambda
-family
is a collection
\{S_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
satisfying

(1)

S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\lambda}=S_{s(\lambda)}
;

(2)

S_{\lambda}S_{\mu}=S_{\lambda\mu}
if
s(\lambda)=r(\mu)
;

(3)

S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}=\bigvee_{\nu\in\lambda\vee\mu}S_{\nu}S_{\nu}^{*}
;

(4)

S_{v}=\bigvee_{\lambda\in F}S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}
if
F\subseteq v\Lambda
is a finite exhaustive set.

The
Cuntz–Krieger algebra
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by a Cuntz–Krieger
\Lambda
-family.

Remark 2.8
.

Relations (S1) and (S2) imply that for each object
v\in\Lambda^{0}
(which we identify with the identity morphism
1_{v}\in\Lambda
), the element
S_{v}=S_{1_{v}}
is a projection (take
\lambda=\mu=1_{v}
in the relations). Thus (S1) implies that each
S_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
is a partial isometry.

2.3.
Groupoids of dynamical origin

Here we recall the definitions of groupoid and inverse semigroup actions on spaces, and of groupoids that encode these actions. We refer the reader to the book
[
24
]
for a more in-depth discussion of these objects.

Definition 2.9
.

A
groupoid

\mathscr{G}
is a small category with inverses: that is, it has a set of units
\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
and morphisms
\mathscr{G}
such that:

(1)

each morphism
g\in\mathscr{G}
has a
range

r(g)\in\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
and a source
s(g)\in\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
;

(2)

there is a partially defined composition on
\mathscr{G}
, where a pair of morphisms
(g,h)\in\mathscr{G}\times\mathscr{G}
is composable if and only if
s(g)=r(h)
;

(3)

each unit
x\in\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
has a corresponding
identity morphism

x\in\mathscr{G}
(to which it will be identified) satisfying
xg=g
for all
g\in\mathscr{G}
with range
x
, and
gx=g
for all
g\in\mathscr{G}
with source
x
; and

(4)

each morphism
g\in\mathscr{G}
has a (unique)
inverse

g^{-1}\in\mathscr{G}
satisfying
gg^{-1}=r(g)
and
g^{-1}g=s(g)
.

A
topological groupoid
is a groupoid which additionally carries a topology such that composition and inversion are continuous. All topological groupoids we consider will be
Hausdorff étale
, meaning that they have a Hausdorff topology and the range map
r:\mathscr{G}\to\mathscr{G}
is a local homeomorphism.

If
x
and
y
are units in
\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
, we will write
x\mathscr{G}
to denote the set of morphisms in
\mathscr{G}
with range
x
, and
x\mathscr{G}y
to denote the set of morphisms in
\mathscr{G}
with range
x
and source
y
. If
U\subseteq\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
is a subset of the unit space of
\mathscr{G}
, we write
\mathscr{G}|_{U}
for the
restriction of
\mathscr{G}
to
U
, which is the subgroupoid of
\mathscr{G}
consisting of morphisms whose range and source are both in
U
.

Definition 2.10
.

An
inverse semigroup
is a semigroup
\mathcal{S}
such that for each element
s\in\mathcal{S}
there is a unique element
s^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
ss^{*}s=s
and
s^{*}ss^{*}=s^{*}
. A
zero element
of
\mathcal{S}
is an element
0\in S
such that
s0=0s=0
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Both groupoids and inverse semigroups act on topological spaces by
partial homeomorphisms
, which we now define.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
X
be a locally compact Hausdorff space. A
partial homeomorphism

\varphi
of
X
is a homeomorphism from a subspace
D(\varphi)
of
X
(the
domain
of
\varphi
) to another subspace
R(\varphi)
of
X
(the
range
of
\varphi
). For two partial homeomorphisms
\varphi,\psi
of
X
, the composition
\varphi\circ\psi
is the partial homeomorphism with domain
\psi^{-1}(R(\psi)\cap D(\varphi))
and range
\varphi(R(\psi)\cap D(\varphi))
satisfying
(\varphi\circ\psi)(x)=\varphi(\psi(x))
for
x\in D(\varphi\circ\psi)
.

Definition 2.12

(Actions)
.

Let
X
be a locally compact Hausdorff space.

(1)

An action of a groupoid
\mathscr{G}
on
X
consists of a surjective
anchor map

\tau\colon X\to\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
, and a collection of partial homeomorphisms
\{\varphi_{g}:g\in\mathscr{G}\}
of
X
such that

(a)

for all
g\in\mathscr{G}
, we have
D(\varphi_{g})=\tau^{-1}(s(g))
and
R(\varphi_{g})=\tau^{-1}(r(g))
;

(b)

for all
g\in\mathscr{G}
, we have
\varphi_{g^{-1}}=\varphi_{g}^{-1}
; and

(c)

for all
g,h\in\mathscr{G}
with
s(g)=r(h)
, we have
\varphi_{gh}=\varphi_{g}\circ\varphi_{h}
.

We will often write
g\cdot x
instead of
\varphi_{g}(x)
, for
g\in\mathscr{G}
and
x\in X
.

(2)

An action of an inverse semigroup
\mathcal{S}
on
X
is a collection of partial homeomorphisms
\{\varphi_{s}:s\in\mathcal{S}\}
of
X
such that for all
s,t\in\mathcal{S}
, we have that
\varphi_{s^{*}}=\varphi_{s}^{-1}
and
\varphi_{st}=\varphi_{s}\circ\varphi_{t}
. As with groupoid actions,
we will often write
s\cdot x
instead of
\varphi_{s}(x)
, for
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
x\in X
.

Definition 2.13

(Groupoids associated to actions)
.

Let
X
be a locally compact Hausdorff space.

(1)

Suppose that the groupoid
\mathscr{G}
acts on
X
. The
transformation groupoid
of the action
\mathscr{G}\curvearrowright X
is the set
\mathscr{G}\ltimes X:=\{(g,x):g\in\mathscr{G},\ x\in X,\ \tau(x)=s(g)\}
, where for
(g,x)\in\mathscr{G}\ltimes X
:

(a)

r(g,x)=x
and
s(g,x)=g\cdot x
;

(b)

if
(h,y)\in\mathscr{G}
satisfies
y=g\cdot x
, then
(h,y)(g,x)=(hg,x)
; and

(c)

(g,x)^{-1}=(g^{-1},g\cdot x)
.

The topology of
\mathscr{G}\ltimes X
is the subspace topology induced by the product topology on
\mathscr{G}\times X
(where
\mathscr{G}
has the discrete topology).

(2)

Suppose that the inverse semigroup
\mathcal{S}
acts on
X
. Write
S*X=\{(s,x):s\in\mathcal{S},\ x\in D_{s^{*}s}\}
and define an equivalence relation on
S*X
by
(s,x)\sim(t,y)
if and only if
x=y
and there is an idempotent
e\in\mathcal{S}
such that
x\in D_{e}
and
se=te
. The
groupoid of germs
of the action
\mathcal{S}\curvearrowright X
is the set
\mathcal{S}\ltimes X=S*X/\sim
with composition given by
[s,x][t,y]=[st,y]
if
x=t\cdot y
, and with inverse given by
[s,x]^{-1}=[s^{*},s\cdot x]
. The topology of
\mathcal{S}\ltimes X
is generated by open sets of the form
[s,U]:=\{[s,x]:x\in U\}
where
U\subseteq D_{s^{*}s}\subseteq X
is an open set.

To any Hausdorff étale groupoid
\mathscr{G}
one can associate a
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(\mathscr{G})
; we refer the reader to
[
28
]
for details. Here we just note that for an action of a group
G
on a locally compact Hausdorff space
X
, the
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(G\ltimes X)
of the associated transformation groupoid is isomorphic to the
full crossed product

C_{0}(X)\rtimes G
for the induced action of
G
on
C_{0}(X)
.

2.4.
Graphs of groups

In this subsection we briefly recall the theory of (undirected) graphs of groups. For a more detailed account, we refer the reader to
[
6
]
.

Definition 2.14
.

A
graph of groups

\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
is a graph
\Gamma
along with:

(1)

a
vertex group

G_{v}
for each
v\in\Gamma^{0}
;

(2)

an
edge group

G_{e}
for each
e\in\Gamma^{1}
, such that
G_{e}=G_{\overline{e}}
for all
e\in\Gamma^{1}
; and

(3)

a monomorphism
\alpha_{e}\colon G_{e}\to G_{r(e)}
for each
e\in\Gamma^{1}
.

Graphs of groups naturally arise as “quotient objects” of group actions on trees: if a group
G
acts on a tree
T
without inversions (that is, there are no
g\in G
,
e\in T^{1}
with
g\cdot e=\overline{e}
), the action induces a graph of groups as follows. We let
\Gamma=G\backslash T
be the quotient graph of
T
under the action of
G
: that is, the graph whose vertices and edges are the vertex and edge orbits of
T
respectively, and the range and source maps are induced from those of
T
. For each
v\in\Gamma^{0}
we pick a lift
v^{\prime}\in T^{0}
, and define the vertex group
G_{v}
to be the stabiliser subgroup of
G
at
v^{\prime}\in T^{0}
; similarly for each
e\in\Gamma^{1}
we pick a lift
e^{\prime}\in T^{1}
, and define the edge group
G_{e}
to be the stabiliser subgroup of
G
at
e^{\prime}\in T^{1}
. Finally, for each
e\in\Gamma^{1}
we define the monomorphism
\alpha_{e}
as follows. Write
v=r(e)
. Then
v^{\prime}
is in the same vertex orbit as
r(e^{\prime})
, so there is some
g\in G
such that
g\cdot r(e^{\prime})=v^{\prime}
. Now, each
h\in G_{e}
fixes
e^{\prime}
and thus also fixes
r(e^{\prime})
; then
ghg^{-1}
fixes
v^{\prime}
, so
ghg^{-1}\in G_{v}=G_{r(e)}
. Hence we can define
\alpha_{e}\colon G_{e}\to G_{r(e)}
by
h\mapsto ghg^{-1}
.

In fact, from any graph of groups
\mathcal{G}
, one can construct a group action on a tree whose induced graph of groups is isomorphic to
\mathcal{G}
(in the sense that there is an isomorphism of graphs, and there are isomorphisms of corresponding vertex and edge groups which preserve the monomorphisms); this is part of what is known as the Fundamental Theorem of Bass–Serre theory, which we quote as Theorem
2.21
. Before we can state this theorem, we recount some more theory concerning graphs of groups. We start by discussing what is called the
fundamental groupoid
of
\mathcal{G}
, a notion introduced in
[
12
]
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
be a graph of groups. The
fundamental groupoid
F(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{G}
is the groupoid defined by the following presentation. The vertex set of
F(\mathcal{G})
is
\Gamma^{0}
; the morphisms of
F(\mathcal{G})
are generated by
\Gamma^{1}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{v\in\Gamma^{0}}G_{v}
, where the range and source of
e\in\Gamma^{1}
in
F(\mathcal{G})
are as they are in
\Gamma
, and for any
v\in\Gamma^{0}
and
g\in G_{v}
we have
r(g)=s(g)=v
; and the relations consist of the relations in each vertex group
G_{v}
, along with

(1)

\overline{e}=e^{-1}
for all
e\in\Gamma^{1}
; and

(2)

\alpha_{e}(g)e=e\alpha_{\overline{e}}(g)
for all
e\in\Gamma^{1}
and
g\in G_{e}
.

Elements of the fundamental groupoid can be represented by
\mathcal{G}
-words
, which we will now define.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
be a graph of groups. A
\mathcal{G}
-word (of length
n
)
is a sequence of the form
g_{1}e_{1}g_{2}e_{2}\dots g_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
, where
e_{1}\dots e_{n}
is a path in
\Gamma
,
g_{i}\in G_{r(e_{i})}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and
g_{n+1}\in G_{s(e_{n})}
(in the case where
n=0
, the
\mathcal{G}
-word is just an element
g_{1}
of some vertex group
G_{v}
). We set the range and source of a
\mathcal{G}
-word to be
r(e_{1})
and
s(e_{n})
respectively (in the case where
n=0
, we set the range and source to be the vertex whose group the element
g_{1}
belongs to).

We will often identify a
\mathcal{G}
-word with its image in
F(\mathcal{G})
. However, note that multiple
\mathcal{G}
-words can represent the same element of
F(\mathcal{G})
. In order to find a unique representative for each element of
F(\mathcal{G})
, we need the concept of a
normalised
word.

Definition 2.17
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
be a graph of groups. A
set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}
is a set
\Sigma=\{\Sigma_{e}:e\in\Gamma^{1}\}
, where each
\Sigma_{e}
is a transversal of
G_{r(e)}/\alpha_{e}(G_{e})
which includes the identity element
1_{r(e)}\in G_{r(e)}
. A
\mathcal{G}
-word
g_{1}e_{1}\dots g_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
is called
\Sigma
-normalised
if
g_{i}\in\Sigma_{e_{i}}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and the
\mathcal{G}
-word does not have any subsequence of the form
\overline{e}1_{r(e)}e
.

Proposition 2.18

(
[
12
, Theorem]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
be a graph of groups, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}
. Each element of
F(\mathcal{G})
is represented by a unique
\Sigma
-normalised word.

The fundamental groupoid
F(\mathcal{G})
acts naturally on the following graph
[
6
, Section 2.4]
.

Definition 2.19
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
be a graph of groups. Define the graph
W_{\mathcal{G}}
as follows. The vertex set of
W_{\mathcal{G}}
is

W_{\mathcal{G}}^{0}=\bigsqcup_{v,w\in\Gamma^{0}}vF(\mathcal{G})w/G_{w}=\{\gamma G_{w}:v,w\in\Gamma^{1},\ \gamma\in vF(\mathcal{G})w\};

and for
\gamma\in vF(\mathcal{G})w
and
\gamma^{\prime}\in v^{\prime}F(\mathcal{G})w^{\prime}
, there is an edge in
W_{\mathcal{G}}
from
\gamma^{\prime}G_{w^{\prime}}
to
\gamma G_{w}
if and only if
v=v^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{-1}\gamma^{\prime}\in G_{v}eG_{w}
for some
e\in v\Gamma^{1}w
.

We note that
W_{\mathcal{G}}
is not connected in general. Each connected component of
W_{\mathcal{G}}
consists of the vertices
\bigsqcup_{w\in\Gamma^{0}}xF(\mathcal{G})w/G_{w}
for some unique, fixed
x\in\Gamma^{0}
; by
[
4
, Theorem 1.17]
, each of these components are trees, so
W_{\mathcal{G}}
is a forest.

The fundamental groupoid
F(\mathcal{G})
acts on
W_{\mathcal{G}}
in the following way. Let
v,w\in\Gamma^{0}
and take
\gamma^{\prime}\in vF(\mathcal{G})w
. An element
\gamma\in F(\mathcal{G})
acts on
\gamma^{\prime}G_{w}\in W_{\mathcal{G}}^{0}
if and only if
v=s(\gamma)
, in which case we have
\gamma\cdot\gamma^{\prime}G_{w}=\gamma\gamma^{\prime}G_{w}
. Since
W_{\mathcal{G}}
is a forest, the action on the vertices induces an action on the graph, and one can check that this action is without inversions.

We can now define the group action on a tree induced by a graph of groups.

Definition 2.20
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
be a graph of groups, and let
x\in\Gamma^{0}
. The
fundamental group of
\mathcal{G}
based at
x
, which we denote by
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
, is the isotropy group of
F(\mathcal{G})
at
x
. The
Bass–Serre tree of
\mathcal{G}
based at
x
, which we denote by
X_{\mathcal{G},x}
, is the connected component in
W_{\mathcal{G}}
of the vertex
G_{x}
(equivalently, it is the restriction of
W_{\mathcal{G}}
to the vertices
\bigsqcup_{w\in\Gamma^{0}}xF(\mathcal{G})w/G_{w}
).

The action of
F(\mathcal{G})
on
W_{\mathcal{G}}
restricts to an action of the group
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
on the tree
X_{\mathcal{G},x}
. The significance of this action comes from the Fundamental Theorem of Bass–Serre theory, which we now state.

Theorem 2.21
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
be a graph of groups, and let
x\in\Gamma^{0}
. The induced graph of groups for the action of
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
on
X_{\mathcal{G},x}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{G}
. Conversely, if
\mathcal{G}
is induced from an action (without inversions) of a group
G
on the tree
T
, then there is an isomorphism of groups
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\cong G
and an equivariant isomorphism of trees
X_{\mathcal{G},x}\cong T
.

Remark 2.22
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
be a graph of groups, and let
x\in\Gamma^{0}
. For any vertex
v\in\Gamma^{0}
, the lifts of
v
in the Bass–Serre tree
X_{\mathcal{G},x}
are precisely the vertices

xF(\mathcal{G})v/G_{v}=\{\gamma G_{v}:\gamma\in xF(\mathcal{G})v\}.

For any edge
e\in\Gamma^{1}
, the lifts of
e
in
X_{\mathcal{G},x}
are the edges with range
\gamma G_{r(e)}
for some
\gamma\in xF(\mathcal{G})r(e)
and with source
\gamma geG_{s(e)}
for some
g\in G_{r(e)}
.

3.
Directed graphs of groups

In this section, we introduce and develop some theory for
directed graph of groups
. We start by defining directed graphs of groups in Section
3.1
, as well as explaining how they relate to group actions on directed trees. In Section
3.2
, we study a canonical (left cancellative small) category associated to a directed graph of groups, which we call its
word category
. Finally, in Section
3.3
we study an action, canonically associated to a directed graph of groups, of a groupoid on a directed forest.

3.1.
Directed graphs of groups

We start by defining directed graphs of groups, and then briefly discuss a ‘directed version’ of Bass–Serre theory.

Definition 3.1
.

A
directed graph of groups

\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
is a graph of groups
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
where the graph
\Gamma
additionally carries an orientation
\Gamma_{+}^{1}
. We call
\mathcal{G}
the
underlying graph of groups of
\mathcal{G}_{+}
.

Remark 3.2
.

We note that directed graphs of groups have also been studied in
[
20
]
, where they were called ‘graphs of groups with a given orientation’.

Just as graphs of groups are induced from group actions on trees,
directed
graphs of groups are induced from group actions on
directed
trees. Indeed, if a group
G
acts on a directed tree
T_{+}
, then it acts on the underlying undirected tree
T
without inversion; this induces a graph of groups
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
, and we can give
\Gamma
the orientation induced by the orientation on
T
, yielding a directed graph of groups
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
. Moreover, we can recover the action
G\curvearrowright T_{+}
from
\mathcal{G}_{+}
, like in the undirected case: the acting group is still the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
, and the directed tree is the Bass–Serre tree
X_{\mathcal{G},x}
with a suitable orientation, which we now describe.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups, and choose a base vertex
x\in\Gamma^{0}
. The
directed Bass–Serre tree
X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
of
\mathcal{G}_{+}
based at
x
is the directed tree with
X_{\mathcal{G},x}
as the underlying undirected tree, and where an edge from
\gamma^{\prime}G_{w}
to
\gamma G_{v}
is in the orientation if and only if
\gamma^{-1}\gamma^{\prime}\in G_{v}eG_{w}
for some
e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1}w
.

We now state and prove the directed analogue of the fundamental theorem of Bass–Serre theory (Theorem
2.21
).

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups, and choose a base vertex
x\in\Gamma^{0}
. The induced directed graph of groups for the action of
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
on
X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. Conversely, if
\mathcal{G}_{+}
is induced from an action of a group
G
on a directed tree
T_{+}
, then there is an isomorphism of groups
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\cong G
and an equivariant isomorphism of directed trees
X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}\cong T_{+}
.

Proof.

In light of Theorem
2.21
, all that we need to check is that the orientation of the quotient graph
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\backslash X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
matches the orientation of
\Gamma_{+}
. Take
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
, and take some lift of
e
in
X_{\mathcal{G},x}
(by Remark
2.22
, the lift goes from
\gamma geG_{s(e)}
to
\gamma G_{r(e)}
for some
\gamma\in xF(\mathcal{G})r(e)
and
g\in G_{r(e)}
). Remark
2.22
also gives that
\gamma G_{r(e)}
is a lift of the vertex
r(e)
and
\gamma geG_{s(e)}
is a lift of the vertex
s(e)
. Since
\gamma^{-1}(\gamma ge)=ge1_{s(e)}\in G_{r(e)}eG_{s(e)}
, there is an edge in the orientation of
X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
from
\gamma geG_{s(e)}
to
\gamma G_{r(e)}
, which matches the direction of
e
in
\Gamma_{+}^{1}
. This proves the theorem.
∎

3.2.
Word category

In this subsection we introduce and study the
word category
of a directed graph of groups, which generalises the notion of the path category of a directed graph. The results in this subsection will be useful in Sections
4
and
5
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups. A
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word
is a
\mathcal{G}
-word where all edges in the word belong to
\Gamma_{+}^{1}
. A
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word maps naturally into the fundamental groupoid
F(\mathcal{G})
of the underlying graph of groups. The
word category
of
\mathcal{G}_{+}
, which we will denote by
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, is the subcategory of
F(\mathcal{G})
consisting of the images of
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-words.

Note that the word category is indeed a category since the concatenation of two
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-words is again a
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word. Moreover, since
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is a subcategory of a groupoid, it must be left cancellative.

Remark 3.7
.

This construction of a category from a directed graph of groups yields the same result as the construction in
[
20
]
. Thus,
[
20
, Theorem 4.17]
tells us that we can recover the directed graph of groups
\mathcal{G}_{+}
from its word category
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
(up to isomorphism).

Recall from Section
2.4
that elements of the fundamental groupoid
F(\mathcal{G})
of a graph of groups
\mathcal{G}
can be uniquely represented by
\Sigma
-normalised
\mathcal{G}
-words. For a
directed
graph of groups
\mathcal{G}_{+}
, we can likewise define the notion of a
\Sigma
-normalised
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word, and we show that these uniquely represent elements of the word category
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups. A
set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
is a set
\Sigma=\{\Sigma_{e}:e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}\}
, where each
\Sigma_{e}
is a transversal of
G_{r(e)}/\alpha_{e}(G_{e})
which includes the identity element
1_{r(e)}\in G_{r(e)}
. A
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word
g_{1}e_{1}g_{2}e_{2}\ldots g_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
is
\Sigma
-normalised
if
g_{i}\in\Sigma_{e_{i}}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Remark 3.9
.

Suppose that
\Sigma
is a set of transversals for a directed graph of groups
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
. We can always extend
\Sigma
to a set of transversals for the underlying graph of groups
\mathcal{G}
by simply including transversals
\Sigma_{e}
for each edge
e\in\Gamma^{1}\setminus\Gamma_{+}^{1}
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. Every element of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is uniquely represented by a
\Sigma
-normalised
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word.

Proof.

Extend
\Sigma
to a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}
. By Proposition
2.18
, every element of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\subseteq F(\mathcal{G})
is uniquely represented by a
\Sigma
-normalised
\mathcal{G}
-word. But any such
\mathcal{G}
-word must only contain edges in orientation
\Gamma_{+}^{1}
(along with group elements), and so the
\mathcal{G}
-word is a (
\Sigma
-normalised)
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word.
∎

Using Proposition
3.10
, we now relate the word category
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
to a certain directed graph. We show that this graph encodes the order structure of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
(Proposition
3.12
), and then we derive some useful consequences.

Notation 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. Write
E_{\Sigma}
for the directed graph defined by

E_{\Sigma}^{0}=\Gamma^{0},\quad E_{\Sigma}^{1}=\{he:e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1},\ h\in\Sigma_{e}\},\quad r(he)=r(e),\quad s(he)=s(e).

(The directed graph
E_{\Sigma}
is essentially just the directed graph
\Gamma_{+}
with its edges replicated a number of times depending on the size of the respective transversal
\Sigma_{e}
.)

Write
q_{\Sigma}\colon\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\to E_{\Sigma}^{*}
for the map which sends (the image in
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
of) a
\Sigma
-normalised
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word
h_{1}e_{1}h_{2}e_{2}\dots h_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
to the path
h_{1}e_{1}h_{2}e_{2}\dots h_{n}e_{n}
in
E_{\Sigma}
(in the case where
n=0
, we define
q_{\Sigma}(g_{1})
to be the vertex
v
for which
g_{1}\in G_{v}
). We will often drop the subscript ‘
\Sigma
’ in the notation and write only ‘
q
’ for this map when
\Sigma
is understood.

Note that the path space
E_{\Sigma}^{*}
can be viewed as a left cancellative small category with unit space
E_{\Sigma}^{0}=\Gamma^{0}
. The relation
\leq
on
E_{\Sigma}^{*}
as in Definition
2.5
is in this case a partial order.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. For any
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, we have
\lambda\leq\mu
in
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
if and only if
q(\lambda)\leq q(\mu)
in
E_{\Sigma}^{*}
.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, and write
\lambda=h_{1}e_{1}h_{2}e_{2}\dots h_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
in
\Sigma
-normalised form. First suppose that
\lambda\leq\mu
, so
\lambda\nu=\mu
for some
\nu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. Write
\nu=g^{\prime}_{1}f_{1}g^{\prime}_{2}f_{2}\dots g^{\prime}_{m}f_{m}g^{\prime}_{m+1}
, and let
g_{n+1}\nu=h^{\prime}_{1}f_{1}h^{\prime}_{2}f_{2}\dots h^{\prime}_{m}f_{m}g^{\prime\prime}_{m+1}
be
\Sigma
-normalised. Then

\mu=\lambda\nu=h_{1}e_{1}h_{2}e_{2}\dots h_{n}e_{n}h^{\prime}_{1}f_{1}h^{\prime}_{2}f_{2}\dots h^{\prime}_{m}f_{m}g^{\prime\prime}_{m+1}

is in
\Sigma
-normalised form, so

q(\lambda)=h_{1}e_{1}h_{2}e_{2}\dots h_{n}e_{n}\leq h_{1}e_{1}h_{2}e_{2}\dots h_{n}e_{n}h^{\prime}_{1}f_{1}h^{\prime}_{2}f_{2}\dots h^{\prime}_{m}f_{m}=q(\mu),

as required.

For the other direction, suppose that
q(\lambda)\leq q(\mu)
, so
\mu
has
\Sigma
-normalised form

h_{1}e_{1}h_{2}e_{2}\dots h_{n}e_{n}h^{\prime}_{1}f_{1}h^{\prime}_{2}f_{2}\dots h^{\prime}_{m}f_{m}g^{\prime\prime}_{m+1}.

So
\mu=\lambda(g_{n+1}^{-1}h^{\prime}_{1}f_{1}h^{\prime}_{2}f_{2}\dots h^{\prime}_{m}f_{m}g^{\prime\prime}_{m+1})
, showing that
\lambda\leq\mu
, as required.
∎

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups. For any
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, if
\lambda
and
\mu
have a common extension, then either
\lambda\leq\mu
or
\mu\leq\lambda
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\lambda
and
\mu
have a common extension
\nu
, so
\lambda,\mu\leq\nu
. Then by Proposition
3.12
, we have that
q(\lambda),q(\mu)\leq q(\nu)
in
E_{\Sigma}^{*}
; that is, both
q(\lambda)
and
q(\mu)
are initial subpaths of
q(\nu)
. But this can only be true if either
q(\lambda)
is an initial subpath of
q(\mu)
or vice versa. Then Proposition
3.12
implies the claim.
∎

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups. The category
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is singly aligned.

Proof.

Suppose that
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
are such that
\lambda\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\cap\mu\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\neq\emptyset
. Then
\lambda
and
\mu
have a common extension, and so by Corollary
3.13
we can assume without loss of generality that
\lambda\leq\mu
. This implies that
\mu\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\subseteq\lambda\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
and therefore that
\lambda\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\cap\mu\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}=\mu\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. This proves the claim.
∎

3.3.
The directed forest
W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}

In this subsection we consider a directed version of the forest
W_{\mathcal{G}}
from Definition
2.19
, and we study the action of the fundamental groupoid
F(\mathcal{G})
on the boundary of this directed forest. This will be useful for Section
5
, where we work with the transformation groupoid
F(\mathcal{G})\ltimes\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. We start by assigning an orientation to
W_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Definition 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups. Write
W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
for the directed forest with
W_{\mathcal{G}}
as its underlying graph, and where an edge from
\gamma^{\prime}G_{w}
to
\gamma G_{w}
is in the orientation if and only if
\gamma^{-1}\gamma^{\prime}\in G_{v}eG_{w}
for some
e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1}w
.

Note that for any
x\in\Gamma^{0}
, the connected component of the vertex
G_{x}
in
W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is exactly the directed Bass–Serre tree of
\mathcal{G}_{+}
based at
x
.

We are interested in studying the boundary
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
of
W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, and for this it is useful to consider
infinite
\mathcal{G}
-words
.

Definition 3.16
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma,G)
be a graph of groups, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}
. An
infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word
is a sequence of the form
g_{1}e_{1}g_{2}e_{2}\dots
, where
e_{1}e_{2}\dots
is an infinite path in
\Gamma
, and
g_{i}\in G_{r(e_{i})}
for each
i\geq 1
. An infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word is
\Sigma
-normalised
if
g_{i}\in\Sigma_{e_{i}}
for all
i\geq 1
, and there is no subsequence of the form
\overline{e}1_{r(e)}e
. The range of an infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word is
r(e_{1})
.

The boundary
\partial X_{\mathcal{G},x}
of the Bass–Serre tree based at
x\in\Gamma^{0}
can be identified with the set of
\Sigma
-normalised infinite
\mathcal{G}
-words with range
x
(see
[
6
, Section 2.3.1]
), so the boundary
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}}
can be identified with the set of all
\Sigma
-normalised infinite
\mathcal{G}
-words. The action of
F(\mathcal{G})
on
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}}
induced from the action on
W_{\mathcal{G}}
is as follows. Take
\gamma\in F(\mathcal{G})
and
\xi\in\partial W_{\mathcal{G}}
with
r(\xi)=s(\gamma)
. Then
\gamma
is represented by some
\mathcal{G}
-word and
\xi
is represented by some infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word, so the concatenation
\gamma\xi
is also an infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word. By a (possibly infinite) number of applications of the relations in
F(\mathcal{G})
, we can turn
\gamma\xi
into a
\Sigma
-normalised infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word, which we call
\gamma\cdot\xi
. It can be verified that this defines an action of
F(\mathcal{G})
on
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}}
; we refer to
[
6
, Section 2]
for more detail.

In this paper we are interested in the action of
F(\mathcal{G})
on the boundary
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
of the
directed
forest
W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. This boundary can be identified with the set of
eventually directed

\Sigma
-normalised infinite
\mathcal{G}
-words, in the following sense.

Definition 3.17
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}
. A
\Sigma
-normalised infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word
g_{1}e_{1}g_{2}e_{2}\dots
is
eventually directed
if there is some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
e_{i}\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
for all
i\geq N
. Equivalently, an eventually directed infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word has the form
\gamma\alpha
for some
\gamma\in F(\mathcal{G})
and some
infinite
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word

\alpha
(that is,
\alpha
is an infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word with all edges belonging to the orientation
\Gamma_{+}^{1}
).

The basic open sets of
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
have the form

Z(\gamma G_{s(\gamma)})=\{\gamma\alpha:\alpha\ \text{an infinite $\mathcal{G}_{+}$-word},\ r(\alpha)=s(\gamma)\}

for a fixed
\gamma\in F(\mathcal{G})
. Note that if we consider
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
to be a subset of
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}}
, then it is invariant under the action of
F(\mathcal{G})
, since the concatenation
\gamma\xi
of a
\mathcal{G}
-word
\gamma
and an eventually directed
\Sigma
-normalised infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word
\xi
will also be eventually directed. Moreover, the action of
\gamma
takes a basic open set
Z(\gamma^{\prime}G_{s(\gamma^{\prime})})
,
\gamma^{\prime}\in s(\gamma)F(\mathcal{G})
to the basic open set
Z(\gamma\gamma^{\prime}G_{s(\gamma^{\prime})})
Thus
F(\mathcal{G})
acts on the space
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
by homeomorphisms.

We now focus on the subspace of
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
corresponding to (
\Sigma
-normalised) infinite
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-words. These words have the form
h_{1}e_{1}h_{2}e_{2}\dots
, where for each
i\leq 1
we have
e_{i}\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
and
h_{i}\in\Sigma_{e_{i}}
. But sequences of this form also describe infinite paths in
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
, and so we can identify
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
with a subset of
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. Moreover, the topology of
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
matches the topology coming from
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, since the basic open set
\alpha E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\subseteq E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
corresponds to the basic open set
Z(\alpha 1_{s(\alpha)}G_{s(\alpha)})\subseteq\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
; thus we can consider
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
to be a subspace of
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. We end this section with the following result which characterises the groupoid elements
\gamma\in F(\mathcal{G})
which send a fixed element of
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\subseteq\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
to another element of
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
.

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of groups, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. Let
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
be an infinite path, considered as an element of
\partial W_{\mathcal{G}}
, and let
\gamma\in F(\mathcal{G})
. Then
\gamma\cdot\alpha
is an element of
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\subseteq\partial W_{\mathcal{G}}
if and only if
\gamma
has the form
\lambda\mu^{-1}
for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, where
q(\mu)\leq\alpha
.

Proof.

First suppose that
\gamma=\lambda\mu^{-1}
for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
with
q(\mu)\leq\alpha
. Let
g_{\mu}\in G_{s(\mu)}
be such that
\mu=q(\mu)g_{\mu}
, and let
\beta\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
be such that
\alpha=q(\mu)\beta
. Then
\gamma=\lambda g_{\mu}^{-1}q(\mu)^{-1}
, and so
\gamma\cdot\alpha=\lambda g_{\mu}^{-1}\beta
is an infinite
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word, and therefore (its
\Sigma
-normalised version) corresponds to some infinite path in
E_{\Sigma}
. This proves the backwards direction.

For the forwards direction, extend
\Sigma
to a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}
, and write
\gamma=g_{1}e_{1}\dots g_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
in
\Sigma
-normalised form. If each
e_{i}\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, then
\gamma\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
and there is nothing to prove, so assume that
e_{i}\notin\Gamma_{+}^{1}
for some
i
. Write
\alpha=h_{1}f_{1}h_{2}f_{2}\dots
. Since
\gamma\cdot\alpha=g_{1}e_{1}\dots g_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}h_{1}f_{1}h_{2}f_{2}\dots
is directed, the path
e_{i}
must be “canceled out” by the relation
\overline{e}1_{r(e)}e=1_{s(e)}
in
F(\mathcal{G})
. But since
\gamma
is already
\Sigma
-normalised, the only possible cancellation that can occur is the sourcemost edge of
\gamma
with the rangemost edge of
\alpha
; that is, we need
e_{n}g_{n+1}h_{1}f_{1}
to have the form
\overline{f_{1}}1_{r(f_{1})}f_{1}
. This forces
g_{n+1}=h_{1}^{-1}
and
e_{n}=\overline{f_{1}}
. Write
\gamma^{\prime}=g_{1}e_{1}\dots g_{n-1}e_{n-1}g_{n}
and
\alpha^{\prime}=h_{2}f_{2}h_{3}f_{3}\dots
, so
\gamma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha^{\prime}=\gamma\cdot\alpha
. If each
e_{i}\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
,
i=1,\dots,n-1
, then we have that
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
and
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}(h_{1}f_{1}1_{s(f_{1})})^{-1}
, so we are done. If not, then we repeat the argument with
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\alpha^{\prime}
. As
\gamma
is a finite
\mathcal{G}
-word, this process will eventually terminate, at which point we will have that for some
k\leq n
, the word
\gamma^{\prime\prime}=g_{1}e_{1}\dots g_{k}e_{k}g_{k+1}
belongs to
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, and
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime\prime}(h_{1}f_{1}\dots h_{n-k}f_{n-k}1_{s(f_{n-k})})^{-1}
. Since
q(h_{1}f_{1}\dots h_{n-k}f_{n-k}1_{s(f_{n-k})})=h_{1}f_{1}\dots h_{n-k}f_{n-k}\leq\alpha
, we have proven the claim.
∎

4.
Directed graph of groups
C^{*}
-algebras

We now associate a universal
C^{*}
-algebra to a directed graph of groups, where the generators and relations of the
C^{*}
-algebra are encoded by the directed graph of groups. Then, we show that this
C^{*}
-algebra is isomorphic to the Cuntz–Krieger algebra of the word category of the directed graph of groups.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. A
(\mathcal{G}_{+},\Sigma)
-family
is a collection of partial isometries
S_{e}
for each
e\in\Gamma^{1}_{+}
and representations
g\mapsto U_{v,g}
of
G_{v}
by partial unitaries for each
v\in\Gamma^{0}
satisfying the relations:

(1)

U_{v,1}U_{w,1}=0
for each
v,w\in\Gamma^{0}
with
v\neq w
;

(2)

U_{r(e),\alpha_{e}(g)}S_{e}=S_{e}U_{s(e),\alpha_{\overline{e}}(g)}
for each
e\in\Gamma^{1}_{+}
and
g\in G_{e}
;

(3)

U_{s(e),1}=S_{e}^{*}S_{e}
for each
e\in\Gamma^{1}_{+}
; and

(4)

U_{v,1}=\sum_{e\in v\Gamma^{1}_{+},h\in\Sigma_{e}}U_{v,h}S_{e}S_{e}^{*}U_{v,h}^{*}
for each
v\in\Gamma^{0}
.

We write
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
for the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by a
(\mathcal{G}_{+},\Sigma)
-family, and we call it the
directed graph of groups
C^{*}
-algebra of
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. We write
\{s_{e}:e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}\}\cup\{u_{v,g}:v\in\Gamma^{0},\ g\in G_{v}\}
for the
(\mathcal{G}_{+},\Sigma)
-family generating
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
.

A priori
, the
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
depends on the choice of the set of transversals
\Sigma
. However, it follows from Theorem
4.2
below that
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
is in fact independent (up to isomorphism) of
\Sigma
, which justifies the omission of
\Sigma
in the notation.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. Let
\{t_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\}
be the Cuntz–Krieger
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
-family generating
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
. The map

\displaystyle\Phi\colon C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})

\displaystyle s_{e}

\displaystyle\mapsto t_{1_{r(e)}e}

\displaystyle u_{v,g}

\displaystyle\mapsto t_{g}

is an isomorphism of
C^{*}
-algebras.

To prove this, we use the universal property of
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
and
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
to find maps
\Phi\colon C^{*}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
and
\Psi\colon\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})\to C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
, and then we show that the maps
\Phi
and
\Psi
are mutual inverses. We first prove some intermediary results, and then tie everything together in a proof of the theorem at the end of this section.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and let
\{S_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\}
be a family satisfying (S4). For any
v\in\Gamma^{0}
and
g\in G_{v}
, we have that
S_{g}S_{g}^{*}=S_{v}
.

Proof.

Fix
v\in\Gamma^{0}
and
g\in G_{v}
. The singleton set
\{g\}\subseteq v\Lambda
is finite exhaustive, since for any
\lambda\in v\Lambda
we have
g\leq g(g^{-1}\lambda)=\lambda
. Thus (S4) gives that
S_{v}=S_{g}S_{g}^{*}
, as required.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources. A family
\{S_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\}
satisfying (S1), (S2) and (S4) satisfies (S3) if and only if it satisfies the relation

(S3’)

S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}=\begin{cases}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}&\text{if}\ \lambda\leq\mu,\\
S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}&\text{if}\ \mu\leq\lambda,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We show that

\bigvee_{\nu\in\lambda\vee\mu}S_{\nu}S_{\nu}^{*}=\begin{cases}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}&\text{if}\ \lambda\leq\mu,\\
S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}&\text{if}\ \mu\leq\lambda,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Fix
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. By Corollary
3.13
, we have that
\lambda\vee\mu\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\lambda\leq\mu
or
\mu\leq\lambda
. In the case that
\lambda\leq\mu
, we have that
\lambda\vee\mu
is the set of
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-words
\nu
such that
\mu\leq\nu
and
\nu\leq\mu
, which by Proposition
3.12
is the set
q^{-1}(\mu)=\{\mu g:g\in G_{s(\mu)}\}
. Now, (S2) and Lemma
4.3
together give that

S_{\mu g}S_{\mu g}^{*}=S_{\mu}S_{g}S_{g}^{*}S_{\mu}^{*}=S_{\mu}S_{s(\mu)}S_{\mu}^{*}=S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}

for all
g\in G_{s(\mu)}
. So in this case

\bigvee_{\nu\in\lambda\vee\mu}S_{\nu}S_{\nu}^{*}=\bigvee_{g\in G_{s(\mu)}}S_{\mu g}S_{\mu g}^{*}=\bigvee_{g\in G_{s(\mu)}}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}=S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}

as required. A similar argument in the case where
\mu\leq\lambda
shows that

\bigvee_{\nu\in\lambda\vee\mu}S_{\nu}S_{\nu}^{*}=\bigvee_{g\in G_{s(\lambda)}}S_{\lambda g}S_{\lambda g}^{*}=\bigvee_{g\in G_{s(\lambda)}}S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}=S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}.

Finally, if neither
\lambda\leq\mu
nor
\mu\leq\lambda
, then
\lambda\vee\mu=\emptyset
and so
\bigvee_{\nu\in\lambda\vee\mu}S_{\nu}S_{\nu}^{*}=0
as required.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. Let
\{t_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\}
be the Cuntz–Krieger
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
-family generating
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
. For each
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
, let
S_{e}=t_{e}
; and for each
v\in\Gamma^{0}
and
g\in G_{v}
, let
U_{v,g}=t_{g}
. Then
\{S_{e}:e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}\}\cup\{U_{v,g}:v\in\Gamma^{0},\ g\in G_{v}\}
is a
(\mathcal{G}_{+},\Sigma)
-family in
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
.

Proof.

Remark
2.8
tells us that
\{S_{e}:e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}\}
is a family of partial isometries. Now fix
v\in\Gamma^{0}
. Each
U_{v,g}=t_{g}
,
g\in G_{v}
is a partial isometry since
t_{g}^{*}t_{g}^{*}=t_{v}=t_{g}t_{g}^{*}
by (S1) and Lemma
4.3
. Moreover, for any
g_{1},g_{2}\in G_{v}
we have
t_{g_{1}}t_{g_{2}}=t_{g_{1}g_{2}}
by (S2), so
G_{v}\ni g\mapsto U_{v,g}
is a representation by partial unitaries.

Now we show that the
S_{e}
’s and
U_{v,g}
’s satisfy the relations in Definition
4.1
. For any
v,w\in\Gamma^{0}
with
v\neq w
, (S3’) gives that
t_{v}t_{w}=t_{v}t_{v}^{*}t_{w}t_{w}^{*}=0
, so relation (1) is satisfied. For any
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
and
g\in G_{e}
, we have
t_{\alpha_{e}(g)}t_{e}=t_{\alpha_{e}(g)e}=t_{e\alpha_{\overline{e}}(g)}=t_{e}t_{\alpha_{\overline{e}}(g)}
using (S2) and the relation
\alpha_{e}(g)e=e\alpha_{\overline{e}}(g)
in the word category, so relation (2) is satisfied. Relation (3) follows immediately from (S1).

Finally, for relation (4), we first claim that for any
v\in\Gamma^{0}
, the set
\{he:e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1},\ h\in\Sigma_{e}\}
is an exhaustive subset of
v\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. To see this, take
\lambda\in v\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. If
\lambda=g_{1}
for some
g_{1}\in G_{v}
, then
\lambda=g_{1}\leq g_{1}(g_{1}^{-1}he)=he
for any
e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1}
and
h\in\Sigma_{e}
; otherwise if
\lambda=h_{1}e_{1}\dots h_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
in reduced form, then
h_{1}e_{1}\leq\lambda
. In either case,
\lambda
is comparable with some element in
\{he:e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1},\ h\in\Sigma_{e}\}
, showing that it is exhaustive. Then (S4) gives that

t_{v}=\bigvee_{e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1},\ h\in\Sigma_{e}}t_{he}t_{he}^{*}.

But (S3’) implies that the
t_{he}t_{he}^{*}
’s are pairwise orthogonal, and so we get that

t_{v}=\sum_{e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1},\ h\in\Sigma_{e}}t_{he}t_{he}^{*}=\sum_{e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1},\ h\in\Sigma_{e}}t_{h}t_{e}t_{e}^{*}t_{h}^{*},

which is relation (4). This proves that
\{S_{e}:e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}\}\cup\{U_{v,g}:v\in\Gamma^{0},\ g\in G_{v}\}
is a
(\mathcal{G}_{+},\Sigma)
-family in
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
, as claimed.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. For each
\lambda=g_{1}e_{1}g_{2}e_{2}\dots g_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
in
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, define

S_{\lambda}=u_{r(e_{1}),g_{1}}s_{e_{1}}u_{r(e_{2}),g_{2}}s_{e_{2}}\dots u_{r(e_{n}),g_{n}}s_{e_{n}}u_{s(e_{n}),g_{n+1}}.

Then
\{S_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\}
is a Cuntz–Krieger
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
-family in
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
.

Proof.

First note that the
S_{\lambda}
’s are well-defined, since if the
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-words
g_{1}e_{1}g_{2}e_{2}\dots g_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
and
h_{1}e_{1}h_{2}e_{2}\dots h_{n}e_{n}h_{n+1}
are two presentations of the same element
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\subseteq F(\mathcal{G})
, then they are related via a finite number of applications of the relation
\alpha_{e}(g)e=e\alpha_{\overline{e}}(g)
which is implemented by relation (2) of Definition
4.1
.

To see that (S1) holds, take
\lambda=g_{1}e_{1}g_{2}e_{2}\dots g_{n}e_{n}g_{n+1}
. Note that we have

u_{r(e_{i}),g_{i}}^{*}u_{r(e_{i}),g_{i}}=u_{r(e_{i}),1}=s_{e_{i}}^{*}s_{e_{i}}

for
i=1,\dots,n
, and also
u_{s(e_{n}),g_{n+1}}^{*}u_{s(e_{n}),g_{n+1}}=u_{s(e),1}
. Applying these relations iteratively to
S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\lambda}
gives that
S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\lambda}=u_{s(e_{n}),1}=t_{s(\lambda)}=S_{s(\lambda)}
, as required.

(S2) holds by definition of the
S_{\lambda}
’s. For (S3), Proposition
4.4
means that it is enough to show that (S3’) holds. Take
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. We have four cases. If
\lambda\leq\mu
, then there exists
\nu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
with
\mu=\lambda\nu
. So

S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}=S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\lambda}S_{\nu}S_{\nu}^{*}S_{\lambda}^{*}=S_{\lambda}S_{\nu}S_{\nu}^{*}S_{\lambda}^{*}=S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}.

Similarly if
\mu\leq\lambda
, then there exists
\nu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
with
\lambda=\mu\nu
. So

S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}=S_{\mu}S_{\nu}S_{\nu}^{*}S_{\mu}^{*}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}=S_{\mu}S_{\nu}S_{\nu}^{*}S_{\mu}^{*}=S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}.

If
r(\lambda)\neq r(\mu)
, then
\lambda
and
\mu
are not comparable, and relation (1) gives that

S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}=S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}u_{r(\lambda),1_{r(\lambda)}}u_{r(\mu),1_{r(\mu)}}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}=0.

Finally we come to the case where
\lambda
and
\mu
have the same range but are not comparable. Write
\nu
for the maximal common initial subpath of
\lambda
and
\mu
. Then

\lambda=\nu(h_{1}e_{1})\lambda^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu=\nu(h_{2}e_{2})\mu^{\prime}

for some
h_{1}e_{1},\lambda^{\prime},h_{2}e_{2},\mu^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
with
h_{1}e_{1}\neq h_{2}e_{2}
. So

S_{\lambda}^{*}S_{\mu}=S_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}S_{h_{1}e_{1}}^{*}S_{\nu}^{*}S_{\nu}S_{h_{2}e_{2}}S_{\mu^{\prime}}=S_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}S_{h_{1}e_{1}}^{*}S_{h_{2}e_{2}}S_{\mu^{\prime}}=0

since
S_{h_{1}e_{1}}
and
S_{h_{2}e_{2}}
have orthogonal range projections due to relation (4).

For (S4), fix
v\in\Gamma^{0}
and a finite exhaustive set
F\subseteq v\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. First note that relation (4) says that
u_{w,1}=\sum_{he\in wE_{\Sigma}^{1}}S_{he}u_{s(e),1}S_{he}^{*}
for all
w\in\Gamma^{0}
, and applying this inductively gives that
u_{v,1}=\sum_{\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{n}}S_{\alpha}S_{\alpha}^{*}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, for any
\lambda\in F
, setting
n=\lvert\lambda\rvert
(that is, the number of edges in the
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word
\lambda
) shows that
S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}=S_{q(\lambda)}S_{q(\lambda)}^{*}\leq u_{v,1}
, so
\bigvee_{\lambda\in F}S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}\leq u_{v,1}
.

It remains to prove that
u_{v,1}\leq\bigvee_{\lambda\in F}S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}
. Let
n=\max_{\lambda\in F}\lvert q(\lambda)\rvert
. Since
F
is exhaustive, for each
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{n}
there is some
\lambda_{\alpha}\in F
such that
\alpha 1_{s(\alpha)}
and
\lambda_{\alpha}
have a common extension. But Corollary
3.13
and the choice of length of
\alpha
force
\lambda_{\alpha}\leq\alpha 1_{s(\alpha)}
. This implies that
S_{\alpha 1_{s(\alpha)}}S_{\alpha 1_{s(\alpha)}}^{*}\leq S_{\lambda_{\alpha}}S_{\lambda_{\alpha}}^{*}\leq\bigvee_{\lambda\in F}S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*}
for all
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{n}
, and so

u_{v,1}=\sum_{\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{n}}S_{\alpha}S_{\alpha}^{*}=\bigvee_{\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{n}}S_{\alpha 1_{s(\alpha)}}S_{\alpha 1_{s(\alpha)}}^{*}\leq\bigvee_{\lambda\in F}S_{\lambda}S_{\lambda}^{*},

as required.
∎

We now put everything together to prove Theorem
4.2
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Proposition
4.5
along with the universal property of
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
show that the map
\Phi
in the statement of the theorem is a well-defined
*
-homomorphism. Proposition
4.6
along with the universal property of
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
give a
*
-homomorphism
\Psi\colon\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})\to C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
satisfying
\Psi(t_{e})=s_{e}
for
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
and
\Psi(t_{g})=u_{v,g}
for
v\in\Gamma^{0}
and
g\in G_{v}
. So
\Psi\circ\Phi
is the identity map on
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
, which means that
\Phi
is injective. To see that
\Phi
is surjective, note that any
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is a finite composition of elements of the form
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
and
g\in G_{v}
,
v\in\Gamma^{0}
. So the elements
t_{e}=\Phi(s_{e})
and
t_{g}=\Phi(u_{v,g})
generate
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
, meaning that
\Phi
is surjective. Hence
\Phi
is a
C^{*}
-isomorphism, as claimed.
∎

5.
A
C^{*}
-algebraic ‘directed’ Bass–Serre theorem

This section is dedicated to proving the following result, which can be seen as the directed version of
[
6
, Theorem 4.1]
, described in that paper as a
C^{*}
-algebraic Bass–Serre theorem. This result also generalises
[
17
, Corollary 4.14]
, which gives the result in the setting of directed graph algebras.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and choose a base vertex
x\in\Gamma^{0}
. Then
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
is Morita equivalent to
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
.

To prove this result, we first describe a groupoid model for
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
in Section
5.1
, and then in Section
5.2
we show that the underlying groupoid is equivalent in a suitable sense to the transformation groupoid
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\ltimes\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
.

5.1.
Groupoid model for
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})

Ortega and Pardo
[
23
]
give a groupoid model for the Cuntz–Krieger algebra of a countable, finitely aligned LCSC using the language of inverse semigroup actions. In this subsection we analyse the model as it applies to our context, and provide a tractable version of the model in Theorem
5.9
. We begin with a general discussion of the groupoid model from
[
23
]
.

Let
\Lambda
be a countable, finitely aligned LCSC. We can associate a semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
to
\Lambda
in the following way.

Definition 5.2

(
[
23
, Section 2]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC. For each
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we define maps

\tau^{\lambda}\colon s(\lambda)\Lambda\to\lambda\Lambda;\quad\mu\mapsto\lambda\mu,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma^{\lambda}\colon\lambda\Lambda\to s(\lambda)\Lambda;\quad\lambda\mu\mapsto\mu.

We write
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
for the semigroup generated by
\{\tau^{\lambda},\sigma^{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
under composition, where a composition is deemed to equal
0
if it has empty domain.

The semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
is an inverse semigroup, called the left inverse hull of
\Lambda
. Theorem 4.16 of
[
23
]
states that the Cuntz–Krieger algebra of
\Lambda
is isomorphic to the
C^{*}
-algebra of what is called the
tight groupoid
of
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
. This groupoid is topologically isomorphic to the groupoid of germs of the action of
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
on a certain space
\Lambda_{tight}

[
23
, Lemma 4.9]
, so
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda)\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}\ltimes\Lambda_{tight})
.

We will now describe the space
\Lambda_{tight}
and the action of
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
on
\Lambda_{tight}
.

Definition 5.3

(
[
23
, Section 3]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC. A non-empty subset
H\subseteq\Lambda
is:

(1)

hereditary
if for any
\lambda\in H
and
\mu\in\Lambda
such that
\mu\leq\lambda
, we have that
\mu\in H
;

(2)

(upwards) directed
if for any
\lambda,\mu\in H
, there exists some
\nu\in H
such that
\lambda,\mu\leq\nu
; and

(3)

tight
if for any
\lambda\in H
,
\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\}\subseteq\Lambda\setminus H
, and finite exhaustive set
Z
of
\lambda\Lambda\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\Lambda
, we have that
Z\cap H\neq\emptyset
.

Write
\Lambda^{*}
for the collection of hereditary directed subsets of
\Lambda
, and write
\Lambda_{tight}
for the collection of tight hereditary directed subsets of
\Lambda
.

We can turn
\Lambda^{*}
and
\Lambda_{tight}
into topological spaces as follows. For finite sets
X,Y\subseteq\Lambda
, define

\mathcal{M}^{X,Y}=\{H\in\Lambda^{*}:X\subseteq H\ \text{and}\ Y\cap H=\emptyset\}.

We can endow
\Lambda^{*}
with a topology generated by open sets
\{\mathcal{M}^{X,Y}:X,Y\subseteq\Lambda\ \text{finite}\}
, and we give
\Lambda_{tight}
the subspace topology.

The semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
acts on
\Lambda^{*}
as follows. The domain and range of a generating element
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
(where
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
with
s(\lambda)=s(\mu)
) are
\{H\in\Lambda^{*}:\mu\in H\}
and
\{H\in\Lambda^{*}:\lambda\in H\}
respectively, and
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}
acts according to the formula

\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\cdot H=\{\xi\in\Lambda:\xi\leq\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}(\nu)\ \text{for some}\ \nu\in H\ \text{such that}\ \mu\leq\nu\}

for
H
in the domain of
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}
. This action restricts to an action on
\Lambda_{tight}

[
23
, Corollary 4.7]
, whose groupoid of germs has
C^{*}
-algebra isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda)
.

We now consider these results in the setting where the category
\Lambda
is the word category of a directed graph of groups. To avoid cluttered notation, we will write
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
instead of
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}}
to denote the left inverse hull of the word category of a directed graph of groups
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. The following proposition describes the structure of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}
be a directed graph of groups. We have that

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}=\{\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}:\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}},\ s(\lambda)=s(\mu)\}\cup\{0\}.

Moreover, for any two nonzero elements
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\tau^{\nu}\sigma^{\xi}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, we have

(1)

\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}=\tau^{\nu}\sigma^{\xi}
if and only if there is
g\in G_{s(\lambda)}
such that
\nu=\lambda g
and
\xi=\mu g
; and

(2)

\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\tau^{\nu}\sigma^{\xi}=\begin{cases}\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\xi\eta}&\text{if}\ \mu=\nu\eta\ \text{for some}\ \eta\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}};\\
\tau^{\lambda\eta}\sigma^{\xi}&\text{if}\ \nu=\mu\eta\ \text{for some}\ \eta\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}};\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is singly aligned (Corollary
3.14
),
[
9
, Theorem 3.2]
gives that

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}=\{\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}:\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}},\ s(\lambda)=s(\mu)\}\cup\{0\},

and that multiplication is described by

\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\tau^{\nu}\sigma^{\xi}=\begin{cases}\tau^{\lambda\sigma^{\mu}(\gamma)}\sigma^{\xi\sigma^{\nu}(\gamma)}&\text{if}\ \mu\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\cap\nu\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}=\gamma\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}};\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(Note that in
[
9
]
, the semigroup
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is denoted as
ZM(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
.) Now, the condition that
\mu\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\cap\nu\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is non-empty is equivalent to
\mu
and
\nu
having a common extension, which by Corollary
3.13
is in turn equivalent to
\mu
and
\nu
being comparable. In this case, we can consider two (overlapping) subcases:
\nu\leq\mu
or
\mu\leq\nu
.

If
\nu\leq\mu
, then
\mu=\nu\eta
for some
\eta\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, and we can take
\gamma
to be
\mu
. Then
\sigma^{\mu}(\gamma)
is just
s(\mu)
and
\sigma^{\nu}(\gamma)=\eta
, so we get that
\tau^{\lambda\sigma^{\mu}(\gamma)}\sigma^{\xi\sigma^{\nu}(\gamma)}=\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\xi\eta}
. If instead
\mu\leq\nu
, then
\nu=\mu\eta
for some
\eta\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, and we can take
\gamma
to be
\nu
. Then
\sigma^{\nu}(\gamma)
is just
s(\nu)
and
\sigma^{\mu}(\gamma)=\eta
, so we get that
\tau^{\lambda\sigma^{\mu}(\gamma)}\sigma^{\xi\sigma^{\nu}(\gamma)}=\tau^{\lambda\eta}\sigma^{\xi}
. This proves (2).

Finally, (1) follows from
[
9
, Proposition 3.3]
, since invertible elements in
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
at the object
s(\lambda)
are exactly elements of the vertex group
G_{s(\lambda)}
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

If all groups in
\mathcal{G}_{+}
are trivial, then
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is the graph inverse semigroup of
\Gamma_{+}
as defined in
[
25
]
.

We now turn to the space
(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
of tight hereditary directed subsets of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. The aim of the next series of results is to prove that
(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
is isomorphic to the infinite path space
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
of
E_{\Sigma}
, for any set of transversals
\Sigma
. We prove this in several steps: first we show that
(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
is in bijection with
(E_{\Sigma}^{*})_{tight}
(Proposition
5.6
); next we show that
(E_{\Sigma}^{*})_{tight}
is in turn in bijection with
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
(Proposition
5.7
); and finally we show that the bijection between
(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
and
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is a homeomorphism (Proposition
5.8
).

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}
be a directed graph of groups, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. The map
q\colon\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\to E_{\Sigma}^{*}
induces a bijection
(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}\to(E_{\Sigma}^{*})_{tight}
, whose inverse is given by taking pre-images under
q
.

Proof.

First note that for any hereditary subset
H\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, if
\lambda\in H
then, by Proposition
3.12
,
H
also contains all
\mu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
for which
q(\mu)=q(\lambda)
; thus
H=q^{-1}(q(H))
. Moreover,
q(H)
is hereditary, since for any
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{*}
and
\mu\in H
such that
\alpha\leq q(\mu)
, Proposition
3.12
gives that
\alpha\leq\mu
in
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
and thus that
\alpha\in H
since
H
is hereditary; so we have
\alpha=q(\alpha)\in q(H)
as required. Likewise, if
K\subseteq E_{\Sigma}^{*}
is hereditary, then its pre-image
q^{-1}(K)\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is also hereditary. So
q
induces a bijection between the hereditary subsets of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
and
E_{\Sigma}^{*}
, with the inverse given by taking pre-images under
q
. Now, Proposition
3.12
also implies that a subset
H\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is directed if and only if its image
q(H)\subseteq E_{\Sigma}^{*}
is directed, so
q
in fact induces a bijection between
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}^{*}
and
(E_{\Sigma}^{*})^{*}
.

We now show that this bijection restricts to a bijection on the level of tight hereditary directed subsets. First suppose that
H\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is a tight hereditary directed subset; we show that
q(H)\subseteq E_{\Sigma}^{*}
is tight. Take
\alpha\in q(H)
and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\in E_{\Sigma}^{*}\setminus q(H)
, and let
Z\subseteq\alpha E_{\Sigma}^{*}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}E_{\Sigma}^{*}
be finite exhaustive. Since
H=q^{-1}(q(H))
, if we consider
\alpha,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
as elements of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, then
\alpha\in H
and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\setminus H
. If we also identify
Z
with its image in
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, then
Z\subseteq\alpha\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is also finite exhaustive: for any
\lambda\in\alpha\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, we have
q(\lambda)\in\alpha E_{\Sigma}^{*}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}E_{\Sigma}^{*}
, so since
Z
is exhaustive there is some
z\in Z
such that
q(\lambda)E_{\Sigma}^{*}\cap zE_{\Sigma}^{*}\neq\emptyset
; but then Proposition
3.12
implies that
\lambda\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\cup z\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\neq\emptyset
also. Then, since
H
is tight, we have
Z\cap H\neq\emptyset
, and so
Z\cap q(H)\neq\emptyset
in
E_{\Sigma}^{*}
. Hence
q(H)
is tight. A similar argument shows that if
K\subseteq E_{\Sigma}^{*}
is a tight hereditary directed subset, then its pre-image
q^{-1}(K)\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
is also tight. Hence
q
induces a bijection
(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}\to(E_{\Sigma}^{*})_{tight}
, as claimed.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. For each infinite path
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
, define a subset
\Delta_{\alpha}\subseteq E_{\Sigma}^{*}
by
\Delta_{\alpha}:=\{\beta\in E_{\Sigma}^{*}:\beta\leq\alpha\}
. Write
\Delta
for the map sending
\alpha
to
\Delta_{\alpha}
. Then
\Delta
is a bijection
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\to(E_{\Sigma}^{*})_{tight}
.

Proof.

It is easy to check that each
\Delta_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is hereditary and directed. To see that it is tight, fix paths
\beta\in\Delta_{\alpha}
and
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in E_{\Sigma}^{*}\setminus\Delta_{\alpha}
, and fix a finite exhaustive subset
Z\subseteq\beta E_{\Sigma}^{*}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}E_{\Sigma}^{*}
; we will show that
Z\cap\Delta_{\alpha}\neq\emptyset
. Take
\beta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\alpha}
such that
\beta\leq\beta^{\prime}
and
\lvert\beta^{\prime}\rvert>\max_{z\in Z}\lvert z\rvert
. Note that
\gamma_{i}\not\leq\beta^{\prime}
for
i=1,\dots,n
(otherwise, since
\Delta_{\alpha}
is hereditary we would have
\gamma_{i}\in\Delta_{\alpha}
, contradicting the choice of
\gamma_{i}
), so
\beta^{\prime}\in\beta E_{\Sigma}^{*}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}E_{\Sigma}^{*}
. Then, since
Z
is exhaustive, there is some
z\in Z
which has a common extension with
\beta^{\prime}
. This implies that either
z\leq\beta^{\prime}
or
\beta^{\prime}\leq z
. But the latter is impossible, since we chose
\beta^{\prime}
to be longer than any path in
Z
; so we must have
z\leq\beta^{\prime}
. Finally, since
\Delta_{\alpha}
is hereditary, we get that
z\in\Delta_{\alpha}
, which implies that
Z\cap\Delta_{\alpha}\neq\emptyset
, as required. Hence each
\Delta_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is tight, and so
\Delta
is a map
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\to(E_{\Sigma}^{*})_{tight}
.

Since every infinite path in
E_{\Sigma}
is uniquely determined by the set of its initial subpaths, we get that
\Delta
is injective. Now, the discussion in
[
23
, Example 3.31]
(where our
\Delta_{\alpha}
is denoted as
E_{\alpha}
) shows that every tight hereditary directed subset of
E_{\Sigma}^{*}
is equal to
\Delta_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
(note that since
\mathcal{G}_{+}
is row-finite and has no sources, the same is true for
E_{\Sigma}
, and so
E_{\Sigma}
has no singular vertices), so
\Delta
is also surjective. Thus
\Delta\colon E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\to(E_{\Sigma}^{*})_{tight}
is a bijection, as claimed.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\Delta\colon E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\to(E_{\Sigma}^{*})_{tight}
be as in Proposition
5.7
. The map
q^{-1}\circ\Delta\colon E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\to(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

Propositions
5.6
and
5.7
together give that
q^{-1}\circ\Delta
is a bijection, so it remains to prove that it is continuous and open. Recall that the codomain
(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
has basic open sets of the form

\mathcal{M}^{X,Y}=\{H\in(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}:X\subseteq H\ \text{and}\ Y\cap H=\emptyset\},\quad X,Y\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ \text{finite}.

Fix finite sets
X,Y\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. For an infinite path
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
, the condition
X\subseteq q^{-1}(\Delta_{\alpha})
means that
q(\lambda)\leq\alpha
for all
\lambda\in X
, and similarly the condition
Y\cap q^{-1}(\Delta_{\alpha})=\emptyset
means that
q(\mu)\not\leq\alpha
for all
\mu\in Y
. So

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}(q(\mathcal{M}^{X,Y}))

\displaystyle=\{\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}:q(\lambda)\leq\alpha\ \text{for all}\ \lambda\in X,\ \text{and}\ q(\mu)\not\leq\alpha\ \text{for all}\ \mu\in Y\}

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcap_{\lambda\in X}q(\lambda)E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\mu\in Y}q(\mu)E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\right),

which is a basic open set in
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
, and moreover, all basic open sets in
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
have this form. This implies that
q^{-1}\circ\Delta
is continuous and open, and hence is a homeomorphism.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this subsection, which gives a more tangible groupoid model for a directed graph of groups
C^{*}
-algebra.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. Then
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty})
, where
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is the groupoid of germs for the action of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
on
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
given by

\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\cdot\alpha=\lambda\mu^{-1}\cdot\alpha,\quad\text{for}\ \alpha\ \text{such that}\ q(\mu)\leq\alpha,

where in the right hand side the action of
\lambda\mu^{-1}\in F(\mathcal{G})
on
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is as in Proposition
3.18
.

Proof.

From Theorem
4.2
we know that
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})\cong\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})
, and
[
23
, Section 4.4]
gives that
\mathcal{O}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight})
. Proposition
5.8
says that the map
q^{-1}\circ\Delta\colon E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\to(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
is a homeomorphism, and so it remains to show that the induced action of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
on
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
has the formula given in the theorem statement (this would also imply that the formula defines a legitimate action).

Recall that the action of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
on
(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
is given by

\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\cdot H=\{\xi\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}:\xi\leq\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}(\nu)\ \text{for some}\ \nu\in H\ \text{such that}\ \mu\leq\nu\}

for
H\in(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
such that
\mu\in H
. Pulling back through the homeomorphism
q^{-1}\circ\Delta
, we get that for
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
such that
q(\mu)\leq\alpha
,

\displaystyle\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\cdot q^{-1}(\Delta_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\{\xi\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}:\xi\leq\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}(\nu)\ \text{for some}\ \nu\in q^{-1}(\Delta_{\alpha})\ \text{such that}\ \mu\leq\nu\}

\displaystyle=\{\xi\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}:\xi\leq\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}(\nu)\ \text{for some}\ \nu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ \text{such that}\ q(\mu)\leq q(\nu)\leq\alpha\}.

But note that the condition that
\xi\leq\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}(\nu)
for some
\nu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
with
q(\mu)\leq q(\nu)\leq\alpha
is equivalent to the condition that
q(\xi)\leq\lambda\mu^{-1}\cdot\alpha
, so
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\cdot q^{-1}(\Delta_{\alpha})=q^{-1}(\Delta_{\lambda\mu^{-1}\cdot\alpha})
. Thus the action of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
on
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
induced from the action on
(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})_{tight}
is given by
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\cdot\alpha=\lambda\mu^{-1}\cdot\alpha
, as claimed.
∎

To finish this subsection, we study the groupoid of germs
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
from Theorem
5.9
. Recall that the groupoid
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
consists of equivalence classes of elements in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}*E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}=\{(\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\times E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}:s(\lambda)=s(\mu)\ \text{and}\ q(\mu)\leq\alpha\}
. We now give a concrete description of the equivalence in our context.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
(\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha),(\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}},\beta)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}*E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
. The germs
[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha]
and
[\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}},\beta]
are the same if and only if
\alpha=\beta
and (assuming without loss of generality that
\mu\leq\mu^{\prime}
) there exists
\eta\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
such that
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda\eta
and
\mu^{\prime}=\mu\eta
.

Proof.

By definition, we have that the germs
[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha]
and
[\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}},\beta]
are the same if and only if
\alpha=\beta
and there is an idempotent
e\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
such that
\alpha\in D_{e}
and
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}e=\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}e
. Note that
e
must be non-zero, since otherwise
D_{e}
would be empty. Now,
[
9
, Proposition 3.5(1)]
gives that the non-zero idempotents of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
are exactly elements of the form
\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}
for some
\xi\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, and we have that
\alpha\in D_{\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}}
if and only if
q(\xi)\leq\alpha
. So the germs
[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha]
and
[\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}},\beta]
are the same if and only if
\alpha=\beta
and there is
\xi\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
such that
q(\xi)\leq\alpha
and
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}=\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}
.

First suppose that there is
\eta\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
such that
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda\eta
and
\mu^{\prime}=\mu\eta
. Then Proposition
5.4
gives that
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}=\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}=\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}
, so we can take
\xi=\mu^{\prime}
to see that the germs
[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha]
and
[\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}},\beta]
are the same.

Now suppose that the germs
[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha]
and
[\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}},\beta]
are the same. Note that since
\alpha=\beta
, we must have both
q(\mu)\leq\alpha
and
q(\mu^{\prime})\leq\alpha
. This forces
q(\mu)
and
q(\mu^{\prime})
to be comparable, since they are both initial subpaths of
\alpha
. Assume without loss of generality that
q(\mu)\leq q(\mu^{\prime})
; then Proposition
3.12
gives that
\mu\leq\mu^{\prime}
, so there is some
\eta\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
such that
\mu^{\prime}=\mu\eta
.

It is left to show that
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda\eta
. Let
\xi\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
be such that
q(\xi)\leq\alpha
and
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}=\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}
. Since
q(\xi)
,
q(\mu)
and
q(\mu^{\prime})
are all subpaths of
\alpha
, we have that
q(\xi)
is comparable with both
q(\mu)
and
q(\mu^{\prime})
. Hence by Proposition
3.12
we have that
\xi
is comparable with both
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
.

We now reduce to the case where
(\mu\leq)\ \mu^{\prime}\leq\xi
. Indeed, suppose that
\xi\leq\mu^{\prime}
, so
\mu^{\prime}=\xi\nu
for some
\nu\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
. Then
\sigma^{\xi}(\mu^{\prime})=\nu
, and
\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}=\tau^{\xi\nu}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}=\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}
. This means that

\displaystyle\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}.

We also have that
\alpha\in D_{\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}}
, so
\tau^{\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}
is also an idempotent in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
with the required properties. Hence we can replace
\xi
with
\mu^{\prime}
, so that now
\mu^{\prime}\leq\xi
.

Finally we prove the statement in the case that
(\mu\leq)\ \mu^{\prime}\leq\xi
. Let
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
be such that
\xi=\mu\nu=\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}
. Then
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}=\tau^{\lambda\nu}\sigma^{\xi}
, and
\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}\tau^{\xi}\sigma^{\xi}=\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\xi}
. So the assumption on
\xi
implies that
\tau^{\lambda\nu}\sigma^{\xi}=\tau^{\lambda^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\xi}
, and then
[
9
, Proposition 3.3]
gives that
\lambda\nu=\lambda^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}
. Now,
\mu\nu=\xi=\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}=\mu\eta\nu^{\prime}
, so by cancellativity of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
we have that
\nu=\eta\nu^{\prime}
. So
\lambda^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}=\lambda\nu=\lambda\eta\nu^{\prime}
, and using cancellativity again gives that
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda\eta
, as required.
∎

5.2.
Groupoid equivalence

We now use the groupoid model for
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
obtained in Theorem
5.9
to prove Theorem
5.1
, that
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
is Morita equivalent to the crossed product
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
. Our strategy is to show that the underlying groupoids of the two
C^{*}
-algebras in question, namely the groupoid of germs
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
and the transformation groupoid
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\ltimes\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
, are equivalent in a suitable sense so that we have a Morita equivalence on the level of
C^{*}
-algebras.

We begin with a discussion of groupoid equivalence. In
[
29
]
, Renault introduced the notion of groupoid equivalence: roughly speaking, two Hausdorff étale groupoids
\mathscr{G}
and
\mathscr{H}
are equivalent if they act in a compatible way on some space. For us, the important property is that equivalent groupoids have Morita equivalent
C^{*}
-algebras (
[
21
, Theorem 2.8]
).

Rather than working with this notion of groupoid equivalence directly, we will consider the notion of
weak Kakutani equivalence
for groupoids, which was studied in
[
10
]
. Recall that a subset
X
of the unit space of a groupoid
\mathscr{G}
is called
full
if it meets the orbit of every unit in
\mathscr{G}
. Two Hausdorff étale groupoids are called
weakly Kakutani equivalent
if there are full open subsets
U\subseteq\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
and
V\subseteq\mathscr{H}^{(0)}
such that
\mathscr{G}|_{U}\cong\mathscr{H}|_{V}

[
10
, Definition 3.8]
. Weak Kakutani equivalence implies equivalence in the sense of Renault
[
10
, Proposition 3.10]
.

We combine these concepts in the following key lemma, which we will use to prove Theorem
5.1
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\mathscr{G}
be a Hausdorff étale groupoid, and suppose that
U,V\subseteq\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
are full open subsets. Then
C^{*}(\mathscr{G}|_{U})
and
C^{*}(\mathscr{G}|_{V})
are Morita equivalent
C^{*}
-algebras.

Proof.

Note that
\mathscr{G}|_{U}
is weakly Kakutani equivalent to
\mathscr{G}
(take
U
to be the full open subset for both groupoids), and similarly
\mathscr{G}|_{V}
is also weakly Kakutani equialent to
\mathscr{G}
. Then
[
10
, Proposition 3.10]
implies that
\mathscr{G}|_{U}
and
\mathscr{G}|_{V}
are both equivalent to
\mathscr{G}
in the sense of Renault. Since equivalence in the sense of Renault is an equivalence relation (see e.g.
[
21
, p. 6]
), we get that
\mathscr{G}|_{U}
and
\mathscr{G}|_{V}
are equivalent, and hence their
C^{*}
-algebras are Morita equivalent by
[
21
, Theorem 2.8]
.
∎

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of groups with no sources, and let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. The groupoid of germs
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is topologically isomorphic to the groupoid
(F(\mathcal{G})\ltimes\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})|_{E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}}
.

Proof.

Propositions
5.10
and
3.18
together mean that we can define a map

\Theta\colon\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}\to(F(\mathcal{G})\ltimes\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})|_{E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}},\quad[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha]\mapsto(\lambda\mu^{-1},\alpha),

and Theorem
5.9
implies that
\Theta
is a groupoid homomorphism.
We claim that
\Theta
is a topological groupoid isomorphism.

Proposition
3.18
gives that
\Theta
is surjective. For injectivity, suppose that
[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha]
and
[\tau^{\nu}\sigma^{\xi},\beta]
are two germs in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
such that
\Theta([\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha])=\Theta([\tau^{\nu}\sigma^{\xi},\beta])
. So
(\lambda\mu^{-1},\alpha)=(\nu\xi^{-1},\beta)
, which means that
\alpha=\beta
and
\lambda\mu^{-1}=\nu\xi^{-1}
. Now, since
\alpha
is in the domain of both
\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu}
and
\tau^{\nu}\sigma^{\xi}
, we must have that
q(\mu)
and
q(\xi)
are both initial subpaths of
\alpha
and therefore must be comparable. Without loss of generality, suppose that
q(\mu)\leq q(\xi)
. Then Proposition
3.12
gives that
\mu\leq\xi
, so there is some
\eta\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
such that
\xi=\mu\eta
. Then
\lambda\mu^{-1}=\nu(\mu\eta)^{-1}=\nu\eta^{-1}\mu^{-1}
in
F(\mathcal{G})
, so
\lambda=\nu\eta^{-1}
and thus
\nu=\lambda\eta
. Then Proposition
5.10
gives that
[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},\alpha]=[\tau^{\nu}\sigma^{\xi},\beta]
, proving that
\Theta
is injective.

Finally we show that
\Theta
is a homeomorphism. Take a basic open set
[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},U]\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
, where
U\subseteq q(\mu)E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is open. The image of this set under
\Theta
is
(\lambda\mu^{-1},U)
, which is open in
(F(\mathcal{G})\ltimes\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})|_{E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}}
. Similarly, the inverse image of the basic open set
(\lambda\mu^{-1},U)\subseteq(F(\mathcal{G})\ltimes\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})|_{E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}}
is
[\tau^{\lambda}\sigma^{\mu},U]
, which is open in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
. Thus
\Theta
induces a bijection between basic open sets, and is therefore a homeomorphism. This proves that the groupoids
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
and
(F(\mathcal{G})\ltimes\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})|_{E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}}
are topologically isomorphic.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
. Proposition
5.12
says that the groupoid of germs
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is topologically isomorphic to the groupoid
(F(\mathcal{G})\ltimes\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})|_{E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}}
, and by definition we have that the transformation groupoid
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\ltimes\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is the groupoid
(F(\mathcal{G})\ltimes\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}})|_{\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}}
. We claim that both
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
and
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
are full open subsets of the unit space of
F(\mathcal{G})\ltimes\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
.

First we consider
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
. This set is open as
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}=\bigsqcup_{v\in\Gamma^{0}}Z(G_{v})
. To see that it is full, take any
\gamma\in\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, viewed as an eventually directed infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word. Write
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}\alpha
, where
\alpha
is an infinite
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word (which we can view as an element of
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
, as in Section
3.3
). Then
\gamma
is in the same orbit as
\alpha\in E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
, showing that
E_{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is full.

Now consider
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
. This set is open as
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}=\bigcup_{\gamma\in x\mathcal{G}^{*}}Z(\gamma G_{s(\gamma)})
. To see that it is full, again take any
\gamma\in\partial W_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}
, viewed as an eventually directed infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word. Write
v
for the range of
\gamma
, and let
\gamma_{v}\in xF(\mathcal{G})v
. Then
\gamma_{v}\gamma
is an eventually directed infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word with range
x
, and so can be viewed as an element of
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
. This shows that
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is full.

Hence we can apply Lemma
5.11
, which together with Proposition
5.12
gives that
C^{*}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty})
is Morita equivalent to
C^{*}(\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\ltimes\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})
. The theorem follows, since
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{G}_{+}}\ltimes E_{\Sigma}^{\infty})
by Theorem
5.9
, and
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\cong C^{*}(\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\ltimes\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})
.
∎

Remark 5.13
.

We can in fact say a little more about the relationship between
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
and
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
, under the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
. Both
C^{*}
-algebras are separable since they have countable generating sets, and two separable
C^{*}
-algebras are Morita equivalent if and only if they are stably isomorphic
[
5
]
. Thus we have that
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
is stably isomorphic to
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
.

6.
A construction of all stable UCT Kirchberg algebras

In this final section we aim to show that the class of directed graph of groups
C^{*}
-algebras includes all stable UCT Kirchberg algebras (recall that a Kirchberg algebra is a
C^{*}
-algebra that is simple, separable, purely infinite, and nuclear). These algebras are the subject of the celebrated Kirchberg–Phillips classification theorem
[
16
,
26
]
, part of which we state below.

Theorem 6.1

(Kirchberg–Phillips classification theorem)
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are both stable UCT Kirchberg algebras. Then
A\cong B
if and only if
K_{*}(A)\cong K_{*}(B)
.

One of the benefits of such a classification result is that it allows us realise these abstractly defined
C^{*}
-algebras via a concrete construction, as has been done in
[
32
]
and
[
14
]
. Our main result of this section, which we state below, adds to this list of constructions; notably, our result gives the first concrete construction of Kirchberg algebras as crossed product
C^{*}
-algebras.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
A
be a stable UCT Kirchberg algebra. There exists a countable, row-finite directed graph of infinite cyclic groups
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
with no sources, such that
A\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})\cong C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{+},x)
for any
x\in\Gamma^{0}
.

In order to show this, we use Theorem
5.1
along with the results in
[
7
]
, which provides both a
K
-theory formula for crossed products coming from group actions on directed tree boundaries as well as criteria for such a crossed product to be a UCT Kirchberg algebra. We then transfer these results to our context of directed graph of groups
C^{*}
-algebras using the observation that the class of UCT Kirchberg algebras is closed under stable isomorphism (see
[
30
]
).

6.1.
C^{*}
-algebras of directed graphs of infinite cyclic groups

In this subsection, we investigate the
C^{*}
-algebras of directed graphs of groups where all vertex and edge groups are infinite cyclic (we will call these
directed graphs of infinite cyclic groups
). In particular, we give a formula for the
K
-theory of these
C^{*}
-algebras (Proposition
6.5
), as well as sufficient conditions for the
C^{*}
-algebra to be a UCT Kirchberg algebra (Proposition
6.6
).

We start with some notation.

Notation 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a directed graph of infinite cyclic groups. We fix generators
1_{v}\in G_{v}
and
1_{e}\in G_{e}
for each vertex
v\in\Gamma^{0}
and each edge
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
so that for each edge
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
, the map
\alpha_{e}\colon G_{e}\to G_{r(e)}
is given by multiplication by some
positive
integer
n_{e}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
(this is always possible to arrange); and the map
\alpha_{\overline{e}}\colon G_{e}\to G_{s(e)}
will be given by multiplication by some non-zero integer
m_{e}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

Remark 6.4
.

By Theorem
3.4
, we have that the vertex stabilisers of the action
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\curvearrowright X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
are infinite cyclic, and that the quotient directed graph of the action is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{+}
. Moreover, for each
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
, the integers
n_{e}
and
m_{e}
from Notation
6.3
are exactly the integers
\omega_{e}
and
\omega_{\overline{e}}
of
[
7
, Notation 4.18]
respectively.

We can now give a formula for the
K
-theory of
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
for a directed graph of infinite cyclic groups
\mathcal{G}_{+}
.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of infinite cyclic groups with no sources. Let
N,M\colon\mathbb{Z}[\Gamma^{0}]\to\mathbb{Z}[\Gamma^{0}]
be the
\mathbb{Z}
-module homomorphisms given by

N(v)=\sum_{e\in v\Gamma^{1}_{+}}n_{e}s(e)\qquad\text{and}\qquad M(v)=\sum_{e\in v\Gamma^{1}_{+}}m_{e}s(e)\qquad\text{for}\ v\in\Gamma^{0}.

Then

\displaystyle K_{0}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+}))

\displaystyle\cong\coker(1-N)\oplus\ker(1-M),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle K_{1}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+}))

\displaystyle\cong\coker(1-M)\oplus\ker(1-N).

Proof.

We know from Theorem
5.1
that
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
is Morita equivalent to the crossed product
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
. Since
K
-theory is invariant under Morita equivalence, it is enough to show the corresponding isomorphisms for the
K
-theory of the crossed product. To do this, we apply
[
7
, Theorem 4.19]
. The tree
X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is row-finite and has no sources since
\Gamma_{+}
has those properties, and it is a finitely aligned multitree in the sense of
[
7
]
since it is a tree. The group
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
is countable since
\mathcal{G}_{+}
is countable, and it acts amenably on
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
by
[
7
, Proposition 6.11]
(as per Remark
6.4
, all vertex stabilisers of the action are infinite cyclic, and hence amenable). So the assumptions of
[
7
, Theorem 4.19]
are satisfied, and we get that

\displaystyle K_{0}(C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes_{r}\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x))

\displaystyle\cong\coker(1-A_{0})\oplus\ker(1-A_{1}),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle K_{1}(C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes_{r}\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x))

\displaystyle\cong\coker(1-A_{1})\oplus\ker(1-A_{0}),

where
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
are the
\mathbb{Z}
-module homomorphisms
\mathbb{Z}[\Gamma^{0}]\to\mathbb{Z}[\Gamma^{0}]
defined by

A_{0}(v)=\sum_{e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1}}\lvert\omega_{e}\rvert s(e)\quad\text{and}\quad A_{1}(v)=\sum_{e\in v\Gamma_{+}^{1}}\operatorname{sgn}(\omega_{e})\omega_{\overline{e}}s(e)

for
v\in\Gamma^{0}
. But by Remark
6.4
, we have that
\omega_{e}=n_{e}
and
\omega_{\overline{e}}=m_{e}
for all
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
, and recall that each
n_{e}
was chosen to be positive. Thus the maps
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
are exactly the maps
N
and
M
from the proposition statement, respectively. Finally, we note that the reduced crossed product
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes_{r}\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
is the same as the full crossed product
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
since the action of
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
on
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is amenable. This concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we give sufficient conditions for
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
to be a UCT Kirchberg algebra. In the following, for
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
we denote by
\langle q\rangle
the smallest positive integer that can appear as a denominator when expressing
q
as a fraction.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{+}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
be a countable, row-finite directed graph of infinite cyclic groups with no sources. Suppose that:

(1)

the directed graph
\Gamma_{+}
is cofinal;

(2)

there is a loop
\eta=\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\dots\eta_{m}
in
\Gamma_{+}
such that either
\eta
has an entrance, or
n_{\eta_{i}}\geq 2
for some
1\leq i\leq m
; and

(3)

there is some infinite path
\alpha=e_{1}e_{2}\ldots\in\Gamma_{+}^{\infty}
such that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left\langle\frac{m_{e_{1}}\dots m_{e_{k-1}}}{n_{e_{1}}\dots n_{e_{k}}}\right\rangle=\infty.

Then
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
is a UCT Kirchberg algebra.

Proof.

Since the class of UCT Kirchberg algebras is closed under stable isomorphism, Remark
5.13
means that it is enough to show that the crossed product
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
is a UCT Kirchberg algebra.

Separability is automatic, since
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
is countable. Note that the action
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\curvearrowright\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is amenable by
[
7
, Proposition 6.11]
, so the crossed product is nuclear
[
1
]
and satisfies the UCT (see
[
34
]
). So it remains to show that the crossed product is simple and purely infinite.

By
[
3
, p.124]
, the crossed product will be simple if the action
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\curvearrowright\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is minimal and topologically free (we already know the action to be amenable); and in this case the crossed product will be purely infinite if the action is locally contractive
[
19
, Theorem 9]
(where local contractivity is called being a
local boundary action
). By Remark
6.4
, conditions (1) and (3) of the proposition are exactly the conditions in
[
7
, Propositions 6.1, 6.6]
which are equivalent to the action
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\curvearrowright\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
being minimal and topologically free, respectively.

Finally, we need to show that the action
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\curvearrowright\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is locally contractive. Take the loop
\eta
from condition (2). Since
n_{e}=[G_{r(e)}:\alpha(G_{e})]
for any
e\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
, we get that
\eta
satisfies condition (i) of
[
7
, Proposition 6.2]
; moreover, the confinality of
\Gamma_{+}
means that there is a path from
r(\eta)
to any vertex
v\in\Gamma^{0}
(consider the infinite path
\eta\eta\dots
), so condition (ii) is also satisfied. Hence
[
7
, Proposition 6.2]
gives that
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\curvearrowright\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is locally contractive, as required.
∎

6.2.
Construction of stable UCT Kirchberg algebras

We now describe how to construct a directed graph of (infinite cyclic) groups whose
C^{*}
-algebra is a stable UCT Kirchberg algebra with any given
K
-theory; together with Theorem
5.1
, this essentially proves Theorem
6.2
. Our construction is inspired by the approach in
[
14
]
, but is adjusted slightly to work in our context. We begin by explaining how we can define a directed graph of infinite cyclic groups from a pair of integer matrices.

Notation 6.7
.

Let
J
be a countable index set, and let
N\in M_{\lvert J\rvert}(\mathbb{N})
and
M\in M_{\lvert J\rvert}(\mathbb{Z})
be two matrices indexed over
J
satisfying
N_{i,j}=0\iff M_{i,j}=0
for all
i,j\in J
. Define
\mathcal{G}_{+}^{N,M}=(\Gamma_{+},G)
to be the directed graph of groups where:

•

the underlying graph
\Gamma_{+}=(\Gamma^{0},\Gamma_{+}^{1},r,s)
is given by

\Gamma^{0}=J,\qquad\Gamma_{+}^{1}=\{(i,j)\in J\times J:N_{i,j}>0\},\qquad r(i,j)=i,\qquad s(i,j)=j;

•

all vertex and edge groups are infinite cyclic, with a choice of generator
1_{i}\in G_{i}
for each
i\in\Gamma^{0}
and
1_{(i,j)}\in G_{(i,j)}
for each
(i,j)\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
; and

•

for each
(i,j)\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
, the monomorphisms
\alpha_{(i,j)}
and
\alpha_{\overline{(i,j)}}
are defined by

\alpha_{(i,j)}(1_{(i,j)})=N_{i,j}1_{i},\qquad\alpha_{\overline{(i,j)}}(1_{(i,j)})=M_{i,j}1_{j}.

We also have the following canonical set of tranvsersals for
\mathcal{G}^{N,M}_{+}
: for each
(i,j)\in\Gamma_{+}^{1}
, we let
\Sigma_{(i,j)}=\{0,1_{i},2\times 1_{i},\dots,(N_{i,j}-1)1_{i}\}
.

Remark 6.8
.

Let
N,M
and
\mathcal{G}_{+}^{N,M}
be as in Notation
6.7
. The maps
N,M\colon\mathbb{Z}[\Gamma^{0}]\to\mathbb{Z}[\Gamma^{0}]
of Proposition
6.5
for
\mathcal{G}_{+}^{N,M}
are precisely the maps given by left multiplication by the matrices
N
and
M
. Thus we have

\displaystyle K_{0}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+}^{N,M}))

\displaystyle\cong\coker(1-N)\oplus\ker(1-M),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle K_{1}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+}^{N,M}))

\displaystyle\cong\coker(1-M)\oplus\ker(1-N).

In order to prove Theorem
6.2
we show that for any pair of countable abelian groups
G_{0},G_{1}
we can find suitable matrices
N,M
such that
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+}^{N,M})
is a UCT Kirchberg algebra with
K
-theory
(G_{0},G_{1})
.

Construction 6.9
.

Let
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
be countable abelian groups. There exist injective homomorphisms
T,S\colon\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}\to\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}
such that
\coker T\cong G_{0}
and
\coker S\cong G_{1}
. We view these as elements of
M_{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
, indexed over
\mathbb{N}
, and we will write
\lvert T\rvert
and
\lvert S\rvert
for the matrices obtained by taking element-wise absolute values of
T
and
S
respectively. Define
X\in M_{\infty}(\mathbb{N})
by

X_{k,l}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ \lvert k-l\rvert=1,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

for
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
, and define
Y\in M_{\infty}(\mathbb{N})
by
Y=2\lvert T\rvert+\lvert S\rvert+X
, both indexed over
\mathbb{N}
. Define matrices
N\in M_{\infty}(\mathbb{N})
and
M\in M_{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
in block form by

N=\begin{pmatrix}2I&T+Y\\
I&I+Y\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad M=\begin{pmatrix}3I&S+2Y\\
I&I+Y\end{pmatrix}.

We index
N
and
M
over
\mathbb{N}\times\{0,1\}
, where the first block of rows/columns corresponds to the indices
\mathbb{N}\times\{0\}
, and the second block corresponds to the indices
\mathbb{N}\times\{1\}
.

Proposition 6.10
.

The matrices
N,M
of Construction
6.9
satisfy:

(1)

N_{i,j}=0\iff M_{i,j}=0
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}\times\{0,1\}
;

(2)

the maps
1-N
and
1-M
are injective; and

(3)

\coker(1-N)\cong G_{0}
and
\coker(1-M)\cong G_{1}
.

Proof.

To prove (1), it is enough to check that
(T+Y)_{k,l}=0\iff(S+2Y)_{k,l}=0
for all
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
. First note that for any integer
a\in\mathbb{Z}
, the number
a+2\lvert a\rvert
is always non-negative and is equal to
0
if and only if
a=0
. Thus we have that, for all
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
, both
(T+Y)_{k,l}=(T_{k,l}+2\lvert T_{k,l}\rvert)+\lvert S_{k,l}\rvert+X_{k,l}
and
(S+2Y)_{k,l}=(S_{k,l}+2\lvert S_{k,l}\rvert)+4\lvert T_{k,l}\rvert+2X_{k,l}
equal
0
if and only if
T_{k,l}=S_{k,l}=X_{k,l}=0
, proving the claim.

For (2) and (3), note that

1-N=\begin{pmatrix}-I&-T-Y\\
-I&-Y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&I\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-T\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&Y\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}

and

1-M=\begin{pmatrix}-2I&-S-2Y\\
-I&-Y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&2I\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-S\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&Y\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}.

Since matrices of the form
\begin{pmatrix}I&*\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}
are invertible (with inverse
\begin{pmatrix}I&-*\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}
), we get that

\displaystyle\ker(1-N)

\displaystyle=\ker\begin{pmatrix}0&-T\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}=0;

\displaystyle\coker(1-N)

\displaystyle=\coker\begin{pmatrix}0&-T\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}=\coker T=G_{0};

\displaystyle\ker(1-M)

\displaystyle=\ker\begin{pmatrix}0&-S\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}=0;\ \text{and}

\displaystyle\coker(1-M)

\displaystyle=\coker\begin{pmatrix}0&-S\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}=\coker S=G_{1},

as claimed.
∎

Remark
6.8
and Proposition
6.10
together give the following result.

Corollary 6.11
.

Let
N
and
M
be as in Construction
6.9
. Then
K_{i}(C^{*}(\mathcal{G}^{N,M}_{+}))\cong G_{i}
for
i=0,1
.

Before we study properties of
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}^{N,M}_{+})
, we make the following observation about the graph of groups
\mathcal{G}^{N,M}_{+}
.

Remark 6.12
.

Let
N
and
M
be as in Construction
6.9
. Regardless of the groups
G_{0},G_{1}
used in the construction, the directed graph
\Gamma_{+}
underlying
\mathcal{G}^{N,M}_{+}
always has the following graph (which we will call
\Gamma_{0}
) as a subgraph:

\scriptstyle(0,0)
\scriptstyle(0,1)
\scriptstyle(1,0)
\scriptstyle(1,1)
\scriptstyle(2,0)
\scriptstyle(2,1)
\scriptstyle(3,0)
\scriptstyle(3,1)
\cdots
\cdots

This is the subgraph with adjacency matrix

\begin{pmatrix}I&X\\
I&X\end{pmatrix},

where the top row of vertices corresponds to the vertices
\mathbb{N}\times\{0\}
, and the bottom row of vertices corresponds to the vertices
\mathbb{N}\times\{1\}
. Note that
\Gamma_{0}
contains all edges emitted from the vertices
\mathbb{N}\times\{0\}
(the top row); moreover, for each loop
e_{n}=((n,0),(n,0))
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
in
\Gamma_{+}
, the monomorphisms
\alpha_{e_{n}}
and
\alpha_{\overline{e_{n}}}
are defined by

\alpha_{e_{n}}(1_{e_{n}})=2\times 1_{(n,0)}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{\overline{e_{n}}}(1_{e_{n}})=3\times 1_{(n,0)}

respectively.

Proposition 6.13
.

For
N
and
M
as in Construction
6.9
, the
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+}^{N,M})
is a stable UCT Kirchberg algebra.

Proof.

We apply Proposition
6.6
. To see that
\Gamma_{+}
is cofinal, note that the subgraph
\Gamma_{0}
described in Remark
6.12
is strongly connected (that is, there is a directed path from any vertex in
\Gamma_{0}
to any other vertex in
\Gamma_{0}
): the vertices in the bottom row of the figure are strongly connected via the horizontal edges between them, and one can travel between the top and bottom rows via the vertical or diagonal edges. Since
\Gamma_{0}
has the same vertex set as
\Gamma_{+}
, we have that
\Gamma_{+}
is also strongly connected and thus cofinal.

For (2) and (3), take the unique loop
e
at
(0,0)\in\Gamma^{0}
. Since
n_{e}=2
, the loop
e
satisfies the condition in (2). Moreover, for
\alpha=ee\dots
, the fraction
m_{e_{1}}\dots m_{e_{k-1}}/n_{e_{1}}\dots n_{e_{k}}
in (3) is equal to
3^{k-1}/2^{k}
, and
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\langle 3^{k-1}/2^{k}\rangle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}2^{k}=\infty
; hence (3) is also satisfied. Then Proposition
6.6
gives that
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}^{N,M}_{+})
is a UCT Kirchberg algebra. Finally, since
\Gamma_{+}
has infinitely many vertices, the algebra
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
is non-unital, and hence is stable by
[
35
, Theorem 1.2]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

Write
G_{0}=K_{0}(A)
and
G_{1}=K_{1}(A)
, and let
N
and
M
be as in Construction
6.9
. Proposition
6.13
gives that
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}^{N,M}_{+})
is a stable UCT Kirchberg algebra, and Corollary
6.11
gives that the
K
-theory of
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}^{N,M}_{+})
is isomorphic to the
K
-theory of
A
. Thus, the Kirchberg–Phillips classification theorem gives that
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})\cong A
.

Now we show that
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})
for any
x\in\Gamma^{0}
. We know from Remark
5.13
that the
C^{*}
-algebras are stably isomorphic, so the crossed product
C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
is also a UCT Kirchberg algebra with
K
-theory
(G_{0},G_{1})
. All that remains is to show that the crossed product is not unital (equivalently that the space
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is not compact); then
[
35
, Theorem 1.2]
implies that it is stable, and again the Kirchberg–Phillips classification theorem would give that
A\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{+})\cong C_{0}(\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x})\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},x)
as claimed.

Since the spaces
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
are isomorphic for each
x\in\Gamma^{0}
, it is enough to show that
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is not compact for a particular choice of
x\in\Gamma^{0}
. We choose
x
to be the vertex
(0,0)\in\Gamma^{0}
. Consider the open cover
\{Z(\gamma G_{s(\gamma)}):\gamma\in xF(\mathcal{G})\}
of
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
; we show that it does not admit a finite subcover. Indeed, let
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in xF(\mathcal{G})
be a finite set of
\Sigma
-normalised
\mathcal{G}
-words in
xF(\mathcal{G})
(where
\Sigma
is some fixed set of transversals for
\mathcal{G}
). Write
k
for the maximum length of
\gamma_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, and write
e
for the unique loop in
\Gamma_{+}
at
x
. Now consider the eventually directed (
\Sigma
-normalised) infinite
\mathcal{G}
-word
\xi=\overline{e}\cdots\overline{e}1_{r(e)}ee\dots
beginning with
k+1
repetitions of the loop
\overline{e}
. We have that
\xi
cannot have the form
\gamma_{i}\alpha
for any
i=1,\dots,n
and any infinite
\mathcal{G}_{+}
-word
\alpha
, since the edge
\overline{e}
can only appear in
\gamma_{i}
and no
\gamma_{i}
can contain
k+1
copies of
\overline{e}
. Hence
\xi\notin Z(\gamma_{i}G_{s(\gamma_{i})})
for all
i=1,\dots,n
, so
\{Z(\gamma_{i}G_{s(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is not a cover of
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
. This proves that
\partial X_{\mathcal{G}_{+},x}
is not compact, as claimed.
∎
