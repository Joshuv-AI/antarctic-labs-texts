Adic spaces
Katharina Hübner

Abstract
. Building up on the theory of Huber pairs presented in John Bergdall’s lecture ( [ Ber24 ] ) we explain the construction of adic spaces. We study some important classes of adic spaces such as rigid analytic spaces and formal schemes and show the connections between them. In the course of the lecture we will illustrate the respective concepts with the fundamental examples of the open and closed disc and the affine line.



1.
Introduction

These lecture notes are based on a series of lectures at the Spring school “Non-archimedean geometry and Eigenvarieties” in March 2023 in Heidelberg.
The objective of the first three courses was to give an introduction to the theory of adic spaces.
Like schemes they are locally ringed spaces but with additional structure.
Their structure sheaf carries a topology and every point comes with a valuation on its residue field.
One could say that the foundation of scheme theory is commutative algebra, i.e., the theory of rings and modules.
In this spirit the foundation of the theory of adic spaces would be “topological” commutative algebra, i.e. the theory of topological rings and modules.
In order to construct adic spaces we only need to consider topological rings of a certain kind, so called Huber rings.
Moreover, in order to encode the valuations on residue fields we need to study subrings of integral elements of a Huber ring.
A Huber ring
A
together with a subring
A^{+}
of integral elements is called a Huber pair and denoted
(A,A^{+})
.
An example of a Huber ring would be
\mathds{Q}_{p}
and an example of a ring of integral elements therein would be
\mathds{Z}_{p}
.
So
(\mathds{Q}_{p},\mathds{Z}_{p})
is a Huber pair.

This foundation on topological rings has been laid in the first lecture by John Bergdall.
In this course we build on top of that and start with the construction of the adic spectrum of a Huber pair together with its structure sheaf.
This defines affinoid adic spaces.
We can then glue affinoid adic spaces together to obtain adic spaces, our main objects of study.
The category of adic spaces encompasses many different types of spaces.
In fact, rigid analytic varieties, formal schemes, and algebraic varieties can all be found as full subcategories in the category of adic spaces.
We will throw a spotlight at each of these incarnations of adic spaces and show connections between them.
In the process we will get to know many of the fundamental examples of adic spaces such as the closed and open unit disc and the affine line.

2.
The adic spectrum of a Huber pair

For a Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
we define its adic spectrum.
As a set it is defined as

\Spa(A,A^{+})=\{v:A\to\Gamma\cup\{0\}~\text{continuous valuation}\mid v(A^{+})\leq 1\}/\sim,

where "
\sim
" refers to equivalence of valuations.
In other words

\Spa(A,A^{+})=\{v\in\Cont(A)\mid v(A^{+})\leq 1\}.

If
A^{+}=A^{\circ}
, we often just write
\Spa(A)
instead of
\Spa(A,A^{\circ})
.

For a point
x\in\Spa(A,A^{+})
we use the following notation for the corresponding valuation:

\displaystyle A\longrightarrow

\displaystyle~\Gamma_{x}\cup\{0\}

\displaystyle a\longmapsto

\displaystyle~|a(x)|.

This notation suggests that we should think of taking the valuation of
a
corresponding to a point
x
as evualuating
a
at
x
in the same way a function is evaluated at a point.

The
support
of a valuation
v
on a ring
A
is the prime ideal

\supp v:=\{a\in A\mid v(a)=0\}

In fact a valuation
v
on
A
can equivalently be viewed as a valuation of the field
k(\supp v)
.
We just need to convince ourselves that
v
naturally defines a valuation
\bar{v}
of
A/\supp v
.
Since
\bar{v}
takes the value zero only if the argument is zero, we can extend
\bar{v}
to the residue field
k(\supp v)=\mathrm{Frac}(A/\supp v)
by setting

\bar{v}(\frac{a}{b})=\frac{\bar{v}(a)}{\bar{v}(b)}.

Conversely, any valuation
\bar{v}
of a residue field
k(\mathfrak{p})
for
\mathfrak{p}\in\Spec A
gives rise to a valuation on
A
by composing with the natural map:

v:A\longrightarrow k(\mathfrak{p})\overset{\bar{v}}{\longrightarrow}\Gamma.

This valuation has support
\mathfrak{p}
and the two constructions are inverse to each other.

.

The space

(k,k^{\circ})
be an algebraically closed non-archimedean field, e.g.

The structure of the open unit disc over

The easiest type of points is of the following form:
Take

Let us study in some more detail the adic spectrum of an
affinoid field
.
By this we mean a Huber pair
(k,k^{+})
, where
k
is a field with valuation ring
k^{+}
and such that
k
is endowed either with the valuation topology or the discrete topology.
In case
k
carries the valuation topology and
k^{+}\neq k
, we need the extra condition that the valuation be
microbial
, i.e., that
k^{+}
contains a nonzero topologically nilpotent element (see
[
Hub96
]
, Definition 1.1.4).
But we are on the safe side if we assume that the rank of
k^{+}
is finite (which is fine for all our examples).
If the topology of
k
is discrete, we call the affinoid field
(k,k^{+})
discrete and otherwise analytic.
The standard example of an analytic affinoid field is
(k,k^{\circ})
for a nonarchimedean field
k
.

Remember that the valuation topology associated with the valuation ring
k^{+}
is the topology defined by the metric
1

1

This is not a metric in the classical sense as it might not take values in
\mathds{R}
(in case the valuation has higher rank) but otherwise satisfies all properties of a metric

d(x,y):=|x-y|,

where
|\cdot|
denotes the valuation associated with
k^{+}
.
So the sets

\{x\in k\mid|x|<\gamma\}

for varying
\gamma\in|k^{\times}|
form a fundamental system of open neighborhoods of

.

Exercise 2.3
.

topology_affinoid_field

The valuation topology on
k
coincides with the topology defined by the pair of definition
(k^{\circ},(\varpi))

Moreover
k^{+}

k

Lemma 2.4
.

k_circ_rank1

The subring
k^{\circ}\subseteq k
of powerbounded elements is a valuation ring of rank
1
with maximal ideal
k^{\circ\circ}
.

Proof.

In general, every subring of
k
containing a valuation ring of
k
(in our case
k^{+}
) is again a valuation ring.
Here is the argument:
Suppose
x\in k
is not contained in
k^{\circ}
.
Then it is not contained in
k^{+}
either.
Since
k^{+}
is a valuation ring,
1/x
is contained in
k^{+}
, hence in
k^{\circ}
.

In order to see that
k^{\circ\circ}
is the maximal ideal of
k^{\circ}
, we have to check that every element in
k^{\circ}\setminus k^{\circ\circ}
is a unit of
k^{\circ}
.
If
x\in k^{\circ}
is not topologically nilpotent, there is an open neighborhood
U\subseteq k
of zero not containing any power of
x
.
Unravelling the definition of the valuation topology we can equivalently formulate this in terms of the valuation
|\cdot|
of
k^{+}
:
There is
\gamma\in|k^{\times}|
such that all powers of
x
are bounded below by
\gamma
:

|x^{n}|\geq\gamma\quad\forall n\in\mathds{N}.

This implies

\left|\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)^{n}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\quad\forall n\in\mathds{N},

so the powers of
1/x
are bounded by
1/\gamma
.
This translates to
1/x
being powerbounded in the valuation topology.
We conclude that
1/x
is contained in
k^{\circ}
, hence
x
is invertible.

It remains to convince ourselves that
k^{+}
has rank
1
, or equivalently Krull dimension
1
.
We claim that
(0)\neq k^{\circ\circ}
and these are the only prime ideals of
k^{\circ}
.
The inequality
(0)\neq k^{\circ\circ}
follows from the existence of the topologically nilpotent unit
\varpi
.
Being a unit just means
\varpi\neq 0
, so
\varpi\in k^{\circ\circ}\setminus\{0\}
.
We already know that
k^{\circ}
is a valuation ring (hence in particular local) with maximal ideal
k^{\circ\circ}
.
Any other prime ideal needs to be contained in
k^{\circ\circ}
.
Therefore it suffices to show that the radical ideal generated by any nonzero element
x
of
k^{\circ\circ}
equals
k^{\circ\circ}
.
To see this we take any other element
y\in k^{\circ\circ}
and use its topological nilpotence to find
n\in\mathds{N}
such that

|y^{n}|\leq|x|

This implies that
a:=y^{n}/x
is contained in
k^{+}
, hence in
k^{\circ}
.
In other words,
y
is contained in the radical ideal generated by
x
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

The valuation
|\cdot|^{\circ}
corresponding to
k^{\circ}
can be obtained from
|\cdot|
in the following way.
Remember that an element
\gamma
of a totally ordered group
\Gamma
is
cofinal
if for any other element
\delta\in\Gamma
there is
n\in\mathds{N}
such that

\gamma^{n}\leq\delta.

Note that
x\in k^{\times}
is topologically nilpotent if and only if
|x|
is cofinal in
|k^{\times}|
.
Now we convince ourselves that the subset

\Delta:=\{\gamma\in|k^{\times}|\mid\text{neither $\gamma$ nor $1/\gamma$ is cofinal in $|k^{\times}|$}\}

of
|k^{\times}|
is a convex subgroup.
Then
\Gamma:=|k^{\times}|/\Delta
is a totally ordered group and we claim that
|\cdot|^{\circ}
is given by the composite

k\overset{|\cdot|}{\longrightarrow}|k^{\times}|\cup\{0\}\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma\cup\{0\}.

Notice that
x\in k^{\times}
is powerbounded if and only if
1/x
is
not
topologically nilpotent.
In this way we identify
k^{\circ}
with the set of topologically nilpotent elements
k^{\circ\circ}
together with those elements
x\in k^{\times}
such that neither
x
nor
1/x
is topologically nilpotent.
This is precisely the valuation ring corresponding to the above defined valuation.

Affinoid fields are important as their adic spectrum is the prototype of something like a point in an adic space.
This is not a hundred percent precise since
\Spa(k,k^{+})
may contain several points as the following example shows.

Example 2.6

(The adic spectrum of an affinoid field)
.

spectrum_affinoid_field

We want to understand the adic spectrum of an affinoid field
(k,k^{+})
.
We first take a look at all continuous valuations.
In case the topology of
k
is discrete, there are no restrictions and any valuation ring of
k
defines a continuous valuation.
For example the continuos valuations of
\mathds{Q}
(with the discrete topology) are precisely all
p
-adic valuations for different prime numbers
p
together with the trivial valuation (this follows from Ostrowski’s theorem).

Let us now assume that the valuation ring
k^{+}
is non-trivial, i.e. not equal to
k
and
k
carries the valuation topology.
In other words
(k,k^{+})
is analytic.
It is not hard to see that the valuation corresponding to a valuation ring
R
is continuous if and only if
R
is contained in the subring of powerbounded elements
k^{\circ}\subseteq k
.
In our setting, where the topology on
k
is the valuation topology defined by the valuation ring
k^{+}
,
k^{\circ}
is a valuation ring of rank
1
(see Lemma
2.4
).
Having an inclusion of valuation rings

R\subseteq k^{\circ}\subseteq k

automatically implies that
k^{\circ}
is the localization of
R
at a prime ideal (in our case a prime ideal of height
1
since
k^{\circ}
has rank
1
).
On the one hand, the image of
R
under

\pi:k^{\circ}\twoheadrightarrow k^{\circ}/k^{\circ\circ}

is a valuation ring
\bar{R}
and
R=\pi^{-1}(\bar{R})
.
On the other hand the preimage of any valuation ring of
k^{\circ}/k^{\circ\circ}
under
\pi
is a valuation ring corresponding to a continuous valuation.
In this way we see that the continuous valuations of
k
correspond to the valuations of
k^{\circ}/k^{\circ\circ}
.
Via this correspondence
k^{\circ}
corresponds to the trivial valuation on
k^{\circ}/k^{\circ\circ}
.

Remember that there are no non-trivial valuations on a finite field.
Therefore, if
k^{\circ}/k^{\circ\circ}
is an algebraic extension of a finite field, there is only one continuous valuation of
k
, namely the one corresponding to
k^{\circ}
.
This is the case for
k=\mathds{Q}_{p}
or
k=\mathds{C}_{p}
.

Finally let us describe the adic spectrum
\Spa(k,k^{+})
.
By definition it consists of all (equivalence classes of) continuous valuations whose valuation ring contains
k^{+}
.
These correspond to valuation rings
R
in between
k^{+}
and
k^{\circ}
:

k^{+}\subseteq R\subseteq k^{\circ}.

All valuation rings of this kind are totally ordered by inclusion and can be obtained by localizing
k^{+}
at a prime ideal.
In this case the rank of
R
equals the height of the prime ideal.
If the topology of
k
is discrete,
k^{\circ}=k
and any prime ideal of
k^{+}
is allowed.
If
(k,k^{+})
is analytic,
k^{\circ}
is of rank
1
and thus only the prime ideals of height greater or equal to
1
occur.
We conclude that
\Spa(k,k^{+})
has
\rk k^{+}
many points if
(k,k^{+})
is analytic and
\rk k^{+}+1
many points if
(k,k^{+})
is discrete.

For abelian topological groups and commutative topological rings there is a general theory of completion (see
[
Bou71
]
, III §3).
In our situation the completion
\hat{A}
of a Huber ring
A
is obtained by
I
-adically completing the ring of definition
A_{0}
(where
I\subseteq A_{0}
is an ideal of definition) and then taking the tensor product:

\hat{A}=\hat{A}_{0}\otimes_{A_{0}}A.

We obtain a ring of integral elements
\hat{A}^{+}\subseteq\hat{A}
by taking the topological closure of
A^{+}
in
\hat{A}
.
Then
\hat{A}^{+}
is automatically integrally closed in
\hat{A}
as can be seen by approximation.

Lemma 2.7
.

spectrum_completion

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a Huber pair with completion
(\hat{A},\hat{A}^{+})
.
Then we have a natural identification

\Spa(\hat{A},\hat{A}^{+})\cong\Spa(A,A^{+}).

Proof.

The crucial point is that the adic spectrum only contains
continuous
valuations.
A continuous valuation

v:A\to\Gamma

naturally extends to the completion of
A
.
For a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
in
A
converging to
a\in\hat{A}
the continuity of the valuation
v
implies that for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
the valuation of the difference
v(a_{n}-a_{m})
is less than
\gamma
for
m
and
n
big enough.
If the sequence
(v(a_{n}))_{n\in\mathds{N}}
becomes arbitrarily small for
n\to\infty
, we set
v(a)=0
.

Otherwise we claim that
(v(a_{n}))_{n\in\mathds{N}}
stabilizes.
By assumption there is
C\in\Gamma
with
C>0
such that

v(a_{n})\geq C

for
n
big enough.
Let
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
0<\gamma<C
.
Then for
n
and
m
big enough,
v(a_{n}-a_{m})<\gamma
and therefore

v(a_{m})=v(a_{n}+(a_{m}-a_{n}))=v(a_{n}).

Here we used that

v(a_{m}-a_{n})<\gamma<C\leq a_{n}

such that we even get an equality in the strong triangle inequality.
Hence we can set
v(a)
equal to the constant value
v(a_{n})
for
n
big enough.

Of course one needs to check that the construction does not depend on the chosen sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
and indeed defines a continuous valuation on
\hat{A}
.
∎

We equip
\Spa(A,A^{+})
with the subspace topology from
\Cont(A)
.
This is the topology generated by the subsets of
\Spa(A,A^{+})
of the form

R\left(\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}\right)=\{x\in\Spa(A,A^{+})\mid|f_{i}(x)|\leq|g(x)|\neq 0~\text{for}~i=1,\ldots,n\},

where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},g
are elements of
A
generating an
open
ideal of
A
.
These subsets are called
rational subsets
.
Note that if
A
is a Tate ring, i.e. contains a topologically nilpotent unit
\varpi
, the only open ideal is
A
itself because some power of
\varpi
would have to be contained in it.

If the elements
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},g
do
not
generate an open ideal, the corresponding subset of
\Spa(A,A^{+})
is not rational (by definition) but still open.
This follows from the following exercise.

Exercise 2.8
.

generalized_rational

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},g
be arbitrary elements of
A
.
For each
r\in\mathds{N}

E_{r}
be a finite set of generators of
I^{r}

I

R\left(\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}\right)=\bigcup_{r\in\mathds{N}}R\left(\frac{{\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}}\cup E_{r}}{g}\right)

\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},g\}\cup E_{r}

.

\Spa(A,A^{+})
is naturally isomorphic to
\Spa(\hat{A},\hat{A}^{+})
.
A priori it is not clear, however, whether
\Spa(A,A^{+})
and
\Spa(\hat{A},\hat{A}^{+})
have the same rational subsets.
There could be more of them in
\Spa(\hat{A},\hat{A}^{+})
because we can consider rational subsets defined by

.
Luckily it turns out that we can approximate

Then there is an open neighborhood

R\left(\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}\right)=R\left(\frac{f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{n}}{g^{\prime}}\right).

[

]

∎

As
A
is dense in
\hat{A}
we can choose
f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{n},g^{\prime}

and then the corresponding rational subset

is defined by elements in

.
However, this does not yet ensure that
f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{n},g^{\prime}
generate an open ideal

.
But we can extend the set
\{f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{n}\}
by elements
f^{\prime}_{n+1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{k}

Example 2.10
.

covering_unit_disc

Let
k
be a nonarchimedean field.
For simplicity we assume it is algebraically closed.
Then

\mathds{D}_{k}=\Spa(k\langle T\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle)

is called the
adic unit disc
over
k
.
It can also be described as the subset

\{x\in\Cont(k\langle T\rangle)\mid|T(x)|\leq 1,~|k^{\circ}(x)|\leq 1\}

The second condition ensures that the restriction of
x
to
k
coincides with the given valuation of
k
.
Without this condition
x|_{k}
could be any continuous valuation of
k
.
As we have seen in Example
2.6
the corresponding valuation ring could be a proper subring of
k^{\circ}
(this happens if the rank of
x|_{k}
is greater than
1
).
The condition
|k^{\circ}(x)|\leq 1
forces
k^{\circ}
to be contained in the valuation ring implying that the valuation ring of
x|_{k}
equals
k^{\circ}
.
If
k=\mathds{C}_{p}
, this is automatic as its residue field is
\bar{\mathds{F}}_{p}
, an algebraic extension of a finite field (see Example
2.6
).

For
\alpha\in k^{\circ}
and
r\in|k^{\times}|
the rational subset

\mathds{D}_{k}(\alpha,r):=\{x\in\mathds{D}_{k}\mid|(T-\alpha)(x)|\leq r\}

is a disc of radius
r
around
\alpha
.
Here
\alpha
is considered as a “classical point”, i.e. the valuation

k\langle T\rangle\overset{T\mapsto\alpha}{\longrightarrow}k\overset{|\cdot|}{\longrightarrow}\mathds{R}_{>0}\cup\{0\}.

The “boundary ring”

\mathds{S}_{k}(0,1):=\{x\in\mathds{D}_{k}\mid|T(x)|=1\}=\{x\in\mathds{D}_{k}\mid|T(x)|\leq 1,1\leq|T(x)|\neq 0\}

is also a rational subset.
One might expect that the union of
\mathds{S}_{k}(0,1)
with all discs
\mathds{D}_{k}(0,r)
of radius
r<1
is all of
\mathds{D}_{k}
.
However the union misses one point:
Consider the abelian group
\mathds{R}_{>0}\times\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
We endow it with the lexicographical ordering, i.e.

(a_{1},b_{1})<(a_{2},b_{2})\Leftrightarrow a_{1}<a_{2}~\text{or}~(a_{1}=a_{2}~\text{and}~b_{1}<b_{2}).

Now we pick an element
\epsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}\times\mathds{R}_{>0}
of the form
(1,\epsilon_{2})
with
\epsilon_{2}<1
.
So
\epsilon
is an element that is less than
1=(1,1)
but infinitesimally close to
1
in the sense that
(r_{1},r_{2})<\epsilon
whenever
r_{1}<1
.
Via the embedding

\mathds{R}_{>0}\hookrightarrow\mathds{R}_{>0}\times\mathds{R}_{>0},\qquad r\mapsto(r,1)

we can consider the valuation on
k
as a valuation
k\to(\mathds{R}_{>0}\times\mathds{R}_{>0})\cup\{0\}
.
We now define a valuation

x_{0,1-}:k\langle T\rangle\longrightarrow(\mathds{R}_{>0}\times\mathds{R}_{>0})\cup\{0\}

that maps
f=\sum_{i}a_{i}T^{i}\in k\langle T\rangle
to

|f(x_{0,1-})|=\max_{i}|a_{i}|\epsilon^{i}.

This is indeed an element of
\mathds{D}_{k}
:
It is continuous as for any pseudouniformizer
\varpi\in k
we have that
|\varpi(x_{0,1-})|=|\varpi|
is cofinal in
\mathds{R}_{>0}\times\mathds{R}_{>0}
and
|\varpi f(x_{0,1-})|<1
for any
f\in k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle
(see the criterion for continuity in
[
Ber24
]
, Proposition 1.2.7.1).
Moreover
|f(x_{0,1-})|\leq 1
for all
f\in k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle
, so
x_{0,1-}\in\mathds{D}_{k}
.
However,

|T(x_{0,1-})|=\epsilon

and

r<\epsilon<1~\forall~r\in[0,1).

So neither is
x_{0,1-}
contained in the ring
\mathds{S}_{k}(0,1)
nor in any
\mathds{D}_{k}(0,r)
, and this is indeed the only point of

\mathds{D}_{k}
not contained in this union, as follows from the classification of the points of
\mathds{D}_{k}
, which is explained in
[
Ber24
]
, §1.4.4.

We want to remark here that the point
x_{0,1-}
defined in this way does not depend on the choice of
\epsilon=(1,\epsilon_{2})
as long as
\epsilon_{2}<1
.
The valuations for different choices of
\epsilon
are equivalent.
This is why
\epsilon
does not show up in the index of
x_{0,1-}
.
By the minus sign we indicate that
\epsilon<1
, or equivalently that we take a radius that is infinitesimally smaller than
1
.
If we choose
\epsilon>1
we obtain a different valuation that will play a role in Example
7.12
.

3.
The structure (pre-)sheaf

We want to define on
X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
a sheaf of complete topological rings
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
together with a subsheaf
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}
providing
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
with an integral structure satisfying

{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(X)=\hat{A},\qquad{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}(X)=\hat{A}^{+}

The construction starts by assigning to each rational subset
U
a pair of rings
(A_{U},A_{U}^{+})
.
This defines two presheavs,
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
and
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}
, on the category of rational subsets.
It is in the same spirit as the construction of the structure sheaf on an affine scheme.
Unfortunately there are Huber pairs
(A,A^{+})
such that
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
does not satisfy the sheaf axioms.
The problem lies in the completion of rings that is involved in the construction.
So a priori we only construct a structure presheaf.
We will then discuss the main instances when
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
is actually a sheaf.

For a rational subset

U=R\left(\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}\right)=\{x\in X\mid|f_{i}(x)|\leq|g(x)|\neq 0~\forall i=1,\ldots,n\}

as above we consider the pair of rings

(1)

\hbox to0.0pt{\vbox to0.0pt{\vss\hbox{{\small{Huber_pair_rational}}}\hrule width=0.50003pt,height=6.45831pt,depth=0.0pt}\hss}\penalty\left(A\left[\frac{1}{g}\right],A^{+}\left[\frac{f_{1}}{g},\ldots,\frac{f_{n}}{g}\right]^{N}\right)

where
(\cdot)^{N}
stands for taking the integral closure in
A_{g}=A[\frac{1}{g}]
(
N
for normalization).
We equip it with a topology in the following way.
Let
A_{0}
be a ring of definition and
I
an ideal of definition for the Huber ring
A
.
Then we turn
A_{g}
into a Huber ring by setting its ring of definition equal to

A_{0}\left[\frac{f_{1}}{g},\ldots,\frac{f_{n}}{g}\right]

and its ideal of definition to

IA_{0}\left[\frac{f_{1}}{g},\ldots,\frac{f_{n}}{g}\right].

One checks that with these definitions the pair of rings defined in (
1
) is indeed a Huber pair.
We then take completions to obtain a complete Huber pair
(A_{U},A_{U}^{+})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

rational_subset_spectrum

The homomorphism of Huber pairs
(A,A^{+})\to(A_{U},A_{U}^{+})
induces an injection

\Spa(A_{U},A_{U}^{+})\hookrightarrow\Spa(A,A^{+})

whose image is the rational subset
U
.

Note that Lemma
3.1
allows us to view the rational subset
U
as the adic spectrum of the Huber pair
(A_{U},A_{U}^{+})
.

Proof.

It is clear that the morphism is injective since
A_{U}
is the completion of
A_{g}
and the valuations of
A_{g}
form a subset of the valuations of
A
.
Also a valuation
x
of
A
extends to
A_{g}
if and only if
|g(x)|\neq 0
.
Moreover
x
is less than or equal to
1
on

A^{+}\left[\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}\right]^{N}

if and only if
|f_{i}(x)|\leq|g(x)|
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
This already shows that the image of
\Spa(A_{U},A_{U}^{+})
is contained in
U
.
In order to show the converse inclusion, we need to check that for any
x\in U
its extension to
A_{g}
is continuous.
Remember that we defined

\left(A_{0}\left[\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}\right],IA_{0}\left[\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}\right]\right)

to be a pair of definition for
A_{g}
for a given pair of definition
(A_{0},I)
of
A
.
By the criterion for continuity explained in
[
Ber24
]
, Proposition 1.2.7.1, it is enough to convince ourselves that
|x(b)|
is cofinal in the value group for
b\in I
and
|x(a)|<1
for all
a\in IA_{0}[\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}]
.
This follows directly from the condition
x\in U
.
∎

We now define the presheaf
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
and its subpresheaf
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}
on the category of rational subsets by

{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U):=A_{U},\qquad{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}(U):=A_{U}^{+}.

As mentioned before, there are examples where the sheaf condition is not satified (see Example
3.5
below).
We call a Huber pair
(A,A^{+})

sheafy
if the structure presheaf
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
on
X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
is a sheaf.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a sheafy Huber pair and set
X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
.
Then
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}
is a sheaf.

Proof.

For a rational open covering
U=\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
of a rational open
U\subseteq X
we consider the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{i}{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U_{i})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{ij}{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U_{i}\cap U_{j})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}(U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{i}{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}(U_{i})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{ij}{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}(U_{i}\cap U_{j}).}

The upper row is exact because
(A,A^{+})
is sheafy.
It follows immediately that the left lower arrow is injective.
Exactness in the middle is also clear once we know the following formula, which will be discussed in John’s lecture (Theorem 3.3):

{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}(U)=\{a\in{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)\mid|a(x)|\leq 1~\forall~x\in U\}.

∎

It turns out that in basically all known cases where we can show sheafiness of the structure presheaf the proof can be enhanced just a bit to show in addition that the structure sheaf is acyclic.
By this we mean explicitly that the sheaf cohomology groups

H^{i}(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X})

vanish for
i>0
.
The following theorem summarizes the known results.
We will discuss them below and also explain all the adjectives showing up in the hypothesis.

Theorem 3.3
.

sheafy_results

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a Huber pair satisfying one of the following hypothesis.

(i)

The topology on
A
is discrete,

(ii)

A
has a noetherian ring of definition,

(iii)

A
is a strongly noetherian Tate ring,

(iv)

(A,A^{+})
is stably uniform.

Then
(A,A^{+})
is sheafy and the structure sheaf is acyclic.

The easiest class of sheafy Huber pairs is the class of Huber pairs with discrete topology.
In this case there is no completion involved and the sheaf condition translates to the sheaf condition for fundamental opens in affine schemes.
So the proof of the theorem is straight forward (see
[
Wed19
]
, Theorem 8.28 (c)).

The second case, where
A
has a noetherian ring of definition, is often referred to as the “formal scheme setting”.
The reason behind this nomenclature is that there is a fully faithful functor from locally noetherian formal schemes to adic spaces (see
[
Hub94
]
, Proposition 4.2).
At the level of affine formal schemes it sends the formal spectrum
\Spf(A^{+})
to
\Spa(A^{+},A^{+})
.
The affinoids in the essential image of this functor satisfy condition (ii) of Theorem
3.3
.
Note that not all affinoids satisfying condition (ii) come from formal schemes.
This is only the case for Huber pairs
(A,A^{+})
with
A=A^{+}
.
However, they are closely enough related to formal schemes to make the sheafiness proof for locally noetherian formal schemes work in this case (see
[
Hub94
]
, Theorem 2.5).

We will now treat the case of Tate Huber pairs, which is most relevant to us.
Remember that a Huber ring is Tate if it contains a topologically nilpotent unit.
This is the case for the classes of Huber pairs described in (iii) and (iv) in Theorem
3.3
.

Let us explain the terminology.
A Tate ring
A
is called
strongly noetherian
if
A\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle
is noetherian for any
n\geq 0
.
Looking at the condition for
n=0
this tells us in particular that
\hat{A}
is noetherian.
An equivalent condition for strong noetherianness is that all
A
-algebras that are topologically of finite type are noetherian.
We call a Tate Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
strongly noetherian if
A
is strongly noetherian.
Examples include all Tate rings of the form

k\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle/I

for a nonarchimedean field
k
and any ideal
I\subseteq k\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle
.
This boils down to the classical result from rigid geometry that
k\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle
is noetherian (
[
BGR84
]
, § 5.2.6, Theorem 1).
The proof is by no means trivial and involves the Weierstraß division and preparation theorems.

A Tate ring
A
is
uniform
if the subset of power bounded elements
A^{\circ}
is bounded.
A Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
is called
stably uniform
if for all rational subsets
U\subseteq X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
the Huber ring
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)
is uniform.

There are strongly noetherian Tate Huber pairs that are not stably uniform and vice versa.
For instance for any nonarchimedean field
k
with pseudouniformizer
\varpi
the Huber ring

A:=k\langle T\rangle/(T^{2})

is strongly noetherian but not uniform.
The reason is that all elements
\varpi^{-n}T
for
n\in\mathds{N}
are nilpotent, hence power bounded.
However, none of the sets

\left(\varpi^{m}k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle/(T^{2})\right)\cdot\{\varpi^{-n}T\mid n\in\mathds{N}\}

for
m\in\mathds{N}
is contained in the open
k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle/(T^{2})
as would be required if
\{\varpi^{-n}T\mid n\in\mathds{N}\}
were bounded.

In fact the same argument shows that more generally a Tate ring
A
with pair of definition
(A_{0},(\varpi))
is
not
uniform as soon as it contains a nilpotent element
a
such that

\{\varpi^{n}a\mid n\in\mathds{Z}\}\nsubseteq A_{0}.

On the other hand, a large class of non-noetherian stably uniform Tate Huber pairs is given by perfectoid Huber pairs
(A,A^{+})
:

Proposition 3.4
.

perfectoid_uniform

Any perfectoid Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
is stably uniform.

Proof.

A perfectoid ring is uniform by definition (see Ben’s notes, Definition 2.1.1).
Moreover, by Theorem 3.1.4 in Ben’s notes, for any rational open
U\subseteq\Spa(A,A^{+})
the ring
A_{U}
is again perfectoid, hence uniform.
We conclude that perfectoid Huber pairs are stably uniform.
∎

Let us get back to the question of sheafiness for Tate Huber pairs.
The following example shows that there are indeed non-sheafy Tate Huber pairs.

Example 3.5

(
[
BV18
]
, §4.1)
.

not_sheafy

Let
k
be a complete non-Archimedean field and

A=k[T,T^{-1},Z]/(Z^{2}).

We turn
A
into a Huber
k
-algebra by considering the ring of definition
A_{0}
generated as an
k^{\circ}
-module by

\varpi^{|n|}T^{n},\qquad\varpi^{-|n|}T^{n}Z,\quad\forall~n\in\mathds{Z}.

Then
A_{0}
is not noetherian as

ZA\cap A_{0}=(Z,\frac{Z}{\varpi T},\frac{Z}{\varpi^{2}T^{2}},\ldots)

is not finitely generated.

We consider the cover of
X=\Spa(A,A^{\circ})
by the two opens

U=\{x\in X\mid|T(x)|\leq 1\},\qquad V=\{x\in X\mid|T(x)|\geq 1\}.

We claim that

{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(X)\to{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)\oplus{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(V)

is not injective.
We have

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(X)

\displaystyle=\hat{A}=\lim_{n}A/(\varpi^{n}A_{0})

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n}A/(\varpi^{n}A_{0}[T])

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(V)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n}A/(\varpi^{n}A_{0}[T^{-1}]).

As
\varpi^{-n}Z\notin A_{0}
for any
n\in\mathds{N}
, we know that
Z\neq 0
in
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(X)
.
But

\displaystyle\varpi^{-n}Z

\displaystyle=(\varpi^{-n}T^{-n}Z)T^{n}\in A_{0}[T],

\displaystyle\varpi^{-n}Z

\displaystyle=(\varpi^{-n}T^{n}Z)T^{-n}\in A_{0}[T^{-1}].

Hence,
Z=0
on
U
and on
V
.

In Example
3.5
even though
A
is noetherian, it is not strongly noetherian.
Neither is
A
uniform because
Z
is nilpotent and
\varpi^{-m}Z
for
m\geq 1
is not contained in
A_{0}
.

Let us now outline the general strategy to study the
question of sheafiness for a Tate Huber pair.
As mentioned before we show sheafiness and acyclicity at the same time.
Let
X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
for a Tate Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
.
For an open covering
U=\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
for rational subsets
U
and
U_{i}
we consider the sequence

(2)

\hbox to0.0pt{\vbox to0.0pt{\vss\hbox{{\small{sequence_acyclicity}}}\hrule width=0.50003pt,height=6.45831pt,depth=0.0pt}\hss}\penalty 0\to{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)\to\prod_{i}{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U_{i})\to\prod_{ij}{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U_{i}\cap U_{j})\to\prod_{ijk}{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U_{i}\cap U_{j}\cap U_{k})\to\ldots

Exactness at the first two terms gives the sheaf condition for the covering
U=\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
and exactness at all other terms is equivalent to the vanishing of the C̆ech cohomology groups

\check{H}^{i}(U,{\mathscr{O}}_{X})

for
i>0
.
It is a general fact (see
[
SP
, Tag 01EV]
) that in order to show that the sheaf cohomology groups
H^{i}(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X})
vanish, it suffices to show that all C̆ech cohomology groups as above for all coverings
U=\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
vanish.

In the proof for sheafiness the first step is to reduce to the case of very simple coverings.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a Tate Huber pair and set
X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
.

•

Let
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
be elements of
A
generating the unit ideal.
The
standard rational covering
generated by
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
is the covering of
X
by

U_{i}=R\left(\frac{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}}{t_{i}}\right),\quad\text{where }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

•

Let
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
be in
A
.
The
standard Laurent covering
generated by
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
is the covering of
X
by

U_{I}=\{x\in X\mid|t_{i}(x)|\leq 1~\forall i\in I,~|t_{i}|\geq 1~\forall~i\notin I\},\quad\text{where }I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}.

•

A
simple
Laurent covering is a standard Laurent covering generated by one element.

It is clear that Laurent coverings are indeed coverings.
In case of standard rational coverings this is ensured by the condition that
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
generate the unit ideal:
For
x\in X
we take
t_{i}
such that
|t_{i}(x)|
is maximal among all
|t_{j}(x)|
.
Because
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
generate the unit ideal, we must have
|t_{i}(x)|\neq 0
.
By definition this implies
x\in U_{i}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Laurent_cover

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a complete Tate Huber pair and
X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
.

(i)

Every covering of
X
has a refinement by a standard rational covering.

(ii)

For every standard rational covering
{\mathscr{U}}
of
X
there exists a Laurent covering
{\mathscr{V}}
of
X
such that for every
V\in{\mathscr{V}}
the covering
{\mathscr{U}}_{V}:=\{V\cap U\mid U\in{\mathscr{U}}\}
is a standard rational covering generated by units.

(iii)

For every standard rational covering
{\mathscr{U}}
of
X
generated by units, there is a Laurent covering
{\mathscr{V}}
of
X
refining
{\mathscr{U}}
.

Proof.

See
[
Mor19
]
, Proposition IV.2.3.3.
∎

Lemma
3.7
tells us that it is enough to show exactnes of sequence (
2
) for Laurent coverings.
Indeed if we start with some covering of
X
, we can refine it by a standard rational covering by part (i) of the lemma.
It is formal to check that exactness of sequence (
2
) for a refinement of the covering implies exactness for the original covering.
We are thus reduced to deal with a standard rational covering
{\mathscr{U}}
.
By part (ii) of Lemma
3.7
we can find a Laurent covering
{\mathscr{V}}
of
X
such that for every
V\in{\mathscr{V}}
the restriction
{\mathscr{U}}_{V}
is a standard rational covering of
V
generated by units.
Now we can convince ourselves that it is enough to prove exactness of sequence (
2
) for the Laurent covering
{\mathscr{V}}
and for each of the restricted coverings
{\mathscr{U}}_{V}
.
But standard rational coverings that are generated by units can be refined by Laurent coverings by part (iii) of the lemma.
We are thus reduced to Laurent coverings.
For a Laurent covering
{\mathscr{U}}
generated by
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
we consider the simple Laurent covering
X=W_{-}\cup W_{+}
with

W_{-}=\{x\in X\mid|t_{n}(x)|\leq 1\},\qquad W_{+}=\{x\in X\mid|t_{n}(x)|\geq 1\}.

The restrictions
{\mathscr{U}}_{W_{-}}
and
{\mathscr{U}}_{W_{+}}
are Laurent coverings generated by
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-1}
.
We can thus use induction to conclude that it is enough to prove exactness of sequence (
2
) for simple Laurent coverings.

We summarize our arguments in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.8
.

reduction_simple_Laurent

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a Tate Huber pair and set
X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
.
Suppose that for any rational open
W\subseteq\Spa(A,A^{+})
and any simple Laurent covering
W=W_{-}\cup W_{+}
, the sequence

(3)

\hbox to0.0pt{\vbox to0.0pt{\vss\hbox{{\small{exactness_structure_sheaf}}}\hrule width=0.50003pt,height=6.45831pt,depth=0.0pt}\hss}\penalty 0\to{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W)\to{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W_{-})\oplus{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W_{+})\to{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W_{-}\cap W_{+})\to 0

is exact.
Then
(A,A^{+})
is sheafy and
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
is acyclic.

We can now discuss case (iii) of Theorem
3.3
, where
A
is a strongly noetherian Tate ring.
The result goes back to Tate’s acyclicity theorem first formulated in the language of Tate’s rigid analytic spaces.
Huber gave a proof in the more general setting of strongly noetherian Tate Huber pairs (see
[
Hub94
]
, Theorem 2.2) using the same approach.

Idea of the proof.

First note that being strongly noetherian is stable under passing to a rational open subset

W=R\left(\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}\right)\subseteq\Spa(A,A^{+}).

with
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})=A
.
The reason is that we can write
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W)
as

{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W)=A\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle/(gT_{i}-f_{i}).

Therefore, we need to check the hypothesis of Lemma
3.8
only for
W=X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
.
We consider a simple Laurent covering defined by an element
t\in A
, i.e., the covering of
X
by

W_{-}=\{x\in X\mid|t(x)|\leq 1\},\qquad W_{+}=\{x\in X\mid|t(x)|\geq 1\}.

We have

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(X)

\displaystyle=\hat{A}

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W_{-})

\displaystyle=A\langle T\rangle/(T-t)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W_{+})

\displaystyle=A\langle S\rangle/(tS-1)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W_{-}\cap W_{+})

\displaystyle=A\langle S,T\rangle/(T-t,ST-1)

The strongly noetherian assumption enters when showing that

\hat{A}\to A\langle T\rangle/(T-t)\times A\langle S\rangle/(tS-1)

is faithfully flat, hence injective.
Showing the remaining exactness properties comes down to an explicit computation.
∎

Finally we want to explain the main argument for sheafiness in case (iv) of Theorem
3.3
, where
(A,A^{+})
is stably uniform.
The proof is due to Kevin Buzzard and Alain Verberkmoes and we refer to their article (
[
BV18
]
, Proposition 12) for the details.

Idea of the proof.

By definition any rational subset of
X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
is again stably uniform.
This reduces us to checking the hypothesis of Lemma
3.8
for a simple Laurent covering of
W=X
.
Explicitly this is a covering of the form

X=\{x\in X\mid|t(x)|\leq 1\}\cup\{x\in X\mid|t(x)|\geq 1\}=:W_{-}\cup W_{+}.

We consider the following topological rings with topology given by the respective ring of definition and ideal of definition generated by
\varpi
:

\displaystyle A_{-}=A,

\displaystyle\qquad(A_{-})_{0}=A_{0}[t]

\displaystyle A_{+}=A[1/t],

\displaystyle\qquad(A_{+})_{0}=A_{0}[1/t]

\displaystyle A_{\pm}=A[1/t],

\displaystyle\qquad(A_{\pm})_{0}=A_{0}[t,1/t].

(Here we are a bit sloppy because the homomorphims
\phi:A\to A[1/t]
might not be injective, so we should write
(A_{+})_{0}=\phi(A_{0})[1/t]
etc.)
Then

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(X)

\displaystyle=\hat{A}

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W_{-})

\displaystyle=\widehat{A_{-}}

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W_{+})

\displaystyle=\widehat{A_{+}}

\displaystyle{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(W_{-}\cap W_{+})

\displaystyle=\widehat{A_{\pm}}

The sequence

0\to A\overset{\epsilon}{\longrightarrow}A_{-}\oplus A_{+}\overset{\delta}{\longrightarrow}A_{\pm}\to 0

is obviously exact and completion gives the sequence (
3
).
However, completion might destroy exactness if
\epsilon
and
\delta
are not strict (quotient topology on image coming from the source is the same as subspace topology coming from the target).
The map
\delta
is always strict because it is surjective and the image
of
(A_{-})_{0}\oplus(A_{+})_{0}
is
(A_{\pm})_{0}
and thus open; therefore the sequence (
3
) is exact if and only if
\epsilon
is strict.
The subspace topology on
A
induced from
A_{-}\oplus A_{+}
is given by the ring of definition
S_{0}=(A_{-})_{0}\cap(A_{+})_{0}
and strictness means that the sets
\varpi^{n}A_{0}
are open with respect to the topology defined by
S_{0}
.
This boils down to checking that there is
m\in\mathds{N}
such that

\varpi^{m}S_{0}\subseteq A_{0}

One can show that
S_{0}\subseteq A^{\circ}
.
By hypothesis
A
is uniform, so
A^{\circ}
, hence
S_{0}
, is bounded.
This implies the claim.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Every perfectoid Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
is sheafy and the structure sheaf is acyclic.

Proof.

This follows from case (iv) of Theorem
3.3
since perfectoid Huber pairs are stably uniform by Proposition
3.4
.
∎

4.
Adic spaces

In this section we explain how to glue adic spectra of sheafy Huber pairs to obtain adic spaces.
The process is similar to what is done for defining schemes.
Remember that a scheme is a locally ringed space that is locally isomorphic to the spectrum of a ring.
An adic space is also a locally ringed space but it has additional structure.
So instead of the category of locally ringed spaces we work in the category of locally
v
-ringed spaces, which is defined as follows.

•

Objects are triples
(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X},\{v_{x}\}_{x\in X})
, where
(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X})
is a locally topologically ringed space and for each
x\in X
we have an equivalence class of continuous valuations
v_{x}
on the local ring
{\mathscr{O}}_{X,x}
whose support is the maximal ideal.

•

Morphisms

(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X},\{v_{x}\}_{x\in X})\longrightarrow(Y,{\mathscr{O}}_{Y},\{v_{y}\}_{y\in Y})

are morphisms of locally topologically ringed spaces
(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X})\to(Y,{\mathscr{O}}_{Y})
such that for a point
x\in X
mapping to
y\in Y
we have
v_{x}|_{{\mathscr{O}}_{Y,y}}=v_{y}
.

The collection of valuations
\{v_{x}\}_{x\in X}
determines a subsheaf
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}
of
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
by setting for an open
U\subseteq X

{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{+}(U)=\{a\in{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)\mid v_{x}(a_{x})\leq 1~\forall~x\in U\}.

Every sheafy Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
determines a locally
v
-ringed space by setting
X=\Spa(A,A^{+})
and considering the triple
(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X},\{v_{x}\}_{x\in X})
, where the valuations
v_{x}
are determined by
x
in the following way.
For every rational open neighborhood
U\subseteq X
of
x
,
x
comes from a unique valuation on
{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)
(see Lemma
3.1
).
In the colimit over all neighborhoods we obtain the valuation
v_{x}
on
{\mathscr{O}}_{X,x}
.
We call a locally
v
-ringed space arising in this way an
affinoid adic space
.

A morphism of
v
-ringed spaces
(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X},\{v_{x}\}_{x\in X})\to(Y,{\mathscr{O}}_{Y},\{v_{y}\}_{y\in Y})
is an
open immersion
if it induces a homeomorphism of
X
onto an open subspace
U\subseteq Y
and an isomorphism

(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X},\{v_{x}\}_{x\in X})\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}(U,{\mathscr{O}}_{Y}|_{U},\{v_{u}\}_{u\in U}).

Inclusions of rational opens in an affinoid naturally give rise to open immersions of locally
v
-ringed spaces.

Definition 4.1
.

An adic space is a locally
v
-ringed space
(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X},\{v_{x}\}_{x\in X})
that admits an open covering
X=\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
by affinoid adic spaces.

Adic spaces can be obtained from affinoid adic spaces by glueing just in the same way as for schemes.

.

Example 4.3

(The affine line)
.

example_affine_line

Let
k
be a nonarchimedean field with pseudouniformizer
\varpi\in k
.
For
n\in\mathds{N}
the
closed (adic) disc of radius

is defined as

\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-n}|)=\Spa(k\langle\varpi^{n}T\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle\varpi^{n}T\rangle),

where
\varpi^{n}T
is to be treated as a variable.
So actually the discs
\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-n}|)
are all isomorphic but we write them like this because of the way they are contained in each other.
The notation is justified by looking at classical points:
For
\alpha\in k
the valuation

x_{\alpha}:k[T]\overset{T\mapsto\alpha}{\longrightarrow}k\overset{|\cdot|}{\longrightarrow}\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}

extends to a continuous valuation on
k\langle\varpi^{n}T\rangle
precisely if

|\varpi^{n}T(x_{\alpha})|\leq 1\iff|\alpha|\leq\varpi^{-n}.

For
m\geq n
we have natural homomorphisms

(4)

\hbox to0.0pt{\vbox to0.0pt{\vss\hbox{{\small{transition_maps_discs}}}\hrule width=0.50003pt,height=6.45831pt,depth=0.0pt}\hss}\penalty(k\langle\varpi^{m}T\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle\varpi^{m}T\rangle)\longrightarrow(k\langle\varpi^{n}T\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle\varpi^{n}T\rangle)

mapping
\varpi^{m}T

\varpi^{m-n}(\varpi^{n}T)

The corresponding morphism of discs

\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-n}|)\to\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-m}|)

into the disc of radius

In fact,

can be identified with the rational subset

\{x\in\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-m}|)\mid|T(x)|\leq\varpi^{-n}\}.

So we have an ascending sequence of discs whose radii become arbitrarily large.
Since the inclusions are open immersions, we can glue the discs to obtain the

As an adic space the affine line is not affinoid.
This can be seen by noting that the discs
\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-n}|)
cover
{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}
but this covering does not have a finite subcovering.
However, all affinoid adic spaces are quasi-compact.

Let us compute the global sections of the structure sheaf of
{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}
.
The sheaf property gives us an exact sequence

{\mathscr{O}}_{{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}}({\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1})\longrightarrow\prod_{m\in\mathds{N}}{\mathscr{O}}_{{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}}(\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-m}|))\longrightarrow\prod_{m,n}{\mathscr{O}}_{{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}}(\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-n}|)\cap\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-m}|)).

For
m\geq n
the disc
\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-n}|)
is contained in
\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-m}|)
.
This simplifies the right hand term.
Throwing out redundant factors we obtain an exact sequence

{\mathscr{O}}_{{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}}({\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1})\longrightarrow\prod_{m\in\mathds{N}}{\mathscr{O}}_{{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}}(\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-m}|))\overset{\varphi}{\longrightarrow}\prod_{m>n}{\mathscr{O}}_{{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}}(\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-n}|)),

where

maps

to

(\alpha_{m}|_{\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-n}|)}-\alpha_{n})_{m\geq n}

The kernel of

identifies with the projective limit

\lim_{m\in\mathds{N}}{\mathscr{O}}_{A_{k}^{1}}(\mathds{D}_{k}(0,|\varpi^{-m}|))

with transition maps given by (
4
).
The rings
k\langle\varpi^{m}T\rangle
are all subrings of the ring of formal power series
k\llbracket T\rrbracket
.
Hence, the transition maps are injective and the above limit can be viewed just as an intersection taking place in
k\llbracket T\rrbracket
.

Remember that

\displaystyle k\langle\varpi^{m}T\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\varpi^{im}T^{i}\in k\llbracket T\rrbracket\mid a_{i}\to 0\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}b_{i}T^{i}\in k\llbracket T\rrbracket\mid b_{i}\varpi^{-im}\to 0\right\}

are the power series converging when inserting
x\in k
with
|x|\leq|\varpi^{-m}|
.
The intersection of these for all
m\in\mathds{N}
are the power series that converge everywhere.
They are also called
entire power series
and denoted by

.
Now what do they look like?
Of course the polynomial ring is contained in
k\{\{T\}\}
.
To construct an infinite series in
k\{\{T\}\}
, we just need to come up with coefficients that decrease very rapidly, faster than any polynomial.
An example would be

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\varpi^{i^{2}}T^{i}.

Indeed, for any

converges to zero as

is topologically nilpotent and

.

Example 4.4

(The open unit disc)
.

(The projective line)

\displaystyle(k\langle S,S^{-1}\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle S,S^{-1}\rangle)

5.
Local Huber pairs

In the theory of schemes taking the intersection of all open neighborhoods corresponds to forming the localization at the corresponding prime ideal at the level of rings.

, so that

∎

This lemma tells us that the spectrum of

consists of the spectrum of

plus additional prime ideals strictly containing

.
These prime ideals are totally ordered by inclusion and identify with the spectrum of

(minus the zero ideal).

The topology of a Huber pair

is particularly nice if

is uniform.
Then

is bounded and thus a ring of definition.
Hence the results of

apply to any ideal of definition

.

In this setting we use the following notation.

(formal for formal scheme).
If in addition,

.
Otherwise, if

.

We want to convince ourselves that in the analytic case the resulting topology of

Let us consider a uniform analytic local Huber pair

.
In particular, the corresponding valuation

is non-trivial or in other words

.

Exercise 5.4
.

I_top_nil

Let

be an ideal of definition.

.

Let

We summarize our results in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a uniform analytic local Huber pair.
Then

Corollary 5.6

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a uniform local Huber pair.
Then
(k_{A},k_{A}^{+})
(with the induced topology) is an affinoid field.

Lemma 5.7

The completion

Now the assertion of the lemma (in the formal case) essentially follows from the fact that the

The closure of the maximal ideal

.

Let us now consider the analytic case.
By Exercise

for a topologically nilpotent unit

For the valuation topology we know that the completion

The denomination

is justified for a local Huber pair by the following result.

Proposition 5.8
.

A complete Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
with ring of definition
A^{+}
is local if and only if
\Spa(A,A^{+})
is local as a topological space (it has a unique closed point and all other points specialize to it).
Furthermore, an adic space is local (as a topological space) if and only if it is the spectrum of a local Huber pair.

Proof.

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a local Huber pair.
We claim that
\Spa(A,A^{+})
is local with closed point
x
given by the valuation ring
k_{A}^{+}\subseteq k_{A}
.
Since
\Spa(A,A^{+})
is spectral, it suffices to show that any open neighborhood of

equals
\Spa(A,A^{+})
.
In fact it is enough to check this for a rational open neighborhood

U:=R\left(\frac{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}}{g}\right)=\{y\in\Spa(A,A^{+})\mid|f_{i}(y)|\leq|g(y)|\neq 0~\forall i=1,\ldots,n\}

of

defined by elements

generating the unit ideal.
Since

is supposed to be contained in

, it satisfies

|f_{i}(x)|\leq|g(x)|\neq 0

for

.
The condition

, so

is invertible in

.
This implies that

for
any
valuation of

.
The inequalities can now be rearranged to

\left|\frac{f_{i}}{g}(x)\right|\leq 1,

or in other words
f_{i}/g\in A^{+}
.
But any point

satisfies

for all

, in particular for

.
We conclude that all points of
\Spa(A,A^{+})
are contained in

.

Conversely suppose that
(A,A^{+})
is a complete Huber pair such that
\Spa(A,A^{+})
is local with closed point

.
We first show that

is local with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}:=\supp x
.
Suppose that

is a maximal ideal different from

.
Since
\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
is a maximal ideal of a complete Huber ring it is possible to construct a continuous valuation

with support

lying in
\Spa(A,A^{+})
(see
[
Hub94
]
, Lemma 1.4).
But

does not specialize to

.
This can be seen by choosing an element

of

that is not contained in
\mathfrak{m}
.
Then the open subset

\{x\in\Spa(A,A^{+})\mid|g(x)|\neq 0\}

of
\Spa(A,A^{+})
contains

but not

.
To conclude,
x^{\prime}
does not specialize to
x
, which gives a contradiction to our assumption that

is not local.
Thus we now know that
A
is local with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
.
We denote its residue field by

.

In order to prove that

is the preimage in
A
of a valuation ring of

, it suffices to check the following:
Every

with

is a unit in

and

(the reader can convince themselves that this criterion holds or consult
[
KZ96
]
, Theorem 2.5).
For such an

we consider the rational subset

R\left(\frac{a}{1}\right)=\{y\in\Spa(A,A^{+})\mid|a(y)|\leq 1\}.

Its complement is nonempty as

a\notin A^{+}=\{b\in A\mid|b(y)|\leq 1~\forall y\in\Spa(A,A^{+})\}.

Since
\Spa(A,A^{+})
is local with closed point

, this implies that
x
is contained in the complement, or in other words

|a(x)|>1.

First this implies
|a(x)|\neq 0
, whence

, so

.
Then we get

\left|\frac{1}{a}(x)\right|<1,

so

We conclude that this rational subset is all of
\Spa(A,A^{+})
and thus
1/a\in A^{+}
.

The condition on the topology of

follows automatically as
x
is a continuous valuation, which means that the valuation topology on

defined by

is coarser than the given topology.

Finally any local adic space needs to be affinoid as adic spaces are locally affinoid.
Therefore, the second assertion of the proposition follows from the first one.
∎

We now have a basic understanding of local Huber pairs.
Our goal is to interpret
X_{x}
for a point
x
of an adic space

as the adic spectrum of a local Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
.
This local Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
should morally be given by
({\mathscr{O}}_{X,x},{\mathscr{O}}_{X,x}^{+})
.
Unfortunately,
({\mathscr{O}}_{X,x},{\mathscr{O}}_{X,x}^{+})
is not uniform, in general.
While it is true that
({\mathscr{O}}_{X,x},{\mathscr{O}}_{X,x}^{+})
is local, we run into trouble when it comes to the topology.
The colimit topology on
{\mathscr{O}}_{X,x}
might be finer than the valuation topology of the valuation corresponding to
x
.
But we don’t want to use the colimit topology because it is quite messy and does not reflect the geometry of
X_{x}
.

We can solve this issue by resorting to the concept of limit of adic spaces defined in
[
Hub96
]
, Definition 2.4.2.
Following Huber an adic space
X
together with compatible maps

\pi_{i}:X\longrightarrow X_{i}

is called a limit of a projective system of adic spaces
(X_{i})_{i\in I}
if the following two assertions hold.

(i)

The morphisms
\pi
induce an identification
X=\lim_{i}X_{i}
as topological spaces.

(ii)

For every
x\in X
there is an open neighborhood
U\subseteq X
such that the image of

\colim_{(i,V)}{\mathscr{O}}_{X_{i}}(V)\longrightarrow{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)

is dense, where
(i,V)
runs over all pairs with
i\in I
and
V
an open subset of
X_{i}
containing the image of
U
.

We then write

X\sim\lim_{i\in I}X_{i}.

Using “
\sim
” instead of “

” reflects the fact that this is not a projective limit in the categorical sense.
For that we would need the union in part (ii) to
equal

{\mathscr{O}}_{X}(U)
.
But it turns out that the categorical limit is too restrictive in many situations and the above defined concept of limit serves our purposes.

Our strategy now is to write down a nice local Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
, define

X_{x}:=\Spa(A,A^{+}),

and show that

X_{x}\sim\lim_{U}U,

where the limit runs over all open neighborhoods

of

.

We start with an affinoid open neighborhood
\Spa(B,B^{+})
of
x
.
Let

denote the support of
x
.
We define

and consider the natural projection

\pi:A=B_{\mathfrak{p}}\twoheadrightarrow k(\mathfrak{p})=k(x)

The point
x
comes with a valuation of
k(x)
whose valuation ring we denote by

.
Then we set

A^{+}:=\{a\in A\mid\pi(a)\in k(x)^{+}\}.

We still need to specify a topology on
A
.
Let
(B_{0},J)
be a pair of definition for

and assume

(otherwise we replace

We then take

(with ring of definition

).
This is the point where we possibly make the topology coarser than the colimit topololgy, in which

might possibly not be bounded.

With this topology

is a local Huber pair and

Exercise 5.9
.

If

Analytic adic spaces

Analytic adic spaces are those one would say are closest to rigid analytic varieties but without finiteness assumptions.

Definition 6.1
.

•

Let
(A,A^{+})
be a Huber pair.
A point
x\in\Spa(A,A^{+})
is
analytic
if the prime ideal
\supp x
of
A
is
not
open.

•

A point
x
of an adic space
X
is
analytic
if it has an affinoid neighborhood
\Spa(A,A^{+})
such that
x
is analytic as a point of
\Spa(A,A^{+})
.

•

An adic space is
analytic
if all its points are analytic.

Let us explain the intuition behind this definition.
We take a point
x
of an adic space
X
and consider the (uniformized) local Huber pair
({\mathscr{O}}_{X,x},{\mathscr{O}}_{X,x}^{+})
.
Then we have the following:

Exercise 6.2
.

With the above notation
x
is analytic if and only if
({\mathscr{O}}_{X,x},{\mathscr{O}}_{X,x}^{+})
is analytic or, in other words, the residue field
k(x)
carries the valuation topology.
Moreover, the points with support
\supp x
correspond precisely to the continuous valuations of
k(x)
.

We learn from the exercise that only in the analytic case there is a continuity condition involved for valuations of
k(x)
.
Or in other words, for a Huber pair
(A,A^{+})
and an open prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
,
any
valuation of the residue field
k(\mathfrak{p})
defines a continuous valuation of
A
.
In order to be an element of
\Spa(A,A^{+})
it just needs to satisfy the condition that it is less or equal to one on
A^{+}
.

Let us get back to the study of analytic points and look at some examples.

Example 6.3
.

\Spa(\mathds{Z}_{p},\mathds{Z}_{p})
has two points: the
p
-adic valuation on
\mathds{Q}_{p}
and the trivial valuation on
\mathds{F}_{p}=\mathds{Z}_{p}/p\mathds{Z}_{p}
.
The former point is analytic and the latter point is not.

The analyticity of a point
x\in X
does not depend on the choice of an affinoid open neighborhood
\Spa(A,A^{+})
of
x
.
This is because for a rational subset
\Spa(B,B^{+})\subseteq\Spa(A,A^{+})
, the homomorphism
A\to B
is adic, which is defined as follows.

Definition 6.4
.

•

A homomorphism of Huber rings
\varphi:R\to S
is
adic
if
R
and
S
have rings of definition
R_{0}
and
S_{0}
with
\varphi(R_{0})\subseteq S_{0}
such that for an ideal of definition
I_{R}\subseteq R_{0}
the ideal
\varphi(I_{R})S_{0}
is an ideal of definition for
S
.

•

A morphism of adic spaces is
adic
if it is locally defined by an adic morphism of Huber pairs.

In other words one could say that for an adic homomorphism
R\to S
, the topology on
S
is defined by the topology on
R
.
In particular, an ideal
J\subset S
is open if and only if its preimage in
R
is open.
This settles the issue brought up above that analyticity is independent of the choice of affinoid open neighborhood.

We want to remark further that the critical point in the above definition is not that we can find rings of definition with
\varphi(R_{0})\subseteq S_{0}
.
This is possible for any homomorphism of Huber rings.
The crucial point is that
\varphi(I_{R})S_{0}
is an ideal of definition.

Proposition 6.5
.

characterize_analytic

Let
x
be a point of an adic space
X
.
The following are equivalent.

(1)

x
is analytic,

(2)

x
has an affinoid open neighborhood
\Spa(A,A^{+})
such that

is a Tate ring (i.e. has a topologically nilpotent unit).

Proof.

Suppose
x
has an open affinoid neighborhood
\Spa(A,A^{+})
such that
\supp x\subseteq A
is not open.
Let
I=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
be an ideal of definition in a ring of definition
A_{0}
of
A
.
We can order the generators
a_{i}
in such a way that the value
|a_{1}(x)|
is the maximum of all
|a_{i}(x)|
.
This maximum cannot be zero as otherwise all
a_{i}
would be contained in
\supp x
and thus
\supp x
would be open.
Hence
x
is contained in the rational subset

R\left(\frac{a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}}{a_{1}}\right)=\{y\in\Spa(A,A^{+})\mid|a_{i}(y)|\leq|a_{1}(y)|\neq 0~\forall~i=1,\ldots,f\}.

The corresponding Huber pair is the completion of

(A_{a_{1}},A^{+}[\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}},\ldots,\frac{a_{r}}{a_{1}}]^{N}),

where
(\cdot)^{N}
refers to taking the integral closure in
A_{a_{1}}
.
Since
a_{1}\in I
, it is topologically nilpotent.
Its image in
A_{a_{1}}
is also topologically nilpotent and moreover, it is a unit.
Therefore
A_{a_{1}}
is a Tate ring.

For the converse direction we just need to note that a Tate ring with topolologically nilpotent unit
\varpi
does not have open prime ideals as every open ideal has to contain the unit
\varpi
.
∎

From this characterization we see that rigid analytic varieties (defined in the next section) and perfectoid spaces (discussed in Ben Heuer’s lecture (
[
Heu23
]
) are analytic.

Lemma 6.6
.

nonanalytic_to_nonanalytic

Let
f:X\to Y
be a morphism of Huber pairs.
Then
f
takes non-analytic points to non-analytic points.

Proof.

We may assume that
f
is given by a homomorphism of Huber pairs
\varphi:(A,A^{+})\to(B,B^{+})
.
A non-analytic point
x
has open support
\supp x
.
Its image in
Y
has support
\varphi^{-1}(\supp x)
, which is again open.
∎

It can happen, however, that a morphism takes analytic points to non-analytic points.
For instance, take a Tate ring
R
with ring of integral elements
R^{+}
.
Let
R_{\mathit{disc}}
be the same ring
R
but endowed with the discrete topology and set
R_{\mathit{disc}}^{+}=R^{+}
.
Then the identity induces a homomorphism of Huber pairs
(R_{\mathit{disc}},R_{\mathit{disc}}^{+})\to(R,R^{+})
.
Consider the corresponding morphism of adic spaces

\Spa(R,R)\to\Spa(R_{\mathit{disc}},R_{\mathit{disc}}^{+}).

All points of
\Spa(R,R^{+})
are analytic and all points of
\Spa(R_{\mathit{disc}},R_{\mathit{disc}}^{+})
are non-analytic.
Something like this cannot happen for adic morphisms:

Lemma 6.7
.

analytic_to_analytic

Every adic morphism
f:X\to Y
takes analytic points to analytic points.

Proof.

This follows from the observation that for an adic homomorphism of Huber pairs
\varphi:(R,R^{+})\to(S,S^{+})
an ideal
J\subseteq S
is open if and only if
\varphi^{-1}(J)
is open.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

analytic_adic

Every morphism of analytic adic spaces is adic.

Proof.

It suffices to consider a morphism induced by a homomorphism of Tate rings
\varphi:(R,R^{+})\to(S,S^{+})
(see Proposition
6.5
).
Let
R_{0}
and
S_{0}
be rings of definition with
\varphi(R_{0})\subseteq S_{0}
.
The ideal generated by any topologically nilpotent unit
\varpi
of
R
which is contained in

is an ideal of definition of
R_{0}
.
But
\varphi(\varpi)
is a topologically nilpotent unit of
S
that is contained in
S_{0}
, so it generates an ideal of definition.
∎

We learn from Lemma

and Lemma

that adic morphisms take analytic points to analytic points and non-analytic points to non-analytic points.
In particular, for an adic space

7.
Morphisms of finite type

In this section we explain what it means for a morphism of adic spaces to be of finite type.
It is too restrictive to require the corresponding ring maps to be of finite type.
Already the Tate algebra

k\langle T\rangle=\left\{\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}T^{n}\in k\llbracket T\rrbracket\mid a_{n}\to 0\right\}

over a nonarchimedean field
k
is not finitely generated but
k\langle T\rangle
is supposed to be a topological analogue of the ring of polynomials
k[T]
.
Note that we can state the property of a ring homomorphism
R\to S
to be of finite type by requiring
S
to be a quotient of a polynomial ring over
R
in finitely many variables.
This notion can be transported to the realm of Huber rings by considering topological quotients:

Definition 7.1
.

A homomorphism
R\to S
of complete Huber rings is a
quotient map
if it is surjective, continuous, and open.
A homomorphism
(R,R^{+})\to(S,S^{+})
of Huber pairs is a
quotient map
if
R\to S
is a quotient map and
S^{+}
is the relative integral closure of the image of
R^{+}
in
S
.

Any closed ideal
I\subseteq R
defines a quotient map from
R
to
S:=R/I
equipped with the quotient topology.
We can enhance this to a quotient map of Huber pairs by defining
S^{+}
to be the integral closure of
R^{+}/(I\cap R^{+})
in
S
.
We often denote this quotient Huber pair by
(R,R^{+})/I
.

Now a straightforward replacement for ring homomorphisms of finite type in the topological setting is the following.

Definition 7.2
.

A homomorphism
R\to S
of Huber rings is
strictly of topologically finite type
if there is a quotient map

R\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle\twoheadrightarrow\hat{S}

of
\hat{R}
-algebras.

However, this class of
R
-algebras results to be too small to cover all important constructions.
Even an (algebraically) finitely generated
R
-algebra might not be a quotient of any Tate algebra
R\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle
.
In order to see this we take a look at rings of definition.

Exercise 7.3
.

ring_of_def_quotient

Let
R
and
S
be Huber rings and

\pi:R\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle\twoheadrightarrow S

a quotient map.
If
R_{0}\subseteq R
is a ring of definition, then
\pi(R_{0}\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle)
is a ring of definition of
S
.

We can now give a simple example of a finite type
R
-algebra that is not strictly of topologically finite type.

Example 7.4
.

We consider the Huber ring
\mathds{Z}_{p}
and the
\mathds{Z}_{p}
-algebra
\mathds{Q}_{p}
.
Since
\mathds{Q}_{p}=\mathds{Z}_{p}[1/p]
, it is finitely generated over
\mathds{Z}_{p}
.
However, the following argument shows that it is not strictly of topologically finite type.
Suppose there is a quotient map

\mathds{Z}_{p}\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle\twoheadrightarrow\mathds{Q}_{p}

The ring
\mathds{Z}_{p}
serves as its own ring of definition.
It thus follows from Exercise
7.3
that
\mathds{Q}_{p}
is its own ring of definition, which is not true.
Therefore,
\mathds{Q}_{p}
cannot be strictly of topologically finite type over
\mathds{Z}_{p}
.

One can also see this more explicitly by checking that for any
a\in\mathds{Q}_{p}\setminus\mathds{Z}_{p}
the homomorphism

\displaystyle\mathds{Z}_{p}[T]

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathds{Q}_{p}

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\longmapsto a

is
not
continuous, where
\mathds{Z}_{p}[T]
is endowed with the
p
-adic topology.
Otherwise we would get a homomorphism

\mathds{Z}_{p}\langle T\rangle\longrightarrow\mathds{Q}_{p}

mapping
T
to
a
.
But
a^{-1}\in\mathds{Z}_{p}
and
a^{-1}T-1
is contained in the kernel (it would in fact generate the kernel).
However, in
\mathds{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket
we have the identity

\frac{1}{a^{-1}T-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a^{-i}T^{i}

and the series is in fact contained in
\mathds{Z}_{p}\langle T\rangle
as
|a|>1
.
Therefore,
a^{-1}T-1
is invertible and generates the unit ideal.
We would get a factorization

\mathds{Z}_{p}\langle T\rangle\longrightarrow\mathds{Z}_{p}\langle T\rangle/(a^{-1}T-1)=0\longrightarrow\mathds{Q}_{p},

a contradiction.

The example suggests that in addition to quotients of Tate algebras one should include quotients of more general
R
-algebras.
These generalized Tate algebras are constructed as follows.
We take finite subsets
M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}
of
R
such that for any
i
and any
r\in\mathds{N}
the ideal

M_{i}^{r}R:=\langle m_{1}\cdots m_{r}a\mid m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}\in M_{i},~a\in R\rangle

is open (the angle brackets stand for the subgroup generated by the listed elements).
Often we write
M
for the tuple
(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
of finite sets.
We associate with each such tuple
M
of finite sets an
R
-algebra of
weighted convergent power series

\displaystyle R\langle T\rangle_{M}

\displaystyle:=R\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle_{M}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}^{n}}a_{i}T^{i}\in\hat{R}\llbracket T\rrbracket\mid\forall~0\in U\subseteq\hat{R}~\text{open}:~a_{i}\in M^{i}U~\text{for almost all~$i$}\right\}

Here we use the short notation
T
for the collection of the variables
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
and set

T^{i}:=T^{i_{1}}\cdots T^{i_{n}}

for
i\in\mathds{N}^{n}
.
Likewise, for an open neighborhood
U\subseteq R
of
0
, we write

M^{i}U:=M_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots M_{n}^{i_{n}}U.

With this notation we often replace the condition that
M_{i}^{r}R
be open for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and for all
r\in\mathds{N}
by the equivalent condition that
M^{i}R
be open for all
i\in\mathds{N}^{n}
.
Let us give this property a name:
We call a tuple
M=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})

voluminous
if
M^{i}R
is open for all
i\in\mathds{N}^{n}
.

Example 7.5
.

We can rewrite the Huber rings
k\langle\varpi^{k}T\rangle
from Example

as

k\langle\varpi^{k}T\rangle=k\langle T\rangle_{\{\varpi^{k}\}}.

We need to give

the structure of a Huber ring.
Starting with a pair of definition
(R_{0},I)
for

we specify the ring of definition

R_{0}\langle T\rangle_{M}:=\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}^{n}}a_{i}T^{i}\in R\langle T\rangle\mid a_{i}\in M^{i}\hat{R}_{0}~\forall i\in\mathds{N}\right\}

and take the ideal of definition to be the ideal of

generated by

.
Then for every open subgroup

.

Let

be a homomorphism of Huber rings.

Example 7.7
.

Now luckily

\mathds{Q}_{p}\cong\mathds{Z}_{p}\langle T\rangle_{\{p\}}/(pT-1).

(iv)

\varphi

We refer to

Corollary 7.9

Any homomorphism
\varphi:R\to S

Proof.

We may assume that

Then by Proposition

Now we want to include the rings of integral elements into the picture.

The following two are the most common:

Definition 7.10
.

Let
\varphi:(R,R^{+})\to(S,S^{+})

\varphi

•

•

of

.

Let

•

For a rational open

.

•

(R\langle T\rangle,R^{+}\langle T\rangle)

.

After these simple examples let us take a look at a more sophisticated one that shows the difference between topologically finite type and weak topologically finite type.

Example 7.12
.

closure_unit_disc

Let
(k,k^{\circ})
be an analytic field with pseudouniformizer
\varpi

We consider the adic unit disc

\mathds{D}_{k}=\Spa(k\langle T\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle).

As a subspace of
{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}
(or a disc of bigger radius, for instance
\mathds{D}_{k}(0,\varpi^{-1})

It turns out there is one point in its closure that is not contained in
\mathds{D}_{k}
itself:
Here we return to the constructions we discussed in Example

Let

In particular, this is a Huber ring which is its own ring of definition.
Its

So as a topological space

What makes the difference is the structure sheaf

For

Theorem 8.1

Definition 8.4
.

A morphism

In this setting an important construction is the (rigid analytic) generic fiber.
Classically, one would do this explicitly on affine opens and then check that the construction glues.
In the language of adic spaces the definition is straight forward:

Definition 8.5
.

Let
(k,k^{\circ})
be an analytic field with pseudouniformizer
\varpi\in k^{\circ}
.
Let
{\mathscr{X}}
be a formal scheme of formally finite type over
k^{\circ}
considered as an adic space over
\Spa(k^{\circ},k^{\circ})
.
The generic fiber of
{\mathscr{X}}
is defined as the open subspace

{\mathscr{X}}_{\eta}=\{x\in{\mathscr{X}}||\varpi(x)|\neq 0\}.

If
{\mathscr{X}}
is a (classical) formal scheme we write
{\mathscr{X}}_{\eta}
for
({\mathscr{X}}^{\ad})_{\eta}
.

Note that
\Spa(k^{\circ},k^{\circ})
has two points.
The generic point is open and cut out precisely by the condition
|\varpi|\neq 0
.
Hence we see that
{\mathscr{X}}_{\eta}
is nothing but the preimage in
{\mathscr{X}}
of the generic point of
\Spa(k^{\circ},k^{\circ})
.

If
{\mathscr{X}}
is even of topologically finite type over
k^{\circ}
, the inclusion of the generic fiber is quasi-compact.
In order to see this we may assume
{\mathscr{X}}
is affinoid, i.e.,
{\mathscr{X}}=\Spa(A,A)
for a
\varpi
-adic ring
A
.
Then the ideal of
A
generated by
\varpi
is open, and thus

{\mathscr{X}}_{\eta}=R\left(\frac{\varpi}{\varpi}\right)

is a rational subset.

Example 8.6
.

generic_fiber_closed_disc

We consider the polynomial ring
k^{\circ}[T]
over the valuation ring
k^{\circ}
of a nonarchimedean field
k
with the induced topology from
k
.
This is the
\varpi
-adic topology for some pseudouniformizer
\varpi\in k^{\circ}
.
The completion of

is

.
We want to determine the generic fiber of

{\mathscr{X}}:=\Spa(k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle).

By definition it is the rational subset

R\left(\frac{\varpi}{\varpi}\right)=\{x\in{\mathscr{X}}\mid|\varpi(x)|\neq 0\}.

We obtain the corresponding Huber pair by inverting
\varpi
in the first component and adjoining

in the second component (so nothing happens there):

(k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle[\frac{1}{\varpi}],k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle[\frac{\varpi}{\varpi}])=(k\langle T\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle).

The adic spectrum of this Huber pair is the closed unit disc
\mathds{D}_{k}(0,1)
.

Example 8.7
.

ZpT_generic_fiber

Here one can see that

is not quasi-compact due to the fact that

Starting with a rigid analytic variety
X
over
(k,k^{\circ})
(i.e., an adic space locally of finite type over

{\mathscr{X}}
over
\Spa(k^{\circ},k^{\circ})
with
{\mathscr{X}}_{\eta}=X
.
We call such a space

a

of

.
Formal models are almost never unique (only if

is quasi-finite over

We can construct new formal models from an existing one - let us call it

.
This is a blowup of
{\mathscr{X}}
with respect to a sheaf of ideals
{\mathscr{I}}
such that
\varpi\in{\mathscr{I}}

It turns out that admissible blowups are the only thing that stand in the way of uniqueness of formal models.

Theorem 8.8

(Raynaud)
.

There is an equivalence of categories

\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{qc $\varpi$-torsion free formal $k^{\circ}$-schemes of tft}\\
\text{localized by admissible formal blowups}\end{subarray}\right\}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\big\{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{qcqs rigid}\\
\text{$k$-spaces}\end{subarray}\big\}.

induced by mapping a formal scheme
{\mathscr{X}}
to its generic fiber
{\mathscr{X}}_{\eta}
.

The theorem lies the foundation for the formal model approach to nonarchimedean geometry.
Instead of rigid analytic spaces one can study formal schemes (up to admissible blowup).
This strategy has been adopted by many researchers, see
[

]
,
[
FK06
]
.

Theorem

was originally formulated in the language of formal schemes and Tate’s rigid analytic varieties.
In this setting the construction of the rigid analytic generic fiber is not so straight forward and one could say the generic fiber was missing some points.
In the language of adic spaces we can derive from Theorem

a more explicit version of the equivalence.
For a formal model
{\mathscr{X}}
of a rigid analytic space
X
we construct the
specialization map
as the composition

\mathrm{sp}_{\mathscr{X}}:X={\mathscr{X}}_{\eta}\longrightarrow{\mathscr{X}}^{\ad}\overset{r_{\mathscr{X}}}{\longrightarrow}{\mathscr{X}}.

For an admissible blowup

it is clear from the construction that the specialization maps
\mathrm{sp}_{\mathscr{X}}
and
\mathrm{sp}_{{\mathscr{X}}^{\prime}}
are compatible.
We thus obtain a morphism

where the limit runs over all formal models
{\mathscr{X}}
of
X
.

9.
The fiber product of adic spaces

Let
Y\to X
and
Z\to X
be morphisms of adic spaces.
The
fiber product
of these two morphisms is an adic space
Y\times_{X}Z
fitting into a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y\times_{X}Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}

satisfying the usual universal property:
For every solid arrow diagram below there is a unique dotted arrow making the diagram commute:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y_{X}Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}

For topological issues fiber products do not exist in general.
We need to impose some finiteness assumptions on
Y\to X
and
Z\to X
.
Moreover we have to make sure the sheaf condition for the structure presheaf is satisfied on the fiber product.
As in the case of schemes the construction of the fiber product involves taking tensor products of the corresponding rings.
However, it is a bit more complicated and the tensor product of affinoids need not be affinoid but rather an ascending union of affinoids.

Definition 9.1
.

A Huber ring
A
is
stably sheafy
if for every
A
-algebra
B
of topologically finite type and every ring of integral elements
B^{+}\subseteq B
the Huber pair
(B,B^{+})
is sheafy.
An adic space is
stable
if it is locally the spectrum of stably sheafy Huber pairs.

The classes of sheafy Huber pairs we have treated so far - those listed in Theorem
3.3
- are all stably sheafy because their defining property is preserved when passing to an adic space of topologically finite type over them.
Hence, all our familiar adic spaces are stable.

Theorem 9.2
.

fiber_product_existence

Let
X
be a stable adic space and
f:Y\to X
and
g:Z\to X
morphisms of stable adic spaces satisfying one of the following conditions

(i)

X
,
Y
, and
Z
are perfectoid,

(ii)

f
is locally of weakly finite type and
g
is adic.

(iii)

f
is locally of finite type,

Then the fiber product
Y\times_{X}Z
exists in the category of adic spaces and is a stable adic space.
In case (i)
Y\times_{X}Z
is perfectoid.

Idea of proof.

As for the fiber product of schemes we reduce to the case of affinoid adic spaces:

X=\Spa(A,A^{+}),\qquad Y=\Spa(B,B^{+}),\qquad Z=\Spa(C,C^{+}).

Moreover, we assume that
f
and
g
come from homomorphisms of Huber pairs:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(B,B^{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(C,C^{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(A,A^{+}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{C}}

Case (i) and (ii) are easier because all morphisms are adic:
Perfectoid spaces are analytic and all morphisms of analytic adic spaces are adic (Lemma
6.8
).
In case (ii)
\varphi_{B}:A\to B
is topologically of finite type, hence adic (Proposition
7.14
) and
A\to C
is adic by assumption.

So there are rings of definition
A_{0}\subseteq A
,
B_{0}\subseteq B
, and
C_{0}\subseteq C
with

\varphi_{B}(A_{0})\subseteq B_{0},\qquad\varphi_{C}(A_{0})\subseteq C_{0}

such that for an ideal of definition
I_{A}\subseteq A_{0}
the ideals

\varphi_{B}(I_{A})B_{0}\subseteq B_{0},\qquad\varphi_{C}(I_{A})C_{0}\subseteq C_{0}

are ideals of definition.
We set

D=B\otimes_{A}C.

We define its ring of definition
D_{0}
as the image of
B_{0}\otimes_{A_{0}}C_{0}
in
D
and let
D^{+}
be the integral closure of
B^{+}\otimes_{A^{+}}C^{+}
in
D
.
Then
(D,D^{+})
is a Huber pair whose ideal of definition is generated by the image of
I_{A}
in
D_{0}
.

The additional assumptions are needed to ensure that
(D,D^{+})
is sheafy.
In the first case one shows that
(D,D^{+})
is perfectoid, so it is sheafy.
In the second case we use that
C\to D
is a basechange of
A\to B
, which is topologically of finite type.
Since
Y=\Spa(C,C^{+})
is stable, this ensures that
(D,D^{+})
is sheafy.

It then turns out that

\Spa(D,D^{+})=Y\times_{X}Z.

For details see
[
Hub96
]
, 1.2.2.

Let us now treat case (iii).
We may assume that

(C,C^{+})=(A\langle T\rangle_{M},A\langle T\rangle_{M}^{+})/I

for a voluminous tuple
M=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
of finite subsets of
A
and a closed ideal
I\subseteq A\langle T\rangle_{M}
.
Let
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
be rings of definition with
\varphi_{B}(A_{0})\subseteq B_{0}
.
Now the problem is that
\varphi_{B}(M)
might not be voluminous because
\varphi_{B}
might not be adic.
We can resolve this by adding additional elements.
Let
L
be a set of generators for an ideal of definition of
B
.
For every
i\in\mathds{N}
we define the tuple

M_{L,i}:=(\varphi_{B}(M_{1})\cup L^{i},\ldots,\varphi_{B}(M_{n})\cup L^{i})

of finite subsets of
B
.
Since
L
generates an ideal of definition, the tuples
M_{L,i}
are voluminous for every

.
Hence we can consider the weighted Tate algebras

For every

[

]
, Lemma 3.5 (i)) gives us a canonical morphism

Likewise we have compatible homomorphisms

Let

be the closure of the ideal of

.
Then we get an injective system of

fitting into the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\langle T\rangle_{M_{L,2}}/J_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B\langle T\rangle_{M_{L,1}}/J_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\langle T\rangle_{M}/I}

and likewise for the rings of integral elements.
At the level of adic spectra we obtain a projective system whose transition morphisms turn out to be open immersions.
Hence we can glue them and one checks that

Y\times_{X}Z=\bigcup_{i}\Spa(B\langle T\rangle_{M_{L,i}},B\langle T\rangle_{M_{L,i}}^{+})/J_{i}.

]
, Proposition 1.2.2

Let us compute the fiber product of the closed unit disc

with itself over
k
.
By Theorem
9.2
(ii) this is done by just taking the tensor product:

(k\langle T\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle)\widehat{\otimes}_{(k,k^{\circ})}(k\langle S\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle)=k\langle T,S\rangle,

where the completion is taken for the
\varpi
-adic topology.
Its adic spectrum is a polydisc (of dimension two):

\mathds{D}_{k}(0,1)\times_{\Spa(k,k^{\circ})}\mathds{D}_{k}(0,1)=\Spa(k\langle T,S\rangle,k^{\circ}\langle T,S\rangle).

Example 9.4
.

example_generic_fiber

In Section
8
we have computed the generic fiber of a formal scheme
{\mathscr{X}}
over the valuation ring
k^{\circ}
of a nonarchimedean field
k
.
This can in fact be expressed as the fiber product of the two morphisms

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathscr{X}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Spa(k,k^{\circ})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Spa(k^{\circ},k^{\circ}).}

The horizontal morphism is of finite type.
So by Theorem
9.2
(iii) the fiber product exists.
If
X
is locally of finite type over
k^{\circ}
, it can even be computed in the easy way by Theorem
9.2
(ii) by just taking tensor products.
For instance, this is the case for
{\mathscr{X}}=\Spa(\mathds{Z}_{p}\langle T\rangle)
, where we have seen in Example
8.6
that

{\mathscr{X}}_{\eta}={\mathscr{X}}\times_{\Spa(\mathds{Z}_{p},\mathds{Z}_{p})}\Spa(\mathds{Q}_{p},\mathds{Z}_{p})=\mathds{D}_{\mathds{Q}_{p}}(0,1)

is the closed unit disc over
\mathds{Q}_{p}
.

If
{\mathscr{X}}
is only locally
formally
of finite type over
\mathds{Z}_{p}
, we have to compute the fiber product by taking an ascending union of affinoids.
This is in line with the example of
{\mathscr{X}}=\Spa(\mathds{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket)
that we have computed in Example
6.12
and Example
8.7
:

{\mathscr{X}}_{\eta}={\mathscr{X}}\times_{\Spa(\mathds{Z}_{p},\mathds{Z}_{p})}\Spa(\mathds{Q}_{p},\mathds{Z}_{p})=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}R\left(\frac{T^{n}}{p}\right)=\overset{\circ}{\mathds{D}}_{\mathds{Q}_{p}}(0,1).

We can also compute the special fiber of the formal scheme
{\mathscr{X}}/k^{\circ}
with this formalism.
Remember that the topologically nilpotent elements
k^{\circ\circ}
form the maximal ideal of
k^{\circ}
.
We then denote by

k^{\succ}:=k^{\circ}/k^{\circ\circ}

the corresponding residue field.
The projection
k^{\circ}\twoheadrightarrow k^{\succ}
is a quotient map and defines a closed immersion

\Spa(k^{\succ},k^{\succ})\hookrightarrow\Spa(k^{\circ},k^{\circ}).

The special fiber of
{\mathscr{X}}
is obtained as the fiber product

{\mathscr{X}}_{s}:={\mathscr{X}}\times_{\Spa(k^{\circ},k^{\circ})}\Spa(k^{\succ},k^{\succ}).

Example 9.5
.

Let us compute the special fiber of
{\mathscr{X}}=\Spa(\mathds{Z}_{p}\langle T\rangle)
.
Since all morphisms involved are adic, this is done by taking the tensor product

{\mathscr{X}}_{s}=\Spa(\mathds{Z}_{p}\langle T\rangle\otimes_{\mathds{Z}_{p}}\mathds{F}_{p})=\Spa(\mathds{F}_{p}[T]).

The topology on
\mathds{F}_{p}[T]
is the discrete topology, so
{\mathscr{X}}_{s}
is a discretely ringed adic space.
In fact one can show (see
[
Tem11
]
, § 3.1) that for any field
F
(or in fact any base scheme) there is a fully faithful functor from the category of
F
-schemes to the category of discretely ringed adic spaces over
F
mapping an affine scheme
\Spec A
to
\Spa(A,F)
.
The special fiber
{\mathscr{X}}_{s}
is of this kind for
F=\mathds{F}_{p}
.
So we can view it rather as the scheme
\Spec\mathds{F}_{p}[T]
.

Let us now take a look at
{\mathscr{X}}=\Spa(\mathds{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket)
.
According to the construction of the fiber product we first need to write
\mathds{F}_{p}
as a quotient of some weighted Tate algebra over
\mathds{Z}_{p}

But
\mathds{F}_{p}
is a quotient of
\mathds{Z}_{p}
on the nose without adding any variables.
This is a degenerate situation where we do not have to add any variables to
\mathds{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket
but just need to take the quotient by the ideal generated by
p
.
There are no affinoids to glue but we directly get

{\mathscr{X}}_{s}=\Spa(\mathds{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket/(p))=\Spa(\mathds{F}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket).

10.
Analytification

section_analytification

In complex geometry, starting from a variety
X
over
\mathds{C}
we can equip
X(\mathds{C})
with the topology coming from
\mathds{C}
to obtain a topological space
X^{\mathrm{an}}
.
This is known as the analytification of
X
.

For a variety
X
over
\mathds{C}_{p}
we want to do a similar construction.
Classically one could turn
X(\mathds{C}_{p})
into a rigid analytic variety.
However, from a more modern viewpoint one should treat the analytification as an adic space.
In this setting it looks more like a fiber product.

For an adic space
(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X},\{v_{x}\}_{x\in X})
we denote by

\underline{X}:=(X,{\mathscr{O}}_{X})

the underlying locally ringed space.
In other words, we forget the topology of the structure sheaf and we forget the valuations
v_{x}
.

Definition 10.1
.

Let
Y\to X
be a morphism of schemes and
\underline{Z}\to X
a morphism of locally ringed spaces where
Z
is an adic space.
The
fiber product

Y\times_{X}Z
is an adic space together with a morphism
Y\times_{X}Z\to Z
whose underlying locally ringed space
\underline{Y\times_{X}Z}
fits into a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{Y\times_{X}Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.}

satisfying the following universal property:
For every adic space
S
and morphism
f:S\to Z
and every solid arrow diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{S}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{Y\times_{X}Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{g}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{f}}

there is a unique morphism of adic spaces
g:S\to Y\times_{X}Z
such that the induced morphism
\underline{g}
(the dotted arrow) makes the diagram commutative.

Proposition 10.2
.

analytification

Let
Y\to X
be a morphism of schemes that is locally of finite type,
Z
a stable adic space, and
\underline{Z}\to X
a morphism of locally ringed spaces.
Then the fiber product
Y\times_{X}Z
exists, is a stable adic space, and the projection

Y\times_{X}Z\to Z

is locally of finite type.

Proof.

We may assume
X
and
Y
are affine,

X=\Spec A,\qquad Y=\Spec A[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/I

and
Z
is affinoid,

Z=\Spa(B,B^{+}).

with a stably sheafy Huber pair
(B,B^{+})
.
For simplicity we assume that
B
is a Tate ring with pseudouniformizer
\varpi
.
The general construction is not much more complicated and can be found in
[
Wed19
]
, Proposition and Definition 8.61.
For
k\in\mathds{N}
we consider the Tate rings

B\langle\varpi^{k}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{k}T_{n}\rangle.

Here
\varpi^{k}T_{i}
are to be considered as variables.
We chose to write them in this way to make the glueing process below more intuitive.
In general one has to work with weighted Tate algebras and make the identification

B\langle\varpi^{k}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{k}T_{n}\rangle=B\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle_{(\varpi^{k},\ldots,\varpi^{k})}.

We have associated rings of integral elements

B^{+}\langle\varpi^{k}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{k}T_{n}\rangle.

For
m\geq k
we have natural homomorphisms

B\langle\varpi^{m}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{m}T_{n}\rangle\longrightarrow B\langle\varpi^{k}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{k}T_{n}\rangle

sending
\varpi^{m}T_{i}
to
\varpi^{m-k}(\varpi^{k}T_{i})
and accordingly for rings of integral elements.
On adic spectra this corresponds to the embedding of the ball of radius
|\varpi^{-k}|
into the ball of radius
|\varpi^{-m}|
.
We have compatible embeddings

B[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]\hookrightarrow B\langle\varpi^{k}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{k}T_{n}\rangle

mapping
T_{i}
to
\varpi^{-k}(\varpi^{k}T_{i})
and we view the ideal
I
as a subset of
B\langle\varpi^{k}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{k}T_{n}\rangle
via this embedding.
Then

B_{k}:=B\langle\varpi^{k}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{k}T_{n}\rangle/IB\langle\varpi^{k}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{k}T_{n}\rangle

is a Huber ring topologically of finite type over
B
.
Defining
B_{k}^{+}
to be the integral closure of
B^{+}\langle\varpi^{k}T_{1},\ldots,\varpi^{k}T_{n}\rangle
in
B_{k}
we obtain a Huber pair
(B_{k},B_{k}^{+})
topologically of finite type over
(B,B^{+})
.
For
m\geq k
we obtain compatible open immersions

\Spa(B_{k},B_{k}^{+})\longrightarrow\Spa(B_{m},B_{m}^{+})

that we can glue together.
The resulting adic space turns out to be the fiber product:

Y\times_{X}Z=\bigcup_{k}\Spa(B_{k},B_{k}^{+}).

In order to see this we need to check that for every map of sheafy Huber pairs
\varphi:(B,B^{+})\to(C,C^{+})
and every commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/I}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

there is
k\in\mathds{N}
and a homomorphism
\psi:(B_{k},B_{k}^{+})\to(C,C^{+})
making the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{k}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}]/I}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

commutative.
Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
be the images of
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
in
C
.
For big enough
k
the elements
\varpi^{k}c_{i}
are all contained in
C^{+}
as
C^{+}
is open and
\varpi
is topologically nilpotent.
Thus we obtain a well defined homomorphism

\psi:(B_{k},B_{k}^{+})\to(C,C^{+})

mapping
\varpi^{k}T_{i}
to
\varpi^{k}c_{i}
and it is clear from the construction that the corresponding diagram commutes.
∎

Definition 10.3
.

Let
(k,k^{+})
be an affinoid field.
The analytification of a variety
X/k
is defined as

X^{\mathrm{an}}:=X\times_{\Spec k}\Spa(k,k^{+}).

Let
(k,k^{\circ})
be a nonarchimedean field.
We now have two ways of constructing an adic space over
k
out of a scheme
X
of finite type over
k
.
The mechanism discussed in this section is the analytification resulting in
X^{\mathrm{an}}
.
But we could also choose a model of
X
over
k^{\circ}
, complete it with respect to a pseudouniformizer to obtain a formal scheme
{\mathscr{X}}
, and then take the generic fiber.
However, these two constructions do not lead to the same result as the following example shows.

Example 10.4
.

Let us consider the affine line
{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}
(as a scheme) over the nonarchimedean field
(k,k^{\circ})
.
We first compute its analytification.
According to the construction in the proof of Proposition
10.2
we just need to glue the affinoids

\Spa(k\langle\varpi^{k}T\rangle)

for
k\in\mathds{N}
.
But we have seen this space in Example
4.3
, it is the adic affine line:

({\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1})^{\ad}={\mathds{A}}_{(k,k^{\circ})}^{1}.

This closes the circle and justifies the nomenclature.

Let us now go the other way via generic fibers of models.
An obvious model of
{\mathds{A}}_{k}^{1}
over
k^{\circ}
is
{\mathds{A}}_{k^{\circ}}^{1}=\Spec k^{\circ}[T]
.
Completing it along the special fiber corresponds to taking the
\varpi
-adic completion of
k^{\circ}[T]
, i.e. the ring of convergent power series
k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle
.
Consequently we have to compute the generic fiber of the formal scheme

\Spa(k^{\circ}\langle T\rangle).

We have done this in Example
8.6
.
The resulting adic space is the closed unit disc
\mathds{D}_{k}(0,1)
.

In analogy to the analytification of complex varieties, nonarchimedean analytification satisfies a GAGA theorem.
It relates coherent sheaves on a proper variety
X
with coherent sheaves on its analytification
X^{\mathrm{an}}
.
In order to treat this GAGA theorem we first have to define coherent sheaves on adic spaces, which will be done in Christian Johansson’s lecture (
[
Joh23
]
) in section 1.1.
The GAGA theorem is also stated there as Theorem 1.2.19.
