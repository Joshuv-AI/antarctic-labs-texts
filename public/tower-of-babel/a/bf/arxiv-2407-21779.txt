On odd powers of nonnegative polynomials that are not sums of squares
Grigoriy Blekherman, Khazhgali Kozhasov, Bruce Reznick

Abstract
. We initiate a systematic study of nonnegative polynomials P P such that P k P^{k} is not a sum of squares for any odd k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 , calling such P P stubborn . We develop a new invariant of a real isolated zero of a nonnegative polynomial in the plane, that we call the SOS-invariant , and relate it to the well-known delta invariant of a plane curve singularity. Using the SOS-invariant we show that any polynomial that spans an extreme ray of the convex cone of nonnegative ternary forms of degree 6 is stubborn. We also show how to use the SOS-invariant to prove stubbornness of ternary forms in higher degree. Furthermore, we prove that in a given degree and number of variables, nonnegative polynomials that are not stubborn form a convex cone, whose interior consists of all strictly positive polynomials.



1.
Introduction

The relationship between nonnegative polynomials and sums of squares is a fundamental problem in real algebraic geometry. Much is now known about constructions and existence of nonnegative polynomials that are not sums of squares (of polynomials)
[
12
,
31
,
35
,
2
,
5
,
6
]
. Decomposing
P^{k}
as a sum of squares, where
k
is an odd integer, also provides a certificate of nonnegativity of
P
, and it is reasonable to ask whether this works for all nonnegative polynomials
P
.
We initiate a systematic study of nonnegative polynomials
P
such that
P^{k}
is not a sum of squares for any odd positive integer
k
. We call such polynomials
stubborn
. Currently not much is known about stubborn polynomials, except for some isolated examples. One of our main results is that all extreme rays of the convex cone of nonnegative ternary sextics (homogeneous polynomials in
3
variables of degree
6
) are stubborn. More generally, for a nonnegative ternary form
P
we develop a new invariant
\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}
of real singularities of
P
, which we call
a sum of squares invariant
or
SOS-invariant
, such that if the sum of
\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}
over all real singularities of
P
is sufficiently large, then
P
is stubborn. This implies the result for ternary sextics, and allows us to construct stubborn forms in higher degrees. We compare the SOS-invariant to the classical delta invariant of a plane curve singularity, and show that they agree for singularities of multiplicity
2
, but are not equal in general. We show that stubborn forms exist, for a fixed degree and number of variables, whenever nonnegative forms are not equal to sums of squares. We also prove that the set of nonnegative forms that are not stubborn is a convex cone, which includes the interior of the cone of nonnegative forms. We now go into more details and review the history of the problem.

For positive integers
n,d
, let
F_{n,d}
denote the space of real forms (homogeneous polynomials) of degree
d
in
n

A form

If

is said to be a

.
Trivially, every sum of squares is nonnegative and the degree

of a nonnegative form must be even.
Following Choi and Lam
[

The interior

Let

Hilbert

The Motzkin form

Another early example of a form in

(1.2)

\displaystyle R\ =

\displaystyle X_{1}^{6}+X_{2}^{6}+X_{3}^{6}+3X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}X_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle-(X_{1}^{4}X_{2}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{4}+X_{1}^{4}X_{3}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}X_{3}^{4}+X_{2}^{4}X_{3}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}X_{3}^{4}),

Hilbert’s 17th Problem asks whether, for

Later, multiple authors studied variations of Hilbert’s 17th problem

18
,
15
,

In particular, for a given strictly positive form

it was desirable to know whether for all

the form

is a sum of squares for some large

Reznick showed
[

that any strictly positive form

for

is a sum of squares (this does not hold for all nonnegative forms
P

[

). More generally, Scheiderer proved

that if
Q\in\textrm{int}(P_{n,d^{\prime}})
and

.
Thus, if

is strictly positive, then

.
In the present work we study this property for non-sum of squares forms
P\in\partial P_{n,d}
in the boundary of the cone of nonnegative forms. Being a square, an even power

of

is a sum of squares for some

.
Otherwise, the form

A nonnegative form

The Motzkin form (

\displaystyle=\ X_{4}^{4}+X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}X_{3}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}X_{3}^{2}-4X_{1}X_{2}X_{3}X_{4}.

Theorem 1
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{E}(P_{3,6})\cap\Delta_{3,6}
be an extremal nonnegative ternary sextic which is not a sum of squares. Then
P
is stubborn, i.e.
P^{2k+1}\in\Delta_{3,6(2k+1)}
is not a sum of squares for
k\geq 0
.

As a direct consequence of this result we have the following.

Corollary 2
.

The forms
M
,
R
and
S
are all stubborn, i.e,
M^{2k+1},R^{2k+1},S^{2k+1}\in\Delta_{3,6(2k+1)}
are not sums of squares for all
k\geq 0
.

Remark 3
.

Theorem
1
does not imply Stengle’s result above, as the ternary sextic
T\in\Delta_{3,6}
is not extremal (see Subsection
3.5
).
Furthermore, by a result of Scheiderer
[
38
]
that we stated above, all sufficiently large powers
P^{2k+1}

of a strictly positive form
P\in\textrm{{int}}(P_{n,d})
are sums of squares.

More generally, we develop a new invariant of a real zero of a nonnegative ternary form, that we call
the SOS-invariant
.
It can be compared to the classical

of a plane curve singularity (see Subsection

). The main idea is that if the sum of SOS-invariants of

over all its real zeros is too large (specifically, greater than

), then

must be stubborn. This happens, for example, for extremal ternary sextics in
\Delta_{3,6}
.
For higher degrees such forms exist due to results of Brugallé et al. from
[
6
]
. However, Theorem
1
does not admit a direct generalization, as no characterization of extremal forms in

in terms of the number of real zeros is known except for

By regarding a form

with more than

real zeros as a form in
n\geq 4
variables we show that stubborn forms exist in arbitrary number of variables, see Theorem
40
.
In Section
4
we also show that the quaternary quartic
Q\in\Delta_{4,4}
defined in (
1.3
) and
the Horn form

F\in\Delta_{5,4}

(1.5)

\displaystyle F\ =\ \left(\sum_{j=1}^{5}X_{j}^{2}\right)^{2}-4\ \sum_{j=1}^{5}X_{j}^{2}X_{j+1}^{2},

are both stubborn.

Remark 4
.

The Horn form was originally defined as a quadric in

.
It was communicated to Hall by Horn in the early 1960s, as a counterexample to a conjecture of Diananda asserting that a quadratic form that is nonnegative on the nonnegative orthant is a sum of a nonnegative form and a quadratic form with nonnegative coefficients only (see
[
16
, p.25]
and
[

In Section

we initiate a systematic study of the set of non-stubborn forms that admit odd sums of squares powers.
For

(1.6)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n,d}(2k+1)\ =\ \left\{P\in P_{n,d}\,:\,P^{2k+1}\in\Sigma_{n,d(2k+1)}\right\}.

Note that
\Sigma_{n,d}(1)=\Sigma_{n,d}
and,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n,d}(\infty)\ =\ \bigcup_{k\,\geq\,0}\Sigma_{n,d}(2k+1).

Let
\Delta_{n,d}(\infty)=P_{n,d}\setminus\Sigma_{n,d}(\infty)
denote the set of stubborn forms in

.
With these notations, we have that
M,R,S,T\in\Delta_{3,6}(\infty)

and

.
Since both

and

are closed convex cones, it is natural to ask this question for
\Sigma_{n,d}(2k+1)
and for

First observe that if

is a sequence of forms converging to

,
then the sequence of powers

converges to

is a sum of squares and

is a sum of squares. This is a special case of a more general Theorem

Note however that
\Sigma_{n,d}(\infty)
is not closed, when
\Delta_{n,d}(\infty)\neq\emptyset

Indeed, a form

lies in the closure of the open cone

of strictly positive forms, each of which admits an odd power which is a sum of squares by

.
Thus, the Motzkin form (

) can be obtained as the limit

, where

is strictly positive for

.
By Theorem

is an interval of the form

\beta_{2k+1}>0
.
The coefficient of

in

Furthermore, one has

Thus, there are infinitely many

Remark 5
.

The property of being nonnegative or a sum of squares is invariant under the (de)homogenization of a polynomial.
Thus,

is a sum of squares for

if and only if so is

for the dehomogenized polynomial

In particular, stubborn forms exist in

if and only if there are
stubborn
nonnegative

-variate polynomials of degree

.

Based on Theorem

Let

be an extremal nonnegative form which is not a sum of squares. Then
P\in\Delta_{n,d}(\infty)
is stubborn.

2.1.
Order of vanishing

A form

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{{d}}^{i}}{\mathrm{{d}}\varepsilon^{i}}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}F(\mathbf{X}^{*}+\varepsilon\mathbf{V})\ =\ 0\quad\textrm{for all}\quad\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\quad\textrm{and}\quad i=0,1,\dots,m-1.

2.2.
Intersection multiplicity

For two bivariate polynomials
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]
and a point
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(x^{*}_{1},x^{*}_{2})\in\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}

the intersection multiplicity
of
f
and
g
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is defined as the dimension of the quotient of
the local ring

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}=\left\{\frac{p}{q}\,:\,p,q\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}],\,q(\mathbf{x}^{*})\neq 0\right\}
of
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
by the ideal generated by
f
and
g
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle I_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f,g)\ =\ \dim_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}/\langle f,g\rangle\right).

In particular, we have that
I_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f,g)>0
is positive if and only if
f(\mathbf{x}^{*})=g(\mathbf{x}^{*})=0
.
In this case,
I_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f,g)=1
if and only the curves
f=0
and
g=0
intersect transversally at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, that is, the gradient vectors
(\partial_{x_{1}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}),\partial_{x_{2}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}))
and
(\partial_{x_{1}}g(\mathbf{x}^{*}),\partial_{x_{2}}g(\mathbf{x}^{*}))
are linearly independent.
If
f
and
g
share a common factor in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]
that vanishes at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, the intersection multiplicity
I_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f,g)=\infty
is infinite.
For ternary forms (homogenous polynomials in three variables)
F,G\in\mathbb{C}[X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}]
and a point
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

, and

is the representative of
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
in the corresponding affine chart.
The celebrated Bézout theorem asserts that the number of intersection points of two projective plane curves counted with multiplicities is equal to the product of their degrees.

Theorem 9

(Bézout theorem)
.

Let
F,G\in\mathbb{C}[X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}]
be ternary forms that have no common factors of positive degree. Then

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{V}(F)\,\cap\,\mathcal{V}(G)}I_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(F,G)\ =\ \deg(F)\cdot\deg(G).

In particular,
\mathcal{V}(F)\cap\mathcal{V}(G)\subset\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
consists of at most
\deg(F)\cdot\deg(G)
points.

A tangent to
\mathcal{V}(F)\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
at
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathcal{V}(F)

is a projective zero
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{X}^{*}\right)^{\perp}
of the homogeneous part of
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\in\left(\mathbf{X}^{*}\right)^{\perp}\mapsto F(\mathbf{X}^{*}+\mathbf{X}^{\prime})
of lowest degree
m_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(F)
.
In particular, tangents to
\mathcal{V}(F)
at
\mathbf{X}^{*}=[0:0:1]\in\mathcal{V}(F)
are projective zeros
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=[X^{\prime}_{1}:X^{\prime}_{2}]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
of the degree
m_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f):=m_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(F)
part of the dehomogenized polynomial
f(X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2})=F(X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2},1)
.
A curve
\mathcal{V}(F)\subset\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
can have at most
m_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(F)
linearly independent tangents.
The following known result inspired our proof of Theorem

Lemma 10
.

For ternary forms

\displaystyle I_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(F,G)\ \geq\ m_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(F)\cdot m_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(G)

with equality if and only if
\mathcal{V}(F)
and
\mathcal{V}(G)
do not share a tangent at

The blow-up of
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle\pi:S\ \rightarrow\ \mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}},\quad\left((x_{1},x_{2}),[X_{1}^{\prime}:X_{2}^{\prime}]\right)\ \mapsto\ (x_{1},x_{2}).

The blow-up

or

The polynomial

We end this subsection with proving that blow-ups preserve nonnegativity of polynomials.

Let

2.3.
The delta invariant and the SOS-invariant

The (local) delta invariant

\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)
is a classical invariant of a singular point
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
of an algebraic curve
f=0
that can be defined as the dimension

(2.7)

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)\ =\ \dim_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\,\overline{\mathcal{O}_{f,\mathbf{x}^{*}}}\,\big/\,\mathcal{O}_{f,\mathbf{x}^{*}}\right)

of
the integral closure

\overline{\mathcal{O}_{f,\mathbf{x}^{*}}}
of the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{f,\mathbf{x}^{*}}:=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}/(f)
of the curve
f=0
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
. We set
\delta_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(F):=\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)
for a form
F\in\mathbb{C}[X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}]
and
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
, where
f
is a dehomogenization of
F
and
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is the affine representative of
\mathbf{X}^{*}
.

Remark 14
.

The sum
\delta(F)
of
\delta_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(F)
over all singular points
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
of a plane curve
\mathcal{V}(F)
is known as
the (total) delta invariant
.
By the genus-degree formula
[
8
, Section 3.11]
,
\delta(F)
is equal to the defect between the geometric genus of
\mathcal{V}(F)
and its arithmetic genus.

Similarly to Noether’s formula, one can define the local delta invariant as
\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)=1
in case of an ordinary singularity and otherwise as

(2.8)

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)\ =\ \frac{m_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)(m_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)-1)}{2}+\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(f^{\prime}),

where the sum is over all first order infinitely near points
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
of
f=0
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

Example 15
.

For the polynomial
f=x_{1}^{3}+(x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{3}-x_{1})^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{4}-2x_{1}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}^{3}+2x_{1}^{4}-2x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}^{6}
from Example
11
the formula (
2.8
) gives us

(2.9)

\displaystyle\delta_{(0,0)}(f)

\displaystyle=\ 1+\delta_{(0,0)}(f^{\prime})\ =\ 1+1+\delta_{(1,0)}(f^{\prime\prime})\ =\ 1+1+1\ =\ 3,

where
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
define strict transforms of
f=0
and
f^{\prime}=0
at
(0,0)
.

For a real
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2}]
and
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
we also define
the real delta invariant

(2.10)

\displaystyle\delta^{\,\mathbb{R}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)\ =\ \frac{m_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)(m_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)-1)}{2}+\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\,-\,\textrm{real}}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(f^{\prime}),

where the sum is over only real first order infinitely near points of
f=0
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

Finally, the quantity that plays a prominent role in our work is
the SOS-invariant

\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)
of a real polynomial
f
at a real zero
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

Definition 16
.

The SOS-invariant
\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)
of a real polynomial
f
at a real zero
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is defined by
\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)=1
in case of an ordinary singularity and

(2.11)

\displaystyle\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)\ =\ \frac{m_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}^{2}}{4}+\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\,-\,\textrm{{real}}}\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(f^{\prime})

in general, where again the sum is over real first order infinitely near points of
f=0
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

It is easy to see that, for real
f
and
\mathbf{x}^{*}
one has that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)\ \leq\ \delta^{\,\mathbb{R}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)\ \leq\ \delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)

and we set
\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(F)=\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)
,
\delta^{\,\mathbb{R}}_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(F):=\delta^{\,\mathbb{R}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(f)
if
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2}]
is the dehomogenization of a real ternary form
F\in\mathbb{R}[X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}]
and
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
is the affine representative of
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Remark 17
.

The notion of the SOS-invariant
\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{(0,0)}(f)
makes sense, if one, more generally, considers a convergent power series
f\in\mathbb{R}\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
at
(0,0)
.

In Section
3
we show that for any coprime
H_{1},H_{2}\in E(P,\mathbf{X}^{*})
in the local SOS-support of
P\in P_{n,d}
we have that
\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P)\geq I_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(H_{1},H_{2})
. We conjecture that the equality holds if
H_{1},H_{2}\in E(P,\mathbf{X}^{*})
are chosen generically.

3.
Ternary forms

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem

and investigate the question of stubbornness of ternary forms in general.
We start with some properties of the SOS-invariant that we introduced in Subsection

.

3.1.
The SOS-invariant and applications

We show that the SOS-invariant gives a lower bound on the intersection multiplicity of any two elements of the local SOS-support.

Proposition 18
.

Let
P\in P_{3,d}
be a nonnegative ternary form with a real zero
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Then for any
H_{1},H_{2}\in E(P,\mathbf{X}^{*})
we have

I_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(H_{1},H_{2})\geq\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P)
.

Proof.

We perform an induction on the number of blow-ups needed to reach a round zero.
If
\mathbf{X}^{*}=[0:0:1]\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a round zero of
P
, then
p(x_{1},x_{2})=P(x_{1},x_{2},1)=ax_{1}^{2}+2bx_{1}x_{2}+cx_{2}^{2}+\dots
does not have real tangents (as the quadratic form
ax_{1}^{2}+2bx_{1}x_{2}+cx_{2}^{2}
must be positive definite) and we have
\delta_{\mathbf{0}}^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(p)=1
.
Any
H_{1},H_{2}\in E(P,\mathbf{X}^{*})
must vanish at
\mathbf{X}^{*}
(see Lemma
8
) and therefore
I_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(H_{1},H_{2})\geq 1=\delta_{\mathbf{0}}^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(p)
.
In general, we have by Theorem
12
that

(3.1)

\displaystyle I_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(H_{1},H_{2})\ =\ I_{{\bf 0}}(h_{1},h_{2})\ =\ m_{\mathbf{0}}(h_{1})m_{\mathbf{0}}(h_{2})+\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}I_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime}),

where the sum is over all common first order infinitely near points
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
of
h_{1}=0
and
h_{2}=0
at
\mathbf{0}
.
Because
H_{1},H_{2}
are in
E(P,\mathbf{X}^{*})
, we have by Lemma

that
m_{\mathbf{0}}(h_{1}),m_{\mathbf{0}}(h_{2})\geq m_{\mathbf{0}}(p)/2
.
Denote by
T(p,\mathbf{0})
the set of real infinitely near points
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
of
p=0
at
\mathbf{0}
. Thus, disregarding all
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
in (

) that do not belong to

, yields

\displaystyle I_{\mathbf{0}}(h_{1},h_{2})\

\displaystyle\geq\ \left(\frac{m_{\mathbf{0}}(p)}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\,\in\,T(p,\,\mathbf{0})}I_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{4}m_{\mathbf{0}}(p)^{2}+\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\,\in\,T(p,\,\mathbf{0})}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}^{\,\textrm{sos}}(p^{\prime})\ =\ \delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{0}}(p),

where the last bound follows by the induction step, see
[
22
, Thm. 1.43]
.
∎

One of the key properties of the SOS-invariant is that it behaves well under taking powers.

Proposition 19
.

Let
P\in P_{3,d}
be a nonnegative ternary form that has a real zero at
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Then for any

\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P^{k})=k^{2}\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P)

Proof.

It suffices to prove the claim for
\mathbf{X}^{*}=[0:0:1]
.
As usual, denote by
p(x_{1},x_{2})=P(x_{1},x_{2},1)
the dehomogenization of
P
.
The strict transform

of
p
satisfies

.
Performing induction on the number of blow-ups needed to reach a real zero without real tangents, it is enough to prove the claim for the latter case.
But in that case
\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(p^{k})=\frac{1}{4}m_{\mathbf{0}}(p^{k})^{2}=\frac{k^{2}}{4}m_{\mathbf{0}}(p)^{2}
, since the multiplicity is multiplied by

under taking
k
-th powers.
∎

, Thm. 1.1]
implies that
\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\,\mathbf{z}
is a sum of squares in
\mathbb{R}[X]
, that is,
\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{z}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}(\mathbf{z})^{2}\mod\mathcal{I}(X)
for some linear forms
H_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{z}]
. Thus,
p(\mathbf{x})=m_{\Delta/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}Q\,m_{\Delta/2}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}(m_{\Delta/2}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}
is a sum of squares in
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{x}]
.
∎

With the help of the above lemma we can demonstrate that for forms in
\Delta_{3,6}
, the delta invariant (
2.8
) agrees with the SOS-invariant defined in Subsection
2.3
. This is a particular case of the following result.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
be a real zero of
P\in P_{3,d}
with
m_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P)=2
. Then
\delta_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P)=\delta_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(P)
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{X}^{*}=[0:0:1]
and consider the dehomogenized polynomial
p(X_{1},X_{2})=P(x_{1},x_{2},1)
.

If
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
is a real first order infinitely near point of
p=0
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\bf 0
, formula (
2.8
) implies that
\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(p)=1+\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})
(recall that
m_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P)=2
).

Also, by its definition, the invariant
\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(p)
decreases by
1
after a blow-up, that is,
\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(p)=1+\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})
.
Moreover,
p^{\prime}
is of multiplicity at most
2
at
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
.
After a finitely many blow-ups we arrive at an ordinary singularity (round zero) for which we have
\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(p)=1=\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(p)
. The claim follows by induction on the number of blow-ups.
∎

We now show that for extreme sextics in
\mathcal{E}(P_{3,6})\cap\Delta_{3,6}
the half-degree invariant (
2.2
) agrees with the delta-invariant (
2.8
) and hence, by Lemma
22
, also with the SOS-invariant.

Lemma 23
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{E}(P_{3,6})\setminus\Sigma_{3,6}
.
Then
\delta^{\,\textrm{{hd}}}(P)=\delta(P)=\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(P)=10
.

Proof.

Let us again assume that
\mathbf{X}^{*}=[0:0:1]
and
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(0,0)\in\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is an isolated real zero of
p(x_{1},x_{2})=P(x_{1},x_{2},1)
.
By Lemma
21
,
m_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(p)=m_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P)=2
.

Let us observe that if
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
is a real first order infinitely near point of
p=0
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, then
\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(p,\mathbf{x}^{*})\leq 1+\delta^{\textrm{hd}}(p^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
decreases at most by
1
with a blow-up. Here we denote
\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(p,\mathbf{x}^{*}):=\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(P,\mathbf{X}^{*})
.
For the delta invariant we have equality
\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(p)=1+\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})
, see (
2.8
).
Since after finitely many blow-ups we reach a round zero for which it would hold
\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(p)=\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(p,\mathbf{x}^{*})=1
(see, for example,
[
35
, p.
24
]
), we have that
\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(p,\mathbf{x}^{*})\leq\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(p)
.

By
[
3
, Thm. 2]
, the curve
\mathcal{V}(P)\subset\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
defined by
P\in\mathcal{E}(P_{3,6})\setminus\Sigma_{3,6}
is rational and, by
[
3
, Rem. 8]
, it is a limit of forms in
\mathcal{E}(P_{3,6})\setminus\Sigma_{3,6}
that span exposed rays of
P_{3,6}
.
Therefore, by the genus-degree formula (see Remark
14
) we have that
\delta^{\mathbb{R}}(P)=\sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}\delta_{\mathbf{X}}(P)=10
, and
\delta(P)=10
as well.
Also,
\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(P)\geq 10
(cf.
[
35
, Thm. 7.8]
) as we explain in the end of the proof.
Combining these inequalities together, we obtain

(3.2)

\displaystyle 10\ =\ \delta(P)\ =\ \sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}\delta_{\mathbf{X}}(P)\ \geq\sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(p)}\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(P,\mathbf{X})\ =\ \delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(P)\ \geq\ 10

and so
\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(P)=\delta(P)=\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}(P)
, where the second equality follows from Lemmas
21
and
22
.

It remains to justify
\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(P)=\sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}\delta(P,\mathbf{X})\geq 10
. If this is false, then

\textrm{codim}\,\left(\bigcap_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}E(P,\mathbf{X})\right)\ \leq\ \sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}\textrm{codim}\,E(P,\mathbf{X})\ =\ \sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}\delta^{\,\textrm{hd}}(P,\mathbf{X})\ <\ 10,

and in the
10
-dimensional space of ternary cubic forms there must exist
H\in\bigcap_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}E(P,\mathbf{X})
.
This in particular means that for a small enough
\varepsilon>0
, a degree six form
P-\varepsilon H^{2}
is globally nonnegative (that is,
P-\varepsilon H^{2}\in P_{3,6}
) and hence
P=(P-\varepsilon H^{2})+\varepsilon H^{2}
is not extremal.
∎

Remark 24
.

The proof of Lemma
23
yields the formula

\displaystyle\delta^{\,\textrm{{hd}}}(P)\ =\ \sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}\delta^{\,\textrm{{hd}}}(P,\mathbf{X})\ =\ 10

for any

, which was conjectured in
[

, Conj. 7.9]
.

Now we are ready to prove our main Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{E}(P_{3,6})\cap\Delta_{3,6}
. By Lemma
23
we know that
\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(P)=10
.
Let now
k\geq 1
be odd. Then we have by Proposition
19
that

\displaystyle\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(P^{k})\ =\ \sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{X}}(P^{k})\ =\ \sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}k^{2}\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{X}}(P)\ =\ k^{2}\,\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(P)\ =\ 10\,k^{2}.

If
P^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}^{2}
was a sum of squares, then necessarily
r\geq 3
(since otherwise
P^{k}=H_{1}^{2}+H_{2}^{2}=(H_{1}+iH_{2})(H_{1}-iH_{2})
and hence
P\in\Delta_{3,6}
is reducible, which is impossible by Lemma
20
).
Then again by Lemma
20
and
[
13
, Lemma
4.5
]
the degree
3k
forms
H_{1}
and
H:=a_{2}H_{2}+\dots+a_{r}H_{r}
(for some

) are coprime and hence

I(H_{1},H)=(3k)\cdot(3k)=9k^{2}
holds by Theorem
9
. On the other hand, for any real zero
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)
of
P
, Proposition
18
gives
I_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(H_{1},H)\geq\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P^{k})
. Combining everything together, we obtain that

\displaystyle 9k^{2}\ =\ I(H_{1},H)\ \geq\ \sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}I_{\mathbf{X}}(H_{1},H)\ \geq\ \sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)}\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{X}}(P^{k})\ =\ \delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(P^{k})\ =\ 10\,k^{2},

which is an obvious contradiction.

∎

3.3.
Ternary forms of higher degree

We note that existence of stubborn ternary forms in degree
6
implies existence of stubborn ternary forms of higher degree via the following well-known technique (see, for example,
[
12
,
(1.4)
]
).

Proposition 25
.

Suppose that
F\in P_{n,d}
is stubborn. Then
X_{1}^{2m}F\in P_{n,d+2m}
is also stubborn for all nonnegative integers
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

It is clear that
X_{1}^{2m}F
is nonnegative. Suppose that
(X_{1}^{2m}F)^{k}
is a sum of squares for some odd
k
, that is,

X_{1}^{2km}F^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}^{2}

Then it follows that
X_{1}^{km}
divides all
H_{i}
, and therefore
F^{k}
is a sum of squares. This contradicts the assumption that
F
is stubborn.
∎

We can also use the same argument as in Theorem
1
to show that nonnegative ternary forms with sufficiently many zeros are stubborn, which leads to more interesting examples of stubborn ternary forms of higher degree.

Theorem 26
.

Let
P\in P_{3,d}
be a nonnegative ternary form of degree
d
with
\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(P)>d^{2}/4
. Then
P
is stubborn.

Proof.

Assume
P
is not stubborn, that is,
P^{k}=H_{1}^{2}+H_{2}^{2}+\dots
for some odd
k\geq 1
.
Then by Propositions
19
and
18
we have
I_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(H_{1},H_{2})\geq\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P^{k})=k^{2}\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}(P)
for every real zero
\mathbf{X}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of
P
. Summing up over all real zeros of
P
, we obtain

\left(\frac{dk}{2}\right)^{2}\ =\ I(H_{1},H_{2})\ \geq\ k^{2}\delta^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(P)\ >\ \frac{k^{2}d^{2}}{4},

which is a contradiction.
∎

Since a real zero contributes to the SOS-invariant by at least one, we have a corollary.

Corollary 27
.

If
P\in P_{3,d}
has more than
d^{2}/4
isolated zeros in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, then
P
is stubborn.

The next example shows that in general the delta invariant, the SOS-invariant and the half-degree invariant can be different.

3.4.
The earlier proof for the Motzkin form

Below we give the elementary proof (alluded to in
[
39
,
10
]
) that the Motzkin form is stubborn.
Recall that the Newton polytope of a polynomial
p=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}
written in the basis of monomials is a convex polytope
\mathrm{{New}}(p)=\textrm{conv}(\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,:\,p_{\alpha}\neq 0\})
.
The following property of the Newton polytope of a sum of squares form is well-known.

Lemma 30
.

[
30
, Thm. 1]

If
p=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}^{2}
is a sum of squares, then
2\cdot\mathrm{{New}}(h_{i})\subseteq\mathrm{{New}}(p)
.

For the Motzkin form, this codifies the elimination of possible terms in a square. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{{New}}(M)\ =\ \textrm{conv}\left((4,2,0),(2,4,0),(0,0,6)\right)

and, if
M=\sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}^{2}
was a sum of squares,
\mathrm{{New}}(H_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,r
, would be contained in a triangle

\Delta:=\textrm{conv}\left((2,1,0),(1,2,0),(0,0,3)\right)
.
The only integer points in
\Delta
are its vertices and the interior point
(1,1,1)
,
hence the only possible terms in
H_{i}
are
X_{1}^{2}X_{2},X_{1}X_{2}^{2},X_{3}^{3},X_{1}X_{2}X_{3}
.
Another useful fact relates to representations of an even form as a sum of squares, this result appeared in
[
14
]
.

Proposition 31
.

[
14
, Thm.4.1]

Suppose
P\in\Sigma_{n,d}
is an even sum of squares form, then we may write
P=\sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}^{2}
, where
H_{i}=\sum_{\alpha}H_{i\alpha}\mathbf{X}^{\alpha}
and all
\alpha
’s belong to
one congruence class modulo 2 componentwise.

The Motzkin form is even and no two of the monomials
X_{1}^{2}X_{2},X_{1}X_{2}^{2},X_{3}^{3},X_{1}X_{2}X_{3}
(corresponding to
(2,1,0)
,

(1,2,0)
,
(0,0,3)
and
(1,1,1)
respectively) belong to the same congruence class modulo 2. Thus, if
M
was a sum of squares,
it would have to be of the form
M=c_{1}(X_{1}^{2}X_{2})^{2}+c_{2}(X_{1}X_{2}^{2})^{2}+c_{3}(X_{3}^{3})^{2}+c_{4}(X_{1}X_{2}X_{3})^{2}
with
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}\geq 0
, which is false.

The third fact is a special case of a result on the square of products of linear polynomials.

Lemma 32
.

Suppose
h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\in\mathbb{R}[t]
and let
k\geq 1
.
If

(3.4)

\displaystyle(t^{2}-1)^{2k}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}(t)^{2},

then each
h_{i}(t)
is a multiple of
(t^{2}-1)^{k}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
k
.
If
k=1
, then
0=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}(\pm 1)^{2}
, so
h_{i}(t)=\tilde{h}_{i}(t)\,(t^{2}-1)
and after cancelling
(t^{2}-1)^{2}
, we have

1=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tilde{h}_{i}(t)^{2}
, so each
h_{i}
must be a constant.
In the inductive step, we just need to factor out
(t^{2}-1)^{2}
from both sides of (
3.4
) and repeat the same argument.
∎

We now prove the stubbornness of
M
.

Theorem 33
.

The Motzkin form (
1.1
) is stubborn, that is,
M^{k}
is not a sum of squares for all odd
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

Suppose that
M^{k}\in\Sigma_{3,6k}
is a sum of squares and write

(3.5)

M^{k}\ =\ \left(X_{1}^{4}X_{2}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{4}+X_{3}^{6}-3X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}X_{3}^{2}\right)^{k}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}^{2}.

We remark for later use that by taking
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=(0,0,1)
above,

(3.6)

1\ =\ M^{k}(0,0,1)\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}(0,0,1)^{2}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i,(0,0,3k)}^{2},

where
H_{i,(0,0,3k)}:=H_{i}(0,0,1)
is the coefficient of
X_{3}^{3k}
in
H_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,r
.
Evidently,

\displaystyle\mathrm{{New}}\left(M^{k}\right)\ =\ k\cdot\mathrm{{New}}(M)\ =\ \textrm{conv}\left((4k,2k,0),(2k,4k,0),(0,0,6k)\right)

and so by Lemma
30
the monomials in each
H_{i}
must be taken from the triangle

\textrm{conv}\left((2k,k,0),(k,2k,0),(0,0,3k)\right).

When is
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
in this triangle? We have
\alpha_{3}=3k-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\geq 0
and the other two edges give
2\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}
and
2\alpha_{2}\geq\alpha_{1}
. Note that
\alpha_{2}\leq 2k
and
if
\alpha_{2}=2k
, then we must have
\alpha_{1}=k
.
In particular,
\alpha_{1}
must be odd. Further, if
\alpha_{2}=0
, then
\alpha_{1}=0
. If
\alpha_{2}=2
, then
1\leq\alpha_{1}\leq 4
and
if
\alpha_{2}=2k-2
, then
k-1\leq\alpha_{1}\leq k+2
.

By Proposition
31
, we may assume that each
H_{i}
contains only terms
X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}X_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}X_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}
of a particular parity.
In view of the interest in
X_{3}^{3k}
(cf. (
3.6
)),
consider those
H_{i}
’s in which
\alpha_{1}

and
\alpha_{2}
are even and
\alpha_{3}
is odd. By the above remarks, we must have
\alpha_{2}\leq 2k-2
, since
\alpha_{1}
is even, and also the only term for which
\alpha_{2}=0

has
\alpha_{1}=0
as well. So we can write such an
H_{i}
in increasing powers of
X_{2}
as

(3.7)

\displaystyle H_{i}\ =

\displaystyle H_{i,(0,0,3k)}X_{3}^{3k}+\left(H_{i,(2,2,3k-4)}X_{1}^{2}X_{3}^{3k-4}+H_{i,(4,2,3k-6)}X_{1}^{4}X_{3}^{3k-6}\right)X_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\cdots+\left(H_{i,(k-1,2k-2,3)}X_{1}^{k-1}X_{3}^{3}+H_{i,(k+1,2k-2,1)}X_{1}^{k+1}X_{3}\right)X_{2}^{2k-2}.

Now observe that
M(1,t,1)=t^{2}+t^{4}+1-3t^{2}=(t^{2}-1)^{2}
. Therefore, (
3.5
) specializes to

\displaystyle M^{k}(1,t,1)\ =\ (t^{2}-1)^{2k}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i}(1,t,1)^{2}.

and by Lemma
32
we have
H_{i}(1,t,1)=c_{i}(t^{2}-1)^{k}
for some
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
On the other hand, from (
3.7
) we obtain

\displaystyle H_{i}(1,t,1)\ =\ H_{i,(0,0,3k)}+(H_{i,(2,2,3k-4)}+H_{i,(4,2,3k-6)})t^{2}+\cdots+(H_{i,(k-1,2k-2,3)}+H_{i,(k+1,2k-2,1)})t^{2k-2}.

and so
\deg(H_{i}(1,t,1))=\deg(c_{i}(t^{2}-1)^{k})\leq 2k-2
. Thus
c_{i}=0
and, from the constant term,
H_{i,(0,0,3k)}=H_{i}(0,0,1)=0
for all
i=1,\dots,r
. This contradicts (
3.6
).
∎

In the case of even
k
, the monomials
X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}X_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}X_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}
entering
H_{i}
with
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
even include
X_{3}^{3k}
,
X_{1}^{2k}X_{2}^{k}
and
X_{1}^{k}X_{2}^{2k}
, so

H_{i}(1,t,1)
has degree
2k
, which does not prevent
H_{i}(1,t,1)
from being a multiple of
(t^{2}-1)^{k}
.

.

Using similar arguments, one can show stubbornness of the form

from (

).

\displaystyle(X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}+X_{3}^{2})^{2k}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{r}Q^{2}_{i}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}),

then
Q_{i}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=\alpha_{i}(X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}+X_{3}^{2})^{k}
for some
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
k=1
, the claim follows by the above argument.
Otherwise, since
Q_{i}
vanish on
X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}+X_{3}^{2}=0
, it must be divisble by
X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}+X_{3}^{2}
and we can write
Q_{i}=(X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}+X_{3}^{2})\tilde{Q}_{i}
for some degree
2k-2
forms
\tilde{Q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}]
. We factor out
(X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}+X_{3}^{2})^{2}
from (
4.3
) and apply the induction hypothesis to derive the claim.

Applying this result to our alleged sum of squares (
4.2
), we see that each
H_{i}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},0,0)
is a multiple of

(X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}+X_{3}^{2})^{k}
,
and so by looking at
X_{j}^{2k}
in
H_{i}
, we see that
H_{i1}=-H_{i2}=H_{i3}
.
Again, by cycling the variables, we obtain that
H_{i2}=-H_{i3}=H_{i4}
,
H_{i3}=-H_{i4}=H_{i5}
and
H_{i4}=-H_{i5}=H_{i1}
,
so that
H_{i1}=-H_{i1}=0
for all
i=1,\dots,r
. From this we have that the coefficient of
X_{1}^{4k}
in
F^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}H^{2}_{i}
is

\sum_{i=1}^{r}H_{i1}^{2}=0
, which is a contradiction.

∎

In contrast to
Q\in P_{4,4}\setminus\Sigma_{4,4}(\infty)
and forms in
P_{3,d}\setminus\Sigma_{3,d}(\infty)
considered in Section
3
, the Horn form
F\in P_{5,4}\setminus\Sigma_{5,4}(\infty)
has infinitely many real zeros. Indeed, by the proof of Theorem
41
(see (
4.1
)), the Horn form vanishes on the plane curve
\mathcal{V}(X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}+X_{3}^{2},X_{4},X_{5})
as well as on the curves
\mathcal{V}(X_{2}^{2}-X_{3}^{2}+X_{4}^{2},X_{5},X_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{V}(X_{5}^{2}-X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2},X_{3},X_{4})
obtained from the former one by cyclically permuting the variables.

5.
Convexity of
\Sigma_{n,d}(\infty)

Our goal here is to prove Theorem
44
below, which implies that the set
\Sigma_{n,d}(\infty)
of non-stubborn forms is convex.
We begin with a special case.

Theorem 42
.

Suppose
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are nonnegative forms such that
P_{1}^{k}
and
P_{2}
are both sums of squares for some odd
k\geq 1
. Then
(P_{1}+P_{2})^{k}
is also a sum of squares.

Proof.

The function
x^{k}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, is strictly monotone. Then for any real
t_{1},t_{2}
,

\displaystyle F_{k}(t_{1},t_{2})\ :=\ \frac{(t_{1}+t_{2})^{k}-t_{1}^{k}}{(t_{1}+t_{2})-t_{1}}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}{k\choose i+1}\,t_{1}^{k-1-i}t_{2}^{i}

is a quotient of two positive numbers when
t_{2}>0
and two negative numbers when
t_{2}<0
, which implies that
F_{k}\in P_{2,k-1}
considered as a binary form is nonnegative.
Thus,
F_{k}(t_{1},t_{2})=G_{k}^{2}(t_{1},t_{2})+H_{k}^{2}(t_{1},t_{2})
for some forms
G_{k},H_{k}
of degree
(k-1)/2
.
The claim now follows, since

\displaystyle(P_{1}+P_{2})^{k}\

\displaystyle=\ P_{1}^{k}+P_{2}\cdot\frac{(P_{1}+P_{2})^{k}-P_{1}^{k}}{(P_{1}+P_{2})-P_{1}}\ =\ P_{1}^{k}+P_{2}\cdot F_{k}(P_{1},P_{2})

\displaystyle=\ P_{1}^{k}+P_{2}\cdot\left(G_{k}^{2}(P_{1},P_{2})+H_{k}^{2}(P_{1},P_{2})\right).

is a sum of squares, since it is a sum of a sum of squares form and a product of sum of squares forms.
∎

For the more general case, we need to look at other truncated binomials sums.
For integers
n\geq r\geq 0
let

\displaystyle f_{n,r}(t)\ =\ \sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{n}{i}\,t^{i}

denote the truncated binomial polynomial. Extensive numerical computation suggested that if

n>2r
, the polynomial
f_{n,2r}>0
is positive. The third author
posed this as an unproved conjecture on MathOverflow, and the solution was quickly presented by Prof. Iosif Pinelis, which is included with his kind permission.

Theorem 43

(Pinelis,
[
26
]
)
.

For all
n>2r
and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
f_{n,r}(t)>0
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
n
for fixed
r
. As before,
f_{2r+1,2r}(t)=(1+t)^{2r+1}-t^{2r+1}
is positive
because
x^{2r+1}
is strictly increasing.
We use two combinatorial identities:

(5.1)

\displaystyle f_{n,r}^{\prime}(t)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{n}{i}\ it^{i-1}\ =\ n\sum_{i=1}^{r}\binom{n-1}{i-1}\ t^{i-1}\ =\ nf_{n-1,r-1}(t),

(5.2)

\displaystyle f_{n,r}(t)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{n-1}{i}\,t^{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\binom{n-1}{i-1}\,t^{i}\ =\ f_{n-1,r}(t)+tf_{n-1,r-1}(t).

Since
f_{n,r}(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\pm\infty
, it suffices to look at values of
f_{n,r}
at its critical points. Suppose
f_{n,r}^{\prime}(t_{0})=0
. Then
by (
5.1
),
f_{n-1,r-1}(t_{0})=0
and so by (
5.2
),

f_{n,r}(t_{0})\ =\ f_{n-1,r}(t_{0})+t_{0}f_{n-1,r-1}(t_{0})\ =\ f_{n-1,r}(t_{0}),

which is positive by the inductive hypothesis.
∎

Observe that by this theorem there are polynomials
g_{n,2r}(t)
and
h_{n,2r}(t)

such that
f_{n,2r}(t)=(g_{n,2r}(t))^{2}+(h_{n,2r}(t))^{2}
, and upon homogenization that there exist binary forms
G,H

of degree
r
so that

F_{n,2r}(t_{1},t_{2})\ =\ \sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{n}{i}\,t_{1}^{i}t_{2}^{2r-i}\ =\ (G_{n,2r}(t_{1},t_{2}))^{2}+(H_{n,2r}(t_{1},t_{2}))^{2}.

We need this representation in the main result of this section.

Theorem 44
.

Let
P\in\Sigma_{n,d}(k)
and
\tilde{P}\in\Sigma_{n,d}(\tilde{k})
, where numbers
k
and
\tilde{k}
are both odd.
Then
P+\tilde{P}\in\Sigma_{n,d}(k+\tilde{k}-1)
.
In particular,
\Sigma_{n,d}(\infty)
is a convex cone.

Proof.

We expand
(P+\tilde{P})^{k+\tilde{k}-1}
, and using
i^{\prime}=i-k
below, we obtain its representation as a sum of squares:

\displaystyle(P+\tilde{P})^{k+\tilde{k}-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k+\tilde{k}-1}\binom{k+\tilde{k}-1}{i}P^{i}\tilde{P}^{\,k+\tilde{k}-1-i}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k+\tilde{k}-1}{i}P^{i}\tilde{P}^{k+\tilde{k}-1-i}+\sum_{i=k}^{k+\tilde{k}-1}\binom{k+\tilde{k}-1}{i}P^{i}\tilde{P}^{k+\tilde{k}-1-i}

\displaystyle=\ \tilde{P}^{\tilde{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k+\tilde{k}-1}{i}P^{i}\tilde{P}^{k-1-i}+P^{k}\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{\tilde{k}-1}\binom{k+\tilde{k}-1}{i^{\prime}+k}P^{i^{\prime}}\tilde{P}^{\tilde{k}-1-i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\ \tilde{P}^{\tilde{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k+\tilde{k}-1}{i}P^{i}\tilde{P}^{k-1-i}+P^{k}\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{\tilde{k}-1}\binom{k+\tilde{k}-1}{\tilde{k}-1-i^{\prime}}P^{i^{\prime}}\tilde{P}^{\tilde{k}-1-i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\ \tilde{P}^{\tilde{k}}F_{k+\tilde{k}-1,k-1}(P,\tilde{P})+P^{k}F_{k+\tilde{k}-1,\tilde{k}-1}(\tilde{P},P)

\displaystyle=\ \tilde{P}^{\tilde{k}}(G_{k+\tilde{k}-1,k-1}^{2}(P,\tilde{P})+H_{k+\tilde{k}-1,k-1}^{2}(P,\tilde{P}))

\displaystyle\ \ \ \,+P^{k}(G^{2}_{k+\tilde{k}-1,\tilde{k}-1}(\tilde{P},P)+H_{k+\tilde{k}-1,\tilde{k}-1}^{2}(\tilde{P},P)).

The second claim follows immediately.

∎

6.
Further remarks and questions

In this section we make some remarks around our results and state some open questions.

Conjecture
6
asserts that any extremal form in
P_{n,d}
that is not a sum of squares is stubborn.

Also, we do not know whether for a fixed odd
k
, the closed cone
\Sigma_{n,d}(k)
is convex or not.
We expect the answer should not depend on the values of the parameters.

We showed in Subsection
3.5
that Stengle’s form (
1.4
) is not extremal. In fact, by Proposition
38
the face containing
T
in its relative interior is two-dimensional.
One can show that all forms in the relative interior of this face are stubborn. This motivates the following question: “What is the maximal dimension of a face
\mathcal{F}\subset P_{n,d}
whose relative interior consists of stubborn forms?”

Extremal ternary sextics
P\in\mathcal{E}(P_{3,6})\cap\Delta_{3,6}
satisfy
\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}(P)=\delta(P)=10
and are stubborn by Theorem
1
.
We saw in Subsection
3.5
that
\delta^{\,\textrm{sos}}(T)=\delta(T)=9
for Stengle’s form, which is stubborn as well
[
39
]
. Based on this and Proposition
38
we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 45
.

Let
P\in\Delta_{3,6}
be a nonnegative form that is not a sum of squares, such that
\delta(P)=9
. Then
P
is stubborn and lies in the relative interior of a
2
-dimensional face of
P_{3,6}
.

It is interesting to see whether there are stubborn nonnegative forms with a “small” SOS-invariant (equivalently,
\delta
-invariant).
For example, are there
P\in\Delta_{3,6}(\infty)
with
\delta(P)<9
?

The Robinson form (
1.2
) (as well as any

that generates an exposed extreme ray) has
10
round zeros. The Motzkin form (
1.1
) has four round zeros
[\pm 1:\pm 1:1]
and two zeros
[1:0:0]
,
[0:1:0]
at each of which the SOS-invariant is equal to
3
. This motivates the following problem.

Problem 46
.

Classify partitions
(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{s})
of
10=\delta_{1}+\dots+\delta_{s}
that can be realized as the set of local SOS-invariants (equivalently, delta invariants) at real zeros of some
P\in\mathcal{E}(P_{3,6})\cap\Delta_{3,6}
.

For
a\leq 3
, let us now define

M_{a}\ =\ X_{1}^{4}X_{2}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{4}+X_{3}^{6}-aX_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}X_{3}^{2}\ =\ M+(3-a)X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}X_{3}^{2}

for which the

Theorem 47
.

There exists a non-decreasing sequence
(c_{k})\subset[0,3)

Proof.

For each

we obviously have

Suppose

X_{1}^{4}X_{2}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{4}+X_{3}^{6}-aX_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}X_{3}^{2}\ =\ \left(X_{1}^{4}X_{2}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{4}+X_{3}^{6}-c_{k}X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}X_{3}^{2}\right)+cX_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}X_{3}^{2},

is in

∎

From the proof that
