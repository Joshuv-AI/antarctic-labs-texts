Understanding Sinusoidal Neural Networks
Tiago Novello IMPA

Abstract
. In this work, we investigate the structure and representation capacity of sinusoidal MLPs — multilayer perceptron networks that use sine as the activation function. These neural networks (known as neural fields ) have become fundamental in representing common signals in computer graphics, such as images, signed distance functions, and radiance fields. This success can be primarily attributed to two key properties of sinusoidal MLPs: smoothness and compactness . These functions are smooth because they arise from the composition of affine maps with the sine function. This work provides theoretical results to justify the compactness property of sinusoidal MLPs and provides control mechanisms in the definition and training of these networks. We propose to study a sinusoidal MLP by expanding it as a harmonic sum. First, we observe that its first layer can be seen as a harmonic dictionary, which we call the input ( ( sinusoidal ) ) neurons . Then, a hidden layer combines this dictionary using an affine map and modulates the outputs using the sine, this results in a special dictionary of sinusoidal neurons . We prove that each of these sinusoidal neurons expands as a harmonic sum producing a large number of new frequencies expressed as integer linear combinations of the input frequencies. Thus, each hidden neuron produces the same frequencies, and the corresponding amplitudes are completely determined by the hidden affine map. We also provide an upper bound and a way of sorting these amplitudes that can control the resulting approximation, allowing us to truncate the corresponding series. Finally, we present applications for training and initialization of sinusoidal MLPs. Additionally, we show that if the input neurons are periodic, then the entire network will be periodic with the same period. We relate these periodic networks with the Fourier series representation.

In this work, we prove that each sinusoidal neuron

In other words, this novel

We explore this sinusoidal expansion in the following applications.

•

We prove that the sinusoidal neurons

However, each

•

We provide an upper bound and a way of sorting the amplitudes in the expansion of a sinusoidal neuron (Secs

•

We explore this Fourier-like interpretation of sinusoidal MLPs to analyze their behavior during training and provide adequate initialization schemes (Secs

•

Finally, we show that our trigonometric formula implies that if the input neurons are periodic, then the entire neural network will be periodic with the same period (Sec

2.
Sinusoidal Neurons

In this section, we show that each sinusoidal neuron expands as a sum of harmonics with their
frequencies
completely determined by the input frequencies
\omega
and
amplitudes
by the coefficients of the hidden matrix
a
.

Precisely, we define a
sinusoidal neuron
as a function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
expressed as

h(x)=\sin\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\sin(\omega_{i}x+\varphi_{i})+b\right)

where
a
,
\omega
,
\varphi
,
b
are the amplitudes, frequencies, phases, and bias, respectively.
Note that, following the common machine learning neuron notation,
h
receives a list of
n
input neurons
\textbf{s}_{i}(x)=\sin(\omega_{i}x+\varphi_{i})
that are combined with the weights
a_{i}
and activated by
\sin
. The number
n
is the
width
of the sinusoidal neuron
h
.

Before presenting the expansion of a sinusoidal neuron with width
n
, let us recall the (Fourier) expansion of a neuron with width
1
and no bias
[
1
, Page 361]
:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sin\big(a\sin(x)\big)\!=\!\!\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ odd}}\!\!\!J_{k}(a)\sin(kx),\,\,\text{with}\,\,J_{k}(a)\!=\!\!\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\!\!\cos\big(kt-x\sin(t)\big)dt.

The functions
J_{k}
are the well-known
Bessel functions
of the first kind.
To provide an expansion of the sinusoidal neuron
h
we must generalize the formula in Equation
2.1
.
For this, we prove the following result using an inductive argument on the width
n
of the sinusoidal neuron
h
.

Theorem 1
.

A sinusoidal neuron
h
with width
n
expands as a harmonic sum

(2.2)

\displaystyle h(x)=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega x+\varphi\right\rangle+b\Big)\quad\text{with}\quad\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}J_{k_{i}}(a_{i}).

Before presenting the proof of Theorem
1
let’s provide some additional details.
Observe that the
frequencies
and
phase-shifts
in the expansion given in Equation
2.2
are integer linear combinations of the input frequencies
\omega_{i}
:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\beta_{\textbf{k}}(\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\omega_{i},\quad\lambda_{\textbf{k}}(\varphi,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\varphi_{i}+b.

As a consequence, we note that the activation of the sinusoidal neuron sum
\textbf{s}_{i}
by the sine function is
producing
a lot of frequencies
\beta_{\textbf{k}}(\omega)
in terms of the input frequencies
\omega
.
More precisely, truncating the expansion, that is, summing over
\left|\textbf{k}\right|_{\infty}\leq B\in\mathbb{N}
, implies that the neuron
h
can learn
\frac{(2B+1)^{n}-1}{2}
non-null frequencies.
To compute this number, we remove the case
\textbf{k}=0
and count
\{\textbf{k},-\textbf{k}\}
as a single case.
Also, note that the
0^{th}
harmonic is present in the expansion of
h
because
\beta_{\textbf{0}}(\omega)=0
, and it corresponds to
\prod_{i=1}^{n}J_{0}(a_{i})\sin(b)
.
This makes the last bias in a sinusoidal neural network probably not necessary.

A natural issue regarding such frequency factoring is the existence of a
B
such that
\left|\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)\right|
is small for
\left|\textbf{k}\right|_{\infty}\leq B
.
In Section
2.1
, we provide an upper bound for
\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)
that implies a rapid decrease of the
k
-amplitudes as
k
increases.

This frequency factoring explains why composing sinusoidal layers
may
compact data information. Specifically, from the above discussion, we deduced that the sinusoidal neuron
h
receiving
n
frequencies, parameterized by
3n+1
coefficients, can be used to approximate a harmonic sum with
\frac{(2B+1)^{n}-1}{2}
terms.

Another important observation is that the weights
a
fully determine the amplitude
\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{a})
of each harmonic term
\sin\big(\beta_{\textbf{k}}(\omega)x+\lambda_{\textbf{k}}(\varphi,b)\big)
in the expansion. Consequently, the input matrix
\omega
determines the frequencies that can be represented by
h
, while the hidden matrix
a
determines which frequencies will be used.

Back to the proof of Theorem
1
. It consists of proving the formula in Equation
2.2
as well as a similar one using the cosine as the activation function:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\cos\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\sin(\omega_{i}x+\varphi_{i})+b\right)=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega x+\varphi\right\rangle+b\Big).

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The proof is by induction in
n
. For the base case
n=1
, we prove
\sin\left(a\sin(y)+b\right)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}J_{k}(a)\sin(ky+b)
with
y=\omega x+\varphi
.
For this, we use the expansion in Equation
2.1
and its cosine analogous expansion
\cos\big(a\sin(y)\big)\!=\!\!\sum J_{l}(a)\sin(ly)
, here the sum is over the even numbers. Thus, applying the trigonometric identity
\sin(a+b)=\sin(a)\cos(b)+\cos(a)\sin(b)
we obtain:

\displaystyle\sin\big(a\sin(y)+b\big)

\displaystyle=\sin\big(a\sin(y)\big)\cos(b)+\cos\big(a\sin(y)\big)\sin(b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ odd}}\!\!\!J_{k}(a)\sin(ky)\cos(b)+\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ even}}\!\!\!J_{l}(a)\cos(ly)\sin(b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ odd}}\!\!\!J_{k}(a)\sin(ky+b)+\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ even}}\!\!\!J_{l}(a)\sin(ly+b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}J_{k}(a)\sin(ky+b).

In the third equality we combined the formula
\sin(a)\cos(b)=\frac{\sin(a+b)+\sin(a-b)}{2}
and the fact that
J_{-k}(a)=(-1)^{k}J_{k}(a)
to rewrite the summations.
The proof of the formula using the cosine as activation function is similar.

Assume that the formulas hold for
m-1
, with
m>1
, we prove that Equation
2.2
holds for
m
(the induction step). Again, we denote
y_{i}=\omega_{i}x+\varphi_{i}
for simplicity.

\displaystyle\sin\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\sin(y_{i})+b\right)

\displaystyle=\sin\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}a_{i}\sin(y_{i})+b\right)\cos\big(a_{m}\sin(y_{m})\big)

\displaystyle+\cos\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}a_{i}\sin(y_{i})+b\right)\sin\big(a_{m}\sin(y_{m})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1},\,l\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ even}}\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)J_{l}(a_{m})\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},y\right\rangle+b\Big)\cos(ly_{m})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1},\,l\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ odd}}\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)J_{l}(a_{m})\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},y\right\rangle+b\Big)\sin(ly_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},y\right\rangle+b\Big)

We use the induction hypothesis in the second equality and an argument similar to the one used in the base case to rewrite the harmonic sum. Again, the cosine activation function case is analogous.
∎

Yüce et al. 2022
presented a similar formula for MLPs activated by polynomial functions.
While it is evident that the sine function can be approximated by a polynomial using Taylor series, our formula requires no approximations.
In addition to providing a simple proof, we also derive the analytical expressions for the amplitudes. These expressions enable us to compute upper bounds for the new frequencies (Theorem
2
).
Furthermore, we apply our formula to show that sinusoidal MLPs are a very close representation of the Fourier series. Finally, we use it to prove a periodicity theorem concerning sinusoidal MLPs (Theorem
5
).

We notice that the expansion of the sinusoidal neuron
h
in Equation
2.2
resembles the
amplitude-phase form
of the Fourier series.
On the other hand, the
sine-cosine
and
exponential forms
follow directly as corollaries of Theorem
1
.

Corollary 1
.

A sinusoidal neuron
h(x)=\sin\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\sin(\omega_{i}x+\varphi_{i})+b\Big)
expands as sum of sines and cosines:

(2.5)

\displaystyle h(x)=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}A_{\textbf{k}}\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+B_{\textbf{k}}\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)

with
A_{\textbf{k}}=\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\varphi\right\rangle+b\Big)
and
B_{\textbf{k}}=\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\varphi\right\rangle+b\Big)
.

We can also rewrite this sine-cosine sum as a sum of complex exponentials:

(2.6)

\displaystyle h(x)=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}c_{\textbf{k}}\text{e}^{i\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x},\text{ with }c_{\textbf{k}}=i\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)\text{e}^{i\left\langle\textbf{k},\varphi\right\rangle}\left(\frac{\text{e}^{ib}-(-1)^{\sum k_{i}}\text{e}^{-ib}}{2}\right).

For simplicity, we have used the notation
A_{\textbf{k}}
instead of
A_{\textbf{k}}(a,b,\varphi)
; the same applies to
B_{\textbf{k}}
and
c_{\textbf{k}}
.
Observe that the sine-cosine and exponential forms only resemble the Fourier expansion of the neuron
h
, as the frequencies
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
could not be integer multiples of
2\pi
. However, we can initialize the coordinates of
\omega
as integer multiples of
2\pi
, which could be used to rearrange the above sum as the Fourier series of
h
.
In Section
4.3
, we provide further details about such initialization.

2.1.
Upper bound for the amplitudes

Here we show that the expansion of the sinusoidal neuron
h
can be truncated by a (small) integer
B>0
:

h(x)\approx\sum_{\left|\textbf{k}\right|_{\infty}\leq B}\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega x+\varphi\right\rangle+b\Big).

Where
\left|\textbf{k}\right|_{\infty}=\max\{|k_{i}|\}
.
To this end, we study how the amplitudes
\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)
behave as
B
grows.
This is presented in the following result which gives an upper bound for the amplitudes
\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)
in terms of
k
and
{a}
.

Theorem 2
.

The amplitude
\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)
associated with the frequency
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
in the harmonic expansion of a sinusoidal neuron is bounded as follows:

(2.7)

\displaystyle|\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)|<\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left(\frac{|a_{i}|}{2}\right)^{\left|k_{i}\right|}}{|k_{i}|!}.

Proof.

Theorem
1
says that

\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}J_{k_{i}}(a_{i}).

To estimate an upper bound for this number, we use the following inequality
[
10
]
, which gives an upper bound for the Bessel functions
J_{i}(a)
.

(2.8)

\displaystyle|J_{i}(a)|<\frac{\left(\frac{|a|}{2}\right)^{i}}{i!}\,,\quad i>0,\quad a>0.

Observe that this inequality also holds for
a<0
since
|J_{i}(-a)|=|J_{i}(a)|
.
Therefore, replacing Inequality
2.8
in
\prod_{i=1}^{n}J_{k_{i}}(a_{i})
and using
|J_{-i}(a)|=|J_{i}(a)|
results in the desired inequality
2.7
.
∎

In particular, assuming
|a_{i}|\leq 1
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, the upper bound provided by Theorem
2
implies that the amplitude
\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)
associated with the harmonics
\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega x+\varphi\right\rangle+b\Big)
is bounded as follows:

(2.9)

\displaystyle\text{If}\quad\left|a\right|_{\infty}\leq 1\quad\text{then}\quad|\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)|<\frac{1}{|k_{1}|!\cdots|k_{n}|!\cdot 2^{|k_{1}|+\cdots+|k_{n}|}}.

This means that the frequencies
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
produced by the sinusoidal neuron
h
are bounded by a number that decreases rapidly as
\left|\textbf{k}\right|_{\infty}
increases.

In practice, the most successful initialization for the hidden weights
a
considers values within the interval
\left(-\sqrt{\frac{6}{n}},\sqrt{\frac{6}{n}}\right)
, as suggested in
[
15
]
. Therefore, the upper bound provided in Inequality
2.9
is applicable.

2.2.
Sorting the amplitudes

In this section, we present a kind of sorting behavior of the amplitudes
\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)
of the frequencies
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
in terms of the integer vectors
\textbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})
.
However, before approaching the general case, let us consider
n=1
.
Specifically, we consider the case presented in
Equation
2.1
:

\sin\big(a\sin(\omega x)\big)\!=\!\!\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ odd}}\!\!\!J_{k}(a)\sin(k\omega x)

Here, we are assuming
a\in\mathbb{R}
. We note that the absolute values of the Bessel functions
\left|J_{k}(a)\right|
exhibit a sorting pattern when
a
is within the interval
\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
:

(2.10)

\displaystyle\text{If}\quad|a|<\frac{\pi}{2}\text{ and }a\neq 0\quad\text{then}\quad|J_{1}(a)|>|J_{2}(a)|>|J_{3}(a)|>\cdots

This behavior is shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Bessel functions.

We also observe that
|J_{0}(a)|>|J_{1}(a)|
for values of
a
within an interval around
[0,1.4]
. However, we begin by proving the case outlined in Equation
2.10
. We note that it is sufficient to show that
J_{k}
is positive when
0<a\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
and that
\frac{J_{k+1}(a)}{J_{k}(a)}<1
for
k>0
.
This is because for
-\frac{\pi}{2}<a\leq 0
, we have
|J_{k}(-a)|=|J_{k}(a)|
.

The above facts are consequences of the following two inequalities presented in
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{J_{k+1}(a)}{J_{k}(a)}

\displaystyle>\frac{a}{2k+2}>0,\quad k\geq 0

\displaystyle\frac{J_{k+1}(a)}{J_{k}(a)}

\displaystyle<\frac{a}{2k+1}<1,\quad k>0.

Equation
2.10
implies that when
|a|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
and
a\neq 0
, the amplitudes
J_{k}(a)
of the expansion of
\sin\big(a\sin(x)\big)
(Equation
2.1
) are sorted in decreasing order by the index
k
.
Additionally, since
|J_{-k}(a)|=|J_{k}(a)|
we obtain a stronger result:

(2.11)

\displaystyle\text{Let }k,l\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ such that }|k|<|l|\text{ then }|J_{k}(a)|>|J_{l}(a)|.

To include the case
k=0
we must reduce the interval containing
a
. In Figure
1
we can see that the intersection between the graphs of
J_{0}
and
J_{1}
happens before
\frac{\pi}{2}
.
We can extend the result of Equation
2.11
to include the case
k=0
if we consider
|a|\leq 1
.
That is,
J_{0}(a)>J_{1}(a)
for
|a|<1
.
This is a consequence of the following recurrence relation given in
[
1
, Page 361]
:

\frac{2k}{a}J_{k}(a)=J_{k-1}(a)+J_{k+1}(a).

As shown in Figure
1
, the inequality can be extended to values near
|a|\!<\!1.4
.
However, determining the exact location involves solving
J_{0}(x)-J_{1}(x)=0
, for which, to our knowledge, there is no closed-form solution.

We now return to the general case
n\geq 1
. For this, we present a generalization of the result in Equation
2.11
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
|a_{i}|\leq 1
and
a_{i}\neq 0
. Then, for every pair of distinct integer vectors
\textbf{k},\textbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfying
|k_{i}|\leq|l_{i}|
, we have
|\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)|>|\alpha_{\textbf{l}}(a)|
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1
,
\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}J_{k_{i}}(a_{i})
. Since
|a_{i}|\!\leq\!1
,
a_{i}\!\neq\!0
, and
|k_{i}|\!\leq\!~|l_{i}|
, Equation
2.11
implies
|J_{k_{i}}(a_{i})|\geq|J_{l_{i}}(a_{i})|
.
Using this, we obtain the inequality:

(2.12)

\displaystyle|\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)|=\prod_{i=1}^{n}|J_{k_{i}}(a_{i})|>\prod_{i=1}^{n}|J_{l_{i}}(a_{i})|=|\alpha_{\textbf{l}}(a)|.

The strict inequality is due to the fact that there is at least one
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
|k_{j}|<|l_{j}|
, which is a consequence of
\textbf{k}\neq\textbf{l}
and
|k_{i}|\leq|l_{i}|
.
∎

In practice, we can apply Theorem
3
when
|a_{i}|\leq 1.4
, which can be estimated numerically. Therefore, by keeping the hidden weights
a
of the sinusoidal neuron
h
bounded by
1.4
, we can effectively sort the amplitudes
\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)
associated with the (new) frequencies
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
based on the integer vectors
k
.

Moreover, Theorem
2
says that increasing
|\textbf{k}|_{\infty}
results in
|J_{\textbf{k}}(a)|
going to zero.
Theorem
3
states that these amplitudes are also arranged in decreasing order. Thus, we can truncate the harmonic expansion of the neuron
h
by summing up to index vectors
k
satisfying
|\textbf{k}|_{\infty}<B
, where
B
serves as an upper bound. In this case, the least important frequencies
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
, for which
\left|\textbf{k}\right|_{\infty}>B
, are discarded, and their corresponding amplitudes
|J_{\textbf{k}}(a)|
are small.

3.
Understanding sinusoidal networks with one hidden layer

In this section, we extend Theorem
1
to a sinusoidal network with one hidden layer
f(x)=\textbf{l}\circ\textbf{h}\circ\textbf{s}(x)+d
; as defined in
Equation
1.1
.

In Section
1
, we observed that the hidden sinusoidal layer
h
receives a list of
n
harmonics
\textbf{s}_{j}(x)=\sin(\omega_{j}x+\varphi_{j})
and outputs a list of
n
sinusoidal neurons
\textbf{h}_{i}(x)=\sin\left(\sum_{j}a_{ij}\textbf{s}_{j}(x)+b_{i}\right)
, see Equation
1
. Then, the linear layer
l
combines these neurons resulting in the following expression:

(3.1)

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\sin\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\sin(\omega_{i}x+\varphi_{i})+b_{j}\right)+d

where
\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, with
a_{i},b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
d\in\mathbb{R}
.

Observe that applying Corollary
1
to each neuron
\textbf{h}_{i}
we obtain the following sine-cosine expansion:

\textbf{h}_{i}(x)=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}A_{\textbf{k}}\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+B_{\textbf{k}}\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big).

We are going to use this formula to expand the whole network
f
in a similar manner.
We choose this expansion form because only the amplitudes
A_{\textbf{k}}
and
B_{\textbf{k}}
depend on the hidden weights
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
.
This allows us to rewrite (in the linear layer) the amplitudes associated with each frequency
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
as a linear combination of the corresponding amplitudes of each neuron.

Theorem 4
.

A sinusoidal network
f(x)=\textbf{l}\circ\textbf{h}\circ\textbf{s}(x)+d
with one hidden layer and width
n
expands as sum of sines and cosines:

(3.2)

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\left\langle c,A_{\textbf{k}}\right\rangle\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+\left\langle c,B_{\textbf{k}}\right\rangle\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+d,

with
A_{\textbf{k}}\!=\!\big(A_{\textbf{k}}(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,A_{\textbf{k}}(a_{n},b_{n})\big)
and
B_{\textbf{k}}\!=\!\big(B_{\textbf{k}}(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,B_{\textbf{k}}(a_{n},b_{n})\big)
.

Proof.

The proof consists of applying Corollary
1
to each sinusoidal neuron
\textbf{h}_{i}
of
f
, which are the outputs of the hidden layer
h
.

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\underbrace{\sin\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\sin(\omega_{j}x+\varphi_{j})+b_{i}\right)}_{\textbf{h}_{i}}+d

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\left(\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}A_{\textbf{k}}(a_{i},b_{i})\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+B_{\textbf{k}}(a_{i},b_{i})\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)\right)+d

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}A_{\textbf{k}}(a_{i},b_{i})\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}B_{\textbf{k}}(a_{i},b_{i})\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+d

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\left\langle c,A_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{a},b)\right\rangle\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+\left\langle c,B_{\textbf{k}}(a,b)\right\rangle\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+d.

In the fourth equality we are defining
A_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{a},{b})
as vector
\big(A_{\textbf{k}}(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,A_{\textbf{k}}(a_{n},b_{n})\big)
; the same for
B_{\textbf{k}}
.
∎

A key observation in the proof of Theorem
4
is that the harmonics given by the expansion of each sinusoidal neuron
\textbf{h}_{i}(x)=\sin\left(\sum_{j}a_{ij}\textbf{s}_{i}(x)+b_{i}\right)
are the same.
In other words, the expansion of each neuron
\textbf{h}_{i}
is not producing any new frequency. Thus, if a given frequency is not represented in the expansion of
f
(Equation
3.2
), increasing the number of hidden neurons will not solve this problem.
This is a consequence of Theorem
1
which states that those harmonics depend only on the weights of the first layer.

Clearly, we can derive an exponential expansion form for the network
f
:

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\left\langle c,c_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{a},b)\right\rangle\text{e}^{i\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega x+\varphi\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\underbrace{\left\langle c,c_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{a},b)\right\rangle\text{e}^{i\left\langle\textbf{k},\varphi\right\rangle}}_{c_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{a},b,c,\varphi)}\text{e}^{i\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}{c_{\textbf{k}}(\textbf{a},b,c,\varphi)}\text{e}^{i\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x}.

Observe that the weights
\textbf{a},b,c,\varphi
determine the amplitudes of each frequency
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
in the expansion.

4.
Applications

4.1.
Training sinusoidal networks

Let
f
be a sinusoidal network
f
with a single hidden layer, to train
f
it is common to define a loss function and use the
gradient descent algorithm
to approximate a minimum.
Parascandolo et al. 2016

reported that we have to be careful in the parameter initialization of
f
because the training can lead to an undesired local minimum.

Sitzmann et al. 2020
overcomes this by providing an initialization scheme that preserves the distributions of activations through the network layers.
It consists of choosing the weights of each hidden layer in
\left(-\sqrt{\frac{6}{n}},\sqrt{\frac{6}{n}}\right)
, where
n
is the width of
f
.
On the other hand, Theorem
4
states that
f
can be expressed as

f(x)=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\left\langle c,A_{\textbf{k}}\right\rangle\cos\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+\left\langle c,B_{\textbf{k}}\right\rangle\sin\Big(\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle x\Big)+d.

Then, using
Cauchy–Schwarz
inequality, Theorem
2
, and
|a_{ij}|,|c_{j}|\!\leq\!\sqrt{\frac{6}{n}}
, results in:

(4.1)

\displaystyle|\left\langle c,A_{\textbf{k}}\right\rangle|,|\left\langle c,B_{\textbf{k}}\right\rangle|<\sqrt{6n}\frac{\left(\frac{3}{2n}\right)^{\frac{|k_{1}|+\dots+|k_{n}|}{2}}}{|k_{1}|!\dots|k_{n}|!}.

Equation
4.1
gives an upper bound for the amplitude associated with the frequency
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
. Thus, if
n>1
and
\left|\textbf{k}\right|
is large, the inequality implies that the amplitude is small.
For an example, consider the case
n=32
, that is,
f
has
32
neurons at each layer. We use Equation
4.1
to compute the upper bounds for the amplitudes associated with the frequencies
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
in the expansion of
f
.

Figure 2.

\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
in the case
n=32
. We consider
\textbf{k}=(i,0,\ldots,0)
for
i=0,\ldots,7
which coincides with the
x
-axis in the graph. In the case
i=7
we obtained an upper-bound of
\approx 2e-06

Figure
2
shows the upper bounds for the amplitudes associated with the frequencies
\left\langle\textbf{k},\omega\right\rangle
with
\textbf{k}=(i,0,\ldots,0)
for
i=0,\ldots,7
.
Note that they decrease rapidly as we increase
i
.
Furthermore, it is evident that increasing the number of null entries in
k
leads to even lower upper bounds.
Moreover, when we increase
n
, the amplitudes decrease even further due to the term
\frac{3}{2n}
in Inequality
4.1
.
This suggests that during training, the sinusoidal network will prioritize the lower frequencies.
Consequently, this provides a justification for the
spectral bias phenomenon

[

]
.

First, we need to define its
width

n
, then, we have to initialize the coefficients to start the training using some variant of the
gradient descent algorithm
. In the examples below we employed the ADAM

[

]
.

4.2.1.
Network width

In this section, we give a lower bound
M
for the network width

gives us an upper bound.

Using Theorem

4.2.2.
Frequency initialization

To find the input frequencies
\omega_{i}
we use Equation
2.3
which provides the frequencies
\beta_{\textbf{k}}(\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\omega_{i}
with
\left|\textbf{k}\right|_{\infty}\leq B
, of the expansion of
f
given by Theorem
3.2
.
Let
\textbf{k}^{1},\ldots,\textbf{k}^{K}
be an enumeration of the integer vectors
k
satisfying
\left|\textbf{k}\right|_{\infty}\leq m
.
We use Theorem
3
to sort these vectors with respect to the amplitudes of the frequencies
\tau_{i}
in the ground truth signal
{f}
. Therefore, a solution
\omega

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\tau_{1}&=k^{1}_{1}\omega_{1}+\dots+k^{1}_{n}\omega_{n}\\

\vdots\\
\omega_{n}\end{array}\right]

As

, we can use the
least-squares
method to find a solution approximation.
The biases (phase shifts)
\varphi_{i}
can be calculated in a similar way using Equation

.

4.3.
On the periodicity of sinusoidal networks

A
periodic function

{f}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function satisfying
{f}(x)\!=\!{f}(x+P)
where
P
is the
period
.
For an example, consider
h(x)\!=\!a\sin(\omega x+\varphi)
where
\omega\!=\!l\frac{2\pi}{P}
, with
l\in\mathbb{Z}
, is the
frequency
,
a
is the
amplitude
, and
\varphi
is the
phase shift
.
Thus, we can force the first layer of a sinusoidal network to be periodic.

Specifically, let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a sinusoidal network, defining
\omega_{i}=l_{i}\frac{2\pi}{P}
, with
l_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, implies that input neurons
\textbf{s}_{i}\!:\!\mathbb{R}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{n}
are periodic functions with period
P
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\textbf{s}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin\big(l_{1}\frac{2\pi}{P}{x}+\varphi_{1}\big)\\
{\footnotesize\vdots}\\
\sin\left(l_{n}\frac{2\pi}{P}{x}+\varphi_{n}\right)\end{array}\right).

We now prove that the composition
\textbf{h}\big(\textbf{s}(x)\big)
outputs a list of neurons
\textbf{h}_{i}
that are periodic with period
P
. For this, we use Theorem
1
to rewrite
\textbf{h}_{i}
as follows:

\displaystyle\textbf{h}_{i}(x)=\sum_{\textbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\alpha_{\textbf{k}}(a)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{P}\left\langle\textbf{k},\textbf{l}\right\rangle x+\left\langle\textbf{k},\varphi\right\rangle+b\right).

Since
f(x)=\sum_{i}c_{i}\textbf{h}_{i}(x)+d
, the network
f
is also periodic with period
P
, and we have proved the following result.

Theorem 5
.

If the input neurons of a sinusoidal network
f
has its weights expressed by
\omega_{i}=l_{i}\frac{2\pi}{P}
, with
l_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
f
is periodic with period
P
.

We define a
periodic sinusoidal network
as a sinusoidal network such that its input neurons are periodic.
To provide an example, we train a periodic network with period
2
to learn a
square wave
with period
2
and amplitude
0.5
defined in the interval
[-1,1]
as follows.

\displaystyle{f}(x)=\begin{cases}0.5\quad&\text{if}\quad-1\leq x\leq 0\\
-0.5\quad&\text{if}\quad\quad 0\leq x\leq 1\end{cases}

We recall that its Fourier series is given by
{f}(x)\!=\!-\!\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{i\pi}\sin\big(i\pi x\big)
, with
i
odd.
Figure
4
(left) illustrates the sum of the first
92
harmonics of this series.

We assume the periodic sinusoidal network
f
to have width
5
. To fit
f
to
{f}
, we initialize the weights of the first layer of the network with the five most important frequencies
\pi[1,3,5,7,9]
of the Fourier series of
{f}
.
The weights
\omega
are kept fixed during training (not optimized).

Figure
4
(right) shows the graph of the trained network. Observe that the
Gibbs phenomenon

is minimized in the network approximation, which is not the case in the truncated Fourier series (left).
