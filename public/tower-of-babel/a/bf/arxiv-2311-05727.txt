Set Valued Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equations
Melih İşeri, Jianfeng Zhang

Abstract
Building upon the dynamic programming principle for set valued functions arising from many applications, in this paper we propose a new notion of set valued PDEs. The key component in the theory is a set valued Itô formula, characterizing the flows on the surface of the dynamic sets. In the contexts of multivariate control problems, we establish the wellposedness of the set valued HJB equations, which extends the standard HJB equations in the scalar case to the multivariate case. As an application, a moving scalarization for certain time inconsistent problems is constructed by using the classical solution of the set valued HJB equation.

Introduction

In this paper we consider set valued functions taking the form:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to 2^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}.

That is, for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the value
\mathbb{V}(t,x)
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying appropriate properties.
Such set valued functions, or their variants, have appeared in many applications, for example, stochastic
viability problems (Aubin-Da Prato
[
2
]
), multivariate
super-hedging problems (Kabanov
[
14
]
and Bouchard-Touzi
[
5
]
), multivariate
dynamic risk measures (Feinstein-Rudloff
[
8
]
), time inconsistent optimization problems (Karman-Ma-Zhang
[
15
]
), stochastic
target problems (Soner-Touzi
[
20
,
21
]
),
and recently, nonzero sum games
with multiple equilibria (Feinstein-Rudloff-Zhang
[
10
]
), and
mean field games with multiple mean field equilibria (İşeri-Zhang
[
13
]
). Many of these problems were considered non-standard or even ill-posed in the literature, and overall we lacked convenient mathematical tools to treat them. When viewed as set values, however, their value functions (named set-value functions in the paper) enjoy many nice properties as the value function of standard control problems, in particular the crucial
dynamic programming principle
, or say the time consistency. Notice that, for a standard control problem, the combination of the dynamic programming principle and the Itô formula leads to the popular PDE approach. Then a natural question is:

can we characterize these set-value functions via set valued PDEs?

(1.1)

This is exactly the goal of the present paper: to introduce the PDE approach and hence recover the standard language for these challenging problems. To be precise, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

Introduce derivatives for set valued functions and establish the set valued Itô formula.

•

Propose a notion of set valued PDEs, and establish its wellposedness in the contexts of multivariate stochastic control problems.

•

As an important application, construct a so called moving scalarization for a time inconsistent problem by using the classical solution of the corresponding set valued PDE.

We hope this paper serves as the first step of our long term project on providing a convenient tool and systematic study for multivariate problems, including games.

Our first main result is the
set valued Itô formula
, which roughly reads:

\displaystyle\quad d\mathbb{V}(t,X_{t})=\Big[\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}+\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}\cdot b+{1\over 2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}:\sigma\sigma^{\top})-{\cal K}_{\mathbb{V}}\zeta+\xi\Big]dt+\Big[\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}\sigma+\zeta\Big]dB_{t},

(1.2)

where
dX_{t}=b_{t}dt+\sigma_{t}dB_{t}
is a diffusion. We refer to Theorem
3.1
below for the precise meaning of the above formula. In particular,
\partial_{t}\mathbb{V},\partial_{x}\mathbb{V},\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}
are derivatives of
\mathbb{V}
defined on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
, the graph of
\mathbb{V}
, which consists of all points
(t,x,y)
where
y
lies on
\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,x)
, the boundary of
\mathbb{V}(t,x)
.
The essence of the Itô formula is to
characterize flows on the boundary surface. Given the surface’s
invariance under tangential deformations, a key feature of the set
valued Itô formula is the inclusion of arbitrary driving forces

\xi
and
\zeta
on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
, which take values in the tangent
space. This, along with the appropriate correction term

{\cal K}_{\mathbb{V}}\zeta
, ensures that the flows are not pushed away from
the surface.

Our set valued HJB equation takes the following form: for some
Hamiltonian function
h_{\mathbb{V}}
and with appropriate terminal condition,

\displaystyle\quad\sup_{a,\zeta}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)\cdot\Big[\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}(t,x,y)+h_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y,\partial_{x}\mathbb{V},\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V},a,\zeta)\Big]=0,\ \ (t,x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}},

(1.3)

where
a
takes values in a control set,
\zeta
takes values in the tangent space, and
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}
is the unit outward normal vector. This is derived by applying the above Itô formula on the dynamic programming principle for the set-value function of the multivariate control problem.
As we see, the introduction of
\zeta
(and
\xi
) in (
1.2
) is crucial. We note that
\xi
disappears in the equation since
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}\cdot\xi=0
. However,
{\cal K}_{\mathbb{V}}\zeta
is nonlinear in
\zeta
and thus
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}\cdot{\cal K}_{\mathbb{V}}\zeta
is an important component in the equation. The equation (
1.3
) can be rewritten equivalently in terms of the signed distance function
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
, see (
5
) below. We emphasize that
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}
is part of the solution here and the equation is satisfied only on the graph
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
, so the wellposedness of (
1.3
) has a completely different nature than that of standard PDEs.

In the scalar case:
m=1
, we can easily see that
\mathbb{V}(t,x)=[\underline{v}(t,x),\overline{v}(t,x)]
, where
\underline{v}
and
\overline{v}
are the value functions of the standard minimization and maximization problems, respectively. In this case,
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}=1
or
-1
, and the tangent space is degenerate and thus
\zeta=0
. Then (
1.3
) reduces exactly back to the standard HJB equations for
\underline{v}
and
\overline{v}
. So our set valued HJB equation is indeed a natural extension of the standard HJB equation to the multivariate setting. Moreover, note that
\underline{v}
and
\overline{v}
are the boundaries of
\mathbb{V}
in this case, namely
\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,x)=\{\underline{v}(t,x),\overline{v}(t,x)\}
, which inspires us to focus on the boundary surface
\mathbb{V}_{b}
instead of on the whole set
\mathbb{V}
. We would like to point that, again since
\xi=0
,
\zeta=0
in this case, then (
1.2
) also reduces back to the standard Itô formula for
\underline{v}(t,X_{t})
and
\overline{v}(t,X_{t})
.

Our main result of the paper is that the dynamic set-value function of
the multivariate stochastic control problem is the unique classical
solution of the set valued HJB equation (
1.3
), provided
\mathbb{V}

has sufficient regularity. We thus obtain the positive answer to our question (
1.1
) in this setting, which further opens the door to the PDE approach for more general multivariate problems. Such PDE characterization helps to understand better the structure and the nice properties of the dynamic set-value function. In particular, it helps to construct (approximate) optimal controls with certain Markovian structure. Indeed, when
\mathbb{V}
is smooth, as in
standard verification theorem we may use the optimal arguments

(a^{*},\zeta^{*})
of the Hamiltonian in (
1.3
) to construct an
optimal control for a scalarized optimization problem, as we will explain in the next paragraph.

At this point we would like to mention that the present paper considers classical solutions only. In particular, this requires that the set-value

Some related literature.
There is a large literature on set valued analysis, we refer to the book Aubin-Frankowska
[
3
]
and the reference therein. However, our approach is completely different from those in set valued analysis. We focus on the dynamics of the boundary surface, rather than the dynamics of the whole set. Roughly speaking, we focus only on those special selectors whose flow remains on the boundary. These selectors have nice properties and are sufficient to characterize the whole sets. Moreover, the boundary is essentially the frontier which has intrinsic optimality and thus is also important from practical point of view. We shall mention the recent paper Ararat-Ma-Wu
[
1
]
on set valued backward SDEs, which is highly relevant to our paper. Given our set valued function
\mathbb{V}(t,x)
and a state process
X
(e.g. a Brownian motion), we may introduce a set valued process
\mathbb{Y}_{t}:=\mathbb{V}(t,X_{t})
. In spirit the process
\mathbb{Y}
should satisfy a set valued backward SDE. However, besides that we employ completely different approaches, except in some simple cases our set valued process
\mathbb{Y}
does not satisfy the equation in
[
1
]
. That is, the objectives of the two works are different. We should mention that the applications mentioned in the beginning of this introduction fall into our framework, although technically our current results do not cover many of them (which we intend to study in our future research).

Our approach is strongly motivated by the studies on surface evolution equations, see e.g. Sethian
[
18
]
, Evans-Spruck
[
7
]
, Soner

[
19
]
, Barles-Soner-Souganidis
[
4
]
, and the monograph Giga
[
11
]
and the references therein. These equations arise in various
applications such as evolutions of phase boundaries, crystal growths, image processing, and mean-curvature flows, to mention a few. These works consider the dynamics of set valued function
\mathbb{V}(t)
, more precisely the boundary
\mathbb{V}_{b}(t)
, without the state variable
x
. In our terms, roughly speaking these works study first order set valued ODEs, while we extend to second order set valued PDEs. In particular, the set valued Itô formula is not involved there. Another difference is, due to the nature of different applications, they study forward equations with initial conditions while we study backward problems with terminal conditions. This difference would be crucial when one concerns path dependent setting (not covered in this paper), where one cannot do the time change freely due to the intrinsic adaptedness requirement.

Furthermore, within the surface evolution literature, our work is closely related to Soner-Touzi
[
21
]
which studies stochastic target problems by using mean curvature type geometric flows. In our contexts, their approach amounts to studying the following set-value function via its signed distance function
{\bf r}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{V}}(t):=\big\{(x,y):x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y\in\mathbb{V}(t)\big\},~\mbox{and thus}~\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{b}(t):=\big\{(x,y):x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(t)\big\}.

Clearly
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}
and
\mathbb{V}
are equivalent, with the same graph:
\mathbb{G}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}=\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
. The major difference here is that, while
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
and
{\bf r}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}
agree on the graph (both are
0
by definition), their derivatives are different on the graph, and consequently, the equation derived in
[
21
]
is different from our set valued HJB equation (
1.3
). In particular, in the scalar case:
m=1
, as mentioned (
1.3
) reduces back to the standard HJB equations, but the equation for
{\bf r}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}
does not seem to connect to the standard HJB equation directly. Moreover, the normal vector
{\bf n}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}
of
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}
is also different from
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}
, and does not serve as a moving scalarization as we discussed. We shall provide more detailed discussions in Section
8.2
below.

Finally, we remark that there are some very interesting studies on (possibly discontinuous) viscosity solutions along this line, see e.g.
[
4
]
, Chen-Giga-Goto
[
6
]
, Soner-Touzi
[

2
Intrinsic derivatives of set valued functions

Throughout the paper all vectors are viewed as column vectors,
\cdot
denotes the inner product, and
{}^{\top},^{c}
denote the transpose and complement, respectively. We denote by
\nabla
the gradient operator, and for a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
, we take the convention that the second derivative
\nabla_{xy}f(x,y):=[\partial_{x_{1}y}f,\cdots,\partial_{x_{d}y}f]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
.

2.1
Some basic materials

In this subsection we present some basic materials in geometry, which will be the starting point of our set valued functions in this paper.

Let
{\cal D}^{m}_{0}\subset 2^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
denote the space of closed set
\mathbb{D}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
and denote by
\mathbb{D}_{o}
and
\mathbb{D}_{b}
the interior and the boundary of
\mathbb{D}
, respectively. We equip
{\cal D}^{m}_{0}
with the metric:

\displaystyle{\bf d}(\mathbb{D},\tilde{\mathbb{D}}):=d(\mathbb{D},\tilde{\mathbb{D}})\vee d(\mathbb{D}_{b},\tilde{\mathbb{D}}_{b}),

where
d
is the standard Hausdorff distance, i.e.

\displaystyle d(\mathbb{D},\tilde{\mathbb{D}}):=\big(\sup_{y\in\mathbb{D}}d(y,\tilde{\mathbb{D}})\big)\vee\big(\sup_{\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}d(\tilde{y},\mathbb{D})\big),\quad d(y,\tilde{\mathbb{D}}):=\inf_{\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}|y-\tilde{y}|.

Introduce the signed distance function of
\mathbb{D}
: denoting by
\mathbb{D}^{c}
the complement of
\mathbb{D}
,

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{\mathbb{D}}(y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}d(y,\mathbb{D}_{b}),\quad~y\in\mathbb{D}^{c};\\
-d(y,\mathbb{D}_{b}),\quad y\in\mathbb{D}.\end{array}\right.

It is obvious that

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{o}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:{\bf r}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)<0\},\quad\mathbb{D}_{b}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:{\bf r}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)=0\},.

We next let
{\cal D}^{m}_{2}
denote the space of
\mathbb{D}\in{\cal D}^{m}_{0}
such that
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{D}}
is twice continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives on
O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{D}_{b}):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:|{\bf r}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)|<\varepsilon\}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. We remark that the boundary
\mathbb{D}_{b}
is a manifold without boundary, as regular as
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{D}}
.
For each
y\in\mathbb{D}_{b}
, let
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote the outward unit normal vector at
y
. It is clear that:

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)=\nabla_{y}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{D}}(y),~y\in\mathbb{D}_{b}\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\nabla_{y}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)|=1,~y\in O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{D}_{b}).

(2.4)

Moreover, for any
y\in O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{D}_{b})
, for possibly a smaller
\varepsilon>0
, let
\pi_{\mathbb{D}}(y)
denote the unique projection of
y
on
\mathbb{D}_{b}
, i.e.
\pi_{\mathbb{D}}(y)\in\mathbb{D}_{b}
satisfies:

\displaystyle y=\pi_{\mathbb{D}}(y)+{\bf r}_{\mathbb{D}}(y){\bf n}_{\mathbb{D}}(\pi_{\mathbb{D}}(y)),\quad y\in O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{D}_{b}).

(2.5)

For any
y\in\mathbb{D}_{b}
, let
\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)
denote the tangent space:

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{D}}(y):=\big\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\xi\cdot{\bf n}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)=0\big\},\quad y\in\mathbb{D}_{b}.

For a function
f:\mathbb{D}_{b}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define its intrinsic derivative
\partial_{y}f(y)\in\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)
by:

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{f(\theta(\varepsilon))-f(y)\over\varepsilon}=\partial_{y}f(y)\cdot\theta^{\prime}(0),~\mbox{for any smooth curve $\theta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{D}_{b}$ with $\theta(0)=y$}.\hskip 0.0pt

(2.6)

Alternatively, for any smooth extension
\widehat{f}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
\widehat{f}=f
on
\mathbb{D}_{b}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{y}f(y)=\nabla_{y}\widehat{f}(y)-[\nabla_{y}\widehat{f}(y)\cdot{\bf n}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)]{\bf n}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)=\nabla_{y}\widehat{f}(y)-{\bf n}_{\mathbb{D}}(y){\bf n}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)^{\top}\nabla_{y}\widehat{f}(y).

(2.7)

We emphasize that
\partial_{y}f(y)
does not depend on the choice of the extension
\widehat{f}
.

We also recall the shape operator
\partial_{y}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)=[\partial_{y}{\bf n}^{1}_{\mathbb{D}}(y),\cdots,\partial_{y}{\bf n}^{m}_{\mathbb{D}}(y)]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, which captures the curvatures of
\mathbb{D}_{b}
at
y
.

2.2
Set valued functions

Consider a continuous function
\mathbb{V}:\mathbb{R}\to{\cal D}^{m}_{2}
. Denote

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{b}(x):=(\mathbb{V}(x))_{b},\quad{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y):={\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}(x)}(y),\quad{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y):={\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}(x)}(y),\\
\displaystyle\pi_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y):=\pi_{\mathbb{V}(x)}(y),\quad\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y):=\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}(x)}(y),\end{array}\right.

and introduce the graph of
\mathbb{V}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}:=\big\{(x,y):x\in\mathbb{R},y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)\big\}.

When there is no confusion, for notational simplicity we may drop the subscript
V
in
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
,
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}
,
\pi_{\mathbb{V}}
and denote them as
{\bf r},{\bf n},\pi
.
We say

if

is twice continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives on
O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})

\varepsilon>0

Remark 2.1

(i) We note that our results in the paper will only involve
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
and its derivatives near
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
. For the convenience of our arguments, throughout the paper, we shall modify
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
outside of

satisfies:

•

\widehat{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}={\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}

•

\widehat{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m})
with bounded derivatives;

•

\widehat{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)\leq-{\varepsilon\over 2}
for all

and
\widehat{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)\geq{\varepsilon\over 2}
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{V}^{c}\backslash O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})
.

We emphasize that all our results will not rely on the choice of such a modification. For notational simplicity, we may identify
\widehat{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
with
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
.

(ii) Similarly we may extend
\pi_{\mathbb{V}}
outside of
O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})
, still denoted as
\pi_{\mathbb{V}}
, such that

•

On
O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})
,
\pi_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)
is the original unique projection of
y
on
\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
such that the counterpart of

2.5
)
holds true;

•

\pi_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)
is jointly measurable and
\pi_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
;

•

There exists a constant
C=C_{\mathbb{V}}
such that (modifying the extension of
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
if needed)

\displaystyle|y-\pi_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)|\leq C|{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)|.

(2.11)

(iii) We can also extend
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}
to the whole space
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, still denoted as
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}
, such that
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}
is continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives. One typical example is
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y):=\nabla_{y}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)
.

We remark that (
2.11
) follows from (
2.5
) when
(x,y)\in O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})
, and the existence of
C
for arbitrary
(x,y)
is due to the fact that
|{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)|\geq{\varepsilon\over 2}
for
(x,y)\notin O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})
.

Fix
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. For each
y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x_{0})
, consider the ODE: in light of Remark
2.1
,

\displaystyle{\Upsilon}^{y}(x)=y-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\nabla_{x}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}~{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(\tilde{x},{\Upsilon}^{y}(\tilde{x}))~d\tilde{x}.

(2.12)

Then clearly the above ODE has a unique solution.

Proposition 2.2

Assume
\mathbb{V}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R};{\cal D}^{m}_{2})
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)=\big\{{\Upsilon}^{y}(x):y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x_{0})\big\}.

(2.13)

Consequently,
(
2.12
)
involves
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
and
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}
only on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
and thus does not depend on the modification of
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
.

Proof
For notational simplicity, we drop the subscripts and denote
{\bf r},{\bf n},\pi
.

We first show that, for any
y_{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x_{0})
and
x>x_{0}
,
{\Upsilon}(x):={\Upsilon}^{y_{0}}(x)\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be such that the original
r
in (
2.1
) is twice continuously differentiable on
O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})
. Note that
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}\subset O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})
. Denote

\displaystyle\tau:=\inf\big\{x>x_{0}:(x,{\Upsilon}(x))\notin O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})\big\}.

Then, for
x\in[x_{0},\tau)
, apply the chain rule we have

\displaystyle{d\over dx}{\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))=\nabla_{x}{\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))-\nabla_{y}{\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))\cdot[\nabla_{x}{\bf r}~{\bf n}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))].

Recall (
2.5
) and denote
\pi(x):=\pi(x,{\Upsilon}(x))\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
. By (
2.4
) we have

\displaystyle{d\over dx}{\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))=\nabla_{x}{\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))\big[\nabla_{y}{\bf r}(x,\pi(x))\cdot{\bf n}(x,\pi(x))-\nabla_{y}{\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))\cdot{\bf n}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))\big].

Therefore, by (
2.5
) and the continuous differentiability of
\nabla_{y}{\bf r}
and
{\bf n}
, we have

\displaystyle{d\over dx}{\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))=\tilde{b}(x){\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x)),\quad x\in[x_{0},\tau),

for some appropriate continuous function
\tilde{b}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Note that
{\bf r}(x_{0},{\Upsilon}(x_{0}))={\bf r}(x_{0},y_{0})=0
. Then the above ODE implies
{\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))=0
for all
x\in[x_{0},\tau)
, which in turn implies
\tau=\infty
. Thus
{\bf r}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))=0
and hence
{\Upsilon}(x)\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
for all
x\geq x_{0}
. This implies that
\{{\Upsilon}^{y}(x):y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x_{0})\}\subset\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
for all
x\geq x_{0}
. Similarly we can show that
\{{\Upsilon}^{y}(x):y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x_{0})\}\subset\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
for all
x\leq x_{0}
, and hence for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

On the other hand, for any
y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
, consider (
2.12
) starting from
x
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Upsilon}^{y}(x^{\prime})=y-\int_{x}^{x^{\prime}}\nabla_{x}{\bf r}~{\bf n}(\tilde{x},\tilde{\Upsilon}^{y}(\tilde{x}))d\tilde{x}.

Then by the above result we have
y_{0}:=\tilde{\Upsilon}^{y}(x_{0})\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x_{0})
. By the wellposedness of the ODE (
2.12
), one can easily show that
\tilde{\Upsilon}^{y}(x^{\prime})={\Upsilon}^{y_{0}}(x^{\prime})
for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, and thus
y=\tilde{\Upsilon}^{y}(x)={\Upsilon}^{y_{0}}(x)
. This proves the opposite inclusion in (
2.13
).

Remark 2.3

(i) Later on we will define
\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}(x,y)=-\nabla_{x}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)
for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
, see
(
2.5
)
below. Then
(
2.12
)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\Upsilon}^{y}(x)=y+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}(\tilde{x},{\Upsilon}^{y}(\tilde{x}))d\tilde{x}.

(2.14)

Thus
(
2.13
)
can be viewed as the fundamental theorem of calculus for set valued functions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)=\mathbb{V}_{b}(x_{0})+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}(\tilde{x},\mathbb{V}_{b}(\tilde{x}))d\tilde{x}.

(2.15)

(ii) However,
(
2.15
)
should be interpreted as
(
2.14
)
and
(
2.13
)
, rather than the meaning in the standard set valued analysis, which roughly speaking considers

\displaystyle\tilde{\Upsilon}(x):=y+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}(\tilde{x},\tilde{\gamma}(\tilde{x}))d\tilde{x},\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x_{0}),\tilde{\gamma}(\tilde{x})\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(\tilde{x}).

The above
\tilde{\Upsilon}(x)
is in general not in
\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
. See also related discussion in Section
8.3
below.

(iii) Let
{\Upsilon}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{m})
be such that
{\Upsilon}(x)\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
for all
x
. Then, by
(
2.4
)
,

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle 0={d\over dx}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))=\nabla_{x}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))+\nabla_{y}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))\cdot\partial_{x}{\Upsilon}(x)\\
\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))+{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,{\Upsilon}(x))\cdot\partial_{x}{\Upsilon}(x).\end{array}\right.

(iv) The set valued Itô formula in the next section, which is one of the main results of this paper, can be viewed as the stochastic version of Proposition
2.2
.

The next result, although technically not used in this paper, is interesting in its own right. We postpone its proof to Appendix.

Proposition 2.4

Assume
\mathbb{V}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R};{\cal D}^{m}_{2})
and
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
. Then the curve
{\Upsilon}(x):={\Upsilon}^{y_{0}}(x)
determined by
(
2.12
)
is a local geodesic of the flow
\mathbb{V}
in the following sense: for any continuous curve
\theta(x)\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
with
\theta(x_{0})=y_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\overline{\rm lim}}_{x\to x_{0}}{1\over|x-x_{0}|}\big[L_{\Upsilon}(x_{0},x)-L_{\theta}(x_{0},x)\big]\leq 0,

where
L_{\Upsilon}(x_{0},x)
(resp.
L_{\theta}(x_{0},x)
) denotes the length of
{\Upsilon}
(resp.
\theta
) between
x_{0},x
.

Consider a function
f:\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}\to\mathbb{R}
. For fixed
x
, the intrinsic derivative
\partial_{y}f(x,y)
for
y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)
is defined (
2.6
) or equivalently by (
2.7
). We next define the intrinsic derivative of
f
with respect to
x
following the local geodesic
{\Upsilon}
defined by (
2.12
):

\displaystyle\partial_{x}f(x_{0},y_{0}):=\lim_{x\to x_{0}}{f(x,{\Upsilon}^{y_{0}}(x))-f(x_{0},y_{0})\over x-x_{0}},\quad(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}.

(2.19)

Equivalently, for any smooth extension
\widehat{f}
of
f
, we have

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\partial_{x}f(x_{0},y_{0})=\lim_{x\to x_{0}}{\widehat{f}(x,{\Upsilon}^{y_{0}}(x))-\widehat{f}(x_{0},y_{0})\over x-x_{0}}\\
\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}\widehat{f}(x_{0},y_{0})-\nabla_{x}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}\nabla_{y}\widehat{f}\cdot{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(x_{0},y_{0}).\end{array}\right.

Again, the right side above does not depend on the choice of the extension
\widehat{f}
.

We say
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})
if
f
has continuous intrinsic derivatives
\partial_{y}f
and
\partial_{x}f
. By (
2.19
), it is obvious that
\partial_{x}f
is linear on
f
, and the product rule and chain rule remain true:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\partial_{x}(fg)=g\partial_{x}f+f\partial_{x}g,~\mbox{for all}~f,g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}});\\
\displaystyle\partial_{x}[f(g)]=f^{\prime}(g)\partial_{x}g,~\mbox{for all}~f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}),g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}).\end{array}\right.

2.3
Intrinsic derivatives of set valued functions

We now extend all the analysis to functions
\mathbb{V}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to{\cal D}^{m}_{2}
. In this and the next section we may allow infinite time horizon
[0,\infty)
. However, for later sections we require
T
to be finite, so for simplicity we consider finite
T
here as well.
Introduce
\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,x)
,
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)
,
{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)
,
\pi_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)
,
\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)
in an obvious manner as in (
2.2
) and denote

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}:=\big\{(t,x,y):(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,x)\big\}.

As before we may use the simplified notations
r,{\bf n},\pi
when there is no confusion, and we will always use their modified version or extension as in Remark
2.1
.

Recall (
2.19
) and (
2.2
) when
\mathbb{V}
is defined on
\mathbb{R}
. Now for our more general
\mathbb{V}
and for any function
f:\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define its intrinsic partial derivatives
\partial_{t}f\in\mathbb{R},\partial_{x}f\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\partial_{y}f\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and the higher order intrinsic derivatives in an obvious manner, for example, the second order derivatives are defined as:

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}f:=\partial_{x_{i}}(\partial_{x_{j}}f).

Moreover, for
f:\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define its intrinsic derivatives component wise.

Finally, by considering the special function
f_{0}(x,y):=y
and its natural extension
\widehat{f}_{0}(x,y)=y
, applying (
2.2
) component wise we define the intrinsic derivatives of
\mathbb{V}
.

Definition 2.5

For any
(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
and by denoting
f_{0}(x,y):=y
, define

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}(t,x,y):=\partial_{t}f_{0}(t,x,y)=-\nabla_{t}{\bf r}(t,x,y){\bf n}(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m};\\
\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}\mathbb{V}(t,x,y):=\partial_{x_{i}}f_{0}(t,x,y)=-\nabla_{x_{i}}{\bf r}(t,x,y){\bf n}(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad i=1,\cdots,d.\end{array}\right.

We recall Remark
2.3
and note that (
2.2
) becomes: for any
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f=\nabla_{t}\widehat{f}+\nabla_{y}\widehat{f}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbb{V},\quad\partial_{x_{i}}f=\nabla_{x_{i}}\widehat{f}+\nabla_{y}\widehat{f}\cdot\partial_{x_{i}}\mathbb{V},\quad\mbox{on}~\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}.

(2.29)

Note that
\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}
and
\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}
are functions on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
, then we may continue to define higher order derivatives of
\mathbb{V}
by applying (
2.2
) or (
2.29
) repeatedly.

Lemma 2.6

Assume
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}\in C^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};{\cal D}^{m}_{2})
. Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}\mathbb{V}(t,x,y)=-\nabla_{x_{i}x_{j}}{\bf r}~{\bf n}(t,x,y)-\nabla_{x_{j}}{\bf r}~\partial_{x_{i}}{\bf n}(t,x,y);

(2.30)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{x}{\bf n}^{i}=\nabla_{xy_{i}}{\bf r};\qquad\partial_{y}{\bf n}^{i}=\nabla_{y_{i}y}{\bf r}.

(2.31)

The proof is quite straightforward, we thus postpone it to Appendix. Throughout the paper, we shall take the notational convention:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}:=[\partial_{x_{1}}\mathbb{V},\cdots,\partial_{x_{d}}\mathbb{V}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d},\quad\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}^{i}:=[\partial_{x_{1}x}\mathbb{V}^{i},\cdots,\partial_{x_{d}x}\mathbb{V}^{i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d};\\
\partial_{x}{\bf n}=[\partial_{x_{1}}{\bf n},\cdots,\partial_{x_{d}}{\bf n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d},\quad\partial_{y}{\bf n}=[\partial_{y_{1}}{\bf n},\cdots,\partial_{y_{m}}{\bf n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}.\end{array}\right.

Remark 2.7

(i) At
(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
, since
|{\bf n}|^{2}=1
, by
(
2.2
)
we have
\partial_{x_{j}}{\bf n}\cdot{\bf n}=0
. That is,
\partial_{x_{j}}{\bf n}(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)
. So
(
2.30
)
provides an orthogonal decomposition of
\partial_{xx}V
. In particular, unlike the first order derivatives in
(
2.5
)
,
\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}\mathbb{V}
is in general not parallel to
{\bf n}
.

(ii) It is clear that

\displaystyle\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}\cdot{\bf n}:=\big[\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}\mathbb{V}\cdot{\bf n}\big]_{1\leq i,j\leq d}=-\nabla_{xx}{\bf r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

is symmetric. However, in general
\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}
is not symmetric:

\displaystyle\nabla_{x_{j}}{\bf r}\partial_{x_{i}}{\bf n}\neq\nabla_{x_{i}}{\bf r}\partial_{x_{j}}{\bf n},\quad i\neq j.

(iii)
\partial_{\bf y}{\bf n}=\nabla_{yy}{\bf r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is symmetric. Moreover, since
\partial_{y_{i}}{\bf n}\cdot{\bf n}=0
, we see that
0
is an eigenvalue of
\partial_{\bf y}{\bf n}
with eigenvector
{\bf n}
.

Example 2.8

(i) Let
w:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to(0,\infty)
be continuously differentiable. Set, with
d=m=2
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(t,x):=\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|y-w(t,x)|\leq u(t,x)\big\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{and thus}\quad\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,x)=\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|y-w(t,x)|=u(t,x)\big\}.

It is clear that

\displaystyle{\bf r}(t,x,y)=|y-w(t,x)|-u(t,x),~(t,x,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Then, for
(t,x,y)
near
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
(so that
|y-w(t,x)|>0
), by straightforward calculation,

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\nabla_{t}{\bf r}=-{(y-w)\cdot\nabla_{t}w\over|y-w|}-\nabla_{t}u;\quad\nabla_{x_{i}}{\bf r}=-{(y-w)\cdot\nabla_{x_{i}}w\over|y-w|}-\nabla_{x_{i}}u;\quad\nabla_{y_{i}}{\bf r}={y_{i}-w^{i}\over|y-w|};\\
\displaystyle\nabla_{x_{i}x_{j}}{\bf r}={\nabla_{x_{i}}w\cdot\nabla_{x_{j}}w-(y-w)\cdot\nabla_{x_{i}x_{j}}w\over|y-w|}-{[(y-w)\cdot\nabla_{x_{i}}w][(y-w)\cdot\nabla_{x_{j}}w]\over|y-w|^{3}}-\nabla_{x_{i}x_{j}}u;\\
\displaystyle\nabla_{x_{i}y_{j}}{\bf r}=-{\nabla_{x_{i}}w^{j}\over|y-w|}+{[(y-w)\cdot\nabla_{x_{i}}w](y_{j}-w^{j})\over|y-w|^{3}},\quad\nabla_{y_{i}y_{j}}{\bf r}={{\bf 1}_{\{i=j\}}\over|y-w|}-{[y_{i}-w^{i}][y_{j}-w^{j}]\over|y-w|^{3}},\end{array}\right.

Then, by
(
2.4
)
, Definition
2.5
, and Lemma
2.6
, at
(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
we have

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle{\bf n}={y-w\over u};\quad\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}=\big[\nabla_{t}w\cdot{\bf n}+\nabla_{t}u\big]{\bf n};\quad\partial_{x_{i}}\mathbb{V}=\big[\nabla_{x_{i}}w\cdot{\bf n}+\nabla_{x_{i}}u\big]{\bf n};\\
\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}{\bf n}={1\over u}\big[-\nabla_{x_{i}}w+[{\bf n}\cdot\nabla_{x_{i}}w]{\bf n}\big],\quad\partial_{y_{i}}{\bf n}^{j}={1\over u}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{i=j\}}-{\bf n}^{i}{\bf n}^{j}\big];\\
\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}\mathbb{V}=-\Big[{1\over u}\big[\nabla_{x_{i}}w\cdot\nabla_{x_{j}}w-(\nabla_{x_{i}}w\cdot{\bf n})(\nabla_{x_{j}}w\cdot{\bf n})\big]-\nabla_{x_{i}x_{j}}w\cdot{\bf n}-\nabla_{x_{i}x_{j}}u\Big]{\bf n}\\
\displaystyle-{1\over u}\big[\nabla_{x_{j}}w\cdot{\bf n}+\nabla_{x_{j}}u\big]\big[\nabla_{x_{i}}w-(\nabla_{x_{i}}w\cdot{\bf n}){\bf n}\big].\end{array}\right.

In particular, we see that in general
\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}\mathbb{V}\neq\partial_{x_{j}x_{i}}\mathbb{V}
for
i\neq j
.

(ii) Consider a special case that
w=0
and
u
satisfies the heat equation:

\displaystyle\nabla_{t}u+{1\over 2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\nabla_{xx}u)=0.

Then by
(
2.8
)
we have
\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}=\nabla_{t}u~{\bf n}
,
\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}\mathbb{V}=\nabla_{x_{i}x_{j}}u~{\bf n}
, on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
.
Thus
\mathbb{V}
satisfies the following equation:

\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}+{1\over 2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V})=0
, on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
.
This clearly implies the following set valued heat equation:

\displaystyle{\bf n}\cdot\big[\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}+{1\over 2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V})\big]=0,~\mbox{on}~\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}.

We remark that we assumed
r
had bounded derivatives on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
in all above analyses. For our applications later, however,
\mathbb{V}(T,x)
could be degenerate, in the sense that
\mathbb{V}(T,x)=\{g(x)\}
is a singleton and hence a degenerate manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Note that in (
2.8
),
\partial_{x}{\bf n}
,
\partial_{y}{\bf n}
, and
\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}
explode when
u=0
. This motivates us to define the following space.

Definition 2.9

(i) We say
\mathbb{V}\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};{\cal D}^{m}_{2})
if
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}\in C^{2}(O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}});\mathbb{R})
for some
\varepsilon>0
such that all the related derivatives are bounded and uniformly Lipschitz continuous in
y
. Consequently,
\partial_{t}\mathbb{V},\partial_{x}\mathbb{V},\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}
,
\partial_{x}{\bf n}
,
\partial_{y}{\bf n}
are bounded and uniformly Lipschitz continuous in
y
on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
.

(ii) We say
\mathbb{V}\in C^{1,2}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d};{\cal D}^{m}_{2})
if
\mathbb{V}\in C^{0}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};{\cal D}^{m}_{0})
, and
\mathbb{V}\in C^{1,2}([0,T-\delta]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};{\cal D}^{m}_{2})
for all
0<\delta<T
. Note that we do not require
\mathbb{V}(T,x)\in{\cal D}^{m}_{2}
here.

3
The set valued Itô formula

We first introduce the probabilistic setting. Let
\Omega:=\{\omega\in C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d}):\omega_{0}=0\}
be the canonical space,
B
the canonical process, i.e.
B(\omega)=\omega
,
\mathbb{P}
the Wiener measure, i.e.
B
is an
\mathbb{P}
-Brownian motion, and
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}^{B}
the natural filtration generated by
B
. For a generic Euclidian space
E
and
p\geq 1
, let
\mathbb{L}^{p}_{loc}(E)
denote the space of
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable
E
-valued processes
\theta
such that
\int_{0}^{T}|\theta_{t}|^{p}dt<\infty
, a.s., and
\mathbb{L}^{p}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{m};E)
the space of
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable random fields
\xi:(t,\omega,y)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to E
such that
\xi(\cdot,\cdot,0)\in\mathbb{L}^{p}_{loc}(E)
and
\xi
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous in
y
.

Fix
\mathbb{V}\in C^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};{\cal D}^{m}_{2})
with corresponding
\varepsilon>0
, and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
b\in\mathbb{L}^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{L}^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
,
\xi\in\mathbb{L}^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
\zeta\in\mathbb{L}^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R}^{m\times d})
. Denote,

\displaystyle X_{t}:=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}dB_{s},

and introduce the (random) differential operators: recalling (
2.3
),

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal L}^{b,\sigma}\mathbb{V}(t,\omega,x,y):=\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}+\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}b+\frac{1}{2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\sigma^{\top}\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}\sigma);

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal K}^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{V}}\zeta(t,\omega,x,y):=\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}\big(\zeta^{\top}\partial_{x}{\bf n}\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{\top}\partial_{y}{\bf n}\zeta\big)~\!{\bf n}.

where
\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\sigma^{\top}\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
i
-th component
\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\sigma^{\top}\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}^{i}\sigma)
. Moreover, recalling Remark
2.1
, we may extend the derivatives of
\mathbb{V}
and
{\bf n}
to
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

We are now ready to establish the set valued Itô formula.

Theorem 3.1

Let
\mathbb{V},x_{0},b,\sigma,X,\xi,\zeta
be as above. Assume, and for each
i
,
\zeta_{i}(t,\omega,y)\in\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,X_{t}(\omega),y)
holds for all
y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,X_{t}(\omega))
, for
dt\times d\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
. For each
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let
{\Upsilon}^{y}
denote the unique strong solution of SDE: recalling Remark
2.1
,

\displaystyle{\Upsilon}_{t}^{y}=y+\int_{0}^{t}\big[{\cal L}^{b,\sigma}\mathbb{V}-\kappa^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{V}}\zeta+\xi\big](s,X_{s},{\Upsilon}_{s}^{y})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\big[\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}\sigma+\zeta\big](s,X_{s},{\Upsilon}_{s}^{y})dB_{s}.\hskip-10.00002pt

(3.1)

(i) Assume
\xi_{t}(y)\in\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,X_{t},y)
, for all
y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,X_{t})
, for
dt\times d\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
. Then

\displaystyle\{{\Upsilon}_{t}^{y}:y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(0,x_{0})\}\subset\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,X_{t})~\mbox{a.s., for all $0\leq t\leq T$}.

In particular, in this case no extension is needed in
(
3.1
)
.

Moreover, if
\mathbb{V}_{b}
takes values in connected compact sets, then the equality holds:

\displaystyle\big\{{\Upsilon}_{t}^{y}:y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(0,x_{0})\big\}=\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,X_{t})~\mbox{a.s., for all $0\leq t\leq T$}.

(ii) Assume
\xi_{t}(y)\cdot{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,X_{t},y)\leq 0
for all
y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,X_{t}(\omega))
, for
dt\times d\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
. Then

\displaystyle\{{\Upsilon}_{t}^{y}:y\in\mathbb{V}_{o}(0,x_{0})\}\subset\mathbb{V}_{o}(t,X_{t})~\mbox{a.s., for all $0\leq t\leq T$}.

(iii) Assume
\xi_{t}(y)\cdot{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,X_{t},y)\geq 0
for all
y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,X_{t}(\omega))
, for
dt\times d\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
. Then

\displaystyle\{{\Upsilon}_{t}^{y}:y\in\mathbb{V}^{c}(0,x_{0})\}\subset\mathbb{V}^{c}(t,X_{t})~\mbox{a.s., for all $0\leq t\leq T$}.

Proof
(i) Fix
y_{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(0,x_{0})
and denote
{\Upsilon}={\Upsilon}^{y_{0}}
. Introduce

\displaystyle\tau:=\inf\big\{t\geq 0:(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t})\notin O_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}})\big\}\wedge T.

Since
(0,x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
, then
\tau>0
, and
r
is smooth on
[0,\tau]
. By the standard Itô’s formula,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad d{\bf r}(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t})=\Lambda(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t})dt+M(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t})dB_{t},\quad\mbox{where}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad\Lambda:=\nabla_{t}{\bf r}+\nabla_{x}{\bf r}\cdot b+\nabla_{y}{\bf r}\cdot({\cal L}^{b,\sigma}\mathbb{V}-\kappa^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{V}}\zeta+\xi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}\Big(\sigma^{\top}\nabla_{xx}r\sigma+(\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}\sigma+\zeta)^{\top}\nabla_{yy}r(\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}\sigma+\zeta))+2(\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}\sigma+\zeta)^{\top}\nabla_{xy}r\sigma\Big);

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad M:=\nabla_{x}r^{\top}\sigma+\nabla_{y}r^{\top}(\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}\sigma+\zeta).

We claim that, when

\displaystyle\Lambda(t,X_{t},y)={\bf n}(t,X_{t},y)\cdot\xi_{t}(y),\qquad M(t,X_{t},y)=\big[{\bf n}\cdot\zeta_{1},\cdots,{\bf n}\cdot\zeta_{d}\big](t,X_{t},y).

(3.3)

Indeed, in this case we have

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!{\bf n}\cdot({\cal L}^{b,\sigma}\mathbb{V}-\kappa^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{V}}\zeta+\xi)=-\nabla_{t}{\bf r}-\nabla_{x}{\bf r}\cdot b-\frac{1}{2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}\big(\sigma^{\top}\nabla_{xx}{\bf r}\sigma+2\zeta^{\top}\nabla_{xy}{\bf r}\sigma+\zeta^{\top}\nabla_{yy}{\bf r}\zeta\big)+{\bf n}\cdot\xi;

\displaystyle\Big(\nabla_{yy}{\bf r}\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}\big)_{ij}=\nabla_{y_{i}y}{\bf r}\cdot\partial_{x_{j}}\mathbb{V}=-\nabla_{y_{i}y}{\bf r}\cdot\nabla_{x_{j}}{\bf r}{\bf n}=0,\quad 1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq d;

\displaystyle\Big((\partial_{x}\mathbb{V})^{\top}\nabla_{xy}{\bf r}\Big)_{ij}=\partial_{x_{i}}\mathbb{V}\cdot\nabla_{x_{j}y}{\bf r}=-\nabla_{x_{i}}{\bf r}{\bf n}\cdot\nabla_{x_{j}y}{\bf r}=0,\quad 1\leq i,j\leq d.

Here we used the facts

\displaystyle M:=\nabla_{x}r^{\top}\sigma-{\bf n}^{\top}[\nabla_{x_{1}}{\bf r}{\bf n},\cdots,\nabla_{x_{d}}r{\bf n}]\sigma+{\bf n}^{\top}\zeta={\bf n}^{\top}\zeta.

Thus (

Since

\displaystyle\Lambda(t,X_{t},y)=M(t,X_{t},y)=0,\quad y\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,X_{t}).

Now for any

\displaystyle M(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t})=M(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t})-M(t,X_{t},\pi(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t}))=\tilde{\sigma}_{t}{\bf r}(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t});\\
\displaystyle\mbox{where}\quad|\tilde{b}_{t}|\leq C[1+|b_{t}|+|\sigma_{t}|^{2}+|\zeta_{t}(0)|^{2}],\quad|\tilde{\sigma}_{t}|\leq C[|\sigma_{t}|+|\zeta_{t}(0)|].\end{array}\right.

(3.8)

Then, recalling

(ii) In this case, by (

4
A multivariate control problem

Recall the canonical setting introduced in the beginning of Section
3
. Given
0\leq t<T
, we shall also consider the shifted Brownian motion
B^{t}_{s}:=B_{s}-B_{t}
, and the shifted filtration
\mathbb{F}^{t}:=\mathbb{F}^{B^{t}}
on
[t,T]
. For a generic Euclidean space
E
, let
\mathbb{L}^{2}({\cal F}_{t},E)
denote the set of
{\cal F}_{t}
-measurable square integrable
E
-valued random variables, and
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{t},E)
the set of
\mathbb{F}^{t}
-progressively measurable square integrable
E
-valued processes on
[t,T]
.

Let
A
be a domain in some Euclidean space. For each
t\in[0,T]
, our set of admissible controls
{\cal A}_{t}
consists of
\mathbb{F}^{t}
-progressively measurable
A
-valued processes
\alpha
. We remark that in this paper we consider open loop controls, which is more convenient to study the regularities and to construct desired approximations for our value functions. However, as in standard stochastic control problems, one can easily see that the set values in this section will remain the same if we consider appropriate closed loop controls.

Given
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, consider the following controlled dynamics: for each
\alpha\in{\cal A}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle X^{t,x,\alpha}_{s}=x+\int_{t}^{s}b(r,X^{t,x,\alpha}_{r},\alpha_{r})dr+\int_{t}^{s}\sigma(r,X^{t,x,\alpha}_{r},\alpha_{r})dB_{r},\\
\displaystyle Y^{t,x,\alpha}_{s}=g(X^{t,x,\alpha}_{T})+\int_{s}^{T}f(r,X^{t,x,\alpha}_{r},Y^{t,x,\alpha}_{r},Z^{t,x,\alpha}_{r},\alpha_{r})dr-\int_{s}^{T}Z^{t,x,\alpha}_{r}dB_{r}.\end{array}\right.

Here
X,Y,Z
take values in
\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, respectively, and
b,\sigma,f,g
are in appropriate dimensions and satisfy certain technical conditions which will be specified later. We emphasize that
Y
is typically multiple dimensional:
m>1
. Our set-value is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(t,x):=\mbox{cl}\big\{Y^{t,x,\alpha}_{t}:\alpha\in{\cal A}_{t}\big\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(4.4)

Here cl denotes the closure. Thus
\mathbb{V}
is a set valued mapping
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to 2^{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
. We now motivate this set-value function in the following remarks.

Remark 4.1

In the scalar case:
m=1
, consider the standard control problems:

\displaystyle\underline{v}(t,x):=\inf_{\alpha\in{\cal A}_{t}}Y^{t,x,\alpha}_{t},\quad\overline{v}(t,x):=\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}_{t}}Y^{t,x,\alpha}_{t}.

Then it is obvious that

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(t,x)=[\underline{v}(t,x),\overline{v}(t,x)].

(4.5)

That is, the standard optimization problems are characterizing the boundary of our set-value function. In this paper, we will characterize the boundary of
\mathbb{V}
through a set valued HJB equation, and thus we extend the scalar optimization problem to the multivariate setting.

Remark 4.2

The set valued functions can be used to analyze some time inconsistent optimization problems. Consider the well known mean variance optimization problem:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle V_{0}:=\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\Big[\mathbb{E}[X^{0,x_{0},\alpha}_{T}]-{\lambda\over 2}\mbox{Var}(X^{0,x_{0},\alpha}_{T})\Big],\\
\displaystyle\mbox{where}~X^{t,x,\alpha}_{s}=x+\int_{t}^{s}\alpha_{r}dr+\int_{t}^{s}\alpha_{r}dB_{r}.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle Y^{t,x,\alpha,1}_{s}=X^{t,x,\alpha}_{T}-\int_{s}^{T}Z^{t,x,\alpha,1}_{r}dB_{r},\quad Y^{t,x,\alpha,2}_{s}=|X^{t,x,\alpha}_{T}|^{2}-\int_{s}^{T}Z^{t,x,\alpha,2}_{r}dB_{r}.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle V_{0}:=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{V}(0,x_{0})}\varphi(y),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\varphi(y):=y_{1}+{\lambda\over 2}|y_{1}|^{2}-{\lambda\over 2}y_{2}.

(4.12)

\omega^{\theta}\in E_{\theta}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\{\tau>t_{\theta}\}\cap E_{\theta}\big)\leq\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{P}(E_{\theta}),\quad\mbox{where}\quad t_{\theta}:=\tau(\omega^{\theta}),\quad x_{\theta}:=X^{\alpha}_{t_{\theta}}(\omega^{\theta}).

(4.30)

Moreover, since
\phi(\omega^{\theta})\in\mathbb{V}(t_{\theta},x_{\theta})
, choose
\alpha^{\theta}\in{\cal A}_{t_{\theta}}
such that

\displaystyle\big|\phi(\omega^{\theta})-Y^{t_{\theta},x_{\theta},\alpha^{\theta}}_{t_{\theta}}\big|\leq\varepsilon.

(4.31)

We then construct
\hat{\alpha}\in{\cal A}_{0}
by: denoting
\omega^{t}_{s}:=\omega_{s}-\omega_{t}
,
0\leq t\leq s\leq T
,

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{t}(\omega):=\alpha_{t}(\omega){\bf 1}_{[0,\tau_{\varepsilon}(\omega))}(t)+{\bf 1}_{[\tau_{\varepsilon}(\omega),T]}(t)\Big[\sum_{\theta}{\bf 1}_{E_{\theta}}(\omega)\alpha^{\theta}_{t}(\omega^{t_{\theta}})+a_{0}{\bf 1}_{E^{c}}\Big],\end{array}\right.

where the summations are over all
\theta=(i,j,k)
with
i=1,\cdots,n
and
j,k\geq 1
, and
a_{0}\in A
is an arbitrary value.

Step 3.
We now verify (
4.26
). First, for any
\theta=(i,j,k)
such that
E_{\theta}\neq\emptyset
, a.s. on
E_{\theta}
we have
\tau_{\varepsilon}=t_{i}\geq t_{\theta}
and, denoting
(X^{\theta},Y^{\theta},Z^{\theta}):=(X^{t_{\theta},x_{\theta},\alpha^{\theta}},Y^{t_{\theta},x_{\theta},\alpha^{\theta}},Z^{t_{\theta},x_{\theta},\alpha^{\theta}})
,

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}=X^{\alpha}_{t_{\theta}}+\int_{t_{\theta}}^{t}b(s,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},\alpha_{s})ds+\int_{t_{\theta}}^{t}\sigma(s,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},\alpha_{s})dB_{s},\quad t\in[t_{\theta},t_{i}],\\
\displaystyle X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}=X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t_{i}}+\int_{t_{i}}^{t}b(s,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},\alpha^{\theta}_{s}(B^{t_{\theta}}))ds+\int_{t_{i}}^{t}\sigma(s,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},\alpha^{\theta}_{s}(B^{t_{\theta}}))dB_{s},\quad t\in[t_{i},T],\\
\displaystyle X^{\theta}_{t}=x_{\theta}+\int_{t_{\theta}}^{t}b(s,X^{\theta}_{s},\alpha^{\theta}_{s}(B^{t_{\theta}}))ds+\int_{t_{\theta}}^{t}\sigma(s,X^{\theta}_{s},\alpha^{\theta}_{s}(B^{t_{\theta}}))dB_{s},\quad t\in[t_{\theta},T].\end{array}\right.

Since
b,\sigma
are bounded, and
|X^{\alpha}_{t_{\theta}}-x_{\theta}|\leq\varepsilon
,
t_{i}-t_{\theta}\leq\varepsilon^{2}
, by standard SDE estimates we get

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}_{t_{\theta}}}\Big[\sup_{t_{\theta}\leq t\leq t_{i}}|X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}-X^{\theta}_{t}|^{2}\Big]\leq C\varepsilon^{2},\ \mbox{and then}\ \mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}_{t_{\theta}}}\Big[\sup_{t_{i}\leq t\leq T}|X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}-X^{\theta}_{t}|^{2}\Big]\leq C\varepsilon^{2}.

(4.34)

Similarly, note that

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}=g(X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}f(s,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},Z^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},\alpha^{\theta}_{s}(B^{t_{\theta}}))ds-\int_{t}^{T}Z^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s}dB_{s},\quad t\in[t_{i},T],\\
\displaystyle Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}=Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t_{i}}+\int_{t}^{t_{i}}f(s,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},Z^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},\alpha_{s})ds-\int_{t}^{t_{i}}Z^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s}dB_{s},\quad t\in[t_{\theta},t_{i}],\\
\displaystyle Y^{\theta}_{t}=g(X^{\theta}_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}f(s,X^{\theta}_{s},Y^{\theta}_{s},Z^{\theta}_{s},\alpha^{\theta}_{s}(B^{t_{\theta}}))ds-\int_{t}^{T}Z^{\theta}_{s}dB_{s},\quad t\in[t_{\theta},T].\end{array}\right.

Then, by (
4.34
) and standard BSDE estimates we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}_{t_{\theta}}}\Big[\sup_{t_{i}\leq t\leq T}|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}-Y^{\theta}_{t}|^{2}\Big]\leq C\varepsilon^{2},\quad\mbox{and then}\quad\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}_{t_{\theta}}}\Big[\sup_{t_{\theta}\leq t\leq t_{i}}|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}-Y^{\theta}_{t}|^{2}\Big]\leq C\varepsilon^{2}.

In particular, this implies that

\displaystyle|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t_{\theta}}-Y^{\theta}_{t_{\theta}}|\leq C\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{a.s. on}~E_{\theta}.

(4.36)

By Assumption
4.4
, one can easily see that
Y^{\theta}
,
Y^{\hat{\alpha}}
are bounded. Consider the BSDE:

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}^{\theta}_{t}=Y^{\theta}_{t_{\theta}}+\int_{t}^{t_{\theta}}f(s,x_{\theta},\tilde{Y}^{\theta}_{s},\tilde{Z}^{\theta}_{s},a_{0})ds-\int_{t}^{t_{\theta}}\tilde{Z}^{\theta}_{s}dB_{s},\quad t\in[t_{i-1},t_{\theta}].

(4.37)

Note that
Y^{\theta}_{t_{\theta}}
is deterministic, then so is
\tilde{Y}^{\theta}_{t}
and thus
\tilde{Z}^{\theta}_{t}=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sup_{t_{i-1}\leq t\leq t_{\theta}}|\tilde{Y}^{\theta}_{t}-Y^{\theta}_{t_{\theta}}|\leq\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{\theta}}|f(s,x_{\theta},\tilde{Y}^{\theta}_{s},0,a_{0})|ds\leq C(t_{i}-t_{i-1})\leq C\varepsilon^{2}.

Moreover, note that
E_{\theta}\in{\cal F}_{\tau}
and

\displaystyle Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}=Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t_{\theta}}+\int_{t}^{t_{\theta}}f(s,X^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},Z^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s},\alpha_{s})ds-\int_{t}^{t_{\theta}}Z^{\hat{\alpha}}_{s}dB_{s},\quad t\in[t_{i-1},t_{\theta}].

Compare this with (
4.37
), by (
4.36
) and standard BSDE estimate we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}_{\tau}}\Big[\sup_{\tau\leq t\leq t_{\theta}}|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}-\tilde{Y}^{\theta}_{t}|^{2}\Big]\leq C\varepsilon^{2},\quad\mbox{a.s. on}~\{\tau\leq t_{\theta}\}\cap E_{\theta}.

Then

\displaystyle|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{\tau}-Y^{\theta}_{t_{\theta}}|\leq|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{\tau}-\tilde{Y}^{\theta}_{\tau}|+|\tilde{Y}^{\theta}_{\tau}-Y^{\theta}_{t_{\theta}}|\leq C\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{a.s. on}~\{\tau\leq t_{\theta}\}\cap E_{\theta}.

This, together with (
4
) and (
4.31
), implies that, for a.e.
\omega\in\{\tau\leq t_{\theta}\}\cap E_{\theta}
,

\displaystyle|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{\tau}(\omega)-\phi(\omega)|\leq|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{\tau}(\omega)-Y^{\theta}_{t_{\theta}}|+|Y^{\theta}_{t_{\theta}}-\phi(\omega^{\theta})|+|\phi(\omega^{\theta})-\phi(\omega)|\leq C\varepsilon.

Note that
\{E_{\theta}\}
form a partition of
E
and
\mathbb{P}(E)=1
, then by (
4.30
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{\tau}-\phi|>C\varepsilon\big)\leq\sum_{\theta}\mathbb{P}\big(\{\tau>t_{\theta}\}\cap E_{\theta}\big)\leq\sum_{\theta}\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{P}(E_{\theta})=\varepsilon^{2}.

Note again that
Y^{\hat{\alpha}}
and
\phi
are bounded. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{\tau}-\phi|^{2}\big]\leq C\varepsilon^{2}+C\mathbb{P}\big(|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{\tau}-\phi|>C\varepsilon\big)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}.

(4.38)

Finally, note that
Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}=Y^{\tau,Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{\tau};\alpha}_{t}
,
0\leq t\leq\tau
. Then, by (
4.38
) and standard BSDE estimates we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}|Y^{\hat{\alpha}}_{t}-Y^{\tau,\phi;\alpha}_{t}|^{2}\Big]\leq C\varepsilon^{2}.

This clearly implies (
4.26
) and hence completes the proof.

5
Set valued HJB equations

We now derive the set valued HJB equation for
\mathbb{V}
from the DPP (
4.23
). Introduce the Hamiltonian: for
(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})^{m}
,
a\in A
,
\zeta\in(\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y))^{d}
,

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\quad{\cal K}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y,a,\zeta):=\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}\Big(\zeta^{\top}\partial_{x}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)\sigma(t,x,a)+\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{\top}\partial_{y}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)\zeta\Big){\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y);\\
\displaystyle\quad h^{0}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y,z,\gamma,a,\zeta):=zb(t,x,a)+\frac{1}{2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\sigma^{\top}\gamma\sigma(t,x,a))-{\cal K}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y,a,\zeta);\\
\displaystyle\quad h_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y,z,\gamma,a,\zeta):=h^{0}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y,z,\gamma,a,\zeta)+f\big(t,x,y,z\sigma(t,x,a)+\zeta,a\big);\\
\displaystyle\quad H_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y,z,\gamma):=\sup_{a\in A,\zeta\in(\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y))^{d}}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)\cdot h_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y,z,\gamma,a,\zeta);\end{array}\right.

where
\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\sigma^{\top}\gamma\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
i
-th component
\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}(\sigma^{\top}\gamma^{i}\sigma)
.
Then our set valued HJB equation takes the form:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}{\cal L}\mathbb{V}(t,x,y)=0,~\forall(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}},\quad\mbox{where}\\
{\cal L}\mathbb{V}(t,x,y):=\partial_{t}\mathbb{V}(t,x,y)\cdot{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)+H_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y,\partial_{x}\mathbb{V}(t,x,y),\partial_{xx}\mathbb{V}(t,x,y)).\end{array}\right.

Equivalently, by (
2.4
), (
2.5
), (
2.30
), (
2.31
), we may rewrite the above equation:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\nabla_{t}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}+\inf_{a\in A,\zeta\in(\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y))^{d}}\Big[\nabla_{x}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}\cdot b+{1\over 2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}\big(\sigma^{\top}\nabla_{xx}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}\sigma+2\zeta^{\top}\nabla_{xy}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}\sigma+\zeta^{\top}\nabla_{yy}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}\zeta\big)\\
\displaystyle-\nabla_{y}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}\cdot f(t,x,y,-\nabla_{y}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(\nabla_{x}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}})^{\top}\sigma+\zeta,a)\Big]=0,\quad(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}.\end{array}\right.

Remark 5.1

(i) For the scalar case as in Remark
4.1
, by
(
4.5
)
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{b}(t,x)=\{\underline{v}(t,x),\overline{v}(t,x)\},\quad{\bf n}(t,x,\underline{v}(t,x))=-1,\quad{\bf n}(t,x,\overline{v}(t,x))=1,

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,\underline{v}(t,x))=\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,\overline{v}(t,x))=0.

In the neighborhood of
y=\underline{v}(t,x)
, we have
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)=\underline{v}(t,x)-y
and
{\bf n}(t,x,\underline{v}(t,x))=-1
. Then (
5
) reduces to the standard HJB equation for
\underline{v}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{t}\underline{v}+\inf_{a\in A}\Big[\nabla_{x}\underline{v}\cdot b+{1\over 2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}\big(\sigma^{\top}\nabla_{xx}\underline{v}\sigma+f(t,x,\underline{v},(\nabla_{x}\underline{v})^{\top}\sigma,a)\Big]=0.

Similarly, in the neighborhood of
y=\overline{v}(t,x)
, we have
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)=y-\overline{v}(t,x)
and

{\bf n}(t,x,\overline{v}(t,x))=1
. Then (
5
) reduces to the standard HJB equation for
\overline{v}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{t}\overline{v}+\sup_{a\in A}\Big[\nabla_{x}\overline{v}\cdot b+{1\over 2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}\big(\sigma^{\top}\nabla_{xx}\overline{v}\sigma+f(t,x,\overline{v},(\nabla_{x}\overline{v})^{\top}\sigma,a)\Big]=0.

(ii) Although
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
is scalar, we emphasize that
(
5
)
holds true only on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
, and the set
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
is in turn determined by the solution
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
. So
(
5
)
is actually quite involved, and we can not apply the standard PDE theory on it.

(iii) It is clear that
\mathbb{V}(T,x)=\{g(x)\}
is degenerate, so we do not require the smoothness of
\mathbb{V}
at
T
. See Definition
2.9
and the paragraph above it.

Remark 5.2

Note that
{\cal K}_{\mathbb{V}}
relies on
\zeta
quadratically, and the space of
\zeta
is typically unbounded, so in general
H_{\mathbb{V}}
could blow up and then the set valued PDE is not well defined.

(i) In the scalar case:
m=1
, we have
\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,x,y)=\{0\}
for all
(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
. Then this issue is trivial.
Indeed, in this case the set valued PDE reduces back to the standard HJB equations, as we saw in Remark
5.1
(i).

(ii) For
m\geq 2
, recall Remark
2.7
(iii) that
\partial_{y}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}=\nabla_{yy}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
is symmetric and
0
is an eigenvalue with eigenvector
{\bf n}
. At any fixed
(t,x,y)\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
, let
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{m-1}
be the other eigenvalues. When
f
has linear growth in
z
, which is implied by the Lipschitz continuity, and
\lambda_{1}>0
, then clearly

. In this case
\mathbb{V}(t,x)
is strictly convex.

(iii) When
f
has linear growth in
z
, one may easily derive from
H_{\mathbb{V}}<\infty
that
\lambda_{1}\geq 0
. So, unfortunately, our classical solution
\mathbb{V}
has to be convex.
Thus one should explore appropriate notions of weak solutions in the noncovex case, which we will leave to future research.

(iv) When
f
has quadratic growth, this convexity is not required, as we will see in Example
5.3
below. We shall remark though such quadratic growth violates Assumption
4.4
, which is assumed for technical reasons and can be weakened.

Example 5.3

Consider a deterministic example where

and hence

are independent of

,

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{b}(t)=\Big\{{\cal Y}(t,\theta):\forall\theta\in[0,2\pi]\Big\},\quad\mbox{where}\\
\displaystyle{\cal Y}_{1}(t,\theta):=(T-t)\cos\theta,\quad{\cal Y}_{2}(t,\theta):=(T-t)\big[1+(T-t)^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\big]\sin\theta.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle d{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},Y^{\alpha,\delta}_{t})=\Lambda(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},Y^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},Z^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\alpha_{t})dt+\tilde{Z}^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}dB_{t},\quad\mbox{where}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Lambda:=\nabla_{t}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}+\nabla_{x}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}\cdot b-\nabla_{y}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}\cdot f+{1\over 2}\hbox{\rm tr$\,$}\big(\sigma^{\top}\nabla_{xx}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}\sigma+2z^{\top}\nabla_{xy}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}\sigma+z^{\top}\nabla_{yy}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}z\big);

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{Z}^{\alpha,\delta}:=\nabla_{x}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}\sigma+\nabla_{y}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}Z^{\alpha,\delta}.

Denote

\displaystyle\pi^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}:=\pi_{\mathbb{U}}(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},Y^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}),\quad\zeta^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}:=Z^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}-{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}^{\top}Z^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}.

Then, by (
3.3

is a classical solution of (

\displaystyle\Lambda(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},\pi^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\partial_{x}\mathbb{U}\sigma+\zeta^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\alpha_{t})=-{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}\cdot\Big[\partial_{t}\mathbb{U}+h_{\mathbb{U}}\big(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},\pi^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\partial_{x}\mathbb{U},\partial_{xx}\mathbb{U},\alpha_{t},\zeta^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}\big)\Big]\geq 0.

Note that, for appropriate processes
\tilde{b},\tilde{\sigma}

\displaystyle\Lambda(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},Y^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},Z^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\alpha_{t})-\Lambda(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},\pi^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\partial_{x}\mathbb{U}\sigma+\zeta^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\alpha_{t})=-\big[\tilde{b}_{t}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{t}\tilde{Z}^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}\big].

Here, due to the regularity of
\mathbb{U}\in C^{1,2}([0,T_{\delta}]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};{\cal D}^{m}_{2})
, as in (

C_{\delta}>0

\displaystyle|\tilde{b}_{t}|\leq C_{\delta}[1+|Z^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}|^{2}],\quad|\tilde{\sigma}_{t}|\leq C_{\delta}[1+|Z^{\alpha,\delta}_{t}|].

Then, for the
\tilde{\Gamma}
in (

\displaystyle d\Big(\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},Y^{\alpha,\delta}_{t})\Big)=\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}\Lambda(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},\pi^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\partial_{x}\mathbb{U}\sigma+\zeta^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\alpha_{t})dt+\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}(\tilde{Z}_{t}-{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}\tilde{\sigma}_{t})dB_{t}.

Since

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{\mathbb{U}}(0,x_{0},Y^{\alpha,\delta}_{0})=-\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T_{\delta}}\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}\Lambda(t,X^{\alpha}_{t},\pi^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\partial_{x}\mathbb{U}\sigma+\zeta^{\alpha,\delta}_{t},\alpha_{t})\Big]\leq 0.

That is,

. Send

Step 2.

Since

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}\cdot\tilde{I}^{\mathbb{U}}_{3}=0;\ \partial_{t}\mathbb{U}+h^{0}_{\mathbb{U}}(\cdot,\partial_{x}\mathbb{U},\partial_{xx}\mathbb{U},I^{\mathbb{U}}_{1},I^{\mathbb{U}}_{2})+I^{\mathbb{U}}_{3}=-f(\cdot,\partial_{x}\mathbb{U}\sigma(\cdot,I^{\mathbb{U}}_{1})+I^{\mathbb{U}}_{2},I^{\mathbb{U}}_{1}).

(6.14)

Since

Since

5

\displaystyle|\bar{\zeta}^{0}|\leq C_{\delta}\quad\mbox{and}\quad 0\leq-{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}\cdot\big[\partial_{t}\mathbb{U}+h_{\mathbb{U}}(\cdot,\partial_{x}\mathbb{U},\partial_{xx}\mathbb{U},\bar{a}_{0},\bar{\zeta}^{0})\big](0,x_{0},y_{0})<\delta.

(6.15)

Set
\tau_{0}:=0
,

, and define

\displaystyle X_{t}^{1}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X_{s}^{1},\alpha^{1}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s}^{1},\alpha^{1}_{s})dB_{s},\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{\delta}.

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\zeta^{1}_{t}(y):=\bar{\zeta}^{0}-{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}^{\top}\bar{\zeta}^{0}(t,X_{t}^{1},y),\quad\xi^{1}_{t}(y):=\tilde{\xi}^{1}_{t}(y)-[{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}\cdot\tilde{\xi}^{1}_{t}]{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(t,X^{1}_{t},y),\\

Then

\delta

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle{\Upsilon}_{t}^{1}=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Big[\partial_{t}\mathbb{U}+h^{0}_{\mathbb{U}}(\cdot,\partial_{x}\mathbb{U},\partial_{xx}\mathbb{U},\alpha^{1}_{s},\zeta^{1}_{s})+\xi^{1}\Big](s,X_{s}^{1},{\Upsilon}_{s}^{1})ds\\
\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\Big[\partial_{x}\mathbb{U}(s,X^{1}_{s},{\Upsilon}^{1}_{s})\sigma(s,X^{1}_{s},\alpha^{1}_{s})+\zeta^{1}_{s}({\Upsilon}_{s}^{1})\Big]dB_{s}.\end{array}\right.

By the Itô formula Theorem

,
{\Upsilon}^{1}_{t}\in\mathbb{U}_{b}(t,X^{1}_{t})
,
0\leq t\leq T_{\delta}
. Note that (

{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(0,X^{1}_{0},{\Upsilon}^{1}_{0})\cdot\tilde{\xi}^{1}_{0}({\Upsilon}^{1}_{0})\leq\varepsilon

. We then set

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tau_{1}:=\inf\big\{t>\tau_{0}:{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(t,X^{1}_{t},{\Upsilon}^{1}_{t})\cdot\tilde{\xi}^{1}_{t}({\Upsilon}^{1}_{t})\geq 2\delta\Big\}\wedge T_{\delta}.

Next, on
\{\tau_{1}<T_{\delta}\}
, by measurable selection theorem, there exist
{\cal F}_{\tau_{1}}

\bar{\alpha}^{1}_{\tau_{1}}\in A

\displaystyle|\bar{\zeta}^{1}_{\tau_{1}}|\leq C_{\delta}\quad\mbox{ and}\quad 0\leq-{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}\cdot\big[\partial_{t}\mathbb{U}+h_{\mathbb{U}}(\cdot,\partial_{x}\mathbb{U},\partial_{xx}\mathbb{U},\bar{\alpha}^{1}_{\tau_{1}},\bar{\zeta}^{1}_{\tau_{1}})\big](\tau_{1},X^{1}_{\tau_{1}},{\Upsilon}^{1}_{\tau_{1}})<\delta.

Set
\alpha^{2}_{t}\equiv\bar{\alpha}^{1}_{\tau_{1}}
,
\tau_{1}\leq t\leq T_{\delta}
, and define

\displaystyle X_{t}^{2}=X^{1}_{\tau_{1}}+\int_{\tau_{1}}^{t}b(s,X_{s}^{2},\alpha^{2}_{s})ds+\int_{\tau_{1}}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s}^{2},\alpha^{2}_{s})dB_{s},\quad\tau_{1}\leq t\leq T_{\delta}.

Similarly introduce, for
\tau_{1}\leq t\leq T_{\delta}
,

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\zeta^{2}_{t}(y):=\bar{\zeta}^{1}_{\tau_{1}}-{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}^{\top}\bar{\zeta}^{1}_{\tau_{1}}(t,X_{t}^{2},y),\quad\xi^{2}_{t}(y):=\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{t}(y)-[{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}\cdot\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{t}]{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(t,X^{2}_{t},y),\\

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle{\Upsilon}_{t}^{2}={\Upsilon}^{1}_{\tau_{1}}+\int_{\tau_{1}}^{t}\Big[\partial_{t}\mathbb{U}+h^{0}_{\mathbb{U}}(\cdot,\partial_{x}\mathbb{U},\partial_{xx}\mathbb{U},\alpha^{2}_{s},\zeta^{2}_{s})+\xi^{2}\Big](s,X_{s}^{2},{\Upsilon}_{s}^{2})ds\\

,

\displaystyle\tau_{2}:=\inf\big\{t>\tau_{1}:{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(t,X^{2}_{t},{\Upsilon}^{2}_{t})\cdot\tilde{\xi}^{2}_{t}({\Upsilon}^{2}_{t})\geq 2\delta\Big\}\wedge T_{\delta}.

Repeat the arguments, we obtain a sequence

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(\tau_{n},X_{\tau_{n}},{\Upsilon}_{\tau_{n}})\cdot\tilde{\xi}^{n}_{\tau_{n}}({\Upsilon}_{\tau_{n}})\leq\delta,\quad{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(\tau_{n+1},X_{\tau_{n+1}},{\Upsilon}_{\tau_{n+1}})\cdot\tilde{\xi}^{n}_{\tau_{n+1}}({\Upsilon}_{\tau_{n+1}})=2\delta,\quad\forall n.

\displaystyle\delta\mathbb{P}(E_{\delta})\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(\tau_{n+1},X_{\tau_{n+1}},{\Upsilon}_{\tau_{n+1}})\cdot\tilde{\xi}^{n}_{\tau_{n+1}}({\Upsilon}_{\tau_{n+1}})-{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(\tau_{n},X_{\tau_{n}},{\Upsilon}_{\tau_{n}})\cdot\tilde{\xi}^{n}_{\tau_{n}}({\Upsilon}_{\tau_{n}})\Big|\Big].

We now define

\displaystyle(\alpha_{t},\zeta_{t},\tilde{\xi}_{t},\xi_{t},X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t}):=(\alpha^{n}_{t},\zeta^{n}_{t},X^{n}_{t},{\Upsilon}^{n}_{t},\xi^{n}_{t}),\quad t\in[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1}),n=0,1,\cdots.

Note that

Define

\displaystyle Z_{t}:=\partial_{x}\mathbb{U}(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t})\sigma(t,X_{t},\alpha_{t})+\zeta_{t}({\Upsilon}_{t}),\quad\eta_{t}:=[{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}\cdot\tilde{\xi}_{t}]{\bf n}_{\mathbb{U}}(t,X_{t},{\Upsilon}_{t}),\quad 0\leq t<T_{\delta}.

Then,

\displaystyle{\Upsilon}_{t}=y_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\Big[f(s,X_{s},{\Upsilon}_{s},Z_{s},\alpha_{s})+\eta_{s}\Big]ds+\int_{0}^{t}Z_{s}dB_{s},\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{\delta}.

Equivalently, we may rewrite it backwardly:

\displaystyle{\Upsilon}_{t}={\Upsilon}_{T_{\delta}}+\int_{t}^{T_{\delta}}\Big[f(s,X_{s},{\Upsilon}_{s},Z_{s},\alpha_{s})+\eta_{s}\Big]ds-\int_{t}^{T_{\delta}}Z_{s}dB_{s},\quad 0\leq t\leq T_{\delta}.

Compare this with (
4.22

\displaystyle\big|y_{0}-Y^{T_{\delta},{\Upsilon}_{T_{\delta}};0,x_{0},\alpha}_{0}\big|^{2}=\big|{\Upsilon}_{0}-Y^{T_{\delta},{\Upsilon}_{T_{\delta}};0,x_{0},\alpha}_{0}\big|^{2}\leq C\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T_{\delta}}|\eta_{s}|^{2}ds\Big]\leq C\delta^{2}.

(6.22)

Finally, fix an arbitrary
a_{*}\in A
, and extend

with
\alpha_{t}\equiv a_{*}
,

, by Assumption

, similarly to (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|Y^{\alpha}_{T_{\delta}}-{\Upsilon}_{T_{\delta}}|^{2}\big]\leq C\mathbb{E}\Big[|Y^{\alpha}_{T_{\delta}}-g(X^{\alpha}_{T})|^{2}+|g(X^{\alpha}_{T})-g(X^{\alpha}_{T_{\delta}})|^{2}+|{\Upsilon}_{T_{\delta}}-g(X^{\alpha}_{T_{\delta}})|^{2}\Big]\leq\rho(\delta),

\displaystyle\big|Y^{T_{\delta},{\Upsilon}_{T_{\delta}};0,x_{0},\alpha}_{0}-Y^{\alpha}_{0}\big|^{2}=\big|Y^{T_{\delta},{\Upsilon}_{T_{\delta}};0,x_{0},\alpha}_{0}-Y^{T_{\delta},Y^{\alpha}_{T_{\delta}};0,x_{0},\alpha}_{0}\big|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\big[|Y^{\alpha}_{T_{\delta}}-{\Upsilon}_{T_{\delta}}|^{2}\big]\leq\rho(\delta).

\displaystyle|y_{0}-Y^{\alpha}_{0}|\leq C\delta+\sqrt{\rho(\delta)}.

Since

7
An application: the moving scalarization

Recall Remark
4.2
, in particular (
4.2
) and (
4.12
) for the mean variance optimization problem. This problem is time inconsistent in the following sense. Consider the general setting (
4
) and (
4.4
). Given
(0,x_{0})
and
\varphi\in C(\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R})
, let
\alpha^{*}_{[0,T]}
be an optimal control for the problem

\displaystyle V_{0}:=\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}_{0}}\varphi(Y^{0,x_{0},\alpha}_{0})=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{V}(0,x_{0})}\varphi(y).

(7.1)

If we follow
\alpha^{*}
on
[0,t]
and denote
X^{*}_{t}:=X^{0,x_{0},\alpha^{*}}_{t}
. Then
\alpha^{*}_{[t,T]}
is not optimal for the optimization problem at
t
: by using the notations heuristically,

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha_{[t,T]}}\varphi\big(Y^{t,X^{*}_{t},\alpha_{[t,T]}}_{t}\big)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{V}(t,X^{*}_{t})}\varphi(y).

It was proposed in
[
15
]
to find a so called dynamic utility function
\Phi(t,{\bf x}_{[0,t]},y)
such that
\Phi(0,x_{0},y)=\varphi(y)
and
\alpha^{*}_{[t,T]}
remains optimal for the alternative optimization problem

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha_{[t,T]}}\Phi\Big(t,X^{*}_{[0,t]},Y^{t,X^{*}_{t},\alpha_{[t,T]}}_{t}\Big)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{V}(t,X^{*}_{t})}\Phi\big(t,X^{*}_{[0,t]},y\big).

(7.2)

In Subsection
7.2
below we will find such an
\Phi
for the mean variance problem explicitly. In the next subsection we first consider the case that
\varphi
is linear.

7.1
The linear scalarization

When
\varphi
is linear:
\varphi(y)=\lambda_{0}\cdot y
for some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we require
\Phi
to be linear as well:
\Phi(t,{\bf x}_{[0,t]},y)=\Lambda(t,{\bf x}_{[0,t]})\cdot y
. This
\Lambda
is exactly the moving scalarization proposed in
[
9
]
. That is, we want to find
\Lambda
such that
\Lambda(0,x_{0})=\lambda_{0}
and
\alpha^{*}_{[t,T]}
is optimal for the problem:

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha_{[t,T]}}\Lambda(t,X^{*}_{[0,t]})\cdot Y^{t,X^{*}_{t},\alpha_{[t,T]}}_{t}=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{V}(t,X^{*}_{t})}\Lambda(t,X^{*}_{[0,t]})\cdot y.

(7.3)

Our set valued HJB equation provides a solution to this interesting problem, provided that (
5
) is wellposed in the sense of Theorem
6.1
(ii) and
\mathbb{V}(t,x)
is strictly convex. Consider a slightly more general setting by letting
\lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be such that
\lambda(x_{0})=\lambda_{0}
. Assume without loss of generality that
|\lambda(x)|=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since
\mathbb{V}(0,x)
is compact and strict convex, we may find a unique optimal argument
y_{\lambda}(x)\in\mathbb{V}_{b}(0,x)
for the problem:
V(0,x):=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{V}(0,x)}\lambda(x)\cdot y
. Recalling
\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{V}
, we construct
X^{*},{\Upsilon}^{*},\alpha^{*}
as in Theorem
6.1
(ii) with initial data
(0,x,y_{\lambda}(x))\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}
. Assume further that
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is nondegenerate, then as in Remark
6.3
(ii)
{\Upsilon}^{*}
is
\mathbb{F}^{X^{*}}
-progressively measurable and hence there exists
\Lambda
such that

\displaystyle\Lambda(t,X^{*}_{[0,t]})={\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(t,X^{*}_{t},{\Upsilon}^{*}_{t}).

(7.4)

We argue that this
\Lambda
is a desired moving scalarization.

First, since
\lambda(x)\cdot y_{\lambda}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{V}(0,x)}\lambda(x)\cdot y
and
|\lambda(x)|=1
, we see that

\displaystyle\lambda(x)={\bf n}_{\mathbb{V}}(0,x,y_{\lambda}(x))=\Lambda(0,x).

Next, from the construction in Theorem
6.1
(ii), it is clear that
{\Upsilon}^{*}_{t}=Y^{t,X^{*}_{t},\alpha^{*}_{[t,T]}}_{t}
. Then, since
\mathbb{V}(t,X^{*}_{t})
is convex, by (
7.4
) we see that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Lambda(t,X^{*}_{[0,t]})\cdot Y^{t,X^{*}_{t},\alpha^{*}_{[t,T]}}_{t}=\Lambda(t,X^{*}_{[0,t]})\cdot{\Upsilon}^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{V}(t,X^{*}_{t})}\Lambda(t,X^{*}_{[0,t]})\cdot y=\sup_{\alpha_{[t,T]}}\Lambda(t,X^{*}_{[0,t]})\cdot Y^{t,X^{*}_{t},\alpha_{[t,T]}}_{t}.

This exactly means
\alpha^{*}_{[t,T]}
is an optimal control for the dynamic optimization problem (
7.3
).

We remark that the mapping
\Lambda:[0,T]\times C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which is path dependent in an adapted way, is time consistent in the following sense. Consider the problem at time
0
with initial condition
(x,\lambda)
. Let
X^{*}
and
\Lambda
be as above, but denoted as
X^{0,x,\lambda,*}
and
\Lambda^{0,\lambda}
to indicate their dependence on the initial conditions. Now fix
0<t<T
, consider the problem on
[t,T]
with initial condition

and
\Lambda^{0,\lambda}(t,\cdot)
, we can easily see that the moving scalarization we find following the same procedure coincides with the original
\Lambda
found at time
0
:

\displaystyle X^{t,X^{0,x,\lambda,*}_{[0,t]},\Lambda^{0,\lambda}(t,\cdot)}_{s}=X^{0,x,\lambda,*}_{s},\quad\Lambda^{t,\Lambda^{0,\lambda}(t,\cdot)}(s,\cdot)=\Lambda^{0,\lambda}(s,\cdot),\quad t\leq s\leq T.

Remark 7.1

When
\mathbb{V}(t,X^{*}_{t})
is nonconvex, as in Example
5.3
, the
\Lambda
in
(

)

In the contexts of stochastic target problem,

We first note that, the stochastic target problem (

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(x)=[x-1,x+1]\subset\mathbb{R},\quad\widehat{\mathbb{V}}:=\{(x,y):x\in\mathbb{R},y\in[x-1,x+1]\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Clearly
\mathbb{V}_{b}(x)=\{x-1,x+1\}
. One can easily verify that,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)=y-(x+1),\quad\mbox{for}~y\approx x+1;\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}(x,y)=(x-1)-y,\quad\mbox{for}~y\approx x-1;

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\bf r}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}(x,y)={y-(x+1)\over\sqrt{2}},\quad\mbox{for}~y\approx x+1;\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\bf r}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}(x,y)={(x-1)-y\over\sqrt{2}},\quad\mbox{for}~y\approx x-1.

We also observe directly from above that, although

on

, their derivatives are in general not equal.

Consequently, although both (

) and (

) characterize the same set
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{V}}=\mathbb{G}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}
, the two equations are fundamentally different. This is partially explained by the above observation that
{\bf r}_{\mathbb{V}}
and
{\bf r}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}
have different derivatives on

. At below we provide more detailed calculation for the set valued heat equation in Example

(ii), but with

.

Example 8.3

Set

,

,
\sigma=1
,

, and the terminal

where

We shall prove in Appendix that

satisfies the following equation:

\displaystyle\nabla_{t}\widehat{\bf r}+{1\over 2}\Big[\nabla_{xx}\widehat{\bf r}-2\nabla_{xy}\widehat{\bf r}{\nabla_{x}\widehat{\bf r}\over\nabla_{y}\widehat{\bf r}}+\nabla_{yy}\widehat{\bf r}\big|{\nabla_{x}\widehat{\bf r}\over\nabla_{y}\widehat{\bf r}}\big|^{2}\Big]=0,\quad\mbox{on}~\mathbb{G}_{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}}.

Another advantage of (

Finally, we remark that

8.3
Comparison with Ararat-Ma-Wu
[
1
]

Mainly motivated by dynamic set valued risk measures for multi-asset or network-based financial models,
[
1
]
studied the following set valued BSDE:

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}_{t}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{G}(B_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}\mathbb{F}(s,B_{s},\mathbb{Y}_{s})ds\big|{\cal F}_{t}\Big].

(8.14)

Here, denoting by
{\cal D}^{m}_{cc}
the space of convex compact sets
\mathbb{D}\in{\cal D}^{m}_{0}
, the terminal
\mathbb{G}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to{\cal D}^{m}_{cc}
, and the driver
\mathbb{F}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{\cal D}^{m}_{cc}\to{\cal D}^{m}_{cc}
(abusing the notation
\mathbb{F}
here). We note that
[
1
]
actually allows
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{F}
to depend on the paths of
B
. By relying on the sophisticated set valued stochastic analysis, especially the Hukuhara difference,
[
1
]
established the wellposedness of the above set valued BSDE. The general case that
\mathbb{F}
depends on
\mathbb{Z}
, and the martingale representation with the term
\mathbb{Z}_{t}dB_{t}
seems to be a quite remote goal.

Formally, the set valued BSDE (
8.14
) is associated with our set valued HJB equation (
5
) in the case
x_{0}=0
,
b=0
,
\sigma=0
,
f=f(t,x,y,a)
. Then
X_{t}=B_{t}
, and we may naturally define

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}_{t}:=\mathbb{V}(t,B_{t}),\quad\mathbb{F}(t,x,\mathbb{D}):=\big\{f(t,x,y,a):y\in\mathbb{D},a\in A\big\}.

(8.15)

In the linear case:
f=f(t,x,a)
and thus
\mathbb{F}(t,x)=\big\{f(t,x,a):a\in A\big\}
, the random set valued process
\mathbb{Y}_{t}:=\mathbb{V}(t,B_{t})
indeed satisfies (
8.14
) in the sense of
[
1
]
.

However, when
f
depends on
y
, the
\mathbb{Y},\mathbb{F}
in (
8.15
) do not satisfy (
8.14
). That is, (
8.14
) is not the stochastic counterpart of (
5
). The reason is the same as in Remark
2.3
(ii). In (
4
), the
Y
in the left side and that in the right side of the BSDE are required to be the same process. In (
8.14
), however, one allows to consider different selectors for the
\mathbb{Y}
in the left side and that in the right side of the equation.
