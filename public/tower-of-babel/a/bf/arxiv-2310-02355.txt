Reasoning about Intuitionistic Computation Tree Logic
Davide Catta, Vadim Malvone, Aniello Murano

Abstract
In this paper, we define an intuitionistic version of Computation Tree Logic. After explaining the semantic features of intuitionistic logic, we examine how these characteristics can be interesting for formal verification purposes. Subsequently, we define the syntax and semantics of our intuitionistic version of CTL and study some simple properties of the so obtained logic. We conclude by demonstrating that some fixed-point axioms of CTL are not valid in the intuitionistic version of CTL we have defined.



1
Introduction

Classical modal and temporal logics are extensions of classical logic, in which some new operators (usually called modalities) qualify the truth of classical formulae. For instance, in a classical or temporal modal logic, one can express that a certain formula is
necessarily
true,
possibly
true, that
it will be true
in some future moment of time and so on. In particular, temporal logics are a family of modal logics in which the modalities permit to express, as the name suggests, temporal properties of formulae. Temporal logics originated in the philosophical work of Prior
[
12
]
in the 50s and were rediscovered and adapted by Pnueli
[
11
]
who defined the Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) and showed how interesting program properties could be expressed using temporal logic and, more importantly, automatically verified on mathematical models of the executions of such programs. Time flow in LTL is linear, meaning that each instant of time has exactly one successor.
In 1981, Edmund M. Clarke and E. Allen Emerson first introduced Computation Tree Logic (CTL)
[
5
]
. CTL is a type of branching-time logic, where time is represented as a tree-like structure with an undetermined future. This logic was first used to reason about abstractions of concurrent programs and subsequently became a milestone in the automatic verification of cyber-physical models.

Intuitionistic Logic.

Intuitionism is a mathematical school developed in the early 1900s by the Dutch mathematician L.E.J. Brouwer. Intuitionism rejects the idea that the truth value of a mathematical statement is independent of our ability to
know or verify
it. Put differently: an intuitionist believes that the truth conditions of a mathematical statement are its provability conditions. As a result, intuitionism rejects the validity of the principle of excluded middle. In fact, given any mathematical statement
\varphi
, it is not always possible to have knowledge of (i.e., prove or verify)
\varphi
or its negation
\neg\varphi
.
In the 1930s, Heyting developed intuitionistic logic
[
8
]
, a logic embodying the underlying principles of intuitionistic reasoning. Intuitionistic logic has found many applications in computer science, e.g., the Curry-Howard isomorphism relating intuitionistic proofs and typed lambda terms
[
14
]
and the constructive type theory of Per Martin-Löf
[
9
]
. The semantics of intuitionistic logic was first specified by means of topological spaces, and later, by Saul Kripke in terms of Kripke models
[
7
]
. This latter semantics is particularly interesting for our means. Kripke’s idea is that a model of intuitionistic logic represents the dynamics of an
idealized agent
(or idealized mathematician) that is expanding her knowledge about mathematical statements over time. In this temporal process, she creates new elements while observing the fundamental facts in her universe. Moving from one moment to the next, she freely decides how to continue her activity, resulting in a partially ordered set of possible stages, known as possible worlds. In this particular interpretation, the truth of an intuitionistic formula
\varphi
in a given moment of time
w
depends upon the future of
w
, i.e, upon the moments of time coming after
w
. For instance, to conclude that a formula
\varphi\to\psi
is true at a given moment
w
, the agent must be certain that for every future moment
w^{\prime}
, if there is a proof of
\varphi
at
w^{\prime}
, it is always possible to obtain a proof of
\psi
as well.

Intuitionistic Modal Logic.

Intuitionistic logic can be extended with modalities in different ways (for an overview see
[
13
]
):
while in classical logic axioms involving only
\Box
provide also description of the behavior of
\Diamond
, for intuitionistic logic this is no more the case since the duality of the two modalities does not hold anymore.
This leads to different approaches.

Constructive modal logics
consider minimal sets of axioms to guarantee the definition of the behaviors of the
\Box
and
\Diamond
modalities.
A second approach, referred to as
intuitionistic modal logic
, considers additional axioms in order to validate the Gödel-Gentzen translation
[
4
]
. This second approach has led to the definition of a class of Kripke models (called birelational models) in which two distinct relations of accessibility are considered: one representing the aforementioned preorder and the other representing the “standard” accessibility relation of a Kripke model. In this paper, for the sake of simplicity, we will follow this second approach. This approach is used, for instance, for the intuitionistic version of LTL studied in
[
3
]
.

Intuitionistic Computational Tree Logic.

In this paper, we aim to define an intuitionistic version of the aforementioned Computation Tree Logic. The intuition we seek to formalize is as follows: we distinguish between two different temporal evolutions within a CTL model. One represents the agent’s knowledge about the system, while the other represents the possible evolutions of the system itself based on the given knowledge. The agent’s goal is to conclusively verify that certain properties hold with respect to the possible evolutions of the model and the potential evolution of its knowledge. In other words, given a property of interest
\varphi
, it wants to ascertain that, in any state of its knowledge regarding the model, and regardless of the evolution of the model itself,
\varphi
is satisfied. We think this type of intuition allows for considering the verification of CTL properties in the imperfect information context.

Structure of the work

In Section
2
we provide the syntax and semantic of Intuitionistic Computation Tree Logic. Then, in Section
3
we provide some properties of our logic. Finally, we conclude in Section
4
with some future directions.

2
Syntax and Semantics of Intuitionistic Computation Tree Logic

In this section we provide the syntax and semantics of our logic. We follow
[
10
]
for the definition of models that will be used in the following.
We fix a countable set
\mathcal{P}
of
atomic propositions
or
atoms
.

Definition 1
.

Formulae of Intuitionistic Computation Tree Logic (ICTL for short) are defined by the following grammar:

\varphi,\psi:=p\mid\bot\mid\varphi\land\psi\mid\varphi\vee\psi\mid\varphi\to\psi

\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\varphi\mid\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)\mid\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{R}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)\mid\mathsf{A}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\varphi\mid\mathsf{A}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)\mid\mathsf{A}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{R}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)

where
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
\bot
is the absurdity symbol. We define the negation of a formula
\varphi
as
\neg\varphi\equiv\varphi\to\bot
. Formulae whose first operator is
\mathsf{E}\,
are called
existential
formulae, while those whose first operator is
\mathsf{A}\,
are called
universal
formulae.

Given the syntax of ICTL, we can now provide the definition of birelational frame.

.

A
birelational frame

\langle W,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}\rangle

W

x\in W

A
path
in a frame
\mathfrak{F}
is an infinite sequence of worlds
\rho=\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\ldots
such that for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
\rho_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}\rho_{i+1}
. If
\rho
is a path then
\rho_{i}
denotes its
(i+1)
-th element,
\rho_{\leq i}
the finite prefix
\rho_{0},\ldots,\rho_{i}
of
\rho
and
\rho_{\geq i}
the infinite suffix
\rho_{i},\rho_{i+1},\ldots
of
\rho
starting at
\rho_{i}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathfrak{F}
be a frame and
w
and
w^{\prime}
two worlds of
\mathfrak{F}
such that
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
. For every path
\rho
such that
\rho_{0}=w
there is a path
\tau
such that
\tau_{0}=w^{\prime}
for which holds that
\rho_{i}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\tau_{i}
for every natural number
i
.

Proof.

Let
A=(w_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an enumeration of the worlds of
\mathfrak{F}
. Given
\rho
, we define
\tau
by induction on
\mathbb{N}
. We define
\tau_{0}=w^{\prime}
and for any
i\geq 1
we let
\tau_{i}
be the smallest element in
A
such that
\tau_{i-1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}\tau_{i}
and
\tau_{i}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rho_{i}
. Remark that
\tau_{i}
exists because of condition
\mathsf{C}_{2}
above: in fact, suppose that for
j<i
it holds that
\rho_{j}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\tau_{j}
, in particular, this means that
\tau_{i-1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rho_{i-1}
. Since
\rho_{i-1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}\rho_{i}
, then by
\mathsf{C}_{2}
there is (at least one and possibly an infinite countable number of)
u\in W
such that
\tau_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}u
and
\rho_{i}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}u
.
∎

Given the definition of frames, we are able to define our models.

Definition 3
.

A
birelational model
(model from now on) is a tuple
\mathfrak{M}=\langle W,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R},\mathcal{V}\rangle

\langle W,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}\rangle

\mathcal{V}:W\to 2^{\mathcal{P}}

w
and that is subject to the
monotonicity condition
, that is: if
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
then
\mathcal{V}(w)\subseteq\mathcal{V}(w^{\prime})
.

\mathfrak{M},\rho_{i}\models\theta
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
or there is a
j\geq 0
such that
\mathfrak{M},\rho_{j}\models\psi
and
\mathfrak{M},\rho_{i}\models\theta
for all
0\leq i\leq j
.

We write
\mathfrak{M},w\not\models\varphi
when
w
does not satisfy
\varphi
. We say that a formula
\varphi
is
valid in a model

\mathfrak{M}
iff it is satisfied in every
w\in W
. A formula
\varphi
is
valid in a frame

\mathfrak{F}=\langle W,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}\rangle
iff it is valid in
\langle W,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R},\mathcal{V}\rangle
for any valuation
\mathcal{V}
. A formula
\varphi
is
valid
iff it is valid in every frame.

Remark that, by the above definition, a formula
\neg\varphi=\varphi\to\bot
is satisfied at
w
if for any
w^{\prime}
such that
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
we have that
w^{\prime}
satisfies
\varphi
implies
w^{\prime}
satisfies
\bot
. Since
\bot
is
never
satisfied, this is equivalent to say that
no state

w^{\prime}
bigger than
w
satisfies
\varphi
. Given the above definition of satisfaction and validity, it is fairly easy to show that the operators are not dual, e.g., we do not have that
\neg\mathsf{A}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\neg\varphi\to\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\varphi
.

3
Main Properties

In this section, we show that CTL and ICTL differs in some important properties. First, we show that the satisfaction relation is monotonous with respect to the preorder. This properties, that is shared by all intuitionist modal logics, intuitively says that the agent’s knowledge can only grow.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a model,
\varphi
a formula, and
w
and
w^{\prime}
a pair of worlds of
\mathfrak{M}
. if
\mathfrak{M},w\models\varphi
and
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
then
\mathfrak{M},w^{\prime}\models\varphi

Proof.

By induction on the structure of
\varphi
using Lemma
1
when we consider existential formulae.
∎

Given a model
\mathfrak{M}
and a formula
\varphi
, we write
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}
to denote the set of worlds of
\mathfrak{M}
satisfying
\varphi
, that is
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}=\{w\in W\mid\mathfrak{M},w\models\varphi\}
. Whenever the model
\mathfrak{M}
is contextually given and no confusion can arise, we omit the superscript
\mathfrak{M}
.
If
w
is a world of
\mathfrak{M}
, we denote by
w^{\uparrow}
the set of worlds that are greater of
w
with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
.
Given
X\subseteq W
, we let
Pre^{\exists}(X)=\{w^{\prime}\mid w^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}w\text{ for some }w\in X\}
and
Pre^{\forall}(X)=\{w^{\prime}\mid w^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}w\text{ implies }w\in X\}
. If
Y\subseteq W
is a set then
Y^{\uparrow}
denotes the set of elements of
w
whose upward closure is in
Y
, that is
Y^{\uparrow}=\{w\in W\mid w^{\uparrow}\subseteq Y\}
. If
Y
is a set
Y^{\mathbf{c}}
denotes its complement.

Proposition 2
.

Given a model
\mathfrak{M}
and a world
w
, we have that:

1.

\mathfrak{M},w\models\varphi\to\psi
iff
w\in(\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathbf{c}}\cup\llbracket\psi\rrbracket)^{\uparrow}

2.

\mathfrak{M},w\models\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\varphi
iff
w\in Pre^{\exists}(\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket)

3.

\mathfrak{M},w\models\mathsf{A}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\varphi
iff
w\in(Pre^{\forall}(\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket))^{\uparrow}

Proof.

We only prove (1) and (3).

1.

For the
(\to)
-direction we reason by contraposition: suppose that there is
w^{\prime}
such that
w^{\prime}\in\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket\cap\llbracket\psi\rrbracket^{\mathbf{c}}
and
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
. This means that
\mathfrak{M},w^{\prime}\models\varphi
and
\mathfrak{M},w^{\prime}\not\models\psi
thus we conclude that
\mathfrak{M},w\not\models\varphi\to\psi
. For the converse direction: suppose that
w\in(\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathbf{c}}\cup\llbracket\psi\rrbracket)^{\uparrow}
. Thus given any
w^{\prime}
such that
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
we have that
w^{\prime}\in\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathbf{c}}
or
w^{\prime}\in\llbracket\psi\rrbracket
. From this fact we deduce that
w^{\prime}\in\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket
(that is
w\notin\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket^{\mathbf{c}}
) implies
w^{\prime}\in\llbracket\psi\rrbracket
and we can conclude.

3.

For the
(\to)
-direction: suppose that
\mathfrak{M},w\models\mathsf{A}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\varphi
. By definition, this means that given any
w^{\prime}
such that
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
all successors of
w^{\prime}

are in
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket
. This proves that
w^{\uparrow}\subseteq Pre^{\forall}(\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket)
and thus
w\in(Pre^{\forall}(\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket))^{\uparrow}
. For the converse direction, suppose that
s\in(Pre^{\forall}(\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket))^{\uparrow}
. This means given any
w^{\prime}
such that
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
we have that all succesors of
w^{\prime}
are in
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket
. Thus any path starting at the given
w^{\prime}
will satisfy
\varphi
on its second component. We thus deduce that
\mathfrak{M},w\models\mathsf{A}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\varphi
.

∎

Proposition 3
.

Define
\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi
as
(\varphi\to\psi)\land(\psi\to\varphi)
. The following formulae are valid:

1.

\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)\leftrightarrow\psi\vee(\varphi\land\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi))

2.

\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{R}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)\leftrightarrow\psi\land(\varphi\vee\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{R}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi))

3.

\psi\vee(\varphi\land\mathsf{A}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{A}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi))\to\mathsf{A}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)

4.

\psi\land(\varphi\vee\mathsf{A}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{A}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{R}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi))\to\mathsf{A}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{R}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)

Proof.

We prove (
2
) and (
3
). Let
\mathfrak{M}
be any model and
w
any of its worlds.

2.

For the
(\to)
-direction, suppose that
\mathfrak{M},w\models\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)
and let
w^{\prime}
be a world such that
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
. We must check that
w^{\prime}\models\psi\vee(\varphi\land\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi))

w
satisfies
\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)

w\in\llbracket\psi\rrbracket
(in this case we conclude by Proposition

w\in\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket
and there is a path
\rho

\rho_{0}=w

\rho_{i}\in\llbracket\psi\rrbracket

\rho_{j}\in\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket

w\in\llbracket\psi\vee(\varphi\land\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)\rrbracket
and, again by Proposition

w^{\prime}\in\llbracket\psi\vee(\varphi\land(\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi))\rrbracket
.
For the converse direction: suppose that
w\in\llbracket\psi\vee(\varphi\land\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)\rrbracket
and let
w^{\prime}
be a world such that
w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}
. Since
w\in\llbracket\varphi\land(\psi\vee\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi))\rrbracket
either
w\in\llbracket\psi\rrbracket
or
w\in\llbracket\varphi\land\mathsf{E}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{E}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi)\rrbracket
. In both cases, we deduce that

and we conclude using Proposition
1
.

3.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{M},w\models\psi\vee(\varphi\land\mathsf{A}\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{X}}\mkern 1.0mu\mathsf{A}\,(\varphi\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\mathsf{U}}\mkern 1.0mu\psi))
and let

be a world such that

w{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}w^{\prime}

1
allows us to conclude that

and since given any

4
Conclusions and Future Works

We have sketched an Intuitionistic variant of CTL (ICTL) and proved some basic properties about this logic. There is still a lot, practically everything, to be done about this logic. Below, we outline some directions we would like to explore, without any specific hierarchical order.

Formal verification
.
In addition to its purely theoretical interest, the study of CTL has been fundamental for the development of applications in formal software verification. This is because Kripke models on which this logic is interpreted are particularly suitable for modeling the evolution of reactive systems. In order to make ICTL appealing, we would like to identify a class of reactive systems, or specific problems within these systems, that lend themselves well to being modeled using birelationals models.

Model Checking
. The model checking problem for ICTL is the same as the one of CTL: given a (finite) birelational model
\mathfrak{M}
, a formula
\varphi
, and a world
w
can we decide whether
\mathfrak{M},w\models\varphi
? This problem is P-space hard. For instance, given a CTL model
\mathfrak{R}
one can see it as a birelational model by setting
u{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}u^{\prime}
iff
u=u^{\prime}
for any world of
\mathfrak{R}
, and thus reduce the model-checking problem for CTL to the one of ICTL. Furthermore, even though the CTL fix points axioms do not hold in ICTL, we think we can characterize the semantics of the release and until operators by the upward-closure of the usual (classical) fix-point characterization.

Axiomatization & fix-points
. The fix-point axioms are not valid in the intuitionistic variant of CTL that we have defined. It would be interesting to find out whether an axiomatization of ICTL can be obtained by other means. Possibly, one could think of adding another condition on birelational models in order to validate the axioms. For instance the following, for all
x,y,z\in W
: if
x{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}y
and
y{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}z
then there exist an
u\in W
such that
x{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}R}u
and
