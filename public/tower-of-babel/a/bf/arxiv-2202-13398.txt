Topological theories and automata
Mee Seong Im and Mikhail Khovanov

Abstract
. The paper explains the connection between topological theories for one-manifolds with defects and values in the Boolean semiring and automata and their generalizations. Finite state automata are closely related to regular languages. To each pair of a regular language and a circular regular language we associate a topological theory for one-dimensional manifolds with zero-dimensional defects labelled by letters of the language. This theory takes values in the Boolean semiring. Universal construction of topological theories gives rise in this case to a monoidal category of Boolean semilinear combinations of one-dimensional cobordisms with defects modulo skein relations. The latter category can be interpreted as a semilinear rigid monoidal closure of standard structures associated to a regular language, including minimal deterministic and nondeterministic finite state automata for the language and the syntactic monoid. The circular language plays the role of a regularizer, allowing to define the rigid closure of these structures. When the state space of a single point for a regular language describes a distributive lattice, there is a unique associated circular language such that the resulting theory is a Boolean TQFT.

Remark 1.1
.

In symmetric monoidal categories, rigidity is simplified compared to just monoidal categories. Universal construction can also be done in rigid monoidal categories that are not necessarily symmetric, see
[
44
]
for examples.

Categories of (decorated) cobordisms provide examples of rigid symmetric monoidal categories.
Assume that we are given a category
\mathcal{C}
of
n
-dimensional cobordisms between
(n-1)
-dimensional objects. These objects may be manifolds, embedded or decorated manifolds, CW-complexes, and so on. This category is monoidal and often symmetric monoidal, with the tensor product denoted by

1.3.
Semiring-valued universal construction

In this paper we discuss topological theories when
\alpha
takes values in a commutative semiring rather than a ring. A commutative semiring

In the present paper we concentrate on the case

Correspondingly, one-dimensional cobordisms may have components (intervals) with one of both endpoints strictly inside the cobordism (

-valued topological theories with

In Section
2
we review the classical notions of a regular language, deterministic and non-deterministic finite automata, and also consider the notion of
circular automata
, for cyclicly invariant languages (
circular languages
). Such languages naturally come out from our interpretation of languages as
\mathbb{B}
-valued evaluations of one-dimensional manifolds with defects. Evaluations of intervals with defects correspond to languages, while those of circles with defects to
circular languages
. Evaluation
\alpha
of arbitrary decorated one-manifolds requires a pair of languages: language

for evaluation of decorated intervals (the

of
\alpha
) and a
circular
language

for evaluation of decorated circles (the

of
\alpha
),

(17)

\alpha\ =\ (\alpha_{I},\alpha_{\circ})\ =\ (L_{I},L_{\circ}).

Here

denotes a language, that is, a subset of the set of all words

(18)

\Sigma^{\ast}\ :=\ \sqcup_{n\geq 0}\{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\,|\,a_{i}\in\Sigma\}

in the alphabet
\Sigma
. Languages are in a bijection with Boolean functions
\alpha_{I}:\Sigma^{\ast}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
, where
L_{I}=\alpha_{I}^{-1}(1)
. Likewise,

denotes a circular language, for a map

If a circular language contains a word

), and the set

Section

\mathbb{B}

Going back to the general case or regular languages

In Section

This section contains a brief review of regular languages and finite state automata, including deterministic finite automata (DFA) and nondeterministic finite automata (NFA). We also suggest how to define

that accept regular

2.1.
Regular languages

Denote by
\Sigma
a finite set (the set of
letters
) and by
\Sigma^{\ast}=\varnothing\sqcup\Sigma\sqcup\Sigma^{2}\sqcup\ldots
the set of finite sequences of elements of
\Sigma
, that is, the set of
words
in
\Sigma
. A subset
L\subset\Sigma^{\ast}
is called a
language
.

A language
L
correspond to a map
\alpha:\Sigma^{\ast}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
, with
L=\alpha^{-1}(1)
. Here

\mathbb{B}\ =\ \{0,1|1+1=1\}

is the Boolean semiring. We can formally write

L\ =\ \sum_{\omega\in L}\omega

and think of this sum as formal noncommutative power series, with
\Sigma
the set of variables, and with coefficients in the Boolean semiring
\mathbb{B}
. Terms with coefficient
0
, that is, terms not in
L
, are omitted.

A language
L
is called
finite
if
|L|<\infty
. A language is called
regular
if it can be inductively obtained as follows:

•

A finite language is regular.

•

Union
L\cup L^{\prime}
and ordered product
LL^{\prime}
of regular languages is regular.

•

The star closure
L^{\ast}=\varnothing\sqcup L\sqcup L^{2}\sqcup\ldots
of a regular language is regular.

A language
L
is regular if and only if the opposite language
L^{\mathsf{op}}
is regular (the latter is given by reading all words in
L
in the opposite direction).

Introduce an equivalence
\sim_{L}
on
\Sigma^{\ast}
by
\omega\sim_{L}\omega^{\prime}
if and only if for any
x,y\in\Sigma^{\ast}
words
x\omega y
and
x\omega^{\prime}y
are either both in
L
or both not in
L
. The set of equivalence classes
E_{L}=\Sigma^{\ast}/\sim_{L}
is naturally a monoid under composition
\omega_{1}\ast\omega_{2}=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}
. The identity of
E_{L}
is given by the equivalence class of the empty sequence
\varnothing
. Monoid
E_{L}
is called the
syntactic monoid
of
L
.

The following proposition is well-known
[
22
, Proposition 10.1]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Monoid
E_{L}
is finite if and only if
L
is a regular language.

This map from regular languages to (isomorphism classes of) finite monoids is easily seen to be surjective, if one allows finite sets of letters
\Sigma
of arbitrary large size.

Remark 2.2
.

The relation between finite monoids and regular languages (and finite automata, see later) to some degree answers the question why an undergraduate course on modern algebra often has a whole semester dedicated to finite groups while the notion of a monoid may not even appear in a year-long course on the topic. On one hand, finite groups bear close relation to number theory, starting with classification of finite cyclic groups and Sylow theorems, while finite monoids are not structurally as beautiful. Their number (up to isomorphism) grows quickly with size, and no similar relation to number theory is in sight. Second, finite monoids are studied implicitly in an introductory course on formal languages in any computer science department. Each finite automaton gives rise to the language
L
that it accepts (see below) and, in turn, to the finite monoid
E_{L}
. In this sense, finite monoids are implicitly encoded in finite state automata and (implicitly) appear in courses in computer science departments.

2.2.
Finite state automata

A

of

and a map

and

associated to automaton

.
The following result holds, see

(Kleene’s theorem)

A language

A circular language

Proof.

Given a circular automaton for

Given a semimodule

\mathbb{B}
-semimodule is the same as an idempotented abelian monoid.

\mathbb{B}
-semimodules are also known as

or

with

, see
[
17
,
34
]
and references therein. Namely,

if and only if

is the minimal element of the semilattice, and the symmetric, associative operation

is taking the “supremum”
\sup(a,b)
of

and

. Properties of

are then

•

a\vee b=b\vee a
and

for

•

for

.

Such semilattices with

are also called
\{\vee,0\}
-semilattices
, see
[
31
]
.
In this paper, by a

we mean a semilattice with
0
, thus a
\{\vee,0\}
-semilattice
.

\mathbb{B}
-semimodule
M
is naturally a semilattice under
+
operation, with the minimal element
0
.
Denote by
M^{\vee}
or just

the semimodule

A nonzero element

is called

the set of irreducible elements of

For a
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule
M
and

S\subset M
a set of generators of

, there’s a surjective homomorphism
\mathbb{B}^{S}\longrightarrow M
from a free semimodule to

taking generators of
\mathbb{B}^{S}
to those of
M
.
A semimodule
M
over
\mathbb{B}
is called

if there exists a surjective semimodule homomorphism
\mathbb{B}^{n}\longrightarrow M
for some

. Here
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is the free
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule on

. An arbitrary element of

for a unique subset

with the addition rule

. Alternatively, elements of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
can be written as column “vectors” with

\mathbb{B}
.
A
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule is finitely-generated if and only if it’s a finite set.

Proposition 3.1
.

A finitely-generated
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule
M
is generated by its set of irreducible elements
\mathsf{irr}(M)
.
Any set
V
of generators of
M
contains
\mathsf{irr}(M)
.

Proof.

The second statement is clear. For the first statement, we only need to check the impossibility of loops in sum decompositions. Namely, if
a=b+c
and
b=a+d
in
M
, then

a=b+c=b+b+c=b+a+d+c=a+a+d=a+d=b.

This proves the proposition.
∎

Proposition
3.1
tells us that in a finitely-generated
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule
M
, the set
\mathsf{irr}(M)
is the unique minimal set of generators.
When
M
is finitely-generated (equivalently, finite), for any surjection
\mathbb{B}^{X}\longrightarrow M
there exists a finite subset
J\subset X
with
|J|=|\mathsf{irr}(M)|
such that generators in

are mapped bijectively to the elements of
\mathsf{irr}(M)
. In particular, a minimal free covering
\mathbb{B}^{J}\longrightarrow M
is unique, with
|J|=|\mathsf{irr}(M)|
.

Category of
\mathbb{B}
-semimodules.

A morphism
f:M\longrightarrow N
of
\mathbb{B}
-semimodules is map of sets such that
f(a+b)=f(a)+f(b)
for
a,b\in M
and
f(0)=0
.

Denote by
\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-mod}
the category of
\mathbb{B}
-semimodules and semimodule maps, and by
\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-fmod}
its full subcategory of finitely-generated (equivalently, finite)
\mathbb{B}
-semimodules. Hom sets in
\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-mod}
are naturally
\mathbb{B}
-semimodules. Endomorphisms
\mathsf{End}_{\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-mod}}(M)
of a
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule constitute an idempotented semiring.

Note that the free semimodule
\mathbb{B}^{n}
has few automorphisms:
\mathsf{Aut}_{\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-mod}}(\mathbb{B}^{n})\cong S_{n}
, the permutation group on
n
elements (automorphisms of a free semimodule are in bijection with permutations of irreducible elements).
More generally, for
M\in\mathsf{Ob}(\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-fmod})
, the group
\mathsf{Aut}(M)\subset S_{\mathsf{irr}(M)}
is usually a proper subgroup. Any automorphism permutes the set of irreducible elements, giving that inclusion of groups. Lack of automorphisms (compared to the linear case) is one feature of semimodules.

A
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule is the same as a
\{\vee,0\}
-semilattice, and the category
\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-mod}
is equivalent to the category whose objects are
\{\vee,0\}
-semilattices and morphisms are maps taking
0
to
0
and intertwining the
\vee
-operation. Recall that a semilattice in this paper stands for a
\{\vee,0\}
-semilattice.

The dual semimodule of
M
is given by
M^{\ast}:=\mathsf{Hom}_{\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-mod}}(M,\mathbb{B})
. Suppose that
M
is finitely-generated. An element
f\in M^{\ast}
is determined by the subset
N_{f}\subset M
of elements that it takes to
0
. Let
a_{f}=\sum_{a\in N_{f}}a
,
a_{f}\in M
be the sum of all elements of
N_{f}
. Then
N_{f}=\{b\in M|b\leq a_{f}\}
. Thus, each element
a\in M
gives rise to a functional
f_{a}\in M^{\ast}
with
f_{a}(b)=0\Leftrightarrow b\leq a
. Vice versa, each functional
f\in M^{\ast}
comes from a unique element
a_{f}\in M
. We obtain a bijection of sets
M\Leftrightarrow M^{\ast}
, for finite
\mathbb{B}
-semimodules
M
, so that
|M|=|M^{\ast}|
.
This bijection does not preserve the addition in
M
and is order-reversing. It takes
0\in M
to
\sum_{f\in M^{\ast}}f
, the largest element in
M^{\ast}
, and vice versa.

Furthermore, elements of
M^{\ast}
separate elements of
M
. Namely, if
a<b
(so that
a+b=b
) and
f(b)=0
then
f(a)=0
. To such a pair
(a,b)
we can assign homomorphism
g\in M^{\ast}
such that
g(x)=0\Leftrightarrow x\leq a
. Then
g(a)=0,g(b)=1
, and
g
separates
a
and
b
.
Suppose now that
a\nleq b
. Then
f_{b}\in M^{\ast}
satisfies
f_{b}(b)=0,f_{b}(a)=1
. Thus, the natural pairing
M\times M^{\ast}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is nondegenerate (separating).

The above implies that the natural homomorphism
M\longrightarrow M^{\ast\ast}
is an isomorphism in
\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-fmod}
. We obtain the following result
[
68
]
and see
[
17
, Proposition 2.3]
for a generalization to arbitrary
\mathbb{B}
-semimodules.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any finite
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule
M
the natural bijection of sets
M\cong M^{\ast}
, described above, is order-reversing. In the natural pairing
M\times M^{\ast}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
elements of
M^{\ast}
separate elements of
M
(and vice versa), and the natural map
M\longrightarrow M^{\ast\ast}
is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{B}
-semimodules. The assignment
M\mapsto M^{\ast}
extends to a contravariant involution on
\mathbb{B}\mathsf{-fmod}

Projective semimodules.

A
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule

is called

if it has a lifting property: any surjective map
M\longrightarrow N
induces a surjective map
\mathsf{Hom}(P,M)\longrightarrow\mathsf{Hom}(P,N)
. Applying this to a surjection

from a free
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule to

shows there is a lifting map
\iota:P\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}^{J}
into a free semimodule such that
p\circ\iota=\mathsf{id}
,

(22)

\mathbb{B}^{J}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}P\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\iota}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{B}^{J},\ \ p\circ\iota=\mathsf{id}_{P}.

Vice versa, having a pair of maps
(\iota,p)
with this property implies that
P
is projective. We see that a
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule is projective if and only if it’s a
retract
of a free semimodule.

associated to the matrix

in (

p:\mathbb{B}^{2}\longrightarrow M,\ \ p(v_{1})=x,\ p(v_{2})=y

admits a section

\iota:M\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}^{2},\ \ \iota(x)=v_{1},\ \iota(y)=v_{1}+v_{2},

where
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{B}^{2}
. Semimodules given by matrices
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
are projective as well, but not the semimodule described by

Remark 3.7

The semilinear category

As mentioned earlier, any finite semilattice with

Tensor product.

The hom space

of

-semimodules is defined to consist of finite sums

\displaystyle 0\ \ \sim\ \ m\otimes 0,

\displaystyle(m_{1}+m_{2})\otimes n

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle m_{1}\otimes n+m_{2}\otimes n,

\displaystyle m\otimes(n_{1}+n_{2})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle m\otimes n_{1}+m\otimes n_{2},

Finite topological spaces and projective semimodules.

It follows from Birkhoff’s theorem that a finite projective
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule
P
can be realized as the semilattice
\mathcal{c}{O}(X)
of open sets of a finite topological space
X
. Assume that
X
is minimal, in the sense that for any two points
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
there exists an open set that contains only one of these two points. For an open set
U
denote by
[U]
the corresponding element of
P
. Each
x\in X
determines the smallest open set
U_{x}
that contains
x
(the intersection of all open sets that contain
x
). Sets
U_{x}
,
x\in X
, are pairwise distinct and constitute the set of smallest non-empty open subsets of
X
.

Elements
[U_{x}],x\in X
are exactly the irreducible elements of the semilattice
\mathcal{c}{O}(X)
, and
X
can be reconstructed from

canonically, by taking its set of points as the set of irreducible elements
\mathsf{irr}(P)
of
P
. The smallest open set that contains

consists of all

such that
x+y=x
.

The dual
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule

is isomorphic to the lattice of all closed sets in
X
, with the union of closed sets as the addition operation. (Any closed set

defines a semilinear map

, taking

to

if and only if

. Since
P
and
P^{\ast}
have the same cardinality, this describes all elements of
P^{\ast}
.) Equivalently,

is the lattice of open sets of the dual finite space
X^{\ast}
. Let

be the smallest closed set that contains

and denote by
[V_{x}]
the corresponding element of
P^{\ast}
. Note that

since we chose
X
minimal. Form the element

(31)

c_{P}\ :\ \sum_{x\in X}[U_{x}]\otimes[V_{x}]\in P\otimes P^{\ast}

and consider the corresponding homomorphism

(32)

\mathsf{coev}_{P}\ :\ \mathbb{B}\longrightarrow P\otimes P^{\ast}.

It is straightforward to check that
\mathsf{coev}_{P}
satisfies the isotopy relation in Figure
1.2.1
. The map
\mathsf{coev}_{P}
is canonically associated to
P
, since minimal

is unique, up to homeomorphism, given

.

For two finite projective
\mathbb{B}
-semimodules

with associated topological spaces

the tensor product

has associated topological space

\mathsf{coev}_{P_{1}\otimes P_{2}}=\mathsf{coev}_{P_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{coev}_{P_{2}}

, where

of such semialgebras such that

is, in addition, a free

-semimodule (of finite rank). Semialgebra

. The set

. Such semialgebras and homomorphisms always exist. For example, one can take for the set of basis elements of

the set

of nonzero elements of

. The multiplication in this basis of

is induced by the multiplication in

and gives a basis element of

or

compared to that of

.

We leave it to the reader to give a general definition of a nondeterministic circular automaton.

Interval-only theories.

Assume that
L_{\circ}=\varnothing
is the empty language. By the empty language
\varnothing
we mean the language that contains no words. It’s different from the language
\{\varnothing\}
that contains the empty word and nothing else. In this case we can identity the syntactic monoid
E_{L_{I}}
of
L_{I}
with the image of the free monoid
\Sigma^{\ast}
in
A(+-)
under the map sending
\omega\in\Sigma^{\ast}
to the arc with endpoints
+,-
and carrying
\omega
. For a general language
L_{I}
, the image of
\Sigma^{\ast}
in
A(+-)
only surjects onto the syntactic monoid, see Remark

. It’s a natural question to characterize circular languages, given
L_{I}
, such that the image of
\Sigma^{\ast}
in
A(+-)
is
E_{L_{I}}
. Such circular languages are, in a sense, the most compatible with the interval language

.

Unoriented cobordisms.

There is an unoriented version of the category
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma}
: one-dimensional cobordisms are now unoriented and the objects are numbers
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, counting the number of top and bottom endpoints of the cobordism. Evaluation
\alpha
must be reflection-invariant:
\alpha_{I}(\omega^{\mathsf{op}})=\alpha_{I}(\omega)
, for
\omega\in\Sigma^{\ast}
and likewise for the circular evaluation:
\alpha_{\circ}(\omega^{\mathsf{op}})=\alpha_{\circ}(\omega)
, for
\omega\in\Sigma^{\ast}_{\circ}.

The state space
A(1)
of a single point in this theory comes with a symmetric pairing
(\>\>,\>\>):A(1)\times A(1)\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
, nondegenerate in the sense that different elements of
A(1)
define different functionals on
A(1)
. We can pick the subset

which consists of images
\langle\omega|
of all words
\omega\in\Sigma^{\ast}
rather than their semilinear combinations. There is an action of
\Sigma^{\ast}
on

via the map

\delta:\Sigma\times Q_{1}\longrightarrow Q_{1}
. This action is self-adjoint with respect to the above pairing
(\>\>,\>\>)

(\>\>,\>\>):Q_{1}\times Q_{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
, with the self-adjointness property,
(\delta_{a}(q_{1}),q_{2})=(q_{1},\delta_{a}(q_{2}))

It is straightforward to turn the above structure on

into a proper definition of a deterministic symmetric automaton for a symmetric regular language

is a projective semimodule and there’s a decomposition of the identity for a symmetric language

Remark 5.1
.

One way to define a deterministic automaton for a pair of languages
(L_{1},L_{2})
is as an automaton with two subsets of accepting states

and
Q^{2}_{\mathsf{t}}
, one for each language. Word

if and only if, after reading it, the automaton is in a state in
Q^{i}_{\mathsf{t}},

i=1,2
. Forgetting one of these subsets produces an automaton for the other language. Similar definitions work for non-deterministic automata, etc. Variations on this definition include

•

Having two initial states (or sets of initial states) for the two languages but the same set
Q_{\mathsf{t}}
of accepting states.

•

Allowing different initial states
q_{\mathsf{in}}^{1},q_{\mathsf{in}}^{2}
and different subsets of accepting states

for the two languages.

Joint complexity.

Define the joint complexity

of languages

as the logarithm of the size of state space

c(L_{1},L_{2})\ :=\ \log_{2}|A_{1,2}(-)|.

. With any language

.
