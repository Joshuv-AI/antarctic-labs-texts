1.
Introduction

The primary goal of this paper is to continue the investigation of the structure of discrete sets
A
such that
{\{e^{2\pi ix\cdot a}\}}_{a\in A}
is a frame for
L^{2}(K)
, where
K
is a bounded symmetric convex body with a smooth boundary and non-vanishing curvature. Recall that such a collection of exponentials is a frame if there exist
0<C_{L}\leq C_{U}<\infty
so that for every
f\in L^{2}(K)
,

(1.1)

C_{L}|K|\cdot{||f||}^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}\leq\sum_{a\in A}{\left|\widehat{f}(a)\right|}^{2}\leq C_{U}|K|\cdot{||f||}^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}.

Let us recall some definitions.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
S
be a separated subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, in the sense that there exists
\delta>0
such that
|s-s^{\prime}|\geq\delta>0
for all
s,s^{\prime}\in S
,
s\not=s^{\prime}
. The upper density of
S
, is given by

\overline{dens}(S)=\limsup_{R\to\infty}\frac{\#\{S\cap{[-R,R]}^{d}\}}{2^{d}R^{d}}.

Similarly, the lower density of
S
is given by

\underline{dens}(S)=\liminf_{R\to\infty}\frac{\#\{S\cap{[-R,R]}^{d}\}}{2^{d}R^{d}}.

If
\overline{dens}(S)=\underline{dens}(S)
, then we say that the Beurling density
dens(S)
exists and is equal to this quantity.

It is not difficult to construct a frame for
L^{2}(K)
by enclosing
K
in a rectangular box and restricting the orthonormal exponential basis for this box to
K
. In this case, the frame is tight, i.e., in the definition of the frame in (
1.1
) we have
C_{L}=C_{U}
. One unfortunate feature of such a frame is that its density is much higher than the volume of
K
.

Also recall that
{\mathcal{E}}(A)={\{e^{2\pi ix\cdot a}\}}_{a\in A}
is a Riesz basis for
L^{2}(K)
if it is equivalent (via an invertible and bounded map) to an orthonormal basis (not necessarily exponential) for
L^{2}(K)
. It is an old open question of whether
L^{2}(B_{d})
,
d\geq 2
, possesses a Riesz basis of exponentials, where
B_{d}
denotes the unit ball.

It is well-known that if
{\mathcal{E}}(A)
is a Riesz basis for
L^{2}(K)
, then
dens(A)=|K|
. In hopes of shedding some light on the question of a possible Riesz basis for
L^{2}(K)
, where
K
is bounded, symmetric, convex and has a smooth boundary with non-vanishing curvature, we prove a result that puts a significant restriction on the structure of a set
A
that generates a frame for
L^{2}(K)
.

A well-known stationary phase formula due to Herz
[
7
]
(see also
[
5
]
and Lemma
3.1
below for a precise formulation) says that

(1.2)

\widehat{\chi}_{K}(\xi)=\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)\sin\left(2\pi\left(\rho^{*}(\xi)-\frac{d-1}{8}\right)\right){|\xi|}^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}

plus an error term, where
\rho^{*}
is the Minkowski functional on
K^{*}
, the dual body of
K
. We are going to show in Theorem
2.1
below that if
{\mathcal{E}}(A)
is a frame,
|\widehat{\chi}_{K}(a-a^{\prime})|
, with
a\not=a^{\prime}
,
a,a^{\prime}\in A
, cannot be much smaller than
{|a-a^{\prime}|}^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}
very often. In other words, the difference set
A-A
must stay away from the zeroes of
\widehat{\chi}_{K}
.

It was shown in
[
12
]
(see also the case of the ball in
[
11
]
) that with
K
as above,
L^{2}(K)
does not possess an orthogonal basis of exponentials. Orthogonality implies that
(A-A)\backslash\{\vec{0}\}
is strictly contained in the zero set of
\widehat{\chi}_{K}
. What we prove in this paper is that
A-A
cannot be close to the zeroes of
\widehat{\chi}_{K}
very often, even on average, in order for
{\mathcal{E}}(A)
to be a frame for
L^{2}(K)
. More precisely, the main results of the current paper are the following.

1.0.1.
Our Contributions

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
A
be a countable and discrete subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, and let

. Let

denote a bounded symmetric convex set with a smooth boundary and everywhere non-vanishing Gaussian curvature. Let
\phi\geq 0
be a continuous monotonically non-increasing function on

such that

(1.3)

{\left(\frac{1}{2^{j}}\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}\phi^{p}(t)dt\right)}^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq c_{j}\left(2^{-j\frac{d+1}{2}}\right)\ \ \ \forall j\in\mathbb{Z},

for some constants

.

Further suppose that

(1.4)

for all

,

.

Then if

the upper density of

is equal to

, and, consequently,

is not

.

.

Note that if

An immediate consequence of Theorem

For example, if

Remark 1.5

It is proved in (

The case

2.1.
Outline of the proof of Theorem
1.2

By the method of stationary phase (see Lemma

|\widehat{\chi}_{K}(a-a^{\prime})|\leq C{(1+|a-a^{\prime}|)}^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}

Let

(2.1)

{\left(\frac{1}{2^{dj}}\sum_{a\in A\cap{\mathcal{A}}_{j}}{|\widehat{\chi}_{K}(a-a^{\prime})|}^{p}\right)}^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq c_{j}2^{-j\frac{d+1}{2}}

then the upper density of

By pigeonholing, we will be able to conclude that there exists a point

This violates a variant of the result due to Bourgain which says that in sets of positive upper Lebesgue density, a pinned distance set with respect to almost every point contains every sufficiently large positive real number. C\int_{c_{1}2^{j}\leq\rho^{*}(u-a^{\prime})\leq c_{2}2^{j+1}}\phi^{p}(\rho^{*}(u-a^{\prime}))du=C\int_{c_{1}2^{j}\leq\rho^{*}(u)\leq c_{2}2^{j+1}}\phi^{p}(\rho^{*}(u))du,

or

C\int_{c_{1}2^{j}\leq\rho^{*}(r\omega)\leq c_{2}2^{j+1}}\phi^{p}(\rho^{*}(r\omega))r^{d-1}d\omega dr=C\int_{c_{1}2^{j}\leq r\rho^{*}(\omega)\leq c_{2}2^{j+1}}\phi^{p}(r\rho^{*}(\omega))r^{d-1}d\omega dr,

where
C
is an independent constant.

Let
t=r\rho^{*}(\omega)
. We obtain

C\int_{c_{1}2^{j}\leq t\leq c_{2}2^{j+1}}\int_{S^{d-1}}\phi^{p}(t)t^{d-1}\frac{d\omega}{{(\rho^{*}(\omega))}^{d}}dt

(4.1)

=C\int_{c_{1}2^{j}\leq t\leq c_{2}2^{j+1}}\int_{S}\phi^{p}(t)t^{d-1}dt\frac{d\sigma_{\rho^{*}}(\eta)}{|\nabla\rho^{*}(\eta)|},

where
d\sigma_{\rho^{*}}
is the surface measure on

S=\{x:\rho^{*}(x)=1\},

the
\rho^{*}
-unit sphere.

The expression (
4.1
) follows from the classical co-area formula. See, for example,
[
14
]
, for a similar calculation.

Since

\rho^{*}(\eta)=\eta\cdot\nabla\rho^{*}(\eta),
it follows that
|\nabla\rho^{*}(\eta)|\geq C>0
, when
\eta
is on the
\rho^{*}
-unit sphere, for some different
C>0
. It follows that (
4.1
) is bounded by

\displaystyle(\ref{almost})\leq C^{\prime}2^{j(d+1)}\cdot 2^{-j}\int_{c_{1}2^{j}}^{c_{2}2^{j+1}}\phi^{p}(t)t^{d-1}dt\leq C^{\prime\prime}c_{j}2^{dj}\cdot 2^{-j\frac{d+1}{2}p},

where the first inequality above follows from the fact that all norms on finite dimensional normed spaces are comparable.

The conclusion follows after dividing both sides by
2^{dj}
and taking
p
’th root. This recovers Theorem
1.2
from Theorem
2.1
.

5.
Sketch of proof of a slight strengthening of Theorem
3.2

In this section we sketch the proof of the following result, which essentially follows from Bourgain’s argument in
[
2
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
has positive upper Lebesgue density if

\overline{dens}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(E)=\limsup_{R\to\infty}\frac{|E\cap B_{R}|}{|B_{R}|}>0,

where
B_{R}
denote the ball of radius
R
.

Definition 5.2
.

Suppose that
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
\overline{dens}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(E)>0
. We say that
E^{\prime}
is a refinement of
E
of relative density
r
if
E^{\prime}\subset E
and

\overline{dens}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(E^{\prime})\geq r\cdot\overline{dens}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(E).

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of positive upper Lebesgue density
\overline{dens}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(E)
. Then there exists a threshold
L_{0}>0
, a threshold
r\in(0,1)
, and
x_{0}\in E
, such that for any refinement
E^{\prime}
of
E
of relative density
r
, and any
L>L_{0}
, there exists
x^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}
such that
\rho^{*}(x_{0}-x^{\prime})=L
.

Bourgain’s proof of the case
d=2
,
\rho^{*}(x)=|x|
, has two key ingredients. The first is the following maximal theorem which follows from Bourgain’s proof of the circular maximal theorem.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\sigma
be the arc-length measure on the circle
S^{1}
, and let
\sigma_{s}
be defined by the relation
\widehat{\sigma}_{s}(\xi)=\widehat{\sigma}(s\xi)
. Let
P_{t}
denote the Poisson semi-group kernel on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, so
\widehat{P}_{t}(\xi)=e^{-t|\xi|}
. For
p>2
, there exist constants
C(p)<\infty
and
\alpha(p)>0
, such that

{\left|\left|\sup_{s\geq t_{0}}|(f-(f*P_{t})*\sigma_{s})|\right|\right|}_{p}\leq C(p){\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)}^{\alpha(p)}{||f||}_{p}.

The fact that the same conclusion is achieved if
S^{1}
is replaced by any symmetric smooth convex curve with non-vanishing curvature follows from
[
18
]
. In higher dimensions, the following result follows from
[
6
]
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\rho
be the Minkowski functional on the convex symmetric body
K
with a smooth boundary and everywhere non-vanishing curvature. Let
\rho^{*}
be as above. Let
S_{\rho}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\rho^{*}(x)=1\}
, and let
\sigma_{\rho}
denotes the surface measure. Let

\sigma_{\rho,s}
denote the dilated surface measure, as above. For
d>2
, there exist
C<\infty
, such that

{\left|\left|\sup_{s\geq t_{0}}|(f-(f*P_{t})*\sigma_{\rho,s})|\right|\right|}_{2}\leq C{\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}{||f||}_{2}.

The second ingredient in Bourgain’s proof is the observation that if the conclusion of Theorem
3.2
is false in the case
d=2,\rho^{*}(\xi)=|\xi|
, then there exists
U\subset{[0,1]}^{2}
,
|U|>\epsilon
, and a sequence of positive numbers

s_{1}>t_{1}>s_{2}>t_{2}>\dots>s_{J}>t_{J},

J
arbitrarily large, satisfying

s_{j+1}<\frac{1}{2}t_{j},

and
x\in U\cap[s_{j},1-s_{j}]
,
s_{j}\in(0,1/2)
, implies that
\sup_{s_{j}<t<s_{j+1}}|(1_{{[0,1]}^{2}}-f)*\sigma_{t}|=1
. The same reduction can be performed in dimensions
d\geq 3
with the Euclidean metric replaced by
\rho^{*}
. The contradiction is then obtained using Theorem
5.4
. In dimensions three and higher, the contradiction is obtained in exactly the same way using Theorem
5.5
.

Moreover, the same argument yields the slightly stronger conclusion, claimed in Theorem
5.3
, that there exists a threshold
L_{0}>0
, a threshold
r\in(0,1)
, and
x_{0}\in E
, such that for any refinement
E^{\prime}
of
E
of relative density
r
, and any
L>L_{0}
, there exists
x^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}
such that
\rho^{*}(x_{0}-x^{\prime})=L
. This is because assuming that this statement is false leads to exactly the same statement as above and the contradiction is obtained in the same way.

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

Suppose that
{\mathcal{E}}(A)
is an orthogonal collection in
L^{2}(K)
in the sense that

\int_{K}e^{2\pi ix\cdot(a-a^{\prime})}dx=0\ \text{for all}\ a\not=a^{\prime}\in A,

where
K
is as in Theorem
1.7
.

The first listed author and Rudnev proved in
[
13
]
that if
A
is infinite,
A
is contained in a line. Moreover, they showed that if
d\not=1\mod 4
, then
A
must be finite.

The proof relies on two estimates that follow from the orthogonality assumption for
{\mathcal{E}}(A)
. The first says that if
{\mathcal{E}}(A)
is an orthogonal set in
L^{2}(K)
, then for all
a,a^{\prime}\in A
,

(6.1)

\rho^{*}(a-a^{\prime})=\frac{k}{2}+\frac{d-1}{8}+O({|a-a^{\prime}|}^{-1})\ \text{for some integer}\ k\ \text{as}\ |a-a^{\prime}|\to\infty.

The second estimate says that if
a_{0},a_{1}\in A
,
a_{0}\not=a_{1}
, then for all
a\in A
,

(6.2)

\rho^{*}(a_{0}-a)-\rho^{*}(a_{1}-a)=\frac{k}{2}+O({|a|}^{-2})\ \text{for some integer}\ k\ \text{as}\ |a|\to\infty.

The first estimate follows directly from Lemma
3.1
. The second estimate uses more refined Bessel expansions (see Lemma 1.4 in
[
13
]
). Using these estimates, the authors established their claim.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1.7
, the same arguments yield the following form of (
6.1
) and (
6.2
):

(6.3)

\rho^{*}(a-a^{\prime})=\frac{k}{2}+\frac{d-1}{8}+O\left(\frac{\phi(|a-a^{\prime}|)}{{|a-a^{\prime}|}^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\right)\ \text{for some integer}\ k\ \text{as}\ |a-a^{\prime}|\to\infty,

where (
1.5
) holds, and (
6.2
) becomes

(6.4)

\rho^{*}(a_{0}-a)-\rho^{*}(a_{1}-a)=\frac{k}{2}+O\left(\frac{\phi(|a|)}{{|a|}^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\cdot{|a-a^{\prime}|}^{-1}\right)\ \text{for some integer}\ k\ \text{as}\ |a|\to\infty.

Observe that

O\left(\frac{\phi(|a-a^{\prime}|)}{{|a-a^{\prime}|}^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\right)=o(1),\ \text{as}\ |a-a^{\prime}|\to\infty,

by assumption. Similarly,

O\left(\frac{\phi(|a|)}{{|a|}^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\cdot{|a-a^{\prime}|}^{-1}\right)=o({|a-a|}^{-1})\ \text{as}\ |a-a^{\prime}|\to\infty.

The geometric observation used to establish the conclusion of the main result in
[
13
]
, namely Lemma 1.5, does not require anything stronger than (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) above. These are all that are needed to establish Lemma 1.5 and Proposition 1.6 in that paper. This results in the following version of the Erdős Integer Distance Principle.

Theorem 6.1
.

(Approximate version of the Erdős Integer Distance Principle) Let
\rho^{*}
be as above, corresponding to a symmetric convex body
K
with a smooth boundary and non-vanishing Gaussian curvature. Let
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, and suppose that for
a,a_{0},a_{1}\in A
,

(6.5)

\rho^{*}(a-a_{0})-\rho^{*}(a-a_{1})=o({|a|}^{-1}),

and there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that for
a,a^{\prime}\in A
,

(6.6)

\rho^{*}(a-a^{\prime})=c_{1}k+c_{2}+o(1).

Then
A
is a subset of a line. If
d\not=1\mod 4
, then
A
is finite.

7.
Remarks and open problems

The purpose of this section is to describe some open problems that may be accessible using the methods of this paper and related ideas.

i)

The authors of this paper previously proved (
[
10
]
) that if
g(x)=\chi_{B_{d}}(x)
, where
B_{d}
is the unit ball in dimensions two and higher, and
d\not=1\mod 4
, then there does not exist
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, such that
{\{g(x-a)e^{2\pi ix\cdot b}\}}_{(a,b)\in S}
is an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. The case when
d=1\mod 4
is work in progress. A reasonable question to ask is whether the same conclusion can be established under the assumption of approximate orthogonality.

ii)

In view of
[
12
]
, it should be possible to show that the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
still holds if we assume that
\partial K
has one point, in the neighborhood of which it is smooth and has non-vanishing Gaussian curvature.

iii)

It is reasonable to conjecture that if
K
is a convex body in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, then there exists
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
such that

{\{\chi_{K}(x-a)e^{2\pi ix\cdot b}\}}_{(a,b)\in S}
is an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if and only if
K
tiles
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by translation, i.e iff
K
is a symmetric polytope of the type classified by Venkov
[
20
]
and McMullen
[
17
]
. It may be possible to approach this problem by combining the techniques of this paper and those of
[
16
]

who recently proved that if
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, is convex, then
L^{2}(K)
has an orthogonal basis of exponentials if and only if
K
tiles
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by translation. The two dimensional case was previously established in
[
12
]
.

iv)

The contrast between the proof of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.7
raises the question of whether there is a strengthening of the approximate version of the Erdős Integer Distance Principle, proved in
[
13
]
, where the point-wise (near) integer distance condition is replaced by an average version. For example, is it true that if
A
is an infinite separated subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, such that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
so that

|a-a^{\prime}|=c_{1}k+c_{2}+O({\mathcal{D}}(|a-a^{\prime}|)),

with

\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{1}{2^{j}}\int_{2^{j}}^{2^{j+1}}{|{\mathcal{D}}(t)|}^{p}dt=0,

then
A
is a subset of a line?

If anything like this is true, it should lead to a considerable strengthening of Theorem
1.2
. Heuristically, the idea (described in two dimensions for the sake of simplicity) is the following. Fix
a_{0},a_{1}
,
a_{0}\not=a_{1}
. Then
90
percent of the points in
A
are nearly
c_{1}k+c_{2}
distance away from
a_{0}
, and the same is true with respect to
a_{1}
. It follows that
81
percent of the points lie on finitely many hyperbolas with focal points at
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
. Now choose
a_{2}
that does not lie on a line that passes through
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
and play the same game with respect to
a_{1},a_{2}
and the points that are nearly shifted scaled integers with respect to
a_{2}
. The hyperbolas with focal points at
a_{0},a_{1}
intersect the hyperbolas with focal points at
a_{1},a_{2}
finitely many times (depending on the distance between those pairs of points) and shows that the resulting subset
A^{\prime}\subset A
(having at least
72.9
percent of the points) is a subset of a line. We now replay the same game, using the same points
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}
with respect to the complement of
A^{\prime}
. This approach can probably be used to show that
A=A_{good}+A_{bad}
, where
A_{good}
is a subset of a line, and
A_{bad}
is arbitrarily sparse. This may lead to an alternate proof of Theorem
1.2
. Whether one can conclude that
A_{bad}
is empty is an interesting question that should be investigated.
