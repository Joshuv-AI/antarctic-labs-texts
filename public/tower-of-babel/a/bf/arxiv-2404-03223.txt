Abstract
. This is the first in a series of papers devoted to the blow up analysis for the quenching phenomena in a parabolic MEMS equation. In this paper, we first give an optimal Hölder estimate for solutions to this equation by using the blow up method and some Liouville theorems on stationary two-valued caloric functions, and then establish a convergence theory for sequences of uniformly Hölder continuous solutions. These results are also used to prove a stratification theorem on the rupture set { u = 0 } \{u=0\} .

35K58;35B44;35B45.

1.
Introduction

Micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) are widely used devices in engineering and technology. The modelling of MEMS poses several interesting partial differential equations. Rigorous mathematical analysis of these PDEs has attracted a lot of attention in the past two decades. For more backgrounds, we refer the reader to the monograph of Esposito-Ghoussoub-Guo
[
8
]
and the survey paper of Laurençot-Walker
[
13
]
, where an extensive list of results and references about this problem can also be found. Some more recent studies on these problems can be found in
[
9
,
10
,
14
]
.

An important feature of these PDEs is that their solutions could exhibit
the quenching phenomena
. Consider for example the second order parabolic MEMS equation

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=-u^{-p}

(1.1)

in
\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T)
, where
p>1
,
\Omega
is a smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(which could be unbounded). A Cauchy-Dirichlet boundary condition
u\lfloor_{\partial^{p}\Omega_{T}}=\varphi
is imposed on the parabolic boundary
\partial^{p}\Omega_{T}
, where
\varphi>0
belongs to
C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\partial^{p}\Omega_{T})
, with

\alpha:=2/(p+1).

This Cauchy-Dirichlet problem has a unique, positive solution, which however could exist only locally in time. This is because, due to the effect of the nonlinearity
-u^{-p}
, the solution could touch down to
0
at some time, where
u
becomes singular. This is
the quenching phenomena
in MEMS problem.

In some special cases, e.g. when the spatial dimension is one or when the initial-boundary value
\varphi
takes some special forms (e.g. being a constant), there are several results describing the nature of this quenching phenomena, see
[
8
, Chapter 10]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, the analysis of this phenomena in general is not done yet. In this series of papers, we will develop a blow up analysis approach to this problem, which allows us to give a precise description of how solutions to (
1.1
) approach
0
in finite time, and the structure of the rupture set
\{u=0\}
.

In this first paper, we will establish an optimal Hölder estimate for solutions to (
1.1
), and use this estimate to give a convergence theory for sequences of solutions to (
1.1
). These two results are used in this paper to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of the rupture set
\{u=0\}
. These results will also be used in sequel papers for further analysis of the quenching phenomena in (
1.1
).

2.
Main results

We assume
u
is a local solution to the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem of (
1.1
) in
\Omega_{T}
, where
T>0
is the first quenching moment, i.e.,
u(x,t)>0
for any
(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T)
, while

\lim_{t\to T^{-}}\inf_{x\in\Omega}u(x,t)=0.

The first main result of this paper is a global Hölder estimate on
u
. In the following
C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{\Omega_{T}})
denotes the space of
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions with respect to the parabolic distance.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
u
is the smooth solution of the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem for (
1.1
) with positive initial boundary value
\varphi\in C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\partial^{p}\Omega_{T})
, then
u\in C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{\Omega_{T}})
.

Notice that (
1.1
) is scaling invariant in the sense that if
u
is a solution, then for any
\lambda>0
,

u_{\lambda}(x,t):=\lambda^{-\alpha}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)

is also a solution.
The exponent
\alpha=2/(p+1)
is compatible with this scaling invariance. This Hölder estimate is optimal in view of some explicit solutions of (
1.1
) such as

u_{0}(t):=(p+1)^{\frac{1}{p+1}}(-t)^{\frac{1}{p+1}},\quad\text{for}~t<0,

and

u(x)=\left[\frac{2}{p+1}\left(n-2+\frac{2}{p+1}\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{p+1}}|x|^{\frac{2}{p+1}},\quad\text{for}~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},~n\geq 2.

By this Hölder estimate, we can define the trace of
u
at time
T
,
u(x,T)
, by continuation. In particular, the rupture set

\Sigma_{T}:=\{x:u(x,T)=0\}

is a closed subset of
\overline{\Omega}
. A direct consequence of this theorem is, if the boundary value
\varphi
is strictly positive, then there is no rupture point on the boundary. This was previously known only in the case when
\Omega
is convex, see
[
8
, Theorem 8.3.1]
.

Corollary 2.2
.

If
\varphi(x,T)>0
on
\partial\Omega
, then
\Sigma_{T}
is compactly supported in
\Omega
.

Theorem
2.1
corresponds to the Hölder estimate for the elliptic version of (
1.1
),

\Delta u=u^{-p},

which was established in Davila-Wang-Wei
[
6
]
. The main idea is to use the blow up method, and then reduce the estimate to some Liouville properties for entire harmonic functions or
stationary two-valued harmonic functions
in the elliptic case, and for ancient solutions to the heat equation or
stationary two-valued caloric functions
in the parabolic case.

Definition 2.3
.

[Stationary two-valued caloric functions]
Given a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
a nonnegative function
u\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
is called a stationary two-valued caloric function if

(i):

\nabla u,\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}_{loc}(\Omega)
;

(ii):

\Delta u-\partial_{t}u\geq 0
in the distributional sense;

(iii):

u(\partial_{t}u-\Delta u)=0
in the sense that, for any
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}uu\eta^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\eta^{2}+2\eta u\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta=0;

(iv):

for any vector field
Y\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}divY-2DY(\nabla u,\nabla u)-2\partial_{t}u\nabla u\cdot Y=0;

(2.1)

(v):

u
satisfies the localized energy inequality, that is, for any
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{2}\eta^{2}\leq&-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\partial_{t}u|^{2}\eta^{2}+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{2}\eta\partial_{t}\eta\\
&-4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}u\eta\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta.\end{split}

(2.2)

Remark 2.4
.

•

Although we only assume
u
to be
L^{1}_{loc}
, by the subcaloric property (ii) and because
u
is nonnegative,
u\in L^{\infty}_{loc}
.

•

The elliptic version of the above notion is stationary two-valued harmonic function. This is a special case of Almgren’s
Q
-valued functions, see
[
1
]
and
[
7
]
. Although in these works only Dirichlet energy minimizers are considered, recently it is found that stationary ones arise in various settings, see
[
2
,
3
,
11
,
12
,
15
,
16
]
.

The method in
[
6
]
is inspired by
the blow up method in Noris-Tavares-Terracini-Verzini
[
17
]
, where they give a uniform Hölder estimate for sequences of solutions to some singularly perturbed elliptic systems such as

\Delta u_{i,\beta}=\beta u_{i,\beta}\sum_{j\neq i}u_{j,\beta}^{2},\quad i=1,\dots,N,\quad\beta\to+\infty.

See also Dancer-Wang-Zhang
[
5
]
for the corresponding parabolic case of this singular perturbation problem.

The reason that stationary two-valued caloric functions arise in this blow up argument is due to the following observation. If the Hölder semi-norm of a solution
u
is too large, then we define a rescaling

\widetilde{u}(x,t):=L^{-1}\lambda^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}u(x_{0}+\lambda x,t_{0}+\lambda^{2}t),

where
(x_{0},t_{0})
is a well-chosen base point,
\lambda
is a scaling parameter and
L
is a large normalization constant which is chosen to make sure that the Hölder semi-norm of
\widetilde{u}
is equal to
1
. (This normalization condition will lead to a contradiction with some Liouville properties.) The introduction of
L
here of course destroys the scaling invariance of the original equation. In fact now the equation is changed to

\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}-\Delta\widetilde{u}=-L^{-p-1}\widetilde{u}^{-p}.

(2.3)

Because
L
is large, we see that
\widetilde{u}
is almost caloric in its positivity set
\{\widetilde{u}>0\}
, which will lead to the condition (ii) in Definition
2.3
after passing to the limit. However, in order to establish Liouville theorems, we also need
the stationary condition
and
the localized energy identity
. This follows by taking limit in a suitable
stationary condition
and
localized energy identity
associated to (
2.3
).

This blow up method can also be used to establish an interior uniform Hölder estimate.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose
(u_{i})
is a sequence of positive solutions of (
1.1
) in
Q_{2}^{-}:=B_{2}\times(-4,0]
, satisfying

\sup\limits_{i}\int_{Q_{2}^{-}}u_{i}(x,t)<+\infty.

(2.4)

Then there exists a constant
C
independent of
i
such that

\|u_{i}\|_{C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{Q_{1}^{-}})}\leq C.

Next we discuss how a sequence of solutions to (
1.1
) converge and what is their singular limit. We need some notions on weak solutions of (
1.1
).

Definition 2.6
.

A function
u
is called an
L^{1}
weak solution of (
1.1
) if
u\geq 0
,
u\in L^{1}_{loc}
,
u^{-p}\in L^{1}_{loc}
, and it satisfies (
1.1
) in the distributional sense.

Definition 2.7
.

A function
u
(which, without loss of generality, is assumed to be defined in
Q_{1}:=B_{1}\times(-1,1)
) is called a suitable
weak solution of (
1.1
) if

(i):

u
is an
L^{1}
weak solution;

(ii):

u
is stationary in the sense that, for any vector field
Y\in C_{0}^{\infty}(Q_{1},\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\int\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{u^{1-p}}{p-1}\right)\text{div}Y-DY(\nabla u,\nabla u)-\partial_{t}u(\nabla u\cdot Y)=0,

(2.5)

where
\text{div}Y
is the divergence of
Y
, and

DY(\nabla u,\nabla u):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial Y_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}};

(iii):

u
satisfies the localized energy inequality, that is, for any
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
,

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{2}-\frac{u^{1-p}}{p-1}\right)\eta^{2}\leq&-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\partial_{t}u|^{2}\eta^{2}-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}u\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta\right)\eta\\
&+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{2}-\frac{u^{1-p}}{p-1}\right)\eta\partial_{t}\eta.\end{split}

(2.6)

In the above, the energy inequality should be understood in the distributional sense. For positive smooth solutions, the stationary condition (
2.5
) can be obtained by multiplying (
1.1
) by
\nabla u\cdot Y
and integrating by parts, and the energy inequality (
2.6
) becomes an identity, which can be obtained by testing (
1.1
) with
\partial_{t}u\eta^{2}
and integrating by parts.

For any sequence of uniformly
\alpha
-Hölder continuous, suitable weak solutions, the following convergence result holds.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
(u_{i})
be a sequence of suitable weak solutions of (
1.1
) in
Q_{2}^{-}
satisfying

\sup_{i}\|u_{i}\|_{C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{Q^{-}_{3/2}})}<+\infty.

(2.7)

Then up to a subsequence,

(i):

u_{i}\to u_{\infty}
uniformly in
Q_{1}^{-}
;

(ii):

\nabla u_{i}\to\nabla u_{\infty}
strongly in
L^{2}(Q_{1}^{-})
,
\partial_{t}u_{i}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}u_{\infty}
weakly in
L^{2}(Q_{1}^{-})
and
u_{i}^{-p}\to u_{\infty}^{-p}
strongly in
L^{1}(Q_{1}^{-})
;

(iii):

outside
\{u=0\}
,
u_{i}
converges to
u_{\infty}
locally smoothly;

(iv):

u_{\infty}
is a suitable weak solution of (
1.1
) in
Q_{1}^{-}
.

Finally, we use this convergence theorem and blow up analysis to investigate the structure of the rupture set
\{u=0\}
.

Theorem 2.9
.

If
u
is a
C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}
-continuous suitable weak solution of (
1.1
) in
Q_{1}
, then the parabolic Hausdorff dimension of
\{u=0\}
is at most
n
.

A more precise result about the stratification of this rupture set and its time slices will be proved in Section
7
, see Theorem
7.10
and Theorem
7.13
.

3.
Preliminary

3.1.
Some notations

Let us first introduce some notations and definitions about the Hausdorff measure with respect to both the Euclidean and parabolic metrics. Throughout this paper, we will use upper case letters
X=(x,t),Y=(y,s),Z=(z,\tau)
,
\dots
to denote points in space-time and lower case letters
x,y,z
,
\dots
to denote points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The parabolic cylinder and backward parabolic cylinder are defined as usual,

Q_{r}(X):=B_{r}(x)\times(t-r^{2},t+r^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{r}^{-}(X):=B_{r}(x)\times(t-r^{2},t].

If the center is the origin
(0,0)
, it will be omitted.

Recall that the parabolic distance is defined by

\delta(X,Y):=\max\left\{|x-y|,|t-s|^{1/2}\right\}.

For a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda>0
, we let
A_{x_{0},\lambda}:={(A-x_{0})}/{\lambda}.
For
X,X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we define the parabolic scaling
\mathcal{D}
to be

\mathcal{D}_{X_{0},\lambda}(X):=\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\lambda},\frac{t-t_{0}}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad E_{X_{0},\lambda}:=\left\{\mathcal{D}_{X_{0},\lambda}(X):X\in E\right\}.

Next we recall the definitions of parabolic Hausdorff measure and parabolic Hausdorff dimension.

Definition 3.1
.

For any
s\geq 0
and
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the
s
-dimensional parabolic Hausdorff measure of
E
is defined by

\mathcal{P}^{s}(E):=\lim\limits_{\delta\to 0}\mathcal{P}^{s}_{\delta}(E),

where

\mathcal{P}^{s}_{\delta}(E):=\inf\left\{\sum_{i}r_{i}^{s}:E\subset\cup_{i}Q_{r_{i}}(X_{i}),~X_{i}\in E,~r_{i}\leq\delta\right\}.

Definition 3.2
.

The parabolic Hausdorff dimension of a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is the number

dim_{\mathcal{P}}(E):=\sup\left\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{P}^{s}(E)=+\infty\right\}=\inf\left\{s\geq 0:\mathcal{P}^{s}(E)=0\right\}.

To avoid abusing of notations, we use
\mathcal{H}^{s}
to denote the usual
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
dim_{\mathcal{H}}
to denote the corresponding Hausdorff dimension.

3.2.
Local well-posedness

Here we clarify the local existence of classical solutions to the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem. We use the assumptions on
\varphi
in Section
1
, i.e.
\varphi\in C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\partial^{p}\Omega_{T})
and
\varphi>0
.

Proposition 3.3
.

There exists
T_{1}>0
and
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{T_{1}})\cap C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{\Omega_{T_{1}}})
solving the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem for (
1.1
) in
\Omega_{T_{1}}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varphi\geq 2\varepsilon
. Set

f_{\varepsilon}(u):=\begin{cases}u^{-p},&\text{if}~~u>\varepsilon,\\
\varepsilon^{-p-1}u,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

which is a Lipschitz function of
u
. By standard parabolic theory, there exists a solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=-f_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\quad\text{in}~~\Omega_{\infty}:=\Omega\times(0,+\infty),\\
&u_{\varepsilon}=\varphi\quad\text{on}~~\partial^{p}\Omega_{\infty}.\end{aligned}\right.

Because
f_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
and
f_{\varepsilon}
is a Lipschitz function, the strong maximum principle implies that
u_{\varepsilon}>0
in
\Omega_{\infty}
.
Furthermore,
u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{\Omega_{\infty}})
. Thus there exists a
T_{1}>0
such that

u_{\varepsilon}>\varepsilon
in
\overline{\Omega_{T_{1}}}
. Then
u_{\varepsilon}
solves (
1.1
) in
\Omega_{T_{1}}
. Its smoothness in
\Omega_{T_{1}}
follows from standard parabolic regularity theory.
∎

This local solution is strictly positive, so it is a classical solution. It can be continued further until it touches
0
, that is, until the first quenching moment.

4.
Hölder estimate

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
2.5
by using the blow up method and the following Liouville theorem on two-valued, stationary caloric functions.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
u
be a stationary two-valued caloric function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
. If
u
is globally
C^{\gamma,\gamma/2}
-continuous for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
, then
u
must be a constant.

This theorem is implicitly contained in
[
5
, Section 5]
. For completeness, a proof will be given in Appendix
A
.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

In this subsection we prove Theorem
2.1
. In fact, we will prove a stronger result, which allows varying initial-boundary values.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose
T_{i}
is a sequence of positive constants tending to a positive limit
T
,
\varphi_{i}
is a sequence of positive
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions on
\partial^{p}\Omega_{T_{i}}
with uniformly bounded
\alpha
-Hölder norm, and
u_{i}\in C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{\Omega_{T_{i}}})
is a sequence of positive solutions to (
1.1
) in
\Omega_{T_{i}}
with initial-boundary value
\varphi_{i}
.
Then

\sup_{i}\|u_{i}\|_{C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}\left(\overline{\Omega_{T_{i}}}\right)}<+\infty.

In the setting of Theorem
2.1
, by our definition of the quenching time
T
, for any
t\in(0,T)
,
u\in C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{\Omega_{t}})
. This theorem then gives

\limsup_{t\to T}\|u\|_{C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{\Omega_{t}})}<+\infty,

which means
u\in C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}\left(\overline{\Omega_{T}}\right)
.

To prove Theorem
4.2
, let us assume by the contrary that

\sup_{X\neq Y\in\Omega_{T_{i}}}\frac{|u_{i}(X)-u_{i}(Y)|}{\delta(X,Y)^{\alpha}}\to+\infty\quad\text{as}~~i\to\infty.

Then there exist
X_{i}=(x_{i},t_{i}),Y_{i}=(y_{i},s_{i})\in\overline{\Omega_{T_{i}}}
such that

L_{i}:=\frac{|u_{i}(X_{i})-u_{i}(Y_{i})|}{\delta(X_{i},Y_{i})^{\alpha}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{X\neq Y\in\Omega_{T_{i}}}\frac{|u_{i}(X)-u_{i}(Y)|}{\delta(X,Y)^{\alpha}},

(4.1)

which tends to
+\infty
as
i\to\infty
.

Let
\lambda_{i}=\delta(X_{i},Y_{i})
and
Z_{i}=(\frac{y_{i}-x_{i}}{\lambda_{i}},\frac{s_{i}-t_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}})
. Define the blow-up sequence

\widetilde{u}_{i}(X):=L_{i}^{-1}\lambda_{i}^{-\alpha}u_{i}(x_{i}+\lambda_{i}x,t_{i}+\lambda_{i}^{2}t),

for
X=(x,t)\in\Omega^{i}:=\mathcal{D}_{X_{i},\lambda_{i}}(\Omega_{T_{i}})
.

A direct calculation shows that
\widetilde{u}_{i}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{i}-\Delta\widetilde{u}_{i}=-\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{-p}~\text{in}~\Omega^{i},

(4.2)

where
\varepsilon_{i}=L_{i}^{-(p+1)}\to 0
. Moreover, a scaling of (
4.1
) yields

\begin{split}|\widetilde{u}_{i}(0)-\widetilde{u}_{i}(Z_{i})|=1\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\overline{\Omega^{i}}}\frac{|\widetilde{u}_{i}(X)-\widetilde{u}_{i}(Y)|}{\delta(X,Y)^{\alpha}}\leq 2.\end{split}

(4.3)

Because
\delta(0,Z_{i})=1
, we may assume
Z_{i}\to Z_{\infty}
. Clearly, we still have
\delta(0,Z_{\infty})=1
.

Since
u_{i}=\varphi_{i}
on
\partial^{p}\Omega_{T_{i}}
,
\widetilde{u}_{i}=\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}
on
\partial^{p}\Omega^{i}
, where

\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(x,t):=L_{i}^{-1}\lambda_{i}^{-\alpha}\varphi_{i}(x_{i}+\lambda_{i}x,t_{i}+\lambda_{i}^{2}t).

Case 1.

\lambda_{i}\to 0
.

As
i\to\infty
,
\Omega^{i}
converges to a limit domain,
\Omega^{\infty}
, which could be

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,T_{0}]
,
H_{\infty}\times\mathbb{R}
,
H_{\infty}\times(-\infty,T_{0}]
,
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[T_{0},+\infty)
or
H_{\infty}\times(T_{0},+\infty)
,
where
H_{\infty}
is an half space in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
T_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant that may be different from one possibility to another.
Next we divide our analysis further into two subcases, depending on the behavior of
A_{i}:=\widetilde{u}_{i}(0)
.

Subcase 1.1.

A_{i}\to+\infty
.

Thanks to (
4.3
), perhaps after passing to a subsequence, we may assume
\widetilde{u}_{i}-A_{i}\to\widetilde{u}_{\infty}
uniformly in any compact subset of
\Omega^{\infty}
. Then sending
i\to\infty
in (
4.3
) leads to

1=|\widetilde{u}_{\infty}(0)-\widetilde{u}_{\infty}(Z_{\infty})|\geq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\Omega^{\infty}}\frac{|\widetilde{u}_{\infty}(X)-\widetilde{u}_{\infty}(Y)|}{\delta(X,Y)^{\alpha}},

(4.4)

which implies
\widetilde{u}_{\infty}
is globally Hölder continuous in
\Omega^{\infty}
.

Since
\varphi_{i}\in C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\partial^{p}\Omega_{T_{i}})
, for any
X=(x,t),Y=(y,s)\in\partial^{p}{\Omega^{i}}
,

\begin{split}|\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(X)-\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(Y)|&\leq L_{i}^{-1}\lambda_{i}^{-\alpha}\left|\varphi_{i}(X_{i}+\mathcal{D}_{\lambda_{i}^{-1}}(X))-\varphi_{i}(X_{i}+\mathcal{D}_{\lambda_{i}^{-1}}(Y))\right|\\
&\leq 2L_{i}^{-1}\delta(X,Y)^{\alpha}.\end{split}

By the uniform convergence of
u_{i}
, we see, when
\partial^{p}\Omega^{\infty}
is not empty, there exists a constant
A
such that
\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\equiv A
on
\partial_{p}Q^{\infty}
.

By (
4.3
), for any
R>0
, if
i
is large enough, then

\begin{split}\inf_{Q_{R}\cap\Omega^{i}}\widetilde{u}_{i}\geq A_{i}-R^{\alpha}\geq\frac{A_{i}}{2}.\end{split}

Plugging this lower bound into (
4.2
), we get

0\geq(\partial_{t}-\Delta)(\widetilde{u}_{i}-A_{i})\geq-2^{p}\varepsilon_{i}A_{i}^{-p}\to 0\quad\text{as $i\to\infty$}.

Hence for any
q>1
, by standard
W^{2,1}_{q}
estimates for heat equation,
(\widetilde{u}_{i}-A_{i})
are uniformly bounded in
W^{2,1}_{q,loc}(\Omega^{\infty})
. Then by Sobolev embedding theorem, for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
(\widetilde{u}_{i}-A_{i})
are uniformly bounded in
C^{1+\gamma,(1+\gamma)/2}_{loc}(\Omega^{\infty})

Lemma 4.4
.

There hold

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{i}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\quad\text{weakly in}~~L^{2}_{loc}(\Omega^{\infty}),\\
&\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}\to\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\quad\text{strongly in}~~L^{2}_{loc}(\Omega^{\infty}),\\
&\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{1-p}\to 0\quad\text{in}~~L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega^{\infty}).\end{aligned}\right.

(4.7)

Proof.

Take an
Q_{R}(X_{0})\subset\Omega^{\infty}
and an arbitrary
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(Q_{R}(X_{0}))
. By our assumption, for any
i
,
\widetilde{u}_{i}>0
in
Q_{R}(X_{0})
and thus
\widetilde{u}_{i}
is smooth in
Q_{R}(X_{0})
. Testing (
4.2
) with
\widetilde{u}_{i}\eta^{2}
, we obtain

-\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{2}\eta\partial_{t}\eta+\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}|^{2}\eta^{2}+\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{1-p}\eta^{2}=-\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}2\eta\widetilde{u}_{i}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}\cdot\nabla\eta.

(4.8)

It follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}|^{2}\eta^{2}+\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{1-p}\eta^{2}\leq C\left(\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{2}|\nabla\eta|^{2}+\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{2}\eta\partial_{t}\eta\right).

(4.9)

Testing (
4.2
) with
\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{i}\eta^{2}
, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (or equivalently, using the localized energy identity for
\widetilde{u}_{i}
), we get

\begin{split}\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}(\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{i})^{2}\eta^{2}&\leq C\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\left(|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}|^{2}+\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{1-p}\right)\eta\partial_{t}\eta.\end{split}

(4.10)

Since
(\widetilde{u}_{i})
are uniformly bounded in any compact set of
\Omega^{\infty}
, (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) imply that
\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{i}
and
\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}
are uniformly bounded in
L^{2}_{loc}(\Omega^{\infty})
. Thus up to subsequence,
\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{i}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}
and
\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}\rightharpoonup\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}
weakly in
L^{2}_{loc}(\Omega^{\infty})
.

Sending
i\to\infty
in (
4.8
) we also get

\begin{split}&\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\left(|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}|^{2}-|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}|^{2}\right)\eta^{2}+\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{1-p}\eta^{2}\\
=&-\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}|^{2}\eta^{2}+2\eta\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\eta+\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\eta^{2}.\end{split}

For any
t
, by Sard theorem, we can take a small
\tau>0
so that
\{\widetilde{u}_{\infty}=\tau\}\cap\{t\}
is a smooth hypersurface. Because
\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}=\Delta\widetilde{u}_{\infty}
in
\{\widetilde{u}_{\infty}>\tau\}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\{\widetilde{u}_{\infty}>\tau\}\cap\{t\}}\left[|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}|^{2}\eta^{2}+2\eta\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\eta+\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\eta^{2}\right]dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\int_{\{\widetilde{u}_{\infty}=\tau\}\cap\{t\}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}_{\infty}}{\partial\nu_{t}}\eta^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\int_{\{\widetilde{u}_{\infty}>\tau\}\cap\{t\}}\left(\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\eta^{2}+\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\eta^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\tau\left(\int_{\{\widetilde{u}_{\infty}>\tau\}\cap\{t\}}|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}|^{2}+|\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\right),

where
\nu_{t}
is the unit outer normal vector to
\{\widetilde{u}_{\infty}=\tau\}\cap\{t\}
. Letting
\tau\to 0
and then integrating in
t
, we see

-\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}|^{2}\eta^{2}+2\eta\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\cdot\nabla\eta+\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\widetilde{u}_{\infty}\eta^{2}=0.

Hence

\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\left(|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}|^{2}-|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{\infty}|^{2}\right)\eta^{2}+\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\int_{Q_{R}(X_{0})}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{u}_{i}^{1-p}\eta^{2}=0.

By the weak lower semi-continuity of
L^{2}-
norm, this establishes (
4.7
).
∎

) and (

(4.11)

Proof of Theorem

.

Take an

4.19
) gives

1\leq\tilde{u}_{\infty}(0)+\tilde{u}_{\infty}(Z_{\infty}).

where
Z_{\infty}:=\lim_{i}Z_{i}
. Thus
D:=\{\bar{u}_{\infty}>0\}
is non-empty. If
\{\bar{u}_{\infty}=0\}=\emptyset
, we argue as in Case 1 to get a contradiction.

If
\{\bar{u}_{\infty}=0\}\neq\emptyset
, following the analysis in Case 2 of the previous subsection, we deduce that
\bar{u}_{\infty}
is a stationary, two-valued caloric function. Therefore, by Theorem
4.1
,
\bar{u}_{\infty}
is a constant function, which contradicts the fact that both
\{\bar{u}_{\infty}=0\}
and
\{\bar{u}_{\infty}>0\}
are nonempty.
∎

5.
Convergence theory

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.8
. Recall that we assume
(u_{i})
is a sequence of suitable weak solutions of (
1.1
) in
Q_{2}^{-}=B_{2}(0)\times(-4,0]
, satisfying

\sup\limits_{i}\|u\|_{C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}(\overline{Q_{3/2}^{-}})}<+\infty.

(5.1)

By Theorem
2.5
, this includes the case that
(u_{i})
are positive solutions satisfying (
2.4
).

We first present several uniform a priori estimates on
u_{i}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
X\in\{u_{i}>0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}
, then
|\nabla u_{i}(X)|\leq Cu_{i}(X)^{\frac{1-p}{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
u_{i}(X)=:h^{\alpha}>0
. By the uniform Hölder continuity assumption (
5.1
), there exists a
\sigma>0
depending only on the Hölder semi-norm of
u_{i}
such that

h^{\alpha}/2\leq u_{i}\leq 2h^{\alpha}\quad\text{in}~~Q_{\sigma h}^{-}(X).

Define

\bar{u}_{i}(Y)=h^{-\alpha}u_{i}(X+\mathcal{D}_{h^{-1}}(Y)).

Then
\bar{u}_{i}
satisfies (
1.1
) and
1/2\leq\bar{u}\leq 2
in
Q_{\sigma}^{-}(0)
. By standard interior gradient estimates for parabolic equations,
|\nabla\bar{u}_{i}(0)|\leq C
. After rescaling back we get the desired estimate.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
i
, such that

(1) for any
i
,
X\in Q_{1}^{-}
and
r\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
,

\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}u_{i}^{-p}\leq Cr^{n+\frac{2}{p+1}},

(5.2)

(2) for any
i
,
X\in Q_{1}^{-}
and
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,

\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}\left(|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}+u_{i}^{1-p}\right)\leq Cr^{n+\frac{4}{p+1}};

(5.3)

(3) for any
i
,
X\in Q_{1}^{-}
and
r\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
,

\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}(\partial_{t}u_{i})^{2}\leq Cr^{n-2+\frac{4}{p+1}};

(5.4)

(4) [Nondegeneracy] for any
i
,
X\in Q_{1}^{-}
and
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,

\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}u_{i}\geq Cr^{n+2+\frac{2}{p+1}};

(5.5)

(5) for any
i
,
X,Y\in Q_{1}^{-}
,

|u_{i}(X)-u_{i}(Y)|\leq C\delta(X,Y)^{\alpha}.

(5.6)

Proof.

Take
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(Q_{2r}^{-}(X)\right)
satisfying

\eta\equiv 1~\text{in}~(Q_{r}^{-}(X))^{\circ},~|\nabla\eta|<Cr^{-1},~|\Delta\eta|+|\partial_{t}\eta|\leq Cr^{-2}.

(1) Testing the equation of
u_{i}
with
\eta
, we get

\begin{split}\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}u_{i}^{-p}&\leq\int u_{i}^{-p}\eta=\int(\Delta u_{i}-\partial_{t}u_{i})\eta\\
&=\int(\Delta\eta+\partial_{t}\eta)\left[u_{i}-u_{i}(X)\right]\leq Cr^{n+\frac{2}{p+1}},\end{split}

where we have used the
\alpha
-continuity of
u_{i}
. This gives the estimate in (
5.2
).

(2) By Hölder inequality,

\begin{split}\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}u_{i}^{1-p}\leq\left(\int_{Q_{r}^{-}}u_{i}^{-p}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}|Q_{r}^{-}|^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq Cr^{n+\frac{4}{p+1}}.\end{split}

Then using Lemma
5.1
, we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}=\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)\cap\{u>0\}}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}\leq C\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}u_{i}^{1-p}\leq Cr^{n+\frac{4}{p+1}},\end{split}

which establishes (
5.3
).

(3) To get inequality (
5.4
), we integrate the energy inequality (
2.6
) on
\mathbb{R}
and use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which leads to

\begin{split}\int_{Q_{2r}^{-}(X)}(\partial_{t}u_{i})^{2}\eta^{2}&\leq C\left[\int_{Q_{2r}^{-}(X)}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}-\frac{1}{p-1}u_{i}^{1-p}\right)2\eta\partial_{t}\eta+\int_{Q_{2r}^{-}(X)}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}|\nabla\eta|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq Cr^{-2}\int_{Q_{2r}^{-}(X)}\left[|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}+u_{i}^{1-p}\right]\\
&\leq Cr^{-2}r^{n+\frac{4}{p+1}}=Cr^{n-2+\frac{4}{p+1}},\end{split}

where we have used (
5.3
) and the fact that
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(Q_{2r}^{-}(X))
with
r\in(0,1/4)
.

(4) Testing (
1.1
) with
\eta
gives

\int_{Q_{r/2}^{-}(X)}u_{i}^{-p}\leq Cr^{-2}\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}u_{i}.

By the Jensen inequality, we also have

\int_{Q_{r/2}^{-}(X)}u_{i}^{-p}\geq\frac{1}{C}r^{-(n+2)(p+1)}\left(\int_{Q_{r/2}^{-}(X)}u_{i}\right)^{-p}\geq\frac{1}{C}r^{-(n+2)(p+1)}\left(\int_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}u_{i}\right)^{-p}.

Combining these two inequalities, we get (
5.5
).

(5) This is a corollary of the uniform Hölder continuity of
(u_{i})
.
∎

By inequalities (
5.3
), (
5.4
) and (
5.6
), we can assume that, up to a subsequence,
u_{i}\to u_{\infty}
uniformly in
Q_{1}^{-}
,
\partial_{t}u_{i}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}u_{\infty}
and
\nabla u_{i}\rightharpoonup\nabla u_{\infty}
weakly in
L^{2}(Q_{1}^{-})
. By this uniform convergence,
u_{\infty}
satisfies (
5.5
) and (
5.6
). Furthermore, for any domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\subset\{u_{\infty}>0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}
and
k\geq 1
, by standard parabolic estimates and Arzelà-Ascoli theorem,
u_{i}
converges to
u_{\infty}
in
C^{k}(\mathcal{D})
.

The remaining proof of Theorem
2.8
is divided into the following lemmas.

Lemma 5.3
.

\mathcal{P}^{n+\alpha}\left(\{u_{\infty}=0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}\right)=0.

Proof.

By inequality (
5.5
),

\sup_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)}u_{\infty}\geq\tfrac{1}{C}r^{\frac{2}{p+1}}\quad\text{for any}~X\in\{u_{\infty}=0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}~\text{and}~r\in\left(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

By the Hölder continuity of
u_{\infty}
, there exists a
Q_{\sigma r}^{-}(Y)\subset Q_{r}^{-}(X)
, where
\sigma
depends only on the Hölder semi-norm of
u_{\infty}
, such that

u_{\infty}\geq Cr^{\frac{2}{p+1}}\quad\text{in}~Q_{\sigma r}^{-}(Y).

This means for any
X\in\{u_{\infty}=0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}
and
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, we can find

Q_{\sigma r}^{-}(Y)\subset\{u_{\infty}>0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}.

As a consequence, the
\mathcal{P}^{n+2}
-density of
\{u_{\infty}=0\}
at
X
is strictly less than
1
. Since
\mathcal{P}^{n+2}
is equivalent to the standard Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, we deduce that
\mathcal{P}^{n+2}(\{u_{\infty}=0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-})=0
.

In any compact subset of
\{u_{\infty}>0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}
,
u_{i}^{-p}
converges to
u_{\infty}^{-p}
uniformly, hence
u_{i}^{-p}\to u_{\infty}^{-p}
,
\mathcal{P}^{n+2}

a.e.
in
Q_{1}^{-}
. By Fatou’s lemma,

\int_{Q_{1}^{-}}u_{\infty}^{-p}\leq\liminf\limits_{i\to\infty}\int_{Q_{1}^{-}}u_{i}^{-p}\leq C.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, take a maximal
\varepsilon
-separated set

\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\{u_{\infty}=0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}.

Then
\{Q_{\varepsilon/2}(X_{i})\}
are disjoint. Moreover, by denoting
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
to be the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
\{u_{\infty}=0\}
in
Q_{1}
, we have
\{u_{\infty}=0\}\subset\cup_{i=1}^{N}Q_{\varepsilon}(X_{i})\subset\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
.
Because

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{P}^{n+2}(\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{P}^{n+2}(\{u_{\infty}=0\})=0,

by the continuity of the Lebesgue integral, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{\varepsilon/2}^{-}(X_{i})}u_{\infty}^{-p}\leq\int_{\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}\cap Q_{1}^{-}}u_{\infty}^{-p}\to 0\quad\text{as}~\varepsilon\to 0^{+}.

(5.7)

Since
X_{i}\in\{u_{\infty}=0\}
and
u_{\infty}
is Hölder continuous,

\sup\limits_{Q_{\varepsilon/2}^{-}(X_{i})}u_{\infty}\leq C\varepsilon^{\alpha}.

This implies that

\int_{Q_{\varepsilon/2}^{-}(X_{i})}u_{\infty}^{-p}\geq C\varepsilon^{n+2-\frac{2p}{p+1}}=C\varepsilon^{n+\alpha}.

(5.8)

Plugging (
5.7
) into (
5.8
) gives us

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{\varepsilon/2}^{-}(x_{i},t_{i})}u_{\infty}^{-p}\to 0\quad\text{as}~~\varepsilon\to 0^{+}.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we conclude that
\mathcal{P}^{n+\alpha}\left(\{u_{\infty}=0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}\right)=0
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

The limit
u_{\infty}
satisfies estimates (
5.2
)-(
5.6
).

Proof.

We only need to show (
5.2
)-(
5.4
). Since
u_{i}^{-1}\to u_{\infty}^{-1}

\mathcal{P}^{n+2}
a.e. in
Q_{1}^{-}
, these estimates follow immediately from Fatou’s lemma and weak convergence of
\partial_{t}u_{i}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}u_{\infty}
and
\nabla u_{i}\rightharpoonup\nabla u_{\infty}
in
L^{2}_{loc}(Q_{3/2}^{-})
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

u_{i}^{-p}
converges to
u_{\infty}^{-p}
in
L^{1}(Q_{1}^{-})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.3
, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a family of parabolic cylinders
\{Q_{r_{k}}(X_{k})\}
with
r_{k}\leq\varepsilon
, which covers
\{u_{\infty}=0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}
and satisfies
\sum_{k}r_{k}^{n+\alpha}\leq\varepsilon
. For each
k
, choose
Y_{k}\in\{u_{\infty}=0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}\cap Q_{r_{k}}(X_{k})
such that
\mathcal{N}^{-}:=\cup_{k}Q_{4r_{k}}^{-}(Y_{k})
is an open backward neighborhood of
\{u_{\infty}=0\}\cap Q_{1}^{-}
. Then by the smooth convergence of
u_{i}
outside
\{u_{\infty}=0\}
,

\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\int_{Q_{1}^{-}\setminus\mathcal{N}^{-}}\left|u_{i}^{-p}-u_{\infty}^{-p}\right|=0.

(5.9)

Concerning the integral in
\mathcal{N}^{-}
, inequality (
5.2
) gives

\int_{\mathcal{N}^{-}}u_{i}^{-p}\leq\sum_{k}\int_{Q_{4r_{k}}^{-}(Y_{k})}u_{i}^{-p}\leq\sum_{k}(4r_{k})^{n+\alpha}\leq C\varepsilon.

(5.10)

The same inequality holds for
u_{\infty}^{-p}
(either by the same argument or simply using Fatou’s lemma).

Combining (
5.9
) and (
5.10
) leads to

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\int_{Q_{1}^{-}}\left|u_{\infty}^{-p}-u_{i}^{-p}\right|\leq C\varepsilon.

Sending

, we complete the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

u_{\infty}
is an

weak solution of (

) in

.

This corollary will be used in the following lemma to show the strong convergence of

in

.

Lemma 5.7
.

As
i\to\infty
, we have

\nabla u_{i}\to\nabla u_{\infty}~in~L^{2}(Q_{1}^{-})~\text{and}~u_{i}^{1-p}\to u_{\infty}^{1-p}~in~L^{1}(Q_{1}^{-}).

Proof.

By the elementary inequality
|t^{1-p}-s^{1-p}|\leq C(p)|t-s|(s^{-p}+t^{-p})
, we get

\begin{split}\int_{Q_{1}^{-}}|u_{i}^{1-p}-u_{\infty}^{1-p}|\leq C\sup\limits_{Q_{1}^{-}}|u_{i}-u_{\infty}|\int_{Q_{1}^{-}}|u_{i}^{-p}+u_{\infty}^{-p}|\leq C\sup\limits_{Q_{1}^{-}}|u_{i}-u_{\infty}|,\end{split}

which tends to zero by the uniform convergence of
u_{i}
to
u_{\infty}
in
Q_{1}^{-}
.

Next we take an
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{0}(Q_{3/2}^{-})
with
\eta\equiv 1
in
(Q_{1}^{-})^{\circ}
. Testing the equation of
u_{i}
by
u_{i}\eta^{2}
and integrating by parts, we get

\begin{split}\int_{Q_{3/2}^{-}}\left(\partial_{t}u_{i}u_{i}+|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}+u_{i}^{1-p}\right)\eta^{2}=\int_{Q_{3/2}^{-}}u_{i}^{2}\Delta\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}.\end{split}

Letting

yields

\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\int_{Q_{3/2}^{-}}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}\eta^{2}+\int_{Q_{3/2}^{-}}\left(\partial_{t}u_{\infty}u_{\infty}+u_{\infty}^{1-p}\right)\eta^{2}=\int_{Q_{3/2}^{-}}u_{\infty}^{2}\Delta\frac{\eta^{2}}{2},

(5.11)

where we have used the strong convergence of

to

in

and the weak convergence of

in

.
Since
u_{\infty}
is a

weak solution of (

), the same process yields

\int_{Q_{3/2}^{-}}|\nabla u_{\infty}|^{2}\eta^{2}+\int_{Q_{3/2}^{-}}\left(\partial_{t}u_{\infty}u_{\infty}+u_{\infty}^{1-p}\right)\eta^{2}=\int_{Q_{3/2}^{-}}u_{\infty}^{2}\Delta\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}.

Combining this equation with (
5.11
) gives the strong convergence

\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\int_{Q_{1}^{-}}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}=\int_{Q_{1}^{-}}|\nabla u_{\infty}|^{2}.\qed

Corollary 5.8
.

The limit function
u_{\infty}
is a suitable weak solution of (

) in

.

Proof.

Since

) with

.

6.
Monotonicity formula

In this section,
u
always denotes a
C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}
continuous suitable weak solution of (
1.1
) in
Q_{2}^{-}
.

Take an
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))
such that
\eta\equiv 1
in
B_{1/2}
and
|\nabla\eta|\leq C
.
Let

G(y,s):=\left(4\pi s\right)^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{|y|^{2}}{4s}}

be the standard heat kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For any
(x_{0},t_{0})\in Q_{1}^{-}
and
s>0
, define

\begin{split}E(s;x_{0},t_{0},u):=&s^{\frac{p-1}{p+1}}\int_{B_{2}}\left[\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u(x_{0}+y,t_{0}-s)|^{2}-\frac{1}{p-1}u(x_{0}+y,t_{0}-s)^{1-p}\right]\\
&\qquad\qquad\times G(y,s)\eta(x_{0}+y)dy\\
&-\frac{1}{2(p+1)}s^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}\int_{B_{2}}u(x_{0}+y,t_{0}-s)^{2}G(y,s)\eta(x_{0}+y)dy.\end{split}

At present, this is only understood as an integrable function of
s
. In the following, we will also use an averaged version of
E
,

\overline{E}(s;x_{0},t_{0},u):=\frac{1}{s}\int_{s}^{2s}E(\tau;x_{0},t_{0},u)d\tau,

which is a continuous function of
s
and
(x_{0},t_{0})
.

The main result of this section is the following monotonicity formula.

Proposition 6.1
.

For any
(x_{0},t_{0})\in Q_{1}^{-}
and
s\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(s;x_{0},t_{0},u)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle s^{-\frac{p+3}{p+1}}\int\left(\frac{u}{p+1}-\frac{y\cdot\nabla u}{2}+s\partial_{t}u\right)^{2}G(y,s)\eta(x_{0}+y)dy

\displaystyle-Ce^{-\frac{1}{Cs}}g(s)

in the distributional sense, where
g(s)
is an integrable function on
(0,1)
, and in the integral
u
etc. are evaluated at
(x_{0}+y,t_{0}-s)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume
(x_{0},t_{0})=(0,0)
. First by the localized energy inequality (
2.6
),

\begin{split}E^{\prime}(s)&\geq\frac{p-1}{p+1}s^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}\int\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{1}{p-1}u^{1-p}\right)G\eta\\
&+s^{\frac{p-1}{p+1}}\int|\partial_{t}u|^{2}G\eta+s^{\frac{p-1}{p+1}}\int\partial_{t}u\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla G\right)\eta\\
&+s^{\frac{p-1}{p+1}}\int\partial_{t}u\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta\right)G+\frac{1}{2}s^{\frac{p-1}{p+1}}\int\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{1}{p-1}u^{1-p}\right)\partial_{s}G\eta\\
&+\frac{s^{-\frac{p+3}{p+1}}}{(p+1)^{2}}\int u^{2}G\eta+\frac{s^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}}{(p+1)}\int u\partial_{t}uG\eta-\frac{s^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}}{2(p+1)}\int u^{2}\partial_{s}G\eta.\end{split}

In the above,
\nabla G
and
\partial_{s}G
can be replaced by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla G=-\frac{y}{2s}G,\\
\partial_{s}G=\Delta G=-\frac{1}{2s}\left(n-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2s}\right)G.\end{array}\right.

(6.1)

Next we take
Y=yG\eta
in the stationary condition (
2.5
). Because

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}divY=(n-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2s})G\eta+\left(y\cdot\nabla\eta\right)G,\\
DY(\nabla u,\nabla u)=\left[|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{(y\cdot\nabla u)^{2}}{2s}\right]G\eta+(y\cdot\nabla u)(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta)G,\\
\end{array}\right.

(6.2)

we get

\displaystyle\int\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{u^{1-p}}{p-1}\right)\left(n-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2s}\right)G\eta+\int\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{u^{1-p}}{p-1}\right)(y\cdot\nabla\eta)G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left[|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{|y\cdot\nabla u|^{2}}{2s}\right]G\eta+\partial_{t}u(y\cdot\nabla u)G\eta+\int(y\cdot\nabla u)(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta)G.

Plugging this identity into the above formula for
E^{\prime}(s)
, we get

E^{\prime}(s)\geq s^{-\frac{2}{p+1}-1}\int\left(\frac{u}{p+1}-\frac{y\cdot\nabla u}{2}+s\partial_{t}u\right)^{2}G\eta dx+I(s),

where
I(s)
is an integral involving
\nabla\eta
. Because
\nabla\eta=0
in
B_{1/2}
, we have

|I(s)|\leq Ce^{-\frac{1}{Cs}}g(s),

where the exponential term comes from the contribution of the heat kernel
G
, and

g(s):=\|u(-s)^{-p}\|_{L^{1}(B_{2})}+\|\partial_{t}u(-s)\|_{L^{2}(B_{2})}+\|\nabla u(-s)\|_{L^{2}(B_{2})}

is an
L^{1}((0,1))
function.
∎

This almost monotonicity of
E
allows us to define the density function

\Theta(x_{0},t_{0};u):=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}E(s;x_{0},t_{0},u).

Proposition 6.2
.

The density function
\Theta
is upper semi-continuous in the sense that, if
(u_{i})
is a sequence of uniformly
\alpha
-Hölder continuous, suitable weak solutions of (
1.1
) in
Q_{2}^{-}
, converging to
u_{\infty}
in the sense of Theorem
2.8
, and
(X_{i})
is a sequence of points in
Q_{1}^{-}
converging to
X_{\infty}
, then

\Theta(X_{\infty};u_{\infty})\geq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\Theta(X_{i};u_{i}).

Proof.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, choose an
s>0
such that
\overline{E}(s;X_{\infty},u_{\infty})\leq\Theta(X_{\infty};u_{\infty})+\varepsilon
. Because
X_{i}\to X_{\infty}
and
u_{i}\to u_{\infty}
in the sense of Theorem
2.8
, we have

\overline{E}(s;X_{i},u_{i})\to\overline{E}(s;X_{\infty},u_{\infty}).

Hence

\begin{split}\limsup_{i\to\infty}\Theta(X_{i};u_{i})&\leq\lim_{i\to\infty}\overline{E}(s;X_{i},u_{i})+Ce^{-\frac{1}{Cs}}\\
&=\overline{E}(s;X_{\infty},u_{\infty})+Ce^{-\frac{1}{Cs}}\leq\Theta(X_{\infty};u_{\infty})+\varepsilon+Ce^{-\frac{1}{Cs}}.\end{split}

Letting
s\to 0
and
\varepsilon\to 0
, we get the desired claim.
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

\Theta(x_{0},t_{0};u)
is finite if and only if
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\{u=0\}
. Equivalently,
\Theta(x_{0},t_{0};u)=-\infty
if and only if
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\{u>0\}
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality
(x_{0},t_{0})=(0,0)
.
Let us estimate
\overline{E}(s)
for
s>0
. Denote

u^{s}(x,t)=s^{-\frac{1}{p+1}}u(\sqrt{s}x,st),\quad\eta^{s}(x)=\eta\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right).

By the scaling invariance of
\overline{E}
, we get

\begin{split}\overline{E}(s)=&\int_{1}^{2}\int_{B_{s^{-1/2}}}t^{\frac{p-1}{p+1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u^{s}(x,-t)|^{2}-\frac{u^{s}(x,-t)^{1-p}}{p-1}\right)G(x,t)\eta^{t}(x)dxdt\\
&-\int_{1}^{2}\int_{B_{s^{-1/2}}}\frac{t^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}}{2(p+1)}u^{s}(x,-t)^{2}G(x,t)\eta^{t}(x)dxdt.\end{split}

We claim that the first integral is always bounded, that is,
there exists a constant
C
such that for any
s>0
,

\int_{1}^{2}\int_{B_{s^{-1/2}}}t^{\frac{p-1}{p+1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u^{s}(x,-t)|^{2}+\frac{u^{s}(x,-t)^{1-p}}{p-1}\right)G(x,t)\eta^{t}(x)dxdt\leq C.

(6.3)

This follows from the estimate (
5.2
) in Lemma
5.2
, which implies that for any
x\in B_{s^{-1/2}}
,

\int_{-2}^{-1}\int_{B_{1}(x)}\left(|\nabla u|^{2}+u^{1-p}\right)\leq C.

If
u(0,0)=0
, by the
2/(p+1)
-Hölder continuity of
u
(and hence
u^{s}
), we see

u^{s}(x,t)\leq C\left(1+|x|\right)^{\frac{2}{p+1}},\quad\forall(x,t)\in B_{s^{-1/2}}\times(-2,-1).

Hence there also exists a constant
C
such that for any
s>0
,

\int_{1}^{2}\int_{B_{s^{-1/2}}}\frac{t^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}}{2(p+1)}u^{s}(x,-t)^{2}G(x,t)\eta^{t}(x)dxdt\leq C.

(6.4)

Combining (
6.4
) and (
6.3
), we see in this case

\Theta(0,0;u)=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\overline{E}(s)\geq-C.

Next if
u(0,0)=h>0
, then by the Hölder continuity of
u
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

u\geq\frac{h}{2}\quad\text{in}~~Q_{ch}^{-}.

Hence for all
s<c^{2}h^{2}
,

u^{s}(x,t)\geq\tfrac{h}{2}s^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}\quad\text{in}~~Q_{chs^{-1/2}}^{-}.

This implies that

\int_{1}^{2}\int_{B_{s^{-1/2}}}\frac{t^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}}{2(p+1)}u^{s}(x,-t)^{2}G(x,t)\eta^{t}(x)dxdt\geq c(h)s^{-\frac{4}{p+1}},

which goes to
+\infty
as
s\to 0^{+}
. Combining this fact with (
6.3
), we get

\Theta(0,0;u)=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\overline{E}(s)=-\infty.\qed

7.
Stratification of rupture set

In this section, we prove a stratification theorem for the rupture set
\{u=0\}
, where
u
is an
\alpha
-Hölder continuous, suitable weak solution of (
1.1
) in
Q_{2}
. We will also show stratification of the time slice
\{u=0\}\cap\{t\}
for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Our method is essentially Federer’s dimension reduction principle. We will mainly use an abstract version of this principle developed by Brian White, see
[
19
, Theorem 2.3 and Theorem 8.2]
. We emphasize that the Hölder regularity and the convergence result established before are crucial for the application of this method to our problem.

7.1.
Blow-up limits

Because
u
is a
C^{\alpha,\alpha/2}
-continuous, suitable weak solution of (
1.1
) in
Q_{2}
, it satisfies the a priori estimates (
5.2
)-(
5.6
), and
\Sigma:=\{u=0\}
is closed.

For any
X_{0}\in\Sigma
, define the blow up sequence

u_{X_{0},\lambda}:=\lambda^{-\alpha}u\left(x_{0}+\lambda x,t_{0}+\lambda t\right),\quad\lambda\to 0.

Thanks to the
\alpha
-Hölder continuity of
u
, we can use Arzel
\grave{a}
-Ascoli theorem to find a subsequence (
\lambda_{i}
) and a limit
u_{0}
such that
u_{X_{0},\lambda_{i}}:=u_{i}\to u_{0}
uniformly in any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The limit may not be unique, so we use
\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
to denote the set of blow-up limits of
u
at
X_{0}
. In the setting of geometric measure theory,
\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
is usually called the set of tangent functions.

By Theorem
2.8
, we have

(i):

u_{i}^{1-p}\to u_{0}^{1-p}
in
L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
;

(ii):

\nabla u_{i}\to\nabla u_{0}
and
\partial_{t}u_{i}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}u_{0}
in
L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
;

(iii):

u_{0}
is a nonzero suitable weak solution of (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

For any
X_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
s>0
, define

\begin{split}E(s;X_{0},u_{0}):=&s^{\frac{p-1}{p+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\tfrac{|\nabla u_{0}(x_{0}+x,t_{0}-s)|^{2}}{2}-\frac{u_{0}(x_{0}+x,t_{0}-s)^{1-p}}{p-1}\right)G(x,s)dx\\
&-\frac{s^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}}{2(p+1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{0}(x_{0}+x,t_{0}-s)^{2}G(x,s)dx.\end{split}

The following result is a simple corollary of the convergence of
u_{i}
specified above.

Lemma 7.1
.

For a.e.
s\geq 0
,

\lim_{i\to+\infty}E(s;X_{0},u_{i})=E(s;X_{0},u_{0}).

Next we establish the self-similar property of
u_{0}
, which will play an important role in the analysis of the density function
\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
X_{0}\in\Sigma
. Then any
u_{0}\in\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
is backward self-similar with respect to
(0,0)
, that is, for any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
and
\lambda>0
,

u_{0}(x,t)=\lambda^{-\alpha}u_{0}(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t).

(7.1)

Moreover,
\Theta(0;u_{0})=\Theta(X_{0};u)=E(s;0,u_{0})
for any
s>0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.1
, for a.e.
0<s_{1}<s_{2}<+\infty
,

\begin{split}&E(s_{2};0,u_{0})-E(s_{1};0,u_{0})\\
&\geq\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}t^{-\frac{p+3}{p+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\frac{u_{0}(x,-t)}{p+1}-\frac{x\cdot\nabla u_{0}(x,-t)}{2}+t\partial_{t}u_{0}(x,-t)\right]^{2}G(x,t)dxdt.\end{split}

By Lemma
7.1
, for a.e.
s>0
,

\begin{split}E(s;0,u_{0})=\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}E(s;0,u_{i})=\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}E(\lambda_{i}^{2}s;X_{0},u)=\Theta(X_{0};u).\end{split}

Thus
\Theta(0;u_{0})=\Theta(X_{0};u)
and

\begin{split}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}t^{-\frac{p+3}{p+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\frac{u_{0}(x,-t)}{p+1}-\frac{x\cdot\nabla u_{0}(x,-t)}{2}+t\partial_{t}u_{0}(x,-t)\right]^{2}G(x,t)dxdt=0.\end{split}

Since
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are arbitrary, we get

\frac{u_{0}(x,t)}{p+1}-\frac{x}{2}\cdot\nabla u_{0}(x,t)-t\partial_{t}u_{0}(x,t)=0\quad\text{a.e. in}~\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0).

Integrating this equation along
\{x=\sqrt{-t}y\}
(for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
) and then using the continuity of
u
, we get (
7.1
).
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

For any
u_{0}\in\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
,
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
and
\lambda>0
,

\Theta(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t;u_{0})=\Theta(x,t;u_{0}).

Proof.

By the self-similarity of
u_{0}
, for any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
and
\lambda>0
,

E(\lambda^{2}s;\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t,u_{0})=E(s;x,t,u_{0}).

Sending
s\to 0^{+}
, we get the desired claim by the definition of
\Theta
.
∎

Proposition 7.4
.

For any
u_{0}\in\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
and
X=(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\Theta(X;u_{0})\leq\Theta(0;u_{0})
.

Proof.

Define the blow-down sequence of
u_{0}
at
X
by

(u_{0})_{X,\lambda}(Y)=\lambda^{-\alpha}u_{0}(x+\lambda y,t+\lambda^{2}s),

where
Y=(y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
and
\lambda\to+\infty
. Since
u_{0}
is backward self-similar with respect to
(0,0)
and globally Hölder continuous, we see

(u_{0})_{X,\lambda}(Y)=u_{0}(\lambda^{-1}x+y,\lambda^{-2}t+s)\to u_{0}(Y)

locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
. This uniform convergence implies that Theorem
2.8
holds for
(u_{0})_{X,\lambda}
. Then for a.e.
s>0
,

\displaystyle\Theta(0;u_{0})

\displaystyle\equiv E(s;0,u_{0})=\lim\limits_{\lambda\to+\infty}E(s;0,(u_{0})_{X,\lambda})=\lim\limits_{\lambda\to+\infty}E(\lambda^{2}s;X,u_{0})

(7.2)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\tau\to+\infty}E(\tau;X,u_{0})\geq\Theta(X;u_{0}).

Here the first equality follows from Lemma
7.1
, and in the last inequality we have used the monotonicity formulat at
X
.
∎

Proposition 7.5
.

If
\Theta(X;u_{0})=\Theta(0;u_{0})
, then

(1)

u_{0}
is backward self-similar with respect to
X
;

(2)

for any
Y=(y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
and
\lambda>0
,

\Theta(X+Y;u_{0})=\Theta(X+\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(Y);u_{0});

(3)

if
t\leq 0
, then for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
Y=(y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
,

u_{0}(ax+y,a^{2}t+s)=u_{0}(y,s).

Proof.

(1) If
\Theta(X;u_{0})=\Theta(0;u_{0})
, (
7.2
) implies that
E(\tau;X,u_{0})\equiv\Theta(X;u_{0})
, which means
u_{0}
is also backward self-similar with respect to
X
.

(2) This is the same with Corollary
7.3
.

(3) For any
a>0
, by Corollary
7.3
and (2),

\Theta(\mathcal{D}_{a^{-1}}(X);u_{0})=\Theta(X;u_{0})=\Theta(0;u_{0}).

Thus
u_{0}
is backward self-similar with respect to
(ax,a^{2}t)
. Since
u_{0}
is also backward self-similar with respect to
(0,0)
,
for any
\lambda>0
, we have

u_{0}(ax+y,a^{2}t+s)=\lambda^{-\alpha}u_{0}(ax+\lambda y,a^{2}t+\lambda^{2}s)=u_{0}(\lambda^{-1}ax+y,\lambda^{-2}a^{2}t+s).

Since
u_{0}
is continuous, for any
(y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
,

\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}u_{0}(\lambda^{-1}ax+y,(a\lambda)^{-2}t+s)=u_{0}(y,s).

Hence

u_{0}(ax+y,a^{2}t+s)=u_{0}(y,s).

A change of variables shows this also holds for
a<0
.
∎

Remark 7.6
.

According to the definition in
[
19
, Section 8]
,
\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})
is a
backward conelike function
.

7.2.
Stratification theorem

For any
X_{0}\in\Sigma
and
u_{0}\in\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
, define

\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})):=\left\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\Theta(X;u_{0})=\Theta(0;u_{0})\right\},

\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})):=\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))\cap\{t=0\},

\mathcal{W}(\Theta(0;u_{0})):=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\Theta(y,s;u_{0})=\Theta(y+x,s;u_{0}),~\forall(y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]\Big\}.

Similar to
[
19
, Theorem 8.1]
, by the backward conelike property (i.e. Proposition
7.4
and Proposition
7.5
), we have

Proposition 7.7
.

For any
X_{0}\in\Sigma
and
u_{0}\in\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
, the followings hold.

(1)

\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))=\mathcal{W}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))
, which is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover,
u_{0}
is translation invariant in all directions of this subspace.

(2)

Either
\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))=\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))
or
\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))\times(-\infty,a]
for some
0\leq a\leq+\infty
. In the later case
\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})
and
u_{0}
are time independent up to
t=a
.

Definition 7.8
.

For any
X_{0}\in\Sigma
and
u_{0}\in\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
, the space-time spine of
\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})
is

S(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))\times\mathbb{R},~~\text{if}~\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))=\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))\times\mathbb{R},\\
\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})),~~\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

The dimension of the space-time spine is defined by

dim(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dim(\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0})))+2,~~\text{if}~\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))=\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))\times\mathbb{R},\\
dim(\mathcal{V}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))),~~\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Notice that
S(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))
is the largest
\mathcal{D}
-invariant subspace contained in
\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))
.

Definition 7.9
.

For any
0\leq k\leq n+2
, define

\Sigma_{k}=\left\{X_{0}\in\Sigma:dim(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))\leq k,~\forall u_{0}\in\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})\right\}.

By definition, we have
\Sigma_{0}\subset\cdots\subset\Sigma_{n+2}
. Our main result on this stratification is

Theorem 7.10
.

(1)

\Sigma_{n+2}\setminus\Sigma_{n}=\emptyset
, that is,
\Sigma=\Sigma_{n}
.

(2)

For any
0\leq k\leq n
,
dim_{\mathcal{P}}(\Sigma_{k})\leq k
, and
\Sigma_{0}
is discrete.

(3)

For
\mathcal{L}^{1}
a.e.
t
, the set

S_{k}(t):=\left\{x:(x,t)\in\Sigma_{k}\right\}

has Hausdorff dimension at most
k-2
.

The proof of this theorem is almost the same with the one for
[
19
, Theorem 8.2]
. The only property needed to check is the first point. The fact that
\Sigma_{n+2}\setminus\Sigma_{n+1}=\emptyset
is trivial, because any
u_{0}\in\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
can not be identically zero by (
5.5
) in Lemma
5.2
. The fact that
\Sigma_{n+1}\setminus\Sigma_{n}=\emptyset
is a consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 7.11
.

For any
X_{0}\in\Sigma
and
u_{0}\in\mathcal{T}(u,X_{0})
,
\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))
can not be
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}
,
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\{0\}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Case 1.
If
\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}
, then
u_{0}(x,0)\equiv 0
. Hence
u_{0}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u_{0}-\Delta u_{0}=-u_{0}^{-p}~~\text{in}~\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,+\infty),\\
u_{0}(x,0)=0~~\text{in}~\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{array}\right.

(7.3)

Because
u_{0}^{-p}\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
and
u_{0}
is globally Hölder continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, by Duhamel principle, we have the representation formula

u_{0}(x,t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{0}(x-y,t-s)^{-p}G(y,s)dyds,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},~t>0.

As a consequence,
u_{0}(x,t)<0
for any
t>0
. This contradicts the fact that
u_{0}\geq 0
.

Case 2.
If
\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\{0\}
, by Proposition
7.5
,
u_{0}
can be viewed as a suitable weak solution of (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}\times(-\infty,0]
. Because it is backward self-similar (see Proposition
7.2
), by
[
8
, Theorem 10.3.3]
, either
u_{0}=k(-t)^{\frac{1}{p+1}}
or
w(x):=(-t)^{-\frac{1}{p+1}}u_{0}(\sqrt{-t}x,t)
is convex in
x
for all
|x|
large enough. For the first possibility, using the fact that
u_{0}\equiv 0
at
t=0
, we get the same contradiction as in Case 1. The second possibility implies that
w
grows linearly at infinity, which contradicts with the fact that it is globally
\alpha
-Hölder continuous.

Case 3.
If
\mathcal{M}(\Theta(\cdot;u_{0}))=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
,
by Proposition
7.7
, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(-\infty,0]
, there holds

u_{0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{a^{-1}}(X)+Y\right)=u_{0}(Y).

In particular,
u_{0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{a^{-1}}(X)\right)=u_{0}(0)=0
and thus
u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(-\infty,0]
, which contradicts the fact that

\mathcal{P}^{n+\alpha}\left(\{u_{0}=0\}\cap Q_{1}\right)=0,

see Lemma
5.3
.
∎

Appendix A
A Liouville theorem

In this appendix, we give a proof of Theorem
4.1
. The proof uses blow down analysis with the help of Almgren monotonicity formula and the global Hölder continuity of the solution.

In this appendix,
u
denotes a globally
\gamma
-continuous, stationary, two-valued caloric function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
, where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is a constant. If
\{u=0\}=\emptyset
,
u
is a positive, globally Hölder continuous solution to the heat equation, so by the generalized Liouville theorem,
u
is a constant. Thus, for the remainder of the proof, we assume
\{u=0\}\neq\emptyset
, say
u(0,0)=0
, and we will prove
u\equiv 0
.

For any
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
and
s>0
, define

H(s;x_{0},t_{0}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x_{0}+x,t_{0}-s)^{2}G(x,s)dx,

and

D(s;x_{0},t_{0}):=s\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u(x_{0}+x,t_{0}-s)|^{2}G(x,s)dx,

where
G
is the standard heat kernel. The frequency is defined to be

N(s;x_{0},t_{0}):=\frac{D(s;x_{0},t_{0})}{H(s;x_{0},t_{0})}=\frac{s\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u(x_{0}+x,t_{0}-s)|^{2}G(x,s)dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x_{0}+x,t_{0}-s)^{2}G(x,s)dx}.

We first establish the Almgren monotonicity formula. For simplicity, we will assume
(x_{0},t_{0})=(0,0)
.

Lemma A.1
.

For any
s>0
,

H^{\prime}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}2u\left(-\partial_{t}u+\frac{x\cdot\nabla u}{2s}\right)Gdx=\frac{2D(s)}{s}.

(A.1)

Hereafter
u
is always evaluated at
(x,-s)
and
G
is evaluated at
(x,s)
.

Proof.

This follows from a direct differentiation and then an integration by parts, using the fact that
\nabla G(x,s)=-\frac{x}{2s}G(x,s)
.
∎

Corollary A.2
.

For any
s>0
, if
H(s)\neq 0
, then

\frac{d}{ds}\log H(s)=\frac{2N(s)}{s}.

(A.2)

Lemma A.3
.

For any
s>0
,

D^{\prime}(s)\geq 2s\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\partial_{t}u-\frac{x\cdot\nabla u}{2s}\right)^{2}Gdx.

(A.3)

Proof.

Direct calculation gives

D^{\prime}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{2}G+s\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\int|\nabla u|^{2}G.

(A.4)

For
m\geq 1
, choose
\rho_{m}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{1+m})
such that

\rho_{m}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,~\text{if}~|x|\leq 1,\\
1+\frac{1-|x|}{m},~\text{if}~1\leq|x|\leq 1+m,\\
0,~\text{if}~|x|\geq 1+m.\end{array}\right.

Clearly,
\rho_{m}
converges to the constant function
1
. Let
\eta_{m}=\rho_{m}\sqrt{G}
. Substituting
\eta_{m}
into the localized energy inequality (
2.2
) and letting
m\to+\infty
, we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{ds}\int|\nabla u|^{2}G&\geq 2\int|\partial_{t}u|^{2}G+2\int\partial_{t}u(\nabla G\cdot\nabla u)-\int\partial_{s}G|\nabla u|^{2}\\
&=2\int|\partial_{t}u|^{2}G+2\int\partial_{t}u\frac{x\cdot\nabla u}{2s}G-\int\Delta G|\nabla u|^{2}.\end{split}

(A.5)

Substituting
Y=xG
into the stationary condition (
2.1
), we get

\begin{split}\int\Delta G|\nabla u|^{2}&=s^{-1}\int\left[|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{(x\cdot\nabla u)^{2}}{2s}+(x\cdot\nabla u)\partial_{t}u\right]Gdx.\end{split}

(A.6)

As a result, (
A.3
) follows from (
A.4
), (
A.5
) and (
A.6
).
∎

Lemma A.4

(Almgren monotonicity formula)
.

On
(0,+\infty)
,
N(s)
is non-decreasing in
s
. Moreover, if
N(s)\equiv d/2
for some
d\geq 0
, then
u
is backward self-similar of degree
d
, i.e., for any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(-\infty,0]
and
\lambda>0
,

u(x,t)=\lambda^{-d}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t).

Proof.

Since

N^{\prime}(s)=\frac{D^{\prime}(s)H(s)-D(s)H^{\prime}(s)}{H(s)^{2}},

it suffices to show
D^{\prime}(s)H(s)-D(s)H^{\prime}(s)\geq 0
. By Lemma
A.1
and Lemma
A.3
,

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(s)H(s)-D(s)H^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2s\left[\left(\int\left(\partial_{t}u-\frac{x\cdot\nabla u}{2s}\right)^{2}G\right)\left(\int u^{2}G\right)-\left(\int u\left(\partial_{t}u-\frac{x\cdot\nabla u}{2s}\right)G\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0,

where we have used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to deduce the last inequality.

If
N(s)\equiv d/2
, by characterization of the equality case in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for any
t<0
, there exists an
h(t)
such that

u(x,t)=h(t)\left[2t\partial_{t}u(x,t)+x\cdot\nabla u(x,t)\right]\quad\text{for a.e. }~~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This is a differential equation in the time variable
t
. An integration shows that there exists a function
\Psi(t)
such that for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

u(x,t)\equiv\Psi(t)u\left(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{-t}},-1\right).

(A.7)

By the continuity of
u
, this holds for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Finally, substituting (
A.7
) into the equation
N(s)\equiv d/2
, we get

\Psi(t)\equiv(-t)^{\frac{d}{2}},

which implies that
u
is backward self-similar with respect to
(0,0)
.
∎

Remark A.5

(Unique continuation property)
.

Combining the above Almgren monotonicity formula with Corollary
A.2
, we can show as in Caffarelli-Karakhanyan-Lin
[
4
, Theorem 30]
that unless
u\equiv 0
, then for any
s>0

H(s)>0
.

Lemma A.6
.

For any
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\{u=0\}
and
s\geq 0
,
N(s;x_{0},t_{0})=\gamma/2
.

Proof.

Assume
(x_{0},t_{0})=(0,0)
.

Step 1.
For any
s>0
,
N(s)\leq\gamma/2
.

Assume by the contrary, there exists an
s_{0}>0
such that
d/2:=N(s_{0})>\gamma/2
. Then by Lemma
A.4
, for any
s\geq s_{0}
,
N(s)\geq d/2
. Hence by Corollary
A.2
,

\frac{d}{ds}\mathrm{log}H(s)=\frac{2N(s)}{s}\geq\frac{d}{s}.

Integrating from
s_{0}
to
s
, we see for any
s\geq s_{0}
,

\frac{H(s)}{H(s_{0})}\geq\left(\frac{s}{s_{0}}\right)^{d}.

(A.8)

However, since
u
is globally
\gamma
-Hölder continuous and
u(0,0)=0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

|u(x,-s)|^{2}\leq C(|x|^{2\gamma}+s^{\gamma})~~\text{for any}~~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},~s>0,

which implies that for any
s>0
,

H(s)\leq Cs^{\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(1+\frac{|x|^{2}}{s}\right)^{\gamma}G(x,s)dx\leq Cs^{\gamma}.

(A.9)

This is a contradiction with (
A.8
) if
s
is sufficiently large.

Step 2.
For any
s>0
,
N(s)\geq\gamma/2
.

Assume by the contrary that there exists
s_{0}>0
such that
d/2:=N(s_{0})<\gamma/2
. Then by Lemma
A.4
, for any
s\in(0,s_{0})
,
N(s)\leq d/2
.
Hence by Lemma
A.1
,

\frac{d}{ds}\frac{H(s)}{s^{d}}=-ds^{-d-1}H(s)+2s^{-d-1}D(s)\leq 0,

which implies that for
s\in(0,s_{0})
,

H(s)\geq\frac{H(s_{0})}{s_{0}^{d}}s^{d}.

(A.10)

But by (
A.9
),
H(s)\leq Cs^{\gamma}
. If

is close to zero, this is a contradiction.
∎

Appendix B
An half space problem

In this appendix we study an half space problem for stationary two-valued caloric functions.

Theorem B.1
.

Suppose
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
u
is a bounded, globally
\gamma
-Hölder continuous, stationary two-valued caloric function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)
, where
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:=\{x_{n}>0\}
. If
u=1
on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)
, then
u\equiv 1
.

First we need a regularity theory for stationary two-valued caloric functions, see
[
18
]
.

Proposition B.2
.

Suppose
u
is a Hölder continuous, stationary two-valued caloric function in
Q_{1}^{-}
. Then for any
t\in[-1,0]
, either
u(t)\equiv 0
in
B_{1}
or
u(t)
cannot vanish in any open subset of
B_{1}
.

Moreover, if it is the later case, then for any
r<1
, in
Q_{r}^{-}
, we have

(i) Lipschitz regularity:

u
is Lipschitz continuous, with the estimate

\sup_{Q_{r}^{-}}|\nabla u|\leq\frac{C}{1-r}\sup_{Q_{1}^{-}}u;

(ii) Hausdorff dimension:

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{P}}(\{u=0\})\leq n+1
;

(iii) Decomposition:

\{u=0\}=\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{R}
is relatively open and
\mathcal{S}
is closed;

(iv) Regular set:

X\in\mathcal{R}
if and only if there exists an
r>0
and a caloric function
\Phi
in
Q_{r}^{-}(X)
, with
\Phi(X)=0
and
\nabla\Phi(X)\neq 0
, such that

\{u=0\}\cap Q_{r}^{-}(X)=\{\Phi=0\}
, which is a smooth hypersurface, and
u=|\Phi|
in
Q_{r}^{-}(X)
;

(iv) Singular set:

X\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if

\limsup_{r\to 0}\sup_{Q_{r}^{-}(X)\cap\{u>0\}}|\nabla u|=0;

(v) Hausdorff dimension for singular set:

\text{dim}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S})\leq n
.

(vi) One dimensional case:

If
n=1
, then
\mathcal{S}
is a discrete set, and
\mathcal{R}
consists of regular curves with (possible) end points in
\mathcal{S}
.

The first claim in this proposition is a unique continuation (in spatial directions) property. Applying this result in our setting, because

u
is continuous and
u=1
on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)
, we see for any
t\leq 0
,
u(t)
cannot be identically zero. Thus the second alternative holds for all of
u(t)
. Consequently, the above properties (i)-(vi) hold for
u
.

Lemma B.3
.

For any constant vector
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
|\partial_{\xi}u|
is continuous and sub-caloric.

Proof.

Since
u
is smooth in
\{u>0\}
,
|\partial_{\xi}u|
is continuous here. Note also
\partial_{\xi}u
satisfies the heat equation in this open set, so
|\partial_{\xi}u|
is sub-caloric in
\{u>0\}
.

Next we show that
|\partial_{\xi}u|
is continuous across
\mathcal{R}
. In fact, for any
X\in\mathcal{R}
, by taking the cylinder
Q_{r}^{-}(X)
and the caloric function
\Phi
from (iv) in the previous proposition, we see

|\partial_{\xi}u|\equiv|\partial_{\xi}\Phi|\quad\text{in}~~Q_{r}^{-}(X).

Hence
|\partial_{\xi}u|
is continuous and sub-caloric in
Q_{r}^{-}(X)
.

Finally, by (iv) of the previous proposition,
|\partial_{\xi}u|
converges uniformly to
0
when approaching
\mathcal{S}
. Therefore
|\partial_{\xi}u|
is continuous near
\mathcal{S}
. Now
\mathcal{S}\subset\{|\partial_{\xi}u|=0\}
, and we have shown that
|\partial_{\xi}u|
is sub-caloric outside
\mathcal{S}
, the corollary then follows by an approximation.
∎

With these tools in hand, we first reduce the proof of Theorem
B.1
to a simpler setting.

Lemma B.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
B.1
,
u
must be independent of
x_{1}
,
\dots
,
x_{n-1}
.

Proof.

For any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)
, by the boundedness of
u
, applying (i) of Proposition
B.2
to
u
in
Q_{x_{n}/2}^{-}(x,t)
, we see there exists a constant
C
independent of
(x,t)
such that

|\nabla u(x,t)|\leq Cx_{n}^{-1}.

(B.1)

Because
u\equiv 1
on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
u
is globally
\gamma
-Hölder continuous,
u>0
in
\{0<x_{n}<\delta\}\times(-\infty,0)
for some
\delta>0
. Thus
u
is smooth near
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)
, and
\partial_{x_{i}}u\equiv 0
on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)
.

Take an aribitrary
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Denote
\varphi:=|\partial_{x_{i}}u|
. By Corollary
B.3
, it is a nonnegative, continuous sub-carloric function. By the discussions in the previous two paragraphs,
\varphi=0
on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0]
and
\varphi\to 0
uniformly as
x_{n}\to+\infty
. We want to show that it is identically zero.

Assume by the contrary that
\varphi
is nonzero. Then

\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)}\varphi>0.

Take a sequence
(x_{k},t_{k})
so that
\varphi(x_{k},t_{k})
approaches this supremum. By the strong maximum principle,
t_{k}\to-\infty
.

Assume
x_{k}=(x_{1,k},\cdots,x_{n,k})
. By (
B.1
),
x_{n,k}
is a bounded sequence. Take a subsequence so that
x_{n,k}\to x_{n,\infty}
.
Let
x_{k}^{\prime}=(x_{k,1},\cdots,x_{k,n-1},0)
,
u_{k}(x,t):=u(x_{k}^{\prime}+x,t_{k}+t)
and
\varphi_{k}(x,t):=\varphi(x_{k}^{\prime}+x,t_{k}+t)
. By the global Hölder continuity of
u
and Proposition
B.2
, up to a subsequnce,
u_{k}
converges to a limit
u_{\infty}
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, and
\varphi_{k}=|\partial_{x_{i}}u_{k}|
converges to the limit function
\varphi_{\infty}=|\partial_{x_{i}}u_{\infty}|
.

Since

\varphi_{\infty}((0,\cdots,x_{n,\infty}),0)=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)}\varphi_{\infty}=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)}\varphi>0,

the strong maximum principle implies that
\varphi_{\infty}
is a constant. This is a contradiction with the boundary value condition
\varphi_{\infty}=0
on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,+\infty)
.
∎

To finish the proof of Theorem
B.1
, it remains only to prove the
n=1
case.

Proof of Theorem
B.1
in the one dimensional case.

By (
B.1
), for any
t\in(-\infty,0]
, the Dirichlet energy

D(t):=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\partial_{x}u(x,t)^{2}dx

is well-defined. Moreover, because
u
is globally
\gamma
-Hölder continuous, there exists a constant
C
such that

|\partial_{x}u(x,t)|\leq C\quad\text{in }~\left\{0<x<\frac{1}{C}\right\}\times(-\infty,0].

Combining this estimate with (
B.1
), we see

\sup_{t\leq 0}D(t)<+\infty.

(B.2)

By choosing suitable cut-off functions in the localized energy inequality (
2.2
) in the definition of stationary two-valued caloric functions, and noticing that
\partial_{t}u(0,t)\equiv 0
(by the boundary condition and the smoothness of
u
near the boundary), we get a global energy inequality: for any
-\infty<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq 0
,

D(t_{2})-D(t_{1})\leq-2\int_{t_{2}}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\partial_{t}u(x,t)^{2}dxdt.

(B.3)

In view of (
B.2
), this implies that

\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\partial_{t}u(x,t)^{2}dx\right)dt<+\infty.

(B.4)

For any
t_{i}\to-\infty
, let

u_{i}(x,t)=u(x,t_{i}+t).

By the global Hölder regularity of
u
and Proposition
B.2
, up to a subsequence,
u_{i}
converges to a limit
u_{\infty}
locally uniformly on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
. Here
u_{\infty}
is still a Hölder continuous, stationary two-valued caloric function. Furthermore, by passing limit in (
B.4
), we deduce that

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\partial_{t}u_{\infty}(x,t)^{2}dx\right)dt=0.

Hence
u_{\infty}
is a function of
x
only. Then condition (ii) in Definition
2.3
implies that
\partial_{xx}u_{\infty}=0
in
\{u_{\infty}>0\}
. However, since we still have
0\leq u_{\infty}\leq 1
in
[0,+\infty)
and
u_{\infty}(0)=1
, the only possibility is that
u_{\infty}\equiv 1
. This limit is independent of the choice of subsequences, so we arrive at the conclusion that

u(\cdot,t)\to 1\quad\text{locally uniformly on }~[0,+\infty)~\text{as}~t\to-\infty.

(B.5)

Next we claim that

\lim_{t\to-\infty}D(t)=0.

(B.6)

To prove this claim, take an arbitrary
R>0
. By (
B.5
), there exists an
T_{R}
such that if
t\leq-T_{R}
,
u(x,t)
is very close to
1
in
[0,2R]
. In particular,
u
satisfies the heat equation in
(0,R)\times(-\infty,-T_{R})
. In view of (
B.5
), by interior and boundary gradient estimates for the heat equation, we get

\lim_{t\to-\infty}\int_{0}^{R}\partial_{x}u(x,t)^{2}dx=0.

(B.7)

For the part on
[R,+\infty)
, we simply use (
B.1
) to get

\int_{R}^{+\infty}\partial_{x}u(x,t)^{2}dx\leq\frac{C}{R}.

(B.8)

Combining (
B.7
) and (
B.8
), and then letting
R\to+\infty
, we get (
B.6
).

With (
B.6
) in hand, by the monotonicity of
D(t)
(from (
B.3
)), we must have

D(t)\equiv 0\quad\text{for any}~t\leq 0.

Therefore
\partial_{x}u=0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(-\infty,0)
. Then
u\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times(-\infty,0]
because this holds on the boundary and
u
is continuous.
∎
