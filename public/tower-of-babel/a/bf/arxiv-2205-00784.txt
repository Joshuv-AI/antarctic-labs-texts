On verifying expectations and observations of intelligent agents
Sourav Chakraborty, Avijeet Ghosh, Sujata Ghosh, François Schwarzentruber

Abstract
Public observation logic ( 𝖯𝖮𝖫 \mathsf{POL} ) is a variant of dynamic epistemic logic to reason about agent expectations and agent observations. Agents have certain expectations, regarding the situation at hand, that are actuated by the relevant protocols, and they eliminate possible worlds in which their expectations do not match with their observations. In this work, we investigate the computational complexity of the model checking problem for 𝖯𝖮𝖫 \mathsf{POL} and prove its 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -completeness. We also study various syntactic fragments of 𝖯𝖮𝖫 \mathsf{POL} . We exemplify the applicability of 𝖯𝖮𝖫 \mathsf{POL} model checking in verifying different characteristics and features of an interactive system with respect to the distinct expectations and (matching) observations of the system. Finally, we provide a discussion on the implementation of the model checking algorithms.

2.1

Definition 1

(Observation expressions)
.

Given a finite set of action symbols

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad::= \quad}l}\pi&\emptyset\mid\ \varepsilon\mid a\mid\pi\cdot\pi\mid\pi+\pi\mid\pi^{*}\\
\end{array}

In the traffic example, the observation expression

Definition 4

The main idea for introducing this logic was to reason about agent knowledge via the matching of observations and expectations. In line of public announcement logic

{\sim^{\prime}_{i}}={\sim_{i}}|_{S^{\prime}\times S^{\prime}},

.

In Definition
6

is the set of worlds

2.3
Public observation logic (
\mathsf{POL}
)

To reason about agent expectations and observations, the language for
\mathsf{POL}
is provided below.

Definition 7

(Syntax)
.

The formulas
\varphi
of
\mathsf{POL}

are given by:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad::= \quad}l}\varphi&\top\mid p\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid K_{i}\varphi\mid[\pi]\varphi,\end{array}

where
p\in\mathbf{P}
,
i\in\mathbf{I}
, and
\pi\in\mathcal{L}_{\it obs}
.

Also further we talk about various fragments, or special cases, of Public Observation Logic. Here various restrictions are promised on the syntax of regular languages or the syntax of the formulas. But before that, we define a normal form of
\mathsf{POL}
formulas called the Negative Normal Form.

Definition 8

(Negative Normal Form (NNF))
.

Given a set of propositional letters
\mathbf{P}
, the Negative Normal Form of a
\mathsf{POL}
formula is defined recursively as following:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad::= \quad}l}\varphi&\top\mid p\mid\neg p\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid K_{i}\varphi\mid\hat{K_{i}}\varphi\mid[\pi]\varphi\mid\langle\pi\rangle\varphi\end{array}

where
p\in\mathbf{P}
,
i\in\mathbf{I}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{L}_{obs}
.

In simpler words, a
\mathsf{POL}
formula is in a Negative Normal Form if and only if the negations (
\neg
) occur in the formula only before a propositional letter. Now we can define formally the various fragments of
\mathsf{POL}

&&\textrm{ we have }\mathcal{M}|_{w},s\vDash\varphi)\end{array}

4.1
On Implementation

The model checking for the
\mathsf{Word}
fragment can be implemented in poly-time with a bottom-up traversal of
the parse tree of the formula, as for CTL
[
12
, Section 6.4]
. In this subsection, we explain how to provide an efficient implementation for the
\mathsf{Star\mbox{-}Free}-\mathsf{Existential}
fragment of POL model checking by providing a reduction to SAT. We explain how to check that
\mathcal{M},w\models\langle\pi\rangle\varphi
, where
\pi
is star-free and
\varphi
is an epistemic formula. In other words, we aim at checking whether there is a guessed word that belongs to the language of
\pi
such that
w
survives the announcement of that word and
\varphi
holds in the updated model. As
\pi
is star-free, the guessed word is bounded by the size of
\pi
. The idea is to introduce propositional variables to model the Boolean values of the following statements: (i) the
t
-th letter of the guessed word is equal to
a
, (ii) a given automaton
A
is in state
q
after having read the first
t
letters of the guessed word, and, (iii) a subformula of the formula
\varphi
to check is true at a given world
u
. The last type of statements are combined in the spirit of the Tseitin transformation
[
13
, p. 91]
. We leave the other cases for future work.

W.l.o.g. we suppose that the guessed word is of length
k
. We also suppose that all the automata are deterministic. This costs an exponential time in principle, but we can rely on an efficient minimization algorithm to obtain small deterministic automata in practical cases.

We now explain how to define a Boolean formula
tr(M,w,\langle\pi\rangle\varphi)
such that
\mathcal{M},w\models\langle\pi\rangle\varphi
iff
tr(M,w,\langle\pi\rangle\varphi)
is a satisfiable Boolean formula.
To this end, we introduce several propositional variables:

•

p_{t,a}
: the
t
-th letter of the guessed word is
a

•

t_{u,\psi}
: subformula
\psi
(of
\varphi
) holds in world
u
in the updated model

•

a_{A,t,q}
: in the automaton
A
, the state after having read
t
letters in the guessed word is
q
. Note that
A
can denote either an automaton for
\pi
or that for any prefix language
\it Exp(u)
.

Given a finite set
P
of propositional variables, we write
\#(P)=1
for a Boolean formula saying that exactly one propositional variable in
P
is true.
We construct the following Boolean formula

t_{w,\varphi}\land guessedWord\land good\land surv\land rules

which is satisfiable iff
\mathcal{M},w\models\langle\pi\rangle\varphi
.
The first part
t_{w,\varphi}
enforces that the formula
\varphi
is true in
w
after the announcement of the guessed word.

The second part
guessedWord
says that the guessed word is uniquely determined by the propositions
p_{t,a}
. More precisely, that part is:

\bigwedge_{t=1..k}\#(\{p_{t,a}\mid a\in\Sigma\})=1
.

In other words, it means that there is a path in the non-deterministic automaton
A
corresponding to
\pi
, starting from the initial state
q_{0}
, following the guessed word, and leading to a final state. Given
A
, the automaton for
\pi
, the formula
good
is the conjunction of:

•

a_{A,0,q_{0}}
;

•

\bigwedge_{t=0..k}\#(\{a_{A,t,q}\mid q\in Q\})=1
;

•

\bigwedge_{t=0..k-1,q,a}a_{A,t,q}\land p_{t,a}\rightarrow p_{A,t+1}{\delta(q,a)}

•

\bigvee_{q\in F}a_{A,k,q}
.

The part
surv
says that the guessed word belongs to the prefix language of
\it Exp(s)
. That formula is similar to
good
but for the automaton corresponding to the prefix language of
\it Exp(s)
.
Finally,
rules
is a formula that mimics the semantics of
t_{w,\varphi}
in the same spirit as Tseitin transformation.
Formula
rules
is the conjunction of:

•

t_{u,p}\leftrightarrow\top
if
u\models p
;

•

t_{u,p}\leftrightarrow\bot
if
u\models p
;

•

t_{u,\lnot\psi}\leftrightarrow\lnot t_{u,\psi}
;

•

t_{u,\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}}\leftrightarrow(t_{u,\psi_{1}}\land t_{u,\psi_{2}})
;

•

t_{u,K_{i}\psi}\leftrightarrow\bigwedge_{v\mid u\rightarrow_{i}v}surv(v)\rightarrow t_{u,\psi}

where
surv(v)
is a propositional variable saying that world
v
survives the announcement of the guessed word. The variable
surv(v)
alone is not sufficient. It is accompanied by a collection of clauses in the same spirit as
surv
but with the automaton of the prefixes of
\it Exp(v)
.

We have
\mathcal{M},w\models\langle\pi\rangle\varphi
iff
tr(M,w,\langle\pi\rangle\varphi)
is a satisfiable Boolean formula. Moreover, the truth values of propositions
p_{t,a}
in a valuation satisfying
tr(M,w,\langle\pi\rangle\varphi)
gives a plan, i.e. the guessed word in the language of
\pi
such that
\varphi
holds after executing that word from
\mathcal{M},w
.
The full case of
\mathsf{Star\mbox{-}Free}-\mathsf{Existential}
fragment follows the same idea but is cumbersome.

Python3
using the library
pySAT
(with the SAT solver

automata-lib
. To get an idea, the running time for checking Example 3 is around 10ms.

Related work

Dynamic epistemic reasoning
The model checking of standard epistemic logic (
\mathsf{EL}
) is
\mathsf{PTIME}
-complete
[
14
]
.
Public Observation Logic (
\mathsf{POL}
) is quite similar to Public announcement logic (
\mathsf{PAL}
)
[
8
]
. When public announcements are performed, the number of possible worlds reduces, making the model checking of
\mathsf{PAL}
still in
\mathsf{PTIME}

[
15
]
as for standard epistemic logic. When actions can be private, the model checking becomes
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for
\mathsf{DEL}
with action models
[
16
]
.

In
\mathsf{PAL}
, a possible world is equipped with a valuation, while in
\mathsf{POL}
it is also equipped with a regular expression denoting the expectation in that world. In
\mathsf{PAL}
, the public announcement is fully specified and its effect is deterministic. In
\mathsf{POL}
, we may reason on sets of possible observations represented by regular expressions
\pi
. When these sets are singletons, we again obtain a
\mathsf{PTIME}
upper bound (Theorem
5
). In this sense,
\mathsf{POL}
is close to Arbitrary
\mathsf{PAL}
(
\mathsf{APAL}
)
[
17
]
whose model checking is also
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete
[
18
]
. In
\mathsf{APAL}
, any epistemic formula can be announced: there are no expectations. However, in
\mathsf{POL}
, we have to reason about the constraints between the possible expectations, and the set of observations (given by
\pi
). Our contribution can be reformulated as follows: we prove that (i) reasoning about these constraints can still be done in
\mathsf{PSPACE}
, and, (ii) this reasoning is sufficiently involved for the model checking to be
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard.

In
\mathsf{POL}
, regular expressions are used to represent sets of observations, while van Benthem et al.
[
19
]
used regular expressions (actually, programs of Propositional dynamic logic (
PDL
)
[
20
]
) to denote epistemic relations. Charrier et al.
[
21
]
considered a logic for reasoning about protocols where actions are public announcements and not abstract observations as in POL: in this sense, POL is more general.

Epistemic temporal reasoning
It is natural to describe computational behaviours with regular expressions. Finite-state controllers, i.e., automata are used to describe policies in planning
[
11
]
. Interestingly, Lomuscio and Michaliszyn
[
22
]
studied an epistemic logic where formulas are evaluated on intervals and the language provides Allen’s operators on intervals: in their setting, the model is an interpreted system, and a propositional variable
p
is true in an interval
I
if the trace of
I
matches a given regular expression associated to
p
. In contrast,
\mathsf{POL}
is not based on an already set-up model but relies on updates in a model. Bozzelli et al.
[
23
]
studied the complexity of the model checking of that logic depending on the restrictions on the allowed set of Allen’s operators. Their framework is similar to ours because it relies on regular expressions but the approach is orthogonal to model updates and hence, to epistemic planning.

Epistemic planning

As far as we know, epistemic planning frameworks (based on DEL
[
4
]
, or the so-called MEP for Multi-agent Epistemic Planning
[
24
]
) all
provide a mechanism for reasoning about preconditions and effects of actions. Expectations about others or about the world are not dealt with. However,
Saffidine et al.
[
25
]
propose a collaborative setup for epistemic planning where each agent executes its own knowledge-based policy/program (KBP) while agents commonly know all the KBPs that are being executed, meaning that agents expect that the other agents follow their own KBP. On the contrary, in
\mathsf{POL}
, observations are public but expectations are in general not commonly known. Reasoning about some epistemic properties that are true after the execution of any kind of KBPs is undecidable, but is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for star-free KBPs. The complexity is high for different reasons: the initial model is represented symbolically; observations are not already public, and KBPs may contain tests.

Strategic reasoning
Usually in logics for strategic reasoning (e.g., alternating-time temporal logic
[
26
]
, and strategy logic
[
27
]
), agents do not have expectations: an agent may consider all possible strategies for the others. Recently, Belnardinelli et al.
[
28
]
propose a variant of strategy logic (SL) where a player may know completely the strategy of another player. In contrast, in
\mathsf{POL}
agents may have partial information about the expectations. In
\mathsf{POL}
, agents also have higher-order knowledge about these expectations. In SL, strategies are abstract objects in the logical language whereas in
\mathsf{POL}
, observations are represented as composite structures that the agents can reason about, similar to the work on games and strategies presented in
[
29
]
. In this sense,
\mathsf{POL}
can be seen as
\mathsf{EL}
extended with
PDL
operators.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we showed that the model checking for
\mathsf{POL}
is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete. Such complexity studies were left open in
[
6
]
. We also identified more tractable fragments (see Figure
1
) of
\mathsf{POL}
. Finally, we discussed the applicability of our study in verifying various features of interactive systems related to epistemic planning. A discussion on implementation is also provided.

We leave the investigations on model checking for
EPL
, an extension of
\mathsf{POL}
, also proposed in
[
6
]
, for future work. We also aim to study the satisfiability problems of
\mathsf{POL}
and
EPL
by adapting the techniques from
[
16
,
30
]
.

Many interesting features of such interactive systems remain to be investigated :
private observations, like in DEL with action models
[
3
]
; dynamic aspects (e.g., changing expectations); richer languages of expectations (e.g., context-free grammars for expectations), among others. Symbolic model checking can be considered as well following the trends of
[
31
]
and
[
21
]
.

This paper also opens up a research avenue for developing variants and extensions for reasoning about expectations and observations that can be expressive enough with reasonable complexities for the model checking problem.

To sum up, POL mixes epistemic logic and language theory for modelling mechanisms of social intelligent agents, and the current investigations on model checking set it up as a useful tool in building social software for AI.

Lemma 17
.

Given a finite
\mathsf{POL}
model
\mathcal{M}=\langle S,R,V,Exp\rangle
, an
s\in S
and a
\mathsf{Word}
formula
\varphi
,
s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\varphi)
iff
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\varphi
.

Proof.

This can be proved by induction over the size of
\varphi
.

Base Case.
Consider the case where
\varphi=p
, where
p\in\mathbf{P}
. In the IF case in
1
, the set
S^{\prime}
is populated with all the worlds
s\in S
where
p\in V(s)
. Hence,
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\varphi
iff
s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\varphi)
.

Induction Hypothesis.
Given a finite
\mathsf{POL}
model
\mathcal{M}=\langle S,R,V,Exp\rangle
, an
s\in S
and a
\mathsf{POL}
formula of the
\mathsf{Word}
fragment
\varphi
,
s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\varphi)
iff
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\varphi
, where
|\varphi|\leq k
, for an integer
k
.

Inductive Step.

•

\varphi=\neg{\psi}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M},s\vDash\neg{\psi}

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }\mathcal{M},s\nvDash\psi\hskip 369.88582pt

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }s\notin\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\psi)\mbox{, by IH}

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }s\in S\setminus\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\psi)

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\neg{\psi})

•

\varphi=\psi_{1}\vee\psi_{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M},s\vDash\psi_{1}\vee\psi_{2}

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }\mathcal{M},s\vDash\psi_{1}\mbox{ or }\mathcal{M},s\vDash\psi_{2}\hskip 349.96898pt

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\psi_{1})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mbox{ or }s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\psi_{2})\mbox{, by IH}

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\psi_{1})\cup\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\psi_{2})

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\psi_{\vee}\psi_{2})

•

\varphi=\hat{K_{i}}\psi

\displaystyle\mathcal{M},s\vDash\hat{K_{i}}\psi

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }\exists t\sim_{i}s\mbox{ and }\mathcal{M},t\vDash\psi\hskip 369.88582pt

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }\exists t\sim_{i}s\mbox{ and }t\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\psi)\mbox{, by IH}

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\hat{K_{i}}\psi)

•

\varphi=\langle w\rangle\psi

Since
w
is a word,
Exp(s)\backslash w
is calculated in line
14
, and hence for all
s\in S
in the loop in
13
-
17

t(\in S)\notin S^{\prime}
iff
Exp(t)\backslash w=\delta
, that is,
\mathcal{L}(Exp(t)\backslash w)=\emptyset
. By Lemma
14
, we have the correctness of the residuation. Hence after the termination of loop
13
-
17
,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{M}|_{w}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M},s\vDash\langle w\rangle\psi

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }\mathcal{L}(Exp(s)\backslash w)\neq\emptyset\hskip 341.43306pt

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \mbox{ and }\mathcal{M}|_{w},s\vDash\psi\mbox{, since $w$ is word}

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }\mathcal{L}(Exp(s)\backslash w)\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \mbox{ and }s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\psi)\mbox{, by IH}

\displaystyle\mbox{ iff }s\in\mathsf{WorldsP}(\mathcal{M},\langle w\rangle\psi)

∎
