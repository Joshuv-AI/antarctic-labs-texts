Can we observe the QCD phase transition-generated gravitational waves through pulsar timing arrays? Preprint: NORDITA-2021-016
Axel Brandenburg, Emma Clarke, Yutong He, Tina Kahniashvili

Abstract
We perform numerical simulations of gravitational waves (GWs) induced by hydrodynamic and hydromagnetic turbulent sources that might have been present at cosmological quantum chromodynamic (QCD) phase transitions. For turbulent energies of about 4% of the radiation energy density, the typical scale of such motions may have been a sizable fraction of the Hubble scale at that time. The resulting GWs are found to have an energy fraction of about 10 − 9 10^{-9} of the critical energy density in the nHz range today and may already have been observed by the NANOGrav collaboration. This is further made possible by our findings of shallower spectra proportional to the square root of the frequency for nonhelical hydromagnetic turbulence. This implies more power at low frequencies than for the steeper spectra previously anticipated. The behavior toward higher frequencies depends strongly on the nature of the turbulence. For vortical hydrodynamic and hydromagnetic turbulence, there is a sharp drop of spectral GW energy by up to five orders of magnitude in the presence of helicity, and somewhat less in the absence of helicity. For acoustic hydrodynamic turbulence, the sharp drop is replaced by a power law decay, albeit with a rather steep slope. Our study supports earlier findings of a quadratic scaling of the GW energy with the magnetic energy of the turbulence and inverse quadratic scaling with the peak frequency, which leads to larger GW energies under QCD conditions.

IV
Gravitational Wave Generation

As mentioned in the introduction (see also Ref.
Burke-Spolaor:2018bvk
),
low frequency GWs can originate from various astrophysical foreground
sources (white dwarfs, SMBH mergers, etc),
and from relic sources related to inflation and cosmic strings, for
example, and in particular, from phase transition-generated turbulence
and primordial magnetic fields.
We focus here on the latter two.
Turbulence and/or magnetic fields would only be generated during a
limited amount of time before they would decay.
The decay process itself remains highly turbulent and could
affect GW production.
Let us therefore begin with some general remarks about turbulent decay.

\Lambda_{ijkl}(\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}})=P_{ik}(\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}})P_{jl}(\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}})-\frac{1}{2}P_{ij}(\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}})P_{kl}(\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}})
, correspondingly.
The polarization tensors
e^{+}_{ij}(\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}})
and
e^{\times}_{ij}(\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}})
can be written as
e^{+}_{ij}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{})={\bf\hat{e}}^{1}_{i}{\bf\hat{e}}^{1}_{j}-{\bf\hat{e}}^{2}_{i}{\bf\hat{e}}^{2}_{j}
and
e^{\times}_{ij}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{})={\bf\hat{e}}^{1}_{i}{\bf\hat{e}}^{2}_{j}+{\bf\hat{e}}^{2}_{i}{\bf\hat{e}}^{1}_{j}
, where
{\bf\hat{e}}^{1}

and
{\bf\hat{e}}^{2}
are unit vectors that are
orthogonal to
\hat{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}}

and each other; see
Chapter 1 (1.2) of Ref.
MaggioreText
.
and Ref.
Pol:2018pao
for further detail.

As in earlier work
Pol:2018pao
;
Pol:2019yex
, we use normalized
conformal time,
\bar{t}=t/t_{*}
, where
t_{*}=H_{*}^{-1}
is our starting
time, and
a_{*}=1
has been chosen.
Therefore,
a=\bar{t}
.
We also use the scaled wave vector,

\bar{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}}=\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}/H_{*}
,
and a scaled
normalized
stress,
\bar{T}_{+/\times}^{\rm TT}=T_{+/\times}^{\rm TT}/{\cal E}_{\rm rad}^{*}
.
The GW equation can then be written in the form
Pol:2018pao
;
Pol:2019yex

\left(\partial^{2}_{\bar{t}}+\bar{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}}^{2}\right)h_{+/\times}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{},t)\,=\,{6\over\bar{t}}\bar{T}_{+/\times}^{\rm TT}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{},t),

(9)

but from now on we omit all overbars.

Throughout this paper, all numerical results will usually be presented
as the scaled variables introduced above.
In particular, we quote the rms strain,
{h}_{\rm rms}=\braket{h^{2}}^{1/2}
,
where
h^{2}=h_{+}^{2}+h_{\times}^{2}=(h_{ij})^{2}/2
, and likewise for the
scaled GW energy,
{\cal E}_{\rm GW}=\braket{\dot{h}^{2}}/6
,
where
\dot{h}_{+/\times}=\partial_{t}h_{+/\times}

with
\dot{h}^{2}\equiv\dot{h}_{+}^{2}+\dot{h}_{\times}^{2}
;
see Ref.
Pol:2018pao
;
Pol:2019yex
for additional
subdominant terms that are applied in the calculations.
We sometimes also quote the (frequency dependent) characteristic
amplitude of the physical strain measured today,
h_{c}(f)={h}_{\rm rms}/a_{0}
;
see Sec.
II
.

IV.2
Turbulent Sources

Turbulent flows in the early universe can be modeled by solving the
hydromagnetic equations for the density
\rho
, the velocity
\textstyle u
,
and the magnetic field
\textstyle B
with
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle B$}}{}=0
, adopting an
ultrarelativistic equation of state in an expanding universe
using conformal time and comoving variables

Brandenburg:1996fc
;
Brandenburg:2017neh
with a forcing
term
{\cal F}
in the induction equation for
\textstyle B

\displaystyle{\partial\ln\rho\over\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{3}\left(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}+\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\ln\rho\right)+{1\over\rho}\left[\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}\cdot(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle J$}}{}\times\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle B$}}{})+\eta\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle J$}}{}^{2}\right],

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}\over 3}\left(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}+\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\ln\rho\right)+{2\over\rho}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\cdot\left(\rho\nu\bm{\mathsf{S}}\right)

\displaystyle{\partial\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle B$}}{}\over\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\times(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}\times\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle B$}}{}-\eta\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle J$}}{}+\mbox{\boldmath${\cal F}$}{}),\quad\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle J$}}{}=\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\times\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle B$}}{}.

We recall that the conformal time
t

is normalized to unity at the time
t_{*}

of magnetic field generation,
\rho
is in units of the initial value,

\textstyle u
is in units of the speed of light, and the magnetic energy density

\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle B$}}{}^{2}/2
is measured in units of the radiation density at the time
of generation.
Furthermore,

{\sf S}_{ij}={\textstyle{1\over 2}}(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i})-{\textstyle{1\over 3}}\delta_{ij}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\nabla$}}{}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}{}

are the components of the rate-of-strain tensor with commas denoting
partial derivatives,
\textstyle J
is the current density,

\nu
is the kinematic viscosity, and
\eta
is the
magnetic diffusivity.
The electromotive force,
{\cal F}
, is used to model magnetic field
generation with

\mbox{\boldmath${\cal F}$}{}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}{},t)={\rm Re}[{\cal N}\tilde{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}{}}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{})\exp(i\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}{}+i\varphi)],

(10)

where the wave vector
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}(t)
and the phase
\varphi(t)
change randomly
from one time step to the next.
This forcing function is therefore white noise in time and consists of plane
waves with average wavenumber
k_{\rm f}
such that
|\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}|
lies in an interval

k_{\rm f}-\delta k/2\leq|\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}|<k_{\rm f}+\delta k/2
of width
\delta k
.
Here,
{\cal N}=f_{0}/\delta t^{1/2}
is a normalization factor, where

\delta t
is the time step and
f_{0}
is varied to achieve a certain
magnetic field strength after a certain time, and

\tilde{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}{}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}})=(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}\times\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle e$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle e$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle e$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle e$}}{})/[\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}^{2}-(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle k$}}{}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle e$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle e$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle e$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle e$}}{})^{2}]^{1/2}

is a nonhelical forcing function.
Here,
\textstyle e
is an arbitrary unit vector that is not aligned with
\textstyle k
.
Note that
|\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}{}|^{2}=1
.
Following earlier work, the forcing is only enabled during the
time interval
1\leq t\leq 2
.
The kinetic and magnetic energy densities are defined as

{\cal E}_{\rm K}(t)=\braket{\rho\mathbf{u}^{2}}/2
and

{\cal E}_{\rm M}(t)=\braket{\mathbf{B}^{2}}/2
, respectively.

.

IV.3
Turbulent Decay Laws

Turbulence is known to decay in power-law fashion
BP56
;
Saf67
such that
the magnetic energy
{\cal E}_{\rm M}(t)
decays with time
t
like
\Delta t^{-p}

and the correlation length
\xi_{\rm M}(t)
increases like
\Delta t^{q}
,
where
\Delta t=t-t_{\rm off}
is the time interval after the forcing
has been turned off.
The exponents
p
and
q
are positive and depend on the physical
circumstances (magnetically or kinetically dominated turbulence),
and whether or not there is magnetic helicity.
In helical turbulence, for example, one finds
p=q=2/3
, while for
non-helical magnetically dominated turbulence one finds
p=1
and

q=1/2
, although other variants are sometimes possible

Brandenburg:2017rnt
;
Hosking21
.

In this paper, we are specifically interested in the dependence of
the decay behavior on the forcing wave number
k_{\rm f}
of the turbulence
while it was still being driven.
The parameter
k_{\rm f}
enters through the prefactor in the decay law.

Furthermore,
\Delta t^{-p}
would become infinite for
p>0

and
\Delta t=0
(when
t=t_{\rm off}
).
The singularity of
\Delta t^{-p}
at
t=0
is a consequence of a
simplified description at the initial time moment.
For this reason, it is convenient to express the decay laws as

{\cal E}_{\rm M}(t)={\cal E}_{\rm M}^{\max}\,(1+\Delta t/\tau)^{-p},

(11)

where
\tau
is the turnover time, which we will treat as an empirical
parameter that we expect to be of the order of
(v_{\rm A}k_{\rm f})^{-1}
, where

v_{\rm A}=(3{\cal E}_{\rm M}^{\max}/2)^{1/2}
is the Alfvén speed, evaluated
at the time when
{\cal E}_{\rm M}
reaches its maximum value
{\cal E}_{\rm M}^{\max}
.
In some simulations of purely hydrodynamic turbulence, we replace

{\cal E}_{\rm M}
by
{\cal E}_{\rm K}
in Eq. (
11
) and use
\tau=(u_{\rm rms}k_{\rm f})^{-1}
with

u_{\rm rms}=(2{\cal E}_{\rm K})^{1/2}
as the nominal turnover time.
We recall here that we are using nondimensional variables where the
radiation energy density is unity.

For all our simulations, we choose
t_{\rm off}=2
, i.e., turbulence
is being driven for one Hubble time during
1\leq t\leq 2
.
In the following, we vary
k_{\rm f}
between 2 and 60.
For
k_{\rm f}=60
, we find that
\tau
is shorter than a Hubble time,
but in all other cases, it exceeds it by up to factors between
ten (in the nonhelical cases) and a hundred (in helical cases).

We arrange the strength of the forcing
f_{0}
such that
{\cal E}_{\rm M}
is similar
for different values of
k_{\rm f}
.
This allows us then to determine the resulting GW energy solely
as a function of
k_{\rm f}
.
For small values of
k_{\rm f}
, the turbulence may not be able to reach
a statistically steady state by the time
t_{\rm off}
, when the
driving is turned off.

It is therefore necessary to adjust
f_{0}
for each value of
k_{\rm f}

separately.
Once we have two values of
{\cal E}_{\rm M}^{\max}
that are close enough
to the target strength, we determine the desired forcing
strength through linear interpolation.
We also consider the case of different values of
f_{0}
for a fixed value
of
k_{\rm f}
(Runs noh5,6 and Runs hel5,6).

In Fig.

The first two cases with

and amplitude range accessible to NANOGrav.
In all cases, the spectra show a sharp drop slightly above the peak
frequency.
This is a consequence of the rapid temporal growth of the spectra,
which leads to a correspondingly large growth at the peak frequency,
while at higher frequencies, the spectrum settled at values that were
determined by somewhat earlier times when the energy was still weaker.

At frequencies below the peak, we now find a spectrum that is even
shallower than the
h_{0}^{2}\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)\propto f
spectrum found
already earlier
Pol:2019yex

A spectrum shallower than proportional to
f
, such as the present

f^{1/2}
spectrum, could perhaps be explained by the finite size of the
computational domain; see Ref.
Brandenburg:2021aln

a
f^{-1/2}
spectrum for
\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)

Alternatively, the shallower spectrum might well be physical,
or at least significantly extended over a substantial frequency
interval below the peak frequency, for example due to inverse
cascading in helical
FPLM75
and nonhelical
BK17
cases.

In the absence of sources, a
\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)\propto f^{\alpha}
spectrum
implies
h_{c}(f)\propto f^{\alpha/2-1}
for arbitrary spectral indices
\alpha

For
\alpha=1/2
, we would thus expect
h_{c}(f)\propto f^{-3/4}

However, the observed strain spectrum,
h_{c}(f)\propto f^{-1/2}
, seems to agree
with that found previously from numerical simulations
Pol:2019yex
.
However, looking more carefully at the strain spectrum for
k_{\rm f}=60
, we see
a
h_{c}(f)\propto f^{-3/4}
spectrum is actually compatible with the simulation;
see the corresponding dashed-dotted line in Fig.
5
(c).
This agreement is probably related to the fact that the turnover time is
shorter for the run with
k_{\rm f}=60
, compared with those at smaller values
(i.e., longer turbulence driving time will allow for
more efficient inverse cascading).

In the runs with helicity, we do find
h_{0}^{2}\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)\propto f
,
together with a slight enhancement just before reaching a maximum.
The subsequent decay for larger values of
f
is much steeper in the
case with helicity than without.
Furthermore, in
h_{c}(f)
we see a sharper drop to the right of the
maximum than in simulations without helicity.
These differences in the spectra for helical and nonhelical cases
are surprisingly strong and might allow us to infer the presence
of magnetic helicity once such a spectrum is detected.

It is important to note that the
h_{0}^{2}\Omega_{\rm GW}\propto f

VI
Conclusions

In the present work, we have shown that the magnetic stress from hydrodynamic and MHD
turbulence with scales comparable to the cosmological horizon scale at the
time of the QCD phase transition can drive GWs in the range accessible
to NANOGrav, if the magnetic energy density is 3–10% of the radiation
energy density.
The low-frequency tail below the peak frequency at
10\,{\rm nHz}
or so is
shallower in the nonhelical case than in the helical one, i.e.,

\propto f^{1/2}
compared to
\propto f
.
Both scalings are, however, shallower than what was expected based on
earlier analytical calculations.
Also the inertial range spectrum above the peak is shallower without
helicity than with, but here, both spectra are steeper than what
is expected if the GW spectrum was a direct consequence of the MHD
turbulence spectrum
Pol:2019yex
;
BB20
.
The reason for this is primarily the relatively short time of turbulent
driving (one Hubble time).
It is short compared with the turnover time which,
for our runs with the smallest
k_{\rm f}
of two, is much longer:
16 (100) Hubble times for our runs without (with) helicity.
Therefore, there was not enough time to fully establish the GW spectrum
at high wave numbers.
For our earlier runs with larger values of
k_{\rm f}
, this effect was less
pronounced than for smaller values of
k_{\rm f}
, but it is still quite noticeable,
especially in the helical case where forward cascading is weaker than
in the nonhelical case.

Our work has led to new insights regarding the possibility of using
an observed GW spectrum for making statements about the nature of the
underlying turbulence in the early universe.
One is the already mentioned slope of the subinertial range spectrum.
Another is the position of the peak of the spectrum.
Finally, there is the strength of the drop of the spectral power for
frequencies above the peak frequency, and the subsequent slope after
the drop, which is most likely too small to be detectable.
This, however, depends on the duration of turbulent driving and could
be higher if the driving time was longer.
The specific features of the spectrum near the peak are different
for helical and nonhelical turbulence.
This could, in principle, give information about the presence
of parity violation, when would also lead to circularly polarized
GWs.
