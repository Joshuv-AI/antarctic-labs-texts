Abstract

This thesis explores the application of Plane Wave Discontinuous Galerkin (PWDG) methods for the numerical simulation of electromagnetic scattering by periodic structures. Periodic structures play a pivotal role in various engineering and scientific applications, including antenna design, metamaterial characterization, and photonic crystal analysis. Understanding and accurately predicting the scattering behavior of electromagnetic waves from such structures is crucial in optimizing their performance and advancing technological advancements.

The thesis commences with an overview of the theoretical foundations of electromagnetic scattering by periodic structures. This theoretical dissertation serves as the basis for formulating the PWDG method within the context of wave equation. The DtN operator is presented and it is used to derive a suitable boundary condition.

The numerical implementation of PWDG methods is discussed in detail, emphasizing key aspects such as basis function selection and boundary conditions. The algorithm’s efficiency is assessed through numerical experiments.

We then present the DtN-PWDG method, which is discussed in detail and is used to derive numerical solutions of the scattering problem. A comparison with the finite element method (FEM) is presented.

In conclusion, this thesis demonstrates that PWDG methods are a powerful tool for simulating electromagnetic scattering by periodic structures.

Chapter 1
Introduction

The study of electromagnetic scattering by periodic structures has been an area of significant interest in the field of electromagnetics and computational physics for many decades. As technology continues to advance, the demand for accurate and efficient numerical methods to analyze scattering phenomena from periodic structures becomes increasingly important. In this context, the Trefftz Discontinuous-Galerkin (TDG) method emerges as a promising approach to tackle the challenges posed by these complex problems.

This thesis explores the application and development of the Trefftz-DG methods for solving scattering problems involving periodic structures. The motivation behind this work arises from the desire to overcome the limitations of traditional numerical techniques, such as finite element methods, finite difference methods, and integral equation methods, when dealing with problems featuring periodicity.

The primary objective of this research is to present a comprehensive study of the TDG method’s theoretical foundation and its practical implementation for scattering analysis. By leveraging the power of Trefftz functions, which are solutions to the governing equations of the problem, the TDG method provides a unique advantage in efficiently capturing the behavior of waves in periodic media.

The thesis starts with a review of the relevant literature on scattering problems, periodic surfaces, Trefftz methods, and discontinuous Galerkin methods. The mathematical formulation of the scattering problem is presented, including the governing equations, boundary conditions, and the discretization strategy based on TDG. The periodicity of the scattering surface leads to quasi-periodic solutions. For this reason, quasi-periodic boundary conditions are applied, together with a radiation condition on part of the boundary.

Next, we develop and implement numerical algorithms for solving the scattering problem: we implement the PWDG method using suitable finite element discretization techniques, incorporating quasi-periodic boundary conditions and appropriate basis functions. Special attention is given to the treatment of quasi-periodic boundary conditions and the construction of the basis functions using appropriate solutions of the governing equations.

To assess the performance and accuracy of the proposed TDG method, a series of numerical experiments are conducted. These experiments in two space dimensions involve various scattering scenarios by periodic structures, including different incident wave angles, frequencies, and material properties.

Finally, the conclusion summarizes the findings of this thesis and highlights the significance of the Trefftz Discontinuous-Galerkin method in the context of scattering by periodic structures. It also points out potential avenues for future research and developments in this area.

Chapter 2
Scattering by Periodic Structures

Scattering phenomena play a fundamental role in a wide range of scientific and engineering disciplines, including optics, acoustics, electromagnetics, and solid-state physics. One intriguing aspect of scattering is wave interaction with periodic structures, where the arrangement of constituents repeats periodically in space. Such periodic structures can be found in various natural and engineered systems, ranging from crystalline materials to photonic crystals, metamaterials, and diffraction gratings.

The study of scattering by periodic structures is crucial for understanding and manipulating wave propagation in materials and devices. It finds applications in diverse fields, including the design of optical filters, waveguides, sensors, and photonic devices with tailored properties. Moreover, scattering by periodic structures is not limited to electromagnetic waves but also extends to other types of waves, such as acoustic waves and matter waves, enabling advancements in fields like ultrasound imaging, sonic crystals, and atom optics.

Due to its multiple applications in engineering and technology, considerable attention has been devoted to the mathematical modeling and computational simulation of acoustic and electromagnetic wave diffraction by periodic or bi-periodic structures in unbounded domains.

as the completion of
H^{0}_{\alpha}(\Gamma)\equiv L^{2}_{\alpha}(\Gamma)
with respect to the norm:

\|u\|_{H^{-s}_{\alpha}(\Gamma)}:=\biggl\|(u\circ z)\hskip 2.84544pt\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle.}}{{z}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\hskip 2.84544pt\biggl\|_{H^{-s}[0,2\pi]},

with
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle.}}{{z}}(t):=(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle.}}{{z}}_{1}(t),\stackrel{{\scriptstyle.}}{{z}}_{2}(t))
and
z
as before.

2.2
Model problem

We now present the problem of scattering of a plane wave by a periodic surface. The diffraction grating problem is referred to as wave scattering by periodic structures. The grating problem has been studied in depth with different approaches in many articles and books such as
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
or
[
14
]
.

First, following
[
5
]
, we will derive the strong formulation and provide an existence result, then we will focus on the weak formulation and describe how to compute a numerical solution of the problem with a Finite Element Method.

We consider linear optics with an
e^{-i\omega t}
dependence on time
t
, where
i=\sqrt{-1}
and
\omega
is the angular frequency of light. This assumption agrees with the notation in
[
5
]
,
[
12
]
and
[
15
]
, while in
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
13
]
the time-dependence is assumed to be
e^{i\omega t}
. The main difference between these two notations is that with the first one a plane wave with the expression
e^{ik\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{d}}
effectively propagates in the direction corresponding to the vector
\mathbf{d}
, while with the second notation the wave propagates in the opposite direction
-\mathbf{d}
. This difference in the notation leads to opposite signs in the Sommerfeld radiation condition and also an opposite impedance boundary condition. We choose to follow the convention of Civiletti
[
5
]
since this is more coherent with the plane wave propagation direction and since the Sommerfeld radiation condition in this case is more standard.

Under the assumption of
e^{-i\omega t}
dependence on time
t
, from Maxwell’s equations one can show that the electric field phasor
\mathbf{E}
solves

\Delta\mathbf{E}=-\omega^{2}\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon\mathbf{E}-\nabla\left(\mathbf{E}\cdot\frac{\nabla\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\right),

(2.2)

where
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(x_{1},x_{2})
is the spatially dependent relative permittivity, and
\varepsilon_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
are respectively the permittivity and permeability of vacuum. We also assume that
\mu=\mu_{0}
everywhere. The domain under consideration is assumed to be invariant in the
\mathbf{e}_{3}=(0,0,1)
direction, so the electric field phasor is invariant in the
\mathbf{e}_{3}
direction, i.e.
\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{E}(x_{1},x_{2})
.

For
s
-polarized light, we also have that
\mathbf{E}=(0,0,E_{3})
, and so the last term on the right hand side of (
2.2
) is zero. The wavenumber in air is denoted by
k=\omega/c_{0}
and the speed of light in air is
c_{0}=1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}
. We obtain the vector Helmholtz equation

\Delta\mathbf{E}+k^{2}\varepsilon\mathbf{E}=0,

(2.3)

where
E_{1}=E_{2}=0
. So we see that this reduces to a scalar Helmholtz equation. A similar result holds for the
p
-polarization case for the magnetic field phasor
\mathbf{H}
, but that case is more difficult because a
1/\varepsilon
term appears in the principal part of the differential operator.

Figure 2.1:
Geometry of the region
\Omega_{0}

We now study the problem of scattering of a plane wave by a periodic structure: we consider a domain
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is periodic in the
x_{1}
direction with period
L
:
\Omega_{0}
is divided into subdomains by different interfaces, the profiles of which are picewise linear of Lipschitz functions with the same period
L
. These interfaces can cross all the domain or can also be scattering objects located inside a domain period. The domain division corresponds to its composition of different materials, which can be penetrable or impenetrable by waves. In our model we assume that the lower interface is a straight line impenetrable by waves, which gives us a Dirichlet boundary condition. A possible scattering region is represented in Figure
2.1
. Here we can see many interfaces
\Gamma_{j}
inside the region, that are periodic in the
x_{1}
direction with period
L
. The relative permittivity
\varepsilon
is assumed to be
L
-periodic in
x_{1}
and invariant in
x_{3}
.

Figure 2.2:
The truncated domain
\Omega

The mathematical formulation is a Helmholtz equation with a periodically variable relative permittivity
\varepsilon
. By a simple change of variables we can assume the period to be
2\pi
. Due to the periodicity of the scattering region and the relative permittivity, we can reduce our study to a cell of the scatterer, which is the domain
\mathcal{G}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0<x_{1}<2\pi\}
defined before. Then it will be necessary to truncate the cell on the
x_{2}
axis, getting a bounded 2D domain
\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0<x_{1}<2\pi,-H<x_{2}<H\},
where
H>0
is an arbitrary positive constant. We will refer to the two straight lines that delimit the domain
\Omega
form above and below as
\Gamma_{H}
and
\Gamma_{-H}
. Formally we have that

\Gamma_{\pm H}:=\{\mathbf{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\hskip 8.5359pt:\hskip 8.5359ptx_{1}\in[0,2\pi],\hskip 14.22636ptx_{2}=\pm H\}.

(2.4)

In Figure
2.2
we can see a possible scattering region, obtained by truncating the region in Figure
2.1
.

We will consider the scattering problem when an
s
-polarized plane wave with electric field phasor polarized in the
\mathbf{e}_{3}
direction is incident on
\Omega
with incidence angle
\theta\in\left(-\pi,0\right)
. The third component of the incident electric field phasor can be stated as

u^{i}(\mathbf{x})=u^{i}(x_{1},x_{2})=\exp\{ik(x_{1}\cos\theta+x_{2}\sin\theta)\}.

Our aim is to compute the total wave on a two-dimensional domain
\Omega
. The wave solves the Helmholtz equation in
\Omega
with quasi-periodic boundary conditions

\begin{cases}\Delta u+k^{2}\varepsilon u=0,&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega,\\
u(2\pi,x_{2})=e^{-i\alpha_{0}2\pi}u(0,x_{2})&\hskip 14.22636pt\forall\hskip 1.42271ptx_{2},\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}u(2\pi,x_{2})=e^{-i\alpha_{0}2\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}u(0,x_{2})&\hskip 14.22636pt\forall\hskip 1.42271ptx_{2}\\
u=0,&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{-H},\end{cases}

(2.5)

where
\alpha_{0}=k\cos\theta
. We need to insert the periodicity condition to achieve the uniqueness of the solution. With this conditions, we can solve the problem in the cell and then extend the solution with quasi-periodicity.

We assume that
\Omega
is divided in different subregions; the lines that divide
\Omega
could either be piecewise linear or smooth functions and could either go from the left to the right boundary or
\Omega
or close on themselves. We call this separating interfaces
\Gamma_{j}
. In our experiments we will only consider the piecewise linear case since we do not want to deal with integrals on curved edges. If we have a total number of
I
lines dividing the domain,
\Omega
is separated into
I+1
subdomains, that we call
\Omega_{j}
, for
j=1,...,I+1
.

Furthermore, we have the following assumptions on
\varepsilon
. First,
\varepsilon\in C^{(1,1)}(\Omega_{k})
for all
k=1,2,...,I+1
. Also,
\varepsilon
is allowed to be complex valued in
\Omega
, and either
\{\Re(\varepsilon)>0,\Im(\varepsilon)\geq 0\}
or
\{\Re(\varepsilon)\leq 0,\Im(\varepsilon)>0\}
in
\Omega
. A standard assumption from the literature is that
\varepsilon
is constant in every
\Omega_{k}
subdomain of
\Omega
, but in the general case we will consider a relative permittivity which changes smoothly in the domain. Typically, we take the relative permittivity in the upper half space
\Omega_{H}^{+}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>H\}
to be
\varepsilon^{+}=1
. Thus, the half space above
\Omega
is air.

On each interface
\Gamma_{j}
, we choose the unit normal to point downwards or outwards. By
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\Gamma_{j}}
we denote the jump of a function
\phi
across the interface
\Gamma_{j}
. Thus,

\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\Gamma_{j}}:=\phi|_{\Gamma_{j}}^{+}-\phi|_{\Gamma_{j}}^{-}

where
\phi|_{\Gamma_{k}}^{+}
is the limit taken from outside
\Omega_{j}
and
\phi|_{\Gamma_{j}}^{-}
is the limit taken from inside
\Omega_{j}
, for
1\leq j\leq I
.

2.3
Variational formulation

We now derive the variational formulation of the scattering problem. First, we need to specify a radiation condition on
\Gamma_{H}
, as we want that the scattered field "goes upward" on
\Gamma_{H}
. To specify this condition we will make use of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator.

We assume that on the upper domain
u
can be represented as a linear combination of upward propagating waves and evanescent waves. Since
u
is
\alpha_{0}
-quasi-periodic in
\Omega
, we can write

u(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{n}(x_{2})e^{i\alpha_{n}x_{1}},

(2.6)

for
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
, where
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{0}+n
and the Fourier coefficients
u_{n}(x_{2})
defined as in (
2.1
). We define, for
a\geq H
,
\Gamma_{a}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}=a\}
; we also define
\Omega_{H}^{+}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0\leq x_{1}\leq 2\pi,\hskip 2.84544ptx_{2}>H\}
.

We can write the total field
u
as the sum of incident and scattered field, so we have

u=u^{i}+u^{s}.

We know that
u^{i}
solves the Helmholtz equation in
\Omega_{H}^{+}
and, since
u^{s}
in
C^{\infty}
for
x_{2}>H
and it is quasi-periodic, we can expand it in terms of Fourier coefficients
u^{s}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}u^{s}_{n}(x_{2})e^{i\alpha_{n}x_{1}}
, with

u^{s}_{n}(x_{2})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-i\alpha_{n}x_{1}}u^{s}(x_{1},x_{2})\hskip 1.42271ptdx_{1}.

(2.7)

We have that
u^{s}
satisfies
\Delta u^{s}+k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}u^{s}=0
for
x_{2}>H
, where
\varepsilon^{+}
is the relative permittivity in the region above the domain
\Omega
, which we usually assume to be equal to
1
; the Fourier coefficients of
u^{s}
must solve the following differential equation:

-\frac{\partial^{2}u^{s}_{n}}{\partial x_{2}^{2}}-(k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}-\alpha^{2}_{n})u^{s}_{n}=0,\hskip 22.76228pt\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptx_{2}>H.

(2.8)

Since each one of these equations has two independent solutions, we are forced to choose between them as follows:

(i)

if
(k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}-\alpha^{2}_{n})<0
, we select the decaying solution

u^{s}_{n}(x_{2})=u_{n}^{s}(H)e^{-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}_{n}-k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}}(x_{2}-H)},

(ii)

if
(k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}-\alpha^{2}_{n})=0
, we choose the constant

u^{s}_{n}(x_{2})=u_{n}^{s}(H),

(iii)

if
(k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}-\alpha^{2}_{n})>0
, we opt for the solution corresponding to an outgoing wave

u^{s}_{n}(x_{2})=u_{n}^{s}(H)e^{i\sqrt{k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}-\alpha^{2}_{n}}(x_{2}-H)}.

Then the scattered field has the following form

u^{s}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}u^{s}_{n}(H)e^{i\beta_{n}(x_{2}-H)}e^{i\alpha_{n}x_{1}},

(2.9)

where we assume that
\alpha_{n}^{2}\neq k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}
for every
n
and

\beta_{n}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}-\alpha_{n}^{2}}&\alpha_{n}^{2}<k^{2}\varepsilon^{+},\\
i\sqrt{\alpha_{n}^{2}-k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}}&\alpha_{n}^{2}>k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}.\end{cases}

(2.10)

We are now able to introduce an appropriate radiation condition to be satisfied by the scattered field

Definition 2.19

(Radiation Condition)
.

We say that
u\in H^{1}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Omega)
satisfies the radiation conditions at infinity if there exists
h>0
such that, for all
x_{2}\geq h
, there holds

u^{s}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{n}e^{i\beta_{n}(x_{2}-h)}e^{i\alpha_{n}x_{1}},

(2.11)

with
u_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\beta_{n}
as in (
2.10
).

We now want to impose this radiation condition on
\Gamma_{H}
; to this purpose, we introduce the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator.

Taking the normal derivative of
u^{s}
on
\Gamma_{H}
, we have that

\frac{\partial u^{s}}{\partial x_{2}}\biggl|_{\Gamma_{H}}=i\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}u^{s}_{n}(H)\beta_{n}e^{i\alpha_{n}x_{1}}.

We want our numerical solution to reproduce this behaviour, so we define the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) operator

\displaystyle T:H^{1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})\to H^{-1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H}),

(2.12)

\displaystyle(T\phi)(x_{1})=i\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{n}\beta_{n}e^{i\alpha_{n}x_{1}},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for}\hskip 5.69046pt\phi\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H}).

This operator maps the Dirichlet trace of
\phi\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})
into its Neumann trace.

The following property holds for the DtN operator:

Lemma 2.20
.

The operator
T:H^{1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})\to H^{-1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})
is continuous.

Proof.

By straightforward computation (recall the definition of the
H^{s}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})
in Theorem
2.12
), it holds

\|T\phi\|^{2}_{H^{-1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+n^{2})^{-1/2}|\beta_{n}|^{2}|\phi_{n}|^{2}\leq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{|\beta_{n}|^{2}}{1+n^{2}}\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})}.

By definition of
\beta_{n}
,

\frac{|\beta_{n}|^{2}}{1+n^{2}}=\frac{k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}-\alpha_{n}^{2}}{1+n^{2}}=\frac{k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}-n^{2}-2n\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{0}^{2}}{1+n^{2}},

which is bounded in
n
.
∎

Using the Dirichlet to Neumann operator
T
, the problem can be restricted to the domain
\Omega
, i.e., given an incident plane wave
u^{i}
, seek an
\alpha_{0}
-quasi-periodic solution
u
such that

\begin{cases}\Delta u+k^{2}\varepsilon u=0&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega,\\
u=0&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{-H},\\
\frac{\partial(u-u^{i})}{\partial\nu}-T(u-u^{i})=0&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{H},\end{cases}

(2.13)

where
\nu
is the outward pointing normal on
\Gamma_{H}
.

We are now ready to derive the variational formulation of (
2.13
). We consider the space

V=\{v\in H^{1}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Omega):v=0\hskip 5.69046pt\text{on}\hskip 5.69046pt\Gamma_{-H}\},

(2.14)

where
H^{1}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Omega)
is the
\alpha_{0}
-quasi-periodic Sobolev space defined in Definition
2.5
. Let
u\in V
be a distributional solution of the Helmholtz problem (
2.13
). Multiplying both sides of the Helmholtz equation by a test function
v\in V
and integrating by parts, we get

\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}-k^{2}\varepsilon u\overline{v}\right)-\int_{\partial\Omega}\overline{v}\nabla u\cdot\nu=0.

The integral over the lower boundary
\Gamma_{-H}
is equal to zero since we have Dirichlet boundary conditions, while the integral over the left and right boundaries cancel for quasi-periodicity. This yields

\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}-k^{2}\varepsilon u\overline{v}\right)d\mathbf{x}-\int_{\Gamma_{H}}\overline{v}\nabla u\cdot\nu\hskip 2.84544ptds=0.

(2.15)

Now we use the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator to replace the normal derivative of the scattered field. Replacing
u
by
u^{i}+u^{s}
, using
Tu^{s}
for
\nabla u^{s}\cdot\nu
and replacing
u^{s}
again by
u-u^{i}
yields

\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}-k^{2}\varepsilon u\overline{v}\right)d\mathbf{x}-\int_{\Gamma_{H}}Tu\hskip 2.84544pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptds=\int_{\Gamma_{H}}\left(\nabla u^{i}\cdot\nu-Tu^{i}\right)\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptds.

(2.16)

The left hand side of the equation above defines the sesquilinear form

b_{\varepsilon}(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}-k^{2}\varepsilon u\overline{v}\right)d\mathbf{x}-\int_{\Gamma_{H}}Tu\hskip 2.84544pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptds.

(2.17)

For the right-hand side of the variational problem, we observe that on
\Gamma_{H}

\frac{\partial u^{i}}{\partial\nu}-Tu^{i}=2i\beta_{0}u^{i},

where
\beta_{0}=k\sin\theta
and
\nu
is the outer normal to
\Omega
. It follows that the weak formulation of the problem is: find
u\in V
such that

b_{\varepsilon}(u,v)=\int_{\Gamma_{H}}2i\beta_{0}u^{i}\hskip 0.85355pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptds\hskip 14.22636pt\forall\hskip 0.85355ptv\in V.

(2.18)

We already proved that the DtN operator
T
is a bounded mapping from
H^{1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})
into
H^{-1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})
. Also,
T
is bijective if and only if
\beta_{n}\neq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. From now on we interpret the integral
\int_{\Gamma_{H}}Tu\hskip 2.84544pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptds
for
u,v\in V
as the dual bracket
\langle Tu,v\rangle_{1/2}
(since
Tu\in H^{-1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})
and
v_{|\Gamma_{H}}\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})
by the trace theorem).

We can split the bilinear form a from (
2.17
) into two parts
a=a_{0}+a_{K}
with

\displaystyle a_{0}(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}+u\overline{v}\right)d\mathbf{x}-\int_{\Gamma_{H}}T_{0}u\hskip 2.84544pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptds,

\displaystyle a_{K}(u,v):=-(k^{2}\varepsilon+1)\int_{\Omega}u\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptd\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Gamma_{H}}(T_{0}-T)u\hskip 2.84544pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptds,

where

T_{0}\phi:=-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\alpha_{0}+n|\psi_{n}e^{i\alpha_{n}\cdot},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for}\hskip 5.69046pt\psi=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi_{n}e^{i\alpha_{n}\cdot}.

Then
-T_{0}
is nonnegative and, therefore,
a_{0}
is coercive in
V
. By the theorem of Lax-Milgram there exists an isomorphism
L
from
V
onto itself with
a_{0}(v,w)=(Lv,w)_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
for all
v,w\in V
. By the Riesz theorem there exists
r\in V
and a bounded operator
K
from
V
into itself with
a_{K}(v,w)=(Kv,w)_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
for all
v,w\in V
and
\int_{\Gamma_{H}}2i\beta_{0}u^{i}\hskip 0.85355pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptds=(r,v)_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
for
v\in V
. Furthermore, the operator
T-T_{0}
is compact since
\beta_{n}=i\hskip 1.42271pt|n+\alpha_{0}|+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{|n|})
for
|n|\to\infty
. This implies that also
K
is compact (see
[
14
]
).

The variational equation (
2.18
) can then be written in the form
Lu+Ku=0
with isomorphism
L
and compact
K
. It is easily seen that any solution
u\in V
of
Lu+Ku=0
is a classical solution of the scattering problem if it is extended by the solution of the Dirichlet problem with boundary data
u
into the region
(0,2\pi)\times(H,\infty)
. It follows that, under appropriate hypothesis on
\varepsilon
,
L+K
is one-to-one. Fredholm’s alternative then again yields the existence of a unique solution of the variational equation (
2.18
) in
V
. This construction can be made for other boundary conditions as well.

2.4
Well-posedness and Stability

There are various formulations of the periodic grating problem. For example, one can solve this problem for the inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation
\Delta u+k^{2}\varepsilon u=f
, with
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, as it is done in
[
5
]
. This difference is given by the choice to approximate the scattered field
u^{s}
instead of the total one. In our problem, we choose to solve the homogeneous Helmholtz problem since the PWDG method is suitable only for this type of equation. Another difference in the scattering problem can be found in the boundary conditions: a common choice is to consider a so-called transparent domain, which is simply a truncated vertical stripe with a Dirichlet to Neumann boundary condition on both the upper and the lower truncation of the domain; obviously, the definition of the lower DtN operator will change, as we want the total wave to point downward. Under
\Gamma_{-H}
the domain is assumed to have constant relative permittivity
\varepsilon^{-}
, which can be different from
\varepsilon^{+}
.

Another substantial difference in the formulations is in the assumptions on the relative permittivity
\varepsilon
. In some cases this is considered to be a function that changes in a continuous way, while in other works the value of
\varepsilon
is constant in every subdomain of
\Omega
, as we assume in our numerical tests.

The boundary value problem in
[
5
]
is the following: given an incident plane wave
u^{i}
, seek an
\alpha_{0}
-quasi-periodic solution
u
such that

\begin{cases}\Delta u+k^{2}\varepsilon u=f&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}-T^{-}u=0&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{-H},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}-T^{+}u=0&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{H},\end{cases}

(2.19)

where
T^{+},T^{-}
are the two DtN operators defined respectively on
\Gamma_{H}
and
\Gamma_{-H}
.

A fundamental property that we want assume on
\varepsilon
is the so-called
non trapping condition
. This property guarantees that the reflected wave does not remain "trapped" by the scatterer and ensures the uniqueness of the solution.

In
[
5
]
, under the assumption
\Re(\varepsilon)>0
and
\Im(\varepsilon)=0
, the following theorem is proved. We recall that these results are referred to a different boundary value problem.

Theorem 2.21
.

Assume that
\varepsilon\in C^{(1,1)}(\Omega_{j})
for all
j=1,2,...,I+1
, and the non-trapping conditions

\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial x_{2}}\geq 0,\hskip 14.22636pt\llbracket\varepsilon\rrbracket_{\Gamma_{j}}>0,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 8.5359pt\Re(\varepsilon^{+}-\varepsilon)\geq 0\hskip 8.5359pt\text{on}\hskip 8.5359pt\Gamma_{H}

(2.20)

hold for all
j=1,2,...,I+1
. Further, assume that
\Re(\varepsilon^{\pm})\geq 0
and
\Im(\varepsilon^{\pm})\geq 0
and
\varepsilon
is real in
\Omega
. Then, for
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique solution
u\in H^{1}_{\alpha}(\Omega)
of the variational problem (
2.19
).

Remark 2.22
.

When the non-trapping condition is not verified, we are not guaranteed to find a unique solution of (
2.19
). In Section 5 of
[
3
]
many non uniqueness examples are presented. In our analysis we will assume that this uniqueness property is satisfied.

Remark 2.23
.

Note that in the classical case where
\varepsilon
is piecewise constant, the first part of the non-trapping conditions is always verified, since
\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial x_{2}}=0
.

For our boundary value problem of interest (
2.13
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{-H}
, we will say that
\varepsilon
verifies the
non-trapping conditions
if the problem is well-posed and it holds a stability estimate for the solution
u
, i.e. if it exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u^{i}\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H})},

(2.21)

for every possible quasi-periodic incident wave
u^{i}
.

In the following results, we will assume
\varepsilon
is non-trapping, although we don’t know exactly what properties it has to verify.

2.5
Truncated Boundary Value Problem

In practical computations, one needs to truncate the infinite series of the DtN operator (
2.12
) to obtain an approximate mapping written as a finite sum

(T_{M}\phi)(x_{1})=i\sum_{n=-M}^{M}\phi_{n}\beta_{n}e^{i\alpha_{n}x_{1}},\hskip 14.22636pt

(2.22)

for all
\phi\in H^{1/2}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Gamma_{H})
. Here
M
is usually an integer greater than
k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}
if
k^{2}\varepsilon^{+}
is real.

The boundary value problem (
2.13
) is replaced by the following modified boundary value problem with a truncated DtN map: Find
u^{M}\in H^{1}_{\alpha_{0}}(\Omega)
such that

\begin{cases}\Delta u^{M}+k^{2}\varepsilon u^{M}=0&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega,\\
u^{M}=0&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{-H},\\
\frac{\partial(u^{M}-u^{i})}{\partial\nu}-T_{M}(u^{M}-u^{i})=0&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{H}.\end{cases}

(2.23)

Remark 2.24
.

It is not guaranteed that the good properties of (
2.13
), such as the uniqueness of the solution, are maintained by the truncated problem. In our analysis, we will assume that the number of Fourier modes
M
that we use to approximate the DtN operator is large enough to guarantee these properties also for (
2.23
).

Remark 2.25
.

Lemma 4.5 of
[
19
]
proves a good approximation property of the truncated DtN operator
T_{M}
for the full operator
T
: in particular, it shows a lower bound on the number of Fourier modes that guarantees that the error derived from the approximation of the DtN operator and the numerical scheme is small enough.

2.6
FEM with DtN boundary conditions

The finite element method for the Helmoltz equation and the diffraction grating problem with DtN boundary conditions has been studied in various works, such as
[
2
]
or
[
19
]
.

Here we propose a simple FEM-P1 method, which is mainly used to provide a benchmark to test the TDG developed in Section
4.1
.

Let
\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}
be a regular triangulation on the domain
\Omega
. Every triangle
K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}
is considered a closed set. To define a finite element space whose functions are quasi-periodic in the
x_{1}
direction, we require that if
(0,x_{2})
is a node on the left boundary, then
(2\pi,x_{2})
also is a node on the right boundary, and vice versa. Let
V_{h}\subset V
denote a conforming finite element space, that is,

V_{h}:=\{v_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega}):v_{h}|_{K}\in P_{p}(K)\hskip 5.69046pt\forall K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h},\hskip 5.69046ptv_{h}(0,x_{2})=e^{-i\alpha_{0}2\pi}v_{h}(2\pi,x_{2}),\hskip 5.69046pt\forall x_{2}\}

where
p
is a positive integer and
P_{p}(K)
is the set of polynomials of degrees
p
on
K
.

The finite element approximation to problem (
2.18
) reads as follows: Find
u_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

b_{\varepsilon}^{M}(u_{h}^{M},v_{h})=\int_{\Gamma_{H}}2i\beta_{0}u^{i}\hskip 0.85355pt\overline{v_{h}}\hskip 2.84544ptdx_{1},\hskip 14.22636pt\forall\hskip 0.85355ptv_{h}\in V_{h}.

(2.24)

where the sesquilinear form
b_{\varepsilon}^{M}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined as

b^{M}_{\varepsilon}(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-k^{2}\varepsilon u\overline{v}\right)dx-\int_{\Gamma_{H}}T_{M}u\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptdx_{1},

(2.25)

where
T_{M}
is the truncated Dirichlet to Neumann operator defined in (
2.22
).

2.6.1
Numerical implementation

We implement the discrete problem in MATLAB: we make use of polynomials of degree one. The basis functions are the usual hat functions with degrees of freedom associated to vertices of the triangulation. The problem
(\ref{discrete})
can be written as a linear system
A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}

The matrix
A
can be written as the sum of three parts,
A=A_{S}+k^{2}\varepsilon A_{M}+A_{DtN}
, the first two being respectively the usual stiffness and mass matrix of the finite element method, with the only difference that when we compute the matrix entries for the basis function associated to the right side of
\Omega
, we have to multiply for the quasi-periodicity constant
e^{-i\alpha_{0}2\pi}
(this is done to ensure the quasi-periodicity of the numerical solution). The matrix
A_{DtN}
is associated to the basis functions defined on the vertices on
\Gamma_{H}
and contains the value of the integrals
\int_{\Gamma_{H}}T_{M}u_{h}\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{v_{h}}\hskip 1.42271ptdx_{1}
. Note that the function
T_{M}u_{h}
is defined on all
\Gamma_{H}
, while
u_{h}
is non-zero only on a part of the boundary, so every basis function associated to the vertices on
\Gamma_{H}
communicates with every other basis function on
\Gamma_{H}
.

For what concerns the right hand side of the system, the only nonzero entries of the vector
\mathbf{b}
are the ones associated to vertices on
\Gamma_{H}
.

For the computation of the line integrals
\int_{\Gamma_{H}}T_{M}u_{h}\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{v_{h}}\hskip 1.42271ptdx_{1}
and the right-hand side
\int_{\Gamma_{H}}2i\beta_{0}u^{i}\hskip 0.85355pt\overline{v_{h}}\hskip 2.84544ptdx_{1}
, we make use of a Gauss-Legendre quadrature rule. In our experiments we choose
M=100
, so that we use
2M+1=201
Fourier modes to approximate the DtN operator.

Here and in the following, to create the domain triangulation, we will use the
distmesh
MATLAB package provided in
[
17
]
.

Chapter 3
Trefftz Discontinuous Galerkin Methods

In this Chapter we describe the Trefftz-DG methods for impedance boundary value problems on bounded domains, following
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
. For the extension to the case with piecewise constant relative permittivity we follow
[
11
]
. We will then extend this method to quasi-periodic problem in unbounded domains in Chapter
4
.

3.1
Trefftz Methods

A Trefftz method is a volume-oriented discretisation scheme, for which all trial and test functions are solutions of the PDE, when restricted to any element of a given mesh. The name comes from the work of Erich Trefftz, dating back to 1926, where this idea was applied to the Laplace equation.

Trefftz methods are related to both finite element (FEM) and boundary element methods (BEM). With the former they have in common that they provide a discretisation in the volume. With the latter they share some characteristics such as the need of integration on lower-dimensional manifolds only.

Trefftz methods possess several distinctive features that set them apart from traditional numerical methods: they provide an analytical representation of the solution in terms of Trefftz functions, allowing for a direct assessment of the global behavior of the field and offer the potential for high accuracy even with a relatively small number of degrees of freedom, leading to significant computational savings.

There are many different variations and extensions of Trefftz methods: the Trefftz-discontinuous Galerkin (TDG), the ultra weak variational formulation (UWVF), the discontinuous enrichment method (DEM), the variational theory of complex rays (VTCR) and the wave based method (WBM). We will only focus on the first method, and in particular we will explore the aspects of Plane Wave Discontinuous Galerkin (PWDG) methods.

3.2
Trefftz-DG method for impedance BVPs

Trefftz Discontinuous Galerkin (TDG) methods are a class of Trefftz-type methods for the spatial discretization of boundary value problems. We rely on a simple model boundary value problem (BVP) for the Helmholtz equation and derive the general DG formulation. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n=2,3
, be a bounded, Lipschitz, connected domain, with
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{R}
, where
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{R}
are disjoint components of
\partial\Omega
. We assume
\Gamma_{R}
to be non-empty, while
\Gamma_{D}
could also be empty. We denote by
\mathbf{n}
the outward-pointing unit normal vector field on
\partial\Omega
. We consider the Robin–Dirichlet BVP:

\begin{cases}\hskip 2.84544pt-\Delta u-\kappa^{2}u=0&\text{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega,\\
\hskip 2.84544ptu=g_{D}&\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{D},\\
\hskip 2.84544pt\nabla u\cdot n-i\kappa u=g_{R}&\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{R}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Here
\kappa=k\sqrt{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{C}
, where
k>0
is the wave number, that is assumed to be constant, and
\varepsilon
is the relative permittivity, which value may vary inside of
\Omega
. The functions
g_{D}
and
g_{R}
are the Dirichlet and impedance boundary data and
i
is the imaginary unit. We assume that
\Omega
,
\varepsilon
,
g_{D}
and
g_{R}
are such that
u\in H^{3/2+s}(\Omega)
, for some
s>0
. Typically,
\Gamma_{D}
represents the boundary of the scatterer, and
\Gamma_{R}
stands for an artificial truncation of the unbounded region where waves propagate.

Note that one can consider other boundary value problems, such as Neumann boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
or mixed conditions as in
[
8
]
. For now, we will only consider the model problem (
3.1
).

We introduce a finite element partition
\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}=\{K\}
of
\Omega
; this partition has to be suited to the composition of the domain
\Omega
, in the sense that
\varepsilon
has to be constant inside a single element
K
. With this assumption, we can consider
\kappa
as constant inside any element and we have to deal with discontinuities only on faces shared by two different elements. We write
\mathbf{n}_{K}
for the outward-pointing unit normal vector on
\partial K
, and
h
for the mesh width of
\{\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}\}
. We denote by
F_{h}=\bigcup_{K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}}\partial K
and
F_{h}^{I}=F_{h}\setminus\partial\Omega
the skeleton of the mesh and its inner part.

Given two elements of the partition
K_{1},K_{2}
, a piecewise-smooth function
v
and vector field
\tau
on
\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}
, we introduce on
\partial K_{1}\cap\partial K_{2}
the averages and the normal jumps:

\displaystyle\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{v\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(v_{|K_{1}}+v_{|K_{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\tau\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tau_{|K_{1}}+\tau_{|K_{2}}\right),

(3.2)

\displaystyle\llbracket v\rrbracket_{N}

\displaystyle:=v_{|K_{1}}\mathbf{n}_{K_{1}}+v_{|K_{2}}\mathbf{n}_{K_{2}},

\displaystyle\llbracket\tau\rrbracket_{N}

\displaystyle:=\tau_{|K_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K_{1}}+\tau_{|K_{2}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K_{2}}.

Furthermore, we denote by
\nabla_{h}
the elementwise application of
\nabla
and write
\partial_{n}=\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla_{h}
and
\partial_{n_{K}}=\mathbf{n}_{K}\cdot\nabla_{h}
for the normal derivatives on
\partial\Omega
and
\partial K
respectively.

For
s>0
, we define the two main spaces where Trefftz functions live, the broken Sobolev space
H^{s}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
and the Trefftz space
T(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
:

\displaystyle H^{s}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}):=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):v_{|K}\in H^{s}(K)\hskip 5.69046pt\forall K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}\},

(3.3)

\displaystyle T(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}):=\{v\in H^{1}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}):-\Delta v-\kappa^{2}v=0\hskip 2.84544pt\text{in}\hskip 2.84544ptK\hskip 2.84544pt\text{and}\hskip 2.84544pt\partial_{\mathbf{n}_{K}}v\in L^{2}(\partial K)\hskip 5.69046pt\forall K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}\}.

The discrete Trefftz space
V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
T(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
and
can be represented as
V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})=\bigoplus_{K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}}V_{p_{K}}(K)
, where
V_{p_{K}}(K)
is a
p_{K}
-dimensional subspace of
T(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
of functions supported in
K
.

We now derive the general DG formulation, following
[
9
]
. In order to derive the method, we start by writing problem (
3.1
) as a first order system:

\begin{cases}-i\kappa\mathbf{\sigma}=\nabla u&\text{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega,\\
-i\kappa u-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\sigma}=0&\text{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega,\\
u=g_{D}&\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{D},\\
-i\kappa\mathbf{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}-i\kappa u=g_{R}&\text{on}\hskip 2.84544pt\Gamma_{R}.\end{cases}

(3.4)

By multiplying the first and second equation of (
3.4
) by smooth test functions
\tau
and
v
, respectively, and integrating by parts on each
K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}
, we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{K}-i\kappa\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma\cdot\overline{\tau}\hskip 2.84544ptdV+\int_{K}u\overline{\nabla\cdot\tau}\hskip 2.84544ptdV-\int_{\partial K}u\overline{\tau\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS=0\hskip 8.5359pt\forall\tau\in\mathbf{H}(\text{div};K),\\
&\int_{K}-i\kappa\hskip 1.42271ptu\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptdV+\int_{K}\sigma\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\hskip 2.84544ptdV-\int_{\partial K}\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n}\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptdS=0\hskip 8.5359pt\forall v\in H^{1}(K).\end{split}

(3.5)

We now replace
u,v
by
u_{p},v_{p}\in V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
and
i\kappa\sigma,\tau
by
i\kappa\sigma_{p},\tau_{p}\in V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})^{d}
. Then, we approximate the traces of
u
and
i\kappa\sigma
across inter-element boundaries by the so-called
numerical fluxes
denoted by
\hat{u}_{p}
and
\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}
, which are defined below.

Replacing the elements in (
3.5
) with their numerical approximations, we get

\begin{split}&\int_{K}-i\kappa\sigma_{p}\cdot\overline{\tau_{p}}\hskip 2.84544ptdV+\int_{K}u_{p}\overline{\nabla\cdot\tau_{p}}\hskip 2.84544ptdV-\int_{\partial K}\hat{u}_{p}\overline{\tau_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS=0\hskip 8.5359pt\forall\tau_{p}\in V_{p}(K)^{d},\\
&\int_{K}-i\kappa u_{p}\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{v_{p}}\hskip 2.84544ptdV+\int_{K}\sigma_{p}\cdot\overline{\nabla v_{p}}\hskip 2.84544ptdV-\int_{\partial K}\frac{1}{i\kappa}\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\overline{v}_{p}\hskip 2.84544ptdS=0\hskip 8.5359pt\forall v_{p}\in V_{p}(K).\end{split}

(3.6)

Integrating again by parts the first equation of (
3.6
), we obtain:

\int_{K}i\kappa\sigma_{p}\cdot\overline{\tau_{p}}\hskip 2.84544ptdV=-\int_{K}\nabla u_{p}\cdot\overline{\tau_{p}}\hskip 2.84544ptdV+\int_{\partial K}(u_{p}-\hat{u}_{p})\overline{\tau_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS.

(3.7)

Now we assume that
\nabla_{h}V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})\subset V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})^{d}
, in this way we can take
\tau_{p}=\nabla v_{p}
in each element; note that with the PWDG method we are going to use in our model, this property is easily verified since the gradient of a plane wave is still a plane wave. Inserting the resulting expression into the second equation of (
3.6
), we obtain:

\int_{K}\left(\nabla u_{p}\cdot\overline{\nabla v_{p}}-\kappa^{2}u_{p}\overline{v_{p}}\right)dV-\int_{\partial K}(u_{p}-\hat{u}_{p})\overline{\nabla v_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS+\int_{\partial K}\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\overline{v_{p}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS=0.

(3.8)

We integrate by parts once more the first term in (
3.8
) and derive:

\int_{K}u_{p}\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{\left(-\Delta v_{p}-\kappa^{2}v_{p}\right)}\hskip 2.84544ptdV+\int_{\partial K}\hat{u}_{p}\overline{\nabla v_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS+\int_{\partial K}\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\overline{v_{p}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS=0.

(3.9)

(the boundary term appearing in this integration by parts cancels out with a boundary term already present in (
3.8
))

The first term in this equation vanishes, since
v_{p}\in V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
, so we simply have:

\int_{\partial K}\hat{u}_{p}\overline{\nabla v_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS+\int_{\partial K}\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\overline{v_{p}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS=0

(3.10)

We now give the explicit expression of the numerical fluxes
\hat{u}_{p}
and
\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}
.

On the internal faces
F\in F_{h}^{I}
we define

\begin{cases}\hat{u}_{p}=\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{u_{p}\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}+\beta\frac{1}{i\xi}\llbracket\nabla_{h}u_{p}\rrbracket_{N},&\\
\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}=-\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\nabla_{h}u_{p}\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}-i\xi\alpha\llbracket u_{p}\rrbracket_{N},&\end{cases}

(3.11)

where
\xi
is defined on an internal face
F
as

\xi=\frac{1}{2}\biggl[\Re(\kappa_{1})+\Re(\kappa_{2})\biggl],

(3.12)

with
\kappa_{1}=k\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}},\kappa_{2}=k\sqrt{\varepsilon_{2}}
being the value of
\kappa
on the two elements sharing the internal face
F
. The definition of
\xi
follows from the formulation in Section 3.3 of
[
11
]
.

On faces in
\Gamma_{R}
we choose

\begin{cases}\hat{u}_{p}=u_{p}-\delta\left(-\frac{1}{i\kappa}\nabla_{h}u_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}+u_{p}+\frac{1}{i\kappa}g_{R}\right),&\\
\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}=-\nabla_{h}u_{p}-(1-\delta)\left(-\nabla_{h}u_{p}+i\kappa u_{p}\mathbf{n}+g_{R}\mathbf{n}\right).&\end{cases}

(3.13)

Finally, on faces in
\Gamma_{D}
we opt for

\begin{cases}\hat{u}_{p}=g_{D},&\\
\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}=-\nabla_{h}u_{p}-i\kappa\alpha(u_{p}-g_{D})\mathbf{n}.&\end{cases}

(3.14)

The parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\delta
are called
flux parameters
; the numerical fluxes also take into account the inhomogeneous boundary conditions.

Now we add (
3.10
) over all elements
K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}
and obtain:

\int_{F_{h}^{I}}\left(\hat{u}_{p}\llbracket\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{p}}\rrbracket_{N}+\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}\cdot\llbracket\overline{v_{p}}\rrbracket_{N}\right)dS+\int_{F_{h}^{B}}\left(\hat{u}_{p}\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}+\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\overline{v_{p}}\right)dS=0.

Finally, we insert the above defined numerical fluxes and derive the DG method as follows: find
u_{p}\in V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
such that, for all
v_{p}\in V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
,

A_{h}(u_{p},v_{p})=L_{h}(v_{p}),

(3.15)

where

\displaystyle A_{h}(u,v):=

(3.16)

\displaystyle\int_{F_{h}^{I}}\left(\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{u\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}\hskip 1.42271pt\llbracket\overline{\nabla_{h}v}\rrbracket_{N}-i\xi^{-1}\beta\llbracket\nabla_{h}u\rrbracket_{N}\llbracket\overline{\nabla_{h}v}\rrbracket_{N}-\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\nabla_{h}u\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}\cdot\llbracket\overline{v}\rrbracket_{N}-i\xi\hskip 1.42271pt\alpha\hskip 1.42271pt\llbracket u\rrbracket_{N}\cdot\llbracket\overline{v}\rrbracket_{N}\right)dS

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma_{R}}\left((1-\delta)\hskip 1.42271ptu\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{\nabla_{h}v\cdot\mathbf{n}}-\delta\hskip 1.42271pti\kappa^{-1}\nabla_{h}u\cdot\mathbf{n}\overline{\nabla_{h}v\cdot\mathbf{n}}-\delta\nabla_{h}u\cdot\mathbf{n}\overline{v}-i\kappa(1-\delta)u\overline{v}\right)dS

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\left(-\nabla_{h}u\cdot\mathbf{n}\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{v}-i\kappa\hskip 1.42271pt\alpha\hskip 1.42271ptu\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{v}\right)dS,

and

L_{h}(v):=\int_{\Gamma_{R}}g_{R}\left(-\delta i\kappa^{-1}\overline{\nabla_{h}v\cdot\mathbf{n}}+(1-\delta)\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544pt\right)dS+\int_{\Gamma_{D}}g_{D}\left(-\alpha i\kappa\overline{v}-\overline{\nabla_{h}v\cdot\mathbf{n}}\right)dS.

(3.17)

3.3
The PWDG Method

Many different basis functions can be chosen to span the discrete Trefftz space
V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
; with the Plane Wave Discontinuous Galerkin (PWDG) method we choose to use plane waves as basis functions.

Consider an element
K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}
; we denote by
V_{p}(K)
the plane wave space on
K
spanned by
p
plane waves,
p\in\mathbb{N}
:

V_{p}(K)=\{\hskip 2.84544ptv\in L^{2}(K):v(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\exp\{i\kappa\mathbf{d_{j}}\cdot\mathbf{x}\},\hskip 8.5359pt\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\hskip 2.84544pt\},

(3.18)

where
\{\mathbf{d}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with
|\mathbf{d_{j}}|=1
are different directions. To obtain isotropic approximations, in 2D, uniformly-spaced directions on the unit circle can be chosen as
\mathbf{d}_{j}=\left(\cos(\frac{2\pi j}{p}),\sin(\frac{2\pi j}{p})\right)
.

Note that in this case we are choosing the same number of directions in every element
K
, so
p_{K}=p
for every
K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}
.

Then, we define:

V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})=\{\hskip 2.84544ptv\in L^{2}(\Omega):v_{|K}\in V_{p}(K),\hskip 5.69046pt\forall K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}\hskip 2.84544pt\}.

(3.19)

The variational formulation (
3.15
) derived above is still valid for the PWDG method. The main advantage of PWDG is that, using the properties of plane waves functions, we can easily find a closed formula to compute all the boundary integrals, reducing the errors caused by numerical approximation.

Proposition 3.1
.

The PWDG formulation is consistent.

Proof.

If
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
solves (
3.1
), then it holds that
\llbracket u\rrbracket_{N}=0
and
\llbracket\nabla_{h}u\rrbracket_{N}=0
on
F_{h}^{I}
, while
\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{u\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}=u
and
\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\nabla_{h}\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}=\nabla_{h}u
. Moreover,
u=g_{D}
on
\Gamma_{D}
and
\nabla_{h}u\cdot n-i\kappa u=0
on
\Gamma_{R}
.

From the definition of the numerical fluxes
\hat{u}_{p}
and
\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}
we have that on all the faces, when we substitute
u
in the expression of the fluxes, we have that
\hat{u}_{p}=u
and
\hskip 1.42271pt\widehat{i\kappa\sigma}_{p}=-\nabla_{h}u
. This gives us that, for every
v_{p}\in V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
,

A_{h}(u,v_{p})=L_{h}(v_{p}),

that is the consistency of the method.
∎

3.3.1
Matrix formulation in the case of
\varepsilon
constant

In this section, we assume
\varepsilon
constant and equal to
1
; under these assumptions we have that
\kappa=k
.

We already introduced the plane wave (
3.18
) space on
K
and the global space (
3.19
). The PWDG formulation is: find
u_{p}\in V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
such that, for all
v_{p}\in V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})

A_{h}(u_{p},v_{p})=L_{h}(v_{p}).

Consider

fixed; since

, we can write

\displaystyle u_{p}(\mathbf{x})_{|K}=\sum_{l=1}^{p}u_{l}^{K}\varphi_{l}^{K}(\mathbf{x}),\hskip 19.91684ptv_{p}(\mathbf{x})_{|K}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}v_{j}^{K}\varphi_{j}^{K}(\mathbf{x}),

where
u_{j}^{K},v_{j}^{K}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\hskip 5.69046pt\forall j=1,...,p
,
\hskip 5.69046pt\forall K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}

\varphi_{j}^{K}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\exp\{ik\mathbf{d_{j}}\cdot\mathbf{x}\}&\mathbf{x}\in K\\
0&\mathbf{x}\not\in K\end{cases},\hskip 11.38092pt\forall j=1,...,p,\hskip 5.69046pt\forall K\in\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h}.

Now, we can write

, where

is the total number of degrees of freedom, i.e.

, where

is the number of elements

Since

is a bilinear form and

is linear, the variational problem is equivalent to:

find
u_{p}\in V_{p}(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
such that

, for all

If we write
u_{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}\varphi_{j}
, we get that

A_{h}(u_{p},\varphi_{l})=A_{h}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}\varphi_{j},\varphi_{l}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}A_{h}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{l})=L_{h}(\varphi_{l}),\hskip 5.69046pt\forall l=1,...,N,

which can be written as a linear sistem
A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{L}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{l}=u_{l},

\displaystyle A_{l,j}=A_{h}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{l}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{l}=L_{h}(\varphi_{l}).

We note that if two basis functions
\varphi_{l}
and
\varphi_{j}
are associated to the same element
K
or to two adjacent elements
K,K^{\prime}
, the corresponding matrix entry is non-zero; in all the other cases the entry of the matrix is equal to zero.
First of all, we have to distinguish between internal and boundary elements. Then, we have to consider two different situations, the first being when
\varphi_{l},\varphi_{j}
are in the same element
K

K,K^{\prime}

Let’s now focus on the case of
\varphi_{l},\varphi_{j}
on the same internal element
K
:

\displaystyle A_{j,l}=A_{h}(\varphi_{l},\varphi_{j})=\int_{\partial K}

\displaystyle\big[\hskip 1.42271pt\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\varphi_{l}\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}\llbracket\overline{\nabla\varphi_{j}}\rrbracket_{N}-ik^{-1}\left(\beta\llbracket\nabla\varphi_{l}\rrbracket_{N}\llbracket\overline{\nabla\varphi_{j}}\rrbracket_{N}\right)

\displaystyle-\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\nabla_{h}\varphi_{l}\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}\cdot\llbracket\overline{\varphi_{j}}\rrbracket_{N}-ik\left(\alpha\llbracket\varphi_{l}\rrbracket_{N}\cdot\llbracket\overline{\varphi_{j}}\rrbracket_{N}\right)\big]\hskip 2.84544ptdS,

(note that the boundary terms are not present since
K
is an internal element).

We first observe that in this case, since
\varphi_{l}
and
\varphi_{j}

\mathbf{n}
of
K

\varphi_{l},\varphi_{j}

\displaystyle A_{j,l}=\int_{\partial K}

\displaystyle\big[\hskip 1.42271pt\frac{1}{2}\hskip 1.42271pt\varphi_{l}\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{\nabla\varphi_{j}}\cdot\mathbf{n}-\beta ik^{-1}\left(\nabla\varphi_{l}\cdot\mathbf{n}\overline{\nabla\varphi_{j}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\varphi_{l}\cdot\overline{\varphi_{j}}\mathbf{n}-\alpha ik\left(\varphi_{l}\mathbf{n}\cdot\overline{\varphi_{j}}\mathbf{n}\right)\big]\hskip 2.84544ptdS.

We observe that, on the element

\displaystyle\varphi_{l}(\mathbf{x})=\exp\{ik\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{l}\},

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi_{l}(\mathbf{x})=ik\mathbf{d}_{l}\exp\{ik\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{l}\}=ik\mathbf{d}_{l}\hskip 1.42271pt\varphi_{l}(\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle A_{j,l}

\displaystyle A_{j,l}

3.4
Error analysis

We develop our a priori error analysis under the following additional assumptions:

2.

We consider the broken Sobolev space

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\text{TDG}}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\|\xi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\beta^{\frac{1}{2}}\llbracket\nabla_{h}w\rrbracket_{N}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(F_{h}^{I})}+\left\|\xi^{\frac{1}{2}}\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}\llbracket w\rrbracket_{N}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(F_{h}^{I})}

\displaystyle+\left\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{h}w\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{R})}+\left\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}w\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{R})}+\left\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}w\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})},

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\text{TDG}^{+}}^{2}

\displaystyle:={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\text{TDG}}^{2}+\left\|\xi^{\frac{1}{2}}\beta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{w\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(F_{h}^{I})}+\left\|\xi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\nabla_{h}w\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(F_{h}^{I})}

\displaystyle+\left\|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}w\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{R})}+\left\|\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{h}w\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}.

It holds that these seminorms are indeed norms in the Trefftz space
T(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The seminorms
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\text{TDG}}
and
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\text{TDG}^{+}}
are norms in the Trefftz space
T(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
.

Proof.

Let
w\in T(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
be such that
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\text{TDG}}^{2}=0
. Then
w\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and satisfies

in
\Omega
,
w=0
on
\partial\Omega
, and

on
\Gamma_{R}
, which implies

on
\Gamma_{D}
and
\nabla w\cdot\mathbf{n}-i\kappa w=0
on
\Gamma_{R}
. The uniqueness of the solution of problem (
3.1
), which is given in Theorem 2.1 of
[

]
, gives

.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

For every
w\in T(\scalebox{1.44}{$\tau\hskip-1.70709pt$}_{h})
it hold that

\Im\hskip 1.42271pt[A_{h}(w,w)]=-{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|w\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\text{TDG}}^{2}\hskip 1.42271pt.

Proof.

Provided that

, local integration by parts permits us to rewrite the bilinear form (

) as

\displaystyle A_{h}(u,v)

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{h}u,\nabla_{h}v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\int_{F_{h}^{I}}\llbracket u\rrbracket_{N}\cdot\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\overline{\nabla_{h}v}\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}\hskip 2.84544ptdS-\int_{F_{h}^{I}}\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\nabla_{h}u\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}\cdot\llbracket\overline{v}\rrbracket_{N}\hskip 2.84544ptdS

\displaystyle-\int_{\Gamma_{R}}\delta\hskip 1.42271ptu\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{\nabla_{h}v\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS-\int_{\Gamma_{R}}\delta\hskip 1.42271pt\nabla_{h}u\cdot\mathbf{n}\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptdS

\displaystyle-i\int_{F_{h}^{I}}\xi^{-1}\beta\llbracket\nabla_{h}u\rrbracket_{N}\hskip 1.42271pt\llbracket\overline{\nabla_{h}v}\rrbracket_{N}\hskip 2.84544ptdS-i\int_{\Gamma_{R}}\kappa^{-1}\delta\hskip 1.42271pt\nabla_{h}u\cdot\mathbf{n}\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{\nabla_{h}v\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS

\displaystyle-\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\hskip 1.42271pt\nabla_{h}u\cdot\mathbf{n}\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{v}\hskip 2.84544ptdS-\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\hskip 1.42271ptu\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{\nabla_{h}v\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS

Therefore, from the expression above we have

\displaystyle A_{h}(w,w)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla_{h}w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\Re\left[\int_{F_{h}^{I}}\llbracket w\rrbracket_{N}\cdot\{\hskip-2.84544pt\{\overline{\nabla_{h}w}\}\hskip-2.84544pt\}\hskip 2.84544ptdS+\int_{\Gamma_{R}}\delta\hskip 1.42271ptw\hskip 1.42271pt\overline{\nabla_{h}w\cdot\mathbf{n}}\hskip 2.84544ptdS\right]

Remark 3.4

We can observe that the well posedness of the PWDG method follows directly from Propositions

Proof.

Chapter 5
Conclusion

We proved that Plane Wave Discontinuous Galerkin methods are well-suited for the problem of scattering by periodic structures. The DtN boundary condition and the quasi-periodicity can be adapted to the PWDG formulation and provide good numerical results. The convergence tests highlight a behaviour comparable to the one of usual plane wave based methods on bounded domains, which leads to similar estimates for the numerical solution.

The DtN-PWDG formulation is continue and quasi-optimal for the boundary value problem and it could be used to simulate the grating problem on different domains,

Many extensions are possible, such as a more precise analytical study of the scattering problem, which could lead to explicit stability estimates, as well as a study of the dependence of the variational solution on the scatterer geometry.
