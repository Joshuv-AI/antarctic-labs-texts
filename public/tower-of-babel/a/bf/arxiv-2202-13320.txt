Revisiting the action of a subgroup of the modular group on imaginary quadratic number fields
Abdulaziz Deajim

Abstract
. Consider the modular group PSL ( 2 , ℤ ) = ⟨ x , y | x 2 = y 3 = 1 ⟩ \mbox{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})=\langle x,\,y\,|\,x^{2}=y^{3}=1\rangle generated by the transformations x : z ↦ − 1 / z x:z\mapsto-1/z and y : z ↦ ( z − 1 ) / z y:z\mapsto(z-1)/z . Let H H be the proper subgroup ⟨ y , v | y 3 = v 3 = 1 ⟩ \langle y,\,v\,|\,y^{3}=v^{3}=1\rangle of PSL ​ ( 2 , ℤ ) \mbox{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z}) , where v = x ​ y ​ x v=xyx . The reference (M. Ashiq and Q. Mushtaq, Actions of a subgroup of the modular group on an imaginary quadratic field , Quasigropus and Related Systems 14 (2006), 133–146) proposed results concerning the action of H H on the subset { a + − n c | a , b = a 2 + n c , c ∈ ℤ , c ≠ 0 } \{\frac{a+\sqrt{-n}}{c}\,|\,a,b=\frac{a^{2}+n}{c},c\in\mathbb{Z},c\neq 0\} of the imaginary quadratic number field ℚ ⁡ ( − n ) \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}) for a positive square-free integer n n . In the current article, the author points out and corrects errors appearing in the aforementioned reference. Most importantly, the corrected estimate for the number of orbits arising from this action is given, where symmetries in the orbits play a crucial role in the proof.

Introduction

The study of the action of the modular group
\mbox{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
and its subgroups on various commutative algebraic structures has been common since the works of Graham Higman and his collaborators in the 1980s (
[
7

[

Stothers in
[
18
]
showed that the modular group
G=\mbox{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
has the finite presentation
\langle x,\,y\,|\,x^{2}=y^{3}=1\rangle
, where
x
and
y
are, respectively, the linear fractional transformations
z\mapsto-1/z
and
z\mapsto(z-1)/z
. For a square-free integer
m
, the action of
G
and its subgroups on certain subsets of the quadratic number field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{m})
has been extensively studied (see for instance
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
11

,
[
12
]
,
[
14
]
, and
[
19
]
). In particular, Ashiq and Mushtaq (in
[
3
]
) studied the proper subgroup
H
of
G
generated by the transformations
y
and
v=xyx:z\mapsto-1/(z+1)
, where they showed that
y^{3}=v^{3}=1
are defining relations for
H
, so
H=\langle y,\,v\,|\,y^{3}=v^{3}=1\rangle
.

For a positive square-free integer
n
, consider the following subset of the imaginary quadratic number field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-n})
:

{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n}):=\{\frac{a+\sqrt{-n}}{c}\,|\,a,b=\frac{a^{2}+n}{c},c\in\mathbb{Z},c\neq 0\}.

Deajim and Aslam in
[
6

studied the action of
G
on
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
, in which the main result is counting the orbits resulting from this action. Ashiq and Mushtaq in
[
2

studied the action of the subgroup
H
on
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
, where they suggested a formula to count the orbits resulting from this action. However, the author of this article has found some technical/mathematical mistakes in
[
2
]
which invited the need to revisit its findings with the aim of correcting these mistakes and, particularly, giving the correct formula for the number of orbits.

In Section 2, the errors of
[
2
]
are listed along with the reasoning for such a judgment. Section 3 aims primarily at correcting the main result of
[
2
]
, namely
[
2
, Theorem 9]
, by giving in Theorem
3.5
the accurate formula for the number of orbits arising from the action of
H
on
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
. Besides, some lemmas which were used to prove Theorem
3.5
help also in correcting some of the mistakes of
[
2
]
mentioned in Section 2. Finally, a conclusion containing a summary of the main findings of the article is given in Section 4.

We summarize below the main errors in
[
2
]
with some comments on them.

Claim 1

[
2
, line 1 of the proof of Theorem 6]
:

Let
\alpha
be a totally positive imaginary quadratic number. Then, by Theorem 3 (i),
y(\alpha)
or
y^{2}(\alpha)
is a totally negative imaginary quadratic number.

Claim 1 is not necessarily true. We show in Lemma
3.1
that if
\alpha
is totally positive, then one of
y(\alpha)
and
y^{2}(\alpha)
is totally positive and the other is either totally positive, totally negative, or of norm zero. For instance, consider the totally positive element
\alpha=(2+\sqrt{-5})/3
. Then, both
y(\alpha)=(1+\sqrt{-5})/3
and
y^{2}(\alpha)=(1+\sqrt{-5})/2
are totally positive as well. Another example is given by
\beta=(1+\sqrt{-1})/2
, then
y(\beta)=\sqrt{-1}
is of norm zero and
y^{2}(\beta)=1+\sqrt{-1}
is totally positive.
On the other hand, it should also be noted that
[
2
, Theorem 3 (i)]
states that
y(\alpha)
and
y^{2}(\alpha)
are totally positive when
\alpha
is totally negative, which is true, but Claim 1 above strangely misquotes it. Note that Claim 1 being false renders the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 6]
false.

Claim 2

[
2
, Theorem 8]
:

(v) If
\alpha=(1+\sqrt{-n})/c_{1}
(
n\neq 3
), where
1+n=c_{1}c_{2}
,
c_{1}\neq 1
or
n+1
, then
\alpha
is the only element of norm 1 in
\alpha^{H}
.

It is strange that their proof of (v) contradicts the statement above, where the authors of
[
2
]
stated explicitly that
y^{2}(\alpha)=(1+\sqrt{-n})/\frac{(n+1)}{2}
when
c_{1}=2
. But then it is obvious that
y^{2}(\alpha)\in\alpha^{H}
and is of norm 1 with
\alpha\neq y^{2}(\alpha)
! This shows that the statement of (v) is false in general. For instance, consider
\alpha=(1+\sqrt{-5})/2
. Then
\alpha
is of norm 1 and satisfies the assumptions of Theorem 8 (v) mentioned above. However,
y^{2}(\alpha)=(1+\sqrt{-5})/3
is also of norm 1 belonging to
\alpha^{H}
and is different from
\alpha
.

As for the other parts of
[
2
, Theorem 8]
, it is also noted that the arguments in the proof of parts (iii) and (iv) of
[
2
, Theorem 8]
are not sufficient although the statements are correct. In fact, parts (iii) and (iv) specify certain cases where an
H
-orbit contains only one element of norm zero. Part of the proof of Theorem
3.5
shows that this is always the case for any
n
and for any
H
-orbit containing an element of norm zero.

Claim 3

[
2
, Theorem 9]
:

If
n\neq 3
, then the total number of orbits of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
under the action of

are:

(i)

2[d(n)+2d(n+1)-6]

(ii)

2[d(n)+2d(n+1)-4]
if

This is the main result of
[

]
, which is inaccurate. Its proof relies on
[

, Theorem 8]
, which has some gaps (see Claim 2). The correct estimate of the number of orbits is given in Theorem

below. We briefly give here some counter-examples to
[

. For odd integers, consider

and

for instance. For

, the above estimate gives

which is absurd. Our estimate (see Theorem

. For

, whereas our estimate gives
|\mathcal{O}^{H}_{-21}|=16

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha=(a+\sqrt{-n})/c\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
.

(1)

If
\alpha
is totally positive, then one of
y(\alpha)
and
y^{2}(\alpha)
is totally positive and the other is either totally positive, totally negative, or of norm zero; whereas both
v(\alpha)
and
v^{2}(\alpha)
are totally negative.

(2)

If
\alpha
is totally negative, then one of
v(\alpha)
and
v^{2}(\alpha)
is totally negative and the other is either totally negative, totally positive, or of norm zero; whereas both
y(\alpha)
and
y^{2}(\alpha)
are totally positive.

(3)

If
\alpha
is of norm zero, then both
y(\alpha)
and
y^{2}(\alpha)
are totally positive and both
v(\alpha)
and
v^{2}(\alpha)
are totally negative.

Proof.

Once and for all, note here and elsewhere that Table 1 is used without mention.

(1)

Assume that
a,b,c>0
(the case
a,b,c<0
is proved similarly). We show first that at least one of
y(\alpha)
and
y^{2}(\alpha)
is totally positive. To the contrary, suppose that neither of them is totally positive. If
y(\alpha)
is totally negative, then (as
c_{y(\alpha)}=b>0
)
a_{y(\alpha)}=-a+b<0
. As
b_{y^{2}(\alpha)}=c>0
and as
b_{y^{2}(\alpha)}
and
c_{y^{2}(\alpha)}
have the same sign, we have
-a+b-a+c=c_{y^{2}(\alpha)}>0
implying that
a_{y^{2}(\alpha)}=-a+c>0
(as
-a+b<0
). So,
y^{2}(\alpha)
is totally positive, a contradiction. Similarly, if
y^{2}(\alpha)
is totally negative, then
y(\alpha)
is totally positive, a contradiction. On the other hand, if
y(\alpha)
is of norm zero, then
a_{y(\alpha)}=-a+b=0
. So,
c_{y^{2}(\alpha)}=-a+b-a+c=-a+c=a_{y^{2}(\alpha)}
implying that (as
c_{y^{2}(\alpha)}\neq 0
)
a_{y^{2}(\alpha)}c_{y^{2}(\alpha)}>0
and thus
y^{2}(\alpha)
is totally positive, a contradiction. Similarly, if
y^{2}(\alpha)
is of norm zero, then
y(\alpha)
is totally positive, a contradiction. This argument leads to the assertion that at least one of
y(\alpha)
and
y^{2}(\alpha)
is totally positive. Suppose that it is
y(\alpha)
which is totally positive (if it were
y^{2}(\alpha)
, then the proof would be similar). It then follows that
y^{2}(\alpha)
is either totally positive, totally negative, or of norm zero depending respectively on whether
a_{y^{2}(\alpha)}>0
,
a_{y^{2}(\alpha)}<0
, or
a_{y^{2}(\alpha)}=0
. As for
v(\alpha)
and
v^{2}(\alpha)
, note that having
a_{v(\alpha)}=-a-c<0
and
c_{v(\alpha)}=2a+b+c>0
implies that
v(\alpha)
is totally negative. Also, having
a_{v^{2}(\alpha)}=-a-b<0
and
c_{v^{2}(\alpha)}=b>0
implies that
v^{2}(\alpha)
is totally negative.

(2)

Similar to the proof of part (1).

(3)

Suppose that
a=0
. Then
a_{y(\alpha)}c_{y(\alpha)}=b^{2}>0
,
a_{y^{2}(\alpha)}c_{y^{2}(\alpha)}=bc+c^{2}>0
(
b
and
c
are of the same sign), and so
y(\alpha)
and
y^{2}(\alpha)
are totally positive. On the other hand,
a_{v(\alpha)}c_{v(\alpha)}=-bc-c^{2}<0
,
a_{v^{2}(\alpha)}c_{v^{2}(\alpha)}=-b^{2}<0
, and so
v(\alpha)
and
v^{2}(\alpha)
are totally negative.

\square

We have the following obvious observations (see
[
6
, Lemmas 3.2 and 3.3]
).

.

For

(i)

\alpha

x(\alpha)

(ii)

\alpha

x(\alpha)

For

Lemma 3.3
.

Under the action of
H
on
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
, the following holds for
\alpha=(a+\sqrt{-n})/c\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
:

(i)

[
6

The cycle
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\widehat{\;\alpha\;}}}
is totally positive if and only if either
(
a>0
,
a<b
,
a<c
)
or
(
a<0
,
a>b
,
a>c
)
.

(ii)

The cycle
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\widehat{\;\alpha\;}}}
is totally negative if and only if either
(
a>0
,
-a>b
,
-a>c
)
or
(
a<0
,
-a<b
,
-a<c
)
.

(iii)

The cycle
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\widehat{\;\alpha\;}}}
is totally positive if and only if the cycle
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\,\widehat{x(\alpha)\,}}}
is totally negative.

(iv)

If the cycle
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\widehat{\;\alpha\;}}}

(i.e.

(v)

If

\alpha

Proof.

(i)

This is
[
6

.

(ii)

Suppose that
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\widehat{\;\alpha\;}}}
is totally negative. As
\alpha
is totally negative, either (
a>0
and
b,c<0
) or (
a<0
and
b,c>0
). Assume that
a>0
and
b,c<0
. Since
b_{v(\alpha)}=c<0
, we get
c_{v(\alpha)}<0
. As further

is totally negative, we must then have

and so
-a>c
. Since also

and

is totally negative,
a_{v^{2}(\alpha)}=-a-b>0
and so
-a>b
. A similar argument yields that if
a<0
and
b,c>0
then
-a<b
and
-a<c
.

Conversely, suppose that
a>0
,
-a>b
, and
-a>c
(the case when
a<0
,
-a<b
, and
-a<c

-a>c
,
c<0

\alpha

a_{v(\alpha)}=-a-c>0
and
b_{v(\alpha)}=c<0

are of the same sign,
v(\alpha)
is totally negative too. As
a_{v^{2}(\alpha)}=-a-b>0
and
c_{v^{2}(\alpha)}=b<0

is totally negative as well. Hence, the cycle
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\widehat{\;\alpha\;}}}
is totally negative.

(iii)

Suppose that
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\widehat{\;\alpha\;}}}
is totally positive. By (i), if
a>0
,
a<b
, and
a<c
, then it follows from (ii) that
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\,\widehat{x(\alpha)\,}}}
is totally negative since
a_{x(\alpha)}=-a<0
,
-a_{x(\alpha)}=a<c=b_{x(\alpha)}
, and
-a_{x(\alpha)}=a<b=c_{x(\alpha)}

a<0
,
a>b
, and

is similar.

Conversely, suppose that
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\,\widehat{x(\alpha)\,}}}

,
-a_{x(\alpha)}=a>b_{x(\alpha)}=c
, and
-a_{x(\alpha)}=a>c_{x(\alpha)}=b
, then it follows from (i) that
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\widehat{\;\alpha\;}}}
is totally positive. The case when
a_{x(\alpha)}<0
,
-a_{x(\alpha)}<b_{x(\alpha)}
, and
-a_{x(\alpha)}<c_{x(\alpha)}
is similar.

(iv)

Suppose that
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\widehat{\;\alpha\;}}}
is totally positive. By (iii),
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\,\widehat{x(\alpha)\,}}}
is totally negative. To show that
\alpha^{H}\neq x(\alpha)^{H}
, we need to show that

for any

. It can be seen that an arbitrary non-identity element of

takes one and only one of the following forms:

\displaystyle h_{1}

\displaystyle=y^{\epsilon},\;\mbox{for}\;\epsilon\in\{1,2\}

\displaystyle h_{2}

\displaystyle=v^{\delta},\;\mbox{for}\;\delta\in\{1,2\}

\displaystyle h_{3}

\displaystyle=y^{\epsilon_{k}}v^{\delta_{k}}\dots y^{\epsilon_{2}}v^{\delta_{2}}y^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\delta_{1}},\;\mbox{for}\;k\geq 1,\epsilon_{i},\delta_{i}\in\{1,2\},1\leq i\leq k

\displaystyle h_{4}

\displaystyle=v^{\delta_{k}}y^{\epsilon_{k-1}}v^{\delta_{k-1}}\dots y^{\epsilon_{2}}v^{\delta_{2}}y^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\delta_{1}},\;\mbox{for}\;k\geq 2,\epsilon_{i},\delta_{j}\in\{1,2\},1\leq i\leq k-1,1\leq j\leq k

\displaystyle h_{5}

\displaystyle=v^{\delta_{k}}y^{\epsilon_{k}}\dots v^{\delta_{2}}y^{\epsilon_{2}}v^{\delta_{1}}y^{\epsilon_{1}},\;\mbox{for}\;k\geq 1,\epsilon_{i},\delta_{i}\in\{1,2\},1\leq i\leq k

\displaystyle h_{6}

\displaystyle=y^{\epsilon_{k}}v^{\delta_{k-1}}y^{\epsilon_{k-1}}\dots v^{\delta_{2}}y^{\epsilon_{2}}v^{\delta_{1}}y^{\epsilon_{1}},\;\mbox{for}\;k\geq 2,\epsilon_{i},\delta_{j}\in\{1,2\},1\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq k-1

The
h_{1}

Since there is no

such that

, we get
h_{1}(\alpha)=y^{\epsilon}(\alpha)\neq x(\alpha)

is totally negative (by Lemma

The
h_{2}
case:

Since there is no

, a contradiction (see the

The

case:

As
\alpha
is totally positive, it follows from Lemma

(1) that

is totally negative and so, by Lemma

The
h_{6}
case:

If we let
h_{6}^{*}=y^{-\epsilon_{k}}h_{6}=v^{\delta_{k-1}}y^{\epsilon_{k-1}}\dots v^{\delta_{2}}y^{\epsilon_{2}}v^{\delta_{1}}y^{\epsilon_{1}}
, then as
y^{\epsilon_{1}}(\alpha)
is totally positive, it follows by an argument similar to that in the
h_{3}
case that
h_{6}^{*}(\alpha)
is totally negative. If
h_{6}(\alpha)=x(\alpha)
, then
h_{6}^{*}(\alpha)=y^{-\epsilon_{k}}x(\alpha)
, which is impossible since
h_{6}^{*}(\alpha)
is totally negative, while
y^{-\epsilon_{k}}x(\alpha)
is totally positive by Lemma
3.1
(2).

Now, by having just shown the impossibility of all cases above, we conclude that there is no
h\in H
such that
h(\alpha)=x(\alpha)
and therefore
\alpha
and
x(\alpha)
belong to distinct
H
-orbits as claimed.

(v)

Remark first that we excluded the case
n=1
since the only elements of
\mathbb{C}
fixed by
x
are
\pm\sqrt{-1}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-1})
(i.e.
x(\pm\sqrt{-1})=\pm\sqrt{-1}
); see
[
6
, Lemma 3.4]
. Let
n\neq 1
and suppose that
\alpha
has norm zero. Then,
x(\alpha)
has norm zero too. We need to show that
h(\alpha)\neq x(\alpha)
for any
h\in H
. By Lemma
3.1
(3), both
y(\alpha)
and
y^{2}(\alpha)
are totally positive, and both
v(\alpha)
and
v^{2}(\alpha)
are totally negative. Now, for
h_{1},\dots,h_{6}
as in (iv), and since none of
y(\alpha),\,y^{2}(\alpha),\,v(\alpha),v^{2}(\alpha)
has norm zero it is easily seen that
h_{i}(\alpha)
is either totally negative or totally positive for every
i=1,\dots,6
(see the argument in (iv)). Thus
h_{i}(\alpha)\neq x(\alpha)
for any
i=1,\dots,6
. This settles the claim.

\square

Example 1
.

For
n=7
and
\alpha=(-1+\sqrt{-7})/(-4)
, it can be checked that
y(\alpha)=(-1+\sqrt{-7})/(-2)
,
y^{2}(\alpha)=(-3+\sqrt{-7})/(-4)
, and so
\alpha
,
y(\alpha)
, and
y^{2}(\alpha)
are all totally positive. On the other hand,
x(\alpha)=(1+\sqrt{-7})/(-2)
,
vx(\alpha)=(1+\sqrt{-7})/(-4)
, and
v^{2}x(\alpha)=(3+\sqrt{-7})/(-4)
which are all totally negative.

Lemma 3.4
.

(
[
6
, Corollary 3.1]
)

Under the action of
G
on
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
, we have the following:

(i)

Every
G
-orbit in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-1})
contains a unique element of norm zero.

(ii)

Every
G
-orbit in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-2})
contains a unique pair of distinct elements of norm zero.

(iii)

Every
G
-orbit in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
, for
n\geq 3
, contains either a unique pair of distinct elements of norm zero or a unique totally positive cycle, but not both.

The aim of our main result below is to compute the cardinality
|\mathcal{O}^{H}_{-n}|
correcting the estimate given in
[
2
, Theorem 9]
(see Claim 3 in Section 2). In particular, we utilize the above results to simply show that

|\mathcal{O}^{H}_{-1}|=|\mathcal{O}^{G}_{-1}|
, and
|\mathcal{O}^{H}_{-n}|=2|\mathcal{O}^{G}_{-n}|
for
n\geq 2
,

and then make use of the precise estimate of
|\mathcal{O}^{G}_{-n}|
given in
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
for all
n\geq 1
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Under the action of
H
, the number of orbits in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})
is

\qquad\qquad|\mathcal{O}^{H}_{-n}|=\begin{cases}|\mathcal{O}^{G}_{-1}|&\mbox{, if $n=1$}\\
\\
2|\mathcal{O}^{G}_{-n}|&\mbox{, if $n\geq 2$}\\
\end{cases}

\;\,=\begin{cases}2&\mbox{, if $n=1$}\\
4&\mbox{, if $n=2$}\\
8&\mbox{, if $n=3$}\\
2d(n)+\frac{4}{3}\;\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}[d(i^{2}+n)-2d_{\leq i}(i^{2}+n)]&\mbox{, if $n>3$.}\end{cases}

Moreover,
|\mathcal{O}^{H}_{-n}|\equiv 0\;(\bmod\,8)
for
n\geq 3
.

Proof.

We begin with the case
n\geq 2
, where we show that
|\mathcal{O}_{-n}^{H}|=2|\mathcal{O}_{-n}^{G}|
. We show this equality by showing that every
G
-orbit splits into two distinct
H
-orbits. Let
\alpha^{G}\in\mathcal{O}_{-n}^{G}
. We know from Lemma
3.4
(ii, iii) that
\alpha^{G}
either contains a unique pair of distinct elements of norm zero or a unique totally positive cycle, but not both. We proceed by dealing with these two possibilities.

Firstly, suppose that
\alpha^{G}
contains a unique pair of distinct elements
\beta
and
x(\beta)
of norm zero. Since
\beta\neq x(\beta)
in this case, we get from Lemma
3.3
(v) that it cannot be the case that both elements belong to
\alpha^{H}
. We show that
\alpha^{G}
splits into two distinct
H
-orbits (
\alpha^{H}
and
\beta^{H}
) or (
\alpha^{H}
and
x(\beta)^{H}
) by showing that
\alpha^{H}
must contain one and only one of these two norm-zero elements while the other belongs to
\alpha^{G}-\alpha^{H}
. If
\beta\in\alpha^{H}
, then (by Lemma
3.3
(v))
\alpha^{G}
is the disjoint union of
\alpha^{H}
and
x(\beta)^{H}
and we are done. Similarly, if
x(\beta)\in\alpha^{H}
, then
\alpha^{G}
is the disjoint union of
\alpha^{H}
and
\beta^{H}
. Now, it remains to show that one of
\beta
or
x(\beta)
must belong to
\alpha^{H}
. Assume that
\beta\not\in\alpha^{H}
; we show that
x(\beta)\in\alpha^{H}
. Since
\alpha\in\alpha^{G}-\alpha^{H}
, there is some
g\in G-H
such that
\beta=g(\alpha)
. In order to show that
x(\beta)\in\alpha^{H}
, we argue that
xg\in H
, which then would imply that
x(\beta)=xg(\alpha)\in\alpha^{H}
as desired. Keeping in mind the forms
h_{0}=1
and
h_{1},\dots,h_{6}
that elements of
H
can take (see the proof of Lemma
3.3
(iv)), we see that
g
, as an element of
G
but not an element of
H
, can take one of the forms
xh_{i}
,
h_{i}x
,
y^{\gamma}xh_{i}
, or
h_{i}xy^{\gamma}
for an appropriate
h_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,6
,
\gamma\in\{1,2\}
. We do the following argument that exhausts all possible cases.

If
g=xh_{i}
, then
xg=h_{i}\in H
for
i=0,\dots,6
.

If
g=y^{\gamma}xh_{i}
, then
xg=xy^{\gamma}xh_{i}=v^{\gamma}h_{i}\in H
for
i=0,\dots,6
.

If
g=h_{0}x=x
, then
xg=1\in H
.

If
g=h_{0}xy^{\gamma}
, then
xg=y^{\gamma}\in H
.

If
g=h_{1}x=y^{\epsilon}x
, then
xg=xy^{\epsilon}x=v^{\epsilon}\in H
.

If
g=h_{1}xy^{\gamma}=y^{\epsilon}xy^{\gamma}
, then
xg=xy^{\epsilon}xy^{\gamma}=v^{\epsilon}y^{\gamma}\in H
.

If
g=h_{2}x=v^{\delta}x=xy^{\delta}
, then
xg=y^{\delta}\in H
.

If
g=h_{2}xy^{\gamma}=v^{\delta}xy^{\gamma}=xy^{\delta+\gamma}
, then
xg=y^{\delta+\gamma}\in H
.

If
g=h_{3}x
, then
xg=x(y^{\epsilon_{k}}v^{\delta_{k}}\dots y^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\delta_{1}})x=v^{\epsilon_{k}}y^{\delta_{k}}\dots v^{\epsilon_{1}}y^{\delta_{1}}\in H
.

If
g=h_{3}xy^{\gamma}
, then
xg=x(y^{\epsilon_{k}}v^{\delta_{k}}\dots y^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\delta_{1}})xy^{\gamma}=v^{\epsilon_{k}}y^{\delta_{k}}\dots v^{\epsilon_{1}}y^{\delta_{1}+\gamma}\in H
.

If
g=h_{4}x
, then
xg=x(v^{\delta_{k}}y^{\epsilon_{k-1}}v^{\delta_{k-1}}\dots y^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\delta_{1}})x=y^{\delta_{k}}v^{\epsilon_{k-1}}y^{\delta_{k-1}}\dots v^{\epsilon_{1}}y^{\delta_{1}}\in H
.

If
g=h_{4}xy^{\gamma}
, then
xg=x(v^{\delta_{k}}y^{\epsilon_{k-1}}v^{\delta_{k-1}}\dots y^{\epsilon_{1}}v^{\delta_{1}})xy^{\gamma}=y^{\delta_{k}}v^{\epsilon_{k-1}}y^{\delta_{k-1}}\dots v^{\epsilon_{1}}y^{\delta_{1}+\gamma}\in H
.

If
g=h_{5}x
, then
xg=x(v^{\delta_{k}}y^{\epsilon_{k}}\dots v^{\delta_{1}}y^{\epsilon_{1}})x=y^{\delta_{k}}v^{\epsilon_{k}}\dots y^{\delta_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{1}}\in H
.

If
g=h_{5}xy^{\gamma}
, then
xg=x(v^{\delta_{k}}y^{\epsilon_{k}}\dots v^{\delta_{1}}y^{\epsilon_{1}})xy^{\gamma}=y^{\delta_{k}}v^{\epsilon_{k}}\dots y^{\delta_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{1}}y^{\gamma}\in H
.

If
g=h_{6}x
, then
xg=x(y^{\epsilon_{k}}v^{\delta_{k-1}}y^{\epsilon_{k-1}}\dots v^{\delta_{1}}y^{\epsilon_{1}})x=v^{\epsilon_{k}}y^{\delta_{k-1}}v^{\epsilon_{k-1}}\dots y^{\delta_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{1}}\in H
.

If
g=h_{6}xy^{\gamma}
, then
xg=x(y^{\epsilon_{k}}v^{\delta_{k-1}}y^{\epsilon_{k-1}}\dots v^{\delta_{1}}y^{\epsilon_{1}})xy^{\gamma}=v^{\epsilon_{k}}y^{\delta_{k-1}}v^{\epsilon_{k-1}}\dots y^{\delta_{1}}v^{\epsilon_{1}}y^{\gamma}\in H
.

This shows that
x(\beta)\in\alpha^{H}
. Similarly, if
x(\beta)\not\in\alpha^{H}
, then
\beta\in\alpha^{H}
.

Secondly, suppose that
\alpha^{G}
contains a unique totally positive cycle
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\widehat{\;\beta\;}}}
. By Lemma
3.3
(iii),
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\,\widehat{x(\beta)\,}}}
is a totally negative cycle which is obviously contained in
\alpha^{G}
. Note also, by Lemma
3.3
(iv), that it cannot be the case that both cycles are contained in
\alpha^{H}
. Following exactly the same argument of the zero-norm elements above, we conclude that
\alpha^{G}
is either the disjoint union of
\alpha^{H}
and
\beta^{H}
or is the disjoint union of
\alpha^{H}
and
x(\beta)^{H}
, and therefore
\alpha^{G}
splits into two distinct
H
-orbits in this case as well.

We have, so far, proved that
|\mathcal{O}_{-n}^{H}|=2|\mathcal{O}_{-n}^{G}|
for
n\geq 2
. The proof would by complete in this case by applying
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
, which precisely gives the value of
|\mathcal{O}_{-n}^{G}|
as follows:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{O}^{G}_{-n}|

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c@{\,,\,}l}2&\mbox{if $n=2$}\\
4&\mbox{if $n=3$}\\
d(n)+\frac{2}{3}\;\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}[d(i^{2}+n)-2d_{\leq i}(i^{2}+n)]&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

and, moreover, declares the congruence
|\mathcal{O}^{G}_{-n}|\equiv 0\;(\bmod\,4)
for
n\geq 3
.

Finally, for the case
n=1
, the only elements of norm zero in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-1})
are
\pm\sqrt{-1}
, and both are fixed by
x
(see the proof of Lemma
3.3
(v)). We also have by Lemma
3.4
(i) that
\alpha^{G}
contains a unique element of norm zero; that is
\alpha^{G}
must contain either
\sqrt{-1}
or
-\sqrt{-1}
but not both. Suppose that
\sqrt{-1}\in\alpha^{G}
. If
\sqrt{-1}\not\in\alpha^{H}
, then, by following the argument in the second paragraph of this proof,
x(\sqrt{-1})\in\alpha^{H}
. But
x(\sqrt{-1})=\sqrt{-1}
, a contradiction. Thus,
\sqrt{-1}\in\alpha^{H}
. Similarly, if
-\sqrt{-1}\in\alpha^{G}
, then
-\sqrt{-1}\in\alpha^{H}
. This shows that
\alpha^{H}=\alpha^{G}
and hence
|\mathcal{O}^{H}_{-1}|=|\mathcal{O}^{G}_{-1}|
, which in turn equals 2 (by
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
).

\square

Conclusion

Consider the subgroup
H=\langle y,\,v\,|\,y^{3}=v^{3}=1\rangle
of
\mbox{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})=\langle x,\,y\,|\,x^{2}=y^{3}=1\rangle
, where
x:z\mapsto-1/z
,
y:z\mapsto(z-1)/z
, and
v=xyx:z\mapsto-1/(z+1)
. For a square-free
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it is shown that the number of orbits in the set

{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}(\sqrt{-n})=\{\frac{a+\sqrt{-n}}{c}\in{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-n})\,|\,a,b=\frac{a^{2}+n}{c},c\in\mathbb{Z},c\neq 0\},

under the action of
H
, is given as follows:

\begin{cases}2&\mbox{, if $n=1$}\\
4&\mbox{, if $n=2$}\\
8&\mbox{, if $n=3$}\\
2d(n)+\frac{4}{3}\;\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}[d(i^{2}+n)-2d_{\leq i}(i^{2}+n)]&\mbox{, if $n>3$}\end{cases}

and is congruent to 0 modulo 8 for all
n\geq 3
, where
d(k)
denotes the number of positive divisors of
k
,
d_{\leq i}(k)
denotes the number of positive divisors of
k
which do not exceed
i
for
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
i\leq k
, and
\lfloor\;.\;\rfloor
is the floor function.
