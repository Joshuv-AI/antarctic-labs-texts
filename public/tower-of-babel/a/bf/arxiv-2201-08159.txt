Exhaustive existence and non-existence results for Hardy–Hénon equations in 𝐑 n \R^{n}
Yoshikazu Giga, Qu ´ ô c Anh Ngô

Abstract
. This paper concerns solutions to the Hardy–Hénon equation − Δ ​ u = | x | σ ​ u p -\Delta u=|x|^{\sigma}u^{p} in 𝐑 n \R^{n} with n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 and arbitrary p , σ ∈ 𝐑 p,\sigma\in\R . This equation was proposed by Hénon in 1973 as a model to study rotating stellar systems in astrophysics. Although there have been many works devoting to the study of the above equation, at least one of the following three assumptions p > 1 p>1 , σ ≥ − 2 \sigma\geq-2 , and n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 is often assumed. The aim of this paper is to investigate the equation in other cases of these parameters, leading to a complete picture of the existence/non-existence results for non-trivial, non-negative solutions in the full generality of the parameters. In addition to the existence/non-existence results, the uniqueness of solutions is also discussed.

1.
Introduction

Over the last several decades, elliptic equations of the following form

-\Delta u=K(x)u^{p}

(1.1)

have captured much attention because they arise in various branches of pure and applied mathematics. For example, the simple model when
K\equiv 1
, also known as the Lane–Emden equation, has its root in astrophysics because the function
u
can be thought of the density of stars. In this note, we are interested in non-trivial, non-negative, classical solutions to the following special case of (
1.1
)

\displaystyle-\Delta u=|x|^{\sigma}u^{p}\quad\text{in }\R^{n},

(
1.2
)
σ

where
n\geq 1
and
p\in\R
is a parameter. Equation (
1.2
)
σ
was first proposed by Hénon in 1973 as a model to study rotating stellar systems. Traditionally, the equation (
1.2
)
σ
is called the Hénon (resp. Hardy or Lane–Emden) equation if
\sigma>0
(resp.
\sigma<0
or
\sigma=0
). However, as we are mostly interested in
\sigma\neq 0
without any sign restriction we shall call (
1.2
)
σ
the Hardy–Hénon equation.

So far, apart from the non-negativity of solutions, we have not precisely mentioned the definition of solutions that we are interested in, especially, we have not yet mentioned what ‘classical solution’ really means. Usually, by a classical solution
u
to (
1.2
)
σ
we mean
u\in C^{2}(\R^{n})
. However, the presence of
\sigma
and
\alpha
plays some role forcing us to work with solutions in suitable sense. For example, in the case
\sigma<0
it does not make sense for (
1.2
)
σ
to define at the origin. Therefore, let us mention precisely the notion of solution involved here. Throughout this paper, a function
u
is called
classical solution
to (
1.2
)
σ
if it belongs to

C^{2}(\R^{n})\;\;\text{ if }\sigma\geq 0,\quad C(\R^{n})\cap C^{2}(\R^{n}\backslash\{0\})\;\;\text{ if }\sigma<0;

(1.3)

and the equation (
1.2
)
σ
is fulfilled pointwisely except at
x=0
for
\sigma<0
. Having this definition, in the case
\sigma\geq 0
, the above notion of classical solutions coincides with the standard one. For simplicity, from now on, by solution we mean classical solution.

Let us now briefly go through some literature review for solutions to the equation (
1.2
)
σ
. Equipped with (
1.2
)
σ
is the important number
p_{\mathsf{S}}(\sigma)
, called the critical Sobolev exponent, which is given by

p_{\mathsf{S}}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{n+2+2\sigma}{n-2}&\text{ if }n>2,\\
+\infty&\text{ if }n\leq 2.\end{cases}

This critical exponent, generalizing the classical ones for the case
\sigma=0
, is important because the solvability of the equation (
1.2
)
σ
often changes when
p
passes through it.

For the autonomous case, that is
\sigma=0
, the existence of solutions to the equation (
1.2
)
0
is now well-understood for general
p
, and there have been many works devoting to (
1.2
)
0
. For example, it is well-known that (
1.2
)
0
has no classical solution if
1<p<p_{\mathsf{S}}(0)
, see
[
GS81
,
CLi91
]
. On the other hand, if
p\geq p_{\mathsf{S}}(0),
the equation (
1.2
)
0
always has positive classical solutions; see for instance
[
GS81
,
LGZ06
]
. In the remaining cases, namely
p\leq 1
and
n\geq 1
we still have non-existence results. For example, if
n\leq 2
, we simply apply the maximum principle to super-harmonic functions. In the case
n\geq 3
and
p<0
, one can use a trick via tackling the auxiliary function
u(x)+\varepsilon|x|^{2}
and let
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. In the case
n\geq 3
and
0<p<1
, this can be achieved by making use of the method developed in
[
BK92
]
and in the case
p=1
this is done by some simple integral estimate. It is worth noting that existence and non-existence results for higher order equations in
\R^{n}
have already been studied. These equations are slightly different from the classical equation (
1.2
)
0
by replacing
-\Delta
by
\Delta^{m}
for any
m\in\{1,2,3,..\}
. For interested readers, we refer to the recent work
[
NN

†

20
]
for a complete picture of existence and non-existence results.

For the non-autonomous case, namely
\sigma\neq 0
, although there have also been many works devoting to the equation, the situation is not well-understood, especially there are some cases for which no result is known, and this is the starting point of this work. Among others, Ni proved in
[
Ni82
,
Ni86
]
the existence of classical solution for

p\geq p_{\mathsf{S}}(\sigma)\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma>-2.

Hence, we are left with the subcritical case
p<p_{\mathsf{S}}(\sigma)
. As far as we know, the subcritical case
1<p<p_{\mathsf{S}}(\sigma)
was firstly classified by Reichel and Zou. In
[
RZ00
]
, they considered a cooperative semilinear elliptic system with refinement of the moving sphere method. The result of Reichel and Zou indicates that (
1.2
)
σ
does not admit any classical solution if

1<p<p_{\mathsf{S}}(\sigma)\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma>-2;

see
[
RZ00
, Theorem 2]
. On the other hand, following the results due to Mitidieri and Pohozaev in
[
MP01
, Theorem 6.1]
, Dancer, Du and Guo in
[
DDG11
, Theorem 2.3]
, Brezis and Cabré in
[
BC98
]
, the condition
\sigma>-2
is necessary for the existence of solutions to (
1.2
)
σ
. Thus, we have a complete picture for the existence problem of solutions to (
1.2
)
σ
in the case
p>1
, namely, we must assume both

\sigma>-2\quad\text{and}\quad p\geq p_{\mathsf{S}}(\sigma).

In the case of higher order Hardy–Hénon equations, we refer the reader to
[
NY20
]
.

From the above discussion, we are left with the case
n\geq 1
,
p\leq 1
, and
\sigma\in\R
. This leads us to a recent work due to Dai and Qin, which also motivates us to write this note; see
[

\left.\begin{aligned} &p\leq 0,&\sigma\in\R,&\quad\text{ and }\quad n\geq 1,\qquad\\

Remark 1.1
.

From Tables
3
and
2
above we have some remarks concerning the work
[
PS12
]
.

(1)

The equation considered in
[
PS12
]
is in
(b,+\infty)
for any
b\geq 0
and solution is only assumed to be of
C^{2}
in
(b,+\infty)
. Hence, solution in the sense of
[
PS12
]
is not necessarily continuous up to
b
. This is very important for us to make use of duality which will be described below.

(2)

Although the result in
[
PS12
]
for the case
n=1
is stated for
p>1
, it is quite clear that their argument actually works well for the case
p=1
.

As mentioned in the preceding remark, in the proof of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
, we make use some duality. To be more precise, if
u
solves

-u^{\prime\prime}(r)=r^{\sigma}u(r)^{p}

in
(0,+\infty)
, then the Kelvin type transformation
v:r\mapsto ru(1/r)
solves

-v^{\prime\prime}(r)=r^{-p-\sigma-3}v(v)^{p}

in
(0,+\infty)
. Using this, it is shown in section
4.2.4
that the non-existence result in the case
\sigma\in(-2,-1)
and
p\in(-1-\sigma,1)
follows from the non-existence result in the case
\sigma\leq-2
and
0<p<1
which is established earlier in section
4.2.1
. Another example is that the non-existence result in the case
\sigma<-2
and
1\leq p\leq-1-\sigma
studied in section
4.2.2
can be obtained from the similar result in the case
\sigma\geq-2
and
p\geq 1
.

Equations of the form (
1.2
)
σ
with
p\neq 1
and
\sigma\neq-2
enjoy some invariant properties, namely, if
u
solves (
1.2
)
σ
then

v(x)=\lambda^{\frac{2+\sigma}{p-1}}u(\lambda x)

also solves the same equation. This immediately tells us that in the existence regime the equation (
1.2
)
σ
in
n\geq 2
has many solutions. However, in the case
n=1
and in the existence regime (either
\sigma<-2
and
p>-1-\sigma
or
\sigma>-2
and
p<-1-\sigma
), our special solution
C|x|^{\frac{2+\sigma}{1-p}}
for some
C>0
does not create any new solution because

\lambda^{\frac{2+\sigma}{p-1}}\big|\lambda x\big|^{\frac{2+\sigma}{1-p}}=|x|^{\frac{2+\sigma}{1-p}}.

Hence, the invariant property applied to the above special solution gives us nothing. Nevetheless, it seems that uniqueness of solutions does not occur. For example, in the special case
\sigma=1
and
p=-4
, the equation admits the following two solutions

C_{1}|x|^{3/5},\quad C_{2}|x|^{3/5}(3|x|+5)^{2/5}.

(1.6)

Since uniqueness is a delicated issue, we relegate this to section
5
.

2.
Preliminaries

In what follows, the notation
B_{r}
is always understood as the open ball
B_{r}(0)
in
\R^{n}
centered at the origin with radius
r
. The boundary of
B_{r}
is denoted by
\partial B_{r}
. When the function
u
is radially symmetric with respect to the origin, instead of writing
u(x)
, we also use the notation
u(r)
with
r
being
|x|
.

Throughout the paper and in the case
n\geq 2
, we use the notation
\overline{u}(r)
with
r\geq 0
to denote the spherical average of
u
centered at the origin on the sphere
\partial B_{r}
, namely
\overline{u}(0)=u(0)
and

\overline{u}(r)=\frac{1}{|\partial B_{r}|}\int_{\partial B_{r}}ud\sigma

if
r>0
. (For simplicity, we shall omit
d\sigma
.) Spherical averaging
\overline{u}
has some nice properties such as it, as a function of
r\geq 0
, is continuous provided
u
is continuous and it enjoys the following identity

\overline{\Delta u}=\Delta\overline{u}

provided
u
is of class
C^{2}
.

In the following subsections, we establish some nice properties for non-negative solutions to (
1.2
)
σ
in
\R^{n}
. Note that, throughout the paper, the symbol
C
denotes a generic positive constant whose value could be different from one line to another.

2.1.
The case
n\geq 2

First we start with the case
n\geq 2
, arbitrary
\sigma\in\R
, and arbitrary
p\neq 0
. Although it is possible to give a combined proof of Lemmas
2.1
and
2.3
below, we prefer to highlight the key difference between the sign of solutions and keep them separate.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
n\geq 2
and that
u\geq 0
is any non-trivial solution to (
1.2
)
σ
for arbitrary
\sigma
and arbitrary
p\neq 0
. Then we have the following claims

(1)

the functions

r\mapsto\overline{u}(r)\quad\text{and}\quad r\mapsto r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)

are decreasing in
(0,+\infty)
;

(2)

there hold

r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\leq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{u}(r)\leq u(0)

for all
r>0
;

(3)

there holds

u(0)>0.

Proof.

As
u\geq 0
everywhere in
\R^{n}
, we claim that

\Delta\overline{u}(r)=-\overline{|x|^{\sigma}u(r)^{p}}\leq 0

in
[0,+\infty)
, which by the equality

\Delta\overline{u}(r)=r^{1-n}\big(r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\big)^{\prime}

for
r>0
implies the decreasing of
r\mapsto r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)
in
[0,+\infty)
. We note that we have not made any assumption on
p
and
\sigma
.

To establish the monotonicity of
r\mapsto\overline{u}(r)
, we need more work and this requires
n\geq 2
. In view of the decreasing of
r\mapsto r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)
there holds

\lim_{r\searrow 0}r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)=L\in(-\infty,+\infty].

Here
L
could be
+\infty
. Our aim is to rule out the possibility of
L>0
.

Case 1
. Suppose that
L\in(0,+\infty)
. Then for some
r_{1}\in(0,1)
we must have

\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq\frac{L}{2}r^{1-n}

for all
r\in(0,r_{1}]
. Integrating both sides of the preceding inequality over
(r,r_{1})
gives

\max_{\overline{B}_{1}}u\geq\overline{u}(r)\geq\overline{u}(r)-\overline{u}(r_{1})\geq\frac{L}{2}\int_{r}^{r_{1}}s^{1-n}ds

for all
r\in(0,r_{1}]
. Keep in mind that
\max_{\overline{B}_{1}}u<+\infty
thanks to the continuity of
u
. Hence, from the above argument and as
n\geq 2
we easily obtain a contradiction if we let
r\searrow 0
because the integral
\int_{0}^{r_{1}}s^{1-n}ds
diverges.

Case 2
. Suppose that
L=+\infty
. Then for some
r_{1}\in(0,1)
we must have

\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq\frac{1}{2}r^{1-n}

for all
r\in(0,r_{1}]
. Repeating the above argument we also get a contradiction.

From the two cases above, we are in position to conclude that

. This and the decreasing of

in
[0,+\infty)
imply that
\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\leq 0
in
(0,+\infty)
. This tells us that the function

is decreasing in
(0,+\infty)
.

Now we establish the inequality

for all

. But this simply follows from the decreasing of

in
(0,+\infty)
. Finally, one can easily conclude that

. This is because otherwise the identity
u(0)=0
and the decreasing of

in
(0,+\infty)
imply that

must be trivial, which violates our hypothesis.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

We have the following two remarks:

(1)

The condition

)

Next we consider the case

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that

,
\sigma\in\R
, and

(3)

Proof.

The argument provided below is essentially the same as that used in the case

-(r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r))^{\prime}=r^{n-1+\sigma}>0

\lim_{r\searrow 0}r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)=L\in(-\infty,+\infty]

Case 1

. Suppose that

\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq\frac{1}{2}r^{1-n}

. Repeating the above argument we also get a contradiction.

2.2.
The case
n=1

Let
u\geq 0
be non-negative, non-trivial solution to the equation (
1.2
)
σ
in
(0,+\infty)
, namely (
1.5
). An analog of Lemma
2.1
is the following.

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume that
u\geq 0
is any non-trivial,
C^{2}
-solution to (
1.2
)
σ
in
(0,+\infty)
with arbitrary
p,\sigma\in\R
, not necessarily continuous up to
0
. Then we have the following claims:

(1)

Respectively, the functions

r\mapsto u(r)\quad\text{ and }\quad r\mapsto u^{\prime}(r)

are increasing and decreasing in
(0,+\infty)
;

(2)

there exist three positive constants
c
,
C
, and
r_{1}
such that

c\leq u(r)\leq Cr

for all
r\geq r_{1}
.

Proof.

As
u^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
in
(0,+\infty)
, we conclude that the function
r\mapsto u^{\prime}(r)
is decreasing in
(0,+\infty)
. This allows us to define

\ell=\lim_{r\nearrow+\infty}u^{\prime}(r)\in[-\infty,+\infty),

here
\ell
could be minus infinity. As
u\geq 0
in
(0,+\infty)
we necessarily have
\ell\geq 0
. Otherwise,
u^{\prime}(r)
becomes strictly negative for large
r
, which is not possible by seeing

u(r)-u(r_{0})=\int_{r_{0}}^{r}u^{\prime}(s)ds\searrow-\infty.

Hence, there holds

u^{\prime}\geq 0\quad\text{in }(0,+\infty),

namely
u
is monotone increasing in
(0,+\infty)
. The monotonicity of
u
tells us that there must exist some
c>0
and some
r_{2}>0
such that

u(r)\geq c

for all
r\geq r_{2}
. Otherwise,
u
must vanish everywhere. To estimate
u
from the above, we make use of the inequality
u^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
. Indeed, this tells us that

u^{\prime}(r)\leq u^{\prime}(r_{2})

and

u(r)\leq u^{\prime}(r_{2})r+u(r_{2})-u^{\prime}(r_{2})r_{2}

for all
r\geq r_{2}
. In other words,
u
grows at most linearly at infinity. Hence, there are
C>0
and
r_{1}\gg r_{2}
such that

u(r)\leq Cr

for all
r\geq r_{1}
. This completes the proof.
∎

It is worth emphasizing that in Lemma
2.3
we do not assume any condition on
\sigma
and
p
. In addition, it is worth recalling that we do not assume any continuity up to the origin. We end this section by establishing a maximum principle type result.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
u\geq 0
is a non-trivial
C^{2}
-solution to the equation (
1.2
)
σ
in
(0,+\infty)
. Then
u
must be strictly positive everywhere.

Proof.

Suppose that
u\geq 0
is a non-trivial
C^{2}
-solution to the equation in
(0,+\infty)
. We claim that

u(r)>0\quad\text{in }(0,+\infty).

By convention, it suffices to consider the case
p\geq 0
. Indeed, by way of contradiction, assume that
u(2r_{*})=0
for some
r_{*}>0
. As
u
is non-decreasing and
u\geq 0
, we must have
u(r)=0
for all
0<r\leq 2r_{*}
. In particular, we have

0=u^{\prime}(r_{*})\geq u^{\prime}(r_{*})-u^{\prime}(r)=-\int_{r_{*}}^{r}u^{\prime\prime}(s)ds=\int_{r_{*}}^{r}s^{\sigma}u(s)^{p}ds

for all

. In the case

, as

is non-trivial, the integral
\int_{r_{*}}^{r}s^{\sigma}u(s)^{p}ds>0
for some large

. This is a contradiction. In the case

, the preceding estimate becomes

0=u^{\prime}(r_{*})\geq\int_{r_{*}}^{r}s^{\sigma}ds

for all

. Again, this is not true. Hence

in
(0,+\infty)
as claimed.
∎

We shall use the above maximum type principle in section
4.2
and section
5
.

3.
The case

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. As mentioned earlier, we are left with either

and

or

and

. For clarity, we divide our analysis into three parts as follows: in subsections

and

By seeing Table

, it is clear that we do not have any existence result in these three cases. Hence, we can argue by contradiction, namely throughout this section we always assume by way of contradiction that

is a solution to (
1.2
)
σ
in
\R^{n}
with

.

3.1.
The case
n\geq 2
and
p<0

By convention we have
u>0
in
\R^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Also, as the map

is convex in
(0,+\infty)
and one can apply Jensen’s inequality to get

\frac{1}{|\partial B_{r}|}\int_{\partial B_{r}}|x|^{\sigma}u(x)^{p}=\frac{r^{\sigma}}{|\partial B_{r}|}\int_{\partial B_{r}}u(x)^{p}\geq r^{\sigma}\Big(\frac{1}{|\partial B_{r}|}\int_{\partial B_{r}}u(x)\Big)^{p}

-\Delta\overline{u}(r)=\overline{|x|^{\sigma}u(r)^{p}}\geq r^{\sigma}\overline{u}(r)^{p}

for all

-\Delta\overline{u}(r)\geq r^{\sigma}\overline{u}(r)^{p}\geq u(0)^{p}r^{\sigma}

for all

-\big(r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\big)^{\prime}\geq u(0)^{p}r^{n-1+\sigma}

To tackle our equation, we consider the two cases
\sigma\geq-2
and

3.1.1.
The case
\sigma>-2

In this scenario, we have

and by Lemma
2.1
we know that

and
\overline{u}^{\prime}(1)\leq 0
. Now integrating both sides of (

\displaystyle-r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq\overline{u}^{\prime}(1)-r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}\big(r^{n+\sigma}-1\big)

for

. Therefore,

\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\leq-\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}r^{1+\sigma}+\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}r^{1-n}

for

. Depending on the size of

, there are two cases:

Case 1
. If

and

, then integrating (

) over
[1,r]

\displaystyle\overline{u}(r)-\overline{u}(1)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}\frac{r^{2+\sigma}-1}{2+\sigma}+\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}\frac{r^{2-n}-1}{2-n}

for

and

in
\R^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

Case 1
. If

and
\sigma>-2
, then integrating (

) over
[1,r]
gives

\displaystyle\overline{u}(r)-\overline{u}(1)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}\frac{r^{2+\sigma}-1}{2+\sigma}+\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}\log r

for

. Again, we still have

as

, and

at infinity. This is still contradiction with

in
\R^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

3.1.2.
The case
n\geq 2
and
\sigma\leq-2

Still by Lemma

we must have

r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\leq 0

for all
r>0
. Integrating both sides of (

) over
(r_{*},r)
to get

\displaystyle-r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq r_{*}^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r_{*})-r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq u(0)^{p}\int_{r_{*}}^{r}s^{n-1+\sigma}ds.

Case 1
. Suppose

. We easily reach a contradiction if we fix

Case 2
. Suppose

\displaystyle-r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}\big(r^{n+\sigma}-r_{*}^{n+\sigma}\big)

\displaystyle-r^{n-1}\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}r^{n+\sigma}

\displaystyle-\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq\frac{u(0)^{p}}{n+\sigma}r^{1+\sigma}

Remark 3.1
.

At first glance, one can notify that the condition

3.2.
The case

In this particular case, we show that our equation (

Remark 3.2
.

Notice that to derive contradiction in the case

in

Case 1

\Big(\overline{u}^{\prime}(1)+\frac{1}{2+\sigma}\Big)\log r+\overline{u}(1)\geq\frac{1}{(2+\sigma)^{2}}\big[r^{2+\sigma}-1\big]

\Big(\overline{u}^{\prime}(1)+\frac{1}{2+\sigma}\Big)\frac{1-r^{2-n}}{n-2}+\overline{u}(1)\geq\frac{1}{(2+\sigma)^{2}}\big[r^{2+\sigma}-1\big]

3.3.
The case

and

Let

solve (
1.2
)
σ
in
\R^{2}
. We observe that in the case

by definition any solution to (

)

in

is now of class

. Hence we simply make use of a Liouville type result for super-harmonic functions in two dimensions to obtain the non-existence result. Therefore, we limit ourselves to the case

. For this reason, by making use of the maximum principle and as

is non-trivial we deduce that

in
\R^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.

We note that in the case

the nonlinearity

is concave, which makes the analysis through spherical averages more involved. To obtain the desired non-existence result, we follow the argument in
[

,

]
. Depending on the size of

, we consider two cases: either

or

.

3.3.1.
The case
p=1

This part is easy to handle because after taking spherical averages centered at the origin, our equation gives

-(r\overline{u}^{\prime}(r))^{\prime}=r^{1+\sigma}\overline{u}(r)

in
(0,+\infty)
. Thanks to Lemma
2.1
we know that
r\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\leq 0
for all
r>0
and
\overline{u}
is decreasing in
(0,+\infty)
. Hence, for arbitrary but small

gives

\displaystyle-r\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq\varepsilon\overline{u}^{\prime}(\varepsilon)-r\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\varepsilon}^{r}(s\overline{u}^{\prime}(s))^{\prime}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\varepsilon}^{r}s^{1+\sigma}\overline{u}(s)ds\geq\overline{u}\big(\frac{r}{2}\big)\int_{\varepsilon}^{r/2}s^{1+\sigma}ds.

Hence, if
\sigma\leq-2
, then we immediately obtain contradiction by letting

because the integral
\int_{0}^{r/2}s^{1+\sigma}ds
diverges. Now we consider the remaining case
\sigma\in(-2,0)
. In this case, we can let

to get

-\overline{u}^{\prime}(r)\geq r^{-1}\overline{u}\big(\frac{r}{2}\big)\int_{0}^{r/2}s^{1+\sigma}ds=\frac{r^{1+\sigma}}{(2+\sigma)2^{2+\sigma}}\overline{u}\big(\frac{r}{2}\big)

for any

. Integrating both sides over
[r,2r]
with
r>0
to obtain

\displaystyle\overline{u}(r)\geq\overline{u}(r)-\overline{u}(2r)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(2+\sigma)2^{2+\sigma}}\int_{r}^{2r}\overline{u}\big(\frac{s}{2}\big)s^{1+\sigma}ds

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2^{2+\sigma}-1}{(2+\sigma)^{2}2^{2+\sigma}}r^{2+\sigma}\overline{u}(r)

for any
r>0
. From this we obtain contradiction by sending
r\nearrow+\infty
, thanks to the positivity of
\overline{u}
and
\sigma>-2
.

3.3.2.
The case
0<p<1

For arbitrary
\delta\in(0,1)
we consider the auxiliary function

. Clearly,
u^{\delta}
solves

-\Delta(u^{\delta})=\delta(1-\delta)u^{\delta-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\delta|x|^{\sigma}u^{p-(1-\delta)}

and following the argument in
[

]
we conclude that

r\mapsto\overline{u^{\delta}}(r)

is monotone decreasing in
(0,+\infty)
. Now by taking spherical averages, there holds

-\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{r}\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\prime}=\delta(1-\delta)\overline{u^{\delta-2}|\nabla u|^{2}}+\delta r^{\sigma}\overline{u^{p-(1-\delta)}}

for all
r>0
. By Hölder’s inequality, one has

\overline{u^{\delta p}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\overline{u^{\delta}}^{1-\frac{1}{\delta}}\leq\overline{u^{p-(1-\delta)}},

which yields

-\frac{1}{\delta}\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}-1}\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{r}\big(\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\big)^{\prime}\geq(1-\delta)\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}-1}\overline{u^{\delta-2}|\nabla u|^{2}}+r^{\sigma}\overline{u^{\delta p}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}

for all
r>0
. By Cauchy–Schwarz’s inequality, we further obtain

-\big(\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\big)^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{r}\big(\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\big)^{\prime}\geq r^{\sigma}\overline{u^{\delta p}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}

for all
r>0
. Hence, by denoting

U=\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}},\quad\phi=\overline{u^{\delta p}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}},

we arrive at

-U^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{1}{r}U^{\prime}(r)\geq r^{\sigma}\phi(r)

for all
r>0
. Observe the following inequalities

U^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{\delta}\overline{u^{\delta}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}-1}\big(\overline{u^{\delta}}\big)^{\prime}\leq 0,\quad\phi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{\delta}\overline{u^{\delta p}}^{1-\frac{1}{\delta}}\big(\overline{u^{\delta p}}\big)^{\prime}\leq 0.

Hence, integrating over
[\varepsilon,r]
gives

\displaystyle-U^{\prime}(r)\geq r^{-1}\int_{\varepsilon}^{r}s^{1+\sigma}\phi(s)ds\geq r^{-1}\phi\big(\frac{r}{2}\big)\int_{\varepsilon}^{r/2}s^{1+\sigma}ds.

As in the case
p=1
, if
\sigma\leq-2
, then we immediately obtain contradiction by letting
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Now we consider the remaining case
\sigma\in(-2,0)
. In this case, we can let
\varepsilon\searrow 0
to get

\displaystyle-U^{\prime}(r)\geq r^{-1}\phi\big(\frac{r}{2}\big)\int_{0}^{r/2}s^{1+\sigma}ds=\frac{r^{1+\sigma}}{(2+\sigma)2^{2+\sigma}}\phi\big(\frac{r}{2}\big)

for any
r>0
. Integrating both sides over
[r,2r]
with
r>0
to obtain

\displaystyle U(r)\geq U(r)-U(2r)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(2+\sigma)2^{2+\sigma}}\int_{r}^{2r}s^{1+\sigma}\phi\big(\frac{s}{2}\big)ds

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2^{2+\sigma}-1}{(2+\sigma)^{2}2^{2+\sigma}}r^{2+\sigma}\phi(r)=Cr^{2+\sigma}\phi(r)

for any
r>0
. Hence, we have just shown that

\displaystyle\overline{u^{\delta}}(r)\geq C^{\delta}r^{(2+\sigma)\delta}\overline{u^{\delta p}}(r)

for any
r>0
. As
\overline{u}(r)\leq u(0)
and by Hölder’s inequality, we easily get

\overline{u^{\delta}}(r)\leq\big(\overline{u}(r)\big)^{1-\frac{1-\delta}{1-\delta p}}\big(\overline{u^{\delta p}}(r)\big)^{\frac{1-\delta}{1-\delta p}}\leq u(0)^{1-\frac{1-\delta}{1-\delta p}}\big(\overline{u^{\delta p}}(r)\big)^{\frac{1-\delta}{1-\delta p}}.

This helps us to estimate

\overline{u^{\delta}}(r)\leq u(0)C^{\delta-1}r^{-\frac{1-\delta}{1-p}(2+\sigma)}

(3.5)

for any
r>0
. We assume for a moment that
u
enjoys the following simple comparison

u(x)\geq\frac{1}{|x|}

(3.6)

for all
|x|\geq 1
. Having this in hand, we easily conclude that

\overline{u^{\delta}}(r)\geq r^{-\delta}

for all
r\geq 1
. Thus, to fulfill (
3.5
), we must have

\delta\geq\frac{1-\delta}{1-p}(2+\sigma).

But this is impossible if we let
\delta\in(0,1)
sufficiently small. Finally, we prove (
3.6
). Let

v(x)=\frac{c}{|x|}

in
\R^{2}\setminus\{0\}
with
c=\min_{|x|=1}u(x)>0
. This choice of
v
guarantees

u-v\geq 0

on
\{x:|x|=1\}
. In
\R^{2}
we know that

\Delta v=\frac{1}{|x|^{3}}>0

everywhere in
\R^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. As
u\geq 0
, we conclude that

\liminf_{|x|\nearrow+\infty}(u-v)(x)\geq 0

and

\Delta(u-v)\leq 0

everywhere in the region
\{x:|x|>1\}
. Hence by the maximum principle, there holds

u-v\geq 0

everywhere in
\{x:|x|>1\}
, and this is exactly (
3.6
).

Remark 3.3
.

The estimate (
3.6
) is valid for any super-harmonic functions in
\R^{2}
. In fact, one can replace
|x|^{-1}
by any
|x|^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha>0
because
\Delta(|x|^{-\alpha})=\alpha^{2}|x|^{-\alpha-2}
.

In this section, we prove Theorem

We consider the two cases

4.1.
Proof of Theorem

: the case

By seeing Table

, we need to consider the two cases: either
\sigma\leq-2
or
\sigma\geq-1

4.1.1.
The case
\sigma\geq-1
and
-1-\sigma\leq p\leq 0

Clearly

. We treat the two cases
p+\sigma+1>0
and
p+\sigma+1=0

Case 1
. Suppose

. Recall from Lemma
2.3
the estimate
u(r)\leq Cr
for all

. This, the non-positivity of

, and the equation imply that

-u^{\prime\prime}(r)=|r|^{\sigma}u(r)^{p}\geq Cr^{p+\sigma}

holds for all

. Keep in mind that

in
(0,+\infty)
, which plays an important role. By integrating both sides of the preceding inequality over

u^{\prime}(r_{1})\geq u^{\prime}(r_{1})-u^{\prime}(r)\geq C\int_{r_{1}}^{r}s^{p+\sigma}ds=\frac{C}{p+\sigma+1}\big[r^{p+\sigma+1}-r_{1}^{p+\sigma+1}\big]

(4.1)

for all

. From this we obtain contradiction by letting

Case 2

u^{\prime}(r_{1})\geq u^{\prime}(r_{1})-u^{\prime}(r)\geq C\int_{r_{1}}^{r}s^{-1}ds=C\big[\log r-\log(r_{1})\big].

From this we obtain contradiction by letting

as the term
\log r
is unbounded.

Remark 4.1
.

Obviously, the above argument does not work if

and

is implicitly used to guarantee the existence of

4.1.2.
The case
\sigma\leq-2
and
p\leq 0

Recall that
u
is increasing in
(0,+\infty)

is decreasing in

u^{\prime}(r)\geq u^{\prime}(r)-u^{\prime}(1)=-\int_{r}^{1}u^{\prime\prime}(s)ds=\int_{r}^{1}s^{\sigma}u(s)^{p}ds\geq u(1)^{p}\int_{r}^{1}s^{\sigma}ds

for all

u^{\prime}(r)\geq\frac{u(1)^{p}}{1+\sigma}\big(1-r^{1+\sigma}\big)

for all

gives

\displaystyle u(1)-u(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{r}^{1}u^{\prime}(s)ds\geq\frac{u(1)^{p}}{1+\sigma}\int_{r}^{1}(1-s^{1+\sigma})ds.

Depending on the size of
\sigma
, we have two possible cases.

Case 1
. Suppose
\sigma<-2
. In this can we compute the integral

to get

\int_{r}^{1}(1-s^{1+\sigma})ds=\frac{1}{2+\sigma}r^{2+\sigma}-r+\frac{1+\sigma}{2+\sigma},

which then implies

\displaystyle-u(1)

\displaystyle\leq u(r)-u(1)\leq-\frac{u(1)^{p}}{(1+\sigma)(2+\sigma)}r^{2+\sigma}+\frac{u(1)^{p}}{1+\sigma}r-\frac{u(1)^{p}}{2+\sigma}

Letting

r^{2+\sigma}\nearrow+\infty
, thanks to
\sigma<-2
.

Case 2
. Suppose
\sigma=-2
. In this case we notice that

\int_{r}^{1}(1-s^{-1})ds=\log r-r+1,

(4.3)

which then implies

\displaystyle u(1)

\displaystyle\geq u(1)-u(r)\geq u(1)^{p}\big(-\log r+r-1\big).

Letting

we obtain contradiction because
\log r\searrow-\infty
.

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3
: the case

Seeing Table

4.2.1.
The case

4.2.3.

Remark 4.4

5.
Non uniqueness of a positive solution when
n=1

In this section, we are interested in the uniqueness issue of a non-negative, non-trivial solution to the one-dimensional equation

u^{\prime\prime}(x)+x^{\sigma}u^{p}(x)=0\quad\text{for}\ x>0.

(5.1)

Thanks to Lemma
2.4
, any non-negative solution to (
5.1
) is indeed strictly positive. Hence, from now on, by a solution to (
5.1
) we mean a strictly positive
C^{2}
-function in
(0,+\infty)
. Seeing Theorem
1.3
, we shall discuss the following two cases

(A)

\sigma>-2
and
p<-1-\sigma
;

(B)

\sigma<-2
and
p>-1-\sigma
.

As already observed in the Introduction, for
p\neq 1
and
\sigma\neq-2
, there is a solution
u_{a}
to (
5.1
) of the form

u_{a}(x)=c_{a}x^{a}

with

\displaystyle a=\frac{\sigma+2}{1-p}\quad c_{a}

\displaystyle:=\left(a(1-a)\right)^{1/(p-1)}.

The two conditions
(A)
and
(B)
are nothing but to guarantee
a\in(0,1)
. Apparently, the solution
u_{a}
is continuous up to
x=0
. Hence, a natural question is that
given
a\in(0,1)
, is there any solution
u
to (
5.1
) in
(0,+\infty)
rather than
u_{a}
, which is continuous up to
x=0
?

By a simple phase plane analysis, see section
5.5.6
, it turns out that the equation (
5.1
) admits other solutions rather than
u_{a}
. More precisely, for the case
(A)
, our non-uniqueness result reads as follows.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume
(A)
.
There exists a one-parameter family of positive, classical solutions to (
5.1
), denoted by
\{u^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha>0}
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{u^{\alpha}(x)}{u_{a}(x)}=1

and if
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>0
, then

\displaystyle u^{\alpha_{1}}(x)>u^{\alpha_{2}}(x)>u_{a}(x)\quad\text{for any}\ x>0.

We prove Theorem
5.1
in section
5.3
below. The role of the parameter
\alpha
is described by (
5.12
) below. It turns out that all solutions to the equation is above
u_{a}
; see section
5.5.5
. For the case
(B)
, our non-uniqueness result reads as follows.

Theorem 5.2
.

Assume
(B)
. We have the following claims:

(1)

If
a>1/2
, then there is a positive solution
u
of (
5.1
) satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{u(x)}{u_{a}(x)}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{x\nearrow+\infty}\frac{u(x)}{u_{a}(x)}=1.

(2)

If
a=1/2
, then there is a positive solution
u
of (
5.1
) satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{u(x)}{u_{a}(x)}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{x\nearrow+\infty}\frac{u(x)}{u_{a}(x)}=0.

(3)

If
a<1/2
, then there is a positive solution
u
of (
5.1
) satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{u(x)}{u_{a}(x)}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{x\nearrow+\infty}\frac{u(x)}{u_{a}(x)}=0.

In addition to the existence, the solution is unique up to scaling transformation
u\mapsto u_{\lambda}
with
u_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{-a}u(\lambda x)
.

We prove Theorem
5.2
in section
5.4
below. Putting the two theorems above together, we deduce that for both cases
(A)
and
(B)
, uniqueness for positive solutions to (
5.1

5.1.
Equivalent equations

Let
u
be a positive solution of (
5.1
). We set

u=u_{a}v.

Then it is easy to verify that
v
is positive and is of class
C^{2}
. Moreover,
v
solves

v^{\prime\prime}+2ax^{-1}v^{\prime}+c^{p-1}_{a}x^{-2}v(v^{p-1}-1)=0\quad\text{for}\ x>0.

A more convenient form of the above equation is

x^{2}v^{\prime\prime}+2axv^{\prime}+c^{p-1}_{a}v(v^{p-1}-1)=0\quad\text{for}\ x>0

(5.2)

or

x(xv^{\prime})^{\prime}+(2a-1)xv^{\prime}+c^{p-1}_{a}v(v^{p-1}-1)=0\quad\text{for}\ x>0.

(5.3)

It is well-known that (
5.3
) can be written in the form of first order system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &xy^{\prime}_{1}=y_{2},\\
&xy^{\prime}_{2}=-(2a-1)y_{2}-c^{p-1}_{a}y_{1}(y^{p-1}_{1}-1)\end{aligned}\right.

(5.4)

for
x>0
by setting
y_{1}=v
and
y_{2}=xv^{\prime}
. The system (
5.4
) is often called a Briot–Bouquet system and it is well studied especially when the nonlinear term is analytic; see e.g.
[
GT96
]
. We next change the independent variable
x
to
z=\log x
. Since
xd/dx=d/dz
, the equation (
5.3
) becomes

\ddot{V}+(2a-1)\dot{V}+c^{p-1}_{a}V(V^{p-1}-1)=0\quad\text{for}\ z\in\R

(5.5)

for
V(z)=v(e^{z})
, where
\dot{V}=dV/dz
and
\ddot{V}=d^{2}V/dz^{2}
. This is now an autonomous differential equation. The behavior of
v
near
x=0
for (
5.3
) corresponds to that of
V
near
z=-\infty
.

5.2.
A local solution to a Briot–Bouquet system

We consider a Briot–Bouquet system

x\frac{dy}{dx}=Ay+f(y)\quad\text{for}\ x>0.

(5.6)

Here
A
is a real
m\times m
matrix and
f
is an
\R^{m}
-valued
C^{1}
-function in a neighborhood of zero of
\R^{m}
satisfying

\lim_{y\to 0}\frac{f(y)}{y}=0;

the unknown function
y
is
\R^{m}
-valued. We start with the existence of a local solution to (
5.6
) with prescribed asymptotic behavior near zero.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\gamma
be a positive eigenvalue of
A
and
y_{0}
be its eigenvector.
Then, there exists a (unique) local solution
y\in C^{1}(0,T)
to (
5.6
) for some
T>0
such that

\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{y(x)}{x^{\gamma}}=y_{0}.

Proof.

Since
y^{\prime}=\gamma x^{\gamma-1}\varphi+x^{\gamma}\varphi^{\prime}
for
y=x^{\gamma}\varphi
, the equation (
5.6
) is equivalent to the equation of
\varphi
of the form

x\varphi^{\prime}=(A-\gamma)\varphi+x^{-\gamma}f(x^{\gamma}\varphi)\quad\text{for}\ x>0.

(5.7)

It suffices to find a solution
\varphi\in C[0,T)\cap C^{1}(0,T)
for (
5.7
) for some
T>0
such that
\varphi(0)=y_{0}
. As
Ay_{0}=\gamma y_{0}
, we see that
\psi=\varphi-y_{0}
solves

x\psi^{\prime}=(A-\gamma)\psi+x^{-\gamma}f\left(x^{\gamma}(y_{0}+\psi)\right)

or

(x\psi)^{\prime}=F(x,\psi)

with

F(x,\psi)=(A-\gamma+1)\psi+x^{-\gamma}f\left(x^{\gamma}(y_{0}+\psi)\right).

Our problem (
5.7
) with
\varphi(0)=y_{0}
is
equivalent to the following integral equation

\psi(x)=\frac{1}{x}\int^{x}_{0}F\left(s,\psi(s)\right)ds\quad\text{with}\ \psi(0)=0,

(5.8)

where
\psi\in C^{1}(0,T)\cap C^{0}[0,T)
for small
T>0
. Since
\gamma>0
and
f(y)/y\to 0
as
y\to 0
, setting
F(0,0)=0
yields that
F\in C^{1}\left([0,\delta)\times(-\delta,\delta)^{m}\right)
for small
\delta>0
provided that
y_{0}
is taken sufficiently small. Since
f(y)/y\to 0
as
y\to 0
, we observe that
(\partial F/\partial\psi)(0,0)=0
. We now apply Lemma
5.2
below to conclude that there is a unique solution to (
5.8
) for small
T>0
. The proof is now complete.
∎

Lemma 5.2

(see
[
GG98
]
)
.

For
F\in C^{1}\left([0,\delta)\times(-\delta,\delta)^{m}\right)
, let
w_{0}\in\R^{m}
satisfy

F(0,w_{0})=w_{0},\quad\frac{\partial F}{\partial w}(0,w_{0})=0.

Then, there exists a unique function
w\in C[0,T)\cap C^{1}(0,T)
for small
T>0
, which solves

w(x)=\frac{1}{x}\int^{x}_{0}F\left(s,w(s)\right)ds,\quad w(0)=w_{0}\quad x\in(0,T).

This can be proved by observing that

w\mapsto(1/x)\int^{x}_{0}F\left(s,w(s)\right)ds

is a contraction mapping in
X=\left\{w\in C[0,T]\mid w(0)=w_{0}\right\}
if
T
is sufficiently small; see
[
GG98
, Lemma 8]
.

As an application of Lemma
5.1
, we obtain the following result which allows us to construct local solutions to (
5.3
).

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
\mu_{+}
be a positive root of

\mu^{2}+b\mu+c=0.

(5.9)

Let
f_{0}
be a
C^{1}
function near
0
such that
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
. Given any
w_{0}\in\R
, there exist some
T>0
and a unique local solution
w\in C[0,T]\cap C^{2}(0,T)
to

x(xw^{\prime})^{\prime}+bxw^{\prime}+cw+f_{0}(w)=0\quad\text{for}\ x\in(0,T)

such that

\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{w(x)}{x^{\mu_{+}}}=w_{0}.

(5.10)

In addition,
w
is non-decreasing near
x=0
if
w_{0}>0
and is non-increasing near
x=0
if
w_{0}<0
.

Proof.

We set
y_{1}=w
,
y_{2}=xw^{\prime}
to get an equation for
y=(y_{1},y_{2})
of the form

x\frac{dy}{dx}=Ay+f(y)

with

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-c&-b\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad f(y)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-f_{0}(y_{1})\\
\end{pmatrix}.

The eigen equation for
A
is nothing but (
5.9
).
Since
y_{0}=(w_{0},\mu_{+}w_{0})^{T}
is an eigenvector for the eigenvalue
\mu_{+}
, applying Lemma
5.1
yields the desired solution
w
satisfying (
5.10
) and

\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{w^{\prime}(x)}{x^{\mu_{+}-1}}=\mu_{+}w_{0}.

The above limit implies the monotonicity of
w
near
x=0
as stated.
∎

In the next two sections, we prove Theorem
5.1
and Theorem
5.2
.

5.3.
The case
(A)
: proof of Theorem
5.1

Let
\alpha\geq 0
be arbitrary but fixed. We note that
v\equiv 1
is a stationary solution to (
5.3
).
We then set

to obtain

x(xw^{\prime})^{\prime}+(2a-1)xw^{\prime}+c^{p-1}_{a}(p-1)w+f_{0}(w)=0,

(5.11)

where

f_{0}(w)=a(1-a)\big[(1+w)^{p}-1-pw\big].

Obviously,
f_{0}
is smooth near

and
f_{0}(w)/w\to 0
as

. Our aim is to show that (
5.11
) admits a solution. This is done through several steps.

First we show that (
5.11
) admits a local solution near
x=0
. By
(A)
, we observe that
p-1<0
. Thus, the equation (
5.9
) with

and

has always two real roots
\mu_{+}
and
\mu_{-}
satisfying
\mu_{+}>0>\mu_{-}
. Then for the rate
w_{0}:=\alpha

, there exists a local solution
w
to (

5.10
). We set

\eta^{\alpha}(x)=\left\{\ \begin{aligned} &x^{-\mu_{+}}w(x)&&\text{for }x>0,\\
&\alpha&&\text{for }x=0.\end{aligned}\right.

By (
5.10
), the function
\eta
is continuous up to
x=0
. Our desired solution
u^{\alpha}
to (
5.1
) is defined as follows

u^{\alpha}(x)=u_{a}(x)\left(1+\eta^{\alpha}(x)x^{\mu_{+}}\right)

(5.12)

at least near

. By definition,
u^{0}\equiv u_{a}
and
u^{0}\not\equiv u_{a}
if

.

We next observe that the class

enjoys the order preserving property in the following sense

u^{\alpha_{1}}(x)>u^{\alpha_{2}}(x)\quad\text{for}\ x\in(0,T_{*}]\ \text{ if}\ \alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}\geq 0

(5.13)

as far as
u^{\alpha_{1}}
and
u^{\alpha_{2}}
exist on

. Admitting this fact and as

, we conclude that
u^{\alpha_{2}}>u_{a}
as far as

exists so it can be extended to global positive solution; in addition, no blow up occurs since

is always concave.

It remains to prove the order preserving property (

by the form (

) of

.
Suppose (
5.13
) were false.
Then, there would exist
x_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle u^{\alpha_{1}}(x_{0})=u^{\alpha_{2}}(x_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad u^{\alpha_{1}}(x)>u^{\alpha_{2}}(x)\quad\text{for}\ x\in(0,x_{0}).

We consider equation (
5.2
) for

, namely

x^{2}(v^{\alpha})^{\prime\prime}+2ax(v^{\alpha})^{\prime}+c^{p-1}_{a}\big((v^{\alpha})^{p}-v^{\alpha}\big)=0.

Subtracting equation for
v^{\alpha_{2}}
from that for
v^{\alpha_{1}}

solves

x^{2}h^{\prime\prime}+2axh^{\prime}+c^{p-1}_{a}\beta(x)h=0

\beta(x)=\int^{1}_{0}g^{\prime}\left(\theta v^{\alpha_{2}}(x)+(1-\theta)v^{\alpha_{1}}(x)\right)d\theta\quad\text{and}\quad g(v)=v^{p}-v.

As
v^{\alpha_{1}},v^{\alpha_{2}}\geq 1

\theta v^{\alpha_{2}}+(1-\theta)v^{\alpha_{1}}\geq 1
for any

. Since

, we see that

g^{\prime}(v)=pv^{p-1}-1<0\quad\text{for}\ v>1

so that

for

. Since
h(0)=h(x_{0})=0
and

for

, there must be a positive maximum at some

. By the equation for

Thus, the proof of Theorem

Remark 5.1
.

Part of the order preserving property (
5.13
) indicates that the specific solution
u_{a}
serves as a lower bound for the one-parameter family
\{u^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\geq 0}
. Remarkably, in Theorem
5.4
below, we show that this is in fact true for all solutions under the case

. However, this is no longer true in the case

as Theorem

shows.

5.4.
The case
(B)
: proof of Theorem
5.2

Recall the decomposition
u=u_{a}v
and
V(z)=v(e^{z})
. We also recall from (
5.5
) that
V
solves the equation

\ddot{V}+(2a-1)\dot{V}+c^{p-1}_{a}V(V^{p-1}-V)=0

with
c^{p-1}_{a}=a(1-a)
. The above equation is a special example of Lienard system and there is a rich literature; see e.g.
[
Per01
, Section 3.8]
. Our aim is still to prove that the preceding equation, namely (
5.5
), admit a global solution
V
. Clearly, one can rewrite (
5.5
) as

\frac{d}{dz}\begin{pmatrix}V\\
\dot{V}\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}\dot{V}\\
(2a-1)\dot{V}+c^{p-1}_{a}(V^{p}-V)\end{pmatrix}.

From this one can easily check that there are two equilibria
(0,0)
and
(1,0)
. The linerization of the preceding system is as follows

\frac{d}{dz}\begin{pmatrix}V\\
\dot{V}\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
c^{p-1}_{a}(pV^{p-1}-1)&2a-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}V\\
\dot{V}\end{pmatrix}.

Keep in mind that
c^{p-1}_{a}=a(1-a)
. Hence, the solution behaves like

\ddot{V}+(2a-1)\dot{V}+a(1-a)(p-1)V=0

near
V=1
and like

\ddot{V}+(2a-1)\dot{V}-a(1-a)V=0

near
V=0
. Since the case
a\leq 1/2
can be obtained by transforming
z\mapsto-z
, we may assume that
a\geq 1/2
. In the sequel, we consider the two cases
a>1/2
and
a=1/2
separately.

5.4.1.
The case
a>1/2

In this case, it suffices to construct a heteroclinic orbit satisfying
V\geq 0
. Because the linearized equation around
V=0
is of the form

\ddot{V}+(2a-1)\dot{V}-a(1-a)V=0,

it is easy to verify that its characteristic roots are
-a
and
1-a
with
1-a>0
. By local existence result established in section
5.2
, there is a solution to

x(xv^{\prime})^{\prime}+(2a-1)xv^{\prime}+c^{p-1}_{a}v(v^{p-1})=0

for
x>0
with the following asymptotic behavior

\lim_{x\searrow 0}\frac{v(x)}{x^{1-a}}=v_{0}

for any given
v_{0}>0
. This guarantees that there is a solution to (
5.5
) for
z<z_{0}
with some
z_{0}\in\R
such that

\lim_{z\searrow-\infty}\frac{V(z)}{e^{(1-a)z}}=v_{0}.

In particular,
V\searrow 0
as
z\searrow-\infty
. Note that
V
is uniquely determined by
v_{0}>0
. (The local existence result to (
5.1
) in section
5.2
corresponds to the existence of a local unstable manifold
[
Per01
]
for (
5.5
).) To be a heteroclinic orbit, it remains to prove that this
V
can be extended globally and tends to
1
as
z\nearrow+\infty
. Let
F
be a primitive of
c^{p-1}_{a}v(v^{p-1}-1)
, i.e.

F(w)=\int^{w}_{1}c^{p-1}_{a}v(v^{p-1}-1)dv=\frac{c^{p-1}_{a}}{2(p+1)}\big(2w^{p+1}-(p+1)w^{2}+p-1\big).

For
w\geq 0
, this
F
has the global minimum
0
at
w=1
. In particular,
F\geq 0
. Now multiply
\dot{V}
with (
5.5
) to get

\frac{d}{dz}E(V,\dot{V})=-(2a-1)\dot{V}^{2}

with the total energy

E(V,\dot{V})=\frac{1}{2}|\dot{V}|^{2}+F(V).

For simplicity, we denote
Y=(V,\dot{V})
. Obviously,
E(Y)\geq 0
and equality occurs at
Y=(1,0)
. Since
a>1/2
, the energy
E
must decrease along
Y
. Note that as
\partial E/\partial V
does not depend on
\dot{V}
each level curve of
E
is transversal to
V
-axis. Consequently,
E(Y)
is strictly decreasing along
Y
. Thus the solution
Y
must converge to the minimizer of

, namely

Y(z)\to(1,0)\quad\text{as }z\nearrow+\infty.

Moreover,
V>0
since
E(Y)\leq E(0,0)
. We have thus constructed a positive solution
V
of (
5.5
) such that

\lim_{z\searrow-\infty}V(z)=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{z\nearrow+\infty}V(z)=1.

This gives a heteroclinic orbit for (
5.5
) as claimed. Transforming to
u
gives the desired solution to (
5.1
).

Finally, the uniqueness (up to translation of
z
) follows from the uniqueness of the orbit near
Y=(0,0)
.

5.4.2.
The case
a=1/2

In this case, as
dE/dz=0
the energy is constant along an orbit and the equation is reduced to a first order equation

\frac{1}{2}\dot{V}^{2}+F(V)=E_{0}

where

is a constant. Taking

to get desired homoclinic orbit; see the bold curve in Figure

. In other words, there is a global (positive) solution

of (

) such that

The uniqueness (up to translation) follows from the uniqueness of the orbit. Since the translation in

, the desired uniqueness follows.

Remark 5.2
.

For
a=1/2
, one can make use of shooting method to obtain the same conclusion. To be more precise, one can prove that there exists a decreasing solution
V
of (
5.5
), which satisfies

\dot{V}(0)=0
and

for

and

. This implies that there is a positive global solution

For interested readers, we refer to
[
Gig86
]
for similar arguments. It is worth noting that this type of method is useful if the system is not autonomous. Apparently, in our current autonomous problem, the phase plane approach is easier.

Remark 5.3
.

In the above proof, one takes

. The argument is based on a version of Sturm’s comparison principle for oscillation, which will be mentioned below, which is similar to that of
[

]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose that
u
and
v
solve

\displaystyle(\sigma u^{\prime})^{\prime}+\sigma q_{1}u\geq 0\geq(\sigma v^{\prime})^{\prime}+\sigma q_{2}v

on
(a,b)
, where
\sigma>0
and
q_{1},q_{2}
are in
C[a,b]
. Suppose that

u>0\quad\text{on}\;(a,b)\quad\text{and}\quad u(b)=0.

Assume that

v(a)u^{\prime}(a)-u(a)v^{\prime}(a)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad v(a)\geq 0

and

\text{either}\quad v(a)>0\quad\text{or}\quad v^{\prime}(a)>0.

If
q_{1}\leq q_{2}
on
(a,b),
then
v\;\text{ has zero in }\;(a,b)
unless
q_{1}\equiv q_{2}
and
u
and
v
are solutions to
(\sigma w^{\prime})^{\prime}+\sigma q_{1}w=0
in
(a,b)
.

Compared with the version in
[
Gig86
]
, our set of hypotheses in Lemma
5.3
is slightly different. However, one can mimic the argument used in
[
Gig86
]
to prove the above lemma without difficulty. For completeness, we sketch its proof. For simplicity, we denote

(\sigma u^{\prime})^{\prime}+\sigma q_{1}u=f_{1}\geq 0\geq-f_{2}=(\sigma v^{\prime})^{\prime}+\sigma q_{2}v.

If
v
has no zero on
(a,b)
, then from the hypotheses on
v
we deduce that
v>0
on
(a,b)
. A simple calculation shows

vf_{1}+uf_{2}=v(\sigma u^{\prime})^{\prime}-u(\sigma v^{\prime})^{\prime}+\sigma(q_{1}-q_{2})uv.

Integrating by parts the above identity over
(a,b)
yields

-\int_{a}^{b}(vf_{1}+uf_{2})+\big[\sigma(vu^{\prime}-uv^{\prime})\big]\Big|_{a}^{b}+\int_{a}^{b}\sigma(q_{1}-q_{2})uv=0

Since
u^{\prime}(b)\leq 0
, the boundary conditions yields

\big[\sigma(vu^{\prime}-uv^{\prime})\big]\Big|^{b}_{a}=v(b)u^{\prime}(b)-u(b)v^{\prime}(b)-\big[v(a)u^{\prime}(a)-u(a)v^{\prime}(a)\big]\leq 0.

This leads a contradiction since the other two terms are strictly negative unless
q_{1}=q_{2}
and
f_{1}=f_{2}=0
. This completes the proof.

As an application of Lemma
5.3
, we obtain a non-existence for a positive solution strictly below

under the condition
(A)
. For convenience, we state this as a theorem.

.
In particular,

Proof.

The first statement is equivalent to saying that there is no global solution

. By changing the independent variable
