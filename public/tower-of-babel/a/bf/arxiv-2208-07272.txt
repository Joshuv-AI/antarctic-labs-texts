Training-Time Attacks against k k -Nearest Neighbors
Ara Vartanian, Will Rosenbaum, Scott Alfeld

Abstract
Nearest neighbor-based methods are commonly used for classification tasks and as subroutines of other data-analysis methods. An attacker with the capability of inserting their own data points into the training set can manipulate the inferred nearest neighbor structure. We distill this goal to the task of performing a training-set data insertion attack against k k -Nearest Neighbor classification ( k k NN). We prove that computing an optimal training-time (a.k.a. poisoning) attack against k k NN classification is NP-Hard, even when k = 1 k=1 and the attacker can insert only a single data point. We provide an anytime algorithm to perform such an attack, and a greedy algorithm for general k k and attacker budget. We provide theoretical bounds and empirically demonstrate the effectiveness and practicality of our methods on synthetic and real-world datasets. Empirically, we find that k k NN is vulnerable in practice and that dimensionality reduction is an effective defense. We conclude with a discussion of open problems illuminated by our analysis.



1
Introduction

Using machine learning (ML) and automated data analysis methods in practice introduces security vulnerabilities.
An attacker can assert their limited influence over the input data to manipulate the output of the learning system.

Attacks can be categorized based on when in the process the attacker asserts their influence.
In training-time (a.k.a. poisoning) attacks, the attacker perturbs data prior to learning.
In deployment-time (a.k.a. evasion) attacks, the attacker instead perturbs test instances.
Here, we consider training-time attacks against nearest-neighbor based classification.

k
-Nearest Neighbors (
k
NN) is a classical non-parametric ML algorithm,
where
a data point label is chosen by selecting the plurality class of its
k

closest neighbors in the training set.
Outside of classification, many algorithms construct a nearest neighbor graph (connecting each data point to its closest neighbors) as a subroutine.
Examples include ISOMAP (
Tenenbaum et al., 2000
), persistent homology (
Zhu, 2013
;
Zhu et al., 2016
), and various spectral clustering algorithms (
Luxburg, 2007
).

We study neighbor-based classification as the canonical process of constructing nearest-neighbor graphs.
An attacker which can affect classifications can similarly alter the nearest-neighbor graph.

For example, consider a facial-recognition system which, given a photograph, reports the
k
most similar photographs in the database.
An agent might insert manufactured photographs into the facial-recognition system’s database with the intent of having images of her classified as someone else.
We lay the foundation for a theoretical understanding of training-time attacks against
k
NN.
The primary contributions are:

1.

We prove that computing an optimal training-time attack against
k
-Nearest Neighbor classification is NP-Hard.

2.

We present a dynamic programming anytime algorithm that, if run to completion, computes an optimal single-point attack against
k
NN. Using this procedure as a subroutine, we employ and provide bounds for a greedy approach to efficiently construct an effective attack of any size against
k
NN.

3.

We perform an empirical investigation on a method of defense against our attack.
For example, reducing the number of dimensions via PCA in the MNIST dataset decreased the attacker’s average effectiveness by as much as 26% while increasing prediction error by less than 1%.

In Section
2
, after defining the learner and threat models for attacking
k
NN, we translate the attacker’s task into a geometric problem.
In Section
3
, we prove that computing the optimal attack is NP-Hard, even for an attacker who targets one class with one additional data point. In Section
4
, we present a (worst-case exponential-time) algorithm for finding the optimal single-point attack against
k
NN that uses dynamic programming to find successively better attacks as it proceeds, allowing it to be used as an anytime algorithm.
We then employ a greedy algorithm to construct a data-insertion attack of any size against
k
NN.
We show a bound for our greedy algorithm which is dependent on
k
(but independent of the attacker’s budget).
We discuss the “plug-and-play” nature of our analysis, in that any future improvements to the single-point attacks algorithm automatically yield a better bound for our general attack.
In Section
5
, we empirically demonstrate the effectiveness and practicality of our attack on synthetic and real world data.
We then turn our attention to defense, and empirically investigate defense strategies based on dimensionality reduction.
We discuss related work in Section
6
and conclude in Section
7
with a discussion of open problems.

For

More specifically,

6
Related Work

Since its introduction (
Fix and Hodges, 1989
),
k
NN and related algorithms (e.g.,
r
NN) have been extensively studied in contexts where no adversary is at play (
Shakhnarovich et al., 2006
;
Bhatia et al., 2010
).

Training-time (a.k.a. poisoning or input manipulation) attacks were developed against various machine learning algorithms from deep networks (
Koh and Liang, 2017
) to support vector machines (
Biggio et al., 2012
) to linear regression models (
Jagielski et al., 2018
;
Alfeld et al., 2019
) to collaborative filtering (
Li et al., 2016
;
Chen et al., 2020
) and online centroid anomaly detection (
Kloft and Laskov, 2010
).
We complement this by furthering knowledge of another canonical learning method.
We believe we are the first to investigate (theoretically or otherwise) training-time attacks against
k
NN.

Deployment-time attacks, often called “adversarial examples” or “adversarial perturbations”, have been given a great deal of attention, especially against neural networks in recent years.
With a focus on graph neural-networks Entezari
et al.
similarly observed that attackers are generally are less effective in low dimensions (
Entezari et al., 2020
).
We direct the reader to
Biggio and Roli, 2018
;
Vorobeychik and Kantarcioglu, 2018
;
Joseph et al., 2018
for a general review and to
Serban et al., 2020
;
Hazan et al., 2017
specifically for neural networks.
Others have developed attacks against and analyzed the robustness of
k
NN and other non-parametric methods
Wang et al., 2018
;
Bhattacharjee and Chaudhuri, 2020
).
In contrast to our work, they model an attacker acting at deployment-time, not training-time.

ATK-KNN
is closely related to the “product positioning problem” in marketing theory
Albers and Brockhoff, 1977
, whose complexity is analyized in
Crama et al., 1995
.
Their analysis gives an alternative proof of the special case of our Theorem
2
when
p=2
via a reduction from the maximum clique problem.
Our proof of Theorem
2
is an adaptation of the argument in
Amaldi and Kann, 1995
, which shows that finding the maximum number of feasible linear constraints is NP-hard.
The advantage of our proof of Theorem
2
is threefold: it is more general in that it covers
\ell_{p}
norms for
p\in(1,\infty)
, it is both technically simpler than the argument of
Crama et al., 1995
and it is conceptually consistent with the remainder of our paper.

Crama et al., 1995
also describes an algorithm for
MAX-INT
that runs in time
O(n^{d+1})
, where
n
and
d
denote the number of points (i.e., size of our training set) and the dimension, respectively.
The high-degree polynomial run-time of Crama et al.’s algorithm, however, is inherent to their approach, as they first test a set of
\Theta(n^{d})
potential single-point attacks before evaluating the scores of these attacks.
As an anytime algorithm,
CHOPPA
finds good (albeit not necessarily optimal) solutions quickly.

7
Conclusions

We investigated the task of performing training-time attacks against nearest-neighbor based classifiers.
We model an attacker adding points to the training set with the goal of affecting the classifications of points in an target pool.
We proved that finding an optimal attack against
k
NN is NP-Hard, even when the attacker can add only a single data point to the training set.
We introduced the anytime algorithm
CHOPPA
to efficiently compute an effective one-point attack and
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
to compute a general attack.
We provided a bound for
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
when
CHOPPA
finds the optimal solution, and this bound holds in general if
CHOPPA
is replaced with a constant-approximation algorithm.

With an eye toward defense strategies, we conducted an empirical investigation of the effects of dimensionality reduction on synthetic and real-world data.
Our experiments demonstrated both that the attack is highly effective in practice and that dimensionality reduction is often an effective defense strategy.

We close with open problems as potential future work:
1. Is there a polynomial-time, constant-factor approximation algorithm which solves the same problem as
CHOPPA
?
Such an algorithm with approximation ratio
\alpha
would yield a
(1-e^{-\alpha})/k^{\prime}
approximation when used as a subroutine by
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
.
2. Is there a bound for
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
’s performance that is independent of
k
?
3. Our defense reduces the dimensionality
d
.
We observe empirically that this reduces the effectiveness of the optimal attack.
However, the worst-case runtime of
CHOPPA
decreases with
d
.
Are there effective defenses which act by increasing the computational burden of the attacker instead of (or in addition to) reducting the effectiveness of the optimal attack?

forms an independent set in

.

Lemma 2
.

Let
G
be a graph on
d
vertices. Suppose
\mathcal{B}=\varphi(G)
and
I\subseteq V
forms an independent set in
G
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{I}=\mathcal{B}\setminus\left\{B_{j}\,\middle|\,j\notin I\right\}
. Then choosing
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{d}
and

, we have
\bigcap_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{I}}B\neq\varnothing
.

Proof.

For
i\in[d]
and
I\subseteq V
an independent set, define the vector
v^{I}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
as follows:

v^{I}_{j}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }i\in I\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(10)

We will show that for a suitable choice of
\varepsilon
and sufficienty small value of
t>0
, a point of the form
\mathbf{x}=tv^{I}
is contained in
\bigcap\mathcal{B}_{I}
. That is, we must show that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{e}_{i}\right\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq 1,and

(11)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\right\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq 2(r-\varepsilon)^{p}=2r-2p\varepsilon-O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(12)

To this end, for each
i\in I
, we first observe that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{e}_{i}\right\|_{p}^{p}=\left|1-x_{i}\right|^{p}+\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}^{p}=1-\frac{1}{2}pt+dt^{p}+O(t^{2}).

From this expression, Taylor’s theorem implies that for all sufficiently small
t>0
, Equation (
11
) is satisfied.

Similarly, for each
ij\in E
with
j\notin I
, we compute

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\right\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=(1-t)^{p}+(1-x_{i})^{p}+\sum_{\ell\neq i,j}x_{\ell}^{p}

\displaystyle=\left|1+x_{i}\right|^{p}+\left|1+x_{j}\right|^{p}+\sum_{\ell\neq i,j}x_{\ell}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\left|1+\frac{1}{2}t\right|^{p}+\left|1-t\right|^{p}+\sum_{\ell\neq i,j}t^{p}

\displaystyle=2-\frac{1}{2}pt+dt^{p}+O(t^{2}).

Setting
\varepsilon=t^{2}
and again applying Taylor’s theorem, we obtain that for sufficiently small
t
, Equation (
12
) is also satisfied. Thus, for sufficiently small

, we have
\mathbf{x}\in\bigcap\mathcal{B}_{I}
, as desired.
∎

Next, we establish the converse of Lemma
2
: if
\mathbf{p}
is contained in many balls

, then

contains a large independent set.

Lemma 3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with
d=\left|V\right|
vertices and
m=\left|V\right|
edges, and let
\mathcal{B}=\varphi(G)
. Suppose there exists a point
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
contained in the intersection of
md+M
balls
B\in\mathcal{B}
for some
M\geq 1
(where each ball of the form
B_{ij}\in\mathcal{B}
is taken with multiplicity

). Then
G
contains an independent set
I\subseteq V
of size

.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{d})
is contained in
md+M
balls
B\in\mathcal{B}
. First, we claim that
\mathbf{p}\in\bigcap_{ij\in E}B_{ij}
. Since each
B_{ij}
appears with multiplicity
d
and
\left|\mathcal{B}\right|=md+d
,
\mathbf{p}\notin B_{ij}
implies that
\mathbf{p}
is contained in at most
\left|\mathcal{B}\right|-d=md
balls, contradicting the hypothesis. Therefore, we have
\mathbf{p}\in\bigcap_{ij\in E}B_{ij}
, as claimed.

Now consider the set
I=\left\{i\,\middle|\,\mathbf{p}\in B_{i}\right\}\subseteq V
. Note that
\left|I\right|=M
by the hypothesis. We claim that
I
is an independent set in
G
. To see this, suppose
i\in I
so that
i\in B_{i}
, and suppose
ij\in E
. Since
i\in B_{i}\cap B_{ij}
(by the previous claim), Lemma
1
implies that
\mathbf{p}\notin B_{j}
. Therefore,
j\notin I
. Thus, for all
i\in I
and

, we have

—i.e.,
I
is an independent set of size
M
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Consider the reduction
\varphi
described above. Clearly, for any graph

,

can be computed in polynomial time. By Lemmas

and

of size

has a maximum intersection of size
\left|V\right|\left|E\right|+M
. Therefore,
\varphi
is a polynomial-time reduction from

is NP-hard.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, and
\mathcal{B}=\varphi(G)
the corresponding

instance described above. We will show that

is realized by an

. Thus, the reduction from

to

Given

. That is,

.

only for

It is straightforward to show that the

Essentially the same argument also yields the NP-hardness of attacking

Proof of Theorem
3

Towards proving Theorem
3
, we prove the following more general result, from which Theorem
3
follows taking
\beta=1
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose all elements
(\mathbf{x},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
have the same label, say
y^{\prime}=y
.
Let
k^{\prime}=\lceil k/2\rceil
, and suppose each call to
CHOPPA
in
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
returns a single-point attack whose score is a
\beta
-fraction of the optimal coverage single point attack (
0\leq\beta\leq 1
). Then the score of the
\mathbf{\Delta}
returned by
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
is a
\frac{1}{k^{\prime}}(1-e^{-\beta})
-fraction of the optimal
b
-point attack’s score. In particular, if each call to
CHOPPA
returns an optimal single point attack (i.e.,
\beta=1
), then
\mathbf{\Delta}
’s score is within
\frac{1}{k^{\prime}}(1-1/e)
of the optimal attack.

Throughout the section, we fix a label
y
that is the target label of all points in
\mathcal{T}
. We first consider the case
k=1
. In this case, the problem of computing an optimal
b
-point attack can be reduced to the
maximum
b
-coverage problem
: given a universal set
U
, a weight function
w:U\to\mathbf{R}
, and a family
\mathcal{S}
of subsets of
U
, find
b
subsets
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{b}\in\mathcal{S}
that maximizes
\sum_{(\mathbf{x},y^{\prime})\in S}w(\mathbf{x})
where
S=S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{b}
.

In our case,
U=\mathcal{T}
, while
\mathcal{S}
is the family of subsets of
U
consisting of sets of elements whose influencing regions mutually intersect:

\textstyle\mathcal{S}=\left\{S\subseteq\mathcal{T}\,\middle|\,\bigcap_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in S}\mathrm{IR}(\mathbf{x})\neq\varnothing\right\}.

For a point
(\mathbf{x},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
and target label
y
, the weight function
w
is determined by

w(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }y=y^{\prime}\text{ and }\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};D]\neq y\\
-1&\text{if }y\neq y^{\prime}\text{ and }\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};D]=y^{\prime}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(13)

Therefore, the optimal single-point attack consists of finding
\arg\max_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S}w(\mathbf{x})
. More generally, the optimal
b
-point attack consists of finding

\arg\max_{S_{1},\ldots,S_{b}\in\mathcal{S}}w(S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{b}),

where for
S\subseteq\mathcal{T}
, we define

w(S)=\sum_{(\mathbf{x},y^{\prime})\in S}w(\mathbf{x}).

That is, in the case where
\left|\mathcal{C}\right|=1
, the optimal
b
-point attack reduces to solving the maximum
b
-coverage problem on
\mathcal{S}
.

Hochbaum and Pathria, 1998
analyze a greedy algorithm for the maximum
b
-coverage problem. Specifically, they show that if
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{b}\in\mathcal{S}
are chosen such that for each
i
,
\left|S_{i}\right|\geq\beta\max_{S}w(S\setminus(S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{i-1}))
, then
S_{1},\ldots,S_{b}
gives a
(1-e^{-\beta})
approximation for the maximum
b
-coverage problem. Since
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
computes
\mathbf{\Delta}
precisely in this greedy manner, the following lemma is an immediate consequence of a of Hochbaum and Pathria’s main result.

Lemma 4

(cf. Theorem 1 in
Hochbaum and Pathria, 1998
)
.

Suppose
k=1
and each call to
CHOPPA
in
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
returns a single-point attack whose score is a
\beta
-fraction of the optimal single point attack (
0\leq\beta\leq 1
). Then the score of the
\mathbf{\Delta}
returned by
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
is a
(1-e^{-\beta})
-fraction of the optimal
b
-point attack’s score.

The lemma establishes Theorem
4
for the case
k=1
. We now establish Theorem
4
for general
k
. The subtlety arises because it may no longer be the case that placing a single attack point
(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathrm{IR}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
changes
\mathcal{A}
’s classification of
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
. However, placing
(\mathbf{x},y)
with multiplicity
k^{\prime}=\lceil k/2\rceil
will always change
\mathcal{A}
’s classification (assuming
\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x}^{\prime};D]\neq y
). We assume that all points in
\mathcal{T}
have the same target label,
y
, so that every possible attack point has a non-negative contribution to
ATKR
’s score. Given an attack
\mathbf{\Delta}
, we define

\displaystyle\texttt{TSI}(\mathbf{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{1}_{(\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}\cup\mathbf{\Delta}]=y)}-\mathbf{1}_{(\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}]=y)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{coverage}(\mathbf{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{IR}(\mathbf{x})\cap\mathbf{\Delta}\neq\varnothing}\cdot w(\mathbf{x}).

Again, by the assumption that all
(\mathbf{x},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
have
y^{\prime}=y
, we have
w(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}
. Similarly, we have

\mathbf{1}_{(\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}\cup\mathbf{\Delta}]=y)}-\mathbf{1}_{(\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}]=y)}\geq 0.

Additionally, we have

\mathbf{1}_{(\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}\cup\mathbf{\Delta}]=y)}-\mathbf{1}_{(\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}]=y)}\leq\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{IR}(\mathbf{x})\cap\mathbf{\Delta}\neq\varnothing}w(\mathbf{x}).

To see this, consider
(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{T}
with
\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}]\neq y^{\prime}
. Then
\mathrm{IR}(\mathbf{x})\cap\mathbf{\Delta}\neq\varnothing
is necessary and sufficient to have
w(\mathbf{x})=1
. This condition is also necessary, though not generally sufficient, to have
\mathbf{1}_{(\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}\cup\mathbf{\Delta}]=y)}-\mathbf{1}_{(\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}]=y)}=1
because adding fewer than
k^{\prime}
points in
\mathrm{IR}(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{D}
may not change
\mathcal{A}[\mathbf{x};\mathcal{D}]
. Thus, in general, we have

\texttt{TSI}(\mathbf{\Delta})\leq\mathrm{coverage}(\mathbf{\Delta})\quad\text{for all}\quad\mathbf{\Delta}.

(14)

We note that for
k=1
, we do have
\texttt{TSI}(\mathbf{\Delta})=\mathrm{coverage}(\mathbf{\Delta})
. More generally, if
\mathbf{\Delta}
has the property that all points in
\mathbf{\Delta}
appear with sufficient multiplicity (that is at most
k^{\prime}
), then we can also guarantee that
\texttt{TSI}(\mathbf{\Delta})=\mathrm{coverage}(\mathbf{\Delta})
. Since
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
always adds points to
\mathbf{\Delta}
with sufficient multiplicity to change classifications, we have the following.

Lemma 5
.

For any attack
\mathbf{\Delta}
returned by
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
, we have

\texttt{TSI}(\mathbf{\Delta})=\mathrm{coverage}(\mathbf{\Delta}).

We also note that the number of
distinct
points in
\mathbf{\Delta}
is at least
b^{\prime}=\lfloor b/k^{\prime}\rfloor
. Since the distinct points are chosen greedily by
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
, we can apply the analysis of
Hochbaum and Pathria, 1998
to obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Let
b^{\prime}=\lfloor b/k^{\prime}\rfloor
, and let
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\prime}
be a maximal coverage
b^{\prime}
-point
coverage
set. That is,

\mathrm{coverage}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\prime})=\max_{\mathbf{\Delta}:\left|\mathbf{\Delta}\right|=b^{\prime}}\mathrm{coverage}(\mathbf{\Delta}).

and let
\mathbf{\Delta}^{*}
be an attack returned by
\texttt{GIT}^{2}\texttt{ACHOPPA}
during an execution in which each call to

returns a point covering a
\beta
-fraction of the optimal coverage single point attack. Then

\mathrm{coverage}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\prime})\leq(1-1/e^{\beta})^{-1}\mathrm{coverage}(\mathbf{\Delta}^{*}).

.

.

Consider an optimal

b^{\prime}

An additional optimization

We offer the following practical optimization to
CHOPPA
(noting that it may, of course, still take exponential time).
We replace line
1
in Algorithm
1
with the following:

\displaystyle\wedge\left(|\mathbf{e}|-1\geq d+1\vee\text{(\ref{eqn:qclp}) has a solution}\right)

(19)

Then, instead of maintaining the point
\mathbf{x}
, we store the hyper-edge
\mathbf{s}
and solve the QCLP only to find the ultimate attack point.
By Helly’s theorem and the fact that hyperspheres are convex, if all subsets of
\mathbf{e}
of size
d+1
are in
E
(have a point of mutual intersection), then
\mathbf{e}
itself is a hyper-edge and there is no need to invoke the QCLP solver.
Note that in checking all subsets of size
|\mathbf{e}|-1>d+1
, we are implicitly checking all subsets of size
d+1
.
Therefore, when
|\mathbf{e}|>d+1
then this condition on both necessary
and sufficient
, thus there is no need to check if (
9
) has a solution.

Note that this optimization is not possible when
CHOPPA
is not run to completion.
While Helly’s theorem guarantees the existence of a region of mutual overlap, it provides no witness (no point within that region).
Such a witness is necessary to compute the
TLR
when terminating before all hyper-edges are constructed (but
TLR
can be computed given a maximal hyper-edge directly).
