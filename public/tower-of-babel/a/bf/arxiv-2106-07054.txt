Inferring the mixing properties of an ergodic process
Azadeh Khaleghi, Gábor Lugosi

Abstract
We propose strongly consistent estimators of the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} norm of the sequence of α \alpha -mixing (respectively β \beta -mixing) coefficients of a stationary ergodic process. We further provide strongly consistent estimators of individual α \alpha -mixing (respectively β \beta -mixing) coefficients for a subclass of stationary α \alpha -mixing (respectively β \beta -mixing) processes with summable sequences of mixing coefficients. The estimators are in turn used to develop strongly consistent goodness-of-fit hypothesis tests. In particular, we develop hypothesis tests to determine whether, under the same summability assumption, the α \alpha -mixing (respectively β \beta -mixing) coefficients of a process are upper bounded by a given rate function. Moreover, given a sample generated by a (not necessarily mixing) stationary ergodic process, we provide a consistent test to discern the null hypothesis that the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} norm of the sequence 𝜶 \boldsymbol{\alpha} of α \alpha -mixing coefficients of the process is bounded by a given threshold γ ∈ [ 0 , ∞ ) \gamma\in[0,\infty) from the alternative hypothesis that ‖ 𝜶 ‖ > γ \left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert>\gamma . An analogous goodness-of-fit test is proposed for the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} norm of the sequence of β \beta -mixing coefficients of a stationary ergodic process. Moreover, the procedure gives rise to an asymptotically consistent test for independence.



1
Introduction

Mixing
is a fundamental notion in the theory of stochastic processes.
Roughly speaking, a stochastic process
{\bf X}=\langle X_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
indexed by “time”
is mixing if events separated by long time intervals are approximately independent. There are various
notions to quantify such asymptotic independence, including
\alpha
-mixing,
\beta
-mixing,
\phi
-mixing,

\rho
-mixing,
\psi
-mixing; see Bradley
[
3
]
for a general survey.
These notions of mixing are particularly useful in time-series analysis where non-asymptotic concentration inequalities
are available for empirical averages of stationary processes, see, for example,
Doukhan
[
4
]
,
Rio
[
11
]
,
Bertail, Doukhan, and Soulier
[
1
]
,
Bradley
[
3
]
,
Bosq
[
2
]
.
In order to be able to take advantage of these tools, in statistical studies one
often assumes that the process is not only mixing but the mixing coefficients are such that
the desired concentration inequalities hold. Despite the widespread use of such assumptions,
little attention has been paid to validating these conditions.

In this paper we study
the problem of estimating
\alpha
and
\beta
-mixing coefficients. More precisely, we address the following problem.
Upon observing a finite sample drawn from the trajectory of a real-valued, discrete-time, stationary and ergodic
stochastic process, how can one consistently estimate its
\alpha
and
\beta
-mixing coefficients?
Since the
\ell_{1}
norm of the sequence of these coefficients plays an important role in quantifying
how much sample averages differ from their expectations (see, e.g., Lemma
2
),
we pay special attention to estimating the
\ell_{1}
norm. Our main results show that consistent
estimation of these norms is indeed possible, under the only assumption that the process is stationary
ergodic. (If the process is not mixing, the estimators diverge to infinity.) We also show how these estimates can be used
to derive consistent hypothesis tests on the mixing coefficients. The main difficulty of the estimation problem stems
from the fact that all mixing coefficients are inherently asymptotic quantities, yet one only has a finite sample
available. This makes estimation a nontrivial task, especially when no a-priori properties are assumed apart
from stationarity and ergodicity of the process.

Our focus in this paper is on
\alpha
and
\beta
-mixing coefficients, as these are arguably the most
widely used notions of mixing with numerous statistical applications. One may, of course, ask the analogous
questions on other measures of mixing. A particularly interesting notion is
\phi
(or uniform) mixing (see, e.g. Bradley
[
3
]
for a definition)
since under such conditions one has Hoeffding-type exponential inequalities in terms of the
\ell_{1}
norm
of the sequence of
\phi
-mixing coefficients, see Rio
[
11
, Corollary 2.1]
and Samson
[
13
]
.
However, it is unclear if the
\ell_{1}
norm of the sequence of
\phi
-mixing coefficients can be consistently estimated. In this case, a key challenge lies in conditioning on potentially rare events whose probabilities may be arbitrarily small.
We leave this interesting challenge for future research.

1.1
\alpha
and
\beta
mixing

We start by defining the notions of mixing relevant to this paper.
Let
(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},\mu)
be a probability space, and suppose that
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{V}
are two
\sigma
-subalgebras
of
\mathfrak{F}
respectively.
A classical measure of dependence between
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{V}
, introduced by Rosenblatt
[
12
]
, is given by

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathfrak{U},\mathfrak{V})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{U\in\mathfrak{U},V\in\mathfrak{V}}|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)|

which gives rise to the sequence of dependence coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:=\langle\alpha(m)\rangle_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
(called the
\alpha
-mixing coefficients)
of a stochastic process
{\bf X}=\langle X_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
, where

\displaystyle\alpha(m):=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(\sigma(\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq j\}),\sigma(\{X_{t}:t\geq j+m\}))~.

The
\beta
-dependence
\beta(\mathfrak{U},\mathfrak{V})
between
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{V}
was originally introduced by Volkonskii and Rozanov
[
14
,
15
]
as follows (see also Rio
[
11
]
). Let
\iota(\omega)\mapsto(\omega,\omega)
be the injection map from
(\Omega,\mathfrak{F})
to
(\Omega\times\Omega,\mathfrak{U}\otimes\mathfrak{V})
, where
\mathfrak{U}\otimes\mathfrak{V}
is the product sigma algebra generated by
\mathfrak{U}\times\mathfrak{V}
.
Let
\mu_{\otimes}
be the probability measure defined on
(\Omega\times\Omega,\mathfrak{U}\otimes\mathfrak{V})
obtained as the pushforward measure of
\mu
under
\iota
. Let
\mu_{\mathfrak{U}}
and
\mu_{\mathfrak{V}}
denote the restriction of
\mu
to
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{V}
respectively. Then

\displaystyle\beta(\mathfrak{U},\mathfrak{V})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{W\in\sigma(\mathfrak{U}\times\mathfrak{V})}|\mu_{\otimes}(W)-\mu_{\mathfrak{U}}\times\mu_{\mathfrak{V}}(W)|~,

where
\mu_{\mathfrak{U}}\times\mu_{\mathfrak{V}}
is the product measure on
(\Omega\times\Omega,\mathfrak{U}\otimes\mathfrak{V})
obtained from
\mu_{\mathfrak{U}}
and
\mu_{\mathfrak{V}}
. This measure of dependence leads to the sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}:=\langle\beta(m)\rangle_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\beta
-mixing coefficients of a stochastic process
{\bf X}
, where

\beta(m):=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\beta(\sigma(\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq j\}),\sigma(\{X_{t}:t\geq j+m\})).

A stochastic process is said to be
\alpha
-mixing or
strongly mixing
, if it exhibits an asymptotic independence of the form
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\alpha(m)=0
. It is said to be
\beta
-mixing or
absolutely regular
if
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\beta(m)=0
. It is straightforward to check that
\beta(\mathfrak{U},\mathfrak{V})\geq\alpha(\mathfrak{U},\mathfrak{V})
so that absolute regularity implies strong mixing.
Moreover, summability of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
(respectively
\boldsymbol{\beta}
) is clearly a sufficient but not necessary condition for a process to be
\alpha
-mixing (respectively
\beta
-mixing).

1.2
The estimation problem

Regardless of whether or not a process is mixing, its mixing coefficients provide a measure of its dependence structure. Moreover, as mentioned above, individual
\alpha
-mixing (respectively
\beta
-mixing) coefficients and/or their sum
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(m)
(respectively
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\right\rVert=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\beta(m)
) commonly appear in concentration inequalities for dependent processes.
Thus, in order to use these bounds in a statistical problem where the samples may be dependent, knowledge of the sequences
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}
or at least of their
\ell_{1}
norms is required.

For certain subclasses of dependent processes, bounds on the mixing coefficients are known. For example, conditions for the geometric ergodicity of Markov chains have been well studied, see Meyn and Tweedie
[
8
]
and references therein. More recently, some upper bounds on the mixing rates of non-stationary ARCH processes were proposed by Fryzlewicz and Rao
[
5
]
.
However, for larger classes of stationary processes, the mixing
coefficients are typically unknown, and surprisingly little research
has been devoted to the problem of estimating mixing coefficients. One
exception is the work of McDonald, Shalizi, and Schervish

[
6
,
7
]
who provide estimators for
\beta
-mixing
coefficients and show consistency of their estimators. Unfortunately,
due to a lack of precision in the presentation, we were unable to
verify some of the main claims of these papers. Our work does not
build upon these results and we consider a more general setting where,
apart from ergodicity, no assumptions are required on the process
distributions (as opposed to some implicit assumptions on the
existence and smoothness of finite-dimensional densities made in

[
6
,
7
]
).

Towards an adaptive approach, given a sample generated by a stationary ergodic process, our objective in this paper is to estimate its dependence structure as reflected by its
\alpha
-mixing and
\beta
-mixing coefficients.
To this end, in Section
3.1
, we first focus on a subclass of stationary ergodic processes which are
\alpha
-mixing with the additional property that their sequences of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients are summable. For this class, without knowledge of any upper-bounds on
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
and merely using the fact that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
,
we provide asymptotically consistent estimators of the individual
\alpha
-mixing coefficients as well as for
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
. The consistency results for these estimators are established via Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
respectively. We rely on Rio’s covariance inequality
[
11
, Corollary 1.1]
to control the variance of partial sums.
Next, we propose an alternative approach for consistently estimating
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
of a (not necessarily mixing) stationary ergodic process where we no longer require
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
to be summable.
In this case, if the process happens to be
\alpha
-mixing with
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
, then the proposed estimator converges to
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
, otherwise the estimator diverges to infinity.
The weak and strong consistency properties of these estimators follow from Theorems
3
and
4
. The results in this section rely on Lemma
1
and Proposition
1
which together show that the approximation given by (
3
) which is based on the cylinder sets converges to
\alpha(m)
.

In Section
3.2
we provide analogous results for the sequence of
\beta
-mixing coefficients of a stationary ergodic process. Most of the arguments are similar to those given in Section
3.1
, and in particular since
\alpha(m)\leq\beta(m)
, the same covariance inequality is sufficient to control the variance of partial sums in the estimation. However, a key challenge in this case is that unlike
\alpha(\mathfrak{U},\mathfrak{V})
, the
\beta
-dependence between
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{V}
is defined for the product space
(\Omega\times\Omega,\mathfrak{U}\otimes\mathfrak{V},\mu_{\otimes})
. In order to propose an approximation that can be estimated from a single sample-path, we rely on the identity,

\beta(\mathfrak{U},\mathfrak{V})=\sup\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}|\mu(U_{i}\cap V_{j})-\mu(U_{i})\mu(V_{j})|

where the supremum is taken over all pairs of countable partitions
\{U_{1},U_{2},U_{3},\ldots\}
and
\{V_{1},V_{2},V_{3},\ldots\}
of
\Omega
such that
U_{i}\in\mathfrak{U}
and
V_{j}\in\mathfrak{V}
for each
i,~j\in\mathbb{N}
; see Bradley
[
3
, Vol. 1, P. 67, Note 2]
. This, together with
[
3
, Vol. 1 Proposition 3.21]
gives rise to Lemma
5
which in turn leads to Proposition
3
. These results are analogues of Lemma
1
and Proposition
1
, and show that the approximation (
59
) that is based on the cylinder sets converges to
\beta(m)
.

1.3
Hypothesis testing

In Section
4
we show how our estimators can be used to construct goodness-of-fit tests. First, given a sample generated by a stationary
\alpha
-mixing (respectively
\beta
-mixing) process
\mu
, we use our estimator of
\alpha(m)
(respectively
\beta(m)
) to test the null hypothesis that the
\alpha
-mixing (respectively
\beta
-mixing) coefficients of
\mu
are bounded by a given
rate function

\gamma:\mathbb{N}\mapsto[0,1]
against the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
that there exists some
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha(m)>\gamma(m)
(respectively
\beta(m)>\gamma(m)
). The consistency of these tests follow from Theorems
7
and
8
, provided that the sequence of
\alpha
-mixing (respectively
\beta
-mixing) coefficients of the process is summable.
Interestingly, Nobel
[
10
]
used hypothesis testing to estimate polynomial decay rates for covariance-based mixing conditions. By contrast, we do not require the rate functions to be polynomial or to belong to any specific function class. Moreover, given a sample generated by a (not necessarily mixing) stationary ergodic process
\mu
, we construct tests to discern the null hypothesis
H_{0}
that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
(respectively
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\right\rVert
) is bounded by a given threshold
\gamma\in[0,\infty)
from the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
that it exceeds
\gamma
. The consistency of these procedures follow from Theorem
9
(for
\alpha
-mixing coefficients) and Theorem
10
(for
\beta
-mixing coefficients).
As a direct consequence, we obtain strongly consistent tests for independence, obtaining an alternative proof of the main result of Morvai and Weiss
[
9
]
; this is stated as Corollary
1
.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we fix our notation and introduce some basic definitions.
Let
\mathscr{X}
together with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathscr{X}}
be a measurable space. In order to keep the notation simple and to avoid uninteresting technicalities, we take
\mathscr{X}=[0,1]
; but we would like to point out that extensions to more general spaces including to
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are straightforward.
Denote by
\mathfrak{B}^{(k)}
the product
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathscr{X}^{k},~k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
be the set of all
\mathscr{X}
-valued infinite sequences indexed by
\mathbb{N}
.
A (discrete-time) stochastic process is a probability measure
\mu
on the space
(\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}},\mathfrak{F})
where
\mathfrak{F}
denotes the
Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
generated by the cylinder sets.
Associated with the process is a sequence of random variables
{\bf{X}}:=\langle X_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
where
X_{t}:\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathscr{X}
are coordinate projections such that
X_{t}(\boldsymbol{a})=a_{t}
for
\boldsymbol{a}=\langle a_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
. We use the term
(stochastic) process
to refer to either the measure
\mu
or to its corresponding sequence of random variables
{\bf{X}}
; the distinction should be clear from the context.
A process is
{\bf{X}}
is stationary if for all
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
and all
B\in\mathfrak{B}^{(k)}
we have

\Pr((X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})\in B)=\Pr((X_{1+i},\ldots,X_{k+i})\in B).

Let
S:\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
denote the (left) shift transformation on
\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
which maps
\boldsymbol{a}:=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)\in\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
to
S\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{2},a_{3},\ldots)
. It is continuous relative to the product topology on
\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
and defines
a set transformation
S^{-1}
given by
S^{-1}A:=\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}:S\boldsymbol{a}\in A\},~A\subseteq\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus, it is straightforward to check that
S^{-1}
is Borel measurable and that
stationarity of
{\bf{X}}
translates to the condition that
\mu(S^{-1}B)=\mu(B)
for all
B\in\mathfrak{F}
. A stationary process is ergodic if every shift-invariant measurable set has measure
0
or
1
so that if
S^{-1}B=B
for some
B\in\mathfrak{F}
then
\mu(B)\in\{0,1\}
. Recalling the definition of a strongly mixing process given in the introduction, note the well-known fact that a stationary
\alpha
-mixing process is ergodic, see, for example, Bradley
[
3
]
.

3
Estimation

Consider a stationary ergodic process
\mu
with corresponding sequence of random variables
{\bf{X}}=\langle X_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and sequences of
\alpha
-mixing and
\beta
-mixing coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:=\langle\alpha(m)\rangle_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}:=\langle\beta(m)\rangle_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
respectively. In this section we introduce estimators of
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
and
\alpha(m)
, as well as of
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\right\rVert
and
\beta(m)
-
and establish their consistency – these are the main results of the paper.

We consider the,
somewhat simpler, case of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients in Section
3.1
. The corresponding estimators for
\beta
-mixing coefficients are given Section
3.2
.
For the most part, the estimators – and proofs – concerning the
\beta
-mixing coefficients are analogous to their
\alpha
-mixing counterparts. However, as discussed in Sections
1.1
and
1.2
, there is a subtle distinction between the measurable spaces on which the two mixing coefficients are defined. This calls for a slightly different treatment in the case of
\beta
-mixing coefficients, giving rise to a number of technical challenges which we address as part of our analysis in Section
3.2
.

The following notation will be used throughout the section.
Let
\Delta_{k,\ell}

be the set of dyadic cubes in
\mathscr{X}^{k},~k\in\mathbb{N}
of side-length
2^{-\ell}
.
That is,

\Delta_{k,\ell}:=\left\{\left[\frac{i_{1}}{2^{\ell}},\frac{i_{1}+1}{2^{\ell}}\right)\times\ldots\times\left[\frac{i_{k}}{2^{\ell}},\frac{i_{k}+1}{2^{\ell}}\right):i_{j}\in\{0,\ldots,2^{\ell}-1\},~j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\right\}

For each
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{k,\ell}:=\mathcal{P}(\Delta_{k,\ell})
the power-set of
\Delta_{k,\ell}
.
For a given
B\in\mathfrak{B}^{(k)},~k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the event
\{X_{i},\ldots,X_{i+k-1}\in B\},~i\in\mathbb{N}
by
[B]_{i}^{i+k}
. For
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
and each
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
define the
\sigma
algebra generated by the sets
[A]_{1}^{j}
for

A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}
by

\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell):=\sigma\left(\left\{[A]_{1}^{j}:A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}\right\}\right)

(1)

and similarly let

\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell):=\sigma\left(\left\{[B]_{j+m+1}^{n}:B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell},~\text{with}~j^{\prime}:=n-m-j\right\}\right)~.

(2)

3.1
Estimating
\alpha(m)
and
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert

Since the mixing coefficients
\alpha(m)
of
{\bf X}
are inherently asymptotic quantities, we first approximate
them by quantities that only depend on finite-dimensional projections of the process and therefore they can be estimated
from finite samples of the process. To this end, for each
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
, define

\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}\\
B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell}\end{subarray}}\left|\mu\left([A]_{1}^{j}\cap[B]_{j+m+1}^{n}\right)-\mu\left([A]_{1}^{j}\right)\mu\left([B]_{j+m+1}^{n}\right)\right|

(3)

where
j^{\prime}:=n-m-j+1
.
In Lemma
1
below we show that to approximate the
\alpha
-dependence between
\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and
\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
, it suffices to use
\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)
where the supremum is taken over the smaller classes of sets
\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}
and
\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell},~\text{with}~j^{\prime}:=n-m-j
.

Lemma 1
.

For
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
we have

\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)=\alpha(\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell),\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)).

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Fix
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
and some
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
. First, it is clear that

\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)\leq\alpha(\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell),\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))
.
Therefore, it suffices to show that
\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)\geq\alpha(\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell),\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))
.
Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell):=\left\{[A]_{1}^{j}:A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}\right\}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell):=\left\{[B]_{j+m+1}^{n}:B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell},~j^{\prime}:=n-m-j\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and
\mathcal{A}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
correspond to algebras over
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and
\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)

respectively.
It trivially holds that
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
forms a
\pi
-system, since by definition, it is non-empty and closed under finite intersections.
On the other hand we have

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathcal{A}_{1}^{j}(\ell)\\
V\in\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)\end{subarray}}\left|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)\right|\leq\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

(4)

To see this, first note that
by definition of
\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)
, we have

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell)\\
V\in\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)\end{subarray}}\left|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)\right|=\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

(5)

Moreover, by construction
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
is already closed under finite unions and intersections.
Therefore, by (
5
) for any
k\in 1,\ldots,|\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell)|
and any
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
we have

\displaystyle\max\left\{\sup_{V\in\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)}\left|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)\right|,\sup_{V\in\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)}\left|\mu(U^{\prime}\cap V)-\mu(U^{\prime})\mu(V)\right|\right\}\leq\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)

where
U:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}
and
U^{\prime}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}
. Moreover, for any
U\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and any
V\in\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mu(U^{c}\cap V)-\mu(U^{c})\mu(V)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mu(V)-\mu(U\cap V)-(1-\mu(U))\mu(V)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mu(U)\mu(V)-\mu(U\cap V)\right|\leq\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the largest algebra of subsets of
\mathfrak{F}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathcal{L}\\
V\in\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)\end{subarray}}\left|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)\right|\leq\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

(6)

It is straightforward to verify that
\mathcal{L}
forms a
\lambda
-system.
To see this, first note that since
\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)\geq 0
, it clearly holds that
\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{L}
.
Next, take
U_{1}\subseteq U_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
.
Since
\mathcal{L}
is an algebra, it is closed under complementation as well as under pairwise unions and intersections.
Therefore,
(U_{2}\setminus U_{1})\in\mathcal{L}
. Finally, consider a countable sequence of increasing subsets
U_{i}\subseteq U_{i+1}\in\mathcal{L},~i\in\mathbb{N}
and define
U:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}
. It follows from the continuity of probability measure that
U\in\mathcal{L}
.
More specifically, for
V\in\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
define
\overline{U}_{i}:=U_{i}\cap V,~i\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\overline{U}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\overline{U}_{i}
. Observe that
\overline{U}_{i}\subseteq\overline{U}_{i+1}
so that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(\overline{U}_{n})=\mu(\overline{U})
. Similarly, it holds that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(U_{n})=\mu(U)
.
Fix
\epsilon>0
there exist
N_{\epsilon},N^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
such that for all
n\geq\max\{N_{\epsilon},N^{\prime}_{\epsilon}\}
we have

|\mu(U_{n})-\mu(U)|\leq\epsilon
and
|\mu(\overline{U}_{n})-\mu(\overline{U})|\leq\epsilon
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\Big|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)\Big|

\displaystyle=\left|\mu(\overline{U})-\mu(U)\mu(V)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mu(\overline{U}_{n})-\mu(U_{n})\mu(V)\right|+2\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)+2\epsilon~.

Since the choice of epsilon is arbitrary, it follows that
U\in\mathcal{L}
.
Therefore
\mathcal{L}
is a
\lambda
-system. Moreover, by (
4
) we have
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{j}(\ell)\subseteq\mathcal{L}
.
Thus, as follows from Dynkin’s
\pi-\lambda
theorem, we can deduce that
\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{j}(\ell))\subseteq\mathcal{L}
so that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{j}(\ell))\\
V\in\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)\end{subarray}}\left|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)\right|\leq\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

(7)

On the other hand, let
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
be the largest algebra of subsets of
\mathfrak{F}
such that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{j}(\ell))\\
V\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)\right|\leq\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

(8)

In much the same way as with
\mathcal{L}
, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is a
\lambda
-system.
Moreover, by (
7
) and an argument analogous to that concerning
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
, we
can conclude that the algebra of subsets of
\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
is included in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, that is,
\mathcal{A}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.
Hence, by another application of the
\pi-\lambda
theorem,
we obtain
\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. This leads to

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{j}(\ell))\\
V\in\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))\end{subarray}}\left|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)\right|\leq\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

(9)

Observing that
\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{j}(\ell))=\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and
\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{j+m}^{j}(\ell))=\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
, we obtain

\alpha(\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell),\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))\leq\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)

(10)

and the result follows.
∎

For
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
let

\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)=\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

(11)

The next proposition shows that indeed,
\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)
approximates

\alpha(m)
for sufficiently large values of
\ell
and
n
.

Proposition 1
.

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n,\ell\rightarrow\infty}\alpha^{\ell}_{n}(m)=\alpha(m)~.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Fix
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
n>m
.
Observe that for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
we have
\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)\subset\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell+1),~\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)\subset\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n+1}(\ell+1),~n,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
are each a sequence of
\sigma
-algebras with
\bigvee_{\ell}^{\infty}\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{j})
and

\bigvee_{n,\ell}^{\infty}\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)=\sigma(\{X_{t}:t\geq j+m+1\})
.
Therefore, by Bradley
[
3
, Vol. 1, Proposition 3.18]
we obtain,

\lim_{n,\ell\to\infty}\alpha(\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell),\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))=\alpha(\sigma(\{X_{t}:t\in\{1,\ldots,j\}\}),\sigma(\{X_{t}:t\geq j+m+1\}))~.

(12)

It is straightforward to check that for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{n,\ell}\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{n\geq j+m+1}\sup_{\ell}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

(13)

To see this
first let
c:=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{n\geq j+m}\sup_{\ell}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)
and
fix some
\epsilon>0
; by the definition of
\sup
there exist
\ell^{*},j^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
and
n^{*}\geq j^{*}+m
such that
\alpha_{n^{*},j^{*}}^{\ell}(m)\geq c-\epsilon
. We have
c-\epsilon\leq\alpha_{n^{*},j^{*}}^{\ell}(m)\leq\sup_{n,\ell}\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)
. Similarly, let
c^{\prime}:=\sup_{n,\ell}\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)
, and note that there exist some
n^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\max_{j\in 1,\ldots,n^{\prime}-m}\alpha_{n^{\prime},j}^{\ell^{\prime}}(m)\geq c^{\prime}-\epsilon
. Hence,

c^{\prime}-\epsilon\leq\max_{j\in 1,\ldots,n^{\prime}-m}\alpha_{n^{\prime},j}^{\ell^{\prime}}(m)\leq\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{n\geq j+m}\sup_{\ell}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m).

Since the choice of
\epsilon
is arbitrary, (
13
) follows.
We obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n,\ell\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n,\ell\rightarrow\infty}\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)

\displaystyle=\sup_{n,\ell}\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)

(14)

\displaystyle=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{n\geq j+m}\sup_{\ell}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)

(15)

\displaystyle=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\lim_{n,\ell\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)

(16)

\displaystyle=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\lim_{n,\ell\rightarrow\infty}\alpha(\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell),\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))

(17)

\displaystyle=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(\sigma(\{X_{t}:t\in\{1,\ldots,j\}\}),\sigma(\{X_{t}:t\geq j+m+1\}))

(18)

\displaystyle=\alpha(m)~,

where (
14
) and (
16
) follow from the fact that for a fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)
is an increasing function of
n,\ell
, (
15
) follows from (
13
), (
17
) follows from Lemma
1
, and (
18
) follows from (
12
).
∎

Now we are ready to introduce the natural empirical estimates of the approximate mixing coefficients
\alpha^{\ell}_{n}(m)
. The key ingredient of the analysis is the concentration inequality of Lemma
2

below.

For
t\in\mathbb{N}

define the empirical measure
\mu_{t}({\bf X},\cdot):\mathfrak{B}^{(k)}\rightarrow[0,1],~k\in\mathbb{N}
as

\mu_{t}({\bf X},B):=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\chi_{B}\{X_{ik+1},\ldots,X_{(i+1)k}\}~,

(19)

where
\chi
is the indicator function. Lemma
3
provides a simple concentration bound on the empirical measure of a cylinder set
[D]_{1}^{k}
for any
D\in\mathcal{D}_{k,\ell},~k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. The proof relies on the following variance bound of Rio:

Lemma 2

(Rio
[
11
, Corollary 1.1]
)
.

Let
\langle Y_{i}\rangle_{i\geq 0}
be a stationary sequence of
[-1,1]
-valued random variable. Define
\overline{\alpha}(m):=\alpha(\sigma(Y_{0}),\sigma(Y_{m}))
and suppose that
\left\lVert\overline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\overline{\alpha}(m)<\infty
. For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, let
S_{t}:=Y_{0}+\ldots+Y_{t-1}
. We have

\var(S_{t})\leq 4t\left\lVert\overline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert.

Lemma 3
.

For all
D\in\mathcal{D}_{k,\ell},~k,\ell,t\in\mathbb{N}
and for every
\epsilon>0
, we have

\Pr\left(\left|\mu_{t}({\bf X},D)-\mu([D]_{1}^{k})\right|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{4\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert}{t\epsilon^{2}}~.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Let
\overline{\mathbb{N}}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
For each
D\in\mathcal{D}_{k,\ell},~k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
define the
[-1,1]
-valued sequence of random variables
\langle Y_{i}\rangle_{i\in\overline{\mathbb{N}}}
where

Y_{i}:=\chi_{D}\{X_{ik+1},\ldots,X_{(i+1)k}\}-\mu([D]_{1}^{k}),~i\in\overline{\mathbb{N}}

(20)

Observe that
\langle Y_{i}\rangle_{ti\in\overline{\mathbb{N}}}
is a zero-mean
[-1,1]
-valued stationary process and for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have,

\displaystyle\alpha(\sigma(Y_{0}),\sigma(Y_{m}))

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}),\sigma(X_{mk+1},\ldots,X_{(m+1)k}))

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(k(m-1)+1)

where the first inequality follows from the fact that by definition we have
\sigma(Y_{0})\subset\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
and that
\sigma(Y_{m})\subset\sigma(X_{mk+1},\ldots,X_{(m+1)k})
and the second inequality follows form the definition of the
\alpha
-mixing coefficients of
{\bf X}
.
Thus, we obtain

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(\sigma(Y_{0}),\sigma(Y_{m}))\leq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(k(m-1)+1)\leq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(m)=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert

where, the last inequality follows from the fact that
\alpha(m)
is a decreasing sequence so that
for every
u>v\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\alpha(u)\leq\alpha(v)
.
Let
S_{t}:=\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}Y_{i}
.
By Chebychev’s inequality and Theorem
2
we have,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\left|\mu_{t}({\bf X},D)-\mu([D]_{1}^{k})\right|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr(|S_{t}|\geq t\epsilon)\leq\frac{\var(S_{t})}{t^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\leq\frac{4\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert}{t\epsilon^{2}}

(21)

where the last is due to the fact that by definition
\overline{\alpha}(m)\leq\alpha(m),~m\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

An empirical estimate of
\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m),~m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},n>m\in\mathbb{N}
can be obtained as

\widehat{\alpha}_{t,n}^{\ell}({\bf X},m):=\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}\\
B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell}\end{subarray}}\left|\gamma_{t,n}^{m,j}({\bf X},A,B)-\mu_{t}({\bf X},A)\mu_{t}({\bf X},B)\right|

(22)

where
j^{\prime}:=n-m-j
,
\mu_{t}({\bf X},\cdot)
is given by (
19
) and

\displaystyle\gamma_{t,n}^{m,j}({\bf X},A,B):=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\chi_{A}(X_{in+1},\ldots,X_{in+j})\chi_{B}(X_{in+j+m..(i+1)n})

(23)

with
t\geq n
.
When
m,n,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
are fixed, we only have finitely many cylinder sets to consider in (
22
), hence the ergodic theorem leads to the following result.

Lemma 4
.

Let
{\bf X}
be a (not necessarily mixing) stationary ergodic process with process distribution
\mu
and sequence of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\langle\alpha(m)\rangle_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
. For every
m,\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\widehat{\alpha}_{t,n}^{\ell}({\bf X},m)=\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)~,~\mu-\text{almost surely}.

For each
m,\ell,k\in\mathbb{N}
define the constant
C_{m,\ell,k}
by

C_{m,\ell,n}:=m(2^{2^{n\ell}+2^{m\ell+1}+1})~.

(24)

When the
\ell_{1}
norm of the sequence of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients is finite, we have the following nonasymptotic inequality for
the empirical version of the approximation of the mixing coefficients.

Proposition 2
.

Let
{\bf X}
be a stationary ergodic process with process distribution
\mu
and sequence of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\langle\alpha(m)\rangle_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
. For every
m,\ell,n,t\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\epsilon>0
we have

\displaystyle\Pr(|\widehat{\alpha}_{t,n}^{\ell}({\bf X},m)-\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)|\geq\epsilon)\leq\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert C_{m,\ell,n}}{mt\epsilon^{2}}~.

Furthermore, for each
M\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{\alpha}_{t,n}^{\ell}({\bf X},m)-\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m))\right|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert C_{{{M,\ell,n}}}}{t\epsilon^{2}}~.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
.
For
t,n\in\mathbb{N}
define

\Omega_{t,n}:=\left\{\max_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n,\ell}}|\mu_{t}({\bf X},D)-\mu([D]_{1}^{n})|\leq\epsilon/2^{2^{m\ell}}\right\}

By Lemma
3
and a union bound we obtain,

\Pr(\Omega_{t,n})\geq 1-(2^{2^{n\ell}+2^{m\ell+1}})\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert}{t\epsilon^{2}}~.

For all
\omega\in\Omega_{t,n}
, and each
A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell},~B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell},~j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\},~j^{\prime}:=n-m-j+1
we have

\displaystyle\Big|\gamma_{t,n}^{m,j}({\bf X},A,B)-\mu([A]_{1}^{j}\cap[B]_{j+m+1}^{n})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{D}_{m,\ell}}\left|\mu_{t}({\bf X},A\times C\times B)-\mu([A\times C\times B]_{1}^{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon~,

and that

\displaystyle\Big|\mu_{t}({\bf X},A)\mu_{t}({\bf X},B)

\displaystyle-\mu([A]_{1}^{j})\mu([B]_{j+m+1}^{n})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq|\mu_{t}({\bf X},A)-\mu([A]_{1}^{j})||\mu_{t}({\bf X},B)-\mu([B]_{j+m+1}^{n})|

\displaystyle~~\quad\qquad\qquad+\mu([A]_{1}^{j})|\mu_{t}({\bf X},B)-\mu([B]_{j+m+1}^{n})|

\displaystyle~~\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mu([B]_{j+m+1}^{n})|\mu_{t}({\bf X},A)-\mu([A]_{1}^{j})|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{2}/(2^{2^{m\ell}+1})+2\epsilon/(2^{2^{m\ell}})\leq\epsilon~.

Recall the convention that for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
we let
j^{\prime}:=j-n-m
.
We obtain

\displaystyle\Pr(|\widehat{\alpha}_{t,n}^{\ell}({\bf X},m)-\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)|\geq\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\left|\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}\\
B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell}\end{subarray}}\left|\gamma_{t,n}^{m,j}({\bf X},A,B)-\mu_{t}({\bf X},A)\mu_{t}({\bf X},B)\right|-\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)\right|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\exists j,~A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell},B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell}:\left|\gamma_{t,n}^{m,j}({\bf X},A,B)-\mu([A]_{1}^{j}\cap[B]_{j+m+1}^{n})\right|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Pr\left(\exists j,~A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell},B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell}:\left|\mu_{t}({\bf X},A)\mu_{t}({\bf X},B)-\mu([A]_{1}^{j})\mu([B]_{j+m}^{n})\right|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\Pr(\Omega_{t,n}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq(2^{2^{n\ell}+2^{m\ell+1}+1})\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert}{t\epsilon^{2}}

Thus, for each
M\in\mathbb{N}
we obtain,

\displaystyle\Pr(|\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{\alpha}_{t,n}^{\ell}({\bf X},m)-\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)|\geq\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(\sum_{m=1}^{M}|\widehat{\alpha}_{t,n}^{\ell}({\bf X},m)-\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)|\geq\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert C_{M,\ell,k}}{t\epsilon^{2}}

with
C_{M,\ell,k}:=M(2^{2^{n\ell}+2^{M\ell+1}+1})
as given by (
24
).
∎

3.1.1
Estimation under finite
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert

Equipped with Propositions
1
and
2
, it is now easy to define estimates of the individual mixing coefficients
\alpha(m)
that are consistent whenever
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
.

Let
\langle\ell_{t}\rangle,\langle n_{t}\rangle
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
be non-decreasing unbounded sequences of positive integers.
Let the sequence of positive numbers
\langle\delta_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\delta_{t}<\infty
.
Let
\langle\epsilon_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be another sequence of positive numbers such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\epsilon_{t}=0
.
For a fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
and each
t\in\mathbb{N}
let

\tau_{t}:=\frac{C_{m,\ell_{t},n_{t}}}{m\epsilon_{t}^{2}\delta_{t}}

(25)

and define,

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m)

\displaystyle:=\widehat{\alpha}_{\tau_{t},n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}({\bf X},m).

(26)

Theorem 1

(
\widehat{\alpha}_{t}
is strongly consistent)
.

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
and any stationary
\alpha
-mixing process
{\bf X}
with process distribution
\mu
and sequence of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
such that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m)=\alpha(m),~\mu-\text{almost surely}.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
.
As follows from Proposition
1
, there exist
L_{\epsilon},~N_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{N}

such that for all
n\geq N_{\epsilon}
and all
\ell\geq L_{\epsilon}
we have,

\left|\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)-\alpha(m)\right|\leq\epsilon.

(27)

Let
T_{\epsilon}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\ell_{t}\geq L_{\epsilon},~n_{t}\geq N_{\epsilon}
for
t\geq T_{\epsilon}^{(1)}
.
Define the sequence of events

E_{t}:=\left\{\left|\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m)-\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)\right|\geq\epsilon_{t}\right\},~t\in\mathbb{N}.

Since as specified by (
25
)
\tau_{t}=\frac{C_{m,\ell_{t},n_{t}}}{\epsilon_{t}^{2}\delta_{t}}
, then by Proposition
2
for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\Pr(E_{t})\leq\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert C_{m,\ell_{t},n_{t}}}{m\tau_{t}\epsilon_{t}^{2}}\leq\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert\delta_{t}}{m}.

(28)

Let
\delta:=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{t}
and note that since
\langle\delta_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is chosen to be summable we have
\delta<\infty
.
Define

E:=\limsup_{t\to\infty}E_{t}=\bigcap_{t=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}E_{t^{\prime}}.

By (
28
) we have
\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\Pr(E_{t})\leq\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert}{m}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{t}=\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert}{m}\delta<\infty
, since
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
. Therefore, by the Borel-Cantelli Lemma we obtain
\Pr(E)=0
.
As a result, there exists some
T^{*}
(which depends on the sample-path) such that for all
t\geq T^{*}
we have

\left|\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m)-\alpha_{n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}(m)\right|\leq\epsilon_{t},~\quad\mu-\text{almost surely}.

(29)

Let
T_{\epsilon}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{N}
be large enough such that for all
t\geq T_{\epsilon}^{(2)}
we have
\epsilon_{t}\leq\epsilon/2
.
Take
t\geq\max\{T^{*},T_{\epsilon}^{(1)},T_{\epsilon}^{(2)}\}
, with probability it holds that,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m)-\alpha(m)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m)-\alpha_{n_{t}}^{{\ell_{t}}}(m)\right|+\epsilon

(30)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{t}+\epsilon

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon

where, (
30
) follows from (
27
) and (
31
) follows from (
29
).
Since the choice of
\epsilon
is arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Next, we introduce an estimator for
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
and prove its consistency under the assumption that the process is stationary
\alpha
-mixing, with a summable
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.
For
t\in\mathbb{N}
recall the parameters
\langle\ell_{t}\rangle,\langle n_{t}\rangle,\langle\delta_{t}\rangle,\langle\epsilon_{t}\rangle
defined earlier, and let
\langle M_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be an increasing sequence of positive integers.
For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
let

\kappa_{t}:=\frac{C_{M_{t},\ell_{t},n_{t}}}{\epsilon_{t}^{2}\delta_{t}},

(32)

and define,

\displaystyle\theta_{t}({\bf X})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\widehat{\alpha}_{\kappa_{t},n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}({\bf X},m).

(33)

Theorem 2

(
\theta_{t}
is strongly consistent)
.

For any stationary
\alpha
-mixing process
{\bf X}
with process distribution
\mu
and sequence of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
such that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\theta_{t}({\bf X})=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert,~\mu-\text{almost surely}.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
, there exists some
M_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{m=M_{\epsilon}+1}^{\infty}\alpha(m)\leq\epsilon/5.

(34)

Let
\epsilon^{\prime}:=\frac{\epsilon}{5M_{\epsilon}}
.
As follows from Proposition
1
, for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
L_{\epsilon^{\prime}},~N_{\epsilon^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}

such that for all
n\geq N_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
and all
\ell\geq L_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
we have,

\max_{m\in 1,\ldots,M_{\epsilon}}\left|\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)-\alpha(m)\right|\leq\epsilon^{\prime}.

(35)

Let
T_{\epsilon}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
M_{t}\geq M_{\epsilon},~\ell_{t}\geq L_{\epsilon^{\prime}(t)},~n_{t}\geq N_{\epsilon^{\prime}(t)}
for
t\geq T_{\epsilon}^{(1)}
.
Define the sequence of events

\kappa_{t}=\frac{C_{M_{t},\ell_{t},n_{t}}}{\epsilon_{t}^{2}\delta_{t}}

\Pr(E_{t})\leq\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert C_{M_{t},\ell_{t},k_{t}}}{t\epsilon_{t}^{2}}\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert\delta_{t}.

Let
\delta:=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{t}
and note that since
\langle\delta_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is chosen to be summable we have
\delta<\infty
.
Define

We have

As a result, there exists some

(which depends on the sample-path) such that for all

\left|\theta_{t}({\bf X})-\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\alpha_{n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}(m)\right|\leq\epsilon_{t},~\quad\mu-\text{almost surely}~.

(37)

Let
T_{\epsilon}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{N}

\epsilon_{t}\leq\epsilon/5
for
t\geq T_{\epsilon}^{(2)}

With probability one, for all

\displaystyle\Big|\theta_{t}({\bf X})-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\theta_{t}({\bf X})-\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\alpha_{n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}(m)\Big|+\Big|\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\alpha_{n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}(m)-\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\alpha(m)\Big|+\epsilon/5

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\epsilon}}\Big|\alpha_{n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}(m)-\alpha(m)\Big|+\sum_{m=M_{\epsilon}+1}^{M_{t}}|\alpha_{n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}(m)-\alpha(m)|+2\epsilon/5

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\epsilon}}|\alpha_{n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}(m)-\alpha(m)|+\sum_{m=M_{\epsilon}+1}^{M_{t}}\alpha_{n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}(m)+\sum_{m=M_{\epsilon}+1}^{M_{t}}\alpha(m)+2\epsilon/5

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon.

(39)

where (

\alpha_{n}^{\ell}(m)\geq 0
and
\alpha(m)\geq 0
for all

, and (

3.1.2
Estimation under arbitrary
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert

Next we address the case when one does not have the guarantee that the
\ell_{1}
norm of the sequence of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients
is finite. This case is significantly more complex, since the conclusion of Proposition
2
is potentially
vacuous, irrespective of the sample size. To circumvent this difficulty, we construct a non-decreasing sequence of
estimates of
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
– starting with the trivial lower bound
0
–
by performing a sequence of tests. The value of the estimate is only increased if there is sufficient evidence
that the value of
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
is indeed larger than the current estimate. This is
possible since if
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
happens to be small, then its empirical estimator must be
small thanks to Proposition
2
.

With the objective to make the presentation more transparent, we start with a simpler estimate that
is “weakly consistent” in the sense that if one prescribes a tolerated probability of error
\delta
, then
one may construct a corresponding estimator that converges to
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Then we introduce a more complex estimator that is almost surely
consistent.

In order to introduce the “weak” estimator, let
\delta\in(0,1)
be the allowed probability of error,
and let
\delta_{t}
be positive numbers such that
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\delta_{t}=\delta
. Fix a countable dense subset
\mathscr{S}
of
[0,\infty)
and let
\mathscr{S}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots\}
be an arbitrary enumeration of
\mathscr{S}
. Given a process
{\bf X}
, set
\psi_{0}({\bf X}):=0
, and for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
define

\displaystyle\psi_{t}({\bf X})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\{s_{t},\psi_{t-1}({\bf X})\}&\text{if}\quad\theta_{t}({\bf X})>s_{t}+\epsilon_{t}\sqrt{s_{t}}\\
\psi_{t-1}({\bf X})&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

with
\epsilon_{t}
being a decreasing positive sequence converging to zero and
\theta_{t}
specified by (
33
).
Clearly,
\langle\psi_{t}({\bf X})\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
form a non-decreasing sequence that is either convergent or diverges to infinity.

Theorem 3

(

Moreover, with

Next we present the main result of the paper, an estimator for
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert

which, as shown below, is strongly consistent regardless of whether this norm is finite or infinite.
To this end, we build on the construction of
\psi_{t}({\bf X})
, which gives rise to our third estimator of
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert
. Recall that
\mathscr{S}
is a countable dense subset of
[0,\infty)
.
Define a sequence
\langle u_{t}\rangle
such that
u_{t}\in\mathscr{S}
for all
t
and for every
s\in\mathscr{S}
, the set
\{t\in\mathbb{N}:u_{t}=s\}
is infinite. The sequence
\langle u_{t}\rangle
“visits” each element of
\mathscr{S}
infinitely often. One such example is:

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=s_{1}

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=s_{1},u_{3}=s_{2}

\displaystyle u_{4}

\displaystyle=s_{1},u_{5}=s_{2},u_{6}=s_{3}

The estimator
\xi_{t}
given by (
53
) below keeps track of a “bit value”
b_{t}(s)\in\{0,1\}
assigned to each
s\in\mathscr{S}
. At each time instance,
b_{t}(s)
marks the outcome of the test the last time the value
s
was tested.
With
b_{0}(s)=1
for all
s\in S
, and
\xi_{0}({\bf X}):=0
, let

b_{t}(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if $u_{t}=s$ and }\quad\theta_{t}({\bf X})\leq s+\epsilon_{t}\sqrt{s}\\
0&\text{if $u_{t}=s$ and }\quad\theta_{t}({\bf X})>s+\epsilon_{t}\sqrt{s}\\
b_{t-1}(s)&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, and define

\xi_{t}({\bf X})=\inf\left\{s\in\mathscr{S}:b_{t}(s)=1\right\}~,

(53)

where the infimum is defined to be zero if the set is empty.

Theorem 4

(
\xi_{t}
is strongly consistent)
.

Let
{\bf X}
be a (not necessarily mixing) stationary ergodic process with process distribution
\mu
and sequence of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.
We have,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\xi_{t}({\bf X})=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert,\quad\mu-\text{almost surely}.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

First we show that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\xi_{t}({\bf X})\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert\quad\text{with probability one}.

\sum_{t:u_{t}>\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert}\chi\{{\theta_{t}({\bf X})>u_{t}+\epsilon_{t}\sqrt{u_{t}}}\}<\infty,~\mu-\text{almost surely}.

Hence, with probability one, there exists a (random) time

(55)

In particular, let

and observe that
|E|<\infty
. Therefore, there exists some time

such that,

.

, with probability one we have
b_{t}(s)=1
for all

and (
54
) follows.
The fact that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\xi_{t}({\bf X})\geq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert~,\quad\text{almost surely}~,

(56)

for
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
follows analogously. To see this, take any
\eta<\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
and define
\varrho:=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert-\eta

As in the proof of Theorem

3.2
Estimating
\beta(m)
and
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\right\rVert

In this section, we derive estimators concerning the
\beta
-mixing coefficients of a stationary ergodic process.
For the most part, the results are analogous to those given in Section
3.1
. An overview of the similarities and differences between the two settings is provided in the introduction.

We start by defining an approximation of the
\beta
-mixing coefficients of
{\bf X}
.
Recall the notation
\mathcal{D}_{k,\ell},~k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
introduced in the beginning of Section
3
corresponding to the power-set
\mathcal{P}(\Delta_{k,\ell})
of the set of dyadic cubes in
\mathscr{X}^{k}
of side-length
2^{-\ell}
.
For
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
, let

\beta_{n,j}^{\ell}(m):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell}}\left|\mu\left([A]_{1}^{j}\cap[B]_{j+m+1}^{n}\right)-\mu\left([A]_{1}^{j}\right)\mu\left([B]_{j+m+1}^{n}\right)\right|

(59)

where
j^{\prime}:=n-m-j+1
. The intuition behind this choice follows from the discussion provided in Section
1.2
. Recall the
\sigma
-subalgebras of
\mathfrak{F}
, denoted
\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and
\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
, with
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
, as given by (
1
) and (
2
) respectively. In a manner analogous to the estimation of the
\alpha
-mixing coefficients, we show via Lemma
5
below that to approximate the
\beta
-dependence between between
\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and
\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
, it suffices to use
\beta_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)
given by (
59
). Note that the
\pi-\lambda
argument used in the proof of Lemma
1
does not carry over to this case where summations are involved in (
59
) in place of the suprema in (
3
).

Lemma 5
.

For
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
we have

\beta_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)=\beta(\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell),\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))~.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

Fix
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
and some
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}
.
Define

\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell):=\left\{[A]_{1}^{j}:A\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}\right\}
and

\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell):=\left\{[B]_{j+m+1}^{n}:B\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime},\ell},~j^{\prime}:=n-m-j\right\}.

Observe that
\sigma(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell))=\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and
\sigma(\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))=\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
, and that
\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and
\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
are atomic, with the elements of the countable collections
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell)
and
\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)
as their atoms respectively. By Bradley
[
3

we have

\beta(\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell),\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}(\ell)}\sum_{V\in\mathcal{C}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)}|\mu(U\cap V)-\mu(U)\mu(V)|=\beta_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)~.

∎

For
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N},~n>m\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\beta_{n}^{\ell}(m)=\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}}\beta_{n,j}^{\ell}(m)

(60)

define an approximation of
\beta(m)
.

Proposition 3
.

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n,\ell\rightarrow\infty}\beta^{\ell}_{n}(m)=\beta(m)~.

Proof sketch of Proposition

.

Fix
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and

.
Observe that for each

we have
\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell+1),~\ell\in\mathbb{N}

\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n+1}(\ell+1),~n,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
are each a sequence of

\bigvee_{\ell}^{\infty}\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell)=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{j})
and

\bigvee_{n,\ell}^{\infty}\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell)=\sigma(\{X_{t}:t\geq j+m+1\})

Therefore, by Bradley
[
3

\lim_{n,\ell\to\infty}\beta(\mathfrak{F}_{1}^{j}(\ell),\mathfrak{F}_{j+m}^{n}(\ell))=\beta(\sigma(\{X_{t}:1\leq t\leq j\}),\sigma(\{X_{t}:t\geq j+m+1\}))~.

In this section we use the estimators provided in Section

to construct consistent goodness-of-fit tests.
Consider a stationary ergodic process
\bf X
with process distribution
\mu
and sequences of
\alpha
-mixing and
\beta
-mixing coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\langle\alpha(m)\rangle_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\langle\beta(m)\rangle_{m\in\mathbb{N}}

A mapping
\gamma:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow[0,1]

\alpha
-mixing

for
\mu
if for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\alpha(m)\leq\gamma(m).

Similarly, it is said to be a
\beta
-mixing rate function for
\mu
if for each fixed

it holds that

\beta(m)\leq\gamma(m).

We wish to test the null hypothesis
H_{0}
that
\gamma
is an
\alpha
-mixing (respectively
\beta
-mixing) rate function for
\mu
against the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
that there exists some
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha(m)>\gamma(m)
(respectively
\beta(m)>\gamma(m)
) .
More formally, let
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
be the class of stationary
\alpha
-mixing processes whose sequence of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\langle\alpha(m)\rangle_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, that is, such that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
. Similarly, let
\mathscr{C}^{\beta}
be the class of stationary
\beta
-mixing processes whose sequence of
\beta
-mixing coefficients is such that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}\right\rVert<\infty
.
For a rate function
\gamma
, let
\mathscr{R}^{\alpha}_{\gamma}
(respectively
\mathscr{R}^{\beta}_{\gamma}
) be the class of all processes which have
\gamma
as an
\alpha
-mixing (respectively
\beta
-mixing) rate function.
We construct a sequence
\langle g_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
of functions
g_{t}:\mathscr{X}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{+1,-1\},~t\in\mathbb{N}
each measurable with respect to the filtration
\mathfrak{F}_{t}
such that given a sample
\bf{X}
generated by
\mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
,
with probability
1
, produce

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}g_{t}(\bf X)=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if}\ \mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}\cap\mathscr{R}^{\alpha}_{\gamma}\\
-1&\text{if}\ \mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}\setminus\mathscr{R}^{\alpha}_{\gamma}~.\end{cases}

(70)

We call such a sequence of function satisfying (
70
) a strongly consistent goodness-of-fit test for rate function
\gamma
on
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
.
Analogously, we construct a sequence
\langle\widetilde{g}_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathfrak{F}_{t}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{g}_{t}(\bf X)=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if}\ \mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\beta}\cap\mathscr{R}^{\beta}_{\gamma}\\
-1&\text{if}\ \mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\beta}\setminus\mathscr{R}^{\beta}_{\gamma}~.\end{cases}

(71)

t\in\mathbb{N}

\langle M_{t}\rangle,\langle\ell_{t}\rangle,\langle n_{t}\rangle

\delta\in(0,1)

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\delta_{t}=\delta
.

\langle\epsilon_{t}\rangle_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be another sequence of positive numbers such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\epsilon_{t}=0
.
For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m):=\widehat{\alpha}_{\tau_{t},n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}({\bf X},m)

(72)

),

\tau_{t}

) and
\widetilde{\tau}_{t}
by (

).
For a given process
\bf{X}

-1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Proof of Theorem
7
.

Consider a rate function
\gamma:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow[0,1]
and denote by
\mathscr{R}^{\alpha}_{\gamma}
the class of processes with
\gamma
as rate function of their
\alpha
-mixing coefficients. Consider a process measure
\mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
with corresponding sequence of random variables
\bf X

First, observe that
g_{t}
is
\mathfrak{F}_{t}
-measurable.
To prove that

is consistent in the sense of (

{\bf X}
with process distribution
\mu
.
Let

If

, we have

\displaystyle\Pr(E_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\Pr(\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m)>\gamma(m)+\epsilon_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\Pr(\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m)>\alpha(m)+\epsilon_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\Pr(|\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m)-\alpha_{n_{t}}^{\ell_{t}}(m)|>\epsilon_{t})

(76)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert\delta_{t}~,

(77)

where (
75
) follows from the fact that
\mu\in\mathscr{R}^{\alpha}_{\gamma}
, and (
76
) from observing that

, and (
77

2
.
Noting that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert<\infty
and
\delta_{t}
is summable, we have
\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\Pr(E_{t})\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{t}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert\delta<\infty
. Therefore, by the Borel-Cantelli Lemma we obtain
\Pr(\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}E_{t})=0
.
This means that there exists some
\tau
(which depends on the sample-path) such that for all

(78)

On the other hand, suppose

for all

. Moreover, since

there exists some

such that

for all

.

. For

\displaystyle\Pr(\widetilde{E}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m^{*})\leq\gamma(m^{*})+\epsilon_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(\widehat{\alpha}_{t}({\bf X},m^{*})\leq\gamma(m^{*})+\delta^{*}/2-\epsilon_{t})
