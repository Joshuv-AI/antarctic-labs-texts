Communication-efficient Distributed Newton-like Optimization with Gradients and M M -estimators
Ziyan Yin

Abstract
In modern data science, it is common that large-scale data are stored and processed parallelly across a great number of locations. For reasons including confidentiality concerns, only limited data information from each parallel center is eligible to be transferred. To solve these problems more efficiently, a group of communication-efficient methods are being actively developed. We propose two communication-efficient Newton-type algorithms, combining the M M -estimator and the gradient collected from each data center. They are created by constructing two Fisher information estimators globally with those communication-efficient statistics. Enjoying a higher rate of convergence, this framework improves upon existing Newton-like methods. Moreover, we present two bias-adjusted one-step distributed estimators. When the square of the center-wise sample size is of a greater magnitude than the total number of centers, they are as efficient as the global M M -estimator asymptotically. The advantages of our methods are illustrated by extensive theoretical and empirical evidences.



1
Introduction

The statistical inference and optimization problem under a distributed setting is a popular topic in modern data science applications. For example, online marketing data are often too big to be stored within one hard drive; medical records are usually stored and processed separately at regional healthcare centers, and the inter-hospital sharing of the patient-level information is highly regulated and restricted. In these scenarios, despite the fact that more accurate inference can be obtained by combining data from all centers, practical realities prevent these centers from sharing the raw data stored therein. Among others, two foremost concerns are the data confidentiality and the technical feasibility.
Consequently, only limited statistics satisfying some restrictive rules are eligible to be infrequently transferred among centers.

Recent years have witnessed a flurry of important technological and methodological developments of the communication-efficient methods to handle such distributed data problems. One broadly applied class is the so-called divide-and-conquer strategy which takes average of certain statistics calculated at each center separately. Recent advances include
Zhang et al., 2013
;
Rosenblatt and Nadler, 2016
;
Lee et al., 2017
;
Zhang et al., 2015
;
Battey et al., 2018
;
Volgushev et al., 2019
;
Chen et al., 2020
;
Wang et al., 2019
;
Fan et al., 2019
, etc.

However, one of the major limitations of these one-shot averaging methods is that, their efficacy is heavily limited by the center-wise sample size. In other words, the dataset cannot be split across too many centers, and adding extra data centers to the existing dataset does not always improve accuracy.
Zhang et al., 2013
showed when the center-wise sample size is fixed, the mean squared error of the simple average of
M
-estimators can hardly be reduced by adding more data centers.

Recently, another communication-efficient framework has received considerable attention.
Wang et al., 2017
and
Jordan et al., 2019
proposed to use some surrogate loss function that can be evaluated efficiently at a preselected data center (also known as the local center). The advantage of this framework is that only gradients are collected from parallel centers, upon receiving an initial estimator. More specifically, this strategy replaces the higher-order derivatives of the global loss function (that requires data from all data centers) with those local surrogates. Due to its convenience and promising properties, this strategy has been broadly investigated and applied in different directions; for example, see
Fan et al., 2021a
;
Duan et al., 2021
;
Yu et al., 2020
;
Yu et al., 2021
;
Li and Zhao, 2021
;
Wang et al., 2019
;
Chen et al., 2021
, among others.

Although the estimator proposed in
Wang et al., 2017
and
Jordan et al., 2019
appears efficient in some cases, when the number of centers
m
and the center-wise sample size
n
are of the same magnitude, the accuracy of the local surrogates becomes the bottleneck, limiting further improvement. The local high-order derivatives remain barely changed no matter how many parallel centers there are. More specifically, in each iteration, this framework uses the local Hessian estimator as the “learning rate”, which always has a fixed
O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
gap to the true Fisher information matrix.

In light of this finding, in this paper, we are motivated to design a more efficient distributed estimation procedure by constructing some more accurate Fisher information estimators. We find in each parallel center, the Fisher information can be seen as the “coefficient” of a “linear” map from the gradient evaluated at the truth to the estimation error of the corresponding
M
-estimator. Although this “true” gradient is unavailable, when a given estimator is close enough to the truth, the “linearity” between the errors of the
M
-estimators and the gradients evaluated at that given estimator still holds with negligible discrepancy. This kind of pseudo-linearity indicates that, its pseudo-coefficient — the true Fisher information matrix — can be estimated via linear regression. Thus, we propose two Fisher information estimators by combining the
M
-estimators and gradients collected from parallel centers. We show that under regular existence and continuity assumptions, the distances of both our estimators to their estimands are
O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1}+m^{-1})
, which outperform the local Hessian estimator used in
Wang et al., 2017
and
Jordan et al., 2019
.

With these
M
-estimator/gradient-enhanced Fisher information estimators, we propose two iterative distributed algorithms correspondingly. Both have higher rates of convergence than the traditional method does. Additionally, we present two bias-adjusted one-step distributed estimators using the aforementioned Fisher information estimators. Their distances to the global
M
-estimator are reduced by removing quadratic terms of the estimation error of the initial estimator. As a result, these two refined one-shot estimators have the same efficiency asymptotically as the global
M
-estimator does when
m=o(n^{2})
.

Motivation and Problem Set-Up

We first state the structure and storage of the dataset.
Let
\{{{\mathbf{X}}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote
N
independent and identically distributed samples with marginal distribution
\mathcal{P}
over some sample space
\mathcal{X}
. For any parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
containing in some convex space
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define a convex and three-times differentiable loss function
L:\Theta\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
is a minimizer of the population risk, that is

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{L(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathbf{X}})}\}.

We consider the evenly distributed setting where
\{{{\mathbf{X}}_{i}}\}_{i}^{N}
are uniformly stored in one local (or central) center
\mathcal{L}
(or
\mathcal{M}_{1}
) and
m-1
global (or parallel) centers
\{{\mathcal{M}_{i}}\}_{i=2}^{m}
with
\mathcal{G}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{M}_{i}
; therefore,
N=mn
. Suppose we have full access to the data stored in
\mathcal{L}
which also plays the role of processing and delivering the final results. Despite the requirement that the data are evenly stored with equal size
n
, our findings can be potentially generalized to unequal sample size cases without compromising the spirit.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}
be the
M
-estimator from the
i
th center
\mathcal{M}_{i}
, and the average
\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=m^{-1}\sum_{i=2}^{m}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}
. We refer the readers to
Zhang et al., 2013
for a thorough picture of properties of
M
-estimators under this distributed setting.

Newton’s method is a powerful tool for optimization problems. However, when the dataset is split across many centers, and the inter-center communication cost is a major concern, it cannot be applied directly because the Hessian matrices can be huge and hard to transfer. One approach is the so-called communication-efficient surrogate likelihood (CSL) framework proposed in In
Jordan et al., 2019
and
Wang et al., 2017
. They replaced the unavailable global Hessian with the local Hessian matrix
\widehat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{L}}\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}}_{j})
. Then, Newton-like methods are applied upon receiving gradients from parallel centers. This method is efficient, and the communication cost is low. However, it depends heavily on the quality and quantity of the local data —
\widehat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
remains almost unchanged no matter how large the global sample size is. That is,

\|{\widehat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\|_{2})+O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2}).

The estimation error of
\widehat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
depends on
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\|_{2}
and
n^{-1/2}
, neither of which decreases with a larger
m
. Also, the whole optimization procedure may be ruined, if we accidentally choose a “bad” data center to be the local one.

Thus, we are trying to find communication-efficient alternatives of
\widehat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and we hope their errors would decrease when
m
increases. This brings the estimators of the Fisher information matrix
{\mathbf{I}}_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}})}\}
and its inverse
{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}}\right\}^{-1}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\left\{{{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\overline{{\mathbf{l}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right\}^{\top}}\right]

(1)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\{{{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\overline{{\mathbf{l}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right\}\left\{{{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\overline{{\mathbf{l}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right\}^{\top}}\right]^{-1}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\{{{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\overline{{\mathbf{l}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right\}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}}\right],

(2)

where
{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}}_{j})
is the gradient evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in the
i
th center, and
\overline{{\mathbf{l}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is their average. Their construction needs no high-order derivative; we only collect gradients and
M
-estimators. We name
\widehat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{-1}
the
M
-estimator/gradient (MG) and gradient/
M
-estimator (GM) Fisher information estimators, and will show they are closer to
{\mathbf{I}}_{0}
than
\widehat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

2.1
Estimation Accuracy of the
M
-Estimator/Gradient Fisher Information Estimators

Let
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
, and define two cross-products involving the first and second derivatives of
L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}_{11}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[{\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}}_{1})\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}}_{1})^{\top}}\right],

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}_{12}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[{\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}}_{1})\otimes\{{\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}}_{1})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}}\}}\right].

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
U(\rho)
=
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta};\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\|_{2}\leq\rho\}
be some Euclidean ball of radius
\rho>0
in which Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
hold. Suppose
n
and
m
are large enough, such that
\widehat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
exist almost surely.

(1)

For any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in U(\rho)
,

\widehat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}={\mathbf{I}}_{0}{\mathbf{Q}}_{11}^{-1}({\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}){\mathbf{Q}}_{12}+{\mathbf{R}}_{D},

where
\|{{\mathbf{R}}_{D}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\{{(n^{3/2}\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{2}^{3}+1)(n^{-1}+m^{-1})}\}
;

(2)

When
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in U(\rho)
and
\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{2}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/4})
,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}={\mathbf{Q}}_{11}^{-1}({\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}){\mathbf{Q}}_{12}{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}+{\mathbf{R}}_{G},

where
\|{{\mathbf{R}}_{G}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}({n\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{2}^{4}+m^{-1}+n^{-1}})
.

It is clear that the estimation errors of
\widehat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
consist of both
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
-constant parts and sub-
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
parts. When
\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
, both
\|{\widehat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}}\|_{2}
and
\|{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}}\|_{2}
are
O(\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{2})+O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1}+m^{-1})
. By contrast, using the local Hessian leads to the error
\|{\widehat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{2})+O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
. For a given
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
, our proposed Fisher information estimators converge to their estimands stochastically faster than the local Hessian does, if
n=o(m^{2})
. Also, for the
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
-constant part, a local estimation can be conducted to further reduce their errors and speed up the whole process; see Theorem
3.1
.

Remarkably, in generalized linear models (e.g., logistic regression),
{\mathbf{Q}}_{11}={\mathbf{I}}_{0}
and
{\mathbf{Q}}_{12}={\mathbf{0}}
. Our proposed estimators would outperform
\widehat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
without any adjustment.

2.2
Decomposition of the Estimation Errors

Proposition
2.1
comes from two key properties: the mean-value theorem describing the gap between two Hessian matrices with different parameters, and the asymptotic “linearity” between the
M
-estimator and the gradient at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.

The first statement is an application of the multivariate mean-value theorem of the distance between
n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}}_{j})
and
n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}}_{j})
: if the third derivative of
L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})
exists and is
m({\mathbf{X}})
-Lipschitz continuous with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, then, for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in U(\rho)
,

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}}_{j})-n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}}_{j})\approx\{{{\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}}\}{\mathbf{Q}},

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}L\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\Delta};{\mathbf{X}}_{j}}\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}}_{j})\approx\frac{1}{2}\{{{\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}}\}{\mathbf{Q}},

where

{\mathbf{Q}}=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla^{2}\left[{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}})}\}}\right]\\
\vdots\\
\nabla^{2}\left[{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{d}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}})}\}}\right]\ \end{pmatrix}.

The second property is the “linear” association between the
i
th sample gradient at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
and the corresponding
M
-estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}
at
\mathcal{M}_{i}
. At the
i
th center, let
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
and
\overline{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}=n^{-1}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}L({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+t{\mathbf{d}}_{i};{\mathbf{X}}_{ij}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
. Then,

{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})=-\overline{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}=-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}+({\mathbf{I}}_{0}-\overline{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}){\mathbf{d}}_{i}.

(3)

Under the conditions given in
Zhang et al., 2013
,
\|{{\mathbf{d}}_{i}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
and
\|{{\mathbf{I}}_{0}-\overline{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
. In (
3
),
({\mathbf{I}}_{0}-\overline{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}){\mathbf{d}}_{i}
becomes subordinate –
\|{({\mathbf{I}}_{0}-\overline{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}){\mathbf{d}}_{i}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2}\|{{\mathbf{d}}_{i}}\|_{2})
. All these facts lead to the “linearity” between
{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
and
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}
:

{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\approx-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}.

In practice, only
{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is available, so we consider
{\mathbf{g}}_{i}={\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Delta}
as the practical implementation of
{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
. The association between
{\mathbf{g}}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}
can be quantified by expanding
{\mathbf{g}}_{i}
around
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.

Let
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}=n^{-1}\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}L({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\Delta};{\mathbf{X}}_{ij}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
. Applying (
3
),

{\mathbf{g}}_{i}={\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})+\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Delta}=-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i},

where the residual
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}=({\mathbf{I}}_{0}-\overline{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}){\mathbf{d}}_{i}+(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}-{\mathbf{I}}_{0})\boldsymbol{\Delta}
. The form
{\mathbf{g}}_{i}=-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
indicates a “regression” estimator of
{\mathbf{I}}_{0}
:

\widetilde{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\left({\sum{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\top}}\right)^{-1}\left({\sum{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{g}}_{i}^{\top}}\right),

(4)

and its estimation error is

\widetilde{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}=-\left({\sum{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\top}}\right)^{-1}\left({\sum{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}}\right).

(5)

In (
5
), the denominator
m^{-1}\sum{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\top}
is less a concern: the pseudo linearity
{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\approx-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}
leads to the fact that
m^{-1}\sum n{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\top}\approx m^{-1}{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}\sum n{\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}){\mathbf{l}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})^{\top}{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}{\mathbf{Q}}_{11}{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}
.

On the other hand, for the numerator, by definition,

\displaystyle m^{-1}\sum{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}=

\displaystyle m^{-1}\sum_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{0}-{\mathbf{H}}_{i})+m^{-1}\sum_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\top}({\mathbf{H}}_{i}-\overline{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i})+

\displaystyle m^{-1}\sum_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}({\mathbf{H}}_{i}-{\mathbf{I}}_{0})+m^{-1}\sum_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{i}-{\mathbf{H}}_{i}).

Bounding each part, we have

m^{-1}\sum_{j}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}\approx n^{-1}({\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}){\mathbf{Q}}_{12}+{\mathbf{R}}_{D}^{\prime},

where
\|{{\mathbf{R}}_{D}^{\prime}}\|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-2}+n^{-3/2}m^{-1/2})+O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2}m^{-1/2}\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\|_{2}^{2})
. Together with the denominator,

\widetilde{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}=\left({\sum n{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{d}}_{i}^{\top}}\right)^{-1}\left({\sum n{\mathbf{d}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}}\right)\approx{\mathbf{I}}_{0}{\mathbf{Q}}_{11}^{-1}{\mathbf{I}}_{0}({\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{\top}){\mathbf{Q}}_{12}+{\mathbf{R}}_{D}^{\prime\prime},

with
\|{{\mathbf{R}}_{D}^{\prime\prime}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1}+n^{-1/2}m^{-1/2}+n^{1/2}m^{-1/2}\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1}}\|_{2}^{2})
.

For practical applications, we implement the sample-based estimators
\widehat{{\mathbf{g}}}_{i}
and
\widehat{{\mathbf{d}}}_{i}
to approximate the unknown
{\mathbf{g}}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}
, which gives us
\widehat{{\mathbf{I}}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Meanwhile, another implication of (
2.2
) is
{\mathbf{d}}_{i}=-{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}{\mathbf{g}}_{i}+{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
, and this brings the estimator of
{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}
—
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, whose distance to
{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}
can be quantified following the similar steps.

Algorithm 2

Distributed Newton-Like Optimization with GM estimators

Under conditions listed in Section
3.3
, these methods converge stochastically faster than the existing method does.

Define

{\mathbf{Q}}_{12}\circ{\mathbf{u}}=({\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}){\mathbf{Q}}_{12}{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{Q}}\circ{\mathbf{u}}=({\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}){\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Let

-estimator:

and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{t}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
and

for

and

, and

, consider the estimators
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}={\mathbf{Q}}_{11}^{-1}({{\mathbf{Q}}_{12}\circ\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{t}})-2^{-1}{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}({{\mathbf{Q}}\circ\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{t}})+{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{1}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}={\mathbf{Q}}_{11}^{-1}({{\mathbf{Q}}_{12}\circ\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{t}^{\prime}})-2^{-1}{\mathbf{I}}_{0}^{-1}({{\mathbf{Q}}\circ\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{t}^{\prime}})+{\mathbf{R}}_{2}^{*},

\displaystyle\|{{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{1}}\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\{{(m^{-1}+n^{-1})(\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{t}}\|_{2}+\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}}\|_{2})+m^{-1}n^{-1}}\}

Theorem

The error of the updated estimator is bounded by the square of its predecessor’s estimation error, which is of the same order of magnitude as Newton’s method with the global Hessian. The same conclusion can be drawn for the GM estimator
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}^{\prime}
as well.

Also, our algorithms have no concern about accidentally choosing a “bad” local center. The whole iteration process depends on the initial estimator and the global data quality, instead of the local one. Therefore, when

and

On the other hand, when

M

3.3
Technical Assumptions

In this section, we list the convexity and identifiability assumptions used in our technical analysis.

Assumption 1
.

The parameter space
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact convex set, with
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\text{int}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
and
l_{2}
-radius
r_{0}=\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\|_{2}
.

Assumption 2
.

That
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is the unique minimizer of the population risk
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}}_{i})}\}
. For any
\delta>0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
, such that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[{\inf_{\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\|_{2}\geq\delta}\left\{{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}}_{i})-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}}_{i})}\right\}\geq\varepsilon}\right]=1.

Assumption 3
.

The population risk is twice-differentiable, and there exist finite constants
\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
such that
\lambda_{-}{\mathbf{I}}\preceq{\mathbf{I}}_{0}\preceq\lambda_{+}{\mathbf{I}},

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{\|{\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}})}\|_{2}^{6}}\}\leq\lambda_{1}^{6}\text{ and }\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{\|{\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}})}\|_{2}^{6}}\}\leq\lambda_{2}^{6}.

Additionally, for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}\in U
,

\|{\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime};{\mathbf{X}})-\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})}\|_{2}\leq h({\mathbf{X}})\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}

We assume
\max({\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{h({\mathbf{X}})^{6}}\},\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}[{\left|{h({\mathbf{X}})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{h({\mathbf{X}})}\}}\right|^{6}}]})\leq\lambda_{h}^{6}
for some finite positive constant
\lambda_{h}
.

We further assume the loss function is three-times differentiable with enough continuity in
U(\rho)
. Recall the definition of the third derivative tensor operator of
L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})
,
{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathbf{X}})=\nabla^{3}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})
, for
{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathbf{X}})=\nabla^{3}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla^{2}\{{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})}\}\\
\vdots\\
\nabla^{2}\{{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{d}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})}\}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that
{\mathbf{Q}}
defined in (
2.2
) is the expectation of
{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},{\mathbf{X}})
.
The definition of
{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathbf{X}})
indicates, for
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in U(\rho)
,

\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})-\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}})=\int_{0}^{1}\{{{\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})^{\top}}\}{\mathbf{T}}\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+t(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}),{\mathbf{X}}}\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

When
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is close to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
, and
{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathbf{X}})
is smooth enough with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})-\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0};{\mathbf{X}})\approx\{{{\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})^{\top}}\}{\mathbf{T}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},{\mathbf{X}}})
. The condition is specified as follows.

Assumption 4
.

The risk function is three-times differentiable, and
{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathbf{X}})
is
m({\mathbf{X}})
-Lipschitz. That is, for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}\in U(\rho)
and
{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|{({{\mathbf{I}}_{d}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}})\{{{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime},{\mathbf{X}})-{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathbf{X}})}\}}\|_{2}\leq m({\mathbf{X}})\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}\|_{2}\|{{\mathbf{u}}}\|_{2},

with
\max({\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{m({\mathbf{X}})^{4}}\},\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}[{\{{m({\mathbf{X}})-\mathbb{E}\{{m({\mathbf{X}})}\}}\}^{4}}]})\leq\lambda_{m}^{4}
for some finite positive constant
\lambda_{m}
. Additionally, at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
,
\|{{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},{\mathbf{X}})}\|_{2}
is bounded by
g({\mathbf{X}})
for some function
g({\mathbf{X}})
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\{{g({\mathbf{X}})^{4}}\}\leq\lambda_{3}^{4}
.

Assumption
4
also implicates Assumption
3
. That is, under Assumption
4
,
\|{{\mathbf{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathbf{X}})}\|_{2}\leq g({\mathbf{X}})+\rho m({\mathbf{X}}).

We can choose
h({\mathbf{X}})=g({\mathbf{X}})+\rho m({\mathbf{X}})
, so that for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}\in U(\rho)

\displaystyle\|{\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime};{\mathbf{X}})-\nabla^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};{\mathbf{X}})}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|{{\mathbf{T}}\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}+t(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta}),{\mathbf{X}}}\}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta})}\|_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq h({\mathbf{X}})\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}.

Lemma 4.16
.
