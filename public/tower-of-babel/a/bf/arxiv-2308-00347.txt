1.
Introduction

The study of partial differential equations (PDEs) has yielded extensive research on parabolic equations, given their ability to provide mathematical descriptions of natural and artificial phenomena. The most basic form of parabolic equations is the heat equation, represented as
\partial_{t}u=\Delta u
, which describes the diffusion of free particles. An interesting observation is that the fundamental solution to the heat equation is the transition density of Brownian motion
B_{t}
, where the Laplacian
\Delta
acts as the infinitesimal generator of Brownian motion;

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}u(x+B_{t})-u(x)}{t}=\Delta u(x).

This observation has inspired researchers to investigate the relationship between stochastic processes and PDEs. In particular, the
subordinate Brownian motion
(SBM)
X_{t}:=B_{S_{t}}
and the corresponding PDE have been thoroughly studied, because SBM can describe not only normal diffusion but also pure jump diffusion. Here,
S_{t}
is a real-valued increasing Lévy process that satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda S_{t}}]=\mathrm{e}^{-t\phi(\lambda)}
, where
\phi
denotes a Bernstein function. For more details, see Section
2.1
.

There are numerous fields, including physics, engineering, and finance, where SBM is used for relevant modeling (see
e.g.

[
8
,
23
,
48
]
). One key characteristic of SBM is its radial symmetry, which makes it particularly suitable for modeling isotropic diffusion processes like
[
27
]
.
However, SBM is not applicable for the analysis or description of anisotropic diffusion models, which are frequently investigated using advanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI).
In
[
17
]
, for instance, the authors examined DTI theory by employing a three-dimensional Lévy process
X_{t}:=(X^{1}_{t},X^{2}_{t},X_{t}^{3})
with independent one-dimensional
\alpha_{1}
-stable,
\alpha_{2}
-stable,
\alpha_{3}
-stable processes
X^{1}_{t}
,
X^{2}_{t}
,
X_{t}^{3}
, respectively, where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in(0,2)
.
While it is evident that each component process
X^{1}
,
X^{2}
,
X^{3}
are SBMs, the process
X
itself is not an SBM, and its radial symmetry is consequently broken.
To help readers understand, we provide detailed elucidations of this phenomenon from two perspectives, starting with the probabilistic viewpoint.
Given the independence of each component process, we can establish the following relationship:

\sum_{0<s<t}|X_{s}^{i}-X_{s-}^{i}||X_{s}^{j}-X_{s-}^{j}|=0\quad a.s,\quad i,j=1,2,3,\,i\neq j.

That is, there are no simultaneous jumps between component processes.
This indicates that
X
only moves parallel to the coordinate axes.
Furthermore, given two open sets

Q_{1}:=(-a_{1},a_{1})\times(-a_{2},a_{2})\times(-a_{3},a_{3}),\quad Q_{2}=:(-a_{1},a_{1})\times(-a_{3},a_{3})\times(-a_{2},a_{2}),

which are identical up to certain rotation, we also observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in Q_{1})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{P}(X^{i}_{t}\in(-a_{i},a_{i}))

\displaystyle\neq\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{1}\in(-a_{1},a_{1}))\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{2}\in(-a_{3},a_{3}))\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{3}\in(-a_{2},a_{2}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in Q_{2}).

This implies that the probability distribution of
X_{t}
does not exhibit radial symmetry. Another perspective is based on analysis and PDEs.
The infinitesimal generator of
X
is a singular anisotropic fractional Laplacian
\mathcal{A}
, given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(u(x+y)-u(x)-\nabla u(x)\cdot y\mathbf{1}_{|y|\leq 1}\right)\pi(\mathrm{d}y),

where
y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
\pi(\mathrm{d}y)
is defined by

\displaystyle\pi(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle:=c_{1}|y_{1}|^{-1-\alpha_{1}}\mathrm{d}y_{1}\,\epsilon_{0}(\mathrm{d}y_{2},\mathrm{d}y_{3})+c_{2}|y_{2}|^{-1-\alpha_{2}}\mathrm{d}y_{2}\,\epsilon_{0}(\mathrm{d}y_{1},\mathrm{d}y_{3})

\displaystyle\quad+c_{3}|y_{3}|^{-1-\alpha_{3}}\mathrm{d}y_{3}\,\epsilon_{0}(\mathrm{d}y_{1},\mathrm{d}y_{2}).

Here
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
are positive constants, and
\epsilon_{0}
is the Dirac measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
centered at the origin. The operator
\mathcal{A}
in (
1.1
) can also be represented by the Fourier multiplier operator:

\mathcal{A}u(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(-|\xi_{1}|^{\alpha_{1}}-|\xi_{2}|^{\alpha_{2}}-|\xi_{3}|^{\alpha_{3}}\right)\mathcal{F}[u](\xi)\mathrm{e}^{ix\cdot\xi}\mathrm{d}\xi,\quad\xi:=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}),

where
\mathcal{F}[u]
is the Fourier transform of
u
. Since the Fourier multiplier of
\mathcal{A}
is not radially symmetric, the operator
\mathcal{A}
is not radially symmetric.

These two perspectives highlight the need for mathematical foundations for anisotropic diffusion models that differ from isotropic diffusion models.
To address this challenge, it is crucial to investigate stochastic processes and corresponding PDEs that are more general than those of SBM.
Additionally, there is a necessity to expand the mathematical tools employed in the study of isotropic diffusion models to accommodate the complexities of anisotropic diffusion models.

In this paper, we establish an
L_{q}(L_{p})
-regularity theory for parabolic equations with non-local operators having a time-measurable anisotropic symbol. Specifically, we consider the equation

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,\vec{x})=\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}(t)u(t,\vec{x})+f(t,\vec{x}),\quad\vec{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
d=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}d_{i}
. Here
\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}(t)
is a time-measurable anisotropic non-local operator defined by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}(t)u(t,\vec{x})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\Big(b_{j}(t)\Delta_{x_{j}}u(t,\vec{x})

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}}\left(u(t,\vec{x}+\vec{y})-u(t,\vec{x})-\vec{y}\cdot\nabla_{\vec{x}}u(t,\vec{x})1_{|\vec{y}|\leq 1}\right)a_{j}(t,\vec{y})J_{j}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y})\Big),

J_{j}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y}):=\mu_{j}(\mathrm{d}y_{j})\epsilon_{0}^{d-d_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y_{1},\cdots,\mathrm{d}y_{j-1},\mathrm{d}y_{j+1},\cdots,\mathrm{d}y_{\ell}),

where
\mu_{j}
is a Lévy measure of SBM,
\vec{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}=:\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}
,
a_{j}(t,\vec{y})
and
b_{j}(t)
are bounded measurable coefficients (see (
2.7
) for detail), and
\epsilon_{0}^{d-d_{j}}
is the centered Dirac measure defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d-d_{j}}
.
We would like to emphasize that our results hold without any assumptions on the regularity or symmetry of coefficients
a_{j}(t,\vec{y})
and
b_{j}(t)
.
Our main result is to prove the existence of a unique solution for (
1.2
) satisfying the following estimate:

\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}(t)u(t,x)|^{p}+|u(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{q/p}\mathrm{d}t\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{q/p}\mathrm{d}t

for
p,q\in(1,\infty)
, and
0<T<\infty
.

The operator
\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}(t)
in (
1.3
) generalizes the singular fractional Laplacian in (
1.1
).
Since
\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}(t)
is not radial and its symbol is quite singular, the approach based on radial decay of symbol (see
e.g.

[
29
,
32
,
31
]
) cannot be applied directly.
Similarly, it seems nontrivial to apply
[
46
, Theorem 8.7]
to treat
L_{q}(L_{p})
-theory for our equation. See Remark
2.14
for more details.

To overcome these difficulties, we use both the Calderón-Zygmund theory and stochastic process theory. The probability theory allows us to solve enough (
1.2
) with coefficient
\vec{a}(t,\vec{y})=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, and
\vec{b}(t)=\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, where
\vec{b}
is a constant vector. Then, we exploit the independence of stochastic processes to represent the fundamental solution of (
1.2
), denoted by
p(t,x)
, as a product of transition densities
p_{i}(t,x_{i})
corresponding to
d_{i}
-dimensional SBMs with Bernstein functions
\phi_{i}
;

p(t,x)=p_{1}(t,x_{1})\times\cdots\times p_{\ell}(t,x_{\ell})=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}p_{i}(t,x_{i}).

To estimate
p_{i}
, we impose a lower scaling condition on
\phi_{i}
:

(1.4)

\displaystyle c_{i}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\delta_{i}}\leq\frac{\phi_{i}(R)}{\phi_{i}(r)},\qquad 0<r<R<\infty\quad(c_{i}>0,\delta_{i}\in(0,1]).

This condition is commonly used in the study of SBM (see
e.g.

[
13
,
36
,
45
]
).
Under the lower scaling condition (
1.4
), we can obtain upper bounds for each transition density
p_{i}
as well as its arbitrary order derivative (see Theorem
3.3
). Based on the upper bounds of
p_{i}
, by applying the Calderón-Zygmund theory, we can obtain a BMO estimates for the solution (see Theorem
3.5
):

\left\|\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}u\right\|_{BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}.

Since the
L_{2}
-estimates of the solution can be obtained easily from the Fourier analysis (see Lemma
3.1
), we use the Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem to derive
L_{p}
-estimates of the solution. Based on
L_{p}
-estimates, we apply the
L_{p}
-valued Calderón-Zygmund theory to obtain
L_{q}(L_{p})
estimates of the solution.

.

-theory of parabolic equations with operators of the form (

) has been investigated from various angles.

2.
Settings and Main results

2.1.
Settings

In this subsection, we review the basic definition and properties of Bernstein functions and introduce the concept of IASBMs.

\bullet

Definition and Representation of Bernstein functions.

A
Bernstein function
is an infinitely differentiable function
\phi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
that satisfies two conditions:
\phi(0+)=0
and
(-1)^{n}D^{n}\phi(\lambda)\leq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
. In other words, the function
\phi
is non-negative, non-decreasing and concave.
It is well-known that every Bernstein function can be represented in the following form (see, for instance,
[
47
, Theorem 3.2]
):

(2.1)

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda)=b\lambda+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\right)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}t),

where
b\geq 0
is the
drift
of
\phi
and
\mu
is a non-negative measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying the condition

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1\wedge t\right)\mu(\mathrm{d}t)<\infty.

The measure
\mu
is called the
Lévy measure
of
\phi
.
For a vector of Bernstein functions
\vec{\phi}:=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{\ell})
, we say that
\vec{b}:=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\ell}
is the
drift
of
\vec{\phi}
if each
b_{i}
is the drift of
\phi_{i}
. Similarly, if we consider a vector of Lévy measures
\vec{\mu}(\mathrm{d}\vec{t}):=\left(\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}t_{1}),\cdots,\mu_{\ell}(\mathrm{d}t_{\ell})\right)
, where
\vec{t}=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\ell}
, we say that
\vec{\mu}
is the
Lévy measure
of
\vec{\phi}
if each
\mu_{i}
is the Lévy measure of
\phi_{i}
.

\bullet

Relation between Bernstein functions and subordinators.

Bernstein functions have a natural connection with subordinators, which are real-valued increasing Lévy processes that start at
0
. Specifically, it is well-known (
e.g.

[
28
,
47
]
) that a function
\phi
is a Bernstein function if and only if it is the Laplace exponent of a subordinator. That is, for a given Bernstein function
\phi
, there exists a unique subordinator
S=(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda S_{t}}]:=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda S_{t}(\omega)}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega)=\mathrm{e}^{-t\phi(\lambda)},\quad\forall(t,\lambda)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

This means that the probability distribution of the subordinator
S_{t}
at any fixed time
t>0
is completely determined by the Bernstein function
\phi
, and vice versa.

\bullet

Subordinate Brownian motion(SBM).
Let
B=(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion that is independent of the subordinator
S
. The process
X=(B_{S_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
is a SBM, and its infinitesimal generator is defined by

\phi(\Delta_{x})f(x)=\phi(\Delta)f(x):=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(x+X_{t})]-f(x)}{t}.

It is well-known that
X
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued radial symmetric Lévy process, and its characteristic exponent is given by
\phi(|\cdot|^{2})
,
i.e.
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{iX_{t}\cdot\xi}]=\mathrm{e}^{-t\phi(|\xi|^{2})},\quad\forall(t,\xi)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

According to
[
28
, Theorem 31.5]
,
\phi(\Delta_{x})
has the following two representations:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\phi(\Delta_{x})f(x)

\displaystyle=b\Delta_{x}f+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(f(x+y)-f(x)-\nabla_{x}f(x)\cdot y\mathbf{1}_{|y|\leq 1}\right)J(y)\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[-\phi(|\cdot|^{2})\mathcal{F}[f]](x),

where
J(y):=j(|y|)
and
j:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is given by

j(r)=\int_{(0,\infty)}(4\pi t)^{-d/2}\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/(4t)}\mu(\mathrm{d}t).

Here
b
is the drift of
\phi
and
\mu
is the Lévy measure of
\phi
. Also we call
J(y)=j(|y|)
the jump kernel of
\phi(\Delta_{x})
.

\bullet

Independent Array of Subordinate Brownian motion(IASBM).

Throughout the rest of this paper, we assume that the spatial dimension
d
satisfies the following equation:

d=\vec{d}\cdot\vec{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}d_{i},\quad\vec{1}:=(1,\cdots,1),\,\vec{d}:=(d_{1},\cdots,d_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell},

where
d_{i}
represents the
i
-th subdimension and
\vec{1}
is a vector of all ones.
For any vector
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}:=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}
, we use the notation

\vec{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}),\quad x_{i}=(x^{1}_{i},\dots,x^{d_{i}}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\quad(i=1,\dots,\ell).

We say that an
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
-valued stochastic process
\vec{S}=(S^{1},\cdots,S^{\ell})
is a
vector of subordinators with Laplace exponent
\vec{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{\ell})
if
S^{1},\cdots,S^{\ell}
are subordinators satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda{S^{i}_{t}}}]=\mathrm{e}^{-t\phi_{i}(\lambda)}
for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
.
Let
\vec{B}=(B^{1},\cdots,B^{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}
be a vector of independent Brownian motions. It is easy to check that
\vec{B}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.
We say that an
\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}
-valued stochastic process
\vec{X}=(X^{1},\cdots,X^{\ell}):=(B^{1}_{S^{1}},\cdots,B^{\ell}_{S^{\ell}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}
is an IASBM corresponding to
\vec{\phi}
if
X_{1},\cdots,X_{\ell}
are independent SBMs.

Now we investigate fundamental properties of IASBMs, including characteristic exponents and infinitesimal generators. Let
\vec{X}
be an IASBM corresponding to
\vec{\phi}
. We note that
X
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Lévy process, and its characteristic exponent is given by

(2.3)

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{\xi}\cdot\vec{X}_{t}}]=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\exp\left(-t\phi_{i}(|\xi_{i}|^{2})\right),\quad\vec{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}.

Since each component of
\vec{X}
is independent, the infinitesimal generator of
\vec{X}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(\vec{x}+\vec{X}_{t})]-f(\vec{x})}{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\phi_{i}(\Delta_{x_{i}})f(x)=:(\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})f(x),

where
\Delta_{\vec{d}}:=(\Delta_{x_{1}},\Delta_{x_{2}},\cdots,\Delta_{x_{\ell}})
,
\Delta_{x_{i}}
is the Laplacian operator on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
and

\phi_{i}(\Delta_{x_{i}})f(\vec{x}):=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(x_{1}\cdots,x_{i-1},x_{i}+X_{t}^{i},x_{i+1},\cdots,x_{\ell})]-f(\vec{x})}{t},\quad 1\leq i\leq\ell.

Now, we show that
(\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})f
can be represented as given in equation (
2.2
).
To begin with, we define the Fourier transform of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to the
i
-th coordinate as
\mathcal{F}_{d_{i}}[f]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{\ell})\mathrm{e}^{-i\xi_{i}\cdot x_{i}}\mathrm{d}x_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq\ell
, where
d_{i}
denotes the dimension of the
i
-th coordinate. We also define the inverse Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{d_{i}}
in a similar way.
Using these notions, we can express the operator
\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}}
as

\displaystyle(\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})f(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\vec{b}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}}f(\vec{x})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(f(\vec{x}+\vec{y})-f(\vec{x})-\nabla_{\vec{x}}f(\vec{x})\cdot\vec{y}\mathbf{1}_{|\vec{y}|\leq 1})\vec{1}\cdot\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{d}\left[-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\phi_{i}(|\xi_{i}|^{2})\mathcal{F}_{d_{i}}[f]\right](\vec{x}).

where
\vec{b}
is the drift of
\vec{\phi}
,
\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y})
is a vector of Lévy measures defined by

(2.4)

\begin{gathered}\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y})=(J_{1}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y}),\cdots,J_{\ell}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y})),\\
J_{i}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y}):=J_{i}(y_{i})\mathrm{d}y_{i}\epsilon_{0}^{i}(\mathrm{d}y_{1},\cdots,\mathrm{d}y_{i-1},\mathrm{d}y_{i+1},\cdots,\mathrm{d}y_{\ell}),\end{gathered}

J_{i}(y_{i}):=\int_{(0,\infty)}(4\pi t)^{-d_{i}/2}\mathrm{e}^{-|y_{i}|^{2}/(4t)}\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}t)
and
\mu_{i}
is the Lévy measure of
\phi_{i}
.

Remark 2.1
.

When
\ell=1
, we can directly see that

\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}}=\phi(\Delta_{x}).

2.2.
Main results

In this subsection, we present the main result of this paper. The theorem is built on the following key assumption.

Assumption 2.2
.

Suppose that
\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{\ell}
are Bernstein functions satisfying that there exist constants
\delta_{0}\in(0,1]
and
c_{0}>0
such that

(2.5)

\begin{gathered}c_{0}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\delta_{0}}\leq\min\left(\frac{\phi_{1}(R)}{\phi_{1}(r)},\cdots,\frac{\phi_{\ell}(R)}{\phi_{\ell}(r)}\right),\qquad 0<r<R<\infty.\end{gathered}

Remark 2.3
.

(i) Assumption
2.2
implies that every
\phi_{i}
satisfies scaling condition (
2.5
). If
\phi_{i}(r)=r^{\alpha_{i}}
with
\alpha_{i}\in(0,1]
(
i=1,\dots,\ell
), then
c_{0}=1
, and
\delta_{0}=\min\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}\}
fulfill (
2.5
). Thus Assumption
2.2
covers vectors consisting of stable processes and Brownian motions. Also, Assumption
2.2
combined with the concavity of
\phi
gives

(2.6)

c_{0}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\delta_{0}}\leq\frac{\phi_{i}(R)}{\phi_{i}(r)}\leq\frac{R}{r},\qquad 0<r<R<\infty.

(ii) Such scaling conditions are closely related to behavior of SBM (or its transition density). For example, if

c_{1}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\delta_{1}}\leq\frac{\phi(R)}{\phi(r)}\leq c_{2}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\delta_{2}}\quad\forall\,0<r<R<\infty,\quad(c_{1},c_{2}>0,\quad\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,1)),

then the corresponding heat kernel is comparable to
(\phi^{-1}(t^{-1}))^{d/2}\wedge t\phi(|x|^{-2})|x|^{-d}

(
e.g.

[
36
, Section 5]
). We also remark that scaling conditions for characteristic exponent like (
2.5
) or for jump kernels are widely used in the study of Markov processes. We refer to
[
1
,
5
,
25
,
26
,
29
,
33
,
35
,
36
,
45
]
.

Next, we introduce Sobolev spaces associated with the operator
\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}}
will serve as our solution spaces.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
1<p,q<\infty
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and
0<T<\infty
.
For a tempered distribution
u
, we define
(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})^{\gamma/2}u
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})^{-\gamma/2}u](\vec{\xi})

\displaystyle:=\left(1-\mathcal{F}[\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}}](\vec{\xi})\right)^{\gamma/2}\mathcal{F}[u](\vec{\xi})

\displaystyle:=\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\phi_{i}(|\xi_{i}|^{2})\right)^{\gamma/2}\mathcal{F}[u](\vec{\xi}).

(i)
The space
H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{p}=H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a closure of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
under the norm

\|u\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{p}}:=\|(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})^{\gamma/2}u\|_{L_{p}}<\infty.

(ii)
The space
H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{q,p}(T)
is a set of measurable mappings
u:(0,T)\to H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{p}
satisfying

\|u\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{q,p}(T)}:=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{p}}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/q}<\infty.

We also denote
L_{q,p}(T):=H^{\vec{\phi},0}_{q,p}(T)
.

(iii)
We denote
C^{\infty}_{p}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
as a collection of functions
u(t,x)
such that
D^{m}_{x}u\in C([0,T];L_{p})
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We also denote
C^{1,\infty}_{p}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
as a collection of functions
u(t,x)
such that
u\in C_{p}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\partial_{t}D^{m}_{x}u\in C([0,T];L_{p})
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

(iv)
We say that
u\in\mathbb{H}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{q,p}(T)
if there exists a defining sequence of functions
u_{n}\in C_{p}^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
u_{n}
converges to
u
in
H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{q,p}(T)
and
\partial_{t}u_{n}
is Cauchy in
H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{q,p}(T)
. In this case, we define
\partial_{t}u
as the limit of
\partial_{t}u_{n}
in
H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{q,p}(T)
. For
u\in\mathbb{H}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{q,p}(T)
, we naturally define

\|u\|_{\mathbb{H}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{q,p}(T)}:=\|\partial_{t}u\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{q,p}(T)}+\|u\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{q,p}(T)}.

(v)
For
u\in\mathbb{H}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{q,p}(T)
, we say that
u\in\mathbb{H}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{q,p,0}(T)
if there is a defining sequence
u_{n}
so that

u_{n}(0,x)=0\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

It is worth noting that if
\vec{\phi}(\lambda)=(\lambda,\cdots,\lambda)
, then
H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}
corresponds to the classical Bessel potential space
H^{\gamma}_{p}
.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that the embedding
H_{p}^{2n}\subseteq H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},2n}
is continuous for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, as shown in
[
43
, Remark 3]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.

(i)
The space
H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}
is a Banach space.

(ii)
For any
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, the map
(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})^{\mu/2}
is an isometry from
H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{p}
to
H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma-\mu}_{p}
.

(iii)
If
\mu>0
, then we have continuous embeddings
H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+\mu}\subset H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}
in the sense that

\|u\|_{H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}}\leq C\|u\|_{H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+\mu}},

where the constant
C
is independent of
u
.

(iv)
For any
u\in H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{p}
, we have

\left(\|u\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{p}}+\|(\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})u\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{p}}\right)\simeq\|u\|_{H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}}.

Proof.

The first and second assertions are direct consequences of the definition. Recall that the function
\psi(\xi):=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\phi_{i}(|\xi_{i}|^{2})

is a continuous negative definite function since each
\phi_{i}(|\xi_{i}|^{2})
is continuous and negative definite (see
e.g.

[
22
]
for detail). Hence, the third assertion comes from
[
22
, Theorem 2.3.1]
. Finally, the last assertion can be obtained by using the second assertion and
[
22
, Theorem 2.2.7]
.
∎

By following the proof of
[
34
, Lemma 2.7]
, replacing time non-local operator therein with usual derivative, and using Remark
2.5
, and Lemma
2.6
, we have the following properties of solution spaces.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
1<p,q<\infty
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and
0<T<\infty
.

(i)
The spaces
H_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}(T)
and
\mathbb{H}_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}(T)
are Banach spaces.

(ii)
The space
\mathbb{H}_{q,p,0}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}(T)
is a closed subspace of
\mathbb{H}_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}(T)
.

(iii)

C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+})
is dense in
\mathbb{H}_{q,p,0}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}(T)
.

(iv)
For any
\gamma,\nu\in\mathbb{R}
,
(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})^{\nu/2}:\mathbb{H}_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}(T)\to\mathbb{H}_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma-\nu+2}(T)
is an isometry, and for any

Here is the main result of this article.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
1<p,q<\infty
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and
0<T<\infty
.
Suppose that
\vec{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{\ell})
is a vector of Bernstein functions satisfying Assumption
2.2
with drift
\vec{b}_{0}=(b_{01},\dots,b_{0\ell})
and vector of Lévy measures
\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y})
defined in (
2.4
).
For vectors of measurable functions

\vec{a}(t,\vec{y}):=(a_{1}(t,y_{1}),\cdots,a_{\ell}(t,y_{\ell})),\quad\vec{b}(t):=(b_{1}(t),\cdots,b_{\ell}(t))

satisfying

(2.7)

a_{i}(t,y_{i}),\in[c_{1},c_{1}^{-1}],\quad b_{i}(t)\in[c_{1}b_{0i},c^{-1}_{1}b_{0i}]\quad\forall\,i=1,\cdots,\ell,

with a positive constant
c_{1}
, define the operator
\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}(t)
as

\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}(t)h(\vec{x}):=\vec{b}(t)\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}}h(\vec{x})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(h(\vec{x}+\vec{y})-h(\vec{x})-\nabla_{\vec{x}}h(\vec{x})\cdot\vec{y}\mathbf{1}_{|\vec{y}|\leq 1})\vec{a}(t,\vec{y})\cdot\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y}).

Then for any
f\in H_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}(T)
, the equation

(2.8)

\partial_{t}u(t,\vec{x})=\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}(t)u(t,\vec{x})+f(t,\vec{x}),\quad t>0,\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}\,;\quad u(0,\vec{x})=0,\quad\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}

admits a unique solution
u
in the class
\mathbb{H}_{q,p,0}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}(T)
, and we have

\|u\|_{\mathbb{H}_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}(T)}\leq C\|f\|_{H_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}(T)},

where
C=C(\vec{b}_{0},d,\delta_{0},c_{0},c_{1},p,q,\gamma,T)
.
Moreover,

(2.9)

\|(\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})u\|_{H_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}(T)}+\|\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}u\|_{H_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}(T)}\leq C_{0}\|f\|_{H_{q,p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}(T)},

where
C_{0}=C_{0}(\vec{b}_{0},d,\delta_{0},c_{0},c_{1},p,q,\gamma)
.

Remark 2.9
.

Note that (
2.7
) means that if
b_{0i}=0
, then we assume that
b_{i}(t)\equiv 0
.

Remark 2.10
.

In Theorem
2.8
, we establish the existence, uniqueness and maximal regularity of the solution
u
to the equation (
2.8
) in the function space
\mathbb{H}^{\vec{\phi},2}_{q,p,0}(T)
. In this remark, we show that a lower regularity of the solution can be also established through the use of the Marcinkiewicz multiplier theorem (
e.g.

[
24
, Theorem 6.2.4]
).

(
i
) Let
u\in H^{\vec{\phi},2}_{p}
and

\phi_{1}(\Delta_{x_{1}})^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\phi_{\ell}(\Delta_{x_{\ell}})^{\alpha_{\ell}}(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})^{-1}u(\vec{x}):=\mathcal{F}_{d}^{-1}[m\mathcal{F}_{d}[u]](\vec{x}),

where
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{\ell}
are nonnegative constants satisfying
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{\ell}\leq 1
, and

(2.10)

\displaystyle m(\vec{\xi}):=\frac{(\phi_{1}(|\xi_{1}|^{2}))^{\alpha_{1}}\times\dots\times(\phi_{\ell}(|\xi_{\ell}|^{2}))^{\alpha_{\ell}}}{1+\phi_{1}(|\xi_{1}|^{2})+\dots+\phi_{\ell}(|\xi_{\ell}|^{2})}:=\frac{A(\vec{\xi})}{B(\vec{\xi})}:=A(\vec{\xi})H(\vec{\xi}).

Using the fact that
(\phi_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}
is also a Bernstein function (see
e.g.

[
47
, Corollary 3.8 (iii)]
) and inequality

(2.11)

\displaystyle\lambda^{n}|\phi^{(n)}(\lambda)|\leq C(n)\phi(\lambda),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\lambda>0,

we can derive the following inequality for
1\leq k\leq d
, where
j_{1},\cdots,j_{k}\in\{1,\cdots,d\}
:

(2.12)

\displaystyle|D_{\xi^{j_{1}}}\cdots D_{\xi^{j_{k}}}A(\vec{\xi})|

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha_{j_{1}},\cdots,\alpha_{j_{k}},d,k)A(\vec{\xi})\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\xi^{j_{i}}|^{-1}.

Here, the inequality (
2.11
) is derived from (
2.1
) and the inequality
t^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\leq N(n)(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t})
. Similarly, we have

(2.13)

\displaystyle|D_{\xi^{j_{1}}}\cdots D_{\xi^{j_{k}}}H(\vec{\xi})|\leq C(d,k)H(\vec{\xi})\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\xi^{j_{i}}|^{-1}.

Then, applying the product rule of differentiation to (
2.10
), and then using Young’s inequality, we obtain the following inequality for any
1\leq k\leq\ell
:

(2.14)

|D_{\xi^{j_{1}}}\cdots D_{\xi^{j_{k}}}m(\vec{\xi})|\leq C(\alpha_{j_{1}},\cdots,\alpha_{j_{k}},d,k)\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\xi^{j_{i}}|^{-1}.

Thus, according to
[
24
, Corollary 6.2.5]
, we have

\displaystyle\|\phi_{1}(\Delta_{x_{1}})^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\phi_{\ell}(\Delta_{x_{\ell}})^{\alpha_{\ell}}u\|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle=\|(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})\phi_{1}(\Delta_{x_{1}})^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\phi_{\ell}(\Delta_{x_{\ell}})^{\alpha_{\ell}}(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})^{-1}u\|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})u\|_{L_{p}}=\|u\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},2}_{p}}<\infty.

Also, if
\alpha_{i}=1
for some
i=1,\dots,\ell
, then by replacing
B(\vec{\xi})
by
\phi_{1}(|\xi_{1}|^{2})+\dots+\phi_{\ell}(|\xi_{\ell}|^{2})
, and following the above argument, we can check that

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|\phi_{i}(\Delta_{x_{i}})u\|_{L_{p}}\leq C\|\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}}u\|_{L_{p}}\quad\forall\,i=1,\dots,\ell.

(
ii
) Assume that
\phi_{n}
(
n\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
) satisfies

(2.16)

\sup_{r>1}\frac{\phi_{n}(r)}{\phi_{i}(r)}\leq C(i,n)\quad\forall\,i=1,\dots,\ell,

and define

m_{n}(\vec{\xi}):=\frac{\phi_{n}(|\vec{\xi}|^{2})}{1+\phi_{1}(|\xi_{1}|^{2})+\dots+\phi_{\ell}(|\xi_{\ell}|^{2})}.

Then like (
2.12
), we see that
A_{n}(\vec{\xi}):=\phi_{n}(|\vec{\xi}|^{2})

satisfies

|D_{\xi^{j_{1}}}\cdots D_{\xi^{j_{k}}}A_{n}(\vec{\xi})|\leq CA_{n}(\vec{\xi})\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\xi^{j_{i}}|^{-1},

where
j_{1},\cdots,j_{k}\in\{1,\cdots,d\}
. Combining this with (
2.13
), we see that
m_{n}
satisfies

|D_{\xi^{j_{1}}}\cdots D_{\xi^{j_{k}}}m_{n}(\vec{\xi})|\leq C(d,k)m_{n}(\vec{\xi})\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\xi^{j_{i}}|^{-1}.

It is obvious that

(2.17)

|D_{\xi^{j_{1}}}\cdots D_{\xi^{j_{k}}}m_{n}(\vec{\xi})|\leq C(d,k)\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\xi^{j_{i}}|^{-1}\quad\forall\,|\vec{\xi}|\leq 2.

Now, suppose that
|\vec{\xi}|\geq 2
and let
J
be the subset of
\{1,\dots,\ell\}
consisting of entries
j
such that

1\leq|\vec{\xi}|/|\xi_{j}|\leq 2
. Then by (
2.6
) and (
2.16
), we have

\displaystyle m_{n}(\vec{\xi})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{|J|}\sum_{j\in J}\frac{\phi_{n}(|\xi_{j}|^{2})}{1+\phi_{1}(|\xi_{1}|^{2})+\dots+\phi_{\ell}(|\xi_{\ell}|^{2})}\leq\frac{4}{|J|}\sum_{j\in J}\frac{\phi_{n}(|\xi_{j}|^{2})}{1+\phi_{j}(|\xi_{j}|^{2})}\leq C.

Combining this with (
2.17
), we check that
m_{n}
also satisfies (
2.14
). Therefore, like (
2.15
) we have

\displaystyle\|\phi_{n}(\Delta)u\|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle=\|(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})\phi_{n}(\Delta)(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})^{-1}u\|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})u\|_{L_{p}}=\|u\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},2}_{p}}<\infty.

This shows that
\phi_{n}(\Delta)u\in L_{p}
. For example, if we let
\phi_{i}(r)=r^{\delta_{i}}

We would like to make a remark regarding the

It is worth noting that when

To illustrate this point, let us consider the simple case where

\phi_{1}(r)=r^{\delta_{1}},\quad\phi_{2}(r)=r^{\delta_{2}},\quad d_{1}=d_{2}=1,\quad 0<\delta_{1},\delta_{2}<1.

Then we claim that the
3
-dimensional Fourier multiplier

\displaystyle m(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):=\frac{|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}}+|\xi_{2}|^{2\delta_{2}}}{i\tau+|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}}+|\xi_{2}|^{2\delta_{2}}}

does not satisfy assumptions of both Mikhlin’s and Marcinkiewicz’s multiplier theorem simultaneously.
For these, taking the derivative with respect to
\xi_{1}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{\xi_{1}}}m(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\frac{2\delta_{1}|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}-2}\xi_{1}(2\tau^{2}(|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}}+|\xi_{2}|^{2\delta_{2}})+i\tau((|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}}+|\xi_{2}|^{2\delta_{2}})^{2}-\tau^{2}))}{|\tau|^{2}+(|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}}+|\xi_{2}|^{2\delta_{2}})^{2}}.

Using this, we see that

\displaystyle\left|\Re\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial{\xi_{1}}}m(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4\delta_{1}|\tau|^{2}|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}-1}(|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}}+|\xi_{2}|^{2\delta_{2}})}{(|\tau|+|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}}+|\xi_{2}|^{2\delta_{2}})^{2}}.

If

1<R<|(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|=\left(|\tau|^{2}+|\xi_{1}|^{2}+|\xi_{2}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}<2R,

then

\left|\Re\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial{\xi_{1}}}m(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\right]\right|\geq C(\delta_{1})R^{-2}|\tau|^{2}|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}-1}(|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}}+|\xi_{2}|^{2\delta_{2}}).

Hence

\displaystyle R^{-\frac{3}{2}+1}\left(\int_{R<|(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|<2R}\left|\Re\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial{\xi_{1}}}m(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\right]\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq CR^{-\frac{5}{2}}\left(\int_{\frac{5R}{4\sqrt{3}}<|\tau|,|\xi_{1}|,|\xi_{2}|<\frac{7R}{4\sqrt{3}}}|\tau|^{4}|\xi_{1}|^{4\delta_{1}-2}(|\xi_{1}|^{2\delta_{1}}+|\xi_{2}|^{2\delta_{2}})^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=CR^{2\delta_{1}-1}(R^{2\delta_{1}}+R^{2\delta_{2}}).

Therefore, if
\delta_{1}>1/4
or
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}>1/2
, then the last term above goes to infinity as

. Hence,
m(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
does not satisfy the condition for the Mikhlin multiplier theorem (see
e.g.

[
24
, Theorem 6.2.7]
). Similarly, for
2^{j}\leq|\tau|,|\xi_{2}|<2^{j+1}

\displaystyle\int_{2^{j}\leq|\xi_{1}|<2^{j+1}}\left|\Re\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial{\xi_{1}}}m(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\right]\right|\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\geq C2^{(2\delta_{1}-1)j}(2^{2\delta_{1}j}+2^{2\delta_{2}j}).

This certainly implies that
m(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
does not satisfy the condition for the Marcikiewicz multiplier theorem (see
e.g.

[
24
, Theorem 6.2.4]
).

. However, in order to obtain BMO-
L_{\infty}
estimates, we utilize the upper bounds of the heat kernel associated with the IASBM. This is where Assumption
2.2
becomes necessary. In other words, the assumption ensures that the heat kernel has certain decay properties, which are crucial in the derivation of the desired estimates. Therefore, in the subsequent sections, we assume that Assumption
2.2
holds.

3.1.
Estimations of IASBM’s heat kernel

In this subsection, we focus on estimating the upper bounds of the heat kernel for the IASBM
\vec{X}=(X^{1},\cdots,X^{\ell})
to derive the desired BMO-
L_{\infty}
estimates.
Since each component process
X^{i}
is independent, the heat kernel of
\vec{X}
, denoted by
p(t,\vec{x})
, can be expressed as a product of the heat kernels of component processes
X^{1},\cdots,X^{\ell}
:

(3.1)

p(t,\vec{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}p_{i}(t,x_{i}),\quad\forall(t,\vec{x})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}},

where
p_{i}
is the heat kernel of
X^{i}
. Therefore, by obtaining upper bounds of the heat kernels for each component process, we can obtain upper bounds for the IASBM.

Before delving further into the main result of this subsection, we first investigate some fundamental properties of the heat kernel for the component processes. Assumption
2.2
implies that

\exp\left(-t\phi_{i}(|\xi_{i}|^{2})\right)\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})\cap L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}),\quad i=1,\cdots,\ell,

which in turn yields that
X^{i}
has the transition density given by

(3.2)

p_{i}(t,x_{i}):=p_{d_{i}}(t,x_{i})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d_{i}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}\mathrm{e}^{i\xi_{i}\cdot x_{i}}\mathrm{e}^{-t\phi_{i}(|\xi_{i}|^{2})}\mathrm{d}\xi_{i}.

By
[
28
, Section 30.1]
, we know that for any
t>0
and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle p_{i}(t,x_{i})=\int_{(0,\infty)}(4\pi s)^{-d_{i}/2}\exp\left(-\frac{|x_{i}|^{2}}{4s}\right)\eta^{i}_{t}(\mathrm{d}s),

where
\eta^{i}_{t}(\mathrm{d}s)
is the distribution of
S^{i}_{t}
. This implies that
X^{i}_{t}
is rotationally invariant in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
. For convenience, we introduce the notation
p_{i}(t,r):=p_{i}(t,x_{i})
if
r=|x_{i}|
. Furthermore, as shown in (
3.3
), we see that
r\mapsto p_{i}(t,r)
is a decreasing function. In order to establish upper bounds for the heat kernels of each component process, we provide the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let Assumption
2.2
hold and
\nu>0
.

(i) Then for each
i=1,\cdots,\ell
,
there exists a constant
C_{i}=C(c_{0},\delta_{0},\nu)
such that

(3.4)

\int_{\lambda^{-1}}^{\infty}r^{-1}(\phi_{i}(r^{-2}))^{\nu}\mathrm{d}r\leq C_{i}(\phi_{i}(\lambda^{2}))^{\nu},\quad\forall\,\lambda>0.

(ii) Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

(3.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}\left(t^{-\nu k}\left(\phi_{i}^{-1}(t^{-1})\right)^{d_{i}/2}\wedge t^{\nu-\nu k}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}}}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{i}\leq C_{i}t^{-\nu k},

where
C_{i}=C_{i}(d_{i},c_{0},\delta_{0},k,\nu)
.

Proof.

(i) By the change of variables and (
2.6
),

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda^{-1}}^{\infty}r^{-1}(\phi_{i}(r^{-2}))^{\nu}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-1}(\phi_{i}(\lambda^{2}r^{-2}))^{\nu}\frac{(\phi(\lambda^{2}))^{\nu}}{(\phi(\lambda^{2}))^{\nu}}\mathrm{d}r\leq C_{i}(\phi_{i}(\lambda^{2}))^{\nu}.

(ii) We can observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}\left(t^{-\nu k}\left(\phi_{i}^{-1}(t^{-1})\right)^{d_{i}/2}\wedge t^{\nu-\nu k}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}}}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{i}

\displaystyle=C_{i}\Big(\int_{|x_{i}|\leq(\phi^{-1}_{i}(t^{-1}))^{-1/2}}t^{-\nu k}\left(\phi_{i}^{-1}(t^{-1})\right)^{d_{i}/2}\mathrm{d}x_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{|x_{i}|>(\phi^{-1}_{i}(t^{-1}))^{-1/2}}t^{\nu-\nu k}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}}}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}t^{-\nu k}+C_{i}\int_{(\phi^{-1}_{i}(t^{-1}))^{-1/2}}^{\infty}t^{\nu-\nu k}(\phi_{i}(\rho^{-2}))^{\nu}\rho^{-1}\mathrm{d}\rho

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}t^{-\nu k}+C_{i}t^{\nu-\nu k}(\phi_{i}(\phi_{i}^{-1}(t^{-1})))^{\nu}\leq C_{i}t^{-\nu k},

where for the third inequality, we used (
3.4
). The lemma is proved.
∎

Now we present upper bounds of the heat kernel for each component processes.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
i=1,\cdots\ell
and Assumption
2.2
hold.

(i) For any
m,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
\nu\in(0,1)
, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle|\phi_{i}(\Delta_{x_{i}})^{\nu k}D^{m}_{x_{i}}p_{i}(t,x_{i})|\leq C_{i}\left(t^{-\nu k}\left(\phi_{i}^{-1}(t^{-1})\right)^{\frac{d_{i}+m}{2}}\wedge t^{\nu-\nu k}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}}\right),

where the constant
C_{i}
depends only on
c_{0},\delta_{0},d_{i},m,k,\nu
. In particular, we have

(3.7)

|\phi_{i}(\Delta_{x_{i}})^{k}D^{m}_{x_{i}}p_{i}(t,x_{i})|\leq C_{i}\left(t^{-k}\left(\phi_{i}^{-1}(t^{-1})\right)^{\frac{d_{i}+m}{2}}\wedge t^{\frac{1}{2}-k}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{1/2}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}}\right),

(ii) For any
k=0,1,\dots
, we have

(3.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}|\phi_{i}(\Delta_{x_{i}})^{k}p_{i}(t,x_{i})|\mathrm{d}x_{i}\leq C_{i}t^{-k},

where the constant
C_{i}
depends only on
c_{0},\delta_{0},d_{i},k
.

Proof.

If we have (
3.6
), then by plugging
\nu=1/2
and
k=2k
in (
3.6
), we obtain (
3.7
).
Similarly we also obtain (
3.8
) due to (
3.5
). Hence, we only focus on proving (
3.6
). By following
[
34
, Lemma A.3]
with
\phi^{\nu k}
in place of
\phi
therein, we have the estimation

|\phi_{i}(\Delta_{x_{i}})^{\nu k}D^{m}_{x_{i}}p_{i}(t,x_{i})|\leq C_{i}t^{-\nu k}\left(\phi_{i}^{-1}(t^{-1})\right)^{\frac{d_{i}+m}{2}}.

Therefore, to finish the proof, we may assume

t^{-\nu k}(\phi^{-1}_{i}(t^{-1}))^{\frac{d_{i}+m}{2}}\geq t^{\nu-\nu k}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d+m}}

(equivalently,
t\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2})\leq 1
) and prove

(3.9)

\displaystyle|\phi_{i}(\Delta_{x_{i}})^{\nu k}D^{m}_{x_{i}}p_{i}(t,\cdot)(x_{i})|\leq C_{i}t^{\nu-\nu k}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d+m}}.

We divide the proof of (
3.9
) into two steps.

Step 1.

k=0
.

When
k=0
, then (
3.6
) is a direct consequence of
[
34
, Lemma A.2 (ii)]
, which shows

\displaystyle|D^{m}_{x_{i}}p_{i}(t,x_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}\sum_{0\leq n\leq\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}|x_{i}|^{m-2n}\left((\phi_{i}^{-1}(t^{-1}))^{d_{i}/2+m-n}\wedge\frac{t\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2})}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+2(m-n)}}\right)

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}\sum_{0\leq n\leq\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}|x_{i}|^{m-2n}\frac{t\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2})}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+2(m-n)}}\leq C_{i}\frac{(t\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}}.

For the remaining part
k\geq 1
, we use the mathematical induction argument.

Step 2.

k\geq 1
.

Now, suppose that (
3.9
) (and hence (
3.6
)) holds for
k=k_{0}-1\geq 0
.

By
[
34
, Lemma A.1]
, we see that
\phi_{i}^{\nu}
is also a Bernstein function with Lévy measure
\bar{\mu}
(recall (
2.2
)), and

\displaystyle\phi_{i}^{\nu}(\Delta_{x_{i}})f(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{d_{i}}[-\phi_{i}(|\cdot|^{2})^{\nu}\mathcal{F}_{d_{i}}[f]]

(3.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}\left(f(x_{i}+y_{i})-f(x_{i})-\nabla_{x_{i}}f(x_{i})\cdot y_{i}\mathbf{1}_{|y_{i}|\leq r}\right)\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(|y_{i}|)\mathrm{d}y_{i}\quad\forall\,r>0,

where

(3.12)

\displaystyle\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(r):=\int_{0}^{\infty}(4\pi t)^{-d_{i}/2}\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/4t}\bar{\mu}(\mathrm{d}t),

and it satisfies

(3.13)

\displaystyle\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(r)\leq c(d_{i})r^{-d_{i}}(\phi_{i}(r^{-2}))^{\nu}\quad\forall\,r>0.

For simplicity, denote
\phi_{i}(\Delta_{x_{i}})^{\nu k}D^{m}_{x_{i}}p_{i}(t,x_{i}):=P^{k}_{i,m}(t,x_{i})
(
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
). Then using (
3.1
) with
r=|x_{i}|/2
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\left|P^{k_{0}}_{i,m}(t,x_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq|P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,m}(t,x_{i})|\int_{|y_{i}|>\frac{|x_{i}|}{2}}\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(|y_{i}|)\mathrm{d}y_{i}+\left|\int_{|y_{i}|>\frac{|x_{i}|}{2}}P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,m}(t,x_{i}+y_{i})\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(|y_{i}|)\mathrm{d}y_{i}\right|

\displaystyle+\int_{\frac{|x_{i}|}{2}>|y_{i}|}\int_{0}^{1}\left|P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,m+1}(t,x_{i}+sy_{i})-P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,m+1}(t,x_{i})\right||y_{i}|\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(|y_{i}|)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y_{i}

\displaystyle=:|P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,m}(t,x_{i})|\times I+II_{k_{0}}+III_{k_{0}}.

By (
3.13
), (
3.4
), and (
2.6
), we have

(3.15)

\displaystyle I\leq C_{i}\int_{\frac{|x_{i}|}{2}}^{\infty}r^{-1}(\phi_{i}(r^{-2}))^{\nu}\mathrm{d}r\leq C_{i}(\phi_{i}(4|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}\leq C_{i}(\phi(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}.

This together with (
3.1
) yields (recall we assume
t\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2})\leq 1
and the assertion holds for
k=k_{0}-1
)

\displaystyle|P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,m}(t,x_{i})|\times I\leq C_{i}t^{\nu-\nu(k_{0}-1)}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{2\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}}\leq C_{i}t^{\nu-\nu k_{0}}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}}.

For
III_{k_{0}}
, by the fundamental theorem of calculus and (
3.1
),

\displaystyle III_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq C(d_{i})\int_{\frac{|x_{i}|}{2}>|y_{i}|}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left|P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,m+2}(t,x_{i}+usy_{i})\right||y_{i}|^{2}\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(|y_{i}|)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y_{i}

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}t^{\nu-\nu(k_{0}-1)}\int_{\frac{|x_{i}|}{2}>|y_{i}|}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(\phi(|x_{i}+usy_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}+usy_{i}|^{d_{i}+m+2}}|y_{i}|^{2}\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(|y_{i}|)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y_{i}

(3.16)

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}\frac{t^{-\nu(k_{0}-1)}(t\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m+2}}\int_{|x_{i}|/2>|y_{i}|}|y_{i}|^{2}\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(|y_{i}|)\mathrm{d}y_{i}.

For the last inequality above, we used
|x_{i}+usy_{i}|\geq|x_{i}|/2
, and (
2.6
). By (
3.13
), and (
2.6
) with
r=|x_{i}|^{-2}
and
R=\rho^{-2}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{|x_{i}|}{2}>|y_{i}|}|y_{i}|^{2}\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(|y_{i}|)\mathrm{d}y_{i}

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}\int_{0}^{|x_{i}|}\rho(\phi_{i}(\rho^{-2}))^{\nu}\mathrm{d}\rho

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}|x_{i}|^{2}(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}.

Therefore, it follows that

III_{k_{0}}\leq C_{i}\frac{t^{-\nu(k_{0}-1)}(t\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m+2}}|x_{i}|^{2}(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}\leq C_{i}t^{\nu-\nu k_{0}}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}}.

Now we estimate
II_{k_{0}}
. By using the integration by parts
m
-times, we have

\displaystyle II_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\int_{|y_{i}|=\frac{|x_{i}|}{2}}\left|\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{n}}\bar{j}_{d_{i}}\right)(|y_{i}|)P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,m-1-n}(t,x_{i}+y_{i})\right|S_{i}(\mathrm{d}y_{i})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{|y_{i}|>\frac{|x_{i}|}{2}}\left|\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{m}}\bar{j}_{d_{i}}\right)(|y_{i}|)P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,0}(t,x_{i}+y_{i})\right|\mathrm{d}y_{i}=:II^{\prime}_{k_{0}}+II^{\prime\prime}_{k_{0}},

where
S_{i}(\mathrm{d}y_{i})
is the surface measure defined on
\partial B_{|x_{i}|/2}
. Differentiating
\bar{j}_{d_{i}}
via (
3.12
), and then using (
3.13
) for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we get

(3.18)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{n}}\bar{j}_{d_{i}}(\rho)\right|\leq C_{i}\sum_{n-2l\geq 0,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\rho^{n-2l}|\bar{j}_{d_{i}+2(n-l)}(\rho)|\leq C_{i}\rho^{-d_{i}-n}(\phi_{i}(\rho^{-2}))^{\nu}.

Combining (
3.18
) and (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle II^{\prime}_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Big(t^{\nu-\nu(k_{0}-1)}|x_{i}|^{d_{i}-1}|x|^{-d_{i}-k}|x|^{-d_{i}-m+1+k}(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{2\nu}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}t^{\nu-\nu k_{0}}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}}

when
t\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2})\leq 1
. Also, using the fact that
\bar{j}_{d_{i}}
is decreasing (recall (
3.12
)), and (
3.18
), we have

\displaystyle II^{\prime\prime}_{k_{0}}\leq C_{i}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}|P^{k_{0}-1}_{i,0}(t,x_{i}+y_{i})|\mathrm{d}y_{i}\leq C_{i}t^{\nu-\nu k_{0}}\frac{(\phi(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}},

where for the last inequality we used (
3.5
) for
k=k_{0}-1
. Therefore

II_{k_{0}}\leq II^{\prime}_{k_{0}}+II^{\prime\prime}_{k_{0}}\leq C_{i}t^{\nu-\nu k_{0}}\frac{(\phi_{i}(|x_{i}|^{-2}))^{\nu}}{|x_{i}|^{d_{i}+m}}.

Hence, we obtain (
3.9
) for

, and thus by the mathematical induction argument, we prove it for all

. The theorem is proved.
∎

3.2.
BMO estimation of solution

In this subsection, we establish the BMO-
L_{\infty}
estimation of the operator
\mathcal{G}
:

(3.19)

\|\mathcal{G}f\|_{BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})},\quad\forall f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})\cap L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1}).

We assume that Assumption
2.2
holds and that the operator
\mathcal{G}
can be expressed as a sum of
\ell
terms, each of which we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{i}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}f(t,\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{t>0}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}p_{j}(t-s,x_{j}-y_{j})\right)q_{i}(t-s,x_{i}-y)f(s,\vec{y})\mathrm{d}\vec{y}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=:\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{G}_{i}f(t,\vec{x}),

where
q_{i}(t,x_{i}):=\phi(\Delta_{x_{i}})p_{i}
. To prove (
3.19
), we need to show that

\|\mathcal{G}_{i}f\|_{BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})},\quad i=1,\cdots,\ell.

We will simplify the proofs by considering only the case
\ell=2
, since the same arguments can be applied for
\ell\geq 3
. Due to the symmetry of the operators
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, we only need to consider the operator
\mathcal{G}_{1}
in the rest of this section.

We begin by the notion of BMO spaces that will be used throughout this section. We define two increasing functions
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
by

(3.20)

\kappa_{i}(b):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\phi^{-1}_{i}(b^{-1})}},\quad b>0,\quad i=1,2.

Here,
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are Bernstein functions that satisfy Assumption
2.2
. We also define
B^{i}_{r}(x_{i})
(
i=1,2
) to be an open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
with radius
r>0
and center
x_{i}
. For
(t,\vec{x})=(t,x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and
b>0
, we denote

(3.21)

Q_{b}(t,x_{1},x_{2}):=(t-b,,t+b)\times B^{1}_{\kappa_{1}(b)}(x_{1})\times B^{2}_{\kappa_{2}(b)}(x_{2}),

and
Q_{b}=Q_{b}(0,0,0)
and
B^{i}_{\kappa_{i}(b)}=B^{i}_{\kappa_{i}(b)}(0)
for
i=1,2
.
For measurable subsets
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with finite measure and locally integrable functions
h
, we define the mean value of
h
over
Q
as

h_{Q}:=-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q}h(s,\vec{y})\mathrm{d}\vec{y}\mathrm{d}s:=-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q}h(s,y_{1},y_{2})\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}s:=\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}h(s,y_{1},y_{2})\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}s,

where
|Q|
is the Lebesgue measure of
Q
.
For a locally integrable function
h
on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we define the BMO semi-norm of
h
on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
as

\|h\|_{BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}:=\|h^{\#}\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}

where

\displaystyle h^{\#}(t,x):=\sup_{(t,x)\in Q_{b}(r,z)}-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}(r,z)}|h(s,y)-h_{Q_{b}(r,z)}|\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y.

Remark 3.4
.

If
\ell>2
, we can naturally define
Q_{b}(t,\vec{x})
as

Q_{b}(t,\vec{x})=Q_{b}(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})=(t-b,t+b)\times\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}B^{i}_{\kappa_{i}(b)}(x_{i}),

where
\kappa_{i}(b)
for other
i
is defined as in (
3.20
). Then, by following the rest of this section, we can establish the
L_{q}(L_{p})
-boundedness of
\mathcal{G}
when
\ell>2
.

Here is the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 3.5
.

For any
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})\cap L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
,

(3.22)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}_{1}f\|_{BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C(d,c_{0},\delta_{0})\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}.

Proof.

The proof is quite standard if we have the following Lemma;

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
and
b>0
. Then,

\displaystyle-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f(t,\vec{x})-\mathcal{G}_{1}f(s,\vec{y})|\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\vec{y}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})},

where
C
depends only on
d,c_{0}
and
\delta_{0}
.

Note that for any
(t_{0},\vec{x}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, by change of variables, we see that

\displaystyle-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}(t_{0},\vec{x}_{0})}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f(t,\vec{x})-(\mathcal{G}_{1}f)_{Q_{b}(t_{0},\vec{x}_{0})}|\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\vec{x}=-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}\tilde{f}(t,\vec{x})-(\mathcal{G}_{1}\tilde{f})_{Q_{b}}|\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\vec{x},

where
\tilde{f}(t,\vec{x}):=f(t+t_{0},\vec{x}+\vec{x}_{0})
. Using this and the invariance of
L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
-norm under translation, we see that it is enough to prove

(3.23)

-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f(t,\vec{x})-(\mathcal{G}_{1}f)_{Q_{b}}|\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})},\quad b>0.

Recall that we already have (
3.23
) due to Lemma
3.6
if
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
.

Now we consider the general case,
i.e.
,
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})\cap L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
.
We choose a sequence of functions
f_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
such that
\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{n}\to\mathcal{G}_{1}f
for almost, and
\|f_{n}\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}
.
Then by Fatou’s lemma,

\displaystyle-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f(t,\vec{x})-(\mathcal{G}_{1}f)_{Q_{b}}|\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\vec{x}

\displaystyle\leq-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f(t,\vec{x})-\mathcal{G}_{1}f(s,\vec{y})|\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\vec{y}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{n}(t,\vec{x})-\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{n}(s,\vec{y})|\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\vec{y}

\displaystyle\leq C\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|f_{n}\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}.

The theorem is proved.
∎

Step 1.
In Step 1, we prove

\displaystyle-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}I_{1}(t,s,\vec{x},\vec{y})\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}t\vec{y}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle:=-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{1}(t,\vec{x})-\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{1}(s,\vec{y})|\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}t\vec{y}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}.

Recall that
f_{1}
is supported in
(-3b,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}
. Since

-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{1}(t,\vec{x})-\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{1}(s,\vec{y})|\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}t\vec{y}\mathrm{d}s\leq 2-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{1}(t,\vec{x})|\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}t,

General case.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}}\left|\phi_{1}(\Delta_{x_{1}})p(t-s,\vec{x}-\vec{y})f(s,\vec{y})\right|\mathrm{d}\vec{y}

\displaystyle I_{1,1}=\int_{(B^{1}_{2\kappa_{1}(b)})^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}|q_{1}(t-s,x_{1}-y_{1})p_{2}(t-s,x_{2}-y_{2})f(s,\vec{y})|\mathrm{d}\vec{y},

Also, for

3.7
), (
3.8
) and (

\displaystyle I_{1,2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}{\bf 1}_{|s|\leq 3b}(t-s)^{-1}\int_{(\phi_{2}^{-1}(b^{-1}))^{-1/2}}^{\infty}(t-s)^{1/2}\frac{(\phi_{2}(\rho^{-2}))^{1/2}}{\rho^{d_{2}}}\rho^{d_{2}-1}\mathrm{d}\rho

Note that if

Step 2.
In Step 2, we prove the estimation of
I_{2}
;

\displaystyle-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{Q_{b}}|\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{2}(t,\vec{x})-\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{2}(s,\vec{x})|\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}t\vec{y}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}.

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{2}(t_{1},\vec{x})-\mathcal{G}_{1}f_{2}(t_{2},\vec{x})|=\Big|\int_{-\infty}^{-2b}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}A(t_{1},t_{2},s,\vec{x},\vec{y})\,f(s,\vec{y})\mathrm{d}\vec{y}\mathrm{d}s\Big|,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{-\infty}^{-2b}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}}A(t_{1},t_{2},s,\vec{x},\vec{y})f(s,\vec{y})\mathrm{d}\vec{y}\mathrm{d}s\Big|

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{\infty}\int_{\kappa_{1}(s)}^{\infty}\frac{(\phi_{1}(\rho^{-2}))^{1/2}}{s^{1/2}\rho^{2}}\mathrm{d}\rho\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{b}^{\infty}s^{-1/2}\left(\phi_{1}^{-1}(s^{-1})\right)^{1/2}\int_{\kappa_{1}(s)}^{\infty}\frac{(\phi_{1}(\rho^{-2}))^{1/2}}{\rho}\mathrm{d}\rho\mathrm{d}s

(3.29)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{b}^{\infty}\left(\phi_{1}^{-1}(s^{-1})\right)^{1/2}s^{-1}\mathrm{d}s.

Second, it is easy to see that

(3.30)

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{\infty}\int_{\kappa_{1}(b)}^{\kappa_{1}(s)}\rho^{d_{1}-1}\frac{\phi_{1}^{-1}(s^{-1})^{(d_{1}+1)/2}}{s}\mathrm{d}\rho\mathrm{d}s\leq C\int_{b}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\phi_{1}^{-1}(s^{-1})\right)^{1/2}}{s}\mathrm{d}s.

Note that if

2.6
) with

and

\phi_{1}^{-1}(s^{-1})\leq bs^{-1}\phi_{1}^{-1}(b^{-1})
.
Therefore,

(3.31)

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\phi_{1}^{-1}(s^{-1})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{s}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\phi_{1}^{-1}(b^{-1})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}b^{1/2}\int_{b}^{\infty}s^{-3/2}\mathrm{d}s=C(\kappa_{1}(b))^{-1}.

Combining this with (
3.29
) and (
3.30
), and going back to (
3.28
), we get

I_{3,1,1}(t,\vec{x},\vec{z})\leq C\kappa_{1}(b)\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}(\kappa_{1}(b))^{-1}=C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}.

For
I_{3,1,2}
, using (
3.8
) and following the argument in (
3.28
), we have

(3.32)

\displaystyle I_{3,1,2}(t,\vec{x},\vec{z})\leq C\kappa_{2}(b)\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\int_{b}^{\infty}s^{-1}\int_{|y_{2}|\geq\kappa_{2}(b)}|\nabla p_{2}(t-s,y_{2})|\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Using (
3.7

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{\infty}s^{-1}\int_{|y_{2}|\geq\kappa_{2}(b)}|\nabla p_{2}(t-s,y_{2})|\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{b}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\kappa_{2}(s)}^{\infty}\frac{(\phi_{2}(\rho^{-2}))^{1/2}}{s^{1/2}\rho^{2}}\mathrm{d}\rho+\int_{\kappa_{2}(b)}^{\kappa_{2}(s)}\rho^{d_{2}-1}\frac{(\phi_{2}^{-1}(s^{-1}))^{(d_{2}+1)/2}}{s}\mathrm{d}\rho\right)\mathrm{d}s.

Thus, using (
3.29
), (
3.30
), and (
3.31
) with
\phi_{2}
in place of
\phi_{1}
, we have

I_{3,1,2}(t,\vec{x},\vec{z})\leq C\kappa_{2}(b)\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}(\kappa_{2}(b))^{-1}=C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})},

and hence
I_{3,1}(t,\vec{x},\vec{z})\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}
.

Step 4-2.
Estimation of

Now we consider

and

\displaystyle I_{3,3,1}(t,\vec{x},\vec{z})

\displaystyle\leq C\kappa_{1}(b)\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\int_{b}^{\infty}\int_{|y_{1}|\leq 3\kappa_{1}(b)}|\nabla q_{1}(s,y_{1})|\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mathrm{d}s.

\displaystyle I_{3,3,1}(t,\vec{x},\vec{z})

\displaystyle\leq C\kappa_{1}(b)\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\int_{b}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{3\kappa_{1}(s)}\rho^{d_{1}-1}s^{-1}(\phi^{-1}_{1}(s^{-1}))^{\frac{d_{1}+1}{2}}\mathrm{d}\rho\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\kappa_{1}(b)\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\int_{b}^{\infty}s^{-1}(\phi_{1}(s^{-1}))^{1/2}\mathrm{d}s\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}.

.

Proof of Theorem
2.13
.

We use the Fefferman-Stein theorem (see
e.g.

[
49
, Theorem I.3.1., Theorem IV.2.2.]
) and the Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem (see e.g.
[
24
, Theorem 1.3.2.]
). We only remark that due to (
2.6
), the cubes
Q_{b}(s,y)
satisfy the conditions (i)-(iv) in
[
49
, Section 1.1]
and map
f\mapsto\mathcal{G}f
is sublinear.

Step 1.
We prove (
2.20
) for the case
p=q
. First assume that
p\geq 2
. Then by Lemma
3.1
and the Fefferman-Stein theorem, for any
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})\cap L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
, we have
\|(\mathcal{G}f)^{\#}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}
.
By (
3.22
),

\|(\mathcal{G}f)^{\#}\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}
.
Hence by the Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem, for any
p\in[2,\infty)
there exists a constant
C
such that
\|(\mathcal{G}f)^{\#}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}
.
Finally, by the Fefferman-Stein theorem, we get (
2.20
) for the case
p=q\geq 2
.

Now let
p\in(1,2)
. Take
f,g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
and
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}\in(2,\infty)
. Using the standard duality argument, we can check that

(3.33)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}g(t,\vec{x})\mathcal{G}f(t,\vec{x})\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\mathcal{G}\tilde{g}(t,\vec{x})f(-t,-\vec{x})\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}t,

where
\tilde{g}(t,\vec{x})=g(-t,-\vec{x})
. By Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}g(t,\vec{x})\mathcal{G}f(t,\vec{x})\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq\|f\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\|\mathcal{G}\tilde{g}\|_{L_{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\|g\|_{L_{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}.

Since
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
is arbitrary, we have
\mathcal{G}f\in L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
and (
2.20
) is also proved for
p\in(1,2)
.

Step 2.
Due to the symmetry, we only consider
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\ell=2
. Now we prove (
2.20
) for general
p,q\in(1,\infty)
. Define
q_{1}(t)p_{2}(t):=0
for
t\leq 0
. For each
(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define the operator
\mathcal{K}(t,s)
as follows:

\mathcal{K}(t,s)f(\vec{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}q_{1}(t-s,x_{1}-y_{1})p_{2}(t-s,x_{2}-y_{2})f(\vec{y})\mathrm{d}\vec{y},\quad f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
. Then,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{K}(t,s)f\|_{L_{p}}\leq\|f\|_{L_{p}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}|q_{1}(t-s,y_{1})|\mathrm{d}y_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}p_{2}(t-s,y_{2})\mathrm{d}y_{2}\leq C(t-s)^{-1}\|f\|_{L_{p}}.

Hence the operator
\mathcal{K}(t,s)
is uniquenly extendible to
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
t\neq s
. Denote

Q:=[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta),\quad Q^{*}:=[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}+2\delta),\quad\delta>0.

Note that for
t\notin Q^{*}
and
s_{1},s_{2}\in Q
, we have
|s_{1}-s_{2}|\leq\delta
and
|t-(t_{0}+\delta)|\geq\delta
.
Also for such
t,s_{1},s_{2}
, and for any
f\in L_{p}
such that
\|f\|_{L_{p}}=1
, using Minkowski’s inequality and the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{K}(t,s_{1})f-\mathcal{K}(t,s_{2})f\|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{t}q_{1}(t-us_{1}-(1-u)s_{2},y_{1})||s_{1}-s_{2}|\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}y_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+C(t-s_{2})^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{t}p_{2}(t-us_{1}-(1-u)s_{2},y_{2})||s_{1}-s_{2}|\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}y_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C|s_{1}-s_{2}|}{(t-(t_{0}+\delta))^{2}},

where the last inequality holds due to (
3.8
). Here, recall that
\mathcal{K}(t,s)=0
if
t\leq s
. Hence,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{K}(t,s_{1})-\mathcal{K}(t,s_{2})\|_{\Lambda}\leq\frac{C|s_{1}-s_{2}|}{(t-(t_{0}+\delta))^{2}}.

where
\|\cdot\|_{\Lambda}
denotes the operator norm on
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus Q^{*}}\|\mathcal{K}(t,s_{1})-\mathcal{K}(t,s_{2})\|_{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}t\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus Q^{*}}\frac{|s_{1}-s_{2}|}{(t-(t_{0}+\delta))^{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C|s_{1}-s_{2}|\int_{|t-(t_{0}+\delta)|\geq\delta}\frac{1}{(t-(t_{0}+\delta))^{2}}\mathrm{d}t\leq N\delta\int_{\delta}^{\infty}t^{-2}\mathrm{d}t\leq C.

Furthermore, by following the argument in
[
37
, Section 7]
, one can easily check that for almost every
t
outside of the support of
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,

\mathcal{G}_{1}f(t,\vec{x})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}(t,s)f(s,\vec{x})\mathrm{d}s

where
\mathcal{G}_{1}
denotes the extension to
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
which is verified in Step 1. Hence, by the Banach space-valued version of the Calderón-Zygmund theorem (
e.g.

[
37
, Theorem 4.1]
), our assertion is proved for
1<q\leq p
.

For
1<p<q<\infty
, define
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}
and
q^{\prime}=\frac{q}{q-1}
. By (
3.33
) and Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}g(t,\vec{x})\mathcal{G}f(t,\vec{x})\mathrm{d}\vec{x}\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{G}\tilde{g}(s,\vec{y})f(-s,-\vec{y})\mathrm{d}\vec{y}\right)\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|f(-s,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}}\|\mathcal{G}\tilde{g}(s,\cdot)\|_{L_{p^{\prime}}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq N\|f\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{R};L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\|g\|_{L_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R};L_{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}

for any
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
, where the last inequality holds due to
1<q^{\prime}<p^{\prime}
. Since
g
is arbitrary, we have (
2.20
) for all
p,q\in(1,\infty)
.
The theorem is proved.
∎

4.
Applications

In this section, we explore various applications derived from our main results presented in Theorem
2.8
. We begin by investigating the solvability of the elliptic equation.

Theorem 4.1

(Elliptic equation)
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
Suppose that
\vec{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{\ell})
is a vector of Bernstein functions satisfying Assumption
2.2
with drift
\vec{b}_{0}=(b_{01},\dots,b_{0\ell})
and vector of Lévy measures
\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y})
defined in (
2.4
).
For a vector of measurable function

\vec{a}(\vec{y}):=(a_{1}(y_{1}),\cdots,a_{\ell}(y_{\ell}))

satisfying
a_{i}(y_{i}),\in[c_{1},c_{1}^{-1}]
for all
i=1,\cdots,\ell

with a positive constant
c_{1}
, define the operator
\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}
as

\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}u(\vec{x}):=\vec{b}_{0}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}}u(\vec{x})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}}(u(\vec{x}+\vec{y})-u(\vec{x})-\vec{y}\cdot\nabla_{\vec{x}}u(\vec{x})\mathbf{1}_{|\vec{y}|\leq 1})\vec{a}(\vec{y})\cdot\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y}).

Then for any
\lambda>0
, and
f\in H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}
, the equation

(4.1)

\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}u-\lambda u=f

admits a unique solution
u\in H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{p}
, and we have

\|u\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma+2}_{p}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}_{p}},

where
C=C(\vec{b}_{0},d,c_{0},c_{1},\delta_{0},p,\gamma,\lambda)
.
Moreover,

\|(\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})u\|_{H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}}+\lambda\|u\|_{H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}}\leq C_{0}\|f\|_{H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}},

where
C_{0}=C_{0}(d,c_{0},\delta_{0},p,\gamma)
.

Proof.

Due to the isometry
(1-\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})^{-\gamma/2}:H_{p}^{\vec{\phi},\gamma}\to L_{p}
, we only consider the case
\gamma=0
. Also, we may further assume that
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
\lambda>0
be given.
Define

G_{\lambda}(\vec{x}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}p(t,\vec{x})\mathrm{d}t.

Here
p
is the transition density function of a Lévy process
X
whose triplet is
(0,D,\nu)
, where

D:=\diag(\vec{b}_{0}),\quad\nu(B):=\int_{B}\vec{a}(\vec{y})\cdot\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y}).

Now, we let
u:=G_{\lambda}\ast(-f)
. Clearly,
u
is a classical solution to

\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}u-\lambda u=f

and we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle\lambda\|u\|_{L_{p}}\leq\lambda\|G_{\lambda}\|_{L_{1}}\|f\|_{L_{p}}\leq\|f\|_{L_{p}}.

If we take
v(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}u(x)-u(x)
, then
v
satisfies (
2.8
) with
-\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}f+\mathcal{L}^{\vec{a},\vec{b}}u
in place of
f
.
By Theorem
2.8
and Lemma
2.15
, if we let
g(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda pt}\mathrm{d}t
, then we have

g(T)\times\|(\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})u\|^{p}_{L_{p}}\leq C\left(g(T)\times\|f\|^{p}_{L_{p}}+T\|(\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})u\|^{p}_{L_{p}}\right).

By letting
T
sufficiently large so that
g(T)>2CT
, we prove the estimation, and thus theorem.
∎

The following theorem establishes the solvability of parabolic equations with the infinitesimal generators of SBMs as the second application of our main results. Note that our proof gives a simpler way without revisiting the proof of Theorem
2.13
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
1<p,q<\infty
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and
0<T<\infty
. Suppose that
\phi
is a Bernstein function with drift
b_{0}
satisfying scaling condition (
2.5
). For measurable functions

a(t,\vec{y})\in[c_{1},c_{1}^{-1}],\quad b(t)\in[c_{1}b_{0},c_{1}^{-1}b_{0}]\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

define the operator
\mathcal{L}^{a,b}(t)
follows

\mathcal{L}^{a,b}(t)h(\vec{x}):=b(t)\Delta h(\vec{x})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(h(\vec{x}+\vec{y})-h(\vec{x})-\nabla_{\vec{x}}h(\vec{x})\cdot\vec{y}\mathbf{1}_{|\vec{y}|\leq 1})a(t,\vec{y})j(|\vec{y}|)\mathrm{d}\vec{y}.

Here,
j(|\vec{y}|)
is a jump kernel of
\phi(\Delta)
.
Then for any
f\in H_{q,p}^{\phi,\gamma}(T)
, the equation

\partial_{t}u(t,\vec{x})=\mathcal{L}^{a,b}(t)u(t,\vec{x})+f(t,\vec{x}),\quad t>0,\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}\,;\quad u(0,\vec{x})=0,\quad\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\vec{d}}

admits a unique solution
u
in the class
\mathbb{H}_{q,p,0}^{\phi,\gamma+2}(T)
, and we have

\|u\|_{\mathbb{H}_{q,p}^{\phi,\gamma+2}(T)}\leq C\|f\|_{H_{q,p}^{\phi,\gamma}(T)},

where
C=C(d,\delta_{0},c_{0},c_{1},p,q,\gamma,T)
.
Moreover,

\|\phi(\Delta)u\|_{H_{q,p}^{\phi,\gamma}(T)}+\|\mathcal{L}^{a,b}u\|_{H_{q,p}^{\phi,\gamma}(T)}\leq C_{0}\|f\|_{H_{q,p}^{\phi,\gamma}(T)},

where
C_{0}=C_{0}(d,\delta_{0},c_{0},c_{1},p,q,\gamma)
.

Proof.

We only consider the estimation of solutions when
\gamma=0
(recall Remark
2.1
), and
a(t,\vec{y})=1
and
b(t)=b_{0}
because we can handle the general case by following the proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Let
\vec{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{\ell})
with
\phi_{i}=\phi
for all
i=1,\dots,\ell
, and let
\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y})
be a vector of Lévy measure defined in (
2.4
).
Then, there exists a Lévy process
\vec{Y}
with triplet
(0,0,\nu)
, where
\nu(\mathrm{d}\vec{y}):=j(|\vec{y}|)\mathrm{d}\vec{y}-\vec{1}\cdot\vec{J}(\mathrm{d}\vec{y})
.
By Lemma
2.12
,

v_{n}(t,\vec{x},\omega):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\omega^{\prime}}[f_{n}(s,\vec{x}-\vec{Y}_{s}(\omega)+\vec{X}_{t-s}(\omega^{\prime}))]\mathrm{d}s\in C^{1,\infty}_{p}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathbb{H}^{\vec{\phi},2}_{q,p}(T)

is a unique classical solution of

\partial_{t}v_{n}(t,\vec{x},\omega)=(\vec{\phi}\cdot\Delta_{\vec{d}})v_{n}(t,\vec{x},\omega)+f_{n}(t,\vec{x}-\vec{Y}_{t}(\omega)),

where
f_{n}\in C_{p}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is an approximating sequence of
f
in
L_{q,p}(T)
and
\vec{X}=(X^{1},\cdots,X^{\ell})
is an IASBM corresponding to
\vec{\phi}
. Hence, by Theorem
2.8
,
v_{n}
satisfies

(4.3)

\|v_{n}\|_{H^{\vec{\phi},2}_{q,p}(T)}\leq C\|f_{n}\|_{L_{q,p}(T)}.

Let
u_{n}(t,\vec{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[v_{n}(t,\vec{x}+\vec{Y}_{t}(\omega),\omega)]
.
Clearly,
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C_{p}^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
u_{n}(0,\vec{x})=0
. Using Fubini’s theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{d}[u_{n}(t,\cdot)](\vec{\xi})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{\xi}\cdot\vec{Y}_{t}(\omega)}\mathcal{F}_{d}[v_{n}(t,\cdot,\omega)](\vec{\xi})]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{\xi}\cdot(\vec{Y}_{t}(\omega)-\vec{Y}_{s}(\omega))}]\mathbb{E}_{\omega^{\prime}}[\mathrm{e}^{i\vec{\xi}\cdot\vec{X}_{t-s}(\omega^{\prime})}]\mathcal{F}_{d}[f_{n}(s,\cdot)](\vec{\xi})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{d}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[f_{n}(s,\cdot+\vec{Z}_{t-s})]\mathrm{d}s\right](\vec{\xi}),

where
\vec{Z}
is an SBM corresponding to
\phi
. Therefore, by Lemma
2.12
,
u_{n}
is a unique classical solution of
